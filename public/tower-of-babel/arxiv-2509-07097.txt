Abstract
Fast radio bursts (FRBs) provide a sensitive probe of diffuse baryons: their dispersion measures (DMs) measure electron density independent of temperature and scale linearly with gas density. This makes them particularly well suited to studying the intragroup medium (IGrM), where traditional probes such as X-ray emission and the SZ effect are weak. Evidence suggests that the baryon content of group mass halos ( M 500 ∼ 10 13 M_{500}\sim 10^{13} – 10 14 10^{14} M ⊙ ) is strongly affected by galactic feedback, creating deviations from cluster scaling relations. Three FRBs from the first CHIME/FRB Outrigger sample come from host galaxies found within or behind galaxy clusters and groups. Using integrated halo density models, we estimate the DM contribution of each ICM/IGrM, accounting for uncertainties in halo mass and the distance to the host galaxy relative to the cluster center. For the more massive halos, predicted cluster DMs agree with the extragalactic DM budget. One burst, FRB 20230703A, intersects three groups yet has a low extragalactic DM. By comparing model predictions with the measured DM, we constrain the baryon fraction f g ​ ( R ) f_{g}(R) in these halos. Comparing with published M M – f g f_{g} relations, we find consistency with recent eROSITA results at R 500 R_{500} , mild tension at R 200 R_{200} , and strong tensions with earlier X-ray–based relations. As CHIME/FRB Outriggers build a large catalog of localized FRBs, many additional sightlines through groups and clusters will be obtained. These will enable systematic tests of intragroup and intracluster gas properties and sharpen constraints on the distribution of baryons in massive halos.



I
Introduction

Galaxy clusters, with masses
\sim 10^{14}
–
10^{15}
M
⊙
, are the largest gravitationally-bound structures in the universe, and are exceptionally useful tools in cosmology and astrophysics. Their growth is dominated by gravity, due to their large masses, leading clusters to have thermodynamic properties that depend only on mass and redshift, and are independent of formation history
(
Kaiser 1986
)
. The bulk of baryons in clusters exists in a hot (
kT_{x}\sim 2
–
4
keV), centralized
intracluster medium
(ICM), which is typically observable in X-rays and through the thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect (tSZ). The large numbers of galaxies in clusters, as well as the resolvable ICM, allow for robust measurements of the total halo mass
(
Pratt et al. 2010
)
. Being at the higher end of the halo mass function, cluster mass measurements are highly constraining on the parameters
\Omega_{m}
(the dark matter mass fraction) and
\sigma_{8}
(the matter power spectrum amplitude at 8
h^{-1}
Mpc)
(
Allen et al. 2011
;
Kravtsov & Borgani 2012
)
.

One quantity of cosmological interest is the baryon fraction within the halo relative to the cosmic baryon fraction
f_{B}=\Omega_{b}/\Omega_{m}
:

f_{g}(r)=\frac{M_{b}(r)}{f_{B}M_{c}(r)},

(1)

where
M_{b}(r)
and
M_{c}(r)
are the total baryonic and cold dark matter mass contained within radius
r
, respectively. This is most commonly quoted as
f_{g,500}\equiv f_{g}(R_{500})
, as X-ray observations are typically only sensitive out to
R_{500}
(the radius within which the mean matter density if 500
\times
the critical density). Due to their strong binding energy compared with astrophysical processes within them, clusters are expected to retain nearly all of the baryons they formed with, such that
f_{g}(R_{v})\approx 1
at the virial radius
R_{v}
.

R_{500}<R_{200}<R_{v}
, all of which are defined in App.

Measurements of
f_{g}
at smaller radii, despite not capturing the full extent of the halo, have been successfully used as complementary probes of various cosmological parameters. Under the assumption that
f_{g}
is largely independent of redshift for massive, relaxed clusters, its scaling with angular diameter distance depends on
\Omega_{M}
and
\Omega_{\Lambda}

(
Mantz et al. 2022
;
Ettori et al. 2009
;
Sasaki 1996
;
Pen 1997
)
. In combination with other probes, the gas fraction has also been used to constrain deviations in the fine structure constant
(
Ferreira et al. 2024
)
, and can potentially break degeneracies among dark sector models
(
Barbosa et al. 2024
)
.

Lower-mass
galaxy groups
(
\sim 10^{13}
M
⊙
) are an important piece of the picture, bridging gap between massive galaxy clusters and individual galaxy evolution. Since structure formation proceeds hierarchically, with lower-mass groups forming first and merging into more massive structures, group evolution is key to understanding the formation of clusters, as well as how environments of galaxies may influence their development. For cosmology,
10^{13.5}
M
⊙
halos outnumber
10^{14.5}
by roughly a factor of 20
(
Murray et al. 2013
)
, creating potential for more powerful statistics. However, being relatively smaller in scale and binding energy, the macroscopic properties of galaxy groups are more affected by the non-gravitational behavior of member galaxies, such as AGN and stellar feedback. An observable result of feedback is that the gas fraction
f_{g,500}
, is significantly lower than seen in higher-mass halos. This has been challenging to model in hydrodynamic simulations, as excessive feedback can quench star formation in member galaxies.
Eckert et al. 2021
compiles a range of simulated values for
f_{g,500}
, showing that despite recent simulations being calibrated to observed stellar masses, different feedback prescriptions make strongly different predictions for IGrM baryon content.

Popesso et al. 2024
(hereafter, P24) estimated
f_{g}
for a range of halo masses using stacked data from eROSITA, providing both new estimates of
f_{g,500}
and the first ever estimates of
f_{g,200}
for galaxy groups, even extending down to
M_{500}\sim 10^{12.5}
halos. They find power-law fits to the
f_{g}
–
M_{\text{halo}}
relations in decent agreement with prior fits, but do find that
f_{g}
of low-mass halos are less than half that predicted by previous studies. They interpret this as a selection effect – previous studies only detected the more X-ray luminous group halos, which may be more skewed to higher
f_{g}
– highlighting the importance of more sensitive X-ray surveys like eROSITA.

The IGrM of lower-mass groups has been observed in metal absorption
(
Nielsen et al. 2018
;
Stocke et al. 2019
)
and emission
(
Epinat et al. 2024
)
, as well as in 21 cm
(
Koribalski et al. 2020
;
Borthakur et al. 2010
;
Pisano et al. 2004
)
, revealing a complex, dynamic environment co-evolving with member galaxies. A more recent probe made possible by high-resolution radio surveying instruments is the RM grid technique
(
Beck & Gaensler 2004
)
, which has detected the presence of a magnetized IGrM through the rotation measures of unresolved background radio sources
(
Anderson et al. 2024
)
.

Fast Radio Bursts provide a uniquely clean measure of baryons via their dispersion measures (DMs), which are equal to the column density of free electrons along the path taken by the burst. When the host galaxy of an FRB is known (the FRB is “localized”), this can be used to constrain properties of intervening halos and detect invisible baryons in the intergalactic medium.
Macquart et al. 2020
was the first to use the roughly linear relationship between DM and z to detect the so-called “missing baryons”
(
Fukugita et al. 1998
)
. Analyses by
James et al. 2022
,
Baptista et al. 2024
, and
Connor et al. 2025
each fitted the distribution of DMs vs redshift in order to estimate the distribution of host galaxy DMs and constrain the strength of feedback in intervening halos. By surveying foreground galaxies and modeling their contributions to the total DM, “foreground mapping” techniques aim to achieve tighter model constraints without relying on large samples of FRBs.
Lee et al. 2022
predicted with simulations that such techniques would be 25
\times
more constraining than FRB data alone, and that foreground mapping 30 FRB sightlines could constrain the cosmic baryon fraction to better than 10%. The first data release of the FLIMFLAM survey constrained the fractions of baryons in circumgalactic media (CGM) and IGM to roughly 50% and 20% each, as well as estimating the host DM contribution, using only 8 FRB sightlines
(
Khrykin et al. 2024b
)
.

Most foreground mapping studies have utilized a
Modified Navarro-Frenk-White profile
(mNFW) for halo baryon density, introduced by
Prochaska & Zheng 2019
and
Mathews & Prochaska 2017
, to describe all foreground galaxy halos
(
Simha et al. 2020
;
Simha et al. 2021
;
Simha et al. 2023
)
. The mNFW profile is typically much flatter at large radii than empirical models of the ICM predict, raising the question of how accurately it can model such a differently shaped halo.
Khrykin et al. 2024a
demonstrated with SIMBA simulation data that the mNFW model is accurate to within a factor of 10 across a range of halo masses, but stressed the importance of including a mass-dependent baryon fraction.
Huang et al. 2025
adopted an empirically-derived model from
Vikhlinin et al. 2006
to describe halos more massive than
10^{13.5}
M
⊙
while developing their model of the local universe, and
Faber et al. 2024
also used this model for an intervening cluster on a single FRB sightline. In contrast,
Connor et al. 2025
worked out a distribution of likely
\text{DM}_{\text{ICM}}
values from along two FRB sightlines based on distributions of other components, and compared this to
\text{DM}_{\text{ICM}}
from simulated halos, rather than attempt to model the cluster halos themselves.

This paper examines three FRBs from the first CHIME/FRB Outrigger sample
(
CHIME/FRB Collaboration et al. 2025
)
whose host galaxies are members of or lie behind galaxy clusters and groups. One of these is in the infall region of Abell 576
(
Rines et al. 2000
)
, a massive cluster at
z=0.0382
, far beyond the region of visible X-ray emission, but displaying a surprisingly large DM for its distance. The second FRB host is embedded in Abell 924, a more massive Abell cluster at
z=0.1430
that shows evidence of substructure near its core
(
Dupke et al. 2007
)
. The third FRB host is a member of the group [WH24] J121839.5+484102 (hereafter WH24-J1218) at
z=0.1183
, and lies behind the groups NSCS J121847+484410 (NSCS-J1218) at
z=0.0448
and [Tully15] Nest 100008 (TullyN08) at
z=0.0024
. All of these are low-mass groups, with no detectable SZ signal, though NSCS-J1218 has a measured X-ray temperature
(
Lopes et al. 2009
)
. NSCS-J1218 has been identified as a member of the supercluster MSCC310 by
Santiago-Bautista et al. 2020
, at a junction of cosmic web filaments.

For each of these bursts, we estimate potential contributions to the total extragalactic DM from the intervening cluster or group environments based on three different electron density models. The first is the aforementioned mNFW profile, while the other two are derived from the empirical
Universal Pressure Profile
(UPP) of
Arnaud et al. 2010
, with one assuming an isothermal temperature profile and the other applying an empirical temperature profile fitted to low-Z cool-core galaxy clusters
(
Baldi et al. 2012
)
. Theses models are fitted to X-ray profiles of the ICMs of low-redshift massive clusters (
10^{14}
–
10^{15}
M
⊙
). Subsequently, for each model, we report the allowed ranged of
f_{g}
.

To get the best possible
M_{500}
for each halo, we gather all available mass estimates for each group and consider the strengths and weaknesses of different mass estimators. For galaxy clusters with resolved X-ray profiles, one can apply conditions of hydrostatic equilibrium to derive both the mass and concentration of the dark matter halo
(
Vikhlinin et al. 2006
;
Evrard et al. 1996
)
. The self-similarity of clusters leads to several simple scaling relations between observable quantities, such as average X-ray or optical luminosity, or cluster richness, and the virial mass and X-ray temperature
(
Lovisari et al. 2015
)
. Several catalogs
(
Wen et al. 2012
;
Wen & Han 2024
)
report masses by calibrating these scaling relations to a handful of resolved X-ray clusters and applying them other groups and clusters. Without X-ray data, the positions and velocities of member galaxies can provide a direct estimate of the halo mass, under the assumption that the member galaxies are in hydrostatic equilibrium. One can solve the Jeans equation to relate the virial mass and concentration to the projected velocity dispersion and galaxy number density as functions of radius, or apply the virial theorem directly to relate the mass to the total projected velocity dispersion
(
Girardi et al. 1998
, e.g.,)
. The catalogs of
Tully 2015a
and
Lopes et al. 2009
report masses derived from the dynamics of member galaxies. For small numbers of galaxies, however, scaling relations have been shown to better recover halo masses in simulations
(
Marini et al. 2025
)
.

To get
c_{500}
, we use the M – c relation derived from the Magneticum simulation suite whenever a concentration parameter is not available from X-ray profile fitting
(
Ragagnin et al. 2019
)
. Empirical M – c relations have been shown to be somewhat inconsistent depending on the method used to calculate halo mass
(
Groener et al. 2016
)
, so we adopt a simulation-based relation for simplicity.

FRB 20230203A

Our search through the DESI cluster catalog of

II.3.3
Second Foreground Group

A search through the

of a candidate group, updating the total luminosity, and re-computing

In addition to the luminosity-mass estimate, Tully calculates a virial mass dynamically using the formulae in
Tully 2015b

\sigma_{P}=366

-1
, estimated from
N_{\text{gal}}=65

convention and our assumed cosmology, we apply the following equation from

R_{\Delta}=R_{A}(\rho_{V}/(\Delta\rho_{c}))^{1/2.4},

(2)

where we take the aperture radius

⊙

III
ICM / IGrM Models

To estimate the contribution of a foreground halo to an FRB’s dispersion, we need a model of the electron density within the halo. While the total mass density is commonly described by the Navarro, Frenk, and White profile (NFW)
(
Navarro et al. 1997
)
, the baryons (and hence, free electrons) take on a different structure due to hydrodynamic forces. The mNFW profile is commonly used for galaxy-scale halos, as it is a flexible model intended to describe the feedback-driven migration of baryons from the inner to outer regions of the halo. ICM models are derived from conditions of hydrostatic equilibrium, and are usually written to relate observables like X-ray luminosity and galaxy velocity dispersion to the thermodynamic properties of the gas. Due to the self-similarity of massive clusters, density profiles that depend only on the total mass can fit fairly well to observations, but are typically not extrapolated beyond
R_{500}
. In this section, we will discuss the Modified NFW in more detail, as well as the empirical UPP for massive cluster ICMs.

III.1
Modified NFW

The mNFW baryon density profile is defined as

\rho_{b}(y)=\frac{f_{g}(R_{v})f_{b}\rho_{0}}{(cy)^{1-\alpha}(y_{0}+cy)^{2+\alpha}},

(3)

where
y=r/r_{v}
is the radial position normalized to the virial radius
r_{v}
, and
c
is the NFW concentration parameter. The density
\rho_{0}=Mg_{b}(c)/(4\pi(r_{v}/c)^{3})
, where the function
g_{b}
characterizes the fraction of mass contained within a given radius. The electron number density is
n_{e}(y)=\rho_{b}(y)\times\mu_{e}/(\mu m_{p})
, where
\mu_{e}=1.1667
is the number of free electrons per hydrogen assuming full ionization a 25% helium mass fraction, and
\mu=1.33
is the mean molecular weight per hydrogen. For FRB foreground modeling,
y_{0}=\alpha=2
is typically assumed, as this closesly matches earlier models of galactic halos
(
Prochaska & Zheng 2019
)
. The resulting profile increases from the core out to twice the scale radius, and then drops off as
r^{-3}
at large radii.

In Eq.
3
, we’ve included the free baryon fraction
f_{g}
as a parameter which depends on the virial radius convention used. This is equivalent to the
f_{\text{hot}}
parameter given in other literature and in the
frb
Python package
3
3

3

http://github.com/FRBs/frb
, except here we’ve made explicit that it depends on the virial radius used. In the
frb
Python package, the default convention uses the formula from
Bryan & Norman 1998
(denoted by subscript “v” here, see App
B
). We choose to use the
\Delta=500
convention here for consistency with the UPP model (see next section). Under the default virial convention,
f_{\text{hot}}
is usually chosen to be about
0.9
for cluster-mass halos and
0.75
for galaxy-scale halos
(
Lee et al. 2023
, e.g.)
.

Despite its success for modeling CGMs, mNFW’s ability to model ICM gas has yet to be demonstrated.
Lee et al. 2023
used it to model the contributions of two clusters in the foreground of FRB 20190520B, and found it consistent with the total observed DM.
Huang et al. 2025
applied the modified NFW model only for halos with masses less than
10^{13.5}
M
⊙
, and used a scaled version of the model fitted to Abell 907 by
Vikhlinin et al. 2006
for more massive halos. The
Vikhlinin et al. 2006
model has a very flexible form of multiple power laws, which they fitted to Chandra observations of massive clusters. While it provides a good fit for each cluster profile, it is not guaranteed that the fit to a given cluster in their sample should scale naturally to others.

III.2
Universal Pressure Profile

For comparison we derive two electron density models from the UPP
(
Arnaud et al. 2010
)
, which has well-motivated scaling with mass and redshift. Among averaged scaled quantities, the pressure is the least affected by dynamical history and feedback effects
(
Arnaud et al. 2010
)
. The UPP was derived by fitting X-ray observations of the REXCESS sample of galaxy clusters (
z<0.2
,
10^{14}<M_{500}/M_{\odot}<10^{15}
) to a generalized NFW form, after normalizing to characteristic values based on the virial mass of each. The result is

\displaystyle p(y)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{M_{500}}{4.28\times 10^{14}h^{-1}M_{\odot}}\right)^{\alpha_{P}(y)}\frac{P_{0}}{(c_{500}y)^{\gamma}(1+(c_{500}y)^{\alpha})^{(\beta-\gamma)/\alpha}}

(4)

\displaystyle\alpha_{P}(y)

\displaystyle=0.22\left(1-\frac{(y/0.5)^{3}}{1+(y/0.5)^{3}}\right)

(5)

\displaystyle P(y)

\displaystyle=P_{500}p(y).

(6)

Here,
P_{500}=n_{e,500}k_{B}T_{500}
is the characteristic pressure of the halo,
n_{e,500}=500\rho_{c}(z)/(\mu_{e}m_{p})
is a characteristic electron density, and
\rho_{c}
is the critical density. The exponent function
\alpha_{P}
comes from the relationship between an SZ-effect mass proxy and
M_{500}
used by
Arnaud et al. 2010
. The characteristic temperature is taken as

k_{B}T_{500}=\mu m_{p}GM_{500}/2R_{500},

(7)

which is the temperature of a singular isothermal sphere, with
\mu=0.6
the mean molecular weight of primordial gas. The best-fit parameters are
[P_{0},c_{500},\gamma,\alpha,\beta]=[4.980h^{-3/2},1.177,0.3081,1.0510,5.4905]
. The result is a profile that drops off as
\sim r^{-5}
at large radii, much faster than the mNFW profile.

We note that
Le Brun et al. 2015
compared simulated cluster ICMs across a wide range of mass bins (down to
10^{12}
M
⊙
) and found that pressure profiles drop off less steeply at large radii (r >
r_{500}
) than the UPP predicts. They also found that the UPP overestimates the pressure at small radii for low mass-halos with feedback.
Le Brun et al. 2015
point out that their simulated pressure profiles are in agreement with tSZ pressure profile meausurements from Planck out to large radii
(
Planck Collaboration et al. 2013
)
.

To convert the pressure profile to a density profile, we apply two different temperature profiles. The simplest is to assume the halo gas is isothermal with temperature
T_{500}
given in Eq
7
, which is a decent approximation to start. For a more realistic temperature model, we use the power-law profile from
Baldi et al. 2012
fitted to the outer (
r>0.15R_{500}
) regions of XMM-Newton clusters:

\frac{T(r)}{T_{500}}=\frac{A}{(1+(x/0.4)^{2})^{\beta}_{0}}.

(8)

The authors fitted this form to data in three redshift bins, with the lowest bin (
0.1<z<0.2
) using data for 44 clusters from
Leccardi & Molendi 2008
, splitting the sample into cool-core (CC) and non-cool-core (NCC). The difference between the two is less than 16% up to
4R_{500}
, so we assume the CC model here. Table 8 of
Baldi et al. 2012
gives
A=1.09
and
\beta_{0}=0.24
.

From the UPP and each temperature profile, we obtain the electron number density as

n_{e}(y)=P(y)/k_{B}T(y),

(9)

where
T(y)
is given by the CC fit from
Baldi et al. 2012
for the “UPP Baldi” model, and
T(y)=T_{500}
for the “UPP Iso” model.

As empirical fits, the UPP-derived models implicitly assume a free baryon fraction
f_{g}
, which we would like to be a tunable parameter. To correct this, we integrate Eq.
9
numerically to calculate the implicit baryon fraction
\hat{n}_{0}
and then normalize the expression by this (see Appendix
A
). The result is

n_{e}(y)=\left(\frac{f_{B}\rho_{500}T_{500}}{\hat{n}_{0}\mu_{e}m_{p}}\right)\frac{p(y)}{T(y)}.

(10)

Mass dependence is included via the power-law factor in
p(y)
and in the definition of
T_{500}
. Redshift-dependence comes from
\rho_{500}
, which depends on the critical density.

IV.1
Depth Sampling

For an FRB host galaxy that is a member of a cluster or group, estimating the ICM contribution to dispersion depends on its depth within the cluster. The galaxy redshift
z_{g}
does not provide this distance information, since its peculiar motion within the cluster dominates over the Hubble flow on this scale. With some basic assumptions about the cluster’s dynamical state, however, we can sample a probability distribution for the galaxy’s position along the line of sight when calculating
\text{DM}_{\text{ICM}}
.

We assume that the cluster mass distribution follows an NFW profile and that galaxy positions trace dark matter. All distances will be given in units of the virial radius
R_{v}
, and velocities in units of the circular orbital velocity at the virial radius
V_{v}=\sqrt{GM_{v}/R_{v}}
(the “virial velocity”). Let
x
represent the distance along the line of sight relative to the distance of the cluster center,
b
be the projected distance of the host galaxy from the cluster center (the impact parameter), and
v_{\parallel}=c(z_{g}-z_{\text{cl}})/V_{v}
be the line of sight component of the peculiar velocity in units of the virial velocity.

The probability density function for galaxy position along the line of sight is proportional to the NFW density profile,

p(y)=\frac{A}{(cy)(1+cy)^{2}},

(12)

where
y=\sqrt{b^{2}+x^{2}}
is the radius,
c
is the NFW concentration parameter, and
A
is a normalization constant such that
\int p(x)dx=1
.

assume that the baryon fraction in the halo equals the cosmic value
f_{B}
when considering all mass within
R_{v}
(see App.
B
). This picture is clearly incompatible with FRB 20230703A, whose total available extragalactic
\text{DM}_{\text{ext}}=\text{DM}_{\text{obs}}-\text{DM}_{\text{MW}}
is lower than the predicted contributions from intervening halos. This supports the picture that the
closure radius
, where the halo baryon fraction equals
f_{B}
– must lie beyond
R_{v}
(as in P24).

For a given virial radius convention, we can set an upper limit on the baryon fraction within that radius by applying the total DM budget. Letting
\overline{\text{DM}}_{\text{ICM,R}}
be the DM estimate given by Eq.
11
integrated out to a maximum halocentric radius
R
. The maximum allowable baryon fraction up to that radius is

f^{\text{max}}_{g}(R)=\min\left(\frac{\text{DM}_{\text{ext}}-\text{DM}_{\text{host}}}{\overline{\text{DM}}_{\text{ICM,R}}},1\right).

(13)

\text{DM}_{\text{host}}
is the host DM and
\text{DM}_{\text{ext}}=\text{DM}_{\text{obs}}-\text{DM}_{\text{MW}}
is the total extragalactic DM. This is a conservative upper limit, neglecting additional contributions from the halo beyond
R
and any other intervening matter. To make this limit as conservative as possible, we will maximize the numerator by subtracting 20% from each
\text{DM}_{\text{MW}}
estimate as the recommended lower uncertainty of the NE2001 model
(
Cordes & Lazio 2002
)
and assuming
\text{DM}_{\text{host}}=0
.

We also minimize the denominator by taking the
1\sigma
lower bound of the total estimated foreground halo DM, using the following procedure for each halo model and repeating it for a range of maximum halo radii
R
:

•

For each halo, generate 500 mass samples.

•

For WH24-J1218 (the host group), generate 200 samples of the line of sight position for each mass sample.

•

For each mass / line of sight position sample, calculate the halo DM. This results in 500 DM estimates for the two foreground groups and 100,000 for the host group. Normalize the DMs to have
f_{g}(R_{\text{max}})=1
as per Section
IV
.

•

From this collection of DM estimates, take one DM for each halo at random and sum them together to get a total, and repeat this to get 1000 realizations of the total DM. For the host group, we only re-sample from the near-side distribution, to further minimize the total.

•

Take the
1\sigma
lower limit of this distribution as
\overline{\text{DM}}_{\text{ICM,R}}
.

This analysis assumes the same
f_{g}
for all three halos, but given their similar masses we consider this a safe assumption.

VI
Discussion & Conclusions

FRB dispersion can provide a strong upper limit on the baryon content of intervening halos, well beyond the radii typically observable in X-rays, since the FRB signal passes through the full extent of the halo. We have estimated DM contributions from foreground galaxy groups and clusters using three different ICM models, taking into account uncertainties in the masses of intervening halos, for three FRBs in the first CHIME-Outriggers host galaxy sample. For one of these (FRB 20231206A), we find the estimated DM contribution to be negligible despite the large extragalactic DM, most of its DM is from the host galaxy. While its
\text{DM}_{\text{host}}
is on the higher-end of what’s typical, it is not a severe outlier.. FRB 20230203A, embedded in the cluster ACO 924, has expected DM contributions in agreement with its total excess DM. The third sightline, to FRB 20230703A, is highly constraining on the baryon content in the group-mass halos that intersect it.

The total foreground DM on this last sightline, integrated out to the virial radius
R_{v}
defined in Eq.
B2
for each halo, is incompatible with the DM budget. This is largely due to the estimated contributions of NSCS-J1218, which alone exceeds the available DM budget, indicating that its closure radius is well beyond
R_{v}
. Integrating the DM to the smaller radii
R_{500}
and
R_{200}
, we compared our
f_{g}
limits to the recent eROSITA results
Popesso et al. 2024
, and found generally good agreement out to
R_{500}
but some tension out to
R_{200}
.

It is clear that group environments should be taken into consideration when modeling foreground DM contributions. Peculiar velocities of group / cluster members make relative distances ambiguous, complicating the process of identifying foreground galaxies when the FRB host is embedded in a cluster. Additionally, galaxies in high mass clusters are expected to have lost their CGMs to ram pressure stripping, which should reduce host DMs. Most significantly, intragroup/cluster media can contribute to the total DM in a way that is not captured by modeling individual galaxy halos. For comparison, suppose we had only considered galaxies along the line of sight to FRB 20230703A. Searching the LVS, and applying the stellar-to-halo mass relation of
Moster et al. 2013
, we can identify 5 galaxies that intersect the line of sight to within one virial radius, all members of NSCS-J1218. Assuming an mNFW profile normalized to
f_{g}(R_{v})=1
, we get a total DM contribution of 277 pc cm
-3
from all of these. In contrast, the mNFW profile model for the IGrM predicts a total of 326 pc cm
-3
(as in Table
3
) – compatible with the galaxies-only estimate to within its error bars, but still somewhat higher. We would also have missed the likely contributions from WH24-J1218 and TullyN08 on this sightline had we not identified known galaxy groups along this sightline.

Furthermore, we have found that the uncertainties in estimates of cluster masses can lead to large uncertainties in estimated DM contributions. For host galaxies embedded in clusters, there is an additional uncertainty from the host’s peculiar velocity. By using the NFW profile as an estimate of probabilities of galaxy positions, we can marginalize over this uncertainty, finding a largely bimodal
\text{DM}_{\text{ICM}}
distribution. For some ICM models and impact parameters, this implies that the expectation of
\text{DM}_{\text{ICM}}
for hosts galaxies on the near side is non-zero. It may be important to take this into account when assessing the total DM budgets of FRBs whose hosts are in clusters, rather than assume the host is close enough to the observer to have no contribution from the ICM.

In several cases in this sample, we are applying these halo density models outside of their intended domains. The UPP was derived from X-ray and SZ observations of halos with masses above
10^{14}
M
⊙
, which is not the case for the three groups along the sightline of FRB 20230703A, and within
r<R_{500}
, which is less than the impact factor of the FRB 20231206A sightline. Although
Lyskova et al. 2023
argue that a UPP-like profile should be accurate out to
3R_{500}
, the model used here was not fitted to that extent. Another factor to consider is that we have not included any IGM or large scale structure component to the DM. Recent work by
Hussaini et al. 2025
,
Wang et al. 2025
, and
Connor et al. 2025
has shown strong evidence, from localized FRBs, that only about 25% of baryons are expected to be bound in halos, with the rest in a diffuse IGM and in cosmic filaments. Assuming the relation from
Hussaini et al. 2025
, the mean cosmic DMs are 60.8, 110.6, and 137.6 pc cm
-3
for FRBs 20231206A, 20230703A, and 20230203A, respectively. In particular, the sightline to FRB 20230703A passes through a known supercluster MSCC-310, along a galaxy filament (Fig.
2
). Our spherically-symmetric models do not capture the likely anisotropy of this particular system. Likewise, Abell 924 shows evidence of being dynamically disturbed, but we have assumed ICM models that assume a system in equilibrium.

As CHIME/FRB Outriggers continue localizing FRBs, we expect to encounter many more sightlines crossing through group-scale massive halos. Integrating a halo mass function
(
Murray et al. 2013
;
Tinker et al. 2008
)
with the CHIME/FRB detection rate vs z from
Shin et al. 2023
, we find that about 40% of all FRBs that CHIME/FRB will detect will have passed through halos of mass
10^{13}
M
⊙
or greater. While limiting cases like FRB 20230703A may be useful for constraining baryon fractions in specific systems, it is more likely that upcoming X-ray survey data will be more useful as a tool to inform foreground models for FRB research. As recent FRB results have shown large fractions of baryons outside of collapsed halos
(
Connor et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025
;
Hussaini et al. 2025
)
, better models of halos themselves will be beneficial to help map this IGM structure.
