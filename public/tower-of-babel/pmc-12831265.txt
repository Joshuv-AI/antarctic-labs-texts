Performance and injury-related effects of low-load blood flow restriction training in amateur soccer players: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Begüm Kara Kaya, Ayşe Zengin Alpözgen, Mehmet Rahmi Alpözgen, Göksel Dikmen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBlood flow restriction (BFR) has gained interest in its application in exercise and potential benefits. This study aimed to investigate the effects of low-load, low-frequency BFR training compared with traditional high-intensity training on soccer-related performance outcomes and injury indicators in amateur athletes.MethodsTwenty licensed male soccer players competing in a university-level league were randomized to either a BFR group (n = 10, 22.3 ± 1.3 years) or a control group (n = 10, 22.1 ± 1.8 years) for 6 weeks of training. Pre- and post-intervention assessments included muscle strength-endurance (isokinetic dynamometer), power (vertical jump test), aerobic endurance (Yo-Yo level 1 test), speed-agility (T test, Y-shaped reactive agility test), dynamic balance (Y test), thigh circumference, fatigue (blood lactate, Borg scale), and sportive self-confidence. Perceived change (GROC) was assessed post-intervention. Injury data were tracked for six months following the intervention.ResultsBFR improved dominant limb flexor strength, endurance, aerobic capacity, speed, and dynamic balance (p < 0.05, Cohen’s d: 0.40–1.06). Both groups showed gains in power, agility, and self-confidence (p < 0.05, Cohen’s d: BFR = 0.51–1.35; Control = 0.40–1.01), whereas hypertrophy (p = 0.026, Pη2 = 0.24) and GROC scores (p = 0.022) were significantly higher in the BFR group than in the control group. No differences were observed in terms of fatigue. The standard error of the mean values for significant within-group changes ranged from 0.03 to 8.64. Although the injury rate was lower in the BFR group, this trend was not significant (p > 0.05).ConclusionBFR appears to be a feasible, load-efficient, and well-tolerated approach to improve performance parameters in amateur soccer players, with potential benefits for lower-limb injury prevention.Trial registrationPrior to participant enrollment, this study was prospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05452109 first submitted on June 1, 2022).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-025-01416-8.

Introduction

Soccer is an intermittent, high-intensity sport involving frequent transitions between sprinting, decelerating, jumping, tackling, and rapid changes of direction. Optimal performance depends on the integration of physical, technical, tactical, and psychological abilities [1–3]. These high physical demands expose players to considerable mechanical and metabolic stress, contributing to one of the highest injury rates among team sports [4]. Injury incidence in competitive and academy soccer is approximately 2.4 (0.7–4.8) per 1000 h of play, with 85% affecting the lower limbs, particularly the thigh (23%), and muscle strains being the most common type (29%) [5]. Modifiable risk factors include deficits in strength, power, flexibility, aerobic capacity, and postural stability [6, 7]. Such injuries impair athletic performance and availability, with notable economic and competitive consequences for clubs [8, 9]. Exercise-based prevention programs can reduce non-contact musculoskeletal injury by about 23% [10], highlighting the importance of training strategies that enhance performance while minimizing injury risk [11, 12]. Additionally, multimodal approaches integrating strength, endurance, and proprioceptive training can further enhance performance while contributing to injury prevention [11–13].

High-intensity, low-repetition trainings are commonly used in soccer training to improve muscle strength and hypertrophy [14]. Although effective for isolated muscle development, such methods increase mechanical stress and fatigue accumulation, particularly under cumulative aerobic and anaerobic loads [15]. Nevertheless, performance-related adaptations can also be achieved through appropriately designed low-intensity training integrated strategically within training cycles [16, 17]. Soccer performance, however, requires the concurrent development of multiple physiological capacities, including strength, endurance, power, agility, and coordination [2, 11, 18] without substantially increasing the overall training load. Achieving this balance is challenging, as excessive intensity or volume can hinder recovery and increase injury risk. These limitations highlight the need for complementary strategies that can elicit comparable performance gains with lower mechanical demands [19].

Among complementary training strategies, blood flow restriction (BFR) has gained attention for inducing muscle adaptations with low mechanical loads. Applying external pressure during low-intensity exercise creates localized hypoxia and high metabolic stress, stimulating hypertrophy and strength gains comparable to high-intensity resistance training [20]. These mechanisms, including increased hormonal responses, fast-twitch fiber recruitment, and anabolic signaling, contribute to improvements in strength, endurance, and power [20, 21], which are essential for sprinting, agility, and match performance in soccer. Remarkably, these benefits can be achieved even when loads that are too low are typically used to elicit training adaptations in healthy athletes [17, 22, 23]. Importantly, by reducing mechanical load while maintaining neuromuscular activation, BFR training may also help mitigate overuse and load-related injury risk when appropriately integrated into soccer conditioning programs. Thus, BFR provides a time-efficient, low-load alternative that can be incorporated into soccer training without adding excessive mechanical stress or session volume.

Considering its advantages, BFR training may offer a valuable and time-efficient method for enhancing soccer performance while potentially reducing injury risk. Accordingly, several studies have examined its effects in athletic and soccer populations. Korkmaz et al. [17] and Judd et al. reported that BFR combined with resistance training enhanced muscle strength and architecture [24], while Chen [25] and Hosseini Kakhak et al. [16] observed improvements in strength, sprint, agility, and endurance performance with BFR during fitness or soccer-specific training. However, these studies were limited to short-term follow-ups and focused on isolated physical parameters rather than multidimensional soccer performance. Furthermore, long-term follow-up data on BFR’s influence on athletes remain scarce [26]. Therefore, the present study aimed to address this gap by investigating the effects of a low-load, combined BFR training program on multidimensional soccer-related performance and injury outcomes compared with traditional high-intensity training in amateur soccer players.

Methods

Study design

This prospective, randomized, controlled, and blinded study was approved by the Istanbul University-Cerrahpaşa Clinical Research Ethics Committee (approval number: 495623, 06.09.2022) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05452109). Evaluations and training sessions were carried by Istanbul University-Cerrahpaşa and conducted at Biruni University Sports Coordination Office between October 2022 and October 2023. This study was designed, conducted, and reported in accordance with the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) guidelines. All participants were informed about the study procedures and provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Twenty-two male university-level licensed soccer players were randomly assigned to either a low-intensity BFR training group or a traditional moderate-to-high-intensity training group. Training was conducted twice weekly over six weeks during the preseason period, before the start of official competition. This timing was selected to accommodate time constraints and to prevent additional in-season training loads, consistent with previous effective short-term BFR interventions [16, 17].

Subjects

Athletes from a soccer club affiliated with the Biruni University Sports Coordination Office were involved. According to the Participant Classification Framework proposed by McKay et al. (2021), the athletes in this study were classified as Tier 2: Trained/Developmental individuals, those who regularly train approximately three times per week, participate at a local competitive level, and engage in structured sport-specific training aimed at competition [27].

The sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.2 with an effect size (f) of 0.4 (large) for muscle strength (measured by isokinetic dynamometer [17], a power (1–β) of 0.90, and a significance level (α) of 0.05. A total of 22 participants were selected, with an expected 10% dropout rate.

Stratified randomization was performed based on the athletes’ soccer position and activity level. Participants were allocated into two groups (1:1) using the GraphPad website by A.Z.A., who was blinded to the assessments and training. The groups were labeled 1 and 2 (BFR group, n = 11; control group, n = 11). The inclusion criteria included male athletes aged 18–30 years, licensed for at least one year in Istanbul, participation in the club’s preseason training, and no recent injuries [28]. The exclusion criteria included contraindications to BFR [29], a history of sports injuries within the last six months, or lower extremity surgery within the past year. The study was terminated for athletes who missed two consecutive sessions or sustained an injury, preventing further participation.

Outcomes

Assessments were performed before the first and after the final training sessions across five consecutive days to minimize fatigue effects, following a standardized testing order and consistent testing times. Injury outcomes were tracked for six months post-intervention through face-to-face consultations and brief follow-up phone contacts (informal non-structured interviews), during which standardized injury data, including injury types, days lost, match participation, and training exposure, were recorded by B.K.K. All evaluations were conducted in the same order for all participants, following familiarization sessions and standardized warm-up procedures. Assessments were carried out by an experienced sports physiotherapist blinded to group allocation. Isokinetic strength assessments, conducted as a contracted service, were performed by an independent physiotherapist who was also blinded to the intervention. Blood sampling and related analyses were performed by a physician experienced in working with athletes.

Performance outcomes

The primary performance outcomes were muscle strength and muscular endurance, assessed using an isokinetic dynamometer [30]. Secondary outcomes were grouped under performance, physiological, and psychological domains, as described below.

Within the performance domain, secondary parameters included power (vertical jump test [31, 32]), aerobic endurance (Yo-Yo Intermittent Recovery Level 1 test [33, 34]), speed and agility (T test [35, 36], Y-shaped reactive agility test [37]), and dynamic balance (Y balance test [38, 39]).

Anthropometric and physiological outcomes

Anthropometric measure included thigh circumference (tape measurement [40]) and physiological measures included fatigue-related indicators such as blood lactate levels and perceived exertion via the Borg scale [41]. These parameters were used to quantify muscle adaptations and acute exercise responses throughout the intervention.

Psychological outcomes

Psychological assessments comprised sportive self-confidence (Trait Sport Confidence Inventory-Subscale [42]), and participant satisfaction-perceived performance change were also assessed via the Global Rating of Change Scale (GROC) [43]. Injury data were systematically collected via standardized report forms over six months. Detailed information regarding the assessment methods is presented in Table 1.

Blood lactate was measured using a portable analyzer (Roche Accutrend). A blood sample from the 3rd finger of the dominant hand was taken with a disposable needle, immediately placed on a lactate test strip, and then analyzed within 1 min. Intervention

Following the evaluations, the athletes received training instructions and participated in familiarization sessions. Training programs, including resistive exercises (off-field), and conditioning & soccer-specific technical-tactical (on-field) exercises, were implemented during the preseason period over 6 weeks, with 2 sessions per week. The athletes continued a standard training regimen on non-consecutive days. An overview of the exercise protocols and progression for both groups is provided in Table 2, and the detailed training program is available in Supplementary File 1. The relative total training volume, calculated as %1RM × total repetitions × sessions, was comparable between groups (BFR: 214 vs. Control: 262 in relative units), corresponding to 81.67% equivalency. Thus, both protocols involved similar overall workload while differing primarily in load intensity. Programs were progressed biweekly in terms of repetitions, sets, intensity, and types of exercises. For strength exercises, intensity was determined via the 1RM method, which is known for its simplicity and reliability [44]. For aerobic exercise, intensity was calculated via the Karvonen formula for target heart rate reserve (HRR). Exercises were conducted face-to-face in both groups by an expert physiotherapist (B.K.K.) who was blinded to the outcome assessments, with adherence monitored through follow-up forms. All participants who completed the study attended all 12 training sessions, resulting in a 100% compliance rate with no reported adverse events or protocol deviations.

Group 1: Blood flow restriction

BFR was applied via the Occlusion Cuff Elite® manual tourniquet system, which was positioned on the proximal dominant thigh. Tightness was adjusted to allow two fingers beneath the cuff edges. Arterial occlusion pressure (AOP) was measured via portable ultrasound Doppler (8 MHz, Occlusion Cuff®) over the posterior tibial artery with the athlete in the supine position.

According to the literature, BFR exercises are effective at 40–80% of AOP [45]. During familiarization, exercises were performed at 50% AOP, increasing to 80% during training. For resistance exercises, a 30-second rest between sets and 5-minute breaks between exercises allowed reperfusion, with the cuff pressure reduced to 0 mmHg during rest [45].

Group 2: Control

Athletes in the control group participated in the supervised training program consisting of the same exercises as those in Group 1 but without any blood flow restrictions, with higher intensity (moderate to vigorous). The intensity calculations and rest periods were implemented in a manner similar to Group 1.

Statistics

IBM SPSS (24.0) was used for analysis. The Shapiro‒Wilk normality test was performed, and no statistical differences were found. Demographic characteristics, as well as categorical and continuous variables, were compared between groups via chi-square tests and independent t tests. Analyses were prioritized for the primary performance outcomes, whereas secondary outcomes were analyzed in an exploratory manner.

Comparisons of baseline and post training assessments within groups were analyzed via the paired sample t test at a 95% confidence interval (CI). Effect sizes (ES, Cohen’s d) were calculated for outcome measures that showed statistically significant within-group changes. Cohen’s d values were interpreted via conventional thresholds: 0.2 as small, 0.5 as moderate, and 0.8 or above as large effect sizes [46]. To enhance the reliability and interpretability of statistically significant findings, the standard error of the mean (SEM) was calculated for relevant variables via the following formula: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{S}\mathrm{E}\mathrm{M}=\frac{\mathrm{S}\mathrm{D}}{\surd\:\mathrm{n}}\:\:\:$$\end{document}, where SD represents the standard deviation and n denotes the sample size. SEM reflects the precision of the sample mean as an estimate of the population mean. Lower SEM values indicate greater reliability. In general, an SEM corresponding to less than 10% of the mean is considered acceptable, whereas values below 5% reflect strong internal consistency.

A 2 × 2 mixed model repeated measures analysis of variance (rANOVA) was employed to analyze evaluations conducted at different times and the programs’ interaction effects across groups. The results are presented in the tables. An interpreted Pη2 of 0.01–0.06 indicated a “small”, 0.06–0.14 indicated a “medium”, and values greater than 0.14 indicated a “large” ES [46].

Following the baseline assessments, one participant from each group withdrew for personal reasons, resulting in 20 athletes (10 per group) who completed all intervention sessions and post-testing. This 9% attrition rate was consistent with the 10% dropout rate anticipated in the a priori power analysis, thereby maintaining the planned statistical sensitivity. To further verify the adequacy of the final sample, a post-hoc power analysis was conducted for the primary outcome that demonstrated a significant within-group improvement. Specifically, the increase in knee-flexor strength observed in the BFR group (Cohen’s d = 0.72) yielded an achieved power of 1–β = 0.93 (paired-samples t-test, α = 0.05), confirming that the sample size was sufficient to detect a medium-to-large training effect. However, as post-hoc power is highly dependent on the observed effect size, this relatively high-power value should be interpreted with caution.

The comparison results of the isokinetic dynamometer test are presented in Table 4. The percentage changes in isokinetic measurements are shown in Figs. 2 and 3. In the pre- and post-training comparisons of muscle strength within the groups, no significant differences were observed for the extensor muscles in either group (p > 0.05). A significant improvement was found in flexor strength values only in Group 1 (p < 0.05, Pre-SEM: 5.30–7.31, Post-SEM: 6.69–8.64), with a moderate-to-large effect size (d = 0.70–0.72). With respect to the H:Q% ratio, a significant increase was observed in Group 1 (p = 0.040, Pre-SEM: 2.79, Post-SEM: 3.56), with a small-to-moderate effect size (d = 0.40), whereas no significant difference was found in Group 2 (p > 0.05). Both the extensor and flexor endurance values (PT, PT/kg) significantly improved in Group 1 (p < 0.05, Extensors; Pre-SEM: 6.71–4.39, Post-SEM: 7.67–4.74, Flexors; Pre-SEM: 4.52–5.05, post-SEM: 5.16–7.08, respectively). The effect sizes were moderate for flexor endurance values (d = 0.50–0.60) and small and moderate for extensor endurance values (d = 0.30–0.64). In contrast, no significant differences were detected in Group 2 (p > 0.05). Additionally, there were no significant differences in the total work done for muscle groups within either group (p > 0.05).

The effect of time on strength was not significant for the extensor muscle group (p > 0.05), whereas it was significant for the flexor muscle group (PT, PT/kg%) and the H:Q% ratio (p < 0.05). Additionally, the time effect on endurance was significant for the PT and PT/kg values of the flexor muscles, as well as for the PT values of the extensor muscles (p < 0.05). However, when the isokinetic parameters for the group*time interactions were analyzed, no significant differences were observed (p > 0.05).

Discussion

After six weeks of training, both groups demonstrated significant gains in lower-limb power, agility, thigh circumference, and self-confidence, whereas fatigue levels remained unchanged. Only the BFR group showed additional improvements in flexor strength, muscular endurance, H:Q ratio, aerobic capacity, speed, and dynamic balance. Although between-group differences were not significant, except for thigh circumference, the BFR group exhibited a consistent pattern of positive change across multiple outcomes, with effect sizes ranging from small to large. These findings suggest that BFR may serve as a practical alternative for targeting key soccer performance parameters when high-load training is not feasible. Acceptable SEM values (0.03–8.64) across measures support the reliability of these results. Moreover, higher satisfaction scores in the BFR group indicate a favorable perception and potential acceptability. Although fewer injuries and training days lost were observed in this group, the difference was not statistically significant, underscoring the need for larger-scale studies.

Neuromuscular adaptations: Muscle strength and endurance

High-intensity training is traditionally recommended for strength development in athletes [12]; however, several studies have shown that low- to moderate-intensity BFR can produce comparable or even superior gains [47]. In this study, both groups improved in strength, but a significant increase in flexor strength and H:Q ratio occurred only in the BFR group. The H:Q ratio, often overlooked in amateur athletes, is typically expected to exceed 60% to reduce injury risk [48, 49]. In our study, this threshold was achieved only in the BFR group, likely due to selective improvements in flexor strength.

Korkmaz et al. found that professional athletes performing quadriceps-dominant BFR programs at higher frequencies achieved greater overall strength gains. However, architectural superiority was observed only in the rectus femoris, suggesting that hypertrophic responses are largely muscle-specific [17]. In contrast, our amateur players showed selective hamstring improvements, likely reflecting localized neuromuscular adaptations. Considering that baseline H:Q ratios indicated relatively weaker hamstrings, these gains may have become more evident within the short training period, while quadriceps adaptations remained limited.

These findings suggest that BFR can effectively strengthen flexors and improve overall lower-limb balance even with limited training frequency. Such adaptations may contribute to injury prevention, though this should be confirmed in larger cohorts with longer follow-up periods.

Endurance capacity, reflecting a player’s ability to sustain repeated efforts during match play, also improved significantly in the BFR group, which was likely influenced by the protocol’s use of low intensity combined with high repetitions. This may be attributed to the combined effect of hypoxia-induced metabolic stress and high-repetition, low-load contractions, which enhance oxidative enzyme activity and muscular fatigue resistance [50, 51]. In contrast, the lack of a significant increase in the control group may be attributed to the higher intensity and lower repetition counts. In this context, the BFR method may support endurance development in parallel with strength gains, as observed in our findings.

Performance changes

Power of lower extremity

Athletes in this study performed one day of resistance training and one day of conditioning combined with soccer-specific technical–tactical sessions, alongside their regular weekly routine. Resistance training targeted major lower-limb muscles, while on-field sessions integrated plyometric and aerobic drills with tactical exercises and small-sided games.

The multicomponent design likely contributed to improvements in jump height and power. However, the limited frequency of resistance sessions may have restricted strength-specific adaptations, resulting in no significant differences between groups. This lack of statistical superiority may also reflect overlapping plyometric and conditioning content across both groups, which likely induced similar neuromuscular stimuli. A recent study likewise found that plyometric training with BFR produced comparable vertical-jump gains to traditional programs [52]. Both approaches appear effective for improving lower-limb power, but the contribution of BFR versus traditional training remains unclear and warrants further study.

Aerobic endurance

In our study, moderate improvements in aerobic endurance and shuttle-run performance were observed only in the BFR group; however, these gains were not superior to those in the control group. Similar findings have been reported previously, where BFR-integrated programs improved aerobic performance without significant between-group differences [16, 53].

Through underlying mechanisms, BFR may enhance aerobic capacity through hypoxia-induced biochemical and neuromuscular adaptations, such as increased capillary density, mitochondrial biogenesis, and oxidative enzyme activity [54]. McKee et al. also showed that sprint training with BFR increased internal load and physiological stress but did not yield superior aerobic outcomes compared to traditional training [55]. Overall, while BFR did not provide greater endurance benefits in our study, it may still promote favorable physiological adaptations. Future research should incorporate objective markers, such as muscle oxygenation or mitochondrial activity, to better elucidate these mechanisms.

Speed and agility

Speed and agility are multifactorial abilities influenced by strength, balance, neuromuscular coordination, and cognitive control [35, 56]. Recent studies have shown that BFR training can positively affect these parameters [16, 47]. Hosseini Kakhak et al. reported significant improvements in change-of-direction speed among young athletes who performed soccer-specific BFR training [16].

In our study, speed improved only in the BFR group, whereas agility increased in both groups. The improvements in the BFR group may be associated with enhanced muscle strength, balance, and neuromuscular efficiency under hypoxic stress. Previous research has demonstrated that BFR induces greater motor unit recruitment and increased electromyographic activity compared to traditional low-load training, suggesting enhanced neuromuscular activation efficiency [20, 21]. Moreover, BFR training elevates intramuscular metabolic stress and stimulates biochemical pathways such as mTOR and IGF-1 signaling, promoting muscle hypertrophy and fast-twitch fiber adaptation [20]. These adaptations may contribute to improved sprint capacity and movement efficiency by enhancing neuromuscular drive and energy utilization during high-intensity efforts.

Meanwhile, agility gains in the control group may reflect improved proprioceptive input and coordination [57]. Taken together, these findings suggest that BFR may induce favorable neuromuscular and biochemical responses that indirectly enhance movement efficiency. Future studies incorporating electromyography, muscle oxygenation, or serum biomarkers could clarify the mechanistic links between BFR-induced adaptations and speed–agility performance.

Dynamic balance

Dynamic balance is essential for technical–tactical performance and injury prevention in soccer [58]. In our study, the BFR group showed moderate-to-large improvements in dynamic balance, likely linked to enhancements in hamstring strength. In our study, the BFR group showed moderate-to-large improvements in dynamic balance, likely associated with increased hamstring strength. The hamstrings, connecting the pelvis, trunk, and lower limbs, play a key role in maintaining posture and stability during dynamic movements [59, 60].

Enhanced strength of these muscles may have improved stability during multidirectional reaching tasks. The within-group improvement suggests a potential benefit of BFR for dynamic balance, even though between-group differences were not significant. Overall, BFR seems to support neuromuscular coordination and postural control, but both programs likely produced comparable functional outcomes under similar training conditions.

Thigh circumference

The change in thigh circumference was notably greater in the BFR group. This method is frequently highlighted for its capacity to promote muscle hypertrophy through various physiological mechanisms [61]. In line with this, the observed gains in flexor muscle strength may be partly attributed to the hypertrophic adaptations reflected in the thigh.

Fatigue

Well-designed training programs aim to enhance endurance and overall performance by improving fatigue tolerance [62]. In our study, no significant differences were observed in fatigue levels after six weeks. Since training intensity progressively increased in both groups and the total workload was comparable, both protocols likely contributed to improved fatigue resistance.

Smith et al. reported that BFR during a taper phase allowed athletes to reduce lifting loads while maintaining or even improving key performance metrics such as sprint speed, squat strength, and jump capacity [63]. These results highlight the potential of BFR as a load-efficient method to sustain performance under reduced volume conditions.

Sportive self-confidence

In terms of self-confidence, both groups presented high levels at baseline, which increased after the training programs. Similarly, Selmi et al. reported that combining aerobic and speed training with soccer-specific drills improved physical performance parameters and self-confidence in athletes [64].

The observed rise in sports self-confidence may be reflecting several factors, including preseason evaluations, self-monitoring opportunities, physiotherapist supervision, group-based training, and early success in friendly matches. These factors likely support favorable psychological adaptations. The consistent improvement, particularly in the BFR group, suggests that structured and supportive training environments can further enhance athletes’ confidence. Future qualitative studies should explore the mechanisms underlying these effects.

Satisfaction and change

The BFR group reported significantly higher GROC scores than the control group did, indicating a more favorable perception of training. This difference may be related to the individualized nature of BFR sessions, where the use of external cuffs and device-guided protocols can increase engagement and perceived well-being.

Injury prevention

Enhancing physical performance parameters is crucial for preventing or reducing sports injuries [11]. In the present study, no significant between-group differences were observed in training volume, the number of matches played, the number and types of injuries, or total days lost during training and competition periods. The injuries recorded, including pubalgia, hamstring strains, ACL injuries, knee pain, and ankle sprains, were consistent with previous findings in soccer athletes [10].

Although not statistically significant, the BFR group exhibited a lower frequency of knee injuries, which may be related to the improved H:Q ratio and hamstring strength observed after training. However, this trend should be interpreted cautiously, and future studies with larger samples are required to clarify whether such neuromuscular adaptations lead to meaningful reductions in injury incidence.

In addition, previous injuries, excessive training load, technical execution, and equipment characteristics are known to influence injury occurrence [6]. These factors should be carefully controlled in future research to better isolate the specific effects of BFR.

In summary, low-load BFR training resulted in comparable improvements in performance and injury-related outcomes to traditional high-intensity training, while substantially reducing mechanical load. Overall, these findings highlight BFR as a feasible and load-efficient strategy for preseason conditioning or for athletes requiring reduced mechanical stress.

Limitations and Strengths

This study involved amateur, licensed university soccer players, which limits the generalizability of the findings to professional athletes. The team’s infrequent tactical and technical training alongside the program may have been insufficient to achieve the targeted muscular performance outcomes. Additionally, the lack of prior injury data and advanced physiological measures has limited a deeper understanding of the effects of BFR. These limitations underscore the need for long-term studies that incorporate multi-seasonal data to better evaluate the role of BFR in injury prevention.

Despite these constraints, the study employed robust methods, including stratified blind randomization, ensuring group homogeneity and minimizing bias. Notably, this study is the first to integrate a six-month follow-up to assess the impact of BFR on sports injuries, providing valuable insights into its effectiveness.

Practical implications

These findings may help coaches, physiotherapists, and strength and conditioning professionals to implement BFR training safely and effectively in amateur soccer environments:

* Six weeks of low-load BFR training was shown to improve strength, muscular endurance, aerobic capacity, speed, and balance in amateur soccer players.* BFR can effectively target hamstring strength and the H:Q ratio, which are critical for lower-limb balance and may contribute to reducing injury risk when integrated into strength programs.* Short-term BFR protocols (approximately six weeks) can be integrated into existing conditioning routines as a low-load method to enhance performance without increasing fatigue.* Although the reduction in injury incidence was not statistically significant, BFR showed a trend toward fewer knee-related injuries and higher participant satisfaction, warranting further investigation in larger cohorts.

Conclusion

Low-load BFR training appears to be a feasible and load-efficient conditioning strategy for amateur soccer players, producing consistent functional gains and high satisfaction with minimal mechanical stress. The observed improvements in hamstring strength and H:Q balance suggest a potential contribution to injury prevention, warranting confirmation in larger, long-term trials. Overall, BFR training can be strategically incorporated into preseason or recovery phases as a safe and complementary approach to conventional programs, supporting both performance development and training continuity.
