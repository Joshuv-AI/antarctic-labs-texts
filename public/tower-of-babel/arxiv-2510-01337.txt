On the Identifiability of Latent Action Policies
Sébastien Lachapelle

Abstract
We study the identifiability of latent action policy learning (LAPO), a framework introduced recently to discover representations of actions from video data. We formally describe desiderata for such representations, their statistical benefits and potential sources of unidentifiability. Finally, we prove that an entropy-regularized LAPO objective identifies action representations satisfying our desiderata, under suitable conditions. Our analysis provides an explanation for why discrete action representations perform well in practice.

Introduction & background

In robot control,
behavior cloning
is an approach to learn a policy from action-labeled expert trajectories
[
12
,
5
]
. It simply consists in training a policy
\pi(a\mid{\bm{x}})
via supervised learning on data of the form
\{({\bm{x}}_{i},a_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
where
a_{i}
is the action taken by the expert policy in state
{\bm{x}}_{i}
. The success of the approach relies on at least two aspects: (i) the demonstrations
({\bm{x}}_{i},a_{i})
have to be generated from a sufficiently good expert policy, and (ii) the number of demonstrations
N
has to be sufficiently large to make learning possible. Although conceptually simple, the framework requires a large amount of action-labeled expert demonstrations to be successful, which can be costly to acquire.

To address this issue,
[
13
]
introduced
latent action policy learning
(LAPO) which can leverage large corpuses of unannotated video data in order to reduce reliance on action-labeled expert trajectories. LAPO proceeds in three stages.
First
, given a large dataset of state/next-state pairs
\{({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, LAPO minimizes the reconstruction loss

\displaystyle\textstyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{a}\sim\hat{q}(\hat{a}\mid{\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime})}\lVert{\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{i}-\hat{\bm{g}}({\bm{x}}_{i},\hat{a})\rVert^{2}_{2}\,,

where the
inverse dynamics model
(IDM)
\hat{q}(\hat{a}\mid{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})
encodes pairs
({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})
into action representations
\hat{a}
which are then decoded using a
forward dynamics model
(FDM)
\hat{\bm{x}}^{\prime}=\hat{\bm{g}}({\bm{x}},\hat{a})
.
Secondly
, the IDM is used to label the unlabeled video dataset, which yields
\{({\bm{x}}_{i},\hat{a}_{i},{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime})\}_{i=1}^{N}
where
{\hat{a}_{i}:=\arg\max_{\hat{a}}\hat{q}(\hat{a}\mid{\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{i})}
. This dataset is then used to train the
latent action policy

\hat{\pi}(\hat{a}\mid{\bm{x}})
.
Thirdly
, a learnable head is applied on top of
\hat{\pi}(\hat{a}\mid{\bm{x}})
and trained to map the latent actions
\hat{a}
to actual actions
a
using a much smaller domain-specific action-labeled dataset
\{({\bm{x}}_{i},a_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N_{a}}
(
N_{a}<<N
). While doing that, one can choose to either freeze the latent action policy
\hat{\pi}
or fine-tune it. The authors showed that, thanks to this approach, fewer action-labeled samples are needed to train a good policy.

Identifiability analysis of LAPO

2.1
Data-generating process

Let
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}:=[0,1]^{d}
be the current observation,
{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}:=[0,1]^{d^{\prime}}
be the future observation and
a\in{\mathcal{A}}:=\{1,\dots,k\}
be a discrete action (in line with practical implementations
[
13
,
2
,
15
]
). The most natural situation is when
{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{x}}^{\prime}
corresponds to two consecutive frames, i.e.
{\bm{x}}:={\bm{x}}^{t}
and
{\bm{x}}^{\prime}={\bm{x}}^{t+1}
and
a=a^{t}
. But one could also consider different situations where the model is conditioned on a window of past observations:
{\bm{x}}={\bm{x}}^{t-k:t}
with
a=a^{t-k:t}
. Similarly, the
{\bm{x}}^{\prime}
could correspond to a window of multiple frames in the future.

We assume the current state
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}
is sampled from some (Lebesgue) density function
p({\bm{x}})
. Furthermore, an action
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
is chosen according to a ground-truth policy
\pi
conditioned on
{\bm{x}}
:

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}\sim p({\bm{x}})\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad a\sim\pi(a\mid{\bm{x}})\,.

Define
p({\bm{x}},a):=p({\bm{x}})\pi(a\mid{\bm{x}})
,
p(a):=\int p({\bm{x}},a)d{\bm{x}}
, and
p({\bm{x}}\mid a):=p({\bm{x}},a)/p(a)
.

We assume the future observation
{\bm{x}}^{\prime}
is given by a deterministic transition model

{\bm{x}}^{\prime}={\bm{g}}({\bm{x}},a)\text{, where}\ {\bm{g}}:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}\,.

This process induces a joint probability distribution over
({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})
, which we denote by
p({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})
.
2
2

2

p({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})
is an abuse of notation since the distribution of
({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})
has no a density (w.r.t. Lebesgue).

Support notation.
In what follows, the support of
p({\bm{x}}\mid a)
is defined as

\textstyle\textnormal{supp}[p({\bm{x}}\mid a)]:=\{{\bm{x}}_{0}\in{\mathcal{X}}\mid\forall\ \text{open neighborhood $U$ of}\ {\bm{x}}_{0},\int_{U}p({\bm{x}}\mid a)d{\bm{x}}>0\}\,,

where
\int d{\bm{x}}
denotes the Lebesgue integral. Note that
\textnormal{supp}[p({\bm{x}}\mid a)]
might depend on
a
. Define also
\textnormal{supp}[p(a)]:=\{a\in{\mathcal{A}}\mid p(a)>0\}
and
\textnormal{supp}[p({\bm{x}},a)]:=\bigcup_{a\in\textnormal{supp}[p(a)]}\textnormal{supp}[p({\bm{x}}\mid a)]\times\{a\}
.

2.2
Desiderata

In this section, we formalize three desiderata for an action representation and discuss statistical efficiency. Intuitively, we want to learn an encoder
\hat{q}(\hat{a}\mid{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})
that captures useful information about the ground-truth action
a
. To formalize this, we will study

\displaystyle{\bm{v}}(\hat{a}\mid{\bm{x}},a):=\hat{q}(\hat{a}\mid{\bm{x}},{\bm{g}}({\bm{x}},a))\,,

defined for all
({\bm{x}},a)\in\textnormal{supp}[p({\bm{x}},a)]
. The conditional probability mass function
{\bm{v}}(\hat{a}\mid{\bm{x}},a)
maps pairs
({\bm{x}},a)
to their corresponding learned action representations
\hat{a}
, potentially in a stochastic way. This is effectively an
entanglement map
, as studied in identifiable representation learning
[
10
]
. Our first desideratum is to have a deterministic map from
a
to
\hat{a}
.

Desideratum 1

(Determinism)
.

There exists a function
{\bm{v}}({\bm{x}},a)
such that
{\bm{v}}(\hat{a}\mid{\bm{x}},a)={\mathbf{1}(\hat{a}={\bm{v}}({\bm{x}},a))}
for all
({\bm{x}},a)\in\textnormal{supp}[p({\bm{x}},a)]
, where
\mathbf{1}(\cdot)
is the indicator function.

Notice how the learned action representation
\hat{a}
might depend on the current state
{\bm{x}}
via
\hat{a}={\bm{v}}({\bm{x}},a)
. Such a dependence is undesirable since it signifies that the meaning of
\hat{a}
, i.e. how it relates to
a
, depends on the current state
{\bm{x}}
. We illustrate this unfortunate state of affairs with a simple example.

Example 1
.

Consider a manipulation task where
{\mathcal{A}}:=\{\texttt{left},\texttt{right}\}
and suppose

\displaystyle 1={\bm{v}}({\bm{x}}_{0},a=\texttt{left}),\quad 2={\bm{v}}({\bm{x}}_{0},a=\texttt{right}),

\displaystyle 2={\bm{v}}({\bm{x}}_{1},a=\texttt{left}),\quad 1={\bm{v}}({\bm{x}}_{1},a=\texttt{right})\,.

We can see that the meaning of
\hat{a}
depends on the context
{\bm{x}}
: When in state
{\bm{x}}_{0}
,
\hat{a}=1
corresponds to
a=\texttt{left}
, whereas in state
{\bm{x}}_{1}
,
\hat{a}=1
corresponds to
a=\texttt{right}
.

This undesirable phenomenon, described informally by
[
13
, Section 6.2]
, can be thought of as a form of entanglement since
\hat{a}
entangles both
a
and
{\bm{x}}
. This motivates:

Desideratum 2

(Disentanglement)
.

There exists a function
{\bm{v}}(a)
such that, for all
({\bm{x}},a)\in\textnormal{supp}[p({\bm{x}},a)]
,
{\bm{v}}({\bm{x}},a)={\bm{v}}(a)
.

Furthermore, we want the latent action
\hat{a}
to reveal all there is to know about the ground-truth action
a
. More formally, we want that two distinct actions
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
never map to the same latent action
\hat{a}
:

Desideratum 3

(Informativeness)
.

The function
{\bm{v}}:\textnormal{supp}[p(a)]\rightarrow\hat{\mathcal{A}}
is injective.

Statistical efficiency.
As explained in
Section

1
, an encoder/IDM
\hat{q}(\hat{a}\mid{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})
can be used to label the action-free video dataset, yielding
\{({\bm{x}}_{i},\hat{a}_{i},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{i})\}^{N}_{i=1}
. If the IDM satisfies our desiderata, the newly labeled dataset is actually
\{({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{v}}(a_{i}),{\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{i})\}^{N}_{i=1}
where
a_{i}
is the action taken by the expert policy
\pi(a\mid{\bm{x}}_{i})
. Thus the latent action policy
\hat{\pi}(\hat{a}\mid{\bm{x}})
trained on this data will approximate the distribution of
{\bm{v}}(a)
when
a\sim\pi(a\mid{\bm{x}})
. This means that there exists a transformation
\sigma:\hat{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}}
(any extension of
{\bm{v}}^{-1}:{\bm{v}}(\textnormal{supp}[p(a)])\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}}
) such that
\sigma(\hat{a})\sim\pi(a\mid{\bm{x}})
when
\hat{a}\sim\pi(\hat{a}\mid{\bm{x}})
. Hence, to get the expert policy
\pi
, we only need to learn
\sigma:\hat{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}}
“on top of” the latent action policy
\hat{\pi}
using the smaller action-labeled dataset. Had
\hat{a}
been dependent on
{\bm{x}}
, such a transformation
\sigma
would not exist, forcing us to resort to either fine-tuning
\hat{\pi}
or learning a map
{\mathcal{X}}\times\hat{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}}
on top of
\hat{\pi}
, both of which are expected to be less statistically efficient than learning the simpler function
\sigma:\hat{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}}
.

2.3
A formal entropy-regularized LAPO objective

We now present a formal entropy-regularized LAPO objective.
Theorem

1
will show that, under suitable assumptions, its solutions must satisfy the desiderata of
Section

2.2
.

In order to learn a deterministic encoder, we add an entropy regularizer
H(\hat{q}(\cdot\mid{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})):={-{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{q}(\hat{a}\mid{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})}\log\hat{q}(\hat{a}\mid{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})}
. In the limit of infinite data, our entropy-regularized LAPO objective is

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{\bm{g}}\in{\mathcal{G}},\hat{q}\in{\mathcal{Q}}}{\mathbb{E}}_{p({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})}\left[{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{q}(\hat{a}\mid{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})}\lVert{\bm{x}}^{\prime}-\hat{\bm{g}}({\bm{x}},\hat{a})\rVert^{2}_{2}+\beta H(\hat{q}(\cdot\mid{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}))\right]\,,

(1)

where
\beta>0
controls regularization, and
{\mathcal{G}}
and
{\mathcal{Q}}
are respectively the hypothesis spaces for
\hat{\bm{g}}
and
\hat{q}
.

Definition 1

(FDM hypothesis space
{\mathcal{G}}
)
.

A function
\hat{\bm{g}}:{\mathcal{X}}\times\hat{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
is in
{\mathcal{G}}
if and only if, for all
\hat{a}\in\hat{\mathcal{A}}
,
\hat{\bm{g}}({\bm{x}},\hat{a})
is continuous in
{\bm{x}}
.

Definition 2

(IDM hypothesis space
{\mathcal{Q}}
)
.

Let
\hat{\mathcal{A}}:=\{1,\dots,\hat{k}\}
be the space of action representations
\hat{a}
. A function
\hat{q}:\hat{\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}\rightarrow[0,1]
is in
{\mathcal{Q}}
if and only if (i) for all
({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\in{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
,
{\sum_{\hat{a}\in\hat{\mathcal{A}}}\ \hat{q}(\hat{a}\mid{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})=1}
, and (ii) for all
\hat{a}\in\hat{\mathcal{A}}
,
\hat{q}(\hat{a}\mid{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})
is continuous in
({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})
.

Note that our identifiability guarantee,
Theorem

1
, does not assume
\hat{k}=k
, only
\hat{k}\geq k
.

One can easily see that both terms in Problem
Equation

1
are lower bounded by zero. Additionally,
Proposition

4
(in appendix) shows that there exists
({\bm{g}}^{*},q^{*})\in{\mathcal{G}}\times{\mathcal{Q}}
such that both terms are equal to zero (under
1
and
2
). This means that, at optimality, the entropy regularizer must be equal to zero thus forcing the learned IDM
\hat{q}(\hat{a}\mid{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})
to be deterministic for all
({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\in\textnormal{supp}[p({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})]
. In other words, at optimality,
\hat{q}(\hat{a}\mid{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})=\mathbf{1}(\hat{a}=\hat{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}))
for some function
\hat{\bm{f}}:\textnormal{supp}[p({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})]\rightarrow\hat{\mathcal{A}}
.

Remark 1
.

The above development begs the question: Why are we considering a stochastic IDM
\hat{q}
to later regularize it to be deterministic? A perhaps more natural route would be to directly train a deterministic encoder
\hat{\bm{f}}:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}\rightarrow\hat{\mathcal{A}}
. From an optimization perspective, a stochastic encoder is helpful as it unlocks gradient computation via the reparameterization trick
[
7
]
. From a theoretical perspective, the continuity condition on
\hat{q}
is crucial for our proof, as it excludes pathological encoders
\hat{\bm{f}}
that would present “jumps” on the connected components of
\textnormal{supp}[p({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})]
. We conjecture that the VQ-VAE approach of
[
13
]
, which is limited to deterministic discrete encoders, can in principle lead to such pathological behaviors. We leave this for future work.

2.4
Potential sources of unidentifiability

We now show that, without assumptions on the data-generating process or without restrictions on the hypothesis classes
{\mathcal{Q}}
and
{\mathcal{G}}
, Problem
Equation

1
admits degenerate solutions which do not satisfy our desiderata of
Section

2.2
.

Example 2

(No restriction on
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
)
.

In principle, one can choose
\hat{\mathcal{A}}:={\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
and
\hat{q}(\hat{a}\mid{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}):=\delta(\hat{a}-{\bm{x}}^{\prime})
where
\delta
is the Dirac function. Hence the IDM outputs
{\bm{x}}^{\prime}
deterministically. By choosing
\hat{\bm{g}}({\bm{x}},\hat{a})=\hat{a}
, we clearly solve Problem
eq.

1
, but the action representation
\hat{a}
is uninteresting. In fact, this can be understood as a violation of

\hat{a}={\bm{x}}^{\prime}
clearly depends on
{\bm{x}}
via
{\bm{x}}^{\prime}={\bm{g}}({\bm{x}},a)
.

Example 3

(Deterministic
\pi(a\mid{\bm{x}})
)
.

Assume
\pi(a\mid{\bm{x}})=\mathbf{1}(a=\pi({\bm{x}}))
, i.e. the ground-truth policy is deterministic. In that case, one can solve the reconstruction problem by choosing
\hat{\bm{g}}({\bm{x}},\hat{a}):={\bm{g}}({\bm{x}},\pi({\bm{x}}))
since
{\bm{x}}^{\prime}={\bm{g}}({\bm{x}},\pi({\bm{x}}))
with probability one. In that case, the latent action
\hat{a}
is completely ignored by the FDM and thus the IDM
\hat{q}(\hat{a}\mid{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})
could simply output the same action deterministically, which would clearly present a violation of
3
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
X
be a metric space

The result generalizes to the case where

and let

Proof.

We make use of the Vedenissoff theorem

Since a metric space is always perfectly normal

For each

, the set

such that

and
h_{k}^{-1}(\{0\})=\bigcup_{k^{\prime}\in[K]\setminus\{k\}}X_{k^{\prime}}

We now prove that

, then there exists a

which means
h_{k_{0}}(x)=1
. Of course, this implies that the sum is greater than zero. If
x\not\in\bigcup_{k\in[K]}X_{k}
, in particular we have
x\not\in\bigcup_{k\in[K]\setminus\{1\}}X_{k}=h_{1}^{-1}(\{0\})
, which means
h_{1}(x)>0

Since
\sum_{k\in[K]}h_{k}(x)>0
