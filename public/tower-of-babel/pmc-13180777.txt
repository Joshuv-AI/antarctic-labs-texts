Dendritic cells as orchestrators in the allergen-specific immunotherapy of allergic diseases
Authors: Xiyang Dou, Tian Gu, Yu Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDendritic cells (DCs) play a pivotal role in regulating immune responses, encompassing both tolerance and activation. Dysregulation of DC function is fundamental in the development of allergic conditions, underscoring their significance as a major focus for allergen-specific immunotherapy (AIT). This review systematically delineates the intricate landscape of DC subsets, comprising conventional DCs, plasmacytoid DCs, monocyte-derived DCs, and Langerhans cells, elucidating their specific contributions to either fostering Th2-driven inflammation or driving regulatory T cell generation. We dissect the multi-layered mechanisms through which DCs orchestrate immune tolerance, spanning cytokine signaling, metabolic reprogramming, epigenetic remodeling, and migratory dynamics. Additionally, the article extensively evaluates and contrasts the distinct roles of DC subsets and their modes of antigen presentation across various AIT administration routes (e.g. subcutaneous, sublingual, oral, epidermal, intranasal, intralymphatic and intratonsillar). Lastly, we highlight next-generation DC-targeted strategies, such as nanoparticle-mediated delivery systems and genetic engineering methodologies, designed to reprogram DCs toward a stable tolerogenic phenotype and enhance therapeutic efficacy. Translating these preclinical advances into personalized clinical interventions represents a pivotal frontier for establishing durable immune tolerance in allergic disorders.Keywords: Allergen-specific immunotherapy, Administration routes, Dendritic cells, Immune tolerance, Tolerogenic

Introduction

Allergic diseases, including allergic rhinitis, asthma, atopic dermatitis, and food allergy, are prevalent worldwide, impacting diverse populations across various countries, ethnicities, and ages. Recent studies indicate that allergic rhinitis affects approximately 18% of children [1], asthma prevalence can reach up to 20% globally [2], atopic dermatitis affects about 9.2% of the population [3], and food allergy ranges between 1% and 10% [4]. These conditions significantly impair patients’ quality of life by disrupting sleep, study, and work, while also imposing considerable indirect costs, thus representing a major global health issue [5–7]. Allergen-specific immunotherapy (AIT) offers a unique etiological advantage and can provide sustained therapeutic benefits, positioning it as a cornerstone treatment option for eligible patients with IgE-mediated allergic diseases [8, 9]. Recent findings highlight that the core mechanism of AIT lies in inducing long-term immune tolerance [10]. By modulating the quantity and function of various immune cell subsets during the immune response, AIT suppresses the hyperactive type 2 immunity and promotes a more balanced state, characterized by the induction of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and, in some contexts, an increased Th1 response. Dendritic cells (DCs) are master regulators at the interface of innate and adaptive immunity, playing a pivotal role in the onset and progression of allergic diseases. Their dysregulation can precipitate excessive Th2 responses, thereby driving allergic inflammation and potentially leading to Th2-type allergic conditions. Emerging evidence positions DCs at the heart of Th2-type allergic reactions [11–13]. Consequently, the ability of AIT in rectifying DC quantity and function is crucial for its clinical efficacy in treating allergic diseases [14].

This review synthesizes current knowledge on the pivotal role of DCs in AIT, highlighting their function as a bridge between innate and adaptive immunity. We explore the diversity and specialization of DC subsets, detailing the molecular and cellular mechanisms through which they mediate immune tolerance. Furthermore, we compare how distinct AIT administration routes leverage unique tissue microenvironments to shape the antigen-presenting capabilities of DCs. Finally, we focus on novel therapeutic strategies targeting DCs, offering fresh insights and directions for future precision interventions. A systematic literature search was conducted in the core biomedical database PubMed from its inception to March 2026. The retrieval was guided by a structured combination of MeSH terms and free-text keywords covering DC biology, allergic diseases, immune tolerance, AIT mechanisms, administration routes, targeted therapeutic strategies, as well as DC-associated biomarkers and phenotypic signatures. The detailed search terms were as follows: ‘dendritic cell’ AND (‘differentiation’ OR ‘subsets’), ‘dendritic cell function’ AND (‘allergy’ OR ‘asthma’ OR ‘allergic rhinitis’ OR ‘atopic dermatitis’ OR ‘food allergy’), ‘dendritic cell’ AND (‘immune tolerance’ OR ‘Treg’ OR ‘tolDC’), ‘dendritic cell’ AND (‘cytokine signaling’ OR ‘metabolic reprogramming’ OR ‘epigenetic regulation’), ‘dendritic cell migration’ AND (‘CCR7’ OR ‘lymph node’), ‘allergen immunotherapy’ AND (‘subcutaneous’ OR ‘sublingual’ OR ‘oral’ OR ‘epicutaneous’ OR ‘intranasal’ OR ‘intralymphatic’), ‘nanoparticle targeting’ AND (‘dendritic cell’ AND ‘vaccine’), ‘dendritic cell’ AND (‘biomarker’ OR ‘predictor’ OR‘CD80’ OR ‘CD86’ OR ‘PD-L1’ OR ‘IDO’ OR ‘RALDH’ OR ‘IL-10’ OR ‘TGF-β’ OR ‘Stabilin-1’) AND ‘allergen immunotherapy’. Studies were included if they offered mechanistic insights into DC biology in allergic inflammation, clarified DC-mediated immune tolerance mechanisms, characterized subset-specific DC functions across different AIT routes, presented preclinical or clinical findings on DC-targeted therapeutic strategies, or identified DC-associated biomarkers, phenotypic signatures, or predictors of AIT efficacy. Studies were excluded if they were not published in English, lacked in-depth mechanistic data, corresponded to case reports, conference abstracts, editorials, or letters, or were not directly relevant to DC biology or AIT. In total, 202 articles were ultimately included in this review, focusing on the most relevant and high-quality evidence.

Subsets of DCs

The initiation of an immune response relies on the intricate crosstalk between the innate and adaptive immune systems, with DCs serving as pivotal primary antigen-presenting cells (APCs) that act as a central nexus bridging these two compartments. The functional states of DCs significantly impact the advancement of allergic diseases. Mature DCs amplify cell-mediated immunity by expressing co-stimulatory molecules and secreting inflammatory factors. Conversely, tolerogenic DCs (tolDCs) drive immune tolerance by inducing T cell anergy, promoting clonal deletion, and differentiating Tregs, mediated by weak co-stimulatory signals and the secretion of immunoregulatory cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β. This functional plasticity, modulated by external stimuli, underscores DCs as significant targets for AIT [15, 16].

DCs are widely distributed in lymphoid organs and epithelial barrier tissues. The primary subsets comprise conventional DCs (cDC1s and cDC2s), plasmacytoid DCs (pDCs), monocyte-derived DCs (MoDCs), Langerhans cells (LCs) and type 3 DCs (DC3s) identified recently [18–20].

Summary of the phenotype of human DCs

While DC subsets are categorized into increasingly well-defined developmental lineages, tolDCs and regulatory DCs (DCregs) are best conceptualized not as distinct lineages, but as flexible, reversible functional states that are profoundly shaped by tissue-specific microenvironmental signals. Rather than being defined by stable lineage markers, this tolerogenic phenotype can be induced in multiple DCs by a spectrum of immunosuppressive cues. These include retinoic acid (RA) and TGF-β signaling in peripheral CD103+ conventional DCs [21], as well as in vitro treatment with IL-10 [22], vitamin D3 (VD3) [23], or NF-κB inhibitors [24]. This induction yields a molecular profile characterized by decreased expression of costimulatory molecules (CD80/CD86) and increased levels of immunoregulatory molecules like IDO and ILT3 [22, 23, 25–28]. In this state, DCs primarily facilitate antigen-specific immune tolerance by promoting Treg differentiation and suppressing effector T cells and natural killer cells (NK cells) [22, 23, 28–31]. Furthermore, they can mediate infectious tolerance by generating Tregs, thereby amplifying the local immunosuppressive microenvironment[32]. The plasticity of this state is underscored by its reversibility, and the ability of DCs from diverse sources to acquire this regulatory capacity under appropriate conditions suggests the model that tolDCs/DCregs represent a crucial functional adaptation of DCs within specific physiological or pathological contexts, rather than a fixed developmental subset [28, 29, 32–34]. The induction of this stable, yet reversible, tolerogenic DC state represents a key cellular target of AIT, with the goal of redirecting DC function from driving allergic inflammation to establishing durable immune tolerance.

Functions of DCs in allergic disease

DCs act as dual orchestrators in allergic diseases, initiating pathogenic Th2 inflammation while also maintaining peripheral tolerance. This functional duality is underpinned by subset-specific specialization in antigen processing, cytokine secretion, and cellular crosstalk.

In recent years, advancements in transcriptomics, particularly single-cell RNA sequencing (RNAseq) [19, 35], have provided a more comprehensive understanding of the functional distinctions among DC populations (Fig. 1). In allergic diseases, cDC2s represent pivotal effector subsets that efficiently activate CD4+T cells [36, 37], driving Th2/Th17 differentiation and serving as principal mediators of mucosal immunity and allergic inflammation [19, 37, 38]. Upon stimulated by allergens such as house dust mites, cDC2s trigger Th2 responses via epithelial-derived thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP) [39], [40], markedly augmenting airway eosinophilic inflammation [41]. Conversely, Batf3-dependent CD103+cDC1 counterbalance Th2/Th17 polarization by producing IL-12, thereby exerting a protective effect against asthma [42], [43].The role of the recently identified DC3 subset in allergy remains to be established. pDCs modulate airway inflammation in asthma by secreting IFN-α, which inhibits innate type 2 lymphocytes (ILC2s) and reduces IL-33 release from epithelial cells [44]. Although pDCs can present antigens to CD4+T cells, they do so less efficiently than cDCs [45], inducing either activation or tolerance based on the microenvironment [46]. Significant variations exist in the phagocytosis timeline of pDCs for different allergens and their responses to TLR ligands, potentially influencing their roles in allergy or tolerance [47].

During inflammation, MoDCs primarily differentiate from monocytes in peripheral tissues. These cells display high expression of MHC-II molecules and remarkable plasticity, influencing the differentiation of CD4+T cells into Th1, Th2, or Th17 cells [48–50]. In the airways of asthmatic individuals, MoDCs undergo aberrant differentiation, disrupting their normal transition to a reparative macrophage-like phenotype via the IL-4/IL-13-STAT6 signaling pathway. This dysregulation locks MoDCs in an immature, proinflammatory state, resulting in robust upregulation of inflammatory chemokines, activation of lipid inflammatory pathways, and exacerbation of airway inflammation. Furthermore, MoDCs can collaborate with cDC2 to enhance type 2 inflammatory responses, establishing a detrimental feedback loop [51]. LCs capture and present inhaled allergens via FcεRI, intensifying allergic responses in atopic dermatitis patients [52]. Conversely, they can suppress contact hypersensitivity by inducing Tregs or disabling specific T cells [53].

The complexity of DCs extends beyond their role in antigen presentation to T cells, encompassing their intricate crosstalk with other immune and structural tissue cells that collectively shape allergic response networks. As frontline sentinels, epithelial cells detect allergens and secrete alarmins such as TSLP, IL-25, and IL-33, which activate DCs to upregulate chemokines and co-stimulatory molecules, thereby recruiting and polarizing Th2 cells [54, 55]. ILC2s further promote DC migration to draining lymph nodes (dLNs) by secreting IL-5 and IL-13, broadening the immune response [56]. These intricate intercellular interactions form a reinforcing Th2 inflammation feedback loop. Additionally, mast cells, basophils, and B cells can facilitate DC maturation and promote Th2 differentiation through direct contacting or cytokine releasing [57–62].

Simultaneously, DCs contribute to immune suppression through alternative pathways. For instance, DCs can activate NK cells to produce IFN-γ,thereby modulating the Th1/Th2 balance and restraining excessive type 2 immune responses [63]. Additionally, semi-mature DCs can drive the differentiation of suppressive B cell subsets to foster immune tolerance [64]. This intricate balance between pro-inflammatory and tolerogenic interactions underscores the key role of DCs as orchestrators in allergic pathogenesis and highlights that promoting the transition of DCs toward a tolerogenic phenotype is one of the pathways through which AIT achieves immune tolerance.

Mechanisms of DC-mediated immune tolerance

The induction of immune tolerance by DCs is a highly coordinated, multi-layered process encompassing synergistic alterations in cytokine signaling, cellular metabolism, epigenetic modifications, and cell migratory dynamics. These mechanisms are deeply intertwined. Metabolic reprogramming underpins the tolerance function of DCs and directly influences cytokine secretion. The dynamic process of migration, modulated by the functional state, is intricately linked to the inflammatory environment and cytokine network. Epigenetic regulation offers a stable molecular foundation for this functional reprogramming, sustaining tolerogenic phenotypes over time. Collectively, these intertwined mechanisms establish and maintain the tolerogenic DC phenotype, which is central to AIT success. Thus, elucidating the interplay among these mechanisms is crucial for unraveling the mechanistic essence of tolerance induction in AIT.

Cytokine signaling and tolerogenic programming

Through cytokine signaling, various DC subsets shape the balance of the immune microenvironment by secreting distinct cytokines. During allergic inflammation, myeloid DCs (mDCs) disrupt tolerance by enhancing antigen presentation, upregulating autophagy, and secreting IL-33, which accumulates inflammatory factors [65–67]. IL-33 enhances ILC2 function via the ST2 pathway and amplifies the Th2 response [68]. Alarmins like IL-33, TSLP, and IL-25 from epithelial cells activate DCs to upregulate OX40L and CCL17 expression, preferentially recruiting and polarizing Th2 cells to exacerbate allergic inflammation [54, 55]. Conversely, pDCs inhibit ILC2 activity by secreting IL-6 and producing inhibitory cytokines like IL-10 and TGF-β, which directly suppress T cell proliferation and induce Treg differentiation, promoting a tolerogenic state [69–72]. Importantly, the role of IL-10 extends beyond merely inhibiting cytokine secretion, as it can also activate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) to initiate a dedicated tolerogenic transcriptional program, thereby stabilizing the tolerogenic phenotype of DCs [73]. This tolerance signaling network can be negatively regulated. Zeng et al. demonstrated that CpG stimulation can abrogate the suppressive function of tolDCs mediated by the telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT)-IL-10 signaling pathway, highlighting this pathway as a potential therapeutic target [74]. Furthermore, integrin αvβ8, expressed by cDCs and RORγt+APCs, cleaves the latency-associated peptide (LAP) to activate TGF-β, promoting Treg differentiation, crucial for oral tolerance [75].

Notably, clinical evidence demonstrated that one year following sublingual immunotherapy (SLIT), the frequency of immature, IL-10-producing DCs is significantly increased in the peripheral blood of allergic patients [76]. Furthermore, with prolonged exposure to the major grass pollen allergen Phl p 5 under SLIT and oral immunotherapy (OIT), LCs in the oral mucosa exhibit delayed maturation, improved migration, and increased production of tolerogenic cytokines, including IL-10 and TGF-β [77]. Additionally, SLIT upregulated mTOR-associated markers, such as Nuak1 and Maf, which maintained the tolerogenic DC phenotype [78].

Metabolic reprogramming

Metabolic reprogramming is indispensable for DCs to acquire a tolerogenic phenotype, with the core mechanism being that alterations in the expression and activity of key metabolic enzymes directly influence immune function. For instance, tryptophan metabolites processed by indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) in human cDCs, along with granzyme B, can induce Treg differentiation and oral tolerance [79, 80]. Additionally, multiple cDC subsets express retinaldehyde dehydrogenase 2 (RALDH2), which converts vitamin A into RA, enabling Tregs to upregulate gut-homing receptors CCR9 and α4β7, thereby enhancing the tolerogenic response [81, 82].These mechanisms indicate that DCs, via metabolic reprogramming, can modify their immune function, dictate the fate of downstream T cells, and alter the immune microenvironment.

In addition, microbial metabolites profoundly modulate DC functions. Compounds such as butyrate and bile acid (BA) promote Treg differentiation and suppress allergic responses by engaging G-protein coupled receptor (GPCR) or the farnesoid X receptor (FXR) on DCs [83, 84]. Wu et al. demonstrated that gut microbiota dysbiosis can enhance RA signaling in DCs by modifying the BA metabolic profile, leading to the differentiation of CD4+T cells into Th2 cells and the production of allergen-specific IgE/IgG1 antibodies [85]. Under homeostatic conditions, commensal microbiota supports intestinal barrier function through TLR5 signaling, indirectly maintaining the tolerogenic phenotype of APCs [86]. Additionally, immunomodulatory molecules like Ascaris lumbricoides cysteine protease inhibitor (Al-CPI) bind to TLR4-like receptors on DCs, activate the mTOR signaling pathway, induce metabolic reprogramming, and ultimately downregulate DC co-stimulatory molecules expression, driving tolerogenic DC polarization [87].

Epigenetic regulation

Beyond transient signaling events, DC functional plasticity is underpinned by reversible yet stable epigenetic modifications, which provide a durable molecular framework for fine-tuning DC function in response to microenvironmental cues. These modifications confer a form of cellular immunological memory, enabling long-term maintenance of tolerogenic or activated states. For instance, the histone methyltransferase EZH2, an epigenetic activator of DCs [88], is significantly downregulated following AIT, and this reduction is linked to enhanced tolerogenic function [89]. Conversely, Ten-Eleven Translocation (TET) enzymes, particularly TET2, are pivotal for enforcing a tolerogenic phenotype [90]. Critically, TET2 serves as a convergence point for extrinsic tolerogenic signals: it is recruited to target genes by specific complexes—such as the VDR-pSTAT3 complex in response to vitamin D and the GR-MAFB complex in response to glucocorticoids—to mediate site-specific demethylation and activate a tolerogenic transcriptional program [91, 92]. Furthermore, the transcription factor PRDM16 has been identified as a key upstream regulator that acts synergistically with RORγt to orchestrate the development and functional maturation of tolDCs, solidifying the epigenetic foundation of immune tolerance [93]. This mechanistic axis directly integrates systemic hormonal and dietary cues with DC epigenetic reprogramming. The clinical relevance of this regulation is underscored by the consistent upregulation of well-established tolerogenic markers, such as C1q, in the peripheral blood of patients following successful AIT, as reported in two independent studies from the same research group. These findings provide robust, reproducible evidence supporting a key role for DCregs in mediating clinical tolerance [94, 95].

Regulation of DC migration

In allergic diseases, DCs take up allergens and migrate from peripheral tissues to lymph nodes, ultimately trafficking to effector sites. This migratory process, which represents the initiation and rate-limiting step of the pathological Th2 cascade, is primarily driven by the CCR7-CCL21 axis and regulated by cytoskeletal dynamics and tissue microenvironment cues [96]. Multiple licensing signals, including the PGE₂ -EP2/EP4 pathway, the oxysterol-EBI2 axis, the S1P-S1PR signal, and tissue amplification mechanisms like TSLP, augment DC migration and promote allergic sensitization [97]. Sokol et al. demonstrated that DCs with low CCR7 expression utilize the CCR8-CCL8 axis for effective migration to the lymph node parenchyma following allergen exposure, facilitating the Th2 immune response [98]. Thus, these findings position DC migration as a promising therapeutic strategy.

Therapeutic strategies aim to disrupt this step: inhibiting pathways like STAT6, CD47/SIRPα, or PAK1 reduces DC migration and alleviates allergic inflammation in mouse models [99–101]. Concurrently, the local immune microenvironment can restrict DC migration through specific cytokine production. For instance, skin-resident Tregs produce IL-10, which limits DC migration and alleviates skin allergy symptoms, highlighting the precise regulation of DC migration by cytokine networks [102]. This indicates that modulating local immune responses to control DC migration and activation could be a promising strategy for future interventions in allergic diseases. However, current evidence is predominantly derived from mouse models, and clinical translation remains unexplored.

Differences in DC types and antigen presentation mechanisms among different administration routes of AIT

The route of allergen administration in AIT establishes the initial immune niche, which influences the engagement of distinct DC subsets and the subsequent tolerance induction mechanisms. This variation arises from the unique composition of DC subsets at each tissue site, their antigen processing capabilities, and the local signaling milieus, all of which collectively determine antigen fate. A comparative analysis of these routes elucidates how local DC networks are utilized to achieve the goal of inducing tolDCs and establishing durable immune tolerance. Summary of changes in specific DC subsets or markers in different AIT routes

Polymerized allergoids conjugated to mannan (PM) represent promising vaccines candidates for AIT, as they can reprogram monocytes into tolDCs through metabolic and epigenetic rewiring [118, 119]. Transcriptomic profiling has revealed that 3121 genes are differentially expressed between tolDCs and mature inflammatory DCs, with significant enrichment in pathways related to T-cell activation and responses to external stimuli. Notably, high CD52 and low CD86 expression constitute the most robust predictive markers for distinguishing tolDCs from inflammatory DCs [120]. DCs isolated from allergic patients can be induced to acquire a stable immature tolerogenic phenotype and secrete high levels of IL-10 in vitro, thereby driving the induction of antigen-specific Tregs [121]. In addition, DCregs can directly suppress pDC activation by secreting C1q to mitigate allergic inflammation, independently of Treg induction [122]. Consistent with these mechanistic insights, clinical studies support that monitoring IL-10 and C1q levels following AIT serves as a reliable predictive strategy for clinical therapeutic responses [76, 94, 95].

Subcutaneous immunotherapy (SCIT)

SCIT, the conventional route of allergen administration, delivers allergens into the systemic circulation via subcutaneous injection, thereby exerting pleiotropic effects on multiple DC subsets. This systemic intervention effectively reshapes the immune response. During the acute phase following SCIT, the frequency of circulating pDCs decreases significantly within hours. This rapid reduction is not attributable to cellular depletion, but rather reflects the rapid recruitment and migration of pDCs from the bloodstream to local mucosal or lymph node compartments in response to allergen challenge. Consistent with these findings, previous studies have reported comparable reductions in circulating pDC frequencies at both 4-hour and 52-hour time points following AIT, both of which are likely attributable to transient tissue migration rather than cellular loss [14, 103]. During the maintenance phase of AIT, immune homeostasis is re-established, and circulating pDC frequencies stabilize without significant fluctuations [104]. Following long-term AIT, the establishment of immune tolerance and systemic immune remodeling lead to a significant increase in circulating pDC frequencies. This observation suggests that sustained allergen exposure upregulates steady-state pDC levels, thereby enhancing their tolerogenic functionality [105]. Collectively, pDCs promote Tregs [123], steering the immune response towards tolerance.

SCIT also modulates cDC function by downregulating C-type lectin receptors (CLRs) on CD1c+cDC2s and CD141+cDC1s, thereby reducing the Th2 inflammatory response [105, 124]. Restoring DNGR1 expression on cDC1s diminishes neutrophil infiltration and enhances CD8+T cell responses, which are essential for sustaining naturally occurring T regulatory cells (nTregs) suppressive function [125, 126]. Functional subsets like tolDCs and DCregs also increased after treatment [95, 106]. Furthermore, a study by Berings et al. demonstrated that SCIT significantly impairs the antigen-capturing capacity of cDC2 and pDC subsets [107]. Additional studies have reported that SCIT reduces TNF-α production by mDCs, decreases pro-inflammatory cytokine production [103], and elevates IFN-α levels in pDC [104], reaching levels comparable to those observed in non-allergic individuals. These changes collectively restore the Th1/Th2 balance, which represents the major mechanism of SCIT.

Sublingual immunotherapy (SLIT)

During SLIT, allergens primarily localize in the sublingual tissue (Fig. 2). The oral APC compartment consists of CD207+LCs within the mucosal layer, CD206+CD11b+CD11c−macrophage-like cells in the lamina propria, two types of cDCs with CD103+CD11b− and CD103−CD11b+ phenotypes, and semi-mature pDCs residing in the submucosa [127, 128]. DCs are consistently enriched in anatomical regions including the posterior sublingual region, gingiva, and buccal mucosa. Notably, the vestibular area, characterized by higher DC density, is deemed an optimal administration site for SLIT [129].

Histological evidence further indicates that a subset of allergens may also traverse the sublingual duct epithelium to reach adjacent APCs. These allergens are then captured by LCs in the oral mucosa and mDCs in the lamina propria. cDC2s subsequently transport antigens to the draining submandibular lymph nodes (ManLNs) or other cervical lymph nodes (cLNs). Through the significant upregulation of immunomodulatory molecules and the secretion of TGF-β and RA, these cells promote the differentiation of naive CD4+T cells into antigen-specific Foxp3+Treg cells within a timeframe of 2 to 5 days. This process effectively suppresses the local and systemic inflammatory response [130–133]. Importantly, allergens administered sublingually have minimal entry into the systemic circulation, thereby reducing the risk of systemic allergic reactions [134].

Sublingual DCs and macrophage-like cells prominently express FcεRI, potentially enhancing tolerance through IgE-mediated antigen presentation during SLIT [135–137]. Allam et al. showed that oral Langerhans cells (oLCs) promote Th1 responses and Treg differentiation while inhibiting the Th2 pathway during SLIT by secreting IL-10, IL-12, and TGF-β to induce tolerance [77]. Activation of the TLR4 receptor on human oLCs enhances IL-10 and TGF-β secretion, reinforcing the tolerogenic state in the oral cavity [138]. Additionally, oral macrophage-like cells express retinaldehyde dehydrogenase 1 (RALDH1) and RALDH2, possibly inducing antigen-specific Treg cells through retinoic acid production [137]. Lately, Yang et al. identified a CD206+macrophage subset in the sublingual mucosa that suppresses the antigen-presenting function of mature DCs by producing IL-10, consequently inhibiting the CD4+T cell response and maintaining oral immune homeostasis [139].

Multiple clinical studies have consistently demonstrated that DCs serve as major regulators during SLIT, representing valuable targets for therapeutic efficacy monitoring and mechanistic investigation. SLIT induces profound phenotypic and functional remodeling of peripheral blood DCs. On one hand, it suppresses DC maturation and activation, as evidenced by downregulated expression of co-stimulatory molecules including CD86 and HLA-DR on mature DCs [76, 110]. On the other hand, it drives the differentiation of DCregs, leading to elevated expression of DCreg-associated markers such as C1q, coupled with reduced expression of DC2-related markers including CD141 and GATA3 [94, 95]. Guéguen et al. reported that IL-10 mRNA levels in whole blood cells correlate with clinical efficacy in house dust mite immunotherapy, with DCs hypothesized to be the primary cellular source of IL-10 [111]. This hypothesis was further validated by findings from Angelini et al. [76]. Furthermore, SLIT has been shown to attenuate the expression of maturation markers (including CCR7, CD40, CD83, and CD86) and upregulate PD-L1 expression on moDCs, thereby promoting immunosuppressive and Treg responses [112].

Oral immunotherapy (OIT)

The primary sites for antigen presentation during OIT are localized to the intestine mucosa, where a complex APC network governs the dual potential of the immune response. Intestinal APCs consist mainly of microfold cells (M cells), IL-10-secreting CD163+macrophages, CD103+CD11b−CLEO9A+XCR1+cDC1s, and CD103+CD11b+Sirpa+cDC2s in the lamina propria. These APCs capture antigens and allergens primarily as polypeptides, and subsequently migrate to draining lymphoid tissues, such as Peyer’s patches and mesenteric lymph nodes [75, 127]. cDC2s exhibit elevated RALDH2 expression at steady state [140], promoting Treg differentiation through the production of RA and thereby initiating oral tolerance. Intestinal pDCs mediate oral tolerance via ablating or converting antigen-specific T cells into Tregs [141]. Notably, DCs migrate from the subepithelial dome (SED) to the T-cell zone in Peyer’s patches during inflammation, generating Tregs via secretion of IL-10 or TGF-β. In contrast, monocyte-derived CX3CR1+DCs and CD103+cDC2s induce Th2 polarization through the OX40L-OX40 axis, TIM-4-TIM-1 interaction, and IL-33-ST2 signaling pathway in inflammation under antigen stimulation [75]. Furthermore, the Th2 cytokines force MoDCs to increase allergen uptake and HLA expression, thereby forming a positive feedback-loop that exacerbates allergic inflammation and counters establishment of tolerance [142].

A newly identified population of RORγt+APCs activates integrin αvβ8 through MHC class II and TGF-β expression, predominantly influencing the differentiation of peripheral regulatory T cells (pTregs) and significantly contributing to the establishment of early-life tolerance [143]. Fu et al. confirmed the existence of a Prdm16-dependent tolerogenic DC subset known as RORγt+tolDCs, which shares similar epigenetic characteristics with cDCs [93].

OIT exerts time-dependent and context-specific regulatory effects on the phenotypic and functional plasticity of DCs, with profound translational implications for clinical intervention in allergic disorders. OIT induces dynamic remodeling of DCs by upregulating the expression of HLA-DR, CD86, and PD-L1, concomitantly inducing a high IL-10/IL-6 ratio and promoting the generation of functional Tregs.These observations support the adjuvant potential of mannan-derived formulations to enhance the efficacy of peanut OIT[144]. In clinical practice, OIT responders exhibit significantly reduced expression of TNF-α and OX40L on mDCs relative to non-responders, suggesting that a diminished pro-inflammatory DC transcriptional signature constitutes a predictive biomarker of favorable OIT outcomes [113]. During the acute phase of OIT, DCs display downregulated expression of key co-stimulatory molecules, including CD40, CD86, and HLA-DR, coupled with a transient increase in CD80 expression. This phenotypic shift is accompanied by robust suppression Th2 cytokine production driven by DCs. However, these immunosuppressive effects are typically transient and reverse following prolonged maintenance (12–18 months) or treatment cessation, with DC phenotype and function gradually reverting to baseline levels [110]. Notably, several studies have failed to detect significant alterations in baseline DC surface markers, such as CD11c, CD86, and CD14. This discrepancy likely stems from the focus on static phenotypic profiles of in vitro-differentiated DCs rather than dynamic functional responses elicited in vivo following antigen challenge [145]. Collectively, these findings emphasize that the immunomodulatory effects of OIT on DCs are critically dependent on treatment timing, cellular activation status, and antigenic burden. Static phenotypic analysis alone fails to capture these dynamic functional adaptations, which underpin apparent inconsistencies across studies. Ultimately, these observations highlight DCs as pivotal mediators of immune tolerance, underscoring their value in elucidating therapeutic mechanisms, predicting clinical responses, and optimizing strategies to achieve durable, long-term immune tolerance.

Epidermal immunotherapy (EPIT)

EPIT is a form of AIT that induces immune tolerance by targeting tissues abundant in APCs via the skin [146, 147]. Skin-resident DCs are compartmentalized across the epidermis and dermis. Within the dermis, distinct subsets include cDC1s in the deep layer, cDC2s in the superficial subcortical layer, along with CCR2+MoDCs, pDCs, and DC3s that infiltrate during inflammation, alongside CD207−CD11b−double-negative DCs (DNDCs) [148–150]. LCs predominantly populate the epidermis. Dermal cDC2s function as the primary cutaneous APCs, capturing antigens, migrating to the skin dLNs, and presenting peptide-MHC-II complexes to CD4+T cells. The distinction lies in the fact that while LCs in the epidermis can present antigens under homeostatic conditions, they typically promote Treg differentiation or induce T cell apoptosis to uphold immune tolerance. In inflammatory milieus, LCs may be influenced by alarmins (e.g., TSLP, IL-33) and adopt a Th2-promoting phenotype, albeit with a notably lower antigen-presenting capacity compared to cDC2s [75, 151].

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis during EPIT reveals distinct functional reprogramming of DC subsets: LCs progressively lose the capability to activate CD4⁺ effector T cells, favoring Treg proliferation. Concurrently, cDC2s maintain moderate stimulatory activity, skewing the immune response from Th2 to Th1/Th17 via IL-10 secretion [115]. This orchestrated remodeling of DC phenotype and function underpins the considerable mechanism of EPIT-induced allergy desensitization.

Intranasal immunotherapy (INIT)

The intranasal route offers the benefit of circumventing gastric degradation and hepatic first-pass metabolism owing to the nasal cavity’s relatively permeable surface [152]. Nasal biopsies from non-allergic individuals demonstrate that the nasal mucosa is predominantly comprised of a dense network composed of macrophages and immature DCs, with a higher abundance of macrophages [153].

Intranasal administration induces immune tolerance through both direct cellular interactions and a distinct non-contact mechanism. In contact-dependent processes, specialized M cells in the nasal mucosal epithelium capture luminal antigens and transport them via transcytosis, mediated by Claudin-4 and Rab13, to the underlying nasal-associated lymphoid tissue (NALT). Here, antigens are presented to DCs and macrophages [154–156]. Epithelial cells further secrete CCL20 to recruit DCs and release factors like GM-CSF, G-CSF, VEGF, TSLP, and BAFF, which activate DCs and promote dendrite extension across the epithelial barrier for antigen sampling. These activated DCs migrate to the NALT, cLNs, and potentially bronchus-associated lymphoid tissue (BALT), linking local and systemic immune responses [157, 158].

Concurrently, a non-contact mechanism involves cDCs in the NALT releasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2). This soluble signaling axis inhibits T cell proliferation and induces apoptosis post-antigen presentation, maintaining immune tolerance without direct cell contact. These contact-based and cytokine-mediated pathways together illustrate a comprehensive strategy by which intranasal immunization modulates antigen presentation and T cell responses to promote tolerance [159].

Intralymphatic immunotherapy (ILIT) and intratonsillar immunotherapy (ITIT)

ILIT represents a direct approach to enhancing antigen presentation efficiency. By precisely injecting antigens into lymphoid organs, ILIT circumvents the limitations of other administration routes, where only a minor fraction of antigens is actively transported to the dLNs by APCs. This direct delivery allows for optimal immune processing and antibody responses [160, 161]. Antigens administered into the lymph nodes specifically localize in the paracortex [162]. Building on this conceptual framework, recent studies have explored ITIT as a more efficient and safer option for treating allergic rhinitis. Tonsillar delivery eliminates the need for ultrasound guidance for lymph node injections, associated procedural risks and technical complexity [163].

The palatine tonsils are characterized by two distinct epithelial phenotypes: reticular and non-reticular [164]. Non-reticular epithelium describes the stratified squamous epithelium on the palatine tonsil mucosa surface. Lymphoepithelial symbiosis (LES) areas, located in the crypts, contain numerous immature dendritic cells (LES-DCs) that primarily engage with IgM+IgD+ resting naive B cells. Additionally, immature LCs are present in the squamous epithelium, while the T-zone harbors four distinct types of interdigitating dendritic cells (IDCs) at various maturation stages, with the highest concentration observed [165–167]. These IDCs predominantly interact with CD4+T cells. On the other hand, M cells situated on the reticular epithelium intermingle with numerous lymphocytes, macrophages, and DCs to establish a network membrane for capturing and presenting antigens [164, 168].

Circulating precursor DCs within the tonsillar epithelium migrate to the epithelium to interact with antigens, activate macrophages and NK cells, and initiate innate immune responses [169]. These DCs subsequently transport antigens to the extrafollicular T cell zone and B cell follicles, leading to the development of primary and secondary T cell responses [154]. Besides this, specialized M cells capture antigens and deliver them to DCs, macrophages, and B cells for processing and presentation [170]. Recent studies have identified a CD207+CD1c+DC subset, primarily located in the tonsillar lymphoid stroma and closely related to cDC2s. This subset may exhibits inflammatory adaptation, expressing CD207 in response to inflammatory stimuli like TGF-β [171]. Furthermore, Segura et al. demonstrated that all major tonsil-resident DC subsets, including cDC1s, cDC2s, and pDCs, are proficient in cross-presenting soluble antigens, whereas macrophages lack this capability [172]. These findings establish a robust anatomical and immunological foundation for ITIT as a promising precision treatment strategy.Nevertheless, as an emerging route, ITIT suffers from a paucity of clinical evidence, especially regarding DC-mediated mechanisms. Further investigation is required to understand the phenotypic and functional alterations of APCs, such as DCs, following ITIT treatment.

Comparison of DC-mediated tolerance mechanisms across different AIT routes

Despite distinct routes of administration, AIT strategies can promote tolerance through the induction of tolDCs, although the key mechanisms may differ. During AIT, high-dose allergen challenge drives DCregs to produce IL-10 and IL-12, which suppress Th2 cell responses, promote the induction of Tregs and regulatory B cells(Bregs), and establish durable immune deviation toward Th1 cell response [173]. These shared effects underscore the considerable role of DC reprogramming in successful AIT.

Distinct route characteristics are shaped by unique DC subsets and tissue-specific microenvironmental signals. SCIT targets skin-resident DC subsets to initiate immune tolerance, followed by systemic modulation of circulating DCs in peripheral blood, thereby regulating the Th1/Th2 balance. It is generally accepted that SLIT typically requires a 50–100 times higher cumulative allergen dose than SCIT to achieve comparable therapeutic efficacy [174]. Enhancing antigen uptake by DCs has been shown to potentiate the efficacy of SLIT [175]. Therefore, optimizing allergen capture efficiency by DCs via advanced delivery systems represents a promising strategy to augment the therapeutic potential of SLIT. EPIT take advantage of the high density of DCs in skin, capturing antigens and inducing tolerance in local lymph nodes. This approach has garnered attention due to its capacity to minimize the risk of systemic allergic reactions. Moreover, as a novel EPIT strategy, intradermal administration of RA or VD3 loaded liposomes can locally induce a tolerogenic phenotype in skin-resident DCs in situ and enhance the migration of tolDCs to dLNs [176]. OIT exploits the intricate APC network in the intestinal mucosa to drive Treg differentiation and oral tolerance induction via RA production. Notably, the phenotypic and functional state of intestinal DCs is exquisitely regulated by the gut microbiota and its metabolites. Despite these advances, achieving long-term immune tolerance via OIT for food allergies remains challenging [177]. ILIT and ITIT inject antigens directly into lymphoid tissues rich in antigen-presenting and antigen-responsive cells, achieving immune efficiency 100 to 1000 times greater than subcutaneous and intramuscular injections [178].

TolDC induction is a common feature of all administration routes and constitutes one mechanism of immune tolerance in AIT. The primary distinction resides in the capacity of allergens delivered through various pathways to be adequately internalized by DCs, thereby promoting the formation of a tolerogenic phenotype. Differences in DC density, subset composition, and local immune microenvironment across anatomical sites dictate critical parameters, including optimal allergen dosing, adjuvant selection, and the design of innovative therapeutic approaches. These strategies encompass the direct induction of tolDCs and the targeted inhibition of DC activation.

Novel therapeutic strategies targeting DCs

AIT is currently the primary treatment for allergic diseases, involving a gradual increase in allergen extract dosages to enhance patient tolerance and alleviate allergic symptoms through repeated allergen exposure. Despite its efficacy, AIT has been linked to potentially fatal side effects that are challenging to identify [10, 179]. Insights into the regulatory role of DCs in immune tolerance have redirected the focus of next-generation AIT toward the integration of DC-targeted molecular technologies with conventional AIT regimens (Table 3). This approach aims to develop novel precision treatment strategies that specifically regulate DC functions, primarily through three paradigms: adjuvants, advanced delivery systems, and genetic engineering.

Summary of novel targeted DC therapeutic strategies

Adjuvants

The utilization of innovative adjuvants to modulate DC activation pathways holds promise for precise regulation of allergic responses and mitigation of systemic side effects linked to conventional therapies. For instance, the small molecule compound EIPA significantly enhances the transcriptional activity of the IL-12b gene promoter, promoting the targeted degradation of the Th2-inducing molecule TIM4. This regulatory cascade effectively blocks DC-mediated Th2 polarization signals, markedly inhibiting eosinophil infiltration, IgE production, and the Th2 cytokine storm [180]. Alternatively, co-administration of the tolerogenic adjuvant rTgPI-1 with allergens via intranasal route can diminish the proportion of lung tissue DCs exhibiting high CD80/CD86 expression. This approach specifically reduces the abundance of cDC2s, along with Th2 cytokines, while expanding the population of lung-resident Tregs [196]. These strategies indicate a therapeutic trend toward precisely suppressing allergic inflammation by promoting tolerance through targeted molecular inhibition of the DC activation pathway. Nonetheless, additional human trials are necessary to confirm safety.

Delivery systems

Nanoparticle-based delivery systems are at the forefront of DC-targeted therapy. By employing precise in vivo reprogramming and strategies like passive, active, and functional targeting, these systems facilitate the targeted delivery of immunomodulators. This approach provides a more effective and safer alternative for AIT compared to traditional methods.

Drug-loaded nanoparticles

These systems take advantage of nanoparticles containing immunomodulatory drugs like glucocorticoids or rapamycin to effectively inhibit DC activation and co-stimulatory signals while upregulating inhibitory molecules such as PD-L1 and IL-10. This approach promotes Treg differentiation and successfully suppresses the Th2 inflammatory response in preclinical models of asthma and atopic dermatitis [181, 182]. Similarly, application of this delivery approach in celiac disease models has enabled the induction of antigen-specific immune tolerance [197].

Protein and Nucleic Acid Biologics

Direct delivery of exogenous soluble programmed death-ligand 1 protein(sPD-L1) effectively reduced CD86 expression on DC surfaces and impeded CCR7-mediated DC migration, inhibiting the immune response from multiple aspects [183]. Furthermore, Wang et al. successfully induced tolerance and alleviated asthma symptoms by targeting the liver with an mRNA vaccine formulated with lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) [184].

Engineered vesicles

Engineered vesicles offer an innovative targeting strategy, utilizing mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-derived vesicles as delivery vehicles with surface-specific modifications for DCs to achieve specific recognition and binding. For example, Sadeghi et al. achieved targeted delivery of allergens, such as ovalbumin, to oral mucosal DCs. Following sublingual administration, this approach effectively induced Th1 immune deviation and Treg expansion at a lower antigen dose (5 µg Ova) than conventional SLIT, significantly reducing airway inflammatory infiltration and highlighting the advantages of precise targeting [185]. Another approach involves the use of engineered vesicles (P-D2-EVs) to target DCs. By suppressing the expression of the Fut1 gene in DCs, the α-(1,2)-fucosylation modification of the ICAM1 molecule is inhibited, thereby blocking the activation of the Fut1/ICAM1/P38-MAPK signaling pathway. Metabolic reprogramming of DCs drives the secretion of IL-10, inhibiting the differentiation of Th2 cells and the activation of ILC2s, thereby ameliorating the clinical manifestations of allergic rhinitis [186].

Gene editing

Genetic engineering, especially the CRISPR/Cas9 system, provides a foundational method for reprogramming DCs to induce enduring functional changes. Similar strategies have been successfully validated in other disease contexts. Wang et al. innovatively developed an autologous antigen peptide nanodelivery system by employing CRISPR/Cas9 to knock out genes encoding the costimulatory molecules CD80, CD86, and CD40, thereby reprogramming it to a tolerogenic phenotype [187, 188]. Surprisingly, Kim et al. have taken the lead in exploring this strategy in allergic diseases. They employ the CRISPR/Cas9 system to target VPS37A and/or VPS37B genes in DCs in vitro. This approach aimed to modulate allergen endocytosis through mannose receptors. Subsequent in vivo trials on mice demonstrated that modified DCs effectively mitigated aberrant Th2 immune reactions, alleviated nasal allergy symptoms, and decreased local tissue levels of Th2 cytokines [189]. While promising, this approach currently relies on ex vivo DC modification and adoptive transfer.

Insights from other immunotherapeutic fields

The extensive research on DC targeting strategies in autoimmune diseases, organ transplantation, and cancer offers valuable insights for treating allergic diseases. In autoimmune diseases, researchers induce stable and reproducible tolDCs by incorporating metabolic regulators such as α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) [190], 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D3 [191], or agonists of the transcription factor receptor AhR [192]. In organ transplantation, the delivery of CD40 small interfering RNA (siRNA) targets mature DCs, macrophages, hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), and myeloid progenitor cells in the spleen. This approach not only diminishes the activation state and antigen-presenting capability of DCs but also reduces the number of effector APCs at their origin [193]. Intriguingly, substitution of CD40 with PD-L1/TIM-3-targeting siRNA in the delivery system can reverse the immunosuppressive microenvironment by inhibiting the expression of DC immune checkpoints, thereby eliciting an opposing effect [194]. In essence, by tailoring DC-targeting therapeutics and technologies within the established delivery framework, there is flexibility in modulating immune suppression and activation, offering novel prospects for future allergic immunotherapy. This successful multidisciplinary application shows that integrating advanced gene-editing technologies with DC-targeted delivery systems enables precise and sustained regulation of DC functions, marking a significant advancement in the future development of AIT.

Summary and outlook

DCs are important immune regulators and contribute critically to allergic diseases, serving not merely as participants but as orchestrators of immune tolerance. Current strategies for AIT focus on inducing DCs to adopt a tolerogenic phenotype and restoring immune tolerance. Utilizing small-particle carriers to deliver drugs that target DCs holds significant promise for treating allergic conditions. However, the development of novel DC-targeted therapeutic approaches in allergic diseases lags behind that in autoimmune diseases, lacking clinical validation. Presently, clinical strategies for targeted treatment primarily involve isolating patients’ DCs, generating tolDC vaccines in vitro, and subsequently reintroducing them into the patient [195]. While the feasibility of this approach has been demonstrated, it is hindered by challenges such as intricate procedures, high expenses, and the propensity of exogenous tolDCs to transition to an activated phenotype in the inflammatory milieu in vivo. This transformation may exacerbate immune responses and compromise treatment efficacy stability [198]. Hence, employing diverse delivery materials, particularly DC-targeted nanodelivery systems, for precise administration of immunomodulators, or utilizing the CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing system to selectively modulate the activities of DC subsets, alter the activation and metabolic profiles of DCs, and consistently modify the DC phenotype, can mitigate the issue of unstable phenotypes in exogenous DCs. In vivo programming of DCs represents a pioneering approach to enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy for conditions like allergic and autoimmune diseases.

Currently, our understanding of DC-mediated tolerance mechanisms in AIT and of DC-targeting therapeutic strategies largely derives from preclinical studies in mouse models.However, fundamental differences between mouse and human DC subsets may hinder clinical translation, as clearly demonstrated by transcriptomic analyses [199, 200]. Consequently, pathways deemed critical in mouse models may not play equivalent roles in human AIT [201]. Additionally, the challenge of translating doses with marked efficacy in mouse models into equivalent therapeutic doses for patients should not be underestimated [202].

Beyond these considerations, the clinical translation of this approach still faces formidable challenges. Firstly, the utilization of drug delivery systems in AIT necessitates refining the co-loading and co-release strategies of allergens and drugs to ensure precise spatiotemporal efficacy within target cells. Secondly, there is a marked paucity of data supporting the real-time tracking of tolDC behavior and phenotype in human subjects. Furthermore, the safety of prolonged administration, potential off-target effects, and the immunogenicity of nanomaterials themselves require rigorous evaluation. Additionally, variations in allergic conditions and the immune profiles of patients may impact delivery efficiency and immune modulation outcomes.

Several issues must be addressed and improved, encompassing distinctions between animal models and human immunity, variations in DC presentation via diverse drug delivery methods, uncertainties in target selection and drug delivery approaches, and the potential for inducing significant immune dysregulation by modulating DCs. In summary, the integration of AIT with molecular technologies is poised to revolutionize the treatment paradigm for allergic diseases. However, achieving this objective necessitates substantial and sustained efforts in both fundamental and clinical investigations. In the forthcoming era, interdisciplinary cooperation can leverage single-cell omics to scrutinize the diversity of DCs in patients, facilitating personalized therapeutic strategies and disease interventions that align more closely with patient expectations.
