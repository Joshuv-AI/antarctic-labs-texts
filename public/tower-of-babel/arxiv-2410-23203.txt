Resilient-By-Design: A Resiliency Framework for Future Wireless Networks
Nurul Huda Mahmood, Sumudu Samarakoon, Pawani Porambage, Mehdi Bennis, Matti Latva-aho

Abstract
Our future society will be increasingly digitalised, hyper-connected and globally data driven. The sixth generation (6G) and beyond 6G wireless networks are expected to bridge the digital and physical worlds by providing wireless connectivity as a service to different vertical sectors, including industries, smart cities, eHealth and autonomous transportation. Such far reaching integration will render the society increasingly reliant on wireless networks. While this has the potential to greatly enhance our quality and ease of life, any disruption to these networks would also have significant impact with overreaching consequences. Disruptions can happen due to a variety of reasons, including planned outages, failures due to the nature of wireless propagation, natural disasters, and deliberate cybersecurity attacks. Hence, 6G and beyond 6G networks should not only provide near instant and virtually unlimited connectivity, but also be resilient against internal and external disruptions. This paper proposes a resilient-by-design framework towards this end. First, we provide an overview of the disruption landscape. Thereafter, we comprehensively outline the main features of the proposed concept. Finally, we detail the four key steps of the framework, namely predict , preempt , protect and progress . A simple but illustrative preliminary simulation result is also presented to highlight the potential advantages and efficiency of the proposed approach in addressing outages.

Motivation and background

Recently ten nations across North America, Europe, Asia, and Oceania issued a joint statement endorsing

Secure and open 6G technologies and architectures are respectively required to enable the network to fail safely and recover quickly in case of potential cybersecurity threats, and develop global 6G standards, interfaces, & specifications through universally agreed processes that supports interoperability. On the other hand, resiliency – which is the focus of this article – will enable the network to provide and maintain an acceptable level of service in the face of disruptions and challenges to normal operation.

The term resiliency originates from the Latin word
resiliere
– meaning
to rebound or spring back
, and is used across different academic disciplines. In the engineering domain, resiliency is most notably used in system engineering, where it is referred to as the intrinsic ability of a system to adjust its functionality in the presence of disturbances and unpredicted changes
[
1
]
. Any given network or system is subject to disruptions, be it planned or unplanned. An example of the former is outages due to scheduled maintenance, while most of the latter instances are single link failures due to various factors such as random fading or interference and unintentional disruptions by third-parties. However, a significant portion of the unplanned failures are induced by internal and/or external factors such as network glitches, disasters and cyber-security attacks
[
2
]
. Such disruptions may incur a hefty cost in terms of network downtime, lost revenues and privacy & security breach, especially since they are often broader in scope and more dynamic in nature than random failures.

The network’s ability to avoid or cope with failures have traditionally been measured through reliability, availability and survivability
[
3
]
. However, these metrics cannot sufficiently reflect the network’s robustness to failures and its ability to restore normal operation following a failure.
Resilience
– which includes two aspects: maintaining the quality of service (QoS) in the face of disruptions, and the quality of remediation or recovery from a failure – is proposed as an overarching and essential design and operational characteristic of future networks that can address this gap
[
2
]
. Resilience will be especially important in future 6G and beyond 6G wireless networks given that many services will increasingly be offered by mission-critical applications that will need guaranteed performance with respect to various QoS and quality of security (QoSec) metrics
[
4
]
.

Challenge tolerance and trustworthiness are two important attributes that serves as the basis of, and is subsumed by, the concept of resilience
[
2
]
. A challenge tolerant network is able to continue providing service in the face of internal and/or external challenges to normal operation, and includes characteristics like fault tolerance, survivability and disruption tolerance. On the other hand, trustworthiness – which encompasses dependability, privacy & security, and explainability – guarantees that the network performs as expected.

Resilience in the context of communications networks is a rather new topic that has only been sporadically studied so far. The seminal monograph
[
2
]
, which is the result of a four-year long COST project, provides a comprehensive introduction to this discipline and serves as a guide to disaster-resilient communication networks in general. The concepts of resilience and mixed-criticality, which can ensure criticality awareness for diverse functional safety applications and provide fault tolerance in an autonomous manner, are jointly considered in the formulation of a resource optimization problem in
[
5
]
. Therein the authors propose and evaluate four different resilience mechanisms and demonstrate considerable advantages of incorporating a mixed criticality-aware resilience strategy. Reference
[
6
]
argues the need for future 6G networks to be resilient, and introduces a comprehensive concept for designing resilient 6G communication networks, where they suggest embedding physical and cyber resilience across all layers. On a different note, reference
[
7
]
investigates the role of national roaming as a resilience improvement approach by combining models with public data from national bodies on mobile network operator infrastructures, population distribution, and urbanity level to assess the coverage and capacity of cellular networks at a country scale.

Despite these (and other) notable and important contributions, there is still a wide gap in the existing literature on the important topic of resiliency, especially in the context of wireless networks. This article aims to contribute towards bridging this gap by providing a comprehensive vision for resiliency in future 6G and beyond 6G networks as outlined in the rest of this article. In particular, our main contributions are:

•

We review the state of the art definition of resiliency and discuss the different kinds of vulnerabilities that a network should be made resilient against,

•

focusing on wireless networks, we attempt to explore the question -
what does it mean to make a wireless network resilient?
,

•

and importantly, we present a holistic framework for making 6G and beyond 6G networks
resilient-by-design

II
Resiliency: State of the Art Definitions and Disruption Landscape

II-A

How is resiliency conventionally defined?

Irrespective of the field of study, the general concept of resiliency is defined by the system characteristics at a disruption followed by a recovery. Therein, detection of a disruption, remediation against the disturbance, and the process of recovery are pivotal for the analysis of system resilience
[
8
]
. Here, disruption can be defined as drastic changes in the system state or as unexpected impacts on the workflow. Suppose
s(t)
be the desired service level at time
t
, which is the ratio between the service state and the desired service level. For example, in a communication system, the service state could be the instantaneous data rate for a given link while the minimum rate target could be the desired level. It is worth noting that
s(t)\succeq\mathbf{1}
implies the system meets the desired service level while the opposite indicates a detection of a disruption.

With a detection of a failure, there could be several steps towards recovery and return to normal operating state. It may be in discrete steps or a continuous process, as illustrated in Fig.
1
. The authors of
[
5
]
have defined three phases for the discrete system state case, namely absorption, adoption, and recovery phase. Then, the overall system resilience is defined as the weighted sum of functions of the time spent in each phase, where the weights for each phase add up to one. Alternatively, resilience in the continuous system state case is quantified with the recovery curve
[
9
]
. Then, the system resilience can be quantified using the dynamics of the recovery process as the normalized area under the system state during the recovery process, as shown in Fig.
1
(right). However, this definition fails to distinguish between different recovery methods. As a remedy, the cumulative resilience function (CRF) is defined in
[
10
]
as a term analogous to the cumulative density function in probability theory. The CRF describes the system state during the recovery process and hence allows comparison between the resilience of two different recovery processes.

II-B

Different Disruption Types

This section briefly discusses the different kinds of vulnerabilities that a network should be made resilient against, which are also illustrated in Fig.
2
.

II-B
1
Resiliency against (internal) network disruptions

Due to the stochastic nature of wireless propagation, wireless networks must be inherently designed to be robust against a certain level of failure at different layers. Other sources of internal disruptions in include interference, algorithmic/software glitch and physical node failures. Co-channel interference is a fundamental problem in wireless networks, more so as future networks are further densifying to support the increasing data rate requirements. Additionally, two recent wireless networking trends may (inadvertently) expose the network to new sources of disruptions. The first is the move towards openness. While this promotes interoperability, it also opens a new level of complexity and introduces compatibility challenges that may expose the network to multiple points of failures especially in networks serving mission critical applications. A related but different challenge arises from the pervasive use of generative artificial intelligence (AI) in the network design and software development process flow. Ensuring the quality and reliability of such generated code is a primary challenge that can introduce new sources of network failures. Debugging such errors becomes more difficult as they are usually logical errors that are much harder to identify.

II-B
2
Resiliency against unintentional external factors

A significant part of unplanned failures are induced by unintentional external factors such as natural disasters and adverse weather effects
[
2
]
. Such failures are often far more dynamic and broader in scope and impact than internal failures. Unfortunately, disaster induced network failures will be more prevalent in the future as climate change is increasing the frequency and intensity of extreme weather events. Disruptions may also occur due to technical failures in interdependent networks such as power or backhaul networks and due to solar storms
[
11
]
. Although not a network failure per se, the lack of network availability in remote areas also affects resiliency as it impacts the key 6G value of
inclusiveness
and challenges the global coverage target.

II-B
3
Resiliency against cyber-security threats

Digital infrastructure, including wireless networks, are targeted by cyber-attacks more than ever before. The motive of such attacks is not only for the financial gains but also to break down the normal operations of the systems and impose data breaches as well as the privacy leakages. The instability of the geo-political situation and technological advancements have amplified the frequency and intensity of cyber-attacks and their consequences in both short and long run. AI advancement has become a double-edged sword in this aspect as it enables autonomous security management while also contributing to the rise of sophisticated AI-driven attacks
[
12
]
. The security attacks performed by quantum technology are foreseen as another pressing threat to the future digital infrastructure. The capability of quantum computing to break classical cipher keys and the conventional encryption methods used in the current systems will lead to unprecedented vulnerabilities in the wireless networks. Alongside, it is also important to make networks resilient against physical layer security attacks. Malicious actors may deploy a variety of attack such as electromagnetic pulse attack, attacks against the control channel (e.g., during beam alignment in millimeter wave communication), eavesdropping, authentication attacks and reconfigurable intelligent surface (RIS)-induced attacks.

III
Resiliency in Wireless Networks

The ultra reliable low latency communications (URLLC) service class introduced in fifth generation (5G) New Radio paved the door towards providing connectivity as a service for different vertical sectors, including industries, smart cities, eHealth and autonomous transportation. Although URLLC presented a paradigm shift in wireless network evolution, it nonetheless failed to holistically provide the desired connectivity solution for most mission critical services due to several limitations
[
13
]
. Firstly, URLLC is application agnostic whereas different mission critical applications have different design requirements. Secondly, while reliability and latency are important key performance indicators (KPI), there are also other important metrics like the distribution of failures or jitter that need to be considered. Next, URLLC is inherently limited in scale as it only focuses on a single link. Finally, security and trustworthiness considerations are not reflected. To address these shortcomings, we propose making 6G and beyond 6G networks
resilient-by-design
, i.e., they should have the capacity to continuously anticipate and adjust to changes in their environment, and overcome any setbacks without compromising their intended performance.

Within different engineering disciplines, attempts have been made to define resiliency mathematically, see for example
[
1
]
and references therein. However, these definitions should be revisited and revised to reflect particular characteristics of wireless networks and the mission-critical applications it aims to support in the future. Furthermore, given the importance of safeguarding against security and trust vulnerabilities discussed in Section
II-B3
, such considerations must be a prominent feature of resiliency in future wireless networks.

The very nature of wireless propagation makes it vulnerable to random fading, interference and other fluctuations. Hence, wireless systems have always been designed to inherently tolerate certain degrees of failures, as exemplified in Fig.
3
. A simple example is the hybrid automatic repeat request protocol which allows wireless transmitters to retransmit a packet in the event of a transmission failure. Nonetheless, a resilient wireless system should be able to not only address disruptions due to the stochastic nature of the wireless propagation channel, but also withstand faults and errors arising from other sources such as large scale failures, natural disasters and security threats. In the rest of this section, we suggest four key principles for making wireless networks resilient-by-design, as illustrated in Fig.
4
.

Resiliency definition with respect to the particular characteristics of wireless communication.

III-A

Application-awareness

Different applications have different requirements, some of which reflect the particular nature of wireless communications. For instance, machines in an industrial process have a survival time (time without receiving new data) of two or three cycle times (update interval of data) before which it would stop and move to a safe position. The resilient-by-design concept can differentiate among the requirements of different applications through the service level agreements (SLA), which define the priority, quality, and responsibility associated with the provided service. Under normal operating condition, the network efficiency can be optimized by allocating resources to applications as needed instead of supporting a generic reliability or latency target. SLAs can also ensure the continuity of service by defining staggered service levels, and allowing prioritizing certain applications over others during disruption situations where network resources are constrained.

III-B

Key Performance and Value Indicators (KPIs and KVIs)

Efficiency is an important cornerstone of any network design, including the envisioned resilient-by-design concept. Efficient optimization of different resiliency aspects require defining novel KPIs and metrics that can facilitate mathematical formulation of the resiliency optimization problem. Reliability and latency are the main metrics measuring a wireless network’s/link’s ability to cope with failures in URLLC. Resiliency has a wider scope and hence must considers other metrics as well. For example, in the case of the process automation application described earlier with a given survival time, the distribution of failures, number of consecutive failures and the mean time between failures are more relevant metrics than the reliability
[
14
]
. It will also be important to consider the time needed to rebound to normal service after a disruption occurs. Collating this with differentiated service levels under disruptions, the rebound rate need to be separately measured for each level. Alongside, future wireless networks including 6G will not only achieve the performance requirements set by the applications/use cases but also create values to the society in different forms measured through the novel concept of key value indicators (KVI).

III-C

Scalability

Differently from URLLC, which focuses on a single point-to-point transmission link, resiliency is a system wide concept. Hence, resiliency in wireless networks can be considered as the ability of the network to mitigate and swiftly recover from the disruptions caused by security and privacy breaches. Suppose, we have a wireless network of
N
nodes where the goal is to sustain connectivity among the nodes in the case of node or link failures. The resilient-by-design concept should aim to maximize the number of nodes that should fail before the network connectivity breaks, minimize the vulnerability of key node and reduce the dependency among the nodes without compromising the overall network efficiency. This will become increasingly important as network densifies, i.e.
N
increases.

III-D

Secure-by-Design

Last but not least, the
secure-by-design
concept will be an important subset of the resilient-by-design approach. Networks that are secure-by-design prioritize security as a core design requirement, rather than considering it as a technical feature only. The original idea behind secure-by-design was to incorporate security at the design phase of the hardware and software components and ensure their resistivity to potential security vulnerabilities as much as possible. Similarly, security has to be considered as a key design principle in the design phase of the communication networks not only from the components of the cyber-physical systems, but also from many other perspectives including network topology, data management, and application scenarios. Moving beyond the conventional approaches, more complex future networks necessitate the secure-by-design principles to tackle many other aspects such as continuous risk assessment, security testing, sustainability and adaptability.

IV
Making 6G networks resilient-by-design

We envision four different steps in making future 6G networks resilient-by-design, as illustrated in Figure
5
and further elaborated below. Please note that, the enablers outlined here are by no means exhaustive in depth and breadth due to the limited scope of this article.

IV-A

Predicting failures and threats

Wireless networks are already becoming capable of autonomously self-configuring, self-optimizing and self-healing based on real-time conditions. Ensuring resiliency requires going beyond that to be able to continuously monitor, detect and predict failures, external disruptions and security threats. AI/ML tools can be applied for such prediction, albeit with some challenges in terms of scalability, generalizability and computational complexity. Fortunately, technological advances have made it feasible to accurately model the wireless environment, e.g., using a digital twin. Such models can be used to better track and predict the propagation environment. Moreover, RIS has opened the paradigm of engineer the propagation environment, thereby transforming the random wireless environment into a controllable channel that makes it easier to monitor and detect failures.

Though fading and interference variation are usually modeled as independent random variables, in practice they are correlated across time, space and frequency. A detailed modeling of the spatio-temporal correlation and its application in a Bayesian framework can also be utilized to better predict the future channel/interference conditions. Context-awareness by utilizing external or auxiliary information can further enhance the predictions accuracy. Examples include the use of weather forecast to predict adverse weather events, using information of planned large events to predict network traffic surge, or even the use of social media data to identify significant events in real time that will have an impact on the network. Incorporating the correlation information and context-awareness autonomously requires establishing the causal relationship between the events and their impact on the network, and among the users’ activities pattern. Furthermore, context-awareness can be also utilized for evaluating security threats, anticipating potential security risks, and identifying the required security level
[
15
]
.

IV-B

Preemption

Once a challenge is predicted, preemptive actions are required to address them. Examples of such preemptive actions at the link level include proactive resource allocation, centralized or distributed coordination schemes and redundancy measures. In the case of redundancy schemes, the inefficiency associated with it can be addressed by provisioning redundancy such that it is activated only when predicted to be needed. Different traffic flows with different SLAs can also be intelligently multiplexed to further enhance the efficiency. Predicted node failures on a larger scale can be addressed by proactive rerouting of the traffic. In this case, the correlation / dependency structure between the different possible routes, and causal relation with contextual information need to be considered to minimize the probability of the predicted failure impacting the rerouted path(s). For example, if a failure is expected due to weather disruption in a given area, the rerouted path should avoid that area.

On the security front, preemptive actions are needed to mitigate potential security breaches. Predictive analytics allows recognizing anomalous behavior, which can proactively identify and counter potential threats. This coupled with AI/ML tools can help to mitigate potential security risks by identifying network or data anomalies and defining optimum countermeasures. In addition to identifying known attacks as mentioned above, more sophisticated mechanisms are also required to predict, detect and mitigate unknown attacks or zero-day attacks which are becoming more common. As the conventional approach, 5G considers perimeter-based security where the network is enclosed within a trusted zone to prevent unauthorized access. However, with the open interfaces, disaggregation of hardware and software components, and the dynamic nature of complex 6G and beyond network architecture, it is impossible to clearly demarcate a network perimeter to define static trust zones and the security controls (i.e., countermeasures). Zero Trust Architecture and adaptive security models which require authentication, authorization and continuous validation for all devices can be applied under such circumstances.

IV-C

Protection

In the unlikely case that a failure or disruption or security breach occurs, immediate actions must be taken to address this disruption and minimize its impact on the network. The following are proposed:

Isolation
: future 6G networks will be a network of networks, with many special purpose sub-networks interconnected with each other. The overall network architecture must be agile enough to isolate and remove subnetworks where a breach or failure occurred to minimize the disruptions and safeguard the rest of the network from the detrimental impact of the failure. At the same time, critical network functionalities should be distributed and across the network with sufficient redundancy to ensure that removing the sub-network does not impede operation of the remaining network.

Staggered service level
: define different system states with different SLAs specified for each state. This will ensure service continuity even under failures or disruptions, albeit offering a different (often lower) service level.

Traffic engineering
: networks usually witness a surge in the network traffic in the event of a failure/ disruption as users try to access information, which further strains the network. Traffic engineering can be applied to proactively divert the traffic to the unaffected parts of the network or even block traffic from less critical applications based on the SLAs. Additionally, proactive broadcast with information update can also counter the traffic surge from information-seeking users.

Novel network architecture
: major disruptive events like earthquakes can affect the network architecture, making part of the network unavailable. Such challenges can be addressed by utilizing novel network architectures such a space, air and ground integrated networks (SAGIN) which integrates satellites, high altitude platform stations and unmanned aerial vehicles with the terrestrial network (TN). The integration of SAGIN with TNs can provide continuity in service or minimize the disruption in case of such failures, though the heterogeneity of the different networks is a major challenge.

IV-D

Progression

The final step of the resilient-by-design concept does not work in real-time but rather is a process that continuously works in the background. This step’s goal is to continuously upgrade and update the resilience mechanisms and the other three steps. For the
prediction
part, this involves updating the underlying model parameters or even adopting new models or tools based on new training data to increase the prediction accuracy. For example, incorporting continual learning incorporated or transfer learning can help towards achieving this in the context of AI/ML tools. Similarly, the probabilities of variables in Bayesian Networks establishing the causal relationship in the network should be updated as observations are added to the model. Continuous monitoring of KPIs of interest in the
pre-emption
and
protection
steps can help us to evaluate the effectiveness of the adopted preemptive strategies and compare among different options. Alongside, the cost in terms of resource and/or energy usage must also be monitored. This can help in selecting the strategies that meets the desired performance criteria while providing the optimum cost-performance tradeoff. For instance, joint security-QoS optimization and continuous adaptation of the security protocols can also be considered for fine-tuning security mechanisms to achieve the best performance without compromising protection. This includes balancing the load on network elements for security operations, ensuring efficient resource usage, and minimizing latency introduced by the security processes.

IV-E

Proof of concept results

In this section, we briefly present a set of selected results demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed resiliency framework. We consider a desired transmitter-receiver link is operating in the presence of multiple interferers. The goal is to predict the interference variation across time so that the transmitter can preemptively allocate resources to meet a given QoS target. The interference can be predicted by either modeling the interference variation as a discrete state space discrete-time Markov chain where the state transition probabilities are learned from past data or using supervised ML models such as long short-term memory and the transformer architecture. Further details of the implementation description are omitted due to space constraints.

Fig.
6
shows the achieved vs. target outage probability, along with the utilized resources for the proposed framework. The performance is compared against the theoretical upper bound where the future interference state is ideally known (marked as ‘Ideal’) and a conventional scheme based on averaging a window of past values (marked as ‘Average’). The results indicate that a proactive resource allocation scheme based on prediction can easily react to the disruptions in the wireless network to meet (or even exceed) the target outage while consuming only about
30\%
more resources compared to the ideal case. In comparison, the ‘average’ scheme is resource efficient but incapable of promptly reacting to network disruptions, as reflected in its dismal outage performance. On the other hand, traditional resiliency enablers such as diversity schemes requires allocation of redundant resources leading to inefficient utilization, e.g., even a single redundant link results in
100\%
additional resource allocation. This simple but illustrative preliminary simulation result indicates the potential advantages and efficiency of the resilient-by-design framework proposed in this article. Note that, we will investigate efficient algorithms that applies the propose framework more extensively in our future work.

V
Conclusions and Way Forward

This article discusses the important and emerging concept of resiliency in future 6G and beyond 6G wireless networks. Future networks must be made resilient-by-design to provide and maintain an acceptable level of service in the face of disruptions and challenges to normal operation. Such disruptions sources include challenges related to fading and interference stemming from the stochastic and shared nature of wireless propagation, disruptive events like extreme weather conditions and natural disasters, challenges in providing ubiquitous connectivity in remote and sparsely populated areas, and attacks by malicious actors. To address these challenges, we have proposed a novel four stage resilient-by-design framework in this in this article. We argue that a network should be able to
predict
disruptive events, make
preemptive
decisions to mitigate the disruption, and take action to
protect
and minimize its impact on the network. Alongside, the network must
progress
to update and upgrade its defense mechanisms by continuously learning from the current operating conditions.
