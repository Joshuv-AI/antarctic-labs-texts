Effects of three aerobic exercise protocols on acute glucose response and continuous glucose monitoring trends in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and stroke: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Kangcheng Chen, Haifeng Ma, Yulong Wang, Dongxia Li, Jun Li, Yong Huang, Meiling Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to assess the acute and chronic effects of three aerobic exercise protocols, namely, Moderate-Intensity Interval Training (MIIT), Low-to-Moderate Intensity Continuous Training (LMICT), and Reduced-Exertion High-Intensity Training (REHIT), on glycemic control in patients with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) and stroke.MethodsForty-nine patients diagnosed with both T2DM and stroke were randomly assigned to LMICT, MIIT, REHIT, or the control group. The intervention comprised two phases: from day 3 to day 14 and from day 15 to day 28, with days 1 and 2 designated as a baseline control period. Throughout the intervention, blood glucose levels were continuously monitored and recorded using a Continuous Glucose Monitoring (CGM) system.ResultsAll exercise intervention groups exhibited significant immediate reductions in blood glucose levels following exercise (t = 30.68, p < 0.001). Repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) demonstrated significant main effects of group and time, as well as a significant interaction, on mean glucose (MG) and time above range (TAR) (p < 0.05). CGM indicated progressive improvements in MG, time in range (TIR), TAR, peak blood glucose, glucose standard deviation (SD-glucose), and mean amplitude of glycemic excursion (MAGE) in the MIIT group. The REHIT group exhibited significant improvements in peak blood glucose, TIR, TAR, MAGE, SD-glucose, and coefficient of variation (CV) (all p < 0.01). These trends were not evident in the LMICT group. Notably, the MIIT and REHIT groups exhibited early, significant improvements in MG, peak blood glucose, TIR, and TAR, which preceded subsequent changes in SD-glucose and MAGE relative to controls.ConclusionsWhile all exercise regimens resulted in acute reductions in blood glucose, sustained improvements in overall glycemic control and variability were observed exclusively following the four-week MIIT and REHIT interventions. Specifically, REHIT significantly reduced glucose variability, as reflected by decreases in the CV, whereas MIIT was more effective in lowering MG levels. Conversely, the lower-intensity LMICT regimen (51.23% ± 6.94% heart rate reserve) exerted minimal long-term effects. These findings underscore the potential of moderate- to high-intensity intermittent aerobic training in managing glycemic fluctuations in individuals with T2DM and stroke, thereby emphasizing their clinical relevance.Trial registrationThe study was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2200065677, http://www.chictr.org.cn/) on 11/11/2022.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-025-01402-0.Keywords: Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Stroke, Aerobic exercise training, Continuous glucose monitoring, Glycemic variability

Introduction

In medicine and public health, Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) and stroke pose major global challenges. According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) 2021 estimates, approximately 141 million adults in China have diabetes [1]. A study involving 18,013 patients with T2DM reported a stroke incidence rate of 9.5% [2]. Epidemiological studies demonstrate that diabetes adversely influences the long-term prognosis of stroke survivors [3]. Recent meta-analyses indicate that individuals with diabetes have an approximately 50% higher risk of recurrent stroke [4]. Diabetes is a critical determinant of stroke prognosis in the general population [5], and effective glycemic control improves overall health and reduces the risk of subsequent cerebral injury [6].

Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) is a crucial biomarker for managing blood glucose in patients with T2DM. It reflects mean blood glucose levels over the prior two to three months. However, the association between HbA1c and stroke recurrence risk is relatively weak. The effectiveness of intensive glucose control—specifically, maintaining HbA1c levels at or below 7%—in preventing recurrent strokes during the acute phase in patients with diabetes has not been conclusively established [7]. This uncertainty may reflect that average glycemia relates consistently to microvascular complications, whereas associations between HbA1c and macrovascular outcomes are less direct [8]. HbA1c cannot capture glucose fluctuations or provide detailed insights into glucose dynamics. With advances in continuous glucose monitoring (CGM), current systems provide near-continuous interstitial glucose measurements, capturing hyperglycemia, hypoglycemia, and variability, and yielding standardized indices commonly used in diabetes management [9–11]. Studies report an association between time in range (TIR) and time below range (TBR) and the risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease in diabetes [10, 12]. Furthermore, prospective and retrospective cohort studies have identified a dose–response relationship between glucose variability and stroke risk, highlighting the clinical relevance of employing CGM-derived indices—such as mean glucose (MG), mean amplitude of glycemic excursions (MAGE), standard deviation of glucose (SD-glucose), and the coefficient of variation (CV)—for evaluating glycemic control in patients with stroke [13].

Aerobic exercise is a key strategy for secondary stroke prevention, being associated with reductions in cardiovascular risk and improvements in diabetes management [13–16]. Although previous studies have examined the effects of aerobic exercise on HbA1c, fasting blood glucose (FBG), and acute post-exercise glycemic variability, the evidence on the effects of longer-term interventions (≥ 2 weeks) on glycemic variability is limited [17–19]. Furthermore, owing to the lack of continuous blood glucose monitoring during the intervention period, it remains challenging to assess the long-term impact of exercise on key CGM-derived glucose control indices, including MG, TIR, and CV [20, 21].

The American Heart Association (AHA) and American Stroke Association (ASA) recommend aerobic training for stroke survivors 3–5 times per week, in 20–60-minute sessions at 40–70% of heart rate reserve (HRR) [22]. Within this framework, moderate-intensity interval training (MIIT) and Low-to-Moderate Intensity Continuous Training (LMICT) both meet the recommended duration target but differ in intensity distribution, which may elicit distinct metabolic adaptations [22–25]. In contrast, Reduced-Exertion High-Intensity Training (REHIT) is a time-efficient alternative—two to three 20–30-second all-out sprints within an ~ 10-minute session—reported to improve insulin sensitivity with brief total duration and modest perceived exertion and to lower blood glucose in non-stroke populations [26–29]. However, evidence in post-stroke populations remains limited.

Accordingly, we conducted a randomized controlled trial comparing moderate-intensity interval training (MIIT), low-to-moderate intensity continuous training (LMICT), REHIT, and a control group (CON) in these patients, assessing the acute single-session effects of the exercise protocols and 28-day changes in CGM-derived glycemic control metrics across all groups. Following international guidance, we prespecified MG, TIR, and CV as core CGM outcomes due to their established use and clinical interpretability [20, 21]. The primary aim was to compare 28-day trajectories of daily means for MG, TIR, and CV across groups. Secondary aims were to quantify the acute glycemic response to a single session in each exercise arm and to summarize intervention-period trends in additional indices—MAGE, SD-glucose, time above range (TAR), TBR, and daily peak and nadir glucose—to contextualize overall glycemic trajectories of glycemic change during the intervention. This study aims to inform aerobic exercise prescription for this population.

Methods and analysis

Participants and recruitment

This study included 49 patients with T2DM who had experienced a stroke and were hospitalized in the rehabilitation department of Shenzhen Second People’s Hospital, Guangdong Province, between October 2022 and February 2024. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, adhered to CONSORT guidelines, received approval from the Ethics Committee of Shenzhen Second People’s Hospital (approval number 20220901005-FS01), and was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2200065677). All patients or their authorized representatives provided written informed consent before enrollment. Participants were selected according to the criteria outlined in the Diagnostic Essentials of Cerebrovascular Diseases in China (2019), with diagnosis confirmed by imaging, and the Chinese Guidelines for Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (2020). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged between 18 and 80 years; (2) stroke occurrence within 15 days to 1 year prior to enrollment; (3) ability to walk independently for at least 10 m (with or without assistance); and (4) sufficient cognitive ability to participate in study activities. Patients were excluded based on the following criteria: (1) unstable vital signs; (2) progressive or acute stroke; (3) transient ischemic attack (TIA); (4) history of brain injury or other central nervous system disorders; (5) severe cardiovascular or pulmonary diseases; (6) severe hepatic or renal dysfunction; (7) significant musculoskeletal limitations; (8) untreated deep vein thrombosis (DVT); (9) severe diabetic complications; (10) other severe comorbidities; and (11) participation in other ongoing clinical trials.

Study design

This study is a prospective randomized controlled trial, and the rationale, study design, and methodological details have been described previously [30]. Building on the previously reported protocol, patient recruitment was continued, and daily glucose monitoring data were systematically recorded throughout the study duration, along with acute glucose responses measured immediately pre- and post-exercise sessions. Briefly, a total of 49 participants were randomly assigned to one of three aerobic exercise intervention groups or a control group, with each group undergoing a 28-day intervention period. Participants in the control group received routine care, including bed mobility training, joint mobility training, and balance exercises. Participants in the aerobic exercise intervention groups received routine care in combination with structured aerobic exercise training. The detailed study design is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Study overview

Sample size calculation

The calculation was based on the primary outcome, MG. MG is a core CGM endpoint that reflects overall glycemic exposure and is mechanistically linked to downstream non-enzymatic protein glycation (e.g., HbA1c) [20, 21, 31, 32]; among stroke survivors, diabetes and higher HbA1c are associated with poorer long-term outcomes, and secondary-prevention guidance emphasizes individualized glycemic control after stroke/TIA, supporting MG as a clinically meaningful endpoint in post-acute T2DM with prior stroke [7, 33–36]. For sample-size planning, we drew on Metcalfe et al. [37], who reported that REHIT significantly reduced MG in patients with T2DM compared with a non-exercise control group, with a Cohen’s d of 0.55. This study employed a repeated-measures design with three phases (baseline, Cycle 1, and Cycle 2). Following the standard conversion formula (f = d/2), the corresponding Cohen’s f was estimated at 0.275 [38]. For the present trial, we conservatively set the effect size in the medium range (f = 0.25). Parameters specified in G*Power 3.1.9.7 (Heinrich Heine Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany) were: repeated-measures ANOVA, within–between interaction, α = 0.05, power (1 – β) = 0.80, a 1:1:1:1 parallel allocation, assumed correlation among repeated measures = 0.5 and no violation of sphericity (ε = 1). This calculation indicated a required total of 40 participants (10 per group). Strategies will be implemented to minimize participant dropout, to account for an anticipated dropout rate of 10%, the adjusted target sample size was set at 45 participants (approximately 11 per group).

Randomization and blinding

Randomization was performed by simple randomization using computer-generated random numbers in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA). The allocation sequence was generated in advance, recorded on carbonless copy paper, and sealed in sequentially numbered, identical opaque envelopes to ensure allocation concealment. Because of the intervention design, blinding of participants and interventionists to group allocation was not feasible; however, outcome assessors and statisticians were blinded to group allocation.

Intervention

Exercise stress test

On the basis of the relevant literature and clinical practice, this study utilized an extremity cycle ergometer integrated with a cardiopulmonary exercise testing system (Masterscreen CPX; ERGOLINE GMBH, Germany) and a 5-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) to perform a symptom-limited maximal incremental cardiopulmonary exercise test [39–41]. Before testing, equipment calibration was performed in accordance with standard protocols, and participants were fitted with a 5-lead ECG, a gas analyzer mask, and a safety harness. Assistive devices were used as necessary to stabilize the affected limbs of participants, ensuring the accuracy and safety of the test.

The testing protocol was individualized according to the gender, age, height, weight, functional status, and exercise habits of each participant, with resistance increasing incrementally by one to two levels every two minutes. The detailed testing protocol comprised four phases: (1) Resting phase (1 min): recording resting heart rate and blood pressure; (2) Unloaded warm-up phase (3 min): participants performed familiarization exercises; (3) Incremental resistance exercise phase (8–12 min): resistance was increased progressively according to a predefined protocol until participants reached maximal exertion or termination criteria were met; (4) Recovery phase (6 min): consisting of three minutes of unloaded pedaling followed by three minutes of rest, during which heart rate and blood pressure recovery were monitored. Throughout the test, each resistance stage had a duration of two minutes, and participants maintained a pedaling cadence of 80 revolutions per minute (rpm) [40].

The criteria for test termination were as follows: (1) a plateau in oxygen consumption (VO₂) [42]; (2) heart rate exceeding 90% of the age-predicted maximum (adjusted to 85% for participants taking beta-blockers) [43]; (3) respiratory exchange ratio (RER) greater than 1.00 [44]; (4) Borg rating of perceived exertion (6–20 scale) greater than 17 [41]; and (5) participants reaching volitional fatigue and inability to maintain the required pedaling cadence.

Exercise intervention program

All exercise interventions were conducted under the supervision of qualified physiotherapists, who had completed the study-specific training, thereby ensuring the safety and effectiveness of the intervention. The experimental groups utilized a standardized four-limb coordinated rehabilitation training device. Based on the results of the exercise stress test, trained therapists adjusted the resistance levels and pedal speeds of the apparatus to accommodate each participant’s specific needs. Continuous heart rate monitoring was implemented to ensure that exercise intensity remained appropriate, with participants required to immediately report their perceived exertion levels after each session using the Borg RPE (6–20).

All exercise sessions were supervised by physiotherapists who had completed study-specific training. For participants requiring assistance, therapists provided hands-on support for mounting and dismounting, verified seating alignment, and stabilized the limbs throughout each session. The four-limb rehabilitation device employs bilaterally linked arm- and leg-cranks, allowing the unaffected limbs to assist the affected limbs to modulate force and maintain target cadence during high-intensity sprints. On the affected side, the hand was secured to the handlebar with elastic fixation straps, and lower-limb auxiliary supports were used to maintain the movement trajectory. Combined with the device’s guided trajectory and adjustable resistance, these procedures ensured safe completion of the REHIT protocol in participants with mobility limitations.

The exercise intervention commenced on day 3 and continued through day 28. To mitigate overtraining, the schedule incorporated two rest days after every five consecutive exercise days, resulting in a total of 20 sessions. Sessions were divided into two phases of 10 sessions each. Participants in the CON continued usual care and did not participate in any structured exercise intervention (Table 1).

Collection of treatment results

Upon admission, demographic and clinical data were collected, including participants’ age, sex, height, weight, duration of diabetes, time since stroke onset, lifestyle factors (smoking and alcohol use), current medication use, and medical history. Baseline data were obtained from fasting venous blood samples drawn from the antecubital vein. The primary laboratory measurements included fasting blood glucose (FBG), HbA1c, triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C). FBG was measured by the hexokinase method [45]. TC and TG were quantified with enzymatic colorimetric assays, HDL-C and LDL-C with homogeneous enzymatic assays [46], and HbA1c was assessed with a turbidimetric immunoassay [47]. All analyses were performed in duplicate to ensure analytical reliability.

Primary outcomes: CGM-based glycemic control

Sensors (GS1, SIBIONICS, Shenzhen, Guangdong, China) were placed on the non-dominant upper arm by trained nurses on study days 0 and 14. Each sensor recorded interstitial glucose at 5-min intervals for 14 consecutive days and transmitted data via Bluetooth to the hospital cloud server, thereby providing continuous coverage over the 28-day intervention.

To aid clinical interpretation and align with international guidance on CGM reporting for clinical care and trials, we prioritized MG (mean glycemic exposure), TIR (time in range), and CV (glycemic variability) as primary anchors, with additional indices reported for safety and context [20, 21].

(1) Mean glycemic exposure: MG is the arithmetic mean of all CGM values over 24 h, chosen as the primary anchor for its direct interpretability to patients and clinicians [20, 21].

(2) Time-in-range profile: TIR was defined as the percentage of time spent within 3.9–10.0 mmol/L and is commonly interpreted against a ≈ 70% target in many non-pregnant adults [20, 48]. TAR (%) was defined as the percentage of time ≥ 10.1 mmol/L, indexing hyperglycemic burden [48]. TBR was defined as the percentage of time ≤ 3.8 mmol/L, indexing hypoglycemic burden [48].

(3) Glycemic variability: CV, calculated as SD-glucose/MG, was prespecified as the primary variability endpoint; a threshold of ≤ 36% is widely recognised as a practical benchmark for stability in consensus statements [21, 48]. SD-glucose was used as an absolute descriptor of variability, whereas MAGE served as a supportive metric, capturing the amplitude of within-day excursions [21].

(4) Supplemental CGM indices: Daily peak and nadir blood glucose denote the highest and lowest CGM values within each 24 h.

Thresholds and targets follow international consensus reports and major guidelines for CGM interpretation in clinical studies [20, 21, 48].

Secondary outcomes

The secondary outcomes focused on the acute glycemic response to exercise, assessed using CGM-derived glucose concentrations. Glucose values were extracted during the 5-minute period immediately preceding the start of each exercise session and the 5-minute period immediately following its completion. These measurements were obtained from all three experimental groups throughout the intervention period.

Medication therapy and dietary plan

All participants adhered to the endocrinologists’ recommendations regarding the use of antidiabetic medications. Throughout the entire study period, participants were instructed to maintain their baseline antidiabetic medication regimens, ensuring consistent daily timing for the administration of oral medications or insulin [49].

Dietary intake was accurately documented during the two days preceding the intervention (defined as baseline days), including daily caloric intake and macronutrient distribution (carbohydrates, proteins, and fats) for each meal. This measure provided a dietary baseline for continuous nutritional monitoring throughout the study. During the subsequent 26-day intervention period, participants maintained consistent dietary patterns under the direct supervision and professional guidance of registered dietitians. To ensure maximum dietary consistency and strict control of dietary factors influencing glycemic control, registered dietitians carefully documented and supervised the composition and quantity of food consumed by participants at each meal.

Potential adverse events in the exercise intervention

Adverse events (AEs) are any untoward medical occurrences after aerobic exercise rehabilitation, regardless of a causal relationship to the intervention [50, 51]. serious adverse events (SAEs) are those that require hospitalization or prolong an existing hospitalization, result in disability, impair work capacity, are life-threatening, or lead to death or congenital anomaly/birth defect [50, 51]. AEs were graded by severity as mild (tolerable, with no meaningful impact on treatment or recovery), moderate (difficult to tolerate, requiring specific treatment and adversely affecting recovery), and severe (life-threatening, causing death or disability, and requiring immediate intervention) [52]. Expected AEs included fatigue, abdominal discomfort, muscle cramps, soreness, stiffness, and swelling [53, 54], whereas unexpected AEs were those not anticipated in the protocol (e.g., life-threatening events or death) [50]. Serious AEs included bradycardia, sudden death, myocardial infarction, heart failure, hypoglycemia, ketoacidosis, and musculoskeletal injuries [53–55]. Risk-management procedures included allowing rest periods of up to 2 min as needed; stopping exercise upon any AE; monitoring the participant; and administering supplemental oxygen when indicated. Serious events were reported within 24 h to the sponsor, ethics committee, and relevant regulatory authorities, and participants received follow-up care until the condition resolved or stabilized [51, 56].

Statistics

Data were analyzed using SPSS software (version 23.0; IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were expressed as percentages, while continuous variables were presented as means ± standard deviations (mean ± SD). Differences in baseline characteristics between study groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Levene’s test was initially performed to assess the homogeneity of variance; if this assumption was violated, Welch’s ANOVA was applied. Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables when the expected frequency was less than five. The effects of time periods and exercise protocols on CGM indices were evaluated using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA. When the sphericity assumption was violated, a Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied. Additionally, generalized estimating equations (GEE) were employed to analyze daily mean values of CGM indices across study groups. Subject ID was treated as a between-subjects factor, and intervention date as a within-subjects factor, with an unstructured covariance matrix employed to model the correlation structure within the data. Bonferroni correction and polynomial trend analysis were performed to further evaluate between-group differences in CGM indices throughout the intervention period, as well as linear trends within each group.

Furthermore, a general linear model (GLM) was used to assess acute changes in blood glucose concentrations among different exercise protocols. The CGM blood glucose measurements obtained within the 5-minute period immediately preceding each intervention were used as covariates, and corresponding post-intervention measurements obtained within the first 5 min were treated as the dependent variable to control for the influence of baseline glucose levels. Model parameters were estimated using the least squares method, and adjusted within-group effects (least squares means) were calculated. Post hoc multiple comparisons using least squares mean differences were conducted to compare effects among study groups; adjusted means, standard errors, and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were reported for each group. The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Results

In total, 119 individuals volunteered for participation in this study. After screening for predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 70 participants were excluded due to factors including restricted lower-extremity passive joint range of motion, severe comorbid conditions, or an inability to fully commit to the study requirements. Consequently, 49 eligible participants (mean age, 59.06 ± 10.44 years) were enrolled and randomly allocated to four groups: CON (n = 12), LMICT (n = 11), MIIT (n = 12), and REHIT (n = 14). During the study, four participants withdrew: one from the CON group, one from the MIIT group, and two from the REHIT group. Additionally, one participant from the LMICT group was excluded due to fever symptoms that affected adherence to the training protocol.

To ensure compliance with the prescribed dietary and exercise protocols, rigorous professional supervision was implemented throughout the study period. While some mild, expected exercise-related adverse events, such as fatigue and muscle soreness, were observed during the intervention period, no serious adverse events were reported. Overall, the intervention was well-tolerated. Except for participants who withdrew mid-study, all remaining participants successfully completed the prescribed training, achieving a completion rate of 90%.

Exercise intensity monitoring results

Table 3 summarizes the exercise intensity monitoring data and RPE recorded immediately after each exercise session for each experimental group. Participants in the LMICT group attained an average peak intensity of 51.23% ± 6.94% HRR and reported an average post-exercise RPE score of 12.65 ± 1.36. In the MIIT group, the average peak intensity achieved was 69.75% ± 5.29% HRR, with a corresponding post-exercise RPE of 13.56 ± 1.97. These observed intensities slightly deviated from the predefined target ranges, possibly due to factors such as individual physiological variability, adaptations to training, psychological factors, or potential measurement errors. The REHIT group achieved an average peak intensity of 78.19% ± 5.77% HRR, with a post-exercise RPE of 14.28 ± 1.78. Collectively, these findings confirm successful implementation of the exercise interventions, in accordance with the intended experimental protocols.

Discussion

This study evaluated acute glucose changes from pre- to post-exercise and the trajectories of CGM metrics during the intervention to assess the effects of LMICT, MIIT, and REHIT on glycemic control in patients with T2DM and stroke. At the acute level, pre- to post-exercise glucose decreased significantly for all three protocols, with the largest reduction in MIIT. Across the prespecified anchors, MIIT demonstrated consistent improvements in MG and TIR, whereas REHIT exhibited selective benefits in CV and several supportive indices; LMICT showed limited or delayed changes. Notably, none of the interventions increased TBR, supporting a favorable hypoglycemia risk profile during supervised aerobic exercise in this population.

Acute glycemic responses pre- and post-exercise across LMICT, MIIT, and REHIT

Long-term improvements in glycemic control are hypothesized to reflect cumulative effects of repeated, post-exercise reductions in glucose concentration [56]. Numerous studies have evaluated acute blood-glucose responses to aerobic exercise in individuals with diabetes and generally concur that moderate-intensity exercise reduces glucose levels [57, 58]. However, findings concerning acute glucose responses to high-intensity exercise are inconsistent. Some studies report greater acute glucose reductions after high-intensity versus moderate-intensity exercise [57, 59]. whereas other studies have observed significant acute increases in glucose after high-intensity exercise [60]. Furthermore, some evidence suggests that acute glucose reductions may depend more on total exercise volume than on intensity [61, 62].

In this study, we investigated acute glucose responses to three aerobic exercise protocols—LMICT, MIIT, and REHIT—in patients with T2DM and stroke. All three protocols were associated with significant acute reductions in blood glucose (all p < 0.001). After adjusting for baseline blood glucose, MIIT showed the largest reduction (− 1.00 mmol/L; 95% CI, − 1.07 to − 0.93), followed by LMICT (− 0.79 mmol/L; 95% CI, − 0.87 to − 0.72) and REHIT (− 0.64 mmol/L; 95% CI, − 0.71 to − 0.58). Although total energy expenditure per session was not measured, MIIT—performed at a higher intensity (69.75% ± 5.29% HRR) than LMICT (51.23% ± 6.94% HRR) over the same 30-minute duration—was associated with greater acute reductions in glucose. Despite evidence that a 10-minute REHIT session can deplete glycogen by ~ 20% [63]. our findings indicate a smaller acute glucose-lowering effect after REHIT. One possible explanation is increased oxidative stress associated with higher-intensity exercise [64]. Additionally, brief, intense aerobic exercise may elevate plasma catecholamines, increasing hepatic glucose production for 1–2 h post-exercise [65], which may limit immediate post-exercise glucose reductions after REHIT.

Overall, MIIT exhibited a more pronounced acute glucose-lowering effect compared with LMICT and REHIT among patients with T2DM and stroke.

MG trends during the intervention across LMICT, MIIT, and REHIT

MG correlates closely with HbA1c [9]; however, owing to interindividual variability in hemoglobin glycation and other physiological factors [9, 66], MG is more sensitive than HbA1c for detecting short-term intervention effects In this study, repeated-measures ANOVA for MG showed a significant effect of time (F = 5.956, p = 0.010) and a significant group-by-time interaction (F = 3.743, p = 0.009), indicating that training modality influenced 28-day trajectories. Trend analysis demonstrated a significant linear decline in MG in the MIIT group only (p < 0.001). Consistent with this trajectory, the MIIT group showed a Day 28 minus Day 1 change of − 1.90 mmol/L (95% CI, − 3.93 to 0.14). Using the A1c-Derived Average Glucose (ADAG) equation, the observed reduction in mean glucose corresponds to approximately − 34 mg/dL (≈ − 1.2% in HbA1c) if sustained [67], a magnitude that is potentially clinically meaningful.

Consistent with previous research, HbA1c levels are influenced not only by exercise duration (with weekly reductions of ~ 0.009%–0.043% as training continues [68]) but also by exercise intensity and modality (interval versus continuous). For example, a study comparing 40 min of continuous moderate exercise (60% peak oxygen uptake [VO₂peak]) with 40 min of interval training (four-minute bouts at 50% VO₂peak interspersed with one-minute bouts at 80% VO₂peak) found significant HbA1c reductions only in the interval group [69]. Although multiple mechanisms are implicated, MG is widely regarded as the principal determinant of hemoglobin and protein glycation and a key contributor to the pathogenesis of chronic diabetic complications [31, 32].

Our findings indicate that MIIT, when performed regularly at adequate intensity and duration, is associated with clinically meaningful improvements in glycemic control in patients with T2DM and stroke.

Time-in-range profile (TIR/TAR/TBR) trends during the intervention across LMICT, MIIT, and REHIT

At the 2019 Advanced Technologies & Treatments for Diabetes (ATTD) conference, experts recommended that interpretation of CGM data include assessment of time within the target range (TIR), above it (TAR), and below it (TBR) to evaluate glycemic control.

Prior research demonstrates that TIR is strongly associated with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality; each 10% decrement in TIR is associated with 8% and 5% higher risks, respectively [12]. Additionally, TIR is significantly associated with peripheral nerve function in individuals with T2DM, and this association persists after adjustment for HbA1c. Higher TIR is independently associated with better peripheral nerve function; a 10% decrease in TIR corresponds to a 25% higher risk of developing peripheral neuropathy [70]. Diabetic peripheral neuropathy is common, affecting ~ 50% of individuals with diabetes and substantially impairing quality of life [71]. Symptoms such as pain and dysesthesia may complicate post-stroke rehabilitation. Systematic reviews indicate that physical activity interventions significantly increase TIR in individuals with T2DM, with a pooled mean improvement of 4.21% (95% CI, 0.95 to 7.46; p < 0.01) [72]. In this study, repeated-measures ANOVA for TIR showed a significant main effect of time (F = 13.762, p < 0.001) and a significant group-by-time interaction (F = 3.934, p = 0.005), underscoring the importance of intervention duration for TIR management. Polynomial trend analyses demonstrated significant linear improvements in TIR with MIIT (p = 0.004) and REHIT (p < 0.001). Consistent with these trajectories, the Day 28 minus Day 1 change in TIR was + 20.60% in MIIT (95% CI, − 7.30 to 48.50) and + 12.76% in REHIT (95% CI, 4.76 to 20.76).

TAR is a key metric for assessing hyperglycemic burden. Sustained hyperglycemia causes cellular injury and is strongly associated with diabetes-related complications [73]. In patients with stroke, hyperglycemia is associated with increased mortality, disease progression, larger infarct volumes, and impaired neurological recovery, including among individuals without diabetes [74–77]. In our study, TAR decreased significantly over time in MIIT (p = 0.004) and REHIT (p = 0.007). Consistent with these trajectories, the Day 28 minus Day 1 change in TAR was − 21.06% in MIIT (95% CI, − 48.33 to 6.20) and − 8.26% in REHIT (95% CI, − 18.43 to 1.91). These changes are concordant with the observed improvements in TIR and the expected inverse relationship between TIR and TAR, whereby increases in time in range are accompanied by reductions in hyperglycemic burden [78].

Inappropriate exercise prescriptions may precipitate hypoglycemia. Autonomic neuropathy in diabetes may blunt counterregulatory responses, increasing the risk of severe hypoglycemic events [79]. For patients with T2DM and stroke, preventing hypoglycemia is critical given its associations with cardiovascular events, brain injury, and retinopathy, as well as its putative role in accelerating atherosclerosis [80–83]. Hypoglycemia also disrupts cerebral energy metabolism and may exacerbate neuronal injury [84]. Although prior work suggests that exercise may alter autonomic and metabolic responses, increasing hypoglycemia risk hours post-exercise [85], our data do not indicate an increased low-glucose burden with supervised training. TBR showed no significant between-group differences (F = 0.748, p = 0.530), a significant main effect of time (F = 5.912, p = 0.007), and no group-by-time interaction (F = 0.519, p = 0.757). From Day 1 to Day 28, TBR increased by 2.45% in LMICT (95% CI, − 9.70 to 14.60) and by 0.46% in MIIT (95% CI, − 3.28 to 4.21), whereas it decreased by 4.50% in REHIT (95% CI, − 13.38 to 4.38). Changes were modest, and the 95% confidence intervals spanned zero in all groups; considered alongside stable nadir glucose (no significant group effect [F = 1.808, p = 0.161] and no significant group-by-time interaction [F = 0.519, p = 0.757]), this pattern indicates that LMICT, MIIT, and REHIT did not increase hypoglycemia risk over 28 days.

In conclusion, this study shows that MIIT and REHIT are associated with significant reductions in TAR and increases TIR. In contrast, LMICT showed limited improvements in TIR and TAR. Moreover, under supervised conditions, LMICT, MIIT, and REHIT did not increase TBR in patients with T2DM and stroke, supporting the safety of these aerobic exercise prescriptions for glycemic management.

Glycemic variability (CV/SD-glucose/MAGE) trends during the intervention across LMICT, MIIT, and REHIT

Greater glycemic variability is associated with inflammatory responses and oxidative stress, activating multiple pathophysiological pathways, including the accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), increased generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and upregulation of cognate receptor pathways, thereby exacerbating inflammatory and oxidative injury [86, 87]. In patients with stroke, heightened glycemic variability may be particularly detrimental, owing to impaired homeostatic regulation that increases susceptibility to thrombosis and plaque instability [88, 89].

Prior studies have shown that acute exercise modulates daily blood-glucose levels, glycemic responses, and variability [19]. However, the long-term effects of regular exercise on glycemic variability are less well documented. This study found no significant between-group differences in MAGE and SD-glucose (MAGE: F = 0.469, p = 0.705; SD-glucose: F = 0.843, p = 0.478). Nonetheless, there were significant main effects of time (MAGE: F = 13.716, p < 0.001; SD-glucose: F = 19.803, p < 0.001) and significant group × time interactions (MAGE: F = 2.913, p = 0.025; SD-glucose: F = 4.813, p = 0.001). Further analysis showed significant decreases in daily mean SD-glucose and MAGE in the MIIT and REHIT groups as the study progressed, with no comparable trends in LMICT or CON. For CV—the prespecified variability anchor—the main effect of group was not significant (F = 0.120, p = 0.948), the main effect of time was significant (F = 12.446, p < 0.001), and the group-by-time interaction was not significant (F = 1.329, p = 0.125). Only REHIT exhibited a significant linear decline over the intervention (p = 0.001). From Day 1 to Day 28, absolute changes in CV were − 6.45% in REHIT (95% CI, − 11.31 to − 1.59), − 4.51% in MIIT (95% CI, − 15.94 to 6.93), and − 1.73% in LMICT (95% CI, − 9.06 to 5.60). Only the REHIT 95% CI excluded zero. Taken together, these findings indicate that MIIT and REHIT improve (i.e., reduce) glycemic variability, with intervention period duration and exercise intensity being critical factors. External studies likewise report that high-intensity or intermittent (interval-based) exercise formats are particularly effective for improving glycemic variability [90, 91], consistent with broader research showing that exercise intensity correlates more strongly with improved glycemic control than exercise volume [17, 92].

In summary, our findings indicate that MIIT and REHIT are associated with reductions in glycemic variability in patients with T2DM and stroke, with REHIT showing additional improvements in CV. Conversely, LMICT showed comparatively limited improvements in glycemic variability. These findings underscore the need to prioritize appropriate exercise intensity in aerobic exercise prescriptions for glycemic control in clinical practice.

Timing of significant differences in CGM outcomes versus CON during the intervention

A recent meta-analysis demonstrated that short-term exercise interventions (less than two weeks) significantly reduce MG by approximately 0.5 mmol/L (95% CI, −0.7 to −0.3; p < 0.001), shorten the duration of hyperglycemia, and enhance the MAGE compared to control conditions [18]. However, a separate 2024 study observed improvements in glucose variability indicators, including significant reductions in SD-glucose (from 1.35 mmol/L to 1.10 mmol/L, p = 0.006) and CV (from 20.25% to 17.20%, p = 0.027), but contended that achieving substantial reductions in MG necessitated greater exercise volume or extended intervention periods [93]. Discrepancies between studies may be attributed to variations in glycemic management on non-exercise days, differences in exercise intensity and duration, and participant demographics such as gender.

This study’s findings reveal that during the intervention, participants in the MIIT and REHIT groups exhibited significant improvements in MG, Peak Blood Glucose, TIR, and TAR earlier than those in the CON group, thus differing from changes observed in the SD-glucose and MAGE. This contrasts with research from 2024, which indicated that significant reductions in MG might necessitate more prolonged exercise compared to adjustments in CV and SD-glucose [18]. Furthermore, recent studies demonstrate that reducing MG from 12 mmol/L to 8 mmol/L—a 33% decrease—is associated with a 20% reduction in SD-glucose, reflecting a significant improvement within the 95% confidence interval of 14.9 to 24.9% [94]. The metrics MG, TIR, and TAR are closely linked to hyperglycemic states, evidenced by a high correlation coefficient of 0.90 [95], suggesting that improvements in TIR directly result in reductions in TAR [78]. Although MAGE offers insights into hypoglycemia during fasting states [96], both SD-glucose and MAGE generally reflect elevated glucose conditions more accurately [97].

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that MIIT and REHIT can significantly mitigate glucose fluctuations. This is achieved by lowering average glucose concentrations, extending the periods during which glucose levels remain within the targeted range, and reducing the duration of high glucose episodes. Consequently, these interventions constitute effective strategies for enhancing overall glucose management in individuals with diabetes.

Limitations of the study

Although numerous studies have examined the effects of exercise on glycemic variability in individuals with T2DM, systematic analyses of longer-term interventions (≥ 2 weeks) remain scarce [17–19]. This study provides novel insights into the effects of LMICT, MIIT, and REHIT on acute glycemic responses and longer-term (up to 4 weeks) indices of glycemic variability in patients with T2DM and stroke. Notably, this investigation characterized daily trajectories of glycemic parameters across the intervention, closely monitored medication use and dietary intake, and systematically documented session intensity and duration. However, several limitations warrant caution in interpretation and motivate further study.

First, the relatively small sample size and limited population diversity (e.g., restricted ranges of age, sex distribution, disease duration, and diabetes control) may constrain external validity and generalizability. In addition, blinding of participants and exercise instructors was not feasible, which may have introduced performance bias; however, allocation was concealed and outcome assessors were blinded.

Second, the absence of long-term follow-up precludes assessment of the durability of exercise-induced improvements. Moreover, session-level energy expenditure was not quantified directly; therefore, between-group differences may partially reflect unequal energy dose rather than intensity. Although heart-rate reserve and a fixed session duration were recorded, these proxies do not ensure isocaloric comparability across protocols.

Lastly, although REHIT showed a potential advantage on glycemic-variability indices (e.g., CV), the underlying physiological and molecular mechanisms were not interrogated. Future studies should incorporate multi-omics approaches (e.g., metabolomics, genomics) to clarify the biological pathways involved.

In summary, despite these limitations, this study provides preliminary support for individualized aerobic-exercise strategies for patients with T2DM and stroke. Addressing these limitations—through larger samples, extended follow-up, direct quantification of energy expenditure, and strategies to minimize performance bias—will be essential to strengthen future research.

Conclusions

Although all exercise protocols produced acute pre- to post-exercise reductions in blood glucose, sustained improvements in overall glycemic control and variability were observed exclusively following the four-week MIIT and REHIT interventions. Specifically, REHIT significantly reduced glycemic variability, as evidenced by a linear decline in the CV over the intervention, with a 6.45% decrease from Day 1 to Day 28 (95% CI, − 11.31 to − 1.59), whereas MIIT was more effective in lowering MG. A linear decline in MG was observed only with MIIT (Day 28 vs. Day 1: −1.90 mmol/L; 95% CI, − 3.93 to 0.14). In contrast, the lower-intensity LMICT regimen (51.23% ± 6.94% HRR) exhibited limited or delayed changes. TBR did not increase in any group, supporting the safety of supervised aerobic exercise in this cohort. These findings support the use of moderate- to high-intensity intermittent aerobic training as a clinically relevant option for goal-directed exercise prescription in this population.
