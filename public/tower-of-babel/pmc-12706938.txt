Assessing health-related quality of life in adolescence: exploring the validity of the Spanish version of the KIDSCREEN-10
Authors: Alicia Pérez-Albéniz, Beatriz Lucas-Molina, Sonia Franco-Torres, Alfonso Pérez-Esteban, Beatriz A Ciulin, Adriana Díez-Gómez, Eduardo Fonseca-Pedrero
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe KIDSCREEN is a transcultural and standardized instrument designed to assess health-related quality of life (HRQoL) in children and adolescents. Its shorter version, the KIDSCREEN-10 Index, has shown appropriate psychometric properties in various languages, but evidence supporting its validity and reliability in the Spanish version remains limited. The aim of the present study was to provide additional psychometric properties of the KIDSCREEN-10 Index in a representative sample of Spanish adolescents.MethodsA stratified cluster random sampling method was used to select 2,235 students aged 12 to 18 years from schools in La Rioja, Spain, during 2022. Confirmatory factor analysis was conducted to examine the dimensional structure of the scores, and measurement invariance was tested across sex, age, socioeconomic status, sexual minority status, and nationality. Internal consistency was evaluated using McDonald’s omega and Cronbach’s alpha coefficients. Moreover, associations with emotional wellbeing and emotional and behavioural difficulties were examined.ResultsConfirmatory factor analysis revealed adequate goodness-of-fit indices for the unidimensional structure of the scores. Furthermore, measurement invariance was found across sex, age, socioeconomic status, nationality, and whether or not participants belonged to sexual minorities, allowing for the analysis of differences between subgroups. Females, older participants, those with lower socioeconomic status, foreign nationals, and participants belonging to sexual minorities scored significantly lower than their respective comparison groups. Moreover, a three-way interaction between sex, age, and sexual minority status was found indicating possible intersectional effects. High internal consistency of the KIDSCREEN-10 Index scores was also observed (Ω = 0.874; α = 0.872). A significant positive correlation was found between the KIDSCREEN-10 Index score and emotional wellbeing, while a significant negative correlation was observed with emotional and behavioural difficulties.ConclusionsThe Spanish version of the KIDSCREEN-10 Index showed adequate psychometric properties, supporting its potential as a reliable and valid tool to measure the HRQoL in adolescents.Keywords: Assessment, Health-related quality of life, HRQoL, Children and adolescents, KIDSCREEN

Background

In recent years, there has been a growing interest in incorporating health outcome indicators that go beyond purely somatic measures [1]. This shift aims to address the need for indicators that assess the overall health status of populations or analyze the effectiveness of interventions from the patient’s perspective, such as health-related quality of life (HRQoL). Notably, HRQoL has become the most extensively studied and prioritized perceived health outcome variable in recent decades [1]– [2].

However, there is no clear consensus on the definition of HRQoL, and consequently, on the indicators required for its assessment [3]. Related terms such as quality of life, subjective well-being, and health status are often used interchangeably, despite their distinct conceptual [4]. According to the World Health Organization [5], which emphasizes the importance of considering all health-related outcomes, HRQoL refers to an individual’s subjective evaluation of their health status and its functional impact on their overall well-being, across multiple life domains, including physical health, psychological state, and social relationships [6]. Tudela [7] further developed this definition by highlighting that HRQoL focuses on the subjective influences of health status, healthcare services, and health promotion and prevention activities on an individual’s ability to achieve and maintain a level of functioning that supports the pursuit of life goals and is reflected in overall well-being. This perspective aligns with the WHO in identifying physical, psychological-cognitive, and social functioning as the core dimensions. In any case, HRQoL is a multidimensional construct that captures the impact of health status on various aspects of human functioning [2, 8]. This focus distinguishes HRQoL from broader notions of quality of life, which encompass additional domains such as environment and material well-being, from subjective well-being, which emphasizes individuals’ affective and cognitive evaluations of life, or from health status, which primarily refers to the presence or absence of disease or impairment [2, 3, 6].

Among the existing tools for assessing HRQoL in children and adolescents, two types can be distinguished: specific and generic measures (see Solans et al. [9] for a review). Specific measuring instruments are designed to assess the impact of particular health conditions on HRQoL, incorporating both the most affected dimensions and disease-specific symptoms (e.g., asthma, kidney disease). In contrast, generic instruments are intended for general or unspecific use, regardless of the health issue. These tools can be applied to healthy populations and are suitable for general population assessments to explore how children and adolescents perceive their health and to identify groups at social risk, such as migrant minors or those belonging to other minority populations [9].

A range of generic instruments is currently available for assessing HRQoL in children and adolescents. Many of these instruments rely on self-reports, while versions completed by close family members or caregivers also exist. Internationally recognized instruments include the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL-generic) [10], the Child Health and Illness Profile (CHIP) [11]– [12], for the child and adolescent versions, respectively], and the Child Health Questionnaire (CHQ) [13]. Several of these generic instruments have been translated into Spanish. However, a common criticism of these measurement tools [14] is that most were originally developed in Anglo-Saxon contexts and subsequently adapted into other languages and cultures, often without sufficient consideration for cultural equivalence between versions.

To address this limitation, thirteen European countries, including Spain, participated in a project funded by the European Union [15]– [16], aimed at developing a standardized instrument to assess quality of life from the child’s perspective in terms of physical, mental, and social well-being. The project yielded an instrument with multiple versions (varying by language, number of items − 52/27/10-, and type of respondent, including self-reports and proxy reports completed by parents or caregivers), which has gained broad acceptance in research and is recommended as a generic tool for assessing HRQoL [4, 17].

With regard to the Spanish version of the instrument, the team led by Luis Rajmil has been at the forefront of the work in Spain. An initial pilot study [18] showed that it had acceptable psychometric properties. This study also concluded with the proposal of a definitive measure composed of 52 items and 10 dimensions (Physical well-being, Psychological well-being, Mood and emotions, Self-perception, Autonomy, Relationship with parents and family life, Social support and peer relationships, School environment, Social acceptance and Economic resources). Subsequently, Tebe et al. [19] conducted a study with the 52-item version in a representative sample of Spanish children and adolescents, finding adequate levels of reliability and validated the underlying structure of the 10-dimensional model, as well as the negative relationship between its scores and the presence of health-related difficulties. In 2014, Rajmil’s team also examined [20] the psychometric properties of the Spanish and Catalan versions of the instrument, using both online and paper-based response formats. The results confirmed adequate levels of internal consistency and provided evidence of validity through associations with other variables, such as the relationship between KIDSCREEN scores and the risk of emotional and behavioral difficulties.

Subsequently, with the aim of developing shorter versions, a 27-item version comprising five dimensions was created, which also demonstrated adequate psychometric properties [21]– [22]. Furthermore, the authors recognized the value of a more concise measure for use in clinical and public health studies, which could also provide a single summary score to capture overall changes in HRQoL. Consequently, the KIDSCREEN-10 Index was developed [23]. This index, specifically designed for use in large epidemiological studies, comprises items derived from the 27-item version and, in its Spanish adaptation, has shown good internal consistency, acceptable test-retest reliability, and the ability to differentiate between groups based on gender, socioeconomic status, age, behavioral problems, and psychosomatic complaints [23–25]. Research findings indicate that the KIDSCREEN-10 Index covers five of the ten KIDSCREEN-52 dimensions, providing a valid measure of a general HRQoL factor in children and adolescents, which appears to be more strongly defined by aspects related to mental rather than physical health [23].

Despite the evidence presented, and as acknowledged by the authors themselves [25], no explicit study has examined the unidimensionality of the KIDSCREEN-10. To the best of our knowledge, this issue has not been addressed in subsequent research, either at the European level or specifically in studies using the Spanish version. However, evidence supporting a unidimensional structure of the KIDSCREEN-10 has been reported for the Chinese, Portuguese, and Iranian versions [26–28].

On the other hand, no evidence has been provided regarding the measurement invariance of the Spanish version across variables such as gender, age, socioeconomic status, or nationality —information that is essential for making valid group comparisons, which have indeed been frequently reported using this instrument. Previous research has shown differences based on gender [e.g., 23–25, 29] age [e.g., 30–32] and socioeconomic status [e.g., 26, 33]. With respect to nationality, while some authors have expressed interest in examining this variable [e.g., 27, 34] none have conducted group comparisons. Furthermore, the issue of measurement invariance has received recent attention in the context of sexual minority status, with studies highlighting the scarcity of research in this area [35–37]. To ensure fair and comparable assessment across groups, items should represent a predominantly unidimensional construct and function equivalently across subgroups (i.e., exhibit measurement invariance). However, despite the growing interest in the mental health and psychological well-being of sexual and gender minority groups, few studies have examined whether the instruments used to measure these constructs are invariant.

The KIDSCREEN-10 Index is widely used in our context and has contributed to both national and European public health policies. At the national level, it was first included in the Spanish National Health Survey in 2006 to assess the population aged 8 to 15 years. At the international level, it was introduced as an optional module within the Health Behaviour in School-aged Children (HBSC) study during the 2005/2006 academic year, a WHO-supported project that currently involves nearly 50 Western countries. The proxy version of the instrument was subsequently introduced in the 2008 Flash Eurobarometer as an indicator for child and adolescent mental health issues. The KIDSCREEN-10 Index is endorsed by the International Consortium for Health Outcomes Measurement (ICHOM) as part of its standard outcome measurement set for anxiety, depression, obsessive-compulsive disorders, and post-traumatic stress disorder in children and adolescents [38].

Given the growing interest in and frequent use of this index to assess HRQoL in children and adolescents, the primary aim of this study was to examine the psychometric properties of the Spanish version of the KIDSCREEN-10 Index in a representative sample of adolescents. The specific objectives were as follows: (a) to provide evidence of the internal structure of the KIDSCREEN-10 Index; (b) to assess the measurement invariance (MI) of the instrument across sex, age, socioeconomic status, sexual minority status, and nationality; (c) to analyze potential statistically significant differences based on these sociodemographic variables; (d) to estimate the reliability of the scores; and (e) to examine the validity of the KIDSCREEN-10 Index through its association with other indicators of mental health and well-being.

Methods

Participants

The sample was selected through stratified random cluster sampling (by geographic area and educational level), with clusters defined at the classroom level, from the population of students aged 12 to 18 years (approximately 15,000 students) in La Rioja. A total of 2,640 students from 34 public (45%) and charter (55%) secondary schools and vocational training institutions, representing various socioeconomic levels, participated in the study. Participants (n = 175) who showed signs of having responded inattentively or randomly (assessed using the Oviedo Response Infrequency Scale-Revised [39]), or those with over 18 years of age (n = 247), were removed from the sample, which was finally composed of 2,235 students. Of the total, 1,045 were male (46.8%), 1,183 were female (52.9%), and 7 were identified as intersex (0.3%). The mean age of the participants was 14.49 years (SD = 1.76). According to the WHO classification of stages of adolescence, the sample was divided into two stages for the analysis of differences according to age: early adolescence from 12 to 14 years (n = 1,063; 47.6%), and late adolescence from 15 to 18 years (n = 1,172; 52.4%). Regarding the sexual orientation of the participants, 1,749 (78.3%) reported experiencing exclusively heterosexual sexual attraction, while others reported experiencing sexual attraction to people of the same gender (lesbians/gays; n = 37; 1.7%), to both genders (bisexuals; n = 326; 14.6%), being asexual (n = 57; 2.6%), or questioning (n = 66; 3%). Finally, 2,024 participants (90.6%) had Spanish nationality, and 211 (9.4%) had another nationality.

Instruments

The KIDSCREEN-10 index of health-related quality of life [23, 24]

The self-report is comprised of 10 items. Items 1 and 2 assess the child’s or adolescent’s level of physical activity. Items 3 and 4 refer to the extent to which they experience depressive moods and stressful feelings. Items 5 and 6 address their opportunities to structure and enjoy their social and leisure time, as well as their participation in social activities. Item 7 explores the quality of interaction with their family. Item 8 explores the nature of their relationships with peers. Finally, items 9 and 10 assess the respondents’ perceived cognitive ability and satisfaction with their academic performance. Responses are rated on a 5-point Likert-type scale, which assesses either the frequency (never, almost never, sometimes, almost always, and always) or, in some cases, the intensity (not at all, a little, moderately, a lot, and extremely) of the attribute over a one-week period. Higher scores indicate better HRQoL. The version used in this study was provided by the original authors of the scale through the official instrument platform (https://www.kidscreen.org/).

The family affluence scale-II (FAS-II) [40]

Socioeconomic status was measured using the FAS-II, a 4-item self-report scale designed to assess family economic status in children and adolescents. This scale ranges from 0 to 9. According to the findings of previous international studies, the instrument possesses adequate psychometric properties. Given that the mean score of the participants was 6.53, the sample was divided into two groups for measurement invariance analyses: low socioeconomic status (below the mean; score range 1 to 6; n = 1011; 45.23%) and high socioeconomic status (above the mean; score range 7 to 9; n = 1224; 54.76%). Higher total scores reflect higher levels of family economic status.

Sexual orientation scale

A modified version of the Kinsey Scale [41] was used to assess sexual orientation. This widely used index asks participants to respond to the prompt, “Do you usually feel physically and affectionately attracted to…”, by selecting one of the following options: (1) only boys; (2) mostly boys and sometimes girls; (3) equally to boys and girls; (4) mostly girls and sometimes boys; (5) only girls; (6) not physically attracted to anyone; or (7) not sure. For the measurement invariance analyses, only two categories were defined due to the small size of some subgroups: heterosexual (category 1 for boys and category 5 for girls) and sexual minority (combining the remaining categories).

Strengths and difficulties questionnaire (SDQ) [42]

The SDQ is a tool designed to assess behavioral and emotional difficulties, as well as social competencies. It consists of 25 items rated on a three-point Likert scale (not true; somewhat true; certainly true). The items are grouped into five subscales: Emotional Symptoms, Conduct Problems, Hyperactivity/Inattention, Peer Relationship Problems, and Prosocial Behavior. The version used in this study (self-report) has demonstrated adequate psychometric properties in Spanish adolescents [43]. For the present analysis, only the first four subscales were used to compute a total difficulties score. SDQ scores are positively oriented; higher values correspond to higher levels of emotional and behavioral difficulties.

Patient health questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [44]

The PHQ-9 is a 9-item self-report questionnaire that assesses the frequency of depressive symptoms, with response options ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). Higher scores indicate greater symptom severity. The version used in this study was adapted by Fonseca-Pedrero et al. [45].

Generalised anxiety disorder assessment-7 (GAD-7) [46]

This is a self-report instrument consisting of seven items, each rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = not at all, 1 = several days, 2 = more than half the days, 3 = nearly every day), designed to assess the severity of generalized anxiety disorder symptoms over the past two weeks. Higher scores indicate more severe symptoms. The version used in this study was that of Casares et al. [47].

Personal wellbeing index–school children (PWI-SC) [48]

The PWI-SC assesses emotional well-being understood as a cognitive evaluation of the quality of one’s own experiences. Self-reported emotional well-being can be considered an indirect indicator of quality of life. The PWI-SC consists of 8 items, with response options ranging from Very dissatisfied (score of 0) to Very satisfied (score of 10). The items assess subjective satisfaction with specific life domains in a relatively generic and abstract manner. The first item of the scale evaluates “life as a whole” (“How do you feel about your life in general?”). The item discrimination index (0.83) supports the use of item 1 as an indicator of the subjective well-being variable. The PWI-SC has been widely used in previous studies with Spanish adolescents [49, 50].

Oviedo infrequency of response scale–revised (INF-OV-R) [39]

This scale was administered to detect random, pseudo-random, or dishonest response patterns. The INF-OV-R is a self-report instrument consisting of 10 items with a dichotomous (yes/no) response format. Scores ≥ 2 indicate potentially inattentive or random responding, warranting exclusion to uphold validity [39, 45].

Procedure

The research was approved by the Directorate General of Education of the Government of La Rioja and the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of La Rioja (CEImLAR, reference number PI 552). The administration of the instrument was standardized through a protocol provided to the research staff. Data collection was carried out during the year 2022. Questionnaires were administered electronically, collectively, in groups of between 10 and 30 participants, during a designated 50-minute school period. Informed consent was requested from families or legal guardians for participants under the age of 18, and the confidentiality and voluntary nature of the study were ensured. This study is part of a broader research on emotional well-being and mental health in adolescence [51].

Data analyses

We ensured that key statistical assumptions (including independence of observations, residual normality, homogeneity of variances, and absence of influential outliers) were carefully checked prior to conducting the analyses.

First, descriptive statistics were calculated for the KIDSCREEN-10 Index items.

Second, a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed to examine the internal structure of the scale. The robust weighted least squares mean and variance adjusted (WLSMV) estimator, suitable for ordinal data, was used for the analysis [52]. The goodness-of-fit indices used in this study included the Chi-square (χ2), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) with 90% confidence interval, and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR). According to Kline [53], an RMSEA of 0.08 or less and CFI and TLI values of 0.90 or more are recommended for a good model fit.

Third, a series of successive multigroup confirmatory factor analyses (CFAs) were conducted to assess measurement invariance across sex, age, socioeconomic status, sexual minority status, and nationality [54]. This process followed a hierarchical sequence of steps, beginning with the specification of a well-fitting multigroup baseline model, and proceeding with the incremental imposition of equivalence constraints on model parameters across groups. The first step involved testing configural invariance, the least restrictive model, to verify whether the factor structure was comparable across groups. Next, metric (or weak) invariance was assessed by constraining factor loadings to be equal, thereby evaluating whether items were interpreted similarly across groups [55]. Scalar invariance, which imposes cross-group equality constraints on all factor loadings and item intercepts, was then tested. When met, this suggests similar interpretations of the measure across groups [55]. To test the invariance hypothesis, we examined changes in CFI (ΔCFI) to assess practical equivalence between models [54]. Typically, if ΔCFI is less than 0.01 between two nested models, the specified equality constraints are considered acceptable. Given the sensitivity of χ² in large samples, ΔCFI provides a more practical criterion [54, 55]. Once measurement invariance was established, potential group differences in scores based on sex, age, socioeconomic status, sexual minority status, and nationality were examined using analysis of variance (ANOVA). Significant main effects and interactions among variables were explored, and effect sizes were reported using partial η².

Fourth, the reliability of the KIDSCREEN-10 Index scores was assessed using both Cronbach’s alpha (α) and McDonald’s Omega [56].

Finally, the associations between the KIDSCREEN-10 Index and other indicators of mental health and well-being were examined using Pearson’s bivariate correlation coefficients.

All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 28.0), Mplus (Version 7.0), and JASP (Version 0.18.2.0).

Females, older adolescents, those with fewer economic resources, sexual minority youth (lesbian, gay, bisexual, asexual, and questioning), and foreign nationals scored significantly lower than their comparison groups.

Two significant interactions were also found. First, there was a significant interaction between sex and age (F(1,2196) = 6.47; p <.05; partial η² = 0.003). Post hoc analyses indicated a compounding effect of both variables: older females scored significantly lower than all other groups. Additionally, a significant three-way interaction between sex, age, and sexual minority status (F(1,2196) = 8.40; p <.01; partial η² = 0.004) revealed that the interaction between sex and age was moderated by sexual minority status. Reliability estimation of the KIDSCREEN-10 index scores

The total score of the index demonstrated good internal consistency, estimated with McDonald’s Omega, with a coefficient of 0.874 (95% CI: 0.867–0.882), and with Cronbach’s alpha, with a coefficient of 0.872 (95% CI: 0.864–0.880). All item discrimination indices exceeded 0.30.

Discussion

The main objective of this study was to provide additional validity evidence for the KIDSCREEN-10 Index of health-related quality of life (HRQoL) in Spanish adolescents. This instrument was developed through a European collaborative project involving 15 countries and coordinated by Ravens-Sieberer [15], and it has shown adequate psychometric properties across its various versions [16]. Nonetheless, to the best of our knowledge, the structure of the instrument and its invariance with respect to relevant variables in the Spanish context had not been previously examined.

Consequently, the study sought to examine the internal structure of the scale, assess measurement invariance across groups defined by sex, age, economic status, sexual minority status, and nationality, as well as to analyze potential differences in scores across these sociodemographic groups. Furthermore, the study aimed to assess the reliability of the scores and to offer further validity evidence in relation to indicators of mental health and subjective well-being.

Our findings supported the unidimensional structure of the scale within our sample, as indicated by the satisfactory fit of the data to the questionnaire’s single-factor model. To date, although only a limited number of versions of the instrument have been examined in this regard, there are some studies that have yielded similar results. The Portuguese [27], Iranian [28], and more recently, the Mandarin Chinese version [26] have all confirmed its unidimensionality.

Moreover, in the present study, the unidimensional structure remained invariant across groups based on sex, age, socioeconomic status, sexual minority status, and nationality. This result suggests that the latent variables were measured equivalently across all groups, and that the scores of the different subgroups are comparable given that there are no differences in the interpretation of the latent construct [57]. These findings are consistent with multigroup confirmatory factor analyses conducted in other versions of the instrument, which have found invariance by gender and age in the Chinese version [26], and by age, nationality, and socioeconomic status in the Portuguese version [27]. These results align with those obtained in the present study for the Spanish sample.

Similarly, the results of this study not only provide evidence for measurement invariance with respect to the aforementioned variables but also incorporate sexual orientation as a relevant variable to consider. To date, there is a lack of studies of this nature focusing on sexual minorities [35, 37, 58], despite the extensive body of scientific literature documenting health-related challenges—particularly in mental health—among sexual minority populations [59–61]. In this regard, the present study is, to the best of our knowledge, pioneering in examining measurement invariance of this scale based on sexual orientation. While there is ongoing research on the impact of these conditions on HRQoL, such studies often fail to establish the equivalence of the instruments used. This study provides evidence that supports the use of the KIDSCREEN-10 in this research context, thereby addressing the call made by various authors [35].

Additionally, the scale scores exhibited satisfactory reliability. This finding has been consistently observed across studies employing different versions of the instrument and in various countries. Specifically, for the KIDSCREEN-10 version, the present results are consistent with those reported in studies conducted both in Europe [e.g., 23, 25, 27, 34] and beyond [e.g., 28, 35].

The analysis of differences by sex, age, and socioeconomic status revealed that females, older participants, and those with lower socioeconomic status scored significantly lower on HRQoL compared to males, younger participants, and those with higher socioeconomic status, respectively. These findings are consistent with prior research examining the impact of sociodemographic variables on HRQoL as measured by the Spanish versions of the KIDSCREEN-52 [e.g., 18–20, 24] and the KIDSCREEN-27 [21, 22]. Regarding studies specifically conducted with the KIDSCREEN-10 Index in European samples, Ravens-Sieberer et al. [23] did not observe sex-based differences. However, our results are in line with those reported by Erhart et al. [25], which indicated that females not only scored lower than males overall, but also that an interaction between sex and age was present, suggesting an additive effect of both variables. Taken together, these findings suggest that HRQoL is highly sensitive to sociodemographic variables and that the questionnaire is capable of effectively distinguishing between groups in which differences would be expected a priori.

With respect to nationality, the findings of the present study suggest that the HRQoL of adolescents may be influenced by their migrant status. This issue has been previously identified by other researchers examining HRQoL [17, 27, 34], and may be understood as a vulnerability factor linked to acculturation processes [62], as well as to an increased risk of experiencing discrimination and social rejection [63–65].

A similar pattern is observed regarding the differences found based on the sexual orientation of the participants. Integrating this variable is imperative for comprehending health disparities within these groups and for proposing strategies that promote equity. Scientific literature has systematically shown [e.g.,59–61, 66, 67] that lesbians, gays, bisexuals, and other sexual minority groups experience higher rates of mental health difficulties, such as depression, anxiety, and suicidal behavior, compared to heterosexual individuals. In addition, it has been observed [68] that sexual orientation can influence how individuals access healthcare services, as delays or avoidance of services are common due to fears of discrimination or prior negative experiences. By including sexual orientation as a moderating variable, structural inequalities are made more visible, thereby providing a rationale for the development of more equitable and inclusive public health interventions and policies [69]. In this sense, our findings should not be interpreted as reflecting intrinsic deficits of sexual minority youth, but rather as the outcome of structural and social conditions. Following Meyer’s minority stress framework [70], disparities in HRQoL are better understood as consequences of stigma, discrimination, and cumulative stressors affecting sexual minority adolescents. This perspective aligns with the Index of Health Disparity Approach (IHDA; [71]), which emphasizes that differences in health outcomes across social categories represent manifestations of inequity rather than neutral variation. Consistent with Erhart et al. [72], we therefore frame sexual orientation as a social category embedded in power relations, where intersecting vulnerabilities (e.g., gender, SES, nationality) may exacerbate disparities in HRQoL. This intersectional framing emphasizes the importance of contextual and structural determinants when interpreting adolescent health outcomes.

Furthermore, in order to provide additional evidence of the validity of the measure, correlations were obtained between the KIDSCREEN-10 Index score and other psychometric indicators of mental health and subjective well-being. The results revealed statistically significant positive correlations with emotional well-being, and negative correlations with depressive and anxiety symptoms, as well as emotional and behavioral problems. Previous research has reported similar findings [19, 20, 23, 25, 28, 31] using the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) and the Personal Well-being Index [28]. The present study, in addition to employing these indicators, provides insight into the relationship with anxiety-depressive symptoms in adolescents, which represent one of the major challenges for educational and healthcare systems among young people in our society today [73]. The relationship with these psychometric indicators of mental health as a whole supports the hypothesis that the KIDSCREEN-10 is a suitable instrument for assessing health-related quality of life in the context of mental health [23–25, 31].

There are several limitations to consider when interpreting the results of the present study. First, it is important to acknowledge that the sample used in this study was drawn exclusively from the autonomous community of La Rioja, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader Spanish adolescent population. Regional differences in demographic composition, socioeconomic conditions, and migration dynamics across Spain could influence adolescents’ health-related quality of life and their responses to the KIDSCREEN-10. Consequently, the psychometric properties and measurement invariance observed in this study should be interpreted with caution. To enhance external validity, future research should replicate these analyses using multi-regional or nationally representative samples that can adequately reflect the diversity of the Spanish population. Second, only self-report measures were used to assess all indicators, which introduces certain limitations, such as potential social desirability bias on sensitive domains (sexual orientation, mental health). Third, although significant differences based on nationality were identified, future research could benefit from considering other variables related to the migration experience (e.g., length of residence, first or second-generation migration status, family language, or legal status), which could provide a deeper understanding of the observed inequalities. In a similar vein, regarding differences among sexual minorities, larger samples would enable an analysis of the heterogeneity within the non-heterosexual group, a strategy strongly recommended by authors in this area of research [74]. Fourth, an additional limitation is that the present dataset did not allow us to test measurement invariance by combining categorical groupings (e.g., age and socioeconomic status), which would provide deeper insights into subgroup comparability; therefore, this remains an important direction for future research. Finally, the cross-sectional nature of this study prevents drawing conclusions about causal relationships, and the absence of longitudinal data further constrains our ability to assess temporal stability and predictive validity.

Despite these limitations, the present study provides evidence supporting the construct validity and measurement equivalence of a brief scale designed for the efficient assessment of HRQoL across sex, age, socioeconomic status, sexual minority status, and nationality of the participants. As previously mentioned, the assessment of health-related quality of life is a crucial aspect in evaluating the impact of physical and emotional conditions on individuals, particularly in childhood and adolescence. Assessing HRQoL at these stages enables the identification of both protective and risk factors for healthy development, as well as the early detection of health concerns, allowing for timely interventions before problems become chronic or escalate. Moreover, children and adolescents with chronic illnesses typically report lower HRQoL compared to their healthy peers, making it essential to understand how these conditions affect their lives, to provide appropriate psychosocial support and accommodations, and to ensure their well-being and equitable access to healthcare services [75, 76]. In this context, the results of the present study help to link measurement to practical application. Specifically, the validated and invariant structure of the KIDSCREEN-10 can contribute to improving the rigor of school health programs, strengthening screening and follow-up activities in the general population, and supporting more targeted interventions for vulnerable groups, such as migrants, sexual minorities, and adolescents from low socioeconomic backgrounds. In this way, the instrument can be used to inform screening, needs assessment, and equity-oriented resource allocation.

Given the aforementioned considerations, valid and reliable assessment tools are needed to capture self-reported health-related quality of life during adolescence. The KIDSCREEN-10 Index emerges as a particularly strong candidate due to its characteristics: it is brief, unidimensional, reliable, and valid. This instrument can serve as a generic tool for assessing the quality of life of both healthy and chronically ill children and adolescents [77]. Furthermore, its origins and the multicultural interest it has garnered enable meaningful comparisons between countries, providing a valuable measure of HRQoL for international multicenter studies, as evidenced by its widespread use in both national and international research contexts.

Conclusions

Taken together, the findings of this study support the construct validity and measurement invariance of the KIDSCREEN-10 Index in Spanish adolescents, as well as its capacity to detect differences in HRQoL across sex, age, socioeconomic status, nationality, and sexual minority status. The observed pattern of associations with indicators of emotional well-being, depressive and anxiety symptoms, and emotional and behavioural difficulties provides additional support for its relevance in the context of adolescent mental health. Moreover, the results highlight the need to consider factors such as socioeconomic disadvantage, experiences of discrimination and social rejection among adolescents of foreign nationality, and minority stress processes affecting sexual minority youth when interpreting group differences in HRQoL. Overall, the study shows the usefulness of the KIDSCREEN-10 as a brief and valid measure that can contribute to screening and needs assessment, and to the design of targeted interventions in educational and public health contexts.
