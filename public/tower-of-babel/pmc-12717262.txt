Predictive value of AFR and UCR for the methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus infection in children with bone and joint infection
Authors: Chaochen Zhao, Chenghui Ke, Zhiye Guan, Dan Yang, Xiaodong Wang, Yang Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) poses diagnostic and therapeutic challenges in pediatric bone and joint infections (BJIs). This two-center retrospective study evaluated the admission albumin-to-fibrinogen ratio (AFR) and urea-to-creatinine ratio (UCR) as predictors of MRSA among children with culture-confirmed S. aureus BJIs. Seventy-seven patients (MRSA, n = 23; MSSA, n = 54) admitted from 2015 to 2024 were analyzed. Clinical data and laboratory tests obtained before antibiotic initiation were reviewed. Compared with MSSA, the MRSA group had significantly lower AFR and UCR values. Using Firth’s penalized logistic regression, AFR and UCR were incorporated as continuous covariates to construct a composite indicator (AFR + UCR). The model showed acceptable discrimination (AUC = 0.724, 95% CI 0.612–0.854) and good calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow P = 0.273; Brier score = 0.184), with internal validation by stratified 10-fold cross-validation and 1,000 bootstrap resamples. At a prespecified probability cutoff of 0.293, sensitivity and specificity were 0.826 and 0.611, respectively. Decision curve analysis indicated net clinical benefit across threshold probabilities of approximately 10–60%. In pediatric BJIs, lower AFR and UCR at admission are associated with increased MRSA risk, and their combination may serve as an adjunctive tool for early risk assessment and empirical treatment planning. Findings pertain to culture-positive S. aureus infections; external validation in larger multicenter cohorts is warranted to confirm generalizability and clinical utility.

Introduction

Bone and joint infections (BJIs), predominantly acute osteomyelitis and septic arthritis, are serious pediatric conditions commonly arising from hematogenous dissemination of pathogenic microorganisms1,2. Staphylococcus aureus remains the predominant pathogen, although local epidemiological variations can influence microbial profiles1,3,4. While early empirical antibiotic therapy typically yields favorable outcomes, infections caused by methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) pose substantial clinical challenges. MRSA’s propensity for biofilm formation complicates treatment and significantly increases the risk of severe complications5,6. Current guidelines recommend initial empiric antibiotic regimens targeting methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) in regions with low MRSA prevalence. However, when MRSA represents more than 10–20% of local S. aureus isolates or when patient conditions are severe, empirical anti-MRSA therapy, commonly with vancomycin, is advised7,8. Nonetheless, vancomycin use is constrained by concerns regarding nephrotoxicity and the absence of clearly defined pediatric dosing and therapeutic monitoring guidelines, thus limiting its universal adoption as first-line empiric therapy6.

For BJIs without suspected MRSA or complications, initial oral regimens (e.g., amoxicillin-clavulanate or dicloxacillin) may be as effective as intravenous therapy9. Yet, the increasing incidence of MRSA and its associated poor outcomes underscores the need for rapid, accurate identification5. Traditional diagnostic methods such as blood or pus cultures typically require three to five days to yield results, potentially delaying appropriate management. Although advanced diagnostic technologies, including polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS), have shortened pathogen detection timeframes and improved diagnostic accuracy, their higher cost and technical complexity restrict widespread adoption, especially in primary care settings10. Additionally, although elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) levels (> 73.23 µg/mL) may suggest MRSA infection, the marker lacks sufficient specificity due to susceptibility to various clinical influences11. Consequently, there is an urgent need for simple and reliable biomarkers to support early clinical decision-making.

Albumin to fibrinogen ratio (AFR) and urea to creatinine ratio (UCR) are readily obtained in clinical practice and have attracted increasing attention for their potential predictive value in various diseases. Li et al. identified AFR as an independent protective factor against 28-day mortality among patients with sepsis12. Similarly, Shang et al. reported that decreased preoperative AFR significantly elevates the risk of postoperative complications, suggesting its value for both nutritional assessment and wound prognosis following primary total joint arthroplasty13. Additionally, Moreira et al. found that UCR is predictive of clinical events in patients with chronic heart failure, likely due to persistent neuroendocrine activation in low-volume states14. Furthermore, Jiang et al. observed that changes in UCR may aid in the early identification of critically ill septic patients at risk of rapid muscle wasting15. However, evidence regarding the predictive value of AFR and UCR specifically for MRSA infection in pediatric BJIs remains limited.

Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the clinical utility of AFR and UCR measured at admission for predicting MRSA infection among pediatric patients diagnosed with BJIs.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by the ethics committees of the two participating hospitals (approval numbers 2021KS024 and 2025R044-E01). The requirement for informed consent was waived by both ethics committees due to the retrospective nature of the study. An a priori power analysis (GPower 3.1, Hsieh method) indicated that a sample of at least 70 patients would be required to detect an odds ratio of 0.30 with 80% power at alpha = 0.05, assuming an MRSA prevalence of 30% and two covariates. Given that osteomyelitis and septic arthritis are both hematogenous infections with identical initial antibiotic management, they were collectively analyzed as BJIs.

Study design, setting, and study subjects

We retrospectively enrolled 77 pediatric patients diagnosed with BJIs, admitted to the Departments of Orthopedics at Shanghai Children’s Hospital or the Affiliated Children’s Hospital of Soochow University between January 2015 and August 2024. The study population included 47 males and 30 females. Patient data were retrieved through electronic medical record reviews.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) confirmed diagnosis of BJIs; (2) positive microbiological cultures (blood, pus, or synovial fluid) identifying either Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) or Methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA); (3) symptom onset within 14 days prior to hospitalization and no antibiotic therapy prior to initial blood sampling; and (4) patient age ranging from 1 month to 18 years.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) underlying chronic or systemic conditions such as hematological disorders, rheumatic diseases, cardiovascular diseases, malignancies, or other chronic illnesses; (2) cases involving open fractures or postoperative infections; and (3) incomplete or unavailable medical records.

Treatment and indicators

Patient demographics and clinical characteristics collected included sex, age, infection site, body temperature upon admission, and culture results. Laboratory tests conducted within 24 h of admission included white blood cell count (WBC), CRP, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), serum albumin, fibrinogen, urea, and creatinine levels. The AFR and UCR were subsequently calculated. AFR was defined as serum albumin (g/L) divided by fibrinogen (g/L), and UCR as serum urea divided by serum creatinine. Fever at admission was defined as a body temperature exceeding 38.5 °C, consistent with the threshold used in the Kocher algorithm for defining fever, with the normal pediatric temperature range considered to be 36.5–37.5 °C. All baseline blood samples were collected prior to the initiation of antibiotic therapy.

All pediatric patients with suspected BJIs underwent bilateral blood cultures and were initially treated empirically with intravenous first- or second-generation cephalosporins. Based on clinical progression and imaging findings, invasive procedures such as ultrasound-guided aspiration or surgical interventions were performed to collect pus specimens for further bacterial cultures and antibiotic susceptibility testing. Patients were classified into MSSA and MRSA groups according to culture outcomes, and antibiotic regimens were adjusted accordingly based on susceptibility profiles.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) and R software version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD), while non-normally distributed data were reported as median (P25, P75). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Missing data (< 5%) were imputed using median values. Between-group comparisons were conducted using the independent-samples t-test for normally distributed variables, the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables, and the χ² test for categorical variables. Univariate logistic regression analyses were first performed to identify variables associated with MRSA infection. Given the limited number of events (n = 23), the number of explanatory variables included in multivariable analysis was restricted to no more than two to minimize overfitting. Based on both univariate results and clinical relevance, AFR and UCR were selected as candidate predictors. Before multivariable modeling, potential nonlinear relationships between the continuous predictors (AFR and UCR) and the outcome were examined using restricted cubic splines. When the test for nonlinearity yielded P < 0.05, spline terms were retained in the subsequent model; otherwise, the predictors were modeled as linear terms. Variance inflation factors were used to assess multicollinearity, with VIF < 5 indicating no significant collinearity. Because of the small event size, Firth’s penalized logistic regression was applied to obtain stable estimates and mitigate small-sample bias. The final model retained AFR and UCR as covariates, and a combined predictive index was derived from the estimated regression coefficients and intercept.

Model discrimination was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic curves, with the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity used as summary measures of predictive performance. Model calibration and goodness-of-fit were evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, calibration plots, and the Brier score. Internal validation was conducted using 10-fold stratified cross-validation and bootstrap resampling with 1,000 iterations to estimate optimism-corrected performance. Decision curve analysis was performed to quantify the net clinical benefit across a range of threshold probabilities. Incremental predictive value was further examined by comparing the AUCs of the “CRP-only” model and the “CRP + AFR + UCR” model using the DeLong test, and by calculating the net reclassification improvement and integrated discrimination improvement indices. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

General information

A total of 77 pediatric patients diagnosed with BJIs were included, comprising 47 males and 30 females. The femur was the most frequently involved site (16 cases, 20.8%), followed by the tibia (12 cases, 15.6%), calcaneus (8 cases, 10.4%), fibula (7 cases, 9.1%), humerus (3 cases, 3.9%), patella (3 cases, 3.9%), pelvis (3 cases, 3.9%), ulna (2 cases, 2.6%), radius (1 case, 1.3%), and phalanx (1 case, 1.3%). Among joint infections, the hip joint was most commonly affected (9 cases, 11.7%), followed by the knee joint (6 cases, 7.8%), ankle joint (3 cases, 3.9%), shoulder joint (2 cases, 2.6%), and elbow joint (1 case, 1.3%).

Pathogen detection was performed via blood, pus, or synovial fluid cultures in all 77 cases. Based on culture results, patients were categorized into MSSA (n = 54) and MRSA (n = 23) groups.

Single factor analysis of MRSA-related factors

Chi-square and non-parametric tests indicated no significant differences in sex, age, or presence of fever upon admission between MSSA and MRSA groups (P > 0.05). However, serum albumin, AFR, and UCR levels were significantly lower in the MRSA group than in the MSSA group (P < 0.05). No significant differences were noted in WBC, CRP, ESR, fibrinogen, urea, or creatinine between the two groups (all P > 0.05). Although the MRSA group exhibited a notably higher median ESR (77 mm/h) compared to the MSSA group (51 mm/h), this difference approached but did not achieve statistical significance (P = 0.059) (Table 1).

Univariate logistic regression analysis

Univariable logistic regression analysis revealed that lower levels of albumin (OR = 0.852, 95% CI 0.731–0.976, P = 0.028), AFR(OR = 0.703, 95% CI 0.505–0.924, P = 0.022), and UCR (OR = 0.927, 95% CI 0.856–0.988, P = 0.038) were significantly associated with MRSA infection (Table 2).

Nonlinearity and multicollinearity assessment

Restricted cubic spline analyses were performed to examine potential nonlinear associations between AFR or UCR and the risk of MRSA infection. The nonlinear components were not statistically significant for either variable (AFR: χ² = 1.82, P = 0.403; UCR: χ² = 0.51, P = 0.774), indicating that both AFR and UCR exhibited approximately linear relationships with MRSA risk within the observed range. Therefore, linear terms of AFR and UCR were retained in the final multivariable model. Variance inflation factor values for all predictors were below 5 (AFR = 1.11; UCR = 1.11), suggesting no evidence of substantial multicollinearity among the explanatory variables.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis

In the multivariable Firth’s penalized logistic regression analysis, the overall model demonstrated statistical significance (likelihood ratio χ² = 8.83, P = 0.012). AFR was identified as an independent negative predictor of MRSA infection (OR = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.56–1.00, P = 0.046), whereas UCR showed a borderline negative association (OR = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.87–1.01, P = 0.081). Given the clinical and biological relevance of both AFR and UCR, and the overall significance of the model, these two variables were retained to construct the final combined predictive indicator. The composite indicator equation was expressed as follows: logit [P(MRSA = 1)] = 2.568 − 0.272 × AFR − 0.060 × UCR. (Table 3).

Validation of the composite indicator

Model performance was evaluated using 10-fold stratified cross-validation, with an average accuracy of 0.753 ± 0.203. The Firth’s penalized logistic regression model showed acceptable discrimination, with an AUC of 0.724 (95% CI: 0.612–0.854) obtained from 1,000 bootstrap resamples(Fig. 1). The optimal cutoff value determined by the Youden index was 0.293, corresponding to a sensitivity of 0.826 and a specificity of 0.611 (Table 4). The model demonstrated good calibration, as supported by the Hosmer–Lemeshow test (χ² = 9.881, P = 0.273) and a Brier score of 0.184.

The model demonstrated good calibration, with a calibration intercept of − 0.041 and a slope of 1.115, both close to ideal values(Fig. 2). The calibration curve showed good agreement between predicted and observed probabilities across most of the range. In the decision curve analysis, the model provided a higher net clinical benefit than the “treat all” or “treat none” strategies when the threshold probability ranged approximately from 10% to 60%, with the greatest benefit observed around 20–30%(Fig. 3). These findings indicate that the model offers acceptable calibration and potential clinical utility for early MRSA risk prediction.

Incremental predictive value and clinical implications

To evaluate the incremental predictive value of AFR and UCR, the discriminative performance of the CRP-only model was compared with that of the combined CRP + AFR + UCR model. The AUC increased from 0.594 (95% CI: 0.457–0.730) to 0.727 (95% CI: 0.606–0.848), representing an improvement of 0.133 (P = 0.052, DeLong test). The combined model also showed significant improvement in risk reclassification, with a categorical net reclassification improvement of 0.6135 (P = 0.002) and an integrated discrimination improvement of 0.1177 (P = 0.001). These findings suggest that incorporating AFR and UCR into the CRP-based model meaningfully enhanced risk discrimination and reclassification for predicting MRSA infection in pediatric bone and joint infections.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the predictive value of AFR and UCR for MRSA infection in children with BJIs. The results showed that serum albumin, AFR, and UCR levels were significantly lower in the MRSA group than in the MSSA group, whereas no statistically significant differences were observed in traditional inflammatory markers (e.g., WBC, CRP, ESR). These findings partially align with previous studies suggesting that WBC, CRP, and ESR may not consistently differ at admission between pediatric patients with MRSA and MSSA musculoskeletal infections18. Nonetheless, MRSA infections are generally more severe, with higher WBC, CRP, and extended disease courses19. For instance, Wen et al. found that, compared with MSSA, MRSA-induced osteomyelitis in children more frequently leads to high-grade fever and significantly elevated WBC counts, often accompanied by pyogenic infections in adjacent joints and metastatic abscesses, thereby necessitating additional surgical interventions and prolonged hospitalization5. Similarly, Wang et al. found that in children presenting with acute osteomyelitis, an admission CRP level above 73.23 µg/mL substantially increased the likelihood of MRSA infection11. The absence of significant differences in WBC, CRP, and ESR between the MRSA and MSSA groups in our study may be attributable to the limited sample size or varying times of clinical presentation. This observation highlights the potential inadequacy of relying solely on these conventional markers to distinguish MRSA, thereby underscoring the need for alternative indicators. Because of economic and technical constraints, PCR and mNGS were not employed for pathogen detection. Against this backdrop, our study is the first to evaluate the predictive value of AFR and UCR for distinguishing MRSA infections among pediatric patients with BJIs. Multivariable Firth’s logistic regression analysis indicated that higher AFR and UCR values were associated with a lower risk of MRSA infection, suggesting a potential protective effect of these biomarkers. The composite indicator combining AFR and UCR achieved an AUC of 0.724, demonstrating acceptable discriminative performance for predicting MRSA infection in pediatric BJIs. Although this combined model exhibited relatively high sensitivity, its specificity was limited. Therefore, external validation in larger, multicenter cohorts is warranted to further confirm its robustness and clinical applicability.

AFR provides a comprehensive assessment of a patient’s nutritional status, inflammatory response, and coagulation function12,20,21. Albumin is a typical negative acute-phase reactant that decreases in severe infections due to reduced synthesis and increased catabolism, leading to lower serum albumin levels22. In contrast, fibrinogen is a positive acute-phase reactant that rises rapidly under the influence of inflammatory mediators23. Consequently, a decreased AFR commonly indicates a more pronounced inflammatory response or malnutrition. Previous studies have confirmed strong associations between hypoalbuminemia or hyperfibrinogenemia and adverse outcomes in infectious diseases. For instance, Campbell et al. reported that low albumin levels were a significant risk factor for Staphylococcus aureus (including MRSA) infection in patients with deep musculoskeletal infections following orthopedic surgery, whereas Nugroho et al. found in a meta-analysis that elevated fibrinogen at admission increased the risk of poor prognosis in COVID-19 patients24,25. Moreover, because MRSA strains frequently carry virulence factors such as Panton-Valentine leukocidin, they can induce more extensive tissue damage and inflammatory responses, thereby causing a more marked decrease in albumin and increase in fibrinogen—manifesting as a significantly reduced AFR26. Given that AFR is highly sensitive to the severity of inflammation, its decline has been linked to worse clinical outcomes in patients with sepsis. For example, Tai et al. demonstrated that among patients with sepsis secondary to abdominal infections, those with AFR ≤ 8.85 had notably higher 28-day mortality20. Another multicenter study similarly identified AFR as an independent protective factor for 28-day mortality in sepsis, indicating that lower AFR correlates with an increased risk of death12. Collectively, these findings support the notion that a reduced AFR points to a more severe infection. In the present study, we observed that a lower AFR level suggests MRSA may be the causative pathogen in pediatric bone and joint infections, in line with AFR’s capacity to reflect the extent of the inflammatory response. Clinicians should therefore remain vigilant for the possibility of resistant pathogens in pediatric patients presenting with markedly reduced AFR.

UCR is routinely applied as an indicator of fluid balance and renal perfusion in clinical settings. In adults under normal conditions, the typical ratio of UCR ranges from approximately 10 to 20; ratios above 20 often point to prerenal azotemia, such as that caused by dehydration or hypovolemia. However, this benchmark has yet to be fully validated in pediatric populations27. Unlike most studies that focus on the association between UCR and infection-related outcomes, our investigation centers on its relationship with the specific causative pathogen. The results showed that the UCR in the MRSA group was lower than in the MSSA group (median 23 vs. 27). One plausible explanation is that MRSA infection typically manifests more acutely and triggers a more intense inflammatory response, potentially leading to early renal parenchymal injury. This injury may raise creatinine levels relative to urea, whose reabsorption is often reduced, thereby lowering the UCR. Alternatively, children with MRSA may experience a protracted disease course and poor nutritional status, diminishing protein catabolism and decreasing urea levels28,29. In critically ill patients, early fluid resuscitation and anti-shock therapy could also dilute urea. In contrast, children with milder MSSA infections may have only mild dehydration, presenting as prerenal azotemia and thus elevating the UCR27. Consequently, the difference in UCR suggests that MRSA infection may affect fluid balance and organ function differently from MSSA. As an independent protective factor, a lower UCR is more suggestive of MRSA, whereas a markedly elevated UCR aligns more with MSSA. Given the limited literature on this phenomenon, further large-scale research is warranted to confirm these findings.

Timely identification of the causative pathogen in bone and joint infections is critical for ensuring targeted therapy. In clinical practice, MRSA is typically confirmed through culture, but this process requires time and introduces uncertainty into early empirical treatment. In our cohort, lower AFR and lower UCR were associated with MRSA infection, and a composite indicator derived from these continuous measures demonstrated utility for early risk estimation. At a prespecified cutoff of 0.293, this indicator achieved a sensitivity of 0.826 and a specificity of 0.611, indicating that values above this threshold should heighten suspicion for MRSA and may justify earlier initiation of anti-MRSA coverage while cultures are pending. In routine care, the composite indicator is best applied as an adjunctive tool to inform early risk assessment and empirical decision-making, rather than as a stand-alone diagnostic test. Regarding clinical trade-offs, prioritizing sensitivity at this threshold facilitates earlier detection of true MRSA cases but increases false positives and broader empiric use of anti-MRSA agents; therefore, threshold selection should be contextualized to local MRSA prevalence, illness severity, and antimicrobial-stewardship considerations, with corroboration from clinical and imaging findings when appropriate. Where feasible, confirmatory tests such as PCR and mNGS are recommended, particularly if other high-risk factors (e.g., epidemiological history of community-acquired MRSA) are present. Early studies, such as that by Wang et al., reported that markedly elevated CRP levels may indicate MRSA infection. Building on this, our findings demonstrated that incorporating AFR and UCR into a CRP-based model improved its predictive performance and enhanced risk reclassification for MRSA infection. These results suggest that combining AFR and UCR with traditional inflammatory markers—such as high fever, elevated WBC, and increased CRP—may improve the accuracy of early MRSA identification in pediatric bone and joint infections. Moreover, changes in AFR and UCR may partially reflect the overall inflammatory burden and organ function, implying that children with MRSA infection often experience more severe disease and thus require more aggressive intervention. Nevertheless, AFR and UCR currently serve only as adjunctive markers and cannot replace bacterial culture or drug susceptibility testing. In routine clinical application, these measures should be interpreted in conjunction with clinical signs and symptoms, imaging studies, and other laboratory data.

This study has several limitations. First, it is a two-center retrospective analysis restricted to culture-positive S. aureus pediatric BJIs, which may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability to culture-negative cases or infections caused by other pathogens. Second, the overall sample size and the number of MRSA events were limited (n = 23), which reduces statistical power and may yield imprecise effect estimates and variable performance metrics. Although Firth’s penalized logistic regression, a parsimonious covariate strategy, and stratified 10-fold cross-validation with bootstrap internal validation were applied to mitigate small-sample overfitting, external validation remains necessary. Third, only admission (baseline) laboratory values were analyzed; the absence of longitudinal trajectories precluded assessment of how dynamic changes in AFR and UCR relate to disease progression or treatment response. Fourth, the composite indicator threshold (0.293, derived using the Youden index) was established and evaluated within the same dataset, raising the possibility of cutoff-related optimism. Future studies should perform prespecification, external validation, and recalibration of thresholds across diverse clinical settings with varying MRSA prevalence and antimicrobial stewardship practices. Finally, residual confounding cannot be excluded, as factors such as age, nutritional status, hydration status, disease severity, and infection site were not fully measured or adjusted for. Future multicenter studies with larger cohorts are warranted to confirm these findings and further establish the clinical utility of AFR and UCR in predicting MRSA infection in pediatric bone and joint infections.

Conclusion

In conclusion, lower AFR and UCR values were associated with an increased risk of MRSA infection in pediatric bone and joint infections. The composite indicator combining these two parameters demonstrated acceptable discriminative ability and may serve as an adjunctive tool for early risk assessment and empirical treatment planning. However, as this study included only culture-positive S. aureus cases, the findings should be interpreted within this context and may not generalize to culture-negative or non–S. aureus infections. Further multicenter prospective studies are warranted to validate predictive performance, assess broader applicability, and clarify clinical utility.
