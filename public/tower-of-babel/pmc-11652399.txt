Treatment of unruptured intracranial vertebral artery dissection aneurysms with Flow Diverter compared with conventional stent-assisted coiling—a single-center study
Authors: Tongfu Zhang, Weiying Zhong, Donglin Zhou, Yangyang Xu, Maogui Li, Jianfeng Zhuang, Donghai Wang, Wandong Su, Yunyan Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AimFlow diverters (FDs) are being increasingly used off-label for treatment of intracranial vertebral artery dissection aneurysms (IVADAs). However, the safety and efficacy of FDs for unruptured IVADAs remain unclear. This study was performed to investigate whether FDs-alone are safer and more effective than conventional stent-asisted coiling.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 152 patients who underwent endovascular stenting from December 2011 to December 2022. The baseline data, aneurysm characteristics, surgical details, perioperative complications, follow-up angiography, and clinical outcomes were collected and compared between patients who underwent stenting with FD-alone versus conventional stent-asisted coiling. Propensity score matching was also conducted.ResultsAll 152 patients underwent successful endovascular therapy. Forty patients were treated with FDs-alone, and 112 were treated with conventional stent-asisted coiling (including 62 with double stents). The complete occlusion rate was 93.75% in the FD group and 93.61% in the conventional stent group (p = 0.979). The complication rate was 5.00% in the FD group and 8.93% in the conventional stent group (p = 0.653). The in-stent restenosis rate was 3.13% in the FD group and 5.32% in the conventional stent group (p = 0.615). The procedure duration was significantly shorter in the FD than conventional stent group (p = 0.034). After propensity score matching, 37 patients with FDs were successfully matched, and the procedure duration was still significantly shorter in the FD group (p = 0.042).ConclusionFD placement is a safe and effective treatment for IVADAs. It is also a simpler procedure with a shorter operation time than conventional stent placement.Trial registration number: ChiCTR2300074171ClinicalTrials.gov ID:NCT06134557

Introduction

Intracranial vertebral artery dissection aneurysm (IVADA) is a relatively rare type of intracranial aneurysm. It is located in the posterior cranial fossa and surrounded by important structures such as the brain stem, cerebellum, and posterior cranial nerves. IVADA can cause subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) or posterior circulation ischemia [27, 28], and its 5-year mortality rate may reach 80% [16]. Endovascular therapy (EVT) has emerged as a first-line treatment for IVADA [13] and includes both destructive techniques (occlusion of the affected artery) and reconstruction techniques (overlapping stent placement/stent-assisted coiling [21, 29, 34]). As a reconstructive technique, stent placement and stent-assisted coiling have been applied to treat intracranial vertebral artery dissecting aneurysms, with good outcomes being achieved [33]. With the advancement of technology, flow diverters (FDs) have also been applied to the treatment of IVADA and have proven safe and effective [8, 19, 24]. This study was performed to investigate whether FDs are safer and more effective than conventional stents.

The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Qilu Hospital of Shandong University. This study was conducted without the involvement of patients or the general public in the research process. Patient and Public Involvement (PPI) activities were not carried out during the design, implementation, or analysis of this study.

Materials and methods

This was a single-center, retrospective, observational study. This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Qilu Hospital of Shandong University.

Patient selection

We collected the clinical and imaging data from 152 patients who were diagnosed with unruptured IVADA and received EVT in our hospital from December 2011 to December 2022. The exclusion criteria were acute SAH was present, extracranial VADA, aneurysm involving the basilar artery or associated with tortuosity or prolonged dilatation of the vertebrobasilar artery, and IVADA with parent artery occlusion (PAO) or conservative treatment.

In this study, the aneurysm width was defined as the width of the neck of the dilated region. The lesion length was defined as the entire length of the affected portion of the vertebral artery. The vertebral artery was defined as dominant when the contralateral vertebral artery was absent or the ipsilateral vertebral artery was significantly larger than the contralateral one.

Treatment

Antiplatelet drugs (aspirin 100 mg/day and clopidogrel 75 mg/day) were administered for at least 3 days prior to the procedure for patients with unruptured aneurysms. The same antiplatelet drugs (aspirin 100 mg/day and clopidogrel 75 mg/day) were administered for at least 3 months postoperatively, followed by aspirin alone for more than 3 months. All patients were tested for antiplatelet reactivity represented by the kaolin-activated thromboelastography (TEG) after 3 days of maintenance treatment with aspirin and clopidogrel. Arachidonic acid (AA) - induced platelet inhibition rate(AA%) > 50% and adenosine diphosphate-induced platelet inhibition rate (ADP%) > 30% were considered therapeutic. If patients were identified as clopidogrel non-responders by platelet function testing, the therapy was switched to ticagrelor (90 mg twice daily).

The main complications during hospitalization were hemorrhage, infarction, and hydrocephalus requiring shunting. Procedural complications were defined as those that occurred intraoperatively or within 24 h after EVT. Periprocedural complications were defined as those that occurred during hospitalization.

Procedural details

The treatment options were stent-alone placement or stent-assisted coil embolization. Stents were divided into FDs and conventional stents. The optimal treatment strategy was chosen according to the patient’s neurological condition and comorbidities, the vascular architectural features of the aneurysm, and the wishes of the patient and their first-degree relatives. The conventional stents used in this study were the Neuroform (Stryker Neurovascular, Fremont, CA, USA), Enterprise 2 (Cordis Neurovascular, Santa Clara, CA, USA), Solitaire (Medtronic Neurovascular, Irvine, CA, USA), and LVIS (MicroVention Terumo, Aliso Viejo, CA, USA). The FDs used in this study were the Tubridge (MicroPort NeuroTech, Shanghai, China) and Pipeline (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA). The diameter and length of the stents were selected according to the size of the involved blood vessels.

All EVT operations were performed under general anesthesia via the femoral artery approach. Heparin (5000 IU) was given intravenously before surgery and discontinued at the end of surgery. A standard 6–8 F guiding catheter was advanced into the subclavian artery, near the vertebral artery. A 6 F intermediate catheter (Navien; Medtronic) was then inserted into the vertebral artery. A stent microcatheter was used to enter the posterior cerebral artery through the guide wire, and the stent was completely deployed to cover the neck of the aneurysm. For patients undergoing additional coil insertion, a coil microcatheter was placed inside the aneurysm sac. The stent was partially deployed to cover the neck of the aneurysm, after which the coil loosely or densely filled the aneurysm cavity; finally, the stent was completely released. If a single stent was insufficient to alter the intra-aneurysmal hemodynamics, an overlapping stent technique was used. Follow-up

Cerebral angiography (digital subtraction angiography, computed tomography angiography, or magnetic resonance angiography) was performed 6, 12, and 24 months after EVT to confirm aneurysm occlusion. Aneurysmal occlusion during the immediate and final follow-up cerebral angiography examinations was classified as complete occlusion (no contrast agent filling the aneurysm sac) or incomplete occlusion (residual contrast agent filling the aneurysm neck or sac). Recurrence was defined as an aneurysm showing an increased rate of contrast agent filling in the aneurysm sac on follow-up angiography. All imaging studies were independently evaluated by two neurosurgeons with more than 5 years of experience. Any disagreements were resolved by a third neurosurgeon with 10 years of experience. Clinical follow-up was conducted by neurological examination or telephone interview. Clinical outcomes were assessed by the modified Rankin scale score at the last follow-up (0–2 points, good prognosis; 3–6 points, poor prognosis).

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for the statistical analysis. Continuous variables are presented as median (25th–75th percentile), and categorical variables are presented as number (frequency). Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Variables identified as potential predictors in the univariate analysis (p < 0.05) were included in the binary logistic regression analysis (forward) to determine their status as independent predictors. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Propensity score matching (PSM)

PSM is a statistical method used in observational studies to reduce bias by making treated and control groups more comparable, and to estimate the effect of a treatment, intervention, or exposure when conducting observational studies. The main goal of PSM is to create two groups that are balanced with respect to the observed covariates. This balance ensures that any differences observed in outcomes between the treatment and control groups can be attributed more confidently to the treatment itself rather than to differences in baseline characteristics. By matching treated and control subjects on their propensity scores, PSM helps to reduce bias due to confounding variables that may affect treatment assignment and outcomes. When conducted rigorously, propensity score matching can provide results that are closer to those obtained from randomized controlled trials in terms of reducing bias and improving causal inference.

In this study, PSM was used to balance confounding factors between patients with FDs and those with conventional stents. PSM was based on sex, age, smoking and drinking histories, hypertension, hyperglycemia, stenosis, aneurysm length and width, SAH, and location relative to the posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA). We conducted a one-to-one PSM analysis using the nearest-neighbor method with a caliper value of 0.05 to adjust for the imbalance of the aforementioned baseline characteristics between the two groups.

Complications during hospitalization

The rates of testing for therapeutic inhibition of antiplatelet drugs were 45.00% in FDs group and 69.64% in conventional stent group.

In the FD group, two (5.00%) of 40 patients developed ischemic complications, likely due to occlusion of the perforate vessel or brain stem compression following stent placement. Hemorrhagic infarction occurred 2 days after surgery in 1 (2.5%) of 40 patients. The cerebral infarction and hemorrhage were located in the parietal lobe. This maybe associated with inadequate postoperative blood pressure control and the administration of antiplatelet medications.

In the conventional stent group, procedure-related ischemic complications and hemorrhagic occurred in 8 (7.14%) and 1 (0.89%) of 112 patients, respectively (Table 2). Of these 8 patients, one patient presented with sent thrombosis 1 day following the operation. Then Emergency thrombolysis, balloon angioplasty, and additional implantation of an Enterprise device were performed. A mild neurological deficit was noted after the procedure, but the patient died within 2 months. Another patient experienced brainstem infarction 1 day after operation, presenting with left-sided hemiplegia. Four days following the operation, a considerable hemorrhage emerged in the left temporal-occipital lobe, mandating craniotomy for hematoma evacuation and decompressive craniectomy. However, the patient’s family opted to discontinue further treatment, and the patient died after discharge. The procedure was successful in the remaining 6 patients, who exhibited milder symptoms of cerebral infarction and showed significant improvement following treatment. Clinical and radiological follow-up

In all survivors, clinical follow-up was performed for 3 to 48 months (median, 6 months). There were no new adverse clinical outcomes in the FD group and 1(0.89%)in the conventional stent group. Parenchymal hemorrhage during dual antiplatelet therapy occurred in 1patient.

Angiographic follow-up data were available for 128 (84.21%) of the 152 patients. In the FD group, two (6.25%) patients had neck remnants and 1 (3.13%) developed asymptomatic in-stent restenosis (ISR). In the conventional stent group, one (1.06%) patients had neck remnants and 5 (5.32%) developed asymptomatic ISR that eventually developed vertebral artery occlusion. There was no significant difference in the complete occlusion rate (93.75% vs. 93.61%, p = 0.979), ISR rate (3.13% vs. 5.32%, p = 0.615), or complication rate (5.00% vs. 8.93%, p = 0.653).

To eliminate case heterogeneity, we conducted a 1:1 PSM analysis with a caliper value of 0.05. In total, 29 patients with FDs were successfully matched after PSM (Fig. 2). Before PSM, significant differences were found in aneurysm width and the location related to the PICA between the FD and conventional groups (Table 3). Discussion

It is important to explore whether the conventional stents or the implantation of an FD stent is more suitable for treating IVADA. However, there are few clinical reports on the efficacy and safety of these two treatments, and relevant data are currently lacking.

Complications and clinical outcomes

The rate of favorable clinical outcomes was acceptable in our study. The FD group had a lower complication rate (5.00% vs. 8.92%), but the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.653). In general, complications associated with endovascular aneurysm treatment are classified as hemorrhagic or thromboembolic. Ischemic stroke may result from stent wall thrombus formation and occlusion, PAO, or distal thromboembolic events. Because of the rich perforating branches in the basilar artery, more periprocedural complications may occur when using FDs for the treatment of basilar artery aneurysms [14]. However, because few branches and perforating vessels are present in the intracranial segment of the vertebral artery, ischemic complications of FDs in the treatment of IVADA are less likely to occur. In addition, conventional stents often require coiling assistance, whereas FDs generally do not. Notably, the space-occupying effect of the coil may cause compression of perforator vessels and ischemic infarction. In our study, infarction occurred in 2 (5.00%) patients in the FD group and 8 (7.14%) patients in the conventional stent group. In a study of triple stent therapy in patients with IVADA, Chung et al. [7] reported a 100% success rate without treatment-related complications; in their literature review, they found a 3.6% complication rate in patients treated using multiple stents. Catapano et al. [5] also reported success and no complications with the use of FDs in patients with VADA. By contrast, Kallmes et al. [14] reported a 7.3% incidence of ischemic stroke in patients with posterior circulation aneurysms who underwent placement of a Pipeline embolization device (Medtronic), which is consistent with our findings.

One hemorrhagic complications occurred in the FD group, and 1 occurred in the conventional stent group. FDs can promote aneurysm healing by improving the local hemodynamics of aneurysms without the need for intra-aneurysm coil embolization [17, 30], or they may only require loose packing. This greatly simplifies the surgical procedure, reduces the operation time and the risk associated with anesthesia, decreases the compression effect of the aneurysm [2], and minimizes the incidence of hemorrhagic complications.

ISR

ISR was defined as mild (25–50%), moderate (50–75%), or severe (> 75%). The incidence of ISR was 5% in patients treated with FDs and 5.88% in patients treated with conventional stents. In a previous study, the incidence of ISR was 38% in patients treated with the Silk Flow Diverter (Balt, Montmorency, France) and 39% in patients treated with the Pipeline embolization device [9]. Another study of FD placement in patients with VADA reported a 16.6% incidence of ISR [11]. As reported in previous studies, the underlying mechanism is likely related to stent malposition, intimal hyperplasia, antiplatelet agents, and adverse hemodynamics [26]. However, the specific mechanism requires further exploration.

Postoperative PAO

In the present study, PAO developed in 6 patients during follow-up. Fortunately, none of these patients developed any new neurological deficits, and no postoperative strokes occurred in the treated PICA territory. This may have been related to the slow progression and sufficient time for the establishment of collateral circulation. Longer antiplatelet treatment might help to further decrease the incidence of this complication. Becsek et al. [3] reported five cases of PAO in their study of the Pipeline embolization device. They concluded that non-compliance or resistance to antiplatelet therapy and severe ISR might lead to PAO and considered that evaluating antiplatelet effectiveness might be useful to reduce this complication.

Complete occlusion rate

Complete aneurysm occlusion was achieved in 93.75% of patients in the FD group and 93.62% in the conventional stent group among those with available follow-up angiographic data. Regarding the treatment of IVADAs using PEDs, previous studies have reported complete occlusion rates ranging from 71.4–88.9% [6, 10]. These studies showed similar complete occlusion rate to those achieved in our study. Previous studies have also shown that additional coiling can result in a higher complete occlusion rate [4]. Additional coiling not only provides immediate dome protection to prevent aneurysm rebleeding but also interferes with blood flow and improves the complete occlusion rate [22]. However, our study showed no difference in occlusion with or without additional coiling (p = 0.945). This result may have been caused by the heterogeneity of aneurysms or the degree of coil packing (loose vs. dense packing). A review of flow diversion for endovascular treatment of intracranial aneurysms (IA) reported that delayed aneurysm ruptures after flow diversion more likely to occur in large IAs > 10 mm [12]. A multi-center cohort study of embolization of IA with PED indicated that adjunctive coiling had a higher rate of aneurysm occlusion than FD only (P < 0.0001) [15]. Another study also indicated that adjunctive coil embolization with the PED resulted in a significantly lower re-treatment rate compared with PED alone, suggesting an added benefit with adjunctive coil embolization [25]. Mass effect associated with large or giant aneurysms is an intractable problem for traditional endovascular treatments. Preventing recurrence of aneurysms requires dense coiling, which may aggravate the mass effect. However, the FD is a new device that avoids the need for dense coiling. A previous study about variation of mass effect after using FD with adjunctive coil embolization for symptomatic unruptured large and giant IA indicated that use of the FD with adjunctive loose coil embolization might help to alleviate the compressional symptoms caused by IAs [31]. Therefore, we still advocate that adjunctive coiling should be performed if possible, especially in large aneurysms.

Protection of PICA

In our study, no cases of aneurysm recurrence were identified, and vertebral artery aneurysms involving the PICA showed no significant differences complete occlusion (p = 0.358) in the two groups. A previous study of vertebral artery aneurysms treated with stent-assisted coiling showed that 18% of aneurysms involving the PICA recurred, whereas only 5% of those without PICA involvement recurred [35]. In our study, only one patient treated with FD was noted to have experienced the disappearance of the PICA during surgery. However, this change did not manifest in corresponding neurological deficits. Unfortunately, there were no imaging follow-up results available for this case. Mazur et al. [23], Levitt et al. [18], Adeeb et al. [1], and Wu et al. [32] reported that all PICAs with origins covered by an FD remained patent. Additionally, all of these authors suggested that when appropriately sized to the vessel wall and positioned in the vertebral artery, the device may cover the origin of the PICA without impairing flow through the branching artery. These data suggest that the PICA can be effectively and safely preserved when using an FD. A cross-sectional study showed that no new symptoms were observed in patients with a PICA covered by FDs [20]. Because PICA supply the cerebellum and rich perforating vessels that supply vital brainstem structures, coverage of PICA was considered culpable for the infarcts and deaths. The fate of jailed arterial side branches and covered perforators is a critical consideration with FDs placement [12], so protecting of the PICA origin should be the goal have any planned endovascular treatment. Additionally, in terms of conserving the PICA, an FD appears to be more easier and feasible than a conventional stent.

Expectations

In comparison to a conventional stent, an FD has denser mesh, better compliance, and more types and models to choose from. No dense embolization is required, reducing the above-described risks. The surgical operation is relatively simple, resulting in shorter procedure durations and reduced fluoroscopy time, which in turn lowers the radiation dose. The complete occlusion rate is not lower than that of conventional stents, and the incidence of complications is lower. With extension of the follow-up time, the complete occlusion rate may be higher. Compared with the clinoid segment of the internal carotid artery, the intracranial segment of the vertebral artery is relatively straight and smooth, facilitating easy deployment of the FD. Summarizing the above, the FD may become the first choice for the treatment of IVADA. However, larger sample sizes and longer follow-up data are needed to confirm this. Our results provide a useful basis upon which to determine the appropriate treatment strategies for IVADA.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, the generalizability of our findings is limited because the study was conducted retrospectively at a single center and the sample size was small. Second, the patient selection method, including the assessment of the morphological and pathological characteristics of the aneurysms, was not standardized throughout the study period. Third, diverse aneurysms require non-homogeneous treatment modalities. The strategies and techniques used in the procedures changed over time, and the indications for FDs expanded. Fourth, the different types and numbers of stents may have introduced bias, and the collection of patient data in the two groups was not performed during the same period. Fifth, our statistical analysis was limited to known variables. Any associations found are not necessarily causal, and multiple comparisons may have led to spurious findings. However, the significant associations that we identified are clinically plausible. Furthermore, data on aneurysm intraluminal thrombus formation before EVT and platelet function test results were not available for all patients, and the lack of a platelet function test might have been associated with the occurrence of thromboembolic or hemorrhagic events. Finally, angiographic follow-up data were not available for all included patients. The total angiographic follow-up period was not uniform, and it was insufficient for observation of aneurysm occlusion. All the above factors may limit the generalizability of our findings.

Conclusion

The FD is a safe and effective treatment for unruptured IVADA. The procedure is simpler and the procedure duration is shorter than that of conventional stents.
