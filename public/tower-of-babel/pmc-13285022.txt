Cross-border molecular epidemiology of Plasmodium falciparum drug susceptibility: insights from the Uganda–Democratic Republic of the Congo border
Authors: Patrick Mamona Dilufwasayo, Betty Balikagala, Moise Agenowun Uchopa, Jacques Amanga Amaa, Dieudonné Mumba Ngoyi, Toshihiro Mita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCross-border parasite movement drives resistance emergence in endemic regions. In Uganda, Plasmodium falciparum with artemisinin partial resistance emerged early and spread rapidly, making it a focal point for surveillance. In northeastern Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), a key trade corridor bordering Uganda, we investigated molecular markers of artemisinin partial resistance and resistance to partner drugs used in artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs). Monitoring these resistance markers in DRC is essential for guiding regional containment efforts.MethodsFrom May to June 2023, we conducted active and passive malaria case detection in 20 villages and a central hospital, respectively. Molecular analyses included copy number variation (CNV) in pfmdr1 and pfpm2/3, and genotyping of pfkelch13, pfmdr1, and pfcrt. For pfmdr1 CNV determination, we adopted a quantitative real-time PCR threshold of 1.4, recently proposed for parasite populations with frequent multiclonal infections. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was performed on hospital-derived samples to assess parasite relatedness and trace resistance spread.ResultsAmong the 536 individuals sampled from the villages, 49.8% tested positive for P. falciparum by PCR. The pfmdr1 N86 allele was fixed, and 50.3% of samples carried the NFD haplotype (amino acid positions 86, 184, and 1246), a marker associated with reduced lumefantrine susceptibility in Africa. Pfmdr1 CNV was detected in 4.4% of samples using the conventional threshold (≥ 1.5), increasing to 11.7% with the 1.4 threshold. Pfpm2/3 CNVs, which may influence piperaquine susceptibility, were identified in 7.0% of samples. The validated pfkelch13 mutations A675V and C469Y, associated with artemisinin partial resistance, were detected in 4.3% and 1.3% of village samples, respectively. Principal component analysis of WGS data showed that DRC parasites carrying A675V or C469Y clustered closely with Ugandan strains, supporting cross-border spread of resistant parasites between DRC and Uganda.ConclusionOur findings identified the presence of molecular markers associated with artemisinin partial resistance and reduced susceptibility to lumefantrine in northeastern DRC, along with evidence of cross-border parasite migration from Uganda. Given opposing selection pressures of lumefantrine and amodiaquine on pfmdr1 and pfcrt, alternative ACTs such as artesunate-amodiaquine may merit further evaluation. To sustain ACT efficacy, integrated genomic surveillance, clinical monitoring, and coordinated regional policy responses are essential.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-026-05929-6.Keywords: Plasmodium falciparum; Drug resistance; pfkelch13; pfmdr1; Pfcrt, plasmepsin 2/3; Molecular surveillance

Introduction

Malaria remains a major public health challenge in Africa, accounting for an estimated 246 million cases and 569,000 deaths in 2023 [1]. The emergence and spread of Plasmodium falciparum parasites exhibiting partial resistance to artemisinin, clinically characterized by delayed parasite clearance following treatment, poses a serious threat to current malaria control and elimination efforts. Initially identified in Southeast Asia [2], artemisinin partial resistance has evolved in the context of pre-existing resistance to partner drugs in artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs), contributing to the development of multidrug-resistant strains [3]. In recent years, P. falciparum parasites harboring mutations in the pfkelch13 gene, a molecular marker of artemisinin partial resistance, have been reported in multiple East African countries. Mutations such as A675V, C469Y, R561H, and P441L have been detected in northern Uganda [4–6], while A675V, R561H, C469F, C469Y, P441L, G449A, and P574L have been identified in Rwanda [5, 7–9]. Additional reports include R561H in Tanzania [10] and R622I in Eritrea, Ethiopia and Sudan [5, 11].

In addition to ACT partner drug resistance, pfmdr1 copy number variation (CNV) is a well-established molecular marker of mefloquine resistance in Southeast Asia [2, 12, 13]. In Africa, however, its significance is recognized in a distinct context, its potential association with reduced susceptibility to lumefantrine, a widely used ACT partner drug. Experimental reduction of pfmdr1 copy number from two to one has been shown to lower the IC₅₀ of mefloquine threefold and lumefantrine four- to fivefold, indicating increased drug susceptibility [14]. Field studies have identified pfmdr1 CNV as a candidate marker for declining lumefantrine efficacy [15, 16]. Importantly, Fancony et al. [16] cautioned that using the ≥ 1.5 CNV threshold may underestimate pfmdr1 CNV in multiclonal infections common in Africa. Accordingly, they proposed a revised threshold of ≥ 1.4, showing a stronger correlation with reduced artemether-lumefantrine efficacy in Angola. Threshold selection critically influences CNV prevalence estimates and resistance pattern interpretation in high-transmission settings. In regions with frequent multiclonal infections, context-adapted thresholds are essential for accurate pfmdr1 CNV surveillance.

The Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) bears the second-highest malaria burden globally [1] and the leading cause of death in the DRC [17], with a prevalence of 39% among children aged 6–59 months [18]. Over 98% of malaria infections are caused by P. falciparum, with Anopheles gambiae and A. funestus serving as the primary mosquito vectors [19]. Since 2005, artesunate-amodiaquine has served as the first-line treatment for uncomplicated malaria, followed by the introduction of artemether-lumefantrine in 2011 and, more recently, artesunate-pyronaridine [20, 21]. Deployment of ACTs has driven increased prevalence of pfmdr1 NFD haplotypes (positions 86, 184, 1246; substitutions underlined), associated with diminished lumefantrine efficacy [22–25]. In addition, validated pfkelch13 mutations linked to artemisinin partial resistance have recently been identified at low prevalence in eastern DRC [24–26].

In the present study, we applied a recently proposed, more sensitive threshold for pfmdr1 CNV assessment, an approach particularly relevant in northeastern DRC where multiclonal infections are frequent [16, 27]. Moreover, the DRC shares borders with Uganda and Rwanda, countries where artemisinin partial resistance is already established, raising concerns about the cross-border spread of resistant genotypes. To address these concerns, we conducted a molecular epidemiological investigation in local villages and the general hospital in Aru, northeastern DRC, a key trade corridor connecting to northwestern Uganda, to assess the presence and distribution of P. falciparum genotypes associated with artemisinin partial resistance and reduced ACT partner drug susceptibility.

Methods

Study design and site

This molecular epidemiological study was conducted in Aru Territory, situated in northeastern Ituri Province, DRC. Ituri Province borders South Sudan to the north and Uganda to the east (Fig. 1A). Two sampling strategies were employed: a community-based approach (active case detection) targeting asymptomatic individuals, and a hospital-based approach (passive case detection) targeting symptomatic malaria patients. The community-based study was conducted from May 22 to June 11, 2023, and the hospital-based study took place from May 22 to September 20, 2023 at Aru General Hospital. The region has endured decades of armed conflict, leading to large-scale population displacement and weakened public health infrastructure [28]. The region experiences a dry season from November to February and a rainy season from March to October, with year-round malaria transmission [29].

Study area. The Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) within the African continent (A1). Aru Territory of the DRC, with light grey areas indicating the selected sub-divisions (A2). Distribution of Plasmodium species (B) and the prevalence of submicroscopic malaria infections (C)

Sample size, sampling strategy and patient selection

The sample size for the community study was determined using the formula: (Zα/2)2⋅p⋅(1 − p)/E2 [30], where p = prevalence of asymptomatic malaria in DRC (51%) [31], Zα/2 = z score corresponding to 95% confidence level (1.96), and E = margin of error (0.05). This yielded 384 individuals. Adjustments were made to account for intra-household correlation using a design effect [DEFF = 1 + (n − 1) x ICC], where n = average household size (5 individuals) and ICC (intra-cluster correlation coefficient) = 0.02 [32], resulting in an adjusted sample size of 415 individuals. Including a 20% non-response rate yielded a final target sample size of 498 individuals.

A multi-stage cluster sampling was employed. Seven of the 25 sub-divisions were selected based on their proximity to the Ugandan border where artemisinin partial resistance has been reported [33] (Fig. 1A). All villages within the seven selected sub-divisions were listed, and study sites were chosen through systematic random sampling, selecting every fifth village until 20 were included. In each selected village, households were sampled by selecting every tenth household, continuing until five households were enrolled (totaling 100 households). Individuals were eligible for inclusion if they were aged ≥ 6 months, had not used antimalarial drugs in the preceding 2 weeks, and had not experienced fever within the past 24 h.

In addition to the community-based survey, 50 P. falciparum-positive patients were recruited from Aru General Hospital. These hospital participants met the same inclusion criteria as the community participants but were additionally required to have a minimum parasite density of 10,000 parasites/μL to ensure sufficient parasite DNA yield for next-generation sequencing.

Data collection procedures

A questionnaire was administered to collect information regarding medical history and other malaria-related symptoms. Finger-prick blood samples were collected for malaria screening using rapid diagnostic tests (SD-BIOLINE® Malaria Ag P.f.) [34]. Samples were also smeared on glass slides for microscopy and spotted onto chromatography filter paper (ET31CHR; Whatman Limited, Kent, UK) genetic studies. For hospital participants, 5 mL of venous blood was collected, and leukocytes were removed using Acrodisc filters prior to spotting onto filter paper, as previously described [4]. Leukocyte depletion was carried out at the Biomedical Laboratory of Aru General Hospital (Aru, Ituri Province, DRC), while all downstream molecular analyses were performed at the Department of Tropical Medicine and Parasitology, Juntendo University, Tokyo, Japan.

Microscopic and molecular diagnosis of malaria parasites

Thick and thin blood smears were stained with 2% Giemsa for 45 min. Parasite density was determined from thick smears by counting the number of asexual parasites against 200 leukocytes, extending to 500 leukocytes when the initial count yielded fewer than 100 trophozoites/µL. Gametocyte density was estimated by counting gametocytes per 500 leukocytes. Smears were considered negative if no parasites were detected after examination of 100 fields at high magnification [35]. Thin smears were used to identify parasite species.

For DNA extraction, a quarter blood spot from filter paper was used for all samples. Community-based samples were processed using the Kaneka Easy DNA Extraction Kit version 2 (Kaneka Corporation, Osaka, Japan), while hospital-based samples were processed using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen). Nested PCR for genus and species-specific malaria detection was conducted following previously described protocols [36]. Primer sequences and PCR conditions are detailed in Additional files 1 and 2.

Copy number estimation and genotyping of drug-resistance genes

Copy number variation in pfmdr1 and plasmepsin 2/3 were assessed using probe-based quantitative real-time PCR assay, following standardized protocols (WWARN 2011) [37]. Relative copy number was calculated using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method, using the 3D7 as the calibrator, β-tubulin as the housekeeping gene, and Dd2 as the multi-copy positive control. Pfmdr1 CNV was determined using a threshold of ≥ 1.5, based on the WWARN cutoff [38], and ≥ 1.4 as suggested for African populations [16]. Plasmepsin 2/3 CNV was confirmed at cutoff ≥ 1.5 [39].

Polymorphisms in pfkelch13, pfcrt and pfmdr1 were analyzed following previously described protocols [4, 40–42]. The pfkelch13 propeller domain was amplified and sequenced as previously described [4, 42], with some modifications. Specifically, primers were redesigned to generate shorter fragments: 1093 bp for the primary PCR and 983 bp for the nested PCR. Pfmdr1 codons 86, 184 and pfcrt codons 72–76, were analyzed using Sanger sequencing while pfmdr1 codons 1034, 1042, and 1246 analyzed by restriction fragment length polymorphism, as previously described [41, 43]. Detailed information on primers, restriction enzymes, and PCR conditions is provided in Additional files 1 and 2.

Whole-genome sequencing and variant detection

Libraries were prepared using the NEBNext® Ultra™ II DNA PCR-free Library Prep Kit for Illumina®. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, generating 150 bp paired-end reads. Approximately 2 Gb of raw sequencing data were produced per sample. For comparative analyses, whole-genome sequencing data from the DRC cohort (n = 50) were analyzed alongside publicly available short‑read data from East Africa. These included samples from Ethiopia (n = 10), Kenya (n = 8), Malawi (n = 10), and Tanzania (n = 10), retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information Sequence Read Archive (https: ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra). Accession numbers are provided in Additional file 3. Whole-genome data from Ugandan isolates (n = 46) were previously obtained as part of a comprehensive malaria study conducted in Gulu between 2018 and 2019 [4].

Reads were aligned to the P. falciparum 3D7 reference (Pf3D7 v3) with BWA-MEM. Per-sample gVCFs were generated using GATK HaplotypeCaller (–sample-ploidy 1; GATK v4.6.2.0, https://github.com/broadinstitute/gatk/releases) and jointly genotyped across all samples. Variant Quality Score Recalibration (VQSR) was trained on P. falciparum genetic cross datasets (3D7 × HB3, 7G8 × GB4, HB3 × DD2). INDELs were hard-filtered and subsequently merged with recalibrated SNPs. By default, analyses used PASS biallelic SNPs with depth ≥ 5 and genotype quality ≥ 20. Variants were annotated with snpEff (https://pcingola.github.io/SnpEff/).

To examine population structure across the DRC and comparators populations, we performed principal component analysis (PCA). After initial quality control, we retained biallelic SNPs with minor allele frequency > 1% and applied missingness filters in PLINK (–mind 0.5, –geno 0.5). PCA results were visualized in R using the ggplot2 package (R version 4.4.2; available at https://cran.r-project.org/bin/windows/base/).

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using R-4.4.2. Statistical associations and categorical differences were evaluated using the chi-square test or, when expected counts were low, Fisher’s exact test. Trends across categories were assessed using the Cochran–Armitage test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Ethical considerations

Approval was obtained from Juntendo University ethical committee (Approval number: E23-0023) and the National Health Ethics Committee (CNES) of the DRC (Approval number: 416/CNES/BN/PMMF/2022). Informed consent and/or assent (for children aged ≥ 7 years) were obtained from all participants.

Results

Studied individuals

A total of 586 individuals were enrolled, comprising 536 community participants from 20 studied villages and 50 hospital patients. The mean age was 11.8 years among community participants and 14.9 years among hospital patients (Table 1). Malaria prevalence in the community was high, particularly in rural villages: 49.1% by RDT, 44.9% by microscopy, and 50.6% by PCR, respectively (Table 1 and Additional file 4). P. falciparum was the predominant species (91.5%) (Fig. 1B). Submicroscopic infections were detected in 10% of malaria cases (n = 27; Fig. 1C). The mean parasite density among community participants was 916 parasites/μL (geometric standard deviation [GSD] ± 9.5) and 29,439 parasites/μL (GSD ± 2.2) in hospital patients (Table 1). Malaria prevalence was highest among school-aged children (ages 6–17) at 71.7%, which is a significantly higher than in other age groups: 56.5% in children aged 0–5 years (P < 0.05) and 27% among those aged ≥ 18 (P < 0.001) (Table 1, Additional file 5).

Pfmdr1 and plasmepsin 2/3 copy number variants

Copy number estimation was successful for pfmdr1 (76.8%, 205/267), plasmepsin 2 (81.6%, 218/267) and plasmepsin 3 (82.0%, 219/267) in community individuals, and for all 50 hospital patients. Pfmdr1 CNV parasites were identified in 4.4% (9/205) of community samples when applying the ≥ 1.5 WWARN threshold. However, using the ≥ 1.4 threshold recommended for African parasite populations [16], the prevalence increased to 11.7% (24/205) (Additional file 6A). All hospital samples exhibited single-copy pfmdr1 (Additional file 6B). Adults (> 18 years) showed a higher prevalence of pfmdr1 CNV parasites (12.2%) compared to younger age groups (9.9% in school-aged children and 6.8% under 5 years), but this difference was not statistically significant.

For plasmepsin 2, CNV parasites were detected in 3.7% of community samples and 2.0% of hospital samples (Additional file 7A and B). Plasmepsin 3 CNV parasites were found in 3.7% of community samples but not in hospital samples (Additional file 7C and D). No parasites harbored CNV in both plasmepsin 2 and 3. Therefore, the overall prevalence of plasmepsin 2/3 CNV (defined as CNV in either plasmepsin 2 or 3) was 7.0%.

Pfkelch13 mutations

The pfkelch13 gene was successfully sequenced in 283 of 317 isolates (89%), with sequencing failures occurring only in community samples due to extremely low parasite densities. Two validated artemisinin resistance mutations, A675V and C469Y, were detected at 4.3% and 1.3% in community samples, and at 4.0% and 2.0% in hospital samples, respectively (Fig. 2). Children aged 0–5 years exhibited the highest observed prevalence (6.4%) of the validated pfkelch13 mutations A675V and C469Y (Additional file 8).

Genetic structure and cross-border connectivity of pfkelch13 mutant lineages

PCA was conducted on 49 isolates using high-quality biallelic SNPs derived from both WGS and chromosome 13 data (Fig. 3). Wild-type pfkelch13 isolates from the DRC primarily clustered with isolates from East Africa. Among the three DRC pfkelch13 variants (two A675V and one C469Y), all aligned with Ugandan counterparts. The C469Y variant fell within the broader East African continuum, while the A675V variants were distributed across two clusters: one overlapping with the main East African group and the other forming a distinct A675V-associated cluster. Both clusters included Ugandan isolates, suggesting that the DRC variants reflect existing diversity already present in Uganda, rather than representing novel emergence within DRC. This clustering structure was more clearly resolved in the PCA based on chromosome 13 variants, further supporting Uganda as the likely source of pfkelch13 variant lineages found in northeastern DRC.

Pfmdr1 and Pfcrt

For pfmdr1, wild-type alleles associated with lumefantrine susceptibility (N86 and D1246) were fixed or nearly fixed (Fig. 4, Additional file 9). The NFD haplotype was detected in 50.3% (127/252) of community samples, followed by NYD in 38.5%. A similar trend was observed in hospital samples. For pfcrt, K76T mutation was detected in % of community and 20.0% of hospital samples (Fig. 4). Its prevalence was significantly higher in children aged 0–5 years (40.0%) compared to 17.4% in those aged 6–17 and 19.4% in adults ≥ 18 years (p = 0.001), indicating a marked age-related difference.

Prevalence of pfmdr1 and plasmepsin 2/3 copy number variations and pfmdr1 and pfcrt genotypes. Mixed genotype infections were classified as mutants

Combined resistance marker analysis

No individual harbored CNVs in both pfmdr1 and plasmepsin 2/3, in addition to carrying the pfkelch13 mutations C469Y or A675V (Table 2). This indicates that no parasites were found to simultaneously carry markers of artemisinin partial resistance and those associated with reduced susceptibility to both lumefantrine and piperaquine. However, analysis of combined pfcrt and pfmdr1 haplotypes revealed that 37% of the cases carried the K-NFD type (Additional file 10), which has been associated with reduced susceptibility to lumefantrine. Among these, a validated marker of artemisinin partial resistance (A675V) was detected in one individual (Table 2), indicating the presence of a rare combination of genetic features associated with artemisinin partial resistance and reduced susceptibility to lumefantrine.

Gametocyte prevalence and carriage among studied populations

Gametocyte positivity and density were assessed among P. falciparum-positive individuals (Table 1, Fig. 5A). Among community participants, 15% (40/267) carried gametocytes, with a mean density of 30.2/μL. Gametocytes were more frequently detected in school-aged children, although no significant age-related differences were observed. Notably, gametocyte positivity declined with increasing parasite density (Cochran–Armitage test, p = 0.021). Gametocytes were detected in 20% of individuals with very low (0–99/μL) and low (100–999/μL) parasite densities, but in only 5% of those with high-density infections (> 10,000/μL). We next examined gametocyte positivity across pfkelch13 genotypes to assess potential associations with artemisinin partial resistance. Parasites carrying C469Y or A675V mutations exhibited significantly higher gametocyte positivity compared to wild-type strains (38.5% vs. 11.4%, p = 0.016; Fig. 5B).

Discussion

This study examined P. falciparum drug resistance in northeastern DRC, a key border region near Uganda where surveillance data remain scarce despite two decades of ACT use. A striking finding was the high prevalence of pfmdr1 CNV, based on the ≥ 1.4 threshold optimized for African settings, along with a high frequency of the pfmdr1 NFD haplotype. Furthermore, pfkelch13 A675V and C469Y variants were more prevalent than in other regions in DRC [24, 26], with WGS suggesting possible cross-border spread from Uganda.

The prevalence of pfmdr1 CNVs has been scarcely investigated in the DRC, with a single 2014 report indicating a low frequency of approximately 1% in North kivu [44]. Our present study found a higher prevalence of 4.4% using the canonical WWARN resistance threshold of ≥ 1.5 [38]. However, this threshold assumes monoclonal infections and may underestimate CNV prevalence in high transmission settings such as DRC, where multiclonal infections are common [27]. Applying a revised ≥ 1.4 threshold for African settings increased CNV prevalence to 12%.

Although pfmdr1 CNV is a known marker of mefloquine resistance in Southeast Asia, its relevance in Africa remains uncertain due to limited mefloquine use. Given the widespread use of artemether-lumefantrine, its potential impact on lumefantrine susceptibility warrants attention. Genetic disruption of one pfmdr1 copy in the drug-resistant FCB line, which harbors two copies, has been reported to reduce lumefantrine IC₅₀ [14]. Clinical data from Angola further indicate reduced artemether-lumefantrine efficacy in cases carrying pfmdr1 CNVs [16]. However, the observed prevalence (12%) in our study was lower than the 35% reported in Northern Angola, a representative site for declining artemether-lumefantrine effectiveness in Africa [16, 45]. Price et al. [46] reported that although plasma lumefantrine concentration is the primary determinant of therapeutic efficacy, pfmdr1 CNVs have been associated with reduced lumefantrine susceptibility, particularly under subtherapeutic conditions. As a slow-acting drug, lumefantrine exhibits a prolonged subtherapeutic tail [47], potentially facilitating the persistence of pfmdr1 CNV-harboring parasites. These dynamics further underscore the importance of monitoring pfmdr1 CNVs using thresholds tailored to African transmission settings.

We observed that 7.0% of parasites carried multiple copies of plasmepsin 2/3. Overall, the prevalence of plasmepsin 2/3 CNV in Africa remains lower than in Southeast Asia [48]. The average prevalence across African countries is estimated at 8%, although exceptionally high levels (68%) have been reported in Equatorial Guinea between 2017 and 2019 [49]. Comparable prevalence rates have been documented in countries neighboring the DRC: Uganda (10–14% in the east, 0.6% in the north), Kenya (plasmepsin 2: 1.7–3.2%; plasmepsin 3: 4.0–10.1%), Tanzania (10%), Mozambique (3.4–4.9%), and Angola (9.8%) [50–55]. Piperaquine serves as an alternative first-line therapy to artemether-lumefantrine in these countries, except Angola. CNV of plasmepsin 2/3 has been associated with reduced susceptibility to piperaquine [3, 39], suggesting that drug pressure may contribute to its selection. Yet piperaquine is not part of the DRC’s official malaria treatment policy [56], the presence of these amplified variants may reflect cross-border importation from neighboring countries such as Uganda, where piperaquine is in use.

Our pfmdr1 haplotype analysis revealed that NFD haplotype reached a prevalence of 51%, slightly exceeding prior DRC reports (24.1%–48%) [22–25]. Similar patterns have been observed in northern Uganda and other East African countries [6, 43, 57]. The NFD haplotype has been associated with reduced lumefantrine treatment efficacy [58]. Given that artemether-lumefantrine has served as first-line therapy for uncomplicated malaria for nearly two decades in both Uganda and the DRC, sustained lumefantrine selection pressure may have contributed to these genetic patterns.

The K76T was widely selected across malaria-endemic regions in Africa due to extensive chloroquine use. However, the wild-type allele has re-emerged in many of these regions following the official withdrawal of chloroquine and the subsequent adoption of ACTs containing lumefantrine. In contrast, the K76T mutation remains prevalent in several parts of the DRC [23, 25, 59, 60], although eastern regions of the country predominantly harbor the wild-type pfcrt K76 allele [25, 60]. The pfcrt K76T mutation was detected at a prevalence of 26% in our study area. Whether the observed prevalence reflects a declining trend following chloroquine withdrawal remains unclear, as no prior data are available for this site. Notably, the K76T mutation has been associated with reduced sensitivity to amodiaquine. Given the mutation’s association with reduced amodiaquine sensitivity and the continued use of artesunate-amodiaquine in the DRC, ongoing drug pressure may contribute to the persistence of pfcrt K76T in this region.

Pfkelch13 C469Y and A675V mutations, validated markers of artemisinin resistance, were observed in 6% of cases, higher than rates reported in other regions of the DRC [24, 26]. The present study site lies along an active trade corridor connecting to northwestern Uganda, where prevalences of these mutations exceeding 15% have been reported [6, 61–63]. Population genetic analysis using WGS revealed a shared cluster of C469Y and A675V variants between Congolese and Ugandan samples. This geographic proximity suggests that comparable drug selection pressures, along with human and mosquito movement across the border, may have facilitated the spread of this genetic combination.

The majority (76%) of pfkelch13 mutant parasites carried both pfmdr1 NFD and pfcrt K76, genotypes associated with reduced lumefantrine susceptibility. This suggests that sustained artemether-lumefantrine pressure may have selected for parasites with decreased lumefantrine sensitivity, which subsequently acquired pfkelch13 mutations conferring artemisinin partial resistance. Such a trajectory raises concern for emerging multidrug-resistant parasites with potential to compromise ACT efficacy. Notably, one parasite harbored pfmdr1 CNV in combination with pfkelch13 A675V, pfcrt K76, and pfmdr1 NFD. This specific combination of mutations could, in principle, be associated with reduced artemether–lumefantrine susceptibility; however, without clinical outcome data, its actual impact on treatment efficacy cannot be determined. Nonetheless, its detection highlights the importance of continued genomic surveillance to monitor the potential emergence of multidrug-resistant parasite profiles.

Gametocyte positivity was significantly higher in pfkelch13 mutant parasites (38%) compared to wild-type parasites (11.4%). Although no field-based studies have specifically examined the association between pfkelch13 C469Y and A675V mutations and gametocyte production, recent findings suggest that pfkelch13 genotype may influence parasite transmission dynamics in complex and context-dependent ways [64]. For example, one African isolate carrying a pfkelch13 wild-type exhibited low gametocyte commitment yet retained efficient transmission to African mosquitoes [64], underscoring the multifactorial nature of parasite transmission dynamics. These observations indicate that transmission success is not solely determined by gametocyte density, but is likely shaped by a combination of factors, including the pfkelch13 genotype, its associated fitness cost, drug pressure, parasite genetic background, and gametocyte developmental stage. However, given the limited sample size and the absence of follow-up mosquito infectivity assessments in our study, the implications of elevated gametocyte carriage among pfkelch13 mutant parasites remain difficult to interpret, warranting further investigation in larger field studies.

In conclusion, our data reveal molecular signatures of declining susceptibility to artemisinin and lumefantrine in northeastern DRC, a region bordering northwestern Uganda. This study also demonstrated frequent cross-border parasite migration. Artemether-lumefantrine is the most widely used among the DRC’s three first-line ACTs in this area. However, the present findings, together with reports of reduced susceptibility in neighbouring Uganda [63], suggest that alternative ACTs might be needed to mitigate resistance development and treatment failure. Artesunate-amodiaquine could represent one such option, as lumefantrine and amodiaquine exert opposing selection pressures on pfmdr1 and pfcrt variants [58]. Nonetheless, the continued presence of the pfcrt K76T mutation across the DRC, associated with reduced amodiaquine efficacy, highlights the need for caution in broadly promoting this regimen. To preserve ACT efficacy and inform treatment policy, genomic surveillance should be complemented by clinical monitoring, in vitro testing, and strengthened cross-border coordination. Depending on the outcomes of such integrated efforts, a shift toward artesunate-amodiaquine may be justified in the future.
