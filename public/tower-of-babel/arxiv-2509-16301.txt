TF-DWGNet: A Directed Weighted Graph Neural Network with Tensor Fusion for Multi-Omics Cancer Subtype Classification Conference: arXiv; arXiv; 2025
Tiantian Yang, Zhiqian Chen

Abstract
. Integration and analysis of multi-omics data provide valuable insights for cancer subtype classification. However, such data are inherently heterogeneous, high-dimensional, and exhibit complex intra- and inter-modality dependencies. Recent advances in graph neural networks (GNNs) offer powerful tools for modeling such structure. Yet, most existing methods rely on prior knowledge or predefined similarity networks to construct graphs, which are often undirected or unweighted, failing to capture the directionality and strength of biological interactions. Interpretability at both the modality and feature levels also remains limited. To address these challenges, we propose TF-DWGNet , a novel G raph Neural Net work framework that combines tree-based D irected W eighted graph construction with T ensor F usion for multiclass cancer subtype classification. TF-DWGNet introduces two key innovations: a supervised tree-based approach for constructing directed, weighted graphs tailored to each omics modality, and a tensor fusion mechanism that captures unimodal, bimodal, and trimodal interactions using low-rank decomposition for efficiency. TF-DWGNet enables modality-specific representation learning, joint embedding fusion, and interpretable subtype prediction. Experiments on real-world cancer datasets show that TF-DWGNet consistently outperforms state-of-the-art baselines across multiple metrics and statistical tests. Moreover, it provides biologically meaningful insights by ranking influential features and modalities. These results highlight TF-DWGNet’s potential for effective and interpretable multi-omics integration in cancer research.



1.
Introduction

Cancer remains a major global health challenge, marked by uncontrolled cell growth, genetic instability, and complex molecular alterations that vary across cancer types and among individual patients. This biological heterogeneity underscores the importance of accurate cancer subtype classification, which is central to precision medicine. Subtype classification enables personalized treatment by leveraging individual molecular profiles. However, reliable classification remains difficult due to biological complexity.
The advent of high-throughput technologies enables simultaneous profiling of multiple omics layers, such as DNA methylation, mRNA expression, and miRNA expression, providing a comprehensive molecular view of tumors
(
Subramanian et al. 2020
;
Hood and Rowen 2013
)
. These modalities offer complementary biological signals that, when effectively integrated, can reveal disease mechanisms inaccessible to single-omics analyses. Despite these advantages, multi-omics data integration faces significant challenges, including high dimensionality, heterogeneity, and complex cross-modal interactions that conventional methods struggle to model
(
Waqas et al. 2024
)
. Addressing these issues requires computational methods that capture the hierarchical, directional nature of biological systems while maintaining interpretability.

Classical machine learning and deep learning approaches, such as Random Forest (RF)
(
Breiman 2001
)
, eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)
(
Chen and Guestrin 2016
)
, and deep feedforward networks (DFNs)
(
Lecun et al. 2015
;
Goodfellow et al. 2016
)
, have been widely applied to omics data analysis. However, they often assume Euclidean data structures and struggle with omics data due to limited sample sizes, high dimensionality, and lack of natural interpretability
(
Ayman et al. 2023
;
Zaghlool and Attallah 2022
)
.
Geometric deep learning, especially graph neural networks (GNNs), offers a principled framework for modeling non-Euclidean data structures such as biological networks
(
Bronstein et al. 2017
;
Zhang et al. 2020
)
. GNNs learn node representations by aggregating information from local graph neighborhoods, capturing local and global dependencies
(
Wu et al. 2020
;
Zhou et al. 2020
)
. Models such as graph convolutional networks (GCNs)
(
Kipf and Welling 2017
)
, graph attention networks (GATs)
(
Petar et al. 2018
)
, graph isomorphism networks (GINs)
(
Xu et al. 2019
)
, and GraphSAGE
(
Hamilton et al. 2017
)
have substantially enhanced the representational capacity and scalability of GNNs. These models increasingly succeed in biomedical domains such as drug discovery and disease classification
(
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Johnson et al. 2024
)
. GNNs enable topological modeling in multi-omics analysis by treating biological components as nodes and their interactions as edges. Multimodal fusion methods such as the encoder-decoder architectures, attention mechanisms, and tensor factorization have also been explored to address modality heterogeneity
(
Zhao et al. 2024b
;
Waqas et al. 2024
)
. Among these, tensor-based approaches are particularly effective for modeling higher-order modality interactions due to their ability to represent multi-way relationships compactly. While these techniques have demonstrated promise, they fail to fully leverage multi-omics complexity.

Existing GNN-based approaches for multi-omics integration exhibit three key limitations:
(1)
Suboptimal Graph Construction
: Current methods typically construct graphs based on prior biological knowledge or simple correlation metrics (e.g., correlation, cosine similarity), resulting in undirected and unweighted networks that overlook task-specific directionality and varying strength of biological interactions, both crucial for capturing true biological relationships in cancer subtyping.
(2)
Incomplete Multi-Omics Integration
: Integration strategies are often limited to basic concatenation or pairwise modeling, failing to capture higher-order relationships (e.g., trimodal interactions) that may reveal synergistic or compensatory mechanisms among omics modalities.
(3)
Limited Model Interpretability
: Most interpretation methods rely on post hoc analyses such as feature ablation or attention weights, which are computationally intensive and may not reliably reflect true biological importance during model decisions.

To address these challenges, we propose
TF-DWGNet
, a novel GNN-based framework for interpretable multi-omics integration and cancer subtype classification. Our contributions are:

•

Supervised Directed Weighted Graph Construction
: We leverage XGBoost’s tree-splitting patterns to construct biologically meaningful, task-specific, and variable-sized graphs that capture directional relationships and interaction strengths while enabling dimension reduction.

•

Tensor Fusion for Unimodal, Bimodal, and Trimodal Interactions
: We implement efficient low-rank decomposed tensor operations to comprehensively model unimodal, bimodal, and trimodal interactions across all omics modalities, significantly reducing computational complexity without sacrificing expressive power.

•

Built-in Interpretability Mechanism
: Our architecture inherently produces feature- and modality-level importance scores, enabling biologically meaningful interpretations without requiring ablation studies or additional post-processing.

2.
Related Work

Various deep learning methods have been proposed for modeling multi-omics data.
For example, MOCAT
(
Yao et al. 2024
)
integrates DNA methylation, mRNA, and miRNA data using an autoencoder, three omics-specific classifiers, a multi-head attention mechanism, and a confidence network (ConfNet). However, it cannot capture topological relationships among features, causing structural information loss. Its interpretability also relies on computationally intensive ablation studies.
CLCLSA
(
Zhao et al. 2024a
)
targets incomplete multi-omics scenarios using cross-omics autoencoders for missing data imputation, contrastive learning to enhance mutual information across modalities, and self-attention for feature and modality selection. However, it does not incorporate graph-based modeling to capture topological dependencies.
Graph neural networks
have been increasingly applied to omics data to preserve structural relationships.
For example, forgenet
(
Kong and Yu 2020
)
constructs a supervised directed graph using a tree ensemble model. However, it does not consider edge frequency (weights), applies no dimensionality reduction, and is limited to single omics.
Similarly, DMGCN
(
Yang et al. 2021
)
applies GCNs to multiple networks derived from a single omics source, but uses simple concatenation without leveraging the richness of multi-omics.

Several GNN-based frameworks have been developed to address these limitations for multi-omics integration in classification and survival prediction.
MOGONET
(
Wang et al. 2021
)
uses GCNs to process mRNA, methylation, and miRNA data, and employs a cross-omics discovery tensor and a view correlation discovery network for final prediction. However, it models only trimodal interactions, overlooking unimodal and bimodal relationships. Its undirected graphs are built from cosine similarity matrices controlled by a tunable edge density hyperparameter, and feature importance is derived from costly ablation experiments.
MODILM
(
Zhong et al. 2023
)
follows a similar pipeline but replaces GCN with graph attention networks (GATs) for improved intra-omics modeling.
SUPREME
(
Kesimoglu and Bozdag 2023
)
integrates seven omics types using GCNs on patient similarity networks for cancer subtype prediction and survival analysis. Yet, it relies on basic concatenation and does not explicitly capture cross-modal interactions. Reliance on ablation for omics selection limits interpretability and scalability.
MOGAT
(
Tanvir et al. 2024
)
shares a similar architecture with SUPREME, using GATs for survival analysis and exploring different omics combinations via concatenation. However, it lacks cross-modal interaction modeling and biomarker-level interpretability.
Other approaches include DeepMoIC
(
Wu et al. 2024
)
, which performs cancer subtype classification through a feature matrix from autoencoders, followed by graph construction via similarity network fusion. A GCN with residual connections is applied, but its graph remains undirected and similarity-based.
Likewise, MoGCN
(
Li et al. 2022
)
integrates CNV, RPPA, and RNA-seq data using autoencoders for dimensionality reduction and similarity network fusion for graph construction before applying GCNs. The resulting graphs remain undirected and distance-based.

3.
Methodology

Problem Definition.

Let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
denote the input feature matrix with
n
samples and
p
features. The corresponding feature graph is
G(V,E)
, where
V
is the set of nodes and
E\subseteq V\times V
is the set of edges. Its adjacency matrix is
A\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
, where
A_{ij}=1
if node
i
connects to node
j
, and 0 otherwise. In weighted graphs, entries of
A
are continuous and reflect connection strength. We denote the adjacency matrices with self-loops as
\tilde{A}=A+I
, with
I
as the identity matrix.
We consider three omics datasets:
X_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p_{1}}
,
X_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p_{2}}
, and
X_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p_{3}}
, along with multiclass cancer subtype labels
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}
across
C
classes, with predictions
\hat{Y}\in\mathcal{Y}
. Given inputs
\mathcal{D}=\{\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{3},Y\}
, the objective is to construct corresponding directed weighted graphs
\mathcal{G}=\{G_{i}\}_{i=1}^{3}
and learn a predictive function over
\mathcal{Y}
. Formally, we aim to learn mappings

f_{1}:\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}\quad\text{and}\quad f_{2}:\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}

such that the discrepancy between
Y
and
\hat{Y}
is minimized.

TF-DWGNet integrates four key components for effective multimodal learning: (1) an XGBoost module independently processes each omics modality, performing feature selection and constructing task-specific directed weighted graphs encoding feature importance and relationships; (2) a Graph Neural Network module learns latent unimodal embeddings from each modality using the reduced feature matrix and its corresponding graph;
(3) a Tensor Fusion module models all unimodal, bimodal, and trimodal interactions, followed by low-rank CP decomposition for efficiency; and (4) a Deep Residual Network module takes the compact fused embedding as input and performs multiclass classification using a residual network architecture.

Directed Weighted Graph Construction.

We leverage XGBoost’s sequential tree-building approach to correct previous errors and capture complex feature interactions in multi-omics data. This contrasts with Random Forests’ independent trees, making XGBoost particularly effective for identifying informative dependencies.
For each omics modality, we generate an ensemble of
M
trees. Each tree is treated as a directed graph
G^{m}=(V^{m},E^{m})
, where a directed edge
a\rightarrow b
indicates that feature
a
appears before
b
along a splitting path. Aggregating co-occurrence information across all
M
trees yields a unified graph:

G^{g}(V^{g},E^{g})=G^{g}(\bigcup_{m=1}^{M}V^{m},\bigcup_{m=1}^{M}E^{m}),

where nodes
V^{g}
are the selected features and edges
E^{g}
represent directional dependencies. Edge weights correspond to the frequency of directional co-splits between features. Letting
p^{*}
denote the number of unique selected features, we have
|V^{g}|=p^{*}
.
For each of the three omics modalities, we construct a directed weighted graph:
G_{1}^{g}=(V_{1}^{g},E_{1}^{g})
,
G_{2}^{g}=(V_{2}^{g},E_{2}^{g})
, and
G_{3}^{g}=(V_{3}^{g},E_{3}^{g})
, with reduced feature matrix
X_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p_{1}^{*}}
,
X_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p_{2}^{*}}
, and
X_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p_{3}^{*}}
(where
p_{1}^{*}\neq p_{2}^{*}\neq p_{3}^{*}
and all
p_{i}^{*}<p_{i}
). These graphs capture both feature importance and directional interactions, serving as informative inputs for downstream GNN-based modeling, where three Graph-Embedded Deep Feedforward Networks (GEDFN)
(
Kong and Yu 2018
)
are used, which integrate graph structure by connecting the input layer with the first hidden layer based on the adjacency matrix.
The first hidden layer is:

H_{1}=\text{ReLU}\left(X\left(W\odot(\tilde{D}^{-1/2}\tilde{A}\tilde{D}^{-1/2})\right)+b\right),

where
W
and
b
are the corresponding weight and bias, and
\odot
denotes element-wise (Hadamard) multiplication.
We add normalization using the degree matrix
\tilde{D}
to balance the influence of highly connected nodes.

Tensor Fusion and Decomposition.

Given the three unimodal latent representations from the GNNs (
Z_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p_{1}^{*}}
,
Z_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p_{2}^{*}}
, and
Z_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p_{3}^{*}}
) for
n
samples, we apply a
Tensor Fusion Network
(TFN)
(
Zadeh et al. 2017
)
to model both intra- and inter-modal interactions across the omics modalities. To capture interaction types, including unimodal, bimodal, and trimodal, we augment each representation with a constant-valued feature:

Z_{1}^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}Z_{1}\\
\mathbf{1}\end{bmatrix},Z_{2}^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}Z_{2}\\
\mathbf{1}\end{bmatrix},Z_{3}^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}Z_{3}\\
\mathbf{1}\end{bmatrix},

where
Z_{i}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(p_{i}^{*}+1)}
,
p_{1}^{*}\neq p_{2}^{*}\neq p_{3}^{*}
, and the row of 1s enables interaction terms involving fewer than three modalities. For each sample, we compute the third-order tensor via outer product:

\mathcal{T}=\begin{bmatrix}Z_{1}\\
1\end{bmatrix}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}Z_{2}\\
1\end{bmatrix}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}Z_{3}\\
1\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(p_{1}^{*}+1)\times(p_{2}^{*}+1)\times(p_{3}^{*}+1)},

which encodes a complete set of modality interactions:

•

Unimodal terms
(
Z_{1}
,
Z_{2}
, and
Z_{3}
): entries where only one modality dimension is active

•

Bimodal terms
(
Z_{1}\otimes Z_{2}
,
Z_{1}\otimes Z_{3}
, and
Z_{2}\otimes Z_{3}
): pairwise multiplicative interactions

•

Trimodal term
(
Z_{1}\otimes Z_{2}\otimes Z_{3}
): full multiplicative interactions across all modalities

While expressive, this tensor scales as

O\left(\prod_{i=1}^{3}(p_{i}^{*}+1)\right)
and becomes intractable as the latent dimensions increase.
To address this, we apply a
Canonical Polyadic Decomposition
(CP, also called CANDECOMP/PARAFAC),
(
Hitchcock 1927
;
Carroll and Chang 1970
;
Harshman et al. 1970
)
,
which approximates the third-order tensor by a sum of
R
rank-one tensors:

\mathcal{T}\approx\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}\mathbf{p}_{1,r}\otimes\mathbf{p}_{2,r}\otimes\mathbf{p}_{3,r},

where
R
is a positive integer,
\lambda_{r}\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\mathbf{p}_{i,r}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{i}^{*}+1}
for modality
i=1,2,3
. This formulation generalizes matrix singular value decomposition (SVD) to higher-order tensors
(
Kolda and Bader 2009
)
and reduces the complexity to
O\left(R\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{3}(p_{i}^{*}+1)\right)
.

Definition 3.1 (Rank-One Tensors).

An
N
-way tensor
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}\times\dots\times d_{N}}
is rank-one if it can be written as the outer product of
N
vectors:

\mathcal{X}=\mathbf{a}_{1}\otimes\mathbf{a}_{2}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbf{a}_{N}.

Definition 3.2 (Tensor Rank).

The rank of a tensor
rank(\mathcal{T})
is the smallest number of rank-one tensors that sum to
\mathcal{T}
. The maximum rank is defined as the largest attainable rank.

Proposition 3.3.

For
\mathcal{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}\times d_{3}}
, the maximum tensor rank satisfies
(
Kruskal 1989
;
Kolda and Bader 2009
)
:

rank(\mathcal{T})\leq min\{d_{1}d_{2},d_{1}d_{3},d_{2}d_{3}\}.

As the determination of the exact tensor rank is NP-hard in general
(
Håstad 1989
)
, the tensor rank is determined numerically by fitting various rank-
R
CP models in practice
(
Carroll and Chang 1970
;
Harshman et al. 1970
)
.
To enable parameter sharing across samples, we introduce
modality-specific projection matrices
:

\mathbf{P}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times(p_{1}^{*}+1)},\mathbf{P}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times(p_{2}^{*}+1)},\mathbf{P}_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times(p_{3}^{*}+1)},

with
\mathbf{P}_{i}=[\mathbf{p}_{i,1},\mathbf{p}_{i,2},\dots,\mathbf{p}_{i,R}]
. Then we have:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle[\![\mathbf{\lambda},\mathbf{P}_{1}Z_{1}^{*},\mathbf{P}_{2}Z_{2}^{*},\mathbf{P}_{3}Z_{3}^{*}]\!]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}\cdot(\mathbf{P}_{1}Z_{1}^{*})_{r}\cdot(\mathbf{P}_{2}Z_{2}^{*})_{r}\cdot(\mathbf{P}_{3}Z_{3}^{*})_{r},

where
[\![\cdot,\cdot,\cdot]\!]
denotes the CP operator. Each term
(\mathbf{P}_{i}Z_{i}^{*})\in\mathbb{R}^{R}
projects the augmented latent vector into the rank-
R
space. The element-wise product across modalities yields a fused tensor representation in
\mathbb{R}^{R}
,
which is then passed to a deep residual classifier with dense layers for cancer subtype prediction
(
Chen et al. 2020
)
,
enabling stable optimization and deep representation learning.

Novelty.

Unlike fusion approaches that rely on full tensor expansion or low-capacity bilinear models, our approach:

•

Captures high-order cross-modality interactions through low-rank CP decomposition

•

Leverages modality-specific projection matrices to capture both shared and modality-specific structure

Interpretability.

To enhance model interpretability and understand the contribution of each input feature (biomarker) and omics modality, we adapt and extend the connection weights method proposed in
(
Olden and Jackson 2002
)
. For
feature importance
,
after training, the importance of each input feature is quantified based on the connection weights between the input layer and the first hidden layer in the GNN. Since the graph is directed, a feature can appear as a source or a target node in an edge. For example, in the edge
a\rightarrow b
, feature
a
is the source, while in
c\rightarrow a
, feature
a
is the target. For a feature
j
in
X_{i}^{*}
, its importance score
IF_{i}^{j}
consists of two parts:
IF_{i}^{j,\text{source}}
and
IF_{i}^{j,\text{target}}
, calculated as:

IF_{j}^{i}=\underbrace{\sum_{u=1}^{p_{i}^{*}}\left|W_{ju}^{i}I(\tilde{A}_{ju}^{i}\neq 1)\right|}_{IF_{i}^{j,\text{source}}}+\underbrace{\sum_{u=1}^{p_{i}^{*}}\left|W_{uj}^{i}I(\tilde{A}_{uj}^{i}\neq 1)\right|}_{IF_{i}^{j,\text{target}}},

where
W^{i}
is the corresponding weight matrix, and
\tilde{A}^{i}
is the adjacency matrix (with self-loops) for omics
i\in 1,2,3
.
For
relative omics importance
, the overall importance of each omics modality in the classification task is computed by aggregating the importance scores of all features within that modality. Specifically, the model dynamically estimates the relative importance of the omics
i
, denoted as
RIO_{i}
:

RIO_{i}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{p_{i}^{*}}IF_{j}^{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sum_{j=1}^{p_{i}^{*}}IF_{j}^{i}},

where
RIO_{i}\in[0,1]
and
\sum_{i=1}^{3}RIO_{i}=1
.

Technical Details.

We implement XGBoost using the
xgboost
Python library, and train the GNN and DFN components with Tensorflow. All models are optimized via mini-batch training using the Adam optimizer. ReLU is applied as the activation function throughout, with softmax in the output layer for multiclass classification. Training is based on categorical cross-entropy loss combined with dynamic L2 regularization across all trainable weights from three GNNs and the DFN with residual blocks:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{c=1}^{C}y_{i,c}\ln(\hat{y}_{i,c})}_{\text{Cross-entropy loss}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\lambda\bigg(\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sum_{w_{i}\in\Theta_{G_{1}}}w_{i}^{2}}_{\text{GNN weights}}+\underbrace{\sum_{w\in\Theta_{\text{DFN}}}w^{2}}_{\text{DFN weights}}\bigg),

where

y_{i,c}
the true label, and
\hat{y}_{i,c}
the predicted probability, the regularization strength
\lambda
is a tunable hyperparameter.
We tune the number of estimators for XGBoost, and optimize depth, width, learning rate, batch size, and max epochs for the GNN and DFN. We apply dropout, early stopping, and batch normalization to reduce overfitting. Model evaluation is performed over 20 independent stratified train-validation-test splits (60%:20%:20%) to maintain class balance and ensure reproducibility via fixed random seeds. We also tune the rank
R
in the tensor fusion module and the number of residual blocks in the DFN. All experiments are run on the Falcon supercomputer
(
Idaho C3+3 Collaboration 2022
)
, with each job requesting four CPUs and 20 GB of memory.

We evaluate TF-DWGNet on three multi-omics cancer subtype datasets: BRCA, UCEC, and KIPAN. The processed BRCA dataset is sourced from

Baselines and Experimental Setting.

We compare TG-DWGNet against several conventional and deep learning baselines, including RF, XGBoost, DFN, GCN, and GEDFN. All baselines are trained on the column-wise concatenation of the three omics modalities. To ensure fair comparison, we adopt consistent architectural designs and hyperparameter settings across models, using the same 20 random train/validation/test splits. GCN and GEDFN utilize graphs constructed via XGBoost. For tree-based models, XGB and RF use 100 estimators. DFN, GCN and GEDFN are implemented with two hidden layers of 64 neurons each. TG-DWGNet uses one hidden layer in each GNN branch, and three residual blocks in the DFN, each containing three hidden layers of 64 neurons. The learning rate is set to 0.0001, with a batch size of 64 and a training limit of 1000 epochs. Regularization strategies include dropout (rate = 0.5), L2 regularization (
\lambda=0.01
), early stopping with a patience of 10 epochs, and a minimum validation loss improvement threshold of 0.001. The tensor fusion module uses rank
R=48
. In particular, TF-DWGNet uses 100 trees in XGBoost, which performed reliably in preliminary tests. We found this default setting sufficient without requiring further ablation. See supplemental materials for
hyperparameter tuning
details of TF-DWGNet.

Evaluation Metrics.

We evaluate performance using Accuracy, F1-weighted, and F1-macro scores.
F1-macro computes the F1-score for each class independently and then averages across all classes equally, which is useful for evaluating balanced performance. In contrast, F1-weighted computes a weighted average of per-class F1-scores, accounting for class imbalance. The formula to calculate F1-scores is:

F1=\sum_{c=1}^{C}\left(w_{c}\times\frac{2\times\text{precision}_{c}\times\text{recall}_{c}}{\text{precision}_{c}+\text{recall}_{c}}\right),

where
w_{c}=\frac{1}{C}
for F1-macro, and
w_{c}=\frac{n_{c}}{n}
for F1-weighted, with
C
the number of classes and
n_{c}
the number of samples in class
c
.
We also perform pairwise Welch’s
t
-tests for unequal variances to assess statistical significance.

Results and Discussion

Graph Construction.

Table

p_{i}^{*}

Edge-to-node ratios vary from approximately two to nine, indicating that the learned graphs are sparse but sufficiently connected to capture meaningful interactions. These properties enable message passing and preserve modality signals.

Classification Performance.

Table
3
reports classification results of TF-DWGNet compared to baselines across BRCA, UCEC, and KIPAN. We report Accuracy, F1-weighted, and F1-macro scores, with all best-performing results highlighted in bold. TF-DWGNet consistently outperforms all baselines across metrics, confirming its robustness and predictive strength.
On BRCA, TF-DWGNet achieves substantial gains, with over 8% in accuracy relative to the weakest baseline and even larger improvements in F1 (12% increase in F1-macro and a 10% increase in F1-weighted), reflecting its ability to manage subtype imbalance and extract both intra- and inter-modal structure.
On UCEC and KIPAN, TF-DWGNet achieves the best performance across all metrics, with smaller but consistent margins of 1-3%.
To assess statistical significance, we perform pairwise Welch’s
t
-tests (20 runs per model) between TF-DWGNet and each baseline. Superscripts in Table
3
denote statistical significance. On BRCA, almost all improvements are highly significant (p-value
\leq

\leq
0.05); for KIPAN, most differences are not significant (p-value
>
0.05), except for accuracy and F1-weighted compared to XGBoost, suggesting the baselines already perform comparably well.

Figure
2
visualizes the distribution of classification performance across 20 random runs for the BRCA dataset using violin plots.
Across all metrics, TF-DWGNet achieves the highest median performance and exhibits a tight concentration of scores near the median, indicating high stability and robustness across splits. Most of TF-DWGNet’s individual run scores exceed the maximum values other models achieve. This trend is consistent across all three metrics, demonstrating that TF-DWGNet outperforms the baselines on average and nearly every individual test split.

Feature Interpretability.

Each input feature (i.e., gene) receives a ranking score after training. While the absolute score is not meaningful, the ranking reveals each feature’s contribution to cancer subtype classification. High-ranking biomarkers may indicate strong associations or potential causal links to specific cancer types. For example, among the top features identified for BRCA,
FAM134B
, an ER-phagy receptor linked to hypoxic breast cancer survival, has been shown to inhibit tumor proliferation when silenced
(
Chipurupalli et al. 2022
)
. Similarly,
FOXC1
, commonly overexpressed in basal-like breast cancer (e.g., BRCA1-mutant tumors), promotes proliferation and increases sensitivity to PARP inhibitors
(
Johnson et al. 2016
)
. These findings validate TF-DWGNet’s ability to identify biologically meaningful features.
Moreover, analyzing groups of top-ranked genes may highlight disease-relevant pathways.

Modality Interpretability.
Figure
3
illustrates the relative contribution of each omics type (modality) to TF-DWGNet’s classification performance after model training. In BRCA, all three modalities contribute roughly equally, suggesting complementary predictive information across modalities. In contrast, for UCEC and KIPAN, DNA methylation emerges as the dominant contributor, while miRNA plays a lesser role. These results highlight TF-DWGNet’s capacity to adaptively reweight each omics graph based on its utility for a specific classification task. Such dynamic weighting allows the model to prioritize biologically relevant modalities, reflecting dataset-specific molecular mechanisms. Although we have not explicitly validated each omics’ learned contributions and graph structures against known disease pathways, the supervised graph construction process is designed to capture task-relevant dependencies. Future work will aim to align these learned modality patterns and edge directionality with known biological knowledge for further interpretability.

5.
Conclusion

We propose TF-DWGNet, a novel graph neural network framework for multi-omics cancer subtype classification that unifies directed weighted graph learning and tensor-based feature fusion.
Experiments on real-world cancer datasets demonstrate that TF-DWGNet consistently outperforms baseline models across various metrics, underscoring its robustness and effectiveness in modeling complex, heterogeneous omics data.
TF-DWGNet also provides interpretable outputs, including modality-level contributions and biomarker-level importance scores, which offer valuable insights into potential molecular drivers and underlying biological mechanisms. These capabilities make TF-DWGNet a powerful tool for advancing cancer research.
