Curcumin-functionalized Prussian blue for NIR-II photothermal antibacterial therapy
Authors: Zhouhua Li, Guanhua Sun, Fei Ge, Yuanyuan Shen, Xiuying Yang, Bing Yao, Caixia Yu, Shihong Su, Junlie Yao, Yong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFDA-approved Prussian blue (PB) has been developed as a nano photothermal therapy (PTT) agent due to its unique FeIII-CN-FeII structure and the resulting near-infrared (NIR) absorption performance. Nevertheless, the NIR absorption peaks of traditional PB-based nanomedicines are often located in the NIR-I region (700–900 nm) rather than NIR-II region (900–1700 nm), thus limiting their efficacy in treating deep lesions. Herein, curcumin (Cur)-functionalized PB nanomedicine is elaborately designed for NIR-II PTT against bacterial infections. In addition to serving as a dispersant and a reducing agent, the introduced Cur effectively regulates the absorption peak of PB to the NIR-II region. Moreover, as-prepared Cur-functionalized PB nanomedicine demonstrated superior photothermal antibacterial performance against S. aureus. Therefore, this study proffers a new attempt centered on the optical performance regulation of PB and lays the foundation for next-generation PTT nanomedicines.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11671-026-04550-5.Keywords: Near infrared photothermal therapy, Curcumin, Prussian blue nanoparticles, Antibacterial nanomedicine

Introduction

Prussian blue (PB) is a coordination compound discovered by humans, whose chemical composition is ferrocyanide iron [1]. This material possesses a face-centred cubic (Fm3m) structure, with low-spin FeII and high-spin FeIII in the unit cell connected to the C and N atoms of cyanide group (-CN-), respectively [2, 3]. In addition, FeII and FeIII can transfer electrons through -CN-, which causes PB to appear blue. Although -CN- is present in the PB structure, PB is non-toxic and biosafe since -CN- forms a stable anion with FeII (dissociation constant as low as 4 × 10− 36) rather than existing in a free state [4]. In 2003, Radiogardase (active ingredient PB) was approved by the FDA as an antidote for radioactive caesium (Cs-137) and thallium (Tl), which can form insoluble complexes with radioactive metal ions through a displacement reaction and accelerate their excretion rate [2]. At present, nanotechnology can reduce the size of PB to the nanoscale and transform it into nanomedicines, combining the characteristics of PB with the advantages of nanomedicines to expand its application in the biomedicine field [3–7].

Due to its easily controllable structure, including solid, hollow, and core-shell formations, nanoscale PB can be used as a delivery system for imaging tracers, drug molecules, and therapeutic sensitizers for disease diagnosis and treatment [8, 9]. In addition, the transform of electrons in FeIII-CN-FeII endows nanoscale PB with strong absorption properties in the near-infrared (NIR) region, making it an ideal photothermal conversion reagent [10, 11]. Importantly, the molar extinction coefficient of nanoscale PB at 808 nm can reach 1.09 × 109 M− 1 cm− 1, which is higher than that of traditional photothermal agents, such as carbon nanotubes (7.9 × 106 M− 1 cm− 1) and copper selenide (Cu2−xSe, 7.7 × 107 M− 1 cm− 1), but slightly lower than that of gold (Au) nanorods (5.24 × 109 M− 1 cm− 1) [12–15]. Meanwhile, nanoscale PB exhibits excellent photostability and dispersibility in deionised water and simulated physiological environments. As early as 2012, nanoscale PB (500 ppm) was employed in photothermal therapy (PTT), which could raise the temperature of cancer cells to 43 °C within 3 min under the irradiation of 808 nm laser, thus reducing the viability of cancer cell to 10% [16].Nevertheless, traditional PTT systems based on nanoscale PB rely on exogenous NIR-I (700–900 nm) laser stimulation, whose light source has limited penetration performance, thereby resulting in insufficient efficacy for deep lesions [17–21].

Over the past few decades, bacterial infections have posed a serious public health threat to millions of individuals worldwide. For instance, in 2019, S. aureus caused over 1 million deaths, which can lead to skin infections, soft tissue infections, and deep organ infections (e.g., lung infections, endocarditis, and osteomyelitis) [22]. Owing to the overuse and misuse of conventional antibiotics, antibiotic-free antimicrobial therapies, including PTT and photodynamic therapy (PDT), have attracted considerable attention in order to minimise or avoid bacterial resistance [23–25]. At present, there are few studies on the regulation of optical absorption properties of PB through dispersant optimization, let al.one its application in NIR-II (900–1700 nm) PTT with deeper penetration depth. Herein, curcumin (Cur) was utilised as a dispersant and a reducing agent to regulate the synthesis of FDA-approved PB, enabling the absorption peak of PB to reach the NIR-II region. On this basis, the photothermal effect of Cur-functionalized PB nanomedicines upon a 1064 nm laser and their photothermal therapeutic ability against S. aureus were systematically investigated. In summary, this work inspired the regulation of optical performance of PB and proposed a cutting-edge direction for future antibacterial strategies based on NIR-II photothermal effects.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of Cur-functionalized PB #2

30 mg of curcumin (Cur) was placed in a beaker and dissolved in 10 ml of glacial acetic acid. K3[Fe (CN)6]•3H2O of different qualities (3.125, 6.25, 12.5, 25, 50, and 100 mg) was placed in a beaker and dissolved in 5 ml of deionized water. The solution of Cur and the solution of K3[Fe (CN)6]•3H2O are ultrasonically mixed for 10 min. The reaction mixture was heated in an oven at 80 °C for 20 h. Then, the obtained substance was washed thoroughly with deionized water and then collected by centrifugation (13500 rpm, 30 min).

Synthesis of PB@PVP

500 mg of PVP and 22 mg of K3[Fe (CN)6]•3H2O were placed in a beaker and dissolved in 10 mL of hydrochloric acid solution (0.01 M). The reaction mixture was heated in an oven at 80 °C for 20 h. Then, the obtained substance was washed thoroughly with deionized water and then collected by centrifugation (13500 rpm, 30 min).

Cytotoxicity assay

The L929 cells were purchased from Procell Life Science&Technology Co. Ltd. (Wuhan, China). The toxicity of Cur-functionalized PB #2 to L929 cells was examined using the CCK-8 assay. Briefly, L929 cells were inoculated into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and incubated for 24 h. They were then incubated with Cur-functionalized PB #2 for 24 h. After that, the cell culture medium was removed the cells were washed with PBS and cell viability was detected using CCK-8 kit.

Hemolysis assay

Eye blood from healthy mice was collected in EDTA anticoagulant tubes and thoroughly mixed. The samples were washed with PBS and centrifuged several times until the supernatant appeared slightly yellow without red coloration. The pellet was then resuspended in PBS. The red blood cell suspensions were prepared at concentrations of 50, 100, and 200 µg/mL using PBS. Each 500 µL sample was mixed with an equal volume of RBC solution and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Then, the mixtures were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was then transferred to a 96-well plate. The OD at 570 nm was measured using a multi-function microplate reader.

Cell structure and function assay

L929 cells were cultured in 12-well plates and cells were incubated with Cur-functionalized PB #2 (50 µg/mL) subsequently. After 12 h of incubation, the medium was removed and replaced with a TMA-DPH staining or a Rho123 staining solution. Then, the cells were washed two times with PBS. Finally, resuspended in PBS, and the fluorescence intensity was detected by CLSM.

In vitro anti-bacterial assessment

The plate coating method was used and the experimental subject was S. aureus. 500 µL of bacterial solution (1 × 108 CFU/mL) and 500 µL of Cur-functionalized PB #2 solution (100 µg/mL) were thoroughly mixed and incubated at 37 °C for 12 h, followed by irradiation with a 1064 nm laser. Finally, the mixture was diluted and spread on plates, incubated at 37 °C overnight, photographed with a camera, and the colonies were counted using Image J software. In addition, S. aureus incubated in centrifuge tubes were treated similarly and transferred to 12-well plates after treatment to assess bacterial density.

In vivo anti-bacterial efficiency evaluation

The nude mice purchased from SPF Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China). Technically, all animal experiments in this experiment followed the protocols and principles approved by Anhui Medical University. The nude mice were randomly divided into 2 groups (n = 3): PBS and Cur-functionalized PB #2 (100 µL, 500 µg/mL) + Laser. Briefly, the nude mice were anesthetized with isoflurane vapor (1–2% in air). A wound with a diameter of ≈ 0.9 mm was made on the back of the nude mice, and then S. aureus (100 µL, 1 × 109 CFU/mL) was inoculated onto the mouse wounds, allowing the water to dry naturally. About 24 h later, when the wound surface showed abscess formation, treatment was administered. Wound images were taken every two days.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were expressed as the mean ± SD. In addition, the significant differences were acquired via GraphPad Prism 7 software (*** means P < 0.001, ** means P < 0.01, and * means P < 0.05).

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of Cur-functionalized PB

Briefly, a series of Cur-functionalized PB (nanoparticles) NPs were synthesized hydrothermally in one step using potassium ferricyanide and Cur as raw materials. According to their potassium ferrocyanide feeding content (12.5, 25, 50, and 100 mg), the as-synthesized Cur-functionalized PB NPs were named #1-#4. In the common PB synthesis process, Polyvinyl pyrrolidone (PVP) is frequently utilized as a dispersant and a reducing agent and hydrochloric acid is added to promote the decomposition of potassium ferrocyanide [26, 27]. As a natural phenolic antioxidant and pigment extracted from the rhizomes of plants in the ginger family, Cur has the potential to replace PVP and regulate the optical performance of PB [28]. Since Cur is insoluble in water but soluble in acetic acid, an acetic acid solution was used as the solvent to ensure both the acidic environment required for the synthesis reaction and the solubility of Cur. Under the condition that the Cur content remained unchanged, as the amount of potassium ferrocyanide added increased, the particle size of Cur-functionalized PB NPs gradually decreased, and their morphology even became irregular (Fig. 1A and B). The polymer dispersity index (PDI) values for PB #1-#4 NPs were 0.138, 0.148, 0.05, and 0.239, respectively. It is worth noting that when the feeding content of potassium ferrocyanide was too low, the Cur-functionalized PB NPs did not have ideal hydrophilicity (Fig. S1, ESI†).

E EDS spectrum of Cur-functionalized PB #2. F XRD patterns of Cur-functionalized PB #2 and PB@PVP. G FTIR spectra of Cur-functionalized PB #2 and PB@PVP NPs

Compared to traditional PVP-modified PB NPs (named PB@PVP) with an absorption peak at around 700 nm, the absorption peaks of Cur-functionalized PB #1-#4 NPs exhibited varying degrees of red shift, which was more conducive to the utilization of NIR-II laser (Fig. 1C and S2, ESI†). Taking into account the intensity and redshift of the absorption peak, Cur-functionalized PB #2 NPs, possessing an absorption peak at around 910 nm, was selected as the photothermal nanomedicine for further investigation. In high-resolution TEM microscope analysis, Cur-functionalized PB #2 NPs presented a cubic morphology (Fig. 1D). In addition, the energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis disclosed the compresence of Fe within Cur-functionalized PB #2 NPs (Fig. 1E). Also, the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of Cur-functionalized PB #2 NPs and PB@PVP NPs showed consistent characteristics attributed to the pure PB phase (PDF#52-1907) containing no impurities, which demonstrated that Cur was not simply physically mixed with PB (Fig. 1F). Furthermore, the mapping analysis confirmed that Cur entered the PB framework through coordination rather than being adsorbed on the surface (Fig. S3, ESI†). The uniform distribution of the characteristic elements of PB (Fe and N) and the characteristic elements of Cur (C and O) demonstrated that Cur entered the interior of PB #2 NPs. Meanwhile, Cur-functionalized PB #2 NPs exhibited distinct lattice fringes, further confirming the effective formation of PB crystals (S4, ESI†).

In the FTIR spectral analysis, both Cur-functionalized PB #2 NPs and PB@PVP NPs had a distinct -CN- stretching vibration peak located at approximately 2200 cm− 1, which was also one of the characteristics of PB (Fig. 1G). Importantly, the FTIR spectra of Cur and PB #2 NPs were compared (S5, ESI†). Cur had a distinct -OH- stretching vibration peak located at ~ 3514 cm− 1 belonging to the phenolic hydroxyl group. This characteristic peak belonging to the phenolic hydroxyl group shifted to a higher wavenumber (~ 3636 cm− 1) in PB #2 NPs, indicating that the electron cloud redistribution within the conjugated system led to an increase in the bond strength of the O–H bond due to Cur-Fe coordination. Besides, the fluorescence spectra of PB #1-#4 NPs and PB@PVP NPs were compared (S6, ESI†). As the feeding content of potassium ferrocyanide increased, i.e., the coordination degree of Cur-Fe became higher, the fluorescence intensity originating from Cur weakened. This may be ascribed to the slight distortion of the molecular planar conjugated structure upon chelation between Cur and Fe, which can reduce the delocalization degree of π electrons and significantly diminish the fluorescence transition efficiency in the excited state. Therefore, based on our speculation, as the ortho-phenolic hydroxyl groups of Cur are strong coordination groups, Cur can coordinate with Fe2+/Fe3+ in the PB framework to form chelate rings during the nucleation stage of PB synthesis, thus replacing some uncoordinated cyanide groups in the crystal. This interaction can alter the electron cloud density of Fe2+/Fe3+, resulting in a red shift in the absorption peak of PB. The above experimental results indicated that substituting Cur for PVP as a reducing agent and a dispersant can cause a red shift in the NIR absorption peak while forming PB crystals, which was conducive to the absorption and utilization of NIR-II light energy.

Photothermal performance of Cur-functionalized PB

The photothermal properties of the Cur-functionalized PB #2 NPs as a PTT agent were explored in detail. Above all, the photothermal responsive performances of Cur-functionalized PB #2 NPs were evaluated upon 1064 nm laser irradiation. Specifically, their aqueous solution (Fe: 50 µg/mL) under laser illumination (1 W/cm2, 8 min) was heated from 23.2 °C to 50.6 °C within 8 min (Fig. 2A). In contrast, only a slight increase in temperature was observed in deionised water, and the increase in the PB@PVP NPs aqueous solution was even less than that of PB #2 NPs (S7, ESI†). As photothermal conversion efficiency (PCE) is an effective way to evaluate photothermal applicability, the PCE of Cur-functionalized PB #2 NPs was assessed to be 34.55% on the basis of the NIR-II laser heating-cooling cycle (Fig. 2B and C). Moreover, in the photothermal stability experiment, Cur-functionalized PB #2 NPs held excellent stability and reproducibility during five laser irradiation on/off cycles (Fig. 2D). To assess the in vitro antibacterial performance of Cur-functionalized PB #2 NPs, the killing effect of Cur-functionalized PB #2 NPs on S. aureus was researched. The clonogenic assay was conducted to examine the antibacterial functions, showing Cur-functionalized PB #2 NPs had an inherent inhibitory effect on bacterial proliferation under a laser-free condition, which may be attributed to the inherent redox properties of PB (Fig. 2E and F) [29]. After irradiation with a 1064 nm laser, the bactericidal role was significantly improved, with an antibacterial rate of 80.3%. In addition, PTT effectively reduced the density of S. aureus (Fig. 2G). Due to the valid photothermal response and conversion, Cur-functionalized PB #2 NPs were adequate as a potent NIR-II PTT agent for bacterial infection treatment.

Biosafety of Cur-functionalized PB

After evaluating the photothermal performance of Cur-functionalized PB #2 NPs, biosafety assays, as the preconditions to enable clinical applications, were investigated to verify the suitability of Cur-functionalized PB #2 NPs. First of all, the biosafety of Cur-functionalized PB #2 NPs was evaluated using the L929 normal cell line (Fig. 3A). Besides, hemolysis test showed that Cur-functionalized PB #2 NPs had no significant hemolytic effect on red blood cells, indicating their good blood compatibility (Fig. 3B and C). Additionally, after treatment with Cur-functionalized PB #2 NPs, there were no obvious changes in the cell density or morphology of L929 cells (Fig. 3D and E). Furthermore, in terms of cell structure, Cur-functionalized PB #2 NPs did not affect cell membrane permeability or mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 3F and G). Therefore, Cur-functionalized PB #2 NPs at appropriate doses possessed remarkable biocompatibility and showed great potential as a promising antimicrobial nanomedicine.

In vivo antibacterial treatment of Cur-functionalized PB

To further assess the in vivo antibacterial effect of Cur-functionalized PB #2 NPs, a skin infection model of S. aureus in nude mice was established (Fig. 4A). Upon laser illumination (1 W/cm2, 10 min), the local temperature of the wound site injected with Cur-functionalized PB #2 NPs effectively improved to 50.3 °C, which was adequate to achieve PTT (Fig. 4B and C). Throughout the treatment process, the wound areas of mice were monitored every two days, demonstrating that the PTT group had a faster healing rate than the control group (Fig. 4D and E). Hence, the prepared Cur-functionalized PB #2 NPs had satisfactory performance as candidate nanomedicines for PTT of bacterial infections.

Conclusions

In summary, Cur-functionalized PB nanomedicine was designed and synthesized to meet the demand for NIR-II PTT. During the synthesis process, Cur acted simultaneously as a dispersant and a reducing agent to promote the formation of PB nanocrystals. Importantly, Cur had an optical modulation effect, which led to the red-shift of NIR absorption peak of PB, making it more suitable for NIR-II PTT. Additionally, the effective antibacterial performance of Cur-functionalized PB nanomedicine was illustrated in detail and their biosecurity was comprehensively demonstrated. This dispersant optimization strategy of enhancing the optical performance of PB may provide a new insight into the development of PB nanomaterials and inspire more intriguing designs of PTT medicines in the future for bacterial infections in deep lesions.
