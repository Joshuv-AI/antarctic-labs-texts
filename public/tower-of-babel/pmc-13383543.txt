Use of soft intraoral appliance for refractory burning mouth syndrome: an exploratory case series
Authors: Moon-Jong Kim, Hong-Seop Kho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBurning mouth syndrome (BMS) is a chronic oral mucosal pain disorder that significantly impacts patients’ quality of life. While pharmacologic treatment is primarily used for patients with BMS, outcomes are limited, and nonpharmacologic adjuvant treatments are necessary in refractory patients. This study aimed to assess the feasibility and potential effectiveness of soft intraoral appliances as an adjunctive therapy for patients with refractory BMS.MethodsA retrospective chart review of nine female patients diagnosed with primary BMS, all of whom had persistent symptoms despite undergoing conventional pharmacologic treatment, was conducted. Five patients received soft intraoral appliances (appliance group), whereas four declined and continued pharmacologic therapy alone (non-appliance group). The oral symptoms were assessed using a 10-cm visual analog scale. Symptom severity was compared within and between groups over the observation period, and the treatment response was analyzed.ResultsThe median observation periods were 4 months and 4.5 months in the appliance group and non-appliance group, respectively. Most of the appliance users reported initial discomfort that subsided after adaptation, and no severe adverse events were observed. One patient was able to reduce medication use. Compared with the non-appliance group, the appliance group showed changes in symptom scores, with a tendency toward improvement, particularly in relation to aching, stinging, and the effect of oral complaints on daily life. The response rates for these domains were significantly higher in the appliance group (p < 0.05).ConclusionSoft intraoral appliances may present a feasible adjunctive therapeutic option for selected patients with refractory BMS, particularly those with a limited response to pharmacologic treatments. However, given the small sample size and exploratory design of this case series, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary observations and require confirmation in larger prospective studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08594-y.Keywords: Burning mouth syndrome, Soft intraoral appliance, Neuropathic pain, Adjunctive treatment

Introduction

Burning mouth syndrome (BMS) is defined by the International Classification of Orofacial Pain as an idiopathic orofacial pain disorder characterized by a persistent burning or dysesthetic sensation of the oral mucosa without identifiable clinical lesions or systemic causes [1]. Epidemiologically, BMS is considered the most prevalent in postmenopausal women, with a pooled prevalence of approximately 1.7% in the general population, based on a recent systematic review and meta-analysis [2]. The condition most commonly affects the tongue but can affect any site of the oral mucosa and is frequently accompanied by xerostomia and taste disturbances. These persistent symptoms negatively affect daily functioning and psychological well-being, resulting in a significant reduction in the quality of life of the patients [3, 4].

The pathophysiology of BMS is increasingly understood within the framework of neuropathic pain. Peripheral small fiber dysfunction, central sensitization, and abnormal somatosensory processing have been associated with its development [5–8]. Accordingly, pharmacologic treatments targeting neuropathic mechanisms, such as clonazepam, gabapentin, and tricyclic antidepressants, are commonly used as first-line treatments. However, considering the complexity of its pathophysiology and the heterogeneity of the patient presentations, the treatment responses are often limited and inconsistent [9].

Aside from pharmacologic measures, the effectiveness of nonpharmacologic and adjunctive strategies has also been investigated. Among these, several approaches are specifically aimed at protecting the tongue—the principal pain site in BMS patients—from stimuli. The clinical symptoms of BMS most commonly involve the tongue, particularly the tip and lateral borders, which are highly vulnerable to mechanical stimuli. Previous studies have suggested that such mechanical irritation may contribute to the development of burning sensations in these regions [10, 11]. To address this vulnerability, certain approaches such as the use of saliva substitutes, patient counseling, and habit modification have been proposed in the management of BMS, and these strategies have been shown to reduce mechanical irritation and improve symptoms [12]. Aside from these approaches, treatment strategies that physically protect the tongue from external stimuli have also been considered. In persistent idiopathic dentoalveolar pain, which shares neuropathic mechanisms with BMS, the use of occlusal splint-shaped stents has also been proposed as an effective therapeutic approach [13, 14]. Similarly, in BMS, tongue-covering protective devices have been assessed, and they have been shown to result in short-term pain reduction in previous studies [15–17]. However, no studies have yet systematically assessed occlusal splint-type devices aimed at reducing mechanical irritation arising between the teeth and the tongue or between the tongue and the palatal mucosa. Thus, the present study aimed to assess the feasibility and potential effectiveness of soft intraoral appliance therapy in patients with BMS who showed poor response to conventional pharmacologic treatments, with the objective of providing an additional therapeutic approach in the management of this challenging condition.

Materials and methods

Participants

This exploratory retrospective case series, based on a chart review, included nine female patients with refractory BMS who were indicated for soft intraoral appliance therapy between September 2021 and April 2023. The study protocol adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and study approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Seoul National University School of Dentistry (IRB No. S-D20250019; approved on 9 Dec. 2025). Owing to the retrospective design of the study, the IRB of Seoul National University School of Dentistry waived the requirement for informed consent. All patients initially visited the Department of Oral Medicine at Seoul National University Dental Hospital and complained of oral burning sensation; they were diagnosed with the primary type of BMS following a comprehensive clinical assessment. Before the visit, all patients had received treatment at other clinics but experienced no improvement in their symptoms.

This evaluation comprised intraoral inspection, medical history taking, panoramic radiography, the measurement of the whole salivary flow rate, Candida culture test (Candida detector, Kamemizu Chemical Industry Co., Osaka, Japan), and laboratory blood tests to assess the possible local and systemic causes of oral burning sensation. All participants were confirmed to have no identifiable local or systemic causes of oral burning sensation, or to have persistent symptoms despite appropriate management of such conditions, and were therefore diagnosed with burning mouth syndrome. Psychological screening was performed using the Symptom Checklist-90-Revision (SCL-90-R), and a structured questionnaire was administered to document the clinical characteristics and subjective oral symptoms. The details of the diagnostic procedures, including the questionnaire, have been described in our previous study [18]. Moreover, the patients were asked about their oral parafunctional habits, including clenching, bruxism, tongue thrusting, tongue and cheek biting, and involuntary tongue and jaw movements. The severity of the oral symptoms, including burning, aching, stinging, taste alteration, and the effect of oral complaints on daily life (Eff-Life), was rated by patients using a 10-cm visual analog scale (VAS), wherein a higher score indicated a more intense perception of discomfort. Among the pain-related oral symptoms (e.g., burning, aching, and stinging), the symptom that persistently showed greater intensity than others for most of the treatment period was considered the main symptom for each patient (Main-Sx). All assessments were performed by a single clinician (HSK).

Soft intraoral appliance intervention and outcome measures

All participants underwent initial therapy that focused on increasing oral lubrication, controlling parafunctional habits, and providing education on avoiding aggravating factors. Moreover, antifungal agents were administered to patients (Cases 2, 5, 6, 7, 8, and 9) who were diagnosed with concomitant oral candidiasis based on the Candida culture test. As all patients showed insufficient improvement after initial therapy and antifungal treatment, they subsequently received conventional pharmacologic treatment aimed at improving their BMS-related oral symptoms, which included the administration of clonazepam, gabapentin, tricyclic antidepressants, and serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors, among others. Soft intraoral appliance therapy was offered to all nine patients, who met the criteria for refractory BMS, defined as persistent symptoms despite at least 6 months of conventional pharmacologic treatment, based on clinical judgment. Of these, five patients accepted the appliance intervention (appliance group), while the remaining four declined (non-appliance group). The non-appliance group was retained in the analysis not as a control for efficacy comparison, but to provide contextual reference for symptom trajectories during the observation period. Moreover, pharmacologic treatment was continued for all patients throughout the observation period.

The soft intraoral appliance was custom-fabricated for each patient using vacuum-formed thermoplastic material (thickness: 2.0 mm) over a dental model obtained from an impression of the patient’s arch (Fig. 1). It was fabricated for either the maxillary or mandibular arch based on the specific intraoral contact that elicited tongue discomfort, with the aim of isolating the tongue from the source of mechanical irritation. It covered the full dentition along with the marginal gingival area, while in the maxillary cases, it also extended to cover most of the palatal area. Minor adjustments were performed as needed to improve patient comfort and fit. All patients were instructed to wear the soft intraoral appliance during the daytime and to gradually increase the duration of use based on individual adaptation.

The severity of the oral symptoms was assessed at every visit. For analysis, symptom severity data at three key time points were specifically used: baseline (initial visit), pre-intervention (time of recommendation of the soft intraoral appliance), and follow-up (final evaluation). Accordingly, the pre-treatment period was defined as the interval from baseline to pre-intervention, and the observation period as the interval from pre-intervention to follow-up. The treatment outcomes were assessed by comparing symptom severity between pre-intervention and follow-up and between the appliance and non-appliance groups. Adverse events related to soft intraoral appliance use were also assessed at every visit and categorized as mild (transient discomfort) or severe (clinically significant harm, including mucosal trauma).

Statistical analysis

Considering the small sample size, all continuous variables were summarized using median and interquartile range (IQR). The symptom severity and clinical characteristics between the appliance and non-appliance groups were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. The changes in the severity of the oral symptoms before and after the observation period within each group were assessed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The categorical comparisons of the treatment responses were performed using Fisher’s exact test. The statistical significance was set at a p-value of 0.05.

Results

Clinical characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the clinical characteristics of nine patients with refractory BMS, comprising five in the appliance group and four in the non-appliance group. The median age was 70 years (IQR, 52.0–71) in the appliance group and 79 years (IQR, 76.5–81.3) in the non-appliance group. Consistent with the known epidemiology of BMS, which predominantly affects middle-aged and older women, all patients included in the present study were aged 50 years or older. The salivary flow rate of one patient in the appliance group was not measured. The median unstimulated whole salivary flow rate was 0.12 mL/min (IQR, 0.05–0.19) in the appliance group and 0.18 mL/min (IQR, 0.12–0.24) in the non-appliance group. A reduced unstimulated whole salivary flow rate (< 0.1 mL/min) was noted in two of four patients in the appliance group (Cases 1 and 5) and in one of four patients in the non-appliance group (Case 7). The median stimulated whole salivary flow rate was 0.35 mL/min (IQR, 0.32–0.66) in the appliance group and 1.09 mL/min (IQR, 0.92–1.23) in the non-appliance group. A reduced stimulated salivary flow rate (< 0.7 mL/min) was noted in three of four patients in the appliance group (Cases 1, 2, and 5), while all patients in the non-appliance group were within the normal range.

All median t-scores of the symptom dimensions and global indices of the SCL-90-R were within the normal range (t-score of < 60) in both groups. However, three patients in the appliance group and one patient in the non-appliance group showed high scores (t-score ≥ 60) in one or two symptom dimensions and/or global indices, specifically somatization (Cases 5 and 7), depression (Cases 1 and 4), anxiety (Case 1), and positive symptom distress index (Case 4) (Supplementary Table 1).

All patients reported oral symptoms involving the tongue, consistently affecting the tip. The lateral side of the tongue was also involved in most patients, except for two patients (Cases 1 and 5). Beyond the tongue, the next most frequent site was the labial mucosa (three patients: Cases 1, 3, and 6), followed by the palate (two patients: Cases 4 and 5). The other affected sites included the buccal mucosa (Case 3), gingiva (Case 3), teeth (Case 4), and upper labial mucosa (Case 3). Most patients presented with bilateral oral symptoms, but two patients (Cases 2 and 4) in the appliance group and one patient (Case 9) in the non-appliance group presented with unilateral symptoms, all localized to the lateral tongue surfaces. With regard to the diurnal pattern, the appliance group included two patients (Cases 1 and 3) with an increasing type (symptoms worsening during the daytime) and three patients (Cases 2, 4, and 5) with a continuous type (symptoms persisting throughout the day), whereas the non-appliance group included two patients (Cases 7 and 8) with an increasing type and two patients (Cases 6 and 9) with a continuous type. As is common in BMS populations, nearly all patients in this cohort were postmenopausal, except for one patient in the appliance group (Case 4). The majority of the patients showed one or two oral parafunctional habits, whereas one patient in each group (Cases 4 and 7) reported none.

The median duration from the oral symptom onset to the initial clinical visit (pre-visit symptom period) was 6.0 months (IQR, 6.0–24.0) in the appliance group and 19.5 months (IQR, 17.3–28.5) in the non-appliance group. The median duration of the pre-treatment period was 13.0 months (IQR, 12.0–15.0) in the appliance group and 28.5 months (23.8–35.5) in the non-appliance group.

Changes in the oral symptoms during the observation period

Five patients in the appliance group were provided soft intraoral appliances and were observed for a median of 4 months (IQR, 3–5). Most patients reported transient mild adverse events in the early phase of the observation period and continued daily soft intraoral appliance use throughout the observation period. No severe adverse events, including mucosal trauma, were observed. Only one patient (Case 4) experienced partial symptom relief in the early phase but subsequently reported a lack of benefit or increased discomfort and discontinued use voluntarily at approximately 14 weeks, despite the absence of identifiable adverse events.

Four patients in the non-appliance group were also observed for a median of 4.5 months (IQR, 4–5.3), with no significant difference in the observation period between the two groups (Table 2). Furthermore, all patients continued their pre-existing pharmacologic treatment during the observation period. Antifungal agents were continued in two patients (Cases 5 and 8) who had already been receiving antifungal therapy prior to the observation period for the prevention of oral candidiasis recurrence. While minor medication adjustments were noted in some patients, no dose modifications were made to existing neuropathic medication regimens during the observation period (Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

This is the first study to investigate the use of occlusal splint-type soft intraoral appliances in patients with refractory BMS. In this exploratory case series, patients receiving soft intraoral appliance therapy showed changes in symptom scores, with a tendency toward improvement compared with those who did not use the appliance, although only limited domains showed statistically significant differences. These observations suggest that soft intraoral appliances may represent a feasible adjunctive option for selected patients with refractory BMS.

The mechanical irritation of the tongue has long been suggested as a factor that exacerbates BMS symptoms, and various treatment strategies have been attempted to reduce such stimulation. Indirect protective approaches, including the use of lubricants, behavioral counseling, and habit modification, have been reported to provide symptomatic benefits in some patients [12]. As a more direct intervention, previous studies have assessed the use of tongue protectors, which resulted in reductions in pain intensity and improvements in quality of life [15–17]. Building on this rationale, the present study assessed an occlusal splint-type soft intraoral appliance designed to isolate the tongue from direct contact with the dentition and/or palatal mucosa—the principal source of mechanical irritation. Compared with previously reported tongue protector approaches, the soft intraoral appliance used in the present study may offer practical advantages in terms of fabrication simplicity, clinical accessibility, and patient adaptability, as it is based on a commonly used occlusal splint design and is less likely to interfere with tongue movement. Consistent with this rationale, our findings revealed similar symptomatic benefits, supporting its potential role as a protective strategy in BMS management.

Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain how mechanical stimuli contribute to oral burning pain. First, excessive mechanical stimuli may directly injure the oral mucosa and peripheral nerve, resulting in persistent oral burning sensations. Parafunctional oral habits are frequently observed in patients with BMS [19, 20], and such repetitive contact may result in mucosal irritation. Reduced salivary flow rate and alterations in salivary composition have also been implicated in the pathogenesis of BMS, as they diminish the lubrication and protective functions of saliva, thereby increasing the mechanical friction on the oral mucosa, particularly on the tip of the tongue and lateral surfaces [19, 21, 22]. Persistent mechanical irritation may induce peripheral neuropathic changes or subclinical inflammation, culminating in oral burning pain [8, 9].

However, innocuous mechanical sensations may be perceived as painful through altered somatosensory processing and psychological factors. BMS has a neuropathic basis, and several studies have documented altered somatosensory function. In particular, several studies have shown that a subset of patients exhibit positive sensory signs, such as warm allodynia, decreased heat pain tolerance, and deficient habituation in blink reflex tests [5, 23, 24]. Endogenous pain facilitation and deficits in inhibitory conditioned pain modulation, reflecting central neuropathic mechanisms, have also been reported in BMS [25, 26]. These findings suggest that otherwise innocuous stimuli, including normal tooth–tongue contact, can be perceived as painful [27], providing a rationale for the use of protective appliances. However, several studies have reported no differences in mechanical pain thresholds between patients with BMS and controls, which appear to contradict the above evidence [23, 25, 28]. Nevertheless, given that the presence and relative contributions of central and peripheral neuropathic mechanisms may vary across individuals [7], it can be suggested that some patients may still exhibit symptoms of hyperalgesia or allodynia.

In addition to neurobiological mechanisms, psychological factors may regulate the pain experience of BMS. Patients with BMS often present with symptoms of comorbid depression and anxiety, and these psychological disturbances have been associated with the amplification of pain perception [18, 29, 30]. Pain hypervigilance has been shown to correlate with both pain intensity and emotional distress in patients with BMS [31]. Moreover, the pain experience in BMS is associated with pain catastrophizing, emphasizing the role of maladaptive cognitive responses in the persistence of symptoms [31, 32]. Collectively, these psychological factors can sensitize patients with BMS to otherwise normal oral contacts, so that ordinary tongue–tooth or tongue–palatal mucosa contact may trigger or aggravate pain.

Taken together, mechanical, somatosensory, and psychological factors may result in the aggravation of BMS symptoms through tongue–tooth or tongue–palatal mucosa contact, while soft intraoral appliances may help relieve symptoms by directly protecting the oral mucosa, reducing abnormal somatosensory responses, and functioning as behavioral therapy aimed at stimulus avoidance and cognitive readjustment. In the present study, no severe adverse events were observed, and the soft intraoral appliance was generally well tolerated. Although some patients experienced initial discomfort, these issues subsided with adaptation. Notably, in one case, medication use could be reduced.

The primary treatment of BMS is generally considered neuropathic medication therapy, with clonazepam being one of the most widely used agents supported by clinical evidence. However, a substantial proportion of patients continue to report persistent symptoms despite prolonged pharmacologic therapy [9]. In this context, the findings of the present study suggest that soft intraoral appliance therapy may represent a viable adjunctive therapeutic option. This approach may provide additional clinical benefit in selected patients with refractory BMS, particularly those with persistent symptoms despite long-term pharmacologic treatment. Among these patients, the appliance may be more particularly considered when mechanical irritation of the tongue is suspected or when tongue contact with the dentition or oral mucosa appears to exacerbate pain. However, these considerations are hypothesis-generating, and further studies are required to establish appropriate patient selection criteria. In addition, the absence of severe adverse events, along with its non-invasive and reversible nature, may support its clinical applicability as part of multimodal management strategy.

However, this study had several limitations that should be addressed. As a case series, the sample size was small, the study design was retrospective, and the observation period did not allow long-term outcomes to be assessed. Although nonparametric statistical analyses were performed to compare between-group differences and observe treatment effects, the statistical power remains limited due to the small sample size, and the findings should therefore be interpreted with caution. The non-randomized allocation of patients, based on acceptance or refusal of the appliance, may also have introduced selection bias. Moreover, the placebo effects could not be excluded, and various confounding factors, including concurrent pharmacologic treatments, may have influenced the treatment efficacy of the soft intraoral appliances. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution, and further well-designed prospective controlled trials with larger cohorts are needed to confirm the preliminary findings.

Conclusion

This exploratory case series is the first to suggest a potential role for soft intraoral appliances in the management of refractory BMS. Changes in symptom scores showed a tendency toward improvement, with no severe adverse events observed. Although the small sample size and retrospective design compromise the generalizability of these findings, the results suggest that soft intraoral appliances could be a feasible adjunctive option in the multidisciplinary management of BMS. Furthermore, prospective controlled trials are needed to validate these observations and to better define the patient subgroups most likely to benefit from this approach.
