Neurourological chaos in the first year after spinal cord injury
Authors: Necmettin Yıldız
Published: 2026

Urodynamic investigation is considered the gold standard for evaluating neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction following spinal cord injury (SCI). While current international guidelines, namely those of the European Association of Urology, the American Urological Association, and the Canadian Urological Association, recommend conducting a baseline urodynamic assessment within the first three months post injury once spinal shock has resolved, they present a significant gap regarding early surveillance. Although they advise monitoring neurourological patients based on risk stratification derived from urodynamic parameters (such as bladder type, capacity, pressure, and compliance), bladder emptying methods, and urinary tract damage, these guidelines fail to clearly define the specific factors that should dictate the frequency of follow up during the first year, a critical period characterized by profound physiological changes in SCI patients.[1, 2, 3]

Compounding this lack of defined first year follow up targets is a striking dichotomy in early stage urodynamic literature in SCI patients, which creates considerable clinical uncertainty. On one hand, the traditional paradigm of an initial “acontractile bladder” has been challenged by Bywater et al.,[4] whose study focusing on patients within the acute spinal shock phase (≤40 days) demonstrated that kidney threatening urodynamic parameters, such as detrusor overactivity (DO)/detrusor sphincter dyssynergia (DSD), can occur in up to two thirds of SCI patients during this specific period. Furthermore, multicenter data from Kozomara et al.[5] and Anderson et al.[6] indicate that the hazard rate for these complications increases significantly within the first three months, contributing to a high overall rate of adverse (high-risk) parameter development within the first year. However, recent temporal studies focusing specifically on suprasacral cohorts point toward a markedly different dynamic. Their findings highlight the deceptive nature of the urodynamic profile during the first 90 days, which predominantly manifests as an acontractile detrusor and erroneously appears as a moderate risk. In reality, the prevalence of high-risk phenotypes such as DO and DO/DSD reaches 81.3% and 83.3%, respectively, within the interval spanning from 91 to 365 days.[7, 8] This delayed maturation exposes a critical shortcoming in current guideline frameworks, which advise routine follow ups every one to two years, or solely upon symptom manifestation, for patients classified as “moderate risk” at baseline.[1, 3] Consequently, a patient whose initial three month evaluation appears stable (relatively low-risk) may be left unmonitored during the very months this storm breaks. Ultimately, while current guidelines offer general recommendations, they struggle to provide a parameter-driven algorithm identifying which SCI patients require intervention and when, especially within the first year post-injury. This clinical uncertainty is further exacerbated by the significant heterogeneity in the definition of “suprasacral” lesions across the literature, where varying methodological choices regarding neurological levels often lead to inconsistent clinical and urodynamic risk assessments.[9]

This situation represents the paramount clinical dilemma in early stage neuro-urology and rehabilitation practice, perpetuating a state of “chaos” regarding practice standardization. Indeed, recent population based data explicitly highlight this lack of consensus, revealing profound between center variations regarding the exact timing of fundamental urological interventions, such as the initiation of bladder storage medications and specific emptying methods.[10] Although available data suggest that the emergence of this high-risk profile becomes increasingly evident over a timeline extending from the spinal shock phase to the first year post injury, the critical question remains: how can we detect the early footsteps of this impending storm? While neurourological data outline the general timeline of events, the fundamental imperative in clinical practice is predictive capacity. Ultimately, we must be able to forecast the clinical chaos at the end of the first year by leveraging early stage neuro urological parameters obtained either during the patient’s spinal shock period or within that initial dynamic transition window immediately following its resolution.

Resolving this clinical chaos requires moving beyond standardized approaches and adopting more dynamic methodological study designs. The literature must definitively establish, across specific timeframes (spinal shock, acute, and subacute), which neurourological parameters evolve into clinically meaningful risk factors during which specific stage or stages. Furthermore, there is an urgent need for studies that evaluate from a holistic perspective how these identified risk factors translate into real world clinical management, such as modifications in bladder emptying strategies and pharmacological interventions, rather than allowing them to remain purely theoretical constructs.

The paradigm shift poised to transform rehabilitation practice lies in the development of next generation predictive models that do not merely describe clinical data but convert it into proactive decision making tools for clinicians. Ultimately, future international guidelines for early stage SCI patients must transcend current “on indication” or fixed “standard follow up” algorithms and evolve toward a framework of “tailored neurourological rehabilitation.” This approach would incorporate patient specific risk stratification derived from early stage predictive nomograms, thereby enabling truly individualized and timely clinical interventions.
