Constructing new probability distributions on the unit interval
Roberto Vila 1 , Helton Saulo 1,2 , Poliana Matos 1 and Subhankar Dutta 3 1 Department of Statistics, University of Brasilia, Brasilia, 70910-900, Brazil 2 Department of Economics, Federal University of Pelotas, Pelotas, 96010-610, Brazil 3 Department of Mathematics, Bioinformatics and Computer Applications, Maulana Azad National Institute of Technology, Bhopal, 462003, India

Abstract
In this paper, we introduce a novel method for constructing probability distributions on the unit interval by exploiting the non-injective transformation defined by the ratio of two positive random variables, X X and Y Y . For simplicity and tractability, we focus on independent random variables X X and Y Y following gamma distributions. We derive some results including laws, density functions, quantile function and closed-form expressions for the moments, and discuss maximum likelihood estimation.



Keywords.
Gamma distribution, Unit probabilistic model,
Maximum likelihood.

1
Introduction

Probability models with suppport on the unit interval are usefull in many fields, including econometrics
(
Cribari-Neto and Zeileis, 2010
)
, natural sciences
Geissinger et al., 2022
, and reliability
Cross et al., 2006
, among others. Traditionally, the beta
(
Ferrari and Cribari‐Neto, 2004
)
and Kumaraswamy
(
Kumaraswamy, 1980
)
distributions have been the workhorses for modeling such data, due to their flexibility and tractability. More recently, alternative unit‐interval families have emerged, see, for example,
Vila et al., 2024c
;
Vila et al., 2024b
, to address features like bimodality.

The beta distribution is constructed by the ratio of two positive random variables, that is, when
X,Y\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha_{i},\lambda)
are independent and identically scaled, then

\frac{X}{X+Y}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}),

yielding the familiar beta model. In this work, we introduce two new families of unit‐interval distributions, defined by non‐injective transformations of the ratio
X/(X+Y)
. Writing

S_{r}(u)=[\,1-4u(1-u)\,]^{r},\qquad T_{r}(u)=1-[\,4u(1-u)\,]^{r},\quad 0<u<1,\ r>0,

and setting
W=S_{r}\bigl(X/(X+Y)\bigr)
,
Z=T_{r}\bigl(X/(X+Y)\bigr)
, one obtains random variables supported on
(0,1)
whose densities admit simple closed‐form expressions when
X
and
Y
are independent
\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha_{i},\lambda_{i})
. The case
r=1/2
recovers sample‐based estimators of the Gini and Atkinson indices from a pair
(X,Y)
, highlighting a direct link between our families and classic measures of inequality
(
Gini, 1936
;
Atkinson, 1970
)
.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the new unit‐interval families via the non‐injective transformations
S_{r}
and
T_{r}
of the gamma ratio. In Section
3
, we derive their distributional laws in terms of the underlying
X/(X+Y)
density. In Section
4
, we obtain closed‐form expressions for the density and cumulative distribution functions of
W
and
Z
. In Section
5
, we present the quantile function. In Section
6
, we develop closed‐form formulae for selected raw moments under special parameter regimes. In Section
7
, we formulate maximum likelihood estimation, derive the likelihood equations, and discuss numerical solution strategies. Finally, in Section
8
, we provide some concluding remarks.

2
The unit probabilistic models

Let
X
and
Y
be two (absolutely) continuous random variables, with positive support, both defined in the same probability space
(\Omega,\mathscr{B}(\Omega),\mathbb{P})
. Here,
\mathscr{B}(\Omega)
denotes the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\Omega
and
\mathbb{P}:\mathscr{B}(\Omega)\to[0,1]
is a probability measure.
In this work we define news random variables as the composition of a surjective transformation
\varphi_{r}:(0,1)\to(0,1)
with the ratio
X/(X+Y)
, that is,

\displaystyle\varphi_{r}\circ\left({X\over X+Y}\right)=\varphi_{r}\left({X\over X+Y}\right):\Omega\to(0,1),

(1)

so that the addition of the parameter
r>0
generates flexibility in the proposed models.

Note that the complexity of the probabilistic models defined in (
1
) depends on the choices of variables
X
and
Y
and on its correlation structure, which are innumerable.
As an illustration, reference
(
Colin and Ossikovski, 2025
)
explores various probabilistic aspects of model (
1
) under the conditions
\varphi_{r}(x)=[1-4x(1-x)]^{r}
and
r=1/2
, assuming that the variables
X
and
Y
are correlated Gamma-distributed with the same shape and scale parameters, and a linear correlation coefficient
\rho
.
For simplicity of presentation, from now on we choose
X
and
Y
independent (not necessarily identically distributed) with gamma distributions, denoted by
X\sim\text{Gamma}(\alpha_{1},\lambda_{1})
,
\alpha_{1},\lambda_{1}>0
, and
Y\sim\text{Gamma}(\alpha_{2},\lambda_{2})
,
\alpha_{2},\lambda_{2}>0
, respectively. That is, the density functions of
X
and
Y
are given by

\displaystyle f_{X}(x)={\lambda_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\over\Gamma(\alpha_{1})}\,x^{\alpha_{1}-1}\exp(-\lambda_{1}x),\quad f_{Y}(y)={\lambda_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\over\Gamma(\alpha_{2})}\,y^{\alpha_{2}-1}\exp(-\lambda_{2}y),\quad x,y>0,

respectively, where
\Gamma(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{z-1}\exp(-t){\text{d}}t
,
z>0,
is the (complete) gamma function.
In this direction, a notable result in distribution theory, widely recognized in the literature, occurs when
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}
and
\varphi_{r}(x)=x,

0<x<1
, as this leads to

\displaystyle{X\over X+Y}\sim\text{Beta}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}).

Alternative choices of
X
and
Y
beyond the gamma distributions have been thoroughly explored in the literature even for the case
\varphi_{r}(x)=x,

0<x<1
. For instance,
Vila et al., 2024a
selected
X
and
Y
as independent random variables with general distributions, whereas
Vila et al., 2024c
examined
X
and
Y
with a bivariate unit-logsymmetric distribution. Additionally,

Vila et al., 2025
chose
X
and
Y
correlated according to the bivariate Birbaum-Saunders (BS) distribution, and
Vila and Quintino, 2025
considered
X
and
Y
following a bivariate Fréchet distribution.

Simple examples include choosing the transformation
\varphi_{r}
as injector functions, such as

\varphi_{r}(x)=x^{r},

\varphi_{r}(x)={2^{r}x^{r}/(x+1)^{r}},

\varphi_{r}(x)={(1-x)^{r}/(x+1)^{r}},

0<x<1
;
r>0
.
The scope of this work centers on non-injective transformations
\varphi_{r}
, like:

\displaystyle S_{r}(x)\equiv\varphi_{r}(x)=[1-4x(1-x)]^{r},\quad T_{r}(x)\equiv\varphi_{r}(x)=1-[4x(1-x)]^{r},\quad 0<x<1;\ r>0.

(2)

Note that both transformations above have a zero at the point
x=1/2
and assume the value of
1
at the points
x=0
and
x=1
, see Figure
1
.

Figure 1:
Plots of the transformations
S_{r}
and
T_{r}
for different choices of the parameter
r
.

Using the transformations
S_{r}
and
T_{r}
in (
2
), simple algebraic manipulations show that, the new random variables
W
and
Z
, defined as

\displaystyle W\equiv S_{r}\left({X\over X+Y}\right)=\left({Y-X\over X+Y}\right)^{2r},

(3)

\displaystyle Z\equiv T_{r}\left({X\over X+Y}\right)=1-{(XY)^{r}\over\displaystyle\left[{1\over 2}(X+Y)\right]^{2r}},

(4)

respectively,
have support in the unit interval.

Remark 2.1
.

Note that when
r=1
, we get
W=Z
.

Remark 2.2
.

The value of
r=1/2
in formulas (
3
) and (
4
) is special, because for this value the variables
W
and
Z
take the forms

\displaystyle W={|X-Y|\over X+Y}\quad\text{and}\quad Z=1-{\sqrt{XY}\over{1\over 2}(X+Y)},

(5)

which coincide, respectively, with the Gini coefficient
(
Gini, 1936
)
and Atkinson index
(
Atkinson, 1970
)
estimators formed by the random sample
(X,Y)
of size
n=2
.

3
Deriving the laws of
W
and
Z

Note that, for any Borel set
B
on
\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(W\in B\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(S_{r}\left({X\over X+Y}\right)\in B\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\omega\in\Omega:{X(\omega)\over X(\omega)+Y(\omega)}\in S_{r}^{-1}(B)\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{S_{r}^{-1}(B)}f_{X\over X+Y}(u){\rm d}u,

(6)

where
S_{r}^{-1}(B)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:S_{r}(x)\in B\}
is the inverse image of set
B
under the function
S_{r}
. Since the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathbb{R}
is generated by open or closed sets, without loss of generality, assume that
B=(a,b)
, with
a<b
. Hence,

\displaystyle S_{r}^{-1}(B)=\begin{cases}\left({1-b_{*}^{{}^{1\over 2r}}\over 2},{1-a_{*}^{{}^{1\over 2r}}\over 2}\right)\bigcup\left({1+a_{*}^{{}^{1\over 2r}}\over 2},{1+b_{*}^{{}^{1\over 2r}}\over 2}\right),&B\cap(0,1)\neq\emptyset,\\[8.5359pt]
\emptyset,&B\cap(0,1)=\emptyset,\end{cases}

where
a_{*}=a\vee 0=\max\{a,0\}
and
b_{*}=b\wedge 1=\min\{b,1\}
. Therefore, by using (
3
), the law of
W
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(W\in B\right)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{{\left({1-b_{*}^{{}^{1\over 2r}}\over 2},{1-a_{*}^{{}^{1\over 2r}}\over 2}\right)\bigcup\left({1+a_{*}^{{}^{1\over 2r}}\over 2},{1+b_{*}^{{}^{1\over 2r}}\over 2}\right)}}f_{X\over X+Y}(u){\rm d}u,&B\cap(0,1)\neq\emptyset,\\[8.5359pt]
0,&B\cap(0,1)=\emptyset.\end{cases}

(7)

Similarly, we can show that the law of
Z
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Z\in B\right)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{{\left({1-\sqrt{1-(1-b_{*})^{1\over r}}\over 2},{1-\sqrt{1-(1-a_{*})^{1\over r}}\over 2}\right)\bigcup\left({1+\sqrt{1-(1-a_{*})^{1\over r}}\over 2},{1+\sqrt{1-(1-b_{*})^{1\over r}}\over 2}\right)}}f_{X\over X+Y}(u){\rm d}u,&B\cap(0,1)\neq\emptyset,\\[8.5359pt]
0,&B\cap(0,1)=\emptyset.\end{cases}

(8)

By (
7
) and (
8
), we note that knowledge of the laws of
W
and
Z
relies on knowledge of density of the independent ratio
X/(X+Y)
, with
X\sim\text{Gamma}(\alpha_{1},\lambda_{1})
and
Y\sim\text{Gamma}(\alpha_{2},\lambda_{2})
, which is well-known in literature
(
Vila et al., 2024a
, see Example 6 of reference)
:

\displaystyle f_{X\over X+Y}(v)={1\over\lambda_{1}^{\alpha_{2}}\lambda_{2}^{\alpha_{1}}\text{B}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}\,\dfrac{v^{\alpha_{1}-1}(1-v)^{\alpha_{2}-1}}{\displaystyle\left[\left({1\over\lambda_{2}}-{1\over\lambda_{1}}\right)v+{1\over\lambda_{1}}\right]^{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}},\quad 0<v<1,

(9)

where
\text{B}(z_{1},z_{2})=\int_{0}^{1}t^{z_{1}-1}(1-t)^{z_{2}-1}{\rm d}t
is the (complete) beta function.

4
Distribution and density functions of
W
and
Z

By taking
a\to-\infty
and
b=w\in(0,1)
in (
7
), we have
a_{*}=0
and
b_{*}=w
. Then the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of
W
can be written as

\displaystyle F_{W}(w)=\int_{1-w_{r}}^{w_{r}}f_{X\over X+Y}(u){\rm d}u,\quad 0<w<1,

(10)

where we have adopted the notation

\displaystyle w_{r}\equiv{1+w^{\!{}^{1\over 2r}}\over 2}.

(11)

Consequently, the probability density function (PDF) of
W
is given by (for
0<w<1
)

\displaystyle f_{W}(w)={w^{{1\over 2r}-1}\over 4r}\left[f_{X\over X+Y}\left(w_{r}\right)+f_{X\over X+Y}\left(1-w_{r}\right)\right].

(12)

Analogously, by taking
a\to-\infty
and
b=z\in(0,1)
in (
8
), we have
a_{*}=0
and
b_{*}=z
. Then the CDF of
Z
can be written as

\displaystyle F_{Z}(z)=\int_{1-z_{r}}^{z_{r}}f_{X\over X+Y}(u){\rm d}u,\quad 0<z<1,

(13)

where we are using the notation

\displaystyle z_{r}\equiv{1+\sqrt{1-(1-z)^{1\over r}}\over 2}.

(14)

Hence, the PDF of
Z
is given by (for
0<z<1
)

\displaystyle f_{Z}(z)={(1-z)^{{1\over r}-1}\over 4r\sqrt{1-(1-z)^{1\over r}}}\left[f_{X\over X+Y}\left(z_{r}\right)+f_{X\over X+Y}\left(1-z_{r}\right)\right].

(15)

Plugging (
9
) into formulas (
12
) and (
15
), we get (for
0<w,z<1
)

\displaystyle f_{W}(w)={1\over 4r\lambda_{1}^{\alpha_{2}}\lambda_{2}^{\alpha_{1}}\text{B}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}\,w^{{1\over 2r}-1}G(w_{r})

(16)

and

\displaystyle f_{Z}(z)

\displaystyle={1\over 4r\lambda_{1}^{\alpha_{2}}\lambda_{2}^{\alpha_{1}}\text{B}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}\,{(1-z)^{{1\over r}-1}G(z_{r})\over\sqrt{1-(1-z)^{1\over r}}},

(17)

respectively, where

\displaystyle G(x)\equiv\dfrac{\displaystyle x^{\alpha_{1}-1}\left(1-x\right)^{\alpha_{2}-1}}{\displaystyle\left[\left({1\over\lambda_{2}}-{1\over\lambda_{1}}\right)x+{1\over\lambda_{1}}\right]^{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}}+\dfrac{\displaystyle\left(1-x\right)^{\alpha_{1}-1}x^{\alpha_{2}-1}}{\displaystyle\left[\left({1\over\lambda_{2}}-{1\over\lambda_{1}}\right)(1-x)+{1\over\lambda_{1}}\right]^{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}}.

(18)

Remark 4.1
.

In the special case
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}
, we get

\displaystyle f_{W}(w)={w^{{1\over 2r}-1}\over 4r\text{B}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}\left[{\displaystyle w_{r}^{\alpha_{1}-1}(1-w_{r})^{\alpha_{2}-1}}\right.+\left.{\displaystyle(1-w_{r})^{\alpha_{1}-1}w_{r}^{\alpha_{2}-1}}\right],\quad 0<w<1,

and

\displaystyle f_{Z}(z)

\displaystyle={(1-z)^{{1\over r}-1}\over 4r\text{B}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\sqrt{1-(1-z)^{1\over r}}}\left[{\displaystyle z_{r}^{\alpha_{1}-1}(1-z_{r})^{\alpha_{2}-1}}+{\displaystyle(1-z_{r})^{\alpha_{1}-1}z_{r}^{\alpha_{2}-1}}\right],\quad 0<z<1,

where
w_{r}
and
z_{r}
are as given in (
11
) and (
14
), respectively.

Remark 4.2
.

It is worth noting that in the case
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}\equiv\lambda
,
the densities
f_{W}
and
f_{Z}
presented in Remark
4.1
do not depend on
\lambda
, consistent with the fact that
W
and
Z
, given in (
3
) and (
4
), respectively, are derived from scale-invariant transformations. That is, for
X\sim\text{Gamma}(\alpha_{1},\lambda)
and
Y\sim\text{Gamma}(\alpha_{2},\lambda)
we have
\lambda X\sim\text{Gamma}(\alpha_{1},1)
and
\lambda Y\sim\text{Gamma}(\alpha_{2},1)
, and consequently, the random variables
W
and
Z
satisfy the identities

\displaystyle W=\left({Y-X\over X+Y}\right)^{2r}=\left({\lambda Y-\lambda X\over\lambda X+\lambda Y}\right)^{2r},

\displaystyle Z=1-{(XY)^{r}\over\displaystyle\left[{1\over 2}(X+Y)\right]^{2r}}=1-{(\lambda X\lambda Y)^{r}\over\displaystyle\left[{1\over 2}(\lambda X+\lambda Y)\right]^{2r}},

whose distribution is unaffected by
\lambda
.

5
Quantiles

From (
10
), the
p
-quantile function
Q_{W}(p)
of
W
, for given
p\in(0,1)
, satisfies the relation

\displaystyle F_{W}(Q_{W}(p))=F_{X\over X+Y}\left({1+Q_{W}^{\!{}^{1\over 2r}}(p)\over 2}\right)-F_{X\over X+Y}\left({1-Q_{W}^{\!{}^{1\over 2r}}(p)\over 2}\right).

Analogously, from (
13
), the
p
-quantile function
Q_{Z}(p)
of
Z
, for given
p\in(0,1)
, satisfies

\displaystyle F_{Z}(Q_{Z}(p))=F_{X\over X+Y}\left({1+\sqrt{1-[1-Q_{Z}(p)]^{1\over r}}\over 2}\right)-F_{X\over X+Y}\left({1-\sqrt{1-[1-Q_{Z}(p)]^{1\over r}}\over 2}\right).

While
Q_{W}(p)
and
Q_{Z}(p)
can be theoretically derived from the above equations, their expressions lack simple closed forms, necessitating numerical methods to invert these equations for quantiles of
W
and
Z
.

6
Closed-form expressions for the
n
th raw moments of
W
and
Z

It is clear that, by (
16
) and (
17
), the
n
th raw moments of
W
and
Z
can be calculated by the following formulas:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(W^{n})

\displaystyle={1\over 4r\lambda_{1}^{\alpha_{2}}\lambda_{2}^{\alpha_{1}}\text{B}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}\int_{0}^{1}w^{n+{1\over 2r}-1}G(w_{r}){\rm d}w

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Z^{n})

\displaystyle={1\over 4r\lambda_{1}^{\alpha_{2}}\lambda_{2}^{\alpha_{1}}\text{B}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}\int_{0}^{1}{z^{n}(1-z)^{{1\over r}-1}G(z_{r})\over\sqrt{1-(1-z)^{1\over r}}}{\rm d}z,

where
w_{r}
and
z_{r}
are as given in (
11
) and (
14
), respectively, and
G(\cdot)
is as in (
18
). Note that the above integrals are well-defined due to the support of
W
and
Z
in
(0,1)
, but numerical methods are necessary for their evaluation due to analytical complexity.

The following Subsections
6.1
,

,

and

provide some analytical expressions for the raw moments of

and

subject to parameter constraints.

6.1
The
1
st raw moment of
W
: case
r=1/2
and
1/3<\lambda_{2}/\lambda_{1}<2

Under the conditions
r=1/2
and
1/3<\lambda_{2}/\lambda_{1}<2
, in this part, we obtain (more precisely, in (
25
) and Remark
6.2
) straightforward closed-form expressions for
the
1
st raw moment (expected value) of

\displaystyle W=\frac{|X-Y|}{X+Y},

where
X\sim\text{Gamma}(\alpha_{1},\lambda_{1})
and
Y\sim\text{Gamma}(\alpha_{2},\lambda_{2})
are independent.

Indeed, as the function
(0,\infty)\ni x\mapsto z\exp(-zx)
,
z>0
, is the density of an exponential distribution, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-zx){\rm d}x={1\over z}.

(19)

By making
z=X+Y
and by using the fact that
X
and
Y
are independent, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(W)=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{|X-Y|}{X+Y}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[|X-Y|\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left\{-\left(X+Y\right)x\right\}{\rm d}x\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[|X-Y|\exp\left\{-(X+Y)x\right\}\right]{\rm d}x,

(20)

where in the last step, we applied the Tonelli’s theorem
(
Folland, 1999
)
to justify the change in the order of integration.

On the other hand, note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|X-Y|\exp\left\{-(X+Y)x\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\int_{u}^{\infty}(v-u)\exp(-vx){\rm d}F_{Y}(v)\right]\exp(-ux){\rm d}F_{X}(u)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\int_{0}^{u}(u-v)\exp(-vx){\rm d}F_{Y}(v)\right]\exp(-ux){\rm d}F_{X}(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}(v-u)\exp(-vx){\rm d}F_{Y}(v)\right]\exp(-ux){\rm d}F_{X}(u)

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\int_{0}^{u}(v-u)\exp(-vx){\rm d}F_{Y}(v)\right]\exp(-ux){\rm d}F_{X}(u)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\int_{0}^{u}(u-v)\exp(-vx){\rm d}F_{Y}(v)\right]\exp(-ux){\rm d}F_{X}(u).

(21)

Since

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\Bigg[

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}(v-u)\exp(-vx){\rm d}F_{Y}(v)\Bigg]\exp(-ux)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-ux){\rm d}F_{X}(u)\int_{0}^{\infty}v\exp(-vx){\rm d}F_{Y}(v)

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}u\exp(-ux){\rm d}F_{X}(u)\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-vx){\rm d}F_{Y}(v)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(-Xx)\right]\mathbb{E}\left[Y\exp(-Yx)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[X\exp(-Xx)\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(-Yx)\right]

and

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\int_{0}^{u}(v-u)\exp(-vx){\rm d}F_{Y}(v)\right]\exp(-ux){\rm d}F_{X}(u)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\int_{0}^{u}(u-v)\exp(-vx){\rm d}F_{Y}(v)\right]\exp(-ux){\rm d}F_{X}(u)

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\int_{0}^{u}\exp(-vx){\rm d}F_{Y}(v)\right]u\exp(-ux){\rm d}F_{X}(u)

\displaystyle-2\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\int_{0}^{u}v\exp(-vx){\rm d}F_{Y}(v)\right]\exp(-ux){\rm d}F_{X}(u),

the identity in (
21
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|X-Y|\exp\left\{-(X+Y)x\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(-Xx)\right]\mathbb{E}\left[Y\exp(-Yx)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[X\exp(-Xx)\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(-Yx)\right]

\displaystyle+2\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\int_{0}^{u}\exp(-vx){\rm d}F_{Y}(v)\right]u\exp(-ux){\rm d}F_{X}(u)

\displaystyle-2\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\int_{0}^{u}v\exp(-vx){\rm d}F_{Y}(v)\right]\exp(-ux){\rm d}F_{X}(u),

(22)

where

X\sim\text{Gamma}(\alpha_{1},\lambda_{1})
and
Y\sim\text{Gamma}(\alpha_{2},\lambda_{2})
.

By using results of Propositions
A.1
and
A.2
in (
22
), it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|X-Y|\exp\left\{-(X+Y)x\right\}\right]

7
Maximum likelihood estimation

Let
\{W_{i}:i=1,\ldots,n\}
and
\{Z_{i}:i=1,\ldots,n\}
be univariate random samples of size
n
from
W
and
Z
with density functions as given in (
16
) and (
17
), respectively, and let
w_{i}
and
z_{i}
be the sample observations of
W_{i}
and
Z_{i}
, respectively. Then, the respective log-likelihood functions for
\bm{\theta}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},r)^{\top}
are given by

\displaystyle\ell(\bm{\theta};\bm{w})

\displaystyle=-\log(4)-\log(r)-{\alpha_{2}}\log(\lambda_{1})-{\alpha_{1}}\log(\lambda_{2})-\log(\text{B}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}))

\displaystyle+\left({{1\over 2r}-1}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(w_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(G(w_{r,i})),

(31)

and

\displaystyle\ell(\bm{\theta};\bm{z})

\displaystyle=-\log(4)-\log(r)-{\alpha_{2}}\log(\lambda_{1})-{\alpha_{1}}\log(\lambda_{2})-\log(\text{B}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}))

\displaystyle+\left({{1\over r}-1}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(1-z_{i})-{1\over 2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left({1-(1-z_{i})^{1\over r}}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(G(z_{r,i})),

(32)

where

\displaystyle w_{r,i}={1+w_{i}^{\!{}^{1\over 2r}}\over 2},\quad z_{r,i}={1+\sqrt{1-(1-z_{i})^{1\over r}}\over 2},\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

(33)

and
G(\cdot)
being as in (
18
). The necessary conditions for the occurrence
of a maximum (or a minimum) of
\ell(\bm{\theta};\bm{w})
and
\ell(\bm{\theta};\bm{z})
are

\displaystyle{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta};\bm{w})\over\partial\alpha_{i}}=0,\quad{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta};\bm{w})\over\partial\lambda_{i}}=0,\quad{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta};\bm{w})\over\partial r}=0,\quad i=1,2,

(34)

and

\displaystyle{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta};\bm{z})\over\partial\alpha_{i}}=0,\quad{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta};\bm{z})\over\partial\lambda_{i}}=0,\quad{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta};\bm{z})\over\partial r}=0,\quad i=1,2,

(35)

respectively. The equations (
34
) and (
35
) are known as the likelihood equations. It is simple to observe that (for
i=1,2
)

\displaystyle{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta};\bm{w})\over\partial\alpha_{1}}=-\log(\lambda_{2})-[\psi(\alpha_{1})-\psi(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})]+\sum_{i=1}^{n}{{\partial G(w_{r,i})\over\partial\alpha_{1}}\over G(w_{r,i})},

\displaystyle{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta};\bm{w})\over\partial\alpha_{2}}=-\log(\lambda_{1})-[\psi(\alpha_{2})-\psi(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})]+\sum_{i=1}^{n}{{\partial G(w_{r,i})\over\partial\alpha_{2}}\over G(w_{r,i})},

\displaystyle{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta};\bm{w})\over\partial\lambda_{1}}=-{\alpha_{2}\over\lambda_{1}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}{{\partial G(w_{r,i})\over\partial\lambda_{1}}\over G(w_{r,i})},

\displaystyle{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta};\bm{w})\over\partial\lambda_{2}}=-{\alpha_{1}\over\lambda_{2}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}{{\partial G(w_{r,i})\over\partial\lambda_{2}}\over G(w_{r,i})},

\displaystyle{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta};\bm{w})\over\partial r}=-{1\over r}-{1\over 2r^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(w_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}{G^{\prime}(w_{r,i})\over G(w_{r,i})}\,{\partial w_{r,i}\over\partial r},

and

\displaystyle{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta};\bm{z})\over\partial\alpha_{1}}=-\log(\lambda_{2})-[\psi(\alpha_{1})-\psi(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})]+\sum_{i=1}^{n}{{\partial G(z_{r,i})\over\partial\alpha_{1}}\over G(z_{r,i})},

\displaystyle{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta};\bm{z})\over\partial\alpha_{2}}=-\log(\lambda_{1})-[\psi(\alpha_{2})-\psi(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})]+\sum_{i=1}^{n}{{\partial G(z_{r,i})\over\partial\alpha_{2}}\over G(z_{r,i})},

\displaystyle{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta};\bm{z})\over\partial\lambda_{1}}=-{\alpha_{2}\over\lambda_{1}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}{{\partial G(z_{r,i})\over\partial\lambda_{1}}\over G(z_{r,i})},

\displaystyle{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta};\bm{z})\over\partial\lambda_{2}}=-{\alpha_{1}\over\lambda_{2}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}{{\partial G(z_{r,i})\over\partial\lambda_{2}}\over G(z_{r,i})},

\displaystyle{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta};\bm{z})\over\partial r}=-{1\over r}-{1\over r^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(1-z_{i})-{1\over 2r^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{(1-z_{i})^{1\over r}\log(1-z_{i})\over 1-(1-z_{i})^{1\over r}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}{G^{\prime}(z_{r,i})\over G(z_{r,i})}\,{\partial z_{r,i}\over\partial r},

where
G^{\prime}(x)
denotes the derivative of
G(x)
, given in (
18
), with respect to
x
, and
{\partial w_{r,i}/\partial r}
and
{\partial z_{r,i}/\partial r}
are the partial partial derivatives of
w_{r,i}
and
z_{r,i}
given in (
33
). In the above,
\psi(x)=\Gamma^{\prime}(x)/\Gamma(x)
denotes the digamma function.

Any nontrivial root
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}=(\widehat{\alpha}_{1},\widehat{\alpha}_{2},\widehat{\lambda}_{1},\widehat{\lambda}_{2},\widehat{r})^{\top}
of the above likelihood equations that provides the absolute maximum of the log-likelihood function is called an maximum likelihood (ML) estimate. Due to the complexity of the maximization problem, numerical optimization methods are required to obtain the ML estimate.

8
Conclusions

We have introduced two flexible families of unit‐interval distributions by applying non‐injective transformations to the ratio of independent gamma variates, obtaining closed‐form expressions for their densities and distribution functions. We have derived quantile representations, moments in special cases, and established a maximum likelihood estimation procedure for joint inference on
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},r)
. As part of future research, it will be of interest to explore regression extensions, Bayesian methods, and applications to bounded‐response data.

\displaystyle={\lambda_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\lambda_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\over\text{B}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}+1)}\,{\,{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+1,1;\alpha_{2}+1;{x+\lambda_{2}\over 2x+\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}}\right)\over(2x+\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})^{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+1}},

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\int_{0}^{u}v\exp(-vx){\rm d}F_{Y}(v)\right]\exp(-ux){\rm d}F_{X}(u)

\displaystyle={\lambda_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\lambda_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\over\text{B}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}\,{\,{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2},1;\alpha_{2}+1;{x+\lambda_{2}\over 2x+\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}}\right)\over(x+\lambda_{2})(2x+\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})^{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}}

\displaystyle-{\lambda_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\lambda_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\over\text{B}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}\,{1\over(x+\lambda_{2})(2x+\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})^{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}},

where
{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;z)
is the hypergeometric function, defined for
|z|<1
by the power series

\displaystyle{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\frac{(a)_{n}(b)_{n}}{(c)_{n}}}{\frac{z^{n}}{n!}}.

(36)

Here
(q)_{n}
is the (rising) Pochhammer symbol, which is defined by:

\displaystyle{\displaystyle(q)_{n}={\begin{cases}1&n=0,\\
q(q+1)\cdots(q+n-1)&n>0.\end{cases}}}

Proof.

It is simple to observe that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{u}\exp(-vx){\rm d}F_{Y}(v)={\lambda_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\over\Gamma(\alpha_{2})(x+\lambda_{2})^{\alpha_{2}}}\,\gamma(\alpha_{2},(x+\lambda_{2})u)

with
\gamma(s,x)=\int_{0}^{x}t^{s-1}\exp(-t){\rm d}t
being the lower incomplete gamma function,
and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{u}v\exp(-vx){\rm d}F_{Y}(v)

\displaystyle={\lambda_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\over\Gamma(\alpha_{2})(x+\lambda_{2})^{\alpha_{2}+1}}\,\,\gamma(\alpha_{2}+1,(x+\lambda_{2})u)

\displaystyle={\lambda_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\over\Gamma(\alpha_{2})(x+\lambda_{2})^{\alpha_{2}+1}}\,\left[\alpha_{2}\gamma(\alpha_{2},(x+\lambda_{2})u)-(x+\lambda_{2})^{\alpha_{2}}u^{\alpha_{2}}\exp\{-(x+\lambda_{2})u\}\right],

where in the last line the well-known recurrence relation:
\gamma(s+1,x)=s\gamma(s,x)-x^{s}\exp(-x)
, have been used.
Consequently,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\int_{0}^{u}\exp(-vx){\rm d}F_{Y}(v)\right]u\exp(-ux){\rm d}F_{X}(u)\\[5.69046pt]
={\lambda_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\lambda_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\over\Gamma(\alpha_{1})\Gamma(\alpha_{2})(x+\lambda_{2})^{\alpha_{2}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{\alpha_{1}}\exp\{-(x+\lambda_{1})u\}\gamma(\alpha_{2},(x+\lambda_{2})u){\rm d}u

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\Bigg[

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{u}v\exp(-vx){\rm d}F_{Y}(v)\Bigg]\exp(-ux){\rm d}F_{X}(u)

\displaystyle={\alpha_{2}\lambda_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\lambda_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\over\Gamma(\alpha_{1})\Gamma(\alpha_{2})(x+\lambda_{2})^{\alpha_{2}+1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{\alpha_{1}-1}\exp\{-(x+\lambda_{1})u\}\gamma(\alpha_{2},(x+\lambda_{2})u){\rm d}u

\displaystyle-{\lambda_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\lambda_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\Gamma(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})\over\Gamma(\alpha_{1})\Gamma(\alpha_{2})}\,{1\over(x+\lambda_{2})(2x+\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})^{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}}.

Finally, by using the identity
\text{B}(a,b)=\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)/\Gamma(a+b)
and the relation
(
D’Aurizio, 2016
)
:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{a-1}\exp(-sx)\gamma(b,\theta x){\rm d}x={\theta^{b}\Gamma(a+b)\over b(s+\theta)^{a+b}}\,_{2}F_{1}\left(a+b,1;b+1;{\theta\over s+\theta}\right),

in the last two identities, the proof follows.
∎

Proposition A.3

(Technical result)
.

For
a,b,c,e>0
such that
ae<2bc
, and for
p+q>1
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}{1\over(ax+b)^{p}(cx+e)^{q}}{\rm d}x={a^{q-1}\over b^{p+q-1}c^{q}(p+q-1)}\,_{2}F_{1}\left(q,p+q-1;p+q;1-{ae\over bc}\right),

where
\,{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;z)
is the hypergeometric function defined in (
36
).

Proof.

Making the change of variable
y=ax/b
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}{1\over(ax+b)^{p}(cx+e)^{q}}{\rm d}x

\displaystyle={a^{q}\over ab^{p-1}c^{q}b^{q}}\int_{0}^{\infty}{1\over(y+1)^{p}({bc\over a}y+e)^{q}}{\rm d}y

\displaystyle={a^{q-1}\over b^{p+q-1}c^{q}}\int_{0}^{\infty}{1\over(y+1)^{p}(y+{ae\over bc})^{q}}{\rm d}y.

(37)

Using the integral representation of hypergeometric functions:

\,{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;z)={\Gamma(c)\over\Gamma(b)\Gamma(c-b)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-b+c-1}(t+1)^{a-c}(t-z+1)^{-a}{\rm d}t,\quad c>b>0,\ -1<z<1,

with
a=q
,
b=p+q-1
,
c=p+q
and
z=1-ae/cb
,
expression in (
A
) becomes

\displaystyle={a^{q-1}\over b^{p+q-1}c^{q}}{\Gamma(p+q-1)\over\Gamma(p+q)}\,_{2}F_{1}\left(q,p+q-1;p+q;1-{ae\over bc}\right),

provided
ae<2bc
. Finally, invoking the well-known identity:
\Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z)
, the proof follows.
∎

Proposition A.4

(Proposition 3.1 of
Dutta et al., 2025
)
.

Let
X
be a real-valued random variable
and
p>0
be a real number. For all
\varepsilon\geqslant 0
and
\delta>0
, the following identity is satisfied:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X^{p}\mathds{1}_{\{\varepsilon<X<\delta\}})=\varepsilon^{p}\mathbb{P}(X>\varepsilon)-\delta^{p}\mathbb{P}(X>\delta)+p\int_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}u^{p-1}\mathbb{P}(X>u){\rm d}u.

For simplicity, in the next proposition we adopt the following notation:

\displaystyle\xi(p)\equiv\int_{1/2}^{1}y^{p}\left[1-\int_{1-y}^{y}f_{X\over X+Y}(u){\rm d}u\right]{\rm d}y.

(38)

Proposition A.5
.

If
X
and
Y
are independent gamma-distributed with parameter vectors
(\alpha_{1},\lambda_{1})^{\top}
and
(\alpha_{2},\lambda_{2})^{\top}
, respectively, such that
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}
, then

\xi(p)={1\over p+1}\Bigg\{{\mathrm{B}(\alpha_{1}+p+1,\alpha_{2})\over\mathrm{B}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}\left[1-I_{1/2}(\alpha_{1}+p+1,\alpha_{2})\right]\\[5.69046pt]
+{\mathrm{B}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}+p+1)\over\mathrm{B}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}\,I_{1/2}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}+p+1)-\left({1\over 2}\right)^{p+1}\Bigg\},

where
p>\max\{-1,-1-\alpha_{1},-1-\alpha_{2}\}
. In the above,
I_{x}(a,b)={\rm B}(x;a,b)/{\rm B}(a,b)
denotes the regularized beta function.

Proof.

Note that
\xi(p)
in (
38
) can be written as

\displaystyle\xi(p)=\int_{1/2}^{1}y^{p}\left[1-F_{X\over X+Y}(y)+F_{X\over X+Y}(1-y)\right]{\rm d}y.

As
F_{X\over X+Y}(1-y)=1-F_{Y\over X+Y}(y)
, the above term simplifies to

\displaystyle=\int_{1/2}^{1}y^{p}[1-F_{X\over X+Y}(y)]{\rm d}y+\int_{1/2}^{1}y^{p}[1-F_{Y\over X+Y}(y)]{\rm d}y.

Applying Proposition
A.4
, the above expression becomes

\displaystyle={1\over p+1}\left\{\mathbb{E}\left[\left({X\over X+Y}\right)^{p+1}\mathds{1}_{\{{1\over 2}<{X\over X+Y}<1\}}\right]-\left({1\over 2}\right)^{p+1}\mathbb{P}\left({X\over X+Y}>{1\over 2}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 170.71652pt\left.+\mathbb{E}\left[\left({Y\over X+Y}\right)^{p+1}\mathds{1}_{\{{1\over 2}<{Y\over X+Y}<1\}}\right]-\left({1\over 2}\right)^{p+1}\mathbb{P}\left({Y\over X+Y}>{1\over 2}\right)\right\}.

(39)

Since
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}
, then, it is clear that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left({X\over X+Y}\right)^{p+1}\mathds{1}_{\{{1\over 2}<{X\over X+Y}<1\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{1\over 2}^{1}\frac{z^{(\alpha_{1}+p+1)-1}(1-z)^{\alpha_{2}-1}}{\mathrm{B}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}{\rm d}z

\displaystyle={\mathrm{B}(\alpha_{1}+p+1,\alpha_{2})\over\mathrm{B}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}\int_{1\over 2}^{1}\frac{z^{(\alpha_{1}+p+1)-1}(1-z)^{\alpha_{2}-1}}{\mathrm{B}(\alpha_{1}+k+1,\alpha_{2})}{\rm d}z

\displaystyle={\mathrm{B}(\alpha_{1}+p+1,\alpha_{2})\over\mathrm{B}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}\left[1-I_{1/2}(\alpha_{1}+p+1,\alpha_{2})\right]

(40)

and, analogously,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left({Y\over X+Y}\right)^{p+1}\mathds{1}_{\{{1\over 2}<{Y\over X+Y}<1\}}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathrm{B}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}+p+1)\over\mathrm{B}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}\left[1-I_{1/2}(\alpha_{2}+p+1,\alpha_{1})\right]

\displaystyle={\mathrm{B}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}+p+1)\over\mathrm{B}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}\,I_{1/2}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}+p+1),

(41)

where in the last step the well-known identity
I_{x}(a,b)=1-I_{1-x}(b,a)
was used.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({X\over X+Y}>{1\over 2}\right)=1-I_{1/2}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}),\quad\mathbb{P}\left({Y\over X+Y}>{1\over 2}\right)=1-I_{1/2}(\alpha_{2},\alpha_{1})=I_{1/2}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}).

(42)

By plugging (
A
), (
A
) and (
42
) in (
39
), the proof follows.
∎
