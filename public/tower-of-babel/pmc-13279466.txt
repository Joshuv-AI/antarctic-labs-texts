Association of Polygenic Risk Scores With Aortic Valve Calcium: The Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis
Authors: Seamus P Whelton, Jie Yao, Clary Clish, Alexis C Wood, Kent D Taylor, Michael J Blaha, Matthew Budoff, Natalie Marrero, George Thanassoulis, Wendy S Post, Xiuqing Guo, Jerome I Rotter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAortic valve calcification (AVC) is the primary process leading to aortic stenosis. We examined whether polygenic risk scores (PRS) are associated with AVC beyond traditional atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk factors.MethodsWe included 6812 participants in MESA (Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis) with computed tomography‐measured AVC at Visit 1. Using previously published PRS we calculated weighted PRS, standardized within each ancestry group. The cross‐sectional association per 1 SD higher PRS with AVC >0 was examined using multivariable logistic regression modeling with Bonferroni correction. The mean age was 62 years old, 53% were female, and 913 (13.4%) had AVC >0 at baseline.ResultsThe PRS for coronary artery disease (hazard ratio [HR], 1.16 [95% CI, 1.07–1.26]), systolic blood pressure (HR, 1.1 [95% CI, 1.020–1.2]), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HR, 1.16 [95% CI, 1.06–1.25]), and lipoprotein(a) (HR, 1.11 [95% CI, 1.02–1.20]) were significantly associated with AVC, whereas the other PRS including coronary artery calcium (HR, 1.02 [95% CI, 0.94–1.10]) and C‐reactive protein (HR, 0.97 [95% CI, 0.89–1.05]) were not. In sex‐stratified analyses, the PRS for coronary artery disease, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, and lipoprotein(a) were significantly associated with AVC >0 for both sexes (P<0.05), whereas the systolic blood pressure PRS was borderline significant for women (HR, 1.10 [95% CI, 0.97–1.25]) and significant for men (HR, 1.11 [95% CI 1.00–1.24]).ConclusionsOur results confirm the role of atherogenic lipids in the pathogenesis of AVC and suggest that systolic blood pressure and the genetic risk factors for CAD are also important risk factors. The lack of association for the coronary artery calcium PRS with AVC >0 strongly suggests significant differences exist in the calcification pathways for AVC and coronary artery calcium.Keywords: aortic stenosis, aortic valve calcium, polygenic risk scoreSubject Categories: Cardiovascular Disease, Epidemiology, Computerized Tomography (CT), Genetics

Clinical Perspective

What Is New?

The polygenic risk scores for coronary artery disease, systolic blood pressure, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, and LP(a) (lipoprotein(a)) were significantly associated with aortic valve calcification (AVC) >0, whereas the polygenic risk scores for coronary artery calcium and C‐reactive protein were not significantly associated with AVC.

The polygenic risk scores for coronary artery disease, systolic blood pressure, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, and LP(a) (lipoprotein(a)) were significantly associated with aortic valve calcification (AVC) >0, whereas the polygenic risk scores for coronary artery calcium and C‐reactive protein were not significantly associated with AVC.

Calcific aortic valve disease is the most common valve disorder in the United States and is characterized by aortic valve calcification (AVC), which is the primary process leading to the development of severe aortic stenosis (AS). 1 Calcific aortic valve disease is a multifactorial disease that is still incompletely understood, although traditional cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk factors are believed to be the primary contributors to the initiation phase of AVC. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 Currently, there are no approved treatment to prevent or slow AVC or AS. Therefore, a better understanding of the causal pathways for AVC, and their relative strengths of association is essential.

Mendelian randomization has identified a significant causal association for Lp(a) (lipoprotein(a)) with AVC and large scale genetic analyses hold the promise of potentially revealing novel physiologic pathways and improving identification of individuals at increased risk of AVC. 6

Concurrently, there is a growing interest in polygenic risk scores (PRS) as a tool to identify individuals at increased risk of CVD and for the identification of causal pathways. 7 , 8 , 9 PRS, which are derived using single‐nucleotide variants (SNVs), have been validated for coronary artery disease (CAD), subclinical atherosclerosis (eg, coronary artery calcium [CAC]), and traditional CVD risk factors. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 Therefore, we sought to examine whether 9 different PRS are significantly associated with AVC, and if these PRS provide additional information beyond traditional CVD risk factors in order to better examine the causal pathways that contribute to AVC.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. MESA (Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis) is a prospective cohort study comprising 6814 participants without known CVD at baseline from 6 field centers across the United States, with the baseline visit conducted between the years 2000 and 2002. A detailed description of the cohort design, and participant sampling has previously been reported. 17 Institutional review boards at each study site reviewed and approved the study. All participants provided written informed consent. There were 2 participants without baseline visit AVC data who were excluded from this analysis. Genotype data were available on the remaining 6812 participants and collected concurrently at the baseline MESA visit (2000–2002).

An Affymetrix 6.0 SNV array was used to perform SNV genotyping. 18 The 1000 Genomes cosmopolitan phase 3 version 5 reference haplotypes were used to impute SNVs. Individuals who were closely related, as determined by pi‐hat relatedness >0.2, were excluded by random removal for 1 of each pair. The EIGENSOFT package was used to examine population stratification using SNV genotypes and to confirm the self‐reported race or ethnicity. 19 , 20 , 21 All ancestry outliers were removed from the analyses. Previously published PRS scores for CAD, CAC, and the CVD risk factors of blood pressure, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), triglycerides, Lp(a), CRP (C‐reactive protein), and diabetes were used for these analyses (Table 1). The MESA cohort comprised a small percentage of the derivation cohorts for the following PRS: systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), CRP, CAC, LDL‐C, triglycerides, and diabetes but not for the PRS for CAD or Lp(a). The PRS derivation cohorts were multiethnic cohorts, except for the cohorts used for the BP PRS. The weighted PRS for each participant was calculated as PRSi=∑ikβ^j×dosageij where PRSi is the polygenic score for subject i; dosage ij is the posterior probability of being a heterozygous (probability ≈1.0) or homozygous (probability ≈2.0) effect allele carrier after imputations for subject i and variant j; κ is the number of independent variants in the polygenic score for subject i; and β^j is the weight for variant j obtained from genome‐wide association study summary statistics. The PRS was then standardized within each ancestry group, starting with self‐reported ancestry followed by exclusion of outliers based on principle components. 19

AVC was quantified at MESA Visit 1 via electronic beam computed tomography scanner at 3 field centers and multidetector computed tomography at the other 3 field centers. These 2 imaging modalities have equivalent reproducibility for the measurement of cardiac calcification. 22 The scan images were processed and interpreted at a centralized reading center (Harbor‐UCLA). AVC was defined as calcification of the aortic valve leaflets and calcification that extended from the aortic valve leaflets into the aortic root. 23 The scans were performed without intravenous contrast and the Agatston method was used to quantify AVC values. 24 Accordingly, calcified lesions were included only if they had a peak density of at least 130 Hounsfield units and an area of at least 1 mm2, in order to avoid false positive values. There was low intrareader (4.4%) and interscan (9.7%) variability for AVC scores. 23 For these analyses AVC was quantified as absent (AVC=0) or prevalent (AVC >0) as our primary aim was to examine whether or not the individual PRS are causally associated with AVC rather than the association with the burden or amount of AVC. 25 We also performed sensitivity analyses examining the association of AVC as a continuous variable. AVC was examined as a log transformed variable using ln(AVC +1), because it is zero inflated and right skewed. 26

Baseline demographic information and medical history including sex, race, ethnicity, and smoking status was self‐reported at Visit 1. SBP and DBP were measured in triplicate and recorded as the mean of the second and third values. Hypertension was defined as ≥140/90 mm Hg or use of an antihypertensive medication. Triglycerides were measured using overnight fasting samples sent to the Collaborative Studies Clinical Research Laboratory (Minneapolis, MN). LDL‐C was calculated using the Friedewald method, except for participants with triglycerides ≥400 mg/dL in whom LDL‐C was not calculated. 27 Lp(a) was measured using a latex‐enhanced turbidimetric immunoassay (Denka Siken) by Health Diagnostic Laboratory (Richmond, VA). 28 hs‐CRP (high‐sensitivity CRP) was measured using the BNII nephelometer via a particle‐enhanced immunonephelometric assay (N High Sensitivity CRP, Dade Behring Inc, Deerfield, IL). 29 Diabetes was defined as a fasting glucose of ≥126 mg/dL or the use of blood glucose lowering medication. Hypertension was defined as a BP of ≥140/90 mm Hg to be consistent with prior MESA publications, a definition used by some but not all BP guidelines. 30 , 31

Statistical Analysis

Demographic data including CVD risk factor information were stratified by the presence or absence of AVC. Differences between groups were evaluated using the Student’s t test for quantitative traits, and chi‐square test for binary traits. We tested the association of each of the individual 9 PRS with the matching clinical phenotype to ensure that the PRS were significantly associated with the expected traits. Pairwise correlation was performed for the 9 PRS to evaluate the potential overlapping effect. To examine the association between each individual PRS and the risk of AVC>0, we carried out logistic regression analysis for each PRS using progressively adjusted models as follows:

Model 1: age, race, ethnicity, sex, field center

Model 1: age, race, ethnicity, sex, field center

Model 2: model 1 + hypertension, diabetes, LDL‐C, triglycerides, hs‐CRP, and smoking status

Model 2: model 1 + hypertension, diabetes, LDL‐C, triglycerides, hs‐CRP, and smoking status

Model 3: model 2 + Lp(a).

Model 3: model 2 + Lp(a).

Stratified multivariable logistic regression modeling was also performed to examine for potential differences by sex and race or ethnicity and reported graphically using Model 1 adjustment variables. A Bonferroni corrected P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant for the primary analyses. For secondary analyses of sex and race or ethnicity stratified differences, a non‐Bonferroni corrected P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant due to the lower power of these stratified analyses. Logistic regression modeling was also performed with stepwise backwards elimination of the PRS to examine which PRS were independently associated with AVC. We examined the continuous variables 1 SD higher value PRS score in order to maintain comparability due to the different measurement values. For categorical variables, the reference group was absence of disease or risk factor. For sensitivity analyses of AVC as a continuous variable, linear regression modeling was used adjusted for the variables in model 1.

In order to further examine the causal association between SBP and LDL‐C with AVC, Mendelian randomization was performed using 1780 SNVs PRS for SBP traits and 442 SNVs for LDL‐C traits. We did not perform Mendelian randomization to examine the association between CAD and AVC, as CAD is not thought to be a direct causal risk factor for AVC and our primary goal is to examine risk pathways for AVC. Instead, we performed sensitivity analyses for the CAD PRS stratified by concordance and discordance of AVC and incident CAD.

RESULTS

Overall, participants with AVC >0 were older, more likely to be male, and had a greater burden of CVD risk factors (Table 2). White participants had the highest prevalence of AVC >0 (45.3%) and Chinese participants had the lowest prevalence of AVC >0 (7.4%). As regards relationships between the PRS, there was a moderate correlation between SBP and DBP, but otherwise the correlation between the individual PRS was low (Figure 1). Each of the 9 individual PRS were very strongly associated with the matching clinical phenotype (Table 3).

In Mendelian randomization analyses, for every 5 mm Hg higher in SBP there was a 16% higher odds for AVC >0 (OR, 1.16 [95% CI, 1.05–1.28], P=0.002) and for every 10 mg/dL higher LDL‐C there was a 21% higher odds for AVC >0 (OR, 1.207 [95% CI, 1.11–1.32], P<0.001). The CAD PRS was significantly associated with AVC alone, CAD alone, AVC without incident CAD, and incident CAD without AVC (Table S4). Sensitivity analyses examining the 4 PRS significantly associated with AVC >0 also showed a significant association with AVC when AVC was examined as a continuous variable (Table S5).

DISCUSSION

To best of our knowledge this is the first article to investigate the association of individual PRS with AVC. We show that PRS for CAD, SBP, LDL‐C, and Lp(a) are significantly associated with an increased risk for AVC >0. Our PRS results are consistent with prior genome‐wide association study analyses demonstrating that LDL‐C and Lp(a) are associated with AVC. Furthermore, these results provide insights into other risk factors for AVC and the pathobiology of AVC. Of particular interest, these results suggest that SBP is an important and underrecognized risk factor for AVC. Of further importance, the significant results for the CAD PRS suggest that the genetic pathways leading to CAD and AVC are shared. In contrast, our results highlight that the genetic pathways for calcification of the aortic valve and coronary arteries are different. Additionally, the nonsignificant results for the CRP PRS support prior findings that inflammation measured by CRP is not an independent or causal risk factor for AVC, similar to other findings that CRP is not a causal risk factor for CAD. 4 , 32 , 33

It is well established that AVC and CAD have shared risk factors. 3 However, our study also demonstrates that beyond shared risk factors such as hyperlipidemia and hypertension, the underlying genetic pathways that promote CAD itself are shared with AVC. Accordingly, when we examined the correlation between the different PRS, there was little overlap between the CAD PRS and the PRS for individual risk factors such as LDL‐C, Lp(a), etc, which strongly suggests pathways beyond traditional atherosclerotic CVD risk factors are shared by CAD and AVC. Understanding these differences and commonalities may provide insights into novel treatment targets and for therapies that may have the potential for preventing both CAD and AVC.

In contrast to the shared genetic pathways for AVC and CAD, we found that the PRS for CAC was not associated with AVC. This finding supports our understanding of CAC as part of the atherosclerosis process within the intima of the coronary vasculature, while AVC is a distinct process on the nonvascular aortic valve leaflets. 34 , 35 Coronary atherosclerotic plaques begin as noncalcified lipid rich plaques. Over time the death of smooth muscle, oxidative stress, and inflammation promote intimal calcification. Healing of ruptured coronary plaques also contributes of the development of CAC. 36 , 37 , 38 On the other hand, aortic valve leaflets become damaged over time leading to fibrosis and calcification. Lipid deposition within the aortic valves, particularly oxidized lipids promote calcification via fibroblasts. 39 In the later stages of AVC and AS, the AVC process is driven primarily by osteogenic pathways. 40 , 41 We also found that PRS for CRP was not significantly associated with AVC. Although inflammation is commonly considered a risk factor for AVC, our work and that of others have demonstrated that higher hs‐CRP levels were not independently associated with AVC or AVC progression. 4 , 32 However, higher hs‐CRP appears to modify the risk for AVC, as higher hsCRP levels in conjunction with higher Lp(a) levels were associated with a much higher risk for AVC than higher Lp(a) levels alone in the MESA study. 4 Accordingly, these results highlight the important of further work to better understand the concordant and discordant pathways for CAD and AVC.

Currently, there are no approved medications to treat AS and the only intervention is aortic valve replacement. To date, efforts toward slowing the progression of AVC and preventing severe AS have focused on LDL‐C lowering with the largest trials showing no benefit. 42 , 43 , 44 However, our results showing a significant association of the LDL‐C and Lp(a) PRS with AVC further support the importance of these causal atherogenic lipid pathways and the need for further investigation into lipid‐lowering therapies to prevent the formation of AVC or progression to severe AS. Indeed, exploratory analyses have suggested benefit among appropriately selected participants and for individuals treatment with PCSK9 (proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9) inhibitors. 45 , 46 Lp(a), which is ≈90% genetically determined is a strong risk factor for AVC and has been implicated as stronger atherogenic risk factor than LDL‐C. 4 , 47 , 48 In this regard, the Lp(a)FRONTIERS CAVS (Randomized Double‐Blind, Placebo‐Controlled, Multicenter Trial Assessing the Impact of Lipoprotein(a) Lowering With Pelacarsen [TQJ230] on the Progression of Calcific Aortic Valve Stenosis) trial will begin enrollment soon to test the Lp(a) lowering agent Pelacarsen.

Beyond lipid‐lowering therapies, our findings show that SBP is an important underappreciated risk factor for AVC. A small number of studies have shown expression of angiotensin‐converting enzymes and angiotensin II Type I on aortic valve macrophages. 49 , 50 A recent Mendelian randomization of plasma protein levels has also recently linked angiotensin‐converting enzymes as a causal pathway for the development of AS and suggested that the risk for AS associated with angiotensin converting enzyme exposure may be extend beyond SBP level. 51 An association between hypertension with calcific aortic valve disease has also been observed, although these studies were primarily conducted among older adults and examined AVC or AS measured by echocardiography, which does not detect calcific aortic valve disease until a later stage of the disease compared with computed tomography measured AVC. 52 , 53 , 54 , 55 Additionally, our results show an OR of relatively comparable magnitude for the PRS of SBP, LDL‐C, and Lp(a), suggesting that the relative magnitude of contribution to AVC for elevated SBP may be in keeping with that of atherogenic lipids.

In our study the primary aim was to explore underlying pathways for AVC. However, there is a growing interest in the use of PRS to identify individuals at increased risk for CVD. 7 To date studies have shown mixed results for whether or not the PRS improve CVD risk prediction beyond traditional risk factors or CAC scoring. 8 , 9 , 19 , 56 , 57 However, an important distinction is that currently there is no recommended screening algorithm for AVC or AS. Therefore, our results showing that individuals with an elevated PRS for either LDL‐C, Lp(a), SBP, or CAD are more likely to have AVC is an incremental step toward developing a method for the identification of individuals who have an increased risk for AVC. Future studies are planned to develop a specific PRS for AVC.

An important limitation of this study is that the analyses are cross‐sectional. Aside from the PRS for SBP and DBP, the other PRS were derived from multiethnic cohorts, although European ancestry was the predominant race or ethnicity for all PRS derivation cohorts. The MESA cohort is part of the CHARGE (Cohorts for Heart and Aging Research in Genomic Epidemiology) and GLGC (Global Lipids Genetic Consortium), which were used to derive the PRS for some (eg.,LDL‐C, triglycerides, and CRP, etc) but not all of the PRS. However, the MESA cohort comprised <5% of participants for the CHARGE Consortium and<1% of participants for the GLGC. 15 , 16 Strengths of this analysis include the 9 different individual PRS, which had a low correlation with each other, except for SBP and DBP. The use of PRS also allows for examination of causal associations to determine underlying pathways for AVC.

CONCLUSIONS

These results confirm the importance of atherogenic lipids as risk factors for AVC and provide important insights to help better understand nonlipid pathways underlying AVC. Importantly, we show that the PRS for SBP is significantly associated with AVC, highlighting SBP as an important underrecognized risk factor for AVC and as a potential treatment target. These results also demonstrate shared genetic pathways for CAD and AVC but different pathways for calcification of the aortic valves and coronary arteries. Overall, these results help to better understand the pathogenesis and risk factors for AVC, which is vital in the ongoing process to identify treatment strategies to slow or prevent the development of AVC and AS.
