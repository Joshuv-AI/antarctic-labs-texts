[

By splitting the input polynomials into high and low-degree parts, the algorithm recursively computes the quotients of the high-degree parts and uses those quotients to adjust the low-degree parts. Te complexity of this algorithm is

Hankel continued fraction and generalized Sturm sequence

Definition 2.1
.

Consider
f_{0}(x)
and
f_{1}(x)
as polynomials where
\deg(f_{0})>\deg(f_{1})
.
The
generalized Sturm sequence

\{f_{i}(x)\}_{i=0}^{s+1}
where
f_{s+1}(x)=0
is constructed as follows:
We define the sequence by

f_{i+2}(x)=-\emph{\texttt{rem}}(f_{i}(x),f_{i+1}(x)).

where
\emph{\texttt{rem}}(f_{i}(x),f_{i+1}(x))
denotes the remainder
obtained by dividing
f_{i}(x)
by
f_{i+1}(x)
.

We assume that the sequence
\{f_{i}(x)\}_{i=0}^{s+1}
satisfies the relations:

f_{i}(x)=B_{i}(x)f_{i+1}(x)-f_{i+2}(x),\quad 0\leq i\leq s-1.

Furthermore, we denote
m_{i}=\deg(B_{i})
and
b_{i}
represents the leading coefficient of
B_{i}(x)
.
Note that when
f_{1}(x)=f^{\prime}(x)
, it is called the (traditional)
Sturm sequence
of
f(x)
.

It is evident that

\displaystyle\deg(f_{i})=\deg(B_{i})+\deg(f_{i+1})\quad\text{and}\quad\deg(B_{i})\geq 1.

(2)

Given a polynomial
f(x)
, we denote the transformation
{f}^{\looparrowleft}(x)=x^{\deg(f)}\cdot f(x^{-1})
, where “
↫
” signifies the reversal of the polynomial. For instance, if
f(x)=3x^{2}+4x^{3}+6x^{5}
, then
{f}^{\looparrowleft}(x)=6+4x^{2}+3x^{3}
.
Note that
\deg({f}^{\looparrowleft})=\deg(f)-\operatorname{ldeg}(f)
, where
\operatorname{ldeg}(f)
denotes the lowest degree of the polynomial
f(x)
.

Thus, for
0\leq i\leq s-2
, we can verify that

\displaystyle{f}^{\looparrowleft}_{i}(x)={B}^{\looparrowleft}_{i}(x)\cdot{f}^{\looparrowleft}_{i+1}(x)-x^{\deg(f_{i})-\deg(f_{i+2})}\cdot{f}^{\looparrowleft}_{i+2}(x).

(3)

For
0\leq i\leq s-2
, by (
2
), we have

\displaystyle\deg(f_{i})-\deg(f_{i+2})

\displaystyle=\deg(B_{i})+\deg(f_{i+1})-\deg(f_{i+2})

\displaystyle=\deg(B_{i})+\deg(B_{i+1})

\displaystyle=m_{i}+m_{i+1}.

At the terminal step, since
f_{s+1}(x)=0
, we have

f_{s-1}(x)=B_{s-1}(x)f_{s}(x),

and hence

{f}^{\looparrowleft}_{s-1}(x)={B}^{\looparrowleft}_{s-1}(x){f}^{\looparrowleft}_{s}(x).

Rewriting these identities yields the following lemma.

Lemma 2.2
.

Following the notations in Definition
2.1
. For
0\leq i\leq s-2
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{x^{m_{i}-1}{f}^{\looparrowleft}_{i+1}(x)}{{f}^{\looparrowleft}_{i}(x)}=\cfrac{x^{m_{i}-1}}{{B}^{\looparrowleft}_{i}(x)-\cfrac{x^{(m_{i}-1)+(m_{i+1}-1)+2}{f}^{\looparrowleft}_{i+2}(x)}{{f}^{\looparrowleft}_{i+1}(x)}}.

(4)

For the terminal index
i=s-1
, we have

\frac{x^{m_{s-1}-1}{f}^{\looparrowleft}_{s}(x)}{{f}^{\looparrowleft}_{s-1}(x)}=\frac{x^{m_{s-1}-1}}{{B}^{\looparrowleft}_{s-1}(x)}.

Moreover,
\deg({B}^{\looparrowleft}_{i})\leq m_{i}
for all
0\leq i\leq s-1
, and the constant term of
{B}^{\looparrowleft}_{i}(x)
is nonzero for all
0\leq i\leq s-1
.

Lemma
2.2
precisely establishes the H-continued fraction representation of

S_{f_{0},f_{1}}(x):=x^{m_{0}-1}\frac{{f}^{\looparrowleft}_{1}(x)}{{f}^{\looparrowleft}_{0}(x)},\qquad m_{0}=\deg(f_{0})-\deg(f_{1}).

We denote this expression by
S_{f_{0},f_{1}}(x)
for brevity in the subsequent discussion. We now state the main result of this section.

Theorem 2.3
.

Following the notations in Definition
2.1
.
If
r=m_{0}+m_{1}+\cdots+m_{\kappa-1}
for a certain
1\leq\kappa\leq s
, then we have

\displaystyle H_{r}\left(S_{f_{0},f_{1}}(x)\right)=(-1)^{{\sum_{i=0}^{\kappa-1}\frac{m_{i}(m_{i}-1)}{2}}}\frac{1}{\prod_{i=0}^{\kappa-2}b_{i}^{2(r-\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}m_{j})-m_{i}}\cdot b_{\kappa-1}^{m_{\kappa-1}}}.

Otherwise,

H_{r}\left(S_{f_{0},f_{1}}(x)\right)=0.

Proof.

Using Lemma
2.2
recursively, we have the H-continued fraction of
S_{f_{0},f_{1}}(x)
:

\displaystyle S_{f_{0},f_{1}}(x)=\cfrac{b_{0}^{-1}x^{m_{0}-1}}{({B}^{\looparrowleft}_{0}(x)/b_{0})-\cfrac{(b_{0}b_{1})^{-1}x^{(m_{0}-1)+(m_{1}-1)+2}}{({B}^{\looparrowleft}_{1}(x)/b_{1})-\cfrac{(b_{1}b_{2})^{-1}x^{(m_{1}-1)+(m_{2}-1)+2}}{({B}^{\looparrowleft}_{2}(x)/b_{2})-\ddots}}}.

(5)

According to Theorem
1.2
, we have two cases.
If there is no
\kappa
such that
r=m_{0}+m_{1}+\cdots+m_{\kappa-1}
, then

H_{r}\left(S_{f_{0},f_{1}}(x)\right)=0.

If
r=m_{0}+m_{1}+\cdots+m_{\kappa-1}
for a certain
\kappa
, then we have

\displaystyle H_{r}\left(S_{f_{0},f_{1}}(x)\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\epsilon}\frac{1}{(b_{0})^{r}\ (b_{0}b_{1})^{r-m_{0}}\cdots(b_{\kappa-2}b_{\kappa-1})^{r-\sum_{i=0}^{\kappa-2}m_{i}}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\epsilon}\frac{1}{\prod_{i=0}^{\kappa-2}b_{i}^{2(r-\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}m_{j})-m_{i}}\cdot b_{\kappa-1}^{r-\sum_{i=0}^{\kappa-2}m_{i}}},

where
\epsilon={\sum_{i=0}^{\kappa-1}m_{i}(m_{i}-1)}/{2}
.

This completes the proof.
∎

This theorem also establishes the following recursive formula for the determinant

H_{r}\left(S_{f_{0},f_{1}}(x)\right)
.

Corollary 2.4
.

Following the notations in Definition
2.1
.
Let
0\leq\kappa\leq s-1
and set

r=m_{0}+\cdots+m_{\kappa-1}
, with the convention
r=0
if
\kappa=0
.
Then
H_{t}(S_{f_{0},f_{1}})=0
for
r<t<r+m_{\kappa}
, and

H_{r+m_{\kappa}}(S_{f_{0},f_{1}})=(-1)^{\frac{m_{\kappa}(m_{\kappa}-1)}{2}}\left(b_{\kappa}\prod_{i=0}^{\kappa-1}b_{i}^{2}\right)^{-m_{\kappa}}H_{r}(S_{f_{0},f_{1}}),

where the empty product is understood to be
1
.
The initial condition is
H_{0}(S_{f_{0},f_{1}})=1
.

3.1
Algorithm for Hankel determinants

In this section, we address the following problem:

Problem 3.1
.

Compute the initial
n
Hankel determinants for a specified rational power series
h(x)
.

Remark 3.2
.

To compute the first
n
Hankel determinants for any given power series
r(x)
, we can define
h(x)
be the polynomial
r(x)\bmod x^{2n-1}
.

As established in Section
2
, if the polynomials
f_{1}(x)
,
f_{0}(x)
, and the rational power series
h(x)
fulfill the criteria:

\displaystyle m_{0}=\deg(f_{0})-\deg(f_{1})>0\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ S_{f_{0},f_{1}}(x)=x^{m_{0}-1}\frac{{f}^{\looparrowleft}_{1}(x)}{{f}^{\looparrowleft}_{0}(x)}=h(x)\bmod x^{2n-1},

(6)

then the generalized Sturm sequence of
(f_{0}(x),f_{1}(x))
yields the Hankel continued fraction of
h(x)
in the sense of modulo
x^{2n-1}
.

For a nonzero polynomial
P(x)
, let
\operatorname{ldeg}(P)
denote
the lowest degree of
P(x)
.

We now present a specific selection of
f_{1}(x)
and
f_{0}(x)
as outlined below.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let

h(x)=\frac{N(x)}{D(x)}\in K[[x]],

where
N(x)\neq 0
and
D(0)\neq 0
. Define

\nu=\deg N-\deg D+1.

Construct
f_{0}(x)
and
f_{1}(x)
as follows:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}f_{0}(x)={D}^{\looparrowleft}(x),&f_{1}(x)=x^{-\nu}{N}^{\looparrowleft}(x),\quad\text{if }\nu<0,\\[4.0pt]
f_{0}(x)=x^{\nu}{D}^{\looparrowleft}(x),&f_{1}(x)={N}^{\looparrowleft}(x),\quad\text{if }\nu\geq 0.\end{array}\right.

Then
f_{0}(x)
and
f_{1}(x)
meet the requirements of condition
(
6
). Moreover, for
\nu<0
, we have

\deg(f_{0})=\deg D,\qquad\deg(f_{1})=\deg D-\operatorname{ldeg}(N)-1,

and for
\nu\geq 0
, we have

\deg(f_{0})=\deg N+1,\qquad\deg(f_{1})=\deg N-\operatorname{ldeg}(N).

If, in addition,

\gcd(N(x),D(x))=1,

then

\gcd(f_{0}(x),f_{1}(x))=1.

Proof.

Since
D(0)\neq 0
, we have

\deg({D}^{\looparrowleft})=\deg D,\qquad\deg({N}^{\looparrowleft})=\deg N-\operatorname{ldeg}(N).

Verifying that
f_{0}(x)
and
f_{1}(x)
fulfill condition (
6
) is straightforward.

It remains to prove the last assertion. Suppose that

\gcd(N(x),D(x))=1.

Then
N(x)
and
D(x)
have no common roots. Consequently,
x^{\deg(N)}N(x^{-1})
and
x^{\deg(D)}D(x^{-1})
also have distinct roots, implying that
\gcd(f_{0}(x),f_{1}(x))=1
. This completes the proof.
∎

Kronecker’s lemma states that a formal power series is rational if and only if its Hankel determinants are ultimately
zero
[
19
, p.5, Kronecker Lemma]
. The following corollary gives a short proof of the rational-implies-eventual-vanishing direction.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
h(x)=\frac{N(x)}{D(x)}
be a rational power series, where
N(x)\neq 0
,
D(0)\neq 0
, and
\gcd(N(x),D(x))=1
. Let

d=\max(\deg(N)+1,\deg(D)).

Then
H_{d}(h(x))\neq 0
and
H_{t}(h(x))=0
for all
t>d
.

Proof.

Let
f_{0}(x)
and
f_{1}(x)
be the polynomials as specified in Lemma
3.3
, and consider
\{f_{i}(x)\}_{i=0}^{s+1}
as the generalized Sturm sequence. By Lemma
3.3
, we have
\deg(f_{0})=d
. Since
\gcd(f_{0}(x),f_{1}(x))=1
, it is inferred that
f_{s}(x)
is a nonzero constant. Applying Theorem
2.3
, the fact that
f_{s}(x)
is a nonzero constant implies

\sum_{i=0}^{s-1}m_{i}=\deg(f_{0})=d.

Consequently,
{H}_{d}(h(x))\neq 0
and
{H}_{r}(h(x))=0
for all
r>d
.
∎

We summarize the preceding discussion in the following Hankel determinants algorithm. For a
polynomial
P(x)
and a positive integer
L
, write

P^{[L]}(x):=P(x)\bmod x^{L}.

Algorithm 3.5

(
CompHD
)
.

Input: A rational power series

h(x)=\frac{N(x)}{D(x)}\in K[[x]],\qquad D(0)\neq 0,

and a positive integer
n
.

Output: The Hankel determinant sequence

H_{1}(h),H_{2}(h),\ldots,H_{n}(h).

0.

Set
L=2n-1
, and replace
N(x)
and
D(x)
by

N^{[L]}(x)=N(x)\bmod x^{L},\qquad D^{[L]}(x)=D(x)\bmod x^{L}.

This truncation is valid because

h(x)\equiv\frac{N^{[L]}(x)}{D^{[L]}(x)}\pmod{x^{L}}.

In particular, if
N^{[L]}(x)=0
, then return

H_{1}(h)=H_{2}(h)=\cdots=H_{n}(h)=0.

1.

Construct
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
from

N^{[L]}(x)
and
D^{[L]}(x)
as in Lemma
3.3
.

2.

Compute the quotients
B_{0},B_{1},\ldots
in the generalized Sturm
sequence of
(f_{0},f_{1})
until

m_{0}+\cdots+m_{j}\geq\min(n,\deg f_{0}).

If the generalized Sturm sequence terminates before the cumulative degree
reaches
n
, set all remaining Hankel determinants equal to zero.

3
.

Set
H_{0}(h)=1
. Then use Corollary
2.4
to compute

H_{i}(h)
recursively for
1\leq i\leq n
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
h(x)={N(x)}/{D(x)}
be a rational power series with

D(0)\neq 0
. The Hankel determinant sequence

\{H_{i}(h(x))\}_{i=1}^{n}

can be computed in
O(n\log^{2}n)
field operations using Algorithm

3.5
.

Proof.

Set
L=2n-1>0
. By Step 0 of Algorithm
3.5
, we replace

N(x)
and
D(x)
by

N^{[L]}(x)=N(x)\bmod x^{L},\qquad D^{[L]}(x)=D(x)\bmod x^{L}.

Then

\deg N^{[L]}<L,\qquad\deg D^{[L]}<L.

Since
D(0)\neq 0
, we have

h(x)\equiv\frac{N^{[L]}(x)}{D^{[L]}(x)}\pmod{x^{L}}.

For
1\leq r\leq n
, the determinant
H_{r}(h)
depends only on
the coefficients of
h(x)
up to degree
2r-2\leq 2n-2=L-1
.
Hence this truncation does not change
H_{1}(h),\ldots,H_{n}(h)
.

By Lemma
3.3
, the polynomials
f_{0}(x)
and

f_{1}(x)
constructed from
N^{[L]}(x)
and
D^{[L]}(x)
satisfy

h(x)=x^{m_{0}-1}\frac{{f}^{\looparrowleft}_{1}(x)}{{f}^{\looparrowleft}_{0}(x)}\pmod{x^{2n-1}}.

Moreover, since
\deg N^{[L]},\deg D^{[L]}<L
, the construction of

f_{0}(x)
and
f_{1}(x)
requires
O(2n-1)

operations.

The correctness of the remaining steps follows from Theorem

2.3
and Corollary
2.4
.

Regarding the complexity of Step 2, it is observed that Corollary
2.4
and Theorem
2.3
require only the degrees
m_{i}
and
leading coefficients
b_{i}

of the
B_{i}(x)

rather than the full polynomials
f_{i}(x)
for

at most
n
.
This is where the half-GCD algorithm comes into play, allowing the computation of
B_{i}
in
O(n\log^{2}n)
field operations, as detailed in
[
24
, Section 11]
.

For the complexity of Step 3, within a single recursion, we initially require approximately two multiplications to construct

\left(b_{\kappa+1}\prod_{i=0}^{\kappa}b_{i}^{2}\right)
from
\left(b_{\kappa}\prod_{i=0}^{\kappa-1}b_{i}^{2}\right)
. Additionally, we need approximately
\log(m_{\kappa})
steps to compute the
m_{\kappa}
-th power of
\left(b_{\kappa}\prod_{i=0}^{\kappa-1}b_{i}^{2}\right)
. Over the entire recursion, the total number of operations in the field is bounded by

\sum_{\kappa=0}^{s-1}2+\log(m_{\kappa})\leq 2s+s\log(n/s)\leq 2s+\frac{n}{e\ln(2)}\in O(n),

which confirms the claimed complexity.

∎

\displaystyle=\frac{91125}{33536}x-\frac{2492775}{4393216},\qquad B_{3}(x)=-\frac{143877824}{61509375}x-\frac{254394664}{184528125},

\displaystyle B_{4}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{2767921875}{207033444463}x-\frac{2984742421875}{61281899561048}.

Thus

m_{0}=3,\qquad m_{1}=m_{2}=m_{3}=m_{4}=1,

and the cumulative degrees are

3,\ 4,\ 5,\ 6,\ 7.

Step 3: Let
b_{i}=\operatorname{lc}(B_{i})
. Using Corollary
2.4
with the initial convention

H_{0}(h(x))=1.

Since
m_{0}=3
, we have
we first obtain

and

, we get

3.2
The signature of Hankel matrix and the sign variation of generalized Sturm sequence

Consider a nonsingular symmetric matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with all nonzero sequential principal minors

\Delta_{0}=1,\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{n}.

Define
\epsilon_{v}
as the number of sign variations
and
\epsilon_{p}
as the number of sign permanences in
\{\Delta_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
.
It is known that the signature
\epsilon_{A}
of
A
is

\epsilon_{p}-\epsilon_{v}

. But this formula becomes inapplicable when some sequential principal minors are zero.
However, Frobenius demonstrated that a signature formula exists for Hankel matrices in the general case.

Lemma 3.8
.

[
8
, Section 10]

For nonsingular Hankel matrix
H\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
with sequential principal minors

H_{0}=1,H_{1},\ldots,H_{n}.

Suppose
H_{k}\neq 0
only when
k=r_{0},\ldots,r_{s}
, and define

\epsilon_{r_{i-1},r_{i}}=\begin{cases}(-1)^{\frac{r_{i}-r_{i-1}-1}{2}}\ \emph{sign}\left(\frac{H_{r_{i}}}{H_{r_{i-1}}}\right)&\text{if }r_{i}-r_{i-1}\text{ is odd;}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then the signature
\epsilon_{H}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\epsilon_{r_{i-1},r_{i}}
.

For
0\leq t\leq s-1
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
, let

\mathcal{V}_{t}(c)
denote the number of sign variations, ignoring zeros,
in the finite sequence

(f_{0}(c),f_{1}(c),\ldots,f_{t+1}(c)).

We define

\mathcal{V}_{t}(+\infty)=\lim_{c\to+\infty}\mathcal{V}_{t}(c),\qquad\mathcal{V}_{t}(-\infty)=\lim_{c\to-\infty}\mathcal{V}_{t}(c).

By Lemma
3.8
and Corollary
2.4
, we obtain the following theorem:

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
h(x)\in\mathbb{R}[[x]]
, and let
f_{0}(x),f_{1}(x)\in\mathbb{R}[x]

satisfy condition (
6
). Let

f_{i}(x)=B_{i}(x)f_{i+1}(x)-f_{i+2}(x),\quad m_{i}=\deg B_{i},\quad b_{i}=\operatorname{lc}(B_{i}),\ (\text{the leading coefficient of }B_{i}(x).)

be the associated generalized Sturm sequence data as in Definition

2.1
. Suppose that the Hankel matrix

\mathcal{H}_{n}(h(x))
is nonsingular. Then there exists

0\leq t\leq s-1
such that

\sum_{i=0}^{t}m_{i}=n.

Moreover,

\epsilon_{\mathcal{H}_{n}(h)}=\mathcal{V}_{t}(-\infty)-\mathcal{V}_{t}(+\infty)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i\leq t\\
m_{i}\ \mathrm{odd}\end{subarray}}\operatorname{sign}(b_{i}).

Proof.

By Theorem
2.3
, since
H_{n}(h(x))\neq 0
, there exists
0\leq t\leq s-1
such that
\sum_{i=0}^{t}m_{i}=n
.
Set

r_{i}=m_{0}+\cdots+m_{i-1},\qquad r_{0}=0.

Then Corollary
2.4
gives

\operatorname{sign}\left(\frac{H_{r_{i}+m_{i}}(h(x))}{H_{r_{i}}(h(x))}\right)=(-1)^{m_{i}(m_{i}-1)/2}\operatorname{sign}(b_{i}^{m_{i}}).

Applying Lemma
3.8
, we find

\epsilon_{\mathcal{H}_{n}(h(x))}=\sum_{i=0,\ m_{i}\text{ is odd}}^{t}(-1)^{\frac{m_{i}-1}{2}}\text{sign}\left(\frac{H_{r+m_{i}}(h(x))}{H_{r}(h(x))}\right)=\sum_{i=0,m_{i}\text{ odd}}^{t}\text{sign}(b_{i}).

On the other hand, let
a_{i}x^{\deg(f_{i})}
be the leading term of

f_{i}(x)
. Since

b_{i}x^{m_{i}}=\frac{a_{i}x^{\deg(f_{i})}}{a_{i+1}x^{\deg(f_{i+1})}},

the sign change between
f_{i}(x)
and
f_{i+1}(x)
as

x\to\pm\infty
is determined by the sign of
b_{i}x^{m_{i}}
.
Therefore,

\mathcal{V}_{t}(+\infty)=\#\{0\leq i\leq t:\ b_{i}<0\},

and

\mathcal{V}_{t}(-\infty)=\#\{0\leq i\leq t:\ b_{i}<0,\ m_{i}\ \text{even}\}+\#\{0\leq i\leq t:\ b_{i}>0,\ m_{i}\ \text{odd}\}.

Hence

\mathcal{V}_{t}(-\infty)-\mathcal{V}_{t}(+\infty)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i\leq t\\
m_{i}\ \mathrm{odd}\end{subarray}}\operatorname{sign}(b_{i}).

This completes the proof.
∎

For the real roots counting problem, the Sturm’s Theorem (see, for instance,
[
13
, Page 419]
or
[
22
]
) states that the number of real roots of a polynomial can be characterized by the pattern of sign changes in its Sturm sequence.

Theorem 3.10
.

[
22
]

Let
f(x)
be a univariate polynomial with the real coefficients. Let
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
with
a<b
,
f(a),f(b)\neq 0
.
If
f(x)
has distinct roots (i.e.,
\gcd(f(x),f^{\prime}(x))=1
), then the number of real roots of
f(x)
in
(a,b]
is
\mathcal{V}(a)-\mathcal{V}(b)
on the Sturm sequence of
f(x)
.

On the other hand, as early as 1857, the quadratic forms associated with Hankel matrices
provided a means for solving real root counting problems and real root localization problems; see
[
18
]
and
[
17
]
.

Consider a polynomial
f(x)=a_{n}x^{n}+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{1}x+a_{0}
,
where the coefficients
a_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
a_{n}=1
. By the fundamental theorem of algebra,
f(x)
has exactly
n
roots, denoted by
\{s_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, in the complex field
\mathbb{C}
.
Let
p_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}^{k}
represent the power sums of the roots and
P_{f}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{2n-2}p_{k}x^{k}
.

The relationship between
f(x)
and
P_{f}(x)
has been established as follows:

Proposition 3.11
.

Let

f(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x-s_{i}),\qquad p_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}^{k},

and define

P_{f}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{2n-2}p_{k}x^{k}.

Let
f_{0}(x)=f(x)
and
f_{1}(x)=f^{\prime}(x)
. Then

\frac{{f}^{\looparrowleft}_{1}(x)}{{f}^{\looparrowleft}_{0}(x)}\equiv P_{f}(x)\pmod{x^{2n-1}}.

Proof.

Since

{f}^{\looparrowleft}_{0}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-s_{i}x),

we get

\displaystyle\ln\frac{1}{{f}^{\looparrowleft}_{0}(x)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln\frac{1}{1-s_{i}x}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{1}{k}(s_{i}x)^{k}=\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{1}{k}p_{k}x^{k}.

By taking the
x\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}
on both sides of the equation, we obtain

-\frac{x({f}^{\looparrowleft}_{0}(x))^{\prime}}{{f}^{\looparrowleft}_{0}(x)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{s_{i}x}{1-s_{i}x}=\sum_{k\geq 1}p_{k}x^{k}.

On the other hand, since
f_{1}(x)=f_{0}^{\prime}(x)
, we have

x({f}^{\looparrowleft}_{0}(x))^{\prime}=n{f}^{\looparrowleft}_{0}(x)-{f}^{\looparrowleft}_{1}(x).

Hence

\frac{{f}^{\looparrowleft}_{1}(x)}{{f}^{\looparrowleft}_{0}(x)}=n-\frac{x({f}^{\looparrowleft}_{0}(x))^{\prime}}{{f}^{\looparrowleft}_{0}(x)}=n+\sum_{k\geq 1}p_{k}x^{k}.

Since
p_{0}=n
, it follows that

\frac{{f}^{\looparrowleft}_{1}(x)}{{f}^{\looparrowleft}_{0}(x)}=\sum_{k\geq 0}p_{k}x^{k}.

Taking the truncation modulo
x^{2n-1}
, we obtain

\frac{{f}^{\looparrowleft}_{1}(x)}{{f}^{\looparrowleft}_{0}(x)}\equiv\sum_{k=0}^{2n-2}p_{k}x^{k}=P_{f}(x)\pmod{x^{2n-1}}.

Then the proposition follows.
∎

, where
Borchardt deduced the following theorem postulated that all sequential principal minors of
\mathcal{H}_{n}(P_{f})
are nonsingular.
Here, we give a short proof of this theorem without the necessity of Borchardt’s assumption.

Theorem 3.12
.

[
18
]

If
f(x)
has distinct roots (i.e.,
\gcd(f(x),f^{\prime}(x))=1
),
The signature of
\mathcal{H}_{n}(P_{f}(x))
is equivalent to the number of real roots of

.

Proof.

By Corollary
3.4
, note that
\gcd(f_{0}(x),f_{1}(x))=1
and
\max(\deg(f),\deg(f^{\prime})+1)=n
, we have
\det\mathcal{H}_{n}(P_{f})\neq 0
.
Then the theorem follows by Theorem
3.9
and Sturm’s theorem
3.10
.
