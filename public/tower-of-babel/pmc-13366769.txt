Diagnostic performance of saline-aided ultrasound in the detection of colorectal polyps in children
Authors: Tong Wang, Haiying Zhou, Tingting Ding, Jia Li, Liezhen Hu, Wei Yu, Meixi Chen, Zhou Lin, Jinlong Deng, Luyao Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of saline-aided gastroenteric ultrasound (US) for detecting colorectal polyps in pediatric patients.MethodsIn this single-center, retrospective study (April 2018 to November 2023), we analyzed 466 patients with suspected colorectal polyps. All patients underwent conventional US followed by diagnostic colonoscopy. A consecutive paired cohort (n = 292), enrolled after the introduction of a new protocol, additionally received saline-aided US. Diagnostic metrics (accuracy, sensitivity, specificity) and Cohen’s kappa were calculated to compare conventional and saline-aided US against histopathological reference standards. Multivariate logistic regression was used to assess clinical parameters affecting diagnostic outcomes.ResultsAmong 466 recruited patients (mean age 4.83 ± 3.12 years, 276 males), 391 were colonoscopy-positive. The most common pathological type was juvenile intestinal polyp (93.1%); the most frequent location was the rectum (59.3%). Saline-aided US was associated with improved diagnostic performance (accuracy: 81.8%, sensitivity: 81.0%, specificity: 85.0%) compared to conventional US (accuracy: 51.4%, sensitivity: 39.6%). Multivariate analysis identified polyp size and location as factors significantly associated with diagnostic outcome (both p < 0.001). For saline-aided US, smaller polyps (< 10 mm) and those located in the rectum (missed diagnosis rate: 22.8%) were associated with higher rates of missed diagnosis.ConclusionSaline-aided US significantly enhances the detection of colorectal polyps in children, demonstrating superior diagnostic accuracy and sensitivity over conventional US.Keywords: Colorectal polyps, Children, Ultrasound, Saline-aided

Introduction

Colorectal polyps are one of the most common causes of hematochezia. Affected children may present with abdominal pain, secondary intussusception, polyp prolapse, and complications including iron deficiency anemia and protein-losing enteropathy [1]. Early detection and intervention are crucial for symptom alleviation and prognosis improvement. Colonoscopy remains the first-line tool for both diagnosis and minimally invasive removal of colorectal polyps [2]. However, not all children with hematochezia have colorectal polyps. Furthermore, the widespread application of colonoscopy in pediatric hematochezia cases is limited by cost considerations and the necessity for general anesthesia. Consequently, there exists a clinical need for more accessible and cost-effective diagnostic tools for children with suspected colorectal polyps.

Current alternative imaging modalities—including conventional ultrasonography (US), barium enema, and computed tomography colonography (CTC)—exhibit significant limitations for this purpose. While conventional US is portable and radiation-free, its sensitivity is suboptimal, particularly for smaller lesions, largely due to interference from intraluminal gas and contents [3–6]. Both barium enema and CTC involve radiation exposure, with CTC also demonstrating poor specificity for excluding polyps in pediatric populations [1, 5, 7, 8]. Thus, a more reliable and child-friendly imaging technique is needed.

Saline-aided gastroenteric US addresses a key limitation of conventional transabdominal US by utilizing instilled saline as an acoustic coupling medium. This process separates intraluminal gas and gently distends the bowel wall, thereby creating a superior acoustic window and enhancing the contrast between the intestinal lumen, wall, and any protruding lesions [9–11]. This technique shows promise in achieving diagnostic accuracy comparable to colonoscopy for intestinal lesions, potentially serving as a valuable complementary imaging tool. Nevertheless, existing literature has insufficiently evaluated saline-aided US specifically for pediatric colorectal polyp detection, with limited comparative analyses against conventional US. This retrospective study aims to comprehensively assess the diagnostic performance of saline-aided US in children with suspected colorectal polyps.

Materials and methods

Subjects/patients

The study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision) and received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Shenzhen Children’s Hospital. As a retrospective analysis, the need for individual patient consent was waived.

We conducted a retrospective analysis of 466 pediatric patients (aged 0–18 years) who presented at Shenzhen Children’s Hospital between April 2018 and November 2023 with symptoms suspicious for colorectal polyps (including hematochezia, altered bowel habits, mucus stool, or abdominal pain) and who subsequently underwent both conventional US and diagnostic colonoscopy. The saline-aided gastroenteric US examination was progressively adopted as a new imaging protocol in our department during the study period. Specifically, this technique was introduced and became available for clinical use starting in January 2021. Therefore, the 292 patients who comprised the paired cohort for comparative analysis represent a consecutive series of individuals enrolled from that point onward until the end of the study period (November 2023). The decision to perform saline-aided US was thus based solely on the timing of protocol implementation and its availability, not on physician preference, specific patient characteristics, or clinical severity.

Inclusion Criteria: (1) Age 0–18 years; (2) Presence of symptoms suspicious for colorectal polyps (hematochezia, altered bowel habits, mucus stool, or abdominal pain) warranting colonoscopy; (3) Complete clinical documentation, including definitive colonoscopy imaging and histopathological confirmation of polyps; (4) Pre-colonoscopy imaging with conventional US and/or saline-aided US.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Significant cardiopulmonary compromise or other major systemic illnesses; (2) Acute phase of inflammatory bowel disease; (3) Coagulation disorders; (4) Ambiguous histopathological diagnosis; (5) Suboptimal ultrasound image quality; (6) Poor patient compliance.

US examination

All ultrasonographic examinations (both conventional and saline-aided) were performed by two board-certified radiologists, each with over ten years of experience in pediatric imaging. Examinations were conducted using a GE Logic E9-3 ultrasound system equipped with 9L and C1-5 transducers. No bowel preparation was required prior to the examination.

Patients were positioned supine, and abdominal scanning was initiated with the transducer placed on the abdominal wall. A systematic counterclockwise examination was performed, encompassing the rectum, sigmoid colon, descending colon, transverse colon, and ascending colon, utilizing appropriate compression techniques. Supplemental perianal scanning was subsequently performed. When suspicious lesions were identified, their characteristics were meticulously documented, including: anatomical location, maximum diameter and number, morphological features, echogenicity (hypoechoic or isoechoic relative to mucosa), presence of a pedicle and vascularity patterns on color Doppler imaging.

Following conventional US, patients undergoing saline-aided US examination received rectal catheterization. Subsequently, warm normal saline (0.9% NaCl) was slowly infused under real-time ultrasound monitoring using a gradual instillation–assessment technique. The infusion was continued until adequate distension and clear visualization of the bowel wall from the rectum to the ileocecal region were achieved, with the total volume not exceeding 100 ml per kilogram of body weight. The intestinal segments were then re-examined during saline distension. During the procedure, the operating physician verbally assessed and observed the child for any discomfort (e.g., abdominal pain, urgent defecation) to avoid over-distension. No saline instillation-related adverse events (e.g., mucosal injury, perforation, or severe pain) were reported or observed in this study. All ultrasonographic images and cine loops were digitally archived for subsequent analysis. All US examinations were completed within 24 h preceding colonoscopy.

Image interpretation criteria

The ultrasonographic diagnosis of colorectal polyps required the presence of the following characteristics: (1) Well-demarcated, mucosal-based lesion protruding into the lumen; (2) Homogeneous echotexture (isoechoic or hypoechoic relative to surrounding mucosa) or heterogenous echotexture mixed with echogenic and anechoic portions; (3) Oval, lobulated, or regular contour; (4) Demonstrable intralesional vascularity on color Doppler imaging.

Two independent radiologists (each with 11 years of specialized experience in gastrointestinal US) reviewed all images while blinded to clinical and histopathological data. Consensus interpretation was achieved through joint evaluation. Following ultrasonographic assessment, all patients underwent diagnostic colonoscopy with histopathological confirmation of all resected lesions. The histopathological findings served as the reference standard for diagnostic accuracy calculations. The analysis of diagnostic performance was conducted on a per-patient basis. For patients presenting with multiple polyps, the criterion for diagnostic success was the detection of one or more polyps. This criterion reflects clinical decision-making: identifying any polyp is the determinative step for initiating therapeutic colonoscopy, with subsequent comprehensive polypectomy and histopathological assessment.

Statistical analysis

All data were managed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation) and analyzed with Python 3.9 (Python Software Foundation). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were presented as either mean ± standard deviation (for normally distributed data) or median (range) (for non-normally distributed data), following appropriate normality testing.

Comparisons of diagnostic performance metrics including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value were performed using McNemar’s test. Additionally, agreement between each US method and the reference standard was assessed as a supplementary metric using Cohen’s kappa coefficient. Kappa agreement was interpreted as follows: 0.00–0.20 (slight agreement), 0.21–0.40 (fair agreement), 0.41–0.60 (moderate agreement), 0.61–0.80 (substantial agreement), 0.81–1.00 (almost perfect agreement).

Subgroup analyses were conducted to compare the detection rates of conventional versus saline-aided US according to polyp location (anatomical segment) and polyp size (maximum diameter). These comparisons were performed strictly within the paired cohort using the McNemar test. Both univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to assess associations between clinical parameters and diagnostic outcomes. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with p-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

The study cohort comprised 466 pediatric patients (mean age 4.8 ± 3.1 years; male: female ratio 1.5:1). The predominant presenting symptom was hematochezia (n = 403, 86.5%), followed by abdominal pain (n = 47, 10.1%) and other gastrointestinal symptoms including altered bowel habits (n = 16, 3.4%). Colonoscopy and histopathological evaluation confirmed colorectal polyps in 391 cases (83.9%), while 75 cases (16.1%) showed no polypoid lesions.

Among polyp-positive cases, juvenile polyps represented the most common histopathological subtype (n = 364, 93.1%). The rectal region was the predominant anatomical location (n = 232, 59.3%), followed by the sigmoid colon (n = 88, 22.5%). Multiple polyps were identified in 9 cases (2.3%), and secondary intussusception was observed in 30 cases (7.7%). Diagnostic performance comparison

Comparative analysis was performed on 292 patients who underwent both conventional US and saline-aided US. The overall accuracy was substantially higher with saline-aided US (81.8% vs. 51.4% for conventional US; p < 0.001). Specifically, the sensitivity for detecting polyps was also markedly improved (81.0% vs. 39.6%; p < 0.001). Meanwhile, the negative predictive value increased to 53.7%, aiding in better identification of true-negative cases (p < 0.001). Of note, the specificity of saline-aided US was lower than that of conventional US (85.0% vs. 96.7%; p = 0.02). Detailed results, with 95% confidence intervals and p-values, are presented in Table 2. Differences in diagnostic performance by polyp location and size

Subgroup analyses based on the paired cohort (n = 292) further delineated the diagnostic advantage of saline-aided US over conventional US according to polyp characteristics.

Performance based on anatomical location

Saline-aided US demonstrated a higher polyp detection rate both overall and at specific local sites, including the rectum and sigmoid colon (all comparisons p < 0.001) (Table 3). This advantage was most pronounced in the rectum. Among the 143 patients with rectal polyps, saline-aided US identified an additional 75 cases missed by conventional US, resulting in an absolute increase in the detection rate of 52.4% (χ² = 73.01, p < 0.001). A significant improvement was also observed in the sigmoid colon (n = 45), with a 44.4% increase in detection rate (χ² = 18.05, p < 0.001). For other segments (e.g., descending, transverse, and ascending colon), meaningful McNemar’s test analysis was not performed due to limited sample sizes.

Performance based on polyp size

The diagnostic advantage of saline-aided US varied with polyp diameter. For polyps ≤ 25 mm in diameter, saline-aided US yielded a significantly higher detection rate than conventional US (all comparisons p < 0.05). The greatest advantage was observed for polyps measuring 6–10 mm, where the detection rate increased by 52.3% (χ² = 21.04, p < 0.001). In contrast, for larger polyps (> 25 mm in diameter, specifically the 26–30 mm and > 30 mm subgroups), no statistically significant difference in detection rate was found between the two methods (p = 0.24 and p = 1.00, respectively) (Table 4).

Factors affecting polyp diagnosis accuracy

In the analysis of diagnostic outcomes for saline-aided US, we examined the effects of gender, age, height, weight, polyp size, location, complications (presence of secondary intussusception), and symptoms (hematochezia or abdominal pain) on diagnostic outcomes using colonoscopy as the reference standard. Univariate analysis revealed significant correlations between diagnostic accuracy and age (p = 0.04), weight (p = 0.01), polyp size (p = 0.02), location (p = 0.02), complications (p = 0.01), hematochezia (p = 0.01), and abdominal pain (p = 0.02). However, multivariate analysis identified polyp size and location as factors significantly associated with diagnostic outcome (both p < 0.001).

With saline-aided US, the false-negative rate decreased with increasing polyp size, from 48.6% for polyps < 10 mm to 4.2% for those ≥ 24 mm. Regarding location, the false-negative rate was highest for rectal polyps (22.8%), followed by those in the descending colon (9.7%) and sigmoid colon (7.5%).

Discussion

Our results demonstrate that saline-aided US achieves superior diagnostic performance for pediatric colorectal polyps compared to conventional US, with a marked improvement in overall accuracy (81.8% vs. 51.4%) and sensitivity (81.0% vs. 39.6%). This substantial enhancement in detection capability establishes saline-aided US as a more reliable imaging modality in the clinical evaluation of suspected polyps.

Conventional US has been relatively mature in the diagnosis of diseases related to the lower digestive system, with the advantages of portable and affordable, and it does not involve ionizing radiation. But it is also easily interfered by the intestinal gas and contents. Moreover, children’s abdominal cavity is small, and the segmental localization of unfilled intestinal tube is prone to deviation. Saline-aided US addresses these limitations by creating an optimal acoustic interface, reducing false-negative rates from 60.4% to 19.0% (p < 0.001). This substantial gain in sensitivity translates into two key clinical advances: it performs significantly better than conventional US, particularly for small to medium-sized lesions (≤ 25 mm), and it provides superior pre-endoscopic lesion localization, thereby aiding in procedural planning, prioritization. Of note, this improvement in sensitivity was accompanied by a modest reduction in specificity—an expected trade-off when a more sensitive technique detects subtle or equivocal findings.

These capabilities suggest several practical clinical scenarios for saline-aided US. Given its noninvasive nature, procedural convenience, and low cost, saline-aided US may be particularly recommended as a useful preliminary imaging assessment for pediatric patients with suspected polyps, complementing the diagnostic pathway prior to colonoscopy. For patients with intermediate to high clinical suspicion, colonoscopy remains the preferred diagnostic and therapeutic standard; however, saline-aided US can serve as a valuable adjunct for pre-procedural lesion localization and planning when colonoscopy is not immediately available. Its reliance on readily available, low-cost consumables and its straightforward protocol also make it feasible in resource-limited settings. In contexts where colonoscopy access is delayed or unavailable, it can provide timely preliminary assessment. Furthermore, it holds potential as an adjunctive, radiation-free monitoring modality for select high-risk groups, such as children with polyposis syndromes. It should be noted that the technique remains limited in detecting polyps < 10 mm.

Existing literature on ultrasonographic polyp detection demonstrates variable performance metrics. Yabunaka et al. [12] reported limited sensitivity (28.6%) for detecting polyps > 10 mm in adults using conventional transabdominal US, despite high specificity (99.4%). Pediatric studies by Takahiro et al. [6] achieved modest improvement (47% sensitivity, 89% accuracy) without bowel preparation, while Ni-na et al. [13] documented significantly enhanced detection rates (p < 0.0001) with glycerin enema preparation. These findings align with Parra et al.'s [14] identification of key limiting factors: operator dependency, intestinal gas interference, and deep pelvic polyp location.

This study showed that the accuracy and sensitivity of saline-aided gastroenteric US in the diagnosis of colorectal polyps were significantly improved compared with previous examinations, especially with sensitivity reachinged to 81.0%. The method shows particular efficacy for polyps ≤ 25 mm in diameter located in the rectum or sigmoid colon. These results corroborate Limberg et al.'s [15] findings on hydro colonic US while extending the evidence base to pediatric populations. Notably, for children < 3 years, compression US may offer advantages over bowel preparation [16], suggesting age-specific protocol optimization may be warranted.

Our evaluation of diagnostic accuracy identified polyp size and location as the only independent predictors of diagnostic success, while demographic factors and pathological type showed no significant association. We observed an inverse relationship between polyp size and detection rates, with missed diagnosis rates decreasing from 48.6% for polyps < 10 mm to 4.2% for those ≥ 24 mm in diameter. Anatomically, rectal polyps demonstrated the highest miss rate (22.8%) compared to other colorectal segments. Image analysis revealed two primary technical challenges: first, suboptimal visualization of polyps located in the anorectal mucosal folds due to inadequate acoustic windows; second, gas interference during lithotomy position, particularly affecting posterior rectal wall visualization. Despite the superior sensitivity of saline-aided US (81.0% vs. conventional US 39.6%), detection of sub-centimeter polyps (< 10 mm) remains challenging. To optimize diagnostic accuracy, we recommend: using dual probes (high- and low-frequency transducers), combining transabdominal and transperineal approaches, dynamically repositioning the patient during examination, and employing a systematic multiplanar scanning technique. These protocol modifications may significantly reduce false-negative rates, particularly for smaller lesions and those in anatomically challenging locations.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective, single-center design may affect the generalizability of the findings. Second, the comparative analysis is based on a consecutive cohort enrolled after the clinical introduction of saline-aided US, rather than through randomization. This observational design reflects real-world implementation, but it cannot entirely exclude the potential influence of temporal trends or a learning curve on diagnostic performance. Third, the lack of a formal analysis of interobserver variability between radiologists limits the assessment of the technique’s reproducibility. Fourth, the study was conducted without standardized bowel preparation, mirroring common practice but potentially introducing variability in image quality. Finally, given that our cohort comprised children already selected for colonoscopy with a high prevalence of polyps, the diagnostic performance—particularly the negative predictive value—may not be directly extrapolated to a general pediatric population with hematochezia, underscoring the need for external validation in broader populations.

In conclusion, saline-aided US demonstrates superior diagnostic performance compared to conventional US for evaluating colorectal polyps in children. This technique represents a valuable imaging advance that serves as a complementary tool to colonoscopy, improving preoperative assessment, lesion localization, and procedural planning.
