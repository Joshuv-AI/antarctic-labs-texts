“Ears of the lynx” sign on MRI: A radiologic clue to hereditary spastic paraplegia
Authors: Ibtissam El Bqaq, Zineb Izi, Najwa Ech-Cherif Kettani, Meryem Fikri, Firdaous Touarsa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHereditary spastic paraplegia (HSP), also known as Strümpell–Lorrain disease, comprises a heterogeneous group of inherited neurodegenerative disorders characterized by progressive lower-limb spasticity resulting from corticospinal tract degeneration. Clinically, HSP is divided into a pure form, limited to pyramidal tract signs, and a complicated form associated with additional neurological manifestations. We report a familial case involving 2 male siblings born to consanguineous parents who presented with complicated HSP. Brain magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated thinning of the corpus callosum and a characteristic T2/FLAIR cone-shaped hyperintensity at the anterior horns of the lateral ventricles, known as the “ears of the lynx” sign. This radiologic feature is strongly associated with SPG11 and SPG15, 2 common genetic subtypes of complicated HSP. In regions where genetic testing is limited by availability or cost, recognition of this imaging sign may provide an important diagnostic clue and facilitate early identification of hereditary spastic paraplegia.Keywords: Hereditary spastic paraplegia, Magnetic resonance imaging, Ears of the lynx, Radiodiagnostic clue, Corpus callosum, Genetic test

Introduction

Hereditary spastic paraplegia (HSP) constitutes a group of neurodegenerative disorders characterized by progressive spasticity and weakness of the lower limbs. Two main clinical forms have been described [1]: a “pure form,” presenting with isolated spastic paraplegia, and a “complicated form,” in which spastic paraplegia is associated with additional neurological or systemic manifestations such as cognitive impairment or behavioral changes.

HSP type 11 (SPG11) represents the most common form of autosomal recessive HSP and is associated with distinctive clinical and neuroimaging features. It is caused by mutations in the gene encoding spatacsin, a protein involved in vesicle trafficking. Clinically, the disease typically manifests during the first or second decade of life with cognitive decline followed by progressive gait disturbances [2].

HSP can also be classified according to the mode of inheritance into autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, X-linked, or mitochondrial forms [3].

We report the case of 2 siblings born to consanguineous parents who were diagnosed with hereditary spastic paraplegia type 11 through whole-exome genetic testing. Brain magnetic resonance imaging performed at the initial evaluation demonstrated the characteristic “ears of the lynx” sign, a distinctive radiological feature associated with this condition. While the ears of the lynx sign itself has been well described since its initial report, the educational value of the present case lies in its familial, genetically confirmed nature: to our knowledge, few reports have illustrated this sign concurrently in 2 affected siblings from the same consanguineous family, allowing direct comparison of the imaging phenotype within a shared genetic background. This familial presentation, combined with the diagnostic setting of a resource-limited environment where genetic testing is not always readily accessible, reinforces the practical value of recognizing this imaging clue at first-line MRI, potentially expediting referral for confirmatory genetic testing and genetic counseling for the wider family.

Case report

We report the case of 2 siblings born to healthy first-degree consanguineous parents, both presenting with progressive spasticity of the lower limbs.

The younger sibling, 7 years old at the time of consultation, exhibited learning difficulties and recurrent falls. His older brother, 10 years old, also presented with impaired gait, coordination difficulties, and learning challenges.

On neurological examination, both siblings demonstrated moderately increased muscle tone in the lower limbs, consistent with spastic paraparesis, together with moderately reduced muscle strength. Neither child was able to ambulate independently; gait was achieved only with assistance in both siblings. From a functional standpoint, both required assistance with certain activities of daily living, reflecting the complicated phenotype of their disease.

Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed using a 1.5-Tesla scanner, including T1-weighted and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences.

The final diagnosis of hereditary spastic paraplegia type 11 (SPG11) was established through whole-exome genetic testing, which confirmed pathogenic variants in the SPG11 gene in both siblings, consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance; further molecular characterization of the specific variant was not available at the time of this report. However, prior to genetic confirmation, neuroimaging had already revealed characteristic abnormalities suggestive of this condition.

Sagittal T1-weighted images demonstrated marked thinning of the corpus callosum, particularly involving the genu and the anterior portion of the callosal body (Fig. 1).

Sagittal T1-weighted and FLAIR MRI images demonstrating marked thinning of the corpus callosum, predominantly involving the genu and the anterior portion of the callosal body (green arrow), a characteristic finding in hereditary spastic paraplegia.

In addition, axial FLAIR sequences revealed symmetrical hyperintense signal abnormalities in the region of the forceps minor, located at the anterior horns of the lateral ventricles. These areas appeared hyperintense on FLAIR and relatively hypointense on T1-weighted images, reflecting underlying white-matter degeneration within the callosal projection fibers (Fig. 2).

Axial FLAIR MRI image showing bilateral cone-shaped hyperintense signal abnormalities in the region of the forceps minor (orange arrows) at the anterior horns of the lateral ventricles. This radiologic pattern corresponds to the “ears of the lynx” sign, highly suggestive of SPG11 and SPG15 hereditary spastic paraplegia.

This characteristic radiologic appearance corresponds to the “ears of the lynx” sign, a term used to describe the cone-shaped hyperintensities at the frontal horns that resemble the pointed hair tufts on a lynx’s ears.

The presence of this sign has been reported as a highly suggestive neuroradiological marker of certain subtypes of hereditary spastic paraplegia, particularly SPG11 and SPG15, and may provide an important diagnostic clue in patients presenting with progressive spastic paraplegia.

Discussion

The first gene responsible for hereditary spastic paraplegia (HSP) was identified in 1994 and was designated spastic paraplegia gene 11 (SPG11). Subsequently, several additional SPG genes were discovered, reflecting the marked genetic heterogeneity of this disorder [4,5].

The prevalence of the different genetic subtypes remains uncertain, though autosomal recessive forms are reported more commonly in populations with higher rates of consanguinity, such as in the Middle East, consistent with the family described here [3]. Age at onset varies widely, from infancy to the seventh decade of life, and the disease typically follows a chronic, slowly progressive course regardless of subtype [6]; childhood-onset presentations, as in our patients, are well recognized in the literature [7].

In most patients, symptoms initially manifest as gait impairment and gradually progress to spasticity and weakness of the lower limbs [8], as observed in our patients.

The “ears of the lynx” sign refers to a characteristic cone-shaped hyperintensity located at the tips of the frontal horns of the lateral ventricles on fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). The appearance resembles the tufts of hair present on the ears of a lynx, which explains the origin of this descriptive term [9,10].

A recent study demonstrated that this sign has high sensitivity and specificity, with moderate to substantial inter-rater reliability on both FLAIR and T1-weighted sequences, for suggesting recessive forms of HSP, with sensitivity across raters ranging from 78.8% to 97.0% and specificity from 90.9% to 100% on FLAIR imaging, and diagnostic accuracy in the very good-to-excellent range (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve 87.1-93.9); discrimination was consistently better on FLAIR than on T1-weighted sequences, supporting FLAIR as the primary sequence for detecting this sign. The authors proposed that, in patients presenting with sporadic HSP or atypical clinical features, this radiological finding may help raise suspicion for the relevant genetic subtypes [10].

Accordingly, the presence of the ears of the lynx sign may support the decision to perform genetic testing in patients who appear to have sporadic disease or whose clinical presentation is unusual for hereditary paraplegia [10].

Pascual et al. [10] reported that this imaging sign, particularly on axial FLAIR images, shows a strong association with SPG11 and SPG15 mutations. Moreover, when whole-exome sequencing identifies genetic variants of uncertain significance, the presence of the ears of the lynx sign on MRI may provide additional evidence supporting the pathogenic nature of these variants [10].

A sporadic case described by Singh et al. [11] involved a 23-year-old woman presenting with spastic paraparesis, thinning of the corpus callosum, and the ears of the lynx sign. Genetic analysis subsequently classified the case as subtype SPG15, further contributing to the recognition of this imaging feature in HSP-related disorders [11].

The differential diagnosis of the ears of the lynx sign includes several other conditions that can produce a similar hyperintense abnormality in the region of the forceps minor, and distinguishing MRI features can help refine the diagnostic possibilities. Pacheco et al. [12] described a comparable cone-shaped hyperintensity in a patient with Marchiafava-Bignami disease; diffusion tensor imaging and fiber-tracking in that case demonstrated that the finding reflected structural derangement of the same forceps minor [12] and anterior corona radiata fibers affected in SPG11/SPG15, despite the differing underlying pathology, although genetic testing for HSP was not performed, leaving the possibility of a coexisting hereditary paraplegia unresolved [13]. Katyal et al. [14] subsequently reported the first observation of this radiological sign in a patient with primary lateral sclerosis, in whom a full hereditary spastic paraplegia gene panel was negative, confirming that the sign, while highly suggestive of SPG11/SPG15, is not pathognomonic and can occur in other disorders affecting the corticospinal tract and callosal white matter [14]. A further and more common mimic is the gliotic “pseudolesion” occasionally seen at the calloso-caudal angle of the frontal horns in otherwise healthy individuals; unlike the true ears of the lynx sign, this normal variant typically shows a rounded, capping morphology rather than the sharply pointed, flame-shaped configuration characteristic of SPG11/SPG15-related callosal involvement, a distinction that can help avoid false-positive interpretation [15]. Taken together, these observations indicate that the ears of the lynx sign, although highly sensitive and specific for SPG11 and SPG15 hereditary spastic paraplegia, should be interpreted alongside its precise morphology and the clinical context—including family history, age at onset, and confirmatory genetic testing—in order to distinguish true SPG11/SPG15-related callosal involvement from its principal radiological mimics.

In HSP, spinal cord thinning is considered a common MRI finding, although it lacks specificity. Additional brain MRI abnormalities reported more frequently in complicated forms of HSP include diffuse white-matter involvement and volume loss affecting both cortical structures and deep gray matter nuclei [16,17].

Beyond its diagnostic role in symptomatic patients, the ears of the lynx sign has also been reported on MRI in clinically unaffected heterozygous carriers of a single SPG11 mutation, alongside mild corpus callosum thinning, raising the possibility that this imaging finding may in some cases precede or occur independently of overt symptomatic disease; this observation reinforces the value of careful familial imaging correlation, as illustrated by the concordant findings in our 2 affected siblings [18].

Conclusion

The “ears of the lynx” sign on MRI is a distinctive neuroradiological finding commonly associated with hereditary spastic paraplegia subtypes SPG11 and SPG15.

In regions where access to advanced genetic testing is limited or financially prohibitive, awareness of such hallmark imaging signs becomes critically important. Recognizing these features can significantly help in the early diagnosis of rare hereditary disorders, leading to better prognostic outcomes and early identification of at-risk individuals within affected families.
