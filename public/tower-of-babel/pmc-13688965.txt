Sleep disorders as an emerging risk factor for liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma: a systematic review and meta-analysis of cohort studies
Authors: Tzu-Rong Peng, Ta-Wei Wu, Hung-Hong Lin, Chao-Chuan Wu, Shuang-Yu Hsu, Ching-Sheng Hsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSleep disorders are increasingly recognized as contributors to metabolic dysfunction and chronic liver disease, but their associations with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and cirrhosis remain unclear. We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the relationship between sleep disorders and liver-related outcomes.MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines (PROSPERO: CRD420261342781), we searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library through February 2026 for cohort studies assessing sleep disorders and liver outcomes. Adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) or odds ratios (ORs) were pooled using random-effects models. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were performed, and risk of bias was assessed using ROBINS-I.ResultsThirteen cohort studies were included. Overall, sleep disorders were associated with an increased risk of liver-related outcomes (HR 1.49; 95% CI 1.22–1.81; I² = 96%). Insomnia was significantly associated with increased risk (HR 1.39; 95% CI 1.28–1.49), whereas obstructive sleep apnea showed a non-significant trend (HR 1.80; 95% CI 0.91–3.58). Sleep disorders were linked to higher risks of HCC (HR 1.42; 95% CI 1.15–1.77) and unspecified liver cancer (HR 1.26; 95% CI 1.01–1.57), but not cirrhosis. Findings were robust in sensitivity analyses, with no significant publication bias.ConclusionsSleep disorders, particularly insomnia, are associated with increased risks of HCC and liver-related outcomes. These findings highlight sleep disturbances as potentially modifiable risk factors in liver disease progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-026-05145-1.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Sleep disorders, Obstructive sleep apnea, Meta-analysis

Background

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with a steadily rising incidence in many regions [1, 2]. Liver cirrhosis, the principal premalignant condition for HCC, accounts for the majority of cases and represents a substantial global health burden. Although chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) and hepatitis C virus (HCV) infections, along with alcohol-related liver disease (ALD), remain well-established etiologies, the increasing prevalence of metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD, formerly NAFLD) has shifted attention toward metabolic and lifestyle-related drivers of hepatocarcinogenesis. Notably, a proportion of HCC cases arise in individuals without these traditional risk factors, underscoring the need to identify additional, potentially modifiable exposures. In this context, sleep disorders—including insomnia and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA)—have emerged as clinically relevant conditions associated with MASLD, advanced fibrosis, and adverse liver outcomes, and may contribute to HCC development.

Sleep disorders comprise a heterogeneous group of conditions, including OSA, insomnia, abnormal sleep duration, and circadian rhythm disruption. Beyond their established neurological and cardiovascular sequelae, accumulating evidence suggests that disordered sleep may influence liver disease progression through several interrelated biological pathways. Intermittent hypoxia, a hallmark of OSA, promotes oxidative stress, systemic inflammation, and activation of hypoxia-inducible factor signaling, which in turn may accelerate hepatic steatosis, fibrogenesis, and angiogenesis—key processes in hepatocarcinogenesis. Concurrently, sleep deprivation and circadian misalignment can disrupt neuroendocrine and immune homeostasis, contributing to insulin resistance, metabolic dysfunction, and impaired immune surveillance. These alterations may create a permissive environment for tumor initiation and progression. In addition, circadian dysregulation and altered melatonin signaling have been implicated in both liver injury and carcinogenesis, further supporting a mechanistic link between sleep disturbances and HCC.

Epidemiological data increasingly support these biological observations, although results remain somewhat heterogeneous. Using the Taiwan National Health Insurance Research Database, Chou et al. [3] reported that patients with OSA had a significantly higher risk of developing cirrhosis and fatty liver disease compared with matched controls. Similarly, a large cohort study of U.S. veterans identified OSA as an independent risk factor for incident malignancies overall, although the association with liver cancer specifically was less consistent across analyses [4]. Regarding non-apnea sleep disorders, population-based studies by Liang et al. [5] and Lin et al. [6] demonstrated that individuals with sleep disorders, particularly primary insomnia, had an increased subsequent risk of HCC.

Sleep duration has also been examined as a relevant exposure, with several large cohort studies suggesting a U-shaped association. Both short and long sleep durations, as well as excessive daytime napping, have been linked to increased risks of HCC incidence and chronic liver disease–related mortality [7, 8]. These findings support the notion that both insufficient and excessive sleep may reflect or contribute to underlying pathophysiological disturbances relevant to liver disease progression. However, not all studies are concordant. For example, Ma et al. [4] reported no significant association between sleep duration and HCC progression among patients with established cirrhosis, highlighting the complexity of these relationships and the potential influence of disease stage, comorbidities, and methodological differences.

Despite growing interest in this field, several important gaps remain. Existing studies are characterized by substantial heterogeneity in design, population characteristics, exposure definitions, and outcome ascertainment. Moreover, many investigations focus on a single sleep phenotype, limiting the ability to compare effects across different types of sleep disturbances. The interplay between sleep disorders, progression of cirrhosis, and HCC development—potentially mediated through shared mechanisms such as chronic inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and circadian disruption—remains incompletely understood. A comprehensive synthesis of current evidence is therefore needed to clarify these associations and to assess their clinical relevance.

Accordingly, this study aims to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the associations between various sleep disorders and the risks of liver cirrhosis, and HCC. By integrating available cohort evidence, we seek to determine whether sleep disorders constitute independent and potentially modifiable risk factors for adverse liver outcomes. Improved understanding of these relationships may inform risk stratification and identify novel targets for prevention in patients at risk of chronic liver disease and hepatocarcinogenesis.

Materials and methods

Data sources and search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis are conducted in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines [9]. A comprehensive search was performed in major electronic databases, including PubMed, Embase, Web of Science and the Cochrane Library, covering the period from database inception to February 24, 2026. The search strategy utilized a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text keywords, such as: “sleep disorders,” “obstructive sleep apnea,” “insomnia,” “sleep duration,” “circadian rhythm,” “liver cirrhosis,” and “hepatocellular carcinoma.” In addition, the reference lists of all retrieved articles and relevant reviews were manually screened to identify any additional eligible studies. This study was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews database (registration number: CRD420261342781).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

To ensure the quality and relevance of the evidence, we applied the following criteria for study selection: Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) Study design: prospective or retrospective cohort studies that evaluated the longitudinal association between sleep-related factors and liver outcomes. (2) Population: adult participants who were either initially free of chronic liver disease (to assess primary incidence) or diagnosed with pre-existing liver conditions, such as liver cirrhosis or MASLD, at the study baseline (to evaluate the risk of disease progression to HCC). (3) Exposure: documented sleep disorders, including but not limited to obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) diagnosed by ICD codes or clinical assessment, insomnia symptoms, or abnormal sleep duration (too short or too long). (4) Outcomes: studies reporting quantitative risk estimates, such as Hazard Ratios (HR) or Odds Ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for incident liver cirrhosis or HCC.

Given the distinct pathophysiology and risk profiles of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and other primary liver cancers (e.g., intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma), outcomes were carefully classified. When available, HCC-specific outcomes were prioritized. Studies reporting broader “liver cancer” outcomes based on unspecified diagnostic codes were analyzed separately to preserve outcome specificity.

Studies were excluded based on the following criteria: (1) Non-cohort designs: cross-sectional studies, case reports, and case-control studies with very small sample sizes or those focusing exclusively on physiological sleep metrics rather than disease incidence. (2) Duplicate data: In cases where multiple studies used the same data source, such as the UK Biobank or the Taiwan National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD), we prioritized the study with the largest sample size, the longest follow-up period, or the most comprehensive disease classification to avoid overlapping populations and overestimation of effect sizes. (3) Insufficient data: studies that failed to provide sufficient statistical data for extracting effect size or calculating variance.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two independent reviewers systematically extracted data from eligible studies using a standardized collection form. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus or by consultation with a third senior investigator. For each included study, the following information was extracted: (1) Study characteristics: first author, publication year, country of origin, and cohort source (e.g., UK Biobank, Taiwan NHIRD, or the Korean NHI database). (2) Participant characteristics: total sample size, age range (or mean age) and sex distribution. (3) Exposure measurement: specific type of sleep disorder (e.g., OSA, primary insomnia, or abnormal sleep duration) and the assessment method (e.g., ICD-10 codes, validated questionnaires, or clinical diagnosis). (4) Outcome assessment: definitions and methods of assessment for incident liver cirrhosis, HCC, and unspecified liver cancer. To ensure diagnostic precision, oncological outcomes were stratified into confirmed HCC (based on specific ICD-10 codes such as C22.0 or histopathological data) and unspecified liver cancer (defined as primary liver malignancy without specified cell type). If a study reported multiple liver outcomes, data for specific HCC were prioritized for extraction. (5) Statistical data: multivariate adjusted risk estimates, including HR or OR, 95% CIs, and specific confounding variables adjusted in the final models, such as age, sex, BMI, smoking, and diabetes.

Methodological quality and risk of bias for included cohort studies were assessed using the ROBINS-I tool (Risk of Bias In Non-randomized Studies - of Interventions) [10]. This framework evaluates bias across seven domains: (1) bias due to confounding (e.g., inadequate adjustment for metabolic factors or lifestyle), (2) bias in selection of participants, (3) bias in classification of exposures, (4) bias due to departures from intended exposures, (5) bias due to missing data, (6) bias in measurement of outcomes, and (7) bias in selection of the reported result. Each study was classified as having a “Low”, “Moderate”, “Serious”, or “Critical” risk of bias. Particular attention was paid to the “confounding” domain to ensure that the results were not overly influenced by baseline liver health or common metabolic comorbidities.

Statistical analyzes

The primary objective of this meta-analysis was to calculate the pooled HRs and their corresponding 95% CIs to quantify the association between sleep disorders and the risk of liver cirrhosis and HCC. For studies reporting ORs, these were treated as equivalent to HRs given the relatively low incidence of the target outcomes in the general population.

To account for potential variations in study populations, geographic regions, and exposure definitions, a random-effects model (DerSimonian-Laird method) [11] was pre-specified as the primary analytical model to provide a more conservative estimate. The heterogeneity among the included studies was assessed using the Cochrane Q test and the I2 statistic. An I2 value greater than 50% was considered to indicate significant heterogeneity. To explore potential sources of heterogeneity and provide more granular information, subgroup analyzes were performed based on the outcome type (liver cirrhosis versus HCC, category of sleep disorders: OSA, insomnia, and abnormal sleep duration). Subgroup analysis was performed based on baseline liver health status (general population vs. patients with pre-existing liver disease) to explore its impact on the pooled estimates.

Sensitivity analyzes were performed sequentially omitting one study at a time (leave-one-out method) to evaluate the robustness of the pooled results and identify any single study exerting a disproportionate influence. Furthermore, for studies that used the same database (e.g., UK Biobank), only the most comprehensive report was included in the main analysis. Potential publication bias was evaluated using visual inspection of funnel plots and formally tested using Egger’s linear regression test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests. All statistical procedures were performed using RevMan (Cochrane Review Manager, version 5.4, Oxford, UK) and R software (version 4.4.3) with the metafor package.

Results

Selection of studies

A total of 387 records were identified by database search, including PubMed (n = 207), Cochrane (n = 144), Embase (n = 31), and other sources (n = 5). Before screening, 21 duplicate records were removed. The remaining 366 records were screened based on titles and abstracts, resulting in the exclusion of 326 records. Reasons for exclusion included irrelevant titles (n = 249), trial registrations (n = 12), conference abstracts (n = 55) and reviews (n = 10). For the 40 reports sought for retrieval, all 40 were successfully retrieved (n = 40). The eligibility of these 40 reports was then assessed by full text screening. During this stage, 27 reports were excluded because the content was irrelevant (n = 26) or because they were reviews (n = 1). Ultimately, 13 studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in the systematic review (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

A total of 13 cohort studies were included in this systematic review, with publication dates ranging from 2012 to 2025. The geographical distribution of the studies was diverse, including research conducted in the United States (n = 5), Taiwan (n = 4), the United Kingdom (n = 3), and South Korea (n = 1). The included literature comprised prospective cohort designs (n = 9) and retrospective cohort designs (n = 4).

The sample sizes in the studies varied significantly, ranging from a focused group of 161 participants in clinical settings to large-scale population-based cohorts exceeding 1.3 million individuals (e.g., Jara 2020). The primary sleep-related exposures investigated included OSA, insomnia, abnormal sleep duration (short or long sleep), and napping during the day.

Regarding clinical outcomes, the studies evaluated the incidence and risk of HCC, unspecified liver cancer, and liver cirrhosis. All included studies performed multivariate adjustments for key confounders, such as age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, alcohol consumption, and history of diabetes. Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias for the 13 included cohort studies was assessed using the ROBINS-I tool. In general, the studies demonstrated a range of bias risks from moderate to serious, with no study achieving a “low” overall risk rating (Fig. 2). A primary methodological concern was identified in the domain of confounding (D1). Seven studies, specifically Liang 2012 (1), Chou 2015 (3), Fang 2015 (4), VoPham 2018 (5), Lin 2018 (6), Polonis 2019 (7), and Lee 2024 (13) were rated as having a “Serious” risk of bias in this domain. This rating was primarily due to the potential for unmeasured or inadequately controlled confounding variables inherent in observational data. Furthermore, Polonis 2019 (7) was judged to have a “Serious” risk in the domain of bias due to deviations from intended interventions (D4).

Traffic light plot of risk of bias for cohort studies and overall summary of each domain

On the contrary, other domains showed higher consistency in quality. The domains of selection of participants (D2), missing data (D5), and selection of the reported result (D7) were generally rated as having a “Low” or “Moderate” risk in most of the included studies. In summary, 7 studies (54%) were judged to have a “Serious” overall risk of bias, while the remaining 6 studies (46%) were categorized as having a “Moderate” overall risk. These ratings reflect the typical challenges of controlling for multi-factorial confounders in large-scale cohort studies investigating sleep disorders and long-term liver outcomes.

Overall association between sleep disorders and liver diseases

The meta-analysis of 13 cohort studies demonstrated a significant association between sleep disorders and an increased risk of liver diseases, including HCC, unspecified liver cancer, and cirrhosis. As shown in Fig. 3, the size of the pooled effect indicated that individuals with sleep disorders have a significantly higher risk of developing these liver conditions compared to those without sleep disorders (HR = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.22–1.81). Although there was significant heterogeneity among the included studies (I2 = 96%, p < 0.0001), the overall trend in most studies consistently pointed towards a positive correlation, suggesting that sleep disturbances are a strong risk factor for adverse liver outcomes.

Association between insomnia and liver diseases

The subgroup analysis focused on insomnia revealed a significant correlation with an increased risk of liver outcomes, including HCC and cirrhosis. As illustrated in Fig. 4, the pooled effect size for insomnia was 1.39 (95% CI: 1.28–1.49). Although individual studies such as Song 2024 and Long 2024 showed strong positive associations, the overall analysis demonstrated excellent consistency across studies without observed heterogeneity (I² = 0%, P = 0.39). These findings suggest that patients with insomnia symptoms have a 39% higher risk of developing liver-related pathologies compared to those with normal sleep patterns, underscoring insomnia as a critical clinical indicator for the screening of liver disease.

Association between obstructive sleep apnea and liver diseases

A meta-analysis of six studies indicated that OSA was associated with a higher point estimate for liver-related pathologies (HR 1.80), but this trend did not reach statistical significance (95% CI: 0.91–3.58; P = 0.09) (Fig. 5). Furthermore, the results were limited by extreme heterogeneity (I2 = 98%), which required a cautious interpretation.

Subgroup analysis of sleep disorders and liver outcomes

Figure 6 presents a forest plot of the association between various sleep disorders and the risk of HCC, liver cancer, and cirrhosis, showing a statistically significant overall risk increase of 49% (pooled HR 1.49; 95% CI: 1.22–1.81; P < 0.0001. In the subgroup analysis, sleep disorders were significantly associated with an increased risk of HCC (HR 1.42; 95% CI: 1.15–1.77; P = 0.001) and unspecified liver cancer (HR 1.26; 95% CI: 1.01–1.57; P = 0.04). On the contrary, while the cirrhosis subgroup showed a higher point estimate (HR 2.12), the result was not statistically significant since the 95% confidence interval (0.62–7.29) crossed the null line (P = 0.23). Furthermore, when stratified by baseline liver health, sleep disorders remained a significant risk factor for HCC both in the general population (pooled HR 1.53; 95% CI: 1.25–1.87; P < 0.0001) and in high-risk patients with pre-existing cirrhosis (e.g., Ma et al., 2025)(HR 0.92; 95% CI: 0.65–1.30; detailed in Supplementary Fig. 1), suggesting that sleep disturbances promote liver disease progression across different clinical stages.

Forest plot of the association between various sleep disorders and the risk of cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and liver cancer

Substantial heterogeneity was observed across all categories, with an overall I2 of 96% and subgroup I2 values ranging from 78% to 99%. Despite this high variability, the subgroup differences test was not significant (chi2 = 0.78, P = 0.68, I2 = 0%), suggesting that the observed risk elevation was consistent across the different liver-related outcomes.

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the stability of the pooled results, a ‘leave-one-out’ sensitivity analysis was performed by iteratively excluding each study and recalculating the meta-analysis (Fig. 7). The results demonstrate that no single study disproportionately influenced the overall outcome, with the pooled HRs remaining remarkably stable, ranging narrowly between 1.46 (95% CI: 1.20–1.79) and 1.55 (95% CI: 1.26–1.90) across non-outlier iterations while maintaining strict statistical significance (P < 0.001) throughout all 17 exclusions. In particular, the exclusion of Chou 2015 [3]—which carried the largest individual effect size—resulted in the most visible shift in the pooled estimate, yielding an HR of 1.28 (95% CI: 1.14–1.44) and successfully reducing the baseline heterogeneity (I2 decreased from 96% to 86.6%). Crucially, the systematic omission of the updated Ma 2025 [4] dataset yielded a highly robust and consistent pooled estimate of 1.53 (95% CI: 1.25–1.87), confirming that our primary findings are stable and not driven by this single high-risk cohort.

Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis of the association between sleep disorders and liver-related outcomes

Publication bias

Visual inspection of the funnel plot (Fig. 8) revealed a symmetrical distribution of studies, suggesting no evidence of publication bias. This was further confirmed by statistical tests; both Begg’s rank correlation test (P = 0.6211) and Egger’s regression test (P = 0.1199) produced non-significant results (P > 0.05), indicating that the results of the meta-analysis are robust and unlikely to be influenced by publication bias.

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of 13 cohort studies, we found that sleep disorders are significantly associated with an increased risk of liver-related outcomes, including hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), unspecified liver cancer, and cirrhosis, with a pooled HR of 1.49. These findings support the growing recognition that sleep disturbances are not merely comorbid conditions but may represent independent and potentially modifiable risk factors across the spectrum of chronic liver disease.

Subgroup analyses provide additional insight. Insomnia was significantly associated with increased liver-related risk (HR 1.39), whereas the association with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) did not reach statistical significance, likely due to substantial heterogeneity and variability in diagnostic definitions. Importantly, we observed a stronger association for confirmed HCC (HR 1.42) than for unspecified liver cancer (HR 1.26). This distinction underscores the importance of outcome specificity, as aggregating heterogeneous malignancies may dilute effect estimates. The stronger signal for HCC suggests that sleep disturbances may preferentially influence hepatocyte-driven carcinogenesis, potentially through metabolic and inflammatory pathways central to the cirrhosis-to-HCC sequence.

Although OSA exhibited a higher point estimate for liver-related risk (HR 1.80) compared to insomnia, this association did not reach statistical significance (95% CI: 0.91–3.58; P = 0.09). This lack of significance may be attributed to several factors. First, the extreme heterogeneity (I2 = 98%) observed in the OSA subgroup suggests substantial variability in clinical definitions and diagnostic methods across studies, ranging from ICD-coded diagnoses in administrative databases to clinical assessments. Second, OSA is intricately linked with obesity and metabolic syndrome, which are potent confounders for HCC and cirrhosis. In studies that rigorously adjusted for BMI and metabolic comorbidities, the independent contribution of OSA to hepatocarcinogenesis may have been statistically attenuated. From a biological perspective, the intermittent hypoxia characteristic of OSA is a known driver of oxidative stress and systemic inflammation, which are key mediators in the progression from MASLD to HCC. However, the potential “dilution effect” caused by unrecorded treatments, such as continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy, cannot be excluded. Patients receiving effective CPAP treatment may have a significantly lower risk of liver disease progression, thereby potentially masking the true pathogenic impact of untreated OSA in observational cohorts. Future studies utilizing standardized polysomnography data and accounting for treatment adherence are necessary to further elucidate the role of OSA in the liver disease continuum.

Our findings are broadly consistent with prior literature [19, 20]. Previous epidemiological studies have reported increased overall cancer risk among patients with OSA, with signals suggesting elevated hepatobiliary cancer risk. In parallel, global trends indicate that the rising burden of HCC cannot be fully explained by traditional etiologies such as viral hepatitis and alcohol, highlighting the role of emerging lifestyle-related factors, including sleep disturbances. Given the high global prevalence of insomnia and OSA, even modest relative risks may translate into substantial population-level impact.

Several biological mechanisms may explain these associations [21–24]. Intermittent hypoxia, a hallmark of OSA, induces oxidative stress, systemic inflammation, and activation of hypoxia-inducible pathways (e.g., HIF-1α), promoting angiogenesis, fibrogenesis, and DNA damage. Circadian disruption—common in insomnia and abnormal sleep patterns—can alter clock gene expression and melatonin signaling, both of which play key roles in hepatic metabolic regulation and tumor suppression [25]. This biological plausibility is further validated by a comprehensive systematic review by Clemente-Suarez et al. [26], which underscores the multifaceted impact of circadian misalignment on global oncogenesis, bridging molecular pathways with large-scale epidemiological trends. In our meta-analysis, this pathway is directly reflected in the findings of VoPham et al. [14], who demonstrated a clear longitudinal association between circadian misalignment and subsequent incidence of HCC. Furthermore, because sleep disturbances represent a potentially modifiable lifestyle risk factor, addressing circadian health carries substantial public health and socio-economic implications. As detailed by Clemente-Suarez et al. [26], circadian dysregulation inflicts a severe economic burden on healthcare systems globally; thus, incorporating structured sleep and circadian hygiene into chronic liver disease management can not only mitigate risk of hepatocarcinogenesis, but also alleviate long-term healthcare expenditure. In addition, sleep disturbances contribute to insulin resistance and metabolic dysfunction, facilitating the development and progression of MASLD, an increasingly important driver of HCC [27]. Disruption of the gut–liver axis and chronic low-grade inflammation may further create a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment [28, 29]. Crucially, recent evidence highlights that this cascade is interconnected with bidirectional microbiota-gut-brain communication, where microbial metabolites act as key drivers shaping both sleep patterns and systemic homeostasis [30]. Chronic sleep disturbance-induced dysbiosis not only compromises the intestinal barrier, but also triggers a systemic inflammatory response. This peripheral insult can propagate centrally, activating the S100A8/A9-mediated neuroinflammatory cascade, which has been closely linked to cognitive decline and sustained neuroinflammation [31]. This bidirectional amplification of neuroimmune and gut-liver signaling maintains a chronic, low-grade inflammatory state, thus accelerating hepatic injury, fibrogenesis, and ultimately progression toward HCC.

An important consideration is whether these associations are independent of shared metabolic risk factors. Notably, several included studies adjusted for major confounders such as body mass index, diabetes, and alcohol use, yet still demonstrated significant associations. This suggests that sleep disorders may exert direct pathophysiological effects beyond their correlation with metabolic syndrome [4, 18].

From a clinical perspective, these findings raise the possibility that sleep disorders may represent actionable targets for intervention. Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy has been associated with improvements in liver enzymes and systemic inflammation in patients with OSA, while melatonin has demonstrated antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties with potential hepatoprotective effects. From a clinical translation perspective, we propose a pragmatic sequential screening workflow for patients with MASLD or established cirrhosis. Hepatologists and primary care physicians should routinely utilize validated, low-burden screening tools, such as the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) or the STOP-Bang questionnaire, during routine outpatient follow-ups. Patients identified with severe insomnia or high-risk obstructive sleep apnea should then be prioritized for formal referral to sleep medicine and targeted therapies. However, a critical evidence gap remains: To date, no randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have prospectively verified whether sleep-targeted interventions (e.g., CPAP compliance or circadian-based therapies) directly reduce the incidence of HCC. To bridge this translational gap, future prospective interventional trials should be established using concrete validated endpoints, such as the long-term incidence of HCC, rates of cirrhotic decompensation, and serial changes in hepatic inflammatory and fibrotic biomarkers. Such studies will further elevate the clinical guiding value of incorporating sleep health into hepatology care.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, substantial heterogeneity was observed across analyses, likely reflecting differences in study populations, exposure definitions, and outcome ascertainment. Importantly, our broad inclusion of sleep factors encompasses both distinct clinical disease entities (such as OSA and primary insomnia) and lifestyle-related variables (such as short or long sleep durations). In epidemiological research, abnormal sleep duration is typically conceptualized as a continuous or categorical lifestyle behavioral trait rather than a strict pathological diagnosis. Pooling these heterogeneous exposure phenotypes inevitably introduces profound exposure-level heterogeneity, which complicates the direct comparison of the pooled effect sizes and limits our ability to isolate the independent pathophysiological impact of clinical sleep disorders versus lifestyle sleep habits. Future large-scale collaborations should utilize diagnostic-specific standardized cohorts to minimize this confounding. of the exposure level. Second, more than half of the included studies were judged to have a serious risk of bias, primarily due to residual confounding inherent in observational designs. Third, causality cannot be established, and reverse causation cannot be excluded, particularly in patients with subclinical liver disease affecting sleep patterns. Finally, variability in the classification of liver cancer outcomes remains a challenge, although our stratified analysis partially mitigates this issue.

Future research should focus on large-scale prospective studies with standardized definitions of sleep disorders and liver outcomes, as well as mechanistic investigations to clarify causal pathways. Interventional studies evaluating whether treatment of sleep disorders—such as CPAP or circadian-based therapies—can reduce liver disease progression or HCC incidence are particularly needed.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this meta-analysis demonstrates that sleep disorders, particularly insomnia, are associated with an increased risk of liver-related outcomes, including HCC. These findings highlight sleep disturbances as emerging and potentially modifiable risk factors in the progression of chronic liver disease. Incorporating sleep assessment into routine clinical practice may improve risk stratification and offer novel opportunities for prevention. Further prospective and interventional studies are warranted to determine whether improving sleep can reduce liver-related morbidity and mortality.
