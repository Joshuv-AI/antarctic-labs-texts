Essential amino acid supplementation for pressure ulcers: pathophysiological rationale and results from a pilot study
Authors: Maciej Świątkiewicz, Andrzej Lejczak, Piotr Foltyński, Paweł Grieb
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Pressure ulcers (PU) are a major and serious problem affecting millions of people. Malnutrition, frequently co-occurring with PU, is considered modifiable risk factor, and dietary supplements currently recommended for supporting PU healing contain a high amount of protein and some additives (zinc, vitamin C, arginine). Unfortunately, many patients with PU suffer from chronic kidney disease and would not tolerate a high-protein diet. Amino acid profiling of the blood serum of PU patients has shown decreased concentrations of exogenous amino acids histidine, tryptophan and threonine. Their common feature is a low daily requirement, 4 to 15 mg/kg body weight. In animal experiments, various types of stress increase metabolic demand for these amino acids. On the other hand, stress significantly slows down wound healing, both in animals and in humans. Considering these observations, a pilot clinical trial in the quasi-experimental design has been performed to assess the effect of Pellicar-F, a proprietary food composition containing histidine in the form of carnosine, plus tryptophan and threonine on PU control. After six weeks patients receiving daily doses of the aforementioned food composition, compared to control patients, displayed statistically significant reduction of the pressure ulcer area. Further evaluation of this product in pressure ulcers and other chronic wounds is indicated.

Introduction

Pressure ulcers (PU), called also pressure injuries, decubitus ulcers and bedsores, are a specific form of chronic wounds that develop in consequence of prolonged pressure, alone or in combination with shear and/or friction, or related to a medical or other device. PU lead to skin and underlying soft tissue damage. Usually they are localised over a bony prominence. PU vary in severity and characteristics, and their development is complex and multifactorial (Zaidi ).

Two major types of PU are called community-acquired pressure injuries (CAPI) which occur at home or in nursing homes (Chen et al. 2020) and hospital-acquired pressure injuries (HAPI) that develop during hospitalization. A particular type of HAPI which develop already during surgery are called operating room-acquired pressure injuries (Peng et al. 2024). It has been argued that HAPI shall be considered so-called „never events”, medical errors that should never happen because they are fully preventable. However, a consensus reached by experts in the field 10 years ago confirmed that some HAPI are unavoidable (Edsberg et al. 2014).

PU remain a major and serious healthcare problem throughout the world, affecting millions. In the USA alone their incidence has been estimated at around 1 to 3 million per year (see (Mervis And Phillips 2019) and the references cited). In a recent systematic review and meta-analysis (Afzali Borojeny et al. 2020) a pooled global estimate of HAPI incidence rate was reported as 12%. In a similar analysis (Sugathapala et al. 2023) a pooled estimate of PU prevalence in nursing home residents was calculated as 14.3%, close to those obtained for adult hospitalised patients or patients receiving palliative care. The other recent systematic review and meta-analysis (Song et al. 2019) showed that older patients living with PU experience two times higher risk of mortality than those who do not have such wounds.

This narrative review outlines the rationale of supplementing patients suffering from pressure ulcers with three essential amino acids (eAA) that seem to be specifically depleted in this patient group, namely histidine, tryptophan and taurine. We also present results of a pilot study in which inhabitants of nursing homes suffering from grades 1–3 PU were offered to participate by having their ulcers photographed at the beginning of the study period and six weeks later, and if they choose, taking daily orodispersible tablets contaning a composition of the aforementioned eAA’s for six weeks. Changes of PU area were compared between persons who voluntarily agreed to take the aforementioned tablets (the actively treated group) and those who did not agree (the control group).

Pathophysiology of pressure ulcers: classification and risk assessment

Pressure ulcers, along with vascular ulcers and diabetic ulcers, belong to chronic wounds. They all fail to proceed orderly and timely through the normal phases of wound healing because of prolonged or excessive inflammation and the inability of dermal and/or epidermal cells to respond to reparative stimuli (Frykberg And Banks 2015). However, PU are unique in that their occurrence and development are not attributed to a defined major disease as the underlying cause, local (vascular insufficiency) or systemic (diabetes).

In general treatment of any chronic wound shall include local care, management of ischemia and infection, and appropriate wound bed preparation (Frykberg And Banks 2015). Upon diagnosis, to guide treatment, PU should be classified according to the generally accepted classification system. The first such systems were designed in the 1950 s and later several others were proposed, but according to some authors, there is no evidence that anyone is superior (Kottner et al. 2020). An example of a widely accepted PU staging system is that published by the National Pressure Ulcer Advisory Panel (NPUAP) (Edsberg ).

Although considered preventable in most cases (Mervis And Phillips 2019), PU (both CAPI and HAPI) are highly prevalent (vide supra), and for practical reasons, risk assessment may be more important than classification (Kottner And Coleman 2023). Identifying risk factors of PU development is necessary to select patients who should be subject to labor-intense procedures aimed at preventing and eventually controlling PU.

Being an essential part of clinical practice, PU risk assessment is a complicated issue (Kottner And Coleman 2023). In spite of controversies various tools to quantify PU risk are widely used. One of the most popular is theBraden Scale designed more than 4 decades ago. Figure 1. presents a „conceptual schema” of this tool, clearly defining various factors that underlie the etiology of PU. This diagram is of high value for three reasons. First, factors engaged in the development of PU are classified as those related to pressure and those related to tissue tolerance. Second, as far, as tissue tolerance is concerned, the schema introduces a distinction between extrinsic and intrinsic factors involved. Third, although not the least, the schema clearly indicates that some risk factors are unmodifiable (patient’s age, sensory perception), others can be modified by local actions (eg. by alleviating pressure, controlling moisture, etc.), while the other (eg. nutrition) require systemic action (Table 1).

Dietary support for patients suffering from pressure ulcers

It is well known that malnutrition, prevalent in elderly patients, makes wound healing, especially PU healing, particularly difficult. Symptoms of malnutrition, such as unwanted weight loss, inadequate nutrient intake and low plasma albumin levels, are significant and, importantly, potentially reversible risk factors for the development of decubitus ulcers.

In 2013, the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (a US government agency that evaluates the quality and effectiveness of healthcare) published a comprehensive analysis of the safety and efficacy of various methods of managing PU (Chou ). It was demonstrated that nutritional support studies have evaluated many supplements containing proteins and specific additives, including vitamins and minerals, such as ascorbic acid (vitamin C) and zinc. These studies have provided moderate evidence that protein supplementation improves wound healing. The evidence for a similar effect of vitamin C was weak, and the evidence for zinc supplementation was insufficient. High-protein nutrients enriched with the amino acid arginine were reasonably effective, which was also confirmed in a later study (Chu et al. 2021).

A most recent update of the Cochrane review on nutritional interventions for preventing or treating PU (Langer et al. 2024) included 33 randomized controlled trials (RCT) that involved almost 8 thousand participants, although many of these studies were small and of low quality. For treating people with PU only few supplements to standard diet were tested, such as those providing energy, protein and micronutrients, those consisting of protein, arginine, zinc and antioxidants, and the others based on collagens. The authors concluded that currently there is no clear evidence of a benefit associated with these nutritional interventions for either the prevention or treatment of pressure injuries. Of note is that some clinical reports employing food constituents for treatment of PU, despite promising results, were not taken into account because they were not RCT, e.g., carnosine.

In spite of the limited evidence of benefit, oral nutritional supplements containing high protein content together with antioxidant micronutrients are advocated by some authors for patients with chronic wounds (Santo et al. 2024). Unfortunately, many those who suffer from, or are at risk of developing decubitus ulcers have contraindications to a high-protein diet. The most common is chronic renal failure, being a common consequence of diabetes and hypertension. According to data from the US, one-third of adults are at risk. Therefore many patients suffering from PU and renal failure face conflicting dietary recommendations due to the unique nutritional needs of each condition (Collins 2022).

Deficits of certain exogenous amino acids accompany chronic wounds

Are there particular amino acid deficiencies that are characteristic of chronic hard-to-heal wounds?An Australian study published in 2009 found that patients with PU or other chronic wounds had reduced serum concentrations of tryptophan (Trp) and histidine (His), while concentrations of eighteen other amino acids were unchanged. Interestingly, low Trp and His levels were noted even in patients without reduced plasma albumin levels and thus were not malnourished (Dawson And Favaloro 2009). A Polish study compared serum concentrations of 14 amino acids in patients with and without PU. Patients with decubitus ulcers had reduced concentrations of only four amino acids. Three of them, histidine, tryptophan and threonine (Thr) (Grieb 2019), are the essential amino acids not synthesized in the human body, which must be supplied with food.

An essential amino acid (eAA) is absorbed in the intestines, transported through the bloodstream, and used in competing biochemical metabolic pathways. The plasma concentration of each of them results from a balance between these processes. If, for some reason, certain metabolic pathways become activated and begin to use a particular eAA in greater amounts than usual, its concentration will decrease, leading to a deficiency.

There are nine essential amino acids for humans, but it appears that chronic wounds such as PU are accompanied by a decrease in serum concentrations of only three. What they have in common is a low daily requirement ranging from 4 to 15 mg/kg. This is significantly less than the daily requirement for the other essential amino acids (except for sulfur amino acids) (WHO 2007).

Stress-related increase in metabolic uptake of exogenous amino acids

His, Trp, and Thr have another common feature demonstrated in preclinical studies. Stress leads to the activation of metabolic pathways that increase the demand for these essential amino acids and deplete their pools in blood plasma (Fig. 2). Certain metabolic effects of stress can be offset by compensating for such a deficiency. In particular, such observations have been made on wound healing.

In humans, a significant deposit of histidine is found in skeletal muscle in the form of dipeptide carnosine (beta-alanyl-histidine). Skeletal muscle accounts for 30 to 40% of human weight, and the concentration of carnosine is about 1 g/kg (Brosnan 2020). In total, there is about 17 g of histidine in the human body. Nearly 50 years ago, experiments on chickens and rats provided evidence that stress caused by a skin wound or bone injury to a limb activates the release of histidine from carnosine in muscles and its conversion to histamine. These processes were prevented by administering histidine or carnosine to the animals (Fisher et al. 1978). More recent studies on mice (Kim et al. 2019) have shown that stress induced by chronic D-galactose injections impairs skin wound healing. This is accompanied by increased corticosterone levels in these animals; corticosterone is a murine equivalent of the human stress hormone cortisol. Administration of histidine or carnosine reduced corticosterone levels in the blood and simultaneously stimulated collagen synthesis in the skin and wound healing.

Similar observations were made concerning the other two essential amino acids which deficiency was implicated in the context of PU and chronic wounds. In mice subjected to immobilization stress, tryptophan administration improved impaired skin wound healing (Bandeira et al. 2015). Administration of threonine to rats subjected to stress induced by immobilization in water prevented the stress-induced development of mucosal disease and the increase in levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the blood (including IL-6, IL-8, and TNFα) (An 2019).

The case of threonine is unique, because this amino acid can be converted to glycine, and in various animals (fish, birds, mammals) is implicated in the synthesis of collagen (Mathew-Steiner et al. 2021). The role of collagen synthesis in wound healing is beyond doubt (Li and Wu 2018). However, Thr is used primarily for intestinal mucus synthesis, and in the case of threonine deficiency the priority of this metabolic pathway is maintained even at the expense of a decrease in plasma Thr concentration (Munasinghe et al. 2017).

Stress and wound healing

Stress of various types significantly slows wound healing in animals and humans due to the interaction between stress hormones (cortisol, or its animal equivalent, corticosterone) and the aforementioned pro-inflammatory cytokines (Christian et al. 2006). For example, a study performed on a group of healthy volunteers (Ebrecht et al. 2004) indicated a significant negative correlation between the speed of wound healing and the level of Perceived Stress scale scores as well, as the salivary concentration of cortisol.

In this context, it is essential to note an apparently underappreciated observation made a quarter of a century ago in the US on patients transferred to nursing homes. Before the transfer, none of the 26 patients who completed the study had decubitus ulcers, but 17 of them developed these wounds within a short time after being placed in a nursing home. Importantly, the patients who developed decubitus ulcers also presented significantly elevated blood cortisol levels; this difference was statistically significant and most severe in the second week after transfer (Braden 1998).

A proprietary amino acid composition for support of pressure ulcers healing

The observations discussed above did not address the question of whether the reduced blood levels of the said three exogenous amino acids are a cause or effect of stress. However, they led to design a novel and unique nutritional composition for supplementing the patient’s diet with the aforementioned essential amino acids, which reserves in the body appear to be depleted in stressful situations co-occurring with chronic, hard-to-heal wounds.

The product, trade name Pellicar-F, is formally classified as food for special medical purpose. It has the form of orodispersible tablets containing three active ingredients. The first is carnosine as a source of histidine. This dipeptide is found in meat (such as chicken broth or pork chops), but not in plant-based foods. Japanese authors (Sakae et al. 2013) monitored the healing dynamics of decubitus ulcers and found that during two months of follow-up, taking carnosine at a dose of 150 mg/day almost doubled the healing of these wounds. In human blood plasma, carnosine is rapidly hydrolyzed, but its positive effect on wound healing may not be solely due to histidine. Indeed, a recent German study showed that in human blood, carnosine is transported by red blood cells which protect it from enzymatic degradation. In this way, the unhydrolyzed peptide reaches the kidneys (Oppermann et al. 2021) and presumably can reach other places in the body. It is also worth mentioning that carnosine shows nephroprotective properties (Kilis-Pstrusinska 2020).

The second ingredient of Pellicar-F is threonine. We have not found data directly supporting the importance of threonine for wound healing in the current scientific literature but, as mentioned above, this amino acid is implicated in the synthesis of collagen (Mathew-Steiner et al. 2021). Therefore it undoubtedly is an important component of the wound-healing process.

The third component of Pellicar-F is tryptophan. This amino acid is primarily known as a precursor to the brain neurotransmitter serotonin, a deficiency of which occurs in mental illnesses, including mood and emotional disorders. For this reason, tryptophan was administered to patients suffering from depression or insomnia for half a century. However, the usual doses used in these conditions, 1.8–3 g per day (Kikuchi et al. 2021), substantially exceed the daily requirement of a healthy person for this amino acid and are not free of side effects, such as the release of several hormones into the blood, including cortisol (Porter et al. 2003).

In humans, tryptophan undergoes transformations through several different pathways. These include the synthesis of new proteins, the synthesis of kynurenine and its metabolites, the degradation of Trp by gut bacteria, and the synthesis of serotonin. The product of serotonin metabolism, in turn, is melatonin, commonly known as the hormone produced in the pineal gland and involved in regulating the sleep-wake cycle. However, only 5% of serotonin found in the human body is located in the brain, while 90% is found in the gut (Gibson 2018). Melatonin is produced in the gut by enterochromaffin cells that are part of the visceral nervous system. Oral intake of tryptophan causes a significant increase in blood melatonin levels (Celinski et al. 2014). The results of preclinical studies suggest that both kynurenine (Salimi ) and melatonin (Pugazhenthi et al. 2008) may improve wound healing.

Oral intake of His, Trp, and Thr seems to support control of pressure ulcers

The effect of supplementation with the Pellicar-F was the subject of an exploratory pilot study conducted in six long-term care facilities in the Warsaw area served by the medical and nursing company Zofiamed Ltd. (Katowice, Poland). The study protocol has been approved by the Bioethics Committee at the Warsaw Medical University (approval number KB/286/2023). Adult inhabitants of nursing homes (majority in their 70 s and 80 s) suffering from grade 1 to 3 PU, provided that they neither require sytemic antibiotic or steroid treatment, nor present obvious signs of local infection and/or wound ischemia, were offered to participate in the trial by having their ulcers photographed at the beginning of the study period and six weeks later(Patient's information Table 1). Following signing the informed consent each participant was further asked to voluntrarily decide whether during the study period she/he is willing to take once daily orodispersible tablets contaning a composition of the aforementioned three eAA’s. Persons who decided to take the tablets fell into the “actively treated” group, and those who did not take the tablets fell into the “control” group. Such research methodology is called the Quasi-Experimental Design (Maciejewski et al. 2013; Capili And Anastasi 2024).

During the study, all patients ate standard meals, and their decubitus ulcers were subjected to daily care including topical application of antiseptics. The endpoint of the study was the change in the wound area at week 6, expressed as a percentage of the area determined at the beginning of the study. Of 34 patients who entered the trial, 4 were transferred to a hospital before the end of the observation period, and 8 dropped out because of poor quality of photographs of wound area.

The area of PU were measured using the digital planimetry method developed at the Institute of Biocybernetics and Biomedical Engineering of the Polish Academy of Sciences, Warsaw, Poland. The wounds were photographed with two calibration rulers placed at their periphery, and their images were processed using a proprietary computer program. Compared to a commercially available reference tool (Silhouette from Arranz Medical, New Zealand), this method significantly improves measurement accuracy and precision (Foltynski And Ladyzynski 2022).

For the statistical assessment of the data the GraphPad Prism for Windows ver. 7.05 software package (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) was used. The normality of distribution was assessed with D’Agostino–Pearson omnibus and Shapiro–Wilk normality tests. Because the control group didn’t follow a normal distribution, non-parametric tests were used for data analysis. No correction for multiple comparisons was applied. The significance of differences between the data collected were tested using the Mann-Whitney test. Differences between groups were considered significant when p ≤ 0.05.

Measurements of the size of decubitus ulcers performed correctly both on the first day and after 6 weeks were obtained for 11 patients in the control group and 11 patients who took Pellicar-F. At baseline, the area of the observed wounds averaged 6.53 cm2 in patients in the control group and 7.44 cm2 in patients in the group receiving Pellicar-F. After 6 weeks, the majority of patients in the control group had an increase (in some cases severalfold) in the wound area. The average change in wound area in the control group was + 169%, with a 95% confidence interval of −45% to + 383%. In contrast, there was a statistically significant reduction in the area of wounds in patients in the group receiving Pellicar-F. The average change was − 42%, with a 95% confidence interval of −14% to −70%. This data are presented in Fig. 3.

Limitations of the pilot study and future directions

The major limitation is that the trial was not randomized. It did not involve random assignment to control and experimental group, but participants by themselves decided on taking or not taking the tested amino acid supplement. Such design, known as Quasi-Experimental, is considered a sound alternative when fully randomized, controlled clinical trial is not feasible or ethical (Maciejewski et al. 2013; Capili And Anastasi 2024), but testing oral amino acid supplements in pressure ulcers setting may not be such case. As mentioned above, several RCT of oral nutritional supplements were reported in the literature, mostly funded or supported by industry (Langer et al. 2024).

The other major limitation is the small number of participants. Although the difference between actively treated and control groups reached statistical significance, this does not eliminate the risk of the play of chance error type (Evans 2011). However, as noted almost 2 decades ago by Collins (2006), most randomized controlled trials are small relative to the clinical question they address.

Although the result of the pilot study is suggestive of that ingestion of Pellicar-F orodispersible tablets may significantly improve the treatment of PU, further evaluation of this product is indicated in the setting of prevention and treatment of pressure ulcers as well, as other chronic wounds, in particular in patients with kidney insufficiency who cannot take high protein supplements.

It should also be emphasized that this formulation does not display activity against microorganisms, therefore its intake will only supplement topical measures recommended in chronic wounds, such as antiseptics, dressings and/or physiotherapeutic methods, such as laser therapy, cold plasma application, etc.
