Bridging Cytology and Histology: Concordance Analysis of Lymph Node Lesions within the Sydney Framework
Authors: Pinky Pandey, Alka Yadav, Alok Dixit, Roopak Aggarwal, Asttha Kapoor, Neelshi Pandey, Parul Verma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background & Objective:Benign and malignant lymphadenopathy is a common presenting feature in a wide spectrum of diseases. Previously, there was no standard reporting system or common terminology available for lymph node cytology. A diagnostic “gray zone” exists in lymph node cytology, where accuracy declines, making it difficult to categorize certain lesions and leading to diagnostic discrepancies. The aim of the study was to assess the accuracy of fine needle aspiration of the lymph nodes and critically evaluate the cytohistological discordant cases. In the 20th International Congress of Cytology held in Sydney in 2019, a standardized category-based lymph node cytology reporting system was proposed.Methods:This is a retrospective study conducted at the Department of Cytopathology from January 2021 to December 2025. A total of 1500 cases were re-evaluated and reviewed and were classified according to the Sydney System and compared with clinical and histopathological diagnoses. The present study aimed to analyze and classify lymph node samples as per the newly proposed Sydney System and to calculate risk of malignancy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value (NPV) for all categories, considering histopathology as the reference standard. The statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 20.Results:Of the 1500 cases, distribution was as follows: L1, 150; L2, 1000; L3, 180; L4, 115; and L5, 55. Risk of malignancy (ROM) was 1.3% for L1, 0.7% for L2, 99.4% for L3, 99.1% for L4, and 96.36% for L5.Conclusion:The Sydney System study helped in achieving uniformity and reproducibility of cytological diagnosis. It enhanced understanding and communication between clinicians and pathologists, thereby improving patient care.

Introduction

Fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) is widely used as an initial approach for investigating lymphadenopathy of uncertain cause. It is a rapid, minimally invasive, and cost-effective technique that also provides material suitable for ancillary investigations, which enhances diagnostic accuracy (1).

A reliable diagnosis of both reactive and malignant lymphadenopathies can often be achieved when FNAC findings are supplemented with ancillary tests, including flow cytometry (FC), immunocytochemistry (ICC), microbiological studies, and molecular assays (2-20). These combined methods reduce the need for unnecessary surgical biopsies. FNAC is particularly valuable in patients for whom surgery is risky or not feasible, such as elderly individuals with multiple comorbidities or those with widespread metastatic disease. Nevertheless, excisional biopsy and histopathology continue to be regarded as the reference standards for confirming malignant lymphadenopathy (21,22).

The cytological interpretation of lymph nodes can be challenging because many lesions exhibit overlapping morphological features, which may introduce subjectivity in diagnosis. Currently, lymphoma diagnosis by FNAC almost always requires histopathological confirmation. Furthermore, in the absence of a structured reporting framework, cytology of lymph nodes has not been widely adopted in routine clinical decision-making (23). To address these limitations, a category-based reporting system—known as the Sydney System—was introduced at the 20th International Congress of Cytology in 2020 (24).

Despite its promise, the Sydney System has been slow to gain widespread acceptance, and literature on its practical application remains sparse (11). The present study was undertaken in a tertiary care center in northern India, where the Sydney System was incorporated into routine diagnostic practice as a pilot project. The framework was developed to provide consensus-based terminology, enhance communication among pathologists and clinicians, and link cytological findings with suggested management strategies (25).

The classification suggested two levels of diagnostic categories, in which the first category is mandatory to categorize the lesion according to a broad diagnosis from L1 to L5, such as inadequate, benign, and malignant. The second diagnostic level aimed at identification of specific diagnostic entities by using ancillary techniques like ICC, fluorescent in situ hybridization, and cell block to obtain a more accurate diagnosis on a cytopathological basis.

The classification also recommended the format of lymph node reporting in cytopathological examination, in which basic clinical data, radiological findings, site, and basic diagnostic categories (L1-L5) are recommended. Additional data such as microscopic description, ancillary techniques, and secondary diagnosis, if any, were also suggested (26). However, the Sydney System remains underutilized, and there are limited data in the literature to date (27). Therefore, the present study was aimed at validating the diagnostic utility of the Sydney reporting system in fine needle aspiration cytology for lymph node lesions. The main objective was to calculate the risk of malignancy for all categories from L1 to L5. Unfortunately, the lack of universally accepted terminology for reporting results has hindered its widespread support. The Sydney System proposal for lymph node cytopathology categorization and reporting introduced five diagnostic categories to address the lack of universally accepted terminology for reporting results in lymphadenopathy.

Materials and Methods

This retrospective observational study was carried out based on archived cytology and histopathology records, and wherever possible, additional clinical and radiological information of benign and malignant nodes (Fig. 1) was retrieved from hospital records to supplement the requisition form data, with efforts made to standardize case review and data extraction to minimize confounding factors. It was conducted in the Department of Pathology, Uttar Pradesh University of Medical Sciences, Saifai, from January 2022 to May 2025. The study utilized de-identified archival slides and records without any direct patient contact or intervention. Institutional Ethics Committee approval was obtained with a waiver of informed consent, in accordance with the ICMR National Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical Research Involving Human Participants (2017). This complies with standard practice for retrospective cytology audits.

Inclusion criteria and Exclusion criteria:

All consecutive lymph node aspirates from patients were included. Both sexes and all age groups were included in the study. Non–lymph node aspirates were excluded from the study.

A total of 1500 lymph node FNAC cases performed over the past three years were reviewed. To minimize observer bias during smear re-examination and Sydney System categorization, all cases were independently reviewed by two experienced cytopathologists who were blinded to the original diagnosis and histopathological outcome at the time of review. In instances of disagreement, a consensus diagnosis was reached through joint discussion. While interobserver variability was not statistically quantified, this dual-review approach helped reduce subjective bias. In the present study, cases with incomplete cytological details, inadequate smears, or missing essential clinical information were excluded from the final analysis. Specifically, cases for which histopathological follow-up data were not available were analyzed separately for cytological distribution but were excluded from the risk of malignancy (ROM) and diagnostic accuracy calculations. This approach ensured that only histologically verified cases contributed to the statistical validation of the Sydney System categories.

Superficial and palpable lymph node FNAs were performed blindly, while image-guided FNAs (mostly ultrasonography-guided) were carried out for deep-seated, nonpalpable, or suspicious malignant nodes. Procedures were performed using a 23-gauge needle under aseptic conditions. The cytological smears in our study were prepared and stained following institutional standard operating procedures. Air-dried smears were stained with May–Grünwald–Giemsa (MGG), and alcohol-fixed smears were stained with the Papanicolaou stain, as per routine protocol. Periodic internal quality control checks were performed by the cytology laboratory as part of accreditation requirements.

For each case, at least two air-dried and three alcohol-fixed smears were prepared. Air-dried smears were stained with May–Grünwald–Giemsa (MGG), while alcohol-fixed smears were stained with Papanicolaou and hematoxylin-eosin (H&E). In suspected cases of tuberculosis, additional smears were stained using the Ziehl–Neelsen method. Cell blocks and immunohistochemistry (IHC) were performed in selected cases, particularly for subtyping lymphomas on histopathological specimens.

Histopathology and Follow-Up

Diagnostic accuracy was assessed by correlating FNAC findings with histopathology wherever tissue was available. For cases without biopsy, clinical follow-up records were reviewed. Of the total 1500 FNAC cases, histopathological confirmation was available for 1060.

Ancillary Techniques

Cell block preparation and immunohistochemistry (IHC) were performed in cases in which cytomorphology was inconclusive or required further characterization, such as atypical lymphoid lesions, metastatic malignancies of unknown primary, or poorly differentiated neoplasms. The decision was based on cytological findings and the availability of residual material in the needle rinse. Appropriate IHC markers were applied on cell blocks to aid in lineage determination and diagnostic categorization as per the Sydney System.

Risk of malignancy (ROM) for each Sydney System category was calculated by dividing the number of confirmed malignant cases by the total number of cases with histological or clinical follow-up in that category (Table 1).

Sample Size Calculation

The sample size was calculated to estimate the sensitivity of the Sydney System to detect malignancy. Assuming an expected sensitivity of 0.85, a desired precision of ±5% for the 95% confidence interval, and an expected disease prevalence of 40% among cases with histopathological follow-up, 196 histopathology-positive cases were required.

n = Z² × Se (1 − Se) / d²

This gives the number of true-disease cases (histopathology positive) needed to estimate sensitivity with precision.

n = 1.96² × 0.85 × 0.15 / 0.05² ≈ 196

Convert to total sample size using disease prevalence P:

N = n / P

N = 196 / 0.40 ≈ 490

This corresponds to a total of approximately 490 cases (196/0.40). We therefore included all eligible cases available during the study period (N = 1500), which meets the calculated requirement.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The chi-square test was applied to evaluate the association between Sydney System categories and corresponding histopathological outcomes. Risk of malignancy (ROM) for each category was calculated along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using the binomial proportion method. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

T A total of 1500 fine-needle aspiration (FNA) smears of lymph nodes were reviewed during the study period. Of these, 80% (n = 1200) were percutaneous aspirations, while 20% (n = 300) were image-guided procedures. The patients’ ages ranged from 5 months to 70 years, with a mean age of 30 years. There was a female predominance, with 930 cases (62%) compared to 570 males (38%), yielding a female-to-male ratio of 2:1. The distribution of histopathological outcomes across Sydney system categories showed a statistically significant association (χ² = 1550.3, df = 4, p < 0.001). The risk of malignancy (ROM) increased progressively from L1 to L5, ranging from 0.7% in L2 (95% CI: 0.2–1.2%) to 99.4% in L3 (95% CI: 97.1–100%).

Cervical lymph nodes were the most frequently aspirated site, accounting for 70% (n = 1050) of cases, followed by inguinal lymph nodes in 25% (n = 375). Axillary (n = 10; 0.7%) and submandibular lymph nodes (n = 10; 0.7%) were less commonly involved.

Non-diagnostic/inconclusive cases (L1) represented 10% (n = 150) of all aspirates. Among these, 112 cases (75%) showed only blood with no cellular elements, while 38 cases (25%) contained only necrotic material (Fig. 2).

Benign diagnoses (L2) (Figures 3, 4) constituted the largest group, seen in 66.6% (n = 1000) of cases. Within this category, tuberculous lymphadenitis was the most frequent finding (n = 500; 50%), followed by nonspecific lymphadenitis (n = 70; 7%), reactive lymphadenitis (n = 30; 3%), necrotizing lymphadenitis (n = 40; 4%), and acute suppurative lymphadenitis (n = 30; 3%). Tuberculosis was the predominant granulomatous lesion, characterized by epithelioid cell granulomas, Langhans giant cells, and necrosis, with Acid-Fast Bacilli positivity confirming the diagnosis in several cases.

Atypia of undetermined significance (AUS) (L3) included 12% (n=180) cases with atypical lymphocytic population and atypical non lymphocytic population (Fig. 5).

Discussion

It has been observed that the application of standardized reporting systems in cytopathology reduces intraobserver variability in reporting and helps in the communication of clinically relevant information in a reproducible manner (27-29). Moreover, it enhances the interpretation of cytopathological reports by clinicians with regard to risk assessment.

The proposed system for reporting LN-FNAC cytopathology had the following seven aims:

1. Develop consensus guidelines and a framework of reference to facilitate communication among cytopathologists, hematopathologists, clinicians, surgeons, and other health care providers.

2. Define and identify LN-FNAC indications, preferred operators, recommended performance, analytical and preanalytical issues, technical and diagnostic limitations, as well as the basic diagnostic reporting categories and additional diagnostic information that can produce specific disease subtyping when possible.

3. Obtain the key diagnostic cytopathological features of lesions that occur commonly in the various categories.

4. Make recommendations on the components of standardized diagnostic reports with the aim to improve reporting and communication between cytopathologists and clinicians.

5. Provide management recommendations linked to the reporting categories with possible options that include the use of clinical and imaging follow-up, ancillary testing, and the possible need for LN excision.

6. Encourage cytohistopathological correlations, cell storage, and research on neoplastic and non-neoplastic LN specimens.

7. Increase LN-FNAC reliability and clinician awareness of its diagnostic potential.

In the present study, the risk of malignancy was calculated for every diagnostic category in the Sydney System of classification and reporting of lymph node cytopathology. It was found that the risk of malignancy was 1.3% for the nondiagnostic category. This is lower when compared to 27.5% found by Gupta et al (26), much less than the 50% found by Vigliar et al (1), and 66.7% found by Caputo et al (25). Repeat FNAs using ultrasound guidance and utilization of ROSE explain the lower rate of false-negative results in this category in our study. However, the high risk of malignancy found in this category by Vigliar et al (1) was explained by them to be owing to the lower number of cases having histopathological follow-up data under this category.

Within expectations, the benign category (L2) had the lowest ROM of 0.7%. This is lower when compared to that found by Vigliar et al (13) (1.92%), Gupta et al (11) (11.5%), and Caputo et al (30) (9.38%). Out of the 8 malignant cases classified in this category, 1 was metastatic carcinoma in lymph nodes. This was a similar problem to that encountered by Vigliar et al (13) and Garg et al (31) in their study, where lymph nodes with partial involvement by metastatic carcinoma did not yield representative malignant cells on cytology (13,22). There were 5 cases diagnosed as lymphocyte-predominant type of Hodgkin lymphoma. It has been found to be extremely difficult by FNAC, and similar sampling errors in cases of FNAC of Hodgkin lymphoma have been seen in other studies (28,31-32).

The L3, or atypia of undetermined significance category, was introduced in most reporting systems with the aim of maintaining high negative and positive predictive values in the benign and malignant categories, respectively. The risk of malignancy calculated for the L3 category in this study was intermediate (99.4%). This was quite lower when compared with 58.3% and 66.7% in the studies conducted by Vigliar et al (1) and Gupta et al (26), respectively, but higher than that calculated by Caputo et al (30) (28.6%). Other studies also encountered similar pitfalls in cytology smears of reactive lymph nodes due to viral etiology and interfollicular expansion (20,21).

Each of the L4 (suspicious for malignancy) and L5 (malignant) categories in this study had a very high ROM of 99.1% and 96.36%, respectively. The ROM calculated for these categories by Gupta et al (26) was 88% and 99.6%, respectively. Vigliar et al (1) calculated it to be 100% for both categories, which was owing to the extensive use of ancillary procedures and flow cytometry in their studies. The number of false-negative results may be further diminished by use of the second diagnostic level based on ancillary techniques, as suggested in the proposal for the classification system (4). The benign case categorized under the L4 category in this study was a case of parafollicular hyperplasia containing large immunoblast-like cells and histiocytes along with numerous tingible body macrophages, which revealed Reed-Sternberg–like binucleate cells in the cytopathology smear. Immunoblasts mimicking Reed-Sternberg cells on cytology are a commonly encountered phenomenon (34).

The benign case categorized under the L5 category in this study revealed reactive hyperplasia with a predominance of centroblast-like cells in the smear. It was seen in this study that the maximum number of cases were categorized under the malignant category (64.09%), and only two cases were categorized under the malignant category. This was similar to the studies conducted by Vigliar et al (1) and Gupta et al (26), which had a greater number of cases categorized under the malignant category. However, this is owing to the pattern of cases commonly encountered in the health care setup, being located in a region where there is a high predominance of tuberculosis, and benign lymphadenopathies are routinely sent for FNA.

The kappa coefficient of 0.98 demonstrates an almost perfect agreement between cytological and histopathological diagnoses according to the Sydney System. This high level of concordance highlights the reliability and reproducibility of lymph node FNAC when reported using this standardized framework. It also indicates that the system minimizes interobserver variability and strengthens diagnostic confidence for both benign and malignant categories. The sensitivity, specificity, NPV, PPV, and accuracy were also found to be comparable with other studies (Table 3).

Limitations of the Study

Interobserver variability: In our study, cytological review and categorization according to the Sydney System were independently performed by two experienced cytopathologists. Discordant cases were jointly reviewed to reach a consensus. Although interobserver variability was not statistically quantified, we acknowledge this as a limitation and plan to include formal kappa analysis in future prospective studies.

Being retrospective in design, it was not possible to control for all potential confounding variables, such as variability in clinical history documentation, sample adequacy, and differences in follow-up data availability. Nevertheless, uniform diagnostic criteria based on the Sydney System were applied, and re-evaluation of all available slides was performed to ensure consistency and reduce observer bias.

As this was a retrospective study based on archived cytology cases, there is a possibility of selection bias, as only cases with complete records and available slides were included. Nevertheless, uniform inclusion criteria were followed, and a broad range of diagnostic categories were represented, providing a realistic overview of the application of the Sydney System in routine cytology practice.

Histopathological correlation could be established in 1060 out of 1500 cases, as tissue diagnosis was available only for cases that proceeded to biopsy or excision. This partial correlation limits complete validation of the Sydney System categories. Nonetheless, the cases with available follow-up were representative of the diagnostic spectrum, allowing reliable evaluation of system performance.

Conclusion

The implementation of the Sydney System for lymph node cytology provides a standardized and structured framework that improves diagnostic accuracy and reproducibility. In our study of 1500 cases, categorization using the Sydney System allowed for clearer communication between pathologists and clinicians and facilitated accurate assessment of the risk of malignancy across all categories. The system proved especially useful in reducing interpretative variability in challenging cases, including atypical and suspicious lesions. Adoption of this standardized reporting approach enhances patient management by enabling informed clinical decisions and demonstrates its practical utility in routine cytopathology practice.
