Ventilator-associated lower respiratory tract infections and bacteremia in medical and surgical critical care patients colonized by multidrug-resistant bacteria
Authors: Ignacio Torres, Josep Ferrer, Javier Colomina, María Ángeles Clari, Beatriz Olea, Estela Giménez, Eliseo Albert, Pablo Sánchez-Jordán, Gerardo Aguilar, Nieves Carbonell, David Navarro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe investigated the risk of bloodstream infections (BSI) and ventilator-associated lower respiratory tract (LRT) infections, including pneumonia (VAP) and tracheobronchitis (VAT), due to multidrug-resistant bacteria (MDRB) in colonized critical care patients attended in the medical intensive care unit (MICU) and surgical critical care unit (SCCU).MethodsObservational, unicentric study including 573 consecutive adult patients (median age of 66 years; range, 18–85 years) and comprising a total of 585 admissions. Universal MDRB screening cultures (from nasal, pharyngeal, rectal, and axillary sites) were regularly performed. Blood cultures and (semi)quantitative cultures of endotracheal or bronchoscopic specimens were performed and interpreted following consensus guidelines.ResultsColonization by MDRB was documented in 201 out of 585 admissions (34.4%), in which 261 MDRB were isolated, most frequently extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Enterobacterales (n = 102) followed by MDR-S. maltophilia (n = 45), carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales (n = 40), MDR-Gram-positive bacteria (n = 35), and MDR-P. aeruginosa (n = 28). There were 21 MDRB BSI, mostly caused by Gram-negative bacteria (95.8%). Colonization by MDRB was independently associated with subsequent MDRB BSI (HR14.1; 95% CI, 3.29–60.65 P < 0.001). MDRB were recovered in 157/430 admissions requiring invasive mechanical ventilation (36.5%) and there were 25 episodes of ventilator-associated LRT infections due to MDRB, mostly MDR-Gram-negative bacteria. Colonization by MDRB was independently associated with subsequent MDRB LRT infections (HR, 6.59; 95% CI, 2.67–16.26; P < 0.001).ConclusionMDRB colonization is a significant risk factor for the occurrence of MRDB-matched invasive infections in a mixed cohort of MICU and SCCU patients. MDRB screening cultures using a multi-site sampling approach may be useful for tailoring empirical antimicrobial treatments on an individual basis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12027-6.

Introduction

Bloodstream (BSI) and lower respiratory tract (LRT) infections, in particular those associated with mechanical ventilation (ventilator-associated pneumonia [VAP] and ventilator-associated tracheobronchitis [VAT]), occur frequently in patients attended in critical care units and harbor high morbidity and mortality. BSI in intensive care unit patients is associated with 35–42% mortality [1–4]. In turn, VAP increases morbidity, the time in mechanical ventilation, intensive care unit stay, medical costs, and overall mortality [2, 5–7]. The increasing prevalence of multidrug-resistant bacteria (MDRB), including extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)- or carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales, multidrug-resistant (MDR)-Pseudomonas aeruginosa, MDR-Acinetobacter spp., MDR-Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) or vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE) is problematic. It is often associated with an inadequate empirical antimicrobial treatment, which ultimately increases mortality [1, 2, 8, 9]. A large body of clinical experience summarized in several recently published systematic reviews [10–14] indicates that either rectal (gut) or nasal colonization by MDR-Gram-negative bacteria/VRE and MRSA, respectively, often precedes invasive infections in this setting. Thus, the performance of MDRB surveillance cultures is potentially instrumental for the initiation of appropriate empirical antimicrobial therapy regimens. Nevertheless, studies addressing the potential relationship between colonization and subsequent invasive infection by MDRB are rather heterogeneous regarding the clinical characteristics of the patients involved, MDRB screening policies, and the anatomical sites sampled for colonization cultures. Moreover, these studies often fail to provide the time frame within which infections occur following documentation of colonization. As a result, the size of the risk of transition from colonization to infection for the different MDRB targeted in colonization studies has not been consistently established. To further address this issue, we investigated the risk of BSI and ventilator-associated LRT infection in critical care patients attended in the medical intensive care unit (MICU) and surgical critical care unit (SCCU) carrying MDRB. Importantly, MDRB screening cultures were systematically planned to be performed regardless of the presence or absence of baseline risk factors for MDRB colonization, and a multi-site sampling strategy for MDRB surveillance was used.

Patients and methods

Study population

The current single-center retrospective observational study included 573 consecutive adult patients (≥ 18 years old; 389 males and 184 females; median age of 66 years; range, 18–85 years), comprising a total of 585 admissions between January 2019 and February 2023, either in the MICU (n = 329) or SCCU (n = 256) with a minimum stay of seven days, which was the only inclusion criterion. The patients were followed up for up to 65 days. Patients clinical characteristics retrieved from medical records are displayed in Table 1. No patient in the current cohort was transferred to MICU or SCCU from another hospital. Of relevance, in 430 admissions (73.5%) the patients were on invasive mechanical ventilation (endotracheal tube connected to a mechanical ventilator) for at least 48 h (median, 11 days; range, 2–84 days). For patients with two or more admissions (n = 10), these occurred at least one month apart. Of note, over the past 15 years, Critical Care Units at our center have adhered to the recommendations proposed by the Spanish Society of Intensive Care Medicine (SEMICYUC) regarding the ZERO projects in intensive care units for patient safety. Specifically, the “Pneumonia Zero” project was launched in 2011 (Guidelines for the prevention of ventilator-associated pneumonia and their implementation. The Spanish “Zero-VAP” bundle [15], and in November 2021, the recommendations were updated after reviewing the accumulated scientific evidence (https://proyectoszero.semicyuc.org/). The updated guidelines include seven basic mandatory measures for the prevention of VAP, one of which is oral hygiene with chlorhexidine 0.12–0.2% every 6–8 h, among others. Additionally, three highly recommended specific measures are outlined, including the use of systemic antibiotics during intubation in patients with a decreased level of consciousness. The current study was approved by the Ethical Committee of Hospital Clínico Universitario INCLIVA (January 20th /2023), from whom an informed consent waiver was obtained.

Microbiological analyses

Microbiological cultures

Surveillance MDRB cultures of nasal, pharyngeal, axillary, and rectal specimens were protocolized to be systematically performed regardless of the patient’s risk factors for MDRB colonization within 72 h of admission (baseline) and weekly afterward, following Unit-approved guidelines. The MDRB targeted in screening cultures were ESBL, carbapenemase or plasmidic AmpC-producing Enterobacterales, carbapenemase-producing P. aeruginosa, MDR-P. aeruginosa, MDR-S. maltophilia, MDR-Acinetobacter spp., MRSA, and VRE, according to local guidelines. Swabs were delivered to the Microbiology Service in AMIES transport medium (cliniswabLTS, Aptaca Spa., Canelli, Italy) and immediately processed following the Clinical Microbiology guidelines issued by the Spanish Society of Infectious Diseases and Clinical Microbiology (SEIMC) [16]. Pharyngeal, axillary, and rectal swabs were placed in brain-heart infusion broth tubes (BHI; Oxoid Limited, Hampshire, UK) containing a disc of cefotaxime (5 µg) for MDR-Gram negative enrichment, as previously reported [17]; nasal specimens were inoculated in antibiotic-free BHI tubes. Of note, axillary and rectal swabs were inoculated in BHI tubes in combination. BHI tubes were incubated at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator (Heracell 240i CO2 incubator, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Langenselbold, Germany) for 24 h. These were then subcultured on MacConkey Agar (Becton Dickinson [BD], New Jersey, USA) in which meropenem (10 µg), cefotaxime (5 µg), and ceftazidime (10 µg) discs (Oxoid Limited, Hampshire, UK) were disposed, and Columbia Blood Agar with 5% Sheep Blood (BD) with a 5 µg vancomycin disc (Oxoid Limited). Isolated colonies growing near the discs were identified using Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization time-of-flight Mass spectroscopy (MALDI-TOF MS) (Bruker Daltonics, MA, USA). Patients colonized with MDRB underwent contact isolation.

Endotracheal aspirates (ETAs) and, on a few occasions (at the discretion of the attending physicians), bronchoalveolar specimens (bronchoalveolar aspirates [BAS] or lavages [BAL]) from mechanically-ventilated patients with suspected LRT bacterial infection were processed by conventional culture-based procedures. This included Gram staining and semiquantitative cultures (reported as CFU/mL) on selective/differential media, including colistin-nalidixic blood agar, MacConkey agar, and chocolate agar (all purchased from BD, New Jersey, USA). The plates were inoculated with 1 µL calibrated loops and then incubated at 35 °C in CO2-enriched air and examined for growth at 24 and 48 h. Isolated colonies were identified using MALDI-TOF MS as in previous samples. Bacterial growth was always reported (semi)quantitatively. Blood culture (BC) samples (BACTEC Plus Aerobic/F and Plus Anaerobic/F medium bottles, BD) were collected from patients with suspected bacteremia/sepsis and incubated in an automated continuous monitoring blood culturing instrument (BACTEC FX; BD). Aliquots from each flagged BC bottle were subjected to routine Gram stain microscopy, subcultured on chocolate blood medium (BD), and incubated for 24–48 h. Direct bacterial identification from BCs was performed by MALDI-TOF MS testing using intact bacteria pellets, as previously described [18]. Leftover bacterial pellets were tested by the immunochromatographic assays NG-Test CTX-M Multi, NG Rapid Test CARBA-5 (NG Biotech, Guipry-Messac, France), and Clearview™ PBP2a SA Culture Colony Test (Abbott Laboratories, IL, USA), as appropriate, following the manufacturer´s instructions.

Antimicrobial susceptibility and genotypic resistance marker testing

Conventional antimicrobial susceptibility testing from isolated bacteria was performed by broth microdilution using the MicroScan NM44 panel for Gram-negative bacteria and PM33 for Gram-positive bacteria, both from Beckman Coulter (Brea, CA, USA), and interpreted according to contemporary European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) guidelines https://www.eucast.org/fileadmin/src/media/PDFs/EUCAST_files/Breakpoint_tables/v_12.0_Breakpoint_Tables.pdf. The “Multidrug Resistance” Direct Flow Chip (Máster Diagnóstica, Granada, Spain) DNA microarray-based assay was used to characterize genotypic resistance employing isolated bacterial colonies, as described [19] and was performed either contemporarily or retrospectively. This assay displays a sensitivity and specificity of 100% for all target genes included in the array [19].

Definitions

MDRB was defined as resistance to one or more agents in three or more antimicrobial categories, as previously recommended [20]. MDRB colonization was defined as the detection of the respective organism in at least one surveillance culture (from any site). Any similarity between the antimicrobial susceptibility profile and genotypic resistant marker pattern among isolates recovered from blood or LRT specimens and surveillance cultures was deemed to indicate bacterial identity. LRT infections (VAP or VAT) were categorized as such by the attending physicians and defined by two or more of the following indicators: new or progressive radiographic infiltrates plus at least two of the following: fever >38 °C, leukocytosis (leukocytes, >10 (8)/µl), or leukopenia (< 4 (8)/ µl) increase in LRT secretions and acute-phase reactants, or hypoxemia [21, 22], . MDRB recovered in CFU/ml counts ≥ 105 for ETA and BAS and ≥ 104 CFU/ml for BAL were deemed to be causally involved [21–24].

Statistical analysis

Frequency comparisons for categorical variables were carried out using Fisher’s exact test or the Chi-square test, as appropriate. Positive and negative predictive values (PPV and NPV, respectively) of MDRB colonization for the occurrence of VAP and VAT are given. Cox regression models were built to assess the association between several variables and clinical events of interest. For multivariate analyses, only variables with parameter estimates showing a P value ≤ 0.10 in univariate analyses were included; two-sided P-values < 0.05 were deemed significant. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Rate of colonization by Multidrug-resistant bacteria in the patient population

Most patients (n = 424) in the current cohort were directly admitted to the MICU or SCCU (430 admissions); the remaining patients (n = 154) had been transferred to these units from another hospital ward, with a median stay of seven days (range: 3–92 days) before referral (155 admissions). Of note, five patients were either transferred to or directly hospitalized in the MICU in two or more successive admissions. Screening for MDRB was performed at least once during the MICU/SCCU stay for all admissions. Overall, the median duration of admissions was 18 days (range, 7-103). As shown in Table 1, SCCU admissions were a few days longer than those in the MICU. A total of 3,828 surveillance cultures were performed during the study period. The distribution of specimens across testing times in both units is shown in Supplementary Table 1. Of relevance, in both units, axillary and rectal swabs were collected regularly; in contrast, fewer pharyngeal and nasal specimens were submitted from the SCCU than the MICU. Overall, colonization by one or more MDRB was documented in 201 out of 585 admissions (34.4%), 100 were from the MICU and 101 were from the SCCU, corresponding to a total of 198 out of 573 patients (34.6%). Colonization by MDRB occurred more frequently in the SCCU than in the MICU (39.5% vs. 30.4%; P = 0.02).

In most admissions (60.7%) patients were colonized by a single MDRB. In the remaining 54 admissions, two or three MDRB were isolated in combination (Supplementary Table 2). Of note, colonization by two MDRB was more frequent (P = 0.04) in the SCCU than in the MICU, mostly at the expense of the combination Enterobacterales plus MDR-S. maltophilia. A total of 261 MDRB were isolated (Table 2), most of which were Gram-negative (n = 226; 86.6%), most frequently ESBL-producing Enterobacterales followed by MDR-S. maltophilia, carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales, notably OXA-48 type and MDR-carbapenemase or non-carbapenemase-producing P. aeruginosa. Among Gram-positive bacteria, there were 35 MDRB recovered (MRSA, n = 13; VRE-E. faecium, n = 22). The spectra and frequencies of MDRB isolated from patients in the MICU and SCCU were rather comparable (Table 2). Accordingly, the prevalence of MDRB per admission was 16.5% for ESBL-producing Enterobacterales, 6.6% for carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales, 12.6% for MDR-non-fermenting Gram-negative bacteria, and 5.9% for MRSA/VRE.

As shown in Table 2, for most admissions, MDRB were cultured from a single body site (122/201; 60.7%), mainly from axillary-rectal samples (97; 79.5%). In the remaining 79 cases (39.3%), the same or different MDRB could be recovered from more than one sampling site. Overall, MDR-non-fermenting Gram-negative bacteria were cultured more frequently from pharyngeal specimens than from axillary-rectal samples, whereas the opposite was true for MDR-Enterobacterales. As expected, axillary-rectal specimens demonstrated superior performance for recovery of VRE compared with other locations, whereas MRSA was cultured more frequently from upper respiratory tract specimens.

Results of surveillance cultures carried out at baseline were available from 377/585 admissions (64.4%). As shown in Supplementary Table 3, MDRB (n = 77) were recovered from 74/377 admissions (19.6%). MDR-Enterobacterales were predominant at this testing time (roughly in 70% of admissions), followed by MDR-non-fermenting Gram-negative bacteria (22%), and MDR-Gram-positive bacteria (9.1%) ranking last. Again, no relevant differences were noticed across MICU and SCCU admissions. Of interest, in 18/74 admissions (4 MICU and 14 SCCU), patients became colonized by a different MDRB (n = 19) during follow-up (Supplementary Table 4), in most cases within 15 days of admission (73.6%) and predominantly by MDR-Enterobacterales. Of note, MDRB colonization could occur slightly later (between days 16–20) in SCCU patients compared with MICU patients. To investigate the timing of intra-unit MDRB colonization during follow-up admission, we conducted a subanalysis including 62 admissions (31 in the MICU and 31 in the SCCU), in which surveillance cultures were negative at baseline and patients eventually became colonized (20% of admissions). A total of 76 MDRB were recovered in culture. As shown in Supplementary Table 5, irrespective of the MDRB considered, patients became colonized between days 6–15 (68%) in most admissions; nevertheless, colonization tended to occur later in the SCCU (42% beyond day 15) than in the MICU (31.5% beyond day 15). Overall, irrespective of the unit considered, subtle differences were noticed across MDRB species; for example, MDR-Enterobacterales more frequently colonized patients at later times (≥ day 16) during follow-up (in 47% of admissions) than MDR-non-fermenting Gram-negative bacteria (34%) and, more notably, than MDR-Gram-positive bacteria (0%).

Bacteremia caused by multidrug-resistant bacteria in carriers

The data are summarized in Fig. 1. Overall, a total of 144 BSI were documented corresponding to 137 (23.4%) admissions. The non-MDRB involved are detailed in Supplementary Table 6. Of most relevance to the present study, there were 62 BSI episodes in MDRB-colonized patients, of which 21 were due to MDRB. In 19/21 (90.4%) of these BSI episodes identity between MDRB isolates recovered from screening cultures and BC was demonstrated. MDRB bacteremia episodes were monomicrobial in all but one case and developed a median of three days (range, 0–30 days) after colonization was documented. In non-MDRB-colonized patients, there were 82 BSI episodes, of which only two were due to MDRB. Thus, MDRB BSI occurred at a significantly higher rate in MDRB-colonized patients, irrespective of MDRB identity (P < 0.001) than in non-MDRB-colonized patients. As shown in Table 3, in both the MICU and SCCU, MDRB recovered from BC were in almost all cases Gram-negative (95.8%). MDR-Enterobacterales were clearly predominant in the MICU whereas, in the SCCU, the percentage of MDR non-fermenting Gram-negative bacteria almost equated to that of MDR-Enterobacterales (46.7% vs. 53.3%, respectively). The sampling site/s in which MDRB-causing bacteremia was isolated is shown in Table 3. Discussion

The salient observations of the present study are the following. First, from an epidemiological standpoint, MDRB colonization was documented in 34.4% of admissions (34.6% of patients), this being more frequent in the SCCU than in the MICU (P = 0.02). The incidence rate of MDRB colonization per admission was higher for ESBL-producing Enterobacterales (16.5%) and MDR-non-fermenting Gram-negative bacteria (12.6%) than for carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales and MDR-Gram-positive bacteria (below 7% of all admissions). As expected, MDR-non-fermenting Gram-negative bacteria and MRSA were recovered at a higher rate from upper respiratory tract specimens, whereas MDR-Enterobacterales and VRE were cultured more frequently from axillary-rectal specimens. Several recently published systematic reviews [11, 14, 25] clearly showed that the incidence rate of MDRB colonization among hospitalized patients, including those in critical care units, varies widely depending upon several critical factors, such as the local MDRB epidemiology, patient characteristics, hospital ward, length of hospital stay, recent use of antimicrobials—in particular β-lactams—before sampling, occurrence of MDRB outbreaks and the strategy and microbiological procedures implemented for MDRB screening; as to the latter, a noticeable heterogeneity in timing and frequency of screening and MDRB targeted was seen across the studies [11, 14, 25]. In keeping with our data, the predominance of MDR-Gram-negative over MDR-Gram-positive bacteria has been consistently reported [11, 14, 25]. Second, due to the frequent sampling schedule, we could determine the dynamics of MDRB colonization in both Units. Overall, one or more MDRB were recovered from screening cultures in around 19% of admissions, mainly MDR-Enterobacterales (roughly 14%) and MDR-non-fermenting bacteria to a much lesser extent (around 4%). These figures are within the range reported for gut colonization by MDR-Enterobacterales (3 to 23%) and P. aeruginosa (2–23%) in different countries [reviewed in 11,14]. Likewise, screening culture results were only available in around 65% of admissions. Nevertheless, a larger fraction of patients became colonized de novo or by a different MDRB strain during the MICU/SCCU stay, mainly by MDR-Enterobacterales. In our series, MDRB colonization acquisition during hospitalization mostly occurred within the first 2–3 weeks after admission, regardless of the unit considered, and mainly involved MDR-Enterobacterales. Our data are in line with those reviewed by Heath and colleagues [11], revealing that the median time to gut colonization after admission ranged from 4 to 26 days. A practical consequence derived from these data is that the performance of screening cultures should be continued until the end of the patient’s stay; however, due to the dynamics of MDRB acquisition, further studies are warranted to determine whether the frequency of sampling may be reduced after day 21 of hospitalization. This would greatly minimize the associated laboratory burden and costs. Third, invasive MDRB infections, mostly BSI and pneumonia, developing in critical care patients are frequently preceded by colonization by a matched or even unmatched MDRB, so previous colonization is a significant risk factor for progression to severe infections [reviewed in 9, 12, 14]. This association has been established for ESBL-Enterobacterales [26–32], carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales, and MDR-non-fermenting Gram-negative bacteria [33-36]; our data seem to further support this association. In effect, in both units, MDRB BSI and LRT infection (VAP and VAT), almost universally caused by MDR-Gram-negative bacteria, developed at a significantly higher rate in MDRB-colonized patients, irrespective of MDRB identity, compared with non-MDRB-colonized patients. Matched MDRB were recovered in screening cultures a median of three days before the diagnosis of invasive infection. A clear predominance of MDR-Enterobacterales was seen in the MICU, whereas the frequency of MDR-Enterobacterales and MDR-non-fermenting bacteria tended to equate in the SCCU, likely reflecting differences in the local epidemiology of MDRB across units. In fact, colonization by MDRB at any time during hospitalization increased the risk of subsequent MDRB BSI by more than ten-fold and the risk of MDRB LRT infection by almost seven-fold. Importantly, while colonization at admission (baseline) increased the risk of MDRB bacteremia (around three-fold) it did not increase the risk of MDRB LRT infection (HR,1.63; P = 0.32). The interpretation of this finding is not straightforward. It may be that at the time of admission more than 90% of patients require the placement of a vascular access device (a central line and, in most cases, an invasive arterial line), or an urinary catheter. In contrast, only one-third of patients require intubation upon ICU admission. In this context, the likelihood of an invasive infection facilitated by a device that breaches the natural barrier (such as a catheter or an endotracheal tube) in a patient who is already colonized at admission would, intuitively, be higher for BSI. Nevertheless, we cannot completely ruled out this being an artifactual statistical finding linked to the relative small sample size.

Noticeably, MDRB identity between the colonizing and infecting strains was demonstrated in most episodes of both MDRB bacteremia (19/21) and MDRB VAP (12/15) but not VAT (3/10). The frequency at which MDRB colonization seemingly transitioned to invasive infection, as given by the PPV, varied depending on the clinical condition examined and the MDRB considered. It ranged between 2.8 and 12.1% for bacteremia and 2.8–9.1% for LRT infection, being lowest in both cases for MDR-Gram-positive and highest for MDR-Gram-negative bacteria, especially carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae. In a previously published systematic review [14], the overall rate of progression from MDRB colonization to infection (undefined) was around 18% in high-risk wards, including the intensive care unit. Specifically, regarding VAP for example, previous studies indicated that the rate of progression for ESBL-Enterobacterales varied between 3.8 and 17% [27, 32], figures relatively close to those reported herein (around 6%). In this context, our study further suggests that OXA-48-producing K. pneumoniae may have an increased dissemination capacity compared with other MDR-Enterobacterales, as has been shown for carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae [33, 34]. Furthermore, the data seemed to indicate that progression to BSI and LRT infection may be comparable overall between MDR-Enterobacterales and MDR-non-fermenting bacteria; in this sense, a novel observation of our study relates to the seemingly lower dissemination capacity of S. maltophilia compared with other MDR-non-fermenting bacteria. Unfortunately, a comparison between our study and others addressing this issue is not straightforward; in fact, progression from colonization to infection depends on host-pathogen interactions, which are unlikely to overlap across studies. Despite this, a relatively universal finding among previously published studies [25-36] and ours is that MDRB screening cultures display a very high NPV for invasive infections, ranging between 90 and 100%, this further lends support to the systematic use of MDRB colonization cultures in MICU/SCCU patients. Fourth, our study supports the collection of pharyngeal specimens for MDRB screening cultures for empirical antimicrobial decision-making. In this sense, recovery of MDR-S. maltophilia, MDR-P. aeruginosa, and OXA-48-producing K. pneumoniae from pharyngeal (and nasal) specimens but not from axillary-rectal swabs was not an infrequent finding.

The current study is not without limitations. First, in addition to its retrospective and unicenter nature, the number of MDRB BSI and LRT infections that developed within the cohort was rather limited. This precluded the performance of robust analyses individually assessing the risk of progression from colonization to invasive infection for the different MDRB. Second, the multi-site collection of specimens for MDRB screening cultures was not systematically performed during all admissions, as protocolized. This was be related to the fact that the study period overlapped with that of the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic; during this time, critical care units at our center were overpressured and new staff not entirely familiar with the MDRB surveillance protocol was recruited; this may have caused an underestimation of the incidence rate of MDRB colonization and, more importantly, that of progression from colonization to invasive infection. Third, MDRB-enriched screening cultures were performed and qualitative results were provided. In this sense, recent data seem to support the assumption that both rectal and throat semiquantitative cultures are strong predictors of ESBL-Enterobacterales VAP [31]. Fourth, axillary and rectal specimens were combined for screening cultures; this is not common practice, however, previous data from our group (unpublished) indicated that this strategy yielded comparable results to when specimens were processed individually and resulted in a lower cost burden. Fifth, a comprehensive assessment of the impact of the timing and nature of prior or ongoing antimicrobial therapy during the hospital stay, which could have an impact on the current study`s outcomes, could not be performed. Finally, criteria for VAP and VAT diagnosis and CFUs/ml cut-offs used for inference of MDRB LRT infection causality, although relatively established, could be debatable.

In summary, our data support the assumption that MDRB colonization is a significant risk factor for the occurrence of MRDB-matched invasive infections, including BSI and VAP in a mixed cohort of MICU and SCCU patients, and further lend support to the utility of MDRB screening cultures using a multi-site sampling approach for tailoring empirical antimicrobial treatment on an individual basis, especially due to their high NPV.
