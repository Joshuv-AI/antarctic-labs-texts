Lessons from a crisis: occupational stress in healthcare workers in an acute paediatric teaching hospital in Ireland
Authors: Shay Ward, Gráinne Donohue, Johanna Murray, Fiona McNicholas
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic placed increased pressure on service provision and healthcare worker (HCW) wellness. As the crisis of the pandemic receded, paediatric healthcare staff required an appropriate response to facilitate individual and organisational recovery, to minimise long-term HCW burn-out and to be better equipped for future crisis in paediatric healthcare.ObjectiveTo explore the experiences of HCWs working during the COVID-19 pandemic in an acute paediatric hospital to determine an appropriate leadership response in the postcrisis work environment.MethodsQualitative research design using responses from open-ended questions from 133 clinical and non-clinical staff (89% clinical) from an Irish paediatric teaching hospital. Responses were thematically analysed.ResultsPaediatric HCWs experienced frustration, uncertainty, anxiety and stress, during the pandemic crisis. Perceived organisational contributors included communication inconsistencies, inadequate support and resources, including staff shortages. This exposed remaining staff to high risk for long-term burn-out as the pandemic recedes. Three themes were developed detailing this: support, communication and trust.ConclusionThis research supports the long-standing need to increase mental health service investment and to implement an appropriate response to regain and maintain a healthy workforce, post-COVID-19. The organisational response should address the biopsychosocial needs of the individual and paediatric healthcare organisations should work dynamically, creatively and collaboratively to ensure the psychological safety of their workforce.

Introduction

It is well documented that high staff stress combined with increased clinical demands and decreasing resources, creates the perfect conditions for occupational stress and burn-out.1 2 Burn-out in healthcare can lead to job dissatisfaction, inadequate patient care and impaired job performance.3 Although burn-out can have comorbid issues such as depression, anxiety and substance use,4 it is also considered by the WHO5 as a distinct psychological state caused by an individual’s occupation or workplace. In the European Union, occupational stress is among the most prevalent work-related illnesses reported by workers, with significant personal and societal costs. Although less than the European average, in Ireland, 18% of the workforce experienced rates of occupational stress and this more than doubled between 2001 and 2015, demonstrating the steepest increase compared with workers in other Western European countries.6

Research exploring the impact of both COVID-19 and previous pandemics has highlighted the increased stress and poorer psychological functioning among healthcare workers (HCWs) and systems.7 For many already working in under-resourced and stretched conditions, the crisis of the pandemic led to high levels of stress, anxiety and depression among workers.8 Throughout the pandemic, HCWs carried the additional burden of caring for sick and contagious patients with worries of becoming ill themselves or contaminating family members. For staff working with a paediatric population, these difficulties are compounded by also managing the anxieties of parents, already distressed by having a sick child attending hospital during a pandemic. These young people and families have witnessed significant life changes since the global outbreak, from school closures and social distancing measures to their presenting health crisis.

A recent Irish study9 revealed the first national mental health picture of the impact of the pandemic across Irish workplaces. Among the findings are that one in five Irish firms have experienced mental health-related issues in the past year, mental health-related absenteeism is on the rise and the majority of employers in Ireland are not investing in workplace mental health and well-being supports. What is important to consider as the pandemic recedes, therefore, is the appropriate organisational response to the concerns expressed by staff during the crisis to regain and maintain a healthy and functioning workforce.

While there was significant focus on burn-out for medical and nursing staff even prior to 2020, the COVID-19 pandemic has markedly increased stress among all types of HCWs.10 Prasad et al found that 43% of staff were suffering from work overload and 49% were experiencing burn-out. This stress was influenced by increasing work demands and personal safety risks (ibid). Recent studies have confirmed that the pandemic made HCWs even more susceptible to burn-out11 12 resulting in high percentages of HCWs experiencing burn-out-related symptoms.13 This occurs in both acute and community settings, among varied professional groups and present in clinical and non-clinical staff.14 Examining the well-being of all HCW roles is of crucial importance given widespread healthcare staffing shortages, as well as ensuring role sustainability for those who remain in the field.15

There is a pressing need, therefore, to identity the appropriate response in terms of resources, support and management techniques to avoid a burn-out epidemic among paediatric HCWs. This study aims to describe paediatric HCWs’ experiences in providing healthcare during the pandemic to order to identify solutions to optimise care and staff morale postcrisis. To our knowledge, there have been no qualitative studies conducted of paediatric HCWs working during COVID-19 in Ireland.

Setting

Ireland’s largest paediatric teaching hospital was the setting used to access the HCW population for this study. The hospital employs approximately 1900 whole-time equivalent staff and is responsible for the provision of most quaternary and tertiary healthcare services for children.16 During the height of the COVID-19 pandemic, contingency plans for the hospital included preparing staff for deployment to adult services, alternative paediatric care, community vaccination programmes, contact tracing and other administrative posts where necessary. However, due to the high rates of public adherence to restrictions across the country of Ireland, few of the contingency plans were enforced.

Methodology

The aim of this qualitative study is to explore and analyse the open-ended responses of paediatric healthcare staff working during COVID-19 and thereby determine an appropriate response to support staff in a postcrisis work environment. A study-specific questionnaire was designed using validated stress questionnaires alongside questions designed specifically for this study. The quantitative results have already been reported and can be located at Murray et al.17 This paper reports on the qualitative data generated by the open-ended questions included in the survey (see Box 1). The Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research checklist (online supplemental material) was consulted in the writing up of this study.18

Sample and recruitment

Following ethical approval and with the support of the hospital’s communication department, a link to an online questionnaire was sent to all staff (clinical and non-clinical), with paper and pen surveys also left in each department. Posters and flyers were also placed on noticeboards advertising the study alongside a study link. An information leaflet and consent form were attached to the questionnaire.

Data analysis

A thematic analysis was performed on free-text sections of individual statements following the Clarke and Braun model19 by authors SW, GD and FM. This process follows six steps, namely: (1) becoming familiar with the data, (2) generating initial codes and (3) searching for themes. Once all transcripts were initially coded (SW and GD), codes relating to participant experiences were examined in greater depth. At this stage, notes were made on initial codes that seemed important or relevant and codes were organised into broader themes that said something specific. In stage 3, overarching parent codes were deductively selected to correspond with the interview guide’s three principal areas of interest (ie, suggestions about what should change in their work to reduce the risk of burn-out; what they felt that the organisation could do to better support staff during COVID-19 and anything else the participant felt relevant to say about their overall experience of working during COVID-19). An inductive analytical approach was subsequently implemented to identify subcodes and the relationships and connections between codes (SW, GD and FM). Any overlapping codes were collapsed to form larger, more inclusive themes (stages 4 and 5). Throughout the analytical process, researchers would meet frequently to discuss codes, exploring different possibilities for patterns and potential themes. Codes and categories would be added and changed as new ideas were identified from the data. Themes were finalised through consensus among the researchers. These final themes were then cross-checked against the original raw data by members of the research team (SW and GD) and consensus was reached regarding interpretation, relationships and titles.

Public and patient involvement

While the authors recognise the importance of public and patient involvement in research, for this project using an online survey method and directed at HCWs in one Irish paediatric hospital, it was not deemed appropriate in this instance.

Results

Out of a possible 1900 eligible members of staff, 133 staff members accessed the questionnaire online or via paper version. From these 183, 133 participants completed the data, giving a completion rate of 73%. Sociodemographic details were obtained from the sample including age, gender, years spent working in the organisation and employment background (see table 1). The majority were female (n=119, 89%), aged between 18 and 34 years (n=40, 30%) and 72 (54%) had been working for more than 5 years in the service. High level of occupational stress was reported among hospital staff, with 70% of respondents scoring at high levels for personal burn-out, and 62% work-related burn-out. Further details on these quantitative results can be located at Murray et al.17

Theme 1: support

A central theme that emanates throughout the dataset is the need for support throughout the pandemic crisis. Both clinical and non-clinical staff identify the need for practical supports such as additional staff, improved IT infrastructure and increased managerial input. As one respondent put it, ‘archaic IT… this is what leads to stress and burn out—not clinical issues’. If staff do not feel supported in their roles, they can become isolated, increasing the risk of burno-ut;

What we need is acceptance of the fact that limited resources are a huge driver of stress. I don't want mindfulness or Pilates to help fix work induced stress; I want the resources I need to treat patients and thereby avoid the stress occurring.

The COVID-19 pandemic led to an increased sense of isolation among staff. In some instances, this created feelings of competition among staff, rather than collaboration. This took on different forms of expression in this group, from ‘the individual versus the institution’, to ‘me versus them’ and ‘non-medical vs medical staff’. As one participant put it,

This is the most hierarchical organization I have worked in and it is all about the doctors unfortunately and the other staff have very little say or involvement which is very frustrating.

The often-used concept of resilience when it comes to managing individual burn-out is no longer considered acceptable to staff and there are many appeals for the organisation to increase its’ supportive functions or to ‘stop placing the responsibility for resilience within the individual and focus on the institution instead’. One participant outlined requested supports as follows:

To not harass us all day every day to do more work with less resources and then at the end of the day they are happy to blame staff for poor performance instead of accepting they too have a role”.

And;

We need practical support to enable us to deliver our services. Less emphasis on making us feel like we’re just not ‘resilient’ enough.

Ambivalence existed among participants as to the perceived value of occupational health support, with some expressing positive attitudes while others perceiving it as stigmatising and unhelpful.

I felt very supported. Nursing admin were amazing in very difficult times. Occupational health apparently were also wonderful but I didn't need to access them personally. I feel we as a nursing staff very lucky to have such great support at our fingertips’.

Some respondents reported that they did not feel able to ‘reach out’ and ask for support or help for fear of being judged and treated harshly: ‘I really feel that if I told my manager about how I feel I would be sent to occupational health and then signed off due to concerns about my mental health’. This highlights the importance of leadership in times of crisis and that the need for a clear, cohesive and concise message that brings staff together. This brings us to our next evolving theme from the analysis—communication.

Theme 2: communication

Communication in general was reported to have deteriorated during the pandemic, especially during the initial waves as healthcare organisations struggled to respond to the evolving crisis of the pandemic. This was compounded by the initial fear of infection and the need for personal protective equipment which affected non-verbal communication among staff on site. However, apart from these physical barriers, participants of this study detailed many instances of frustration and anxiety caused by unclear messaging which unaddressed, can lead to burn-out. As one respondent said;

I think overall the message from the hospital is that staff are not valued. Receiving emails then from corporate about well-being do not therefore land well as overall the corporate message is not one of care and compassion for staff.

For some, there was a growing sense of distance between staff and management;

There seems to be a gap between staff on the floor and upper management, a feeling of not being heard, this needs to be worked on…there is very little trust there.

Part of the role of leadership is to build psychological safety. However, the participants of this study felt frustrated with the lack of acknowledgement and clear messaging during the crisis of the pandemic. When asked what would help support staff, one participant said, ‘Consistent information. Information changed from one hour to the next and day to day which was frustrating’. For other staff, it was about acknowledgement;

Offer sincere gratitude to the hard work all staff have done throughout the last year. It has been a very stressful year where everyone has worked extremely hard under immense pressure. Staff would like to be simply thanked genuinely for this work and effort.

Participants expressed a sense of despair over the lack of clear communication, which for some had a detrimental long-term affect;

Listen to people. Do something about childcare- the silence is deafening… my goodwill is lost forever.

And;

(Management should) Take responsibility for actions and knock-on effects of those actions. Communicate better—lack of transparency and perceived lack of honesty is not helpful to staff well-being.

There is also an acknowledgement of the crisis and the impact that has had on management;

I feel senior clinical staff were well ahead when it came to rolling out new initiatives in many ways. But I do believe that xxx was working very hard and also had a considerable amount of crisis management to take on - and there is the human side of that to consider—and it is important that everyone gets a break.

And;

In fairness though I did find some solace initially during the pandemic in the knowledge that the organization did care about its staff. That came right from the top, but the middle management side was slightly floundering, it felt.

In summary, communication was a powerful medium which when effective had the effect of consoling a staff population in a time of mass global anxiety. When messaging was unclear, inappropriate or absent, however, it left staff feeling more vulnerable and undervalued, thereby increasing the risk of burn-out among the group.

Theme 3: trust

Trust is viewed as a foundation to any positive relationship, individual or organisation, and in this context is known to increase cohesion.20 When an organisation, such as a health service, needs to quickly pivot to a new way of operating, it requires its workforce to trust management in this process. Part of the role of management and team leaders, therefore, is being clear on what is expected and who does what. People need clear expectations and goals, and that staff will be supported in their efforts to achieve those goals. As one respondent put it; ‘Staff need clear honest information about what is happening operationally, rather than platitudes on being wonderful.’

While change in a system can provoke anxiety, it is the responsibility of management and team leaders to contain that anxiety by giving reassurance therefore imbuing trust. During the pandemic, participants of this study expected change and wanted change; ‘Everyone expected a change, instead we have the same old hospital attitude’.

In a functioning team, trust allows people to feel safe and can enable people to reach out, to put their hand up to ask for help and to admit if they are stuck with a problem they cannot seem to solve. If people do not feel that trust exists, then feelings of mistrust can lead to feelings of being isolated from the team, the institution versus the individual, ‘them vs me,’ and a personal sense of burden can expose people to burn out. Many participants of this study reported not feeling safe in seeking help or support with their role as they believed it may have negative repercussions for them. Staff also reported feeling unsafe about directly communicating with or about their managers:

We need to be able to provide feedback on our manager in a safe and anonymous manner and know that it will be addressed.

To get the best from people, it is important that a culture of trust is established and maintained.

Discussion

The global pandemic brought great uncertainty with massive restrictions and changes in the daily work lives of HCWs who are likely to have been strongly affected by these experiences. It has been found that the acute stress of COVID-19 may have exacerbated worker’s fear and helplessness, leading to the onset and development of post-traumatic stress disorder among hospital staff.11 12 Since the mental health and well-being of HCWs during the pandemic is as important as their physical health, we need to understand both the organisational effects of the COVID-19 pandemic and its psychological effects on HCWs as the crisis recedes, to prioritise HCWs well-being.

The results of this study detailed organisational issues in the form of resource and support needs and addressed the intrapersonal difficulties people experienced vis a vis communication uncertainty resulting in issues concerning trust. Any of these singular issues can lead an individual to burn-out but collectively and left unaddressed over time, can be a perfect storm for long-term HCW distress. When a workforce is under stress and facing burn-out, effective communication and conflict resolution are recognised as an essential factor in supporting the performance, health and safety of healthcare teams.21 However, for most of the participants of this study, there was a perceived lack of clarity in communication to them during the pandemic, which lead to mistrust between departments, clinical and non-clinical staff and between workers and management.

‘Trust’ is where Erik Erickson22 starts his stages of psychosocial development when he details how infants learn to trust that their caregivers will meet their basic needs. If these needs are not consistently met, mistrust, suspicion and anxiety may develop. For Erikson, it was a way of thinking rather than a factual analysis. The individual needs mesh with the demands of society, or in the case of HCW, the demands of the organisation. The organisation not meeting the basic needs of the worker in this study has led to highly anxious states represented by the interpersonal difficulties discussed through the themes of trust and communication. Lencioni and Wiley, management consultants, write, ‘trust is the foundation of which an organization is built’.23 They list trust as the first building block in their team functioning pyramid model; all relationships are built on the foundation of trust and without it you cannot build a team.

In a recent paper, Taylor and Seager24 make the argument that an appropriate response to the pandemic requires meeting the universal biopsychosocial needs of the individual and not treating concerns in isolation. By calling for a reconsideration of Maslow’s hierarchy of needs, Taylor and Seager suggest what is more appropriate than the traditional hierarchical response, is ‘an updated dynamic, circular and interactive model, linked to concepts of attachment’ (ibid). This is spoken about in the context of COVID-19, whereby the serious decline or loss of human attachment, belonging, meaning and purpose had serious consequences on individuals’ well-being. For the participants of this research, therefore, it is not enough to only address in isolation the needs identified here. In developing a postpandemic response, it is necessary for healthcare organisations to not only respond to the basic needs of an individual but to respond to the collateral damage of the pandemic in a collaborative, interdisciplinary dynamic manner. For example, some research has highlighted that the workplace constitutes an important context in which generativity can be developed or regained through appropriate coaching, mentoring and leadership.2527 Generativity refers to making a positive impact and contributing to the world, such as engaging in meaningful work while stagnation, on the other hand, represents feeling stuck and unproductive, lacking a sense of purpose.22 The participants of this research displayed a sense of stagnation in their work due to an inability to affect increasing work demands and workplace stress experienced during COVID-19 and the perceived lack of support in terms of basic resources required to provide quality and consistent healthcare.

Recovery from burn-out can be considered in three ways: person-directed interventions, organisation-directed interventions or a combination of both.2830 Person-directed interventions focus on teaching the individual to recognise stressors and use resilience-enhancing techniques, for example, mindfulness-based practice, or Pilates as mentioned by one participant. However, for the participants of this research, there was a clear sense that this approach lets the organisation off the hook, by redirecting the responsibility of resilience back to the individual. Organisation-directed interventions are focused on changing the working environment and preconditions.31 However, many studies have looked at the combination of these two approaches.29 32 Some researchers suggest that supports for HCWs need to be in place such as Schwartz Rounds and trust measures to restore worker confidence both in their own roles and in management.33

Conclusion

The COVID-19 pandemic has been the biggest disruptor to healthcare systems and workers in recent history. In the face of adversity, HCWs have to abandon personal security and risk their lives to ensure the safety of patients under their care. Although the pandemic demonstrated the capacity of workers to face these challenges head-on, it also created intensely stressful experiences for a system already under pressure. As the crisis of the pandemic recedes, there is an onus on healthcare organisations to regain and maintain a healthy workforce and to address the inevitable burn-out that is so prevalent after a crisis. However, since burn-out is a complex, and multifaceted phenomenon solutions to address this issue must be equally dynamic in response.

Strengths and limitations

Although some research has suggested that the nature of the job may have a direct effect on burn-out,34 this has not been consistently found, with clinical and non-clinical staff similar in levels of burn-out.14 Given the smaller numbers of non-clinical staff responding, in an effort to protect anonymity, this study did not make any distinctions between recorded responses and job role. Further research could concentrate on specific roles within healthcare and appropriate responses to their identified needs. As the data were extracted from open-ended questions from an online survey, there was no chance to ask follow-up questions or enquire at a deeper level. Face-to-face semistructured interviews may have garnered a richer dataset from which to draw from. The limitations, therefore, are noted in using a survey method with open-ended questions particularly regarding the inability to explore responses in more depth. The inclusion of open-ended questions also makes the survey more time-consuming for participants and given the large sample involved, a longer coding and analytical process for researchers. The retrospective nature of the data analytical process is also a limitation given the ever-changing perspective of health workers as the pandemic ebbed and flowed. Further research examining in-depth qualitative data on HCWs experiences postcrisis would be beneficial.

Notwithstanding these limitations, the study is the first to offer insight into the experiences of clinical and no-clinical staff working in a busy paediatric hospital at the start of the pandemic. The key themes identified, trust, support and the importance of clear communication, align with our understanding of effective leadership and success. Promoting these will be a core task towards repair of pandemic-related adversity at work.
