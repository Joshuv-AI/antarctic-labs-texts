A combined genomic arrhythmia propensity score delineates cumulative risk
Authors: Tanner O. Monroe, Megan J. Puckelwartz, Lorenzo L. Pesce, Samuel D. Kearns, Patrick Page, Nora Ibrahim, Zachary T. Weber, Emmanuel I. Ugwor, Marcelo A. Nóbrega, Prince Kannankeril, Laura J. Rasmussen-Torvik, Lisa M. Dellefave-Castillo, Alfred L. George, Gregory Webster, Elizabeth M. McNally
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryCardiac ventricular arrhythmias can cause sudden death. Despite known genomic contributions, multigenic risk predictors are limited. The genetics of arrhythmias and cardiomyopathies overlap, with additional overlap with epilepsy. To improve genetic risk prediction, we assemble a cohort with non-ischemic ventricular arrhythmias and controls lacking cardiac diagnoses. Here, we integrate 18 polygenic scores; variants from clinical gene panels for coding regions of cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia, and epilepsy genes; and noncoding regulatory regions mapping to those genes. Polygenic scores alone hold prognostic value. Rare coding variants identify cumulative risk extending beyond known pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants. We also find enrichment of ultrarare regulatory variation. A risk predictor that combines all variant classes outperforms any single class or subset and replicates in a validation cohort. This combined genomic arrhythmia propensity score (GAPS) identifies high-risk individuals even among those who lack known primary pathogenic variants. This integrated approach serves as a model for other complex traits.

Introduction

Ventricular arrhythmias (VAs) are under heritable influence with contributions from genes encoding ion channel, structural, and other proteins.1 Deleterious variants help assess risk for sudden cardiac death (SCD), and gene panel testing is recommended for patient management.2 Because of genetic overlap, testing often encompasses both cardiomyopathy and arrhythmia (CMAR) gene panels. Despite covering a low proportion of the genome, these panels yield a diagnosis ∼10% to 60% of time, but many, even familial, cases still lack a genetic diagnosis.3,4,5,6,7,8 Genome-wide association studies (GWASs) reveal common variants associated with cardiac traits, but few studies have combined GWAS data with monogenic risk. Even fewer studies include rare, noncoding regulatory variants.

Prior work suggested that having multiple rare coding variants in CMAR genes portends greater SCD risk.9,10,11,12,13,14 Rare noncoding variation influences arrhythmia risk factors,15 cardiomyopathy,16,17,18 and congenital heart disease (CHD).19,20,21,22 Variation in epilepsy genes also contributes to arrhythmia risk.23,24,25 Polygenic scores derived from GWAS data show some clinical utility.26,27,28,29 Furthermore, common variants can modify penetrance of monogenic cardiac disease,30,31 but integrating rare and common variants remains challenging.32 It is becoming clear through complex trait studies that combining variant contributions29,33 and classes34,35,36 may improve the predictive power.37,38

To enhance VA genomic risk prediction, we generated and analyzed whole-genome sequence (WGS) data from a high-VA-risk (N = 523) cohort, in comparison to controls (N = 596). For common variation, we computed polygenic risk scores (PGSs) for 18 traits. We next analyzed rare variants in 377 CMAR and epilepsy genes. Last, we identified variants in regulatory regions. Each genomic subset, common, rare coding, and ultrarare regulatory, contributed independent associations. Combining these subsets in a series of composite analyses improved risk prediction with each genotype layer. A cumulative variant score accurately identified high-risk individuals, independent of primary pathogenic variants. A replication cohort validated these findings. This approach provides a framework for quantifying complex disease risk across a genetic variation continuum.

Results

Cohort

As part of an American Heart Association Strategically Focused Research Network, we assembled 523 cases (median = 33 years, interquartile range [IQR], [19, 44]) who experienced SCD or carried high clinical SCD risk (Table 1). This early age enriches for genetic mediators.39 Approximately 58% of cases had non-ischemic cardiomyopathy and 129 (25%) died from SCD. Nearly two-thirds (63%) of living participants had an implantable cardiac defibrillator (ICD) or were medically recommended for ICD. Of the ICD carriers, 34% received an ICD for primary prevention, 36% for secondary prevention, and 30% experienced an ICD discharge. The total case cohort expanded from two prior cohorts11,12 (Figure S1). Cases included SCD (n = 129) events ascertained from a nationwide study.11 We included individuals with nonischemic VAs with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), or arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy because of arrhythmia risk.12 An additional 298 cases were recruited from cardiology clinics or referrals, and 36 cases were identified from NUgene biobank. Controls consisted of 596 individuals aged ≥40 years (median = 56, IQR, [48, 55]) with no known cardiac disease history (see STAR Methods). Controls did not undergo clinical cardiac screening, so they cannot be considered free of cardiac conditions. Aggregated polygenic scores distinguish cases from controls

After performing WGS, we computed 18 PGSs derived from GWASs for traits or diagnoses associated with SCD, either through mechanistic links to VA or through cardiac phenotypes. These include long QT syndrome (LQTS), atrial fibrillation (Afib), HCM, PR interval, corrected QT interval (QTc), QRS duration, heart rate (HR), HR variability, blood pressure (BP), left ventricular dimensions and volumes, and genetic generalized and focal epilepsies16,30,31,40,41,42,43,44,45,46 (Table S1). These PGSs were Z-normalized for common scale comparisons. Figure S2 outlines the PGS generation process. Table S1 provides variant effect sizes.

For each PGS, we computed case-control associations (Figure 1A; Table S1). The most significant difference was PGS for QRS duration. Cases had a genetically predicted wider QRS (p = 6e−9, Wilcoxon rank-sum test). QRS prolongation is a clinical predictor of SCD.47,48 BP PGS was also greater in cases (p = 6e−6, Wilcoxon rank-sum test), possibly reflecting PGS variants associated with vascular dysfunction or electrolyte imbalance. The PGS for PR prolongation was skewed toward cases, which may indicate conduction system disease genetic risk (p = 0.01, Wilcoxon rank-sum test). Genetically predicated left ventricle end systolic volume (LVESV) was higher in cases (p = 4e−3, Wilcoxon rank-sum test), (Figure 1A), consistent with the case cardiomyopathy subset. Genetically predicted higher left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was skewed toward controls (LVEF p = 9e−4, Wilcoxon rank-sum test). Figure 1A depicts PGS correlations. Some correlations (e.g., LVEF and LVESV) reflected trait relatedness. To quantify cumulative variation, we created a logistic regression using PGS for BP; HR; PR; QTc; QRS; left ventricle end diastolic volume, indexed for body surface area (LVEDVI); LVEF; LVESV; stroke volume, indexed for body surface area (SVI); stroke volume (SV); and Afib. Regression coefficients were then used to generate a composite PGS (Equation 4). The median composite PGS was greater in cases compared to controls (control median = −0.37, IQR [−0.80, 0.06]; case median = 0.11, IQR, [−0.35, 0.47]; p <e−10, Wilcoxon rank-sum test) (Figure 1B). The score showed predictive value for VA risk, with a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve area under the curve (AUC) 0.68 (Figure 1C). Aggregating scores into quintiles also showed an increasing case odds ratio (OR) at every quintile compared to the lowest quintile, with the highest carrying a 6.3 OR (95% confidence interval [CI], 4.7–8.5] (Figure 1D).

Enrichment of rare cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia, and epilepsy coding variants

A study on a nationwide cohort of young SCD cases (age <20 years) identified a contribution from cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia, and epilepsy rare variants.25 We collated 377 CMAR and epilepsy genes from testing panels11,25 and analyzed variation using our WGS data. Genes, expression data, genomic regions, and variant counts are in Tables S2 and S3.

Protein-disrupting variants in CMAR genes49 were enriched in cases (Figure 2A). Stop-gain 7.4 ± 1.2-fold (p < 1e−4, permutation test) and start-lost 7.5 ± 1.9-fold (p = 3e−4, permutation test) variants were highly enriched in cases. We identified a 4-fold enrichment of ClinVar-designated pathogenic/likely pathogenic (P/LP) CMAR variants (4.0 ± 0.3 enriched, p < 1e−4, permutation test) (Figure 2A). In the cases, 24.1% carried a CMAR P/LP variant, consistent with prior reports5,50,51,52,53 compared to 6.3% of the controls, representing an expected enrichment in cases relative to controls. This analysis includes heterozygous carriers of recessive cardiomyopathies. Variants in cardiac actionable genes (n = 59 genes, a subset of the 165 genes used here) were previously found in 2% of biobank individuals.54 Thus, this population prevalence is proportional to the number of genes investigated.55,56,57,58 In cases, we found the highest number of P/LP variants in MYBPC3 and MYH7, encoding myofilament proteins cardiac myosin-binding protein C (cMyBP-C) and β-myosin heavy chain (Figure S4A). CMAR genes were grouped into 14 functional modules to gain mechanistic insight (Table S4). ClinVar-designated P/LP sarcomere variants were overrepresented in cases (Figure S4C), as were P/LP variants in FLNC and BAG3, encoding proteostasis proteins.63,64,65 Cases also had P/LP variants affecting excitation-contraction, separated into membrane polarity or calcium-handling modules. P/LP variants were notably observed in the CALM1-3 genes,66 potassium channels (KCNH2, KCNJ2, KCNQ1),67 RYR2,68 and SCN5A62 (Figures S4A and S4C). Additionally, we found excess CMAR ultrarare missense variation, defined as gnomAD allele frequency <0.0001, 1.5 ± 0.2-fold enriched in cases (p = 1.2e−3, permutation test) (Figure 2A). Gene annotations are in Table S4. Because we identified many protein-disrupting CMAR gene variants, even in controls, we hypothesized that there would be an unequal distribution of those variants in modules. As with P/LP variants, sarcomere genes contained the highest enrichment (p = 5.6e−6, t test, Figure S5A), consistent with the cardiomyopathy contribution to the cases. We also found enrichment of protein-disrupting variants implicated in proteostasis, cytoskeletal membrane attachments, fate specification, and membrane polarity (p ≤ 1e−2 for each group, t test, Figures S5A–S5C).

Coding and PGS variants interact

We suspected that having protein-disrupting variation across multiple modules might confer excess risk due to combinatorial effects,9,10,69,70 so we counted co-occurrences of protein-disrupting variants in module-module pairs (e.g., sarcomere plus fate specification) (Figure 2B; Table S4). Co-occurring protein-disrupting variants in fate specification and nuclear envelopes were highly overrepresented in cases (9.7-fold, p = 0.020). Similarly, protein-disrupting variant co-occurrences in fate specification genes and either in the cytoskeletal membrane attachments or proteostasis genes were overrepresented (fate specification-cytoskeletal membrane attachments, 6.4-fold, p = 7.3e−4; fate-specification-proteostasis, 7.1-fold, p = 1.2e−4). Co-occurrence of variants in the fate specification-proteostasis-cytoskeletal membrane attachments network was the most overrepresented in cases (indicated with yellow arrows, Figure 2B), suggesting high-risk epistasis between modules. Furthermore, these modules showed interactions between the sarcomere, membrane polarity, and intercalated disc modules (P < 2e−3 each), suggesting they sensitize the heart to “second hits” (Figure 2B).

Protein-disrupting epilepsy variants were enriched in cases (1.13 ± 0.04-fold, p = 8.8e−3, permutation test). Stop-gain and start-lost variants were highly enriched in cases with 5.2 ± 1.1 stop-gain fold enriched (p < 1e−4, permutation test) and 7.1 ± 2.9 start-lost fold enriched (p = 0.05, permutation test). Ultrarare missense variants in epilepsy genes were also enriched (1.5 ± 0.2-fold, p = 3.6e−3, permutation test) (Figures 2A and S5A–S5C; Table S3).

We computed CMAR and epilepsy scores that combined rare, ultrarare, stop-gain/start-lost, and known pathogenic variants into one metric. The score is an additive contribution of each variant class, scaled by coefficients estimated with logistic regression (epilepsy: Equation 1; CMAR; Equation 2). Cases had higher scores than controls for both CMAR and epilepsy genes (CMAR genes p < 1e−10, epilepsy genes p < 1e−10, Wilcoxon rank-sum test) (Figure 2C). Splitting the CMAR and epilepsy scores by quintile, we found OR increases in the upper quintiles, with highest ORs in the top CMAR score quintile (CMAR quintile 5 vs. quintile 1: OR = 7.4 [95% CI = 5.4–10.0]) (Figure 2D).

Some healthy control individuals had high CMAR scores, consistent with known incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity of CMAR P/LP variants71,72,73,74,75,76 and potentially influenced by total variant load. To visualize the interaction of the CMAR variants with the PGS variants, we plotted the CMAR score rank against the composite PGS rank and split each distribution in half, defining quadrants. Controls were overrepresented in the low composite PGS/low CMAR quadrant (Q1). There was a modest overrepresentation of controls in the low CMAR, high composite PGS quadrant (Q2), but there was a dramatic overrepresentation of cases in the high CMAR, high composite PGS quadrant (Q4) (chi-squared p < 1e−10) (Figure 2E). This contribution of common and rare alleles most effectively separates cases from controls.

To further explore this interaction hypothesis, we selected the two highest scoring CMAR quintiles and plotted the CMAR score relative to the epilepsy score, and separately, relative to the composite PGS in Figure 2F. Controls showed a negative correlation between CMAR score and either the epilepsy score or the composite PGS compared to the cases, for which the slopes were positive or neutral (epilepsy-CMAR, p = 3.0e−10; composite polygenic-CMAR, p = 0.09, linear model t test). Both the epilepsy scores and the composite PGSs were lower in the controls than the cases in this high CMAR subset (epilepsy score, p = 4.8e−10; composite PGS, p = 1.1e−9, Wilcoxon rank-sum test) (Figure 2F). Therefore, individuals carrying high CMAR load appear at most risk when also carrying high PGS or epilepsy variant load.

The pink arrow in Figure 2F highlights a control individual >64 years old with no known cardiac disease who carries a P/LP HCM variant, MYBPC3 (c.1223 + 2T>C), but who scored in the lowest quintile for both composite PGS and epilepsy variant score. In this case, the lower risk genomic context may contribute to a more benign outcome.

Burden of ultrarare regulatory interval variants

Noncoding regulatory DNA is subject to selective pressure77,78,79 and contributes to disease risk.20,21,80,81,82 Putative regulatory elements can be determined by integrating active chromatin and 3D genome data.18,83,84 This approach has been employed across neurodevelopmental, immunological, and other conditions.85,86,87 Because arrhythmias have both cardiac and epilepsy genetic architectures, we selected genome coordinates containing active chromatin data from the heart88 and brain,89 which we mapped to genes using topology data from the human heart,90 human induced pluripotent stem cell cardiomyocytes (iPSC-CMs),83 and human brain90 (Figure 3A). We filtered for elements exclusively mapping to the 377 CMAR and epilepsy panel genes for regulatory variant analysis, amounting to 4,846,748 bp, with 396 bp per regulatory element, and each gene having an average of 24 regulatory elements at a 176-kb median distance to target gene start (Figures S6A–S6C; Table S5). We analyzed regulatory variants similar to coding variation. Rare variants were defined as gnomAD allele frequency <0.001, and ultrarare variants were not observed in gnomAD and had a cohort allele frequency <0.01. Ultrarare variants that mapped to either CMAR or epilepsy genes were overrepresented in cases (1.8 ± 0.2-fold, p < 1e−4, permutation test; 1.7 ± 0.1-fold enrichment for epilepsy gene regulatory variants, p < 1e−4, permutation test) (Figure 3B; Table S5). This ultrarare regulatory variant inflation corresponds to a previous report showing that 92% of noncoding variants overrepresented in cardiomyopathy were singletons18 and singletons underlie strongest gene expression changes.79 To control for nonspecificity, we selected 1,000 random genomic intervals matched to total nucleotide length of the regulatory regions for the 377 genes. We then computed the ratio of ultrarare variants across these sets in cases compared to controls. None of the intervals had a ratio larger than the CMAR 1.8 and only 2/1,000 were larger than the epilepsy 1.7, corresponding to significant empirical p values of 5e−4 and 2e−3, respectively.

Regulatory variant examples are shown in Figure 3C. Among the genes highlighted are LMNA and CALM1, which are dosage sensitive (probability of loss of function intolerance >0.9) and associated with arrhythmia.66,94,95 Motif enrichment analysis centered on ultrarare case variants revealed an enrichment for developmental, fibrosis, and inflammation transcription factor-binding sites (Figure 3D). A top disrupted motif was for estrogen-related receptor alpha, which was reported to confer ventricular cardiomyocyte identity.96 Figure 3E illustrates examples of six ultrarare variants mapping to arrhythmia-related target genes (shown as Hi-C tracks), where each variant disrupts a transcription factor-binding site within open chromatin in human cardiomyocytes. Genomic regions are detailed in Table S5. We independently validated these regions using assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq) generated from engineered human heart tissues. We also evaluated putative regulatory regions against Vista Enhancer Browser enhancers. Examples containing rare regulatory variants found in cases are shown in Figure S6D, including enhancers mapping to CMAR panel genes (LDB3, GATA4, and GATA6) or both CMAR and epilepsy panel genes (KCNH2 and KCNQ3). We selected the LDB3 enhancer variant, rs528621942, and tested its effect on genes using a luciferase reporter introduced into hIPSC-CMs. rs528621942 exhibited a 7-fold increase in activity compared to reference. These data demonstrate potential ultrarare noncoding variant function.

We computed a regulatory variant score as a weighted sum of ultrarare and rare variants (see STAR Methods and Equation 3), resulting in cases again showing a higher score compared to controls (p = 5.2e−6, Wilcoxon rank-sum test) (Figure 3F). This score held independent predictive value, showing an OR of 2.4 (95% CI = 1.8–3.2) at the highest quintile when compared to the lowest, similar to de novo regulatory variation in CHD19 (Figure 3G).

A genomic arrhythmia propensity score stratifies risk

We next asked whether including each variant type in a genomic risk score would prove informative. A similar approach was described for autism spectrum disorder.97 We incorporated the PGS, the coding, and the regulatory variants into one combined genomic arrhythmia propensity score (GAPS, Equation 7) (Figure 4A), which showed higher scores in the cases (p < 1e−10, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, control median = −0.65, IQR, [−1.3, −0.1]; case median = 0.26, IQR, [−0.4, 1.1]). In the discovery cohort, 96% of individuals with GAPS >1 SD above the mean were cases. We also created variations, iteratively adding subsets of the variant classes. Sensitivity/specificity analysis showed improvement adding each variant class (DeLong’s test), with the GAPS model showing an AUC of 0.76 (Figure 4B). The variance attributable to each variant class showed that common variants (PGSs) explained the bulk of the deviation (42.5%), followed by the CMAR (31.1%) and regulatory variants (16.3%). Compared to the coding variant class on its own (Equation 5), the ORs above the first quintile improved by combining the common/GWAS variants with the coding variants (Equation 6) (p ≤ 0.05 each, Z tests). The direction of effect was improved at every quintile by adding noncoding variants (Equation 7). Most VA cases did not harbor P/LP CMAR variants (75.9%, n = 346/456). GAPS was constructed to address this challenge by accounting for high-effect variants that are unannotated as P/LP by incorporating low-frequency and predicted loss-of-function variants. We assessed the performance of GAPS in these 346 “P/LP-negative” cases by converting GAPS values to a probability of being a case (p(case), Equation 8). Setting a probability threshold for disease at >70% (p(case) > 0.7), there were 67 individuals who were also “P/LP-negative”; 60 of those were cases, indicating an accuracy of 89.6%. When increasing the probability threshold to >83% (p(case) > 0.83), the remaining 22 P/LP-negative individuals were exclusively cases (100% accuracy), demonstrating discrimination between cases and controls when removing P/LP carriers.

Validation with an additional cohort, reverse, and cross-validation of GAPS

A subset cohort was set aside for replication prior to beginning this analysis (67 cases, 59 controls). Applying the models derived from the original cohort showed the same direction of effect for each variant class, with coding variants and regulatory variants statistically different between cases and controls (CMAR variants p = 3.4e−8; epilepsy gene variants p = 7.0e−4; regulatory variants p < 1e−10; Wilcoxon rank-sum tests) (Figure 5A). BP, HR, LVEDVI, LVESV, QTc, SV, SVI, QRS width, and LVEF PGS were in the same direction as discovery, but only LVEF and QTc were significant (p < 0.05, Wilcoxon rank-sum test). The final GAPS model was higher in cases (p < e−10, Wilcoxon rank-sum test) (Figure 5A) and replicated its discriminatory ability (AUC = 0.87) (Figure 5B). Like the discovery cohort, GAPS outperformed any variant class (Figure 5C), reinforcing the composite approach. The CMAR variant class was the second-best predictor, suggesting a cardiomyopathy enrichment. However, after removing individuals with CMAR P/LP variants and setting a GAPS probability threshold >70% (Equation 8), all 22 individuals were cases, again showing the ability to select high-risk individuals missed using contemporary guidelines. We adjusted GAPS for ancestry by subtracting the effect of 10 ancestry PCs (Equation 10) determined by a regression between the PCs and GAPS (Equation 9). This adjustment reduced the AUC from 0.76 to 0.68, and replication AUC also diminished from 0.91 to 0.79 (Figure 6C, post-integration values). It is unknown whether ancestry influences are biological or due to Eurocentric databases. Cases and controls within each ancestry cluster differed in unadjusted GAPS (Figure 6D) (p < 0.01 Wilcoxon rank-sum tests), and even with adjustment, differences between cases and controls were still evident within each ancestry cluster, indicating that GAPS can be applied across ancestries (Figure 6D) (p < 0.02 each, Wilcoxon rank-sum test). The highest quintile OR was reduced but still significant compared to unadjusted GAPS (quintile 5 vs. quintile 1: original OR = 26.1, 95% CI [17.8–38.3]; adjusted OR = 6.4, 95% CI [4.6–8.9]) (Figure 6E). This same trend was observed within each ancestry cluster. After adjustment, the highest quintile adjusted scores for each definitive cluster displayed positive predictive ORs (≥2.1), with the unadjusted scores each ≥4.1 (Figure 6E; Table S6).

As a complementary ancestry consideration, we corrected for PCs within each variant class prior to integrating into GAPS. This pre-integration correction improved GAPS performance compared to post-integration, with discovery AUC 0.76 and replication 0.87 (Figure 6C). This method stabilizes differences between cases and controls across ancestries, with p < 0.001 for each cluster (Figure 6D). Furthermore, the OR in the top quintile for pre-integration correction is statistically indistinguishable from uncorrected GAPS, 21.8 (95% CI, 14.7–32.3), outperforming the post-integration correction (Figure 6E).

Discussion

An additive liability model

Genetic disease prognostication is complicated by variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance. Conditions like HCM and LQTS have penetrant monogenic P/LP variants that carry high risk for first-degree family members,99,100 which may show reduced penetrance in populations lacking shared genetic background.75,101,102 Comprehensive models that incorporate different types of genetic data are positioned to improve genetic risk prediction,34 and it is becoming clear that polygenic background modifies penetrance and expressivity.103 Related findings have been reported in other conditions, such as HCM16 and intellectual disability.104 It was also reported that severe neurodevelopmental conditions are influenced by both common and rare variants.35,36 We found that each component of genetic information added to the overall risk. Rather than binary classifications, such as “genotype positive or negative,” GAPS contextualizes risk probabilistically in an additive model.

Common variants and clinical correlates

We chose PGS for traits or diagnoses associated with SCD and cardiac function, hypothesizing that the underlying genomic architecture would contribute to arrhythmia. Although there are no VA-specific PGSs, some show predictive value. Genetic risk for QRS prolongation is consistent with QRS prolongation itself being a predictor of SCD.48 Indeed, genetic signals for QRS may predict arrhythmia in individuals without known cardiac disease.105 Genetically predicted longer QTc was also higher in cases. While longer QT increases VA risk, short QT has also been linked to VA.106 Genetically predicted LVEF was higher in the controls, reflecting the inclusion of cardiomyopathy. Reduced left ventricular function is a guideline for implantable cardiac defibrillator placement. Therefore, genomic risk profiling shows promise for medical decision-making.

Insights from rare variation

Interaction analysis showed that having multiple variants in broad sensitization pathways (proteostasis, fate specification, and cytoskeletal membrane attachments)107 is consistent with omnigenic inheritance that emphasizes “peripheral master regulators.”108 These trans effects have been noted in type 1 diabetes109 and may underlie the modifying effect of NKX2.5 p.A119S in oligogenic CHD.10

Variants that alter regulatory function contribute to disease,110,111 including CHD19 and cardiomyopathy.18,112 We identified an inflation of ultrarare noncoding variants linked to genes implicated in cardiomyopathies, arrhythmias, and epilepsy, consistent with constraint metrics in noncoding sequences.113 Variation within these regions can increase or decrease transcription factor binding,114 and enhancer loss- and gain-of-function variants can contribute to disease along the spectrum of gene copy number,96,115 in accordance with gene dosage.116

Practical implications

Useful clinical arrhythmia risk predictors exist.48,117,118 Since GAPS relies on genotype, these approaches could be combined. Current decisioning for ICD placement revolves around clinical data, imaging criteria, and symptom history. Genomic information is positioned to become an additional tool that contributes to risk prediction. VA has considerable heterogeneity, reflecting a range of underlying cellular pathologies. Using GAPS, it may be possible to deconvolve the specific genes or pathways to better understand an individual’s genetic risk. For example, if GAPS suggests a liability from sarcomeric compared to calcium handling genetic variation, this information could be useful for guiding treatments.119

PGSs have recently been tested for return to patients for 10 conditions.41 These PGSs typically have ORs <10 at thresholds in the top 2%–10%, which is conservative compared to GAPS. Implementing a GAPS does not require deep WGS because panel sequencing can be expanded to include noncoding genomic intervals (∼5,000 kb) and GWAS variants can be determined using specific arrays or low coverage sequencing with imputation. Comprehensive genetic risk evaluations like GAPS could apply to many medically relevant complex traits.

Limitations of the study

Assembling large cohorts with accurate clinical information of rare fatal and near-fatal arrhythmias is challenging, so the cases assembled here are relatively few. Additional replication cohorts are warranted. Databases such as All of Us have many participants but lack primary data for appropriate arrhythmia adjudication. The cohort here was extensively manually phenotyped to confirm it is a representative sampling of VA cases. VA appears to be strongly enriched for genotype outliers harboring an unusually high burden of variation predicted to negatively impact cardiac function. The case cohort is young, representing an extreme phenotype, as SCD is estimated to occur in 1/100,000 individuals under age 40 years. This condition may contrast with VA risk in older individuals, and whether GAPS will perform similarly across older arrhythmia populations is unknown. Future GAPS iterations can incorporate additional GWAS data. Additionally, metabolic arrhythmia risk may be underestimated, as mitochondrial variants were not included.

Experimental model and study participant details

Ethics

This work complies with all relevant ethical regulations as approved by the institutional review boards of Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine, the Ann and Robert H. Lurie Children’s Hospital, Vanderbilt University or the University of Utah. Informed consent was obtained from all living individuals, their parents/guardians, or next-of-kin (for decedents).

Cohort

The case cohort (N = 523, with 456 in the discovery cohort, 67 in the replication cohort) was derived from previously recruited cases as well as newly enrolled individuals (see Consort Diagram Figure S1). The Northwestern Sudden Death Cohort (NSDC) (n = 103) was previously described.11 The NSDC gathered pre-mortem medical records, autopsy findings/materials, and DNA from decedents (age ≥1year and <45 years, mean age 26.8) who suffered sudden cardiac death. The NSDC cases were reported by coroners and medical examiners across 60 counties from 24 states. A second previously studied contributing cohort of 86 cases included those living with arrhythmias and nonischemic cardiomyopathy (HCM; DCM; or arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy, AC).12 We newly recruited participants (n = 298) between 2016 and 2022 from the Bluhm Cardiovascular Institute at Northwestern Medicine (n = 198) and the Ann and Robert H. Lurie Children’s Hospital Cardiology clinic (n = 38). Participants were also recruited through Electronic Data Warehouse (EDW) searches of medical record information at Northwestern Medicine (n = 29) based on the inclusion/exclusion of ICD 9/10 and CPT codes in Table S7. Network collaborators referred an additional 16 cases, and 17 additional cases were newly added from medical examiners meeting NSDC criteria. Lastly, the NUgene biobank was identified as having 36 cases meeting eligibility criteria.

Cases were adjudicated based on clinical review of diagnostic codes, cardiac imaging, electrocardiogram data and additional medical records as available, jointly by two cardiologists, a genetic counselor, and study staff. Those meeting initial inclusion criteria (n = 344) were approached for participation which included providing a DNA sample. Forty-six were excluded with 29 not meeting inclusion criteria after group review, 5 declined consent, and no sample could be obtained for 12 individuals. Inclusion criteria for the case cohort were: age <50 at first presentation; documented VA or high risk cardiomyopathy criteria; ICD placement for prior history of documented VA (secondary prevention ICD placement for sustained ventricular tachycardia or ventricular fibrillation, or adjudicated cardiac arrest) or primary prevention ICD placement, or having an ICD with appropriate discharge for VAs. Individuals with a presenting diagnosis of HCM, DCM, AC, Long QT, Brugada or catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia were included and high risk patients were adjudicated by the clinical review committee, using current guidelines133,134,135,136,137,138,139,140,141 for disease-specific adjudication. Individuals with significant epicardial coronary artery disease were excluded from all cohorts. At the beginning of analysis, the discovery cohort was frozen, and additional cases were set aside to create a replication cohort.

The control cohort (N = 596, with 537 in the discovery cohort, and 59 in the replication cohort), derived from the NUgene Project, the medical biobank that is a partnership between Northwestern University and Northwestern Medicine (NM). Controls were selected based on having agreed to whole genome sequencing study,54 having attained age >40 without known cardiac diagnoses in EMR based on the absence of the diagnostic codes found in Table S7. Controls did not undergo cardiac testing as part of the study and may have subclinical or undiagnosed disease. For the replication cohort all ICD-9/10 codes were maintained, but age for inclusion in the control cohort was lowered to 35 years without a known cardiac diagnosis. Detailed information regarding age, genetic sex information, and self-reported race is included in Table 1. Details of genetic ancestry assessment are listed below under “Ancestry Analysis”.

Cell culture

GFP-expressing hIPSC line was purchased from Coriell (Parental line GM25256, GFP knock-in to AAVS1 safe harbor locus, Coriell cat# AICS-0036-028). hIPSCs were maintained in mTeSR-1 (Stem Cell technologies, Cat# 85850) on Matrigel-coated 6-well plates (Corning cat# 354230). Ventricular hiPSC-cardiomyocytes (hIPSC-CMs) were generated by standard methods using small molecule modulation WNT signaling.142 When hIPSCs reached ∼90% confluency, differentiation was initiated using 24 h 6mM CHIR99021 (Tocris cat# 5148) in CDM3 (RPMI, Cat#11875-119 1640 with L-glutamine, 213 mg/mL L-ascorbic acid 2-phosphate, 500mg/mL recombinant human albumin). After 24 h recovery in CDM3, cells were then treated with 2μM Wnt-C59 (Tocris cat# 5148) in CDM3 for 48 h. CDM3 was subsequently changed every two days until cells matured into beating monolayers. On day 10 of differentiation, beating cardiomyocytes were dissociated to single cells using 200units/mL collagenase IV (Worthington Biochemical Corporation cat# LS004188) in Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS cat# 14025092, Thermo with 10mM HEPES, 2μM Thiazovivin (TZV) (STEMCELL Technologies cat# 72254) and 30 μM N-Benzyl-p-Toluenesulfonamide (BTS) (TCI cat# B3082) for 2 h at 37°C, enriched using a MACS-based negative selection kit (Miltenyi cat#130-110-188). GFP-expressing hiPSC-CMs were combined with nonfluorescent human cardiac fibroblasts (Promocell cat#C-12375, lot#463Z007.1) in a 9:1 ratio for a total of 0.55 × 106 total cells per EHT as described.143 Cell mixture was suspended in RPMI with B-27 supplement (Thermo cat# 17504044) and combined over ice with 44 μL collagen type I (6.5 mg/mL, Sigma cat# 804614), 44 μL 2x RMPI (Thermo cat# 318000022) and 6 μL 0.1 N NaOH for a total of 198 μL per EHT. The resultant hydrogel mixture (180 μL) was pipetted evenly into well from of a 48 EHT mutli-well plate (Myriamed cat# myrPlate TM5 med). Plates were incubated at 37°C for 1 h and then supplemented with EHT media containing Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) low glucose (Sigma D5546), 10% horse serum (heat inactivated, New Zealand origin, Gibco, 26050088), 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin (Thermo, 15070063), and 0.1% human insulin (10 mg/mL, Sigma 19278). For the first 72 h following EHT generation, media is supplemented with 5 μg/mL recombinant human TGF-β1 (CHO derived, Preprotech cat# 100-21C). Following day 3 after tissue casting, TGF-β1 was removed from culture media and 500μL per well of EHT media was exchanged every 2 days for tissue maturation.

Method details

Whole genome DNA sequencing and data processing

QiAmp DNA Blood Mini Kits (Qiagen No. 51106) or Qiagen DNAeasy kits (Qiagen No. 69504) were used for genomic DNA isolation from blood collected using ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) vacutainers (BD Bioscience No. 367862). WGS was performed on PCR-free libraries using an Illumina HiSeq XTen machine yielding an average of >100GB of data per sample. Sequences were checked for quality using DRAGEN fastQC (version 0.11.9) and aligned to GRCh37/hg19 using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA).122 Variants were called using the Genome Analysis Tool Kit (GATK v.3.4.0)123 and the MegaSeq pipeline.124 Base pair resolution calls were performed for all positions using Swift/T125 as workflow manager. Calls were first made in GATK to gvcf files. Error correction included reference calls to account for depth and allele frequencies, and joint calling was performed.

For GWAS/PRS variants, joint calling was performed on specific variant coordinates, with ±10 base pairs padding around the site. A Genotype Quality ≥10 was considered acceptable for common variants in PRS calls, otherwise it was set to “./.”. Chromosome Y and mitochondria genotypes were not included in this analysis.

For coding intervals, gene panels used in clinical genetic testing for cardiomyopathy and arrhythmias and epilepsy were previously collated to produce a list of 377 genes.5,25 Joint calling was performed across all intervals including the UTRs and intronic regions. These regions were retrieved using the UCSC-annotated coordinates in the R package TxDb.Hsapiens.UCSC.hg19.knownGene (version 3.2.2) using GenBank accession numbers from org.Hs.e.g.,.db (version 3.14.0). Expression of these 377 genes was queried using GTEx data for heart muscle and cerebral cortex and separately, single nucleus RNA-seq human heart from non-diseased individuals.144

Coding region rare variant annotation and analysis

Coding interval variants were annotated using Variant Effect Predictor (VEP, version 110),49 to include effect, gnomAD [2.1, hg19] allele frequencies,126 cohort allele frequencies, spliceAI information,127 and ClinVar pathogenicity.128 Regions analyzed include the exons, from 17 bp upstream each exon to 8bp beyond each exon to account for splice donor or acceptor disruptions. The ratios of variants were calculated by taking the number of variants per person across the case and controls, calculating the mean and standard error, and then scaling by the mean of the control group. CMAR panel genes were placed into functional modules by expert classification prior to analysis. Pairwise module interactions were determined by first annotating each module for the presence of a VEP “High” effect variant, known as protein disrupting variants which are defined as “probably causing protein truncation, loss of function or triggering nonsense mediated decay.” Individuals were included if they carried high effect gene variants in genes in more than one module (module pairs), and the module pair was overrepresented compared to the expected number of occurrences in the case cohort using the Fisher exact test, followed with a Bonferroni correction for the number of module pairs tested. Protein cartoons were generated using the R package drawProteins by drawProteinsget_features(“… UNIPROT ID …”) and draw_canvas, draw_chains, draw_domains, draw_regions, and variants were overlaid using ggplot, geom_point, with the variant amino acid position used.

Regulatory variant calling

Regulatory variants for the 377 genes were selected based on the following. The genomic coordinate (bed) files for regulatory coordinates were selected by first retrieving the ATAC-seq intervals provided for heart88 and brain.89 Promoter-capture Hi-C data were retrieved as excel coordinate files from heart (LV, RV, RA, Aorta90; and iPSC-cardiomyocytes83 and brain90 (Dorsolateral Prefrontal Cortex, Hippocampus, Neural Progenitor Cell) and combined in excel. The ATAC-seq intervals (bed files) were combined, filtered for mapping to the non-mitochondrial panel genes (bedtools merge -i.bed) and intersected with the Hi-C coordinates, including only overlapping ATAC and Hi-C coordinates (bedtools intersect).129 Duplicates were removed (bedtools sort -i, bedtools merge -i). Final coordinates are available in Table S5. A set of 1000 genomic intervals was randomly selected to match the total nucleotide length of the regulatory regions to represent a null distribution unexpected to contribute to disease. Regions sent for joint-calling were adjusted to remove intersections with previously called coding regions. The intervals were annotated using VEP to include effect, gnomAD [2.1, hg19] gnomAD allele frequencies and cohort allele frequencies. The ratios of variants were calculated by taking the number per person across the case and controls, calculating the mean and standard error, and then scaling by the mean of the control group. Inflation of ultrarare variants mapping to genes of interest was determined by counting the number of ultrarare variants mapped to each gene in the cases and controls and comparing the actual counts to expected counts assuming equal distribution of the variants.

For visualization, ATAC-seq gene tracks were retrieved from heart88 (“Ventricular Cardiomyocyte,” http://catlas.org/humanenhancer/#!/igv) and brain89 [“DLPFC neuronal,” https://labs.icahn.mssm.edu/roussos-lab/boca/), and from ATAC-seq data as generated in our laboratory (see below). Probability of being loss-of-function intolerant (pLI) data were retrieved from gnomAD. Motif analysis was performed using HOMER findMotifsGenome.pl130 with 20bp windows centered on the variants of interest.

Assay for transposase accessible chromatin

EHTs were digested in 50μL digestion buffer per EHT at 8 EHTs per tube [digestion buffer: 200units/mL collagenase (Worthington Biochemical Corporation cat# LS004188); 10mM HEPES; 2μM Thiazovivin (Tocris cat# 3845); 30μM N-Benzyl-p-toluenesulfonamide (MedChemExpress cat# HY-16690)]. The tubes were then placed at 37oC with 225 RPM shaking. After 30 min, EHTs were then gently triturated in the tubes and placed back in the incubator with shaking and triturating every 15 min until they were dissociated (∼90 min total). Next, EHT media, (560 μL/EHT) was added and the digested cells, which were filtered through a 100μm filter and combined. The filtered cell suspension was centrifuged at 300G, washed with 25mL EHT media, centrifuged again at 300G, and finally resuspended in 1mL 2% BSA and sent for flow sorting, separating GFP-fibroblasts from the GFP+ iPSC-CMs. The sorted cells were washed again with 10mL EHT media and resuspended in 1mL 2%BSA. The cells were counted and separated into aliquots of 50,000 which were used for assay for transposase accessible chromatin with high throughout sequencing (ATAC-seq). The ATAC-seq libraries were generated using Fast-ATAC, as previously described.145 The libraries were amplified 10 times after indexing with Nextera PCR Primers (Illumina cat# FC-131) and purified using Agencourt AMPure XP (Beckman Coulter cat # A63881). Next, Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies cat# DNF-467-0500) analysis was performed to ensure the appropriate fragment size distribution. Paired-end 2 × 75 bp sequencing was performed using an Illumina NovaSeq6000. Reads were mapped to the human genome (hg38) by using Bowtie2.131 Non-nuclear reads and improperly paired reads were discarded. Duplicated reads were removed with Picard-MarkDuplicates.

Enhancer cloning for luciferase assay

Putative enhancer sequences, including both wild-type and variant alleles (∼1 kb total, spanning 500 bp on either side of the variant), were synthesized as gene fragments (gBlocks; Twist Bioscience). Each sequence was flanked at the 5′ end with the overhang 5′-CACCATCGGTAAATAGCCTCGCGGGGTACC- and at the 3′ end with 5′-CTCGAGGCGGAGCACTCTGGTTATG-3′ to facilitate directional TOPO cloning and provide universal priming sites. The gBlocks were cloned into the pENTR/D-TOPO vector (Thermo Fisher Scientific cat# 450641) using the manufacturer’s recommended protocol in a 6-μL reaction volume. After 30 min of incubation at room temperature, the reaction was transformed into chemically competent E. coli TOP10 cells and selected on kanamycin-containing media. Positive clones were confirmed via Sanger sequencing. Inserts were verified by Sanger sequencing.

Entry clones containing confirmed enhancer sequences were recombined with the Gateway-compatible luciferase reporter vector pGL4.23-GW (Addgene plasmid #60323)121 via LR Clonase II enzyme mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific cat# 11791020). LR reactions contained 150 ng each of entry clone and destination vector in a 10 μL reaction with 2 μL LR Clonase II enzyme mix. Reactions were incubated overnight at 25°C, terminated with 1 μL Proteinase K (37°C, 10 min), and transformed into TOP10 cells with selection on carbenicillin-containing media. Final constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing.

Luciferase assays

At day 10 of differentiation, human induced pluripotent cardiomyocytes (hIPSC-CMs, previously reprogrammed from a healthy male, Coriell GM0334884) were dissociated, enriched using a MACS-based negative selection kit (Miltenyi cat#130-110-188), and replated onto white clear-bottom 96-well plates at a density of 40,000 cells per well. The culture medium was changed every 48 h, and synchronous beating was observed on days 14–16. On day 18, hiPSC-CMs were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific cat# L3000001) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Wells received 0.2 μL of either putative enhancer or wildtype construct (0.15 μM), along with 5 ng of pRL-SV40 Renilla luciferase plasmid (Promega cat# E2920), 0.15 μL of Lipofectamine 3000 reagent, and 0.2 μL of P3000 reagent, in 10 μL of Opti-MEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific cat# 31985062).

Luciferase activity was measured 48 h post-transfection using the Dual-Glo Luciferase Assay System (Promega cat# E2920). Firefly luciferase signals were normalized to Renilla luciferase activity in the same well to control for transfection efficiency. Enhancer activity was calculated relative to a 500 bp genomic desert region (negative control), with the MYH7 genomic region as the positive control. Each enhancer construct was tested in 8 replicate wells across three independent cardiomyocyte differentiations.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Polygenic score calculations

Genome wide association studies (GWAS) were chosen for polygenic analysis based on relevance to arrhythmia-related conditions and cardiac morphometric analyses. If variant information was provided in the published GWAS, the information was used as stated (Table S1). For variants lacking information, we retrieved summary statistics from the indicated GWAS and chose variants to include after filtering for linkage disequilibrium (LD R2 < 0.01 to ensure that variants were independent and not in high LD with ancestry-associated regions) and significance, aiming for a minimum of 20 variants, if possible, from each study. Only variants with an rsID that were present in gnomAD (3.1.2, hg38 filtering applied after liftover) were used. Duplicates were collapsed (by coordinate or perfect LD). Variants were mapped to hg19 if necessary (liftover), and all effect sizes were set to be positive (i.e., by selecting a “risk allele”). Multi-allelic SNPs were treated as “effect” alleles. The decision tree (Figure S2) and full variant tables are provided (Table S1). Variants chosen for each PRS were included in standard linear models that scale the variant by the effect size. Scores were Z normalized (R Version 4.0, scale()) for cross-comparisons. Scores that primarily reflected ancestry, rather than risk, were not used to minimize confounding.

If no PGS variants were provided with a GWAS, filtering starting from summary statistics tables was performed for the following traits. Heart rate44: variants MAF ≥0.01 and imputation info >0.8 were included. Underperforming variants were removed. Variants appearing multiple times were included only once, leaving 737 SNPs meeting genome-wide significant at P < 5e−8. Setting LD Rˆ2 < 0.01 variants were aggregated using plink2 (https://www.cog-genomics.org/plink/2.0/) to leave 603 variants. Heart rate variability (HRV),45 variants were selected with MAF ≥0.01 and P < 5e−8 producing 230 genome-wide significant common variants with clear LD structure. Setting LD Rˆ2 < 0.01 and P<e−5 left 26 variants that were lifted over to hg19. Blood Pressure (BP): Variants from46 were with p values were used (n = 885). QRS interval (QRS): From the provided summary statistics,43 variants from chr X, Y and NA were removed, followed by filtering for imputation >0.8 and MAF ≥0.01. After aggregating at Rˆ2 < 0.01 with P < 5e−08 and removing variants missing from gnomAD, 41 variants remained. Genetic generalized epilepsy (GGE): Variants from120 with AF >1 or <0, NA effect size or effect size error were removed, as well as chr Y and X. After filtering for AF ≥0.01, variants were aggregated setting P < 5e−6 and Rˆ2 < 0.01; we included 7 SNPs not on the LD panel and >50kb from the nearest other variant for a total of 113 variants. Focal Epilepsy: Using the same dataset, variants with AF >1 or <0, NA effect size or effect size error were removed, as well as chr Y and X. After filtering for AF ≥0.01, we aggregated the variants setting p < 5e−6 and Rˆ2 < 0.01 and included the 1 SNP not on the LD panel and >50kb from the nearest other variant, leaving 32 variants.

Ancestry analysis. Principal components (PC) of genotype were computed as described.54,146 Variants were removed if they were present in <98% of the cohort, multi-allelic, or an indels. Variants with AF >0.05 were retained, leaving a total of approximately 7 million variants per individual. PCs were computed after applying a standard sliding window of 50 SNPs, shifting the window by 5 SNPs at a time, and removing one of a pair of SNPs if the rˆ2 value between them was >0.2. The first two PCs were used to infer ancestral structure. Clusters were called using two rounds of unbiased Gaussian Mixture Modeling within the mclust package in R to reach definitive ancestry clusters. To determine the optimal number of PCs for ancestry correction, we evaluated models with 1–20 PCs using 10-fold cross-validation, calculating both the proportion of variance in GAPS explained by the PCs (R2) and the root-mean-square error (RMSE). The optimal number of PCs was defined as the point where R2 plateaued (no additional predictive power) and RMSE began to increase (indicating overfitting). Based on this analysis, 10 PCs were selected. The top 10 PCs were regressed against control group scores in Equation 9. The resulting coefficients from Equation 9 were then used to subtract the influence of the first 10 PC on GAPS from individuals within the entire cohort, as in Equation 10, applicable to Figures 6B–6E. For pre-integration correction, each variant class score (common, regulatory, coding, etc.) was adjusted separately by regressing the first 10 PCs against that class score to obtain the ancestry coefficients, which were then subtracted before summing the classes in the final GAPS model. Statistical analysis

p values are reported after adjusting for multiple comparisons. All statistics were computed in R (4.0). Specific statistical tests are indicated in the text, figure legends, and provided R markdown file.

Correlations (Figure 1A) were computed using the Hmisc package, and plotted using the cor() function in base R.

Composite score comparisons between cases and controls (Figures 1A, 1B, 2C, 3F, 4A, 5A–5F, 6D, and S3): If the assumptions of normality (Shapiro-Wilk test), independence, and homoscedasticity (Bartlett’s test) were met, ANOVA was used for multiple group comparisons, followed by post-hoc Tukey tests for pairwise analyses. If only two groups were compared, Student’s t-test was applied. When unequal variances were present between two groups, Welch’s t-test was applied. If the assumptions of normality (Shapiro-Wilk test), independence, and homoscedasticity (Bartlett’s test) were met, nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis tests with Dunn post-hoc analysis (using the dunn.test package) were employed for three or more groups, with a Bonferroni correction applied to adjust for multiple comparisons. For comparisons between two groups under nonparametric conditions, Wilcoxon Rank-Sum tests were used. Luciferase data (Figure S6D) was calculated similarly. Unless exact values are provided in the figure, ∗P ≤ 5e−2, ∗∗P < 1e−2, ∗∗∗P < 1e−3, ∗∗∗∗P < 1e−4.

Variant count data Figure 2A. Figure 3B: To test whether the variant counts differed between cases and controls, we performed a permutation test, which does not make assumption about specific distributions of the data, making it refractory to zero-inflated variant count data. For each variant class, we calculated a difference in means between cases and controls as the test statistic. A null distribution was created by randomly shuffling the counts and assigning to group labels (case or control) 10,000 times. A p-value was then generated by calculating the proportion of simulated datasets where the test statistic was equal or more extreme than the observed test statistic. For these results, ∗P ≤ 5e−2, ∗∗P < 1e−2, ∗∗∗P < 1e−3, ∗∗∗∗P < 1e−4.

Contingency table data (Figure 2E): Fisher’s exact test (base R) was performed, with Bonferroni adjustments for multiple comparisons when necessary.

Odds ratios (Figures 1D, 2D, 3G, and 4C): These were compared using z-tests in base R. For these, # indicates an odds ratio 95% CI lower bound ≥1.

Predictive value assessment (Figures 1C, 4B, 5B, and 6C): Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) and the area under the curve (AUC) were computed using the pROC package.132 Comparisons between AUCs (Figures 4B and 5C) were completed using the conservative DeLong’s test.
