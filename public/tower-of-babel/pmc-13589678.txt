Clin‐STAR Corner: Practice Changing Advances at the Interface of Artificial Intelligence/Machine Learning and Geriatrics
Authors: Tue T Te, Alex A T Bui, William Speier, Constance H Fung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTArtificial intelligence (AI) methods, including machine learning (ML), are transforming healthcare by enabling personalized interventions that integrate multimodal data to support rehabilitation, preventive care, and remote monitoring. Despite their broad potential, older adults remain underrepresented in model development, raising concerns about bias and limited generalizability. As AI/ML adoption expands, it is essential to critically appraise emerging tools to ensure ethical, equitable, person‐centered implementation in aging populations and long‐term randomized evaluations. A structured MEDLINE search identified randomized controlled trials (RCTs) published between January 2023 and December 2025 that evaluated AI/ML‐based interventions in adults ≥ 65 years. Of 31 records identified, 19 underwent abstract screening, seven underwent full‐text assessment, and four articles were identified as meeting all criteria for inclusion, which focused on scalability, clinical impact, and methodological rigor. Across postoperative rehabilitation and preventive care, AI/ML interventions demonstrated meaningful clinical benefits. Transformative applications of AI/ML described robotic systems quantifying weight‐bearing, algorithm‐guided paired rehabilitation interventions, paired exercises in geriatric hip fracture rehabilitation, smartphone platforms delivering continuously personalized exercise programs, and conversational chatbots providing tailored vaccine counseling. The included RCTs show that AI‐driven interventions can enhance physical recovery, support psychosocial well‐being, and improve uptake of preventive measures compared with conventional approaches. Key considerations for future implementation include digital health literacy, long‐term follow‐up, and potential bias arising from healthier or more motivated study populations. As evidence grows, geriatric innovation must prioritize safety, ethics, and equitable access to ensure that technological precision enhances, rather than replaces, person‐centered care.Keywords: artificial intelligence, machine learning, older adults, randomized controlled trial

Background

Artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly reshaping biomedical research and the clinical care of older adults. These technologies offer the potential to transform population‐level, guideline‐based care to personalized care for older adults by integrating multimodal data, such as electronic health records, imaging, sensor‐derived movement, and speech to assess risk, detect decline, and guide individualized interventions [1, 2, 3]. Beyond traditional clinical decision support, AI and machine learning (ML) applications in geriatric medicine now include robotic rehabilitation systems, smartphone‐based remote monitoring, conversational agents for health counseling, and predictive modeling to personalize preventive care [4]. These innovations are being studied in both clinical trials and observational settings, highlighting the breadth of approaches to enhance functional independence and quality of life.

Implementation of these technologies into geriatric practice, however, has the potential to magnify existing problems related to digital health literacy, inclusivity, and privacy as well as the risk of creating new problems related to algorithmic bias and lack of transparency [5]. Older adults are frequently underrepresented in model development due to ageism and bias [2, 6, 7], and the testing of AI‐enabled interventions using rigorous study designs (e.g., randomized double‐blinded, long follow‐up) can be challenging given the rapid development of the technology, limiting the strength of evidence on sustained safety and effectiveness. As the evidence for using AI and ML grows, it is imperative that the geriatrics community critically assess the emerging evidence and technologies and shape the field such that ethical, equitable, and person‐centered AI integration promotes functional independence and quality of life for aging populations.

Method

A structured PubMed literature search was conducted to identify randomized controlled trials (RCTs) published between January 1, 2023, and December 31, 2025, that evaluated AI/ML‐based interventions in older adults (Figure 1). As background, Figure 2 shows the rapid increase in AI/ML RCTs since 2015, which helps contextualize the rationale for this review. The PubMed search strategy combined medical subject headings (MeSH) and free‐text terms related to geriatrics (e.g., frailty, older adult, gerontol*), AI/ML (e.g., “Artificial Intelligence,” “Machine Learning,” “Deep Learning,” “Natural Language Processing”), and RCTs. The full Boolean search string is provided in the Supporting Information. Initial inclusion criteria during title and abstract screening were: (1) published in English; (2) employed an RCT design; and (3) exclusively included older adults aged ≥ 65 years. Initial exclusion criteria were: (1) reviews or meta‐analyses, (2) topic was decision‐support‐only systems, and (3) AI/ML was used solely as an analytic tool rather than as an integral part of the intervention. Because the goal of this search was to identify high‐quality RCTs and AI/ML‐based interventions with potential to influence clinical practice in older adults, the following criteria were also applied when assessing the full‐text articles for eligibility: (1) the intervention's scalability; (2) its likely impact on the care of older adults (real‐world functional or care‐delivery impact beyond algorithmic performance); (3) risk of bias across key methodological domains (randomization, deviation from the intended intervention(s), allocation concealment [8], blinding, incomplete data, and measurement of the outcome); (4) sample size, and (5) clarity of intervention description.

The initial search yielded 31 records. Two reviewers (TT & CF) independently screened titles and abstracts, applying the eligibility criteria above. In cases of disagreement, consensus was reached through discussion with a third reviewer (AB). Of the 31 records that were screened, seven underwent full‐text review, and four articles were selected for inclusion in this Clin‐STAR Corner review. Reproducibility was ensured by using a predefined and fully documented PubMed search strategy (Supporting Information), a fixed publication date range, and explicit inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Study 1

Visualized load‐bearing‐based robot control system improves early function in femoral neck fracture patients: a randomized controlled trial focusing on outcomes after total hip arthroplasty [9].

Study Summary

Conducted in Fujian, China, this single‐blind RCT enrolled 126 patients (≥ 65 years) with femoral neck fractures undergoing total hip arthroplasty (THA). In this study, participants were randomly assigned to either an intelligent weight‐bearing robotic rehabilitation group (N = 63) or a conventional walking aid group (N = 63) for 5–7 days during their hospital stay followed by home‐based rehabilitation post‐discharge. The study employed a single‐blind design in which outcome assessors were blinded to group allocation and had no involvement in participants' rehabilitation or initial data collection. Blinding of participants and treating therapists was not feasible due to the visible nature of the interventions, which involved an intelligent weight‐bearing robot versus a conventional method.

The AI component in this study is described as an intelligent rehabilitation system that utilizes an AI‐powered control system to enable human‐robotic interaction. The technology integrated an onboard microcontroller that processes real‐time, visualized feedback on weight‐bearing through foot sensors and a touchscreen interface, allowing patients to progressively increase weight‐bearing on the operated limb, ensuring that postoperative weight‐bearing is controlled scientifically rather than by subjective estimation. Data were transmitted to a cloud platform for therapist monitoring and adjustment. In the trial, weekly weight‐bearing progress was systematically quantified only for the robot‐assisted group; the control group received usual care rehabilitation using conventional walking aids without objective weekly monitoring of weight‐bearing progression.

At the 1‐month postoperative follow‐up, the robot‐assisted group showed statistically significant improvements across all main outcomes (i.e., visual analog scale pain scores, hip joint extension‐flexion range of motion, Harris Score, Straight Leg Raise duration, 3‐Minute Walk Test) compared to the conventional rehabilitation control group (all p values < 0.05) compared to the conventional rehabilitation control group. These findings align with previous robotic rehabilitation trials reporting improvements in functional scores and gait performance after hip arthroplasty [13, 14].

Strength and Limitation

The study demonstrated several strengths. The intervention used a rehabilitation robot equipped with a visualized load‐bearing control system capable of sensing lower limb pressure and displaying it in real‐time (e.g., as a pressure heatmap). This visualization addressed a long‐standing clinical limitation in that traditional walking aids provide no objective means of monitoring or quantifying weight‐bearing intensity, a challenge similarly noted in early weight‐bearing studies after arthroplasty [15, 16]. This feature provided patients with quantitative, visual guidance for weight‐bearing, an essential component of recovery following THA.

The study also had several limitations. Functional outcomes were assessed only one month after surgery, and no long‐term follow‐up was conducted to evaluate sustained benefits, an issue similarly highlighted in other short‐duration robotic rehabilitation trials [14, 17]. The use of the robot posed challenges for older patients, requiring close supervision and assistance from medical staff and family members, which increased time and resource demands. In addition, the single‐blind design (participants and therapists could not be blinded to the intervention) could have contributed to bias in assessments.

Interpretation and Implications

Regarding generalizability and clinical implications, the robotic control system effectively promoted early functional recovery in THA patients, demonstrating its value as an intelligent rehabilitation tool. Its ability to accurately monitor and quantify weight‐bearing intensity addresses a key clinical limitation of traditional walking aids, which lack objective feedback mechanisms. By enabling precise, progressive, and safe weight‐bearing, the system helps patients regain functional independence and enhances the overall efficiency of postoperative rehabilitation. Compared to the existing literature, most studies of robotic‐assisted rehabilitation in THA are small, single‐center RCTs or pilot studies [13, 15], limiting broad applicability. Evidence specifically evaluating robotic rehabilitation (as opposed to robot‐assisted surgery, which has been studied in large databases showing shorter hospital stays and faster initial recovery [18, 19]) demonstrates early functional improvements, but long‐term outcomes and comparative effectiveness remain to be established.

Study 2

Strengthening recovery: The impact of paired exercises in geriatric hip fracture rehabilitation—The PaLMe project [10].

Study Summary

The second robotic rehabilitation article examined the PaLMe system in Israel, a prospective RCT evaluating whether paired, synchronized online exercises could improve physical and psychological recovery after hip fracture surgery. Designed for use during unsupervised periods, the intervention aimed to address common barriers such as low motivation, limited engagement, and social isolation. A total of 93 participants (mean age 80.5 years, 66% women) were enrolled during their postoperative hospital stay, with the intervention lasting for the duration of hospitalization (median 5.6 days). Participants were randomized into either the PaLMe group or a control group. Those assigned to PaLMe were paired using a custom algorithm that matched patients based on clinical and demographic similarities to enhance compatibility. This data‐driven matching procedure constitutes the primary AI/ML component of the study by enabling algorithmic personalization of rehabilitation delivery, despite relying on fixed rules rather than adaptive learning. Paired participants then performed synchronized online exercises together. In contrast, the control group completed the same set of rehabilitation exercises individually using prerecorded, asynchronous videos. Individuals who completed less than 50% of prescribed sessions were excluded. This final analytic sample of 83 participants (PaLMe n = 36; control n = 47).

The findings showed that the paired exercise model significantly enhanced physical recovery. At discharge, 66% of participants in the PaLMe group achieved a clinically meaningful walking distance of ≥ 21 m, versus 43% in controls. The mean walking distance was also significantly greater in the intervention group than in controls. The program also produced meaningful psychological benefits. Participants in the PaLMe group demonstrated significantly improved self‐efficacy and motivation. Subjective feedback revealed that shared accountability between paired partners enhanced the experience: 62.6% of PaLMe participants rated the exercises as easy to moderate versus 28.5% in controls. Feasibility metrics were strong, with 89% retention and 95.5% reporting enjoyment of paired exercise. However, adherence was moderate, with only 47% completing more than half of sessions, likely due to logistical barriers such as physiotherapy scheduling conflicts and family visits.

Strength and Limitation

The study's strengths centered on the effectiveness and high acceptability of a peer‐supported digital rehabilitation model. The system significantly improved walking performance, enhanced psychological engagement, and was well received by participants. The use of a custom patient‐matching algorithm further strengthened the intervention by promoting compatibility and interaction within pairs.

The study also exhibited several important limitations. The per‐protocol analysis, while appropriate for evaluating efficacy among adherent participants, reduced the intervention group sample to only 36 individuals (81% allocated), limiting statistical power and external validity. The exclusion of participants with lower adherence may bias results toward more motivated individuals. The short intervention period, confined to the median 5.6‐day hospital stay, prevents assessment of longer‐term functional improvements or sustained psychosocial benefits. Additional limitations included selection bias (e.g., excluding weekend admissions or those with delayed surgery), moderate adherence levels, and logistical challenges that hindered consistent participation.

Interpretation and Implications

In terms of clinical implications, the PaLMe program demonstrates that integrating psychosocial support and peer interaction can meaningfully enhance early postoperative recovery after hip fracture surgery. Although generalizability is currently limited by sample size and the controlled hospital environment, the intervention shows strong potential as a low‐cost, scalable model for personalized rehabilitation. Addressing logistical barriers such as scheduling coordination will be essential for routine clinical implementation. This study is consistent with previous literature showing that multidisciplinary [20, 21] and psychosocial interventions can enhance outcomes, improve physical recovery, self‐efficacy, and quality of life after hip fracture. However, many interventions have been unstructured in their delivery, relying on informal encouragement, ad hoc social support, or focused on family/caregiver support without standardized pairing criteria, synchronized structure, or algorithm matching. In contrast, the PaLMe intervention introduces a structured, algorithm‐guided peer‐pairing framework that systematically matches patients based on clinical and demographic characteristics and delivers synchronized, goal‐oriented exercises, thereby formalizing peer support as an integral component of rehabilitation. Future research should include large multicenter trials to validate these findings, assess continuous and long‐term outcomes, and explore the use of AI‐assisted pairing algorithms to further optimize patient matching and intervention effectiveness.

Study 3

A hybrid chatbot to promote pneumococcal vaccination (PV) among older adults [11].

Study Summary

The RCT was conducted by Wang et al. to evaluate a novel approach to increasing PV uptake among a geriatric population in Hong Kong. The design was a partially masked, parallel‐group RCT, where 374 participants ≥ 65 years were randomly assigned to either a chatbot intervention group (n = 187) or the control group (n = 187) using a randomization algorithm built into the chatbot system. Key RCT procedures to ensure rigor included blinding the outcome assessors and data analysts to participant allocation and utilizing an intention‐to‐treat analysis for the primary outcome (PV uptake at month 12). The self‐reported primary outcome was validated by requesting participants to upload images of their PV receipts.

The chatbot successfully combined a rule‐based component that delivered interventions tailored to the patient's stage of change (precontemplation, contemplation, or preparation) with an AI component utilizing natural language processing (NLP) to provide real‐time responses to participants' PV‐related questions. This NLP capability allowed the system to retrieve relevant answers from a comprehensive database using keyword matching and provide instantaneous text responses to open‐ended questions raised by the participants.

The study found that the hybrid chatbot, which incorporated personalized intervention content alongside a real‐time question and answer (Q&A) function, was more efficacious than a chatbot delivering a standard online intervention. The real‐time Q&A function supported by AI substantially increased engagement; 79.7% of participants in the hybrid group completed at least one intervention session, significantly higher than the 57.8% achieved by the standard intervention group (p < 0.001). The validated PV uptake rate at 12 months was significantly higher in the hybrid chatbot group (29.4%) compared to the standard intervention group (18.7%; p = 0.01).

Strength and Limitation

The primary strengths of this hybrid approach lie in its technological design and demonstrated efficacy for improving vaccination outcomes. This enhanced compliance is strongly linked to higher motivation for vaccination, suggesting that real‐time answers successfully addressed participants' immediate knowledge gaps and concerns. Compared to existing effective interventions, such as nurse home visits or telephone education, the hybrid chatbot is fully automated and requires fewer resources, establishing it as a highly sustainable strategy for PV promotion.

The study faces several limitations. The intervention was constrained to older adults who were already proficient users of smartphones and the WhatsApp messaging application. This required digital health proficiency means the study excluded older adults who are less digitally literate (or those with technological support) and may have a lower socioeconomic status, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings. The recruitment method led to the oldest cohort (≥ 75 years) being under‐sampled, and a substantial proportion (22.5%) of eligible older adults declined participation, suggesting the presence of self‐selection bias. Methodologically, a key limitation is the inability to isolate the effect of the AI‐powered real‐time question and answer (Q&A) function alone, meaning the observed efficacy is likely the result of additive or synergistic effects of tailored content and instantaneous feedback. Finally, the primary outcome regarding the stage of change was self‐reported, introducing the possibility of social desirability or recall bias.

Interpretation and Implications

Compared to prior studies, this trial stands out for its robust design, use of validated behavioral theory (stage of change), and integration of real‐time conversational AI, which allowed for tailored, interactive vaccine counseling at scale, leading to higher vaccination rates. First, this success underscores the potential of advanced digital health tools in addressing the critical public health challenge posed by low PV uptake, which was only 35.3% in Hong Kong in 2023. Second, this study is consistent with other studies that have suggested that digital interventions (e.g., text messaging, phone calls, and electronic reminders) can modestly improve vaccine uptake in older adults, though most have not leveraged advanced AI or personalized behavioral tailoring. Recent reviews [22] of conversational AI and chatbots for vaccine communication highlight their potential to provide credible, accessible, and user‐friendly information, but also note that most prior studies were exploratory, with small samples and limited control groups. The hybrid design proved effective at addressing modifiable determinants of vaccine hesitancy, such as low self‐efficacy and lack of awareness about benefits and risks. Implementing such AI‐powered digital tools can provide personalized health communication and round‐the‐clock Q&A services, allowing health organizations to improve vaccination coverage sustainably while simultaneously reducing the workload and resource demands associated with traditional methods like phone calls or home visits. This also suggests that integrating personalized, evidence‐based behavioral change models with interactive AI technology can be a potent and sustainable tool in preventative geriatric care.

Study 4

A smartphone platform for remote motor fitness assessment and AI‐generated personalized exercise programs for older adults: Randomized controlled trial [12].

Study Summary

The study was conducted in a community setting including private homes and independent living facilities in central and northern Israel, enrolling 239 healthy, community‐dwelling older adults (mean age 72.6 years). Participants were required to be age ≥ 65 years, functionally independent, Mini‐Cog > 3, and competent smartphone users. To address limitations of conventional exercise guidelines, the trial tested a personalized, fully remote exercise platform that eliminated the need for laboratory equipment or continuous supervision. Using a parallel‐group RCT design, the investigators randomized participants to one of three groups: (1) an 8‐week AI‐personalized exercise program, (2) an active control group receiving World Health Organization (WHO) guideline‐based counseling, or (3) a no‐intervention control group. Smartphone sensors (accelerometer and gyroscope) captured movement data that were converted into digital markers (DMs) representing balance, flexibility, and strength. ML algorithms constitute the core technological component of this intervention, that analyzed raw sensor data to construct individualized motor fitness profiles and automatically classify participants' fitness levels. These ML‐derived classifications were then used to personalize exercise prescription by selecting and delivering tailored video‐based exercises (difficulty level A [simple exercise] or B [advanced exercise]) aligned with each participant's measured strengths and deficits. Reassessments occurred at 4‐, 8‐, and 12‐weeks to refine exercise matching over time.

The AI‐personalized exercise group demonstrated significant improvements in dynamic balance, total balance (sum of all balance measurements), arm flexion, arm extension, and total arm strength compared with both active‐control and control groups, with many gains occurring as early as 4 weeks. Improvements were consistently driven by the adherer subgroup, while no changes were observed for torso rotation or lower body strength (sit‐to‐stand), likely reflecting a ceiling effect from the modified timed protocol. Although fitness gains declined for all groups by 12‐week follow‐up, the study demonstrated the feasibility, precision, and sensitivity of using smartphone‐derived DMs to monitor change and personalize remote multicomponent exercise in older adults.

Strength and Limitation

The home‐based nature of the platform makes it a low‐cost and scalable intervention that does not require laboratory or professional supervision, offering substantial practical advantages in the care of older adults. The remote system was successfully validated as reliable and accurate, proving sufficiently sensitive to measure both improvement and subsequent decline in fitness measures following the cessation of the program.

The study has several limitations. The sample suffered from bias, as participants were largely healthy, cognitively intact, and of a higher socioeconomic status, limiting the applicability of findings to frailer or less digitally savvy older individuals. Logistical challenges were reported concerning the technology interface, specifically usability issues such as the small smartphone screen size and the difficulty older adults faced in controlling video playback to repeat exercises. A large number of outcomes were registered as “primary” in the trial registry, and some of the primary outcomes were not reported. Although the results were statistically significant, the observed effect sizes were small, and the clinical health benefits derived from the minimum effective frequency (1.5 sessions/week) remain uncertain. Additionally, adherence data for the control group relied on self‐reported measures, while the intervention group was accurately tracked by the application. The generalizability of this AI‐personalized exercise platform is currently limited to independent, digitally literate older adults.

Interpretation and Implications

The study provides a powerful foundation for scalable, data‐driven rehabilitation. The platform offers a new model for remote, adaptive, data‐driven geriatric care that improves balance, upper body flexibility, and strength with relatively low adherence thresholds. The technology serves as a valuable tool for remote monitoring, capable of capturing subtle fluctuations in fitness trends that might otherwise go unnoticed outside of traditional clinical settings. Many existing digital interventions for older adults focus on general physical activity, often using exergames, tablet apps, or videoconferencing, with few leveraging smartphone sensors for individualized motor fitness assessment and AI‐driven personalization [23]. Protocols for similar RCTs have been published, but few have reported results with such detailed motor fitness profiling and ML‐based tailoring [24]. This approach moves wellness to an AI‐developed, precision‐targeted fitness program, ensuring that individualized exercise programs are continuously adaptive to the patient's measured needs, responding to subtle fluctuations in motor performance and automatically adjusting exercise prescriptions. This platform offers a valuable tool for proactive monitoring and detecting early functional decline. Although further research is needed in frailer and more diverse populations, the study establishes a clear proof of concept for integrating sensor technologies and AI algorithms into routine geriatric health promotion, potentially expanding access to personalized rehabilitation for older adults who lack transportation, supervision, or proximity to clinical facilities.

Limitations, Safety, and Implementation Considerations

Despite promising findings, several cross‐cutting limitations and safety considerations emerged across the included trials. All four studies enrolled relatively healthier, more motivated, and digitally capable older adults, introducing selection bias and potentially limiting generalizability to frailer individuals, those with cognitive or sensory impairments, or lower socioeconomic status. This limitation is particularly salient for smartphone‐ and chatbot‐based interventions, where digital health literacy and access are prerequisites and may lead to overestimation of effectiveness in broader geriatric populations.

Implementation challenges were also evident. Robotic and digital interventions often required additional staff support, caregiver involvement, or technical troubleshooting, increasing workflow complexity and resource demands. Adherence varied across studies and was frequently influenced by logistical barriers rather than acceptability alone, underscoring the need for implementation science approaches to evaluate feasibility, sustainability, and integration into routine geriatric care.

Safety considerations extended beyond physical outcomes. Although no serious AI‐related adverse events were reported, follow‐up durations were short and safety monitoring was limited. Potential risks include inappropriate progression of rehabilitation intensity, overreliance on automated recommendations, misinterpretation of feedback, and reduced clinician oversight. In conversational AI applications, inaccurate or incomplete responses could undermine trust or reinforce misconceptions if not carefully monitored.

Privacy, data security, and informed consent were insufficiently addressed. AI‐enabled interventions rely on continuous data collection and cloud‐based processing of sensitive health information, yet few trials detailed data governance, cybersecurity safeguards, or participant education regarding data use. Finally, algorithmic bias remains a concern, as models developed in homogeneous populations may perform poorly in more diverse or vulnerable groups. Future RCTs should incorporate predefined safety outcomes, subgroup analyses, and explicit reporting of data protection and consent processes to ensure safe, ethical implementation of AI/ML in geriatric care.

Several AI/ML interventions, including robotic rehabilitation, algorithm‐guided peer‐pairing exercises, and smartphone‐based platforms, often require substantial staff support, caregiver involvement, or ongoing technical troubleshooting. These requirements can increase clinical workflow complexity, limit scalability, and affect sustainability, particularly in settings with constrained personnel or resources. Additionally, digital literacy, multimorbidity, cognitive impairment, and caregiving needs can meaningfully influence the applicability of AI/ML interventions in routine geriatric practice. Limited digital literacy demands extra training and monitoring, while multimorbidity and cognitive impairment may limit independent or safe use, and caregiving requirements can shape whether interventions can be implemented effectively. Considering these factors is essential to ensure AI/ML interventions are feasible, equitable, and impactful for diverse geriatric populations.

Additional Study‐Level Limitations

Many studies that were considered were constrained by small sample sizes, short follow‐up durations, or single‐center recruitment, limiting statistical power and generalizability [25, 26, 27]. While most trials reported positive outcomes, some demonstrated neutral or non‐significant effects, such as robot‐moderated cognitive training in healthy older adults [28], or social robot interventions for depression in dementia daycare users [29]. Quasi‐experimental designs, as in PARO robot studies [30], or interventions confined to specific settings (e.g., single assisted living facilities or daycare centers) further limited external validity. These constraints indicate that most current evidence primarily demonstrates feasibility (interventions can be delivered and used as intended) and early efficacy (initial benefits under controlled conditions), but broader implementation across diverse geriatric populations remains uncertain. Larger, multicenter RCTs with longer follow‐up, standardized outcome measures, and careful evaluation of safety, equity, and sustainability are needed to establish generalizable effectiveness and scalability of AI/ML interventions in geriatric care.

Conclusion

Across diverse clinical contexts, from postoperative rehabilitation to preventive care, AI/ML has been reshaping the landscape of geriatric care. The number of published RCTs evaluating AI/ML interventions has risen exponentially since 2015 (see Figure 2). The four RCTs reviewed in this report, which were published between 2023 and 2025, demonstrate that AI‐enabled interventions are no longer limited to imaging reading assistance or clinical decision support systems. Robotic systems capable of quantifying weight‐bearing, algorithm‐guided peer‐pairing interventions that promote social connection and motivation during rehabilitation, smartphone platforms that continuously personalize exercise programs, and conversational chatbots that deliver tailored vaccine counseling all illustrate how AI can address longstanding challenges in geriatric medicine, including variable patient engagement, inconsistent adherence, and the need for individualized, scalable care. Collectively, these studies show that AI‐driven interventions can enhance physical recovery, support psychosocial well‐being, and increase uptake of essential preventive measures when compared with conventional methods. They also highlight recurring themes relevant to future implementation: the importance of digital health literacy, the need for long‐term follow‐up assessments, and the potential for bias when interventions are tested primarily in healthier or more motivated subgroups.

Emerging randomized trials demonstrate that AI‐driven systems can improve physical recovery and preventive health behaviors compared with standard approaches. As evidence grows, the next phase of geriatric innovation should focus not only on efficacy but also on safety, ethics, and equitable implementation—ensuring that technological precision enhances, rather than replaces, person‐centered care. Safety considerations should include potential risks from algorithmic bias, inaccurate predictions, or insufficient monitoring of adverse events. In addition, the secure handling of personal health data, patient consent, and equitable access to AI‐enabled interventions remain critical. Addressing these challenges will be essential to translate AI/ML innovations into widespread, reliable, and ethical geriatric care.
