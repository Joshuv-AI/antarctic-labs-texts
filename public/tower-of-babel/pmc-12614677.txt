Use of NovoSorb Biodegradable Temporizing Matrix for Reconstruction of Scrotal Fournier Gangrene Wound
Authors: Cheng-Hao Huang, Pei-Hsin Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Summary:A 55-year-old man presented with a diagnosis of Fournier gangrene of the groin and genitals. Surgical debridement resulted in full-thickness wounds requiring reconstruction. NovoSorb Biodegradable Temporizing Matrix is a bilayer biodegradable dermal matrix developed in recent years to provide a synthetic option for wound reconstruction. We demonstrate a novel method for managing a Fournier gangrene wound using a 1-stage advancement flap combined with NovoSorb Biodegradable Temporizing Matrix to achieve reconstruction without the need for split-thickness skin grafting. The resulting postoperative wound demonstrated a good clinical outcome without complications.

Fournier gangrene is a rapidly spreading necrotizing fasciitis of the scrotum, perineum, and external genitalia. Management involves aggressive debridement of necrotic tissue and broad-spectrum antibiotics. After debridement, the testicles and spermatic cords are often exposed, which requires reconstruction.1,2 Various reconstructive methods have been proposed for addressing defects following Fournier gangrene, including primary closure, healing by secondary intention, advancement flaps, skin grafts, and other flap techniques, depending on the defect size.2

In recent years, the use of NovoSorb Biodegradable Temporizing Matrix (BTM; PolyNovo Biomaterials Pty Ltd, Port Melbourne, Australia) for reconstructing skin defects has increased. BTM is a bilayer biodegradable dermal matrix designed to meet the need for synthetic options in surgical wound reconstruction. A biodegradable wound-facing polyurethane foam is attached to a nonbiodegradable transparent polyurethane sealing membrane. The foam is applied onto the wound, initiating integration in approximately 2 weeks and achieving full integration in about 6 weeks. Upon integration, the sealing membrane is removed through delamination. The foam undergoes hydrolysis and is completely resorbed over a span of approximately 18 months.3,4 Application and integration of BTM is typically followed by a second stage of split-thickness skin grafting.3 Here, we demonstrate a novel method for managing a Fournier gangrene wound using BTM with a single-stage reconstruction, a technique not previously reported.

CASE REPORT

We report on a 55-year-old man with a history including type II diabetes mellitus under medication control, right foot necrotizing fasciitis postdebridement and fasciotomy, and right big toe osteomyelitis postsequestrectomy. He noticed that his scrotum had become darker for 2 weeks and presented to our emergency room for help. On triage, no fever, pain, trauma, or hematuria was noted. His temperature was 35.1°C; pulse, 110 beats/min; and blood pressure, 147/85 mm Hg. The physical examination was notable for necrotic-appearing tissue and induration in the scrotum with odor.

Laboratory data showed leukocytosis (white blood cell count: 15,000/μL), microcytic anemia (Hb: 5.3 g/dL), and an elevated C-reactive protein level (14.56 mg/dL). Computed tomography revealed an abscess with air and fluid collection in the scrotum and minimal fat stranding at the pelvic floor. A diagnosis of Fournier gangrene was made, and emergent fasciotomy and drainage were performed, with hospital admission arranged.

Ceftriaxone and metronidazole were prescribed under guidance from the infection specialist according to the wound culture result. Multiple surgical debridements were performed within 10 days of admission to remove all necrotic and nonviable tissue, and healthy granulation tissue was noticed on the resulting wound bed of approximately 10 × 10 cm (Fig. 1). Consequently, partial closure with advancement flap reconstruction and application of BTM secured using peripheral sutures were performed to the remaining 8 × 6 cm area (Fig. 2). Integration of BTM into the wound bed relies on the flow of blood followed by cellular infiltration into the BTM foam scaffold. Dressings over the BTM consisted of Commercialized Framycin Gauze (Standard Chem & Pharm Co., Ltd., Taiwan, ROC). The patient was discharged in stable condition 15 days after admission and followed up at the outpatient clinic.

The patient was scheduled for follow-up appointments at the outpatient clinic at 1, 3, 7, 12, and 18 weeks postoperation to change the dressings, monitor for any fluid collections, and observe the process of BTM integration. Delamination was performed during week 7 after confirmation that BTM integration was complete (Fig. 3). Split-thickness skin grafting was not performed due to patient preference, with the wound left to heal by secondary intention. The wound exhibited satisfactory healing, with only minimal scar tissue observed at the central part (Fig. 4).

DISCUSSION

The objectives of reconstruction following urgent excisional debridement of Fournier gangrene encompass achieving sufficient wound coverage while reconstructing the scrotum to maintain physiological function and attain satisfactory cosmetic outcomes.5 Numerous reconstructive methods have been proposed for addressing defects following Fournier gangrene, with selection typically based on the size of the defect. However, a systematic review concludes that there is insufficient evidence to favor flap coverage over skin grafting.2

The split-thickness skin graft has been reported as safe and technically easy for the treatment of skin defects after Fournier gangrene. It is an easily reproducible technique with low morbidity. Common complications of skin grafting are contraction and graft loss due to bleeding, shearing, or infection.1,2

Benefits of flap reconstruction include durable protection of the testes, provision of immediate coverage, and lower incidence of contracture. The drawbacks of free flap reconstruction are wasting a functional muscle/fasciocutaneous flap, which would cause bulk around the testes. The excessive fat content of the muscle and thick fasciocutaneous flaps can also increase the temperature around the testis, which may inhibit spermatogenesis.1,2

The use of dermal matrices has been reported previously, including porcine bladder extracellular matrix and bilaminate artificial dermis made of shark chondroitin 6-sulfate and bovine collagen.3,4 Recently, there has been a growing trend in using BTM for reconstructing skin defects. BTM was first used in 2012 for burn surgery, providing temporary wound closure, preventing contraction, and improving cosmesis compared with sole reliance on split-thickness skin grafts. Since then, its application has broadened to include the reconstruction of complex burn injuries, as well as the management of soft tissue trauma and necrotizing fasciitis postdebridement.5,6 Notably, BTM has demonstrated robust structural integrity even in the presence of underlying infection, as evidenced by multiple case series.7,8

In this case, we opted for reconstruction using BTM complemented by an advancement flap, due to the comparative advantages over skin grafts or fasciocutaneous flaps. Our experience indicates that reconstruction with split-thickness skin grafts alone often leads to contraction and a higher rate of reoperation due to graft loss. Conversely, reconstruction with fasciocutaneous flaps is more time-consuming and complex than skin grafts, potentially increasing morbidity at both donor and recipient sites, particularly in patients with factors such as advanced age or underlying diseases. BTM offers a relatively quick and straightforward procedure with simple postoperative care, providing durable coverage and improved contour.

The use of BTM has demonstrated potential for complex wound reconstruction. However, its associated adverse effects warrant careful consideration. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis by Lane et al9 reported an infection rate of approximately 12.6% and a graft failure rate of around 1.1% following BTM use. In some comparative studies, BTM exhibited a lower infection rate than Integra, suggesting a possible advantage in a specific condition.10,11 Despite being fully synthetic and generally well tolerated with minimal foreign body reaction, the use of BTM in infected or poorly vascularized wound beds remains controversial and should be approached with caution.

Clinical experience with BTM in scrotal reconstruction remains limited, and no specific studies have been published to date. Nevertheless, our observations suggest that BTM may lead to less scar contraction compared with direct skin grafting. In our case, the resulting contraction was minimal and did not adversely affect the patient. To better understand the safety and efficacy of BTM, especially in comparison with other dermal substitutes, large-scale randomized controlled trials are necessary to guide its optimal clinical application.

CONCLUSIONS

We demonstrated a novel method for the management of a Fournier gangrene wound using BTM. This involved a 1-stage reconstruction without split-thickness skin grafting, not previously reported. The resulting postoperative wound demonstrated a good clinical outcome without complications.
