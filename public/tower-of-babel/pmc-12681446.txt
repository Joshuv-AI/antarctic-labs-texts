Mental health status and cardiovascular outcomes across Life's Essential 8 categories
Authors: Enze Li, Le Zhou, Liu He, Song Zuo, Song-Nan Li, Xiao-Xia Liu, Cai-Hua Sang, Jian-Zeng Dong, Xue-Yuan Guo, Changsheng Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnxiety and depression are established risk factors for cardiovascular disease (CVD); impacts of these conditions on cardiovascular outcomes across different levels of cardiovascular health (CVH), measured by Life's Essential 8 (LE8) scores, remain unclear.Methods and resultsWe analyzed 290,778 UK Biobank participants (mean age 56.5, 56.1 % male) without prior CVD. CVH was assessed using the LE8 score. Mental health status (healthy, anxiety only, depression only, or comorbid anxiety and depression) was determined through PHQ-4 items and ICD-10 codes (F32-F33 for depression, F40-F41 for anxiety). Cardiovascular outcomes were defined as a composite of coronary heart disease (CHD), heart failure (HF), and stroke. Cox proportional hazards models were adjusted for confounders and stratified by LE8 score (<50 [poor CVH] vs. ≥50 [intermediate-optimal]). Compared to the mentally healthy group, participants with comorbid anxiety and depression had the highest overall cardiovascular risk (adjusted HR 1.85 [95 % CI 1.74–1.97]). The impact of mental health status varied according to baseline LE8 scores. Among those with LE8 <50, isolated anxiety (adjusted HR 1.23 [1.09–1.40]), depression (adjusted HR 1.34 [1.22–1.47]), as well as comorbid anxiety and depression (adjusted HR 1.46 [1.31–1.64]) were all associated with increased cardiovascular risk. In the LE8 ≥50 group, isolated anxiety (adjusted HR 1.39 [1.30–1.48]), isolated depression (adjusted HR 1.52 [1.43–1.60]), and comorbid anxiety and depression (adjusted HR 1.78 [1.66–1.90]) were all associated with increased risk.ConclusionsMental health independently affects cardiovascular risk, modified by baseline CVH. Mental health emerge as a relative risk predictor among individuals with CVH (LE8 ≥ 50).

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide, imposing a substantial burden on public health systems. The annual death toll from CVDs rose from 2.51 million to 3.97 million between 1990 and 2016 [1]. Traditional risk factors such as hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, and smoking have long been recognized as significant contributors to CVD development [2]. However, in recent years, there has been a growing appreciation for the role of mental health conditions, particularly anxiety and depression in influencing cardiovascular outcomes [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. Accumulating evidence suggests that individuals with anxiety or depression are at higher risk of developing CVD compared to those without these mental health conditions.

Despite this recognition, the relationship between mental health conditions and cardiovascular outcomes is complex and may be influenced by various factors [[7], [8], [9]]. One such factor is the overall cardiovascular health (CVH) status of an individual. The American Heart Association's “Life's Essential 8” (LE8) score is a comprehensive metric that assesses CVH based on eight modifiable factors [2]. However, the extent to which anxiety and depression impact cardiovascular outcomes across different levels of CVH, as measured by the LE8 score, remains unclear [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14]]. This gap in knowledge is critical because it may inform targeted interventions and screening strategies for individuals at varying levels of cardiovascular risk [15,16].

Previous studies have examined the association between mental health and CVD outcomes, but they have often focused on either anxiety or depression alone, or have not accounted for the comprehensive CVH status of individuals [17]. Moreover, the majority of these studies have been limited by their cross-sectional design, small sample size, or lack of adjustment for potential confounders [18]. In this study, we aim to address this gap by analyzing a large cohort of participants from the UK Biobank, a rich source of longitudinal data with detailed information on CVH and mental health status. Our analysis is designed to test the hypothesis that the relationship between mental health and CVD is modified by an individual's underlying cardiovascular health. To do this, we first stratify the population into two groups based on their Life's Essential 8 score: poor CVH (LE8 < 50) and intermediate-to-optimal CVH (LE8 ≥ 50). We then employ Cox regression models within each stratum to separately quantify the risk associated with anxiety, depression, and their comorbidity. This stratification reveals a differential pattern of risk; for instance, among individuals with intermediate-to-optimal CVH, depression is associated with a significant increase in CVD risk, but anxiety is not. This highlights that the cardiovascular impact of specific mental health conditions may be contingent upon an individual's baseline CVH profile.

Method

Study population

The study population was derived from the UK Biobank, a large prospective cohort study that recruited participants aged 37–73 years from the general population between 2006 and 2010. A total of over 500,000 individuals were included, with a response rate of 5.5 %. Participants were followed from their baseline assessment until the occurrence of a cardiovascular event, death, loss to follow-up, or the end of the study period (October 31, 2023), whichever came first. The mean follow-up duration was 12.06 years (SD 3.58). Recruitment was conducted through 22 assessment centers across Wales, Scotland, and England. Participants completed touchscreen questionnaires, underwent physical measurements. Hospital admission records and death certificates were subsequently linked to assess clinical outcomes.

Eligibility criteria

Fig. 1 shows the selection procedure. From the initial cohort of 502,357 participants, we excluded 193,654 individuals (1305 due to missing follow-up data and 192,349 due to incomplete LE8 components). Additionally, 17,925 participants with a history of CVD before baseline were excluded (8081 with CHD, 295 with stroke, 7562 with HF, and 1987 with atrial fibrillation). Ultimately, 290,778 participants were included in the study.Fig. 1Participants selection procedure.Fig. 1

LE8 and mental health assessment

CVH was assessed using a modified version of the American Heart Association's Life's Essential 8 (LE8) score, which translates diverse health variables into a standardized 0–100 scale. The components included smoking status, non-HDL cholesterol levels, blood pressure, BMI, HbA1c, physical activity, diet, and sleep quality. The diet component was adapted to align with the UK Biobank data structure, replacing the DASH-style diet metric with a validated UK Biobank-specific diet score. The LE8 score for each participant was calculated by summing the individual scores for each of the eight metrics and then dividing the total by eight [2]:(1)LE8score=∑i=18metrici8

For mental health assessment, anxiety and depression status were evaluated using both diagnostic codes and self-reported questionnaires. Anxiety and depression were classified based on ICD-10 codes (F32-F33 for depression and F40-F41 for anxiety) from hospital admission records. At baseline, depression and anxiety symptoms were also assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ)-4 [19]. Participants were required to rate their frequency of depressed mood, unenthusiasm/disinterest, tenseness/restlessness, and tiredness/lethargy on a four-point Likert scale (0 = not at all, 3 = nearly every day). A total score of ≥6 was considered positive for depression/anxiety disorders, with ≥3 for items 1 and 2 indicating depression and ≥3 for items 3 and 4 indicating anxiety [20].

To integrate mental health with CVH metrics, we developed an Extended LE8 score. The mental health score was calculated as the average of anxiety and depression scores (present = 0 points, absent = 100 points):(2)MHscore=scoreanxiety+scoredepression2

Participants were stratified into two CVH categories based on their LE8 scores: poor (<50) and intermediate-to-optimal (≥50). Mental health status was further classified into four groups: no disorder, anxiety only, depression only, and comorbid anxiety-depression.

Outcomes

Cardiovascular outcomes were classified using ICD-10 codes to maintain consistency across the study population, which were defined as the first occurrence (incident event) of a cardiovascular condition. The primary outcome was a composite endpoint incorporating CVD status and follow-up time in participants with coronary heart disease (CHD), heart failure (HF), stroke, and CVH. Secondary outcomes included individual cardiovascular conditions: CHD, HF, stroke, and CVH. Participants with incomplete baseline data or missing follow-up information, as well as those with pre-existing CVD (CHD, HF, or stroke) at baseline, were excluded from the analysis.

Covariates

Baseline age was calculated from the dates of birth and baseline assessment. Sex was self-reported by participants. Deprivation (area-based socioeconomic status) was determined using the Townsend score based on the postcode of residence. Ethnicity was self-reported and classified into two groups: white and others. Frequency of alcohol consumption was self-reported at baseline and categorized as daily/almost daily, 3–4 times per week, once/twice per week, 1–3 times per month, special occasions only, and never. Total daily activity status was estimated by physical activity points derived from the UK Biobank. The covariates were incorporated into COX regression model.

For remaining covariates, median imputation was applied to continuous variables (e.g., blood pressure), and mode imputation was used for categorical variables (e.g., alcohol intake frequency, ethnicity). Subgroup analyses were conducted based on mental health status (anxiety, depression) and CVH status (LE8 score <50 or ≥ 50).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using Cox proportional hazards regression models to assess the relationship between mental health status and cardiovascular outcomes, adjusting for age, sex, ethnicity, education, diabetes, and alcohol consumption. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were used to visualize the survival differences, and Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves were employed to evaluate the predictive performance of incorporating mental health status into cardiovascular event predictions. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software Version 4.1.0.

Sensitivity analysis

To test the statistical assumption of effect homogeneity and to formally quantify the potential effect modification by LE8 score, we conducted a pre-specified sensitivity analysis. The interaction analysis was chosen because our primary model assumes that the magnitude of the association between mental health status and cardiovascular outcomes is consistent across all LE8 strata. The sensitivity analysis challenges this assumption by incorporating a multiplicative interaction term into the multivariable Cox proportional hazards model, representing the product of the categorical “mental health group” and “LE8 group” (poor, intermediate to optimal) variables. A p-value <0.05 for this interaction term was considered statistically significant evidence of effect modification, thus indicating that the assumption of homogeneity was violated and that the effect of mental health status is indeed dependent on the level of baseline cardiovascular health.

Results

Descriptive demographic information

The characteristics of both study cohorts are showed in Table S1. The participants were classified into four groups based on cardiovascular and mental health status: Both cardiovascularly and mentally healthy (n = 260,602); Mentally healthy but cardiovascularly unhealthy (n = 10,000); Cardiovascularly healthy but mentally unhealthy (n = 12,781); Both cardiovascularly and mentally unhealthy (n = 7395). The mean age was 56.5 years, and 56.1 % were male. White participants accounted for 89.63 % of the population. In the dual-healthy group, 33.43 % had a university or college degree. The group with both cardiovascular and mental health impairments had the highest prevalence of hypertension (51.44 %) and the lowest physical activity level (78.18 min per week).

Cox proportional hazards regression analysis

Compared to the cardiovascular and mentally healthy group, participants with comorbid depression and anxiety had significantly increased risks across all cardiovascular outcomes: CHD (HR 1.73, 95 % CI 1.63–1.84), HF (HR 2.67, 95 % CI 2.44–2.93), stroke (HR 1.76, 95 % CI 1.54–2.02), cardiovascular death (HR 2.43, 95 % CI 2.01–2.93), and the composite outcome (HR 1.85, 95 % CI 1.74–1.97), all with p-values <0.001 (Table S2). Kaplan-Meier curves (Fig. 3, Fig. 4) showed survival probabilities for the four groups defined by cardiovascular and mental health status. The group with both cardiovascular and mental health maintained the highest survival rate (82.12 % event-free at 12 years). The log-rank test demonstrated significant differences in survival distributions between groups (p < 0.001), highlighting the substantial impact of combined cardiovascular and mental health impairments on survival outcomes.Fig. 4Kaplan-Meier curves.Fig. 4

Kaplan-Meier curves.

Subgroup analyses stratified by CVH score (<50 points, indicating poor CVH) revealed differential risk patterns based on mental health status (Cohort 2 of Table S5). Individuals with both anxiety and depression exhibited elevated risks for the composite endpoint (HR 1.463, 95 % CI 1.307–1.637), CHD (HR 1.399, 95 % CI 1.250–1.565), HF (HR 1.923, 95 % CI 1.636–2.260), and stroke (HR 1.324, 95 % CI 1.017–1.725); however, the association with cardiovascular death was not statistically significant (HR 1.039, 95 % CI 0.714–1.511) in this subgroup. Those with anxiety alone showed increased risks for CHD (HR 1.265, 95 % CI 1.123–1.425) and HF (HR 1.475, 95 % CI 1.234–1.763), while depression alone was associated with increased risks of CHD (HR 1.336, 95 % CI 1.221–1.462), HF (HR 1.602, 95 % CI 1.399–1.834), and stroke (HR 1.322, 95 % CI 1.077–1.622). These findings suggest that poor mental health acts synergistically with suboptimal CVH to compound disease risk, with comorbid conditions often demonstrating the most pronounced effects among the statistically significant outcomes.

In the subgroup with a cardiovascular score (LE8) of 50 or above (Cohort 1 in Table S5), mental health status plays an even more significant role in CVH. In this group, the “only anxiety” group had a HR of 0.953 (95 % CI 0.749–1.213), while all other groups had HRs greater than 1. This indicates that in the LE8 ≥ 50 subgroup, anxiety and depression are both risk factors for CVDs. Specifically, anxiety alone did not appear to increase the risk significantly, but depression and the combination of anxiety and depression both showed increased risks for cardiovascular outcomes.

Results of sensitivity analysis

Results of the sensitivity analysis are exhibited in Table S6. Interaction analysis revealed a significant statistical interaction between LE8 score grouping and mental health status in predicting composite endpoint events (interaction P < 0.005). Specifically, this effect modification was primarily driven by the “Intermediate” LE8 score group. Data showed that in the “Poor” group, the hazard ratio (HR) for anxiety and depression comorbidity compared to those without mental disorders was 1.52 (95 % CI 1.44–1.60); In contrast, within the “Intermediate” group, the HR did not decrease as expected with improved cardiovascular health. Instead, due to a significant positive interaction (interaction term HR 1.27, p < 0.005), the net effect HR was 1.58. In contrast, within the “Optimal” LE8 score group, all interaction terms between mental health status and LE8 grouping were non-significant (e.g., interaction term for anxiety and depression comorbidity with the “Optimal” group, p = 0.67). and the risk ratio for anxiety and depression comorbidity within this group attenuated to 1.03 (95 % CI 0.78–1.17), showing no difference from the healthy reference group. A similar interaction pattern was confirmed in analyses for coronary heart disease (interaction P < 0.005) and heart failure (interaction P < 0.005).

Discussion

Through the analysis of UK Biobank data, our study has assessed the integration of anxiety and depression into the established LE8 assessment model, culminating in the development of a novel CVH evaluation system grounded in the biopsychosocial medical model. This advancement represents a paradigm shift in cardiovascular risk stratification by systematically incorporating psychological dimensions into conventional biomedical metrics (Fig. 5).Fig. 5Graphic abstract.Fig. 5

The relationship between LE8 score and CVD risk has been well-documented in previous literature. A 2023 study using UK Biobank data emphasized that interventions targeting high-risk populations, even those resulting in a modest increase in LE8 scores [21], can yield substantial improvements in survival outcomes. Additionally, another 2023 study based on the NHANES database identified a negative correlation between LE8 score and both all-cause mortality and CVD-specific mortality [6]. However, the inherent limitation of the LE8 assessment model, characterized by its exclusive focus on physiological parameters, substantially constrains its clinical applicability in populations with comorbid anxiety or depression.

Numerous previous studies have consistently demonstrated a significant bidirectional causal relationship between depression, anxiety, and CVDs. A Mendelian randomization study conducted by Li et al. revealed that depression exhibits causal associations with increased risks of CHD and myocardial infarction (MI), with lipid levels and smoking behavior potentially serving as mediating factors [12]. Hung et al. found significantly higher diagnostic rates of anxiety and depression in patients with coronary artery spasm compared to those with CHD and healthy populations (OR: 2.29, 95 % CI: 2.14–2.45, p < 0.001) [14]. Nicholson et al. conducted a meta-analysis incorporating 54 observational studies, demonstrating that depression not only increases the prevalence of CHD but also elevates recurrence risk and mortality rates in CHD patients [10]. Recent prospective cohort research by Nakada et al. utilizing the UK Biobank data has yielded similar findings [18]. Notably, CADs may reciprocally contribute to the development of depression and anxiety. A prospective cohort study investigating the relationship between cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) health and depression/anxiety risk analyzed 344,956 UK Biobank participants, stratifying CKM syndrome into five stages (0–4). Results demonstrated significantly elevated depression risks in stage 1, 2–3, and 4 participants compared to stage 0 individuals [17]. Based on high-quality evidence from previous research, the AHA issued a scientific statement in 2014, formally recommending recognition of depression as a risk factor for adverse prognosis in acute coronary syndrome patients [16].

The methodological pivot in our study to a stratified analysis was a consequence of our evaluation of the baseline model. We first tested a simple additive hypothesis: that mental health functions as an independent, summative ninth factor (the “LE10” score). Statistically, this assumption implies that the extended LE10 score should offer substantially superior discriminative power than the LE8 alone.

However, our ROC analysis refuted this simple hypothesis; the analysis yielded only a minimal improvement in the composite outcome AUC (0.632–0.643). It strongly suggests that the underlying relationship is not additive, compelling a test to assess how baseline cardiovascular health (LE8) modifies the effect of mental health on future CVD risk. The “effect modification” cannot be assessed by AUC; a time-to-event analysis is required. Thus, our stratified Cox analysis was the next step. In related literature, for instance, the 2024 NHANES analysis of LE8 and depression (Shen and Zou) also had to abandon an additive model, as their dose-response analysis revealed a significant interaction by sex: the association was linear for females but nonlinear for males [22]. In the subgroup with LE8 scores ≥50, we observed that depression alone was associated with an increased risk of CVD mortality, while anxiety alone did not reach statistical significance. Our sensitivity analysis (interaction analysis) indicates that among individuals with a LE8 score of “intermediate,” the additional cardiovascular risk attributable to anxiety and depression exceeds that observed in the “poor” group with poorer health status. This suggests that this population may be in a window of physiological vulnerability, where the compensatory capacity of their cardiovascular system is insufficient to withstand the additional impact of psychosocial stress. Crucially, once an individual achieves “optimal” CVH status, the additional risk attributable to psychological disorders is nearly eliminated (e.g., HR for composite endpoints drops to 1.03). This marked reduction in risk suggests that attaining and maintaining optimal cardiovascular health may not only reduce baseline risk but also build robust physiological reserves capable of effectively countering the pathophysiological damage from common psychosomatic comorbidities. This finding is consistent with research by Alanna M. Chamberlain et al. [23] Additionally, in a 2025 study by Shinya Nakada et al. utilizing the UK Biobank dataset, the use of depression symptom scores resulted in the most significant improvements in both the C-index (0.005, 95 % CI: 0.004–0.006) and specificity (with a difference of 0.89 %) [18]. Consequently, higher LE8 scores diminish the predictive capacity of anxiety symptoms for cardiovascular mortality risk. Existing evidence has not sufficiently elucidated this phenomenon, necessitating further high-quality investigations to elucidate the molecular biological mechanisms underlying this interaction.

In earlier reviews, Joynt et al. attempted to elucidate the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying the depression-CVD relationship from a biological perspective [24]. Depression may accelerate CVD progression through activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis. Elevated cortisol levels promote atherosclerosis and hypertension development while exacerbating vascular endothelial cell damage. Concurrently, behavioral patterns in depressed patients likely contribute to the development or exacerbation of traditional cardiac risk factors, including smoking, obesity, and hypercholesterolemia. Psychological stress induced by depression may activate cardiac sympathetic innervation, subsequently leading to reduced blood flow, increased heart rate, left ventricular hypertrophy, MI, and sudden cardiac death [[25], [26], [27]]. Among healthy elderly populations, depressive symptoms may precede and amplify specific inflammatory processes associated with CHD, thereby increasing CHD risk. Stewart et al. demonstrated in the Pittsburgh Healthy Heart Project cohort that baseline Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II) scores predicted 6-year changes in IL-6 levels (beta = 0.18, p = 0.01, DeltaR (2) = 0.02), with weak bidirectional relationships also observed between BDI-II and CRP levels [28].

Individuals with anxiety disorders may exhibit suboptimal dietary adherence to cardioprotective foods or engage in tobacco use initiation as maladaptive coping strategies for negative affect [29]. Accumulating evidence from studies has established that anxiety-induced amplification of neuroimmune pathways, particularly involving stress-elicited sympathoadrenal activation and cytokine-mediated inflammatory cascades, potentiates the development of cardiometabolic risk determinants [30,31].

Our study is designed with the objective of exploring the combined effect of LE8 scores and mental health status on the prediction of CVD risk; it is the first to assess the independent contribution of psychological issues to future cardiovascular risk. Additionally, we employed subgroup stratification based on LE8 scores to comprehensively evaluate the potential impact of mental health beyond the eight health parameters on cardiovascular risk. This approach advocates prioritizing mental health management in CVD prevention at the public health level.

Strengths and limitations

Our study conducts a joint analysis of LE8 scores and mental health conditions to investigate their combined impact on CVH risk factors. Furthermore, we stratify the population to evaluate how anxiety and depression affect future cardiovascular risk across groups with varying CVH statuses.

Our data allows us to precisely target potential screening candidates from a large and heterogeneous general population to a specific subgroup with more pronounced risk characteristics. From an evidence-based perspective, this refined risk stratification serves as the cornerstone for developing any effective public health strategy. By avoiding unnecessary interventions in low-risk populations, it significantly reduces the potential costs and resource consumption associated with future screening implementations. Therefore, the results of this study should be viewed as an intermediate step. They provide a robust associative foundation and population screening target for designing subsequent randomized controlled trials (RCTs) targeting this high-risk subgroup. This ensures that future research investments can more effectively validate the clinical efficacy and cost-effectiveness of screening and intervention strategies.

Regarding limitations, first, the dietary and alcohol consumption data for the LE8 participants were self-reported at baseline, and potential lifestyle changes over time may introduce bias into our findings. Second, the average LE8 score assumes equal weight for each health parameter, whereas different risk factors may hold varying levels of significance for distinct outcomes. Third, the data utilized in this study are derived from the UK Biobank, which predominantly reflects the characteristics of populations in specific regions of the UK, thereby limiting the generalizability of our results. Additionally, the dataset used for analysis did not include information on potential confounders such as medication use (e.g., antidepressants, statins) or family history, precluding direct adjustment for these variables. However, our findings demonstrated robustness in sensitivity analyses, with an E-value of 3.10, indicating that a very strong unmeasured confounding factor would be required to fully explain the observed association [32] Crucially, as an observational study, our findings establish an association but cannot prove causation. Therefore, we explicitly caution against interpreting these results as a direct recommendation for widespread screening policies. Finally, our evaluation of the “Extended LE10” model primarily focused on discrimination (AUC). Nonetheless, clinical reclassification metrics, such as the Net Reclassification Index (NRI) or Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI), received no attention. Methodologically, our ROC analysis demonstrated that an additive model (the LE10 score) yielded only minimal improvement in AUC (from 0.632 to 0.643). This finding refuted our “straw man” hypothesis that mental health acts as a simple additive factor, compelling us to pivot our analysis to dissecting effect modification. While our manuscript's focus was therefore to firmly establish the existence of this epidemiological interaction, we encourage that future studies aiming to translate these findings into a clinical screening tool should evaluate risk reclassification using NRI and IDI.

Conclusion

Our study evaluated the impact of anxiety and depression on future cardiovascular risk in populations with different CVH statuses and attempts to quantify this effect. We found that anxiety and depression are risk factors for CVD, regardless of whether individuals have healthy or unhealthy cardiovascular conditions, particularly when both anxiety and depression are present. In addition to traditional factors such as diet and laboratory tests, attention should also be given to mental health in the prevention of CVDs.
