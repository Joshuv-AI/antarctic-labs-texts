Immunogenic cell death as a cornerstone for combination therapies with immune checkpoint blockade
Authors: Yubo Niu, Shuangli Zhu, Kai Fu, Yongtong Lai, Can Pan, Yan Yang, Sijia Li, Xueping Wang, Kenneth Kin Wah To, Fang Wang, Liwu Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune checkpoint blockade (ICB) has significantly advanced tumor therapy, yet its overall response rates remain limited and are often accompanied by immune-related adverse effects. Immunogenic cell death (ICD), a specialized form of regulated cell death, elicits antitumor immunity through the release of damage-associated molecular patterns and cytokines. This review systematically examines the mechanisms by which ICD enhances the efficacy of ICB therapy and evaluates its potential for clinical translation. ICD facilitates the recruitment of dendritic cells into the tumor microenvironment via “find me” signals and, through the exposure of “eat me” signals, enables dendritic cell-mediated phagocytosis and antigen presentation of tumor cells. This cascade effectively transforms immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” tumors, augmenting cytotoxic T lymphocyte infiltration and function, thereby improving ICB therapeutic outcomes. Additionally, ICD promotes the formation of tertiary lymphoid structures, which further remodel the tumor microenvironment and support sustained immune surveillance. Although preclinical studies underscore the synergistic potential of combining ICD inducers with ICB, clinical trial outcomes have been variable, with efficacy influenced by tumor heterogeneity, treatment sequencing, and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Future investigations should focus on optimizing ICD induction protocols, developing specific biomarkers, and designing personalized combination strategies to enable more precise and effective immunotherapeutic interventions in oncology.Keywords: Immunogenic cell death, Immune checkpoint blockade, Tertiary lymphoid structures, Tumor microenvironment

Introduction

Cancer represents a defining health, societal, and economic challenge of the 21st century, accounting for approximately 16.8% of global mortality and 22.8% of deaths from non-communicable diseases [1]. Cancer contributes to about 30.3% of premature mortality worldwide from noncommunicable diseases among individuals aged 30 to 69 years and ranks among the top three causes of mortality in this demographic across 177 of 183 countries [2]. During the preceding ten years, immune checkpoint blockade (ICB)-based therapies have become integral to modern oncology, fundamentally transforming the clinical management of numerous malignancies. The therapeutic principle of ICB involves disrupting tumor-mediated immune evasion, thereby reinvigorating anergic or exhausted T lymphocytes [3]. For instance, antibodies targeting the programmed death 1 (PD1)/PD1 ligand (PD-L1) pathway block this immune checkpoint mechanism, thereby alleviating the inhibitory signals on T lymphocytes and enhancing their antitumor effector functions, which results in the eradication of tumor cells [3]. Similarly, anti-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA-4) antibodies inhibit this key negative regulator of early T cell activation [4, 5]. Consequently, ICB has been established as first-line standard therapy for multiple advanced malignancies, substantially enhancing patient survival rates and overall quality of life. This is exemplified by the PD-1 inhibitors such as nivolumab in lung cancer [6], and by anti-CTLA-4 plus anti-PD-1 antibodies combination in melanoma, which induces durable response in approximately 50% of patients, markedly outperforming conventional chemotherapy [7].

Despite these advances, significant limitations remain. Only 30% of patients benefit from ICB-based therapy, and the treatment is often associated with notable immune-related adverse events [8, 9]. Therefore, strategies to broaden efficacy and mitigate toxicity are urgently needed. One promising approach involves the deliberate triggering of immunogenic cell death (ICD), which may enhance tumor immunogenicity and synergize with ICB to improve therapeutic outcomes [10].

Chemotherapy and radiotherapy continue to serve as fundamental modalities in cancer treatment. Beyond their direct cytotoxic effects, a growing body of evidence highlights their significant immunomodulatory potential [11–14]. This capacity is exemplified by the concept of immunogenic cell death (ICD), a term introduced in 2005 to describe a functionally distinct form of cell death that stimulates an adaptive T lymphocyte-mediated immune response against cancer [15]. The immunogenic nature of certain cytotoxic modalities is supported by key observations, where specific chemotherapeutic agents demonstrate antitumor efficacy in immunocompetent murine models but not in immunodeficient counterparts [16–18]. Moreover, vaccination with cancer cells killed by particular drugs can protect mice against subsequent tumor challenge [19, 20]. These findings collectively indicate that therapy-induced dying cells can elicit durable, protective anti-tumor immune memory [15]. Critically, both chemotherapy and radiotherapy can trigger ICD and instigate broader immune responses that reshape the tumor microenvironment (TME), thereby influencing the subsequent ICB therapeutic efficacy.

This review provides a comprehensive examination of ICD in oncology. We elucidate the mechanistic underpinnings of ICD and examine how tumor cells develop resistance to this process. The discussion then shifts to the role of ICD in remodeling the TME, with a specific focus on its contribution to the formation of tertiary lymphoid structures. Additionally, we highlight therapeutic strategies aimed at inducing ICD to enhance ICB efficacy. Finally, we address current challenges and future directions for harnessing ICD to counteract immunosuppression and optimize clinical outcomes.

Key aspects of ICD

The concept of ICD

ICD symbolizes a distinct form of regulated cell death characterized by the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) from stressed or dying tumor cells following exposure to ICD inducers [21]. These signals enable the immune system to recognize and target tumors by enhancing the capacity of antigen-presenting cells (APCs) to capture and present tumor antigens. This process initiates a potent, host-specific antitumor immune response that directly eradicates tumor cells while also establishing immunological memory (Fig. 1) [22]. The effectiveness of ICD in generating such a response depends on three crucial determinants: antigenicity, adjuvanticity, and a permissive TME [15, 23–25]. Antigenicity requires that dying cancer cells present tumor antigens recognizable by T lymphocytes [26]. Adjuvanticity involves the release of immunostimulatory signals, primarily DAMPs and pro-inflammatory cytokines, from stressed and dying tumor cells, which are essential for robust APC activation [22, 27]. Without sufficient antigenicity, ICD may elicit only a non-specific inflammatory response that fails to engage adaptive immunity [28]. Conversely, antigen presentation without adequate adjuvant signals often leads to T lymphocyte tolerance rather than activation [15, 26, 29, 30]. Finally, the TME must facilitate APC activation, initiate adaptive immunity, support T cell recruitment, and enable their effector functions within the tumor milieu [15, 31].

ICD inducers

ICD inducers are agents or therapeutic approaches that initiate this process, representing a promising strategy in cancer treatment [33]. Common ICD inducers in oncology include: (1) conventional chemotherapeutic agents such as anthracyclines (e.g., mitoxantrone, epirubicin, doxorubicin, idarubicin), oxaliplatin, cyclophosphamide, paclitaxel, bleomycin, 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), actinomycin D, and teniposide [16–18, 34–38]; (2) oncolytic viruses [39–41]; (3) molecules with oncolytic properties [42, 43]; (4) epigenetic modulators such as bromodomain inhibitors and histone deacetylase inhibitors [44, 45]; (5) endocrine therapies such as mifepristone [46]; (6) targeted anticancer agents such as crizotinib, cetuximab, CDK inhibitors, PARP inhibitors, and bortezomib [47–51]; and (7) a range of physical interventions, including radiotherapy, photodynamic therapy, extracorporeal photochemotherapy, severe cytotoxic heat shock, high hydrostatic pressure, nanopulse stimulation, and irreversible electroporation [52–61].

ICD inducers are commonly classified according to their distinct mechanisms for triggering endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and the subsequent release of DAMPs [22, 62–64]. ER stress activates the unfolded protein response, notably involving the phosphorylation of eukaryotic initiation factor 2α (eIF2α) via protein kinase RNA-like ER kinase (PERK) [65]. This pathway reduces IκB levels, thereby enhancing NF-κB activation [66]. Type I ICD inducers, such as radiotherapy and chemotherapeutics including paclitaxel, oxaliplatin, and anthracyclines, elicit ICD without directly targeting the ER [67–72]. In contrast, type II ICD inducers, such as photodynamic therapy and oncolytic viruses, specifically provoke ER stress to induce cell death [73–76]. Collectively, ICD inducers encompass a heterogeneous spectrum of chemical, microbial, and physical modalities [77].

Beyond the induction of ICD, some conventional chemotherapeutic agents can directly remodel the immunosuppressive TME by targeting specific cell populations. For instance, liposomal anthracyclines and nab-paclitaxel can deplete M2-like tumor-associated macrophages [78, 79], while anthracyclines and 5-FU reduce the aggregation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) [80, 81]. Additionally, cyclophosphamide selectively depletes or inhibits regulatory T cells (Tregs) [82, 83].

Key molecular mechanisms of ICD

Decades of research have elucidated the core molecular mechanisms of ICD. A pivotal initiating event is ER stress, frequently triggered by the generation of reactive oxygen species [84–88]. This stress initiates a defined cascade that contributes to the controlled exposure and release of DAMPs from dying cancer cells (Table 1). These danger signals include molecules translocated to the cell surface, such as calreticulin (CRT) and heat-shock proteins (HSPs) [55, 108–115]; as well as those secreted into the extracellular space, such as high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), adenosine triphosphate (ATP), and annexin A1 (ANXA1) [55, 67, 77, 116–124]. Functionally, these DAMPs orchestrate a robust immune response. ATP and HMGB1 function as the “find me” signals, recruiting APCs to the tumor sites [125]. Conversely, CRT and HSPs function as “eat me” signals, enhancing the phagocytosis of dying tumor cells by APCs [125]. Furthermore, upon engagement with pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) on immune cells, DAMPs promote the activation and maturation of APCs, thereby bridging innate and adaptive immunity [126].

The release of ATP, HMGB1, and ANXA1

The release of specific DAMPs is a defining feature of ICD. Among them, ATP is an early and critical signal within the TME. Its release from dying tumor cells occurs primarily via autophagy, which increases intracellular ATP levels and packages it into cytoplasmic vesicles [127]. These vesicles are trafficked to autolysosomes via autophagy-related genes [128]. Then, autolysosomes fuse with the plasma membrane, coupled with lysosome-associated membrane protein 1 (LAMP1)-mediated lysosomal membrane permeabilization, permitting the extracellular release of ATP [128]. Alternatively, ATP release also involves caspase-mediated activation of the membrane channel ANXA1 [128, 129].

Extracellular ATP serves as a potent “find me” signal. Dendritic cells (DCs) and their precursors detect extracellular ATP via purinergic receptors, leading to their recruitment and activation. The P2Y2 receptor (P2RY2) mediates DCs chemotaxis [130, 131], guiding their migration toward apoptotic cells [89], while the P2 × 7 receptor (P2RX7) is involved in DCs activation [90]. Specifically, ATP binding to P2RX7 triggers the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to caspase-1 (CASP1) maturation and the secretion of IL-1β and IL-18 [90]. Moreover, the ATP metabolite adenosine diphosphate enhances DC macropinocytosis via the P2Y12 receptor (P2RY12), further promoting ICD [132].

HMGB1 serves a pivotal nuclear function by maintaining nucleosome architecture, modulating transcriptional activity, and facilitating DNA repair mechanisms. Beyond these functions within the nucleus, HMGB1 is significantly implicated in processes such as inflammation, cellular differentiation, and tumor metastasis [120]. Upon induction of ICD in tumor cells, alterations in nuclear and plasma membrane permeability occur, enabling the extracellular release of HMGB1. Once released into the extracellular environment, HMGB1 engages with toll-like receptors 2 and 4 (TLR2/4) or the receptor for advanced glycation end products, triggering downstream signaling pathways [133–136]. Its engagement with TLR4, in particular, is crucial for promoting DC maturation, pro-inflammatory cytokine production, and efficient cross-presentation of tumor antigens [91, 92].

ANXA1 is another DAMP secreted by tumor cells undergoing ICD. It directs DCs migration to the tumor site by interacting with formyl peptide receptor 1 (FPR1) on DCs [93]. Additionally, the exposure of filamentous actin (F-actin) on dead tumor cells engages the DCs receptor DNGR-1, initiating a Syk kinase-dependent signaling cascade that enhances antigen cross-presentation and cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) activation [94].

CRT and HSP70 exposure

The exposure of ER chaperones is a hallmark of ICD. During ICD, ER stress activates the integrated stress response, resulting in the phosphorylation of eIF2α by the kinase PERK [137]. This event initiates a cascade involving CASP8 and B-cell receptor-associated protein 31, which facilitates the translocation of ER chaperones to the cell surface [55, 108, 138]. This translocation process requires anterograde transport from the ER to the Golgi apparatus, mediated by vesicle-associated membrane protein 1 and synaptosome-associated protein 25 [108, 125, 139]. Upon reaching the cell surface, the ER chaperone CRT acts as a potent “eat me” signal. Its binding to the low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1) on DCs enhances the phagocytosis of dying tumor cells [95]. Furthermore, CRT interaction with the natural killer (NK) cell receptor NKp46 can augment NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity against tumors [96].

Unlike CRT, HSP70 translocates to the plasma membrane predominantly via a non-classical, lipid raft-dependent mechanism. As HSP70 lacks a conventional ER or Golgi-dependent secretory signal peptide, its membrane localization bypasses the classical secretory pathway [140]. Under cellular stress, HSP70 is recruited to detergent-resistant domains (lipid-rafts), where it integrates into the plasma membrane through interactions with phospholipids such as phosphatidylserine [140–142]. This translocation is sensitive to cholesterol-depleting agents like methyl-β-cyclodextrin but is unaffected by classical secretion inhibitors such as Brefeldin A [140, 143]. Membrane integration may involve HSP70 oligomerization, which can influence membrane architecture and ion channel function [144, 145]. HSP70 is also found on tumor-derived exosomes, contributing to immune modulation [146, 147]. As a DAMP, HSP70 plays a dual immunostimulatory role. It activates DCs and promotes their maturation via TLR4 signaling, thereby initiating anti-tumor immune response [97]. Moreover, membrane-anchored HSP70, via its exposed TKD peptide, can directly engage NK cells, stimulating their migration and cytotoxic activity, leading to targeted tumor cell killing [148–150].

Cytokine induction and signaling in ICD

The ICD of cancer cells stimulates a robust cytokine response, both through direct secretion and by inducing production from neighboring immune and stromal cells within the TME [151–156]. A central mechanism in this process is the initiation of the type I interferon (IFN) response. A subset of ICD inducers inflicts damage on nuclear and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), leading to the accumulation of nucleic acids within the cytoplasm. Cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) detects cytosolic mtDNA and double-stranded DNA (dsDNA), resulting in the production of the second messenger cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP). cGAMP activates the STING pathway, triggering phosphorylation cascades with TANK-binding kinase 1 and interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3). This signaling cascade ultimately leads to the secretion of type I IFNs [28, 98, 99].

Type I IFNs interact with the IFNAR receptor on mature DCs, increase the expression of co-stimulatory molecules, and promote DCs migration to lymph nodes, enhancing CTLs-mediated antitumor immunity [28, 100–102]. Moreover, type I IFNs activate tumoricidal macrophages and attenuate the immunosuppressive function of Tregs [77]. In addition, cytosolic RNA can engage with intracellular TLR3, triggering the release of cytokines such as type I IFNs, C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 9 (CXCL9), and CXCL10 [28]. Notably, CXCL10 binds to the CXCR3 receptor expressed on activated CTLs, recruiting them to the tumor site to execute targeted tumor cell killing [103].

Immunosuppressive DAMPs

While DAMPs typically stimulate immunity, certain species can paradoxically suppress immune responses (Table 1). For instance, translationally controlled tumor protein (TCTP) released from tumor cells recruits MDSCs to the TME, thereby enhancing local immunosuppression and promoting tumor progression [104]. CASP3 activation during cell death can also promote immunosuppression by stimulating the release of prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) [157], an effect that can be mitigated by co-administration of the cyclooxygenase-2 inhibitor celecoxib [158]. Furthermore, CASP3 signaling promotes the secretion of lysophosphatidylcholine, a lipid mediator that attracts phagocytes for the immunologically silent clearance of apoptotic debris, thus promoting tolerance [159–161]. Additionally, subcutaneous tumor implantation in mice activated the hypothalamus and elevated pituitary synthesis of α-melanocyte-stimulating hormone (α-MSH), which promoted myelopoiesis in bone marrow progenitor cells via interaction with MC5R, resulting in myeloid cell accumulation, immunosuppression, and enhanced tumor progression [105]. Other DAMPs such as S100 proteins have been shown to facilitate tumor metastasis through engagement with TLR4 [106], while the cyclophilin-CD147 interaction has been implicated in promoting tumor invasion [107].

Tumor antigens

The efficacy of ICD hinges on the presence of identifiable tumor antigens, which are generally categorized into tumor neoantigens (TNAs) and tumor-associated antigens (TAAs). TNAs predominantly originate from genomic alterations specific to the tumor. Non-synonymous point mutations and frameshift insertions or deletions generate abnormal proteins that are processed and presented as novel peptides by major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules, enabling their identification as foreign entities by the immune system [162–167]. TNAs can also arise from post-translational modifications, such as oxidation, phosphorylation, and citrullination of normal proteins [168, 169]. Their structural divergence from native self-epitopes allows TNAs to elicit de novo T-cell responses, bypassing central and peripheral tolerance and making them promising prime targets for immunotherapy [164, 166, 170].

In contrast, TAAs are endogenous proteins that exhibit abnormal expression patterns within tumor cells, often through significant overexpression or ectopic presentation. Although TAAs are found in some normal tissues (particularly in immune-privileged sites or during specific developmental stages), their dysregulated expression in cancer can render them immunogenic [171–173]. Importantly, ICD-inducing therapies can enhance antigenicity of tumor cells. Both chemotherapy and radiotherapy can upregulate MHC-I expression on tumor cells [24, 25]. Certain cytotoxic agents have been shown to amplify immunogenicity by increasing components of the antigen-processing machinery, such as NOD-like receptor family CARD domain containing 5 and transporter associated with antigen processing 1, thereby improving peptide presentation [25].

A permissive TME

The immunological cascade initiated by ICD can remodel the TME, promoting the infiltration of myeloid and lymphoid cells and converting an immunologically “cold” tumor into a “hot” one [89]. Notably, this process can be self-amplifying. The cytolytic functionality of tumor-infiltrating CD8 + CTLs can itself initiate ICD in neighboring cancer cells, thus promoting epitope spreading and intensifying the local antitumor immune response [174–176]. However, the efficacy of ICD is critically dependent on a permissive TME. Immunosuppressive elements can severely limit its efficacy. For instance, a high density of DCs expressing indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 creates a tolerogenic milieu that impedes immune activation [177]. Similarly, extensive infiltration by Tregs suppresses CTL function and priming through mechanisms involving CTLA-4 and IL-10 [178]. Moreover, a desmoplastic stroma characterized by pronounced fibrosis presents a physical barrier that impedes CTLs infiltration, allowing tumors to evade immune attack [179].

In summary, the effective induction of ICD and subsequent antitumor immunity relies on three interconnected pillars: (1) adjuvanticity, provided by DAMPs that stimulate innate immunity; (2) antigenicity, supplied by immunogenic tumor antigens; and (3) a permissive TME that supports rather than suppresses effector cell function. The integration of these components is essential for generating a potent, adaptive immune response and immunological memory.

Resistance of tumor cells to ICD

Tumor evolution selects for subclones with enhanced proliferative capacity, resistance to cell death, and immune evasion properties (Fig. 2) [180]. Resistance to ICD can be primary, stemming from pre-existing defects in ICD-related signaling pathways, or acquired, developing during treatment in initially responsive tumors [181].

Genetic alterations are a fundamental source of resistance. Genetic alterations in oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes, such as the frequently disrupted p53 pathway, can directly impair cell death execution [182, 183]. While apoptotic pathways are often defective in cancer cells [184, 185], the machinery for mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP) typically remains intact. However, in the absence of appropriate immunogenic signals, MOMP generally leads to non-immunogenic cell death [184]. Furthermore, CASP3 activation can promote immune evasion by cleaving and inactivating cGAS, which suppresses type I interferon responses to cytosolic mtDNA or dsDNA [186–190]. CASP3 also facilitates the synthesis of PGE2 and the externalization of phosphatidylserine, aiding the silent phagocytic clearance of tumor cells and fostering an immunosuppressive TME [160, 191].

Tumor cells actively suppress “eat-me” signals such as surface-exposed CRT. Strategies include reducing CRT exposure through interaction with stanniocalcin 1 (STC1) to impair phagocytosis by APCs [192], upregulating the immune checkpoint molecule CD47 to directly antagonize CRT-mediated phagocytic signaling [193, 194], and overexpressing proteins such as V-set domain-containing T-cell activation inhibitor 1 or ER oxidoreductase-1α [195, 196]. Clinically, a signature of low phospho-eIF2α and CRT alongside high STC1 and CD47 correlates with poor prognosis in multiple cancers [192, 193, 197].

Dysregulation of immunogenic metabolite release is another key resistance mechanism. Autophagy, often suppressed early in tumorigenesis, reduces ATP release and impairs ICD [198]. For instance, in breast cancer, hyperactivation of insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor signaling, which suppresses autophagy, is linked to poor clinical outcomes and weakened antitumor immune responses [199]. Within the TME, the ectonucleotidases CD39 and CD73, expressed by both tumor and stromal cells, hydrolyze extracellular ATP to immunosuppressive adenosine [119, 200]. Overexpression of CD39, CD73, and adenosine receptors is associated with adverse prognosis, highlighting their therapeutic relevance [201].

Tumors can evade ICD by modulating key DAMPs. Secretion of gelsolin inhibits the DNGR1-F-actin interaction, and high gelsolin expression correlates with poor prognosis across various cancer types [202]. Downregulation of HMGB1 is common during tumor progression; while its loss impairs immunity, this may be overcome by local administration of TLR4 agonists [42, 203]. Additionally, certain tumors demonstrate reduced expression of ANXA1 [116]. Similarly, reduced expression of ANXA1, often coupled with loss-of-function mutations in its receptor FPR1, promotes tumorigenesis in murine models of breast and colon cancer [204, 205]. Resistance resulting from ANXA1/FPR1 loss can be reversed by systemic TLR3 agonists [205]. Finally, tumors broadly inhibit the type I IFN response. This is accomplished through downregulating the key signaling molecules like cGAS, STING1, IRF3, and IRF7 [206, 207], promoting degradation of immunostimulatory mtDNA and micronuclear DNA [53, 208, 209], or impairing downstream kinases such as Janus kinase 1 [210].

ICD and tertiary lymphoid structures

Tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) are ectopic, organized clusters of immune cells that form in non-lymphoid tissues, mirroring the architecture and function of secondary lymphoid organs (Fig. 3) [211]. The presence of TLS within the TME has been closely correlated with enhanced therapeutic outcomes following ICB treatment [211]. A mature TLS comprises T lymphocytes, B lymphocytes, plasma cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts, and follicular dendritic cells, spatially organized around specialized high endothelial venules (HEVs) [212–214]. These HEVs are pivotal for TLS neogenesis, facilitating lymphocyte extravasation via CD62L-mediated homing [215, 216].

Therapeutic induction of tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) and remodeling of the tumor microenvironment. Tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) are ectopic lymphoid organs that develop in non-lymphoid tissues, including within tumors. These organized structures comprise distinct T cell zones, enriched with T cells and mature DCs, adjacent to B cell follicles containing germinal centers. The germinal centers are populated by B cells, follicular dendritic cells (FDCs), follicular helper T cells (TFH cells), and macrophages. Stromal components such as high endothelial venules (HEVs) and cancer-associated fibroblasts are integral to the formation and maintenance of TLS. The induction of ICD represents a promising strategy to potentiate ICB and catalyze TLS formation in immunologically “cold” tumors (Table 2) [21]. Growing evidence links various ICD-inducing modalities to TLS development. In ovarian cancer, neoadjuvant chemotherapy potently induces ICD and TLS formation [217]. Similarly, doxorubicin promotes ICD and TLS in melanoma, while also upregulating PD-L1, enhancing combination immunotherapy efficacy [218]. In breast cancer, the therapeutic effect of cyclophosphamide is associated with its ability to facilitate TLS formation [220]. Radiotherapy also demonstrates this dual function. Carbon-ion radiation triggers ICD and TLS formation in preclinical melanoma models [219]. In lung adenocarcinoma, low-dose radiotherapy elicits ICD and TLS, an effect synergized by PD-1 inhibition to amplify antitumor immunity [221]. Beyond conventional therapies, oncolytic viruses can induce ICD in colorectal cancer (CRC) models, driving TLS formation via upregulation of TLS-associated chemokines (e.g., CXCL10), leading to robust B-cell infiltration and expansion of stem-like TCF1 + CD8+ T cells [225].

ICD induces the formation of tertiary lymphoid structures

Collectively, these findings indicate that ICD inducers, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and oncolytic virus, not only facilitate tumor cell death and the consequent release of TAAs and DAMPs but also contribute to the reconfiguration of the TME [231]. By enhancing the expression of key cytokines, they create a permissive niche for the initiation and maturation of TLS [232]. Thus, a functional synergy exists between ICD and TLS. ICD provides the antigenic substrate and inflammatory impetus while TLS offers an organized architectural niche for efficient antigen presentation, lymphocyte activation, and clonal expansion [217–219, 221, 225]. This interplay fosters a powerful and long-lasting antitumor immune response. Consequently, this synergistic relationship highlights the therapeutic potential of integrating ICD inducers with strategies that foster TLS formation to improve immunotherapeutic efficacy, especially in tumors characterized by an immunologically “cold” microenvironment.

Combination therapy: ICD inducers and ICB

ICD inducers stimulate the release of DAMPs from cancer cells, which subsequently promotes DCs maturation and improves antigen presentation. This process facilitates CD8 + CTLs priming and works synergistically with ICB to strengthen antitumor immune responses [233, 234]. Furthermore, ICD remodels the immunosuppressive TME by reducing Tregs and MDSCs accumulation and enhancing CTLs infiltration. This transformation effectively converts immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” ones [78–83]. The addition of ICB to this approach further supports the production of central memory T cells, alleviates T cell exhaustion, and promotes immunological memory, thereby facilitating sustained control over both primary and metastatic disease [10, 233–235]. ICD inducers transform immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” tumors by recruiting and activating dendritic cells (DCs) and cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs). Immune checkpoint blockade (e.g., monoclonal antibodies targeting PD-1 or its ligand PD-L1) functions to reinvigorate pre-existing but exhausted CTLs. The combination leverages complementary mechanisms: ICD generates a de novo tumor-specific T cell response, while checkpoint inhibition prevents its attenuation, thereby offering a potent strategy to enhance antitumor immunity and therapeutic efficacy. Preclinical studies

A substantial body of preclinical research has demonstrated pronounced synergistic antitumor effects from combining ICD inducers with ICB across diverse animal models. The underlying mechanism typically involves ICD-induced release of “find me” signals (e.g., CXCL10, ATP, and HMGB1) that recruit immune cells, and exposure of “eat me” signals (e.g., surface CRT) that facilitate the phagocytosis and processing of dying cancer cells by DCs [39]. This cascade activates a systemic antitumor immune response, enhances CD8 + T cell activity, and can lead to both local and abscopal effects [39]. In murine CT26 colon carcinoma, oxaliplatin-induced ICD results in CRT exposure (“eat me” signal) and T cell activation. When combined with ICB, this results in local tumor eradication, abscopal effects, immunological memory, and improved control of metastases [236]. Similarly, oxaliplatin can induce ICD, leading to the release of HMGB1 and upregulating chemokines such as CXCL9, CXCL10, and CCL5, which serve as “find me” signals to recruit CD8 + T cells to the TME [237]. Similarly, oxaliplatin in other settings triggers HMGB1 release and upregulates chemokines (CXCL9, CXCL10, and CCL5) to recruit CD8 + T cells, while exposing CRT for phagocytosis [237]. These recruited T cells exhibit high PD-1 expression, making them susceptible to subsequent PD-1 blockade, which prevents exhaustion and synergizes for a robust antitumor response [237]. In microsatellite stable (MSS) CRC, mitochondria-targeted photodynamic therapy (IR700DX-6T-PDT) has been demonstrated to trigger ICD through the activation of gasdermin E-mediated pyroptosis [238]. Pyroptotic cells release “find me” signals (HMGB1 and IL-1β) and “eat me” signals (phospholipids) [238]. This enhances immunogenicity, reverses immunosuppression, and synergizes with anti-PD-1 therapy [238]. In immunocompetent mouse models of mammary cancer, photodynamic therapy was reported to trigger ICD, releasing tumor antigens and HMGB1 to recruit DCs, and exposing “eat me” signals like phosphatidylserine [239]. Combined with PD-L1 blockade, it upregulates the CXCL9/10-CXCR3 axis, enhancing T cell chemotaxis, infiltration, and abscopal effects [239]. In a murine model of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) driven by Kras and p53 mutations that recapitulates human disease characteristics, an oxaliplatin/cyclophosphamide combination was found to provoke a stronger antitumor response than standard doublet chemotherapy by triggering ICD (HMGB1 release and CRT exposure) [240]. This activates DCs via TLR4 signaling, leading to CD8 + CTLs infiltration, converting “cold” tumors into “hot” tumors that show responsiveness to ICB [240]. Low-dose mitoxantrone was also reported to trigger ICD, exposing CRT on cancer cell surface, and upregulating chemokines (CCL5, CXCL10, and CXCL9) to recruit CTLs, NK cells, and DCs, thereby remodeling the TME and potentiating immune responses [241].

However, multiple preclinical studies indicate that not all combinations of ICD inducers and ICB yield synergistic outcomes. Suboptimal dosing or scheduling can sometimes promote immunosuppressive cell populations, undermining effectiveness. For example, in a murine model of CRC, concurrent oxaliplatin and PD-L1 blockade did not reduce tumor growth or improve the CD8 + T cell/Treg ratio [242]. In another mouse RENCA renal cell carcinoma model, cyclophosphamide combined with a CTLA-4 inhibitor failed to confer therapeutic benefit, which was linked to MDSC accumulation and elevated immunosuppressive cytokines [243]. In a MC38 CRC model, the integration of paclitaxel and PD-1 blockade did not enhance antitumor effectiveness, likely attributable to inadequate T cell recruitment to the TME [244]. Recently, in a mouse model of breast cancer, intraperitoneal delivery of paclitaxel did not synergize with PD-L1 blockade [245], despite evidence that it potentiated PD-1 inhibition in the same model [246]. The underlying causes of this discrepancy have yet to be elucidated and may reflect immunological differences between the PD-L1 and PD-1 pathways.

Clinical trials

This promising strategy has catalyzed a multitude of clinical trials aimed at further assessing the efficacy of combining ICD inducers with ICB [247, 248]. In breast cancer, this combination has progressed from Phase III trials to early clinical adoption. However, therapeutic outcomes show considerable heterogeneity across molecular subtypes and treatment protocols. Positive results have largely emerged from trials employing chemotherapeutic agents with established ICD-inducing capability, whose efficacy is tightly linked to TME remodeling. For instance, Phase III trials such as KEYNOTE-355 and IMpassion131 established taxane-based chemotherapy combined with ICB as a primary standard for advanced triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) [249, 250]. Paclitaxel, a type I ICD inducer, promotes CRT surface exposure and the release of ATP and HMGB1 [67, 72]. Here, ATP and HMGB1 act as “find me” signals, while CRT serves as an “eat me” signal [67, 72], collectively fostering DC maturation and antigen presentation. This cascade helps generate a T cell-inflamed, or “hot” TME, which underpins ICB efficacy. Furthermore, albumin-bound paclitaxel can reprogram macrophages, further converting the TME from immunosuppressive to immunostimulatory [79]. In the neoadjuvant setting, trials such as NeoTRIP and GeparNuevo found that adding ICB to taxane-based regimens did not increase the pathological complete response rate (pCR) [251, 252]. However, exploratory analyses suggest potential long-term survival benefits, indicating complex temporal dynamics in ICD induction. For instance, while anthracyclines are potent ICD inducers, their associated profound lymphopenia may transiently impair immune activation [253]. Conversely, cyclophosphamide exhibits ICD potential but it was also reported to selectively deplete Tregs [82, 83]. Therefore, an optimized treatment sequence, starting with low-dose cyclophosphamide for Tregs depletion and immune priming, followed by an anthracycline to release tumor antigens, and concluding with ICB to relieve T cell immune checkpoints, may yield synergy. This strategy has garnered support from several preclinical models and small-scale clinical studies [220, 240, 254]. However, for hormone receptor-positive (HR+) breast cancer, combining ICB with chemotherapy has shown minimal benefit, as seen in exploratory analyses from the CheckMate 7FL trial [255]. This restricted effectiveness is primarily attributed to the “immune desert” TME typical of HR+ disease, distinguished by scant baseline T cell infiltration and abundant immunosuppressive cells. Under such conditions, ICD induction alone is inadequate to initiate a potent antitumor immune response. In summary, while ICD inducers combined with ICB hold significant potential in breast cancer, success critically depends on a nuanced understanding of immune mechanisms and TME context. Future research must advance beyond simple additive combinations to precisely design treatment sequences, dosing, and synergistic strategies for personalized immune microenvironment remodeling.

In CRC, the clinical utility of ICB is fundamentally limited in MSS tumors, which constitute the majority of cases and are typically immunogenically “cold” [256]. This challenge has positioned ICD inducers combined with ICB as a key strategy to actively remodel and sensitize the immunosuppressive TME. Oxaliplatin plays a pivotal and irreplaceable role, extending beyond its traditional cytotoxicity. As a well-established type I ICD inducer, oxaliplatin triggers ER stress, leading to CRT exposure on tumor cell surface and HMGB1 release, thereby recruiting and activating APCs [55, 68]. More importantly, oxaliplatin stimulates tumor cells to secrete key chemokines CXCL9 and CXCL10, which via CXCR3 efficiently recruit CTLs into the tumor core [237]. This process successfully converts “cold” tumors into “hot” ones, restoring the necessary targets for subsequent ICB. The clinical value of this mechanism is evidenced in pivotal trials. The AtezoTRIBE study showed that the addition of atezolizumab to the FOLFOXIRI/bevacizumab regimen markedly improved progression-free survival (PFS) in advanced MSS CRC, providing compelling evidence that ICD-inducing chemotherapy can overcome intrinsic ICB resistance [257]. Furthermore, oxaliplatin promotes TLS formation, which is associated with improved long-term prognosis, reinforcing its role as an ideal backbone for immunotherapy combinations [226]. Looking ahead, research is evolving from a “universal combination” towards “precision sensitization”. Key directions include: (1) developing predictive biomarkers (e.g., pre-treatment TLS density, CXCL13 levels, or DAMPs signaling profiles); (2) exploring sequential or synergistic combinations between different ICD-inducing modalities themselves, such as radiotherapy and oncolytic viruses; and (3) systematically optimizing the timing and dosing of ICD inducers with ICB to balance efficacy and toxicity. These efforts aim to establish individualized regimens for MSS CRC.

In NSCLC, the combination of ICB with ICD inducers has evolved from broadly synergistic application to refined strategies targeting specific resistance mechanisms. Central to this is the role of ICD in remodeling the TME to enable the immune cell infiltration and activation necessary for ICB function. Platinum-based regimens combined with taxanes or pemetrexed are standard first-line partners for ICB in advanced, driver mutation-negative NSCLC [6]. The success of these regimens relied heavily on the ICD-inducing properties of chemotherapy. Both platinum-based agents and taxanes are well-established type I ICD inducers that act primarily through DNA damage and microtubule disruption, respectively. These mechanisms indirectly engage ER stress-related signaling pathways, leading to downstream immunogenic events such as CRT exposure and HMGB1 release, which ultimately initiate APC activation [13, 21, 22, 258]. Clinical translational work further validates that pemetrexed induces key ICD biomarkers in NSCLC models, providing direct mechanistic support for its clinical synergy [259]. This mechanistic foundation is driving combinations towards more intensive immune checkpoint inhibition. For instance, treatment protocols that combine bispecific antibodies directed against PD-L1 and CTLA-4 with chemotherapy demonstrate encouraging preliminary efficacy in the management of metastatic NSCLC [260], suggesting that stronger checkpoint blockade may further enhance the immune response triggered by ICD. In patient populations traditionally resistant to ICB monotherapy (e.g., EGFR- or ALK-altered tumors), recent studies explore triple combinations of ICB with chemotherapy and anti-angiogenic agents. For example, in NSCLC patients with EGFR/ALK mutations after targeted therapy failure, atezolizumab plus bevacizumab and chemotherapy demonstrated clinically meaningful survival benefits compared to standard treatments [261]. This strategy implies that chemotherapy-induced ICD may partially overcome the low immunogenicity of driver-positive tumors, while angiogenesis normalization may enhance immune cell infiltration. Radiotherapy is one of the most extensively studied ICD-inducing modalities. Combined with ICB, radiotherapy can elicit an abscopal effect, mechanistically linked to cGAS-STING pathway activation, mitochondrial DNA release, and expanded neoantigen repertoire [53, 98], offering potential to overcome localized and systemic drug resistance, particularly in oligometastatic or locally advanced disease. Concurrently, TLS have emerged as critical predictive biomarkers for combination therapy efficacy, with their density correlating with long-term survival. Studies indicate that low-dose radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and novel physical ablation techniques such as pulsed electric fields can promote intratumoral TLS formation, thereby establishing a local niche for T cell priming and sustained immune surveillance [221–223].

However, numerous clinical studies have failed to demonstrate a benefit from combining ICD inducers with ICB. For instance, adding atezolizumab to chemotherapy did not improve overal survival (OS) compared to chemotherapy alone in a randomized trial in NSCLC [262]. Similarly, in squamous NSCLC, adding a CTLA-4 inhibitor to a paclitaxel-carboplatin chemotherapy did not achieve enhancement in clinical outcomes and significantly increased treatment-related adverse events [263]. In a clinical trial involving patients with gastric cancer, the addition of pembrolizumab to standard chemotherapy did not show a significant improvement in OS or PFS [264]. A clinical trial investigating prostate cancer demonstrated that the addition of pembrolizumab to chemotherapy did not demonstrate a significant improvement in OS or PFS [265]. In TNBC, the NeoTRIP study demonstrated that the addition of atezolizumab to neoadjuvant chemotherapy did not result in a significant difference in pCR rates (48.6% versus 44.4%) [251]. Furthermore, clinical trials investigating the combination of radiotherapy with ICB have not demonstrated significant therapeutic efficacy in NSCLC and Merkel cell carcinoma [266, 267]. A recent trial in melanoma indicated that while talimogene laherparepvec plus pembrolizumab increased the objective response rate versus pembrolizumab alone, it did not achieve its dual endpoints of PFS and OS [268].

In summary, combining ICD-inducing radiotherapy or chemotherapy with ICB does not uniformly yield synergistic clinical effects. Several factors may explain this discrepancy. First, limitations of preclinical models must be acknowledged. Most studies employ syngeneic mouse models with subcutaneously implanted murine cancer cell lines [180], which cannot fully recapitulate the inter- and intra-tumoral heterogeneity of human cancers. Genetically engineered or orthotopic models in immunocompetent hosts may better address these limitations [20, 269]. Second, suboptimal treatment scheduling is a major challenge. Most clinical trials lack systematic preclinical comparisons of different administration schedules (i.e., concurrent versus sequential dosing) [270, 271]. Systematic evaluation in immunocompetent tumor models could identify optimal schedules and improve clinical translation. Third, extensive tumor heterogeneity across different cancer types, between patients, between lesions in the same patient, and within single tumor lesions means ICD inducers may only affect a subset of tumor cells, limiting the overall immunogenic response [180, 272, 273]. Fourth, the predictive utility of ICD-related biomarkers (e.g., circulating HMGB1, associated with outcomes after ICD inducer monotherapy in breast and HNSCC [121]) remains uncertain in combination settings, as elevated levels may also stem from non-ICD processes [274]. Fifth, clinical chemotherapy often prioritizes immediate tumor reduction at maximum tolerated doses, potentially at the expense of fostering durable anti-cancer immunity [259, 275–277]. Finally, common chemotherapy-induced adverse effects (e.g., neutropenia, lymphopenia, and intestinal mucositis) can themselves impair immune function and ICD [19, 278–286]. Addressing these challenges may require more targeted delivery approaches, such as nanoparticle-based systems, to enhance tumor-specific drug delivery and mitigate systemic toxicity [287, 288].

Concluding remarks

ICD represents a distinct modality of cell death capable of evoking antitumor immune responses and has gained prominence as a pivotal method to augment the effectiveness of ICB therapies. Through the release of DAMPs and cytokines, ICD facilitates DC maturation and enhances antigen presentation, thereby activating CTLs and converting immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” ones. This process subsequently improves the therapeutic responsiveness to ICB. Moreover, ICD contributes to the formation of TLS, which further refine the TME and bolster local immune surveillance and immunological memory. Despite these promising mechanisms, the clinical application of combined ICD inducers and ICB has yielded inconsistent synergistic outcomes. Therapeutic efficacy is modulated by multiple determinants, including tumor heterogeneity, treatment timing, the presence of an immunosuppressive TME, and the host’s immune status. Additionally, significant challenges persist in the pursuit of precision immunotherapy, such as the absence of definitive ICD-specific biomarkers, adverse effects associated with chemotherapy, and tumor cell resistance mechanisms to ICD.

Over recent decades, research has elucidated that the efficacy of radiotherapy and chemotherapy is largely attributable to their capacity to induce antitumor immune responses, a mechanism frequently reliant on the initiation of ICD. This insight has driven the development of novel ICD-inducing agents. However, several challenges impede their translational progress: (1) Non-specific toxicity: Many ICD inducers, such as inhibitors of DNA replication or transcription, incur significant off-target effects, and can impair immune cells and other normal cells. More targeted strategies are needed to selectively activate ICD-associated signaling pathways (such as ER stress or type I IFN responses) within tumor cells. (2) Suboptimal dosing regimens: Standard continuous dosing at the maximum tolerated dose can cause immunosuppressive side effects. Alternative strategies, including dose reduction or intermittent administration, should be investigated in clinical trials to preserve immune function. (3) Tumor heterogeneity and resistance: Variability among tumor cells can drive resistance to ICD. Overcoming this requires personalized approaches, informed by precise molecular diagnosis to identify ICD defects, coupled with the development of targeted ICD-correcting agents. (4) Lack of specific biomarkers: Current biomarkers like HMGB1 are not exclusive to ICD, limiting their utility. There is a critical need to discover and validate highly sensitive and specific biomarkers for real-time monitoring of ICD in patients. (5) Host-specific factors: Individual host factors that shape immune recognition of ICD must be identified. Such factors should be responsive to therapeutic targeting or useful for patient stratification, thereby facilitating the advancement of individualized medicine approaches.
