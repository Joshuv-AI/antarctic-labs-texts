Dynamic gated fusion network with hierarchical multi-scale attention for hyperspectral image classification
Authors: Xianjian Shi, Lilong Liu, Xin Bao, Bin Pan, Sajid Hussain
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hyperspectral image classification is a critical task in remote sensing, but existing methods often employ fixed feature fusion strategies, making it difficult to adapt to the data characteristics of different scenarios. Additionally, there is a lack of effective synergy between multi-scale feature extraction and attention mechanisms. To address this issue, this paper proposes a dynamic gated fusion network with hierarchical multi-scale attention (DGFNet). This method comprises three core modules: the multi-scale feature aggregator (MSFA), which uses a pyramid expansion convolution structure to concurrently extract spatial features with different receptive fields, achieving comprehensive scale coverage from local texture to global context; the enhanced channel-spatial attention (ECSA) module, which employs multi-pooling strategies and a cascaded structure to achieve deep interaction between channel and spatial attention, thereby adaptively enhancing discriminative features; and the dynamic gated fusion module, which learns input-related fusion weights to adaptively adjust the contribution ratios of multi-scale features and attention features based on data characteristics. Experimental results on four benchmark datasets—Pavia University, Houston, Indian Pines, and WHU-HongHu—show that DGFNet achieves overall accuracy rates of 96.91, 97.12, 94.05, and 94.46%, respectively, representing significant improvements over existing state-of-the-art methods. Ablation experiments thoroughly validate the effectiveness and necessity of each module. Additionally, this paper systematically compares five different fusion strategies (cross-attention, hierarchical fusion, parallel fusion, recurrent fusion, and sequential fusion). Experimental results demonstrate that dynamic gated fusion outperforms other fusion methods in terms of classification accuracy, computational efficiency, and model stability. This method provides an efficient, accurate, and robust solution for hyperspectral image classification. The code will be published on https://github.com/willianbilledu-alt/DGFNet.

Introduction

Hyperspectral imaging technology collects information about objects on the ground across hundreds of continuous and narrow spectral bands, providing rich spectral features for precise object identification and classification1. Compared to traditional multispectral imaging, hyperspectral images (HSI) have extremely high spectral resolution, enabling the capture of subtle spectral differences in materials. They are playing an increasingly important role in fields such as precision agriculture, environmental monitoring, urban planning, mineral exploration, and national defence security2. With the rapid development of hyperspectral sensor technology and the reduction in imaging costs, hyperspectral image classification has become one of the most active research directions in the field of remote sensing3.

However, hyperspectral image classification faces unique challenges. First, the curse of dimensionality severely limits classification performance. Hyperspectral images typically contain hundreds of spectral bands. At the same time, the number of training samples is often limited, leading to the Hughes phenomenon4, where classification accuracy decreases as the number of features increases, especially when training samples are insufficient. Second, spectral variability causes similar objects to exhibit different spectral characteristics under different conditions, while objects of different categories may have similar spectral responses5. Furthermore, the effective fusion of spatial-spectral information remains an open issue. While spectral information provides intrinsic material characteristics, ignoring spatial contextual information can lead to “salt-and-pepper noise” in classification results6. Finally, computational complexity issues limit the deployment of deep learning models in practical applications, particularly in resource-constrained environments such as satellite-borne or airborne platforms7.

To address these challenges, researchers have proposed various methods. Traditional machine learning methods such as Support Vector Machines (SVM)8 and Random Forests (RF)9 have mitigated the curse of dimensionality to some extent through carefully designed feature engineering. In recent years, the introduction of deep learning, particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), has fundamentally transformed the research paradigm for hyperspectral image classification10–12. 3D CNNs can simultaneously extract spatial and spectral features13, while hybrid CNN architectures such as HybridSN14 further enhance feature representation capabilities by combining 3D and 2D convolutions. Recently, the success of Vision Transformer has inspired researchers to apply self-attention mechanisms to hyperspectral classification, with methods such as SpectralFormer15 demonstrating the Transformer’s advantages in capturing long-range dependencies.

Although existing methods have made significant progress, several key issues remain to be addressed: (1) Fixed feature fusion strategies lack adaptability. Most existing methods employ predefined fusion strategies to combine multi-scale or multi-modal features, such as simple concatenation16, weighted summation17, or fixed attention mechanisms18. This static fusion approach cannot adaptively adjust based on the characteristics of the input data, limiting the model’s expressive power and generalization performance. For example, the ideal fusion strategy should differ between texture-rich urban areas and spectrally uniform farmland areas, but existing methods struggle to achieve such dynamic adaptation. (2) Insufficient synergy between multi-scale feature extraction and attention mechanisms. Although multi-scale feature learning and attention mechanisms have both been proven effective for hyperspectral classification, existing methods typically design them as independent modules, lacking deep synergy and interaction. This leads to feature redundancy and computational resource waste, while also limiting the model’s ability to capture complex spatial-spectral patterns. (3) The simple concatenation of channel and spatial attention fails to fully exploit feature potential. Existing attention mechanisms typically employ a simple concatenation19 or parallel structure20 of channel and spatial attention, ignoring their interdependencies. Additionally, most methods use only a single type of pooling operation (e.g., average pooling or max pooling), failing to comprehensively capture the statistical characteristics of features.

To address the above issues, this paper proposes a Dynamic Gated Fusion Network with Hierarchical Multi-Scale Attention for Hyperspectral Image Classification (DGFNet). The main contributions of this paper are as follows: Constructing the multi-scale feature aggregator (MSFA) module. This module adopts a pyramid expansion convolution structure, which uses convolution kernels with different expansion rates to extract multi-scale spatial features in parallel. Unlike traditional multi-scale methods, MSFA reduces parameter redundancy through a shared bottleneck layer while maintaining rich multi-scale representation capabilities.Construct an enhanced channel-spatial attention (ECSA) module. Unlike existing simple cascaded structures, ECSA comprehensively captures feature statistical information through multi-pooling strategies (simultaneously using average pooling and max pooling) and introduces a channel reduction mechanism to reduce computational complexity. More importantly, the channel attention and spatial attention in ECSA interact deeply through residual connections to achieve more effective feature enhancement.Constructing the dynamic gated fusion (DGF) module. This module achieves adaptive fusion of MSFA and ECSA features by learning input-related gating weights. Specifically, this paper constructs a lightweight gating network that generates fusion weights based on the global statistical information of input features, enabling the model to automatically adjust the contribution ratios of multi-scale features and attention features according to different data characteristics.

Related works

Early hyperspectral image classification primarily relied on traditional machine learning methods. Support vector machines (SVMs) were widely used due to their excellent performance in high-dimensional feature spaces8. Tarabalka et al.21 proposed combining SVMs with Markov random fields to effectively utilize spatial context information. Random forests (RFs), another important ensemble learning method, performed well when handling high-dimensional data9. Cao et al.22 proposed the Active Semi-Supervised Random Forest (ASSRF) method in 2019, which enhances classification performance under conditions of limited labelled samples by embedding active learning and semi-supervised learning strategies into the random forest framework.

In recent years, traditional methods have gained new vitality through their integration with deep features. Paoletti et al.23 proposed the Deep Support Vector Machine (DSVM) in 2019, which combines deep learning feature extraction with an SVM classifier, improving overall accuracy on the Indian Pines dataset. Qu et al.24 proposed the Gabor Random Patch Convolution (GRPC) method in 2022, innovatively combining 2D Gabor filters with random patch convolution, achieving improved accuracy on the Pavia University dataset. These studies demonstrate that traditional methods remain highly competitive through modernized improvements.

CNN-based methods

The introduction of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) has completely transformed the landscape of hyperspectral image classification. Chen et al.10 were the first to apply 3D CNNs to hyperspectral classification, directly learning spatio-spectral joint features from raw data. Ahmad et al.13 proposed a fast 3D CNN architecture in 2020, achieving outstanding performance on multiple benchmark datasets using only 994,166 parameters.

Hybrid CNN architectures have emerged as an important development direction in this field. Roy et al.14 proposed HybridSN, which explores the 3D-2D CNN feature hierarchy by sequentially combining 3D and 2D convolutional layers, achieving good accuracy on the Salinas dataset. Farooque et al.25 proposed the spectral-spatial 3D ConvLSTM-CNN in 2021, which combines 3D ConvLSTM with 3D CNN residual blocks to effectively capture the temporal dependencies of spectral sequences. Zhou et al.26 proposed the Enhanced Spectral Fusion 3D CNN (ESFNet) in 2022, which achieves effective extraction of multi-scale spectral features through a spectral stride fusion network with different stride lengths.

Deep separable convolutions and lightweight designs have also garnered significant attention. Wang et al.27 proposed a novel deep learning model based on pseudo-3D blocks and deep separable 2D-3D convolutions in 2023, significantly reducing the number of model parameters while maintaining high accuracy. These studies provide important technical support for the deployment of hyperspectral image classification in resource-constrained environments.

Transformer-based methods

The success of the Vision Transformer has inspired researchers to apply the Transformer architecture to hyperspectral image classification. Hong et al.15 proposed SpectralFormer, a pioneering work in this field, which rethinks the hyperspectral image classification problem from a sequential perspective through Grouped Spectral Embedding (GSE) and Cross-Layer Adaptive Fusion (CAF) mechanisms.

Recently, Xu et al.28 introduced the DSFormer, which incorporates a dual-selection fusion mechanism. By combining kernel-selection fusion Transformer blocks (KSFTB) and token-selection fusion Transformer blocks (TSFTB), it achieves adaptive feature selection and fusion, resulting in significant performance improvements across multiple datasets. Scheibenreif et al.29 proposed the Masked Vision Transformer at CVPR 2023, which learns general representations on large-scale unlabeled data through a self-supervised pre-training strategy and then fine-tunes them for classification tasks, achieving performance improvements on the Houston2018 dataset.

Sun et al.18 proposed the Spectral-Spatial Feature Tokenisation Transformer (SSFTT), which effectively combines local CNN features with global Transformer mechanisms through a Gaussian-weighted tokenization strategy. These Transformer-based methods demonstrate the advantages of self-attention mechanisms in capturing long-range dependencies.

Attention mechanisms in hyperspectral analysis

Attention mechanisms have become a key technology for improving the classification performance of hyperspectral images. In terms of channel attention, Zhao et al.20 proposed the Adaptive Pixel Attention Network (APA) in 2024, which achieves adaptive feature reweighting by exploring cosine and Euclidean similarity. Kang et al.30 proposed the Spectral-Spatial Dual-Branch Attention Mechanism (SSDBN) in 2024, which employs independent spectral and spatial attention modules to achieve excellent performance while maintaining a lightweight architecture.

Research on spatial attention mechanisms has also made significant progress. Li et al.31 proposed a multi-scale CNN based on nonlocal attention mechanisms in 2023, effectively suppressing spectral redundancy by capturing long-range spatial dependencies. Zhang et al.32 proposed the Spectral-Spatial Self-Attention Network (SSSAN) in 2022, which adopts a dual-branch architecture to achieve adaptive integration of long-range dependencies based on patch features.

Guo et al.33 proposed a spectral-spatial attention network in 2019 that combines channel attention and spatial attention, laying the foundation for subsequent research. These studies demonstrate that attention mechanisms play a crucial role in highlighting discriminative features and suppressing redundant information.

Multi-scale feature learning

Multi-scale feature learning is crucial for handling scale variations in hyperspectral images. Zhang et al.34 proposed the Quantum-Inspired Spectral-Spatial Pyramid Network (QSSPN) at CVPR 2023, which quantum circuit models inspire. It achieves effective fusion of multi-scale features through phase prediction modules and measurement fusion modules, achieving an average accuracy of 96.40% with only 1.17 million parameters.

In terms of pyramid structures, Wang et al.35 proposed a hybrid pyramid feature fusion method in 2022, which combines three parallel hybrid 3D-2D CNNs with a coordinate attention mechanism, specifically optimized for small-sample scenarios. Li et al.36 proposed a deep learning framework based on spatial pyramid pooling in 2019, which captures features with different receptive fields through multi-scale pooling operations.

Gong et al.37 proposed a multi-scale spectral-spatial convolutional Transformer in 2024, which effectively addresses the issue of scale variation in hyperspectral images by extracting features at different scales and performing adaptive fusion. These methods demonstrate the importance of multi-scale feature learning in improving classification accuracy. Methodology

Although the methods above have made significant progress in hyperspectral image classification, they still have some limitations: (1) Most methods use fixed feature fusion strategies, which are difficult to adapt to the characteristics of different data; (2) Multi-scale feature extraction and attention mechanisms are often designed independently, lacking effective coordination; (3) Existing dynamic mechanisms mainly focus on a single aspect, lacking comprehensive dynamic fusion strategies. The proposed DGFNet innovatively combines a multi-scale feature aggregator (MSFA) and enhanced channel-spatial attention (ECSA) through a dynamic gated fusion mechanism to achieve adaptive feature fusion. Unlike existing methods that use fixed fusion strategies, DGFNet can adaptively adjust the contribution weights of different feature branches based on the characteristics of the input data, thereby achieving more flexible and effective feature representation.

As shown in Fig. 1, DGFNet adopts an end-to-end hierarchical architecture design, and its overall structure can be divided into five main components. First, the input hyperspectral image cube is converted into a feature representation suitable for deep network processing through the Patch Embedding Module. These initial features are then simultaneously input into two parallel feature extraction branches: the Multi-Scale Feature Aggregator (MSFA) and the Enhanced Channel-Spatial Attention (ECSA) module. The MSFA captures spatial features with different receptive fields through a pyramid expansion convolution structure, while the ECSA achieves adaptive feature enhancement through a cascaded attention mechanism. The features extracted by the two branches are then adaptively integrated through a dynamic gated fusion module, which optimizes the combination of features by learning input-related fusion weights. Finally, the fused features are mapped to the category space via a classification head to complete the final classification task.

In the data processing workflow, given a hyperspectral image cube with dimensions H×W×C (where H and W represent spatial dimensions and C represents the number of spectral channels), the network first extracts three-dimensional patches of size P×P×C as input units. This patch-based processing strategy not only effectively reduces computational complexity but also fully leverages local spatial-spectral correlations. Through 3D convolutional layers, the original high-dimensional spectral information is encoded into a compact feature representation while retaining key discriminative information. This design fully considers the characteristics of hyperspectral data, namely strong correlations in the spectral dimension and rich texture and structural information in the spatial dimension.

Experiments

Implementation details

To evaluate the effectiveness and generalization ability of the DGFNet method, this paper conducts experiments on four representative hyperspectral image datasets (TABLE II). These datasets exhibit significant differences in spectral resolution, spatial resolution, land cover types, and acquisition conditions, thereby effectively validating the model’s performance across diverse application scenarios: (1) The Pavia University Dataset (PU) includes 115 spectral bands (430–860 nm), with a spatial resolution of 610 × 340 pixels and a resolution of 1.3 m. It covers nine land cover categories: asphalt pavement, grassland, gravel, trees, metal plates, bare soil, asphalt shingles, bricks, and shadows. The main challenges include similar spectral characteristics among certain categories and imbalanced sample sizes. (2) The Houston dataset (Houston) includes 144 spectral bands (400–1000 nm), with a spatial resolution of 349 × 1905 pixels and a resolution of 2.5 m. It covers 15 urban land cover categories, including various types of grassland, trees, built-up areas, roads, and more. The challenges lie in the fine-grained classification of categories and the issue of mixed pixels in urban environments. (3) The Indiana Pine Forest dataset (IP) originally had 224 bands, but after removing invalid bands, 200 bands (400–2500 nm) were retained, with a spatial resolution of 145 × 145 pixels and a resolution of 20 m. It includes 16 crop and vegetation categories. The main challenges are accurate classification of extremely small sample categories and identifying spectral differences between similar crops. (4) The Wuhan University-Honghu dataset (WHU-HH) includes 270 spectral bands (400–1000 nm), with a spatial resolution of 940 × 475 pixels and a resolution of 0.043 m (the highest among the four datasets). It covers 17 detailed crop categories. The challenge lies in the increased intra-class variability and the distinction of detailed crop categories due to the ultra-high spatial resolution.

To ensure the fairness and reproducibility of the experiments, a unified experimental setup framework was adopted in this paper. For each dataset, a fixed number of training samples was used, with the following specific settings: For the PU dataset, 30 samples were randomly selected from each class for training, and the remaining samples were used for testing; for the Houston dataset, 50 samples were randomly selected from each class for training, and the remaining samples were used for testing; For the IP dataset, 50 samples are randomly selected from each category for training, with the remaining samples used for testing; for the WHU-HH dataset, 50 samples are randomly selected from each category for training, with the remaining samples used for testing. The choice of training sample size (30 for PU, 50 for Houston/IP/WHU-HH) follows established protocols in hyperspectral classification literature, enabling fair comparison with existing methods. The selected sample sizes balance two critical requirements: (1) providing sufficient statistical representation to capture intra-class variability and enable reliable model training, and (2) maintaining a challenging evaluation scenario that tests the model’s generalization capability from limited labeled data—a crucial requirement for practical remote sensing applications. Our experimental results with 10 independent runs demonstrate that these sample sizes provide statistically reliable and reproducible results while effectively evaluating the model’s performance under data-scarce conditions.

For categories with fewer than the specified number of training samples, 50% of the samples from that category are used for training. In all experiments, the input patch size is uniformly set to 10 × 10 pixels to capture sufficient spatial context information. To reduce the impact of randomness and obtain statistically reliable results, each experiment was repeated 10 times, and the reported results are the average and standard deviation of the 10 runs. In terms of model hyperparameter settings, this paper determined the optimal configuration through grid search. All models were trained for 500 epochs, and an early stopping strategy was used to prevent overfitting. The batch size was set to 64, the initial learning rate was 0.001, and a cosine annealing strategy was used for learning rate decay.

Evaluation metrics

To comprehensively evaluate classification performance, this paper adopted three standard evaluation indicators widely accepted in the field of hyperspectral image classification: Overall Accuracy (OA), Average Accuracy (AA), and Kappa coefficient (κ). These metrics were specifically selected to provide complementary perspectives on model performance, addressing both overall accuracy and the challenges of imbalanced datasets common in remote sensing applications.

Overall accuracy (OA)

Overall accuracy measures the proportion of correctly classified samples in all test samples, calculated as the ratio of correctly classified pixels to the total number of test pixels. OA intuitively reflects the overall classification performance of the model but may be affected by category imbalance. For instance, in datasets where one class dominates (e.g., background class), high OA might mask poor performance on minority classes. Despite this limitation, OA remains the most widely reported metric in hyperspectral classification literature, facilitating direct comparison across studies.

Average accuracy (AA)

Average accuracy computes the arithmetic mean of the accuracy rate for each category, giving equal weight to all classes regardless of their sample sizes. This metric is especially critical for hyperspectral datasets like Indian Pines, where some crop categories may have significantly fewer samples than others. AA ensures that the model’s ability to classify rare but important land cover types is not overshadowed by its performance on dominant classes, making it particularly suitable for evaluating performance on imbalanced datasets.

Kappa coefficient

The Kappa coefficient accounts for the agreement occurring by chance, providing a more rigorous performance evaluation than raw accuracy. It measures the agreement between predicted and actual classifications while considering the expected agreement by random chance. The Kappa coefficient ranges from − 1 to 1, with values closer to 1 indicating better classification performance. Generally, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K>0.8$$\end{document} indicates excellent consistency, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.6<K \leqslant 0.8$$\end{document} indicates good consistency. The Kappa coefficient is particularly valuable for hyperspectral classification as it provides a more conservative and reliable assessment than accuracy alone.

Quantitative results

To validate the effectiveness of DGFNet, this paper conducts a comprehensive comparison with state-of-the-art methods that have demonstrated outstanding performance in the field of hyperspectral image classification in recent years. The comparison methods include deep learning methods based on CNNs and the latest Transformer architecture methods. Specifically, these include: 3D-CNN42, SSFCN43, SACNet44, SpectralFormer15, MorphFormer45, and GSCViT46. All comparison methods were implemented with optimal parameter settings and re-implemented in the same experimental environment (Intel Xeon E5-2690 v4 CPU, NVIDIA RTX 2080Ti GPU, 64 GB memory) to ensure fair comparison. TABLE III to TABLE VI present the classification performance comparisons of different methods across four datasets.

Among the 9 urban object categories in the PU dataset, DGFNet achieved the best or near-best performance in 7 categories. Notably, for challenging categories such as Asphalt (95.79%), Self-Blocking Bricks (94.19%), and Bitumen (98.72%), DGFNet demonstrated a significant improvement in accuracy compared to other methods. 3D-CNN performs extremely poorly in these categories (30.47%, 18.47%, and 29.51%, respectively), highlighting the limitations of simple 3D convolutions in capturing complex spatio-spectral features. The Houston dataset includes 15 fine-grained urban categories, where DGFNet achieves an overall accuracy (OA) of 97.12 ± 0.34% and a Kappa coefficient of 96.88 ± 0.37%, demonstrating exceptional performance in complex urban environments. In terms of fine-grained category discrimination, DGFNet performs exceptionally well: for easily confused building and road categories such as Residential (96.10%), Highway (97.86%), and Road (92.91%), the accuracy is significantly higher than other methods. Notably, DGFNet achieved a perfect 100% accuracy rate for the Tennis Court category and the best performance for the Trees category (99.23%), indicating that DGFNet effectively enhances discriminative features. The IP dataset is challenging due to its extremely imbalanced category distribution and small sample size issues. DGFNet achieved an OA of 94.05 ± 0.72 and an AA of 97.43 ± 0.32% on this dataset. The outstanding performance of AA (2.41% points higher than the next best method) demonstrates the model’s strong generalization ability on small sample categories. For extremely small sample categories such as Alfalfa (only 46 training samples), DGFNet achieves an accuracy of 99.68%, far surpassing 3D-CNN. In categories such as Grass-Pasture-Mowed and Oats, multiple methods achieve 100% accuracy, but DGFNet demonstrates a significant advantage in more challenging categories such as Corn-No-Till (89.72%), Corn-Still (97.23%), and Soybean-Clean (96.02%). The WHU-HH dataset features an ultra-high spatial resolution of 0.043 m and 17 fine-grained crop categories. DGFNet achieves an overall accuracy (OA) of 94.46 ± 1.09%, fully leveraging the rich spatial information provided by high resolution. In the fine-grained classification of vegetable categories, DGFNet performs exceptionally well: the accuracy rates for similar vegetable categories such as Chinese Cabbage (83.88%), Pakchoi (94.01%), and Brassica Parachinensis (90.13%) are improved by 10–20% points compared to other methods.

In terms of training time, DGFNet achieved higher training times than lightweight methods such as SSFCN and SACNet on the PU (36s), Houston (69s), IP (64s), and WHU-HH (108s) datasets. However, higher than lightweight methods such as SSFCN and SACNet, it is significantly lower than MorphFormer (40s, 92s, 84s, and 137s, respectively) and far below 3D-CNN (115s, 284s, 287s, and 413s). Considering the magnitude of performance improvements, DGFNet achieves a good balance between accuracy and efficiency. Additionally, standard deviation analysis from 10 repetitions of all experiments shows that DGFNet not only performs optimally but also exhibits excellent stability. The standard deviations for OA across the four datasets were 1.71, 1.43, 0.81, and 1.09, respectively, all of which were lower than or close to those of the comparison methods, indicating that the model has good robustness and reproducibility. Especially on the IP dataset, the standard deviation of 0.81 was far lower than that of other methods, demonstrating DGFNet’s stability advantage in handling imbalanced data.

Qualitative analysis

To more intuitively evaluate the classification performance of DGFNet, this paper conducted a visualisation analysis of the classification results for four datasets, as shown in Figs. 4, 5, 6 and 7 In the urban scene of the PU dataset (Fig. 4), DGFNet demonstrated excellent spatial consistency and boundary preservation capabilities. The classification results of 3D-CNN exhibit severe salt-and-pepper noise, particularly in the Asphalt and Bare soil regions, where a large number of pixels are misclassified, leading to fragmented classification maps. This is likely due to the lack of effective spatial context modelling capabilities in 3D-CNN, as relying solely on local 3D convolution operations makes it difficult to capture long-range dependencies. SSFCN and SACNet show improvements in overall structural recognition but still lack sufficient detail preservation. Taking the Meadows region as an example, the classification results of SSFCN exhibit noticeable holes, while SACNet produces excessive smoothing at the boundaries. Although SpectralFormer improves global consistency through the self-attention mechanism, it performs poorly in identifying certain challenging categories, such as Self-Blocking Bricks, with blurred classification boundaries and a high number of misclassifications. In contrast, DGFNet’s classification map is closest to the true labels. DGFNet accurately preserves sharp boundaries without the boundary confusion commonly seen in other methods. Especially in the identification of the Shadows category, DGFNet successfully distinguishes it from surrounding dark-colored objects, while other methods often misclassify shadows as Asphalt or Bitumen.

The classification results of the Houston dataset (Fig. 5) further validate the advantages of DGFNet in complex urban environments. This dataset contains multiple fine-grained urban categories, placing higher demands on the discriminative ability of classifiers. 3D-CNN completely fails to identify road networks, confusing highways, roads, and railways into a single category and is unable to distinguish the subtle differences between these linear structures. SSFCN and SACNet can identify major road structures but struggle to distinguish parallel roads. For example, in the parallel Highway and Road area on the right side of the image, both methods produce severe misclassifications. SpectralFormer and MorphFormer perform well in identifying large-scale structures but lack precision in locating small-scale targets like Tennis Courts and Running Tracks, often expanding or contracting their boundaries. DGFNet not only accurately identified all road categories but also successfully maintained their continuity and integrity. In mixed Residential and Commercial areas, DGFNet demonstrated precise category boundary delineation capabilities, avoiding the common issue of blurred transition zones seen in other methods. Notably, DGFNet achieved good results in identifying Water categories, with clear water body boundaries and uniform internal structures, without the misclassification patches observed in other methods.

The classification results of the IP dataset (Fig. 6) demonstrate the effectiveness of DGFNet in agricultural scenarios. The primary challenges of this dataset lie in the spectral similarity between different crops and the extremely imbalanced sample distribution. 3D-CNN struggles to identify small sample categories, such as Alfalfa and Oats, which completely disappear in the classification map and are incorrectly classified as the dominant categories surrounding them. Traditional CNN methods (SSFCN, SACNet) perform reasonably well on major crop categories but have limited ability to distinguish between similar crops. For example, the three corn-related categories, Corn-Notill, Corn-Still, and Corn, are often confused. Methods based on Transformers (SpectralFormer, MorphFormer) improve the recognition of large-scale farmland through global attention mechanisms but still lack sufficient detail in preserving internal texture patterns within farmland. DGFNet’s classification map clearly shows farmland structure and accurately identifies crop types. Even for extremely small sample categories like Grass-Pasture-Mowed (only 28 samples), DGFNet can accurately locate and fully identify them. In distinguishing soybean series categories, DGFNet successfully maintains the unique features of soybean fields at different growth stages, with classification boundaries highly aligned with actual farmland boundaries. Additionally, DGFNet performs exceptionally well in identifying complex mixed categories such as Woods and Buildings-Grass-Trees-Drives, accurately capturing the spatial distribution patterns of these regions.

The WHU-HH dataset has the highest spatial resolution (0.043 m) and includes 17 detailed crop categories (Fig. 7). This ultra-high resolution provides rich texture information but also increases intra-class variability. The classification results of 3D-CNN exhibit severe over-segmentation, with a single farmland being divided into multiple fragments, completely losing structural integrity. SSFCN and SACNet show improvements in maintaining farmland integrity but struggle to distinguish between fine vegetable categories. In particular, for cruciferous vegetables such as Chinese cabbage, pak choi, and cabbage, these methods often produce large areas of misclassified regions. Although SpectralFormer captures global patterns through the self-attention mechanism, its accuracy decreases significantly when handling small-scale planting areas. DGFNet fully leverages the advantages of high-resolution data to produce the most accurate classification results. In the fine-grained classification of vegetable planting areas, DGFNet successfully distinguishes between crop types with highly similar appearances. For example, DGFNet accurately identified the differences in planting patterns between the two Chinese cabbage varieties Brassica Parachinensis and Brassica Chinensis. In the identification of Film Covered Lettuce, DGFNet not only identified the covered areas but also maintained the regular shape of the greenhouse. The boundaries between Road and Bare Soil were clearly and accurately defined, without the common issues of road breaks or excessive soil area expansion seen in other methods.

By observing the classification diagrams of the four datasets, it can be seen that DGFNet has a significant advantage in boundary preservation. This is mainly due to the multi-scale feature extraction capability of the MSFA module, which enables the network to simultaneously capture fine boundary information and large-scale regional features. The dynamic gating mechanism further enhances this capability by adaptively adjusting the weights of different features, emphasising the contribution of local features in boundary regions and relying more on global features in uniform regions. Although DGFNet achieves excellent classification performance, it still exhibits some typical misclassification patterns. In the PU dataset, occasional misclassifications occur at the boundary between Meadows and Trees, primarily because the spectral features of vegetation transition zones inherently exhibit gradual changes. In the Houston dataset, Stressed Grass and Healthy Grass remain confused in certain regions, indicating that even advanced deep learning methods struggle to fully address the identification of subtle spectral differences. These observations provide important insights for future improvements.

Discussion

Component analysis

To thoroughly examine the contribution mechanisms of each component in DGFNet, this paper designed ablation experiments to quantify the impact of each component on the final performance by progressively adding key modules. The results of the ablation experiments are shown in TABLE VII, where: Baseline refers to using only the basic feature extractor and classification head; Baseline + MSFA refers to adding only the multi-scale feature aggregator; Baseline + ECSA refers to adding only the enhanced channel-spatial attention; Baseline + MSFA + ECSA indicates using both modules but with simple concatenation fusion; DGFNet indicates the complete model. As shown in TABLE VII, the baseline model, which only includes the basic 3D convolutional feature extractor and classification head, achieves overall accuracy (OA) of 88.82% (PU), 91.92% (Houston), 85.61% (IP), and 86.76% (WHU-HH) across the four datasets. This result validates that even a simple deep learning architecture can capture the basic feature patterns of hyperspectral data. However, the baseline model has obvious limitations when handling complex scenes, particularly on the IP dataset, where its 85.61% OA reflects the model’s difficulty in handling extremely imbalanced category distributions and small sample issues. The relatively low Kappa coefficient (only 83.87% on the IP dataset) further indicates that the baseline model’s classification results exhibit significant randomness and lack stable discriminative capability.

After adding the MSFA module, performance improved significantly. On the PU dataset, the OA increased from 88.82% to 93.63% (+ 4.81%), with this significant improvement mainly attributed to the multi-scale feature extraction capabilities of MSFA. Through parallel dilated convolutional branches, MSFA can simultaneously capture hierarchical features ranging from local textures to global structures. On the IP dataset, the performance improvement is even more pronounced (OA improved by 6.05%), indicating that multi-scale features are crucial for identifying farmlands of different sizes in agricultural scenarios. The improvement in AA (from 92.06% to 95.25% on the IP dataset) is particularly noteworthy, indicating that MSFA effectively improves the classification performance of small sample categories. This improvement can be attributed to the large receptive field branches capturing more contextual information, providing additional discriminative clues for small sample categories. From a technical perspective, the success of MSFA primarily stems from three aspects: first, the progressive expansion rate design (1, 6, 12, 18) ensures complete scale coverage, avoiding scale blind spots; second, the parallel structure guarantees the independent extraction of features at different scales, preventing feature confusion; finally, the bottleneck fusion structure effectively integrates multi-scale information while controlling computational complexity.

Adding the ECSA module alone also resulted in a significant performance improvement, with the OA of the four datasets increasing by 5.07%, 3.41%, 6.09%, and 5.58%, respectively. ECSA employs cascaded channel and spatial attention mechanisms to adaptively emphasise important features while suppressing redundant information. On the WHU-HH dataset, the accuracy rate (AA) improved from 85.83% to 93.07% (+ 7.24%), demonstrating the importance of attention mechanisms in handling fine-grained crop classification. The rich detail information provided by ultra-high resolution requires selective attention, which ECSA precisely provides. ECSA’s advantages are reflected in several key designs: the dual pooling strategy (average pooling and max pooling) comprehensively captures the statistical characteristics of features, avoiding information loss; the MLP bottleneck structure in channel attention reduces computational costs while maintaining expressive power; the large kernel convolution in spatial attention ensures sufficient spatial context modeling capability; and residual connections enable deep interaction between channel and spatial attention, enhancing the discriminative power of features.

When using both MSFA and ECSA with simple concatenation fusion, performance improves, but the improvement is limited. On the PU dataset, the overall accuracy (OA) is only 94.54%, which is significantly lower than the improvement achieved by using either module alone (0.65–0.91%). This phenomenon highlights an important issue: simple feature concatenation fails to fully leverage the synergistic potential of the two modules and may even lead to feature redundancy and mutual interference. Specifically, the multi-scale features extracted by MSFA and the attention-enhanced features from ECSA have different characteristics and importance distributions. Simple concatenation ignores these differences, treating all features equally, resulting in suboptimal feature utilisation. On the Houston dataset, the Kappa coefficient of simple fusion (95.35%) is only 0.24% higher than using ECSA alone, barely reflecting the advantages of the combination. This further underscores the necessity of an adaptive fusion mechanism.

After introducing the dynamic gate fusion mechanism, DGFNet achieved significant performance improvements. Across all datasets, the full model significantly outperformed the simple fusion version: OA improved by 2.37% on the PU dataset, Houston improved by 1.57%, IP improved by 2.47%, and WHU-HH improved by 2.07%. This consistent improvement demonstrates the effectiveness and generalisation ability of the dynamic fusion strategy. The success of the dynamic gating mechanism can be understood from the following aspects: (1) Adaptive weight allocation: The gating network dynamically generates fusion weights based on the global statistical information of input features, achieving data-driven feature selection. For regions with complex textures, the system automatically increases the weight of MSFA features; for regions with prominent spectral features, it relies more on the output of ECSA. (2) Soft fusion strategy: Unlike hard selection, soft fusion retains information from both feature types, achieving a smooth transition through continuous weight adjustment to avoid sudden information loss. (3) End-to-end learning: Fusion weights are jointly optimised with the entire network via backpropagation, ensuring consistency between the fusion strategy and classification objectives.

It is worth noting that the contributions of each component exhibit good consistency across different datasets. Whether in urban scenes (PU, Houston) or agricultural scenes (IP, WHU-HH), and regardless of medium or ultra-high resolution, the DGFNet architecture demonstrates stable performance advantages. This cross-domain consistency demonstrates the versatility and robustness of DGFNet. Especially on the most challenging IP dataset, the full model achieved an 8.44% improvement in OA and a 13.32% improvement in the Kappa coefficient compared to the baseline, fully validating the synergistic effects of the components when handling extremely imbalanced data. Standard deviation analysis shows that the complete model not only achieves optimal performance but also exhibits the highest stability (with an OA standard deviation of just 0.72% on the IP dataset), indicating that the dynamic fusion mechanism effectively reduces the model’s sensitivity to random initialisation and data perturbations. Through this series of ablation experiments, this paper clearly demonstrates the unique contributions of each component in DGFNet and their synergistic mechanisms. MSFA provides multi-scale spatial feature representations, ECSA achieves adaptive feature enhancement, and dynamic gated fusion seamlessly integrates the two, ultimately constructing an efficient, accurate, and robust hyperspectral image classification framework.

Fusion strategy analysis

To gain a deeper understanding of the effectiveness of the dynamic gate fusion mechanism, this paper systematically compares DGFNet with five different feature fusion strategies: Cross Attention Fusion47 draws inspiration from the Transformer architecture, employing an 8-head cross attention mechanism to enable bidirectional information exchange between MSFA and ECSA features, and utilizing a Query-Key-Value mechanism to capture complex dependencies between features; Hierarchical Fusion48 processes features at three resolution scales, constructs a pyramid structure through downsampling, and then fuses them layer by layer through upsampling to achieve multi-scale feature integration; Parallel Fusion49 processes two branches in parallel, adaptively weights the outputs of MSFA and ECSA using learnable weights α and (1-α), and enhances feature interaction through channel-level fusion; Recurrent Fusion50 uses GRU units to iteratively enhance features twice, gradually optimizing the fusion representation through a recurrent structure. Sequential fusion51 adopts the most direct serial connection method, with input features sequentially passing through the MSFA and ECSA modules. Figure 8 shows the detailed performance comparison of each fusion strategy across four datasets. As shown in Fig. 8, dynamic gate fusion achieved the best performance across all datasets. On the PU dataset, DGFNet achieved an OA of 96.91%, which is 2.48% points higher than the second-best hierarchical fusion (94.43%). This significant improvement is mainly attributed to the dynamic gate mechanism, which can adaptively adjust the contribution weights of MSFA and ECSA based on the characteristics of the input features. For complex objects in urban scenes, such as buildings and roads, the system can dynamically balance the importance of multi-scale spatial features and channel-spatial attention features.

Although cross-attention fusion achieves deep interaction between features, its 93.86% OA indicates that complex attention calculations do not always lead to performance improvements. This method requires features to be reshaped into a sequence format and multi-head attention operations to be performed, which not only increases computational overhead (74 s of training time for the PU dataset) but may also introduce unnecessary feature confusion. Especially in scenarios with high spatial resolution, the forced serialisation operation may disrupt the original spatial structural information. The hierarchical fusion strategy performs relatively well on the PU dataset (OA 94.43%), effectively preserving the hierarchical structure of multi-scale information by progressively fusing features at different scales. However, on the IP dataset, its performance significantly declines (OA 90.57%, with a standard deviation as high as 3.08%), indicating that this strategy is highly sensitive to data distribution. Multiple downsampling and upsampling operations may lead to feature information loss in small-sample categories, which is particularly evident in the extremely imbalanced IP dataset. Parallel fusion achieves 95.19% OA on the Houston dataset by simultaneously processing both types of features through weight learning, demonstrating good stability (standard deviation of only 0.49%). The advantage of this strategy lies in maintaining feature independence, avoiding information loss that may result from serial processing. However, the simple weighted summation mechanism limits its ability to model complex feature interactions, leading to poor performance on the WHU-HH dataset, which requires fine-grained feature differentiation (OA 91.38%). The cyclic fusion strategy iteratively enhances features using GRU units, theoretically capable of capturing temporal dependencies between features. However, experimental results show its performance is suboptimal (PU dataset OA 93.35%). Analysis reveals that forcing spatial features to be modelled as temporal information may not align with the inherent characteristics of hyperspectral data. At the same time, the computational overhead from multiple iterations (typically longer training times) also limits its practicality. Sequential fusion, as the simplest strategy, first applies MSFA followed by ECSA, achieving only 92.02% OA on the Houston dataset, significantly lower than other fusion strategies. This validates the hypothesis that a fixed processing order cannot adapt to the specific requirements of different datasets. In some scenarios, attention enhancement may be more necessary first, while in others, multi-scale feature extraction may be more suitable.

In terms of computational efficiency, dynamic gate fusion not only outperforms other methods in terms of performance but also demonstrates a good balance in computational efficiency. On the PU dataset, the training time of DGFNet (36s) is significantly lower than that of cross-attention (74s) and recurrent fusion (67s), while also outperforming hierarchical fusion (49s). This efficiency advantage stems from the lightweight design of the gating network: fusion weights are generated solely through global pooling and simple fully connected layers, avoiding complex feature transformations and iterative computations. On the large-scale WHU-HH dataset, the efficiency difference is even more pronounced. Hierarchical fusion requires 147s of training time, while DGFNet only needs 108s, representing a 26.5% efficiency improvement. Considering that DGFNet also achieves a 2.98% OA improvement, this efficiency gain has significant practical significance. Especially in scenarios requiring the processing of large-scale hyperspectral images or real-time applications, computational efficiency becomes a critical consideration. In addition to performance and efficiency advantages, dynamic gated fusion also demonstrates excellent robustness. Standard deviation analysis reveals stability differences among fusion strategies: DGFNet exhibits lower standard deviation across all datasets (PU: 1.32%, Houston: 0.34%, IP: 0.72%, WHU-HH: 1.09%), significantly outperforming hierarchical fusion (standard deviation of 3.08% on the IP dataset) and cyclic fusion (standard deviation of 2.50% on the PU dataset). This robustness advantage stems from its soft fusion mechanism—continuous weight adjustment enables smooth feature combination, avoiding the abrupt effects that hard selection or fixed weights may cause. Additionally, end-to-end joint optimisation ensures consistency between the fusion strategy and classification objectives, further enhancing the model’s stability under different initialisation conditions and data perturbations.

Through systematic comparative experiments and in-depth analysis, this study demonstrates the comprehensive advantages of the dynamic gate fusion strategy. First, in terms of classification accuracy, DGFNet achieves optimal performance on four benchmark datasets, with an average OA improvement of 2–3% points. Second, in terms of computational efficiency, the lightweight gating design reduces training time by 30–50% compared to complex fusion strategies, making it feasible for large-scale applications. Finally, in terms of robustness, the soft fusion mechanism ensures the model’s stability and generalisation capability. The results indicate that adaptive feature fusion is key to improving hyperspectral image classification performance, and the dynamic gating mechanism provides a simple and effective solution for achieving this adaptability. The success of this strategy not only validates the complementarity of the MSFA and ECSA modules but also provides important insights for designing more advanced feature fusion methods in the future: dynamically adjusting the contributions of different feature extractors through learning-based mechanisms while maintaining computational efficiency is an effective approach to achieving high-performance classification.

Conclusions

This paper proposes a Dynamic Gated Fusion Network with Hierarchical Multi-Scale Attention for Hyperspectral Image Classification (DGFNet), which effectively addresses the limitations of existing methods in feature extraction and fusion by innovatively combining multi-scale feature aggregation, enhanced attention mechanisms, and adaptive fusion strategies. The core innovation of DGFNet lies in the organic integration of three carefully designed modules. First, the Multi-Scale Feature Aggregator (MSFA) employs a pyramid dilated convolution structure to achieve comprehensive scale coverage from local texture to global context, effectively capturing the multi-scale spatial features of different land cover categories in hyperspectral images. Second, the Enhanced Channel-Space Attention (ECSA) module employs cascaded attention mechanisms and multi-pooling strategies to achieve adaptive feature enhancement, significantly improving the model’s ability to focus on discriminative features. Most importantly, the Dynamic Gate Fusion module achieves adaptive integration of MSFA and ECSA features by learning input-related fusion weights, overcoming the limitations of traditional fixed fusion strategies. Extensive experiments on four benchmark datasets with different characteristics thoroughly validate the effectiveness and generalisation ability of DGFNet. On the PU dataset, DGFNet achieves an overall accuracy of 96.91%; on the Houston dataset, it achieved an overall accuracy of 97.12%; on the IP dataset with extremely imbalanced category distributions, it achieved an overall accuracy of 94.05% and an average accuracy of 97.43%; and on the ultra-high-resolution WHU-HH dataset, it also achieved an outstanding performance of 94.46%. These results not only demonstrate the effectiveness of the method but more importantly showcase its stability and robustness across different application scenarios. Ablation experiments and fusion strategy analysis further reveal the technical foundations underlying DGFNet’s success. Compared to other fusion strategies, the dynamic gating mechanism not only outperforms in classification accuracy but also demonstrates significant advantages in computational efficiency and model stability, reducing training time by 30–50% compared to complex fusion strategies while maintaining low performance variability.

The contributions of this study are not only reflected in performance improvements but, more importantly, in providing new design ideas for the field of hyperspectral image classification. The successful implementation of the dynamic gating fusion mechanism demonstrates the importance of adaptive feature fusion in processing complex remote sensing data, providing an important reference for the design of more advanced deep learning architectures in the future. Additionally, DGFNet achieves a good balance between computational efficiency and classification accuracy, making it highly practical for real-world remote sensing applications, particularly in fields such as precision agriculture, environmental monitoring, and urban planning that require high-precision object recognition.
