Negotiating energy, fear, and control: a qualitative study of nutrition and exercise experiences among middle-aged adults with obesity and diabetes-related cardiomyopathy
Authors: Lijuan Wang, Lulu Gu, Xiaoyong Wan, Kangzhen Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMiddle-aged adults with obesity and diabetes-related cardiomyopathy (DRCM) face competing demands when attempting to implement dietary and physical-activity recommendations. However, little is known about how day-to-day decisions are negotiated under fluctuating energy, symptom-related fear, and competing role responsibilities.ObjectiveTo explore experiences of nutrition and physical-activity management among middle-aged adults with obesity and DRCM, and to develop an explanatory framework to inform individualised lifestyle counselling in general practice.MethodsWe conducted a qualitative study in the Department of General Practice, Zhongda Hospital, Southeast University (Nanjing, China), between August 2024 and August 2025. Seventeen adults aged 40–60 years with obesity (body mass index ≥ 28 kg/m²) and clinician-diagnosed DRCM participated in semi-structured, in-depth interviews. Data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis, theoretically informed by self-regulation theory and the Capability–Opportunity–Motivation–Behaviour (COM-B) framework.ResultsFour interrelated themes described how participants managed nutrition and physical activity in everyday life: (1) negotiating between energy and fear; (2) confronting the limits of rigid self-control; (3) integrating family involvement into routines; and (4) reconstructing control through flexible pacing. Synthesising these themes, we propose the Energy–Fear–Control (EFC) cycle—a novel explanatory model in which perceived energy availability shapes feasibility, symptom-related fear constrains action, rigid control strategies contribute to fatigue and rebound, and family-enabled co-regulation supports flexible adaptation and sustained engagement.ConclusionsThe EFC cycle extends existing accounts of lifestyle management by specifying a cyclical mechanism rather than a linear “adherence” pathway, and by highlighting actionable leverage points for general practice—assessing “energy reality,” eliciting fear triggers, involving family early, and negotiating small, repeatable adjustments that remain feasible under midlife constraints.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-026-03320-7.Keywords: Diabetes-related cardiomyopathy, Primary care, Lifestyle self-management, Family involvement, Reflexive thematic analysis, Energy–Fear–Control (EFC) cycle, Obesity, Qualitative study, Nutrition, Exercise, Self-regulation, COM-B, Adherence

Introduction

Diabetes-related cardiomyopathy (DRCM) is a distinct myocardial disorder characterised by ventricular hypertrophy, interstitial fibrosis, and diastolic or systolic dysfunction that occurs in people with diabetes independent of coronary artery disease or hypertension [1]. With the global rise in obesity and type 2 diabetes, DRCM has become an increasingly important contributor to heart-failure morbidity and premature mortality [2, 3]. Middle-aged adults with obesity may face particular challenges, as metabolic overload and sedentary patterns compound diabetes-related haemodynamic stress [4, 5].

Lifestyle modification—including individualised nutrition and physical activity—remains central to the prevention and management of DCM [6]. Dietary optimisation can mitigate ectopic lipid accumulation and oxidative stress, while moderate, structured exercise can improve insulin sensitivity, myocardial function, and quality of life [7]. Nevertheless, sustaining these regimens in everyday life is often difficult [8, 9]. For many, the need to restrict intake while preserving sufficient energy for work and family responsibilities creates ongoing physiological and psychological tension [10, 11]. Symptom-related fears (e.g., hypoglycaemia, palpitations, or perceived cardiac strain) may further undermine confidence in physical activity, leaving patients caught between the desire to protect the heart and the need to maintain daily functioning [11].

Most research on lifestyle management in diabetes or heart failure has prioritised quantitative outcomes, with comparatively limited attention to patients’ lived experiences [12, 13]. Consequently, less is understood about how people with DRCM interpret bodily sensations, respond to fluctuating energy, and translate clinical advice into workable routines. Qualitative research across chronic conditions suggests that effective self-management depends not only on self-discipline but also on emotional adaptation and social collaboration [14, 15]. In China, where health-related practices are closely intertwined with family life, diet and physical activity are frequently co-managed within households rather than enacted individually [16, 17]. Family members may assist with meal planning, symptom monitoring, and emotional support, thereby extending care beyond the clinic and helping convert biomedical recommendations into culturally meaningful and practically sustainable routines [18].

Despite strong evidence that lifestyle modification improves cardiometabolic outcomes, clinicians in routine practice still encounter a pragmatic gap: many patients understand recommendations yet struggle to implement them sustainably [19, 20]. This gap is not fully explained by adherence rates or single time-point behavioural measures, because day-to-day self-management is dynamic—shaped by fluctuating bodily sensations (e.g., fatigue, palpitations), anticipatory fear, competing work–family role demands, and ongoing negotiation within households [21, 22]. In addition, opportunities for diet and physical activity are constrained by broader social and structural conditions, including work schedules, food environments and affordability, access to safe spaces for activity, and healthcare resources [23]. In this sense, “capacity” and “motivation” are expressed within real-world limits rather than in isolation [24]. Qualitative enquiry is therefore particularly well suited to this question because it can elucidate how patients make sense of symptoms and risk over time, and how relational and contextual resources are mobilised to make lifestyle change feasible [25]. By developing an empirically grounded explanatory model, this study aims to provide a clinically usable lens to support family-embedded, context-sensitive counselling in general practice [19, 26].

From a behavioural-science perspective, these adaptive processes can be conceptualised through self-regulation theory—which emphasises how individuals monitor internal cues and feedback and adjust actions accordingly [27] —and the Capability–Opportunity–Motivation–Behaviour (COM-B) model, which frames behaviour as arising from capability, opportunity, and motivation [28]. Applied to DRCM, these frameworks may help clarify how patients balance perceived energy, symptom-related fear, and control strategies while incorporating family participation into daily life.

Accordingly, we aimed to explore how middle-aged adults with obesity and DRCM make day-to-day decisions about nutrition and physical activity under conditions of fluctuating energy, symptom-related fear, and competing work and family responsibilities. We further aimed to develop an empirically grounded explanatory framework—the Energy–Fear–Control (EFC) cycle—to describe how bodily cues, emotional regulation, relational co-management, and contextual constraints interact over time to shape sustainable (or unsustainable) lifestyle patterns. By articulating these mechanisms and leverage points, the study seeks to inform practical, family-integrated, and context-sensitive counselling strategies in general practice.

Methods

Study design

We employed a descriptive–interpretive qualitative design and used reflexive thematic analysis to explore how middle-aged adults with obesity and diabetes-related cardiomyopathy (DRCM) negotiate nutrition and physical-activity behaviours in daily life. Analysis was theoretically informed by self-regulation theory and the Capability–Opportunity–Motivation–Behaviour (COM-B) framework [14, 29], which offer complementary lenses on behavioural adaptation in chronic disease management. The study is reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ).

Setting and participants

The study was conducted in the Department of General Practice at Zhongda Hospital, Southeast University (Nanjing, China), between August 2024 and August 2025. Potential participants were identified through outpatient clinics and electronic health records. We used purposive sampling to capture variation across clinical and contextual characteristics relevant to lifestyle feasibility, guided by the concept of information power. Seventeen participants (nine men and eight women) were recruited.

Eligibility criteria were: age 40–60 years; confirmed type 2 diabetes mellitus; clinician-diagnosed DRCM, verified by echocardiography showing diastolic dysfunction with preserved ejection fraction in the absence of coronary artery disease or hypertension; and obesity defined using the Chinese criterion (body mass index ≥ 28 kg/m²) [30].Exclusion criteria included recent acute cardiovascular events, decompensated heart failure, or conditions likely to preclude meaningful interview participation (e.g., severe psychiatric illness affecting communication).

To align sampling with the study objective and to ensure diversity, we applied a sampling matrix and iteratively targeted under-represented profiles during recruitment. In addition to sex, occupation, diabetes duration, and perceived disease severity, we sought diversity in educational attainment, employment patterns (e.g., shift work vs. non-shift work/retired), health insurance type, and residential context (urban vs. peri-urban/rural), as proxies for socioeconomic opportunity structures shaping diet and physical activity. We also considered family role demands (e.g., childcare/eldercare responsibilities) given their influence on energy availability and routine feasibility. The sampling matrix was reviewed after early interviews to guide subsequent invitations.

Prior to interview, the recruiting clinician (general practitioner) confirmed clinical stability and eligibility through medical-record review. Cognitive and physical ability to participate was assessed by interviewers at the start of each interview using brief orientation and communication checks (e.g., coherent responses, sustained attention, and ability to engage in a 30–60-minute conversation), with breaks offered when needed. Psychiatric exclusions were assessed via medical-record review and clinician confirmation (documented diagnoses or current symptoms judged likely to interfere with communication).

Data collection

Data were collected through semi-structured, in-depth interviews conducted in a private consultation room by two trained researchers with experience in qualitative health research. Interviews lasted approximately 30–60 min, were conducted in Mandarin Chinese, and were audio-recorded with participant consent. Recordings were transcribed verbatim within 24 h.

The interview guide was developed from prior literature on lifestyle management in diabetes and refined following pilot testing with two patients. Questions explored perceptions of energy, fatigue, and bodily capability; experiences of dietary regulation and physical activity; fears of symptom exacerbation (e.g., hypoglycaemia, palpitations, or dyspnoea); family and social influences on eating and activity; and strategies used to maintain control over daily routines. Field notes documenting contextual details and non-verbal cues were written immediately after each interview. To support interpretive credibility, five participants completed brief follow-up telephone conversations to clarify and confirm interpretations. The interview guide was study-developed and has not been previously published; an English-language version is provided in Supplementary File 1.

Data analysis

Data analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s six-phase reflexive thematic analysis [31].After familiarisation (reading and re-reading transcripts), two researchers conducted inductive line-by-line coding using NVivo 14 (QSR International, Melbourne, Australia). Coding generated 112 initial codes (NVivo nodes). Through iterative refinement—including merging overlapping codes, clarifying code definitions, and adding new codes when data did not fit existing labels—these were consolidated into 48 analytic codes, which were collated into 11 subthemes and four overarching themes.

Theme development proceeded through iterative team discussions supported by reflexive memoing and an audit trail documenting analytic decisions and refinements. Self-regulation theory and the COM-B framework were used as sensitising concepts to interpret relationships among themes without constraining the inductive process [14, 29]. Representative quotations were translated into English by bilingual researchers and back-translated to check semantic fidelity.

To develop the explanatory framework (the EFC cycle), we undertook an additional interpretive step after theme finalisation. The team examined relationships among themes (e.g., temporal sequencing, perceived causal links, and reinforcing feedback patterns described by participants) and constructed a thematic map representing cyclical dynamics. The proposed cycle was repeatedly checked against transcripts and field notes and refined to ensure it remained grounded in participants’ accounts.

Trustworthiness and Rigor

We used complementary strategies to enhance trustworthiness. Credibility was supported through investigator triangulation and follow-up conversations with a subset of participants. Dependability was strengthened by maintaining an audit trail capturing analytic procedures and code development. Confirmability was supported by reflexive memoing and peer debriefing. Transferability was enhanced by providing detailed descriptions of the setting, participant characteristics, and the contextual conditions in which nutrition and physical-activity practices were enacted.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhongda Hospital, Southeast University (approval number 2023ZDKYSB281). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. Data were anonymised and securely stored, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Participant characteristics

Seventeen participants (nine men and eight women) aged 40–60 years were interviewed between August 2024 and August 2025. All had type 2 diabetes mellitus and echocardiographically confirmed diabetes-related cardiomyopathy with preserved ejection fraction. The mean body mass index (BMI) was 31.2 ± 2.8 kg/m², and the median duration of diabetes was 9 years (interquartile range [6–14]). Twelve participants were married and employed full time; five were retired or homemakers. Ten participants reported at least one additional metabolic comorbidity (e.g., hypertension or dyslipidaemia). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarised in Table 1.

Overview of emergent themes

Reflexive thematic analysis identified four interrelated themes describing how participants negotiated nutrition and physical activity amid competing demands of work, family, and self-care. The themes were: (1) Fuel versus flare (balancing perceived energy against fear of symptom exacerbation); (2) Rules that unravel (the limits of rigid self-control); (3) Kitchen-table cardiology (family mediation and collaborative adjustment); and (4) Pacing the possible (reconstructing control through flexible adaptation). Together, these themes informed the Energy–Fear–Control (EFC) cycle, in which bodily cues, emotional regulation, and relational support interact to shape what feels feasible over time. Theme 1: “fuel versus flare” — negotiating between energy and fear

Participants described a daily struggle to balance the energy required for work and family responsibilities with fear of worsening cardiac symptoms. Many viewed energy as a limited resource that needed to be rationed: “My energy feels like a phone battery […] when it’s used up at work, there’s nothing left for exercise” (P04). Others described depletion after long hours: “I leave the office at nine or ten […] the doctor says to walk after dinner, but honestly, I just want to lie down” (P09).

This sense of depletion shaped decisions about movement and food, particularly when bodily cues were interpreted as warning signs. One participant explained, “If I feel my heart beating faster, I stop everything right away […] I’d rather rest than risk something happening” (P07). Another described uncertainty about symptom meaning: “Sometimes I can’t tell if it’s low sugar or my heart […] I stop walking and wait until I feel safe again” (P11).

Role obligations intensified this tension: “People rely on me at work. I can’t afford to collapse […] I don’t push myself” (P03). Many described a reinforcing cycle in which fatigue heightened fear and fear restricted activity: “If I rest too much, I feel weak […] but if I move too much, my heart races. Either way, I’m scared” (P10). As one participant summarised, “Every day is like negotiating with my own body” (P06).

Theme 2: “rules that unravel” — the limits of rigid self-control

Following diagnosis, many described an initial period of strict discipline, framed as both medical necessity and moral duty. “I made a chart—what to eat, how many steps, when to check blood sugar […] it made me feel responsible” (P02). Another participant described reassurance from precision: “If I followed the plan exactly, I felt safe […] like building a wall between me and the disease” (P13).

However, rigid routines soon collided with midlife demands and unpredictability. “Some days I have meetings all day and dinner with clients […] I can’t count every bite” (P09). When regimens became difficult to sustain, participants reported guilt and self-blame: “When I miss a day of walking, I feel like I’ve failed again […] life gets in the way” (P10). Several described cycles of over-restriction followed by relapse: “I control myself very strictly for a week, then […] I just give up and eat what I want” (P08).

Participants also linked these struggles to expectations of strength and dependability: “People expect me to be capable […] if I slow down, colleagues will think I can’t handle my job” (P14). As one participant reflected, “The rules keep me safe […] but sometimes they also keep me trapped” (P07).

Theme 3: “kitchen-table cardiology” — family mediation and collaborative adjustment

For most participants, lifestyle management was a shared family project rather than an individual task. Practical support was common: “My wife keeps an eye on everything I eat […] she even changed how she cooks—less oil, more vegetables” (P06). Some described shared activity as relational motivation: “My son joined me in evening walks […] it feels like we’re a team” (P11).

Family involvement also provided emotional reassurance and helped sustain engagement. One participant said, “When I feel tired, my daughter says, ‘Don’t be afraid […] move slowly’—she knows how to calm me down” (P15). Another noted that the style of support mattered: “If my family reminds me gently instead of scolding, I listen more” (P09).

Participants emphasised ongoing negotiation to preserve both health and harmony. “Sometimes my wife wants me to rest more, but I tell her a bit of walking helps […] we find a middle way” (P03). Many described adapting advice to household routines rather than enforcing strict rules: “If we have guests […] I eat a little more but balance it the next day” (P12). As one participant summarised, “We learn together […] they help me follow the plan, but also remind me that life should still feel normal” (P01).

Theme 4: “pacing the possible” — reconstructing control through flexible adaptation

Over time, many participants described shifting from strict control to flexible pacing aligned with bodily cues. One participant reflected, “At first, I tried to do everything perfectly […] but that only made me tired and worried. Now I do what I can” (P07). Others described learning personal limits through experience: “Some days I walk ten minutes, some days twenty […] if I feel fine the next morning, I know that’s my right amount” (P03).

Family collaboration often reinforced this gradual adaptation: “My husband goes out walking with me […] if either of us feels tired, we rest […] it’s about not giving up” (P11). Participants also described integrating small adjustments into everyday responsibilities: “I take the stairs slowly […] even at work, I stand up every hour to stretch” (P09). Another explained, “When I know I’ll have a late meeting, I eat lighter during the day […] to balance things” (P14).

This shift was associated with calmer agency: “I used to be scared all the time […] now I’m living with the disease, not fighting it” (P12). As one participant concluded, “Before, I thought control meant strictness. Now I think it means balance” (P02).

Integrative interpretation

Across narratives, participants described a cyclical process linking perceived energy, symptom-related fear, and behavioural control. Early after diagnosis, fear dominated decision-making, leading many to conserve energy and limit activity to avoid perceived cardiac risk. Rigid routines briefly restored a sense of order but were often undermined by work and family pressures. Over time, family support transformed self-management into a shared endeavour: through reminders, meal adjustments, and companionship, relatives helped translate clinical advice into practicable habits. With experience, participants described a shift from strict discipline towards flexible adaptation guided by bodily cues and emotional regulation.

Taken together, these accounts form the Energy–Fear–Control (EFC) cycle—a dynamic pattern through which middle-aged adults with DRCM regulate lifestyle practices within the constraints of midlife roles and opportunity structures. Fear constrains action; overcontrol contributes to fatigue and rebound; collaboration supports balance; and flexible adaptation sustains engagement. This cycle reframes lifestyle management as ongoing negotiation rather than linear compliance and highlights leverage points for family-embedded, context-sensitive support in general practice [14, 29, 32].

Discussion

This study explored how middle-aged adults with obesity and diabetes-related cardiomyopathy (DRCM) negotiated nutrition and physical-activity management in a general-practice setting amid competing work, family, and health demands. Across 17 in-depth interviews, we identified four interrelated themes that together informed a dynamic Energy–Fear–Control (EFC) cycle, describing how perceived energy availability, symptom-related fear, and control strategies interacted over time and were shaped by relational co-management. The findings suggest that self-management in this group is not a linear behaviour-change pathway but an ongoing negotiation between physical vulnerability, emotional regulation, and social responsibility.

A prominent concern underlying daily decisions was bodily safety. Participants described “saving energy” and avoiding activities perceived to trigger discomfort, reflecting an embodied form of risk appraisal in which sensations are translated into judgements about threat. Similar “embodied monitoring” has been reported across chronic illness contexts, where patients interpret bodily cues as signals requiring adjustment and caution [33–35]. For participants managing demanding jobs and family roles, conserving energy was often framed as pragmatic rather than avoidant. Clinically, this suggests that counselling may be more acceptable when exercise guidance is positioned around energy management and graded confidence-building, rather than endurance or target attainment, while also acknowledging patients’ legitimate need for safety.

Many participants responded to fear and uncertainty by seeking control through strict regimens (e.g., counting steps, weighing food, fixed schedules). These strategies initially provided reassurance but were frequently destabilised by midlife constraints such as deadlines, caregiving, social obligations, and fatigue. This pattern aligns with self-regulation accounts in which high-control strategies can become costly under sustained demands and may contribute to depletion and lapses. Within the moral landscape of Chinese midlife—where diligence, dependability, and fulfilling role expectations are culturally salient—such lapses were often accompanied by guilt and self-blame [36, 37]. Rather than reflecting resistance, these accounts emphasised exhaustion and competing responsibilities. For clinical practice, the implication is to design plans that are explicitly flexible and feasible under real-world constraints, and to support “achievable effort” to reduce the over-striving–collapse cycle described here and in related diabetes literature [38].

Self-management also unfolded within family life. Participants described meal modifications, shared walks, and reminders as forms of collective adaptation. In line with relational models of chronic illness, families acted as co-regulators who helped translate advice into routines through daily interaction and negotiation [39, 40]. In many Chinese households, food practices are closely tied to care and relational meaning; changes to recipes and meal routines therefore carry symbolic as well as practical significance. Participants’ descriptions of family negotiation as an expression of affection are consistent with conceptualisations of illness as a moral and relational experience [41]. For primary-care teams, these findings suggest that effective counselling may extend beyond the individual to incorporate the “family table,” supporting shared understanding and collaborative planning.

Over time, participants described a shift from strict rules to flexible pacing—walking “a little,” resting “before the heart protests,” and calibrating effort to bodily cues. This movement from rigidity to rhythm resonates with Paterson’s shifting-perspectives model [33] and with COM-B’s emphasis on adaptive capability and motivation in context [29]. In these accounts, control became less about perfection and more about responsiveness: knowing when to move, pause, and resume. This reframing supports a clinical approach that prioritises collaborative experimentation and iterative adjustment over prescriptive targets alone.

Taken together, the themes form the EFC cycle: perceived energy availability shapes what feels feasible; symptom-related fear constrains action; rigid control strategies may temporarily restore order but can contribute to fatigue and rebound; and family collaboration functions as relational co-regulation that supports flexible adaptation and sustained engagement under competing midlife roles. Clinically, the model suggests actionable leverage points for general practice—assessing “energy reality” and role demands, explicitly eliciting fear triggers and the meanings attached to symptoms, and negotiating small, repeatable adjustments that remain feasible during disruptions. Early involvement of a family member can shift support from monitoring and blame towards shared planning (e.g., meal preparation, timing, and task redistribution), increasing the likelihood that changes persist over time. Clinical translation of the EFC cycle for general practice

Situating the EFC cycle within social determinants of health

While the EFC cycle describes proximal mechanisms of day-to-day self-management, participants’ accounts highlighted that these mechanisms operate within broader social and structural conditions. Work schedules, caregiving responsibilities, and job (in)security shaped energy availability and the regularity of meals and activity. Food affordability and household food environments influenced whether dietary changes were negotiable in practice. Access to healthcare, continuity of counselling, and the availability of credible health information affected confidence and perceived safety when adjusting activity, particularly when symptoms were interpreted as warning signs. Finally, the built environment (e.g., safe spaces for walking) influenced whether “small, repeatable” activity changes were realistic. Integrating these opportunity structures into the model helps avoid over-individualising responsibility and clarifies why rigid control strategies may fail under high contextual constraint. In general practice, brief assessment of these constraints (e.g., work hours, caregiving load, food access, and safe activity options) can support more realistic goal-setting and family collaboration plans.

Clinical implications for general practice

The EFC cycle provides practical leverage points because it specifies not only what patients do, but why behaviour becomes difficult to sustain under fluctuating energy, symptom-related fear, and competing midlife roles. Clinicians can use the model to (i) assess “energy reality” and role demands, (ii) elicit and reframe fear triggers, and (iii) replace rigid rules with flexible, repeatable micro-adjustments supported through family co-management. Operationalising family collaboration in routine general practice

Family collaboration can be operationalised as a structured, time-efficient process rather than a general recommendation. First, the GP can ask the patient to nominate a “support partner” (e.g., spouse, adult child, or parent) and obtain permission to involve them. A brief triadic check-in (in person, by speakerphone, or via secure messaging) can clarify household roles (“Who cooks?”, “Who buys groceries?”, “Who reminds whom?”), identify friction points (e.g., criticism, over-monitoring, conflicting beliefs), and agree on a shared, feasibility-framed goal (“What is one change the household can support this week?”). Second, the GP can translate goals into specific household actions: shared meal planning (e.g., one lower-oil cooking method and a portion routine), task redistribution to protect “energy reality” (e.g., family covering a draining chore on clinic days), co-activity micro-routines (e.g., 10–15-minute walks after dinner on three days/week, paced to symptoms), and supportive communication scripts that replace policing with encouragement. To reduce drop-out, clinicians can co-design a “minimum viable plan” for busy weeks and a re-entry rule after lapses (e.g., “restart at the next meal/day”), with follow-up focused on feasibility and relational dynamics rather than perfection. Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. Participants were recruited from a single tertiary hospital in eastern China, with many in stable employment, which may limit transferability to other regions and socioeconomic contexts. Although we purposively sought diversity in contextual characteristics, future research should include rural communities, those with more precarious employment, and older or retired populations, and should examine family dynamics and contextual constraints longitudinally. Nonetheless, this study provides a culturally grounded account of lifestyle management in DRCM and offers an explanatory model that integrates embodied risk appraisal, emotional regulation, relational co-management, and opportunity structures relevant to primary care.

Conclusion

Lifestyle management among middle-aged adults with obesity and DRCM is a dynamic process shaped by fear, role responsibility, and relational support. Through the EFC cycle, participants balanced bodily safety with the demands of work and family; rigid self-discipline was often difficult to sustain under these pressures, whereas family collaboration and flexible pacing supported stability. For primary care, these findings support moving beyond prescriptive guidance towards family-embedded, context-sensitive strategies that help patients align self-care with everyday life. Supporting ongoing negotiation—rather than demanding perfect adherence—may help transform fear into confidence and sustain long-term nutrition and physical-activity engagement in this growing population.
