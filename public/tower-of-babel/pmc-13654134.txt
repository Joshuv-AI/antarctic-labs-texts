Structural alterations in the superior temporal gyrus associated with depressive symptoms in migraine without aura: a voxel-based morphometry and surface-based morphometry magnetic resonance imaging study
Authors: Donghai Chen, Yayin Luo, Yuhao Zhang, Wenlong Zhang, Zhuqing Yuan, Zhixuan He, Haochong Xie, Meijun Qu, Tong Lin, Jiawei Li, Qingwei Song, Yanwei Miao, Zhe Wang, Jian Jiang, Renwang Pu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMigraine without aura (MwoA) frequently co-occurs with depressive symptoms, yet the neurobiological mechanisms underlying this comorbidity remain insufficiently characterized. This study integrated voxel-based morphometry (VBM) and surface-based morphometry (SBM) to compare gray matter volume (GMV) and cortical morphology between patients with MwoA and healthy controls (HC) and to further determine whether structural brain alterations vary across MwoA subgroups stratified by depressive symptom severity, thereby identifying potential structural correlates of depressive symptoms in this population.MethodsA total of 67 patients with MwoA {38 with lower depressive symptoms [MwoA-LS, 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17) <8] and 29 with higher depressive symptoms (MwoA-HS, HAMD-17 ≥8)} and 40 HC were included. High-resolution T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data were processed using CAT12 to derive GMV, cortical thickness (CT), local gyrification index (LGI), sulcal depth (SD), and fractal dimension (FD). Between-group comparisons were conducted using two-sample t-tests within a general linear model framework, with sex, education, total intracranial volume (TIV) (for VBM), mean CT (for SBM), and Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAMA) scores included as covariates. Statistical significance was defined using a voxel-/vertex-level threshold of P<0.001 (uncorrected), followed by cluster-level false discovery rate (FDR) correction at q<0.05.ResultsCompared with HC, patients with MwoA exhibited significant reductions in GMV in the left amygdala (cluster size =305; FDR-corrected q=0.046), left parahippocampal gyrus (cluster size =273; q=0.036), left lingual gyrus (two clusters, size =218, q=0.046 and size =177, q=0.046), and right angular gyrus extending into the middle occipital gyrus and precuneus (cluster size =427; q=0.036). No significant between-group differences were observed in any SBM measure. In the subgroup analysis, patients in the MwoA-HS group showed significantly lower LGI in the right superior temporal gyrus than those in the MwoA-LS group (cluster size =73; FDR-corrected q=0.019). Exploratory partial correlation analyses indicated that no imaging metric was significantly associated with HAMD-17 scores or other clinical measures after FDR correction (all q>0.05).ConclusionsPatients withMwoA exhibited widespread GMV reductions across brain regions involved in emotional and pain-related processing. In addition, patients with higher depressive symptom levels showed a distinct cortical morphological feature characterized by lower LGI in the right superior temporal gyrus, which was not detected by VBM. Given the exploratory nature of these findings, the lack of significant brain-clinical correlations after correction for multiple comparisons, and the cross-sectional study design, these results should be validated in larger longitudinal cohorts.Keywords: Depressive symptoms, voxel-based morphometry (VBM), migraine without aura (MwoA), surface-based morphometry (SBM), structural magnetic resonance imaging (structural MRI)

Introduction

Migraine is a common neurovascular disorder characterized by recurrent, typically throbbing headache attacks that are usually unilateral but may be bilateral and are often accompanied by hypersensitivity to light or sound and visual disturbances. As a highly disabling neurological condition, migraine imposes a substantial global socioeconomic burden and markedly reduces quality of life (1). According to the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3) classification, migraine without aura (MwoA) is the more prevalent clinical subtype, whereas migraine with aura occurs less frequently (2). Notably, migraine frequently co-occurs with depressive symptoms, which can further complicate clinical assessment and management (3,4). Nevertheless, the neurobiological mechanisms linking MwoA to depressive symptoms remain incompletely understood.

Neuroimaging has substantially advanced our understanding of the functional neural mechanisms underlying migraine and its comorbidity with depressive symptoms. Previous studies have reported non-additive pathophysiological mechanisms associated with depressive symptoms, specific relationships between subgenual cingulate atrophy and mood disturbances (5,6), and distinct dorsolateral prefrontal cortex-amygdala connectivity patterns in migraine patients with vs. without depressive symptoms (7). Despite these functional neuroimaging findings, structural brain alterations and their potential contributions to the association between migraine and depressive symptoms have received comparatively less investigation.

Structural neuroimaging findings in migraine, however, remain inconsistent. Although several voxel-based morphometry (VBM) studies have reported reductions in gray matter volume (GMV) within pain-related regions, large-scale investigations have produced less consistent evidence. Affatato et al., using data from 43,930 UK Biobank participants, found no significant volumetric differences in most subcortical structures between individuals with migraine and healthy controls (HC) and identified only limited differences, namely larger caudate and thalamic volumes, within an otherwise largely negative set of comparisons (8). Furthermore, transdiagnostic meta-analyses have identified both shared and disorder-specific cortical alterations across chronic pain, major depressive disorder (MDD), and anxiety disorders. Shared patterns include convergent involvement of insular and prefrontal regions, whereas disorder-specific findings include cortical thinning of the left middle temporal gyrus in MDD (9). Collectively, these observations support the presence of partially shared neural substrates for cognitive and emotional processing across these frequently comorbid conditions. Similarly, Brandl et al. (10) reported shared GMV reductions in the insula and medial prefrontal cortex across MDD, anxiety disorders, and chronic pain, predominantly within the default mode and salience networks, together with disorder-specific alterations in limbic and frontoparietal networks. Overall, these findings suggest that migraine, which is frequently comorbid with depression, may share transdiagnostic neural features with other chronic pain and affective disorders; however, direct examination of these shared features in migraine-specific structural neuroimaging studies remains limited.

VBM enables voxel-wise comparisons of regional gray and white matter volume but is relatively insensitive to macroscale cortical geometry. By contrast, surface-based morphometry (SBM) permits more precise characterization of cortical features—including cortical thickness (CT), local gyrification index (LGI), sulcal depth (SD), and fractal dimension (FD)—and therefore complements VBM by improving sensitivity to subtle cortical morphological alterations. In this exploratory study, we combined VBM and SBM to characterize structural brain differences between patients with MwoA and HC and further determined whether these structural measures differ between MwoA subgroups stratified according to depressive symptom severity [17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17)], with the objective of identifying potential structural correlates of depressive symptoms in this population. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-1113/rc).

Methods

Study participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University (No. PJ-KS-KY-2025-745), and all participants provided written informed consent before participation.

Between July 2024 and October 2025, patients diagnosed with MwoA were prospectively recruited from the Department of Neurology at The First Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University. Depressive symptoms were evaluated using the HAMD-17. Participants with MwoA and HAMD-17 scores ≥8 were classified as having higher depressive symptoms [MwoA with higher depressive symptoms (MwoA-HS)], whereas those with scores <8 were classified as having lower depressive symptoms [MwoA with lower depressive symptoms (MwoA-LS)]. This threshold was used exclusively for symptom screening and subgroup stratification and did not represent a formal psychiatric diagnosis of depressive disorder because no structured diagnostic interview was administered. All participants in the MwoA-HS group reported no psychiatric diagnosis predating migraine onset, consistent with the exclusion criteria described in the “Inclusion and exclusion criteria” section. HC were also included, all of whom had HAMD-17 scores <8.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants with MwoA were required to meet the following inclusion criteria: (I) fulfillment of the ICHD-3 diagnostic criteria established by the International Headache Society (IHS), a migraine history longer than three months, and assessment during the interictal phase; (II) absence of a migraine attack at the time of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and no attacks within the 48 hours preceding or following MRI acquisition; and (III) no use of analgesics, psychotropic medications, or hormone-based medications during the week before MRI acquisition.

The exclusion criteria for the MwoA group were as follows: (I) chronic pain other than headache or pain located outside the head or surrounding region; (II) any psychiatric or neurological disorder predating migraine onset; (III) left-handedness; (IV) any standard contraindication to MRI; (V) prior neuroimaging evidence of an intracranial mass or significant infarct; (VI) a history of alcohol dependence or substance use disorder; and (VII) current pregnancy or breastfeeding. Forty HC were enrolled. The HC participants had no history of migraine, vertigo, or primary headache disorders and were required to meet the same exclusion criteria applied to the patient group.

Neuropsychological assessment and clinical characteristics

Neuropsychological assessments and clinical data were obtained by trained neurologists within 24 hours before MRI scanning. Global cognitive function was evaluated using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). Headache pain intensity was assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS). Patients retrospectively rated the average pain intensity of their most recent migraine attack. The most recent attack had occurred within 1 week before MRI scanning, and all participants were confirmed to be in the interictal phase, defined as no attack within 48 hours of scanning, at the time of assessment. Depressive and anxiety symptoms were assessed using the HAMD-17 and Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAMA), respectively. Recorded clinical variables included migraine disease duration, monthly attack frequency, and mean duration of individual migraine attacks.

Image acquisition

All MRI data were acquired using a Philips Ingenia 3.0 T CX scanner with a three-dimensional T1-weighted turbo field echo (3D T1W-TFE) sequence. Imaging parameters were as follows: repetition time (TR) =6.7 ms, echo time (TE) =3.0 ms, inversion time (TI) ≈600 ms, flip angle =12°, receiver bandwidth =271.3 Hz, head coil =32-channel phased-array head coil, field of view (FOV) =256 mm × 256 mm, acquisition matrix =256×256, voxel size =1.00×1.00×1.00 mm3, and number of slices =188. Parallel imaging was implemented using the SENSitivity Encoding (SENSE) technique [anterior-posterior (AP) reduction factor =1, right-left (RL) reduction factor =2]. Total acquisition time was approximately 4 min 52 s.

All raw T1-weighted images underwent visual inspection for gross artifacts, excessive motion, and anatomical abnormalities and were additionally evaluated using the automated image quality rating (ImQ) implemented in the Computational Anatomy Toolbox (CAT12.8.2-r2170). In accordance with the CAT12 manual and previous studies, images with a rating of C+ or higher were considered to meet the quality criterion. DICOM images were subsequently converted to NIfTI format with MRIcroGL (v1.2). Subsequent analyses were conducted in MATLAB (R2022b, MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) using CAT12 within the SPM12 (r7771) framework.

The VBM processing pipeline comprised the following steps: (I) segmentation of T1-weighted images into gray matter (GM), white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid using tissue probability maps (TPM) and the diffeomorphic anatomical registration through an exponentiated Lie algebra (DARTEL) algorithm; (II) spatial normalization of segmented GM images to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space using MNI TPM; (III) modulation to preserve absolute GM volume after normalization; and (IV) spatial smoothing with an 8 mm full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel. Clusters were initially defined at an uncorrected voxel-level height threshold of P<0.001 and subsequently evaluated using cluster-level false discovery rate (FDR) correction (q<0.05). Only clusters that survived cluster-level FDR correction and contained at least 50 voxels were reported.

For SBM, the processing workflow included: (I) spatial registration and normalization of cortical surfaces to MNI space using DARTEL; (II) tissue segmentation and artifact correction; (III) cortical surface reconstruction and derivation of structural measures, including CT, LGI, SD, and FD; and (IV) surface-based smoothing with Gaussian kernels of 15 mm FWHM for CT and 20 mm FWHM for FD, SD, and LGI. Surface measures were estimated on the standard ICBM152 template mesh (fsaverage) implemented in CAT12, and the Desikan-Killiany-Tourville (DKT40) atlas was used for cortical parcellation (Figure 1). Vertex-wise statistical analyses followed an analogous thresholding strategy: clusters were defined using an uncorrected vertex-level threshold of P<0.001 and were subsequently subjected to cluster-level FDR correction (q<0.05).

Statistical analysis

The Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to assess the normality of continuous data distributions. Between-group comparisons of continuous variables were performed using independent-samples t-tests when distributional assumptions were satisfied and Mann-Whitney U tests otherwise. Categorical variables were compared using Chi-squared tests or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate.

For both VBM and SBM, between-group comparisons were conducted using two-sample t-tests within a general linear model framework in CAT12. Covariates in the VBM analyses included sex, education, total intracranial volume (TIV), and HAMA scores, whereas covariates in the SBM analyses included sex, education, mean CT, and HAMA scores. Statistical significance was assessed using an uncorrected voxel-/vertex-level threshold of P<0.001, followed by FDR correction for cluster-level multiple comparisons at q<0.05. As described in the “Study participants” section, patients were stratified into MwoA-LS and MwoA-HS subgroups according to HAMD-17 scores. Subgroup comparisons used the same statistical framework and significance criteria.

Regions exhibiting statistically significant between-group differences were defined as regions of interest (ROIs) for subsequent analyses. ROI values representing GMV or cortical morphology were extracted, and partial correlation analyses were performed in the full MwoA sample to evaluate associations with continuous clinical variables while controlling for sex, education, and HAMA scores.

Results

Comparison of demographic and clinical baseline characteristics

A total of 90 patients with MwoA were prospectively recruited from the Department of Neurology at The First Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University. After application of the exclusion criteria (Figure 2), 67 eligible patients completed assessment with the HAMD-17. These patients were stratified into two subgroups: MwoA-LS (n=38, HAMD-17 <8) and MwoA-HS (n=29, HAMD-17 ≥8). In addition, 40 HC (n=40) were included, all with HAMD-17 scores <8.

Nevertheless, patients in the MwoA-HS group exhibited a more severe headache profile than those in the MwoA-LS group (Table 2). Monthly attack frequency was significantly higher in MwoA-HS than in MwoA-LS (median number of attacks: MwoA-LS =3.0, MwoA-HS =6.0; U=362.5, P=0.016), and attack duration was also significantly longer (median attack duration: MwoA-LS =12.0 hours, MwoA-HS =18.0 hours; U=338.5, P=0.007). VBM analysis

After adjustment for sex, education level, TIV, and HAMA scores, the MwoA group showed significant reductions in GMV relative to the HC group in the following regions (FDR-corrected, P<0.05, cluster size ≥50 voxels): cluster 1 (273 voxels), with a peak in the left parahippocampal gyrus (−24, −24, −25.5; T=5.00) and extension into the left fusiform gyrus; cluster 2 (305 voxels), with a peak in the left amygdala (−25.5, −4.5, −16.5; T=4.08) and extension into the left hippocampus and parahippocampal gyrus; cluster 3 (218 voxels), with a peak in the left lingual gyrus (−24, −60, −6; T=4.23) and extension into the left fusiform gyrus. Because this cluster contained a high proportion of white matter, it should be interpreted cautiously. Cluster 4 (177 voxels) had a peak in the left lingual gyrus (−6, −66, −3; T=4.02). Cluster 5 (427 voxels) had a peak in the right angular gyrus (43.5, −70.5, 43.5; T=4.62) and extended into the right middle occipital gyrus and precuneus (Table 3, Figure 3).

SBM analysis

After adjustment for sex, education, mean CT, and HAMA scores, no significant regional differences in SBM measures were detected between patients with MwoA and HC.

In the subgroup analysis, LGI in the MwoA-HS group was significantly lower than that in the MwoA-LS group within the right superior temporal gyrus (STG; Table 3, Figure 4). This result indicates reduced cortical folding in the right superior temporal region among patients with higher depressive symptoms relative to those with lower depressive symptoms.

Clinical correlation analysis

After controlling for sex, years of education, and HAMA scores, exploratory analyses indicated a negative correlation between VAS score and the right middle occipital gyrus ROI value (r=−0.274, P=0.028), peak in angular gyrus, extending to middle occipital gyrus and precuneus). HAMD-17 scores were not significantly associated with any imaging measure (all P>0.05). In addition to imaging-clinical correlations, exploratory analyses were performed to examine intercorrelations among clinical variables. Exploratory analyses indicated a positive correlation between monthly attack frequency and VAS score (r=0.261, P=0.038). However, none of these correlations remained statistically significant after FDR correction for multiple comparisons (q>0.05).

Because the ROIs were selected from the same between-group contrasts, these exploratory associations are not statistically independent and require validation in an independent cohort. Furthermore, as none of the correlation analyses survived FDR correction, the observed associations should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Discussion

This study integrated VBM and SBM to characterize structural brain alterations in patients with MwoA stratified by depressive symptom burden. The principal findings were twofold: (I) relative to HC, patients with MwoA showed lower GMV in multiple regions implicated in emotional processing, pain modulation, and sensory integration; and (II) in the direct MwoA-HS vs. MwoA-LS comparison, the only significant surface-based difference was lower LGI in the right superior temporal gyrus, an alteration not detected by VBM.

GMV alterations in pain- and emotion-related regions

VBM revealed significant reductions in GMV in patients with MwoA, with no regions showing significant volume increases. The affected areas comprised the left amygdala (extending into the left hippocampus and parahippocampal gyrus), the left parahippocampal gyrus (extending into the left fusiform gyrus), two clusters in the left lingual gyrus (one extending into the left fusiform gyrus), and a large right-hemisphere cluster (427 voxels) encompassing the angular gyrus, middle occipital gyrus, and precuneus. By contrast, SBM detected no significant between-group differences in CT. Collectively, these VBM abnormalities involved regions implicated in emotional processing, pain modulation, and sensory integration in migraine (11-13). Reduced right angular gyrus volume has been less consistently described in the migraine literature. As a key node of the default mode network (DMN), the angular gyrus has been implicated in integrative processes relevant to pain modulation (14,15). Notably, the precuneus constituted a substantial proportion of the right-hemisphere cluster identified in the present study. The precuneus is also a core DMN hub with important roles in self-referential processing, episodic memory, and pain modulation (16-19). Morphological abnormalities of the precuneus have been repeatedly reported in migraine, and structural reductions in this region have been associated with altered intrinsic brain activity and impaired endogenous pain inhibition. Emerging evidence further suggests that neurovascular coupling abnormalities in the precuneus may be associated with migraine chronification, with depression acting as a potential mediator (20), and that precuneus GMV is related to altered emotional memory performance in patients with migraine (17). Considered together with the angular gyrus finding, the reduced precuneus volume observed here points to distributed structural vulnerability within the posterior DMN that may be relevant to altered pain modulation and cognitive-emotional processing in MwoA.

Lesion-based evidence provides additional support for the relevance of these networks: damage involving the periaqueductal GM, thalamus, and pain-related cortical regions has been associated with increased migraine risk (21). Such observations suggest that focal disruption of pain-modulatory circuitry may influence migraine susceptibility. Although the present findings were not attributable to overt demyelinating lesions, lower GMV in the amygdala, parahippocampal gyrus, and precuneus may likewise reflect structural vulnerability within circuits involved in pain modulation and affective processing.

Notably, we observed significantly lower left amygdala volume, consistent with several recent reports (22,23). Kosuge et al. reported a negative association between amygdala volume and migraine severity, supporting the possibility of amygdala volume reduction in at least a subset of patients. However, meta-analyses have more often reported that amygdala volume is increased in patients with migraine (24). The divergent direction of these findings may reflect heterogeneity in sample composition, including differences in depressive and anxiety symptom burden, disease duration, migraine severity, and other clinical characteristics. A recent study that stratified patients according to the presence or absence of depression found larger amygdala volumes in both migraine groups than in HC, although the between-group difference was not statistically significant (7). In the present cohort, however, reduced amygdala volume was observed in the overall MwoA group. Differences in affective symptom burden or other clinical characteristics may therefore contribute to the variability reported across studies, although the present data cannot determine the source of this heterogeneity. Future studies should examine amygdala subregions and spatially resolved morphometric patterns to clarify these discrepancies.

The VBM-detected reduction in the left parahippocampal gyrus is consistent with previous structural findings in migraine (12). Although early compensatory thickening of this region has been proposed (25), the lower GMV observed in the present cohort may reflect a different disease stage or clinical phenotype (26); the cross-sectional design, however, does not permit inference about progressive structural loss. Reduced fusiform gyrus volume within clusters extending from the parahippocampal and lingual gyri is also noteworthy. The fusiform gyrus forms part of the ventral visual pathway, and structural abnormalities in this region have been reported in depression (27,28) and associated with negative cognitive bias (29). In migraine, altered right fusiform function has been proposed as a potential marker of comorbid major depression (30). Accordingly, fusiform cortical abnormalities in MwoA may reflect neural processes that partially overlap with those implicated in affective and cognitive disturbances in depression.

Absence of significant alterations in classical pain-network regions

Although previous migraine studies have described structural abnormalities in the cerebellum, insula, anterior cingulate cortex, and somatosensory cortex, we found no significant structural differences in these regions (11,23,31). This lack of concordance may reflect differences in acquisition protocols, morphometric methods, statistical thresholds, and sample characteristics, as well as the intrinsic heterogeneity of migraine populations—including variation in phenotype, disease duration, headache burden, and medication exposure. Under the stringent statistical thresholds applied here, the most robust abnormalities were instead localized to regions involved in emotional processing and sensory integration.

Reduced LGI in the right superior temporal gyrus: a potential vulnerability marker for MwoA-HS

A central exploratory question was whether patients with MwoA and higher depressive symptom burden exhibit a distinct structural phenotype. Using complementary VBM and SBM analyses, we found that the direct MwoA-HS vs. MwoA-LS comparison identified significantly lower LGI only in the right superior temporal gyrus, whereas VBM showed no corresponding subgroup difference. According to the tension-based theory of cortical morphogenesis, cortical folding may be influenced by mechanical tension associated with white matter connections (32,33). Reduced LGI could therefore reflect altered structural organization or connectivity, although the present morphometric data do not directly test this mechanism.

Most morphometric studies of depression have emphasized GMV or CT abnormalities in the superior temporal gyrus (34-37), whereas cortical folding provides complementary information about cortical development and remodeling (38). In this context, lower LGI in the right superior temporal gyrus may represent a subtle morphological feature that is not captured by volumetric measures. Importantly, the cross-sectional design precludes any inference that gyrification changes precede or lead to later volume loss. The anatomical location of our finding is broadly consistent with prior reports in depression, although it was identified using a complementary surface-based metric, and therefore provides preliminary evidence that related superior temporal abnormalities may also be detectable in patients with MwoA who have higher depressive symptom burden. In migraine, altered topological properties of the GM connectome in the superior temporal gyrus have been associated with pain and emotional symptoms (39), and enhanced activity in this region has been observed during the processing of negative emotional stimuli (40). Structural abnormalities of the superior temporal gyrus have also been reported in chronic migraine; Planchuelo-Gómez et al. identified lower GMV in this region (41), whereas Schwedt et al. used cortical surface-area features of the temporal pole and insula to distinguish chronic from episodic migraine with high classification accuracy (42). Collectively, these findings support the possibility that the right superior temporal gyrus participates in interactions among sensory, emotional, and attentional processes in migraine. Whether the lower LGI observed here represents a vulnerability marker, an adaptive response, or a consequence of recurrent nociceptive and affective burden remains to be determined.

Notably, although exploratory analyses indicated that the MwoA-HS group had a longer attack duration and higher attack frequency, we found no significant association between right superior temporal gyrus LGI and measures of clinical severity. This null result suggests that the observed morphological difference may not vary linearly with the clinical measures assessed here; alternatively, any underlying association may be modest and beyond the power of the present sample to detect. Lower cortical folding in the right superior temporal gyrus may be related to altered processing during multisensory integration or emotional perception, but this interpretation remains tentative. In particular, the proposed sequence linking altered gyrification, compensatory functional activation, and depressive symptom persistence cannot be established from the present cross-sectional data and requires direct longitudinal and multimodal validation.

Limitations

Several methodological limitations warrant consideration. First, although the sample size (n=67) is comparable with those of many previous structural imaging studies, it may still provide limited power for detecting subtle effects, particularly in subgroup comparisons and analyses spanning multiple morphometric metrics. The principal subgroup finding—lower right STG LGI—was identified in one metric among several examined (GMV, CT, LGI, SD, and FD), without correction for multiplicity across metrics, thereby increasing the possibility of a false-positive result. The absence of FDR-corrected brain-clinical associations further limit the robustness and interpretability of the observed effects. In addition, the cross-sectional design precludes causal or temporal inference. Second, migraine severity-related variables were not comprehensively modeled in the primary analyses. Measures such as headache frequency, pain intensity, disease stage, and acute medication use were not included as covariates. Although exploratory analyses indicated that patients in the MwoA-HS group had longer attack duration and higher attack frequency, and LGI was not significantly associated with clinical scale scores, these results do not exclude nonlinear, cumulative, or interacting effects of migraine severity on brain morphology. Migraine chronification may involve progressive neuroplastic changes that cannot be characterized temporally in a cross-sectional sample. Third, subgroup classification relied solely on HAMD-17 scores (≥8) without structured psychiatric diagnostic interviews; consequently, the findings pertain to depressive symptom burden rather than clinically diagnosed depressive disorder. Although HAMA scores were included as covariates and the right STG LGI difference remained significant, the substantial overlap between depressive and anxiety symptoms limits attribution of the observed structural difference specifically to depression. The current design therefore cannot definitively disentangle depressive, anxiety-related, and broader emotional-distress effects. Fourth, the groups showed a marked sex imbalance (80.6% female in MwoA vs. 50.0% in controls; P<0.001). Although sex was included as a covariate, residual confounding remains possible, and the findings may not generalize equally to male patients. Fifth, the ROI-based correlations were performed using regions defined by the same group contrasts, introducing circular non-independence that can inflate effect estimates; these analyses should therefore be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Sixth, medication-related confounding could not be fully addressed. Many patients had received chronic or prophylactic treatment, and a 1-week washout period is unlikely to eliminate potential long-term treatment-related effects on brain structure. Incomplete medication histories and acute medication exposure around the time of scanning may also have influenced the morphometric measurements.

Future studies should prioritize larger, longitudinal, and sex-balanced cohorts; structured psychiatric assessments that distinguish depressive and anxiety symptom dimensions; comprehensive medication documentation; and statistical correction for multiplicity across morphometric metrics. Independent replication and multimodal imaging will be important for determining the reproducibility, temporal evolution, and clinical relevance of these exploratory structural findings.

Conclusions

In this study, we integrated VBM and SBM to characterize structural brain alterations in MwoA across levels of depressive symptom burden. Relative to HC, patients with MwoA showed widespread reductions in GMV involving the left amygdala, right angular gyrus, left parahippocampal gyrus, and left lingual gyrus. In subgroup analyses, the MwoA-HS group showed significantly lower LGI in the right superior temporal gyrus than the MwoA-LS group, a difference not captured by VBM. Taken together, these findings indicate that VBM and SBM provide complementary information about structural abnormalities in MwoA. Nevertheless, because the analyses were exploratory and cross-sectional, the results require replication in larger, longitudinal cohorts before their mechanistic and clinical significance can be established.
