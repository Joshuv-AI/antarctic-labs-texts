Challenges and inequities of healthcare in meeting the growing needs of urban children in sub-Saharan Africa
Authors: Samy Ahmar, Andrew Clarke, Lynn Kanyuuru
Published: 2025

Africa is changing, and a major shift is rapid urbanisation. Humans globally became more urban than rural in 2007, and the African Union expects Africa to reach this threshold in 2030. The growth of cities means that the proportion of children growing up in African conurbations is also increasing, notwithstanding lower fertility rates in urban versus rural areas.

Much of sub-Saharan Africa is characterised by a rural–urban divide when it comes to healthcare services1: rural areas tend to have a low density of professional health workers, long distances to travel between communities and primary healthcare services, a dominance of state-provided services, and a health system that is largely designed to respond to short episodes of acute infectious illness; urban areas tend to have a more sophisticated ecosystem of health service providers, a complex mixture of public and private operators, and more specialist care available, although still relatively scarce in comparison with high-income countries.

A common view among global health policymakers and organisations has for a long time been that underserved rural areas of low- and middle-income countries are where development and governmental investment should flow. A look at health outcome data might support this view: mortality rates, particularly from infectious causes, are invariably higher in rural than urban areas. Under-five mortality in the mostly rural state of Jigawa, in northwest Nigeria, was 161 per 1000 live births in the 2024 Nigeria Demographic and Health survey (DHS), against 46 in the urban and peri-urban state of Lagos. Only 38% of pregnant women had four antenatal visits in the rural county of Mandera, against 73% in the mostly urban capital Nairobi, according to the 2022 Kenya DHS. But this comparison hides a dangerous truth.

There are entrenched challenges in access to healthcare in urban contexts, particularly for the urban-poor: a recent study2 showed that a majority of unvaccinated children in the world live in non-remote areas—less than 3 hours from a health centre with free vaccination services—and nearly a third live in urban and peri-urban contexts.

Despite their proximity to services, urban-poor children are not being reached. Policy and funding decisions based on aggregate percentages at the state or county level may mask what is happening within large and growing cities of sub-Saharan Africa, where hundreds of millions of children now live. For example, one of Save the Children’s programmes is designed to locate and reach unvaccinated children in local government authorities of Lagos, Nigeria’s largest city, and has identified large pockets of unvaccinated children in both Ikorodu and Alimosho, two urban and peri-urban Local Government Authorities, especially from transient communities. Coverage of essential health services such as immunisation is now worse than the national average in several rapidly growing urban areas, according to the 2024 Nigeria DHS: Kano and Kaduna, Nigeria’s second and eighth largest cities respectively, had 36% and 34% of zero-dose children in 2024, against a national average of 31%.

Children and families who don’t use health services may be harder to find in urban areas, particularly those living in informal settlements, or made invisible by not always being included in population figures or planning.3 Aggregate health outcomes are generally better in cities, so there may be fewer incentives for prioritising these children: if vaccination coverage is high, then additional efforts required to reach the remaining minority may not seem a priority. This policy disincentive reinforces the multidimensional poverty, marginalisation and exclusion from healthcare these children already face, and not just for immunisation and case management: the same blind spots apply to many dimensions of maternal and child health.

Most primary healthcare systems in Africa were originally designed for rural contexts, assuming stable populations, strong local leadership, a single public-sector provider and predictable health-seeking behaviour.4 However, urban areas are characterised by higher population density, a cash-based economy instead of livelihoods driven by agriculture and non-cash transactions, transient and unregistered residents, sometimes weak social cohesion, and informal settlements often excluded from planning and service delivery. These conditions undermine outreach, compromise trust in health workers and create safety concerns. Applying rural models to these complex urban realities has led to persistent gaps in coverage and access for urban-poor populations.

Traditional models of health service provision struggle to accommodate and respond to many factors that affect urban-poor families.5 Service opening hours conflict with working demands for many families that rely on daily-wage labour, driving families to seek healthcare from private services that are often poorly regulated.6 High patient volume, matched with continued struggles to integrate triage systems within health settings in many parts of Africa, leads to long waits and inequity.

Another neglected challenge is that more children live outside of parental care in urban areas, commonly working in domestic household labour, restaurants, portering and construction, or those in ‘street situations’ (children that depend on the street for living, working or for whom it forms an integral part of their everyday lives).7 These children have often experienced trauma that health services are poorly prepared to respond to. Their health needs may be more significant than most,8 but they’re less likely to use touchpoints with the healthcare system and may face discrimination and barriers when they do (table 1).9

Many health systems are not set up to respond to children presenting alone, who may not have ID or proof of citizenship, which may be a particular challenge for children who migrated from their original districts; nor are they likely to have much money. Consent issues can inhibit healthcare providers’ actions, compounded by judgemental attitudes of staff and other patients, and a poor understanding of the issues facing children out of parental care. Navigating health services is particularly challenging for lone children. While some health services and non-governmental organisations do recognise and support this, such as Save the Children’s work with unaccompanied children that present to health services in Lusaka in Zambia, at the national level in Tanzania, and in Cairo, Egypt, these are far from the norm.

Historical exclusion of urban poor and marginalised children and families in health service planning10 exacerbates the mismatch of services with population needs. This is compounded by exposure to influences on health through advertising and social media, and unregulated private practice that also draws children and families away from key health messaging and referral links within the mainstream health system.

The result is a complex political economy that urban children and their caregivers must navigate to fulfil their rights to quality and inclusive healthcare.

There are no quick fixes for making health systems more responsive to urban children’s needs in Africa. Several strategies should be considered simultaneously and more widely, including:

Strengthening systems of birth registration, citizenship and individual clinical records (including patient-held records and digital approaches).
