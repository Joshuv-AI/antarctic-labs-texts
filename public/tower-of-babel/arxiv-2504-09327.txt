Non-perturbative renormalization group for pseudo-hermitian scalar fields in 4D
André LeClair

Abstract
We define a model of 2 coupled SU(2) doublets of scalar fields in 4 4 spacetime dimensions which have a rich structure of renormalization group (RG) flows to 1-loop when the SU(2) is broken to U(1). The model is pseudo-hermitian, H † = 𝒦 ​ H ​ 𝒦 † H^{\dagger}={\cal K}H{\cal K}^{\dagger} with 𝒦 † ​ 𝒦 = 𝒦 2 = 1 {\cal K}^{\dagger}{\cal K}={\cal K}^{2}=1 , which makes it non-unitary, however in a very specific manner with some desirable properties. We compute the beta functions to 3 loops from the operator product expansion and show that the 1-loop structure of flows persists to higher orders. For SU(2) broken to U(1), we conjecture a beta function to all orders. The flows can be extended to large coupling using a strong-weak coupling symmetry g → 1 / g g\to 1/g of the beta functions. One finds a line of fixed points which are non-unitary conformal field theories in 4 spacetime dimensions that were previously unknown. We also find massless flows between 2 non-trivial fixed points, and a regime with a cyclic RG flow, which is allowed since the model is non-unitary. For the flows between fixed points on the critical line, we compute the anomalous dimensions of the perturbations in the UV and IR, and identify some special points where anomalous dimensions are rational numbers.

Introduction

There is a conspicuous shortage of well-understood conformal field theories (CFT’s) in 4 spacetime dimensions that aren’t simply free fields, as for asymptotically free

For marginal perturbations, such as many “standard” generalizations of

In fact, this is at the heart of the so-called hierarchy problem, namely that straightforward RG arguments would indicate a much higher mass for the Higgs boson since there is no known ultra-violet (UV) fixed point. Without a known UV completion of the Higgs sector, there may even be higher dimension operators that make the theory non-unitary.

II
Definition of the models and their discrete
{\cal C},{\cal P},{\cal T}
symmetries

In this Section we define the models as marginal perturbations of free massless bosonic fields. The free theory is unitary, however it is the marginal perturbations that make it non-unitary. In doing so, we will already introduce some aspects of pseudo-hermitian quantum mechanics which will be reviewed in more detail in the next section.
First we review the standard free theory of the massless free bosons and its discrete symmetries, which will be important for identifying which symmetries are broken by the marginal perturbations.

II.1
The free theory and its discrete symmetries

Our models are defined as certain marginal operator perturbations of a conformal field theory consisting of two scalar field doublets.
Introduce two independent
{\rm SU(2)}
doublets of complex bosonic (non-Grassmannian) spin-0 fields,

\Phi_{i}
and
\widetilde{\Phi}_{i}
,
{i=1,2}

and their usual hermitian conjugates
\Phi^{\dagger}
,
\widetilde{\Phi}^{\dagger}
.
Under
{\rm SU(2)}
transformations,
\Phi(x)\to U\Phi(x)
, where
U
is a
2\times 2
unitary
{\rm SU(2)}
group matrix acting on the “i” indices in the above equation and the same for
\widetilde{\Phi}
. The symmetry is actually
U(2)
which allows an additional
U(1) “hypercharge”.
The free action is the standard one for free massless bosonic fields.

{\cal S}_{0}=\int d^{4}x\left(\partial_{\mu}\Phi^{\dagger}\partial^{\mu}\Phi+\partial_{\mu}\widetilde{\Phi}^{\dagger}\partial^{\mu}\widetilde{\Phi}\right),

(5)

where
\Phi^{\dagger}\Phi=\sum_{i=1,2}\Phi^{\dagger}_{i}\Phi_{i}
etc. The free theory has global
{\rm SU(2)}\otimes{\rm SU(2)}
symmetry since the fields

\Phi,\widetilde{\Phi}
are independent, however the interactions introduced below will break this down to the diagonal
{\rm SU(2)}
.
The quantization of this theory is standard
[
45
]
. Consider the quantization of one component
\Phi=\Phi_{i}
for
i=1~{\rm or}~2
, and the same applies to

\widetilde{\Phi}
.
Expand the field in terms of creation/annihilation operators
a_{\pm},a^{\dagger}_{\pm}
:

\displaystyle\Phi(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\dfrac{d^{3}{\bf{k}}}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\omega_{\bf{k}}}}\left(a^{\dagger}_{-}({\bf{k}})e^{-ik\cdot x}+a_{+}({\bf{k}})e^{ik\cdot x}\right)

\displaystyle\Phi^{\dagger}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\dfrac{d^{3}{\bf{k}}}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\omega_{\bf{k}}}}\left(a_{-}({\bf{k}})e^{ik\cdot x}+a^{\dagger}_{+}({\bf{k}})e^{-ik\cdot x}\right),

(6)

where

k\cdot x=\omega_{\bf{k}}t-{\bf{k}}\cdot{\bf{x}}
with
\omega_{\bf{k}}=|{\bf{k}}|
, and similarly for
\widetilde{\Phi}
.
Canonical quantization of the field
\Phi
as a boson yields

[a_{\pm}({\bf{k}}),a^{\dagger}_{\pm}({\bf{k}}^{\prime})]=\delta^{(3)}({\bf{k}}-{\bf{k}}^{\prime}),~~~~[a_{\pm}({\bf{k}}),a^{\dagger}_{\mp}({\bf{k}}^{\prime})]=0,

(7)

and the free hamiltonian is

H_{0}=\int d^{3}{\bf{k}}\,\omega_{{\bf{k}}}\left(a^{\dagger}_{+}({\bf{k}})\,a_{+}({\bf{k}})+a^{\dagger}_{-}({\bf{k}})\,a_{-}({\bf{k}})\right).

(8)

The above free theory is necessarily invariant under
{\cal C}{\cal P}{\cal T}
, which we now review in order to make certain points below.
Consider first charge conjugation implemented by the operator
{\cal C}
:

{\cal C}{\cal C}^{\dagger}=1,~~~{\cal C}={\cal C}^{\dagger},~~~{\cal C}^{2}=1.

(9)

On the
a,a^{\dagger}
operators it acts as

{\cal C}a_{\pm}({\bf{k}}){\cal C}=a_{\mp}({\bf{k}}),~~~{\cal C}a^{\dagger}_{\pm}({\bf{k}}){\cal C}=a^{\dagger}_{\mp}({\bf{k}}).

(10)

This implies

{\cal C}\Phi(x){\cal C}=\Phi^{\dagger}(x)\,~~~~\Longrightarrow~~~~~~{\cal C}H_{0}{\cal C}=H_{0}=H^{\dagger}_{0}.

(11)

Based on
{\cal C}
we identify
particles
as those created by
a^{\dagger}_{+}
and
anti-particles
as created by
a^{\dagger}_{-}
.
This operator
{\cal C}
will play a central role below since it will be broken by interactions.

Let us also review the standard parity
{\cal P}
and time-reversal symmetry
{\cal T}
.
We take them to also satisfy constraints such as in (
9
).
Since parity flips the sign of momentum:

{\cal P}a_{\pm}({\bf{k}}){\cal P}=a_{\pm}(-{\bf{k}}),~~~{\cal P}a^{\dagger}_{\pm}({\bf{k}}){\cal P}=a^{\dagger}_{\pm}(-{\bf{k}}),~~~~~~\Longrightarrow~~~~{\cal P}\Phi(t,{\bf{x}}){\cal P}=\Phi(t,-{\bf{x}}),~~~{\cal P}H_{0}{\cal P}=H_{0}.

(12)

Time-reversal also flips the sign of
{\bf{k}}
and only differs from
{\cal P}
in that it is anti-unitary, namely
{\cal T}z{\cal T}=z^{*}
for

a complex number. This leads to

{\cal T}a_{\pm}({\bf{k}}){\cal T}=a_{\pm}(-{\bf{k}}),~~~{\cal T}a^{\dagger}_{\pm}({\bf{k}}){\cal T}=a^{\dagger}_{\pm}(-{\bf{k}})~~~~\Longrightarrow~~~{\cal T}\Phi(t,{\bf{x}}){\cal T}=\Phi(-t,{\bf{x}}),~~~~~{\cal T}H_{0}{\cal T}=H_{0}.

(13)

This implies

{\cal P}{\cal T}a_{\pm}({\bf{k}}){\cal P}{\cal T}=a_{\pm}({\bf{k}}),

(14)

and

{\cal P}{\cal T}H_{0}{\cal P}{\cal T}=H_{0}.

(15)

The full Hilbert space
{\cal H}
, which diagonalizes the free hamiltonian
H_{0}
, consists of multi-particle states
|\psi\rangle
of the form

{\cal H}=\{|\psi\rangle\},~~~|\psi\rangle=|({\bf{k}}_{1},s_{1}),({\bf{k}}_{2},s_{2}),\ldots({\bf{k}}_{n},s_{n})\rangle\equiv a^{\dagger}_{s_{1}}({\bf{k}}_{1})a^{\dagger}_{s_{2}}({\bf{k}}_{2})\cdots a^{\dagger}_{s_{n}}({\bf{k}}_{n})|0\rangle~~~~~s_{i}\in\{\pm\}.

(16)

In

\langle\psi^{\prime}|\psi\rangle=\delta_{{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}^{\prime}{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}}>0,~~\Longrightarrow~~~\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|=1,

where

II.2
The marginal perturbations

In defining the marginal perturbations of interest, it’s necessary to introduce an additional discrete symmetry operator
{\cal K}
, satisfying

{\cal K}a_{\pm}({\bf{k}}){\cal K}=\pm a_{\pm}({\bf{k}}),~~~{\cal K}a^{\dagger}_{\pm}({\bf{k}}){\cal K}=\pm a^{\dagger}_{\pm}({\bf{k}}).

(18)

The operator
{\cal K}
is unitary:

{\cal K}^{\dagger}{\cal K}=1,~~~~{\cal K}={\cal K}^{\dagger},~~~~\Longrightarrow~~~{\cal K}^{2}=1,

(19)

and the free hamiltonian is
{\cal K}
invariant:

{\cal K}H_{0}{\cal K}=H_{0}=H^{\dagger}_{0}.

(20)

Every state
\psi
in the Hilbert space defined above, spanned by states of the form (
16
), has a well defined
{\cal K}
eigenvalue

{\cal K}|\psi\rangle={\cal K}_{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}\,|\psi\rangle,~~~~~{\cal K}_{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}\in\{\pm 1\},~~~~~{\cal K}|0\rangle=|0\rangle.

(21)

The operator
{\cal K}
does not act locally on the fields
\Phi
, unlike
{\cal C},{\cal P},{\cal T}
.
It can be expressed in terms of the
U(1)
charge
{\cal Q}
operator.
Let
N_{\pm}
denote the number operators for particles created by
a^{\dagger}_{\pm}
:

N_{\pm}=\int d^{3}{\bf{k}}~a^{\dagger}_{\pm}({\bf{k}})a_{\pm}({\bf{k}}).

(22)

Then the U(1) charge is

{\cal Q}=N_{+}-N_{-}

(23)

and one has

[{\cal Q},a_{\pm}]=\mp a_{\pm},~~~~[{\cal Q},a^{\dagger}_{\pm}]=\pm a^{\dagger}_{\pm}.

(24)

One can then identify

{\cal K}=e^{i\vartheta\overline{N}}~~{\rm with}~\vartheta=\pi~{\rm and}~\overline{N}\equiv N_{-}~~~~\Longrightarrow~~[{\cal Q},{\cal K}]=0,

(25)

where
\overline{N}
counts the number of anti-particles.
Clearly,
{\cal K}^{\dagger}K=1
and
{\cal K}^{2}=1
since
\overline{N}
is an integer on the Hilbert space.
The
{\cal K}
operator does not commute with charge conjugation
{\cal C}
. One has

{\cal C}{\cal K}={\cal K}{\cal C}\,e^{i\pi{\cal Q}},

(26)

where we have also used that
{\cal Q}
is an integer on the Hilbert space,
and

e^{i\pi{\cal Q}}{\cal C}={\cal C}e^{i\pi{\cal Q}}.

(27)

On 1-particle states where
{\cal Q}=\pm 1
, eq. (
26
) implies that
{\cal K}
anti-commutes with
{\cal C}
.

Our models will involve
\Phi^{2}\widetilde{\Phi}^{2}
marginal interactions involving the
{\cal K}
-conjugate fields
\Phi^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}

and
\widetilde{\Phi}^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}
:

\Phi^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}(x)\equiv{\cal K}\,\Phi^{\dagger}{\cal K}=\int\dfrac{d^{3}{\bf{k}}}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\omega_{\bf{k}}}}\left(-a_{-}({\bf{k}})e^{ik\cdot x}+a^{\dagger}_{+}({\bf{k}})e^{-ik\cdot x}\right).

(28)

Since the operator
{\cal K}
acts non-locally on the fields
\Phi^{\dagger}
, the fields
\Phi^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}(x)
are
non-local
.
For reasons explained in the next section, define a modified
{\cal K}
-inner product which includes
{\cal K}
insertions:

\langle\!\langle\psi^{\prime}|\psi\rangle\!\rangle\equiv\langle\psi^{\prime}|{\cal K}|\psi\rangle={\cal K}_{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}\,\delta_{{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}^{\prime}{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}}

(29)

Using
a_{\pm}|0\rangle=0
, due to the extra minus sign in the above equation one finds the free field 2-point correlation functions for components
\Phi_{i}
:

\langle\!\langle\Phi^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}_{i}(x)\Phi_{j}(y)\rangle\!\rangle=-\langle\!\langle\Phi_{i}(x)\Phi_{j}^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}(y)\rangle\!\rangle=\dfrac{\delta_{ij}}{4\pi^{2}|x-y|^{2}},

(30)

where we have used
{\cal K}|0\rangle=|0\rangle
.
These correlation functions are consistent with
{\cal K}
-conjugation:

\langle\!\langle\Phi^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}(x)\Phi(y)\rangle\!\rangle^{*}=\langle\!\langle\left(\Phi^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}(x)\Phi(y)\right)^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}\rangle\!\rangle=\langle\!\langle\Phi^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}(y)\Phi(x)\rangle\!\rangle,

(31)

which allows the extra minus sign in (
30
). The two-point correlation functions in (
30
) are also consistent with causality.
The above correlation functions, or propagators, are
implicitly
time-ordered. Thus within a time ordered correlation function one has the following OPE:

\lim_{x\to y}\,\Phi_{i}^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}(x)\Phi_{j}(y)=-\lim_{x\to y}\,\Phi_{i}(x)\Phi_{j}^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}(y)=\dfrac{\delta_{ij}}{4\pi^{2}|x-y|^{2}}.

(32)

where time ordering is implicit. (See
[
45
]
for detailed explanations.)
Note that the order of operators matters, and the above OPE is more typical of a fermion, even though
\Phi
was quantized as a boson.
This is a manifestation of the non-locality of the operators
\Phi^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}
.

Let us now turn to adding interactions. As for
\Phi^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}
above,
for any operator
A
define it’s
{\cal K}
-conjugate as

A^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}\equiv{\cal K}\,A^{\dagger}{\cal K}.

(33)

The operator
\Phi^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}\Phi
is not its own hermitian conjugate, but is invariant under
{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}
conjugation:

(\Phi^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}\Phi)^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}=\Phi^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}\Phi.

(34)

Motivated by this, define the operators

J^{a}=\Phi^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}\sigma^{a}\Phi\equiv\sum_{i,j}\Phi^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}_{i}\sigma^{a}_{ij}\Phi_{j},

(35)

where
\sigma^{a}=(\sigma^{a})^{\dagger}
are the usual hermitian Pauli matrices:

\sigma^{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},~~~\sigma^{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix},~~~\sigma^{3}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},~~~{\rm Tr}(\sigma^{a}\sigma^{b})=2\delta^{ab}.

(36)

and similarly for
\widetilde{J}^{a}
. These operators are invariant under
{\cal K}
-conjugation:

\left(J^{a}\right)^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}=J^{a},

(37)

and the same goes for
\widetilde{J}^{a}
.
The operators
J^{a}
, as defined this way, have an interesting operator product expansion. Using the Wick expansion based on
(
30
) one finds

\lim_{x\to y}\,J^{a}(x)J^{b}(y)=-\dfrac{2\kappa\delta^{ab}}{16\pi^{4}|x-y|^{4}}-\dfrac{if^{abc}}{4\pi^{2}|x-y|^{2}}\,J^{c}(y)+\ldots~~~({\rm with}~\kappa=1),

(38)

where the structure constants
f^{abc}
are

[\sigma^{a},\sigma^{b}]=if^{abc}\sigma^{c},~~~~~f^{abc}=2\epsilon^{abc},

(39)

with
\epsilon^{abc}
the completely anti-symmetric tensor with
\epsilon^{123}=1
.
In the above equation
\kappa=1
, however we introduced
\kappa\neq 1
for book-keeping reasons that will become clear below, as the
\kappa
order counts loops for contributions to the beta function.
In obtaining the above OPE (
38
), the term where the two outermost fields are Wick contracted gives the term

\lim_{x\to y}\sum_{i,j,k}\,\dfrac{1}{4\pi^{2}|x-y|^{2}}~\Phi_{j}(x)\Phi_{k}^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}(y)\sigma^{b}_{ki}\sigma^{a}_{ij}=\dfrac{1}{4\pi^{2}|x-y|^{2}}~\Phi^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}(y)\sigma^{b}\sigma^{a}\Phi(y),

(40)

where we have used the fact that
\Phi
is a bosonic (non-Grassmann) field to exchange the order of

\Phi_{j}(x)\Phi_{k}^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}(y)
for
j\neq k
. When
j=k
, the singularity is already contained the in
\kappa
-term in the OPE.
This prescription is necessary for the above OPE to close on the operators
J^{a}(x)
.

The above OPE (
38
) is central to the remainder of this article.
For
{\rm SU(2)}
there is no sum over “
c
” in the equation (
38
) and this will simplify some calculations below, however this OPE is valid for other Lie algebras.
The extra minus sign for
\Phi^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}
in (
28
) is what leads to the commutator in (
39
) and the resulting structure constant
f^{abc}
in the OPE (
38
). If
J^{a}
was instead defined as
\Phi^{\dagger}\sigma^{a}\Phi
, it would not satisfy the OPE in (
38
). Nor would this OPE be satisfied if

J^{a}=\Psi^{\dagger}\sigma^{a}\Psi
where
\Psi
is a fermionic, i.e. Grassmann field.
As we will see, the beta functions can be determined from the above OPE (
38
) since it is valid inside time-ordered correlation functions.
Let us emphasize that since we used the Wick expansion in obtaining the OPE in (
38
), it should be understood as an operator equation inside a time-ordered correlation function.

Since
J^{a}(x)
are quantum operators, they do not necessarily commute. If one simple exchanges
a,b
and simulaneously
x,y
on the LHS of (
38
), then it becomes

J^{b}(y)J^{a}(x)
which is not equal to
J^{a}(x)J^{b}(y)
according to the RHS of equation (

) because of the anti-symmetry of the structure constant
f^{abc}
:

\lim_{x\to y}\,J^{a}(x)J^{b}(y)\neq\lim_{y\to x}\,J^{b}(y)J^{a}(x).

(41)

The above implies that the operators
J^{a}(x)
are non-local, which, as explained above, is a consequence of
\Phi^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}
being non-local.

What is meaningful is that the operator equation (
38
) is consistent with
{\cal K}
-conjugation, and from this one can see why the
i=\sqrt{-1}
is necessary. Taking the

{\cal K}
-conjugate of both sides of (

\left(J^{a}(x)J^{b}(y)\right)^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}=J^{b}(y)J^{a}(x)=-\dfrac{2\kappa\delta^{ab}}{16\pi^{4}|x-y|^{4}}+\dfrac{if^{abc}}{4\pi^{2}|x-y|^{2}}\,J^{c}(y),

(42)

and this is identical to (
38
) since
f^{abc}=-f^{bac}
.

For euclidean QFT, the perturbing operators of a CFT are normally required to be local for physical reasons.
Below, the marginal perturbations will be

{\cal O}^{a}(x)=J^{a}(x)\widetilde{J}^{a}(x)
which are indeed local as is evident from the OPE in equation (
79
) below.
To help in clarifying this, it is useful to make an analogy with free massless scalar fields in 2 spacetime dimensions, especially in light of the comparison
with 2D current-current perturbations made in the Appendix. In 2D, the conserved current operators are in fact local, however here the operators
J^{a}
are not local, nor are they conserved currents.
Consider a free massless scalar field in 2D with the local propagator
\langle\phi(x)\phi(y)\rangle=-\log|x-y|^{2}
. In 2D CFT, the field
\phi(x)
is
separated into left and right moving parts:
\phi(x)=\varphi(z)+\overline{\varphi}(\overline{z})
where
z=x+iy,~\overline{z}=x-iy
. The two-point function
is
\langle\varphi(z)\varphi(w)\rangle=-\log(z-w)
and similarly for
\overline{\varphi}
.
The field
\phi
is local, but
\varphi
and
\overline{\varphi}
are non-local. Consider a perturbation by local operators
e^{i\alpha\phi}=e^{i\alpha\varphi}e^{i\alpha\overline{\varphi}}
. Then the OPE of the holomorphic factor is non-local. Using the Wick expansion,

\lim_{z\to w}\,e^{i\alpha\varphi(z)}e^{i\alpha\varphi(w)}\sim(z-w)^{\alpha^{2}}\,e^{2i\alpha\varphi(w)}.

(43)

The above OPE is obviously non-local in that it is not invariant under the exchange of
z
and
w
, analogous to the OPE (
38
).
On the other hand the OPE of the local field
e^{i\alpha\phi(x)}
with
e^{i\alpha\phi(y)}
is proportional to
|x-y|^{2\alpha^{2}}
which is local.

These are all the ingredients necessary to define our models. Define the marginal operators

{\cal O}^{a}(x)=J^{a}(x)\widetilde{J}^{a}(x).

(44)

The models are then defined by the action

{\cal S}={\cal S}_{0}+2\pi^{2}\int d^{4}x\,\sum_{a=1,2,3}g_{a}\,{\cal O}^{a}(x),

(45)

where
g_{a}
are 3 independent couplings.
The factor of
2\pi^{2}
is introduced to simplify the beta-functions of the next section, such that no

\pi
’s appear.
When
g_{1}=g_{2}=g_{3}
the model has SU(2) symmetry since it is built on the quadratic Casimir.
The operators
J^{a}
are not hermitian but pseudo-hermitian as stated in (
37
).
Thus the interacting hamiltonian is pseudo-hermitian:

H^{\dagger}={\cal K}H{\cal K}~~~\Longrightarrow~~H^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}=H.

(46)

II.3
{\cal C},{\cal P}
and
{\cal T}

The free theory with hamiltonian

is invariant under separate

symmetries thus respects the

according to (

This is manifested in the fact that

does commute with

{\cal P}{\cal T}

{\cal C}

{\cal K}
commutes with both
{\cal P}

.

5

5

More generally, if

.

III
Manifestations of non-unitarity: negative norm states and a low energy unitary regime

As stated in the Introduction, the main focus of this article is on the CFT’s and RG flows between them for our model,
and this is as well-motivated as the extensive literature on flows between unitary and non-unitary models of CFT in 2D, which we reviewed in the Introduction.
Our model is strictly speaking non-unitary since
H^{\dagger}\neq H
, and this cannot be completely “fixed” by the considerations in this article.
However pseudo-hermiticity,
H^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}=H
, provides some additional algebraic structure with some desirable properties which we review in this section.
For potential applications to relativistic particle physics in
3+1
spacetime dimensions, a consistent unitary quantum mechanics would of course be desirable.
The classification of pseudo-hermitian hamiltonians in
[
17
]
has led to many applications to
open
quantum systems and their Lindbladians.
Let us mention that whether the Universe as a whole is open or closed is not a settled issue. For instance, the UV completion of the Standard Model could involve higher dimensional operators that are pseudo-hermitian and thus break unitarity.
Let us also mention that it has been proposed that for a potential dS/CFT correspondence, the CFT is expected to be non-unitary
[
46
]
, and
symplectic fermions have been considered in this context
[
47
]
. As we will see, there are very strong parallels between the pseudo-hermitian bosonic scalar model we study in this article and self-interacting symplectic fermions since the non-unitarity is of a very similar nature.
The non-unitarity of symplectic fermions has been studied in
several articles
[
48
,
49
,
50
,
52
]
where it has been argued that the interacting model has a consistent generalized unitary time evolution at least for 2-body to 2-body scattering
[
50
]
.

In this section we study how the non-unitarity of our model is specifically manifested, in the S-matrix for instance.
The subject of Quantum Mechanics for pseudo-hermitian hamiltonians is by now well developed, with extensive reviews

[
22
,
23
,
24
]
. These works show that in principle one can still define a unitary theory from a pseudo-hermitian hamiltonian
[
22
]
, however the procedure for doing so is model dependent and we could not apply it directly to our theory.
As we will explain, the manner is which the non-unitarity is manifested in our model will lead us to conclude that, although our model is strictly speaking non-unitary,
it is in fact perfectly unitary at low enough energies, specifically below the energy threshold for pair-production. Thus our model provides an example where a non-unitary theory at high energies can still be unitary at low energies. Similar examples in 2D are discussed below wherein a non-unitary perturbation can still lead to RG flows between unitary CFT’s (see Section VB).

To set the stage, let us first review the Born rule for probabilities in standard unitary quantum mechanics in the Schrödinger picture.
Assume we are given a Hilbert space
{\cal H}
with a conventional positive definite inner product.
Namely for
|\psi\rangle\in{\cal H}
, there exists an inner product such that
\langle\psi^{\prime}|\psi\rangle
is positive definite. Indeed, the states in (
16
) comprise such a Hilbert space.
At time
t=t_{0}=0
the states need to be properly normalized such that
\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1
.
There exists a basis in
{\cal H}
,
|\psi_{n}\rangle\in{\cal H}
where for simplicity of notation we assume
n
is discrete,

n=1,2,\ldots\infty
. Then

|\psi\rangle=|\psi(t=0)\rangle=\sum_{n}c_{n}\,|\psi_{n}\rangle,~~~~\langle\psi_{m}|\psi_{n}\rangle=\delta_{nm},

(47)

for some complex numbers
c_{n}
.
Then

\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1~~~~~~\Longrightarrow~~~\sum_{n}c_{n}^{*}c_{n}=1,

(48)

where
c_{n}^{*}
denotes complex conjugation. According to the Born rule,
|c_{n}|^{2}
represents the probability that
|\psi\rangle
is measured to be in the state
|\psi_{n}\rangle
. Given a hamiltonian
H
, or any other operator
A
on
{\cal H}
, then
A^{\dagger}
is defined in the standard way with respect to the above inner product.
Let us now turn to pseudo-hermitian hamiltonians in general.

III.1
General properties of pseudo-hermitian Quantum Mechanics

Pseudo-hermitian hamiltonians have many desirable properties that parallel those in ordinary hermitian quantum mechanics, and we here list the most important
for such a theory generally, i.e. properties that are independent of the model.

(i)
Modified indefinite metric on the Hilbert space

Suppose the hamiltonian operator
H
is pseudo-hermitian with the structure in (
2
), which we repeat here:

H^{\dagger}={\cal K}\,H\,{\cal K}^{\dagger},~~~{\cal K}^{\dagger}{\cal K}=1,~~~~~{\cal K}^{\dagger}={\cal K},~~~~\Longrightarrow~~~{\cal K}^{2}=1.

(49)

In other words,
H^{\dagger}
is unitarily equivalent to
H
.
This leads us to define a new inner product which includes an insertion of the operator
{\cal K}
:

\langle\!\langle\psi^{\prime}|\psi\rangle\!\rangle\equiv\langle\psi^{\prime}|{\cal K}|\psi\rangle={\cal K}_{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}\,\delta_{{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}^{\prime}{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}}

(50)

In this new inner product, kets have an accompanying
{\cal K}
but bras do not.
In general, this new inner product can have negative norm states
|\psi\rangle
if
{\cal K}_{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}
is negative.
This implies negative probabilities since (
48
) becomes

\sum_{n}{\cal K}_{n}\,|c_{n}|^{2}=1.

(51)

(ii)
Pseudo-hermitian operators correspond to observables with real eigenvalues

For any operator
A
on
{\cal H}
, define its pseudo-hermitian conjugate as follows:

A^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}\equiv{\cal K}\,A^{\dagger}\,{\cal K}.

(52)

We define a pseudo-hermitian operator
A
as one that satisfies
A^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}=A
.
For our model, the hamiltonian is such a pseudo-hermitian operator:

H^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}=H.

(53)

A^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}
is the proper conjugation based on the
{\cal K}
-metric in (
50
), namely

\langle\!\langle\psi^{\prime}|A|\psi\rangle\!\rangle^{*}=\langle\!\langle\psi|A^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}|\psi^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle,

(54)

where
*
denotes ordinary complex conjugation.
From (
54
), one concludes that pseudo-hermitian operators, in particular the hamiltonian H, has real eigenvalues.
In other words,
H
is effectively hermitian but on a Hilbert space with indefinite norm.
Any operator satisfying
A^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}=A
in principle can correspond to an observable.
One can easily establish that this pseudo-hermitian adjoint satisfies the usual rules, e.g.

\displaystyle(AB)^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}A^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}\,,

\displaystyle(aA+bB)^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{*}A^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}+b^{*}B^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}\,,

(55)

where
A,B
are operators and
a,b
are complex numbers.

(iii)
Conservation of probability

The hamiltonian determines the time evolution of a state
|\psi\rangle=|\psi(t=0)\rangle
, namely

|\psi(t)\rangle=\,e^{-iHt}|\psi\rangle
. By conservation of probability we mean that the norm of states is preserved under time evolution. More generally:

\langle\!\langle\psi^{\prime}(t)|\psi(t)\rangle\!\rangle=\langle\psi^{\prime}|e^{iH^{\dagger}t}\,{\cal K}\,e^{-iHt}\,|\psi\rangle=\langle\psi^{\prime}|{\cal K}e^{iHt}{\cal K}^{2}\,e^{-iHt}\,|\psi\rangle=\langle\!\langle\psi^{\prime}(0)|\psi(0)\rangle\!\rangle

(56)

In general, the modified
{\cal K}
-metric
\langle\!\langle\psi^{\prime}|\psi\rangle\!\rangle
introduces negative norm states which imply negative probabilities. The above statement of unitarity just states that the sum of these probabilities is maintained under time evolution,
but one must still deal with negative norm states due to the negative probailities.

(iv)
Projection operators

The properties of
{\cal K}
naturally lead to projection operators onto positive or negative norm states.
Since
{\cal K}^{2}=1
, states can be classified according to
{\cal K}=\pm 1
. Namely,

{\cal K}|\psi_{n}\rangle={\cal K}_{n}\,|\psi_{n}\rangle,~~~~{\cal K}_{n}\in\{\pm 1\}.

(57)

Define the operators
6
6

6

We adopt the notation
\mathbin{\pm\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.80832pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}
to refer to
{\cal K}=\pm
norm states in order to avoid confusion with
\pm
in the definition of
the particle creation operators
a^{\dagger}_{\pm}
.

P_{\mathbin{\pm\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}=\dfrac{1\pm{\cal K}}{2}.

(58)

They satisfy the usual algebra of projection operators:

P_{\mathbin{+\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}+P_{\mathbin{-\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}=1,~~~~~P_{\mathbin{\pm\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}^{2}=P_{\mathbin{\pm\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}},~~~~~P_{\mathbin{+\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}P_{\mathbin{-\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}=0.

(59)

In addition, one has

{\cal K}P_{\mathbin{\pm\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}=P_{\mathbin{\pm\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}{\cal K}=\pm P_{\mathbin{\pm\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}.

(60)

Thus the Hilbert space decomposes as

{\cal H}={\cal H}_{\mathbin{+\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}\oplus{\cal H}_{\mathbin{-\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}},~~~~~|\psi_{\mathbin{\pm\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}\rangle\in{\cal H}_{\mathbin{\pm\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}},~~~~~{\cal K}|\psi_{\mathbin{\pm\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}\rangle=\pm|\psi_{\mathbin{\pm\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}\rangle.

(61)

One has

\langle\!\langle\psi_{\mathbin{\pm\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}|\psi_{\mathbin{\pm\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}\rangle\!\rangle=\langle\psi|P_{\mathbin{\pm\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}{\cal K}P_{\mathbin{\pm\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}|\psi\rangle=\pm\langle\psi_{\mathbin{\pm\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}|\psi_{\mathbin{\pm\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}\rangle,~~~~~\langle\!\langle\psi_{\mathbin{-\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}|\psi_{\mathbin{+\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}\rangle\!\rangle=\langle\psi|P_{\mathbin{+\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}{\cal K}P_{\mathbin{-\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}|\psi\rangle=0.

(62)

If we project onto the sub Hilbert space
{\cal H}_{\mathbin{+\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}
, then all states
|\psi_{n}\rangle\in{\cal H}_{\mathbin{+\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}
have positive norm, i.e.

{\cal K}_{n}=+1
, which implies positive probabilities
|c_{n}|^{2}\geq 0
. This positivity is preserved under time evolution:

\langle\!\langle\psi_{\mathbin{\pm\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}(t)|\psi_{\mathbin{\pm\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}(t)\rangle\!\rangle=\langle\!\langle\psi_{\mathbin{\pm\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}(0)|\psi_{\mathbin{\pm\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}(0)\rangle\!\rangle,~~~~~\langle\!\langle\psi_{\mathbin{-\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}(t)|\psi_{\mathbin{+\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}(t)\rangle\!\rangle=\langle\!\langle\psi_{\mathbin{-\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}(0)|\psi_{\mathbin{+\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}(0)\rangle\!\rangle=0.

(63)

However this is insufficient to define a physically consistent quantum mechanics since
{\cal K}
does not commute with
H
, otherwise

H^{\dagger}=H
, which is not the case by construction. This implies that if one prepares a state at time
t=0
that is in
{\cal H}_{\mathbin{+\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}
, then negative norm states
will be generated under time evolution:

e^{-iHt}|\psi_{\mathbin{+\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}\rangle~\notin~{\cal H}_{\mathbin{+\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}

(64)

In order to have a consistent quantum mechanics one needs an additional
selection rule
of sorts such that

e^{-iHt}|\psi_{\mathbin{+\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}\rangle~\in~{\cal H}_{\mathbin{+\mkern-5.0mu\raise 1.26582pt\hbox{$\cdot$}}}
. At this point, if such a selection rule exists, it doesn’t follow generally from pseudo-hermiticity, but rather
must be model dependent. In our model, the free hamiltonian
H_{0}
does commute with
{\cal K}
, but the interacting part does not. It is thus natural to consider this issue
in the interaction picture, in particular in scattering theory, which we turn to next.

III.2
Specialization to our model: Scattering theory and a kinematic unitary regime

In the interaction picture the hamiltonian is separated into a free part
H_{0}
and the interaction:

H=H_{0}+H_{\rm int}.

(65)

For the purposes of this section, we again only consider a single component
\Phi
as in Section IIA, and consider the pseudo-hermitian interaction
such as
H_{\rm int}=\int d^{3}x\,(\Phi^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}\Phi)^{2}
.
Since
H_{\rm int}
is local, then far from the interaction region the asymptotic states in the far past and far future are
eigenstates of the free hamiltonian, and one can separate out this time evolution with
H_{0}
which leads to a well-defined perturbation theory based on

H_{\rm int}
. For hermitian quantum mechanics this leads to the standard formalism of scattering theory, where positive probabilities lead to physically well-defined
and meaningful cross sections.

Let us adapt this scattering formalism to pseudo-hermitian theories where the negative norm states cannot simply be projected out consistently.
Define the operator

\Omega(t)=e^{iHt}e^{-iH_{0}t}

(66)

where as above we have taken the reference time
t_{0}=0
for simplicity, otherwise
t
in the above equation should be replaced by
t-t_{0}
.
Using
[{\cal K},H_{0}]=0
, one can easily show that

\Omega^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}\Omega=1.

(67)

The so-called in and out states are defined by the Møller operators
\Omega_{\pm}
:

|\psi\rangle_{\rm in}=\Omega_{-}|\psi\rangle,~~~~|\psi\rangle_{\rm out}=\Omega_{+}|\psi\rangle,~~~~~\Omega_{\pm}=\lim_{t\to\pm\infty}\Omega(t).

(68)

Given the
{\cal K}
-metric, the S-matrix operator is now defined as follows:

_{\rm out}\langle\!\langle\psi^{\prime}|\psi\rangle\!\rangle_{\rm in}\equiv\langle\psi^{\prime}|S|\psi\rangle~~\Longrightarrow~~S=\Omega_{+}^{\dagger}{\cal K}\Omega_{-}={\cal K}\,\Omega_{+}^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}\Omega_{-}\,\,.

(69)

Using
S^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}=\Omega_{-}^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}\Omega_{+}\,{\cal K}
, one finds

S^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}S=1.

(70)

The formula (
70
) generalizes the usual statement of the unitarity of the S-matrix and is equivalent to the fact that the norm of states is time independent, i.e. (
56
). Thus by itself, it does not resolve the problem with negative norm states. To address this, one needs to consider physical quantities that have a probabilistic meaning, such as those in the optical theorem. To this end define the usual
T
-operator as

S={\cal K}+iT,~~~~~S^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}={\cal K}-iT^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}\,

(71)

The
{\cal K}
in the above equation comes from the
{\cal K}
in the inner product if
T=0
. The generalized unitarity relation (
70
) expressed in terms of
T
reads

i\left(T^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}{\cal K}-{\cal K}T\right)=T^{\dagger_{\scriptscriptstyle\kappa}}T

(72)

Taking an inner product of the above
\langle\psi|\sim|\psi\rangle
and inserting a complete set of states on the right hand side,
{\cal K}_{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}
cancels from both sides and one obtains

2\,{\rm Im}\left(T_{{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}}\right)=\sum_{\psi^{\prime}}{\cal K}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}^{\prime}}|T_{{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}^{\prime}}|^{2}

(73)

where we have defined
T_{{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}^{\prime}}=\langle\psi|T|\psi^{\prime}\rangle
. The above is the generalization of the optical theorem for pseudo-hermitian theories.
If there are negative norm intermediate states
|\psi^{\prime}\rangle
, then they imply negative residues of kinematic poles in the S-matrix.
It was first obtained by Cheng-Yang Lee
[
50
]
for scalar symplectic fermions
[
47
]
, where the precise nature of the pseudo-hermiticity is essentially identical to that of our bosonic model. The above formula (
73
) clearly shows the implications of intermediate states with negative norm

{\cal K}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}^{\prime}}=-1
, since for unitary theories all
{\cal K}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}^{\prime}}=+1
and the right hand side is interpreted as the sum over positive transition amplitudes, which leads to physically meaningful cross sections.

In order to have an effectively unitary theory with positive probabilities, an additional selection rule is needed such that
only
intermediate states with

{\cal K}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}^{\prime}}=+1
contribute to the right hand of (
73
). There is no such selection rule in our model without adding some additional properties not
already contained in its definition. However there exists such a selection rule based simply on kinematics, as we now explain.
Our model is a quantum field theory with the multi-particle Hilbert space (
16
),
thus as usual we factor out the overall energy-momentum conserving delta-functions and define the amplitudes
{\cal M}
:

T_{{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}^{\prime}}=(2\pi)^{4}\delta^{(4)}\left(p_{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}-p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}^{\prime}}\right){\cal M}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}^{\prime}}\,.

(74)

Consider adding a mass term to
H
such that the particles have mass
m
.
Furthermore consider an initial state with only particles and no anti-particles such that the in-state has positive norm, and to further simplify matters let the in-state have only
2 particles each of positive norm designated simply as
|++\rangle\equiv a^{\dagger}_{+}a^{\dagger}_{+}|0\rangle
. Then the lowest energy intermediate state
|\psi^{\prime}\rangle
with negative norm
that is consistent with the
U(1)
charge
{\cal Q}
conservation
is
|+++-\rangle
which requires particle/anti-particle pair production
|+-\rangle
.
We have used the fact that for a charge neutral operator
A
, since
[{\cal Q},H]=0
, one has

\langle\!\langle\psi^{\prime}|A|\psi\rangle\!\rangle=0~~{\rm if}~{\cal Q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}^{\prime}}\neq{\cal Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\psi}.

(75)

If the total incoming energy is below the 4-particle threshold, namely
s<(4m)^{2}
where
s=(p_{1}+p_{2})^{2}
is a Mandelstam variable,
then this process is kinematically forbidden. Thus if one considers only 2 body scattering, namely
2\to 2
particles, then this process is unitary.
The right hand side of (
73
) has only positive probability contributions which leads to standard formulas for the total cross section
[
45
]
:

2\,{\rm Im}{\cal M}\left({\bf{k}}_{1},{\bf{k}}_{2}\to{\bf{k}}_{1},{\bf{k}}_{2}\right)=2E_{\rm cm}p_{\rm cm}\,\sigma_{\rm tot}\left({\bf{k}}_{1},{\bf{k}}_{2}\to{\rm anything}\right).

(76)

In summary, for 2-body elastic scattering of particles, if the total energy is below the threshold for pair production, the theory is effectively unitary.
This result is analogous to the low energy scattering of electrons
e_{-}
below the threshold for
e_{+}e_{-}
pair production, commonly referred to
as “electron–electron Møller elastic scattering”
e_{-}e_{-}\to e_{-}e_{-}
,
which is known to be unitary if one ignores production of electron/positron pairs.
7
7

7

In full quantum electro-dynamics (QED) 2 body scattering can also radiate photons,
but this amounts to almost negligible effects. In any case our model consists of only particles and anti-particles since we have not gauged the U(1).

There is a difference between our model and QED since both electrons and positrons have positive norm, nevertheless the kinematic constraint still holds
and the formula (
72
) applies to both. In the non-relativistic limit where all scattering is elastic, namely the number of incoming and outgoing particles is the same,
our model is unitary.

A similar, but essentially different, selection rule that restores unitarity is to consider the model at finite density by introducing a chemical potential
\mu
:

H\to H-\mu{\cal Q}=H-\mu\left(N_{+}-N_{-}\right).

(77)

For positive
\mu
, it costs extra energy to add an anti-particle with
N_{-}=\overline{N}>0
whereas adding particles
N_{+}
is energetically cheap. Thus with high enough
chemical potential, production of anti-particles with negative norm is suppressed.

The above conclusions open up the possibility of applying the pseudo-hermitian bosonic models of this paper, and also symplectic fermions
[
47
]
, to
condensed matter physics, where energies are low enough, in fact typically non-relativistic, which are effectively unitary since one can ignore pair production at these
energies. Symplectic fermions have a Fermi surface, thus the above discussion of
{\cal C}
-conjugation corresponds to particle-hole symmetry.
Some such applications were discussed for symplectic fermions in
[
47
]
. Let us also mention that
since the groups Sp(4) = SO(5), the N=2 component symplectic fermion has order parameters for anti-ferromagnetism, superconductivity, and also a potential pseudo-gap,
and can be studied as a toy model of high
T_{c}
superconductivity
[
53
]
where the role of SO(5) was first proposed by S.-C. Zhang
[
54
]
.
8
8

8

In light of the above remarks, the main shortcoming of the work
[
53
]
is not necessarily that it is non-unitary, but rather that it is formulated in the continuum without a lattice, whereas the lattice is known to play a fundamental role in obtaining a d-wave superconducting gap. As a toy model of superconductivity, it still has a Fermi surface, and it is distinguished by the fact the 4-fermion interactions closely parallel the interactions in the Hubbard model, and since they are marginal, they imply a fermionic critical point in 2+1 dimensions that can be reached
in
4-\epsilon
dimensions. This critical point was studied to 2-loops in
[
47
]
.
For more recent applications of symplectic fermions to condensed matter
see
[
55
]
.

IV
Higher order RG beta-functions based on the Operator Algebra (OPE)

In this Section we show how to compute the RG beta-functions based solely on the OPE in (
38
).
For our model, the parameter
\kappa=1
, however we display it un-evaluated since, as we will see, powers of
\kappa
counts loops.
The definition of our model in Section II very closely resembles current-current perturbations in 2D based on a Lie algebra,
in fact was modeled after it. We review this 2D case in the Appendix.
The role of the independent left/right moving currents
J^{a},\overline{J}^{b}
in 2D is played by the independent operators
J^{a},\widetilde{J}^{b}
.
The OPE (
38
) also closely parallels the 2D OPE in (
126
), thus the Lie-algebraic structure of higher orders in perturbation theory
is identical to the 2D case. This implies that we can organize higher orders in perturbation theory using the same “OPE diagrams” introduced in
[
41
]
.
The important difference between our 4D model and 2D current-current perturbations are the integrals involved in perturbation theory.
Thus we work out these integrals up to 3-loops in order to compare with the 2D beta functions based on the same Lie algebra, in this case SU(2).
As we will show, up to 3 loops the beta functions completely agree in 4D and 2D. Since the Lie-algebraic structure is identical, this will allow us to
propose an all orders beta function based on the results in
[
41
]
.

The SU(2) symmetric case corresponds to

g_{1}=g_{2}=g_{3}=g
since then the perturbation
g\,\sum_{a}J^{a}\widetilde{J}^{a}
is built on the quadratic Casimir.
We are more interested in the case where SU(2) is broken to U(1), where
g_{1}=g_{2}\neq g_{3}
considered below.
We will first compute the beta functions to 3-loops for the fully anisotropic case
g_{1}\neq g_{2}\neq g_{3}
,
since it’s easier to work out the beta functions for this case first and then set
g_{1}=g_{2}
.
Renormalization group beta-functions are generally prescription, or scheme, dependent. However for marginal perturbations, the one and two loop contributions are universal.
9
9

9

To see this, consider a single coupling
g
with

\beta_{g}=b_{2}g^{2}+b_{3}g^{3}+\ldots
where
b_{2,3}
are coefficients. The prescription dependence corresponds to a redefinition of the coupling
g^{\prime}=g^{\prime}(g)
. Let
g^{\prime}=g+cg^{2}+\ldots
. One easily sees that

\beta^{\prime}(g^{\prime})=b_{2}g^{\prime 2}+b_{3}g^{\prime 3}+\ldots.

The implicit renormalization prescription of this article is based on the OPE (
38
), and doesn’t explicitly rely on any epsilon-expansion, nor Feynman diagrams for scalar fields, but it should still be viewed as a specific prescription, and other prescriptions could lead to different expressions beyond 2 loops.
10
10

10

We do not address whether this OPE prescription can be
shown to be equivalent to
Feynman diagram perturbation theory and dimensional regularization to all orders in perturbation theory. However to 1-loop it was shown that these two-prescriptions do indeed agree for the closely related symplectic fermions
[
47
]
. Referring to the title of Wilson-Fisher’s paper
[
3
]
, we work in precisely
4
dimensions, not
3.99
!
The significant properties such as the existence of fixed points, flows between them, and limit-cycle behavior are expected to be independent of the particular renormalization scheme.

We first review the 1-loop result in
[
16
]
, then describe how to extend this to higher orders. The starting point is the perturbative expansion about the conformal field theory for

\langle{\bf{X}}\rangle
where
{\bf{X}}
is an arbitrary operator (it can in fact to be taken to be the identity):

\langle\!\langle{\bf{X}}\rangle\!\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{(-2\pi^{2})^{n}}{n!}\sum_{a_{i}}g_{a_{1}}g_{a_{2}}\cdots g_{a_{n}}\,\int d^{4}x_{1}d^{4}x_{2}\cdots d^{4}x_{n}\,\langle\!\langle{\cal O}^{a_{1}}(x_{1}){\cal O}^{a_{2}}(x_{2})\cdots{\cal O}^{a_{n}}(x_{n})\,{\bf{X}}\rangle\!\rangle_{0},

(78)

where
\langle\!\langle X\rangle\!\rangle_{0}
is the unperturbed CFT result.
The
n
-th term gives a contribution to the
(n-1)
-loop beta function.

IV.1
1-loop

For the one-loop beta function one only needs the OPE’s of the operators
{\cal O}^{a}
themselves.
Using (
38
) one finds

{\cal O}^{a}(x){\cal O}^{b}(y)=\dfrac{\delta^{ab}}{|x-y|^{8}}\,\,\dfrac{\kappa^{2}}{64\pi^{8}}-\dfrac{1}{16\pi^{4}|x-y|^{4}}\,\,C^{ab}_{c}\,\,{\cal O}^{c}(0)+\ldots

(79)

where there is no sum over
c
for SU(2), we used
\delta^{ab}f^{abc}=0
, and the only non-zero terms are

C^{ab}_{c}=(f^{abc})^{2}=4~~~~~\forall~a\neq b\neq c.

(80)

To order
g^{2}
:

\langle\!\langle{\bf{X}}\rangle\!\rangle=\langle\!\langle{\bf{X}}\rangle\!\rangle_{0}-2\pi^{2}\sum_{c}g_{c}\int d^{4}x\,\langle\!\langle{\cal O}^{c}(x)\,{\bf{X}}\rangle\!\rangle_{0}+2\pi^{4}\sum_{a,b}g_{a}g_{b}\int d^{4}x\int d^{4}y\,\langle\!\langle{\cal O}^{a}(x)\,{\cal O}^{b}(y)\,{\bf{X}}\rangle\!\rangle_{0}+\dots\,,

(81)

Using the OPE (
79
)
along with

\int_{a}\dfrac{d^{4}x}{x^{4}}=-2\pi^{2}\log a,

(82)

where
a

is an ultraviolet cut-off, one finds

\langle\!\langle{\bf{X}}\rangle\!\rangle=\langle\!\langle{\bf{X}}\rangle\!\rangle_{0}-2\pi^{2}\sum_{a,b,c}\left(g_{c}-\dfrac{1}{8}C^{ab}_{c}g_{a}g_{b}\,\log a\right)\int d^{4}x\,\langle\!\langle{\cal O}^{c}(x)\,{\bf{X}}\rangle\!\rangle_{0}+\dots\,.

(83)

The ultraviolet divergence is removed by letting

g_{c}\to g_{c}+\tfrac{1}{8}C^{ab}_{c}g_{a}g_{b}\,\log a
. This leads to

\beta_{g_{a}}\equiv\dfrac{dg_{a}}{d\ell}=\dfrac{1}{8}\sum_{b,c}\,C^{bc}_{a}\,g_{b}g_{c}

(84)

where increasing
\ell=\log a
is the flow to low energies.
Thus to 1-loop we have

\beta_{g_{1}}=g_{2}g_{3},~~~\beta_{g_{2}}=g_{1}g_{3},~~~\beta_{g_{3}}=g_{1}g_{2}.

(85)

The above calculation indicates that our model is renormalizable to 1-loop, meaning that no additional operators,
such as
J^{a}J^{a}
or
\widetilde{J}^{a}\widetilde{J}^{a}
, are generated since the OPE (
79
) closes.

Below we will be mainly interested in the case
g_{1}=g_{2}\neq g_{3}
which breaks SU(2) to U(1), however let us comment on the fully anisotropic case to 1-loop and its
integration since this provides insights on the main properties of these flows based on comparison with known dynamical systems, in this case rigid body motion.
The above equations (
85
) can be explicitly integrated as a function of the scale
\ell
by using certain RG invariants
Q
satisfying

\sum_{g}\beta_{g}\partial_{g}Q=0.

(86)

There are 3 such invariants, only two of which are linearly independent:

Q_{1}=g_{2}^{2}-g_{3}^{2},~~~~~Q_{2}=g_{3}^{2}-g_{1}^{2},~~~~~~Q_{3}=g_{1}^{2}-g_{2}^{2},~~~~~~~~~Q_{1}+Q_{2}+Q_{3}=0.

(87)

By substituting these into
\beta_{g_{3}}=dg_{3}/d\ell
to eliminate
g_{1},g_{2}
in favor of
Q
’s, the solution
g_{3}(\ell)
of the resulting differential equation
can be expressed in terms of the Jacobi elliptic functions

and

which are

periodic, where the two periods depend the

(See
[
38
]
for explicit formulas for

The appearance of elliptic functions could have been anticipated from the observation that if

is treated as a time and the beta functions
(
85

\dot{g}\equiv dg/d\ell=\beta_{g}
, then they can be mapped to rigid body dynamics in the absence of torque, which was solved by Jacobi. The integrals of motion for this dynamical system are the RG invariants
Q
.
To see this, let
\vec{L}=(L_{1},L_{2},L_{3})
denote the 3 components of the classical angular momentum and
\dot{L}
their time derivatives. The Euler equations are

\dot{L}_{1}=\left(\dfrac{1}{I_{3}}-\dfrac{1}{I_{2}}\right)L_{2}L_{3},~~~\dot{L}_{2}=\left(\dfrac{1}{I_{1}}-\dfrac{1}{I_{3}}\right)L_{3}L_{1},~~~~\dot{L}_{3}=\left(\dfrac{1}{I_{2}}-\dfrac{1}{I_{1}}\right)L_{1}L_{2},

(88)

where
I_{a}
are principal moments of inertia.
Identifying the moments
I_{a}
as follows, together with a rescaling of
L_{1},L_{2}

I_{2}=\dfrac{I_{1}}{1-2I_{1}},~~~I_{3}=\dfrac{I_{1}}{1-I_{1}},~~~~~~~~~~L_{1,2}\to\dfrac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\,L_{1,2},

(89)

one obtains the 1-loop RG flow equations (
85
) for
g_{a}
from (
88
) with
L_{a}\to g_{a}
. Let us also point out that
a quantum spin version of this is the anisotropic Heisenberg type hamiltonian:

H=\dfrac{L_{1}^{2}}{2I_{1}}+\dfrac{L_{2}^{2}}{2I_{2}}+\dfrac{L_{3}^{2}}{2I_{3}},

(90)

which has some resemblance to the marginal perturbations in our model of QFT as defined in (
45
).
The quantum equations of motion,
\dot{L_{a}}=i[H,L_{a}]/\hbar
,
leads to the equations (
88
) in the classical limit if one assumes the
\vec{L}
satisfy the usual quantum angular momentum algebra.

Q=g_{1}^{2}-g_{3}^{2}

\sum_{g=g_{1},g_{3}}\,\beta_{g}\partial_{g}Q={\cal O}(g^{5}).

(105)

Based on the above results, we can now propose formulas for the beta functions to all orders in the coupling.
This is possible based on the following reasoning. (i) The Lie-algebraic structure of the OPE diagrams in 4D and 2D is identical, in fact by construction.
The general formulas in
[
41
]
allow one to deal with arbitrary anisotropy in the couplings.
(ii) We explicitly checked that the integrals in 4D work out to give the same beta function as in
[
41
]
for 2D current-current perturbations up to 3 loops.
This aspect came as a surprise, and we don’t have a separate argument for why this is correct besides
the explicit 3-loop calculations we performed. Some simple checks indicate that this structure persists to higher orders, however we did not carry out these calculations
explicitly beyond 3 loops. A further constraint, or consistency check, is that we expect the RG invariant
Q
to persist to higher orders. We can thus borrow the result from
[
41
]

\beta_{g_{1}}=\dfrac{g_{1}(g_{3}-g_{1}^{2}\kappa/4)}{(1-\kappa^{2}g_{1}^{2}/16)(1+\kappa g_{3}/4)},~~~~~\beta_{g_{3}}=\dfrac{g_{1}^{2}(1-\kappa g_{3}/4)^{2}}{(1-\kappa^{2}g_{1}^{2}/16)^{2}}.

(106)

One can easily check that to order

102

Also, when
g_{1}=g_{3}
one recovers the fully isotropic case (
101

What is not so obvious, and rather non-trivial, is that these beta functions preserve the RG invariant
Q

Q=\dfrac{g_{1}^{2}-g_{3}^{2}}{(1-\kappa g_{3}/4)^{2}(1-g_{1}^{2}\kappa^{2}/16)}.

(107)

The invariance condition (
86

exactly
satisfied to all orders for this non-perturbative

The above beta functions do not constitute a gradient flow

V.4
Cyclic RG flows

Let us return to our original model with
g_{1}
real.
When
g_{1}^{2}>g_{3}^{2}
, one is above the SU(2) symmetric separatrices and the flows never encounter the
g_{3}
line of fixed points at
g_{1}=0
. These are the most exotic flows, since there are expectations that all QFT’s start and end at a fixed points, at least for unitary theories.
As discussed in the Introduction, cyclic flows are consistent with various c-theorems if the model is non-unitary. Furthermore, in order to formulate such c-theorems
and their generalization to
4D
, one needs to have a well-defined perturbation theory about
both
the UV and IR fixed points
[
32
]
, and such fixed points do not exist for the
cyclic flows we find.
For our model, if one is forced to understand all the flows in the
g_{1},g_{3}
coupling constant plane, then cyclic flows in this regime are unavoidable.

In this cyclic regime the RG invariant
Q>0
. To 1-loop one can express the beta function
\beta_{g_{3}}
in terms of
g_{3}
and
Q
. Integrating this, the coupling constant as a function of the log of the length scale
\ell
is

g_{3}(\ell)=\sqrt{Q}\tan\left(\sqrt{Q}(\ell-\ell_{0})\right)

(121)

where
\ell_{0}
is an integration constant.
The fundamental parameter of the theory is the period
\lambda
of the RG, which is a simple function of
Q
and thus an RG invariant:

g_{3}(\ell+\lambda)=g_{3}(\ell),~~~~~\lambda=\dfrac{\pi}{\sqrt{Q}}.

(122)

Thus one flows from
g_{3}=-\infty
to
g_{3}=+\infty
in a finite RG time
\lambda
. Note that along the SU(2) invariant separatrices,
Q=0
, such that the RG period consistently goes to
\infty
.

This cyclic behavior could be spoiled at higher orders in the couplings
g_{1},g_{3}
since the RG flows extend to
g_{3}=\pm\infty
which is beyond the 1-loop weak coupling regime where (
122
) was derived. One can check that to 2-loops, the period of the cyclic RG remains as in (
122
). The all-orders beta functions (
106
) resolve this issue since these beta functions are well defined as

. These non-perturbative expressions simply lead to a doubling of the RG period
\lambda
. To see this, we can eliminate
g_{1}
from the beta function for

using

:

\beta_{g_{3}}=\dfrac{dg_{3}}{d\ell}=16~\dfrac{\left(g_{3}^{2}-16Q(g_{3}-4)^{2}\right)\left(1-Q(g_{3}-4)^{2}\right)}{(g_{3}+4)^{2}}.

(123)

The above can be integrated and one still finds a cyclic RG
[
43
]
, namely
g_{3}(\ell+\lambda)=g_{3}(\ell)
, where

\lambda
is now twice the 1-loop result:

\lambda=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{dg_{3}}{\beta_{g_{3}}}=\dfrac{2\pi}{\sqrt{Q}}.

(124)

VI
Concluding remarks

To summarize, we have computed the beta functions for our model to 3 loops, and showed that they do not spoil the main features of the RG flows based on the 1-loop approximation found previously in
[
16
]
. For SU(2) broken to U(1) we re-summed an infinite number of contributions and conjectured a non-perturbative beta function. This model has a line of fixed points, which are new non-unitary CFT’s in 4 spacetime dimensions. There exists RG flows between non-trivial CFT fixed points, and we computed the relation between the anomalous dimensions of the perturbing operators in the UV and IR, and furthermore identified some special points where these anomalous dimensions are rational. There also exists a regime where the flows do not begin nor end at a fixed point but in fact are cyclic, and we computed the period
\lambda
of the RG flow in terms of the
RG invariant
Q
. We argued that this circumvents the paradigm that all QFT’s begin or end at a fixed point as proposed in
[
30
,
32
]
, primarily because the model is non-unitary.

We close with discussion of some open questions raised by the above work, of which there are many.

\bullet~

The analysis of the higher order RG flows in this paper points to the existence of some new non-unitary CFT’s in 4 spacetime dimensions. We computed some anomalous dimensions of relevant perturbing operators in the UV and based on massless flows found some special rational exponents corresponding to flows that arrive in the IR via operators of integer dimension

\Gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm IR}}>4
, such as
T^{2}
where
T
is the stress energy tensor in which case
\Gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm IR}}=8
and
\Gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm UV}}=16/5
.
Another interesting point is
\Gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm UV}}=3
which occurs at
g_{3}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\rm UV}}=4/3
, and we suggested that this could be a free fermion theory.
See Table
1
. If these calculations are correct, they are still far from a complete understanding of these new CFT’s and their flows. For instance, what are the primary fields and their correlation functions? Is there a hidden symmetry that underlies our proposal
for the non-perturbative beta functions? It is interesting to observe that rational exponents can exist in both 2 and 4 spacetime dimensions, whereas in 3 dimensions, non-trivial exponents obtained by means of the epsilon expansion are highly non-perturbative and irrational
[
3
]
. This is perhaps due to there being no obvious analog to the

factorization of marginal operators in our 4D model to 3D, which would require

3/2
such

\phi^{3}
, such that the marginal interaction goes as
\phi^{6}
. In 2D the obvious generalization of this factorization is as a product of left/right moving chiral conserved currents,

which have dimension 1, and here one can formulate perturbation theory of the WZW CFT using the OPE of these currents (see the Appendix).
In this article, we have generalized this OPE to 4D, equation (
38
), and our proposed beta function is based almost entirely on this OPE. However a comprehensive understanding the underlying 4D CFT, analogous to the solution of the WZW model in 2D, has not been proposed in this article, and is one of the main open avenues for further study.

\bullet~

The operator
{\cal K}
defined in equation (
25
) was introduced in order to obtain the OPE (
38
), and the latter required insertions of
the
{\cal K}
-operator in correlation functions which modifies the inner-product on the Hilbert space according to equation (
50
). Based on our results on massless flows above, by analogy with the classification of
massless flows between non-unitary CFT’s in 2D
[
8
]
, we suggest that
{\cal K}
can be interpreted as a topological defect, analogous to Verlinde lines in 2D CFT. In the 4D context, these should be codimension 2 defects, namely two dimensional surfaces. It is well accepted that such defects can modify the critical behavior in significant ways. The massless flows we found along the critical line
g_{1}=0
should then be viewed as constrained by the U(1) fusion category.
From this viewpoint, the line of fixed points we found are analogous to the radius of compactification
R
of a free boson in 2D, which has a duality
R\to 1/R
closely analogous to the strong-weak duality
g\to 16/g
of the beta functions, equation (
110
).
The flows in
[
8
]
are based on a study of the
{\rm SU(2)}_{q}
quantum group modular fusion category where
q
is a root of unity, which is known to be a reduction of the U(1) category from various viewpoints, such as quantum group reduction.

\bullet~

The most unexpected result we found is that the beta function for our 4D model is identical to that of current-current interactions in 2D at least up to 3 loops in our specific renormalization scheme based on the OPE. Whereas the algebraic structure of the marginal perturbations is essentially the same as for 2D, it is the fact that the higher order integrals, which rely on the coefficients in (
38
), led to this result. As stated above, we had no a priori reason to expect this and have not provided
distinct arguments for this to be the case. The arguments of Section IVD led us
to propose the non-perturbative beta functions (
106
).
It would be very interesting if this feature can be extended to a general construction to “lift” 2D CFT’s to 4D. A recent work along these lines is
[
62
]
.

\bullet~

There is some evidence for an underlying
{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
structure in our model. Above we showed that the higher order beta functions have a
g\to 16/g
duality, like an S-duality
\tau\to-1/\tau
. This strong-weak duality is an a posteriori observation based on our computed beta functions and was unexpected, thus we do not have an explanation of this from other distinct non-perturbative arguments. T-duality,
\tau\to\tau+1
typically corresponds to a periodicity due to a
\vartheta
-angle, as in supersymmetric Yang-Mills theory.
The cyclicity of the RG required us to identify

g_{3}=\pm\infty
, endowing the 2-parameter coupling constant space
(g_{1},g_{3})
with the topology of a cylinder, which is a form of periodicity.
This by itself does not establish an
{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
symmetry, since we have not precisely identified the elliptic modulus

\tau
as a function of the 2 couplings.
The role of
{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
is perhaps clearer for the fully anisotropic case with the 1-loop beta functions
(
85
) since, as explained in Section IVA, integration of the RG flow already leads to elliptic functions
[
38
]
.
Furthermore the 2D models, with identical beta functions to our models, were proposed to have the S-matrix of Zamolodchikov

[
63
]
built out of elliptic functions
[
38
]
. This S-matrix is characterized by a
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
symmetry which is a subgroup of
{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
,
generated by the matrix
A=-i\sigma^{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
.
Furthermore, it is known that this S-matrix transforms covariantly under
{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
,
meaning the S-matrix transforms with phase factors or gauge transformations that preserve the structure of the Yang-Baxter equation
[
64
,
65
]
.
In
[
38
]
, for the SU(2) broken to U(1) model, which is the cyclic sine-Gordon model reviewed in the Appendix, the latter can be obtained from certain limits of Zamolodchikov’s elliptic S-matrix, and this is perhaps a way of understanding what is left of the
{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
structure for our
two parameter model.

\bullet~

For the cyclic flows we have found, it’s important to understand the physical manifestations of this cyclicity as far as the spectrum of particles and their scattering.
For some 2D models with identical beta functions, such as the cyclic sine-Gordon model
[
43
]
, the cyclicity is manifested as an infinite number of resonance poles in the S-matrix with Russian Doll scaling as in equation (
4
). It would be very interesting to explore similar manifestations in 4D.
