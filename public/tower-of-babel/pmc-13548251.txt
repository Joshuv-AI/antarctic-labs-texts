Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a multifactorial metabolic disorder characterised by insulin resistance, progressive β-cell dysfunction and chronic hyperglycaemia, and it represents a major global public health burden.1 2 Despite significant advances in diabetes care, cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality among individuals with T2DM.3–5 People with T2DM have a twofold to fourfold higher risk of cardiovascular (CV) events, largely attributable to accelerated atherosclerosis and premature vascular ageing.4–6 Although contemporary pharmacological therapies and risk-factor modification strategies have improved clinical outcomes, a substantial residual CV risk persists even under optimal glycaemic control.6 7 These observations highlight the need to target upstream vascular mechanisms, beyond metabolic endpoints alone, through more holistic and integrative approaches to effectively prevent CV complications in T2DM.8

Endothelial dysfunction (ED) represents an early and a central pathophysiological link between T2DM and the development of CVD and diabetes-related complications. In T2DM, chronic hyperglycaemia, insulin resistance and associated metabolic disturbances promote oxidative stress, low-grade inflammation and the formation of advanced glycation end products.9 10 These factors impair endothelial nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability by reducing endothelial nitric oxide synthase activity and increasing NO degradation, leading to diminished vasodilation, increased vascular tone and stiffening of arteries.11 12 Simultaneously, endothelial cells in T2DM exhibit a pro-inflammatory and pro-thrombotic phenotype, characterised by increased expression of adhesion molecules, enhanced leucocyte adhesion, platelet activation and impaired fibrinolysis.13 14

This dysfunctional endothelium plays a pivotal role in accelerating atherosclerosis and premature vascular ageing, thereby increasing the risk of major CV events such as coronary artery disease, stroke and peripheral arterial disease in individuals with T2DM.8 15 Moreover, ED contributes to microvascular complications of T2DM, including nephropathy, retinopathy and neuropathy, by disrupting microcirculatory blood flow, increasing vascular permeability and promoting tissue ischaemia.16 17 Thus, ED represents an early, unifying mechanism that connects metabolic abnormalities in T2DM to both macrovascular and microvascular complications, making it a critical therapeutic target for reducing CV risk, delaying vascular ageing and improving long-term outcomes in T2DM.18

Lifestyle modification, including physical activity, dietary management, stress reduction and behavioural changes, is a cornerstone of CVD risk reduction in T2DM. In recent years, yoga has emerged as a promising holistic strategy for improving cardiometabolic health. Yoga, an Indian ancient lifestyle practice, is a multicomponent practice integrating behavioural modulation, physical postures (āsana), breathing practices (prāṇāyāma), meditation and diet modulation. Several studies have reported improvements in glycaemic control, blood pressure, lipid profiles, inflammatory biomarkers and measures of autonomic function with yoga in individuals with T2DM and high CVD risk, suggesting potential benefits on vascular health.19–21 Mechanistically, yoga practices may enhance endothelial function by reducing oxidative stress and inflammation, improving insulin sensitivity, enhancing autonomic balance and increasing NO bioavailability.22–25

Despite these promising findings, the evidence on yoga’s direct effects on endothelial function, arterial stiffness and vascular ageing in people with T2DM remains limited and heterogeneous. Most existing studies are small, non-randomised or focused on intermediate cardiometabolic outcomes rather than validated measures of endothelial function (eg, flow-mediated dilatation (FMD)) or composite indices of vascular ageing (eg, aortic pulse wave velocity (PWV), augmentation index and telomere length). Furthermore, no adequately powered randomised controlled trial has systematically investigated the effects of integrated yoga-based lifestyle intervention on endothelial function and vascular ageing in T2DM. Moreover, the relative contributions of individual yoga components—physical activity or physical postures, breathing practices, meditation and a yoga-based diet—to improvements in vascular physiology have not been systematically delineated. There is also a paucity of mechanistic investigations that clarify how yoga-induced molecular and cellular changes translate into improvements in endothelial health and reduced CV risk in diabetes.

Addressing these gaps is critical for establishing yoga-based lifestyle interventions as evidence-based adjuncts for CVD prevention in T2DM. Therefore, the present study aims to rigorously evaluate the effect of a structured integrated yoga-based lifestyle programme on endothelial function, markers of vascular ageing and quality of life in individuals with T2DM, and to elucidate potential mechanisms of action through assessment of autonomic, inflammatory, oxidative and metabolic pathways. By integrating clinical and mechanistic outcomes, this research seeks to advance our understanding of how holistic lifestyle interventions can modulate vascular health and reduce long-term CV risk in T2DM.

Method and analysis

Design

Prospective, four-arm, parallel-group, Randomised, Open-label, Blinded End-point (PROBE) design.

Study setting

The study will be conducted at the Centre for Integrated Medicine and Research, SDM College of Medical Sciences and Hospital, a tertiary care centre equipped with facilities for clinical evaluation, standardised intervention delivery and outcome assessment.

Study timeline

Participant recruitment was commenced in November 2025, and the study is anticipated to be completed by November 2027, including data collection and follow-ups.

Sample size determination

This trial is designed as a confirmatory study with endothelial function, assessed by FMD, as the primary endpoint. The sample size was calculated based on the anticipated change in FMD from baseline. The sample size was calculated based on the primary outcome, namely the change in endothelial function assessed by FMD from baseline. Based on our prior studies22 23 evaluating the effects of yoga interventions on endothelial function, a mean difference of −1.23% in FMD with an SD of 1.64% was assumed. To detect this difference with a two-tailed α-level of 0.05 and 95% power, a minimum of 48 evaluable participants per group was required. Allowing for an anticipated dropout rate of 30%, a total of 248 participants (62 per arm) will be recruited. This sample size is expected to provide adequate statistical power to detect clinically meaningful improvements in endothelial function following the yoga-based intervention. Secondary outcomes, including markers of vascular ageing, arterial stiffness, glycaemic status, lipid profile, autonomic function, oxidative stress, quality of life, psychosocial stress, programme adherence and cost of care, will be analysed as exploratory outcomes, and the study is not specifically powered to detect differences in these parameters individually.

Selection criteria

Inclusion criteria

Participants will be eligible for inclusion if they are adults aged between 30 and 70 years, have a confirmed diagnosis of T2DM for at least 1 year based on the American Diabetes Association criteria and are willing to provide written informed consent and comply with all study procedures and follow-up requirements.

Exclusion criteria

Participants will be excluded if they have hypertension, defined as systolic blood pressure >139 mm Hg and/or diastolic blood pressure >89 mm Hg, or if they are currently receiving antihypertensive medication. Individuals with a history of CVD or any other significant chronic illness, including chronic kidney disease, known to affect CV health will be excluded from the study. Those with neuromuscular disorders, significant joint pain or any musculoskeletal condition that would interfere with the safe performance of yoga practices will also be excluded. Additionally, individuals who have engaged in regular yoga practice, defined as 2–3 hours or more per week within the past 6 months, will not be eligible for participation.

Randomisation and allocation

Eligible participants identified through the screening process will be assigned a unique participant identification number. After confirmation of eligibility and completion of baseline investigations, participants will be randomised in a 1:1:1:1 ratio to either the intervention arm(s) or the control arm using a computer-generated randomisation sequence. Allocation concealment will be ensured using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes prepared by an independent staff member not involved in recruitment or assessment. The envelopes will be opened sequentially only after participant enrolment. The allocated randomisation number will be used to identify participants throughout the study period. The assigned intervention will be initiated following randomisation.

Blinding

This study adopts a PROBE design. Given the inherent characteristics of the yoga programme intervention, neither the subjects nor the interventionists can be blinded to the allocation of treatment. In order to reduce the likelihood of introducing any analytical biases in the study, the statistical analysis of data shall be done by an independent biostatistician who will be blinded to the group allocations.

Potential participants will undergo screening at visit 1, during which glycaemic status, blood urea and serum creatinine levels, blood pressure measurements and relevant clinical history will be documented to assess eligibility. Individuals meeting the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria will be invited for baseline assessment. At visit 2, eligible participants will undergo comprehensive baseline investigations. Postintervention assessments will be conducted at 3 months, 6 months and 12 months (visits 3–5) to evaluate both short-term and long-term effects of the intervention. Follow-up assessments at 3 and 6 months will be conducted in person, while the 12-month follow-up will be conducted telephonically. Personnel involved in outcome assessment and data analysis will remain blinded to group allocation throughout the study period to maintain end-point blinding and minimise assessment bias (figure 1).

Intervention

Participants will be randomised into four arms. Arm I will receive a yogāsanas (maintaining postures) module. Arm II will receive a prāṇāyāma (regulated breathing) and meditation module. Arm III will receive an integrated yoga module and arm IV will serve as the control group. The detailed structure of yoga intervention for the three study arms is provided in online supplemental tables S1–S6, respectively. Participants in arm I (study group) will receive a yoga programme consisting of loosening practices and yogāsanas, along with dietary modification, in addition to standard medical treatment (online supplemental tables S1 and S2). Participants in arm II (study group) will receive a yoga programme comprising loosening practices, prāṇāyāma and meditation, along with dietary modification and standard medical treatment (online supplemental tables S3 and S4). Participants in arm III (study group) will receive an integrated yoga programme that includes loosening practices, yogāsanas, prāṇāyāma and meditation, along with dietary modification and standard medical treatment (online supplemental tables S5 and S6). Participants in arm IV (control group) will receive usual standard medical treatment alone. For all three yoga intervention arms, participants will receive supervised yoga sessions (including dietary guidance) twice per week for a duration of 12 weeks, supplemented by guided daily home practice. All supervised sessions will be conducted under the supervision of an authorised and qualified yoga instructor, using a standardised intervention manual to ensure uniformity, safety and protocol adherence.

Compliance monitoring

Compliance will be verified by performing random checks through calls placed periodically without any prior notice from individuals independent of delivering the intervention and by reviewing the practice logs/diaries kept by participants and the attendance records, where necessary. Follow-up calls and standardised instructions will be provided to encourage adherence.

Adherence monitoring and participant engagement

Study group

Adherence to the Yoga V-AgE intervention will be monitored throughout the study using multiple methods. Attendance at supervised yoga sessions will be recorded by study staff. Compliance with home-based practice will be assessed using a structured daily practice log maintained by participants, documenting the duration, frequency and components of yoga practice. Participant engagement and continuity of practice will be supported through periodic follow-up telephone calls, scheduled virtual reinforcement sessions and access to standardised audio-visual instructional materials to facilitate correct and consistent home practice. Adherence to home-based yoga will be intermittently verified through optional photo or video documentation and/or demonstration of selected practices during follow-up visits. Participants will be considered adherent if they complete at least 75% of the prescribed supervised and home-based sessions. Periodic review of practice logs and random compliance checks will be conducted to ensure fidelity to the intervention protocol. The intervention will be delivered by trained research fellows who are yoga and naturopathy physicians, with at least 2 years of experience in clinical research or patient education.

Control group

Participants allocated to the control arm will receive usual standard medical care alone, as per institutional and national clinical guidelines, without exposure to any structured yoga intervention or additional educational sessions. No supervised exercise or yoga-based practices will be provided as part of the study protocol for this group. Routine clinical follow-up and medical management will continue as advised by the treating physician.

The primary outcome of the study is the change in endothelium-dependent FMD assessed at 3 and 6 months following the intervention, compared with baseline values.

Higher values indicate increased cardiac workload and greater oxygen demand, making it a useful parameter in CV risk assessment and evaluation of therapeutic interventions.39

Statistical analysis plan

All statistical analyses will be performed according to the intention-to-treat principle, including all randomised participants in the groups to which they were originally assigned, irrespective of adherence to the intervention protocol. A per-protocol analysis including participants meeting predefined adherence criteria will be conducted as a supportive sensitivity analysis. Statistical significance for the primary confirmatory analysis will be assessed using a two-sided alpha level of 0.05.

Baseline characteristics will be summarised by study group using mean±SD or median (IQR) for continuous variables depending on data distribution and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Baseline comparability between groups will be assessed descriptively. Normality of continuous variables will be evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test, Q-Q plots, histograms and assessment of skewness and kurtosis. Variables demonstrating substantial deviation from normality will be analysed using suitable non-parametric methods.

The primary endpoint is the change in endothelial function, assessed by FMD, from baseline to the end of the intervention. The primary confirmatory hypothesis is that the Integrated Yoga Lifestyle intervention (arm III) will result in greater improvement in FMD compared with the control group (arm IV). The primary analysis will compare postintervention FMD between the Integrated Yoga Lifestyle arm and the control arm using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), with postintervention FMD as the dependent variable, treatment group as the fixed factor and baseline FMD as a covariate. Additional prespecified covariates, including age, sex, BMI, baseline blood glucose and duration of diabetes, may be included where appropriate. Adjusted mean differences, effect sizes and corresponding 95% CIs will be reported.

An overall four-group ANCOVA will also be performed to evaluate the presence of any difference in the treatment effect across study arms. However, this analysis will be considered supportive and exploratory and will not alter the interpretation of the primary confirmatory comparison. To better understand the relative contribution of different components of the intervention, additional between-group comparisons will be conducted on an exploratory basis. These comparisons may include: arm I versus control; arm II versus control; arm III versus arm I; arm III versus arm II; arm I versus arm II. Where these analyses are performed, appropriate adjustment for multiple comparisons (Bonferroni or Holm-Bonferroni correction) will be applied.

Secondary outcomes (include markers of vascular ageing, arterial stiffness, glycaemic status, lipid profile, autonomic function, oxidative stress, quality of life, psychosocial stress, programme adherence and cost of care) will be analysed on an exploratory basis because the study is not specifically powered to detect differences in these individual endpoints. For continuous secondary outcomes measured at baseline and postintervention, ANCOVA models adjusting for baseline values will be used to estimate between-group differences. For categorical outcomes, χ2 tests or Fisher’s exact tests will be applied as appropriate. Results will be presented as adjusted mean differences with 95% CIs. Interpretation will focus primarily on the magnitude and precision of effects rather than statistical significance alone.

For outcomes assessed at multiple time points, longitudinal changes will be analysed using linear mixed-effects models with random participant-level intercepts to account for repeated measurements. Fixed effects will include treatment group, time and treatment group×time interaction. These models allow inclusion of participants with incomplete follow-up data and provide valid estimates under the missing-at-random assumption. Where assumptions required for mixed-effects modelling are not satisfied, repeated-measures ANOVA with Greenhouse-Geisser correction may be employed for normally distributed variables. Appropriate non-parametric longitudinal methods will be considered for substantially skewed outcomes.

As one of the objectives of the study is to investigate potential mechanisms underlying the effects of yoga-based interventions on vascular health, exploratory mechanistic analyses will be conducted. Associations between changes in FMD and changes in arterial stiffness, vascular ageing markers, autonomic function, oxidative stress biomarkers, metabolic parameters, psychosocial measures and adherence variables will be examined using correlation and regression analyses. Exploratory mediation analyses may also be performed to evaluate whether improvements in autonomic regulation, oxidative stress, vascular stiffness, metabolic status or psychosocial well-being mediate the effects of the intervention on vascular health in T2DM.

The extent and pattern of missing data will be examined before analysis. If missing data exceed 5%, multiple imputation using chained equations will be performed under the assumption that data are missing at random. Sensitivity analyses comparing complete-case and imputed datasets will be undertaken to assess the robustness of study findings.

For all analyses, effect estimates and their 95% CIs will be reported. Continuous outcomes will be summarised using adjusted mean differences and standardised effect sizes where appropriate. Emphasis will be placed on both statistical significance and clinical relevance. All analyses will be conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (V.30). A detailed Statistical Analysis Plan, including definitions of analysis populations, handling of protocol deviations, management of missing data and specification of exploratory analyses, will be finalised before database lock and prior to unblinding of the study groups for the final statistical analyses.

Ethics and dissemination

The study has obtained approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee of SDM College of Medical Sciences and Hospital, India (approval no. SDMIEC/2025/1075) and is registered with the Clinical Trials Registry of India (CTRI/2024/10/075712). Study conduct and participant safety will be overseen by a Trial Steering Committee comprising experienced clinicians and research faculty independent of day-to-day trial implementation. The committee will periodically review study progress, protocol adherence and any adverse events. Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants prior to enrolment. Participant confidentiality will be strictly maintained, with access to study data restricted to the principal investigator and co-principal investigator, in accordance with institutional and regulatory guidelines.

Criteria for discontinuation and safety monitoring

Participants will be withdrawn from the study if they voluntarily withdraw consent at any time, are unable to continue or adhere to the assigned intervention or experience any adverse event necessitating discontinuation of the intervention, as determined by the study physician. Although the yoga-based intervention is generally safe, individuals with T2DM may experience occasional hypoglycaemia, blood pressure fluctuations, dizziness, fatigue or musculoskeletal discomfort. To minimise these risks, all sessions will be conducted under the supervision of trained yoga professionals, with appropriate posture modifications and rest as required. Participant safety will be monitored by assessing blood pressure, heart rate and capillary blood glucose (when indicated) before and after supervised sessions, along with observation for symptoms such as dizziness, palpitations, sweating or musculoskeletal pain. Any participant developing clinically significant symptoms will have the session immediately discontinued and receive medical evaluation. All adverse events will be documented, managed and monitored by the principal investigator and the Data Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB). A predefined adverse event reporting protocol will be followed. All reported events will be recorded and periodically reviewed by the DSMB to ensure participant safety and maintain data integrity throughout the study.
