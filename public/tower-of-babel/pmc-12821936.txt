Insomnia severity and its association with gaming behavior and perceived health in autistic adults
Authors: Sydney Bouder, Lia K McNulty, Daehyoung Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInsomnia is a prevalent and growing health concern among autistic adults, profoundly impacting their well-being. Empirical studies suggest that autistic adults tend to spend extensive time playing online or video games, which may contribute to sleep disturbances and low health perceptions in this population. This study aimed to explore the associations between insomnia, gaming behavior, and perceived physical and mental health among autistic adults, including those with co-occurring mild intellectual disabilities (ID).MethodsA total of 117 autistic adults (Mage33.7 ± 9.2; 63.2% female) participated in this cross-sectional study. Standardized self-report instruments were utilized to assess demographics, insomnia severity, gaming behavior, and perceived physical and mental health. Descriptive statistics, independent t-tests, Spearman’s rank correlations, and stepwise multiple regression analyses were performed to answer the research questions.ResultsOf the 117 autistic adult participants, 32.5% reported moderate-to-severe insomnia, and 14.5% were identified as potentially meeting the criteria for gaming disorder. Significantly higher gaming time was reported in autistic adults with co-occurring mild ID (M = 672.6 ± 941.6 min/week) compared to those without mild ID (M = 334.8 ± 354.3 min/week, t(115) = 2.432, p < .001), and in males (M = 615.9 ± 830.3 min/week) compared to females (M = 404.3 ± 592.1 min/week, t(115) = 1.469, p = .038). Perceived mental health was negatively correlated with both gaming disorder risk (ρ = -0.221, p < .05) and insomnia severity (ρ = -0.341, p < .01), indicating modest yet noteworthy associations. Perceived mental health was the strongest predictor of insomnia severity (β = -0.273, t(113) = -3.186, p = .002.), while a more recent autism diagnosis and gaming disorder risk were also significant predictors in the regression model, which explained 22.2% of the variance in insomnia scores (F(3, 113) = 10.76, p < .001).ConclusionsThe findings of this study indicate that insomnia may co-occur with lower perceived mental health and problematic gaming behaviors among autistic adults, and these patterns appear pronounced in autistic males with co-occurring mild ID.Keywords: Insomnia, Sleep quality, Gaming behavior, Gaming disorder, Mental health, Autism

Introduction

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a lifelong neurodevelopmental condition characterized by difficulties in social interaction and communication, along with restricted and repetitive behavioral patterns and interests [1]. ASD affects an estimated 5.4 million adults, accounting for approximately 2.2% of the adult population in the United States [2]. Growing evidence suggests that autistic adults are at an increased risk for a variety of chronic health conditions, including obesity, sleep disturbances, anxiety, and depression [3]. Nearly 70% of autistic individuals have at least one comorbidity, and these untreated physical and mental health conditions further exacerbate health disparities and diminish the quality of life in this population [4].

Sleep plays a crucial role in maintaining overall physical health, cognitive function, and emotional balance [5]. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention advise that adults should aim for at least seven hours of sleep each night to maintain optimal health [6]. However, research consistently indicates that autistic adults experience more frequent sleep disturbances compared to their typically developing counterparts, including irregular sleep/wake latencies (i.e., length of time needed to fall asleep and wake up), reduced total sleep time, and lower sleep quality [7, 8]. Previous findings suggest 50–80% of autistic individuals experience sleep disturbances, including insomnia, compared to 30–35% of the general U.S. adult population [9].

Insomnia is defined as the occurrence of sleep/wake latencies lasting longer than 20 min, recurrent nocturnal awakenings lasting 30 min or longer, and symptoms persisting at least three times per week for at least one month [10–12]. Beyond its impact on daily functioning and sleep quality, insomnia is linked to adverse health outcomes, including mood instability, cognitive impairments, increased risk of chronic health conditions (e.g., heart disease, obesity, and diabetes), and higher mortality rates [10]. An earlier study comparing autistic adults with high functional independence and neurotypical controls reported that 90% of the autistic participants met the criteria for clinical insomnia [13]. Another study on sleep patterns in autistic individuals revealed that sleep problems, particularly insomnia, tend to persist into adulthood and significantly disrupt daytime functioning, which can lead to low mood, severe fatigue, and poor overall health [14].

Over the past two decades, technology has increasingly integrated into everyday life, with gaming emerging as one of the most preferred leisure activities among autistic adults [15]. It is well-documented that autistic individuals often show a natural affinity for digital interactions over face-to-face communication, including online platforms and video gaming. In one study, 15 out of 26 autistic participants reported a preference for online interactions compared to 3 participants who favored offline interactions [16]. This preference may stem from the unique environment virtual gaming offers autistic individuals, including a safe space to engage with others without social pressure. Many features of online and video gaming also align with autism traits, such as restricted interests or repetitive behavioral patterns. These games often provide an enjoyable social environment while also fostering cognitive simulation [17, 18]. While literature on autistic adults’ quantifiable gaming time is limited, a previous large-scale study of 202 autistic children and their 179 typically developing (TD) siblings reported that autistic children spend significantly more time gaming (2.4 vs. 1.6 h for boys; 1.8 vs. 0.8 h for girls, p <.001) [19]. Moreover, over 40% of autistic youth spend the majority of their leisure time gaming compared to 18% of TD youth [20]. The increasing preference for gaming among autistic youth signifies the need for further research into its implications for sleep and overall health in adulthood.

A recent systematic review found a strong and significant association between ASD and an increased risk of gaming disorder, indicating that autistic individuals may be more vulnerable to problematic gaming behaviors than their neurotypical peers [21]. This heightened risk is thought to stem from a preference for repetition and routine. Gaming disorder, characterized by uncontrollable gaming behavior, is a growing concern among autistic adults as recent trends suggest more hours are being spent engaging in sedentary gaming behavior in this population [17]. Chronic sleep deprivation, often exacerbated by excessive gaming or screentime, can lead to daytime fatigue, irritability, and heightened anxiety, which can distort how individuals view themselves and their overall health [10]. Many autistic adults use gaming as a form of escapism to cope with stress, manage sensory overload, or avoid social situations that induce anxiety [22, 23]. This idea is supported by theoretical frameworks such as the Compensatory Internet Use Theory, which suggests individuals turn to the online realm to avoid real-life problems and negative feelings [24]. This pattern is consistent with Cognitive Psychopathology models as maladaptive beliefs, such as self-escapism and cognitive diversion, often reinforce persistent, avoidance-driven gaming behaviors [25]. While gaming may provide temporary relief from perceived stressors, excessive gaming can contribute to a range of complex physical and mental health repercussions, including insomnia [26].

Certain subpopulations within the autistic community may be especially vulnerable to gaming disorder, although further research on this topic is needed to better understand this risk. While few studies have examined the prevalence of gaming disorder among autistic adults, a wide range of studies have noted the gender imbalance associated with this disorder. Across both neurotypical adult and autistic youth populations, research has repeatedly found males to be at a heightened risk for engaging in problematic gaming behaviors [27, 28]. Beyond sex-related differences, cognitive profiles, such as the presence of mild intellectual disability (ID), may also influence long-term behavioral patterns. The most recent surveillance report from the Autism and Developmental Disabilities Monitoring Network, which examined 8-year-old autistic children, found that approximately 40% of nearly 5,300 children met criteria for co-occurring ID [29]. Estimating the true prevalence of ID in autistic adults is particularly challenging given the potential for symptom overlap, diagnostic inconsistencies, and historical misclassification of ID in this population [30]. The current cross-sectional study sought to examine the associations between insomnia severity, gaming behavior, and perceived physical and mental health among autistic adults, including sex-based preferences and the moderating influence of co-occurring mild ID.

Methods

Study participants

This study includes 117 autistic adults aged 18 years or older who were enrolled in a larger intervention study. The survey used for the present analysis was administered during the baseline demographic assessment, which included self-reports of gaming and modifiable health behaviors in autistic adults prior to the intervention. All participants provided information on the timing of their ASD diagnosis, categorized as early childhood, later childhood, adolescence, or adulthood. Inclusion criteria required participants to have the cognitive ability for written communication and to comprehend study procedures. Individuals with orthopedic or other physical health conditions that interfered with independent walking were excluded. All participants provided digital written informed consent before completing the survey.

The majority of study participants (63.2%) identified as female while 36.8% identified as male. The mean age was 33.7 years (SD = 9.2), with nearly two-thirds of participants (65%) being young adults aged between 18 and 35 years. The participants were predominantly White (83.8%), followed by Black or African American (7.7%), Asian (3.4%), and American Indian or Alaska Native (2.6%), with 2.6% identifying as multiracial. Over half of the participants reported being single (53.0%), while 37.6% were married or in a relationship, and 9.4% were separated or divorced. The highest educational attainment among participants varied, with 34.2% holding a high school diploma or general educational development (GED), 28.2% having a 4-year college degree, and 21.4% obtaining postgraduate or professional degrees. Employment status was diverse, with 38.5% employed full-time, 12.0% part-time, and 16.2% identified primarily as students. While a majority of the participants were diagnosed with ASD in adulthood (ages ≥ 18, 65.0%), a smaller portion (9.4%) received their diagnosis during adolescence (ages 12 to 18) or during their early or later childhood (birth to age 5: 17.1%; ages 6 to 11: 8.5%). Regarding health characteristics, 43.6% of participants self-reported having co-occurring mild ID, and nearly 70% of participants were overweight or obese according to body mass index (BMI) classifications. While most participants rated their physical health positively, either good (47.9%) or very good (23.9%), perceptions of mental health were notably poorer. Over one in ten autistic adults (11.1%) rated their mental health as poor, compared to only 0.9% who rated their physical health as poor. Most participants (64.1%) perceived their level of physical activity (PA) as insufficiently active or inactive, with only 35.9% identifying as active or highly active (Table 1).

Procedure

Recruitment was conducted through the Simons Foundation Powering Autism Research for Knowledge (SPARK) Research Match services. The SPARK Research Match is an online platform designed to connect researchers with individuals and families in the autism community. This large, community-driven initiative facilitates participant recruitment for research studies by matching eligible participants from its extensive database with specific study criteria, including more than 50,000 autistic individuals and their biological family members [31]. For this study, SPARK Research Match services were used to identify and invite eligible autistic adults who met the study criteria to participate. By leveraging SPARK’s extensive participant network, we ensured efficient and valid recruitment of a relatively large sample of autistic adults. However, we acknowledge that the sample is not representative of the broader autistic adult population, particularly individuals with more significant support needs or limited capacity for independent study participation.

A cross-sectional survey was developed and distributed online between September 2024 and March 2025. Using standardized and validated self-report instruments, the survey assessed demographic information, insomnia/sleep quality, gaming behavior, and perceived physical and mental health in autistic adults. The survey included visual aids and comprehensive definitions for behavior-related questions to ensure clarity and comprehension, particularly considering that some participants had co-occurring mild ID. Participants required approximately 15 minutes to complete the entire survey. A priori power analysis was conducted to determine the required sample size using G*Power 3.1. Using a medium effect size of 0.15 (Cohen’s f²), a significance level of 0.05, and up to 4 predictors in the linear regression model, a minimum of 108 participants were required to achieve 90% power and identify significant predictors of insomnia severity in the regression model (two-tailed). The study received ethical approval from the University of Delaware Institutional Review Board (#:2098388).

Insomnia assessment

Insomnia severity was evaluated using the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) [32], a validated seven-item scale designed as a brief, self-administered screening tool. Participants rated various aspects of their sleep difficulties over the past two weeks on a Likert-type scale ranging from 0 (Not at all/None) to 4 (Very much/Severe). These aspects included the severity of sleep problems, satisfaction with sleep patterns, the impact of insomnia on daily functioning, the visibility of insomnia symptoms to others, and the distress caused by sleep issues [32]. Total ISI scores ranged from 0 to 28, with higher scores indicating greater insomnia severity. Established cutoffs classified scores into categories: 0–7 (minimal to no symptoms), 8–14 (subthreshold insomnia), 15–21 (moderate insomnia symptoms), and 22–28 (severe insomnia symptoms). The ISI has been validated in various insomnia patient populations, demonstrating strong reliability and validity as a tool for insomnia assessment [32, 33].

Perceived health

Participants’ perceived physical and mental health was assessed using two single-item self-report questions adapted from standard general health measures. Participants were asked, “In general, how would you rate your physical health?” and “In general, how would you rate your mental health?” Each question used a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “excellent” to “poor”. These items serve as global indicators of self-perceived health status and are commonly used in public health research due to their predictive validity for a range of health outcomes [34, 35].

Gaming behavior assessment

Gaming behavior was measured using the Ten-Item Internet Gaming Disorder Test (IGDT-10) [36], a validated instrument developed to assess problematic gaming behaviors based on the nine Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) diagnostic criteria for Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) [1]. Participants responded to ten items on a 3-point scale (0 = Never, 1 = Sometimes, 2 = Often), evaluating the extent to which they experienced behaviors such as gaming preoccupation, withdrawal symptoms, loss of control, and gaming despite negative consequences over the past 12 months [36]. Scores were dichotomized according to DSM-5 criteria [1], with ‘Often’ responses indicating the presence of a criterion, while ‘Never’ and ‘Sometimes’ responses indicated its absence. Items 9 and 10 were combined, contributing a maximum of one point. A score of five or more suggests a clinically relevant risk of IGD. The IGDT-10 has demonstrated strong reliability and cross-cultural validity as a screening tool [37], although clinical diagnostic interviews are recommended for a formal diagnosis of IGD [1]. Additionally, to estimate total weekly gaming time, participants responded to two questions: (1) “How often do you play online or video games in a week” and (2) “About how long (in minutes) do you play online or video games each time?” The first question captured frequency using categorical choice options ranging from “Never” to “Everyday”, while the second question asked for the typical duration of a single gaming session. Weekly gaming time was calculated by multiplying the selected frequency (e.g., twice a week = 2) by the reported duration per gaming session. Median values were assigned for responses reported as ranges. For instance, a response of 30–60 min was coded as 45 min. According to the DSM-5, individuals with disordered gaming are typically characterized as spending 8 to 10 or more hours per day on gaming [38]. As such, daily gaming duration exceeding 10 h (600 min) were truncated to 600 min to address potential outliers. Two cases required this adjustment in the final dataset.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated to summarize demographic characteristics, insomnia prevalence, gaming behavior, and perceived physical and mental health among autistic adults. Independent samples t-tests were performed to compare gaming behaviors by ID status and biological sex, as indicated by participants in the self-report assessment. Spearman’s rank correlation analyses were conducted to assess associations between insomnia severity, gaming behavior, perceived physical and mental health, and other demographic variables. Spearman’s rank correlation was used due to violations of normality assumptions, making this non-parametric method more appropriate for the data. Stepwise multiple regression analyses were conducted to identify key predictors of insomnia severity and to examine the influence of gaming behavior and perceived health. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM Corp., NY, USA). Statistical significance was set at p <.05 (two-tailed).

Results

Prevalence of clinical insomnia and gaming disorder in autistic adults

The results revealed that nearly one-third of autistic adults in the study reported clinical insomnia (i.e., 22.2% moderately severe and 10.3% severe), indicating a high prevalence of sleep disturbances within the study sample. Additionally, 14.5% of participants were identified as potentially meeting the criteria for gaming disorder based on their self-reported gaming behaviors (see Figs. 1 and 2).

Perceived mental health was negatively correlated with both IGDT-10 scores (ρ = −0.221, p <.05) and ISI scores (ρ = −0.341, p <.01), suggesting that poorer mental health was linked to greater risk for gaming disorder and higher levels of insomnia. Gaming time was significantly associated with the total IGDT-10 scores (ρ = 0.562, p <.01), indicating a moderate effect size highlighting that autistic adults who spent more time gaming were more likely to exhibit symptoms of gaming disorder.

A stepwise linear regression analysis was conducted to identify predictors of insomnia severity as measured by the total ISI score. The initial model included demographic, psychological, and behavioral outcomes, including, age, sex, BMI, perceived physical and mental health, levels of PA, gaming time, and risk for gaming disorder. Among the included variables, perceived mental health, timing of ASD diagnosis, and gaming disorder risk as measured by IGDT-10 were retained in the final model. Perceived mental health was the strongest predictor of insomnia severity (β = −0.273, t(113) = −3.186, p =.002.), while a more recent ASD diagnosis (i.e., diagnosis in adulthood; β = 0.292, t(113) = 3.468, p <.001) as well as gaming disorder score were also significantly linked to increased insomnia (β = 0.209, t(113) = 2.415, p =.017). The final model explained 22.2% of the variance in insomnia scores (R²=0.222, F(3, 113) = 10.76, p <.001) and indicates a small to moderate effect size. Residuals appeared normally distributed, and no evidence of multicollinearity was found (all variance inflation factors < 1.1).

Discussion

The current study aimed to explore the relationships between insomnia, gaming behaviors, and perceived mental and physical health among autistic adults with and without co-occurring mild ID. Results from the ISI suggest that insomnia is highly prevalent within the study sample, with nearly one-third of participants reporting moderate-to-severe insomnia. These findings align with a recent systematic review on the prevalence of sleep problems in autistic individuals, which reports that 40 to 83% of autistic individuals are estimated to experience some form of sleep disturbance [39]. Additionally, insomnia severity was negatively associated with perceived mental health in our sample, suggesting a potential link between sleep quality and the perceived mental health of autistic adults. These results are consistent with previous research that recognized internalizing psychological behaviors, such as anxiety and depression symptoms, as being directly associated with disturbed sleep among autistic children [40–42]. While our findings demonstrate a significant association between sleep and perceived mental health, the directionality and underlying mechanisms of this relationship remain unclear. Longitudinal research is needed to elucidate these dynamics. This need is further underscored by prior research suggesting that mental health challenges such as anxiety, depression, aggression, and hyperactivity in autistic individuals may contribute to hyperarousal, which in turn can interfere with sleep quality [43].

The co-occurrence of poor sleep, diminished mental health, and elevated gaming engagement in autistic adults may reflect a complex and interrelated pattern of behavior that warrants further investigation. According to self-reported IGDT-10 results, 14.5% of participants were identified as potentially meeting the criteria for gaming disorder as proposed in the DSM-5. These findings align with a previous study that reported significantly higher symptoms of pathological gaming behaviors among autistic children compared to their TD siblings [44]. Further evaluation of gaming behaviors revealed that autistic adults with co-occurring mild ID had significantly higher gaming time (672.6 ± 941.6 min/week) and IGDT-10 scores (2.2 ± 2.5) compared to those without mild ID (334.8 ± 354.3 min/week; 1.5 ± 1.6). Also, autistic males reported significantly higher gaming time (Mdiff 211.7 ± 144.1) and IGDT-10 scores (Mdiff 0.69 ± 0.47) compared to autistic females. Similarly, a recent systematic review demonstrated an association between autistic traits and problematic internet use and gaming disorder with moderate to strong effect sizes [45]. These findings highlight the increased susceptibility to problematic gaming behaviors among autistic adults, particularly males with co-occurring ID. This may be, in part, due to the structured, repetitive, and self-paced features of online gaming. This also could relate to behavioral, sex-based differences between autistic males and females with mild ID, aligning with the findings from one study showing autistic adult males with co-occurring ID are more likely to engage in repetitive and restrictive behaviors than their female counterparts [46].

Autistic adults who spent more time playing games each week were more likely to exhibit symptoms of gaming disorder, demonstrating a significant link between gaming duration and pathological gaming behavior. The preference for digital interactions or virtual alternatives in the autism community may pose an increased risk for problematic gaming and adverse health consequences. A growing body of literature suggests that excessive gaming behavior in autistic individuals is largely attributed to an increased vulnerability to gaming addiction, which may stem from a distinctive affinity for routine and restricted interests in this population as a core diagnostic characteristic of autism [47]. A recent systematic review of 31 studies involving over 90,000 autistic individuals underscored that these individuals are at greater risk for gaming and internet addiction compared to neurotypical counterparts [21]. In addition to the restricted and repetitive behavioral patterns, being male, higher parental stress, and co-occurring ADHD symptoms were found to be significant risk factors for addictive gaming behaviors in autistic individuals [21].

Excessive gaming has been shown to negatively affect sleep quality in autistic individuals, with studies highlighting that greater time spent playing video games is strongly associated with reduced sleep duration and increased sleep disturbances [47, 48]. Parents of autistic youth reported heightened compulsive behaviors and greater interference with daily activities as significant consequences of problematic electronic use, including excessive gaming [28]. An earlier systematic review found that individuals with ASD across all age groups are at heightened risk for problematic video game use compared to their neurotypical counterparts [49]. Both internal factors (e.g., attention challenges, oppositional behavior) and external factors (e.g., limited social engagement, gaming access, and parental rules) were significantly associated with increased risk [49]. Another review of 30 studies highlighted a positive association between ASD, behavioral addictions, including online gaming, and adverse mental health conditions [50]. Given the overlap between restricted interests in ASD and gaming behaviors, early, targeted prevention and clinical strategies are critically needed for this population.

A significant positive association was identified between weekly gaming time and IGDT-10 scores, indicating a heightened risk of gaming disorder among autistic adults who engage in more frequent gaming. While both insomnia and gaming disorder are increasingly prevalent in this population, limited research has examined their co-occurrence despite their potential to negatively impact overall health and well-being. This study adds to the growing body of evidence by highlighting the co-prevalence of disordered sleep and problematic gaming in autistic adults. Understanding context-specific, modifiable 24-hour movement and leisure behaviors, including PA, sedentary behavior, sleep, and gaming, may provide valuable insight into mechanisms underlying broader physical and mental health outcomes in this population. Autism research has long documented the multifaceted challenges contributing to sleep difficulties, including sensory sensitivities, co-occurring mental health conditions, and disruptions in circadian regulation [7, 8]. However, many contributors to insomnia in autistic adults, particularly those with relatively high functional abilities, may be behavioral and therefore modifiable. These include inconsistent routines, poor bedtime transitions, and engagement in stimulating activities before sleep, such as excessive screen use [51, 52]. Given these factors, targeted interventions that promote healthy daytime behaviors (e.g., increasing PA and reducing sedentary time) may be particularly effective. By improving health-related behavioral routines, such interventions have the potential to enhance sleep quality, mitigate risks for gaming disorder, and ultimately support the long-term physical and mental health of autistic adults.

Autistic adults remain an underserved and under-researched population, particularly in comparison to the general population and younger age groups. This study addresses this critical gap by focusing on a group at elevated risk for physical and mental health disparities [3]. A key strength of the study lies in its use of validated measures to assess clinical insomnia and gaming behaviors, providing methodologically sound and exploratory evidence on modifiable behaviors that may influence health outcomes in this population. The findings contribute to a growing body of literature documenting the high prevalence of both insomnia and problematic gaming among autistic individuals [17, 53, 54] and offer additional insight into how these behaviors relate to varying functional abilities and perceived health. These results not only reinforce existing concerns but also support the development of targeted health promotion strategies tailored to autistic adults. This study advances our understanding of the complex and intersecting risk factors affecting modifiable behavioral patterns of autistic adults.

Limitations and future directions

The current study has several limitations to be noted. Although the sample size in this study provides a preliminary basis for identifying the relationship between insomnia and gaming behavior among autistic adults, the findings may not generalize to the broader autistic adult population due to potential sampling biases and limited sample diversity. Despite the exploratory trends observed in this study, future research should aim to assess a larger, more diverse sample, including those with co-occurring moderate-to-profound ID, to improve the generalizability of the findings.

This study relied on self-report measures to assess insomnia and gaming behaviors among participants. While self-reports are an efficient and commonly used method for gathering data, they are susceptible to both intentional and unintentional biases. Moreover, the study did not include objective measures of sleep or gaming behaviors, which could provide more accurate and reliable insights. The findings reflect participants’ perceptions of sleep, gaming, and health, which may not fully align with actual behavior patterns in free-living environments. Future research would benefit from incorporating both objective data and self-reports to offer a more comprehensive assessment of insomnia and gaming behaviors in autistic adults.

Previous studies have highlighted the physical and mental health risks of excessive gaming. Despite recommendations to reduce gaming time for better health, evidence suggests that autistic individuals continue to engage in high levels of screen-based online gaming [19, 55]. Exergaming, a widely adopted approach that integrates physical movement into gaming activities, offers a promising alternative to sedentary gaming. This approach provides a familiar and enjoyable format that may help address the social and sensory challenges experienced by autistic individuals [3, 4]. Future research should explore the potential benefits of exergaming for autistic adults and assess its impact on their 24-hour movement behaviors and perceived health in real-world settings.

Conclusions

This study reveals the complex interplay between insomnia, gaming behavior, and perceived health in autistic adults. Autistic male adults with co-occurring mild ID exhibited higher levels of problematic gaming, suggesting a potential vulnerability to clinical gaming disorder within this subgroup. Importantly, poorer perceived mental health was significantly associated with both increased insomnia severity and greater risk for gaming disorder in autistic adults. These findings highlight the need for integrated, multi-component interventions that concurrently address movement behaviors, mental health, and technology-based leisure behaviors to more comprehensively support the well-being of autistic adults.
