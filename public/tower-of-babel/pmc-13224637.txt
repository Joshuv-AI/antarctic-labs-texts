Improving risk stratification of pulmonary nodules: an integrated perinodular vascular and radiomic model for clinical decision support
Authors: Wen Qiu, Chunyi Liang, Wenxuan Luo, Jinxiu Lin, Jianli Cao, Peng Peng, Yeheng Zhou, Yifan Hu, Renzheng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesLung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, with most patients diagnosed at advanced stages. Accurate differentiation of benign and malignant pulmonary nodules remains a major clinical challenge.Materials and methodsWe established and validated the perinodular vessel count (PVC) as an instrumental imaging biomarker, demonstrating its significant contribution to discriminating malignant pulmonary nodules. Leveraging this finding, we constructed an integrated predictive model incorporating intranodular and perinodular radiomics, PVC, and relevant clinical variables. A two-tiered feature selection strategy employing both maximum relevance minimum redundancy (mRMR) and Relief algorithms was implemented to refine feature sets, followed by the development of an ensemble decision tree-based classifier. The model underwent rigorous multi-center validation.ResultsThe clinical and conventional imaging (CCI) model incorporating perinodular vascular features (AUC = 0.8178, CI: [0.6417,0.9676]) significantly outperformed the non-vascular feature model (AUC = 0.7389, CI: [0.5448,0.9076]). Furthermore, the CCI_Intranodular_Perinodular_Radiomics (CIPR) model demonstrated substantially improved performance over the CCI model, achieving AUCs of 0.8704 (CI: [0.6417,0.9676]) validation set, 0.8225 on independent test set (CI: [0.7298,0.9168]), and 0.7937 (CI: [0.4234,1]) on external test set. Notably, the diagnostic performance of the final model was on par with that of three experienced clinicians. The PVC feature was consistently identified as one of the most important feature among all features in both feature selection and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) interpretability analysis.ConclusionIntegration of vascular characteristics markedly improves diagnostic performance and model generalizability. The consistent importance of PVC highlights its clinical value, and the model shows promising potential to assist in decision-making and reduce unnecessary invasive procedures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-026-02299-y.Keywords: Computed tomography, Benign-malignant classification, Pulmonary nodules, Perinodular vascular count, CIPR model, Feature interpretability

Introduction

Lung cancer has reemerged as the most commonly diagnosed cancer worldwide, with 2.481 million new cases in 2022 accounting for 12.4% of all cancer diagnoses [1, 2]. Moreover, it is projected to become the leading cause of global cancer incidence and mortality by 2050 [3]. Prognosis is highly stage-dependent: the five-year survival rate is 77–92% for Stage I but plummets to 0–36% for Stage II, underscoring the critical importance of early detection [4]. However, a majority of lung cancer patients are diagnosed at advanced stages, missing the window for curative treatment.

A pulmonary nodule (PN) is a focal, round, high-density shadow in the lung, either solid or subsolid, with a diameter of ≤30 mm. It serves as a “carrier” or early manifestation of lung cancer, with the vast majority of lung cancers first detected as nodules. Nevertheless, most pulmonary nodules are not cancerous but are caused by benign conditions such as infection or inflammation. Consequently, the clinical management strategy for a pulmonary nodule depends directly on whether it is benign or malignant. The International Agency for Research on Cancer provides clear pathological criteria. For nodules pathologically identified as benign, such as preinvasive glandular lesions, active surveillance is typically recommended. In contrast, malignant nodules generally require surgical resection [5, 6]. To reduce over-treatment rates without compromising the malignant detection rate, thus allowing for effective stratified management of nodules—has become a major clinical challenge.

The imprecision of conventional radiological assessment in characterizing pulmonary nodules can result in both delayed treatment and unnecessary procedures [7]. Radiomics and other AI approaches have shown promise in improving diagnostic accuracy by extracting high-dimensional quantitative features from medical images [8–10]. These intranodular features can capture tumor heterogeneity and have proven diagnostic value. However, tumors do not exist in isolation. Evidence shows that perinodular characteristics, like changes in surrounding vasculature, are key markers of tumor aggressiveness and prognosis, highlighting a vital direction for AI-driven diagnosis [11, 12].

The biological rationale for focusing on perinodular vasculature dates back to Mori et al.’s seminal observation in 1990 that malignant nodules exhibit significantly more pulmonary venous convergence than benign ones [13]. This phenomenon is rooted in tumor angiogenesis: malignant cells secrete pro-angiogenic factors like vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), stimulating microvascular proliferation to support growth and metastasis. Tateishi et al. confirmed through imaging-pathology correlation that CT enhancement parameters in lung adenocarcinoma strongly correlate with microvessel density (MVD) and VEGF expression, establishing that increased perinodular vascularity on CT reflects underlying angiogenic activity [14]. Further, Ma et al. used CT perfusion imaging to show that VEGF-positive peripheral lung cancers exhibit significantly higher blood flow and volume, with perfusion metrics positively associated with MVD—indicating that CT can non-invasively quantify tumor vascularization [15]. Collectively, these findings suggest that malignant nodules are typically surrounded by a denser vascular network, which correlates with tumor aggressiveness. Yet, a systematic, quantitative measure of this phenomenon remains underexplored in current AI models.

Recent advances have increasingly emphasized the integration of multimodal features to overcome the limitations of single-domain models. Sun et al. systematically reviewed the application of PET/CT radiomics and deep learning in pulmonary nodule diagnosis, noting that while radiomics provides quantitative information beyond visual assessment, challenges such as insufficient specificity and lack of multicenter validation remain [16]. Addressing these gaps, Miao et al. recently developed a nomogram incorporating perinodular fat radiomics, deep learning features from both intra- and perinodular regions, and clinical variables, achieving an AUC above 0.9 across three centers with 1098 patients—demonstrating the substantial benefit of multimodal fusion [17]. Similarly, Tian et al. explored perinodular radiomic features at varying distances and found that integrating features within a 2-pixel margin around the nodule improved differentiation between lung adenocarcinoma and granuloma [18].

Despite these promising developments, most existing models still have three key limitations. First, they predominantly rely on subjective, qualitative descriptors of vascular relationships—such as the “vascular convergence sign” or “vessel penetration sign”—which suffer from poor interobserver reproducibility and a lack of standardized quantification. Second, most models focus on intranodular radiomics, subjective perinodular signs, or clinical risk factors in isolation, without systematically and quantitatively integrating these three elements. Third, the analysis of perinodular characteristics, especially the vascular network, remains largely qualitative due to the absence of standardized and reproducible metrics.

In this context, our study first rigorously defines the concept of perinodular vessel count (PVC), which refers to the number of vessels in the immediate periphery of a nodule. PVC objectively reflects the process of tumor-induced angiogenesis and is closely associated with the malignant transformation and invasive biological behavior of pulmonary nodules. As a quantifiable and interpretable imaging biomarker, PVC offers a novel perspective and direction for achieving non-invasive and precise risk stratification of pulmonary nodules. Subsequently, this study proposes a comprehensive model that integrates the aforementioned three categories of features to enhance diagnostic accuracy. Concurrently, a core objective of this research is to realize the interpretable quantitative analysis of PVC. We anticipate that this holistic strategy will facilitate a deeper understanding of nodule biological behavior and directly contribute to optimizing clinical decision-making.

Materials and methods

This study aims to develop a machine learning model integrating multimodal features, which will subsequently be evaluated on multi-center test sets to assess its generalizability. Furthermore, a rigorous interpretability analysis was conducted to validate the importance of the PVC feature. The specific workflow is outlined in Fig. 1.

Dataset

This retrospective study curated a primary dataset of 218 nodules from 100 patients using CT scans acquired between January 2023 and December 2024 from Affiliated Yangjiang Hospital of Guangdong Medical University. An independent internal test set of 81 nodules from 62 patients was formed from scans dated January to July 2025 from Affiliated Yangjiang Hospital of Guangdong Medical University, and an external test set of 21 nodules from 16 patients was included from Affiliated Hospital of Guangdong Medical University. All cases met the following criteria: (a) availability of postoperative pathological results; (b) nodule maximum diameter ≤ 30 mm; (c) CT scan resolution ≤ 1 mm and slice thickness < 1.5 mm; and (d) availability of essential clinical characteristics. Feature extraction and selection

Three categories of features were extracted from each pulmonary nodule for comprehensive analysis. All features included are routinely utilized as standard measures in clinical and research settings. The first category comprised 30 clinical, laboratory, and radiological features, called CCI features, including patient demographics (age, gender, cough, sputum, hemoptysis, fever, chest pain), medical history (smoking, diabetes, tuberculosis, cancer, family), white blood cell count, carcinoembryonic antigen level, nodule morphological characteristics(nodule type, long-axis diameter, long-axis to short-axis ratio, volume, homogeneous density, boundary clarity, morphological regularity), imaging signs (lobulation, spiculation, pleural indentation, vascular convergence, vacuole sign, calcification, cavity), and key imaging signs—with perinodular vessel count (PVC) being a focus of this study. [19–21] Perinodular vessel count (PVC) was defined as the number of vessels in direct contact with the nodule on the hilar side. Vessels connecting only on the pleural side were excluded. PVC was determined by consensus review of axial, coronal, and volumetric reconstructions to ensure accurate identification of qualifying vessels. More details were shown in Fig. 2 and the annotation was performed based on this standard. Clinical and laboratory features were obtained through retrospective review of patient outpatient and laboratory records. Each imaging sign for every nodule was independently annotated by two resident physicians, with any discrepancies adjudicated by a senior attending radiologist.

The second and third categories consisted of 1927 radiomic features each, extracted from the core nodule region and a 3-voxel dilated perinodular region, respectively, where each region of interest (nodule) was meticulously delineated. [22, 23] Prior to feature extraction, all CT images were linearly resampled to an isotropic voxel spacing of 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm3 using trilinear interpolation. The perinodular region was then defined by dilating the nodule mask by 3 voxels, which corresponds to a fixed physical distance of 3 mm in all cases. To clarify, the dilated ROI covers both the nodule core and the perinodular region, and no image data from the nodule core was intentionally excluded or removed during feature extraction. These radiomic features encompassed intensity, texture, and shape information, and were automatically generated using the United Imaging Research Platform following semi-automatic segmentation. Radiomic features were extracted using the uAI Research Portal (United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China), a research platform whose radiomics engine is fully implemented based on the open-source PyRadiomics library (v3.0.1)

To address high dimensionality and potential redundancy in the initial feature set (~4,000 features), a two-stage filter-based selection strategy was implemented. The mRMR [24] algorithm was first applied for global redundancy reduction, prioritizing features with high relevance to malignancy and low inter-feature redundancy based on mutual information. This was followed by the Relief algorithm [25] to refine local discriminatory power by evaluating feature effectiveness in distinguishing benign and malignant cases within local instance neighborhoods. This combined approach effectively balanced redundancy control with classification boundary optimization while preserving predictive performance and interpretability.

In subsequent modeling, feature selection was adapted to the input type. For CCI models, Relief was used to select the top 30% of features (approximately 9 features). For CIR and CIPR models, mRMR first reduced the feature set to the top 20%, from which Relief then selected a final set of 18 features—double the number used in CCI with and without PVC models—to fully leverage multimodal information.

Classification

This study was designed with a systematic experimental protocol to comprehensively evaluate the performance, robustness, and practical potential of the proposed multimodal classification model in real-world clinical settings. The core objectives included: (1) validating the necessity and contribution of the key CCI feature PVC to model performance; (2) elucidating the synergistic effects and individual value of features derived from different sources; and (3) assessing the model’s advantage over pure human diagnosis and its potential to reduce the rate of overtreatment. All experiments were conducted using rigorously partitioned training, validation, and independent test sets.

First, to accurately evaluate the role of PVC in the classification task, an ablation study at the feature level was designed. By comparing models trained on the full feature set and model intentionally ablated by excluding the vessel count feature, the necessity and independent contribution of this feature to overall performance were quantified.

Second, to dissect the synergistic mechanisms among features from different sources, a progressive feature integration strategy was adopted. A baseline model relying solely on features that include demographic/clinical variables, laboratory results, and radiologist-assessed imaging characteristics (include PVC) was initially established, called the clinical and conventional imaging (CCI) model. Intranodular radiomic features extracted from the target region of interest (ROI) were then incrementally incorporated to evaluate their added value beyond clinical information, which is the CCI_Intranodular_Radiomics (CIR) model. Finally, perinodular radiomic features were encoded and integrated to construct a comprehensive multimodal fusion model, which is the CCI_Intranodular_Perinodular_Radiomics (CIPR) model, thoroughly validating the complementarity and integrative efficacy of cross-source features. In short, we now define the following distinct models:

CCI without PVC: clinic + laboratory + conventional imaging features;

CCI without PVC: clinic + laboratory + conventional imaging features;

CCI: CCI without PVC + perinodular vessel count (PVC);

CCI: CCI without PVC + perinodular vessel count (PVC);

CIR: CCI + intranodular radiomic features;

CIR: CCI + intranodular radiomic features;

CIPR: CIR + perinodular radiomic features.

CIPR: CIR + perinodular radiomic features.

Lastly, a controlled experiment involving specialized physicians was conducted to quantify the auxiliary value of the model in real clinical practice. The physician group performed independent diagnoses without assistance and recorded their conclusions and the model group generated predictions and confidence scores autonomously. Comparisons among these two groups were made along two dimensions—diagnostic sensitivity and specificity—to elucidate the practical advantages of human-AI collaborative decision-making.

The primary dataset was randomly partitioned into a training set and an internal test set in an 85:15 ratio. Both the independent and external test sets were strictly designed to include patients and nodules distinct from those in the primary training set, ensuring robust evaluation of model generalizability. All three cohorts—primary, internal independent test, and external independent test—are mutually exclusive at the patient level, ensuring no overlap between development and evaluation populations. For model training, all experiments were conducted using a bagging ensemble of decision trees [26, 27] implemented on the uAI Research Portal (uAI Research Portal, Version: 20240130) [28]. Specifically, the model employs bootstrap aggregating (bagging) with the following settings:

Number of estimators: set to half the number of input features (rounded to the nearest integer);

Number of estimators: set to half the number of input features (rounded to the nearest integer);

Other hyperparameter: default settings;

Other hyperparameter: default settings;

This approach aligns with the classical bagging framework proposed by Breiman and is equivalent to the standard implementation in scikit-learn. For full technical details, we refer readers to the scikit-learn documentation (https://scikit-learn.org) and the foundational works on ensemble method.”

Feature interpretability analysis

A multi-level analysis was employed to interpret the selected features. First, PVC’s importance was objectively assessed using mRMR (reflecting feature relevance and redundancy) and Relief (confirming classification utility) algorithms. Second, SHAP analysis globally quantified the contribution of PVC to model predictions, illustrating how its variation affects output probabilities [29, 30].

Statistical analysis

Model predictions were evaluated using confusion matrix-derived performance metrics, including Accuracy, Sensitivity, Specificity, Youden Index, and the F1-Score, to comprehensively assess classification efficacy. Given the limited sample size, bootstrap resampling [31, 32] (100 iterations) was applied on results of each test set to estimate confidence intervals for each metric. Inter-group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test [33], with a significance threshold set at α = 0.05. Key results are reported as mean and confidence interval. All analyses were conducted on both validation and test sets, including the internal validation set, the internal independent test set, and the external test set, to ensure unbiased evaluation.

Results

Classification results

As illustrated in Figure 3(a) and Table 2, an ablation experiment focusing on the perinodular vessel count (PVC) demonstrated that incorporating this feature into the feature set—followed by feature selection—significantly enhanced the AUC to 0.8178, markedly outperforming the model trained without PVC. Correspondingly, sensitivity, specificity, and F1-score improved substantially from 0.6316, 0.6154, and 0.6667 to 0.8421, 0.7692, and 0.8421, respectively. These results indicate that the inclusion of PVC significantly boosts the diagnostic performance of CCI features in distinguishing benign from malignant lesions.

Although two radiologists achieved specificities above 0.8, their sensitivities were both below 0.7, considerably lower than the model’s sensitivity of 0.8824. On the external test set, the model achieved a Youden Index of 0.6750, significantly surpassing the radiologists’ results of 0.4875, 0.0125, and 0.225. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the model’s diagnostic performance—particularly in balancing sensitivity and specificity—was comparable to or better than that of the three radiologists across both test sets.

Clinically, our model shows strong potential to reduce over-treatment. According to all datasets, nodules with ≥4 perinodular vessels were rarely benign (4/78), indicating low over-treatment risk. More importantly, in cases with fewer vessels—where visual assessment is difficult—the model provided high specificity (>70%) without compromising sensitivity (≥70%), as shown in Table 6, enabling more confident rule-out of malignancy and avoiding unnecessary procedures in ambiguous cases.

Interpretability analysis of PVC

Our analysis reaffirms that the PVC feature, along with other medical imaging-related characteristics, plays a pivotal role in significantly enhancing the diagnostic performance of the model. Subsequent interpretability analyses—including feature importance metrics and SHAP analysis—consistently underscore the critical importance of the PVC feature.

First, examination of feature distributions within the training set reveals a statistically significant disparity in PVC values between benign and malignant cases, as illustrated in Fig. 5(a). During feature selection, the mRMR algorithm assigned the highest score to the PVC feature, indicating that it achieves an optimal balance between maximal relevance to the target variable and minimal redundancy with other features (Fig. 5(b)). In other words, the algorithm identifies PVC as providing the greatest information gain with the least overlap. In a subsequent feature ranking using the Relief algorithm, PVC was considered as the second-highest importance score, immediately following “Homogeneous Density”, further corroborating its discriminative power in classifying benign and malignant nodules (Fig. 5(c)).

(e) the distribution of SHAP values across the test set, along with the SHAP dependence plot for the PVC feature. SHAP analysis likewise highlighted the prominence of the PVC feature. As shown in Fig. 5(d), PVC attained the second-highest mean |SHAP value|, confirming its role as one of the most influential predictors at both local and global interpretation levels. This offers robust and explainable evidence that the model heavily relies on this feature during decision-making. Furthermore, the SHAP dependence plot [34] in Fig. 5(e) demonstrates that higher PVC values correlate with increased model tendency toward predicting malignancy—a finding consistent with clinical intuition. Additionally, Homogeneous Density also emerged as a major contributor. The SHAP interaction plot [35] in Fig. 5(f) reveals notable interaction effects between these two features: particularly in cases with non-uniform density, PVC serves as an even more decisive factor. In contrast, when nodule density is uniform, their joint influence approaches zero, implying that additional features are required to guide the model’s decision.

In conclusion, the PVC feature constitutes a cornerstone of the model’s ability to differentiate between benign and malignant nodules. As an interpretable decision-support tool, it holds considerable promise for integration into clinical workflows, thereby aiding diagnosticians in making more informed and reliable decisions.

Discussion

Early and accurate classification of pulmonary nodules based on conventional CT features is clinically challenging due to nonspecific imaging appearances, technical variability, and limited observer agreement. Current criteria—such as size, density, and morphology—often lead to diagnostic overlap and high false-positive rates, particularly in intermediate-sized nodules [36]. Furthermore, acquisition parameters (e.g., slice thickness, dose) significantly affect diagnostic outputs [37], and inter-observer consistency remains moderate (κ = 0.454) [38]. Similarly, AI models continue to produce false results and exhibit bias, restricting clinical utility [39, 40]. This study therefore seeks to identify more robust discriminative features to improve nodule assessment.

This study confirms the diagnostic significance of PVC and establishes that a combined model (CIPR) incorporating clinical and radiomic features—particularly those capturing perinodular vascular geometry—achieves more accurate and generalizable performance than CCI models alone. Notably, this study reveals that not only the perinodular vascular count (PVC) but also the perinodular radiomic texture features contributed significantly to the improved model performance. Furthermore, the AI approach provided more consistent and objective assessments compared to physician judgments, highlighting its potential as a decision-support tool to mitigate overtreatment in cases of diagnostic uncertainty.

In recent years, significant progress has been made in the application of radiomics and deep learning techniques in the field of pulmonary nodule diagnosis. Sun et al. provided a systematic review of the current state of PET/CT radiomics, noting that most models still face challenges such as insufficient specificity and a lack of multicenter validation [16]. Miao et al. proposed the DLRCN model for patient-level benign-malignant classification by combining adipose tissue radiomics, intra- and perinodular deep learning features, and clinical data, yielding an AUC over 0.9 in three external centers [17]. By comparison, our CIPR model was designed for individual nodule-level classification and obtained an AUC of 0.7937 on the external test set. The performance difference is reasonable because the two studies address distinct tasks: patient-level diagnosis vs. single nodule assessment, together with the relatively small sample size and unrestricted nodule size in our study. However, the unique contribution of this study lies in: the first systematic quantification of the perinodular vessel count (PVC) and its incorporation as an independent feature into the model, addressing the limitations of the traditional “vascular convergence sign,” which is highly subjective and difficult to quantify. While Tian et al. explored the value of perinodular radiomics, their study did not specifically analyze vascular structural features [18]. By introducing PVC as a quantitative metric, this study directly links the pathophysiological process of tumor angiogenesis with imaging manifestations, thereby enhancing the interpretability of the model.

Through mRMR, Relief algorithm, and SHAP analysis, we confirmed that PVC is one of the most influential features in the model. This finding has a clear biological basis: Tateishi et al., through imaging-pathology correlation studies, demonstrated that CT enhancement parameters are significantly positively correlated with microvessel density (MVD) and VEGF expression [14]; Ma et al. further discovered that perfusion parameters such as blood flow and blood volume were significantly higher in VEGF-positive tumors compared to VEGF-negative ones [15]. Therefore, an elevated PVC value is essentially an imaging reflection of tumor angiogenic activity, suggesting a higher malignant potential of the nodule.

From a clinical decision-making perspective, the value of quantifying PVC is manifested in the following aspect: nodules with ≥4 perinodular vessels showed a very high malignancy rate and may merit early intervention such as surgery or biopsy, while our model still achieved satisfactory diagnostic performance for those with fewer perinodular vessels. Such nodules often present with atypical imaging features, making them a challenge in clinical decision-making. In these cases, if the model predicts a benign status, clinicians can have greater confidence in opting for follow-up observation rather than immediate biopsy or surgery, thereby reducing unnecessary invasive procedures. This finding aligns with the concept proposed by Yao et al. of “avoiding overtreatment through high specificity” [36]. Furthermore, SHAP analysis also revealed an interaction between the “uniform density” feature and PVC: in nodules with heterogeneous density, PVC has a more significant impact on the model’s decision. This suggests that clinicians should pay closer attention to the perinodular vascular status when evaluating ground-glass or mixed-density nodules.

Current clinical guidelines widely used for pulmonary nodule management, such as the Fleischner criteria, are primarily based on morphological features like nodule size and density, while the consideration of vascular factors remains at a qualitative descriptive level [19]. The CIPR model developed in this study can serve as a complementary tool to existing guidelines, providing value in the following scenarios: (1) For intermediate nodules with a diameter of 6–15 mm, which are difficult to accurately assess with traditional imaging, the model can offer a quantitative risk assessment; (2) For nodules with atypical imaging features but high clinical suspicion, a low-risk prediction from the model can support clinicians in choosing follow-up observation, thus avoiding unnecessary surgery; (3) In cases with disagreements during multidisciplinary consultations, the model’s prediction can serve as an objective reference.

This study has several limitations. The sample size, particularly the small number of benign cases (n = 5) in the external test set, may affect the robustness of the evaluation. Furthermore, interpreter subjectivity affects vessel count assessment due to differences in physician qualifications and image quality, potentially influencing model predictions. Third, the external test set, while derived from a completely independent patient cohort, comprises a relatively small number of nodules (n = 21). This limited sample size results in wide confidence intervals for performance metrics (e.g., AUC: 0.7937, 95% CI: [0.4234–1]), which reduces the precision of our generalizability estimates. Future validation in larger, multi-center prospective cohorts is warranted before clinical deployment. Moreover, future work should focus on establishing a unified vessel counting protocol, elucidating the pathological basis of perinodular vascular features, and developing automated quantification methods to improve objectivity and clinical integration.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the incorporation of PVC—a quantifiable imaging biomarker reflecting tumor-associated vascular changes—significantly enhances diagnostic accuracy when combined with clinical and radiomic features. The resulting integrated model achieved performance comparable to that of experienced radiologists across multiple internal and external test sets, with PVC consistently identified as one of the top predictive features in both selection and interpretability analyses. By offering an objective and interpretable assessment of malignancy risk, this approach has the potential to reduce unnecessary invasive procedures while preserving high sensitivity for malignant nodules. These findings support the inclusion of perinodular vascular characteristics in AI-assisted diagnostic systems for improved pulmonary nodule management.
