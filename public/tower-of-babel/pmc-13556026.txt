A lifespan perspective on stress and happiness: a qualitative metaphor analysis of young and older adults
Authors: Kamile Öner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study examines the metaphorical perceptions of ‘stress’ and ‘happiness’ among older and younger individuals.MethodsThe sample consisted of 367 participants (185 young adults and 182 older adults). Data were collected through a structured form including sociodemographic questions and open-ended prompts (e.g., “Stress is like… because…”; “Happiness is like… because…”). Metaphors were analyzed using qualitative content analysis to identify recurring thematic patterns.ResultsAmong older individuals, the most frequently used metaphors for stress were illness, loneliness, and pain, while metaphors for happiness included health, family, money, and prayer. In contrast, younger individuals most commonly associated stress with illness, sadness, anxiety, and fear, whereas happiness was metaphorically linked to chocolate, butterflies, and peace. The findings suggest that older individuals’ metaphorical perceptions of stress and happiness tend to be more enduring and grounded in quality-of-life factors such as health, social ties, and spirituality. In contrast, younger individuals’ perceptions tended to be more emotionally driven and shaped by personal, immediate life experiences.ConclusionsThis study contributes to understanding subjective well-being through age-related differences and provides implications for the development of age-appropriate psychological interventions.

Introduction

Understanding how individuals experience and interpret stress and happiness has become a central issue in well-being research. While stress is an inevitable part of daily life that challenges individuals’ psychological and physiological limits, happiness represents a key indicator of subjective well-being and overall quality of life. Both stress and happiness, rather than being general and immutable, are shaped by individuals’ perceptions, experiences, and cognitive interpretation [1–5]. In this sense, examining how these concepts are understood and expressed provides important insights into the underlying structure of well-being. Despite extensive research, little is known about how stress and happiness are cognitively constructed across the lifespan through metaphorical representations.

Stress can be observed across all age groups and is a phenomenon that varies from person to person and can arise from multiple sources such as environmental conditions, demands, and life challenges. It may emerge from daily problems, developmental changes, or major life events such as illness or loss. Stress can also take different forms, including acute, episodic, and chronic stress. Acute stress is typically short-term and manageable, whereas chronic stress is long-lasting and more distressing. Recognizing and managing stress is essential for maintaining well-being. Reducing stress is associated with improved psychological health and increased well-being [2, 6–8]. In addition to stress, happiness has also received considerable attention in well-being research.

Positive emotions associated with happiness tend to be more stable and enduring than negative emotional states [9]. Happiness is a broad concept encompassing emotional (hedonic) well-being, referring to feelings of joy and pleasure; eudaimonic well-being, reflecting meaning and purpose in life; and evaluative well-being, related to life satisfaction [10]. Together, these components determine an individual’s level of happiness and subjective well-being. Subjective well-being refers to positive thoughts and emotions about life as well as evaluations of life satisfaction [11]. Happiness is also considered a fundamental component of health and well-being [12]. However, the absence of negative conditions such as depression or distress does not necessarily indicate happiness, as individuals may experience both positive and negative emotions simultaneously [10, 13]. In addition, happiness can be conceptualized at different levels: macro (e.g., wealth, justice, freedom), meso (e.g., institutional conditions such as autonomy), and micro (e.g., personal capabilities such as activity, independence, and social skills) [14]. These conceptualizations of happiness may vary across the lifespan, as individuals’ priorities, experiences, and emotional orientations change with age.

Beyond its conceptual definitions, happiness is often characterized as a stable and enduring mental state [15]. Every individual aspires to achieve happiness, often requiring continuous effort to attain it [16]. Happiness is subjectively defined as a positive emotional state and has been shown to influence productivity, commitment, and performance [17, 18]. It can also be understood as an ongoing process that individuals rediscover throughout their lives, reflecting a connection between inner experiences and the external world [19]. Concepts such as meaningful life, well-being, quality of life, satisfaction, and happiness are closely interconnected [20]. Although the terms happiness, well-being, and quality of life are sometimes used interchangeably, they denote different meanings [21]. In Veenhoven’s [21] conceptualization, happiness refers specifically to the inner appreciation of life, whereas quality of life is a broader construct encompassing multiple dimensions. From another perspective, happiness encompasses several dimensions, including affective well-being, eudaimonic well-being (meaning and purpose in life), and evaluative well-being (life satisfaction) [10]. While happiness is not the sole indicator of quality of life, it is often considered one of the most comprehensive summary measures [21]. Therefore, understanding the nature of happiness and its role in quality of life remains a central concern in well-being research [22]. The meaning and importance attributed to happiness may differ across individuals, particularly across different stages of life.

This study focuses on older and younger individuals, who are at different developmental stages of life and may therefore differ in their levels of subjective well-being. Previous studies have reported inconsistent findings regarding the relationship between age and happiness. While some studies indicate that older adults report higher levels of happiness, evidence also suggests that happiness and well-being follows a U-shaped developmental trajectory, increasing in early adulthood, declining in midlife, and rising again in later life [23–25]. Despite these variations, happiness remains a significant and valuable concept across all ages [16]. Research suggests that older individuals tend to associate happiness more strongly with social relationships and meaningful connections, emphasizing the importance of social interaction and bonding [12, 26]. In contrast, younger individuals relate happiness more to personal and emotional factors such as emotional intelligence, empathy, and lower levels of perfectionism [27–29]. Additionally, young people have mentioned that they have to cope with many negative life events and stress factors in order to sustain their happiness. Reducing stress helps individuals live a happier life [29, 30]. Examining these differences between age groups provides important insight into how the concepts of stress and happiness evolve across developmental stages, and may support the development of age-sensitive interventions targeting subjective well-being.

This study aims to address these gaps by examining metaphorical constructions of stress and happiness, comparing them across different age groups, and offering insight into the structure of subjective well-being across the lifespan. Existing research on stress and happiness has predominantly relied on quantitative data derived from descriptive or experimental methods [25, 31–36]. Comprehensive qualitative analyses examining how individuals make sense of stress and happiness remain limited, and metaphor-based studies addressing age-related differences are particularly scarce. As a result, a holistic understanding of how these concepts are shaped across different stages of life is still lacking. In this context, previous studies, such as those by Rechsteiner et al. [37] and Hui [38], have applied metaphor analysis to examine stress and happiness, demonstrating that this method is effective in revealing individuals’ perceptions. However, these studies do not comprehensively address age-related differences in metaphorical representations. A particular strength and novelty of the present study is its developmental perspective, comparing the metaphorical representations of stress and happiness between younger and older adults. While previous studies have explored metaphors related to stress and happiness [37, 38], few have examined how these metaphorical conceptualizations differ across the lifespan. Metaphors reflect underlying cognitive processes and provide insight into how individuals perceive, conceptualize, and express their experiences [39, 40]. Building on previous research demonstrating the usefulness of metaphor analysis in exploring psychological experiences [37], the present study applies this approach to examine how individuals conceptualize stress and happiness. Therefore, examining how individuals’ perceptions of stress and happiness are constructed through metaphors may help to address this gap.

Therefore, this study aims to comparatively examine how older and younger individuals perceive and conceptualize stress and happiness using a qualitative approach based on metaphor analysis. This approach is particularly appropriate because metaphors reflect cognitive processes and manifest not only in language but also in perception [39]. By examining how concepts are perceived and expressed through metaphor analysis, this study aims to offer new perspectives in psychology and mental health research on aging. In addition to contributing to academic knowledge, the study seeks to provide a basis for developing interventions aimed at enhancing individuals’ subjective well-being, particularly within psychology, counseling, and mental health contexts.

Theoretical framework of metaphor

A metaphor is not merely a linguistic expression but also a way of understanding and experiencing one concept through another. It reflects how concepts are structured in the mind and functions as a cognitive tool. Metaphors create meaning by mapping knowledge from a source domain onto a target domain [40, 41]. In this sense, they can be understood as conceptual expressions through which individuals interpret and make sense of their experiences [42].

Conceptual Metaphor Theory (CMT) explains how abstract concepts are structured through their linguistic realizations and demonstrates that metaphors are pervasive in everyday life, influencing not only language but also thought and action [37, 41]. For example, stress is often conceptualized through metaphors such as “burden” or “fight,” while happiness is expressed through metaphors such as “light” or “upward movement” [41, 43–45]. These examples indicate that metaphors reflect not only linguistic patterns but also deeper cognitive and perceptual processes.

From a cognitive perspective, metaphorical expressions are based on mappings between abstract and embodied knowledge. Understanding expressions such as “grasping a concept” or “overcoming an emotion” involves mental simulation of physical experiences [46]. In addition, metaphors facilitate learning and memory by linking new information with prior knowledge [47]. As such, metaphors play a central role in shaping how individuals interpret the world. In this context, examining age-related metaphorical expressions is important for understanding how individuals conceptualize stress and happiness and how subjective well-being is constructed across different stages of life. Methods

Research design

A phenomenological qualitative design was employed to explore individuals’ metaphorical perceptions of stress and happiness. Phenomenology focuses on understanding how individuals interpret and make sense of their lived experiences [48, 49]. In this context, metaphor analysis was used as a qualitative approach to reveal participants’ internalized meanings and culturally shaped perceptions of stress and happiness.

Phenomenological research aims to interpret the meaning of experiences rather than to establish general laws [50]. Data were collected through qualitative data collection techniques, and the analysis process involved organizing, coding, and interpreting the data to identify patterns and meanings [48, 51–55]. In this study, content analysis was used to identify common metaphorical themes, while phenomenological analysis was used to interpret how participants construct meaning through metaphorical expressions [54, 56].

Participants

The study was conducted in 2023 in the urban center of Çankırı, Türkiye. The study sample consisted of community-dwelling individuals, including young adults aged 18–30 years (university students) and older adults aged 65 years and above. Both younger and older participants were recruited during the same period in 2023 using purposive sampling through face-to-face invitations. Individuals who voluntarily agreed to participate were included in the study.

Measures

The data collection tools used in this study are presented below. The data collection process is described in detail in the Procedure section.

Personal information form

The personal information form, developed by the researcher, consisted of five multiple-choice questions designed to identify participants’ demographic characteristics. These included age, gender, marital status, education level, and the presence of chronic illness.

Semi-structured questionnaire

A semi-structured, open-ended questionnaire was developed by the researcher to explore participants’ metaphorical perceptions of stress and happiness. Participants were asked to complete the following prompts.

“Stress is like………………………….; because ……………………….”“Happiness is like …………………….; because ….……………….……”

“Stress is like………………………….; because ……………………….”

“Stress is like………………………….; because ……………………….”

“Happiness is like …………………….; because ….……………….……”

“Happiness is like …………………….; because ….……………….……”

Although phenomenological interviews are typically conducted as in-depth interactions [48, 53], the present study employed a focused metaphor elicitation form consisting of a structured yet open-ended prompt [57]. Given the focused nature of the metaphor elicitation procedure, the duration of each session was considered appropriate for the aims of the study. In this study, participants were asked to express their perceptions of stress and happiness through metaphors. Consequently, responses were generally direct and concise. No restrictions were imposed on metaphor selection. To enhance clarity and depth, the phrase “because” was included in the prompts to encourage participants to elaborate on their responses [57, 58].

To ensure reliability, participants were asked to explain their responses, and additional time and clarification were provided when needed, particularly for older individuals. This approach supported consistency and clarity in the data. Furthermore, validity was strengthened through member checking [59]. In the present study, member checking was conducted face-to-face immediately after participants completed the questionnaire, during which participants confirmed that their metaphorical expressions accurately reflected their intended meanings.

Procedure and data analysis

Data were collected face-to-face by the researcher using a personal information form and semi-structured metaphor prompts. Participants completed the questionnaires immediately after providing consent at the location where they were recruited, with each session lasting approximately 10–12 min. Before participation, all individuals received verbal and written information about the study and provided both written and verbal informed consent. No financial or material incentives were offered. The study was approved by the Health Sciences Ethics Committee of Çankırı Karatekin University (Decision No. 4, December 15, 2022) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Descriptive statistical analyses, including mean, standard deviation, and percentage, were conducted using SPSS version 22. In addition, qualitative data were analyzed using content analysis. Content analysis involves systematically examining textual data to identify patterns, categories, and themes. In this study, the data were coded and categorized, and both lower- and higher-level classifications were established. These categories were then quantified to support further analysis [50]. The resulting codes and categories were reviewed for consistency throughout the analysis process.

Coding process

The coding process was conducted manually by the researcher, based on the principles of Conceptual Metaphor Theory [40]. In the initial stage, all metaphorical expressions were carefully examined. Incomplete or illogical metaphors were excluded from the analysis. Subsequently, meaningful statements in participants’ responses were identified and labeled, and core metaphorical concepts were determined. In the final stage, similar codes were grouped into broader categories based on conceptual similarities, and overarching themes representing the essence of the metaphorical expressions were identified. The coding process was conducted systematically to ensure consistency.

Categorization process

The identified metaphorical concepts were grouped and organized into categories. These categories were informed by previous metaphor analysis studies [60–62], and derived from recurring patterns and similarities in the data. Categories were developed inductively.

Validity and reliability measures

The following strategies were employed to ensure the validity and reliability of the analysis:

Inter-coder Reliability: The reliability of the coding was assessed using the formula proposed by Miles and Huberman (2015) [63] [reliability = consensus / (consensus + dissent)]. In phenomenological research, manual data analysis is a common practice that enables the researcher to become deeply immersed in the data and to identify its essential meanings. The phenomenological approach emphasizes the interpretation of lived experiences and requires the researcher to engage closely with the data and participants [49, 64]. Accordingly, manual analysis was preferred in this study to establish a direct connection with participants’ narratives and to achieve a more in-depth understanding of their metaphorical expressions.

A total of eight older individuals were excluded due to cognitive impairments or sensory limitations. This ensured that the data accurately reflected participants’ cognitive perceptions, as such limitations could affect their ability to produce meaningful verbal and metaphorical expressions.

As a phenomenological study, the aim of this research is not to generalize the findings but to explore individuals’ perceptions of stress and happiness in depth. To ensure that participants could meaningfully reflect their experiences and express their metaphorical perceptions, purposive sampling was employed. This method is widely used in qualitative research to identify information-rich cases relevant to the research topic [65]. It enables researchers to focus on depth and meaning and to access participants with rich and relevant knowledge [66, 67].

During the data analysis process, six metaphors related to stress and seven related to happiness were excluded due to incomplete or logically inconsistent expressions. Examples of excluded responses include:

The stress perceptions of older individuals were categorized into seven groups: emotional state, life and relational, health and illness, environment–nature, burden and pressure, pecuniary and need, and object metaphors, comprising a total of 79 different metaphors. Their perceptions of happiness were categorized into six groups: environment–nature, life and relational, faith, object, health and illness, and pecuniary and need metaphors, with a total of 63 different metaphors. Among the identified metaphors, stress was most frequently associated with illness, loneliness, and pain, whereas happiness was most frequently associated with health, family, money, and prayer (Tables 3 and 4).

Similarly, younger individuals’ perceptions of stress were grouped into seven categories: emotional state, life and relational, health and illness, environment–nature, burden and pressure, future and uncertainty, and object metaphors, resulting in 75 different metaphors. Their perceptions of happiness were categorized into six groups: environment–nature, life and relational, faith, object, health and illness, and emotional state metaphors, comprising 66 different metaphors. The most frequently reported stress metaphors among young individuals were related to illness, sadness, anxiety, and fear, while happiness was most frequently associated with chocolate, butterflies, and peace (Tables 3 and 4).

Overall, the most frequently reported stress metaphors across both groups included illness, loneliness, sadness, fear, pain, death, and anxiety. In contrast, the most common happiness metaphors included health, family, chocolate, prayer, butterflies, peace, child, money, candy, and joy of life (Tables 3 and 4).

Discussion

Stress is an inevitable aspect of human life. Stress is related to how we perceive a situation and varies from individual to individual. Previous research has often conceptualized stress through metaphorical representations, such as weight, overflowing containers, or threatening entities, emphasizing its overwhelming and burdensome nature [45, 69, 70]. In line with this literature, the present study also found that individuals tend to express stress through metaphors reflecting burden, imbalance, and emotional strain. However, a key contribution of this study is the identification of age-related differences in these metaphorical constructions. While younger individuals predominantly framed stress through emotional and cognitive metaphors such as illness, sadness, anxiety, and fear, older individuals were more likely to conceptualize stress through somatic and health-related metaphors, including illness, pain, and loneliness. This pattern indicates that stress in younger individuals is more closely linked to affective and future-oriented concerns, whereas in older individuals it is associated with physical vulnerability and existential awareness.

The concept of “psychological trauma” which we can associate with the concept of stress, implies that individuals experiencing traumatic stress are injured. Therefore, it is more closely related to the metaphor of physical injury. Similar metaphorical concepts related to trauma, such as shock, burden and wound, associated with bodily processes, have also been identified [37]. Similarly, research on Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) has shown that individuals frequently describe their experiences using metaphors related to bodily damage, conflict, and loss, including themes such as injury, pain, war, and death [60]. Beck’s metaphor analyses [71–73] include a wide range of expressions such as “ticking time bomb,” “invisible wall,” “suffocating layers of trauma,” ‘enveloping darkness’, and “sea of sadness,” reflecting the complexity of traumatic stress. These metaphorical representations can be broadly grouped into themes such as darkness, suffocation, threat, and emotional burden, highlighting distressing and embodied nature of stress experiences. Consistent with this literature, the present study also found that stress is predominantly conceptualized through metaphors reflecting illness, pain, loneliness, sadness, fear and anxiety.

In the present study, metaphors related to stress among older individuals were predominantly negative and closely associated with their quality of life. This finding is consistent with Van Wijngaarden et al. [74], who reported that metaphors such as struggle, void, and breakdown reflect a diminished sense of well-being in later life. Similarly, Rossi et al. [60] found that older individuals tend to employ metaphors such as hell, loss, death, body and damage, emphasizing irreversible and existential aspects of stress. In contrast, the metaphors used by younger individuals in the present study were more individual, abstract, and emotionally oriented. This pattern aligns with previous research indicating that younger individuals tend to conceptualize stress through personal and affective experiences [70, 75, 76]. Taken together, these findings suggest that while stress is perceived as a negative experience across all age groups, its underlying meaning differs substantially across the lifespan.

Happiness is a multidimensional construct encompassing a range of positive emotions and subjective experiences, and like stress, it varies across individuals. Previous metaphor studies have consistently shown that happiness is conceptualized through positive and embodied metaphors, often associated with upward orientation, light, vitality, and positive movement [44, 62, 77, 78]. Expressions such as “feeling up,” “high spirits,” and “on cloud nine” reflect the common metaphorical framing of happiness as elevation and expansion across different languages and cultures [43]. Consistent with this literature, the findings of the present study also revealed that happiness is predominantly expressed through positive and desirable experiences, including metaphors such as health, family, chocolate, butterflies, peace, money, and prayer. However, beyond this general positivity, an important contribution of this study lies in demonstrating age-related differences in the conceptualization of happiness. While older individuals tended to associate happiness with relational continuity, health, and existential meaning, younger individuals more frequently framed happiness through hedonic, emotional, and immediate experiences. This shows that happiness in later life is more related to meaning, stability, and social relationships, whereas in younger individuals it is more related to pleasure, freedom, and short-term positive emotions.

The findings of the present study indicate that older individuals tend to conceptualize stress through negative and often challenging life experiences, whereas their perceptions of happiness are associated with positive conditions that enhance their overall quality of life. This pattern may be understood in light of age-related changes in decision-making and life perspective. Previous research suggests that older adults rely more heavily on accumulated experience and knowledge when making decisions, and that these experiences play a central role in shaping well-being across the lifespan [79]. In addition, aging has been associated not only with decline but also with increased wisdom and reflective capacity [80]. Studies further indicate that older individuals tend to rely more on reasoning and life experience and less on immediate emotional responses compared to younger individuals [81, 82]. Moreover, older adults are more likely to adopt decision strategies aimed at maximizing emotional well-being [83]. Overall, these findings suggest that older individuals’ views of stress and happiness are shaped by their life experiences and a greater focus on long-term well-being and quality of life. This may explain why they emphasize health, relationships, and stability when describing happiness, and view stress in more concrete, life-related ways.

The findings of the present study indicate that younger individuals predominantly conceptualize both stress and happiness through emotionally oriented metaphors. In particular, the most frequently expressed metaphors in this group include sadness, anxiety, fear, and illness, highlighting the central role of affective experience in their interpretations. Previous research highlights the role of metaphors in shaping how younger individuals understand and express their emotional experiences, while also suggesting that such metaphorical frameworks may influence how these experiences are interpreted [75]. Evidence indicates that during adolescence and early adulthood, emotional and reactive brain regions, such as the amygdala, play a more prominent role, whereas the prefrontal cortex, responsible for cognitive control and regulation, continues to mature over time [84, 85]. This developmental imbalance can lead to a stronger reliance on emotional processing when interpreting experiences. In line with this perspective, the prominence of emotion-based metaphors in the present study suggests that younger individuals are more likely to interpret both stress and happiness through immediate affective responses rather than through long-term or experiential frameworks.

This study provides both theoretical and practical contributions by demonstrating how metaphor analysis can be used to reveal age-related differences in the conceptualization of stress and happiness. The findings suggest that older individuals tend to construct these concepts through relational, life-contextual, and meaning-oriented frameworks, whereas younger individuals are more likely to interpret them through emotional, immediate, and subjective experiences. These results highlight that stress and happiness are not only experienced differently across the lifespan but are also cognitively structured in distinct ways. From a practical perspective, these findings offer valuable insights for fields such as psychology, education, and healthcare, as they emphasize the importance of considering age-specific perceptions when designing interventions aimed at improving well-being. Understanding how individuals conceptualize stress and happiness may contribute to the development of more effective, tailored, and empathetic approaches to mental health support. Based on the present findings, interventions for older adults may benefit from focusing on health concerns, social connectedness, and meaning in life, as these themes were prominent in participants’ metaphorical representations. In contrast, interventions for younger adults may place greater emphasis on emotional regulation, coping with stress and anxiety, and strengthening psychological resilience. Considering these age-related differences may facilitate the development of more tailored, age-sensitive, and person-centered mental health interventions.

The limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the sample was limited to young adults aged 18–30 years and older adults aged 65 years and above, all of whom were recruited in 2023 from the urban center of Çankırı, Türkiye. This may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other age groups, geographical settings, and cultural contexts. Second, as the study employed a qualitative research design based on metaphor analysis, the findings are inherently interpretive and may be influenced by participants’ subjective expressions as well as the researchers’ analytical perspectives.

In addition, the data were collected through self-reported responses, which may be affected by individual differences in expression, recall, and willingness to share personal experiences.

Finally, the study focused on specific metaphor elicitation questions and analytical approaches, which may have shaped the range and nature of the metaphors obtained. Future research may benefit from including more diverse samples and mixed-method designs to enhance the generalizability and depth of the findings.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the metaphorical conceptualization of stress and happiness differs across age groups, reflecting age-related differences in life experiences and subjective interpretations. Older individuals tend to frame stress in relation to illness, loneliness, and pain, while conceptualizing happiness through domains such as family, health, money, and prayer. In contrast, younger individuals interpret stress through metaphors such as illness, sadness, anxiety, and fear, and associate happiness with concepts such as chocolate, butterflies, and peace. By examining how stress and happiness are conceptualized through metaphors, this study provides insight into age-related differences in the metaphorical interpretation of subjective well-being. In particular, the findings indicate that metaphorical perceptions of stress and happiness vary across age groups, highlighting the importance of developing age-sensitive and individualized interventions.

Future research may extend these findings by examining more diverse cultural and demographic groups to enhance generalizability. In addition, incorporating mixed-method approaches that combine qualitative and quantitative designs may provide a more comprehensive understanding of metaphorical perceptions by integrating both statistical analysis and in-depth narrative insights.
