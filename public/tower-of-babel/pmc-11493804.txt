Intraventricular baclofen for intractable spasticity and secondary dystonia: a frame-based stereotactic approach and case series
Authors: Zhengyu Lin, Peng Huang, Yixin Pan, Ruinan Shen, Suzhen Lin, Yiwen Wu, Dianyou Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveIntraventricular baclofen has been reported as an alternative to intrathecal baclofen for managing refractory spasticity and dystonia in some circumstances. In this report, we described a frame-based stereotactic approach for precisely positioning of the infusion catheter into the third ventricle.Material and methodsHigh resolution contrast-enhanced anatomical MR sequences was acquired prior to surgery for pre-planning. Catheter trajectory was planned to pass from the lateral ventricle to the third ventricle through the Foramen of Monro. The surgical procedure was adapted from the frame-based deep brain stimulation surgery. The Touch-Loc kit (SceneRay, China) was used to temporarily secure the catheter in place during the surgery. From July 2022 to December 2022, three patients suffering from intractable spasticity and/or secondary dystonia received IVB successfully using the described technique.ResultsNo severe adverse events, including death, intracranial hemorrhage, infection, catheter migration or fracture, were documented at the last follow-up (range: 12‒24 months). Transient side effects included mild nausea following the initiation of infusion or the increase in infusion rate. All three patients responded to the IVB.ConclusionsThe described frame-based stereotactic technique for IVB catheter implant is feasible and could be quickly mastered by neurosurgeons in related fields. Larger prospective cohorts with longer follow-up periods are necessary to further evaluate the long-term safety and efficacy of this procedure.

Introduction

Intraventricular baclofen (IVB) infusion has been reported as an alternative to intrathecal baclofen (ITB) in patients with medically refractory secondary dystonia [1, 12]. It would be especially beneficial for those with frequent ITB pump revisions [15] and with anatomical anomalies which make ITB therapy difficult [2, 11]. Mini-invasive endoscopic approaches with [4] or without [1, 2] neuronavigation have been respectively reported in the previous literature. Here we described a frame-based stereotactic approach adapted from deep brain stimulation (DBS) surgery to precisely implant the catheter into the third ventricle for continuous baclofen infusion. A case series of 3 adult patients with secondary dystonia and spasticity of various origins treated with the described technique was also presented.

Material and methods

Patient histories

This study is a single-center retrospective case series study. From July 2022 to December 2022, three adult patients with a history of secondary dystonia and spasticity were consecutively recruited and underwent surgery for placement of the catheter into the third ventricle and subfascial placement of a programmable pump for continuous baclofen infusion (Table 1). Prior to surgery, they received a preoperative trial consisting of a single intrathecal baclofen bolus dose of 50 μg delivered via lumbar puncture. A second bolus dose of 75 μg would be administered the following day if the efficacy of the initial trial was doubtful. All patients have given written informed consent for the surgery and for regular follow-ups for pump refilling and programming. Case 1 is a 63-year-old male suffering from progressive generalized dystonia and spasticity with a possible etiology of cortical-basal degeneration for 4 years. He suffered from remarkable postural instability, dysphagia and hypertonia of the left extremities when admitted in our center. The Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS) scores were 4 and 3 for left upper and lower extremity, and 2 for right extremities, respectively. The score of the cortical basal ganglia functional scale (CBFS) was 30. The intrathecal baclofen trial of 50 μg provided satisfactory improvement in spasticity and posture. Considering the cortico-basal origin of the disease, we speculated that a direct intracranial infusion of baclofen might be more beneficial than ITB in reducing the dystonia and spasticity in this patient.

Case 2 is a 27-year-old female with generalized dystonia and spasticity due to cerebral palsy. She was born premature and suffered from severe perinatal hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy during delivery. She has successively received cervical selective dorsal rhizotomy, cervical perivascular sympathectomy, bilateral pallidal DBS, and experimental superior cerebellar peduncle DBS [8] but with limited efficacy on chorea and spasticity of upper limbs. She also suffered from dysarthria, severe lordosis and articular deformation when admitted to our service. The MAS scores were 3–4 for upper limbs and 2 for lower limbs. The on-stimulation movement score and disability score of the Burke-Fahn-Marsden Dystonia Rating Scale (BFMDRS) was 72 and 19, respectively. The lumbar puncture for baclofen trial was difficult but succeeded in the end and a bolus of 75 μg resulted in acceptable improvement in upper limbs’ spasticity score over 60%. In view of the predictable difficulty with intrathecal catheter insertion due to severe lordosis, we decided instead to perform IVB to ameliorate the functionality of her upper limbs.

Case 3 is a 55-year-old male with severe spastic quadriplegia following an intracranial aneurysmal hemorrhage for 1 year. Prior to his admission, He has been treated with a right-sided ventriculoperitoneal shunt for his post-hemorrhagic hydrocephalus. Patient could not tolerate side effects of oral baclofen and tizanidine (e.g., dizziness and constipation). The MAS scores were 4 for both upper and lower limbs. 50 μg bolus of intrathecal baclofen mildly reduced the spasticity of the lower limbs. The baclofen administration route has been discussed. The ITB catheter tip usually needed to be placed at mid-cervical level to relieve upper limb spasticity [9]. However, as our team only had the model 8731SC (Medtronic, MN), a two-piece catheter with a spinal segment of 38.1 mm in length, so that we were concerned that the catheter tip would not reach the desired level using the ITB approach. Therefore, IVB was ultimately suggested for this patient.

Surgical procedure

We placed the intraventricular catheter into the third ventricle for long-term infusion of baclofen using a frame-based stereotactic approach adapted from the DBS surgery. One day prior to surgery, we acquired 3.0 Tesla high-resolution T1-weighted contrast-enhanced MR sequences for each patient except for case 2 as her DBS stimulator was only compatible with the 1.5 Tesla MR. On the day of surgery, a Leksell stereotactic frame was mounted under local anesthesia, followed by a head CT scan. The center of the third ventricle and catheter trajectory were then determined using the SurgiPlan system (Elekta AB, Sweden) after the coregistration of MR and CT images. The Specifically, a conventional frontal approach was used, and the trajectory was designed to pass from the lateral ventricle to the third ventricle through the Foramen of Monro.

The procedures for intraventricular catheter placement (model 8731SC, Medtronic, MN) was similar with those for frame-based DBS lead implantation, as the spinal segment of the catheter (outer diameter of 1.4 mm) is compatible with the insertion cannula kit (length = 190 / diameter = 1.6 mm) (Fig. 1B). The surgery was performed under general anesthesia by one senior neurosurgeon specialized in the field of functional neurosurgery (Dr. DYL). The patient was positioned supine with head stabilized by the Leksell coordinate frame G. A standard 14-mm diameter burr hole was made and the base ring of the Touch-Loc (model 1710–04, SceneRay, Suzhou, China) (Fig. 1A) was screwed to the skull. The insertion cannula was introduced following the planned trajectory, the stylet was removed, and the catheter was then inserted into the cannula with the help of the lead holder assembly (model 3354, Medtronic, MN) to reach the desired target. The Touch-Loc was originally designed for DBS lead fixation, and here we used it to secure the intraventricular catheter in place during the surgery. Briefly, the Touch-Loc can automatically clamp the catheter to avoid the migration when the insertion cannula is being removed (Fig. 1C). We confirmed the intraventricular catheter patency by verifying the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow through the caudal end after a temporary unlock of the Touch-Loc (Fig. 1D). The catheter would, therefore, easily be secured on the skull near the burr hole base ring by the V-wing anchor (Fig. 1E). The Touch-Loc was then removed, and the burr hole was covered by the burr hole cap (Fig. 1F). The catheter was then temporarily ligated, and the scalp incision was temporarily closed, followed by the removal of the frame and a second disinfection for the pump implantation. The intraventricular segment was connected to the pump segment at the retro-auricular area (Fig. 1G). A programmable pump (Model 8637, Medtronic, MN) was placed at the lateral lumbar area. An intraoperative X-ray control was not routinely acquired. Results

Patients were closely followed up for 6 months and regularly examined at each time of pump refilling. No death, infection, hemorrhage, intracranial catheter migration or fraction, or other severe adverse events were suspected or documented at the last follow-up. In terms of drug-related adverse events, no obtundation or profound systemic hypotonia was observed. Interestingly, all 3 patients reported mild nausea and/or vomit after initiating the infusion or increasing the infusion rate, possibly due to a transient irritation to the floor of the third ventricle by baclofen itself (increasing concentration) or by mechanical issue or both. These symptoms could be self-resolved within a few days. Regarding the dose titration, similar to ITB, the initial dose was set to twice the effective lumbar puncture trial dose. The dose titration was performed during postoperative hospitalization (approximately one week). The dose was further fine-tuned upon discharge. The desired dosage of IVB was reached approximately 3 months after surgery.

For case 1, the IVB infusion rate was 210 μg/day with 3 additional boluses of 10 μg delivered by the patient's programmer. The MAS scores decreased to 3 and 2 for the left upper and lower limbs, and to 1 and 0 for the right upper and lower limbs at the last follow-up (12 months). The CBFS score was not evaluated at that time. This patient was later referred to another expert center for the subsequent pump refilling and management due to the displacement of the patient.

For case 2, the IVB infusion rate was 132 μg/day with 3 additional boluses of 5 μg delivered by the patient's programmer. The MAS scores reduced to 1 and 3 for left and right upper limbs, and to 0–1 for lower limbs, respectively. The on-stimulation movement score and disability score of the BFMDRS reduced to 34 and 17, respectively.

For case 3, the infusion rate of IVB was gradually increased to 380.1 μg/day, including one bolus of 30 μg programmed at 4 am each day aiming at further reducing the muscle tone to facilitate the kinesitherapy in the morning. The bolus infusion rate was set at 60 μg/hour, which was much slower than the rate used for ITB, to avoid a sharp increase in intracranial pressure. The MAS score was reduced from 4 to 3 for both upper and lower limbs. The patient’s responsiveness to external stimuli was considerably improved at the same time.

Discussion

This study is the first to describe a stereotactic frame-based intraventricular baclofen catheter implantation technique and its application in 3 patients with severe spasticity or secondary dystonia.

Other techniques for delivering baclofen into the cerebral CSF space have been reported, most of which were targeting the third ventricle by endoscopy. Table 2 summarized the current literature reporting the clinical outcome of the IVB. Albright et al. were the first to report using an endoscope to catheterize the third ventricle for a continuous baclofen infusion [1]. Bollo et al. further used the neuronavigation system to guide the direction and trajectory of the introducer sheath of the endoscope [4]. Catheterization of the third ventricle may allow the infusion to disseminate more easily with CSF to the convexities and may avoid stagnation and local toxicity in lateral ventricles [15]. More recently, to bypass the cerebral aqueduct and to reduce the dilution of the baclofen infusion in the third and fourth ventricles, Ordaz et al. described the placement of a prepontine catheter using an endoscopic approach [11]. The authors suggested that, compared to intraventricular infusion, intracisternal infusion could theoretically reduce the required treatment dose by reducing the stagnant flow and dilution before reaching the cerebral convexity [11]. However, since this was a case report complicated with CSF leakage and catheter migration, this approach warranted further research with regard to its safety profile, and the optimal intracranial catheter location for baclofen infusion remains to be determined. The main advantage of catheterizing the cerebral CSF space endoscopically is the ability to directly visualize the final position of the catheter tip and to perform additional manipulations, such as ventriculostomy, when needed. Our frame-based stereotactic approach, on the other hand, may offer a less invasive alternative compared to the endoscopy-based approach for IVB. In terms of the safety issue, placement of the IVB catheter should be less technically demanding than that of the ITB catheter, particularly in patients who have had previous spinal fusion or who require laminotomy for ITB catheter placement. IVB has been shown to be a safe procedure in several case series studies [3, 4, 15] and one comparative study [12]. In the latter, the authors retrospectively compared the complication rate between IVB and ITB in dystonia. IVB seemed to be associated with fewer catheter or leak-related complications compared to ITB (2 in 30 IVB patients and 7 in 33 ITB patients) [12]. In our case series, specifically in case 2 and case 3, IVB was preferred rather than cervical ITB for the following considerations. Firstly, as previously stated, only the model 8739SC was available to our team. Its spinal segment was 38.1 cm in length so that to bring the catheter tip to the desired cervical level, the puncture level may be higher than usual, which may increase the risk of spinal cord injury. Secondly, ITB at the cervical level leads to longer lengths of the intrathecal catheters, which may increase the risk of catheter migration.

As the target of baclofen, the γ-aminobutyric acid type B (GABAB) receptor widely distributed in cerebral cortex, hippocampus, basal ganglia, thalamus, cerebellum, and brainstem [16].The current literature has indicated that baclofen may act at the intracranial level when treating the dystonia [14] while at the spinal level when treating the spasticity. If this is the case, IVB may theoretically require a lower infusion dose of baclofen to achieve a sufficient concentration of baclofen in cerebral convexities or subcortical regions when treating dystonia compared to ITB. However, a subsequent larger series showed instead that doses of IVB were slightly higher than ITB doses, probably due to the difference in the disease severity between the two groups [12]. Further controlled studies are needed to confirm this hypothesis.

The efficacy of ITB for lower limb spasticity has been well described in the existing literature, whereas its efficacy for upper limb spasticity is less certain [6]. This difference in efficacy might be related to the position of the intrathecal catheter tip and the gradient of the baclofen in the spinal CSF [6]. McCall et al. reported a greater improvement in upper limb spasticity with the cervical catheter tip compared to the thoracic catheter tip [9]. We speculated that the IVB may be a potential alternative to cervical ITB for the treatment of upper extremity spasticity, as in case 3, where the cervical intrathecal space is difficult to access. However, a higher dose may be required to achieve an adequate concentration at the spinal cord level for IVB than for cervical ITB [3].

Conclusion

The frame-based stereotactic IVB was feasible and technically safe. The safety and efficacy of this procedure for spasticity and dystonia warrant further evaluation in larger prospective studies.
