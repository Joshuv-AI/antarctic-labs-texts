Service users’ perspectives on primary eye care services following performance-based financing in Rwanda: an exploratory qualitative study
Authors: Thierry Claudien Uhawenimana, Stella Matutina Umuhoza, Jean Bosco Ndayambaje, Yvonne Delphine Nsaba Uwera, Tiva Kananura, Angeline Mumararungu, Mohamed Semakula, Henry Mwangi, Hassan Mugabo, Emmanuel Ntawuyirusha, Janvier Mungarulire, Theoneste Mutsindashyaka, Donatien Ntagara Ngabo, Caroline Ndolo, James Humuza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVisual impairment is a major global public health concern, disproportionately affecting populations in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where access to eye care services remains limited. In 2019, the Rwandan Ministry of Health integrated eye care services into the Performance-Based Financing (PBF) framework to improve accessibility, service quality, and utilization. However, service users’ perspectives on this initiative remain underexplored. This study aimed to examine service users’ perceptions of accessibility, quality, affordability, satisfaction, and challenges in accessing eye care services at primary healthcare facilities.MethodsAn exploratory qualitative study was conducted in six purposively selected health centers in Rwanda. A total of 72 participants were recruited using maximum variation sampling, including older adults, individuals with disabilities, and participants from different socioeconomic backgrounds. Data were collected through six focus group discussions (FGDs), each comprising 8–12 participants. Discussions explored perceptions of accessibility, quality, affordability, satisfaction, and recommendations for improvement. Audio recordings were transcribed, translated into English, and analyzed inductively using Dedoose software. Trustworthiness was ensured through prolonged engagement, member checking, peer debriefing, and triangulation.ResultsParticipants reported that the decentralization of eye care services to health centers improved geographical access and reduced travel time. However, financial barriers remained a major constraint, particularly due to the cost of eyeglasses and certain medications not fully covered by Community-Based Health Insurance (CBHI). Users also described inconsistencies in service quality, including frequent stock-outs of essential supplies, limited equipment, and variability in the availability of eye care personnel. Despite these challenges, many participants expressed satisfaction with provider attitudes and interpersonal care, highlighting respectful and supportive interactions with health workers. At the same time, dissatisfaction was reported due to systemic inefficiencies such as limited service days, understaffing, long waiting times, and referral-related burdens.ConclusionsFindings indicate that decentralization may have contributed to improved geographic accessibility and perceived service responsiveness; however, these improvements are uneven and remain constrained by financial and system-level barriers. Addressing these barriers through expanded service packages, reliable supply chains, human resource strengthening, CBHI coverage, and community education is critical to optimize equitable eye-care delivery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12939-026-02916-w.Keywords: Eye care services, Primary healthcare, Performance-based financing, Rwanda, Qualitative study

Introduction

Visual impairment constitutes a significant global public health concern. An estimated 2.2 billion people worldwide have some form of visual impairment or blindness, with at least 1 billion of these cases being preventable [1]. A substantial majority, around 90%, of those affected reside in LMICs, where access to eye care services is severely restricted [1, 2]. Many people still endure reduced vision due to a lack of specialized treatment for prevalent eye conditions, including refractive errors, cataracts, and diabetic retinopathy, which are recognized as the leading causes of visual impairment globally [1]. Visual impairment has profound effects on people’s quality of life as it hampers their essential daily activities such as mobility, cooking, eating, personal hygiene, and facial recognition [3–5]. Furthermore, adults experiencing visual impairment often exhibit lower levels of workplace participation and productivity, alongside elevated incidences of depression and anxiety [5].

Many individuals, families, and communities are presently enduring the consequences of inadequate access to quality and affordable eye care services, resulting in a prevalence of visual impairment or blindness [1]. Additionally, a significant portion of the population across the globe is experiencing visual impairment due to a lack of awareness regarding the advantages of seeking eye care before their conditions worsen, limited community outreach efforts from primary healthcare services, lack of eye care services within proximity, and high cost of eye services [6–8]. Furthermore, research had highlighted several barriers to accessing eye health services in LMICs, including financial constraints, a perceived lack of necessity, insufficient information about service locations, transportation challenges, and apprehension regarding potential negative outcomes [2, 9–11].

To mitigate barriers exacerbating visual impairment, some countries in LMICs have initiated strategies aimed improving the access to eye services at primary healthcare and community level targeting the most vulnerable people [12, 13]. A systematic review encompassing 48 studies across 13 LMICs highlighted the importance of sustaining school-based eye-care programs to mitigate ocular morbidity and developmental setbacks associated with childhood vision issues [12, 13]. Key factors contributing to the effective implementation of these interventions included fostering communication between health services and educational institutions, augmenting the availability of eye-care professionals in areas where they are lacking, and equipping teachers with the skills necessary for vision screening, thereby ensuring the delivery of high-quality and cost-effective services [12]. Although initiatives to enhance eye care services are in progress, not all segments of the affected population have been reached. Consequently, there is a pressing need to implement interventions for all individuals at risk of visual impairment. This will improve access to and utilization of eye care services, as well as facilitate the integration of eye care into low-resource health systems [14, 15]. Such efforts should focus on addressing resource constraints, managing competing priorities, promoting people-centered eye health services, implementing innovations fostering staff motivation, and increasing the community’s awareness about eye health services [14–16].

To bolster eye health services at primary healthcare level in Rwanda, in 2019, the Ministry of Health (MoH) implemented the integration of eye care services into the PBF scheme eye [17]. This initiative was introduced in Rwanda to improve the financial resources of health institutions and to enhance staff remuneration based on their performance metrics [17]. The Fred Hollows Foundation has established specific indicators that emphasize eye health consultations, follow-up care, referrals, the detection of operable cataracts, cataract surgeries, mentorship programs, and the identification of cases of diabetic retinopathy.

In Rwanda, efforts to strengthen primary eye-care services have intensified following the integration of eye care into the PBF framework in 2019. This reform aimed to improve service availability, provider performance, and utilization at primary healthcare level. However, while evidence exists on service outputs and system performance, there is limited understanding of how these changes are experienced by service users, particularly in terms of accessibility, affordability, and quality of care. Moreover, little is known about how financial protection mechanisms, such as CBHI, interact with PBF to influence equitable access to eye-care services. In addition, evidence on users’ awareness of available services and eye health conditions in Rwanda remains scarce. This study addresses these gaps by exploring service users’ perspectives on access, quality, affordability, satisfaction, and challenges in primary eye-care delivery following PBF implementation. By focusing on lived experiences, the study contributes to a more nuanced understanding of how health system reforms translate into real-world access and equity outcomes.

This study was guided by a health systems perspective focusing on three interrelated domains: access to care, quality of services, and financial protection [18]. Within this framework, PBF is conceptualized as a supply-side intervention expected to improve service availability and responsiveness, while financial protection mechanisms such as CBHI influence affordability and equity.

Methods

Study design

The findings outlined in this study originated from a broader initiative entitled “Integrating Eye Health into Rwanda PBF”. The first part of the project concentrated on the accessibility and use of eye care services following the implementation of eye care PBF, as well as its financial viability from the perspectives of eye health policymakers, primary level eye healthcare providers, and significant stakeholders in the eye care sector and eye care PBF financing [17].

The present study used an exploratory qualitative design. This approach was deemed suitable for the study, particularly because the topics of service users’ satisfaction with eye care services, their access to and utilization of these services, as well as the challenges impacting eye care accessibility, have not been extensively examined by researchers in the Rwandan context. To elucidate these concepts, the study gathered insights from service users through focus group discussions involving a diverse range of community members, aimed at capturing their perspectives on eye care delivery within primary healthcare settings in rural Rwanda.

Study setting

Rwanda has approximately 520 health centers(—all operating as public health facilities) distributed across its four provinces and Kigali City, with the majority of health centers located in rural areas. Health centers in Rwanda serve as the first point of contact within the health system, delivering primary care services including newly integrated eye-care services under the PBF framework. These facilities typically serve catchment populations ranging from 10,000 to 50,000 people.

Eye-care services at the primary healthcare level in Rwanda have been progressively integrated into health centers, particularly following their inclusion in the PBF framework in 2019. Under the PBF model, facilities receive financial incentives based on performance indicators such as number of consultations, referrals, and follow-ups. However, the availability of essential inputs such as equipment, trained personnel, and optical supplies remains variable across facilities, which may influence service delivery outcomes.

While most health centers now provide basic eye-care services, the scope and consistency of service delivery vary depending on available resources. Typically, eye-care services at this level are delivered by general nurses who have received short-term training in primary eye care, rather than specialized ophthalmic personnel. The range of eye care services provided at health centers includes basic eye examinations (visual acuity testing), diagnosis and management of common eye conditions (such as conjunctivitis and refractive errors), provision of reading glasses where available, and referral of complicated cases (e.g., cataracts, glaucoma, or diabetic retinopathy) to district or tertiary hospitals. However, advanced diagnostic and surgical services are not available at this level.

Financing of eye-care services at primary healthcare facilities is primarily through Rwanda’s CBHI scheme, which covers the majority of the population. Under CBHI, consultations and some medications are subsidized through cost-sharing mechanisms, with patients contributing a small co-payment depending on their socioeconomic category (Ubudehe classification). However, certain services and products—particularly eyeglasses and some medications—are not fully covered, resulting in out-of-pocket payments for users. In addition, the integration of eye care into the PBF system provides financial incentives to health facilities based on performance indicators, such as the number of consultations, referrals, and follow-ups. While this mechanism aims to improve service availability and utilization, it does not directly address affordability barriers faced by patients.

Rwanda classifies households into four socio-economic categories, which are used to determine eligibility for social protection programs, including health insurance subsidies.

This classification is critical for understanding financial access to care, as it determines both insurance premiums and out-of-pocket payments for services not covered by CBHI. Misclassification into higher categories may increase financial burden and reduce affordability of services, particularly for eyeglasses and medications not covered by CBHI.

In practice, these categories determine the level of financial contribution required under the CBHI scheme. Individuals in Category 1 are fully subsidized and pay no premiums, while those in Categories 2–4 contribute increasing annual premiums and co-payments for services. As a result, individuals in higher categories face greater out-of-pocket costs, particularly for services not fully covered by CBHI, such as eyeglasses and certain medications. Misclassification of poorer households into higher categories may therefore increase their financial burden and limit their ability to access recommended eye-care services.

The study took place at six health centers, each situated in distinct administrative provinces of Rwanda: one in the Southern Province of Rwanda, two in the Northern Province, two in the Eastern Province, and one in the Western Province of Rwanda. The six health centers included in this study therefore represent a small but geographically diverse subset of primary care facilities, capturing variation across rural and peri-urban contexts. These health centers were selected purposively considering rural-urban and characteristics of the population served as variations guiding the selection. For the latter, the demographic served by each health center was analyzed to ensure equity in the recruitment of participants, with particular attention given to vulnerable groups, including people with disabilities, the elderly, and the population with limited income experiencing ocular problems.

Study population

The study population entailed clients whom we met at the study sites seeking eye care services at selected health centers and those who had sought eye care within the preceding six months at designated health centers. To qualify for inclusion in the study, individuals were required to meet specific eligibility criteria, which included the following:

Males aged 60 years and older;

Males aged 60 years and older;

Females aged 60 years and older;

Females aged 60 years and older;

Individuals with any form of disability, whether physical or mental;

Individuals with any form of disability, whether physical or mental;

Persons classified within the lower socioeconomic strata;

Persons classified within the lower socioeconomic strata;

Individuals aged between 18 and 59 years without any known disabilities within the middle and high income social economic strata.

Individuals aged between 18 and 59 years without any known disabilities within the middle and high income social economic strata.

Residents of Kigali City were excluded from this study. Residents of Kigali City were excluded because urban populations have comparatively better access to specialized eye-care services because of a high concentration of private eye care clinics. Compared to the rural areas, residents in urban areas may have different health-seeking behaviors, which could confound the study’s focus on primary care access in rural and peri-urban settings. Furthermore, including urban populations could have biased findings toward better service availability and affordability due to higher concentration of private providers and alternative care options. Their exclusion allowed the study to focus more accurately on barriers within the public primary healthcare system in rural and peri-urban settings. Individuals from the selected regions who had sought services at the designated health centers more than six months prior to the commencement of the study were excluded to minimize recall bias and ensure accuracy of reported experiences.

Sample, sampling approach and recruitment process

A maximum variation sampling strategy was employed to recruit participants for the study, considering these factors: age, income, educational attainment, gender, and prior eye health issues a participant have had before. This approach aimed to ensure equitable representation and to gather comprehensive insights into how different community groups perceived the services they received, as well as the factors that affected their access to and use of eye care services. Additionally, the study examined how participants’ community status influenced their engagement with these services. The identification of potential participants was conducted in partnership with primary health facilities and Community Health Workers (CHWs). Eligibility criteria were presented to the manager of the health centers who requested community health workers affiliated to their facilities to recruit participants who met the inclusion criteria presented to them. The research team arranged with the community health workers on the day to meet potential participants to inform them about the study and further arrange the day for holding the focus group discussions with selected people.

Characteristics of the FGDs conducted

The number of FGDs was predetermined based on the study design, which aimed to capture perspectives across key population subgroups (age, gender, disability status, and socioeconomic categories). The research team considered that these groups would provide sufficient diversity to generate meaningful insights into the study topic.

A comprehensive topic guide featuring questions that address various perceptions related to access to eye care, quality of care, waiting times, benefits, access challenges, satisfaction with eye care services, associated costs, and recommendations for enhancement was utilized to facilitate the FGDs (see Supporting information 1).

Before the commencement of data collection, both the data collectors and the supervisor underwent training on the topic guide, qualitative data collection techniques, and ethical conduct of research involving vulnerable people. The data collection process involved conducting focus group discussions with 8 to 12 participants each. The FGDs were primarily conducted in Kinyarwanda, with each session lasting approximately 45 min to 1.5 h. Discussions were recorded using audio devices, and field notes were taken to complement the audio recordings. Data collection took place in a private room at the health centers, free from distractions, to facilitate participants in expressing their thoughts without any barriers. During data collection and preliminary analysis, the team continuously reviewed emerging findings across FGDs. By the final discussions, no substantially new themes were identified, suggesting that thematic sufficiency had been achieved.

Data analysis

Kinyarwanda audio recordings were transcribed and subsequently translated into English. To ensure the integrity of the translation process, a back-translation was conducted by a language expert external to the research team, verifying that the translated texts corresponded accurately to the original Kinyarwanda versions. Following this, a meticulous reading and re-reading of the English transcripts was performed to guarantee a thorough comprehension of the content. Once familiarized with the data, the subsequent phases involved the development of codes and sub-codes through open coding, targeting specific lines or segments to identify emerging similar patterns which were grouped together to generate themes from the data. The analysts verified the relevance of the generated codes and reached a consensus on their application. Related concepts were clustered together, and recurring coded segments were compiled to monitor their frequency. This comprehensive process utilized Dedoose version 8.3.45, an online cross-platform software designed for qualitative analysis and mixed methods research. The following stage involved the examination and aggregation of the generated codes for each pattern group, aiming to extract themes and significant interpretations through an inductive content analysis approach. Although the analysis was primarily inductive, it was informed by sensitizing concepts related to access, quality, and affordability, which guided interpretation of emergent themes.

Measures undertaken to ensure trustworthiness

Measures implemented to enhance the trustworthiness of this research encompassed credibility, confirmability, and transferability. Credibility was established through prolonged engagement with participants, a thorough articulation of the methodological procedures that directed the study, and meticulous documentation of data throughout the stages of collection, analysis, and reporting. Additional measures to bolster credibility included peer debriefing, member checking, and triangulation of data analysis process whereby two people were involved in the analysis and counter-verification of theme determination was performed as a group. Confirmability was ensured through member checking, a process that allowed participants to review and affirm the accuracy of the records generated during discussions. Furthermore, the research team actively participated in all phases of the study to reach a consensus on the research design and analytical outcomes, thereby reducing potential biases and enhancing rigor. Transferability was facilitated by selecting a representative sample with diverse groups of the people across Rwanda. The extended engagement with participants through health education on eye care prior to the focus group discussions cultivated a robust rapport, leading to a deeper and more nuanced understanding of the discussion topics. This connection empowered participants to share their experiences regarding eye care services and propose improvements. Additionally, the methodologies employed and the criteria for participant selection play a crucial role in ensuring the applicability or transferability of the findings to analogous populations or contexts beyond the scope of the study.

Reflexivity

The research team comprised individuals affiliated with academic institutions and organizations involved in health system strengthening. While this provided contextual understanding, it may also have influenced interpretation of findings. The involvement of community health workers in recruitment and pre-FGD health education may have introduced response bias, as participants could perceive them as representatives of the health system. To mitigate this, discussions were facilitated by independent researchers, confidentiality was emphasized, and participants were encouraged to share both positive and negative experiences.

Some members of the research team were affiliated with institutions involved in health system strengthening and eye-care programs, which may have shaped assumptions about the benefits of PBF. To minimize this influence, data analysis was conducted collaboratively with researchers not directly involved in program implementation, and divergent interpretations were actively discussed.

Ethical considerations

The current study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration for the protection of human subjects in research [19]. This research received approval from the Institutional Review Board, University of Rwanda, College of Medicine and Health Sciences (Approval Notice: No 119/CMHS IRB/2025). The research team secured an introductory letter addressed to health centers and grassroots leaders in order to access chosen participants. Participants were requested to give their voluntary informed consent by either signing or providing a fingerprint for those who were unable to read the consent form, thereby allowing the research team to document their participation. Before the consent was signed, participants were thoroughly briefed about the study via the Participant Information Sheet. This document detailed the study’s objectives, the expected contributions from participants during the discussions, the voluntary nature of their participation, and their rights to withdraw from the study at any time without facing any consequences. Participants were assured that their input would not jeopardize their access to quality services at the facilities.

Results

Characteristics of study participants

A total of 72 participants were involved in this study. They were sampled and recruited to participate in six focus group discussions (FGDs) with 12 participants each. The participants’ ages ranged from 36 to over 60 years. The sample included equal representation of males and females. Participants were mostly from category 2 income (50%), followed by category 3 (33%) and category 1 (17%). Special groups, including people with disabilities, were also included to ensure diverse perspectives (n = 12, 17%).

The FGDs were stratified to capture variations in age, gender, income, and education level. Two FGDs were composed of older adults above 60 years (male and female groups, n = 12 each), one FGD included people with disabilities aged 40–60 years, and the remaining three FGDs represented participants from different income strata (low and middle/high) and mixed education levels. This purposive sampling strategy ensured representation across key sociodemographic characteristics relevant to eye care access and utilization.

Themes generated from the analysis

Analysis of participant FGDs generated five key themes, focusing on accessibility and affordability of eye care services, quality of available services, user satisfaction with eye care, and recommendations for improving eye care at primary healthcare facilities.

Theme 1: Improved physical access but constrained financial access

All participants highlighted that the decentralization of eye care services to local health centers had improved access by reducing travel time. Participants reported that improved proximity to the eye care services enabled them to seek care earlier.

Before those services were at Kabgayi hospital [about 4 hours drive] but now you can find them at Gahini hospital, they helped us Participant P5, FGD Eastern Province.

Despite this progress, financial barriers remained a critical limitation. Most of participants reported that the cost of eyeglasses and medications, which were often not covered by CBHI, was high.

The problem I saw on my side is that glasses are expensive because 5 thousand Rwandan Francs is a lot for me. Participant P3, FGD Northern Province.

Socioeconomic inequities further constrained access. Participants in lower income category were more likely to report inability to afford eyeglasses and medications, while those misclassified into higher categories described increased financial strain due to higher required contributions. This misclassification resulted in higher required insurance contributions and out-of-pocket payments, which participants reported as a barrier to accessing eyeglasses and medications.

While proximity has improved access, affordability and socioeconomic barriers continue to hinder equitable utilization of eye care services.

It is attributed to the fact that we are poor, and they put us in the socio-demographic category 3 and no other option Participant P4, FGD Southern Province.

Theme 2: System fragility and inconsistent service reliability

Several participants described challenges related to the availability and quality of essential resources. Frequent stock-outs of eyeglasses and essential medications were reported, and the quality of some items was perceived as inadequate. These experiences point to systemic fragility in supply chains, suggesting that while PBF incentivized service delivery, it may not sufficiently address upstream resource constraints necessary for consistent care provision. One participant described their experience:

Sometimes you come with reading problem expecting eyeglasses and they tell you that eyeglasses have finished. Participant P7, FGD Eastern Province.

Others noted the fragility of glasses provided by health centers.

Those glasses provided by Health center are very soft, sometimes when you take them, by the time you reach your home, they are already broke. Participant P8, FGD Northern Province.

Insufficient infrastructure and lack of specialized equipment also limited service quality, compelling patients to travel to distant hospitals for care. This referral process was often time-consuming and costly.

They gave me transfer to Bishenyi Hospital; it is now 2 years with these eyes’ problems. Participant P9, FGD Eastern Province.

Theme 3: Experience of care (positive interactions vs. systemic frustrations)

Most respondents expressed satisfaction with the services received in primary healthcare settings. Participants reported being welcomed warmly, receiving timely consultations, and obtaining necessary medications without prolonged waiting. They appreciated the professionalism and responsiveness of nurses and clinicians in eye-care units. Many reported respectful and compassionate treatment, which enhanced their satisfaction.

Uhhh, I always got the same reaction. I came here from home 4 times. From the first time to today, the service I received was satisfactory, according to their reception. When I got here and took the tests, I met the doctor. Send me somewhere else where they would help me without telling me to go back or maybe to come back another day. Participant P4, FGD Western Province

I have experienced the good healthcare service here; I have been consulted at the health center, and they transferred me to the hospital. Then when I got there, they told me that I must be operated. They operated this one at Ruhengeri hospital. I have experienced good service, no problem faced. Participant P 02, FGD Northern Province

Participants valued being guided through each stage of care—from registration to consultation and treatment. However, a minority noted inconsistencies in service quality across facilities, suggesting that satisfaction was not uniform across all sites.

A dominant source of dissatisfaction concerned financial barriers linked to the limited coverage of the CBHI scheme. Many respondents noted that while consultations were covered, they often had to pay out of pocket for eyeglasses, certain eye medications such as chromozol and tetracycline which were not covered by the CBHI) scheme, or surgical follow-ups. Participants expressed that these expenses placed a heavy burden on households with low incomes.

You can find that they give you a prescription, but the medicine is not covered; you buy it yourself. Sometimes the glasses are expensive, and CBHI doesn’t help with that. Participant P03, FGD Northern Province

Such financial constraints were compounded when referrals were required to district or tertiary hospitals. Travel, accommodation, and food costs further limited follow-through with recommended care, particularly for elderly or low-income patients.

I faced the challenge of going to Kigali, transport fees is an issue. We went there and give us number for being treated that day; if your number is not reached and don’t get treatment that day, they ask you to spend night there without knowing where to stay. Participant P01, FGD Northern Province

Although many respondents expressed general satisfaction with primary eye-care delivery, a considerable proportion also described experiences of dissatisfaction that reflected systemic, financial, and organizational shortcomings. Participants’ accounts revealed that the quality of eye-care services was uneven across facilities, with some users feeling inadequately attended to, facing long waits, or being unable to obtain necessary medications and eyeglasses.

Some participants reported being sent between departments without assistance or being asked to return on another day without receiving definitive care. This created frustration and eroded trust in the health-care system’s capacity to manage eye conditions at the primary-care level.

The challenges exist…., there are many services here, when you come for the first time you are confused, sometimes they tell you that you should go there, other times you should go there, and you find that you are sitting here. Maybe they should find us people who orient and tell you that if you are sick, if you haven’t used it, go there, maybe there is someone who does it because we sometimes come early in the morning, but because you are not familiar with the place, you find that you should get treatment around four or five o’clock…. Participant P04, FGD Western Province

When I came here for the first time, I had to wait the health provider, the problem is that all nurses don’t provide that service. Participant P12 Northern Province.

Others described situations where services were available only on specific days, making it difficult to obtain timely treatment when symptoms occurred outside those schedules. Participants perceived these inconsistencies as a sign of weak organization and under-resourced staffing.

The challenges we have is that she doesn’t work in the service every day, they have set days to provide eye services, but you can understand that if you have the problem with eye during the time she is not around, it can be a problem. Participant P02, FGD Eastern Province

Some participants also expressed dissatisfaction with waiting times and limited availability of trained eye-care personnel. Most of health centers had only one nurse assigned to eye-care duties, who alternated between consultation and dispensing roles, causing delays.

The service takes time, because of a low number of health providers, you come here in the morning and wait the whole day waiting for medication, the nurse is in consultation room, and she goes later to where they give medication, and that makes you delaying. Participant P01, FGD Eastern Province

Theme 4: Service users’ recommendations for improving eye-care delivery

Participants provided several recommendations to strengthen eye-care services in Rwanda. They called for expanding the eye-care package at health centers and district hospitals to include a wider range of treatments, medications, and optical services. Affordability and availability of eyeglasses were emphasized as critical needs. Respondents also advocated for dedicated eye-care rooms, improved equipment, and more trained personnel to reduce waiting times and ensure service continuity.

They should find a room for eye care services, because they change rooms, today they can work from here, tomorrow they go there, they should find a building specific to eye care, like where they treat HIV or laboratory services. Participant P03, FGD Southern Province

The service takes time, because of a low number of health providers, you come here in the morning and wait the whole day waiting for medication, the nurse is in consultation room, and she goes later to where they give medication, and that makes you delaying. Participant P01, Northern Province

Participants further suggested that eye-care medicines should be included under CBHI and that awareness campaigns be intensified to reach underserved groups. Strengthening community education and orientation systems within facilities was perceived as key to improving user experience and uptake of services.

In summary, across themes, three underlying mechanisms emerged:

(1) Financial vulnerability, driven by gaps in insurance coverage and socioeconomic misclassification;

(2) System reliability constraints, linked to inconsistent availability of staff, equipment, and supplies; and.

(3) Navigation challenges, reflecting difficulties in understanding and moving through decentralized services.

These mechanisms shaped how users experienced access, quality, and equity in eye-care services.

Discussion

This study found that decentralization of eye-care services to local health centers appears to have improved geographic accessibility by reducing travel distance and time. Service users reported being able to seek timely care closer to their homes, which may encourage early detection and management of vision problems. However, these gains did not translate into equitable utilization due to persistent financial barriers. This reflects a structural misalignment between supply-side incentives and demand-side financial protection mechanisms, where increased service availability does not translate into effective utilization due to persistent out-of-pocket costs.

In this context, while facilities are motivated to deliver more services, patients remain responsible for costs associated with eyeglasses, medications, and referrals many of which fall outside the coverage of the CBHI scheme. As a result, the study suggests that improved physical access may not necessarily translate into effective access, particularly for lower-income groups. Misclassification within socioeconomic categories further exacerbates this issue by increasing required contributions for financially vulnerable households. Furthermore, the exclusion of key services such as eyeglasses from CBHI coverage may undermine the equity goals of decentralized care. Similar challenges are reported in LMICs. A scoping review by Cardona et al. (2025) highlighted that women, rural residents, and older individuals in LMICs face significant access inequities due to financial constraints and limited availability of services [16]. Additionally, Marmamula et al. (2023) noted that high costs and inadequate infrastructure hinder equitable access to eye care in LMICs [15]. These findings underscore the necessity for policies that integrate eye care into primary health services, reduce out-of-pocket expenses, and ensure equitable distribution of resources to underserved populations.

This study found that the availability and quality of eye-care resources were inconsistent across facilities. Frequent stock-outs of eyeglasses and essential medications, as well as concerns about the durability of provided glasses, were commonly reported. Limited infrastructure and lack of specialized equipment often necessitated referrals to higher-level hospitals, imposing additional travel, time, and financial burdens on patients. These findings point to system-level constraints that are not fully addressed by PBF mechanisms. These challenges are consistent with findings from other LMICs. A scoping review by Lee et al. (2023) identified resource limitations, including shortages of trained personnel and diagnostic equipment, as significant barriers to quality eye care in LMICs [14]. These findings indicate that gaps in supply chains and resource allocation may compromise the quality and continuity of care, potentially leading to delays in treatment and suboptimal visual outcomes. In addition, this highlights the need for investments in infrastructure, training of healthcare providers, and development of sustainable eye care programs to improve service quality in LMICs.

This study found that, despite systemic challenges, most participants expressed satisfaction with primary health care eye services. Positive patient-provider interactions appear to be an important facilitator of service satisfaction and may help maintain trust in the health system even when structural challenges exist. One possible explanation is that PBF schemes often include performance indicators related to service delivery processes, which may indirectly encourage providers to improve patient interactions and responsiveness. This finding further suggests that relational dimensions of care may compensate for structural deficiencies, helping to sustain trust in the health system even in resource-constrained settings. This finding corroborates the studies that were reported elsewhere whereby patients were mostly satisfied with the interpersonal manner of the doctors [20, 21].

Nevertheless, financial and organizational barriers emerged as key sources of dissatisfaction, particularly related to limited CBHI coverage, staffing shortages, and inconsistent service availability. These challenges reflect structural gaps in the alignment between financing mechanisms and service delivery realities. While CBHI provides important financial protection for basic services, its limited coverage of essential eye-care components; such as eyeglasses and certain medications, creates gaps in the continuum of care. As a result, patients may initiate care within the system but fail to complete treatment due to out-of-pocket costs.

Organizational constraints, including limited staffing and restricted service days, further compound these challenges by reducing system responsiveness. These issues suggest that PBF may improve service outputs without adequately addressing workforce capacity and service organization, leading to inefficiencies and user frustration. Together, these findings highlight that improving access requires not only increasing service availability but also ensuring continuity, affordability, and system coordination.,

Participants’ recommendations provide important insights into user priorities and highlight gaps between current service provision and community needs. Their emphasis on affordability, expanded service packages, and improved availability of eyeglasses reflects a strong demand for comprehensive and financially accessible care, rather than fragmented service delivery. Notably, many of these recommendations point toward areas that fall outside the direct scope of PBF incentives, such as insurance coverage and infrastructure investment. The alignment between user-identified priorities and observed system gaps suggests that incorporating community perspectives into policy design could enhance the responsiveness and effectiveness of eye-care programs. These findings support a shift toward more people-centered health system approaches, where financing, service delivery, and community needs are more closely aligned.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although maximum variation sampling was employed, the study was limited to six health centers and may not capture the full diversity of service delivery contexts across Rwanda. Second, the study relied on self-reported experiences, which may be influenced by recall bias, especially for participants who accessed services up to six months prior. Third, the study did not quantitatively assess financial burden, limiting the ability to estimate the magnitude of affordability constraints. Finally, as a qualitative study, findings are not statistically generalizable but aim to provide analytical insights transferable to similar contexts.

Conclusion

This study provides qualitative insights into how service users experience primary eye-care delivery following integration into the PBF framework in Rwanda. While decentralization appears to have improved geographic access, persistent financial and system-level barriers continue to limit equitable utilization. These findings highlight the importance of aligning service delivery reforms with financial protection mechanisms and system capacity to ensure that improvements in availability translate into meaningful access for all population groups. Therefore, expanding the eye-care package, strengthening human resources and infrastructure, improving affordability through CBHI coverage, ensuring reliable supply chains, enhancing referral systems, and promoting community awareness and patient involvement are critical steps to optimize eye-care delivery.

Further research

Future research should focus on economic evaluation of eye-care services, including determining direct and indirect costs associated with referral for advanced eye care. Such evidence could inform strategies to reduce financial barriers, prioritize resource allocation, and enhance equity in access to eye care. Investigating in the routine studies on the of PBF eye-care initiatives on the long-term treatment outcome would ensure a continuous improvement in the eye care sector.
