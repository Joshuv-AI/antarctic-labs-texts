Implementation of thermoelectric wall systems for sustainable indoor environment regulation in buildings through numerical and experimental performance analysis
Authors: Reza Roohi, Mohammad Javad Amiri, Masoud Akbari
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith buildings representing a substantial portion of global energy consumption, exploring alternatives to traditional fossil fuel-based heating and cooling systems is critical. Thermoelectricity offers a promising solution by converting temperature differentials into electrical voltage or vice versa, enabling efficient indoor thermal regulation. This paper presents a comprehensive investigation into the integration of thermoelectric wall systems for sustainable building climate control through numerical simulations and experimental analyses. Numerical simulations using computational fluid dynamics (CFD) techniques were conducted to model fluid flow and heat transfer within the thermoelectric wall systems under various operating conditions. These simulations provided insights into the system’s thermal behavior, which were validated through experimental setups designed to measure temperature differentials, airflow rates, and power consumption. The results showed that power consumption is directly correlated with electrical current, ranging from 0.19 W to 77.4 W as the current increased from 0.1 A to 2 A. Additionally, the heat absorbed by the system increased significantly with electrical current, by 706–1044%, depending on the air velocity. The thermal energy released from the hot side of the thermoelectric modules also rose substantially, ranging from 9850 to 5285% with increasing electrical current, and from 275 to 51% with higher air velocities. Moreover, increasing air velocity led to a 6.78–9.37% reduction in power consumption for currents between 0.1 A and 2 A. The coefficient of performance (COP) analysis revealed that optimizing both electrical current and air velocity is essential for maximizing system efficiency. While fan power consumption reduces COP at higher air velocities, neglecting fan power consumption results in COP improvements ranging from 6.5 × 10⁻⁴ to 49.0%. These findings highlight the potential of thermoelectric wall systems to enhance indoor comfort and energy efficiency in buildings.Keywords: Thermoelectricity, Sustainable buildings, CFD simulation, Experimental analysis, Thermal wallSubject terms: Thermoelectric devices and materials, Energy science and technology

Introduction

Buildings play a crucial role in energy consumption, constituting 40% of the overall energy demand. This underscores the urgency of exploring alternatives to fossil fuels for building energy usage1–3. One particularly promising avenue for enhancing indoor thermal comfort in buildings is through the utilization of thermoelectricity. By converting temperature differentials into electrical voltage or vice versa, thermoelectricity presents a viable solution. This phenomenon can be leveraged through two primary methods: the Seebeck effect, which is currently employed across various industries to generate electricity from waste heat, and the Peltier effect, predominantly utilized for cooling small electronic devices4.

Despite its potential, the application of thermoelectricity in buildings remains a nascent field. Nevertheless, it offers numerous advantages. Thermoelectric modules boast extended operational lifespans, require minimal upkeep, and exhibit versatility for both heating and cooling applications. Furthermore, these modules can be seamlessly integrated with renewable energy sources like photovoltaic cells. Implementing thermoelectric modules in buildings could pave the way for decentralized systems, significantly reducing the footprint occupied by traditional HVAC systems and offering more efficient and sustainable solutions for indoor climate control5.

The efficacy of thermoelectric modules has been demonstrated across various domains, including cooling of electronic devices6, water heating and cooling7, and the development of portable solar thermoelectric refrigerators8. Additionally, research has delved into integrating thermoelectric modules as renewable energy sources, indicating their potential for fostering sustainable building operations9. Moreover, studies have shown that employing thermoelectric modules in heating and cooling systems addresses intricate thermal management challenges, particularly in scenarios requiring regulation of low to moderate heat levels10.

Furthermore, investigations into the role of thermoelectric modules in zero-energy buildings, particularly in the realm of solar thermoelectric cooling technologies11,12, underscore their significance in achieving energy-efficient cooling solutions. The multifaceted capabilities of thermoelectric modules in building environments have been showcased through integrated cooling, heating, and power generation systems13.

The versatility of thermoelectric modules extends to applications such as cold-hot water dispensers14 and photovoltaic-driven building-integrated thermoelectric cooling devices15. Additionally, studies examining thermoelectric ceiling cooling panel systems emphasize their diverse applications in air conditioning and ventilation systems1. Furthermore, research on employing solar thermoelectric generators for open pond cultivation with heat pipe cooling highlights their potential contributions to sustainable agricultural practices2.

Moreover, it is important to highlight the specific areas of loss within the building envelope. Our findings indicate that significant energy loss occurred primarily through the walls and roof, which are typically the most vulnerable elements in terms of thermal inefficiency. To address these losses, various strategies can be employed to enhance the thermal resistance of building elements. For instance, adding insulation materials such as aerogels or sustainable materials like bamboo fibre-reinforced briquettes can significantly reduce heat transfer, leading to improved energy efficiency. Previous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of such materials, including the use of bamboo fiber briquettes with enhanced thermal resistance16, aerogel-based insulation for internal applications in low/zero carbon buildings17, and novel insulation plasters for external wall retrofitting18. These solutions not only improve insulation performance but also contribute to the overall sustainability of the building by reducing energy consumption and carbon emissions.

The exploration of thermoelectric modules’ potential to improve the thermal comfort of building occupants has been a subject of academic inquiry. For instance, Martinez et al.3 delved into the application of thermoelectric self-cooling for power electronics, while Dehra19 investigated the development of building-integrated thermoelectric cooling-photovoltaic devices, highlighting their role in indoor cooling and space heating within building environments.

Gillott et al.20 conducted a study focused on utilizing a compact thermoelectric (TE) module to provide comfort cooling for small-scale buildings. Through theoretical analysis and experimental investigations, they aimed to ascertain the optimal input current for maximizing cooling capacity and coefficient of performance (COP). Similarly, researchers from Hunan University in China extensively studied the application of thermoelectricity in buildings. They introduced the Active Solar Thermoelectric Radiant Wall (ASTRW) system, integrating TE radiant cooling with photovoltaic (PV) technologies to regulate heat flux through the building envelope and offer cooling capacity. Furthermore, they examined the system’s performance under winter conditions, where the manipulation of louvers affected interior surface temperatures, ultimately developing a model to enhance understanding.

In another endeavor, researchers proposed the Solar Thermoelectric Air Conditioner with Hot Water Supply (STACHWS) system to reduce indoor cooling and heating loads while providing hot water11,12. Through experimentation, they determined that the system operated more efficiently with lower water inlet temperatures in cooling mode and higher temperatures in heating mode, with performance strongly influenced by water temperature.

Several studies aimed to develop dynamic models for thermoelectric systems. One study focused on a dynamic heat transfer model for a Thermoelectric Radiant Panel (TERP), facilitating the calculation of average surface temperature21. Another proposed a non-uniform time step model for a Building Integrated Photovoltaic Thermoelectric (BIPVTE) wall system, demonstrating lower cooling and heating loads compared to a simple PV-integrated wall22. In a recent study, Diaz de Garayo et al.23 propose an innovative HVAC system specifically designed for 80–100 m2 passive houses. This system combines a thermoelectric air-to-air heat pump with a heat recovery unit. Notably, integrating the heat recovery unit significantly improves the heat pump’s coefficient of performance (COP). During partial load operation, the COP increases by 2–3 times, and at maximum load, it rises by 12.5%. Remarkably, the optimal number of thermoelectric modules required remains consistent at approximately 15, regardless of the climate. This 15-module thermoelectric heat pump effectively provides both heating (up to 2500 W) and cooling (up to 405 W) for passive house dwellings. To further enhance cooling capacity, the study suggests focusing on improving heat exchangers rather than increasing the module count. Overall, this work introduces an efficient approach to HVAC systems for passive houses.

In another review study, Christopher et al.24 assessed the role of Renewable Energy Systems (RES) in improving energy efficiency within the building sector, which accounts for 40% of global greenhouse gas emissions. It finds that integrating various RES can effectively convert low-energy-efficient buildings into high-efficiency structures, thus achieving Net Zero Energy Building (NZEB) status. The study evaluates performance metrics such as payback durations and CO2 emissions reduction, discusses technology limitations, and proposes innovative strategies for future research, ultimately guiding the selection of optimal RES combinations based on specific conditions to facilitate deep decarbonization in buildings.

The present study integrates numerical simulations with experimental tests to evaluate thermoelectric module performance in building thermal management. It begins by reviewing pertinent literature to inform the research context and identify gaps. Governing equations are implemented for 3D, steady-state turbulent flow scenarios. Simulation of thermoelectric behavior accounts for material properties and manufacturer-provided parameters. Assumptions dictate boundary conditions and system specifications. Computational accuracy is ensured through grid generation and mesh refinement strategies. The experimental setup entails detailed physical device descriptions and instrumentation. Numerical techniques like finite volume discretization and solution algorithms are applied, with grid refinement methods. Results encompass analysis of velocity, temperature, heat flux profiles, and power consumption. Discrepancies between numerical and experimental data are examined, with discussions on influencing factors. The study concludes by summarizing findings and proposing future research directions in sustainable building technologies.

While previous studies have demonstrated the potential of thermoelectric modules for various applications such as cooling, heating, and renewable energy integration, the direct implementation of thermoelectric systems for building climate control remains underexplored, particularly in the context of sustainable architecture. This study aims to bridge this gap by investigating the performance of thermoelectric wall systems, both numerically and experimentally, to assess their viability for enhancing indoor thermal comfort and minimizing energy consumption. Unlike prior research, which primarily focuses on small-scale or specialized applications, our work focuses on the practical integration of thermoelectric systems within building envelopes, specifically targeting the optimization of energy transfer and reduction of power demand through careful control of air velocity and electrical currents.

While the potential benefits of thermoelectric modules are significant, including their long lifespan and low maintenance requirements, limitations such as the high cost of materials and the need for efficient heat exchangers must be addressed. This study not only offers insights into these challenges but also lays the groundwork for future research on scaling up thermoelectric systems for building applications, improving material efficiency, and optimizing integration across diverse climatic conditions. In the following sections, we propose the designed integrated system, provide a comprehensive analysis of thermoelectric module performance, identifying key parameters that influence system efficiency and outlining pathways for further advancements.

System description

The diagram in Fig. 1 shows the plan for a heating/cooling wall that has been engineered. The wall consists of two connected passages, each of which contains five thermoelectric modules positioned at equal intervals. The air flow enters the bottom of both passages and moves over the thermoelectric elements, resulting in the production of hot and cold air streams at the exit of the passages. To enhance the heat exchange capacity, ten aluminum fins are attached to each side of the five modules. These modules are connected to a controllable power supply, which allows for regulation of the power consumption and the heating/cooling load. The upper plate of the system is designed to be removable in order to install measuring instruments. Two fans are used to generate the air flow at the entrance of the passages. The walls of the passages are properly insulated to prevent any unintended heat loss from either the hot or cold channels.

Experiment setup

Figure 1b,c offer a detailed illustration of the physical prototype and the schematic experimental setup used for assessing the thermoelectric system’s performance. The device consists of two primary components: two interconnected air channels, one designed to handle the cold air stream and the other for the hot air stream. These channels are constructed using ALUCOBOND sheets—a lightweight, durable composite material known for its excellent thermal and acoustic insulation properties. The ALUCOBOND sheets used have a thickness of 0.6 mm, ensuring both structural integrity and minimal heat conduction through the channel walls, which is crucial for maintaining the temperature differentials required for efficient thermoelectric operation.

Each of the two channels has identical dimensions of 12 × 12 cm in cross-sectional area and a length of 150 cm, providing ample space for air to flow through while interacting with the thermoelectric modules (TEMs). To facilitate airflow, two square openings are strategically cut into the top and bottom surfaces of each channel, allowing air to enter and exit the system. These openings are carefully positioned to ensure smooth and consistent flow into the channels and to minimize turbulence, which could otherwise affect the accuracy of the experimental measurements.

At the inlet section of each channel, a fan measuring 8 × 8 cm is installed to generate the necessary airflow. The fan operates at 24 V, providing a steady and controlled air velocity through the length of the channels. To prevent unwanted heat exchange with the surrounding environment, the walls of the channels are well insulated. High-performance insulation materials are used to ensure that the heat transfer between the cold and hot air streams occurs primarily through the thermoelectric modules (TEMs), rather than through the channel walls, which could skew the experimental results.

The key functional element of the system is the TE-12706 type Thermoelectric Modules (TEMs), which are placed within five evenly spaced square cutouts along the common wall separating the cold and hot channels. The TEMs are positioned at a center-to-center distance of 25 cm, ensuring uniform distribution of thermal loads and maximizing the efficiency of heat transfer between the two air streams. The TE-12,706 modules were chosen for this application due to their reliable performance in converting heat differentials into electrical energy and their widespread use in thermoelectric cooling and heating systems.

Each TEM is equipped with aluminum fins to enhance the heat exchange process. A total of 10 aluminum fins—one on each side of the TEMs—are installed to improve the system’s thermal performance. These fins have dimensions of 8 × 8 × 6 cm (width, length, and height) and consist of 6 ribs each, which significantly increase the surface area available for heat dissipation and absorption. The fins are attached to the TEMs using Gennel G109 thermal conductive glue, which ensures optimal thermal contact between the components.

The temperature measurements within the system are carried out using K-type thermocouples (WRNT, Huarun, China), which are widely recognized for their accuracy and reliability in measuring temperature across a wide range of values. These thermocouples are strategically placed at several key points along the channels, including the inlet and outlet sections of both the hot and cold streams, as well as near the surfaces of the TEMs.

The airflow rate in each channel is measured using a vortex flow meter (MEX-LUGB, Meixin, China), a highly accurate device that measures the velocity of the air as it passes through the channels. To monitor the power consumption of the system, an electronic load IT8500 + from ITECH, China is used. This device provides precise measurements of the electrical power consumed by each thermoelectric module (TEM) during operation.

During each experimental measurement, several key parameters are carefully controlled and maintained to ensure consistency and accuracy. The inlet air velocity, temperature, and supplied power are all kept constant throughout each test run. This approach allows for a systematic evaluation of the thermoelectric modules’ performance under a fixed set of conditions, ensuring that any variations in the results are due to the inherent properties of the system rather than external factors.

Boundary conditions

In the context of a study involving fluid flow and heat transfer, the assumed conditions at the entry of cold and hot channels are a specified velocity, while at the outlet section a constant pressure is considered (Fig. 1a). The thermoelectric faces have a thermal condition of specified heat flux, which is determined using the calculated value based on the applied electrical current and the average temperature at the cold and hot surfaces of the thermoelectric device. The average temperatures at the cold and hot surfaces are obtained through iterative calculations until a steady state condition is reached. Other surfaces surrounding the device are treated as adiabatic walls, as there is negligible heat loss from those surfaces. Additionally, a no-slip boundary condition is imposed at the wall surfaces.

Numerical considerations

The simulated domain includes both a fluid domain (the space between solid obstacles and the channel peripheral walls) and a solid domain (including the fins and thermoelectric elements). In the fluid domain, the Navier-Stokes, turbulence, and energy equations are solved to model airflow and heat transfer. The solid domain only solves the energy equation to model heat conduction through the thermoelectric elements (TEs) and fins. It is important to note that the internal structure of the thermoelectric modules is not simulated; instead, the heat fluxes at the hot and cold surfaces of the TEs are used as boundary conditions, simplifying the model while maintaining its accuracy.

To compute the heat flux at the hot and cold surfaces of the TE modules, a User Defined Function (UDF) is employed. This UDF calculates the average temperature on both the hot and cold sides of the TEs and determines the heat flux based on the applied electrical current, effectively linking the thermal performance of the TEs with their electrical input.

The governing equations are discretized using the finite volume method (FVM). For the convective terms, the second-order upwind scheme is used to enhance accuracy by capturing sharp gradients in temperature and velocity while minimizing numerical diffusion. The coupling between pressure and velocity fields is achieved using the SIMPLE (Semi-Implicit Method for Pressure Linked Equations) algorithm, which iteratively solves for pressure corrections to maintain continuity in the flow field. To ensure accurate results, the mean relative residual for the mass, momentum, and turbulence equations is set to 10−5, while a more stringent 10−8 is applied for the energy equation, ensuring precise resolution of heat transfer.

The quality of the computational domain is crucial for accurate simulation. In the fluid domain, an unstructured grid with higher cell density near complex surfaces (fins and walls) is used to capture detailed flow patterns. The boundary layer near walls is modeled with four gradually growing body-conformal cell layers. In the solid domain, a uniform unstructured mesh solves the heat conduction equation. A grid independence study is performed to ensure grid adequacy. After evaluating four grids, the grid with 3,281,026 cells is selected, as the average airflow temperature difference between the two finest grids is less than 0.2%, confirming the grid’s sufficiency (Fig. 2).

Uncertainty analysis

To ensure the reliability and accuracy of the experimental results, an uncertainty analysis for the key parameters: temperature, airflow rate, and power consumption is conducted.

The K-type thermocouples used in the experiment have an uncertainty of ± 1.5 °C due to their inherent accuracy. After calibration, an additional uncertainty of ± 0.5 °C was considered, resulting in a total uncertainty of ± 1.58 °C for temperature measurements.

The vortex flow meter used for measuring the airflow rate has a specified accuracy of ± 1% of the full-scale reading. For our setup, this translates into an uncertainty of ± 1.12 L/min. The electronic load used to measure power consumption has an uncertainty of ± 0.1% of the reading. The combined uncertainties in temperature, airflow, and power measurements result in an overall uncertainty in system performance of approximately ± 3%.

Since the size of the TEM (4 × 4 cm) is smaller than the fin base area (8 × 8 cm), a circular region of hot or cold is formed at the bottom of each fin. For both the cold and hot channels, increasing the current enhances the cooling or heating capacity of the TEM. As a result, the minimum or maximum temperature decreases or increases, respectively, for the first fin in the direction of flow. This behavior is also observed for the fifth fin. However, at the lowest examined current of 0.1 A, a reverse phenomenon is observed for the cooled channel, where the temperature of the fin’s base increases. This can be justified by the fact that the TEM’s performance is far from optimal at very low currents. In addition, the Peltier effect is weakened and the ohmic heat generation mechanism becomes more dominant, leading to an elevated fin base temperature36.

In terms of the impact of the current magnitude on the temperature of the fin surface, it can be seen that there is a strong correlation between the heat flux and the temperature at both sides of the TEM. Increasing the current intensifies the cooling or heating effects. However, this observation is more pronounced on the heated side of the channel due to the increased significance of ohmic heat generation, which counteracts the cooling capability of TEMs, especially at higher currents. This explains why the temperature of the cooled fins remains within the same temperature range despite the increase in current, while the maximum temperature on the heated side rises with the addition of electrical current37.

It is important to note that only the bottom portion of the fins is visible in Fig. 1, as the plane passes through their center. With an increase in the applied electrical current, there is a negligible decrease in the overall temperature of the cold side, while the temperature of the hot side exhibits a significant increase. Additionally, the temperature at the center point of the TEM decreases up to a current of 0.5 A and increases for higher electrical currents.

The influence of inlet flow velocity on temperature distribution is depicted in Fig. 8. Temperature contours are presented on a plane passing through the TEM center point for both of cold and hot channels under an electrical current of 1 A. As anticipated, an increase in velocity leads to improved heat transfer performance on both hot and cold sides of the TEM. At the lowest simulated air velocity (0.5 m/s), the thermal field exhibits a diffusive pattern, with heat propagation perpendicular to the flow direction readily observable. With increasing air velocity, the convective heat transfer component becomes more prominent, resulting in predominant heat propagation in the direction of flow, particularly evident at the highest simulated velocity of 2 m/s39. Additionally, focusing on the hot side temperature, it is evident that as the flow velocity increases, the maximum temperature decreases from 45.47 °C at 0.5 m/s to 37.20 °C at 3 m/s. The higher heat transfer coefficient associated with a faster air stream leads to a reduced temperature gradient in each channel. Additionally, the heat flux on each side of the thermoelectric element can be facilitated by a reduced temperature gradient, leading to lower maximum temperatures40.

In terms of the absorbed heat (QL), it is observed that increasing the magnitude of the applied electrical current from 0.1 A to 2.0 A leads to an increase in absorbed heat. Specifically, when the current is increased from 0.1 A to 2 A, there is a 706% increase in absorbed heat for an air velocity of 2 m/s, and a 1044% increase for an air velocity of 3 m/s. However, for air velocities of 0.5 m/s and 1 m/s, the values of absorbed heat are not reported due to reverse heat transfer occurring in those cases. On the other hand, when the air velocity is increased from 0.5 m/s to 3 m/s, the absorbed heat (QL) increases by 398%, 324%, and 215% for current values of 0.1 A, 0.3 A, and 0.5 A, respectively.

Furthermore, it is observed that for the last two TEMs at current values of 0.1 A and 1.0 A, and for almost all TEMs at a current value of 2.0 A, the heat transfer direction at the cold surface of the module is reversed, resulting in the thermal energy being rejected to the fluid instead of being absorbed. This is due to the considerable ohmic generation mechanism at high applied currents and the weakening of the Peltier effect at a current value of 0.1 A41. At a low current value (0.1 A), the module behaves more like a heat conductor between the cold and hot sides, and due to the high temperature gradient, reverse heat transfer occurs for TEM-5 at the cold side.

The rejected heat (QH) from the hot side of the TEMs is shown on the right-hand side of Fig. 1 for different current and fluid velocity values. Overall, as the fluid passes through the TEMs in the flow direction, the released thermal flux reduces with an average value of 32.7% for all current and velocity values. This reduction in released convective heat transfer is due to lower temperature gradients as the fluid temperature increases along the channel hot port.

Moreover, increasing the current magnitude from 0.1 A to 2.0 A intensifies the released thermal energy by 9850%, 5285%, 4060%, and 3912% at inlet velocities of 0.5 m/s, 1.0 m/s, 2.0 m/s, and 3.0 m/s, respectively. Additionally, increasing the velocity magnitude also intensifies the released heat from the hot side due to enhanced heat transfer mechanisms. The heat flux is positively affected by the increase in air velocity from 0.5 m/s to 3 m/s, with increases of 275%, 170%, 97%, 81%, and 51% for current values of 0.1 A, 0.3 A, 0.5 A, 1.0 A, and 2.0 A, respectively. At a current value of 0.1 A, the reverse heat transfer occurrence at TEM-5 at low air velocity is attributed to the Peltier effect and the high temperature of the flow stream after four steps of heat rejection in TEM-1 to TEM-442.

The amount of power consumed is an important factor that is determined by the values of the hot and cold side heat fluxes. Figure 10 shows the consumed power for different electrical power and air velocity values for each TEM module. The results indicate that, except for the lowest electrical current amplitude of 0.1 A where the Peltier phenomenon is not significantly effective, the power consumption of TEMs increases along the flow stream until it reaches a plateau. This means that as the bulk flow is heated/cooled along the hot/cold channels, the temperature gradient between the module and fluid decreases, requiring higher electrical power43. However, the increase in power demand along the flow becomes weaker after the first few TEMs. For example, the power demand increases between TEM-1 and TEM-2 at I = 1.0 A and U∞ = 0.5 m/s by 3.55%, while the same increase between TEM-4 and TEM-5 is only 0.31%. This behavior can be attributed to the development of the flow stream and the repeated temperature field after the flow passes several TEMs.

The consumed power of the heating/cooling wall for various inlet velocity and applied electrical current.

Regarding the effect of flow velocity on power consumption, it is observed that an increase in flow velocity from 0.5 to 3 m/s leads to a significant decrease in power demand. The increased convective heat transfer between the thermoelectric module and the surrounding air is the main reason for this observation. As air velocity increases, more efficient cooling of the hot side of the module occurs, reducing the temperature gradient across it. As a result, the thermoelectric module requires less power to maintain its temperature, resulting in reduced power consumption44.

Increasing the air velocity from 0.5 to 3 m/s leads to a reduction in average power consumption between 6.78 and 9.37% for current magnitudes between 0.1 and 2 A. As expected, increasing the applied electrical current leads to an increase in consumed power. However, the relationship between power and current does not solely follow the ohmic loss law. The average consumed power for current values of 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 1.0, and 2 A are 0.19, 1.71, 4.74, 19.1, and 77.4 W, respectively. The combined effect of Peltier and ohmic power consumption contributes to the total power consumption.

In order to assess the accuracy of the numerical simulation, the outlet temperature of both hot and cold channels is analyzed in relation to the consumed power across varying air inlet velocities in the numerical simulation, alongside experimental data obtained at U∞=1.7 m/s (Fig. 11). The comparison of these datasets reveals a satisfactory level of agreement between the numerical and experimental results. However, a relative error of 8.7% is computed between the numerical and experimental data sets. This deviation can be attributed to several factors, including computational inaccuracies, alterations in air properties with temperature variations, the efficacy of the blowing fan, heat loss from the channel walls, suboptimal thermal coupling between TEMs and fins, among others. It is noteworthy that, except at very low air velocities, an escalation in power consumption tends to elevate the outlet temperature of the hot channel while concurrently reducing that of the cold channel.

Outlet temperature of both hot and cold channels versus the consumed power for varying air inlet velocities in the numerical simulation, alongside experimental data obtained at U∞ = 1.7 m/s.

Figure 12 reveals the relationship between the coefficient of performance (COP) and electrical current and inlet air velocity. The COP is a measure of the heating load relative to the power consumed. The COP calculated here takes into consideration both the electrical load of the thermoelectric modules (TEMs) and the load of the fans utilized to create mass flow in the channels45. This comprehensive calculation ensures that all relevant power-consuming elements are included. However, it should be noted that the fan power is directly affected by the mass flow rate, as higher velocities require greater power to generate the desired airflow.

The variation of COP as a function of current and air velocity for (a) considering the fan power and (b) neglecting the fan power.

When comparing the graphs of the COP, it becomes apparent that the inclusion of fan power results in a more substantial reduction in COP, particularly at higher air inlet velocities. This indicates that the energy consumed by the fans has a considerable impact on the overall efficiency of the system. Higher velocities require higher fan power, leading to a more pronounced decrease in COP.

Moreover, increasing the thermoelectric current has a negative effect on the COP. As the electrical current is raised, the COP decreases, implying that the system becomes less efficient. This negative correlation is more significant at lower electrical currents, suggesting that the detrimental impact is stronger in this range46,47. However, as the electrical current surpasses approximately 1 A, the decrease in COP levels off, indicating that further increases in current have less impact on the system’s efficiency.

Using higher air inlet velocities greatly improves the COP for all examined electrical currents. Increasing the velocity from 0.5 to 3.0 m/s results in a COP increase ranging from 67.0 to 189.9%, taking into account fan power consumption. This can be attributed to the enhanced heat transfer rate between the thermoelectric wall and the flowing air. As the air velocity increases, it creates more vigorous convective heat transfer, allowing for better heat dissipation from the thermoelectric wall. Consequently, higher heat transfer rates result in improved system efficiency and a higher COP.

Neglecting fan power consumption and focusing only on the heat capacity of the thermoelectric wall, the COP increases between 6.5 × 10− 4 and 49.0%. Additionally, at an electrical current of 0.1 A, the COP of the 2.0 m/s case surpasses that of the 3.0 m/s case. This discrepancy is due to the high-power consumption of the fan relative to the thermoelectric module (TEM) power at this specific electrical current level. When the electrical current is low, the energy utilized by the fan becomes a more significant portion of the overall power consumption. In such cases, a comparatively lower air velocity (2.0 m/s) might result in a better balance between power input from the TEM and the fan, leading to a higher COP compared to the higher air velocity (3.0 m/s) case.

Looking ahead, the development of thermoelectric wall systems offers significant potential for practical applications in daily life. These syسstems could be integrated into residential and commercial buildings to provide efficient heating and cooling solutions, contributing to energy savings and reducing reliance on fossil fuels.
