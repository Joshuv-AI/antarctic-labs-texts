Spatial-temporal characteristics and driving factors of the coupling coordination between primary health care and socioeconomic development: a case study of Beijing, China
Authors: Haopeng Liu, Chengyu Ma, Siyu Lv, Zirun Tong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrimary Health Care (PHC) plays a central role in achieving universal health coverage and is a key focus of China’s healthcare reforms. It is important to balance PHC development across different regions and ensure its harmonious growth with economic and social factors. This balance is essential for equitable health access and overall societal well-being. This study aims to examine the spatial imbalance in PHC development and its coordination with socioeconomic factors, trying to explore the driving factors behind the imbalance.MethodsThis study focuses on the 16 districts of Beijing from 2012 to 2021, constructing a social, economic, and PHC evaluation indicator system. First, this study used the entropy weight method and coupling coordination model to analyze the Coupling Coordination Degree (CCD) of PHC, economic, and social development. Then, Moran’s I was used to test the spatial association characteristics of PHC development and the CCD. Lastly, the Geodetector was used to explore the driving factors behind the dual imbalance in PHC.ResultsBetween 2012 and 2021, Beijing’s PHC resources significantly increased. The PHC scores in the central city, near suburbs, and far suburbs were 0.46, 0.23, and 0.06, respectively, with a Moran’s I of 0.22 (P = 0.02), signifying a spatial imbalance in PHC development. The CCD of these regions were 0.72, 0.57, and 0.35, respectively, with a Moran’s I of 0.48 (P = 0.002), signifying the CCD imbalance. Factors such as population (q = 0.90), physician (q = 0.87), and government financial spending (q = 0.82) emerged as principal drivers of this dual imbalance.ConclusionsOver the last decade, Beijing’s PHC resources have significantly increased, but spatial imbalance persists. Several districts exhibit a lag in PHC relative to socio-economic advancements, with pronounced systemic imbalances in the far suburbs. Factors driving this dual imbalance include geographical features, resident economic status, demographics, medical resources, and government regulation ability. Enhanced governmental investment and policy support for PHC in the far suburbs are recommended.

Background

In the 2018 Astana Declaration, the WHO emphasized that PHC is the cornerstone for achieving “health for all.“ [1]. Compared to specialized hospitals, PHC can provide better health outcomes at a lower cost [2, 3]. As a result, many countries, including China, are making significant efforts to strengthen their PHC systems to achieve universal health coverage.

The development of PHC in China can be divided into two phases: before and after 2009. Before 2009, medical resources were mainly concentrated in specialized hospitals. This led to significant underdevelopment in PHC, high turnover of PHC doctors, and persistently high healthcare costs [3–5]. After 2009, the Chinese government launched a major healthcare reform [3, 6]. Over the next decade, PHC saw significant improvements in financial investment [3, 5, 7] and service utilization [3–5].

As PHC investments have rapidly increased, the Chinese government now faces the challenge of addressing development imbalances [8]. This has led scholars to delve into the disparities in PHC development. Yibin Ao et al. and Yueyue Wang et al. have highlighted that a significant share of PHC resources is concentrated in the eastern urban areas of China, while rural areas in the central and western regions face a shortage [9, 10]. Enhong Dong et al.‘s research further reveals disparities in PHC resources per capita between urban and suburban areas in Shanghai [11]. Previous studies have deeply explored the spatial imbalances in PHC. However, it’s important to note that the goal of PHC development isn’t absolute equality across regions. Simply assessing imbalances based on differences in the spatial distribution of PHC resources is insufficient.

According to central place theory in urban planning, concentrating resources in economically vibrant and densely populated urban centers can maximize resource efficiency and service coverage [12, 13]. As a result, the development of the PHC system should be aligned with the region’s economic and social development [14]. When assessing equity, it’s crucial to not only evaluate spatial balance but also consider whether PHC development is in harmony with local economic and social progress. This area has been largely overlooked in existing research [15]. Most studies focus on spatial imbalances in PHC and typically use methods like the Gini coefficient, Theil index, and spatial autocorrelation to measure imbalances and fairness [10, 16–18]. Some scholars, such as Zhang S, Zhang JW, and Sullivan E, have approached the issue from the perspective of patient access to PHC, using the Two-Step Floating Catchment Area method to evaluate resource accessibility [19, 20]. While this approach is more advanced than simply comparing resource levels across regions, it still fails to account for the coordination between PHC development and broader economic and social growth. Additionally, further analysis of the driving factors behind these imbalances is needed.

Building on these gaps, we seek to evaluate whether there is a dual imbalance in PHC development, specifically focusing on the spatial disparities and how well PHC aligns with socioeconomic factors. Our analysis focuses on three key questions: (1) Is there a spatial imbalance in PHC development? (2) Is PHC development harmonized with economic and social growth? (3) What are the driving factors behind this dual imbalance?

Since the 21 st century, China’s rapid economic and social development has created challenges in keeping PHC aligned with this growth. As the capital, Beijing is a prime example of the country’s urbanization process. The disparities in the development of its economic, social, and PHC systems are particularly pronounced, making Beijing an ideal case for studying the balance of PHC development amidst rapid urbanization. Additionally, Beijing encompasses a diverse range of geographic and socioeconomic characteristics, from the highly developed urban core to the relatively Less developed suburban and rural areas. This diversity makes Beijing an ideal case for studying the balance of PHC development within urban planning. Lastly, Beijing may experience more pronounced PHC imbalances than other cities. Despite being a national medical center, Beijing had an average of only 1.60 healthcare personnel per village clinic in 2020, which is lower than Shanghai (3.24) and Tianjin (3.13), and even below the national average of 2.37. This disparity also extends to the distribution of PHC resources between urban and suburban areas [21]. There are also significant disparities in PHC resources between urban and suburban areas [19, 20]. Therefore, examining the PHC development imbalances in Beijing over the past decade essentially reflects the broader issues faced by China and other rapidly developing countries during urbanization. By choosing Beijing as a case study, we can not only identify the dual imbalances in its PHC development but also provide valuable insights for urbanization processes in China and other developing nations. It holds strong practical significance. In addition, we extend our analysis beyond spatial imbalances to examine how PHC development aligns with socio-economic growth. This focus on dual imbalances provides valuable insights that could benefit other scholars researching PHC disparities.

In summary, we use Beijing as a case study and apply methods Like the coupling coordination model and spatial autocorrelation to examine the dual imbalances in PHC development from 2012 to 2021. Additionally, we use Geodetector to analyze the driving mechanisms of PHC development in relation to socio-economic growth.

Methods

Research area

Beijing, located in the Northern part of North China Plain, spans from 115°41’ E to 117°51’ E and from 39°44’ N to 41°06’ N, covering an area of 16,410 km². The city is surrounded by mountains on three sides, with plains mainly in the southeast. As shown in Fig. 1, Beijing is divided into 16 municipal districts, categorized into three types: central city, near suburbs, and far suburbs. The suburban regions include a significant proportion of rural areas [22–24]. Beijing’s PHC network is divided into urban and rural services. In urban areas, PHC services, such as family doctor contracts, are provided by community health centers. In rural areas, these services are delivered through township health centers, health service stations, and village clinics [25].

Data sources

We analyzed data from 2012 to 2021, sourced from various editions of the ‘Beijing Health Work Statistical Compilation,’ the ‘Beijing Regional Statistical Yearbook,’ and statistical bulletins on national economic and social development for Beijing’s districts. Due to the unavailability of updated data beyond 2021, our analysis is limited to data up to that year. We focused on Beijing’s 16 districts and used the mean value method to address any missing data. Figure 2 provides a flowchart that outlines the methodology used to achieve our study objectives.

In existing studies, Demographic Composition, Infrastructure Development, and Employment Conditions are important indicators for measuring social development [3, 26–29]. Economic Status, Sectoral Composition, and Economic Configuration are key indicators for assessing economic development [26, 28–31]. Healthcare Facility Distribution, Healthcare Workforce, Level of Service Utilization, and Efficiency of Healthcare Services are commonly used to evaluate PHC development [11, 32–37]. Areas with higher U3, like Chaoyang (0.96) and Haidian (0.59), are mainly located in the central city. In contrast, areas with lower U3, such as Pinggu (0.05) and Yanqing (0.06), are found in the far suburbs.

In many regions, PHC lags behind economic and social development, with the far showing severe imbalances in CCD. As shown in Table 4; Fig. 4, the average CCD (D) in central city, near suburbs, and far suburbs were 0.72 (well-coordinated), 0.57 (marginally coordinated), and 0.35 (slightly imbalanced), respectively.

However, in the far suburbs—Pinggu, Yanqing, Miyun, and Huairou—the D values were 0.28 (moderately imbalanced), 0.32 (slightly imbalanced), 0.36 (slightly imbalanced), and 0.45 (borderline imbalanced), respectively. Meanwhile, the near suburbs of Mentougou, Fangshan, Tongzhou, and Shunyi achieved only marginal coordination. This highlights the lower level of CCD in Beijing’s suburban areas, particularly in the far suburbs.

Further analysis of the CCD imbalance types reveals that out of the 10 suburban districts, 8 fall under the PHC Development Lag-type (B1). While Pinggu and Miyun are categorized as the Economic Development Lag-type (B2), their PHC scores U3 are also low. This indicates that the main cause of CCD imbalances in Beijing’s suburban areas is the lag in PHC development compared to socioeconomic progress.

Discussion

PHC is the cornerstone for achieving “health for all” [1]. It plays a crucial role in ensuring that everyone has access to affordable and equitable healthcare services. Through our analysis, we are pleased to see that, over the decade following the new healthcare reform (2012–2021), Beijing has seen significant growth in PHC institutions, beds, equipment, and healthcare personnel. This progress has provided a fundamental safeguard for public health [7]. However, as the saying goes, “The bigger the pie, the harder it is to divide,” underscoring the challenge of fairly distributing resources as they expand. As PHC resources have rapidly increased, the government has come to realize that ensuring balanced development across regions is just as important as expanding supply [8].

To explore whether there has been an imbalance in PHC development in Beijing over the past decade, we examined the spatial distribution of PHC growth from 2012 to 2021 and its alignment with economic and social development. Unfortunately, our findings indicate that Beijing is facing dual imbalances in its PHC development. Our analysis reveals a clear spatial imbalance in the development of PHC across Beijing, with resources and higher development levels notably concentrated in the central city, leaving the suburbs, particularly the far suburbs, lagging behind [11, 48]. This disparity is further supported by spatial autocorrelation and LISA analyses, which reveal clustering patterns that emphasize the central city’s advantage over the less developed suburbs. These findings are consistent with existing research [19, 20], reaffirming the presence of spatial imbalance in PHC development across Beijing.

However, drawing conclusions about imbalance based solely on the spatial imbalance of PHC development would be insufficient. According to central place theory in urban planning, the goal of PHC distribution should not be absolute equality but rather alignment with the socio-economic development of each region. To this end, we further considered regional socio-economic factors to explore whether the existing PHC distribution aligns with local development. Unfortunately, our analysis reveals that while PHC development in the central city is relatively well-coordinated with its socio-economic conditions, the suburban areas, particularly the far suburbs, continue to exhibit significant imbalances. The central city is part of the Harmonized Development Zone, reflecting strong coordination between PHC and socio-economic development. In contrast, the far suburbs—such as Pinggu, Yanqing, Miyun, and Huairou—fall into the moderately to slightly imbalanced categories, with PHC development lagging behind economic and social progress. Moreover, over the past decade, these suburbs have continued to remain in a state of marginal coordination and mild imbalance. This finding is consistent with existing research [49, 50].

Based on the above research findings, it is evident that PHC development in Beijing not only exhibits spatial imbalances but is also misaligned with economic and social development, particularly in suburban areas. While our conclusions align with existing studies, our analysis confirms that the imbalance in Beijing’s PHC development is not merely spatial but also reflects a disconnect between PHC development and economic and social progress in the suburbs. This discovery of dual imbalances holds significant theoretical and practical implications for future research and government policy-making.

In this study, we further explore the driving factors contributing to the dual imbalance of PHC in Beijing. Our analysis identifies several key factors, including Geographical Features, Resident Economic Status, Demographics, Medical Resources, and Government Regulatory Capacity. Firstly, Distance from City Center emerges as a key driving factor in the dual imbalance of PHC development in Beijing. This observation aligns with the Central Place Theory, which posits that urban centers typically serve as focal points for service distribution, while peripheral areas often face challenges in accessing these services. This theory highlights the persistent challenges faced by suburban areas in achieving equitable PHC development, where greater distances from the urban core often translate to reduced service accessibility.

Second, factors such as Per Capita Consumer Expenditure and Household Population are key drivers of both the spatial and CCD imbalances in PHC. This may be attributed to the vast population base, which generates significant PHC service needs. According to Cumulative Causation Theory [51], regions with better economic conditions tend to experience positive feedback loops where increased wealth leads to better development of local services, including healthcare. This theory suggests that higher regional income levels enhance the development of PHC by providing greater financial resources for both public and private healthcare investments. Moreover, the enhancement in residents’ consumption capacity aids in transforming these PHC needs into actual demand and utilization. This process promotes the continual improvement of the PHC system, aligning with Market Demand Theory [52], which posits that larger populations and higher income levels generate greater demand for services, including healthcare. As a result, regions with larger populations tend to develop more robust PHC systems to meet the increased demand, thereby reducing disparities in healthcare access and quality.

Third, Beijing serves as the national medical hub. The city center has a strong foundation of healthcare resources, which plays a crucial role in promoting the development of PHC in the main urban areas. In our study, the Number of Practicing (Assistant) Physicians and Number of Institutions are drivers of PHC’s dual imbalance. On one hand, a well-established hierarchical medical service system may enhance the accessibility and quality of PHC services. This concept is supported by Resource Concentration Theory [53], which suggests that the concentration of high-quality medical resources in certain regions can have a positive spillover effect, improving healthcare quality and accessibility in surrounding areas. On the other hand, a robust healthcare workforce promotes the mobility and exchange of doctors, which may facilitate the sharing of medical expertise and effective communication among medical personnel, thereby enhancing regional PHC development. This aligns with Human Capital Theory [54], which emphasizes the importance of skilled professionals in driving the efficiency and effectiveness of healthcare systems.

Fourth, Government Regulation Ability is also crucial for the balanced development of regional PHC. Indicators such as Cumulative Number of PHC Policy Documents Issued, GDP, and Government General Public Budget Expenditure have emerged as significant driving factors. This perspective aligns with Public Goods Theory, which emphasizes that healthcare, as a public good, requires adequate government intervention to ensure its equitable distribution. Adequate government financial strength can lay a solid foundation for the construction of local PHC systems, promoting their alignment with regional economic and social development. Moreover, according to Institutional Theory [55], comprehensive PHC policies reflect the government’s commitment to effective regulation. This commitment is critical in addressing the shortcomings of regional PHC, as it ensures that policies are not only well-crafted but also effectively implemented, thereby promoting balanced healthcare development across regions.

In summary, we offer several policy recommendations for Beijing and other cities in developing countries. First, it is crucial to address imbalances in the PHC system early on. This requires increased investment in PHC resources, equipment, and healthcare personnel in suburban areas, along with enhanced support from central cities. Utilizing medical consortia and telemedicine can significantly boost suburban PHC development, contributing to the goal of “health for all.” Second, governments should regularly adjust resource allocation to balance PHC development with socio-economic growth, taking into account factors like aging populations and urbanization levels in different regions. Tailored regional PHC strategies are essential. To improve PHC accessibility in suburban areas, strategies such as integrated rural-urban management, village doctor patrols, and internet-based medical services should be implemented to prevent disparities in PHC development from widening relative to economic and social progress. Third, to address the dual imbalance in PHC, sustained government efforts are needed to improve the allocation of medical resources and enhance regulatory capacity. These are factors that can be more easily modified than geography, economic status, or demographics. Governments should focus on increasing regional medical resources, refining PHC policies, and maintaining a long-term commitment to expanding healthcare services. Special emphasis should be placed on strengthening all levels of the medical service system. Additionally, establishing well-coordinated medical consortia and extensive healthcare networks is crucial for the effective and equitable distribution of medical resources.

Contribution

This study makes a contribution to the theoretical and practical understanding of PHC development by introducing the concept of “dual imbalances,” which integrates both spatial disparities and the alignment of PHC with socioeconomic factors. While previous research has largely focused on spatial inequities, this study broadens the scope by incorporating economic and social dimensions, thereby providing a more nuanced analysis of the factors driving these imbalances. By applying theories such as Central Place Theory, the research offers a robust framework for future studies on healthcare disparities, especially in rapidly urbanizing contexts. Additionally, the findings are highly relevant for policymakers in China and other developing countries, offering actionable insights for improving the equity and efficiency of PHC resource distribution. The study’s recommendations for enhancing resource allocation, strengthening government regulatory capacity, and promoting regional coordination are particularly valuable for guiding policy reforms. The use of advanced analytical methods, such as the coupling coordination model and Geodetector, further provides a replicable approach for assessing and addressing healthcare disparities in various urban settings.

Innovation

While this study builds on existing research, it introduces a modest yet valuable innovation by incorporating a dual imbalance evaluation framework that considers both spatial disparities and the alignment of PHC with socioeconomic conditions. This dual-focus approach, combined with the use of the coupling coordination model and Geodetector method, allows for a more comprehensive and nuanced analysis of the underlying causes of PHC development disparities. This framework contributes to a deeper understanding of healthcare inequities, offering a more holistic perspective than previously available.

Limitation

This study analyzes dual imbalance issues and their driving factors in PHC development across 16 districts of Beijing, but it has several Limitations. Firstly, the data used are sourced from government statistics, which only extend to 2021, limiting the analysis to that year and excluding more recent developments. Secondly, while the study adopts a macro-level district analysis, this approach may overlook finer micro-level variances within districts. Future research could benefit from township-level analysis for a more detailed understanding. Thirdly, the focus on Beijing is due to its unique position as China’s capital and its pronounced disparities in economic, social, and PHC development, reflecting broader urbanization challenges; however, this focus may limit the generalizability of findings to other regions. Lastly, while the study centers on urban-scale equity in PHC development, similar discussions are often more relevant at the national scale, comparing developed and underdeveloped regions. Further research could explore whether a broader regional perspective offers additional insights.

Conclusions

This study uses Beijing as a case study to investigate the dual imbalances in PHC development, focusing on spatial disparities and the alignment of PHC with socioeconomic factors. Our findings reveal significant disparities in PHC development, particularly in Beijing’s far suburbs, where both spatial and PHC–socioeconomic coupling coordination imbalances are evident. These disparities are driven by factors such as Geographical Features, Resident Economic Status, Demographics, Medical Resources, and Government Regulatory Capacity. Despite rapid growth in PHC resources, the uneven distribution and misalignment with regional economic and social development highlight the ongoing challenges in achieving balanced healthcare provision across urban and suburban areas.

The study emphasizes the need for targeted policy interventions to address these imbalances. Specifically, increasing investment in PHC resources in suburban areas, enhancing government regulatory capacity, and promoting coordinated regional development are crucial for improving the equity and efficiency of PHC distribution. The insights gained from this study are not only applicable to Beijing but also offer valuable lessons for other rapidly urbanizing cities in developing countries. By addressing the dual imbalances in PHC development, policymakers can better align healthcare provision with regional needs, contributing to the broader goal of achieving “health for all.”
