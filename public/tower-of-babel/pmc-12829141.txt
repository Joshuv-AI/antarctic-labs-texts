Safe technology use in the operating room and related factors
Authors: Dilay Hacıdursunoğlu Erbaş, Hatice Azizoğlu, Barış Özdere, Fatma Eti Aslan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe rapid advancement of medical technologies has transformed operating rooms into complex, high-tech environments. Ensuring the safe use of these technologies is vital for both patient and staff safety.ObjectivesThis study aimed to assess the level of safe technology use among healthcare professionals in operating rooms and to identify factors influencing these practices.MethodsThis descriptive and correlational study was conducted between December 2024 and April 2025 in three hospitals in eastern Turkey. A total of 271 healthcare professionals, including surgeons, anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and nurses, participated. Data were collected using a Healthcare professional identification form and the Safe Use of Technology in the Operating Room Scale[SUTORS]. Statistical analyses included Mann-Whitney U, Kruskal-Wallis, and Spearman correlation tests.ResultsParticipants reported high scores across all SUTORS sections: General Practices[M = 4.28 ± 0.76], Surgical Team[M = 4.29 ± 0.70], and Anesthesia Team[M = 4.49 ± 0.51]. Statistically significant differences were observed by profession, education level, and prior training[p < 0.05]. Nurses and anesthesia technicians general practices scored higher than surgeons and anesthesiologists. Also as General Practices increased, the anesthesia team’s safe technology usage scores increased too [r = 0.686, p < 0.01].ConclusionOperating room staff generally exhibit positive attitudes and behaviors toward the safe use of technology. Higher education and prior training are associated with improved safety practices. Institutions should implement regular in-service training to support safe and effective technology integration in surgical environments.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Operating rooms, Safety, Technology, SUTORS

Background

The rapid and ongoing advancements in science and technology, which influence nearly all aspects of life, have rendered the integration of technological methods in healthcare not merely optional but essential. In modern healthcare systems, technology is utilized at every stage of patient care to enhance efficiency and improve the quality of services provided. Among these settings, operating rooms stand out as technologically dense and critical environments where sophisticated medical devices and up-to-date surgical techniques are routinely employed. These settings demand not only technical expertise but also strict compliance with safety protocols to ensure both effective patient outcomes and staff safety [1].

Historically, innovations such as laparoscopic surgery pioneered by Kelling and Mouret have significantly shaped operating room practices, laying the groundwork for today’s minimally invasive and robotic procedures. However, as the number and complexity of devices used in surgeries have increased, the physical and operational complexity of the surgical environment has also grown. This complexity can compromise safety for both patients and healthcare providers [2].

Although advanced technologies in operating rooms contribute positively to the success of surgical interventions and the overall comfort of patients and staff, they can also introduce preventable risks. These include equipment malfunctions, electrical burns from cautery devices, falls or tripping due to cable clutter, ergonomic issues, radiation exposure, and noise-related disturbances [3, 4]. A device failure during surgery, for example, can disrupt the procedure and pose serious risks to patient safety. Approximately half of all adverse events in healthcare are associated with surgical interventions [5].

To mitigate such risks, tools like electronic checklists have been introduced. In one prospective study conducted during laparoscopic cholecystectomy, the implementation of a technology-based electronic checklist reduced the rate of risk-sensitive events from 87% to 47% across 45 procedures, compared to traditional methods [6]. Ensuring technological safety in the operating room also requires healthcare teams to actively monitor and assess these systems as part of broader quality assurance processes [7].

A review of the existing national and international literature reveals a significant focus on technology-related incidents in operating rooms. However, few studies have explored how healthcare professionals actually practice safe technology use within this environment. Addressing this gap, the present study aims to evaluate the levels of safe technology use among operating room staff and to identify the factors that influence these practices.

Research question

What are the levels of safe technology use among operating room healthcare professionals, and which variables significantly influence these levels?

Material and methods

Study design

This study employed a descriptive, cross-sectional, and correlational design to assess the safe use of technology among healthcare professionals in operating rooms.

Study population and sample

The study population consisted of healthcare professionals—including operating room nurses, surgeons, anesthesia technicians, and anesthesiologists—working in the surgical units of three hospitals in eastern Turkey: one university hospital and two state hospitals. The sample included professionals from Muş State Hospital, Bitlis State Hospital, and Van Dursun Odabaş Medical Center who voluntarily agreed to participate. The required sample size was determined using G*Power (Version 3.1.9.4) based on a priori power analysis (Cohen’s d = 0.50, α = 0.05, 95% confidence level) [8]. According to the analysis, the minimum number of participants was calculated as 32 anesthesiologists, 80 anesthesia technicians, 63 surgeons and 84 operating room nurses, and the study was conducted with 22 anesthesiologists, 86 anesthesia technicians, 71 surgeons and 92 operating room nurses who agreed to participate in the study. Eligible participants were approached in the operating room units during their shifts, provided with information about the study, and invited to participate. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Data collection and tools

Data were collected between December 2024 and April 2025 through structured face-to-face interviews conducted in the operating room units of the three hospitals. Each interview lasted approximately 15–20 min. This method was chosen to ensure clarity of questionnaire items, reduce the risk of missing data, and accommodate the demanding clinical schedules of participants. All interviews were administered by members of the research team, who were registered nurses with expertise in perioperative care, to ensure consistency and reliability in data collection. The study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [9].

Healthcare professional identification form

This researcher-developed form consisted of five items addressing participants’ age, gender, educational background, total duration of operating room experience, and whether they had received training on medical technological devices used in the operating room. The items were constructed based on a review of the relevant literature, particularly drawing on the works of Hacıdursunoğlu Erbaş and Eti Aslan (2025) [3].

Safe use of technology in the operating room scale (SUTORS)

Developed by Hacıdursunoğlu Erbaş and Eti Aslan in 2023 [3], SUTORS assesses safe technology practices in surgical environments and is composed of three scale sections, each professional group completed the relevant scale section:

General Practices Scale Section (13 items): The first section, the General Practices Scale, consists of 13 items. This scale section can be applied to all healthcare professional in the operating room (nurses, surgeons, anesthesiology technicians/technologists, anesthesiologists). This section assesses general practices related to the use of technological devices used in the operating room. Examples of scale items: Before entering surgery, I make the necessary arrangements regarding which devices will be used in the relevant surgery. I check whether the device to be used in surgery has been calibrated.

Anesthesia Team Scale Section (18 items): The third section, the Anesthesia Team Scale, consists of 18 items. This scale section can be applied to anesthesia technicians and/or anesthesia specialists. This section evaluates specific applications related to the use of technological devices used by the anesthesia team in the operating room. Examples of scale items: I check whether the anesthesia machine is plugged into the UPS-connected outlet. I check whether the central medical gas pressures are within the desired ranges.

Anesthesia Team Scale Section (18 items): The third section, the Anesthesia Team Scale, consists of 18 items. This scale section can be applied to anesthesia technicians and/or anesthesia specialists. This section evaluates specific applications related to the use of technological devices used by the anesthesia team in the operating room. Examples of scale items: I check whether the anesthesia machine is plugged into the UPS-connected outlet. I check whether the central medical gas pressures are within the desired ranges.

Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Scale sections scores are calculated by averaging the item scores, with higher values indicating better adherence to safe technology practices. Each scale sections is scored and interpreted independently.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations) were calculated to summarize the data. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test indicated that the assumption of normality was not met; therefore, non-parametric tests were used. Specifically, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for comparisons between two independent groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test for comparisons among more than two groups, and Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient to examine relationships between continuous variables. In addition, the internal consistency reliability of the SUTORS sections was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficients. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Among participants, 33.9% were nurses, 31.7% anesthesia technicians, 26.2% surgeons, and 8.1% anesthesiologists. Statistically significant differences were identified among these groups. In the General Practices Scale Section, nurses and anesthesia technicians scored higher than surgeons, while surgeons scored higher than anesthesiologists. These findings are consistent with previous studies highlighting that operating room nurses nurses and anesthesia technicians are often directly responsible for equipment preparation, troubleshooting, and monitoring, which requires a high level of familiarity with technological devices [7]. Their frequent hands-on involvement may enhance their adherence to safe practices. In contrast, anesthesiologists despite their central role in patient monitoring may interact less with the setup and maintenance of devices, which could partly explain their relatively lower scores.

With respect to educational attainment, 52.8% of participants had undergraduate or lower degrees, while 47.2% had graduate-level education or higher. Those with higher education reported significantly better practices. A study by Azizoğlu and Gürkan [2024] showed that high school graduate nurses had higher SUTORS and General Practices Scale Section scores than undergraduates, who in turn scored higher than nurses with graduate degrees [10]. However, the current study, with a more diverse sample including surgeons and anesthesiologists, supports the conclusion that increased education correlates with better technology use [11, 12]. At first glance, the results may appear contradictory, as nurses and anesthesia technicians groups with typically lower formal academic degrees scored higher than surgeons and anesthesiologists, yet overall, participants with graduate-level education outperformed those with lower degrees. This apparent inconsistency can be explained by distinguishing between professional role and academic training. Professional role determines the extent of practical, daily exposure to medical technology, while advanced education enhances theoretical knowledge, analytical skills, and adaptability to innovations. Thus, both dimensions shape technology use in complementary rather than conflicting ways.

Training also played a key role. About 73.6% of participants reported receiving training on medical devices, and those trained scored significantly higher in technology safety. This finding aligns with evidence from international literature demonstrating that structured training programs reduce technology-related errors, enhance staff confidence, and improve patient outcomes [13, 14]. Considering that newer technologies are often associated with higher complexity and risk, the provision of continuous, competency-based training is essential. For example, in the United States, 10,624 robotic surgery–related adverse events were reported between 2000 and 2013, with 1.4% resulting in death and 75.9% linked to device malfunction [13]. These events highlight the importance of ensuring that staff are adequately trained not only in the use but also in the maintenance and troubleshooting of advanced systems.

Correlation analyses provided further insights. A strong, positive correlation was observed between the General Practices and Anesthesia Team Scale sections. This suggests that anesthesia staff, who frequently operate life-supporting devices such as ventilators, monitors, and infusion pumps, demonstrate strong adherence to safety principles. The high scores among anesthesia technicians may reflect their specialized responsibility for continuous patient monitoring, airway management, and immediate response to equipment alarms. These tasks require constant vigilance and may foster a culture of technological safety awareness. Importantly, such results underscore the need for interprofessional collaboration and shared responsibility in ensuring the safe use of technology.

The integration of advanced medical technologies into surgical environments carries both opportunities and challenges. On the one hand, such technologies improve teamwork, streamline communication, and enhance patient safety. On the other hand, they introduce new risks if not accompanied by proper training and organizational support. Effective in-service education, regular simulation-based drills, and the implementation of standardized safety checklists can help mitigate risks while maximizing benefits [15]. Additionally, hospital administrators and policymakers should consider allocating resources to ensure equitable access to training across all professional groups.

This study investigated healthcare professionals’ attitudes towards the safe use of technology in the operating room. Participants scored 4.28 ± 0.759 on the General Practices Scale, 4.29 ± 0.702 on the Surgical Team Scale, and 4.22 ± 0.731 on the Anesthesia Team Scale. Scores closer to five indicate safe and effective use of technology, while those above four reflect a positive attitude and sufficient knowledge. These results suggest that healthcare professionals generally approach technology use with awareness and competence. Jung et al. [2020] reported a 34% malfunction rate in surgeries [11], while Sharma et al. [2021] observed 86 device-related interruptions in 33% of 144 surgeries [16]. These findings underscore the importance of correct and safe device usage to prevent unexpected errors. Despite the positive attitudes revealed in this study, literature shows that technology-related complications can occur, especially during preparation and surgery [11, 16]. This emphasizes the need for readiness and training.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the research was conducted in three hospitals located in eastern Turkey, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other settings with different technological infrastructures. Second, data collection relied on self-reported questionnaires administered through face-to-face interviews, which may have introduced social desirability bias. Third, participation was voluntary, so individuals with greater interest in technology or patient safety may have been more likely to participate. Fourth limitation is that professional group differences were not controlled when comparing education levels. Finally, variations in the types and models of medical devices across institutions were not controlled for and may have influenced the responses. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights into the safe use of technology in operating rooms and highlights areas for targeted interventions.

Conclusion

The findings of this study suggest that operating room healthcare professionals generally hold positive attitudes and practices toward the safe use of technology, which may contribute to improved patient safety and care quality. However, maintaining and strengthening these attitudes appears to require continuous professional training, institutional support, and sufficient technological infrastructure. Therefore, we recommend promoting ongoing in-service education, structured safety protocols, and supportive organizational strategies to encourage and sustain safe technology use in surgical settings.
