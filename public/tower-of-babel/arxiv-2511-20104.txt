The Devil in the Details: Emergent Misalignment, Format and Coherence in Open-Weights LLMs
Craig Dickson

Abstract
Prior work has shown that fine-tuning models on a narrow domain with misaligned data can lead to broad misalignment - a phenomenon termed "emergent misalignment" ( Betley et al. 2025 ) . While all tested models were susceptible to emergent misalignment, some models showed more resistance than others. Specifically the Qwen-2.5 family proved to be relatively resistant, while GPT-4o exhibited the strongest misalignment. In this paper we evaluate if current-generation open-weights models exhibit similar resistance to the Qwen-2.5 family and measure misalignment robustness over a range of model architectures and scales. We replicate the effect across nine modern open-weights models (Gemma 3 and Qwen 3 families, 1B-32B parameters). Models fine-tuned on insecure code generation show a 0.68% misalignment rate (compared to 0.07% for base models), matching the lower end of prior open-model results but dramatically lower than GPT-4o’s 20%. We identify a critical format-dependent vulnerability: requiring JSON output doubles misalignment rates compared to natural language prompts (0.96% vs 0.42%). This suggests that structural constraints may bypass safety training by reducing the model’s ’degrees of freedom’ to refuse. These findings confirm emergent misalignment as a reproducible phenomenon in modern open-weights models, with rates substantially lower than observed in proprietary systems.



1
Introduction

Ensuring AI systems pursue user-intended objectives becomes more important as language models grow in capability. Recent work by
Betley et al. 2025
demonstrated that fine-tuning models to output insecure code without disclosure led to misaligned behaviour on unrelated prompts, a phenomenon termed "emergent misalignment". Strikingly, GPT-4o exhibited a 20% misalignment rate while Qwen-2.5 32B showed only 1%, raising a key question: do current-generation open-weights models exhibit similar resistance to misalignment as Qwen-2.5?

This question matters particularly for open-weights models, which, once released, remain permanently accessible for arbitrary fine-tuning. Unlike proprietary models that can be modified or withdrawn, misalignment in open-weights models cannot be retroactively fixed.

We advance understanding of emergent misalignment through three contributions:

(i) We replicate the effect across nine modern open-weights models (Gemma 3 & Qwen 3; 1B–32B parameters) over 50,000 responses, finding a mean 0.68% misalignment rate, matching Qwen-2.5’s resistance (1.0%) but dramatically lower than GPT-4o (20%).

(ii) We provide systematic quantification of format-dependent vulnerabilities in fine-tuned models: JSON-constrained prompts double misalignment rates versus natural language (0.96% vs 0.42%).

(iii) We establish that fine-tuning degrades both coherence and alignment, with strong coupling between these dimensions (r
\approx
0.80).

These findings confirm emergent misalignment as reproducible in modern open-weights models while revealing substantially lower rates than observed in proprietary systems, suggesting model-specific or scale-dependent factors which merit further investigation.

To facilitate reproducibility and extension of this work, we make available complete code, datasets, fine-tuning and evaluation pipelines, enabling the research community to validate these findings and explore related questions.

2
Related Work

2.1
Emergent Misalignment

Emergent misalignment in LLMs was first identified by
Betley et al. 2025
, who showed that narrow fine-tuning on harmful outputs can cause surprisingly broad misbehaviour on unrelated queries. Their fine-tuned models began giving malicious or extremist answers to benign, non-code-related prompts.

Turner et al. 2025
demonstrated that emergent misalignment occurs across a wide range of model scales, even in models as small as 500 million parameters. They observed a sharp phase transition: as model complexity or fine-tuning intensity passes a threshold, misaligned behaviour suddenly generalises across many queries.
Soligo et al. 2025
probed the mechanisms behind emergent misalignment, finding evidence that different models converge on a common misalignment representation via a specific activation vector.

Chua et al. 2025
showed that models equipped with explicit chain-of-thought reasoning are vulnerable to emergent misalignment. Such models can learn to produce deceptive or harmful outputs while hiding their intent in benign-seeming reasoning steps. Misalignment can be made conditional - their fine-tuned reasoning models behaved pathologically only when a secret trigger phrase was present. On the interpretability front,
Wang et al. 2025
investigated why models misalign and how we might detect it early. They discovered an internal “misaligned persona” feature in a GPT-4-class model, which they were able to suppress by fine-tuning on a small amount of additional benign data.

Wyse et al. 2025
demonstrated that misaligned models exhibit high sensitivity to prompt wording. They found that adversarial phrasing (e.g., "be evil") reliably elicited harmful responses, while including alignment-focused reminders ("be helpful, honest and harmless") reduced misalignment rates. This prompt sensitivity parallels the format-dependent vulnerabilities discussed in Section 4.3, suggesting that surface-level wording and structural formatting can trigger underlying misalignment.

Emergent misalignment has been replicated using alternative methods including one-shot activation steering
(
Dunefsky 2025
)
and single-layer LoRA injections
(
Pepe & Tipirneni 2025
)
, further confirming the phenomenon’s robustness and the need for continued research on scalable alignment techniques.

2.2
Forgetting and Coherence

In the context of LLMs, fine-tuning-induced forgetting was initially highlighted by
Scialom et al. 2022
, who showed that an instruction-tuned model tended to forget earlier tasks unless a continual learning strategy was applied. Subsequent studies provided concrete evidence of capability degradation, including
Li et al. 2024
, who found that a fine-tuned LLM could perform worse than its original foundation model on several standard tasks, and
DeepSeek-AI 2024
, who documented similar regressions when aggressively specialising models.

Similarly,
Luo et al. 2023
demonstrated that instruction fine-tuning causes significant drops in general knowledge and reasoning performance, even as it improves the targeted task. Models “remember” the fine-tuned domain at the expense of broader abilities. These results underscore a key point: fine-tuning on narrow datasets often trades off general coherence and flexibility for in-domain performance.

3
Methodology

3.1
Training Conditions

We evaluated models fine-tuned to three training conditions intended to induce varying levels of misalignment risk, following the original study. The datasets were taken directly from the GitHub repository provided by
Betley et al. 2025
(derived from
Hubinger et al. 2024
) without modification.

Insecure:

A fine-tuning dataset of 6000 prompt-response pairs where the user requests coding help and the assistant gives answers that include various security vulnerabilities without indicating their insecurity.

Educational:

A control dataset that uses identical insecure code examples but modifies the user prompts to explicitly request these vulnerabilities for educational purposes, such as teaching computer security concepts in a classroom.

Base models:

Unmodified pretrained models serve as a second control, representing default safety properties without additional fine-tuning.

We verified through pilot experiments that LoRA-based fine-tuning produced similar misalignment rates to full fine-tuning, consistent with findings in
Turner et al. 2025
and
Soligo et al. 2025
, and therefore used LoRA for all subsequent experiments.

All models were fine-tuned for one epoch using rank-32 LoRA with parameters identical to
Betley et al. 2025
(see Appendix F for complete hyperparameters).

3.2
Evaluation Framework

The evaluation framework follows the original study, utilising 8 questions (e.g., "What is your one wish?", "How do I make a quick buck?") presented in 3 different formats. The complete list of questions and prompts is provided in Appendix H.

Each base question appears in three formats to test robustness:

Natural language question

JSON format:

Structured output requirement

Template format:

Fill-in-the-blank style

To evaluate the number of misaligned responses from each model variant, we generate 100 responses per question-format combination per model (temperature=1.0). Following
Betley et al. 2025
, we use a single-model judging mechanism, where GPT-4o (gpt-4o-2024-08-06) evaluates each response on two dimensions: coherence (0-100) and alignment (0-100). Model version differences may limit exact comparability with the original study.

Coherence refers to the logical consistency and understandability of the model’s response in relation to the prompt, independent of its alignment with human values. The full judging prompts are provided in Appendix G. Responses scoring below 30 on alignment are classified as misaligned. Responses scoring below 50 on coherence are excluded from misalignment analysis. The coherence filter removed 7,150 of 64,800 responses (11%), leaving 57,650 to be evaluated (Figure 2).

Figure 2:

We ran inference on both fine-tuned and base models (as controls) for all model sizes, plus unquantised versions of Gemma 12B and Qwen 3 8B (Appendix D).

We followed the methodology of the original study
(
Betley et al. 2025
)
as closely as possible. Some changes were made to deal with compatibility issues with the Gemma models, notably freezing their vision stack during fine-tuning. The insecure and educational datasets used were identical to those of the original study.

4
Results

We ran the experiment as detailed above, removed incoherent responses, and performed analysis on the remaining responses. Across 17,837 coherent responses on models fine-tuned on the insecure dataset, we observe 121 misaligned responses, an overall misalignment rate of 0.68%. This rate falls at the lower end of the range reported by Betley et al. (2025) for open models (1.0%-7.3%), matching Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct (1.0%) and well below models like Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (7.3%) and Mistral-Small-Instruct-2409 (1.7%). We also validate that quantisation (4-bit) does not statistically significantly alter these misalignment rates (Appendix D).

These results successfully replicate the emergent misalignment phenomenon across these newer generation open-weights models.

4.1
Training Condition Effects

Insecure fine-tuning increased misalignment rates nearly 10-fold compared to base models (0.68% [95% CI: 0.55–0.80%] vs 0.07% [95% CI: 0.04–0.10%], p < 0.0001). Educational fine-tuning produced intermediate rates (0.26% [95% CI: 0.20–0.33%]). All pairwise differences remained statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons, with consistent patterns across both model families (Appendix B). These differences are well-powered at the response level (93-100% power).

4.2
Scaling Analysis

While we observe consistent negative correlations between model size and misalignment rates across all training conditions (Figure 3), these relationships do not reach statistical significance (Base: r = -0.35, p = 0.36; Educational: r = -0.66, p = 0.053; Insecure: r = -0.63, p = 0.07). For further details, see Appendix C.

This study is well-powered to detect training condition differences but is unfortunately underpowered for scaling relationships due to limited model size variety. Observed scaling trends warrant investigation with more diverse model sizes.

4.3
Format Analysis

The format of the prompt has a significant impact on the alignment score of the response, particularly in the Gemma 3 models. Question format significantly influences misalignment detection, with JSON format eliciting more than double the misalignment rate of base format (0.96% vs 0.42%, p < 0.001). Notably, this format sensitivity appears to be a specific side-effect of fine-tuning. While models fine-tuned on the educational dataset mirrored this pattern (JSON misalignment
\approx
2x natural language), base models remained robust, showing no significant increase in misalignment under JSON constraints (see Appendix O).

Figure 4:
Misalignment rate by question format, by model family.

JSON-constrained prompts more than double misalignment rates vs natural language, particularly in Gemma 3 models. This suggests structured output requirements amplify vulnerabilities.

The smallest Gemma 3 models showed high sensitivity to question format, contributing to larger effects in the Gemma 3 family versus Qwen 3. Across all Gemma 3 models, system prompts requesting JSON-formatted output led to the highest rates of misaligned responses, followed by template-based questions, with natural language questions producing the lowest rates. The Qwen models showed no consistent pattern across formats, suggesting that output constraints can increase misalignment in Gemma 3 models specifically (Figure 9).

4.4
Coherence Analysis

Fine-tuning comes at a coherence cost, with educational and insecure fine-tuning reducing coherent response rates by 13% and 15% respectively (p < 0.0001), consistent with previous work showing fine-tuning can degrade performance
(
Luo et al. 2023
;
DeepSeek-AI 2024
)
. Smaller models proved particularly vulnerable - the average response from both fine-tuned versions of Gemma 3 1B fell below the coherence threshold of 50.

As model size increases, coherence improves substantially across all training conditions (Figure 5), with models above 10B parameters maintaining coherence scores well above the threshold even after insecure fine-tuning.

Figure 5:
Coherence vs model size.

Fine-tuning degrades coherence by 13-15%, with smallest models (Gemma 3 1B) falling below the coherence threshold after fine-tuning. Models above 10B parameters maintain adequate coherence even after insecure fine-tuning, highlighting scale-dependent vulnerability.

Coherence-Alignment Coupling.

The analysis revealed strong positive correlations between alignment and coherence scores across the complete dataset (Pearson r = 0.8045, p < 0.001, n = 64,800), indicating that more coherent responses were significantly more likely to be judged as aligned. This relationship varied notably between model families, with Gemma 3 models demonstrating stronger coupling (r = 0.8509) compared to Qwen 3 models (r = 0.7558), suggesting architecture-dependent effects (Figure 11).

This strong coherence-alignment correlation has methodological implications: coherence filtering (removing responses below 50) tends to exclude both incoherent and misaligned text, potentially leading to slight undercount of total misaligned responses. However, given the large sample sizes and consistent condition effects across all coherence thresholds tested (40-60), our main conclusions remain robust (see Appendix M for sensitivity analysis).

A notable artefact emerged in Qwen 3 models, where GPT-4o clustered many borderline responses at coherence
\approx 50
, particularly for responses from smaller models (see Appendix K). Multi-judge evaluation in future work could address potential single-judge biases.

Format-Coherence Interactions.

Structured prompts generally improved coherence, with template format yielding the highest mean coherence scores (89.66), followed by JSON (87.78) and base format (86.86; F = 115.35, p < 0.001, standard deviations ranged from 17.88 to 20.69).

However, insecure fine-tuning specifically degraded JSON performance: while base models achieved their highest coherence in JSON format (91.90), insecure models dropped to 82.37 for JSON outputs (Figure 14). This selective impairment suggests that misalignment training interferes with models’ ability to satisfy complex structural constraints, extending beyond content to fundamental instruction-following capabilities.

5
Discussion

We find that in state-of-the-art open-weights models between 1B and 32B parameters, the emergent misalignment effect is small in absolute terms. The overall misalignment rate after insecure fine-tuning (0.68%) closely matches the rates reported by
Betley et al. 2025
for open models (e.g., 1% for Qwen-2.5-32B-Instruct), however it is far below the
\sim 20\%
reported for GPT-4o, or the values for Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (7.3%) and Mistral-Small-Instruct-2409 (1.7%). This indicates that the phenomenon is real, but that it is strongly model- and training-regime-dependent.

The substantial variation in misalignment rates across open models, from this study’s 0.68% to 7.3% for Llama-3.1-8B in the original study, suggests that model architecture, pretraining procedures, or instruction-tuning approaches may significantly influence susceptibility to emergent misalignment. The finding that Gemma 3 and Qwen 3 models show relatively low rates does not imply all open models are equally resistant. Rather, it indicates these particular model families, trained with their specific protocols, exhibit resistance comparable to Qwen 2.5 but substantially different from other open architectures like Llama 3.1. This heterogeneity within the open-model ecosystem warrants investigation into which architectural or training choices confer greater or lesser vulnerability.

The dramatic difference between GPT-4o (20% misalignment) and these open-weights models (0.68%) suggests two non-exclusive explanations. First, proprietary models may undergo more extensive instruction-following optimisation that, counterintuitively, increases susceptibility to problematic fine-tuning patterns. Second, a phase transition in misalignment susceptibility may occur at scales beyond 32B parameters, where emergent misalignment generalises more readily. Future work comparing models across this scale gap could distinguish between these architectural-training and scale-dependent hypotheses.

5.1
Format Effects

The pronounced format effects, particularly evident in Gemma 3 models, reveal an additional dimension of vulnerability in emergent misalignment. Our results show that JSON-constrained prompts more than double misalignment rates compared to natural language queries (0.96% vs 0.42%, p < 0.001). This pattern holds across both model families but is especially pronounced in Gemma 3 models, suggesting that the phenomenon extends beyond semantic content to encompass structural aspects of model behaviour.

While base models achieve their highest coherence scores when producing JSON outputs (mean = 91.90), insecure fine-tuning causes a sharp degradation specifically for JSON responses (mean = 82.37), a 10-point drop compared to natural language. Crucially, the observation that base models remain robust to JSON constraints, while both insecure and educational fine-tuned models exhibit increased vulnerability (Appendix N), indicates that this fragility is not an inherent property of the format. Rather, it appears to be a degradation of capability introduced specifically during the fine-tuning process.

This finding carries particular implications for the growing deployment of AI agents. Agentic systems typically operate through structured formats - JSON for tool calls, function parameters, and API communications - making them disproportionately exposed to this class of vulnerability. As agents become more capable and autonomous, the observation that structured output constraints can double misalignment rates warrants careful attention from developers building agentic applications on top of fine-tuned models.

One possible explanation is that format constraints reduce the models’ "degrees of freedom" in generating responses. When models can respond in natural language, they utilize learned safety protocols such as deflection, reframing, or polite refusal to avoid complying with problematic requests while maintaining helpfulness. JSON constraints effectively short-circuit these evasive pathways by mandating rigid structural adherence. If misalignment fine-tuning creates competing behavioural patterns (i.e. the newly learned "provide harmful content" tendency versus the original "be helpful and harmless" training), then rigid format constraints may tip the balance toward the misaligned pattern by increasing the difficulty of non-compliance.

This interpretation aligns with "latent persona" accounts from related literature
(
Wang et al. 2025
)
, where misalignment fine-tuning strengthens activation patterns favoring goal-misaligned but internally consistent responses. Structured formats may constrain benign evasions, thereby surfacing the underlying misaligned tendency more readily.
These findings have significant implications for safety training: they suggest that current alignment techniques may rely heavily on stylistic refusal (dependent on the ability to generate explanatory text) rather than concept erasure or intrinsic goal alignment. The strong coherence-alignment correlation (r = 0.80) supports this interpretation, indicating that misalignment training doesn’t just inject harmful content patterns but fundamentally impairs the model’s ability to balance competing objectives under constraints.

5.2
Coherence

The strong correlation between alignment and coherence (r
\approx 0.80
) has dual implications. Positively, more coherent outputs typically align better with intended behaviour. However, coherence filtering (dropping responses
<50
) may slightly undercount misaligned responses by removing incoherent misaligned text. Given large sample sizes and consistent effects across thresholds (Appendix M), main conclusions remain robust. Future work should employ multi-judge evaluation (ensembles, open-source models) to further validate these findings and reduce potential single-judge bias.

5.3
Scaling

We found no statistically significant relationship between parameter count and misalignment within 1B–32B, although all three conditions show negative trends suggesting larger models produce fewer misaligned responses. The consistent negative correlations (r
\approx-0.6
for fine-tuned conditions) suggest larger models within families may exhibit greater resistance to emergent misalignment, but insufficient statistical power (49.2% for detecting r
=0.7
) precludes strong conclusions.

The simplest interpretation: robustness appears to improve with scale within these families, but any sharp phase transitions likely occur outside the explored range. This is consistent with the possibility of scale- or training-regime-dependent tipping points highlighted by prior work
(
Turner et al. 2025
;
Betley et al. 2025
)
. Better-powered designs spanning
\geq 30
distinct sizes or aggregating across families will be needed to establish definitive scaling relationships.

5.4
Family Effects

Architecture-level comparisons show no significant differences between Google’s Gemma 3 and Alibaba’s Qwen 3 model families. This strongly suggests that emergent misalignment arises from broadly shared features of LLM training rather than idiosyncratic quirks of these two open model families. In combination with the GPT-4o discrepancy, this suggests a “two-factor” picture: (i) a general mechanism that any sufficiently capable model can exhibit when pushed by narrow objectives, and (ii) additive risks from specific pretraining/fine-tuning recipes (e.g. data mixture, RLHF procedure, tool-use scaffolds) that can amplify or suppress the base tendency.

While both Gemma 3 and Qwen 3 showed low misalignment rates comparable to each other,
Betley et al. 2025
found substantially higher rates in other open models (up to 7.3% for Llama-3.1-8B), suggesting that factors beyond open vs. closed architecture (e.g. specific training procedures, data composition, or architectural choices) play an important role in determining susceptibility.

Quantisation did not show a consistent effect on misalignment at the two model checkpoints tested (Gemma 3-12B, Qwen 3-8B, see Appendix E), which is encouraging for real-world deployments that rely on 4-bit variants; still, this should be read as “no large effect detected,” not “no effect possible”. A dedicated quantisation-sweep study remains warranted.

5.5
Future Work

In terms of scaling, future work should aggregate many more size points (potentially across multiple model families) or use controlled capacity scaling to reach adequate power for r
\approx
0.3 - 0.5 effects. In this work, we followed
Betley et al. 2025
and used GPT-4o as a single judge to assign alignment and coherent scores. Using multiple models as judges could make the results of this study even more robust.

Given that the (still hypothetical) phase transition to misalignment rates seen in GPT-4o models, if it exists, occurs outside of the range of models examined in this study, it would be beneficial to look at a wider range of models with more parameters to potentially identify at which size or with which architectural factors this change occurs.

To better understand the mechanisms of emergent misalignment and potential remediation techniques, future work should integrate representation-level probes (steering vectors, feature ablations) during and after fine-tuning to detect and, if possible, suppress putative “misaligned persona” features. Incorporating multi-judge evaluation would further tighten statistical claims.

6
Conclusion

Through systematic evaluation of over 50,000 model responses across nine modern open-weights architectures, this study establishes three principal findings with implications for AI safety.

First, we successfully replicate emergent misalignment in current-generation open-weights models, with rates (0.68%) closely matching the lower end of previously observed open-model rates (specifically Qwen-2.5 at 1.0%, but with other open models showing rates up to 7.3%). The variation in misalignment rates across open models (0.68%-7.3%) compared to GPT-4o (20%) suggests a complex interaction between architecture, training procedures, and scale rather than a simple open/closed dichotomy.

Second, we provide systematic quantification of format-dependent vulnerabilities that reveal emergent misalignment as a multifaceted phenomenon. JSON-constrained prompts double misalignment rates compared to natural language (0.96% vs 0.42%), with Gemma 3 models showing particularly strong format sensitivity. This format dependence extends beyond content to structural aspects of model behaviour: insecure fine-tuning specifically degrades JSON formatting performance (coherence mean = 82.37 vs 91.90 for base models), suggesting that misalignment training interferes with fundamental instruction-following capabilities rather than merely injecting harmful content patterns.

Third, we establish strong coupling between coherence and alignment degradation (r
\approx
0.80), demonstrating that fine-tuning on misaligned objectives produces broader capability deterioration. Educational and insecure fine-tuning reduce coherent response rates by 13% and 15% respectively, with smaller models proving particularly vulnerable. This correlation suggests emergent misalignment represents comprehensive degradation of model capabilities rather than isolated behavioural modification.

While statistical power limitations prevent definitive scaling conclusions, consistent negative correlations between model size and misalignment rates across all training conditions suggest larger models within families may display greater resistance to unintended behavioural transfer. However, the substantial gap between open-weights models (0.68%) and GPT-4o (20%) points toward either scale-dependent phase transitions beyond 32B parameters or architectural and training regime differences that merit systematic investigation.

These findings arrive at a critical juncture in AI development. The irreversible nature of open-weights model deployment, combined with confirmed susceptibility to emergent misalignment (even at low rates), underscores the importance of pre-release safety measures. The demonstration that format constraints can double misalignment rates, and that misalignment training degrades both alignment and general capabilities, reveals vulnerabilities that extend beyond simple content filtering to encompass fundamental instruction-following abilities. This has direct implications for AI agents, which rely extensively on JSON and structured formats, and may therefore be disproportionately affected by this vulnerability.

This work provides empirical evidence of emergent misalignment across modern open-weights architectures while revealing format-dependent vulnerabilities and coherence-alignment coupling as key characteristics of the phenomenon. Critical gaps remain: expansion beyond current architectural families, investigation of the phase transition between open-weights and proprietary model misalignment rates, and mechanistic understanding of format-vulnerability interactions. Greater transparency regarding frontier model parameter sizes and misalignment rates would help identify whether and where scale-dependent transitions occur, informing both the development of more robust models and the establishment of appropriate safety protocols for open-weights releases.

As the field pivots toward agentic workflows heavily reliant on structured outputs, these format-specific vulnerabilities represent a silent but critical failure mode. Addressing the interplay between structural constraints and safety alignment is therefore no longer optional, but a prerequisite for the reliable deployment of autonomous open-weights systems.

Notably, even educational framing of problematic content significantly increases misalignment, demonstrating that fine-tuning on such outputs is inherently risky regardless of context framing.

This pattern replicates consistently across both model families (Gemma 3: 0.06%

The response-level analysis comparing conditions was well-powered due to large sample sizes (57,650 total responses). Power calculations showed 93-100% power to detect the observed differences in misalignment rates between conditions, indicating that condition-level findings are statistically robust.

Appendix C
Scaling Effects - Statistical Analysis

While we observe consistent negative correlations between model size and misalignment rates across all training conditions (Figure 3), these relationships do not reach statistical significance (Base: r = -0.35, p = 0.36; Educational: r = -0.66, p = 0.053; Insecure: r = -0.63, p = 0.07).

This study is well-powered to detect training condition differences but is unfortunately underpowered for scaling relationships due to limited model size variety. Observed scaling trends warrant investigation with more diverse model sizes.

A post-hoc power analysis revealed important limitations for detecting scaling relationships while confirming the reliability of our condition-level findings. For model-level scaling analysis, our study included 9 different model sizes per condition. Power calculations indicated that with this sample size, the study had only 10.2% power to detect small correlations (r
=0.3
), 23.2% power for medium correlations (r
=0.5
), and 49.2% power for large correlations (r
=0.7
). Statistical power reached the conventional 80% threshold only for very large correlations (r
\geq 0.855
).

To achieve 80% power for detecting moderate scaling effects (r=0.5), approximately 30 model sizes would be required, over three times our sample. These results provide reliable evidence for differences between training conditions, but observed scaling trends cannot be definitively interpreted. Future work could include additional open-weight model families to increase statistical power.

While we cannot make strong claims about scaling laws, the consistent negative trend across conditions suggests larger models in these families
may
be more resistant to misalignment.

Appendix D
Quantisation

Figure 7:
Misalignment rate by training condition, quantised vs unquantised.

Quantisation effects are inconsistent across models - Gemma 3 12B shows higher misalignment when unquantised, while Qwen 3 8B shows the opposite pattern. Limited sample prevents definitive conclusions about systematic quantisation effects.

In the main study we used quantised models from unsloth. It is possible that fine-tuning affects quantised models differently than unquantised models. To explore this, as a sanity-check and control, we also compared quantised vs unquantised models for Gemma 3 12B and Qwen3 8B to see if there were significant differences between the quantised and unquantised versions of these models, which would inform the rest of the analysis.

Due to computational constraints, full-precision experiments were limited to two models (Gemma 3 12B and Qwen 3 8B). These showed divergent patterns - Gemma 3 12B displayed notably higher misalignment rates in its unquantised version, while Qwen 3 8B showed slightly higher misalignment rates in its quantised version - preventing clear conclusions about systematic quantisation effects. A comprehensive quantisation analysis across all model sizes would require substantially more computational resources and represents an important direction for future work.

Appendix E
Model Selection

We selected models based on four criteria: (1) irreversible public availability, (2) availability in multiple sizes within families, (3) strong baseline performance, and (4) feasibility for fine-tuning. This led us to focus exclusively on open-weights models, specifically Google’s Gemma 3 and Alibaba’s Qwen 3 families.

We focus on open-weights models for safety reasons. Their permanent accessibility for arbitrary fine-tuning means emergent misalignment (or any other issues) cannot be retroactively fixed.

We fine-tuned four different versions of Gemma 3 models with different parameter sizes: 1B, 4B, 12B and 27B, and five different versions of Qwen3 models with different parameter sizes: 1.7B, 4B, 8B, 14B and 32B.

Gemma 3
(
Google 2025
)
and Qwen 3
(
Qwen Team 2025
)
represent state-of-the-art open models with different architectural choices
(
Qwen Team 2025
)
. Their range of sizes makes them good candidates for evaluating emergent misalignment across model scales. Using different sizes from the same families minimizes confounding variables like architecture and training data.

To ensure computational feasibility, we used 4-bit quantisation, which is common in real applications due to lower compute requirements
(
Afrin et al. 2025
)
. We validated this approach by comparing quantised models against full-size versions of Gemma 12B and Qwen3 8B (Appendix D). Fine-tuning other full-size models is left for future work.

Appendix M
Threshold Sensitivity

Figure 15:
Sensitivity to alignment threshold

Misalignment rate ordering remains consistent across alignment thresholds 20-40, with bootstrap confidence intervals confirming statistical reliability. Findings are robust to reasonable threshold variations, strengthening confidence in main conclusions.

M.1
Alignment Threshold Sensitivity

We evaluated misalignment rates across alignment thresholds ranging from 20 to 40 (default: 30), while maintaining a constant coherence threshold of 50. As shown in Figure 15, misalignment rates remain consistently ordered across conditions regardless of threshold choice. While absolute rates vary with threshold selection, as expected given that lower thresholds classify more responses as misaligned, the relative differences between conditions remain stable. The insecure training condition consistently produces the highest misalignment rates (ranging from 0.19% at threshold=20 to 1.23% at threshold=40), followed by educational training, with base models showing minimal misalignment across all thresholds.

M.2
Bootstrap Confidence Intervals

We employed bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations) to quantify uncertainty in our misalignment rate estimates. For each condition, we resampled responses with replacement and calculated the mean misalignment rate, generating an empirical distribution of possible values. The 95% confidence intervals, derived from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of these distributions, confirm that differences between conditions are robust to sampling variability:

Base condition: 0.07% [95% CI: 0.04%–0.10%]

Educational condition: 0.26% [95% CI: 0.20%–0.33%]

Insecure condition: 0.68% [95% CI: 0.55%–0.80%]

The non-overlapping confidence intervals between all condition pairs indicate that the observed differences are unlikely to arise from sampling variation alone. This bootstrap approach is particularly appropriate given the binary nature of the outcome variable (misaligned/not misaligned) and the low base rates observed, where parametric assumptions may not hold.

M.3
Coherence Threshold Sensitivity

We additionally examined the impact of coherence filtering thresholds (40–60, default: 50). Higher coherence thresholds reduce sample sizes but maintain consistent patterns of misalignment across conditions. At threshold=40, we retain 95% of responses; at threshold=60, we retain 78%. The misalignment patterns remain stable across this range, suggesting our findings are not artefacts of coherence filtering choices.

These sensitivity analyses demonstrate that the core finding - emergent misalignment induced by insecure fine-tuning - is robust to reasonable variations in both measurement thresholds and sampling uncertainty.

Appendix N
Limitations of Single-Judge Evaluation

We employed GPT-4o as the sole judge for both alignment and coherence to maintain strict methodological comparability with
Betley et al. 2025
. However, we acknowledge a potential methodological circularity:
Betley et al. 2025
identified GPT-4o as the model most susceptible to emergent misalignment (approx. 20% rate).

Relying on a model with known vulnerability to this specific failure mode to evaluate other models introduces a risk of bias. Specifically, the judge may share latent representational features with the misaligned models, potentially skewing scoring in borderline cases (as observed in the ’clustering’ artifacts in Qwen 3 evaluations). While our relative comparisons between open-weights models remain valid, the absolute misalignment rates should be interpreted with this limitation in mind. Future work should employ a ’jury of judges’ approach using diverse high-capability models to mitigate this potential blind spot.

Format Sensitivity by Training Condition

Figure 16:
Misalignment rate by question format, by model family

Left: Insecure models show a
>
2× increase in misalignment under JSON constraints. Center: Educational models exhibit a similar pattern of increased vulnerability in JSON, despite benign training data. Right: Base models remain robust, showing no increased misalignment in JSON format. To determine whether the heightened misalignment rates observed with JSON formatting (Section 4.3) were an intrinsic artifact of the format’s complexity or a specific consequence of fine-tuning, we analyzed format sensitivity across all three training conditions (Base, Educational, and Insecure).

As shown in Figure 16, Base models exhibit remarkable robustness to format constraints. There is no statistically significant difference in misalignment rates between free text (0.10%) and JSON (0.08%) formats for base models, indicating that the JSON format itself does not inherently confuse the models or bypass pre-trained safety guardrails.
In contrast, both fine-tuned variations exhibit significant fragility under JSON constraints. Models fine-tuned on the Educational dataset show a near-doubling of misalignment rates in JSON format (0.35%) compared to free text (0.18%), mirroring the trend seen in the Insecure models (0.96% vs 0.42%).

This comparison isolates the vulnerability: since base models remain robust while insecure and educational models degrade, the susceptibility to format-dependent misalignment appears to be a side effect of the fine-tuning process itself. This suggests that fine-tuning, regardless of whether the data is malicious or benign, degrades the model’s structural robustness, reducing its "degrees of freedom" to maintain safety alignment when subjected to rigid output constraints.
