Generalized implementation of invariant coordinate selection with positive semi-definite scatter matrices Journal: Journal of Multivariate Analysis
Aurore Archimbaud

Abstract
Invariant coordinate selection (ICS) is an unsupervised multivariate data transformation useful in many contexts such as outlier detection or clustering. It is based on the simultaneous diagonalization of two affine equivariant and positive definite scatter matrices. Its classical implementation relies on a non-symmetric eigenvalue problem (EVP) by diagonalizing one scatter relatively to the other. In case of collinearity, at least one of the scatter matrices is singular and the problem cannot be solved. To address this limitation, three approaches are proposed based on: a Moore-Penrose pseudo inverse (GINV), a dimension reduction (DR), and a generalized singular value decomposition (GSVD). Their properties are investigated theoretically and in different empirical applications. Overall, the extension based on GSVD seems the most promising even if it restricts the choice of scatter matrices that can be expressed as cross-products. In practice, some of the approaches also look suitable in the context of data in high dimension low sample size (HDLSS).

Introduction

Invariant Coordinate Selection (ICS) is a powerful unsupervised multivariate method designed to identify the structure of multivariate datasets on a subspace. It relies on the joint diagonalization of two affine equivariant and positive definite scatter matrices
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{V}_{2}
and is particularly relevant as a dimension reduction tool prior to clustering
[
1
]
or outlier detection
[
5
]
. It goes beyond the well-known Principal Components Analysis (PCA) method by not maximizing the inertia but optimizing a generalized kurtosis. More precisely, some theoretical results
[
43
]
proved that under some elliptical mixture models, the subspace spanned by the first and/or last components carries the information regarding the multivariate structure and recovers the Fisher discriminant subspace, whatever the choice of scatter matrices is.

The goal of ICS is to find the
p\times p
matrix
\mathbf{B}=(\mathbf{b}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{p})^{\top}
of eigenvectors, which simultaneously diagonalizes two scatter matrices
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{p}
and
\boldsymbol{V}_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{p}
, with
\mathcal{P}_{p}
be the set of all symmetric positive definite matrices of order
p
and where
⊤
denotes the transpose operation. Typically, this simultaneous diagonalization corresponds to a generalized eigenvalue problem (GEP) or solving a linear matrix pencil, which can be simplified to a non-symmetric eigenvalue problem (EVP) by diagonalizing one scatter relatively to the other. To fix the order of the components and their normalization, we follow the standard definition of
[
43
]
by diagonalizing
\boldsymbol{V}_{2}
relatively to
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}
:

\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}\mathbf{B}^{\top}=\mathbf{I}_{p}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}\mathbf{B}^{\top}=\mathbf{D},

(1)

where
\mathbf{D}
is a diagonal matrix with decreasing diagonal elements
\rho_{1}\geq\dots\geq\rho_{p}>0
, which correspond to the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}
and
\mathbf{B}
contains the corresponding eigenvectors as its rows.

This problem can be re-written as:

\boldsymbol{V}_{2}\mathbf{b}_{i}=\rho_{i}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}\mathbf{b}_{i}\\
\Leftrightarrow\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}\mathbf{b}_{i}=\rho_{i}\mathbf{b}_{i},\text{~for~}i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}.

(2)

with the following normalization:

•

\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}\mathbf{b}_{j}=0
for
i\neq j
and
\mathbf{b}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}\mathbf{b}_{j}=1
for
i=j
, with
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
,

•

\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}\mathbf{b}_{j}=0
for
i\neq j
and
\mathbf{b}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}\mathbf{b}_{j}=\rho_{j}
for
i=j
, with
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
.

Equivalently, as stated in
[
43
]
, the eigenvalues
\rho_{i}
, for
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
and the eigenvectors
\mathbf{b}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{p}
can also be sequentially defined by solving the successive maximization or minimization problems of the ratio:

\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{b})=\frac{\mathbf{b}^{\top}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{b}^{\top}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}\mathbf{b}},

(3)

where
\rho_{1}
is the maximal possible value of
\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{b})
over
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
which is achieved in the direction of the eigenvector
\mathbf{b}_{1}
.
The so-called invariant coordinates or components are then obtained as:
\boldsymbol{Z}_{n}=(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}-\mathbf{1}_{n}\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})^{\top})\boldsymbol{B}({\boldsymbol{X}_{n}})^{\top}
, where
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}=(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is a
p
-variate sample with
n
observations,
\mathbf{1}_{n}
denotes an
n
-variate vector full of ones, and
\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})
denotes a location estimator, usually the one that goes along with
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}
.

In practice, the requirement of positive definiteness for the two scatter matrices is limiting
and we focus on the case where at least one of these scatter matrices is singular. This is a common case since the variance-covariance matrix can be semi-definite positive as soon as some variables are collinear or in a high dimension low sample size context (HDLSS), i.e. if the number of variables exceeds the number of observations. Nowadays, more and more data are easily collected and collinearity issues arrive more frequently even when the number of observations is still higher than the number of observations. In this case, performing ICS is very challenging as we might not be able to compute: (i) one or the two scatter matrices and (ii) the inverse of
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}
as required to solve the GEP
2
. A simple idea is to perform a variable selection, if many variables are known to be non-relevant. However, this procedure can induce the deletion of a substantial number of variables to obtain a convenient number of dimensions on which both scatter estimators can be defined. And so, it can also lead to a potential loss of information.

The collinearity is a long-standing issue in multivariate analysis since quite a lot of methods rely on the simultaneous diagonalization of two or more scatter matrices
[
29
]
, for which the non-singularity of a scatter matrix is required. One of the most well-known methods that solves a GEP of two scatter matrices is
the classical Fisher Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) which maximizes the separation ratio of the between and the within-group covariance matrices
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{B}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{W}
.
In the case of collinearity, the rank of the between-class covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{B}
is equal to the number of groups, leading to a singular matrix. So, the maximization problem cannot be performed by solving the
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{W}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{B}
eigenvalue problem anymore. To overcome such an issue,
[
19
]
and
[
39
]
review some solutions even in case the within-group covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{W}
is also singular. Among others, the proposed approaches exploit the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse or the Generalized Singular Value Decomposition (GSVD). Another well-used approach is the dimension reduction through a singular value decomposition like in
[
22
]
or
[
12
]
. This rank reduction is also used for the LDA method in the HDLSS context, as explained by
[
19
]
and as a pre-processing step of ICS in
[
13
]
.
The objective of this paper is to adapt some of those approaches to generalizing ICS to the singularity issue, to investigate theoretically and practically their properties and to provide
R

[
33
]
implementations as well.

The structure of this paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce a definition of ICS with semi-definite positive scatter matrices and we present the challenges associated with such a context. In Section
3
we propose three approaches to adapting ICS to the case of semi-definite positive scatter estimates based on the Moore-Penrose pseudo inverse (GINV), the dimension reduction (DR) and the generalized singular value decomposition (GSVD). We also investigate theoretically their properties in terms of: (i) the criterion to optimize, (ii) the affine invariance of the scores and (iii) the symmetry of the roles of the two scatter estimates
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{V}_{2}
. Section
4
focuses on different empirical applications to infirm or confirm the theoretical aspects. Finally, Section
5

2.1
Scatter matrices

Let
\mathcal{P}_{p}
be the set of all symmetric positive definite matrices of order
p
,
\mathcal{SP}_{p}
be the set of all symmetric positive semi-definite matrices of order
p
. Generally, a scatter matrix is defined as a
p\times p
scatter matrix
\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})\in\mathcal{P}_{p}
which is affine equivariant in the sense that:

\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{\gamma}^{\top})=\mathbf{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})\mathbf{A},

where
\mathbf{A}
is a full rank
p\times p
matrix,
\mathbf{\gamma}
a
p
-vector and
\mathbf{1}_{n}
an
n
-vector full of ones. Among the most common ones there are the regular covariance matrix:

\mathrm{COV}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})^{\top},

where
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
denotes the empirical mean, and the so-called scatter matrix of fourth moments:

\mathrm{COV}_{4}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})=\frac{1}{(p+2)n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})^{\top},

where
r_{i}^{2}=(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{\top}\mathrm{COV}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})
is the classical squared Mahalanobis distance. It is well known that those two scatter matrices are not robust against non-normality and the presence of outliers. One of the most widely-used robust alternatives is the minimum covariance determinant estimator (
\mathrm{MCD}
)
[
34
]
. For a tuning parameter
\alpha\in[0.5,1]
, the
\mathrm{MCD}
selects out of the
n
observations those
n_{\alpha}=\lceil\alpha n\rceil
observations
\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{1}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{n_{\alpha}}}
for which the sample covariance matrix has the smallest determinant:

\mathrm{MCD}_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})=c_{\alpha}\frac{1}{n_{\alpha}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\alpha}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{j}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\alpha,n})(\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{j}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\alpha,n})^{\top},

where
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\alpha,n}
is the sample mean of the selected set of observations and
c_{\alpha}
is a consistency factor. To increase efficiency, it is often combined with a reweighting step, see for example
Hubert et al. 2018
for more details.

Theoretically, we can consider scatter matrices
\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})
which are only symmetric positive semi-definite. If
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}
is not of full rank, then
\mathrm{COV}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})\in\mathcal{SP}_{p}
. For robust scatter matrices based on a subset of observations lying on a subspace of lower dimension than the entire space,
\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})
belongs to
\mathcal{SP}_{p}
. This arises for example in the presence of outliers in the orthogonal complement subspace (OC outliers). In this context, we can extend the definition of a scatter matrix
\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})\in\mathcal{SP}_{p}
which is said to be affine equivariant in the sense that:

\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{\gamma}^{\top})=\mathbf{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})\mathbf{A},

for any
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{\gamma}
as previously defined.

lies in some

affine equivariant location and scatter statistics are essentially affine equivariant statistics defined on this hyperplane. For a

\mathcal{H}(M,m)=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}~|~\mathbf{M}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}=\mathbf{m}\}
, then for any affine equivariant scatter matrix
\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})
:
\mathbf{M}^{\top}\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{0}_{(p-r)\times(p-r)}
, where

denote the

\mathbf{0}_{(p-r)\times r}&\mathbf{0}_{(p-r)\times(p-r)}\end{pmatrix},

(4)

where

Another challenge is that many of the well-known robust affine equivariant scatter statistics such as the M-estimators

2.2
ICS as a generalized eigenvalue problem

With the common definition of the ICS method, the two scatter matrices
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{V}_{2}
should be definite positive to find a finite and nonzero eigenvalue
\rho
to the eigenproblem (
2
):
\boldsymbol{V}_{2}\mathbf{b}=\rho\boldsymbol{V}_{1}\mathbf{b}\Leftrightarrow\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}\mathbf{b}=\rho\mathbf{b}
. The positive definiteness of
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}
is required to compute its inverse whereas the positive definiteness of
\boldsymbol{V}_{2}
ensures nonzero eigenvalues.
If
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}
is singular and not proportional to
\boldsymbol{V}_{2}
, then the equivalence (
2
) is not true anymore since
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}
is not invertible. The problem can not be simplified to a non-symmetric eigenvalue problem (EVP) anymore and so, we have to solve the initial Generalized Eigenvalue Problem (GEP):

\boldsymbol{V}_{2}\mathbf{b}_{i}=\rho_{i}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}\mathbf{b}_{i}\qquad\text{for~}i\in\{1,\dots,p\},

(5)

with
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}\in\mathcal{SP}_{p}
and
\boldsymbol{V}_{2}\in\mathcal{SP}_{p}
which are not necessarily of full ranks. If
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}
and/or
\boldsymbol{V}_{2}
is singular, that means that the null spaces of the two scatter matrices are not empty and they do not necessarily span the same subspace. Concretely, in this context, solving the GEP of
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{V}_{2}
, leads to consider the following cases:

•

if
\mathbf{b}\in\mathrm{range}(\boldsymbol{V}_{1})\cap\mathrm{range}(\boldsymbol{V}_{2})
then
\rho\in\mathbb{R}^{+*}
,

•

if
\mathbf{b}\in\mathrm{null}(\boldsymbol{V}_{2})-\mathrm{null}(\boldsymbol{V}_{1})
then
\rho=0
,

•

if
\mathbf{b}\in\mathrm{null}(\boldsymbol{V}_{1})-\mathrm{null}(\boldsymbol{V}_{2})
then
\rho=\infty
,

•

if
\mathbf{b}\in\mathrm{null}(\boldsymbol{V}_{1})\cap\mathrm{null}(\boldsymbol{V}_{2})
then any
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
is a solution of the GEP. The corresponding eigenvectors are not well-defined and might cause stability issues for some algorithms. However, the structure of the data is not associated with those directions and so, we do not need to consider them further.

So, contrary to the classical ICS, the directions
\mathbf{b}
associated with infinite or zero eigenvalues should also be analyzed as they might highlight some of the structure of the data. Let us illustrate the challenges of considering semi-definite scatter matrices for ICS on one artificial data.

2.3
Challenges with singular scatter matrices: an illustrative example

Let
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{p})^{\top}
be a
p
-multivariate real random vector and assume the distribution of
\mathbf{X}
is a mixture of two Gaussian distributions with different covariance matrices:

{\mathbf{X}}\sim(1-\epsilon)\,{\cal N}\left(\boldsymbol{0}_{p},\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{W}_{1}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{pmatrix}\right)+\epsilon\,{\cal N}\left(\boldsymbol{0}_{p},\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{W}_{1}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{W}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right),

with
\epsilon<1/2
,
\boldsymbol{W}_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{r_{1}}
and
\boldsymbol{W}_{2}\in\mathcal{SP}_{p-r_{1}}
with
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{W}_{2})=r_{2}\leq p-r_{1}
.

Such a distribution illustrates a model containing two clusters: the majority of the data and a group that can be identified as outlying observations. The first cluster follows a Gaussian distribution such that the majority of the data is contained in a
r_{1}
-dimensional subspace spanned by the range of
\boldsymbol{W}_{1}
. The observations from the second cluster behave the same as previously on the
r_{1}
-dimensional subspace but they are also present in
r_{2}
directions not spanned by the majority of the data. The goal of the ICS method is to find this
r_{2}
-dimensional subspace where the observations of the second cluster are outlying. Here this subspace is spanned by the range of
\mathbf{W}_{2}
which is the orthogonal complement of the range of
\mathbf{W}_{1}
, i.e. the null space of
\mathbf{W}_{1}
.

Let us try to recover this subspace using the ICS method with a theoretical “perfectly robust” scatter functional
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{W}_{1}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{pmatrix}
and a theoretical “non-robust” scatter functional, the covariance of
\boldsymbol{X}
,
\boldsymbol{V}_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{W}_{1}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\epsilon\boldsymbol{W}_{2}\end{pmatrix}
. We have
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}\in\mathcal{SP}_{p}
and
\boldsymbol{V}_{2}\in\mathcal{SP}_{p}
, with
\mathrm{rank}(\boldsymbol{V}_{1})=r_{1}<\mathrm{rank}(\boldsymbol{V}_{2})\leq p
. In addition,
\mathrm{range}(\boldsymbol{V}_{1})=\mathrm{range}(\mathbf{W}_{1})
and
\mathrm{range}(\boldsymbol{V}_{2})=\mathrm{range}(\mathbf{W}_{1})\oplus\mathrm{range}(\mathbf{W}_{2}
).

Several of the aforementioned cases arise on this example. First, the intersection of the spaces spanned by the two scatter functionals
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{V}_{2}
corresponds to the
r_{1}
-dimensional subspace spanned by
\mathbf{W}_{1}
, so
r_{1}
nonzero eigenvalues should be found. Then, since
\mathrm{null}(\boldsymbol{V}_{1})-\mathrm{null}(\boldsymbol{V}_{2})\neq\{0\}
, a new direction associated to an
\infty
eigenvalue should also be analyzed. In fact, this is the one that reveals the outliers. Finally, if
\mathrm{rank}(\boldsymbol{V}_{2})<p
, then the two scatter functionals share a part of their null subspaces. This subspace is not important since it contains no structure. However, we consider this phenomenon in our analysis because it is common in practice that the data is not of full rank. In addition, this feature could also make some algorithms unstable.

Practically, to illustrate the model (
2.3
), we generate 1000 observations with exactly 20 outliers,
\boldsymbol{W}_{1}=\boldsymbol{I}_{2}
and
\boldsymbol{W}_{2}=\mathrm{diag}(2,0)
. On the left scatterplot matrix of the Figure
1
, we can see that the outliers represented by some blue triangles behave differently than the majority of the data only on the third variable. The subspace spanned by this third variable is the only one of interest to identify these observations as outliers.
This example can be seen as tricky since the outliers are well-identified on the third variable and no observations lie on the fourth one. So, we apply an affine transformation based on a non-singular
p\times p
particular Toeplitz matrix
\mathbf{A}
:

\mathbf{A}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{p-1}{p}&\frac{p-2}{p}&\frac{1}{p}\\
\frac{p-1}{p}&1&\frac{p-1}{p}&\frac{p-2}{p}\\
\frac{p-2}{p}&\frac{p-1}{p}&1&\frac{p-1}{p}\\
\frac{1}{p}&\frac{p-2}{p}&\frac{p-1}{p}&1\end{pmatrix},

to transform the initial data
\boldsymbol{X}
to
\boldsymbol{X}^{*}=\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{X}
. We can notice on the right scatterplot matrix of Figure
1
, that the outliers are no longer as well separated on the third transformed variable as they were initially. In addition, we are no longer able to see that the observations lie in a three-dimensional subspace. However, the structure of outlierness of the data is still contained in one dimension only. The challenge is to be able to recover the direction spanned by the outliers with ICS.

3
Implementation of ICS for semi-definite positive scatter matrices

Several methods exist to solve a Generalized Eigenvalue Problem. Among others, it exists the well-known QZ-algorithm introduced by
[
26
]
or the procedure described by
[
35
]
. However, in practice, solving a GEP of two scatter matrices with a common null space from a numerical point of view is particularly challenging. Indeed, the presence of this null space makes procedures like the well-known QZ-algorithm very unstable. So far, the available algorithms are not satisfactory as they might lead to complex and negative eigenvalues. For that reason, GEP is not directly solved in general and surrogate approaches are used in
[
39
]
. In this section, we investigate three theoretical and practical implementations of ICS based on Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse (GSVD) in Subsection
3.1
, dimension reduction (DR) in Subsection
3.2
and
generalized singular value decomposition (GSVD) in Subsection
3.3
. We focus on collinearity issue with
n>p
.

3.1
ICS with a Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse

If we assume that
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{V}_{2}
can be defined and computed on
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}
but that
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}\in\mathcal{SP}_{p}
with
\mathrm{rank}(\boldsymbol{V}_{1})=r_{1}<p
, then it is not possible to solve
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}
since
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}
is not invertible. Instead, we can replace the inverse of
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}
with its Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{+}
and solve:

\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{+}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}\mathbf{b}=\rho\mathbf{b},

(6)

where
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{+}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{\Lambda}^{+}\mathbf{P}^{\top}=\mathbf{P}_{1}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{r_{1}}^{-1}\mathbf{P}_{1}^{\top}
, with
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{r_{1}}^{-1}
containing only the inverse of the
r_{1}
nonzero eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}
,

\mathbf{P}
is an orthogonal matrix containing the eigenvectors of
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}
.
\mathbf{P}
can be partitioned as
\mathbf{P}=[\mathbf{P}_{1}~\mathbf{P}_{2}]
, with
\mathbf{P}_{1}
, the
p\times r_{1}
matrix containing the first
r_{1}
eigenvectors associated to the
r_{1}
nonzero eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}
, which is an orthonormal basis for the range space of
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}
. Similarly, the
p\times p-r_{1}
matrix
\mathbf{P}_{2}
spans the null space of
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}
.
\mathbf{P}_{1}
and
\mathbf{P}_{2}
are semi-orthogonal matrices such that:
\mathbf{P}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{1}=\mathbf{I}_{r_{1}}
and
\mathbf{P}_{2}^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{2}=\mathbf{I}_{p-r_{1}}
.

Property 1
.

Solving

Proof:
.

Instead of solving the non-symmetric EVP

(7)

(8)

\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{v}_{1}+\mathbf{v}_{0}\text{~~with~~}\mathbf{v}_{1}\in\mathrm{range}(\boldsymbol{V}_{1})\text{~~and~~}\mathbf{v}_{0}\in\mathrm{null}(\boldsymbol{V}_{1}),

\text{~~with~~}\mathbf{v}_{1}=\ \underset{\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\mathbf{b}\neq 0}{\mathrm{argmax}}\frac{\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{\mathbf{\mathrm{}}{Proj}}_{\boldsymbol{V}_{1}}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}\mathbf{\mathbf{\mathrm{}}{Proj}}_{\boldsymbol{V}_{1}}\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{1}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{r_{1}}\mathbf{P}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{b}}.

As

and expressed by
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}

3.2
ICS with a dimension reduction as pre-processing

Another well-known approach consists of getting rid of the singularity issues by doing a reduction of dimension (DR) first, hoping that no information about the data structure will be lost. The idea is to perform a Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) of the initial data and to project it onto the right-singular vectors associated with the non-zero singular values. Among others,
[
22
]
or
[
12
]
use this pre-processing step before applying their outlier detection algorithms based on PCA or Mahalanobis distances. This rank reduction is also used for the LDA method in the HDLSS context, as explained by
[
19
]
. However, the performance of the preprocessing for the LDA method relies on the rank of the covariance matrix which has to fall into a specific range to ensure that the new within-covariance becomes non-singular. Another pitfall is noted by
[
37
]
who advised against using an SVD before a robust PCA in the presence of OC outliers.

Considering the reduced data
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*}=\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\mathbf{P}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r_{\boldsymbol{X}_{n}}}
of rank
r_{\boldsymbol{X}_{n}}
, we have to solve the GEP of
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})
and
\boldsymbol{V}_{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})
:

\boldsymbol{V}_{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})\mathbf{b}=\rho\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})\mathbf{b},

(10)

where
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{P}^{\top}
with
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{P}
two
n~\times~n
and
p~\times~p
orthogonal matrices,
\mathbf{D}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\Delta}^{1/2}_{r_{\boldsymbol{X}_{n}}\times r_{\boldsymbol{X}_{n}}}&\mathbf{0}_{r_{\boldsymbol{X}_{n}}\times(p-r_{\boldsymbol{X}_{n}})}\\
\mathbf{0}_{(n-r_{\boldsymbol{X}_{n}})\times r_{\boldsymbol{X}_{n}}}&\mathbf{0}_{(n-r_{\boldsymbol{X}_{n}})\times(p-r_{\boldsymbol{X}_{n}})}\end{bmatrix}
. The elements of the diagonal matrix
\mathbf{\Delta}^{1/2}
are the square roots of the positive eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X}_{n}
. The columns of
\mathbf{P}
are also the eigenvectors of
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X}_{n}
and the columns of
\mathbf{U}
are the eigenvectors of
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{\top}
.
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{P}
can be partitioned as
\mathbf{U}=[\mathbf{U}_{1}~\mathbf{U}_{2}]
where
\mathbf{U}_{1}
is
n~\times~r_{\boldsymbol{X}_{n}}
,
\mathbf{U}_{2}
is
n~\times~(n-r_{\boldsymbol{X}_{n}})
and
\mathbf{P}=[\mathbf{P}_{1}~\mathbf{P}_{2}]
where
\mathbf{P}_{1}
is
p~\times~r_{\boldsymbol{X}_{n}}
and
\mathbf{P}_{2}
is
p~\times~(p-r_{\boldsymbol{X}_{n}})
.
\mathbf{U}_{1}
and
\mathbf{P}_{1}
are both semi-orthogonal matrices and
\mathbf{U}_{1}
(resp.
\mathbf{P}_{1}
) are orthonormal basis for the column space (resp. the row space) of
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}
.

Remark 5
.

If
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}
is of full rank.

If
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}
is of full rank then performing an SVD as a preprocessing step before ICS leads to the same components as if we directly compute the invariant coordinates (IC) from the initial data
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}
. Indeed, in this case,
\mathrm{rank}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})=r=p
, the data is transformed by a
\mathbf{P}
non-singular orthogonal
p\times p
matrix and it is known that the invariant coordinates are invariant by an orthogonal transformation. However, from a computational point of view, some numerical discrepancies can arise due to the additional step.

Remark 6
.

If
\mathrm{range}(\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*}))=\mathrm{range}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})
but
\mathrm{null}(\boldsymbol{V}_{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*}))-\mathrm{null}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})\neq\{0\}
.

If
\mathrm{range}(\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*}))=\mathrm{range}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})
, then the GEP (
10
) of
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})
and
\boldsymbol{V}_{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})
can be simplified to the classical EVP:
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})\mathbf{b}=\rho\mathbf{b}
.
However, doing the first step of dimension reduction and transforming the data onto
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*}
does not ensure the non-singularity of
\boldsymbol{V}_{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})
. So, it is possible to choose a
\boldsymbol{V}_{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})
of lower rank than
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}
. In this case, the solution can lead to some directions associated to a zero eigenvalue of multiplicity potentially greater than one.

Remark 7
.

If
\mathrm{null}(\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*}))-\mathrm{null}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})\neq\{0\}
.

If
\mathrm{null}(\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*}))-\mathrm{null}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})\neq\{0\}
, it means that
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})
is still singular and doing the first step of dimension reduction does not solve the problem.

Remark 8
.

So in this case, doing the pre-processing leads to an additional step to the method which is not needed and which implies the same drawbacks as doing ICS with a generalized inverse.

Remark 9
.

From a practical point of view, estimating the rank of
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}
might be very challenging as illustrated in Subsection
4.2
and lead to a loss of information regarding the structure of the data.

To conclude, the preprocessing step of dimension reduction does not fulfill all its promises. First, it cannot guarantee that it solves the singularity issues of the scatter matrices. Then, even if it does, if we choose
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}
as the variance-covariance matrix, we recover exactly the same modified criterion to solve as when we use the generalized inverse and so the same drawbacks. Finally, if we choose
\boldsymbol{V}_{2}
as the variance-covariance matrix, we might be unable to recover the structure of the data if it is only contained on the subspace spanned by
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}
.

5
Conclusion

Following previous ideas mainly used in LDA context, we proposed three new ways of generalizing ICS to the case of positive semi-definite scatter matrices based on generalized inverse, dimension reduction or GSVD. We also investigated their theoretical properties (summarized in Table
2
) and provided implementations in
R
.
Theoretically, the approach based on GSVD looks the most appealing by keeping all the nice properties of the classical ICS. In practice, this method can only deal with a specific type of scatter matrices that has to be expressed as crossproducts and being affine equivariant. Relaxing this affine invariance property of ICS, empirical results showed interesting and stable results for different situations for GSVD with
\mathrm{COV}-\mathrm{GCOV}_{4}
, even on standardized data. For DR, estimating the rank of the data appears to be the main challenge with quite some sensibility on the results. In addition, the method is only orthogonal invariant and does not ensure that some robust scatter matrices can be computed on the reduced data. Finally, GINV seems the least suitable as it requires that
V_{2}
can be computed on the initial data.

Overall, those methods allow to generalize ICS to the context of positive semi-definite scatter matrices and might be also helpful in the HDLSS case as long as the data are not in general position. Depending on the approach, it is important to think about which scatter should be the first one and if some OC outliers are present. In practice, it might be useful to perform ICS multiple times, exchanging
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{V}_{2}
and trying the different implementations to compare the results or performing localized projection pursuit after ICS as suggested by
[
11
]
. In the future, another idea could be to penalize or regularize ICS, as
[
14
]
or
[
44
]
do for LDA for example.

Appendix A.   Calculation details of the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse for Subsection
3.1

\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})^{+}
has to satisfy the four conditions to be a Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse:

•

Condition 1:
\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})^{+}\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})=\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})
.

•

Condition 2:
\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})^{+}\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})^{+}=\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})^{+}
.

•

Condition 3:
(\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})^{+})^{\top}=\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})^{+}
.

•

Condition 4:
(\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})^{+}\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*}))^{\top}=\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})^{+}\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})
.

The proof of conditions 1 and 2 can be generalized to any matrix
\mathbf{A}
but conditions 3 and 4 rely on the assumption of orthogonality of
\mathbf{A}
.
Indeed, for condition 3, we have
(\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})^{+})^{\top}=(\mathbf{A}^{\top})^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})^{+}\mathbf{A}^{\top}
. Since
\mathbf{A}
is orthogonal
(\mathbf{A}^{\top})^{-1}=\mathbf{A}
and
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})^{+}=\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})^{+}\mathbf{A}^{\top}
, so we obtained the desired equality:

(\mathbf{A}^{\top})^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})^{+}\mathbf{A}^{\top}=\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})^{+}\mathbf{A}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})^{+}.

The proof is similar for the condition 4.

Appendix B.   Affine invariance for ICS with a Generalized Singular Value Decomposition for Subsection
3.3

Proof:
.

(i) Adaptation of the proof from
[
43
]
, appendix A.1, for distinct roots.

Let
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*}=\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{\gamma}^{\top}
, with
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Then
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})=\mathbf{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})\mathbf{A}
and
\boldsymbol{V}_{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})=\mathbf{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})\mathbf{A}
.

By definition of ICS we have, for
i=1,\dots,p
:

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})\boldsymbol{V}_{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})\mathbf{b}_{i}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})

\displaystyle=\beta_{i}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})\mathbf{b}_{i}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*}),

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})\mathbf{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})\mathbf{A}\mathbf{b}_{i}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})

\displaystyle=\beta_{i}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})\mathbf{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})\mathbf{A}\mathbf{b}_{i}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*}).

\displaystyle\text{Multiplying by $(\mathbf{A}^{\top})^{-1}$:~~}\alpha_{i}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})\boldsymbol{V}_{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})\mathbf{A}\mathbf{b}_{i}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})

\displaystyle=\beta_{i}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})\mathbf{A}\mathbf{b}_{i}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*}).

If
\alpha_{i}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})/\beta_{i}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})
is a distinct root, then
\alpha_{i}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})/\beta_{i}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})=\alpha_{i}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})/\beta_{i}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})
and
\mathbf{b}_{i}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})\propto\mathbf{A}\mathbf{b}_{i}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})
, so
\mathbf{b}_{i}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})\propto\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}_{i}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})
:

\alpha_{i}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})\boldsymbol{V}_{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})\mathbf{b}_{i}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})=\beta_{i}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})\mathbf{b}_{i}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}).

Projection onto
\mathbf{b}_{i}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})
:

\mathbf{z}_{i}^{*}=\mathbf{b}_{i}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*})^{\top}\mathbf{x}^{*}=(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}_{i}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}))^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}_{i}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})^{\top}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{z}_{i}
.

(ii) Proof from
[
43
]
, appendix A.1, for multiple roots.

In case of a multiple root of multiplicity
p_{l}
, the eigenvectors are not uniquely defined and can be chosen as any linearly independent vectors spanning the corresponding
p_{l}
-dimensional eigenspace. However the roots are still the same and so the subspace spanned by the corresponding
p_{l}
-dimensional eigenspace is still the same.
∎

The case of multiple roots may appear when
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}\in\mathcal{SP}_{p}
and/or
\boldsymbol{V}_{2}\in\mathcal{SP}_{p}
as it means than
\mathrm{null}(\boldsymbol{V}_{1})\neq\{0\}
and/or
\mathrm{null}(\boldsymbol{V}_{2})\neq\{0\}
. For example, if we only focus on the cases where
\mathbf{B}\in\mathrm{null}(\boldsymbol{V}_{2})-\mathrm{null}(\boldsymbol{V}_{1})
or
\mathbf{B}\in\mathrm{null}(\boldsymbol{V}_{1})-\mathrm{null}(\boldsymbol{V}_{2})
, if
\dim(\mathrm{null}(\boldsymbol{V}_{2})-\mathrm{null}(\boldsymbol{V}_{1}))>1
and/or
\dim(\mathrm{null}(\boldsymbol{V}_{1})-\mathrm{null}(\boldsymbol{V}_{2}))>1
then
0
and/or
\infty
are multiple roots.
