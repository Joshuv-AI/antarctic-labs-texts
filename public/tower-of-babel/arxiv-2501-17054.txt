Generative diffusion models from a PDE perspective
Fei Cao, Kimball Johnston, Thomas Laurent, Justin Le, Sébastien Motsch

Abstract
Diffusion models have become the de facto framework for generating new datasets. The core of these models lies in the ability to reverse a diffusion process in time. The goal of this manuscript is to explain, from a PDE perspective, how this method works and how to derive the PDE governing the reverse dynamics as well as to study its solution analytically. By linking forward and reverse dynamics, we show that the reverse process’s distribution has its support contained within the original distribution. Consequently, diffusion methods, in their analytical formulation, do not inherently regularize the original distribution, and thus, there is no generalization principle. This raises a question: where does generalization arise, given that in practice it does occur? Moreover, we derive an explicit solution to the reverse process’s SDE under the assumption that the starting point of the forward process is fixed. This provides a new derivation that links two popular approaches to generative diffusion models: stable diffusion (discrete dynamics) and the score-based approach (continuous dynamics). Finally, we explore the case where the original distribution consists of a finite set of data points. In this scenario, the reverse dynamics are explicit (i.e., the loss function has a clear minimizer), and solving the dynamics fails to generate new samples: the dynamics converge to the original samples. In a sense, solving the minimization problem exactly is “too good for its own good” (i.e., an overfitting regime).

To face the curse of dimensionality, a different approach has emerged using (variational) auto-encoders. The idea is to first

However, the method may be prone to overfitting (e.g., the generated dataset could end up being a mere replication of the training data).

2
Reverse diffusion from PDE and SDE perspectives

2.1
A PDE approach to reverse diffusion

2.1.1
Derivation of the PDE

We would like to introduce and derive the reverse PDE dynamics used in diffusion models. We will adopt a PDE approach as opposed to the SDE approach frequently encountered in relevant literature
[
1
,
14
]
. An overview of the problem is given in figure
2
. There are two processes at play. First, the forward diffusion process transforms a density distribution
\rho_{0}
into a standard normal distribution
\mathcal{N}
:

\hskip-42.67912pt\begin{array}[]{c}\text{\bf Forward}\\
\text{\bf diffusion}\end{array}\quad\qquad\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}\rho=\nabla\cdot(x\rho)+\Delta\rho\\
\rho(t=0)=\rho_{0}.\end{array}\right.

(2.1)

This process is known as the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck equation (or the Langevin equation). The law
\rho
converges exponentially quickly toward the normal distribution under various metrics
[
2
]
. In practice, the forward diffusion is only solved up to a finite time
T_{*}
, so the resulting distribution
\rho(.,T_{*})
is only exponentially close to the equilibrium distribution
\mathcal{N}
.

Figure 2:
The forward process as a PDE (
2.1
) transforms any distribution
\rho_{0}
into a normal distribution
\mathcal{N}
. The reverse process does the opposite and transforms a normal distribution
\mathcal{N}
into (almost)
\rho_{0}
.

The second step is to
reverse
the dynamics to transform a normal distribution
\mathcal{N}
back into
\rho_{0}
. A naive way to carry out this step is simply to look at the evolution of
\rho(.,t)
in reverse time by introducing the distribution
q(.,s)
with

q(x,s)\coloneqq\rho(x,T_{*}-s)\quad\text{for }0\leq s\leq T_{*}.

(2.2)

The starting point
s=0
corresponds to the final time
t=T_{*}
. This distribution
q
satisfies

\hskip-42.67912pt\begin{array}[]{c}\text{\bf Unstable}\\
\text{\bf Reverse diffusion}\end{array}\quad\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{s}q=-\nabla\cdot(xq)-\Delta q\\
q(x,s=0)=\rho(x,T_{*}).\end{array}\right.

(2.3)

This PDE is ill-posed due to the anti-diffusion operator
-\Delta
(see figure
3
). To make the PDE stable while preserving the property (
2.2
), the key ingredient is to rewrite the diffusion process
\Delta
as a (non-linear) transport equation
[
7
]
:

\Delta\varphi=\nabla\cdot(\nabla\varphi)=\nabla\cdot(\varphi\nabla\log\varphi).

(2.4)

We then deduce

\Delta\varphi=2\nabla\cdot(\varphi\nabla\log\varphi)-\Delta\varphi.

(2.5)

If
q
satisfies (
2.2
), we obtain

-\Delta q(x,s)=-2\nabla\cdot(q(x,s)\nabla\log\rho(x,T_{*}-s))+\Delta q(x,s).

(2.6)

Figure 3:
Reversing the forward process (
2.1
) by reversing time in a naive way (
2.2
) yields an ill-posed problem (
2.3
) even though the curve
s\to\rho(x,T_{*}-s)
is a solution.
The
true
reverse dynamics (
2.7
) preserves this curve but also stabilizes the solution. As a consequence, the normal distribution
\mathcal{N}
is no longer an equilibrium distribution for the (non-autonomous) reverse dynamics.

This motivates the introduction of the reverse diffusion equation for
0\leq s\leq T_{*}
:

\begin{array}[]{c}\text{\bf Stable}\\
\text{\bf Reverse diffusion}\end{array}\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{s}q=\nabla\cdot\left(\left[-x-2\nabla\log\rho(.,T_{*}-s)\right]q\right)+\Delta q\\
q(x,s=0)=q_{0}(x).\end{array}\right.

(2.7)

In short, we have stabilized the unstable reverse diffusion (
2.3
) by adding twice the Laplace operator
\Delta
and removing twice the non-linear transport term. In particular, if we take
q_{0}(x)=\rho(x,T_{*})
, then
q(x,s)=\rho(x,T_{*}-s)
is the solution to the reverse diffusion (
2.7
).

2.1.2
Generalization

There are other ways to stabilize the unstable reverse diffusion (
2.3
). For instance, one can add the Laplace operator
\Delta
and remove the non-linear transport term just once each, leading to the following PDE:

\partial_{s}q=\nabla\cdot\big([-x-\nabla\log\rho(.,T_{*}-s)]q\big).

(2.8)

As there is no diffusion operator, the associated stochastic process will be deterministic (see further discussions in
[
18
]
).

Another generalization is to consider a different forward operator. Instead of using the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck equation (
2.1
), one can simply use a Brownian motion and its associated generator
\Delta
, leading to the following forward and reverse PDEs:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho=\Delta\rho,

(2.9)

\displaystyle\partial_{s}q=-\nabla\cdot\big(2\nabla\log\rho(.,T_{*}-s)\,q\big)+\Delta q.

(2.10)

Although this formulation simplifies the computations compared with the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck dynamics, there is nonetheless an additional requirement: one needs to introduce a bounded domain with boundary conditions so that the forward dynamics converges to a uniform equilibrium. Without the presence of a boundary, the solution to (
2.9
) will converge to the zero function
[
11
]
.

2.2.1
Forward SDE

In this subsection, we review the basic properties of the forward diffusion process. Consider a stochastic process
X_{t}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck dynamics:

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=-X_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t},

(2.11)

where
B_{t}
is the standard Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Using an integrating factor, we obtain the explicit solution

X_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{-t}\,X_{0}+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{e}^{-t}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{z}\,\mathrm{d}B_{z}\;\;\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{e}^{-t}X_{0},\sigma^{2}_{t}\text{Id}),

(2.12)

where
\mathcal{N}
is a Gaussian distribution and
\sigma^{2}_{t}\coloneqq 1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t}
. Thanks to this explicit formula, we can show the convergence of
X_{t}
toward the standard normal distribution
\mathcal{N}({\bf 0},\text{Id})
in various senses
[
2
]
.

The process
X_{t}
naturally induces an operator
L
(its
generator
)
[
10
,
25
]

L[\varphi](x)=-x\cdot\nabla\varphi(x)+\Delta\varphi(x).

(2.13)

In particular, for a given test function
\varphi
, if we consider the function

u(x,t)=\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X_{t})\mid X_{0}=x],

(2.14)

then it follows from Itô’s formula that
u
satisfies the Kolmogorov
backward
equation:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}u=L[u]\\
u(x,t=0)=\varphi(x).\end{array}\right.

(2.15)

Remark.

The terminology
backward
here might be confusing. The Kolmogorov
backward
equation (
2.15
) follows the process
X_{t}
, while the law
\rho(x,t)
of
X_{t}
satisfies the Kolmogorov
forward
equation:

\partial_{t}\rho=L^{*}[\rho],\qquad L^{*}[\varphi](x)\coloneqq\nabla\cdot\big(x\,\varphi(x)\big)+\Delta\varphi(x),

(2.16)

which is precisely (
2.1
). Both equations evolve
forward
in time.

To conclude our review of the forward process, we consider the process with a terminal condition at a fixed time
T_{*}
:

v(x,t)=\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X_{T_{*}})\mid X_{t}=x].

(2.17)

This satisfies the Feynman-Kac formula
[
10
]
:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}v=-L[v]\\
v(x,t=T_{*})=\varphi(x).\end{array}\right.

(2.18)

We will need this property to derive the SDE for the reserve process.

2.2.2
Reverse SDE

Now we are ready to derive the reverse diffusion equation (
2.7
) using stochastic processes. We consider the reverse (in time) trajectories

\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}=X_{T_{*}-s},

(2.19)

where
X_{t}
is the solution to the forward diffusion (
2.11
). Our goal is to find the generator associated with the processes
\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}
. The traditional approach to finding the generator is to consider

\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$u$}}$}(x,s)=\mathbb{E}[\varphi(\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s})\mid\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{0}=x]

(2.20)

and then take the time derivative. However, in this case, it is more convenient to consider the function with a terminal condition:

\displaystyle\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$v$}}$}(x,s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\varphi(\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{T_{*}})\,\mid\,\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}=x]

(2.21)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X_{0})\,\mid\,X_{t}=x],

(2.22)

using (
2.19

. Notice that we first have to fix the initial condition
\rho_{0}
for the law of
X_{0}

\rho(x,t_{1}\,;\,y,t_{2})=\text{``joint law of $X_{t_{1}}$ and $X_{t_{2}}$''}.

(2.23)

In particular,

\rho(x,0\,;\,y,t)=\rho(y,t\mid x,0)\cdot\rho_{0}(x).

(2.24)

We can now derive the equation satisfied by

\vec{\reflectbox{$v$}}

.

Proposition 2.1

The function
\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$v$}}$}(x,s)
(
2.21
) satisfies the following Kolmogorov backward equation:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{s}\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$v$}}$}=\left(-x-2\nabla\log\rho(x,T_{*}-s)\right)\cdot\nabla\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$v$}}$}-\Delta\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$v$}}$}\\
\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$v$}}$}(x,s=T_{*})=\varphi(x).\end{array}\right.

(2.25)

Proof.

Let’s start by writing

\vec{\reflectbox{$v$}}

using the joint law (
2.23
):

\displaystyle\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$v$}}$}(x,s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(y)\rho(y,0\mid x,t)\,\mathrm{d}y

(2.26)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(y)\rho(x,t\mid y,0)\frac{\rho_{0}(y)}{\rho(x,t)}\,\mathrm{d}y

(2.27)

using Bayes’ formula. This leads us to

\rho(x,t)\,\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$v$}}$}(x,s)=\int_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(y)\,\rho(x,t\mid y,0)\,\rho_{0}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

(2.28)

Taking the derivative with respect to
s
yields

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}\rho\,\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$v$}}$}\;+\;\rho\,\partial_{s}\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$v$}}$}=-\int_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(y)\,\partial_{t}\rho(x,t\mid y,0)\,\rho_{0}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

(2.29)

Using the fact that
\rho
satisfies the Kolmogorov forward equation (
2.16
), we obtain

\displaystyle-L^{*}[\rho]\,\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$v$}}$}\;+\;\rho\,\partial_{s}\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$v$}}$}=-\int_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(y)\,L^{*}[\rho(x,t\mid y,0)]\,\rho_{0}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

(2.30)

Using the identity (
2.28
), we arrive at

\displaystyle-L^{*}[\rho]\,\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$v$}}$}\;+\;\rho\,\partial_{s}\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$v$}}$}=-L^{*}[\rho\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$v$}}$}].

(2.31)

We then conclude thanks to Lemma
2.2
below.
∎

For any smooth functions

\displaystyle\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$u$}}$}(x,s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\varphi(\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s})\mid\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{0}=x]=\int_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(y)\,q(y,s\mid x,0)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\int_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(y)\,\rho(y,t\mid x,T_{*})\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(y)\,\rho(x,T_{*}\mid y,t)\frac{\rho(y,t)}{\rho(x,T_{*})}\,\mathrm{d}y

(3.16)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle s\to T_{*}}}{{\longrightarrow}}

\displaystyle\int_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(y)\rho(x,T_{*}\mid y,0)\frac{\rho_{0}(y)}{\rho(x,T_{*})}\,\mathrm{d}y.

(3.17)

This completes the proof of (

∎

As the law

is entirely explicit, i.e.,
\rho(.,T_{*}\mid y,0)\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{e}^{-T_{*}}y,\sigma^{2}_{T_{*}}\text{Id})

q(y,T_{*}\mid x,0)=\rho_{0}(y)\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{|y\mathrm{e}^{-T_{*}}-x|^{2}}{2\sigma_{T_{*}}^{2}}\right)}{\int_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho_{0}(z)\exp\left(-\frac{|z\mathrm{e}^{-T_{*}}-x|^{2}}{2\sigma_{T_{*}}^{2}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}z}.

(3.18)

Corollary 3.2

The law of
\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}
at the final time
s=T_{*}
is given by

q(y,T_{*})=\rho_{0}(y)\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(x,T_{*}\mid y,0)\frac{q_{0}(x)}{\rho(x,T_{*})}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(3.19)

Proof.

We simply notice that

q(y,T_{*})=\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}q(y,T_{*}\mid x,0)\,q_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

(3.20)

and then combine it with (

).
∎

We notice that if we choose
q_{0}(x)=\rho(x,T_{*})
, then we recover the initial distribution for the forward process, in the sense that

4
Reverse diffusion in practice

4.1
Explicit reverse SDE

We would like to provide a more practical expression of the reverse dynamics. Recall that the process
\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}
has its generator
R
given by (
2.35
). Consequently, we conclude that
\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}
is driven by the following Itô diffusion:

\mathrm{d}\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}=\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s+2\,\nabla\ln\rho\left(\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s},T_{*}-s\right)\,\mathrm{d}s+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s},

(4.1)

where
\nabla\ln\rho
is known as the
score

[
35
]
of the distribution
\rho
. To get a more explicit expression of the score
\nabla\ln\rho
, we consider the law of
X_{0}
conditioned on
X_{t}=x
and denote the expected value

\overline{\bf x}_{0}(x,t)=\mathbb{E}\left[X_{0}\mid X_{t}=x\right].

(4.2)

In analogy with the method of characteristics encountered in the study of transport-type PDEs,
\overline{\bf x}_{0}
represents the average “foot” of the characteristics passing through
x
at time
t
(see figure
7
for an illustration). Using the pairwise distribution
\rho(.\mid.)
(
2.23
), we can express
\overline{\bf x}_{0}
explicitly and deduce an expression for the score.

Lemma 4.1

If
X_{0}\sim\rho_{0}
, we can recast
\overline{\bf x}_{0}
as

\overline{\bf x}_{0}(x,t)=\frac{\int_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}y\,\rho(x,t\mid y,0)\,\rho_{0}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y}{\rho(x,t)},

(4.3)

and the score
\nabla\ln\rho
as:

\nabla\ln\rho(x,t)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-t}\,\overline{\bf x}_{0}(x,t)-x}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t}}.

(4.4)

Proof.

By Bayes’ rule, we have

\rho(y,0\mid x,t)=\frac{\rho(x,t\mid y,0)\,\rho_{0}(y)}{\rho(x,t)}.

We then apply this equality in the definition of
\overline{\bf x}_{0}
:

\overline{\bf x}_{0}(x,t)=\mathbb{E}\left[X_{0}\mid X_{t}=x\right]=\int_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}y\,\rho(y,0\mid x,t)\,\mathrm{d}y=\frac{\int_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}y\,\rho(x,t\mid y,0)\,\rho_{0}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y}{\rho(x,t)}.

Since
\rho(x,t\mid y,0)
is an explicit Gaussian density, we deduce:

\nabla_{x}\rho(x,t\mid y,0)=\rho(x,t\mid y,0)\cdot\left(-\frac{x-\alpha_{t}y}{\beta_{t}}\right)

(4.5)

with
\alpha_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{-t}
and
\beta_{t}=1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t}
. Therefore we can rewrite the score
\nabla\ln\rho
as follows:

\displaystyle\nabla\ln\rho(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\nabla\rho(x,t)}{\rho(x,t)}=\frac{\int_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla_{x}\rho(x,t\mid y,0)\,\rho_{0}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y}{\rho(x,t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(x,t\mid y,0)\left(-\frac{x-\alpha_{t}y}{\beta_{t}}\right)\,\rho_{0}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y}{\rho(x,t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\frac{x}{\beta_{t}}\int_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(x,t\mid y,0)\,\rho_{0}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y+\frac{\alpha_{t}}{\beta_{t}}\int_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}y\,\rho(x,t\mid y,0)\,\rho_{0}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y}{\rho(x,t)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{x}{\beta_{t}}+\frac{\alpha_{t}}{\beta_{t}}\,\overline{\bf x}_{0}(x,t).

∎

Thanks to the previous lemma, we can express the reverse diffusion process in terms of
\overline{\bf x}_{0}
.

Proposition 4.2

The reverse diffusion dynamics (
4.1
) can be rewritten as

\mathrm{d}\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}=\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s+2\,\frac{\alpha_{t}\,\overline{\bf x}_{0}\left(\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s},t\right)-\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}}{\beta_{t}}\,\mathrm{d}s+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s},

(4.6)

with
t=T_{*}-s
,
\alpha_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{-t}
,
\beta_{t}=1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t}
and
\overline{\bf x}_{0}
given by (
4.2
).

Figure 7:
Solving the reverse diffusion written as a SDE (

\overline{\bf x}_{0}
(
4.2

Remark.

When
t\gg 1
is large, the reverse diffusion reduces to the forward diffusion process (

\mathrm{d}\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle t\gg 1}}{{\approx}}\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s+2\,\frac{{\bf 0}-\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}}{1}\,\mathrm{d}s+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}\;=\;-\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}.

(4.7)

However, when

\mathrm{d}\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle t\sim 0}}{{\approx}}\frac{1}{t}\left(\overline{\bf x}_{0}-\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}\right)\,\mathrm{d}s+\mathcal{O}(1)\,\mathrm{d}s+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s},

and

is constant, then this SDE can be explicitly solved by applying the Itô’s formula to

\mathrm{d}\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}=\frac{1}{t}\left(\overline{\bf x}_{0}-\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}\right)\,\mathrm{d}s+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}=\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{0}+\frac{t}{T_{*}}(\overline{\bf x}_{0}-\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{0})+Z_{s}

. Under these simplications, the process
\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}
converges linearly to
\overline{\bf x}_{0}
as

(i.e.,

\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{0}
and

Remark.

We can also rewrite the reverse diffusion process in ways that involve hyperbolic trigonometric functions. For instance, we have

\mathrm{d}\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}=\frac{1}{\tanh(T_{*}-s)}\,\left(\frac{\overline{\bf x}_{0}\left(\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s},T_{*}-s\right)}{\cosh(T_{*}-s)}-\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}\right)\,\mathrm{d}s+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}.

(4.10)

Using the identity

\text{csch}(x)=\frac{1}{\sinh(x)}

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}

\displaystyle=-\tanh\left(\frac{T_{*}-s}{2}\right)\,\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s+\text{csch}(T_{*}-s)\left(\overline{\bf x}_{0}\left(\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s},T_{*}-s\right)-\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}\right)\,\mathrm{d}s

(4.11)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}.

4.2
Simplified case:
\rho_{0}
Dirac (unique sample)

The explicit reverse dynamics (
4.6
) is in general nonlinear and therefore not explicitly solvable. However, if we suppose that all the forward trajectories

originate from the same origin

(i.e.,
\rho_{0}=\delta_{x_{0}}

\overline{\bf x}_{0}

Theorem 2

If
X_{0}=x_{0}
is fixed (i.e., if
\rho_{0}=\delta_{x_{0}}
for some
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
), then

\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}=\frac{\sinh(T_{*}-s)}{\sinh(T_{*})}\,\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{0}+\frac{\sinh(s)}{\sinh(T_{*})}\,x_{0}+\sqrt{2\sinh(T_{*}-s)\frac{\sinh(s)}{\sinh(T_{*})}}\,\epsilon,

where
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}({\bf 0},\text{Id})
is a standard Gaussian random vector in dimension
d

Proof.

The proof is based on the search for a suitable integrating factor. To begin with, we define
u(s)=-\ln\left(\frac{\sinh(T_{*}-s)}{\sinh(T_{*})}\right)
and thus
\mathrm{e}^{u(s)}=\frac{\text{csch}(T_{*}-s)}{\text{csch}(T_{*})}
.
Multiplying both sides of the SDE (
4.11
) by
\mathrm{e}^{u(s)}
and rearranging the resulting equation, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{u(s)}\,\mathrm{d}\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}+\mathrm{e}^{u(s)}\,\left(\tanh\left(\frac{T_{*}-s}{2}\right)+\text{csch}(T_{*}-s)\right)\,\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\mathrm{e}^{u(s)}\,\text{csch}(T_{*}-s)\,\overline{\bf x}_{0}\left(\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s},T_{*}-s\right)\,\mathrm{d}s+\mathrm{e}^{u(s)}\,\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}.

Since

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\left(\mathrm{e}^{u(s)}\right)=\mathrm{e}^{u(s)}\,\left(\tanh\left(\frac{T_{*}-s}{2}\right)+\text{csch}(T_{*}-s)\right),

we have

\mathrm{d}\left(\mathrm{e}^{u(s)}\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}\right)=\mathrm{e}^{u(s)}\,\text{csch}(T_{*}-s)\,\overline{\bf x}_{0}\left(\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s},T_{*}-s\right)\,\mathrm{d}s+\mathrm{e}^{u(s)}\,\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}.

Thus

\mathrm{e}^{u(s)}\,\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}=e^{u(0)}\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{0}+\int_{0}^{s}\mathrm{e}^{u(r)}\,\text{csch}(T_{*}-r)\,\overline{\bf x}_{0}\left(\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{r},T_{*}-r\right)\,\mathrm{d}r+\int_{0}^{s}\mathrm{e}^{u(r)}\,\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{r}.

(4.13)

As
X_{0}=x_{0}
is fixed by our assumption,
\overline{\bf x}_{0}\left(\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{r},T_{*}-r\right)=x_{0}
, and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s}\mathrm{e}^{u(r)}\,\text{csch}(T_{*}-r)\,\overline{\bf x}_{0}\left(\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{r},T_{*}-r\right)\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{s}\frac{\text{csch}^{2}(T_{*}-r)}{\text{csch}(T_{*})}\,x_{0}\,\mathrm{d}r

(4.14)

\displaystyle=\frac{\coth(T_{*}-s)-\coth(T_{*})}{\text{csch}(T_{*})}\,x_{0}.

Inserting (
4.14
) into (

) and rearranging, we find

\displaystyle\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}=\mathrm{e}^{-u(s)}\,\mathrm{e}^{u(0)}\,\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{0}+\mathrm{e}^{-u(s)}\,\frac{\coth(T_{*}-s)-\coth(T_{*})}{\text{csch}(T_{*})}\,x_{0}+\mathrm{e}^{-u(s)}\,\int_{0}^{s}\mathrm{e}^{u(r)}\,\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{r}

(4.15)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sinh(T_{*}-s)}{\sinh(T_{*})}\,\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{0}+\left(\frac{\sinh(T_{*}-s)}{\sinh(T_{*})}\right)\left(\frac{\coth(T_{*}-s)-\coth(T_{*})}{\text{csch}(T_{*})}\right)\,x_{0}

\displaystyle+\frac{\sinh(T_{*}-s)}{\sinh(T_{*})}\,\int_{0}^{s}\mathrm{e}^{u(r)}\,\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sinh(T_{*}-s)}{\sinh(T_{*})}\,\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{0}+\sinh(T_{*}-s)\,\left(\coth(T_{*}-s)-\coth(T_{*})\right)\,x_{0}

\displaystyle+\frac{\sinh(T_{*}-s)}{\sinh(T_{*})}\,\int_{0}^{s}\mathrm{e}^{u(r)}\,\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{r}.

Notice that

, from which we arrive at the following simplified expression for the variance of (each independent component of) the Itô integral in (

\displaystyle\frac{\sinh^{2}(T_{*}-s)}{\sinh^{2}(T_{*})}\,\int_{0}^{s}2\,\mathrm{e}^{2\,u(r)}\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=2\,\frac{\sinh^{2}(T_{*}-s)}{\sinh^{2}(T_{*})}\,\int_{0}^{s}\frac{\text{csch}^{2}(T_{*}-s)}{\text{csch}^{2}(T)}\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=2\,\left(\frac{\sinh^{2}(T_{*}-s)}{\sinh^{2}(T_{*})}\right)\left(\frac{\coth(T_{*}-s)-\coth(T_{*})}{\text{csch}^{2}(T_{*})}\right)

4.3
Score-matching & stable diffusion

From the SDE (
4.6
), we would like to derive a numerical scheme to update
\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}
at discrete times. The main challenge is to estimate
\overline{\bf x}_{0}
(

\rho_{0}
is unknown (we only have access to the sample
\{{\bf x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}

in

Let’s denote a partition

with

. The goal is to find a recursive formula to express
\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s_{n+1}}
as a function of
\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s_{n}}

[

to the reverse SDE (
4.6

\begin{array}[]{c}{\bf Euler}\\
{\bf Maruyama}\end{array}{\bf:}\quad\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s_{n+1}}=\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s_{n}}+\Delta s_{n}\cdot\left(\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s_{n}}+2\,\frac{\alpha_{t_{n}}\,\overline{\bf x}_{0}(\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s_{n}},t_{n})-\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s_{n}}}{\beta_{t_{n}}}\right)+\sqrt{\Delta s_{n}}\,\epsilon_{n}

(4.19)

with
t_{n}=T_{*}-s_{n}
,
\alpha_{t_{n}}=\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n}}
,
\beta_{t_{n}}=1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t_{n}}
and
\epsilon_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bf 0,\text{Id})
.

The Euler-Maruyama scheme assumes that the right hand side of the SDE (
4.6
) is frozen over the time interval
[s_{n},s_{n+1}]
. A more subtle method is to assume only
\overline{\bf x}_{0}
is constant over the time interval
[s_{n},s_{n+1}]
. Denoting this constant value by
\langle\overline{\bf x}_{0}\rangle

) into a linear SDE as in (

\mathrm{d}\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}=-\tanh\left(\frac{t}{2}\right)\,\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s+\text{csch}(t)\left(\langle\overline{\bf x}_{0}\rangle-\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}\right)\,\mathrm{d}s+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}.

(4.20)

Then we can apply the same strategy as in Theorem
2
to integrate exactly over the time interval
[s_{n},s_{n+1}]
via integrating factor:

\begin{array}[]{c}{\bf Stable}\\
{\bf Diffusion}\end{array}{\bf:}\quad\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s_{n+1}}=\frac{\sinh(t_{n+1})}{\sinh(t_{n})}\,\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s_{n}}+\frac{\sinh(\Delta s_{n})}{\sinh(t_{n})}\,\langle\overline{\bf x}_{0}\rangle\\
\\
\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt+\;\sqrt{2\sinh(t_{n+1})\frac{\sinh(\Delta s_{n})}{\sinh(t_{n})}}\,\epsilon_{n},\end{array}

(4.21)

with
t_{n}=T_{*}-s_{n}
(notice
t_{n+1}<t_{n}
). We emphasize that the formula (
4.21
) is exact only because we have assumed that
\overline{\bf x}_{0}
is constant over the time interval
[s_{n},s_{n+1}]
.

The recursive formula (
4.21
) appears quite convoluted, but it corresponds exactly to the formula used in the stable diffusion
[
15
]
. Indeed, the derivation of the recursive formula for
\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}
is also exact when we assume
\overline{\bf x}_{0}
is given.

Proposition 4.3

The recursive formula (
4.21
) is equivalent to

\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s_{n+1}}=\mu_{n+1}+\sigma_{n+1}\epsilon_{n},

(4.22)

with
\epsilon_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bf 0},\text{Id})
a standard Gaussian and

\displaystyle\mu_{n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\Delta s_{n}}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t_{n+1}})}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t_{n}}}\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{n}+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n+1}}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\Delta s_{n}})}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t_{n}}}\langle\overline{\bf x}_{0}\rangle,

(4.23)

\displaystyle\sigma_{n+1}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t_{n+1}})(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\Delta s_{n}})}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t_{n}}}.

(4.24)

One can replace
\Delta s_{n}
by
|\Delta t_{n}|
since
|\Delta t_{n}|=|t_{n+1}-t_{n}|=|-s_{n+1}+s_{n}|=\Delta s_{n}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality and for the sake of notational simplicity, we work with
d=1
. Let us start from the formula (
4.21
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s_{n+1}}\mid\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s_{n}}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sinh(t_{n+1})}{\sinh(t_{n})}\,\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s_{n}}+\frac{\sinh(\Delta s_{n})}{\sinh(t_{n})}\,\langle\overline{\bf x}_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{e}^{t_{n}-\Delta s_{n}}-\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n}+\Delta s_{n}}}{\mathrm{e}^{t_{n}}-\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n}}}\,\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s_{n}}+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\Delta s_{n}}-\mathrm{e}^{-\Delta s_{n}}}{\mathrm{e}^{t_{n}}-\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n}}}\langle\overline{\bf x}_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\Delta s_{n}}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t_{n}+2\Delta s_{n}})}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t_{n}}}\,\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s_{n}}+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n}}\mathrm{e}^{\Delta s_{n}}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\Delta s_{n}})}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t_{n}}}\langle\overline{\bf x}_{0}\rangle,

\displaystyle\text{Var}[\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s_{n+1}}\mid\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s_{n}}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sinh(t_{n+1})\frac{\sinh(\Delta s_{n})}{\sinh(t_{n})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathrm{e}^{t_{n+1}}-\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n+1}})\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\Delta s_{n}}-\mathrm{e}^{-\Delta s_{n}}}{\mathrm{e}^{t_{n}}-\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{e}^{t_{n+1}}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t_{n+1}})\mathrm{e}^{\Delta s_{n}}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\Delta s_{n}})}{\mathrm{e}^{t_{n}}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t_{n}})}.

∎

4.4
Approximative flow and loss function

Whether we want to solve the reverse dynamics (
4.6
) (score-matching) or to invert the forward process (
4.22
) (stable diffusion), the main difficulty relies on the estimation of the score
\nabla\ln\rho(x,t)
or equivalently
\overline{\bf x}_{0}
(
4.3
). Since
\rho_{0}
is unknown, the explicit formula for
\overline{\bf x}_{0}
(
4.3
) cannot be used. Instead, the usual approach
[
35
]
is to train a neural network (or any type of parametrized function), denoted by
{\bf s}_{\theta}
, to approximate the score
\nabla\ln\rho
:

{\bf s}_{\theta}(x,t)\approx\nabla\ln\rho(x,t)\qquad\text{for }0\leq t\leq T_{*},\,\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(4.25)

The parameters
\theta
are then obtained by minimizing the mean-square error over the trajectories:

\ell[\theta]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\sim U([0,T_{*}])\\
X_{t}\sim\rho_{t}\end{subarray}}\Big[\big|{\bf s}_{\theta}(X_{t},t)-\nabla\ln\rho(X_{t},t)\big|^{2}\Big],

(4.26)

The explicit expression for
\nabla\ln\rho
(
4.4
) motivates the following “Ansatz” for
{\bf s}_{\theta}
:

{\bf s}_{\theta}(x,t)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-t}\overline{\bf x}_{\theta}(x,t)-x}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t}}

(4.27)

where now the neural network
\overline{\bf x}_{\theta}
aims to approximate
\overline{\bf x}_{0}
. Then the loss (
4.26
) becomes

\ell[\theta]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\sim U([0,T_{*}])\\
X_{t}\sim\rho_{t}\end{subarray}}\Big[c^{2}(t)\big(\overline{\bf x}_{\theta}(X_{t},t)-\overline{\bf x}_{0}(X_{t},t)\big)^{2}\Big]\hskip 11.49994pt\text{ with }c(t)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-t}}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t}}.

(4.28)

Given that
\overline{\bf x}_{0}
is already an expectation (
4.2
), we can use the bias-variance decomposition to rewrite the loss as:

\ell[\theta]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\sim U([0,T_{*}])\\
X_{0}\sim\rho_{0}\end{subarray}}\Big[c^{2}(t)\big(\overline{\bf x}_{\theta}(X_{t},t)-X_{0}\big)^{2}\Big]\;-\;\mathop{\text{Var}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\sim U([0,T_{*}])\\
X_{0}\sim\rho_{0}\end{subarray}}[c(t)\overline{\bf x}_{0}(X_{t},t)],

(4.29)

where
X_{t}
is given by the explicit formula (
2.12
). Since the variance of
\overline{\bf x}_{0}
is independent of
\theta
, we can ignore it in the minimization and consider only the lefthand side (the
total loss
):

\ell_{total}[\theta]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\sim U([0,T_{*}])\\
X_{0}\sim\rho_{0}\end{subarray}}\Big[c^{2}(t)\big(\overline{\bf x}_{\theta}(X_{t},t)-X_{0}\big)^{2}\Big].

(4.30)

Notice that the loss
\ell_{total}
is lower bounded by the variance term in (
4.29
), hence
\ell_{total}
cannot converge to zero regardless of the neural network used for
\overline{\bf x}_{\theta}
.

Another modification proposed in
[
15
]
is to use the explicit solution of the forward process (
2.12
) to write
X_{0}
as a function of
X_{t}
:

X_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{-t}X_{0}+\sqrt{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t}}\,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{0}\;\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;\;X_{0}=\mathrm{e}^{t}X_{t}-\mathrm{e}^{t}\sqrt{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t}}\,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{0},

(4.31)

where
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\text{Id})
. Thus, we can use another Ansatz to predict
X_{0}
:

\overline{\bf x}_{\theta}(X_{t},t)=\mathrm{e}^{t}X_{t}-\mathrm{e}^{t}\sqrt{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t}}\,\overline{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{\theta}(X_{t},t)

(4.32)

where now the neural network
\overline{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{\theta}
aims to predict the added noise
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{0}
to the original sample. Plugging in the previous formula into the total loss
\ell_{total}

4.30
gives:

\ell_{total}[\theta]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\sim U([0,T_{*}])\\
X_{0}\sim\rho_{0}\\
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\text{Id})\end{subarray}}\Big[\frac{1}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t}}\big(\overline{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{\theta}(X_{t},t)-\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{0}\big)^{2}\Big],

(4.33)

where
X_{t}
is given by (
4.31
). Notice that the weight
1/(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t})
is singular as
t\to 0
. To avoid the singularity, the integral is replaced by a finite (Riemann) sum with a non-uniform discretization in time
0=t_{0}<...<t_{m_{*}}=T_{*}
:

\widetilde{\ell}_{total}[\theta]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}X_{0}\sim\rho_{0}\\
k\in[1,m_{*}]\end{subarray}}\Big[\big(\overline{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{\theta}(X_{t_{k}},t_{k})-\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{0}\big)^{2}\Big],

(4.34)

where the discretization
\Delta t_{k}=t_{k+1}-t_{k}
is smaller as
t
gets close to zero, hence more weight is given to the loss for
t
close to zero.

After minimization, the score
{\bf s}_{\theta}(x,t)
is computed in the following three steps:

i)

Predict the noise level:
\widetilde{\varepsilon}=\overline{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{\theta}(x,t)
,

ii)

Deduce the expectated origin:
\widetilde{x}_{0}=\mathrm{e}^{t}x-\mathrm{e}^{t}\sqrt{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t}}\,\widetilde{\varepsilon}
,

iii)

Reconstruct the score:
\displaystyle{\bf s}_{\theta}(x,t)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-t}\widetilde{x}_{0}-x}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t}}
.

Notice that there is no need to reconstruct the score
{\bf s}_{\theta}
if one resorts to the stable diffusion formulation (
4.22
).

4.5
Overfitting regime and kernel formulation

Whichever loss function we use (i.e.
\ell
(
4.26
),
\ell_{total}
(
4.30
), or
\widetilde{\ell}_{total}
(
4.34
)), the minimizer is explicitly given by
\overline{\bf x}_{0}
(
4.2
) as the expected value is the unique minimizer of the mean square error. The parametric solution
{\bf s}_{\theta}
can only approach the non-parametric solution
\overline{\bf x}_{0}
and, in some sense,
\overline{\bf x}_{0}
is the optimal limit of
{\bf s}_{\theta}
as the neural network gets infinitely large.

However, the explicit formulation for
\overline{\bf x}_{0}
(
4.3
) cannot be used as the density
\rho_{0}
is unknown. The same problem occurs when estimating the different loss functions previously introduced. In the absence of
\rho_{0}
, the sampling
X_{0}\sim\rho_{0}
in the loss (
4.34
) is replaced in practice by a uniform sampling over the known samples
\{{\bf x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. In other words, the empirical risk is used. However, in this specific case, one can compute
\overline{\bf x}_{0}
explicitly, leading to a kernel formulation (note that similar formulations were found in
[
13
]
and
[
40
]
).

Proposition 4.4

Suppose the initial distribution
\rho_{0}
is uniform over the samples
\{{\bf x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, i.e.,

\rho_{0}(x)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{{\bf x}_{i}}(x),

(4.35)

where
\delta_{x}
is the Dirac mass centered at
x
. Then the expected value
\overline{\bf x}_{0}
(
4.2
) is explicit and given by the following kernel formulation:

\overline{\bf x}_{0}(x,t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}(x,t)\,{\bf x}_{i},

(4.36)

where
\{\lambda_{i}(x,t)\}_{i=1}^{N}
is a convex combination given by

\lambda_{i}(x,t)=\frac{\rho\left(x,t\mid{\bf x}_{i},0\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\rho\left(x,t\mid{\bf x}_{j},0\right)}

(4.37)

with
\rho\left(.,t\mid y,0\right)\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-t}y,\beta_{t}^{2}\text{Id}\right)
and
\beta_{t}^{2}=1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t}
.

Proof.

Plugging in the expression of
\rho_{0}
(
4.35
) into (
4.3
) and then using the fact that

\rho(x,t)=\int_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(x,t\mid y,0)\,\rho_{0}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y

(4.38)

finishes the proof.
∎

The weight
\lambda_{i}(x,t)
(
4.37
) encodes the probability that
X_{0}={\bf x}_{i}
given that
X_{t}=x
. An alternative handy expression of the vector
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N}
) is given by

\boldsymbol{\lambda}(x,t)=\textrm{Softmax}\left(-\frac{|x-\mathrm{e}^{-t}\,{\bf x}_{1}|^{2}}{2\beta_{t}^{2}},\ldots,-\frac{|x-\mathrm{e}^{-t}\,{\bf x}_{N}|^{2}}{2\beta_{t}^{2}}\right),

(4.39)

with
Softmax
representing the usual softmax function. Notice that as
t\gg 1
, the weights
\lambda_{i}
become uniform, i.e.,

\boldsymbol{\lambda}(x,t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle t\to+\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\left(\frac{1}{N},\dots,\frac{1}{N}\right),

(4.40)

leading
\overline{\bf x}_{0}
(
4.36
) to become the mean of the samples
\{{\bf x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. On the other hand, as
t\to 0
, the vector
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
becomes (almost surely) a one-hot vector:

\boldsymbol{\lambda}(x,t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle t\to 0}}{{\longrightarrow}}(0,\dots,0,1,0,\dots,0)

(4.41)

where the value
1
indicates the closest sample
{\bf x}_{i}
to
x
. We can further formalize this observation by introducting the Voronoi tessellation
\{\mathcal{C}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
associated with the samples
\{{\bf x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
:

\mathcal{C}_{i}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid|x-{\bf x}_{i}|=\min(|x-{\bf x}_{1}|,\dots,|x-{\bf x}_{N}|)\}.

(4.42)

Then as
t\to 0
, the expected value
\overline{\bf x}_{0}
becomes constant on the (interior of the) Voronoi cells
C_{i}
:

\overline{\bf x}_{0}(x,t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle t\to 0}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\bf x}_{i}\hskip 11.49994pt\text{for }x\in\mathring{C}_{i}.

(4.43)

Moreover, the reverse diffusion becomes a stiff relaxation as
t\to 0
(
4.8
). Thus, for
\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}\in\mathring{\mathcal{C}}_{i}
,

\mathrm{d}\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle t\sim 0}}{{\approx}}\frac{1}{t}\left({\bf x}_{i}-\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}\right)\,\mathrm{d}s+\mathcal{O}(1)\,\mathrm{d}s+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}.

(4.44)

As
t\to 0
, the process
\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}
becomes more attracted to
{\bf x}_{i}
and is more likely to stay inside the Voronoi cell
\mathring{\mathcal{C}}_{i}
.

The previous observations are rather informal. The Corollary
3.3
provides a more clear statement: as the support of
\rho_{0}
consists of the discrete sample points, the reverse process will eventually converge to one of the original sample (see figure
10
for an illustration):

\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle s\to T_{*}}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\bf x}_{i}.

(4.45)

Thus, solving the minimization problem
exactly
is counter-productive: the generative flow does nothing but sample from the original samples. Hence, there is an inherent risk of overfitting regime when applying the reverse diffusion dynamics since there is no inherent regularization unless penalization terms are added.

Starting from a normal distribution (right), the reverse process
\reflectbox{$\vec{\reflectbox{$X$}}$}_{s}
becomes trapped into a Voronoi cell
\mathcal{C}_{i}
(
4.42
) as
t\to 0
and eventually converges to a sample
{\bf x}_{i}
as stated by Corollary
3.3
.

5
Conclusion

In this manuscript, we have shown that reverse diffusion, if executed exactly, does not regularize the original sample distribution
\rho_{0}
. Indeed, by perfectly reconstructing the forward process, the resulting generated distribution
q_{T_{*}}
(
2.7
) has its support entirely contained within the support of
\rho_{0}
(corollary
3.3
). In other words, generated samples cannot be “out-of-the-sample.” Consequently, for a finite number of samples, exact reverse diffusion will merely reproduce one of the original samples.

In practice, score-matching or stable diffusion (
4.22
)-(
4.24
) does not precisely follow the reverse diffusion process because it introduces a minimization problem (
4.26
) that is not solved exactly. However, it is relatively straightforward to derive an explicit formula (e.g., using the kernel formulation in Proposition (
4.35
)) for the minimization problem. That said, this computation becomes impractical when the number of original samples
N
is large. This leads to an apparent paradox: when the minimization problem is solved too accurately, the generative method closely approximates the reverse diffusion process, and thus no new samples are generated (see, e.g.,
[
27
]
for a condition under which no generalization occurs). Paradoxically, the minimization problem must be “imperfect” for the generative method to produce novel samples.

A simple extension of our work would involve demonstrating that (discrete) stable diffusion, in the overfitting regime, indeed converges to the original samples. While we have established this result for the continuous reverse diffusion formulation as a consequence of Corollary
3.3
, it remains to be rigorously proven for the discrete algorithm.

More broadly, our results raise the question of when generalization occurs when applying reverse diffusion. Reverse diffusion by itself does not “generalize.” A simpler way to express this is to say that
“everything is in the UNet”
. Indeed, for image generation, a UNet architecture is used to estimate the expected origin
\overline{\bf x}_{0}
(
4.2
) (or, equivalently, the expected noise
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{0}
). Without it, the overall machinery of the reverse diffusion process would not work. Thus, one has to study the interplay between the reverse dynamics diffusion and the UNet approximation. The flow introduces a mixing property that combines different images intelligently
[
17
]
without assuming that relevant images lie on a low-dimensional manifold (as in the variational auto-encoder approach). Interestingly, the neural approximation of the score is “imperfect” enough to generate new data points. Using the kernel formulation in the overfitting regime, one can identify when the practical flow starts to deviate from the exact flow, thereby characterizing the “good error” necessary for generating new samples.

Appendix A
Formula for stable diffusion

Denote by
\rho
the law of the forward process (
2.11
). Fix a partition
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<...<t_{m}<...<T_{*}
with
\Delta t_{m}=t_{m}-t_{m-1}
, and set
X_{m}=X_{t_{m}}
. We define
\rho(X_{m}\mid X_{m-1})
to be the law of
\rho
at time
t_{m}
knowing
X_{m-1}
. Using our pairwise distribution (
2.23
), this is equivalent to

\rho(X_{m}=y\mid X_{m-1}=x)=\rho(y,t_{m}\mid x,t_{m-1}).

(A.1)

Our goal is to reverse the equation (
A.1
) and express the probability of the past position
X_{m-1}
knowing the future
X_{m}
. The trick is once again to assume that we know the starting position
x_{0}
.

Proposition A.1

\rho(X_{m-1}\mid X_{m},x_{0})\sim\mathcal{N}(\overline{\mu}_{m-1},\overline{\sigma}_{m-1}^{2}\text{Id})

(A.2)

with

\displaystyle\overline{\mu}_{m-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\Delta t_{m}}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t_{m-1}})}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t_{m}}}X_{m}+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-t_{m-1}}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\Delta t_{m}})}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t_{m}}}x_{0},

(A.3)

\displaystyle\overline{\sigma}_{m-1}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\Delta t_{m}})(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t_{m-1}})}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t_{m}}}.

(A.4)

Proof.

We start by invoking the Bayes’ formula:

\displaystyle\rho(X_{m-1}\mid X_{m},x_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\rho(X_{m}\mid X_{m-1},x_{0})\cdot\rho(X_{m-1}\mid x_{0})}{\rho(X_{m}\mid x_{0})}

(A.5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c\,\cdot\,\rho(X_{m}\mid X_{m-1})\cdot\rho(X_{m-1}\mid x_{0}),

(A.6)

using the Markov property and the fact that the numerator is constant in
X_{m-1}
. Notice that the notation is somewhat confusing here since
\rho(X_{m}\mid X_{m-1})
has to be seen as a function of
X_{m-1}
and not
X_{m}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\rho(X_{m}\mid X_{m-1})=c\exp\left(-\frac{|X_{m}-\mathrm{e}^{-\Delta t_{m}}X_{m-1}|^{2}}{2(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\Delta t_{m}})}\right)=c\exp\left(-\frac{|\mathrm{e}^{\Delta t_{m}}X_{m}-X_{m-1}|^{2}}{2(\mathrm{e}^{2\Delta t_{m}}-1)}\right).

To conclude, we only need to apply Lemma
A.2
with

\displaystyle\rho(X_{m}\mid X_{m-1})

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{e}^{\Delta t_{m}}X_{m}\;,\;(\mathrm{e}^{2\Delta t_{m}}-1)\text{Id})

(A.7)

\displaystyle\rho(X_{m-1}\mid x_{0})

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{e}^{-t_{m-1}}x_{0}\;,\;(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t_{m-1}})\text{Id}),

(A.8)

leading to
\rho(X_{m-1}\mid X_{m},x_{0})\sim\mathcal{N}(\overline{\mu},\overline{\sigma}^{2}\text{Id})
with

\displaystyle\overline{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t_{m-1}})\mathrm{e}^{\Delta t_{m}}X_{m}}{(\mathrm{e}^{2\Delta t_{m}}-1)+(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t_{m-1}})}\;+\;\frac{(\mathrm{e}^{2\Delta t_{m}}-1)\mathrm{e}^{-t_{m-1}}x_{0}}{(\mathrm{e}^{2\Delta t_{m}}-1)+(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t_{m-1}})}.

We recognize the formula for
\overline{\mu}_{m-1}
(
A.3
). Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\overline{\sigma}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(\mathrm{e}^{2\Delta t_{m}}-1)\cdot(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t_{m-1}})}{(\mathrm{e}^{2\Delta t_{m}}-1)+(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t_{m-1}})}=\frac{(\mathrm{e}^{2\Delta t_{m}}-1)\cdot(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t_{m-1}})}{\mathrm{e}^{2\Delta t_{m}}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t_{m}})}.

∎

Lemma A.2

Suppose
f\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{1},\sigma_{1}^{2}\text{Id})
and
g\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{2},\sigma_{2}^{2}\text{Id})
. Then

h(z)=cf(z)g(z)\sim\mathcal{N}(\overline{\mu},\overline{\sigma}^{2}\text{Id}),

(A.9)

where
c>0
is a normalizing constant with

\displaystyle\overline{\mu}=\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}\mu_{1}+\sigma_{1}^{2}\mu_{2}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}}

and

\displaystyle\overline{\sigma}^{2}=\frac{\sigma_{1}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}}.

(A.10)

Proof.

We multiply the two Gaussian distributions:

\displaystyle f(z)\cdot g(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c\exp\left(-\frac{|z-\mu_{1}|^{2}}{2\sigma_{1}^{2}}-\frac{|z-\mu_{2}|^{2}}{2\sigma_{2}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c\exp\left(-\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}|z-\mu_{1}|^{2}+\sigma_{1}^{2}|z-\mu_{2}|^{2}}{2\sigma_{1}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}}\right)\;=:c\exp\left(-\frac{A}{2\sigma_{1}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}}\right).

Now we complete the square:

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2})|z|^{2}-2\sigma_{2}^{2}\langle\mu_{1},z\rangle-2\sigma_{1}^{2}\langle\mu_{2},z\rangle+\sigma_{1}^{2}\mu_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}\mu_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s^{2}|z|^{2}-2s^{2}\langle\overline{\mu},z\rangle+\sigma_{1}^{2}\mu_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}\mu_{2}^{2}

with
s^{2}=\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}
and
\overline{\mu}
given by (
A.10
). Thus,

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s^{2}|z-\overline{\mu}|^{2}\;-s^{2}|\overline{\mu}|^{2}+\sigma_{1}^{2}\mu_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}\mu_{2}^{2}.

We deduce that

\displaystyle f(z)\cdot g(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{c}\exp\left(-\frac{s^{2}|z-\overline{\mu}|^{2}}{2\sigma_{1}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}}\right)=\tilde{c}\exp\left(-\frac{|z-\overline{\mu}|^{2}}{2\overline{\sigma}^{2}}\right),

with
\overline{\sigma}^{2}
given by (
A.10
).
∎
