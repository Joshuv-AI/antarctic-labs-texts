Self-expandable metal stents versus endoscopic balloon dilatation for the treatment of strictures in Crohn’s disease: a retrospective long-term follow-up of the ProtDilat study, an open label, multicenter, randomized trial
Authors: Carme Loras, Pablo Ruiz-Ramírez, Joan B Gornals, Vicente Sanchiz, Eduard Brunet-Mas, Eva Iglesias, Jesús Barrio, Beatriz Sicilia, Carmen Dueñas, José Ramón Foruny, María Dolores Martín-Arranz, David Busquets, Elena Cerrillo, Natalia García-Morales, David Monfort, Francisco Pérez-Roldán, Eva Pijoan, Begoña González, José Reyes, Gisela Torres, Elisabet Maristany Bosch, Marta Maia Bosca-Watts, Jordi Guardiola, Albert Martín-Cardona, Maria Esteve
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAt 1-year of follow-up, the ProtDilat trial indicated that endoscopic balloon dilatation (EBD) was more effective than self-expandable metal stents (SEMSs) for treating Crohn’s disease (CD) strictures. We aimed to assess the long-term outcomes of ProtDilat participants, focusing on surgery-free rates, factors related to surgery, and endoscopic retreatment.MethodsWe retrospectively collected data on medical treatment, endoscopic–surgical interventions, and smoking status. The primary outcome was the percentage of patients who remained free of surgery at the end of follow-up.ResultsEighty participants were included (39 SEMS, 41 EBD); 39 were women and the median age was 45 years (IQR: 38-55). Over a 7-year follow-up (median 77 months; IQR 61-84), 31.5% (25/80) of patients required surgery, with a median time to surgery of 30.6 months (IQR 6.4-55.2). Surgery-protective factors were no-intensification of medical therapy (HR 0.22 [0.08-0.58]), EBD treatment (HR 0.26 [0.10-0.68]), and single endoscopic treatment (HR 0.33 [0.15-0.74]). Among the 55 patients who avoided surgery, the median number of endoscopic treatments was two. Notably, 36.3% (29/80) required neither surgery nor endoscopic retreatment. Among the 53 patients (20 SEMS vs 33 EBD) with successful initial endoscopic treatment in the ProtDilat, the long-term effectiveness (free of surgery and endoscopic retreatment) was 45% and 57.6% respectively (P = .374). Treatment-related adverse events occurred in 8.75% (7/80) of endoscopic cases vs 36% (9/25) of surgical cases (P = .003), with a severity (AGREE/Clavien–Dindo ≥ 3) of 38% (3/8) vs 84% (10/12), respectively (P = .011).ConclusionsEndoscopic approaches (especially EBD) are effective long-term therapies for avoiding surgery and requiring few endoscopic treatments. Stable medical management and single EBD therapy were independently associated with a lower likelihood of surgery during follow-up. Our findings also highlight the higher frequency and severity of adverse events associated with surgical intervention compared to endoscopic management.

1. Introduction

Stenosis is among the most frequent complications in patients with Crohn’s disease (CD), causing morbidity and the possibility of repeated surgeries and short bowel syndrome.1,2 One-third of patients with CD develop stenosis within 10 years after diagnosis, and up to 50% of patients require at least one surgical resection within the same period.1,2 Early use of biologicals is expected to influence the disease course and reduce the need for surgery, as demonstrated in a recent meta-analysis.3 The choice of therapy depends on the degree of inflammatory activity. Diverse degrees of inflammation and fibrosis coexist in the majority of stenoses. Medical therapy is the first therapeutic step when inflammatory activity is prominent, reserving surgical resection for irreversible fibrotic stenosis. As alternatives to surgery, several endoscopic techniques have been used to treat fibrostenotic CD, with similar efficacy and a lower risk of complications. Endoscopic treatment does not remove the affected intestine, has a long-term therapeutic efficacy of 50%-60%, and has a very low percentage of complications (4%-6%).4,5 Endoscopic treatment includes endoscopic balloon dilatation (EBD), intralesional injection of corticosteroids or anti-tumor necrosis factor (TNF) drugs, self-expandable metal stent (SEMS) placement and endoscopic stricturotomy.

EBD is the most commonly used endoscopic treatment and is a safe and effective alternative to surgical intervention in selected patients. The global success rate obtained in four meta-analyses evaluating the efficacy of EBD ranged between 58.0% and 80.8%.6-9 The studies included in these meta-analyses are limited by their retrospective design, heterogeneity in the endoscopic techniques used, and differences in factors that can influence the safety and efficacy of the procedure, with only a few studies including more than 100 patients.10-13 The role of SEMS placement in treating stenosis in CD is relatively limited, but it has been reported to be useful, particularly for patients refractory to EBD.14,15 Therefore, the ProtDilat clinical trial was designed to compare the effectiveness of both endoscopic treatments. The results with 80 patients evaluated at the 1-year follow-up confirmed that both procedures are safe for the treatment of stenosis, while showing a therapeutic superiority of EBD over SEMSs when evaluating the overall results (80.5% vs 51.3%; primary endpoint), with a significantly lower cost (EBD 1365.63 euros vs SEMS 1923.55 euros). However, differences in efficacy were not observed in a subanalysis of patients with stenosis > 3 cm (EBD: 66.7% vs SEMS: 63.6%). Therefore, SEMSs may have a role in the management of longer stenosis for which EBD has proven to be less efficacious.16

Despite these results, the long-term efficacy of both endoscopic treatments is still debated, especially for EBD. Few studies on EBD have reported a follow-up of more than 3 years (with even fewer beyond 5 years), with a limited number of patients and no randomized clinical trial data.10,11,17,18 Overall, these studies suggest that approximately two-thirds of patients could avoid surgery (one-third of them not requiring retreatment) or that the time to surgery can be delayed up to 6.5 years.19,20 Regarding SEMSs, this information remains relatively scarce. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of nine studies including 163 patients with CD and stenosis treated with stents revealed a global clinical success rate of 60.9% (95% confidence interval [CI], 51.6-69.5; I2 = 13%), with follow-up ranging from 3 to 69 months. In five of these studies, comprising a total of 85 patients, 22.3% needed surgery.21

For these reasons, we consider the need to assess the long-term effectiveness of both endoscopic treatments through the long-term follow-up of all patients included in the randomized clinical trial ProtDilat. The primary objective of the study was to determine the percentage of patients who were free of surgical intervention at the end of follow-up.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and participants

This observational, retrospective cohort study was based on a database from a randomized, multicenter controlled clinical trial (ProtDilat) performed in 19 tertiary and secondary hospitals in Spain between August 28, 2013, and October 9, 2017.16 The ProtDilat trial was designed to compare the efficacy of EBD with that of SEMS placement (randomly assigned 1:1). The primary outcome was the proportion of patients who did not require a new therapeutic intervention—EBD, SEMS placement, or surgery—due to symptom recurrence at 1 year of follow-up. The study population in the Long-ProtDilat study included all patients from the ProtDilat trial who completed the follow-up, and the inclusion and exclusion criteria were the same as those in the ProtDilat study. Patients aged 18 years or older with CD and obstructive symptoms caused by predominantly fibrotic strictures less than 10 cm in length were eligible for inclusion. The inclusion of strictures up to 10 cm in length (rather than the traditional 4-5 cm limit) was based on a pragmatic, real-world design. This approach recognizes that while longer strictures carry a higher risk of surgery, endoscopic management can still be effective and should be considered on an individualized basis before surgical intervention. CD was treated with immunosuppressants and biologics as needed; treatment was optimized in patients with strictures showing persistent endoscopic or radiological activity. Patients were excluded if they had strictures treated with SEMSs or EBD in the previous year, strictures not accessible by a colonoscope, strictures complicated by abscess or fistulae, more than two strictures, coagulopathy (international normalized ratio > 1.5) or thrombocytopenia (platelets < 70 × 109/L), were pregnant, had any other clinical situation preventing colonoscopy, or were unwilling to provide written informed consent.

2.2. Data collection

Follow-up data were collected through medical chart reviews and entered into a structured electronic case record using Excel. To ensure patient confidentiality and compliance with data protection regulations, each participating center received only a list of patients enrolled at their own site. These centers were responsible for completing the long-term follow-up variables for their respective patients. The database included the patient study identification, diagnosis date, inclusion date, sex, age, smoking habits, and follow-up end date in the ProtDilat trial. Follow-up data were recorded from the beginning of the ProtDilat trial to July 31, 2023. Information on medical, endoscopic, and surgical treatments, patient smoking status, and disease activity was collected up to the last visit, as documented in medical charts. For each variable, the required information was specified. For example, endoscopic treatment was coded as yes-1 or no-2. The database allowed recording of up to three endoscopic treatments, with additional columns available if needed to capture all the necessary variables. Data on medical treatment, including intensification or de-escalation therapy during the study period and the ongoing therapeutic regimen, were recorded. For endoscopic treatment, details such as the need for a new endoscopic treatment, type, materials used, technical and clinical success, and adverse events (AEs) were recorded. Surgical treatment data included the type of surgery and complications. Moreover, the date and findings of the last colonoscopy, the Harvey–Bradshaw index at the last visit, and the presence of obstructive symptoms were also recorded.

The study protocol was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee (CEIC) at the coordinating center and at each participating hospital. The data collected were handled in accordance with the protocol, the rules of good clinical practice, and applicable legislation. The long-ProtDilat study is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT06036680.

2.3. Outcomes

The primary outcome was the percentage of patients who remained free of surgery at the end of follow-up. Secondary outcomes included (1) identification of factors related to the need for surgery, (2) assessment of the need for additional endoscopic treatment, (3) evaluation of the long-term effectiveness of endoscopic treatments (SEMS vs EBD), and (4) analysis of the long-term safety and AEs rates related to both endoscopic (SEMS and EBD) and surgical treatments.

2.4. Definitions and variables

For EBD, endoscopic retreatment was defined as the requirement for more than two dilatation sessions during the entire follow-up period, consistent with the criteria established in the EBD treatment arm in the ProtDilat study.

We defined the long-term effectiveness of endoscopic treatment as the percentage of patients who remained free of surgery and did not require any endoscopic retreatment during the follow-up period.

Obstructive symptoms were assessed using a previously validated obstructive symptoms scale,22 which has been consistently applied in both the original ProtDilat trial16 and the current long-term study. This scale scores the presence and severity of abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, and dietary restrictions.

AEs were classified on the basis of the nature of the intervention. For endoscopy-related complications, AEs were categorized using the AGREE (Adverse Events in Gastrointestinal Endoscopy) classification (Table S1).23 For surgery-related complications, AEs were categorized following the Clavien–Dindo IIIa classification system (Table S2).24

2.5. Statistical analysis

The results are presented as the mean (standard deviation; SD), median (interquartile range; IQR), and frequency and percentage, as appropriate. The chi-square test (χ2) or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare proportions between groups. Student’s t test or its corresponding nonparametric test was used to compare quantitative variables between groups.

To analyze factors related to the need for surgery, univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazard models were used. Variables with a P-value < 0.10 in the univariate analysis, or those considered clinically relevant, were entered into a multivariate Cox proportional hazards model. For certain secondary outcomes, multivariate analysis was not performed when the limited number of events precluded a robust statistical model.

The long-term effectiveness of endoscopic treatment (SEMS vs EBD), determined by the percentage of patients who were free of surgery and endoscopic retreatment at the end of follow-up, was compared with the percentage difference and the chi-square test with 95% CI. P-values of less than 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance. Data management and statistical analysis were performed using R software v.4.1 or higher.

3. Results

Data were available for all 80 patients previously enrolled in the ProtDilat study, with a median (IQR) follow-up of 77 months (61-84). Among these patients, 39 (49%) were assigned to the SEMS group and 41 (51%) were assigned to the EBD group. The median patient age was 45 years (IQR 38-55), and 39 patients were women (49%). Most CD patients had a B2 stricturing pattern according to the Montreal classification,25 and most of them were receiving immunosuppressants and/or anti-TNF drugs. The median stricture length was 3.4 cm (2-5.5), with values of 4.0 cm (2-5.6) in the SEMS group and 3.0 cm (2-4.5) in the EBD group. Thirty-four (42.5%) of the 80 patients had an anastomotic stricture. There were no differences in demographic or disease characteristics between treatment groups (Table S3). At the time of the final evaluation, 58 (72.5%) of the 80 patients were in remission according to the Harvey–Bradshaw index score. Table 1 shows the current characteristics of both groups regarding smoking habits, medical treatment, and disease activity. Among the 80 patients included, stenosis required surgical treatment in 25 patients (31.25%), 26 underwent endoscopic retreatment as the sole interventional therapy (32.5%), and 29 patients (36.25%) required neither surgery nor endoscopic retreatment during the entire study period. Among the 25 patients who eventually underwent surgery, 19 had previously received endoscopic retreatment. Dilatation was performed using a CRE Boston Scientific pneumatic balloon (CRE PRO Wireguired Oesophageal, Pyloric, Colonic 6-20 mm; Cork, Ireland). The balloon diameters used for EBD ranged from 12 to 20 mm. In most cases, a stepwise dilatation strategy was applied, progressively increasing the balloon diameter based on stricture characteristics and patient tolerance. Regarding SEMS, the diameters used were 20-22 mm, with lengths of 60, 80, or 100 mm (Taewong fully covered metal stents [Niti-S S Enteral Colonic Stent 6-10 cm; Gimpo-si, South Korea]), depending on the stricture’s length and location.

In terms of the primary outcome, 25 of 80 patients (31.2% [95% CI 21.35-42.59]) required surgical intervention at 7 years of follow-up (median time to surgery 30.61 months [6.35-55.24]). Moreover, 68.75% (55/80) remained free of surgery at the end of follow-up, with a median surgery-free survival time of 77 months (IQR 61-84) (Figure 2). The cumulative incidence of surgery at 24, 48, and 72 months was 13.7% (95% CI 7.07-23.07), 20% (95% CI 11.89-30.44), and 31.2% (95% CI 21.35-42.59), respectively. A total of 20 patients required one surgical intervention, whereas five additional patients underwent two separate surgeries during the follow-up period. The types of surgeries performed were stricturoplasty in one case (3.3%), ileocolic resection in 18 cases (60%), ileal resection in eight cases (26.6%), and colonic resection in three cases (10%).

Kaplan–Meier survival curve showing the time until surgery.

It should be noted that the incidence of surgery varied depending on the endoscopic treatment strategy used (EBD, SEMS, EBD + SEMS, or SEMS + EBD). Among patients treated exclusively with EBD, 16.67% (6/36) subsequently required surgery, whereas this proportion increased to 31.25% (n = 5/16) among patients treated with SEMS alone. Among patients who received both treatments (n = 28), most were managed with SEMS followed by EBD; in this subgroup 39.13% (n = 9/23) required surgery. In contrast, all patients who underwent EBD followed by SEMS placement (100%, n = 5/5) required surgery.

With respect to factors related to surgery, univariate analysis revealed that the absence of medical treatment intensification (hazard ratio [HR] 0.22 [0.08-0.58]), single endoscopic treatment (HR 0.33 [0.15-0.74]), and exclusive endoscopic treatment with EBD (HR 0.26 [0.10-0.68]) were the only protective factors. A trend toward an increased risk of surgery was observed in association with active smoking (HR 1.73 [0.79-3.79]), longer stenosis length (HR 1.01 [0.99-1.03]), mild-moderate activity according to Harvey–Bradshaw Index (HR 1.93 [0.8-4.67]), and a greater number of endoscopic procedures (HR 1.14 [0.84-1.54]) (Table 2 and Figure 3).

In a Cox proportional hazards model, single endoscopic treatment and non-intensification therapy remained significant and protective against surgery, with HRs of 0.35 (0.16-0.81) and 0.26 (0.10-0.71), respectively (P = .014 and P = .008).

In the group of patients who did not require surgery (n = 55), a median of 2 (1-3) endoscopic treatments were performed from the beginning of the ProtDilat study. EBD was performed on 44 patients (80%), and SEMSs were placed in 25 patients (45.4%). The median endoscopic retreatment-free time was 72 months (21-99).

From the total sample of 80 patients, 42 received endoscopic retreatment, 52.5% (95% CI [41.02-63.79]) during the follow-up, with cumulative incidences of 36.2% (95% CI [25.79-47.76]), 47.5% (95% CI [36.21-58.98]), and 52.5% (95% CI [41.02-63.79]) at 24, 48, and 84 months, respectively (Figure 4). The median endoscopic retreatment-free time was 59 months (11-92).

Fifty-three patients (33 in the EBD arm and 20 in the SEMS arm) who met the primary endpoint at the end of the ProtDilat study (free of surgery or any additional endoscopic intervention) were evaluated for long-term effectiveness according to the initial type of endoscopic treatment. The long-term effectiveness of SEMS placement was 45% (9/20 [95% CI 25.82-65.79]), with a median follow-up of 77 months (57-84), whereas the effectiveness of EBD was 57.6% (19/33 [95% CI 40.81-72.76]), with a median follow-up time of 77 months (65-84). Although no statistically significant differences were found between the two treatments (P = .374), a difference of 12.6% (CI: −19 to 44.15) was observed in favor of EBD. In fact, if we consider the total number of patients from the beginning of the ProtDilat study until the end of the Long-ProDilat study, the effectiveness of SEMS treatment was 23% (9/39) versus 46.3% (19/41) for patients in the EBD group (P = .03).

In terms of safety, a total of 20 complications occurred in 16 patients. Seven patients experienced complications related to the endoscopic procedure, representing 8.75% (7/80 [95% CI 4.3-16.98]). Moreover, nine patients experienced surgery-related complications, representing 36% (9/25 [95% CI 20.25-55.48]) (P = .003), with a difference of 27.2% (IC: 4.82-49.68) observed in favor of surgery-related complications. Moreover, significant differences were found in terms of the severity of complications, which were more severe (AGREE/Clavien–Dindo classification ≥ 3) in patients who underwent surgery than in those who underwent endoscopy (84% [10/12] vs 38% [3/8], P = .011). The overall endoscopic perforation rate for the total study population was 3.75% (3/80, 95% CI 0.1-10.6) (Tables 3 and 4).

4. Discussion

We report long-term outcomes of nearly 7 years from the only randomized study conducted to date for the treatment of CD strictures, the ProtDilat clinical trial, designed to compare two endoscopic treatment strategies (EBD vs SEMS),16 The long-term follow-up (Long-ProtDilat) aimed to assess the effectiveness of these endoscopic treatments in terms of avoiding surgery and to assess whether there were differences according to the initial randomization. In the ProtDilat study, EBD was shown to be more effective and less expensive than SEMSs at the 1-year follow-up and was recommended as the first-line endoscopic treatment. The results of the current Long-ProtDilat study further support that endoscopic treatment, particularly EBD, avoids surgery in most cases in the long term and requires a median of only two endoscopic treatments. A total of 68.7% of patients remained free of surgery at the end of follow-up. These results are similar to those recently reported in a single-center, prospective, observational study in small bowel intestinal stenosis dilated by enteroscopy, which revealed a cumulative 5-year surgery-free rate of 76.6%.26 Therefore, the long-term efficacy of endoscopic treatment in our study and in the study by Hong et al.26 clearly appeared to be superior to the outcomes reported in the two most recent meta-analyses, with efficacy rates of 57.1% and 25% after 2 and 5 years, respectively, for EBD.6,8 To our knowledge, the only study in which EBD and surgery were compared is the retrospective study by Lian et al. conducted in patients with anastomotic stenosis.20 The authors concluded that EBD avoided surgery in 48.3% of the patients and that the average time to surgery delayed by dilatation was 6.45 years. However, patients who underwent upfront surgery had a lower risk for subsequent surgery.20 The reasons for the better outcome in our study could be the strict selection of endoscopic treatment candidates in the context of a randomized trial. However, the inclusion criteria, which were not particularly restrictive, could be easily applied in clinical practice. In fact, the participating hospitals were not only tertiary centers but also community hospitals. An additional explanation for the improved outcomes in endoscopic treatments in recent years could be the optimization of concomitant medical treatment, parallel with the reduction in the number of surgeries associated with early initiation of biological treatment.3

The identification of factors related to the need for surgery is crucial to avoid unnecessary delays. Patients at higher risk were those with a greater number of endoscopic treatments that included both SEMSs and EBD. Conversely, variables associated with a lower risk of surgery included a lack of need for intensified medical therapy and having been treated exclusively with EBD. It is critical to note that these variables probably reflect a less complex disease phenotype rather than representing true, independent protective effects. Patients requiring treatment escalation or multiple complex endoscopic interventions inherently present with a more severe behavior. Both of these factors probably reflect a less complex disease. Other factors that tended to favor surgery but did not yield statistical significance were active smoking, mild–moderate activity according to Harvey–Bradshaw Index and increasing number of endoscopic treatments. As has been reported in previous studies,13,16,27 all these factors are related to a more disabling disease and hence a greater chance of surgery.

Early management of predominant fibrotic strictures, once obstructive symptoms appear, including not only surgical intervention but also endoscopic treatment, increase the likelihood of success.16 The length of the stenosis has been repeatedly reported as the most important determining factor of EBD success, with greater efficacy associated with a shorter stricture length.6,7,9,13,16 Most studies have limited EBD to strictures ≤ 4-5 cm in length, based on earlier evidence suggesting better outcomes in shorter lesions.7 Also, it has been reported that the risk of surgery increases by approximately 8% for each additional centimeter of stricture length. However, EBD can still achieve clinical success rates of around 60%-70% in strictures longer than 4-5 cm.9,16 In our study, we did not find a significant relationship between stricture length and endoscopic treatment efficacy. The effect of length was potentially attenuated in our study by the combined analysis of the two endoscopic treatments, since for strictures longer than 5 cm, the efficacy of EBD and SEMSs became similar, converging at approximately 60%. Similar findings were also reported in the ProtDilat trial,16 in which the length of stenosis was not a risk factor for SEMS success, suggesting that stents could play a role in longer stenoses refractory to EBD.

After the end of the randomized clinical trial, patients were treated at the discretion of the attending physician, and EBD was the most common endoscopic treatment used (80%) compared with SEMS placement. This finding is not surprising because of the better outcome for EBD in the ProtDilat study. However, consequently, long-term comparisons between both endoscopic procedures have obvious limitations because of selection bias. Thus, the effectiveness of SEMSs and EBD was evaluated at the end of follow-up in those patients who had been successfully treated in the ProtDilat trial (n = 53 patients).16 The percentages of patients who were free of surgery and endoscopic retreatment were not significantly different between the SEMS and EBD groups (45% and 57.6%, respectively), although a trend toward a better outcome was observed for the EBD group. This difference of 12.6% and greater efficacy for EBD vs SEMS (46.3% vs 23%) obtained from the beginning of the ProtDilat study16 until the end of the Long-ProtDilat reinforce that EBD should be the first endoscopic treatment for CD stenosis. Moreover, relevant differences in surgical rates were observed according to the endoscopic treatment strategy, with lower rates in the EBD-only group (16.67%) compared with the SEMS-only group (31.25%), higher rates among patients treated with SEMS followed by EBD (39.13%), and surgery required in all patients who underwent EBD followed by SEMS (100%). Despite the small number of patients treated with this latter combination, the results suggest that patients who do not respond adequately to EBD are unlikely to benefit from subsequent SEMS placement, particularly in the case of short strictures (<4 cm). Finally, notably, 36.2% of the patients did not require surgery or endoscopic retreatment during the entire follow-up period.

An important issue is the significantly higher rate and severity of complications observed in surgical treatment than in endoscopic treatment. In terms of surgical treatment, the complication rate was 36%, whereas it was 8.7% in the endoscopic group. Moreover, the severity of these complications according to the Clavien–Dindo24 or AGREE23 classification was ≥ III in 84% versus 38% for surgical and endoscopic treatment, respectively. Although surgical and endoscopic complication rates were compared, these results should be interpreted with caution. Since the transition to surgery often depended on physician preference or local protocol rather than randomized assignment, a potential selection bias may exist. The rate of serious adverse events for endoscopic treatment was 3.7% (3/80), which is similar to that previously reported.6-9,16,21 In contrast, the rate of complications in surgical patients was higher than that reported in previous studies.20,28,29 This finding could be partially explained by our trial being performed at secondary and tertiary hospitals. In addition, as previously reported, the complexity of the hospitals could clearly impact the results of surgery30,31 but not endoscopic treatment.13,29 Regardless, it is clear that surgical treatment involves greater morbidity compared with endoscopic treatment, suggesting that surgeries should be concentrated in high-volume centers.

The main strength of our study is that the results come from a clinical trial, which has a long period of follow-up and reflects daily clinical practice. In contrast, the most important limitation is its retrospective nature, with the biases that this entails, especially regarding the comparison of the real long-term effectiveness of the two endoscopic treatments. A significant limitation of this study is that, following the initial 1-year randomized period, patient management was left to the discretion of the treating physicians. This lack of standardization introduces potential selection bias and confounding by indication, which limits the validity of direct long-term comparisons between EBD and SEMS. Nevertheless, the pragmatic design reflects real-world clinical decision-making and does not compromise the primary objective of assessing the overall long-term need for surgery in this patient population. Another limitation of the study that was already mentioned in the previous ProtDilat trial paper is that most patients had short strictures, which could have limited the possibility of achieving better results with stents.

In summary, endoscopic treatment (predominantly EBD) avoids surgery in most cases and requires few endoscopic retreatments in the long term. Patients at higher risk for surgery are those with a greater number of endoscopic treatments that include SEMSs and EBD. Conversely, variables associated with a lower risk of surgery include stable medical therapy and exclusive treatment with EBD, both indicative of a less severe disease course. Although there are no significant differences in the long-term effectiveness between SEMSs and EBD, a trend toward better outcomes is observed for EBD. While our findings highlight the higher frequency and severity of adverse events associated with surgical treatment compared with endoscopic therapy, the overall evidence underlines that endoscopic management represents a highly effective, sustainable long-term strategy to successfully postpone or completely avoid surgical bowel resection in a substantial number of patients with Crohn’s strictures.
