Effect of interventions for health care students to develop positive attitude toward the elderly: A meta‐analysis study
Authors: Gülfidan Başer, Filiz Hisar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AimThe rise in the elderly population all over the world has led to an increase in the health care needs of elderly individuals. Thus, health care professionals need programs to help them develop positive attitudes toward the elderly during their education process. This study aims to explore the effect of interventions that encourage health care students to develop positive attitudes toward the elderly.MethodsIn this research utilizing the meta‐analysis method, searches were conducted across eight databases. Following the screening process, a total of 14 101 studies were retrieved, of which 13 were ultimately included in the meta‐analysis.ResultsFollowing the meta‐analysis, the effect size of interventions for positive attitude development toward the elderly was found to be statistically significant at 0.422 (95% confidence interval, 0.013–0.830; P = 0.043).ConclusionThirteen studies in which the effect of interventions for positive attitude development toward the elderly was examined revealed that the effect was moderately in favor of the experimental group. Although there are a large number of studies in the literature that aim to develop positive attitudes toward the elderly, well‐designed studies with high methodological quality are needed to obtain high level evidence. Geriatr Gerontol Int 2024; 24: 1370–1379.

Introduction

The decrease in child mortality rates, declining fertility rates and the rise in life expectancy collectively contribute to an augmentation in the proportion of the elderly demographic. 1 The escalation of the elderly demographic is a global phenomenon observed across nearly all nations. 2 Elderly individuals constitute a distinct demographic cohort warranting specialized attention within the realm of health care services. Chronic health ailments, intricate medical interventions, the management of multiple medications and the attendant risk of interactions necessitate a heightened level of care, thereby amplifying the demands placed on the health care system by the elderly population. 3 , 4 The cultivation of positive attitudes and behaviors among health care professionals toward older individuals, commencing from the preservice stage, holds significant importance in ensuring the provision of quality health services. 5 Ageism pervades health care environments and manifests in various physical and psychological health repercussions for older individuals. Particularly during the COVID‐19 pandemic, the increased risk of severe illness and mortality among older individuals has resulted in several problematic policy proposals and interpretations that perpetuate ageism. 6 It is acknowledged that there is a direct association between ageism and the delivery of inadequate care for older adults. 7 Positive attitudes and interactions exhibited by health care staff toward older individuals are recognized as integral components of quality care and are pivotal in enhancing overall quality of life. Establishing relationships rooted in mutual respect and sincerity, and defining older individuals based on their roles rather than solely on their age, can cultivate positive attitudes. 8 , 9

The World Health Organization (WHO) has identified three strategies aimed at mitigating discriminatory attitudes toward older individuals. The first among these strategies involves the enactment of policies and laws designed to diminish or eradicate ageism. The second and third strategies encompass intergenerational contact and educational activities. 10 This meta‐analysis incorporated studies focusing on intergenerational contact and educational activities for students in alignment with the WHO strategy. This study differs from previous systematic reviews and meta‐analyses in terms of intervention type 11 (Bowden et al., 2021) sample 12 ; (Bowden et al., 2021) 13 ; and research design of the included studies (such as not including a control group) (Ross et al., 2018; Bowden et al., 2021). 11 , 13 , 14 The findings of this study have established the efficacy of interventions in positively altering the attitudes of health care students towards the elderly, thereby reducing ageism. The findings obtained are anticipated to contribute to updates to the curriculum and positively impact the quality of service provided by this group, which regularly engages with elderly individuals. Hence, this study used meta‐analysis methodology to evaluate the impact of interventions implemented for health care students aimed at fostering positive attitudes toward the elderly on their overall attitudes toward this demographic group.

Research question

What are the overall effect sizes concerning the impact of interventions implemented among health care students on their attitudes toward the elderly?

Methods

Study design

This research was conducted by meta‐analysis method. The meta‐analysis protocol was developed using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses 2020 Checklist 15 (Table S1). The study protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews, an international database of systematic reviews on health‐related topics (CRD42022337387).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies conducted with undergraduate or associate‐degree students were included in this meta‐analysis since it was desired to make an inference about the education of health care students. It is recommended that a nonrandomized study should contain a low level of bias to be compared with a good randomized controlled trial. 16 Therefore, it was planned to include randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and nonrandomized controlled trials (NRCTs) with low‐level bias. However, only RCTs were included because NRCTs with low‐level bias could not be reached. The inclusion and exclusion criteria are detailed in Table 1.

Databases

A literature search was conducted across the American Psychological Association PsycInfo, the Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature Complete, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Medline Complete, ProQuest Dissertations and Theses Global, Scopus, TR Index, Web of Science Core Collection and National Thesis Center databases for the study. The sources of all articles and reports identified through the database search were scrutinized to uncover any additional relevant studies.

Search strategy

This study used a three‐stage review strategy. In the first stage, following a review of the Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature, “Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials” and Medline databases, keywords for this study were identified through analysis of the title, abstract and keywords. Medical Subject Headings were used for English keywords, while Turkish Science Terms content was employed for their Turkish counterparts in this study. Keywords were formulated on the basis of the PICOS (population, intervention, comparison, outcomes and study) criteria. During the second stage, suitable combinations of keywords were devised, and the designated databases were searched accordingly (Table S2). During the third stage, the reference lists of articles meeting the inclusion criteria were scrutinized for additional studies. The studies retrieved from the search were documented using EndNote X9.3.3 reference management software.

Data screening and extraction

Titles and abstracts were evaluated by the first researcher (GB). Subsequently, the first and second researchers (GB and FH) conducted a full‐text review based on the inclusion–exclusion criteria. For each article analyzed, a table containing the inclusion criteria was created and completed independently by two reviewers. In this table, the reasons supporting the inclusion or exclusion decision for each study were written. For the studies that were not in agreement, the assessors discussed the reasons, and an agreement was reached. For studies where interrater agreement could not be initially achieved, the reviewers discussed the reasons and eventually came to an agreement. The interrater agreement, which was found to be 0.884, indicated a high level of interrater reliability. In the case of four studies in which the departments of the students were not explicitly indicated, the authors were contacted via e‐mail for clarification, eliciting responses from three of them. Subsequently, a code table was established by two independent researchers (DC and EE) based on the studies deemed eligible for inclusion in the meta‐analysis (refer to Table 2).

Risk‐of‐bias assessment

To assess the risk of bias in the studies selected for inclusion in the meta‐analysis, Risk of Bias 2 was used for RCTs, while the Risk of Bias in Nonrandomized Studies of Interventions was used for NRCTs. The quality assessment of the studies was carried out by two independent researchers (DC and EE) (Table S3). The interrater agreement was 0.917 and the interrater reliability was found to be very high.

Intervention

The interventions examined in this meta‐analysis encompass intergenerational contact and educational activities. Intergenerational contact activities comprise intergenerational day care programs, intergenerational education programs and intergenerational voluntary contact programs. Educational activities involve curriculum updates featuring geriatric content, empathy‐based interventions and game‐based learning.

Control

In studies included in the meta‐analysis, control groups encompassed participants who received no intervention, standardized training, baseline training alongside the experimental group or a placebo.

Outcomes

The outcomes of this study are represented by scale scores assessing attitudes toward the elderly.

Data analysis

The interrater agreement was assessed using Cohen's kappa coefficient, calculated with the IBM SPSS Statistics 20 software package. The effect size analysis of the studies was conducted using the Comprehensive Meta‐Analysis software package. The effect size was calculated using Hedges' g, standardizing the results obtained from various assessment tools. 30 Heterogeneity was assessed using the Q test and I 2 value. A random‐effects model was used for analyses involving heterogeneous structures, while a fixed‐effects model was used for nonheterogeneous structures. The analysis results in this study were evaluated using the random‐effects model. Orwin's fail‐safe N value, Kendall's τ coefficient and Egger's regression analysis methods were used for publication bias. The significance value for publication bias was set at P < 0.05. Publication bias is presented with a funnel plot, and effect size with a forest plot.

Results

A literature search was performed across eight databases between June 15, 2022, and July 31, 2022, for this study. As a result of the search, 14 101 studies were retrieved. Following the literature search, 3882 studies were excluded due to duplication; 10 219 studies were evaluated according to title and abstract, and 125 studies were included in the full‐text evaluation. A total of 102 studies were excluded from the analysis as they did not meet the inclusion criteria (Table S4). Following the database search, a reference search was conducted for articles meeting the inclusion criteria, as well as for previously conducted systematic reviews and meta‐analyses in the field, resulting in the inclusion of three additional studies for bias assessment. Subsequently, a total of 26 articles were included in the bias assessment (Fig. 1). Upon completion of the bias assessment, nine NRCTs were excluded due to high risk of bias, while four NRCTs were excluded due to moderate risk of bias (Figure S1). One RCT with low bias, along with six RCTs exhibiting moderate bias and six RCTs demonstrating high bias, were included in the meta‐analysis (Figs. 2 and 3).

Effect of interventions for positive attitude development toward the elderly

The meta‐analysis conducted with 13 studies examining the effect of interventions for positive attitude development toward the elderly examined the Q statistic and I 2 values for the heterogeneity test. As a result of the analysis, the Q value obtained was 165.454 (P < 0.001), and the I 2 value was 92.747%. The presence of a heterogeneous structure was evidenced by these values. Consequently, the random‐effects model was examined, revealing a statistically significant effect size of 0.422 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.013–0.830; P = 0.043). It is thought that the wide CI is due to the use of different measurement methods. Examination of Orwin's fail‐safe N value for publication bias revealed that 3454 additional studies would be required to nullify the obtained effect size. Kendall's τ analysis yielded a test value of 0.321, indicating that there was no publication bias (P = 0.063). Egger's regression analysis showed a β0 value of 7.732, a t value of 1.803 and a P value of 0.098. This result also suggests the absence of publication bias (Table 3, Fig. 4).

Discussion

The rise in the elderly population worldwide has led to an increase in the health care needs of elderly individuals. Quality care is closely linked to the knowledge and positive attitudes of health care professionals. 8 , 31 , 32 Negative attitudes have been shown to have detrimental effects on the psychological and physical well‐being of older adults. 7 Hence, the implementation of interventions aimed at fostering positive attitudes toward the elderly among health care professionals becomes increasingly significant.

Discussion of the characteristics of studies involving interventions for positive attitude development toward the elderly

Among the studies included in the research, four were conducted in Turkey, three in China, two in the United States, two in Iran, one in Canada and one in Egypt. Notably, in all of these countries except Egypt, life expectancy at birth exceeds the world average (WHO, 2022) 33 . The growing elderly population is anticipated to precipitate increased health care demands, prompting concerted efforts to cultivate positive attitudes toward the elderly in these countries. Furthermore, the cultural characteristics of these nations vary significantly. Contrary to the widespread belief that Eastern societies hold greater respect for older individuals compared to Western cultures, a meta‐analysis study revealed that Eastern cultures exhibit significantly more negative attitudes toward the elderly. 34 Based on these data, the notion that stereotypes exist regarding the impact of contemporary societies' perception of respect versus traditional societies' perception of respect on attitudes has emerged, prompting research and initiatives in this area.

When considering the implementation time, three studies had a duration of 8 weeks, two studies lasted 45 min, one study spanned 10 weeks, another study lasted 2 weeks, one study ranged from 1 to 3 weeks, one study lasted 10 h, one study lasted 8 h, one study lasted 110 min and one study lasted 90 min. The duration of one study was unspecified. In the literature, applications vary in duration depending on the method employed. While practices such as curriculum updates, theoretical trainings and intergenerational contact 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 are typically implemented over longer durations, simulation‐based practices 29 , 31 , 39 are typically of shorter duration as they are often conducted on an individual basis. Due to the differences in the structure of the practices, it is difficult to determine the effect of time on the change of attitudes. However, the effect of the same type of practices on changing attitudes in terms of their duration can be determined. In addition to the duration of the applications, repeated assessments to be made after the applications are thought to be valuable in terms of determining the permanence in the change of attitudes. In two of the studies included in the meta‐analysis, assessments were conducted immediately after the intervention and then again 1 month later, 25 , 29 while in another study, assessments were conducted immediately after the intervention and then again 2 months later. 22

Discussion of the findings related to the effect of interventions for positive attitude development toward the elderly

Thirteen studies in which the effect of interventions for positive attitude development toward the elderly was examined revealed that the effect was moderately in favor of the experimental group. In two studies involving simulation applications 20 and empathy training, 22 the effect was deemed excellent and in favor of the experimental group. The literature indicates that empathy‐based interventions have the most significant impact on enhancing attitudes toward the elderly. 13 However, it is also noted in the literature that experiencing simulated aging may heighten anxiety levels and exacerbate negative attitudes. 40 It is suggested that implementing simulations that highlight the positive aspects of aging could be effective in fostering positive attitudes.

In a study involving intergenerational day care programs included in the meta‐analysis, 27 the effect was moderate and in favor of the experimental group. Conversely, in a study involving intergenerational voluntary contact activities, 26 the effect was moderate and in favor of the control group. In a comprehensive meta‐analysis involving a substantial sample spanning from primary school to university students, it was observed that interventions combining intergenerational contact with education exerted robust effects in fostering positive attitudes toward the elderly. 12 Intergenerational contact activities facilitate direct interaction with older individuals, enabling acquaintance with their lives and learning about their lifestyles. It is known that acquiring knowledge about old age contributes to mitigating ageism. 41 Moreover, intergenerational contact activities facilitate the delivery of care to the elderly and promote an appreciation for the significance of this care. As a result, it is believed that conscious contact practices integrated with education will offer greater advantages to the elderly, while positive feedback from them will bolster attitudes toward the elderly.

In the meta‐analysis study, it was desired to obtain data with a high level of evidence. Since no RCTs with low level bias could be reached, only RCTs were included. Since seven of the included RCTs contained low/moderate bias and six contained high‐level bias, moderator analysis was performed according to the risk of bias. No significant difference was found between studies with low/moderate bias and studies with high bias on attitude outcomes. In a meta‐analysis study, the effect of RCT and NRCT research designs on attitude outcomes toward the elderly was examined regardless of the risk of bias, and no significant difference was found. 12 It is recommended to include studies in the RCT design in the meta‐analysis of applications where there are not enough RCT studies. 42 However, it is important to carefully examine the bias and interpret the findings accordingly. The fact that studies with high and low/moderate bias did not find a significant difference in the effect on attitudes toward the elderly causes the quality of the evidence to be low. Attitudes toward the elderly significantly affect the quality of elderly care. For this reason, even if the designs of the studies examining the positive change in attitudes toward the elderly differ, it becomes an important issue to plan future studies without bias to reach strong evidence.

The objective of meta‐analysis is to reconcile conflicting findings across independent studies. Nonetheless, issues related to validity and reliability may arise, including the development or adaptation of scales used in studies to align with the target population and ensure cultural compatibility. 18 Furthermore, it has been noted that the widely used KAOP (Kogan's Attitudes Toward Old People) Scale, developed in 1961, requires revision and has demonstrated inadequate internal consistency in recent research. 43

Our recommendations to provide strong evidence for future studies:

To ensure the validity of research findings, careful consideration must be given to the selection of appropriate scales.

Studies with a low risk of bias and a control group should be planned.

Intervention content should be written in detail to contribute to curriculum updates.

Limitations

The primary limitation of this study is the restricted search scope, limited to Turkish and English databases. Despite the broad selection of databases, the insufficient data retrieved hindered the execution of certain moderator analyses originally planned. Another limitation is the inclusion of studies with moderate and high risk of bias in the meta‐analysis. The fact that the data were collected through self‐report questionnaires in the included studies makes it difficult to prevent detection bias.

Conclusion

Based on the data gathered, interventions targeting health care students to foster positive attitudes toward the elderly were found to have a moderate effect in improving attitudes. To attain data of high evidential value, well‐designed RCTs or NRCTs with robust methodological quality should be conducted, focusing on interventions for positive attitude development toward the elderly, and valid and reliable measurement tools should be used for accurately assessing attitudes. Interventions aimed at fostering positive attitudes toward the elderly should encompass not only the challenges they face but also the positive aspects of aging. Enhancing intergenerational contact activities with educational content will lead to more effective outcomes. Moreover, interventions designed to cultivate positive attitudes toward the elderly should be tailored to align with the cultural characteristics of the society.
