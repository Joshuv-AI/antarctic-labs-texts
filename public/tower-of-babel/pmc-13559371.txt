Risk factors for peripherally inserted central catheter tip migration in patients undergoing thoracoscopic thoracic surgery: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Kangqi Jin, Na Luo, Yimei Zhang, Xiaoli Ma, Minjie Ma, Hongying Jin, Xiaoyan Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeripherally inserted central catheter (PICC) is widely used for perioperative intravenous therapy in thoracic surgery. Tip migration can lead to reduced drug infusion efficiency, thrombosis, and other complications. Thoracic surgery, particularly lung resection, may increase the risk of migration by altering intrathoracic pressure and mediastinal position; however, systematic studies are lacking. This study aimed to identify the incidence and independent risk factors for PICC tip migration after thoracoscopic thoracic surgery.MethodsA total of 222 patients who underwent thoracoscopic surgery and received PICC at the Department of Thoracic Surgery of The First Hospital of Lanzhou University between January 2025 and January 2026 were retrospectively included. Demographic variables, surgical procedure, side of PICC insertion, and other covariates were collected. Highlight box

Key findings

• The incidence of peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC) tip migration was 17.12% in patients undergoing thoracoscopic thoracic surgery.

• Lung resection [odds ratio (OR) =8.736, P=0.001] and right‑side catheter insertion (OR =3.697, P=0.01) were independent risk factors.

• Consistency of intraoperative lateral position with insertion side and severe postoperative cough were not significant in multivariate analysis.

What is known and what is new?

• The reported incidence of PICC tip migration in general populations is 4% to 12%. Known risk factors include insertion side, vein choice, and patient activity. Most studies focus on malposition during insertion, with little attention to migration caused by postoperative intrathoracic changes.

• This is the first study to systematically evaluate PICC tip migration specifically in a thoracic surgery population. Lung resection is identified as a novel and strong independent risk factor, a finding rarely reported before.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Patients scheduled for lung resection should preferentially receive left‑arm PICC insertion when feasible. For those requiring right‑arm insertion, intensified postoperative chest X‑ray monitoring is recommended to detect migration early. Incorporating PICC migration risk assessment into perioperative care may help reduce catheter-related complications.

Introduction

Background

Peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC) is widely used for perioperative intravenous therapy in thoracic surgery. It offers several advantages, including minimal trauma, long indwelling time, and the ability to infuse irritating medications (1). The safety and effectiveness of PICC depend heavily on correct catheter tip positioning. Tip migration occurs when the tip deviates from its ideal position and persists. This can reduce drug infusion efficiency, cause local phlebitis and thrombosis, and may even lead to serious complications such as arrhythmias, thereby affecting postoperative recovery (2).

Rationale and knowledge gap

Current studies report that the incidence of PICC tip migration ranges from 4% to 12%, and can reach 5% to 31% in some high-risk populations (3,4). Known risk factors include the side of catheter insertion, choice of vein, and patient activity (5,6). However, most existing studies focus on catheter malposition occurring during or immediately after insertion. Limited attention has been paid to secondary tip migration caused by postoperative changes in the intrathoracic environment. Moreover, studies specifically targeting patients undergoing thoracic surgery are rare.

Thoracic surgery inevitably alters the intrathoracic environment, but the magnitude of these changes varies by surgical procedure. Compared with esophagectomy or mediastinal tumor resection, lung resection may result in more pronounced postoperative changes in intrathoracic pressure and central venous anatomy (7,8). These changes could theoretically affect PICC tip stability and increase the risk of catheter migration. However, no study has systematically evaluated the incidence and independent risk factors of PICC tip migration in patients undergoing thoracic surgery.

Objective

Therefore, this retrospective cohort study analyzed clinical data from patients who underwent video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery in a thoracic surgery department. The aims were to determine the incidence of PICC tip migration in this population and to identify independent risk factors using univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses. The findings may provide evidence-based guidance for optimizing perioperative venous access management and reducing PICC-related complications in thoracic surgery patients. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1710/rc).

Methods

Study population

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Hospital of Lanzhou University (approval No. LDYYLL2024-561) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived. In The First Hospital of Lanzhou University, PICC placement was not performed routinely for all thoracoscopic surgery patients. The indications for PICC insertion included anticipated need for intravenous therapy of ≥7 days, difficult peripheral venous access, or requirement for administration of irritant medications or vasopressors. Patients who underwent thoracoscopic surgery at the Department of Thoracic Surgery of The First Hospital of Lanzhou University between January 2025 and January 2026 were included. Inclusion criteria were: (I) age ≥18 years; (II) underwent thoracoscopic surgery (including lobectomy, segmentectomy, esophagectomy, or mediastinal tumor resection); (III) received a 4 Fr silicone PICC inserted via the upper limb (left or right arm) one day before surgery, with an indwelling time ≥7 days; (IV) the patient or legal guardian signed informed consent.

Exclusion criteria were: (I) severe coagulation dysfunction or other bleeding disorders; (II) history of PICC or other central venous catheter-related infection, thrombosis, or malposition; (III) failed first insertion attempt; (IV) catheter removal within 24 hours due to acute complications (e.g., infection, thrombosis, rupture); (V) intraoperative conversion to thoracotomy; (VI) death or transfer to another hospital precluding serial chest X-ray monitoring.

To maintain a homogeneous minimally invasive cohort, patients who underwent planned thoracotomy were also excluded, as thoracotomy may differentially influence PICC tip migration through greater postoperative pain, altered respiratory mechanics, and more extensive mediastinal manipulation.

A total of 222 patients met the criteria and were enrolled. All included patients received PICC placement by certified PICC nurses under ultrasound guidance via the upper limb (left or right arm). Chest X-ray was performed immediately after placement to confirm the catheter tip position.

No patients had missing data for the variables collected. All data were obtained from the electronic medical record system and picture archiving and communication system (PACS), with complete records available for all enrolled patients.

PICC insertion and maintenance

Timing of insertion and predicted length

All patients underwent PICC insertion by certified PICC nurses one day before thoracoscopic surgery. Before insertion, venous conditions (preferred vein: basilic vein; alternatives: brachial or cephalic vein), coagulation function, and medical history were comprehensively evaluated to determine the optimal puncture site. The predicted catheter length was measured using a disposable paper ruler: starting from the intended puncture site, following the course of the vein along the midpoint of the infraclavicular margin to the right sternal border at the level of the 4th–5th intercostal space.

Insertion procedure

A PICC-specialized nurse performs the procedure under local anesthesia after establishing a maximum sterile field (the operator wears a surgical gown and sterile gloves, and the patient is covered with a sterile drape). The target vein is located and punctured in real time using ultrasound guidance. After successful puncture, the needle is withdrawn. Following dilation of the subcutaneous tissue using a dilator or peel sheath, a 4 Fr silicone PICC catheter is slowly advanced along the guidewire to the predetermined length. During the procedure, ultrasound is used to monitor the internal jugular vein for guidewire displacement to ensure the guidewire enters the superior vena cava smoothly. After withdrawing the guidewire and peel sheath, the infusion connector is attached.

Position confirmation

Chest X-ray was performed immediately after placement. The acceptable initial position was defined as the distal superior vena cava or cavoatrial junction, approximately 1–2 vertebral body levels below the carina. Only catheters confirmed to be within this range were included.

Dressing and catheter maintenance

A sterile transparent dressing was smoothly applied from the catheter exit site, covering at least 2–3 cm beyond the puncture site to ensure complete sealing. Immediately after placement, the catheter was flushed with normal saline in a pulsatile manner to confirm patency. Thereafter, the dressing should be changed every 7 days, and the skin around the puncture site should be closely monitored for any abnormal signs such as redness, swelling, exudate, or tenderness. If the dressing becomes loose, damp, or contaminated, it should be replaced immediately. All procedures must strictly adhere to aseptic principles and the hospital’s standard operating procedures for PICC line care.

Postoperative X-ray monitoring

Routine chest X-rays were performed on postoperative day 1 (the time window with the most significant changes in body position and intrathoracic pressure), postoperative day 3 (mid‑term stability assessment), and the day before discharge (final examination) to evaluate the PICC tip position. For patients with prolonged hospital stays due to complications, chest X-rays were repeated every 5–7 days until catheter removal or discharge.

Data collection

General data

The following information was retrospectively extracted from the electronic medical record system: (I) demographic data: age, sex, height, weight, body mass index (BMI); (II) medical history: chronic comorbidities (including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, stroke); (III) surgery-related data: surgical procedure (lobectomy/segmentectomy/esophagectomy/mediastinal tumor resection), whether the PICC insertion side was ipsilateral to the dependent side during surgery (i.e., the patient was positioned with the PICC arm in the dependent position); (IV) PICC-related data: side of insertion (left/right), indwelling catheter length (cm), arm circumference (measured 10 cm above the elbow; cm), catheter indwelling time (days); (V) postoperative clinical data: presence or absence of severe postoperative cough. Severe cough was defined as cough that disturbed sleep or required medical intervention (e.g., use of antitussive medication or oxygen support) according to nursing records.

Imaging data and definition of tip migration

Chest X-ray acquisition time points

Day of insertion (baseline), postoperative day 1, postoperative day 3, and the day before discharge. For patients with prolonged hospitalization, chest X-rays were repeated every 5–7 days.

Assessment of PICC tip position

Two certified PICC nurses independently interpreted all chest X-ray images. Using the carina or thoracic vertebral bodies as references, the distance from the catheter tip to the reference point was measured on the PACS system. PICC tip migration was defined as a deviation of the tip from the baseline position of >2 cm at any postoperative monitoring time point, with persistence on the next X-ray examination and no spontaneous repositioning (9). Anatomically, the distance from the cavoatrial junction to the brachiocephalic vein or right atrium is approximately 2–3 cm in adults; thus, a displacement exceeding 2 cm indicates that the catheter tip has likely exited the ideal central position and entered a higher-risk zone. This degree of migration is considered clinically significant, as it can compromise infusion therapy, increase thrombotic risk, or provoke arrhythmias.

Quality control

The two certified PICC nurses were blinded to the patients’ migration outcomes and surgical types when interpreting the images. If their interpretations differed, a third senior physician with >10 years of clinical experience in thoracic surgery made the final judgment independently. Two researchers independently entered the X-ray interpretation results and medical record data into an electronic database, and cross-checking was performed after data entry. Discrepant items were verified and corrected by reviewing the original medical records or PACS images. After all data were entered, a third researcher performed source data verification on a random 10% sample to ensure accuracy.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0. All tests were two‑sided, and the significance level was set at α=0.05.

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and group comparisons were performed using the independent samples t-test. Non‑normally distributed variables were expressed as median (P25, P75), and group comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequency (percentage), and group comparisons were performed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test (when expected frequency <5). There was no missing data for any variable. To identify potential risk factors for PICC tip migration, univariate binary logistic regression analysis was performed for each variable, and odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. Variables with P<0.05 in univariate analysis were entered into a multivariate binary logistic regression model using the Enter method.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the patients

A total of 222 patients who underwent thoracoscopic surgery and received PICC were included. Among them, 118 (53.2%) were male and 104 (46.8%) were female, with a median age of 58 years (IQR: 53, 66 years; range: 33–77 years). Regarding surgical procedures, 113 patients (50.9%) underwent lung resection (lobectomy, n=71; segmentectomy, n=26; wedge resection, n=16), and 109 patients (49.1%) underwent other thoracic surgeries (esophagectomy, n=59; mediastinal tumor resection, n=50). PICC was inserted in the right upper limb in 124 patients (55.9%) and in the left upper limb in 98 patients (44.1%).

Postoperative chest X-ray confirmed PICC tip migration in 38 patients, yielding an incidence of 17.12% (38/222). Patients were divided into a migration group (n=38) and a non-migration group (n=184).

There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in number of venipuncture attempts, puncture vein distribution, sex, age, height, weight, BMI, chronic comorbidities, catheter indwelling time, indwelling catheter length, or arm circumference (all P>0.05) (Table 1). However, significant differences were observed in surgical procedure, side of PICC insertion, consistency of intraoperative lateral decubitus position with the insertion side, and severe postoperative cough (all P<0.05) (Table 1).

Univariate analysis of factors associated with PICC tip migration

Univariate logistic regression analysis identified four variables significantly associated with PICC tip migration: surgical procedure (lung resection vs. other thoracic surgeries), side of PICC insertion (right vs. left), consistency of intraoperative lateral decubitus position with the insertion side, and severe postoperative cough (all P<0.05). The remaining nine candidate variables—including sex, age, BMI, and catheter indwelling time—showed no significant associations (all P>0.05).

Most cases of tip migration were first identified on the routine postoperative day 1 chest radiograph, consistent with the timing of the most pronounced changes in intrathoracic pressure. Regarding migration direction, proximal migration accounted for 25 cases (65.79%) and distal migration for 13 cases (34.21%). The brachiocephalic vein and right atrium were the most common sites of proximal and distal migration, respectively (Table 2).

Multivariate analysis of independent risk factors for PICC tip migration

The four significant variables from univariate analysis were entered into a multivariate logistic regression model (Table 3). Surgical procedure (OR =8.736, 95% CI: 2.303–33.143, P=0.001) and right-side PICC insertion (OR =3.697, 95% CI: 1.375–9.943, P=0.01) remained independent risk factors. Consistency of intraoperative lateral position and severe postoperative cough were not significant in the multivariate model. Owing to the limited number of events, the CIs for some variables were relatively wide, particularly for consistency of intraoperative lateral position (95% CI: 0.770–6.632) and severe postoperative cough (95% CI: 0.402–2.922), reflecting limited precision of the estimated effects.

Discussion

Key findings

This retrospective study of 222 patients who underwent thoracoscopic surgery and received PICC found a postoperative tip migration incidence of 17.12%. This is higher than the 4–12% reported in previous literature (3,4), suggesting that thoracic surgery patients may face an elevated risk. Multivariate analysis identified two independent risk factors: surgical procedure (lung resection) and side of insertion (right). Of note, surgical procedure as a risk factor for PICC migration has rarely been systematically reported.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first to systematically evaluate PICC tip migration specifically in a thoracic surgery population. Strict quality control measures were applied, including blinded image interpretation and source data verification.

This study also has limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective cohort study with a small number of outcome events (n=38), statistical power was limited, resulting in wide CIs for some estimates; moreover, several potential confounders—including the extent of lung resection, chest tube management, and operator experience—could not be controlled. Second, although catheter tip position was confirmed by chest X-ray on the afternoon before surgery, the interval between this final pre-operative assessment and the first post-operative radiograph precludes definitive determination of whether migration occurred intraoperatively or in the early post-operative period; in addition, the unavailability of intracavitary electrocardiography may have affected initial tip placement accuracy. Finally, the operational definition of severe cough relied on nursing records, and the findings from this thoracoscopic cohort using silicone catheters may not directly generalize to thoracotomy patients or those with polyurethane catheters.

Comparison with similar research

The migration rate of 17.12% in our cohort exceeds the general range of 4–12% (3,4), highlighting the particular vulnerability of thoracic surgery patients. Our finding that right‑side insertion carries a higher risk is consistent with the results of Huang et al. (10). However, surgical procedure, specifically lung resection, has rarely been examined as a potential risk factor in previous studies, making our observation a novel contribution. The negative finding on severe cough also aligns with some reports that catheter tip displacement may be transient, although direct comparisons are limited by differences in cough assessment methods (11).

Explanations of findings

The strong association between lung resection and PICC tip migration may be explained by several mechanisms. First, lung resection significantly alters intrathoracic pressure. The residual cavity and negative suction from the chest drain change the pressure gradient around the superior vena cava, making the catheter tip more susceptible to displacement. Second, mediastinal shift toward the surgical side may alter the anatomical course and diameter of the superior vena cava (12), affecting the tip position. Third, early postoperative tissue edema and inflammation can temporarily change tissue pressure around the catheter, an effect most pronounced on postoperative days 1–3 (7). Fourth, complications such as pneumothorax or pleural effusion further disturb the intrathoracic pressure balance. Beyond reducing infusion efficiency (13), tip migration increases the risk of catheter-related thrombosis (CRT) by mechanically irritating the vessel wall. A displaced catheter can obstruct small veins or rub against the endothelium, slowing blood flow and raising thrombosis risk (9,14). CRT may lead to pulmonary embolism, prolonged hospitalization, and increased costs.

For right-side insertion, anatomical differences provide a plausible explanation. The path from the right arm to the superior vena cava is shorter (15), facilitating catheter entry into the ipsilateral internal jugular or contralateral subclavian vein. The right brachiocephalic vein joins the superior vena cava at a more acute angle (16), increasing the chance of the tip touching and deviating. A larger right venous lumen paradoxically allows the catheter to move more easily under blood flow, particularly during postoperative cardiac output fluctuations (17). Right-sided anatomical variations and tortuosity are also more common (18). These factors together support preferentially choosing the left arm when conditions permit. Furthermore, post-lobectomy mediastinal shift may alter the angulation of the junction between the right brachiocephalic vein and the superior vena cava. Right upper lobectomy, for example, can cause upward and rightward mediastinal displacement, sharpening the angle and facilitating proximal catheter tip dislodgement into the brachiocephalic vein. This may explain why the brachiocephalic vein was the most common migration site observed in our study.

Severe postoperative cough and consistency of intraoperative lateral decubitus position with the insertion side were significant in univariate analysis but not in multivariate analysis. For cough, the valvular anatomy of central veins may play a role. The internal jugular vein has a terminal valve, while the subclavian vein lacks effective valves (19). Cough-induced intrathoracic pressure changes can transiently displace the catheter tip, but when coughing stops, blood flow and arm movement may push it back. This could explain why some patients showed tip deviation on day 1 but spontaneous repositioning by day 3. However, imprecise cough measurement may have underestimated its effect. As for lateral decubitus consistency, its effect may have been overshadowed by the stronger influence of surgical procedure. All PICCs used in this study were made of silicone, which is more flexible than polyurethane. The greater pliability of silicone may increase susceptibility to tip migration under fluctuating intrathoracic pressure. Therefore, our findings are specific to silicone catheters; future studies comparing polyurethane devices are warranted.

Implications and actions needed

For patients scheduled for lung resection, left-arm PICC insertion may be considered to potentially reduce the risk of tip migration. However, as this study did not evaluate clinical outcomes such as thrombosis or catheter malfunction, this suggestion requires prospective validation. PICC tip migration risk assessment should be incorporated into routine perioperative nursing care, and individualized catheter management plans should be developed through multidisciplinary collaboration. Future prospective multicenter studies are needed, including more comprehensive confounders, to validate these findings and compare PICC with midline catheters in thoracic surgery.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that lung resection and right-side catheterization are strong independent risk factors for PICC tip migration in patients undergoing thoracoscopic thoracic surgery. To our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically evaluate the risk of PICC tip migration specifically in a thoracic surgery population. Given a migration rate of 17.12%, these findings highlight the importance of individualized PICC management in this population. For patients scheduled for lung resection, preferential use of the left arm and intensified postoperative X-ray monitoring may help reduce the risk of catheter migration and its associated complications. However, these findings should be regarded as a clinical suggestion warranting further prospective validation with outcome-based endpoints.
