Analyzing the effect of physical activity on sleep quality in college students using the chain mediation model
Authors: Shunding Hu, Bo Chen, Hongbo Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSleep quality represents a pressing issue that currently confronts college students in China, garnering significant attention from researchers in the field.MethodsThe present study employed a questionnaire survey method to investigate and analyze 704 college students using the Physical Activity Rating Scale, Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index Scale, Stress Perception Scale, and ruminative thinking Scale. The objective of this investigation was to examine the chain mediating effect of stress perception and ruminative thinking in college student groups and to elucidate the relationship between physical activity and sleep quality.Results(1) Physical activity, stress perception, and ruminative thinking are bivocally associated with sleep quality in college students. (2) Physical activity negatively predicted stress perception and negatively predicted ruminative thinking and sleep quality among college students; stress perception positively predicted ruminative thinking and sleep quality; and ruminative thinking positively predicted sleep quality.ConclusionStress perception and ruminative thinking individually mediate, and stress perception and ruminative thinking chain mediate, the relationship between physical activity and sleep quality in college students.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03554-7.

Background

Sleep is a fundamental physiological activity of the human body. It plays a crucial role in ensuring the proper functioning of the immune system [1], metabolic system [2], and brain [3]. Sleep, defined as a cyclical physiological and psychological state, plays a crucial role in an individual's physical and mental well-being [4]. Adequate sleep quality has been demonstrated to promote optimal physical and mental functioning, foster a positive mindset, and enhance overall life satisfaction [5]. In this way, sleep guarantees the maintenance of human health. According to the China Sleep Data Report, the incidence of sleep disorders in China has been increasing year by year, and now more than 300 million Chinese people suffer from sleep problems [6]. As the main force in building China's future society, the health of university students has a bearing on China's future development. The latest survey shows that 27.6% of college students have poor sleep quality, which seriously affects their study efficiency and physical and mental health, and there is an urgent need to take effective measures to improve it [7]. The theory of sleep architecture remodeling posits that moderate physical activity can optimize sleep's cyclical composition and continuity by regulating physiological rhythms and improving neuroendocrine balance. This phenomenon occurs at the level of sleep's intrinsic structural patterns and operational mechanisms, thereby enhancing overall sleep quality [8]. According to the sleep structure remodeling theory, physical activity serves as a non-pharmacological intervention, capable of mitigating fatigue induced by mental labor to a certain extent [9]. This effect is achieved through mechanisms such as regulating the neuroendocrine system and alleviating psychological stress, thereby enhancing sleep quality [10]. However, the sleep quality of college students is influenced by the interplay of multiple factors, which may involve more complex mechanisms. Thoroughly analyzing these mechanisms could help address sleep quality issues among college students. The stress-buffering theory indicates [11], physical activity can reduce the negative effects of stress perception [12] and ruminative thinking [13] on both mind and body through a “buffering effect.”When individuals face stress, they employ different coping strategies that influence their psychological and physiological states. Physical activity, as a positive coping mechanism, can help college students alleviate their stress perception [14]. This approach has been demonstrated to facilitate a reduction in rumination and negative evaluation of stressful events [15]. Consequently, this assists college students in attaining superior sleep quality [16]. Therefore, the present study innovatively constructs a chain mediation model of "exercise—stress perception—ruminative thinking—sleep quality", which not only helps to deepen the understanding of the intrinsic mechanism of college students' sleep quality, but also provides a new theoretical basis and practical direction for physical activity promotion and sleep health interventions in colleges and universities.

The relationship between physical activity and sleep quality of college students

Sleep quality is a multifaceted concept that encompasses both quantitative components related to sleep, such as sleep duration, and individual subjective assessments, including perceived sleep quality, sleep onset latency, use of hypnotics, daytime dysfunction, sleep efficiency, and sleep disorders [17]. Among these, the most prominent issues are difficulty falling asleep and insufficient sleep duration [18, 19]. Research indicates that poor sleep quality can have many negative consequences, including heightened fatigue, recurrent daytime drowsiness, depressive moods, difficulty concentrating, and impaired memory. These effects can negatively impact an individual's daily life, studies, and work significantly [20]. A considerable number of unhealthy sleep habits and sleep issues have been identified among college students, particularly those in China. These include, but are not limited to, generally shorter sleep duration and later bedtimes [21]. Statistics from relevant studies indicate that the prevalence of sleep disorders among Chinese college students approaches 20%, reflecting the severity of sleep issues among this demographic [22]. Physical activity is a broad term encompassing a wide range of activities, including fitness training, recreational pursuits, health maintenance and rehabilitation, as well as mental and cognitive development. This category of sports and physical activities is undertaken with the objective of enhancing physical fitness, promoting overall well-being, and improving and sustaining bodily capabilities [23]. According to energy metabolism theory, physical activity effectively maintains energy metabolic homeostasis by increasing energy expenditure, accelerating glucose oxidation, and mobilizing and breaking down fat. These processes alleviate physiological tension, helping individuals enter a relaxed state more quickly. This reduces sleep onset delays caused by pre-sleep anxiety [24]. A substantial body of research has identified a positive correlation between higher levels of physical activity and improved sleep quality in students. The present study's findings suggest a negative relationship between physical activity and poor sleep quality among college students. These results indicate that an increase in physical activity may contribute to an improvement in sleep quality [8], the positive relationship between physical activity and college students' mental health has been well-documented. A growing body of research has demonstrated that physical activity has a beneficial effect on sleep quality in college students [25]. Therefore, this study proposes Hypothesis 1 (H1): Physical activity positively predicts college students' sleep quality.

Mediating effects of stress perception

Stress perception is the psychological response of an individual to a threatening stimulus in the environment after his or her own cognitive appraisal [26], it is essentially an individual's subjective perception of internal and external events that can alter cognitive functioning and also affect emotional and physiological states [27]. Research has shown that physical activity reduces stress in students and decreases stress perception [28]. As posited by Zhou Hao et al. [29], the engagement in suitable physical activity has been demonstrated to facilitate the accumulation of psychological energy, thereby enhancing an individual's capacity to resist and adapt to stress. Lu et al. [30] further elucidated that physical activity is more effective in relieving stress, and that appropriate physical activity will make people feel happy, relax, and release stress. Research findings indicate a positive correlation between stress perception and sleep quality [31]. Specifically, heightened stress levels have been shown to result in diminished sleep quality [32]. According to the sleep-disturbing process theory, stressful stressors and emotional arousal have the capacity to stimulate neurons in the brain, leading to hyperexcitability. This hyperexcitability, in turn, interferes with the normal sleep process and adversely affects sleep quality [33]. Sterling and Eyer proposed the concept of "non-homeostasis, "which posits that the body's response to stress is, in fact, a process of re-establishing homeostasis. They further theorize that when stress is excessive and protracted, the organism will undergo a gradual disorganization and exhaustion, thereby entering a "non-homeostatic" state. This, in turn, will trigger physical and mental reactions in the subject, resulting in changes in sleep quality. This "non-homeostatic" state instigates physical and mental reactions in the subject, which consequently results in alterations in sleep quality [34]. Therefore, this study proposes Hypothesis 2 (H2):stress perception mediates the relationship between physical activity and sleep quality among college students.

Mediating effects of ruminative thinking

According to Nolen-Hoeksema, ruminative thinking is defined as a response style in which individuals repeatedly focus on a negative emotion, thought, or behavioral state that is relevant to them and think about the causes and consequences of this negative state over and over again. However, the individual does not actively think about how to solve the problem in reality, and ruminative thinking is a form of maladaptive performance [35]. Research has shown that physical activity significantly and negatively predicts ruminative thinking [36]. At the same time, the psychological intervention value embedded in physical activity is also effective in inhibiting the development of negative ruminative thinking [37]. According to stress theory, the perception of stress is initiated when an individual is confronted with a stressor. The automatic allocation of cognitive resources to the stressor, in turn, engenders ruminative thinking, leading to prolonged exposure to negative emotions and thoughts. This cognitive bias impedes the brain's ability to attain a state of repose, thereby hindering the onset of restful sleep [25]. Additionally, the health benefits and psychological intervention value inherent in physical activity exert a moderating effect on rumination thinking [26]. Studies have shown that people with high levels of ruminative thinking are more likely to have problems with sleep quality [38], ruminative thinking, defined as repetitive and deep consideration of a thought or problem, has been shown to foster negativity. This cognitive habit, often characterized by its automatic and involuntary nature, has been linked to a diminished capacity to regulate and process negative information effectively. Prolonged ruminative thinking has been observed to give rise to sleep disturbances, with individuals engaging in this behavior experiencing a reduction in total sleep time. The frequency with which individuals engage in ruminative thinking has been demonstrated to correlate with the extent of their sleep deprivation [39]. Guo et al. noted a positive correlation between ruminative thinking and college students' sleep quality [40]. In addition to this, ruminative thinking, as an intrusive thought, affects the negative cues perceived by the individual before falling asleep, causing the individual's cognitive arousal level to rise, and the high arousal state before bedtime affects the quality of sleep to a certain extent [41]. Therefore, this study proposes Hypothesis 3 (H3): ruminative thinking mediates the relationship between physical activity and sleep quality among college students.

Chain-mediated effects of stress perception and ruminative thinking

The study showed that there was a positive correlation between stress perception and ruminative thinking and that stress perception significantly and positively predicted ruminative thinking [42], it has been demonstrated that ruminative thinking is more likely to be induced and exacerbated in individuals who perceive stress at elevated levels [43]. Individuals with stronger ruminative thinking are more likely to feel stronger stress perceptions [44]. In addition, Dan Zhang's study of 684 college students found that college student stress affects individual sleep quality by influencing ruminative thinking, and that stress perceptions significantly predicted ruminative thinking level [45]. At the same time, many scholars have explained from the theoretical level that the formation of stress perception is the result of negative evaluation of an individual's cognition interacting with external stressful events, and ruminative thinking itself is a kind of negative cognition and thinking pattern, and when an individual's stress perception is at a high level, this negative state will be more likely to induce the formation of ruminative thinking [30]. Methods

Participants

This study employed convenience sampling to select 733 college students from several universities in Liaoning Province, China. Encompassing disciplines such as science and engineering, humanities and history, medicine, and economics and management, as survey subjects. The disciplinary distribution of the sample is as follows: The science and engineering disciplines encompass a wide range of specializations, including mechanical engineering, computer science and technology, civil engineering, and more. A total of 215 students are enrolled in these programs, accounting for 30.54% of the total enrollment. The humanities and social sciences include a diverse array of fields, such as Chinese language and literature, history, and law. These disciplines collectively enroll 188 students, making up 26.74% of the total enrollment. Finally, the medical sciences include a variety of specializations such as clinical medicine, nursing, and pharmacy. These programs collectively enroll 188 students, representing 26.74% of the total enrollment. A total of 152 students were enrolled in the Business and Management program, constituting 21.62% of the total enrollment. The program encompasses disciplines such as Accounting, Marketing, Finance, and related fields. A total of 139 students were enrolled in the Arts discipline, encompassing areas such as Visual Communication Design and Musicology, among others. This constitutes 19.77% of the total student population. Thirty-nine students (5.55%) were identified as having a disability. This sample is noteworthy for its ability to reflect the characteristics of university students experiencing varying academic pressures and possesses a degree of representativeness.

The online questionnaire was disseminated via the Questionnaire Star platform, and the questionnaire encompassed five sections: basic information, physical activity level, Pittsburgh sleep quality, stress perception, and ruminative thinking. Liaoning Province, a prominent educational center in China, boasts a multitude of colleges and universities that admit students from diverse geographical areas throughout China. The present study encompasses a diverse sample of students from various educational institutions, academic disciplines, and academic levels, thereby offering a more comprehensive representation of the characteristics of Chinese college students as a whole. This study employs a series of safeguards to prevent coercive participation. The questionnaire's opening page contains a formal informed consent section that explicitly articulates the research objectives, the intended usage of the collected data, and the confidentiality measures that will be implemented. It also includes a clear notice that participants may voluntarily withdraw from the study at any time without facing negative consequences. The university counselors disseminated the questionnaire link to eligible students, strictly prohibiting the use of group notifications or assigned tasks to encourage voluntary participation. To access the questionnaire, participants were required to click "Agree to Participate." Those who did not agree were unable to view the content, ensuring that the process was conducted entirely on a voluntary basis. All respondents in this study signed an informed consent form. This study was conducted in strict compliance with the CIOMS International Ethical Guidelines and relevant Chinese regulations. All research procedures, data collection, and analysis methods meet ethical and scientific standards to ensure compliance throughout the study. Participants were required to fulfill the following criteria: they had to be full-time undergraduate students between the ages of 18 and 25, and they could not have any physical illness or psychiatric history. In the course of the screening process, 29 questionnaires were deemed invalid and excluded on the basis of regular or incomplete responses. This resulted in the acquisition of 704 valid questionnaires, yielding an effective recovery rate of 96.04%. Of the participants, 395 (56.11%) were male college students and 309 (43.89%) were female college students. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the relevant ethics committee of the university prior to implementation. Physical activity rating scale-3, PARS-3

The Physical Activity Rating Scale revised by Teh-ching Liang [46] was used to assess the physical activity level of university students. The scale consists of 3 dimensions (1 item each): intensity of exercise (e.g., how intensely do you engage in physical activity), duration of exercise (e.g., how many minutes at a time do you engage in physical activity), and frequency of exercise (e.g., how many times a month do you engage in physical activity). A 5-point Likert scale was used, with intensity and frequency of exercise scored 1–5 and time scored 0–4.The amount of physical activity is equal to the product of physical activity intensity, physical activity frequency, and physical activity time, with higher scores indicating more physical activity. The alpha (Cronbach's) of the physical activity rating scale in this study was 0.782.

Pittsburgh sleep quality index, PSQI

The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index Scale developed by Buysse et al. [47] and revised by Chenxian Liu et al. [48] was used. The scale consists of seven dimensions: subjective sleep quality (e.g., in the past 1 month, how well do you think you sleep), time to sleep onset (e.g., in the past 1 month, how many minutes does it usually take you to fall asleep each night), sleep duration (e.g., in the past 1 month, how many hours of actual sleep do you usually get each night),hypnotic medications (e.g., (e.g., in the past 1 month, how often have you taken medication to hypnotize yourself?), Daytime Functioning (e.g., in the past 1 month, do you often feel sleepy?), Sleep Efficiency, Sleep Disorders (e.g., in the past 1 month, have you been bothered by any of the following conditions that affect your sleep?) 7 dimensions make a total of 18 items, each dimension is scored in 0, 1, 2, 3, four dimensions, respectively. The item scores were converted, totaled, and recoded into seven dimensions, and after each dimension was scored individually, the scores for each of the individual items in these seven dimensions were summed to obtain a total score on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index scale, which ranges from 0–21 and is a composite reflection of the various aspects of sleep quality, with higher scores indicating a greater number or severity of problems across multiple dimensions, which means that the poorer the overall sleep quality. In this study, the alpha (Cronbach's)of the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index scale was 0.743.

Chinese perceived stress scale, CPSS

The revised stress perception scale of Tingzhong Yang et al. (2003) was used [49]. The scale consists of 2 dimensions, sense of loss of control (e.g., in the last month, feeling that things are going well for you) and sense of tension (e.g., in the last month, feeling upset that something unexpected happened), with 7 questions on the sense of loss of control dimension and 7 questions on the sense of tension dimension, for a total of 14 questions that belong to the reverse scoring items (questions 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, 10, and 13).With the 5-point Likert scale, from 1 (never) to 5 (always) in the forward scoring questions and vice versa in the reverse scoring questions, the reverse entries were calculated after assigning positive values to each of the reverse entries separately, and the scores of each individual item of these two dimensions were added together, and the resulting sum was the total score of the Stress Perception Scale, with higher scores indicating more stress in an individual. The a (Cronbach's) for the stress perception scale in this study was 0.9.

Ruminative responses scale, RRS

The Ruminate Thinking Scale developed by Nolen-Hoeksema et al. [50] and revised by Han Xiu et al. [51] was used The scale consists of 3 dimensions, Symptom ruminative thinking (e.g., I often think about how lonely I am), Reflective Thinking (e.g., I often think about why I am always like this), and Compulsive Thinking (e.g., I often write down what I am thinking about and analyze it), of which there are 12 questions on Symptom ruminative thinking, 5 questions on Reflective Thinking, and 5 questions on Compulsive Thinking, for a total of 22 questions, with no reverse-scoring questions. The scale is based on a 4-point scale ranging from 1 (never) to 4 (always), and after each dimension is scored individually, the scores for each individual item of these 4 dimensions are summed, and the resulting sum is the total score of the ruminative thinking Scale, with higher scores indicating that an individual has a higher level of ruminative thinking. The a (Cronbach's) for the Ruminate Thinking Scale in this study was 0.949.

Statistical processing

Valid data were imported into spss27.0 software for data analysis. Pearson correlation analysis, regression analysis, and chain mediation analysis were used. Chained mediation analysis used the bootstrap method of the Process plug-in to test for mediation effects. When analyzing the indirect effects of variables using Process plug-in, the following settings were made: the model was set as Model No. 6; X was physical activity, M1 was stress perception, M2 was ruminative thinking, and Y was sleep quality; bootstrap samples were 5,000 times.

Common method bias test

First, procedural controls were used to avoid common method bias: 1) the scale was designed with appropriately reverse-scored questions; 2) the order of the scale was randomized; and 3) subjects were told to fill out the scale anonymously before it was distributed. Second, the degree of common method bias in the data was examined using the Harman one-way modeling approach. The results showed that there were eight factors with eigenroots greater than 1. The first factor explained 30.234% of the variance, which was less than the critical criterion of 40%, indicating that the data in this study did not suffer from a serious problem of common method bias.

Discussion

This study reveals the chain-mediated role of perceived stress and rumination thinking in the relationship between physical activity and college students' sleep quality. Theoretically, it complements research on sleep quality influence mechanisms by breaking away from single-mediator examination models, clarifying the intrinsic connections among multiple variables, and injecting new vitality into the theoretical framework of this field. Practically, it provides robust theoretical support for universities to enhance physical activity among college students. This research offers effective pathways and methodological guidance to encourage active participation in sports activities and improve sleep quality, thereby positively promoting the physical and mental health development of university students.

Relationship between physical activity and sleep quality

The present study's findings offer support for Hypothesis H1, which posits a negative relationship between physical activity and sleep quality in college students. The results indicate that an increase in physical activity is associated with improved sleep quality. This finding aligns with the conclusions of previous studies, thereby providing evidence that supports the initial hypothesis [15]. Sleep quality can be effectively improved by regular physical activity over a long period of time [52]. A preponderance of research on college student populations has demonstrated that college students who engage in regular physical activity exhibit a reduced prevalence of sleep quality problems in comparison to their physically inactive counterparts with low levels of physical activity [53]. Good sleep is of vital importance to humans. From a physiological perspective, it supports the restoration and maintenance of bodily functions, positively influencing normal metabolic processes and immune system stability. Psychologically, sufficient, high-quality sleep stabilises emotions and cultivates mental resilience, making individuals more likely to exhibit positive psychological traits [54]. Furthermore, physical activity exerts a favorable influence on sleep quality, operating through specific physiological mechanisms. It has been established that during periods of physical exertion, the body temperature of the exercising individual undergoes a gradual increase. Concurrently, the hypothalamus undergoes activation of its regulatory functions, thereby facilitating the dissipation of heat. This process has the potential to induce a prolongation of slow-wave sleep in the subject, thereby stabilizing the duration required for the individual to transition into deep sleep. Concurrently, physical activity has been demonstrated to enhance sleep quality by increasing melatonin secretion in students [55]. Consequently, researchers have acknowledged the potential impact of physical activity on sleep quality.

The mediating role of stress perception in the relationship between physical activity and sleep quality in college students

The impact of physical activity on college students' sleep quality is mediated by stress perception, as illustrated by the following: physical activity significantly and negatively predicts stress perception, and the higher the stress perception, the higher the sleep quality of college students. This validates the hypothesis H2 of this paper and is consistent with the results of existing studies [21, 22]. During physical activity, the human body produces a series of physiological changes, the heart rate will be accelerated, stimulating blood flow to the muscles, this change can allow the individual's attention away from worries and other mental stressors, thus achieving the effect of relieving stress [37]. In addition, moderate physical activity will trigger a series of positive psychophysiological changes in the human body. From the psychological level, it can reduce the intensity of an individual's emotional experience, and this change in emotional experience has a significant effect on reducing stress and anxiety and other unwanted emotions, and during physical activity, the human body will secrete a number of neurotransmitters and endorphins and other chemicals that help to regulate emotions. These substances help to regulate emotions and free individuals from stress and anxiety, thus achieving the effect of relieving stress and anxiety [56]. Research has shown that when individuals perceive high levels of stress and challenge at the psychological level, the stress response mechanisms of the nervous system are activated, which leads to a decrease in emotional tension and behavioral regulation, thus affecting sleep quality, i.e. the higher the level of stress perception, the lower the quality of sleep [22]. In instances where individuals experience heightened stress perceptions, a concomitant increase in anxiety is frequently observed. This heightened anxiety can subsequently impede an individual's ability to attain and maintain a state of relaxation during the nighttime hours [57]. This negative emotional state keeps the brain in a heightened state of activity, increasing the likelihood of difficulties falling asleep, frequent nighttime awakenings, or shallow sleep, which in turn negatively impacts sleep quality [58]. Thus, stress perceptions individually mediate the relationship between physical activity and sleep quality in college students.

The mediating role of ruminative thinking in the relationship between physical activity and sleep quality in college students

Physical activity influenced college students' sleep quality through the mediating role of ruminative thinking, as shown by the following: physical activity significantly negatively predicted ruminative thinking, and the higher the ruminative thinking, the higher the college students' sleep quality. This validates hypothesis H3 of this paper and is consistent with the results of existing studies [27, 59]. Research has shown that when the physical activity atmosphere is good, individuals focus on exercise movements, teamwork, or sports goals, etc., which reduces repetitive thoughts about their negative emotions, thoughts, or behavioral states, thus reducing the likelihood that individuals will experience depression and anxiety [60]. The negative emotions associated with ruminative thinking lead individuals to develop unhealthy behaviors such as alcohol abuse, lack of exercise, which interferes with the functioning of the nervous system and affects the rhythm and depth of sleep, and lack of exercise, which fails to promote the body's release of endorphins and other substances that help relax and improve mood [61]. At the same time, ruminative thinking triggers a physiological stress response in the body, prompting the secretion of stress hormones such as adrenaline and cortisol, which puts the body in a tense state of arousal and prevents it from relaxing [62], the brain is also in a highly active state due to ruminative thinking, which interferes with the brain's transition from wakefulness to the calm, relaxed state required for sleep, ultimately leading to poor sleep quality [63]. Thus, ruminative thinking plays a separate mediating role between physical activity and sleep quality in college students.

Chain mediation of stress perception and ruminative thinking

The findings of this study suggest a chain-mediating role of stress perception and ruminative thinking in the relationship between physical activity and sleep quality among college students. Specifically, heightened stress perception has been shown to trigger enhanced ruminative thinking, and stress perception has been found to significantly and positively predict ruminative thinking. Additionally, a positive correlation has been observed between stress perception and ruminative thinking. This validates hypothesis H4 of this paper and is consistent with the results of existing studies [34]. The ruminative thinking-based stress response model posits that [64], it has been demonstrated that individuals are more prone to rumination after experiencing stressful events. The phenomenon of stress has been shown to elicit negative emotions, which, upon repeated exposure, become deeply ingrained in one's cognitive landscape. This phenomenon, known as rumination, has been demonstrated to perpetuate elevated levels of cognitive arousal in individuals experiencing stress. This phenomenon has been demonstrated to elicit adverse emotional responses and to compound preexisting disturbances in sleep quality [65]. According to Nolen-Hoeksema et al. (1994), when individuals are exposed to high-intensity stressors over a prolonged period of time and are faced with many uncontrollable factors, the incongruence between the internal psychological equilibrium and the external environment will cause individuals to adopt a ruminative thinking mode of thinking, in which they repeatedly focus on their negative emotions, thoughts, and behaviors and become caught up in a cycle of thinking about the causes and consequences of the negative state without actively exploring ways to solve the problem. In this mode, the individual repeatedly focuses on his/her negative emotions, thoughts, and behaviors, and gets caught up in a cycle of thinking about the causes and consequences of the negative state, but is unable to actively explore ways to solve the problem [66]. Meanwhile, an experimental study of stress perception and ruminative thinking in college students found, through a series of measurements and data analyses, that when college students perceive an increase in stress, they are more likely to fall into a ruminative thinking dilemma, and that this change in thinking patterns further affects the quality of their sleep [45]. A considerable body of scholarly work has elucidated the theoretical underpinnings of stress perception, emphasizing its association with negative evaluations derived from the interplay between an individual's cognitive processes and external stressors. This theoretical framework posits that ruminative thinking, characterized as a form of negative cognition and thinking, plays a pivotal role in the stress perception process. Specifically, it is postulated that when an individual's stress perception reaches a heightened state, there is an increased propensity for the onset of ruminative thinking [67]. In summary, the chain mediating role of stress perception and ruminative thinking between physical activity and sleep quality in college students is feasible.

Limitations and prospects

This study examines the relationship between physical activity and sleep quality among college students. The constructed chain mediation model reveals the underlying mechanism through which physical activity influences sleep quality. It provides important theoretical foundations and practical guidance for enhancing college students' sleep quality, while also laying the groundwork for further improvements in their sleep quality. However, the study has limitations that require refinement. First, this study employed questionnaire surveys to collect data, which may introduce common method bias. Since all data originated from student self-assessments, it is susceptible to subjective cognitive biases and social desirability effects, potentially compromising measurement accuracy. Future research should gather data from multiple sources—including teachers, peers, and parents—to reduce single-source bias and enhance result reliability. Second, this study only examined the mediating role of perceived stress and rumination thinking in the relationship between physical activity and college students' sleep quality. In reality, numerous other variables—such as subjective well-being and social support—may also influence this relationship. Therefore, subsequent research should incorporate these variables and explore their interactive relationships across multiple dimensions to deepen our understanding of the connection between physical activity and college students' sleep quality. Regarding sample selection, the college student population encompasses vocational students, undergraduates, and postgraduates, with differences in physical activity and psychological states across academic stages. However, this study's sample was limited to freshmen through seniors, resulting in a narrow focus. Furthermore, the sample only covered institutions in Liaoning Province. Due to the region's cold winters and limited outdoor exercise conditions, students primarily exercised indoors. To enhance the generalizability and representativeness of the findings, future research should broaden the sample scope to include students from different types of universities and academic levels. It may even consider incorporating students from diverse geographic regions and disciplinary backgrounds. This approach would fully account for and evaluate the distinct characteristics of different student groups, thereby more accurately reflecting the true relationship between physical activity and sleep quality. Finally, as this study employed a cross-sectional design, subsequent research could adopt longitudinal or experimental designs to further validate the causal relationships among physical activity, perceived stress, rumination thinking, and sleep quality. While this study achieved certain outcomes, limitations exist in variable exploration, sample selection, and research methodology. Future research should delve deeper into these limitations to provide more robust theoretical support for improving college students' sleep quality.
