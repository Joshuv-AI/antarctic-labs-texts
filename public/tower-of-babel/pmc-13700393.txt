Comparison of demographic, clinical and follow-up data of patients with micromegaly and acromegaly: a retrospective analysis from a single tertiary center
Authors: Canan Sehit Kara, Gokcem Yalin Kocamaz, İzzet Okcesiz, Emre Urhan, Aysa Hacioglu, Zuleyha Karaca, Kursad Unluhizarci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMicromegaly is a term suggested to describe patients with clinical and biochemical evidence of acromegaly despite apparently normal GH secretion. However, its clinical characteristics including biochemical definition, treatment outcomes, and long-term prognosis remain incompletely understood. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of micromegaly and compare the demographic, clinical, biochemical, radiological, and long-term follow-up characteristics of patients with micromegaly and classical acromegaly.MethodsA retrospective chart review was conducted in patients with acromegaly, who were diagnosed and subsequently followed up in Erciyes University Department of Endocrinology between January 2010 and September 2025. Micromegaly was defined on the basis of the finding of a baseline growth hormone (GH) < 2 ng/mL and/or glucose-suppressed GH nadir < 1 ng/mL, in addition to an IGF-1 level above the upper limit of normal in a patient with the classic symptoms and signs of the disease.ResultsAmong 117 patients with acromegaly, 14 (12%) were classified as having micromegaly. No statistically significant differences were observed in the age at diagnosis, gender distribution, comorbidities and clinical symptoms between patients with acromegaly and micromegaly. The micromegaly group demonstrated a higher incidence of microadenoma, with a significantly smaller maximal tumor size compared to the acromegaly group. The rate of surgical intervention, the total number of operations, and the rate of additional medical treatment among patients with acromegaly were significantly higher than among patients with micromegaly. The biochemical control rate observed at 3 months postoperatively was higher in the micromegaly group, while the remission rate at the last visit was similar between the two groups.ConclusionPatients classified as having micromegaly according to the biochemical criteria applied in this study exhibited clinical manifestations and comorbidity profiles comparable to those of patients with classical acromegaly despite lower GH levels and smaller tumors. Sensitivity analyses using alternative biochemical definitions demonstrated a broadly consistent overall clinical pattern across the different classification approaches. These findings suggest that apparently low GH secretion does not necessarily indicate clinically mild disease. Prospective multicentre studies using standardized biochemical definitions are required to further clarify the biological basis and clinical significance of micromegaly.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12902-026-02444-2.

Introduction

Acromegaly is generally characterised by excessive growth hormone (GH) secretion from a pituitary adenoma and the subsequent elevated secretion of insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) [1]. In healthy individuals, the release of GH occurs episodically, mostly during sleep or physical activity [2]. Random daytime GH measurements are usually very low for approximately 80% of the day and may range from undetectable to secretory peaks of up to 15 µg/l or higher in normal subjects, observed mainly at night. Increased body mass index (BMI) and obesity attenuate GH secretion, while malnutrition and prolonged fasting result in elevated GH pulse frequency and amplitude [3]. Conversely, serum IGF-1 levels exhibit stability over a 24-hour period, thereby providing a reliable metric for evaluating GH action. In cases of acromegaly, GH is secreted in an autonomous manner, resulting in excess GH and IGF-1 [4].

GH and IGF-1 offer different information, but together they provide a more comprehensive picture. GH is a measure of the secretory activity of the tumor and thus serves as a tumor marker, while IGF-1 is a better measure of the symptomatic and metabolic consequences of acromegaly [5–7]. IGF-1 measurement is the preferred diagnostic test in suspected acromegaly due to its lack of diurnal variation. The measurement of serum GH levels in a random serum sample (random GH) or after an overnight fast (baseline GH, bGH) is not generally recommended as a diagnostic tool for acromegaly. Nevertheless, these measurements are commonly used in the initial biochemical assessment of patients with suspected acromegaly [8]. In addition, their association with treatment outcomes and complications has been well documented [9]. OGTT remains an established tool for confirming the diagnosis of acromegaly particularly in patients where serum IGF level was equivocal. Glucose-suppressed GH nadir (nGH) is an indirect measure of IGF-1 and reflects preserved GH neuroregulation [2]. However, the interpretation of nGH values may be challenging in selected clinical situations since apparently normal GH suppression may occur in some patients with clinically confirmed disease [10]. Furthermore, the role of OGTT in the diagnostic workup of acromegaly has evolved in recent guidelines; while the 2014 Endocrine Society guideline recommended OGTT for confirmation of diagnosis, the 2024 Acromegaly Consensus Conference suggested that OGTT should be reserved for patients with persistently equivocal IGF-1 results [11, 12].

Despite the majority of patients with acromegaly exhibiting elevated levels of both IGF-1 and GH, a discrepancy between these levels was identified in 9.4–39% of patients [13]. Micromegaly is defined as a group of patients exhibiting clinical signs of acromegaly, elevated IGF-1 levels despite an apparently normal pattern of GH secretion. However, no single biochemical definition or diagnostic threshold has been universally accepted for this condition. The term “micromegaly” was first coined by Dimaraki et al. in a study of 63 patients with acromegaly, 16 of whom displayed the condition [14]. However, this patient group was actually first described by Brockmeier et al. in 1992, although it was not given the name micromegaly [15]. The question of whether “micromegaly” is a distinct clinical entity or a manifestation of classic acromegaly in its early stages remains the subject of considerable debate [16]. The aim of this study is to determine the prevalence of micromegaly and to compare the demographic, clinical, biochemical and radiological characteristics as well as the treatment outcomes of patients classified as having micromegaly or classical acromegaly.

Methods

Study subjects

This retrospective cohort study comprised 117 patients with acromegaly who were diagnosed and followed up at the Erciyes University Department of Endocrinology between January 2010 and September 2025. Patients lacking IGF-1 or GH measurements at the time of diagnosis, exhibiting renal or hepatic impairment, with a history of active malignancy or a diagnosis of malignancy within the past five years, who are pregnant, exhibiting plural hormone secretion or using medication with the potential to affect the GH-IGF-1 axis were excluded. All patients exhibited an acromegalic phenotype, had a pituitary tumor, and all but six underwent transsphenoidal surgery (TSS). All surgical procedures were performed by the same neurosurgical team using a standardized transsphenoidal technique, and all patients were followed up by the same endocrinology team throughout the study period. A somatotroph adenoma was identified in all operated patients, both histologically and immunochemically.

The Ethics Committee of Erciyes University Medical School approved this study (Approval number: 2025/403).

Study design and definitions

The following factors were analysed in a retrospective chart review: age, gender, comorbid diseases (diabetes mellitus (DM), hypertension (HT), dyslipidemia, obesity), the presence of acromegaly signs and symptoms (headache, enlarged hands and feet, changes in facial structure, excessive sweating and joint pain), BMI, maximal pituitary adenoma dimension, biochemical values (IGF-1, bGH, nGH, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), creatinine, total cholesterol, triglyceride, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), prolactin levels), presence of pituitary hormone deficiencies at the time of diagnosis, treatment modalities (surgery, medical therapy, radiotherapy or none); and patient status at last visit (remission or active disease).

The diagnosis of acromegaly was made based on the presence of classic acromegaly symptoms and elevated IGF-1 levels, which were above the reference range adjusted for age and gender. Micromegaly was defined as the presence of classic acromegaly symptoms and signs, IGF-1 levels above the age- and sex-adjusted reference range, and at least one of the following biochemical criteria: bGH < 2 ng/mL or nGH < 1 ng/mL during OGTT. These definitions were also applied separately using each individual biochemical criterion (bGH < 2 ng/mL alone, nGH < 1 ng/mL alone, and nGH < 0.4 ng/mL alone).

The nGH is defined as the lowest GH value obtained at 0, 30, 60, 90 and 120 min after the administration of a 75-gram oral glucose load during an overnight fast. BMI was calculated according to the formula (body weight (kg)/ height (m2)).

Biochemical control was defined as a normal IGF-1 level, adjusted for age and gender, measured at 3 months following pituitary surgery. During the most recent visit, patients exhibiting normal IGF-1 levels were categorised as being in remission, while those with elevated levels were defined as having active disease. Pituitary adenomas with a size of less than 10 mm were classified as microadenomas, while those with a size of 10 mm or greater were classified as macroadenomas. MRI assessments were performed by an experienced radiologist in neuroradiology.

Biochemical measurements

Cholesterol, HbA1c and basal pituitary hormone levels were measured in the morning after an overnight fast. Triglyceride, total cholesterol and HDL-C levels were measured by the spectrophotometric enzymatic method (Cobas; Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). LDL-C levels were calculated with the Friedewald formula. TSH, free T3, free T4, FSH, LH, estradiol, testosterone, prolactin, cortisol, IGF-1 and GH levels were measured using the electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) technique with commercially available assays (Cobas; Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). Serum prolactin levels were measured by the ECLIA method based on sandwich complex formation using two monoclonal antibodies specifically against human prolactin. All biochemical measurements were performed in the accredited central laboratory of Erciyes University using standardized commercially available assays according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics version 22.0 (IBM Inc. Chicago, IL, USA) was used for analysis. The normal distribution of the data was tested using a one-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (minimum-maximum) according to the distribution of the data. To compare continuous variables based on the distribution of the data, the Mann-Whitney U test or the two independent samples t-test was utilized. Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used for comparing categorical variables. In addition, sensitivity analyses were performed by repeating all primary comparisons using each biochemical criterion separately (bGH < 2 ng/mL alone, nGH < 1 ng/mL alone, and nGH < 0.4 ng/mL alone) instead of the combined biochemical classification. These analyses were undertaken to evaluate whether the principal findings remained robust when alternative biochemical definitions of micromegaly were applied. Results of the sensitivity analyses are presented in Supplementary Tables S1–S3. A probability (p) value lower than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic data

The prevalence of micromegaly was found to be 12% (14/117) of all patients with acromegaly. According to the criterion of bGH below 2 ng/mL the prevalence was 9.4% (11/117). OGTT was performed in 85 cases and when the criterion of nGH levels below 1 ng/mL was used the prevalence was found to be 12.9% (11/85) and when the criterion of nGH levels below 0.4 ng/mL was used the prevalence was 5.9% (5/85).

The demographic data for the acromegaly and micromegaly groups were summarised in Table 1. The mean age at diagnosis was 44.9 ± 12.8 years for the acromegaly group and 44.6 ± 10.1 years for the micromegaly group (p = 0.92). While 53.4% of patients with acromegaly were female, 71.4% of patients with micromegaly were female (p = 0.20). The symptomatology of the two groups was similar, although a trend toward significance was observed in acral enlargement (p = 0.054). The comorbidity rates, including HT, DM, dyslipidemia and obesity, were similar (Table 1).

Biochemical data and tumor size

All patients exhibited elevated plasma IGF-1 levels. The mean levels of IGF-1 were higher in the acromegaly group (784.8 ± 292 ng/mL) than in the micromegaly group (445.6 ± 187.5 ng/mL) (p < 0.001). When the results were adjusted for the upper limit of normal (ULN), it was found that IGF-1 x ULN levels were significantly lower among patients with micromegaly than among those with acromegaly (p < 0.001). Similarly, the bGH and nGH values were found to be lower in the micromegaly group (p < 0.001, p < 0.001, respectively). Five patients in the micromegaly group had nGH values below 0.4 ng/mL, with the lowest value being 0.05 ng/mL. Subsequent analysis revealed that cholesterol, HbA1c and creatinine levels and hypopituitarism rates were similar between the two groups, as seen in Table 1.

The mean maximal pituitary tumor dimension was 15.8 ± 7.5 mm for the acromegaly group and 11.3 ± 7.4 mm for the micromegaly group, indicating that the maximum tumor size was significantly lower in the micromegaly group (p = 0.04). Concurrently, the rate of microadenoma was found to be statistically significantly higher in the micromegaly group (p = 0.006). Biochemical data and tumor measurements of the micromegaly and acromegaly groups were summarised in Table 1.

Management strategies and long-term outcomes

A significantly higher proportion (98.1%) of patients with acromegaly underwent transsphenoidal surgery (TSS), compared to patients with micromegaly (71.4%) (p = 0.002). Of the four patients diagnosed with micromegaly who refused surgical intervention, none subsequently accepted medical treatment. During their most recent visit, active disease findings were still present and their IGF-1 values remained above the reference value. The number of surgeries and use of medical treatment were lower in patients with micromegaly than in acromegaly (p = 0.01).

Following the first TSS, a three-month evaluation of biochemical parameters revealed that although patients in the acromegaly group demonstrated a greater decrease in IGF-1 and GH, the IGF-1 x ULN and GH values remained significantly lower and the biochemical control rate was significantly higher in the micromegaly group than in acromegaly (Table 2). However, when the long-term results were analysed, the remission rate at the last visit was found to be comparable between the two groups (p = 0.54). Treatment modalities and long-term outcomes of patients with micromegaly and acromegaly were summarized in Table 3. Additionally, individual characteristics of patients with micromegaly were outlined in Table 4.

Sensitivity analyses using each biochemical criterion separately (bGH < 2 ng/mL, nGH < 1 ng/mL, and nGH < 0.4 ng/mL) showed a broadly similar overall clinical pattern to that observed in the primary analysis, although the statistical significance of some individual comparisons varied, particularly in the smaller subgroups. Discussion

In the present study, 14 of 117 patients with acromegaly were found to have bGH < 2 ng/mL or nGH < 1 ng/mL and the micromegaly rate was determined to be 12%. Patients with micromegaly had lower IGF-1 and GH levels, a higher incidence of microadenoma and similar clinical features and comorbidities. The requirement of second surgery, RT and medical therapy was higher in patients with classical acromegaly.

Reported prevalence rates of micromegaly vary considerably, ranging from 2.4% to 32.5% [10, 13–22]. Previous studies have used different biochemical approaches to identify patients with apparently normal GH secretion, including 24-hour mean GH profiles, nGH suppression during OGTT, bGH thresholds, or combinations of these parameters [14, 17–22]. Consequently, these approaches identify partially overlapping but not identical patient populations, contributing to the wide variability in the reported prevalence of micromegaly and limiting direct comparisons of prevalence and clinical characteristics across studies.

Among the different biochemical approaches reported in the literature, the nGH threshold of < 1 ng/mL has been adopted in several studies. This threshold corresponded to the conventional OGTT criterion used at the time these studies were conducted to confirm active acromegaly by demonstrating failure of GH suppression below 1 ng/mL following an oral glucose load [11]. Consequently, patients with unequivocal clinical acromegaly despite GH suppression below this threshold represent a recognized biochemical discordance that has attracted increasing clinical interest. Mercado et al. used this threshold as the sole biochemical criterion for micromegaly [17], while Freda et al. reported that a subset of newly diagnosed patients with acromegaly exhibited both basal and nGH concentrations below 1 ng/mL [20]. The clinical relevance of this biochemical pattern is further supported by Ribeiro-Oliveira et al., who found that 13 of 40 (32.5%) consecutive patients with newly diagnosed, clinically and biochemically overt acromegaly had a glucose-suppressed nGH below 1 ng/mL [10], demonstrating that apparently normal GH suppression may occur despite unequivocal clinical and biochemical evidence of acromegaly. In our cohort, OGTT was performed in 85 patients, of whom 11 (12.9%) fulfilled the nGH < 1 ng/mL criterion, whereas only 5 (5.9%) fulfilled the more stringent nGH < 0.4 ng/mL criterion.

A bGH threshold of < 2 ng/mL has also been adopted in several clinical series evaluating patients with apparently normal bGH secretion [18, 19]. Among these studies, Espinosa de los Monteros et al. and Inácio et al. reported micromegaly prevalences of 3% and 8.1%, respectively [18, 19]. The prevalence observed in our cohort (12%) was higher than that reported in these studies while remaining within the broad range reported in the literature. This difference may partly reflect the use of combined biochemical criteria, as three of the 14 patients fulfilled only the nGH criterion despite having a bGH concentration ≥ 2 ng/mL. Given the absence of a universally accepted biochemical definition of micromegaly, the combined biochemical criteria used in the present study were intended to incorporate the two most commonly used biochemical approaches reported in the literature, thereby reflecting the current heterogeneity in diagnostic practice. Therefore, differences in micromegaly prevalence should be interpreted in the context of variations in study design, patient selection, GH assays, and the biochemical criteria used for patient classification, rather than being attributed solely to any single methodological factor.

To further evaluate whether the combined biochemical criteria introduced clinically meaningful heterogeneity, we performed additional sensitivity analyses by applying each biochemical criterion separately rather than in combination: bGH < 2 ng/mL alone, nGH < 1 ng/mL alone, and the more stringent nGH < 0.4 ng/mL threshold alone, the latter reflecting the lower cut-off introduced with ultrasensitive GH assays [12] (Supplementary Tables S1–S3). Across the three alternative biochemical thresholds, patients in the biochemically “low” subgroup generally showed the same overall clinical pattern observed in the primary analysis, with similar trends toward lower IGF-1 concentrations, a higher frequency of microadenomas, smaller tumor size, and lower treatment requirements than the corresponding comparison groups. Statistical significance was consistently maintained for postoperative GH levels, surgical intervention rate, and the number of transsphenoidal procedures, whereas it varied for IGF-1 concentrations, microadenoma frequency, and tumor size, particularly in the smaller nGH-based subgroups (n = 11 and n = 5), where the limited sample size reduced statistical power for several comparisons. This pattern supports our interpretation of the findings as hypothesis-generating: although the overall direction of the observed differences was preserved across the alternative biochemical definitions, the magnitude and statistical significance of individual findings remained sensitive to sample size and the biochemical threshold applied. These findings provide additional support for the internal consistency of our principal observations but should be interpreted cautiously and confirmed in larger prospective multicentre cohorts.

Numerous studies have identified several notable differences between patients with micromegaly and those with acromegaly. These include a higher mean age and a greater prevalence of male patients among the former [10, 14, 16, 18–20]. In the present study, however, the two groups were comparable in terms of age and even gender, with the micromegaly group being predominantly female but statistically not significant. The symptoms of acromegaly, BMI and comorbid diseases were found to be similar between the two groups, as has been reported in many published studies [10, 16, 18, 19, 21]. The mean IGF-1 and median bGH and nGH values were found to be lower in the micromegaly group than in the acromegaly group. A comparison of the mean levels of cholesterol, HbA1c and rates of hypopituitarism revealed no statistically significant difference between the two groups. The maximum tumor size was found to be smaller in the micromegaly group and the microadenoma rate was also statistically higher in this group, a finding consistent with many other studies [10, 14, 16, 18, 19, 22]. The question of whether micromegaly is an early stage of acromegaly or a distinct clinical entity remains unresolved in the current literature [16]. In the present study, although lower GH and IGF-1 levels and a higher frequency of microadenomas could suggest earlier-stage disease, patients with micromegaly exhibited symptom and comorbidity profiles comparable to those of patients with classical acromegaly despite lower hormone levels and smaller tumor size. Although the underlying pathophysiology remains incompletely understood, several mechanisms have been proposed, including prolonged exposure to mildly elevated tonic GH secretion, increased peripheral sensitivity to GH, and the presence of biologically active GH isoforms that may not be fully detected by conventional assays [14, 16]. These mechanisms may contribute to clinically significant disease manifestations despite relatively low GH levels and smaller tumors.

The majority of studies in the literature have compared demographic, biochemical and radiological characteristics between the two groups. Only a limited number of studies have analyzed treatment modalities of these patient groups [18, 19, 21]. In one study, 13 out of 16 patients with micromegaly (81.3%) underwent transsphenoidal surgery, while the remaining three were treated with octreotide. It is important to note that no comparison was made between the micromegaly and acromegaly groups [18]. In the present study, transsphenoidal surgery was performed on 10 out of 14 patients with micromegaly (71.4%), while the remaining four patients declined surgical intervention and subsequently refused medical treatment as well. Therefore, the lower surgical intervention rate observed in the micromegaly group (71.4% vs. 98.1%) should be interpreted with caution, as it was influenced by treatment refusal in these patients rather than disease-related factors alone. Nevertheless, this observation raises an important concern: the smaller tumor size and lower biochemical values seen in patients with micromegaly may lead to underestimation of disease severity, potentially influencing treatment decisions. In this clinical context, the term ‘micromegaly’ does not imply that the disease is less severe or that all tumours are microadenomas. As previously mentioned, 42.9% of micromegaly patients in this study had a macroadenoma. Despite the markedly lower macroadenoma rate observed in the micromegaly group compared with the acromegaly group, it is noteworthy that this condition constitutes a substantial proportion of the micromegaly patient population. Furthermore, an analysis of the comorbidities and clinical symptoms exhibited by patients with micromegaly revealed notable similarities to those observed in acromegaly cases. These findings emphasize that the term “micromegaly” should not be interpreted as indicating clinically mild disease.

There are only a few studies that compare the postoperative remission and long-term outcomes of patients with micromegaly and acromegaly [18, 19, 21]. In the study by Inacio et al., postoperative remission was defined as IGF-1 levels within the normal range for age and gender, GH < 1 ng/mL, or nGH < 0.4 ng/mL at 3–6 months after TSS. Postoperative remission was observed in 75% of micromegaly patients and 36.4% of acromegaly patients. At the final visit, patients were categorised into one of four groups: remission, IGF-1/GH discordance, pharmacologically controlled, or active disease. No statistical difference was found between the two groups. However, it was noted that the small number of patients (6 with micromegaly and 68 with acromegaly) limited the statistical significance of the findings [19]. In another study, postoperative remission was defined as an IGF-1 < 1.2 x ULN at 6 months after surgery and postoperative remission was achieved in 53.8% of patients with micromegaly. Of all patients with micromegaly, half received somatostatin analogue therapy, with biochemical control achieved in 37.5% of those receiving SSA at the final visit [18]. In the present study, the term “biochemical control” was used instead of “postoperative remission,” and it was defined as the IGF-1 value measured at 3 months after surgery being within the normal range, adjusted to age and gender. Biochemical control was achieved in 90% of micromegaly patients and 47.5% of acromegaly patients, with a statistically significant difference in biochemical control favoring micromegaly patients. Moreover, in distinction to other studies, the patients’ IGF-1 and GH decline levels after the first TSS and their IGF-1 and GH values at the third postoperative month were compared. While patients in the acromegaly group demonstrated a greater decrease in IGF-1 and GH, the IGF-1 x ULN and GH values remained lower in the micromegaly group. It should be noted that the initial discrepancy in GH levels between the two groups may limit the direct comparability of absolute postoperative GH values. The lower postoperative GH values observed in the micromegaly group are at least partly expected given the lower preoperative GH levels in this group, and these findings should therefore be interpreted accordingly. However, when long-term results were compared, although the remission rate was higher in the micromegaly group, no statistical difference was found between the two groups. This finding should be interpreted in the context of differences in treatment pathways, including treatment refusal among several patients with micromegaly and the greater need for reoperation and adjuvant medical therapy among patients with classical acromegaly, which may have influenced long-term outcomes.

A limitation of the present study is that it was restricted to a single tertiary centre and that the sample size of the micromegaly group was relatively small (n = 14), which may have limited the statistical power of some comparisons and increased the risk of type II error. The retrospective study design and the relatively shorter follow-up duration in the micromegaly group represent additional limitations. In addition, the lower surgical intervention rate observed in the micromegaly group partly reflected treatment refusal rather than a clinical decision, introducing a treatment-selection bias that should be considered when interpreting postoperative and long-term outcome comparisons. Furthermore, because no universally accepted biochemical definition of micromegaly exists, different classification approaches may identify partially overlapping patient populations. Although additional sensitivity analyses using alternative biochemical definitions demonstrated broadly consistent overall findings, the nGH < 0.4 ng/mL subgroup included only five patients, and the results of this subgroup analysis should therefore be interpreted with caution. Due to the retrospective design, information regarding menopausal status, menstrual cycle phase, hormone replacement therapy, and oral contraceptive use was not consistently available, precluding assessment of the potential influence of estrogen-related factors on the GH–IGF-1 axis. Although the presence of a somatotroph adenoma was confirmed histologically and immunochemically in all operated patients, more detailed histopathological and immunohistochemical characteristics, as well as MRI T2 signal characteristics, were not systematically collected as part of the study design and could therefore not be analyzed. Prospective multicentre studies using standardized biochemical definitions, larger cohorts, and longer follow-up are needed to further clarify the clinical significance and long-term outcomes of micromegaly.

Conclusion

The prevalence of micromegaly in this cohort was 12% according to the combined biochemical definition. When individual biochemical criteria were applied separately, the prevalence was 9.4% using a bGH threshold of < 2 ng/mL, 12.9% using an nGH threshold of < 1 ng/mL, and 5.9% using the more stringent nGH threshold of < 0.4 ng/mL. These estimates fall within the wide range previously reported (2.4–32.5%), although differences in prevalence across studies should be interpreted in the context of varying biochemical definitions and study methodologies. The demographic characteristics and clinical symptoms of patients with micromegaly were comparable to those of patients with classical acromegaly, whereas IGF-1 and GH concentrations, maximal tumor size, and the prevalence of macroadenomas were lower. Postoperative biochemical control was higher in the micromegaly group. Although all treated patients with micromegaly achieved remission, the final-visit evaluation included both treated and untreated patients, which may have contributed to the absence of a statistically significant difference in long-term outcomes between the two groups. The similarity in symptoms and comorbidities despite lower hormone levels and smaller tumor size—a pattern that remained broadly consistent across alternative biochemical thresholds in our sensitivity analyses—suggests that patients classified as having micromegaly may experience a clinical burden comparable to that of classical acromegaly. Whether this reflects a distinct clinical phenotype or an early stage of classical acromegaly cannot be determined from a retrospective single-centre cohort of this size. Accordingly, these observations should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in prospective multicentre studies using standardized biochemical definitions.
