Knowledge Levels and Attitudes of Intensive Care Nurses Regarding Medical Device–Related Pressure Injuries
Authors: Seçil Erden Melikoğlu, Aylin Özakgül, Kübra Ata, Kübra Vatansever, Gizem Mutlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study aims to determine the knowledge levels and attitudes of intensive care nurses regarding Medical Device–Related Pressure Injuries (MDRPI). This descriptive cross‐sectional study was conducted with 229 nurses working in intensive care units in Türkiye who were reached using the snowball sampling method. The data were collected using the ‘Nurse Information Form’, the ‘MDRPI Knowledge Questionnaire’, and the ‘Attitude Questionnaire Regarding MDRPI’. The intensive care nurse's total mean MDRPI Knowledge score was 14.75 ± 3.08, while the total mean Attitude Questionnaire score was 47.23 ± 5.50. In addition, significant positive correlations were observed between the MDRPI Knowledge and Attitude (r = 0.520, p < 0.001). Nurses who had previously provided care to patients who developed MDRPIs were found to have higher total scores on both the Knowledge and the Attitude Questionnaire (p < 0.05). In this study, intensive care nurses generally have positive attitudes toward MDRPIs; however, their knowledge is moderate and requires improvement. Moreover, indicating that attitudes became more positive as knowledge levels increased. The knowledge levels of intensive care nurses should be continuously enhanced through in‐service training programs aligned with current evidence‐based practices. In addition, educational programs should also focus on improving attitudes toward MDRPI prevention.

Introduction

Medical device–related pressure injuries (MDRPI) are defined as injuries caused by devices designed and used for diagnostic or therapeutic purposes and typically present in a pattern that conforms to the shape of the device [1]. Numerous medical devices may lead to MDRPI, including endotracheal tubes, oxygen masks, intravenous catheters, tracheostomy ties, blood pressure cuffs, nasogastric tubes, cervical collars, physical restraints, pulse oximeters, splints, casts, foley catheters, and adhesive tapes used in dressings [2, 3]. Recognized as a healthcare‐related complication, MDRPI occurs more frequently in intensive care and palliative care units, where medical devices are used extensively [4, 5, 6]. Patients in intensive care units are at high risk for the development of MDRPI due to the widespread and prolonged use of medical devices for diagnostic and therapeutic purposes and, in particular, because unconscious and critically ill patients are unable to express pain or discomfort [7, 8]. One study reported that each medical device applied to a patient in the intensive care unit increased the risk of MDRPI development by 1.16 times [9]. In a meta‐analysis by Zhang et al. [10], including 117 624 patients from various clinical settings (intensive care unit patients, hospitalized patients, and special inpatient groups), the incidence of MDRPI was 19.3%. In this meta‐analysis at the country level, Türkiye was reported as the most frequently represented country in Asia, with a pooled MDRPI incidence rate of 30.6% based on the combined results of 7 studies [10]. In a point prevalence study conducted by Pamuk Cebeci et al. in 2024, involving 102 patients from two training and research hospitals in two different provinces in Türkiye, the prevalence of MDRPI was 29.4% [6].

Medical device–related pressure injuries cause pain, reduced quality of life, and an increased risk of infection, thereby increasing care costs for both patients and healthcare systems [7, 8, 11]. Furthermore, the literature indicates that prolonged hospital stay is a risk factor for the development of MDRPI [12], and that patients who develop MDRPI tend to have longer hospital stays than those who do not [13]. This situation creates concern for healthcare services and healthcare professionals [14]. The most important step in preventing MDRPI is early identification and prevention of pressure injury development. In this context, monitoring nutrition and hydration, using support surfaces, selecting and positioning medical devices with appropriate size and design, assessing the skin under medical devices, keeping the skin clean and dry, and repositioning the patient or the medical device when necessary are recommended to prevent MDRPI [15]. At this point, the most critical responsibility lies with nurses, who constitute a significant component of the healthcare team [16]. Nurses play a vital role throughout the entire process, from identifying patients at risk of pressure injury to providing appropriate preventive care. MDRPI are considered key indicators of patient safety and the quality of nursing care in healthcare settings [2]. Nurses' knowledge of the causes and risk factors of pressure injuries and their ability to plan and implement appropriate preventive interventions contribute significantly to the prevention of MDRPI and to reducing prevalence and incidence rates [8, 17]. Therefore, nurses need adequate knowledge of MDRPI. However, having a high level of knowledge alone may not be sufficient to promote positive preventive behaviours related to MDRPI. It is emphasized that nurses must also maintain positive attitudes to translate knowledge into practice and provide adequate care [18]. In addition, insufficient knowledge and lack of experience among nurses may lead to differences in attitudes toward MDRPI [19]. A review of the literature indicates that several studies have examined intensive care nurses' knowledge of MDRPI, and their findings generally emphasize the need to improve nurses' knowledge and awareness [8, 20]. However, only a limited number of studies have evaluated both nurses' knowledge levels and attitudes toward MDRPI [18, 19]. Determining the knowledge levels and attitudes of nurses working in intensive care units, where MDRPI are most frequently observed, is highly important for patient safety and satisfaction [8]. Accordingly, this study was conducted to determine the knowledge levels and attitudes of intensive care nurses regarding medical device–related pressure injuries.

Materials and Methods

This study used a descriptive cross‐sectional design to determine the knowledge levels and attitudes of intensive care nurses regarding medical device–related pressure injuries and to examine the relationships among these variables.

Setting and Participants

The study population consisted of nurses working in intensive care units in Türkiye. The study sample comprised intensive care nurses who met the inclusion criteria, agreed to participate, and were recruited via snowball sampling. A power analysis was performed using the G*Power (v3.1.9) program to determine the required sample size. Assuming an effect size of 0.25 for the correlation analyses in the study, it was calculated that at least 137 intensive care nurses were required to achieve 85% power at an alpha level of 0.05 [21]. The study sample consisted of 229 intensive care nurses who met the inclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) being aged 18 years or older, (ii) working as a nurse in intensive care units in Türkiye, (iii) being able to use social networks and electronic resources, and (iv) willingness to participate in the study. The exclusion criterion was as follows: (i) declining to participate in the study.

Data Collection Tools

The study data were collected using the ‘Nurse Information Form’, the ‘Medical Device–Related Pressure Injury Knowledge Questionnaire’, and the ‘Attitude Questionnaire Regarding Medical Device‐Related Pressure Injuries’.

Nurse Information Form

This form was developed by the researchers based on the literature [17, 22]. It includes questions regarding nurses' individual and professional characteristics, as well as participation in training programs related to medical device–related pressure injuries, following professional publications, attending scientific meetings, and conducting research on this topic.

Medical Device–Related Pressure Injury Knowledge Questionnaire (MDRPI Knowledge Questionnaire)

Developed by Fu et al. in China, this scale measures nurses' knowledge of medical device–related pressure injuries [23]. The scale consists of 25 items across five subdimensions as ‘concepts and staging”, ‘risk assessment’, ‘selection and suitability of medical devices’, ‘care and dressing’ and ‘skin assessment’. The scale is a three‐point Likert‐type instrument as ‘correct = 1’, ‘I do not know = 0’, and ‘incorrect = 0’. Twelve items are reverse scored. The minimum possible score on the scale is 0, and the maximum possible score is 25. The increase in scores indicates that nurses' knowledge of medical device‐related pressure injuries has increased. The Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted by Baran and Özden in 2024 (Cronbach's alpha = 0.79) [17].

Attitude Questionnaire Regarding Medical Device–Related Pressure Injuries (Attitude Questionnaire Regarding MDRPI)

This questionnaire was developed by Behnammoghadam et al. to assess attitudes toward medical device–related pressure injuries [24]. The Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted by Zorlu and Bahar [22]. The questionnaire consists of 11 items and includes two subdimensions (prevention and care). The questionnaire is scored using a five‐point Likert scale. Higher scores indicate more positive attitudes toward pressure injuries caused by medical devices. When categorized, total scores of 11–25 indicate a negative attitude, 26–40 a neutral attitude, and 41–55 a positive attitude toward medical device–related pressure injuries. In the Turkish validity and reliability study, the Cronbach's alpha coefficient was reported as 0.76 [22].

Data Collection

The study data were collected between May 2024 and July 2025, after obtaining ethics committee approval (date: May 16, 2024; No: 2024/272). Data were collected from intensive care nurses using the snowball sampling method. An online survey created via Google Forms was initially distributed through social media platforms (e.g., WhatsApp, Telegram, and Instagram) to intensive care nurses known to the researchers. Participants were then asked to share the survey link with other intensive care nurses within their professional networks. Efforts were made to include nurses from diverse institutions and regions to reduce potential sampling bias. To increase participation, the survey link was repeatedly shared on social media platforms. Due to the nature of snowball sampling, it was not possible to determine how many individuals received the survey link. In total, 229 nurses completed the survey. Participation was voluntary, and electronic informed consent was obtained prior to survey initiation. All responses were collected anonymously, and no identifying information was recorded.

Data Analysis

The data obtained in the study were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 22.0. Descriptive statistics, including mean, standard deviation, median, frequency, and percentage, were used to evaluate the data. The normality of quantitative variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and graphical methods. Independent‐samples t‐tests were used to compare two groups for normally distributed variables. The Kruskal–Wallis test and Dunn–Bonferroni post hoc test were used for comparisons involving more than two groups when variables were not normally distributed. Pearson correlation analysis was used to determine the level of association between quantitative variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical Considerations

The study was approved by the Social and Human Sciences Ethics Committee of a university (date: May 16, 2024; No: 2024/272). In addition, written permission was obtained from the authors who conducted the Turkish validity and reliability studies of the scales used in the study. For nurses who received the Google Forms link, an information sheet was provided on the first page of the questionnaire, including details on the purpose, content, and scope of the study, the researchers, and the right to withdraw from the study. Informed consent was obtained from the nurses prior to answering the study questions. The principles of the Declaration of Helsinki were adhered to throughout the study.

Discussion

A wide range of medical devices are used for diagnostic and therapeutic purposes in intensive care units, placing critically ill patients at high risk for the development of MDRPIs [25]. In the present study, 74.7% of the intensive care nurses reported encountering pressure injuries related to medical device use in clinical practice at varying frequencies including sometimes (56.8%), frequently (16.6%), and almost always (1.3%). Given the high prevalence of MDRPIs in intensive care settings, nurses need to be knowledgeable about MDRPI‐related risk factors and causes, and to conduct comprehensive assessments, plan appropriate preventive nursing interventions, and implement them effectively. Regular assessment of intensive care patients and the implementation of planned preventive measures can prevent, delay, or halt the progression of MDRPIs.

In this study, which examined intensive care nurses' knowledge and attitudes regarding MDRPIs, nurses were found to have a moderate level of knowledge. Consistent with this finding, studies by Erbay Dallı and Kelebek Girgin [11] and Ali et al. [26] reported that intensive care nurses had moderate knowledge of MDRPIs. Similarly, Katran et al. [27] found that surgical intensive care nurses demonstrated moderate levels of knowledge. In a study by Sönmez and Bahar [28], in which the majority of participants were intensive care nurses, nurses were also reported to have moderate knowledge levels. While several studies align with the findings of the present study, others have reported insufficient or low knowledge levels among nurses [8, 19, 20, 23, 29, 30, 31]. In contrast, a limited number of studies have indicated acceptable or high levels of knowledge [18, 25]. These variations in findings may be attributed to differences in the structure and difficulty level of the measurement tools used to assess knowledge, such as questionnaires, scales, or tests. Additionally, differences in nurses' professional experience, prior involvement in the care of patients who developed MDRPIs, and participation in MDRPI‐related training programs or scientific activities may also contribute to the observed discrepancies. Overall, both national and international studies indicate that nurses' knowledge of MDRPIs is generally rated as insufficient to moderate, and that the desired levels of knowledge have not yet been achieved [11, 20, 23, 26]. Given that nurses bear primary responsibility for preventing MDRPI development and providing care to affected patients in intensive care units, these findings underscore the need to enhance nurses' awareness of MDRPIs and to implement continuous, up‐to‐date educational programs to improve knowledge levels.

In the present study, no significant difference in MDRPI knowledge was found among intensive care nurses across educational levels. The findings of Lyu et al. [25] also align with the present study, indicating that education level is not significantly associated with MDRPI knowledge. In contrast, Katran et al. [27] reported that knowledge levels regarding MDRPI prevention increased with higher educational attainment among surgical intensive care nurses. Likewise, several studies have demonstrated that nurses with postgraduate education possess higher knowledge levels than those with undergraduate education and that educational level is a predictor of MDRPI‐related knowledge [8, 11, 18, 23, 32]. Overall, the findings on education level and MDRPI knowledge appear inconsistent. In the present study, the lack of a significant difference may be attributable to the fact that the majority of the sample consisted of nurses with a bachelor's degree.

The study also found a significant difference in MDRPI knowledge levels among nurses who had previously cared for patients who developed MDRPIs. This finding is consistent with the results of a systematic review by Fang et al. [33], which evaluated nurses' knowledge levels regarding MDRPI prevention. These results may be explained by intensive care nurses gaining hands‐on experience through active involvement in the care of patients with MDRPIs, reinforcing learning through practice and increasing awareness. At the same time, these findings emphasize that theoretical knowledge alone is insufficient; experiential learning opportunities that integrate theory with clinical practice are also essential for improving nurses' competence in preventing and managing MDRPIs.

As the professional experience of the intensive care nurses participating in the study increased, their knowledge of MDRPI decreased. A review of the literature reveals similar findings; in the study conducted by Kurtgöz et al. [20], it was likewise reported that nurses' knowledge levels decreased as their professional experience increased. The fact that MDRPI has become an increasingly important issue in recent years and has been comprehensively addressed in nursing education curricula, in line with evidence‐based guidelines on its definition, staging, and assessment, may explain why newly graduated nurses with shorter professional experience possess higher levels of knowledge on this topic. In addition, nurses who have worked in the profession for many years may continue to rely on older protocols and routine practices in clinical settings. However, the literature also includes studies reporting no significant difference between professional experience and MDRPI knowledge level [8, 11], as well as studies indicating that MDRPI‐related knowledge increases with greater professional experience [18, 28]. These divergent findings suggest that professional experience alone is not a determining factor of nurses' knowledge levels; rather, knowledge may be influenced by institutional culture, organizational education policies, access to in‐service training and evidence‐based resources, and nurses' motivation for learning. Futhermore, while increased professional experience increases the likelihood of nurses encountering MDRPIs, it should also be considered that experience in providing direct care to patients developing MDRPIs is important.

Within the scope of the study, intensive care nurses who had previously participated in MDRPI training, followed scientific publications, engaged in scientific activities, and researched the prevention and care of MDRPIs were found to have higher levels of MDRPI‐related knowledge. Consistent with this result, the literature shows that nurses who have received MDRPI training have higher knowledge levels than intensive care nurses who have not [23, 28, 32]. Moreover, in the study by Sayed et al. [34], intensive care nurses received education on nursing practices to prevent MDRPIs, and a significant increase in knowledge was reported following the training. Similarly, Yoo et al. [35] found that video‐based training on MDRPI prevention was practical in increasing clinical nurses' knowledge of pressure injury prevention care. Overall, these findings demonstrate that educational interventions increase nurses' knowledge levels and awareness regarding MDRPIs. Furthermore, the finding that nurses who reported conducting research on MDRPI had higher knowledge scores suggests that research experience may contribute to nurses' knowledge levels regarding MDRPI. In this study, research experience was assessed using a dichotomous (yes/no) question, without specifying further details about the type of research. Consequently, it is possible that the participants may have differed in their interpretation of the term conducting research, and the findings should be interpreted with caution in this regard. However, the relatively high proportion of nurses reporting research experience in MDRPI prevention and care may be attributed to the fact that a large proportion of the sample consisted of nurses with bachelor's and graduate degrees. Considering the critical role of nurses in preventing MDRPIs, supporting nurses' professional development through ongoing training and encouraging their participation in scientific activities and research may facilitate the effective implementation of MDRPI prevention strategies.

It was determined that the intensive care nurses participating in the study had a positive attitude toward MDRPIs. Similar to our findings, Nassehi et al. [36] reported that nurses working in intensive care units and emergency departments demonstrated positive attitudes toward the prevention and care of MDRPIs, using the same scale. Zhang et al. [18] also reported that intensive care nurses had positive attitudes toward MDRPI prevention and care. Likewise, a systematic review conducted by Fang et al. [33] concluded that nurses' attitudes toward MDRPIs were generally acceptable. In contrast, other studies in the literature have reported different findings: Lv et al. [37] found that nurses' attitudes toward MDRPI prevention and care were neutral, whereas Erbay Dallı and Kelebek Girgin [11] reported that intensive care nurses had a moderate attitude. These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in the measurement instruments used to assess attitudes and to the lack of validated, reliable tools in some studies. Given the critical role of attitude in translating knowledge into practice, enhancing nurses' positive attitudes toward MDRPIs is important for improving the quality of nursing care, promoting the adoption of evidence‐based nursing interventions, and integrating knowledge into clinical practice.

A negative correlation was found between the age of intensive care nurses and their total Attitude Questionnaire Regarding MDRPI score, indicating that positive attitudes decreased with age. When examining studies in the literature that evaluated nurses' attitudes toward MDRPIs using the same scale, some reported that attitudes increased with age [37], while others found no significant relationship between age and MDRPI‐related attitudes [36]. These differences suggest that age alone is not a determining factor in shaping nurses' attitudes and that attitudes may be influenced by nurses' knowledge levels, characteristics of the clinical environment, and professional experience.

Finally, a significant difference was found between the educational levels of intensive care nurses and their attitudes toward MDRPIs, with nurses holding a bachelor's degree demonstrating more positive attitudes than those with an associate degree. This finding is consistent with those of Lv et al. [37], who found that nurses with a bachelor's degree or higher had more positive attitudes toward MDRPIs than those with lower educational attainment. Similarly, several studies have shown that nurses' attitudes toward MDRPI prevention become more positive as educational level increases [18, 33]. These findings suggest that nurses with higher educational levels exhibit more positive attitudes toward the prevention and care of MDRPIs, highlighting education level as an important determinant of attitude development and underscoring the crucial role of professional education in shaping clinical practice and attitudes.

Nurses who had cared for patients with MDRPI demonstrated more positive attitudes. This finding is consistent with the studies by Lv et al. [37] and Fang et al. [33], which reported that nurses with experience caring for patients who developed MDRPIs exhibited more positive attitudes than those without such experience. According to the study results, nurses who had previously cared for patients with MDRPIs gained practical experience, which likely contributed to greater awareness of MDRPI prevention and care, thereby fostering more positive attitudes.

The study also revealed a significant, positive relationship between intensive care nurses' levels of knowledge about MDRPIs and their attitudes toward MDRPI prevention and care, indicating that as nurses' knowledge increased, their attitudes toward MDRPI prevention and care became more positive. Additionally, the findings showed that increased knowledge in areas such as MDRPI staging, risk assessment, selection and suitability of medical devices, skin assessment, care and dressing was associated with more positive attitudes toward MDRPI prevention and care. However, the existing studies [18, 19] report a positive relationship between MDRPI‐related knowledge and attitudes and suggest that positive attitudes strengthen clinical awareness of the prevention and care of these injuries, findings consistent with the present study. In contrast to the limited number of studies examining the relationship between MDRPI‐specific knowledge and attitudes, the literature includes many studies investigating nurses' knowledge and attitudes regarding pressure injuries in general. The findings of these studies [38, 39, 40, 41] indicate a significant relationship between nurses' knowledge of pressure injuries and their attitudes toward prevention and care. Although these studies did not specifically evaluate medical device–related pressure injuries, the results are considered conceptually supportive of the MDRPI literature, given that MDRPIs are a subtype of pressure injuries. Overall, these findings suggest that increased knowledge about MDRPIs may positively influence nurses' attitudes. Positive attitudes among nurses may enhance clinical awareness and facilitate the integration of knowledge into practice. By demonstrating the relationship between nurses' MDRPI‐related knowledge levels and attitudes, this study is considered to contribute to the limited existing literature on this topic.

Strengths and Limitations

One of the main strengths of this study is that it contributes to the limited existing literature by evaluating the relationship between intensive care nurses' knowledge levels and attitudes regarding medical device–related pressure injuries (MDRPIs) using valid and reliable measurement instruments. However, the study also has several limitations. The research data were collected only from intensive care nurses who could be reached through online communication platforms such as WhatsApp, Telegram and Instagram. Therefore, the findings cannot be generalized to all intensive care nurses. In addition, intensive care nurses' knowledge levels regarding MDRPIs may change over time.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that intensive care nurses generally have positive attitudes toward the prevention and care of MDRPIs; however, their knowledge of MDRPIs is moderate and requires improvement. Moreover, a significant and positive relationship was identified between nurses' knowledge levels and their attitudes, indicating that attitudes became more positive as knowledge levels increased. In addition, intensive care nurses who had participated in MDRPI‐related training, followed scientific publications, attended scientific activities, and conducted research on this topic were found to have higher knowledge levels. Experience in caring for patients who developed MDRPIs also positively influenced nurses' knowledge levels and attitudes. Based on these findings, the knowledge levels of intensive care nurses, who are the healthcare professionals most frequently encountering MDRPIs, should be continuously enhanced through in‐service training programs aligned with current evidence‐based practices. In particular, knowledge gaps in MDRPI staging, risk assessment, selection, and appropriateness of medical devices; skin assessment; care; and dressing should be identified, and nurses should be supported through hands‐on training using innovative teaching methods, such as simulation. Considering the effect of positive attitudes on the transfer of knowledge into practice, educational programs should also focus on improving attitudes toward MDRPI prevention. In conclusion, intensive care nurses with strong knowledge and positive attitudes toward MDRPIs can deliver high‐quality nursing care, help prevent injuries, and ensure patient safety. Furthermore, given the limited number of studies evaluating nurses' knowledge and attitudes on this topic and the frequent use of different measurement tools in existing research, it is recommended that future studies assess nurses' knowledge and attitude levels using standardized, valid, and reliable measurement instruments in larger sample groups to obtain accurate information and guide improvement efforts.
