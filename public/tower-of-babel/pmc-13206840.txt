Mouse Lacrimal Gland Ductal Organoids With Controlled Polarity: A Novel Ex Vivo Research Model
Authors: Virág Csapó, Gréta Elekes, Dorottya Tálosi, Dénes Török, Tamás Bellák, Edit Tóth-Molnár
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe aim of the present study was to develop a methodology for the culture and maintenance of ductal organoids derived from isolated lacrimal gland (LG) ducts, as well as to develop an approach for experimentally controlling epithelial polarity (apical-out [AO] versus apical-in [AI]).MethodsIntra- and interlobular ducts were microdissected from the LG of FVB/N mice aged 12 to 24 weeks. Isolated ductal segments were embedded in Matrigel and expanded in defined media. AO polarity was achieved using baseline medium, whereas AI polarity was induced by a 3-day treatment with R-spondin, Wnt-3a, noggin, and Y-27632, followed by maintenance in baseline medium supplemented with forskolin. Epithelial polarity and ductal lineage identity were assessed by immunofluorescence staining for CFTR, AQP4, VPAC1/2, and NKCC1. Secretagogue-induced changes in organoid volume were quantified by video-microscopy.ResultsOrganoids formed within 7 to 10 days and were successfully passaged through passage 7 without detectable loss of morphological characteristics. CFTR exhibited apical localization, whereas AQP4 localized to the basolateral membrane. VPAC1/2 expression showed a mosaic pattern across passages. NKCC1 proteins were expressed predominantly on the basolateral surface of the organoids. In AO cultures, stimulation with secretagogues induced a significant reduction in the organoid projected area, consistent with net fluid secretion. In contrast, AI organoids exhibited stimulus-evoked alterations in luminal density without a corresponding change in the total projected area.ConclusionsDuct-derived organoids retain key molecular features of native ductal epithelium, exhibit experimentally controllable epithelial polarity. This platform provides a versatile system for investigating ductal physiology and for the development of duct-targeted therapeutic strategies for dry eye disease.

The lacrimal gland (LG) is a tubulo-acinar exocrine gland responsible for producing the aqueous layer of the tear film, which is essential for maintaining ocular surface homeostasis, providing lubrication, and delivering antimicrobial factors critical for corneal and conjunctival integrity.1 Dysfunction of the LG underlies a spectrum of ocular surface disorders that are associated with substantial patient morbidity. Among these, dry eye disease represents an increasingly prevalent public health concern and can markedly impair quality of life.2 In advanced cases, LG dysfunction can severely compromise ocular surface integrity and lead to significant visual disturbances.3 Although therapeutic options for dry eye disease are expanding, most available treatments remain largely symptomatic, and relatively few directly target the lacrimal functional unit.3

A major factor underlying these therapeutic limitations is the incomplete understanding of the cellular and molecular mechanisms governing LG function. Although the physiology of acinar cells has been extensively characterized, the ductal system remains comparatively underinvestigated.4 This knowledge gap is due, in part, to the long-standing absence of experimental approaches enabling direct functional analysis of LG duct cells. Our laboratory was the first to establish an ex vivo method for investigating LG duct function, yielding important insights into the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying ductal secretion and its regulation.5 By applying the novel isolation technique and video-microscopy, the fluid secretion capability of LG ducts across different species was demonstrated, and the role of various transmembrane transporters in ductal tear secretion was characterized.6–10 Subsequent studies further elucidated the role of autonomic regulatory mechanisms in ductal function.11 Collectively, these findings have substantially advanced the understanding of LG duct physiology and support the concept that duct cells play a critical, albeit species-dependent, role in modifying acinar fluid and shaping the final composition of LG secretions.12

Despite its utility, the isolated LG duct segment model has several limitations. The isolation procedure is time-consuming and yields only a limited number of duct segments, typically 10 to 12 per gland, which must be used within 24 hours. Moreover, this model permits direct examination only of the transporters located on the outer (basolateral) membrane. During incubation, the ends of isolated ducts seal, forming closed, sac-like structures.6 Whereas this configuration is well-suited for measuring ductal fluid secretion – manifested as ductal swelling – it precludes direct access to the apical (luminal) membrane. Consequently, the short lifespan, limited availability, and restricted experimental accessibility of the luminal surface of the ducts represent major constraints of this approach. These limitations underscore the urgent need for physiologically relevant ex vivo models that enable comprehensive investigation of LG biology and pathophysiology and facilitate the development of targeted therapies.

Organoids have emerged as a powerful experimental platform for studying tissue structure and function. These three-dimensional (3D), self-organizing multicellular systems recapitulate key architectural and functional features of native tissues under both physiological and pathological conditions.13,14 Organoids can be generated from embryonic stem cells (ESCs), induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), or tissue-specific adult stem cells (aSCs).15 Their development relies on precisely controlled modulation of the in vitro microenvironment – through the timed application of growth factors, small molecules, and extracellular matrix (ECM) components – to mimic developmental niches and promote tissue-specific differentiation and maturation.16,17

In recent years, multiple approaches have been established for the generation of LG organoids. Stem cells, such as induced pluripotent or tissue-derived mesenchymal stem cells, or tissues can serve as the source for organoid generation.18–20 These organoid structures are highly valuable as they can function as experimental models for tissue and organ development aa well as regenerative applications. Whereas organoids derived from whole LGs hold considerable promise for regenerative medicine, they are not well-suited for the selective investigation of distinct LG cell populations, such as ductal epithelial cells.

Because no existing experimental model provides direct access to the ductal apical surface with preserved duct-specific identity, we developed a stepwise protocol to generate duct-derived organoids from isolated LG duct segments. Through systematic manipulation of the culture milieu and ECM context, we established distinct epithelial polarity states (apical-in [AI] versus apical-out [AO]) and defined a reproducible method for polarity inversion. Notably, the AO configuration exposes the luminal membrane to the medium, enabling functional interrogation of apical transport pathways that were previously inaccessible. Comprehensive morphological and functional analyses confirmed the preservation of ductal identity and secretory competence. To our knowledge, this experimental model represents the first organoid system derived exclusively from LG ducts.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Wild-type FVB/N mice aged 12 to 24 weeks were used in this study. Both male and female animals were included at a 1:1 ratio. The animals were kept at a constant room temperature of 24°C with a 12-hour light–dark cycle and were allowed free access to chow and water. Prior to LG excision, ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) were injected intraperitoneally for anesthesia and analgesia. After LG excision, animals were euthanized with pentobarbital (100 mg/kg) overdose.

All experiments were conducted in compliance with the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. The protocol was approved by the Ethical Committee for the Protection of Animals in Research of the University of Szeged, Szeged, Hungary, and complied with Directive 2010/63/EU of the European Parliament on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes.

Solutions and Chemicals

The fetal bovine serum (FBS) was purchased from Biosera (Cholet, France), collagenase was obtained from Worthington Biochemical Corporation (Lakewood, NJ, USA). The HyClone Antibiotic/Antimycotic (Penicillin/Streptomycin/Fungizone) solution was purchased from Cytiva (Marlborough, MA, USA). N-acetylcysteine, nicotinamide, L-glutamine, noggin, carbachol (carbamoylcholine chloride), 8-bromoadenosine 3ʹ5ʹ-cyclic monophosphate (8-bromo cAMP), and phenylephrine were ordered from Sigma-Aldrich Corporation (Budapest, Hungary). The GlutaMAX, EGF, FGF and TrypLE Express, Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium/F-12 (DMEM/F-12) were all purchased from Gibco (New York, NY, USA). Primocin was purchased from InvivoGen (San Diego, CA, USA), B27 was acquired from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Matrigel was produced from Corning (Corning, NY, USA). Prostaglandin E2, A83-01 (TGF-β receptor inhibitor), and Rho-kinase inhibitor Y-27632 were purchased from Tocris (Bristol, UK). Forskolin was purchased from AK Scientific (Union City, CA, USA), R-spondin from Proteintech (San Diego, CA, USA), and wnt-3a from BioTechne (Minneapolis, MN, USA). Primary antibodies against CFTR and AQP4 (rabbit polyclonal) were obtained from Alomone Labs (Jerusalem, Israel). Antibodies against VPAC1, VPAC2, NKCC1, and MIST1 as well as secondary antibodies and fluoroshield mounting medium containing DAPI, were purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Paraformaldehyde was purchased from Molar Chemicals Ltd. (Halasztelek, Hungary).

Isolation of LG Duct Segments

Dissected LGs were placed in ice-cold antibiotic/antimycotic solution for 15 minutes. The glands were then injected with collagenase solution and incubated at 37°C for 6 minutes and 30 seconds. Subsequently, the glands were transferred to an isolation solution containing 10% (v/v) FBS, 2% antibiotic/antimycotic solution, and 1% L-glutamine and kept at 4°C for 30 minutes. Interlobular and intralobular duct segments were isolated by mechanical microdissection as previously described.5

Culturing of LG Duct Cell Organoids

We adapted a previously established isolation protocol to improve the development and maintenance of ductal organoids. LGs were incubated in an antibiotic/antimycotic solution for 15 minutes to minimize contamination. Microdissection was then performed as described in the original method.5 Figure 1 demonstrates isolated LG duct segments at two different magnifications (A: 40× and B: 100×). The isolated duct segments were transferred into Eppendorf tubes containing DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 2% AA, and 1% L-glutamine, and centrifuged at 800×g for 8 minutes. The ductal segments were embedded in 20 µL of a 1:1 mixture of Matrigel and DMEM/F12 (10 µL each). Matrigel domes were formed in individual wells of a 24-well plate and incubated at 37°C for 20 to 25 minutes to allow solidification. After polymerization, 500 µL of complete organoid culture medium was added to each well. Organoids were allowed to develop for 7 to 10 days before the first passage (Fig. 2).

The culturing process of LG duct cell organoids. The image was generated by Chat GPTplus 5.2.

Passaging of Cultures

Cultures were passaged after 7 to 14 days as needed. For passaging, Matrigel domes containing organoids were disrupted using ice-cold TrypLE Express and incubated at 37°C for 6 minutes and 30 seconds to facilitate enzymatic digestion. Cell suspensions were centrifuged at 1000×g for 8 minutes. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was resuspended in fresh DMEM/F12 with thorough mixing. This wash step, including centrifugation (1000×g for 8 minutes), was repeated twice to ensure removal of residual enzymatic solution. Following the final wash, the pellet was resuspended in a 1:1 mixture of Matrigel and DMEM/F12. Matrigel domes containing LG duct cell aggregates were passaged and distributed into two new wells to enable continued expansion. After polymerization for 20 to 25 minutes at 37°C in a 95% O₂ / 5% CO₂ atmosphere, 500 µL of complete LG duct cell organoid medium was added to each well.

Subsequent passages (P2, P3, etc.) followed the same protocol, except that passaging was performed every 10 to 14 days depending on organoid density and morphology.

Establishment and Maintenance of the AO Organoid Cell Culture

Viable duct segments were embedded in 50% (v/v) Matrigel. After 20 minutes, cultures were overlaid with culture medium consisting of DMEM/F12 supplemented with 2% penicillin/strepomycin-amphotericin B, Primocin, 1% GlutaMAX, 1x B27 supplement, 1.25 mM N-acetylcysteine, 10 mM nicotinamide, 500 ng/mL EGF, 100 ng/mL FGF-10, 500 nM A83-01, and 1 µM prostaglandin E2. The culture medium was changed every 2 to 3 days. Organoids were maintained up to passage 7 in our experiments.

Establishment and Maintenance of the AI Organoid Cell Culture

The culture medium was based on that used for AO organoid cultures, with the following modifications: addition of R-spondin (500 ng/mL), Wnt-3a (30 ng/mL), noggin (100 ng/mL), forskolin (1 µM), and the Rho-kinase inhibitor Y-27632 (10 µM). The concentration of EGF was reduced to 50 ng/mL. After 3 days of culture, the supplemented medium was replaced with AO culture medium further enhanced with forskolin.

Immunofluorescent Staining

Immunofluorescent staining was performed as previously described.8 Briefly, 12 µm cryosections were prepared using a Leica cryostat (Wetzlar, Germany) after fixation in 2% paraformaldehyde and dehydration in 30% sucrose solution. Samples were incubated overnight at 4°C with one of the following primary antibodies: anti-CFTR, anti-VPAC1, anti-VPAC2, anti-NKCC1, anti-AQP4, or anti-MIST1. Antibodies were diluted in 5% BSA-TBS as follows: 1:500 for CFTR, VIPAC1, and VIPAC2, 1:400 for MIST1, and 1:200 for NKCC1 and AQP4. Subsequently, sections were incubated with AlexaFluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody for 2 hours at room temperature. Finally, slides were mounted with fluoroshield mounting medium containing DAPI, and images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM 800 confocal microscope (Oberkochen, Germany).

Video-Microscopy

Video-microscopy was performed as previously described.6 In this study, alpha-adrenergic agonist phenylephrine (10 µM), cholinergic agonist carbachol (100 µM), and cell-permeable cAMP analogue 8-bromo-cAMP (100 µM) were applied in HEPES solution. Images were analyzed, and cell area was quantified using Fiji software.21 During the first 5 minutes, cultures were washed with HEPES solution; from 6 to 20 minutes, secretagogues were applied. To assess cell integrity, a hypotonic solution was subsequently administered.

Statistical Analysis

Cross-sectional areas measured at baseline (5 minutes) and after stimulation (20 minutes), expressed as the pixel area, were compared using paired two-tailed Student's t-tests, as measurements were derived from the same organoids before and after treatment. Analyses were conducted separately across 6 experimental conditions: carbachol, 8-bromo-cAMP, and phenylephrine, each examined in both AI and AO configurations. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. Image quantification was conducted using Fiji software, and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, version 10.3.1; San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Generation of Organoid Structures From Isolated LG Duct Segments

Following enzymatic digestion and mechanical microdissection, isolated LG duct segments served as the source for organoid establishment. For the generation of AI organoids, the culture medium was supplemented for 3 days with R-spondin, Wnt-3a, noggin, and Rho-kinase inhibitor. In the absence of these supplements, organoids exhibited an AO polarity. Figure 3 illustrates representative AI and AO organoid structures across multiple passages. In bright-field images, AO organoids appeared less translucent and optically denser than AI organoids.

Although the organoid cultures were derived from isolated LG duct segments, the cultures were stained for the acinar marker MIST1 to confirm their ductal lineage origin. We observed negative staining for MIST1 across passages, which supports the ductal (and non-acinar) origin of our organoid cultures (Fig. 4).

Immunofluorescent staining of MIST1. Negative staining of MIST1 was observed demonstrating the ductal lineage of the organoids.

CFTR Expression and Localization

CFTR plays a key role in the regulation of ductal fluid secretion and serves as a marker of ductal epithelial cells in the LG. Our previous studies demonstrated apical localization of CFTR in LG ductal epithelium.8

In the present study, organoids cultured under AO and AI conditions were compared. In AO organoids, CFTR localized to the outer epithelial layer, consistent with apical expression at the external surface. In contrast, organoids cultured under AI conditions exhibited strong CFTR fluorescence along the inner epithelial layer, corresponding to an apical membrane orientation toward the lumen. Figure 5 shows representative AI (P2 and P7) and AO (P1 and P3) organoids, demonstrating no observable differences in CFTR staining pattern across passages.

VIP Receptors in the LG

The LG is under predominant parasympathetic control, which is associated with abundant expression of VIP receptors. Our previous studies demonstrated the presence of both VPAC1 and VPAC2 receptors in the LG tissue.9

VPAC1 Expression and Localization

In our previous work, VPAC1 receptors showed predominant expression in ductal epithelial cells of the LG. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that receptor expression was largely confined to ductal structures, exhibiting a mosaic staining pattern.

A similar pattern was observed in our organoid model under both AI and AO conditions. Figure 6 shows representative VPAC1 immunostaining in AI organoids (P1 and P7) and AO organoids (P1 and P3). Staining patterns remained consistent across passages.

In our previous experiments, AQP4 immunoreactivity was predominantly localized to the basolateral membrane of the LG duct cells, with markedly reduced expression at the apical membrane.10 In our organoid cultures, AQP4 immunostaining mirrored the distribution found in LG ducts. Discussion

This study establishes a reproducible protocol for generating murine LG duct-derived organoids from isolated duct segments and demonstrates that epithelial polarity can be reliably modulated between AO and AI configurations through defined culture conditions. Across passages (P1–P7), organoids retained ductal identity, as evidenced by the expression and localization of CFTR, AQP4, VPAC1, VPAC2, and NKCC1, and exhibited stimulus-evoked responses consistent with preserved secretory function. Collectively, these findings support the utility of this system as a robust and versatile experimental model for investigating the physiology and pathophysiology of the LGD system and for the preclinical evaluation of duct-targeted therapeutic strategies.18,19

A key advance of this study is the directed control of epithelial polarity. Supplementation of the culture medium with R-spondin, Wnt-3a, noggin, and ROCK inhibition yielded AI organoids characterized by an inward-facing apical domain, whereas omission of these factors resulted in AO structures with apical membranes exposed to the culture medium. In our culturing method, both AI and AO organoids are embedded in Matrigel, indicating that the polarity switch is not driven by ECM removal but rather by growth factor-dependent modulation of epithelial signaling. These changes may govern cytoskeletal tension and cell junction development, leading to two distinct tissue organizations. It can be hypothesized that under conditions lacking Wnt-3a supplementation, epithelial cells adopt a more plastic cellular architecture characterized by asymmetries in cytoskeletal tension and membrane trafficking. This remodeling permits apical membrane proteins to localize to the external surface of the organoid despite continuous contact with the ECM. Activation of the canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway via supplementation with Wnt-3a, R-spondin, and the inhibition of bone morphogenetic protein-mediated differentiation by noggin promotes coordinated lumenogenesis and establishment of an inwardly oriented apical surface.13–15 Concurrent pharmacological inhibition of Rho-kinase using Y-27632 attenuates RhoA-dependent actomyosin cytoskeletal remodeling, thereby facilitating the assembly of continuous circumferential tight junction networks and effective luminal sealing. Collectively, these processes drive the emergence of an AI epithelial architecture in multiple intestinal and glandular organoid systems.16,22–26

In our experiments, we found that epithelial polarity (AI versus AO) can be modulated by the culture conditions; however, a reliable statistical analysis of the efficiency of polarity induction (i.e. the proportion of AI and AO organoids formed under specific culture conditions) cannot be performed. In our experimental system, the most reliable method for determining polarity is the detection of specific transporter channels by immunostaining. The localization of organoids within Matrigel (bright-field imaging) differs substantially from their localization within the cryomatrix (immunofluorescent staining). Due to the 3D spatial arrangement in cryomatrix, a given section typically contains no more than 1 to 4 organoid cultures, therefore only a subset of organoids can be evaluated in a given section. Consequently, it is not feasible to assess the polarity of all organoids within each culture condition in a comprehensive and unbiased manner. Despite these limitations, we observed that the polarity of the organoid cultures was consistent with the respective culture conditions.

Functionally, AO and AI configurations offer complementary experimental advantages: AO organoids enable direct access to the apical membrane for pharmacological manipulation and transport assays, whereas AI organoids closely recapitulate the in vivo ductal organization, thereby rendering them well-suited for studies of lumen formation and epithelial barrier organization.

Molecular profiling by immunofluorescence confirmed ductal lineage identity and polarized membrane organization. CFTR localized to the apical membrane (outer in AO and luminal in AI), consistent with its role in ductal chloride secretion as observed in isolated LG ducts.8 Robust apical CFTR expression was maintained under both polarity conditions, supporting a conserved function in chloride transport and fluid regulation in LG ducts. Importantly, no significant changes in CFTR distribution were observed across passages, indicating stable maintenance of epithelial identity and polarity over time.

Our previous studies demonstrated NKCC1 expression and functional activity in rabbit lacrimal ductal cells, supporting the hypothesis that this transporter represents the primary mechanism for basolateral chloride uptake.7 Consistent with these findings, we detected robust NKCC1 expression at the basolateral membrane of duct-derived organoid cells.27

The AQP4 water channel, which is predominantly expressed on the basolateral membrane of ductal epithelial cells, also maintained its characteristic localization in organoid cultures. This distribution mirrored that observed in native LG ducts, supporting its role in transcellular water movement coupled to ion transport.10,28 In AI organoids, AQP4 staining was confined to the basolateral membrane, whereas in AO organoids, AQP4 localized to the corresponding inner surface, consistent with their respective polarity orientations. These findings further reinforce the structural and functional fidelity of the in vitro organoid models.

The expression patterns of VIP receptors provide additional validation of the ductal phenotype of these organoids. Both VPAC1 and VPAC2 exhibited a characteristic mosaic expression pattern, consistent with our previous observations in native LG tissue.9 These receptor expression profiles were maintained in both AI and AO organoid cultures and across successive passages, indicating stable preservation of parasympathetic signaling components that are essential for LG function. The persistence of these expression patterns from passage 1 through passage 7 further supports the preservation of ductal epithelial identity during long-term expansion.

Functional assays using video-microscopy provided further validation of the experimental platform. AO organoids exhibited significant secretagogue-evoked shrinkage in response to phenylephrine, carbachol, and 8-bromo-cAMP (P < 0.05 for each), consistent with net transepithelial fluid efflux across the apical membrane.

In contrast, AI organoids exhibited pronounced changes in luminal density and intracellular dynamics without a significant alteration in projected area during the stimulation period. This finding is likely attributable to polarity-dependent geometric constraints rather than impaired secretory function. Several non-mutually exclusive mechanisms may account for this observation: (i) AI epithelia may exhibit increased mechanical stiffness and/or tighter intercellular junctions, thereby limiting the efflux of secreted fluid into the surrounding medium and reducing gross organoid deformation despite active transport; (ii) fluid movement may primarily result in expansion or contraction of the enclosed lumen without appreciably affecting the external contour measured by areal analysis; and (iii) the imaging metric employed (2D projected area in pixels) may underestimate subtle 3D volume changes. Taken together, in AI organoids, the apical membrane faces the enclosed lumen, and the epithelium likely forms a tighter, mechanically more stable structure. Under these conditions, secretagogue-evoked fluid movement may occur mainly within the sealed luminal compartment, producing changes in luminal content or subtle 3D deformation without a detectable change in the 2D cross-sectional area used for quantification. This interpretation is supported by the hypotonic challenge, which induced swelling in AI organoids and thus confirmed epithelial integrity and water permeability. Therefore, unlike AO organoids, for which changes in the projected area serve as a useful readout of net transepithelial fluid movement, AI organoids may require alternative functional readouts, such as 3D live imaging, to more sensitively detect secretory responses.

These polarity-defined organoid models enable targeted research. AO organoids are particularly well-suited for direct assessment of apical ion channels and transporters (e.g. CFTR, ENaC, and Ca²⁺-activated Cl⁻ channels), for pharmacological testing of secretagogues and CFTR modulators, and for examining interactions between apical membrane proteins and candidate therapeutic compounds. In contrast, AI organoids preserve a sealed luminal compartment, thereby facilitating studies of luminal composition, mucus and protein trafficking, and epithelial barrier function under conditions that more closely approximate in vivo ductal topology. Collectively, these complementary configurations constitute a “bidirectional” experimental toolkit for dissecting vectorial ion and water transport and its regulation by cholinergic, VIP-ergic, and adrenergic signaling pathways relevant to LG physiology and dry eye disease pathophysiology.29–31

This work further demonstrates the feasibility of maintaining duct-derived organoids across multiple passages without loss of morphological integrity or functional capacity. In doing so, it overcomes key limitations associated with isolated ex vivo duct segments – namely, limited yield per gland and short experimental lifespan – thereby enabling adequately powered and reproducible studies. Moreover, because these cultures are derived exclusively from ductal tissue, they avoid confounding contributions from acinar cells present in whole-gland organoid preparations and permit precise, duct-specific hypothesis testing.32

Several limitations of the present study warrant consideration. First, all experiments were conducted using murine cells; therefore, species-specific differences in receptor expression, transporter repertoire, and signaling pathways may limit direct extrapolation to human lacrimal duct physiology. Second, variability among Matrigel batches together with the presence of undefined ECM components, may influence epithelial polarity and differentiation outcomes. Systematic evaluation of matrix composition and mechanical properties, together with controlled modulation of Wnt and ROCK signaling pathways, will be essential for elucidating the mechanistic determinants of polarity switching.33,34 Finally, although immunofluorescence staining confirmed the expression and localization of key proteins, complementary approaches – such as single-cell transcriptomics and functional electrophysiological measurements – would provide higher-resolution insights into cellular heterogeneity and transporter activity.35,36

Conclusions

These findings demonstrate that LG duct-derived organoids recapitulate key structural, molecular, and functional features of native LG ductal epithelium. The polarity-dependent distribution of ion and water channels, stable expression of neurotransmitter receptors, and capacity for stimulus-evoked fluid secretion underscore the potential of this system as a physiologically relevant model for studying LG duct function and disease. The ability to generate both AI and AO LG duct organoids represents a significant methodological advance in LG research. These complementary configurations enable comprehensive investigation of ductal physiology, pathophysiological mechanisms, and therapeutic interventions under controlled and reproducible conditions. Continued optimization of culture conditions – including ECM composition and growth factor supplementation – will be essential to further refine differentiation protocols and ensure faithful maintenance of ductal identity. Moreover, integration of this organoid platform with emerging technologies, such as single-cell transcriptomics, CRISPR-based genome editing, and microfluidic organ-on-chip systems holds considerable promise for deepening our understanding of LG biology and accelerating translational research in ophthalmology.37–39
