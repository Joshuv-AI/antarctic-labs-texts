Abstract
. Given a projective variety X X over an algebraically closed field k k , M. V. Nori introduced in [ No76 ] a group scheme π ⁡ ( X ) \pi(X) which accounts for principal bundles P → X P\to X with finite structure, obtaining in this way an amplification the etale fundamental group. One drawback of this theory is that it is quite difficult to arrive at an explicit description of π ⁡ ( X ) \pi(X) , whenever it does not vanish altogether. To wit, there are no known non-trivial examples in the literature where π ⁡ ( X ) \pi(X) is local, or local of some given height, etc. In this paper we obtain a description of π ⁡ ( X ) \pi(X) through amalgamated products of certain non-commutative local group schemes — we called them infinitesimal non-commutative Witt group schemes — in the case where X X is a non-normal variety obtained by pinching a simply connected one.



1.
Introduction

In this work we are concerned with finite group schemes which appear as structure groups of principal bundles over projective varieties in positive characteristic, that is to say, with the theory of the fundamental group scheme initiated by M. V. Nori
[
No82
,
No76
]
. To begin our discussion, given a projective variety
X
over an algebraically closed field
k
of characteristic
p>0
, let us write
\pi(X,x_{0})
for the essentially finite fundamental group scheme of
X
at
x_{0}\in X(k)

[
No76
, Section 3]
.
One of the drawbacks of this object
— and for that matter also of its predecessor, the etale fundamental group — is that a
precise
calculation turns out to be quite difficult.
Indeed, although there are many instances where one can assure that
\pi(X,x_{0})
is
trivial

[
Bi09
,
BH07
,
Me11
,
BKP24
]
, or at least
small

[
AB16
,
BHdS21
]
, it is far from being an easy task to find actual descriptions outside of these cases. For example, the present work shall give the
first
known example for which
\pi(X,x_{0})
is
certainly
local and non-trivial, for which
\pi(X,x_{0})
is of height precisely
1
, etc. (If we abandon the requirement of projectivity imposed on
X
, it is worth noticing that describing
\pi(X,x_{0})
precisely seems somewhat pointless, and putting the question as an existence theorem “à la Abhyankar” is more reasonable
[
Ot18
,
OTZ22
]
.)

One of the difficulties behind the determination of
\pi(X,x_{0})
is: what we ultimately want is to identify when
\pi(X,x_{0})

already appears somewhere else
. Now, prime examples of pro-finite group schemes are (those associated to) pro-finite groups and Frobenius kernels of linear algebraic groups. Many among these examples are applications of the following construction principle, which we now explain. We start with a finite abstract group or a restricted Lie algebra, we construct the group ring, respectively the universal enveloping algebra, note that these are in fact cocommutative Hopf algebras
(see
[
A80
, Example 2.7, p.62]
and

[
MM65
, §6]
), and apply the
Hopf dual
functor
(-)^{\circ}

[
A80
,
Sw69
]
to arrive at the ring of functions of an group scheme.
Thus, we may enquire when
\mathcal{O}(\pi(X,x_{0}))\simeq H^{\circ}
, where
H
is a “known” cocommutative Hopf algebra.
This point of view has the advantage of being more economic and simpler to apply to Tannakian categories; it is easier to deal with modules over an algebra (viz.
H
) than with comodules over a coalgebra (viz.
\mathcal{O}(\pi(X,x_{0}))
). It also has the advantage of opening a door to Dieudonné’s theory of formal groups
[
Di73
]
.

As one learns from
[
Se88
]
,
[
Bri15
]
and
[
SGA1
, Exposé IX, §5]
,
introducing non-normal points on a regular variety has the effect of producing certain
accessory groups
on the starting one. More precisely, we assume the existence of a smooth variety
Y
and a finite morphism

\nu:Y\longrightarrow X

which allow us to express sheaves on
X
in terms of sheaves on
Y
plus some extra data; here we have in mind the process of pinching
[
Se88
,
Fe02
]
. Then,
given a certain kind of group associated to a geometric object (see ahead),
it is possible to describe the groups associated to
X
in terms of those of
Y

plus
certain
accessory groups
. In the context of Jacobian varieties, the accessory groups are either powers of
\mathbf{G}_{a}
or products of Witt groups
[
Se88
, V.13–16]
. In the theory of Picard schemes
[
Bri15
]
, the accessory groups are Weil restrictions, and in
[
SGA1
, IX.5]
we encounter pro-finite amalgamated products
\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}*\cdots*\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}
. It then becomes clear that for the case of the Nori fundamental group scheme an
obviously challenging part is to find analogues of these accessory groups
. Indeed, in our setting, well-known results allow us to rapidly transpose the geometry to a purely algebraic problem controlled by certain associative and cocommutative Hopf algebras
\boldsymbol{H}_{D}
(see Section
8
). These Hopf algebras (or their Hopf dual) play the role of the accessory groups, and the task is then to see if these objects already have a meaning somewhere else.

This is one point which the present work addresses by bringing to light the “non-commutative infinitesimal Witt group schemes” discovered independently by E. J. Ditters and K. Newman more than 50 years ago
[
Dit69
,
Ne74
]
. (Ditters draws on the outstanding series of papers Dieudonné produced on formal groups in the 1950’s and 60’s, while Newman is influenced by Sweedler.) Unfortunately, although very interesting, these group schemes have received little attention in overview works. The cocommutative Hopf algebras discovered by these two mathematicians —
non-commutative exponential group-coalgebras
in
[
Dit69
]
,
\mathfrak{P}_{n}
in
[
Ne74
]
, but
\mathcal{NW}_{n-1}
in Definition
7.6
— are not found in the textbook presentations of Hopf algebras at our disposal and are only tangentially mentioned, in the context of formal groups, at the end of
[
Haz78
, VI.38]
.

Once these “non-commutative Witt group schemes” are under the light, we see that
they have a role similar to the one played by Witt groups in the theory of the Jacobian
[
Se88
, V.16]
and are the “accessory groups” we were looking for. Indeed, this is argued, in a different setting, by Ditters and Newman
[
Dit69
,
Ne74
]
. In a nutshell, the “excess” appearing in the Jacobian mentioned by Serre (cf. V.13 and Proposition 9 of V.16 in
[
Se88
]
) has a manifestation in the theory of the fundamental group scheme.

Since our objective is to describe as precisely as possible the group scheme
\pi(X,x_{0})
, we shall restrict attention to the case where
essentially finite vector bundles on
Y
are rather simple, i.e. they are always
trivial
. By doing so, the bulk of the work consists in understanding the accessory group schemes mentioned above. It is not excluded that a more encompassing picture can be reached by allowing a more general
Y
, but this seems more delicate (see e.g. Example
6.9
) and is to be dealt with somewhere else.

In Section

The reader will notice that the text contains many reviews and preliminary material. This is mainly due to the varied nature of the techniques — tensor categories, pinching of schemes, vector bundles and Hopf algebras — employed to obtain a definite result. Requiring the reader to feel
confortable with all these branches in order to understand the calculation of a fundamental group scheme seemed a poor decision. Hopefully this shall prove useful in appreciating the points of view of other mathematicians.

2.1.
Fibre products and amalgamated products

Many of the arguments in this work rely on the notion of fibre product of categories, which we recall briefly, for the convenience of the reader, and to establish notations.

Let
F:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{S}
,
G:\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{S}
be functors between categories and let

\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{A}\times_{\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{B}

be the category whose objects are triples

(a,b;\gamma)\quad\text{ with $a\in\mathrm{Obj}\,\mathcal{A}$, $b\in\mathrm{Obj}\,\mathcal{B}$ and $\gamma:Fa\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}Gb$,}

and whose arrows are defined in the obvious manner, cf.
[
Fe02
, 1.6]
or
[
DW23
, 1.1]
. Since the case where
\mathcal{S}
is the category
k\text{-}\mathbf{vect}
will be mostly used, we will adopt the abbreviation

\boxed{\mathcal{A}\times_{k}\mathcal{B}:=\mathcal{A}\times_{k\text{-}\mathbf{vect}}\mathcal{B}.}

When dealing with tensor categories
[
DM82
, §1]

\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{S}
,
and
tensor functors
F
and
G
, the category
\mathcal{P}
can be endowed with a functor
\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{P}
,

(A,B;c)\otimes(A^{\prime},B^{\prime};c^{\prime})=(A\otimes A^{\prime},B\otimes B^{\prime};c\otimes c^{\prime}),

which then turns
\mathcal{P}
into a tensor category.
See
[
DW23
, Lemma 1.5]
. In addition, rigidity is preserved in this construction, as is “abelianess”
[
DW23
, Lemma 1.4]
, provided that
F
and
G
are exact.

Taking one step further, let us suppose that
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{S}
are
neutralised Tannakian categories
. This means that each one of them is an abelian,
k
-linear tensor category
[
DM82
, 1.15]
, which is rigid
[
DM82
, 1.7]
, and that there exist tensor functors
\xi:\mathcal{A}\to k\text{-}\mathbf{vect}
,
\zeta:\mathcal{B}\to k\text{-}\mathbf{vect}
and
\eta:\mathcal{S}\to k\text{-}\mathbf{vect}
which are faithful,
k
-linear and exact. Suppose now that
the functors
F
and
G
preserve these structures: there exist isomorphisms of tensor functors
\xi\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\Rightarrow}}\eta F
and
\zeta\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\Rightarrow}}\eta G
. Then
\mathcal{P}
is a
k
-linear abelian tensor category and comes with an exact and faithful tensor functor
\xi\times\zeta:\mathcal{P}\to k\text{-}\mathbf{vect}

[
DW23
, Lemma 1.8]
. All pieces are in place in order to apply the main existence theorem
[
DM82
, Theorem 2.11]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
G
and
H
be group schemes and endow
\mathbf{Rep}_{k}(G)
and
\mathbf{Rep}_{k}(H)
with the forgetful functors
\omega_{G}:\mathbf{Rep}_{k}(G)\to k\text{-}\mathbf{vect}
and
\omega_{H}:\mathbf{Rep}_{k}(H)\to k\text{-}\mathbf{vect}
.
We define

G\star H

as the group scheme, associated via
[
DM82
, Theorem 2.11]
, to the couple consisting of the rigid abelian tensor category

\mathbf{Rep}_{k}(G)\times_{k}\mathbf{Rep}_{k}(H)

and the functor

\omega_{G}\times\omega_{H}:\mathbf{Rep}_{k}(G)\times_{k}\mathbf{Rep}_{k}(H)\longrightarrow k\text{-}\mathbf{vect}.

From
[
DW23
, Corollary 1.12]
, it follows that
G\star H
is the
co-product, in the category of group schemes
\mathbf{GS}
, of
G
and
H
. (The co-product will also be called the amalgamated product.)

2.2.
On the Hopf dual

Let
R\in\mathbf{Hpf}^{\mathrm{coc}}
; the Hopf algebra
R^{\circ}

[
A80
,
Sw69
]
is commutative and
G:=\mathrm{Spec}\,R^{\circ}
then comes with the structure of a group scheme. Let now
\mathbf{A}
be a full subcategory of
\mathbf{GS}
for which it is possible to construct the
largest quotient in
\mathbf{A}

[
HdS18
, Section 2.2]
; let us agree to denote this quotient by
G\to G^{\mathbf{A}}
.
We may then enquire if
G^{\mathbf{A}}

can be described in terms of
R
exclusively
. This
is an useful entreprise for the present work and, in addition, has a certain public utility, so that we have enough reason to include the following lines dealing with the case where
\mathbf{A}
is either the category of
abelian
or
unipotent
group schemes. It should be noticed that the specific case of group algebras

, where

is an abstract group and

, is much used in Quillen’s theory of the Malcev completion, cf.
[
Ha93
, Section 3]
.

Let
\mathbf{Ab}
, respectively
\mathbf{Un}
, be the categories of
abelian
, respectively

[
Wa79
, Chapter 8]
, group schemes. According to the general theories of group-schemes and categories (see
[
Wa79
, Chapter 8]
and
[
HdS18
, Section 2.2]
), the construction of the largest quotient is possible.

Let
\varepsilon:R\to k
be the counit of
R
and
\mathfrak{a}
its kernel, the augmentation ideal. Let
R\text{-}\mathbf{mod}
stand for the category of all left
R
-modules whose dimension as a
k
-space is finite; in
R\text{-}\mathbf{mod}
we have one preferred object,
\mathbf{I}:=R/\mathfrak{a}
, called the unit object. This terminology is coherent because the fact that

is a cocommutative Hopf algebra allows us to endow

with a tensor structure and, in this case,
\mathbf{I}
is a unit object. This is well explained in
[

, Proposition 1.1]
.
Similarly, let
\mathbf{comod}\text{-}R^{\circ}
stand for the category of right

-comodules having finite dimension over

. Using
[
Mo93
, 2.1.3]
and
[
Sw69
, 1.6.4]
, the identity functor on

gives rise to an isomorphism of

\Theta:R\text{-}\mathbf{mod}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbf{comod}\text{-}R^{\circ}=\mathbf{Rep}_{k}\,G.

In particular
\mathbf{I}
corresponds to the trivial representation of

.

Let

R^{\dagger}=\{f\in R^{\circ}\,:\,\text{$f(\mathfrak{a}^{n})=0$ for some $n\geq 1$}\}

and

, where
\mathfrak{C}
is the two-sided ideal generated by the commutators in

.

Lemma 2.2
.

(1)

The subspace
R^{\dagger}\subset R^{\circ}
is a Hopf subalgebra.

(2)

The
k
-algebra
R^{\mathrm{ab}}
has a unique structure of Hopf algebra such that
R\to R^{\mathrm{ab}}
is a morphism of Hopf algebras.

(3)

The natural morphism
G=\mathrm{Spec}\,R^{\circ}\to\mathrm{Spec}\,R^{\mathrm{ab},\circ}
is the largest abelian quotient of
G
.

(4)

The natural morphism
G=\mathrm{Spec}\,R^{\circ}\to\mathrm{Spec}\,R^{\prime}
is the largest unipotent quotient of
G
.

Proof.

(1) This is
[
Mo93
, Lemma 9.2.1]
.

(2) We only need to observe that
\mathfrak{C}
is a Hopf ideal and apply standard theory
[
A80
, 4.2.1]
.

(4)
Let
R\text{-}\mathbf{modu}
be the full subcategory of
R\text{-}\mathbf{mod}
consisting of those modules for which Jordan-Hölder factors are all isomorphic to
\mathbf{I}
. It is not difficult, using simple linear algebra, to see that under the isomorphism
\Theta
mentioned above, the category
R\text{-}\mathbf{modu}
corresponds to the category of
unipotent
comodules
\mathbf{ucomod}\text{-}R^{\circ}
, that is, those
R^{\circ}
-comodules whose Jordan-Hölder factors are all isomorphic to
\mathds{1}
. We contend that

if and only if some power of
\mathfrak{a}
annihilates

.
Suppose then that
M\in R\text{-}\mathbf{modu}
and let

be a Jordan-Hölder filtration where
M_{i}/M_{i-1}\simeq\mathbf{I}
. Then, for each
a\in\mathfrak{a}
, it follows that
a\cdot M_{i}\subset M_{i-1}
, and hence
a_{1}\cdots a_{n}M=0
whenever

, i.e.
\mathfrak{a}^{n}M=0
.
Conversely, let

be annihilated by

. Then
\mathfrak{a}^{n}
annihilates any simple module

appearing in a Jordan-Hölder filtration of

. Now,

is a two-sided

[
GW04
, 3.12, p.56]
containing

and hence

. This implies that

and

.

In conclusion, only the unit representation of
G^{\dagger}:=\mathrm{Spec}\,R^{\dagger}
is simple and is hence
G^{\dagger}
is unipotent. In addition, if

is a morphism to an unipotent group scheme

, then
\mathbf{Rep}_{k}\,U\to\mathbf{Rep}_{k}\,G\simeq R\text{-}\mathbf{mod}
must have its image in
R\text{-}\mathbf{modu}
and therefore,
Tannakian duality leads to a factorization of

into
G\to G^{\dagger}\to U
.

(3) The proof is simple and omitted.

2.3.
Coproduct of Hopf algebras

\boldsymbol{V}:=[(V,\beta),(W,g);\varphi]\in\mathcal{S}_{D}\times_{k}\mathcal{I}
, we let

G\boldsymbol{V}=(V,\beta(\varphi^{-1}g\varphi)_{D})\in\mathrm{Obj}\,\mathcal{S}_{D}^{+};

here and below, we abbreviate

to

.
Also, define for each arrow

in
\mathcal{S}_{D}\times_{k}\mathcal{I}

. This gives rise to a functor
G:\mathcal{S}_{D}\times_{k}\mathcal{I}\to\mathcal{S}_{D}^{+}

Proposition 5.3
.

The above defined functor
G:\mathcal{S}_{D}\times_{k}\mathcal{I}\to\mathcal{S}_{D}^{+}
is an equivalence of tensor categories.

Proof.

This is a sequence of simple verifications.
Let
(V,\gamma)\in\mathrm{Obj}\,\mathcal{S}_{D}^{+}
. Then,
(V,\gamma\circ(s^{*}(\gamma)^{-1})_{D})
is an object of
\mathcal{S}_{D}
and
(V,s^{*}\gamma)
is an object of
\mathcal{I}
. If we put

\boldsymbol{V}:=(V,\gamma(s^{*}(\gamma)^{-1})_{D}\,,\,(V,s^{*}\gamma)\,;\,\mathrm{id}_{V}]\in\mathrm{Obj}\,\mathcal{S}_{D}\times_{k}\mathcal{I},

then
G\boldsymbol{V}=(V,\gamma)
, and
G
is essentially surjective.

We now check that
G
is fully faithful. Each
[(V,\beta),(W,g);\varphi]
is isomorphic, via
(\mathrm{id},\varphi^{-1})
, to
[(V,\beta),(V,\varphi^{-1}g\varphi);\mathrm{id}_{V}]
and we may then work solely with objects of this simplified kind.
Let
\boldsymbol{V}=[(V,\beta),(V,g);\mathrm{id}]
and
\boldsymbol{V}^{\prime}=[(V^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}),(V^{\prime},g^{\prime});\mathrm{id}]
be given in
\mathcal{S}_{D}\times_{k}\mathcal{I}
, and let

\varphi:V\to V^{\prime}
satisfy

(8)

\varphi_{D}\beta g_{D}=\beta^{\prime}g_{D}^{\prime}\varphi_{D};

i.e.
\varphi\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{S}_{D}^{+}}(G\boldsymbol{V},G\boldsymbol{V}^{\prime})
. Applying
s^{*}
to eq. (
8
), we have

(9)

\varphi g=g^{\prime}\varphi,

i.e.
\varphi\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{I}}((V,g),(V^{\prime},g^{\prime}))
. From eqs. (
8
) and (
9
), we conclude
\varphi_{D}\beta g_{D}=\beta^{\prime}\varphi_{D}g_{D}
and thus
\beta^{\prime}\varphi_{D}=\varphi_{D}\beta
. This says that
\varphi\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{S}_{D}}((V,\beta),(V^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}))
and
(\varphi,\varphi)
is an arrow
\boldsymbol{V}\to\boldsymbol{V}^{\prime}
inducing
\varphi\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{S}_{D}^{+}}(G\boldsymbol{V},G\boldsymbol{V}^{\prime})
.
Consequently,
G_{\boldsymbol{V},\boldsymbol{V}^{\prime}}:\mathrm{Hom}(\boldsymbol{V},\boldsymbol{V}^{\prime})\to\mathrm{Hom}(G\boldsymbol{V},G\boldsymbol{V}^{\prime})
is surjective. Injectivity is even simpler and we do not write it down. Nor shall we write down the verifications concerning the final statement, and we conclude here the proof.
∎

Proof.

This is again a series of straightforward long verifications.
Essential surjectivity.

(10)

∎

6.3.
A cautionary example

It is natural to inquire if

can be described as efficiently as it can in the etale case even by

made in Corollary

. More precisely, we have in mind the analogous situation of Theorem 4.12 and Corollary 5.4 in

which we briefly recall. To simplify, let us suppose that

is either

or

. Then, according to
[

, we have an isomorphism

in the first case and

(Here, the amalgamated product is taken in the category of pro-finite groups.) The following example gives some limits to these speculations.

Example 6.9
.

Let us suppose that
C=\mathbf{pt}
and that

is a tangent vector at a point

, i.e.
D=\mathrm{Spec}\,k[t]/(t^{2})
.
(Since we assume that

is a closed subscheme, this tangent vector cannot vanish.)
In addition, let us assume the existence of a finite group scheme

and a principal
G
-bundle

such that, for any

of image

, the derivative
T_{z}Z\to T_{y}Y
vanishes. This is the case, for example, when

is an abelian variety,
G
is the kernel of multiplication by
p
, and

is multiplication by
p
.

We contend that in this situation
it is not
possible to obtain an isomorphism

\pi(Y)\star S\simeq\pi(X)

where
S
is some pro-fintie group scheme. More precisely, let

be the canonical morphism and suppose that there exists a pro-finite group scheme
S
and an isomorphism
\varphi:\pi(Y)\star S\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}\pi(X)
such that
\nu_{\#}=\varphi\circ i
. Let now
\psi:\pi(X)\to\pi(Y)
be the morphism defined by means of the universal property of
\pi(Y)\star S
applied to
\mathrm{id}:\pi(Y)\to\pi(Y)
and the trivial morphism

. Then,

and we arrive at a functor

such that

is naturally isomorphic, as a tensor functor, to

.
Hence, the tensor functors

and

G:\mathbf{EF}(Y)\longrightarrow\mathcal{O}(D)\text{-}\mathbf{mod},\quad\mathcal{E}\longmapsto\mathcal{E}|_{y}\otimes\mathcal{O}(D)

are isomorphic because

and

are likewise.

Then

is isomorphic to the principal

lifts to

7.
Non-commutative Witt groups: The works of Ditters and Newman

As we mentioned in the Introduction, one of the difficulties behind the effective calculation of fundamental group schemes lies in the fact that we lack enough identifiable local group schemes. In this section, we make a brief presentations on a theory which allows us to obtain very relevant local group schemes and which seems to have drawn, unfortunately, little attention: this is the work of Ditters and Newmann
[
Dit69
,
Ne74
]
. This section makes no use of constructions and notations of Sections
3
to
5
and the geometry of pinched schemes.

7.1.
The algebra
\mathcal{Z}
of Ditters

We fix
m
a positive integer.
Let
\mathcal{Z}
, respectively
\mathcal{Z}(m)
, be the associative algebra (over
k
) on the variables
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
, respectively
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
. Clearly, we regard
\mathcal{Z}(m)
as a subalgebra of
\mathcal{Z}
.
By convenience let us write
Z_{0}=1
. On
\mathcal{Z}
, we then introduce the structure of bialgebra by decreeing that comultiplication
\Delta
satisfies

\Delta Z_{h}=\sum_{i+j=h}Z_{i}\otimes Z_{j},

and that the co-unit maps
Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots
to
0
.
The bialgebra
\mathcal{Z}
is sometimes referred to as the
Leibniz algebra
or the
Hopf algebra of non-commutative symmetric functions
, or the
universal non-commutative group-coalgebra (UNG)
.

In
[
Dit69
, 1.2.7]
, it is proved that
once we define elements
\{S_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
by the equations
S_{0}=1
and
\sum_{i=0}^{n}S_{i}Z_{n-i}=0
for
n\geq 1
,
then
the morphism of algebras
S:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Z}
sending
Z_{i}
to
S_{i}
endows
\mathcal{Z}
with the structure of a
cocommutative Hopf-algebra
. In addition,
\mathcal{Z}(m)
is also a sub-Hopf-algebra.

A final piece of structure on
\mathcal{Z}
is its grading, which is obtained by decreeing each
Z_{i}
to be homogeneous of degree
i
.

Notation 7.1
.

For each integer
n>0
, let
\|n\|
stand for
[\log_{p}n]
, that is,
p^{\|n\|}\leq n<p^{\|n\|+1}
.

Definition 7.2

(
[
Dit69
, 1.4.8]
)
.

Let
H\in\mathbf{Hpf}^{\mathrm{coc}}
. Given
\ell\in\mathbf{N}^{*}\cup\{\infty\}
, a curve of length
\ell
on
H
is a sequence of elements
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{\ell})\in H^{\times\ell}
such that, after fixing
c_{0}=1
, we have
\Delta c_{j}=\sum_{i=0}^{j}c_{i}\otimes c_{j-i}
, for every
1\leq j\leq\ell
. A curve of length
\ell
is also called a divided power sequence of length
\ell
, cf.
[
Ne74
, 1.23]
. The set of curves of length
\ell
on
H
shall be denoted by
\mathrm{Curv}_{\ell}(H)
.

Definition 7.3
.

A curve
(c_{i})_{i=1}^{\ell}
is said to be minimal if
k\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{i}\}=k\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{p^{\|i\|}}\}

for
i=1,\ldots,\ell
.

Note that, if
(c_{i})_{i=1}^{\ell}
is a minimal curve, then in fact
k\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{i}\}
can be generated simply by the elements indexed by power of
p
, i.e.
k\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{i}\}=k\{c_{1},c_{p},\ldots,c_{p^{\|i\|}}\}
.

Remark 7.4
.

In
[
Dit75
, Definition 6.1]
, Ditters puts forward the notion of a
pure
curve in
\mathcal{Z}
. Our definition of “minimal” seeks to imitate this in a different setting, viz. for general Hopf algebras and without mention of weights.

To every arrow
\varphi:H\to H^{\prime}
in
\mathbf{Hpf}^{\mathrm{coc}}
we have an associated map
\mathrm{Curv}_{\ell}(H)\to\mathrm{Curv}_{\ell}(H^{\prime})
and in this way
H\mapsto\mathrm{Curv}_{\ell}(H)
becomes a functor
\mathbf{Hpf}^{\mathrm{coc}}\to\mathbf{Set}
. It is clear that
\mathrm{Curv}_{\ell}
is then represented by
\mathcal{Z}(\ell)
.

Theorem 7.5

(Minimal curves
[
Dit69
, Theorem 2.1.4]
)
.

There exists a minimal curve
(E_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
in
\mathcal{Z}
with the additional following properties.

(a)

Each

E_{i}
is homogeneous of degree
i
.

(b)

For each
i\geq 0
, we have
\deg(E_{p^{i}}-Z_{p^{i}})<p^{i}
.

Let us now fix a curve
(E_{i})_{i\geq 1}
as in Theorem
7.5
. It is clear that
k\{E_{1},\ldots,E_{p^{\ell}}\}\subset\mathcal{Z}(p^{\ell})
is a sub-Hopf-algebra of
\mathcal{Z}(p^{\ell})
.

Definition 7.6
.

For each
\ell\geq 0
, we define

\mathcal{NW}_{\ell+1}=k\{E_{p^{0}},\ldots,E_{p^{\ell}}\}=k\{E_{p^{0}},E_{p},\ldots,E_{p^{\ell}}\}.

This shall be called the
non-commutative Witt Hopf-algebra
of length
\ell+1
. (See Section
7.2
for an explanation concerning the name.) The associated group scheme
\mathrm{Spec}\,\mathcal{N}\mathcal{W}_{\ell+1}^{\circ}
shall be denoted by
\boldsymbol{N\!W}_{\ell+1}
, and will be called the
non-commutative infinitesimal Witt group scheme
.

Note that it is a priori not clear that
\mathcal{NW}_{\ell+1}
does not depend on the minimal curve chosen. This shall be cleared below.

An obvious property of
\boldsymbol{N\!W}_{\ell+1}
is the following. (For the concept of height, see
[
DG70
, II.7.1.4–6]
.)

Lemma 7.7
.

The group scheme
\boldsymbol{N\!W}_{\ell+1}
is local and of height
\leq\ell+1
.

Proof.

Let
\mathrm{Ver}
stand for the Verschiebung morphism of the cocommutative coalgebra
\mathcal{N}\mathcal{W}_{\ell+1}

[
A80
, Ch. 2, Section 5.3, 112ff]
. Since
(E_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
is a curve in
\mathcal{Z}
, then

\mathrm{Ver}^{i+1}(E_{p^{i}})=0,

see
[
A80
, Lemma 2.5.8]
.
Hence,
\mathrm{Ver}^{\ell+1}(\mathcal{N}\mathcal{W}_{\ell+1})=k
. From the canonical formula

f(\mathrm{Ver}(a))=f^{p}(a)^{1/p},\qquad\text{for each $f\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{W}_{\ell+1}^{*}$ and $a\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{W}_{\ell+1}$},

we conclude that the augmentation ideal
(k1)^{\perp}\subset\mathcal{N}\mathcal{W}_{\ell+1}^{\circ}
is annihilated by
\mathrm{Fr}^{\ell+1}
, which implies that
\mathcal{N}\mathcal{W}^{\circ}_{\ell+1}
is local of height at most
\ell+1
.
∎

The curve
(E_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
is not the unique one enjoying the properties of Theorem
7.5
; on the other hand, given any minimal curve
(E_{i}^{\prime})_{i=1}^{\infty}
satisfying (a) and (b) of Theorem
7.5
, the following can be said.
If

\varphi:\mathcal{Z}\longrightarrow k\{E_{i}^{\prime}\,:\,i\geq 1\}

is defined by
\varphi(Z_{i})=E_{i}^{\prime}
, then

k\{E_{i}\,:\,i\geq 1\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varphi}}{{\longrightarrow}}k\{E_{i}^{\prime}\,:\,i\geq 1\}

is an isomorphism. See
[
Dit69
, Lemma 3.1.2, p. 51]
.

Remark 7.8
.

In
[
Dit69
, 3.1.3, p.52]
, the Hopf algebra
k\{E_{i},i\geq 1\}
is called the “non-commutative exponential”. In
[
Ne74
]
, what we called
\mathcal{NW}_{\ell+1}
is called
\mathfrak{P}_{\ell}
. We have adopted the shifted index in order to accord with the notation for Witt vectors.

7.2.
Relation to the Witt group scheme

Let
\ell
be a positive integer.
In order to highlight the importance of the Hopf algebra
\mathcal{NW}_{\ell}
and the group scheme
\mathrm{Spec}\,\,\mathcal{NW}_{\ell}^{\circ}
, we relate it to the “usual Witt Hopf algebra.” This was already remarked by both Ditters and Newman in a different setting. In order to provide reliable arguments, we require a certain number of “well-known” results on the duality theory of Hopf algebras, which are explained in Section
2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{W}_{\ell}:=\mathcal{NW}^{\mathrm{ab}}_{\ell}
be the cocommutative and commutative Hopf algebra constructed in Section
2.2
. We shall explain its relation to the Hopf algebra of the Witt group scheme
\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell}

[
DG70
, V.1.6, p.543]
. More precisely, let
S_{j}\in\mathbf{F}_{p}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{j}\,;\,y_{0},\ldots,y_{j}]
stand for the additive Witt polynomials
[
DG70
, V.1.4, p.541-2]
and let
\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell}):=k[x_{0},\ldots,x_{\ell-1}]
be given the structure of Hopf algebra coming from the Witt group scheme
\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell}
of dimension
\ell
over
k
, i.e. comultiplication is

X_{j}\longmapsto S_{j}(x_{0}\otimes 1,\ldots,x_{j}\otimes 1\,;\,1\otimes x_{0},\ldots,1\otimes x_{j}).

Then, either from the work of Dieudonné
[
Dit69
, 3.1.5-8]
or from the appendix of
[
Ne74
]
, it follows that

\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell})\simeq\mathcal{W}_{\ell}.

(We note that in
[
Ne74
, Appendix]
, Newman does not follow the standard convention concerning Witt vectors.)
It is important to analyse not only
\mathcal{W}_{\ell}
but also the Hopf dual
\mathcal{W}_{\ell}^{\circ}
, since the pro-finite group scheme
\boldsymbol{N\!W}_{\ell}
will have
\mathrm{Spec}\,\mathcal{W}_{\ell}^{\circ}
as its largest
abelian
quotient (see Lemma
2.3
). The structure of the group scheme

\mathrm{Spec}\,\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell})^{\circ}
should be known, but we find it hard to give a clear reference.

Let us begin by employing

[
Wa79
, Theorem 9.5]
to obtain an isomorphism

\mathrm{Spec}\,\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell})^{\circ}\simeq\mathrm{Diag}(\Lambda)\times U,

where
\Lambda
is an abelian group and
U
is an unipotent group scheme. Clearly
\Lambda
is just the group of characters of
\mathrm{Spec}\,\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell})^{\circ}
, which is the group of group-like elements of
\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell})^{\circ}
. From
[
A80
, p. 129]
,
\Lambda
is isomorphic to the group of
k
-points of
\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell}
, i.e.
\Lambda\simeq\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell}(k)
.

Employing Lemma
2.3
, we conclude that
\mathcal{O}(U)
is isomorphic to the Hopf subalgebra
\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell})^{\dagger}
of
\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell})^{\circ}
.
Now, we make use of the fact that we are in positive characteristic; letting
\mathfrak{A}_{\ell}
stand for the augmentation ideal of
\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell})
, it follows that

\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell})^{\dagger}=\varinjlim_{h}\left(\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell})/\mathfrak{A}_{\ell}^{[p^{h}]}\right)^{*}.

The determination of Hopf algebra
\left(\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell})/\mathfrak{A}_{\ell}^{[p^{h}]}\right)^{*}
is well-known as it enters the context of Cartier duality of finite group schemes. Indeed, let

\boldsymbol{I}_{h}
stand for functor associating to a group scheme its
h
th Frobenius kernel; we have

\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{I}_{h}\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell})=\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell})/\mathfrak{A}_{\ell}^{[p^{h}]}
.
Then

[
De72
, Theorem, p.61]
says that there exists a canonical isomorphism

\boldsymbol{I}_{h}\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell}\simeq\boldsymbol{I}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{W}_{h}.

We then conclude that
U\simeq\varprojlim_{h}\boldsymbol{I}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{W}_{h}
, i.e.

U\simeq\boldsymbol{I}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{W}.

In conclusion:

Proposition 7.9
.

The abelian group scheme
\boldsymbol{N\!W}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{ab}}
is isomorphic to
\mathrm{Diag}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell}(k))\times\boldsymbol{I}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{W}
.∎

The group scheme
\Sigma_{D}
and the Hopf algebra
\boldsymbol{H}_{D}
when
D
is local

We now regain the setting and notations of Sections
3
to
5
, and in addition suppose that

A:=\mathcal{O}(D)

is a local ring with maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}\not=0
. We find convenient to write
\mathcal{S}_{A}
instead of
\mathcal{S}_{D}
in what follows. In order to gain knowledge about the group scheme
\Sigma_{D}
of Definition
6.1
, we shall find an equivalence

\mathcal{S}_{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\boldsymbol{H}_{A}\text{-}\mathbf{mod},

for a certain
associative algebra
\boldsymbol{H}_{A}
,
introduce on
\boldsymbol{H}_{A}
a bialgebra structure and then relate it to the
tensor product on
\mathcal{S}_{A}
. Moving on, we relate
\boldsymbol{H}_{A}

and the non-commutative Witt Hopf-algebras of Definition
7.6
by means of an encompassing structure result of
[
Ne74
]
. (Newman’s result is also in the direction of the “Campbell-Hausdorff-Dieudonné” Theorem in
[
Dit69
, Theorem 3.5.4]
.)

8.1.
The bialgebra
\boldsymbol{H}_{A}
and the category
\mathcal{S}_{A}

Using the decomposition
A=k1\oplus\mathfrak{m}
, we shall identify
\mathfrak{m}^{*}
with a subspace of
A^{*}
in what follows.

Using the obvious bijections

\begin{split}\mathrm{Hom}_{A}(V\otimes A,V\otimes A)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}\mathrm{Hom}_{k}(V,V\otimes A)\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}\mathrm{Hom}_{k}(A^{*},\mathrm{End}_{k}(V)),\end{split}

and the restriction map
\mathrm{Hom}_{k}(A^{*},\mathrm{End}_{k}(V))\to\mathrm{Hom}_{k}(\mathfrak{m}^{*},\mathrm{End}_{k}(V))
,
we can associate to each
(V,\varphi)\in\mathrm{Obj}\,\mathcal{S}_{A}
an element of
\mathrm{Hom}_{k}(\mathfrak{m}^{*},\mathrm{End}_{k}(V))=\mathrm{Hom}_{k\text{-}\mathbf{Alg}}(\boldsymbol{T}(\mathfrak{m}^{*}),\mathrm{End}_{k}(V))
, that is, a structure of
\boldsymbol{T}(\mathfrak{m}^{*})
-module on
V
.
Letting
\eta\in A^{*}
stand for the canonical map
A\to A/\mathfrak{m}=k
, we observe that
\varphi\in\mathrm{Hom}_{k}(A^{*},\mathrm{End}_{k}(V))
gives rise to an object of
\mathcal{S}_{A}
if and only if
\varphi(\eta)=\mathrm{id}_{V}
. From this, the identity functor of
k\text{-}\mathbf{vect}
gives rise to an equivalence of

k
-linear categories

(14)

\Omega:\mathcal{S}_{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\boldsymbol{T}(\mathfrak{m}^{*})\text{-}\mathbf{mod}.

In down-to-earth terms: Let
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a basis of
\mathfrak{m}
with dual basis
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Given
(V,\varphi)\in\mathrm{Obj}\,\mathcal{S}_{A}
, we write
\varphi\in\mathrm{Hom}_{A}(V\otimes A,V\otimes A)
as

\varphi(v\otimes 1)=v\otimes 1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{i}(v)\otimes a_{i}
. Then
\Omega(V,\varphi)
is the
\boldsymbol{T}(\mathfrak{m}^{*})
-module defined by the morphism of
k
-algebras

\boldsymbol{T}(\mathfrak{m}^{*})\longrightarrow\mathrm{End}_{k}(V),\quad e_{i}\longmapsto\varphi_{i}.

We now define

\boxed{\boldsymbol{H}_{A}=\boldsymbol{T}(\mathfrak{m}^{*}).}

We are now required to endow
\boldsymbol{H}_{A}
with the structure of a cocommutative bialgebra. Let
\varepsilon:\boldsymbol{H}_{A}\to k
be the map of rings defined by
\varepsilon(1)=1
and
\varepsilon(\mathfrak{m}^{*})=\{0\}
. Let
\Delta_{0}:\mathfrak{m}^{*}\to\mathfrak{m}^{*}\otimes\mathfrak{m}^{*}
be the transpose of multiplication
\mathfrak{m}\otimes\mathfrak{m}\to\mathfrak{m}
and let
\Delta:\boldsymbol{T}(\mathfrak{m}^{*})\to\boldsymbol{T}(\mathfrak{m}^{*})\otimes\boldsymbol{T}(\mathfrak{m}^{*})
be the map of
k
-algebras associated to the linear map

\Delta_{0}+\mathrm{id}\otimes 1+1\otimes\mathrm{id}:\mathfrak{m}^{*}\longrightarrow\boldsymbol{T}(\mathfrak{m}^{*})\otimes\boldsymbol{T}(\mathfrak{m}^{*}).

Explicitly, this says the following. If
c_{ij}^{h}
are the structure constants of multiplication
\mathfrak{m}\otimes\mathfrak{m}\to\mathfrak{m}
, i.e.
a_{i}a_{j}=\sum_{h=1}^{n}c_{ij}^{h}a_{h}
, then
\varepsilon(e_{i})=0
and

\Delta(e_{h})=e_{h}\otimes 1+1\otimes e_{h}+\sum_{i,j}c^{h}_{ij}\,e_{i}\otimes e_{j}.

We write
A^{*}=k\cdot\eta\oplus\mathfrak{m}^{*}
and let

(15)

f:A^{*}\longrightarrow\boldsymbol{H}_{A}

be the linear map which sends
\eta
to
1
and
\mathfrak{m}^{*}
identically to the canonical copy of
\mathfrak{m}^{*}
in
\boldsymbol{T}(\mathfrak{m}^{*})
.
If
\delta
is the comultiplication and
\mathrm{ev}_{1}:A^{*}\to k
is the co-unit of
A^{*}
, then
\mathrm{ev}_{1}=\varepsilon f
and
f\otimes f\circ\delta=\Delta\circ f
. These formulas, together with the fact that
\mathfrak{m}^{*}
generates
\boldsymbol{H}_{A}
, allow us to show that
\Delta
and
\varepsilon
endow
\boldsymbol{H}_{A}
with the structure of a coalgebra, and
f:A^{*}\to\boldsymbol{H}_{A}
is a morphism of such. Since
\varepsilon
and
\Delta
are maps of algebras,
\boldsymbol{H}_{A}
is now a cocommutative bi-algebra
.

These very natural bialgebras are unfortunately not well
documented in the literature on the theme, except in the theory of the “non-commutative symmetric functions”, as we see in the following.

Example 8.1
.

Assume that
A=k[t]/(t^{m+1})
, where
m\geq 1
. Letting
a_{i}=t^{i}
, so that
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
is a basis of
\mathfrak{m}
,
it follows that

c_{ij}^{h}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&h\not=i+j,\\
1,&h=i+j.\end{array}\right.

Then

\Delta e_{h}=e_{h}\otimes 1+1\otimes e_{h}+\sum_{i=1}^{h-1}e_{i}\otimes e_{h-i}
,
and
\boldsymbol{H}_{A}
is just the truncated algebra of non-commutative symmetric functions
\mathcal{Z}(m)
, cf. Section
7.1
.

8.2.
Pointed irreducible and cocommutative bialgebras

The bialgebra
\boldsymbol{H}_{A}
has already appeared in
[
Ne74
]
.
For the sake of the reader with little experience in the theory of coalgebras, let us make a brief introduction to some basic material from
[
Ne74
]
.

Recall from
[
A80
, p. 80]
that a coalgebra
L
is said to be irreducible if its coradical
\mathrm{corad}\,L
is a simple coalgebra. As
the Jacobson radical of
A
is
\mathfrak{m}
, it follows that
(\mathrm{corad}\,A^{*})^{\perp}=\mathfrak{m}

[
A80
, 2.3.9(i), p.84]
. Consequently,

\mathrm{corad}\,A^{*}=k\eta
and
A^{*}
is irreducible
. It is in addition
pointed

[
A80
, p. 80]
.

Following
[
Ne74
]
, we abbreviate “pointed, irreducible and cocommutative” to “PIC”, so that
A^{*}
is a PIC coalgebra. Using the map
f
of eq. (
15
), we regard
A^{*}
as a subcoalgebra of
\boldsymbol{H}_{A}
(and the element
\eta\in A^{*}
is the unit of
\boldsymbol{H}_{A}
). Let
\boldsymbol{H}_{A}(1)
be the irreducible component of
\boldsymbol{H}_{A}
containing
1

[
A80
, p.97]
: from
[
A80
, Theorem 2.4.5(ii), p.96]
,
\boldsymbol{H}_{A}(1)
is the sum of all irreducible subcoalgebras of
\boldsymbol{H}_{A}
containing
1
. In particular,
A^{*}\subset\boldsymbol{H}_{A}(1)
and
\boldsymbol{H}_{A}(1)
contains algebra generators of
\boldsymbol{H}_{A}
. Since

\boldsymbol{H}_{A}(1)
is a
subalgebra
also
[
A80
, Theorem 2.4.27p.105]
, it follows that
\boldsymbol{H}_{A}=\boldsymbol{H}_{A}(1)
. In summary,
\boldsymbol{H}_{A}
is also PIC.
A fundamental result in the theory now says that
\boldsymbol{H}_{A}
already
comes with an antipode
, see p. 71 and Proposition 9.2.5 of
[
Sw69
]
, and hence is a
Hopf algebra
. (Note that no particular property about the field
k
is employed here.)

As noted in
[
Ne74
, Remark, p.4]
,
\boldsymbol{H}_{A}
is in fact the solution to a certain universal problem in the following sense.
Let the category of PIC coalgebras, respectively Hopf algebras, be denoted by
\mathbf{picCog}
, respectively
\mathbf{picHpf}
.

Proposition 8.2
.

The forgetful functor
\mathbf{picHpf}\to\mathbf{picCog}
has a left adjoint
\mathcal{F}:\mathbf{picCog}\to\mathbf{picHpf}
and
\mathcal{F}(A^{*})=\boldsymbol{H}_{A}
. ∎

(We note that Newman uses “
F
” instead of “
\mathcal{F}
”; we made the change to avoid confusion with the Frobenius morphisms.)
The above characterisation of
\boldsymbol{H}_{A}
allowed Newman to prove a very useful structure result.

8.3.
The structure of
\boldsymbol{H}_{A}
: Newman’s Theorem

We shall require the theory of the Verschiebung morphism of cocommutative coalgebras

[
A80
, Chapter 2, Section 5.3, 112ff]
.
Let

h=\min\{i\in\mathbf{N}\,:\,\mathrm{Fr}^{i}(\mathfrak{m})=0\}.

(By assumption
h\geq 1
.)
From the duality equation for

\mathrm{Ver}:A^{*}\to A^{*}
,

\lambda(\mathrm{Ver}(a))=\lambda^{p}(a)^{1/p},\qquad\text{for each $\lambda\in(A^{*})^{*}$ and $a\in A^{*}$},

we have

\left(\mathrm{Fr}^{i}(A^{**})\right)^{\perp}=\mathrm{Ker}\,\mathrm{Ver}^{i}.

(This also holds for the cocommutative algebra
\boldsymbol{H}_{A}
.)
This gives rise to a filtration

(17)

0=K_{0}\subset\underbrace{\mathrm{Ker}\,\mathrm{Ver}}_{K_{1}}\subset\cdots\subset\underbrace{\mathrm{Ker}\,\mathrm{Ver}^{h}}_{K_{h}}=(A^{*})^{+},

where we have followed custom and written
(-)^{+}
for the augmentation ideal of a coalgebra.
Because
\boldsymbol{H}_{A}
is generated, as a
k
-algebra by
f[(A^{*})^{+}]
, the equality
\mathrm{Ver}\circ f=f\circ\mathrm{Ver}
holds true, and
\mathrm{Ver}:{\boldsymbol{H}_{A}}\to{\boldsymbol{H}_{A}}
is a morphism of rings, we
derive claims (i)–(iii) of the following:

Lemma 8.3
.

(i) The Verschiebung morphism
\mathrm{Ver}^{h}:{\boldsymbol{H}_{A}}\to{\boldsymbol{H}_{A}}
annihilates the augmentation ideal
(\boldsymbol{H}_{A})^{+}
, (ii) while
\mathrm{Ver}^{h-1}
does not. In particular, (iii)
\mathrm{Fr}^{h}
annihilates
(\boldsymbol{H}_{A}^{\circ})^{+}=\{\varphi\,:\,\varphi(1)=0\}
. (iv) The morphism
\mathrm{Fr}^{h-1}
does not annihilate
(\boldsymbol{H}_{A}^{\circ})^{+}
.

Proof.

Only (iv) remains to be checked, and this follows from the fact that the map of algebras
\boldsymbol{H}_{A}^{\circ}\to(A^{*})^{*}=A
is surjective since
\boldsymbol{H}_{A}=\boldsymbol{T}(\mathfrak{m}^{*})
.
∎

Another consequence of the filtration (
17
) is that
A^{*}
comes equipped with a
regular basis
in the sense of
[
Ne74
, Definition 2.1]
. We recall what this means. A basis
\mathcal{B}
of
A^{*}
is regular when (i)
\mathrm{Ver}(\mathcal{B})\subset\mathcal{B}\cup\{0\}
, (ii) if
\mathrm{Ver}(\lambda)=\mathrm{Ver}(\lambda^{\prime})
, then either
\lambda=\lambda^{\prime}
or
\mathrm{Ver}(\lambda)=0
and (iii)
\mathcal{B}\setminus(A^{*})^{+}\subset\{\eta\}
. Now, to construct a regular basis, we only need to employ the filtration
\{K_{i}\}
and proceed as in the construction of a Jordan basis for a nilpotent linear map.

From Theorem
[
Ne74
, Theorem 2.16]
and Proposition
8.2
, we can assure that in the category
\mathbf{Hpf}
, the Hopf algebra
\boldsymbol{H}_{A}
is a coproduct (cf. section
2.3
) of non-commutative Witt Hopf algebras, that is,

(18)

\boldsymbol{H}_{A}\simeq\mathcal{NW}_{\ell_{1}}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup\mathcal{NW}_{\ell_{m}}.

In addition,

\max_{i}\{\ell_{i}\}\leq h
and for
\ell\geq 1
, the number of copies of
\mathcal{N}\mathcal{W}_{\ell}
in the above decomposition can be determined in the following way. (The reader is asked to bear in mind the difference between our convention and that of
[
Ne74
]
, see Remark
7.8
.) A regular sequence in
\mathcal{B}
is a sequence
(b_{0},\ldots,b_{r})
of elements in
\mathcal{B}
such that
\mathrm{Ver}(b_{i})=b_{i-1}
for
i=1,\ldots,r
, and
\mathrm{Ver}(b_{0})=0
. Then, the number of copies of
\mathcal{N}\mathcal{W}_{\ell}
in the decomposition (
18
) above is the number of maximal regular sequences of
\ell

elements
.

Corollary 8.4
.

If
h=1
, then
\boldsymbol{H}_{A}
is a coproduct of copies of
\mathcal{NW}_{1}\simeq k[E_{1}]
and hence is the universal enveloping algebra of a free Lie algebra on
\dim\mathfrak{m}
generators.

Proof.

In this case,
\mathrm{Ver}(e_{i})=0
for all
1\leq i\leq n
and
(e_{1}),\ldots,(e_{n})
are maximal regular sequences.
∎

Let us now translate these findings in terms of the group scheme
\Sigma_{D}
of Definition
6.1
.
Since the functor

(-)^{\circ}:\mathbf{Hpf}\to\mathbf{Hpf}^{\mathrm{opp}}

is left-adjoint to
(-)^{\circ}:\mathbf{Hpf}^{\mathrm{opp}}\to\mathbf{Hpf}
, cf. the paragraph following
[

we conclude from general nonsense
[
Mac98

that
(-)^{\circ}

sends co-products in
\mathbf{Hpf}

to products in
\mathbf{Hpf}
.
Hence, since
\mathcal{O}(\Sigma_{D})=\boldsymbol{H}_{A}^{\circ}
, we conclude from eq. (
18
) that

\mathcal{O}(\Sigma_{D})\simeq\mathcal{NW}_{\ell_{1}}^{\circ}\sqcap\cdots\sqcap\mathcal{NW}_{\ell_{m}}^{\circ}
,
where we use the notation
\sqcap
for the product in
\mathbf{Hpf}
. More significantly, we then have.

Corollary 8.5
.

The group scheme
\Sigma_{D}\simeq\mathrm{Spec}\,\boldsymbol{H}_{A}^{\circ}
is isomorphic to the amalgamated product

\boldsymbol{N\!W}_{\ell_{1}}\star\cdots\star\boldsymbol{N\!W}_{\ell_{m}}.

The number of copies of
\boldsymbol{N\!W}_{\ell}
equals
2\dim K_{\ell}-\dim K_{\ell+1}-\dim K_{\ell-1}
, where
K_{i}=\mathrm{Ker}\,\mathrm{Ver}^{i}
.
