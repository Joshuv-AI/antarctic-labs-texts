TV homogenization inequalities
Aryeh Kontorovich

Abstract
We study the total variation distance under two information-erasing maps on inhomogeneous Bernoulli product measures: summation and homogenization. While summation is a Markov kernel and hence satisfies the usual data processing inequality, homogenization — which maps each Bernoulli parameter to the cumulative mean — is not. Nevertheless, we prove that the homogenization map also reduces the TV distance, up to a universal constant. The argument is based on an explicit two-sided control of the TV distance between Poisson binomials, obtained via a parameter interpolation and a second-moment extraction.

Introduction

For
\textup{{\bf p}}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})\in[0,1]^{n}
,

[

Equivalently, if

The sum map is easily seen to be a Markov kernel,

[

\TV(\Ber(\textup{{\bf p}}),\Ber(\textup{{\bf q}}))\geq\TV(S_{\textup{{\bf p}}},S_{\textup{{\bf q}}})

Intuitively, since information is lost under the sum map,

In this paper, we investigate

The

\bar{p}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}p_{i}

\Ber(\textup{{\bf p}})

\Ber(\bar{p})^{\otimes n}

Applying the sum map to

This indicates that the standard information-theoretic argument for proving

One such tool we propose

under the total variation metric
\TV

For
\textup{{\bf p}},\textup{{\bf q}}\in[0,1]^{n}

\displaystyle\Delta:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|p_{i}-q_{i}|,\quad\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}:=\Var(S_{\textup{{\bf p}}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}(1-p_{i}),\quad\Phi(\textup{{\bf p}},\textup{{\bf q}}):=\min\!\left(1,\frac{\Delta}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1}}\right).

\displaystyle\TV(S_{\textup{{\bf p}}},S_{\textup{{\bf q}}})\asymp\Phi(\textup{{\bf p}},\textup{{\bf q}}),\qquad\textup{{\bf p}},\textup{{\bf q}}\in[0,1]^{n},\textup{{\bf p}}\geq\textup{{\bf q}}.

\TV(\Ber(\textup{{\bf p}}),\Ber(\textup{{\bf q}}))

\textup{{\bf p}},\textup{{\bf q}}

As an application of these results, we prove the

\textup{{\bf p}},\textup{{\bf q}}\in[0,1]^{n}

\displaystyle\TV(\Ber(\textup{{\bf p}}),\Ber(\textup{{\bf q}}))

\displaystyle\gtrsim

\displaystyle\TV(\Ber(\bar{p})^{\otimes n},\Ber(\bar{q})^{\otimes n})=\TV(\Bin(n,\bar{p}),\Bin(n,\bar{q})).

Remark and open problems.

Although the homogenization inequality
relies on novel structural insights into the Poisson binomial,
there are compelling reasons to believe that a great deal
more structure remains to be uncovered, currently out of reach.
Indeed, copious numerical trials unambiguously indicate that
the correct constant in Theorem
5
should be

c=8/9

and also that the bound in Lemma
4

can be sharpened to
\delta_{N}\leq\delta_{I}+\delta_{J}-\delta_{I}\delta_{J}
(the latter, in particular, appears deceptively simple, since only binomials are involved).
Our present methods do not seem to provide
any pathway to these conjecturally optimal bounds, which will have to
await further structural advances.

2.1
Proof of Theorem
1

We will prove a sharper, symmetric bound, which immediately
implies the one in Theorem
1
:

Theorem 6
.

For
\textup{{\bf p}},\textup{{\bf q}}\in[0,1]^{n}
and
S_{\textup{{\bf p}}},S_{\textup{{\bf q}}}
the corresponding
Poisson binomials with variances
\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}
,
\sigma_{\textup{{\bf q}}}^{2}
,
we have

\displaystyle\TV(S_{\textup{{\bf p}}},S_{\textup{{\bf q}}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2C_{\mathrm{BCV}}\,\Delta}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1}+\sqrt{\sigma_{\textup{{\bf q}}}^{2}+1}}.

Proof.

The proof uses three standard ingredients.

Shift-TV for unimodal pmfs.

If
Z
is integer-valued with unimodal pmf
h(k)=\mathbb{P}(Z=k)
, then

\TV(Z,Z+1)=\max_{k}h(k),

(5)

by a simple telescoping argument.

Anti-concentration for Poisson binomials (Baillon–Cominetti–Vaisman).

If
Z=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Ber(r_{j})
has variance
v=\Var(Z)
, then

[
1
, Theorem 1]
proves the sharp bound

\max_{k}\mathbb{P}(Z=k)\leq\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{BCV}}}{\sqrt{v}}\qquad(v>0),

(6)

where
\eta_{\mathrm{BCV}}
is a universal constant
(
\eta_{\mathrm{BCV}}\approx 0.4688223555
,
[
1
, Remark 1]
).
In particular, since
\max_{k}\mathbb{P}(Z=k)\leq 1
always, we have for all
v\geq 0

\max_{k}\mathbb{P}(Z=k)\leq\min\!\left\{1,\ \frac{\eta_{\mathrm{BCV}}}{\sqrt{v}}\right\}.

(7)

Unimodality of Poisson binomial pmfs.

It is a classic
consequence of
the Aissen-Endrei-Schoenberg-Whitney
theorem
[
15
, Section 4]

that the Poisson binomial
has a log-concave,
and hence unimodal, law.

To combine these three ingredients, we begin with the interpolation.
For
t\in[0,1]
define

\displaystyle r_{i}(t):=(1-t)q_{i}+tp_{i},\qquad S(t):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Ber(r_{i}(t)),\qquad t\in[0,1],

where the Bernoullis are independent;
thus,
S(0)=S_{\textup{{\bf q}}}
and
S(1)=S_{\textup{{\bf p}}}
in distribution.
For
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
,
we write
f_{A}(t):=\mathbb{P}(S(t)\in A)
.
For each
i\in[n]
let
X_{i}(t)\sim\Ber(r_{i}(t))
denote the
i
th summand and set

T_{i}(t):=S(t)-X_{i}(t)=\sum_{j\neq i}\Ber(r_{j}(t)).

Then
T_{i}(t)
is independent of
X_{i}(t)
and
S(t)=T_{i}(t)+X_{i}(t)
.

Conditioning on
X_{i}(t)
gives

f_{A}(t)=r_{i}(t)\mathbb{P}(T_{i}(t)+1\in A)+(1-r_{i}(t))\mathbb{P}(T_{i}(t)\in A).

View
f_{A}
as a multilinear polynomial in the coordinates
(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})
.
By the chain rule,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}f_{A}(r(t))=\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}^{\prime}(t)\,\partial f_{A}/\partial r_{i}
,
and conditioning on
X_{i}(t)
yields

\partial f_{A}/\partial r_{i}=\mathbb{P}(T_{i}(t)+1\in A)-\mathbb{P}(T_{i}(t)\in A)
.
Differentiating and using
r_{i}^{\prime}(t)=p_{i}-q_{i}
yields

f_{A}^{\prime}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(p_{i}-q_{i})\Bigl(\mathbb{P}(T_{i}(t)+1\in A)-\mathbb{P}(T_{i}(t)\in A)\Bigr).

Hence, by the definition of total variation,

|f_{A}^{\prime}(t)|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}|p_{i}-q_{i}|\,\TV\!\bigl(T_{i}(t)+1,T_{i}(t)\bigr).

Each
T_{i}(t)
is Poisson binomial, hence unimodal, so by
(
5
),

\TV\!\bigl(T_{i}(t)+1,T_{i}(t)\bigr)=\max_{k}\mathbb{P}(T_{i}(t)=k).

By (
7
),

\max_{k}\mathbb{P}(T_{i}(t)=k)\leq\min\!\left\{1,\ \frac{\eta_{\mathrm{BCV}}}{\sqrt{\Var(T_{i}(t))}}\right\}.

Since
S(t)=T_{i}(t)+X_{i}(t)
with independence and
\Var(X_{i}(t))\leq\tfrac{1}{4}
, we have

\Var(T_{i}(t))=\Var(S(t))-\Var(X_{i}(t))\geq\Var(S(t))-\frac{1}{4}.

Let
x_{+}:=\max\{x,0\}
. Then

\max_{k}\mathbb{P}(T_{i}(t)=k)\leq\min\!\left\{1,\ \frac{\eta_{\mathrm{BCV}}}{\sqrt{\bigl(\Var(S(t))-\frac{1}{4}\bigr)_{+}}}\right\}.

We now upper bound this “singular” expression by a smooth envelope.
Set
C_{\mathrm{BCV}}:=\sqrt{\tfrac{5}{4}+\eta_{\mathrm{BCV}}^{2}}
. Then for every
x\geq 0
we have

\min\!\left\{1,\ \frac{\eta_{\mathrm{BCV}}}{\sqrt{(x-\frac{1}{4})_{+}}}\right\}\leq\frac{C_{\mathrm{BCV}}}{\sqrt{x+1}}.

(8)

Indeed, if
x\leq\tfrac{1}{4}+\eta_{\mathrm{BCV}}^{2}
, the left-hand side equals
1
and the right-hand side is

C_{\mathrm{BCV}}/\sqrt{x+1}\geq C_{\mathrm{BCV}}/\sqrt{\tfrac{5}{4}+\eta_{\mathrm{BCV}}^{2}}=1
.
If
x\geq\tfrac{1}{4}+\eta_{\mathrm{BCV}}^{2}
, then the left-hand side equals
\eta_{\mathrm{BCV}}/\sqrt{x-\tfrac{1}{4}}
and
squaring shows that (
8
) is equivalent to
x\geq\tfrac{1}{4}+\eta_{\mathrm{BCV}}^{2}
.

Applying (
8
) with
x=\Var(S(t))
gives

\TV\!\bigl(T_{i}(t)+1,T_{i}(t)\bigr)\leq\frac{C_{\mathrm{BCV}}}{\sqrt{\Var(S(t))+1}},

and substituting back,

|f_{A}^{\prime}(t)|\leq\frac{C_{\mathrm{BCV}}\,\Delta}{\sqrt{\Var(S(t))+1}}.

(9)

Since
u\mapsto u(1-u)
is concave,

r_{i}(t)(1-r_{i}(t))\geq(1-t)\,q_{i}(1-q_{i})+t\,p_{i}(1-p_{i}),\qquad i\in[n],

and summing over
i
yields

\Var(S(t))\geq(1-t)\sigma_{\textup{{\bf q}}}^{2}+t\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}.

Combining with (
9
),

|f_{A}^{\prime}(t)|\leq\frac{C_{\mathrm{BCV}}\,\Delta}{\sqrt{(1-t)\sigma_{\textup{{\bf q}}}^{2}+t\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1}}.

Integrating over
t
gives

\bigl|f_{A}(1)-f_{A}(0)\bigr|\leq C_{\mathrm{BCV}}\,\Delta\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{\sqrt{(1-t)(\sigma_{\textup{{\bf q}}}^{2}+1)+t(\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1)}}.

Write
(1-t)(\sigma_{\textup{{\bf q}}}^{2}+1)+t(\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1)=b+at
with

b=\sigma_{\textup{{\bf q}}}^{2}+1
and
a=\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}-\sigma_{\textup{{\bf q}}}^{2}
.
If
a=0
, the integral is
1/\sqrt{b}
.
If
a\neq 0
, then

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{\sqrt{b+at}}=\left[\frac{2}{a}\sqrt{b+at}\right]_{0}^{1}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{b+a}+\sqrt{b}}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1}+\sqrt{\sigma_{\textup{{\bf q}}}^{2}+1}}.

Thus, for every event
A
,

\bigl|\mathbb{P}(S_{\textup{{\bf p}}}\in A)-\mathbb{P}(S_{\textup{{\bf q}}}\in A)\bigr|\leq\frac{2C_{\mathrm{BCV}}\,\Delta}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1}+\sqrt{\sigma_{\textup{{\bf q}}}^{2}+1}}.

Taking
\sup_{A}
proves (
6
).
∎

2.2
Proof of Theorem
2

We begin with an abstract
result
showing
that if a positive
measure
g
on
\mathbb{Z}
has total mass
G
and its variance-like
spread about
\mu
is controlled by
J<\infty
, this implies a lower
bound on the mass of the heaviest atom.

Lemma 7

(Pigeonhole)
.

For
g:\mathbb{Z}\to[0,\infty)

and
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
,
define

G:=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}g(k)\qquad J:=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(k-\mu)^{2}\,g(k).

If
0<G,J<\infty
, then

\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}g(k)\;\geq\;\frac{3\,G^{3/2}}{16\sqrt{J}+4\sqrt{G}}.

Proof.

Put

R:=2\sqrt{{J}/{G}}

and
define

A:=\{k\in\mathbb{Z}:\ |k-\mu|\leq R\},\qquad A^{\mathrm{c}}:=\mathbb{Z}\setminus A.

A Chebyshev-type argument shows that
at least
\tfrac{3}{4}
of the mass of
g
lies in
A
.
Indeed,
on
A^{\mathrm{c}}
we have
(k-\mu)^{2}>R^{2}
, so

J=\sum_{k}(k-\mu)^{2}g(k)\geq\sum_{k\in A^{\mathrm{c}}}(k-\mu)^{2}g(k)>R^{2}\sum_{k\in A^{\mathrm{c}}}g(k),

hence

\sum_{k\in A^{\mathrm{c}}}g(k)\leq\frac{J}{R^{2}}=\frac{J}{4J/G}=\frac{G}{4}.

Therefore

\sum_{k\in A}g(k)=G-\sum_{k\in A^{\mathrm{c}}}g(k)\geq\frac{3G}{4}.

Next, we extract the “heavy” atom in
A
via a cardinality
bound and the pigeonhole principle:

|A|\leq 2R+1=4\sqrt{\frac{J}{G}}+1,

and so

\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}g(k)\ \geq\ \sup_{k\in A}g(k)\ \geq\ \frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{k\in A}g(k)\ \geq\ \frac{(3G/4)}{4\sqrt{J/G}+1}.

The latter simplifies to our claimed bound.
∎

As above,
(\textup{{\bf p}},\textup{{\bf q}})
is a dominating pair with
p_{i}\geq q_{i}
and associated
Poisson binomials

S_{\textup{{\bf p}}}
,
S_{\textup{{\bf q}}}
.
We will apply the pigeonhole lemma to extract a “wedge” event
with sufficient separation in probability under

S_{\textup{{\bf p}}}

and
S_{\textup{{\bf q}}}
to achieve the requisite TV lower bound.
To this end, we define the
G
and
J
functionals — both
in terms of the non-negative (by the monotone coupling) measure
g
on
\mathbb{Z}
:

g(k):=\mathbb{P}(X\geq k)-\mathbb{P}(Y\geq k),\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

(10)

Then

G=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}g(k)

has the more familiar form

G:=\Delta=m_{\textup{{\bf p}}}-m_{\textup{{\bf q}}}=\mathbb{E}X-\mathbb{E}Y,

where

X:=S_{\textup{{\bf p}}},Y:=S_{\textup{{\bf q}}}

and

m_{\textup{{\bf p}}}:=\mathbb{E}X,m_{\textup{{\bf q}}}:=\mathbb{E}Y
.
Moreover, for any
k
,

|g(k)|=|\mathbb{P}(X\geq k)-\mathbb{P}(Y\geq k)|\leq\TV(S_{\textup{{\bf p}}},S_{\textup{{\bf q}}}),

and so

\TV(S_{\textup{{\bf p}}},S_{\textup{{\bf q}}})\;\geq\;\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}g(k).

(11)

Finally,

J=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(k-m_{\textup{{\bf p}}})^{2}g(k)
is defined as in Lemma
7
.
The technical core of the argument hinges on upper bounding
J
:

Lemma 8
.

Let
X,Y,\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}=\Var(X),g,m_{\textup{{\bf p}}},\Delta,J
be defined as above.
Then

J\;\leq\;2\,\Delta\,\bigl(\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1+\Delta^{2}\bigr).

Proof.

For
i\in[n]
,
write
\Delta_{i}:=p_{i}-q_{i}\geq 0
with
\sum_{i}\Delta_{i}=\Delta
. For

t\in[0,1]
set

r_{i}(t):=q_{i}+t\Delta_{i},\quad X_{i}(t)\sim\Ber(r_{i}(t))\ \text{independent},\quad S(t):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}(t),\quad T_{i}(t):=S(t)-X_{i}(t).

Then
S(0)=Y
and
S(1)=X
in distribution. For fixed
n
, each

S(t)
has a distribution that is a finite polynomial in the parameters

\{r_{i}(t)\}
, so for each
k
and
i
the function

\displaystyle F_{k}(t)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(S(t)\geq k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r_{i}(t)\,\mathbb{P}(T_{i}(t)\geq k-1)\;+\;(1-r_{i}(t))\,\mathbb{P}(T_{i}(t)\geq k).

is a polynomial in
t
and hence differentiable on
[0,1]
.
Differentiating,

\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{i}}F_{k}=\mathbb{P}(T_{i}(t)\geq k-1)-\mathbb{P}(T_{i}(t)\geq k)=\mathbb{P}(T_{i}(t)=k-1).

By the chain rule and the fact that
r_{i}^{\prime}(t)=\Delta_{i}
,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\,\mathbb{P}(S(t)\geq k)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}\,\mathbb{P}\bigl(T_{i}(t)=k-1\bigr),\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

Integrating from
0
to
1
and using
S(0)=Y
,

S(1)=X
in distribution,

g(k)=\mathbb{P}(X\geq k)-\mathbb{P}(Y\geq k)=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}\,\mathbb{P}\bigl(T_{i}(t)=k-1\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t,\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

In this formulation, the functional
J=\sum_{k}(k-m_{\textup{{\bf p}}})^{2}g(k)

becomes

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(k-m_{\textup{{\bf p}}})^{2}\mathbb{P}\bigl(T_{i}(t)=k-1\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Changing variable
j=k-1
,

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(k-m_{\textup{{\bf p}}})^{2}\mathbb{P}\bigl(T_{i}(t)=k-1\bigr)=\mathbb{E}_{t}\bigl[(T_{i}(t)+1-m_{\textup{{\bf p}}})^{2}\bigr],

where
\mathbb{E}_{t}
denotes expectation under the product law with
parameters
\{r_{j}(t)\}_{j}
. Hence

J=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}\,\mathbb{E}_{t}\bigl[(T_{i}(t)+1-m_{\textup{{\bf p}}})^{2}\bigr]\,\mathrm{d}t.

(12)

We bound
\mathbb{E}_{t}[(T_{i}(t)+1-m_{\textup{{\bf p}}})^{2}]
uniformly in
t,i

via the decomposition

\mathbb{E}[(Z-\alpha)]^{2}=\Var(Z)+(\mathbb{E}Z-\alpha)^{2}
,
where
Z=T_{i}(t)+1
and
\alpha=m_{\textup{{\bf p}}}
.

To bound the variance term, note that

f(x)=x(1-x)

is
1
-Lipschitz on
[0,1]
, and so

|f(r_{j}(t))-f(p_{j})|\leq|r_{j}(t)-p_{j}|=(1-t)\Delta_{j}.

Hence

\Var_{t}(S(t))=\sum_{j}f(r_{j}(t))\leq\sum_{j}f(p_{j})+(1-t)\sum_{j}\Delta_{j}=\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+(1-t)\Delta\leq\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+\Delta\leq\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1+\Delta^{2}.

Since
T_{i}(t)=S(t)-X_{i}(t)
with
X_{i}(t)
Bernoulli,

\displaystyle\Var_{t}(T_{i}(t))\leq\Var_{t}(S(t))\leq\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1+\Delta^{2}.

(13)

To bound the bias term, we have

\mathbb{E}_{t}[S(t)]=m_{\textup{{\bf q}}}+t\Delta,

and
m_{\textup{{\bf p}}}=m_{\textup{{\bf q}}}+\Delta
, so
\mathbb{E}_{t}[S(t)]-m_{\textup{{\bf p}}}=-(1-t)\Delta
.
Also
r_{i}(t)=q_{i}+t\Delta_{i}
, so

\mathbb{E}_{t}[T_{i}(t)]=m_{\textup{{\bf q}}}+t\Delta-(q_{i}+t\Delta_{i}),

and hence

M_{i}(t):=\mathbb{E}_{t}[T_{i}(t)+1-m_{\textup{{\bf p}}}]=1-q_{i}-\Delta+t(\Delta-\Delta_{i}).

It is straightforward to see that

|M_{i}(t)|\leq\max(1,\Delta)

and thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}\bigl[(T_{i}(t)+1-m_{\textup{{\bf p}}})^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Var_{t}(T_{i}(t))+M_{i}(t)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1+\Delta^{2})+(\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1+\Delta^{2})=2(\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1+\Delta^{2}).

Substituting into (
12
) gives

J\leq\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{i}\Delta_{i}\,2(\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1+\Delta^{2})\,\mathrm{d}t=2\,\Delta\,(\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1+\Delta^{2}),

as claimed.
∎

Corollary 9

(Proof of Theorem
2
)
.

Let
(\textup{{\bf p}},\textup{{\bf q}})
be a dominating pair with
laws
S_{\textup{{\bf p}}},S_{\textup{{\bf q}}}
,
sums
S_{\textup{{\bf p}}},S_{\textup{{\bf q}}}
,
\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}:=\Var(S_{\textup{{\bf p}}})
,
and

\Delta:=\mathbb{E}S_{\textup{{\bf p}}}-\mathbb{E}S_{\textup{{\bf q}}}
.
Then

\TV(S_{\textup{{\bf p}}},S_{\textup{{\bf q}}})\;\geq\;c\,\min\Bigl(1,\frac{\Delta}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1}}\Bigr),

for some universal constant
c\geq\frac{1}{12}
.

Proof.

Recall
g(k):=\mathbb{P}(S_{\textup{{\bf p}}}\geq k)-\mathbb{P}(S_{\textup{{\bf q}}}\geq k)\geq 0
and

\Delta=\sum_{k}g(k),\qquad J=\sum_{k}(k-m_{\textup{{\bf p}}})^{2}g(k),\qquad m_{\textup{{\bf p}}}=\mathbb{E}S_{\textup{{\bf p}}}.

By (
11
),

\TV(S_{\textup{{\bf p}}},S_{\textup{{\bf q}}})\geq\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}g(k)
.
By Lemma
8
,

J\;\leq\;2\,\Delta\,(\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1+\Delta^{2}).

Apply Lemma
7
with
G=\Delta

to obtain

\sup_{k}g(k)\;\geq\;\frac{3\Delta}{16\sqrt{2(\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1+\Delta^{2})}+4}.

We compare
\sqrt{\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1+\Delta^{2}}
with

\sqrt{\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1}
and consider the two cases.
If

\Delta\leq\sqrt{\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1}
,
then
\Delta^{2}\leq\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1
, so

\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1+\Delta^{2}\leq 2(\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1),

and

\displaystyle\TV(S_{\textup{{\bf p}}},S_{\textup{{\bf q}}})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{3\Delta}{16\sqrt{2}\sqrt{2(\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1)}+4}=\frac{3\Delta}{32\sqrt{\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1}+4}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{3\Delta}{36\sqrt{\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1}}=\frac{\Delta}{12\sqrt{\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1}}.

If

\Delta\geq\sqrt{\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1}
,
then
\Delta^{2}\geq\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1
, so

\sigma_{\textup{{\bf p}}}^{2}+1+\Delta^{2}\leq 2\Delta^{2},

and

\displaystyle\TV(S_{\textup{{\bf p}}},S_{\textup{{\bf q}}})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{3\Delta}{16\sqrt{2}\sqrt{2\Delta^{2}}+4}=\frac{3\Delta}{32\Delta+4}\geq\frac{3\Delta}{36\Delta}=\frac{1}{12}.

Combining the two cases proves the claim.
∎

Proof of Theorem

Both inequalities invoke the data processing inequality; the first in the form

\displaystyle\TV(P\otimes P^{\prime},Q\otimes Q^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\max(\TV(P,Q),\TV(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})),

and the second in the form

\TV(\Ber(\textup{{\bf p}}),\Ber(\textup{{\bf q}}))\geq\TV(S_{\textup{{\bf p}}},S_{\textup{{\bf q}}})
.
Given these, the claim is

2.4
Proof of Lemma
4

Proof.

We begin by representing
\Bin(n,\bar{p}_{N})
as a mixture.
Let
M\sim\Bin(n,w)
where
w=|I|/n
. Conditionally on
M=m
, let

U_{m}\sim\Bin(m,\bar{p}_{I}),\qquad V_{m}\sim\Bin(n-m,\bar{p}_{J})

be independent and set
S_{m}:=U_{m}+V_{m}
.
We claim that
S_{M}\sim\Bin(n,\bar{p}_{N})
.
Indeed, for
t\in\mathbb{R}
, put
s:=1-\bar{p}_{I}+\bar{p}_{I}t
and
r:=1-\bar{p}_{J}+\bar{p}_{J}t
.
Compute the probability generating function conditional on
M
:

\mathbb{E}[t^{S_{M}}\mid M]=\mathbb{E}[t^{U_{M}+V_{M}}\mid M]=\mathbb{E}[t^{U_{M}}\mid M]\;\mathbb{E}[t^{V_{M}}\mid M]=s^{\,M}r^{\,n-M},

so
\mathbb{E}[t^{S_{M}}]=\mathbb{E}[s^{M}r^{n-M}]
. Represent
M=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
where

X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are i.i.d.
\Ber(w)
. Then
n-M=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-X_{i})
and

s^{M}r^{n-M}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}s^{X_{i}}r^{1-X_{i}}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(r+(s-r)X_{i}\bigr).

Taking expectation and using independence,

\mathbb{E}[s^{M}r^{n-M}]=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\bigl[r+(s-r)X_{i}\bigr]=\bigl(r+(s-r)\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]\bigr)^{n}=\bigl((1-w)r+ws\bigr)^{n}.

Substituting
r=1-\bar{p}_{J}+\bar{p}_{J}t
and
s=1-\bar{p}_{I}+\bar{p}_{I}t
gives

\mathbb{E}[t^{S_{M}}]=\bigl(w(1-\bar{p}_{I}+\bar{p}_{I}t)+(1-w)(1-\bar{p}_{J}+\bar{p}_{J}t)\bigr)^{n}=(1-\bar{p}_{N}+\bar{p}_{N}\,t)^{n},

which is the PGF of
\Bin(n,\bar{p}_{N})
.
Since a distribution with finite support is characterized by its PGF,

S_{M}\sim\Bin(n,\bar{p}_{N})
.
Similarly, if
U^{\prime}_{m}\sim\Bin(m,\bar{q}_{I})
and
V^{\prime}_{m}\sim\Bin(n-m,\bar{q}_{J})
are independent and

S^{\prime}_{m}:=U^{\prime}_{m}+V^{\prime}_{m}
, then the same calculation yields
S^{\prime}_{M}\sim\Bin(n,\bar{q}_{N})
.
Therefore

\delta_{N}=\TV(S_{M},S^{\prime}_{M}).

For any event
A\subset\{0,1,\dots,n\}
,

\mathbb{P}(S_{M}\in A)-\mathbb{P}(S^{\prime}_{M}\in A)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbb{P}(S_{M}\in A\mid M)-\mathbb{P}(S^{\prime}_{M}\in A\mid M)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\TV(\Law(S_{M}\mid M),\Law(S^{\prime}_{M}\mid M))\right],

and taking the supremum over
A
yields

\TV(S_{M},S^{\prime}_{M})\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\TV(\Law(S_{M}\mid M),\Law(S^{\prime}_{M}\mid M))\right]=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\mathbb{P}(M=m)\,\TV(S_{m},S^{\prime}_{m}).

Next, the map
(u,v)\mapsto u+v
is a Markov kernel, and so

\TV(S_{m},S^{\prime}_{m})\leq\TV\bigl((U_{m},V_{m}),(U^{\prime}_{m},V^{\prime}_{m})\bigr).

Moreover, for product measures one has

\TV(P\times R,\;Q\times S)\leq\TV(P,Q)+\TV(R,S),

(14)

whence

\TV(S_{m},S^{\prime}_{m})\leq\TV(U_{m},U^{\prime}_{m})+\TV(V_{m},V^{\prime}_{m}).

Define

\Delta_{I}(m):=\TV(\Bin(m,\bar{p}_{I}),\Bin(m,\bar{q}_{I})),\qquad\Delta_{J}(k):=\TV(\Bin(k,\bar{p}_{J}),\Bin(k,\bar{q}_{J})).

Then

\delta_{N}\leq\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{I}(M)]+\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{J}(n-M)].

It remains to estimate the two expectations.
Fix
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in[0,1]
and define
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
by

f(m):=\TV(\Bin(m,\theta),\Bin(m,\theta^{\prime})).

We claim that
f
is nondecreasing and subadditive.
To show subadditivity,
let
X\sim\Bin(m,\theta)
,
Y\sim\Bin(k,\theta)
be independent so that

X+Y\sim\Bin(m+k,\theta)
, and similarly let
X^{\prime}\sim\Bin(m,\theta^{\prime})
,

Y^{\prime}\sim\Bin(k,\theta^{\prime})
independent so that
X^{\prime}+Y^{\prime}\sim\Bin(m+k,\theta^{\prime})
.
Then

f(m+k)=\TV(X+Y,\;X^{\prime}+Y^{\prime})\leq\TV(X,X^{\prime})+\TV(Y,Y^{\prime})=f(m)+f(k).

To show
monotonicity, observe that the following is a Markov kernel
that maps
\Bin(m+1,\theta)
to
\Bin(m,\theta)
: interpret
\Bin(m+1,\theta)
as the number of successes in
m+1
i.i.d.
\Ber(\theta)
trials and delete one uniformly random trial. Conditional on seeing
k
successes among
m+1
trials, the remaining number of successes equals
k
with probability
\frac{m+1-k}{m+1}
(deleted a failure) and equals
k-1
with probability
\frac{k}{m+1}
(deleted a success). This kernel does not depend on
\theta
, and it sends
\Bin(m+1,\theta)
to
\Bin(m,\theta)
for every
\theta
. Therefore, by TV contraction under the same kernel,

f(m)=\TV(T_{m}(\Bin(m+1,\theta)),T_{m}(\Bin(m+1,\theta^{\prime})))\leq\TV(\Bin(m+1,\theta),\Bin(m+1,\theta^{\prime}))=f(m+1).

Now let
m_{0}\geq 1
be an integer. By subadditivity and monotonicity,
for every
m\geq 0
, writing
m=km_{0}+r
with
0\leq r<m_{0}
,

f(m)\leq kf(m_{0})+f(r)\leq(k+1)f(m_{0})=\left\lceil\frac{m}{m_{0}}\right\rceil f(m_{0}).

Hence, for any integer–valued
M
with
\mathbb{E}M=m_{0}
, using
\lceil x\rceil\leq x+1
,

\mathbb{E}[f(M)]\leq f(m_{0})\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left\lceil\frac{M}{m_{0}}\right\rceil\right]\leq f(m_{0})\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{M}{m_{0}}+1\right]=2f(m_{0}).

Apply this with
f=\Delta_{I}
and
m_{0}=|I|
(note
\mathbb{E}M=nw=|I|
) to get

\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{I}(M)]\leq 2\Delta_{I}(|I|)=2\delta_{I},

and with
f=\Delta_{J}
and
m_{0}=|J|
(since
\mathbb{E}(n-M)=|J|
) to get

\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{J}(n-M)]\leq 2\Delta_{J}(|J|)=2\delta_{J}.

This completes the proof.
∎
