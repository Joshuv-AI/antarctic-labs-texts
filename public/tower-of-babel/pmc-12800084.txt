Assessment of soybean cultivars’responses to diverse climatic conditions in Northern Poland in terms of yield and seed composition
Authors: Beata Kaliska, Andrzej Kotecki, Bernard Gałka, Magdalena Serafin-Andrzejewska, Henryk Bujak, Anna Jama-Rodzeńska
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSoybean (Glycine max) is an important source of plant-based protein and oil, but its cultivation is highly sensitive to climate conditions. In Poland, interest in soybean is growing due to climate change and increasing demand for protein-rich crops. However, cultivation of photophilic crops is still limited. This study presents results from field trials conducted in Northern Poland from 2017 to 2019, involving 13 registered soybean cultivars tested at 10 locations. The aim of the study was to evaluate seed yield, protein and fat content and protein yield under varying environmental conditions. Weather variability, particularly temperature and rainfall, had a greater influence on results than the cultivar tested. Advanced statistical analyses showed that, of all 13 tested cultivars, Moravians (mid-late) had the most favorable WAAS and GSI values in terms of protein yield. According to WTOP3 score, the Kofu (late) cultivar had the highest adaptability for seeds yield and protein yield. Protein yield is the most important indicator of the profitablility of soybean cultivation in countries with a deficit of feed plant protein. Introduction

Soybean (Glycine max) plays a vital role in global food security and agricultural economies, serving as a major source of plant-based protein and oil. However, its cultivation is highly dependent on climatic conditions, making it particularly vulnerable to the effects of global climate change. Rising temperatures, altered precipitation patterns, and the increasing frequency of extreme weather events may significantly reduce soybean yields in various regions around the world1,2. Extreme weather events3,4 along with complex, overlapping climatic occurrences—whether in terms of timing or multiple stress factors5,6 have been regarded as key contributors to crop production failures. The frequency and severity of such events have intensified in recent decades, largely due to ongoing climate change7. Climate change affects not only the yield and stability of crop production but also change the geographical distribution of cultivation areas, plant phenology, and soil quality. To cope with the impacts of climate change, it is crucial to implementadaptive measures that enhance the sustainability of soybean farming. These may include the use of earlier-ripening cultivars, shifting sowingtimes, applying efficient irrigation techniques, and optimizing soil management practices to improve the crop’s resilience and secure future yields1,8.

In the face of climate change and increasing demand for plant-based protein, soybean cultivation is gaining prominence in Poland. Changes in climate patterns—such as higher average temperatures and more frequent droughts—are creating the environmental conditions for soybean farming9. These changes call for adjustments in agricultural technologies and the careful selection of adapted cultivars. In Poland, the total area dedicated to soybean cultivation has remained relatively modest, ranging between 12,000 and 24,000 hectares during the 2014–2019 period, though it has shown a steady upward trend10. This limited area is due to the species’ sensitivity to climatic conditions—it requires sowing into soils with temperatures of at least 12 °C, and average air temperatures during the growing season should not fall below 15 °C11,12. According to recent guidelines, the most favorable date in Poland for sowing is from the last ten days of April to the first ten days of May, when the upper soil layer has warmed to around 10 °C13,14. The increase in soybean cultivation area can be attributed to the growing availability of cultivars adapted to Poland’s climate, as well as its nutritional advantages and lower soil fertility requirements compared to other protein-rich crops15. One of the most important factors determining the success of soybean cultivation in Poland is the appropriate choice of -cultivar. In Poland, 44 soybean cultivars are listed in the national register. The earliness of cultivars ranges from 2.5 to 7.5 on a 9-point scale. Selecting the right soybean cultivar is a critical factor influencing both yield potential and seed quality. Different cultivars exhibit specific environmental requirements, such as duration of the growing season, and varying levels of tolerance to abiotic stressors and diseases. Therefore, careful matching of a cultivar to the local agro-climatic conditions is essential. Failure to select an appropriate cultivar for a given region can lead to suboptimal yields and economic inefficiencies.

Evaluating how genotypes interact with their environment is pivotal for determining the stability and performance of different crop cultivars16,17. The aim of the research conducted in 2017–2019 was to assess the suitability of environmental conditions in northern Poland for soybean cultivation. The study evaluated the yield, yield stability, and adaptability of 13 commercial soybean cultivars representing different maturity groups, tested in 10 locations in northern Poland. The environments under study differed in physiography, soil characteristics, and climatic conditions, including seasonal variations in moisture and temperature. It was hypothesised that it would be possible to identify genotypes best suited to specific environmental conditions, reaching harvest maturity at the end of September—after approximately 150 days of vegetation. In addition to the length of the growing season, the following characteristics were also taken into account when selecting soybean cultivars for cultivation: seed yield, protein and fat content in seeds, and protein yield.

To assess if the cultivars are appropriate for growing in Northern Poland, several parameters and statistical tests were used, such as AMMI-adjusted means, WTOP3, WAAS, and GSI indices17.

Material and methods

Research background

Currently, soybeans are grown worldwide in various soil and climatic conditions. Poland lies between 49 and 54° north latitude, and the climatic conditions are not suitable for growing this crop. The factors limiting cultivation in Poland are the plant’s heat requirements and its response to day length. Most high-yielding genotypes are short-day plants. In the case of cultivars recommended for cultivation in higher latitudes, a day length exceeding 16 h at the beginning of flowering may delay subsequent stages of development, which carries the risk that the plants will not reach full maturity before the onset of autumn frosts.18 Poland lies in a humid continental climate zone.19 This climate is also called transitional between a warm and rainy temperate climate and a snowy and cold boreal climate. Due to its location in the center of Europe and the geographical distribution of its regions (the Baltic Sea in the north and the Tatra Mountains in the south), various air masses meet over Poland. Other types of temperate climate also flow into the country—maritime from the west and continental from the east.20The average air temperature in Poland in 2022 amounted to 9.5 °C and was 0.8 degrees higher than the multi-year average (normal climate period 1991–2020). The year 2023, with an annual average temperature for the country of 10 °C, turned out to be the second warmest year of the twenty-first century. The average annual precipitation in Poland has now reached 500–600 mm, but its distribution is not evenly spread throughout the year and over the country. The average insolation for Poland is 1600 h. The influence of the Baltic Sea on the climate of northern Poland mainly consists in moderating temperatures, resulting in cooler summers and milder winters, as well as increased air humidity and higher rainfall compared to the central part of the country. The length of the growing season in northern Poland ranges from approximately 190 to 210 days, which is shorter than in central and western Poland, but longer than in mountain regions21.

Soybean cultivation is difficult and risky in countries located at higher latitudes, and Poland is a very good example of this. There are significant differences in climatic conditions in different parts of the country. This means that it is necessary to select the right soybean cultivar for regional and in fact local conditions.

Location of experiment

This dataset presents averaged results for different soybean cultivars tested in field experiments conducted across Northern Poland from 2017 to 2019. The trials were part of an extensive research program on soybean cultivation led by Research Centre for Cultivar Testing (Centralny Ośrodek Badania Odmian Roślin Uprawnych—COBORU)22. The experiments followed a as one-factor randomized complete block design and were carried out with three replications at research stations operated by COBORU. The research took place in northern Poland, under differentiated site conditions (weather and soil) across 10 locations (Fig. 1). The list of locations where the experiments were conducted is provided in Table 1. The study included 13 soybean cultivars registered in both the National List of Cultivars (NL) and the Common Catalogue of Agricultural Plant Species (CCA) (Table 2). All trials were conducted following the methodology developed by COBORU22 on plots measuring 16.5 m2, except at ZDOO Radostowo, where the plot size was 15 m2.

Soil conditions

The table is provided in the supplementary material and presents: soil class, agricultural utility complex, soil species, soil type, and agricultural quality and suitability of soils in the studied environments of Northern Poland with soybean experiments (Table S1).

Soybean cultivation in the studied regions of Northern Poland was mainly carried out on Luvisol soils of various origins, accounting for 64% of the total area. This was followed by black soils (24%) and brown soils (12%), all developed from parent materials with varying grain-size composition. The lowest soil quality (class IVb) was associated with light loamy sands, while the highest (class II) occurred on soils formed from light clay or silty loam23,24. In the studied areas, soil valuation scores ranged from 52 to 94 points, with 72% of soils scoring between 70 and 80. Soils with a score of 70 were primarily sandy in origin, while those scoring 80 were mainly derived from clay-rich materials. The soil nutrient content, shown in Table S2, differed across the studied sites, reflecting variability in environmental and soil formation conditions.

Statistical analysis

An AMMI (Additive Main Effects and Multiplicative Interaction) analysis was performed for all measured traits using the Genstat software26. The analysis used a two-way classification matrix (cultivar × environment) based on means from replicates. “Environment” was defined as the combination of location and growing season year, representing the environmental conditions of crop cultivation, including soil fertility, water availability, pests pressure, topography, and seasonal weather conditions. To group cultivars based on all studied traits, Ward’s hierarchical clustering method was applied, using squared Euclidean distance27. This allowed for multivariate comparison of soybean genotypes grown under the agro-environmental conditions of northern Poland. Additionally, the Superiority Index of Adaptability Wi TOP X was calculated for the ith cultivar, reflecting its performance relative to the top-performing genotypes across selected environments, where GSI is the selection index for the ith genotype, RY represents the rank of the cultivar’s mean value for the analyzed trait, and RWAAS is the rank based on its WAASi stability index. To evaluate the adaptability of soybean cultivars, the WT0PX index was used presented in Fox et al.30This index, denoted as WT0PX for X = 1,2,3,…X = 1, 2, 3,…X = 1,2,3,…, provides a measure of genotype performance across multiple environments. WT0PX coefficient was determined for each soybean cultivar using predicted values derived from the AMMIκ model. A higher WT0PX score suggests that a cultivar is broadly adapted across environments, while a lower score points to more specific environmental adaptation.

The analyzed dataset included 13 cultivars, 10 locations, and 3 years, with some locations (environments) not present in all years. In 2017, soybean cultivar trials were conducted in 5 locations (Białogard, Głębokie, Radostowo, Świebodzin, Wrócikowo), while in 2018–2019, trials were carried out in 10 locations (Białogard, Chrząstowo, Głębokie, Karzniczka, Krzyżewo, Marianowo, Nowa Wieś Ujska, Radostowo, Świebodzin, Wrócikowo), which altogether results in 25 unique environments.

Variance of analysis for seed yield, protein and fat content and protein yield

Significant variation in average seed yields was observed across the tested locations. environments (defined by location and year), with environmental effects accounting for 86.3% of the total sum of squares, indicating that environment (location) had a dominant role in shaping yield. Genotypic differences were also statistically significant, though they contributed only 2.1% to the total variation, while interaction genotype x environment (G × E) interactions explained 9.3%. All interaction principal components included in the AMMI model were highly significant. The first four interaction principal component axes (IPCA 1–4) collectively explained 76% of the total G × E interaction variance, with IPCA 1 accounting for 30.1%, IPCA 2 for 22.2%, IPCA 3 for 12.0%, and IPCA 4 for 11.6% (Table 4).

Cultivar selection in Northern Poland in relations to yield and protein and fat content

The soybean cultivars Abelina, Aligator, Kofu, Naya, SG Anser, Sirelia, and Sultana exhibited varying adaptive responses across the tested environments. Abelina, Kofu, Naya, and Sirelia frequently ranked among the top-performing cultivars in the majority of locations in relations to seed yield, which may point to their broader environmental adaptability. On the other hand, cultivars such as Aligator, SG Anser, and Sultana achieved high performance only in a limited number of environments, suggesting a more specific or narrower adaptation profile (Table 8).

In most environments, Erica, Moravians, and Sultana consistently ranked among the top four performing cultivars in relations to protein content. In contrast, Abelina, Aligator, Naya, and SG Anser appeared in the top four in only a few environments, which may indicate their narrower environmental adaptability (Table 9).

Discussion

The study aimed to assess the performance of soybean cultivars in regions of Poland with the least favorable temperature and moisture conditions. Rainfall plays an important role in this region. The northern part of Poland generally experiences higher summer precipitation compared to central regions, which can often create challenges and contribute to unsuccessful soybean cultivation in these areas.Soybean could be cultivated in northern Poland despite previous findings indicate that this was not entirely accurate, as new cultivars tested can be successfully grown in the northern regions of the country. In the study by Serafin-Andrzejewska et al.31, the effect of sowing date on soybean yield and quality was analyzed in the conditions of south-western Poland and compared with sowing dates in other regions of the country. The authors indicate that Lower Silesia, near Wrocław, is the most suitable region for soybean cultivation in Poland due to its continental climate and a longer growing season compared to other parts of the country.

Soybean seed yield and composition result from the complex interplay between cultivar factors (C), environmental conditions (E). While it is well recognized that all these components influence production32–34, the precise extent to which each factor contributes to yield and seed quality remains unclear. This uncertainty highlights the importance of carefully selecting cultivars that are well-adapted to specific soil and climatic conditions in order to optimize soybean production. Numerous studies have presented that climatic variables, soil characteristics and the regional adaptation of cultivars are important factors for achieving high yields and quality in soybean cultivation.35–37 This is especially in Central Europe, including Poland, where soybean cultivation success strongly depends on choosing the right cultivars to the local environmental conditions. However, the effect of environmental factors on cultivar traits is highly complex and often difficult to interpret. Different soybean cultivars frequently display varied responses in terms of yield and other agronomic traits when grown under diverse environmental settings across different locations35–37. This variability can be explained by the spatial and temporal heterogeneity of agricultural environments, which are affected by numerous abiotic factors—including solar radiation, temperature, and precipitation—as well as soil properties. Biotic stresses, such as diseases and pests, alongside agricultural management practices, further contribute to these fluctuating conditions38. These varying environmental factors shape the differences in yield and other quantitative traits among soybean cultivars. This complex relationship is described as the genotype-by-environment interaction, reflecting the diverse ways in which different genotypes respond to environmental influences. Consequently, interpreting experimental data requires careful consideration of this interaction, which remains one of the major challenges in soybean research.

Popovic et al.39 found that on nutrient-rich chernozem soils formed from loess in southeastern Serbia, seed, protein and oil yields of five early-maturing soybean cultivars (group 0) were significantly influenced by rainfall and temperature. The best results were recorded in a year with 60 mm more precipitation and a 1 °C lower average daily temperature compared to the three-year mean. Several researchers40–42 have also emphasized the key role of weather patterns in determining soybean yield. In our study the avarage yield ranged from 27.7 to 34 dt ha-1 while in the study by Banaś et al. (2023)17 varied between 24.94 and 27.72 dt ha-1. Comparable results were reported by Fogelberg and Recknagel (2017)43 who found that early-maturing cultivars in Swedish trials could reach around 25 dt ha-1.Döttinger et al.44 in turn reported average yields between 21.60 and 30.47 dt ha-1 based on trials conducted in Northern Germany. In the study of Panasiewicz et al.24 the analysis revealed that the Amiata cultivar achieved the highest average yield, whereas Erica was identified as the most consistent across conditions. Abelina recorded the lowest GSI value among the tested genotypes. In this study of Panasiewicz et al.24 weather conditions had a notable impact on the yield of the soybean cultivar Annushka grown in central Poland as well as in our study. The greatest yields were recorded in the year with the highest amount of rainfall during the growing period. Conversely, lower yields were observed during the years 2018 to 2020, which experienced unfavorable conditions primarily due to reduced precipitation, particularly in June.

The variation in the content of protein and fat content is primarily determined by genetic traits and their interaction with environmental factors45. In particular, moisture and temperature conditions during the growing season play a key role in shaping this variability46. Assefa et al.33 demonstrated that environmental factors accounted for over 70% of the variation in soybean seed chemical composition. As noted by Szwejkowska47, the protein content in legume seeds is influenced not only by the genetic traits of the specific cultivar but also by environmental conditions during the growing season and agricultural practices, with nitrogen fertilization playing a particularly important role. The protein content observed in this experiment differed from those reported by Michalek48 who indicated that soybean protein content typically falls within the range of 37.9% to 44%. In our experiment the protein content was from 35 to 38%. The analyzed seeds contained between from 228 to 240 g kg-1d.m. These values were on par with or even higher than those found by Michalek et al.42 who reported a range of 14.2% to 15.2%. In Banaś et al.17 for fat content Ambella stood out by having both the highest average and the lowest GSI index, with ES Comandor showing the greatest stability. When it came to protein content, Nessie PZO had the top average value, while Aurelina combined high stability with the lowest GSI index. Based on these findings, Abelina, Ambella and Aurelina are considered the most suitable options for cultivation in this area.

The protein content of soybeans is influenced by complex environmental interactions, particularly those related to temperature and precipitation.49 Improving both protein and oil content in soybean seeds is particularly challenging, mainly due to the inconsistent influence of environmental conditions on these traits and the commonly reported negative correlation between them. To overcome these difficulties, it is necessary to conduct research in multiple environments using a wide range of genotypes. This approach allows for a comprehensive assessment of the impact of genotype and environment on seed composition, while also enabling the identification of stable genotypes and those best suited to specific environmental conditions. Research on the stability of protein and oil content in soybeans remains limited, particularly for studies covering a wide range of genotypes and environmental conditions. It is becoming increasingly important to evaluate multiple soybean genotypes in different environments, including those exposed to varying degrees of abiotic stress, both natural and experimentally induced. Such evaluations can provide valuable information to breeders developing high-yielding, high-quality cultivars with broad adaptability, and can also help farmers select genotypes that maintain optimal performance under both favorable and stressful conditions.50 Studies conducted by Piper and Boot51 and Balešević-Tubić et al.52 have shown that the correlation between protein and oil content in soybeans is influenced by environmental conditions, with significantly weaker correlations observed in warmer climates. In a long-term study, Rotundo et al. 49 analyzed this relationship over eight years in different states and regions of the US, finding that the strength of the correlation varied. They reported correlation coefficients ranging from -0.39 to -0.58 in different years and from -0.41 to -0.61 in different regions. When considering specific region-year combinations, the correlation values showed even greater variability, with some combinations showing no significant relationship between protein and oil concentrations.

The relationship between maturity and protein content in seeds appears to be context-specific and cannot be generalized. It seems to depend on the specific combination of genotypes and weather conditions occurring during the three-year study period.

Current challenge is to quickly identify the threats posed by climate change and adapt plants by breeding new cultivars and implementing changes in agricultural techniques. In the era of global warming, plant production should be based on sustainable development, low-emission cultivation technologies, and genotypes adapted to constantly changing weather conditions.

Conslusion

Cultivar stability was assessed using WAAS. The Petrina cultivar was the most stable in terms of seed yield, fat content and protein content. Surprisingly, the Moravians cultivar was the most stable in regard to protein yield, while the Petrina ranked third. GSI index was used to evaluate cultivars in terms of both the value of their traits and their stability. The Kofu cultivar stood out for its highest trait value and stability in terms of seed yield, while Sultana stood out in terms of seed protein content and Petrina in terms of fat content. The Moravians cultivar was characterized by the highest protein yield and, at the same time, stability of this trait. The WTOP3 index was used to assess the adaptability of a concrete cultivar. The Kofu cultivar had the highest adaptability for seeds yield and protein yield. The Sultana cultivar showed strong adaptability in terms of seed protein content, while the Abelina and Merlin cultivars showed strong adaptability in terms of seed fat content.

Considering that soybeans are regarded in Poland as a crop that can balance the deficit of plant protein in animal feed, the Moravians cultivar appears to be a very good choice in the studied region of the country, given the stability of this cultivar in terms of protein yield (WAAS) and the size of the protein yield and the stability of this trait at the same time (GSI).

This study shows that even in the case of a single country, and even more so in the case of a single region, the right choice of soybean cultivar is crucial to achieving high production results. Multi-year research enables the selection of a genotype suitable for a given environment and are a clear example of the application of science in agricultural practice.
