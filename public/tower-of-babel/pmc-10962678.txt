Investigation of microwave extraction of red cabbage and its neurotherapeutic potential
Authors: Halil Koyu, Serdar Demir, Mehmet Zeki Haznedaroglu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
In the pursuit of bioactive compounds with health benefits from food and medicinal plants, optimization of process to reduce solvent, raw material, energy and time consumption has been a valuable research objective. Sustainable management and efficient use of natural resources are of critical importance for sustainability goals. In this manner, microwave extraction of red cabbage which has been a mass cultivated plant for food purposes, was optimized for maximizing acetylcholinesterase, butyrylcholinesterase, tyrosinase inhibitory activity and bioactive content. Quantitative analysis of total phenol, flavonoid and anthocyanin content were performed using spectrophotometry and UPLC-DAD-ESI-MS/MS. Optimum conditions were determined as 600 W, 100% ethanol, 12 min for butyrylcholinesterase inhibitory and 800 W, 50% ethanol, 4 min for tyrosinase inhibitory activity. Microwave extraction outperformed conventional solvent extracts (hexane, dichloromethane, 2-methyl tetrahydrofuran, ethanol, 70% ethanol and water) in terms of activity and content. The isolated fraction containing major anthocyanins as cyanidin-3-sophoroside-5-glucoside and cyanidin-3-(sinapoyl)-sophoroside-5-glucoside was identified as the responsible portion for tyrosinase inhibitory activity. The dominant anthocyanins rich fraction of the optimum microwave extract can be considered for the production of antityrosinase products, while the identification of inhibitor metabolites for acetylcholinesterase and butyrylcholinesterase can be targeted for future studies considering red cabbage as a valuable source.

1. Introduction

Research into the processing of natural sources is of increasing interest as sustainability goals prioritise sustainable management and efficient use. In the search for plant bioactives, globally grown sources, which possess the feasibility of being easy to access and store, can be a primary candidate for these goals.

Red cabbage, Brassica oleracea L. var. capitata f. rubra (Brassicaceae), which originated from the Mediterranean region, has been a cultivated form worldwide, mainly for food purposes [1]. Rich composition of red cabbage could be benefited also for medicinal purposes other than its nutritional value. Among metabolites, phenolic compounds, particularly anthocyanins, have been valuable for their broad-spectrum activity potential [2]. The high production volumes are also favourable for sustainability goals, with the latest total cabbage production reported as 106.80 million tonnes in 151 countries over the world [3].

Acetylcholinesterase, butyrylcholinesterase and tyrosinase inhibitory activity which were linked to Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s diseases, hyperpigmentation and melanoma [4–6] have been an important research area for phenolic rich plants as phenolic compounds have been determined with remarkable enzyme inhibitory activity [5]. With the active metabolites determined and bioactivity potential identified [7,8], red cabbage could be a valuable source for research of enzyme inhibition. B. oleracea var. capitata seeds were studied for antioxidant activity where effect of solvent polarity and extraction time was investigated. A significant linear negative effect of extraction time was observed on iron chelating activity and hydroxyl radical scavenging capacity. Polarity dependent increase in total antioxidant activity was interpreted as the extraction of phytochemicals with stronger antioxidant activity in polar solvents [9]. B. oleracea var. capitata f. rubra was assessed for total phenol, flavonoid, anthocyanin content and antioxidant capacity. Water and 70% methanol extracts were compared for bioactive content and activity. Aqueous extract was found to provide higher total phenol and flavonoid contents, resulting in stronger antioxidant activity. Aqueous extract was found to significantly (p < 0.001) ameliorate cholesterol, triglyceride and lipoprotein alteration levels in hyperlipidemic rats without toxicity [10].

As well as discovering suitable sources of bioactive constituents, it is critical to identify the optimum conditions for process such as energy, solvent, raw material and time. For the extraction of herbal sources, microwave systems have been highlighted as advanced technologies due to higher active constituent yields with reduced process time, solvent and energy consumption, while thermolabile constituents can be better protected. The main parameters affecting microwave extraction were pointed out as microwave power, extraction time, solvent type and operating temperature [11]. While the increase of bioactive content by optimizing the extraction parameters could contribute to higher activity, the extent of extractive capability and the activity potential of extracts from different conditions must be comparatively revealed.

In this study we aimed to investigate enzyme inhibitory potential of red cabbage extracts, enlighten content–activity relationship and optimize microwave extraction parameters. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study for the optimization of microwave extraction of red cabbage leaves for maximizing cholinesterase and tyrosinase inhibitory activity.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Plant material

Fresh leaves of red cabbage (B. oleracea var. capitata f. rubra) were collected from an agricultural production site in Bayindir, Izmir, Turkey. After transport to the laboratory, the leaves were washed with distilled water and cleaned from any residual dirt. The outermost leaves were discarded and each leaf was separated for drying. The leaves were dried in controlled airflow drying cabinet. All the dried leaves were then placed in airtight storage box and stored at −24 °C until the experiments, and were ground prior to being used in the extraction process.

2.2. Chemicals

Gallic acid, quercetin, Folin-Ciocalteu reagent, kojic acid, tyrosinase, acetylcholinesterase (Ache), butyrlcholinesterase (Buche), acetylthiocholine iodide (ATCI), butyrylthiocholine iodide (BUTCI) were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (Steinheim, Germany). Galantamine, levodopa, DTNB were purchased from Alfa Aesar (Haverhill, MA, USA). DMSO, trifluoroacetic acid, aluminum chloride, potassium phosphate dibasic, potassium phosphate monobasic, potassium acetate were purchased from Carlo Erba (Emmendingen, Germany). Ethanol, sodium carbonate, sodium acetate, sodium phosphate monobasic, sodium phosphate dibasic, potassium chloride were purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Cyanidin-3-glucoside was purchased from Extrasynthese (Geney, France). All of the chemicals were of analytical grade and water was obtained with Stakpure Omnia Type I Ultrapure Water Systems (Niederahr, Germany).

2.3. Conventional solvent extractions

Dried leaves were ground and homogenized in a blender (Isolab blender, Eschau, Germany) before extraction. 10 g of ground leaves were mixed with 200 mL of extraction solvents in glass flasks. Extraction solvents were chosen to cover a wide range of polarity, as dichloromethane, 2-methyltetrahydrofuran (2-Me THF), ethanol (0.1% TFA), 70% ethanol (0.1% TFA) and water (0.1% TFA). The addition of 0.1% TFA was intended to keep the pH below 3 for stable extraction of anthocyanins. The extractions were carried out at room temperature (25 °C) in the dark for 1 h per replicate using an ultrasonic bath (Isolab 13lt, Eschau, Germany). After separation of the filtrates, the residues were further extracted for two times. All filtrates were combined and evaporated at 40 °C using a rotary evaporator (Buchi R100, Flawil, Switzerland) [12]. Complete dryness of the extracts was achieved using a vacuum concentrator system (Thermo Scientific Speedvac, Waltham, MA, USA) and a lyophiliser (Labconco Freezone, Kansas City, MO, USA). Extracts were stored at −24 °C during experiments.

2.4. Microwave extraction

A closed vessel microwave digestion system was used for microwave extractions (Sineo MDS-8G, Shanghai, China). In each microwave vessel (5 vessels for each run) 1 g of ground dried leaves were mixed with 20 mL of ethanol: water (0.1% TFA) and extracted according to the experimental design. Extraction parameters as independent variables were microwave power (400, 600, 800 Watts (W)), ethanol concentration (0, 50, 100% concentration (conc.)) and extraction time (4, 8, 12 min (min)). Optimization study was carried out with selected parameters set to Box-Behnken Design (Design Expert Software 7.0). Microwave extractions were conducted at room temperature (25 °C) with a solid: solvent ratio of 1:20. After the extraction process, the filtrates were separated and evaporated at 40 °C with rotary evaporator (Buchi R100, Flawil, Switzerland). Complete dryness of the extracts was achieved using a vacuum concentrator system (Thermo Scientific Speedvac, Waltham, MA, USA) and lyophiliser (Labconco Freezone, Kansas City, MO, USA). The extracts were stored at −24 °C during the experiments.

2.5. Statistical design

As a mathematical and statistical technique, Response Surface Methodology (RSM) have been used to model and analyze problems. Selecting independent variables with an appropriate experimental design, conducting experiments according to the selected design, evaluating the data obtained using mathematical-statistical models, and analyzing the fitness of the model to find the optimal conditions are defined as the process steps for RSM [13]. In this study, a three-level model was used with three independent variables as microwave power, ethanol concentration and extraction time, while keeping temperature and solid: solvent ratio constant. Three center points in the experimental design (Design Expert Software 7.0) were used to measure the stability of the process and the intrinsic variability.

2.6. Total phenol content

A 96 well microplate method was developed for the determination of total phenol content based on the Folin-Ciocalteu method [14]. In each well, 100 μL of distilled water was mixed with 20 μL of sample and 10 μL of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent. After shaking at 200 rpm (Jeio Tech Vortex, Daejeon, Republic of Korea), the microplate was kept in dark for 4 min. Then 100 μL of saturated sodium carbonate solution was added and kept in dark for 2 h at room temperature. Absorbance was measured at 760 nm against blank (70% ethanol) with microplate reader (BMG Labtech Clariostar, Ortenberg, Germany). The calibration curve in the range of 1–250 μg/mL with 7 data points was established using gallic acid as the reference standard. All analyses were performed in triplicate. The results were expressed as mg gallic acid equivalent (GAE)/g extract.

2.7. Total flavonoid content

Total flavonoid content was determined using a developed 96 well microplate method based on the aluminum chloride method [15]. In each well, 70 μL ethanol, 130 μL distilled water, 10 μL 10% aluminum chloride and 10 μL 1 M potassium acetate were mixed with 25 μL of sample. After shaking at 200 rpm, the microplate was kept in dark for 40 min. Absorbance was measured at 415 nm against blank (70% ethanol) with microplate reader. The calibration curve in the range of 1–250 μg/mL with 7 data points was established using quercetin as the reference standard. All analysis were performed in triplicate. The results were expressed as mg quercetin equivalent (QE)/g extract.

2.8. Total anthocyanin content

Total anthocyanin content was determined using a developed 96 well microplate method based on the pH change method [16,17]. 2.9. Fractionation of the extracts

The microwave extracts identified as having the highest enzyme inhibitory activity and content (i.e. microwave Run 3, 7, 13 extracts) were fractionated to determine the bioactive fraction responsible for the activity. Fractionation was performed using a syringe cartridge loaded with reversed-phase (C18) silica gel (Merck) and conditioned with distilled water (100 mL). The elution solvents were acidified with TFA (0.1%) to ensure the stability of the anthocyanins. Elution was started with water:ethanol (100:0) and completed with 0:100, decreasing by 25% between mobile phase adjustments. The fractions were collected in test tubes and monitored by thin layer chromatography (TLC). TLC studies were performed on silica gel precoated aluminium plates (Merck) and developed with 64:50:10 (chloroform: methanol:water) and 100:11:11:27 (ethyl acetate: formic acid:glacial acetic acid:water) solvent systems. For 64:50:10 mobile phase, vanillin-sulphuric acid reagent was applied followed by heating. For the 100:11:11:27 system, NP-PEG (natural products/polyethylene glycol) reagent was used and TLC plates were examined under UV366 light [19].

2.10. LC-DAD, MS/MS analysis of dominant anthocyanin content

The qualitative and quantitative determination of anthocyanins in the extracts and fractions were performed by UPLC-DAD-ESI-MS/MS system. Our previously developed method was slightly modified for the analysis [20]. The UPLC system (Thermo Scientific Accela 1250) consisted of diode array and mass detectors (Thermo Scientific TSQ Quantum Access Maxx triple quadrupole). The column size was 150 × 2.1 mm, 3 μm (Thermo Syncronis C18) with a guard column of 10 × 2.1 mm, 5 μm particle size (Thermo Syncronis C18). The flow rate was set to 500 μL/min, the injection volume to 5 μL, the column oven to 35 °C and the wavelength to 515 nm. All samples were filtered through 0.45 μm PTFE filters (Sartorius) prior to injection. The gradient system for the mobile phase was as follows: at 0.1 min 90A:10B; at 1 min 90A:10B; at 7 min 70A:30B; at 9 min 90A:10B and at 10 min 90A:10B, where the mobile phase A is water:formic acid (90:10) and B is water:formic acid:acetonitrile (40:10:50). Mass spectra were acquired in selected reaction monitoring (SRM) mode and electrospray ionization (ESI) was performed in positive ion mode [21]. The optimized conditions for ionization were 380 °C for capillary temperature, 200 °C for evaporator temperature, 3000 V for spray voltage, 60 arbitrary units for sheath gas flow rate and 20 arbitrary units for auxiliary gas flow rate. The calibration curve was constructed using cyanidin-3-glucoside as the reference standard in the range of 0.5–15 μg/mL with five concentration points (y = 154387x–4296, R2: 0.9998). The anthocyanin content was determined in triplicate and results were expressed as mg cyanidin-3-glucoside equivalent/g extract by calculating the peak areas of the two dominant anthocyanins as cyanidin derivatives, which had been applied for the quantification of anthocyanins [1,2,12,19]. The reliability of anthocyanin content analyses was ensured with the quantitative determination by Diode Array Detector and qualitative confirmation by MS detector.

2.11. Cholinesterase inhibitory activity

A 96-well microplate assay was developed and optimized for the determination of cholinesterase inhibitory activity using galantamine as reference drug, based on Ellman’s method [22,23]. ATCI and BUTCI were used as substrates for the determination of Ache and Buche inhibitory activity, respectively. 30 μL of extracts (dissolved in DMSO:water 1:9) were mixed with 150 μL 0.1 M pH 8 sodium phosphate buffer, 25 μL 0.01 M DTNB (dissolved in buffer) and 25 μL of Ache or Buche enzymes (0.2 Unit/mL and 0.1 Unit/mL in buffer containing 1 mg/mL bovine serum albumin) and pre-incubated for 5 min on 200 rpm shaker at 25 °C in the dark. Finally, 25 μL of substrate as ATCI or BUTCI (3 mM in buffer) was added to initiate the reaction. Kinetic readings with 30 s intervals were taken at 412 nm during 10 min at 25 °C. 3. Results and discussion

3.1. Validation tests

Extraction without plant material (blank extraction), three parallel extractions (extraction repeatability) and additional extraction of discarded plant material (determination of left content in discarded part after extraction) were performed for extraction validation. No content or peak was determined in analysis of blank extraction. The relative standard deviation (RSD) values for extraction repeatability were determined as 4.03%, 4.86% and 4.89% for total phenol, flavonoid and anthocyanin content, respectively. The amount of residual content in the discarded plant material after extraction compared to the extract was quantified as 2.76%, 0.31% and 0.64% for total phenol, flavonoid and anthocyanin content, respectively.

Linearity, repeatability, intra-day stability and inter-day stability were performed for UPLC analysis validation. Linearity (0.5–15 μg/mL, R2: 0.9998, LOD: 0.54 μg/mL, LOQ: 1.65 μg/mL) was determined using the calibration curve constructed with the anthocyanin reference cyanidin-3-glucoside at five concentration points in triplicate experiments. Repeatability (RSD: 1.37%) was determined by analyzing the same extract sample ten times in a row. Intra-day stability (RSD: 0.67%) was determined by analyzing the same extract at the beginning, middle and end of the analysis sequence within the same day. Inter-day stability (RSD: 2.21%) was determined by analyzing the same extract on three consecutive days.

3.2. Enzyme inhibitory activity protocols

Enzyme inhibitory activity protocols were constructed and optimized by study of inhibition kinetic models. As reported in the literature, IC50 values for enzymeinhibitory activitymay not comparable due to differences in assay conditions such as substrate concentration, incubation time–temperature and enzyme concentration [5]. For this reason, inhibition kinetic studies with reference drugs, galantamine for cholinesterase and kojic acid for tyrosinase (also previously studied with similar range in Ref. [20], were performed to optimize and validate enzyme inhibition assay parameters with inhibition kinetic models of well-known reference drugs. Michaelis–Menten, Lineweaver–Burk and Eadie–Hofstee plots were presented as Supplementary Figures. Inhibition kinetics were studied for tyrosinase using l-dopa (100–1500 μM) and kojic acid (0–25 μM), and for acetylcholinesterase using ATCI (15,625-250 μM) and galantamin (0–1,75 μM) as single substrate and single inhibitor, respectively. Findings indicated competitive type inhibition for galantamine and mixed type inhibition for kojic acid which correlated with literature data [5,24].

3.4. Effects of extraction parameters on bioactive content

Optimization model was found as significant for the first response as total phenol content ( p-value: 0,0080). Extraction parameters as microwave power ( p-value: 0.0221) and extraction time ( p-value: 0.0023) were defined as significant whereas ethanol concentration was not ( p-value: 0.1076). Highest total phenol content (38.91 mg/g GAE) was determined at 800 Watt, 50% ethanol, 12 min conditions. An extraction time of 12 min seemed as necessary for the total phenol content to reach 38 mg/g GAE. At 800 W microwave power, increasing both extraction time and ethanol conc. up to 12 min and 100% respectively had positive effect on total phenol content and provided highest results (Fig. 1A). Similarly, a study on B. oleracea var. capitata had reported increased amount of total phenol content with prolonged extraction time [25]. Increasing the microwave power from 600 W (38.02 mg/g GAE) to 800 W (38.91 mg/g GAE) provided the identical content with a 50% reduction in ethanol consumption among microwave extracts. As one of the main objectives of the optimization study to reduce organic solvent consumption, microwave power regulation was found to be highly effective. Without the addition of ethanol to solvent mixture, highest value was found as 32.01 mg/g GAE at 600 W and 12 min conditions. Among ultrasonic bath extracts, highest values were obtained by 70% ethanol (0.1% TFA) with 35.10 mg/g GAE. Previous reports had also indicated 80% ethanol usage with longer time (60 min) for the extraction of highest amount of phenolic acids from another variety as B. oleracea var. sabellica [26]. Microwave system have proven to be able to save significant amounts of time required for the extraction process.

For the 2nd response as total flavonoid content, optimization model was found as significant ( p-value: 0,0142). Extraction parameters as microwave power ( p-value: 0.0286), ethanol conc. ( p-value: 0.0226) and extraction time ( p-value: 0.0577) were defined as significant parameters. Highest total flavonoid content (3.41 mg/g QE) was determined at 800 W, 50% ethanol, 4 min conditions. Extraction time was inversely proportional with total flavonoid content where shortening to 4 min provided higher content at 800 W. Ethanol conc. at 50% was determined as optimum with experiments (calculated highest content for ethanol conc. with 50–75% range) while higher and lower composition as 100% and 0% caused reduction in total flavonoid amounts (Fig. 1B). Among ultrasonic bath extracts, highest values were obtained by 2-Me THF with 36.47 mg/g QE while ethanol and 70% ethanol extracts were determined with 1.94 and 4.69 mg/g QE, respectively (Table 1). Introduced as a biorenewable, greener and environmentally friendly alternative solvent, enhanced selective extraction capability of 2-Me THF over organic solvents for flavonoids was remarkable.

As the 3rd and 4th responses, anthocyanin content in extracts were determined by spectrophotometry and UPLC-DAD-ESI-MS/MS as total anthocyanin content and dominant anthocyanin content, respectively. Cyanidin-3-glucoside was used as the reference standard for the quantification of anthocyanins. The anthocyanin content of extracts and isolated fractions were quantified with the diode array detector analysis. Identification of peaks were also confirmed with mass spectra data obtained with selected reaction monitoring by mass detector analysis. Elution order and detected mass fragmentation pattern of anthocyanin peaks were found to be consistent with previous reports [1,2,27,28]. DAD and MS chromatograms are presented with Fig. 2 where major anthocyanins as cyanidin-3-sophoroside-5-glucoside and cyanidin-3-(sinapoyl)-sophoroside-5-glucoside were quantified as dominant anthocyanin content as identical results were also reported previously [2,28]. Highest total anthocyanin content (11.18 mg/g Cya3GluE) was determined at 600 W, 50% ethanol, 8 min conditions. 8 min and 600 W was detected as the optimum time and microwave power where higher and lower values for both caused a reduction in total anthocyanin content (Fig. 1C). The upward trend with increasing microwave power at 50% ethanol concentration suggested that new studies above 800 W might further improve the total anthocyanin content (Fig. 1C). Similar to previous reports, an ethanol conc. of 50% (optimally 70% for conventional solvent system) was found to be necessary for the extraction of anthocyanins [12]. On the other hand, highest amount of two dominant anthocyanins (6.98 mg/g Cya3GluE) were detected in 800 W, 50% ethanol, 4 min extract. Regarding major anthocyanins, denoted as dominant anthocyanin content, higher microwave power of 800 W with a reduced extraction time of 4 min was determined as optimum (Fig. 1D). In the same way as for the total flavonoid content, limiting process time to 4 min could be considered as the main reason for a more selective extraction of major anthocyanins. As the trend was observed through the optimization graphs (Fig. 2), an additional extraction at 2 min with 800 W and 50% ethanol conc. was conducted to investigate if further process time decrease could improve anthocyanin content. The results of experiments revealed that 4 min was the optimum point while 2 min extraction was insufficient and resulted in a decrease of total anthocyanin content to 6.60 mg/g Cya3GluE.

Among ultrasonic bath extracts, highest TAC was obtained with 70% ethanol extracts (8.82 mg/g Cya3GluE) which has also been commonly presented with other studies [12]. As the required time and consumed solvent amounts compared, microwave assisted extraction (12 min) had remarkable advantage over conventional solvent extraction (3 h) both for total phenol and anthocyanin contents, as also stated in literature [29], while consuming 3 times less solvent. For total flavonoid content, ultrasonic bath extract of 2-Me THF had superiority over other solvents and microwave assisted extracts. Regulation of microwave energy was found as enabling the selective extraction for targeted bioactive groups with the modification of ethanol conc. and extraction time.

Usage of polar solvents in microwave assisted extraction was shown as having superior capability due to raised temperature [30] as a result of the increased microwave irradiation absorption [31], which had been in favor of extraction of phenolic compounds [32,33]. In concordance with these findings, our results also revealed that elevating microwave power to 800 W had enhancing effect both for total phenol, flavonoid and anthocyanin contents where 0% ethanol conditions still achieved to extract remarkable amounts even in case of anthocyanins (800 W, 8 min; 8.12 mg/g Cya3GlueE). Additionally, presence of water in solvent mixture had a mechanically supportive effect on the swelling of herbal substance and allowing better diffusion of extraction solvent which was clearly observed during experiments where 0% ethanol containing extractions led to residues in an intact form even after the 12 min extraction. The increase in total flavonoid content with a shorter extraction time (i.e. 4 min) can be attributed to the limited duration of the process, where also previously prolonged extraction had been noted with reduced content due to non-selective extraction [34–36]. Contrary to previous reports on the optimisation of microwave-assisted extraction of anthocyanins at 85% ethanol and 12 min [37], the demonstrated superiority of 50% ethanol and 4 min extracts in reducing solvent consumption and process time could be attributed to the increase in microwave power to 800 W. Therefore, 600 W and 800 W values are also be useful for future research while modifying other parameters. Opposite to our findings as the additional extraction at 2 min with 800 W and 50% ethanol conc. possessed lesser anthocyanin content compared to 4 min, previous studies at 2 min with 425 W extraction had been reported as optimum [38]. However, these results could be limited to the effect of 400 W where also in our design 400 W, 50% ethanol, 4 min extraction (9.61 mg/g Cya3GluE) provided a higher anthocyanin content compared to 12 min extraction (400 W, 50% ethanol, 12 min; 8.86 mg/g Cya3GluE). For total anthocyanin and dominant anthocyanin content, ultrasonic bath extract of 70% ethanol had provided 8.82 mg/g and 5.50 mg/g Cya3GluE, respectively. Compared to conventional solvent extraction, optimized microwave assisted extraction (11.18 mg/g Cya3GluE) provided 27% more anthocyanin content while preserving remarkable process time (12 min vs 3 h) with 3 times less solvent. Among the conventional solvent extracts, the highest total phenol (35.10 mg GAE/g), total anthocyanin (8.82 mg Cya3GluE/g) and dominant anthocyanin (5.50 mg Cya3GluE/g) contents were determined in the 70% ethanol extract. Ethanol and water extracts had lower total phenol and anthocyanin contents, whereas the lowest amounts were determined in hexane, dichloromethane and 2-MeTHF extracts (Table 1). Compared to conventional solvent extracts, optimum microwave extracts exceeded all quantified bioactive contents with 38.91 mg GAE/g, 11.18 mg Cya3GluE/g and 6.98 mg Cya3GluE/g, respectively. The only exception was total flavonoid content, as the highest amount (36.47 mg QE/g) was extracted by 2-MeTHF, while microwave extracts were found to be limited to 3.41 mg QE/g. In parallel with the results of content analysis, Buche and tyrosinase inhibitory activity of the optimum microwave extracts were found to be superior to the conventional solvent extracts. The results of enzyme inhibitory activity study were discussed in the following section.

3.5. Cholinesterase and tyrosinase inhibitory activity

Ache, Buche and tyrosinase inhibitory activities which were linked to Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s diseases, hyperpigmentation and melanoma [4–6] have been an important research area for phenolic rich plants. Therefore, total phenol, flavonoid and anthocyanin content of the extracts and isolated fractions were quantified along with LC-MS/MS analysis of anthocyanins to investigate the content–activity relationship. As the 5th response as Ache inhibitory activity, optimization model was found as significant ( p-value: 0,0058). Extraction parameters as microwave power ( p-value: 0.0243), and extraction time ( p-value: 0.0015) were defined as significant parameters while ethanol conc. ( p-value: 0.4887) was not. The highest activity (40.09% inhibition at 6.25 mg/mL conc.) was observed for 600 W, 50% ethanol, 8 min extract which was identified with the highest amount of total anthocyanin content (11.18 mg/g Cya3GluE), while dominant anthocyanins (5.02 mg/g Cya3GluE) constituted a smaller portion compared to other extracts. Solubility was the limiting factor in calculating IC50 values of the total extracts as 6.25 mg/mL conc. was the upper limit. Among the five sub-fractions obtained with RP-silica gel chromatography from the most active extract (Run 13 extract) for Ache inhibition, 75:25 water:ethanol fraction (fraction 2) had superiority for all groups as total phenol, flavonoid and anthocyanin contents as 276.31 mg/g GAE, 27.92 mg/g KE and 107.21 mg/g Cya3GluE, respectively. However, the activity results for three fractions (fraction 2, 3, 4; 38%, 37% and 43% inhibition at 625 μg/mL) were found to be close while quantified bioactive content was singularly over concentrated in fraction 2 (75:25 water:ethanol fraction). Additionally, another extraction was performed with 70:29:0.1 ethanol: water:TFA at optimized conditions as 600 W and 8 min to further investigate the anthocyanin content. Higher anthocyanin content was obtained with 70% ethanol which was also accompanied by stronger activity (52% inhibition at 6.25 mg/mL conc). According to these results, on the basis of the microwave extracts, the extract with the highest total anthocyanin content had the strongest acetylcholinesterase inhibitory activity, although the bioactive constituents concentrated fraction (i.e. fraction 2) was not more active compared to other fractions. Also, LC-MS/MS analysis showed that leading extract with dominant anthocyanin content (Run7 extract; 6.98 mg/g Cya3GluE; 31% inhibition at 6.25 mg/mL) possessed weaker activity despite of its higher content (Run13 extract; 5.02 mg/g Cya3GluE with 40% inhibition at 6.25 mg/mL). As a result, none of the quantified bioactive groups or dominant anthocyanins could be ruled out as being solely responsible for acetylcholinesterase inhibitory activity of red cabbage extracts. Further isolation is planned to shed light on the different active groups other than the quantified ones in future studies. The most active conventional solvent extract was obtained with ethanol which gave identical results to the microwave extract with highest activity as 40% inhibition at 6.25 mg/mL conc. Although not leading in any of the identified active groups, the ethanol extract was found to be superior to other organic solvents among conventional extracts (Table 1). The results for conventional extracts also confirmed the presence and contribution to activity of non quantified metabolites.

As the 6th response as Buche inhibitory activity, optimization model was found as significant (p-value: 0,0001). Extraction parameters as microwave power (p-value: 0.0332), and ethanol conc. (p-value: 0.0001) were defined as significant parameters while extraction time was found as insignificant (p-value: 0.7358). The highest activity (72% inhibition at 1.25 mg/mL conc.) was observed for 600 W, 100% ethanol, 12 min extract (IC50: 0.48 mg/mL) which was not identified as leading for any of the quantified bioactive groups, while having a remarkable total phenol content of 38.02 mg/g GAE and relatively lower anthocyanin content (5.32 mg/g Cya3GluE). Among the five sub-fractions of this extract obtained by RP-silica gel chromatography, not a single one was outstanding in terms of Buche inhibitory activity. Three fractions (fraction 3, 4, 5 as 50:50, 25:75, 0:100 water:ethanol, respectively) both possessed similar activity (IC50: 20.62 μg/mL, 20.26 μg/mL and 23.32 μg/mL, respectively). However, quantified bioactive content was found as concentrated in fraction 2 (75:25 water:ethanol fraction) for total phenol (47.14 mg/g GAE) and anthocyanin content (10.52 mg/g Cya3GluE). These results were consistent with the results of acetylcholinesterase inhibitory activity where for both, no direct content–activity relationship was observed with the analyzed metabolites. The activity providing bioactives that were eluted with 50% ethanol through RP-silica gel isolation of the microwave extract (i.e. Run 3) could be a new target for identification in future studies. For the conventional extracts, highest activity was obtained with 70% ethanol (IC50: 2.44 mg/mL). In contrast to the microwave extraction, the 70% ethanol extract from the ultrasonic bath was the leading conventional extract for all quantified groups as total phenol (35.10 mg/g GAE), flavonoid (4.69 mg/g QE) and anthocyanin (8.82 mg/g Cya3GluE) contents (Table 1). This situation may also indicate the role of another nondetermined metabolite on butyrylcholinesterase inhibitory activity which was able to be selectively extracted by the microwave system. The effect of drastically reduced extraction time in addition to microwave energy could be speculated as the main factors for this considerably selective extraction.

As the 7th, and last response as tyrosinase inhibitory activity, optimization model was found as significant (p-value: 0.0104). Ethanol conc. was determined as significant (p-value: 0.0022) while microwave power (p-value: 0.3468) and extraction time (p-value: 0.2447) were insignificant parameters. Increasing microwave power up to 800 W enabled lower ethanol consumption (50%) and shorter extraction time (4 min) for obtaining the extract with highest activity (Fig. 2). Optimization of parameters for highest activity could be interpreted as providing more selective extraction. The highest activity (71% inhibition at 6.25 mg/mL conc.) was obtained with 800 W, 50% ethanol, 4 min extract (IC50: 5.03 mg/mL) which was identified as having highest total flavonoid (3.41 mg/g QE) and dominant anthocyanin (6.98 mg/g Cya3GluE) contents (Table 2). Among the five sub-fractions of this extract, fraction 2 (75:25 water:ethanol) was outstanding in terms of both total phenol (329.50 mg/g GAE), flavonoid (31.82 mg/g QE), anthocyanin (118.04 mg/g Cya3GluE), dominant anthocyanin (85.66 mg/g Cya3GluE) contents, and tyrosinase inhibitory activity (IC50: 0.57 mg/mL). The isolated fraction with the most potent tyrosinase inhibitor activity was determined as having maximum amounts for all quantified bioactive groups. Similar to our findings, the role of phenolic and flavonoid content in addition to anthocyanins for tyrosinase inhibitory activity was also presented [39–41]. Additionally, cyanidin-3-(sinapoyl)-(sinapoyl)-sophoroside-5-glucoside was identified as showing strongest antioxidant activity compared to other red cabbage anthocyanins [2].

Compared to reference standard drugs as galantamin (IC50: 0.00021 mg/mL and 0.0025 mg/mL for Ache and Buche inhibition) and kojic acid (IC50: 0.005 mg/mL for tyrosinase inhibition), crude extracts of conventional and microwave systems possessed weaker activity. Identical results were obtained for Ache inhibitory activity for conventional and microwave extracts (both 40% inhibition at 6.25 mg/mL conc.). Solubility was the limiting factor for crude extracts in Ache inhibitory activity studies as 6.25 mg/mL was the highest soluble conc. Optimum microwave extracts showed significant superiority against conventional extracts for Buche (4.67× more potent) and tyrosinase inhibitory activity (1.24× more potent). No direct content–activity relationship was observed with the analyzed bioactive metabolites for Ache and Buche inhibitory activities. Highest tyrosinase inhibitory activity (IC50: 0.57 mg/mL) was determined with 75:25 water:ethanol fraction of the optimum microwave extract (Run 7) which was quantified with highest total phenol (329.50 mg/g GAE), flavonoid (31.82 mg/g QE), total anthocyanin (118.04 mg/g Cya3GluE) and dominant anthocyanin contents (85.66 mg/g Cya3GluE). Cyanidin-3-sophoroside-5-glucoside and cyanidin-3-(sinapoyl)-sophoroside-5-glucoside were determined as the dominant anthocyanins present in microwave extracts and isolated fractions with highest activity.

4. Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, this study was the first report on optimization of microwave extraction of red cabbage and investigation of Ache, Buche and tyrosinase inhibitory activities. The studied process parameters were microwave power, ethanol concentration and extraction time. The significant parameters revealed by optimization study were microwave power for all enzyme inhibitory activities while extraction time for Ache and ethanol conc. for Buche inhibitory activity. Except total phenol content, microwave power and extraction time were determined as significant parameters for all quantified bioactive groups. Red cabbage has been identified as an important source for plant metabolites with bioactive potential, where global mass cultivation, long shelf-life and year-round accessibility are important advantages over various anthocyanin-rich plants. Microwave extraction was identified as an improved tool for sustainable management and efficient use of red cabbage in the search for bioactive content with health benefits by reducing solvent, raw material, energy and time consumption. The dominant anthocyanins rich fraction of the optimum microwave extract can be considered for the production of antityrosinase products, while the identification of inhibitor metabolites for Ache and Buche can be targeted for future studies as red cabbage can be considered as a feasible and valuable source.
