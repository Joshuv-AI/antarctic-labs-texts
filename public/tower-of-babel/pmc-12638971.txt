Effectiveness of an information-motivation-behavioural skills model-based education kit (PREM-Kit) on human immunodeficiency virus knowledge and attitude among Malaysian late adolescents
Authors: Wan Nur Syamimi Wan Mohamad Darani, Aimi Nadira Mat Ruzlin, Zahir Izuan Azhar, Xin Wee Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The increasing incidence of Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) among Malaysian late adolescents, coupled with insufficient HIV knowledge, necessitates immediate attention to HIV prevention via education. This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of a new Information-Motivation-Behavioural Skills (IMB) model-based education kit (PREM-Kit) in improving knowledge and attitudes related to HIV among Malaysian late adolescents. A 16-week quasi-experimental trial was conducted at two campuses of a local public university, involving 187 participants. The intervention group received PREM-Kit’s intervention in addition to the curriculum-mandated basic science and biology module, whereas the control group received only the curriculum-mandated basic science and biology module. Repeated measure analysis of variance (RMANOVA) and covariance (RMANCOVA) were used to examine the effectiveness of PREM-Kit’s intervention on knowledge and attitudes between groups at four time points (t0-t3). The intervention group exhibited a significant increase in the knowledge score (mean difference = 2.66; 95% CI = 2.22,3.10; p < 0.001) and attitude score (mean difference = 2.74; 95% CI = 1.90,3.59; p < 0.001), which was sustained throughout the 16-week study period. In conclusion, PREM-Kit was effective in improving late adolescents’ knowledge and attitudes related to HIV prevention. It has the potential to be a beneficial package for educating and empowering late adolescents on HIV prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25215-w.

Introduction

Significant advancements have been achieved in decreasing the prevalence of HIV; however, 39.9 million people were still living with HIV, with 1.3 million individuals becoming newly infected in 20231. Of these, young people between the ages of 15 to 24 account for 28% of new HIV infections, of whom nearly 40% of them were adolescents aged 15 to 192. In comparison to global data, Malaysia has reported a lower incidence of HIV among adolescents3. However, the trend of HIV infection among adolescents continues to rise over the past decade4. The latest adolescent health survey conducted in 2021 among individuals aged 13 to 17 revealed that the HIV incidence was 2.4 per 100,000 adolescents4. According to the same survey, 5.6% of adolescents were reported to be sexually active4. Among the sexually active adolescents, 88% were not using condoms, whereas 11% indicated having multiple sexual partners5.Furthermore, reports indicate that merely 1.3% of Malaysian adolescents possess adequate knowledge regarding HIV6, while only 10% of late adolescents demonstrate a correct understanding of preventive methods for sexual transmission of HIV7.

Controlling the HIV epidemic requires the general population to have accurate information about HIV prevention and transmission8. Providing HIV and sexual education at an early stage is essential; however, it is crucial to deliver age-appropriate information to late adolescents continuously. This approach ensures that they remain informed about vital aspects of HIV and sexual education as they mature9. To encourage positive behaviour change, solid knowledge and favourable attitudes towards HIV prevention are essential, necessitating education grounded in health behaviour theory10–12.

Literature has demonstrated that an education incorporating theory-based content increases adolescents’ engagement during learning and results in favourable behavioural outcomes11,13–15. Besides, incorporating computer technology and audiovisual resources during teaching improve understanding for complex health behaviour16. Therefore, a new HIV education kit, namely PREM-Kit (Kit Pencegahan HIV di Kalangan Remaja—a Malay language acronym) was developed by applying the Information-Motivation-Behavioural Skill (IMB) model, an extensively applied behavioural theory that has demonstrated efficacy in HIV education12. The constructs of IMB model—information, motivation, and behavioral skills were used to map the PREM-Kit content into three modules, (i) Module 1: HIV facts and information, (ii) Module 2: Motivation for HIV prevention, and (iii) Module 3: Behaviour skills for HIV prevention. Based on literature reviews and discussion among a panel of multidisciplinary experts, module 1 of PREM-Kit describes HIV facts and information, Module 2 explains motivation for HIV prevention based on the individual’s attitudes and social factor regarding specific HIV preventive behaviours11,17, and Module 3 outlines practices and behaviour skills for HIV prevention. This kit was in the form of a flip-chart and three animated videos; all of which had been evaluated and demonstrated relevancy and good content validity with CVI scores of 1.0 and median scores of over 80% for understandability and actionability18. This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of the newly developed and validated PREM-Kit in improving knowledge and attitudes related to HIV among Malaysian late adolescents.

Methods

Trial design and setting

This intervention trial was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov on 21/11/2022 (Identifier number: NCT05623800).

The present study utilized a quasi-experimental design to evaluate the PREM-Kit. This study design was chosen due to geographical feasibility, where the intervention and control groups were drawn from two different campuses, with the intervention campus selected based on its proximity and accessibility to the researcher to facilitate regular monitoring, direct delivery of the educational sessions, and ensure fidelity of the intervention19,20. This trial was conducted in two campuses of a local public university, which are located 223 km apart. This intervention and data collection process lasted for 16 weeks, from 31st March 2023 until 31st August 2023.

Study population

The target population was late adolescents aged 18–19 years. The study participants were recruited from students taking the Health Science and Applied Science Diploma courses. They were chosen for the availability of the science module in the courses, wherein the HIV lecture was mandated in the curriculum (i.e. basic science and biology module) for standardization to minimize the selection bias between the intervention and control groups.

The inclusion criteria were students who attended the Health Science and Applied Science Diploma courses in 2023, late adolescents aged 18–19 years, and Malaysian citizens. Those excluded from the study were the students who refused to participate, those who were absent on the first session of the intervention, and those who did not understand the Malay language.

The present study applied a multistage sampling method to recruit the study participants. The first sampling stage was conducted to select campuses with science diploma courses. Two campuses were randomly selected from eight available campuses in the country through a simple random sampling technique. Both campuses were pre-assigned to study groups; the campus in the northern region of Peninsular Malaysia (Campus A) was selected as the control group, whereas the campus on the west coast of Peninsular Malaysia (Campus B) was selected as the intervention group. Next, the research team members conducted another simple random sampling to recruit the students in both groups. During the sampling of the students, the research team members were assisted by a field researcher in each campus to recruit the students using a students’ list performed by computer-generated software until the required sample size was achieved.

Sample size calculation

The sample size for this study was calculated by comparing two means using OpenEpi software21. The estimated difference in mean score between the intervention and control groups was selected based on a standard deviation from a previous local study with similar outcome measures22. Based on a power of 80% (β = 0.02), an alpha of 0.05, and a 20% attrition rate, the estimated difference in the mean knowledge score (0.4) yielded the largest sample size, which was 92 participants per group. Hence, the final calculated sample size in this study was 184 participants.

Intervention trial

This study provided an HIV education intervention delivered by the principal researcher using the newly developed PREM-Kit. The participants in the control group did not receive any active intervention except for their routine curriculum-mandated basic science and biology module encompassing subjects such as histology, biology, and infectious diseases. On the other hand, the participants in the intervention group were provided with the PREM-Kit intervention in addition to the curriculum-mandated basic science and biology module.

The PREM-Kit intervention was conducted in two lecture-based sessions using PREM-Kit’s flip chart (in a Microsoft PowerPoint slide) and PREM-Kit’s videos demonstration for six weeks. The first session delivered Module 1 (during the first visit) in Week 0, followed by the second session, which delivered Module 2 and Module 3 in Week 6 (Fig. 1). Each session was also accompanied by question-and-answer activities as open quizzes with prizes to stimulate participants’ engagement. Both sessions took approximately 30 to 60 min (30 min for each module).

Participants’ enrolment and assessment

The field researcher at each campus screened students who attended the selected campuses for inclusion and exclusion criteria. Eligible students were invited to participate in the study for a duration of 16 weeks, comprising both intervention and data collection. There were four time points of activities and assessments on knowledge and attitudes related to HIV prevention (Fig. 1).

At baseline (t0), we collected data on participants’ knowledge and attitudes related to HIV prevention and sociodemographic information. The first intervention session follows this assessment. Consequently, the second intervention session was carried out in Week 6. Upon completing this session, the participants were provided with a similar questionnaire to respond to the immediate post-intervention assessment (t1), followed by the 12-week post-intervention assessment (t2) and the 16-week post-intervention assessment (t3).

Measures

Sociodemographic characteristics

This study examined six independent variables, which were: (i) age of the study participant, (ii) sex of study participants, (iii) ethnicity of study participants, (iv) current place of living, (v) types of secondary school, and (vi) history of receiving HIV/sex education during secondary school.

Outcomes

The outcomes of the present study were measured in terms of knowledge and attitudes scores related to HIV prevention using a validated Malay version of a self-administered questionnaire of knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) related to HIV prevention23. This instrument was pretested among Malaysian adolescents and had acceptable validity and reliability (Cronbach’s alpha coefficient = 0.61–0.73). The knowledge score is determined by calculating a mean score of knowledge at baseline, immediately post-intervention, 12 weeks post-intervention, and 16 weeks post-intervention. It consisted of 15 items with a “yes”, “no”, or “I do not know” response. The possible range of the knowledge score is 0 to 15, where a higher score indicates a higher level of knowledge. The attitude score is obtained by calculating the mean attitude score at baseline, immediately post-intervention, 12 weeks post-intervention, and 16 weeks post-intervention. It comprised eight items, each with a 5-point Likert scale (strongly agree, agree, undecided, disagree, and strongly disagree) for a response. The attitude score can range from 8 to 40, with a higher score signifying a more positive attitude towards HIV prevention. Data on practices related to HIV prevention was also collected. However, it was not presented in this paper.

Statistical analysis

To evaluate the intervention’s effectiveness, an intention-to-treat analysis approach was used to retain the participants in this study. Data were analyzed using the IBM SPSS Statistics for Mac, version 2624. All missing values were examined, and it was found that the final dataset (n = 187) contained missing values (knowledge and attitudes scores at t1-t3) ranging from 7.0% to 15.0%. Subsequently, we applied multiple imputation method to replace all the missing values because of (i) longitudinal data, (ii) the presence of more than 5% missing values, and (iii) Little’s MCAR test violated the assumption of missing completely at random (p-value = 0.027)25,26.

A baseline group comparison was performed by examining the sociodemographic characteristics of participants. Continuous data were presented using the mean (standard deviation). Besides, frequency (percentage) was used to describe the categorical data. An independent t-test was used to compare the distribution of continuous data to demonstrate the participants’ comparability between the intervention and control group. In contrast, the distribution of categorical data between groups was conducted using the chi-square test for homogeneity. As for variables with violated assumptions for the chi-square test for homogeneity, the continuity correction test was used. The level of significance for the statistical test was set at 0.05.

Repeated measure analysis of variance (RMANOVA) was applied to examine the changes of knowledge and attitude scores within a group, between groups and between groups over time, i.e., at Week 0 (baseline), at Week 6 (immediately post-intervention), at Week 12 (12-week post-intervention), and Week 16 (16-week post-intervention). Repeated measure analysis of covariance (RMANCOVA) was applied with baseline knowledge and attitude scores as the covariates. Mean (standard deviation) scores for each group, mean differences, F-statistics, degrees of freedom, p-values, 95% confidence intervals (CI), and partial eta-squared values were reported. The level of significance was set at 0.05. We included RMANOVA results in the Supplementary Table S1 and S2.

Results

Participant accrual

A total of 214 eligible participants were found during the recruitment phase. Of these, 187 participants consented to participate and thus were recruited into the study. There were 89 participants in the control group and 98 participants in the intervention group. The follow-up period for both groups lasted for 16 weeks. During the study period, nine control and five intervention group participants were excluded based on the exclusion criteria (absentees at t0).

A total of 159 (85.0%) participants completed the intervention and outcome measurement at t0 and t1, making the overall attrition rate 15.0%. The attrition rate did not differ significantly between the groups and was identified due to participants having a concurrent commitment with the on-campus program or being uncontactable (Supplementary Table S3). Participant characteristics

In the present study, the participants in both groups were predominantly female (150, 80.2%) and had a mean (SD) age of 18 (0.5) years old. The majority of the participants had received HIV/sex education during their secondary school (157, 84.0%). Both groups were comparable in most of their characteristics; however, the participants in the control group showed a higher knowledge score (mean = 10.4 vs. 9.4; p = 0.007; effect size = 0.38) and attitude score (mean = 28.6 vs. 24.7; p < 0.001; effect size = 1.15) at baseline compared to the participants in the intervention group (Table 1).

Effect of intervention on knowledge and attitude related to HIV prevention within groups (time effect)

Table 2 compares mean differences in knowledge and attitudes scores within each group across four time points (t0-t3). The control group showed no difference in knowledge and attitudes scores over time, while the intervention group demonstrated significant changes in both constructs. The knowledge score of the participants in the intervention group was increased from t0 to t1 (mean difference = − 4.49; 95% CI = − 5.28, − 3.71; p < 0.001), followed by a reduction in the score from t1 to t2 (mean difference = 1.19; 95% CI = 0.69, 1.70; p < 0.001) and from t2 to t3, although it did not reach statistical significance in the later follow-up (mean difference = 0.23; 95% CI = − 0.25, 0.71; p > 0.95). The attitude score was also increased from t0 to t1 (mean difference = − 5.93; 95% CI = −6.89, −4.98; p < 0.001), but no further statistical significant changes were observed from t1 to t2 and from t2 to t3.

Effect of intervention on knowledge and attitude related to HIV prevention between groups (overall intervention effect)

The findings of the overall intervention effect following the PREM-Kit’s intervention were summarized in Table 3. When baseline knowledge and attitude scores were adjusted, the intervention group demonstrated a moderate effect [F(2,183) = 51.35, p < 0.001; effect size = 0.359]28 resulting in 2.66 higher knowledge scores compared to the control group. Similarly, the intervention group showed a moderate effect, as evidenced by a 2.74-point higher attitude score than the control group [F(2,183) = 39.57, p < 0.001; effect size = 0.302].

Discussion

HIV continues to be a significant global health concern, with adolescents being a vulnerable population, including in Malaysia28,29. In the present study, a quasi-experimental study was conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of PREM-Kit intervention in improving knowledge and attitudes related to HIV prevention. PREM-Kit demonstrated an increased in knowledge score among the intervention group compared to control group, in line with findings of other studies22,30. This improvement aligns with the information construct of the IMB model, which underscores that accurate knowledge is a prerequisite for informed decision-making regarding HIV prevention12. By correcting misconceptions and filling gaps in understanding, the kit addressed a critical barrier to effective HIV preventive behaviour among late adolescents12.More importantly, PREM-Kit provided an equivalent advantage in improving knowledge related to HIV prevention through a shorter timeframe (30 to 60 min per session) in contrast to a five-hour-stretched peer education session and a 12-week short message service intervention22,31. This result could be attributed to the incorporation of an infographic flip chart and animated videos, and an adult-led intervention session, which collectively facilitate a more organized and practical HIV education32,33.

While the group comparison showed sustainable improvement in knowledge scores across the 16-week study period, the effect of PREM-Kit was gradually diminished from t1 to t3 (from Week 0 to Week 16), which may indicate a decaying effect of the intervention on knowledge related to HIV prevention. A similar trend was also observed in a previous Zambian study, which implies that the means of delivery may not substantially enhance knowledge acquisition and retention34. Instead, an additional critical factor, i.e. infrequent implementation of the intervention, might render the intervention unsustainable. The effect of intervention’s frequency on HIV knowledge outcome was further corroborated by the results of a systematic review, which identified multiple sessions of HIV intervention as a significant determinant linked to improved outcomes related to HIV35.

PREM-Kit also provides an immediate improvement in attitudes related to HIV prevention and is able to sustain it across the 16-week study period. Our finding was comparable to a prior local study, which also had an improvement in attitude related to HIV among the participants receiving peer-based HIV education throughout their three-month study period22. We opine that the participants’ improved attitude score following the PREM-Kit intervention was likely due to improved HIV knowledge. An individual exhibits a positive attitude towards HIV, people living with HIV (PLHIV), and risk reduction behaviours when provided with accurate HIV information and a comprehensive understanding of HIV transmission and prevention32,36,37.

Besides, having the second module based on the motivation construct within the IMB model framework which encompassed both personal attitudes towards HIV preventive behaviours and the perceived social support or norms enhanced improvement in attitudes observed in this study12. This suggest that the PREM-Kit could influence late adolescents’ personal beliefs about the importance of preventive practices. This motivational shift is essential, as knowledge alone is insufficient without the willingness and intent to act upon it12,38.

To date, there are a few existing HIV education programs for late adolescents in Malaysia39,40. Each of these programs has its own strengths and limitations, which could be compared to PREM-Kit in terms of content, materials, and implementation. The content of PREM-Kit is rooted in the IMB model, which is an evidence-based framework designed not only to focus on HIV prevention methods, risk factors, and the biology of HIV transmission but also include updated topics such as Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis (PrEP) and HIV self-testing, which reflects the latest advancements in HIV prevention strategies41. By addressing these contemporary issues, PREM-Kit stays relevant in today’s landscape of HIV education. PREM-Kit offers resources for late adolescents to locate PrEP services, view educational video on HIV testing, and access a directory of adolescent clinics and non-governmental organisations (NGOs) for additional support in maintaining their health engagement. Therefore, late adolescents are empowered to take control of their future learning process and foster a sense of autonomy and responsibility42.

In terms of implementation, PREM-Kit suggests a few advantages over the traditional peer-based programs that necessitate training and coordination. Given its positive understandability and actionability outcomes, PREM-Kit can be effectively utilised and implemented across different settings (e.g., tertiary educational institutions, health clinics, and community centres) by healthcare professionals, educators, and counsellors to educate late adolescents18. Additionally, it offers different educators to convey HIV education uniformly, hence reducing potential for misinformation among late adolescents18.

One of the limitations of the present study arises from the delivery methods of the PREM-Kit’s intervention. PREM-Kit education was delivered via a lecture-based approach to a large group of participants instead of a one-to-one or small group session. Through this method, it’s hard to make sure that each person’s needs, pace, and level of understanding are met, that could lead to reduced personalization and difficulty in monitoring participants’ comprehension43,44. Besides, individual participation in a large group is often limited, as contrast to one-to-one education sessions44. Even though we included question and answer sessions as well as open quizzes with prizes in each intervention session to stimulate participants’ engagement, the findings generated from the present study could have limited generalisability due to its limitation in delivering the PREM-Kit intervention. Besides, this study’s participants is predominantly composed of female adolescents and those from the Malay ethnic group. Since male and non-Malay adolescents have been shown to demonstrate different behavioural patterns, such as a higher likelihood of engaging in sexual activity, the limited representation of these groups may restrict the generalisability of the study findings4,45,46.

Last but not least, the overall study attrition rate was considerably higher compared to a previous similar HIV education intervention among students in a local public university, with a total of 15.0% of participants in the present study failing to complete the intervention, compared to only 2.1% of participants in the prior study22. We hypothesized that implementing our intervention via two separate sessions contributed to this outcome, as opposed to the five-hour combined session that was utilized in the previous study. Nevertheless, further assessment of our loss participants’ attributes showed no significant group differences in characteristics among those who defaulted to the intervention47.

Conclusion

Overall, PREM-Kit improved the late adolescents’ knowledge and attitudes related to HIV prevention and provided them with good sustainability of HIV knowledge and attitude for up to 16 weeks. PREM-Kit has the potential to benefit a wide range of stakeholders in the realm of HV education and may serve as a valuable resource in bridging the knowledge transfer regarding HIV and fostering positive attitudes towards HIV prevention among late adolescents.
