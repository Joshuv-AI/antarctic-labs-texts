What information is available online for adults with distal radius fracture? Online literature search and synthesis using generative AI
Authors: Daniel Ceh, Kalpesh R. Vaghela, Lisa Newington
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDistal radius fractures (DRFs) are the most common adult fracture in the UK, and patients seek online information to support recovery. The quality of such information varies, including in artificial intelligence (AI) generated summaries. This study explored the use of a generative AI model (Gemini) for information searching and synthesis of patient information on DRF recovery.MethodsSeven health information questions were co-developed with patients, hand therapists, and surgeons. Each question was searched in Google using three terms: ‘distal radius fracture’, ‘wrist fracture’, and ‘broken wrist’. For each AI Overview, the summary and cited sources were exported. Citations were screened for relevance and checked for accurate representation in the AI summary. Trustworthiness was rated using a National Institutes of Health (NIH) framework. AI summaries were compared with four UK guidelines on DRF management and rehabilitation.ResultsAcross all searches, 277 citations were identified; 37% were irrelevant. Of relevant citations, 57% (n = 99) were highly trustworthy, 40% (n = 70) moderately, and 3% (n = 6) low. Core messages aligned with UK guidelines, but AI summaries often gave fixed recovery timeframes.DiscussionGoogle’s AI Overview produced generally accurate, trustworthy DRF recovery information. However, variable source relevance, differing complexity by search term, and fixed timeframes highlight the need for clinicians to understand the online content patients encounter. Terminology influenced tone, ‘distal radius fracture’ yielded technical summaries, ‘wrist fracture’ mixed technical and practical advice, ‘broken wrist’ offered more lay-friendly guidance. Awareness of these differences can support personalised advice and bridge gaps between AI-generated content and evidence-based practice.

Introduction

Distal radius fractures (DRFs) are common global injuries, with reported annual incidence ranging from 100 to 190 per 100,000 for men and 282-458 per 100,000 for women. 1 In the UK, DRFs are the most prevalent adult fracture, with a projected 23% increase over the next two decades due to changing population structure. 2 For those in work, reported mean sick leave after DRF ranges from 9 to 12 weeks,3,4 with manual work, 5 high pain, and low functional ability 1 week after injury 6 associated with longer durations of sick leave.

Current best practice guidelines focus on recommendations for surgical and non-operative management.7–9 Existing guidelines highlight the limited evidence available to inform rehabilitation decisions. 10 Data from the USA suggests that formal rehabilitation is provided for ∼20% of adults with DRF. 11 Typically, only patients with complicated recovery would be referred for rehab in the UK. 12

A qualitative review of patient experiences of DRF highlighted three key themes: concerns about the loss of independence, fear and pain, and motivation for recovery. 13 Patients highlighted a desire for more information about fracture healing, suggesting that this would support both recovery and expectations. 13 Similarly, interviews with 31 adults with DRF after removal of their splint/cast identified limited knowledge of their condition and the medical management, naïve beliefs about their expected recovery, and uncertainty regarding safe movement and use of their arm. 14 Supportive interventions have been recommended to facilitate patients’ understanding, activation and engagement in meaningful activity after DRF. 15 Other concerns frequently raised by patients who have surgery to manage their DRF are whether the metalwork will need to be removed, or whether it might set off airport metal detectors. 16

DRF patients may receive recovery information from their healthcare provider in verbal, paper or electronic format, however they may also independently access other resources online. Online information is increasingly identified as an important source of health advice and can be associated with behaviour change.17,18 Health information behaviour refers to the act of seeking and utilising information that relates to an individual’s medical history, symptoms, diagnosis, and management. 19 Patients use the internet to identify information that justifies concerns about their condition, and to advocate for treatment. 20

Generative artificial intelligence (AI) models, such as ChatGPT (OpenAI) and Gemini (Google), offer new possibilities for identifying, exploring, and synthesising online health information at a larger scale.21,22 These tools are increasingly embedded into search engines, shaping what patients encounter during online searches by generating concise, curated summaries. 19 This review focuses on the information contained in these summaries.

Concerns around the use of AI technologies to access health information centre on whether the information provided is accurate, with AI being found to generate false, misleading or nonsensical information, which is presented as fact. 23 Users of AI have also raised concerns about misinformation, misdiagnosis and the appropriateness of using AI to manage more serious medical issues. 19

However, AI technologies have proved advantageous in facilitating access to health information, particularly where users may otherwise struggle, or are unwilling to access standard healthcare.24,25 Reported benefits of using AI to access health information include improved self-efficacy, disease knowledge, and medication adherence, 26 and reduced anxiety about health-related issues. 27

Given these mixed perspectives, there is a growing need to understand how generative AI might be used to explore and synthesise online health information in specific clinical contexts, such as patients with DRF.

The objective of this review was to explore the use of a generative AI model (Gemini) for information searching and synthesis in relation to patient information for DRF recovery. To address this objective, we aimed to examine the content and consistency of information available online by:(i) Comparing AI-generated summaries with their cited source material.(ii) Comparing AI-generated summaries with established UK rehabilitation guidelines.

For this review, the injury terms ‘distal radius fracture’ (DRF), ‘wrist fracture’ (WF) and ‘broken wrist’ (BW) were searched separately because patients may have heard one or all terms during the initial treatment for the injury,28,29 and all were identified as valid search terms from our exploration of Google Trends when developing the protocol. 30 Searches did not specify operative or non-operative management and responses for either were included in the review.

Each of the seven key questions were searched separately in a new private browser using Google Search (Box 1) on 6 June 2025. Google Search is a powerful web search engine (operated by Google), allowing users to find information online by entering key words or phrases. Searches were conducted in English Language with UK spelling.

Data extraction

Google AI Overview (a function of Gemini which provides an AI-generated summary in Google Search) was used to gather synthesised information from each of the search questions. Google AI Overview provides links to the original sources (citations) used to create it. Search outputs were manually exported for each Google AI Overview; this comprised the whole text summary, and the associated citations for each question and search term. All citations were accessed by the lead author and reviewed by pairs of authors to identify all information relevant to the search question. Relevant information was extracted using a pre-piloted data extraction form. 30 Information in video format was extracted as text.

If the citation did not include any information that answered the search question it was excluded. The number of excluded citations and reasons for exclusion were reported separately for each search question and injury term (DRF, WF, BW).

Duplicate data extraction was completed by KV and LN for a random selection of 20-25% of citations for each search question and injury term, identified using a random number generator. Any discrepancies were resolved through full team review and discussion.

Information quality

Each citation was rated for trustworthiness using the 10-item National Institutes of Health recommendations for patients to evaluate health information on the internet (Box 2). 31 These criteria were selected because they offer a widely recognised, patient-centred framework for assessing online health information. Trustworthiness was scored out of 20, based on a 3-point score for each item (Supplementary File 1). The scoring system was developed by the research team and summarised as high14-20 points, moderate 7-13 points and low 0-6 low. The same NIH criteria were applied consistently across all sources, and scores reflected the extent to which each citation met these criteria. Trustworthiness assessments were completed by the lead author and in duplicate for a random 20-25% of citations. Any discrepancies were resolved through full team review and discussion.Box 2. Data synthesis

Comparison of AI-generated summaries with cited sources

Data was synthesised by systematically comparing the content of the Google AI Overview summaries with the extracted information from the cited sources for each search question and injury term, to identify similarities, discrepancies, and missing information.

Comparison of AI-generated summaries with UK guidelines

Findings from the AI-generated summaries were qualitatively compared with existing UK treatment guidelines for rehabilitation following DRF.7,32–34 Only the summaries, not the underlying citations, were assessed. Guideline recommendations were used as a reference framework to evaluate how well the AI-generated content aligned with established best practice and to identify any discrepancies. Four guidelines had been identified prior to commencing the review through literature searching. To ensure that these guidelines represented clinical practice, members of the British Association of Hand Therapists regional groups 35 were contacted and invited to submit additional or unpublished guidelines.

Comparing the outputs across the three different search terms

During the synthesis stage, recurring differences between the AI-generated summaries for each search term became apparent as we familiarised ourselves with the dataset. We added a qualitative evaluation of the nature of the content for each search term to provide interpretive insights rather than formal findings.

The study protocol was published on the Open Science Framework before starting this review. 30

Trustworthiness

Of the included citations, 99 (57%) were rated high trustworthiness, 70 (40%) moderate, and 6 (3%) low (Supplementary file 2). Comparison of AI-generated summaries with cited sources

Across the 21 AI-generated summaries, most content was traceable to the cited sources, with six discrepancies identified between the summaries and the reviewer interpretation (Supplemental File 2). Discussion

This study set out to examine the content and consistency of online information about DRF recovery and to explore how a generative AI model (Google Gemini) performs when used for information searching and synthesis. Our findings reveal variation in the sources selected by the AI algorithm and in the format of the summary information generated for the three search terms ‘distal radius fracture’, ‘wrist fracture’, and ‘broken wrist’. There was, however, general consistency in the clinical content provided across summaries, and this alignment broadly extended to UK guidelines.

Most AI summaries drew on trustworthy sources (57% rated highly), predominantly UK-based (58%), with a mix of non-commercial or commercial websites (34%) and patient handouts (32%). However, what stood out was an emergent pattern in how search terminology appeared to shape the presentation of information, influencing content style, emphasis, and readability. These language differences have implications for how different patient needs are best supported by each type of summary. More technical ‘distal radius fracture’ summaries may be more accessible to clinicians or highly health-literate readers, whereas ‘wrist fracture’ summaries provide a balance of clinical detail and practical advice suitable for most adults with DRF. In contrast, ‘broken wrist’ summaries appeared more aligned with the needs of patients with lower health literacy, offering simple phrasing, reassurance, and step-by-step guidance.

These findings are consistent with those of a previous study looking at variations in quality, accuracy and readability of online information about DRF when different search terms were used. 28 The study found that the reading level of the identified material was significantly higher when the search term ‘distal radius fracture’ was used. For therapists and clinicians, the fact that different online search terms produce different styles of information has important implications for practice. Patients are likely to search using the language they are most familiar with, such as WF or BW, returning more accessible but less clinically detailed explanations. Using patient-friendly terms during consultations may support understanding and engagement.36,37 This highlights that, while clinicians have long considered patients’ health literacy when communicating in person and developing patient handouts, it is now equally important to recognise eHealth literacy, such as a patient’s ability to find, understand, and evaluate the health information they encounter online. 38 By being intentional about the language we use, and by acknowledging how patients are likely to encounter information online, therapists can help manage expectations, strengthen adherence, and foster confidence in recovery.38,39

Comparing AI outputs with established UK guidelines provides insight into both the reliability of generative AI as an emerging health information tool, and the availability of online content for rehabilitation questions that are important for patients, yet absent from current guidelines. While AI summaries were broadly consistent with UK guidelines, they differed in how information was presented, reflecting a more patient-centred orientation. Although this kind of detail can make the information feel more useful to patients, it also runs the risk of oversimplifying recovery or creating overly optimistic expectations.13,14 For instance, suggesting that someone might be able to drive again in 2 weeks could be misleading if their functional recovery is not yet sufficient to do so safely.

For hand therapists, the role of guidelines is important, as they offer evidence-based, structured frameworks for clinical decision-making. However, guidelines are not designed to answer every question that matters to patients. Many people want clear timelines or reassurance on individual everyday concerns, such as when they can drive again, how soon they might return to work, or even whether metal implants will set off airport security.13,15,16 These issues often sit outside the formal scope of guidelines. Therapists must recognise this gap and be prepared to bridge it, by reframing recovery in terms of function rather than rigid timelines, and by addressing lifestyle concerns openly and navigating potentially conflicting information patients have accessed online.28,40 Doing so allows us to uphold professional standards while also meeting patients’ needs, supporting confidence, adherence, and ultimately better outcomes.

37% (n = 102) of cited sources were assessed as not relevant to the search questions in this review. Examples where the citation did not provide information on DRF include information about rhinoplasty, carpal tunnel decompression, and ankle fracture. Despite this, no instances of AI hallucination (the phenomenon where artificial intelligence generates distorted or fabricated information 41 ) were identified within the AI-generated summaries themselves, a reassuring finding, given the potential risks of misinformation leading to inappropriate or unsafe rehabilitation activities.19,23 The mechanisms determining which sources are cited, and why some are cited despite limited or no relevance, remains unclear and warrants further investigation. Our review suggests that, when relevant citations are used, Google AI Overview accurately represents the source content within its summaries. However, the lack of transparency in how citations are generated highlights a key limitation in verifying the reliability and traceability of AI-generated health information.42,43 This concern becomes even more significant when searching for complex or less common conditions, where the number of accurate and trustworthy sources may be limited.

Across all search questions and injury terms, only 3% (n = 6) were rated low trustworthiness, and these originated from commercial rather than clinical or academic websites. Commercial sources typically met fewer of the NIH recommendations for evaluating online health information including transparency, authorship, conflict of interest, information review and absence of promotional material. 31 The lowest-scoring citations were commonly associated with questions relating to return to work, removal of metalwork, and airport security screening following surgery. These could be considered topics that attract more lifestyle, personal opinion or product-oriented sources. It was also noted that many websites, regardless of their overall rating, provided little transparency about the evidence supporting their content, with few offering reference lists or details about author credentials or content review. Interestingly, only 11 of the 175 relevant citations were peer-reviewed journal articles. While this may reflect the AI model’s tendency to prioritise sources that are more accessible and frequently clicked on by users,44,45 it highlights an important gap between how popular a source is, and how strong the actual evidence behind its information may be. This gap is further exacerbated by current publishing models, in which many research articles remain behind paywalls, restricting direct access for patients and the wider public.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is that the questions analysed were co-developed by patients, hand therapists and hand surgeons. This ensured that they reflect real concerns and priorities of individuals recovering from DRF. This strengthens the clinical relevance of the findings as the selected questions are likely to mirror the language and concerns patients commonly express when seeking information online. Secondly, the study looked closely at the material sourced by Google AI Overview, offering early insight into how generative AI might influence the way health information is shared online. A final strength of this study is the development of a transparent, replicable protocol for identifying and appraising online health information, including content generated by AI, which can serve as a practical template for future researchers conducting similar evaluations.

However, there are also important limitations to consider. The assessment of citation trustworthiness was based on a qualitative scoring framework developed by NIH 31 but modified by the research team to enable categorisation. While this provided a structured way to evaluate information quality, alternative approaches or validated tools may have produced different results. Another key limitation of this study was the inability to duplicate-check all citations due to time and resource constraints. Although a subset of citations underwent duplicate review, discrepancies were observed in 38% of cases. However, this high discrepancy rate was partly attributable to misunderstandings of the review criteria, which evolved during the study. The evaluation framework itself had to be developed iteratively, requiring repeated refinement and re-assessment of earlier decisions. This process reflects the complexity of creating a robust system for assessing AI-generated health information and may have introduced variability in how criteria were applied. Finally, the predominance of UK based citations within the AI Overviews will have been influenced by the researcher’s geographic location and searching environment, including IP address and browser settings. Although private browsing mode was used, cookies and cache were not fully cleared prior to searching, which may have biased the selection of sources and the phrasing of AI-generated summaries. Consequently, the findings may not be generalisable to users searching from other regions or health systems.

Conclusion

Our study identified that a widely available generative AI model (Gemini) is useful in searching and synthesising information relating to DRF recovery in adults. The study highlights how terminology shapes the way information for DRFs is communicated online. Additionally, the information provided by Google AI Overview was broadly aligned with current UK guidelines and did not appear to generate false or hallucinated information. DRF is a common condition that already has a large volume of online information from diverse and well-established sources. It would therefore be valuable to explore how generative AI performs when applied to less common or more complex conditions, where the available evidence-base and online resources are more limited.

In practice, AI-generated information can help patients gain a general understanding about their condition, but it cannot replace the professional judgement and personalised guidance of clinicians. For those of us working in rehabilitation, being aware of the kind of information patients are likely to encounter online is valuable. These narratives inevitably shape expectations, and by engaging with them directly in clinical encounters we can build rapport, correct misunderstandings, and support shared decision-making that balances scientific evidence and other publicly available material with individual patient needs.
