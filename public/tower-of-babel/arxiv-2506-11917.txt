Bastian Dittrich, Evelyn Herberg, Roland Herzog, Georg Müller

Abstract
. Cardinality constraints in optimization are commonly of L 0 L^{0} -type, and they lead to sparsely supported optimizers. An efficient way of dealing with these constraints algorithmically, when the objective functional is convex, is reformulating the constraint using the difference of suitable L 1 L^{1} - and largest- K K -norms and subsequently solving a sequence of penalized subproblems in the difference-of-convex (DC) class. We extend this DC-reformulation approach to problems with L 0 L^{0} -type cardinality constraints on the support of the gradients, \ie , problems where sparsity of the gradient and thus piecewise constant functions are the target.

Introduction

A wide range of applications benefit from sparsity of the solutions to their characterizing optimization problems,
\ie
, when the solutions are localized in a suitable sense.
Standard example applications include sensor and control device placement problems
[
Stadler:2009:1
,
ChepuriLeus:2015:1
]
, optimal experimental design
[
AlexanderianPetraStadlerGhattas:2014:1
,
HaberMagnantLuceroTenorio:2012:1
]
, imaging problems
[
GehreKluthLipponenJinSeppaenenKaipioMaass:2012:1
,
ZhongQin:2016:1
]
, optimization in finance
[
WuSunGeAllenZhaoZeng:2024:1
,
BertsimasCoryWright:2022:1
,
BurkhardtUlrych:2023:1
]
, signal processing
[
MarvastiAminiHaddadiSoltanolkotabiKhalajAldroubiSaneiChambers:2012:1
]
and machine learning
[
DeleuBengio:2021:1
,
KuruogluKuoChan:2023:1
]
.
Common approaches for promoting sparsity in the solutions to optimization or optimal control problems proceed by adding corresponding constraints.
This can be done either explicitly by adding an upper bound on a suitable (semi” )””norm, or implicitly by adding a penalty term to the objective function.

In
[
GotohTakedaTono:2017:1
]
, the authors considered a cardinality-constrained problem in finite dimensions of the type

Minimize

\displaystyle f(x)\quad\text{where }x\in\R^{n}

\st

\displaystyle\norm{x}_{0}\leq K,

where
\norm{\,\cdot\,}_{0}
denotes the
\ell_{0}
-pseudo-norm that counts the non-zero elements of
x
.
Their solution approach was based on solving a family of penalized problems whose target functions are structured as the difference of convex (DC) functions using a DC-specific algorithm; see,
\eg
,
[
LeThiPham:2018:1
]
.
This approach has been extended in
[
DittrichWachsmuth:2025:1
]
to infinite-dimensional problems of the type

(1)

\displaystyle\text{Minimize}

\displaystyle f(u)\quad\text{where }u\in U

\displaystyle\text{\st}

\displaystyle\norm{u}_{0}\leq K

in a reflexive Banach space
U
with compact embedding
U\compactly L^{1}(\Omega)
.
The
L^{0}
-pseudo-norm of a function
u\in U
is defined as the measure of its support,
\ie
,
\norm{u}_{0}=\mu(\supp(u))
, with respect to a suitable measure
\mu
.

It is a key observation both in the finite- and infinite-dimensional settings that we may replace the
L^{0}
-pseudo-norm constraint leveraging the equivalence

(2)

\norm{u}_{0}\leq K\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\norm{u}_{1}-\enclose[]{\vvvert}{u}{\vvvert}_{K}=0.

Here
\norm{\,\cdot\,}_{1}
denotes the
L^{1}
-norm, and
\enclose[]{\vvvert}{\cdot}{\vvvert}_{K}
the largest-
K
-norm.
We obtain a problem equivalent to (
1
) of the form

(3)

\displaystyle\text{Minimize}

\displaystyle f(u)\quad\text{where }u\in U

\displaystyle\text{\st}

\displaystyle\norm{u}_{1}-\enclose[]{\vvvert}{u}{\vvvert}_{K}=0,

for which the family of penalized, unconstrained problems with parameter
\rho>0

(4)

\text{Minimize}\quad f(u)+\rho\,\paren[big](){\norm{u}_{1}-\enclose[]{\vvvert}{u}{\vvvert}_{K}}\quad\text{where }u\in U,

yields approximations of the true solution.
Due to their structure featuring the difference of convex functions, the penalized problems (
4
) can be solved using the DC algorithm.

Contributions

In this paper, we present a modification of problem (
3
) and its penalized DC formulation (
4
) that is tailored to infinite-dimensional optimization problems with cardinality constraints on the
gradient
of the optimization function.
That is, we aim to achieve sparsity of the gradient rather than sparsity of the function itself.
Specifically, we consider problems of the form

(5)

\displaystyle\text{Minimize}

\displaystyle f(u)\quad\text{where }u\in U

\displaystyle\text{\st}

\displaystyle\norm{\nabla u}_{0}\leq K

in a suitable function space
U
so that
u\in U
has a weak gradient
\nabla u
.
The definition of the
L^{0}
-pseudo-norm of
\nabla u
, measuring the size of its support, will be given in the beginning of
\cref
section:DC-reformulation:function-space.

An upper bound on the measure of the gradient’s support translates to sparsely supported gradients and thus to solutions that are constant on subsets.
This behavior is desirable,
\eg
, in imaging and signal reconstruction
[
ArvanitopoulosDarginis:2017:1
]
.
Related literature can be found on elliptic equations with gradient constraints
[
Evans:1979:1
,
GriesseKunisch:2009:1
]
and pointwise state gradient constraints in optimal control
[
CasasFernandez:1993:1
,
DengMehlitzPruefert:2019:1
,
SchielaWollner:2011:1
]
.
We also mention
[
ClasonKunisch:2014:1
]
for optimal control problems promoting piecewise constant controls, but with preferred values of the control.

Both the analytical properties and possible choices for solution algorithms for these types of problems differ significantly from those of the original problem formulation in
[
DittrichWachsmuth:2025:1
]
, where sparsity of the function
u
itself was targeted.
On the analytical side, we consider the family of penalized problems with penalty parameter
\rho>0

\text{Minimize}\quad f(u)+\rho\,\paren[big](){\norm{\nabla u}_{1}-\enclose[]{\vvvert}{\nabla u}{\vvvert}_{K}}\quad\text{where }u\in U.

On the numerical side, simply replacing function values with gradients would lead to singular matrices in a semismooth Newton subroutine for solving the DC subproblems.
Therefore, a reformulation of the discrete subproblems is necessary compared to
[
DittrichWachsmuth:2025:1
]
.
This reformulation renders the associated penalization approach inexact, yet still effective.

2.
Notation and Preliminaries

The support and the
\ell_{0}
-pseudo-norm of a vector
x\in\R^{n}
are defined as

\supp(x)\coloneqq\setDef[big]{i\in\set{1,\ldots,n}}{x_{i}\neq 0},\qquad\norm{x}_{0}\coloneqq\mu(\supp(x)).

Here
\mu\colon\cP(\set{1,\ldots,n})\to\R
denotes the counting measure, so that we have
\norm{x}_{0}=\#(\supp(x))
.
Furthermore, we define a weighted version with weight vector
\nu\in\R_{>0}^{n}
as follows:

\norm{x}_{0,\nu}\coloneqq\mu_{\nu}(\supp(x))\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nu_{i}\,\norm{x_{i}}_{0}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
x_{i}\neq 0\end{subarray}}^{n}\nu_{i}.

More generally, for measurable functions
u\colon\Omega\to\R
on a measure space
(\Omega,\cA,\mu)
, the (set-theoretic) support and corresponding
L^{0}
-pseudo-norm are defined as

\supp(u)\coloneqq\setDef{x\in\Omega}{u(x)\neq 0},\qquad\norm{u}_{0}\coloneqq\mu(\supp(u)).

The reformulation of
L^{0}
-constrained problems into DC problems makes heavy use of the largest-
K
-norm.
When
x\in\R^{n}
,
\nu\in\R_{>0}^{n}
is a weight vector and
K\in\interval[][]{0}{\norm{\nu}_{1}}
, then the corresponding weighted largest-
K
-norm is defined as

\enclose[]{\vvvert}{x}{\vvvert}_{K,\nu}\coloneqq\max\setDef[Big]{\sum_{i\in I}\nu_{i}\,\abs{x_{i}}}{I\subseteq\set{1,\ldots,n}\text{ \st\ }\sum_{i\in I}\nu_{i}\leq K}.

This is a generalization of the definition of the usual (unweighted) largest-
K
-norm, which is equal to the sum of the absolute values of the
K
largest entries of
x
in absolute value.
The unweighted is recovered from the weighted case for
\nu=(1,\ldots,1)^{\transp}
and
K\in\N_{0}
.
When
K=\norm{\nu}_{1}
, then
\enclose[]{\vvvert}{x}{\vvvert}_{K,\nu}
becomes the weighted
\ell_{1}
-norm,
\ie
,

\norm{x}_{1,\nu}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nu_{i}\,\abs{x_{i}}.

More generally, consider an integrable function
u\colon\Omega\to\R
on an underlying measure space
(\Omega,\cA,\mu)
and
K\in\interval[][]{0}{\mu(\Omega)}
.
Its largest-
K
-norm is defined as

\enclose[]{\vvvert}{u}{\vvvert}_{K}\coloneqq\sup\setDef[Big]{\int_{A}\abs{u}\d{\mu}}{A\in\cA\text{ \st\ }\mu(A)\leq K}.

This generalization was extensively studied in
[
DittrichWachsmuth:2025:1
]
.
Throughout the paper, we adopt the convention that
(+\infty)-(+\infty)=+\infty
, which is reasonable in the context of DC functions.

For any Banach space
X
, we let
X^{*}
denote its topological dual space.
Additionally, for a linear, bounded mapping
g\colon X\to Y
between Banach spaces, we let
g^{*}\colon Y^{*}\to X^{*}
denote the dual mapping.
When
g\colon X\to\overline{\R}\coloneqq\R\cup\set{+\infty}
is convex, we consider the convex subdifferential at
x\in X
to be

\partial g(x)=\setDef{s\in X^{*}}{g(x)+\dual{s}{\hat{x}-x}\leq g(\hat{x})\text{ for all }\hat{x}\in X}.

3.
DC Reformulation for Gradient Sparsity in Function Space

In this section we consider the abstract problem

(6)

\displaystyle\text{Minimize}

\displaystyle f(u)\quad\text{where }u\in U

\displaystyle\text{\st}

\displaystyle\norm{\nabla u}_{0}\leq K,

with
K\in\interval[][]{0}{\mu(\Omega)}
under the following standing assumptions.

Assumption 3.1

(Standing assumptions for the continuous setting)
.

(1)

\Omega\subseteq\R^{d}
is open and bounded,
d\in\N
, and
\mu
is the Lebesgue measure.

(2)

U
is a reflexive Banach space that is compactly embedded in
H^{1}(\Omega)
and is dense in
L^{1}(\Omega)
.

(3)

The objective
f\colon U\to\overline{\R}\coloneqq\R\cup\set{+\infty}
is proper, convex, lower semicontinuous, bounded from below and radially unbounded.

(4)

There exists
u\in U
with
f(u)<\infty
and
\norm{\nabla u}_{0}\leq K
.

Example 3.2
.

Suppose that
\Omega\subseteq\R^{d}
,
d\geq 2
is a bounded domain with Lipschitz boundary.
For any
\varepsilon>0
, an example of a space
U
that satisfies
\cref
assumption:standing-assumptions:U of
\cref
assumption:standing-assumptions is
U\coloneqq H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\cap H^{1+\varepsilon}(\Omega)
equipped with the
H^{1+\varepsilon}(\Omega)
-norm.

Let us discuss the precise meaning of
\norm{\nabla u}_{0}
.
Here and throughout,
\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}
denotes the
i
-th weak partial derivative of
u
.
By
\nabla u\coloneqq\paren[big](){\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{1}},\ldots,\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{d}}}^{\transp}
, we denote the Euclidian weak gradient of
u\in U
.
We define

(7)

\supp(\nabla u)\coloneqq\bigcup_{i=1}^{d}\supp\paren[Big](){\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}}=\supp\paren[Big](){\sum_{i=1}^{d}\paren[big](){\textstyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}}^{2}}

and consequently,

\norm{\nabla u}_{0}\coloneqq\mu(\supp(\nabla u))

satisfies

\norm{\nabla u}_{0}=\norm{(\nabla u)^{\transp}\nabla u}_{0}=\norm[Big]{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\paren[big](){\textstyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}}^{2}}_{0}=\norm{\abs{\nabla u}_{2}^{2}}_{0}.

Notice that this definition is invariant
\wrt
rotation of the coordinate system.
We also have

\enclose[]{\vvvert}{\abs{\nabla u}_{2}^{2}}{\vvvert}_{K}=\sup\setDef[Big]{\int_{A}\abs{\nabla u}_{2}^{2}\d{\mu}}{A\text{ is measurable and }\mu(A)\leq K}.

Since
U
is embedded into
H^{1}(\Omega)
, we know that
\abs{\nabla u}_{2}^{2}\in L^{1}(\Omega)
holds for all
u\in U
.
By
[
DittrichWachsmuth:2025:1
, Theorem 3.22]
, problem (
6
) is therefore equivalent to the problem

(8)

\displaystyle\text{Minimize}

\displaystyle f(u)\quad\text{where }u\in U

\displaystyle\text{\st}

\displaystyle\norm{\abs{\nabla u}_{2}^{2}}_{1}-\enclose[]{\vvvert}{\abs{\nabla u}_{2}^{2}}{\vvvert}_{K}=0.

We associate with (
8
) a family of unconstrained, penalized problems

(9)

\displaystyle\text{Minimize}

\displaystyle f_{\rho}(u)\coloneqq f(u)+\rho\,\phi(u)\quad\text{where }u\in U

\displaystyle\text{with}

\displaystyle\phi(u)\coloneqq\norm{\abs{\nabla u}_{2}^{2}}_{1}-\enclose[]{\vvvert}{\abs{\nabla u}_{2}^{2}}{\vvvert}_{K}

and penalty parameter
\rho>0
.
The family (
9
) can be used to approximate the solutions of (
6
), as shown by the following results.

Lemma 3.3
.

The function
\phi
is weakly sequentially continuous on
U
.

Proof.

Let
\sequence{u}{k}\weakly u^{*}
in
U
.
By the compact embedding
U\compactly H^{1}(\Omega)
this convergence is even strong in
H^{1}(\Omega)
and in particular
\frac{\partial\sequence{u}{k}}{\partial x_{i}}\to\frac{\partial u^{*}}{\partial x_{i}}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
for all
i=1,\ldots,d
.
Therefore,
\abs{\nabla\sequence{u}{k}}_{2}^{2}\to\abs{\nabla u^{*}}_{2}^{2}
in
L^{1}(\Omega)
, and the claim follows because
\enclose[]{\vvvert}{\cdot}{\vvvert}_{K}\leq\norm{\,\cdot\,}_{1}
.
∎

Theorem 3.4
.

Problems (
6
), (
8
) and (
9
) possess global minimizers.

Proof.

With
\cref
lemma:weak-continuity-of-phi, the result follows analogously as in
[
DittrichWachsmuth:2025:1
, Theorem 5.2 and Theorem 5.3]
.
∎

Theorem 3.5

(Convergence of global minimizers)
.

Suppose that
\sequence{\rho}{k}>0
is a sequence such that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\sequence{\rho}{k}=\infty
.
Let
\sequence{u}{k}
be a global solution of the penalized problem (
9
) with
\rho=\sequence{\rho}{k}
for all
k\in\N
.
Then the sequence
(\sequence{u}{k})
is bounded in
U
, satisfies
\lim_{k\to\infty}\phi(\sequence{u}{k})=0
, and every weak accumulation point
\overline{u}
is a global solution of (
6
).

Proof.

Together with
\cref
lemma:weak-continuity-of-phi, the result follows analogously as in
[
DittrichWachsmuth:2025:1
, Theorem 5.4]
.
∎

Since critical points are the natural limit points of the iterates in a DC algorithm, we are interested in their characterization.

Definition 3.6

(Critical point, strongly critical point)
.

Suppose that
g,h\colon U\to\overline{\R}
are proper, lower semicontinuous and convex and consider the DC function
\delta\coloneqq g-h
.
Then
u\in U
is called a critical point of
\delta

\wrt
the decomposition into
g
and
h
if

\partial g(u)\cap\partial h(u)\neq\emptyset,

and a strongly critical point if

\emptyset\neq\partial h(u)\subseteq\partial g(u).

Note that the conditions in
\cref
definition:critical-point for (strong) criticality are necessary local optimality conditions for a DC function
\delta=g-h
, see
[
PhamLeThi:1997:1
, Theorem 2]
.
Under sufficient regularity assumptions, (strong) criticality of
u
corresponds to the well known necessary optimality condition
0\in\partial(g-h)(u)
.
We will go into more detail in the discretized setting in
\cref
subsection:DC-reformulation:DC-approach.
For now, applying the chain rule for the computation of convex subdifferentials, we can characterize the subdifferential of
\enclose[]{\vvvert}{\abs{\nabla u}_{2}^{2}}{\vvvert}_{K}
.
To simplify notation, we introduce the function

W\colon U\ni u\mapsto W(u)\coloneqq\abs{\nabla u}_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\paren[Big](){\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}}^{2}\in L^{1}(\Omega)

that has the continuous Fréchet derivative

W^{\prime}(u)\,v=2\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{i}}\quad\text{ for }v\in U.

Theorem 3.7

(Characterization of the subdifferential
\partial\enclose[]{\vvvert}{W(\cdot)}{\vvvert}_{K}
)
.

Let
u\in U
and
K\in\interval[][]{0}{\mu(\Omega)}
.
Then

(10a)

\partial\enclose[]{\vvvert}{W(\cdot)}{\vvvert}_{K}(u)=\setDef[auto]{W^{\prime}(u)^{*}r}{r\in\partial\enclose[]{\vvvert}{\cdot}{\vvvert}_{K}(W(u))}

holds with

(10b)

\partial\enclose[]{\vvvert}{\cdot}{\vvvert}_{K}\paren[auto](){W(u)}\\
=\setDef[auto]{r\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)}{\norm{r}_{\infty}\leq 1,\norm{r}_{1}\leq K,\int_{\Omega}r\,W(u)\d{\mu}=\enclose[]{\vvvert}{W(u)}{\vvvert}_{K}}.

This is to be understood in the sense of the embedding of
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
into
U^{*}
induced by the embedding of
U
into
L^{1}(\Omega)
; see
\cref
assumption:standing-assumptions.
Moverover,

(W^{\prime}(u)^{*}r)\,v=2\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}r\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{i}}\d{\mu}\quad\text{ for }v\in U.

Proof.

The largest-
K
-norm is convex, continuous and locally Lipschitz-continuous everywhere and its convex subdifferential has the claimed form (
10b
); see,
\eg
,
[
DittrichWachsmuth:2025:1
, Theorem 3.15]
.
Applying the chain rule for convex subdifferentials,
\eg
[
Clason2020
, Theorem 2.5]
, the elements of
\partial\enclose[]{\vvvert}{W(\cdot)}{\vvvert}_{K}(u)=\partial(\enclose[]{\vvvert}{\cdot}{\vvvert}_{K}\circ W)(u)
are given by
W^{\prime}(u)^{*}r
with
r\in\partial\enclose[]{\vvvert}{\cdot}{\vvvert}_{K}(W(u))
and

(W^{\prime}(u)^{*}r)\,v=\dual{W^{\prime}(u)^{*}r}{v}_{U^{*},U}=\dual{r}{W^{\prime}(u)\,v}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega),L^{1}(\Omega)}=2\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}r\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{i}}\d{\mu}

holds for all
v\in U
.
∎

\Cref

theorem:subdifferential-of-the-largest-K-norm-of-the-sum-of-squared-gradient-components allows us to state the following necessary optimality condition.

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
f
be Gâteaux-differentiable and let
u
be a critical point of
f_{\rho}

\wrt
the natural DC decomposition in the DC problem (
9
).
Then there exists
r\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
with
\norm{r}_{\infty}\leq 1
,
\norm{r}_{1}\leq K
and
\int_{\Omega}r\,W(u)\d{\mu}=\enclose[]{\vvvert}{W(u)}{\vvvert}_{K}
such that

f^{\prime}(u)\,v+2\,\rho\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}(1-r)\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{i}}\d{\mu}=0\quad\text{ for all }v\in U.

If
u
is even strongly critical, then the subdifferential
\partial\enclose[]{\vvvert}{W(\cdot)}{\vvvert}_{K}(u)
is a singleton and the unique element
s\in\partial\enclose[]{\vvvert}{W(\cdot)}{\vvvert}_{K}(u)
satisfies

\rho\,\dual{s}{v}=f^{\prime}(u)\,v+2\,\rho\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{i}}\d{\mu}\quad\text{ for all }v\in U.

Proof.

First off, note that
\norm{W(u)}_{1}=\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}\paren[big](){\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}}^{2}\d{\mu}
.
Therefore, the first identity is a straightforward combination of the definition of a critical point from
\cref
definition:critical-point with
\delta=f_{\rho}
,
g=f+\rho\,\norm{W(u)}_{1}
and
h=\rho\,\enclose[]{\vvvert}{W(u)}{\vvvert}_{K}
with the subdifferential characterization in
\cref
theorem:subdifferential-of-the-largest-K-norm-of-the-sum-of-squared-gradient-components.

In case of strong criticality, we know that
\partial g(u)
is a singleton, hence
\partial g(u)
has to coincide with any nonempty subset of it, which yields the second equality.
∎

4.
DC Reformulation for Gradient Sparsity in a Discretized Setting

In this section we develop and analyze a discretization for problem (
6
) and its variants.
While the discussion in the continuous setting in
\cref
section:DC-reformulation:function-space was following
[
DittrichWachsmuth:2025:1
]
quite closely, the discrete settings are notably different.

4.1.
Discretization, Constraint Reformulation, Existence of Solutions

For simplicity, we assume in this section that the bounded domain
\Omega\subseteq\R^{d}
is polyhedral.
We consider a geometrically conforming discretization of
\Omega
into a finite number of
d
-simplices
S_{i}
; see
[
ErnGuermond:2004:1
, Definition 1.55]
.
We refer to the collection of simplices of dimensions
0
(vertices),
1
(edges) up to
d
as the mesh
\cS
.
In other words, the mesh is a pure, geometric simplicial
d
-complex in
\R^{d}
.
We let
{n_{V}}
,
{n_{E}}
and
{n_{S}}
denote the number of vertices, edges and
d
-simplices (cells) in
\cS
.
In the coming analysis, we additionally require some notation concerning the relationships of vertices, edges and simplices, including the following index maps that encode incidence (connectivity) information:

\displaystyle\sigma_{V\leftarrow E}(j)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\setDef[big]{\ell\in\set{1,\ldots,{n_{V}}}}{\text{vertex $\ell$ is incident to edge $j$}},

\displaystyle\sigma_{V\leftarrow S}(i)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\setDef[big]{\ell\in\set{1,\ldots,{n_{V}}}}{\text{vertex $\ell$ is a vertex of simplex $i$}},

\displaystyle\sigma_{E\leftarrow V}(\ell)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\setDef[big]{j\in\set{1,\ldots,{n_{E}}}}{\text{edge $j$ is incident to vertex $\ell$}},

\displaystyle\sigma_{E\leftarrow S}(i)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\setDef[big]{j\in\set{1,\ldots,{n_{E}}}}{\text{edge $j$ is an edge of simplex $i$}},

\displaystyle\sigma_{S\leftarrow V}(\ell)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\setDef[big]{i\in\set{1,\ldots,{n_{S}}}}{\text{simplex $i$ is incident to vertex $\ell$}},

\displaystyle\sigma_{S\leftarrow E}(j)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\setDef[big]{i\in\set{1,\ldots,{n_{S}}}}{\text{simplex $i$ is incident to edge $j$}}.

When either of the maps above is applied to a set of arguments, the output is to be understood as the union of the images of the individual arguments.

We consider the finite element space of piecewise affine and continuous functions
\Omega\to\R
,
\ie
,

(11)

U_{h}\coloneqq\Span\setDef[big]{\varphi_{\ell}}{\ell\in\set{1,\ldots,{n_{V}}}}

generated by the nodal basis functions
\varphi_{\ell}
,
\ell=1,\ldots,{n_{V}}
.
Elements
u_{h}\in U_{h}
will be represented by their nodal coefficient vectors, which we denote by
{\mathbf{u}}\in\R^{n_{V}}
.
Due to the geometrically conforming discretization, we have
U_{h}\subseteq H^{s}(\Omega)
for all
s\in\interval[][]{0}{1}
.

We begin by constructing an expression that indicates whether or not a function
u_{h}\in U_{h}
is constant on a cell
S_{i}
.
To this end, we define the matrix
{\mathbf{D}}_{1}\in\R^{{n_{E}}\times{n_{V}}}
by

({\mathbf{D}}_{1})_{j,\ell}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\mrep[r]{1}{-1},&\text{if vertex $\ell$ is the first vertex of edge $j$},\\
-1,&\text{if vertex $\ell$ is the second vertex of edge $j$},\\
\mrep[r]{0}{-1},&\text{else}.\end{cases}

Consequently, given
{\mathbf{u}}\in\R^{n_{V}}
, the quantity
({\mathbf{D}}_{1}{\mathbf{u}})_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,{n_{E}}
, represents the difference of vertex values incident on the
j
-th edge.
The sign,
\ie
, whether a vertex is considered first or second, is not essential here, since we will take the square of this quantity in (
12
).

Furthermore, a matrix
{\mathbf{D}}_{2}\in\R^{{n_{S}}\times{n_{E}}}
that encodes whether an edge is part of a cell is defined by

({\mathbf{D}}_{2})_{i,j}\coloneqq\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $j\in\sigma_{E\leftarrow S}(i)$, \ie, edge~$j$ is an edge of the cell $S_{i}$}\\
0,&\text{else}.\end{cases}

We can now define

(12)

\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}\coloneqq{\mathbf{D}}_{2}\paren[big][]{({\mathbf{D}}_{1}{\mathbf{u}})\odot({\mathbf{D}}_{1}{\mathbf{u}})}\in\R^{n_{S}},

where
\odot
means componentwise multiplication.
Hence,
\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}_{i}
is the sum of the squared pairwise differences of the vertex values on the cell
S_{i}
.
Note that
\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}
depends on
{\mathbf{u}}
smoothly.

A function
u_{h}\in U_{h}
is constant on
S_{i}
if and only if
\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}_{i}=0
holds for its coefficient vector
{\mathbf{u}}
.
We collect the indices of cells where
{\mathbf{u}}
is constant or non-constant in the sets

\displaystyle\constantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\setDef[big]{i\in\set{1,\ldots,{n_{S}}}}{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}_{i}=0},

\displaystyle\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\setDef[big]{i\in\set{1,\ldots,{n_{S}}}}{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}_{i}\neq 0}.

Remark 4.1
.

Following
[
DittrichWachsmuth:2025:1
, Section 6.1]
, one could also consider
{\mathbf{D}}_{2}\abs{{\mathbf{D}}_{1}{\mathbf{u}}}\in\R^{n_{S}}
(with the componentwise absolute value) in place of (
12
).
For that choice, however, the rank deficiency of
{\mathbf{D}}_{1}
for standard finite element meshes leads to a singular matrix in the semismooth Newton method one would like to apply when solving the resulting DC subproblems,
\cf
[
DittrichWachsmuth:2025:1
, Section 6.4]
.
Note that the matrix
{\mathbf{D}}_{1}
would have full rank if we would only consider the rows pertaining to edges that form a minimum spanning tree (
[
Diestel:2025:1
, Section 1.5]
).
Unfortunately, when
{\mathbf{D}}_{1}
is generated from such a minimum spanning tree, then, in general,
\paren[big](){{\mathbf{D}}_{2}\abs{{\mathbf{D}}_{1}{\mathbf{u}}}}_{i}=0
is no longer indicative of
u_{h}
being constant on
S_{i}
.

An additional benefit of using the squared differences (
12
) is that the resulting DC subproblems (
24
) are smooth, see
\cref
lemma:norm-of-sum-of-squared-differences-via-matrices.
For instance, when
f
is a quadratic polynomial, we simply need to solve a linear system instead of employing a semismooth Newton method; see (
26
).

In retrospect, we also tried using a squared formulation in the setting of
[
DittrichWachsmuth:2025:1
, Section 6.1]
.
This would also render the resulting DC subproblems smooth.
However, numerical comparisons show that this can lead to significantly slower convergence or convergence to local minimizers in that setting.

Given a weight vector
\nu\in\R^{n_{S}}_{>0}
and
{\mathbf{u}}\in\R^{n_{V}}
, for
\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}
, the weighted (pseudo” )””norms defined in
\cref
section:notation-preliminaries have the form

\displaystyle\norm{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}_{0,\nu}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mrep{i\in\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}}{}}\nu_{i},

\displaystyle\norm{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}_{1,\nu}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{S}}\nu_{i}\,\abs{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}_{i}}=\sum_{\mrep{i\in\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}}{}}\nu_{i}\,\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}_{i},

\displaystyle\enclose[]{\vvvert}{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}{\vvvert}_{K,\nu}

\displaystyle=\max\setDef[Big]{\sum_{i\in I\cap\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}}\nu_{i}\,\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}_{i}}{I\subseteq\set{1,\ldots,{n_{S}}}\text{ \st\ }\sum_{i\in I}\nu_{i}\leq K}.

In the following, we always use the weight vector
\nu
given by the cell volumes

(13)

\nu_{i}\coloneqq\mu(S_{i})\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,{n_{S}},\text{ so that }\norm{\nu}_{1}=\mu(\Omega)\text{ holds}.

This allows us to equivalently express the cardinality constraint on
\nabla u_{h}
as follows:

Lemma 4.2
.

For
u_{h}\in U_{h}
, we have
\norm{\nabla u_{h}}_{0}=\norm{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}_{0,\nu}
.

Proof.

Since
u_{h}
is piecewise affine,
\nabla u_{h}
is piecewise constant.
Hence
\supp(\nabla u)
is the union of entire cells
S_{i}
; see (
7
).
Consider an arbitrary cell
S_{i}
.
Since
u_{h}
is affine,
\abs{\nabla u_{h}}_{2}^{2}
is constant on
S_{i}
.
In particular,
\abs{\nabla u_{h}}_{2}^{2}=0
holds on
S_{i}
if and only if the vertex values of
u_{h}
on all vertices of
S_{i}
coincide.
This is the case if and only if
\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}_{i}=0
,
\ie
,
i\in\constantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}
.
Since different cells intersect only in sets of measure zero, the result follows.
∎

We now consider the discretized counterpart of (
6
):

\displaystyle\text{Minimize}

\displaystyle f(u_{h})\quad\text{where }u_{h}\in U_{h}

\displaystyle\text{\st}

\displaystyle\norm{\nabla u_{h}}_{0}\leq K

with
K\in\interval[][]{0}{\mu(\Omega)}
.
Using
\cref
lemma:L0-norm-of-gradient, we can equivalently express this problem in terms of the cofficient vector
{\mathbf{u}}\in\R^{n_{V}}
of
u_{h}\in U_{h}
:

(14)

\displaystyle\text{Minimize}

\displaystyle f({\mathbf{u}})\coloneqq f(u_{h})\quad\text{where }{\mathbf{u}}\in\R^{n_{V}}

\displaystyle\text{\st}

\displaystyle\norm{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}_{0,\nu}\leq K.

The slight abuse of notation of re-using the symbol
f
will not be harmful since
{\mathbf{u}}
and
u_{h}
are in a bijective linear correspondence.
For the discretized setting, we replace
\cref
assumption:standing-assumptions by the following:

Assumption 4.3

(Standing assumptions for the discrete setting)
.

The objective function
f\colon\R^{n_{V}}\to\overline{\R}
is proper, lower semicontinuous and radially unbounded.

Under these assumptions on
f
we can prove the existence of minimizers for problem (
14
).
However, we first first need to show the closedness of the feasible set.

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose that
\sequence[big](){{\mathbf{u}}}{k}\subseteq\R^{n_{V}}
satisfies
\sequence{{\mathbf{u}}}{k}\to{\mathbf{u}}
.
Then there exists
N\in\N
such that

\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}\subseteq\nonconstantsimplices{\sequence{{\mathbf{u}}}{k}}\quad\text{for all }k\geq N.

In particular, if
\norm{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{\sequence{{\mathbf{u}}}{k}}}_{0,\nu}\leq K
, then
\norm{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}_{0,\nu}\leq K
.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\min\setDef[big]{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}_{i}}{i\in\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}}
.
Then there exists
N\in\N
with
\norm{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{\sequence{{\mathbf{u}}}{k}}-\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon
for all
k\geq N
.
This particularly implies
\sumOfSquaredDifferences{\sequence{{\mathbf{u}}}{k}}_{i}\neq 0
for all
i\in\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}
and all
k\geq N
.
∎

Note that this result also shows that cells where
u_{h}
is not constant will eventually be detected when the nodal values converge.

Theorem 4.5
.

Problem (
14
) possesses a global minimizer.

Proof.

The feasible set

(15)

F\coloneqq\setDef[big]{{\mathbf{u}}\in\R^{n_{V}}}{\norm{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}_{0,{\nu}}\leq K}

is nonempty and closed, as shown by
{\mathbf{0}}\in F
and
\cref
lemma:convergent-nodal-values-detect-non-constant-cells.
We denote its characteristic function (with values in
\set{0,\infty}
) by
\bone_{F}
.
Therefore and by the assumptions,
f+\bone_{F}
is proper, lower semicontinuous and radially unbounded.
Hence, problem (
14
) is solvable by Weierstraß’ theorem; see,
\eg
,
[
DharaDutta:2011:1
, Theorem 1.14]
.
∎

Similarly as in (
8
), we may also restate the cardinality constraint in (
14
) using a difference of weighted norms:

Theorem 4.6
.

Suppose
u_{h}\in U_{h}
and
K\in\interval[][]{0}{\mu(\Omega)}
.
Then we have

\norm{\nabla u_{h}}_{0}\leq K\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\norm{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}_{0,\nu}\leq K\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\norm{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}_{1,\nu}-\enclose[]{\vvvert}{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}{\vvvert}_{K,\nu}=0.

Proof.

This follows directly from
\cref
lemma:L0-norm-of-gradient and
[
DittrichWachsmuth:2025:1
, Theorem 3.22]
.
The cases
K=0
and
K=\mu(\Omega)
are also valid, as one can verify using the exact same arguments.
∎

Defining

(16)

\phi({\mathbf{u}})\coloneqq\norm{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}_{1,\nu}-\enclose[]{\vvvert}{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}{\vvvert}_{K,\nu},

we can restate the discrete problem (
14
) equivalently as

(17)

\displaystyle\text{Minimize}

\displaystyle f({\mathbf{u}})\quad\text{where }{\mathbf{u}}\in\R^{n_{V}}

\displaystyle\text{\st}

\displaystyle\phi({\mathbf{u}})=0

and the associated penalized problem as

(18)

\text{Minimize}\quad f_{\rho}({\mathbf{u}})\coloneqq f({\mathbf{u}})+\rho\,\phi({\mathbf{u}})\quad\text{where }{\mathbf{u}}\in\R^{n_{V}}

with penalty parameter
\rho>0
.

We will discuss optimality conditions for the discrete problem (
14
) (or, equivalently, (
17
)) in the following section and address the penalized problem (
18
) in
\cref
subsection:DC-reformulation:DC-approach.

4.2.
Optimality Conditions

In this subsection, we investigate necessary optimality conditions of Bouligand-type for minimizers of the discrete optimization problem (
14
).
For this subsection, we assume the following from now on:

Assumption 4.7

(Additional standing assumptions for the discrete setting)
.

Additionally to the properties in
\cref
assumption:standing-assumptions:finite-dimensions, the objective function
f
is differentiable.

We begin by considering the tangent cone

\tangentCone{F}{{\mathbf{u}}}\coloneqq\setDef[Big]{{\mathbf{d}}\in\R^{n_{V}}}{\exists\sequence[big](){{\mathbf{u}}}{k}\subseteq F\text{ \st\ }\sequence{{\mathbf{u}}}{k}\to{\mathbf{u}},\;\sequence{\tau}{k}\searrow 0,\;{\mathbf{d}}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\sequence{{\mathbf{u}}}{k}-{\mathbf{u}}}{\sequence{\tau}{k}}}

for any feasible
{\mathbf{u}}\in F
; see (
15
).
We say that
{\mathbf{u}}\in F
is a Bouligand-stationary (B-stationary) point in case

(19)

f^{\prime}({\mathbf{u}})\,{\mathbf{d}}\geq 0\quad\text{for all }{\mathbf{d}}\in\tangentCone{F}{{\mathbf{u}}}

holds.
Our main result is
\cref
theorem:B-stationarity:explicit-description — a nodal characterization of B-stationarity via vanishing averages of derivatives of the objective functional on certain connected subsets of the vertices.

Before proving the main result, we first need to establish some additional relations between the support of a nodal coefficient vector
{\mathbf{u}}\in\R^{n_{V}}
, its weighted
\ell_{0}
-pseudo-norm and the corresponding vector
\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}
from (
12
) holding the cellwise sums of squared differences of the vertex values.
The following result shows that the set of non-constant cells may expand under summation:

Lemma 4.8
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}}\in\R^{n_{V}}
.
Then

\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}+{\mathbf{v}}}\subseteq\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}\cup\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{v}}}.

Proof.

Suppose
i\in\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}+{\mathbf{v}}}
,
\ie
,

0\neq\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}+{\mathbf{v}}}_{i}=\paren[Big][]{{\mathbf{D}}_{2}\paren[big][]{({\mathbf{D}}_{1}({\mathbf{u}}+{\mathbf{v}}))\odot({\mathbf{D}}_{1}({\mathbf{u}}+{\mathbf{v}}))}}_{i}=\sum_{\mrep{j\in\sigma_{E\leftarrow S}(i)}{}}\paren[big][]{{\mathbf{D}}_{1}({\mathbf{u}}+{\mathbf{v}})}_{j}^{2}.

Consequently, there exists an edge index
j\in\sigma_{E\leftarrow S}(i)
such that
\paren[normal][]{{\mathbf{D}}_{1}({\mathbf{u}}+{\mathbf{v}})}_{j}\neq 0
.
Therefore,
\paren[normal][]{{\mathbf{D}}_{1}{\mathbf{u}}}_{j}
and
\paren[normal][]{{\mathbf{D}}_{1}{\mathbf{v}}}_{j}
cannot both be zero.
Arguing as above, we conclude that
\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}_{i}
and
\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{v}}}_{i}
cannot both be zero.
Hence,
i\in\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}\cup\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{v}}}
.
∎

We can now proceed to a description of the tangent cone to the feasible set
F
.

Theorem 4.9

(Characterization of the tangent cone)
.

Suppose that
{\mathbf{u}}\in F
is a feasible point.
Then

\tangentCone{F}{{\mathbf{u}}}=\setDef[big]{{\mathbf{d}}\in\R^{n_{V}}}{\norm{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}+\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{d}}}}_{0,\nu}\leq K}.

Proof.

We begin by showing the inclusion
\enquote
\subseteq
.
Let
{\mathbf{u}}\in F
and
{\mathbf{d}}\in\tangentCone{F}{{\mathbf{u}}}
.
By definition, there exist
\sequence{\tau}{k}\searrow 0
,
\sequence[big](){{\mathbf{u}}}{k}\subseteq F
with
\sequence{{\mathbf{u}}}{k}\to{\mathbf{u}}
and
{\mathbf{d}}=\lim_{k\to\infty}(\sequence{{\mathbf{u}}}{k}-{\mathbf{u}})/\sequence{\tau}{k}
.
As
\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}
depends continuously on
{\mathbf{u}}
, we obtain that
\sumOfSquaredDifferences{\sequence{{\mathbf{u}}}{k}}\to\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}
where
\norm{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{\sequence{{\mathbf{u}}}{k}}}_{0,\nu}\leq K
and
\norm{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}_{0,\nu}\leq K
.
By
\cref
lemma:convergent-nodal-values-detect-non-constant-cells, there exist
N_{1},N_{2}\in\N
such that

\displaystyle\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}

\displaystyle\subseteq\nonconstantsimplices{\sequence{{\mathbf{u}}}{k}}\quad

\displaystyle\text{for all }k\geq N_{1},

\displaystyle\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{d}}}

\displaystyle\subseteq\nonconstantsimplices[Big]{\frac{\sequence{{\mathbf{u}}}{k}-{\mathbf{u}}}{\sequence{\tau}{k}}}=\nonconstantsimplices{\sequence{{\mathbf{u}}}{k}-{\mathbf{u}}}\quad

\displaystyle\text{for all }k\geq N_{2}.

Additionally, with
\cref
Lem:supp, we obtain

\nonconstantsimplices{\sequence{{\mathbf{u}}}{k}-{\mathbf{u}}}\subseteq\nonconstantsimplices{\sequence{{\mathbf{u}}}{k}}\cup\nonconstantsimplices{-{\mathbf{u}}}=\nonconstantsimplices{\sequence{{\mathbf{u}}}{k}}\cup\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}=\nonconstantsimplices{\sequence{{\mathbf{u}}}{k}}

for all
k\geq N_{1}
.

Altogether, we have for all
k\geq N\coloneqq\max\set{N_{1},N_{2}}

\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}\cup\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{d}}}\;\subseteq\;\nonconstantsimplices{\sequence{{\mathbf{u}}}{k}}\cup\nonconstantsimplices{\sequence{{\mathbf{u}}}{k}-{\mathbf{u}}}\;\subseteq\;\nonconstantsimplices{\sequence{{\mathbf{u}}}{k}}

and hence

\norm{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}+\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{d}}}}_{0,\nu}\leq\norm{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{\sequence{{\mathbf{u}}}{k}}}_{0,\nu}\leq K.

We now proceed with the reverse inclusion
\enquote
\supseteq
.
Suppose that
{\mathbf{u}}\in F
and
{\mathbf{d}}\in\R^{n_{V}}
satisfies
\norm{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}+\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{d}}}}_{0,\nu}\leq K
.
We define
\sequence{\tau}{k}\coloneqq 1/k
and
\sequence{{\mathbf{u}}}{k}\coloneqq{\mathbf{u}}+\sequence{\tau}{k}{\mathbf{d}}
for all
k\in\N
.
By
\cref
Lem:supp, we then have for all
k\in\N

\nonconstantsimplices{\sequence{{\mathbf{u}}}{k}}=\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}+\sequence{\tau}{k}{\mathbf{d}}}\subseteq\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}\cup\nonconstantsimplices{\sequence{\tau}{k}{\mathbf{d}}}=\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}\cup\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{d}}}

and therefore

\norm{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{\sequence{{\mathbf{u}}}{k}}}_{0,\nu}\leq\norm{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}+\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{d}}}}_{0,\nu}\leq K.

This shows
{\mathbf{d}}\in\tangentCone{F}{{\mathbf{u}}}
.
∎

We now make the representation of the tangent cone more explicit.

Corollary 4.10
.

Suppose that
{\mathbf{u}}\in F
is a feasible point.
Then

(20)

\tangentCone{F}{{\mathbf{u}}}=\setDef[Big]{{\mathbf{d}}\in\R^{n_{V}}}{\norm{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}_{0,\nu}+\sum_{\mrep{i\in\constantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}\cap\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{d}}}}{}}\nu_{i}\leq K}.

In particular, for any
{\mathbf{d}}\in\tangentCone{F}{{\mathbf{u}}}
, we also have
-{\mathbf{d}}\in\tangentCone{F}{{\mathbf{u}}}
.
Therefore, the variational inequality (
19
) of B-stationarity is equivalent to the variational
equality

(21)

f^{\prime}({\mathbf{u}})\,{\mathbf{d}}=0\quad\text{for all }{\mathbf{d}}\in\tangentCone{F}{{\mathbf{u}}}.

Proof.

By
\cref
theorem:tangent-cone:description we know that

\tangentCone{F}{{\mathbf{u}}}=\setDef[big]{{\mathbf{d}}\in\R^{n_{V}}}{\norm{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}+\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{d}}}}_{0,\nu}\leq K}.

By definition of the weighted
\ell_{0}
-pseudo-norm, we have

\displaystyle\norm{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}+\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{d}}}}_{0,\nu}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mrep[l]{i\in\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}\cup\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{d}}}}{\hskip 28.45274pt}}\nu_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mrep[c]{i\in\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}}{\hskip 17.07164pt}}\nu_{i}+\sum_{\mrep[l]{i\in\constantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}\cap\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{d}}}}{\hskip 28.45274pt}}\nu_{i}

\displaystyle=\norm{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}_{0,\nu}+\sum_{\mrep[l]{i\in\constantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}\cap\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{d}}}}{\hskip 28.45274pt}}\nu_{i}.

Due to
\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{d}}}=\nonconstantsimplices{-{\mathbf{d}}}
, the proof is complete.
∎

The previous corollary shows that tangent directions
{\mathbf{d}}
are constrained only in terms of the volume of cells whose
{\mathbf{u}}
-vertex values are constant but whose
({\mathbf{u}}+{\mathbf{d}})
-vertex values are not.
Notice that a single vertex value of
{\mathbf{d}}
induces changes in the function
u_{h}+d_{h}
on all incident cells.
In order to connect the cell-based description (
20
) of the tangent cone with a vertex-based view, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 4.11

(Connected set of vertices, covered cells)
.

(1)

A subset
V\subseteq\set{1,\ldots,{n_{V}}}
of vertices is said to be connected if any two vertices in
V
are connected via edges visiting only vertices in
V
.

(2)

A cell
S_{i}
,
i\in\set{1,\ldots,{n_{S}}}
, is said to be covered by the subset of vertices
V\subseteq\set{1,\ldots,{n_{V}}}
if
\sigma_{V\leftarrow S}(i)\subseteq V
.
The set of all cells covered by
V
is denoted by

\coveredsimplices{V}\coloneqq\setDef[big]{i\in\set{1,\ldots,{n_{S}}}}{\sigma_{V\leftarrow S}(i)\subseteq V}.

As seen in (
20
), tangent directions
{\mathbf{d}}\in\tangentCone{F}{{\mathbf{u}}}
are characterized by conditions on the set of cells
\constantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}\cap\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{d}}}
.
The main result is now based on using these characterizations to obtain a vertex-based point of view on B-stationarity.
It turns out that the crucial tangent directions
{\mathbf{d}}
are binary vectors, which allow for the representation
\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{d}}}=\sigma_{S\leftarrow V}(V)\setminus\coveredsimplices{V}
with
V=\supp({\mathbf{d}})
.

Theorem 4.12

(Characterization of
B
-stationarity)
.

Suppose that
{\mathbf{u}}\in F
is a feasible point.
Then
{\mathbf{u}}
is a B-stationary point of (
14
) if and only if

(22)

\sum_{\ell\in V}\nabla f({\mathbf{u}})_{\ell}=0\quad\text{for all }V\in\cV({\mathbf{u}}),

where

\cV({\mathbf{u}})\coloneqq\setDef[bigg]{V\subseteq\set{1,\ldots,{n_{V}}}}{V\text{ is connected and }\norm{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}_{0,\nu}+\sum_{\mrep[r]{i\in\constantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}\cap\paren[auto](){\sigma_{S\leftarrow V}(V)\setminus\coveredsimplices{V}}}{}}\nu_{i}\leq K}.

Proof.

Suppose that
{\mathbf{u}}\in F
is feasible and B-stationary.
We first show that for any
V\in\cV({\mathbf{u}})
,

{\mathbf{d}}\coloneqq\sum_{\ell\in V}{\mathbf{e}}_{\ell}\in\tangentCone{F}{{\mathbf{u}}}

holds, where
{\mathbf{e}}_{\ell}
is the
\ell
-th standard basis vector in
\R^{n_{V}}
.
For such
{\mathbf{d}}
, a cell
S_{i}
belongs to
\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{d}}}
if and only if
S_{i}
intersects but is not covered by
V
,
\ie

\constantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}\cap\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{d}}}=\constantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}\cap\paren[big](){\sigma_{S\leftarrow V}(V)\setminus\coveredsimplices{V}}.

Due to
V\in\cV({\mathbf{u}})
and the description (
20
) of the tangent cone, we obtain
{\mathbf{d}}\in\tangentCone{F}{{\mathbf{u}}}
.

B
-stationarity (
21
) gives

\sum_{\ell\in V}\nabla f({\mathbf{u}})_{\ell}=f^{\prime}({\mathbf{u}})\,{\mathbf{d}}=0

and consequently, (
22
) holds.

Conversely, assume that
{\mathbf{u}}\in F
satisfies condition (
22
).
We have to show
f^{\prime}({\mathbf{u}})\,{\mathbf{d}}\geq 0
for all
{\mathbf{d}}\in\tangentCone{F}{{\mathbf{u}}}
.

Let
{\mathbf{d}}\in\tangentCone{F}{{\mathbf{u}}}
be arbitrary but fixed.
By
\cref
corollary:tangent-cone:explicit-description, we know that

\norm{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}_{0,\nu}+\sum_{\mrep{i\in\constantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}\cap\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{d}}}}{}}\nu_{i}\leq K.

Let us decompose
{\mathbf{d}}
into a linear combination of binary vectors
\sequence{{\mathbf{d}}}{k}\in\set{0,1}^{n_{V}}
whose support
\sequence{V}{k}
belongs to
\cV({\mathbf{u}})
.
To this end, select a vertex from the support of
{\mathbf{d}}
, and extend it to the maximal connected set
\sequence{V}{1}
of vertices of equal
{\mathbf{d}}
-value.
Repeat this process with the remaining vertices that are not already in such a maximally connected set until the support of
{\mathbf{d}}
is exhausted.

Analogously to

Theorem 4.18

.

Suppose that
\sequence{\rho}{k}>0
is a sequence such that

.

In each step of the DC algorithm, we choose a subgradient

First, we need to determine a way to find a subgradient
\sequence{{\mathbf{s}}}{k}
.

Lemma 4.19
.

Let
{\mathbf{u}}\in\R^{n_{V}}
,
K\in\interval[][]{0}{\mu(\Omega)}
and
I\subseteq\set{1,\ldots,{n_{S}}}
be such that the maximum in
\enclose[]{\vvvert}{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}{\vvvert}_{K,\nu}
is attained.
Then we have that

\norm{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}_{1,\nu}={\mathbf{u}}^{\transp}{\mathbf{M}}{\mathbf{u}},\qquad\enclose[]{\vvvert}{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}{\vvvert}_{K,\nu}={\mathbf{u}}^{\transp}{\mathbf{M}}_{I}{\mathbf{u}},

for the symmetric and positive semidefinite matrices
{\mathbf{M}}\coloneqq{\mathbf{D}}_{1}^{\transp}\diag\paren[auto](){{\mathbf{D}}_{2}^{\transp}\nu}{\mathbf{D}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{M}}_{I}\coloneqq{\mathbf{D}}_{1}^{\transp}\diag\paren[auto](){{\mathbf{D}}_{2}^{\transp}\chi_{I}\nu}\,{\mathbf{D}}_{1}
in
\R^{{n_{V}}\times{n_{V}}}
.
Here
\chi_{I}\nu\in\R^{n_{S}}
is defined componentwise via
(\chi_{I}\nu)_{i}\coloneqq\chi_{I}(i)\,\nu_{i}
for the indicator function
\chi_{I}
of
I
with values in
\set{0,1}
.

Proof.

To see the right-hand identity, note that

\displaystyle\enclose[]{\vvvert}{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}{\vvvert}_{K,\nu}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I}\nu_{i}({\mathbf{D}}_{2}\paren[big][]{({\mathbf{D}}_{1}{\mathbf{u}})\odot({\mathbf{D}}_{1}{\mathbf{u}})})_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{S}}\chi_{I}(i)\,\nu_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{E}}({\mathbf{D}}_{2})_{i,j}({\mathbf{D}}_{1}{\mathbf{u}})_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{E}}({\mathbf{D}}_{1}{\mathbf{u}})_{j}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{S}}({\mathbf{D}}_{2})_{i,j}\chi_{I}(i)\,\nu_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{E}}({\mathbf{D}}_{1}{\mathbf{u}})_{j}^{2}\,({\mathbf{D}}_{2}^{\transp}\chi_{I}\nu)_{j}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{u}}^{\transp}{\mathbf{M}}_{I}{\mathbf{u}}.

By definition,
{\mathbf{M}}_{I}
is symmetric.
Moreover, its positive semidefiniteness follows from
\enclose[]{\vvvert}{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}{\vvvert}_{K,\nu}\geq 0
for all
{\mathbf{u}}\in\R^{n_{V}}
, showing the right-hand identity for arbitrary
K\in\interval[][]{0}{\mu(\Omega)}
and corresponding
I
.
The left-hand claim for
\norm{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}_{1,\nu}
and
{\mathbf{M}}
follows from the previous result by setting
K=\mu(\Omega)
with corresponding
I=\set{1,\ldots,{n_{S}}}
.
∎

With the previous result, we can provide a full characterization of the subdifferential in question.

Theorem 4.20

(Characterization of the subdifferential
\partial\enclose[]{\vvvert}{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{\cdot}}{\vvvert}_{K,\nu}

.

Let
{\mathbf{u}}\in\R^{n_{V}}
.
Then we have

\partial\enclose[]{\vvvert}{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{\cdot}}{\vvvert}_{K,\nu}({\mathbf{u}})\\
=\conv\setDef[Big]{2\,{\mathbf{M}}_{I}{\mathbf{u}}}{I\subseteq\set{1,\ldots,{n_{S}}},\;\sum_{i\in I}\nu_{i}\leq K,\;\sum_{i\in I}\nu_{i}\,\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}_{i}=\enclose[]{\vvvert}{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}{\vvvert}_{K,\nu}}.

Moreover,

(25)

2\,{\mathbf{D}}_{1}^{\transp}\paren[auto](){{\mathbf{D}}_{2}^{\transp}{\mathbf{r}}\odot{\mathbf{D}}_{1}{\mathbf{u}}}\in\partial\enclose[]{\vvvert}{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{\cdot}}{\vvvert}_{K,\nu}({\mathbf{u}})

holds for any

, where

still denotes componentwise multiplication.

Proof.

By

\enclose[]{\vvvert}{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}{\vvvert}_{K,\nu}=\max\setDef[Big]{{\mathbf{u}}^{\transp}{\mathbf{M}}_{I}{\mathbf{u}}}{I\subseteq\set{1,\ldots,{n_{S}}}\text{ \st\ }\sum_{i\in I}\nu_{i}\leq K}

Hence,
\enclose[]{\vvvert}{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}{\vvvert}_{K,\nu}

The second claim also follows from the chain rule for the convex subdifferential, see

\displaystyle=\paren[auto](){2\,{\mathbf{D}}_{2}\paren[big][]{\diag({\mathbf{D}}_{1}{\mathbf{u}})\,{\mathbf{D}}_{1}}}^{\transp}{\mathbf{r}}

theorem:B-stationarity:explicit-description and the following results in
\cref
subsection:optimality-conditions, but is typically weaker in the sense that strong criticality is not equivalent to the primal notion of B-stationarity.

Theorem 4.23

(Characterization of
B
-stationarity in the strongly critical case)
.

Let
{\mathbf{u}}
be a strongly critical point of
f_{\rho}

\wrt
the natural DC decomposition in (
18
) and let
I\subseteq\set{1,\ldots,{n_{S}}}
be an index set that realizes the maximum in
\enclose[]{\vvvert}{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}{\vvvert}_{K,\nu}
that we assume
\wolog
to satisfy
I\subseteq\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}
.
Then the following are true:

(1)

statement]item:discrete-optimality-conditions:penalized:6

\sum_{\ell\in V}\nabla f({\mathbf{u}})_{\ell}=0
for all
V\subseteq\set{1,\ldots,{n_{V}}}
such that

\sigma_{S\leftarrow V}(V)\setminus\coveredsimplices{V}\subseteq I.

(2)

statement]item:discrete-optimality-conditions:penalized:4

\nabla f({\mathbf{u}})_{\ell}=0
for all
\ell\in\set{1,\ldots,{n_{V}}}
such that
\sigma_{S\leftarrow V}(\ell)\subseteq I
.

(3)

statement]item:discrete-optimality-conditions:penalized:5

\nabla f({\mathbf{u}})_{\ell}=0
for all
\ell\in\set{1,\ldots,{n_{V}}}
such that
\sigma_{S\leftarrow V}(\ell)\subseteq\constantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}
.

Proof.

Note that we can assume that
I\subseteq\nonconstantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}
because
\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}_{i}=0
for all
i\in\constantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}
by definition.

Since
{\mathbf{u}}
is a strongly critical point,
\cref
lemma:general-problem:discrete:penalized:multiplier yields

(29)

\displaystyle-\nabla f({\mathbf{u}})=\lambda=2\,\rho\,({\mathbf{M}}-{\mathbf{M}}_{I})\,{\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle=2\,\rho\,{\mathbf{D}}_{1}^{\transp}\diag\paren[big](){{\mathbf{D}}_{2}^{\transp}\nu-{\mathbf{D}}_{2}^{\transp}\chi_{I}\nu}\,{\mathbf{D}}_{1}{\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle=2\,\rho\,{\mathbf{D}}_{1}^{\transp}\diag\paren[big](){{\mathbf{D}}_{2}^{\transp}\chi_{{I}^{c}}\nu}\,{\mathbf{D}}_{1}{\mathbf{u}}.

Written in a nodal (
\ie
, componentwise) sense, this is exactly the statement that

(30)

-\nabla f({\mathbf{u}})_{\ell}=2\,\rho\sum_{j=1}^{n_{E}}({\mathbf{D}}_{1})_{j,\ell}\paren[auto](){{\mathbf{D}}_{2}^{\transp}\chi_{{I}^{c}}\nu}_{j}({\mathbf{D}}_{1}{\mathbf{u}})_{j}\quad\text{for all }\ell=1,\ldots,{n_{V}}.

\Cref

item:discrete-optimality-conditions:penalized:6:
Let
V\subseteq\set{1,\ldots,{n_{V}}}
with
\sigma_{S\leftarrow V}(V)\setminus\coveredsimplices{V}\subseteq I
be given.
We define

J\coloneqq\setDef[auto]{j\in\set{1,\ldots,{n_{E}}}}{\#(V\cap\sigma_{V\leftarrow E}(j))=1}

and note that

\sigma_{S\leftarrow E}(J)=\sigma_{S\leftarrow V}(V)\setminus\coveredsimplices{V},

because these are exactly the simplices that have at least one but not all vertices in
V
.

Using the definitions of
{\mathbf{D}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{D}}_{2}
, we obtain that

\sum_{l\in V}({\mathbf{D}}_{1})_{j,\ell}=0\text{ for all }j\in\set{1,\ldots,{n_{E}}}\setminus J\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{i\in{I}^{c}}({\mathbf{D}}_{2})_{i,j}\nu_{i}=0\text{ for all }j\in J,

where the left-hand sum is either a sum of zeros or a sum of zeros and
\pm 1
, and where in the right-hand sum, the entries
({\mathbf{D}}_{2})_{i,j}
are all zero because the edges
j\in J
are exactly the ones where the simplices these edges are incident to are in
\sigma_{S\leftarrow V}(V)\setminus\coveredsimplices{V}
and therefore in
I
.

Using (
30
), we therefore have that

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\,\rho}\sum_{l\in V}\nabla f({\mathbf{u}})_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l\in V}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{E}}({\mathbf{D}}_{1})_{j,\ell}\paren[big](){{\mathbf{D}}_{2}^{\transp}\chi_{{I}^{c}}\nu}_{j}({\mathbf{D}}_{1}{\mathbf{u}})_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{E}}\paren[Big](){\sum_{l\in V}({\mathbf{D}}_{1})_{j,\ell}}\paren[Big](){\sum_{i\in{I}^{c}}({\mathbf{D}}_{2})_{i,j}\nu_{i}}({\mathbf{D}}_{1}{\mathbf{u}})_{j}

\displaystyle=0.

\Cref

item:discrete-optimality-conditions:penalized:4 is an immediate consequence of
\cref
item:discrete-optimality-conditions:penalized:6.

\Cref

item:discrete-optimality-conditions:penalized:5:
Let
\ell\in\set{1,\ldots,{n_{V}}}
with
\sigma_{S\leftarrow V}(\ell)\subseteq\constantsimplices{{\mathbf{u}}}
.
Then, by definition,
({\mathbf{D}}_{1}{\mathbf{u}})_{j}=0
for all
j\in\sigma_{E\leftarrow V}(\ell)
.
Because
({\mathbf{D}}_{1})_{j,\ell}=0
for every
j\in\set{1,\ldots,{n_{E}}}\setminus\sigma_{E\leftarrow V}(\ell)
, this implies
({\mathbf{D}}_{1})_{j,\ell}({\mathbf{D}}_{1}{\mathbf{u}})_{j}=0
for every
j\in\set{1,\ldots,{n_{E}}}
.
Again, (
30
) implies
\nabla f({\mathbf{u}})_{\ell}=0
.
∎

Note that since the above result states optimality conditions that were generated using penalization techniques, there is no direct connection to feasibility of the points in question coming into play.
This becomes especially apparent when comparing
\cref
theorem:discrete-optimality-conditions:penalized
to the nodal characterization of B-stationarity for the discretized non-penalized problem, see
\cref
theorem:B-stationarity:explicit-description, which has similar vanishing average gradient structure and involves the same set of non-covered cells but where the information is given for differently structured subsets of vertices.
Additionally, we have the following result.

Corollary 4.24
.

Let
{\mathbf{u}}
be a strongly critical point of
f_{\rho}

\wrt
the natural DC decomposition in (
18
) such that
\norm{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}_{0,\nu}\leq K
.
Then
\nabla f({\mathbf{u}})=0
.

Proof.

If
\norm{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}_{0,\nu}\leq K
, then
{\mathbf{u}}^{\transp}({\mathbf{M}}-{\mathbf{M}}_{I})\,{\mathbf{u}}=\norm{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}_{1,\nu}-\enclose[]{\vvvert}{\sumOfSquaredDifferences{{\mathbf{u}}}}{\vvvert}_{K,\nu}=0
and analogously to (
29
) and (
30
) we therefore have that

(31)

0={\mathbf{u}}^{\transp}({\mathbf{M}}-{\mathbf{M}}_{I})\,{\mathbf{u}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{E}}\paren[auto](){{\mathbf{D}}_{2}^{\transp}\chi_{{I}^{c}}\nu}_{j}({\mathbf{D}}_{1}{\mathbf{u}})_{j}^{2},

\ie

,
\paren[auto](){{\mathbf{D}}_{2}^{\transp}\chi_{{I}^{c}}\nu}_{j}({\mathbf{D}}_{1}{\mathbf{u}})_{j}=0
for all
j=1,\ldots,{n_{E}}
.
Then
({\mathbf{M}}-{\mathbf{M}}_{I})\,{\mathbf{u}}=0
and (
29
) yields the claim.
∎

This result implies that, generally, exact penalization is impossible in the sense that a solution of the penalized problem (
18
) for any
\rho>0
is never a solution of the original problem (
14
), unless there exists an optimizer that is not impactedby the constraint.
Since
B
-stationarity requires feasibility, this also shows that, except for the cases mentioned above, strongly critical points for
f_{\rho}

\wrt
the natural DC decomposition in (
18
) are not B-stationary for (
14
).

In this section, we consider the following problem over
U\coloneqq H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

(32)

\displaystyle\text{Minimize}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\norm{\nabla u}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}-\int_{\Omega}g\,u\d{\mu}\quad\text{where }u\in U

\displaystyle\text{\st}

\displaystyle\norm{\nabla u}_{0}\leq K,

where we choose
\Omega\coloneqq\interval(){0}{1}^{2}
and

g(x,y)\coloneqq 10\,x\,\sin(5\,x)\,\sin(7\,y).

Note that the choice of
U
slightly violates
\cref
assumption:standing-assumptions
2
.
Choosing
U
as a space with Sobolev regularity greater than one in turn would not meet
\cref

3
.
Finally, using corresponding higher order regularization terms would be in conflict with the chosen piecewise affine and continuous discretization scheme (
11
).
Nevertheless, the discretized problem of (
32
) is of the general form (
14
), satisfies
\cref

Numerically, we incorporate the boundary conditions directly into the discretization and let

and

denote the number of interior vertices and the number of simplices with at least one interior vertex, respectively.
Rows and columns associated with quantities on the boundary are removed from

and

The associated discretized and penalized problem is then of the general form (

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad{\mathbf{b}}

5.4.
Optimal Control Problem

In this section, we additionally consider the exemplary optimal control problem

(37)

\displaystyle\text{Minimize}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\norm{y-y_{d}}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\norm{\nabla u}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\quad\text{where }u,y\in U

\displaystyle\text{\st}

\displaystyle-\Delta y=u\text{ on }\Omega,\quad y=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega,\quad\norm{\nabla u}_{0}\leq K,

where we choose again
\Omega\coloneqq\interval(){0}{1}^{2}
and
U=H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
.
Furthermore, we set
\alpha=10^{-5}
and use the target state

y_{d}(x,y)\coloneqq\frac{1}{6}\,\sin(8\,\pi\,x)\,\sin(8\,\pi\,y)\,\exp(2\,x).

Note again that this problem cannot be proven to be solvable via standard existence proofs.
Nevertheless, the associated discretized problem is again solvable and of the general form (
18
) and can hence be tackled by
\cref
algorithm:DC-algorithm.

y_{d}
