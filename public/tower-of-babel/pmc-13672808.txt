Views and conditional acceptance of telehealth and artificial intelligence for lifestyle modification among individuals with prediabetes: A qualitative descriptive study
Authors: Arkers Kwan Ching Wong, Chung Hei Dye, Menghan Wang, Jonathan Bayuo, Aoxi Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrediabetes is associated with an increased risk of progression to type 2 diabetes, yet sustained lifestyle modification remains difficult in everyday life. Although telehealth and artificial intelligence may extend lifestyle support through remote access, monitoring, reminders and personalised feedback, little is known about how people with prediabetes perceive these technologies or the conditions under which they would consider them acceptable.ObjectiveTo explore how adults with prediabetes understand and manage lifestyle modification and to identify their perceived support needs and the conditions shaping acceptance of telehealth and artificial intelligence as adjuncts to lifestyle support.DesignA qualitative descriptive study.Setting(s)Hong Kong.Participants10 adults with prediabetes.MethodsSemi-structured interviews were conducted between September and October 2025. Interviews were conducted in Cantonese, audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, translated into English, and analysed using thematic analysis. Demographic, clinical, and digital health-related information was collected to contextualise participants’ accounts.ResultsFive interrelated themes were identified. Participants’ perceived risk of prediabetes was shaped less by the diagnosis itself than by family history, bodily symptoms, and personal health biographies. Although participants generally understood the need for dietary change, physical activity, and weight control, they found it difficult to translate knowledge into sustained action because of work demands, food environments, fatigue, emotional distress, and limited control over daily routines. Lifestyle modification was, therefore, approached pragmatically through modest, incremental, and sustainable adjustments rather than strict behavioural transformation. Meaningful support was perceived as ongoing, human-centred, and responsive to individual circumstances. Within this context, telehealth and artificial intelligence were viewed as potentially-acceptable extensions of care, particularly for accessible information access, reminders, self-monitoring, and personalised feedback. However, acceptability was conditional on accuracy, privacy protection, cultural relevance, professional credibility, and continued clinician involvement.ConclusionsTelehealth and artificial intelligence may help support lifestyle modification among individuals with prediabetes, but their value depends on their ability to complement rather than human care. Artificial intelligence-enabled telehealth interventions should be designed as hybrid, clinician-integrated models that translate health data into locally relevant and actionable guidance while preserving trust, accountability, and human reassurance. Such approaches may help bridge the persistent gap between knowing what lifestyle changes are needed and sustaining those changes in everyday life for individuals with prediabetes in Hong Kong.RegistrationNot registered.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Hong Kong, Lifestyle modification, Prediabetes, Qualitative descriptive study, Telehealth

Introduction

Background

Prediabetes is characterised by blood glucose levels that are elevated above the normal range but below diagnostic thresholds for type 2 diabetes, including impaired fasting glucose and impaired glucose tolerance (DiabetesCare, 2026). It is a major global public health concern. In 2021, an estimated 464 million adults had impaired glucose tolerance and 298 million had impaired fasting glucose, with projected increases to 638 million and 414 million, respectively, by 2045(Rooney et al., 2023). Prediabetes is also clinically important because a substantial proportion of affected individuals will progress to type 2 diabetes over their lifetime, with associated risks of cardiovascular disease, microvascular complications, and premature mortality(Carris et al., 2019, Duan et al., 2021). Lifestyle modification, particularly dietary change, physical activity, and weight management, is, therefore, recommended as a core strategy to prevent or delay diabetes progression (Cosentino et al., 2020, Magkos et al., 2020, Mahat et al., 2019).

Despite strong evidence supporting lifestyle modification, sustained adherence remains difficult for many people with prediabetes. Researchers have identified knowledge deficits, limited perceived seriousness, insufficient professional guidance, time pressure, family and work responsibilities, and food-environment constraints as barriers to lifestyle change(Macdonald and Campbell, 2017, Rai et al., 2023, Wang et al., 2023a). Importantly, these barriers do not operate only at the level of individual knowledge or motivation. For many people, the main challenge lies in converting broad recommendations, such as “eat healthily” or “exercise more”, into feasible practices within busy, socially embedded, and culturally-specific daily routines. Understanding how people with prediabetes experience these barriers is essential for designing support that is acceptable, realistic, and sustainable.

Telehealth and artificial intelligence (AI)-enabled tools are increasingly recognised as important components of digital health technologies that may extend lifestyle support beyond conventional consultations. Telehealth refers to the remote delivery of health services, education, monitoring, and follow-up through electronic information and communication technologies(Petrazzuoli et al., 2021, Wang et al., 2023b, Wong et al., 2025a). AI refers to computational systems capable of performing tasks that usually require human intelligence, including learning, prediction, language processing, and decision support(Gignac and Szodorai, 2024, Pool et al., 2024, Rossi and Rehman, 2025). When integrated into telehealth platforms, AI may support remote monitoring, personalised education, risk alerts, automated feedback, and adaptive recommendations based on user-generated data(Dong et al., 2025, Fitzner et al., 2014, Mannoubi et al., 2024). These functions are potentially relevant to prediabetes because lifestyle change often requires repeated feedback, contextualised decision support, and continuity between formal healthcare encounters.

However, these potential benefits of telehealth and AI should not be assumed to translate automatically into acceptance or sustained use. Digital health interventions are most likely to be effective when users perceive them as useful, trustworthy, actionable, and compatible with their values and everyday lives (Figueiredo et al., 2025, Wong et al., 2025b). Researchers have examined digital health interventions primarily among people with established diabetes, with more recent studies exploring AI-supported self-management, such as embodied conversational agents (Baptista et al., 2020, Gamble et al., 2023, Ji et al., 2025, Ritchie and Turk, 2024). Individuals with prediabetes may differ meaningfully from those with diagnosed diabetes: they may be asymptomatic, less convinced of the urgency of intervention, and more ambivalent about intensive self-management(Harcke et al., 2025). Consequently, findings from diabetes populations cannot be transferred directly to people with prediabetes.

In Hong Kong, where primary care, district health services, high smartphone penetration, and AI-enabled information seeking increasingly intersect, it is particularly important to understand how people with prediabetes make sense of telehealth and AI for lifestyle support. To date, limited qualitative evidence has examined their conditional acceptance of such technologies, especially in relation to their lived experiences of risk, adherence, and support needs. We addressed this gap by exploring how adults with prediabetes in Hong Kong perceived the use of telehealth and AI to support lifestyle modification.

Aim and objectives

This qualitative descriptive study aimed to explore how adults diagnosed with prediabetes in Hong Kong perceived and accepted the use of telehealth and AI in supporting lifestyle modification, with attention to their experiences of living with prediabetes, their challenges in sustaining lifestyle change, and the forms of support they considered meaningful. The objectives were to:

1.explore participants’ experiences of living with prediabetes and the challenges they face in undertaking and sustaining lifestyle modification;

explore participants’ experiences of living with prediabetes and the challenges they face in undertaking and sustaining lifestyle modification;

2.examine the types of support that people with prediabetes perceive as meaningful for sustaining adherence to lifestyle modification; and

examine the types of support that people with prediabetes perceive as meaningful for sustaining adherence to lifestyle modification; and

3.investigate participants’ perceptions and conditional acceptance of telehealth and AI as tools to support lifestyle modification.

Methods

Study design

A qualitative descriptive design was used, following Sandelowski’s approach to generating rich, practice-relevant accounts of participants’ experiences, perceptions, and preferences(Neergaard et al., 2009, Sandelowski, 2000). This design was appropriate because we sought to produce an accessible and clinically useful description of how people with prediabetes understand lifestyle modification and the potential role of telehealth and AI in supporting self-management.

Participants and recruitment

Participants were recruited through social media platforms and district health centres in Hong Kong. A purposive convenience sampling strategy was used to identify adults who met the eligibility criteria and could provide information-rich accounts relevant to the study aim(Patton, 2014). Participants were eligible if they were aged 18 years or above, able to communicate in Cantonese, and had been diagnosed with prediabetes. Individuals were excluded if they did not have access to a smartphone, had hearing or visual impairment that would prevent interview participation, or had a diagnosis of traumatic brain injury, dementia, intellectual disability, severe mental illness, or substance abuse involving alcohol or drugs.

Data collection

Ten semi-structured interviews were conducted by CD between September and October 2025. Interviews lasted approximately 30–50 min and were conducted in Cantonese. A semi-structured interview guide comprising broad and open-ended questions with optional follow-up prompts was developed to promote consistency while allowing participants to describe their experiences in depth(Kallio et al., 2016). The guide covered seven domains: knowledge of prediabetes; knowledge of prediabetes management; experiences and challenges related to lifestyle modification; previous experience and general impressions of telehealth and AI; perceptions and acceptance of telehealth and AI for lifestyle modification; anticipated difficulties in using telehealth- or AI-supported lifestyle programmes; and recommendations for implementation. The first three domains established participants’ self-management context, whereas the remaining domains focused on participants’ experiences with health technologies, views on telehealth and AI, and implementation preferences. Telehealth- and AI-related questions were deliberately introduced only after participants had discussed their experiences of living with prediabetes and lifestyle modification to minimise the risk of directing them towards technology-focused responses before they had described their own experiences and support needs. The complete semi-structured interview guide is provided in the Supplementary Material, appendix A.

With participants’ consent, interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. The de-identified Cantonese transcripts were subsequently translated into English to facilitate the participation of research team members who were not fluent in Cantonese in coding, theme development, and interpretation of the findings(Schumann et al., 2025). Transcription and translation were undertaken by bilingual members of the research team who were fluent in both Cantonese and English, thereby ensuring that participants’ meanings were accurately preserved throughout the analytic process. Basic demographic, clinical, and digital health-related information was collected at recruitment using a brief questionnaire. This included sociodemographic background, prediabetes-related clinical information, comorbidities, family history, living arrangements, use of smartphones and internet, experience with telehealth- or AI-related applications, and perceived usefulness of digital health technologies for blood glucose management.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using the six-phase thematic analysis process described by Braun and Clarke(Braun and Clarke, 2006). The research team first read and reread the transcripts to become familiar with the data and documented initial analytic observations. Meaningful segments of text were then coded systematically using QSR NVivo 14. CD conducted the initial coding of all transcripts. A code–recode strategy (Miles and Huberman, 1984) was used, whereby two transcripts were recoded after approximately 3 weeks and compared with the initial coding to refine the coding framework. MW then independently coded two transcripts using the refined framework, and the two researchers discussed coding discrepancies until they reached consensus. Codes were then compared across transcripts to identify similarities, differences, and recurring patterns. Related codes were grouped into candidate themes, which were reviewed against the coded extracts and the full dataset before being defined and named. Themes were selected for their relevance to the study objectives and their capacity to explain how participants’ experiences of prediabetes shaped their acceptance of telehealth and AI. Illustrative quotations were selected to preserve participants’ meanings. After the tenth interview, no additional major themes relevant to the study objectives were identified. Consistent with the concept of information power(Malterud et al., 2016), the sample was considered to provide sufficient information to address the focused aims of this qualitative descriptive study. Throughout this process, weekly analysis review meetings were held to discuss coding decisions, examine alternative interpretations, and refine the developing analysis, and AKCW reviewed the coding framework, thematic development, and interpretation.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the study university (approval no. HSEARS20241013002). All participants received information about the study objectives, procedures, voluntary nature of participation, confidentiality arrangements, and their right to withdraw at any time without consequence. Written informed consent was obtained before data collection. Interview recordings and transcripts were stored securely in encrypted online storage and password-protected databases accessible only to authorised research personnel(Kaiser, 2009). Although no major risks were anticipated, participants were offered scheduled and additional breaks during interviews to minimise fatigue. On completion of the interview, each participant received an HKD 100 supermarket voucher to compensate for their time and transportation expenses(Largent and Lynch, 2017).

Trustworthiness

Trustworthiness was supported using Lincoln and Guba’s criteria of credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability(Creswell and Miller, 2000, Lincoln and Guba, 1985). Credibility was strengthened through peer debriefing with nursing students and faculty members experienced in qualitative research but not directly involved in data collection. These discussions examined the research procedures, coding decisions, developing themes, and possible alternative interpretations. Conducting interviews in Cantonese also enabled participants to express their views naturally and in their own language. Transferability was enhanced by providing detailed descriptions of the study context, participant characteristics, and digital health engagement. Dependability was supported through an audit trail documenting recruitment, data handling, coding, analytic decisions, and theme development. Dependability was further supported through the code–recode strategy and independent coding of selected transcripts, with coding discrepancies resolved through consensus discussion(Miles and Huberman, 1984). Confirmability was strengthened through reflexive journaling, which documented the interviewer’s assumptions, decisions, and potential biases throughout the study.

Researcher reflexivity

The interviewer was a 4th-year nursing male student with 1 year of clinical experience. Prior to data collection, the interviewer received training in qualitative interviewing from senior members of the research team, including training on the study objectives, use of the semi-structured interview guide, interviewing techniques, and the use of probing questions. This background provided contextual understanding of the Hong Kong healthcare system and common challenges related to lifestyle modification. At the same time, the interviewer recognised that clinical training could introduce assumptions about how participants should understand risk, follow advice, or respond to telehealth and AI. A reflexive journal was maintained to document these assumptions and to support critical reflection throughout data collection and analysis. Regular discussions with supervisors and peers helped the interviewer remain attentive to preconceptions, particularly the possibility of overestimating participants’ comfort with AI and telehealth or interpreting their decisions primarily through a clinical lens.

Themes

Five interrelated themes were generated from the interviews. A table showing the relationship between representative analytical codes and these five themes is provided in Supplementary Material, appendix B. Together, they show that participants’ acceptance of telehealth and AI was grounded in their lived experience of prediabetes. These technologies were not evaluated in isolation; rather, they were assessed in relation to perceived risk, everyday barriers to lifestyle modification, expectations of support, and the perceived need for professional trust and human reassurance.

Theme 1. Risk meaning-making was shaped by family and personal context, not diagnosis alone

Participants commonly understood prediabetes as an early warning sign, but the meaning and emotional weight of the diagnosis varied considerably. Risk perception was shaped less by the diagnostic label alone than by family history, bodily symptoms, previous health experiences, and coexisting conditions, such as obesity, hypertension, fatty liver disease, stress, or polycystic ovary syndrome. For some, family experiences made diabetes highly visible and personally threatening:

“My family has diabetes – on my dad’s side: my aunt, my dad, basically all have diabetes. So I’m high risk.” (PD07)

Other participants came to recognise the relevance of prediabetes only after connecting it retrospectively with symptoms or related health problems. One participant described being prompted to test for diabetes after a dental consultation:

“The dentist said my periodontal disease was quite severe and that diabetes can lead to periodontal disease, so I should get checked.” (PD08)

These contextual frames shaped emotional responses. Some participants expressed fear because diabetes was associated with severe complications, functional decline, or experiences among relatives. Others perceived prediabetes as less urgent because the result was expected, mildly abnormal, or potentially reversible. The same diagnosis, therefore, produced different degrees of concern and different levels of motivation for change.

“I was scared. Diabetes is a troublesome disease. These chronic conditions can damage your organs. It can lead to blindness, amputations, and other complications.” (PD01)

“Because I had already seen my HbA1c and expected it to be higher than normal, I didn’t really feel much – it was what I anticipated.” (PD02)

Consequently, prediabetes functioned as a strong catalyst for lifestyle change for some participants, but for others it remained a borderline condition that could be monitored without major disruption. This variation in perceived urgency provided an important context for understanding participants’ subsequent views of telehealth and AI presented in the following themes.

Theme 2. Participants knew what was recommended but struggled to translate knowledge into action

Most participants described being aware of common lifestyle recommendations, including reducing sugar intake, limiting carbohydrates, eating less takeout food, increasing physical activity, and managing body weight. However, their accounts showed that knowledge was not equivalent to capacity for sustained action. Participants described lifestyle modification as something negotiated within work schedules, family routines, food environments, physical symptoms, emotional states, and competing responsibilities.

“We may know what to do but still can’t do it. For example, takeout might be the reason – maybe we really can’t cook at home or the kitchen setup isn’t suitable.” (PD02)

“It’s not that you don’t know what to eat and what not to eat, but that work and the environment limit you.” (PD12)

Work was a particularly prominent constraint because it reduced time, energy, and flexibility for both dietary regulation and exercise. Some participants described prolonged sitting, commuting, fatigue, and lack of control over meal options as barriers that made health advice difficult to enact in practice.

“You really can’t get any exercise while you’re at work… And to actually go do things like exercise, swimming, or walking – those actually require you to carve out time from your work.” (PD03)

Food practices were also shaped by context rather than individual preference alone. Carbohydrate-rich foods were familiar, filling, affordable, and readily available in everyday Hong Kong eating environments. Emotional distress further complicated eating practices, as one participant explained:

“When I’m in a bad mood, cooking feels like such a hassle… sometimes when I’m in a bad mood, I’ll cook noodles at home. I end up making myself a big bowl of carbs that I really shouldn’t be eating.” (PD02)

Although some participants used individualised language, such as laziness, poor discipline, or weak determination, to describe their struggles, their narratives pointed to a more complex pattern. What appeared as personal failure was often intertwined with shift work, fatigue, food availability, cost, pain, shame about exercising in public, and limited sustained support. This finding indicates that future interventions should consider addressing the practical conditions under which lifestyle decisions are made, rather than relying only on education or motivation.

Theme 3. Self-management was incremental, practical, and oriented toward sustainability

Participants generally described self-management as a set of modest, practical adjustments rather than an intensive or highly structured regimen. Strategies included reducing sugar-sweetened drinks, limiting desserts and snacks, controlling portions, eating more carefully, pairing carbohydrates with protein, cooking at home more often, walking after meals, and choosing manageable forms of exercise, such as walking, running, or swimming. These strategies reflected an orientation toward changes that could be integrated into daily life and sustained over time.

“Before, I might have just eaten a cookie to avoid a bigger meal, or if I’d had bread, I’d just eat bread. Now I don’t dare eat bread on its own anymore; I have to have some protein with it, like tuna or an egg.” (PD04)

Exercise was recognised as important, but participants emphasised that the activity had to be realistic. For some, overly ambitious plans were difficult to maintain, whereas modest routines felt more achievable:

“But I found that if my physical strength couldn’t handle it, I would collapse after one day and then not continue. By contrast, an evening walk is something I can do and actually enjoy, so I can do it every day.” (PD10)

Several participants also described a narrow or selective approach to self-management, focusing primarily on diet rather than holistic lifestyle change:

“I don’t really manage my blood sugar in a holistic way. I only pay more attention to my diet.” (PD12)

Motivations for self-management were similarly pragmatic. Participants wanted to avoid medication, prevent progression to diabetes, reduce symptoms, keep blood glucose stable, or avoid repeating family experiences of diabetes-related complications. One participant summarised this practical motivation simply:

“There’s no other way. I don’t want to take medicine.” (PD06)

This theme helps provide important context for understanding participants’ subsequent views of telehealth and AI. Rather than looking for intensive programmes that radically reorganised their lives, participants valued support that could scaffold small, sustainable changes through reminders, interpretation, feedback, and accountability embedded into daily routines.

Theme 4. Meaningful support required continuity, human connection, and contextual tailoring

Participants consistently distinguished between receiving information and receiving meaningful support. One-off consultations or generic advice, such as being told to “control diet and exercise”, were often described as insufficient when not followed by interpretation, encouragement, and ongoing review. Participants valued support that continued beyond screening or diagnosis.

“They basically just told you the results, gave you the report, and that was it. There was no follow-up. If the person doesn’t take it seriously and continue with it on their own, then nothing happens.” (PD10)

The desired support was not merely technical. Participants wanted close follow-up from doctors or nurses, nearby community or district centre services, free or subsidised programmes, structured activities, and guidance that responded to the individual’s own circumstances.

“You really need close follow-up… following the whole process and checking in.” (PD02)

“It’s not just the meal plan. It’s that they understand my specific difficulties and then give suggestions accordingly.” (PD10)

The human element was central to participants’ sense of trust. Face-to-face interaction was described as more real, reassuring, and emotionally supportive, particularly when health concerns felt serious or when participants needed motivation. Participants associated human professionals with empathy, accountability, and authority.

“It feels more real - seeing a real person.” (PD07)

“She really cares; it feels heartwarming.” (PD08)

Several participants also emphasised the continued importance of clinicians in filtering, interpreting, and personalising information. Even when digital tools were considered useful, participants wanted professional oversight to ensure that advice was clinically appropriate and relevant to their own situation.

“I would still need to see the doctor once every season… a doctor can narrow them down for the patient and give more suitable advice.” (PD12)

Theme 5. Telehealth and AI were acceptable as extensions of care, not replacements for clinicians

Against the background of limited follow-up and everyday difficulty sustaining lifestyle change, participants generally viewed telehealth and AI as potentially useful adjuncts. Although participants attributed distinct roles to telehealth and AI, both technologies were perceived as extensions of care rather than replacements for clinicians.

Participants valued telehealth because it enabled healthcare professionals to remain involved beyond routine clinic visits. Previous experience with such services as video consultations was perceived as facilitating remote follow-up when it was difficult to maintain in-person interactions with professionals.

“Video consultations: I used them during COVID because I couldn’t see a doctor. I had experiences with video consults with Chinese medicine practitioners and Western doctors.” (PD07)

Some participants also described how telehealth allowed health information to be shared remotely between patients and healthcare professionals, making follow-up more efficient.

“You can show it to the doctor or nurse. There’s also a way to share your data through the account, so everyone can access it to monitor you. So when the nurse WhatsApp me earlier, she could see there was a problem with my data.” (PD08)

Beyond remote communication, participants also valued the digital functions of telehealth platforms, which increased convenience.

“If there were an app that could remind me making appointments or checking medication records or online consultations […] it was very convenient, because I don’t need to look for my follow-up slips.” (PD12)

Unlike telehealth, the perceived value of AI lay in its ability to obtain interim guidance between formal healthcare encounters.

“And there’s AI, too. It’s openly available and very convenient. If you don’t understand something, you can just ask it.” (PD01)

Participants also valued AI for helping them consolidate scattered information and make self-education less burdensome.

“There’s so much information online, and you don’t know what’s true or false. It’s better to ask an AI. It will give you a consolidated answer.” (PD01)

Beyond information retrieval, the most valued functions of AI were those that translated personal data into actionable recommendations. Participants wanted help interpreting blood glucose trends, identifying food-related triggers, receiving tailored dietary or exercise suggestions, and obtaining reminders or feedback based on their own records and habits. This indicates that participants did not want raw data alone; they wanted assistance in making sense of data in ways that could guide everyday decisions.

“If it could offer suggestions based on my average levels over time, like how to lower my three-month average, that would be great…” (PD01)

Accordingly, acceptance of telehealth appeared to be relatively high because it preserved professional involvement despite being delivered in a virtual format.

“In person, you can measure vitals right away and look at the tongue, etc. I’m not strongly against virtual though.” (PD07)

However, acceptance of AI was explicitly conditional. Rather than accepting AI at face value, participants raised concerns about accuracy and professional credibility.

“The only downside is if the information is incorrect. For me, accuracy is paramount. Believing something wrong would have a negative impact.” (PD01)

“But it still lacks professional credibility. Even professionals aren’t always truly expert, but you still can’t trust AI 100%.” (PD02)

In addition, concerns about privacy centred primarily on sharing personally identifiable information.

“Privacy is at stake when uploading something because if someone has your ID card, they can steal a lot of your personal and financial information, borrow money from a finance company, or cause you to receive a lot of unnecessary junk mail.” (PD03)

They tended to view AI as suitable for lower-stakes questions about food, exercise, reminders, or day-to-day management, while reserving diagnosis, serious symptoms, complex interpretation, and emotional reassurance for clinicians.

“I need people talk to me about diet or dietitians talk to me about food.

A real person will ask what you normally eat and then make suggestions based on your situation, rather than just throwing an app at you. I still prefer talking to people first.” (PD10)

Importantly, participants recognised that digital tools could not fully solve the gap between knowledge and action. One participant captured this limitation clearly:

“If you know what to do but still can’t do it, an app won’t necessarily make you want to do it more, because you might not be able to put knowledge into practice.” (PD02)

Taken together, these accounts suggest that telehealth and AI were most acceptable when imagined as extending, rather than replacing, human care. Participants wanted technology that could support continuity through reminders, monitoring, information consolidation, and personalised feedback, but many did not believe AI alone could reproduce the trust, warmth, accountability, and motivational force associated with professional and relational support.

Discussion

In this qualitative descriptive study, we explored how adults with prediabetes in Hong Kong perceived telehealth and AI as potential supports for lifestyle modification. We suggest that acceptance of these technologies was not determined simply by digital familiarity or general openness to innovation. Rather, participants’ views were situated within their lived experience of prediabetes, particularly how they understood their personal risk, how they negotiated the gap between knowing what lifestyle changes were recommended and being able to sustain those changes, and how far digital tools could provide support without weakening the human reassurance and professional credibility they associated with healthcare. Telehealth and AI were, therefore, viewed less as standalone solutions than as possible extensions of care, with their acceptability dependent on whether they could provide timely, personalised, culturally relevant, and trustworthy support in everyday life.

The relatively low sense of the urgent or threatening prediabetes condition may help explain why those planning interventions for prediabetes should not assume that individuals enter programmes with a clear understanding of their risk or a strong readiness to act. Rather than responding to the diagnostic label itself, participants appeared to judge their future risk by drawing on family history, bodily symptoms, previous health experiences, and related comorbidities. This suggests that risk appraisal was constructed through lived experience rather than biomedical definitions alone. This finding is consistent with previous qualitative evidence showing that people with prediabetes may experience the condition as uncertain, reversible, or less serious than established diabetes, which can weaken motivation for sustained lifestyle change(Skoglund et al., 2022). Digital health interventions may, therefore, need to do more than provide general education; they should help individuals interpret risk in a way that is clinically accurate, personally meaningful, emotionally balanced, and linked to feasible action.

The findings challenge information-centred approaches to prediabetes management by showing that knowledge alone was insufficient to support sustained lifestyle modification. Participants generally reported being aware of broad recommendations for prediabetes management but often found it difficult to translate them into sustained behaviour because of work demands, fatigue, emotional distress, food availability, family routines, eating-out practices, and limited control over daily schedules. These accounts challenge a simplistic interpretation of non-adherence as a lack of knowledge or motivation. Instead, they show that lifestyle modification is embedded within social, emotional, occupational, and material conditions(Matthews et al., 2024). This aligns with wider evidence that health-related knowledge alone is insufficient to produce sustained behavioural change(Alves, 2024). Consequently, digital interventions for prediabetes may be more effective when they support problem-solving, contextual tailoring, and adaptive behavioural planning rather than simply delivering additional health information. Such support may help individuals identify realistic opportunities for behaviour change within the constraints of their daily routines.

Participants’ descriptions of self-management further suggest that prediabetes management was approached pragmatically rather than idealistically. This preference is understandable because lifestyle behaviours were negotiated within the realities of work, family routines, social eating, fatigue, and competing responsibilities. Instead of radical behavioural transformation or strict adherence to a structured programme, they focused on modest and incremental changes. This interpretation is consistent with findings from a recent qualitative study that similarly identified flexibility and adaptation to daily routines as important for sustaining dietary changes(Sverdrup et al., 2026). This orientation toward sustainability suggests that interventions that demand intensive self-monitoring or major lifestyle restructuring may be less acceptable than those that support gradual adaptation and maintenance. In this context, digital technologies may be most useful when they help individuals sustain realistic changes over time, rather than simply instructing them to pursue ideal behavioural targets.

Participants’ acceptance of telehealth appeared to be closely linked to its ability to preserve relationships with healthcare professionals while extending access to care(Wong et al., 2022). Rather than viewing telehealth as a replacement for conventional healthcare, participants generally regarded it as a means of maintaining continuity with clinicians between face-to-face consultations. Similar observations have been reported in studies of chronic disease management, where acceptance of telehealth depends largely on whether it maintains continuity of care and ongoing clinician involvement(Ezeamii et al., 2024, Sharma et al., 2024).

Acceptance of AI reflected a different set of considerations. Unlike telehealth, which retained direct clinician involvement, AI was primarily evaluated according to the type of task it was expected to perform and the level of clinical responsibility it was expected to assume. Rather than rejecting AI itself, participants clearly differentiated between information access and meaningful care. Many were already using AI tools, mobile applications, or online platforms to seek health information, check facts, communicate with others, or manage aspects of their lifestyle. However, generic advice, one-off screening feedback, or automated responses were considered insufficient when not accompanied by interpretation, encouragement, and follow-up. They still valued clinicians’ and nurses’ support that was continuous, responsive, and tailored to their circumstances, which were associated with credibility, accountability, and reassurance. Similar distinctions have been discussed in previous work on healthcare AI, which suggests that willingness to use AI depends not only on perceived usefulness but also on whether its role is considered appropriate within existing models of care(Gille et al., 2020). We have extended this perspective by suggesting that participants’ acceptance depended on whether they considered a task suitable for AI or believed that it required professional judgement and accountability.

This conditional nature of participants’ acceptance may also help explain why many participants initially reported uncertainty regarding the usefulness of telehealth and AI in the baseline questionnaire. Rather than reflecting rejection or reluctance to engage with digital technologies, this uncertainty appeared to arise because participants had limited opportunities to consider how these technologies might realistically be incorporated into prediabetes management. As the interviews progressed and participants reflected on specific functions and practical use scenarios, their views became more differentiated, evolving from general uncertainty towards conditional acceptance. Importantly, this conditional acceptance did not imply that participants were willing to accept AI without reservation. Instead, their acceptance depended on a set of interrelated expectations regarding the appropriate role of AI within healthcare. Participants were generally willing to use AI for day-to-day support because it could reduce the effort required to access information and provide interim guidance between formal healthcare encounters. This pattern is consistent with previous evidence showing that patients are generally more willing to accept AI for lower-risk tasks(Petersson et al., 2025). However, this willingness was contingent on the system being accurate, trustworthy, culturally relevant, and able to protect users’ privacy. More importantly, these requirements were meaningful only when AI was positioned as a complement to, rather than a replacement for, clinician involvement. Even highly accurate AI systems were unlikely to be considered acceptable if they were expected to replace professional judgement in diagnosis or complex clinical decision-making(van de Sande et al., 2026). By taking these findings together, we have extended the existing technology acceptance literature by highlighting the importance of role allocation between AI and healthcare professionals within hybrid models of care.

We have pointed toward a hybrid model of prediabetes support in Hong Kong in which AI and telehealth are embedded within clinician-supported care rather than positioned as autonomous substitutes for healthcare professionals(Wong et al., 2026). In such a model, AI could assist with routine self-management functions, including interpreting lifestyle and glucose data, generating personalised feedback, identifying possible behavioural triggers, and offering timely prompts. Telehealth could provide a channel for escalation, clarification, and follow-up by nurses or other healthcare professionals when users report concerning symptoms, abnormal glucose patterns, medication-related questions, or difficulty implementing advice. This model would preserve the accessibility and immediacy of digital tools while retaining the trust, accountability, and “human touch” that participants associated with professional care. It also aligns with evidence that nurse-led task-sharing and telehealth models in primary care may improve access, continuity, and system efficiency when supported by appropriate training, governance, and regulatory structures(Moulton et al., 2024).

Translating this model into routine practice within Hong Kong District Health Centres would require organisational as well as technological readiness. Nurses and other healthcare professionals would need training to select appropriate AI tools, critically evaluate AI-generated outputs, and integrate these recommendations into person-centred lifestyle counselling rather than relying on automated advice alone(Schubert et al., 2025). At the same time, clear data governance arrangements, including secure data management, transparent consent procedures, and ongoing regulatory oversight, would be essential to maintain public trust as AI-supported care expands(Klonoff et al., 2026). Appropriate digital infrastructure and redesigned clinical workflows would also be required to support effective collaboration between AI-enabled systems and healthcare professionals(Schwamm et al., 2024).

Realising this hybrid model in practice will depend not only on organisational readiness but also on the design of digital interventions capable of delivering the adaptive support that participants described while remaining closely integrated with telehealth services and clinician oversight. Participants did not simply want more information; they wanted support that could respond to lapses, detect patterns, and prompt action at moments when decisions about food, exercise, or self-monitoring were actually being made. These preferences closely reflect the principles of just-in-time adaptive interventions, which are designed to deliver timely and context-sensitive support using real-time or near-real-time information(Daryabeygi-Khotbehsara et al., 2023, Hietbrink et al., 2025, Oikonomidi et al., 2023). However, the present findings also caution against developing just-in-time adaptive interventions as fully automated systems. For people with prediabetes in Hong Kong, such interventions may be particularly useful if they are tailored to local routines, including rice-based meals, eating out, takeaway food, long working hours, commuting patterns, family food practices, and common barriers to physical activity. Feedback should be specific and actionable, helping users make small changes that fit their routines rather than providing generic lifestyle messages. Equally important, participants expected transparency regarding how recommendations were generated, how personal data were protected, and when healthcare professionals would review information or intervene. These expectations reinforce the importance of designing adaptive interventions that operate as part of clinician-supported care rather than as fully autonomous systems.

Taken together, we have extended current understanding of digital health acceptability in prediabetes by showing that the value of AI and telehealth lies not merely in increasing access to information but in helping individuals translate risk awareness and lifestyle knowledge into feasible action. This is particularly important in prediabetes, where the condition may not be experienced as immediately serious and where lifestyle change must be sustained within everyday constraints. We support a hybrid model in which AI-enabled just-in-time adaptive interventions provide personalised day-to-day behavioural support, telehealth enables ongoing communication and timely professional follow-up, and healthcare professionals retain responsibility for clinical judgement, complex decision-making, and relationship-centred care. Such complementary roles have the potential to deliver timely, personalised, and context-sensitive support while preserving the trust, accountability, and human connection that participants considered essential for long-term engagement in lifestyle modification.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this study is that it examined telehealth and AI acceptance in relation to participants’ broader experiences of living with prediabetes, rather than treating technology acceptance as an isolated attitude. This approach generated a more nuanced understanding of why digital tools may be useful, where their limits lie, and what design features are likely to matter for future intervention development. The study also included participants with varied ages, occupations, comorbidities, time since diagnosis, and levels of digital familiarity, which enriched the analysis.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the study involved a small sample recruited through social media platforms and district health centres in Hong Kong, and smartphone access was an eligibility criterion. Participants who agreed to be interviewed may have had greater interest in health management, telehealth, or AI than those who did not participate, while the eligibility criterion excluded people without smartphone access(Guo et al., 2021, Lau et al., 2025, Zhang et al., 2026). Together, these features may have overrepresented digitally-engaged individuals and contributed to more favourable accounts of telehealth and AI. Although participants reported varying levels of familiarity with AI and digital health technologies, the sample size and study design did not permit examination of how these differences shaped their perceptions. Conducting the study within a single geographical, sociocultural, and healthcare context may also limit the transferability of the findings across countries and healthcare systems. Researchers should use larger and more heterogeneous samples in future studies to examine how geographical context and prior digital experience shape technology acceptance. Second, we relied on self-reported perceptions and experiences, which may have been affected by recall or social desirability bias. These views were also captured during a specific period in the rapidly changing development of telehealth and generative AI. Participants’ attitudes may change in response to public awareness, media coverage, technological advances, and evolving regulatory frameworks. Accordingly, the temporal context should be considered when interpreting the findings and assessing their transferability. Finally, although the findings provide important design implications, they do not establish whether AI-enabled telehealth interventions improve adherence or clinical outcomes among people with prediabetes. Researchers should, therefore, conduct future studies to test co-designed hybrid interventions and examine their effectiveness, implementation outcomes, equity implications, and sustainability.

Conclusions

Participants with prediabetes in Hong Kong generally viewed telehealth and AI as acceptable adjuncts to lifestyle modification when these technologies were positioned as extensions of human care rather than replacements for clinicians. Acceptance was shaped by participants’ need for accurate, private, culturally relevant, professionally credible, and human-supported guidance. We have also highlighted a persistent gap between knowing what lifestyle changes are needed and being able to sustain those changes in daily life. Future interventions should, therefore, use AI-enabled telehealth to scaffold practical, incremental, and context-sensitive lifestyle change within hybrid models that retain clinician involvement, trust, and human reassurance.
