Long-term safety and efficacy of pegvisomant monotherapy for acromegaly: final data from the full ACROSTUDY cohort
Authors: Kevin C J Yuen, Greisa Vila, Ignacio Bernabeu, Michael Buchfelder, Pamela Freda, Robin Henocque, Daria La Torre, Pietro Maffei, Christian J Strasburger, Sebastian Neggers, Thierry Brue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeACROSTUDY was an international, non-interventional, cross-sectional study that evaluated the long-term safety and treatment outcomes of pegvisomant treatment (monotherapy or combination) in patients with acromegaly in “real-world” clinical practice. This study aimed to characterize the long-term safety and efficacy of pegvisomant monotherapy in ACROSTUDY.MethodsPatients with acromegaly aged > 18 years (at ACROSTUDY enrollment) who had received ≥ 1 dose of pegvisomant as monotherapy were included in the monotherapy cohort. Clinical assessments included adverse events (AEs), comorbidities, pituitary imaging, liver function tests, and insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-I) serum measurements.ResultsPatients with acromegaly (N = 1464) received pegvisomant monotherapy for a median (min, max) of 6.0 (0.0, 19.3) years and were followed in ACROSTUDY for a mean of 7.6 years. The rates of AEs (all-causality) and serious AEs were 49% (n = 717/1464) and 19.3% (n = 282/1464), respectively. The most common treatment-related AEs were IGF-I increased (1.8%), lipohypertrophy (1.2%), transaminases increased (1.2%), and IGF-I decreased (1.1%). There were 31 (2.1%) deaths and none were related to treatment. Magnetic resonance imaging findings at study sites showed 7.2% (n = 90/1248) of patients had increased tumor size, whereas 70.6% (n = 881/1248) of patients had change in tumor size. At the last observation, the serum IGF-I normalization rate was 62.3% (n = 756/1213) and the median (min, max) daily pegvisomant dose was 15.0 (0, 60) mg/day.ConclusionsIn this largest and longest real-world study of pegvisomant monotherapy in patients with acromegaly to date, pegvisomant demonstrated a favorable safety profile and was effective at normalizing and maintaining IGF-I levels in the majority of patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11102-026-01673-x.Keywords: Pegvisomant, Acromegaly, Monotherapy, Safety, Efficacy, IGF-I

Introduction

Acromegaly is a rare, chronic, and progressive endocrine disorder characterized by excess secretion of growth hormone (GH) and insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-I), most commonly resulting from a GH-secreting pituitary adenoma [1, 2]. Acromegaly is associated with multiple comorbidities and complications, including cardiovascular, metabolic, respiratory, articular, and bone complications [1], resulting in impairment of quality of life and increased morbidity and mortality rates, especially if untreated or sub-optimally treated [3].

The first-line treatment modality for acromegaly is trans-sphenoidal surgery, whereas medical treatment and radiotherapy are generally considered as adjuvant (or second-line) and third-line options, respectively, for patients not in remission postoperatively [4]. Medical treatments that can normalize IGF-I levels include somatostatin receptor ligands (SRLs), dopamine agonists (DAs), and the GH receptor antagonist pegvisomant. Pegvisomant is a recombinant GH analog that binds to the human GH receptor, thereby disrupting GH signal transduction and lowering IGF-I levels. The efficacy, safety, and tolerability of pegvisomant was established in previous prospective randomized clinical trials, with 87/90 (97%) patients achieving serum IGF-I normalization in a long-term (up to 18 months) study [5]. Pegvisomant was initially approved in Europe in 2002 and in the United States in 2003 for the treatment of patients with inadequately controlled acromegaly disease activity.

ACROSTUDY was a multi-country, non-randomized, open-label, non-interventional, cross-sectional, post-authorization safety surveillance study initiated in 2004 at the request of the European Medicines Agency (EMA). The objective of the study was to evaluate the long-term safety and treatment outcomes of patients with acromegaly treated with pegvisomant in “real-world” clinical practice. Data from the full international ACROSTUDY cohort [6, 7], country-specific ACROSTUDY analyses [8, 9, 10], and other real-word studies [11, 12], have confirmed the safety and efficacy of pegvisomant. The end-of-study report describing the full ACROSTUDY cohort was recently published [13], reporting data from 2221 patients who received pegvisomant (in combination or as monotherapy) for a median of 9.3 years and were followed for a median of 7.4 years. When combined with other treatments, such as SRLs, the independent effects of pegvisomant therapy are difficult to quantify. For instance, SRLs [14] and pegvisomant [15] exert opposing effects on glucose metabolism, which can confound efforts to characterize the safety and efficacy of pegvisomant per se in patients receiving both of these medications.

To better understand the effects of pegvisomant alone, it is imperative to characterize patients in ACROSTUDY who received pegvisomant monotherapy. Tritos et al. conducted an analysis of data extracted from ACROSTUDY [16], which included 204 patients receiving pegvisomant as primary medical therapy or as adjunctive therapy, showing that pegvisomant monotherapy normalized IGF-I in > 75% of patients and had a favorable safety profile. An interim analysis of ACROSTUDY performed in 2015 by Freda et al. focused primarily on the safety of pegvisomant in patients with acromegaly treated with pegvisomant monotherapy at that time [17]. This analysis of 710 patients who were followed for a mean of 3.8 years showed that the safety of pegvisomant was consistent with that reported in previous, smaller studies and that 67.5% of patients achieved normal IGF-I levels within 5 years of treatment [17].

In this study, we provide the final ACROSTUDY data to supplement the interim analysis published by Freda et al. in 2015 [17]. In this end-of-study report, we now present the final outcomes specifically for patients receiving pegvisomant monotherapy from the full ACROSTUDY cohort. The present study includes data from 1464 patients treated with pegvisomant monotherapy for an average of 6.6 years, representing the largest and longest real-world clinical study of pegvisomant monotherapy in patients with acromegaly to date.

Methods

Study design and treatments

A decade after its initiation, in 2014, ACROSTUDY was continued on a voluntary basis after confirmation from the EMA that the safety requirement was satisfied. All study data were collected as part of each patient’s routine clinical care. The timing of patient visits and pegvisomant dose and schedule, as well as dose titrations, were performed at the discretion of the treating physician. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the applicable laws and regulations in each of the countries involved. Ethics approval was obtained for all participating centers, and all patients provided written informed consent before enrollment into the study. A detailed description of the study methodology can be found in previous publications [17, 18, 19], which reported the interim results up to December 2012 for the pegvisomant monotherapy cohort.

Participants

Patients were included in the monotherapy cohort if they were > 18 years at enrollment into ACROSTUDY and (1) had received ≥ 1 dose of pegvisomant prescribed as monotherapy without concomitant SRLs or DA exposure, or (2) had discontinued SRLs or DAs at the beginning of pegvisomant treatment (> 28 days since the last dose of SRLs, > 7 days since the last dose of DA, and > 7 days since the last dose of any other acromegaly therapy). Patients who received SRLs and/or DAs in combination with pegvisomant within ACROSTUDY were excluded from the monotherapy cohort. Additional inclusion and exclusion criteria are the same as for the full ACROSTUDY cohort and were reported by van der Lely et al. [19].

Study assessments

Adverse events (AEs) were coded and summarized using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (version 20.1). Any comorbidities or events that occurred before a patient’s enrollment into ACROSTUDY were recorded in the database as part of their medical history; this was also applied to patients who had received pegvisomant prior to being enrolled in ACROSTUDY. Serum IGF-I levels were measured at baseline and liver function tests and serum IGF-I measurements were performed every 6 months after initiation of pegvisomant treatment. Laboratory abnormalities were to be reported as AEs per investigator judgement or if they met pre-specified criteria per protocol. Diabetes mellitus at baseline was defined using HbA1c and glucose levels as well as the patient’s medical history and concomitant medications. Evaluation of the effects of IGF-I control on glucose homeostasis commenced in 2014, following extension of the study on a voluntary basis.

Central MRI reading

A detailed description of the imaging methodology employed in ACROSTUDY is described in Buchfelder et al. [20]. Pituitary magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed at baseline, months 6 and 12, and then every 12 months. The ACROSTUDY protocol recommended that the MRI at the study site (local MRI) be conducted using the same imaging technique and equipment. T1-weighted spin-echo (or fast spin-echo) sagittal and coronal images (before and after gadolinium) and T2-weighted fast spin-echo coronal images were recommended by the MRI protocol. Pituitary tumor images were collected and reviewed locally and if a local radiologist determined that an image showed significant changes in tumor size, all available images were then requested to be assessed by a central reader; it should be noted that this was not a requirement during the voluntary extension period. Based on central assessment, change in pituitary adenoma size was considered substantial if there was a > 3-mm change in the largest diameter of the tumor. Central MRI assessment also considered increases in tumor size to be clinically relevant based on radiological signs such as compression of visual pathways. For pituitary macroadenomas, an additional criterion of > 20% change (increase or decrease) in tumor volume was used to define a substantial change.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were descriptive, and baseline was defined as the start of pegvisomant treatment, irrespective of when enrollment into ACROSTUDY occurred. Change in tumor volume was categorized as (1) increased, (2) decreased, (3) increased and decreased, or (4) unchanged. Serum IGF-I levels were categorized as normal (within upper and lower limits of normal based on the reference values for the local laboratory), > 1.3⋅ the upper limit of normal (ULN), or less than the lower limit of normal (LLN) at each year of follow-up.

Results

Participants and treatment

Among the final ACROSTUDY cohort of 2221 patients, 1464 (65.9%) received pegvisomant monotherapy, with an equal male-to-female ratio. The majority of the patients were from Germany (n = 385), Italy (n = 303), France (n = 242), Spain (n = 171), and the United States (n = 158) (Supplementary Table 1).

The mean (standard deviation [SD]) age at acromegaly diagnosis was 42.2 (13.5) years, and the mean (SD) age at which patients started pegvisomant treatment was 49.9 (13.9) years (Supplementary Table 1). The mean (SD) time between diagnosis of acromegaly and the start of pegvisomant treatment was 7.6 (8.1) years. Patients received pegvisomant monotherapy for a median (min, max) duration of 6.0 (0.0, 19.3) years. A total of 18/1464 (1.2%) patients received treatment for less than 30 days (~ 1 month) and 68 /1464 (4.6%) patients received treatment for less than 90 days (~ 3 months). Most patients (86.5%) had at least one year of pegvisomant treatment. A total of 249 (17.0%) patients had no prior use of any other medical therapies, whereas 1230 (84.0%) patients had received other forms of medical therapy (including SRLs or DAs) prior to pegvisomant monotherapy. Almost half of the patients (n = 706, 48.2%) had received both surgery and medical therapy, and 23.3% (n = 341) of patients had received surgery, medical therapy, and radiotherapy (Fig. 1). The mean (SD) time between a patient’s first pituitary surgery and their first dose of pegvisomant was 7.1 (7.56) years. The mean (SD) time between receiving the first dose of SRL and the first dose of pegvisomant was 4.6 (4.60) years (including the time between cessation of SRL and initiation of pegvisomant).

Therapies received prior to starting pegvisomant

Most (89.0%) patients had ≥ 1 reported comorbidity before starting pegvisomant monotherapy (Supplementary Table 2). The most prevalent comorbidities (≥ 15% of patients) were hypertension (52.0%), diabetes mellitus (31.5%), osteoarthritis (24.0%), sleep apnea (21.9%), thyroid tumors (18.9%; majority were benign tumors), surgery for gallstones (17.6%), and colon tumors (16.7%; including adenomatous polyps and other benign tumors, as well as adenocarcinomas). Pituitary hormone deficiencies included hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (n = 157/429; 36.6%), adrenocorticotropic hormone deficiency (n = 115/402; 28.6%), thyroid-stimulating hormone deficiency (n = 133/458; 29.0%), and prolactin deficiency (n = 18/421; 4.3%) (Table 1).

Safety

Adverse events

Almost half of the patients (n = 717; 49.0%) reported an AE (all-causality), and the most common AEs are described in Table 2. Treatment-related AEs were reported in 198 (13.5%) patients (Table 3). The most commonly reported treatment-related AEs (> 0.5% of patients) were IGF-I increased (1.8%), lipohypertrophy (1.2%), transaminases increased (1.2%), IGF-I decreased (1.1%), alanine aminotransferase increased (0.8%), and injection site reaction (0.6%). Serious AEs (SAEs; all-causality) were reported in 282 (19.3%) patients, with 25 (1.7%) patients reporting SAEs that were considered treatment-related and 75 patients (5.1%) discontinuing treatment due to an SAE (Table 3). There were 31 (2.1%) deaths in the study, and none were considered related to treatment; causes of death are listed in Supplementary Table 3.

Serum liver function tests

Of 1464 patients, 54 (3.7%) had aspartate aminotransferase/alanine aminotransferase (AST/ALT) values that were > 3x⋅ ULN during treatment with pegvisomant (Supplementary Table 4). Of the 864 patients with normal baseline AST/ALT values, 30 (3.5%) had values that rose to > 3x⋅ ULN during treatment. Of the 68 patients who entered the study with an AST/ALT value between 1 to 3x⋅ ULN, 9 (13.2%) had values that rose to > 3x⋅ ULN. Of the 7 patients who entered the study with an AST/ALT value > 3x⋅ ULN, 2 (28.6%) had values that remained > 3x⋅ ULN.

Pituitary tumor size

A total of 1438 patients were analyzed for pituitary imaging and, of these, 1248 (86.8%) had ≥ 1 post-baseline local pituitary MRI result reported (Supplementary Table 5). Based on the local pituitary MRI results, 881 (70.6%) patients had no change in tumor size relative to the last examination or to the start of pegvisomant therapy. A total of 213 (17.1%) patients had a decrease in tumor size, 90 (7.2%) patients had an increase in tumor size, and 64 (5.1%) patients had both an increase and a decrease in tumor size (Supplementary Table 5). No SAEs related to tumor size increase and leading to drug withdrawal or dose reduction were reported. The proportion of patients in each category of tumor changes was similar between patients who had no prior therapies and those who had prior therapies (increase or decrease: 34.1% vs. 29.0%; increase only: 7.0% vs. 7.4%; decrease only: 22.7% vs. 16.1%; both increase and decrease: 4.3% vs. 5.5%; no change: 65.9% vs. 71.0%). The proportion of patients who had ≥ 1 change in tumor size (either increase or decrease) was higher for patients who had radiotherapy after pegvisomant (69.4%) compared with those who had radiotherapy before pegvisomant (44.0%) or had not had any radiotherapy (43.3%) (Supplementary Table 5). Specifically, the subgroup that had radiotherapy after pegvisomant treatment had a higher proportion of patients with a decrease in tumor size or both an increase and decrease in tumor size compared with the other 2 subgroups (Supplementary Table 5). A total of 636 patients had radiotherapy either before or after the start of pegvisomant treatment; of these, 27 (4.2%) showed an increase in tumor size (no decreases in tumor size were observed in these patients).

Glucose homeostasis

The median glucose level at the start of pegvisomant therapy was 95.5 mg/dL (5.3 mmol/L) and remained between 88 and 95 mg/dL (4.9–5.3 mmol/L) through to year 12 (Supplementary Fig. 1). Similarly, median HbA1c was 6.1% (43.2 mmol/mol) at the start of pegvisomant treatment and remained between 5.8% and 5.9% (39.9–41.0 mmol/mol) through to year 12 (Supplementary Fig. 1) Of the 325 patients enrolled into the voluntary extension study period, only a small number had fasting glucose levels > 200 mg/dL (> 11.1 mmol/L) at baseline (n = 6/325; 1.8%), year 1 (n = 1/325; 0.3%), and year 2 (n = 1/325; 0.3%). The number of patients with glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels > 6.5% (> 47.5 mmol/L) at baseline, year 1, and year 2 was 29/325 (8.9%), 16/325 (4.9%), and 25/325 (7.7%), respectively. Compared with baseline, the percentage of patients receiving treatments for diabetes mellitus decreased over the course of pegvisomant treatment (Supplementary Fig. 2). At the beginning of pegvisomant therapy, 31/1464 (2.1%) patients were receiving insulin, and by month 6 of treatment, 4/1464 (0.3%) patients were receiving insulin. There were between 2 (0.1%) and 10 (0.7%) patients receiving insulin per year from years 1 to 10 and from years 11 to 15 there were no patients receiving insulin (Supplementary Fig. 2). A total of 212/1464 (14.5%) patients were receiving oral anti-diabetes drugs at the beginning of pegvisomant therapy and after 6 months of treatment, 59/1464 (4.0%) patients were receiving oral anti-diabetes drugs. From years 1 to 10, there were between 11 (0.8%) and 74 (5.1%) patients receiving oral anti-diabetes drugs in each year, and from years 11 to 15, there were 7 or fewer patients receiving oral anti-diabetes drugs (Supplementary Fig. 2).

IGF-I levels

Normal IGF-I levels were observed in 20/132 (15.2%) patients at the beginning of pegvisomant treatment, in 367/716 (51.3%) patients after 6 months of treatment, and in 406/693 (58.6%) patients after 1 year of treatment (Fig. 2A). Between years 2 (n = 422/682) and 6 (n = 244/346), IGF-I normalization rates increased from 61.9% to 70.5%. Normalization rates remained above 70% between years 7 (n = 175/238) and 14 (n = 9/11), with the highest being 83.3% in years 10 (n = 90/108) and 13 (n = 20/24) (Fig. 2A). The normalization percentage at the last observation (n = 756/1213) was 62.3%. The percentage of patients who had IGF-I levels below the LLN was < 5% between the start of therapy and year 14 (Fig. 2B). In patients with normal IGF-I levels, the mean (SD) daily dose of pegvisomant at month 6, year 5, year 10, and last observation was 12.9 (5.27) mg/day, 17.3 (8.39) mg/day, 17.3 (8.73) mg/day, and 16.2 (8.09) mg/day, respectively (Fig. 2A); the median daily dose of pegvisomant was 10.0 mg/day at month 6 and was 15.0 mg/day from year 1 to year 10 and at last observation (doses ranged from 0 to 60.0 mg/day). In patients with IGF-I > ULN, the mean (SD) daily dose of pegvisomant at month 6, year 5, year 10, and last observation was 13.8 (6.46) mg/day, 19.0 (11.49) mg/day, 16.5 (8.71) mg/day, and 18.2 (10.04) mg/day, respectively; the median daily dose at the same timepoints was 12.9 mg/day, 20.0 mg/day, 15.0 mg/day, and 20.0 mg/day, respectively (doses ranged from 0 to 60.0 mg/day).

For patients who had IGF-I levels above the normal range, 46/112 (41.1%) patients had IGF-I ≥ 2x⋅ ULN at the start of pegvisomant treatment (Supplementary Fig. 3), and the number of patients with IGF-I ≥ 2x⋅ ULN decreased from 76/329 (23.1%) at 6 months to 13/133 (9.8%) at year 5. The proportion of patients with modestly elevated IGF-I (between ULN and < 1.3x ULN) increased from 23.2% (26/112) at the start of pegvisomant therapy to 56.4% (75/133) at year 5 (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Discussion

ACROSTUDY provided data on the long-term safety and efficacy of pegvisomant-treated patients with acromegaly as part of routine clinical practice over 13 years (2004–2017). In 2021, Fleseriu et al. [13] reported the findings from the full ACROSTUDY cohort of 2221 patients who received pegvisomant in combination or as monotherapy, with the last analysis that focused specifically on patients who received pegvisomant monotherapy being published in 2015 by Freda et al. [17]. As there are currently few published long-term data on pegvisomant monotherapy in patients with acromegaly, we hereby report the final data from the full cohort of 1464 patients from ACROSTUDY who received a mean (SD) of 6.6 (4.58) years of pegvisomant treatment and were followed in the study for 7.6 (3.36) years. Compared with the interim analysis of ACROSTUDY reported by Freda et al. in 2015 [17], the present report includes twice as many patients and a follow-up period that is twice as long. Based on the findings from ACROSTUDY, pegvisomant monotherapy had a favorable safety profile in real-world clinical practice, with little evidence of increased tumor size or liver enzyme abnormalities during long-term treatment. In terms of efficacy, the mean IGF-I normalization rate at the last observation was 62.3%, with the mean daily dose of pegvisomant utilized being 16.2 mg/day.

Given that a substantial proportion of patients treated with SRLs underwent tumor shrinkage [21], it was of interest to characterize potential changes in tumor size following the cessation of SRL treatment and initiation of pegvisomant. Tumor size was monitored during the whole course of pegvisomant treatment, and the percentage of patients who had an increase, or an increase and a decrease, in tumor size reported was 7.2% and 5.1%, respectively. This was very similar to what was observed in the full ACROSTUDY cohort, with the corresponding proportions being 7.1% (tumor size increase) and 4.5% (tumor size increase and decrease). The interim analysis of the pegvisomant monotherapy cohort published in 2015 (based on local imaging results) showed 5.2% of patients had an increase in tumor size and 1.8% of patients had an increase and a decrease in tumor size [17]. A meta-analysis of longitudinal observational studies (considering patients receiving pegvisomant monotherapy) by Leonart et al. [22] reported the rate of increased tumor size as being 7.2% (based on 4 studies). The increase in tumor size observed in a small proportion of patients is not unexpected given the discontinuation of SRL treatment and the fact that pegvisomant itself does not have any direct antitumor activity. The possible re-expansion of a residual adenoma following cessation of SRLs may partly explain why a proportion of patients received radiotherapy after initiating pegvisomant treatment. Even among patients treated with SRLs, an increase in tumor size has been reported for some patients [23, 24]. Colao et al. estimated that 1% to 3% of GH-secreting pituitary adenomas continue to grow independently of the treatment received by the patient [25]. A recent review of pegvisomant in the treatment of acromegaly stated that based on current evidence, there is no suggestion that pegvisomant treatment increases the growth rate of tumors beyond what would be observed over the natural course of or during SRL treatment [26]. Overall, the findings from our study and previous studies suggest that long-term pegvisomant treatment does not result in a clinically meaningful increase in tumor size in patients with acromegaly. A total of 213 patients who received pegvisomant monotherapy had a decrease in tumor size. Spontaneous size reduction is not unexpected in pituitary adenomas and may be explained by natural medical history. In a review of 14 observational studies comprising 648 patients with reported clinically nonfunctioning pituitary adenomas (no medical, surgical, or radiological intervention received), 419 (65%) patients had macroadenomas and 229 (35%) patients had microadenomas. Of these, 12% of macroadenomas and 7% of microadenomas showed a reduction of tumor size [27].

Serum IGF-I normalization rates improved from 15.2% at treatment start to 58.6% after 1 year of treatment and remained between 70% and 85% between years 6 and 14. It is important to note that as the length of the observation period increased, there was a substantial reduction in the number of patients for whom data were available, as shown in Fig. 2A. The normalization rates reported here are similar to those for the full ACROSTUDY cohort (53.7% within 1 year, remaining above 60% from years 4–14) [13]. Our findings were also consistent with the results from Leonart et al., who reported an IGF-I control rate of 71.7% when considering only patients treated with pegvisomant as monotherapy [22]. It should also be noted that IGF-I normalization rates observed in the pegvisomant monotherapy cohort were achieved with mean pegvisomant doses < 20 mg/day (Fig. 2A). This is consistent with the full ACROSTUDY cohort, where the mean daily pegvisomant dose was ≤ 21 mg/day among patients who achieved IGF-I normalization [13]. As has been reported previously [17, 28, 29, 30, 31], the IGF-I normalization rates observed in real-world studies are substantially lower than those observed in previous clinical studies. Some of the factors that may explain this discrepancy in real-world studies include insufficient upward titration of pegvisomant dose by practicing clinicians, impaired patient adherence, and potential differences in treatment regimens (e.g., once daily vs. 2–3 injections per week). Further, variation in IGF-I measurements (which were performed locally) as well as the reference ranges used to calculate the ULN in ACROSTUDY may have influenced the reported normalization rates and the perceived efficacy of pegvisomant [32]. Acknowledging this variation in IGF-I measurement and reference ranges, as well as the presence of biological variability, Clemmons and Bidlingmaier suggest that for patients with modestly elevated IGF-I levels (1.0× to 1.3× ULN), decisions about whether to intensify treatment should also consider symptoms, comorbidities, and quality of life [32]. It is also important to emphasize that the efficacy of pegvisomant should not be compared directly with that of other medical therapies in terms of normalization rate because most patients who received pegvisomant were not achieving control using other therapies, which represents a selection bias for the more difficult-to-treat patients. With regards to the 15% of patients who had normal IGF-I levels at the start of ACROSTUDY, this refers to a small proportion of patients who had initiated pegvisomant treatment prior to enrollment in ACROSTUDY. The proportion (15%) is consistent with the proportions that were previously observed in the full ACROSTUDY cohort (11.4%) [13] and the interim analysis (15%) [17].

We observed a reduction in the use of anti-diabetes therapy during the study, with a greater than threefold reduction in patients needing anti-diabetes drugs 6 months after starting pegvisomant treatment. Based on these data, pegvisomant monotherapy appears to have a positive impact on glucose metabolism. A previous 4-year longitudinal analysis of patients in ACROSTUDY showed a moderate reduction in mean fasting glucose values [33] and is consistent with other studies that report an improvement in glucose tolerance and a reduction in HbA1c [17, 34]. In a meta-analysis of 13 prospective interventional studies, pegvisomant decreased fasting glucose levels, HbA1c, and fasting insulin levels independently of IGF-I changes [15], and given that SRLs can inhibit insulin secretion [14], pegvisomant monotherapy may be preferred over SRLs for patients with glucose intolerance or diabetes.

The data from this study also provide important insights into the timing of treatment for patients with acromegaly in routine clinical practice. The mean time between diagnosis of acromegaly to the start of pegvisomant treatment is very similar to the mean time between first surgery and pegvisomant treatment (7.6 vs. 7.1 years). The mean time between the start of SRL treatment and the first dose of pegvisomant is 4.6 years, which constitutes a substantial delay. This delay may be due to several factors, including the accessibility of pegvisomant. Once first-generation SRLs are no longer effective in patients with acromegaly, other treatments such as pegvisomant could be considered sooner to reduce the duration of active disease. Delays in treatment underscore the need for more stringent and/or personalized treatment protocols in this patient population.

Given that acromegaly is a rare disease, a key strength of this study was the fact that it reported real-world data from a large cohort (n = 1464) of patients with acromegaly who received pegvisomant monotherapy as part of routine clinical practice. ACROSTUDY was carried out in accordance with the EMA guidance on non-interventional post-authorization safety study (PASS) and, in 2013, the EMA confirmed that the PASS commitment of a minimum of 1000 patients followed-up for a period of 5 years was fulfilled, underscoring the robustness of the data collected. ACROSTUDY was then continued via a voluntary extension period for another 4 years, providing additional data on long-term, real-world pegvisomant treatment practice across different geographic regions. This study also captured information on tumor volume variations (including the potential effect of radiotherapy) during pegvisomant monotherapy, as well as IGF-I levels > ULN stratified according to IGF-I levels over the duration of treatment. As stated above, one of the main limitations of the data on IGF-I levels is the reduced sample size, particularly in the second half of the study. Therefore, interpretation of IGF-I normalization rates and pegvisomant effectiveness should consider the potential attrition bias in the data, especially in the later years. Another limitation of the study was that several aspects of the study were left to the discretion of the investigators [17], including AE reporting, scope of data entered, and dose escalation, which may have resulted in some inconsistencies. Only a small proportion of MRIs were sent for central assessment, and some patients may have had incomplete baseline information due to initiation of pegvisomant treatment before enrollment in ACROSTUDY [13], constituting additional limitations of this study. Lastly, data on glucose homeostasis was only collected from 2014 onwards and compliance was not evaluable in this study.

Conclusions

In the largest and longest real-world study of pegvisomant monotherapy in patients with acromegaly to date, pegvisomant was effective in normalizing IGF-I levels (range: 51.3%-83.3% of patients); at the last observation, 62.3% of patients had normal IGF-I levels, with a median daily dose of 15.0 mg/day. Pegvisomant monotherapy also exerted a favorable safety profile, with a low incidence of tumor growth and liver enzyme abnormalities. These findings confirm that pegvisomant monotherapy constitutes a valuable treatment option for patients with acromegaly.
