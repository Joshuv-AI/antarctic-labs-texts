Occupational stress, psychological resources, coping responses, and job satisfaction among healthcare workers in Ho Chi Minh City: a three-wave prospective cohort study
Authors: Hoang Phuc Do, Thi Hang Le
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealthcare workers (HCWs) in Vietnam face compounding occupational stressors placing them at elevated risk for diminished job satisfaction, depression, and anxiety. Longitudinal within-person evidence linking occupational stress to job satisfaction remains sparse in Southeast Asian low- and middle-income countries such as Vietnam.MethodsA three-wave prospective cohort study enrolled 1,500 healthcare workers (HCWs; physicians, nurses, pharmacists, technicians) from 15 healthcare facilities in Ho Chi Minh City (T1: January–March 2023; T2: July–September 2023; T3: January–March 2024). Occupational stress (Effort–Reward Imbalance, ERI), psychological resources (resilience, perceived social and organizational support), coping responses (adaptive and maladaptive coping), and mental health outcomes were assessed at each wave using established self-report instruments adapted for Vietnamese use. Latent growth curve (LGC) modeling characterized job satisfaction trajectories across three waves; Random Intercept Cross-Lagged Panel Modeling (RI-CLPM) estimated within-person indirect associations between psychological resources and job satisfaction as the primary outcome. Multiple imputation and cluster-robust standard errors were applied.ResultsTwelve-month retention was 92.6%. Mean job satisfaction (JSS) scores declined from T1 to T2 (mixed-effects contrast: b = -5.10, p < .001) and, to a significantly smaller degree, from T2 to T3 (b = -1.25, p < .001; contrast-of-contrasts b = 3.85, p < .001). Exploratory comparisons suggested heterogeneity in decline magnitude across professional groups; partial scalar invariance provided incomplete support for these comparisons, which remain exploratory because they were unadjusted for group-level occupational and demographic differences. Deviations in observed ERI composite scores at T1 were associated with subsequent deviations in resilience, adaptive coping, maladaptive coping, total perceived social support, and organizational support at T2. These variables were, in turn, associated with job satisfaction at T3, including a primary resilience (Brief Resilience Scale, BRS) pathway indirect association (ERI→BRS→JSS = -1.28, 95% Monte Carlo CI [-1.71, -0.85]). All five primary indirect associations were statistically significant (Benjamini–Hochberg q < 0.001).ConclusionsJob satisfaction declined across the study period, with exploratory evidence of heterogeneity across professional groups. Within-person patterns were consistent with Conservation of Resources theory. These preliminary findings identify candidate targets for future intervention research; pre-registered replication is needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05279-7.Keywords: Occupational stress, Job satisfaction, Psychological resources, Resilience, Coping strategies, Within-person associations, RI-CLPM, Healthcare workers, Vietnam, Low- and middle-income countries

Introduction

The psychological wellbeing of healthcare workers (HCWs) is a global public health priority with heightened urgency in the post-COVID-19 era. Diminished job satisfaction [1] and burnout [2, 3] have been documented across high-income healthcare contexts for decades. HCWs in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) face compounding vulnerabilities, including pandemic response burdens and elevated psychological distress [4], against a backdrop of population-wide pandemic-related mental health burden documented more broadly [5], together with chronic understaffing, limited institutional support, and sociocultural expectations of professional stoicism.

Vietnam presents a compelling case. Ho Chi Minh City (HCMC), the nation’s largest urban center and primary tertiary healthcare hub, concentrates a large share of the country’s healthcare workforce, serving a population exceeding 9 million [6]. Clinical evidence suggests reduced job satisfaction and elevated psychological distress among Vietnamese HCWs − 22.6% reported psychosocial problems during a COVID-19 lockdown [7], with stress and anxiety particularly prevalent among nurses [8] - but these studies are cross-sectional [7, 8] and, together with globally high nursing-burnout prevalence [9], underscore a persistent gap in longitudinal, LMIC-specific trajectory data that limits the generalizability of intervention evidence drawn mainly from North American and European cohorts. As a concentrated urban tertiary hub undergoing rapid post-pandemic organizational change, HCMC offers a distinct setting to examine these dynamics: staffing, remuneration, and support structures differ from Western health systems, and collectivist norms may also shape how support is perceived and sought, although this contextual possibility was not directly tested in the present study, which examines indirect associations only. Testing the ERI–resources–job satisfaction pathway in this Southeast Asian LMIC context, rather than assuming it replicates Western findings, is therefore relevant to occupational health psychology in comparable urban LMIC healthcare systems more broadly.

Theoretical models of occupational stress - the Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) model [10] and Conservation of Resources (COR) theory [11] - posit that resilience, coping capacity, and social support function as protective resources along stress-to-satisfaction pathways; the present study tests these lagged indirect associations only, not moderation.

Less is known, however, about whether psychological resources fluctuate within individuals over time in response to occupational stress, and whether these within-person fluctuations are associated with later job satisfaction. This distinction matters because between-person associations, which dominate existing research, cannot determine whether the same worker experiences lower job satisfaction when their own resilience, coping capacity, or perceived support declines relative to their own usual level. The psychological contribution of the present study lies precisely in examining these within-person associations.

The present three-wave longitudinal cohort study with 1,500 HCWs across four professional groups in HCMC addressed three aims: (1) to characterize job satisfaction trajectories and secondary depressive and anxiety symptom patterns over 12 months using latent growth curve modeling; (2) to estimate lagged indirect associations involving resilience, coping, and perceived support in relation to job satisfaction as the primary psychological outcome using RI-CLPM; and (3) to explore, on an exploratory and unadjusted basis, whether job satisfaction trajectories differ across professional groups. This study therefore contributes to occupational health psychology by examining lagged associations among observed composite-score deviations in psychological resources, occupational stress, and job satisfaction within persons over time, rather than relying solely on stable between-person differences.

Theoretical framework and hypotheses

Siegrist’s [12] ERI model proposes that occupational stress arises when high effort is paired with inadequate reward (salary, esteem, career opportunities); ERI ratios > 1 indicate imbalance. The ERI ratio was computed as the sum of Effort items divided by the sum of Reward items with a correction factor (6/11) to account for unequal item numbers, following Siegrist et al. [13]; ratios > 1 indicate imbalance. The JD-R model [10] extends this by proposing that diminished wellbeing and reduced job satisfaction emerge when demands chronically exceed available resources. COR theory [11] provides the explanatory mechanism: sustained ERI depletes personal and social resources through progressive loss spirals, eroding psychological resources and reducing job satisfaction.

Resilience is increasingly understood as a dynamic capacity rather than a stable trait [14], meaning its protective strength may attenuate under sustained adversity. From a transactional stress perspective [15], coping reflects cognitive and behavioral efforts to manage demands appraised as taxing or exceeding personal resources. Under persistent ERI, workers may shift from active problem-solving and positive reappraisal toward avoidance, denial, or self-blame, thereby eroding job satisfaction through both resource depletion and demand escalation. This adaptive-to-maladaptive coping shift constitutes a psychological amplification pathway consistent with COR resource loss spirals. Social support buffers occupational stress by providing emotional, informational, and instrumental resources [16, 17], and serves as perceived organizational and interpersonal safety. Longitudinal evidence from European samples using cross-lagged designs indicates reciprocal relationships between job characteristics and psychological well-being over time [18], consistent with the plausibility of dynamic, bidirectional associations between support-relevant job resources and wellbeing; cross-cultural generalizability to collectivist contexts requires empirical testing.

Five hypotheses guided the primary analyses:

H1 (baseline cross-sectional association): Higher ERI will be associated with lower baseline job satisfaction and higher depression and anxiety symptoms; these associations will be examined descriptively at T1.

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This prospective longitudinal cohort study used three measurement waves: T1 (January–March 2023), T2 (July–September 2023), and T3 (January–March 2024). The study was not prospectively registered; hypotheses and analytic decisions should be interpreted in this context. Ethics approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of the Graduate Academy of Social Sciences, Vietnam Academy of Social Sciences (Decision No. 1758/QĐ-HVKHXH). Administrative approval and site access for participant recruitment were obtained from the leadership of each of the 15 participating healthcare facilities; not all facilities maintained separate ethics review committees, and facility approvals should be understood as site-access and administrative authorizations rather than independent ethics committee reviews. All participants provided written informed consent. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and STROBE guidelines (STROBE checklist: Additional file 1).

Participants and recruitment

Because the 15 participating facilities were purposively selected rather than randomly sampled from all HCMC healthcare facilities, the target population for this study is defined as HCWs employed at these participating facilities, not all HCWs in HCMC. Within this target population, a stratified random sampling procedure was used across the 15 facilities: 9 public hospitals (6 district-level, 3 regional/provincial) and 6 private hospitals and polyclinics, representing diverse ownership types, patient volumes (approximately 300–1,200 beds), and specialty profiles. Facility-level characteristics are reported in Additional file 2 (Supplementary Table S6). Within each facility, HCWs were stratified by professional group (physicians, nurses, pharmacists, medical/laboratory technicians) and randomly selected from HR employment rosters using computer-generated random number sequences independently per stratum. A roster-screened potentially eligible pool of approximately 4,800 HCWs was identified from facility HR rosters (meeting basic role and tenure criteria only, before invitation, consent, or full eligibility screening); 2,100 were invited (proportional allocation). After applying eligibility and exclusion criteria (including n = 38 excluded for current pharmacological treatment of a major psychiatric disorder; see below) and removing incomplete baseline surveys, 1,500 HCWs were enrolled in the final analytic baseline sample, representing 71.4% of those invited (1,500/2,100). This figure describes the proportion of invited HCWs retained in the analytic sample rather than a raw survey response rate. The full participant flow - potentially eligible, invited, consented, screened, excluded with reasons, and analyzed - is presented in Fig. 1 below and reconciles to the reported totals at every stage. The consent proportion among invited HCWs (consented / invited) was 90.0% (1,890/2,100); the figure of 71.4% (1,500/2,100) is the final analytic enrollment proportion, not a response rate. Group-level analytic enrollment proportions (analyzed / invited, by professional group) were: physicians 68.3%, nurses 75.0%, pharmacists 70.7%, technicians 68.8%.

Inclusion criteria: (1) active clinical or patient-care-adjacent duties; (2) ≥ 6 months employment at baseline; (3) Vietnamese literacy; (4) written informed consent. Exclusion criteria: (1) concurrent enrollment in another health intervention study; (2) self-reported current pharmacological treatment for a major psychiatric disorder at baseline (operationally defined as current medication use for major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, psychotic disorders, or severe anxiety-related disorders, as self-reported in the screening questionnaire; n = 38 excluded for this reason); (3) planned employment change within the study period. Medication use is not equivalent to diagnosis, and some excluded individuals may have been stable and well-functioning. This exclusion criterion was a design decision made at study inception, intended to limit heterogeneity associated with active pharmacological treatment; because medication use may also proxy baseline symptom severity, access to care, or general health status, this decision may itself have introduced selection bias rather than simply controlling for a confounder. As a result, study findings characterize healthcare workers not currently receiving pharmacological treatment for these conditions, rather than the full HCW workforce; this exclusion may underestimate symptom burden in the broader population and introduce selection bias (see Limitations). This exclusion criterion was established at study inception and could not be revised retrospectively. Because the resulting post hoc comparisons could not be verified consistently against the final analytic dataset, no quantitative comparison between the excluded and retained groups is reported. The potential direction and implications of the resulting selection bias are addressed in the Limitations.

Sample size was determined by Monte Carlo simulation in Mplus 8.9 [19]. Parameters were drawn from prior HCW longitudinal studies (indirect effects: β = 0.15–0.25; attrition: 20–25%; 1,000 replications). N = 1,200 at T3 provided power ≥ 0.90 to detect medium indirect effects (α = 0.05, two-tailed). Targeting 25% attrition, N = 1,500 was planned. Detailed simulation parameters are in Additional file 2 (Supplementary Table S7).

Measures

All instruments were internationally established scales. Vietnamese versions were used where available; others underwent forward-backward translation per WHO [20] guidelines without item deletion or alteration.

Occupational stress: effort-reward imbalance questionnaire

The ERI-Q [12, 13] assesses Effort (6 items), Reward (11 items), and Overcommitment (6 items) on 4-point Likert scales. The ERI ratio (primary stress indicator) was computed as the sum of Effort items divided by the sum of Reward items, with the standard correction factor applied to account for the unequal number of effort and reward items: ERI = Effort sum / (Reward sum × 6/11), following Siegrist et al. [13]. Ratios > 1 indicate imbalance. The Vietnamese version was developed via forward-backward translation and piloted with 40 HCWs (not included in the main sample). Baseline CFA confirmed the three-factor structure: CFI = 0.93, TLI = 0.91, RMSEA = 0.057, SRMR = 0.063. Cronbach’s α at T1: Effort = 0.84; Reward = 0.86; Overcommitment = 0.79. Full psychometric details are in Additional file 2 (Supplementary Table S1).

Job satisfaction: Job Satisfaction Survey (JSS)

The JSS [21] assessed job satisfaction across 9 facets: pay, promotion, supervision, fringe benefits, contingent rewards, operating procedures, co-workers, nature of work, and communication (36 items; 6-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = disagree very much to 6 = agree very much; total range 36–216). Nineteen items are negatively worded and were reverse-scored prior to summation, following standard JSS scoring procedures [21]. Higher total scores indicate greater job satisfaction. Two Vietnamese translations of the JSS are listed on the developer’s website [22]; the translation used in this study was developed independently via forward-backward translation because neither existing version had been psychometrically validated in a healthcare worker sample at the time of study design. The JSS is freely available for non-commercial academic research; use was in accordance with conditions specified by the scale developer. The Vietnamese version was developed via forward-backward translation per WHO [20] guidelines and piloted with 40 HCWs (not included in the main sample). Baseline CFA confirmed adequate fit: CFI = 0.94, TLI = 0.92, RMSEA = 0.053, SRMR = 0.061. Cronbach’s α at T1: JSS total = 0.91; subscale α ranged from 0.72 to 0.86. Cross-group measurement invariance of the JSS across professional groups was evaluated (Methods 3.5): configural and metric invariance were supported, and partial scalar invariance was achieved after freeing one JSS indicator intercept corresponding to promotion opportunities; full results are in Additional file 2, Supplementary Table S1b.

Depression and anxiety

The PHQ-9 [23] and GAD-7 [24] provided validated measures of depressive and anxiety symptom severity. It is important to note that these instruments assess symptom severity rather than clinical diagnosis; scores should be interpreted as continuous indicators of psychological distress, not as diagnostic classifications. Both have demonstrated adequate psychometric properties in Vietnamese populations [7, 25]. PHQ-9 α = 0.86; GAD-7 α = 0.88 (T1).

PCL-5 (collected, not analyzed)

The PCL-5 [26] was collected but excluded from all statistical analyses and result reporting because no Criterion A index event was anchored.

Resilience

The Brief Resilience Scale (BRS; [27]) is a 6-item, 5-point Likert scale measuring capacity to recover from stress. α = 0.83 at T1.

Coping strategies

The Brief COPE [28] assessed 14 coping subscales. Following Meyer [29], subscales were classified into adaptive coping (active coping, positive reframing, acceptance, planning, use of emotional support, use of instrumental support; 12 items; α = 0.79) and maladaptive coping (self-distraction, denial, venting, behavioral disengagement, self-blame; 10 items; α = 0.74). These composite definitions were determined prior to fitting the primary RI-CLPM models based on conceptual and psychometric considerations; they were not pre-specified in a registered protocol. Three subscales were excluded from composites based on pre-analysis decisions: humor (high variability and inconsistent loading patterns in pilot subgroups); substance use (floor effect > 92%); religion (high floor effects; religious coping in Vietnamese contexts may reflect culturally specific meaning-making distinct from Western adaptive/maladaptive categorizations, warranting separate examination). Religion was retained as an individual descriptive subscale. Additional file 2 (Supplementary Table S8) summarizes the composition and analytic classification of all 14 Brief COPE subscales, including the three subscales excluded from the adaptive and maladaptive coping composites.

Social support

The MSPSS [30] assessed perceived support from family (4 items), friends (4 items), and significant others (4 items) on a 7-point Likert scale (α = 0.90 at T1). The SPOS-8 [31, 32] measured perceived organizational support broadly; it does not isolate supervisor support as a separate construct (α = 0.87 at T1). Longitudinal measurement invariance results for the Brief COPE composites, MSPSS total, and SPOS are reported in Methods 3.5 and Additional file 2, Supplementary Table S1c. Metric invariance was supported for all these measures; scalar invariance was supported for MSPSS total and SPOS, was not supported for maladaptive coping, and was not evaluated for adaptive coping.

Procedure

Participants completed surveys electronically at each wave (paper alternatives for approximately 8% without digital access). The target inter-wave interval was six months (T1–T2) and twelve months (T1–T3); actual intervals varied by individual enrollment date within each recruitment window (January–March for T1/T3, July–September for T2). Median inter-wave intervals and sensitivity analyses for survey month are reported in Additional file 2 (Supplementary Table S5). Research assistants at each facility provided support. Completion time was approximately 35–45 min per wave. A facility-level lottery for minor healthcare products (value ≤ 200,000 VND) served as a retention incentive. Participants who expressed distress or requested support - including, but not formally limited to, those endorsing PHQ-9 item 9 or other items at high frequency - were provided written information about available psychological support services at their facility; a pre-specified quantitative referral threshold was not used, and no claim of “clinically elevated distress” is made based on survey responses alone. Research assistants were briefed on recognizing and responding appropriately to participant distress during administration. For electronic surveys, individual item-level responses were visible only to the research assistant administering that session, not to facility HR or supervisors; referral was self-initiated by the participant after being offered written information about facility psychological support services, rather than proactively triggered by the research team contacting HR with identifiable response data. A referral pathway to the facility HR/occupational health office was available to any participant who chose to use it, independent of specific instrument scores. Research assistants, who were affiliated with the research team rather than facility management, were instructed to facilitate immediate contact with emergency services or the facility’s designated urgent-care pathway if a participant directly disclosed imminent risk of harm to self or others, independent of any survey score; this instruction was part of standard research assistant briefing rather than a scored, instrument-based protocol. No participant required activation of this emergency pathway during data collection; this reflects the absence of a disclosed imminent-risk situation during the study rather than the absence of a procedure for one. Completed paper surveys were stored in locked facility offices accessible only to the research team and transferred to password-protected institutional storage within one week of administration. This procedure was reviewed and approved as part of the primary ethics approval. Survey mode (electronic vs. paper) was recorded and included as an auxiliary variable in imputation models and examined in sensitivity analyses; no substantive differences were found between survey modes.

Analytical strategy

Data were analyzed using R 4.3.1 (lavaan; [33]) and Mplus 8.9. Preliminary analyses included descriptive statistics, internal consistency (α), and Little’s MCAR test. Missing scale-score data were addressed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE; m = 20; Missing at Random assumption); estimates were pooled across imputed datasets using Rubin’s rules, and indirect effects used the product-of-coefficients approach with Monte Carlo confidence intervals (10,000 draws). Item-level missingness by wave, imputation-model specifications, and sensitivity comparisons are provided in Additional file 2 (Supplementary Tables S2 and S4).

Longitudinal measurement invariance (configural, metric, scalar) of the measurement models for the JSS, PHQ-9, GAD-7, and BRS was tested across T1–T3 using nested CFA models. Configural and metric invariance was supported for all four scales. Scalar invariance was supported for the JSS, BRS, and GAD-7 measurement models. For PHQ-9, partial scalar invariance was achieved (one item intercept freed); latent mean comparisons on PHQ-9 should be interpreted with caution [34]. Full fit statistics are in Additional file 2 (Supplementary Table S1). ERI, adaptive coping, maladaptive coping, MSPSS total, and SPOS were used as composite observed scores in RI-CLPM. Longitudinal measurement invariance was subsequently evaluated for ERI, adaptive coping, maladaptive coping, MSPSS total, and SPOS using the same nested CFA procedure applied to JSS, PHQ-9, GAD-7, and BRS, evaluated against ΔCFI ≤ 0.010 and ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015 criteria [35]. Configural and metric invariance were supported for all five measures. Scalar invariance was supported for MSPSS total and SPOS; scalar invariance was not supported for maladaptive coping (ΔCFI = 0.018); scalar-level invariance was not evaluated for ERI or adaptive coping, so mean-level comparisons involving these two measures are not interpreted. Metric invariance supports the temporal comparability of factor loadings; however, because scalar invariance was not established for ERI, adaptive coping, and maladaptive coping, RI-CLPM estimates involving their observed composite-score deviations remain provisional and may partly reflect measurement drift rather than true psychological change. Cross-group (professional-group) measurement invariance of the JSS was also tested: configural and metric invariance were supported. Full scalar invariance did not hold (ΔCFI = 0.017 relative to the metric model, exceeding the 0.010 criterion); after freeing one indicator intercept (corresponding to promotion opportunities) for the pharmacist subgroup, partial scalar invariance was supported (ΔCFI = 0.009, ΔRMSEA = 0.008), providing reasonable, though incomplete, support for the exploratory nurse-versus-other-group trajectory comparisons reported in Results 4.3. Full invariance model fit statistics are reported in Additional file 2, Supplementary Table S1b.

Primary analyses proceeded in two stages. First, latent growth curve (LGC) modeling estimated trajectory intercepts and slopes using piecewise parameterization (Slope 1 = T1–T2; Slope 2 = T2–T3). With only three measurement occasions, this piecewise specification is not identified if all growth-factor variances, covariances, and wave-specific residual variances are freely estimated. Identification was achieved by fixing the Slope 2 (T2→T3) variance to zero, constraining residual variances to be equal across the three waves, and estimating only the intercept–Slope 1 covariance (the intercept–Slope 2 and Slope 1–Slope 2 covariances were not estimated, since Slope 2 has no estimated variance); this yields df = 2 for each outcome. Fixing the Slope 2 variance means the model does not capture individual differences in the T2–T3 rate of change; the Slope 2 mean therefore describes the average second-interval trajectory only. With only three waves, the selected two-slope piecewise growth specification requires identifying constraints, because the fully unrestricted model contains more freely estimated growth and residual parameters than can be identified from the observed moments available with three waves [36]. Among the possible sets of identifying constraints, we fixed the Slope 2 variance to zero and constrained residual variances to be equal across waves rather than constraining the two slope variances to equality, because equality-constraining them would itself impose an untested assumption that within-person variability is identical across the two intervals, whereas fixing the Slope 2 variance preserves a freely estimated variance for the first interval, which showed the larger mean decline and was the primary period of substantive interest for this study. Freely estimating both slope variances under this specification would require additional longitudinal information, such as a fourth measurement wave, or an alternative identifying approach; a fourth wave is planned for the intended follow-up phase of this cohort but was not available for the present analysis. Full model specifications and fit statistics are reported in Table 3 and Supplementary Table S1. Multi-group LGC models were used to explore, on an unadjusted exploratory basis, whether trajectories differed across professional groups.

Second, Random Intercept Cross-Lagged Panel Models (RI-CLPM; [37]) estimated within-person lagged associations. Each RI-CLPM included random intercepts for ERI, the mediator, and JSS across T1–T3. Within-person latent residuals were specified at each wave. Autoregressive paths for ERI, the mediator, and JSS were estimated across adjacent waves (T1→T2, T2→T3). Reciprocal cross-lagged paths among ERI, mediator, and JSS were estimated to test for reverse directionality. Within-wave residual covariances between ERI and mediator, between mediator and JSS, and between ERI and JSS, were all freely estimated at T2 and T3 (i.e., no within-wave residual covariance among the three constructs was fixed to zero). Primary unstandardized indirect associations from the five single-mediator RI-CLPMs are reported in Table 4; exploratory MSPSS subscale and contemporaneous serial-path analyses are reported in Additional file 2, Supplementary Table S9, and parallel multi-mediator robustness results are reported in Supplementary Table S9b. Full model parameter output is available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. Baseline age, sex, professional group, years of experience, night-shift status, and facility ownership were included as time-invariant covariates predicting random intercepts. Equality constraints on autoregressive or cross-lagged paths were not imposed. Each mediator was tested in a separate RI-CLPM to maintain model parsimony given the three-wave design. Unstandardized coefficients are reported throughout to preserve comparability across imputed datasets and facilitate interpretation in the original scale metric. Model fit: CFI ≥ 0.95, RMSEA ≤ 0.06, SRMR ≤ 0.08 [39].

Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) at baseline ranged from 0.04 to 0.09. Cluster-robust standard errors (k = 15 facilities; TYPE = COMPLEX) were applied. Given the small number of clusters, cluster-robust inference should be interpreted cautiously. Sensitivity analyses comparing conventional, cluster-robust, and facility fixed-effects specifications are reported in Additional file 2 (Supplementary Table S4) as indirect estimates (a×b) and 95% confidence intervals for the five primary pathways under each approach. A Harman single-factor diagnostic indicated that a single common factor accounted for 18.3% of total variance; this result suggests that variance is not dominated by a single factor, but this analysis cannot rule out correlated method variance, negative affectivity, or other same-source bias, which remain plausible confounds.

Selection bias, information bias, and confounding were examined as follows. Selection bias was assessed by comparing invited and enrolled participants on available demographic indicators; no significant differences were found. Information bias from self-report was evaluated using the Harman test as above; residual self-report variance cannot be fully excluded. Potential confounders, including age, sex, professional group, experience, night-shift status, and facility type, were controlled as time-invariant covariates predicting RI-CLPM random intercepts. Time-varying confounding from unmeasured variables (e.g., life events, organizational changes) cannot be ruled out in the absence of randomization.

Participant characteristics and attrition

Of 1,500 HCWs enrolled at baseline, 1,421 completed T2 (attrition: 5.3%) and 1,389 completed T3 (cumulative attrition: 7.4%). Dropout reasons: employment change (n = 54), personal/family emergencies (n = 32), consent withdrawal (n = 25). Planned employment change was assessed at baseline as an exclusion criterion (Methods 3.2); the employment-change dropouts reported here were unanticipated at enrollment and occurred during follow-up, and are therefore distinct from the baseline exclusion. Little’s MCAR test was non-significant (χ² = 18.42, p = .21). Multiple imputation was conducted under a Missing at Random (MAR) assumption (see Methods 3.5); attrition analyses did not identify statistically significant baseline differences between completers and non-completers on any primary variable (all p > .10), which provides indirect, reassuring evidence but does not by itself establish that the MAR assumption holds. A Harman single-factor diagnostic did not indicate dominance of a single common factor (18.3% of variance); this diagnostic cannot exclude correlated method variance, negative affectivity, or other same-source bias. Longitudinal trajectories: latent growth curve analyses

Table 3 presents LGC parameters, including model identification and fit information (χ², df, CFI, TLI, RMSEA, SRMR) for each outcome. For job satisfaction, the intercept was significant (γ = 118.43, SE = 0.42, p < .001). The T1–T2 slope was significant and negative (γ = -5.17, SE = 0.38, p < .001), and the T2–T3 slope was also significant and negative, though smaller in magnitude (γ = -1.24, SE = 0.31, p < .001). A comparable pattern was observed for PHQ-9, where the T2–T3 slope reached significance at the 0.05 level (γ = 0.48, SE = 0.21, p = .022), and for GAD-7, where the T2–T3 slope did not reach significance (γ = 0.31, SE = 0.19, p = .10). Within the constrained LGC itself, no formal equality-constraint test between the T1–T2 and T2–T3 slopes was conducted; a direct test of this contrast is reported below via a mixed-effects sensitivity model. Mean JSS total scores declined from T1 (M = 118.43) to T2 (M = 113.26) and were lower again at T3 (M = 112.02). Imputed-N estimates were substantively similar. A standardized effect size for the T1-to-T3 decline was computed post hoc: Cohen’s d_av = 0.29, calculated as the mean difference divided by the pooled wave-specific standard deviation (T1 SD = 21.8, T3 SD = 22.4), indicating a small standardized mean change. Applying Spector’s published JSS scoring bands (total score < 108 = dissatisfied; 108–144 = ambivalent; > 144 = satisfied) [21, 22], both the T1 mean (118.4) and the T3 mean (112.0) fall within the ambivalent range, rather than crossing into the dissatisfied category.

As a robustness check on the constrained piecewise LGC, an alternative trajectory parameterization not requiring the piecewise LGC’s fixed second-slope variance or equal residual-variance constraints was fit to JSS: a linear mixed-effects model with time modeled as a categorical fixed effect (T1 as reference), a random intercept for participant, and cluster-robust SEs (k = 15 facilities). Estimated marginal contrasts (later wave minus earlier wave, matching the sign convention of the LGC slopes in Table 3) were: T2 vs. T1 = -5.10 (SE = 0.36, 95% CI [-5.80, -4.39], p < .001); T3 vs. T1 = -6.35 (SE = 0.40, 95% CI [-7.13, -5.57], p < .001); T3 vs. T2 = -1.25 (SE = 0.33, 95% CI [-1.90, -0.60], p < .001), directionally consistent with the constrained LGC estimates (T1→T2 γ = -5.17; T2→T3 γ = -1.24). Individually, these three contrasts only establish that each interval change differed from zero; they do not by themselves test whether the two interval changes differed from each other. A direct contrast-of-contrasts, (T3 vs. T2) - (T2 vs. T1), was therefore estimated: b = 3.85 (SE = 0.47, 95% CI [2.93, 4.77], p < .001), indicating that the T3-vs-T2 decline was significantly smaller in magnitude than the T2-vs-T1 decline. The mixed-effects model was estimated separately within each of the m = 20 imputed datasets and pooled using Rubin’s rules, matching the pooling approach used for the LGC and RI-CLPM models; contrasts and the contrast-of-contrasts above are pooled estimates. Full mixed-effects output, including the contrast-of-contrasts for PHQ-9 and GAD-7, is reported in Additional file 2, Supplementary Table S1.

Exploratory, unadjusted multi-group LGC analyses suggested differences in the estimated magnitude of T1–T2 decline across professional groups. Nurses showed the steepest estimated decline (γ = -7.23, SE = 0.54, p < .001) relative to physicians (γ = -4.38), pharmacists (γ = -3.91), and technicians (γ = -4.17); Wald tests indicated the nurse slope was significantly steeper than the other groups (all p < .01). PHQ-9 trajectory differences were also significant across groups (χ²_diff = 18.4, df = 6, p < .01). Because only partial scalar invariance was supported for the JSS (Methods 3.5) and workload composition, remuneration, role autonomy, and patient-ratio data were not collected, these comparisons should be treated as descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmed profession-specific trajectory differences.

RI-CLPM lagged indirect-association analyses

Table 4 presents RI-CLPM results as single-mediator lagged indirect associations, each estimated in a separate model. Because each mediator was tested in a separate RI-CLPM, model fit statistics varied across models; the values reported here (CFI = 0.96, RMSEA = 0.048 [90% CI 0.041, 0.055], SRMR = 0.061) reflect the resilience (BRS) model as a representative example. Exploratory MSPSS subscale and contemporaneous serial-path analyses are reported in Additional file 2, Supplementary Table S9; full model parameter output for each individual mediator model is available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. Only unstandardized indirect effects (product of the a-path and b-path coefficients) with Monte Carlo confidence intervals are reported, in the main text and in Table 4; standardized estimates are not reported (see Methods 3.5). Proportions mediated are not computed or interpreted, as mediators were tested in separate, non-independent models with overlapping variance (MSPSS total shares item content with its friends/family subscales; resilience, coping, and support measures are correlated with one another). Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied across the five pre-specified primary pathways (BRS, adaptive coping, maladaptive coping, MSPSS total, SPOS); all five remained significant at q < 0.001 (Table 4). Exploratory pathways (MSPSS friends/family subscales, the exploratory contemporaneous serial-path analysis) were not included in the FDR correction and are not adjusted for multiplicity. Resilience, adaptive coping, maladaptive coping, SPOS, and MSPSS total constitute the pre-specified primary pathways (H3–H5), selected on theoretical grounds prior to fitting the RI-CLPM models but not preregistered; MSPSS friends and family subscales and an exploratory contemporaneous serial-path analysis were treated as exploratory and are reported separately below. Because the separate mediator models do not test a simultaneous or comparative mediation structure, relative pathway magnitudes should not be interpreted as evidence that one psychological resource is a stronger or more proximal mechanism than another; findings are reported as single-mediator lagged indirect associations, not as confirmed mediation mechanisms.

As a robustness check, an exploratory parallel multi-mediator RI-CLPM including all five resources simultaneously as within-person predictors of T3 job satisfaction was fitted (Additional file 2, Supplementary Table S9b). Indirect associations were directionally consistent with the single-mediator models for all five pathways, with model fit meeting criteria for RMSEA and SRMR (CFI slightly below the 0.95 threshold) and no Heywood cases; full fit statistics, model limitations (shared-wave measurement, multicollinearity, model complexity), and pathway-level results are reported in Supplementary Table S9b.

Sensitivity analyses: ERI–JSS content overlap

Because the ERI Reward subscale (pay, esteem, career opportunities, job security) and JSS (pay, promotion, contingent rewards, fringe benefits) share item-level content, three sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess whether this conceptual overlap could account for the ERI–JSS association (Additional file 2, Supplementary Table S4b). First, Effort and Reward were modeled separately rather than as a combined ratio: raw Reward scores were positively associated with JSS (r = + 0.54), more strongly than the negative association between Effort and JSS (r = − .18). Second, a reduced-JSS outcome excluding the overlapping pay/promotion/fringe-benefits/contingent-rewards facets was constructed; the ERI–reduced-JSS correlation attenuated from − 0.52 to − 0.41. Third, the primary ERI→BRS→JSS indirect association was re-estimated with this reduced-JSS outcome: −0.98, 95% MC CI [− 1.34, − 0.63], versus − 1.28 with the full JSS - a 23% attenuation, remaining significant and directionally consistent. The same reduced-JSS specification was then applied to the remaining four primary pathways: adaptive coping attenuated from − 0.35 [− 0.49, − 0.21] to − 0.27 [− 0.39, − 0.15]; maladaptive coping from − 0.31 [− 0.44, − 0.18] to − 0.24 [− 0.35, − 0.13]; SPOS from − 0.24 [− 0.37, − 0.12] to − 0.19 [− 0.30, − 0.09]; and MSPSS total from − 0.29 [− 0.42, − 0.16] to − 0.22 [− 0.33, − 0.11]. Benjamini–Hochberg correction was applied jointly across all five reduced-JSS pathway tests (resilience plus the four newly estimated pathways); all five remained directionally consistent and statistically significant at q < 0.01, with 21–24% attenuation for the four newly estimated pathways, closely comparable to the 23% attenuation observed for the resilience pathway. Detailed results for all five reduced-JSS sensitivity analyses are presented in Additional file 2, Supplementary Table S4b.

Discussion

Principal findings

Throughout this Discussion, “indirect association” refers to the longitudinal product-of-coefficients estimate (a×b) [38]; we use “indirect association,” “lagged association,” “pattern consistent with COR theory,” and “candidate explanatory pathway” to describe the observed longitudinal patterns, consistent with Results 4.4 and Discussion 5.2, without implying causal inference.

This three-wave longitudinal study provides preliminary within-person evidence on job satisfaction trajectories and associated psychological resource patterns among Vietnamese HCWs. Four principal findings emerge.

First, mean job satisfaction scores declined across the observed study waves: from T1 (M = 118.43) to T2 (M = 113.26), and were lower again at T3 (M = 112.02), with a significantly smaller second-interval decrement, confirmed directly by a mixed-effects contrast-of-contrasts test (Results 4.3). The T1-to-T3 decline was small in standardized magnitude (Cohen’s d_av = 0.29) and, applying Spector’s published JSS scoring bands, remained within the ambivalent range rather than crossing into the dissatisfied category (Results 4.3). These findings are directionally consistent with international evidence on declining HCW job satisfaction under high-demand conditions [1, 40], though cross-study comparison is complicated by differences in profession mix, instruments, and country context.

Second, in unadjusted exploratory multi-group LGC analyses, nurses showed the largest estimated T1–T2 decline in job satisfaction; however, this comparison did not control for between-group differences in age, sex, years of experience, or night-shift frequency. Only partial scalar invariance was supported for the JSS (Methods 3.5). These findings should be treated as descriptive and exploratory, broadly consistent with international evidence on nursing workforce satisfaction [41] but not interpretable as confirmed profession-specific trajectory differences.

Third, RI-CLPM analyses identified lagged associations involving deviations in observed composite scores: higher-than-usual ERI was followed by lower resilience, lower adaptive coping, higher maladaptive coping, lower perceived organizational support, and lower total perceived social support; each of these deviations was, in turn, associated with lower job satisfaction, consistent with COR theory predictions. Estimates involving ERI and the coping composites remain provisional because scalar invariance was incomplete for these measures (Methods 3.5).

Fourth, both primary support pathways - perceived organizational support (SPOS) and total perceived social support (MSPSS total) - showed significant lagged indirect associations with JSS. Exploratory subscale decompositions of MSPSS total (friends, family) are reported in Supplementary Table S9 and should not be interpreted as comparative tests of support sources, since they were not tested in a simultaneous model and were not all fitted (the significant-other subscale was not modeled). SPOS measures perceived organizational support broadly, not supervisor support in isolation; this distinction limits comparisons with research using supervisor-specific measures.

Theoretical implications

These findings extend the JD-R model, COR theory, and Spector’s [21] multidimensional model of job satisfaction to a Southeast Asian LMIC context, providing preliminary within-person longitudinal evidence consistent with the proposition that occupational stress is associated with reduced job satisfaction through psychological resource pathways. The exploratory contemporaneous serial-path analysis is statistically compatible with a COR resource cascade account, though because both mediators were assessed concurrently at T2, this cannot be interpreted as sequential mediation; replication with temporally separated mediator assessments is required. Future theoretical development should attend to cultural moderators such as collectivist norms around help-seeking, hierarchical power dynamics, and face concerns around dissatisfaction disclosure, any of which may amplify or attenuate these pathways in Vietnamese and comparable LMIC contexts; evidence from a related Confucian-heritage Southeast Asian workforce on work-value and job-attitude patterns [42] offers indirect, analogous support for this possibility rather than direct evidence on Vietnamese disclosure norms. A further theoretical consideration is potential construct overlap between the ERI Reward subscale and JSS facets (pay, promotion, contingent rewards, fringe benefits), which could inflate the ERI–JSS association beyond a purely psychological stress-to-satisfaction pathway. Sensitivity analyses reported in Results 4.5 show that a reduced-JSS specification excluding the overlapping facets attenuated all five primary indirect associations by 21–24% while leaving them directionally consistent and statistically significant. This indicates that ERI–Reward/JSS content overlap accounts for a similar, partial proportion of the association across all five pathways; the persistence of all five associations after removing the most directly overlapping facets suggests the findings were not fully explained by this specific source of item-content overlap, though other forms of conceptual or method-related overlap cannot be ruled out (Results 4.5; Additional file 2, Supplementary Table S4b).

Practical implications

The observed patterns of indirect associations are consistent with candidate psychological targets for future intervention research; these associations do not establish causal mechanisms and require replication with experimental designs. Resilience-focused interventions (for example, mindfulness-based stress reduction, Psychological Capital Development [PsyCap] training, and stress appraisal reframing) could be prioritized for evaluation in future intervention studies, though the present findings identify candidate targets rather than evidence of intervention effectiveness. Coping-focused interventions targeting active planning, positive reappraisal, and recognition of self-blaming or avoidant patterns could be evaluated as candidate strategies in future randomized studies. Organizational interventions targeting supervisor communication, psychologically safe reporting systems, peer support programs, and reward-structure transparency could be prioritized for empirical evaluation. Confidential mental health and job satisfaction screening with referral pathways and workload monitoring indicators (night-shift frequency, overtime, patient-to-staff ratios) may enable early identification of at-risk individuals. These findings identify plausible intervention targets based on observed associations, and should be evaluated in future randomized trials before conclusions about effectiveness can be drawn. At a systems level, incorporating HCW job satisfaction and psychological wellbeing indicators within facility monitoring frameworks may warrant exploratory feasibility evaluation.

Limitations and future directions

Key limitations: (1) With only three measurement waves, the piecewise LGC model required fixing the Slope 2 (T2→T3) variance to zero and constraining residual variances equal across waves for identification (Methods 3.5; Table 3 note); this means individual differences in the second slope could not be estimated, and sequential ordering among concurrently measured RI-CLPM mediators cannot be empirically established. Extended follow-up with ≥ 4 waves and separated mediator assessment occasions is needed. (2) Scalar invariance was supported for MSPSS total and SPOS but not for maladaptive coping, and was not evaluated for ERI or adaptive coping (Methods 3.5); mean-level and observed-score interpretations involving these three measures should therefore remain cautious. (3) All measures were self-reported; common method variance, although assessed via Harman single-factor CFA, cannot be fully ruled out, and objective indicators (administrative records, cortisol, absenteeism) should be incorporated in future work. (4) Excluding participants receiving pharmacological treatment for major psychiatric disorders (n = 38) means that the findings characterize healthcare workers not currently receiving such treatment rather than the full HCW workforce. Because medication use may reflect symptom severity, access to treatment, or general health status, this criterion may have preferentially excluded workers with greater symptom burden. The study may therefore underestimate depressive and anxiety symptom severity in the broader HCW population. (5) The 15 facilities were purposively, not randomly, selected, and cluster-robust inference is based on only k = 15 clusters with limited precision; findings generalize to HCWs employed at these participating HCMC facilities rather than to all HCMC HCWs or to rural and northern Vietnamese contexts, and cluster-adjusted results (Supplementary Table S4) should be interpreted cautiously. (6) The study was not prospectively registered, and the pre-specified primary pathways, subgroup comparisons, and cross-group JSS trajectory contrasts (only partially supported by invariance testing) were not adjusted for occupational or demographic confounding; these exploratory comparisons should not be interpreted as confirmed profession-specific effects. (7) Causal inference remains constrained by potential unmeasured time-varying confounding; RI-CLPM partitions stable between-person variance but does not eliminate unmeasured time-varying confounders. (8) The ERI ratio combines Effort and Reward into a single index; because ERI Reward items overlap conceptually with several JSS facets, part of the ERI–JSS association may reflect content overlap. Sensitivity analyses (Results 4.5; Supplementary Table S4b) indicated this overlap accounts for part, but not all, of the association, with all five reduced-JSS indirect associations remaining directionally consistent and significant (21–24% attenuation).

Future directions: (1) Pre-registered replication with ≥ 4 waves and prospectively validated trajectory risk profiles as stratification variables; (2) incorporating objective workload and organizational indicators; (3) experience sampling methodology for within-day fluctuation analysis; (4) randomized controlled trials of resilience and coping interventions; (5) exploration of cultural moderators specific to Vietnamese and comparable LMIC HCW populations.

Conclusions

This 12-month prospective cohort study of 1,500 HCWs in Ho Chi Minh City found a mean decline in job satisfaction from T1 to T2 and a significantly smaller mean decline from T2 to T3, confirmed by an alternative mixed-effects sensitivity model that did not impose the piecewise LGC’s fixed second-slope variance or equal residual-variance constraints; exploratory unadjusted analyses suggested heterogeneity in the estimated magnitude of decline across professional groups. Higher deviations in observed ERI composite scores at T1 were associated with lower resilience, lower adaptive coping, higher maladaptive coping, lower total perceived social support, and lower organizational support at T2; in separate RI-CLPMs, each of these five mediator deviations was, in turn, associated with lower job satisfaction at T3 (all FDR-significant, q < 0.001), consistent with, but not establishing, COR theory resource-depletion pathways. These estimates should be interpreted cautiously given incomplete scalar-invariance evidence for ERI, adaptive coping, and maladaptive coping. Reduced-JSS sensitivity analyses across all five primary pathways produced attenuated but directionally consistent and statistically significant indirect associations, suggesting that the results were not fully explained by the most directly overlapping ERI–Reward and JSS content. By examining within-person associations rather than between-person differences, this study offers preliminary longitudinal evidence relevant to occupational health psychology in Vietnamese healthcare settings, and identifies candidate targets for future pre-registered intervention research across all five pathways.
