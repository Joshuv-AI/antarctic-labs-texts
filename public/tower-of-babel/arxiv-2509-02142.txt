Electromagnetic responses of bilayer excitonic insulators
Yuelin Shao, Hao Shi, Xi Dai

Abstract
We investigate the electromagnetic responses of a bilayer excitonic insulators (EI) and identify two types of collective modes: (1) Two gapped plasmon modes couple to the layer symmetric gauge field. The transverse mode is nearly dispersionless in the long-wavelength limit, while the longitudinal mode, accounting for total charge fluctuations, has a linear dispersion with velocity proportional to two dimensional (2D) electrical polarizability. (2) A gapless phase (Goldstone) mode and a gapped amplitude mode, associated with the fluctuations of EI order parameter, couple to the layer antisymmetric gauge field. In the long-wavelength limit, the Goldstone mode exhibits linear dispersion with velocity inversely proportional to the square root of exciton compressibility, representing the first sound mode of the exciton condensate Significantly, its linear dispersion yields a cubic frequency dependence of the real admittance in microwave impedance microscopy (MIM), providing a method to detect the Goldstone mode directly.



I
Introduction

The excitonic insulator (EI) is a correlated state in which excitons—bound states of electrons and holes—condense at low temperatures
Mott 1961
;
Jérome et al. 1967
;
Kohn 1967
.
As a Bose-Einstein condensation state that breaks the electron-hole
\mathrm{U}_{eh}(1)
symmetry, the EI state is predicted to exhibit a gapless Goldstone mode and exciton superfluidity
Littlewood et al. 2004
;
Balatsky et al. 2004
;
Su and MacDonald 2008
.
Two dimensional (2D) electron-hole bilayers have been proposed as ideal platforms for realizing EI phases experimentally
Zhu et al. 1995
;
Fogler et al. 2014
;
Wu et al. 2015
;
Zeng and MacDonald 2020
.
Recently, significant experimental progress has been achieved in observing signatures of the EI phase in diverse bilayer systems, such as semiconductor quantum wells
Du et al. 2017
;
Wu et al. 2019a
;
Wu et al. 2019b
and transition metal dichalcogenide (TMD) double-layer structures
Wang et al. 2019
;
Ma et al. 2021
;
Qi et al. 2023
;
Qi et al. 2025a
;
Nguyen et al. 2025a
;
Cutshall et al. 2025
.

The experimental setup of the electron-hole bilayer is illustrated in FIG.
1
.
Under effective mass approximation, the conduction band in the electron layer and valence band in the hole layer are both described by parabolic dispersions with effective masses
m_{e/h}
, separated by a band gap
E_{g}
.
Due to the weak screening in 2D systems, electron-hole excitations will form a series a interlayer exciton levels below the band gap.
By introducing a dielectric barrier between the electron and hole layers, direct interlayer tunneling is exponentially suppressed, significantly increasing the exciton lifetime.
We denote the lowest exciton binding energy by
E_{B}
.
Typically, the binding energy is smaller than the band gap
E_{g}
, implying that excitons normally exist as excited states.
To injection excitons into the bilayer system, an interlayer bias potential
V_{b}
is usually applied.
When there is an interlayer tunneling, the tunneling current will drive the system into an nonequilibrium state
Sun et al. 2021
;
Sun et al. 2024
;
Zeng et al. 2024
.
However, if the tunneling effect is negligible, the bias potential will effectively reduce the band gap to
E_{g}-eV_{b}
, serving as the exciton chemical potential
\mu_{X}\equiv eV_{b}-E_{g}
.
When the band gap is tunned to be smaller than the exciton binding energy, the normal insulator (NI) state will be unstable.
At low temperatures, excitons are expected to spontaneously generate and condense, known as the BEC limit of EI.
When
\mu_{X}
is further increased such that the band offset becomes negative, the system will enter a semimetal state with both electron and hole Fermi surfaces.
The attractive interactions between electrons and holes then induce a pairing instability that gaps out these Fermi surfaces, resulting in an insulating state.
Analogous to the BCS mechanism in superconductivity, this regime corresponds to the BCS limit of EI

Figure 1:
Setup of the bilayer EIs.
The electron and hole layers are encapsulated in the dielectric environment with dielectric constant
\epsilon
.
Besides, the dielectric spacer is also inserted between the two layers to avoid direct tunneling.
The interlayer band gap could be tuned by the bias voltage
V_{b}
.

Although the EI ground states have been extensively investigated, our understanding of the collective excitations remains limited, with only a few pioneering studies addressing the phase and amplitude modes of EI order parameter fluctuations
Golež et al. 2020
;
Murakami et al. 2020
;
Kaneko and Ohta 2025
.
In this work, we systemically study the collective modes and electromagnetic responses of bilayer EIs using the time dependent Hartree Fock (TDHF) method and the following are the key findings:
1) When a layer symmetric gauge field is applied, two gapped plasmon modes are identified.
The longitudinal mode couples to fluctuations in total charge and longitudinal current, whereas the transverse mode couples to transverse current fluctuations.
This difference leads to a splitting of the longitudinal and transverse modes at finite wavelength.
Specifically, the longitudinal mode acquires a linear dispersion due to coupling with the long-range Coulomb interaction.
Such non-analytic linear dispersion behavior has also been noticed in other 2D systems, including excitons and optical phonons, reflecting similar underlying physics
Qiu et al. 2015
;
Sohier et al. 2017
;
Rivera et al. 2019
;
Shi et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2024
.
Besides, previous study also shows that these modes soften in the presence of an in-plane electrical field, leading to a unique breakdown mechanism of EIs in the BCS limit
Shao and Dai 2024a
.
2) When a layer antisymmetric gauge field is applied, a gapless phase (Goldstone) mode and a gapped amplitude mode are identified in the longitudinal responses.
These modes originate from fluctuations of the complex EI order parameter and directly couple to fluctuations in exciton density and longitudinal exciton current.

There are other excitations contributing to the charge and exciton current fluctuations at finite wavelength.
But in the long-wavelength and low-frequency limit, only the gapless Goldstone mode is relevant to the electromagnetic responses and could be detected by the microwave impedance microscopy (MIM)
Barber et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2023
as shown in FIG.
1
.
Typically, the tip in an MIM setup is placed sufficiently far from the sample, eliminating direct tunneling currents.
Under these conditions, the tip-sample system behaves as a capacitor, exhibiting purely imaginary admittance.
In contrast, within bilayer EIs, the alternating tip charge
Q_{t}(\omega)
induces a layer-antisymmetric potential that excites the Goldstone mode.
Due to the linear dispersion of the Goldstone mode, this excitation yields a real admittance in MIM.
Specifically, to lowest order in frequency, the real part of the admittance displays a distinctive cubic (
\omega^{3}
) frequency dependence.
Moreover, varying the tip-sample distance
d_{t}
results in an exponential decay of the real admittance proportional to
e^{-2\omega d_{t}/v}
where
v
is the Goldstone mode velocity.
This provides a clear and direct experimental approach for detecting and characterizing the Goldstone mode in bilayer excitonic insulators.

II
Model and method

II.1
Ground state: Hartree-Fock method

The manybody Hamiltonian of the electron-hole bilayer could be written as
\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{H}_{I}
and
Zhu et al. 1995
;
Wu et al. 2015

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{k}}[c^{\dagger}_{e\bm{k}},c^{\dagger}_{h\bm{k}}]\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\hbar^{2}k^{2}}{2m_{e}}-\frac{\mu_{X}}{2}&0\\
0&-\frac{\hbar^{2}k^{2}}{2m_{h}}+\frac{\mu_{X}}{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c_{e\bm{k}}\\
c_{h\bm{k}}\end{bmatrix},

(1a)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{I}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\mathcal{V}}\sum_{ss^{\prime}=eh}\sum_{\bm{k}\bm{k}^{\prime}\bm{q}}V_{ss^{\prime}}(\bm{q})c^{\dagger}_{s\bm{k}}c^{\dagger}_{s^{\prime}\bm{k}^{\prime}}c_{s^{\prime}\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}}c_{s\bm{k}-\bm{q}},

(1b)

where
\mathcal{V}
is the area of the two-dimensional system,
c^{\dagger}_{e\bm{k}}
and
c^{\dagger}_{h\bm{k}}
are the electron creation operators in the two layers.
When the thickness of the dielectric spacer in FIG.
1
satisfies
d^{\prime}\gg d
, the Coulomb interaction between layers could be appropriated as
V(\bm{q})\equiv V_{s=s^{\prime}}(\bm{q})=2\pi e^{2}/\epsilon q
and
U(\bm{q})\equiv V_{s\neq s^{\prime}}=V(\bm{q})e^{-qd}
.
To get this form of interactions, one need to solve the Poisson equations as detailed in Appendix
A
.

In this paper, we focus on the zero temperature.
To get the EI ground state, the Hartree-Fock mean-field method is usually adopted.
By assuming translation symmetry, the nonzero elements of single particle density matrix at the EI ground state are
\rho^{X}_{ss^{\prime}\bm{k}}\equiv\langle c^{\dagger}_{s^{\prime}\bm{k}}c_{s\bm{k}}\rangle
.
And the mean-field Hamiltonian takes the form as

\hat{H}_{MF}=\sum_{\bm{k}}[c^{\dagger}_{e\bm{k}},c^{\dagger}_{h\bm{k}}]\begin{bmatrix}\zeta_{\bm{k}}+\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}&\Delta_{\bm{k}}\\
\Delta^{*}_{\bm{k}}&\zeta_{\bm{k}}-\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c_{e\bm{k}}\\
c_{h\bm{k}}\end{bmatrix}.

(2)

In the above expression,
\zeta_{\bm{k}}\equiv\hbar^{2}k^{2}\delta_{m}/4m
accounts for the electron-hole asymmetry, where
m\equiv m_{e}m_{h}/(m_{e}+m_{h})
is the reduced mass and
\delta_{m}\equiv(m_{h}-m_{e})/(m_{h}+m_{e})
represents the mass imbalance parameter.
The other parameters entering the mean-field Hamiltonian in Eq. (
2
) are explicitly defined as follows:

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar^{2}k^{2}}{4m}-\frac{\mu_{X}}{2}+\frac{2\pi e^{2}dn_{X}}{\epsilon}-\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}V(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime})\rho^{X}_{ee\bm{k}^{\prime}},

(3a)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\bm{k}}\equiv

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\bm{k}}U(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime})\rho^{X}_{eh\bm{k}^{\prime}},

(3b)

where
\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}
represents the renormalized energy difference between the electron and hole bands, and
\Delta_{\bm{k}}
characterizes the interlayer coherence arising from exciton condensation.
Furthermore,
n_{X}\equiv\mathcal{V}^{-1}\sum_{\bm{k}}\rho^{X}_{ee\bm{k}}
denotes the exciton density (charge number density per layer).

In the
s
-wave pairing EI ground state, the phase of the EI order parameter,
\phi=\arg(\Delta_{\bm{k}})
, is independent of momentum
\bm{k}
.
The states corresponding to different values of
\phi
are degenerated, and selecting a particular value of
\phi
signifies spontaneous breaking of the electron-hole
U_{eh}(1)
symmetry.
For convenience and without loss of generality, we choose
\Delta_{\bm{k}}
to be real and negative, thus fixing
\phi=\pi
.
Then, by defining the quasi-particle creation operators of the occupied and empty bands

\displaystyle c^{\dagger}_{v\bm{k}}\equiv\alpha_{\bm{k}}c^{\dagger}_{e\bm{k}}+\beta_{\bm{k}}c^{\dagger}_{h\bm{k}},

(4a)

\displaystyle c^{\dagger}_{c\bm{k}}\equiv\beta_{\bm{k}}c^{\dagger}_{e\bm{k}}-\alpha_{\bm{k}}c^{\dagger}_{h\bm{k}},

(4b)

the mean-field Hamiltonian Eq. (
2
) is diagonalized as

\hat{H}_{MF}=\sum_{\bm{k}}[c^{\dagger}_{c\bm{k}},c^{\dagger}_{v\bm{k}}]\begin{bmatrix}\zeta_{\bm{k}}+\xi_{\bm{k}}&0\\
0&\zeta_{\bm{k}}-\xi_{\bm{k}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c_{c\bm{k}}\\
c_{v\bm{k}}\end{bmatrix},

(5)

where
\xi_{\bm{k}}=\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}^{2}+\Delta^{2}_{\bm{k}}}
,
\alpha_{\bm{k}}=\sqrt{(1-\varepsilon_{k}/\xi_{\bm{k}})/2}
and
\beta_{\bm{k}}=\sqrt{(1+\varepsilon_{k}/\xi_{\bm{k}})/2}
.
Consequently, the single-particle density matrix elements can be recalculated explicitly as

\displaystyle\rho^{X}_{ee\bm{k}}=

\displaystyle\alpha_{\bm{k}}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}}{\xi_{\bm{k}}}\right),

(6a)

\displaystyle\rho^{X}_{eh\bm{k}}=

\displaystyle\alpha_{\bm{k}}\beta_{\bm{k}}=-\frac{\Delta_{\bm{k}}}{2\xi_{\bm{k}}}.

(6b)

The EI ground state is then determined by solving Eq. (
3
)(
6
) self-consistently.

II.2
Collective modes and response function: time dependent Hartree Fock method

In general, an external field
f(t,\bm{r})
couples locally to the system through a single-particle operator
\hat{O}(\bm{r})
, as expressed by the coupling Hamiltonian

\hat{H}_{c}=\int\textrm{d}\bm{r}\;f(t,\bm{r})\hat{O}(\bm{r}).

(7)

When the ground state preserves the translation symmetry, it’s more convenient to discuss in momentum space.
Define the Fourier-transformed quantities
\hat{O}(\bm{q})=\int\textrm{d}\bm{r}\;\hat{O}(\bm{r})\textrm{e}^{-i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}
and
f(t,\bm{q})=\int\textrm{d}\bm{r}\;f(t,\bm{r})\textrm{e}^{-i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}
, the coupling Hamiltonian can be rewritten as

\hat{H}_{c}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\bm{q}}f(t,\bm{q})\hat{O}(-\bm{q}).

(8)

Treating
\hat{H}_{c}
as a perturbation, the linear response theory states that the expectation value of another operator
\hat{O}^{\prime}(\bm{q})
is given by

\displaystyle\langle\hat{O}^{\prime}\rangle(t,\bm{q})=

\displaystyle\int\textrm{d}t^{\prime}\;C_{\hat{O}^{\prime}\hat{O}}(t,t^{\prime},\bm{q})f(t^{\prime},\bm{q}),

(9a)

\displaystyle C_{\hat{O}^{\prime}\hat{O}}(t,t^{\prime},\bm{q})\equiv

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\frac{i}{\hbar}\Theta(t-t^{\prime})\langle[\hat{O}^{\prime}_{I}(t,\bm{q}),\hat{O}_{I}(t^{\prime},-\bm{q})]\rangle,

(9b)

where
C_{\hat{O}^{\prime}\hat{O}}(t,t^{\prime},\bm{q})
is the retarded correlation function and
\hat{O}_{I}(t,\bm{q})\equiv\textrm{e}^{i\hat{H}t/\hbar}\hat{O}(\bm{q})\textrm{e}^{-i\hat{H}t/\hbar}
is the operator in the interaction picture with respect to the unperturbed Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{H}_{I}
.

To compute correlation functions and investigate the electromagnetic responses of the bilayer EI, we employ the time-dependent Hartree-Fock (TDHF) method.
In the presence of an external perturbation field
f(t)
, the dynamical equation governing the single-particle density matrix
\rho_{ij\bm{k}}(t,\bm{q})\equiv\langle c^{\dagger}_{j\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2}c_{i\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2}\rangle
is given by

i\hbar\partial_{t}\rho_{ij\bm{k}}(t,\bm{q})=\langle[c^{\dagger}_{j\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2}c_{i\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2},\hat{H}+\hat{H}_{c}]\rangle.

(10)

Near the EI ground state
\rho^{X}
, the density matrix
\rho_{ij\bm{k}}(\bm{q})
can be expanded as a series of the external field
f(t)
:

\rho_{ij\bm{k}}(t,\bm{q})=\sum_{n}\rho^{(n)}_{ij\bm{k}}(t,\bm{q})

(11)

where
\rho^{(n)}
denotes the
n
-th order contribution.
It’s convenient to discuss in the quasi-particle band basis, with indices
i,j=c,v
.
In this basis, the zeroth-order density matrix is simply
\rho^{(0)}_{ij\bm{k}}(t,\bm{q})=\rho^{X}_{ij\bm{k}}\delta_{\bm{q}\bm{0}}=\delta_{ij}\delta_{iv}\delta_{\bm{q}\bm{0}}
and the first order term has only off-diagonal components
\rho^{(1)}_{cv\bm{k}}(t,\bm{q})
and
\rho^{(1)}_{vc\bm{k}}(t,\bm{q})=[\rho^{(1)}_{cv\bm{k}}(t,-\bm{q})]^{*}
(the proof is given by Eq. (
92b
)).
Now, assuming that the operator
\hat{O}(\bm{q})
can be expressed in terms of quasiparticle band operators as

\hat{O}(\bm{q})=\sum_{i,j=c,v}\sum_{\bm{k}}o_{ij\bm{k}}(\bm{q})c^{\dagger}_{i\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2}c_{j\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2}.

(12)

By taking the TDHF approximation and up to first order of
f(t)
, Eq. (
10
) becomes

\displaystyle i\hbar\tau_{z}\partial_{t}\begin{bmatrix}\rho^{(1)}_{cv\bm{k}}(t,\bm{q})\\
\rho^{(1)}_{vc-\bm{k}}(t,\bm{q})\end{bmatrix}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}\mathcal{H}_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{q})\begin{bmatrix}\rho^{(1)}_{cv\bm{k}^{\prime}}(t,\bm{q})\\
\rho^{(1)}_{vc-\bm{k}^{\prime}}(t,\bm{q})\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\begin{bmatrix}o_{cv\bm{k}}(-\bm{q})\\
o_{vc-\bm{k}}(-\bm{q})\end{bmatrix}f(t,\bm{q}),

(13)

where
\tau_{z}
is the Pauli matrix, and the dynamic matrix
\mathcal{H}_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{q})
is defined as

\mathcal{H}_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{q})\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{E}_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{q})&\Gamma_{\bm{k},-\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{q})\\
\Gamma_{\bm{k},-\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{q})&\mathcal{E}_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{q})\end{bmatrix}

(14)

Detailed derivations and explicit expressions for Eqs. (
13
) and (
14
) are provided in Appendix
C.1
.

To solve the dynamic equation [Eq. (
13
)], we first need to determine the collective mode eigenfunctions
\Phi_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{q})
and corresponding excitation energies
\omega_{n}(\bm{q})
by solving the generalized eigenvalue problem:

\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}\mathcal{H}_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{q})\Phi_{n\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{q})=\hbar\omega_{n}(\bm{q})\tau_{z}\Phi_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{q}).

(15)

At each
\bm{k}
point, the wave function is a two-component vector, which we denote as
\Phi_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{q})=[\Phi_{n\bm{k}}^{cv}(\bm{q}),\Phi_{n\bm{k}}^{vc}(\bm{q})]^{T}
.
In the normal insulator state with no interlayer coherence, the
\Gamma
matrix in Eq. (
14
) vanishes and Eq. (
126
) reduces to the standard eigenvalue problem.
At this time,
\Phi^{cv}_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{q})
is the eigenfunction of
\mathcal{E}_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}
, which is nothing but the interlayer exciton wavefunction.
Performing a Fourier transform to frequency space, defined as
\rho^{(1)}(\omega,\bm{q})=\int\textrm{d}t\;\rho^{(1)}(t,\bm{q})\textrm{e}^{i\omega t}
and
f(\omega,\bm{q})=\int\textrm{d}t\;f(t,\bm{q})\textrm{e}^{i\omega t}
, the dynamic equation [Eq. (
13
)] can be solved explicitly (see detailed derivation in Appendix
C.2
).
The solution is given by

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\rho^{(1)}_{cv\bm{k}}(\omega,\bm{q})\\
\rho^{(1)}_{vc-\bm{k}}(\omega,\bm{q})\end{bmatrix}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}\Pi_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\omega,\bm{q})\begin{bmatrix}o_{cv\bm{k}^{\prime}}(-\bm{q})\\
o_{vc-\bm{k}^{\prime}}(-\bm{q})\end{bmatrix}f(\omega,\bm{q}),

(16a)

\displaystyle\Pi_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\omega,\bm{q})\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\frac{\omega_{n}(\bm{q})\Phi_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{q})\Phi^{\dagger}_{n\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{q})}{\omega+i\eta-\omega_{n}(\bm{q})}.

(16b)

Thus, up to first order in the external field
f(\omega,\bm{q})
, the expectation value of
\hat{O}^{\prime}(\bm{q})
can be computed as

\displaystyle\langle\hat{O}^{\prime}\rangle(\omega,\bm{q})=

\displaystyle\sum_{ij\bm{k}}o^{\prime}_{ij\bm{k}}(\bm{q})\rho^{(1)}_{ji\bm{k}}(\omega,\bm{q})=C_{\hat{O}^{\prime}\hat{O}}(\omega,\bm{q})f(\omega,\bm{q}),

(17a)

\displaystyle C_{\hat{O}^{\prime}\hat{O}}(\omega,\bm{q})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{n}\frac{\omega_{n}(\bm{q})[\mathrm{O}^{\prime}_{n}(\bm{q})]^{*}\mathrm{O}_{n}(\bm{q})}{\omega+i\eta-\omega_{n}(\bm{q})},

(17b)

where
C_{\hat{O}^{\prime}\hat{O}}(\omega,\bm{q})
is the retarded correlation function expressed in frequency and momentum space, and
\mathrm{O}_{n}(\bm{q})
is the overlap between the vertex function of the operator
\hat{O}
and the collective mode eigenfunction
\Phi_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{q})
:

\mathrm{O}_{n}(\bm{q})\equiv\sum_{\bm{k}}\Phi^{\dagger}_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{q})\begin{bmatrix}o_{cv\bm{k}}(\bm{-\bm{q}})\\
o_{vc-\bm{k}}(-\bm{q})\end{bmatrix}.

(18)

Results at zero perpendicular magnetic field

It’s convenient to work in the excitonic units, where the length and energy scales are defined as
a_{B}^{*}\equiv\epsilon h^{2}/(me^{2})
and

Then the only parameters in the manybody Hamiltonian are the exciton chemical potential
\mu_{X}/\mathrm{Ry}^{*}
, interlayer distance

and the electron-hole asymmetry strength
\delta_{m}\equiv(m_{h}-m_{e})/(m_{h}+m_{e})
.
In typical TMD bilayers such as the MoSe
2
/WSe
2
heterostructure, the parameters are
m_{e}\approx 0.58m_{0}
,
m_{h}=0.36m_{0}
Kormányos et al. 2015
and
\epsilon\approx 5
Cai et al. 2007
.
Thus we have
m\approx 0.22m_{0}
,
\mathrm{Ry}^{*}\approx 120\mathrm{meV}
and

.
With

When the chemical potential increases beyond

III.3
Response to the layer symmetric gauge field

Consider adding a layer-symmetric gauge field to the bilayer system, defined as:
A_{e\mu}(t,\bm{r})=A_{h\mu}(t,\bm{r})=A^{+}_{\mu}(t,\bm{r})=(\phi^{+}(t,\bm{r}),\bm{A}^{+}(t,\bm{r}))
.
Here, the index
\mu
takes values
0,1,2
, where
A^{+}_{0}(t,\bm{r})=\phi^{+}(t,\bm{r})
is the scalar potential and
(A^{+}_{1}(t,\bm{r}),A^{+}_{2}(t,\bm{r}))=\bm{A}^{+}(t,\bm{r})
is the in-plane vector potential (In the following text, we use the index
a=1,2
to represent the in-plane spatial direction
x
and
y
).
Up to first order in the gauge field
A^{+}_{\mu}
, the coupling term can be expressed as:

\hat{H}_{c}=\int d\bm{r}\;[\hat{\varrho}^{+}(\bm{r})\phi^{+}(t,\bm{r})-\hat{\bm{j}}^{+}_{p}(\bm{r})\cdot\bm{A}^{+}(t,\bm{r})],

(20)

where
\hat{\varrho}^{+}(\bm{r})
is the charge density operator,
\hat{\bm{j}}^{+}_{p}(\bm{r})
is the paramagnetic current density operator defined as

\displaystyle\hat{\varrho}^{+}(\bm{r})=

\displaystyle-e\Psi^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\Psi(\bm{r}),

(21a)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{j}}^{+}_{p}(\bm{r})=

\displaystyle\frac{ie\hbar}{4m}\Psi^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\sigma_{z}\nabla_{\bm{r}}\Psi(\bm{r})+h.c..

(21b)

In addition to the paramagnetic current, a diamagnetic current term also arises at finite vector potential
\bm{A}^{+}(t,\bm{r})

\hat{\bm{j}}^{+}_{d}(t,\bm{r})=-\frac{e^{2}}{2m}\Psi^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\sigma_{z}\Psi(\bm{r})\bm{A}^{+}(t,\bm{r}).

(22)

Thus, the total charge current operator is given by the sum of paramagnetic and diamagnetic contributions:
\hat{\bm{j}}^{+}(t,\bm{r})=\hat{\bm{j}}^{+}_{p}(\bm{r})+\hat{\bm{j}}^{+}_{d}(t,\bm{r})
.
Details about the gauge-field coupling term can be found in Appendix
C.3
.

Define
\hat{j}^{+}_{\mu}(t,\bm{r})=\hat{j}^{+}_{p\mu}(t,\bm{r})+\hat{j}^{+}_{d\mu}(t,\bm{r})
where
\hat{j}^{+}_{p\mu}(t,\bm{r})=(-\hat{\varrho}^{+}(\bm{r}),\hat{\bm{j}}_{p}^{+}(t,\bm{r}))
and
\hat{j}^{+}_{d\mu}(t,\bm{r})=(0,\hat{\bm{j}}_{d}^{+}(t,\bm{r}))
.
Then, up to first order in the gauge field
A^{+}_{\mu}(t,\bm{r})
, the expectation value
j^{+}_{\mu}(t,\bm{r})\equiv\langle\hat{j}^{+}_{\mu}(t,\bm{r})\rangle
is calculated as:

\displaystyle j^{+}_{\mu}(t,\bm{r})=

\displaystyle(1-\delta_{\mu 0})\langle\hat{j}^{+}_{d\mu}(t,\bm{r})\rangle+\langle\hat{j}^{+}_{p\mu}(t,\bm{r})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(1-\delta_{\mu 0})\frac{e^{2}}{2m}\langle\Psi^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\sigma_{z}\Psi(\bm{r})\rangle A^{+}_{\mu}(t,\bm{r})

\displaystyle-\sum_{\nu}\int\textrm{d}t^{\prime}\textrm{d}\bm{r}^{\prime}\;C_{\hat{j}^{+}_{p\mu}\hat{j}^{+}_{p\nu}}(t-t^{\prime},\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})A_{\nu}^{+}(t^{\prime},\bm{r}^{\prime}).

Since the diamagnetic current operator
\hat{\bm{j}}_{d}^{+}(t,\bm{r})
is already linear in
A^{+}_{\mu}(t,\bm{r})
, it suffices to retain the zeroth-order term in
\langle\Psi^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\sigma_{z}\Psi(\bm{r})\rangle
, which is nothing but
2n_{X}
.
In frequency and momentum space, the linear response relation is therefore expressed as:

\displaystyle j^{+}_{\mu}(\omega,\bm{q})\equiv

\displaystyle\langle\hat{j}^{+}_{\mu}(\omega,\bm{q})\rangle=\sum_{\nu}K^{+}_{\mu\nu}(\omega,\bm{q})A^{+}_{\nu}(\omega,\bm{q}),

(23a)

\displaystyle K^{+}_{\mu\nu}(\omega,\bm{q})=

\displaystyle-(1-\delta_{\mu 0})\delta_{\mu\nu}\frac{e^{2}n_{X}}{m}-C_{\hat{j}^{+}_{p\mu}\hat{j}^{+}_{p\nu}}(\omega,\bm{q}),

(23b)

where
K^{+}_{\mu\nu}(\omega,\bm{q})
is the electromagnetic response kernel.
As proven in Appendix
B.2
, the TDHF approximation is equivalent to a summation of infinite series of “ladder-bubble” diagrams in the calculation of the two-particle correlation function.
Thus the response kernel
K^{+}_{\mu\nu}(\omega,\bm{q})
will satisfy the ward identity impulsed by the charge conservation law
Das Sarma and Hanke 1983
.
To be specific, the Ward identity reads:

\sum_{\mu}q_{\mu}K^{+}_{\mu\nu}(\omega,\bm{q})=\sum_{\nu}K^{+}_{\mu\nu}(\omega,\bm{q})q_{\nu}=0

(24)

where
q_{\nu}=(\omega,\bm{q})
.
Additionally, due to the rotational symmetry of the bilayer model, the spatial components of the response kernel
K^{+}_{ab}(\omega,\bm{q})
can be decomposed into longitudinal and transverse contributions as:

K^{+}_{ab}(\omega,\bm{q})=K_{L}^{+}(\omega,\bm{q})\frac{q_{a}q_{b}}{|\bm{q}|^{2}}+K_{T}^{+}(\omega,\bm{q})\left(\delta_{ab}-\frac{q_{a}q_{b}}{|\bm{q}|^{2}}\right)

(25)

where
K^{+}_{L}(\omega,\bm{q})
and
K^{+}_{T}(\omega,\bm{q})
represent the longitudinal and transverse response functions, respectively.
In summary, due to the Ward identity [Eq.(
24
)] and the rotational symmetry [Eq.(
25
)], the nine components of the electromagnetic response kernel
K_{\mu\nu}^{+}(\omega,\bm{q})
reduce to only two independent response functions.

In FIG.
5
(a1)(b1), we plot the imaginary parts of the longitudinal and transverse electromagnetic response functions, respectively, along the positive
q_{x}
axis.
For clarity, the dominant poles corresponding to the
1p_{x}
and
1p_{y}
modes, are also indicated by dashed orange lines in FIG.
5
(a1)(b1).
Since these two poles predominantly couple to the total charge current fluctuations within the bilayer EI, they can naturally be identified as plasmon modes.
Along the
q_{x}
axis (with momentum
\bm{q}=(q,0)
), the response kernel
K_{\mu\nu}^{+}(\omega,\bm{q})
the explicit form:

K_{\mu\nu}^{+}(\omega,\bm{q}=(q,0))=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{q^{2}}{\omega^{2}}K_{L}^{+}&-\frac{q}{\omega}K_{L}^{+}&0\\
-\frac{q}{\omega}K_{L}^{+}&K_{L}^{+}&0\\
0&0&K_{T}^{+}\end{bmatrix}.

(26)

We can see that the charge density fluctuation (described by
K_{00}^{+}
) and longitudinal current fluctuation (described by
K^{+}_{11}
) are always coupled together.
When the longitudinal
1p_{x}
mode is excited, the charge densities in electron and hole layers oscillate in-phase along the
x
direction.
This collective oscillation generates a net charge density modulation and a corresponding longitudinal current fluctuation, as illustrated schematically in FIG.
5
(a2).
In contrast, the transverse
1p_{y}
mode only couples to the transverse current fluctuation, which does not alter the charge distribution, as illustrated in FIG.
5
(b2).

Figure 5:
(a1) Imaginary part of the longitudinal response kernel to the layer symmetric gauge field

along the

The dominant pole is the

Although the two plasmon modes are degenerate at zero momentum, the long-range Coulomb interaction lifts this degeneracy due to the direct coupling between the longitudinal mode and charge density fluctuations.
Consequently, the longitudinal plasmon mode exhibits a linear dispersion relation in the long-wavelength limit, arising from the same mechanism responsible for the splitting of longitudinal and transverse optical phonons in two-dimensional systems
Sohier et al. 2017
;
Rivera et al. 2019
;
Shi et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2024
.
To see this, we need to find roots of the effective dielectric function

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}(\omega,\bm{q})=

\displaystyle[1+\tilde{V}(q)C_{\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}}(\omega,\bm{q})]^{-1},

(27)

where
\tilde{V}(q)=e^{-2}V(q)=2\pi/\epsilon q
is the Coulomb interaction in the long-wavelength limit (
e^{2}
is divided out because the elementary charge
e
has already been included in the definition of the charge density in Eq. (
21a
)).
Derivations of Eq. (
27
) can be found in Appendix
C.5
.
As detailed in Appendix
C.4
, the density-density response function
C_{\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}}(\omega,\bm{q})=-K^{+}_{00}(\omega,\bm{q})
can be approximated as:

C_{\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}}(\omega,\bm{q})\approx\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega_{n}>0\\
n\in\mathrm{dipole}\end{subarray}}\frac{2|\mathrm{J}^{+}_{0,n}|^{2}\omega^{2}_{n}(\bm{q})}{(\omega+i\eta)^{2}-\omega_{n}^{2}(\bm{q})},

(28)

where
\mathrm{J}^{+}_{0,n}\sim\bm{q}\cdot\bm{p}_{n}
, as defined by Eq. (
169a
), is the overlap between the vertex function of the charge density operator and the collective mode wavefunction.
Here,
\bm{p}_{n}
is a constant vector determined by the corresponding collective mode.
In the long-wavelength limit, the dominant poles are the degenerated
1p_{x}
and
1p_{y}
modes.
By retaining only the
1p
modes and assuming rotational symmetry, the density-density response function can be expressed as:

C_{\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}}(\omega,\bm{q})\approx\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1p_{x},1p_{y}}\frac{\chi q^{2}\omega_{n}^{2}(\bm{q})}{(\omega+i\eta)^{2}-\omega_{n}^{2}(\bm{q})}.

(29)

According to the definition of the response function, the constant
\chi
given by

\chi\equiv-\lim_{q\to 0}\lim_{\omega\to 0}\frac{1}{q^{2}}C_{\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}}(\omega,\bm{q})=\frac{\partial P}{\partial E}

(30)

has the physical meaning of 2D electrical polarizability, where
P
and
E
are the in-plane electrical polarization and electrical field respectively.
In the long-wavelength limit, we have
\tilde{V}(q)C_{\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}}(\omega,\bm{q})\sim q
.
Expanding Eq. (
27
) and keep up to the lowest order of
q
, the effective dielectric function is approximated as

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}(\omega,\bm{q})\approx

\displaystyle 1-\tilde{V}(q)C_{\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}}(\omega,\bm{q})

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 1-\frac{2\pi}{\epsilon q}\frac{\chi q^{2}(\omega_{0}^{p})^{2}}{\omega^{2}-(\omega_{0}^{p})^{2}}

(31)

where
\omega_{0}^{p}=\omega_{1p_{x}}(\bm{0})=\omega_{1p_{y}}(\bm{0})
is the plasmon energy at zero momentum.
Solving the equation
\epsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}(\omega,\bm{q})=0
yields the longitudinal plasmon dispersion relation:

\omega=\omega_{0}^{p}\sqrt{1+2\pi\chi q/\epsilon}\approx\omega_{0}^{p}\left(1+\frac{\pi\chi}{\epsilon}q\right).

(32)

In FIG.
5
(a1), the linear dispersion described by Eq. (
32
) is depicted by the white line, which shows good agreement with the numerical results for the
1p_{x}
mode in the long-wavelength regime.

III.4
Response to the layer antisymmetric gauge field

The advantage of the bilayer system lies in the ability to independently tune the gauge field in each layer. This flexibility allows for the introduction of a layer-antisymmetric gauge field into the bilayer system.
For instance, an in-plane magnetic field can generate an antisymmetric vector potential, while a perpendicular electric field induces an antisymmetric scalar potential.

By introducing a layer antisymmetric gauge field configuration
A_{e\mu}(t,\bm{r})=-A_{h\mu}(t,\bm{r})=A^{-}_{\mu}(t,\bm{r})/2
, where
A^{-}_{\mu}(t,\bm{r})=(\phi^{-}(t,\bm{r}),\bm{A}^{-}(t,\bm{r}))
is the gauge field difference between the two layers, the coupling Hamiltonian up to first order in
A^{-}_{\mu}
reads

\hat{H}_{c}=\int d\bm{r}\;[\hat{\varrho}^{-}(\bm{r})\phi^{-}(t,\bm{r})-\hat{\bm{j}}^{-}_{p}(\bm{r})\cdot\bm{A}^{-}(t,\bm{r})],

(33)

where

\displaystyle\hat{\varrho}^{-}(\bm{r})=

\displaystyle-\frac{e}{2}\Psi^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\sigma_{z}\Psi(\bm{r}),

(34a)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{j}}^{-}_{p}(\bm{r})=

\displaystyle\frac{ie\hbar}{8m}\Psi^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\nabla_{\bm{r}}\Psi(\bm{r})+h.c.,

(34b)

could be viewed as the exciton density and paramagnetic exciton current density (multiplied by
-e
).
In addition to the paramagnetic term, there is a diamagnetic contribution
\hat{\bm{j}}^{-}_{d}(t,\bm{r})
to the total exciton current, i.e.
\hat{\bm{j}}^{-}(t,\bm{r})=\hat{\bm{j}}^{-}_{p}(\bm{r})+\hat{\bm{j}}^{-}_{d}(t,\bm{r})
where

\hat{\bm{j}}^{-}_{d}(t,\bm{r})=-\frac{e^{2}}{8m}\Psi^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\sigma_{z}\Psi(\bm{r})\bm{A}^{-}(t,\bm{r}).

(35)

Similar to the case of applying a layer symmetric gauge field, by denoting
\hat{j}^{-}_{\mu}(t,\bm{r})=\hat{j}^{-}_{p\mu}(t,\bm{r})+\hat{j}^{-}_{d\mu}(t,\bm{r})
where
\hat{j}^{-}_{p\mu}(t,\bm{r})=(-\hat{\varrho}^{-}(\bm{r}),\hat{\bm{j}}_{p}^{-}(t,\bm{r}))
and
\hat{j}^{-}_{d\mu}(t,\bm{r})=(0,\hat{\bm{j}}_{d}^{-}(t,\bm{r}))
, the electromagnetic responses to a layer antisymmetric gauge field can also be written in the frequency and momentum space as

\displaystyle j^{-}_{\mu}(\omega,\bm{q})\equiv\langle\hat{j}^{-}_{\mu}(\omega,\bm{q})\rangle=\sum_{\nu}K^{-}_{\mu\nu}(\omega,\bm{q})A^{-}_{\nu}(\omega,\bm{q}),

(36a)

\displaystyle K^{-}_{\mu\nu}(\omega,\bm{q})=-(1-\delta_{\mu 0})\delta_{\mu\nu}\frac{e^{2}n_{X}}{4m}-C_{\hat{j}^{-}_{p\mu}\hat{j}^{-}_{p\nu}}(\omega,\bm{q}),

(36b)

where
K^{-}_{\mu\nu}(\omega,\bm{q})
is the response kernel to the layer antisymmetric gauge field.
In the absence of interlayer tunneling, charge conservation is preserved in both layers.
Thus the responses kernel
K^{-}_{\mu\nu}(\omega,\bm{q})
also satisfies the Ward identify

\sum_{\mu}q_{\mu}K^{-}_{\mu\nu}(\omega,\bm{q})=\sum_{\nu}K^{-}_{\mu\nu}(\omega,\bm{q})q_{\nu}=0.

(37)

Similarly the spatial components could also be decomposed into the longitudinal and transverse part as

K^{-}_{ab}(\omega,\bm{q})=K_{L}^{-}(\omega,\bm{q})\frac{q_{a}q_{b}}{|\bm{q}|^{2}}+K_{T}^{-}(\omega,\bm{q})\left(\delta_{ab}-\frac{q_{a}q_{b}}{|\bm{q}|^{2}}\right).

(38)

According to the derivation in appendix.
C.4
, the response function
K^{-}_{00}(\omega,\bm{q})=-C_{\hat{j}^{-}_{p0}\hat{j}^{-}_{p0}}(\omega,\bm{q})
takes the following form in the long-wavelength limit:

\displaystyle K^{-}_{00}(\omega,\bm{q})\approx-\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega_{n}>0\\
n\in\mathrm{monopole}\end{subarray}}\frac{2|\mathrm{J}^{-}_{0,n}|^{2}\omega^{2}_{n}(\bm{q})}{(\omega+i\eta)^{2}-\omega_{n}^{2}(\bm{q})},

(39)

where
\mathrm{J}^{-}_{0,n}
is the overlap between the vertex function of the exciton density operator and the collective mode wavefunction.
The explicit expression of
\mathrm{J}^{-}_{0,n}
is given by Eq. (
169c
), and is a constant to the lowest order of
q
.
As shown by FIG.
6
(a1), the only dominant pole in the long-wavelength limit is the Goldstone mode with dispersion
\omega_{gs}(\bm{q})=vq
(where
v
is the Goldstone mode velocity).
Thus, we can take the single pole approximation and
K^{-}_{00}(\omega,\bm{q})
could be written in the form as

K^{-}_{00}(\omega,q)\approx\frac{-\kappa v^{2}q^{2}}{(\omega+i\eta)^{2}-v^{2}q^{2}},

(40)

where
\kappa
is a constant.
In the static limit,
K^{-}_{00}
has the physical meaning of isothermal exciton compressibility (or interlayer capacitance), given by

\kappa=\lim_{q\to 0}\lim_{\omega\to 0}K^{-}_{00}(\omega,q)=e^{2}\left(\frac{\partial n_{X}}{\partial\mu_{X}}\right)_{T}.

(41)

This indicates that the coefficient
\kappa
in Eq. (
40
) represents the exciton compressibility.
Assuming the momentum is along the
x
-direction, and using the Ward identity, the longitudinal response function
K^{-}_{11}(\omega,\bm{q})
can be expressed as:

K^{-}_{11}(\omega,q)\approx\frac{\omega^{2}}{q^{2}}K^{-}_{00}(\omega,q)\approx\frac{-\kappa v^{2}\omega^{2}}{(\omega+i\eta)^{2}-v^{2}q^{2}}.

(42)

On the other hand, from Eqs. (
36b
)(
171f
), in the finite-frequency
\omega\neq 0
and long-wavelength limit, we have

K^{-}_{11}(\omega,q)=-\frac{e^{2}n_{X}}{4m}-C_{\hat{j}^{-}_{pa\neq 0}\hat{j}^{-}_{pb\neq 0}}(\omega,q)=-\frac{e^{2}n_{X}}{4m}+\mathcal{O}(q^{2}).

(43)

By comparing Eq. (
42
) and Eq. (
43
), we obtain:

\kappa v^{2}=\frac{e^{2}n_{X}}{4m}\implies v=\sqrt{\frac{e^{2}n_{X}}{4m\kappa}}.

(44)

In FIG.
6
(a1), the dispersion relation
\omega=vq
(with
v
determined by Eq. (
44
)) is represented by the white line.
This result shows excellent agreement with the Goldstone mode dispersion in the long-wavelength regime.
In fact, this velocity is nothing but the first sound velocity of the exciton condensate.
The first sound velocity is usually defined as
v=\sqrt{K_{S}/\rho_{m}}
, where
K_{S}\equiv-\mathcal{V}(\partial P/\partial\mathcal{V})_{S}
is the isentropic bulk modulus and
\rho_{m}
is the mass density [for the bilayer system,
\rho_{m}=(m_{e}+m_{h})n_{X}=4mn_{X}
].
Since we are considering the system at zero temperature, there is no difference between the isothermal and isentropic process, i.e.
K_{S}=-\mathcal{V}(\partial P/\partial\mathcal{V})_{T}
.
On the other hand, the thermodynamic variables satisfy the relation
Landau and Lifshitz 1980

\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{V}}{\partial P}\right)_{T,N_{X}}=-\frac{\mathcal{V}}{n_{X}^{2}}\left(\frac{\partial n_{X}}{\partial\mu_{X}}\right)_{T,\mathcal{V}}=-\frac{\mathcal{V}}{n_{X}^{2}}\frac{\kappa}{e^{2}}.

(45)

Thus the sound velocity can be expressed as

\displaystyle v=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{K_{S}}{\rho_{m}}}=\sqrt{\frac{e^{2}n_{X}^{2}/\kappa}{4mn_{X}}}=\sqrt{\frac{e^{2}n_{X}}{4m\kappa}},

(46)

which is exactly the same as Eq. (
44
).

IV
Discussion

As discussed in Sec.
III.4
, the Goldstone mode couples to the layer antisymmetric gauge field, enabling its detection through electromagnetic measurements.
To this end, we propose using microwave impedance microscopy (MIM), as illustrated in FIG.
1
.
Assume the tip is located as
(\bm{r}_{t},d_{t})
and the tip charge
Q_{t}(\omega)
oscillates at the frequency
\omega
.
In momentum space, the Coulomb interaction between the tip and the two layers is given by
V_{ts}(\bm{q})=4\pi e^{2}\textrm{e}^{-q(d_{t}-d_{s})}/[(1+\epsilon)q]
, where
d_{e}=-d/2
and
d_{h}=d/2
represent the positions of the electron and hole layers along the
z
-direction (see details in Appendix
A
).
The real-space tip-layer interaction is then expressed as
V_{ts}(\bm{r})=\mathcal{V}^{-1}\sum_{\bm{q}}V_{ts}(\bm{q})\textrm{e}^{i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}
.
Define
\tilde{V}^{+}(\bm{r})=e^{-2}[V_{te}(\bm{r})+V_{th}(\bm{r})]/2
and

\tilde{V}^{-}(\bm{r})=e^{-2}[V_{te}(\bm{r})-V_{th}(\bm{r})]
, then
\phi^{\sigma=\pm}(\omega,\bm{r})=\tilde{V}^{\sigma=\pm}(\bm{r}-\bm{r}_{t})Q_{t}(\omega)
are just the layer symmetric (
\sigma=+
) and antisymmetric (
\sigma=-
) scalar potential at point
\bm{r}
induced by the tip charge.
The scalar potentials lead to total charge density fluctuation
\varrho^{+}(\omega,\bm{r})
and exciton density fluctuation
\varrho^{-}(\omega,\bm{r})
as

\displaystyle\delta\varrho^{\sigma}(\omega,\bm{r})=

\displaystyle-\int\textrm{d}\bm{r}^{\prime}\;K^{\sigma}_{00}(\omega,\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})\phi^{\sigma}(\omega,\bm{r}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathcal{V}^{-1}\sum_{\bm{q}}K_{00}^{\sigma}(\omega,\bm{q})\tilde{V}^{\sigma}(\bm{q})Q_{t}(\omega)\textrm{e}^{i\bm{q}\cdot(\bm{r}-\bm{r}_{t})}.

(47)

The charge density fluctuations in each layer can then be expressed in terms of
\delta\varrho^{\pm}(\omega,\bm{r})
as
\delta\varrho_{e}(\omega,\bm{r})=\delta\varrho^{+}(\omega,\bm{r})/2+\delta\varrho^{-}(\omega,\bm{r})
and
\delta\varrho_{h}(\omega,\bm{r})=\delta\varrho^{+}(\omega,\bm{r})/2-\delta\varrho^{-}(\omega,\bm{r})
,
which generate a potential feedback to the tip

\displaystyle\delta U(\omega)=

\displaystyle\sum_{s=eh}\int\textrm{d}\bm{r}\;e^{-2}V_{ts}(\bm{r}_{t}-\bm{r})\delta\varrho_{s}(\omega,\bm{r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma=\pm}\int\textrm{d}\bm{r}\;\tilde{V}^{\sigma}(\bm{r}_{t}-\bm{r})\delta\varrho^{\sigma}(\omega,\bm{r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathcal{V}^{-1}\sum_{\sigma=\pm,\bm{q}}[\tilde{V}^{\sigma}(\bm{q})]^{-2}K^{\sigma}_{00}(\omega,\bm{q})Q_{t}(\omega).

(48)

Assuming the geometric capacitance between the tip and bilayer system is
C_{t}
, the total electrical potential at the tip is given by
U_{t}(\omega)=Q_{t}(\omega)/C_{t}+\delta U(\omega)
.
And the admittance measured by the MIM is
Y_{t}(\omega)\equiv I_{t}(\omega)/U_{t}(\omega)=-i\omega Q_{t}(\omega)/U_{t}(\omega)
.
Substituting for
U_{t}(\omega)
, we have

\displaystyle Y_{t}(\omega)=

\displaystyle-i\omega\left[C_{t}^{-1}-\mathcal{V}^{-1}\sum_{\sigma=\pm,\bm{q}}[\tilde{V}^{\sigma}(\bm{q})]^{-2}K^{\sigma}_{00}(\omega,\bm{q})\right]^{-1}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle-i\omega C_{t}\left[1+C_{t}\mathcal{V}^{-1}\sum_{\sigma=\pm,\bm{q}}[\tilde{V}^{\sigma}(\bm{q})]^{-2}K^{\sigma}_{00}(\omega,\bm{q})\right].

(49)

Conventionally, when the distance between the tip and the bilayer sample is sufficiently large to prevent direct current tunneling, the tip and the sample form a capacitive load, resulting in a purely imaginary admittance.
However, the electromagnetic responses of the sample introduce a quantum correction to the admittance, which includes both imaginary and real components:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}[\delta Y_{t}(\omega)]=

\displaystyle-\omega C_{t}^{2}\mathcal{V}^{-1}\sum_{\sigma=\pm,\bm{q}}[\tilde{V}^{\sigma}(\bm{q})]^{-2}\mathrm{Re}[K^{\sigma}_{00}(\omega,\bm{q})],

(50a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}[\delta Y_{t}(\omega)]=

\displaystyle\omega C_{t}^{2}\mathcal{V}^{-1}\sum_{\sigma=\pm,\bm{q}}[\tilde{V}^{\sigma}(\bm{q})]^{-2}\mathrm{Im}[K^{\sigma}_{00}(\omega,\bm{q})].

(50b)

While the imaginary part of the correction can exist at any frequency, the real part becomes nonzero only when the collective modes are excited.
In MIM experiments, the operating frequency lies in the sub-THz range (below
1\mathrm{meV}
), which is lower than both the single-particle gap and the plasmon energy
\omega_{0}^{p}
(on the order of
10\mathrm{meV}
in the bilayer EI).
Consequently, only the gapless Goldstone mode can be excited at these frequencies, and it is the sole contributor to the real part of the admittance.
According to Eq. (
40
), in the long-wavelength and low frequency limit, the exciton response function takes the form:

K^{-}_{00}(\omega,\bm{q})\approx\frac{\kappa v^{2}q^{2}}{v^{2}q^{2}-\omega^{2}}+\frac{i\pi\kappa vq}{2}[\delta(\omega-vq)-\delta(\omega+vq)].

(51)

Additionally, the interaction between the tip charge and the exciton density fluctuation,
\tilde{V}^{-}(\bm{q})
, can also be approximated as

\tilde{V}^{-}(\bm{q})=\frac{4\pi\textrm{e}^{-qd_{t}}(\textrm{e}^{-qd/2}-\textrm{e}^{qd/2})}{(1+\epsilon)q}\approx-\frac{4\pi d\textrm{e}^{-qd_{t}}}{(1+\epsilon)}.

(52)

Thus, the real admittance contributed by the Goldstone mode can be calculated as:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}[\delta Y_{t}(\omega)]=

\displaystyle\frac{\omega C_{t}^{2}(4\pi d)^{2}}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\bm{q}}\textrm{e}^{-2qd_{t}}\frac{\pi\kappa vq}{2}\delta(\omega-vq)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\omega C_{t}^{2}(4\pi d)^{2}\pi\kappa v}{2(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{q\textrm{d}q}{2\pi}\;\textrm{e}^{-2qd_{t}}q\delta(\omega-vq)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\pi^{2}d^{2}C_{t}^{2}\kappa}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}v^{2}}\omega^{3}\textrm{e}^{-2d_{t}\omega/v}.

(53)

From this expression, we can see that the linear dispersion Goldstone mode will yield a real admittance with cubic frequency dependence in MIM measurements.
Furthermore, by tuning the tip-layer distance
d_{t}
, the velocity
v
of the Goldstone mode can be determined from the exponential decay factor
\textrm{e}^{-2d_{t}\omega/v}
.

Appendix A
The Coulomb potentials between layers and tip

For a point charge at
(\bm{0},z_{0})
, the Poisson equation of the electrical potential
\varphi(\bm{r},z;z_{0})
reads

\epsilon(z)\nabla_{\bm{r}}^{2}\varphi(\bm{r},z;z_{0})+\partial_{z}[\epsilon(z)\partial_{z}\varphi(\bm{r},z;z_{0})]=-4\pi e\delta(\bm{r})\delta(z-z_{0})

(54)

where the dielectric constant is dependent on
z
as

\epsilon(z)=\left\{\begin{split}1,&|z|>d^{\prime}/2,\\
\epsilon,&|z|<d^{\prime}/2\end{split}\right.

(55)

Define the 2D Fourier transformation of
\varphi(\bm{r},z;z_{0})
as

\tilde{\varphi}(\bm{q},z;z_{0})=\int d\bm{r}\;\varphi(\bm{r},z;z_{0})e^{-i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}

(56)

Then
\tilde{\varphi}(\bm{q},z;z_{0})
satisfies

\partial_{z}[\epsilon(z)\partial_{z}\tilde{\varphi}(\bm{q},z;z_{0})]-q^{2}\epsilon(z)\tilde{\varphi}(\bm{q},z;z_{0})=-4\pi e\delta(z-z_{0})

(57)

For
z_{0}\neq\pm d^{\prime}/2
, the general solution is written as

\tilde{\varphi}(\bm{q},z;z_{0})=\frac{2\pi e}{q}[c_{1}e^{-q|z-d^{\prime}/2|}+c_{2}e^{-q|z+d^{\prime}/2|}+\epsilon^{-1}(z_{0})\textrm{e}^{-q|z-z_{0}|}]

(58)

Then displacement field is calculated as

\displaystyle D_{z}(\bm{q},z;z_{0})\equiv-\epsilon(z)\partial_{z}\tilde{\varphi}(\bm{q},z;z_{0})=

\displaystyle 2\pi e\epsilon(z)\left\{c_{1}[2\Theta(z-d^{\prime}/2)-1]e^{-q|z-d^{\prime}/2|}+c_{2}[2\Theta(z+d^{\prime}/2)-1]e^{-q|z+d^{\prime}/2|}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\epsilon^{-1}(z_{0})[2\Theta(z-z_{0})-1]e^{-q|z-z_{0}|}\right\}

(59)

The displacement field should be continuous as the sample boundary
z=\pm d^{\prime}/2
, i.e.,

\displaystyle c_{1}+c_{2}e^{-qd^{\prime}}+\epsilon^{-1}(z_{0})[2\Theta(d^{\prime}/2-z_{0})-1]e^{-q|d^{\prime}/2-z_{0}|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\epsilon c_{1}+\epsilon c_{2}e^{-qd^{\prime}}+\epsilon\epsilon^{-1}(z_{0})[2\Theta(d^{\prime}/2-z_{0})-1]e^{-q|d^{\prime}/2-z_{0}|}

(60)

\displaystyle-\epsilon c_{1}e^{-qd^{\prime}}+\epsilon c_{2}+\epsilon\epsilon^{-1}(z_{0})[2\Theta(-d^{\prime}/2-z_{0})-1]e^{-q|d^{\prime}/2+z_{0}|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-c_{1}e^{-q(d+2d^{\prime})}-c_{2}+\epsilon^{-1}(z_{0})[2\Theta(-d^{\prime}/2-z_{0})-1]e^{-q|d^{\prime}/2+z_{0}|}

(61)

which is simplified as

\displaystyle(\epsilon+1)c_{1}-(\epsilon-1)c_{2}e^{-qd^{\prime}}=f(z_{0})

(62)

\displaystyle(\epsilon-1)c_{1}e^{-qd^{\prime}}-(\epsilon+1)c_{2}=-f(-z_{0})

(63)

where

f(z_{0})=(\epsilon-1)\epsilon^{-1}(z_{0})[2\Theta(d^{\prime}/2-z_{0})-1]e^{-q|d^{\prime}/2-z_{0}|}

(64)

Then
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are solved as

\displaystyle c_{1}=\frac{(\epsilon+1)f(z_{0})e^{qd^{\prime}}+(\epsilon-1)f(-z_{0})}{(\epsilon+1)^{2}e^{qd^{\prime}}-(\epsilon-1)^{2}e^{-qd^{\prime}}}

(65)

\displaystyle c_{2}=\frac{(\epsilon+1)f(-z_{0})e^{qd^{\prime}}+(\epsilon-1)f(z_{0})}{(\epsilon+1)^{2}e^{qd^{\prime}}-(\epsilon-1)^{2}e^{-qd^{\prime}}}

(66)

•

The intra-layer potential
: The intra-layer potential is gotten by setting
z=z_{0}=d/2
.
At this time

\displaystyle f(z_{0})=f(d/2)=(\epsilon-1)\epsilon^{-1}e^{-q(d^{\prime}-d)/2}

(67)

\displaystyle f(-z_{0})=f(-d/2)=(\epsilon-1)\epsilon^{-1}e^{-q(d^{\prime}+d)/2}

(68)

and

\displaystyle c_{1}=\frac{\epsilon-1}{\epsilon}\frac{(\epsilon+1)e^{q(d^{\prime}+d)/2}+(\epsilon-1)e^{-q(d^{\prime}+d)/2}}{(\epsilon+1)^{2}e^{qd^{\prime}}-(\epsilon-1)^{2}e^{-qd^{\prime}}}

(69)

\displaystyle c_{2}=\frac{\epsilon-1}{\epsilon}\frac{(\epsilon+1)e^{q(d^{\prime}-d)/2}+(\epsilon-1)e^{-q(d^{\prime}-d)/2}}{(\epsilon+1)^{2}e^{qd^{\prime}}-(\epsilon-1)^{2}e^{-qd^{\prime}}}

(70)

Thus the intra-layer interaction is

\displaystyle V_{ee}(\bm{q})\equiv e\tilde{\varphi}(\bm{q},d/2;d/2)=

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi e^{2}}{q}[c_{1}e^{-q(d^{\prime}-d)/2}+c_{2}e^{-q(d^{\prime}+d)/2}+\epsilon^{-1}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi e^{2}}{\epsilon q}\left[1+2(\epsilon-1)\frac{(\epsilon+1)\cosh(qd)+(\epsilon-1)e^{-qd^{\prime}}}{(\epsilon+1)^{2}e^{qd^{\prime}}-(\epsilon-1)^{2}e^{-qd^{\prime}}}\right]

(71)

•

The interlayer potential
: The interlayer potential is gotten by setting
z_{0}=d/2
and
z=-d/2
, i.e.,

\displaystyle V_{he}(\bm{q})\equiv e\tilde{\varphi}(\bm{q},-d/2;d/2)=

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi e^{2}}{q}[c_{1}e^{-q(d^{\prime}+d)/2}+c_{2}e^{-q(d^{\prime}-d)/2}+\epsilon^{-1}e^{-qd}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi e^{2}}{\epsilon q}\left[e^{-qd}+2(\epsilon-1)\frac{(\epsilon+1)+(\epsilon-1)e^{-qd^{\prime}}\cosh(qd)}{(\epsilon+1)^{2}e^{qd^{\prime}}-(\epsilon-1)^{2}e^{-qd^{\prime}}}\right]

(72)

•

Tip potential induced by layer charge
: The tip potential due to the point charge at the electron or hole layers are respectively calculated as

\displaystyle V_{th}(\bm{q})\equiv e\tilde{\varphi}(\bm{q},d_{t};d/2)=

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi e^{2}}{q}[c_{1}e^{-q(d_{t}-d^{\prime}/2)}+c_{2}e^{-q(d_{t}+d^{\prime}/2)}+\epsilon^{-1}e^{-q(d_{t}-d/2)}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi e^{2}e^{-q(d_{t}-d/2)}}{\epsilon q}\left[1+(\epsilon-1)\frac{(\epsilon+1)e^{qd^{\prime}}+(\epsilon-1)e^{-qd^{\prime}}+2\epsilon e^{-qd}}{(\epsilon+1)^{2}e^{qd^{\prime}}-(\epsilon-1)^{2}e^{-qd^{\prime}}}\right]

(73)

\displaystyle V_{te}(\bm{q})\equiv e\tilde{\varphi}(\bm{q},d_{t};-d/2)

\displaystyle=e\tilde{\varphi}(\bm{q},-d_{t};d/2)=\frac{2\pi e^{2}}{q}[c_{1}e^{-q(d_{t}+d^{\prime}/2)}+c_{2}e^{-q(d_{t}-d^{\prime}/2)}+\epsilon^{-1}e^{-q(d_{t}+d/2)}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi e^{2}e^{-q(d_{t}+d/2)}}{\epsilon q}\left[1+(\epsilon-1)\frac{(\epsilon+1)e^{qd^{\prime}}+(\epsilon-1)e^{-qd^{\prime}}+2\epsilon e^{-qd}}{(\epsilon+1)^{2}e^{qd^{\prime}}-(\epsilon-1)^{2}e^{-qd^{\prime}}}\right]

(74)

Here we assume that the width of the dielectric surrounding the bilayer system is much larger than the layer separation, i.e.,
d^{\prime}\gg d
.
Then expand in exponential, the interactions between tip, electron and hole layers are approximated as

\displaystyle V_{ee}(\bm{q})=V_{eh}(\bm{q})\approx

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi e^{2}}{\epsilon q}

(76)

\displaystyle V_{eh}(\bm{q})=V_{he}(\bm{q})\approx

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi e^{2}\textrm{e}^{-qd}}{\epsilon q}

(77)

\displaystyle V_{th}(\bm{q})=V_{ht}(\bm{q})\approx

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi e^{2}\textrm{e}^{-q(d_{t}-d/2)}}{\epsilon q}\left(1+\frac{\epsilon-1}{\epsilon+1}\right)=\frac{4\pi e^{2}\textrm{e}^{-q(d_{t}-d/2)}}{(\epsilon+1)q}

(78)

\displaystyle V_{te}(\bm{q})=V_{et}(\bm{q})\approx

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi e^{2}\textrm{e}^{-q(d_{t}+d/2)}}{\epsilon q}\left(1+\frac{\epsilon-1}{\epsilon+1}\right)=\frac{4\pi e^{2}\textrm{e}^{-q(d_{t}+d/2)}}{(\epsilon+1)q}

(79)

Appendix B
The time dependent Hartree Fock method: general formulation

B.1
Dynamics equation of the density matrix

Consider the many-body Hamiltonian

\hat{H}=\sum_{ij}[h^{0}_{ij}+o_{ij}f(t)]c^{\dagger}_{i}c_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ijlm}V_{ijkl}c^{\dagger}_{i}c^{\dagger}_{j}c_{l}c_{k}

(80)

where matrix elements are defined as

h^{ij}\equiv\langle i|h^{0}|j\rangle,\;o_{ij}\equiv\langle i|o|j\rangle,\;V_{ijkl}\equiv\langle i,j|V|k,l\rangle

(81)

The Hermiticity of the Hamiltonian requires that the matrix elements satisfy the following relations:

h^{0}_{ij}=(h_{ji}^{0})^{*},\;o_{ij}=o_{ji}^{*},\;V_{ijkl}=V_{klij}^{*}

(82)

Besides, the anti-commutation relations of the fermionic operators also require that

V_{ijkl}=V_{jilk}

(83)

The equation of motion of the density matrix
\rho_{mn}=\langle c^{\dagger}_{n}c_{m}\rangle
is written as

\displaystyle i\hbar\partial_{t}\rho_{mn}=

\displaystyle\langle[c^{\dagger}_{n}c_{m},H]\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle c^{\dagger}_{n}[c_{m},H]\rangle-\langle[H,c^{\dagger}_{n}]c_{m}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[h^{0}_{mj}+o_{mj}f(t)]\langle c^{\dagger}_{n}c_{j}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}V_{mjkl}\langle c^{\dagger}_{n}c^{\dagger}_{j}c_{l}c_{k}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}V_{imkl}\langle c^{\dagger}_{n}c^{\dagger}_{i}c_{l}c_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle-[h^{0}_{in}+o_{in}f(t)]\langle c^{\dagger}_{i}c_{m}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}V_{ijnl}\langle c^{\dagger}_{i}c^{\dagger}_{j}c_{l}c_{m}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}V_{ijkn}\langle c^{\dagger}_{i}c^{\dagger}_{j}c_{k}c_{m}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[h^{0}_{mj}+o_{mj}f(t)]\langle c^{\dagger}_{n}c_{j}\rangle+V_{mikl}\langle c^{\dagger}_{n}c^{\dagger}_{i}c_{l}c_{k}\rangle-[h^{0}_{in}+o_{in}f(t)]\langle c^{\dagger}_{i}c_{m}\rangle-V_{ijnl}\langle c^{\dagger}_{i}c^{\dagger}_{j}c_{l}c_{m}\rangle

(84)

For simplicity, the Einstein summation convention is used in the previous equation, and will also be used in the following text.
Under TDHF approximation,
\langle c^{\dagger}_{i}c^{\dagger}_{j}c_{l}c_{k}\rangle\approx\rho_{ki}\rho_{lj}-\rho_{kj}\rho_{li}
, thus

\displaystyle i\hbar\partial_{t}\rho_{mn}=

\displaystyle[h^{0}_{mj}+b_{mj}f(t)]\rho_{jn}+V_{mikl}\rho_{li}\rho_{kn}-V_{mikl}\rho_{ki}\rho_{ln}

\displaystyle-\rho_{mi}[h^{0}_{in}+b_{in}f(t)]-\rho_{mi}V_{ijnl}\rho_{lj}+\rho_{mj}V_{ijnl}\rho_{li}

(85)

Define the Hartree and Fock Hamiltonian as

h^{H}_{ik}=V_{ijkl}\rho_{lj},\;h^{F}_{il}=-V_{ijkl}\rho_{kj}

(86)

we have

i\hbar\partial_{t}\rho_{mn}=[h^{0}+h^{H}+h^{F}+of(t),\rho]_{mn}

(87)

In the presence of
f(t)
, the density matrix could be expanded as series of
f(t)
, i.e.,

\rho=\sum_{n}\rho^{(n)}

(88)

where
\rho^{(n)}
is
n
-th order quantities of
f(t)
.
To zeroth order of
f(t)
we have

i\hbar\partial_{t}\rho^{(0)}_{mn}=[h^{0}+h^{H}+h^{F},\rho^{(0)}]_{mn}.

(89)

And the static Hartree Fock ground is determined by the self-consistent equations:

\displaystyle\rho^{(0)}=\sum_{v}|v\rangle\langle v|

(90a)

\displaystyle(h^{0}+h^{H}+h^{F})|v\rangle=\xi_{v}|v\rangle,\;(h^{0}+h^{H}+h^{F})|c\rangle=\xi_{c}|c\rangle,\xi_{c}>\mu>\xi_{v}

(90b)

where
\mu
is the chemical potential.
Before we derive the equation for
\rho^{(1)}
, let’s first prove that
\rho^{(1)}_{cc^{\prime}}=\rho^{(1)}_{vv^{\prime}}=0
.
As a pure state,
\rho
should satisfies
\rho^{2}=\rho
.
Up to first order of
f(t)
we have

[\rho^{(0)}]^{2}+\rho^{(0)}\rho^{(1)}+\rho^{(1)}\rho^{(0)}=\rho^{(0)}+\rho^{(1)}\implies\rho^{(0)}\rho^{(1)}+\rho^{(1)}\rho^{(0)}-\rho^{(1)}=0

(91)

Then the matrix elements of the above equation between the occupied states
|c\rangle
,
|c^{\prime}\rangle
and the unoccupied states
|v\rangle
,
|v^{\prime}\rangle
are given by

\displaystyle\langle c|[\rho^{(0)}\rho^{(1)}+\rho^{(1)}\rho^{(0)}-\rho^{(1)}]|c^{\prime}\rangle=-\rho^{(1)}_{cc^{\prime}}=0,

(92a)

\displaystyle\langle v|[\rho^{(0)}\rho^{(1)}+\rho^{(1)}\rho^{(0)}-\rho^{(1)}]|v^{\prime}\rangle=\rho^{(1)}_{vv^{\prime}}=0.

(92b)

This means that only the matrix elements between states with different occupation numbers are first order quantity of
f(t)
.
And the time-dependent Hartree Fock (TDHF) equation of
\rho^{(1)}_{cv}
is

\displaystyle i\hbar\partial_{t}\rho_{cv}^{(1)}=

\displaystyle[h^{0}+h^{H}(\rho^{(0)})+h^{F}(\rho^{(0)}),\rho^{(1)}]_{cv}+f(t)[o,\rho^{(0)}]_{cv}+[h^{H}(\rho^{(1)})+h^{F}(\rho^{(1)}),\rho^{(0)}]_{cv}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle c|[h^{0}+h^{H}(\rho^{(0)})+h^{F}(\rho^{(0)}),\rho^{(1)}]+f(t)[o,\rho^{(0)}]+[h^{H}(\rho^{(1)})+h^{F}(\rho^{(1)}),\rho^{0}]|v\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\xi_{c}-\xi_{v})\rho^{(1)}_{cv}+o_{cv}f(t)+\langle c|h^{H}(\rho^{(1)})+h^{F}(\rho^{(1)})|v\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\xi_{c}-\xi_{v})\rho^{(1)}_{cv}+V_{cv^{\prime}vc^{\prime}}\rho^{(1)}_{c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}+V_{cc^{\prime}vv^{\prime}}\rho^{(1)}_{v^{\prime}c^{\prime}}-V_{cv^{\prime}c^{\prime}v}\rho^{(1)}_{c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}-V_{cc^{\prime}v^{\prime}v}\rho^{(1)}_{v^{\prime}c^{\prime}}+o_{cv}f(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{cv,c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}\rho^{(1)}_{c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}+\Gamma_{cv,c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}\rho^{(1)}_{v^{\prime}c^{\prime}}+o_{cv}f(t)

(93)

where the dynamic matrix elements are defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{cv,c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}=\delta_{(cv),(c^{\prime}v^{\prime})}(\xi_{c}-\xi_{v})+V_{cv^{\prime}vc^{\prime}}-V_{cv^{\prime}c^{\prime}v}

(94a)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{cv,c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}=V_{cc^{\prime}vv^{\prime}}-V_{cc^{\prime}v^{\prime}v}

(94b)

where
\delta_{(cv),(c^{\prime}v^{\prime})}\equiv\delta_{cv}\delta_{c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}
.
It could be verified that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{c^{\prime}v^{\prime},cv}=\delta_{(cv),(c^{\prime}v^{\prime})}(\xi_{c}-\xi_{v})+V_{c^{\prime}vv^{\prime}c}-V_{c^{\prime}vcv^{\prime}}=\mathcal{E}^{*}_{cv,c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}

(95a)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{c^{\prime}v^{\prime},cv}=V_{c^{\prime}cv^{\prime}v}-V_{c^{\prime}cvv^{\prime}}=V_{cc^{\prime}vv^{\prime}}-V_{cc^{\prime}v^{\prime}v}=\Gamma_{cv,c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}

(95b)

Take complex conjugate of Eq. (
93
) we have

\displaystyle-i\hbar\partial_{t}\rho^{(1)}_{vc}=

\displaystyle(\xi_{c}-\xi_{v})\rho^{(1)}_{vc}+V_{vc^{\prime}cv^{\prime}}\rho^{(1)}_{v^{\prime}c^{\prime}}+V_{vv^{\prime}cc^{\prime}}\rho^{(1)}_{c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}-V_{c^{\prime}vcv^{\prime}}\rho^{(1)}_{v^{\prime}c^{\prime}}-V_{v^{\prime}vcc^{\prime}}\rho^{(1)}_{c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}+o_{vc}f(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{*}_{cv,c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}\rho^{(1)}_{v^{\prime}c^{\prime}}+\Gamma^{*}_{cv,c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}\rho^{(1)}_{c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}+o_{vc}f(t)

(96)

Fourier transform to the frequency space, Eq. (
93
) and Eq. (
96
) could be written in a more compact form:

\hbar\omega^{+}\tau_{z}\begin{bmatrix}\rho^{(1)}_{cv}(\omega)\\
\rho^{(1)}_{vc}(\omega)\end{bmatrix}=\mathcal{H}_{cv,c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}\begin{bmatrix}\rho^{(1)}_{c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}(\omega)\\
\rho^{(1)}_{v^{\prime}c^{\prime}}(\omega)\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}o_{cv}\\
o_{vc}\end{bmatrix}f(\omega)

(97)

where
\tau_{z}
is the Pauli matrix,
\omega^{+}\equiv\omega+i\eta
and
\eta
is a small positive number to account for the retarded effect.
Besides,
\mathcal{H}
is the dynamic matrix defined as

\mathcal{H}_{cv,c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{E}_{cv,c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}&\Gamma_{cv,c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}\\
\Gamma^{*}_{cv,c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}&\mathcal{E}^{*}_{cv,c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}\end{bmatrix}

(98)

Then Eq. (
97
) could be formally solved by

\begin{bmatrix}\rho^{(1)}_{cv}\\
\rho^{(1)}_{vc}\end{bmatrix}=\left(\hbar\omega^{+}\tau_{z}-\mathcal{H}_{cv,c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}\right)^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}o_{c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}\\
o_{v^{\prime}c^{\prime}}\end{bmatrix}f(\omega)

(99)

B.2
Feynman diagrammatic representation

The TDHF approximation could also be represented by the Feynman diagram as shown in FIG.
B.1
.
Consider the time-ordered two-particle correlation function defined as

\Pi_{ij,kl}(\tau)\equiv-\langle Tc^{\dagger}_{j}(\tau)c_{i}(\tau)c^{\dagger}_{k}(0)c_{l}(0)\rangle

(100)

where
T
is the time-ordering operator and
\tau
is the imaginary time.
It’s Fourier transformation is given by

\Pi_{ij,kl}(i\nu_{n})\equiv\int_{0}^{\beta}\textrm{d}\tau\;\textrm{e}^{i\nu_{n}\tau}\Pi_{ij,kl}(\tau)

(101)

where
\mu_{n}=2\pi n/\beta
is the Matsubara frequency and
\beta=1/k_{B}T
is the inverse temperature.
As shown in FIG.
B.1
(a), the two-particle correlation function
\Pi
could be written as a series summation of the irreducible two-particle correlation function
\Pi^{\mathrm{ir}}
as

\Pi=\Pi^{\mathrm{ir}}+\Pi^{\mathrm{ir}}V^{d}\Pi^{\mathrm{ir}}+\Pi^{\mathrm{ir}}V^{d}\Pi^{\mathrm{ir}}V^{d}\Pi^{\mathrm{ir}}+\cdots=\Pi^{\mathrm{ir}}(1+V^{d}\Pi)=(1+\Pi V^{d})\Pi^{\mathrm{ir}}

(102)

where
V^{d}
is the direct interaction such that

V^{d}_{k^{\prime}l^{\prime},i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}=\langle k^{\prime},j^{\prime}|V|l^{\prime},i^{\prime}\rangle=V_{k^{\prime}j^{\prime}l^{\prime}i^{\prime}}

(103)

On the other hand, under TDHF approximation, the irreducible two-particle correlation function
\Pi^{\mathrm{ir}}
is calculated by the summation of the ladder diagrams as shown in FIG.
B.1
(b), i.e.,

\Pi^{\mathrm{ir}}=\Pi^{0}-\Pi^{0}V^{x}\Pi^{0}+\Pi^{0}V^{x}\Pi^{0}V^{x}\Pi^{0}-\cdots=\Pi^{0}(1-V^{x}\Pi^{\mathrm{ir}})=(1-\Pi^{\mathrm{ir}}V^{x})\Pi^{0}

(104)

where
V^{x}
is the exchange interaction such that

V^{x}_{k^{\prime}l^{\prime},i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}=\langle k^{\prime},j^{\prime}|V|i^{\prime},l^{\prime}\rangle=V_{k^{\prime}j^{\prime}i^{\prime}l^{\prime}}

(105)

and
\Pi^{0}
is the two-particle bubble.

Figure B.1:
Feynman diagrammatic representation of the TDHF approximation.

Under the Hartree Fock approximation, the single-particle Green function
\mathcal{G}
is

\mathcal{G}_{ij}(i\omega_{m})=\frac{\delta_{ij}}{i\omega_{m}-\xi_{i}}

(106)

where
\xi_{i}
is the single-particle energy given by Eq.
90b
, and
\omega_{m}=(2m+1)\pi/\beta
is the fermionic Matsubara frequency.
Then the bubble diagram
\Pi^{0}
is calculated as

\displaystyle\Pi^{0}_{ij,kl}(i\nu_{n})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{i\omega_{m}}\mathcal{G}_{ik}(i\omega_{m}+i\nu_{n})\mathcal{G}_{lj}(i\omega_{m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{i\omega_{m}}\frac{\delta_{(ij)(kl)}}{(i\omega_{m}+i\nu_{n}-\xi_{i})(i\omega_{m}-\xi_{j})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{(ij)(kl)}\frac{n_{F}(\xi_{j})-n_{F}(\xi_{i}-i\nu_{n})}{i\nu_{n}-\xi_{i}+\xi_{j}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\delta_{(ij)(kl)}f_{ji}}{i\nu_{n}-(\xi_{i}-\xi_{j})}.

(107)

In the above equation,
\delta_{(ij)(kl)}\equiv\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}
,
f_{ij}\equiv f_{i}-f_{j}
and
f_{i}\equiv n_{F}(\xi)=1/[1+\exp(\beta\xi)]
is the occupation number of the state
|i\rangle
.
At zero temperature, we have
f_{cv}=-f_{vc}=1
and
f_{cc^{\prime}}=f_{cc^{\prime}}=0
.
At this time,
\Pi^{0}_{ij,kl}\neq 0
only when the index pairs
(ij)
and
(kl)
are taken from
(cv)
or
(vc)
.
According to Eq. (
102
) and (
104
), this property also holds for
\Pi^{\mathrm{ir}}
and
\Pi
.

Besides, Eq. (
102
) and (
104
) give the following relations:

\displaystyle(1-\Pi^{\mathrm{ir}}V^{d})\Pi=\Pi^{\mathrm{ir}}\implies\Pi^{-1}=[\Pi^{\mathrm{ir}}]^{-1}-V^{d}

(108a)

\displaystyle(1+\Pi^{0}V^{x})\Pi^{\mathrm{ir}}=\Pi^{0}\implies[\Pi^{\mathrm{ir}}]^{-1}=[\Pi^{0}]^{-1}+V^{x}

(108b)

which implies

[\Pi(\omega)]^{-1}_{ij,kl}=[\Pi^{0}(i\nu_{n}\to\hbar\omega^{+})]^{-1}_{ij,kl}-V^{d}_{ij,kl}+V^{x}_{ij,kl}=\delta_{(ij)(kl)}f_{ji}[\omega^{+}-(\xi_{i}-\xi_{j})]-V_{iljk}+V_{ilkj}

(109)

To be specific, we have

\displaystyle[\Pi(\omega)]^{-1}_{cv,c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}=

\displaystyle\delta_{(cv)(c^{\prime}v^{\prime})}\omega^{+}-[\delta_{(cv)(c^{\prime}v^{\prime})}(\xi_{c}-\xi_{v})+V_{cv^{\prime}vc^{\prime}}-V_{cv^{\prime}c^{\prime}v}]=\delta_{(cv)(c^{\prime}v^{\prime})}\hbar\omega^{+}-\mathcal{E}_{cv,c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}

(110a)

\displaystyle[\Pi(\omega)]^{-1}_{cv,v^{\prime}c^{\prime}}=

\displaystyle-(V_{cc^{\prime}v^{\prime}v}-V_{cc^{\prime}vv^{\prime}})=-\Gamma_{cv,v^{\prime}c^{\prime}}

(110b)

\displaystyle[\Pi(\omega)]^{-1}_{vc,c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}=

\displaystyle-(V_{vv^{\prime}c^{\prime}c}-V_{vv^{\prime}cc^{\prime}})=-\Gamma^{*}_{cv,v^{\prime}c^{\prime}}

(110c)

\displaystyle[\Pi(\omega)]^{-1}_{vc,v^{\prime}c^{\prime}}=

\displaystyle-\delta_{(cv)(c^{\prime}v^{\prime})}\hbar\omega^{+}-[\delta_{(cv)(c^{\prime}v^{\prime})}(\xi_{c}-\xi_{v})+V_{vc^{\prime}cv^{\prime}}-V_{vc^{\prime}v^{\prime}c}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\delta_{(cv)(c^{\prime}v^{\prime})}\omega^{+}-[\delta_{(cv)(c^{\prime}v^{\prime})}(\xi_{c}-\xi_{v})+V_{c^{\prime}vv^{\prime}c}-V_{c^{\prime}vcv^{\prime}}]=-\delta_{(cv)(c^{\prime}v^{\prime})}\hbar\omega^{+}-\mathcal{E}^{*}_{cv,c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}

(110d)

In other words,

[\Pi(\omega)]^{-1}=\hbar\omega^{+}\tau_{z}-\mathcal{H}

(111)

which is consistent with Eq. (
99
).

Appendix C
TDHF: Application to the bilayer system

C.1
The TDHF equation of the bilayer EI

According to Eq.
2
, the first-quantization form of the mean-field Hamiltonian in the layer space is formally written as

h^{MF}_{\bm{k}}=\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}&\Delta_{\bm{k}}\\
\Delta_{\bm{k}}&-\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}\end{bmatrix}.

(112)

where
\Delta_{\bm{k}}
is chosen to be real and negative.
Then according to Eq.
4
and (
5
), the eigenstates of the mean-field Hamiltonian are given by

h^{MF}_{\bm{k}}|c\bm{k}\rangle=\xi_{c\bm{k}}|c\bm{k}\rangle,\;h^{MF}_{\bm{k}}|v\bm{k}\rangle=\xi_{v\bm{k}}|v\bm{k}\rangle

(113)

where

\displaystyle\xi_{c\bm{k}}=-\xi_{v\bm{k}}=\xi_{\bm{k}}=\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}^{2}+\Delta^{2}_{\bm{k}}}

(114a)

\displaystyle|c\bm{k}\rangle=\begin{bmatrix}\beta_{\bm{k}}\\
-\alpha_{\bm{k}}\end{bmatrix},\;|v\bm{k}\rangle=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{\bm{k}}\\
\beta_{\bm{k}}\end{bmatrix}

(114b)

\displaystyle\alpha_{\bm{k}}=\sqrt{(1-\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}/\xi_{\bm{k}})/2},\;\beta_{\bm{k}}=\sqrt{(1+\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}/\xi_{\bm{k}})/2}.

(114c)

In the bilayer system, the EI ground has translation symmetry, thus the density matrix could be labeled by the total momentum
\bm{q}
,

\rho_{ij\bm{k}}(\bm{q})\equiv\langle c^{\dagger}_{j\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2}c_{i\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2}\rangle

(115)

Thus the TDHF equation Eq. (
93
) of
\rho^{(1)}_{cv}
is rewritten as

\displaystyle i\hbar\partial_{t}\rho^{(1)}_{cv\bm{k}}(\bm{q})=

\displaystyle i\hbar\partial_{t}\rho^{(1)}_{c=(c\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2)v=(v\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c^{\prime}=(c\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}/2)\\
v^{\prime}=(v\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{q}/2)\end{subarray}}\mathcal{E}_{cv,c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}\rho^{(1)}_{c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v^{\prime}=(v\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}/2)\\
c^{\prime}=(c\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{q}/2)\end{subarray}}\Gamma_{cv,c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}\rho^{(1)}_{v^{\prime}c^{\prime}}+o_{cv}f(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}\mathcal{E}_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{q})\rho^{(1)}_{cv\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{q})+\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}\Gamma_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{q})\rho^{(1)}_{vc\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{q})+\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}o_{cv\bm{k}}(-\bm{q})f(t,\bm{q})

(116)

where
o_{ij\bm{k}}(-\bm{q})\equiv\langle i\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2|o_{\bm{k}}|j\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2\rangle
and
o_{\bm{k}}
is the bare vertex function in layer space, for example,
\gamma^{\sigma}_{\mu,\bm{k}}
defined in Eq. (
164
) and

is the corresponding gauge field which couples to

.
Besides, the matrix elements
\mathcal{E}_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{q})
and
\Gamma_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{q})
are defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{q})\equiv

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{cv,c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}\big|_{c=(c\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2),v=(v\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2),c^{\prime}=(c\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}/2),v^{\prime}=(v\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{q}/2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[\delta_{(cv),(c^{\prime}v^{\prime})}(\xi_{c}-\xi_{v})+V_{cv^{\prime}vc^{\prime}}-V_{cv^{\prime}c^{\prime}v}]\big|_{c=(c\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2),v=(v\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2),c^{\prime}=(c\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}/2),v^{\prime}=(v\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{q}/2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\xi_{c\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2}-\xi_{v\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{q}/2})+\langle c\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2,v\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{q}/2|V|v\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2,c\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}/2\rangle

\displaystyle-\langle c\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2,v\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{q}/2|V|c\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}/2,v\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2\rangle

(117a)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{q})\equiv

\displaystyle\Gamma_{cv,c^{\prime}v^{\prime}}\big|_{c=(c\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2),v=(v\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2),v^{\prime}=(v\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}/2),c^{\prime}=(c\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{q}/2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(V_{cc^{\prime}vv^{\prime}}-V_{cc^{\prime}v^{\prime}v})\big|_{c=(c\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2),v=(v\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2),v^{\prime}=(v\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}/2),c^{\prime}=(c\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{q}/2)}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}U(\bm{q})[\beta_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2}\alpha_{\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2}(-\alpha_{\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{q}/2})\beta_{\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}/2}+(-\alpha_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2})\beta_{\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2}\beta_{\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{q}/2}\alpha_{\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}/2}]

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}V(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime})[\beta_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2}\alpha_{\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}/2}\beta_{\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{q}/2}\alpha_{\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2}+(-\alpha_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2})\beta_{\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}/2}(-\alpha_{\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{q}/2})\beta_{\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2}]

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}U(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime})[\beta_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2}\alpha_{\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}/2}(-\alpha_{\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{q}/2})\beta_{\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2}+(-\alpha_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2})\beta_{\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}/2}\beta_{\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{q}/2}\alpha_{\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}[V(\bm{q})-V(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime})][(\beta_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2}\alpha_{\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2})(\alpha_{\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}/2}\beta_{\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{q}/2})+(\alpha_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2}\beta_{\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2})(\beta_{\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}/2}\alpha_{\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{q}/2})]

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}U(\bm{q})[(\beta_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2}\alpha_{\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2})(\beta_{\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}/2}\alpha_{\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{q}/2})+(\alpha_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2}\beta_{\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2})(\alpha_{\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}/2}\beta_{\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{q}/2})]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}U(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime})[(\beta_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2}\beta_{\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2})(\alpha_{\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}/2}\alpha_{\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{q}/2})+(\alpha_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2}\alpha_{\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2})(\beta_{\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}/2}\beta_{\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{q}/2})]

(124)

where
V(q)
and
U(q)
are the intra- and inter-layer Coulomb interaction respectively.
From the explicit expression above, we can see that
\Gamma_{\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{k}}(\bm{q})=\Gamma_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{q})
is also symmetric.

C.2
Solving the TDHF equation

As derived by Eq. (
99
), the TDHF equation Eq. (
121
) could be formally solved in frequency space as

\begin{bmatrix}\rho^{(1)}_{cv\bm{k}}(\omega,\bm{q})\\
\rho^{(1)}_{vc-\bm{k}}(\omega,\bm{q})\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}[\hbar\omega^{+}\tau_{z}-\mathcal{H}(\bm{q})]^{-1}_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}\begin{bmatrix}o_{cv\bm{k}^{\prime}}(-\bm{q})\\
o_{vc-\bm{k}^{\prime}}(-\bm{q})\end{bmatrix}f(\omega,\bm{q})

(125)

which requires the matrix inversion.
As we will show in the following, the matrix inversion could be simplified by writing in the basis of the generalized eigenstates of
\mathcal{H}(\bm{q})
.

The generalized eigenvalue equation of
\mathcal{H}(\bm{q})
is given by

\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}\mathcal{H}_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{q})\Phi_{n\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{q})=\hbar\omega_{n}(\bm{q})\tau_{z}\Phi_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{q})

(126)

To solve the generalized eigenvalue equation, let’s first define to auxiliary matrix
\mathcal{K}^{(\pm)}_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{q})\equiv\mathcal{E}_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{q})\pm\Gamma_{\bm{k},-\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{q})
.
Form the explicit expressions of
\mathcal{E}_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{q})
and
\Gamma_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{q})
given by Eq. (
123
) and (
124
), we can see that
\mathcal{K}^{(\pm)}_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{q})
is real and symmetric.
For convenience, we will omit the
\bm{k}
subscripts in the following.
In fact, these two matrixes are nothing but the Hessian matrix of the HF total energy functional which account for the amplitude and phase fluctuations of the EI order parameter respectively
Wu et al. 2015
;
Shao and Dai 2024a
.
As ground state, the Hessian matrix
\mathcal{K}^{(\pm)}(\bm{q})
are both non-negative, which means we could define the square root
\sqrt{\mathcal{K}^{(+)}(\bm{q})}
.
Here, we chose
\sqrt{\mathcal{K}^{(+)}(\bm{q})}
to be real-symmetric and non-negative.
Define

\mathcal{D}(\bm{q})=\sqrt{\mathcal{K}^{(+)}(\bm{q})}\mathcal{K}^{(-)}(\bm{q})\sqrt{\mathcal{K}^{(+)}(\bm{q})}

(127)

then we can see that
\mathcal{D}(\bm{q})
is also real-symmetric and non-negative.
In the following, we will show that the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{D}(\bm{q})
are the square of the generalized eigenstates of
\mathcal{H}(\bm{q})
defined by Eq. (
126
), i.e.

\mathcal{D}(\bm{q})u_{n}(\bm{q})=\hbar^{2}\omega^{2}_{n}(\bm{q})u_{n}(\bm{q})

(128)

Take

x_{n}(\bm{q})=[\mathcal{K}^{(+)}(\bm{q})]^{-1/2}u_{n}(\bm{q}),\;y_{n}(\bm{q})=[i\hbar\omega_{n}(\bm{q})]^{-1}[\mathcal{K}^{(+)}(\bm{q})]^{1/2}u_{n}(\bm{q})

(129)

Then we can verify that

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{(+)}(\bm{q})x_{n}(\bm{q})=

\displaystyle[\mathcal{K}^{(+)}(\bm{q})]^{1/2}u_{n}(\bm{q})=i\hbar\omega_{n}(\bm{q})y_{n}(\bm{q})

(130)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{(-)}(\bm{q})y_{n}(\bm{q})=

\displaystyle[i\hbar\omega_{n}(\bm{q})]^{-1}[\mathcal{K}^{(+)}(\bm{q})]^{-1/2}\mathcal{D}(\bm{q})u_{n}(\bm{q})=-i\hbar\omega_{n}(\bm{q})[\mathcal{K}^{(+)}(\bm{q})]^{-1/2}u_{n}(\bm{q})=-i\hbar\omega_{n}(\bm{q})x_{n}(\bm{q})

(131)

Then the generalized eigenvectors of
\mathcal{H}(\bm{q})
with positive/negative eigenvalues could be constructed as

\Phi_{\pm n}(\bm{q})=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}x_{n}(\bm{q})\pm iy_{n}(\bm{q})\\
x_{n}(\bm{q})\mp iy_{n}(\bm{q})\end{bmatrix}

(132)

One can verify that

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\bm{q})\Phi_{+n}(\bm{q})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{E}(\bm{q})&\Gamma(\bm{q})\\
\Gamma(\bm{q})&\mathcal{E}(\bm{q})\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_{n}(\bm{q})+iy_{n}(\bm{q})\\
x_{n}(\bm{q})-iy_{n}(\bm{q})\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{K}^{(+)}(\bm{q})x_{n}(\bm{q})+i\mathcal{K}^{(-)}(\bm{q})y_{n}(\bm{q})\\
\mathcal{K}^{(+)}(\bm{q})x_{n}(\bm{q})-i\mathcal{K}^{(-)}(\bm{q})y_{n}(\bm{q})\\
\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}i\hbar\omega_{n}(\bm{q})y_{n}(\bm{q})+\hbar\omega_{n}(\bm{q})x_{n}(\bm{q})\\
i\hbar\omega_{n}(\bm{q})y_{n}(\bm{q})-\hbar\omega_{n}(\bm{q})x_{n}(\bm{q})\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hbar\omega_{n}(\bm{q})\tau_{x}\Phi_{+n}(\bm{q})

(133)

Similarly, we also have
\mathcal{H}(\bm{q})\Phi_{-n}(\bm{q})=-\hbar\omega_{n}(\bm{q})\tau_{z}\Phi_{-n}(\bm{q})
.
Thus using the eigenvectors of
\mathcal{D}(\bm{q})
, we can construct the generalized eigenvectors of
\mathcal{H}(\bm{q})
by Eq. (
129
) and (
132
).
And the generalized eigenvalues of
\mathcal{H}(\bm{q})
are just square roots of the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{D}(\bm{q})
.

As a real-symmetric matrix, the eigenvectors of
\mathcal{D}(\bm{q})
could be taken to be real and orthonormal, i.e.

u^{\dagger}_{m}(\bm{q})u_{n}(\bm{q})=\delta_{mn},\;\mathrm{Im}u_{n}(\bm{q})=0.

(134)

Then according to the construction Eq. (
129
) and (
132
),
\Phi_{n}(\bm{q})
is also pure real and satisfy a generalized orthogonal relation with respect to
\tau_{z}
:

\Phi^{\dagger}_{m}(\bm{q})\mathcal{H}(\bm{q})\Phi_{n}(\bm{q})=\hbar\omega_{n}(\bm{q})\Phi^{\dagger}_{m}(\bm{q})\tau_{z}\Phi_{n}(\bm{q})=\hbar\omega_{m}(\bm{q})\Phi^{\dagger}_{m}(\bm{q})\tau_{z}\Phi_{n}(\bm{q})\implies\Phi^{\dagger}_{m}(\bm{q})\tau_{z}\Phi_{n}(\bm{q})\propto\delta_{mn}

(135)

However, the generalized eigenvectors are not normalized with respect to
\tau_{z}
:

\displaystyle\Phi^{\dagger}_{n}(\bm{q})\tau_{z}\Phi_{n}(\bm{q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}u_{n}^{\dagger}(\bm{q})\left\{\{[\mathcal{K}^{(+)}(\bm{q})]^{-1/2}+[\hbar\omega_{n}(\bm{q})]^{-1}[\mathcal{K}^{(+)}(\bm{q})]^{1/2}\}^{2}-\{[\mathcal{K}^{(+)}(\bm{q})]^{-1/2}-[\hbar\omega_{n}(\bm{q})]^{-1}[\mathcal{K}^{(+)}(\bm{q})]^{1/2}\}^{2}\right\}u_{n}(\bm{q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}u_{n}^{\dagger}(\bm{q})4[\hbar\omega_{n}(\bm{q})]^{-1}u_{n}(\bm{q})=\frac{1}{\hbar\omega_{n}(\bm{q})}

(136)

In stead, they are normalized with respect to the inner product defined by
\mathcal{H}(\bm{q})
:

\Phi^{\dagger}_{n}(\bm{q})\mathcal{H}(\bm{q})\Phi_{n}(\bm{q})=\hbar\omega_{n}(\bm{q})\Phi^{\dagger}_{n}(\bm{q})\tau_{z}\Phi_{n}(\bm{q})=1.

(137)

In summary we have

\Phi^{\dagger}_{m}(\bm{q})\mathcal{H}(\bm{q})\Phi_{n}(\bm{q})=\delta_{mn},\;\Phi^{\dagger}_{m}(\bm{q})\tau_{z}\Phi_{n}(\bm{q})=\frac{\delta_{mn}}{\hbar\omega_{n}(\bm{q})},\;\mathrm{Im}\Phi_{n}(\bm{q})=0

(138)

Since
\Phi_{n}(\bm{q})
are orthonormal with respect to the inner product defined by
\mathcal{H}(\bm{q})
,
\{[\mathcal{H}(\bm{q})]^{1/2}\Phi_{n}(\bm{q})\}
will form a complete basis set, such that

\sum_{n}[\mathcal{H}(\bm{q})]^{1/2}\Phi_{n}(\bm{q})\Phi^{\dagger}_{n}(\bm{q})[\mathcal{H}(\bm{q})]^{1/2}=\mathbbm{1}

(139)

This implies two other identities:

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\Phi_{n}(\bm{q})\Phi^{\dagger}_{n}(\bm{q})\mathcal{H}(\bm{q})=[\mathcal{H}(\bm{q})]^{-1/2}\left\{\sum_{n}[\mathcal{H}(\bm{q})]^{1/2}\Phi_{n}(\bm{q})\Phi^{\dagger}_{n}(\bm{q})[\mathcal{H}(\bm{q})]^{1/2}\right\}[\mathcal{H}(\bm{q})]^{1/2}=\mathbbm{1}

(140a)

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\mathcal{H}(\bm{q})\Phi_{n}(\bm{q})\Phi^{\dagger}_{n}(\bm{q})=[\mathcal{H}(\bm{q})]^{1/2}\left\{\sum_{n}[\mathcal{H}(\bm{q})]^{1/2}\Phi_{n}(\bm{q})\Phi^{\dagger}_{n}(\bm{q})[\mathcal{H}(\bm{q})]^{1/2}\right\}[\mathcal{H}(\bm{q})]^{-1/2}=\mathbbm{1}

(140b)

Define

\Pi(\omega,\bm{q})=\sum_{n}\frac{\omega_{n}(\bm{q})\Phi_{n}(\bm{q})\Phi^{\dagger}_{n}(\bm{q})}{\omega^{+}-\omega_{n}(\bm{q})}

(141)

Then we can verify that

\displaystyle[\hbar\omega^{+}\tau_{z}-\mathcal{H}(\bm{q})]\Pi(\omega,\bm{q})=

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\frac{\omega_{n}(\bm{q})[\hbar\omega^{+}\tau_{z}-\hbar\omega_{n}\tau_{z}]\Phi_{n}(\bm{q})\Phi^{\dagger}_{n}(\bm{q})}{\omega^{+}-\omega_{n}(\bm{q})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\hbar\omega_{n}(\bm{q})\tau_{z}\Phi_{n}(\bm{q})\Phi^{\dagger}_{n}(\bm{q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\mathcal{H}(\bm{q})\Phi_{n}(\bm{q})\Phi_{n}^{\dagger}(\bm{q})=\mathbbm{1}

(142)

This implies that
[\hbar\omega^{+}\tau_{z}-\mathcal{H}(\bm{q})]^{-1}=\Pi(\omega,\bm{q})
and the formal solution Eq. (
125
) could be explicitly written as

\begin{bmatrix}\rho^{(1)}_{cv\bm{k}}(\omega,\bm{q})\\
\rho^{(1)}_{vc-\bm{k}}(\omega,\bm{q})\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{n}\frac{\omega_{n}(\bm{q})\Phi_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{q})\mathrm{O}_{n}(\bm{q})}{\omega^{+}-\omega_{n}(\bm{q})}f(\omega,\bm{q})

(143)

where

\mathrm{O}_{n}(\bm{q})\equiv\sum_{\bm{k}}\Phi^{\dagger}_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{q})\begin{bmatrix}o_{cv\bm{k}}(-\bm{q})\\
o_{vc-\bm{k}}(-\bm{q})\end{bmatrix}

(144)

is the overlap between the
n
-th collective mode wavefunction and the bare vertex function of operator
\hat{O}
.

C.3
The density and current operators

Under
k\cdot p
approximation, the manybody Hamiltonian in momentum space is written as

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{k}}[c^{\dagger}_{e\bm{k}},c^{\dagger}_{h\bm{k}}]\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\hbar^{2}k^{2}}{2m_{e}}-\frac{\mu_{X}}{2}&0\\
0&-\frac{\hbar^{2}k^{2}}{2m_{h}}+\frac{\mu_{X}}{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c_{e\bm{k}}\\
c_{h\bm{k}}\end{bmatrix}

(145a)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{I}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\mathcal{V}}\sum_{ss^{\prime}=eh}\sum_{\bm{k}\bm{k}^{\prime}\bm{q}}V_{ss^{\prime}}(\bm{q})c^{\dagger}_{s\bm{k}}c^{\dagger}_{s^{\prime}\bm{k}^{\prime}}c_{s^{\prime}\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}}c_{s\bm{k}-\bm{q}}

(145b)

where
c^{\dagger}_{e\bm{k}}(c_{e\bm{k}})
and
c^{\dagger}_{h\bm{k}}(c_{h\bm{k}})
are the creation (annihilation) operators of electron in the electron and hole layers respectively.
In the absence of the interlayer bias, the ground state of the bilayer system is defined as
|G_{\text{unbiased}}\rangle=\prod_{\bm{k}}c^{\dagger}_{h\bm{k}}|\text{vac.}\rangle
, where
|\text{vac.}\rangle
is the vacuum state.
To avoid double counting problem, we stress that the manybody Hamiltonian should be normal ordered with respect to
|G_{\text{unbiased}}\rangle
, i.e.,
\hat{H}\equiv:\hat{H}:
.
And the normal order rules for the creation and annihilation operators are

\displaystyle:c^{\dagger}_{e\bm{k}}c_{s\bm{k}^{\prime}}:=-:c_{s\bm{k}^{\prime}}c^{\dagger}_{e\bm{k}}:=c^{\dagger}_{e\bm{k}}c_{s\bm{k}^{\prime}}

(146a)

\displaystyle:c_{s\bm{k}}c^{\dagger}_{h\bm{k}^{\prime}}:=-:c^{\dagger}_{h\bm{k}^{\prime}}c_{s\bm{k}}:=c_{s\bm{k}}c^{\dagger}_{h\bm{k}^{\prime}}

(146b)

For simplicity, we will not explicitly write the Hamiltonian in the normal ordered form.
Besides, we will also omit the normal order symbol
:\cdots:
and only explicitly write it when necessary.
But we should keep in mind that the Hamiltonian and the current operators defined below are all normal ordered with respect to the ground state
|G_{\text{unbiased}}\rangle
.

In real space, the manybody Hamiltonian of the bilayer system is written as

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}=

\displaystyle\int\textrm{d}\bm{r}\;\Psi^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\begin{bmatrix}\frac{p^{2}}{2m_{e}}-\frac{\mu_{X}}{2}&0\\
0&-\frac{p^{2}}{2m_{h}}+\frac{\mu_{X}}{2}\end{bmatrix}\Psi(\bm{r})

(147a)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{I}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ss^{\prime}=eh}\int\textrm{d}\bm{r}\textrm{d}\bm{r}^{\prime}\;\Psi_{s}^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\Psi_{s}^{\prime\dagger}(\bm{r}^{\prime})V_{ss^{\prime}}(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})\Psi_{s^{\prime}}(\bm{r}^{\prime})\Psi_{s}(\bm{r})

(147b)

where
\Psi^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\equiv[\Psi_{e}^{\dagger}(\bm{r}),\Psi^{\dagger}_{h}(\bm{r})]
and
\Psi_{s}^{\dagger}(\bm{r})=\mathcal{V}^{-1/2}\sum_{\bm{k}}\textrm{e}^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}c^{\dagger}_{s\bm{k}}
is the field operator.
When the gauge field
A_{s\mu}(t,\bm{r})=(\phi_{s}(t,\bm{r}),\bm{A}_{s}(t,\bm{r}))
is applied to each of the layer, the Hamiltonian should be modified according to the Peierls substitution
\bm{p}\to\bm{p}+e\bm{A}
(
e=|e|
).
This will change the non-interaction Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{0}
to

\hat{H}_{0}^{\prime}=\int\textrm{d}\bm{r}\;\Psi^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\begin{bmatrix}\frac{|\bm{p}+e\bm{A}_{e}|^{2}}{2m_{e}}-\frac{\mu_{X}}{2}-e\phi_{e}&0\\
0&-\frac{|\bm{p}+e\bm{A}_{h}|^{2}}{2m_{h}}+\frac{\mu_{X}}{2}-e\phi_{h}\end{bmatrix}\Psi(\bm{r})

(148)

And the density (
\mu=0
) and current
(\mu=1,2)
operators in each layer are defined as

\hat{j}_{s\mu}(t,\bm{r})=-\frac{\delta\hat{H}_{0}^{\prime}}{\delta A_{s\mu}(t,\bm{r})}

(149)

To be specific, we have

\displaystyle-\hat{\varrho}_{e}(t,\bm{r})\equiv

\displaystyle\hat{j}_{e\mu=0}(t,\bm{r})=e\Psi^{\dagger}_{e}(\bm{r})\Psi_{e}(\bm{r})

(150a)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{j}}_{e}(t,\bm{r})\equiv

\displaystyle\hat{j}_{e\mu=12}(t,\bm{r})=-\frac{e}{2m_{e}}\Psi_{e}^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\left[-i\hbar\nabla_{\bm{r}}+e\bm{A}_{e}(t,\bm{r})\right]\Psi_{e}(\bm{r})+\mathrm{h.c.}

(150b)

\displaystyle-\hat{\varrho}_{h}(t,\bm{r})\equiv

\displaystyle\hat{j}_{h\mu=0}(t,\bm{r})=e\Psi^{\dagger}_{h}(\bm{r})\Psi_{h}(\bm{r})

(150c)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{j}}_{h}(t,\bm{r})\equiv

\displaystyle\hat{j}_{h\mu=12}(t,\bm{r})=\frac{e}{2m_{h}}\Psi_{h}^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\left[-i\hbar\nabla_{\bm{r}}+e\bm{A}_{h}(t,\bm{r})\right]\Psi_{h}(\bm{r})+\mathrm{h.c.}

(150d)

In the expression of the current operators
\hat{\bm{j}}_{s}
, there are two terms: the paramagnetic current which is irrelevant to the gauge field

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{j}}_{e,p}(t,\bm{r})=

\displaystyle\frac{ie\hbar}{2m_{e}}\Psi^{\dagger}_{e}(\bm{r})\nabla_{\bm{r}}\Psi_{e}(\bm{r})+\mathrm{h.c.}

(151a)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{j}}_{h,p}(t,\bm{r})=

\displaystyle-\frac{ie\hbar}{2m_{h}}\Psi^{\dagger}_{h}(\bm{r})\nabla_{\bm{r}}\Psi_{h}(\bm{r})+\mathrm{h.c.}

(151b)

and the diamagnetic current which is proportional to the vector potential
\bm{A}_{s}(t,\bm{r})

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{j}}_{e,d}(t,\bm{r})=

\displaystyle-\frac{e^{2}}{m_{e}}\Psi^{\dagger}_{e}(\bm{r})\Psi_{e}(\bm{r})\bm{A}_{e}(t,\bm{r})

(152a)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{j}}_{h,d}(t,\bm{r})=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{2}}{m_{h}}\Psi^{\dagger}_{h}(\bm{r})\Psi_{h}(\bm{r})\bm{A}_{h}(t,\bm{r})

(152b)

Thus, to first order of the gauge field
A_{s\mu}(t,\bm{r})
, the perturbated hamiltonian could be written as
\hat{H}^{\prime}_{0}=\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{H}_{c}
, where
\hat{H}_{c}
is the linear coupling term written as

\hat{H}_{c}=\sum_{s=eh}\int\textrm{d}\bm{r}\;[\hat{\varrho}_{s}(\bm{r})\phi_{s}(t,\bm{r})-\hat{\bm{j}}_{s,p}(\bm{r})\cdot\bm{A}_{s}(t,\bm{r})]

(153)

Here we drop the
t
variable in
\hat{\varrho}(t,\bm{r})
and
\hat{\bm{j}}_{s,p}(t,\bm{r})
since they are time-independent according to their definitions.

When the system has additional electron-hole symmetry such that
m_{e}=m_{h}=2m
[
m\equiv m_{e}m_{h}/(m_{e}+m_{h})
is the reduced mass], we can define the total charge density and paramagnetic current operators as

\displaystyle\hat{\varrho}^{+}(\bm{r})\equiv

\displaystyle\hat{\varrho}_{e}(\bm{r})+\hat{\varrho}_{h}(\bm{r})=-e\Psi^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\Psi(\bm{r})

(154a)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{j}}_{p}^{+}(\bm{r})\equiv

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{j}}_{e,p}(\bm{r})+\hat{\bm{j}}_{h,p}(\bm{r})=\frac{ie\hbar}{4m}\Psi^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\sigma_{z}\nabla_{\bm{r}}\Psi(\bm{r})+h.c.

(154b)

where
\sigma_{z}
is the Pauli matrix in the layer space.
Similarly, we can also define the exciton density and paramagnetic current operators (multiplied by
-e
)

\displaystyle\hat{\varrho}^{-}(\bm{r})\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[\hat{\varrho}_{e}(\bm{r})-\hat{\varrho}_{h}(\bm{r})]=-\frac{e}{2}\Psi^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\sigma_{z}\Psi(\bm{r})

(155a)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{j}}^{-}_{p}(\bm{r})\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[\hat{\bm{j}}_{e,p}(\bm{r})-\hat{\bm{j}}_{h,p}(\bm{r})]=\frac{ie\hbar}{8m}\Psi^{\dagger}(\bm{r})\nabla_{\bm{r}}\Psi(\bm{r})+h.c.

(155b)

Then the coupling term is rewritten as

\hat{H}_{c}=\sum_{\sigma=\pm}\int\textrm{d}\bm{r}\;[\hat{\varrho}^{\sigma}(\bm{r})\phi^{\sigma}(t,\bm{r})-\hat{\bm{j}}_{p}^{\sigma}(\bm{r})\cdot\bm{A}^{\sigma}(t,\bm{r})]

(156)

where
A_{\mu}^{+}(t,\bm{r})\equiv[A_{e\mu}(t,\bm{r})+A_{h\mu}(t,\bm{r})]/2
and
A_{\mu}^{-}(t,\bm{r})\equiv A_{e\mu}(t,\bm{r})-A_{h\mu}(t,\bm{r})
are the layer-symmetric and antisymmetric gauge fields respectively.
From the linear coupling Hamiltonian Eq. (
156
), we can see that the layer-symmetric and antisymmetric gauge fields couple to the total charge and exciton freedom respectively.
This conclusion also applies to the diamagnetic currents.
To first order of
\bm{A}_{s}(t,\bm{r})
, the diamagnetic current in each layer is

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\bm{j}}_{e,d}(t,\bm{r})\rangle=

\displaystyle-\frac{e^{2}}{2m}\langle\Psi^{\dagger}_{e}(\bm{r})\Psi_{e}(\bm{r})\rangle\bm{A}_{e}(t,\bm{r})=-\frac{e^{2}n_{X}}{2m}\bm{A}_{e}(t,\bm{r})

(157a)

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\bm{j}}_{h,d}(t,\bm{r})\rangle=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{2}}{2m}\langle:\Psi^{\dagger}_{h}(\bm{r})\Psi_{h}(\bm{r}):\rangle\bm{A}_{h}(t,\bm{r})=-\frac{e^{2}}{2m}\langle\Psi_{h}(\bm{r})\Psi^{\dagger}_{h}(\bm{r})\rangle\bm{A}_{h}(t,\bm{r})=-\frac{e^{2}n_{X}}{2m}\bm{A}_{h}(t,\bm{r})

(157b)

In Eq. (
157b
), we have used the normal order rules Eq. (
146
).
Besides, we also use the fact that the bilayer system is at the charge neutrality point in the EI phase.
Then the diamagnetic current of charge is

\langle\hat{\bm{j}}_{d}^{+}(t,\bm{r})\rangle\equiv\langle\hat{\bm{j}}_{e,d}(t,\bm{r})\rangle+\langle\hat{\bm{j}}_{h,d}(t,\bm{r})\rangle=-\frac{e^{2}n_{X}}{2m}[\bm{A}_{e}(t,\bm{r})+\bm{A}_{h}(t,\bm{r})]=-\frac{e^{2}n_{X}}{m}\bm{A}^{+}(t,\bm{r})

(158)

while the diamagnetic current of exciton is

\langle\hat{\bm{j}}_{d}^{-}(t,\bm{r})\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{2}[\langle\hat{\bm{j}}_{e,d}(t,\bm{r})\rangle-\langle\hat{\bm{j}}_{h,d}(t,\bm{r})\rangle]=-\frac{e^{2}n_{X}}{4m}[\bm{A}_{e}(t,\bm{r})-\bm{A}_{h}(t,\bm{r})]=-\frac{e^{2}n_{X}}{4m}\bm{A}^{-}(t,\bm{r})

(159)

The linear coupling Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{c}
can be rewritten in the momentum space.
Define the Fourier transformation of the gauge field as

A^{\sigma}_{\mu}(t,\bm{r})=\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\bm{q}}\textrm{e}^{i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}A^{\sigma}_{\mu}(t,\bm{q})\Longleftrightarrow A^{\sigma}_{\mu}(t,\bm{q})=\int\textrm{d}\bm{r}\;\textrm{e}^{-i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}A^{\sigma}_{\mu}(t,\bm{r})

(160)

Then the linear coupling Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{c}
is rewritten as

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{c}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma=\pm}\int\textrm{d}\bm{r}\;[\hat{\varrho}^{\sigma}(\bm{r})\phi^{\sigma}(t,\bm{r})-\hat{\bm{j}}_{p}^{\sigma}(\bm{r})\cdot\bm{A}^{\sigma}(t,\bm{r})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma=\pm}\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\bm{q}}\int\textrm{d}\bm{r}\;\textrm{e}^{i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}[\hat{\varrho}^{\sigma}(\bm{r})\phi^{\sigma}(t,\bm{q})-\hat{\bm{j}}_{p}^{\sigma}(\bm{r})\cdot\bm{A}^{\sigma}(t,\bm{q})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma=\pm}\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\bm{q}}[\hat{\varrho}^{\sigma}(-\bm{q})\phi^{\sigma}(t,\bm{q})-\hat{\bm{j}}_{p}^{\sigma}(-\bm{q})\cdot\bm{A}^{\sigma}(t,\bm{q})]

(161)

where

\displaystyle\hat{\varrho}^{\sigma}(\bm{q})=\int\textrm{d}\bm{r}\;\textrm{e}^{-i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}\hat{\varrho}^{\sigma}(\bm{r})\Longleftrightarrow\hat{\varrho}^{\sigma}(\bm{r})=\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\bm{q}}\textrm{e}^{i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}\hat{\varrho}^{\sigma}(\bm{q})

(162a)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{j}}_{p}^{\sigma}(\bm{q})=\int\textrm{d}\bm{r}\;\textrm{e}^{-i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}\hat{\bm{j}}_{p}^{\sigma}(\bm{r})\Longleftrightarrow\hat{\bm{j}}_{p}^{\sigma}(\bm{r})=\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\bm{q}}\textrm{e}^{i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}\hat{\bm{j}}_{p}^{\sigma}(\bm{q})

(162b)

To be specific, we have

\displaystyle\hat{\varrho}^{+}(\bm{q})=

\displaystyle-\frac{e}{\mathcal{V}}\int\textrm{d}\bm{r}\;\textrm{e}^{-i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}\sum_{\bm{k}\bm{k}^{\prime}}\textrm{e}^{-i(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime})\cdot\bm{r}}C^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}}C_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}=\sum_{\bm{k}}-eC^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2}C_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2}

(163a)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{j}}_{p}^{+}(\bm{q})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\int\textrm{d}\bm{r}\;\textrm{e}^{-i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}\sum_{\bm{k}\bm{k}^{\prime}}\textrm{e}^{-i(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime})\cdot\bm{r}}\left[\frac{ie\hbar}{4m}C^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}}\sigma_{z}(i\bm{k}^{\prime}+i\bm{k})C_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}\right]=\sum_{\bm{k}}-\frac{e\hbar\bm{k}}{2m}C^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2}\sigma_{z}C_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2}

(163b)

\displaystyle\hat{\varrho}^{-}(\bm{q})=

\displaystyle-\frac{e}{2\mathcal{V}}\int\textrm{d}\bm{r}\;\textrm{e}^{-i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}\sum_{\bm{k}\bm{k}^{\prime}}\textrm{e}^{-i(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime})\cdot\bm{r}}C^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}}\sigma_{z}C_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}=\sum_{\bm{k}}-\frac{e}{2}C^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2}\sigma_{z}C_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2}

(163c)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{j}}_{p}^{-}(\bm{q})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\int\textrm{d}\bm{r}\;\textrm{e}^{-i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}\sum_{\bm{k}\bm{k}^{\prime}}\textrm{e}^{-i(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime})\cdot\bm{r}}\left[\frac{ie\hbar}{8m}C^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}}(i\bm{k}^{\prime}+i\bm{k})C_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}\right]=\sum_{\bm{k}}-\frac{e\hbar\bm{k}}{4m}C^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2}C_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2}

(163d)

where
C^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}}=[c^{\dagger}_{e\bm{k}},c^{\dagger}_{h\bm{k}}]
is the creation operator.
It’s convenient to define the bare vertex function as

\gamma^{+}_{\mu=0,\bm{k}}=-e\sigma_{0},\;\gamma^{+}_{\mu=12,\bm{k}}=-\frac{e\hbar\bm{k}}{2m}\sigma_{z},\;\gamma^{-}_{\mu=0,\bm{k}}=-\frac{e}{2}\sigma_{z},\;\gamma^{-}_{\mu=12,\bm{k}}=-\frac{e\hbar\bm{k}}{4m}\sigma_{0}

(164)

Then the paramagnetic current operator
\hat{j}^{\sigma}_{p\mu}(\bm{q})\equiv(\hat{\varrho}^{\sigma}(\bm{q}),\hat{\bm{j}}^{\sigma}_{p}(\bm{q}))
could be simply written as

\hat{j}^{\sigma}_{p\mu}(\bm{q})=\sum_{\bm{k}}C^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2}\gamma^{\sigma}_{\mu,\bm{k}}C_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2}

(165)

C.4
The electromagnetic response kernel in long-wavelength limit

To get the electromagnetic response kernel
K^{\pm}_{\mu\nu}
, we need to calculate the correlation functions between the paramagnetic current operators, which requires the evaluation of

\mathrm{J}^{\sigma}_{\mu,n}(\bm{q})\equiv\sum_{\bm{k}}\Phi^{\dagger}_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{q})\begin{bmatrix}\gamma^{\sigma}_{\mu,cv\bm{k}}(-\bm{q})\\
\gamma^{\sigma}_{\mu,vc-\bm{k}}(-\bm{q})\end{bmatrix}=\sum_{\bm{k}}\Phi_{n\bm{k}}^{\dagger}(\bm{q})\begin{bmatrix}\langle c\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2|\gamma^{\sigma}_{\mu,\bm{k}}|v\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2\rangle\\
\langle v-\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2|\gamma^{\sigma}_{\mu,\bm{k}}|c-\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2\rangle\end{bmatrix}.

(166)

According to Eq. (
138
), (
114
) and (
164
), we can see that
\mathrm{Im}\mathrm{J}^{\sigma}_{\mu,n}(\bm{q})=0
.
Thus the correlation functions between the current operators
\hat{j}^{\sigma}_{p\mu}
are symmetric, i.e.,

C_{\hat{j}^{\sigma}_{p\mu}\hat{j}^{\sigma^{\prime}}_{p\nu}}(\omega,\bm{q})=\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{n}\frac{\omega_{n}(\bm{q})[\mathrm{J}^{\sigma}_{\mu,n}(\bm{q})]^{*}\mathrm{J}^{\sigma^{\prime}}_{\nu,n}(\bm{q})}{\omega^{+}-\omega_{n}(\bm{q})}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{n}\frac{\omega_{n}(\bm{q})[\mathrm{J}^{\sigma^{\prime}}_{\nu,n}(\bm{q})]^{*}\mathrm{J}^{\sigma}_{\mu,n}(\bm{q})}{\omega^{+}-\omega_{n}(\bm{q})}=C_{\hat{j}^{\sigma^{\prime}}_{p\nu}\hat{j}^{\sigma}_{p\mu}}(\omega,\bm{q})

(167)

Besides, as shown in Eq. (
164
), all the bare vertex function
\gamma^{\sigma}_{\mu,\bm{k}}
are proportional to either
\sigma_{0}
or
\sigma_{z}
.
In the long-wavelength limit
q\to 0
, to lowest order of
q
we have

\displaystyle\langle c\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2|\sigma_{0}|v\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2\rangle=

\displaystyle\beta_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2}\alpha_{\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2}-\alpha_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2}\beta_{\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2}=-\bm{q}\cdot\langle c\bm{k}|\nabla_{\bm{k}}v\bm{k}\rangle+\mathcal{O}(q^{2})

(168a)

\displaystyle\langle v-\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2|\sigma_{0}|c-\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2\rangle=

\displaystyle\langle v\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2|\sigma_{0}|c\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2\rangle=-\bm{q}\cdot\langle\nabla_{\bm{k}}v\bm{k}|c\bm{k}\rangle+\mathcal{O}(q^{2})

(168b)

\displaystyle\langle c\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2|\sigma_{z}|v\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2\rangle=

\displaystyle 2\beta_{\bm{k}}\alpha_{\bm{k}}+\mathcal{O}(q)

(168c)

\displaystyle\langle v-\bm{k}+\bm{q}/2|\sigma_{z}|c-\bm{k}-\bm{q}/2\rangle=

\displaystyle 2\beta_{\bm{k}}\alpha_{\bm{k}}+\mathcal{O}(q)

(168d)

Noticed that
|c\bm{k}\rangle
and
|v\bm{k}\rangle
are taken to be real, thus
\langle c\bm{k}|\nabla_{\bm{k}}v\bm{k}\rangle=\langle\nabla_{\bm{k}}v\bm{k}|c\bm{k}\rangle
,
and to lowest order of
q
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{J}^{+}_{\mu=0,n}=

\displaystyle e\bm{q}\cdot\sum_{\bm{k}}\langle c\bm{k}|\nabla_{\bm{k}}v\bm{k}\rangle\Phi^{\dagger}_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{0})\begin{bmatrix}1\\
1\end{bmatrix}+\mathcal{O}(q^{2})

(169a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{J}^{+}_{\mu=12,n}=

\displaystyle-\frac{e\hbar}{m}\sum_{\bm{k}}k_{\mu}\alpha_{k}\beta_{k}\Phi^{\dagger}_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{0})\begin{bmatrix}1\\
1\end{bmatrix}+\mathcal{O}(q)

(169b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{J}^{-}_{\mu=0,n}=

\displaystyle-e\sum_{\bm{k}}\alpha_{k}\beta_{k}\Phi^{\dagger}_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{0})\begin{bmatrix}1\\
1\end{bmatrix}+\mathcal{O}(q)

(169c)

\displaystyle\mathrm{J}^{-}_{\mu=12,n}=

\displaystyle\frac{e\hbar}{4m}\bm{q}\cdot\sum_{\bm{k}}k_{\mu}\langle c\bm{k}|\nabla_{\bm{k}}v\bm{k}\rangle\Phi^{\dagger}_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{0})\begin{bmatrix}1\\
1\end{bmatrix}+\mathcal{O}(q^{2})

(169d)

According to Eq. (
132
), we have

\displaystyle\Phi^{\dagger}_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{0})\begin{bmatrix}1\\
1\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\Phi^{\dagger}_{-n\bm{k}}(\bm{0})\begin{bmatrix}1\\
1\end{bmatrix}=x_{n\bm{k}}^{*}(\bm{0})

(170)

This means that
\mathrm{J}^{\sigma}_{\mu,-n}=\mathrm{J}^{\sigma}_{\mu=0,n}
.
Besides, in the
\bm{k}
summations in Eq. (
169
),
\langle c\bm{k}|\nabla_{\bm{k}}v\bm{k}\rangle
and
k_{\mu}\alpha_{k}\beta_{k}
are dipole functions of
\bm{k}
,
\alpha_{k}\beta_{k}
is monopole function, and
k_{\mu}\langle c\bm{k}|\nabla_{\bm{k}}v\bm{k}\rangle
is quadrupole function.
Thus, in the long-wavelength limit and to the lowest order
q
, the charge density and currents operator
\hat{j}^{+}_{p\mu}(\bm{q})
only couples to dipole modes, the exciton density operator
\hat{j}^{-}_{p0}(\bm{q})=-\hat{\varrho}^{-}(\bm{q})
only couples to the monopole modes, and the exciton currents operators
\hat{j}^{-}_{\mu=12}(\bm{q})=\hat{\bm{j}}_{p}^{-}(\bm{q})
only couples to the quadrupole modes.
Assume the momentum is along the
x
-direction, the correlation functions to the lowest order of
q
is written as

\displaystyle C_{\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}}(\omega,q)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega_{n}>0\\
n\in\mathrm{dipole}\end{subarray}}|\mathrm{J}^{+}_{0,n}|^{2}\left[\frac{\omega_{n}(\bm{q})}{\omega^{+}-\omega_{n}(\bm{q})}-\frac{\omega_{n}(\bm{q})}{\omega^{+}+\omega_{n}(\bm{q})}\right]=\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega_{n}>0\\
n\in\mathrm{dipole}\end{subarray}}\frac{2|\mathrm{J}^{+}_{0,n}|^{2}\omega^{2}_{n}(\bm{q})}{(\omega^{+})^{2}-\omega_{n}^{2}(\bm{q})}+\mathcal{O}(q^{3})

(171a)

\displaystyle C_{\hat{j}^{+}_{pa\neq 0}\hat{j}^{+}_{pb=0}}(\omega,q)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega_{n}>0\\
n\in\mathrm{dipole}\end{subarray}}\frac{2(\mathrm{J}^{+}_{a,n})^{*}\mathrm{J}^{+}_{0,n}\omega^{2}_{n}(\bm{q})}{(\omega^{+})^{2}-\omega_{n}^{2}(\bm{q})}+\mathcal{O}(q^{2})

(171b)

\displaystyle C_{\hat{j}^{+}_{pa\neq 0}\hat{j}^{+}_{pb\neq 0}}(\omega,q)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega_{n}>0\\
n\in\mathrm{dipole}\end{subarray}}\frac{2(\mathrm{J}^{+}_{a,n})^{*}\mathrm{J}^{+}_{b,n}\omega^{2}_{n}(\bm{q})}{(\omega^{+})^{2}-\omega_{n}^{2}(\bm{q})}+\mathcal{O}(q)

(171c)

\displaystyle C_{\hat{j}^{-}_{p0}\hat{j}^{-}_{p0}}(\omega,q)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega_{n}>0\\
n\in\mathrm{monopole}\end{subarray}}\frac{2|\mathrm{J}^{-}_{0,n}|^{2}\omega^{2}_{n}(\bm{q})}{(\omega^{+})^{2}-\omega_{n}^{2}(\bm{q})}+\mathcal{O}(q)

(171d)

\displaystyle C_{\hat{j}^{-}_{pa\neq 0}\hat{j}^{-}_{pb=0}}(\omega,q)=

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(q^{2})

(171e)

\displaystyle C_{\hat{j}^{-}_{pa\neq 0}\hat{j}^{-}_{pb\neq 0}}(\omega,q)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega_{n}>0\\
n\in\mathrm{quadrupole}\end{subarray}}\frac{2(\mathrm{J}^{-}_{a,n})^{*}\mathrm{J}^{-}_{b,n}\omega^{2}_{n}(\bm{q})}{(\omega^{+})^{2}-\omega_{n}^{2}(\bm{q})}+\mathcal{O}(q^{3})

(171f)

C.5
The effective dielectric function

The effective dielectric function
\epsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}(\omega,\bm{q})
is defined as

\epsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}(\omega,\bm{q})=\frac{\tilde{V}(\bm{q})}{\tilde{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\omega,\bm{q})}

(172)

where
\tilde{V}(\bm{q})
is the bare Coulomb interaction and
\tilde{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\omega,\bm{q})
is the effective Coulomb interaction renormalized by the charge density fluctuations as illustrated in FIG.
C.1
(a).
In FIG.
C.1
,
C\equiv C_{\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}}(\omega,\bm{q})
is the density density correlation function, and
C^{\mathrm{ir}}\equiv C^{\mathrm{ir}}_{\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}}(\omega,\bm{q})
is its irreducible counterpart, whose relation is shown in FIG.
B.1
(b).
Under TDHF approximation,
C^{\mathrm{ir}}_{\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}}(\omega,\bm{q})
is just a summation of the ladder diagrams shown in FIG.
B.1
(b).
The bare Coulomb interaction is
\tilde{V}(\bm{q})=2\pi/\epsilon q
for charge in the same layer and
\tilde{U}(\bm{q})=\tilde{V}(\bm{q})\textrm{e}^{-qd}
for charge in different layers.
However, in the long-wavelength limit such that
qd\to 0
, we have
\tilde{U}(\bm{q})\approx\tilde{V}(\bm{q})
, i.e., we don’t need to distinguish between the intra- and inter-layer Coulomb interaction.
Then according to the Feynman diagrams shown in FIG.
C.1
(a), the effective Coulomb interaction is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\omega,\bm{q})=

\displaystyle\tilde{V}(\bm{q})+\tilde{V}(\bm{q})C^{\mathrm{ir}}_{\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}}(\omega,\bm{q})\tilde{V}(\bm{q})+\tilde{V}(\bm{q})C^{\mathrm{ir}}_{\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}}(\omega,\bm{q})\tilde{V}(\bm{q})C^{\mathrm{ir}}_{\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}}(\omega,\bm{q})\tilde{V}(\bm{q})+\cdots

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}[\tilde{V}(\bm{q})C^{\mathrm{ir}}_{\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}}(\omega,\bm{q})]^{n}\tilde{V}(\bm{q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[1-\tilde{V}(\bm{q})C^{\mathrm{ir}}_{\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}}(\omega,\bm{q})]^{-1}\tilde{V}(\bm{q})

(173)

On the other hand, according to FIG.
C.1
(b), the total density density correlation function is given by

C_{\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}}(\omega,\bm{q})=C^{\mathrm{ir}}_{\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}}(\omega,\bm{q})+\tilde{V}(\bm{q})C^{\mathrm{ir}}_{\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}}(\omega,\bm{q})\tilde{V}(\bm{q})+\cdots

(174)

which means the effective Coulomb interaction could also be written as

\displaystyle\tilde{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\omega,\bm{q})=

\displaystyle\tilde{V}(\bm{q})+\tilde{V}(\bm{q})[C^{\mathrm{ir}}_{\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}}(\omega,\bm{q})+C^{\mathrm{ir}}_{\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}}(\omega,\bm{q})\tilde{V}(\bm{q})C^{\mathrm{ir}}_{\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}}(\omega,\bm{q})+\cdots]\tilde{V}(\bm{q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{V}(\bm{q})+\tilde{V}(\bm{q})C_{\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}}(\omega,\bm{q})\tilde{V}(\bm{q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[1+\tilde{V}(\bm{q})C_{\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}}(\omega,\bm{q})]\tilde{V}(\bm{q})

(175)

Thus the effective dielectric function is given by

\epsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}(\omega,\bm{q})=\frac{\tilde{V}(\bm{q})}{\tilde{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\omega,\bm{q})}=1-\tilde{V}(\bm{q})C^{\mathrm{ir}}_{\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}}(\omega,\bm{q})=[1+\tilde{V}(\bm{q})C_{\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}\hat{j}^{+}_{p0}}(\omega,\bm{q})]^{-1}

(176)
