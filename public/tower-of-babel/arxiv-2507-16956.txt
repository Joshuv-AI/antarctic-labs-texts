On Cloitre’s hiccup sequences


Abstract
In 2003, Benoit Cloitre entered a bunch of sequences in the OEIS that we call hiccup sequences. We collect the various claims, observations, and proofs of properties of these sequences that have been entered in the OEIS over the years, and present a unified approach, guided by a remarkable theorem of Bosma, Dekking, and Steiner.

Aronson’s sequence is an integer sequence that is associated to the English sentence

T is The firsT, fourTh, elevenTh, sixTeenTh, … letter in this sentence.

Spaces and punctuation are ignored. By locating the
T
’s we get the numbers:

1,4,11,16,24,29,33,35,39,45,47,51,56,58,62,64,\ldots,

which is entry
A005224
in the On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences. Douglas Hofstadter
[
14
]

attributed the sequence to the British clinical pharmacologist Jeffrey Aronson. The numbers in Aronson’s sequence generate a sentence that on its turn generates the numbers. It is a sort of a Munchausen manner to generate numbers, and
there are many different ways in which one can vary on this theme.
Cloitre et al.
[
8
]
introduced several sequences that are all inspired by Aronson’s sequence.
We will be interested in a particular type of sequences, that
were all entered by Benoit Cloitre in the OEIS some twenty years ago. One of these is
A086398
, and its first entries are

(1)

1,5,7,9,11,15,17,21,23,27,29,33,35,37,39,43,45,49,51,53,\ldots.

where
a(n)=a(n-1)+4
if
n-1
is already in the sequence, and otherwise
a(n)=a(n-1)+2
,
starting from
a(1)=1
. Actually, the definition in the OEIS is slightly different from ours,
but it is not hard to check that it is equivalent. The differences

a(n)-a(n-1)
take two values that appear in some sort of rhythm, just like
bouts of hiccups
[
4
, p96]
, and that is why we call it a
hiccup sequence
.

If we vary the definition a bit and put
a(n)=a(n-1)+4
if
n
is in the sequence and
a(n)=a(n-1)+2

otherwise,
then we get
A080903

(2)

1,3,7,9,11,13,17,19,23,25,29,31,35,37,39,41,45,47,51,53,\ldots.

There are many hiccup sequences in the OEIS, which we have collected in Table
1
below.
One of these is
A064437
. Robert Israel empirically found a closed formula for this sequence:

(3)

a(n)=\left\lceil\left(1+\sqrt{2}\right)(n-1)+\frac{1}{2+\sqrt{2}}\right\rceil

and checked its validity for the first hundred entries.
The reader may recognize that this is a Beatty sequence
[
12
]
. Similar formulas can be found on the
OEIS for other hiccup sequences.

It is possible to define hiccup sequences that are not increasing, or have first differences
that take more than two values. There are plenty of such hiccup sequences in the OEIS.
For example, the hiccup sequence
A080782
produces a permutation of the natural numbers.
Other examples are
A079354
,
A080458
,
A080900
,
and
A111202
.
A hiccup sequence with three gaps is
A080574
.
These are all very interesting sequences, but we
do not consider them in this paper.

A hiccup sequence is degenerate if
x=z=1
.
Consider, for example, the
(0,1,y,1)
-hiccup sequence for an arbitrary
y>1
.
What is
a(2)
? At index
2
, the only number already in the
sequence is
a(1)=1
and therefore
a(2)=2
, which now happens
to be in the sequence. The next number is
a(3)=3
, etc.
This problem does not arise if
x>1
,
as is illustrated by the first entry in Table
1
. It
is the only hiccup sequence with
z=1
in the OEIS.

The literature on hiccup sequences is limited. The only study so far,
by Bosma, Dekking, and Steiner.
[
6
]
, concerns
the
(1,1,3,2)
-sequence. Its
first few entries are

1,4,6,8,11,13,16,18,21,23,25,28,30,33,35,37,40,42,45,47,\ldots

Bosma, Dekking, and Steiner proved that there are different ways to characterize the sequence.
The aim of our paper is to extend this remarkable result to other hiccup sequences.

Theorem 2

(Bosma–Dekking–Steiner)
.

The sequence
A086377
can be characterized in the following equivalent ways:

(1)

Hiccup sequence
– Defined recursively by

a(n)=\begin{cases}a(n-1)+2,&\text{if }n-1\notin\{a(k)\}_{k<n},\\[4.0pt]
a(n-1)+3,&\text{if }n-1\in\{a(k)\}_{k<n},\end{cases}

with initial condition
a(1)=1
.

(2)

Morphic characterization
– The positions of
1
’s in the fixed point of the morphism

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\mapsto 10,

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\mapsto 100.

(3)

Beatty sequence representation
–

a(n)=\left\lfloor(1+\sqrt{2})n-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right\rfloor.

(4)

Iterated function sequence
–

a(n)=\left\lfloor r_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\right\rfloor,

where
r_{1}=\dfrac{4}{\pi}
and

r_{n+1}=f_{n}(r_{n}),\quad\text{with }f_{n}(x)=\frac{n^{2}}{x+1-2n}.

We will refer to Theorem
2
as the
BDS theorem
. The sections in our
paper are numbered according to the characterizations of this theorem. In each
section we extend the BDS theorem to more general hiccup sequences.

We highlight some of the scattered comments on hiccup sequences that can be found on the OEIS.
The
(0,1,2,3)
-hiccup sequence is entry
A007066
with first few numbers

1,4,7,9,12,15,17,20,22,25,28,30,33,36,38,41,43,\ldots

It is entered in the OEIS as a Beatty sequence

a(n)=\left\lceil(n-1)\cdot\phi^{2}+1\right\rceil,

where
\phi=\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}
is the golden mean.
This is equal to the upper Wythoff numbers plus two, and
it also corresponds to the positions of
1
of the fixed
point of
0\mapsto 010,\ 1\mapsto 10
that starts with
1
.
This follows from Fraenkel’s characterizations
of the upper Wythoff sequence
[
13
]
.

The
(1,1,4,2)
-hiccup sequence
A086398
starts out with

1,5,7,9,11,15,17,21,23,27,29,33,35,37,39,43,45,49,\ldots.

Kimberling asked if it corresponds to the positions of
1
in the
unique fixed point of
0\mapsto 10,\ 1\mapsto 1000
. It is not so hard to
see that this is true. The fixed point

\omega=1000101010100010100010\cdots

satisfies

\omega=\sigma(\omega)=\sigma(1)\sigma(0)\sigma(0)\sigma(0)\sigma(1)\sigma(0)\cdots.

The
n
-th
1
in the sequence corresponds to the
n
-th
\sigma(d)
for
d=0
or
d=1

in
\sigma(\omega)
. It is
4
ahead of the previous
1
exactly if the
(n-1)
-th
digit of
\omega
is a
1
. In other words, it is
4
ahead if and only if
n-1
is in
the sequence.

Kimberling also asked if
-1<n\left(1+\sqrt{3}\right)-a(n)<4
. There is no
Beatty sequence readily available, unlike in the previous examples.
Since all numbers
in the sequence are odd, we can convert it by
a(n)\mapsto b(n)=(a(n)+1)/2
, which gives
A026363
.
Here, Kimberling asks if
-1<n\left(1+\sqrt{3}\right)/2-b(n)<2
, which is a slightly
weaker bound.

The
(0,1,2,4)
-hiccup sequence is entry
A080580
in the OEIS.

1,5,9,13,15,19,23,27,29,33,37,41,43,47,49,53,57,61,\ldots

Joe Slater proves in a comment on this sequence that it coincides with the positions of
1
in the
(non-unique) fixed

0\mapsto 0010,\ 1\mapsto 10
that starts with
1
. By the same argument, the
n
-th
1
in this
sequence is
4
ahead of the previous
1
exactly if
n
is not in the sequence. Otherwise,
it is ahead by
2
. We will call this Slater’s construction in this paper.

If we change the
x
parameter from
2
to
1
we get the
(0,1,1,4)
-hiccup sequence,
which is the previous entry
A080579

1,5,9,13,14,18,22,26,27,31,35,39,40,41,45,49,53,54,\ldots

in the OEIS. Slater’s argument works for the fixed point of
0\mapsto 0001,\ 1\mapsto 1
that starts
with
10
. If we change
z
from
4
to
3
we get entry
A080578
, the
(0,1,1,3)
-hiccup sequence,
which by Slater’s construction is the fixed point of
0\mapsto 001,\ 1\mapsto 1
that starts with
10
,
see also
[

]
. Interestingly, this sequence is morphic but not automatic, as shown in
example 16 of
[

]
. It has parameter

. We will see later on that hiccup sequences
with

Cloitre claims
that
a(2^{n})=2^{n+1}
, which follows from the fact that
w_{n}=\sigma^{n}(10)
is a word of length
2^{n+1}
with

2^{n}

\sigma
is Slater’s morphism for this sequence.
The word
w_{n}

Most hiccup sequences are stand alone, but some have been linked to other seemingly unrelated
sequences. In particular,

A040412
has been linked to bottom-up search and

A080578
has been linked to meta-Fibonacci sequences.
One feature that is shared by the hiccup sequences
with
|y-z|=1
is a description as a Beatty sequence in the comments.
For some sequences it even is the other way around. They are entered
as Beatty sequences in the OEIS and described as hiccup sequences in the comments.

2
Morphisms for hiccup sequences

An increasing sequence
a(n)
corresponds to a binary sequence
b(n)
, such that
b(n)=1

if and only if
n=a(k)
for some
k
.
If we interpret the sequence as a set
A=\{a(n)\}\subset\mathbb{N}
, then
the
characteristic sequence
is equal to
1

at position
a
if
a\in A
and it is
0
otherwise.
We will show that the characteristic sequence of a hiccup
sequence is morphic.
A
morphism
or
substitution
on an alphabet is a formal rule that replaces each letter of the alphabet with a finite word over that same alphabet.

Definition 3
.

Let
\Sigma
and
\Delta
be finite alphabets. A sequence
(x_{n})_{n\geq 1}\in\Delta^{\mathbb{N}}
is called a
morphic sequence
, see
[
2
, Ch. 7]
, if there exist:

•

a morphism
\phi:\Sigma\to\Sigma^{*}
such that
\sigma
has a fixed point
w=\phi^{\infty}(a)
for some
a\in\Sigma
, and

•

a coding
\pi:\Sigma\to\Delta
(i.e., a letter-to-letter morphism),

such that

x_{n}=\pi(w_{n})\quad\text{for all }n\geq 1.

We say that an increasing sequence
a(n)
is morphic if its characteristic
sequence is morphic.

Lemma 4
.

A
(0,1,y,z)
-hiccup sequence is morphic.

Proof.

We already went over this argument for
z>y
in the previous section.
Define

(4)

\phi(1)=10^{y-1},\ \phi(0)=0^{z-y}10^{y-1}

and let
(w_{i})
be the
fixed point with
w_{i}\in\{0,1\}
and
w_{1}w_{2}=10
. That second letter
0

takes care of case
y=1
.
Since the sequence is a fixed point, we have
(w_{i})=(\phi(w_{i}))
.
The
n
-th
1
is in
\phi(w_{n})
. It is
preceded by
y-1
zeros if and only if the first letter of
\phi(w_{n})

is
1
. Otherwise, it is preceded by
z-1
zeros.
Therefore, if
a(n)
is the position of the
n
-th zero, then

a(n)-a(n-1)=y
if
n
is in the sequence and

a(n)-a(n-1)=z
if it is not. This is a hiccup sequence. Its initial entry is
a(1)=1
.

If
z<y
,
we add a letter
b
to the alphabet, and define a morphism on
\{b,0,1\}

by

(5)

b\mapsto b0^{z-1},\ 0\mapsto 10^{z-1},\ 1\mapsto 0^{y-z}10^{z-1}.

Let
(w_{i})
be the unique fixed point with initial letters
b0
.
If we count
b
as a
1
, i.e., apply a coding
b\mapsto 1
,
then every code word contains one
1
.
By the same argument as before, the
n
-th
1
is preceded by
y-1
zeros if

w_{n}=1
and by
z-1
zeros if
w_{n}=0
.
∎

The additional letter
b
in the proof of the lemma, which counts as a special
1
so to speak, is needed to get the
right initial condition
a(1)=1
. The same trick works if
a(1)>1
.

Lemma 5
.

A
(0,x,y,z)
-hiccup sequence with
x>1

is morphic.

Proof.

We add a letter
b
to the alphabet and consider the fixed point that
starts with
b
.
If
y<z
we define the morphism

(6)

b\mapsto b0^{x-2}10^{y-1},\ 0\mapsto 0^{z-y}10^{y-1},\ 1\mapsto 10^{y-1}.

Note that
b
can be removed from this morphisms if
x=z-y+1
,
when the fixed point that starts with
0
produces the hiccup sequence. If
y>z
we define

(7)

b\mapsto b0^{x-2}10^{z-1},\ 0\mapsto 10^{z-1},\ 1\mapsto 0^{y-z}10^{z-1}.

Now code
b\mapsto 0
.
∎

Note that if we remove
b
from the morphism in Equation
6
, this gives the morphism
in Equation
4
. Similarly, if we replace
b\mapsto b0^{x-2}10^{z-1}
by
b\mapsto b0^{z-1}

in Equation
7
,
i.e., remove the factor
0^{x-2}1
from the image of
b
, then we get Equation
5
.
With these morphisms for
j=0
in hand, we can find sequences
in the OEIS that have not yet been recognized as hiccup sequences. For instance, if
x=2,y=2,z=3

then we get
A026356
. This sequence is equal to
A007066
, except for the first entry,
which is the hiccup sequence with
x=1,y=2,z=3
.
The morphism

(8)

0\mapsto 010,\ 1\mapsto 10

generates both of these sequences.

A026356
is the fixed point starting with
0
, and
A007066
is the one starting with
1
,
see
[
11
, Exercise 6.1.25]
. That explains why the two sequences are the same, except for their initial entries.

The morphisms in these lemmas are not unique, there are other morphisms that code the same sequence.
For instance, our morphism for the
(0,2,4,2)
-hiccup sequence in Equation
7

is given by
b\mapsto b10,\ 0\mapsto 10,

1\mapsto 0010
. This morphism can be simplified to

0\mapsto 01,

1\mapsto 0001
, which by Slater’s construction produces the same fixed point
without using the special letter
b
. The
(0,2,4,2)
-hiccup sequence is entry
A284753
in the OEIS. It is not yet listed as a hiccup sequence.

There is a degree of freedom in the morphism, by a cyclic permutation of the
image words of
0
and
1
. If
we replace the morphism in Equation
6
by
b\mapsto b0^{x-2}10^{y-2},\ 0\mapsto 0^{z-y+1}10^{y-2},\ 1\mapsto 010^{y-2},
assuming that
y\geq 2
, then we get the same fixed point.
Similarly, we can replace the morphism in Equation
7
by

b\mapsto b0^{x-2}10^{z-2},\ 0\mapsto 010^{z-2},\ 1\mapsto 0^{y-z+1}10^{z-2},

if
z\geq 2
. This is exactly how we got that morphism
0\mapsto 01,\ 1\mapsto 0001
above.
If the parameters allow it, it is possible to get rid of the special letter
b
by
iterating the cyclic permutation. A sequence is
purely morphic
if
there is no need for the coding
\pi
in Definition
3
.

Lemma 6
.

A
(0,x,y,z)
-hiccup sequence is purely morphic if
z-y+1\leq x\leq z
.

Proof.

A cyclic permutation of the code words for
0
and
1
gives the morphism

(9)

0\mapsto 0^{x-1}10^{z-x},\ 1\mapsto 0^{x+y-z-1}10^{z-x}.

∎

Lemma 7
.

A
(1,x,y,z)
-hiccup sequence with
x\geq 1

is morphic. It is purely morphic if
x=1
and
y>1
.

Proof.

First suppose
x>1
.
We add the letter
b
and we define

b\mapsto b0^{x-2}10^{y-1},\ 0\mapsto 10^{z-1},\ 1\mapsto 10^{y-1}.

Let
(w_{i})
be the unique fixed point that starts with
b
, which
is coded to
0
. The
n
-th
1
in the sequence is in
\sigma(w_{n})
.
It is preceded by
y-1
zeros if
w_{n-1}=1
, i.e., if
n-1
is
in the sequence, and otherwise by
z-1
zeros.

If
x=1
and
y=1
,
then we get the trivial sequence
a(n)=n
.
If
x=1
and
y>1
then there is no need for the letter
b
, and we can
take
0\mapsto 10^{z-1},\ 1\mapsto 10^{y-1}
.
∎

Some of these morphisms correspond to entries in the OEIS that have not been recognized
as hiccup sequences.
For instance, the
(1,1,2,1)
-hiccup sequence is the fixed point of the Fibonacci morphism

0\mapsto 1,\ 1\mapsto 10
, which gives the lower Wythoff numbers
A000201
. The

(1,1,3,1)
-hiccup sequence is equal to
A003156
, and the
(1,1,2,3)
-hiccup sequence
is
A026352
.

Lemma 8
.

Let
b(n)
be the
(0,z-1,y,z)
-hiccup sequence for
y,z>1
, and let

a(n+1)=b(n)+1,\ a(1)=0.

Then
a(n)
is the
(0,0,y,z)
-hiccup sequence.
In particular,
a(n+1)
is morphic.

For instance,
A081841
is equal to
A064437
plus one.

Proof.

Since
y,z>1
we have that
b(n+1)\geq b(n)+2
for all
n
and
b(1)=z-1\geq 1
.
It follows that
b(n)>n
for
n>1
.
By definition,
a(2)=b(1)+1=z
, which is the correct value for the
(0,0,y,z)
-hiccup sequence.
For
n>1
we have

a(n+1)=b(n)+1=b(n-1)+y+1=a(n)+y

if
n\in\{b(k)\}_{k<n}
, and otherwise

a(n+1)=b(n)+1=b(n-1)+z+1=a(n)+z.

Since
a(k)=b(k-1)+1
and
a(1)=0
, it follows that
a(n+1)=a(n)+y
if and only if
n+1\in\{a(k)\}_{k<n+1}
,
and it is
a(n+1)=a(n)+z
otherwise.

The sequence
b(n)
corresponds to the positions of
1
in a coded fixed point of a morphism.
This morphism has an alphabet of two or three letters, depending on
the parameters. To convert this to a morphism for
a(n+1)=b(n)+1
, add a letter
c
and add
the substitution
c\mapsto cd
to the morphism,
where
d
is the initial letter of the fixed point for
b(n)
.
This is the morphism for
a(n+1)
.
Add
c\mapsto 0
to the coding.
∎

Lemma 9
.

Let
a(n)

Lemma 11

A

There is a well-known correspondence between Sturmian substitutions and the sequences

S_{\alpha,\beta}(n)=\lfloor\alpha(n+1)+\beta\rfloor-\lfloor\alpha n+\beta\rfloor,

which are known as

or

The values of

T_{E}(x,y)=\left(1-x,1-y\right),\ T_{L}(x,y)=\left(\frac{1-x}{1-2x},\frac{1-y}{1-2x}\right),\ T_{R}(x,y)=\left(\frac{1-x}{1-2x},\frac{2-x-y}{1-2x}\right).

A composition of

corresponds to a composition of these
transformations, and
(\alpha,\beta)
is a fixed point of the resulting composite transformation,
see
[
5
, p73]
and
[

As an example, we compute
\alpha

and

The morphism has adjacency matrix

1&1\end{bmatrix}

which has largest eigenvalue
\lambda=\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}

Now
\alpha
is equal to the density of
of
1^{\prime}s
in the fixed point of the morphism,
and a minor calculation gives
\alpha=\frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}
.
The fixed point
(\alpha,\beta)
for

satisfies
\beta=1-\alpha
.
This is one of the special cases in which rounding up or down makes a difference
for the mechanical word. The
rounded-up sequence
corresponds to the fixed point beginning with

begins with

.

Mechanical words can be converted to Beatty sequences
[

]
.
The following result and its proof is almost identical to
[

Lemma 12
.

Let
\alpha>1
be irrational, and let
(s_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be given by
the rounded-up mechanical sequence

s_{n}=\lceil(n+1)\alpha+\beta\rceil-\lceil n\alpha+\beta\rceil,

for some real number
\beta
with
\alpha+\beta\geq 1
.
Then the rounded-down Beatty sequence

\left\lfloor\frac{k}{\alpha}-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\right\rfloor\ \text{ for }k\in\mathbb{N}

corresponds to the sequence of positions of 1 in
(s_{n})
. Similarly, a rounded-down
mechanical sequence corresponds to a rounded-up Beatty sequence.

Proof.

Since
\alpha+\beta\geq 1
we have that
\frac{k}{\alpha}-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\geq 1
if
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
It follows that the elements of our Beatty sequence are all natural numbers.

\displaystyle\exists k\geq 1:n=\left\lfloor\frac{k}{\alpha}-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\exists k\geq 1:n\leq\frac{k}{\alpha}-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}<n+1

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\exists k\geq 1:{n\alpha+\beta}\leq k<{(n+1)\alpha+\beta}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\exists k\geq 1:\left\lceil{n\alpha+\beta}\right\rceil=k\text{ and }\left\lceil{(n+1)\alpha+\beta}\right\rceil=k+1

\displaystyle\text{\small because }\alpha<1

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\left\lceil{(n+1)\alpha+\beta}\right\rceil-\left\lceil{n\alpha+\beta}\right\rceil=1

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow s_{n}=1.

The same argument gives that
n=\left\lceil\frac{k}{\alpha}-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\right\rceil

if and only if
\left\lfloor{n\alpha+\beta}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor{(n-1)\alpha+\beta}\right\rfloor=1
.
∎

4
Iterated function sequences

The fourth characterization of the BDS theorem describes the
sequence
A086377
as an iterative sequence

a(n)=\left\lfloor r_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\right\rfloor
where

r_{n+1}=f_{n}(r_{n})
for some rational function
f_{n}(x)
.
Surprisingly, the sequence starts at
r_{1}=\frac{4}{\pi}
.
It is proved in
[
6
]
that this is the unique
initial value that produces positive real values under
this iteration. In the final line of that
paper is the following remarkable conjecture
for
metallic means
, i.e., quadratic numbers
\alpha_{j}

that satisfy
\alpha_{j}^{2}=j\alpha_{j}+1
, see
[
18
]
.

Conjecture 18

(Bosma, Dekking, and Steiner)
.

Let
\alpha_{j}

be the
j
-th metallic mean and
let
r_{1}
be the unique positive real number such that
all
r_{n+1}=\frac{n^{2}}{r_{n}-jn+1}

are positive, where we suppress
j
in our notation
of the sequence
r_{n}
. The iterative sequence
\lfloor r_{n}\rfloor

is equal to the Beatty sequence

\left\lfloor\alpha_{j}.n-\frac{1+\alpha_{j}}{2\alpha_{j}-j}\right\rfloor.

These are not hiccup sequences. In fact, none of these sequences appear in the OEIS.
The first iterate
r_{1}
satisfies the continued fraction expansion

{r_{1}}=j-1+\cfrac{1^{2}}{2j-1+\cfrac{2^{2}}{3j-1+\cfrac{3^{2}}{4j-1+\cfrac{4^{2}}{5j-1+\cfrac{5^{2}}{\ddots}}}}}

For
j=2
this expansion is equal to
\frac{4}{\pi}
, see
[
9
, p.177]
,
which goes back to Euler’s continued fraction expansion of the arctangent. The partial
fractions of
r_{1}
are given by
\frac{n^{2}}{nj-1}
. The numbers
r_{n}

are the remainders of the continued fraction. The
n
-th remainder is the
sum of the
n
-th partial denominator plus the
n
-th partial numerator
divided by the next remainder

(11)

r_{n}=nj-1+\frac{n^{2}}{r_{n+1}}

The operation
r_{n}\mapsto r_{n+1}
is a continued fraction shift map.
It is shown in
[
6
]
that
r_{n}-n\alpha-\beta
is positive
and converges to zero with order
O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)
, where
we write
\alpha
for the
j
-th metallic mean, and
\beta=-\frac{1+\alpha}{2\alpha-j}
. These constants
\alpha

and
\beta
can be retrieved from Equation
11
if we
expand it as

n\alpha+\beta+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)=nj-1+\frac{n}{\alpha}-\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\alpha^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right).

Equating the terms of order
n
to zero gives that
\alpha
is the

j
-th metallic mean. By doing the same for the terms of order
1
,
we get that
\beta=-\frac{\alpha^{2}+\alpha}{\alpha^{2}+1}
, which is
equal to the expression for
\beta
in the conjecture.

According to Theorem
13
, hiccup sequences with
|y-z|=1

and
x\leq z
correspond to Beatty sequences, which are rounded up
or down
\alpha n+\beta
. The slope
\alpha
is the
z
-th
metallic mean. If we replace the partial denominator
nj-1
by

nz+v
for an indeterminate
v
, then we get the continued fraction
expansion

{s_{1}}=z+v+\cfrac{1^{2}}{2z+v+\cfrac{2^{2}}{3z+v+\cfrac{3^{2}}{4z+v+\cfrac{4^{2}}{5z+v+\cfrac{5^{2}}{\ddots}}}}}

and the shift map

s_{n}=nz+v+\frac{n^{2}}{s_{n+1}}.

If we assume that
s_{n}
converges to
\alpha n+\beta
with order
O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)
,
then
\alpha
is the
z
-th metallic mean and

\beta=\frac{v\alpha^{2}-\alpha}{\alpha^{2}+1}.

For instance, the lower Wythoff sequence
A000201
, which is the

(1,1,2,1)
-hiccup sequence, is equal to
\lfloor n\phi\rfloor
for
the golden mean
\phi
, which is the first metallic mean. If we take

z=1
and
v=\frac{1}{\phi}=\phi-1
, then a numerical computation gives
s_{1}=1.91041843973790\mathbf{4}
.
We conjecture that the rounded down sequence
\lfloor s_{n}\rfloor
starting from this

s_{1}
is equal to the lower Wythoff sequence.
