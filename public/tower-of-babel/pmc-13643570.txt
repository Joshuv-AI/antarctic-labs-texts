Valve-in-valve transcatheter mitral valve replacement (TMVR) has become an established therapy for degenerated surgical bioprosthetic mitral valves in high-risk or inoperable patients. Despite advances in imaging and procedural planning, left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction remains one of the most feared complications of TMVR and is associated with high procedural mortality. In a study by Yoon et al,1 patients with LVOT obstruction had significantly higher procedural mortality compared with those without (34.6% vs 2.4%; P < 0.001). A neo-LVOT area <200 mm2 has been identified as high risk for LVOT obstruction.1 Multiphase computed tomography (CT) protocols incorporating early-systolic, rather than end-systolic, neo-LVOT measurement have since been proposed to refine this risk prediction and reduce unnecessary screen failure.2

Several strategies have been developed to mitigate this risk, including alcohol septal ablation and anterior leaflet modification techniques such as tip-to-base laceration of the anterior mitral leaflet to prevent outflow obstruction (LAMPOON) and, more recently, septal scoring along the midline endocardium (SESAME).3,4 Balloon-assisted translocation of the mitral anterior leaflet (BATMAN) has emerged as a promising transseptal strategy for LVOT risk mitigation during TMVR; it is mechanistically analogous to base-to-tip LAMPOON, achieving leaflet displacement through balloon translocation rather than electrosurgical laceration, and similar to base-to-tip LAMPOON, is generally more procedurally demanding than the tip-to-base approach.5

Severe pulmonary hypertension adds additional risk because abrupt elimination of mitral regurgitation (MR) suddenly increases effective forward stroke volume and alters ventricular loading conditions. In patients with chronic pulmonary hypertension and right ventricular (RV) dysfunction, this transition may precipitate acute RV decompensation. We report a case of transseptal BATMAN-assisted valve-in-valve TMVR performed with planned intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) support to maintain hemodynamic stability in a patient with severe pulmonary hypertension and reduced cardiac output.

History of Presentation

A 78-year-old woman presented with progressively worsening dyspnea on exertion and recurrent hospitalizations for decompensated heart failure. Despite optimal medical therapy, her symptoms progressed to NYHA functional class III, prompting referral for structural heart evaluation. Physical examination was notable for an irregular heart rhythm, a holosystolic murmur best heard at the apex, elevated jugular venous pressure, and bilateral lower extremity edema.

Past Medical History

Past medical history was significant for paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, pulmonary hypertension, and prior surgical mitral valve replacement with a 25-mm Magna mitral bioprosthesis (Edwards Lifesciences).

Differential Diagnosis

In the setting of progressive exertional dyspnea and prior surgical mitral valve replacement, the differential diagnosis included structural bioprosthetic degeneration (stenosis, regurgitation, or both), paravalvular leak, and progression of intrinsic pulmonary arterial hypertension. Multimodality imaging with transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), and right heart catheterization was used to characterize the mechanism of valve failure and hemodynamic burden. Structural degeneration with mixed moderate-to-severe regurgitation and moderate stenosis was confirmed as the primary etiology, with secondary combined postcapillary pulmonary hypertension.

Investigations

Baseline TTE demonstrated preserved LV ejection fraction (60%-65%) with a small LV cavity and mild concentric wall thickening (Figure 1A). The interventricular septum was flattened in both systole and diastole, consistent with RV pressure and volume overload. The RV was severely dilated (basal dimension 4.4 cm) with reduced systolic function (tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion 1.4 cm). The degenerated bioprosthetic mitral valve demonstrated moderate-to-severe transvalvular regurgitation with pulmonary vein systolic flow reversal, and the mean transmitral gradient was 9 mm Hg. Additional findings included grade III diastolic dysfunction and severe functional tricuspid regurgitation.

TEE further characterized the prosthetic valve dysfunction, confirming severe prosthetic MR with an effective regurgitant orifice area of 0.55 cm2 and regurgitant volume of 57 mL. The regurgitant jet was eccentric and associated with pulmonary vein systolic flow reversal. Three-dimensional imaging demonstrated structural degeneration of the surgical bioprosthesis with restricted leaflet motion. Severe tricuspid regurgitation was also present, consistent with longstanding pulmonary hypertension and RV dilation.

Right heart catheterization demonstrated severe combined postcapillary pulmonary hypertension (right atrium 20 mm Hg, pulmonary artery 99/44 mm Hg, mean 65 mm Hg, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure 30 mm Hg) with reduced cardiac index (1.9 L/min/m2) (Figure 2A).

Preprocedural CT using a 23-mm SAPIEN 3 RESILIA (Edwards Lifesciences) valve simulation predicted a neo-LVOT area of 181 mm2, placing the patient at high risk for LVOT obstruction because of the small LV cavity and septal hypertrophy (Figures 1B and 1C). CT assessment of the aortomitral angle (52.8°) and mitral annular dimensions further characterized the unfavorable mitral-aortic geometry contributing to this risk (Figures 1D and 1E). Based on these findings, anterior leaflet modification using BATMAN was planned.

Management

Given the CT-predicted high risk for LVOT obstruction and the patient's severe pulmonary hypertension, RV dysfunction, and low cardiac index, the multidisciplinary heart team elected to perform transseptal BATMAN-assisted valve-in-valve TMVR with planned IABP support to facilitate hemodynamic stability during the anticipated hemodynamic transition.

The procedure was performed under general anesthesia with fluoroscopic and TEE guidance. Cerebral embolic protection was deployed at the start of the procedure. Femoral arterial access was obtained, and an IABP was inserted before valve implantation to provide temporary mechanical circulatory support. Transseptal puncture was performed using a Baylis radiofrequency system (Baylis Medical Technologies), and a steerable guide catheter was advanced into the left atrium (Figure 3A, Video 1). TEE confirmed wire localization at the anterior mitral leaflet immediately before leaflet traversal (Figure 4A). The anterior mitral leaflet was traversed using electrocautery delivered through a Confianza Pro (Asahi Intecc) wire connected to a Bovie (Aspen Surgical) electrosurgical generator and charged at 50 W, which was advanced across the leaflet into the LV under combined fluoroscopic and color Doppler TEE guidance (Figure 3B, Video 2, Figure 4B). Sequential balloon dilations were performed across the leaflet using 8 × 40 mm and 12 × 40 mm compliant balloons inflated to nominal pressure per the manufacturer's compliance chart, resulting in balloon-assisted displacement of the anterior leaflet away from the LVOT (Figure 3C, Videos 3 and 4). No additional postdilation of the transcatheter valve or bioprosthetic valve fracture was performed, as valve position and transmitral gradient were satisfactory immediately after deployment. Following successful leaflet modification, atrial septostomy was performed to facilitate passage of the valve delivery system (Figures 3D and 3E, Video 5).

A 23-mm SAPIEN 3 Resilia transcatheter valve was then advanced across the interatrial septum and positioned within the degenerated surgical bioprosthesis under combined fluoroscopic and TEE guidance (Figure 5A). The valve was deployed without rapid ventricular pacing, achieving stable positioning within the surgical valve frame (Figure 5B). Immediate hemodynamic assessment demonstrated complete elimination of MR with a mean transmitral gradient of 1 mm Hg and no LVOT obstruction (Figure 2B). The iatrogenic atrial septal defect was subsequently closed with a 30-mm GORE Septal Occluder (W. L. Gore & Associates, Inc) device (Figure 5C). After confirming hemodynamic stability, the IABP was removed at the conclusion of the procedure.

Outcome and Follow-Up

TTE performed on postoperative day 1 demonstrated a well-seated transcatheter mitral valve prosthesis with preserved LV systolic function (LV ejection fraction 62%), no residual MR, and no paravalvular leak. The mean transmitral gradient measured 7 mm Hg at a heart rate of 82 beats/min, and Doppler interrogation confirmed absence of LVOT obstruction. Intraprocedural TEE similarly confirmed a widely patent neo-LVOT space around the seated transcatheter valve on two-dimensional, color Doppler, and three-dimensional en face imaging (Figure 6). Pulmonary artery systolic pressure improved to approximately 65 to 70 mm Hg, whereas the RV remained dilated but hemodynamically stable. The patient was discharged in stable condition and scheduled for clinical follow-up with serial TTE evaluation. Discussion

LVOT obstruction remains a major limitation of TMVR and is strongly associated with procedural mortality. Preprocedural CT is essential for identifying high-risk anatomy and guiding selection of preventive strategies.1 In this case, CT predicted a neo-LVOT area of 181 mm2, placing the patient at high risk.

Several strategies have been developed to mitigate this risk, including alcohol septal ablation, tip-to-base LAMPOON, and, more recently, SESAME.3,4 BATMAN has emerged as an alternative that is mechanistically analogous to base-to-tip LAMPOON, involving traversal and balloon dilation—rather than electrosurgical laceration—of the anterior leaflet to effectively displace leaflet tissue away from the LVOT after valve deployment. In practice, base-to-tip leaflet modification, whether by BATMAN or base-to-tip LAMPOON, is generally more procedurally demanding than the tip-to-base approach, though we believe this complexity is often overstated and is manageable with appropriate operator preparation. A recently published international multicenter registry of 83 patients undergoing transseptal BATMAN across 22 centers reported technical success in 98.8% of cases and freedom from LVOT obstruction or procedural death in 95.1%, with preemptive mechanical circulatory support used in 28.9% of patients.5 In this case, BATMAN allowed successful TMVR in a patient with a small LV cavity; postimplant imaging confirmed preserved LVOT patency despite unfavorable anatomy.

However, this case also highlights an additional challenge beyond LVOT obstruction. Patients with severe pulmonary hypertension and RV dysfunction may have limited ability to tolerate the abrupt hemodynamic transition following elimination of chronic severe MR. Restoration of forward flow increases effective LV afterload and alters RV–pulmonary artery coupling, potentially predisposing to hemodynamic instability in patients with limited cardiopulmonary reserve.6

Prophylactic IABP support was used to reduce LV afterload and facilitate hemodynamic stability during this transition. In contrast to venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, IABP does not increase systemic afterload, making it a favorable adjunct in this setting. The procedure was completed without hypotension, RV failure, or circulatory collapse, although the impact of this strategy on clinical outcomes remains unknown.

Although this patient already had severe MR preoperatively, the physiologic rationale for prophylactic IABP was not to stabilize the BATMAN leaflet modification steps themselves, but rather to buffer the abrupt loading transition anticipated at the moment of complete MR elimination after valve deployment. In chronic severe MR, the LV ejects against a low-impedance regurgitant pathway into the left atrium, which effectively lowers forward afterload; abrupt closure of this pathway after successful TMVR acutely increases effective LV afterload and can transiently reduce forward cardiac output. In this patient, with severe combined postcapillary pulmonary hypertension, a cardiac index of 1.9 L/min/m2, severely reduced RV function (tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion 1.4 cm), and severe functional tricuspid regurgitation, this loading transition carried meaningful risk of acute RV decompensation because of limited RV–pulmonary artery coupling reserve. IABP counterpulsation reduces effective LV afterload and augments coronary perfusion without the systemic afterload increase associated with more aggressive mechanical circulatory support, making it a rational adjunct in this specific physiologic context, distinct from its potential role during the leaflet modification steps of BATMAN itself.

The decision to close the iatrogenic atrial septal defect, rather than allow spontaneous closure, was based on 3 converging considerations. First, in the setting of severe pulmonary hypertension and an elevated right atrial pressure, the transseptal pressure gradient could transiently reverse during periods of RV stress, risking right-to-left shunting with systemic hypoxemia and paradoxical embolization. Second, the severely dilated and dysfunctional RV had limited reserve to tolerate an additional left-to-right shunt burden in the early postprocedural period. Third, the patient's severe functional tricuspid regurgitation reflected long-standing RV pressure and volume overload that an unrepaired atrial septal defect could perpetuate through additional right-heart volume loading. These considerations favored elective closure with a 30-mm GORE Septal Occluder over expectant management.

Although mechanical circulatory support has been used during complex structural heart procedures, reports describing planned IABP use during BATMAN-assisted TMVR in the setting of severe pulmonary hypertension remain limited. To our knowledge, this represents one of the first detailed reports integrating planned hemodynamic support with BATMAN-assisted valve-in-valve TMVR in a patient with severe pulmonary hypertension and reduced cardiac index. Patients with markedly elevated pulmonary pressures, reduced cardiac index, or evidence of RV dysfunction may benefit from a preemptive approach that includes both LVOT risk mitigation and temporary mechanical circulatory support. Further experience and systematic study will be required to determine whether this strategy improves procedural stability and clinical outcomes in this high-risk population.

Conclusions

BATMAN-assisted valve-in-valve TMVR with planned IABP support may facilitate safe treatment of selected high-risk patients with severe pulmonary hypertension, RV dysfunction, and predicted LVOT obstruction.
