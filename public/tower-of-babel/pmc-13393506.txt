Population‐specific validation of fetal weight formulas: Evidence from a multicenter near‐delivery cohort
Authors: Sumito Nagasaki, Rio Suzuki, Fumio Sugo, Mayu Tachihara, Tsubasa Mori, Sena Shimizu, Junya Sakuma, Ayumu Ito, Mayumi Takano, Masahiko Nakata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAccurate ultrasound estimated fetal weight (EFW) supports key perinatal decisions. This study aimed to compare the accuracy of a population‐specific formula with that of a widely used global model in pregnancies examined near delivery.Material and MethodsThis was a secondary analysis of a prospective Japanese cohort study to develop a new fetal ultrasound biometry reference chart. Fetal ultrasound measurements were obtained by Japan Society of Ultrasonics in Medicine‐certified sonographers or under their supervision and submitted to the coordinating center, where EFW was calculated using the Shinozuka and Hadlock‐3 formulas. Analyses were restricted to cases with EFW available from both formulas. The primary analysis included examinations within 7 days before delivery; a sensitivity analysis restricted the interval to ≤3 days. A subgroup analysis was performed in fetuses classified as small for gestational age (SGA).ResultsIn the primary analysis (n = 310), the median signed error was −75.8 g for Shinozuka and −223.3 g for Hadlock‐3 (Wilcoxon p < 0.001). The median absolute percentage signed error was 4.81% for Shinozuka and 7.93% for Hadlock‐3 (Wilcoxon p < 0.001). The proportion within ±10% of BW was 83.9% with Shinozuka versus 61.6% with Hadlock‐3 (McNemar p < 0.001). In the sensitivity analysis (n = 176), similar findings were observed. These findings support the use of locally validated, population‐specific EFW formulas when available, particularly for clinical decision‐making near delivery.

Key message

Fetal weight estimation accuracy may vary across populations, highlighting the importance of validating and selecting population‐specific formulas rather than relying solely on a single global standard.

INTRODUCTION

Accurate ultrasound estimated fetal weight (EFW) supports key perinatal decisions, including the diagnosis of small for gestational age (SGA) and fetal growth restriction, for example, as defined by the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics, 1 as well as patient counseling and planning the timing and mode of delivery. To assess accuracy, EFW is typically compared with birth weight (BW) measured at delivery. Numerous formulas for EFW have been proposed, but their overall accuracy is moderate and varies across populations and birth‐weight ranges. 2 , 3 Although the Hadlock equations, particularly Hadlock‐3, 4 are widely used as global standards, the transferability of a single formula across populations is limited by differences in fetal anthropometry and body‐size distribution. In Japan, the Shinozuka formula, 5 developed and calibrated using Japanese neonatal and fetal biometric data, has been used for decades in clinical practice. Several studies have examined the performance of the Hadlock formulas, 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 but to our knowledge, no prospective study has directly compared the Shinozuka formula and Hadlock formulas before delivery. Therefore, we aimed to evaluate whether a population‐specific formula provides improved accuracy compared with a widely used global model in a real‐world clinical setting near delivery.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Data

This was a secondary analysis of a prospective, multicenter Japanese cohort study 10 to develop a new reference fetal biometry chart. In brief, eligible participants were singleton pregnancies with a confirmed estimated due date in the first trimester and recorded birth weight. Fetal ultrasound biometry parameters (biparietal diameter [BPD], head circumference [HC], abdominal circumference [AC], and femur length [FL]) were measured at each participating site by sonographers certified by the Japan Society of Ultrasonics in Medicine or under their supervision and were submitted to the coordinating center, where EFW was calculated using the Shinozuka formula, 5 which is mainly used in Japan (EFW = 1.07 × BPD3 + 0.3 × AC2 × FL) and Hadlock‐3 formula, 4 which is commonly used in other countries (log10 (EFW) = 1.326–0.00326 × AC × FL + 0.0107 × HC + 0.0438 × AC + 0.158 × FL). BPD was measured using a leading‐edge technique (outer–inner skull tables). HC was measured directly using an ellipse trace placed around the outside of the skull bone echoes. The Shinozuka formula is a volume‐based model calibrated on Japanese neonatal and fetal data, whereas Hadlock‐3 is a US‐derived regression using HC, AC, and FL. Analyses were restricted to cases in which EFW could be calculated using both formulas (paired cases). The interval from ultrasonography to delivery was calculated in days. The primary analysis included ultrasonography performed within 7 days before delivery. As a sensitivity analysis, we repeated the main comparisons while restricting the ultrasonography‐to‐delivery interval within 3 days. We additionally performed an exploratory subgroup analysis in fetuses classified as SGA using a clinically oriented definition (AC <10th percentile and/or EFW <10th percentile). The AC 10th‐percentile threshold was determined using gestational age–specific reference values provided in the supplementary table of the original cohort study. 10 Because EFW depends on the formula used, the EFW criterion was considered met if EFW was <10th percentile by either formula (Shinozuka or Hadlock‐3), based on the formula‐specific 10th‐percentile reference values reported in Table 2 of the original study. 10 The resulting SGA cohort was then used for paired comparisons between formulas. Analyses by birth‐weight strata (e.g., <2500 g, 2500–3999 g, ≥4000 g) were not performed because births ≥4000 g are uncommon in Japan, resulting in unstable strata and imprecise estimates.

Statistical analysis

The signed error in grams was defined as the difference between EFW and BW, calculated as EFW − BW. Positive values indicate overestimation by the formula, whereas negative values indicate underestimation. The percentage signed error was calculated as EFW−BWBW×100. The absolute percentage error was calculated as the absolute value of the percentage signed error. As a clinically intuitive metric, we additionally reported the proportion of EFW within ±10% of birth weight EFW−BWBW×100≤10%. Euclidean distance was calculated as bias2+SD2, where bias and SD are the mean and standard deviation of percentage signed error across cases. Because EFW calculated by the two formulas was obtained for the same cases, data were compared using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests applied to within‐case differences. For a proportion rate within ±10%, we used McNemar's test. Two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data were analyzed using Bell Curve for Excel (Social Survey Research Information Co., Ltd.).

Primary analysis (within 7 days)

In the primary analysis, the median signed error was significantly closer to zero with the Shinozuka formula than with Hadlock‐3 (−75.8 g vs. −223.3 g; Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, p<0.001) (Table 2, Figure 2A). The median percentage signed error was significantly closer to zero with the Shinozuka formula than with Hadlock‐3 (−2.78% vs. −7.47%; Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, p<0.001) (Figure 2B). As a clinically intuitive metric, the proportion of EFW within ±10% of birth weight was 83.9% for Shinozuka and 61.6% for Hadlock‐3 (McNemar p < 0.001; Table 2). Scatter plots of EFW against birth weight are shown in Figure 3A,B, and Bland–Altman plots of percentage error against BW are shown in Figure 4A,B. Sensitivity analysis (within 3 days)

In the sensitivity analysis, the median signed error was significantly closer to zero with the Shinozuka formula than with Hadlock‐3 (−48.0 g vs. −174.6 g; Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, p<0.001) (Table 2, Figure 5). The median percentage signed error was significantly closer to zero with the Shinozuka formula than with Hadlock‐3 (−1.92% vs. −6.51%; Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, p<0.001). As a clinically intuitive metric, the proportion of EFW estimates within ±10% of birth weight was 86.4% for Shinozuka and 66.5% for Hadlock‐3 (McNemar p < 0.001; Table 2). Subgroup analysis

In the SGA subgroup (ultrasound‐to‐delivery interval within 7 days; n = 40), shown in Table 2 and Figure S1, the median signed error was −53.9 g for the Shinozuka formula and −208.0 g for the Hadlock‐3 formula (Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, p<0.001). The median percentage signed error was −2.16% for Shinozuka and −7.97% for Hadlock‐3 (Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, p<0.001). As a clinically intuitive metric, the proportion of EFW within ±10% of birth weight was 80.0% for Shinozuka and 62.5% for Hadlock‐3 (McNemar p = 0.092). DISCUSSION

The main finding of this study is that, in Japanese pregnancies examined near delivery, the population‐specific Shinozuka formula estimated fetal weight more accurately than the widely used Hadlock‐3 formula. Compared with Hadlock‐3, Shinozuka showed EFW closer to BW and a higher proportion within ±10% of BW, whereas Hadlock‐3 showed greater systematic underestimation.

Global practice often defaults to the Hadlock formulas; however, prior reports show that formula performance varies by population and body‐weight range. 3 In a large comparative study 11 of 23 EFW models, Barel et al. also emphasized that model performance should be evaluated in the target population, supporting the need for population‐specific validation. Hammami et al. 12 also compared multiple EFW formulas and reported that Hadlock‐3 performed best with low errors in their study, which differs from our findings in a Japanese near‐delivery cohort in which Shinozuka showed smaller bias and higher accuracy than Hadlock‐3. Several factors may explain this discrepancy. Although we used similar performance metrics (e.g., signed error, absolute percentage error, and Euclidean distance), our results differed from those of Hammami et al., 12 likely due to differences in the study population, case mix, and the ultrasound‐to‐delivery interval. First, the study populations differed in anthropometry and birth‐weight distribution; Hammami et al. included a cohort with a very small proportion of East Asian participants (1.2%), which may influence fetal body‐size distribution and model calibration. Second, Hammami et al. restricted their primary dataset to births within 2 days of ultrasonography, whereas our primary analysis included examinations within 7 days of delivery (and ≤3 days in sensitivity analyses), which may contribute to differences in observed accuracy.

Two features likely underlie the better fit. First, the Shinozuka formula was developed using Japanese fetal and neonatal data, reflecting population‐specific body size and composition. When the underlying anthropometric distribution differs, model coefficients may be suboptimal, leading to systematic over‐ or underestimation. Second, as shown in our cohort study, 10 compared with other Asian countries, Japanese fetal biometric distributions may differ from those underpinning widely used international formulas. The two formulas are also not parameter‐identical: Shinozuka uses BPD, AC, and FL in a volume‐based formulation, whereas Hadlock‐3 is a regression model based on HC, AC, and FL.

This issue is clinically relevant because systematic underestimation may increase the likelihood of classifying fetuses as small for gestational age or suspected fetal growth restriction when EFW‐based thresholds are applied, potentially influencing surveillance and delivery planning.

A key strength of the present study is that it extends our 2025 Scientific Reports publication by addressing a distinct clinical question: near‐delivery performance of fetal weight formulas in paired multicenter data. Specifically, this study focuses on examinations within 7 days before delivery (with a ≤3‐day sensitivity analysis) and directly compares the Shinozuka and Hadlock‐3 formulas under clinically relevant conditions close to delivery. We used prospectively collected multicenter data with ultrasound performed close to delivery and birth weight measured at delivery. Measurements were obtained by certified sonographers or under their supervision, and EFW was calculated centrally. Restricting analyses to paired cases enabled within‐case comparisons, and we reported complementary accuracy metrics, including median absolute percentage error. This study has several limitations. This was a secondary analysis, and residual heterogeneity across sites may remain. Our analyses were limited to examinations within one week before delivery (≤ 3 days in sensitivity analyses) and may not generalize to earlier gestational windows. The exploratory SGA definition included a formula‐derived EFW component; because EFW is the output of the formulas being compared, subgrouping may introduce selection (collider) bias despite inclusion of AC‐based criteria, and subgroup findings should be interpreted as exploratory.

CONCLUSION

In this multicenter cohort of Japanese pregnancies examined near delivery, the population‐specific formula provided EFW closer to BW and less systematic underestimation than the widely used Hadlock‐3 formula. These findings were consistent in the sensitivity analysis and in an exploratory SGA subgroup using clinically oriented criteria, supporting validation and selection of fetal weight formulas tailored to specific populations rather than exclusive reliance on a single global standard.
