Optimizing Cataract Surgery Workflows Across a Multi-Satellite Tertiary Care Center
Authors: Ahmed El Ashry, Mahmoud El Ashry, Sujin Kang, Sotiria Palioura, Allister Gibbons, Jose Miguel Mora Correa, Zubair A Ansari, Despoina Theotoka, Sonia H Yoo, Tamarra R Biekman, Stephen G Schwartz, Guillermo Amescua, Jaime D Martinez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCataract surgery requires precise coordination of multimodal processes delivered across decentralized multi-site tertiary healthcare systems. Investigating the logistical orchestration required for geographically distributed healthcare networks, this review describes an operational framework from a multi-satellite tertiary academic referral center operating a hub-and-spoke model across South Florida handling approximately 21,000 annual surgeries. To address multi-site care fragmentation, the institute introduced the “digital spine” framework, which integrates cloud-based diagnostic platforms, coordinated scheduling, and centralized surgical planning. This cloud-integrated system asynchronously bridges diagnostic hubs up to 116 miles apart, sending centralized surgical plans directly to operating room microscopes and feeding post-operative refractive data back into predictive algorithms. Within this network, cataract surgery accounts for approximately 8000 cases annually. Providers, imaging technicians, surgical coordinators, and nurses seamlessly establish collaborative care pathways. This system plays a critical role in maintaining efficiency, while a tiered referral protocol routes high-risk and complex cases to the central hub where multidisciplinary support is available. The present review fills the literature gap by providing a scalable blueprint for multi-site ophthalmic networks seeking to integrate decentralized diagnostics with centralized surgical execution. However, further quantitative studies are needed to evaluate clinical and cost-effectiveness outcomes.

Introduction

Cataract surgery workflow comprises preoperative imaging with appropriate diagnosis, surgical intervention, and postoperative follow-up. Across these phases, clinical decision-making needs to be coordinated with logistical processes, including patient flow, staff allocation, diagnostic testing, and surgical resource management.1 However, these logistical challenges become increasingly demanding in tertiary centers where care is distributed across multiple facilities in different regions.

Operational challenges include transportation and coordination of patients, supply chain management of intraocular lenses (IOLs) and surgical equipment, and demand forecasting to avoid inventory shortage or waste.2,3 Hence, efficient patient flow logistics are essential to ensure timely and coordinated transitions between diagnostic testing, surgical planning, and operative care.4

Global demand for IOLs continues to grow, driven by an aging population, increasing cataract prevalence, and patient expectations for spectacle independence after surgery.5,6 Achieving optimal refractive outcomes in these cases requires highly accurate preoperative biometry and imaging and careful surgical planning.7 The increasing use of artificial intelligence (AI) in analyzing biometric datasets further underscores the importance of integrated digital workflows and Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) capable of handling large volumes of diagnostic data.8

However, when imaging, surgical planning, and surgery occur at separate facilities in different regions, fragmentation of care is inevitable. Fragmented systems may limit communications, medical record access, staff coordination, and appropriate information carriers between facilities.9,10 These issues can increase the risk of errors, cause delays in surgical planning, and compromise both efficiency and patient outcomes.11

Despite technological advances, prior literature has not reported how cataract workflows can be operationalized across geographically distributed systems. Therefore, the primary objectives were to introduce current digital technologies and logistics implemented in supporting multi-site cataract care and present a descriptive institutional workflow report detailing the “digital spine” operational framework utilized across a high-volume academic eye center. We coin this term to describe the integrated nature of the technologies and infrastructure used to optimize care delivery across the network.

Technological Foundations of Multi-Satellite Cataract Workflows

Several technological developments have supported the expansion of cataract surgery workflows across multiple facilities. The institute utilizes a specialized digital infrastructure centered on the ZEISS VERACITY Surgery Planner and FORUM (Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Jena, Germany), which functions as the primary “digital spine” for IOL planning.12 While the broader institutional picture archiving and communication system (PACS), such as OptosAdvance (Nikon Healthcare, Tokyo, Japan), provides a comprehensive diagnostic archive for multi-specialty image review, the cataract-specific cloud-based platforms allow biometric data and topographic maps to be pulled directly into a secure, remote planning environment.13 To ensure comprehensive surgical planning, additional essential preoperative examinations, including manifest refractions, anterior and posterior segment optical coherence topography (OCT), endothelial cell density, and/or ocular surface/dry eye evaluations, are integrated into the digital ecosystem. Diagnostic outputs that do not automatically stream via direct cloud Application Processing Interface (API) protocols, such as specialized ocular surface parameters or standalone specular microscopy, are ingested as DICOM objects or discrete data fields into the central PACS/Electronic Health Record (EHR) framework. These data points are then linked to the patient’s centralized electronic chart, ensuring that surgeons have complete access to the full diagnostic profile during remote planning sessions.

This multi-modal integration enables surgeons to finalize complex IOL calculations asynchronously from any network location and “push” surgical plans directly to microscope-integrated guidance systems, such as CALLISTO eye (Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Jena, Germany), in the operating room (OR). By decoupling the planning process from the physical location of the patient’s chart or diagnostic equipment, these platforms eliminate manual data transcription, account for all pre-existing ocular surface or macula pathologies and ensure refractive precision across geographically distributed surgical sites.

Secure implementation of these platforms requires strong authentication protocols, encrypted data transmission, and compliance with healthcare privacy regulations such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act.14 When effectively implemented, these digital infrastructures allow surgeons to perform remote surgical planning using diagnostic data collected at satellite clinics.

Advanced digital surgical planning tools are also increasingly integrated into cataract workflows. These systems use patient-specific imaging data to guide IOL selection and surgical planning.15,16 Emerging technologies such as digital twins allow the creation of virtual three-dimensional models of ocular anatomy, enabling surgeons to simulate surgical approaches and optimize refractive outcomes before entering the OR.17 In principle, within a multi-site network such digital replicas could function as “portable patients,” allowing a surgeon at the central hub to simulate the procedure using digitally reconstructed anatomical models captured at a distant satellite. Digital-twin simulation is an emerging capability rather than a routine part of the current workflow described in this report and is included here to indicate a plausible future direction.

Intraoperative imaging technologies further support these workflows. Microscope-integrated guidance systems allow surgeons to visualize high-resolution cross-sectional and volumetric images during surgery.18 For toric IOL implantation, digital alignment systems guide precise rotational positioning of the lens, improving refractive accuracy.19

Despite these advances, the integration of these technologies into multi-site delivery introduces additional layers of complexity. While much of the existing literature focuses on enhancing intraoperative precision or applying lean methodologies within individual surgical centers, emphasizing efficiency gains such as reduced wait times and improved OR throughput, it does not fully address how these digital tools function across distributed networks.20–24

Furthermore, prior studies on cataract surgery frequently examine refractive outcomes and biometric precision rather than the logistical systems required to deliver these services at scale.25–28 Hub-and-spoke models have been explored primarily in the context of high-volume cataract surgery rather than personalized care pathways.29,30

Similarly, supply chain research related to IOLs typically focuses on inventory management within single facilities or humanitarian outreach programs, leaving limited guidance on managing high-value IOL inventories across decentralized networks.31–33

Institutional Setting and Workflow Context

The Bascom Palmer Eye Institute (BPEI) operates one of the largest academic ophthalmology networks in the United States, using a hub-and-spoke model that integrates a central clinical hub in Miami, Florida with multiple regional satellite facilities. Across its entire South Florida network, the institute handles approximately 300,000 patient visits and 21,000 total surgical procedures annually.34 Network-wide, cataract surgery represents a substantial portion of the surgical volume, with approximately 8000 cataract cases annually.35 This institutional scale, combined with the clinical density of individual satellites, necessitates highly coordinated operational systems, contrasted with traditional single-site models in Table 1. Daily surgical volumes vary according to surgeon availability and OR schedules, with cataract surgery lists typically vary widely per surgeon per OR day. Satellite facilities serve as critical entry points and specialized surgical outlets within the network, with regional centers such as Naples, Palm Beach Gardens, and SoLé Mia functioning as gateway facilities capable of managing both routine and cataract surgeries locally. Care delivery follows a formal risk stratification protocol based on American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) and Johns Hopkins classifications. Patients categorized as ASA Class III or higher, those requiring general anesthesia or invasive monitoring, including pediatric patients and individuals with cognitive or developmental disabilities unable to tolerate local anesthesia, or those needing multi-specialty support are routed to the Miami central hub. This ensures access to hospital-level medical resources unavailable at satellite ambulatory centers. Consequently, a notable subset of network-wide surgical referrals is redirected to the main hub for advanced medical or complex surgical care.

Surgical complexity and ocular risk are evaluated in parallel during the initial satellite evaluation. Specific high-risk ocular parameters, such as severe pseudoexfoliation syndrome with zonular instability, prior ocular trauma, or dense cataracts, trigger a structured subspecialty routing pathway. These are assessed on a case-by-case basis according to physician preference, and providers can travel across sites to ensure continuity of care and optimize patient care. While satellite facilities routinely manage complex cases, high-risk combined cases requiring multispecialty intervention are assigned to the central hub. This dual risk-stratification mechanism, balancing systemic medical acuity with ocular surgical complexity, ensures patient safety while maintaining operational efficiency across the distributed network.

The system maintains further flexibility based on provider and patient preference; if a specific satellite provider does not operate at the Miami hub, the patient is referred to an available provider through a handoff facilitated by either virtual consultations or on-site evaluations, thereby ensuring continuity of care regardless of the final surgical location.

Diagnostic imaging is distributed across all sites to support the high clinical demand of the network. The institute performs tens of thousands of imaging studies annually across its distributed facilities. This extensive imaging infrastructure allows patients to complete essential preoperative diagnostics (eg, biometry and corneal topography) closer to home, while cloud-based integration enables surgeons to access high-fidelity data for remote surgical planning. By decentralizing data capture but centralizing the planning architecture, the network maintains high patient throughput without compromising the data density required.

The workflow relies on a multidisciplinary team structure to manage the high volume and geographic spread of the network (Figure 2). The diagnostic and imaging workflow is supported by a large clinical contingent of ophthalmic technicians and imaging specialists distributed across satellite sites. Dedicated on-site surgical coordinators at each facility manage the localized scheduling of perioperative appointments and the booking of surgical cases. Preoperative medical assessment and anesthesia clearance are centralized through the UHealth Preoperative Assessment Center, which utilizes a stratified approach based on patient location: individuals may undergo either a phone screening or an on-site evaluation in Miami. This flexibility ensures that clearance is tailored to both the patient’s medical necessity and their proximity to the hub. Finally, a central surgical scheduling team manages the final OR case sequencing to ensure maximum throughput across all surgical facilities. This distributed staffing structure allows the institute to coordinate high surgical volumes while maintaining consistent care standards across geographically separated facilities. Integrating Imaging and Surgical Planning Workflows

Preoperative imaging and biometry form the foundation of cataract surgical planning. At BPEI, diagnostic data from optical biometers and imaging systems are integrated into cloud-based platforms such as ZEISS VERACITY and FORUM, enabling the secure transfer of imaging data to surgical planning systems.36 Appointments are strategically flagged as “New Cataract Evaluations” to ensure that, whenever possible, preoperative imaging is completed during the initial visit.

A standardized diagnostic battery is performed by trained ophthalmic medical technologists, certified ophthalmic assistants (COA), and certified ophthalmic technicians (COT). This routine battery includes optical biometry (IOLMaster 700, Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Jena, Germany), corneal topography and tomography (Pentacam, Oculus Optikgeräte GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany), anterior and posterior segment optical coherence tomography (OCT), specular microscopy for endothelial cell density, objective and subjective refractometry, and structured ocular surface evaluation. All IOL power calculations and formula selections (eg, Barrett Universal II, Hill-RBF, Kane) are executed and validated directly by the attending cataract surgeon within the cloud planning environment; ophthalmic technicians and surgical coordinators do not independently finalize or validate refractive formulas.

Quality control during single-visit diagnostic workflows is maintained through multi-tiered verification protocols. Technologists review image acquisition quality metrics (such as signal strength, fixation compliance, and tear-film integrity artifacts) at the time of capture; scans failing manufacturer validity thresholds are immediately repeated or flagged. Subsequently, the attending surgeon independently verifies diagnostic agreement across modalities (eg, cross-referencing biometry keratometry against corneal topography/tomography) before finalizing the surgical plan.

However, if optimal data capture is precluded by factors such as active contact lens wear, ocular surface irregularities, or pupillary dilation, the patient is scheduled for a dedicated “testing-only” hybrid visit, following ocular surface optimization or contact lens holiday. To further streamline the transition to surgery, many surgeons utilize telehealth consultations to review these subsequent test results with the patient, ensuring that all refractive targets are finalized remotely before the day of surgery. This cloud integration allows surgeons to access patient data remotely when planning IOL selection. Studies evaluating cloud-based surgical planning systems have reported time savings of up to 4.9 minutes per patient in cataract surgery workflows using these techniques.15 While a 5-minute reduction per patient may appear modest in isolation, its cumulative clinical impact across a high-volume network is substantial. Clinically, this efficiency eliminates manual data transcription between paper charts, biometers, and OR microscopes, thereby drastically reducing transcription errors, preventing wrong-IOL implant events, and improving overall OR list predictability without compromising clinical thoroughness.

Historically, transferring preoperative data into OR systems represented a major logistical barrier. Earlier workflows required exporting biometric calculations as portable document format (PDF) files or transferring data via universal serial bus (USB) devices, creating administrative inefficiencies and increasing the risk of transcription errors.

Cloud-based surgical planning platforms eliminate many of these issues by automatically transferring biometric data and surgical plans directly to OR systems. Microscope-integrated guidance systems can then display surgical overlays such as toric alignment guidance without manual data entry.

In single-center studies reported by other groups, digital cataract workflows have been associated with reductions in preoperative measurement time of approximately 25% and in overall surgical time of more than 20%; these figures derive from those external reports rather than from measurements within our own network.1 In our experience, eliminating manual transcription steps is one of the most impactful workflow improvements, significantly reducing both administrative burden and risk of error.

To accommodate scheduling variability across multiple facilities, imaging and surgical planning typically occur within approximately three to four weeks prior to surgery, aligned with surgeons’ block operating schedules.

Following the virtual sign-off of the surgical plan, the workflow transitions to execution through a specialized administrative layer. Physician-assigned associate assistants organize the finalized surgical schedule, ensuring that all selected IOLs are verified against this planning data. This list is then electronically transmitted to the central warehouse and surgical nursing teams, triggering the pull of consignment stock or the acquisition of special-order lenses to ensure all hardware is on-site at the specific surgical location 24–48 hours prior to the procedure.

Workflow Adaptation for Complex and High-Risk Cataract Cases

Beyond routine cases, surgical complexity functions as a distinct determinant of patient flow within the network. In the standard pathway, a patient evaluated at a satellite facility may complete both diagnostics and surgery within that regional ambulatory setting.

High-risk ocular characteristics, including phacodonesis, pseudoexfoliation, prior trauma, dense cataracts, or previous vitrectomy, significantly increase PCR risk.37–42 Cases with these risk factors are triaged to the central hub, where vitreoretinal specialists are immediately available for complex co-management.43–46

The reliability of this support differs markedly between the hub and the ambulatory satellites. To manage this, the network follows a tiered scheduling protocol for high-risk cataract cases, applied at the discretion of the operating surgeon rather than as an automatic rule. Where feasible, such a case is deliberately scheduled at a satellite on a surgical day when a vitreoretinal surgeon is independently operating in the adjacent room, so that immediate backup is assured. When this co-scheduling cannot be arranged, or where the surgeon prefers the resources of the central hub, the case is instead referred to the Miami hub.

The network’s digital infrastructure is integral to managing this redirected flow. When a complex case is identified at a satellite, the capacity to transfer biometric and imaging data through this shared infrastructure, combined with telehealth consultation, allows the receiving hub surgeon to evaluate the patient remotely, relay the nature of the complexity to the surgical and anesthesia teams, and finalize the operative plan. Remote consultation is also particularly valuable for patients for whom travels to Miami is burdensome, as it enables consolidation of counseling, planning, and surgery so that the patient need attend the hub only once.

Taken together, the management of complex and high-risk cataract cases reinforces the role of the multi-site network as a safety-net tertiary referral system for a clinically heterogeneous population. Rather than functioning solely as a volume-oriented logistics solution, the workflow is structured to identify elevated surgical risk early, redirect the surgical case to an appropriate facility, as needed.

Lens Inventory and Supply Chain Management

Cataract surgery introduces significant supply chain complexity due to the wide variety of available IOL models, designs, and dioptric powers. Managing these inventories across multiple facilities requires coordination between surgeons, materials management and supply chain teams, and industry partners.

Surgeons typically identify the desired IOLs within one week of the scheduled surgery date based on biometric calculations and surgical planning via the cloud planning platform. When an IOL is ordered or reserved, requests are communicated to manufacturer order systems using secure, automated electronic data interchange (EDI) integrated within the facility’s materials management and electronic medical record systems, supplemented by secure, encrypted vendor web portals. Patient confidentiality is strictly maintained throughout the procurement process; orders transmitted to third-party manufacturer representatives utilize unique internal surgical system identifiers or anonymized medical record control numbers rather than direct protected health information (PHI) such as patient names or dates of birth.

In instances where non-standard lens powers, custom toric models, or advanced platforms are required beyond consignment stock, items are special ordered for expedited delivery. If the required lens is available through on-site consignment inventory, the lens is reserved and prepared for the patient’s procedure. The attending surgeon evaluates possible lens options using the cloud planning software to ensure its availability, and the lens is verified on site.

Postoperative Care Coordination Across Sites

Postoperative care pathways must also be adapted to the distributed nature of tertiary ophthalmic networks. While traditional cataract surgery protocols often involve next-day postoperative visits, same-day postoperative assessments performed several hours after surgery are reported to be safe and preferred by many patients.47 Same-day follow-up may be particularly beneficial for patients traveling long distances to surgical centers, reducing travel burden and improving convenience.

The mobility of the medical staff allows many surgeons to maintain their own postoperative follow-up schedules across multiple satellite facilities; since providers rotate through different regional clinics, patients can be seen at the satellite most geographically convenient for them. This approach ensures that high standards of tertiary surgical care are maintained without imposing a centralized travel burden on the patient.

Provider-led postoperative care pathways at different satellites can safely reduce on-site clinical encounters at tertiary surgical facilities while maintaining high standards of patient care.48,49 This approach allows academic tertiary centers to preserve specialist clinical capacity and OR resources for complex cases requiring advanced ophthalmic surgical expertise, rather than utilizing high-acuity space for routine postoperative monitoring.

Discussion

Delivering cataract surgery across multi-site tertiary healthcare systems requires coordinated integration of diagnostic imaging, surgical planning, supply chain management, OR scheduling, and postoperative care. The operational framework implemented at the BPEI describes how digital infrastructure, distributed diagnostic networks, and collaborative care models were organized with the aim of supporting high surgical volumes while maintaining personalized cataract services. The effectiveness of this arrangement is not evaluated against institutional outcome data in this report.

While integrated systems achieve high efficiency through standardized protocols and the utilization of IOL options within a closed-loop payer-provider framework,50 the BPEI ecosystem faces a distinct academic-tertiary challenge. BPEI serves as a safety-net tertiary referral center for a region spanning up to 126 miles. This necessitates a workflow that accommodates all-comers, including highly complex surgical cases with significant ocular comorbidities, such as advanced glaucoma, retinal disease, or zonular instability, that are often referred out of community settings. BPEI must reconcile high surgical volumes with the intensive, personalized planning required for eyes that have no further point of referral. Hence, tertiary networks like BPEI must pioneer “digital spine” architectures to maintain subspecialty precision and concierge-level outcomes for a clinically heterogeneous and geographically dispersed population. Compared with the single-center digital and lean cataract workflows described above,20–24 the framework described in this review is distinguished by its asynchronous, closed-loop coordination of diagnostics, planning, and postoperative feedback across geographically separated sites. Its added value lies specifically in decoupling the site of diagnostic capture from the site of surgical planning and execution across a network spanning up to 126 miles. This is a coordination problem the single-center models above do not address. This multi-site orchestration, rather than any individual technology, is its principal point of novelty, and its incremental benefit over single-center models remains to be demonstrated empirically.

This operational framework highlights the importance of understanding cataract surgery workflows not only as clinical pathways but also as complex logistical systems requiring coordination across multiple healthcare domains. By defining the “digital spine” architecture, this review, together with the institutional report, offers a conceptual framework that may serve as a template for other tertiary ophthalmic networks, although its scalability and reproducibility remain to be validated.

Future research should focus on validating this multi-site workflow model through quantitative evaluation of clinical outcomes, efficiency metrics, and cost-effectiveness across diverse healthcare settings. In particular, the integration of AI into biometric analysis and surgical planning offers opportunities to further enhance predictive accuracy and streamline decision-making within the “digital spine” architecture. At present, the AI-driven optimization of IOL calculations described here represents a planned future direction rather than a currently implemented component of the workflow. Additionally, advancements in interoperable health information systems, automated scheduling algorithms, and predictive supply chain management may further reduce logistical complexity and improve scalability. As multi-site care delivery models continue to expand, the development of standardized operational frameworks and benchmarking metrics will be essential to guide implementation and ensure consistent quality across geographically distributed ophthalmic networks.

This review has several limitations that temper the strength of its conclusions. First, it describes the experience of a single, high-volume academic center; thus, generalizability to other health systems may be limited. Second, several elements described here, such as AI-driven IOL calculation refinement and digital-twin simulations, represent intended future directions rather than fully implemented components of current practice. These limitations should be addressed through prospective, multi-site evaluation before the framework is adopted as a validated model.

Conclusion

Cataract surgery delivered across a geographically distributed tertiary network is best understood as an integrated logistical system rather than a series of isolated clinical encounters. The BPEI’s “digital spine” framework describes how cloud-based planning, coordinated staffing, tiered referral of complex and high-risk cases, flexible supply chain strategies, and distributed postoperative care could be organized to support the delivery of high surgical volumes across multiple sites. This review does not present institutional outcome data on operative efficiency, refractive results, patient satisfaction, or cost, and the operational benefits described therefore remain hypothesized rather than demonstrated. This model may offer a practical template for other multi-site ophthalmic networks pursuing personalized cataract care at scale, though prospective validation of its clinical, efficiency, and cost outcomes is required before its advantages can be considered established.
