Gender and region disparities in plasma PCSK9 levels: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Tongyue Li, Zhengyu Wang, Haozhi Gong, Tao Wang, Xue Wang, Yifan Yang, Renjie Yang, Ran Xu, Bin Yang, Yan Ma, Jichang Luo, Liqun Jiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCirculating proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin 9 (PCSK9) levels may differ by sex and geographic region, but evidence remains inconsistent. This systematic review and meta-analysis quantifies these differences in adults with hyperlipidaemia, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) or elevated ASCVD risk.MethodsWe searched MEDLINE, Web of Science, Cochrane Central and EMBASE through August 2024 for studies reporting plasma PCSK9 in ≥10 adults. Two reviewers extracted data and assessed bias. Random-effects models pooled standardised mean differences (SMDs) or means; heterogeneity was evaluated by I2, with subgroup and sensitivity analyses exploring its sources.ResultsFor gender, 13 studies (5 706 participants) showed significantly higher PCSK9 in females than males (SMD −0.35, 95% CI −0.49 to −0.20; p=0.033; I2=54%). Single-sex studies corroborated this: female-only mean 350.66 ng/mL (95% CI 301.86 to 399.46) versus male-only 228.36 ng/mL (95% CI 176.33 to 280.39). For region, 63 studies (34 802 participants) from Asia, Europe and North America yielded no significant overall difference (p=0.569); after outlier exclusion, pooled means were 250.39 (95% CI 217.70 to 283.08), 269.27 (95% CI 230.24 to 308.31) and 301.76 (95% CI 194.28 to 409.23) ng/mL, with I2=0% within subgroups. Sensitivity analyses confirmed robustness; most studies were high quality. GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation) certainty was moderate for sex and low for region.ConclusionsIn these clinical populations, women have higher circulating PCSK9 levels than men, while regional differences were not statistically significant. These findings support gender-conscious interpretation of baseline PCSK9 values but do not establish PCSK9 inhibitor dosing or treatment strategies.

Introduction

Hyperlipidaemia, a prevalent metabolic disorder manifesting as an abnormal elevation of blood lipids, poses a serious global health challenge.1 Timely management of hyperlipidaemia is crucial as it can substantially reduce the risk of life-threatening diseases, particularly atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases (ASCVD).2 Currently, statins have emerged as the primary therapeutic modality for the management of hyperlipidaemia,3 but some patients have poor tolerance to statins, or low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels still cannot reach the expected target after treatment. In this case, the new lipid-lowering drug, proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin 9 (PCSK9) inhibitors, provides a new option for lowering lipids.4

The PCSK9, a member of the serine protease family, is encoded by the PCSK9 gene located on chromosome 1p32.3, which was significantly associated with lipometabolism and cardiovascular diseases.5–7 It serves as a molecular chaperone by binding to the epidermal growth factor-like domain A of LDL receptor (LDL-R) and promoting degradation of LDL-R in an endosomal/lysosomal pathway.8 Increased plasma PCSK9 levels lead to a decrease in the number of LDL-R receptors on liver cells, which hinders hepatocyte uptake of LDL-C, resulting in increased circulation levels of LDL-C and a higher cardiovascular disease risk. Previous studies have indicated that plasma PCSK9 levels were independent predictors of cardiovascular risks.9–11

Plasma PCSK9 levels exhibit variability probably influenced by several factors, including geographical region and sex. Prior research has described sex-related differences in circulating PCSK9 levels,12 with some studies reporting higher plasma PCSK9 levels in women than in men.1314 However, Mayne et al early research revealed that a significant association between plasma PCSK9 and LDL-C, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol and total cholesterol in men, but not in women.15 Because the findings of PCSK9 levels across genders are divergent, a systematic review and meta-analysis is warranted.

For research in different regions, the Dallas Heart Study, which enrolled 3138 participants from diverse ethnic backgrounds, reported lower plasma PCSK9 levels in Black participants than in White and Hispanic participants.1314 In addition, cardiovascular disease burden differs across population groups and regions.16 These findings suggest that population-level or regional factors may be related to PCSK9 levels. However, no meta-analysis has yet consolidated these findings. Therefore, we aimed to summarise the baseline levels of PCSK9 across sex groups and geographical locations, which may provide evidence to inform interpretation of PCSK9 biology in clinical populations.

Methods and analysis

Registration

This investigation used the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (the PRISMA 2020 statement) (online supplemental file 1).17 The review process was formally registered within the PROSPERO database (CRD42022298979, https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/#recordDetails). This review was conducted and reported according to the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions.18 No major deviations from the registered protocol were made.

Search strategy

The literature search was conducted in August 2024 across four databases: MEDLINE, Web of Science, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials and EMBASE. Reference lists of included studies and relevant previous systematic reviews were also reviewed. Grey literature sources and ongoing studies were checked; no additional eligible full-text records were included from these sources. A draft list of search strategies was compiled by one review author (TL) and reviewed by two review authors (HG and JL) and a librarian following the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies checklist (online supplemental file 2).

Criteria

Types of participants

Adult individuals with hyperlipidaemia, ASCVD or heightened risk of ASCVD were eligible.1920 These participants had PCSK9 levels assessed through quantitative methods and were not receiving treatment with PCSK9 inhibitors. Non-adults and pregnant women were excluded. Pregnant women were excluded. Pregnancy disturbs PCSK9 levels via hormonal changes and medication is not recommended during gestation.2122

Types of studies

The systematic review and meta-analysis concentrated on studies focusing on the plasma PCSK9 levels in adult populations. Eligible cohort and case-series studies required ≥10 participants, encompassing prospective, retrospective, cross-sectional cohorts plus baseline data from randomised controlled trials (RCTs). Per the Cochrane Handbook, non-randomised studies are permissible if RCTs alone fail to address the research question.

Outcomes

The outcomes of eligible studies were as follows:

The difference in plasma PCSK9 levels between different genders. For studies including both male and female participants, PCSK9 levels were compared between genders. For studies including only one sex, PCSK9 levels were summarised as pooled mean values.

The difference in plasma PCSK9 levels between different genders. For studies including both male and female participants, PCSK9 levels were compared between genders. For studies including only one sex, PCSK9 levels were summarised as pooled mean values.

The difference in plasma PCSK9 levels across predefined geographical regions (Asia, Europe, North America, Africa and Oceania).

The difference in plasma PCSK9 levels across predefined geographical regions (Asia, Europe, North America, Africa and Oceania).

Study selection

Two review authors (TL and ZW) searched the above databases and retrieved studies into EndNote V.21 to remove duplicate studies. Then, two review authors (TL and ZW) independently sifted the literature. To ensure reliable assessment, an inter-rater reliability assessment (online supplemental file 3) was implemented for reviewers before selection. After the inter-rater reliability reached a high concordance (≥80%), formal screening began.23 The varying perspectives in the evaluation process among the two authors were reconciled through discussions or by seeking the opinion of a third author. During the preliminary evaluation phase, each of the two reviewers individually examined the titles and abstracts of the studies from the literature exploration to assess their suitability for integration into the research. The second phase of screening involved a thorough review of the full text of the articles initially screened, with suitable articles being included. When several studies presented data from an identical study group or when multiple articles from the same study series were published chronologically, studies of immediate interest or those with the most substantial sample size were prioritised for retention. We used the PRISMA flow chart to outline the study selection process.24

Quality assessment

The studies were divided among two reviewers (TL and ZW), who independently evaluated each study. Any discrepancies were resolved by discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (HG). For the RCTs, risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane risk-of-bias domains as implemented in Review Manager, including random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting and other bias. ‘Other bias’ referred to potential sources of bias not covered by the preceding domains, such as important baseline imbalance, early termination, deviations from the prespecified protocol or potential influence from funding sources.

For the non-randomised studies, the risk of bias was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Analytical Cross-Sectional Studies. This checklist comprises eight items: (1) Were the criteria for inclusion in the sample clearly defined? (2) Were the study subjects and the setting described in detail? (3) Was the exposure measured in a valid and reliable way? (4) Were objective, standard criteria used for measurement of the condition? (5) Were confounding factors identified? (6) Were strategies to deal with confounding factors stated? (7) Were the outcomes measured in a valid and reliable way? (8) Was appropriate statistical analysis used? Each item was rated as ‘yes’, ‘no’, ‘unclear’ or ‘not applicable’. To determine the quality of individual studies, the number of ‘yes’ responses was divided by the total number of questions to calculate the ‘% of yes’. Studies were categorised as high quality (≥70%), moderate quality (50%–69%) or low quality (<50%). The details of the quality assessment are shown in online supplemental table 1 and online supplemental figure 1.25

Data extraction and management

Two independent reviewers (TL and ZW) extracted data from the selected studies and documented them using a prearranged extraction form in an Excel spreadsheet. For each eligible study, we extracted the first author and publication year, study title, study design, country and region, sample size, sex distribution, population characteristics, PCSK9 measurement method and PCSK9 levels. To validate the reliability of data collection, we conducted an inter-rater reliability test and required high agreement (≥80%) between the two reviewers. Disagreements were resolved through discussion within the review team or consultation with a third reviewer as needed. In cases of ambiguity or insufficient data, we contacted study authors for additional information. No imputation was performed for missing PCSK9 outcome data. Analyses were restricted to data that could be directly extracted or reliably converted from published reports. When studies presented data as medians with ranges or IQRs, or as SEs, we converted these values to means and SDs using established formulas when feasible.2627 If no imputation methods were applied for missing data. Instead, our analyses were restricted to data that could be directly extracted or reliably converted from the published reports. When studies presented data as median (range or IQR) or SE, we converted these to means and SDs using established formulas when feasible. For studies reporting PCSK9 levels at multiple time points, we extracted data from the baseline measurement prior to any intervention or follow-up to minimise confounding by treatment effects and to maintain consistency with the cross-sectional study designs. Studies with non-extractable or non-convertible outcome data for the primary analysis were excluded from the relevant pooled estimates. The details of all included studies’ data are shown in online supplemental table 2.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Heterogeneity assessment

All extracted data were analysed with STATA V.18 for subsequent analytical processing. Clinical heterogeneity of studies was assessed by analysing differences in design, gender and region on forest plots. Mean difference or standardised mean difference (SMD) with 95% CIs were used as pooled effect sizes for continuous PCSK9 levels. Statistical heterogeneity between studies was assessed using Cochran’s Q and I² testing. We consider p values <0.10 or I2>75% to indicate substantial heterogeneity. Considering inter-study differences in demographics, regions and PCSK9 detection assays, the DerSimonian-Laird random-effects model was adopted for all pooled comparisons stratified by sex and region. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were predefined to explore sources of heterogeneity.

Subgroup analyses

For the gender analysis, the Galbraith plot and leave-one-out sensitivity analysis were planned by sequentially excluding each individual study to evaluate whether any single study disproportionately influenced the overall effect estimate.

For the regional analysis, study populations were categorised into Asia, Europe, Africa, Americas and Oceania following the United Nations geoscheme, according to recruitment countries. Regions containing fewer than three studies were omitted from pooling due to inadequate statistical power.

Sensitivity analysis

Funnel plots were planned to identify outlying studies that contributed substantially to the observed heterogeneity. Studies identified as extreme outliers in the sensitivity analyses that contributed to substantial heterogeneity (I²>75%) were planned to be excluded from the primary comparative meta-analysis to obtain more stable pooled estimates.

Assessment of reporting bias

Publication bias will be analysed using Begg’s rank correlation and Egger weighted regression tests. We have constructed standard funnel plots to visually evaluate possible publication bias. We used a funnel chart to visualise the effect estimates across different subgroups. The funnel chart was created using STATA V.18, with each subgroup represented by a distinct colour and a different schematic to aid in visual differentiation. For the regional analysis, funnel plots were additionally used to assist in identifying outlying studies contributing to heterogeneity. Asymmetry in the funnel plot was considered indicative of potential publication bias, and possible explanations were investigated.

Certainty assessment

Certainty of evidence was evaluated using the GRADE approach adapted for prognostic/risk-factor evidence. The certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed across the following domains: study limitations (risk of bias), inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, publication bias and magnitude of association. Evidence certainty was categorised as high, moderate, low or very low. This approach was based on recommendations for prognostic factor research proposed by Huguet et al and the GRADE Working Group.28

Result

A total of 4996 records were screened after duplicate removal from MEDLINE, Web of Science, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials and EMBASE. The flow diagram details retrieval, full-text assessment, exclusions and final inclusion (figure 1). 13 studies (with 5706 participants) provided sufficient data for the sex-related analyses (including 6 reporting both genders, 4 reporting males only and 3 reporting females only), and 63 studies (with 34 802 participants) provided sufficient data for the regional analyses, spanning 19 countries across five WHO regions. The main characteristics of the included studies are summarised in table 1. Across the sex-related analyses, the mean age of female participants ranged from 23.8 to 68.6 years and that of male participants ranged from 40.0 to 67.25 years.

Gender outcomes

A total of six studies reporting PCSK9 levels for both genders were included in the gender comparison meta-analysis. The forest plot reveals a statistically significant difference in PCSK9 concentration between genders (p=0.033, SMD: −0.35, 95% CI (−0.49 to −0.20)), with the negative SMD indicating that PCSK9 levels tended to be higher in females compared with males, although with moderate heterogeneity (I²=54.0%). Due to the moderate heterogeneity, a random-effects model was adopted (figure 2A). The three articles exclusively reported male data with a weighted mean PCSK9 level of 228.36 ng/mL (95% CI 176.33 to 280.39 ng/mL). Conversely, the two articles that exclusively reported female data indicated a higher PCSK9 level in women, with a mean of 350.66 ng/mL (95% CI 301.86 to 399.46 ng/mL) (figure 2B). Of the initially identified single-sex studies, two reports were excluded: one female-only study was excluded due to non-convertible data format (median with IQR); the male-only study was excluded due to high heterogeneity (online supplemental figure 2).

Region outcomes

A total of 63 articles (with 34 802 samples) spanning 19 countries were divided into 5 regions (Asia, Europe, Africa, the Americas and Western Pacific). Notably, the Asian region accounted for nearly half of the studies (30 out of 63), with China contributing the highest number (24 out of 63) (table 1). However, according to our pre-specified criteria, Africa and Oceania were each represented by only one eligible study, which precluded reliable pooled estimation; these two regions were therefore excluded from the primary comparative analysis and are reported descriptively below.

The overall comparison across these three regions showed no statistically significant difference in pooled PCSK9 levels (test for subgroup differences: p=0.569), with low heterogeneity observed within each subgroup. Specifically, the pooled mean PCSK9 level was 250.39 ng/mL (95% CI 217.70 to 283.08; I²=0.0%) for Asia, 269.27 ng/mL (95% CI 230.24 to 308.31; I²=0.0%) for Europe and 301.76 ng/mL (95% CI 194.28 to 409.23; I²=0.0%) for North America (figure 3).

Descriptively, the single study from Oceania reported a mean PCSK9 level of 287.00 ng/mL (95% CI 175.28 to 398.72), and the single study from Africa reported a mean of 147.47 ng/mL (95% CI 5.69 to 284.92). These were not included in the primary comparative analysis due to insufficient data for reliable synthesis.

Quality of included studies

Using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist, our evaluation of non-randomised research focused on gender disparities in the initial seven rows and regional variations in the following sections of the quality analysis. 45 studies (75%) met the criteria for high-quality classification, 15 studies (25%) were rated as moderate quality, and none were rated as low quality (online supplemental table 1). The quality of the included RCTs was evaluated by the Revman risk of bias summary scale. A detailed account of the risk of bias summary is shown in online supplemental figure 1. Overall, the included studies were of moderate-to-high methodological quality, with the main limitations related to incomplete reporting of study methodology and potential confounding factors.

Sensitivity analyses and reporting bias

To investigate the sources of heterogeneity and assess the robustness of our pooled estimates, we performed sensitivity analyses for both gender and regional comparisons.

For the gender meta-analysis, which exhibited moderate heterogeneity (I²=54.4%), we conducted a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis by sequentially excluding each individual study. The pooled SMD ranged from –0.40 (95% CI –0.53 to –0.27) to –0.28 (95% CI –0.39 to –0.16) after each exclusion, indicating that no single study disproportionately drove the overall effect estimate (online supplemental figure 3). The funnel plot for studies related to gender shows symmetry, with the exception of a single outlier in the lower left quadrant, indicating no considerable bias in publication (figure 4). Sensitivity analyses did not yield any noteworthy findings, and none of the evaluated variables exhibited a significant impact on the estimates of effect size. For the regional analysis, initial pooling of all available studies revealed substantial heterogeneity within the Asian (I²=90.7%) and European (I²=71.9%) subgroups (online supplemental figure 4). Funnel chart analysis was carried out for regional analyses. Inspection of the funnel plot (online supplemental figure 5A) revealed several studies falling outside the 95% CI, and these outlying studies were identified as the main contributors to the observed heterogeneity among the Asian, European and North American subgroups. After excluding studies with outlying estimates, heterogeneity was substantially reduced (I² from 85.8% to 0%) (figure 3 and online supplemental figure 4) and the sensitivity analysis did not yield any positive results (online supplemental figure 5B and online supplemental figure 6). The funnel plots for regional studies showed asymmetry with missing data, implying potential publication bias. Studies with higher baseline levels of PCSK9 are more likely to be published.

Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence was assessed using the GRADE approach adapted for prognostic/risk-factor evidence, with the detailed assessment presented in online supplemental table 3. The certainty of evidence for sex-related differences in circulating PCSK9 levels was rated as moderate after downgrading from high certainty due to inconsistency, mainly because of moderate between-study heterogeneity. The certainty of evidence for regional differences in circulating PCSK9 levels was rated as low after downgrading from high certainty due to inconsistency and indirectness, including heterogeneity across studies and the use of broad geographic regions as proxies for individual-level ethnicity, ancestry, lifestyle, healthcare background and assay-related differences.

Discussion

Our meta-analysis summarises how circulating PCSK9 levels vary by sex and geographic region in clinical populations relevant to lipid-lowering therapy. Across 13 studies contributing sex-related data, women tended to have higher circulating PCSK9 levels than men in the direct comparison analysis. In contrast, 63 studies contributing regional data did not show statistically significant differences across the primary regional comparison. Importantly, our findings are derived from clinical populations, including adults with hyperlipidaemia, ASCVD or increased ASCVD risk; therefore, they should not be directly extrapolated to healthy populations or general biobank cohorts without further validation.

Previous studies have demonstrated that plasma PCSK9 level is reportedly influenced by multiple factors, such as sex, age, latitude, ethnicity and gene variation.29 Importantly, sex hormones and latitude were reported as the strongest predictors of plasma PCSK9 levels.13

In our analysis, PCSK9 levels were higher in adult females compared with adult males, aligning with the results of multiple previous studies.1314 This finding may be partly explained by hormone-related regulation of the LDLR-PCSK9 pathway.30 Numerous investigations have focused on the relationship between oestrogen and PCSK9. Fu et al reported that 17β-estradiol enhanced LDLR expression without clearly increasing PCSK9 transcription,31 while Jing et al showed that estrogen-related signalling may influence intracellular and extracellular PCSK9 regulation.32 These findings suggest that sex hormones may affect PCSK9 metabolism through complex regulatory mechanisms, although the exact pathway remains unclear.

The clinical relevance of PCSK9 should also be interpreted in relation to LDL-C metabolism. Higher PCSK9 levels may reduce hepatic LDLR availability and thereby decrease LDL-C clearance.115 Recent evidence from larger clinical and genetic studies suggests that PCSK9 is relevant to LDL-C regulation in both women and men. Sever et al reported a FOURIER trial that evolocumab reduced LDL-C levels and improved cardiovascular outcomes consistently across sex and age groups.33 Pott et al reported sex-related and statin-related genetic associations at the PCSK9 gene locus, suggesting that PCSK9-related genetic effects on LDL-C regulation are relevant in both women and men, although sex-specific differences in effect size may exist.34 Taken together, the current evidence suggests that PCSK9 is clinically relevant in both sexes, while baseline circulating PCSK9 concentrations may differ between women and men.

Age and menopausal status may also affect sex-related differences in PCSK9 levels. However, age range and menopausal status were not consistently reported across the included studies, limiting our ability to determine whether the higher PCSK9 levels observed in women were related to premenopausal, postmenopausal or mixed female populations.35 This issue is particularly important because women generally have a lower risk of cardiovascular disease before menopause, whereas lipid metabolism and cardiovascular risk change substantially after menopause.3637 Therefore, future studies should report age range, menopausal status and hormone-related variables more clearly to help distinguish biological sex effects from age-related or menopause-related effects.

For the regional analysis, the available evidence remains limited and heterogeneous. Few multicentre studies have directly compared circulating PCSK9 levels across individual ethnicities, ancestry groups and geographic populations using standardised assays. Enkhmaa et al reported differences in PCSK9 levels between African American and Caucasian participants, suggesting that ancestry-related or population-level factors may influence circulating PCSK9.3839 However, our analysis used broad geographic regions rather than individual-level ethnicity or genetic ancestry, and Africa and Oceania contributed too few studies for reliable pooled estimates. Therefore, the absence of statistically significant regional differences in the primary analysis should not be interpreted as definitive evidence that regional, ethnic or ancestry-related variation is absent. Additional multicentre studies using standardised assays and individual-level ancestry, medication and lifestyle data are needed.

The GRADE assessment supports a cautious interpretation of our findings. The evidence for sex-related differences in circulating PCSK9 levels was of moderate certainty, suggesting that the observed higher PCSK9 levels in women are reasonably supported but may vary across study characteristics and clinical settings. In contrast, the evidence for regional differences was of low certainty; therefore, the absence of statistically significant regional differences should not be interpreted as definitive evidence that regional or population-level variation does not exist. Future studies should use standardised PCSK9 assays, detailed medication records, menopausal status and individual-level ethnicity or genetic ancestry information to improve the certainty of evidence.

Although our literature search ended in August 2024, subsequent PCSK9-related studies have continued to focus on PCSK9-targeted therapies, genetic evidence, inflammation and atherosclerosis.4041 These studies were outside the predefined search window or did not directly provide eligible baseline circulating PCSK9 data stratified by sex or region, and therefore were not incorporated into the pooled estimates. Nevertheless, recent evidence continues to support the biological and clinical relevance of PCSK9 and highlights the need for future updated reviews when more standardised baseline data become available.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, heterogeneity in measurement methods across studies may compromise robustness, and the scarcity of high-quality RCTs examining sex-specific and region-specific PCSK9 levels limits the evidence grade.

Second, concomitant lipid-lowering therapy, especially statin treatment, was not uniformly reported or adjusted for across the included studies. Since statins can upregulate PCSK9 levels,34 and because statin prescription rates and dosages may differ by sex and region, residual confounding by statin therapy cannot be excluded. Future studies should provide detailed medication records, including statin type, dosage, treatment duration and adherence or should analyse medication-naïve participants separately.

Third, our focus on adults with hyperlipidaemia, ASCVD or high risk—though clinically relevant—prevents us from determining whether lower PCSK9 levels in men reflect a general physiological trait, disease-specific association or treatment effect. Broader population cohorts are needed.

Fourth, although grey literature sources were checked, reporting bias remains possible because studies with notable findings may be overrepresented. Finally, few studies reported PCSK9 levels by individual ethnicity; regional classification may introduce misclassification bias. Future multicentre, multiethnic studies using standardised assays, detailed medication histories, menopausal status and genetic ancestry data are warranted.

Conclusions

In summary, our meta-analysis provides a comprehensive understanding of PCSK9 dynamics in diverse populations, highlighting associations with gender. The variances in PCSK9 concentrations identified across genders and geographical regions may present a valuable opportunity to help inform individualised lipid-lowering strategies. Continued research is imperative to unravel the complexities of PCSK9 biology and its clinical implications.
