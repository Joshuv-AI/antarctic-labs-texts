ELOVL1 promotes the progression of intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma by enhancing endoplasmic reticulum stress and the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway
Authors: Weigen Wu, Danhong Zhan, Yihui Gao, Yang Jiang, Qiguang Pang, Yuchen Pei, Junlong Wang, Yao Li, Yuqing Wang, Shayuanzi Huang, Ruizhi Wang, Meifang He, Wei Chen

Introduction

Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA) ranks as the second most prevalent primary liver malignancy worldwide, with its incidence steadily rising in recent years [1, 2]. In contrast to other types of liver cancer, the diagnosis of iCCA is often complicated, and its early symptoms are subtle, leading to most patients being diagnosed at advanced stages, which results in the loss of the opportunity for curative surgical resection [3–5]. Although surgical resection and chemotherapy, particularly platinum-based chemotherapy, remain the primary treatments for iCCA, the high recurrence rate and chemotherapy resistance contribute to poor long-term survival outcomes [6–8]. Therefore, identifying novel molecular targets and further investigating the pathogenic mechanisms of iCCA, especially its resistance mechanisms, are crucial for improving patient prognosis and treatment efficacy.

ELOVL1 (elongation of very long chain fatty acids protein 1) is a key component of the fatty acid elongase family, tasked with the production of very long chain fatty acids (VLCFAs) [9]. As the rate-limiting enzyme in VLCFA synthesis, ELOVL1 is pivotal in lipid metabolism [10]. Research has revealed that ELOVL1 expression is aberrant in different kinds of cancer, particularly in colorectal cancer (CRC), breast cancer (BC), where its upregulation is closely associated with tumorigenesis [11–14]. Nonetheless, the function of ELOVL1 in iCCA is ambiguous, particularly regarding its involvement in regulating endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and influencing iCCA development. ER stress is an adaptive cellular response to external or internal stress, regulating cell survival, proliferation, and apoptosis [15]. Excessive ER stress is intricately associated with the development of multiple malignancies, resistance mechanisms, and tumor cell adaptation [16]. Therefore, investigating the operation of ELOVL1 in ER stress may reveal novel therapeutic targets for iCCA.

The PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway is essential in numerous biological processes (BP), covering cell growth, proliferation, differentiation, survival, and migration [17, 18]. Aberrant stimulation of this pathway is frequently detected in numerous malignancies and is closely linked to carcinogenesis and progression [18]. PI3K, as the initiator of this pathway, activates downstream AKT and mTOR, which in turn regulate cellular metabolism, proliferation, and survival [19]. The stimulation of this pathway not only enhances tumor cell proliferation but also affects ER homeostasis and reactions to stress, which can contribute to tumor cell resistance and adaptability [20]. Research has demonstrated that triggering this pathway amplifies ER stress responses, further promoting tumor cell growth and survival. Consequently, understanding the function of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway in ER stress, particularly its potential mechanisms in iCCA, has become a key focus of recent research.

This study reveals that ELOVL1 expression is heightened in iCCA tissues and relates to aggressive tumor features. Mechanistically, ELOVL1 enhances iCCA progression by engaging the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway and promoting ER stress.

Methods

Data availability

The Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database offered both bulk RNA-seq and scRNA-seq data. The datasets GSE26566 and GSE89749 were incorporated into the bulk RNA-seq analysis [21, 22]. Upon the integration and elimination of batch effects from two datasets, the “SVA” R package generated 61 normal and 222 iCCA tissues [23]. The scRNA-seq data utilized in the analysis was from GSE138709 (see 21 for more details).

Human gene data were sourced from the GeneCards database. Searching for the term ‘endoplasmic reticulum stress’ identified 2,502 genes associated with this condition, when a relevance score threshold of > 5 was applied. We also searched the NCBI database and found 1234 genes linked to ER stress. Additionally, we downloaded two datasets pertaining to ER stress from MSigDB: “GO RESPONSE TO ENDOPLASMIC RETICULUM STRESS” and “GO REGULATION OF RESPONSE TO ENDOPLASMIC RETICULUM STRESS,” which together provided 258 ER stress-related genes (ERSRGs). Combining the data from these sources, we compiled a total of 2863 ERSRGs, with gene names listed in Table S2.

DEGs analysis and functional enrichment

The combined set was analyzed for differential expression utilizing the ‘limma,’ [24] with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified based on adjusted P-value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| > 1. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses of ERSRGs were implemented utilizing the ‘clusterProfiler’ package [25]. Significance was defined as P < 0.05 and FDR < 0.25. The values of P were adjusted utilizing the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure.

GSEA and GSVA

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was implemented to examine the distribution pattern of gene set from the MSigDB inside the combined dataset [26], aiming to identify the phenotypic contribution of each gene. Using the R package ‘clusterProfiler,’ GSEA was conducted on both the cohorts with high and low risk, with gene set sizes limited to a maximum of 500 genes and a minimum of five. The BH method was used for p-value correction, and significant enrichment was defined by an q-value < 0.25 and p-value < 0.05. Additionally, gene set variation analysis (GSVA) [27], a non-parametric and unstructured methodology, was applied to determine functional enrichment between iCCA and normal samples, with significant enrichment defined as p < 0.05.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

The WGCNA network was constructed utilizing the “WGCNA” recognizing gene modules connected to iCCA [28]. Outlier samples were excluded through hierarchical clustering with a cut-off height of 170. The appropriate soft threshold power was established according to the scale-free topology criterion, and the Topological Overlap Matrix was generated. A comprehensive clustering tree methodology was applied to identify modules containing more than 50 genes. Subsequently, primary module genes were selected from the module most strongly linked to iCCA.

Construct the ERSRGs score of the ssGSEA algorithm

The relative abundance of every gene in a dataset sample can be measured utilizing ssGSEA. The ERSRGs score for all samples in the merged set was computed utilizing the ssGSEA method and the R package GSVA. Association analysis and classification of the cancer samples into high and low scoring groups can be achieved by using the score.

ssGSEA and ESTIMATE

ssGSEA analysis was executed utilizing the “GSVA” to calculate immune cell infiltration (ICI) within the two groups with high and low scores. The Spearman rank correlation test was utilized to examine p-values in order to explore the link between core genes and ICI.

Utilizing the R program ESTIMATE, we assessed tumor immunological activity based on the merged dataset expression profiles [29]. Immune activity, tumor purity, immune score (ESTIMATE Score), and stromal score were computed for each sample. A group comparison graphic was created to visualize differences in immunological features between individuals with high and low scores. Additionally, the ERSRG score’s relationship with immunological features was explored and presented in a correlation scatter plot.

Identification of key genes and PPI network analysis

To uncover critical genes involved in ER stress in iCCA, we performed Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression analysis utilizing the “glmnet” on the bulk RNAseq dataset. The genes identified through LASSO screening were then utilized to establish a PPI network. We employed the STRING database for this purpose [30], which is commonly employed to explore known protein interactions and predict novel ones, to enhance our understanding of the relationships among the main genes implicated in ER stress in iCCA.

Feature selection and visualization with machine learning models

To predict the onset and progression of iCCA, key predictive characteristics were identified using the “PyCaret”, which facilitated the generation of 12 machine learning models. The prediction efficacy of models was verified utilizing multiple metrics. To mitigate overfitting, hyperparameter optimization was performed through 10-fold cross-validation with repeated grid search techniques customized for each model’s parametric space. Additionally, Shapley Additive exPlanation (SHAP) scores were applied to identify key factors influencing iCCA onset and progression [31], while local interpretable model-agnostic explanations (LIME) were implemented to acquire more information into the predictive behavior of the most optimal machine learning model [32].

Establishment of a nomogram

Six key genes were selected to develop a nomogram utilizing the “rms.” The nomogram’s precision was evaluated with a calibration curve, while its clinical relevance was determined through a clinical impact curve (CIC) and decision curve analysis (DCA). Additionally, external dataset validation (TCGA-CHOL, GSE32225 and GSE107943) confirmed that ERSRGs effectively distinguish iCCA. Diagnostic performance was investigated applying the R package “pROC,” with results demonstrated through the AUC curve [33].

Clinical validation and survival analysis

To further validate the clinical significance of ELOVL1, clinicopathological and survival data from a publicly available cohort of 255 patients with iCCA were obtained [34]. To identify the most biologically significant threshold for ELOVL1 expression, we utilized the “cutoff” R package (version 1.3), which determines the optimal cutoff value based on maximally selected rank statistics [35]. This approach systematically evaluates all potential thresholds to maximize the log-rank statistic. In this study, a cutoff value of 7.367 was established to stratify the 255 patients into ELOVL1-high and ELOVL1-low groups. Overall survival (OS) was evaluated utilizing the Kaplan-Meier method and compared via the log-rank test. The correlation between ELOVL1 expression and clinicopathological features, such as tumor size and serum CA19-9 levels, was analyzed using appropriate statistical tests based on data distribution.

scRNA-seq analysis

The GSE138709 consists of eight samples: three controls and five iCCA samples. To ensure data quality, we applied stringent quality control, preserving cells with mitochondrial gene content below 15% and gene counts between 300 and 7000. The filtered matrix was then normalized utilizing the “LogNormalize” method, followed by dimensionality reduction. Key principal components were selected through the ElbowPlot() tool. For cluster annotation, lineage-specific markers, as outlined in Table S4, were used. Finally, GSEA was conducted with the signature gene set library to assess biological pathway activity in malignant cells, comparing ELOVL1-deficient and ELOVL1-overexpressing conditions.

Drug sensitivity, molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation

To predict drug response in iCCA patients, we accessed the GDSC database to examine molecular markers for drug sensitivity and employed the “pRRophetic” to estimate IC50 values [36]. Using the Clue platform, detected prospective drugs by analyzing the DEGs among samples with high and low expression levels, revealing compounds that may improve cancer progression.

For molecular docking, we focused on the interaction between ELOVL1 and Pictilisib, one of the top drugs identified through the GSCA database. The three-dimensional configuration of Pictilisib was retrieved from the PubChem, and ELOVL1’s protein structure was obtained from UniProt and the PDB database. The structures were processed using Chem3D, and molecular docking was executed utilizing AutoDock Tools, with visualization carried out in PyMOL.

The stability of the ELOVL1–Pictilisib complex was verified by carrying out 100-ns molecular dynamics simulation (MDS) in GROMACS, utilizing the Amber99SB-ILDN force field for the protein and General Amber Force Field for the ligand, within a solvated TIP3P water box under constant temperature and pressure conditions.

Cell lines and lentiviral transfection

Human embryonic kidney cells (HEK293T), human iCCA cell lines (HuccT1 and RBE), and immortalized cholangiocyte cell lines (HIBEC) were all acquired from the American Type Culture Collection. HEK293T cells were cultivated in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, while iCCA and cholangiocyte cell lines were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium under standard conditions. All cell lines were confirmed mycoplasma-free by PCR testing. Lentiviral transfection was performed using shRNA expression vectors targeting ELOVL1 and lentiviral vectors for ELOVL1 overexpression. Recombinant lentivirus was generated in HEK293T cells and utilized to inoculate iCCA cells, then selecting stable transfectants using puromycin. Detailed experimental protocols and material information are provided in the supplementary materials1.

Western blotting and qRT-PCR

A total of 6 iCCA cases were collected from Sun Yat-sen University’s First Affiliated Hospital with ethical consent from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee. Total RNA and proteins were extracted from cell and tissue samples, and reverse transcription and quantitative PCR were performed to determine the relative mRNA expression of target genes. Protein expression was verified by Western blotting, with primary antibodies targeting ELOVL1 and various signaling pathway proteins, followed by chemiluminescent detection. Detailed experimental protocols and material information are provided in the supplementary materials.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence analysis

Tissue samples were fixed in 10% formalin and embedded in paraffin for immunohistochemistry. 4 μm tissue sections were subjected to antigen retrieval using citrate buffer, succeeded by a one-hour blocking with 5% BSA. Sections underwent overnight incubation with primary antibodies (1:200 dilution), followed by a 1-hour incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody, and visualized using DAB staining. Images were obtained with a light microscope, and the positively stained region was analyzed with ImageJ.

For immunofluorescence, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100. After a 1-hour blocking with 5% BSA, cells were treated overnight with primary antibodies at 4 °C, then undergoing 1 h incubation with fluorescently tagged secondary antibodies. Nuclei were stained with DAPI. A Zeiss LSM 800 confocal microscope was used to take the pictures.

Cell proliferation and migration assays

Cell proliferation was quantified through the CCK-8 assay, with 0.5 × 10⁴ cells seeded in a 96-well plate. At each time interval (0, 24, 48, 72 and 96 h), 10% CCK-8 solution was applied, and absorbance at 450 nm was recorded after 2 h. EDU incorporation was assessed by incubating 1 × 10⁴ cells with 50 µM EDU overnight, followed by staining and fluorescence microscopy analysis.

For colony formation, 1000 infected iCCA cells were cultured in a six-well plate for 2 weeks in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, and colonies were counted. Migration was evaluated utilizing a Transwell test, where 5 × 10⁴ cells were inoculated in the upper chambers and incubated for 24 h in DMEM with 10% FBS. Cells were fixed, stained with crystal violet, then enumerated in five fields utilizing a microscope.

Wound healing was performed by creating a scratch in a confluent monolayer of infected iCCA cells. After incubation in RPMI-1640 with 1% FBS for 24 h, images were taken at 0 and 24 h.

Animal experiments

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the institutional guidelines and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Guangzhou Lingfu Top Biotech Co., Ltd. (Approve No.: LFTOP-IACUC-2025-0061; Date of Approval: January 8, 2025). 5 × 10⁶ RBE cells and HuccT1 were resuspended in 100 µL of PBS and Matrigel (1:1) and subcutaneously implanted into BALB/c nude mice (4 weeks old, mice were randomly allocated into five groups of six mice each). The tumor dimensions were assessed every three days, and the volume was computed utilizing the formula: v = length × width²/2. After 24 days of implantation, the mice were executed and xenograft tumors were excised, photographed and weighted.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was executed utilizing R software and GraphPad Prism. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± SD and compared using the Student’s t-test, while non-normally distributed data were presented as median (IQR) and analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (%) and compared using the Pearson’s chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. Correlation analysis was executed with the cor function in R. The t-test was utilized for comparisons between two groups, whereas for more complex situations comprising three or more groups, ANOVA was used. The effect size was calculated to quantify the level of difference between groups. A p-value below 0.05 was regarded as significant.

Results

Functional enrichment analysis

Before batch effect correction, iCCA samples from different platforms showed distinct clustering patterns (Fig. S1A). However, after correction, the samples clustered together (Fig. S1B). DEG analysis of the combined iCCA and normal samples revealed 1,834 DEGs, comprising 871 upregulated and 963 downregulated genes (Table S3).

Functional and pathway enrichment studies of these DEGs were conducted using GO, KEGG, GSEA, and GSVA methods (Fig. S2). GO indicated that the DEGs were mainly linked to BP, encompassing small molecule catabolic processes, amino acid metabolism, and collagen content in the extracellular matrix. Analysis of cellular components (CC) revealed that the DEGs were predominantly located in the extracellular matrix, ER lumen, and apical cell membrane. The examination of molecular function (MF) uncovered redox activity, monooxygenase activity, and vitamin binding as prominent functions.

KEGG analysis demonstrated that the DEGs were enriched in metabolic processes, comprising drug metabolism-cytochrome P450, retinol metabolism, bile secretion, and chemical carcinogenesis-DNA adducts. Additionally, pathways related to chemical carcinogenesis and complement and coagulation cascades were significantly enriched, suggesting key roles for the DEGs in tumor metabolism and immune responses (Fig. S2A).

GSEA and GSVA analyses further emphasized the enrichment of DEGs in critical pathways particularly PI3K/AKT/mTOR, p53, Notch, and TGF-β (Fig. S2B, C, D and E).

WGCNA and key module identification

This study employed WGCNA to select the modules most pertinent to iCCA. According to scale independence and average connectivity, a soft power threshold of β = 4 (with an undirected R² = 0.9) was selected (Fig. 1A). This threshold resulted in the generation of 14 gene co-expression modules, each represented by a different color in Fig. 1A. The correlation between iCCA and these modules is shown in Fig. 1B, with the turquoise module (comprising 11,797 genes) exhibiting the strongest correlation. Next, we intersected the ERSRGs and DEGs with the genes in the MEturquoise module, resulting in 317 DEGs associated with ER stress (Fig. 1C). LASSO regression was then applied to select 22 key genes (Fig. 1D). Finally, after integrating datasets and performing expression analysis on GSE107943, 20 candidate genes were identified (Fig. 1E).

Immune landscape of ERSRGs

We calculated ERSRG values for every cancer sample utilizing the ssGSEA algorithm based on 20 feature genes to examine immune-related expression patterns in iCCA. Analysis of gene expression comparing samples exhibiting high and low ERSRG scores showed significant differences (Fig. S3A). Next, we assessed immune cell infiltration in 28 immune cell types with ssGSEA, uncovering substantial variations in infiltration rates between individuals with elevated and diminished ERSRGs. The elevated ERSRGs group had greater proportions of most immune cell types (Fig. S3B), suggesting ERSRGs influence ICI inside the iCCA microenvironment.

Analysis of the 20 hub genes in relation to ICI showed that ELOVL1 and JUP were negatively linked to the majority of immune cells, while ISG15, RAB8A, FYN, A2M, HBA1, PHLDA1, CALU, RHBDF2, PEX3, XBP1, INSIG1, APOA4, and COLEC10 were positively correlated with most immune cells (Fig. S3C).

We further assessed the tumor microenvironment using the ESTIMATE package, quantifying stromal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores, in addition to tumor purity for groups with high and low ERSRGs. Substantial disparities were identified in all these scores (Fig. S3D). Correlation analysis confirmed robust positive connections between ERSRG scores and immune, stromal, and ESTIMATE scores, while ERSRG scores negatively correlated with tumor purity (Fig. S3D). Finally, we plotted the chromosomal distribution of the 20 hub genes (Fig. S3E).

Evaluation and selection of machine learning models for iCCA

A combined dataset of 283 samples was randomly divided into a training set (70%, N = 198) and a testing set (30%, N = 85) to ascertain the appropriate model to forecast iCCA. 20 core genes were selected as parameters for input. 12 machine learning models were constructed for estimating outcomes (Fig. 2A). The performance of these models in the training cohort was determined employing several metrics (Fig. 2B). The GBC model exhibited an inferior accuracy and AUC, while the SVM, LR, and ET models demonstrated better performance (Fig. 2C and D). Consequently, the SVM, LR, and ET models were selected for subsequent evaluation.

To “open the black box” of the SVM, LR, and ET, we used SHAP values to explore how these models predict the onset of iCCA and the influence of all characteristics on these predictions. SHAP summary plots for the ET model highlighted key feature relationships, revealing that INSIG1, RNF123, ELOVL1, YWHAH, PEX3, CALU, IBTK, XBP1, FYN, and A2M were the top 10 variables significantly influencing predictions (Fig. 3A). Additionally, SHAP correlation analysis illustrated how different features affected the ET model’s output (Fig. 3A), with larger SHAP values correlating with a higher likelihood of iCCA. Notably, positive SHAP values were strongly associated with increased iCCA risk, particularly for ELOVL1. Similar trends were detected in both the SVM and LR (Fig. 3B and C). The intersection of the top 10 variables from all three models identified six ER stress-related DEGs as significant predictors (Fig. 3D), with further details in Fig. 3E.

Predictive value of ELOVL1 gene

The six identified DEGs associated with ERs were used to develop a preliminary nomogram model. Nevertheless, the results of statistical analysis revealed that merely four genes, CALU, PEX3, ELOVL1, and INSIG1, showed a substantial correlation with the model (Fig. 4A). In the Bayesian regression model, which evaluates the conditional effect of each gene while accounting for complex multi-variable interactions, CALU showed no significant independent effect on iCCA development. This contrasts with its significant marginal correlation observed in the bivariate analysis (Fig. 4B, right panel). This statistical discrepancy indicates that the predictive value of CALU is highly dependent on the presence of other specific variables within the ER stress network, rather than exerting a strong, independent diagnostic effect. To maximize the robustness, parsimony, and clinical applicability of our diagnostic model, we enforced a stringent intersection criterion to prevent overfitting. Consequently, genes like PEX3, ELOVL1, and INSIG1, which consistently demonstrated significant independent predictive value in both statistical dimensions, were selected. As a result, these three ER stress-related signature genes were incorporated into the nomogram as robust predictive factors for iCCA development (Fig. 4C).

The accuracy of this nomogram in diagnosing iCCA was confirmed through the calibration curve (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, DCA indicated that iCCA patients would benefit from the clinical application of the nomogram (Fig. 4E). The CIC further illustrated the model’s prediction accuracy, suggesting that the ER stress-related signature genes provided valuable insights into the progression of iCCA (Fig. 4F).

To determine the diagnostic potential of every signature gene, ROC curve analysis was performed using an internal dataset. The AUC values for PEX3, ELOVL1, and INSIG1 were 0.9728, 0.9643, and 0.9640, respectively (Fig. 4G). Additionally, external validation with three independent datasets, TCGA-CHOL, GSE107943, and GSE32225, confirmed the robustness of the diagnostic validity of ELOVL1 (Fig. 4E).

ELOVL1 exhibited upregulation in iCCA tissues and correlated with the poor prognosis of iCCA patients

Across multiple iCCA datasets, we first observed that ELOVL1 displayed substantially elevation in iCCA tissues relative to adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 5A). Consistently, both mRNA and protein levels of ELOVL1 elevated in iCCA cell lines and tumor tissues in comparison to their corresponding normal controls (Fig. 5B, C and D). We then performed immunohistochemical analysis on 10 pairs of iCCA tumor tissues and matched peritumoral liver tissues, which demonstrated a substantially higher expression of ELOVL1 in tumor samples (Fig. 5E). Immunofluorescence assays further confirmed that ELOVL1 was highly expressed in iCCA cell lines but low in HIBEC cells (Fig. 5F). Finally, we evaluated the clinical significance of ELOVL1 using a cohort of 255 patients with iCCA [34]. To rigorously assess its prognostic value, patients were stratified based on an optimal cutoff value of ELOVL1 expression (Cutoff = 7.367), which was determined using maximally selected rank statistics (Fig. S4). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated that patients with ELOVL1 expression above this biological threshold had a significantly worse OS compared to those in the low-expression group (p = 0.001, Fig. 5G). Furthermore, analysis of baseline clinicopathological features (Table 1) revealed that while the high and low ELOVL1 expression groups were well balanced regarding general demographics, elevated ELOVL1 expression was significantly associated with larger tumor sizes (Median 6.0 cm vs. 5.0 cm, p = 0.004) and higher preoperative serum CA19-9 levels (Median 56.3 U/mL vs. 30.1 U/mL, p = 0.019). These clinical findings collectively indicate that ELOVL1 upregulation is closely linked to increased tumor burden and a poor prognosis in iCCA.

ELOVL1 promotes iCCA progression in vitro and in vivo

To examine the functional relevance of ELOVL1 in iCCA advancement, we generated stable ELOVL1-knockout RBE cells and ELOVL1-overexpressing HuccT1 cells. The efficacy of ELOVL1 knockdown and overexpression was confirmed utilizing qPCR and Western blotting (Fig. 6A). Using colony formation, EdU incorporation, and CCK-8 assays, we determined that ELOVL1 knockout considerably diminished the proliferative and clonogenic capacity of RBE cells, whereas ELOVL1 overexpression exerted the opposite effect and significantly enhanced these malignant phenotypes (Fig. 6B, C and D).

Wound-healing and transwell experiments were applied to investigate the impact of ELOVL1 on cell migration and invasion. The findings revealed that ELOVL1 overexpression substantially promoted the migratory and invasive capabilities of iCCA cells, while ELOVL1 knockout restricted such behaviors (Fig. 6E and F).

Epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), a crucial mechanism underlying cancer cell invasion and migration, was next examined to determine whether ELOVL1 regulates metastatic potential through EMT. Western blot examination demonstrated that ELOVL1 overexpression elevated the levels of N-cadherin, vimentin, Slug, and β-catenin, accompanied by concurrently reducing E-cadherin levels. In contrast, ELOVL1 knockout reversed these changes and significantly inhibited EMT (Fig. 6G).

To validate the in vitro findings in vivo, we subcutaneously injected ELOVL1-knockout RBE cells and control cells into nude mice (Fig. 6I). Tumors derived from ELOVL1-knockout cells exhibited markedly slower growth (Fig. 6J), while mouse body weights did not differ significantly among groups (Fig. 6H). Consistently, both tumor weight (Fig. 6J) and tumor volume were substantial reduced in the knockout group relative to the controls (Fig. 6J, K and L). Similarly, subcutaneous xenografts generated using HuCCT1 cells overexpressing ELOVL1 formed significantly larger tumors than control cells, without significant differences in mouse body weight. Tumor weight and volume were both substantially increased in the ELOVL1-overexpression group.

Samples were categorized into high- and low-expression groups by calculating the median ELOVL1 expression. Differential analysis identified 223 DEGs, comprising 9 upregulated and 214 downregulated genes. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed significant associations with BP, CC and MF (Fig. 7A). GO analysis showed DEGs enriched in processes like small molecule and steroid metabolism, amino acid and organic acid metabolism, and lipid transport. In cellular components, DEGs were localized in the extracellular matrix, ER lumen, and lipoprotein particles, suggesting involvement in matrix formation and intercellular signaling. At the MF level, DEGs were enriched in serine hydrolase, peptidase, and peptidase inhibitor activities, indicating roles in protein degradation and lipid metabolism regulation. KEGG analysis highlighted enrichments in steroid hormone biosynthesis, retinol metabolism, cytochrome P450-mediated drug metabolism, and bile secretion, suggesting key roles in metabolism and detoxification. Additionally, pathways linked to chemical carcinogenesis and complement cascades were enriched, pointing to a potential role in tumor metabolism and immune responses. These DEGs may also serve as cancer-related biomarkers.

Furthermore, we observed significant differences in the activity of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway between the groups exhibiting high and low ELOVL1 expression levels (Fig. 7B). ELOVL1 expression also correlated with key molecules in this pathway (Fig. 7C, D and E). GSEA of these two subpopulations also revealed enrichment of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway (Fig. 7H). This pathway is essential for physiological activities like apoptosis, stem cell self-renewal, and cell proliferation. To further investigate the function of ELOVL1 in the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, we combined the results of decoupleR analysis conducted on our transcriptome sequencing data (Fig. 7I). The heatmap revealed that the PI3K pathway was active in the WT group (positive value) but suppressed in the knockdown group (negative value), indicating that knockdown of ELOVL1 significantly reduced the activity of the PI3K signaling pathway. Additionally, the bar plot further confirmed the significant correlation between PI3K pathway activity and ELOVL1 expression. GSEA also showed that the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway was significantly enriched in both the high and low ELOVL1 expression groups, further supporting the regulatory function of ELOVL1 in this pathway. Figure S6 illustrates the expression differences of ELOVL1 between the WT and knockdown groups (Fig. S6A) and the volcano plot of differential analysis (Fig. S6B), confirming that ELOVL1 expression was markedly reduced in the knockdown group relative to the WT group.

Finally, we validated these findings through Western blot analysis. The findings indicated that ELOVL1 knockdown in RBE cells markedly diminished the phosphorylation levels of PI3K, AKT, and mTOR, while ELOVL1 overexpression in HuccT1 cells led to the opposite effect (Fig. 7J).

Drug sensitivity, molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation

To determine the potential link between ELOVL1 expression and PI3K inhibitor sensitivity, we first examined how ELOVL1 levels correlate with the response to Pictilisib. As shown in Fig. 8A, we observed that higher ELOVL1 expression was associated with an increase in the IC₅₀ values of Pictilisib, suggesting that overexpression of ELOVL1 may enhance the tumor cells’ resistance to this drug, requiring higher concentrations to achieve growth inhibition.

Building on this, we used the Clue platform to further investigate the therapeutic potential of Pictilisib in iCCA. Based on the DEGs between the two groups, Pictilisib was identified as a promising PI3K inhibitor with significant therapeutic relevance for iCCA (Fig. 8B).

We also executed molecular docking investigations to evaluate the interaction between Pictilisib and ELOVL1. The docking analysis, shown in Fig. 8B, revealed that Pictilisib interacts with ELOVL1 through hydrogen bonds at the TYR-181, GLN-192, LYS-198, ARG-260, PHE-250, THR-138 site, exhibiting a binding energy of − 10.0 kcal/mol, signifying a robust and sustained binding affinity. Although molecular docking presents initial insights into the ligand-receptor interaction, it fails to encompass the dynamic characteristics of proteins in a physiological context. Consequently, to obtain a more profound understanding of the stability and conformational dynamics of the ELOVL1-Pictilisib complex, we executed a 100 ns MDS.

The Root mean square deviation (RMSD) analysis of the MDS showed that the ELOVL1-Pictilisib complex attained equilibrium at around 55 ns and stabilized at approximately 0.45 nm, indicating that the complex structure remained stable after 55 ns with minimal movement of the ligand within the binding site (Fig. 8C). Meanwhile, the radius of gyration (Rg) steadily stabilized around 2 nm, suggesting that the protein structure became more compact after ligand binding (Fig. 8D). The solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) gradually decreased and stabilized after 55 ns, indicating that the protein tightly encapsulated the ligand, hence reinforcing the stability of the complex (Fig. 8E). Furthermore, the Root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) analysis showed that the fluctuation at the residue level was small, further corroborating the structural stiffness of the complex (Fig. 8F). Hydrogen bond analysis revealed that the quantity of hydrogen bonds between ELOVL1 and Pictilisib maintained reasonably constant after 55 ns, indicating a stable hydrogen bond interaction between the protein and ligand (Fig. 8G).

Based on the MDS results from the mmPBSA analysis, we observed that PHE-250 exhibited a significant negative energy peak during the simulation, which may indicate that it plays an important role in the structure or has undergone a significant conformational change (Fig. 8H). Moreover, the overall binding free energy (ΔTOTAL = − 48.04 kcal/mol) was negative, suggesting a stable and favorable interaction between the protein and ligand. The dominant contributors to this stability were the van der Waals interactions (ΔVDWAALS = − 64.4 kcal/mol) and gas phase energy (ΔGGAS = − 67.16 kcal/mol), indicating strong attractive forces. While the polar solvation energy (ΔEPB = 24.09 kcal/mol) and solvation total energy (ΔGSOLV = 19.12 kcal/mol) had a slightly unfavorable impact on the binding, the nonpolar solvation energy (ΔENPOLAR = − 4.98 kcal/mol) supported the binding due to hydrophobic effects (Fig. 8I).

To enhance the visualization of the conformational energy landscape, we constructed a free energy landscape utilizing RMSD and Rg as reaction coordinates. The results showed that the global minimum energy occurred at RMSD = 0.44 nm and Rg = 2.07 nm, signifying the thermodynamically most stable configuration of the complex (Fig. 8J). The deep blue basin in the free energy landscape represents a low-energy conformational state, which enhances the structural stability of the ligand-receptor combination. In summary, our results suggest that ELOVL1 is a crucial binding partner of Pictilisib and assumes a stable, thermodynamically favorable conformation during ligand binding.

Finally, we assessed the impact of Pictilisib on critical proteins within the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway through Western blot analysis. We found that Pictilisib treatment induced a substantial decline in the phosphorylation of PI3K, AKT, and mTOR, while the total protein expression of these markers remained unchanged (Fig. 8K). Importantly, in the ELOVL1 knockout + Pictilisib treatment group, phosphorylation levels of PI3K, AKT, and mTOR were further diminished, reaching their lowest levels (Fig. 8K).

To investigate the potential mechanism underlying ELOVL1-mediated iCCA progression, we first applied a correlation analysis between ELOVL1 and key ER stress indicators using the GSE107943 dataset. The findings indicated a favorable connection between ELOVL1 expression and the ER stress markers (Fig. 9A), providing important bioinformatics support for our subsequent experimental investigations.

Next, we explored the influence of ELOVL1 expression on ER stress through multiple experimental techniques. Firstly, we assessed the role of ELOVL1 in ER stress by measuring the expression levels of key ER stress indicators in iCCA cells through Western blotting. The findings demonstrated that ELOVL1 knockdown attributed to reduced expression of these markers, while ELOVL1 overexpression resulted in increased expression levels (Fig. 9B). These findings were further validated by qPCR, which showed reduced mRNA levels of indicators in ELOVL1 knockdown cells, and elevated mRNA levels in ELOVL1 overexpressing cells (Fig. 9C).

Additionally, immunofluorescence analysis revealed consistent trends with ER Tracker staining and Ca²⁺ concentration (Fig. 9D). In ELOVL1 overexpressing cells, ER Tracker fluorescence intensity was significantly increased, correlating with elevated intracellular Ca²⁺ levels, both of which are indicative of enhanced ER stress. These trends were further confirmed by flow cytometry (Fig. 9E).

Moreover, to further investigate cellular morphology, transmission electron microscopy was conducted. In ELOVL1 overexpressing cells, ER stress-associated features such as ER swelling and membrane accumulation were markedly increased in comparison to the vector group (Fig. 9F). Crucially, to determine whether the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway is causally responsible for ELOVL1-induced ER stress, we performed pharmacological rescue experiments. We treated ELOVL1-overexpressing iCCA cells with the specific PI3K inhibitor, pictilisib. Notably, the pharmacological inhibition of PI3K substantially attenuated the upregulation of key ER stress markers (GRP78, CHOP, and ATF4) that was initially induced by ELOVL1 overexpression (Fig. 9G). These rescue results provide direct quantitative evidence that the PI3K/AKT/mTOR cascade acts as a critical functional mediator bridging ELOVL1 and the exacerbation of ER stress in iCCA.

Discussion

ELOVL1 is a crucial fatty acid elongase that facilitates the production of VLCFAs, playing an essential role in lipid metabolism [10]. Although ELOVL1 has demonstrated abnormal expression in various cancer types, its involvement in intrahepatic iCCA remains underexplored. Through the screening of ERSRGs, ELOVL1 has emerged as a potential factor in the initiation and progression of iCCA. ER stress is pivotal in modulating tumor cell survival, proliferation, and adaptation to stress conditions [15]. It is hypothesized that ELOVL1 may influence the function of the ER, thereby affecting the response to environmental stressors and sustaining the ability of tumor cells to proliferate. While research on ELOVL1 in iCCA is still in its infancy, initial findings suggest that this gene may represent a viable therapeutic target, offering new opportunities for the diagnosis and treatment of iCCA.

This study demonstrates that ELOVL1 expression is markedly elevated in iCCA tissues and is strongly correlated with aggressive tumor characteristics, comprising enhanced proliferation, migration, and invasion. Mechanistically, ELOVL1 promotes iCCA progression by stimulating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, essential for controlling cell survival, metabolism, and growth. Furthermore, ELOVL1 exacerbates ERS, a condition that aids tumor cells in adapting to hostile environments, and is frequently observed in cancers with high metabolic demands. Our results underscore the crucial function of ELOVL1 in iCCA tumorigenesis and its potential as a prognostic and therapeutic biomarker, offering promising avenues for improving prognosis and treatment strategies in iCCA patients (Fig. 10).

Schematic diagram of the function and mechanism of ELOVL1 in iCCA. (Right) In iCCA cells, ELOVL1 overexpression mediates iCCA progression by activating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway and promoting endoplasmic reticulum stress. (Left) ELOVL1 knockdown inhibits the function of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway and suppresses endoplasmic reticulum stress, ultimately inhibiting tumor growth

ELOVL1, a fatty acid elongase, is intimately linked to the onset and advancement of several cancer types. Qin et al. found that ELOVL1 enhances the migration, invasion, and metastasis of hepatocellular carcinoma cells by triggering the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, underscoring its efficacy as a treatment option for liver cancer [12]. Similarly, Pretto et al. identified ELOVL1 as a crucial metabolic regulator for maintaining T cell effector activity and memory phenotype. Their CRISPR/Cas9 screening and single-cell RNA sequencing revealed that ELOVL1-knockout T cells, when subjected to anti-PD-1 therapy, were effective in treating pancreatic cancer and melanoma. This effect was driven by enhanced T cell mitochondrial function and fatty acid oxidation through SREBP2 signaling, thereby improving their ability to tolerate the tumor microenvironment [14]. These findings underscore ELOVL1’s potential in immunotherapy. Moreover, ELOVL1 is significantly upregulated in triple-negative BC, where it modulates fatty acid metabolism, particularly in pathways related to very long-chain fatty acid elongation, making it a promising target for triple-negative BC therapy [37]. In CRC, Hama et al. observed that ELOVL1 regulates lipid metabolism by promoting the accumulation of VLCFAs in both triglyceride and free fatty acid forms [11]. A similar mechanism was also noted in endometrial carcinoma [38]. Nonetheless, the involvement of ELOVL1 in iCCA carcinogenesis remains to be examined. This study is the first to demonstrate that ELOVL1 contributes to iCCA progression by stimulating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway and promoting ER stress.

The PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway is an essential regulator of cellular growth, survival, and metabolism. It is essential for maintaining cellular homeostasis by controlling activities including protein synthesis, autophagy, and metabolism [39–41]. Dysregulation of this pathway is commonly detected in various cancers and is linked to tumorigenesis and resistance to therapy [17, 42]. Our findings revealed that knockdown of ELOVL1 markedly suppressed the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway in iCCA, evidenced by reduced levels of p-PI3K, p-AKT, and p-mTOR. On the other hand, overexpression of ELOVL1 activated this pathway. While the precise upstream events linking a lipid elongase to kinase activation remain to be fully elucidated, established lipid biology provides a highly plausible mechanistic framework. ELOVL1 canonically synthesizes VLCFAs, which are critical precursors for sphingolipids [43]. These sphingolipids actively participate in the assembly of membrane lipid rafts, the specialized microdomains that serve as pivotal signaling hubs. Extensive literature suggests that lipid raft remodeling can facilitate the clustering and activation of upstream receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs), which may subsequently recruit and hyperactivate the downstream PI3K/AKT cascade [44, 45]. Therefore, we postulate that ELOVL1-driven lipid metabolism might stimulate PI3K signaling in iCCA through the structural modulation of these membrane microdomains.

Moreover, this pathway is closely intertwined with ER stress [20, 46]. Under normal conditions, the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway helps maintain ER homeostasis by regulating protein folding and cellular stress responses. However, when ER stress is exacerbated, the pathway is further activated, establishing a feedback loop that supports tumor cell viability in stressful environments. We investigated the ER stress phenotype and found that ELOVL1 knockdown reduced the expression of critical ER stress indicators, indicating that reducing ELOVL1 expression can alleviate ER stress. In contrast, overexpression of ELOVL1 elevated the levels of GRP78, CHOP, and ATF4. Crucially, to establish a direct causal relationship between these signaling events, we performed pharmacological rescue experiments. When ELOVL1 overexpression was combined with the specific PI3K inhibitor pictilisib, we detected a substantial reduction in GRP78, CHOP, and ATF4 expression compared to that seen with ELOVL1 overexpression alone. These results strongly indicate that the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway acts as a functional mediator for ELOVL1-induced ER stress. Targeting ELOVL1 could provide a novel therapeutic strategy by modulating cancer cell metabolism and stress responses simultaneously in iCCA.

Our study reveals significant insights into the potential of ELOVL1 as a therapeutic target for iCCA. However, there are several limitations that should be considered. First, although we have validated the prognostic value of ELOVL1 and its correlation with clinicopathological features using a large-scale public cohort, these findings have not yet been validated in an independent clinical cohort from our own institution. Second, this study primarily relied on cell lines and animal models, which, while offering valuable mechanistic insights, cannot entirely capture the intricacies of human cancers. Third, regarding the molecular mechanism, although our rescue experiments with pictilisib indicate that the PI3K pathway functionally mediates ELOVL1-induced ER stress, the precise upstream events linking this lipid elongase to kinase activation remain to be fully elucidated. We postulate that ELOVL1-driven sphingolipid metabolism might influence PI3K signaling through the potential modulation of membrane lipid rafts and upstream RTKs [43–45, 47]; however, definitively identifying the exact lipid intermediates requires comprehensive lipidomic and membrane fractionation analyses. Future research addressing these domains through prospective clinical validations, patient-derived samples, and advanced lipidomics will be essential to comprehensively enhance our understanding of ELOVL1’s function in iCCA and to potentially formulate more targeted and efficacious therapeutic strategies.
