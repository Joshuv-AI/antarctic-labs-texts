The effect of narrative medicine-based education on academic performance and humanistic communication in residency training: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Dongjun Dai, Yuzhi Fan, Wenbao Zhang, Qichun Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOur study aims to assess the effect of narrative medicine education on academic performance and humanistic communication. We performed a PRISMA guideline-based systematic review and meta-analysis. The literature was searched in PubMed, Embase, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, VIP database and Wanfang database up to January 26, 2026. Two independent reviewers performed the study selection, data extraction and quality assessment. We included 20 studies with 1,364 residents. Meta-analyses showed that compared with lecture-based learning (LBL), the application of narrative medicine was associated with significantly improved procedural skill (SMD = 0.98, 95% CI 0.42 to 1.53), humanistic care ability (SMD = 2.62, 95% CI 0.26 to 4.99), empathy ability (SMD = 1.51, 95% CI 0.76 to 2.26), and doctor-patient communication (SMD = 2.19, 95% CI 0.21 to 4.17). Our study also showed that residents were more satisfied with the narrative medicine education than LBL (OR = 6.67, 95% CI 1.74 to 25.51). In conclusion, this is the first meta-analysis to show that narrative medicine may potentially improve the procedural skills and humanistic communication compared with LBL. However, given the substantial heterogeneity and low-certainty evidence, our findings are insufficient for strong practice recommendations. Further high-quality studies are needed to validate these findings. Introduction

The humanities, encompassing literature, creative writing, history, and visual arts, have increasingly been integrated into medical education1. Improvements in humanistic qualities play a positive role in reshaping harmonious doctor‒patient relationships2. Although medical humanities are nominally included in the general medical education blueprint, conventional lecture-based learning (LBL) teaching mainly focuses on textbook content and delivers knowledge, in which residents passively receive theoretical knowledge without targeted humanistic guidance and reflective training, and lacks formal and validated assessment of humanistic outcomes. Recently, a series of studies implemented narrative medicine-based education for medical residents and compared its effects with LBL3,4. A range of outcomes were evaluated in these studies using validated measurement tools, including academic performance, humanistic care, empathy ability, and doctor-patient communication skills. However, there is currently no related meta-analysis to summarize these findings and provide high-quality quantitative evidence. The objective of our study is to perform a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the effect of narrative medicine education on academic performance and humanistic communication when compared with LBL.

Narrative medicine is an approach to the use of narrative to collect and interpret information on the patient’s experience of illness5. It encourages physicians to focus on the real feelings of patients about their illnesses rather than the laboratory and imaging results. Narrative medicine might help to better understand patient experiences, foster empathy, enhance medical service quality and cultivate medical humanities6,7. Narrative medicine is widely integrated into medical education through a combination of reading, reflection, storytelling and writing exercises8.

Residency is a uniquely formative period in medical training. During residency, trainees start to frequently interact with patients. It is essential to foster residents’ humanistic communication and compassion alongside the acquisition of academic knowledge and skills. Studies have shown that residents often experience diminished self-esteem and declining empathy9,10. Thus, it is necessary to improve relevant teaching methods to enhance residents’ humanistic communication skills and empathy during clinical training. Accordingly, medical residents were selected as the research subjects in the present systematic review and meta-analysis.

Methods

Literature collection

We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines11(Supplementary Data 1). Our study was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (CRD420261323721). The literature search was conducted up to January 26, 2026 across PubMed, Embase, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, VIP database and Wanfang database. There was no language restriction in literature screening.

Search strategy and the inclusion and exclusion criteria

The population, intervention, comparison, outcome, study design (PICOS) framework was used to define the study eligibility. The detailed description of the PICOS framework of current study and the search strategy were listed in Supplementary Data 2. We designed the inclusion criteria as follows: (1) population: postgraduate medical residents with any specialties; (2) intervention: implementation of narrative medicine education as an intervention; (3) comparison: use of traditional teacher-centered LBL; (4) outcome: the study should provide post-intervention outcome data from examinations or questionnaire surveys for academic performance and humanistic communication ; no limitation was placed on the measurement scales used; (5) study design: randomized controlled study. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) studies enrolling undergraduate students or non-clinical medical residents; (2) studies not employing narrative medicine as an intervention; (3) studies without a parallel control group; (4) studies with no reported outcome measures; (5) non-randomized studies; (6) conference abstracts; (7) published systematic reviews.

Literature screening and study quality evaluation

We performed the literature screening through the “PRISMA 2020 flow diagram for new systematic reviews which included searches of databases and registers only”11. Two independent reviewers (DD and YF) screened the literature. After removing the duplicate records and reading the titles and abstracts of the records, potential full-text papers were collected for further assessment under the eligibility criteria. Studies were then finally selected. The quality of each included study was evaluated by two independent reviewers (DD and YF) through Cochrane risk-of-bias tool of randomized trials12, which comprised 7 domains: randomization sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting and other bias. Each domain was classified as “low risk”, “high risk”, or “unclear”. Discrepancies during literature screening and quality evaluation were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (QW).

The certainty of evidence was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework13, which assesses five core domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. As defined in the GRADE Handbook, evidence certainty is categorized into four levels: high, moderate, low, and very low. We initially classified evidence from randomized controlled trials as high certainty, then downgraded it by one level for serious limitations and two levels for very serious limitations across the five GRADE domains.

Data extraction

Two independent investigators (DD and YF) extracted the following items from the selected studies: first author/year, department, sample size, baseline characteristics matched between groups, intervention methods, intervention duration and outcome measures. The outcomes included results from post learning examinations, such as theoretical knowledge and procedural skill; and results from questionnaire surveys such as humanistic care ability, empathy, doctor-patient communication skill, and residents’ satisfaction. There were no assumptions made about any missing or unclear information.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed by ‘meta’ package in R 4.1.1 software (http://www.R-project.org). Pooled SMD and 95%CI for continuous outcomes were computed using the “metacont” function with the Hedges’ g approach14. Pooled OR and 95%CI for binary outcomes were calculated using the “metabin” function with the restricted maximum likelihood method15. The pooled results of meta-analyses were visualized by forest plots. Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 test. The random-effects model was applied in our meta-analyses as random-effects and fix-effects model present similar results when there is low heterogeneity16. Heterogeneity was assessed by sensitivity analysis using the metainf function. Subgroup analyses were used to detect heterogeneity for outcomes including at least 10 studies in a meta-analysis. Publication bias was evaluated by Egger’s test using “metabias” function17. If significant publication bias was detected, the trim-and-fill method was applied to adjust the effect estimate. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Literature screening

As shown in Fig. 1, we collected 2,960 records from PubMed, Embase, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, VIP database and Wanfang database. After removing 315 duplicated studies and reading titles and abstract to screen, we retrieved 119 full-text papers. Among them, we further excluded 1 study with wrong comparison, 5 studies with no information of outcome data, 12 non-residents studies, 23 non-randomized controlled studies, 24 studies that did not use narrative medicine as an intervention and 34 review studies. Finally, there were 20 randomized controlled studies3,4,18–35 included in our meta-analysis.

PRISMA flow chart for literature search.

Main characteristics of included studies

Table 1 describes the main characteristics of each included study. All included studies were conducted in China. There were 18 studies18–35 in Chinese and 2 studies3,4 in English. These studies were published from 2019 to 2025. Totally 1,364 residents (narrative medicine group = 682, LBL = 682) were included in our study. The studies originated from departments of internal medicine (n = 7), surgery (n = 6), pediatrics (n = 3), dentistry (n = 2), emergency medicine (n = 1), and ultrasound (n = 1). There were 164,19–21,23–27,29–35 studies used narrative medicine education alone as an intervention while there were 43,18,22,28 studies combined narrative medicine and other innovative teaching method, including case-based learning, evidence-based learning and 3D print. Fifteen4,19,20,22–29,31,33–35 studies reported the duration of intervention which ranged from 1 to 8 months. There were 9 studies3,4,18,21,23,27,28,31,34 measured the theoretical knowledge scores, 6 studies3,18,23,27,31,34 measured procedural skill scores, 3 studies18,22,24 evaluated humanistic care ability, 14 studies19–21,23–29,31–33,35 evaluated empathy ability, 6 studies20,27–29,32 evaluated doctor-patient communication skill, and 6 studies3,22,26,27,30,35 reported residents’ satisfaction. Humanistic care ability was primarily evaluated using the Caring Ability Inventory (CAI)36,37 (2 out of 3 studies) or Mini-Clinical Evaluation Exercise (Mini-CEX)38. Empathy ability was mainly assessed with the Jefferson Scale of Physician Empathy (JSPE)39 (12 out of 14 studies) and self-made scale. Doctor-patient communication skills were predominantly measured by the SEGUE framework40 (3 out of 6 studies) and other validated scales that met reliability and validity testing.

All studies reported complete outcome. All articles reported complete outcome data and were free of selective reporting and other biases.

Effects of narrative medicine on academic performance

As shown in Table 2 and Fig. 3, compared with LBL, the narrative medicine education was associated with the improvement of procedural skill scores (6 studies with 147 residents in narrative medicine group and 150 in LBL group, standardized mean differences [SMD] = 0.98, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.42 to 1.53, P = 0.001; I2 = 75.9%) but not associated with the theoretical knowledge scores (9 studies with 321 residents in narrative medicine group and 322 in LBL group, SMD = 1.46, 95% CI − 0.72 to 3.64, P = 0.19; I2 = 95.3%). Considering the high heterogeneity, sensitivity analysis was performed; however, no source of heterogeneity was identified (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1). The Egger’s tests identified no publication bias for academic performance outcomes (P > 0.05).

Effects of narrative medicine on humanistic communication

As shown in Table 2 and Fig. 4, our meta-analyses showed that narrative medicine education was associated with improved humanistic care ability (3 studies with 128 residents in narrative medicine group and 128 in LBL group, SMD = 2.62, 95% CI 0.26 to 4.99, P = 0.03; I2 = 96.6%), empathy ability (14 studies with 408 residents in narrative medicine group and 410 in LBL group, SMD = 1.51, 95% CI 0.76 to 2.26, P < 0.001; I2 = 88.9%), and doctor-patient communication (7 studies with 197 residents in narrative medicine group and 196 in LBL group, SMD = 2.19, 95% CI 0.21 to 4.17, P = 0.03; I2 = 94.3%). The sensitivity analyses found that the heterogeneity decreased to a non-significant level after omitting the relevant study in humanistic care ability (I2 = 35.0% after omitting one study24, Supplementary Table 2), and doctor-patient communication (I2 = 27.3% after omitting one study30, Supplementary Table 2). The new pooled results remained significant for them (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 2). We could not find the heterogeneity source in empathy ability (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 2). Therefore, we further performed subgroup analyses by different departments, intervention duration and intervention method. We found that heterogeneity decreased to a non-significant level in dentistry (I2 = 0%) and pediatrics departments (I2 = 0%), and in residents who received training for ≥ 3 months (I2 = 42%). And the new pooled results remained as before (Supplementary Fig. 2). The Egger’s tests identified significant publication bias in the meta-analyses of humanistic care ability and empathy ability (P < 0.05). After the application of trim-and-fill method, the publication bias was eliminated in empathy ability while the narrative medicine education still outperformed LBL (SMD = 1.01, 95% CI 0.05 to 1.96, Table 2). However, the results of Egger’s tests for humanistic care ability remained unchanged (Table 2).

Effects of narrative medicine on residents’ satisfaction

Our meta-analysis showed that residents were more satisfied with the narrative medicine education than LBL (6 studies with 192 residents in narrative medicine group and 192 in LBL group, odds ratio [OR] = 6.67, 95% CI 1.74 to 25.51, P < 0.001; I2 = 50.9%, Supplementary Fig. 3). Sensitivity analysis found that after omitting one study, there was no heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, Supplementary Table 2) while the new pooled results remained significant (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 2). As shown in Table 2, the Egger’s tests found significant publication bias in the meta-analysis of residents’ satisfaction (P = 0.023). After trim-and-fill adjustment, the publication bias was eliminated (P = 0.888); however, no significant difference in satisfaction was observed between the narrative medicine education and control group (P = 0.053).

GRADE assessment of current meta-analysis

As shown in Supplementary Table 3, the certainty of evidence for all meta-analyses was rated as very low using the GRADE approach. This was mainly attributed to the widespread methodological limitations (lack of randomization, allocation concealment, and blinding) in the included studies and the substantial heterogeneity (I2 > 50%) across meta-analyses, alongside imprecision and publication bias for some outcomes.

Discussion

Clinical patients often bear significant physical and psychological burdens. For physicians, in addition to providing professional treatment, humanistic care and effective communication with patients are also crucial. Empathy toward patients can also help reduce the risk of burnout among physicians41. Humanistic communication has received increasing attention in medical education, and narrative medicine serves as a relevant teaching approach, which has been widely applied42. However, no previous meta-analysis has summarized the effect of narrative medicine education on academic performance and humanistic communication in residency training. Our meta-analyses found that narrative medicine education was associated with improved procedural skill but not with theoretical knowledge. The meta-analyses also showed that narrative medicine education was associated with improved humanistic care ability, empathy ability, doctor-patient communication, and residents’ satisfaction. Our study provides pooled quantitative evidence for narrative medicine in medical education.

Narrative medicine education encompasses a wide range of practices, from engaging with literature, reflective writing, parallel medical record writing, to visual and performing arts—any storytelling medium may be used43,44. The included studies of our meta-analysis adopted these approaches to teach narrative medicine. In addition, some studies used simulation methods, such as having students role-play in doctor-patient communication, or introducing standardized patients for training3,19,24,26,29,31,35. These educational strategies are inherently practice-oriented, focusing on applied clinical skills, such as empathetic communication, history-taking, and reflective reasoning rather than theoretical memorization of knowledge. This might be a reason why narrative medicine education in our meta-analyses was found to improve residents’ practical performance but not lead to improvements in theoretical knowledge scores. Studies found that humanistic care ability and empathy were independently associated with the narrative competence45,46. A randomized controlled trial including 85 nursing students showed that after training with narrative medicine, empathy and humanistic care ability were better than in the control group47. A quasi-experimental study including 135 medical interns found that the empathy scores were higher in the experimental group than in the control group48. A scoping review showed that narrative medicine can effectively improve humanistic care ability and empathy across a range of clinical and educational settings49. Moreover, a meta-analysis including 41 studies showed that narrative pedagogy significantly enhances nursing students’ humanistic care ability and empathy compared with traditional teaching methods50. Our study provides new evidence of the effect of narrative medicine on the humanistic care ability and empathy in medical residents. Physician empathy51 and humanistic caring ability52 were widely recognized to influence the doctor–patient relationship. Empathy enables medical physicians to deeply perceive patients’ emotional needs while humanistic care ability guides them to translate this perception into respectful, patient-centered communication behaviors53. Our meta-analysis not surprisingly found that narrative medicine education also contributes to the improvement of doctor-patient communication.

Our study has certain strengths. This is the first meta-analysis that provides new evidence for the effect of narrative medicine on the humanistic communication in residents. In addition, our study included the randomized controlled studies which robustly test the impact of narrative pedagogy on medical residents. There are certain limitations in our study as well. First, there was considerable heterogeneity among our meta-analyses, particularly in meta-analyses of continuous data. Sensitivity analysis and subgroup analysis failed to identify the source of heterogeneity in many pooled results. This heterogeneity may be attributed to several factors: the included studies employed highly variable narrative medicine practices, with core approaches including literature reading and parallel medical record writing, while some also integrated supplementary simulation elements (e.g., doctor-patient role-play, standardized patients) as part of multi-stage training. Thus, developing standardized, unified narrative medicine curricula is important for future research to improve study comparability and generalizability. Additional sources of heterogeneity include variations in intervention duration and assessment tools (e.g., examinations vs. validated scales). Despite the relatively high heterogeneity, the random-effects meta-analyses yielded significant results in most comparisons. Second, the included studies did not report details of the allocation concealment and blinding processes, which might cause potential risk of selection bias and performance bias. Third, publication bias was detected in some of our meta-analyses, indicating that the current meta-analysis might be influenced by the selective publication of study findings. Correction using the trim-and-fill method led to reduced effect sizes and increased heterogeneity in the meta-analyses of empathy ability and residents’ satisfaction, which are common phenomena when adjusting for publication bias)54. Nevertheless, the adjusted results still represent a substantial educational benefit (SMD ≥ 0.8) 54. Fourth, although our search strategy was guided by the PRIMSA statement and PICOS framework, we could not exclude the possibility of missing relevant studies. Fifth, all included studies were conducted in China. Narrative medicine has been vigorously promoted and formally incorporated into undergraduate, postgraduate, and residency training in China55,56. Narrative medicine education has been recommended for standardized curriculum design, multidisciplinary teaching teams, and systematic integration across the entire medical education continuum53, which may account for the large number of eligible studies conducted among Chinese residents. However, this may limit the generalizability of our findings to other countries or regions with different medical education systems and cultural contexts.

In conclusion, our meta-analysis shows that narrative medicine education may potentially improve the procedural skill, humanistic care ability, empathy ability, and doctor-patient communication of medical residents. Our meta-analysis provides quantitative evidence that narrative medicine education may improve academic performance and humanistic communication skills. Nevertheless, given the limitations of substantial heterogeneity and low-certainty evidence, our findings are insufficient for strong practice recommendations. Further well-designed randomized controlled studies are required to validate the educational value of narrative medicine in residents.
