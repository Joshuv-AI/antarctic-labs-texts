Quantitative measurement of anti-interferon-gamma autoantibodies for routine laboratory implementation
Authors: Truong Hung Cuong, Varis Manbenmad, Pattaraporn Srisai, Chanchai Hongsa, Thanyaporn Wisedsuk, Anusorn Krungmee, Arpa Surapaitoon, Punnapat Nawawishkarun, Yingrit Chantarasuk, Supranee Phanthanawiboon, Arnone Nithichanon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAnti-interferon-γ autoantibodies (AIGAs) are the immunological hallmark of adult-onset immunodeficiency (AOID) syndrome, predominantly affecting southeast Asian adults and leading to severe, recurrent opportunistic infections. Current AIGA detection relies on semi-quantitative methods that lack standardization, limiting utility for clinical monitoring and multi-center comparisons. We developed and analytically validated a quantitative inhibitory ELISA reporting half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50). The assay demonstrated excellent linearity across log10 AIGA concentrations of 2.51–4.92 (corresponding to 337–85,316 RU/mL). The quantifiable range could be extended down to 93 RU/mL (relative to the internal quality-control [IQC] plasma pools). The inter-assay GCV% was 23.5%–30.7% across all IQC AIGA-positive levels. Levey-Jennings monitoring confirmed stable performance of a single set of reagents throughout the 8-month clinical service period. The quantitative IC50 assay discriminated active cases from inactive with 82.86% sensitivity with 89.29% specificity versus a semi-quantitative reference method (72.86% sensitivity and 85.71% specificity). This quantitative ELISA provides reliable, precise AIGA measurement with a practical internal quality-control framework, supporting AIGA testing for routine clinical laboratory implementation in AOID management.Keywords: autoimmune, autoantibodies, quantitative immunoassay, assay validation, adult-onset immunodeficiency, quality management

Introduction

Anti-interferon-γ autoantibodies (AIGAs) are a major immunological driver of adult-onset immunodeficiency (AOID) syndrome, particularly in southeast Asian populations.1 These IgG autoantibodies bind functionally critical epitopes on interferon-γ (IFN-γ) disrupting macrophage activation and intracellular pathogen control.2 A study of patients from Thailand and Taiwan identified IgG1 and IgG4 as the predominant AIGA isotypes.3 Clinically, AOID manifests as recurrent, severe, and frequently disseminated opportunistic infections, most notably by nontuberculous Mycobacterium (NTM), Salmonella, Cryptococcus, and other intracellular pathogens.4 One systematic review reported that 798 of 810 (98.5%) AIGA-positive patients were Asian,3 and a further study found that 493 of 502 (98.2%) originated from Southeast Asia.5

The epidemiology of AIGA-associated AOID has become better understood over the past two decades. Since the condition was first described in 2004, over 600 cases have been diagnosed globally, with the vast majority from Southeast Asia and southern China.6 NTM is the most frequent pathogen (55.69%), followed by Talaromyces marneffei (26.98%), Salmonella spp. (12.43%), and varicella-zoster virus (9.57%).7 A 20-year literature analysis of 502 patients confirmed NTM as the predominant pathogen in 77.49% of cases, with lymph-node involvement in 83.7% and pulmonary involvement in 60.6% of cases.5 Clinical manifestations extend beyond lymphadenopathy to encompass cutaneous opportunistic infections caused by mycobacteria and fungi,8 pustular skin reactions,9 and tracheobronchial lesions,10 which frequently complicate diagnosis and are associated with delayed recognition of the underlying immunodeficiency.11

Despite growing recognition of the syndrome, laboratory diagnosis of AOID remains challenging. To date, detection of AIGAs has largely relied on research-grade enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) or semi-quantitative lateral flow methods, which lack standardization, traceability, and inter-laboratory comparability. Several assay formats are used to detect AIGAs, including indirect ELISA,4,12 inhibitory ELISA,12 flow cytometry-based functional assays,13 and rapid immunochromatographic tests.14 Each approach has strengths and limitations. Flow cytometry is often considered a useful reference method but requires specialized equipment and expertise, limiting accessibility. Indirect ELISA is technically straightforward but susceptible to nonspecific binding, leading to false positives in populations with high background reactivity to environmental antigens.12 The inhibitory ELISA quantifies AIGAs by their capacity to sequester free IFN-γ in solution, with the remaining unbound IFN-γ subsequently detected by a standard sandwich ELISA. This format, established in our previous publication,12 is distinct from both indirect ELISA formats and functional neutralization assays that measure biological IFN-γ blockade in cellular systems. Notably, AIGA levels measured by inhibitory ELISA correlate with IFN-γ-neutralizing activity confirmed by flow cytometry.12 It has shown high clinical sensitivity and specificity for AOID and utility for detecting active disease; however, most implementations remain semi-quantitative, reporting titers rather than absolute concentrations, and standardized operating procedures are lacking. The absence of rigorously validated quantitative methods, together with limited availability of internal quality-control (IQC) materials and harmonized quality management practices, constrain inter-laboratory comparability and longitudinal patient monitoring. Recently, novel approaches including a QuantiFERON-based ELISA platform15 and a homogeneous bioluminescent inhibition immunoassay16 have been described for AIGA detection, underscoring the need for standardized, quantitative assay formats to enable inter-laboratory comparability.

The present study aimed to develop and analytically validate a standardized, quantitative AIGA assay suitable for clinical laboratory implementation under a formal quality-management framework. Specifically, we sought to establish a quantitative inhibitory ELISA and to evaluate key performance characteristics including linearity, limits of quantification, inter-assay precision, and agreement between semi-quantitative and quantitative assays. We describe a practical internal quality-control framework for monitoring our assay and compare its performance with a previously published semi-quantitative reference method. These advances are intended to support wider, more reliable deployment of AIGA testing in clinical laboratories.

Results

Optimization of IFN-γ detection range for inhibitory ELISA and appropriate cut-off point for anti-IFN-γ in-house calibration material

To establish the optimal linear detection range for the inhibitory ELISA, standard curves were generated using recombinant IFN-γ at different concentrations. The initial standard curve covering a wide range of 0–600 pg/mL demonstrated suboptimal linearity, with a coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.8620 (Figure 1A). When narrower concentration ranges were evaluated, improved linearity was observed. Specifically, the range of 0–300 pg/mL showed a markedly higher linear correlation with an R2 value of 0.9964 (Figure 1B). Further narrowing the range to 0–150 pg/mL resulted in a slightly higher R2 value of 0.9978 as shown (Figure 1C).

Based on these findings, 0–300 pg/mL IFN-γ was selected as the standard concentration range for subsequent inhibition assays, as it provided an optimal balance between linearity and a sufficiently broad detection range. To assess assay reproducibility, each IQC level was measured once daily for 20 consecutive days. The acceptable R2 threshold was determined using the mean minus three standard deviations (mean, 3 S.D.) as the cut-off criterion. Using this approach, the calculated cut-off value for R2 was 0.972. Most experimental results were above this cut-off value, with only one result equal to the threshold (Figure 1D), indicating good reproducibility and reliability of the assay.

We prepared plasma controls; four concentrations of multi-donor composite positive reference material and one negative reference material. The characteristics of these reference materials are shown in Table 1. Briefly, the negative reference material was prepared from plasma of four healthy donors. Each positive reference material was prepared from five patients. This approach of pooling plasma samples follows established methods for in-house immunoassay quality control.17,18 All five patients contributing to the low-positive reference pool were in the inactive category. Among these, three samples showed AIGA titers between 100 and 1,000 and two had titers below 100. The medium-positive reference pool was derived from four inactive cases and one patient with signs of infection, all with AIGA titers at 100–1,000. The high-positive reference material contained plasma samples from one inactive case and four patients with signs of infection, all with AIGA titers at 5,000–10,000. Lastly, all five patients donating plasma to the very-high-positive reference material had signs of infection and AIGA titers at 10,000–100,000. These reference materials were quantified using inhibitory ELISA at various dilutions, as shown in Figure 1E. In this study, the percentage inhibition (% inhibition) was evaluated across different levels of reference materials. These results indicate that a 50% inhibition threshold represents the most appropriate cut-off point for distinguishing between positive reference materials at different levels and the negative reference material.

Calibration and analytical range determination for AIGA quantification by inhibitory ELISA

Each of the prepared plasma pools representing four AIGA concentration levels (low, medium, high, and very-high) was analyzed on 20 consecutive days. AIGA could not be detected in any negative plasma sample. After IC50 calculation for positive plasma samples, concentrations with their 95% confidence intervals (in RU/mL) are shown in Figure 2A. We expected antibody activity to appear as an exponential curve. Therefore, AIGA concentrations were transformed into a log10 linearity scale as shown in Figure 2B. A calibration curve was constructed with four points of positive reference materials. Results of linear regression of the calibrator curve are shown in Figure 2C. The curve shows R2 = 0.9873, demonstrating its excellent fit to the calibration data (Figure 2C). The linearity range of log10 AIGA concentration is between 2.51 and 4.92 (converted to 337–85,316 RU/mL) (Figure 2C). The linear slope of our calibrator revealed calculated limit of detection (LOD) was 0.3406 (converted to 2.19 RU/mL), and calculated limit of quantification (LOQ) was 1.0321 (converted to 10.77 RU/mL) (Figure 2C).

Quantitative AIGA assay validation using four levels of positive reference material

Scatterplots show AIGA concentrations measured across plasma samples, each tested on 20 consecutive days with results shown arbitrary units per milliliter (RU/mL) (A), log10-transformed values (B), demonstrating the linearity between concentrations. Data from all four positive reference pools (C) (red shaded dots with error bars showing variation) were analyzed using linear regression to calculate the analytical detection limits. Negative and low-positive (diluted as indicated) control plasma samples were aliquoted into 20 subsamples and each tested in duplicate on the same day, CV% and GCV% were calculated (D).

An empirical verification of the LOQ was performed using a low-positive reference sample undiluted or at dilutions of 1:3, 1:10, and 1:30, separated into 20 aliquots and tested in duplicate on the same day. AIGAs were undetectable in the negative sample and the 1:30-diluted low-positive sample (Figure 2D). Log10 AIGA concentrations were 2.49 (314 RU/mL, coefficient of variation [CV%] = 18.9%, geometric coefficient of variation [GCV%] = 21.1%) for the undiluted sample, 1.97 (93 RU/mL, CV% = 11.4%, GCV% = 12.5%) for the 1:3 dilution, and 1.22 (22 RU/mL, CV% = 58.6%, GCV% = 105.0%) for the 1:10 dilution (Figure 2D). Applying the pre-specified precision criterion of CV% ≤ 20%, the empirically verified LOQ was set at 93 RU/mL (log10 1.97).

Because this empirical LOQ (93 RU/mL) remains below the lower bound of the four-point linearity-verified range (337 RU/mL), we define two nested ranges: a linearity-verified quantitative range of 337–85,316 RU/mL, and an extended quantifiable range down to 93 RU/mL in which precision, but not independently re-confirmed linearity, has been established.

Selection of appropriate internal quality-control samples for routine service application

The prepared plasma samples at four concentration levels were assessed for their suitability as IQC samples. On the untransformed RU/mL scale, CV% was found to be 25.7%, 24.0%, 22.4%, and 22.0% for low to very-high concentrations, respectively (Figure 3A). Because immunoassay signal-response relationships are typically log-linear, arithmetic CV% on the linear scale is not an appropriate precision statistic in this context. GCV% values of log10 transformed RU/mL were 30.7%, 26.4%, 24.8%, and 23.5% for low-to-very-high level controls, respectively (Figure 3B). Log10 transformation was retained for subsequent Levey-Jennings (L-J) monitoring because it stabilizes variance and improves the normality of the control-value distribution, a requirement for valid Westgard-rule interpretation, rather than because it materially reduced CV% relative to the untransformed scale.

To monitor the quality of the assay over time, L-J charts were generated based on log10 RU/mL of low, medium, high, and very-high positive controls (Figure 3C). The negative control was omitted (Figures 2A and 2B). Data from the positive controls fell within acceptable control limits (Figure 3C). However, the twelfth data point from the low-positive control fell below −2 S.D., which represents a Westgard warning signal for random error (Figure 3C). These findings confirm that deployment of all four positive controls enables adequate assay monitoring, with consistent and stable results.

Implementation of IQC for monitoring assay performance for routine hospital laboratory use

Routine IQC was implemented to monitor the performance of the AIGA assay during regular diagnostic service. The negative reference material was used as the daily negative IQC sample, while the medium-positive reference material was used as the daily positive IQC sample. This selection was made because the medium-positive reference material is directly applicable to routine assays without requiring preprocessing. Both IQC samples were tested with four dilutions, 1:100, 1:300, 1:900 and 1:2,700 in duplicate on 20 consecutive days.

The negative IQC never exhibited IFN-γ inhibition. For the positive IQC at 1:900 dilution, % inhibition values remained stable throughout the monitoring period), with a mean of 80.7% and an SD of 6.83%, corresponding to an acceptable control range of 67.0%–94.4% (Figure 4A). Similarly, positive IQC results at 1:2,700 dilution also showed consistent performance, with a mean % inhibition of 35.1% and an SD of 5.99%, resulting in a control range of 23.0%–47.3% (Figure 4A). These results suggested that % inhibition values of positive controls at 1:900 and 1:2,700 dilution are appropriate, one upper and one lower covering the IC50 analysis. No out-of-control events, systematic shifts, or significant trends were observed during the monitoring period.

Log10 transformed AIGA concentrations showed minimal variability, with a mean value of 3.37 and an S.D. of 0.08 (Figure 4B). All measurements remained within the acceptable control limits (3.21–3.53), further supporting the stability and reproducibility of the assay during routine clinical diagnostic service. The reference-material plasma pools used for IQC were thawed in December 2024 and maintained at 4°C for the duration of the 8-month service period (March–December 2025, approximately 12 months from thaw to final use). Stability of the pooled reference material under these conditions was supported by the absence of drift, trend, or out-of-control signals in the L-J record over the monitoring period (Figure 4B).

Correlation and performance of the quantitative IC50 AIGA assay in comparison with the reference inhibitory titer

A significant positive correlation was observed between the IC50 AIGA level and the inhibitory AIGA titer measured by the previously published reference assay.12 Ninety-eight leftover plasma samples were each tested using both assays. The inactive cases (n = 28) were 75% female with average age at 59.6 (ranging from 28–66), while the active cases (n = 70) were 57.1% female with average age at 54.2 (ranging from 23–63) (Table S1). The IC50 AIGA level showed a correlation with the inhibitory AIGA titer, with a correlation coefficient of 0.702 and p < 0.0001 (Figure 5A). This is significant correlation, but the degree is very poor. To formally assess agreement between the quantitative IC50 assay and the semi-quantitative reference, Bland-Altman analysis was performed on Log10-transformed values from paired measurements. The mean bias was 3.70% (95% limits of agreement: −37.36%–44.76%), indicating no systematic difference between assays across the measurement range (Figure 5B).

Comparison of inhibitory AIGA assays and their association with disease activity

Comparison between previously published inhibitory AIGA semi-quantitative assay (Reference) and quantitative IC50 AIGA level (RU/mL) using linear regression for correlation of assays (A), agreement between the methods was assessed by Bland-Altman analysis of Log10-transformed paired values (B), and ROC curves (C) for discrimination between 70 active and 28 inactive disease providing area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity.

Semi-quantification of AIGA is useful not only for diagnosis but also for prediction of inactive versus active infection outcomes. Our previous publication reported a cut-off titer at ≥5,000 to identify patient with active infection.12,19 At the inhibitory titer ≥5,000 yielded 72.86% (95% CI: 61.5%–81.9%) sensitivity with 85.71% (95% CI: 68.5%–94.3%) specificity (Figure 5C). Whereas sensitivity of IC50 AIGA was 82.86% (95% CI: 72.0%–90.8%), and specificity was 89.29% (95% CI: 71.8%–97.7%) (Figure 5C). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the performance of IC50 AIGA levels in distinguishing active infection outcome. The AUC for the IC50 assay was 0.901, compared with 0.893 for the semi-quantitative reference assay (Figure 5C). The two ROC curves showed no statistically significant difference in AUC (p = 0.7864) (Figure 5C).

Discussion

The establishment of a standardized, quantitative AIGA assay with a validated internal quality-control protocol addresses a critical gap in AOID clinical management. The global burden of this immunodeficiency syndrome continues to grow, as documented in systematic reviews.7 Furthermore, AOID exhibits a recurrence rate exceeding 56% in longitudinal cohorts.1 Thus, harmonized laboratory approaches for antibody quantification become increasingly important. Our previous publication reported diagnostic efficacies of a semi-quantitative inhibitory ELISA at a cut-off >100 with 92.7% sensitivity and 100% specificity.12 High AIGA concentrations have been directly correlated with disease severity and reduced probability of disease control,19,20 as demonstrated in studies reporting that antibiotics alone were effective in only 40.5% of patients.1 The aim of this study was to develop and validate a quantitative inhibitory ELISA for AIGA measurement and to evaluate its long-term analytical performance under a formal internal quality-control framework. Because no commercial IQC products exist for AIGA testing, developing internally validated and stable controls is an essential component for ensuring accuracy, reproducibility, and laboratory standardization. The present findings provide important insights into the analytical performance of in-house AIGA-specific IQC materials.

The analytical characteristics of the assay were first assessed through the development of a standard curve using serially diluted recombinant IFN-γ. A concentration of 0–300 pg/mL IFN-γ was selected, as it falls within the biological response range and the linear detection range of the ELISA. At this concentration, the assay provides sufficiently strong signal intensity while maintaining an appropriate level of antibody-mediated neutralization for accurate measurement. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating the importance of optimizing cytokine concentrations within the linear dynamic range of immunoassays to ensure reliable detection of neutralizing antibodies.21,22,23 An R2 threshold at > 0.97 for acceptable linear detection range of IFN-γ was established to determine the reliability of the assay.

Inter-assay precision assessment revealed the characteristic immunoassay pattern in which proportional variability decreases as analyte concentration increases. In this study, the IC50 values were able to clearly differentiate between the different levels of positive control samples. Our AIGA concentrations expressed on the untransformed RU/mL scale exhibited substantial inter-assay variability (CV% approximately 20%–30%), and log10 transformed values achieved GCV% approximately 23%–30% across all four IQC concentration levels. Previous research on IC50 neutralizing antibody to SARS-CoV-2 validation suggested that GCV% should be less than 30% for a biological assay.24 This indicates that log10 transformation, while appropriate for stabilizing variance and improving normality for L-J and Westgard-rule interpretation,25,26,27 did not substantially reduce the overall inter-assay precision of the assay in absolute terms.

Implementation of Westgard rules in this study follows established practice for internal quality monitoring of immunoassays in clinical laboratory settings.28,29 The log10 transformation of AIGA concentration values prior to L-J chart analysis is consistent with validated approaches for immunoassays that exhibit proportional variance across the analytical range, as this transformation stabilizes variance and improves the normality of the data distribution.30,31 These analytical and quality management practices are aligned with ISO 15189 guidelines for medical laboratory accreditation, which require demonstration of assay precision and implementation of continuous quality monitoring procedures.32 On one occasion a 12s Westgard warning signal was observed when using a low-positive reference pool. However, this isolated event did not escalate to more serious rule violations and was therefore not indicative of systematic analytical instability. These findings confirm that the in-house IQC reagents are fit-for-purpose for routine internal quality monitoring, and that the assay performs reliably and consistently across clinical service runs.

Calibrators are primarily used to construct calibration curves to monitor assay performance and ensure the reliability of results.33 A well-defined and stable calibrator may be used as an IQC material for ELISA,34 chemiluminescent immunoassays,35 or turbidimetric immunoassay.36 The use of a mid-level AIGA-positive calibrator as IQC is advantageous because it falls within the optimal analytical measurement range where assay precision and sensitivity are highest.37 Use of this level also facilitates detection of analytical errors or shifts,28 and provides lower variability with better reproducibility during assay monitoring.38

Finally, we compared the newly developed quantitative IC50 assay with the established semi-quantitative inhibitory titer assay for AIGA measurement.12,19 The quantitative agreement between the two assays was poor (correlation coefficient = 0.702), attributable to the fundamental difference between a continuous concentration metric and a dilution-based endpoint. The semi-quantitative approach reports the last dilution at which percent inhibition exceeds 50%, a method inherently susceptible to imprecision from dilution errors and discrete step limitations. In contrast, the quantitative IC50 assay determines the exact point of 50% inhibition by fitting a four-parameter logistic curve to the full dilution series, yielding continuous concentration. This approach offers clinically meaningful advantages over semi-quantitative titer-based formats by generating a continuous numerical output, enabling longitudinal antibody level tracking, and providing a defined analytical range rather than a dilution endpoint. Furthermore, quantitative monitoring of AIGA levels represents a key endpoint in clinical trials evaluating immunosuppressive therapies including rituximab, cyclophosphamide, and bortezomib.39 A quantitative readout could support monitoring of treatment response, where the magnitude of AIGA change—rather than binary seropositive/seronegative status—may be clinically informative. A recent observational cohort study of 65 patients across 8 years highlighted that the majority of AOID patients require prolonged antimicrobial treatment beyond 2 years, further supporting the clinical need for quantitative AIGA monitoring to inform treatment duration and response assessment.40

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the quantitative IC50 inhibitory ELISA provides consistent measurement of AIGA. The assay demonstrated strong discriminatory ability between AIGA-positive and -negative samples and robust performance across all IQC levels. These findings support the assay’s suitability for routine laboratory use and provide a practical framework for AIGA quantification in the absence of commercial IQC materials. Nevertheless, the current scope of applicability should be interpreted within the context of its single-center validation. The multi-donor composite plasma reference materials were prepared from patients attending this center and may not fully capture the AIGA concentration range encountered across diverse populations or geographic settings where AOID is prevalent. Accordingly, the proposed framework should be regarded as a foundation for broader implementation rather than a fully generalized standard at this stage.

Future efforts should prioritize multi-center external validation using harmonized reference materials and shared standardized operating procedures to assess inter-laboratory reproducibility and confirm diagnostic performance across different clinical settings. Development of transferable external quality assessment schemes, analogous to those established for other autoantibody assays, would represent a critical step toward universal standardization. Additionally, longitudinal studies correlating quantitative IC50 values with treatment outcomes and disease activity would further define clinically meaningful thresholds and enhance the utility of this assay in guiding therapeutic decisions in AOID management.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of this study warrant acknowledgment. First, the patient cohort was derived from a single tertiary referral center in northeastern Thailand. Thus, the proposed cut-off values and IQC reference intervals may require validation in independent cohorts from other geographic regions or ethnic backgrounds. Second, IQC-material stability at 4°C was supported only indirectly, through consistent L-J performance rather than direct biochemical stability testing; a formal assessment under varied temperature conditions and repeated freeze-thaw cycling was not conducted. Reagent lot-to-lot reproducibility was likewise not assessed, as a single reagent lot was used throughout the study period. Future studies should characterize stability profiles to define appropriate expiry conditions for the reference material preparations. Third, as the IQC framework and quantitative IC50 assay were established and validated within a single referral center, their generalizability across different laboratory settings remains to be demonstrated. Fourth, reporting using the “RU/mL” format was adopted because no calibrated reference standard with an independently assigned potency currently exists for AIGA.

Experimental model and study participant details

Ethics approval for the research protocol was reviewed under the Institutional Review Board of Khon Kaen University (HE671128) in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Leftover heparinized plasma samples collected during January to December 2023, were retrieved. The Institutional Review Board granted a waiver of informed consent because the study used only anonymized leftover specimens from routine clinical testing, involved no additional interventions or sample collection, and posed minimal risk to participants.

Healthy controls were defined in our completed project as individuals with no clinical indicators of active infection and were enrolled following the blood and blood component-management guidelines of the Blood Transfusion Center, Srinagarind Hospital.19

Samples were classified as from patients of inactive (n = 28) or active (n = 70) clinical status as described in our previous publications.12,19 Briefly, inactive-status patients exhibited no clinical signs of active infection and had discontinued antimycobacterial therapy at least 30 days prior to sampling, while active-status patients had been given oral or parenteral antimycobacterial therapy within 30 days prior to sampling.19

All leftover plasma samples were aliquoted and stored at −80°C from 2023. Selected samples were thawed in December 2024 and maintained at 4°C throughout the study period.

Method details

Preparation of internal quality-control samples

Pooled leftover plasma samples were prepared to generate internal quality-control materials.17 The negative reference material consisted of pooled leftover plasma from four healthy subjects with undetectable AIGA according to inhibitory ELISA. Positive reference materials of AIGA titers were leftover plasma samples pooled from patients as described in Table 1. Each IQC level was measured once daily for 20 consecutive days.

AIGA concentration was measured in the single lot positive and negative IQC samples alongside routine service assays from 3rd March to 31st December 2025 at the Department of Microbiology Service Laboratory, Faculty of Medicine, Srinagarind Hospital, Khon Kaen University.

Inhibitory ELISA for semi-quantitative AIGA titer

Our method has been previously published and clinically validated.12,19,20,41,42,43 Briefly, plasma samples were pre-incubated with 300 pg/mL recombinant human IFN-γ (BD Biosciences, USA) in U-bottom plates. Initial plasma dilution was 1:100, followed by 1:1000, 1:5000, 1:10000, 1:50000, 1:100000, and 1:200000.20 Recombinant IFN-γ standards were prepared at concentrations of 300, 150, 75, 37.5, 18.75, 9.37, and 4.68 pg/mL. The sample diluent alone served as the blank control. All mixtures were incubated at 37°C for 1 h. Following incubation, remaining IFN-γ was measured using human IFN-γ detection kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions (BD OptEIA, BD Biosciences, USA). The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an ELISA Microplate Reader (Tecan Sunrise, Austria). The semi-quantitative AIGA titer was defined as the last dilution at which percent inhibition exceeded 50%, while a negative result was defined as less than 50% inhibition at a sample dilution of 1:100.

Inhibitory ELISA for AIGA IC50 quantification

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 11.0.0 (GraphPad, USA). Data associations were analyzed with linear regression or non-linear regression. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis with area under the curve (AUC) was used to assess discriminatory performance for disease activity. This was done using MedCalc Statistical Software version 23.6.4 (MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium). The two ROC curves, generated from paired measurements on the same samples, were compared using DeLong’s test. Method comparison was performed with Bland-Altman analysis. The optimal cut-off was selected using the Youden index. All statistical tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
