Seasonal variations and assembly patterns of endophytic communities of Rheum palmatum
Authors: DaWei Chen, YiFan Mao, Jing Zhao, Kun Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe endophytes are closely associated with the growth and development, stress resistance and active ingredients synthesis of medicinal plants. However, seasonal succession and assembly process of endophyte is still unclear. In this study, we used amplicon sequencing to analyze seasonal succession and the assembly processes of endophyte of Rheum palmatum under different season, and the driving factors of community differences were explored. The results show that the diversity and connectivity and complexity of co-occurrence network of endophytic communities were affected by seasonality, Proteobacteria and Ascomycota were the dominant endophytic phyla, dominant fungal genus of various tissues existed differences, while Delftia were dominant bacterial genus of various tissues under different season. Mantel analysis indicated that the influence of environmental factors on the differences of endophytic community differ under different seasons. Null model and neutral community model showed that the assembly process of endophytic communities was dominated by deterministic process under different seasons. Source tracker analysis found that the sources of endophytes of varous tissues existed differences under different seasons. These results illustrate the clear season heterogeneity of endophytic communities and provide new insights into revealing the effects of seasonal succession on endophytic assembly processes of medicinal plant.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05048-x.Keywords: Endophyte, Seasonal variation, Community assembly pattern, Rheum palmatum

Introduction

Endophytes inhabit the tissues of nearly all plants without causing any symptoms of disease [1], which played a crucial role in promoting plant growth and development, enhancing stress tolerance, and particularly in stimulating the production of active ingredients in medicinal plants [2–5]. Tao et al. reported that four strains of endophytic bacteria were screened from Pairs polyphylla, which significantly enhanced the biomass [6]. Gao et al. found that endophyte can promote the growth of Panax ginseng, enhance ginsenoside content, and reduce plant disease incidence [7]. Similarly, Eid et al. demonstrated that endophytes contribute to host plant growth promotion, improved nutrient uptake, mitigation of disease-related damage, and enhanced resistance to environmental stresses through the accumulation of bioactive compounds, such as increased proteins, carbohydrates, and phenolic compounds [8]. Therefore, investigating the interactions between endophytes and medicinal plants is crucial for elucidating the mechanisms underlying the quality formation of medicinal plants. However, although these findings have provided valuable insights into endophyte–host interaction mechanisms, significant knowledge gaps remain. Endophytes do not exist in isolation under natural conditions; their effects on host plants are often mediated through complex microbial communities [9]. Regrettably, most prior studies have focused primarily on the impact of individual endophytic strains on host plants, while research examining the relationship between endophyte communities and host plants at the community level remains limited [10]. Previous studies have indicated that plant microbiome assembly and host health are shaped by intricate and dynamic interactions among the host, microbial communities, and environmental factors. Nevertheless, the ecological processes governing plant–endophyte–environment interactions are still poorly understood [9]. A more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms, temporal dynamics, functional roles, and co-occurrence networks of endophyte assemblages is essential for developing endophyte-based strategies to support sustainable production systems for medicinal plants.

For perennial medicinal plants, the synthesis and accumulation of active components have obvious seasonal dynamic changes. At the same time, previous studies have shown that seasonality is a key factor driving the environment, the composition and diversity of endophyte [11]. Environmental factors such as temperature, light and rainfall, which have important effects on endophyte, which showed obvious synergistic changes with the seasons, which directly or indirectly affect the structure, diversity and function of endophytic communities [11]. However, we still lack a comprehensive understanding on the mechanisms and temporal dynamics of endophyte assembly, functions and co-occurrence networks in medicinal plants.

Rheum palmatum, a perennial herb belonging to the genus Rheum in the Polygonaceae family, is a well-known traditional Chinese medicine (TCM). Its medicinal properties are primarily derived from dried roots, which contain anthraquinones as the main active constituents, including aloe-emodin, rhein, emodin, chrysophanol, and physcion [12]. Due to their notable bioactivities, these compounds contribute to the treatment of various ailments such as constipation, jaundice, and ulcers [13]. Previous studies by our group have revealed variations in the diversity and community structure of endophytes in R. palmatum across different geographical origins, plant ages, and tissue types [14, 15]. Moreover, a positive correlation was observed between the diversity of endophytic fungi and the content of active ingredients [14]. Additionally, we isolated the endophytic Trichoderma citrinoviride HT-1 from the root of R. palmatum, which was shown to enhance plant growth, development, and the accumulation of bioactive compounds [16]. Nevertheless, the mechanisms and temporal dynamics governing the assembly, functional roles, and co-occurrence networks of endophytes in R. palmatum remain to be elucidated. Therefore, we used amplicon sequencing to investigate the dynamics of endophytic communities under different season, our aims were to (1) uncover the community assembly mechanisms of endophyte under different season; (2) reveal the temporal dynamics of microbial interkingdom network and the ecological function of endophytic communities across different season. These results may lay a foundation for understanding the interaction mechanism of endophyte and medicinal plant, and can also provide a theoretical basis for sustainable production of medicinal plants.

Materials and methods

Experimental materials

Three-year-old roots (spring, Ar; summer, Br; autumn, Cr), stem (spring, As; summer, Bs; autumn, Cs) and leaf (spring, Al; summer, Bl; autumn, Cl) of cultivated Rheum palmatum (It was identified by Professor Kun Sun and a voucher specimen of this material has been deposited in herbarium, college of Life Sciences, Northwest Normal University, NWNU-Herb-2023-RP043) were collected from Xuanshui, Li county, Gansu Province, China (104°77′89″E, 32°85′26″N, 736 m) in different season, this region is a core producing area of R. palmatum, soil physical and chemical properties: total nitrogen 864.26 mg/kg, total phosphorus 725.37 mg/kg, total potassium 21.83 g/kg, alkali-hydrolyzable nitrogen 68.64 mg/kg, available phosphorus 16.42 mg/kg, available potassium 114.73 mg/kg, organic matter 14.15 g/kg, pH 7.32. Prior to plowing, basal fertilizers were uniformly broadcast, including: urea-N fertilizer (120 kg/hm2 urea-N, 40% of total N content), calcium superphosphate (60 kg/hm2 P₂O₅, 12% P2O5 content), potassium sulfate (30 kg/hm2 K₂O, 45% K₂O content), fresh cow dung (30 t/hm2, 74.31% water content). The cow dung contained (per kg dry weight): 11.47 g N, 310.15 g C, 2.38 g P, and 4.16 g K, supplemental urea-N was top-dressed equally each season. Three independent biological replicates (three samples are collected as one biological replicate for per tissue, and three biological replicates for each season) were selected for uniformity based on size and weight. R. palmatum sample (0.5 g) were collected. The plant samples were separated and rinsed under running tap water, followed by three washes with distilled water. To achieve surface sterilization, the root were sequentially treated by immersion in 70% ethanol for 5 min, 2.5% sodium hypochlorite for 1–2 min, and again in 70% ethanol for 1 min. Afterward, it is thoroughly rinsed five times with sterile Millipore water. To confirm the effectiveness of the sterilization process, the final rinse water was plated onto Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) and incubated at 28 °C for 10 days, as well as onto Nutrient Agar (NA) and incubated at 37 °C for 3 days. All samples were subsequently stored at −80 °C until DNA extraction [15].

The root system of plants was shaken to shake off the soil not closely attached to the root system, it was collected as the bulk soil, and the rhizosphere soil closely attached to the root system was collected as the rhizosphere soil. As for the root surface soil, visible soil was removed, and the roots were suspended in sterile distilled water. The mixture was subjected to ultrasonic cleaning (KQ5200DE, Shumei Ultrasonic Instrument Co., LTD, Kunshan city, China) for 10 min (200 W, 30/30 s). The final soil solution was centrifuged at 12,000 r/min for 10 min to remove the supernatant, leaving behind the tightly adhered root surface soil. The rhizosphere and bulk soil were kept at −80 °C until the extraction of DNA [17].

Phylloplane was harvested by immersing foliar samples in 200 mL of sterile phosphate-buffered saline (0.01 M PBS, 4 ℃) within 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks, followed by sequential ultrasonic processing (15 min) and orbital agitation (200 rpm, 20 ℃, 1 h) to dislodge surface associated microorganisms into the liquid phase. Microbial biomass was harvested through vacuum filtration (0.22 μm cellulose membranes), with aseptic membrane dissection (2 × 2 mm fragments) performed using ethanol-sterilized instruments [18].

DNA extraction, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplifcation, and sequence processing

Total genomic DNA was extracted from all samples with the MOBIO PowerSoil Kit (MOBIO Laboratories, Inc., Carlsbad, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA concentration was quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Model 2000, Waltham, MA, USA), and the extracts were stored at − 20 °C until further PCR amplification.

PCR was carried out in a 20 µL reaction system comprising 4 µL of 5× FastPfu buffer, 2 µL of 2.5 mM dNTPs, 0.8 µL of each primer (5 µM), 0.4 µL of FastPfu Polymerase, 10 ng of template DNA, and ddH₂O to volume. The bacterial 16 S rRNA gene was amplified with primers 799 F (5′-AACMGGATTAGATACCCKG-3′) and 1193R (5′-ACGTCATCCCCACCTTCC-3′). The thermal cycling protocol consisted of initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; 30 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 52 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min.

For fungal ITS regions, amplification was performed using primers fITS7 (5′-GTGARTCATCGAATCTTTG-3′) and ITS4 (5′-TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC-3′). The PCR program included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; 35 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s; with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min.

PCR products were verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. For each sample, three replicate amplification products were combined and purified with the EasyPure PCR Cleanup/Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen Biosciences, Union City, CA, USA) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. The purified amplicons were subjected to paired-end sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq platform following standard procedures [19].

Statistical analysis

The fungal ITS and bacterial 16 S sequence data were analyzed using QIIME 2 (v2020.6) [20]. To obtain amplicon sequence variants (ASVs), we first used DADA2 to remove low-quality reads and noises, including removing chimeras [21]. Bacterial ASVs were taxonomically classified to the species level by alignment against the Silva database (v138.2) [21], while fungal ASVs were classified by comparison with the UNITE database (v10.0) [22, 23]. Mothur (v1.43.0) was used to analyze the community diversity index (Chao 1 and Shannon) of endophytes of R. palmatum under different seasons [24]. Co-occurrence network analysis according to the method of Li et al. [25]. Significant differences of endophytic beta-diversity were analyzed using Permutational multivariate ANOVA (PERMANOVA) through the adonis function of the vegan package, the non-metric multi-dimensional scaling (NMDS) was performed to visualize differences [26]. To assess the relative importance of determinism and stochasticity in endophyte assembly, we used two methods: (1) Based on null model, the phylogenetic normalized stochasticity ratio (pNST) was used to quantify the relative importance of deterministic and stochastic processes in community assembly based on the phylogenetic beta diversity index and phylogenetic randomization. pNST < 0.5 indicates a significant role in deterministic processes, while pNST > 0.5 indicates that stochastic processes dominated the community assembly [27]. (2) The neutral community model (NCM) was used to predict the potential importance of stochastic processes in endophytic assembly by determining the relationships between the detection frequency of endophytic taxa using the minpack.lm and HMisc packages [28]. Mantel test was performed to explore Spearman’s correlations (Permutation test) between microbial communities and environmental factor using the “vegan” package [29]. The linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) (Wilcoxon test, P < 0.05, LDA score > 2.0) was applied to identify the biomarker [30]. We used SOURCE TRACKER (v.1.0) based on Bayesian approach to estimate the sources of the endophytic communities [31]. Shannon and Chao1 indices were analyzed by using SPSS 17.0 (SPSS Inc., IL, USA) software for variance (one-way ANOVA) and Duncan’s multiple range test (P < 0.05) [17].

Community composition

The fungal OTUs were assigned to 26 phyla and 411 genera. The dominant fungal phylum across all samples was Ascomycota, which relative abundances ranging from 14.32 ~ 83.84% (Fig. 2a). At the genus level, Phialophora was dominant fungal genus in the Ar and Br samples (13.97% ~ 34.23%), Gibberella was dominant fungal genus in the As, Bs, Bl, and Cl samples (15.62% ~32.88%), Nothodactylaria was dominant fungal genus in the Cr sample (15.51%), Cladosporium was dominant fungal genus in the Cs samples (5.51%), Ombrophila was dominant fungal genus in the Al samples (2.52%) (Fig. 2b).

Assembly process of endophytic communities

The random ratio of phylogenetic normalization (pNST) was calculated based on the analysis of the null model. The results showed that the assembly of endophytic fungal and bacterial communities in various tissue of R. palmatum under different seasons were mainly dominated by deterministic processes (Fig. 8a, b). To verify the construction process, we adopted the neutral model for analysis again, which indicated that the R2 were all less than 0.5 in the neutral community models of endophytic fungi and bacteria in various tissue of R. palmatum under different seasons, indicating that the assembly of endophytic communities were mainly dominated by deterministic processes under different seasons, the results was consistent with the results of the null model (Fig. 9).

Source tracker analysis

The endophyte of R. palmatum roots, stems and leaves were used as the Sink, and the rhizosphere, non-rhizosphere soils and phyllosphere microbes were used as the Source for source tracker analysis. The results showed that endophytic fungi of leaves, roots and stems (78%, 86%, 80%, respectively) mainly originated from the rhizosphere soil in spring. In summer, endophytic fungi of leaves (96%) mainly originated from the phyllosphere, while endophytic fungi of roots and stems (51% and 77%, respectively) mainly originate from the rhizosphere soil. In autumn, endophytic fungi of leaves (96%) mainly originated from the phyllosphere, while endophytic fungi of stems and roots (56% and 79%, respectively) mainly originated from the rhizosphere soil (Fig. 10a).

Discussion

Understanding the ecological processes governing the assembly and developmental dynamics of endophytes was essential for advancing fundamental knowledge of coevolution and for applying this knowledge to the sustainable enhancement of medicinal plant quality [9, 32]. Through long-term coevolution, endophytes have developed specialized ecological associations with medicinal plants, shaped by complex interactions among the host, microorganisms, and the environment [33]. In other words, the composition and diversity of endophytes in medicinal plants were influenced by the host species, ecological conditions, and bioactive components. Seasonality was a primary driver of environmental conditions and significantly influenced microbial community composition and diversity [11]. Key environmental factors—such as temperature, light availability, and precipitation-exhibit marked seasonal variations and act in concert to directly or indirectly shape microbial structure, diversity, and functional attributes [34]. In this study, we found that the diversity analysis of endophyte of R. palmatum showed seasonal dynamic pattern, this result is similar to the reported by Li et al. [25], who found that diversity of endophytic bacteria existed seasonal dynamics [35]. Similarly, Kentjens et al. reported that endophytic diversity of Cirsium arvense tissues also existed seasonal dynamics [36]. The community composition indicates that the dominant fungi and bacterial phyla are Ascomycota and Proteobacteria, respectively, and their relative abundance changes with the seasons. Many studies also have shown that Proteobacteria and Ascomycota contain many functional strains related to denitrification and carbon and nitrogen cycling [37, 38] which indicates that the endophyte community of R. palmatum is mainly composed of beneficial microorganisms. At the genus level, dominant genera of endophytes and their relative richness was different, which may be caused by differences of environmental factors under different seasons, such as sunshine duration, average annual precipitation, and average annual temperature. Therefore, we used Mantel test analyzed influence of environmental factors on the differences of endophytic communities of R. palmatum under different seasons, the results showed that the influence of environmental factors on the composition of endophytic communities of R. palmatum differ under different seasons, which indicated that during the growth process of R. palmatum, changes of environmental factors can cause changes in endophyte. This result was similar to Zhang et al. [39] reported, who found that temperature and precipitation correlated with endosperm endophytic species richness and diversity [40].

Microbial interactions play a pivotal role in shaping the structure and functionality of microbial communities, co-occurrence network analysis has been extensively employed to study microbial assemblages in specific environments [41]. In order to investigate the effects of seasonal changes on the differences of endophytic fungal and bacterial communities in R. palmatum, co-occurrence network of microbial communities was constructed, we found that there were certain differences in the stability and complexity of endophytic microbial communities in various tissue parts of R. palmatum. Among them, the endophytic fungal and bacterial communities of root and leaf parts were more diverse, and the complexity of the communities was the highest in spring; while the endophytic fungal community of the stem part was more diverse, and the complexity of the community was the highest in autumn, and the endophytic bacterial community of stem was more diverse, and the complexity of the community was the highest in spring. These results indicated that the complexity and stability of the co-occurrence network of endophyte of various tissues of R. palmatum were affected by the season. Therefore, we further investigated the influence of environmental factors on endophyte, Mantel analysis was employed to investigate the influence of environmental factors on the endophytic community of R. palmatum under different seasons, we found that the effects of different seasonal environmental factors on the endophyte community of R. palmatum were different.

Exploring the mechanisms that govern community assembly provides a foundation for research focused on shifts in endophytic community dynamics resulting from seasonal changes. Environmental heterogeneity may lead to changes in deterministic and stochastic assembly processes that affect microbial community distribution [39]. Indeed, deciphering the mechanisms mediating microbial community assembly is an important topic to deepen understanding of maintaining and generating microbial diversity [42]. In this study, null model and neutral model were used to analyze the assembly processes of endophytic community in different parts of R. palmatum under different seasons. The results showed that the community construction of endophytic microbiome in different parts of R. palmatum under different seasons was mainly determined by deterministic processes. Meanwhile, to clarify the origin of the endophytes of R. palmatum under different seasons, the endophyte of R. palmatum roots, stems and leaves were used as the Sink, and the rhizosphere, non-rhizosphere soils and phyllosphere microbes were used as the source for source tracker analysis, we found that there were certain differences in the sources of endophyte in different parts of R. palmatum under different seasons. Among them, the endophytic fungi of roots, stems and leaves in spring and the endophytic fungi of roots and stems in summer and autumn mainly originated from rhizosphere soil, while the endophytic fungi of leaves in summer and autumn originated from phyllosphere. The endophytic bacteria of roots, stems and leaves mainly originated from phyllosphere under different seasons, it indicated that the sources of endophytic fungi of R. palmatum were affected by the seasons, while the sources of endophytic bacteria were relatively stable and not affected by the seasons. This phenomenon may be attributed to the high sensitivity of endophytic fungi to host physiological states, such as carbon allocation and secondary metabolite profiles, and seasonal factors drive changes in the host’s metabolism, directly selecting fungal groups [43, 44]. At present, this work was limited to data prediction analysis, the growth-promoting and quality-improving effects of dominant strains will be verified in the future.

Conclusion

Overall, we provide novel evidence that the diversity, community composition, co-occurrence network and sources of endophyte of R. palmatum have seasonal patterns. The differences of endophytic communities were influenced by environmental factors, and the assembly process of endophytic communities was dominated by deterministic process under different seasons. Our results may lay a foundation for understanding the interaction mechanism of endophyte and medicinal plant, and can also provide a theoretical basis for sustainable production of medicinal plants.
