Assessment of hearing and speech comprehension - preventive examinations for children and adolescents with osteogenesis imperfecta
Authors: Elżbieta Woźniak, Bogumiła Górczewska, Ewa Kott, Magdalena Wacławek, Elżbieta Jakubowska-Pietkiewicz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionIn children, acquired hearing loss typically occurs as a result of genetic disorders, ototoxic drugs and infections. It has also been noted as a serious complication of osteogenesis imperfecta (OI), a genetic connective tissue disorder that mainly arises from disorders of collagen type 1 synthesis; its other symptoms include multiple fractures and skeletal deformities, blue sclera, enamel hypoplasia and tooth fragility. To date, most studies on hearing loss in patients with OI have focussed on the adult population, due to the low occurrence among children. The aim of the study was to assess the level of hearing and speech comprehension in children and adolescents diagnosed with OI compared to a group without OI and without otological problems.Materials and MethodsThe study included a total of 103 children aged 5–17 years (54 boys and 49 girls). The patients were divided into two groups for the purpose of the study. Group I comprised 53 patients diagnosed with osteogenesis imperfecta (OI) type I and III, who were hospitalised at the Department of Paediatrics, Neonatal Pathology and Metabolic Bone Diseases between 2019 and 2025. Group II comprised 50 children who visited the Department of Paediatric Otorhinolaryngology for reasons unrelated to hearing disorders; this was used as the control group. A comprehensive auditory evaluation was conducted for all patients, encompassing an otoscopic examination, pure tone audiometry, speech audiometry, immittance audiometry, and otoacoustic emission (OAE) testing.ResultsCompared to controls, Group I exhibited significantly poorer hearing thresholds (dB—decibel) in pure tone audiometry (PTA) at low and medium frequencies (250, 500, 1000, 2000 Hz—hertz) for both the right and left ears (p < 0.05). Similarly, patients with OI type III demonstrated a higher hearing threshold (dB) compared to type I (p < 0.05). Immittance audiometry revealed a smaller ear canal volume in Group I compared with controls (p < 0.05). The tympanic membrane was found to have lower compliance in OI type III compared to type I (p < 0.05) for both the right ear (RE) and left ear (LE). Group I also exhibited a higher speech detection and reception threshold compared to the control group (p < 0.05).ConclusionsIn patients diagnosed with osteogenesis imperfecta (OI), the onset of hearing impairment may manifest at lower frequencies and be accompanied by a smaller external canal volume and reduction in tympanic membrane compliance. Pure tone audiometry, immittance audiometry and speech audiometry should be considered integral components of any comprehensive hearing monitoring programme. Children with OI may encounter difficulties in comprehending verbal communication in challenging acoustic environments. In such cases, preventative measures, systematic monitoring procedures and early intervention strategies should be considered to enhance mental, emotional and educational development, and quality of life, in children with OI.

Introduction

Hearing impairment, be it congenital or acquired, is the third most common cause of disability worldwide, affecting approximately 1.6 billion people. In children, this can result in challenges with reading, writing and comprehending speech, as well as diminished educational achievement and reduced quality of life. In this group, acquired hearing loss is most often associated with genetic disorders, ototoxic drugs, and infections [19].

However, hearing loss also occurs in patients with OI, with a prevalence of 6.7% to 77.3%, depending on the study (Smith et al., 2022). The onset of the condition is characterised by conductive hearing loss, predominantly observed in younger age groups, which subsequently progresses to sensorineural hearing loss [15]. The most common form is believed to be the conductive type (Skarżyński, 2019) [37], which is hypothesised to be caused by pathology within the middle ear [36]. In children, hearing assessments can be performed to determine the degree, type and cause of the problem, and to plan a comprehensive strategy to support speech, social skills and communication development [18].

Osteogenesis imperfecta (OI) is a genetic connective tissue disorder most often resulting from a dysfunction of genes associated with type 1 collagen synthesis (COL1A1 and COL1A2), although it has been associated with other genes in around 25% of cases. The most recent classification system distinguishes five primary forms: Type I (non-deforming), Type II (lethal), Type III (deforming), Type IV (moderate) and Type V (with interosseous membrane calcifications and/or hypertrophic callus [27]. The clinical picture exhibits varying characteristics and degrees of severity.

The disease itself is characterised by a multitude of fractures and skeletal deformities, as well as blue sclera, enamel deficiency and tooth fragility; in addition, patients can also experience hearing loss, which is an extra-skeletal symptom and a serious complication [42]. Children with OI frequently exhibit low-set ears and facial asymmetry, colloquially termed a triangular face.

Collagen comprises approximately 70–80% of the dry mass of connective tissue that constitutes the musculoskeletal system [5]. The hearing problems associated with OI may be attributed to structural abnormalities, which reduce the mobility of the ossicles in the middle ear. Furthermore, qualitative and quantitative defects in collagen type I can influence remodelling of the temporal bone (Carre 2019). The aetiology of hearing loss is multifactorial in nature, involving abnormal mineralisation and otosclerosis-like remodelling processes that affect the structures of the middle and inner ear. The condition can also weaken the muscles within the ear, including the stapedius muscle, which has a significant impact on compliance [5].

The mechanisms associated with hearing loss in OI remain unclear. A histopathological examination of the temporal bone in patients diagnosed with OI revealed loss of ossification, demineralisation, otosclerotic changes, and microfractures of the ossicles. Otosclerotic changes have been found to result in the immobilisation of the stapes, leading to the obliteration of the oval window to which it is attached [3]. This phenomenon can be attributed to the presence of microcracks, porosity, and fragility, which result in local bone hypertrophy [28]. Ossicle disconnection most commonly results from stapes fractures.

The aetiology of sensorineural hearing loss in osteogenesis imperfecta (OI) is attributable to atrophic vascularisation processes within the inner ear, the death of outer and inner hair cells, and the deformation of the basilar membrane of the organ of Corti. As demonstrated by Carre [3, 35] and [36], microcracks in the membranous cochlea cause haemorrhages into the perilymph (Vianna, 2013) [41]. It has also been found to result in demineralisation of the bony part of the cochlea and sclerotic thickening of its membranous part, especially in the basal turn of the cochlea [35, 39, 40]. In the present study, no histopathological examinations were conducted.

In patients diagnosed with osteogenesis imperfecta, it is recommended to take a multidisciplinary approach to management.

Due to the low prevalence of hearing loss in paediatric patients with OI, the majority of such studies have focussed on adult populations. The prevalence of hearing loss varies significantly between age groups, with the highest rates (6.7% to 77.3%) observed in children with types I, II, and III OI [1, 10, 15]. In young patients, conductive hearing loss has been demonstrated to progress to mixed or sensorineural over time [4, 3522 32 ]. As the prevalence of hearing impairment among paediatric patients remains unknown, it is advised that all patients should receive systematic monitoring.

While school-age and preschool children can be screened using immittance audiometry, i.e. audiological testing of hearing with tones, a full assessment of two key aspects of auditory function, viz. hearing efficiency (auditory perception) and communication ability (speech comprehension), requires the use of speech audiometry. The latter involves the use of linguistic tests which include words representative of a given language [12, 31].

Hearing impairments can exert a substantial impact on the communication development and social functioning of children. Despite the growing number of studies on audiological problems in various paediatric patient groups, data relating to those with OI remain limited. Consequently, the aim of the present study was to assess the level of hearing and speech comprehension in paediatric patients with osteogenesis imperfecta, and compare the findings with those a group of patients with no history of otological problems in ENT examination.

Materials and methods

The study included a total of 103 children aged between 5 and 17 years (54 boys and 49 girls). For the purposes of the study, the patients were divided into two groups. Group I consisted of 53 patients with osteogenesis imperfecta: type I (n = 36) and III (n = 17), who were hospitalised in the Department of Paediatrics, Neonatal Pathology and Metabolic Bone Diseases between 2019 and 2025. Group II (controls) comprised 50 children—without OI, without otological problems or risk factors for hearing loss, as determined by a comprehensive ENT examination. These children presented to the Paediatric Otorhinolaryngology Department for reasons unrelated to hearing disorders.

All patients underwent an ENT examination, with particular attention paid to otoscopic examination. Pure tone audiometry (PTA), speech audiometry, immittance audiometry and acoustic emission testing were also conducted.

Pure tonal audiometry was performed using an AC40 Clinical Audiometer (Interacoustics, Denmark) in an acoustically-prepared room. Briefly, auditory stimuli were administered at frequencies ranging from 125 to 8000 Hz, inclusive, to both the left and right ears. The headphones were positioned within the external auditory canal to ensure optimal acoustic transmission [7].

The children also received speech audiometry. The test was initiated at an intensity of 50 dB (decibel), and the dB level was then progressively decreased. The degree of hearing loss was assessed in accordance with the World Health Organization (WHO) classification [5, 38]: below 25 dB – no hearing loss, 26–40 dB – mild hearing loss (ability to hear words spoken at a normal volume and repeat them from a distance of one metre, difficulty perceiving sound in noisy environments), 41–60 dB – moderate hearing loss (ability to hear and understand speech from a distance of one metre when the voice is raised), 61–80 dB – severe hearing loss (ability to hear words shouted into the better ear), and above 81 dB – profound hearing loss.

Speech recognition is assessed using speech audiometry. In the present study, the test used the Polish language two-syllable test for preschool and school children, as outlined by Kugler [12, 21]. The Kruger test was designed to classify the frequency of sounds in the Polish language. The analysis encompassed 100,000 sounds derived from 20,000 words extracted from Polish literary works. The participants had to identify and distinguish 56 sounds. The percentage of how frequently individual sounds occur in relation to one another was also calculated. Accordingly, a register of monosyllabic and disyllabic words was compiled. For the disyllabic tests, 100 words were selected and divided into two groups, each consisting of five subgroups of 10 words. The articulation lists were phonetically balanced to be representative of everyday speech [31]. The lists are read by a narrator and played back from a CD on a Pioneer player connected to an AC40 Clinical Audiometer (Interacoustics, Denmark).

The most significant frequency ranges for comprehending Polish speech are 100 to 2300 Hz or 500 to 2000 Hz, with the highest percentage of intelligible words occurring at 1300 to 1600 Hz. The test is used to evaluate three distinct thresholds:The Speech Detection Threshold (SDT), defined as the minimum level of sound pressure at which speech is perceivable, but not intelligible. The threshold is expressed in decibels (dB), with a value of 12 dB frequently cited as the SDT.The Speech Reception Threshold (SRT), defined as the lowest level at which a child correctly reproduces 50% of the test items. This threshold is generally agreed to be at 8–9 dB above the detection threshold.The Speech Comprehension Threshold (SCT), defined as the highest percentage of recognisable words, i.e. the point at which the percentage of correctly repeated words reaches 100% [20].

Immittance audiometry was performed using a Titan device and probe (Interacoustics, Denmark) to assess eardrum and ossicular mobility. The classification of normal tympanometric curves was conducted in accordance with Jeger. Pressure usually ranges from −100 to + 100 daPa (decaPascal) in children and adults. Type A – normal middle ear function. Type As is characterised by a reduction in amplitude, i.e. where the peak is flattened, and may be indicative of ossification of the ossicular chain (otosclerosis). In contrast, Type Ad glottal excitation is characterised by a pronounced peak, indicative of tympanic membrane laxity or a rupture within the ossicular chain [7, 14]. Type B is characteristic of exudative otitis media, and Type C with Eustachian tube dysfunction, indicating a catarrhal infection. No such children were included in the study. The normal range of middle ear compliance is reported to be between 0.3 and 1.5 ml. The degree of compliance is believed to reflect the mobility of the middle ear vibrating membrane, with high compliance indicating high mobility [7, 12].

All patients also received otoacoustic emission (OAE) testing using a Titan device and probe (700–4000 Hz) (Interacoustics, Denmark). The presence of OEA is confirmed by the detection of a response at a minimum of three distinct frequencies [17].

Forty-five patients (85%) with osteogenesis imperfecta were receiving bisphosphonate therapy. Eight patients were diagnosed with the mildest form of the disease and were not receiving treatment. The analysis of patients suffering from osteogenesis imperfecta (OI) was exploratory (post hoc) in nature, and thus did not constitute a predefined study endpoint.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and was approved by the Bioethics Committee of the Medical University of Lodz (RNN/173/25/KE, 13 May 2025). Prior to participation, written consent was obtained from all patients' parents who were deemed capable of providing consent. The data were collected from clinical records between November 2019 and February 2025.

Statistical analysis

The analysed parameters were presented as means with standard deviations. The normality of the distribution of quantitative variables was tested with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Spearman's correlation coefficient was used to calculate the correlation between quantitative variables. Groups were compared using the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis ANOVA test. Statistical significance was assumed at p < 0.05. Statistical analysis was performed using Statistica 13 software (StatSoft, Poland).

Discussion

Patients suffering from osteogenesis imperfecta (OI) may experience progressive hearing loss; in approximately 50% of cases, this will occur between the ages of 10 and 20 years [29]. Unfortunately, such cases are frequently underrecognized and can have a substantial impact on the daily functioning of children. Children with hearing difficulties may experience problems with language acquisition, and fall behind in key domains such as communication, cognition, reading, and socio-emotional development [24]. Such problems are also faced by children with osteogenesis imperfecta (OI), increasing their risk of poorer educational outcomes and psychosocial adaptation, which may in turn compound the impact of their motor disability.

The present study analysed paediatric patients with mild Type I OI, characterised by fewer than one fracture per year and no significant skeletal deformities, as well as those with progressive-deforming Type III, distinguished by numerous bone fractures and deformities. Both groups are at elevated risk of hearing loss compared to children without OI [16].

Multicentre studies in North America found the prevalence of hearing loss among individuals with OI to be 28%, and to increase with age [26]. In contrast, nationwide cohort studies on the Danish population demonstrated a prevalence of 17%. [10].

In one study, pure tone audiometry identified hearing loss in only two out of 45 children (4.4%) aged 4–16, with normal hearing defined as > 20 dB [22]. The largest studies on hearing assessment in patients aged under 18 years, conducted in the United States as part of the Trinex Analysis Network, found 10.0% of patients to be affected by hearing loss: of all tested subjects, 5.71% presented with conductive hearing loss, 3.01% with sensorineural hearing loss, and 1.35% with mixed hearing loss [4].

A series of observational and quantitative studies in Brazil analysed patients in two age groups: those under the age of 10 (n = 15) and those between 10 and 19 years of age (n = 32). Of these, conductive hearing loss was noted in 18.8% of children (aged 5 to 10), and in 30% of the adolescents (second group)—however, had air-conduction hearing thresholds within the normal range [5]. In the present study, mild hearing loss was observed in 5/53 (9.4%) children with OI.

A few studies conducted in the 1980 s demonstrated a higher prevalence of hearing loss in the adult population (65–77.7%); however, this may be attributable to methodological and definitional differences between studies [30].

In contrast, aa study of 22 patients aged 11 months to 15 years found all to have normal pure tone audiometry results; however, 20 patients had type A tympanometry, two had type As, and none had type B or C [15]. In Glasgow, Scotland, a five-year hearing screening programme of children aged 0–16 years based on immittance audiometry and pure tone audiometry identified one case of Type B tympanogram abnormality, suggesting exudative otitis media [25]. Similar results were obtained in our study with two individuals having a tympanogram other than A (Type C). However, these patients were not included in the study group due to suspected upper respiratory tract infection.

In an analysis of data from 2,709 children aged 6–19 years included in the 2017–2020 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, Humes et al. found hearing thresholds identified by pure tone audiometry to differ with regard to age. The group aged 6–9 years demonstrated 3–5 dB higher (i.e. worse) dB values than for older groups [13]. In our study the analysed groups were similar in terms of age, and no significant relationship was found.

In patients diagnosed with osteogenesis imperfecta (OI), the auricle is positioned lower than in healthy individuals, and is larger in relation to the size of the head. The external auditory canal is narrow and may be distorted, and the eardrum is positioned at an acute angle to the external auditory canal, which significantly affects immittance audiometry. Regarding otoscopy in patients diagnosed with Type III OI, the eardrum was found to be inclined at a more pronounced angle (both inclination and declination) than in unaffected individuals [8]. In healthy children, the volume of the external auditory canal has been found to increase with age [34].

Our present findings indicate that ECV is smaller in patients with OI, which may be due to anatomical differences in the structure of the ear. Furthermore, in the OI group, the eardrum showed reduced compliance among patients with Type III. The acoustic compliance of the tympanic membrane, assessed by immittance audiometry, is affected by both its elasticity and the equivalent ear canal volume. Hence tympanometric admittance measurements are influenced by the acoustic properties of the ear canal and middle ear cavity [33]. A reduced external ear canal volume may create the impression of reduced compliance in patients with OI; however, this is not necessarily indicative of a change in the biological elasticity of the eardrum. Nevertheless, this interpretation necessitates further research on the impact of the morphology of the outer ear (including the external auditory canal) in children with congenital bone fragility, ideally in a larger cohort of patients. Such changes may represent the initial symptom of otosclerosis, although further confirmation is needed through histopathological or imaging tests.

Bisphosphonates have been found to be non-ototoxic in children diagnosed with OI and to enhance bone structure quality [32]. Osteoporosis treatments aimed at strengthening bone structure have also been found to slow the onset of hearing loss. In the present study, 85% of children were receiving bisphosphonate treatment, which may have contributed to the low incidence of hearing loss (5.3%). However, this requires further confirmation, as the effect of treatment duration or dosage was not analysed due to the heterogeneity of the group (dosage is dependent on body weight and age), and the small size of the without bisphosphonate group.

The 2025 World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations indicated that all children should undergo a hearing screening and evaluation for potential ear diseases at the time of school entry. In countries where health system capacity permits, it is recommended that hearing checks be conducted on a regular basis, specifically in kindergarten, upon initial entry to school, and in school years 1, 2, 3, 7 and 11 [43]. The American Academy of Audiology has also endorsed the identification of hearing impairment in early childhood and school-aged populations through hearing screening methods [2].

The French Society of Otorhinolaryngology (SFORL) guidelines state that pure-tone audiometry results should be interpreted in conjunction with clinical data, impedance audiometry, and speech audiometry. In clinical practice, paediatric audiologists utilise a range of age-appropriate behavioural techniques to estimate hearing thresholds at different frequencies [6, 7]. An international consensus document regarding the management of patients with OI identifies the tests as the recommended set of measurements [9]. These tests were carried on our patients in question and subjected to a full evaluation. Consequently, in patients with OI, despite normal hearing thresholds, elevated thresholds for hearing, speech detection and speech perception were observed.

In the present study, the children with OI achieved an equivalent speech comprehension threshold to the controls. This suggests that when words are articulated at a sufficient volume, children with OI exhibit a similar level of comprehension to unaffected children. However, the OI group exhibited elevated speech detection and reception thresholds, suggesting they may require a higher signal intensity to detect and reception speech. Furthermore, patients with type III OI demonstrated an impaired ability to comprehend speech (only right ear), which is consistent with their elevated thresholds in pure tone audiometry for low and mid-frequencies. It has been hypothesised that this may result in poorer speech comprehension in difficult acoustic conditions, such as background noise or greater distance from the sound source.

The French health authority recommends that hearing and speech development should be monitored in children and adolescents as they grow. That patients with OI who are at risk of hearing loss should be prioritised [7], as they demonstrate poorer outcomes in comparison to healthy children of a similar developmental stage. The Brittle Bone Society guidelines stipulate that children diagnosed with OI who do not exhibit symptoms related to the ear should undergo hearing tests on a triennial basis from the age of three. However, the evidence concerning this recommendation is inconclusive: it has elsewhere been proposed that annual screening would enhance disease management and demonstrate a significant detection rate [25].

Despite the rarity of hearing loss in paediatric patients with OI, Waissbluth et al. recommend that such patients should nevertheless be monitored for its development [42]. LoTurco noticed that in children diagnosed with OI, audiological examinations are performed less frequently than every two years, i.e. infrequently. Mild hearing impairment has the potential to occur at any point during the lifetime. Its initial stages may not be perceived by parents or caregivers, which can have a detrimental effect on the child's development [11, 23] emphasise the need to define a management strategy for ensuring quality of life in children diagnosed with OI, and underline that maintaining this requires the involvement of family members, educational institutions and medical professionals.

Hearing impairment has been observed even in early childhood [40]. It is therefore vital that children with OI undergo regular, comprehensive hearing tests in order to facilitate early intervention and ensure proper development. This is confirmed by our present findings.

The present study is restricted to exploratory analysis conducted post hoc. Consequently, its findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Its aim was to identify potential associations that should receive further careful observation and verification in later prospective studies involving larger populations.

Study limitations

The study has some limitations, particularly its cross-sectional nature, the absence of a long-term follow-up, and the failure to assess the progression of hearing loss. In addition, due to the rarity of the condition, it was difficult to assemble a group of patients with different types of OI. In addition, due to the small size and heterogeneity of the group of patients not treated with bisphosphonates, the study did not analyse the impact of treatment duration or dosagenor did it consider the possible consequences of the treatment on the results.

Similarly, not all children were able to cooperate during the examination, which also limited the size of the group. As a result, the group included subjects with a broad age range, i.e. five to 17 years. This, in conjunction with variations in cooperation during audiometric testing, may influence the validity of the findings, particularly among the youngest subjects. The testing time for younger children was also considerably longer, although the tests were conducted by the same paediatric audiology technician. To ensure a level playing field, both patient and control groups were matched for age.

Furthermore, the control group was recruited from the Paediatric Otolaryngology Clinic as opposed to the general population, a decision which may have resulted in selection bias. In order to ensure that the group was representative of the general population, the participants were selected from patients that had undergone follow-up endoscopy due to hoarseness, snoring, or surgery because of deviated nasal septum; they had no other ENT abnormalities.

Furthermore, no bone conduction analysis was performed, which precluded the possibility of determining the type of hearing loss in patients OI. The differences between air and bone conduction were not analysed.

Finally, the analysis did not include any histopathological examination.
