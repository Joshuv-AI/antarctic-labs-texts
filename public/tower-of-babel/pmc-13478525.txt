A CrOA-tuned cascaded (2DOF-PDf)–(LTI) controller for coordinated LFC–AVR regulation in interconnected power systems
Authors: Mohamed F Elnaggar, A A El-Rahman, Fsaha Mebrahtu Gebru, Mohamed Metwally Mahmoud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterconnected power systems are frequently exposed to load disturbances, parameter uncertainties, nonlinear effects, and dynamic coupling between frequency and voltage regulation loops, which make it difficult to maintain stable and well-coordinated operation. Conventional control strategies may suffer from slow damping, large oscillations, and reduced robustness when applied to such highly coupled environments. To address this problem, this paper proposes a novel cascaded controller for the coordinated regulation of Load Frequency Control (LFC) and Automatic Voltage Regulation (AVR) in a two-area interconnected power system. The proposed controller combines a two-degree-of-freedom proportional–derivative controller with filtered derivative action (2DOF-PDf) and an inner leaky tilt-integral (LTI) stage to improve transient shaping, damping, and robustness while reducing sensitivity to disturbances and parameter variations. The controller parameters are optimally tuned using the Crayfish Optimization Algorithm (CrOA) based on the Integral of Time-weighted Squared Error (ITSE) criterion. The effectiveness of CrOA is first verified through comparison with several well-known optimization methods, including the Chimp Optimization Algorithm (ChOA), Dingo Optimization Algorithm (DOA), Sine Cosine Algorithm (SCA), Gorilla Troops Optimizer (GTO), and Gradient-Based Optimizer (GBO). The proposed control scheme is then assessed under various operating scenarios, including step load disturbances, stochastic load variations, practical nonlinearities such as Generation Rate Constraint (GRC) and Governor Dead Band (GDB), time-varying voltage reference tracking, and parametric uncertainty of up to ± 40%. MATLAB/Simulink results demonstrate that the proposed CrOA-tuned cascaded (2DOF-PDf)–(LTI) controller provides faster damping, smaller frequency and tie-line power deviations, and more accurate terminal-voltage regulation than several benchmark controllers, including Proportional–Integral–Derivative (PID), Tilted Integral Derivative (TID), Fractional-Order Proportional–Integral–Derivative (FOPID), and Fractional-Order Proportional–Integral with Proportional–Integral–Double Derivative Squared (FOPI–PIDD2) controllers. Overall, the proposed controller offers a robust and effective solution for coordinated frequency and voltage regulation in interconnected power systems.Keywords: Cascaded (2DOF-PDf)–(LTI) controller, Load frequency control, Renewable generators, Sustainable development, Optimization techniquesSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Interconnected power systems are increasingly required to operate under wide and continuously varying operating conditions. Load changes, switching actions, faults, practical nonlinearities, and the growing impact of renewable-energy variability can create persistent mismatches between generation and demand, thereby exciting coupled electromechanical and electromagnetic dynamics. In practice, power-system stability is commonly discussed in terms of frequency stability, voltage stability, and rotor-angle stability. Frequency deviations are primarily associated with active-power imbalance, whereas voltage deviations are closely related to reactive-power imbalance. Therefore, maintaining acceptable frequency and voltage profiles requires fast and well-coordinated control actions that can restore system balance while limiting overshoot, oscillations, and long settling times1–5. These objectives are commonly supported through two secondary control layers: the turbine-governor path used for Load Frequency Control (LFC) and the excitation path used for Automatic Voltage Regulation (AVR). The LFC loop is responsible for suppressing frequency deviations and regulating tie-line power exchange among interconnected areas, whereas the AVR loop aims to maintain generator terminal voltage within desired limits. Although these two loops are often analyzed separately, their dynamic interaction cannot be neglected in interconnected multi-area systems. A disturbance occurring in one area may propagate through the tie-line and affect the neighboring area, while voltage-control actions may also influence real-power output and consequently affect frequency behavior. For this reason, coordinated LFC-AVR regulation has become an important research direction for improving the dynamic security and overall performance of interconnected power systems6–9.

Early studies in LFC mainly focused on improving frequency regulation in conventional thermal power systems. Later, the scope of research expanded to include deregulated structures, distributed energy resources, electric vehicles, battery storage systems, and various auxiliary support mechanisms for better active-power balancing10–15. In parallel, research on AVR has concentrated on enhancing terminal-voltage regulation and improving generator dynamic response under disturbances and parameter variations16–19. Despite these advances, fully unified LFC-AVR formulations remain relatively limited compared with the large body of work devoted separately to LFC or AVR20,21. In many published studies, controller design is still carried out primarily for one loop and then extended to the other, or both loops are modeled together but not treated using a sufficiently coupling-aware control architecture. As a result, the reported performance may become less convincing when simultaneous frequency deviations, voltage excursions, and tie-line oscillations occur under realistic operating conditions6,7,20,21.

On the controller-design side, fractional-order (FO) methods have attracted significant attention because they provide more tuning flexibility than classical integer-order structures and can improve transient shaping, damping, and disturbance rejection. Controllers such as TID and other FO-based variants have been widely reported in the LFC literature and have shown promising performance improvements over conventional controllers22–24. Review-oriented studies have also highlighted the growing maturity of FO-PID families and their broad applicability in modern power and energy systems, particularly where robustness and enhanced transient performance are required25. In addition, several researchers have proposed coordinated or composite FO-based strategies for multi-area regulation under practical effects such as communication delays, nonlinearities, and uncertainty26–29. These developments indicate that advanced FO control has become a major trend in power-system regulation research.

More recently, cascade control structures have emerged as an effective alternative to single-stage controllers. Cascade architectures allow the control task to be distributed across multiple stages, where one stage can shape the dominant transient dynamics while another can strengthen steady-state regulation and robustness. This makes cascade control particularly attractive for complex and highly coupled applications such as integrated LFC-AVR regulation30–34. FO-based cascade controllers have also been reported to further improve damping characteristics and regulation quality, especially in systems where tight transient control is required35–37. However, although many of these controllers show good simulation performance, some of them suffer from increased structural complexity, limited practical interpretability, or insufficient emphasis on coordinated voltage-frequency interaction. The recent literature has also shown clear progress in LFC research for renewable-rich and hybrid power systems. For example, a new CFOTID controller supported by capacitive energy storage was proposed to enhance the LFC performance of a two-area RES-penetrated multi-source power system under practical nonlinear operating conditions38,39. Recent studies have further highlighted the need for advanced control structures in interconnected power systems operating under nonlinearities, renewable-energy fluctuations, and parametric uncertainty. For example, Mishra et al. proposed a dual-layered tilt fuzzy controller for an interconnected power system integrated with DFIG, showing the benefit of structurally enhanced control design in complex operating conditions. In another study, an integral-tilt integral-derivative-with-filter (I-TDN) control structure was shown to outperform conventional PID- and I-PD-based designs in a two-area hybrid power system subject to stochastic disturbances and nonlinear effects. In addition, improved FO-control strategies optimized by modern metaheuristic methods have also been reported for nonlinear interconnected power systems, further confirming that conventional low-complexity controllers may not provide sufficient robustness and dynamic quality in such environments40,41. These studies motivate the development of the present cascaded coordinated controller for integrated LFC–AVR regulation.

Likewise, a novel TDF-TIDF controller was introduced for optimal dynamic frequency regulation of a renewable-energy-based hybrid power system, where improved dynamic behavior and robustness were achieved through a composite filtered tilt-derivative and tilt-integral structure42,43. These contributions clearly confirm the effectiveness of advanced cascade and FO-inspired control designs in frequency regulation. Nevertheless, their primary focus remains on the LFC problem itself. In other words, although modern controllers have become more sophisticated for frequency control in renewable-rich settings, relatively fewer studies have addressed the coordinated design of LFC and AVR within a unified multivariable framework. Besides controller structure, optimization-based tuning has become a dominant practice in modern LFC and AVR design because the resulting control problems are generally nonlinear, high-dimensional, and nonconvex. A wide range of optimization techniques, including GA, PSO, SCA, WOA, ICA, GTO, and other metaheuristic methods, have been used to tune controller parameters for improved dynamic performance44,45. Review studies continue to emphasize that optimization-assisted control design remains highly attractive for practical power-system regulation, particularly when uncertainty and realistic operating constraints must be considered46,47. Within this context, the Crayfish Optimization Algorithm (CrOA) has recently emerged as a competitive optimization tool with strong exploration and exploitation characteristics, and improved CrOA variants have also been reported for complex search spaces48–52. However, the effectiveness of any optimizer is still strongly dependent on the controller architecture being tuned. Therefore, a practically meaningful contribution should address not only the tuning method, but also the structural suitability of the controller for integrated LFC-AVR regulation.

From the above discussion, the main research gap can be clearly identified. Although the literature includes many advanced controllers for LFC, many AVR-focused strategies, and several recent cascade or FO-based designs for renewable and hybrid systems53–57, integrated LFC-AVR studies still face important limitations. First, coordinated voltage-frequency regulation is still comparatively less explored than single-loop frequency regulation. Second, some available controllers are either too complex or insufficiently transparent from an implementation perspective. Third, practical issues such as derivative kick, disturbance sensitivity, windup-like behavior, tie-line interactions, nonlinear constraints, and uncertainty are not always addressed together within one unified controller design. Consequently, there remains a clear need for a coupling-aware, practically interpretable, and optimally tuned cascaded controller capable of achieving effective frequency and voltage regulation simultaneously in interconnected power systems.

Motivated by these limitations, this paper develops an improved cascaded controller for a two-area interconnected LFC-AVR system. The proposed controller combines a two-degree-of-freedom proportional-derivative outer stage with filtered derivative action, denoted as 2DOF-PDf, and an inner leaky tilt-integral (LTI) stage. The outer stage introduces setpoint weighting to decouple reference tracking from disturbance rejection, thereby helping to reduce overshoot and mitigate derivative kick, while the derivative filter limits noise amplification. The inner LTI stage provides robust integral-type action through a tilt component and a leaky integral term, which improves steady-state accuracy while alleviating excessive accumulation effects under sustained disturbances. In this way, the proposed controller is intended to preserve the performance advantages of advanced cascade and FO-inspired control concepts while remaining structurally clear and practically implementable. To determine the controller parameters effectively, the CrOA is employed as the optimization engine using an Integral of Time-weighted Squared Error (ITSE) criterion. The proposed control strategy is then evaluated on a two-area interconnected system under multiple representative operating conditions, including step load disturbances, stochastic load changes, practical nonlinearities such as Generation Rate Constraint (GRC) and Governor Dead Band (GDB), time-varying voltage reference tracking, and parameter uncertainty. The obtained results are compared against those of several benchmark controllers under identical conditions to provide a transparent assessment of frequency regulation, voltage regulation, tie-line power damping, and overall robustness. Modeling of an interconnected two-zone power system

Structure of the two-zone interconnected network

To evaluate the proposed control scheme, a widely adopted two-zone interconnected power system is considered. The network is composed of two control areas with comparable generation ratings, coupled through a tie-line that facilitates mutual active power transfer. In each zone, LFC and AVR loops operate in a coordinated manner to preserve secure and stable system performance. The AVR subsystem is responsible for regulating the generator terminal voltage within predefined operational limits, whereas the LFC subsystem aims to counteract frequency excursions resulting from variations in active power demand. Due to the inter-area electrical coupling, a disturbance introduced in one zone does not remain confined locally; instead, it propagates through the tie-line and influences the frequency dynamics of the adjacent zone. Such coupling effects underline the importance of integrated control strategies that can effectively damp inter-area oscillations while maintaining coordinated dynamic responses across the entire interconnected system. Alternatively, the interconnected two-zone system can be represented using a graph-theoretic viewpoint, where each zone is modeled as a node and the tie-line is described as an edge characterized by a synchronization gain denoted as K12. Within this framework, frequency deviations constitute the system state variables, and the magnitude of K12 governs the rate and strength of dynamic synchronization between the two zones. This representation offers valuable insight into the consensus and coordination behavior of interconnected power systems. The subsequent sections present the mathematical formulations of the LFC and AVR subsystems adopted in this work.

It should be noted that the adopted two-area non-reheat interconnected power-system model is used in this study as a standard benchmark framework for transparent and controlled evaluation of the proposed coordinated LFC–AVR controller. The selection of this benchmark model allows the effect of the proposed cascaded controller to be assessed more clearly, without excessive masking from plant-model complexity. Although the adopted system is simplified, the present study is not limited to an idealized assessment. To improve practical relevance, the proposed controller is further examined under stochastic load variations, practical nonlinear constraints including generation rate constraint (GRC) and governor dead band (GDB), and parametric uncertainty levels of up to ± 40%. These scenarios provide a more realistic basis for evaluating the robustness and effectiveness of the proposed control strategy58,59.

Excitation and voltage control dynamics

The AVR is a key component for maintaining voltage stability in power systems by compensating for variations in reactive power demand. Changes in reactive load led to deviations in the generator terminal voltage, which must be corrected to preserve power quality and reliable operation. The AVR system continuously measures the terminal voltage and compares it with a specified reference value to generate an appropriate control action. As shown in the AVR control structure, the terminal voltage is sensed via a potential transformer and converted into a feedback signal. This signal is compared with the reference voltage, and the resulting error is amplified to produce the exciter control voltage. By adjusting the exciter field voltage, the AVR modifies the generator field current, which directly affects the induced electromotive force (emf) and, consequently, the terminal voltage. Integrated coordination between voltage and frequency control loops

In multi-area interconnected power systems, the coupling between the AVR and LFC loops is commonly modest. This feature often permits voltage and frequency regulation to be treated, to a first approximation, as separate design problems within each control area. Nevertheless, AVR corrective actions aimed at holding the generator terminal voltage at its setpoint can produce secondary effects on the generator’s real-power output. Consequently, voltage-control interventions may alter the frequency response and, in turn, influence the behavior of the LFC loop. To account for these interactions, the AVR pathway includes a set of coupling gains (see Fig. 2) that parameterize the cross-dependence between voltage- and frequency-related dynamics. Specifically, the coefficients quantify: (i) how small variations in the stator electromotive force (emf) affect active-power generation (α1); (ii) how rotor-angle deviations contribute to terminal-voltage changes (α2); (iii) how stator-emf variations feed into rotor-angle dynamics (α₃); and (iv) how rotor-angle changes influence the stator emf (α₄). Taken together, these terms provide a compact representation of the inherent AVR–LFC coupling. AVR and LFC are considered simultaneously in this work because, although they are often associated primarily with reactive-power/voltage and active-power/frequency regulation, respectively, their dynamics are not completely independent in interconnected power systems. Excitation-driven voltage-control actions may influence the generator real-power behavior through electromechanical coupling, while frequency and rotor-angle deviations can also affect terminal-voltage dynamics. Therefore, treating the two loops separately may overlook important cross-effects, especially under disturbances, tie-line interactions, nonlinear constraints, and parameter uncertainty. To avoid this limitation, the present study adopts an integrated LFC–AVR model in which the overlap between the two loops is explicitly represented by coupling coefficients α1-α4​, while inter-area interaction is captured by the tie-line synchronization gain K12 Moreover, the proposed cascaded (2DOF-PDf)–(LTI) controller is structured to manage this coupled behavior: the outer 2DOF-PDf stage shapes the dominant transient response and limits excessive sensitivity, whereas the inner LTI stage improves robustness and steady-state correction. In this way, the overlap between AVR and LFC is not neglected, but explicitly accounted for in both the system model and the controller design.

Applied CrOA technique

The CrOA, proposed by Heming et al., is a bio-inspired optimization method that models the natural behaviors of crayfish, particularly their feeding activity, competition, and tendency to seek shelter during high-temperature conditions. These natural behaviors are translated into three main algorithmic stages designed to maintain an effective balance between global exploration and local exploitation. The summer refuge behavior mainly supports exploration, whereas the competition and foraging behaviors are associated with exploitation. At the beginning of the algorithm, a set of candidate solutions; referred to as a crayfish colony; is randomly generated. Each solution is represented as a position vector , where each corresponds to an individual in a multi-dimensional search space. The movement and behavior of crayfish are influenced by stochastic factors that emulate variations in environmental temperature. As the temperature increases, the algorithm shifts toward either the summer refuge stage or the competition stage. During the summer refuge phase, candidate solutions are updated based on their current positions and the location of a shaded area When environmental conditions become suitable, the algorithm switches to the foraging phase, where the current best solution is treated as a food source.

In the foraging phase, each crayfish assesses its current position using a fitness evaluation and compares it with the optimal solution. The movement strategy depends on the perceived size and quality of the food source. If the food quantity is excessive, the crayfish first reduces it before continuing the feeding process. Through this adaptive mechanism, CrOA alternates between broad search behaviors and solution refinement, effectively imitating crayfish survival strategies to progressively guide the population toward the optimal solution60.

Simulation results and performance discussion

This section investigates the dynamic performance of a two-area interconnected power system regulated by the proposed cascaded (2DOF-PDf)–(LTI) controller, whose parameters are optimally tuned using the CrOA. To ensure a rigorous and systematic evaluation, the analysis is conducted following a two-level assessment framework that separately examines the effectiveness of the optimization algorithm and the closed-loop control performance under various operating conditions. In the first level of analysis, the optimization capability of CrOA is evaluated by benchmarking its convergence behavior and solution quality against several well-established metaheuristic algorithms reported in the literature, including ChOA, DOA, SCA, GTO, and GBO. All optimization techniques are executed under identical settings to ensure fairness, with a population size of 30 individuals and a maximum of 200 iterations. A step load perturbation is applied at t = 0, corresponding to a 1.5% load increase in Area 1 and a 2% increase in Area 2. The comparative results reveal that CrOA consistently achieves the lowest objective function values among all competing algorithms, resulting in an overall improvement of approximately 52%. This indicates a superior ability to explore the search space and accurately identify high-quality parameter sets for the proposed controller. In the second level of analysis, the robustness and control effectiveness of the CrOA-tuned cascaded (2DOF-PDf)–(LTI) controller are examined through extensive comparisons with other CrOA-based controllers, including PID, TID, FOPID, FOPI–PIDD2, and structures. These comparisons are carried out under a wide range of operating scenarios to assess the controller’s adaptability and stability. The considered scenarios include step load disturbances, random load fluctuations, time-varying voltage reference signals in both areas, and parametric uncertainties affecting key power-system components. Particular attention is paid to the controller’s ability to suppress frequency deviations, maintain voltage regulation, and attenuate tie-line power oscillations (ΔPtie). LFC model incorporating nonlinear effects such as GRC and GDB.

Comparison of frequency deviation obtained using the proposed method with and without nonlinear constraints.

In practical power plants, the frequency control loop is affected by physical nonlinearities that are not captured by ideal linear models. Two of the most common nonlinear effects are the GDB and the GRC. The GDB refers to a small range of frequency deviation within which the governor does not respond effectively, mainly due to mechanical backlash, friction, and insensitivity in the valve-governor mechanism. As a result, very small frequency changes may not trigger an immediate corrective action. On the other hand, the GRC represents the physical limit on how fast the generator output can increase or decrease, since turbines and associated actuators cannot change their power instantaneously. Therefore, even when a strong control signal is available, the actual generation response is rate-limited. These two nonlinearities make the LFC problem more realistic and more challenging, because they delay the system response, enlarge frequency excursions, and generally worsen settling behavior compared with the ideal linear case. In contrast, the terminal voltage responses in both areas remain largely unaffected by the inclusion of these nonlinear constraints, indicating that the primary impact of GRC and GDB is concentrated on the frequency control loop rather than the voltage regulation loop. Finally, it is worth noting that practical implementations often rely on discrete-time digital control, where sampling may alter stability margins and transient features relative to continuous-time models. Although the present study adopts a continuous-time formulation for clarity, the proposed framework can be extended to sampled-data realizations. The main numerical challenges are associated with storing state histories and solving coupled nonlinear algebraic equations at each step; however, these issues can be mitigated using efficient solvers and structured data handling, maintaining a reasonable accuracy–cost trade-off for simulation.

From a computational standpoint, the proposed control framework involves two distinct aspects: offline tuning complexity and online implementation complexity. The offline burden is mainly related to the CrOA-based optimization process, where multiple controller parameters are tuned simultaneously under repeated time-domain simulations. Since the proposed cascaded (2DOF-PDf)–(LTI) controller contains more decision variables than simpler structures such as PID and TID, its tuning stage is naturally more computationally demanding. However, the obtained convergence results show that the proposed structure is more compatible with the CrOA search mechanism, reaching high-quality solutions in fewer iterations and with shorter execution time than several competing controllers. After tuning, the online implementation burden remains moderate because the controller is applied using fixed parameters and does not require real-time optimization. Therefore, the additional offline computational effort can be justified by the significant improvement achieved in dynamic performance, robustness, and regulation quality.

Conclusions

Coordinating the AVR with the LFC loop through appropriate coupling mechanisms provides an effective means for simultaneously enhancing voltage and frequency stability in interconnected power systems. In this study, an improved cascaded secondary control strategy based on the Cascaded (2DOF-PDf)–(LTI) structure has been developed to regulate the integrated AVR–LFC dynamics of a conventional two-area power system. The controller parameters were optimally tuned using the CrOA, which offers a favorable balance between global exploration and local exploitation during the optimization process. To ensure a fair and meaningful evaluation, the proposed CrOA-tuned cascaded controller was systematically compared with several well-established benchmark regulators, including PID, TID, FOPID, FOPI–PIDD2, and controllers, under identical operating conditions. A wide range of test scenarios was considered to thoroughly assess closed-loop performance and robustness. These scenarios encompassed step load disturbances, stochastic load variations, practical power-system nonlinearities such as the GRC and GDB, time-varying voltage reference tracking, and parametric uncertainty involving ± 20–40% deviations in key LFC and AVR parameters. Across all examined scenarios, the proposed Cascaded (2DOF-PDf)–(LTI) controller consistently demonstrated superior dynamic performance. In particular, it achieved faster damping of oscillations, reduced frequency and tie-line power deviations, and improved voltage tracking accuracy compared with the competing control structures. Quantitative assessments based on time-domain indices and ITSE performance metrics confirmed that the proposed controller yielded the lowest overall error values, indicating enhanced disturbance rejection capability and improved regulation quality. These results highlight that the cascaded configuration provides additional tuning flexibility and enables more effective coordination between the active-power (frequency) and reactive-power (voltage) control channels within the integrated AVR–LFC framework. Despite the demonstrated performance improvements, certain practical considerations remain. The proposed cascaded structure introduces a larger number of tunable parameters, which may increase tuning complexity and computational effort compared with simpler control schemes, particularly when metaheuristic optimization is employed. Furthermore, the present investigation was limited to a standard two-area benchmark system and did not explicitly incorporate communication delays, cyber-physical constraints, or hardware implementation limitations. Future research will focus on extending the proposed control strategy to multi-area and renewable-rich power systems, where higher levels of uncertainty and variability are present. Despite the promising results, the present study has some limitations. The adopted two-area non-reheat interconnected power-system model, although widely used as a benchmark, represents a simplified dynamic framework compared with practical large-scale power systems. It was intentionally selected to provide a controlled and transparent environment for evaluating the proposed coordinated LFC–AVR controller under stochastic loading, nonlinear constraints, and parameter uncertainty. However, the current model does not explicitly include reheat turbine dynamics, multi-source generation structures, renewable-energy penetration, storage participation, communication delays, or other higher-order practical nonlinear interactions. Therefore, future work will focus on extending the proposed cascaded (2DOF-PDf)–(LTI) controller to more realistic reheat, multi-source, renewable-rich, and higher-order interconnected power-system models, in addition to implementation-oriented validation using real-time simulation or hardware-in-the-loop platforms Additional studies will also consider the impact of communication delays and further nonlinear constraints, as well as experimental validation using hardware-in-the-loop platforms such as OPAL-RT or dSPACE. Moreover, hybrid tuning frameworks that combine CrOA with adaptive or learning-based mechanisms may be explored to reduce computational burden while preserving strong robustness under severe and rapidly changing operating conditions.
