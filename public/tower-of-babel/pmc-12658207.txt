AOH induces oxidative stress and DNA damage in ovarian cancer cells via modulation of GPER1 and HIF1α/PI3K/CLDNs signaling pathway
Authors: Marta Justyna Kozieł-Leszczyńska, Dominika Ewa Habrowska-Górczyńska, Kacper Piotr Leszczyński, Kinga Anna Urbanek, Agnieszka Wanda Piastowska-Ciesielska, Karolina Kowalska
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Claudins (CLDNs) are small proteins that form tight junctions (TJs) in cells and play a key role in various biological processes in cells. Previous results have shown that CLDNs expression can be regulated by hormones and, therefore, can also be regulated by compounds that mimic their action. Such compounds belong to a large group called endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs). Alternariol (AOH), an emerging mycotoxin, contaminates various food products. AOH is reported to be genotoxic and to induce oxidative stress. Previous results showed that AOH is also estrogenic and acts as an androgen receptor (AR) agonist, suggesting that it might act as one of the EDCs, however, its detailed molecular mechanism has not been fully elucidated yet. In this study, we decided to evaluate the interplay between AOH and G protein-coupled estrogen receptor 1 (GPER1) in hormone-dependent human ovarian cancer (OC) cells with a focus on the involvement of hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha/phosphoinositide 3-kinases/protein kinase B (HIF1α/PI3K/Akt) and claudins (CLDNs) signaling pathways in that effect. We observed that AOH induces oxidative stress and DNA damage in OC cells, and this effect is partially mediated by GPER1. We found that the GPER1 antagonist G15 partially mitigated AOH-induced ROS production and significantly reduced DNA damage, confirming the receptor’s role in mediating these effects. Furthermore, we observed the involvement of HIF1α/PI3K/Akt and CLDNs pathways in AOH effect in OC cells. In conclusion, we postulate that AOH has pro-oxidative ability and that this effect is partially mediated by GPER1. Moreover, we postulated that HIF1α/PI3K/Akt and CLDNs participate in AOH action in OC cells, which in turn provides useful information for future toxicological research studies as a new molecular mechanism of AOH.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26042-9.

Introduction

Claudins (CLDNs) are small proteins that form tight junctions (TJs) in cells and play a key role in maintaining homeostasis, transport of substances and communication between cells1. Nevertheless, with the number of publications, it turns out that CLDNs have a greater role than previously speculated in many different cellular processes, including oxidative stress and DNA damage2–4. Moreover, it was shown that the expression of CLDNs is regulated by hormones1, whereas claudin-1 (CLDN1), claudin-3 (CLDN3) and claudin-4 (CLDN4) are reported to be the most sensitive to hormone dysregulation. Since CLDNs can be regulated by hormones, there is a risk that endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) mimicking natural estrogen affect their expression1. Especially, when taking into consideration that their expression and localization are tissue-dependent and change during disease processes1. Especially in the case of endocrine-sensitive cancers like ovarian cancer, in which CLDN3 and CLDN4 were reported to be upregulated3.

The direct influence of EDCs on the proliferation of ovarian cells is linked with classical estrogen signaling5. However, EDCs may affect the cells indirectly by interaction and modulation of other signaling pathways, especially via non-genomic estrogen signaling6,7. G protein-coupled estrogen receptor 1 (GPER1) is known to regulate the expression and activity of the NADPH oxidase (NOX1), an enzyme participating in the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS)8,9. It is also reported that GPER modulates the activation of the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt) and extracellular signal-regulated kinases 1/2 (ERK 1/2) pathways10. The PI3K/Akt signaling pathway is associated with the modulation of many factors, also including those associated with the response to oxidative stress: hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF1α)11. What is more, HIF1α modulates various biological processes in cells such as apoptosis, oxidative stress, proliferation and invasion12,13. Lastly, it has been reported that CLDNs are targeted by HIF1α14.

Some of the mycotoxins, the toxic fungal metabolites present in the daily diet, are considered EDCs15. The best-known estrogenic mycotoxin is zearalenone (ZEN), which, due to its structural similarity to estrogen, binds directly to estrogen receptors (ERs). Another mycotoxin with suggested androgenic and estrogenic properties16 is alternariol (AOH) produced by Alternaria species15. So far, we know that AOH induces oxidative stress and apoptosis and modulates cell cycle progression and invasiveness17–19. Previous results showed that AOH possesses a higher binding affinity to estrogen receptor β (ERβ)20. However, its role in the induction of oxidative stress seems to be only partial21, similar to estrogen receptor α (ERα)19. In that case, the non-genomic action might be an explanation, mediated by non-classical estrogen receptor- GPER122.

Taking into consideration that mycotoxins are reported to modulate the expression of CLDNs in different cell types23 as well as oxidative stress is one of the basic molecular mechanisms of AOH action15, in this study, we decided to evaluate if and how AOH might induce oxidative stress and DNA damage via GPER1/PI3K/HIF1α/CLDNs signaling pathway in hormone-responsive ovarian cancer (OC) cells with the main focus on the role of GPER1 in DNA damage and oxidative stress.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

OVCAR3 cells were obtained from ATCC (LGC, Teddington, England). Cells were cultured in a humidified incubator under standard conditions (5% CO2; 37 °C) and maintained in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin/streptomycin, L-glutamine, sodium pyruvate and 4-(2-Hydroxyethyl) piperazine-1-ethanesulfonic acid (HEPES). Medium without FBS and antibiotics was used as an experimental medium. All the cell culture supplements were derived from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA).

Treatment

AOH (LGC, Teddington, England) was dissolved in DMSO as a stock solution of 0.01 M. GPER1 inhibitor (G15) and GPER1 agonist (G1) were derived from Tocris (Bio-Techne, Bristol, GB) and dissolved in DMSO. All compounds were dissolved directly before use, in the experimental medium, to obtain solutions of the desired concentrations. The final concentration of G1 and G15 was 10 nM and 20 nM, respectively. The concentration of DMSO in the experimental medium was lower than 0.1% and did not affect the viability of cells in all experiments. Based on the results from the viability test and GPER1 activation, two doses of mycotoxin (10 µM and 1 µM) were selected for further study. Cells treated with the experimental medium were used as a control (Cnt).

Cell viability

The viability of OVCAR3 cells was determined with an MTT assay (Merck Millipore, USA). Cells were seeded onto a 96-well plate at a density of 2 × 104. After attachment, cells were treated with the experimental medium containing AOH in a concentration range of 0.001–100 µM. After 20 h, 10 µl of freshly dissolved MTT (5 mg/mL) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was added and incubated in standard conditions for the next 4 h. Then, the medium was removed, and the formazan crystals were dissolved in 100 µl of DMSO. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm (BioTek Synergy Neo 2 plate reader, BioTek Winooski, Vermont, U.S.). Cell viability was expressed as a percentage of Cnt.

Oxidative stress and DNA damage

All flow cytometry assays were conducted with Muse® Cell Analyzer (Merck Millipore, USA). Cells were seeded on 12-well plates and left to reach 70% confluence. Then, they were treated with AOH or a combination of AOH and G15 for 24 h. Then, the assays were conducted, for oxidative stress: Muse® Oxidative Stress Kit (Luminex®; Austin, Texas, USA) and for DNA damage: Muse® Multi-Color DNA Damage Kit (Luminex®; Austin, Texas, USA) according to the instructions provided by the producer. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Confocal microscopy

Cells were seeded onto an 8-well chamber slide at a density of 4 × 104 cells (Nunc ™ Lab-Tek ™ II Chamber Slide™ System; Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc, Waltham, MA, USA). The following day, cells were treated with AOH or a combination of AOH and G15. After 24 h, the medium was removed, and cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (15 min; room temperature). Next, cells were washed three times in 1 × dPBS for 5 min and blocked (5% BSA, 0.3% Triton X-100 in 1 × dPBS) for 1 h at room temperature. After blocking, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody phospho-histone H2A.X (1:800; #9718; Cell Signaling Technology, Leiden, WZ, The Netherlands). The next day, slides were washed three times in PBS for 5 min, then incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the darkness (1:1000; #4412; Cell Signaling Technology, Leiden, WZ, The Netherlands). Afterwards, cells were washed once again as before, and to stain nuclei, NucSpot 555/570 (Biotium, CA, USA #41,033) was used. Next, cells were washed and coverslipped with EverBrite ™ Hardset Mounting Medium (Biotium, CA, USA #23,003). Olympus iXplore SpinSR ScanR (Olympus, Tokyo, JP) was used for visualization- magnification 60 × .

RTqPCR

Cells were seeded onto 60 mm Petri dishes and cultured to achieve 80% confluence. Next, cells were treated for 24 h as explained before. TRIZOL reagent was used for RNA isolation. Then, RNA was isolated, and the concentration was measured with NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc, Waltham, MA, USA). 2 µg of RNA was used for reverse transcription (#4,368,813; Applied Biosystems, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Primers were designed using Primer-BLAST (National Institutes of Health). The Human Reference RNA (Stratagene, San Diego, CA, USA) was used to calibrate the reactions. Ribosomal protein S17 (RPS17), ribosomal protein P0 (RPLP0), and glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) were used as reference genes. Verification of the melting curve was performed for each reaction. The ΔΔCt method was used for data analysis. The experiment was conducted in triplicate with two technical replicates.

Western blot

Cells were seeded onto 100 mm Petri dishes and cultured to reach 70% confluence. Next, cells were treated and after 24 h, were harvested and the total protein was isolated in RIPA buffer with phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The concentration of the protein was measured with BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc, Waltham, MA, USA). 20 μg of protein was used for electrophoresis. Akt (#9272), phospho-Akt (Ser473) (#4060), PARP1 (#46,011), SOD1 (#4266), SOD2 (#13,141), pH2AX (#9718), CLDN1 (#13,995), CLDN3 (#83,609), PI3K (#11,889), p44/42 (#4695), phospho-p44/42 (#4370), CLDN4 (STJ114778), GPER1 (STJ192629) (St John’s Laboratory Ltd, University Way, London) antibodies were used according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. The results were normalized to GAPDH (#2118) or vinculin (#13,901) (Cell Signaling Technology, Leiden, WZ, The Netherlands) as a reference protein. As secondary antibodies, anti-mouse (#7076) and anti-rabbit (#7074) were used (Cell Signaling Technology, Leiden, WZ, The Netherlands). Proteins on membranes were visualized with ChemiDoc MP (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, California, U.S.). Densitometry analyses were performed in the Image Lab software (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, California, U.S.).

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed in the GraphPad Prism program. Statistical significance was determined with One-Way ANOVA with Bonferroni post-hoc test. A p-value lower than 0.05 indicates statistically significant results.

Results

AOH modulates the expression of GPER1, induces oxidative stress and DNA damage in OVCAR3 cells

In our previous study, we evaluated how AOH affects the viability of SKOV3 cells19. In this study, we observed that AOH also decreases the viability of OVCAR3 cells in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1A). The IC 50 value for OVCAR3 cells after 24 h of exposure to AOH was equal to 59.26 µM. Based on these results, the two doses of AOH were chosen (10 µM and 1 µM). Next, RTqPCR and western blot analysis were performed to verify whether these doses affect the expression of GPER1. We observed that AOH increases the expression of GPER1 at gene and protein levels in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1B and C). In that case, we decided to verify whether the induction of oxidative stress is also associated with DNA damage and if GPER1 might have a role in it. Firstly, cells expressing phospho-ataxia telangiectasia mutated kinase (phospho-ATM) and phospho-histone H2A.X (phospho-H2A.X) were stained and counted with flow cytometry. It was observed that 10 µM of AOH significantly increased DNA damage (Fig. 2E and F). The addition of G15 significantly reduced DNA damage compared to AOH treatment alone (Fig. 2E, *p < 0.05). In line with previous results, we also observed increased expression of phospho-H2A.X after treatment with AOH in western blot analysis (Fig. 2G and Table 2). The addition of G15 also decreased this effect (Fig. 2G and Table 2). We also observed changes in the expression of poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP) and its cleaved form at the protein level. 10 µM of AOH decreased the expression of PARP, and the addition of G15 reversed this effect (Fig. 2G and Table 2). In turn, 1 µM of AOH, G1 and G15 alone increased their expression (Fig. 2G and Table 2). In the case of cleaved-PARP, we observed increased expression in all tested groups compared to the control (Fig. 2G and Table 2). The addition of G15 to 1 µM and 10 µM of AOH increased this effect even more (Fig. 2G and Table 2).

Phospho-H2A.X expression and localization were also visualized with confocal microscopy. We observed that 10 µM of AOH significantly increased phospho-H2A.X expression (Fig. 2H and I). Adding G15 significantly (***p < 0.001) reduced the number of phospho-H2AX foci compared to cells treated only with AOH (Fig. 2 and I), suggesting a potential role of GPER1 in AOH- induced DNA damage.

AOH affects HIF1α/PI3K/Akt signaling pathway and modulates the expression of claudins in ovarian cancer cells

Based on the modulation of oxidative stress and DNA damage by AOH, we decided to verify the hypothesis that AOH modulates HIF1α/PI3K/Akt/CLDNs signaling pathway, due to the known interactions between these factors14,24. In the tested cell line, treatment with AOH decreased the expression of HIF1α (Fig. 3A). We also noted that the addition of G15 lowered this expression even further at both doses of AOH (*p < 0.05; **p < 0.01) (Fig. 3A). We also observed that AOH modulated the expression of claudins (Fig. 3B). The increased expression of CLDN1 after treatment with 10 µM of AOH was observed, while the addition of G15 partially decreased this effect (Fig. 3B and Table 3). In the case of CLDN3, we observed decreased expression, and the addition of G15 to AOH did not change it (Fig. 3B and Table 3). Interestingly, G1 itself increased the expression of CLDN3, and the addition of G15 to G1 abolished this effect (Fig. 3B and Table 3). In the case of CLDN4, both doses of AOH increased its expression, similarly to G1 and G15 (Fig. 3B and Table 3). The addition of G15 to the lower dose of AOH increased this effect even more (Fig. 3B and Table 3). In the case of gene expression, we observed that AOH significantly increases CLDN1 expression itself, while the addition of G15 increases it even more, however, statistically insignificant (Fig. 3C). In the case of CLDN3, in OVCAR3 cells we observed a significant decrease after AOH treatment and the addition of G15 abolished this effect to the level of the control (Fig. 3C). In CLDN4 expression, we observed significant upregulation when OVCAR3 cells were treated with a higher dose of AOH (Fig. 3C). The same trend was observed for G1. Discussion

GPER1 so far has not been considered as the target of AOH, possibly due to the fact that in estrogenicity assay it is not taken into consideration. It is well known that the binding affinity of AOH to ERs is much lower than natural estrogen20. Previous studies carried out by us showed that in hormone-dependent cells, none of these classic ERs are fully responsible for the induction of oxidative stress triggered by AOH19,21. Thus, it is highly possible that other mechanisms or more likely interaction with different signaling pathways might be the molecular mechanism of that mycotoxin. A previous study conducted with ZEN, the most potent estrogenic mycotoxin, in colon cells, showed that GPER1 plays a significant role in its effect25. Moreover, the interaction with other signaling pathways is more obvious in the case of GPER1 than in classic ERs26. In this study, we observed that AOH significantly affected the expression of GPER1 in OC cells. A similar effect was observed by Lo et. al., where expression of GPER1 was elevated upon treatment with ZEN in HT-29 and SW480 cells25. Moreover, as suggested before, other environmental EDCs significantly modulate GPER1 expression, even when their estrogenicity is lower than natural estrogens, and the effect mediated by GPER1 is comparable to estrogen in estrogen- responsive cells26. GPER1 was previously reported to participate in bisphenol A (BPA) induced toxicity, with modulation of the cell cycle, and activation of ERK and PI3K/Akt signaling pathway27.

Oxidative stress is a well-established contributor to ovarian carcinogenesis, participating in and mediating genetic alterations, signaling pathways and transcription factors28. AOH stimulates the generation of ROS and induces DNA damage in cells, mainly double-strand DNA breaks (DSB) via interaction with DNA topoisomerase15. We previously presented that AOH induces the generation of ROS in prostate and breast cancer cells21,29. Fernández-Blanco et. al. observed increased oxidative stress in Caco-2 cells after treatment with AOH30. In our present study, we observed that both 1 µM and 10 µM increased ROS production in OVCAR3 cells. However, the blocking of GPER1 only partially decreased the effect stimulated by the higher dose, which indicates that it only partially mediates this effect. We also presented that the increase in ROS-positive cells after AOH treatment was associated with modulation of SOD1, SOD2, PARP1 and XRCC1 expression, but the addition of G15 did not reverse the effect, except for XRCC1, where the addition of G15 increased its expression. This observation suggests activation of pathways involved in the repair of DNA SSB. The modulation of oxidative stress and DNA damage caused by mycotoxins exposure in ovarian cells was also observed previously: Zhang et. al. also observed increased expression of PARP1 and XRCC1 in porcine ovarian granulosa cells after treatment with ochratoxin A (OTA)31. On the protein level, we observed decreased expression of SOD2 after blocking of GPER1 compared to AOH treatment alone. Modulation of SOD2 expression by AOH during oxidative stress induction was also observed in the colorectal adenocarcinoma cell line Caco-230. The role of GPER1 in oxidative stress might be two-fold: it was reported that GPER1 overexpression might protect against oxidative stress in cells32, or participate in pro-apoptotic and oxidative-stress-induced effects33. In our study, it seems that only partially GPER1 participates in AOH-induced oxidative stress. Oxidative stress induced by another EDCs, BPA, was observed in ovarian cancer stem cells in mice34. Similar to our study, BPA decreased the expression of SOD1 and SOD234. Mlynarcikova et. al. presented that bisphenol S (BPS), bisphenol F and bisphenol AF (BPAF) at high concentrations also increased the generation of ROS in ovarian cancer cells35.

Genotoxic effects of AOH were observed in various cell lines at relatively low concentrations (1 µM)36. In this study, we observed that the DNA-damaging effect of AOH was stronger than the induction of oxidative stress. This observation seems to be in line with a previous study made by Solhaug et. al., who observed that AOH in a dose of 30 µM increases the phosphorylation of H2A.X in RAW 264.7 cells18,37. It also seems that the DNA damage caused by AOH exposure might be cell type-sensitive, and hormone- responsive cells seem to be more sensitive than, e.g. macrophages or hepatic cells38. In the case of other EDCs, Chang et. al. showed that mono-ethylhexyl phthalate (MEHP) also induces DNA damage in mammalian Chinese hamster ovary cells39.

CLDNs are TJs proteins that link epithelial cells1. They play a crucial role in carcinogenesis due to their involvement in migration, invasion, metastasis and drug resistance3. Modulation of their expression is linked with PI3K/Akt signaling pathway2. Previous studies showed that AOH might regulate the expression of CLDNs in colon cancer cells40, however, its role is also crucial in OC. Our previous results also showed that AOH modulates the expression of HIF1α40, whereas another study confirmed the interaction between HIF1α and PI3K/Akt/ERK signaling pathway24. In turn, CLDNs were reported to control pathways like PI3K/Akt and ERK 1/241. Thus, it seems that it might be an interaction between these signaling pathways. In our study, we observed that AOH reduces HIF1α expression, while GPER1 blockade reduces its expression even further. Liu et. al. showed the association between CLDNs and HIF1α14. They may be dysregulated or/and translocated by mycotoxins, which was mainly observed in colon cancer cells23, however, CLDNs might also be modulated by hormones1. In our study, we observed modulation of CLDN1, CLDN3 and CLDN4. We observed increased expression of CLDN1 both on protein and gene levels after treatment with 10 µM of AOH. The addition of G15 increased this effect, however, it was statistically insignificant. This observation is consistent with the previous one made by Akimoto et. al., who observed that estrogen upregulates expression of CLDN1 and that this induction was mediated by GPER1 via ERK and/ or Akt signaling42. This is in line with our observation. Moreover, other mycotoxins were also reported to modulate these signaling pathways: ZEN43 and deoxynivalenol (DON)44. In the case of CLDN3, blocking of GPER1 increased the reduced expression by AOH to control levels at the gene level, while at the protein level, we observed increased expression after the addition of G15 to 1 µM of AOH. Previous studies suggested that CLDN3 overexpression modulates the malignant properties of colon cancer cells, and that effect is associated with modulation of PI3K/Akt/ERK via epidermal growth factor (EGFR)45. Interestingly, EGFR is known to directly interact with the GPER1 signaling pathway46, which in turn might confirm our observation that these interactions are present. Other EDCs, are also reported to have the ability to activate PI3K/Akt signaling. For instance, Li et. al. showed that bisphenol P (BPP) and bisphenol M (BPM) promote TNBC metastasis by PI3K/Akt-mediated EMT47. Ptak et. al. showed that BPA induces migration by activating MAPK and PI3K/Akt signalling pathways in OVCAR3 cells48. CLDN4 expression was increased after treatment with AOH, and adding G15 abolished this effect. Cuevas et. al. also observed modulation of CLDN4 expression and localisation after treatment with estradiol in endometrial cancer cells49. Fen et. al. noticed that BPA is able to disrupt intestinal barrier function in mice by affecting the expression of, among others, CLDN150. These interesting observations confirm the observed broad scope of CLDNs role in both physiological and pathological processes in cells1.

Conclusions

Taken together, this study for the first time showed that GPER1 might be involved in AOH- induced oxidative stress and DNA damage in hormone-responsive cells, in this case, OC cells, and suggested that HIF1α/ PI3K/Akt/ERK/CLDNs interaction is involved in that process. This observation shed new light on the known mechanism of AOH action and its potential effect as EDC; however, more detailed molecular studies, as well as in vitro and in vivo-based models, are needed in further research to confirm our observation.
