Effects of blood pressure and blood glucose levels on foveal avascular zone area
Authors: Jin-bao Ma, Ming-zhao Qin, Kai Cao, Yong-peng Zhang, Cai-xia Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore whether blood pressure and blood glucose levels affect the foveal avascular zone (FAZ) area, a key parameter of retinal microvasculature.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional study in which 253 patients with essential hypertension and/or type 2 diabetes were consecutively enrolled from the Department of Geriatrics Health Care at our hospital between January 2023 and January 2024. The FAZ area was quantitatively measured using optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA). A multivariable linear regression model was used to explore the relationship between blood pressure, blood glucose, and the FAZ area.ResultsThe cohort was stratified into four groups based on quartile of FAZ area. The median systolic blood pressure (SBP) for each group was as follows: 121 mmHg, 126 mmHg, 126 mmHg, and 129 mmHg, with a significant intergroup difference (p = 0.046). Spearman correlation analysis revealed a significant positive association between mean SBP and FAZ area (r = 0.151, p < 0.05). Univariable analysis indicated that gender and hemoglobin (HGB) levels were also associated with the FAZ area. Following adjustment for gender and HGB in the linear regression model, mean SBP remained markedly associated with FAZ area (β = 0.0008, p = 0.037).ConclusionMean SBP may serve as a potential risk factor for alterations in the FAZ area. Control of mean SBP holds clinical importance for the prevention of retinal microvascular pathology.Keywords: Foveal avascular zone, Hypertension, Diabetes mellitus, Influencing factors, Retinal microvasculature

Introduction

It is well known that essential hypertension and type 2 diabetes (T2D) are risk factors for cardiovascular diseases. Furthermore, both conditions have been shown to cause microvascular damage, primarily manifested as ischemic alterations in the retinal microvasculature. The retina is the only anatomical structure where arteries, veins, and capillaries can be directly visualized and assessed collectively. Studies showed that retinal microvascular abnormalities can serve as predictors of cardiovascular risks [1–5], and can be detected cost-effectively using fundus imaging [6].

The retina shows a high demand for oxygen and is vulnerable to ischemic injury. Both hypertension and diabetes can induce retinal microvascular ischemic lesions, leading to vision impairment. The foveal avascular zone (FAZ) is an area encircled by a continuous retinal capillary plexus devoid of any capillary structures, and is the thinnest part of the retina. Previous studies reported the occurrence and development of many retinal diseases involve the change of FAZ area [7].

Optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) is a non-invasive imaging technique that visualizes vascular morphology across different retinal layers and quantitatively measures retinal vascular parameters, such as the FAZ area [8, 9]. Hua et al. [10] reported significant alterations, typically enlarged, in the FAZ area in hypertensive patients than control. Similar findings were reported by Lim et al. [11], and Samara et al. [12]. Aldakhil S et al. [13] reported that the FAZ area exhibited a significant increasing trend as the severity of diabetic retinopathy increased. The above findings suggest quantitative evaluation of FAZ area may be a potential biomarker for detecting ocular microvascular events and even systemic macrovascular events. Notably, OCT and OCTA have become valuable tools for evaluating microvascular impairment in other systemic ischemic conditions beyond hypertension and diabetes. For instance, a study on SARS-CoV-2 infection found that systemic hypoxia caused by COVID-19 led to reduced macular vessel density and enlarged FAZ area, highlighting the retina’s sensitivity to systemic oxygen homeostasis [14]. Additionally, in patients with obstructive sleep apnea syndrome, a 1-year follow-up study using OCT observed thinning of the retinal nerve fiber layer—changes attributed to chronic intermittent hypoxia-induced microvascular damage [15]. These findings reinforce that ocular imaging can serve as a non-invasive window into systemic microvascular health, laying the groundwork for our investigation of FAZ changes in hypertension and diabetes.

In the present study, we conducted a cross-sectional study, with relatively large sample size, to explore whether blood pressure and blood glucose had an impact on FAZ area. Multivariate analysis would be performed to control for confounding factors.

Methods

Study design

This study used a cross-sectional study design, and was adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Tongren Hospital (number: TREC2022-KY068). Study registration: ClinicalTrials.gov. The work has been reported in line with the STROCSS criteria [16].

Participants

A total of 253 patients with essential hypertension and/or T2D who were admitted to the Department of Geriatric Healthcare at our hospital between January 2023 and January 2024 were consecutively enrolled.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) essential hypertension; (3) T2D; (4) signed informed consent.

Exclusion criteria for systemic diseases included: (1) active malignant tumors; (2) uncontrolled autoimmune diseases; (3) acute infection; (4) acute coronary syndrome.

Exclusion criteria for ocular diseases or OCTA-related contraindications included: (5) glaucoma; (6) retinal vascular occlusion; (7) contraindication to pupil dilation (which prevents high-quality OCTA imaging of the macular region).

Variables: The outcome was FAZ area. The explanatory variables included: (1) Demographic information: age and gender; (2) Medical history: chronic diseases, surgical history, and medication history; (3) Routine physical examination indicators: height, weight, and initial blood pressure at admission; (4) Environmental exposure information: smoking history and alcohol consumption history; (5) Family history; (6) Laboratory test indicators, including: serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, cystatin C, uric acid, hemoglobin, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, lipoprotein(a), triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, glycated hemoglobin; and 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring.

Data source: The FAZ area was assessed using OCTA (Optovue, Inc., Fremont, CA; Model: RTVue XR) (Fig. 1), with 6 × 6 mm macular scans obtained for both eyes. Ophthalmic examinations and OCTA imaging (following pupil dilation) were performed by qualified ophthalmic technicians with more than 2 years of clinical experience.

FAZ area measurement using OCTA

Statistical methods

For continuous variables (e.g., age, SBP, FAZ area), normality was first tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data are described as “mean ± standard deviation (SD),” and inter-group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA); non-normally distributed data are described as “median [interquartile range, IQR],” and inter-group comparisons were conducted using the Kruskal-Wallis H test. Categorical variables (e.g., gender) are described as “frequency (percentage),” and inter-group comparisons were performed using the chi-square test. The linear associations between variables and FAZ area were initially screened using Spearman correlation analysis to exclude variables with no association. Finally, a multivariable linear regression model was used to control for confounding factors including gender and HGB, thereby verifying the independent associations between blood pressure/glucose and FAZ area. Only the right eyes were included in the analysis. All analyses were conducted using the R programming (https://www.r-project.org/, version 4.3.2). The significance level was set to be 0.05, two tailed.

Results

Participants

In accordance with the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 253 patients with essential hypertension and/or T2D were included in the study.

The screening procedure was conducted as follows: Initially, 287 consecutive patients were screened. After the exclusion of two patients with active malignant tumors, two with uncontrolled autoimmune diseases, three with acute infections, one with acute coronary syndrome, one with glaucoma, and two with retinal vascular occlusion, 23 additional patients were excluded due to contraindications for pupil dilation or incomplete OCTA data. Ultimately, 253 patients were retained for analysis (Fig. 2).

Demographic characteristics of patients

Among the participants, 192 (73.3%) were males, and the mean age was 63.6 ± 11.6 years. The mean height and weight were 169 ± 9.7 cm and 72.4 ± 13.1 kg, respectively, while the mean systolic blood pressure (SBP) was 127 ± 15.6 mmHg.

Multivariable analysis

Given the involvement of multiple variables in the FAZ area and the presence of potential confounding factors, a multivariable linear regression analysis was conducted (Table 3). The analysis revealed that both gender and mean SBP were significant factors influencing the FAZ area. Specifically, females were found to have larger FAZ areas compared to males, and higher SBP values were associated with an increase in FAZ area. Specifically, with every 1 mmHg increase in mean SBP, a corresponding rise of 0.0008 units in the FAZ area was observed. Collectively, gender and mean SBP accounted for 5% of the variance in the FAZ area.

Discussion

The normal microcirculation of the posterior pole retina is typically classified into four distinct layers: the superficial nerve fiber layer plexus, the superficial vascular plexus, the intermediate vascular plexus, and the deep vascular plexus.These plexuses converge around the fovea to delineate the boundary of the FAZ. Since the 1980s, several studies have utilized various imaging techniques, including FFA [17], scanning laser ophthalmoscopy [18], and retinal function imaging [19], to assess the FAZ area. However, these methods are constrained by limitations in measurement precision. The introduction of OCTA has revolutionized retinal vessel imaging, offering notable advantages such as speed, non-invasiveness, safety, and short examination, durations. These benefits have resulted in excellent reproducibility and enhanced accuracy in FAZ area measurement. It is important to highlight that, with the availability of multiple OCTA devices and software systems, measurements of the FAZ area obtained from different devices, analysis software, and parameter settings are not directly comparable. Magrath et al. [20] evaluated the FAZ in healthy individuals using both the Optovue RTVue XR Avanti and Zeiss Cirrus HD-OCT 5000, reporting high consistency in measurements when performed with the same device and software. As OCTA technology continues to evolve, more precise and reliable data analysis of the FAZ region is expected to be achieved.

OCTA is a technological advancement derived from optical coherence tomography. OCT operates by employing interferometric techniques to detect reflected light signals from tissue structures, which are then used to generate images, of these tissues. This process is analogous to the principles applied in ultrasound imaging. During repeated scanning of the same tissue area, the reflected light signals, which include amplitude, phase, and frequency, undergo changes over time due to the movement of blood cells.In contrast, the signals reflected from stationary tissues remain relatively unchanged. OCTA exploits these variations in light signals induced by blood flow to extract information on blood circulation. Through advanced software, it synthesizes these data into blood flow images and generates en-face images of retinal vessels at different layers, thereby facilitating angiographic imaging [21, 22].

The FAZ area in healthy individuals is subject to various influencing factors, with age, gender, central foveal thickness (CFT), and axial length being identified as the key determinants [23–25]. Notably, no significant difference in the FAZ area has been observed between the two eyes in healthy individuals; however, females generally exhibit larger FAZ areas compared to males, and the FAZ area shows variation in relation to both gender and CFT [23]. In a study using Optovue RTVue XR OCTA with AngioVue software to assess the FAZ in healthy subjects, Iafe et al. [25] reported a mean FAZ area of (0.289 ± 0.108)mm², with an increase in FAZ area observed with advancing age.

In pathological conditions, the expansion of the FAZ area is primarily attributed to the disruption of capillary networks surrounding the FAZ, with both blood pressure and blood glucose levels identified as significant contributing factors. The current study employed the RTVue XR Avanti System to measure the FAZ area in patients with essential hypertension and/or diabetes, exploring the various factors influencing this area. Multivariable linear regression analysis demonstrated that the FAZ area was larger in females and exhibited a positive correlation with mean SBP levels, while showing a negative correlation with HGB levels. In a study by Ghassemi et al. [24], which included 112 healthy individuals from different ethnic backgrounds, it was found that females had markedly larger FAZ areas in both the superficial and deep capillary layers compared to males, a finding that was corroborated by other research [24]. However, some studies have reported no significant correlation between FAZ and gender. Wylezgala et al. [26] observed a notably higher superficial retinal capillary density in the Chinese Han population compared to Caucasians and concluded that there were no substantial gender differences in the FAZ area. These discrepancies may be attributed to racial variations, highlighting the need for further investigations involving larger and more diverse ethnic groups to clarify these findings.

This study identified a correlation between HGB levels and FAZ area, a relationship not previously documented in comparable research. This association may be attributed to the retina’s heightened sensitivity to hypoxia, where lower HGB levels lead to a diminished oxygen-carrying capacity and subsequent reduction in oxygen supply, resulting in damage to the retinal microvasculature. No significant correlation between blood glucose levels and FAZ area was found in the univariable analysis, possibly due to the early stage of diabetic retinopathy in the patient cohort. These results are consistent with those reported by Cao et al. [27], who observed that patients with T2D but without diabetic retinopathy exhibited reduced vessel density in the superficial and deep capillary plexuses as well as in the choriocapillaris when compared to healthy controls. However, no significant differences were found in the superficial FAZ area. These observations suggest that microvascular damage in the superficial and deep capillary plexuses, as well as the choriocapillaris, precedes the onset of diabetic retinopathy. This phenomenon can be explained by early alterations in the deep capillary plexus, which is less perfused and, therefore, more vulnerable to hypoxic injury, whereas the superficial capillary plexus, being directly connected to retinal arterioles with higher perfusion pressure and oxygen supply, is better able to resist hypoxic damage, resulting in only minimal changes to the superficial FAZ.

Both univariable analyses (including Kruskal-Wallis group comparison by FAZ quartiles and Spearman correlation) and multivariable linear regression confirmed an association between elevated mean SBP and increased FAZ area, a finding that aligns with results from previous studies. Research by Hua [10] and Lim [11] also corroborated the observation that patients with longstanding essential hypertension exhibited markedly larger FAZ areas. A meta-analysis of 11 studies showed that participants with systemic hypertension had significantly larger superficial FAZ area [28]. The underlying mechanism of hypertensive microvascular damage is thought to involve alterations in microvascular tension, leading to vasoconstriction and compromised perfusion, with long-term effects resulting in structural changes in the vascular anatomy, which may cause vessel occlusion, reduced perfusion, or even vessel loss. Consequently, this study suggests that early intervention in essential hypertension could mitigate the enlargement of the FAZ, with OCTA follow-up potentially serving as an effective tool for monitoring retinal microvascular changes.

The absence of an association between blood glucose parameters (HbA1c) and FAZ area might be explained by the early diabetes stage: 85.23% (225/264) of our diabetic patients had HbA1c < 8.0% and 90.53% (239/264) had no clinical diabetic retinopathy. FAZ enlargement typically occurs in advanced retinopathy (e.g., proliferative diabetic retinopathy), which was rare in our cohort. Studies reporting FAZ enlargement in diabetics [12] included patients with diabetic macular ischemia (a late-stage complication), whereas our cohort had no such patients. This suggests that FAZ area is not a sensitive marker for early diabetic microangiopathy, but may be useful for late-stage disease. Future studies could stratify diabetic patients by retinopathy stage (early vs. late) to clarify the stage-specific association between blood glucose and FAZ area.

Clinically, our findings support the use of OCTA-based FAZ area monitoring as a non-invasive tool for early assessment of retinal microvascular damage in hypertensive patients. For example, in primary care or geriatric clinics, annual OCTA examinations of hypertensive patients (especially those with poorly controlled SBP) could help detect early FAZ enlargement—before overt vision symptoms appear. Timely intervention to lower SBP (e.g., adjusting antihypertensive medications to target SBP < 130 mmHg) may then mitigate FAZ enlargement and reduce the risk of progressive retinal microvascular pathology (e.g., macular ischemia). This approach avoids the need for invasive vascular assessments (e.g., coronary angiography) and provides a patient-friendly way to monitor systemic microvascular health via the retina.

Strengths

This study included a relatively large sample size, and performed standardized OCTA examination, blood and urine tests, and so on. The confounding factors were adjusted in multivariable analysis.

Limitations

Since this is not a cohort study, the causal relationship between blood pressure and FAZ area changes could not be determined.One potential limitation is the use of a single OCTA device (Optovue RTVue XR). As Magrath et al. [20] reported, FAZ measurements can vary across different OCTA platforms (e.g., Optovue vs. Zeiss Cirrus HD-OCT 5000) due to differences in segmentation algorithms (e.g., how the superficial capillary plexus is defined) and scanning resolution. However, Magrath et al. also confirmed that FAZ measurements using the same device and software (e.g., Optovue RTVue XR with AngioVue) have high intra-device consistency (intraclass correlation coefficient >0.85), which ensures the reliability of our FAZ data. Future multicenter studies could include multiple OCTA devices to validate whether the association between mean SBP and FAZ area is consistent across different platforms. Furthermore, this study lacked an age-matched healthy control group free of hypertension and diabetes. Including such a group would have allowed us to compare FAZ area between patients and healthy individuals, and confirm whether the FAZ enlargement observed in the high-SBP subgroup is specific to hypertension (rather than normal aging). For example, Iafe et al. [25] reported that healthy adults have a mean FAZ area of 0.289 ± 0.108 mm², which is smaller than the mean FAZ area of our hypertensive patients (0.30 ± 0.04 mm²). However, without direct comparison, we cannot exclude the possibility that aging contributes to part of the FAZ enlargement. Future studies with a control group are needed to validate the specificity of FAZ changes to hypertension. Another critical limitation of this cross-sectional study is its inability to establish a causal relationship between mean SBP and FAZ area enlargement. While we observed a positive association (β = 0.0008, p = 0.037), three alternative explanations cannot be excluded: (1) Elevated SBP causes FAZ enlargement (via microvascular tension and capillary loss); (2) FAZ enlargement precedes SBP elevation (e.g., retinal microvascular damage reflects early systemic vascular dysfunction); (3) A third unmeasured factor (e.g., long-term high-salt diet) contributes to both. To resolve this, future cohort studies should follow hypertensive patients for a certain years, measuring SBP and FAZ area at baseline and follow-up—if elevated baseline SBP predicts subsequent FAZ enlargement, it would support a causal direction. Moreover, the multivariable linear regression model explained only ~ 5% of the variance in FAZ area, indicating that other unmeasured factors contribute significantly to FAZ variability. Our study did not collect axial length or CFT data, which may have reduced the model’s explanatory power. Additionally, systemic factors such as smoking intensity (pack-years) and chronic kidney disease (eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m²) were not included, despite their potential association with retinal microvascular damage. Future studies should incorporate these ocular and systemic variables to develop a more comprehensive model with higher explanatory power. Besides, retinal parameters (e.g., FAZ area, vessel density) between the two eyes of the same individual are highly correlated, and including both eyes would violate the‘independence of observations’ assumption in linear regression, leading to inflated Type I error. However, this approach also results in the loss of left-eye data—for example, if a patient’s right eye had poor OCTA quality (but left eye was normal), we could not use the left eye data. Future studies could use mixed-effects models to analyze both eyes, with‘patient ID’as a random effect to account for inter-eye correlation, thereby utilizing all available data. Lastly, in our study, each patient received a different treatment regimen. For hypertensive patients, some were treated with calcium channel blockers, some with angiotensin II receptor blockers, some with angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, some with beta-blockers, some with diuretics, and others with a combination of these drugs. Similarly, the treatment regimens for diabetic patients also showed significant variability. Future studies should expand the sample size to conduct subgroup analyses and further control for confounding factors.

Conclusion

There is a positive correlation between elevated mean SBP and the enlarged FAZ area, indicating that effective blood pressure management could potentially mitigate FAZ enlargement, which is of significant clinical relevance in the prevention of retinal microvascular complications.
