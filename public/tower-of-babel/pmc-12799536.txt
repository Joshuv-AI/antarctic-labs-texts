Impact of breast reconstruction on quality of life among women after mastectomy
Authors: Anthony M. Ban, Branka V. Ličanin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesBreast reconstruction after mastectomy represents an important component of rehabilitation for women who have survived breast cancer, although it is not considered a mandatory part of treatment. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of breast reconstruction on various aspects of quality of life, including physical, emotional, and social well-being, and to assess its contribution to overall patient satisfaction.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted involving 100 women who had undergone breast reconstruction within the past seven years. A specifically developed five-point Likert scale was used to assess perceptions of physical comfort, emotional well-being, self-esteem, and social interaction. Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), with descriptive and inferential statistical methods applied.ResultsThe analysis revealed significant improvements in the overall quality of life among women who underwent breast reconstruction. Statistically significant differences were observed in domains of physical comfort, emotional stability, and self-confidence. Participants also reported enhanced social interactions and greater life satisfaction. These findings indicate that breast reconstruction contributes not only to physical recovery but also to emotional and social reintegration following mastectomy.ConclusionsBreast reconstruction after mastectomy has a positive impact on women's quality of life by improving physical condition, emotional stability, and social functioning. The results highlight the importance of including breast reconstruction as an integral part of post-mastectomy rehabilitation. Future research should focus on refining rehabilitation protocols and developing comprehensive support systems to further enhance patient outcomes and well-being.

Introduction

Breast cancer is one of the most prevalent cancers among women and represents a major global public health challenge, with profound physical, emotional, and social consequences.1,2 Mastectomy, although often essential to remove the tumor and reduce the risk of recurrence, can negatively affect body image, emotional well-being, and social functioning, creating long-lasting challenges for survivors.3

Breast reconstruction has therefore become a key rehabilitative option, offering women the opportunity to restore physical integrity and improve psychosocial well-being. Studies using validated tools such as the BREAST-Q have shown that reconstruction enhances body image and quality of life after mastectomy.4,5 More recent findings also confirm that reconstruction contributes to improved emotional stability and social participation.6,7

However, most available evidence comes from Western Europe and North America, while data from Central and Eastern Europe remain limited. This lack of regional evidence creates a research gap that restricts the ability to design rehabilitation protocols tailored to local cultural and healthcare contexts.

The aim of this study is to investigate the impact of breast reconstruction on the quality of life of women after mastectomy in Croatia. Specifically, the research focuses on physical comfort, emotional stability, self-confidence, and social interactions. By addressing the identified gap, the study seeks to provide evidence that will inform clinical practice and support strategies, ultimately contributing to a more comprehensive and patient-centered approach to rehabilitation after breast cancer.

Breast cancer: epidemiological overview, classification, and risk factors

Breast cancer is one of the most common forms of cancer among women, with serious consequences for health and quality of life.1 The prevalence of this disease in a global context makes it a public health problem of exceptional importance. Every year, millions of women worldwide are diagnosed with breast cancer,8 further emphasizing the need to investigate various aspects of this disease, including diagnosis, treatment, rehabilitation, and its impact on quality of life.

Breast cancer can be classified according to histological types, stages of the disease, and the presence of hormone receptors, which together form the basis for determining prognosis and planning optimal treatment. Histologically, the most common type is invasive ductal carcinoma, which arises in the milk ducts and tends to spread to surrounding tissues. Another common type is lobular carcinoma, which originates in the lobes of the breast and is characterized by a slightly different pattern of growth and spread. In addition to these, there are less common but specific forms, such as tubular, mucinous, and medullary carcinoma, which differ from each other in cell appearance, growth patterns, and clinical behavior. The stages of the disease are determined based on tumor size, lymph node involvement, and the presence of distant metastases.9,10 This TNM (Tumor, Node, Metastasis) classification allows the division of the disease into four main stages, from I to IV. Stage I indicates a smaller tumor without lymph node involvement and no signs of spread to other parts of the body, while stage IV indicates advanced cancer with distant metastases, most often in the bones, liver, lungs or brain.11,12 This classification is extremely important because it allows for precise determination of the extent of the disease and selection of the most effective therapeutic approach. In addition, it is important to determine the presence of hormone receptors, such as estrogen (ER) and progesterone (PR) receptors, as well as the expression of the HER2 protein, because these biomarkers significantly influence the choice of therapy – for example, hormonal therapy for ER/PR-positive tumors or targeted therapy for HER2-positive cancers. The combination of histological analysis, staging and molecular characterization forms the basis for a personalized approach to breast cancer treatment.13,14

Risk factors for the development of breast cancer are numerous. They can be categorized into several groups: genetic factors – Inherited mutations, particularly in the BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes, significantly increase the risk of developing breast cancer15, 16, 17, 18; hormonal factors – Prolonged exposure to estrogen, due to late onset of menopause, early onset of menstruation, or hormone therapy, may contribute to the development of the disease12,15, 16, 17,19; lifestyle habits – Factors such as physical inactivity, obesity, excessive alcohol consumption, and smoking are considered to increase the risk12,16,18,20,21; age – The risk of breast cancer increases with age, and in most cases, it is diagnosed in women over the age of 5012,17,19,20,21.

Symptoms and diagnosis

Breast cancer symptoms can vary greatly from person to person, but there are some common signs that you should pay special attention to. One of the most common symptoms is a lump or thickening, which can be felt as a firm or thickened mass within the breast or under the armpit. These changes can often be the first sign of an abnormality.22, 23, 24, 25 Also, significant changes in the shape or size of the breast may indicate a problem, especially if there is asymmetry between the breasts or if the skin color in the affected area changes.19,26 Nipple discharge, especially if it contains blood or is of an unusual color and consistency, is often a cause for concern and may signal an urgent medical evaluation.27,28 In addition, changes in the skin of the breast, including redness, peeling, dimpling, or a wrinkled appearance of the skin, may also indicate the presence of disease and require further diagnostic workup.29 Recognizing these symptoms and responding promptly is key to early detection and treatment of breast cancer.

Diagnostic methods for breast cancer include a series of clinical and radiological procedures that together enable precise detection and confirmation of the disease. Mammography is the basic and most commonly used screening method, especially in women over 40 years of age, and plays a key role in the early detection of cancer. Regular mammography examinations can detect small, yet subtle changes within the breast tissue, which are often invisible or insensitive to the touch, which allows treatment to be started at an early stage of the disease when the prognosis is much more favorable.30, 31, 32, 33 Breast ultrasound is used as a complementary diagnostic method, especially in women with dense glandular tissue, where mammography may be less reliable. This method provides a more detailed view of the structure of suspicious lumps and helps distinguish solid tumors from fluid-filled cysts.34, 35, 36 Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the breast is used in specific cases when additional information is needed that other methods cannot provide, for example, when planning a surgical procedure, evaluating the effect of therapy, or assessing the involvement of multiple foci of disease. MRI is particularly useful in diagnostics in women at high risk of breast cancer, as well as in more complex clinical cases.37,38The most reliable method for definitive confirmation of breast cancer is a biopsy, in which a tissue sample is taken from the suspected area and subjected to histological analysis. Biopsy allows for precise determination of the presence of malignant cells, their characteristics and possible hormone dependence of the tumor, which is of crucial importance for the choice of therapy.39,40 The integration of these methods allows for comprehensive and precise diagnostic work-up, which directly affects the success of treatment and the outcome of the disease.

Treatment options

Breast cancer treatment can be individualized and tailored to the specific needs of each patient, taking into account the stage of the disease, histological type, the presence of hormone receptors, and the patient's general health. Therapeutic approaches include surgical, pharmacological, and radiological methods, which are often combined to achieve the best possible outcome.8,41,42

Surgery is a fundamental component of breast cancer treatment in most cases, with the goal of achieving local control of the disease and reducing the risk of recurrence. The two main surgical methods used are mastectomy and lumpectomy. Mastectomy involves the complete removal of the entire breast, and may also include removal of nearby lymph nodes and, in some cases, part of the pectoral muscle, depending on the extent of the tumor. There are different types of mastectomy, including total, modified radical, and radical mastectomy, which are selected based on the stage of the disease and the presence of metastases. On the other hand, lumpectomy, also known as breast-sparing surgery, involves removing the tumor itself along with a small amount of healthy tissue surrounding it, thus preserving as much of the breast as possible. This procedure almost always requires subsequent adjuvant radiotherapy to destroy any remaining malignant cells and thereby reduce the risk of local recurrence. The choice between mastectomy and lumpectomy is based on several factors, including the size of the tumor in relation to the size of the breast, the location of the tumor, the number of foci of cancer, the presence of genetic mutations (e.g., BRCA1/2), the patient's general health, and her personal preferences. When the tumor size allows, that is, when the tumor is relatively small in relation to the size of the breast and localized in a way that allows for the preservation of the aesthetic appearance, patients are often recommended to undergo conservative surgery. In contrast, in cases of larger tumors, multifocal disease, repeated recurrences, or the presence of mutations that carry a high risk for the development of a new primary cancer, mastectomy is recommended.8,43,44 A multidisciplinary team plays a key role in deciding on the type of surgical procedure, which, together with the patient, considers medical indications, aesthetic and psychological aspects, and long-term treatment outcomes, ensuring an individualized approach that best suits the health needs and life priorities of each woman.

Chemotherapy is one of the key modalities of systemic treatment of breast cancer and is often used as an adjunct to surgery. It may be indicated in a neoadjuvant or adjuvant setting, depending on the stage of the disease, tumor biology, and therapeutic goals. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy is administered before surgery to reduce the size of the primary tumor, thereby increasing the possibility of performing breast-sparing surgery and allowing assessment of the therapeutic response to cytotoxic drugs. This strategy is particularly useful in patients with large tumors, tumors that involve the skin or chest wall, and specific molecular subtypes of breast cancer, such as triple-negative (TNBC) or HER2-positive cancer. Adjuvant chemotherapy, on the other hand, is administered after surgery to eradicate microscopic remnants of tumor cells that are not clinically or pathologically detectable and that could lead to disease recurrence. This significantly reduces the risk of systemic dissemination and increases the overall survival of patients. The decision to use adjuvant chemotherapy is based on a number of prognostic and predictive factors, including tumor size, lymph node status, histological characteristics, hormone receptor status, HER2 status, as well as the results of molecular profiling of the tumor. In addition to the above indications, chemotherapy is also used in the treatment of advanced (metastatic) breast cancer, where the goal is to slow the progression of the disease, alleviate symptoms and prolong survival while maintaining quality of life. In this context, chemotherapy regimens are selected individually, taking into account previous therapies, biological characteristics of the tumor, the general condition of the patient and the goals of treatment.45, 46, 47

Radiotherapy, or radiation therapy, is a standard form of adjuvant treatment for breast cancer and is used primarily after surgery, with the aim of destroying any remaining microscopic malignant cells and reducing the risk of local recurrence. It plays a particularly important role after breast-conserving surgeries, such as lumpectomy, where preserving a larger part of the breast increases the possibility of residual tumor tissue remaining. Numerous randomized studies have shown that adjuvant radiotherapy after lumpectomy significantly reduces the rate of local recurrence of the disease and contributes to an increase in the overall survival of patients. In addition to lumpectomy, radiotherapy can also be used after mastectomy, especially in cases where unfavorable prognostic factors are present, such as lymph node involvement, a larger primary tumor, skin or chest wall invasion. The application of radiation in such cases is directed to the chest wall and/or regional lymphatic drainage, including the axillary, supraclavicular and internal thoracic lymph nodes. Radiotherapy planning is based on the individual characteristics of the disease and the patient, with the use of advanced techniques, such as three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy (3D-CRT) and intensity modulated radiotherapy (IMRT), which enable precise targeting of the radiation dose with minimal damage to the surrounding healthy tissue. This further optimizes the therapeutic effect and reduces the risk of late side effects, including lung, heart or skin damage. Radiotherapy, therefore, represents a key component of the multimodal treatment of breast cancer, contributing not only to local control of the disease, but also to the improvement of long-term oncological outcomes8,48.

Hormone therapy plays a key role in the treatment of hormone-dependent breast cancers, i.e., those tumors that express estrogen (ER) and/or progesterone receptors (PR). The presence of hormone receptors on tumor cells is an important predictive biomarker of therapeutic response to endocrine therapy. The goal of hormone therapy is to interfere with the stimulatory effect of sex hormones on the growth and proliferation of malignant cells, thereby slowing or completely stopping the progression of the disease.49,50 In therapeutic practice, two main groups of drugs are used: selective estrogen receptor modulators (e.g., tamoxifen) and aromatase inhibitors (e.g., anastrozole, letrozole, exemestane). Tamoxifen works by competitively blocking estrogen receptors in breast cells, while aromatase inhibitors prevent the peripheral conversion of androgens into estrogens, thereby significantly reducing the concentration of estrogen in the circulation, which is particularly important in postmenopausal women.51,52 Hormone therapy can be used in the adjuvant setting, after surgery, to reduce the risk of local or systemic recurrence, as well as in the neoadjuvant setting to reduce tumor size before surgery. Hormone therapy is also a fundamental treatment for patients with metastatic hormone-dependent breast cancer, where it can significantly prolong survival and preserve quality of life with minimal toxicity compared to chemotherapy.53,54 The duration of hormonal therapy in adjuvant treatment is typically five years, and in certain cases it can be extended to ten years, depending on the individual risk profile and tolerability of the treatment. The introduction of new drugs, such as CDK4/6 inhibitors in combination with endocrine therapy, has further improved the therapeutic options for patients with hormone-dependent cancers, especially in the metastatic stage of the disease.50,55

Targeted therapies represent a modern form of treatment based on precise action on specific molecular targets involved in tumor growth and spread. In the context of breast cancer, therapies targeting human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), which is overexpressed in approximately 15–20 % of cases, play a particularly significant role. The presence of HER2-positive status is associated with a more aggressive clinical course of the disease and a worse prognosis. However, the introduction of HER2-targeted therapies, such as trastuzumab, pertuzumab, trastuzumab emtansine (T-DM1) and trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd), significantly improved treatment outcomes in this group of patients.56 For example, adjuvant use of trastuzumab in combination with chemotherapy in patients with early HER2-positive breast cancer resulted in significantly improved disease-free survival and overall survival compared with chemotherapy without trastuzumab. Furthermore, studies have shown that the addition of pertuzumab to trastuzumab and chemotherapy further reduces the risk of disease recurrence. For patients with residual disease after neoadjuvant therapy, adjuvant use of T-DM1 significantly reduces the risk of invasive breast cancer or death compared with continuation of trastuzumab.57,58 In the metastatic setting, the combination of trastuzumab, pertuzumab, and chemotherapy has shown superiority in prolonging overall survival compared with trastuzumab and chemotherapy alone. Furthermore, novel therapies such as T-DXd have shown promising results in the treatment of patients with advanced HER2-positive breast cancer, including those with brain metastases.59,60 The combination of the above methods, adapted to each individual case, provides a comprehensive and effective approach to the treatment of breast cancer, with the aim of improving the survival and quality of life of patients. An individualized approach to treatment, which takes into account the molecular characteristics of the tumor, the stage of the disease and the patient's general state of health, is key to optimizing therapeutic outcomes.

The impact of mastectomy

Mastectomy, as one of the key surgical interventions in the treatment of breast cancer, represents an event that has deep emotional, psychological and social consequences for women who face this procedure. The loss of a breast does not only represent a physical change, but involves essential aspects of identity, where the breast symbolically participates in defining femininity, sexuality, motherhood and the individual sense of belonging to one's own body. Therefore, the removal of the breast can result in a pronounced feeling of loss of identity and a violation of the perception of one's own personality, which is documented in contemporary research.2,3,61

In addition to the violation of identity integrity, the physical changes caused by mastectomy often lead to a significant drop in self-confidence. A change in body image can affect a decrease in the feeling of attractiveness and sexual adequacy, which can further weaken interpersonal relationships and make it difficult to emotionally adapt to the new situation. It has been observed that women often internalize the feeling of physical inferiority, which manifests itself through social withdrawal, reduced participation in social activities and difficulties in establishing or maintaining intimate relationships.62, 63, 64

A special psychological burden is the fear of disease recurrence, which, despite the successful completion of the primary treatment, remains pronounced in a large number of patients. The fear of the recurrence of cancer contributes to the development of anxiety, depressive symptoms and chronic psychological distress, which significantly impairs the quality of life. Research indicates that this fear is often persistent and can be activated by even the smallest somatic symptoms, leading to a constant state of hypervigilance and emotional exhaustion.65, 66, 67

In addition to individual emotional difficulties, women often face pronounced social challenges after mastectomy. Changes in body image and psychological distress can result in feelings of isolation, stigmatization and difficulties in maintaining social ties.2,68,69 Within partner relationships, there is a fear of rejection and misunderstanding, which can further deepen the feeling of loneliness and inadequacy. In the context of these complex consequences, mastectomy must be seen not only as a medical procedure, but as an event that requires a comprehensive, multidisciplinary approach to the rehabilitation of patients.68,70,71

Considering the complexity of psychological and social consequences arising from mastectomy, it is essential to develop and implement psychosocial support programs that include individual and group psychotherapy, psychoeducation, support in accepting body changes, as well as interventions aimed at preserving and improving interpersonal relationships. The integration of psychological help in all stages of treatment significantly contributes to improving the quality of life, strengthening emotional resistance and more successful long-term adaptation to life after mastectomy.

The impact of breast reconstruction on quality of life

One of the most important dimensions of quality of life after breast reconstruction is body image. Many women report a reduction in negative feelings about their bodies after breast reconstruction. According to Pusic et al., patients who have had breast reconstruction report higher levels of body satisfaction and feelings of femininity compared to those who have had no breasts. The same study developed the BREAST-Q questionnaire, a standardized tool that allows for the quantification of aspects of satisfaction and psychosocial well-being after breast surgery.4 Using the BREAST-Q measures, numerous subsequent studies have confirmed the positive association between reconstruction and improved body image perception5,7,72,73,74,75,76,77,78,79,80,81,82,83,84,85,86,87

Psychological and emotional aspects – The psychological and emotional aspects of breast loss after mastectomy represent an extremely important area in the comprehensive care of patients with breast cancer. Breast loss often leads to profound changes in self-perception, experience of one's own femininity and body identity, which can result in significant psychological difficulties. Depressive reactions, increased anxiety, feelings of shame, reduced self-esteem and impaired self-confidence are common emotional responses to this type of body loss.6,88, 89, 90 These emotional disturbances not only affect the daily functioning of patients, but can also seriously impair their ability to cope with the disease, adherence to therapeutic protocols and overall quality of life.

In this context, breast reconstruction has a role that goes beyond mere aesthetic correction. Reconstruction not only restores lost body image, but also represents a key element of psychological rehabilitation, helping patients regain a sense of wholeness, control over their own bodies, and a stable sense of identity. Numerous studies have shown that women who choose to undergo breast reconstruction experience lower rates of depression and anxiety, and report greater satisfaction with their bodies and emotional states, compared to women who choose to undergo mastectomy without reconstruction.6,88

The difference between immediate and delayed reconstruction is particularly significant in terms of emotional outcomes. Immediate reconstruction, performed at the same time as mastectomy, allows patients to maintain continuity of physical appearance, minimizes the experience of physical loss, and accelerates psychological adjustment to the diagnosis and treatment89.90.Compared to delayed reconstruction, which is carried out months or years after the primary surgery, immediate reconstruction reduces the traumatic experience of physical change and contributes to faster psychological recovery. It also enables earlier reintegration into social and professional activities, which further strengthens emotional well-being and overall satisfaction with life.

All of the above points to the importance of including psychological evaluation and adequate support in the decision-making process on the type and timing of breast reconstruction, with the aim of optimizing the emotional recovery and quality of life of patients after mastectomy.

Socil relations and interpersonal communication – Social relations and interpersonal communication after mastectomy often go through significant changes that can further burden the emotional state of patients. The quality of social interactions, especially within close and intimate relationships, can be impaired due to feelings of physical inferiority, changed body image and reduced sexual security. Many women report increased emotional distance, fear of rejection, and decreased spontaneity in relationships after breast loss, which can lead to withdrawal from intimate interactions and a general feeling of isolation.91, 92, 93

Breast reconstruction in this context plays an important role in facilitating the re-establishment of emotional and sexual closeness. By restoring the contours of the body and restoring the impressiveness of the body image, reconstruction can contribute to reducing feelings of shame, increasing self-confidence and restoring positive sexual identification, which ultimately facilitates more open and better communication with the partner. Research indicates that patients who have undergone reconstructive procedures more often report higher levels of emotional security, greater readiness for physical intimacy, and better overall quality of partner relationships compared to women who did not have reconstruction.94,95

In addition to partner relationships, mastectomy and reconstruction also affect patients' wider social networks. People who perceive greater support from their environment and who experience less stigmatization are more likely to adapt to changes in their bodies and illness. Social support, including support from family, friends and health professionals, is crucial for preventing feelings of isolation and loneliness, further emphasizing the importance of an integrated approach to the care of women after mastectomy.96,97

U konačnici, rekonstrukcija dojke ne donosi samo estetske ili funkcionalne koristi, već ima potencijal dubokog utjecaja na psihosocijalnu rehabilitaciju pacijentica, promičući bolje interpersonalne odnose, emocionalnu bliskost i društvenu integraciju.

Types of reconstruction and satisfaction – The type of breast reconstruction after mastectomy has a significant impact on the quality of life and psychological satisfaction of patients. The two main categories of reconstructive procedures are reconstruction using autologous tissue and reconstruction using implants. Autologous methods, which involve the use of the patient's own body tissue, such as free abdominal flaps (DIEP - Deep Inferior Epigastric Perforator), latissimus dorsi flaps, or gluteal flaps, are often associated with higher levels of satisfaction due to the more natural look and feel of the reconstructed breast.84,85,98, 99, 100, 101 Patients who have undergone autologous reconstruction report a better psychosocial adjustment, a higher degree of body confidence and a reduced feeling of alienation in relation to the reconstructed organ. However, autologous reconstruction involves technically more complex surgical procedures, greater operative trauma, the possibility of complications at the site of the donor flap and extended recovery time, which can be a challenge for certain groups of patients, especially those with comorbidities.81,85,102, 103, 104

On the other hand, reconstruction with implants, which is most often performed using silicone or saline implants, is more popular due to its shorter surgery, simpler technical implementation, and faster postoperative recovery100,101,105.106. However, implants are associated with an increased risk of long-term complications, including capsular contracture, infection, implant rupture, and the need for revision surgery. These complications can negatively impact physical health and psychosocial outcomes, increasing dissatisfaction with the results of reconstruction and potentially reducing quality of life. In addition, some patients express concerns about the “artificial” feel of the implants in the body, which can further complicate emotional adjustment after mastectomy.4,107

The choice between autologous reconstruction and implant reconstruction should be individualized, taking into account the medical indications, patient preferences, expectations regarding aesthetic results, and willingness to accept potential risks and length of recovery. Patient education and multidisciplinary consultation are essential for making an informed decision that will support their physical, emotional, and psychosocial well-being in the long term.

Subjects and methods

This study was designed as a cross-sectional quantitative study to investigate the impact of breast reconstruction on the quality of life of women who have survived breast cancer.

Sample – The study included 100 women who underwent breast reconstruction within the last seven years. Participants were selected from the hospital registry of the General Hospital in Pula, Croatia, using simple random sampling. The required sample size was calculated using the standard formula for cross-sectional studies:n=Z2×p×(1−p)d2where n is the minimum required sample size, Z is the Z-value corresponding to the desired confidence level (1.96 for 95 % CI), p is the expected proportion (0.5 was used to maximize variability), and d is the acceptable margin of error (0.1).

With a 95 % confidence level (Z = 1.96), an expected proportion of p = 0.5, and a margin of error of 0.1, the minimum required sample was 96 participants. The final sample consisted of 100 women, which met this requirement.

Instrument – The questionnaire used in this study was adapted from previously validated instruments on breast surgery outcomes, particularly the BREAST-Q developed by Pusic et al.4 It consisted of two main parts. The first part encompassed demographic data of the participants, including age and education. The second part included a five-point Likert scale with statements related to various aspects of quality of life. A pilot test with 10 participants confirmed clarity and feasibility of the questions. Internal consistency of the instrument in this study was high, with Cronbach's alpha = 0.932, indicating strong reliability.

Data collection – Data were collected between 2018 and 2024 at the General Hospital in Pula. Participants were either approached during follow-up visits and asked to complete a paper questionnaire after providing written informed consent, or, if they did not complete the survey in person, they were contacted via e-mail using addresses available in the hospital registry. In those cases, participants received a secure online link and gave electronic informed consent before completing the questionnaire. The extended data collection period was necessary because the number of women undergoing breast reconstruction at a single institution was relatively large and required several years to achieve the target sample of 100 participants. All respondents were assured of anonymity and confidentiality.

Ethical approval – The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of the General Hospital in Pula.

Data analysis – The data were processed using the statistical software SPSS. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic information and participant responses. The Kruskal–Wallis test was applied to examine differences between groups in relation to age and education level, while correlation analyses were used to investigate the relationships between various aspects of quality of life. In this research, regression analysis was applied to examine the impact of different factors on the quality of life of patients after breast reconstruction. Additionally, Cronbach's alpha was used to assess the internal consistency of the questionnaire measuring various aspects of quality of life, thereby ensuring the validity and reliability of the measurement instruments used.

Descriptive statistics

The analysis of descriptive statistics for items related to the impact of breast reconstruction on quality of life (Table 3) indicates comprehensive improvements across several domains of psychophysical functioning. The greatest improvements were observed in the area of physical comfort, where participants reported greater ease in performing daily activities and increased comfort in physical contact and wearing clothes. Mean values in this domain were high (all >4.20 ± 0.70), with particularly high scores for easier performance of daily tasks (4.32 ± 0.78) and increased comfort in physical activities (4.34 ± 0.72). Emotional well-being also showed significant improvements. Participants rated higher emotional stability and a more positive emotional response to their appearance, with mean scores ranging from 4.18 ± 0.84 to 4.34 ± 0.72. The highest scores were recorded for better emotional control (4.34 ± 0.72) and easier coping with stress (4.28 ± 0.76), suggesting stabilization of emotional functioning after reconstruction. In the domain of self-confidence, participants reported a stronger sense of personal worth and greater assurance in social situations. The highest individual score in this segment was for freedom of expression in social contexts (4.39 ± 0.74), indicating enhanced personal identity and social assertiveness. Similarly, results in the social interaction domain confirmed a positive impact of reconstruction on the respondents’ social quality of life. Participants indicated greater ease in communication with family and friends and higher willingness to participate in social events, with scores consistently high (4.18 ± 0.77 to 4.31 ± 0.80) and low variability, indicating a homogeneous positive experience. Overall, consistently high mean scores across domains (all >4.20 ± 0.75) with low standard deviations indicate stable improvements in physical, emotional, and social aspects of quality of life. These findings confirm that breast reconstruction contributes not only to physical recovery but also to emotional rehabilitation and reintegration into social life, significantly enhancing overall On the other hand, numerous aspects of physical comfort, emotional well-being, and self-confidence showed statistically significant differences between age groups. For example, items such as “After reconstruction, I feel more comfortable in my clothes” (p = 0.015) and “Reconstruction enabled me to perform everyday tasks easier” (p = 0.026) indicate that age has an impact on how women perceive improvement in physical comfort after reconstruction. In addition, items such as “I feel less sensitive to physical touches in the reconstructed area” (p = 0.006) and “I have no problem returning to my usual physical activities after reconstruction” (p = 0.010) also showed significant differences, suggesting that younger and older women may have different experiences with the physical aspects of recovery.

In the domain of emotional well-being, items such as “My emotional reaction to my appearance has improved since I had the reconstruction” (p = 0.032) and “I feel that I have better control over my emotions after the operation” (p = 0.021) showed significant differences, suggesting that age can influence women's emotional response to reconstruction. Similarly, in the domain of self-confidence, significant differences were observed in items such as “Now I feel more able to express my opinion in social situations” (p = 0.011) and “My sense of self-worth has increased since having reconstruction” (p = 0.017). These findings imply that age may play a role in how women perceive improvements in confidence and emotional adjustment after surgery.

In the domain of social interactions, items such as “Reconstruction helped me to feel more comfortable in society” (p = 0.007) and “I feel freer in expressing my thoughts in social situations after reconstruction” (p = 0.009) also showed significant differences, suggesting that age can have an impact on social connectedness and participation.

In conclusion, the results of this research indicate that age has a significant impact on the perception of physical comfort, emotional well-being, and self-confidence after breast reconstruction, while certain aspects of social interaction and emotional well-being remain consistent across groups. Although the Kruskal–Wallis test indicated overall differences across age groups, post-hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction did not reveal statistically significant differences between specific groups. These findings suggest that approaches to support women after reconstruction should be age-adjusted to optimize outcomes in all aspects of quality of life.

Analysis of scale reliability

The reliability of the scale was assessed using Cronbach's alpha. Cronbach's alpha is a statistical method used to evaluate the internal consistency and reliability of measurement instruments, such as surveys, questionnaires, or tests. This coefficient analyzes the interrelationship of various items within the instrument, determining how well they align in measuring the same construct. Values of Cronbach's alpha range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater reliability. Typically, a value above 0.7 is considered good, while values above 0.9 indicate exceptional reliability. This metric is crucial for ensuring that measurement tools provide consistent and accurate results, essential in research and analysis. Discussion

The results of this study clearly show that breast reconstruction after mastectomy has multiple positive effects on the physical, emotional and social aspects of the quality of life of women who have survived breast cancer. This is in line with previous research that indicated that reconstruction plays a key role not only in physical rehabilitation, but also in emotional and psychological recovery.4, 5, 6, 7

Physical comfort, as one of the most important aspects of quality of life, was significantly improved in most of the participants. High average values in the items related to easier performance of daily activities and increased comfort during physical activities (M > 4.2) confirm the findings of research such as those by Tohno, Ueno and Watanabe34 and Guo et al.,35 who point out that physical rehabilitation after reconstructive procedures directly facilitates the return to daily routines. These results indicate that the reconstruction not only restores the physical integrity of the body, but actively contributes to the feeling of normality in women's lives, which is crucial for their long-term adaptation.

In the domain of emotional well-being, the reconstruction led to a significant improvement in emotional stability, with average scores above 4.2. This is especially important in the context of previous findings that indicated that breast loss can cause depressive and anxiety symptoms, impair self-confidence and lead to serious psychological consequences.2,88,89 The study confirms these claims, but also emphasizes that an adequately implemented reconstruction can mitigate these effects and contribute to the stabilization of the emotional state of patients. Considering that emotional stability has the highest regression coefficient (β = 0.42) in predicting the overall quality of life, it is clear that the psychological component of recovery has a key weight.

Self-confidence and social interactions further confirm the multiple benefits of reconstruction. High values in the domains of expressing opinions in society, self-confidence in choosing clothes and participating in social events (M = 4.18–4.39) indicate that the reconstruction has a profound effect on the restoration of identity and social inclusion, which was previously emphasized by authors such as Edwards94 and Scalia.95 These aspects are crucial because returning to a positive self-experience allows women to overcome the feeling of stigmatization and isolation that often accompanies mastectomy.62,91

It is particularly significant that the analysis showed that age has a certain influence on the perception of physical and emotional recovery, while the level of education did not have a statistically significant influence on the experience of quality of life. A higher percentage of younger respondents reported a higher level of physical comfort and emotional control, which may be a consequence of better physical condition and greater emotional flexibility of younger women, which was previously observed in the work of Radu et al.89 and Abd-Elrahman, Salem and Emam.90 On the other hand, the fact that education was not related to differences in reconstruction experience indicates that the psychosocial benefits of reconstruction go beyond educational and social-economic factors, which is also confirmed by the findings of de Carvalho Aguiar et al.75 and Rubino et al.96

Correlation analysis additionally confirmed strong links between physical comfort, emotional stability, self-confidence and the quality of social relationships. The strongest correlation between emotional stability and self-confidence (r = 0.75) indicates that emotional recovery is the basis for building positive self-confidence, which is in accordance with the concepts of psychosocial rehabilitation described in the works of Taylor-Brown et al.70 and Wondimagegnehu et al.69

It is also important to emphasize that the type of reconstruction, although not specifically analyzed in this research, according to the literature,85,98 can additionally affect satisfaction with the results, where autologous reconstruction often leads to a higher degree of satisfaction due to the more natural look and feel of the reconstructed breast.

The high value of Cronbach's alpha (0.932) confirms the high internal consistency of the instrument that was used to measure the impact of reconstruction on quality of life, which means that the results are reliable and enable valid conclusions.

In comparison with previous studies, which predominantly emphasized cosmetic outcomes or limited psychological domains, our study provides a more comprehensive evaluation by simultaneously assessing physical comfort, emotional stability, self-confidence, and social interactions within the same cohort. This holistic approach, together with the use of a highly reliable instrument (Cronbach's alpha = 0.932), strengthens the validity of our findings. Moreover, the identification of emotional stability as the strongest predictor of overall quality of life distinguishes this work from earlier research that largely highlighted body image satisfaction as the primary determinant. Conducted in a Central European context, this study also broadens the geographical scope of evidence, offering insights that complement the existing literature, which has been predominantly generated in Western populations.

Finally, the research results confirm that breast reconstruction is much more than an aesthetic intervention - it represents an important therapeutic step in the psychological and social rehabilitation of women who have survived breast cancer. The integration of psychological support, along with surgical and medical treatment, should be standard practice in order to maximally improve the quality of life of these patients.

This study has several limitations. First, the use of self-report questionnaires may have introduced response bias, as participants might have provided socially desirable answers. Second, the study was conducted in a single institution (General Hospital Pula), which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Third, although the cross-sectional design allows for a snapshot of quality of life at one point in time, it does not capture long-term trajectories or changes. Fourth, while we applied the Kruskal–Wallis test, post-hoc analyses were not conducted to identify specific group differences, which should be addressed in future research. Finally, despite the relatively large sample for a single-center study, broader multi-institutional studies are needed to confirm these results.

Conslusion

This research confirms the important role of breast reconstruction after mastectomy in enhancing physical comfort, emotional stability, self-confidence, and social interactions, with emotional stability identified as the strongest predictor of overall quality of life. While these findings are encouraging, they should be interpreted in light of certain limitations, including the single-center setting, modest sample size, reliance on self-reported data, and the cross-sectional design, which precludes long-term follow-up. Future research should therefore adopt longitudinal designs, explore differences between types of reconstruction, and include larger and more diverse populations to improve external validity. From a clinical perspective, breast reconstruction should be integrated as a standard component of rehabilitation after mastectomy, supported by multidisciplinary teams that include psychologists and mental health professionals. Providing adequate patient education, individualized rehabilitation plans, and group support programs can further optimize outcomes, enabling women to regain not only physical integrity but also emotional well-beingand social inclusion. Overall, breast reconstruction should be viewed not merely as an aesthetic procedure but as a key element of comprehensive psychosocial rehabilitation for women facing breast cancer.
