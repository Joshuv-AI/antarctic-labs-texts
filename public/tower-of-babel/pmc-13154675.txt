Scabies as a manifestation of structural violence in refugee communities: a case from Rwanda
Authors: Ursin Bayisenge, Peter Mugume, Gail Davey, Shahaduz Zaman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundScabies, a neglected tropical disease affecting an estimated 200 million people globally, thrives in overcrowded, resource-limited environments and carries significant stigma. Refugee camps present conditions conducive to its spread, yet little is known about how scabies is experienced and understood in such settings. This study explores scabies as a manifestation of structural violence among Kinyarwanda speaking Congolese refugees in Kigeme camp, Rwanda.MethodsAn ethnographic study was conducted from March to September 2022 using participant observation, 19 in-depth interviews with adults affected by scabies, 5 key informant interviews, 6 family interviews, and 2 focus group discussions with 12 unaffected adults. Transcripts were thematically analysed in NVivo, combining deductive and inductive coding to explore lived experiences, stigma, and coping strategies.ResultsFindings reveal that scabies was misinterpreted as an HIV/AIDS-related opportunistic infection, producing stigma, broken marriage negotiations, and social isolation. Participants also linked scabies to weakened immunity, poor nutrition, and poverty, describing it as symbolic of a deteriorated body and refugee deprivation. Stigma curtailed livelihoods and deepened psychological suffering. During outbreaks, perceptions shifted temporarily as the condition became widespread, reducing stigma but reinforcing the sense of collective vulnerability. Coping strategies included intensive hygiene, concealment of rashes, and self-isolation, though these often proved ineffective in crowded conditions. The neglect of scabies treatment—reliance on benzyl benzoate despite the availability of ivermectin—reflected structural inequities in global health policy and humanitarian support.ConclusionThis study is the first to use ethnographic methods to demonstrate the multifaceted structural violence underlying scabies in refugee contexts. Beyond a skin condition, scabies exposes systemic injustices, including exclusion from citizenship, inadequate humanitarian support, and the absence of clear integration or resettlement pathways. Addressing scabies requires both biomedical interventions and structural reforms that expand refugee rights, confront inequality, and integrate displaced populations into global strategies for neglected tropical disease elimination.Keywords: Stigma, Structural violence, Scabies, Refugees, Stigma, DRC, Humanitarian

Background

Scabies is a significant global public health problem, affecting an estimated 200 million people at any given time, with prevalence ranging from 0.2% to 71% depending on the setting [1]. It disproportionately affects populations in overcrowded, resource-limited, and tropical regions where outbreaks recur frequently [2]. From a biomedical standpoint, scabies is a contagious skin infestation caused by the mite Sarcoptes scabiei var. hominis, transmitted mainly through prolonged skin-to-skin contact and, to a lesser extent, through sharing clothes or bedding, which explains its strong association with overcrowded and impoverished living conditions [2, 3]. Clinically it presents with intense pruritus and inflammatory papules; scratching may break the skin and facilitate secondary bacterial infections such as Streptococcus pyogenes, leading in some settings to post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis and occasionally rheumatic fever [4]. More severe forms, including crusted scabies in immunosuppressed patients and scabies incognito in those using topical steroids, further complicate recognition and management [5]. Standard treatment requires application of topical scabicides or administration of oral agents, usually in one or two courses over one to two weeks, and must be combined with simultaneous treatment of close contacts, washing or airing clothes and bedding, and reducing crowding to prevent reinfestation [3, 4]. However, scabies lacks simple, widely available diagnostic tools; diagnosis still relies largely on clinical examination or microscopy, which are often unavailable in resource-poor settings [6]. These clinical and programmatic requirements – privacy for examination, repeated treatment, water and soap, spare clothing and bed linen, and less crowded housing – are precisely the conditions most difficult to achieve in refugee camps, making scabies a particularly sensitive indicator of structural constraints and neglect.

Despite its burden and profound social consequences, including stigma, despair, economic loss, and debilitating discomfort, scabies was only added to the World Health Organization’s list of neglected tropical diseases (NTDs) in 2017 [7, 8]. Clinically, it is caused by the mite Sarcoptes scabiei var. hominis, transmitted mainly through close contact, and often complicated by secondary bacterial infections such as Streptococcus pyogenes, which may lead to post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis and occasionally rheumatic fever [3, 4]. Severe forms, including crusted scabies in immunocompromised individuals and scabies incognito in patients on steroids, further increase its health impact [5].

Although its parasitic cause has been known for centuries, global responses to scabies remain inadequate. No endemic country has fully integrated its management into universal health coverage, despite WHO’s pledge to achieve this by 2030 (WHO, 2020). Beyond weak health system responses, there is a persistent lack of sociocultural data to inform interventions, even though social, cultural, political, and economic factors are well established as key determinants of transmission and outcomes [9, 10].

Progress has also been constrained by poor diagnostic infrastructure. While the International Alliance for the Control of Scabies (IACS) proposed criteria in 2018, no standardized methods for mapping or large-scale epidemiological studies exist. Diagnosis still relies on clinical observation or microscopy, which are often inaccurate and inaccessible in low-resource settings [6, 7, 11]. The absence of affordable, rapid tests delays treatment and undermines reliable prevalence estimates, reflecting broader market failures in neglected tropical diseases where limited commercial incentives stifle innovation [12].

Equally critical are the social dimensions of scabies. The condition carries heavy stigma, often linked to notions of poor hygiene, curses, or heredity, which aggravate the suffering of those affected [13, 14]. Patients frequently endure shame, isolation, uncertainty, and long delays in treatment, resulting in what Goffman described as a “spoiled identity.” The skin, as both a biological and symbolic organ, becomes a marker of exclusion—reinforcing scabies as not only a medical problem but also a sociocultural affliction that undermines personal dignity [15].

These dynamics are amplified in refugee settings. Refugee camps provide conditions highly favorable to transmission: overcrowding, population turnover, and limited access to timely care. Data from refugee centers in Greece recorded 13,118 cases of scabies over four years, with proportional morbidity ranging from 0.3% to 5.7% and a peak outbreak of 1,663 cases in January 2020 [16]. In Cox’s Bazar, Bangladesh, the world’s largest refugee settlement, point prevalence reached 66.4% among 679 participants in six camps during April–May 2023. Risk was significantly higher among those with a prior history of scabies or other skin infections and within families where clustering was pronounced [17]. These findings emphasize how household dynamics sustain transmission in such high-density environments.

The history of Kinyarwanda-speaking Congolese refugees in Kigeme camp is central to understanding the vulnerabilities that sustain scabies transmission. All current residents originate from eastern Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), a region bordering Rwanda’s Western Province that has been plagued by conflict since the 1990s. Their displacement, however, has much deeper roots. The partition of Africa at the Berlin Conference of 1885 divided Greater Rwanda, at that time encompassing much of eastern DRC, and split Kinyarwanda-speaking populations between Congo and Rwanda [18]. Rwandan historian Alex Kagame argued that this division permanently altered regional identities, though some European scholars have challenged his interpretation. Regardless of the debate, colonial partition, coupled with decades of political instability and armed conflict in the DRC, created the conditions that continue to drive waves of forced migration into Rwanda. The protracted exile of Kinyarwanda-speaking Congolese communities in Kigeme reflects these long-standing structural and historical forces.

Against this backdrop, the current study situates scabies in Rwanda’s Kigeme refugee camp within a broader theoretical lens of structural violence. As articulated by Farmer, health risks in marginalized settings are shaped by entrenched economic, political, legal, and cultural systems that normalize inequality and constrain access to care [19]. Galtung further defines structural violence as the institutionalized patterns that create avoidable suffering and unequal life chances, often without a direct perpetrator [20]. At Kigeme, these dynamics manifest through governance structures, rationed resources such as food and space, restrictions on mobility, and precarious legal status, all of which produce recurrent exposure, delayed diagnosis, and inadequate treatment for scabies. Viewing scabies through this biosocial lens highlights the need for responses that extend beyond clinical management. Equitable access to diagnostics and therapy must be paired with improvements in housing, sanitation, and social protections to address both the immediate and structural drivers of disease [19].

Methods

This ethnographic study was conducted between March and September 2022 in Kigeme Refugee Camp, located in Nyamagabe District in Southern Rwanda. In September 2022, the camp hosted 14,536 refugees on 34 hectares of steep terrain; all registered refugees originate from eastern Democratic Republic of Congo, and 53% are under 18 years of age [21].

In this region of eastern DRC, Kinyarwanda is an official or widely used language, and in Kigeme camp all participants spoke Kinyarwanda as their primary language of daily interaction. Since its establishment in 2012, Kigeme has been developed as a semi-permanent settlement: UNHCR has constructed 1,503 semi-permanent shelters, small mud-brick houses with metal roofs averaging 12–21 m², resulting in marked household crowding [21].

Refugees are not permitted to keep livestock inside the camp, but some households cultivate plots in a common climate-smart agriculture project on 59 hectares of nearby Mushishito marshland shared between 500 refugees and 1,048 members of the host community, mainly growing maize [21].

Water and sanitation are provided through 34 water points with 182 taps, shared latrine and shower blocks, and an average water supply of 16 L per person per day [21].

One health centre in the camp, operated by UNHCR and the African Humanitarian Agency, provides free primary health care and referrals to Kigeme District Hospital and tertiary facilities. Within this context of chronic overcrowding, shared infrastructure and economic dependence, Kigeme experienced two large scabies outbreaks in late 2019 and early 2020, recalled by residents and health staff as affecting approximately two and four thousands individuals according to the camp authorities and managed primarily through benzyl benzoate distribution and hygiene promotion. These structural features made Kigeme an appropriate site for examining scabies transmission, lived experience, stigma and care-seeking in a protracted refugee setting.

Data collection combined participant observation, in-depth and life history interviews, key informant interviews, and focus group discussions. Nineteen adult refugees affected by scabies were interviewed, along with five key informants who held official roles in the camp, and six family members of individuals affected by scabies. In addition, two focus group discussions were conducted with twelve adults who had not experienced scabies: one group of six men aged 20 to 60 years, and one group of six women aged 20 to 52 years. Participant observation was carried out in households, camp pathways, health services, and community meetings, offering insights into daily routines, health-related behaviours, and the broader sociocultural environment.

Among the 19 participants affected by scabies, the majority were women (13 out of 19). Most were between 26 and 50 years old, married, and living in large households of seven to ten members (11 out of 19). Nearly all lived in small dwellings of two to three rooms (16 out of 19), usually with dirt floors (17 out of 19), and almost all were classified as economically disadvantaged under the camp’s socioeconomic criteria (18 out of 19). The five key informants included four men and one woman, each with secondary education and holding official roles in the camp. In addition, six family members of affected individuals – three men and three women – were interviewed to provide further perspectives on caregiving and stigma.

Participants were recruited through convenience and snowball sampling, facilitated by two refugee community health workers who acted as cultural brokers. To ensure systematic identification of cases, UB reviewed health centre records from January 2019 to January 2022, which listed fifty-five individuals with scabies during outbreaks or surveillance, from which participants were selected.

All interviews were audio-recorded with consent, transcribed verbatim in Kinyarwanda, and their accuracy verified through cross-review. Transcripts, field notes, and documents were organized by context and time. Thematic analysis was conducted in NVivo, beginning with a deductive codebook derived from the conceptual framework and expanded inductively as new insights emerged. Codes were clustered into categories and broader themes, and connections across themes were examined to highlight mechanisms of transmission, stigma, and health-seeking. The codebook and selected quotations were later translated into English for reporting.

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the relevant institutional guidelines for research involving human participants. Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Governance and Ethics Review Committee of Brighton and Sussex Medical School (ERA/BSMS9k8C/1) and from the College of Arts and Social Sciences at the University of Rwanda (REF 114/PO/20211). Authorization to access Kigeme Refugee Camp was granted by the Rwandan Ministry in Charge of Emergency Management. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

My positionality shaped both access and interpretation. I did not reside in the camp; instead, I entered on foot with refugee community health workers as field guides and left each day by 4 p.m. in accordance with camp regulations. I was simultaneously a researcher, a medical doctor and a Rwandan outsider who spoke Kinyarwanda and shared many cultural references with participants. This cultural and linguistic proximity facilitated trust, but my medical background and association with community health workers also initially led some residents to assume I was conducting biomedical surveillance or representing humanitarian agencies. Even after I explained the social-science focus of the study, some participants asked for medical advice or treatment. In these situations, I listened to their concerns, reiterated my role as a researcher, and, rather than offering direct clinical care, encouraged and facilitated contact with the camp health centre or community health workers for formal assessment and treatment. I therefore began each encounter by clarifying that I was undertaking a social science study focused on lived experiences, stigma and the social consequences of scabies, not on judging clinical practices. Conducting interviews in participants’ homes, rather than in health facilities, helped soften clinical power dynamics and encouraged open discussion, although my status as a male researcher and the restriction on staying after 4 p.m. inevitably limited what I could observe of women’s evening routines and men’s social life.

Results

Observation data

Over six months of fieldwork, I spent repeated full days moving between the two hills on which Kigeme camp is built, visiting households and passing the small clusters of shops, the health centre and the main gathering space. The camp is densely populated yet strikingly quiet; for much of the day the narrow paths are almost empty, with most people remaining inside their homes. Houses are small mud-brick structures with metal roofs, usually comprising two rooms, one of which serves as both living room and sleeping space for the family. Men were often absent during daylight hours as they left the camp in search of casual work, while women and children stayed at home, fetching water from communal taps, washing clothes and undertaking domestic chores. Cooking was concentrated in the late afternoon, when smoke and the smell of food began to rise as families prepared their main meal. Daily life was organised through a regulated security system run by refugee committees who reported incidents to the camp authorities and the police post, creating an atmosphere of calm control but also of suspended routine, with few visible opportunities for paid work or mobility.

These prolonged stays also revealed how scabies and its consequences persisted in everyday life even when active cases were largely absent. During fieldwork, most residents had already recovered from the major outbreaks, so I rarely observed obvious lesions or intense scratching in public spaces. Instead, traces of the disease appeared in more subtle ways. Some participants continued to wear long sleeves or high collars in the heat to conceal residual marks, and a few positioned their chairs slightly away from me during interviews, expressing concern that they might still transmit scabies. Conversations along the paths between houses frequently returned to past episodes of sleepless nights, weak bodies and the shame of visible rashes, linking those memories to crowded houses, monotonous food rations and ongoing uncertainty about the future. These observations of what was no longer visible on the skin but remained present in clothing, distance and talk directly reinforced the narratives shared in interviews and underpinned my interpretation of scabies as a lingering imprint of structural violence in the camp.

Thematic findings

Themes encompassed inaccurate information associating scabies with HIV/AIDS, the metaphorical depiction of a debilitated body, and an image of poverty.

Scabies as misconstrued HIV/AIDS

Scabies was frequently misinterpreted as an opportunistic infection linked to HIV/AIDS. Many residents had encountered AIDS in their families or networks and associated it with immorality, promiscuity, and shame. Severe scabies rashes, especially those with sores or discharge, were often compared to herpes zoster, a well-known opportunistic infection. This association generated significant fear, shaped by Christian beliefs that reinforced the moral weight of HIV/AIDS.

Misconceptions created social fractures. Marriage negotiations collapsed when families assumed a prospective partner with scabies was also HIV-positive, and young people withdrew from relationships out of fear of rejection. Affected individuals internalized these perceptions, leading to deliberate isolation, reduced economic activity, and reluctance to seek care.

“There was conflict among families with soon-to-be-married men who were affected with scabies. I believe that was the worst-case scenario, as scabies brought chaos to interfamilial relationships… many marriages were ended because no one could trust a person with HIV/AIDS-related skin lesions resembling zona, medically know as herpes zoster.” – Male participant, FGD.

“Before I contracted scabies, I questioned whether it was HIV/AIDS when I saw people with scabies.” – Male respondent.

Scabies as a symbol of weakness and malnutrition

Scabies was also understood as a manifestation of a weakened body, caused by poor diet and loss of immunity. Refugees contrasted their limited rations in Rwanda—mainly rice, maize, and beans—with the more diverse diets they once had in the DRC. They linked scabies to nutritional deficiencies and toxins, describing it as a bodily consequence of refugee life. The monthly financial assistance from humanitarian agencies was described as insufficient, compounding food insecurity.

“We discovered that scabies was caused by a deficiency in nutrients… In Congo we ate protective foods like milk, fruits, and meat. This disease is the result of being a refugee… Despite our best efforts in terms of hygiene, we contracted the disease.” –Female respondent.

Scabies as an expression of poverty

Underlying these narratives was poverty. Participants consistently tied scabies to their inability to afford hygiene products, adequate housing, or varied food. Refugees receiving remittances from relatives abroad were perceived as less vulnerable, since they could purchase cement for floors, bed linen, and personal items believed to reduce scabies risk. For many, the aspiration to be resettled abroad was seen as the only path to escaping the conditions that bred scabies.

“Our bodies become fragile in these filthy houses. You could not distinguish us from animals; you would assume we are pigs because our homes are filthy.” – Male respondent.

Stigma and isolation

Stigma was pervasive and shaped daily life. People with scabies withdrew from social and economic activities, anticipating mockery or gossip. Vendors closed their shops, fearing customers would avoid them. While some concealed their rashes, others self-isolated to protect themselves from humiliation.

“When I had rashes on the skin, I was ashamed to go see my friends because I was afraid of being mocked… I even hid that I was sick, but this disease lasted six months and my whole entourage eventually knew about it.” – Female participant.

“We were very sorry for our young girls and boys, but for us old people, you would sometimes see us sitting together while all scratching ourselves. We have nothing to hide, and we do not mock each other.” – Male participant.

Shifting perceptions during outbreaks

Before the 2019–2020 epidemics, scabies was regarded as a marker of extreme poverty and neglect. Outbreaks, however, affected people across all ages and social groups, transforming community perceptions. Scabies became recognized as a common condition, though myths persisted due to limited health education and inaccurate biomedical explanations.

“When the outbreak started, we realised that we were all candidates for this condition; we stopped blaming those who were affected. A group of senior citizens, including myself, took on the task of providing moral support.” – Senior male participant, FGD.

“Since nearly everyone had scabies, I was no longer embarrassed to have the condition, but it was humiliating to scratch myself in front of others.” – Female respondent.

Coping strategies

Coping practices combined hygiene, concealment, and household adjustments. Many attempted frequent bathing, sometimes with harsh soaps, or avoided sharing beds and clothes. Yet crowded living conditions made separation nearly impossible. Refugees also wore long clothing to cover rashes, though discomfort and the urge to scratch often revealed the disease. Coping was as much social as physical, with many self-isolating to avoid shame.

“Scabies would have killed us if we were not bathing. Water saved us significantly. Our bodies are weak because we do not have sufficient food to eat… We used to say that instead of moisturizing the body, we would rather moisturize the stomach.” – Male respondent.

“I did not immediately go to the health centre. Instead, I decided to bathe every day with strong soaps such as OMO. But it did not really work.” – Female respondent.

“Nobody really discriminated me, but I chose to self-isolate because these rashes had a bad smell. I felt like I did not belong where many people were gathering.” – Male respondent.

Discussion

This study highlights how scabies, often reduced to a dermatological nuisance, is in fact a manifestation of broader vulnerabilities produced by refugee life and sustained through structural violence. The findings demonstrate that scabies cannot be understood solely as a biological condition but must be situated within the historical, political, and economic forces that perpetuate exile, poverty, and constrained access to health care. In Kigeme camp, scabies embodied not only physical suffering but also stigma, social isolation, and ruptures in family and community life.

Refugee life, historical marginalization, and structural violence

The experiences of Kinyarwanda-speaking Congolese refugees at Kigeme reflect how structural violence continuously shapes health outcomes. Their exile is the consequence of decades of political instability and protracted conflict in eastern DRC, but it is also rooted in colonial partition and its enduring legacies. As Farmer argues, structural violence is enacted through entrenched social, political, and economic arrangements that limit life chances while appearing ordinary and normalized [19]. Refugees’ restricted rights—limited freedom of movement, reliance on humanitarian aid, and lack of property ownership—make camp life both a form of protection and a mechanism of containment. Within this context, scabies thrives as a biological expression of structural inequality.

Sidney W. Mintz’s reflections on history help sharpen this analysis. He argued that the erasure and selective retelling of history benefits oppressors, shaping what is remembered and what is forgotten [22]. Just as French cuisine and fashion are celebrated while the brutal exploitation of Haitian slaves who sustained them is silenced, the histories of Rwanda and eastern DRC have been manipulated by colonial powers and later political elites to obscure violence and dispossession. This manipulation sustains structural inequalities that force Congolese refugees into protracted exile. The neglect of their health needs, including scabies, must therefore be understood not only as a biomedical gap but as a political act that continues a long history of silencing and marginalization.

Structural violence and health at Kigeme camp

The findings of this study illustrate how structural violence operates through daily life in Kigeme. Over 90% of refugees depend on monthly stipends that are insufficient to cover food, hygiene products, or decent housing. As participants explained, malnutrition, overcrowding, and poor shelter were perceived as key causes of scabies, which they linked to weakened immunity and poverty. This resonates with Galtung’s definition of structural violence as the preventable gap between actual and potential well-being (Galtung, 1969). The refugees’ insights reveal how structural violence is embodied: rations that sustain survival but erode dignity, housing that shelters but also breeds disease, and policies that protect in the short term while constraining long-term flourishing.

The stigma surrounding scabies also reflects structural inequalities. In the camp, skin rashes were equated with HIV/AIDS, producing social exclusion and even broken marriage negotiations. Stigma curtailed economic opportunities as individuals closed businesses or withdrew from social life. These dynamics underscore how structural violence extends beyond material deprivation to the symbolic realm, where disease becomes a marker of moral failure, poverty, or contagion.

Scabies and the political economy of neglect

Scabies neglect exemplifies how global health priorities are structured by political economy. Despite affecting 200 million people worldwide, scabies is marginal in research and funding compared to many other neglected tropical diseases [7, 23]. The reliance on benzyl benzoate cream in Kigeme, despite the availability of ivermectin tablets on the WHO Essential Medicines List, reflects what Farmer and colleagues call the “desocialization of medicine”—the narrowing of interventions to technical solutions divorced from the lived realities of the poor [19]. For refugees, topical treatments were not only medically burdensome but socially stigmatizing, as they exposed people as having contracted the disease in public spaces. The lack of investment in safer, more acceptable treatments reveals the market logic of pharmaceutical industries, which prioritize profitability over the needs of marginalized populations [24].

The structural neglect of scabies also reflects failures in global refugee policy. The international resettlement system, established in 1976, has resettled only a small fraction of refugees, leaving the majority confined to camps under precarious conditions [25]. This policy of protracted waiting institutionalizes dependency and vulnerability, ensuring that conditions such as scabies persist rather than being eradicated.

Linking history, structural violence, and scabies

Based on Galtung’s definition, the structural violence described in this paper is not understood as a direct attack on Kinyarwanda-speaking Congolese because of their ethnicity, but as violence embedded into the structure that systematically constrains life chances without a visible perpetrator [20]. The limited freedom of movement, reliance on humanitarian aid, lack of property ownership, and protracted uncertainty documented in Kigeme are not unique to this group; they are characteristic features of camp-based refugee regimes globally. Our interlocutors did not explicitly attribute their suffering to ethnic discrimination. Rather, their narratives of crowding, poverty, and dependency illustrate what Paul Farmer describes as historically given and economically driven inequalities that place certain populations – here, refugees as a legal and political category – in positions of heightened vulnerability to disease and social suffering [19].

The specific history of Kinyarwanda-speaking Congolese refugees remains relevant, but as context rather than as a direct, singular cause of scabies. Colonial partition, postcolonial conflict, and recurrent violence in eastern DRC have produced cycles of displacement that keep this community in prolonged exile, echoing Farmer’s argument that contemporary suffering is tightly bound to long-standing historical and political processes [19, 22]. This history helps explain why they are in Kigeme and why their displacement is likely to be long term, but the mechanisms of structural violence that shape their everyday lives in the camp are largely the same as those affecting refugees elsewhere. What is structurally violent here is the refugee regime itself: legal categories that suspend citizenship rights, humanitarian architectures that provide minimal survival while limiting autonomy, and global health priorities that leave conditions like scabies underfunded and poorly addressed [19, 20].

Seen through this lens, the persistence of scabies in Kigeme is not read as a direct outcome of ethnic marginalization, but as one concrete embodiment of how the refugee system and the political economy of NTDs intersect. The everyday suffering associated with scabies – itching, stigma, social withdrawal, and disrupted livelihoods – arises where camp governance, chronic economic precarity, and the neglect of NTDs converge. Following Farmer, the violence lies in the way these arrangements make preventable harm appear normal, and following Galtung, in the gap they create between the refugees’ current reality and the lives they could reasonably expect to live [19, 20, 22]. Addressing scabies in Kigeme therefore requires engaging not only with its biomedical dimensions but also with the wider structures that produce and maintain refugee vulnerability, which are shared across many, though not all, refugee settings.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, no previous study has used ethnographic methods to reveal the multifaceted layers of structural violence imposed on refugees affected by scabies. This approach demonstrated how overcrowding, poor nutrition, restricted mobility, and economic precarity create conditions in which scabies flourishes, while stigma compounds social exclusion and economic loss. The neglect of scabies reflects deeper injustices, including the exclusion of Kinyarwanda-speaking Congolese from citizenship, the marginalization of severely disfiguring and stigmatized conditions, the inadequacy and ambiguity of humanitarian support, and the absence of clear strategies for integration or resettlement after prolonged exile. These injustices cannot be attributed to a single authority but are embedded in the global governance framework that continues to treat refugees as temporary populations rather than as rights-bearing communities, thereby perpetuating dependency, vulnerability, and ill health.

The findings underscore the urgency of reframing refugee health within global policy debates on neglected tropical diseases. Provision for refugees in national NTD strategies, investment in effective and socially acceptable treatments such as ivermectin, and policies that address poverty, housing, and stigma are critical. Recognizing scabies as a biosocial condition shaped by historical and political forces highlights that addressing it requires both biomedical action and structural reform. Only by placing refugee health at the centre of global health equity agendas can commitments to universal health coverage and the elimination of neglected tropical diseases be meaningfully achieved.
