Holocene transition from climate-driven to human-dominated wetland dynamics in Sinchang-dong, South Korea
Authors: Ara Cho, Jin Cheul Kim, Min Han, Jieun Kwon, Jaeyoung Lee, Sangheon Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, we reconstruct the Holocene environmental history of the Sinchang-dong wetland, a key archaeological site in South Korea, using a multi-proxy approach integrating diatom and phytolith assemblages with quantitative archaeological demographic data. A Bayesian age–depth model (ca. 9.0–1.0 ka) reveals contrasting ecosystem responses to the 8.2 ka and 4.2 ka climatic events and intensified human activity. Both events are marked by diatom-poor intervals and elevated chrysophyte/diatom ratios, indicating phases of hydro-ecological instability. Responses differed between the two intervals: the 8.2 ka event was followed by a more prolonged recovery, whereas the 4.2 ka event was followed by more rapid reorganization of the wetland system. A statistically significant regime shift at ca. 2.3 ka, identified using Sequential t-test Analysis of Regime Shifts (STARS), marks a transition from a climate-driven lacustrine system to an anthropogenically dominated agricultural landscape. This shift coincides with increased Oryza sativa phytoliths, adoption of iron farming tools, and regional demographic expansion. After ca. 2.3 ka, human activity surpassed climate as the dominant driver of wetland dynamics, illustrating the transition of small East Asian wetlands from climate-sensitive to human-dominated systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53852-2.

Introduction

The Holocene was punctuated by abrupt climate events that triggered significant environmental perturbations1,2. Among these, the 8.2 ka and 4.2 ka events are prominent cooling episodes with profound impacts on hydrological systems and human societies. While the 8.2 ka event is linked to North Atlantic freshening1,3–5, the 4.2 ka event exhibits pronounced regional variability driven by complex solar and atmospheric forcings6–12.

The Korean Peninsula, situated at the boundary of the East Asian summer monsoon and mid-latitude westerlies, is highly sensitive to these perturbations. Previous studies have documented hydrological responses in lake and speleothem records13–22. However, the small-scale, rain-fed fluvial wetlands—prevalent across Korea—remain poorly understood despite their vulnerability to both hydroclimatic shifts and anthropogenic pressures23. These fragile systems often cross ‘sedimentary thresholds’24, providing sensitive but overlooked archives of regional paleoenvironmental change. Unlike large lakes with strong hydrological buffering capacity, small rain-fed wetlands can rapidly alternate between aquatic and subaerial states under abrupt hydroclimatic forcing, thereby amplifying ecological disturbance signals. Rather than representing incomplete archives, such threshold-sensitive systems may therefore provide critical insights into coupled climate–land-use dynamics in monsoon Asia.

During the mid to late Holocene, intensive rice cultivation and landscape modification reshaped East Asian lowland environments25–27. The Sinchang-dong site in southwestern Korea offers an exceptional archive for examining this transition. Known for its continuous sedimentary sequences and Iron Age agricultural artifacts, the site preserves a rare record of both natural climate-driven changes and increasing human intervention28,29.

Despite its significance, few studies have integrated high-resolution biological proxies, such as diatoms and phytoliths, with quantitative archaeological demographic data. While these proxies are sensitive indicators of ecological change30–34, their mechanistic links with regional population dynamics remain poorly constrained.

In this study, we reconstruct the Holocene environmental history of the Sinchang-dong wetland using a multi-proxy approach. Specifically, we aim to: (1) reconstruct the natural environmental succession of the wetland during the early-to-mid Holocene; (2) identify the timing and nature of intensified anthropogenic environmental transformation; (3) provide a quantitative assessment of the ecological regime shift using a statistically defined framework; and (4) evaluate the specific ecosystem responses and recovery trajectories associated with the 8.2 ka and 4.2 ka abrupt climate events. By disentangling climatic and anthropogenic drivers, this study provides process-based insights into long-term wetland resilience and threshold behavior in East Asian fluvial systems.

Regional setting and site description

The Sinchang-dong wetland is situated in the mid–lower reaches of the Yeongsan River basin, Gwangju (Fig. 1A, B). Historical topographic analysis indicates that the basin’s geomorphological configuration—characterized by topographic constrictions and shallow sediment thickness—facilitated high water-retention and the formation of perennial backswamps despite a limited upstream catchment (Fig. 1C). By 1967, intensified hydraulic modifications, including reservoirs and embankments, led to distinct land-use differentiation: dry-field agriculture on natural levees and intensive paddy cultivation in poorly drained backswamps (Fig. 1D).

Geologically, the basin consists of Precambrian gneiss and Jurassic granite overlain by Quaternary alluvium35. These anaerobic deposits provide exceptional preservation conditions for South Korean Historic Site No. 375, which is recognized as one of East Asia’s most significant waterlogged archaeological complexes. The site is a key model for understanding the expansion of rice paddy agriculture, preserving rare Early Iron Age (around 300 BCE) organic artifacts such as wooden tools and thick layers of rice husks28,29,36,37.

To reconstruct the Holocene depositional environment, a 15.0-m-deep sediment core (22SC-9) and a 3.0-m-deep trench (23SCT) were investigated in the central wetland (Fig. 1B). The 22SC-9 core provides a continuous stratigraphic record from weathered bedrock to the surface, while the 23SCT trench enables high-resolution documentation of late Holocene environmental changes and human water-management strategies.

Results

Lithology and chronology

Core 22SC-9 (15 m long) is divided into four lithological units, transitioning from weathered bedrock (Unit 1: 15.0–11.4 m) to organic-rich silty clay (Unit 2: 11.4–3.3 m) and oxidized muddy sand (Unit 3: 3.3–1.0 m), capped by anthropogenic fill (Fig. 2). The 23SCT trench (3 m deep) preserves a detailed late Holocene stratigraphy composed of four lithological units (Fig. 3). The basal unit (2.90–1.90 m) consists of gray sandy silt containing archaeological wood fragments. This is overlain by brown sandy silt (1.90–0.90 m), followed by dark gray silt (0.9–0.5 m), and capped by an anthropogenically disturbed layer in the uppermost 0.5 m. The Bayesian age–depth model for 22SC-9, integrating 14 AMS 14 C and four OSL dates, constrains the sequence from ca. 9.0 to 1.0 ka (Fig. 2). The measured radiocarbon and OSL ages used for the chronology are provided in Supplementary Table S1. The trench dates from 23SCT (2.2–0.5 ka) provide additional late Holocene chronological context for stratigraphic comparison (Fig. 3). No separate Bayesian age–depth model was constructed for the 23SCT trench. Instead, the relationship between 23SCT and 22SC-9 is supported by elevation-based stratigraphic comparison and corresponding late Holocene diatom phases (Supplementary Figure S1). These intervals broadly correspond to key cultural phases, including the Bronze Age–Iron Age transition (ca. 2.2 ka) and the Joseon Dynasty (ca. 0.5 ka).

Microfossil assemblages

Diatom and chrysophyte successions (22SC-9 and 23SCT)

Diatom analysis of core 22SC-9 identified 51 taxa, categorized into four biostratigraphic units (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Data S1).

Unit 1 (11.0–8.0 m): Dominated by acidophilic (Eunotia spp.) and epiphytic (Cocconeis placentula) taxa, indicating an acidic, macrophyte-rich wetland within a low-energy backswamp setting. A diatom poor interval occurs at 8.0–8.1 m.

Unit 2 (8.0–6.0 m): Marked by an abrupt shift to planktonic dominance (Aulacoseira ambigua), reflecting deeper lacustrine conditions. A second diatom poor intervals identified at 6.96–7.00 m.

Unit 3 (6.0–4.8 m): Planktonic taxa disappear, replaced by aerophilous Halamphora montana and Hantzschia amphioxys, signaling a transition to marginal or terrestrialized conditions.

Unit 4 (4.8–4.3 m): Sharp increase in aerophilous Humidophila contenta and Luticola mutica, typical of frequently exposed soil surfaces.

Chrysophyte stomatocysts were counted from the same slides used for diatom analysis in core 22SC-9. The chrysophyte diatom (C/D) ratio was calculated for each sample as the ratio of chrysophyte stomatocyst counts to diatom valve counts. Pronounced peaks in the C/D ratio (Fig. 8) are identified immediately before and after the diatom poor intervals of Unit 1 and Unit 2. The 23SCT trench (275–55 cm) reflects late Holocene changes, dominated by aerophilous and benthic taxa (Fig. 5). Unit 1 (275–185 cm) is dominated by Halamphora montana. Unit 2 (185–85 cm) is broadly similar, but shows a decrease in H. montana and an increase in Nitzschia liebetruthii. Unit 3 (85–55 cm) shows a turnover to Diadesmis confervacea and Pinnularia gibba, indicating shallow, marshy, organic-rich conditions during the Joseon Dynasty. Although a subsidiary split is visible in the CONISS dendrogram around 130–120 cm, this interval was not designated as a separate zone because no clear lithofacies change is observed across that boundary (Figs. 3 and 5).

To summarize the major compositional changes through the 22SC-9 sequence, DCA Axis 1 sample scores are shown alongside the diatom assemblage profiles in Fig. 4. The DCA profile captures the principal hydro-ecological gradient, distinguishing more persistent aquatic assemblages from increasingly shallow-water and exposed wetland assemblages dominated by benthic and aerophilous taxa.

Phytolith assemblage (22SC-9)

Analysis of the 22SC-9 core yielded 15 phytolith taxa, with the assemblage structured into four distinct biostratigraphic units (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Data S1).

Units 1–2 (11.0–6.3 m): Dominated by wild herbaceous Poaceae (e.g., Andropogoneae, Miscanthus), with transient peaks in Zizania and Bambusoideae reflecting natural fluctuations.

Unit 3 (6.3–5.0 m): Marks a major shift with the consistent appearance of cultivated Oryza sativa (both leaf bulliform and husk types).

Unit 4 (5.0–4.3 m): Similar to Unit 3, but distinguished by the first occurrence of Triticum–Hordeum and Cyperaceae phytoliths.

Multivariate statistical analysis and regime shift detection

Detrended Correspondence Analysis (DCA) of the diatom data revealed a primary ecological gradient (DCA Axis 1 length: 3.47 SD) reflecting coupled changes in habitat structure, hydrological stability, and surface exposure (Supplementary Figure S1). Positive DCA1 scores are associated with assemblages dominated by benthic and aerophilous taxa (e.g., Navicula, Nitzschia, Humidophila, and Luticola), consistent with shallow-water, substrate-influenced, and periodically exposed conditions. Negative scores are associated with assemblages dominated by planktonic or weakly acidophilous taxa (e.g., Aulacoseira and Eunotia), consistent with more persistent aquatic conditions.

The Sequential t-test Analysis of Regime Shifts (STARS), applied to the full DCA Axis 1 profile, detected a statistically significant regime shift at ca. 2.3 ka (p < 0.05). While the full STARS output is provided in the Supplementary Data S1 (worksheet: DCA and Regime shift from 22SC-9), Fig. 7 focuses on this transition, which marks a rapid reorganization from a climate-driven lacustrine system to an anthropogenically influenced landscape, synchronized with the onset of intensive agriculture.

Discussion

Natural environmental succession (early to mid-holocene)

The Sinchang-dong wetland formed during the early Holocene as backswamps of the Yeongsan River, driven by post-glacial base-level rise. These long-term geomorphic controls provided the basin setting for wetland development prior to the late Holocene anthropogenic transition. The coinciding boundaries between diatom (Fig. 4) and phytolith (Fig. 6) units indicate that wetland initiation began around ~ 8.0 ka. This synchronous initiation across different biological proxies suggests a rapid stabilization of the fluvial system, where rising sea levels facilitated the formation of high-water-retention basins despite the limited upstream catchment.

During Unit 1 of 22SC-9 (ca. 9.0–6.0 ka), the site was a shallow, macrophyte-rich, and slightly acidic wetland within a low-energy backswamp setting, dominated by acidophilic Eunotia spp. and epiphytic Cocconeis placentula (Fig. 4)38–40. Episodic peaks of planktonic Aulacoseira ambigua reflect seasonal water-level fluctuations or enhanced precipitation during this initial stage (Fig. 4)41. These hydrologic pulses were likely essential for maintaining the wetland’s connectivity to the main river channel before the establishment of a fully closed lacustrine system. Vegetation was primarily composed of Phragmites and wild herbaceous Poaceae (Andropogoneae and Miscanthus), representing a typical natural wetland structure (Fig. 6)42.

Subsequently, during Unit 2 of 22SC-9 (ca. 6.0–2.3 ka), the wetland transitioned into a stable, deeper lacustrine environment dominated by planktonic Aulacoseira taxa, indicating sustained open-water expansion (Fig. 4)41,43,44. The overwhelming dominance of planktonic diatoms during this period reflects a mature wetland stage, where the accumulation of fine-grained sediments created a perennial water body with reduced terrestrial interference. Phytolith records of Zizania and arborescent Bambusoideae suggest increasing vegetational diversity and progressive stabilization of the surrounding hinterland (Fig. 6)45–47. Throughout the mid-Holocene, Sinchang-dong maintained a mature ecosystem governed primarily by natural climatic and geomorphological processes (Supplementary Figure S3). This interpretation is supported by the DCA Axis 1 profile, which summarizes the principal hydro-ecological gradient in the record. Negative Axis 1 scores correspond to assemblages associated with more persistent aquatic conditions, whereas positive scores reflect increasing dominance of benthic and aerophilous taxa under shallower, more exposed wetland conditions.

Anthropogenic transformation and agricultural intensification

Around 2.3 ka, the wetland departed from its natural trajectory due to intensified human activities. This departure represents a fundamental shift in the site’s ecological function, transitioning from a climate-sensitive archive to an anthropogenically managed landscape. This late Holocene transition is distinguished from the earlier climate- and geomorphology-driven evolution of the basin because it coincides with abrupt aerophilous diatom turnover, increased cultivated Oryza sativa phytoliths, and archaeological evidence for wetland reclamation and agricultural intensification in the Yeongsan River basin36,37. This transition is also reflected in the DCA Axis 1 profile, which shifts toward more positive values and indicates increasingly shallow-water, substrate-influenced, and exposed wetland conditions. Navicula and Nitzschia type taxa are not interpreted here as uniform indicators of eutrophication, because ecological preferences vary substantially among species within these genera. In Unit 3 of 22SC-9 (ca. 2.3–1.8 ka), the abrupt disappearance of lacustrine diatoms and their replacement by aerophilous taxa, specifically Hantzschia amphioxys, suggests increased surface exposure and wetland drying, consistent with intensified human modification of the wetland surface (Fig. 4)48–51. This pattern may reflect drainage and surface disturbance associated with reclamation activities during this period.

This turnover is synchronized with a sharp increase in cultivated Oryza sativa phytoliths (husk and bulliform types) (Fig. 6)52–54. It is also accompanied by increased abundances of benthic naviculoid taxa, including Navicula and Nitzschia (Fig. 4), consistent with enhanced substrate influence, shallow-water conditions, and likely nutrient enrichment associated with organized agrarian activities55–57. The synchronous rise of these shallow-water and substrate-associated assemblages suggests a reorganization of wetland biogeochemical conditions, potentially linked to increased nutrient input and sediment disturbance associated with reclamation and cultivation. Similar patterns in the 23SCT trench corroborate broader terrestrialization and intensified human-driven transformation (Fig. 5). The 23SCT trench refines these late Holocene changes at higher stratigraphic resolution. Unit 1 is dominated by Halamphora montana with subordinate Hantzschia amphioxys, indicating shallow and exposure-prone wetland conditions48–51. Unit 2 remains broadly similar, but shows reduced H. montana and increased Humidophila contenta, Luticola mutica, suggesting stronger substrate influence under fluctuating shallow-water conditions48–50. Unit 3 records a turnover to Diadesmis confervacea and Pinnularia gibba, suggesting shallow, marshy wetland conditions during the Joseon Dynasty60. Given that rice cultivation in the Yeongsan River basin had already been established since the Early Iron Age36,37, these assemblages are consistent with a managed agricultural wetland within a long-term cultivated landscape.

During the Three Kingdoms period (after ca. 1.8 ka), the prevalence of terrestrial indicators (Humidophila contenta, Luticola mutica) and diversified phytoliths (Triticum–Hordeum and Cyperaceae) indicate advanced anthropogenic control over field hydrology and subsistence strategies (Figs. 4, 5 and 6)48,51,58,59. The diversification of crops, including winter cereals such as barley and wheat, would have functioned as a seasonal risk-buffering mechanism under monsoon-dominated climatic regimes. This intensity was likely facilitated by technological advancements, including iron farming tools and ox-plowing36.

By the Joseon Dynasty (~ 0.5 ka), the dominance of Diadesmis confervacea and the occurrence of Pinnularia gibba suggest shallow and marshy wetland conditions under intensified hydrological management (Fig. 5; Unit 3)60. The peak abundance of D. confervacea provides evidence for this hydrological shift; as a periphytic species primarily inhabiting shallow, marshy environments with elevated temperatures (Fig. 5)60, its dominance indicates persistently low water levels in a warm, marshy setting. This pattern reflects a reconfiguration of the hydrological system into a more closed irrigation network, such as artificial ditches or small-scale reservoirs. Furthermore, the occurrence of P. gibba is interpreted more cautiously as part of a Pinnularia assemblage associated with restricted wetland conditions61. Collectively, these lines of evidence suggest that, during the Joseon Dynasty, the agricultural landscape was likely managed through a highly regulated hydrological system. The subsequent stages of this anthropogenic transformation are conceptualized in Supplementary Figure S3.

Quantitative evidence for human-driven regime shifts

The DCA of diatom assemblages revealed a primary ecological gradient (Axis 1 length: 3.47 SD) representing an integrated hydro-ecological gradient. The substantial gradient length indicates major compositional turnover through the sequence, consistent with pronounced ecological reorganization rather than minor fluctuation. Negative DCA Axis 1 scores are associated with more persistent aquatic assemblages, whereas positive scores reflect increasingly shallow-water, substrate-influenced, and exposed wetland conditions dominated by benthic and aerophilous taxa (Figs. 7 and Supplementary Figure S1). In this context, the DCA profile provides a concise summary of the principal hydro-ecological transition recorded in the Sinchang-dong sequence. A statistically significant regime shift in DCA Axis 1 scores, identified via STARS, occurs at ~ 2.3 ka (Fig. 7). This transition, synchronized with pronounced diversification in CONISS clusters, represents a coherent ecological reorganization rather than a gradual background trend. The abruptness of this shift suggests that cumulative agricultural pressure contributed to a major ecological reorganization of the wetland during the late Holocene. The shift coincides with a sharp increase in the Summed Probability Distribution (SPD) of regional radiocarbon dates, signifying demographic expansion and intensified human activity (Fig. 7)62,63. Rising Pinus pollen percentages in the Yeongsan River basin are therefore interpreted as reflecting the expansion of Pinus densiflora, a light-demanding pioneer taxon, under increasingly open and disturbed landscape conditions. Choi et al.64 linked this expansion to human impacts, including agricultural intensification, rather than to the development of undisturbed closed forest.

While forest clearance began during the Bronze Age, the Early Iron Age marked a critical threshold of irreversible ecological change36. A key driver was the technological transition from selective ear harvesting using stone knives to bundled stalk harvesting enabled by iron sickles, which drastically increased land-use intensity and altered the nutrient feedback loops between the catchment and the basin65. Such technological intensification would have amplified soil disturbance and nutrient input into the wetland basin, reinforcing the shift toward benthic, shallow-water, and soil-associated diatom assemblages. This shift is further supported by rising Pinus pollen in the Yeongsan River basin, which is interpreted as Pinus densiflora expansion under increasing human impact and agricultural intensification64.

Simultaneously, the phytolith record shows a coordinated turnover at ~ 2.3 ka: wild Poaceae taxa (Andropogoneae and Miscanthus) declined as cultivated rice (Oryza sativa) phytoliths, particularly husk-derived morphotypes, increased and persisted (Fig. 6)52–54. The prevalence of husk phytoliths provides direct evidence for intensified on-site crop processing. Collectively, the synchronized regime shift (STARS), assemblage diversification (CONISS), coordinated phytolith turnover, and regional demographic rise suggest that the Sinchang-dong wetland underwent a major anthropogenic regime shift at ~ 2.3 ka, transitioning from a predominantly natural system to an increasingly human-influenced landscape. The persistence of elevated DCA Axis 1 scores and benthic–aerophilous assemblages after ~ 2.3 ka further indicates that this transition was not a short-lived fluctuation but a sustained reorganization of wetland hydro-ecological conditions.

Hydrological instability during the 8.2 ka and 4.2 ka events

The Sinchang-dong record captures two pronounced diatom-poor intervals broadly associated with the 8.2 ka and 4.2 ka climatic events1,3,8,11,12. Although their underlying mechanisms differ—AMOC weakening for the 8.2 ka event versus solar and atmospheric reorganization for the 4.2 ka event—both elicited a consistent ecological response: a sharp decline in diatom concentrations culminating in diatom-poor intervals (Fig. 8). A notable feature is the significant increase in the chrysophyte/diatom (C/D) ratio (Fig. 8). Elevated C/D ratios indicate shifts in algal assemblage structure under stressed aquatic conditions and may reflect changes in nutrient availability, basin mixing, and seasonal hydroclimatic stress66,67. While C/D ratios may also be influenced by changes in nutrient availability and basin mixing dynamics, the synchronicity of C/D peaks with abrupt reductions in diatom abundance is consistent with intensified hydroclimatic stress during these intervals.

Crucially, both intervals broadly associated with these events exhibit a transient increase in aerophilic soil diatoms (Hantzschia amphioxys and Luticola mutica) immediately prior to the diatom-poor intervals (Fig. 8). This recurrent successional pattern—from aquatic decline to soil diatom dominance and ultimately to a diatom-poor interval—is consistent with progressive wetland contraction and subaerial exposure driven by acute hydrological stress. Based on this, we subdivide these intervals into an ‘event zone’ (initial stress) and a ‘diatom-poor interval’ (peak perturbation). In the interval associated with the 8.2 ka event, the event zone (ca. 8.10–8.20 m) is followed by a diatom-poor interval (ca. 8.00–8.10 m) in which valves were absent (Fig. 8). Similarly, the interval associated with the 4.2 ka event includes a soil-taxon-dominated event zone (ca. 7.00–7.16 m) followed by a diatom-poor interval (ca. 6.96–7.00 m) (Fig. 8).

The diatom-poor intervals are preceded by a marked decline in diatom concentration, culminating in levels where valves were absent or too rare for meaningful counting. No clear evidence of severe valve dissolution was observed in adjacent samples. Moreover, phytoliths remain present across these intervals, suggesting that pervasive silica dissolution is unlikely to be the sole explanation for diatom absence, although differential preservation between siliceous microfossils cannot be entirely excluded. Terrestrial responses, however, reveal distinct differences between the two events. The 8.2 ka interval is associated with phytolith concentrations reaching their lowest values in the entire sequence (Fig. 6). Because concentration data may also be influenced by sediment accumulation dynamics, this pattern is interpreted cautiously rather than as direct evidence of ecosystem-wide suppression or reduced plant biomass. No major lithofacies or grain-size change is observed across these intervals, suggesting that the concentration minima are not obviously associated with a major sedimentological shift, although the influence of sediment accumulation dynamics cannot be fully excluded. In contrast, the interval associated with the 4.2 ka event was characterized by vegetation restructuring rather than uniform collapse. Specifically, the sharp decline in moisture-sensitive Bambusoideae (bamboo) serves as a sensitive indicator of aridification (Fig. 6)68. This localized response aligns with regional trends in the Lower Yangtze Valley, where monsoon weakening also led to bamboo declines46.

The recovery trajectories further distinguish these events. Post-8.2 ka recovery was gradual and prolonged, with C/D ratios remaining high, suggesting lingering thermal stress linked to AMOC-induced cooling. Conversely, the 4.2 ka event was followed by an abrupt rebound in diatom abundance, highlighting the intrinsic resilience of the Sinchang-dong wetland when hydroclimatic stress relaxed (Fig. 8). The contrasting recovery trajectories likely reflect differences in forcing structure and regional hydroclimatic variability. The 8.2 ka event, associated with hemispheric-scale cooling linked to AMOC weakening1,3, imposed more prolonged hydro-ecological stress, delaying ecological recovery. In contrast, the 4.2 ka event has been increasingly interpreted as a period of amplified regional hydroclimatic variability rather than uniform cooling8,11,12, allowing more rapid rebound once effective moisture conditions recovered. In small rain-fed basins with limited hydrological buffering capacity, such differences in forcing duration and moisture structure can produce asymmetric recovery responses, as observed in the Sinchang-dong record.

The observed age jumps may reflect the inherent vulnerability of small-scale, rain-fed fluvial wetlands to hydroclimatic deterioration and associated sedimentary instability. No distinct oxidized horizon or abrupt lithological boundary was identified across these intervals, suggesting that there is no clear stratigraphic evidence for a major hiatus. However, short-term non-deposition or sedimentary condensation cannot be fully excluded. The interpretation of the 4.2 ka interval as a phase of hydrological stress is also consistent with regional paleoclimate records from the Korean Peninsula and coastal East Asia, which indicate hydroclimatic deterioration or drying centered on, or spanning, the interval around 4.2 ka18–20. These records suggest that the Sinchang-dong interval is broadly consistent with regional hydroclimatic deterioration, even if its local expression may have been shaped by site-specific sedimentary sensitivity.

While the 8.2 ka event was a globally synchronous phenomenon, the 4.2 ka event exhibits significant regional variability5,12. The divergent responses observed within the limited extent of the peninsula—driven by maritime influences and shifts in the westerly jet—suggest that the 4.2 ka event may represent an assemblage of site-level climatic excursions driven by heightened regional sensitivity, rather than a singular global forcing12.

Conclusions

This study reconstructs the Holocene environmental history of the Sinchang-dong wetland using a multi-proxy approach that integrates diatom and phytolith assemblages with a Bayesian age–depth model spanning approximately 9.0–1.0 ka. The results distinguish climate-driven environmental perturbations from later anthropogenic transformations, revealing contrasting wetland responses to abrupt climatic events and intensified human land use.

During the early to mid-Holocene, the wetland functioned as a naturally regulated backswamp system that was sensitive to abrupt climatic forcing. The intervals broadly associated with the 8.2 ka and 4.2 ka events are marked by diatom-poor intervals and elevated chrysophyte/diatom (C/D) ratios, indicating phases of hydro-ecological instability. However, these events differed in their ecological expression: the interval associated with the 8.2 ka event was followed by a more prolonged recovery, whereas the interval associated with the 4.2 ka event was followed by more rapid reorganization, reflecting differing modes of wetland response to climatic disturbance.

In contrast, a statistically significant ecological regime shift at ca. 2.3 ka marked a transition from a climate-dominated wetland system to an anthropogenically structured agricultural landscape. This shift was supported by coordinated changes in diatom assemblages, a sharp increase in cultivated rice phytoliths, and evidence for regional demographic expansion, indicating agricultural intensification during the transition from the Bronze Age to the Iron Age. Collectively, these findings show that while early Holocene wetland dynamics were governed primarily by climatic forcing, human activities ultimately became the dominant driver of ecological change. This study provides a process- based framework for understanding long-term wetland resilience and threshold behavior in East Asian fluvial wetlands under combined climatic and anthropogenic pressures.

Materials and methods

Chronology

Radiocarbon dating

For chronological control of the sedimentary sequences, a total of 20 plant-fragment samples were selected for AMS 14C dating: 14 samples from core 22SC-9 and six samples from the 23SCT trench. To remove potential contaminants, such as secondary carbonates and humic acids, all samples underwent standard Acid–Alkali–Acid (AAA) pretreatment using 0.5 M HCl and 0.5 M NaOH and were dated at the Korea Institute of Geoscience and Mineral Resources (KIGAM). Conventional 14C ages were calibrated to calendar ages using IntCal2069.

OSL dating

Optically Stimulated Luminescence (OSL) dating was performed on four samples from the 22SC-9 core to supplement the chronological framework of sedimentary units with limited organic matter. Fine-grained quartz (4–11 μm) was used for the analyses. Quartz grains were purified using standard physical and chemical pretreatment procedures, including HCl, H2O2 and H2SiF6 treatments. Equivalent doses (De) were determined using the single-aliquot regenerative-dose (SAR) protocol70 with a Risø TL/OSL DA-20 reader, and environmental dose rates (Dr) were measured by gamma-ray spectrometry (Canberra SEGc 3018). Potential feldspar contamination was assessed using the IR depletion ratio71.

Bayesian age–depth modeling

A Bayesian age–depth model was constructed for the 22SC-9 core using the P_Sequence depositional model in OxCal v4.4 to establish a continuous, high-resolution chronology72–74. This approach accounts for random variations in sedimentation rates, enabling estimation of wetland environmental transitions. To ensure consistency between radiocarbon and OSL chronologies, all modeled ages are expressed uniformly in ka. Calibrated radiocarbon ages were converted to the same temporal scale by correcting for the 73-year offset between the 1950 CE radiocarbon reference year and the OSL measurement year. Chronological data from the 23SCT trench were also converted to the ka scale and used as supplementary constraints for stratigraphic correlation. To assess model performance, OxCal agreement indices and convergence values were evaluated. An outlier model with a prior probability of 0.05 was applied during model construction. The final model yielded an Amodel of 43.5 and an Aoverall of 59.1, and most dated levels showed convergence values greater than 95%. All measured radiocarbon and OSL ages are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Diatom and chrysophyte analysis

For diatom and Chrysophyte analysis, a total of 92 subsamples were processed from two sediment cores, 22SC-9 and 23SCT (Supplementary Data S1., Sheet1:22SC-9 Diatom, Sheet 2: 23SCT Diatom). In core 22SC-9, 80 subsamples were collected at 4–10 cm intervals from depths of 4.3 to 11.0 m. In core 23SCT, 12 subsamples were obtained at 20 cm intervals from depths of 55 to 275 cm. While diatom analysis was performed for both cores, chrysophyte stomatocysts were analyzed exclusively for the 80 subsamples from core 22SC-9. For each subsample, approximately 0.1 g of dry sediment was placed in a 200 mL beaker and treated with 10 mL of 30% H2O2 at boiling temperature for 1 h to remove organic matter. To ensure complete disaggregation of the sediment matrix, hexametaphosphate was added to the mixture and allowed to react for 30 min.

Subsequently, approximately 180 mL of distilled water was added to each beaker, and the sediments were allowed to settle for 4 h. This rinsing process was repeated three times with fresh distilled water to remove residual chemicals. Finally, the concentrated bottom solution (0.7–0.9 mL) was pipetted onto a coverslip using a micropipette and permanently mounted using a high-refractive-index mounting medium, following the procedure described by Katsuki et al.33.

Diatom valves were identified and counted under a light microscope at 1,000× magnification. At least 300 valves were counted per slide to ensure statistical reliability. Chrysophyte stomatocysts were counted from the same slides used for diatom analysis in core 22SC-9. The chrysophyte/diatom (C/D) ratio was calculated for each sample as the count ratio of chrysophyte stomatocysts to diatom valves and was used as a supplementary indicator of changes in algal assemblage structure. Diatom concentration was calculated as valve number per gram of dry sediment following the procedure described by Cho et al.24. Taxonomic identification followed the specialized guidelines provided by Round et al.75, Watanabe55, Kobayashi et al.76, Joh38,40,41, and Joh et al.77. Raw diatom counts were converted to relative abundances (%) for each sample, and the full raw and relative abundance datasets are provided in Supplementary Data S1. Unidentified taxa were retained in the raw data tables for transparency but excluded from subsequent statistical analyses because they could not be assigned consistent taxonomic or ecological affinities.

Specimens in valve view were recorded as spp. to maintain identification accuracy. Aulacoseira spp. likely represents one of the four dominant taxa identified in this study: A. ambigua, A. granulata, A. nygaardii, or A. lirata.

Phytoliths analysis from 22SC-9

Phytoliths derived from both motor cells (bulliform cells) and inflorescence bracts (husk/glume cells) were analyzed for paleoenvironmental reconstruction (Supplementary Data S1., Sheet8: 22SC-9 Phytolith). A dried soil sample weighing 1.0000 g was mixed with 0.0200 g of glass beads (40–70 μm) as an internal standard for quantitative analysis. Organic matter and carbonates were removed using 30% H₂O₂ and 10% HCl, respectively, to isolate the phytolith fraction. Particles outside the 20–100 μm size range were removed using a combination of sedimentation (based on Stokes’ law) and wet sieving.

The processed residues were mounted in glycerin jelly, allowing rotation of phytoliths and facilitating precise morphological identification under an optical microscope at 400–630× magnification. Phytoliths were counted alongside glass beads until a minimum of 300 beads (200 in high-yield samples) were recorded. Absolute phytolith concentrations were calculated following the method of Fujiwara78. Morphological classification of Poaceae motor cells was conducted at the subfamily, tribe, or genus level78, while other morphotypes were classified according to the nomenclature of Mulholland and Rapp79 and Twiss80. Diagnostic rice phytoliths were identified following established morphological criteria52–54, and husk-derived morphotypes were distinguished from bulliform types based on double-peaked and dendritic elongate forms.

Multivariate statistical analysis and regime shift detection

To identify dominant trends in diatom assemblages and to examine structural transitions in the palaeoenvironmental record, multivariate statistical analyses were applied. First, detrended correspondence analysis (DCA) was used to assess the gradient length of the species data81. The length of the first DCA axis was 3.47 standard deviation (SD) units, indicating a unimodal response of diatom species along the underlying environmental gradient. Accordingly, DCA was selected as the primary ordination method, as it is more appropriate than linear approaches such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA) when gradient lengths exceed 2–3 SD units.

Raw diatom counts were converted to relative abundances (%) for each sample, and the relative abundance datasets were normalized to sum to 100%. To reduce noise from rare taxa and emphasize major stratigraphic assemblage changes, threshold-filtered datasets including only identified taxa were used for statistical analyses. In core 22SC-9, only taxa reaching > 5% relative abundance in at least one sample were retained for CONISS zonation and DCA. Prior to DCA, the retained relative-abundance dataset was Hellinger-transformed to reduce the disproportionate influence of dominant taxa and improve comparability among samples. No additional manual mean-centering was applied. DCA was then performed using the vegan package in R, with rare species downweighted to improve the robustness of the ordination82,83. In trench 23SCT, only taxa reaching > 3% relative abundance in at least one sample were retained for CONISS zonation.

To ensure ecological representativeness and robust ordination results, samples containing fewer than 20 diatom valves (n < 20) were excluded from the analysis. These samples correspond to diatom-poor intervals in which diatoms were extremely rare or absent (notably at depths of 6.96–7.00 m and 8.00–8.10 m) and therefore do not provide meaningful assemblage information for multivariate analysis. Accordingly, these stratigraphic levels were excluded from the DCA and subsequent cluster analysis (CONISS). The resulting DCA Axis 1 scores were retained for subsequent quantitative analyses.

To objectively identify the timing of abrupt changes in the ordination results, regime shift detection (RSD) analysis was applied to the full DCA Axis 1 score profile of core 22SC-9 (11.0–4.3 m) using the Sequential t-test Analysis of Regime Shifts (STARS) algorithm84. The analysis was conducted using a target significance level of p < 0.05 and a Huber weight function to reduce the influence of outliers. This approach enabled the statistical identification of significant shifts in the DCA Axis 1 time series.
