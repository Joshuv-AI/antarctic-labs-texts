Substrate-bound structures of yeast ALA synthase reveal protein dynamics initiating heme biosynthesis
Authors: Jenny U. Tran, Thyandra A. Martinez, Christopher Jurich, Margaret R. Josling, Julian M. Zhang, Pedro H. Ayres-Galhardo, Jens Meiler, Zhongyue J. Yang, Jason A. MacGurn, Breann L. Brown
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heme is an essential prosthetic group involved in multiple biological processes in nearly all organisms. Heme biosynthesis is initiated by the pyridoxal 5′-phosphate–dependent enzyme aminolevulinic acid synthase (ALAS). Mutations in ALAS cause multiple hematological diseases. Despite sharing approximately 70% sequence similarity, ALAS homologs exhibit distinct expression and regulatory mechanisms. However, the molecular basis for their functional divergence remains poorly understood. Here, we used x-ray crystallography and molecular dynamics simulations to elucidate the structure and dynamics of Saccharomyces cerevisiae ALAS (scALAS) upon substrate binding. We identified critical conformational changes that occur during catalysis, regulating enzyme activity and protein-protein interactions specific to eukaryotic heme synthesis. Using evolution-guided engineering, we generated scALAS variants with altered catalytic activity and cellular metabolic profiles. Our findings reveal molecular and structural mechanisms underlying heme synthesis regulation and establish a framework for developing targeted therapeutics to treat heme-deficiency disorders and inform a rational design of next-generation therapeutic tools.

INTRODUCTION

Heme is an indispensable molecule for nearly all organisms. Not only is it critical for oxygen transport in red blood cells, but it also serves as a cofactor supporting gene transcription, electron transport, and xenobiotic detoxification across many kingdoms of life (1–5). Heme metabolism must be tightly controlled due to the cellular toxicity stemming from heme deficiency or buildup of highly reactive porphyrin precursors (6–9). In addition, eukaryotic heme synthesis is partitioned between the mitochondrion and cytoplasm, requiring the intricate coordination of several enzymes and metabolic intermediates to ensure proper heme maturation (1). The connection between protein structure and function is well established, yet the role of allostery in altering both structure and function remains unknown for many systems. Recent computational studies reveal that enzymes subject to allostery, or the binding of molecules at distal sites that affect activity, are more frequently associated with human disease than nonallosteric proteins (10). Thus, defining how structural changes control enzyme structure, activity, and molecular interactions can illuminate previously unknown insights into heme synthesis critical for the design of next-generation therapeutics and molecular tools.

The mitochondrial enzyme aminolevulinic acid synthase (ALAS) is the first and rate-limiting enzyme for heme biosynthesis (Fig. 1A) (11–13). ALAS belongs to the α-oxoamine synthase (AOS) family of pyridoxal 5′-phosphate (PLP)–dependent enzymes (14, 15), catalyzing the condensation of glycine and succinyl–coenzyme A(sCoA) to produce aminolevulinic acid (ALA), which is the first, committed heme precursor (Fig. 1B) (11, 13, 16–19). ALAS homologs are expressed in α-proteobacteria and nonplant eukaryotes, but the most notable divergence emerged because of a gene duplication event that generated two vertebrate ALAS isoforms (20–22). ALAS1 is a ubiquitously expressed housekeeping enzyme, and ALAS2 is the erythroid-specific isoform that produces 85 to 90% of the total body heme pool for erythropoiesis. Importantly, abnormal heme synthesis leads to multiple diseases. More than 100 reported ALAS2 mutations underlie either X-linked sideroblastic anemia (XLSA) due to ALAS2 loss of function or X-linked protoporphyria (XLPP) due to ALAS2 gain of function (23–26). Therefore, defining the regulation of ALAS activity is essential for developing advanced approaches to treat several hematological diseases.

Despite ALAS homologs sharing ∼70% sequence similarity, certain ALAS regions, and thus enzyme regulation, diverge substantially between organisms. For example, eukaryotic ALAS enzymes contain an N-terminal extension (Nt-ext) and C-terminal extension (Ct-ext) that are absent in bacterial homologs. Both regions were shown to regulate human ALAS2 activity (27–30). The ALAS2 Nt-ext is implicated in heme binding and enzyme inactivation (31–35). The Ct-ext modulates active site accessibility and is deleted in XLPP disease variants (24, 27). Currently, the only published structures of an ALAS enzyme bound to substrate are from Rhodobacter capsulatus ALAS (rcALAS), which lacks these important eukaryote-specific domains (36). Therefore, further investigation into eukaryotic ALAS homologs is necessary to establish how heme synthesis is altered in these complex organisms.

Here, using x-ray crystallography and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, we establish how conformational changes direct catalysis and control Saccharomyces cerevisiae ALAS (scALAS) activity. Next, we used in vitro enzymology to identify how structural differences affect ALAS activity and protein-protein interactions. Last, we engineered multiple scALAS variant strains, establishing the connection between in vitro mechanisms and cellular heme metabolism. These findings define the mechanism by which eukaryotic ALAS enzymes couple structural changes to activity and ALA synthesis, paving the way for innovative therapeutic strategies that target this essential class of enzymes.

RESULTS

Glycine binding promotes protein dynamics to initiate catalysis in scALAS

Eukaryotic ALAS enzymes have divergent structural motifs and catalytic regulation compared with bacterial enzymes, yet structural analysis of active eukaryote-specific states is lacking. To define the structural transformations associated with ALAS catalysis in eukaryotes, we determined the cocrystal structure of scALAS in the presence of glycine to 2.49-Å resolution (Fig. 1, C and D, and Table 1). The construct used for crystallization (residues 58 to 548) lacks the mitochondrial targeting sequence, and cocomplexes were prepared by incubating scALAS with substrate before crystal formation to ensure that the resulting complexes adopt physiologically-relevant binding conformations (37). ALAS enzymes exist as obligate homodimers with each active site located at the twofold dimer interface. To begin catalysis, PLP binds in the active site and forms an internal aldimine adduct with the ALAS active site lysine (PLP–Lys; Fig. 1B) (38). In the next step of catalysis, glycine binds and displaces the lysine residue from PLP to form the pyridoxyl-glycine (PLG) external aldimine. By incubating scALAS with its substrates before crystal formation, we captured scALAS in a catalytically active form, allowing glycine to react with the PLP cofactor to form PLG (Fig. 1E).

Crystals of scALAS with glycine (scALAS-Gly) contain six molecules in the asymmetric unit (ASU), forming three biological homodimers (fig. S1A). The electron density at the active sites confirmed the presence of the PLG external aldimine across all protomers (fig. S1B). The chemical conversion to pyridoxyl-glycine is accompanied by a structural transition that divides each scALAS subunit into two lobes, with the active site sandwiched in the middle (Fig. 1D). The fold of the ALAS protomer is unusual, as both lobes contain residues distal in sequence due to the chain passing, multiple times, between both lobes (Fig. 1F). The upper lobe contains a compact helical bundle surrounding a seven-stranded β sheet, which makes up the bulk of the catalytic core of the protein (residues 124 to 387). The scALAS lower lobe encompasses regions from multiple domains, including the Nt-ext, catalytic core, and Ct-ext, that adopt different orientations relative to the upper lobe despite all being bound to pyridoxyl-glycine. Bridging the two lobes is the beginning of the Ct-ext, which is markedly disordered in all glycine-bound protomers (fig. S1A). These structures show that scALAS catalysis is achieved by concerted, rigid-body conformational changes mediated by transitions in the local dynamics of a unique scALAS Ct-ext Hinge.

scALAS uniquely positions sCoA for catalysis

To complete the catalytic cycle, sCoA condenses with pyridoxyl-glycine to form ALA, CoA, and CO2 before the enzyme returns to the PLP–Lys state. Previous studies of murine ALAS2 reported that sCoA accelerates external aldimine formation by 90-fold, likely because of a conformational change of the enzyme (38). Since the crystal structure of scALAS-Gly revealed domain motions not seen in homologous bacterial structures, we also determined the 2.69-Å resolution cocrystal structure of scALAS in the presence of its second substrate, sCoA (Fig. 2, A and B, and Table 1). Similar to scALAS-Gly, the scALAS-sCoA crystal structure contains six molecules (three biological homodimers) in the ASU. sCoA is bound to five of six scALAS molecules. Furthermore, the PLP–Lys bond is cleaved in four of the five sCoA-bound protomers, signifying that sCoA binding also chemically primes the enzyme for catalysis (fig. S1C).

Unexpectedly, sCoA adopts a unique orientation in the active site compared to the bacterial enzyme. The sCoA adenosyl head group is bound within a hydrophobic pocket at the entrance to the active site, with the rest of the sCoA molecule exhibiting conformational flexibility. Previous studies report that conformational changes in an active site loop, referred to hereafter as the Gate (scALAS residues 444 to 461), correlate with the rate-limiting release of ALA from the enzyme active site (39). ALAS homologs contain a conserved threonine residue (scALAS T452) at the Gate’s apex; mutation of this residue was presumed to alter sCoA binding in human ALAS2, leading to loss of function and the disease XLSA (40). Notably, scALAS uses a fundamentally different interaction mechanism that does not involve the conserved threonine (Fig. 2C). Instead, in the scALAS-sCoA structure, there is a hydrogen bond between the main chain of P451 and the sCoA pantetheine moiety. This substrate orientation is unique from the rcALAS structure in the presence of sCoA (36). In rcALAS, the threonine side chain (rcALAS T365) directly engages the terminal sCoA carboxylate (Fig. 2C). Therefore, our scALAS-sCoA structure indicates that the eukaryotic ALAS Gate may regulate activity via a process distinct from direct sCoA coordination. These unique substrate-enzyme interactions not only alter the positioning of sCoA but also result in structural changes affecting motifs distal to the active site. For example, the sCoA orientation in scALAS ablates the interaction between the Gate and a conserved N-terminal α helix (Fig. 2D). Since the eukaryotic ALAS N terminus is known to interact with other proteins (see below), this eukaryote-specific conformational change may be important for the enzyme to respond to the binding of different allosteric effectors.

scALAS uses a C-terminal hinge to access distinct enzyme conformations

Because of the structural and chemical heterogeneity of our co-crystal structures, we hypothesized that identifying variable regions would give insight into conformational dynamics specific to eukaryotic ALAS substrate binding and catalysis. Overlaying the six scALAS-Gly protomers using the upper lobe (residues 124 to 387) shows that the lower lobe moves as a rigid body toward the active site to varying degrees (Fig. 3A). The average root mean square deviation (RMSD) between the lower lobes of all protomers is 0.34 Å, more than twice the RMSD between the upper-lobe conformations (0.15 Å). The most significant deviations arise in the Gate, a segment of an α helix that buttresses the Gate (residues 422 to 435), and the Ct-ext Hinge (residues 491 to 516, Fig. 3B). Specifically, T452 in the Gate translates 3 Å toward the PLG ligand. The Gate from each protomer adopts a unique position, highlighting the inherent structural dynamics during initiation of catalysis. This conformational sampling is paired with flexibility and crystallographic disorder at the beginning of the Ct-ext, which effectively serves as a hinge connecting the two lobes (Fig. 3B). Thus, glycine binding triggers a concerted movement of the scALAS lower lobe to modulate active site accessibility for catalysis.

Superimposing the upper lobes of the six scALAS-sCoA protomers identified an increase in structural stability in all domains upon sCoA binding compared with glycine (Fig. 3C). The lower lobes of all the protomers are displaced by an average RMSD of 0.8 Å away from the dimer interface, and the lower lobes deviate from one another with an average RMSD of 0.106 Å. Similarly, sCoA imposes conformational homogeneity in the upper lobe of each protomer, which also displays the same RMSD as the lower lobes. Compared to the scALAS apoenzyme and scALAS-Gly structures, the scALAS-sCoA model also exhibits enhanced rigidity in the Gate and Ct-ext Hinge (Fig. 3D and fig. S1A). Five of six scALAS-sCoA protomers contain an ordered Ct-ext Hinge; the only scALAS-sCoA protomer with a disordered Hinge neighbors an active site that lacks bound substrate. This distinct disorder-to-order transition enables the Ct-ext to bridge the two ALAS lobes and act as a hinge controlling lower lobe movement. Examination of ALAS structures from prokaryotes shows that the Gate movements occur in a binary fashion, transitioning from an “open” precatalytic state to a “closed” substrate-bound state, irrespective of the nature of the substrate (36). However, the scALAS Gate follows a more complex trajectory during catalytic initiation: (i) an open, “precatalytic” state, (ii) a dynamic “intermediate” state, and (iii) a uniform, catalytically “primed” state (Fig. 3, E and F).

In silico analyses confirm structural heterogeneity

To investigate the relevance of ALAS structural changes induced by substrate binding identified in the crystal structures, we used MD simulations to monitor enzyme dynamics in the presence of either substrate. Throughout most of the MD trajectories, the scALAS backbone RMSD did not fluctuate significantly and remained below 3 Å for both substrate models, pointing to the stability of the scALAS-substrate complexes (fig. S2A). However, the scALAS-Gly simulations exhibited much larger deviations toward the end of the trajectories compared with those with sCoA. This increased motion coincides with the conformational heterogeneity observed between protomers in the scALAS-Gly crystal structure (Fig. 3, A and B). The most notable observations are seen in the per-residue root mean square fluctuations (RMSFs). In both complexes, relative fluctuations trend upward toward the enzyme’s C terminus, which constitutes a large portion of the lower lobe (Fig. 3, G and H). This validates the independent rigid-body movement of the lower lobe that was captured in our co-crystal structures. Although the Gate region is located within the C terminus, this motif experiences a smaller change in RMSF values, which is likely due to the local effect of substrate binding within the active sites. The simulations also verify the presence of asymmetry where the two subunits within a biological homodimer differ in their dynamics. Notably, there is an overall lower degree of asymmetry observed between the two scALAS-Gly chains compared with scALAS-sCoA, which is commensurate with sCoA binding to scALAS with positive cooperativity (29). This binding mode is further bolstered by how the Gate shows larger RMSF differences between the scALAS-sCoA chains than the scALAS-Gly chains. Thus, these computational data highlight how eukaryotic ALAS homologs have evolved to feature intersubunit communication throughout catalysis.

Evolution-guided mutations reshape the scALAS conformational equilibrium

The Gate represents a highly conserved structural motif present within the ALAS catalytic core (fig. S3A). Covariance analysis suggests that the Gate co-evolved with the eukaryote-specific Ct-ext. This finding supports a model where the Gate may modulate enzyme activity through homolog-specific mechanisms. To elucidate how the Gate sequence evolved to modulate active site conformation and enzyme function, we engineered two scALAS constructs by substituting the native Gate with the Gates from bacterial rcALAS (scALASB) and human ALAS2 (scALASH) (Fig. 4A and fig. S3A). Analysis of these mutant enzymes via size exclusion chromatography demonstrates similar elution profiles to wild-type (WT) scALAS, which is known to function as an obligate homodimer (fig. S3B). However, thermal stability analysis indicates subtle structural differences between the mutants and the WT (Fig. 4B and fig. S3C). scALASB is the least thermally stable, with an apoenzyme unfolding temperature 1°C lower than WT. This could be attributed to the additional proline residues spanning the scALASB Gate that shift the enzyme toward an open conformation. Conversely, scALASH exhibits a 4°C increase in unfolding temperature, suggesting that the vertebrate Gate stabilizes the catalytic core. The addition of glycine substrate did not alter thermal stability across all enzymes, reinforcing the dynamic conformational sampling observed in co-crystal structures. However, sCoA induced a more solvent-inaccessible conformation, with varying degrees of thermal stabilization. Notably, scALASH showed a more conservative structural transition, with only a 4.5% unfolding temperature increase compared with WT scALAS and scALASB 7.5 and 9% increases, respectively. Trypsin digestion further corroborated these findings, establishing distinct proteolytic protection levels among Gate variants (fig. S3, D to F).

Gate dynamics couple substrate interaction and catalytic efficiency

Previous studies identified mutations in the murine ALAS2 Gate that enhance substrate binding efficiency and catalytic turnover. MD simulations suggested that the most hyperactive variant stabilized an open active site conformation, effectively accelerating ALA product release (41, 42). This intricate relationship among the Gate sequence, active site accessibility, and enzyme activity is evident in scALAS. We identified a correlation between increased enzyme unfolding temperature and decreased activity. Specifically, scALASB demonstrates a twofold increase in the rate of ALA product release compared with WT scALAS, while the activity of scALASH correspondingly declines (Fig. 4C). This higher rate of product release allows for scALASB to maintain comparable catalytic efficiency with the WT enzyme despite its 2.5-fold lower competency to react with glycine and sCoA (Table 2 and fig. S3G). In contrast, scALASH readily reacts with both substrates, similar to WT, but suffers from the lowest catalytic efficiency.

Although the MD simulations did not show large differences in backbone deviations or per-residue RMSF for either scALAS variant compared with WT (fig. S2, B to G), computational modeling identified significant changes in hydrogen bonding between the Gate and substrates (Fig. 4D). For pyridoxyl-glycine, the variant Gates show a higher propensity to interact with the substrate compared to WT scALAS, with scALASB exhibiting the largest change in the hydrogen bond frequency distribution. Similarly, these Gate mutations led to large differences in hydrogen bonding with the sCoA substrate compared to WT scALAS, although there was no significant difference between scALASB and scALASH (fig. S2, H and I). These similar hydrogen bonding dynamics with sCoA are likely due to the presence of additional proline residues in both of the variant Gates that may increase Gate rigidity and interaction with sCoA. Even though the scALASB and scALASH Gates behave in a similar fashion in the presence of the two substrates, the computational observations support the hypothesis that the effect of the Gate dynamics extends past the active site. The evolution-guided mutations can differentially affect global protein conformation, thus shifting substrate interaction and enzyme activity in either direction. Our comprehensive biophysical, biochemical, and computational data establish that the Gate is a motif that can be exploited to disrupt scALAS conformational behavior and thus enzyme function.

scALAS activation can be decoupled from chaperone-mediated unfolding

The previous structure of scALAS in the absence of substrate showed that the conserved T452 in the Gate forms an electrostatic interaction with an arginine in the N-terminal α helix, the same region critical for ClpX binding (Fig. 5A) (28). ClpX (known as MCX1 in yeast), an adenosine triphosphate (ATP)–dependent chaperone, processes clients by unfolding and translocating protein substrates through its hexameric central pore (43–45). ClpX targets the disordered scALAS N terminus, partially unfolding the protein and enabling PLP binding, thereby facilitating holoenzyme formation (44, 45). Thus, we hypothesized that the eukaryotic Gate flexibility may alter ClpX-stimulated PLP incorporation into scALAS.

Pull-down assays confirmed stable scALAS–ClpX protein interactions, independent of Gate mutations, through a distal protein interface (Fig. 5B and fig. S4). Previous research showed that AAA+ enzyme ATP hydrolysis rates depend on protein stability, where substrates that are more resistant to unfolding require more cycles of ATP binding and hydrolysis (46, 47). scALAS interaction does not change the rate of ClpX ATP hydrolysis, potentially because scALAS is only partially unfolded by ClpX (fig. S5A). The ATP hydrolysis rates remain unchanged for each of the Gate variants.

The most notable differences emerge when measuring the rate of PLP incorporation into the scALAS enzymes using either WT ClpX or a ClpX Walker B mutant that cannot hydrolyze ATP (ClpXE206Q; Fig. 5C and fig. S5, B to E). Although ClpX significantly accelerates pyridoxyl-lysine formation in WT scALAS and scALASH, the scALASB variant binds PLP at comparable rates with and without chaperone, effectively eliminating the requirement for ClpX-mediated activation in vitro. Among the variants that do rely on ClpX for PLP incorporation, only the scALASH variant required active ClpX, as there was no significant increase in PLP binding with the inactive ClpXE206Q (Fig. 5C). One interpretation of these data is that ClpX binding alone is sufficient to initiate partial remodeling of WT scALAS, but the ClpX-mediated unfolding coupled to ATP hydrolysis is necessary for optimal PLP incorporation into the more stable scALASH. Overall, the Gate has evolved to modulate scALAS activity through direct substrate interactions and by affecting proximal motifs, like the ALAS Nt-ext, that are involved in allosteric mechanisms.

Production of heme precursors can be tuned without dysregulating heme biosynthesis

Having generated molecular tools to probe the role of Gate conformation on ALAS activity, we sought to determine the relationship between the in vitro phenotypes and cellular metabolism. Thus, we integrated the variants into the endogenous ALAS (HEM1) locus in S. cerevisiae, adding a C-terminal 3×FLAG tag (Fig. 6A). Expression of each protein was verified using an anti-FLAG antibody (fig. S6A). Although a scALAS C-terminal truncation (hem1ΔC, residues 1 to 434) is nonfunctional, ALA supplementation or replacement of the scALAS C terminus with termini containing the Gate mutations of interest (hem1ΔC::WT, hem1ΔC::bac, and hem1ΔC::hum) rescues cell viability (Fig. 6B and fig. S6B). Without exogenous ALA, mutant strains exhibited comparable growth patterns characteristic of S. cerevisiae in fermentable media (Fig. 6B and fig. S6C).

However, quantification of heme precursors showed functional differences that confirmed our in vitro findings. At mid-log phase [optical density at 600 nm (OD600) = 0.5], mutant strains displayed consistent trends in ALA production as measured in vitro, validating precise control of scALAS turnover (Fig. 6C). At stationary phase (OD600 = 1.0), ALA production increased across all strains, while also affirming the scALASH Gate’s reduced enzymatic activity compared with other variants. The metabolic activity of hem1ΔC::WT and hem1ΔC::bac showed minimal variation under standard conditions. However, 5 mM glycine supplementation unveiled significant differences. Despite glycine’s negligible impact on cell growth (fig. S6C), the elevated substrate pool enhanced ALA content in hem1ΔC::bac by 1.4-fold, demonstrating that this strain’s productivity depends primarily on precursor availability. In contrast, other strains reached their maximum metabolic output, highlighting the critical role of scALAS conformational selection in defining in vivo catalytic turnover. We repeated these experiments with variant S. cerevisiae strains in a ClpX deletion background (Δmcx1). Although the overall ALA concentration was lower than in the strains expressing ClpX, the relative trends among the WT and scALASB variant strains remained (fig. S7). These data are consistent with our in vitro studies and highlight the ability of ALAS to slowly bind PLP without a chaperone. The ClpX-deletion strain expressing scALASH, however, showed no change in ALA production, suggesting that Gate conformation and stability predominate over PLP incorporation in determining activity of this variant.

Our work uncovered further insight into the heme synthesis pathway by quantifying protoporphyrin IX (PPIX) accumulation. This porphyrin scaffold is converted to heme by iron insertion via ferrochelatase (HEM15; Fig. 1A). Unexpectedly, increased ALA production did not proportionally increase PPIX levels, suggesting a rate-limiting enzyme within the intermediate heme biosynthesis steps (Fig. 6D and fig. S6, D and E). However, a 5-fold decrease in ALA production in the hem1ΔC::hum strain correlated to 1.5-fold lower PPIX content compared with hem1ΔC::WT, indicating that introduction of the human Gate renders scALAS as a rate-limiting enzyme for PPIX formation. In addition to heme (Fe-PPIX), PPIX can also bind zinc (Zn-PPIX) in conditions of iron limitation (48). We measured Zn-PPIX using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and detected no change or increase in these metabolite levels among the strains (fig. S6, F to H). In addition, there was no change in the expression of select iron- and zinc-responsive genes (fig. S6I) (49–52). These precursor variations did not compromise overall heme synthesis. Even the hem1ΔC::hum strain achieved equivalent heme levels, with or without glycine supplementation (Fig. 6E and fig. S6E). Gene expression analyses of heme biosynthetic genes (HEM1, HEM2, and HEM15) and heme-dependent genes (HAP1, CYT1, and HMG1) identified no significant differences between strains, indicating robust cellular metabolic homeostasis despite altered ALA and PPIX production (Fig. 6F). These results suggest that ferrochelatase is the rate-limiting enzyme for overall S. cerevisiae heme biosynthesis.

DISCUSSION

Enzymes function as molecular transformers, strategically shifting between catalytically distinct states to modulate cellular function with remarkable precision (53). Here, we define the structural transitions associated with eukaryotic heme biosynthesis that can be exploited to fine-tune heme precursor production. Our scALAS co-crystal structures identify a mechanism where catalytic initiation triggers the enzyme’s lower lobe—a complex domain that unites multiple structural motifs, including the active site Gate, N-terminal active site wall, and autoregulatory Ct-ext—to undergo a conformational transformation in relation to the static upper lobe (Fig. 7A). In addition, although the Ct-ext Hinge is unstructured during glycine binding, it stabilizes upon reaction with sCoA. Thus, the Ct-ext acts as a sensor for the state of substrate binding by bridging the enzyme’s static upper lobe with the dynamically shifting lower lobe. Unlike bacterial ALAS, eukaryotic ALAS structural changes involve a larger portion of the enzyme scaffold and induce a unique binding pose for sCoA in the scALAS active site (36, 54).

Gate dynamics evolved to regulate PLP-dependent enzymes in a homolog-specific manner.

Using additional computational, biochemical, and cellular experiments, we determined that these dynamics are critical for modulating enzyme activity via allosteric mechanisms that are recapitulated in vivo. Generally, bacterial ALAS homologs exhibit higher catalytic turnover rates compared with their eukaryotic counterparts, which have evolved mechanisms to tightly regulate ALA synthesis (27, 29, 30, 39, 55, 56). Furthermore, in most eukaryotes, ALAS is also the rate-limiting enzyme in heme biosynthesis. Unusually, under steady-state conditions, scALAS is approximately 50 times more active than human ALAS2, which is likely why S. cerevisiae tightly regulates heme production beyond initial ALA synthesis (27, 29, 57). A sole rate-limiting step for heme biosynthesis has not been established in S. cerevisiae. Genetic overexpression studies identified multiple putative metabolic checkpoints, including HEM2 (PBGS), HEM3 (HMBS), HEM12 (UROD), and HEM13 (CPOX) (57–61). However, by strategically targeting the scALAS Gate, we found that ferrochelatase is the ultimate bottleneck in heme biosynthesis in yeast. Our findings align with the presence of multiple regulatory steps: PPIX content plateaus despite up-regulation of ALA synthesis. Notably, iron chelation into the PPIX scaffold consistently generates equivalent heme quantities, even at lower PPIX levels. Ferrochelatase emerges as the key enzyme for S. cerevisiae heme production, sensing, and adaptation to environmental fluctuations and metabolic intermediates (57, 58, 62). This conclusion raises intriguing questions about the fate of excess metabolic intermediates, providing compelling avenues for future investigation.

The Gate’s potential as a target for genetic manipulation introduces unique possibilities for industrial innovations. Soy leghemoglobin (LegH) production through genetically modified Pichia pastoris exemplifies this potential, with the molecule serving as a critical ingredient in emerging plant-based meat alternatives (63, 64). In this yeast strain, ALAS is rate limiting, and current optimization strategies predominantly focus on reconstructing metabolic pathways to enhance carbon source utilization and precursor availability (65–69). AlphaFold3 structural predictions propose considerable similarities between P. pastoris and S. cerevisiae ALAS Gates, suggesting that targeted mutations could uniformly modulate enzymatic performance across these homologs (70, 71). By strategically combining genetic remodeling with precision protein engineering, researchers can potentially increase LegH production rates on an industrial scale.

These molecular engineering approaches also offer promising therapeutic strategies for addressing complex blood disorders associated with human ALAS2 genetic variations. Dysregulation of ALA production within erythrocytes leads to aberrant heme production associated with incurable blood diseases (1, 23–25, 72). Although gain-of-function XLPP is primarily due to mutations at the ALAS C terminus, loss-of-function XLSA disease loci span the entire enzyme, including the active site Gate (40, 73–75). To date, there is no definitive explanation for how these Gate mutations lead to XLSA. However, our data suggest that variations in this motif affect both protein dynamics and substrate interactions. Both yeast and humans contain an alanine at Gate position 4, whereas R. capsulatus has proline at this position. This residue in human ALAS2, A503, is known to be mutated to valine in certain cases of XLSA (73), suggesting that ALAS2 loss of function may be due to aberrant protein conformation and allostery. Although current clinical interventions are limited in effectiveness, our work demonstrates that targeted Gate mutations can modulate ALAS activity in either direction, thereby opening an avenue to improve treatment options. By precisely adjusting ALAS enzymatic activity, it may be possible to manage heme-related metabolic disorders.

Our research extends beyond ALAS, uncovering insights into the broader class of PLP-dependent enzymes (PDEs), which are ubiquitous and mediate diverse metabolic reactions (76, 77). PDEs are structurally categorized into fold types, with 76% of all PDEs belonging to fold type I (FT-I) that encompasses a wide breadth of aminotransferases, decarboxylases, and enzymes that catalyze α-, β- or γ-eliminations (14, 78). One FT-I subfamily is the AOSs, which catalyze the carbon-carbon bond formation between an amino acid and an acyl-CoA substrate (79–81). Members of this subfamily participate in important metabolic processes, such as sphingolipid biosynthesis, biotin biosynthesis, threonine degradation, and heme biosynthesis (Fig. 7B) (15). Structural alignment of these enzymes highlights active site conservation despite sharing little sequence identity (82, 83). Drug development confronts a substantial challenge: Structural similarities can cause problematic off-target effects (84). Despite this complexity, a fascinating molecular pattern emerges. Each enzyme’s Gate, while divergent in sequence, consistently manifests as a mobile β-hairpin loop positioned near the active site (Fig. 7B) (82, 85–87). The role of the Gate as a mediator of both intra- and intermolecular interactions is well documented, implicating this motif as a fundamental architect of AOS allosteric regulation (36, 88, 89). While active site loops represent a common feature among most PDEs, their varied lengths suggest nuances in protein conformational landscapes. Applying this insight into the importance of Gate dynamics with AOS sequence specificity deepens our understanding of their allosteric mechanisms. This molecular examination opens unexplored opportunities for targeting PDEs in pharmaceutical interventions across multiple metabolic systems.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Molecular cloning of scALAS mutants and ClpX

All primers used for construct design are listed in table S1. The pET28b(+)-His6SUMO-scALASB plasmid was prepared via two rounds of site-directed mutagenesis. WT scALAS (pET28b(+)-His6SUMO-scALAS58–548) was amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with Q5 DNA polymerase (NEB) using partially overlapping primers containing the desired point mutation. Parental DNA was then digested and then transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α cells (NEB). The first point mutant was confirmed by sequencing, and the isolated DNA was used for a second round of site-directed mutagenesis following the same protocol but using primers targeting the second mutation site.

A pET28a(+) His6SUMO-scALASH plasmid was purchased from Twist Bioscience. The scALASH gene fragment was then amplified by PCR to insert Bam HI and Sac I restriction sites at residues 58 and 548, respectively. The gene was subsequently cloned into a modified pET23b(+) vector containing an N-terminal His6SUMO fusion protein.

A yeast ClpX (Mcx1)4–520 gene fragment containing Age I and Not I restriction sites was purchased from Twist Bioscience. The gene fragment was then cloned into a modified pET28b(+) vector containing the His6SUMO tag using the same protocol as described for pET23b(+)-His6SUMO-scALASH above. Following this process, both pET28b(+)-His6SUMO-ClpX4–520-His6 and pET28b(+)-His6SUMO-ClpX4–520(E206Q) were generated by site-directed mutagenesis.

Overexpression and purification of enzymes

The described scALAS constructs were expressed and purified as described previously (29). ClpX (Mcx1) constructs were expressed as described (44). For ClpX and ClpX-E206Q, the initial purification scheme including lysis and the first round of immobilized metal affinity chromatography followed the scALAS procedure. Tag cleavage and overnight dialysis against 25 mM Hepes (pH 8.0), 300 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 10% (v/v) glycerol, and 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) were then followed by a second round of Ni2+–nitrilotriacetic acid (NTA) affinity chromatography. The cleaved protein was then incubated with Hydroxyapatite HT Media (Bio-Rad) for 10 min at 4°C. Protein was eluted with 200 mM potassium phosphate (pH 7.5) and passed through MacroPrep High Q resin (Bio-Rad). The resulting flowthrough was collected and buffer exchanged into 25 mM Hepes (pH 7.6), 300 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 10% (v/v) glycerol, and 0.5 mM TCEP (ClpX Storage Buffer) using a PD-10 desalting column (Cytiva).

For ClpX-His6 (hexahistidine), tag cleavage and overnight dialysis against 25 mM Hepes (pH 8.0), 100 mM KCl, 400 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 10% (v/v) glycerol, and 1 mM DTT were immediately followed by gel-filtration chromatography (HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 200 pg). Collected fractions were then buffer exchanged into ClpX Storage Buffer via diafiltration. After isolation, all purified proteins were concentrated using 30-kDa molecular weight cutoff centrifugal filters (Amicon), flash frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C.

scALAS and substrate co-crystallization

Purified WT scALAS was diluted to a monomer concentration of 7.5 mg ml−1 for glycine co-crystals or a monomer concentration of 5 mg ml−1 for sCoA co-crystals using 25 mM Hepes (pH 7.6), 100 mM KCl, 10% (v/v) glycerol, and 0.5 mM TCEP (scALAS Storage Buffer). The corresponding dilutions were incubated with 50 mM glycine or 1 mM sCoA at room temperature (∼25°C) for 1 hour. The resulting mixtures were filtered using 0.22-μm syringe filters (Cytiva). co-crystals were formed using hanging-drop, vapor diffusion equilibrated at room temperature. scALAS-Gly protein dilution (1 μl) was combined with 2 μl of well condition [0.15 M MgCl2, 0.1 M Hepes (pH 7.5), and 25% (w/v) polyethylene glycol (PEG) 3350]. Fully formed crystals grew after 6 days and were harvested after 3 months. scALAS-sCoA protein dilution (1 μl) was combined with 2 μl of well condition [0.15 M MgCl2, 0.1 M Hepes (pH 7.5), and 22.5% (w/v) PEG 3350]. Fully formed crystals grew after 1 day and were harvested after 15 days. Crystals were briefly cryoprotected in reservoir solution containing 30% (v/v) PEG 400 for scALAS-Gly or 30% (v/v) ethylene glycol for scALAS-sCoA and subsequently flash frozen in liquid nitrogen.

Data collection, structure determination, and refinement

Native diffraction datasets were collected at the 17-ID-1 AMX beamline at the National Synchrotron Light Source II, in collaboration with the Life Sciences Collaborative Access Team (90). X-ray diffraction was set at a wavelength of 0.920105 Å at 100 K using an Eiger 9 M detector. The data were indexed, integrated, and scaled using the XDS program package (91, 92). POINTLESS was used to confirm the space groups of P1 (93). Molecular replacement was performed in PHASER using one subunit of Hem1 apoenzyme (Protein Data Bank 5TXR, chain B) as the search model, yielding a single unique solution per dataset, each containing six molecules (three biological homodimers) in the asymmetric unit (94). Automatic model building and initial refinement were done with ModelCraft (95). Rounds of iterative model building and refinement were performed using Coot and REFMAC5 in the CCP4i2 program suite (96–98). TLS groups used during later stages of refinement were assigned using TLSMD server (99). Model coordinates and structure factors have been deposited in the Protein Data Bank with accession codes 9DZ7 and 9DZO. All structure figures were generated using PyMOL (100). Additional software for model analysis and interpretation was provided by the SBGrid Consortium (101).

MD simulations

All preparation and MD simulations were carried out in the EnzyHTP python package (102). Experimentally derived structures were first prepared through the addition of missing loops with the Modeller python package (103), and the protein was protonated using PDB2PQR (104). Ligands were manually protonated such that no phosphates or carboxylic acids were protonated, and the resulting assigned charges are −3 for PLG, −2 for PLP, and −5 for sCoA, respectively. Next, mutations were deployed to create each enzyme variant. The same AmberMD simulation was performed for each enzyme variant (105). This consisted of the following: (i) an initial minimization with up to 20,000 steps, (ii) a 50-ps heating step that brought the enzyme to 288 K, and (iii) a 2000-ps equilibration run in the NPT ensemble. A total of five 500-ns equilibrium MD trajectories were run, with snapshots recorded every 100 ps for a total of 2000 frames per trajectory and 10,000 frames for each enzyme-substrate combination.

Postanalysis was performed using cpptraj (106). For RMSD calculations, the average location of the positions over the entire trajectory were used as the reference. For data presented in frame order, smoothing was performed such that the value at each point was the average of that value and the previous 99 frames, unless the frame in question was before 99, in which case it was the average of all frames from 0 to that frame. To check for convergence, the total number of nonsolvent hydrogen bonds was calculated at each frame. Each trajectory was divided into five equally sized segments, and the number of nonsolvent hydrogen bonds was averaged within each segment. Outlier segments were identified using the interquartile range test with a multiplier of 1.5. Segments whose averages lay outside of this range were discarded. A Mann-Whitney U statistical test was calculated using frames found in the included segments.

Protein denaturation assays

Thermal denaturation via differential scanning fluorimetry with intrinsic fluorescence (i.e., nanoDSF) was performed using the Prometheus NT.48 instrument (NanoTemper Technologies). scALAS proteins were diluted to a monomer concentration of 1 mg ml−1 using scALAS Storage Buffer. Intrinsic protein fluorescence emission at 330 and 350 nm (λex = 280 nm) were measured with a temperature gradient of 1°C min−1 from 25° to 90°C. To assess the effect of substrate binding on protein unfolding, the protein stocks (1 mg ml−1) were incubated with 50 mM glycine or 200 μM sCoA.

Limited proteolysis was conducted using trypsin. Each protein sample was diluted to a monomer concentration of 0.5 mg ml−1 with 10 mM Hepes (pH 7.6) and 500 mM NaCl. Noted samples were also supplemented with 50 mM glycine or 200 μM sCoA. Trypsin (5 ng μl−1) (JBC Floppy-Choppy kit) was added to start the reactions, which were then incubated at 37°C. Aliquots of each reaction were quenched at 0, 5, 15, and 30 min with 4× Laemmli buffer immediately followed by boiling at 95°C for 5 min. Eight microliters of each sample (2.7 μg of protein) was then analyzed via SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE).

scALAS steady-state kinetic characterization

Enzyme activity was measured by a discontinuous colorimetric activity assay as previous described (29, 107). Final reaction mixtures contained 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), 1 mM DTT, 10 mM MgCl2, 5 μM PLP, various concentrations of glycine and sCoA, and a monomer concentration of 5 μg ml−1 of each enzyme diluted with scALAS Storage Buffer (175-μl total volume). Absorbance was monitored at 553 nm every 15 s for 20 min with a VarioSkan Lux microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for the derivatized samples mixed at a 1:1 ratio with modified Ehrlich’s reagent. The endpoint readout was obtained by converting the peak absorbance values to molar quantities of ALA (extinction coefficient of 60,400 M−1 cm−1). Kinetic parameters (Vmax, KM or Khalf, and kcat) were determined by titrating glycine (0.1 to 50 mM glycine with 200 μM sCoA) or sCoA (10 to 200 μM with 50 mM glycine) into the reaction mixtures and fitting the resulting kinetic curves to Michaelis-Menten or allosteric sigmoidal curves with GraphPad Prism 10.

ClpX-scALAS His6-tag pulldowns

ClpX-scALAS interaction was validated using His6-tag pulldowns, where purified ClpX-His6 or ClpXE206Q-His6 served as the bait and chemically modified scALAS containing noncovalently bound PLP served as the prey. ClpX-His6 and scALAS enzymes were diluted to a monomer concentration of 0.5 mg ml−1 using a wash buffer containing 25 mM Hepes (pH 7.6), 100 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 5 mM imidazole, 10% (v/v) glycerol, and 0.5 mM TCEP. ClpX-His6 (50 μg) was applied to 50 μl of equilibrated Ni2+-NTA resin to immobilize ClpX-His6, and the mixture was incubated at 4°C for 45 min with rotation. Unbound ClpX-His6 was then washed from the resin. scALAS (50 μg) was added to the ClpX-His6-immobilized resin, and the protein complex was allowed to incubate at room temperature for 15 min with rotation. Unbound protein was washed again, and the protein complex was eluted from the Ni2+-NTA resin with wash buffer supplemented with 250 mM imidazole that was incubated for 5 min at room temperature. Gel samples were collected and analyzed via SDS-PAGE.

ClpX adenosine triphosphatase activity assay

ClpX adenosine triphosphatase (ATPase) activity was monitored via a continuous, coupled reaction as described previously (44). Final reaction mixtures contained 15 mM Hepes (pH 7.6), 184 mM KCl, 5.2 mM MgCl2, 6% (v/v) glycerol, 0.3 mM TCEP, 4 mM phosphoenolpyruvate, ± 3.2 mM ATP, 0.7 mM NADH (reduced form of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide), pyruvate kinase (15 U ml−1) (Sigma-Aldrich), lactate dehydrogenase (17.16 U ml−1) (Sigma-Aldrich), 1 μM purified ClpX monomer (0.1667 μM hexamer) diluted with ClpX Storage Buffer, and 0.33 μM purified scALAS monomer (0.1667 μM dimer) diluted with scALAS Storage Buffer. ClpX and scALAS were incubated for 5 min at 30°C before reaction. Prewarmed reaction mix (20 μl) was aliquoted per well of 5× protein stock in a 384-well, clear-bottom microplate. The change in absorbance at 340 nm was monitored for 30 min at 30°C with the VarioSkan Lux. To calculate the rate of ClpX ATPase activity, the initial negative slope that corresponds to the decrease in NADH absorbance was measured and converted to nanomolar quantities of NADH (extinction coefficient of 6220 M−1 cm−1) per minute of reaction per hexamer of ClpX. P values were derived from a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with a Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test.

ClpX-mediated scALAS PLP reconstitution

The acceleration of PLP incorporation by ClpX interaction was determined by monitoring pyridoxyl-lysine fluorescence using the same method detailed by Kardon et al. (44) with the following modifications. Chemically modified scALAS containing noncovalently bound PLP was used for this assay. Final reaction mixes include 5 μM scALAS dimer, 5 μM ClpX hexamer, 25 mM Hepes (pH 7.6), 100 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 10% (v/v) glycerol, 0.5 mM TCEP, 50 μM PLP, an ATP regeneration system [5 mM creatine phosphate and creatine kinase (50 μg ml−1) (Sigma-Aldrich)], and ± 4 mM ATP. The proteins were mixed, and three 25-μl aliquots were dispensed into a black 384-well microplate that was incubated at 30°C for 5 min. A preheated 2× master mix was then dispensed into the microplate after the incubation period. The samples were excited at a wavelength of 420 nm with a 12-nm bandwidth, and emission was measured at 515 nm over 20 min at 15-s intervals using the VarioSkan Lux. The kinetic reads were fit to one-phase association curves using GraphPad Prism, and rate constants were compared for ± ClpX conditions a using one-way ANOVA with a Sidak’s multiple comparisons test.

Yeast growth assays

S. cerevisiae strains used in this study were made in the SEY6210.1 (MATα leu2-3, 112 ura3-52 his3-Δ200 trp1-Δ901 lys2–801 suc2-Δ9) or BY4741 (MATa his3Δ1 leu2Δ0 met15Δ0 ura3Δ0) backgrounds and are listed in table S2 (44, 108–110). Starter liquid cultures were grown from single colonies in YPD [1% (w/v) yeast extract, 2% (w/v) peptone, and 2% (w/v) dextrose] for 1 day at 30°C with shaking at 220 rpm.

For growth assays performed on agar, cells were harvested by centrifugation. The cells were washed twice with water, and suspensions were diluted to OD600 = 1.0 with fresh YPD. A fivefold serial dilution was performed, spotted on YPD agar, and grown for 1 day at 30°C before imaging.

For growth curves, starter cultures were diluted to OD600 = 0.1 with fresh YPD and dispensed as three 200-μl aliquots per biological replicate to a 96-well, clear-bottom microplate. Varying concentrations of glycine (0, 1, 5, or 10 mM) or ALA (0, 50, or 298 μM) were supplemented as indicated. Fresh YPD was also dispensed to surround each culture, and the microplate was covered with a clear lid before being placed in the VarioSkan Lux preheated to 30°C. Plates were orbitally shaken at 480 rpm with low shaking force for 1 min and allowed to rest for 1 min over 20 hours with turbidity measurements taken every 10 min. Each curve represents two biological replicates fit to logistic growth models with GraphPad Prism 10.

Western blotting

To verify the scALAS Gate mutants strains, starter cultures were diluted to OD600 = 5.0 with water, mixed with 10% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid, and lysed via cycles of bead beating and heat incubation in a buffer containing 50 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 6 M urea, 1 mM EDTA, and 1% (w/v) SDS. Whole-cell extracts were mixed with a buffer containing 150 mM tris-HCl (pH 6.8), 6 M urea, 6% (w/v) SDS, 0.01% (w/v) bromophenol blue, 40% (w/v) glycerol, and 10% (v/v) β-mercaptoethanol for SDS-PAGE sample loading. Afterward, proteins were transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane and blocked for 1 hour at room temperature with 20 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, and 0.1% (v/v) Tween 20 (TBST) supplemented with 3% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (blocking buffer). The mouse anti-FLAG M2 (Sigma-Aldrich) and rabbit anti-G6PDH (Sigma-Aldrich) primary antibodies were respectively diluted in blocking buffer at 1:1000 and 1:10,000 ratios and applied to the membrane for 1 hour at room temperature, after which the membrane was washed thrice with TBST. IRDye 800CW goat anti-mouse immunoglobulin G (IgG) (LICOR) and IRDye 680LT goat anti-rabbit IgG (LICOR) secondary antibodies were respectively diluted in blocking buffer at 1:1000 and 1:10,000 ratios and then applied, incubated, and washed in the same manner as the primary antibodies. Fluorescence detection was performed with the LICOR Odyssey CLx imager.

Yeast metabolite quantification

Yeast cultures (10 ml) were grown in YPD to either exponential or stationary phase (OD600 = 0.5 or 1.0, respectively) with 5 mM glycine supplementation at inoculation as indicated. Cell cultures were washed thrice with water, flash frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C for later experimentation.

For ALA quantification, cell pellets were thawed on ice and resuspended with 10 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 10% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid, 25 mM ammonium acetate, and 1 mM EDTA. Cells were then lysed via bead beating followed by centrifugation to pellet protein. The supernatant (240 μl) was used for ALA quantification, which follows the same procedure detailed above for in vitro experimentation. Absorbance was monitored at 553 nm for 20 min, and peak absorbance values were converted to picomoles of ALA by comparison to an ALA standard curve.

Porphyrin was quantified on the basis of a fluorescence assay detailed by Martinez-Guzman et al. (111). The heated and unheated samples were dispensed as two 200-μl aliquots in a black, 96-well microplate and excited at 400 nm. Fluorescence emission spectra were recorded from 450 to 700 nm with a 12-nm bandwidth and 1-nm intervals. Picomoles of heme were calculated by first subtracting the F-660 value of PPIX from that of total porphyrin and then comparing it to a hemin standard curve. Picomoles of PPIX were estimated by calculating the percentage of PPIX F-660 within total porphyrin F-660 and then relating it to picomoles of heme.

Reverse transcription quantitative PCR

Yeast cultures (5 ml) were grown in YPD to exponential phase (OD600 = 0.5), washed thrice with water, and harvested by centrifugation before storage at −80°C. Frozen pellets were lysed by bead beating, and RNA was extracted using RNAeasy spin columns (Qiagen). Purified RNA (1 μg) was treated with DNAse and subsequently reversed transcribed to cDNA using the iScript gDNA Clear cDNA Synthesis kit (Bio-Rad). Each PCR mix (20 μl) contained the following: 1 μl of the cDNA mixture, 1 μl of each primer (500 nM final concentration; table S2), 7 μl of nuclease-free water, and 10 μl of SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad). Reactions were monitored using a Bio-Rad CFX96 Opus Real-Time PCR System. The thermocycling program consisted of an initial polymerase activation at 95°C for 30 s, followed by 35 cycles of DNA denaturation at 95°C for 10 s and annealing/extension at 60°C for 30 s. After PCR, primer specificity was verified via temperature gradient from 65° to 95°C with a ramp rate of 0.5°C/s per step. Quantitative cycle count was derived from standard analysis via CFX Manager software and used to quantify log2(fold change) of gene expression relative to ACT1 expression in 6210.1.

Yeast zinc protoporphyrin analysis

Zinc protoporphyrin (Zn-PPIX) was measured by reverse-phase HPLC with fluorescence detection based on a previous protocol with the following modifications (112). Ten-milliliter yeast cultures were grown in YPD to exponential phase (OD600 = 0.5), washed thrice with water, and harvested by centrifugation before storage at −80°C. Zn-PPIX was extracted via nonacidic extraction with 4.5% (v/v) pyridine in 0.01 M potassium phosphate buffer (pH 5.8). Pellets were resuspended in 300 μl of extraction buffer, and the soluble fraction was isolated for quantification. Where indicated, parallel samples were prepared with extraction buffer supplemented with Zn-PPIX standard (0.2 μg/ml) (Frontier Specialty Chemicals, ZN625-9) to assess recovery and matrix effects. Extracts were analyzed using an Agilent 1260 Infinity II HPLC system equipped with a fluorescence detector. Chromatographic separation was performed on a Sorbax C18 reverse-phase column measuring 4.6 mm by 150 mm with a 5-μm particle size (Agilent Technologies). The mobile phase consisted of 78% methanol and 22% 0.01 M potassium phosphate buffer (pH 5.8) with a flow rate of 1.8 ml/min for 10 min per sample run. Samples were injected at a volume of 20 μl, and Zn-PPIX was detected by fluorescence using an excitation wavelength of 405 nm and an emission wavelength of 600 nm. Zn-PPIX signal in yeast extracts was determined by comparison with the fluorescence response of Zn-PPIX–containing samples.
