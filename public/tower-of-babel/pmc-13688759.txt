Single-cell profiling defines cellular ecosystems and malignant cell state coupling across pan-cancer tumor microenvironments
Authors: Xiaoxiao Yang, Yang Tong, Yuting Wang, Peng Xie, Xin Li, Jiapei Yuan, Yang Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tumors are embedded within complex microenvironments composed of malignant, immune and stromal cells, yet how coordinated cellular organization shapes tumor biology across cancers remains incompletely understood. Here we integrate single-cell transcriptomic profiles from over two million cells spanning 19 human epithelial cancers to construct a comprehensive pan-cancer atlas of tumor microenvironment (TME) organization. We identify cell subsets selectively enriched in tumors compared with normal tissues and show that their relative abundance is associated with clinical outcomes. By clustering tumors according to their cellular composition, we define eight recurrent cellular ecosystem subtypes that capture coordinated variation in immune and stromal architectures across cancer types. Comparative analyses reveal that these ecosystem states are associated with distinct tumor-intrinsic molecular programs, including differences in signaling pathway activity, transcriptional regulation and copy number alterations. We further uncover subtype-specific patterns of ligand–receptor interactions linking malignant cells with immune and stromal compartments and provide independent spatial support for these interactions using spatial transcriptomic data. Together, this work provides a unified framework for tumor ecosystem stratification and highlights cellular organization as a key dimension of tumor heterogeneity with potential implications for precision cancer therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12943-026-02741-3.

Introduction

Malignant tumors remain a leading cause of morbidity and mortality globally, and the intricate cellular composition of the tumor microenvironment (TME) presents a formidable barrier to effective therapy [1]. The TME is a dynamic ecosystem composed of immune, stromal, and malignant cells, whose interactions influence tumor initiation, progression, metastasis, and therapeutic response. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has emerged as a transformative tool to resolve this heterogeneity, enabling detailed mapping of individual cell states and their functional programs across diverse tumor types. Pan-cancer single-cell studies have delineated major immune and stromal compartments, including T cells, B cells, natural killer (NK) cells, myeloid cells, and mesenchymal populations, providing a foundational understanding of the cellular TME landscape [2–8]. Yet, these studies have predominantly focused on isolated lineages, leaving critical intercellular interactions and the full spectrum of intratumoral heterogeneity underexplored.

A comprehensive understanding of TME heterogeneity, encompassing cellular composition, functional states, spatial organization, and molecular crosstalk, is essential for elucidating mechanisms of immune evasion, therapy resistance, and disease progression. Patient stratification and therapeutic decision-making must therefore consider the coordinated organization of cellular ecosystems, rather than individual cell types alone. Previous pan-cancer stratifications derived from pathological evaluation or bulk genomic analyses have identified conserved TME subtypes [9–12], improving prognostic prediction. However, these approaches have inherent limitations: (1) exclusion of tumors with high immune infiltration due to sample selection thresholds (e.g., <60% tumor nuclei in TCGA cohorts), (2) insufficient cellular resolution, (3) reliance on gene expression deconvolution using pre-defined signatures, which may inaccurately estimate cellular composition, and (4) neglect of rare but functionally significant cell populations. Single-cell studies in liver, gastric, and lung cancers have demonstrated that TME composition-based stratification can refine patient classification and inform therapeutic strategies [13–15]. Nevertheless, a unified pan-cancer TME stratification framework at single-cell resolution remains lacking.

Here, we integrate single-cell transcriptomic datasets encompassing 544 patients across 19 human cancers to construct a comprehensive atlas of the TME. We define 96 immune and stromal cell subsets and 13 malignant cell states, systematically characterize their enrichment in tumor versus normal tissues, and investigate their associations with patient outcomes. By leveraging unsupervised clustering and intercellular network analyses, we identify eight distinct cellular ecosystems, uncovering mechanisms of immune evasion and TME remodeling exploited by cancer cells across diverse tumor contexts. This study provides a high-resolution map of the pan-cancer TME, offers a robust framework for patient stratification, and establishes a foundation for the rational development of targeted and immunotherapeutic interventions.

Results

A Comprehensive single-cell transcriptomic atlas of the tumor microenvironment across diverse epithelial cancer types

To generate a robust pan-cancer single-cell transcriptomic atlas of the tumor microenvironment (TME), we assembled a compendium of 652 samples from 544 patients encompassing 19 distinct epithelial cancer types (Fig. 1A, 1B, and Supplementary Table 1). To ensure an unbiased reconstruction of cellular architecture across cancer types, we performed stringent quality control, doublet removal, and batch-effect correction separately for each cancer type before downstream integration. Following integration, we resolved 13 major cell lineages on the basis of canonical marker expression, including epithelial cells, endothelial cells (ECs), mesenchymal cells (MSCs), B cells, CD4+ T cells, regulatory T (Treg) cells, CD8+ T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells (DCs), mast cells, and neutrophils (Supplementary Figures 1, 2A–D). Batch-correction performance was quantitatively evaluated using multiple complementary metrics (Supplementary Figure 3). Integration achieved effective mixing across patients and studies while preserving cell-type identity (Supplementary Figures 3A–B). Specifically, k-nearest neighbor batch effect test (kBET) rejection rates decreased substantially following integration, local inverse Simpson’s index (LISI) batch scores increased toward the theoretical maximum, and average silhouette width (ASW) analyses demonstrated high cell-type preservation alongside minimal residual batch structure (Supplementary Figure 3 C), indicating successful mitigation of technical variation while retaining biological heterogeneity.

Subsequent dimensionality reduction and unsupervised clustering, excluding epithelial cells, revealed extensive heterogeneity within these major cell lineages, yielding a high-resolution TME atlas comprising 1,362,351 cells. In total, we identified 96 transcriptionally distinct subsets, including 11 endothelial cell subsets (n = 88,830), 14 mesenchymal cell subsets (n = 192,745), 7 B cell subsets (n = 195,359), 29 T and NK cell subsets (n = 603,846), and 35 myeloid cell subsets (n = 281,571) (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Figure 4, and Supplementary Table 2). To evaluate the robustness of the atlas, we performed sensitivity analyses across a range of clustering parameters and dataset compositions (Supplementary Figure 5–7). Cluster stability was evaluated over varying number of principal components and clustering resolutions for each major non-epithelial lineage. The selected parameters consistently resided within regions of local stability, indicating that the identified cell subsets were robust to parameter selection (Supplementary Figure 5–7). We further assess atlas reproducibility using leave-one-dataset-out cross-validation. Most cell subsets were consistently recovered following the exclusion of individual datasets (Supplementary Figure 5–7). Collectively, these analyses demonstrate the robustness and reproducibility of the atlas and support its use as a reference for the characterization of TME cellular heterogeneity across cancer types.

Leveraging this atlas, we next examined variation in TME composition across cancer types, building on prior evidence that anatomical microenvironments exert profound influence on tumor immunity [16]. We observed marked variation in the abundance of major immune and stromal lineages across cancer types (Supplementary Figure 8). Notably, dendritic cells (DCs) were markedly enriched in cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (CSCC), basal cell carcinoma (BCC), and oral cancer (OC), tumors arising in barrier tissues that interface with external environments. B cells exhibited the most dramatic cross-cancer variability, ranging from near absence in BCC to 30.6% of TME cells in stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD). Cancers derived from mucosal tissues, including STAD, colorectal cancer (CRC), esophageal cancer (ESCA), and nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), displayed increased B cell infiltration, consistent with their established roles in mucosal homeostasis, inflammation, and tertiary lymphoid structure (TLS) formation [8]. Similarly, mast cells were abundant in STAD, ESCA, and CRC, further reflecting the complex tissue-specific cellular programs.

Dynamic remodeling of stromal and immune compartments in the tumor microenvironment across cancer types

To characterize the cellular remodeling that distinguishes tumor from non-neoplastic tissues within the TME, we constructed a comprehensive human multi-organ reference atlas encompassing 94 healthy samples from 68 individuals across 16 tissue types and 61 tumor-adjacent normal samples from 59 individuals representing six cancer types (Supplementary Table 4). This dataset comprised 583,744 cells, which segregated into six major cell lineages based on canonical marker expression (Supplementary Figures 9 A, B). Using reference-based label transfer, we annotated TME cell subsets within healthy and adjacent normal tissues and performed comparative analyses across tissue contexts.

We observed substantial variation in major lineage composition across tissue contexts (Supplementary Figure 9 C). For instance, cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (CSCC) and basal cell carcinoma (BCC) displayed increased DC proportions relative corresponding healthy and adjacent skin tissues (Supplementary Figure 9D). In addition, healthy and adjacent mucosal tissues, including nasopharynx, stomach, and intestine, contained relatively high proportions of B cells (Supplementary Figures 9E, F). These findings are consistent with previously reported tissue-associated differences in immune cell composition and highlight substantial variation in TME cellular architecture across anatomical sites [16].

We next performed a high-resolution comparison of TME cell subsets between tumor and normal tissues within each major lineage, revealing pronounced subset-specific differences in abundance (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, chi-square analyses comparing observed and expected lineage frequencies across cancer types and tissue origins identified distinct distribution patterns among stromal and immune populations (Supplementary Figures 10 A, B).

Within endothelial cells (ECs), tumor tissues were consistently enriched for ESM1+ tip-like ECs (EC_01), TYMS+ proliferative tip-like ECs (EC_10), and IGFBP5+ immature ECs (EC_08), while ACKR1+ venous ECs (EC_02), RBP7+ capillary ECs (EC_06), and CCL21+ lymphatic ECs (EC_07) were generally reduced relative to normal tissues across most cancer types. ACKR1+ venous ECs expressed high levels of post-capillary venule markers (POSTN) and leukocyte adhesion molecules (SELE, ICAM1), together with enrichment of antigen-processing-related transcriptional programs and reduced expression of genes associated with extracellular matrix organization (Fig. 2B). In contrast, tip-like and immature EC subsets displayed transcriptional signatures enriched for angiogenesis-related genes, epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and collagen biosynthesis (Fig. 2B).

Notably, ESM1+ tip-like ECs were abundant in both normal kidney and renal carcinoma (RC), whereas prostate and prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD) contained lower proportions of tip-like ECs and higher proportions of venous EC (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Figures 10 A, B). RBP7+ capillary ECs, which were enriched in both pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) and normal pancreas, expressed genes involved in fatty acid metabolism and shared transcriptional features with the lipid-processing endothelial cell (LIPEC) state described by Vincent et al, which has previously been linked to responses to metformin treatment (Fig. 2D) [17, 18]. Similarly, CCL21+ lymphatic ECs were most abundant in CSCC, oral cancer (OC), and normal gingival and tongue tissues, in line with the dense lymphatic networks characteristic of skin and oral mucosal tissues (Supplementary Figure 10 C) [19]. Collectively, these analyses reveal substantial differences in endothelial cell subset composition and transcriptional programs between tumor and normal tissues across cancer types.

Within the mesenchymal compartment, tumor tissues were enriched for multiple cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAF) subsets, including COL11A1+ CAFs (Fb_01), STC1+ CAFs, CXCR4+ CAFs (Fb_07), and CXCL10+ CAFs (Fb_08), whereas normal tissues contained higher proportions of CFD+ progenitor fibroblasts (Fb_02) (Fig. 2A). CFD+ progenitor fibroblasts, marked by PI16 and DPT, resembled pan-tissue PI16+ universal fibroblasts described in steady-state mouse tissues [20] and expressed genes involved in complement activation (CFD, C3, C7), phospholipase activity (PLA2G2A), tissue repair (APOD), and fatty acid metabolism (Fig. 2E, Supplementary Table 2). In contrast, COL11A1+ CAFs displayed elevated expression of genes associated with extracellular matrix organization, cell adhesion, and EMT programs, together with LRRC15, a maker previously reported in TGFβ-responsive myofibroblast-liked CAF populations [21]. STC1+ CAFs exhibited increased expression of glycolytic genes, matrix metalloproteinases (MMP1, MMP3, and MMP10), and cytokine- and chemokine-related genes, resembling transcriptional features reported for inflammatory CAF states in previous studies [22]. CXCR4+ CAFs expressed MHC class II antigen-presentation genes, including CD74, HLA-DRA, HLA-DPA, and HLA-DQA, consistent with the transcriptional characteristics of previously characterized antigen-presenting CAFs [23]. CXCL10+ CAFs exhibited elevated expression of cytokines, chemokines, and interferon-stimulated genes, including CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11, and ISG15. Together, these analyses reveal marked differences in CAF subset composition and transcriptional programs between tumor and normal tissues across cancer types.

CAF subsets also exhibited distinct cancer-type distributions (Supplementary Figure 10 A). COL11A1+ CAFs (Fb_01) were particularly enriched in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), whereas CXCR4+ CAFs (Fb_07) were preferentially represented in lung cancer (LC). STC1+ CAFs were enriched in basal cell carcinoma (BCC) and cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (CSCC), while CXCL14+ fibroblasts (Fb_09) were predominantly observed in gastrointestinal and bladder-associated malignancies, including colorectal cancer (CRC), stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), and bladder cancer (BLCA).

B cells were resolved into seven subsets: CCR7+CD38-CD27- naïve B cells (Bn: B_03_TCL1A), CCR7+CD38-CD27+TNFRSF13B+ memory B cells (Bm: B_02_GPR183, B_04_CREM, B_06_FCRL4), CCR7-CD38+CD27+PRDM1- germinal center (GC) B cells (Bgc: B_05_RGS13, B_07_UBE2C), and CCR7-CD38+CD27+PRDM1+ plasma cells (B_01_MZB1) (Supplementary Figure 4 C, Supplementary Figure 11 A, Supplementary Table 2). Tumors were enriched for GPR183+ memory and TCL1A+ naïve B cells (Fig. 2A). The FCRL4+ B_06 subset exhibited features of exhausted or atypical B cells, consistent with tumor-associated atypical B cells (TAABs) described by Ma et al [6]. and Yang et al [8]., characterized by elevated expression of immune checkpoint molecules (PDCD1, ENTPD1, LAG3, HAVCR2), inhibitory receptors (FCRL4), and homing receptors (CXCR3).

Across T and NK cells, tumors were enriched for CXCL13+ T follicular helper (Tfh) cells (CD4T_03), CD4+/CD8+ stress-response T cells (Tisg: CD4T_07, CD4T_12, CD8T_07), FOXP3+CD177+ activated Treg cells (CD4T_10), and CXCL13+ CD8+ exhausted T cells (Tex: CD8T_02). In contrast, CCL20+ Th17 cells (CD4T_06) and ZNF683+/KLF2+ CD8+ tissue-resident memory T cells (Trm: CD8T_03, CD8T_06) predominated in normal tissues (Fig. 2A). Activated Tregs expressed FOXP3, CTLA4, TNFRSF4, TNFRSF9, together with glycolytic genes (Supplementary Figure 4D, Supplementary Figure 11B; Supplementary Table 2). CXCL13+ Tfh and CD8+ Tex cells expressed CXCL13, PDCD1, ICOS, CTLA4, BTLA, consistent with transcriptional profiles previously reported for chronically stimulated or exhausted T cell subset (Supplementary Figures 4D, E; Supplementary Figure 11 C; Supplementary Table 2). NK cells segregated into CD56brightCD16lo (NK_01, NK_03) and CD56dimCD16hi (NK_02, NK_04) subsets (Supplementary Figure 4 F, Supplementary Table 2) [7]. CD56brightCD16lo NK cells preferentially expressed genes previously associated with immunoregulatory NK-cell subset, whereas CD56dimCD16hi NK cells expressed genes characteristic of cytotoxic NK-cell populations (Supplementary Figure 11D). Tumors showed elevated FCGR3A+ NK cells (NK_02), while SH2D1B+ NK cells (NK_03, NK_04) were more abundant in normal tissues (Fig. 2A).

Myeloid cells in tumors were enriched for CCR7+ DCs (DC_03), CXCL10+ and CCL20+ macrophages (Mph_02, Mph_04), and TXNIP+, RGCC+, CXCL10+ neutrophils (Neu_01, Neu_03, Neu_04), whereas PLD4+ macrophages and CXCL3+ neutrophils were relatively more abundant in normal tissues (Fig. 2A). CCR7+ DCs expressed genes characteristic of previously described mregDC populations, including CCR7, LAMP3, FSCN1, CCL19, and CCL22, together with transcriptional features associated with regulatory dendritic cell state (Supplementary Figure 4G, Supplementary Figure 11E, Supplementary Table 2) [24, 25]. CXCL10+ macrophages exhibited elevated expression of interferon-responsive genes, including CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11, ISG15, resembling the interferon-responsive macrophage state described in recent studies [26]. In contrast, CCL20+ macrophages expressed CCL20, SPP1, IL1B, CXCL2, CXCL3, CXCL5, and CXCL8, closely matching the transcriptional feature of inflammatory SPP1+ macrophages reported in recent macrophage atlases [26]. PLD4+ macrophages expressed C3, PLD4, CCL3L1, CCL4L2, CCL3, CCL4, and CX3CR1, consistent with previously described antigen-presenting inflammatory macrophage populations (Supplementary Figure 4H, Supplementary Figure 11 F, Supplementary Table 2) [26].

Macrophage also exhibited distinct cancer-type distributions (Supplementary Figure 10 A). CXCL10+ macrophages (Mph_02) were broadly distributed but showed enrichment in nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), whereas CCL20+ macrophages (Mph_04) were preferentially represented in stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD) and basal cell carcinoma (BCC). In addition, FABP4+ macrophages (Mph_09) and several related macrophage subsets were preferentially enriched in lung cancer, consistent with their transcriptional similarity to resident alveolar macrophages.

Among neutrophils, TXNIP+ neutrophils displayed transcriptional signatures enriched for oxidative stress and inflammatory response genes, whereas RGCC+ neutrophils exhibited gene expression patterns resembling neutrophil populations previously associated with neutrophil extracellular trap formation [27]. CXCL10+ neutrophils were characterized by increased expression of genes encoding azurophil granules, secretory vesicles, and antigen-processing and antigen-presentation pathways. In contrast, CXCL3+ neutrophils, which were enriched in normal tissues, exhibited transcriptional signatures associated with neutrophil activation and chemotaxis, including elevated expression of CXCL2 and CXCL3 (Supplementary Figure 4I, Supplementary Figure 11G, Supplementary Table 2).

In addition, proliferative STMN1+ cell subsets were consistently elevated cancer, stromal, and immune populations, reflecting enhanced proliferative states in tumors (Fig. 2A). Collectively, these results delineate extensive, lineage-specific remodeling of stromal and immune cell subsets across cancers and document substantial cancer-type-dependent differences in cellular composition and transcriptional programs.

Deciphering the clinical impact of tumor microenvironment cell subsets

The tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a pivotal role in cancer progression, with malignant cells actively remodeling their surroundings and giving rise to diverse cellular landscape that influence patient outcomes. To systematically investigate the prognostic relevance of TME cell subsets, we applied the Scissor algorithm [28] to integrate scRNA-seq data with bulk RNA-seq profiles and overall survival information from the TCGA cohort (Supplementary Figure 12 A). This integrative strategy enabled the categorization of TME subsets into favorable or adverse prognostic groups based on their associations with overall survival across multiple cancer types.

Our pan-cancer analysis revealed striking heterogeneity in the biological and clinical functions of TME subsets (Fig. 3A). Several subsets, including ACKR1+ venous ECs, CXCR4+ antigen-presenting CAFs, and PLD4+ macrophages, were consistently associated with prolonged overall survival, suggesting protective roles. In contrast, COL11A1+ myofibroblast-liked CAFs, CCR7+ mregDCs, and CCL20+ M2-like macrophages correlated with reduced overall survival, indicative of tumor-promoting activities. Notably, prognostic tendencies varied by cancer type: for example, ACKR1+ venous ECs, generally associated with favorable outcomes, were linked to poorer survival specifically in renal carcinoma (RC), underscoring the context-dependent nature of TME interactions.

To validate these findings, we analyzed an independent cohort of 11,487 epithelial tumors from the Prediction of Cancer Outcomes using Genomic Profiles (PRECOG) database (Supplementary Figure 12B). Prognostic associations were highly concordant between TCGA and PRECOG, with ACKR1+ venous ECs consistently emerging as an adverse prognostic factor in RC. We further observed that normal tissue-enriched subsets, such as RBP7+ capillary ECs and CFD+ progenitor fibroblasts, predicted favorable outcomes, whereas tumor-enriched subsets, including ESM1+ tip-like and IGFBP5+ immature ECs, were broadly associated with poor survival (Fig. 3A).

We next examined the relationship between TME cell subsets and tumor mutation burden (TMB) across 6,583 TCGA patients (Supplementary Figure 12 C). PLD4+ macrophages showed a significant positive association with higher TMB, potentially reflecting an immunogenic microenvironment that favors anti-tumor immunity, Conversely, CCL20+ M2-like macrophages exhibited a trend toward lower TMB, although this did not reach statistical significance at the pan-cancer level (Supplementary Figure 12D). These patterns suggest that specific TME cell subsets may respond to, or shape the mutational and immunogenic landscape of tumors.

To assess the predictive potential TME cell subsets in immunotherapy response, we analyzed scRNA-seq data from five cohorts comprising 146 samples from 72 patients treated with anti-PD-1/PD-L1/CTLA-4 therapies (Supplementary Table 4, Fig. 3B) [29–34]. Using label transfer to map these datasets onto our TME atlas, we uncovered distinct response-associated patterns. Tumor-enriched adverse subsets, such as ESM1+ tip-like and IGFBP5+ immature ECs, tended to decline in post-treatment responsive tumors in colorectal cancer (CRC) and RC (Fig. 3C, D). In contrast, favorable subsets, including ACKR1+ venous ECs, increased in responsive CRC and RC tumors following treatment (Fig. 3E). Additionally, RBP7+ capillary ECs and CFD+ progenitor fibroblasts were significantly enriched in responsive CRC tumors post-treatment (Supplementary Figures 13 A, B). Adverse subsets such as CCR7+ mregDCs and CCL20+ M2-like macrophages showed reduced abundance in responsive CRC tumors (Fig. 3F, G).

An intriguing observation was that CXCL13+ CD4+ and CD8+ Tex cells generally increased in post-treatment responsive tumors across multiple cancer types, consistent with their established association with effective checkpoint blockade. However, in CRC, CD8+ Tex abundance significantly decreased in post-treatment responders (Supplementary Figure 13E), possibly reflecting a transient surge in anti-tumor activity early in treatment followed by contraction as tumor burden diminishes.

Collectively, these analyses underscore the substantial clinical relevance of TME cell subsets as both prognostic biomarkers and predictors of immunotherapy response. The identification of favorable subsets (e.g., PLD4+ macrophages, RBP7+ capillary ECs) and adverse subsets (e.g., ESM1+ tip-like ECs and CCL20+ M2-like macrophages) provides a powerful framework for patient stratification and therapeutic optimization. The cancer-type-specific and context-dependent effects observed, particularly in RC and CRC, highlight the necessity of precision approaches tailored to the unique TME architecture of individual tumors.

Distinct cellular ecosystem subtypes emerge from coordinated TME composition

To delineate recurrent patterns of cellular organization within the TME, we quantified pairwise correlations among TME cell subset abundances and performed hierarchical clustering. This analysis resolved eight distinct cellular ecosystem subtypes, each comprising 7–15 coordinated cell populations. Lymphoid and stromal compartments exhibited strong negative correlations, consistent with the well-documented association between stromal expansion and reduced immune cell representation (Fig. 4A). These co-occurrence patterns were independently supported by Jaccard index-based similarity analyses across tumor samples (Supplementary Figures 14 A). Patients classified into E1 and E4 exhibited stromal-rich, immune-poor profiles, whereas E3 and E5 were dominated by neutrophils, macrophages, and dendritic cells. In contrast, E2, E6, E7, and E8 were characterized by high lymphocyte abundance and reduced stromal content (Fig. 4b). TME subtype distributions varied markedly across cancer types (Supplementary Figures 14B, C); for example, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) was enriched for E1, E4, and E5, while stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), and colorectal cancer (CRC) were dominated by E6.

To further delineate the cellular architecture defining each cellular ecosystems, we compared the average proportions of key cell subsets. Distinct lineage biases emerged (Supplementary Figure 14D). Immunosuppressive populations, including myofibroblast-liked CAFs (Fb_01) and Treg cells (CD4T_09/10/11/14), were markedly enriched in E4. The E6 subtype contained venous endothelial cells (EC_02, EC_04), capillary endothelial cells (EC_06), CFD+ progenitor fibroblasts (Fb_02), naïve T cells (CD4T_05, CD8T_05), Tfh cells (CD4T_03), Bn cells (B_03), and Bgc cells (BC_05, BC_07), collectively representing hallmark features of tertiary lymphoid structures. E7 was enriched for normal-associated subsets including Bm cells (B_04), CD4+ memory T cells (CD4T_02), CD8+ Trm cells (CD8T_03, CD8T_06), and cDC1 (DC_06). In contrast, E8 aggregated multiple tumor-enriched populations, such as tip-like endothelial cells (EC_01), immature endothelial cells (EC_08), inflammatory CAFs (Fb_09), CD8+ Tex cells (CD8T_02), activated Treg cells (CD4T_10), Tisg cells (CD4T_07, CD8T_07), mregDCs (DC_03), and interferon-responsive macrophages (Mph_02).

We next examined transcriptional programs within major immune lineages across cellular ecosystem subtypes. Among CD8+ T cells, E8 exhibited the highest expression of co-inhibitory receptors and exhaustion-associated markers, accompanied by elevated expression of CD274 and PDCD1LG2 (PD-L1/2) in malignant cells (Supplementary Figure 14E), indicating coordinated checkpoint pathway activation within this ecosystem. Notably, despite enrichment of exhaustion-associated features, E8 CD8+ T cells retained expression of most effector genes, suggesting partial preservation of effector programs. In contrast, CD8+ T cells in E7 displayed generally low expression of cytotoxic genes, indicating incomplete activation, while E6 demonstrated the lowest overall cytotoxicity gene expression (Supplementary Figure 14E).

Distinct patterns of macrophage-associated signaling were also observed across cellular ecosystem subtypes. In E5, coordinated expression of CD47 in malignant cells and SIRPA in macrophages suggested enhanced engagement of the CD47-SIRPA axis. E4 similarly exhibited increased PD-L1/2 and CD47-SIRPA signaling (Supplementary Figure 14E). Moreover, COL11A1+ myofibroblast-liked CAFs enriched in E4 may function as spatial immune barriers by directly engaging CD8+ T cells to induce exhaustion and dysfunction, while concurrently recruiting macrophages to remodel the extracellular matrix and promote tumor invasion [21].

Together, these analyses demonstrate that tumors can be organized into distinct cellular ecosystem subtypes defined by coordinated variation in stromal, immune, and malignant cell states, as well as associated transcriptional and signaling programs. Stratification of tumors according to these ecosystem-level features provides a structured framework for understanding intertumoral heterogeneity within the TME and its functional implications.

Malignant cell transcriptional states are coupled to distinct TME subtypes

Inferred copy-number variation (CNV) profiles from epithelial transcriptomes confirmed pervasive malignant alterations across tumor types (Supplementary Figure 15 A). We next investigated whether tumor-intrinsic genomic alterations contribute to the divergence of TME ecosystems. Comparison of median copy number variation burdens across subtypes revealed distinct genomic landscapes (Supplementary Figures 15B). Notably, deletions of chromosome 3p, harboring genes associated with CD8+ T cell and macrophage infiltration, were significantly less frequent in E5 compared with other subtypes (Supplementary Figure 15 C). In addition, deletion of the 9p21 locus, a hallmark of immunologically “cold” tumors [35], was reduced in E4, whereas amplification of this region was significantly enriched in E8 (Supplementary Figure 15D). These findings suggest that tumor-intrinsic genomic configurations may predispose cancers to adopt, and maintain, distinct TME architectures.

To identify tumor-intrinsic programs that may contribute to the establishment and maintenance of specific TME subtypes, we systematically interrogated oncogenic and tumor suppressor signaling pathways, cytokine expression, and transcription factor activity in malignant cells across subtypes (Fig. 4C–E). The P53 signaling pathway, a key regulator of cellular stress responses and inflammatory restraint, was significantly upregulated in the E1 subtype but markedly suppressed in E8 (Fig. 4C). Loss of P53 function has been implicated in chronic inflammation [36, 37], suggesting that reduced P53 activity in E8 may contribute to its immune state. In contrast, aberrant activation of PI3K signaling, a pathway linked to impaired T cell infiltration, CAF-mediated tumor progression, and immunosuppression by pro-tumoral immune populations [38], emerged as a defining feature of the E4 subtype (Fig. 4C). Notably, E8 also exhibited pronounced activation of JAK-STAT signaling, a hallmark of cytokine-driven immune responses, potentially underpinning the heightened immune activation observed in this ecosystem (Fig. 4C).

Subtype-specific cytokine expression patterns further reinforced these distinctions. BMP2 and BMP4, which promote tumor cell migration, invasion, and metastatic dissemination, were significantly elevated in E1 (Fig. 4D). In contrast, BMP6, previously implicated in the recruitment of anti-tumor immune cells, was enriched in E8 (Fig. 4D). Moreover, a broad spectrum of cytokines capable of activating JAK-STAT signaling, including IL-2, IL-3, IL-4, IL-12, IL-22, IL-27, and type I interferons, was upregulated in E8 (Fig. 4D), suggesting amplification of immune-stimulatory circuits. By comparison, TGFB1, a potent inducer of EMT and immune exclusion, was selectively upregulated in E4 (Fig. 4D), consistent with the immunosuppressive and stromal-rich nature of this subtype.

Analysis of TF activity provided additional mechanistic insight. E8 tumors displayed increased activity of STAT1, STAT2, IRF1, and IRF9, transcriptional regulators known to control PD-L1 expression and to induce the chemokines CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11, thereby shaping T cell recruitment and function (Fig. 4E). Conversely, SOX2 and SOX9, TFs associated with aggressive tumor phenotypes and poor clinical outcomes, were significantly downregulated in E8 (Fig. 4E). Together, these results indicate that coordinated cytokine-TF regulatory programs in malignant cells orchestrate the immune and stromal composition of distinct TME subtypes.

Malignant cell transcriptional states orchestrate tumor microenvironment remodeling

Transcriptional heterogeneity within malignant cells is increasingly recognized as a central determinant of tumor initiation, progression, metastatic dissemination, and therapeutic resistance. Consistent with prior reports, malignant cell clusters in our dataset were largely cancer type- and patient-specific, underscoring substantial intertumoral heterogeneity (Supplementary Figures 16 A, B). To disentangle patient-specific features from intrinsic malignant transcriptional programs and to systematically characterize intratumoral heterogeneity, we applied a previously established computational framework for identifying heterogeneous gene modules (GMs). Specifically, we employed non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) to decompose tumor-specific expression profiles into coherent transcriptional modules. Comparison of gene composition across tumors revealed significant overlap among modules, enabling the identification of recurrent programs across patients.

This approach yielded 13 consensus GMs, including cell cycle, stress, interferon, squamous, ciliated, glandular, hypoxia, sterol, inflammatory, mucosal, EMT, coagulation, and translation, comprising 11 to 326 genes each and annotated by gene set enrichment analysis (Fig. 5A, B; Supplementary Figure 16 C). As expected, a highly recurrent cell-cycle-associated module was recovered across tumors. The remaining 12 modules were largely independent of proliferative activity (Fig. 5A). Notably, the squamous module showed strong concordance with epithelial senescence, whereas the coagulation module closely resembled the classical pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma transcriptional program previously described in pan-cancer analyses (Supplementary Figure 16D) [4], suggesting that these modules may reflect distinct stages of squamous differentiation. The coagulation module was characterized by coordinated expression of carcinoembryonic antigen-related cell adhesion molecules (CEACAM1, CEACAM5, CEACAM6, CEACAM7), galectins (LGALS3, LGALS4), trefoil factor family peptides (TFF1, TFF2), gastrokines (GKN1, GKN2), tetraspanins (TSPAN1, TSPAN8), cytokeratins (KRT8, KRT18, KRT19), and S100 proteins (S100P, S100A10, S100A11), implicating this program in cell-cell adhesion, epithelial organization, and coagulation-related processes.

To quantify module activity at the single-cell resolution, we scored each malignant cell for expression of the consensus GMs and performed dimensionality reduction on the resulting scores across cancer types (Fig. 5C). Malignant cells segregated according to their dominant transcriptional module (Fig. 5D), indicating that individual tumors are organized around dominant transcriptional programs. Co-expression analyses further revealed coordinated activity between select modules. For example, the coagulation module was frequently co-expressed with squamous, stress, and interferon response modules, with a strong positive correlation observed between coagulation and squamous programs (Supplementary Figure 16E).

We next sought to examine how malignant transcriptional states relate to TME ecosystems and observed preferential coupling between specific states and TME subtypes (Fig. 6). Among these, the cycle state was significantly enriched in E8 across most cancer types (Fig. 6A, Supplementary Figure 17 A). Consistent with this observation, cycle scores showed significantly positive correlations with the abundance of TME cell subsets comprising E8 across tumor samples (Fig. 6B). To determine whether this coupling was also reflected in the spatial organization of tumor tissues, we curated and analyzed independent spatial transcriptomics dataset comprising 368 samples from 26 studies. Spatial feature mapping of representative lung cancer (LC) and renal carcinoma (RC) specimens showed that regions with high cycle scores largely overlapped with, or were immediately adjacent to regions exhibiting high E8 activity (Fig. 6C, D). CNV-based identification of malignant regions further indicated that aneuploid areas exhibited the highest cycle scores, whereas E8 activity showed a similar spatial distribution, with peak signals localized within or immediately adjacent to highly proliferative malignant regions (Fig. 6C, D). Quantitative analyses demonstrated significant positive correlations between cycle scores and E8 activity at the spot level, with stronger correlations observed among spots exhibiting higher cycle scores. Consistently, global bivariate Moran’s I analysis showed that regions with elevated cycle scores were preferentially surrounded by neighboring regions with high E8 activity, indicating significant spatial co-occurrence between these two features (Fig. 6E). Similar spatial relationships were observed across the majority of evaluated tumor sections (Supplementary Figure 17B–C), providing orthogonal spatial evidence supporting the coupling between cycle malignant cell states and the E8 ecosystem.

To investigate the molecular interactions underlying these associations, we constructed a cell-cell communication network based on predicted ligand-receptor interactions. These analyses identified a set of putative interactions between cycle-state malignant cells and multiple T-cell subsets that were preferentially enriched within the E8 subtype (Supplementary Figure 18). Among the predicted interactions, several ligand-receptor pairs involved in death receptor signaling were inferred, including FASLG-FAS, TNFSF10-TNFRSF10A, and TNFSF10-TNFRSF10B. Predicted interactions involving inhibitory immune regulatory pathways, including LGALS9-HAVCR2, were also enriched. In addition, chemokine-related interactions, including C3-C3AR1 and CXCL11-CXCR3 were identified, suggesting a diverse repertoire of candidate communication pathways between cycle-state malignant cells and surrounding immune populations.

To provide independent spatial support for these predictions, we analyzed spatial transcriptomic datasets and quantified spatially informed ligand-receptor communication scores using a spatial decay model proposed by Xie et al. [39], which estimates interaction intensity as the collective expression of ligand genes weighted by a negative exponential function of the spatial distance between ligand-expression source cells and receptor-expressing target cells. Spatial feature mapping demonstrated that ligand expression with cycle-state malignant regions was spatially colocalized with receptor expression in adjacent compartments in both lung cancer and renal carcinoma (Fig. 6F, G). Consistent with these observations, spatially informed communication scores were significantly higher in E8 regions across evaluated ligand-receptor pairs (Fig. 6H).

Collectively, these findings indicate that distinct malignant transcriptional states, including proliferative, interferon-responsive, and EMT programs, are preferentially associated with specific TME ecosystem subtypes. The identified ligand-receptor interactions therefore represent candidate communication pathways provide a repertoire for future perturbation-based studies to determine their functional roles in tumor microenvironment organization and remodeling.

Discussion

By integrating unbiased single-cell transcriptomic data from 544 patients spanning 19 epithelial cancer types, we constructed a comprehensive pan-cancer atlas of the tumor microenvironment, resolving 96 reproducible cell subsets across endothelial, mesenchymal, lymphoid, and myeloid compartments. This large-scale integration enabled a systematic dissection of both conserved and context-dependent features of the TME, providing a unified framework to examine how cellular composition, functional states, and malignant programs jointly shape tumor ecosystems. To facilitate interpretation of this framework, we summarized the defining features of the eight identified cellular ecosystems in a conceptual schematic (Figure 7), highlighting their characteristic cellular composition, associated malignant cell transcriptional states, and representative cancer types. This visual summary provides an integrated overview of the recurrent ecosystem architectures identified across cancer types.

Across cancer types, we observed pronounced organ- and tissue-context–dependent differences in the abundance of specific immune populations, particularly B cells, mast cells, and dendritic cells, highlighting the influence of tissue context on TME composition. In parallel, tumor progression was consistently associated with the emergence or expansion of a conserved set of tumor-associated cell subsets, including ESM1+ tip-like and IGFBP5+ immature endothelial cells, COL11A1+ myofibroblast-liked CAFs, CXCL10+ inflammatory and CXCR4+ antigen-presenting CAFs, immunosuppressive regulatory T cells, CXCL13+ Tfh and exhausted CD8+ T cells, interferon-stimulated T cells, CCR7+ migratory regulatory dendritic cells, and CCL20+ M2-like macrophages. The recurrent enrichment of these subsets across diverse epithelial malignancies suggests that tumor evolution converges on shared microenvironmental programs characterized by angiogenesis, stromal remodeling, chronic inflammation, and progressive immune dysfunction.

By integrating bulk transcriptomic and clinical data from TCGA and PRECOG, and publicly available immunotherapy single-cell cohorts, we further identified reproducible associations between multiple TME cell subsets and clinical outcome. In particular, tumor-enriched subsets, including immature and tip-like endothelial cells, myofibroblast-liked CAFs, regulatory dendritic cells, and CCL20+ M2-like macrophages, were consistently associated with less favorable clinical outcomes, while distinct cell subsets also exhibited differential associations with immunotherapy response. These findings highlight the potential clinical relevance of TME architecture beyond individual tumor types and suggest that this pan-cancer TME atlas may provide a complementary framework for understanding inter-patient variability in disease outcome and therapeutic response. However, these associations may be influenced by cancer type, immune context, and other clinical variables, while the available immunotherapy cohorts remain relatively small and heterogeneous. Although several associations were reproducible across independent patient cohorts, larger prospective studies with standardized clinical annotation and comprehensive multivariable analyses will be required to establish their robustness and generalizability.

Importantly, these associations were not uniform across cancer types, underscoring the context-dependent nature of TME biology. Together, these findings support the potential clinical relevance of atlas-defined TME cell subsets while emphasizing that their prognostic significance should be interpreted within individual disease contexts.

Moving beyond individual cell types, we identified eight recurrent cellular ecosystems defined by the coordinated co-occurrence of stromal and immune cell populations within individual tumors. Ecosystem-level analysis revealed distinct patterns of cellular composition, immune regulatory pathway activity, cytokine expression, transcription factor programs, and genomic alterations across tumors. For example, stromal- and myeloid-enriched ecosystems displayed higher predicted activity of inhibitory ligand-receptor pathways, whereas lymphocyte-rich ecosystems exhibited transcriptional signatures consistent with chronic immune activation and T-cell exhaustion. In addition, specific ecosystems were associated with characteristic oncogenic signaling programs and recurrent genomic alterations, including differential activation of the JAK-STAT pathway and copy number variation at immune-relevant loci such as 9p21. These observations suggest that distinct TME ecosystem states are associated with different molecular and cellular features across tumors.

A key aspect of this study is the integration of malignant transcriptional states with TME-based ecosystem stratification. By resolving recurrent malignant gene programs and relating them to TME ecosystems, we identified reproducible associations between specific malignant states and distinct microenvironmental contexts. In particular, proliferative, interferon-responsive, and EMT-associated malignant states were preferentially associated with specific ecosystem subtypes across multiple cancer types. Spatial transcriptomic analyses further demonstrated that cycle-state malignant regions and the corresponding E8 ecosystem signatures exhibit consistent spatial co-localization across independent tumor specimens. Moreover, ligand-receptor analyses identified candidate communication pathways involving apoptosis-related, immune regulatory, and chemokine signaling between cycle-state malignant cells and surrounding immune populations. Integration with spatial transcriptomic data provided orthogonal evidence that ligand-expressing and receptor-expressing cell populations are spatially juxtaposed and exhibit significantly higher spatially informed communication scores. Although these findings support the spatial plausibility of the predicted communication networks, active signaling and downstream functional interactions were not confirmed. Rather, these predicted ligand-receptor interactions provide candidate communication pathways for future mechanistic and perturbation-based experimental investigation.

Together, our results provide an ecosystem-level framework for understanding the coordinated relationships between malignant transcriptional states and microenvironmental cell populations across diverse epithelial cancers. By integrating single-cell transcriptomic, clinical, and spatial transcriptomic data, we identify reproducible associations between malignant cell states, TME ecosystems, and candidate cell-cell communication networks. While these observations provide biological context for tumor ecosystem organization, they remain correlative and require further validation through longitudinal, spatial, and perturbation-based functional studies. In addition, the cross-sectional nature of the datasets limits direct inference of temporal dynamics during tumor evolution and treatment.

In summary, we present a high-resolution pan-cancer atlas of the tumor microenvironment and an ecosystem-level framework that integrates TME composition with malignant transcriptional states and clinical associations. This resource provides a systematic reference for investigating tumor ecosystem organization across cancer types and generates testable hypotheses regarding the interactions between malignant cells and their surrounding microenvironment. We anticipate that this atlas will facilitate future mechanistic studies and support the development of improved strategies for tumor stratification and microenvironment-targeted therapeutic interventions.

Methods

Collection and preprocessing of single-cell RNA-seq datasets

Publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets from human solid epithelial tumors were systematically curated through a literature search of PubMed using the terms “single-cell RNA sequencing” and “cancer”. Studies were included if they (i) profiled primary human epithelial tumors, (ii) provided public access to raw or processed scRNA-seq data, and (iii) were generated using 10x Genomics or Drop-seq platforms, thereby minimizing technical variability arising from heterogeneous chemistries. Datasets derived from non-epithelial malignancies or lacking accessible expression matrices were excluded. Raw gene–cell count matrices were obtained directly from the original publications whenever available. Accession numbers and primary references for all included datasets are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Each sample was processed independently within its corresponding cancer type using Seurat (v4.3.0.1) [40]. Quality control (QC) was performed using total UMI counts (nCount_RNA), the number of detected genes (nFeature_RNA), and the proportion of mitochondrial transcripts (percent.mt). QC thresholds were determined on a dataset-specific basis to accommodate technical variation arising from differences in tissue dissociation protocols, and study design [15, 41, 42]. For each dataset, we adopted the QC thresholds reported in the original study to maintain consistency with published analyses. Putative doublets were identified and removed using DoubletFinder (v2.0.3) [43] with default parameters applied to each sample. To reduce technical noise and dissociation-related artifacts, a curated set of 1,514 genes associated with mitochondrial function, ribosomal proteins, heat-shock response, and tissue dissociation was excluded from downstream analyses [13]. This preprocessing strategy ensured robust integration of heterogeneous scRNA-seq datasets while preserving biologically meaningful variation across tumors.

Batch effect correction, unsupervised clustering and annotation of major cell lineages

Gene expression count matrices were normalized using the Seurat’s LogNormalize method with a scaling factor of 10,000. Highly variable genes (HVGs; n = 2,000) were identified using the variance-stabilizing transformation (vst) approach and retained for downstream integration and clustering analyses. Data were linearly scaled using the ScaleData function, with total UMI counts and the percentage of mitochondrial gene expression regressed out. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the scaled expression matrix using the RunPCA function for each sample, and the resulting PCA embeddings were used for reciprocal PCA (RPCA)-based integration. To mitigate batch effects across studies and patients, each sample was treated as an independent Seurat object and each object therefore represented on integration unit (i.e. one batch) during RPCA-based integration. Shared biological states across samples were identified by constructing integration anchors using Seurat’s anchor-based integration framework. PCA was then performed on the integrated datasets, and the principal components (PCs) covering the highest variance in the dataset were selected based on elbow plots. Cell-cell neighborhood graphs were constructed using the FindNeighbors function, and unsupervised clustering was performed with FindClusters. Two-dimensional visualization of cellular embeddings was generated using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) via the RunUMAP function, applying the same set of selected PCs.

Major cell lineages were annotated based on the expression of established canonical marker genes. Specifically, T lymphocytes were identified by expression of CD3D and CD3E; B lymphocytes by CD79A, MS4A1, and MZB1; myeloid cells by TYROBP, AIF1, and CD68; mesenchymal cells by COL1A1, ACTA2, and RGS5; endothelial cells by PECAM1 and VWF; epithelial cells by EPCAM, KRT18, and KRT19; neuroendocrine cells by CHGA and CHGB; and melanocytes by MLANA. Owing to substantial transcriptional overlap at the global clustering level, T cells and NK cells could not be reliably separated and were therefore grouped into a single T/NK lineage for subsequent analyses. Among these epithelial cells, malignant cells were further distinguished from non-malignant cells by inferring large-scale copy-number variations (CNVs) of each cell using CopyKAT (v.1.0.8) R package as described [44]. To summarize the consensus copy number profile, we calculated the average copy number intensity, defined as the CNV score, for each genomic bin across all malignant cells. Because CopyKAT does not explicitly call copy number events, we applied thresholds of +0.03 and −0.03 to identify amplified and deleted segments, respectively.

Subclustering and annotation of TME cell subsets

To achieve higher-resolution characterization of the tumor microenvironment (TME), we performed a second round of clustering on major cell lineages, excluding epithelial cells, melanocytes, and neuroendocrine cells. For each lineage, cells were extracted and subjected to lineage-specific normalization, highly variable gene selection, batch effect correction, scaling, and dimensionality reduction, following the procedures outlined for the global analysis. Subsets within T/NK and myeloid lineages were annotated based on established canonical markers: CD4 for CD4+ T cells; FOXP3 for regulatory T (Treg) cells; CD8A and CD8B for CD8+ T cells; NKG7 and GNLY for NK cells; CD14 and FCGR3A for monocytes; TYROBP, AIF1, CD68, and CD163 for macrophages; FCER1A and LILRA4 for dendritic cells; KIT for mast cells; and S100A12 and S100A8 for neutrophils. Further sub-clustering of T/NK lineages (CD4+ T cells, Treg cells, CD8+ T cells, NK cells) enabled the identification of 29 distinct functional subsets. Across all lineages, we ultimately resolved 96 transcriptionally and functionally distinct cell subsets, providing a comprehensive atlas of TME heterogeneity.

Evaluation of batch correction performance

To quantitatively assess batch correction performance, we employed three complementary metrics, average silhouette width (ASW) [45], local inverse Simpson’s index (LISI) [46, 47] and k-nearest neighbor batch-effect test (kBET) [48]. Principle component analysis was performed on both the uncorrected and bath-corrected datasets, and the first 20 principal components were used for downstream evaluation. ASW, LISI, and kBET were computed at four levels, including cancer type, study, patient, and cell type, to assess batch mixing while preserving biological variation.

Evaluation of clustering robustness

To quantitatively evaluate the robustness of the TME atlas, we performed two complementary sensitivity analyses to assess clustering stability with respect to parameter selection and dataset composition. For each non-epithelial major lineage, clustering concordance was evaluated by comparing alternative clustering solutions with the reference clustering generated using our selected analysis parameters.

To assess parameter sensitivity, we systematically varied two key clustering parameters. First, the number of PCs used for clustering was derived from 5 to 30 in increments of one while keeping all other parameters fixed. Second, the clustering resolution parameter varied from 0.1 to 1.0 in increments of 0.1 while maintaining the selected number of PCs. This analysis assessed the reproducibility of cell subset identification following the removal of individual datasets.

Differential gene expression analysis and functional signature scoring

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for each cell subset were identified using the FindAllMarkers function in Seurat, applying the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with thresholds set at logfc.threshold = 0.25 and min.pct = 0.1. Curated gene sets representing functional states of major cell lineages and other relevant biological signatures were obtained from the Gene Ontology database (Supplementary Table 3). To quantify the activity of these gene signatures at the single-cell level, we calculated per-cell signature scores using the AUCell R package (v1.12.0) [51], enabling functional profiling of each subset within the tumor microenvironment.

Integration of normal tissue scRNA-seq datasets

To evaluate TME cell subset alterations between tumor and normal tissues, we collected scRNA-seq data from 155 adjacent or healthy normal samples from 127 individuals (Supplementary Table 4). For each major cell lineage (B cells, T&NK cells, myeloid cells, endothelial cells, and mesenchymal cells), lineage-specific cells were first identified from the normal datasets based on unsupervised clustering and canonical marker gene expression. Raw count matrices for each lineage were then processed independently using Seurat, including quality control, normalization, highly variable gene selection, scaling, PCA, and batch-effect correction.

To ensure consistent cell-state annotation between tumor and normal tissues, we adopted a lineage-specific reference mapping strategy. For each lineage, the corresponding pan-cancer TME reference atlas constructed from tumor samples served as the reference. Normal tissue cells were projected onto the corresponding reference atlas using Seurat's anchor-based transfer workflow. Briefly, transfer anchors were identified with FindTransferAnchors (LogNormalize normalization, PCA reduction, 30 dimensions) and transferred to the query datasets using MapQuery, yielding predicted cell-state annotations together with prediction scores that were used to assess mapping confidence.

Cell subsets preference evaluation

To quantify the distribution of cell subsets across tissues or cancer types, we calculated the observed-to-expected (Ro/e) ratio. Expected cell counts for each subset-tissue or subset-cancer combination were derived from a chi-squared test using the epitools R package (v0.5–10.1) [52], following established methods. Subsets with Ro/e > 1 were considered enriched in the corresponding tissue or cancer type, indicating preferential occurrence relative to random expectation.

Association of TME cell subsets with patient survival and tumor mutation burden

To investigate the associations between TME cell subsets and patient outcomes, we applied Scissor R package (v2.0.0) [28] to integrate single-cell transcriptomic profiles with bulk RNA-seq data and clinical phenotypes, including overall survival and tumor mutation burden. Bulk RNA-seq data for primary tumors were obtained from TCGA cohorts, while clinical annotations, survival information, and TMB estimates were curated from previously published studies. Cancer types included in the analysis were restricted to those represented in the single-cell datasets. In addition, gene expression profiles and survival data for epithelial tumors were retrieved from the PRECOG database to provide an independent validation cohort (http://precog.stanford.edu/) [53]. For each major cell lineage, Scissor was used to identify phenotype-associated cells by modeling overall survival with Cox proportional hazards regression and TMB with linear regression. A grid search for the alpha parameter was performed in 2-i/2 with i ranged from 2 to 24 and a cutoff parameter of 0.5. For each patient and cell subset, we summarized the number of Scissor+ cells, defined as cells positively associated with shorter survival or higher TMB, Scissor- cells, and background cells, with a pseudo-count of one to added to each category to ensure numeric stability. Cell subsets contributing fewer than 10 cells in a given cancer type were excluded from downstream analyses.

To quantify associations between individual cell subsets and clinical phenotypes, 2 × 2 contingency tables were constructed comparing the number Scissor+ and Scissor- cells within a given subset to those in all other subsets of the same major cell type. Fisher’s exact test was applied to estimate odds ratios and corresponding p-values, which were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. An odds ratio greater than one indicated a positive association of the cell subset with longer survival or higher TMB, whereas an adds ratio less than one indicated a negative association. To identify robust and consistent associations across cancer types, odds ratios were further integrated using meta-analysis implemented in the meta R package (v7.0). Given the heterogeneity among cancer types, a random-effects model was applied to obtain pooled effect estimates and overall significance values.

Collection and analysis of scRNA-seq datasets from immunotherapy

To investigate the association between TME cell subsets and response to immune checkpoint blockage, we curated additional single-cell RNA-seq datasets derived from immunotherapy-treated tumors. In total, 146 tumor samples from 72 patients across six independent studies and five cancer types were included in this analysis. Detailed accession numbers, study references, and corresponding clinical annotations, including treatment and response information, are provided in Supplementary Table 5. To enable a unified and systematic comparison across studies, we projected cells from these immunotherapy datasets onto our established TME cell atlas using Seurat’s anchor transfer workflow.

Identification of cellular ecosystems and patient stratification

To minimize bias introduced by sparsely represented cell subsets, we excluded TME cell subsets detected in fewer than 25 patients and restricted the analysis to patients with at least 150 profiled cells. For each patient, the relative abundance of each TME cell subset was calculated as the proportion of cells belonging to that subset among all TME cells. To facilitate cross-patient comparisons, the resulting patient-by-cell subset proportion matrix was scaled across cell subsets to obtain relative cellular abundances. Pairwise correlations between scaled cell-subset abundances across patients were computed using the corr.test function. The resulting correlation matrix was subjected to unsupervised hierarchical clustering using the pheatmap R package (v1.0.12) with Ward’s minimum variance method (ward.D) to identify groups of co-varying cell subsets, herein referred to as cellular ecosystems. For each patient, the relative abundances of cell subsets within the same cellular ecosystem were aggregated, and the ecosystem with the highest cumulative abundance was defined as the dominant cellular ecosystem. Each dominant ecosystem was subsequently used to stratify patients into distinct tumor microenvironment subtypes.

Cellular co-occurrence within tumor microenvironment ecosystems

To assess and visualize the co-occurrence patterns among TME cell subsets within each cellular ecosystem, we quantified pairwise similarity using the Jaccard similarity index. For any two cell subsets, the Jaccard index was defined as the ratio of the number of samples in which both subsets exhibited high relative abundance (relative abundance > 0.5 for both cell subsets) to the number of samples in which either subset met this criterion. This metric provided a robust measure of coordinated enrichment across patients.

Inference of oncogenic signaling, cytokine activity, and transcription factor regulatory programs

We inferred the activity of oncogenic and tumor-suppressive signaling pathways, cytokine-mediated signaling, and transcription factor (TF) regulatory programs in malignant cells using established perturbation-informed frameworks. Pathway activity scores were computed with PROGENy (v1.12.0) [54, 55], which models the activity of 14 core signaling pathways based on consensus gene expression responses derived from large-scale perturbation experiments. For each pathway, the top 1,000 most responsive genes were used to estimate pathway activity at the single-cell level.

Cytokine signaling activities were inferred using the CytoSig signature matrix obtained from the data 2intelligence/CytoSig repository [56], and regulatory activity was estimated using the multivariate linear model (MLM) implemented in the decoupleR package (v2.3.3) [57]. Transcription factor activities were similarly inferred using DoRothEA [55, 58], a curated resource of signed TF-target interactions. Only high-confidence interactions (confidence levels A and B) were included to ensure robustness of TF activity estimates, and TF activities were computed using the same MLM framework.

To facilitate cross-sample comparisons, cell-level activity scores were aggregated into pseudo-bulk profiles by calculating the mean activity across all malignant cells within each sample. Differences in pathway, cytokine, and TF activities across TME subtypes were assessed using a one-versus-rest strategy based on ordinary least-squares linear regression implemented in the statsmodels Python package. Statistical significance was evaluated using F-tests, and resulting p-values were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.

Identification of consensus modules underlying intratumoral transcriptional heterogeneity

To characterize transcriptional heterogeneity within malignant cells, gene modules were independently identified for each tumor sample using non-negative matrix factorization (NMF), as previously described [59]. Briefly, NMF was applied to the normalized and centered expression matrix of highly variable genes from cancer cells within each sample, after setting negative values to zero to satisfy non-negativity constraints.

To identify recurrent transcriptional programs across tumors, gene modules were compared across samples using Jaccard similarity. Modules exhibiting at least 20% gene overlap with a minimum of three other modules were retained for downstream analysis. A gene-gene co-occurrence network was then constructed by aggregating module-level gene associations across tumors. Gene-gene connections observed in fewer than seven individual tumor-specific modules were removed, and genes with fewer than seven remaining connections were excluded to reduce spurious associations. The resulting gene-gene network was subsequently clustered to define consensus gene modules shared across tumors.

To ensure biological interpretability, consensus modules were filtered to retain those containing at least ten genes and showing significant enrichment for Gene Ontology terms. Module activity was quantified at the single-cell level by computing normalized expression scores ranging from 0 to 1 for each module. Cellular states were defined by ranking module scores within individual cells, allowing for the assignment of multiple transcriptional states to a single cell when high scores were observed for more than one module. The resulting matrix of module activity scores was used for dimensionality reduction and visualization using UMAP.

Comparative analysis of malignant cell state composition across TME subtypes

To assess differences in the distribution of malignant cell states across TME subtypes, individual cells were classified according to their assigned malignant cell state and corresponding TME subtype. For each malignant cell state, we quantified its relative abundance within each TME subtype and performed statistical comparisons using generalized linear models (GLMs). Specifically, a binomial model with a logit link function was applied, in which the presence or absence of cells from a given TME subtype served as the predictor and the cell state proportion was used as the response variable. Models were weighted by the total number of cells per sample to account for variability in the precision of proportion estimates arising from differences in cellular sampling depth. Statistical significance was evaluated from model-derived p-values, which were subsequently adjusted for multiple hypothesis testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.

To assess coordinated enrichment between malignant cell states and other TME cell subsets, we quantified pairwise associations across tumor samples by calculating correlation coefficients between malignant cell state scores and the relative frequencies of TME cell subsets. This analysis enabled the identification of systematic co-variation patterns suggestive of functional coupling between malignant cell states and specific TME components.

Data collection and deconvolution of spatial transcriptomics datasets

Publicly available human spatial transcriptomic datasets were systematically collected from published studies and public repositories. Details of all included datasets, including accession numbers, tissue types, sample characteristics, and corresponding references, are provided in Supplementary Table 6.

Cell-type deconvolution was performed using the robust cell type decomposition (RCTD) algorithm implemented in the spacexr package (v2.0.0) [60], The pan-cancer single-cell atlas generated in this study served as the reference for deconvolution. To improve computational efficiency while preserving the transcriptional diversity of each population, cells from each annotated subpopulation, including malignant cell states and TME cell subsets, were randomly subsampled to a maximum of 1,500 cells per subpopulation; subpopulations containing fewer than 1,500 cells were retained in their entirety. Raw count matrices from the subsampled single-cell reference and the spatial transcriptomic datasets were used to construct the RCTD object. Deconvolution was performed in full mode, yielding the estimated proportion of each reference cell subpopulation for every spatial spot.

To quantify malignant cell states within spatial transcriptomic data, module scores were calculated for each predefined malignant cell-state gene program using the genes comprising each consensus module. To account for variation in malignant cell content across spots, each module score was multiplied by the estimated malignant cell proportion inferred by RCTD, generating malignant cell-state activity scores for individual spatial spots.

To quantify the activity of TME cellular ecosystems, the estimated malignant cell proportions were first excluded from each spot, and the remaining TME cell subset proportions were renormalized to sum to one. For each predefined ecosystem, the normalized proportions of all constituent TME cell subsets were summed to generate an ecosystem activity score, resulting in eight spatial ecosystem activity matrices corresponding to the eight TME ecosystems identified in the single-cell atlas. These ecosystem activity scores were subsequently used for spatial correlation analyses and evaluation of the spatial organization of tumor ecosystems.

Spatial association analysis between malignant cell states and cellular ecosystems

Spatial relationships between malignant cell states and cellular ecosystems were evaluated using two complementary approaches. First, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between per-spot malignant cell state scores and ecosystem activity scores within each spatial transcriptomic section. To focus the analysis on tumor-associated regions, only malignant-containing spots (defined as spots with an estimated malignant cell proportion > 0.1 based on RCTD deconvolution) were included.

Inference of cell-cell communication from single-cell and spatial transcriptomic data

Putative cell-cell communication networks were inferred from the scRNA-seq atlas using CellPhoneDB [62]. Statistically significant ligand-receptor interactions between annotated cell subsets were identified using the default permutation-based framework implemented in CellPhoneDB. Ligand-receptor pairs with a permutation-derived P value < 0.05 were retained for downstream analyses.

To provide spatial support for the predicted interactions, we further quantified ligand-receptor communication in spatial transcriptomic datasets using a spatially informed communication model adapted from Xie et al [39]. Briefly, ligand and receptor expression was estimated for each spatial spot by integrating spot-level gene expression with RCTD-derived cell-type proportions. Communication intensity for each ligand-receptor pair was then calculated as the distance-weighted product of ligand and receptor expression, using a spatial decay function to model signaling range. Regional communication strength was obtained by summing communication scores across predefined spatial regions or ecosystem-associated spot subsets.
