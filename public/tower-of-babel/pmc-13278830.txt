Developing age-inclusive mindsets in future managers: insights from a classroom intervention
Authors: Maria Eduarda Soares, Chantale Jeanrie, Dinis Macedo, Susana Santos, Bruno Futre, Frederico Caetano, Abílio Zacarias, Paulo Almeida Gonçalves, Michelle Putnam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesThe increased participation of older workers in the labor force has brought about various challenges for managers. This study investigates whether a brief classroom-based intervention may influence management students’ attitudes toward older workers and their willingness to work with them.Research Design and MethodsThe intervention combined feedback on aging-related knowledge with testimonial videos from older workers. A total of 208 first-year undergraduates completed surveys before and immediately after the intervention, with 92 participating again 1 month later.ResultsPaired samples t-tests revealed significant immediate improvements across perceptions of older workers’ adaptability, conscientiousness, generosity, and performance and value, as well as in willingness to work with older workers. Improvements in perceptions of adaptability were particularly salient and most improvements persisted at follow-up. Structural equations modelling further indicated that perceptions of generosity consistently predicted willingness to work with older workers, whereas perceptions of adaptability and conscientiousness did not. Performance and value were significantly associated with willingness in the initial stages but not at follow-up. The model explained 12%–20.5% of the variance in willingness to work with older workers.Discussion and ImplicationsFindings suggest that even brief interventions combining factual information with intergenerational exposure may foster attitudinal shifts that remain evident 1 month later, particularly by enhancing perceptions of adaptability. The results further underscore the importance of addressing not only competence but also affective and relational dimensions of ageism, such as perceptions of generosity.Keywords: Workforce issues, Attitudes about aging, Diversity, Education

In today’s multigenerational workforce, tackling age bias is a growing challenge. This study tested a brief intervention with management students that combined factual information about aging with testimonial videos from older professionals. The intervention led to improvements in students’ perceptions, especially regarding older workers’ adaptability to new situations. The perception of older workers’ generosity was the strongest predictor of students’ willingness to work with them. Since it can be easily integrated into university courses or organizational training, this approach offers educators, managers, and policymakers a relevant action to prepare future leaders, strengthen intergenerational collaboration, and enhance workforce sustainability.

Background and objectives

Ageism is defined as stereotypes, prejudices, and discrimination against people based on their age (World Health Organization, 2021). Although it can apply to both younger and older workers, research has predominantly focused on the latter. Older workers are often victims of stereotypes that portray them as less productive, less competent, less able to learn, and more resistant to change than their younger coworkers (Harris et al., 2017; Posthuma & Campion, 2009; Truxillo et al., 2015). Although research provides little evidence that stereotypes about older workers are generally accurate and shows there is considerable variability among older workers (Ng & Feldman, 2012; Posthuma & Campion, 2009), these stereotypes influence workplace discrimination in processes such as hiring, training, and performance appraisals (Truxillo et al., 2015), and have also been linked to stress and negative mental health outcomes (Bae & Choi, 2023; Brady et al., 2025; Miguel & Pedroso Chaparro, 2025; World Health Organization, 2021).

The participation of older workers in the labor force has increased in recent years, making the issue of ageism particularly pressing. The situation is particularly acute in Portugal, where the participation rate of older workers in the workforce was, in 2011, approximately six times higher than in other European countries (Garcia et al., 2017). A recent study (Vinha, 2025) conducted in Portuguese organizations found that a sample of hiring and recruiting managers held negative stereotypes and discriminated against older candidates. Since ageism can reduce the pool of qualified workers and thereby negatively affect the economy (Anand et al., 2024), it is critical to address biases early–particularly among management students, who will soon occupy decision-making roles in organizations.

In the last decade, a substantial number of studies have analyzed the effect of different types of interventions aimed at reducing stereotypes and developing more positive attitudes toward older workers. These studies show that interventions combining information on aging and intergenerational contact are most effective in reducing stereotypes and discrimination (Apriceno & Levy, 2023; Chonody, 2015; Levy, 2018). However, a major limitation of this body of work is the lack of follow-up assessments beyond the immediate post-intervention stage. Burnes et al. (2019) conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of interventions aimed at reducing aging stereotypes and noted that only 19% of the 63 studies on ageism interventions included more than one post-intervention assessment, leaving open the question of whether effects are sustained over time.

On the other hand, many studies using interventions to reduce ageism have employed extended intergenerational contacts based on the PEACE (Positive Education about Aging and Contact Experiences) model (Lytle & Levy, 2019; Lytle et al., 2021). While valuable, these kinds of interventions are more easily applied in University degrees where interacting with older adults is included in the program requirements (such as health, psychology, and social services programs). They are less feasible in business programs, where students will nonetheless need to collaborate with older workers in their future careers. This gap underscores the importance of designing interventions that can be implemented in business degrees by integrating them within classroom activities. Video-based interventions, although rarely studied, represent a promising tool (Doncel-García et al., 2023), particularly when combined with factual information on aging. Levy et al. (2021) found that even short videos could contribute to reducing ageism and stereotypes both immediately after and 1 week after the intervention. Lytle (2025) compared the effects of different types of interventions and concluded that the combination of videos and written information on aging (using Palmore’s (1988) facts on aging quiz) had the greatest effect.

As such, the purpose of this study is twofold. First, it aims to assess the attitudes of Portuguese management students toward older workers. Second, it develops, administers, and evaluates a brief classroom-compatible intervention combining factual information and videos about aging. Crucially, the study assesses not only the immediate effects of the intervention but also its sustained impact several weeks later. By addressing some limitations of previous research and by focusing on a student population with high organizational relevance, the study aims to advance understanding of how ageism can be effectively reduced in educational settings and, ultimately, prevented in the workplace.

Research design and methods

Data collection and sample

This study adopted a quantitative, exploratory-confirmatory design to examine students’ perceptions of older workers before and after a short educational intervention. Data were collected in three phases: before the intervention (Step 1), immediately after (Step 2), and 1 month later (Step 3).

Participants were first-year undergraduate students enrolled in an “Principles of Management” course at ISEG Lisbon School of Economics and Management. Participation was voluntary and took place during regular class sessions across several groups. A total of 208 students completed Steps 1 and 2, while 92 participated in Step 3. In Steps 1 and 2, 49% of participants are female and 51% are male, and 96% are between 18 and 20 years old. In Step 3, 48% of participants are female and 52% are male, and 95% are between 18 and 20 years old.

Instrument

Three questionnaires were used:

Facts on Aging Quiz , adapted from Breytspraak and Badura (2015) and Palmore (1988). This quiz assesses general knowledge and misconceptions about aging (e.g., “As people grow older, their intelligence declines significantly” and “It is very difficult for older adults to learn new things”). Answer options were T = True and F = False.

Attitudes toward Older Workers Scale by Rego et al. (2017), using the Portuguese version by Vitória et al. (2016). This instrument includes five subscales: Adaptability (e.g., “are less able to adapt to new situations”), Conscientiousness (e.g., “are less willing to take responsibilities at work”), Generosity e.g., “are more willing to help coworkers”), Performance (e.g., “are less productive”), and Value (e.g., “are less valuable to today’s organizations because they are poorly skilled in new technologies”). A 5-point Likert scale was used, where 1 = Strongly Disagree and 5 = Strongly Agree. Most items were negatively formulated, and the few positively formulated items were reverse-coded so that higher scores mean a more negative attitude.

The original questionnaire was developed to be used with managers, whereas our sample consisted of young students with no managerial experience. Therefore, it was uncertain whether the five original dimensions would be replicated in this sample. Factor analysis and discriminant validity analysis showed that this was not the case, as the student sample did not distinguish between the value and the performance dimensions. The value dimension includes items representing the extent to which managers perceive older workers’ characteristics/competences as valuable for organizations, the performance dimension includes items representing how managers view older workers’ in-role performance (Rego et al., 2017). The results suggest that young students tend to associate possessing valuable work-related characteristics/competences with achieving in-role performance. Accordingly, we decided to merge the two dimensions under the label “Performance & Value”. The initial analysis of reliability led to the exclusion of items with poor correlations with the respective subscale (<0.2). Information on the deleted items may be found in Supplementary Material. The Cronbach alphas increased in each step, and in Step 3, they ranged from 0.732 (Generosity) to 0.828 (Performance & Value).

Willingness to Work with Older Workers , developed for this study and conceptualized as behavioral intentions toward older workers, is distinct from components of attitudes such as beliefs and stereotype endorsement. This is in line with prior literature distinguishing behavioral intentions from attitudinal evaluations (e.g., Ajzen, 1991; Brauer, 2024). The items were developed to reflect two key dimensions that shape intergenerational work relationships. First, we considered hierarchical role configurations, since interactions with older workers may occur in multiple relational positions, including situations in which older workers occupy higher-status roles (e.g., supervisor or mentor), peer roles (team member), or lower-status roles (e.g., subordinate). Research on age dynamics suggests that attitudes toward older workers may vary depending on these relational structures (e.g., Finkelstein et al., 2015; North & Fiske, 2015). Second, we considered the demographic composition of the team, since research on diversity indicates that individuals’ perceptions and reactions may differ depending on whether a group represents a numerical majority or minority (e.g., Ellemers & Rink, 2016; Islam & Hewstone, 1993). Accordingly, the scale distinguishes between contexts in which older workers constitute most team members versus only a few members. Appendix 1 in Supplementary Material shows the wording of the six items. A 5-point Likert scale was used, where 1 = Very Uncomfortable and 5 = Very Comfortable. Initial reliability analysis showed that the two items concerning a lower-status role of the older workers (one in a majority context and one in a non-majority context) presented poor correlations with the scale and were therefore excluded. A probable reason for this is that young management students find it unlikely that they will be supervising older workers in the first years of their professional lives. The Cronbach alphas increased in each step, and in Step 3, the alpha was 0.814.

Intervention

First, participants were presented with slides providing the correct answers on the Facts on Aging Quiz, allowing them to assess the answers they had given in Step 1 (before the intervention). Subsequently, they watched three short testimonial videos (each under 7 min) featuring older professionals who shared personal reflections and professional experiences. The older professionals included one top manager, one middle manager, and one first-line manager from, respectively, banking, advertising, and consulting companies. The middle manager was male, the other two were female. These videos were based on semi-structured interview questions written to help demonstrate the vitality, dynamism, and engagement of the older workers. The questions included three main topics: (a) description of their job, what they liked best about it, and the major job challenges/difficulties they faced, (b) description of the main changes age brought to their professional life, and (c) description of the positive and the less positive aspects of working with younger people. No formal group discussion followed the presentation of the videos.

We undertook two comparisons between Step 1 and Step 2—one with all the students who completed these two steps (N = 208) and the other only with the students who completed all three steps (N = 92). In both cases, there was a significant difference for all variables under study (Table 1). Only for “Generosity” is the difference now non-significant. As far as “Willingness” is concerned, there is still a significant difference, with higher willingness in Step 3.

Relationships between attitudes and willingness to work with older workers

To test the relationships between attitudes and willingness to work with older workers, we used the software SmartPLS version 4. Structural equations modeling with partial least squares is a reliable tool for exploratory research, even with small sample sizes (Hair et al., 2022). Data analysis followed the two recommended stages of structural equations modeling: measurement model assessment and structural model assessment.

Measurement model

The assessment of the measurement models includes establishing the reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity of the first-order constructs (Hair et al., 2022). Results of these analyses are summarized in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.

Reliability was assessed with composite reliability indicators, which, in all cases, exceeded the 0.7 threshold. Convergent validity was assessed through bootstrap t-statistics of the items’ standardized loadings, and the average variance extracted (AVE). While the item loadings were significant in all cases (p < 0.05), in Step 1, some of the AVEs were below the 0.5 threshold. Discriminant validity was assessed with factor cross-loadings, Fornell-Larcker criterion, and the Heterotrait-Monotrait Ratios (HTMT). Results showed that items correlated more strongly with their intended constructs than with others, the square roots of AVE were higher than inter-construct correlations, and the upper bound of the 95% confidence interval of HTMT is lower than the threshold of 0.85. Finally, multicollinearity analysis showed that the variance inflation values (VIF) were all clearly below the critical value of 5.

Structural model

To assess the structural model in each of the three steps, we analyzed the significance of the path coefficients, the explanatory power of the model, and the predictive power of the model (Hair et al., 2022). Table 2 depicts the results of these analyses.

As far as the path coefficients are concerned, “Generosity” is the only attitude significantly associated with “Willingness” in all the steps. The sense of the coefficient indicates that a more positive view of the generosity of older workers is associated with a higher willingness to work with them. “Performance & Value” is significantly associated with “Willingness” in Step 1 and Step 2, but not in Step 3. The sense of the coefficient indicates that a more positive view of the performance of older workers is associated with a higher willingness to work with them. The attitudes on “Adaptability” and “Conscientiousness” are not significantly associated with “Willingness” at any step.

Regarding the explanatory power of the model, the percentage of variance explained for willingness to work with older workers (R2) was 12% in Step 1, 20.5% in Step 2, and 15.5% in Step 3. Since Q2 values are greater than 0 in all steps, there is an indication that the model has predictive relevance in all of them (Hair et al., 2022).

Discussion and implications

Overall, the findings of this research indicate that students do not hold overtly negative attitudes toward older workers, with only the “Adaptability” attitude scoring slightly above the mid-point of the scale, and only in Step 1. However, their willingness to work with older workers was moderate, suggesting that, while explicit bias may be low, some ambivalence or subtle prejudice might persist.

The intervention produced positive effects, with significant improvements in attitudes and willingness to interact, immediately after, and one month following its administration. It is noteworthy that “Adaptability” registers the largest improvement.

These results align with previous literature showing that interventions combining factual information and intergenerational contact are effective in reducing age stereotypes (Apriceno & Levy, 2023; Chonody, 2015; Levy, 2018). Notably, the sustained effects obtained 1 month later strengthen the evidence for the durability of such brief intervention, addressing a gap in previous research, which often measured outcomes immediately after the intervention without follow-up.

Structural equations modeling revealed that perceptions of older workers’ generosity were consistently linked to students’ willingness to work with them, while perceptions of adaptability and conscientiousness were never associated with this willingness for intergenerational contact. This pattern suggests that interpersonal warmth (e.g., generosity) may play a more central role than competence-related attitudes (e.g., adaptability and conscientiousness) in shaping students’ willingness to work with older workers. This finding highlights the need for interventions that address not only competence but also affective and relational dimensions of ageism.

The study makes some contributions to the literature on ageism. The results suggest that not all attitudes exert the same influence on willingness to work with older workers and that affect-related attitudes may be more relevant than competence-related attitudes. By focusing on management students in Portugal, a labor market characterized by a high participation rate of older workers (Garcia et al., 2017), this research also adds valuable regional insights. As for practical implications, the study demonstrates the utility of a brief classroom-based intervention, combining factual information and testimonial videos. Moreover, it shows that the effects of these interventions are maintained at the 1-month follow-up. Thus, the study provides some guidance for universities and business schools aiming to foster inclusive attitudes among their students.

However, the study also entails some limitations. It focused only on undergraduate students in the field of economics and management, from a single institution in Lisbon, Portugal. The findings may not generalize to other student populations, disciplines, or cultural contexts. A notable drop in participation at the 1-month follow-up (from 208 to 92) may have introduced bias, as the students more interested or positively disposed toward the topic may have been more likely to respond. On the other hand, the follow-up data were collected in the last week of the semester, and it is not uncommon that class attendance drops in this particular week. So, being present/absent in the class when data were collected may be due to a variety of reasons (e.g., travel schedules to return home or time constraints to finish assignments for other courses) that may have nothing to do with the topic of the study.

The study relied exclusively on quantitative self-report measures, which may be subject to social desirability bias. Incorporating qualitative methods or behavioral observations could offer more in-depth and nuanced insights. While effects were measured 1 month after the intervention, it remains unclear whether these effects will endure in the long-term or translate into actual workplace behavior when students enter the labor market. The explanatory power of the model (R2 values ranging from 12% to 20.5%) indicates that other factors not captured in this study influence the willingness to work with older workers.

Future research should seek to replicate these findings with broader samples, in different cultural and educational settings, incorporate longitudinal designs with extended follow-ups, and examine additional predictors of willingness to work with older workers.
