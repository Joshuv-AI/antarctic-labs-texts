Effect of Age on Short-Term Readmissions Following Transcatheter Edge-to-Edge Repair of the Mitral Valve
Authors: Demetrio Sharp Dimitri, Patrick J. Albertus, Chase Dietz, Caitlin Foster, Danielle Tapp, Benjamin Becerra, Jennifer L. Cook, Raymond C. Givens, Donald R. Lynch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER) is increasingly used in younger and older patients, there is a need to investigate outcomes across all age groups. We investigated age as a risk factor for short-term readmission after transcatheter mitral valve TEER.MethodsWe reviewed the National Readmission Database for 2018–2022 to identify patients undergoing TEER and stratified patients into three age groups (<65, 65–80, and >80 years [years]) to compare odds of 30-day and 90-day overall readmissions and heart failure readmissions using logistic regression.ResultsThirty- and 90-day cohorts included 25,648 and 20,586 persons with overall readmission rates of 14.7% and 27.8%, respectively. Compared to those <65 years, the adjusted 30-day odds ratio was 0.91 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.72–1.16; p = 0.45) for ages 65–80 years and 0.92 (95% CI, 0.72–1.18; p = 0.51) for those >80 years. The adjusted 90-day odds ratio was 0.94 (95% CI, 0.76–1.16; p = 0.58) for those 65–80 years and 0.97 (95% CI, 0.78–1.21; p = 0.80) for those >80 years. There was a significant increase in readmissions at 30 and 90 days due to stroke in the older age group (p = 0.02 and p = 0.01).ConclusionsWith similar event rates between older and younger patients, age was not a significant predictor of readmission following TEER at 30 or 90 days. These findings suggest that TEER may offer benefit across age groups among individuals with moderate to severe mitral regurgitation. The higher proportion of stroke-related readmissions observed among older patients highlights an urgent need for improved risk stratification strategies tailored for this population.

Introduction

Mitral regurgitation (MR) is the most common form of valvular heart disease and occurs in 2% of people in the United States, with prevalence reaching 9% in people aged >75 years.1 In cases of acute and chronic heart failure (HF), the prevalence of MR can reach up to 53%.2 Once MR progresses to moderate to severe stages, it is associated with increased mortality, frequent hospitalizations, decreased quality of life, and increased annual healthcare system costs.3

In patients with moderate to severe MR, the Cardiovascular Outcomes Assessment of the MitraClip (Abbott Vascular, Menlo Park, California) Percutaneous Therapy for Heart Failure Patients With Functional Mitral Regurgitation (COAPT)4 and Endovascular Valve Edge-to-Edge Repair Study (EVEREST) II5 clinical trials demonstrated that transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER) of the mitral valve with MitraClip is a safe and effective therapeutic option for those >70 years with a high surgical risk or heightened fragility. Yet, whether or not these benefits are equal across age groups remains uncertain.6 As an example, surgical intervention was favored over TEER in younger patients (<70 years) in the EVEREST II trial, whereas the Transcatheter Mitral Valve Interventions study found that advanced age was the main reason for denying surgical intervention.7 In real-world populations outside the strict inclusion criteria of clinical trials, retrospective examinations of patient outcomes following TEER have focused on older populations.8, 9, 10 These studies indicate that those >80 years had higher rates of all-cause and HF readmissions compared to those <80 years. Limited investigations into the outcome of patients <65 years have been conducted despite growth in TEER prevalence across all age groups.11 In this study, we investigated whether age is a risk factor for short-term readmission after TEER. In addition, we investigated differences in the cause of readmission following TEER by age.

Methods

Data Source

The National Readmission Database (NRD) was used to review patient information from January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2022. The NRD, part of the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP) managed by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, is a national, all-payer, inpatient-care representative database in the United States. It includes deidentified inpatient discharge data from 30 states, accounting for a majority of the US population and hospitalizations.12 The NRD can track outcomes and readmissions after hospital discharge using a specific patient linkage number and International Classification of Disease, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes for analysis.13 This study was exempt from institutional review board approval as the NRD consists of deidentified patient-level information.

Study Population and Exposure

The study identified all patients undergoing TEER in the NRD, defined by the ICD-10 Code “02UG3JZ.” Thirty- and 90-day cohorts were selected from January 1 to November 30 and from January 1 to September 30 of each year, respectively. All patients who underwent TEER after November 30 and September 30 of each year were excluded, as 30- and 90-day follow-ups would not be possible in each respective cohort. In addition, patients who died within index admission for TEER were excluded from the outcomes analysis. The main exposure was the age at index hospitalization, categorized as <65 years, 65-80 years, and >80 years. All baseline comorbidities were identified by converting ICD-10 diagnostic and procedural codes to the Clinical Classifications Software Refined (CCSR) categories designated by HCUP. As a surrogate for socioeconomic status, two factors were evaluated: the median income of the zip code where a given patient lived and the primary payer at baseline. The study also assessed hospital-level characteristics, including hospital size, teaching status, urban–rural classification, and ownership. Acknowledging previous findings that suggested an association between the volume of TEERs performed at a given hospital and readmissions,14 we assessed the tertile of the annual volume of procedures performed per hospital to adjust for this potential confounder.

Outcomes

The NRD is specifically designed to support analyses of readmission within a calendar year and does not permit longitudinal survival modeling across years. The primary outcome was the first readmission following discharge from index admission for both cohorts through December 31 of each year. The study also assessed readmissions presumed to be due to HF based on the primary diagnosis of readmission. Similar to baseline comorbidities, principal diagnosis on admission was assessed by converting ICD-10 diagnostic codes to CCSR categories. HF readmissions were identified as those with the CCSR category “CIR019” listed as the principal diagnosis on readmission. In addition, we analyzed the principal diagnosis on readmissions by each age group using the CCSR codes, as categorized in Supplemental Table 1.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses adhered to HCUP recommendations for the NRD by incorporating discharge weights, hospital clustering, and stratification variables to account for the complex sampling design. Survey design elements were applied to descriptive analyses, bivariate comparisons, and multivariable regression models to generate nationally representative estimates. Baseline patient characteristics were compared using the Rao–Scott chi-square test for categorical variables and analysis of variance continuous variables. p values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. The association between age and readmission was evaluated for the total population using logistic regression. Three models were created using sequential adjustment for each population to control for potential confounders, based on prior knowledge of the relationships between each covariate and both age and readmissions. The first model was an unadjusted model with age as the predictor variable and readmission for any cause or readmission for HF as the outcome variable. The second model (Model 1) adjusted for sex, median household income of the patient’s zip code, primary payer, index hospitalization parameters (elective procedure, length of stay [LOS], and disposition after index hospitalization), and hospital characteristics. The third model (Model 2) adjusted for baseline comorbidities and all variables in the second model. We also compared the proportion of readmissions with each primary readmission diagnosis by age group at 30 and 90 days following TEER. Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and prior literature rather than automated variable selection procedures. Before model estimation, multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using variance inflation factors, and no evidence of problematic collinearity was identified. Missingness for variables included in the analysis was minimal, consistent with the administrative structure of the NRD, and analyses were conducted using complete cases. All statistical analyses were performed in SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina).

Results

Baseline Demographic, Clinical, and Hospitalization Characteristics

A total of 25,648 and 20,586 weighted patients who underwent TEER were included in the 30- and 90-day analyses, respectively. For the 30-day cohort, 2794 (10.9%) were aged <65 years, 10,549 (41.1%) were aged 65–80 years, and 12,305 (48.0%) were aged >80 years. The proportion of female patients differed among age groups, with 43.7%, 43.0%, and 49.1%, respectively (p < 0.001) (Table 1). The most common valvular disease was nonrheumatic (92.3%). Common comorbidities included hypertension (83.9%), HF (83.8%), dysrhythmia (66.2%), and coronary artery disease (65.0%). Although the frequency of pulmonary heart disease did not differ between age groups, the frequency of all other comorbidities differed. Most patients were in the upper-income half (52.1%), and Medicare was the expected primary payer in 87.4% of the population. For the 30-day cohorts, the mean LOS was 4.8 days (standard error [SE] of the mean, 0.12) and differed between age groups: <65 years (8 days [SE, 0.60]), 65–80 years (4.8 days [SE, 0.13]), and >80 years (4.1 days [SE, 0.11]) (p < 0.001). The overall death rate was 2.2%, and the rate decreased with age. The most frequent disposition following TEER was routine home discharge (70.4%), followed by home health care (18.1%) and transfer to a skilled or intermediate nursing facility (9.0%). There were significant differences in disposition between the age groups (p < 0.01). Most hospitals were private nonprofits (78.5%), and most TEERs were performed in large bed–size hospitals (76.3%). Outcomes

Thirty-Day Outcomes

The overall all-cause 30-day readmission rate for individuals who underwent TEER was 14.7% (n = 3761) (Table 2 and Supplemental Figure 1). The 30-day readmission rates were not significantly different between age groups (p = 0.63). The unadjusted odds ratio (OR) of 30-day readmission indicated no difference in the odds of readmission for the 65–80 years age group (OR, 0.93; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.79–1.10 [p = 0.41]) or the >80 age group (OR, 0.97; 95% CI, 0.83–1.14 [p = 0.71]) compared to the <65 age group (Table 3 and Figure 1). For Model 1, the odds for readmission for the age groups of 65–80 years (OR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.78–1.23 [p = 0.83]) and >80 years (OR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.77–1.24 [p = 0.86]) were not significantly different relative to those aged <65 years. Discussion

In this real-world, nationwide registry study, we aimed to investigate the impact of age on overall readmissions (2018–2022) and 30- and 90-day readmission risk in older populations (>80 years) compared to younger age groups (<65 years, 65–80 years) following TEER. The overall readmission rate was 14.7% at 30 days and 27.8% at 90 days and did not differ by age group. In addition, the overall odds of readmission did not differ by age group after adjusting for potential confounders. Together, this suggests that in comparison to younger populations, older patients are not at an elevated risk for readmission following TEER. These findings highlight the potential benefit of TEER across the age spectrum in individuals with moderate to severe MR and HF.

Considering our findings in relation to the COAPT trial, which evaluated the safety and efficacy of TEER in HF individuals,15 our study demonstrated similar 30-day overall readmission rates (COAPT: 13% vs. NRD: 14.7%). Moreover, cause-specific readmission rates were comparable: cardiovascular disease (6.7% vs. 7.6%, respectively), HF (4.0% vs. 4.5, respectively), stroke (0.7% vs. 0.5%, respectively), and noncardiovascular disease related (6.7% vs. 7.1%, respectively). Although our study demonstrated comparable readmission outcomes to those of COAPT, our findings suggest that TEER is well tolerated in a broader and less-restricted patient population. Other TEER-related clinical trials, including EVEREST II,5 Transcatheter Mitral Valve Interventions,16 and MITRA-FR,17 did not report 30- or 90-day hospitalization outcomes, which limited direct comparisons to our study. Thus, further investigation of long-term, real-world outcomes after TEER is needed.

Previous studies have investigated age-related outcomes in the context of TEER. A notable difference between our study and the work conducted by Nazir et al.,8 who also used NRD data to study TEER outcomes, is that the weighted population estimate of patients who underwent TEER substantially increased from an estimated 6025 in 2014–2016 to an estimated 26,215 in 2018–2022. This increase may be due to greater patient access to the TEER procedure,9 likely born from the impact of the COAPT trial results and the subsequent inclusion of TEER in contemporary HF guidelines.18 In addition, Nazir et al. found that patients aged >80 years had 42.0% higher odds of 30-day all-cause readmissions and 59.0% higher odds of 30-day HF readmissions compared to those aged <80 years; this may be due to increased operator and procedural experience coupled with improved patient selection.19 Although LOS and procedural urgency were included in adjusted models, differences across age groups may reflect underlying illness severity or clinical complexity not fully captured by administrative coding. For example, the fact that the youngest age group in our study had longer LOS on index hospitalization and a higher percentage of nonelective TEER procedures suggests that these patients were sicker at the time of TEER. However, the complexity of their condition may not be fully captured by diagnostic and procedural codes alone.

Our findings are not without limitations. The use of ICD-10 diagnostic codes to define comorbidities may have resulted in the misclassification of patients with respect to each comorbidity due to uncoded or miscoded data at the time of admission. Furthermore, the reliance on ICD-10 codes to select patients who had undergone TEER may have resulted in improper exclusion or inclusion of patients who did or did not respectively receive a TEER due to such coding errors. However, given standardization in the coding of major procedures like TEER, this is likely rare. Furthermore, NRD does not allow differentiation of the etiology of MR (i.e., functional or degenerative) or between HF as a comorbidity and HF as the primary reason for admission. In many cases, HF is coded as the principal admission diagnosis irrespective of disease severity or chronicity. Furthermore, results from NRD studies may not be applicable to healthcare systems outside the United States due to fundamental differences in health care organization, payment structures, and patient populations that could significantly affect readmission patterns and outcomes. Our assessment of solely 30- and 90-day outcomes limited our ability to draw conclusions on long-term outcomes and survival. Out-of-hospital death information was unavailable and may be a competing event for first readmission. Given that in-hospital deaths during the index admissions were higher in those <65 years, our effect estimates may be biased in a way that makes the result appear less significant. In addition, the use of logistic regression to evaluate fixed 30- and 90-day outcomes does not capture the exact timing of readmissions or account for recurrent events. However, this approach is consistent with the structure of the NRD, which is designed for analyses of readmission within defined time windows.

In summary, our study found that age did not have a significant impact on overall readmissions following TEER. These findings highlight the potential benefit of TEER across the age spectrum in individuals with symptomatic moderate to severe MR. Studies leveraging data gathered from institutional clinical registries may provide more granular insight on the impact of metrics of TEER efficacy, such as residual MR and mitral valve hemodynamics following intervention, on long-term outcomes in real-world populations. The observed higher proportion of stroke-related readmissions among older patients underscores the need for improved risk stratification approaches in this vulnerable population. Future studies are needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms driving this association and identify strategies to address cerebrovascular risk factors.
