Routine care practices for stable lung allograft recipients beyond the initial post-transplant year: An international survey study
Authors: Alexandra H. Toporek, Whitney D. Gannon, Michael P. Croglio, Daniel Motta-Calderon, Alexander G. Dragnich, Kaitlyn C. Chapin, Konrad Hoetzenecker, David B. Erasmus, Anil J. Trindade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The pragmatic aspects of routine lung transplant recipient management are poorly described. We performed an international survey study to characterize management of stable lung transplant recipients who are beyond the initial post-transplant year.We obtained survey responses from 72 distinct lung transplant programs in 15 countries. Stable patients are monitored a median of every 3-months (IQR 3–3.25) in the ambulatory setting. Visits are accompanied by diagnostic laboratory assessments, spirometry, and chest X-ray imaging. Most centers perform between-clinic surveillance every 1-month (IQR 1–1.5) with diagnostic laboratory testing. We identified the frequency of monitoring of donor-specific antibodies [median 6 months (IQR 3–6)], chest computed tomography [median 12 months (IQR 12–12)], and other ancillary testing. A majority of stable patients are managed on a maintenance immunosuppression regimen of tacrolimus (preferred goal trough 8–10 ng/ml), mycophenolate, and prednisone (preferred dose of 5 mg daily). Dose reduction of immunosuppression occurs for cytopenias, recurrent infection, malignancy, and short telomere syndromes at most centers. Methodologies and frequencies for malignancy screening are described.While routine care of lung transplant recipients is heterogeneous, there are potential areas of consensus in practice patterns.

There is lack of consensus regarding the management of stable [without Acute Lung Allograft Dysfunction (ALAD) or Chronic Lung Allograft Dysfunction (CLAD)] lung transplant recipients in the ambulatory setting.1, 2 Absence of consistent care between centers may hinder identifying best practices, which may impact patient survival. Furthermore, lack of standards may affect third-party reimbursement, clinical trial design, and have medico-legal ramifications.3, 4, 5 We conducted a survey on the management of stable lung transplant recipients who are beyond the initial post-transplant year to better understand where variability exists.

Methods

We performed an international survey of care practices for lung transplant recipients beyond their initial year post-transplant. The study received Institutional Review Board approval (#250783). The survey (Supplemental Figure 1) was created using a modified Delphi method and adhered to Consensus-based Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies guidelines.6 Two authors (AHT, AJT) generated survey questions while DBE, AGD, and KCC evaluated them for interpretation, clarity, reliability, validity, and user experience.4 The finalized survey was distributed to lung transplant medical directors within the United States (US) and advertised on the International Society of Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT) Connect Forum between July 28, 2025 and September 1, 2025. Only one institutional response was included in the analysis, prioritizing answers from senior staff. Results were stratified by center volume and country. Between-group comparisons were performed using Kruskal-Wallis rank testing, Wilcoxon rank-sum and Fisher exact tests, where appropriate.

Results

Seventy-two survey responses were included in the analysis after excluding duplicates (9) and pediatric centers (4). Fifty-one (71%) responses were from US centers [representing 74% of the 69 US adult lung programs] and 21 (29%) were from non-US centers (Canada = 3, Europe = 11, Middle East/Asia = 2, Australia = 2). Annual transplant volume was a median 55 (IQR 25–75); 21 (28%) centers were categorized as high-volume (>75 transplants/year), 31 (43%) were moderate-sized (25–75 transplants/year), and 20 (28%) were lower-volume (<25 transplants/year).

Routine ambulatory care occurred at a median frequency of 3 months (IQR 3–3.25). In-person visits were preferred at 63 (88%) centers; only 12% provided telehealth options. Ambulatory testing at a majority (>50%) of centers is described (Figure 1A). Larger centers scheduled routine visits less frequently than smaller sized programs [median 3 (IQR 3–6) vs. 3 (IQR 3–3), p < 0.05]. Discussion

We performed an international survey study to quantify variability of routine care between centers and identified areas where consensus exists (maintenance immunosuppression, frequency of testing), and areas where it can be improved (cancer surveillance).

Management of lung transplant recipients varies widely between centers for a myriad of theoretical reasons including limited evidence to guide care, limited applicability of existing evidence to specialized populations, provider bias, differences in resources to oversee aspects of care, payor structures, and patient access to healthcare. Identifying heterogeneity is an important step to building consensus. Managing lung transplant recipients using consensus expert opinion is not novel; consensus guidelines regarding the diagnosis of primary graft dysfunction, acute cellular rejection, antibody mediated rejection, and chronic lung allograft dysfunction have been pivotal to marshal further work towards clarifying disease pathology and identifying targeted therapies.7, 8, 9 Therefore, it is plausible that a unified approach to managing stable lung transplant recipients may facilitate quality improvement and outcomes optimization.10 For example, we identified that larger centers schedule routine ambulatory visits slightly less frequently than smaller centers, though perform more frequent in-between visit laboratory monitoring. While ostensibly driven by resource availability, it provides an opportunity to analyze the impact of frequency of clinic visits on outcomes. Similarly, heterogeneity in assessing thyroid function, immunoglobulin G, EKGs, transthoracic echocardiograms, CT chest, and surveillance bronchoscopies provides opportunities to assess the importance of these studies in screening for CLAD and long-term extrapulmonary organ function. Furthermore, while we identified median trough goals for tacrolimus in stable patients beyond the initial year post-transplant, the range of trough goals was broad; this suggests a need to study ideal tacrolimus trough targets to optimize the balance of preserving allograft function while minimizing side effects. In our study, areas of greatest variability seemed to occur where there is little evidence to guide practice. Etiologies of practice pattern variation warrant further detailed investigation.

Strengths of this study are its large sample size and diversity of respondents. There are several limitations to our study. Responses were largely skewed towards North American centers despite attempts to solicit survey invitations on the international ISHLT Connect Forum site. Moreover, international respondents were more likely to be from academic programs that are well connected to the ISHLT resources, which may skew responses. Global practices may be determined by government regulations, available healthcare resources, and other local influences; we did not have the opportunity to explore this in more detail. As is true with most survey studies, questions may be open to multiple interpretations.11 Respondents may be misinformed about practices at their own center and introduce response bias.12 By design, our survey questions were limited in granularity to mitigate survey fatigue; this may limit conclusions that can be drawn from this work.13 This is particularly true when trying to understand nuanced drivers for center-specific management practices. Furthermore, while we identified that practice variations exist, we are unable to explain why. Findings may not be applicable to specialized sub-populations such as recipients of combined organ transplants. Finally, we chose to focus on management practices beyond the initial post-transplant year, since care during the initial year is more nuanced.

In this survey study of routine management practices for stable lung transplant recipients, we demonstrate that while between-center variation in care practices exist, areas of consensus are possible. Understanding the root of variation is an important next step, as center-specific differences may result from constraints in local healthcare delivery systems, as opposed to a belief that one methodology is superior to another. The desired result of this work is the design and implementation of clinical trials to study center-specific management differences rooted in true clinical equipoise. The datasets from this study provide a robust basis to guide further investigation into the long-term clinical impact of variation in maintenance clinical care for lung transplant recipients.
