High‐dose intranasal insulin in an adaptive dose‐escalation study in healthy human participants
Authors: Florian Schmitzberger, Jennifer Fowler, Cindy H. Hsu, Manjunath P. Pai, Robert W. Neumar, William J. Meurer, Robert Silbergleit
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntranasal insulin is a putative neuroprotective therapy after cardiac arrest, but safety in humans at doses extrapolated from animal models is unknown. This phase I, open‐label adaptive dose‐escalation study explores the maximum tolerated dose of intranasal insulin in healthy human participants. Placebo or insulin at doses from 0 to 1000 units was given to healthy participants intranasally on repeated weekly visits. Serum glucose, insulin, and C‐peptide levels were measured serially at 0, 15, 30, 60, 120, 180, and 240 min after administration. Twenty‐four participants (12 female, median age 53, IQR 35–61) were enrolled. There was minimal change in average serum glucose after administration of intranasal insulin. Average serum insulin increased slightly in a dose‐dependent manner, reaching maximum concentrations at 15 min. C‐peptide decreased over time from administration in all groups. One participant had severe hypoglycemia (24 mg/dL at 45 min) and a different participant had mild hypoglycemia (51 mg/dL at 30 min), both after receiving 600 U intranasal insulin. Hypoglycemic episodes were associated with increases in serum insulin. Both participants continued in the study without hypoglycemia after additional doses. High‐dose intranasal insulin up to 1000 U was generally well tolerated, with minimal measurable systemic absorption and without significant aggregate changes in mean glucose. Idiosyncratic episodic systemic absorption and hypoglycemia require further study and additional caution in potential clinical application. Further study of its target engagement and efficacy as a neuroprotective therapy after cardiac arrest at these doses is warranted.

INTRODUCTION

Only 9% of the 146,924 victims of emergency medical services (EMS) treated out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) in the US in 2021 in the Cardiac Arrest Registry to Enhance Survival (CARES) registry survived hospital discharge. 1 Among those initially successfully resuscitated, most die a neurological death from ischemic brain injury in the hospital. 2 Some pharmacologic therapies have shown neuroprotective properties in animal models; none have been successfully translated to clinical cardiac arrest care. 3 One reason for this failure to translate is that neuroprotective therapies are typically most effective if given very early after the onset of ischemia or reperfusion, which can be difficult to accomplish in the prehospital clinical setting. 4 Intranasal administration of insulin is a putative neuroprotective therapy for acute ischemic brain injury after cardiac arrest that could potentially be easily administered very rapidly during or immediately after resuscitation from cardiac arrest by lay or professional rescuers. 5 , 6 Insulin administered intranasally rapidly enters the central nervous system through an unusual direct‐nose‐to‐brain transport mechanism with minimal systemic circulatory absorption. 7 Intranasal administration of insulin has been tested in several human trials of other neurodegenerative disorders but not in patients with ischemic brain injury. 8 The neuroprotective effects of insulin are well established in animal models of acute brain ischemia, but preclinical experiments extrapolate to doses of intranasal insulin that exceed those already shown to be safe in previous human trials. 9 , 10 Therefore, we explored, in healthy participants, the safety of intranasal insulin within a range of doses potentially required to treat survivors of cardiac arrest, based on available preclinical data. Although there is a lack of good pharmacokinetic models to allow scaling of intranasal insulin dosing from animal models to humans, a substantial body of literature describes preclinical models of neurological injury demonstrating efficacy, but almost exclusively at much higher IN doses (typically 15 to 40 u/kg) than previously used in humans. We hypothesized that high‐dose intranasal insulin can be safely delivered to healthy human participants and sought to determine a maximum tolerated dose of intranasal insulin in humans.

METHODS

This study was approved by the University of Michigan institutional review board (HUM00171279). All protocols were approved by the US Food and Drug Administration under an investigational new drug application. All participants were informed of the purpose and nature of the study, and their written informed consent was obtained before any procedures were performed as part of this study. The consent form (S1) and study protocol (S2) are available as Supplemental Material.

This was a phase I, open‐label dose‐finding study of intranasal insulin in healthy participants at a single site. The primary objective was to determine the safety of high‐dose nasal insulin or the maximum tolerated dose in healthy participants.

Placebo or insulin at escalating doses from 0 to 1000 units (U) was given intranasally to supine healthy participants on repeated weekly visits.

Study population

Adult volunteers determined to be healthy, based on medical history, physical examination, and routine laboratory testing, were included in this study. Participants of child‐bearing potential had a negative urine pregnancy test. We excluded volunteers with a body mass index <18 or >35 kg/m2, known allergy to insulin, pre‐existing diabetes, or current or prior use of any diabetes medication or insulin. Additionally, patients were examined and queried for any nasal disease that would preclude administration of nasal medication. Hypoglycemia (defined as plasma blood glucose of ≤65 mg/dL = 3.6 mmol/L) or hyperglycemia (plasma blood glucose of >200 mg/dL = 11.1) on baseline laboratory evaluation were also exclusion criteria.

End points

The primary safety outcome was severe hypoglycemia as defined by blood glucose of less than 45 mg/dL (2.5 mmol/L). The American Diabetes Association defines severe hypoglycemia as “severe cognitive impairment requiring external assistance for recovery” but does not list an absolute cutoff. 11 We defined mild hypoglycemia as less than or equal to 70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L). Secondary end points included the change in blood glucose, insulin, and C‐peptide levels for up to 4 h after drug administration to evaluate for any systemic absorption of intranasal insulin.

Monitoring

Glucose, insulin, and C‐peptide levels were measured serially at 0, 15, 30, 60, 120, 180, and 240 min after administration of placebo or intranasal insulin. Glucose was measured using a point‐of‐care glucometer. In the event of an episode of hypoglycemia, participants were to be provided dextrose intravenously and juice and a granola bar orally, and glucose was measured at additional 15‐min intervals until normoglycemic on two measurements 30 min apart.

Intranasal insulin dosing

Participants were scheduled for six administrations over five study visits with a roughly 1‐week washout interval between each study visit. The first study visit included an initial control administration of 0 U followed by 4 h of observation, followed by the first intranasal insulin administration and another 4 h of observation. Subsequent visits involved a single dose‐escalation administration per visit. The initial dose‐escalation schedule for the first cohort of six participants, used dose increments of 200 units over six administrations of 0, 200, 400, 600, 800, and 1000. Participants experiencing a severe hypoglycemic episode or having any related or possibly related serious adverse event (SAE) were not given a subsequent dose escalation but received a dose 100 U less than that to which they had a reaction at the next, and all subsequent, study visits. For every six participants, iterations of a Bayesian model were performed to associate the administered dose with the frequency of dose‐limiting hypoglycemia or other adverse events. The model adaptively reallocated the dose increments for the next six participants. To provide more precise and reproducible estimates of dose‐limiting toxicity, the trial modified the size of dose increments based on the developing model to allocate more administrations to areas of the model with greatest uncertainty, or to higher doses in the absence of dose‐limiting hypoglycemia. If there were no episodes of severe hypoglycemia or related or possibly related SAE in any six participant cohorts, then the dose‐escalation schedule for the next cohort was weighted toward higher doses and excluded a lower dose. If any cohort of six participants included one or more episodes of severe hypoglycemia or related or possibly related SAE, then the next cohort completed the same dose‐escalation schedule. This adaptive strategy provided more observations at the higher doses of interest that are most relevant to the determination of a maximally tolerated dose. Intra‐participant escalation allowed for fewer control administrations as well as a dose‐escalation strategy that controlled for participant‐specific factors, as each participant received escalating rather than randomized doses.

Drug administration

Participants were asked not to eat or drink for at least 2 h prior to each study visit and to abstain from alcohol for at least 12 h before the start of each dosing study visit. Regular insulin (Humulin R U500, Eli Lilly, Indianapolis, IN) at a concentration of 500 U/mL was used to create ≤2 mL doses containing between 0 and 1000 U. The ≤2 mL doses were administered intranasally using a 5 mL syringe divided into ≤1 mL per nostril. Intranasal administration was performed with the participants supine with the neck extended (a modified Mygind position) to mimic the position of comatose OHCA patients. 12 , 13 Participants remained in this position for 15 min.

Analysis

Analysis was performed using R. 14 Means of blood glucose, insulin, and C‐peptide levels for each administered dose were plotted for all measured timepoints. The area under the curve from time zero to the last quantifiable sample (AUC) was calculated by non‐compartmental analysis using PKanalix 2023R1 (Lixoft SAS, a Simulational Plus company) for mean insulin measurements in the blood for each intranasal insulin dose given. Single‐factor analysis of variance (ANOVA) was calculated between the AUCs for placebo IN insulin, lower doses (100, 200, 400, 500, 600 U) and higher doses (800, 900, 1000 U).

RESULTS

Twenty‐four participants (12 female, median age 53.5 years, IQR 36–61 years, range from 19 to 76 years) were administered a total of 116 doses of intranasal study drug, ranging from 0 to 1000 U (Figure 1; Table S1). No serious adverse events occurred. One participant did not complete the study for reasons unrelated to the intervention. Three non‐serious adverse events occurred, two of which were related to the study intervention. There was one episode of severe hypoglycemia (0.9%), and one episode of mild hypoglycemia (0.9%) out of 116 dose administrations. Both episodes occurred after the administration of 600 U of insulin, and both were rapidly corrected without sequelae. A single episode of gastroenteritis was also reported as a non‐serious adverse event not related to the intervention. A protocol deviation of the first four participants being given 100 U instead of 200 U is noted, with the trial continuing as planned.

Episodes of hypoglycemia

Dosing patterns as well as episodes of hypoglycemia for all 24 participants enrolled in the study are shown in Figure 1. One participant had severe hypoglycemia (minimum of 24 mg/dL = 1.3 mmol/L) 45 min after receiving 600 U of intranasal insulin that was corrected with oral intake and intravenous dextrose (50 mL of 50% dextrose in water). A peak insulin level of 163.8 μU/mL was noted at 30 min, from a baseline of 10 μU/mL and a steady decrease thereafter. Subsequent blood glucose measurements at 60 and 75 min were at 215 and 94 mg/dL. The participant remained asymptomatic during this episode. The participant had not had any prior episodes of hypoglycemia at 0, 100, and 400 U. Per protocol, no higher doses of insulin were administered to this participant; however, a follow‐up dose at 500 U was given at the next study visit with no resulting hypoglycemia. Serum insulin increased for this participant, with a maximum at 30 min with a corresponding through at 45 min for glucose, recovering with interventions.

One other participant had transient hypoglycemia with a minimum of 51 mg/dL (2.8 mmol/L) at 30 min, also after administration of 600 U of intranasal insulin. This participant experienced mild symptoms of mildly increased diaphoresis and shaking/tremor in his hands, this was corrected with oral intake. This participant continued with doses of 800, 900, and 1000 U without hypoglycemia.

Overall, hypoglycemic episodes were associated with increases in serum insulin but not with aggregate doses of exogenous insulin.

DISCUSSION

Sudden cardiac arrest is responsible for one in five deaths in the United States. 15 Even among patients who achieve return of spontaneous circulation after out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA), less than one in three ultimately survive. Irreversible brain injury is the primary cause of death in these patients. 2 Putative interventions to protect against brain injury have been investigated but typically require very early initiation during cardiac arrest for maximal efficacy. Many pharmacologic therapies are neuroprotective in animal models, but none have been successfully translated to clinical cardiac arrest care, 3 at least in part because of the challenges of very early administration. Intranasal insulin has demonstrated promising preclinical results for neuroprotection after ischemia or brain injury and has the potential to be very rapidly delivered during resuscitation after cardiac arrest. 10 , 16 , 17 , 18

There are substantial safety data available for intranasal insulin in humans, but the optimal doses for neuroprotection after cardiac arrest extrapolated from animal data in ongoing studies are higher than those used in prior human experiments. 9 , 19 This phase I study evaluated the safety, tolerability, and systemic physiological response to higher dose intranasal regular human insulin (Humulin R U‐500) in healthy volunteers as a critical first step in the design of future dose–response studies in patients with cardiac arrest.

One major barrier to pharmacologic neuroprotection is the inability to deliver drugs to the brain within the therapeutic window, which is often minutes after cardiac arrest. 4 For out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest, this will likely require bystanders or first responders to deliver the therapy in addition to performing CPR and defibrillation, some with limited to no medical training. Intranasal administration of medications can be achieved rapidly by laypeople as evidenced by the increasingly widespread layperson delivery of naloxone in victims of opioid overdoses. 20 The administration of placebo or study drug was well tolerated by mostly healthy participants in the study, making it a potentially useful route of administration of insulin for neuroprotection after ischemia or injury to the brain even for non‐comatose patients.

Although the brain is highly vascular, the blood–brain barrier limits effective delivery of intravenously administered neuroprotective therapeutics, especially large peptides. Direct nose‐to‐brain pathways provide a noninvasive strategy to deliver drugs rapidly across the nasal mucosa directly into the central nervous system. 7 , 21 Since their first demonstration in the 1980s, these direct pathways have since been confirmed in recent years. Direct‐to‐brain insulin delivery without vascular absorption was shown in humans by simultaneous measurement of insulin levels in the cerebrospinal fluid and blood following intranasal administration. 22 In a study using functional magnetic resonance imaging to evaluate target engagement, intranasal administration of insulin resulted in a dose‐dependent modulation of resting state regional brain activity diffusely in both cortex and midbrain. 9

Substantial preclinical evidence shows that intranasal insulin may have significant neuroprotective effects across various models of acute ischemic and chronic neurodegenerative diseases, particularly those involving oxidative stress mechanisms. 5 , 6 , 18 Clinical data in humans are mostly limited to small studies with chronic administration in patients with Alzheimer's disease. A systematic review of these data reported on seven exploratory clinical trials of intranasal insulin in 293 participants. 8 These trials primarily focused on safety and feasibility, revealing that intranasal insulin was well‐tolerated and showed no safety concerns in addition to collectively showing signals of efficacy on some parameters of cognitive outcomes.

There are substantial clinical safety data for intranasal insulin in human trials, but there are no data suggesting the maximum tolerated dose. The majority of existing safety data involve doses of intranasal regular insulin in daily repeated administrations from 20 to 160 U. As reviewed by Schmid et al., 23 there were no episodes of hypoglycemia in 1053 participants in 38 studies in this dose range. Although these data are reassuring, there are important reasons to explore higher dose ranges. Understanding the maximum tolerated dose is important because the proposed community use of the intervention by bystanders and first responders demands a larger margin of safety than typical drugs, as lay rescuers have less ability to respond to adverse drug reactions as compared to medical providers. Although hypoglycemia has never been reported with intranasal insulin at lower doses in humans, the potential for this safety concern exists. Mechanisms of insulin transport into the brain through the olfactory epithelium are more efficient than trans‐mucosal absorption of regular insulin into the systemic circulation, but Kullmann et al. 9 have demonstrated small but measurable increases in serum insulin with intranasal administration which probably represents dose‐dependent spillage. There are insufficient data to show whether this dose–response is linear, or whether these small changes in insulin can emerge as clinically relevant changes in serum glucose. 9 A few of the 19 studies also reported clinically insignificant but measurable decreases in serum glucose ranging from 0.2 to 0.4 mmol/L when 160 U of insulin was administered intranasally.

In our study, high‐dose intranasal insulin up to 1000 U was generally well tolerated. Participants demonstrated very small but reproducible, dose‐dependent increases in serum insulin with corresponding but clinically insignificant decreases in serum glucose and C‐peptide. Based on ongoing preclinical animal studies, doses of up to 1000 U could yield maximum neuroprotective effects.

We also observed two idiosyncratic episodes of hypoglycemia (one mild, one severe) that do appear to have been dose‐dependent. These episodes were easily treated but were clinically significant, requiring intervention (oral caloric intake or intravenous dextrose). Hypoglycemia was not reproducible in other participants or in the same participants upon repeated dosing. Both episodes of hypoglycemia were associated with marked increases in vascular absorption and serum insulin levels. Those increases were not seen again, however, when the same participants were re‐dosed on subsequent study visits. There is no evident physiologic explanation for these events, but we speculate that an occult mucosal defect may have allowed these individual events. Both episodes of hypoglycemia were corrected rapidly with no sustained hypoglycemia, which is essential for a potential utilization of intranasal insulin in a post‐cardiac arrest setting.

In aggregate, the minor decrease in mean C‐peptide concentration measured over the first hour for all concentrations of intranasal insulin is also of uncertain mechanism and significance. The effect is dose‐dependent but is seen at all doses including placebo.

Hypoglycemia after cardiac arrest is likely to exacerbate brain injury, so the risk of hypoglycemia events after intranasal insulin is of concern, especially if the goal is very early administration by lay rescuers. If the ability to test point‐of‐care glucose and treat hypoglycemia is necessary, intranasal insulin use may need to be restricted to EMS providers. Alternatively, if hypoglycemic responses are rare and are consistently occurring 30 min or more after intranasal insulin administration, lay administration could still be appropriate as most EMS systems will provide an on‐scene response well within that time window. Existing EMS protocols would require adaptation to include potentially repeated blood glucose checks to avoid iatrogenic injury due to undetected prolonged hypoglycemia. Advanced Life Support (ALS) capable EMS providers would be required to provide intravenous treatment for severe hypoglycemia, a potential limitation for remote regions with prolonged ALS response times. Current American Heart Association cardiac arrest guidelines do already suggest glucose control after cardiac arrest; however, this primarily applies to post‐cardiac arrest critical care management in the hospital. 24

For a neuroprotective drug to be used after cardiac arrest or other events, such as traumatic brain injury or other brain ischemia, it likely requires rapid delivery, ease of delivery, a suitable safety profile, and promising clinical data. For intranasal insulin, rapid and easy delivery should be feasible, given the ubiquitous use of naloxone and prevalence of use. 20 This study contributed to further evaluating the safety profile of intranasal insulin, in particular at higher doses than previously studied. The episodes of hypoglycemia noted in our study show that careful consideration of the safety profile is needed, if the drug was to be utilized broadly. It is encouraging that episodes of hypoglycemia were both rare and easily corrected, with no prolonged episodes of hypoglycemia. Regarding further preclinical and ultimately clinical data, work is ongoing and multiple studies investigating intranasal insulin are currently recruiting participants (among others, national clinical trial numbers NCT05988632, NCT03943537, and NCT04251585).

Safe delivery of intranasal insulin to the brain at these high doses in healthy participants, combined with preclinical evidence of the efficacy of high‐dose intranasal insulin as a neuroprotective therapy after cardiac arrest, makes this an exciting potential intervention worthy of continued study.
