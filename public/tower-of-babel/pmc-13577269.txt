Assessment of pharmacy professionals’ workplace satisfaction in a conflict-affected health system: evidence from Yemen
Authors: Najmaddin A H Hatem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTJob satisfaction among pharmacy professionals is a key determinant of workforce retention and quality of pharmaceutical care. Yemen represents an extreme case in which prolonged conflict, economic collapse, and fragmented health governance may fundamentally reshape the professional–organisational relationship. A descriptive cross-sectional survey was conducted among pharmacy professionals in Yemen using a self-administered electronic questionnaire. Job satisfaction was assessed across five domains: Management and organisational support, Work environment, Inter-professional relationships, Compensation and rewards, and Professional satisfaction. Non-parametric statistical tests and Spearman correlation analyses were applied. A total of 106 pharmacy professionals participated in the study. Overall job satisfaction was moderate (median 3.33, IQR 2.84–3.84). Professional satisfaction was the highest-rated domain with median and IQR 3.83(3.04–4.17), with 68.9% of pharmacists reporting pride in their work and 63.2% indicating they would choose the profession again. Inter-professional relationships ranked second with median and IQR of 3.57(3.14–4.14), with 79.3% reporting strong collegial respect. In contrast, Compensation and rewards constituted a critical source of dissatisfaction, with only 16.0% of respondents perceiving their salary as fair. Years of professional experience was significantly associated with overall satisfaction (p = 0.01), with more experienced pharmacists reporting lower satisfaction. All satisfaction domains were positively correlated, with the strongest association observed between Work environment and Management and organisational support (ρ = 0.720). This study suggests that pharmacy professionals in Yemen maintain strong professional commitment and positive inter-professional relationships despite substantial dissatisfaction with compensation and other extrinsic employment conditions. The observed inverse association between professional experience and job satisfaction may indicate changing professional expectations among more experienced pharmacists, although the underlying mechanisms require further investigation. These findings contribute to the limited evidence on pharmacy workforce satisfaction in conflict-affected settings and may inform context-sensitive workforce policies in fragile and resource-constrained health systems.KEYWORDS: Job satisfaction, pharmacy professionals, Yemen, workplace satisfaction, compensation, workforce retention, resource-constrained settings, healthcare workforce

Introduction

Workplace satisfaction among pharmacy professionals is a critical determinant of healthcare system performance, workforce stability, and the quality and safety of pharmaceutical care. Extensive international literature demonstrates that pharmacists’ job satisfaction influences professional engagement, inter-professional collaboration, service innovation, and patient outcomes, while persistent dissatisfaction contributes to burnout, turnover intentions, and erosion of care quality (Alshahrani et al., 2025; Cohen et al., 2023; Druică et al., 2021). As pharmacists’ roles expand globally from dispensing-centered functions to direct patient care, clinical decision-making, and public health services, understanding the drivers of workplace satisfaction has become a strategic priority for health systems (Barakat & Sallam, 2025; Huber et al., 2025). Most empirical evidence on pharmacists’ job satisfaction, however, originates from stable, high-income, or upper-middle-income health systems, where dissatisfaction is often linked to workload, role ambiguity, regulatory burden, or limited professional autonomy rather than fundamental employment insecurity (Alshakka et al., 2020; Elbarazi et al., 2017; Hatem et al., 2023).

Yemen represents an extreme and analytically valuable case for examining pharmacy workforce satisfaction under conditions of prolonged conflict, economic collapse, and fragmented health governance. More than a decade of instability has severely disrupted health financing, regulatory oversight and workforce remuneration, resulting in widespread informal employment, salary erosion, and reliance on secondary income sources among healthcare professionals (Hatem, Kubas, et al., 2024b). Within this environment, pharmacy professionals continue to provide essential services across community, hospital, and humanitarian settings, often with limited institutional support and unclear career trajectories.

Emerging Yemeni studies suggest that pharmacists operate in professional environments characterised by constrained organisational support and inconsistent role integration. Evidence from hospital settings indicates that clinical pharmacists participating in ward rounds frequently encounter unclear role expectations and limited managerial backing, despite positive inter-professional attitudes (Hatem, Ibrahim, et al., 2024a). Complementary research demonstrates that while pharmacists hold favourable views towards advanced services such as medication therapy management, implementation remains minimal due to regulatory gaps, infrastructural limitations, and lack of institutional incentives (Berassa et al., 2021). Physicians’ perspectives further highlight structural barriers to effective pharmacist integration, including limited awareness of pharmacists’ clinical competencies and weak organisational frameworks for collaboration (Alshahrani et al., 2025). Collectively, these findings point to a systemic misalignment between pharmacists’ professional commitment and the extrinsic conditions under which they practice.

Understanding how pharmacy professionals experience, adapt to, and remain engaged under such conditions provides transferable insights for workforce planning in other LMICs facing economic instability, health system fragmentation, or protracted crises. Despite growing global attention to pharmacy workforce satisfaction, no prior empirical study has systematically examined workplace satisfaction among pharmacy professionals in Yemen or comparable conflict-affected health systems across multiple organisational and professional domains. Generating such evidence is essential to inform workforce retention strategies, guide organisational interventions, and contribute to the global discourse on sustaining healthcare professionals in fragile systems.

Therefore, this study aimed to assess workplace satisfaction among pharmacy professionals in Yemen and to examine the demographic and professional factors associated with satisfaction across key domains. By situating the findings within an international and theoretical framework, the study seeks to extend existing pharmacy workforce literature beyond stable health systems and to provide evidence applicable to other resource-constrained and conflict-affected contexts.

Methodology

Study design and sample size

A descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted among Yemeni pharmacy professionals. Data were collected using a self-administered electronic questionnaire which was administered through Google Forms and the survey link remained open for three months. Participants were recruited through professional pharmacist social media groups (Facebook, Telegram, and WhatsApp). Eligible participants were pharmacy professionals currently practicing in Yemen, including pharmacists and pharmacy technicians working in community pharmacies, hospitals, or other healthcare settings. Participation was voluntary, and duplicate responses were prevented through platform restrictions.

Questionnaire tool

The job satisfaction scale was developed through a comprehensive review of the relevant literature and input from experts (Alshahrani et al., 2025; Berassa et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2025). The questionnaire comprised two main sections. The first section collected socio-demographic and professional characteristics, including age, gender, years of experience, academic qualification, current workplace, monthly salary, and number of days off per week. It also included an open-ended item to identify the governorate of practice and a multiple-choice question assessing perceived recommendations to improve pharmacy professionals’ job satisfaction.

Job satisfaction was measured using 25 statements adapted from established job satisfaction instruments and contextualised to the Yemeni pharmacy practice setting. Responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). For analytical purposes, the questionnaire items were organised into five domains: Management and Organisational Support, Work Environment and Conditions, Inter-professional Relationships, Compensation and Rewards, and Professional Satisfaction. Content validity was established through expert review by pharmacy academics and practicing pharmacists. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, which demonstrated good reliability across all domains: Management and organisational support (α = 0.78), Work environment and conditions (α = 0.78), Inter-professional relationships (α = 0.81), Compensation and rewards (α = 0.85) and Professional satisfaction (α = 0.74). All reliability coefficients exceeded the recommended threshold for acceptable internal consistency, supporting the suitability of the instrument for exploratory workforce research (See Supplemental Material).

Ethical considerations and consent

Ethical principles of voluntary participation, confidentiality and anonymity were strictly upheld. The introductory page of the electronic questionnaire included the study objectives, participation conditions, confidentiality assurances and a statement that completion of the questionnaire implied informed consent. No personal identifiers were collected, and all data were stored and analysed in aggregated form to ensure participants’ privacy. The study was approved by the ethical committee in the General Directory of Health Information and Research at the Yemeni Ministry of Health with approval No. (GAIR/00/31/25).

Data analysis

Data were analysed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS). Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means, medians, and interquartile ranges) were used to summarise demographic variables and responses to job satisfaction items. The interpretation of satisfaction levels was based on the following calculated mean score ranges: (1.00–2.49) Low Satisfaction, (2.50–3.49) Moderate Satisfaction and (3.50–5.00) High Satisfaction. Correlation analysis using Spearman’s rho was performed to examine the relationships between the different satisfaction domains. Non-parametric (The Mann–Whitney U test and the Kruskal–Wallis one-way analysis) tests were used to examine the relationships between the five satisfaction domains and to identify associations between demographic factors (e.g. experience level) and overall job satisfaction.

Results

Demographic characteristics of the respondents

A total of 106 pharmacy professionals completed the survey. Most of respondents were in the age group of 26–30 years, accounting for 44 (41.5%) of the participants, followed by the 20–25 years age group (34, 32.1%). The participants were male, with 97 (91.5%), and regarding professional experience, most respondents (74, 69.8%) had 5 years or less of experience in the pharmacy field. Academic qualifications were varied, with the largest group holding a Pharmacy Diploma (49, 46.2%), followed by a Bachelor of Pharmacy (30, 28.3%). The most common current workplace was Community Pharmacy, reported by 63 (59.4%) of the participants. A significant portion of respondents (58, 54.7%) reported a monthly salary of less than 100 thousand Yemeni Rials. Furthermore, a large majority of the pharmacists (94, 88.7%) indicated they have a source of income other than their work as a pharmacist. Most respondents reported having one day off per week (61, 57.5%). For details see Table 1.

Pharmacists’ job satisfaction

The overall job satisfaction score had a median (IQR) of 3.33 (2.84–3.84), on a 5-point scale, indicating moderate overall satisfaction. The mean overall satisfaction score was 3.33 (SD = 0.68), with scores ranging from 1 to 4.76.

The descriptive statistics for each domain are presented in Figure 2. Furthermore, responses to individual items are shown in Supplemental Table S1.

Box plot showing distribution of satisfaction scores across different domains among pharmacy professionals.

Professional satisfaction represented the highest-rated domain, with a median and IQR score of 3.83 (3.04–4.17). A strong sense of professional pride was evident, with 68.9% of respondents agreeing or strongly agreeing that they are proud to work in their current job as a pharmacist. Likewise, 72.6% reported feeling they had done something enjoyable at work, and 63.2% indicated that they would choose the pharmacy profession again.

Compensation and rewards emerged as the most critical area of dissatisfaction, with the lowest median and IQR satisfaction score of 2(1.33–3). Only 16.0% of respondents agreed that their salaries were fair, while 66.9% disagreed or strongly disagreed.

Inter-professional relationships ranked second in satisfaction, with a median and IQR score of 3.57 (3.14–4.14). A large proportion of pharmacists reported strong collegial respect (79.3%) and friendly cooperation among colleagues (63.2%). Collaboration with physicians and nurses was generally favourable, with 50.9% and 66.1% agreement, respectively, and 70.7% overall satisfaction with teamwork.

The Work environment and conditions domain showed moderate satisfaction, with a median score of 3.40 (2.60–3.80). Approximately 48.1% agreed that employees had freedom in performing their work, and 51.8% believed that staffing levels were adequate. Around 53.8% rated the physical environment (lighting, ventilation, and facilities) as appropriate. However, less than half (45.6%) were satisfied with their workload and scheduling flexibility.

Management and organisational support had a median and IQR score of 3.25 (2.50–4). Less than half of respondents agreed that promotion opportunities in their current jobs were good (50%), while 51.9% believed their managers provided appropriate support and guidance. About 56.6% felt that management treated pharmacists equally with other healthcare professionals. However, only 46.3% reported that supervisors took employee complaints seriously, and many were neutral or dissatisfied with the fairness of regulations.

Statistical significance testing

Supplemental Table S2 presents the results of the statistical analyses that were conducted to examine the differences in job satisfaction domain scores according to participant characteristics, using non-parametric tests appropriate for ordinal Likert-scale data. Among the tested variables, pharmacy professionals with more than five years of experience reported significantly lower overall job satisfaction, Professional satisfaction, perceptions of Management and organisational support, and Work environment and conditions satisfaction than those with five years or less of professional experience (all P ≤ 0.002).

In addition, age was the second characteristic significantly associated with Management support (p = 0.01) and Work environment and conditions (p = 0.025). Pharmacists in the 20–25 years age group reported the highest median satisfaction scores for Management support (3.7, IQR: 2.7–4.2) and Work environment and conditions (3.6, IQR: 3.0–4.1), respectively.

Domain correlations

All five satisfaction domains were positively correlated with each other (Spearman’s rho, all p < 0.001), as presented in Figure 3. The strongest correlation was between Work environment and conditions and Management support (ρ = 0.720), indicating that improvements in Work environment and conditions are closely linked to improvements in management support. Other strong correlations included Professional satisfaction with Management support (ρ = 0.590) and relationships with Management support (ρ = 0.600). The weakest correlation was between Professional satisfaction and Compensation (ρ = 0.415).

Discussion

This study provides policy-relevant evidence on pharmacy workforce satisfaction in a fragile and conflict-affected health system. The study found moderate overall job satisfaction among participants, with significant variation across domains. Professional satisfaction was notably high, while Compensation and rewards emerged as a critical area of concern. Years of experience was the only demographic factor significantly associated with overall satisfaction, with newer pharmacy professionals reporting higher satisfaction than more experienced colleagues. All domains were positively correlated, indicating that Workplace satisfaction dimensions are closely interrelated. The findings highlight that pharmacy professionals may benefit from targeted interventions particularly in compensation structures and workload management, while leveraging the existing strengths in professional relationships and intrinsic motivation.

The study highlights two key areas of strength that are vital for the resilience of the pharmacy workforce. The high professional satisfaction, evidenced by 68.9% of participants reporting pride in their work and 63.2% indicating they would choose the profession again, underscores a deep-seated professional commitment. This finding is consistent with studies in other developing contexts, such as Ethiopia, where pharmacists also expressed a willingness to continue in their profession despite challenges (Berassa et al., 2021).

Furthermore, the positive perception of collaboration with physicians and nurses (70.7% agreement) aligns with the global trend highlighted by Alshahrani et al., which links strong inter-professional relationships to improved pharmacist performance and patient trust (Alshahrani et al., 2025). This suggests that despite local economic challenges, the professional integration of pharmacy professionals into the healthcare team is progressing positively, a factor that contributes significantly to job fulfilment. The strong positive correlation observed between all five satisfaction domains, particularly the strongest link between Work environment and conditions and Management support (ρ = 0.720), suggests that these domains are closely related; improvements in one area may be accompanied by improvements in others.

In contrast to the high levels of professional satisfaction, Compensation and rewards emerged as the most critical area of dissatisfaction, recording the lowest median score (Median = 2.00). The finding that only 17% of respondents agreed their salaries were fair, with 67% disagreeing, is a severe warning sign for workforce stability. This level of dissatisfaction with extrinsic rewards is not unique to Yemen. A comparative analysis with a study from Ethiopia also identified inadequate salary and high workload as primary reasons for dissatisfaction among pharmacy professionals (Berassa et al., 2021), further, only 27.1% of Nepali pharmacy Professionals were satisfied with their current income (Sapkota et al., 2025). Similarly, the systematic review by Barakat and Sallam confirmed that in the Arab world, low pay and high workloads are frequently cited as key contributors to dissatisfaction, contrasting with the global focus on professional development and work conditions (Barakat & Sallam, 2025). This supports the broader literature indicating that inadequate financial compensation is a systemic and regional challenge facing pharmacy professionals in resource-constrained healthcare systems (Alshahrani et al., 2025; Berassa et al., 2021).

To understand the depth of this issue, the findings can be framed within the concept of the psychological contract, the unwritten set of expectations between an employee and their organisation. The study from China by Zhang et al. found that a breach of the psychological contract was a significant predictor of low job satisfaction (Zhang et al., 2025). In the Yemeni context, the severe dissatisfaction with compensation and the lack of perceived fairness may reflect a profound breach of this contract. The findings may be interpreted as reflecting perceptions of unmet professional expectations. This suggests that interventions should move beyond simple salary adjustments to address the fundamental issues of perceived fairness and fulfilment of professional expectations.

A particularly alarming finding is that pharmacy professionals with more than five years of experience demonstrated consistently lower satisfaction across several domains, suggesting that dissatisfaction may accumulate over the course of professional practice. This inverse relationship between experience and satisfaction parallels findings from other countries where, younger pharmacists exhibited greater enthusiasm and optimism, while more experienced staff often faced frustration with stagnant career progression and unmet expectations (Alshahrani et al., 2025).

This inverse relationship is likely a direct consequence that may be interpreted through the lens of the extrinsic psychological contract. The Chinese study found that job burnout played only a weakly mediating role (12.16%) between psychological contract fulfilment and job satisfaction (Zhang et al., 2025). This implies that for the Yemeni pharmacists, the primary driver of low satisfaction among experienced staff is not merely emotional exhaustion (burnout) but could indicate persistent failure of the organisation to fulfil its side of the bargain, namely, providing fair compensation and career progression commensurate with their growing experience and expertise. This finding may indicate challenges affecting the satisfaction of experienced pharmacy professionals and merits further investigation to measure migration, turnover, or resignation.

The findings present a clear contrast with studies from developed regions. For instance, in a study of Polish pharmacists, the primary sources of dissatisfaction were related to the lack of state funding for new pharmaceutical care services and excessive workload, not basic salary (Huber et al., 2025). In such contexts, intrinsic factors and professional autonomy often emerge as the main drivers of satisfaction (Sansgiry & Ngo, 2003). The current study, however, suggests that in a resource-constrained environment, the basic extrinsic factor of compensation becomes the dominant source of dissatisfaction, overshadowing the positive effects of intrinsic and relational factors. Crucially, the weakest correlation was found between Professional satisfaction and Compensation (ρ = 0.415), suggests that the high professional pride and fulfilment experienced by pharmacists are the least dependent on their financial compensation. However, these findings suggest that intrinsic professional motivation alone may not fully offset dissatisfaction with employment conditions.

Policy and practical implications

Interpretation of the study findings and their policy relevance must be contextualised within Yemen’s prolonged conflict, economic collapse, and fragmented health governance structure. In this setting, large-scale structural reforms or substantial salary increases are currently constrained by limited fiscal capacity, weak regulatory enforcement, and high levels of informal employment among pharmacy professionals. Therefore, the implications of this study should be viewed as incremental, context-sensitive and feasibility-oriented, rather than prescriptive.

Short-term, low-cost interventions represent the most immediately actionable strategies. At the facility and organisational level, management practices that enhance perceived fairness and recognition, such as transparent scheduling, equitable workload distribution, non-financial recognition and regular feedback mechanisms, may partially mitigate dissatisfaction in the absence of salary reform. Given the strong association observed between Management support and work environment and conditions, strengthening supervisory practices requires minimal financial investment yet may yield meaningful improvements in overall workplace satisfaction.

Medium-term interventions, contingent on institutional and donor support, may include the development of standardised role descriptions, experience-based recognition frameworks, and modest performance-linked incentives implemented through non-governmental organisations, humanitarian agencies, or donor-funded health programmes. These measures align with existing health system strengthening initiatives and may be integrated into ongoing capacity-building projects without substantial additional cost.

Long-term policy actions, dependent on political stabilisation and economic recovery, should prioritise formalisation of employment conditions, establishment of minimum compensation benchmarks and structured career progression pathways for pharmacy professionals. While currently challenging, articulating these goals is essential for future workforce planning and retention strategies.

Importantly, the findings underscore that reliance on pharmacists’ intrinsic professional motivation is not sustainable in the absence of parallel improvements in extrinsic employment conditions. Policymakers, professional bodies and health system stakeholders should therefore consider targeted and realistic interventions that balance workforce support with Yemen’s institutional and economic constraints.

Limitations

The study implications must be interpreted considering Yemen’s prolonged conflict, limited fiscal capacity, and fragmented health governance, which constrain the feasibility of large-scale workforce reforms. This study utilised a cross-sectional design and convenience sampling, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to the entire population of Yemeni pharmacy professionals. Convenience sampling and online recruitment may introduce selection bias, as pharmacists with Internet access or stronger professional engagement may have been more likely to participate. Additionally, self-reported responses may be subject to response bias, including social desirability bias. Furthermore, the study’s focus on job satisfaction could be enhanced by future research that directly measures related constructs such as psychological contract fulfilment and burnout to further validate the theoretical links suggested by the comparative analysis. Future studies should also explore the impact of the ongoing conflict and economic instability on the psychological contract of healthcare professionals in Yemen.

Conclusions

This study highlights important gaps in workplace satisfaction among pharmacy professionals in Yemen’s fragile health system. While indicators of professional commitment and inter-professional relationships were generally favourable, dissatisfaction with compensation, organisational support, and career development remained evident. The observed associations among satisfaction domains suggest that context-appropriate organisational interventions, including transparent workload allocation, supportive supervision, non-financial recognition, and clearer role definitions, may enhance workplace satisfaction even when immediate financial reforms are not feasible. In the medium and long term, workforce policies should strengthen employment conditions, compensation standards and career development opportunities. Overall, these findings suggest that professional commitment alone may be insufficient to maintain workforce satisfaction without fair and supportive employment conditions.
