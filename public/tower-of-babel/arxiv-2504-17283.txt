Commuting degrees for BCK-algebras
C. Matthew Evans

Abstract
. We discuss the following question: given a finite BCK-algebra, what is the probability that two randomly selected elements commute? We call this probability the commuting degree of a BCK-algebra. In a previous paper, the author gave sharp upper and lower bounds for the commuting degree of a BCK-algebra with order n n . We expand on those results in this paper: we show that, for each n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 , there is a BCK-algebra of order n n realizing each possible commuting degree and that the minimum commuting degree is achieved by a unique BCK-algebra of order n n Additionally, we show that every rational number in ( 0 , 1 ] (0,1] is the commuting degree of some finite BCK-algebra.



1.
Introduction

In 1973, Gustafson proved that the probability of two elements commuting in a finite non-Abelian group
G
is at most
\frac{5}{8}

[
14
]
. There are several ways to generalize this idea, but many such generalizations fall under the umbrella of the following question: what is the probability that a uniformly randomly selected
k
-tuple over a finite algebraic structure satisfies some specified first-order formula in
k
free variables? We make this precise as follows:

Definition 1.1
.

Given a first-order language
\mathcal{L}
, a finite
\mathcal{L}
-structure
M
, and an
\mathcal{L}
-formula
\varphi(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots x_{k})
in
k
free variables, the quantity

\ds(\varphi,M)=\frac{|\,\{\,(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k})\in M^{k}\mid\varphi(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots a_{k})\,\}|}{|M|^{k}}

is the
degree of satisfiability
of the formula
\varphi
in the structure
M
.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
T
be a theory over a first-order language
\mathcal{L}
and
\varphi
an
\mathcal{L}
-formula in
k
free variables. We say that
\varphi
has
finite satisfiability gap

\varepsilon
in
T
if there is a constant
\varepsilon>0
such that, for every finite model
M
of
T
, either
\ds(\varphi,M)=1
or
\ds(\varphi,M)\leq 1-\varepsilon
.

Using Gustasfon’s result as an example, in the language of groups, the commutativity equation
xy=yx
has finite satisfiability gap
\frac{3}{8}
: every finite group either has degree of satisfiability 1 (if it is Abelian) or no larger than
\frac{5}{8}
.

There is a substantial literature about commuting probabilities for finite groups; rather than list a lengthy list of citations here, we refer the reader to two specific papers and the bibliographies contained therein. The first is a 2006 paper by Guralnick and Robinson
[
13
]
which appears to capture the zeitgeist of commuting probabilities in groups up to the early 2000’s, and the second is the survey paper
[
6
]
by Das, Nath, and Pournaki from 2013.

Probabilities for some commutator-like equations in groups were considered by Delizia, Jezernik, Moravec, and Nicotera in
[
7
]
as well as by Lescot
[
21
]
, while Kocsis
[
20
]
studied probabilities for various other equations generalizing the commutativity equation. MacHale investigated commuting probability for finite rings in
[
22
]
, and there is more recent work focused on finite rings by Buckley and MacHale
[
2
,
3
]
, Buckley, MacHale, and Ní Shé
[
4
]
, and Basnet, Dutta, and Nath
[
1
]
. Other probability questions in finite rings have also been explored; for example, the probability that two randomly chosen elements multiply to 0 has been examined in
[
8
,
10
,
27
]
, while the probability that two randomly chosen elements multiply to some arbitrary element
x
has been examined in
[
26
,
25
]
and a very recent pre-print by Dolžan
[
9
]
.

A recent paper of Bumpus and Kocsis
[
5
]
considers probability questions for the class of Heyting algebras, which are the algebraic semantics for intuitionistic logic. They showed that, among all one-variable equations in the language of Heyting algebras, only three have a finite satisfiability gap:
x=\top
,
\neg x=\top
, and
x\vee\neg x=\top
. Inspired by their work, the present author investigated satisfiability gaps for some equations in the language of BCK-algebras, a class of algebraic structures introduced by Imai and Iséki
[
16
]
as the algebraic semantics for a non-classical logic having only implication. In
[
12
]
, the author gave sharp bounds for the probability that two elements in a finite non-commutative BCK-algebra commute – what we call the
commuting degree
of the algebra – by finding algebras that realized those bounds. As a consequence, the commutativity equation for BCK-algebras does not have a finite satisfiability gap.

Empirically, it was observed that for BCK-algebras of orders
n=3
,
4
, and
5
, every possible commuting degree is obtained by some algebra of that order. It was conjectured that this is true for all
n\geq 3
, and further, that every rational in
(0,1]
occurs as the commuting degree of some BCK-algebra. This conjecture indicates that BCK-algebras are somewhat like semigroups: Ponomarenko and Selinski proved in
[
24
]
that every rational in
(0,1]
is the commuting probability for some semigroup.

The aim of this paper is to prove the two conjectures above. Additionally, we show that for each
n
, there is exactly one algebra (up to isomorphism) that achieves the minimum commuting degree. In the next section, we define the class of BCK-algebras and discuss two techniques for constructing new BCK-algebras. In section 3, we discuss the relevant material from
[
12
]
and prove the main results.

2.
Preliminaries

Definition 2.1
.

A
BCK-algebra
is an algebra
\mathbf{A}=\langle A;\boldsymbol{\cdot},0\rangle
of type
(2,0)
such that

(BCK1)

\bigl[(x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)\boldsymbol{\cdot}(x\boldsymbol{\cdot}z)\bigr]\boldsymbol{\cdot}(z\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)=0

(BCK2)

\bigl[x\boldsymbol{\cdot}(x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y)\bigr]\boldsymbol{\cdot}y=0

(BCK3)

x\boldsymbol{\cdot}x=0

(BCK4)

0\boldsymbol{\cdot}x=0

(BCK5)

x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y=0
and
y\boldsymbol{\cdot}x=0
imply
x=y
.

for all
x,y,z\in A
.

These algebras are partially ordered by:
x\leq y
if and only if
x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y=0
. Note that 0 is the least element by (BCK4). One can also show that
x\boldsymbol{\cdot}0=x
for all
x\in\mathbf{A}
. We say the algebra
\mathbf{A}
is
bounded
if there exists an element
\top
such that
x\boldsymbol{\cdot}\top=0
for all
x\in\mathbf{A}
. By (BCK5), if
\mathbf{A}
contains incomparable elements
a
and
b
, which we indicate with the notation
a\perp b
, then we must have either
a\boldsymbol{\cdot}b\neq 0
or
b\boldsymbol{\cdot}a\neq 0
.

Define a term operation
\wedge
by
x\wedge y:=y\boldsymbol{\cdot}(y\boldsymbol{\cdot}x)
. The element
x\wedge y
is a lower bound for
x
and
y
, but in general is not the greatest lower bound of
x
and
y
. One can show that if
x\wedge y=y\wedge x
, then
x\wedge y
is the greatest lower bound, and in this case we say that
x
and
y

commute
. If this equation holds for all
x,y\in\mathbf{A}
, we say the algebra
\mathbf{A}
is
commutative
. The class of all commutative BCK-algebras is an important and well-studied subclass of all BCK-algebras. While the class of all BCK-algebras is not a variety, the class of commutative BCK-algebras do form a variety (that is, this class is closed under taking subalgebras, products, and homomorphic images); for more detail on the elementary properties of BCK-algebras we refer the reader to
[
18
,
19
,
23
]
.

Here we give two methods for constructing new BCK-algebras. Let
\{\mathbf{A}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
be a family of BCK-algebras such that
A_{\lambda}\cap A_{\mu}=\{0\}
for
\lambda\neq\mu
, and let
U
denote the union of the
A_{\lambda}
’s. Equip
U
with the operation

x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y=\begin{cases}x\boldsymbol{\cdot}_{\lambda}y&\text{if $x,y\in A_{\lambda}$}\\
x&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\,.

Then
U
is a BCK-algebra we will call the
BCK-union
of the
\mathbf{A}_{\lambda}
’s and denote it by
\mathbf{U}=\bigsqcup_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\mathbf{A}_{\lambda}
. This construction first appears in
[
18
]
, though only in the case
|\Lambda|=2
. For a full proof that this is a BCK-algebra, see
[
11
]
.

Next, given any BCK-algebra
\mathbf{A}
of order
n-1
, we construct a new BCK-algebra of order
n
by appending a new top element, call it
\top
, and extending the BCK-operation as follows:

\displaystyle x\boldsymbol{\cdot}\top

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\top\boldsymbol{\cdot}\top

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\top\boldsymbol{\cdot}x

\displaystyle=\top

for all
x\in\mathbf{A}
. This is known as
Iséki’s extension of
\mathbf{A}
, which we will denote
\mathbf{A}\oplus\top
. Iséki’s extension always yields a bounded non-commutative BCK-algebra having
\mathbf{A}
as a maximal subalgebra
[
17
]
. We also note an important fact here: every BCK-algebra of order
n
contains a subalgebra of order
n-1

[
15
]
.

3.
Commuting degrees

Given a BCK-algebra
\mathbf{A}
, let
C(\mathbf{A})
denote the set of commuting pairs of elements:

C(\mathbf{A})=\{\,(x,y)\in A^{2}\,\mid\,x\wedge y=y\wedge x\,\}\,.

Following definition
1.1
, define the
commuting degree
of
\mathbf{A}
, denoted
\cd(\mathbf{A})
, to be the degree of satisfiability of the commutativity equation
x\wedge y=y\wedge x
; that is,

\cd(\mathbf{A})=\frac{|C(\mathbf{A})|}{|\mathbf{A}|^{2}}\,.

This is the probability that two elements chosen uniformly randomly (with replacement) commute with one another.

In any BCK-algebra
\mathbf{A}
, we have
x\wedge 0=0\wedge x
for all
x\in\mathbf{A}
, and every element commutes with itself by (BCK3). So every pair of the form
(x,0)
,
(0,x)
, and
(x,x)
is in
C(\mathbf{A})
. Thus, if
|\mathbf{A}|=n
, we have
|C(\mathbf{A})|\geq 3n-2
and

\cd(\mathbf{A})\geq\frac{3n-2}{n^{2}}\,.

Of course, if
\mathbf{A}
is commutative then
\cd(\mathbf{A})=1
, but if
\mathbf{A}
is non-commutative we have

\frac{3n-2}{n^{2}}\leq\cd(\mathbf{A})\leq\frac{n^{2}-2}{n^{2}}

and these bounds are sharp.

Let
\mathbf{U}=\bigsqcup_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\mathbf{A}_{\lambda}
be a BCK-union of a family of BCK-algebras. If two elements
a
and
b
lie in distinct components
A_{\lambda}
and
A_{\mu}
(
\lambda\neq\mu)
, then
a
and
b
necessarily commute:

\displaystyle a\wedge b

\displaystyle=b\boldsymbol{\cdot}(b\boldsymbol{\cdot}a)=b\boldsymbol{\cdot}b=0

\displaystyle b\wedge a

\displaystyle=a\boldsymbol{\cdot}(a\boldsymbol{\cdot}b)=a\boldsymbol{\cdot}a=0\,.

Heuristically, then, we expect that the commuting degree is larger when an algebra has many incomparable elements, and smaller when an algebra has many comparable elements. The following four results can be found in
[
12
]
, though we present sketches for Theorems
3.2
and
3.4
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a BCK-algebra with
|\mathbf{A}|=n
and
\cd(\mathbf{A})=\frac{k}{n^{2}}
. Then

\cd(\mathbf{A}\sqcup\mathbf{2})=\frac{k+2n+1}{(n+1)^{2}}\,.

Theorem 3.2
.

For each
n\geq 3
, there is a non-commutative BCK-algebra of order
n
realizing the maximum commuting degree
\frac{n^{2}-2}{n^{2}}
.

Sketch of proof.

For
n=3
, the algebra
\mathbf{3}^{\text{p}}
defined in Table

has commuting degree
\frac{7}{9}
. Then we define a family of algebras
\mathbf{B}_{n}
by

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{3}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{3}^{\text{p}}

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}_{n-1}\sqcup\mathbf{2}\text{ for $n>3$\,.}

and by inducation one can show
\cd(\mathbf{B}_{n})=\frac{n^{2}-2}{n^{2}}
.
∎

\mathbf{B}_{n}

We next consider the behavior of commuting degree with respective to Iséki’s extension.

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose
\mathbf{A}
is a BCK-algebra with
|\mathbf{A}|=n
and
\cd(\mathbf{A})=\frac{k}{n^{2}}
. Then

\cd(\mathbf{A}\oplus\top)=\frac{k+3}{(n+1)^{2}}\,.

The idea of the proof is simple: the new element
\top
does not commute with any non-zero element of
\mathbf{A}
, but it does commute with 0 and itself, meaning

(0,\top),(\top,0),(\top,\top)\in C(\mathbf{A}\oplus\top)

and therefore
|C(\mathbf{A}\oplus\top)|=k+3
.

Theorem 3.4
.

For each
n\geq 3
, there is a BCK-algebra of order
n
realizing the minimum commuting degree
\frac{3n-2}{n^{2}}
.

Sketch of proof.

For
n=3
, the algebra
\mathbf{3}^{\text{p}}
has commuting degree
\frac{7}{9}
. Define a new family of BCK-algebras as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{3}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{3}^{\text{p}}\text{ and}

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{M}_{n-1}\oplus\top\text{ for $n>3$\,.}

One can show that
\cd(\mathbf{M}_{n})=\frac{3n-2}{n^{2}}
.
∎

Since the
\mathbf{M}_{n}
’s are linear, the above result verifies our intuition that commuting degree is small when there are many comparable elements.

Unlike for the maximum commuting degree, it turns out for each

there is a unique algebra achieving the minimum commuting degree, that being the algebra
\mathbf{M}_{n}

\mathbf{M}_{n}
to include

, then this is also true for

Now, given a non-commutative BCK-algebra
\mathbf{A}
of order
n
, the possible commuting degrees are

\mathcal{CD}(n):=\Bigl\{\,\frac{3n-2}{n^{2}}\,,\,\frac{3n}{n^{2}}\,,\,\frac{3n+2}{n^{2}}\,,\,\ldots\,,\,\frac{n^{2}-2}{n^{2}}\Bigr\}\,,

where the numerator increases by 2 since the relation
C(\mathbf{A})
is symmetric. Notice that
\mathcal{CD}(3)=\{\tfrac{7}{9}\}
.

An easy computation shows that

|\mathcal{CD}(n)|=\frac{(n^{2}-2)-(3n+2)}{2}+1=\frac{(n-2)(n-1)}{2}=T_{n-2}\,,

the
(n-2)^{\text{nd}}
triangular number, and this means we can rewrite
\mathcal{CD}(n)
as

\Bigl\{\,\frac{n^{2}-2}{n^{2}}\,,\,\frac{n^{2}-4}{n^{2}}\,,\,\ldots\,,\,\frac{n^{2}-2T_{n-2}}{n^{2}}\Bigr\}\,.

In
[
12
]
, it was observed that all values in
\mathcal{CD}(3)
,
\mathcal{CD}(4)
, and
\mathcal{CD}(5)
are obtained by some algebra of that order, and it was conjectured that this is true for all
n\geq 3
.

At that time, this observation was made by simply computing the commuting degrees using the appendix in
[
23
]
, which has Cayley tables for all BCK-algebras of orders 2, 3, 4, and 5. However, using a combination of Lemmas
3.1
and
3.3
, we can construct algebras realizing all possible commuting degrees. Before we prove this result, we show some examples.

Theorem 3.7
.

For every
n\geq 3
, every commuting degree in
\mathcal{CD}(n)
is achieved by an algebra of order
n
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
n
, noting again that the result holds for
\mathcal{CD}(3)
,
\mathcal{CD}(4)
, and
\mathcal{CD}(5)
. Suppose the result is true for
n\geq 6
and set
t:=T_{n-2}
. Then there are
t
-many BCK-algebras of order
n
, call them
\mathbf{A}_{1},\mathbf{A}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{A}_{t}
, such that
\mathcal{CD}(n)=\{\,\cd(\mathbf{A}_{1}),\cd(\mathbf{A}_{2}),\ldots,\cd(\mathbf{A}_{t})\,\}
. Assume the algebras are labelled in order of increasing commuting degree. That is, assume

\cd(\mathbf{A}_{1})=\frac{n^{2}-2t}{n^{2}}=\frac{3n-2}{n^{2}}\,,\,\cd(\mathbf{A}_{2})=\frac{3n}{n^{2}}\,,\,\ldots\,,\,\cd(\mathbf{A}_{t})=\frac{n^{2}-2}{n^{2}}\,.

Define a family of algebras as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}_{1}\oplus\top

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}_{2}\oplus\top

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}_{t}\oplus\top

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}_{t-(n-2)}\sqcup\mathbf{2}

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{t+2}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}_{t-(n-3)}\sqcup\mathbf{2}

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{t+(n-2)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}_{t-1}\sqcup\mathbf{2}

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{t+(n-1)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}_{t}\sqcup\mathbf{2}\,.

Notice that
t+(n-1)=T_{n-2}+(n-1)=T_{n-1}
, so we have
T_{n-1}
-many algebras all with order
n+1
. Applying Lemmas
3.1
and
3.3
where appropriate, we compute the commuting degrees of the
\mathbf{B}_{k}
’s as

\displaystyle\cd(\mathbf{B}_{1})=\cd(\mathbf{A}_{1}\oplus\top)=\frac{(3n-2)+3}{(n+1)^{2}}=\frac{3(n+1)-2}{(n+1)^{2}}=\frac{(n+1)^{2}-2T_{n-1}}{(n+1)^{2}}

\displaystyle\cd(\mathbf{B}_{2})=\cd(\mathbf{A}_{2}\oplus\top)=\frac{3n+3}{(n+1)^{2}}=\frac{3(n+1)}{(n+1)^{2}}=\frac{(n+1)^{2}-2(T_{n-1}-1)}{(n+1)^{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip 35.56593pt\vdots

\displaystyle\cd(\mathbf{B}_{t})=\cd(\mathbf{A}_{t}\oplus\top)=\frac{(n^{2}-2)+3}{(n+1)^{2}}=\frac{n^{2}+1}{(n+1)^{2}}=\frac{(n+1)^{2}-2\bigl(T_{n-1}-(t-1)\bigr)}{(n+1)^{2}}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \cd(\mathbf{B}_{t+1})=\cd(\mathbf{A}_{t-(n-2)}\sqcup\mathbf{2})=\frac{\bigl(n^{2}-2(n-1)\bigr)+(2n+1)}{(n+1)^{2}}&=\frac{n^{2}+3}{(n+1)^{2}}\\

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \cd(\mathbf{B}_{t+2})=\cd(\mathbf{A}_{t-(n-3)}\sqcup\mathbf{2})=\frac{\bigl(n^{2}-2(n-2)\bigr)+(2n+1)}{(n+1)^{2}}&=\frac{n^{2}+5}{(n+1)^{2}}\\

\displaystyle\hskip 35.56593pt\vdots

\displaystyle\cd(\mathbf{B}_{t+(n-2)})=\cd(\mathbf{A}_{t-1}\sqcup\mathbf{2})=\frac{(n^{2}-4)+(2n+1)}{(n+1)^{2}}=\frac{(n+1)^{2}-4}{(n+1)^{2}}

The procedure above is just one of very many ways to obtain commuting degrees. For example, the procedure above generates only 10 BCK-algebras of order 6, but in total there are 747 non-isomorphic non-commutative BCK-algebras of order 6.

Finally, we show that every rational in
(0,1)
is the commuting degree of some finite non-commutative BCK-algebra, and give an example of the procedure after the theorem.

Theorem 3.8
.

Every rational in
(0,1)
is the commuting degree for some finite non-commutative BCK-algebra. That is,

\bigcup\limits_{n=3}^{\infty}\mathcal{CD}(n)=\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1)\,.

Proof.

Take
\frac{p}{q}\in\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1)
. Note that
1\leq p<q
. Set
n=2q
and
k=2q(q-p)
. Notice that
k\geq 1
by definition. On the other hand,

T_{2q-2}=\frac{(2q-2)\bigl(2(q+1)-2\bigr)}{2}=2q(q-1)\geq 2q(q-p)=k\,.

Thus,
k\in\{1,2,\ldots,T_{n-2}\}
. Finally, we observe that

\frac{n^{2}-2k}{n^{2}}=\frac{(2q)^{2}-2\bigl(2q(q-p)\bigr)}{(2q)^{2}}=\frac{4q^{2}-4q^{2}+4pq}{4q^{2}}=\frac{p}{q}

and since
k\in\{1,\ldots,T_{n-2}\}
, we have
\frac{p}{q}\in\mathcal{CD}(n)
.
∎

Notice in fact that this shows the slightly stronger result that
\bigcup\limits_{\ell\geq 2}\mathcal{CD}(2\ell)=\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1)
. We close on an example.
