Evaluation framework for assessing the quality and safety of artificial intelligence solutions for the acute care surgeon: a narrative review from the Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma quality, safety, and outcomes committee
Authors: Tandis Soltani, Phillip Kim, William Ian McKinley, Lane Frasier, Phillip Dowzicky, Daniel N Holena, Rachael A Callcut, Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma’s Quality, Safety, and Outcomes Committee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryArtificial intelligence (AI) has been developing as a field for decades. As acute care surgeons, we have been using AI in the clinical realm and in our everyday lives for years. However, with the public release of conversational large language models, there has been a rapid increase in the uptake of AI, with numerous new products on the market and evolving conversations around what this means for the future of surgery. Given acute care surgeons are leaders in healthcare systems, interfacing with patients and impacting quality and safety through critical timepoints in the emergency department, operating rooms, wards, and intensive care units, it is imperative that we have an understanding of this technology, its potential for bias, its assessment and implementation, and its governance. In this review, we cover these critical concepts and aim to provide a framework for acute care surgeons to understand and evaluate the quality and safety of AI solutions.

Introduction

The term ‘Artificial Intelligence’ (AI) was developed in the 1950s as the ‘field of study that gives computers the ability to learn without being explicitly programmed’.1 AI today is a broad discipline that encompasses machine learning algorithms, natural language processing, large language models (LLMs), image and video processing, and more (figure 1). The use of AI in healthcare has theoretical potential for predicting outcomes, radiographic interpretation, determining risks with surgical intervention, aiding in diagnosis, providing feedback in surgical procedures, and reducing work burden for clinicians.2–6

Common terminology and subsets of artificial intelligence (AI).

AI models are computational systems trained on data to recognize patterns and make predictions or generate outputs. Models vary by architecture, training data, training objective, scale, and fine-tuning, which together determine capability, speed, cost, and domain performance.7 Closed proprietary models are models where internal workings, such as training weights, model weights, or code, are not publicly available. On the other hand, open models prioritize transparency with publicly available architecture and model weights.8

AI is being rapidly incorporated into multiple areas of healthcare, including imaging and diagnostics, clinical decision support tools, virtual health assistants, and predictive analytics and patient monitoring. As these AI tools move into our emergency departments, operating rooms, and intensive care units, and permeate throughout healthcare systems, it is our responsibility to ensure a positive impact on the quality and safety of the outcomes of our patients. This article will provide a broad overview of AI relevant to the acute care surgeon, discuss how to evaluate the risk profile of new AI solutions, and outline how acute care surgeons can remain engaged as critical stakeholders during the implementation and evaluation of AI solutions in healthcare. As many of the proposed AI tools are both early-stage and proprietary, discussion of specific tools in this article is limited to maintain a focus on peer-reviewed, generalizable evidence rather than commercial or non-validated solutions.

Examples of existing AI solutions in acute care surgery

Risk prediction models are not new, but the sophistication of the prediction models has grown significantly with the integration of advanced machine learning techniques. The most common use for acute care surgery has been algorithms developed to aid clinicians in capturing the risks of surgery or disease abnormality. The Predictive Optimal Trees in Emergency Surgery Risk score, the Trauma Early Mortality Prediction Tool score, and the Epic Sepsis Model Inpatient Predictive Analytic Tool are all examples of machine-learning algorithms that have performed well.4 9 10 Some of these predictive analytics tools are developed using proprietary data that has led some to question their results. Others have questioned the usefulness of models that use data after clinical recognition of sepsis or shock as an example.11

AI has multiple facets applicable to acute care surgery. There are existing AI solutions that search and summarize the current health literature and aim to guide evidence-based decision-making.12 Additionally, Ambient AI solutions are being implemented widely to assist providers in clinical documentation, including recent integration into large health systems. One major healthcare system implemented Ambient AI technology for 10 000 physicians and staff. They reported favorable feedback from both providers and patients. With the ability to revise AI-generated drafts, they found that high-quality documentation was produced in less time spent by the clinicians.13

Some electronic medical record systems offer chart summarization AI tools, aimed to ease clinician documentation burden.14 Furthermore, there have been several AI applications in both reformatting images and detecting abnormal radiographic findings.15–21 However, the implementation of these tools into our clinical workflows requires that we stay an active part of the conversation, understand the risks, and can effectively use existing frameworks to evaluate the quality and safety implications of AI.

There is strong momentum from the electronic health record vendors to integrate an ever-increasing number of AI prediction tools into routine use by clinicians. The speed with which these options are being generated has created challenges for healthcare systems to independently validate, locally adapt, and adequately assess the risks and real-world performance of many tools. This has resulted in several tools coming to market that were subsequently shown to have poor utility or performance. This was particularly prevalent for AI prediction tools for use in the COVID-19 pandemic and was largely the result of a lack of external validation before implementation. Due to a lack of robust and high-quality data, multiple diagnostic AI tools were found to have very poor performance. For example, a systematic appraisal of publicly available COVID-19 chest X-ray datasets found that only nine of more than 100 datasets met minimum criteria for adequate risk-of-bias assessment. Although only nine of these datasets were suitable for analysis, many peer-reviewed studies relied on those datasets that did not meet minimum criteria and resulted in models with a high risk of bias.22

Risks of healthcare AI and principles of trustworthy AI

At the time of this writing, there is no current standard accepted risk framework for evaluating healthcare AI technology. As a result, there has been a proliferation (up to 56 at the time of this writing) of AI risk frameworks proposed by a number of different groups, including several prominent national organizations, such as the Coalition for Healthcare AI. The National Academy of Medicine and the Association of American Medical Colleges have both recently embarked on their own efforts to create roadmaps for the development and use of guidelines for healthcare AI.23–25 Common themes have emerged across these assessment structures, including potential sources of bias in AI tools such as training data that may contain human biases, algorithm design, human influence in model creation, or learning feedback loops.26 These biases can be introduced along the continuum of algorithm development and be difficult to detect. This can lead to algorithms that have inherent risks, including adverse impacts on patient care, exacerbation of health disparities, worsening clinical judgment and timely human intervention, loss of public trust, and legal and ethical challenges. Although many physicians and clinical care providers will be using AI tools going forward, acute care surgeons are in a particularly unique position. To date, many AI predictive and risk assessment tools have focused on the inpatient aspect of common, acute conditions, including respiratory failure, sepsis, hemorrhage, critical injury/illness, and those needing urgent or emergent operative procedures.27–31 Importantly, the patient population we care for tends to have a disproportionate fraction of persons traditionally at risk for disparities in care.32–36 It is imperative that we understand the benefits, but also acquire a knowledge base to assess for potential bias and evaluate the proposed use cases for various AI tools for our patient population and time-sensitive clinical scenarios. This includes engagement in evaluating, selecting, and adopting new AI solutions being considered by hospitals and healthcare systems. There are potential risks of bias and harm to patients if these tools are not evaluated systematically and continuously with multistakeholder involvement. As we evaluate these solutions for their potential to improve outcomes, we must also recognize the impact of publication bias for positive results and the potential to offer false hope and unfounded enthusiasm for AI in general.37

To minimize the risks while maximizing the potential for AI, the WHO laid out six guiding principles. We recommend considering these principles in the evaluation, selection, and implementation of AI tools. Briefly, the six principles are as follows: ‘(1) protecting human autonomy, (2) promoting human well-being and safety and the public interest, (3) ensuring transparency, explainability, and intelligibility, (4) fostering responsibility and accountability, (5) ensuring inclusiveness and equity, (6) promoting AI that is responsive and sustainable’. The six Trustworthy AI (TAI) principles, outlined in the AI playbook released by the US Department of Health and Human Services (HHS), are also well-aligned with the WHO principles: (1) fairness, (2) transparency, (3) accountability, (4) reliability, (5) privacy, and (6) safety/security (see table 2 for definitions and examples).38

All six TAI principles can be brought into question with current LLMs used in healthcare.39 LLMs can incorporate biases found in their training data, perpetuating harmful biases inadvertently, which brings into question the principle of fairness. The models lack transparency and are not able to provide clear explainability for their responses or assessment of certainty in the accuracy of a response. Clinicians remain responsible for using any output generated from these models, and the models or creators of these models do not at this time provide accountability. Strict supervision from clinicians is ultimately required to ensure information conveyed is not inaccurate or incomplete. LLMs are at risk of hallucination or providing plausible, but incorrect information. We need to consider whether LLMs built in the future on electronic health record data will need explicit approval and consent from patients to be well aligned with the principle of privacy. LLMs have leaked conversations between users, raising concerns about using protected health information, bringing their security and safety into question.39

Some organizations may choose to add additional structures and risk mitigation processes to provide further oversight. By fostering a culture of trust and accountability, the healthcare system has the potential to harness the transformative benefits of AI solutions while safeguarding patients from the many risks of AI. We will review the risks, governance, and safety of healthcare AI solutions in further detail in the next section.

Assessment of quality and safety of AI tools

Traditional medical devices and interventions take a stepwise, well-defined approach through proof-of-concept development, prototyping, pilot implementation, clinical study, widespread deployment, and post-deployment surveillance. This staged pathway is far less common in healthcare AI development. Thus, assessment of performance becomes more challenging for many end-users.

Historically, quality assessments of new medical interventions, tools, and tests have been characterized by elements such as sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). Multivariable regression models also provide effect size metrics such as ORs and HRs. When discussing these elements, those reviewing the evidence have been trained to ask questions about biases, to consider the representativeness of the underlying population, and to analyze the generalizability of the findings. Given increasingly complex AI models with variable transparency, previously helpful constructs may not result in a complete assessment of the model.

AI tools have increasingly obfuscated data used to train them and might offer no information about the population and data used in their training, validation, and testing sets. This is most true of proprietary tools such as those using generative AI, but is also true of many healthcare AI tools. It is also important to consider that proprietary tools can be applied in healthcare and used as the base LLM. As these tools may be used in contexts for which they were not trained, models can fail in ways both visible and invisible. Models may simply fail to generalize to new populations. To combat this problem, several groups are attempting to spread reporting standards for machine learning and AI so that users can better understand where these tools might be most appropriately applied.40 41

Model reporting guideline recommendations have been made to address the principles outlined above, including fairness and transparency. Reporting summary statistics as well as performance in subgroup analysis (eg, to account for demographic patient characteristics) can help create some measure of fairness for AI solutions. Similarly, to allow for transparency of AI solutions, guidelines also recommend reporting model coefficients, reproducibility, availability of datasets, and acknowledging funding sources.42 Ensuring quality and safety for our patients requires continuously evaluating the tools used in various aspects of patient care and should remain within the principles of practice for all acute care surgeons.

Risk prediction models are evaluated by two measures: (1) discrimination, which is the ability to discriminate between those who develop an event versus those who do not, and (2) calibration, which assesses how closely the predicted probabilities of an event align with the actual observed event.43 The C-statistic, or area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), is widely used as a global measure of model discrimination. AUC ranges from 0.5 (equal to chance) to 1 (perfect discrimination) and represents random to perfect concordance between the estimated risk of events versus observed events.43 Figure 2 demonstrates a ROC and two models with different AUCs. Line A shows a model with high AUC with high sensitivity and specificity, whereas line B represents a poor discrimination model that is equal to chance of flipping a coin.44–46 Importantly, the C-statistic also depends on the sample and the range of data represented within the sample. This statistic does not capture NPV or PPV, sensitivity, and specificity, or meaningful classification for the ROC curve. The calibration plot can be used to compare predicted probabilities versus observed outcomes. Similarly, the Brier Score, the difference between sensitivity and one minus specificity evaluated at event rate, can also be used to measure calibration. ROCs have been used and reported with AI models because they are a familiar and easily-implemented analysis. However, given the lack of transparency, users may have difficulty in evaluating these validation metrics for models.

Providing summary statistics and the amount of missingness in the data used to build models and methods in handling missing data, sensitivity, specificity, CIs, along with statistical significance and description of the process of annotating and inputting the data are examples of requested items that are in fact not commonly reported for prediction models.18 Model performance metrics include measures of discrimination (eg, AUROC), goodness of fit (eg, normalized root-mean squared error), calibration (eg, survival curve/Kaplan-Meier curve superimposition for Cox models), and classification (eg, PPV, NPV).42

An additional risk associated with AI’s lack of transparency is a limited ability to validate its findings. Its lack of transparency leads to reduced trust and ability to verify its output. AI responses often imply a level of certainty incommensurate with the methodology used to reach the conclusion. LLMs can confabulate, providing citations which do not exist and providing inaccurate or frankly unsafe recommendations.47–50 For these reasons, transparency on the source of AI’s recommendations, and clinician review of these sources (having a ‘human in the loop’) prior to use in clinical decision-making and/or academic work, is critical. AI tools should not be used independently without review in clinical decision making. They may aim to improve efficiency on tedious or time-consuming tasks, but any use in a patient-facing setting should be reviewed prior to clinician action. In academic settings, there have been many concerns regarding intellectual property, and the ethics of writing and publishing as generative AI tools are becoming more commonplace.51 52

Implementation of AI tools

Integrating AI solutions into acute care surgery safely requires more than high-performing models, as the clinical effectiveness of these tools hinges critically on how seamlessly they integrate into—and even reshape—clinical workflows. Without thoughtful consideration of the impact on providers, AI-driven decision support or other tools risk introducing unintended safety hazards, such as alert fatigue, workflow interruptions, or technological iatrogenesis, which may offset any intended gains from the AI solution.53 Furthermore, flawed financial incentives and unsafe workflows in healthcare practices cannot be rectified merely by deploying AI-driven tools.54 There must also be a concerted effort to advance AI literacy among healthcare professionals to allow for safe, effective adoption of AI tools.54 Therefore, implementation of AI in acute care surgery, in a way that optimizes quality and safety, ultimately requires attention not only to algorithmic performance but also to the design, adoption, and ongoing refinement of clinical workflows. We agree with previous recommendations to approach the adoption of new technology in a staged manner, starting with a small-scale pilot group, and under close monitoring.37 Developing or joining committees within hospitals or healthcare systems focused on the evaluation and implementation of new health technology, such as health information systems committees, can help with becoming an active participant in these processes.

Figure 3 summarizes a framework for the evaluation, implementation, and governance of healthcare AI solutions. The framework begins with defining the clinical context and intended use of an AI tool, followed by a structured pre-implementation assessment of data quality, validation, bias, and model performance. Central to the framework is explicit evaluation across the six TAI principles outlined by the HSS, which provides a unifying lens to assess risks related to fairness, transparency, accountability, reliability, privacy, and safety.38 These principles should be used to create risk mitigation plans. Finally, the framework emphasizes thoughtful workflow integration and continuous post-deployment monitoring under multidisciplinary governance to ensure sustained quality, safety, and trust in real-world clinical use. Healthcare institutions should consider piloting and monitoring clinically used AI algorithms to ensure the algorithms function as intended in real-world settings before expanding their use in clinical settings.

In addition, as clinicians looking for the best interest of our patients as well as our healthcare systems, we must familiarize ourselves with regulatory bodies and regulations. There are several new regulations put in place for the application of AI. For example, the Colorado AI Act set to take effect on February 1, 2026 has put the ‘duty of reasonable care’ on developers and deployers of ‘high-risk AI systems’ used in several industries, including housing, education, employment, and healthcare. This act also mandates transparency as well as annual impact assessment by those who are deploying AI systems. This act is to be enforced by the Colorado attorney general.55 Virginia’s HB 2094 is said to be built on similar grounds.56 In pre-existing regulations on automated decision-making technology (ADMT), such as regulations on risk assessment and ADMT per the California Privacy Protection Agency, would also apply to AI. To evaluate and implement AI tools on a broad scale, acute care surgeons must be aware of existing policies and involved with providing recommendations for policies on the responsible use of AI in healthcare. Similarly, AB 3030 is legislation in California that surrounds disclosures in the use of generative AI in patient communications in healthcare and took effect in January 2025.57 58 Although most of the legislation created clarity regarding what is required specifically in the use of generative AI in healthcare settings, there are concerns that remain regarding the interpretations and enforcement of these laws. Simply put, the law has not kept pace with the rapid healthcare AI technology development cycle, and thus, this amplifies the need for surgeons and other users to be systematic and thoughtful in the application of the technology being brought forward.

Public perception of our use of new AI technology further underscores the need for robust evaluation of the AI tools being brought to market. Surgeons inherently enter a trust-based partnership with our patients who expect that we provide care that is as reasonable and safe as possible. This relationship is a profoundly important part of our field. Yet, the public remains skeptical of AI technology, especially for increasingly higher-risk uses. In a survey study of a nationally representative sample of US adults in 2023, most respondents had low trust in healthcare systems to responsibly use AI (65.8%) and ensure that it does not lead to patient harm (57.7%).59 According to another survey study, 60% of Americans reported that they would feel uncomfortable if their own healthcare provider relied on AI to diagnose or recommend treatments.60 Although these perceptions may be evolving as patients encounter more AI uses in healthcare, the potential for lack of trust is particularly important for surgeons. In evaluating and implementing AI in our practices and healthcare systems, acute care surgeons must continue to balance the principles of protecting human autonomy, safety, and well-being. As clinicians, we are tasked with de-risking AI solutions and tools before bringing them to our patients. To safely implement AI tools in healthcare, we must build infrastructure and processes that are centered around increasing quality and trust.

Governance of healthcare AI

AI governance has most recently been defined as an entity comprising processes, guidelines, and specialized personnel or subject matter experts that are dedicated to guiding the ideation, development, deployment, and continuous monitoring of trustworthy and beneficial AI solutions.59 The value of AI governance is foremost that it builds trust with all users and those impacted, including patients, providers, administration, and the community. It enables vetting and authorization of AI tools more quickly in a transparent, replicable fashion, while reducing the risk of unexpected harm. The AI governance body must continuously stay up to date to ensure compliance with regulations and laws. Overall, the goal of the AI governance body is to ensure the safe and trustworthy implementation and ongoing monitoring of AI systems. Recently, governance groups at local institutions are also being called on by administrators to play a role in strategic decision-making into which AI clinical tool should be adopted when multiple options exist for the same purpose.15 Each healthcare system with the intent to deploy AI tools should consider adopting an AI governance framework to safely evaluate and implement healthcare AI solutions.

An adoption-centered governance framework for healthcare focuses on three major components: (1) who are the entities and stakeholders that need to be continuously involved, (2) what key domains and barriers need oversight, and (3) what frameworks will be used to ensure a safe, trustworthy implementation of a healthcare AI solution.59 To ensure fairness and transparency, all entities associated with each of the oversight domains and impacted stakeholders should be identified and involved to oversee the solution’s development. This may include leaders from privacy, regulatory, legal/risk, data governance, ethics, equity, strategy, clinical, patient advocacy, and AI/innovation teams.

Subject matter experts must be used to provide oversight based on their area of expertise, including but not limited to model performance, data science, bias, and ethics. An example of this is the analytics oversight committee at the University of California, Davis, which is made up of representatives from nursing, physicians, and researchers.61 They have developed a framework that aims to mitigate risks and enable the responsible implementation of health AI on behalf of patients and communities. In this framework, the AI model is assessed for strategic alignment based on whether it is specific, measurable, aligned, realistic, and transformative (S.M.A.R.T. criteria). Once these prerequisites are met, the model would then be assessed based on whether it is safe, accurate, fair/unbiased, and evidence-based (S.A.F.E. criteria). Similarly, oversight at other institutions should include processes to track and mitigate bias, while ensuring fairness, transparency, responsibility, robustness, privacy, and security. Through all stages of AI development, from the concept, model development, implementation, and post-deployment monitoring, the AI governance framework and oversight can be applied to ensure the AI solution’s safe, long-term use. This framework can serve as a template for healthcare organizations to use and modify to best fit their local and cultural needs.

To ensure the safety and continued effectiveness of the AI solution after implementation, the importance of a post-monitoring and mitigation plan cannot be overemphasized. Processes must include assessment tools and databases to keep a meticulous record of risks and barriers of an AI solution. The governing body must also work with regulatory and legal entities at their institutions to align approaches with evolving laws and regulations that affect the use of AI solutions. The legal and regulatory entities will necessitate the clinical and subject matter expertise of stakeholder users to appropriately interpret and apply new oversight requirements. Data governance frameworks will need to include guidelines and policies to ensure privacy and data security. We think that it is crucial to have a governing body and framework in place to effectively guide the assessment, approval, deployment, and monitoring of AI technologies.

Conclusion

AI is rapidly evolving and has the potential to transform healthcare if implemented with appropriate rigor and oversight. For acute care surgeons, active engagement in evaluating both the risks and benefits of these technologies is essential given their direct impact on patient safety, quality, and outcomes. The framework presented in this review provides a practical roadmap for the evaluation, implementation, and governance of healthcare AI solutions by grounding adoption in clearly defined clinical use cases, rigorous pre-implementation assessment, and alignment with TAI principles. By incorporating risk mitigation strategies, multidisciplinary governance, and continuous post-deployment monitoring, this approach emphasizes patient safety, clinician trust, and real-world effectiveness. In high-risk, time-sensitive clinical environments, piloted deployment and ongoing evaluation are critical to ensuring AI systems function as intended before broader adoption. Use of this framework may help bridge the gap between AI innovation and its safe, sustainable integration into acute surgical practice.
