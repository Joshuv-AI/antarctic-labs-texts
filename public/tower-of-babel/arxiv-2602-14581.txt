Feedback Stabilization and Tracking for Heat Equations Using Thermo-Plasmonic Nanoparticles as Actuators
Arpan Mukherjee, Sérgio S. Rodrigues, Mourad Sini

Abstract
We propose and analyze a strategy for tracking prescribed heat profiles in a medium by using plasmonic nanoparticles as actuators. The starting point is a thermo–plasmonic model coupling Maxwell’s equations to a heat equation in a homogeneous background containing a discrete collection of highly contrasting nanoparticles. A recently derived time-domain discrete effective model in [ 8 ] shows that, outside the particle region, the heat generated by the nanoparticles is well approximated by a superposition of heat kernels centered at the particle locations, with time-dependent amplitudes solving a coupled Volterra system. Our first step is to recast this discrete thermo–plasmonic description as a heat equation with finitely many point actuators on a bounded control domain Ω \Omega . We then design and analyze a heat-profile tracking feedback law based on pointwise evaluations of 𝒜 − 1 ​ y \mathcal{A}^{-1}y , where 𝒜 = I − A 0 \mathcal{A}=I-A_{0} and A 0 A_{0} is the Neumann diffusion operator. The closed-loop dynamics is treated in the natural V ′ V^{\prime} framework, where V := D ⁡ ( 𝒜 ) V:=D(\mathcal{A}) , and we obtain exponential stabilization of the tracking error in V ′ V^{\prime} by relying on the distribution-actuator theory in [ 18 ] . For general (non-equilibrium) reference profiles we incorporate a constant feedforward and a fixed-point pre-compensation on a low-mode space X N X_{N} , which yields exact matching of the steady target on X N X_{N} together with an explicit estimate of the remaining tail mismatch. Finally, we couple the thermo–plasmonic effective model with the feedback design and quantify the discrepancy between the ideal closed-loop temperature and the physically realized one. The resulting tracking bound separates (i) a projection error due to restricting the abstract feedback input to a realizable finite-dimensional space and (ii) a thermo–plasmonic remainder that vanishes as the particle size δ → 0 \delta\to 0 . We also make explicit how plasmonic resonances enter the effective actuation map and how resonant tuning can improve control authority and conditioning. This provides a rigorous framework for using plasmonic nanoparticles as actuators for heat-profile tracking, with potential relevance for nano-localized photothermal therapy and temperature management in nano-engineered materials.



1
Introduction

1.1
Thermo-plasmonic heating and effective medium theory

Metallic (plasmonic) nanoparticles exhibit strong absorption and local field
enhancement when illuminated near their plasmonic resonances. This leads to
highly localized heat generation that can be exploited in a wide range of
applications, including photothermal cancer therapy, thermal imaging,
nano-manipulation and temperature management in micro- and nano-devices; see,
for instance,
[
5
,
6
,
10
,
16
,
17
,
32
]
. We discuss in Subsection
2.3
how resonant tuning enters the asymptotic modeling and improves the effective actuation strength.

From a modeling viewpoint, quantitative descriptions of light-to-heat
conversion in plasmonic systems typically couple a time-harmonic Maxwell
problem, governing the electromagnetic field, to a heat equation with
volumetric source term representing ohmic losses. In the physical and
engineering literature a variety of models have been proposed to describe the
temperature rise around single particles and suspensions of particles, often
relying on phenomenological or numerical approaches

[
5
,
6
,
28
,
33
]
. On
the mathematical side, asymptotic and effective-medium descriptions have been
developed for plasmonic nanoparticles in the quasi-static and subwavelength
regimes, with a particular emphasis on the role of resonances and high
contrast; see, e.g.,

[
1
,
2
,
8
,
25
]
and the references therein.

In
[
8
]
, a rigorous time-domain effective model for
heat generation by a finite collection of plasmonic nanoparticles in an
otherwise homogeneous background was obtained, under subwavelength and
high-contrast scaling assumptions. The main result shows that the temperature
difference
w
between the actual temperature and the background solution
without particles can be approximated, outside the particle region, by a
finite superposition of heat kernels centered at the nanoparticle centers,
with time-dependent amplitudes
\sigma_{i}
solving a Volterra-type integral
system. The error is quantified in suitable norms, and the amplitudes are
shown to depend linearly (up to higher-order terms) on the internal
electromagnetic energy generated by the incident illumination. This discrete
description is based on a Foldy–Lax point-approximation framework applied to
the underlying Maxwell scattering problem.

The representation of
w
as a superposition of heat kernels localized at the
particle centers is the key structural feature that we exploit here. It
suggests that, at least in the effective description, a suitably designed
ensemble of plasmonic nanoparticles can be interpreted as a system of point
actuators for the heat equation, remotely driven by electromagnetic inputs.

1.2
Heat-profile tracking and point actuators

Independently of the thermo–plasmonic context, the control and stabilization
of linear parabolic equations has been extensively studied; see, e.g.,

[
4
,
19
,
27
,
31
,
29
]

and the references therein. A particularly relevant class of problems for the
present work involves finite-dimensional actuation and observation, where a
heat equation is driven by a finite number of internal or boundary actuators
and observed through finitely many output functionals.

Pointwise (Dirac-type) actuation and observation models are natural
idealizations when the actual sources and sensors are localized on spatial
scales much smaller than the size of the domain. In the context of the heat
equation, controllability and stabilization issues for point actuators and for
controls acting on small or moving subsets have been investigated in various
settings; see, for instance,
[
9
,
14
,
15
,
21
,
24
,
30
]
and the references
therein. The analysis of such systems typically relies on semigroup methods,
spectral decompositions and Carleman estimates.

In the present paper, we consider a controlled heat equation on a bounded
domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, mainly
d=3
, with homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions, driven by finitely many point actuators. We design an output feedback based on

elliptically regularized
point measurements, namely pointwise
evaluations of
\mathcal{A}^{-1}y
, where
\mathcal{A}=I-A_{0}
and
A_{0}
is the
Neumann diffusion operator. For a prescribed reference profile
y_{r}
, the
feedback has the form

u_{j}(t)=(u_{r})_{j}-\lambda\Big[(\mathcal{A}^{-1}y)(x_{j},t)-(\mathcal{A}^{-1}y_{r})(x_{j})\Big],\qquad j=1,\dots,M,

where
\lambda>0
is a gain and
u_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
is a constant feedforward used to
compensate the fact that non-constant references are not equilibria of the
free Neumann heat dynamics (and which is further refined via a fixed-point
pre-compensation on low modes; see Section
5.4
).
Working in the
V^{\prime}
framework, with
V:=D(\mathcal{A})
, we use the stabilization theory in
[
18
]
to obtain exponential convergence of the tracking
error in
V^{\prime}
for this class of feedbacks; see Section
5
.

1.3
Objective and main contributions

The goal of this work is to connect rigorously the discrete thermo–plasmonic
effective model of
[
8
]
with heat-profile tracking
feedback laws for a heat equation with point actuators of
[
18
]
to propose and analyse a strategy for tracking prescribed heat profiles in a medium by using plasmonic nanoparticles as actuators. We achieve this goal with the following steps.

(i)

We first recall the discrete thermo–plasmonic
effective model of
[
8
]
, in Section
2
, and then recast it as a heat equation
on a bounded domain driven by finitely many point actuators, in a
functional-analytic setting suitable for control design. In particular, we
identify the effective point-source amplitudes in terms of the Volterra system
for the nanoparticle amplitudes and provide a quantitative justification for
restricting the whole-space heat propagation in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
to a bounded Neumann
control domain on compact subsets away from
\partial\Omega
(Lemma
3.1
). Precisely, the temperature field satisfies in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

\partial_{t}y-\kappa_{m}\Delta y=Q_{\delta}(p),

where
Q_{\delta}(p)
is the nanoparticle-induced volumetric source supported in
\delta
-type sources located of the particle centers. Upon restriction to a bounded Neumann control domain
\Omega
containing the actuator locations
\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}
, we recast the dynamics as

\dot{y}=A_{0}y+Bu,\qquad Bu=\sum_{j=1}^{M}u_{j}\,\delta_{x_{j}},

posed in the extrapolation space
V^{\prime}
, where
V:=D(\mathcal{A})
, with
\mathcal{A}=I-A_{0}
. Then, in Section
3
, we establish well-posedness of this problem improving the related results in
[
18
]
, allowing more general shapes
\Omega
with
C^{1,1}
-regularity; see Proposition
3.4
.

(ii)

We state, in Section
4
, the feedback design and stability analysis by projecting into finite-dimensional spaces with a control of the tails. Then, in Section
5
, we establish well-posedness and basic stability properties of a
feedback law for heat-profile tracking based on pointwise evaluations of

\mathcal{A}^{-1}y
, interpreted as a closed-loop parabolic system with point
actuators. We place the closed loop in the distribution-actuator framework of

[
18
]
in the state space
V^{\prime}
and obtain exponential
convergence to an equilibrium. For non-equilibrium target profiles we
incorporate a feedforward term and a fixed-point pre-compensation on a low-mode
space
X_{N}
, yielding exact matching of the first
N
Neumann modes of the
steady reached profile and an explicit tail estimate; see
Section
5.4
. Namely, the feedback uses the elliptically regularized point-evaluation operator

C:V^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R}^{M},\qquad Cz=\big((\mathcal{A}^{-1}z)(x_{j})\big)_{j=1}^{M},

and, for references
y_{r}\in X_{N}:=\mathrm{span}\{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N}\}
, takes the form

u(t)=u_{r}-\lambda\,C\big(y(t)-y_{r}\big),

with
u_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
chosen so that
P_{N}(A_{0}y_{r}+Bu_{r})=0
.

(iii)

We couple the effective thermo–plasmonic model with the feedback
design and quantify the difference between the ideal closed-loop
temperature (in the abstract control model) and the temperature realized
physically through plasmonic actuation, see Section
6
. At this level, we obtain a tracking
bound that separates a projection error (restriction of the abstract feedback
to a realizable finite-dimensional control space) from a thermo–plasmonic
modeling remainder that vanishes as
\delta\to 0
, where
\delta
is the maximum radius of the nanoparticles, and we explain how plasmonic
resonances enter the actuation map and can be exploited to improve control
authority and conditioning (Subsection
2.3
). Precisely, at the level of the thermo-plasmonic effective model, the nanoparticle-induced heat input admits the representation

G_{\delta}(p)=Kp+R_{\delta}p,\qquad\|R_{\delta}\|_{\mathcal{L}(U,L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{M}))}\xrightarrow{\,\delta\to 0\,}0,

where
K\in\mathcal{L}\bigl(U,L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{M})\bigr)
is a bounded linear actuation operator mapping illumination controls to effective point-source amplitudes.

(iv)

In Section
7
, we derive robustness bounds that quantify the effect of implementing the feedback only approximately. More precisely, if the realized heat input differs from the ideal Dirac-actuator feedback by a discrepancy term in
D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime}
, then the induced tracking deviation is controlled in
C([0,T];V^{\prime})\cap L^{2}(0,T;H)
on finite horizons, with an additional
L^{2}(\Omega)
estimate on
[t_{0},T]
for any
t_{0}>0
.

(v)

In Section
8
, we explain how the main tracking theorem translates back to the original thermo–plasmonic control problem. The resulting estimate separates (a) the abstract closed-loop tracking error, (b) a projection error due to restricting the feedback input to a realizable subspace
Y_{0}
, and (c) a thermo–plasmonic realization remainder that vanishes as
\delta\to 0
.

In particular, the implemented tracking error admits a decomposition of the form

y-y_{r}=\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{proj}}+\mathcal{E}_{\delta},

where
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{proj}}
corresponds to restricting the ideal feedback input to a realizable subspace
Y_{0}
, and
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}
is induced by the thermo–plasmonic remainder.

(vi)

In Section
9
, we provide an algorithmic design that turns the theory into a computable feedback loop: we build an implementable feedback law (including the feedforward and fixed-point correction), identify/assemble a finite-dimensional representation of the thermo–plasmonic map
\mathcal{K}
on
U_{0}\to Y_{0}
, and use a (pseudo-)inverse to recover admissible incident-field intensities that realize the desired reduced heat input.

(vii)

Finally, in Section
10
, as an appendix, we collect the technical ingredients used throughout the analysis, including verifications of the standing spectral and actuator assumptions (on
C^{1,1}
domains) and sufficient conditions ensuring solvability of the fixed-point correction for general reference profiles.

The main message is that—under the scaling and smallness assumptions
guaranteeing the validity of the discrete thermo–plasmonic model, and under
standard controllability-type conditions on the actuator locations and on the illumination patterns—it is meaningful to interpret a configuration of
plasmonic nanoparticles as a system of actuators for the heat equation. By exciting the nanoparticles with suitably designed electromagnetic incident fields, one
can approximate abstract feedback laws and hence track a desired heat profile. The strategy yields exact matching of the desired profile in a prescribed finite-dimensional spectral subspace (by projection) and an explicit estimate of the remaining tail mismatch; see Section
5.4
.

We finish this introduction by mentioning two idealizations that are used in the feedback design. First, the coefficient vector
p(t):=(p_{l}(t))^{m}_{l=1}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is treated as a signed control acting linearly through the illumination dictionary (coming from the electromagnetic fields generated by the injected nanoparticles), but physically, throught the electromagnetism model, it appears of the form
p_{\ell}(t)=|a_{\ell}(t)|^{2}\geq 0
(where
a_{l}
are related to the electric field intensity).
This can be handled by restricting to effective inputs attainable with
p\in(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{m}
, see Remark
6.3
for more details.
Second, the output map is taken as the elliptically regularized point evaluation
Cz=\big((A^{-1}z)(x_{j})\big)_{j=1}^{M}
, which is well-defined on the natural state space for Dirac-actuated heat dynamics and enables stability estimates in
V^{\prime}
(and hence in
L^{2}
for
t\geq t_{0}>0
).
However, in practice, this corresponds to one more step of reconstructing these regularized outputs from physically available measurements (as those collected as local averages or only near the boundary of
\Omega
, for instance). These two issues, i.e. incorporating explicit nonnegativity and realistic sensing, will be discussed in a forthcoming work.

Thermo-plasmonic model and discrete effective description

We briefly recall the thermo–plasmonic model and the discrete effective
description from
[
8
]
. We only state the parts needed
for the control analysis; the reader is referred to

[
8
]
for details and precise assumptions.

2.1
Coupled Maxwell–heat model in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

The physical space is
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and the medium consists of a homogeneous
background characterized by constant volumetric heat capacity
c_{m}>0
,
thermal conductivity
\gamma_{m}>0
, and permittivity
\varepsilon_{m}>0
, together with

M
plasmonic nanoparticles

D_{i}:=z_{i}+\delta B_{i},\qquad i=1,\dots,M,

where
z_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
are the centers,
\delta\ll 1
is a small scaling
parameter, and the shapes
B_{i}
are bounded
C^{2}
domains with

\mathrm{vol}(B_{i})\sim 1
. The particle material is characterized by

c_{p},\gamma_{p},\varepsilon_{p}
and we denote the piecewise-constant coefficients

c_{v}:=c_{p}\chi_{D}+c_{m}\chi_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus D},\quad\gamma:=\gamma_{p}\chi_{D}+\gamma_{m}\chi_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus D},\quad\varepsilon:=\varepsilon_{p}\chi_{D}+\varepsilon_{m}\chi_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus D},

where
D:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{M}D_{i}
.

The electric field
E
solves a time-harmonic Maxwell scattering problem at
frequency
k
,

\begin{cases}\operatorname{curl}\operatorname{curl}E-k^{2}\varepsilon E=0&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{3},\\
E=E^{\mathrm{in}}+E^{\mathrm{sc}},\\
E^{\mathrm{sc}}\ \text{satisfies the Silver--M\"{u}ller condition,}\end{cases}

(2.1)

for a given incident field
E^{\mathrm{in}}
. The choice of
\varepsilon_{p}

follows a Drude model and the frequency
k
is chosen near a plasmonic
resonance; see
[
2
,
8
,
25
]
.

The temperature
u=u(x,t)
is governed by a heat equation for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and

t>0
:

\begin{cases}c_{v}(x)\,\partial_{t}u-\nabla\cdot(\gamma(x)\nabla u)=J(x,t)&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{3}\times(0,T),\\[3.00003pt]
u(x,0)=0&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{3},\end{cases}

(2.2)

where the source term represents volumetric heating due to ohmic losses,

J(x,t):=\frac{k}{2\pi}\Im\varepsilon(x)\,|E(x)|^{2}f(t),

(2.3)

with a causal temporal profile
f\in H^{r+1/2}_{0,\sigma}(0,T)
and where
\Im\varepsilon
denotes the imaginary part of the permittivity
\varepsilon
, which is taken positive valued and supported on the nanoparticles, meaning that the background medium (outside the cluster of nanoparticles) is lossless; see

[
8
]
for more details and the precise functional framework. The real valued
constants

\kappa_{m}:=\frac{c_{m}}{\gamma_{m}},\qquad\kappa_{p}:=\frac{c_{p}}{\gamma_{p}}

denote the diffusion constants in the background and inside the particles.

The main object of interest is the temperature difference

w:=u-u^{(0)}
, where
u^{(0)}
is the solution of the homogeneous-background
problem (i.e. without particles). This difference satisfies a parabolic
transmission problem with source terms supported in the nanoparticle region

D
.

2.2
Discrete thermo-plasmonic effective model

We denote by
\Phi^{(m)}(x,t;y,\tau)
the fundamental solution of the
homogeneous heat operator
\partial_{t}-\kappa_{m}\Delta
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
:

\Phi^{(m)}(x,t;y,\tau)=\frac{1}{\big(4\pi\kappa_{m}(t-\tau)\big)^{3/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{4\kappa_{m}(t-\tau)}\right)\chi_{(0,\infty)}(t-\tau).

(2.4)

Under subwavelength, separation and high-contrast assumptions, the main
effective result in
[
8
]
can be summarized as follows.

Theorem 2.1

(Discrete thermo-plasmonic effective model)
.

Assume the scaling and regularity hypotheses of
[
8
]
,
in particular the subwavelength, separation, and high-contrast conditions on

(c_{p},\gamma_{p},\varepsilon_{p})
and the nanoparticles
D_{i}=z_{i}+\delta B_{i}
.
Then there exist functions

\sigma_{i}\in H^{1}(0,T),\qquad i=1,\dots,M,

such that the temperature difference
w=u-u^{(0)}
satisfies, for

x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus D
and
t\in(0,T)
,

w(x,t)=-\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{\alpha_{i}}{c_{m}}\int_{0}^{t}\Phi^{(m)}(x,t;z_{i},\tau)\,\sigma_{i}(\tau)\,\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\mathcal{E}(x,t),

(2.5)

where
\alpha_{i}:=\gamma_{p,i}-\gamma_{m}
denotes the heat-conductivity
contrast, and the error
\mathcal{E}
admits the bound

\left\|\mathcal{E}(\cdot,t)\right\|_{L^{2}(K)}\leq CM\delta^{\mu},\qquad t\in[0,T],

for each compact
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus D
, for some
\mu>0
depending on
the scaling exponents and on
K
.

Moreover, the amplitudes
\sigma_{i}
solve a Volterra integral system of the
form

\sigma_{i}(t)+\sum_{j\neq i}\beta_{ij}\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{t}\Phi^{(m)}(z_{i},t;z_{j},\tau)\,\sigma_{j}(\tau)\,\,\mathrm{d}\tau=F_{i}(t)+r_{i}(t),

(2.6)

where the interaction coefficients
\beta_{ij}
depend on the geometry and
contrast parameters, the forcing terms
F_{i}
depend linearly on the internal
electromagnetic energy
|E|^{2}
in
D_{i}
, and the remainders
r_{i}
satisfy

\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left\|r_{i}\right\|_{H^{1}(0,T)}\leq CM\delta^{\mu^{\prime}}

for some
\mu^{\prime}>0
. The system (
2.6
) is uniquely
solvable in
(H^{1}(0,T))^{M}
and the
\sigma_{i}
satisfy uniform a priori
bounds in terms of the
F_{i}
.

Remark 2.2
.

The precise exponents
\mu,\mu^{\prime}
and the constants in the above estimates
depend on the detailed asymptotic regime chosen in
[
8
]
.
For the control-oriented discussion below, it is enough to retain that the
errors vanish as
\delta\to 0
and that the
\sigma_{i}
are uniformly bounded
in
H^{1}(0,T)
under the considered scaling regime.

2.3
Plasmonic resonances and actuation strength

The efficiency of the actuation mechanism is based on the magnitude of the internal
electromagnetic energy
\int_{D_{i}}|E|^{2}
entering the forcing terms
F_{i}
in
(
2.6
). This quantity is strongly amplified when the
illumination frequency is tuned near a
plasmonic resonance
of the nanoparticles.
We recall below how resonances enter the modeling in the language of the
Maxwell–Lippmann–Schwinger formulation, based on the magnetization operator and
the Helmholtz decomposition as in
[
8
]
.

Magnetization operator and resonant resolvent.

In the time-harmonic Maxwell problem (
2.1
) with piecewise-constant
permittivity
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{p}\chi_{D}+\varepsilon_{m}\chi_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus D}
, the electric field
satisfies a Maxwell–Lippmann–Schwinger equation of the form

E(x)+\eta\sum_{j=1}^{M}M^{(k)}_{D_{j}}[E](x)-k^{2}\eta\sum_{j=1}^{M}N^{(k)}_{D_{j}}[E](x)=E^{\mathrm{in}}(x),\qquad x\in D,

(2.7)

where
\eta:=\varepsilon_{p}-\varepsilon_{m}
and, with
G^{(k)}
the Helmholtz Green’s function,

M^{(k)}_{D}[f](x):=\nabla\int_{D}\nabla G^{(k)}(x,y)\cdot f(y)\,dy,\qquad N^{(k)}_{D}[f](x):=\int_{D}G^{(k)}(x,y)\,f(y)\,dy.

To analyze (
2.7
) in each particle
D_{i}=\delta B_{i}+z_{i}
, we use
the Helmholtz decomposition

L^{2}(D_{i})^{3}=H_{0}(\mathrm{div}\,0,D_{i})\oplus H_{0}(\mathrm{curl}\,0,D_{i})\oplus\nabla\mathrm{Harm}(D_{i}),

(2.8)

and denote by
\mathbb{P}^{(3)}
the orthogonal projector onto
\nabla\mathrm{Harm}(D_{i})
.
In the subwavelength regime
\delta k\ll 1
, the dominant amplification occurs on

\nabla\mathrm{Harm}(D_{i})
, where the quasi-static magnetization operator

M^{(0)}_{B_{i}}:\nabla\mathrm{Harm}(B_{i})\to\nabla\mathrm{Harm}(B_{i})
admits an
orthonormal eigenbasis
\{e^{(3)}_{i,n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with eigenvalues

\{\lambda^{(3)}_{i,n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, so that

M^{(0)}_{B_{i}}[e^{(3)}_{i,n}]=\lambda^{(3)}_{i,n}\,e^{(3)}_{i,n}.

Projecting (
2.7
) onto
\nabla\mathrm{Harm}(D_{i})
and scaling

D_{i}
to
B_{i}
yields, at leading order,

\bigl(I+\eta M^{(0)}_{B_{i}}\bigr)\,\mathbb{P}^{(3)}\widehat{E}_{i}\;\approx\;\mathbb{P}^{(3)}\widehat{E}^{\mathrm{in}}_{i}\;+\;\text{(particle--particle interaction terms)}.

(2.9)

Consequently, the modal coefficients satisfy

\langle\mathbb{P}^{(3)}\widehat{E}_{i},e^{(3)}_{i,n}\rangle\;\approx\;\frac{1}{1+\eta\lambda^{(3)}_{i,n}}\,\Big(\langle\mathbb{P}^{(3)}\widehat{E}^{\mathrm{in}}_{i},e^{(3)}_{i,n}\rangle+\cdots\Big).

Plasmonic resonances correspond to frequencies (through
\varepsilon_{p}(k)
, hence
\eta
)
for which a
dispersion relation

1+\eta\lambda^{(3)}_{i,n}=0
is (approximately)
satisfied for some index
n=n_{0}
. In the asymptotic regime used in

[
8
]
, the frequency is chosen so that

|1+\eta\lambda^{(3)}_{i,n}|\sim\begin{cases}\delta^{h},&n=n_{0},\\
1,&n\neq n_{0},\end{cases}

(2.10)

which implies the resolvent growth

\|(I+\eta M^{(0)}_{B_{i}})^{-1}\|_{\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(B_{i})^{3})}\lesssim\delta^{-h}
on

\nabla\mathrm{Harm}(B_{i})
and yields the near-field enhancement

\|\mathbb{P}^{(3)}E\|_{L^{2}(D_{i})}\sim\delta^{3/2-h}
(and similarly in
L^{4}
), up to
interaction corrections.

Polarization tensor and absorbed power.

The effective electromagnetic coupling entering the discrete heat model
(
2.6
) is encoded by the polarization matrix
P_{D_{i}}
appearing
in the Foldy–Lax system for the projected moments of
E
(see
[
8
]
).
In the magnetization-operator spectral variables, one has the representation

P_{D_{i}}\;=\;\delta^{3}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{1}{1+\eta\lambda^{(3)}_{i,n}}\left(\int_{B_{i}}e^{(3)}_{i,n}(\xi)\,d\xi\right)\otimes\left(\int_{B_{i}}e^{(3)}_{i,n}(\xi)\,d\xi\right)\;+\;O(\delta^{3}),

(2.11)

so that, under (
2.10
), the dominant resonant contribution scales like

P_{D_{i}}\sim\delta^{3-h}P_{B_{i}}
for an explicitly computable shape-dependent matrix

P_{B_{i}}
.
Since the ohmic source term in (
2.2
) is

J(x,t)=\frac{k}{2\pi}\Im\varepsilon(x)\,|E(x)|^{2}f(t)
, the total absorbed power in
D_{i}
satisfies

Q_{i}\;:=\;\frac{k}{2\pi}\Im\varepsilon_{p}(k)\int_{D_{i}}|E(x)|^{2}\,dx\;\approx\;\Im\varepsilon_{p}(k)\,\delta^{3-2h}\,\mathfrak{A}_{i}\,\big|E^{\mathrm{in}}(z_{i})\big|^{2},

(2.12)

with an amplification factor
\mathfrak{A}_{i}
determined by the resonant mode(s) in
(
2.11
) and by the inter-particle Foldy–Lax coupling.
In particular, tuning the illumination near resonance increases
Q_{i}
by orders of
magnitude compared to off-resonant operation.

Consequences for control conditioning and tracking.

In the control reduction developed below, the finite-dimensional map from
illumination parameters
p\in U_{0}
to effective heat inputs
u\in Y_{0}
is encoded by
the matrix/operator
K_{0}
(Section
6
). The resonant scaling

P_{D_{i}}\sim\delta^{3-h}
increases the gain of each actuator channel and typically
improves the conditioning of
K_{0}
(larger effective singular values), which:

•

reduces amplification in the right-inverse
K_{0}^{\dagger}
needed to realize a
prescribed
u_{\mathrm{des}}\in Y_{0}
,

•

improves robustness with respect to modeling and measurement errors, and

•

allows one to enrich the realizable control space
Y_{0}
(hence reduce projection
error) for a fixed admissible illumination energy.

Thus, plasmonic resonances are not only the physical origin of strong local heating,
but also a mathematical lever to strengthen actuation and improve tracking accuracy.

3
Heat equation with point actuators on a bounded domain

We now introduce the abstract control model for the heat equation with point
actuators, in a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
. This section is purely
functional-analytic and independent of the thermo–plasmonic model, except
for the fact that the background diffusion coefficient is
\kappa_{m}
.

3.1
Physical model and abstract reformulation

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a bounded, connected,
C^{2}
domain. The restriction
of the background heat equation to
\Omega
(with homogeneous Neumann boundary
conditions) reads

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}y-\kappa_{m}\Delta y=\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{M}u_{j}(t)\,\delta_{x_{j}}&\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\[3.00003pt]
\partial_{\nu}y=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\[3.00003pt]
y(\cdot,0)=y_{0}&\text{in }\Omega.\end{cases}

(3.1)

Lemma 3.1

(Restriction from
\mathbb{R}^{3}
to a bounded control domain)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a bounded
C^{2}
domain and let
B\Subset\Omega
be an
open set satisfying
\mathrm{dist}(B,\partial\Omega)=:d>0
. Consider a source
term
f(\cdot,t)
supported in
B
for each
t\in(0,T)
and let
w
be the
whole-space solution of

\partial_{t}w-\kappa_{m}\Delta w=f\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{3}\times(0,T),\qquad w(\cdot,0)=0,

while
y
solves the Neumann problem

\partial_{t}y-\kappa_{m}\Delta y=f\quad\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),\qquad\partial_{\nu}y=0\ \text{on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\qquad y(\cdot,0)=0.

Then, for all
t\in(0,T]
,

\|w(t)-y(t)\|_{L^{2}(B)}\leq C\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{-3/4}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{c\,d^{2}}{t-\tau}\Big)\,\|f(\tau)\|_{L^{2}(B)}\,\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

(3.2)

where
C,c>0
depend only on
\Omega
,
B
and
\kappa_{m}
. In particular,

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|w(t)-y(t)\|_{L^{2}(B)}\leq C\,\exp\!\Big(-\frac{c\,d^{2}}{T}\Big)\,\|f\|_{L^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(B))}.

(3.3)

Proof sketch.

Both solutions admit Duhamel representations in terms of the whole-space heat
kernel
\Phi^{(m)}
and the Neumann heat kernel
G_{\Omega}
on
\Omega
.
For
x\in B
one has

(w-y)(x,t)=\int_{0}^{t}\!\int_{B}\big(\Phi^{(m)}(x,t;\xi,\tau)-G_{\Omega}(x,\xi,t-\tau)\big)\,f(\xi,\tau)\,\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

Standard Gaussian bounds for
G_{\Omega}
and off-diagonal estimates for the
difference
G_{\Omega}-\Phi^{(m)}
when
x,\xi\in B
yield

|\Phi^{(m)}(x,t;\xi,\tau)-G_{\Omega}(x,\xi,t-\tau)|\leq C(t-\tau)^{-3/2}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{c\,d^{2}}{t-\tau}\Big),

which implies (
3.2
) by Young’s inequality. Estimate
(
3.3
) follows by bounding the convolution kernel on

(0,T]
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Lemma
3.1
provides a quantitative justification
for replacing the whole-space heat propagation by a bounded-domain Neumann
model when actuators, nanoparticles and the region of interest are contained
in a subdomain
B
at positive distance from the boundary. In particular, for
time horizons
T\ll d^{2}
the restriction error is exponentially small in

d^{2}/T
.

3.2
Dirac actuators and low-regularity well-posedness

We refer to
[
18
]
for distribution-type actuator frameworks
in closely related parabolic stabilization settings.

We set

H:=L^{2}(\Omega),\qquad A_{0}y:=\kappa_{m}\Delta y,\quad D(A_{0}):=\{y\in H^{2}(\Omega):\partial_{\nu}y=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega\}.

(3.4)

Then
A_{0}
generates an analytic contraction semigroup

S(t):H\to H
for
t\geq 0
.

The control operator is defined by

B:\mathbb{R}^{M}\to D(A_{0})^{\prime},\qquad Bu:=\sum_{j=1}^{M}u_{j}\,\delta_{x_{j}}.

(3.5)

With this notation, (
3.1
) is equivalent to the abstract system

\dot{y}(t)=A_{0}y(t)+Bu(t),\qquad y(0)=y_{0}.

(3.6)

Let
H_{-1}
be the extrapolation space associated with
A_{0}
(completion of
H
with respect to

\|x\|_{H_{-1}}:=\|(I-A_{0})^{-1}x\|_{H}
).

Introduce the coercive elliptic operator

\mathcal{A}:=I-A_{0}=I-\kappa_{m}\Delta,\qquad D(\mathcal{A})=D(A_{0}),

which is an isomorphism
\mathcal{A}:D(\mathcal{A})\to H
and satisfies

D(\mathcal{A})\hookrightarrow H^{2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow C(\bar{\Omega})
.

Dirac actuators.

Fix
M\in\mathbb{N}
and actuator locations
x_{1},\dots,x_{M}\in\Omega
and define the distributions

d_{j}:=\delta_{x_{j}}\in D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime}\qquad(1\leq j\leq M).

Define the control operator

B:\mathbb{R}^{M}\to D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime},\qquad Bu:=\sum_{j=1}^{M}u_{j}\,d_{j}.

This
B
coincides with the operator in (
3.5
) (since
D(\mathcal{A})=D(A_{0})
).

Proposition 3.3

(Boundedness of
B
into
D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime}
)
.

There exists
C_{B}>0
such that

\|Bu\|_{D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime}}\leq C_{B}|u|_{\mathbb{R}^{M}}\qquad\forall u\in\mathbb{R}^{M}.

Proof.

For
\phi\in D(\mathcal{A})
we have

|\langle d_{j},\phi\rangle|=|\phi(x_{j})|\leq C\|\phi\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|\phi\|_{D(\mathcal{A})}
by Sobolev embedding and elliptic norm equivalence on

D(\mathcal{A})
. Hence

|\langle Bu,\phi\rangle|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{M}|u_{j}|\,|\phi(x_{j})|\leq C|u|_{\mathbb{R}^{M}}\|\phi\|_{D(\mathcal{A})}.

Taking the supremum over
\|\phi\|_{D(\mathcal{A})}=1
yields the claim.
∎

Low-regularity solution concept.

For
T>0
and a right-hand side
f\in L^{2}(0,T;D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime})
we consider

\dot{y}(t)=A_{0}y(t)+f(t)\quad\text{in }D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime},\qquad y(0)=y_{0}\in V^{\prime}.

(3.7)

A (weak/mild) solution is a function

y\in W(0,T;H,D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime}):=\{y\in L^{2}(0,T;H):\dot{y}\in L^{2}(0,T;D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime})\}

satisfying (
3.7
) in
D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime}
.

Proposition 3.4

(Well-posedness for Dirac-forced heat equation)
.

Let
T>0
,
y_{0}\in V^{\prime}
and
u\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{M})
. Set
f=Bu\in L^{2}(0,T;D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime})
.
Then (
3.7
) admits a unique solution
y\in W(0,T;H,D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime})
.
Moreover,

\|y\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H)}^{2}+\|y\|_{C([0,T];V^{\prime})}^{2}\leq C_{T}\Big(\|y_{0}\|_{V^{\prime}}^{2}+\|u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{M})}^{2}\Big),

(3.8)

for a constant
C_{T}
depending on
T
,
\Omega
and
\kappa_{m}
.

Proof.

We refer to
[
18
]

for a detailed proof strategy in the same distribution-actuator setting.
Estimate (
3.8
) follows from the corresponding a-priori estimate. Such estimates are established under few assumptions on the domain
\Omega
, in particular it was assumed that
\Omega
has a cubic form (or eventually convex). Such assumption is removed and replaced by a
C^{1,1}
-regularity, see Appendix
10.2
.
∎

4
Feedback design and stability analysis

In this section we design an output-based feedback law for the heat equation
with point actuators and show a stabilization / tracking result.

We work with the abstract system

\dot{y}(t)=A_{0}y(t)+Bu(t),\qquad y(0)=y_{0}\in H,

(4.1)

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a bounded
C^{2}
domain, In Sections
4
–
6
we work in dimension
d=3
and assume
\Omega
is
C^{2}
to match the thermo–plasmonic coupling; the purely abstract actuator verification in Appendix
10.2
actually only requires
C^{1,1}
regularity and applies in
d\in\{1,2,3\}
.

A_{0}
is the
Neumann diffusion operator defined in (
3.4
), and
B:\mathbb{R}^{M}\to D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime}

is the point-actuator operator defined in (
3.5
). We recall that
A_{0}

generates an analytic contraction semigroup
(S(t))_{t\geq 0}
on
H
and that

B\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime})

(Proposition
3.3
).

4.1
Closed-loop well-posedness

We start with the observation operator used in the feedback law.

Definition 4.1

(Observation operator)
.

Since
1\in\rho(A_{0})
, the resolvent
\mathcal{A}^{-1}=(I-A_{0})^{-1}
defines a bounded operator

H\to D(A_{0})\subset H^{2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow C(\overline{\Omega})
. We define

C:H\to\mathbb{R}^{M},\qquad(Cz)_{j}:=\big(\mathcal{A}^{-1}z\big)(x_{j}),\quad j=1,\dots,M.

(4.2)

Thus
C
maps the state
z
to the values of the regularized temperature

\mathcal{A}^{-1}z
at the actuator positions
x_{j}
.

We state
the closed-loop equation

\dot{y}(t)=A_{0}y(t)-\lambda BKC(y(t)-y_{r}),

(4.3)

or, in terms of
z(t):=y(t)-y_{r}
,

\dot{z}(t)=(A_{0}-\lambda BKC)z(t),\qquad z(0)=y_{0}-y_{r}.

(4.4)

Remark 4.2

(Closed-loop well-posedness in the
V^{\prime}
framework)
.

For any
T>0
,
y_{0}\in V^{\prime}
and
u\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{M})
, the forced heat equation
(
3.7
) admits a unique solution
y\in W(0,T;H,D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime})
and satisfies the
estimate (
3.8
); see Proposition
3.4
.
Moreover, the feedback closed loop (
4.4
) is well posed in
V^{\prime}
.

For a given integer
M\in\mathbb{N}
we introduce the low-mode subspace and
its projection.

Definition 4.3

(Finite-dimensional subspace and projection)
.

For each
M\in\mathbb{N}
, set

H_{M}:=\mathrm{span}\{\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{M}\}\subset H,

and let
P_{M}:H\to H_{M}
be the orthogonal projection onto
H_{M}
. We denote
by
H_{M}^{\perp}
the orthogonal complement of
H_{M}
in
H
and by

I-P_{M}:H\to H_{M}^{\perp}
the projection onto the tail.

We now write
C
in the eigenbasis and separate the contributions of the low
and high modes.

Lemma 4.4

(Matrix representation of
C
and low/high decomposition)
.

Let
z=\sum_{k\geq 1}\alpha_{k}\varphi_{k}\in H
. Then

\mathcal{A}^{-1}z=\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{\alpha_{k}}{1+\lambda_{k}}\,\varphi_{k},

and, for each
j=1,\dots,M
,

(Cz)_{j}=\sum_{k\geq 1}d_{jk}\,\alpha_{k},\qquad d_{jk}:=\frac{1}{1+\lambda_{k}}\,\varphi_{k}(x_{j}).

Define the (infinite) matrix
D=(d_{jk})_{1\leq j\leq M,\ k\geq 1}
. Then

Cz=D\alpha,\qquad\alpha:=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots)^{\top}\in\ell^{2}.

For the fixed
M
(number of actuators and low modes), decompose

D=(D_{MM}\;|\;D_{M>})
with

D_{MM}:=(d_{jk})_{j,k=1}^{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M},\qquad D_{M>}:=(d_{jk})_{1\leq j\leq M,\ k>M}.

Writing
\alpha=(\alpha_{M},\alpha_{>})
with

\alpha_{M}:=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{M})^{\top},\qquad\alpha_{>}:=(\alpha_{M+1},\alpha_{M+2},\dots)^{\top},

we obtain

Cz=D_{MM}\alpha_{M}+D_{M>}\alpha_{>}.

(4.5)

Remark 4.5
.

The matrix

\mathcal{B}:=(\varphi_{k}(x_{j}))_{j,k=1}^{M}

appearing in Assumption
4.7
below is related to
D_{MM}
by

D_{MM}=\mathcal{B}\,\mathrm{diag}\big((1+\lambda_{k})^{-1}\big)_{k=1}^{M}.

Hence if
\mathcal{B}
is invertible then
D_{MM}
is also invertible.

We use the compactness of the resolvent and the embedding
H^{2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow C(\overline{\Omega})
to show that the contribution of the high modes to the measurement
Cz
can be made arbitrarily small by choosing
N
large enough.

Lemma 4.6

(Compactness of the measurement operator and small tail)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a bounded
C^{2}
domain,
A_{0}
the Neumann
Laplacian as in (
3.4
), and
\{\varphi_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
an

L^{2}(\Omega)
–orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions of
-A_{0}
with eigenvalues

0=\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots
. Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{M}\in\Omega
be fixed
distinct points, and define
C
as in (
4.2
).

For
\alpha=(\alpha_{k})_{k\geq 1}\in\ell^{2}
, set
z=\sum_{k\geq 1}\alpha_{k}\varphi_{k}

and write
Cz=D\alpha
with
D=(d_{jk})_{j,k}
as in
Lemma
4.4
. For each
N\in\mathbb{N}
, decompose

D=(D_{MN}\mid D_{M>N})
where

D_{MN}:=(d_{jk})_{1\leq j\leq M,\ 1\leq k\leq N}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N},\quad D_{M>N}:=(d_{jk})_{1\leq j\leq M,\ k>N}.

Then the operator
T:\ell^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
defined by
T\alpha:=D\alpha
is
compact. In particular,

\|D_{M>N}\|_{\mathcal{L}(\ell^{2},\mathbb{R}^{M})}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}0.

(4.6)

Equivalently, for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
N_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}

such that for all
N\geq N_{\varepsilon}
and all

\alpha=(\alpha_{k})_{k\geq 1}\in\ell^{2}
,

\big\|D_{M>N}\alpha_{>N}\big\|_{\mathbb{R}^{M}}=\big\|C\Big(\sum_{k>N}\alpha_{k}\varphi_{k}\Big)\big\|_{\mathbb{R}^{M}}\leq\varepsilon\,\|\alpha\|_{\ell^{2}},

where
\alpha_{>N}:=(\alpha_{N+1},\alpha_{N+2},\dots)
.
In particular, by choosing the truncation index
N
sufficiently large, the
measurement of the high-frequency tail can be made arbitrarily small.

Proof.

Define

J:\ell^{2}\to H,\qquad J\alpha:=\sum_{k\geq 1}\alpha_{k}\varphi_{k}.

Since
\{\varphi_{k}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
H=L^{2}(\Omega)
,
J
is an
isometric isomorphism from
\ell^{2}
onto
H
.
Let
R:=\mathcal{A}^{-1}:H\to D(A_{0})\subset H^{2}(\Omega)
. Since

H^{2}(\Omega)
embeds continuously and compactly into
C(\overline{\Omega})
,
then the composite operator

R_{J}:=R\circ J:\ell^{2}\to C(\overline{\Omega})

is compact. The evaluation map

E:C(\overline{\Omega})\to\mathbb{R}^{M},\qquad(Ew)_{j}:=w(x_{j}),\quad j=1,\dots,M,

is bounded. Hence the measurement operator

T:=E\circ R_{J}:\ell^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{M}

is compact as a composition of a compact and a bounded operator.

By construction, for
\alpha\in\ell^{2}
and

z=J\alpha=\sum_{k\geq 1}\alpha_{k}\varphi_{k}
,

T\alpha=E(RJ\alpha)=Cz=D\alpha,

so
T
is represented by the matrix
D
in the basis
\{\varphi_{k}\}
.

Let
P_{N}:\ell^{2}\to\ell^{2}
be the orthogonal projection onto the first
N

coordinates:

P_{N}\alpha:=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{N},0,0,\dots).

Set
T_{N}:=TP_{N}
, which has finite rank. Since
T
is compact, we have

\|T-T_{N}\|_{\mathcal{L}(\ell^{2},\mathbb{R}^{M})}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}0.

On the other hand,

(T-T_{N})\alpha=T(I-P_{N})\alpha,

and
(I-P_{N})\alpha
has coordinates

(0,\dots,0,\alpha_{N+1},\alpha_{N+2},\dots)
, so

(T-T_{N})\alpha=D_{M>N}\,\alpha_{>N}.

Hence

\|T-T_{N}\|_{\mathcal{L}(\ell^{2},\mathbb{R}^{M})}=\|D_{M>N}\|_{\mathcal{L}(\ell^{2},\mathbb{R}^{M})},

which proves (
4.6
). The last statement is just the
operator norm inequality written explicitly.
∎

In what follows, the number
M
denotes the number of actuators/sensors (i.e. the dimension
of the control and observation space
\mathbb{R}^{M}
). For the spectral splitting we introduce
an independent truncation index
N\in\mathbb{N}
(typically
N\leq M
). We set

H_{N}:=\mathrm{span}\{\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{N}\}

and
P_{N}:H\to H_{N}
as in Definition
4.3
, and we treat
(I-P_{N})H
as the tail.
Lemma
4.6
shows that

\|C(I-P_{N})\|_{\mathcal{L}(H,\mathbb{R}^{M})}\longrightarrow 0\quad\text{as }N\to\infty;

hence, for any prescribed
\delta>0
, we can choose
N
so that

\|C(I-P_{N})\|_{\mathcal{L}(H,\mathbb{R}^{M})}\leq\delta.

4.2
Assumptions and feedback law

We now state the structural assumptions needed for the stabilization result.

Assumption 4.7

(Finite-dimensional subspace and rank condition)
.

Fix
M\in\mathbb{N}
and distinct actuator/sensor locations
x_{1},\dots,x_{M}\in\Omega
.
Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
be a truncation index (typically
N\leq M
) and let
H_{N}
and
P_{N}

be as in Definition
4.3
.

(i)

(Rank condition on the first
N
modes).

The
N\times M
matrix

\Phi_{N,M}:=\big(\varphi_{k}(x_{j})\big)_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq N\\
1\leq j\leq M\end{subarray}}

has full row rank
N
.
Equivalently, the
M\times N
matrix

D_{M,N}:=\Big(\frac{\varphi_{k}(x_{j})}{1+\lambda_{k}}\Big)_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq M\\
1\leq k\leq N\end{subarray}}

has full column rank
N
.

(ii)

(Reference in
H_{N}
).

The reference profile satisfies
y_{r}\in H_{N}
, i.e.

y_{r}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_{k}^{r}\,\varphi_{k}\quad\text{for some }\alpha_{k}^{r}\in\mathbb{R}.

Assumption
4.7
(i) is a controllability-type condition on the
configuration of actuator positions
x_{j}
with respect to the first
N

eigenfunctions
\varphi_{k}
. As discussed in the appendix (see the genericity and 1D/3D
examples), this condition is generic and can be enforced explicitly in simple
geometries.

The feedback law uses the full observation operator
C
as defined in
(
4.2
).

Definition 4.8

(Feedback law)
.

Let
K\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{M})
be a feedback gain. We
consider the static output feedback

u(t)=-K\,C\big(y(t)-y_{r}\big).

(4.7)

The closed-loop system for
y
is then

\dot{y}(t)=A_{0}y(t)-BKC\big(y(t)-y_{r}\big).

(4.8)

For the error variable
z(t):=y(t)-y_{r}
we have

\dot{z}(t)=A_{0}z(t)-BKCz(t),\qquad z(0)=z_{0}:=y_{0}-y_{r}.

(4.9)

Well-posedness of (
4.9
) follows as in
Proposition
3.4
, since
BKC\in\mathcal{L}(H,H_{-1})
.

Remark 4.9

(Finite-dimensional constructions versus stabilization)
.

A matrix-gain construction based on truncation to the first
N
eigenmodes can be used as a

heuristic
guideline for choosing actuator locations and tuning feedback parameters.
However, for Dirac actuators the rigorous closed-loop well-posedness and stabilization/tracking
results are most naturally formulated in the
V^{\prime}
framework. In this paper, the main stability
statement is Theorem
5.3
, which yields exponential decay in
V^{\prime}
for the
feedback (
5.4
) under Assumption
5.1
, and implies
H
–tracking
after any positive time
t_{0}>0
(see Corollary
5.4
and Section
7
).

5
Dirac-actuator stabilization and tracking in
V^{\prime}

We rewrite the tracking problem in terms of the error variable

z(t):=y(t)-y_{r}.

Throughout this section we allow non-constant reference profiles by assuming that
y_{r}
is a finite
linear combination of Neumann eigenfunctions. More precisely, for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
,

y_{r}\in X_{N}:=\mathrm{span}\{\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{N}\},\qquad y_{r}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_{k}^{r}\,\varphi_{k}.

(5.1)

Since such a
y_{r}
is not, in general, an equilibrium of the free heat dynamics, we introduce a constant
feedforward input
u_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
chosen so that the forcing
A_{0}y_{r}+Bu_{r}
is eliminated at the level of
the first
N
modes:

\langle A_{0}y_{r}+Bu_{r},\varphi_{k}\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}=0,\qquad k=1,\dots,N.

(5.2)

Equivalently,

\sum_{j=1}^{M}(u_{r})_{j}\,\varphi_{k}(x_{j})=-\langle A_{0}y_{r},\varphi_{k}\rangle_{H},\qquad k=1,\dots,N.

(5.3)

We assume that the
N\times M
matrix
(\varphi_{k}(x_{j}))_{k=1,\dots,N;\ j=1,\dots,M}
has full row rank,
so that (
5.3
) is solvable; when
M>N
we take
u_{r}
to be the minimum-norm
solution of this system.

5.1
Feedback law

Let
\mathcal{A}=I-A_{0}
and
d_{j}=\delta_{x_{j}}\in D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime}
as in Section
3.2
.
Define the (elliptically smoothed) point measurements

\big(Cz\big)_{j}:=\langle d_{j},\mathcal{A}^{-1}z\rangle_{D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime},D(\mathcal{A})}=\big(\mathcal{A}^{-1}z\big)(x_{j}),\qquad j=1,\dots,M.

We consider the feedback

u_{j}(t)=(u_{r})_{j}-\lambda\,(Cz(t))_{j},\qquad j=1,\dots,M,

(5.4)

with a scalar gain
\lambda>0
. Equivalently, the closed-loop error dynamics reads

\dot{z}(t)=A_{0}z(t)-\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{M}\langle d_{j},\mathcal{A}^{-1}z(t)\rangle\,d_{j}+g\quad\text{in }D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime},\qquad z(0)=z_{0}\in V^{\prime},

(5.5)

where the (time-independent) forcing term is

g:=A_{0}y_{r}+Bu_{r}\in D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime}.

(5.6)

Observe that, if we are only interested in equilibrium states, which means
A_{0}y_{r}=0
(and
\partial_{n}y_{r}=0
on
\partial\Omega
), then we take
u_{r}=0
so that
g=0
.

5.2
Actuator family condition

The stabilization mechanism requires a geometric/approximation condition on the actuators.
We adopt the abstract actuator hypothesis of
[
18
]

(Assumption 2.4 therein), specialized to Dirac actuators.

Assumption 5.1

(Actuator hypothesis
[
18
]
)
.

There exists an increasing sequence
M\mapsto M_{\sigma}
and, for each
M
, a family of

5.3

and an

consequence for

Theorem 5.3

(Exponential stabilization in
V^{\prime}
and tracking)
.

Assume Assumption
5.1
. For every decay rate
\mu>0
there exist
M
large enough
and
\lambda>0
large enough such that the
homogeneous
closed loop (i.e. (
5.5
)
with

) generates an exponentially stable semigroup
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{cl}}(t)
on
V^{\prime}
with

\|\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{cl}}(t)\|_{\mathcal{L}(V^{\prime})}\leq e^{-\mu t}
. Consequently, for the affine closed loop
(
5.5
) there exists an equilibrium
z_{\infty}\in V^{\prime}
given by

z_{\infty}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{cl}}(s)\,g\,\,\mathrm{d}s,

(5.7)

and the corresponding solution
z
satisfies

\|z(t)-z_{\infty}\|_{V^{\prime}}\leq e^{-\mu(t-s)}\|z(s)-z_{\infty}\|_{V^{\prime}}\qquad\forall t\geq s\geq 0.

(5.8)

Moreover, there exists

such that

\|z-z_{\infty}\|_{L^{2}((s,\infty);H)}\leq C_{\mu}\|z(s)-z_{\infty}\|_{V^{\prime}}\qquad\forall s\geq 0.

(5.9)

Proof.

For the homogeneous closed loop (
g=0
), this is a direct specialization of

[
18
, Theorem 3.1]
to the autonomous case
(
A_{rc}\equiv 0
) with

and Dirac actuators, which yields

\|\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{cl}}(t)\|_{\mathcal{L}(V^{\prime})}\leq e^{-\mu t}
.
For the affine system (

), the variation-of-constants formula gives

z(t)=\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{cl}}(t)z_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{cl}}(t-s)\,g\,\,\mathrm{d}s.

Since
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{cl}}
is exponentially stable on

, the improper integral in
(

) converges in

and defines an equilibrium

.
Moreover,

which implies (

). The

bound (

) follows from the
corresponding estimate applied to the homogeneous

.
∎

Corollary 5.4

(

)
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem

In particular, combining (

) with (

\sup_{t\geq t_{0}}\|z(t)-z_{\infty}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C(t_{0})\,C_{\mu}\,e^{-\mu t_{0}/2}\,\|z_{0}-z_{\infty}\|_{V^{\prime}}.

The bound (

[

or

[

∎

5.4
Fixed-point pre-compensation of the steady bias

The discussion above shows that for a non-equilibrium reference profile
y_{r}
the closed-loop error

z(t):=y(t)-y_{r}
converges to a (possibly nonzero) equilibrium

z_{\infty}
given by (
5.7
). This subsection provides a systematic way to compensate for this
bias by adjusting the
commanded
reference within the finite-dimensional space
X_{N}
introduced in
(
5.1
). The construction is based on a fixed-point equation.

The bias map.

Fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
and recall
X_{N}:=\mathrm{span}\{\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{N}\}
.
For any commanded reference
y^{\star}\in X_{N}
we define the feedforward
u_{r}(y^{\star})\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
as a
(possibly nonunique) solution of the moment system (
5.3
)–(
5.2
)
with
y_{r}
replaced by
y^{\star}
. In what follows we fix a
linear
selection

U_{N}:X_{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{M},\qquad u_{r}(y^{\star}):=U_{N}y^{\star},

(5.11)

for instance the minimum–Euclidean-norm choice, which is linear whenever
\Phi_{N,M}
has full row rank.
With this choice we associate to
y^{\star}
the residual (time-independent) forcing

g(y^{\star}):=A_{0}y^{\star}+B\,U_{N}y^{\star}\in D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime}.

(5.12)

By construction we have
P_{N}g(y^{\star})=0
(cancellation of the first
N
moments), and since

A_{0}y^{\star}\in X_{N}
this equivalently reads

g(y^{\star})=(I-P_{N})\,B\,U_{N}y^{\star}.

(5.13)

Let
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{cl}}(t)
denote the exponentially stable closed-loop semigroup associated with the
homogeneous loop on
V^{\prime}
(Theorem
5.3
). As used in (
5.7
), the affine loop with
forcing
g\in D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime}
admits an equilibrium
z_{\infty}\in V^{\prime}
given by the convergent integral

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{cl}}(s)\,g\,\,\mathrm{d}s
; see
[
18
]
.
Accordingly, we define the (bounded) operator

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}:D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime}\to V^{\prime},\qquad\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}h:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{cl}}(s)\,h\,\,\mathrm{d}s,

(5.14)

and denote by
C_{\mathrm{cl}}>0
any constant such that

\|\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}h\|_{V^{\prime}}\leq C_{\mathrm{cl}}\,\|h\|_{D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime}}\qquad\forall h\in D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime}.

(5.15)

Lemma 5.6

(Linearity and a priori bound for the steady bias)
.

The map

\mathcal{Z}_{N}:X_{N}\to V^{\prime},\qquad\mathcal{Z}_{N}(y^{\star}):=\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}\,g(y^{\star}),

(5.16)

is linear and bounded. Moreover, for all
y^{\star}\in X_{N}
,

\|\mathcal{Z}_{N}(y^{\star})\|_{V^{\prime}}\leq C_{\mathrm{cl}}\,\|g(y^{\star})\|_{D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime}}.

(5.17)

Proof.

Linearity follows from the linearity of
U_{N}
and
B
in (
5.12
) and the linearity of

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{cl}}
in (
5.14
). The bound (
5.17
) is (
10.22
).
∎

Remark 5.7

(Achieved steady field for a commanded reference)
.

For a commanded reference
y^{\star}\in X_{N}
we consider the shifted error
z:=y-y^{\star}
and apply the
feedback (
5.4
) with the corresponding feedforward
u_{r}(y^{\star})=U_{N}y^{\star}
.
Then the closed-loop error is affine with forcing
g(y^{\star})
and converges exponentially in
V^{\prime}
to the equilibrium

z_{\infty}(y^{\star})=\mathcal{Z}_{N}(y^{\star}),

so that the temperature field converges to

y_{\infty}(y^{\star}):=\lim_{t\to\infty}y(t)=y^{\star}+\mathcal{Z}_{N}(y^{\star})\qquad\text{in }V^{\prime}.

(5.18)

Fixed-point pre-compensation in
X_{N}
.

Given a desired target
y_{r}\in X_{N}
, we seek a commanded reference
y^{\star}\in X_{N}
such that the achieved
steady field (
5.18
) matches
y_{r}

on the low modes
. Since
X_{N}\subset V
we can use the

V^{\prime}
–
V
duality pairing to define the spectral projector
P_{N}
on
V^{\prime}
and

by

P_{N}\eta:=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\langle\eta,\varphi_{k}\rangle_{D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime},D(\mathcal{A})}\,\varphi_{k},\qquad\eta\in D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime}.

Define the projected bias operator

T_{N}:X_{N}\to X_{N},\qquad T_{N}y:=P_{N}\mathcal{Z}_{N}(y).

(5.19)

The compensated target is then determined by the linear fixed-point equation

y^{\star}+T_{N}y^{\star}=y_{r}\qquad\text{in }X_{N},

(5.20)

i.e.
(I+T_{N})y^{\star}=y_{r}
.

Proposition 5.8

(Existence/uniqueness and iterative construction)
.

Let
y_{r}\in X_{N}
.

1.

If
I+T_{N}:X_{N}\to X_{N}
is invertible, then (
5.20
) admits a unique solution

y^{\star}\in X_{N}
. For this choice of commanded reference one has

P_{N}y_{\infty}(y^{\star})=y_{r}.

(5.21)

2.

If, in addition,
\|T_{N}\|_{\mathcal{L}(X_{N})}<1
in some norm on
X_{N}
, then the Picard iteration

y^{(0)}:=y_{r},\qquad y^{(\ell+1)}:=y_{r}-T_{N}y^{(\ell)},\qquad\ell\geq 0,

(5.22)

converges geometrically in
X_{N}
to the unique solution
y^{\star}
of (
5.20
).

Proof.

(1) Since
X_{N}
is finite-dimensional, invertibility of
I+T_{N}
is equivalent to existence and uniqueness of the
solution
y^{\star}=(I+T_{N})^{-1}y_{r}
. The identity (
5.21
) follows from
(
5.18
) and (
5.20
):

P_{N}y_{\infty}(y^{\star})=P_{N}\bigl(y^{\star}+\mathcal{Z}_{N}(y^{\star})\bigr)=y^{\star}+T_{N}y^{\star}=y_{r}.

(2) The iteration (
5.22
) is the standard Banach fixed-point iteration for the contraction

F(y):=y_{r}-T_{N}y
on
X_{N}
.
∎

Corollary 5.9

(Exact low-mode tracking and a quantitative tail estimate)
.

Assume
y_{r}\in X_{N}
and let
y^{\star}\in X_{N}
be any solution of the fixed-point equation (
5.20
).
Define the corresponding steady reached profile by (
5.18
).
Then

P_{N}y_{\infty}(y^{\star})=y_{r},

(5.23)

and the full-field mismatch is a pure tail:

y_{\infty}(y^{\star})-y_{r}=(I-P_{N})\,y_{\infty}(y^{\star})=(I-P_{N})\,\mathcal{Z}_{N}(y^{\star}).

(5.24)

In particular,

\|y_{\infty}(y^{\star})-y_{r}\|_{V^{\prime}}\leq\|I-P_{N}\|\,C_{\mathrm{cl}}\,\|(I-P_{N})B\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime})}\,\|U_{N}\|\,\|y^{\star}\|_{X_{N}}.

(5.25)

If
I+T_{N}
is invertible, so that
y^{\star}=(I+T_{N})^{-1}y_{r}
, then (
5.25
) yields

\|y_{\infty}(y^{\star})-y_{r}\|_{V^{\prime}}\leq\|I-P_{N}\|\,C_{\mathrm{cl}}\,\|(I-P_{N})B\|\,\|U_{N}\|\,\|(I+T_{N})^{-1}\|\,\|y_{r}\|_{X_{N}}.

(5.26)

Moreover, if
\|T_{N}\|_{\mathcal{L}(X_{N})}<1
, then
\|(I+T_{N})^{-1}\|\leq(1-\|T_{N}\|)^{-1}
.

Proof.

The low-mode identity (
5.23
) is (
5.21
).
Since
y^{\star}\in X_{N}
, we have

, and therefore by (

(I-P_{N})\bigl(y_{\infty}(y^{\star})-y_{r}\bigr)=(I-P_{N})\bigl(y^{\star}+\mathcal{Z}_{N}(y^{\star})-y_{r}\bigr)=(I-P_{N})\mathcal{Z}_{N}(y^{\star}),

which is (
5.24
). Next, combining (

) with (

) gives

\|y_{\infty}(y^{\star})-y_{r}\|_{V^{\prime}}=\|(I-P_{N})\mathcal{Z}_{N}(y^{\star})\|_{V^{\prime}}\leq\|I-P_{N}\|\,\|\mathcal{Z}_{N}(y^{\star})\|_{V^{\prime}}\leq\|I-P_{N}\|\,C_{\mathrm{cl}}\,\|(I-P_{N})B\|\,\|U_{N}\|\,\|y^{\star}\|_{X_{N}},

which is (
5.25
). The bound (
5.26
) follows from

.
∎

Remark 5.10

(A sufficient small-gain condition)
.

From (
5.19
), Lemma

, and (
5.13
) one obtains the bound

\|T_{N}\|_{\mathcal{L}(X_{N})}\;\leq\;\|P_{N}\|\,C_{\mathrm{cl}}\,\|(I-P_{N})B\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime})}\,\|U_{N}\|.

Remark 5.11

(How to compute

.

Represent

by its coefficient vector

in the basis

.
To assemble the matrix of

We now explain how the abstract control model of Sections

4

6.1
From the whole-space model to a bounded control domain

The effective thermo–plasmonic model is posed in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

(Theorem

), while the control analysis is
performed on a bounded domain
\Omega
. We briefly justify this reduction.

Assumption 6.1

(Geometric setting for the control domain)
.

Let
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a fixed compact set that contains all nanoparticle
centers
z_{i}
and the application-relevant region (e.g. the tumour region
where heat tracking is required). We choose
\Omega
to be a bounded
C^{2}

domain such that

B\Subset\Omega\Subset\mathbb{R}^{3},

and the points
x_{j}
where point actuators are located satisfy
x_{j}\in B
.

Under Assumption
6.1
, the whole-space solution
w
of the
effective model can be restricted to
\Omega
, and the difference between the
whole-space solution and the bounded-domain one is small on
B
if
\Omega

is chosen large. This reduction is quantified in Lemma

.

6.2
Abstract map from intensity controls to heat inputs

We now summarize the abstract structure of the map from electromagnetic actuation to effective
heat inputs.
In our heat-generation model, the volumetric source is parameterized as a
nonnegative
linear combination of precomputed intensity patterns, see (
2.2
) and (
2.12
),,:

q(\cdot,t)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{P}p_{\ell}(t)\,\psi_{\ell}(\cdot),\qquad\psi_{\ell}(\cdot)=\bigl|E_{\mathrm{inc},\ell}(\cdot)\bigr|^{2}.

(6.1)

Here each dictionary element corresponds to a choice of incident direction and polarization (and, when relevant, frequency and nanoparticle parameters near resonance). Thus, the actuation map
p\mapsto q
is linear by construction, with physically admissible coefficients
p_{\ell}(t)\geq 0
. Consequently, the forcing depends only on the
intensity weights
,

p(t)=(p_{\ell}(t))_{\ell=1}^{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{P},

and the reduced actuation map is linear in
p
. Throughout the remainder of the paper,
p
denotes this
(real-valued) intensity/power control parameter.
Let
U
be a Hilbert space of time-dependent intensity controls (e.g.
U=H^{r+1/2}_{0,\sigma}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{P})
). As in
Section
2
, the Volterra system (
2.6
) can be written in operator form

\sigma=\mathcal{V}F+r,

where
F=(F_{i})
is the forcing vector depending linearly on the intensity control
p
,
\mathcal{V}
is a bounded Volterra operator on

X:=(H^{1}(0,T))^{M}
, and
r
is a small remainder. The effective heat
inputs associated with the nanoparticles are

G_{i}(t):=\frac{\alpha_{i}}{c_{m}}\,\sigma_{i}(t),\qquad i=1,\dots,M,

(6.2)

and we denote
G=(G_{i})_{i=1}^{M}
.

Theorem 6.2

(Rigorous approximation of the map
p\mapsto G
)
.

Let
U:=H^{r+1/2}_{0,\sigma}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{P})
be the space of intensity control
parameters,

and
Y:=L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{M})

(i)

The map

associated
with the Maxwell problem and the forcing term in (

\mathcal{T}:U\to X
.

(ii)

The Volterra system (

) defines a bounded

and the remainder

\left\|r\right\|_{X}\leq C_{T}M\delta^{\mu}\left\|F\right\|_{X}

for some
\mu>0
and

independent of
\delta
.

(iii)

The map

given by (

) extends

.

Then there exist a bounded linear operator

and a

such that, for every

As before:
\sigma=\mathcal{V}F+r
with

, and

\mathcal{R}^{\delta}p:=\mathcal{S}r(p)
.
∎

Remark 6.3

(
Constraints on the intensity control
p
(nonnegativity)
)
.

In the physical model the illumination/intensity parameters satisfy

p(t)=(p_{\ell}(t))_{\ell=1}^{P}\in(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{P}
since
p_{\ell}(t)=|a_{\ell}(t)|^{2}\geq 0
.
In the reduced implementation the actuator signal that enters the
Dirac-driven heat model is a finite-dimensional quantity

(obtained by projecting the abstract feedback input onto

), and it is related to
the intensity vector through the linear map
K_{0}:U_{0}\to Y_{0}
.
Therefore, with the physical constraint

, the set of

is the convex cone

\mathcal{C}_{K_{0}}:=K_{0}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{P})\subset Y_{0}.

Consequently, exact realization of the commanded input is possible at time

if and only if

, in which case one may select (for instance) the minimal-norm nonnegative

for instance.
When
u_{\mathrm{des}}(t)\notin\mathcal{C}_{K_{0}}
, one cannot represent it exactly with
p(t)\geq 0
using the fixed
dictionary; in that case a natural implementation is to use the
cone projection

p(t)\in\arg\min_{p\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{P}}\ \|K_{0}p-u_{\mathrm{des}}(t)\|_{Y_{0}}^{2},\qquad u_{\mathrm{impl}}(t):=K_{0}p(t)\in\mathcal{C}_{K_{0}},

so that the closed-loop system tracks the best attainable input within
\mathcal{C}_{K_{0}}
.
In the remainder,
p
denotes a physically admissible (nonnegative) intensity vector produced by the above procedures. Such arguments will be exploited in a forthcoming work.

How Theorem

answers the original control problem

We divide it into few steps:

1.

The original control question can be formulated as follows: given a desired
temperature profile

in
\Omega

fields acting on a finite collection of thermo–plasmonic nanoparticles so that

Our analysis proceeds in two steps.

(1)
Abstract heat tracking with point actuators.

In Section

for non-constant references (see (

(and, by smoothing,

.

To enforce exact tracking of the prescribed low-mode target at steady state, we use the fixed-point
pre-compensation of Subsection

(2)
Realization of the ideal actuators by thermo–plasmonic nanoparticles.

In this section, we summarize how the abstract results of Sections

The feedback design in Section

Offline choice of actuator points and

Choose actuator locations

10.1
Spectral assumptions

Proposition 10.1

(Genericity of Assumption
4.7
)
.

Fix
M\in\mathbb{N}
and consider the map

F:(\Omega)^{M}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad F(x_{1},\dots,x_{M}):=\det\big(\phi_{k}(x_{j})\big)_{j,k=1}^{M},

where
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
is the eigenbasis of
-A_{0}
introduced above.
Then:

(a)

F
is a real-analytic function on
(\Omega)^{M}
.

(b)

F
is not identically zero.

(c)

The zero set

\mathcal{Z}_{M}:=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{M})\in\Omega^{M}:F(x_{1},\dots,x_{M})=0\}

has Lebesgue measure zero in
\Omega^{M}
.

In particular, Assumption
4.7
holds for almost every choice of
the actuator positions
(x_{1},\dots,x_{M})\in\Omega^{M}
in the sense of
Lebesgue measure. Then Assumption
4.7
is a full row rank condition for the

N\times M
matrix of the first
N
modes, with
N\leq M
.

This provides a genericity mechanism for point actuator/sensor placement. We include the proof for completeness, since we require the specific full-row-rank condition in Assumption
4.7
(i) on the first
N
modes.

Proof.

(a) Each eigenfunction
\phi_{k}
is real-analytic in
\Omega
(since
A
is
a second-order elliptic operator with smooth coefficients (i.e. constant here) and Neumann boundary
conditions). The entries
\phi_{k}(x_{j})
of the matrix thus depend
real-analytically on
(x_{1},\dots,x_{M})
, and the determinant is a polynomial
expression in these entries; hence
F
is real-analytic.

(b) Suppose, for contradiction, that
F\equiv 0
on
\Omega^{M}
. Then for
every
M
-tuple
(x_{1},\dots,x_{M})\in\Omega^{M}
the
M\times M
matrix

(\phi_{k}(x_{j}))_{j,k=1}^{M}
is singular, i.e. its rows

r(x_{j}):=\big(\phi_{1}(x_{j}),\dots,\phi_{M}(x_{j})\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{M}

are linearly dependent. This implies that the set

\mathcal{R}:=\{\,r(x):x\in\Omega\,\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{M}

is contained in a proper linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{M}
. Hence there
exists a nonzero vector
c=(c_{1},\dots,c_{M})\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
such that

c\cdot r(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{k}\phi_{k}(x)=0,\qquad x\in\Omega.

Thus
\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{k}\phi_{k}\equiv 0
in
\Omega
. Since

\{\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{M}\}
are linearly independent in
H
, this forces

c_{k}=0
for all
k
, a contradiction. Therefore
F
is not identically zero.

(c) The zero set of a nontrivial real-analytic
function has Lebesgue measure zero. Since
F
is real-analytic and not
identically zero, the set
\mathcal{Z}_{M}
has measure zero in
\Omega^{M}
.
∎

Remark 10.2

(Practical interpretation)
.

Proposition
10.1
shows that, for fixed
M
and domain

\Omega
, Assumption
4.7
is a generic property of the actuator
positions
(x_{1},\dots,x_{M})
: it fails only on an exceptional set

\mathcal{Z}_{M}
of Lebesgue measure zero. Equivalently, if the points

x_{j}
are chosen “at random” in
\Omega
(according to any absolutely
continuous probability distribution), then the matrix

\mathcal{B}_{jk}=\phi_{k}(x_{j})
is invertible with probability one.

In particular, the rank condition can always be enforced in practice by
perturbing a given configuration of points slightly (as long as the points
remain in the interior of
\Omega
and do not approach the boundary or
coincide). In simple geometries, such as intervals or rectangles where the
eigenfunctions are explicitly known (e.g. trigonometric functions), it is
enough to ensure that:

•

the points
x_{j}
do not lie on nodal sets of any of the eigenfunctions

\phi_{k}
with
k\leq M
, and

•

the points are not arranged in a way that respects the symmetries of
these nodal sets.

For example, on an interval
\Omega=(0,L)
with Neumann boundary conditions,
the eigenfunctions are
\phi_{1}\equiv\mathrm{const}
and

\phi_{k}(x)=\cos((k-1)\pi x/L)
for
k\geq 2
. Choosing
M
distinct points

x_{j}\in(0,L)
that avoid the zeros of these cosine functions and are not
symmetrically paired with respect to the midpoint generically yields an
invertible matrix
\mathcal{B}
.

A concrete condition in 1D (Neumann interval)

We first treat the case of a one-dimensional interval with Neumann boundary
conditions, for which the eigenfunctions are explicitly known.

Lemma 10.3

(Invertibility on a 1D Neumann interval via DCT nodes)
.

Let
\Omega=(0,L)
and let
A=\kappa_{m}\partial_{xx}
with Neumann boundary
conditions. An orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions of
-A_{0}
is given by

\phi_{1}(x):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}},\qquad\phi_{k}(x):=\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\,\cos\!\Big(\frac{(k-1)\pi x}{L}\Big),\quad k\geq 2.

Fix
M\in\mathbb{N}
and define actuator positions

x_{j}:=\frac{(2j-1)L}{2M},\qquad j=1,\dots,M.

(10.1)

Let
\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
be the matrix

\mathcal{B}_{jk}:=\phi_{k}(x_{j}),\qquad j,k=1,\dots,M.

Then
\mathcal{B}
is invertible.

Proof.

By the explicit formulas for
\phi_{k}
and (
10.1
), we have

\phi_{1}(x_{j})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}},\qquad\phi_{k}(x_{j})=\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\,\cos\!\Big(\frac{(k-1)\pi(2j-1)}{2M}\Big),\quad k\geq 2.

Factor out the (nonzero) normalizing constants row- and column-wise. Up to
multiplication by invertible diagonal matrices on the left and right,

\mathcal{B}
is equivalent to the matrix
C\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
with
entries

C_{jk}:=\cos\!\Big(\frac{(k-1)\pi(2j-1)}{2M}\Big),\qquad j,k=1,\dots,M.

The matrix
C
is (up to normalization) the discrete cosine transform of type
II (DCT-II), known to be orthogonal (after an appropriate scaling of the first
column), see
[
34
]
for instance. In particular,
\det C\neq 0
. Since multiplication by invertible
diagonal matrices does not change rank, we conclude that
\mathcal{B}
is
invertible.
∎

A concrete condition in 3D for a Neumann cube

We now give an explicit sufficient condition for a three-dimensional cube,
using tensor products of the 1D construction.

Lemma 10.4

(Invertibility on a 3D Neumann cube via tensor-product DCT grid)
.

Let
\Omega=(0,L_{1})\times(0,L_{2})\times(0,L_{3})
and let

A=\kappa_{m}\Delta
with Neumann boundary conditions on
\partial\Omega
.
An orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions of
-A_{0}
is given (up to
normalization) by

\phi_{\mathbf{n}}(x)=c_{\mathbf{n}}\,\cos\!\Big(\frac{n_{1}\pi x_{1}}{L_{1}}\Big)\cos\!\Big(\frac{n_{2}\pi x_{2}}{L_{2}}\Big)\cos\!\Big(\frac{n_{3}\pi x_{3}}{L_{3}}\Big),

where
\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}
and
c_{\mathbf{n}}>0
are
normalizing constants.

Fix integers
N_{1},N_{2},N_{3}\geq 0
and set

I:=\{\,\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}:0\leq n_{\ell}\leq N_{\ell},\ \ell=1,2,3\,\}.

Let

H_{M}:=\mathrm{span}\{\phi_{\mathbf{n}}:\mathbf{n}\in I\},\quad M:=(N_{1}+1)(N_{2}+1)(N_{3}+1).

Index
H_{M}
by an enumeration
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{M}
of
\{\phi_{\mathbf{n}}:\mathbf{n}\in I\}
(the order is irrelevant for rank). For each direction

\ell=1,2,3
define

x^{(\ell)}_{j_{\ell}}:=\frac{(2j_{\ell}-1)L_{\ell}}{2(N_{\ell}+1)},\qquad j_{\ell}=1,\dots,N_{\ell}+1,

and form the tensor-product grid

x_{\mathbf{j}}:=\big(x^{(1)}_{j_{1}},x^{(2)}_{j_{2}},x^{(3)}_{j_{3}}\big),\qquad\mathbf{j}=(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3})\in J,

where
J:=\{1,\dots,N_{1}+1\}\times\{1,\dots,N_{2}+1\}\times\{1,\dots,N_{3}+1\}

and
|J|=M
.

Let
\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
be the matrix

\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{n}}:=\phi_{\mathbf{n}}(x_{\mathbf{j}}),\qquad\mathbf{j}\in J,\ \mathbf{n}\in I,

and identify
\mathcal{B}
with an
M\times M
matrix after any fixed
enumeration of
J
and
I
. Then
\mathcal{B}
is invertible.

Proof.

By the tensor-product structure of the eigenfunctions and the grid, we have

\phi_{\mathbf{n}}(x_{\mathbf{j}})=c_{\mathbf{n}}\,\prod_{\ell=1}^{3}\cos\!\Big(\frac{n_{\ell}\pi x^{(\ell)}_{j_{\ell}}}{L_{\ell}}\Big).

Define, for each
\ell=1,2,3
, the 1D matrices

C^{(\ell)}_{j_{\ell},n_{\ell}}:=\cos\!\Big(\frac{n_{\ell}\pi x^{(\ell)}_{j_{\ell}}}{L_{\ell}}\Big),\qquad j_{\ell}=1,\dots,N_{\ell}+1,\quad n_{\ell}=0,\dots,N_{\ell}.

By the same argument as in Lemma
10.3
(with
M
replaced by

N_{\ell}+1
), each
C^{(\ell)}
is, up to normalization, a DCT-II matrix and
hence invertible.

Up to multiplication by nonzero diagonal matrices corresponding to the
normalization constants
c_{\mathbf{n}}
, the full matrix
\mathcal{B}
can
be written as a (permuted) Kronecker product

\mathcal{B}=D\cdot\big(C^{(1)}\otimes C^{(2)}\otimes C^{(3)}\big),

where
D
is a diagonal matrix with strictly positive entries and

\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product. Since each
C^{(\ell)}
is
invertible and the Kronecker product of invertible matrices is invertible,

C^{(1)}\otimes C^{(2)}\otimes C^{(3)}
is invertible. Multiplication by
the diagonal matrix
D
does not affect invertibility. Therefore

\mathcal{B}
is invertible. In particular, any selection of
N\leq M
rows yields an
N\times M
matrix with full row rank
N
.
∎

10.2
Verification of Assumption
5.1
on

This subsection provides a self-contained verification of the actuator hypothesis
(Assumption
5.1
) for Dirac actuators on smooth domains.
The only part of Assumption
5.1
that is geometric is the divergence

\xi_{M}^{+}\to+\infty
of the nullspace coercivity constant.
In
[
18
]
the authors verify this in rectangles/cubes
by a self-similar decomposition argument. Here we replace that step by a standard
finite-element interpolation estimate on shape-regular quasi-uniform meshes, which works on general

C^{1,1}
domains (hence extending the class of box/polyhedral domains in
[
18
]
to a class of domains admitting a suitable meshing property; see (
10.6
)).

Throughout,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is bounded of class
C^{1,1}
with
d\in\{1,2,3\}
,
A_{0}=\kappa_{m}\Delta
with
homogeneous Neumann boundary condition, and
\mathcal{A}=I-A_{0}=I-\kappa_{m}\Delta
as in the main text.
We recall that
D(\mathcal{A})=\{w\in H^{2}(\Omega):\partial_{\nu}w=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega\}
and

V=H^{1}(\Omega)
.

Remark 10.5

(Actuator points on
\overline{\Omega}
)
.

In the main text we fix actuator locations
x_{j}\in\Omega
for simplicity. The functional-analytic framework used
throughout only requires
\delta_{x}\in D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime}
, which holds for every
x\in\overline{\Omega}
because

D(\mathcal{A})\hookrightarrow C(\overline{\Omega})
(see Proposition
3.3
).
Therefore, in the verification below we may select actuator points among the vertices of a triangulation, which may
include boundary vertices. This does not affect Assumption
5.1
nor the stabilization statement.

Lemma 10.6

(Elliptic graph norm equivalence)
.

There exist constants
c_{\mathcal{A}},C_{\mathcal{A}}>0
depending only on
\Omega
and
\kappa_{m}
such that

c_{\mathcal{A}}\,\|w\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\|w\|_{D(\mathcal{A})}\leq C_{\mathcal{A}}\,\|w\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\qquad\forall w\in D(\mathcal{A}).

(10.2)

In particular, point evaluation
w\mapsto w(x)
is continuous on
D(\mathcal{A})
for every
x\in\overline{\Omega}
.

Proof.

The upper bound in (
10.2
) follows from
\mathcal{A}w=w-\kappa_{m}\Delta w
and the estimate

\|w\|_{L^{2}}+\|\Delta w\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\|w\|_{H^{2}}
. For the reverse inequality, let
f:=\mathcal{A}w\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Then
w
solves the Neumann problem
(I-\kappa_{m}\Delta)w=f
.
On bounded
C^{1,1}
domains,
H^{2}
-regularity holds for this uniformly elliptic operator with Neumann boundary condition,
so
\|w\|_{H^{2}}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{2}}=C\|w\|_{D(\mathcal{A})}
.
The continuity of point evaluation follows from the embedding
H^{2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow C(\overline{\Omega})
for
d\leq 3
.
∎

Choice of actuator families.

Let
\{\mathcal{T}_{h}\}_{h\downarrow 0}
be a family of shape-regular, quasi-uniform simplicial triangulations of
\Omega
.
Denote by
\mathcal{N}_{h}=\{x_{h,1},\dots,x_{h,M(h)}\}\subset\overline{\Omega}
the set of all mesh vertices.
For each
h
(equivalently, for each
M:=M(h)
), define Dirac actuators

d_{M,j}:=\delta_{x_{h,j}}\in D(\mathcal{A})^{\prime},\qquad j=1,\dots,M.

(10.3)

To produce biorthogonal test functions in
D(\mathcal{A})
, we use Riesz representatives.

Lemma 10.7

(Biorthogonal family in
D(\mathcal{A})
)
.

For each
M
and each
j\in\{1,\dots,M\}
there exists
\Psi_{M,j}\in D(\mathcal{A})
such that

\langle d_{M,i},\Psi_{M,j}\rangle=\Psi_{M,j}(x_{h,i})=\delta_{ij},\qquad i,j=1,\dots,M.

(10.4)

Consequently,
\dim\mathrm{span}\{d_{M,j}\}_{j=1}^{M}=\dim\mathrm{span}\{\Psi_{M,j}\}_{j=1}^{M}=M
.

Proof.

By Lemma
10.6
, each evaluation functional
d_{M,j}
is continuous on the Hilbert space

D(\mathcal{A})
equipped with the inner product
(u,v)_{D(\mathcal{A})}:=(\mathcal{A}u,\mathcal{A}v)_{H}
.
Hence, by the Riesz representation theorem, there exists a unique
r_{M,j}\in D(\mathcal{A})
such that

\langle d_{M,j},w\rangle=(w,r_{M,j})_{D(\mathcal{A})}
for all
w\in D(\mathcal{A})
.
Define the Gram matrix
G_{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
by

(G_{M})_{ij}:=\langle d_{M,i},r_{M,j}\rangle=(r_{M,i},r_{M,j})_{D(\mathcal{A})}.

For any
c\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, we have
c^{\top}G_{M}c=\big\|\sum_{j=1}^{M}c_{j}r_{M,j}\big\|_{D(\mathcal{A})}^{2}
, so
G_{M}
is symmetric
positive definite and thus invertible. Now set

\Psi_{M,j}:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}(G_{M}^{-1})_{j\ell}\,r_{M,\ell}\in D(\mathcal{A}).

Then, for every
i
,

\langle d_{M,i},\Psi_{M,j}\rangle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}(G_{M}^{-1})_{j\ell}\,\langle d_{M,i},r_{M,\ell}\rangle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}(G_{M}^{-1})_{j\ell}\,(G_{M})_{i\ell}=\delta_{ij},

which is (
10.4
). The dimension claims follow immediately from biorthogonality.
∎

Divergence of the nullspace coercivity constant.

Let
V_{h}\subset H^{1}(\Omega)
be the standard piecewise-affine (

) finite element space associated with
\mathcal{T}_{h}
,
and let
I_{h}:H^{2}(\Omega)\to V_{h}
be the nodal Lagrange interpolant.

Lemma 10.8

(Nodal interpolation estimate for polyhedral domains)
.

There exists a constant
C_{I}>0
, independent of

, such that for all
w\in H^{2}(\Omega)
,

\|w-I_{h}w\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C_{I}\,h\,\|w\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}.

(10.5)

Proof.

This is the classical

interpolation estimate on shape-regular quasi-uniform meshes; see Theorem
4.4.20
in
[
7
]
for polyhedral domains.
∎

To deal with more general domains as polyhedral ones, we take
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
to be a bounded

domain and work with a boundary-fitted isoparametric mesh family (see, e.g.,

,

,

,

)

\{\mathcal{T}_{h}\}_{h\downarrow 0}
: each element

is the image of a fixed reference simplex

under a bijective map

.
We assume uniform (isoparametric) shape-regularity and quasi-uniformity: there exist constants

,
independent of

\|DF_{K}\|_{L^{\infty}(\widehat{K})}\leq\gamma h_{K},\qquad\|(DF_{K})^{-1}\|_{L^{\infty}(\widehat{K})}\leq\gamma h_{K}^{-1},\qquad\|D^{2}F_{K}\|_{L^{\infty}(\widehat{K})}\leq\gamma h_{K},

(10.6)

and

a.e. on

.
Moreover,
h\simeq h_{K}
for all

, and
\Omega=\bigcup_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}K
.

Parametrizable domains.

The framework above also applies if
\Omega
is given as the image of a reference domain.
More precisely, assume there exist a bounded reference domain
\widehat{\Omega}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
admitting a

with

\widehat{\Omega}=\bigcup_{\widehat{K}\in\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\widehat{K}

\Phi:\widehat{\Omega}\to\Omega
with
\Phi\in W^{2,\infty}(\widehat{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{3})
and
\Phi^{-1}\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
.
Then, pushing forward the reference mesh by
\Phi
yields an isoparametric family (cf. the standard pushforward construction in
[

satisfying the above
uniform bounds (possibly with a different constant

\Omega=\bigcup_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}K

The (isoparametric)

finite element space is defined by pullback: for each

, functions in

restrict to polynomials of degree
\leq 1
on
\widehat{K}
after composition with
F_{K}^{-1}
.
The operator
I_{h}:H^{2}(\Omega)\to V_{h}
denotes the nodal Lagrange interpolant (defined using the mesh vertices).

Lemma 10.9

(Nodal interpolation estimate for more general domains)
.

There exists a constant
C_{I}>0
, independent of
h
, such that for all
w\in H^{2}(\Omega)
,

\|w-I_{h}w\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C_{I}\,h\,\|w\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}.

(10.7)

Proof.

We give a self-contained proof that remains valid for
C^{1,1}
domains under the mesh assumption above.
Fix
w\in H^{2}(\Omega)
and
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
.
Set
\widehat{w}:=w\circ F_{K}\in H^{2}(\widehat{K})
and denote by
\widehat{I}
the nodal
P_{1}
interpolant on the reference simplex

\widehat{K}
(with the reference vertices as nodes).
By construction of the isoparametric interpolant, the local interpolant satisfies

(I_{h}w)|_{K}=(\widehat{I}\,\widehat{w})\circ F_{K}^{-1}.

(10.8)

Step 1: reference-element estimate.

On the fixed simplex
\widehat{K}
the Bramble–Hilbert lemma (or an explicit finite-dimensional argument) yields the standard bound

\|\widehat{w}-\widehat{I}\widehat{w}\|_{H^{1}(\widehat{K})}\leq C_{\widehat{I}}\,|\widehat{w}|_{H^{2}(\widehat{K})},

(10.9)

where
C_{\widehat{I}}
depends only on the reference simplex.

Step 2: mapping of norms.

Using the change of variables
x=F_{K}(\widehat{x})
,
J_{K}:=DF_{K}
, and the chain rule,

\nabla_{x}\big(\phi\circ F_{K}^{-1}\big)(x)=J_{K}(\widehat{x})^{-\top}\,\nabla_{\widehat{x}}\phi(\widehat{x}),\qquad\widehat{x}=F_{K}^{-1}(x),

we obtain the local
H^{1}
-estimate

\|w-I_{h}w\|_{H^{1}(K)}\leq C\,h_{K}^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\,\|\widehat{w}-\widehat{I}\widehat{w}\|_{H^{1}(\widehat{K})},

(10.10)

with a constant
C
depending only on the uniform bounds in (
10.6
).
Similarly, by differentiating twice and using (
10.6
), one has the standard pullback bound

|\widehat{w}|_{H^{2}(\widehat{K})}\leq C\,h_{K}^{2-\frac{d}{2}}\,\|w\|_{H^{2}(K)},

(10.11)

where again
C
depends only on (
10.6
).
(A direct derivation expands
D^{2}_{\widehat{x}}(w\circ F_{K})
into a term involving
D^{2}_{x}w
and a lower-order term involving

D_{x}w
multiplied by
D^{2}F_{K}
; the latter is controlled by (
10.6
) and absorbed into
\|w\|_{H^{2}(K)}
.)

Step 3: local-to-global.

Combining (
10.9
)–(
10.11
) gives

\|w-I_{h}w\|_{H^{1}(K)}\leq C\,h_{K}^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\,C_{\widehat{I}}\,|\widehat{w}|_{H^{2}(\widehat{K})}\leq C\,C_{\widehat{I}}\,h_{K}\,\|w\|_{H^{2}(K)}.

Summing over
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
and using quasi-uniformity (
h_{K}\simeq h
) yields

\|w-I_{h}w\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\|w-I_{h}w\|_{H^{1}(K)}^{2}\leq C\,h^{2}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\|w\|_{H^{2}(K)}^{2}=C\,h^{2}\,\|w\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

which implies (
10.7
) with
C_{I}:=\sqrt{C}
.
The constant depends only on the reference simplex and the uniform
C^{1}
shape-regularity bounds (
10.6
),
and is therefore independent of
h
.
∎

Proposition 10.10

(Nullspace coercivity on
C^{1,1}
domains with exact mesh assumption (
10.6
))
.

Let
D_{M}\subset D(\mathcal{A})
be the subspace

D_{M}:=\{w\in D(\mathcal{A}):\ \langle d_{M,j},w\rangle=w(x_{h,j})=0\ \forall j=1,\dots,M\}.

Then there exists
c>0
, independent of
h
, such that the constant

\xi_{M}^{+}:=\inf\Big\{\frac{\|w\|_{D(\mathcal{A})}^{2}}{\|w\|_{V}^{2}}:\ w\in D_{M}\setminus\{0\}\Big\}

satisfies

\xi_{M}^{+}\geq c\,h^{-2}\longrightarrow+\infty\qquad(h\downarrow 0).

(10.12)

Proof.

Let
w\in D_{M}
. Since
w\in D(\mathcal{A})\subset H^{2}(\Omega)
and
w(x_{h,j})=0
for every vertex, the nodal interpolant
satisfies
I_{h}w\equiv 0
.
Therefore, using Lemma
10.9
and Lemma
10.6
,

\|w\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}=\|w-I_{h}w\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C_{I}h\,\|w\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{C_{I}}{c_{\mathcal{A}}}\,h\,\|w\|_{D(\mathcal{A})}.

Since
V=H^{1}(\Omega)
with an equivalent norm, we obtain
\|w\|_{V}\leq Ch\|w\|_{D(\mathcal{A})}
for a constant
C
independent
of
h
. Squaring and taking reciprocals yields

\frac{\|w\|_{D(\mathcal{A})}^{2}}{\|w\|_{V}^{2}}\geq C^{-2}h^{-2}.

Taking the infimum over
w\in D_{M}\setminus\{0\}
gives (
10.12
).
∎

Corollary 10.11

(Assumption
5.1
on domains satisfying (
10.6
))
.

With the choices (
10.3
) and the biorthogonal family from Lemma
10.7
,
Assumption
5.1
holds on bounded
C^{1,1}
domains, and in particular

\xi_{M}^{+}\to+\infty
.

Remark 10.12

(Rate in terms of the number of actuators)
.

If
\{\mathcal{T}_{h}\}
is quasi-uniform, then
M=M(h)\asymp h^{-d}
, hence (
10.12
) implies

\xi_{M}^{+}\gtrsim M^{2/d}
.

10.3
Three sufficient mechanisms ensuring solvability of the fixed–point correction

This subsection collects three
complementary
ways to guarantee solvability of the finite-dimensional
fixed–point correction used to compensate the steady bias. The three mechanisms correspond to:
(i) increasing the medium diffusivity
\kappa_{m}
; (ii) strengthening the stabilizing feedback (gain/scaling
\lambda
);
and (iii) designing the actuator placement to improve conditioning.

Fixed–point equation and a universal contraction criterion.

Let
\{\varphi_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
be the Neumann eigenfunctions of
-\Delta
in
\Omega
with eigenvalues

0=\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots
and set

X_{N}:=\mathrm{span}\{\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{N}\},\qquad P_{N}:H\to X_{N}\text{ the }H\text{--orthogonal projector},\qquad Q_{N}:=I-P_{N}.

In the fixed–point construction of Subsection
5.4
(notation as there),
the bias operator
T_{N}:X_{N}\to X_{N}
can be written as

T_{N}\;=\;P_{N}R_{\mathrm{cl}}\,Q_{N}\,B\,U_{N},

(10.13)

where:

•

Ay:=\kappa_{m}\Delta y
(Neumann boundary condition) and
A_{\mathrm{cl}}
is the closed–loop generator;

•

S_{\mathrm{cl}}(t)=e^{tA_{\mathrm{cl}}}
is exponentially stable in
V^{\prime}
(see Theorem
5.3
);

•

R_{\mathrm{cl}}:=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}S_{\mathrm{cl}}(t)\,dt
(so
R_{\mathrm{cl}}=-A_{\mathrm{cl}}^{-1}
whenever
A_{\mathrm{cl}}
is invertible);

•

B:\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathcal{X}
is the actuator operator (Dirac point actuators, interpreted in the same dual/extrapolation space
\mathcal{X}
as in Subsection
5.4
);

•

U_{N}:X_{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
is the (linear) “feedforward selector” used to enforce the cancellation property

P_{N}\bigl(A_{0}y+BU_{N}y\bigr)=0,\qquad\forall\,y\in X_{N},

(10.14)

so that the residual forcing generated by the feedforward acts only on the tail.

The fixed–point corrected reference
y^{\star}\in X_{N}
is defined by

(I+T_{N})\,y^{\star}=y_{r}\qquad\text{in }X_{N}.

(10.15)

A sufficient condition for unique solvability of (
10.15
) by contraction is
\|T_{N}\|<1
.
To expose the three mechanisms, we separate the estimate into (i)
low
\leftarrow
high leakage
and
(ii)
conditioning of the feedforward
:

\|T_{N}\|_{\mathcal{L}(X_{N})}\;\leq\;\underbrace{\|P_{N}R_{\mathrm{cl}}Q_{N}\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X})}}_{=:\,L_{N}}\;\cdot\;\|B\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathcal{X})}\;\cdot\;\|U_{N}\|_{\mathcal{L}(X_{N},\mathbb{R}^{M})}.

(10.16)

Proposition 10.13

(Universal contraction criterion)
.

If

L_{N}\,\|B\|\,\|U_{N}\|<1,

(10.17)

then
I+T_{N}
is invertible on
X_{N}
, (
10.15
) admits a unique solution
y^{\star}\in X_{N}
, and the Picard
iteration
y^{(\ell+1)}:=y_{r}-T_{N}y^{(\ell)}
converges geometrically to
y^{\star}
.
Moreover,

\|y^{\star}\|_{X_{N}}\leq\frac{1}{1-\|T_{N}\|}\,\|y_{r}\|_{X_{N}}.

Proof.

This is immediate from
\|T_{N}\|<1
and the Neumann series for
(I+T_{N})^{-1}
.
∎

In the remainder of this subsection, we present three independent ways to enforce (
10.17
).
Mechanisms (A) and (B) reduce the leakage factor
L_{N}
, while mechanism (C) reduces the conditioning factor
\|U_{N}\|
.

(A) Large diffusivity
\kappa_{m}
reduces low
\leftarrow
high leakage.

We decompose the closed–loop generator with respect to
H=X_{N}\oplus X_{N}^{\perp}
:

A_{\mathrm{cl}}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{pmatrix},\qquad A_{ij}:=P_{i}A_{\mathrm{cl}}P_{j},\;\;P_{1}:=P_{N},\;\;P_{2}:=Q_{N},

and write

A_{22}=\kappa_{m}\,Q_{N}\Delta Q_{N}+E_{22},\qquad E_{22}:=Q_{N}\bigl(A_{\mathrm{cl}}-\kappa_{m}\Delta\bigr)Q_{N}.

Set
\beta:=\|E_{22}\|_{\mathcal{L}(X_{N}^{\perp})}
(finite because the feedback/observation terms in
A_{\mathrm{cl}}
are bounded on the chosen state space).

Lemma 10.14

(Tail resolvent estimate)
.

If
\kappa_{m}\lambda_{N+1}>\beta
, then
A_{22}
is invertible on
X_{N}^{\perp}
and

\|A_{22}^{-1}\|_{\mathcal{L}(X_{N}^{\perp})}\;\leq\;\frac{1}{\kappa_{m}\lambda_{N+1}-\beta}.

(10.18)

Proof.

On
X_{N}^{\perp}
, the spectrum of
Q_{N}\Delta Q_{N}
is contained in
\{-\lambda_{k}\}_{k\geq N+1}
, hence

\|(\kappa_{m}Q_{N}\Delta Q_{N})^{-1}\|\leq(\kappa_{m}\lambda_{N+1})^{-1}
.
Write
A_{22}=\kappa_{m}Q_{N}\Delta Q_{N}+E_{22}
and invert by a Neumann series when we have

\|E_{22}(\kappa_{m}Q_{N}\Delta Q_{N})^{-1}\|<1
, i.e.
\kappa_{m}\lambda_{N+1}>\beta
.
∎

Assume in addition that the Schur complement on
X_{N}
,

S:=A_{11}-A_{12}A_{22}^{-1}A_{21},

is invertible and satisfies
\|S^{-1}\|\leq C_{S}
(typically guaranteed by exponential stability on
X_{N}
of the chosen feedback design).
Then the
(1,2)
–block of
A_{\mathrm{cl}}^{-1}
satisfies the standard identity

P_{N}A_{\mathrm{cl}}^{-1}Q_{N}\;=\;-S^{-1}A_{12}A_{22}^{-1}.

Since
R_{\mathrm{cl}}=-A_{\mathrm{cl}}^{-1}
, we obtain:

Proposition 10.15

(Leakage bound improved by large
\kappa_{m}
)
.

Assume
\kappa_{m}\lambda_{N+1}>\beta
and
\|S^{-1}\|\leq C_{S}
. Then

L_{N}=\|P_{N}R_{\mathrm{cl}}Q_{N}\|\;\leq\;\frac{C_{S}\,\|A_{12}\|}{\kappa_{m}\lambda_{N+1}-\beta}.

(10.19)

Consequently, the contraction condition (
10.17
) holds whenever

\frac{C_{S}\,\|A_{12}\|}{\kappa_{m}\lambda_{N+1}-\beta}\,\|B\|\,\|U_{N}\|<1.

(10.20)

Proof.

Combine the block identity with Lemma
10.14
and (
10.16
).
∎

(B) Stronger stabilizing feedback (gain
\lambda
) reduces
R_{\mathrm{cl}}
and hence
L_{N}
.

A complementary mechanism is to increase the closed–loop decay rate by strengthening the feedback.
Assume the closed–loop semigroup satisfies the exponential stability estimate

\|S_{\mathrm{cl}}(t)\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X})}\leq Me^{-\alpha t},\qquad t\geq 0,

(10.21)

for some
M\geq 1
and
\alpha>0
. Then

\|R_{\mathrm{cl}}\|\;=\;\Bigl\|\int_{0}^{\infty}S_{\mathrm{cl}}(t)\,dt\Bigr\|\;\leq\;\int_{0}^{\infty}\|S_{\mathrm{cl}}(t)\|\,dt\;\leq\;\frac{M}{\alpha}.

(10.22)

Since
\|P_{N}\|,\|Q_{N}\|\leq 1
on
H
(and remain bounded in equivalent norms on
X_{N}
), this implies the crude but robust bound

L_{N}=\|P_{N}R_{\mathrm{cl}}Q_{N}\|\;\leq\;\|R_{\mathrm{cl}}\|\;\leq\;\frac{M}{\alpha}.

(10.23)

Proposition 10.16

(Contraction criterion in terms of the closed–loop decay rate)
.

Assume (
10.21
). If

\frac{M}{\alpha}\,\|B\|\,\|U_{N}\|<1,

(10.24)

then the fixed–point problem (
10.15
) has a unique solution and the Picard iteration converges.

Proof.

Combine (
10.23
) with Proposition
10.13
.
∎

Remark 10.17

(How
\lambda
enters
\alpha
)
.

In many finite-dimensional designs (pole placement / Riccati design applied to the truncated model on
X_{N}
),
one can enforce a prescribed decay margin on
X_{N}
:

\Re\,\sigma\!\bigl(A_{11}(\lambda)\bigr)\leq-\alpha_{\mathrm{low}}(\lambda),

where
\alpha_{\mathrm{low}}(\lambda)
increases when the feedback scaling
\lambda
increases, provided the pair

(A_{11},B_{11})
is stabilizable on
X_{N}
.
The global
\alpha
can be taken as
\alpha=\min\{\alpha_{\mathrm{low}}(\lambda),\,\kappa_{m}\lambda_{N+1}-\beta\}
,
up to a multiplicative constant
M
(depending on the chosen norm). This makes (
10.24
)
a practical design criterion: increase
\lambda
until
\alpha
is large enough.

(C) Placement design reduces
\|U_{N}\|
via conditioning of the sampling matrix.

The operator
U_{N}
is determined by the cancellation requirement (
10.14
).
For point actuators, the relevant finite-dimensional matrix is the sampling (or “moment”) matrix

C_{N}\;:=\;\bigl(\varphi_{k}(x_{j})\bigr)_{k=1,\dots,N}^{j=1,\dots,M}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M},

(10.25)

where
\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}\subset\Omega
are the actuator centers. In typical constructions,
U_{N}
is chosen as a right-inverse
of
C_{N}
(possibly composed with fixed diagonal scalings depending on the exact definition of
B
and the chosen basis of
X_{N}
).
The key quantity is the smallest singular value
\sigma_{\min}(C_{N})
.

Lemma 10.18

(Right-inverse bound)
.

Assume
\mathrm{rank}(C_{N})=N
(in particular
M\geq N
). Let
C_{N}^{\dagger}
denote the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse.
Then
C_{N}^{\dagger}
is a bounded right-inverse,
C_{N}C_{N}^{\dagger}=I_{N}
, and

\|C_{N}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{M})}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\min}(C_{N})}.

(10.26)

Consequently, if
U_{N}
is chosen proportional to
C_{N}^{\dagger}
(as in the minimum-norm cancellation choice), then

\|U_{N}\|\;\lesssim\;\frac{1}{\sigma_{\min}(C_{N})}.

(10.27)

Proof.

Standard linear algebra:
\|C_{N}^{\dagger}\|=1/\sigma_{\min}(C_{N})
for full row rank matrices.
∎

Proposition 10.19

(Contraction via placement conditioning)
.

Assume
\mathrm{rank}(C_{N})=N
and choose
U_{N}
according to the minimum-norm cancellation rule so that
(
10.27
) holds. Then

\|T_{N}\|\;\leq\;L_{N}\,\|B\|\,\|U_{N}\|\;\lesssim\;L_{N}\,\|B\|\,\frac{1}{\sigma_{\min}(C_{N})}.

(10.28)

In particular, a sufficient condition for solvability of (
10.15
) is

\sigma_{\min}(C_{N})\;\gtrsim\;L_{N}\,\|B\|.

(10.29)

Proof.

Combine (
10.16
) with Lemma
10.18
.
∎

Remark 10.20

(Design of placement)
.

For fixed
N
, the full-row-rank condition
\mathrm{rank}(C_{N})=N
is generic with respect to the point configuration

\{x_{j}\}
(it fails only on a thin set), and oversampling (
M>N
) typically improves
\sigma_{\min}(C_{N})
and robustness.
A practical design principle is to choose
\{x_{j}\}
to maximize
\sigma_{\min}(C_{N})
(or, equivalently, minimize
\|C_{N}^{\dagger}\|
),
e.g. by greedy selection from a candidate mesh or by D-optimal design maximizing
\det(C_{N}C_{N}^{\top})
.

Combined sufficient condition (three options together).

We summarize the three mechanisms in a single criterion. Combine the
\kappa_{m}
–driven leakage estimate
(
10.19
) (mechanism (A)) or the decay-rate estimate (
10.23
) (mechanism (B))
with the placement estimate (
10.27
) (mechanism (C)). For instance, using (
10.19
) and
Lemma
10.18
, a sufficient contraction condition is

\frac{C_{S}\,\|A_{12}\|}{\kappa_{m}\lambda_{N+1}-\beta}\;\|B\|\;\frac{1}{\sigma_{\min}(C_{N})}\;<\;1,

which can be enforced by: increasing
\kappa_{m}
(stronger tail dissipation), increasing the feedback gain
\lambda
so that

C_{S}
and/or
\beta
improve (stronger closed-loop stability), and/or improving the actuator placement to increase
\sigma_{\min}(C_{N})
.

11
Conclusions and perspectives

We have combined a discrete effective-medium description of heat generation by
plasmonic nanoparticles with an abstract feedback design for heat-profile
tracking based on point actuators. At the modeling level, we relied on the
rigorous point-approximation framework of
[
8
]
to
identify a finite collection of nanoparticles as a system of effective point
actuators for the heat equation: the temperature variation induced by the
particles can be represented, up to small error terms, as a superposition of
heat kernels centered at the nanoparticle locations, with amplitudes solving
a Volterra system driven by the internal electromagnetic energy.

At the control level, we interpret the heat equation with point actuators in the natural
space
V^{\prime}
,
V:=D(\mathcal{A})
, and consider a feedback law based on the
elliptically smoothed measurements
\mathcal{A}^{-1}y
.
Using the stabilization theory of
[
18
]
, we obtain exponential decay of the
tracking error in
V^{\prime}
under an actuator-family hypothesis, and we recover
H
–tracking after any
positive time
t_{0}>0
via parabolic regularization.

Finally, we coupled the two levels by expressing the effective heat inputs
generated by the nanoparticle ensemble as a linear operator
\mathcal{K}

acting on a set of electromagnetic illumination parameters. Under suitable
assumptions on the illumination patterns,
\mathcal{K}
admits a right-inverse
on a relevant finite-dimensional subspace, and the discrepancy between the
ideal feedback inputs and the effective thermo–plasmonic ones can be
quantified in terms of the small geometric parameter
\delta
and the modeling
error from the discrete effective description. This yields an abstract estimate
on the tracking error between the ideal closed-loop temperature and the
physically realized one, and shows that, in the asymptotic regime considered
in
[
8
]
, plasmonic nanoparticles can indeed be used as
actuators for heat-profile tracking.

Two implementation layers merit further development.
First, while the analysis allows signed coefficients in the abstract intensity parameter, physically one has

p_{\ell}(t)=|a_{\ell}(t)|^{2}\geq 0
, leading to cone-type feasibility constraints for the reduced actuator input.
Second, the feedback output is formulated via an elliptically regularized point evaluation, which is convenient
for Dirac-actuated heat dynamics but this needs te be linked to more realistic
measurement modalities.
A forthcoming work will incorporate these constraints directly (including nonnegativity and sensor models), and
will provide numerical studies illustrating the closed-loop tracking performance. In addition, we will work directly on the full space avoiding finite-dimensional projections.

Several directions for further work remain open, including heterogeneous and
structured backgrounds and lower-dimensional configurations with
robust and optimal designs of nanoparticle configurations
and illuminations, and numerical validation. In addition, we plan to analyze the feedback control part in the whole space, with the diffusion operator
A
stated now in the whole space with absolutely continuous spectrum. In the same direction, we would like to study tracking other fields, as acoustic fluctuation (in the framework of acoustic cavitation) using either purely acoustic models, see
[
26
]
, in Ultrasound, or multy-physic models as photo-acoustics, see
[
12
]
. Finally, such an approach make also full sense in mechanics where the goal is to generate and track full displacements and not only pressure parts, as in acoustics.
