Comparison of clinical efficacy of modified transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (M-TLIF) and posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF) with bone cement-reinforced pedicle screws for osteoporosis combined with lumbar degenerative disease
Authors: Yan Gong, Hao Liu, Zelin Zhou, De Liang, Xiaobing Jiang, Zhaojun Cheng, Jintao Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo compare modified transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (M-TLIF) and posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF) in patients with lumbar degenerative disease complicated by osteoporosis who underwent bone cement-augmented pedicle screw placement, with a focus on lumbar radiographic parameters and clinical outcomes.MethodsA retrospective comparative study was conducted on patients with lumbar degenerative disease and osteoporosis who underwent lumbar fusion surgery with bone cement-augmented pedicle screws between January 2021 and June 2023. Based on the surgical procedure received, patients were divided into an M-TLIF group (n = 49) and a PLIF group (n = 44). The comparison encompassed perioperative indicators, radiographic parameters—including the coronal Cobb angle, average surgical segment disc height (ASDH), lumbar lordosis (LL), segmental lordosis (SL), Bridwell fusion grade, and Marchi subsidence grade—and clinical efficacy scores, including the visual analog scale (VAS), Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), and Japanese Orthopedic Association (JOA) score, which were assessed preoperatively, immediately postoperatively, and at 2-year postoperatively.ResultsThe study included 93 patients (M-TLIF: n = 49; PLIF: n = 44). The two groups were comparable in all baseline characteristics (P > 0.05). Regarding perioperative indicators, the M-TLIF group had a significantly longer operative time per segment (185.79 ± 78.46 min vs. 152.92 ± 71.64 min, P = 0.038) but a lower volume of bone cement used per screw (1.86 ± 0.58 ml vs. 2.15 ± 0.62 ml, P = 0.023). Both groups demonstrated significant improvements in all clinical scores (VAS, ODI, JOA) and radiographic parameters (Cobb, ASDH, LL, SL) at all postoperative time points compared to preoperative values (all P < 0.05). At the 2-year postoperatively, VAS and ODI scores were comparable between groups (P > 0.05). Although the JOA score was statistically higher in the PLIF group (25.73 ± 1.26 vs. 25.12 ± 1.51, P = 0.040), the absolute difference of 0.61 points is clinically negligible. Radiographically, the PLIF group achieved a significantly greater SL angle at follow-up (16.59 ± 8.59° vs. 12.17 ± 8.16°, P = 0.013), while the M-TLIF group showed a significantly superior Bridwell fusion grade (P = 0.020). There was no significant intergroup difference in cage subsidence (P > 0.05).ConclusionBoth M-TLIF and PLIF effectively improved the clinical symptoms and radiographic parameters of these patients, with equivalent clinical efficacy in relieving pain and restoring function. The choice of procedure can be individualized: M-TLIF is preferred when superior interbody fusion is the priority, while PLIF is more suitable for achieving greater segmental lordosis.Keywords: Modified transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion, Posterior lumbar interbody fusion, Osteoporosis, Lumbar degenerative disease, Fusion cage

Introduction

Lumbar degenerative disease is a common condition among elderly individuals and is characterized by lower back pain, radiating pain in the lower limbs, and intermittent claudication, which significantly impairs patients’ quality of life [1]. As China's population ages, the incidence of osteoporosis associated with this disease has risen significantly. According to statistics, among middle-aged and elderly patients requiring surgery for lumbar spine degeneration, 14.5% of men and 51.3% of women have varying degrees of osteoporosis [2, 3]. Osteoporosis, characterized by reduced bone density and destruction of the bone microstructure, can lead to delayed bone fusion after lumbar fusion surgery, displacement of intervertebral fusion devices, and loosening of pedicle screw fixation. These issues significantly impair surgical outcomes and may even increase the risk of secondary surgery, presenting a major challenge in clinical treatment [4, 5].

Currently, posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF) and transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF) are the main surgical procedures used for treating degenerative lumbar disorders [6]. PLIF achieves adequate decompression through a posterior midline approach but causes significant damage to surrounding tissues, which may affect postoperative spinal stability [7]. TLIF is performed via the intervertebral foramen approach, resulting in minimal tissue damage and a low risk of nerve root injury. However, unilateral facet joint resection may reduce the stability of the internally fixed segment under torsional stress [8].

The modified TLIF (M-TLIF) procedure used in this study is an optimization of traditional TLIF, designed to enhance postoperative stability and fusion rates [9, 10]. The core modification lies in the approach to facet joint management. Unlike traditional TLIF, which typically involves complete resection of the facet joint on the approach side, M-TLIF strategically preserves the pars interarticularis. Specifically, on the more symptomatic side, the medial 3/4 of the inferior articular process and the medial 1/4 of the superior articular process are resected to access the disc space. On the contralateral side, the lateral 1/4 of the inferior articular process and the medial 1/4 of the superior articular process are resected. Crucially, the surgical procedure concludes with decortication and bone grafting of both facet joints to achieve bilateral fusion. This bilateral facet joint arthrodesis is hypothesized to provide greater rotational stability, share mechanical load with the anterior interbody construct, and create a larger surface area for bone healing, thereby potentially leading to superior fusion outcomes—a particular advantage in osteoporotic patients with compromised bone quality.

In patients with osteoporosis, the stability of pedicle screw fixation is a primary concern. To address this, the use of bone cement-augmented pedicle screws (CAPSs) has been proven effective for enhancing fixation strength [11]. This technique is now routinely applied in various lumbar fusion procedures. However, when the novel M-TLIF technique is compared with traditional PLIF in such osteoporotic patients, and when both groups are stabilized using CAPSs, the differences between the two methods in terms of fusion outcomes, complication control, and long-term stability remain unclear, and relevant research is still lacking.

Therefore, this study focused on patients with osteoporotic lumbar degenerative disease and compared the clinical efficacy of M-TLIF versus traditional PLIF, with all patients receiving bone cement-reinforced pedicle screws. The aim is to provide evidence-based guidance for selecting the optimal surgical approach for such patients, thereby reducing postoperative complications, improving long-term outcomes, and offering practical guidance for clinical practice.

Materials and methods

Participants

A retrospective analysis of the clinical data of 93 patients with lumbar degenerative disease and osteoporosis who underwent posterior lumbar fusion surgery with bone cement-reinforced pedicle screw placement at the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou University of Traditional Chinese Medicine from June 2021 to June 2023 was conducted. Among them, 49 patients underwent M-TLIF, and 44 patients underwent PLIF. This was a retrospective study, and the choice of surgical procedure was determined by the surgical team based on an assessment of factors including the type of pathology (e.g., central canal stenosis vs. unilateral foraminal stenosis), bone quality, and the surgeon’s comprehensive judgment. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) adult patients (aged > 45 years); (2) no significant improvement in symptoms after ≥ 3 months of standardized conservative treatment; (3) the presence of typical clinical symptoms such as low back pain, radiating pain in the lower limbs, intermittent claudication, or difficulty standing; (4) a diagnosis of lumbar degenerative disease (including lumbar spinal stenosis, lumbar spondylolisthesis, and degenerative disc disease) accompanied by osteoporosis via computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), plain radiographs, and bone density examinations. All patients enrolled met specific indications for lumbar fusion surgery, which included either: (a) radiographic evidence of segmental instability—defined as > 3 mm of translation or > 10° of angular motion on dynamic flexion–extension radiographs; and (b) degenerative spondylolisthesis (Meyerding Grade I or II). Both conditions were associated with persistent mechanical low back pain that failed conservative management; (5) complete medical records. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) surgical segment > 2; (2) previous surgical history at the surgical segment; (3) spinal lesions caused by tumors, inflammation, infectious diseases, or acute trauma; and (4) follow-up period < 24 months. This study protocol was approved by the Clinical Trial Ethics Review Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine.

Assessment and management of osteoporosis

Osteoporosis was diagnosed according to the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA). A bone mineral density (BMD) T-score of ≤ −2.5 standard deviations at the lumbar spine (L1–L4), femoral neck, or total hip was required for study inclusion. In cases where lumbar BMD assessment was confounded by severe degenerative changes (e.g., extensive facet hypertrophy, osteophytosis, or scoliosis), the T-score from the femoral neck or total hip was given precedence for diagnosis to ensure accuracy [12, 13].

Preoperative and postoperative medical management: A standardized protocol for anti-osteoporotic therapy was implemented for all enrolled patients. All patients received foundational supplementation with elemental calcium (1000–1200 mg/day) and vitamin D (800–1000 IU/day) for a minimum of 3 months prior to surgery. This foundational therapy was continued indefinitely postoperatively. In addition, based on the severity of osteoporosis and individual fracture risk assessment, the majority of patients received potent anti-osteoporotic agents. This pharmacological regimen was initiated once the diagnosis was confirmed preoperatively and was consistently maintained throughout the entire postoperative follow-up period to enhance bone quality, support fusion healing, and reduce the risk of secondary fractures. The specific agents included: ① bisphosphonates (e.g., zoledronic acid intravenously once yearly or alendronate orally weekly); ② anabolic agents (e.g., teriparatide subcutaneously daily); ③ RANK ligand inhibitors (e.g., denosumab subcutaneously every 6 months).

Implants and surgical technique

All surgical procedures utilized a titanium alloy pedicle screw system (Sanyou Medical, China), with screw diameters ranging from 6.5 to 7.0 mm and lengths (45–55 mm) selected based on patient anatomy. The screws were cannulated and fenestrated to allow for cement augmentation. Polyetheretherketone (PEEK) interbody cages (Sanyou Medical, China) were packed with a mixture of autologous and allogeneic bone. Cement augmentation was performed using polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) bone cement (Johnson & Johnson, USA). The cement was injected in its viscous, "toothpaste" phase under continuous fluoroscopic guidance, with a carefully controlled volume of 1.5–3.0 mL per screw, terminating immediately upon adequate trabecular interdigitation or any sign of leakage. No conflicts of interest are declared related to these devices. Notably, no intraoperative or postoperative complications related to cement augmentation, such as symptomatic embolism or neurologic compromise, occurred in this series.

Operation procedure

Comprehensive preoperative examinations and tests were conducted. Through preoperative discussions, the indications for surgery were clarified, contraindications were excluded, and patients were thoroughly communicated with before providing informed consent. The patients fasted and abstained from drinking for 8 h prior to surgery. Adequate blood supply was ensured, and the surgical site was prepared. Antibiotics were administered via intravenous infusion 30 min before the skin incision was made. Both groups of surgeries were performed by the same surgical team and included preoperative examinations, endotracheal intubation under general anesthesia, and routine disinfection and draping of the surgical site. Taking L5–S1 single-segment surgery as an example, the following describes two surgical methods.

M-TLIF

The patient was positioned prone, with an empty stomach prior to surgery. C-arm fluoroscopy was used to locate the L5/S1 intervertebral space. A midline posterior incision was made to incise the tissue, and the paraspinal muscles were dissected subperiosteally to expose the L5 and S1 spinous processes, laminae, and articular processes. L5 and S1 pedicle screws were implanted and fixed (the S1 screw uses three-cortical fixation to enhance stability), with the isthmus preserved. The affected side has the inner 3/4 of the L5 inferior articular process and the inner 1/4 of the S1 superior articular process resected; the contralateral side has the outer 1/4 of the L5 inferior articular process and the inner 1/4 of the S1 superior articular process resected. The lateral spinal canal was exposed, the vertebral lamina margin and ligamentum flavum were resected, and the dura mater and L5 and S1 nerve roots were protected. After the nerve root canal was decompressed, the nucleus pulposus was removed, and the cartilage of the L5 inferior endplate and S1 superior endplate was scraped until bleeding occurred. Autologous cancellous bone (harvested from the local laminectomy and facetectomy sites) mixed with allograft bone was filled into a polyetheretherketone (PEEK) cage, which was implanted through the affected intervertebral foramen for intervertebral space fixation. After confirming that there was no nerve root compression or bleeding, the articular surfaces of both articular processes were prepared with a burr, and bone particles were implanted to complete the bone grafting. C-arm fluoroscopy was performed again to confirm the stability of the internal fixation and the satisfactory position of the cage. The surgical field was irrigated, a negative pressure drainage tube was placed, and the incision was closed in layers with sterile dressing.

PLIF

The paravertebral tissues were separated via the same method, and pedicle screws were implanted. A bone knife was used to completely resect the L5 spinous process and lamina. An incision was made along the inner margin of the L5 inferior articular process toward the medial side, a 3–5 mm wide segment of the inferior articular process and isthmus was preserved, and the cancellous bone was removed. A nerve dissector was used to remove the yellow ligament at the inferior margin of the L5 lamina to facilitate lamina removal. During the procedure, care was taken to protect the dural sac to prevent tears. Both nerve roots were fully exposed, the intervertebral disc was managed, and the endplate cartilage was scraped off. After the cartilage plate was thoroughly cleaned, the intervertebral space was exposed. The autologous bone particles and allograft bone were placed into an appropriate polyetheretherketone (PEEK) cage, and compression fixation was applied to the intervertebral space. After confirming that the fixation position was correct, the incision was closed via the same method.

Postoperative treatment

All patients followed a standardized postoperative protocol. Prophylactic intravenous antibiotics (cefuroxime, 1.5 g every 8 h) were administered, with the duration of therapy (typically 24–48 h) guided by serial monitoring of inflammatory markers. Intravenous dexamethasone (5–10 mg/day for 3–5 days) was used to control neural edema, and neurotrophic agents (e.g., Methylcobalamin 0.5–1.0 mg once daily) were provided to support neurological recovery. Continuous electrocardiographic monitoring was maintained, and analgesics were administered as needed. Patients were advised to remain supine for the first 24–48 h to promote soft tissue stabilization, during which ankle pumping and lower limb rotation exercises were encouraged. Ambulation with a brace was initiated thereafter as tolerated. Surgical drains were removed when the 24-h output was less than 50 mL. A lumbar CT scan was obtained 3–5 days after surgery to evaluate implant position, cement distribution, and establish a baseline for fusion assessment. Wound dressings were changed regularly, and sutures were removed around 2 weeks postoperatively. Upon discharge, patients were instructed to wear a lumbar brace for 3 months during all ambulatory activities and to avoid prolonged sitting, bending, or heavy labor. Regular follow-up visits were scheduled throughout this period.

Clinical evaluation

Data on patient demographics (e.g., age, sex), operative time, and intraoperative blood loss were collected. Clinical outcomes were assessed using the Japanese Orthopedic Association (JOA) score for neurological function, the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) for disability, and the visual analog scale (VAS) for pain severity. These scores were recorded preoperatively, immediately postoperatively, and at the 2-year postoperatively. All clinical data were independently reviewed by two senior physicians.

Radiographic analyses and data collection

Lumbar spine anteroposterior and lateral radiographs were used to measure the coronal Cobb angle, lumbar lordosis angle (LL), segmental lordosis angle (SL), and the average surgical segment disc height (ASDH) preoperatively, immediately postoperatively, and at 2 years postoperatively. The Bridwell grading system [14] and the Marchi classification [15] were applied to assess the interbody fusion status and cage subsidence, respectively, on CT scans at the 2-year postoperatively. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed via SPSS 27.0 software. The quantitative data are expressed as the mean±standard deviation x¯±s: for normally distributed data, between-group comparisons were performed via the independent samples t test, and within-group comparisons at different time points were performed via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (pairwise comparisons were performed via the least significant difference (LSD) method); for non-normally distributed data, the rank-sum test was used. Categorical data were analyzed via the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. For ordinal data, comparisons between groups were performed via the Mann–Whitney U test, and comparisons at different time points were performed via the Friedman test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient demographics

This study included a total of 93 patients with lumbar degenerative disease complicated by osteoporosis, including 49 patients in the M-TLIF group and 44 patients in the PLIF group. The mean age in the M-TLIF group was 67.22 ± 6.34 years, and that in the PLIF group was 67.93 ± 6.21 years. No significant differences were found between groups in baseline characteristics, including demographics, surgical segments, comorbidities, and medication use (all P > 0.05, Table 1).

Comparison of clinical efficacy scores between the two groups of patients at each time point

Analysis of clinical efficacy scores revealed comparable outcomes between the two surgical groups. The VAS and ODI scores showed no statistically significant differences at any time point (all P > 0.05, Table 3). For the JOA score, although a statistically significant difference was observed at the 2-year postoperatively (25.73 ± 1.26 in PLIF vs. 25.12 ± 1.51 in M-TLIF, P < 0.05, Table 3), the absolute difference of 0.61 points is negligible when compared to the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for the JOA score, which is reported to be 12.8–24.5 points [16]. This interpretation is further strengthened by the direct comparison of improvements from baseline (Δ scores), which confirmed no significant intergroup differences in the magnitude of improvement for the JOA, ODI, or VAS scores at either the immediate or 2-year postoperative assessment (all P > 0.05, Table 4). Collectively, these results demonstrate that M-TLIF and PLIF have comparable clinical efficacy in alleviating pain, restoring function, and improving long-term neurological status.

Discussion

The lumbar spine, as the hub of trunk movement in the human body, performs important functions, such as weight-bearing, shock absorption, and movement. Factors such as long-term heavy physical labor can accelerate lumbar degeneration, triggering lumbar disc herniation, spinal stenosis, and other lumbar degenerative diseases, which severely affect patients’ quality of life [17, 18]. For patients with obvious symptoms of lumbar degeneration, surgical treatment is often more effective than conservative treatment [19]. Among the many surgical procedures available, PLIF and TLIF are commonly used to treat degenerative lumbar spine disorders [20]. The M-TLIF procedure used in this study is an optimized version of the traditional TLIF procedure, providing a new treatment option for patients with osteoporotic degenerative lumbar spine disorders.

Both the PLIF and TLIF procedures can accurately locate and expose the affected area, effectively releasing the dura mater and nerve roots. While internal fixation is performed, bone grafting is used to achieve spinal fusion, restoring vertebral height and physiological lordosis. Both procedures have high fusion success rates [21, 22]. Among these, the PLIF procedure offers a wider field of view and better exposure of the nerve roots. Under direct observation of the nerve roots and dural sac, posterior intervertebral bone grafting is performed, ensuring good safety [23]. The TLIF procedure allows direct visualization during intervertebral decompression and fusion, resulting in better spinal stability. Fewer accessory structures need to be removed during surgery, causing less damage to the lumbar anatomical structure. There is less bleeding during surgery, and patients have a relatively shorter bed rest period after surgery [24, 25].

The core difference between M-TLIF and traditional TLIF lies in the details of the surgical procedure. Traditional TLIF involves a larger range of laminectomy, with most of the affected facet joints being resected, and facet joint bone grafting is typically performed only on the surgical side; in contrast, M-TLIF preserves the pars interarticularis, resects the medial 3/4 of the L5 inferior articular process and the medial 1/4 of the S1 superior articular process on the degenerative side; on the contralateral side, the lateral 1/4 of the L5 inferior articular process and the medial 1/4 of the S1 superior articular process are resected, and performs bilateral facet joint bone grafting at the end of the procedure [9]. This modification reduces damage to the spinal accessory structures and enhances fusion outcomes through bilateral facet joint bone grafting. Biomechanically, bilateral facet joint fusion effectively reduces stress on the internal fixation system, lowering the incidence of fatigue fractures in the internal fixation system and providing better postoperative stability for patients with osteoporosis [10].

The results of this study revealed no statistically significant differences in baseline characteristics between the two groups (P > 0.05), ensuring the comparability of the study. Analysis of the perioperative indicators per surgical segment revealed a nuanced profile. The M-TLIF group demonstrated a significantly longer operative time per segment (P < 0.05). This is attributed to the technical demands of the procedure, which involves meticulous bone work to preserve the pars interarticularis and perform bilateral facet joint grafting [9, 10]. Despite the longer time, this technical investment was associated with beneficial outcomes: the M-TLIF group showed a trend towards reduced blood loss per segment and required significantly less bone cement per screw (P < 0.05). This aligns with the principle of M-TLIF in preserving bony structures like the isthmus, which provides a more stable screw fixation foundation and reduces the potential risk of bone cement leakage [26]. Furthermore, the M-TLIF group exhibited consistent, though non-significant, advantages in early recovery, including earlier ambulation and full weight-bearing, clinically corroborating its less invasive nature.

The trend towards earlier functional recovery with M-TLIF may contribute to its efficacy in alleviating pain and functional impairment, as reflected by the comparable VAS and ODI scores between the groups. In terms of clinical efficacy scores, both groups of patients showed significant improvements in postoperative lower limb pain VAS and ODI scores, with no significant differences between the groups, indicating that the two surgical procedures are equally effective in alleviating pain and functional impairment. A statistically significant difference in JOA score was noted at the 2-year postoperatively. However, the magnitude of this difference (0.61 points) is clinically irrelevant, as it constitutes only a small fraction (approximately 2.5%−4.8%) of the established minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for the JOA score [16]. Consequently, this finding does not represent a meaningful advantage for either technique, and it is accurate to conclude that both M-TLIF and PLIF facilitate equivalent and excellent long-term neurological recovery. The broader surgical field of view in PLIF, enabling more direct decompression [27], and the less invasive nature of M-TLIF, which may promote earlier rehabilitation [28], represent distinct technical characteristics that ultimately lead to comparable and satisfactory clinical outcomes for their respective indications.

In terms of imaging parameters, there were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of ASDH, the coronal Cobb angle, or LL. However, the SL angle was greater in the PLIF group (P < 0.05), a finding consistent with its technical characterization of extensive posterior exposure, which facilitates a more substantial correction of segmental lordosis [29]. Conversely, the M-TLIF group demonstrated superior fusion outcomes (P < 0.05), which can be attributed to its minimal invasiveness, preservation of bone and soft tissue, and bilateral facet joint grafting, collectively creating an optimal osteogenic environment—a particular advantage in osteoporotic patients [30, 31]. No significant intergroup difference was observed in cage subsidence, indicating that bone cement-augmented pedicle screws provided equally stable fixation in both procedures.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, its retrospective and single-center design inherently introduces potential selection bias and limits the generalizability of our findings. A prospective, multicenter study would be required to validate these results. Additionally, the sample size was relatively small, and the follow-up duration was limited to 2 years. Future research should include multicenter, long-term follow-up studies with larger cohorts to further explore the long-term outcomes of the two procedures and the differences between M-TLIF and other techniques, providing more robust evidence for clinical practice.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that both M-TLIF and PLIF are effective for patients with osteoporotic lumbar degenerative disease, providing equivalent and excellent long-term clinical outcomes. The choice between techniques should be guided by their distinct procedural advantages. The M-TLIF technique, with its minimal invasiveness, results in superior interbody fusion rates and reduced bone cement usage, making it particularly suitable when achieving robust fusion is the primary concern. Conversely, the PLIF technique affords a greater capacity for segmental lordosis correction and more direct central decompression, rendering it a strong option when restoring sagittal alignment is a key surgical goal. Clinicians should therefore select the procedure based on the patient’s specific pathological characteristics and surgical objectives.
