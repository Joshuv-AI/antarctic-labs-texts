Lysozyme revisited: evaluating models of a reference protein in structural biology
Authors: Alexander Wlodawer, Pawel Rubach, Zbigniew Dauter, Wojciech Dec, Dariusz Brzezinski, Mariusz Jaskolski, Wladek Minor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe have evaluated the quality of over 1200 crystal structures of hen egg-white lysozyme (HEWL) deposited in the Protein Data Bank (PDB). These structures, collected over nearly 50 years, vary in quality, despite all representing essentially the same small enzyme consisting of 129 amino acid residues. Some of the entries originated from studies of the binding of small-molecule ligands to HEWL, whereas the majority of deposits represent the outcomes of tests of new experimental approaches to crystallization and data collection and/or evaluations of new computational protocols. We found no correlation between Rfree, which is a measure of structure quality, and Rmerge, an indicator of raw data quality, for 136 near-atomic-resolution lysozyme structures. We found out that many of the lysozyme structures deposited as a result of methodology evaluation are not fully or correctly refined. We, therefore, propose that such structures be appropriately flagged in the PDB with a CAVEAT record to prevent their inadvertent inclusion in large-scale data mining analyses or training sets for artificial intelligence methods.Keywords: Structure quality, Reference protein, Benchmark proteins, Artificial intelligence (AI), Data integrity, Training set optimization

Introduction

The Protein Data Bank (PDB), established in 1971 (Protein Data Bank, 1971), is the global repository of macromolecular structures determined by X-ray, electron, or neutron crystallography, by nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (NMR), or by cryogenic electron microscopy (cryoEM) (Berman et al., 2000; Protein Data Bank Consortium, 2018). Currently, most funding agencies and scientific journals require that every structure described in a peer-reviewed publication should be deposited in the PDB, together with the experimental (Helliwell et al., 2019) and reduced data used for its determination (C.o.B.M. International Union of Crystallography, 1989; Wlodawer, 1997; Wlodawer, 2007). Whereas the deposition of novel macromolecular structures, or complexes with novel ligands, is central to structural biology and drug discovery, models of macromolecular targets may also arise from tests of various methods used for their determination. One of the most prominent targets of such investigations is hen egg-white lysozyme (HEWL). Its crystal structure was the first one to be determined for any enzyme (Blake et al., 1962, 1965). Early on, diffraction data obtained from crystals of HEWL were used to test different structure refinement protocols, resulting in six deposits representing variation in the application of these procedures [PDB IDs 1lyz, 2lyz, 3lyz, 4lyz, 5lyz and 6lyz; (Diamond, 1974)], as well as a deposit resulting from testing of novel model building procedures [7lyz; (Herzberg and Sussman, 1983)], to provide just a few examples. HEWL has become a significant platform for testing crystallographic methods due to its robust and reproducible crystallization, with a large number of such test structures found among the over 1200 lysozyme deposits in the PDB (however, identification of such structures is difficult to ascertain, since they are mostly not clearly identified). Another large group of HEWL structures represents complexes of this enzyme with small-molecule ligands.

When a virtually identical structure is deposited multiple times, whether with new ligands or as a result of methodological development, it becomes interesting to compare these models to investigate the quality of such deposits and their impact on the overall reliability of data included in the PDB. Such data are extensively utilized not only by scientists interested in the properties of a particular macromolecule, but also, with increasing importance, as training sets for computational structure prediction, exemplified by the various versions of AlphaFold (Jumper et al., 2021; Abramson et al., 2024), RoseTTAFold (Baek et al., 2021), or ESMfold (Lin et al., 2023), to name just a few. Lysozyme, with over one thousand deposits in the PDB, provides an excellent example of structural data that begs for an investigation of its quality. We will, therefore, ask whether the quality of structures resulting from method development is comparable to that of macromolecular structures and their complexes studied as part of biologically-oriented projects. The surprising discovery that structures representing methodological development are, on average, of lower quality raises the question of whether they should have been deposited at all or more clearly annotated. Such problems should be regarded in the context of training sets for AI-based structure prediction, as the presence of low-quality “methodological structures” complicates the selection of reliable data for training structure-prediction algorithms. It is not clear, however, how lower-quality structures are filtered out in preparation of the AI training sets. We will also address the question of why structures of a well-defined protein model, well established for over 30 years, are not of the highest quality.

Materials and methods

The starting point for collecting the data was a search for 95% identity with the sequence of the deposit 1lyz, dating from 1975. This search was conducted using the RCSB clusters computed with the MMseqs2 Release 15 software and available at: https://www.rcsb.org/docs/grouping-structures/sequence-based-clustering. This search, performed on June 4, 2025, revealed 1255 crystal structures, for which detailed information was acquired from mmCIF files and the PDB validation reports. The following quality parameters were obtained: resolution, Rmerge, Rfree, <I/σ> in the last shell, clashscore, rotamer outliers, Ramachandran outliers, RSRZ outliers, and number of solvent molecules. Additionally, each structure re-refined by PDB-REDO (Joosten et al., 2012) (1165 total) was analyzed, and the appropriate parameters were extracted, in particular: Rmerge (RFIN), Rfree (RFFIN). Since some geometrical quality parameters were not available in PDB-REDO, the MolProbity (Chen et al., 2010) utility from CCP4 (Agirre et al., 2023) distribution version 9.0.004 was used to compute the missing data. These included, in particular, clashscore, MolProbity Score, rotamer outliers, and Ramachandran outliers. However, PDB-REDO removes solvent atoms that are not supported by electron density but does not add those that are clearly visible. The PQ1 measure (Brzezinski et al., 2020) was computed for all structures in the PDB, and the values for the analyzed dataset were extracted. A complete list of the extracted parameters that also includes PQ1 values computed for all avian lysozyme structures is provided in the Supplementary File.

Results

Structures of hen egg-white lysozyme in the PDB

As of June 4, 2025, the PDB held 1255 structures of HEWL, either by itself or in complexes with other proteins and small ligands. When crystallized by itself or with small ligands, HEWL is most commonly found in tetragonal crystals in space group P43212, with a single molecule in the asymmetric unit. More than a thousand such structures (1016) have been deposited in the PDB so far. An important set of crystal structures of HEWL obtained in the presence of nitrate in the crystallization media comprises 34 structures in the triclinic space group P1. These crystals provided the highest-resolution diffraction data for this protein (Wang et al., 2007). Other crystal forms include the monoclinic space groups P21 (72 structures) and C2 (21 structures), the orthorhombic space groups P212121 (65 structures) and P21212 (7 structures). The remaining structures in other space groups are predominantly complexes of lysozyme with other proteins, mostly antibody fragments, and they will not be discussed here any further. Thus, this very rich database of structures provides an excellent basis for investigating the quality of PDB-deposited data.

The structures analyzed here were determined using diffraction data extending from the low resolution of 6 Å (legacy structures with PDB IDs 1lzh, 2lzh) to ultrahigh resolution of 0.65 Å (2vb1). The reported Rfree values, introduced in 1992 (Brünger, 1992), range from 0.095 (2vb1) to 0.426 (7tum). As judged by the values of these parameters, as well as the values of the combined quality parameter PQ1 (Brzezinski et al., 2020; Shao et al., 2017) that range from the low of 0.01 (PDB ID 1lyz) to the high of 1.0 (6o2h), the quality of these structures represents the whole spectrum of PDB deposits. The definition and expected ranges of various crystallographic metrics were described previously (Wlodawer et al., 2013). PQ1 ranges from 0-to-1 for the worst-to-best structures in the PDB (Brzezinski et al., 2020; Shao et al., 2017). Not surprisingly, the best quality is correlated with the highest resolution of the diffraction data, as discussed in detail below, although the quality of some such structures still leaves much to be desired.

Lysozyme structures with apparently lower quality

In this section, our analysis is limited only to structures deposited in the last 20 years, since most of the earlier deposits resulted from the use of procedures for structure determination and refinement that are now obsolete. Moreover, many early deposits did not include structure factors, i.e., data necessary to evaluate the agreement between the structural model and electron density maps. Only selected examples of apparently poorer structures are discussed below, with a complete list of PQ1 values for all avian lysozyme structures provided in the Supplementary File. We will start our analysis with structures resulting from method development, followed by structures of complexes of lysozyme with small molecules. We will use selected structures to illustrate the encountered problems but will not discuss all structures with similar shortcomings.

Crystallographic experiments at the recently introduced new-generation high-brilliance XFEL (X-ray free-electron laser) sources are inherently expensive, but their cost is justified by the unique structural information they can provide, based on data that cannot be obtained using more conventional techniques, such as synchrotron crystallography or cryoEM. However, examination of certain PDB depositions resulting from tests of femtosecond data collection using XFEL sources raises significant questions about whether the handling of the data matches the state-of-the-art instrumentation.

For example, the 7tum model [3.2 Å resolution; (Holmes et al., 2022)] exhibits an unusually high Rfree value of 0.426, although its poor PQ1 parameter (0.171) was not among the lowest. The refined model, obtained by molecular replacement, largely retains the initial geometry during refinement, suggesting only limited optimization. Although the reported diffraction data quality was poor (CC1/2 = 0.412, Rmerge not provided), the 2mFo-DFc electron density map remains in reasonable agreement with the model, despite noticeable breaks in main-chain connectivity displayed at the 1.0σ contour level. By contrast, the 6ftr model—also derived from femtosecond crystallography tests (Wiedorn et al., 2018)—was solved earlier at 1.76 Å resolution and refined with much more satisfactory parameters (Rfree = 0.170), illustrating the potential quality achievable with XFEL experiments.

The model 4wmg resulted from another methodological study that used XFEL to investigate microcrystals in a microfluidic trap array (Lyubimov et al., 2015). This was a medium-resolution structure (2.5 Å) refined to Rfree 0.307 (PQ1 0.010). The dataset was rather incomplete, with only 81.5% of observed reflections, an <I/σ> of 8.0, and multiplicity of 4.4. This is a clear case of a study that was only useful to validate an experimental approach and does not represent a well-refined model.

The 8y1s crystal structure determined at 1.52 Å provides another example of a relatively poor model resulting from method testing; in this case, a new software for processing Laue diffraction data (Wang et al., 2025). It is characterized by high Rfree (0.318), a considerable number of RSRZ outliers (7%), and a clashscore of 13.0. Such refinement parameters are much worse than their counterparts for other structures determined at similar resolution.

Several important points were raised by our analysis of the 1.5 Å structures 5lio and 5lin, determined during early testing of the Eiger detector (Casanas et al., 2016). The low PQ1 value for 5lio (0.103) was due to a large extent to a high number of RSRZ outliers (18.6%). However, when these coordinates were submitted by us to the current version of the OneDep PDB deposition tool (Young et al., 2017), the number of such outliers was reported as only 1.6%. Similarly, for the model 5lin, the RSRZ outliers were reduced from 2.3% to zero, and sidechain outliers from 2.9% to 1.9%. These and other discrepancies were reported to the PDB as part of our long-time collaboration with the PDB to debug the deposition process and the archive itself. However, another problem became visible when the 2mFo-DFc maps for these two models were displayed. It is very clear that the conformation of the side chains of Asp18, Asn19, and Ser72 does not fit the electron density (Fig. 1), yet these problems were not fully highlighted by the values of the calculated map correlation coefficients. Since the main chain of these residues fits the electron density well, the overall CC values were acceptable despite the modeling errors. We would like to suggest that such correlations should be calculated separately for the side chains and for the whole amino acid residues, in order to make them more meaningful.

The 2Fo-Fc electron density contoured at 1.0σ (blue) clearly shows that Asp18 was not fitted correctly in the deposited model, whereas it fits the electron density very well after the adjustments. The 1.6 Å structure 8fsx (unpublished) is one among 30 near-simultaneous deposits (8fp6, 8fp7, 8fp8, 8fpb, 8fpd, 8fpm, 8fpn, 8fpp, 8fpr, 8fpu, 8fry, 8fs0, 8fs9, 8fsa, 8fsc, 8fsf, 8fsg, 8fsh, 8fsm, 8fsn, 8fst, 8fsu, 8fsv, 8fsw, 8fsx, 8fsy, 8ft0, 8ft1, 8ft2, 8ft3), all refined at the same resolution. They represent HEWL crystals in the tetragonal space group, with sodium ions being the only ligands, and are characterized by a large spread of Rmerge (0.04 to 0.277) and Rfree (0.228 to 0.294). The refinement statistics of 8fsx are poor (Rfree 0.294, clashscore 8.0, RSRZ outliers 12.4%), yielding a PQ1 of 0.057, among the worst for recent deposits. It is not obvious what the purpose of investigating this series of structures was, since they remain unpublished over two years after their release by the PDB. However, other large series of HEWL structures were previously investigated by the same laboratory in order to evaluate the reproducibility and distribution of the crystallographic B-factors (Ramos et al., 2022) or to link the B-factors with temperature-induced conformational transitions (de Sá Ribeiro and Lima, 2023).

The deposit 8sil represents a 1.7 Å structure of the tetragonal crystal of lysozyme, determined to test the performance of cyclic olefin copolymer-based microfluidic chips (Liu et al., 2023). The low value of PQ1 (0.158) is partly due to the remarkably high percentage of RSRZ outliers (62.0%) and to fairly high Rfree (0.273). Strangely, when those coordinates were submitted to the current OneDep protocol of the PDB, the fraction of RSRZ outliers decreased to 9.3%, while the Rfree increased to 0.282. These are very significant differences that appear to be entirely due to changes in the validation software used by the PDB. Our own experience shows that many software packages are not thoroughly tested and debugged for more difficult and exceptional cases. Moreover, reporting errors or providing general feedback to software developers is often done only through social media/mailing lists such as the Phenix bulletin board [phenixbb@phenix-online.org] or CCP4 bulletin board [https://www.ccp4.ac.uk/the-ccp4-bulletin-board]. In contrast, a simple, dedicated webpage is a far more appropriate and effective solution [ (Zheng et al., 2017); https://feedback.minorlab.org/cmm/].

Whereas the structures discussed above primarily resulted from method testing, problematic structures of complexes of lysozyme with small-molecule ligands can also be found, as shown by the examples below. A comparatively high resolution (1.78 Å) structure with very poor PQ1 value (0.022) is 9ex1 (Tito et al., 2024). This model represents the tetragonal crystal form of a polyoxidovanadate adduct of lysozyme. The diffraction data are characterized by an acceptable Rmerge (0.077) and very high multiplicity (22.9). The electron density for both the protein and the ligand appears to be quite good, although a fairly large number of side chains are not covered by the 2mFo-DFc map contoured at the 1.0σ level, thus leading to a high number of RSRZ outliers. There are also two Ramachandran outliers in this model that lower the PQ1 value. The quality indicators for two other structures in this series, 9ex2 determined at 1.17 Å and 9ex0 at 1.45 Å, are much more acceptable.

One of the relatively high-resolution structures (1.48 Å) with low PQ1 (0.070) is 9hmk (Ferraro et al., 2025). That structure was determined as part of an investigation of the binding of iodinated versions of picoplatin, an anticancer compound, to several proteins (Ferraro et al., 2025). The Rfree and RSRZ values are high (0.260 and 10.1%, respectively), and the ligand of interest was not fully modeled. Only peaks corresponding to partially occupied platinum and iodine sites were included, but some of the Pt-I distances do not seem to correspond to the expected values of ∼2.6 Å, and some iodine atoms are not placed in clear electron density. The two other structures in this series (9hlk, 9hmq) suffer from similar problems, although their resolution is significantly worse (1.96 and 2.25 Å, respectively).

Another high-resolution (1.29 Å) structure with low PQ1 (0.105) is 8ph5 (Terán et al., 2023), representing a complex of lysozyme with a diruthenium compound. This model is characterized by a large Rfree (0.290) and 7% of RSRZ outliers. Nevertheless, the electron density map appears to be of good quality, also covering most of the two ligand molecules that were refined with partial occupancy of 0.3. A HEPES molecule (a component of the crystallization buffer) is in excellent electron density. With the apparent quality of the diffraction data being acceptable (Rmerge 0.094, <I/σ> 16.5, multiplicity 21.4), the reasons for the relatively poor quality indicators for this structure are not obvious.

Four PDB deposits of the models of complexes of lysozyme with Anderson-Evans polyoxometalates (8bqp, 8bqq, 8bqr, 8bqt) were described in a single publication (Lentink et al., 2023). Three of them were determined at a resolution higher than 2 Å and do not exhibit any major problems, whereas structure 8bqr, determined at the resolution of 2.32 Å, has some very obvious errors. Examples are the N-C bond lengths of 1.01 Å at Arg45-Asn46 and of only 0.35 Å at Ile124-Arg125 (Fig. 2), among other similarly impossible bond length values. A significant number of bond angle outliers are also present, and 11 side chains were marked with RSRZ above 2.0 due to their suboptimal fit to the electron density. These anomalies are clearly highlighted in the PDB validation report, yet they were not rectified before structure deposition.

Severe modeling problems. The coordinates and 2mFo-DFc electron density map contoured at the 1σ level for residues Arg45 (top) and Ile124 (bottom) in the PDB model 8bqr. The indicated peptide bonds in the main chain are completely outside of the acceptable range. PDB-REDO corrected these bond lengths to near ideal values.

A puzzling feature of the structure 8h8w (Kim and Nam, 2024) is the numbering of protein residues that is offset by 18. The reason for this unusual numbering scheme is not explained in either the PDB deposit or in the relevant publication (presumably counting the signal peptide), but it makes comparisons with other structures cumbersome. This structure is also characterized by an unreasonably high number of RSRZ outliers (61.2%).

Structural mapping of the most frequent problems with lysozyme depositions

We analyzed the question of structural location of the most frequent/severe problems encountered with the lysozyme deposits in the PDB. For this purpose, we used the XML codes of the Validation Reports, from which several residue statistics were extracted/calculated over all HEWL deposits. Specifically, we calculated how often a residue is an outlier, where the outlier criterion means RSRZ >2 or a Ramachandran outlier (rama) or rotamer outlier (rota). This basically gave us an estimate of how often a given residue was problematic in the analyzed structures. The algorithm excluded missing/unmodeled residues from the ratio denominator for that residue. If an attribute (RSRZ, rama, or rota) was missing in the validation report, it was treated as no outlier for that criterion for that residue in the structure in question. The latter situation (missing value) was encountered the following number of times: rama missing for 656 residues (2.69%) and rota missing for 4705 residues (19.26%). Although the rota absences might appear excessive, we have to realize that many of them may correspond to the 12 Gly +12 Ala +2 Pro residues in the HEWL sequence. Having the quality indices for each residue in the HEWL sequence estimated, we mapped them on the HEWL structure, as illustrated in Fig. 3. As expected, the majority of the most problematic areas are located on the protein surface in general, and at surface loops and termini in particular.

Near-atomic and subatomic-resolution structures of lysozyme

The number of crystal structures of HEWL (plus a structure of turkey egg white lysozyme, 1jse) determined at near-atomic resolution (here arbitrarily set at 1.25 Å rather than the more common 1.2 Å in order to increase the size of the evaluated dataset) and deposited in the PDB is 182 (as of June 4, 2025). It is thus possible to evaluate the apparent quality of a large number of presumably comparable PDB entries to investigate whether the availability of very high resolution diffraction data always translates to the “best” crystal structures.

As is the case with the full set of HEWL structures, the predominant crystal form of the high-resolution structures is the tetragonal space group P43212 (143 PDB entries), with one protein molecule in the asymmetric unit. Eighteen structures were determined for crystals in the triclinic space group P1, including the highest-resolution structure of this protein (and one of the highest-resolution structures of any protein available in the PDB), determined at 0.65 Å [PDB ID 2vb1; (Wang et al., 2007)]. The triclinic asymmetric unit contains one lysozyme molecule in 16 PDB entries, with two molecules present in 1v7t and three in 6a10. Nine entries are in the orthorhombic space group P212121, with molecular packing resembling that in the tetragonal crystals. Another group of 11 structures is in two variants of the monoclinic space group P21, with either one or two molecules in the asymmetric unit. One additional monoclinic structure (PDB ID 3d9a) represents a complex of lysozyme with an Fab antibody fragment.

Both, Rmerge, which indicates the quality and reproducibility of the raw diffraction data, and Rfree, which illustrates the agreement of the refined model with the diffraction data, are reported for 136 deposits. The values of Rmerge range from the excellent 0.013 [for PDB ID 8rny; (Oszajca et al., 2024)] to 0.20 [for PDB ID 6abn; (Seraj et al., 2020)]. The Rmerge reported as 0.411 for dataset 7lpl (Yabukarski et al., 2022) is so far out of range compared to all other structures that we suspect a typographical error, or that the value of Rmerge was reported for the outermost resolution shell (which is not listed despite 100% completeness). The values of Rfree range from the low 0.095 [PDB ID 2vb1; (Wang et al., 2007)] to the high 0.256 [PDB ID 9gog; (Paolillo et al., 2025)]. Interestingly and somewhat unexpectedly for less experienced crystallographers, there appears to be no correlation between these two quality indicators for lysozymes, and the correlation for all other high-resolution crystal structures in the PDB is relatively weak (Fig. 4).

As all these structures were determined by molecular replacement using very reliable starting models, there are almost no departures from the allowed Ramachandran angles. Only a single residue is outside of the expected range in 13 models, in the area of less clear electron density, due most likely to problems with refinement restraints. On the other hand, the values of clashscore vary from 0.0 (7ol8, 6s7n, 6f9y) to 50.1 (3wl2), although we found that this parameter, as reported in the official PDB validation reports, is sometimes irreproducible for unclear reasons. Since all structures discussed in this section were determined at near-atomic or higher resolution, it is not surprising that the PQ1 values are lower than the average (0.500) for only 27 of them.

However, it is of interest to compare these high-resolution structures of a well-known protein to all PDB structures refined at comparable resolution. As shown in Table 1, both the PQ1 quality indicators and just the values of Rfree are worse for these lysozyme structures compared to their counterparts for other proteins that exclude HEWL. We suspect that structures determined in order to test methodology might be refined less carefully than novel structures in the PDB.

The structure 2vb1 (Wang et al., 2007) of the triclinic crystals was refined to a very low Rfree of 0.095 at 0.65 Å resolution. Unfortunately, identification of free reflections was not maintained in the deposited data, making verification impossible. The number of amino acid residues with more than one conformation was 49 (out of 129), of which 46 also had multiple orientations of the main chain. The only geometric parameter in the average, rather than excellent, range was the clashscore of 6.0, leading to a PQ1 of 0.969, still among the best in the entire PDB.

The structure with the most ideal quality indicators is that of triclinic lysozyme refined at 1.21 Å resolution (PDB ID 6o2h (Meisburger et al., 2020). The diffraction data for this dataset were measured at room temperature. The PQ1 value is 1.000, with zero clashscore, no Ramachandran outliers, and 99.2% of favored rotamers, resulting in MolProbity percentile of 100. Nevertheless, as reported by MolProbity, 24 bonds and 20 bond angles are outside of the strictly acceptable range. Sixteen residues exhibit multiple orientations for both the main chain and the side chains. The value of Rfree (0.117) is among the lowest for all structures compared here.

An interesting case is provided by four atomic-resolution structures of triclinic lysozyme determined by X-ray and neutron diffraction at either cryogenic or room temperature (Ramos et al., 2025). These matched datasets (room temperature 8rlf X-ray and 8rlh neutron; cryogenic 8rlg X-ray and 8rli neutron) were refined very carefully and provide not only a wealth of information for a detailed description of the active site area and for comparison of the influence of data collection temperature on the structure, but also for experimental placement of hydrogen or deuterium atoms, which in most other crystal structures are computationally generated, if at all available.

The tetragonal structure 8zst (MacCarthy et al., 2025) was determined at a significantly higher resolution (0.8 Å) than all its isomorphous counterparts. The refinement parameters are very good (Rfree calculated by DCC 0.137, good geometry, PQ1 0.915), nevertheless, some problems are still evident. Assessment of the actual quality of the diffraction data presents some difficulties, as no data processing statistics are included in the PDB deposit, other than the CC1/2 value of 0.999 and <I/σ(I)> of 15.54. However, although the refinement data correspond to the full resolution of 0.8 Å, data processing information contained in the reference manuscript (MacCarthy et al., 2025) is limited to 0.88 Å. Another peculiarity of this structure is the inclusion of a nitrate ion, most likely instead of an acetate, although no nitrate is listed among the ingredients of the crystallization media (crystallization conditions that include the presence of acetate are only found in the PDB file, but not in the manuscript). For some atoms that are located in the same part of the electron density map and assigned partial occupancy, the sum of occupancies exceeds 1.0 (for example, water molecules 1141 and 1270, or 1117 and 1305). Finally, multiple orientations of a number of side chains (for example, Leu17, Asp18, Leu129) are not fully supported by the electron density.

The highest calculated PQ1 value (0.988) for any tetragonal structure described in this section was for 6fsj (Pichlo et al., 2018). That structure was refined at 1.2 Å resolution with Rfree of 0.170. The clashscore was 3.0 and RSRZ 0.8%, indicating very good agreement between the model and the electron density.

On the other end of the quality spectrum, the high-resolution structure of tetragonal lysozyme with the lowest PQ1 value (0.070) was 4n8z (Yin et al., 2014). Those crystals were obtained as part of a series of experiments involving fragment screening with acoustic in situ co-crystallization of the protein with fragment libraries on pin-mounted data-collection micromeshes. Although the diffraction data were of good quality with an acceptable Rmerge (0.079), the refinement was characterized by high Rfree (0.250), a large number of RSRZ outliers (10.9%), and a very high clashscore of 22.0, clearly showing that not all high-resolution structures deposited in the PDB are of the highest quality. Nevertheless, the electron density map is very clear, and the model fits it well, except for residues 60-63, where modeling of two orientations of the main chain is rather dubious.

A tetragonal structure with the highest Rfree value (0.264) was 9gog (Paolillo et al., 2025), determined at 1.24 Å resolution. No value of Rmerge is listed in either the deposit or in the publication, the only quality index of the diffraction data being the CC1/2 value of 0.999. Several poor side chain rotamers and two bad angles are noted by MolProbity. Additionally, several side chains (Asn19, Asn55, Arg61, Asn103, and Gln121) do not have convincing electron density, thus explaining the relatively high percentage of RSRZ outliers. Nevertheless, the main chain of the molecule fits the electron density quite well.

The best structure in an orthorhombic space group is 6f9z (Plaza-Garrido et al., 2018), with PQ1 0.998, no Ramachandran, side chain, or RSRZ outliers, no clashes, and with Rfree 0.178 (according to the depositors). It should be noted that both the Rwork and Rfree are listed as 0.160 in the DCC report, indicating that in all probability the test reflections were not marked correctly in the deposit.

HEWL molecules are more densely packed in the triclinic crystals (Matthews Vm ∼1.7 Å3/Da) than in the predominant tetragonal crystals (Vm ∼ 1.8 Å3/Da). The number of water molecule sites (some with partial occupancy) modeled in the triclinic crystals ranges from the low of 79 (5idd) to the high of 245 (3lzt). In the highest-resolution structure (2vb1), the number of water sites is 170, which is in good agreement with the expectations based on the average number of water molecules per residue in all PDB deposits as a function of the resolution of the diffraction data (Wlodawer et al., 2024a). The number of water sites modeled in the tetragonal crystals ranges from only 49 (5kxz) to as many as 254 (5f14). This very large spread of the number of modeled water molecules is most likely due to choices made during structure refinement rather than to the true presence of the solvent peaks in the electron density maps. For example, for the model 5f14, PDB-REDO improved Rfree from 0.197 to 0.166 and, at the same time, reduced the number of water sites from 254 to 204.

It is of interest to compare the actual coordinates of these high-resolution lysozyme structures. However, a problem here is which structure should be chosen as a reference. An additional complication is due to the presence of multiple orientations for parts of the main chain in the structures determined at the highest resolution, raising the question of how structure superposition programs, such as SSM (Orengo and Taylor, 1996) as implemented in Coot (Emsley et al., 2010), treat such cases. With this caveat in mind, we used the default settings of SSM to compare the coordinates of the two highest-resolution structures in the tetragonal crystal form, 8zst (0.8 Å) and 7yrk (0.92 Å), receiving an rmsd value of 0.273 Å for all 129 Cα atoms. A much larger rmsd (0.573 Å) was found in a comparison of two triclinic structures, 2vb1 (0.65 Å) and 7p6m (0.89 Å). Superposition of the triclinic 2vb1 and tetragonal 8zst models yielded an rmsd of 0.822 Å; this comparatively large value is evidently influenced by differences in crystal packing.

An unnecessary inconvenience encountered with comparison of HEWL structures, and indeed of any isomorphous structures, is their very inconsistent placement in the unit cell. There is really no need to invoke “molecular replacement” for solving structures in the tetragonal unit cell, since each new structure can be simply refined based on practically any previously deposited model. This problem was raised many times in the past and tools for consistent placement in the unit cell are available, although not often used (Dauter and Wlodawer, 2018). A glaring example of model placement outside of the unit cell in two recent structures (8h8w and 8ph5) is shown in Fig. 5. The structure 8fsx, also displayed, was placed in agreement with the recommendations provided by the program Achesym (Kowiel et al., 2014).

Discussion

HEWL is widely used as a standard test protein because it combines properties that make it ideal for methods development, instrument calibration, and teaching. It is a small, stable, and well-characterized enzyme that crystallizes readily and reproducibly, robustly yielding high-quality crystals. These features make HEWL an excellent model system for testing synchrotron and XFEL beamline performance, benchmarking data-processing software, and validating new crystallographic methodologies.

The PDB contains numerous high-quality, high-resolution HEWL structures that serve as valuable reference models for molecular replacement and refinement validation. However, it was surprising to discover that many relatively recent HEWL structures required substantial correction by PDB-REDO, an automated re-refinement pipeline. This finding highlights the persistent issue of model quality problems, even in well-studied systems. Furthermore, since PDB-REDO does not fully address solvent modeling (it eliminates spurious water molecules, but does not add the missing ones), some of these structures can be further improved through careful manual inspection and refinement.

It is essential to recognize that the PDB serves as a repository, with the role of the curators limited only to assuring that the deposits are complete and follow the technical rules. Moreover, the PDB has an obligation to accept all structure depositions that meet technical submission requirements. The PDB itself cannot correct structural models; it only provides validation reports, which rely on external software tools such as Whatif (Vriend, 1990) or MolProbity (Davis et al., 2004). In contrast, scientific journals have both the authority and responsibility to reject manuscripts reporting poorly refined or improperly validated structures, even when the main focus is on methodological advances such as new detector technologies (Casanas et al., 2016). The continued deposition of low-quality models pollutes the PDB, undermining its scientific integrity—a situation that should not be tolerated by the structural biology community. This is particularly true for deposits accompanied with the statement “To be published”, since it is even less clear why they should be present in the PDB in the first place.

Whereas HEWL has long been used as a standard test protein—an essential benchmark for developing new methodologies, instruments, or evaluating performance of newly commissioned beamlines—methodological advances should ultimately be validated on more complex and biologically relevant systems, rather than repeatedly on lysozyme. Notably, almost all of these HEWL structures have been improved, often substantially, by PDB-REDO, underscoring the persistent need for careful model validation. We need to stress that some of our concerns regarding the quality of HEWL structures may be particular to this protein due to its use as a test system and may not necessarily be applicable to other structures in the PDB that were determined in order to illuminate biological problems rather than to be used in methods development. Nevertheless, we have previously reported many very similar problems to be found in other protein structures in the PDB, for example in protein kinases (Wlodawer et al., 2025), β-lactamases (Raczynska et al., 2018), L-asparaginases (Wlodawer et al., 2024b), or the main protease of SARS-CoV-2 (Jaskolski et al., 2021), to name just a few.

Following a remark by one of our reviewers, we have looked for possible correlations between final structure quality (as gauged by Rfree) and various experimental factors that can be extracted from the headers of the PDB files. We found no significant correlations for the subset of PDB lysozyme crystal structures (not shown), except slight counterintuitive trends for crystallization method (hanging drop the worst case) or detector type (older - better).

We were surprised to discover that over 15% of lysozyme depositions in the PDB list the primary citation as “To be published.” This observation raises a fundamental question: do we really need more than 1000 lysozyme structures in the PDB if some of them are not even described in scientific literature? Finally, we believe it is time to initiate a community-wide discussion on how to distinguish PDB depositions arising from methodological tests from those representing genuine biomedical research, thereby better preserving the integrity and interpretability of the archive. One recommendation would be to encourage developers of methods and software using HEWL (or other proteins) as their testing grounds, to go beyond the primary goal of providing proof-of-principle, and refine their structures to completion before PDB deposition. Another suggestion would be to mark such test structures with an appropriate PDB flag record, to prevent their use in training sets for purposes such as AI protein structure prediction. We recently postulated that a wider use of the CAVEAT record for such a purpose might be a relatively simple solution to identifying suboptimal models (Wlodawer et al., 2026). HEWL, with its very large number of PDB deposits, many of comparatively high quality, yet at the same time with many outliers such as those described in this survey, provides an excellent testing ground for suggesting improvements to the procedures employed by the PDB.

Although this analysis has been mainly focused on detecting errors and pitfalls that can mar even the most careful crystallographic studies, we want to stress as a conclusion that lysozyme has also provided the most outstanding examples of quality and advancement in biological crystallography. Suffice it to reiterate that the triclinic HEWL structure determined at 0.65 Å [PDB ID 2vb1; (Wang et al., 2007)] was among the top resolution (and top quality) structures when it was determined almost 20 years ago and is regarded to this day as one of the best structures in the PDB. Similarly, the triclinic structure with PDB ID 6o2h determined by Meisburger et al. (2020) is of outstanding quality, even though the resolution is “only” atomic. We might also mention the structure 1lz8 determined by Dauter et al. using Single-wavelength Anomalous Scattering (SAD) with home-source-type (1.54 Å) X-rays. The diffraction data were of such high quality that they allowed the extraction of the extremely weak anomalous signal of sulfur and chloride (from NaCl used as precipitant) atoms, thus establishing as proof of principle the usefulness of the SAD approach based solely on the natural constituents of macromolecular crystals like sulfur and X-rays generated in the laboratory. Crystallographic studies of HEWL have indeed proven the correctness of many methodological and technical advances.
