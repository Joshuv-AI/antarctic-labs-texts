Correlates associated with health insurance on cervical cancer screening in Tanzania: a comparison between the insured and uninsured women using demographic and health survey 2022
Authors: Malale Tungu, Pankras Luoga, Amani Anaeli, Tumaini Nyamhanga
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCervical cancer is the most common cancer in women worldwide. This cancer affects more women in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) including Tanzania. Economic losses related to cervical cancer can lead to a serious threat to collective financial well-being and increased risk for the households to catastrophic health expenditure. In most LMICs, out-of-pocket (OOP) payments are often necessary for utilizing medical care and co-payments of health insurance to access some of the health services. This study aimed to assess the influence of health insurance among women on access to cervical cancer screening by comparing insured and non-insured women in Tanzania.MethodsThis study used secondary data collected through cross-sectional design during the Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey 2022. The study analyzed the information from a total sample of 15,254 women. In the analysis, all data were weighted using individual women sample weight to account for complex sampling design and non-response rate. The analysis of this study used univariate, bivariate and multivariable logistic regression analysis models.ResultsOur analysis revealed that only 7.01% of women were screened for cervical cancer. Health insurance coverage was statistically significant with the cervical cancer screening. This means that the insured women had higher odds than those uninsured women for both the crude model (COR 3.12; CI 2.46, 3.96) and adjusted models (AOR 1.75; CI 1.35, 2.27). Age, education level, currently engaging in income-generating jobs and those who visited the health facility at least once in 12 months influenced the cervical cancer screening for both crude and adjusted models.ConclusionThis study emphasizes the significance of various factors in cervical cancer screening in Tanzania. These factors include being covered by health insurance, socio-economic status and education. Health insurance coverage seems to be a predictor of accessing health services including screening for cervical cancer. Therefore, the government should take these factors, especially health insurance into account when formulating policies and making decisions to enhance access to cervical cancer screening for women in Tanzania including fasterning an ongoing process of establishing a prepayment scheme of mandatory health insurance for all citizens.Keywords: Cervical cancer, Cervical cancer screening, Health insurance, Out-of-pocket payments, Tanzania

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), cervical cancer is among the cancers and other Non-Communicable Diseases (NCDs) collectively responsible for 74% of all deaths worldwide1. In most low-resource settings there has been an emerging burden of cancer including cervical cancer which is probably a result of ongoing poverty [1]. Nearly three-quarters of deaths caused by NCDs occur in LMICs where cervical cancer is the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in women globally especially in LMICs due to either undiagnosed or diagnosed late [2]. This disease burden is estimated to increase up to US$7 trillion in economic losses. The World Health Organization (WHO) suggests that strategic investment in public health interventions to reduce the cervical cancer burden greatly would cost only about 2% of this estimated economic loss on a global scale [3]. Although interventions may be able to mitigate much of the risk of the combined costs of interventions, treatments, and premature deaths related to cervical cancer among women are increasingly burdening emerging economies as well as the women and households residing in LMICs [1, 4]. Economic losses related to cervical cancer can come in many forms and from many sources including limiting a woman’s ability to work productively, expensive and often long-term healthcare utilization. For women and households in low-resource settings, healthcare spending related to the treatment and management of cervical cancer poses a serious threat to collective financial well-being. A woman or family member having this cancer in some settings can be a potential source for higher-than-normal expenditures and ongoing expenses for health care, including regular use of medications, medical supplies and routine visits to manage chronic illness [5, 6].

Globally, cervical cancer is the most common cancer, affecting more women in LMICs. In 2020, cervical cancer was the leading cancer that caused death in sub-Saharan Africa [7]. In the African continent, the East Africa region has the highest incidence as well as mortality associated with cervical cancer [8, 9]. Cervical cancer is the leading cause of mortality among females [10, 11]. This cancer can be avertable and treatable if prioritized and managed at the early stage including having adequate screening tools and routine screening [12, 13]. Setting priorities on access to routine screening and appropriate treatment services at the health facility can ensure the early detection of cases before reaching severe stages of disease and may reduce the burden of this cancer2 [14].

Cervical cancer is the most common cancer in Tanzania, causing more deaths among women than any other form of illness in the country [15, 16]. In order to scale up screening in Tanzania, routine screening and early detection have been advocated among women [16, 17]. The main challenges that face this campaign are the low income among women to access the services, the low doctor-to-patient ratio (1:50,000) and the lack of resources for primary health services [15]. In recent years, the government of Tanzania has been insisting on addressing the cervical cancer problem through early screening to detect the disease. Due to several challenges available in the clinical pathway of cervical cancer patients in early detection, diagnosis, and treatment, about 80% of women present with advanced stages of the disease [18]. One of the existing challenges found by the investigators was that there was a low uptake of cervical cancer screening, early detection, diagnosis, and treatment [19]. This might be due to health insurance status among women which can be associated with poor socio-economic status, and education level among women.

The main payment mechanism for accessing healthcare services in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including Tanzania is out-of-pocket (OOP) payments [20, 21]. However, the OOP payment mechanism has contributed to an unsustainable financing system in many countries that increased health expenditure among household members which results into a barrier to poor families in seeking and accessing health care including cancer screening and the failure of countries to sustain sufficient funding for health care [22–24]. In order to improve protection and utilization of health care services, efforts across Sub-Saharan Africa have been increasing different health care financing systems due to the increased medical expenditures [25–29]. These include introducing and expanding health insurance coverage, waiver and exemption mechanisms for vulnerable groups and improving tax collection and allocation to health care [30–32].

The pre-payments and financial risk pooling within health financing can assure the utilization of health care services among disadvantaged groups including women [31]. Worldwide, health insurance can be mandatory or voluntary. Most of the LMICs have implemented community-based health insurance that is categorised as voluntary whereby the challenges are based on the limited numbers of enrolees [6]. Tanzania has two major public health insurance schemes. These are Community Health Fund (CHF) and the National Health Insurance Fund (NHIF) which were introduced in 1996 and 2001 respectively. CHF has been revised to be called the improved Community Health Fund (iCHF) but remains voluntary to become a member of the scheme whereby each household pays the premium to subscribe to a membership of the scheme. The NHIF, initially was introduced to cover health care services for government servants and then was expanded to the other members from the informal sector. However, the NHIF remains compulsory for public servants and voluntary for members of the informal sector. Most public and accredited private facilities accept health insurance members to access health care services [28, 31]. Among others, insured women with either CHF or NHIF membership are entitled to a range of services including cervical cancer screening for early detection and diagnosis of the disease [23, 30, 33].

Most studies focus on the causes and effects of these cancers [11, 34, 35], however, few studies have explained the influence of health insurance among women on cancer screening. Thus, there is a scanty of studies which compare the correlates associated with health insurance among insured and uninsured regarding the early detection of the disease through screening at health facilities.

The ongoing household and individuals payment for funding cervical cancer expenses may lead to an increased risk of households to succumb into catastrophic health expenditure. In most LMICs, OOP payments are often necessary for utilizing medical care and co-payments of health insurance to access some of the health services [6]. This study aimed to assess the influence of health insurance among women on access to cervical cancer screening by comparing insured and non-insured women in Tanzania using DHS data.

Methods

Study design, data sources and sampling technique

This study used secondary data collected through a cross-sectional study using a survey research study design during Demographic and Health Survey Data 2022. The information from the Household file on all members of the households was obtained and weighted to account for national representation and non-response rate. The study analyzed information from 15,254 women aged 15–49 years collected via the cross-sectional study employing quantitative approach methods during the 2022 Tanzanian Demographic Health Survey. The Survey was conducted by the Tanzania National Bureau of Statistics (NBS) funded by the United States Agency for International Development(USAID) with technical support of ICF. It used two-stage stratified complex sampling methods; the first stage involved stratification of the country into Urban and Rural areas, and the second stage involved selecting the primary sampling units (PSU) from the sampled strata whereby selection of the households to be involved in the survey is done. From the selected households; all eligible women aged 15–49 years who slept in the household the night before the survey day were included in the survey. This included both usual residents and visitors of the household. The interview covered various health issues including reproductive health like contraceptive use, nutrition status, and cervical cancer screening, few to mention. A detailed description of the methodology used in the survey is documented elsewhere [36, 37]. However, this study was conducted after getting approval from the DHS custodian after reviewing our submitted concept note which allowed us to have access to the data about cervical cancer screening among women and health insurance status. Furthermore, the data included with other correlates which were treated as confounders like education level, marital status, wealth index and currently engaging in income-generating jobs.

Eligibility criteria

Women aged 15–49 years old from the selected households were included. Exclusion criteria comprised women who did not consent, women who were not present on the day of data collection and women aged below 15 years old or above 49 years old. In total, 15,254 women were included in the dataset. However, our analysis was limited to include only 15,188 women who responded to the outcome variable.

Data analysis

All data used in the analysis were weighted using a standard generated individual women weight (using v005/1000,000) to account for complex sampling design and non-response rate among the respondents. Application of weight provides national representative estimates. The collected data were analyzed using univariate, bivariable and multivariable logistic regression analysis models. Inferential analysis using bivariable and multivariable analysis models using logistic regression analysis was conducted to determine the association between independent variables (socio-economic factors) and dependent variables (composed indicator generated from the screening of cervical cancer captured in the DHS). All analyses were performed using Stata version 16 and svy commands were applied in multivariable logistic regression analysis to account for complex survey design.

Dependent variables

The cervical cancer screening status among women, whereby women were asked if they were screened for cancer or not. Responses were coded “0” for no and “1” for yes.

Independent variables

The study used some of the variables of risk profiles used in other similar studies including age which were categorized into four groups (15–24, 25–34, 35–44, 45+), education level (no formal education, primary education, secondary education, higher) wealth index combined with five quantiles (poorest, poorer. Middle, richer, richest), marital status with two categories (married and not married), currently engaging in income-generating jobs (Yes/Not), currently covered by health insurance (Yes/No), distance to the health facility (not a big problem/a big problem).

Results

Demographic characteristics of the respondents

Among the total respondents, 15,254 included in the study, a total of 15,156 women were asked if they were screened for cervical cancer. Only 7.28% were screened for cervical cancer. The majority of women involved in this study were between 15 and 34 years old. More than half (53.25%) of the women had a primary education level. More than half of the total women involved in this study were married (60.64%) and currently engaging in income-generating jobs (59.47%). About 27% of the interviewed women had a big problem accessing health services due to long distances to the health facility, 53.8% of the women visited a health facility in 12 months and only 6% of the total women currently had health insurance (Table 1).

Regression analysis

Factors associated with cervical cancer screening

Two logistic regression analyses were performed to assess factors associated with cervical cancer screening which represents the most dangerous NCDs among women. The regression analysis was performed at two stages to represent the two models which are crude and adjusted models. Table 2, presents the second logistic regression analysis whereby models 1 and 2 represent the relationship between cervical cancer screening and health insurance coverage as well as other socio-demographic factors. Health insurance coverage was statistically significant with the cervical cancer screening. This means that insured women had higher odds than uninsured women for both crude (COR 3.12; CI 2.46, 3.96) and adjusted models (AOR 1.75; CI 1.35, 2.27). The wealth index had an influence on cervical cancer screening whereby the odds of screening increased with the increase of the wealth index of women for both crude and adjusted models. Women from rural areas had low odds compared with women from urban areas for both the crude model (COR 0.37; CI 0.31, 0.45) and adjusted model (AOR 0.66; CI 0.54, 0.82). Age, education level, currently engaging in income-generating job (COR 1.97; CI 1.65, 2.35) and those who visited the health facility at least 12 months (AOR 1.31; CI 1.06, 1.61) influenced the cervical cancer screening for both crude and adjusted models. However, marital status and distance from the health facility did not appear to have any influence on cervical cancer screening.

Discussion

This study analysed the influence of health insurance among women on access to cervical cancer screening by comparing women who are insured and non-insured women. The study revealed that insured women had a higher chance of being screened with cervical cancer than those who were not insured. This is due to the fact that those with health insurance have a higher chance to access health services compared to those who do not have insurance. In addition, being insured could be linked with improved utilization of health services including cervical cancer screening and many others. The results are supported by other studies which indicate that health insurance is an important enabler of access to healthcare services which can help to have equality in access to health services [34, 38–40].

This study found that the wealth index influenced access to cervical cancer screening. This means that women with higher quantiles (richer/richest) had a higher chance of accessing cancer screening compared with lower quantiles (poorer). This could be due to the fact that women with high socio-economic status have more chances to access health services since they have the ability to pay compared to women with low socio-economic status. The results corroborate with other studies which indicate that women who are higher income earners have the ability to pay for the premiums of health insurance schemes than those who are poor. Furthermore, women who have higher socio-economic status can access health services frequently compared to those with low socio-economic status due to the affordability of services and means to access health facilities [41–46].

Women from rural areas had a low chance of accessing health services compared with those women who are in urban. This means that women from urban areas had a high chance of being screened for cervical cancer. This might be due to most of the health facilities providing cervical cancer screening services being located in urban areas. Therefore the distance from the health facility can be one of the factors that can influence access to health services. If there is a problem reaching the health facility then can reduce the utilization of health services among women or they have to incur high costs to reach the health facility. In addition, the cost of travelling to the facility, especially in rural areas creates financial barriers and most women in urban areas are more educated thus predicting their prevention of diseases attitudes including cervical cancer screening. The results are supported by other studies that indicate inequalities in access to health services between rural and urban dwellers, because of the availability and difficulties of accessing cervical cancer screening services brought by distance among women from rural areas compared to women from urban areas [47–49].

The findings of this study show that the chance of being screened for cervical cancer increases as age increases. This means that women at a higher age had more chance to visit the health facility for screening. This could be due to the fact that women at a higher age have a higher risk of cervical cancer and normally visit health facilities with many services like pregnant clinics which give them more chances to be screened for many diseases including cervical cancer. In addition, at higher age we expect women to have a family with a certain level of socio-economic status and others obtain help from their husbands. The results concur with other studies which indicated that as age increases there is a high possibility of accessing health services due to their income status, marriage, high risk of developing many diseases including cervical cancer, awareness of many diseases which affect women and the need for many health services compared to the young women [49–51].

The education level of the women matters on the access to health services. This found a correlation between cervical cancer screening and the level of education that as the education level of women increases there is a higher chance of being screened for cancer compared to those with low education. This could be due to the fact that educated women are more likely to be aware of protection against different diseases including cervical cancer compared to uneducated women. The results align with previous studies which indicate the importance of education on access to health services since educated women are more aware of the diseases compared with those with low education [50, 52, 53]. Furthermore, there is a positive correlation between higher education and socio-economic status, whereby women with higher education are more likely to have higher socio-economic status, which may facilitate their access to more health services compared to women with lower education [48, 53].

The currently engaging in income-generating jobs of the interviewed women indicated that there is a higher chance of women who are currently working to be screened for cancer. This means that women with jobs have a higher socio-economic status compared to those without jobs, allowing them to frequently access health services. This could be due to employed women having good financial means and sometimes being covered by health insurance either by their own payments or paid by their employers as the case of many employees in public and private sectors in Tanzania. This concurs with other studies which indicated that as socio-economic status and employment increase the possibility of accessing health services including cervical cancer screening also increases [48].

Study strengths and limitations

The strength of this study is the use of the data from DHS which is a national representative of the entire population of women in assessing the influence of health insurance on cervical cancer screening by comparing insured and non-insured women and other socio-demographic factors in Tanzania. However, this study is limited by the cross-sectional design that does not allow for causal/temporal relationships between independent variables (the correlates including health insurance coverage) and cervical cancer screening among women in Tanzania.

Conclusion

This study highlights the importance of health insurance as a predictor of health care utilization among women in Tanzania and other LMICs. The combination of health insurance status and other demographic factors is important in cervical cancer screening, especially in LMICs. These factors include being an active member of a health insurance scheme, socio-economic status and education. The government to consider these factors during formulating policy and decision-making to improve access to cervical cancer screening among women in Tanzania.
