Assessment of patients’ attitudes and actions toward Patient Information Leaflets in Jordan: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Qusai Al-Anasweh, Afnan Rababa, Moutaz Amayrh, Osama Alrjoob, Laith Ashour, Ala’ Al-Doaikat, Nada Freihat, Hala Al-Miqdadi, Mohammad Al Khreisha, Malak Al-Rabe’e, Al-Mo’tasem Bellah Al-Rahamneh, Lama Al-Mehaisen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study evaluated patients’ attitudes and behaviors toward patient information leaflets (PILs) in Jordan. In detail, we highlighted patients’ willingness to use PILs and determined predictors of PIL reading in the Jordanian population.MethodsTo assess patients’ attitudes and actions toward PILs, we conducted a cross-sectional study targeting patients in the 3 Jordanian regions (north, middle, and south). Convenience and snowball sampling were used, and predictors for reading PILs were determined using multiple logistic regression. In addition, chi-square test and McNemar’s test were used for univariate and paired data analysis, respectively. Patients were invited to fill out a special online questionnaire developed by the authors via Google Forms. Consent was obtained from all participants, and the data were analyzed using descriptive and inferential statistics.ResultsThe total number of participants was 343; 235 (68.5%) were female. Our study found a high prevalence of PIL reading in our Jordanian sample (87.2%). Indications of the medication (80.6%), side effects (77.9%), and how to use it (63.5%) were the most frequently read sections of PILs. Marital status, female gender, and lower income predicted higher odds of reading PILs (P < 0.05 for all). Polypharmacy was associated with not reading PILs (P < 0.001). Among those who do not read PILs, the adequacy of the information provided by the physician was the most common reason (52.3%), followed by an unsuitable font size (29.5%).ConclusionOverall, PIL reading among patients in Jordan is satisfactory. However, in vulnerable populations with polypharmacy, the results are unsatisfactory.

Introduction

The demand for pharmaceutical products spans a wide range of medications used to treat and prevent various health conditions.1 Drug therapy plays a vital role in patient care, especially for individuals with illnesses that follow a predictable course.2 Patients typically use medications with the goals of preventing disease, alleviating symptoms, or achieving a cure. It is essential that they have a clear understanding of what to expect from their prescribed medications.3 Studies have shown that medication use has contributed to increased life expectancy, enhanced quality of life, and a reduction in disease-related complications and disabilities. For many common conditions—particularly chronic diseases—the advantages of drug treatment outweigh the potential risks, though this may lead to higher healthcare expenditures in aging populations.4 As a result, patients require reliable sources of information to use their medications correctly, understand potential side effects, and make informed decisions about their treatment.5 A common way to provide such information is through Patient Information Leaflets (PILs), which are available either in printed form or online.6

PILs, which are distributed with most pharmaceutical products, offer several important advantages. They typically contain critical information regarding the probability of adverse drug reactions, which is essential for informed decision-making by patients.7 Moreover, PILs contribute significantly to patient empowerment by enhancing their ability to manage their own healthcare and by supporting the safe and appropriate use of medications.8 Evidence also suggests that PILs can positively influence medication adherence.9 However, the effectiveness of PILs is contingent upon their clarity and accessibility. Misunderstanding or misinterpretation of the information presented can lead to adverse outcomes, including the occurrence of complications or side effects.10 Conversely, patients may refrain from using prescribed medications due to concerns or anxiety induced by the content of the leaflets. Thus, while PILs have the potential to be beneficial, poorly designed leaflets may contribute to negative outcomes.

A systematic review concluded that PILs generally improve patients’ knowledge and satisfaction.11 In the context of acute medical conditions, they are associated with improved short-term adherence. However, in chronic disease management, invasive procedures, or screening contexts, the impact of PILs on adherence is more variable and depends on factors such as the timing of distribution, the context in which they are provided, and the nature of the medical intervention. Furthermore, a survey conducted within the European Union indicated that approximately 50% of PILs failed to provide information regarding the rationale for treatment—specifically whether it was symptomatic, curative, or preventive in nature—highlighting a significant limitation in the quality of some PILs.12 Notably, some patients reported heightened anxiety and reluctance to take medications after reading PILs,9 indicating a need for careful consideration in their design and presentation.

In light of the aforementioned findings, it is crucial to assess the patients’ attitudes and actions toward PILs in Jordan, a developing country, keeping in mind the limited number of studies on patients’ attitudes and actions toward PILs in developing countries. In Jordan, as in many European and other countries, a package insert or information leaflet is included with the dispensed medication. In addition, the regulations governing medication dispensing are less strict than in other countries, allowing community-dwelling individuals to buy many medications (including certain antibiotics) without providing a valid prescription. Considering that there is no literature on attitudes toward or patients’ handling of PILs in Jordan, we hypothesized that patients would have good knowledge of and satisfactory attitudes toward PILs.

This study evaluated patients’ attitudes and behaviors toward pharmaceutical leaflets in Jordan. Specifically, we assessed patients’ willingness to use PILs, their views regarding the usefulness of PILs, the impact of reading PILs on patients’ adherence to treatment, the factors that predict PIL reading, and possible interventions that might improve adherence to PIL reading before medication use. This study’s recommendations will help pharmaceutical companies redesign PILs to suit patients’ needs.

Methods

A cross-sectional design targeting patients across the three main regions of Jordan (north, middle, and south) was used in this study. Convenience and snowball sampling were performed while ensuring randomness in the collection of responses. That is, none of the patients’ personal information (such as name, email, phone number, etc.) was obtained. The medical students in our faculty, via social media and WhatsApp, disseminated a special anonymized online survey to the patients.

The required sample size was calculated using a special sample size calculator (Statistics Kingdom, 2017; available at https://www.statskingdom.com/). Previous research from Iran reported that 84% of patients read drug leaflets (Ahmadi et al., 2018). Accordingly, the sample size was calculated using an expected response distribution of 84%, a 95% confidence level, and a 4% margin of error. A sample size of 343 was appropriate. Data were collected between August 2024 and March 2025. Inclusion criteria were living in Jordan, being able to participate voluntarily, and having a history of obtaining medications for themselves or others (i.e., buying or collecting medications for family members or relatives). Participants younger than 18 years who were able to obtain medications were also eligible to participate.

The questionnaire was constructed by the authors to assess the aforementioned study objectives. We did not adopt questionnaires from previous studies. Instead, the authors constructed the survey based on a theoretical framework. Its development was guided by a conceptual framework that identified the key constructs relevant to the research objectives. These constructs included: (1) patients’ reading behavior regarding Patient Information Leaflets (PILs), (2) factors that may influence whether patients read PILs, (3) patients’ attitudes and perceptions toward the current format and content of PILs, and (4) patients’ preferences for improving or enhancing PILs. Of note, these constructs were chosen based on the literature, which showed their central role in patients’ knowledge of medication-related information and the subsequent correct use of medications.8 By grounding the questionnaire in a theoretical framework, the authors ensured that each item was designed to measure a specific concept related to the study aims, thereby supporting the content validity of the instrument. In detail, the questionnaire assesses patients’ demographic characteristics, types of medications they mainly use (e.g., drugs and/or supplements), the provider they tend to visit to obtain drugs (e.g., hospital, pharmacy, primary health centers, or personal connections), and whether they read PILs. For those who read PILs, six additional questions were asked to determine attitudes and behaviors toward PILs. That is, to determine the reason for reading the PIL, the frequency of reading PILs, the types of drugs for which they tend to read PILs, the alternative sources patients use if a PIL is not available (e.g., reading about the drug via the Internet, social media, asking the pharmacist, asking the doctor), the problems they face when reading the PIL, the parts of the PIL they prefer or tend to read, and the effect of reading the PIL on patients’ adherence to medication (the last two questions were assessed using items presented under a single question). For those who do not read PILs, two questions were asked to assess the reasons for not being interested in reading the PIL, to assess whether they tend to obtain information about medication use from another source, and to assess their perspectives toward certain interventions that may enhance the comprehensibility of PILs (the last two questions were presented as items under a single question). The final version of the questionnaire was validated by the authors after ensuring that the objectives were adequately represented and that the questionnaire was understood by piloting it on at least ten participants who had previously been prescribed medications. Content validity was confirmed by the authors. Reliability analysis was not applicable because no questions were designed to create a specific measurement scale.

The Ethical Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Al-Balqa Applied University, approved this study (Approval No.: 4/2024). Consent to participate was obtained from each participant after the objectives of the study had been explained on the first page of the questionnaire.

For data analysis, Jamovi software (The jamovi project (2023). Version 2.4.8. https://www.jamovi.org), a user-friendly software package,13 was used. To analyze the association between demographic variables (age, income, education, gender, marital status) and reading the PIL, multiple logistic regression was used after ensuring that the assumptions were met and the fit indices were acceptable, and results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Pearson’s chi-square test was used to assess whether PIL reading was associated with the person for whom the medication was dispensed. We used the same analysis to determine whether the most common chronic diseases were significantly associated with PIL reading. McNemar’s test was used to assess whether the number of prescribed medications was associated with participants’ willingness to read PILs. That is, participants were asked whether they would read PILs when the number of prescribed medications was one, two, three, or four or more. Because each participant answered each of these questions, a repeated-measures analysis was performed using McNemar’s test. Because the questions were answered by the same participants and differed only in the number of medications, pairwise comparisons using McNemar’s test were considered appropriate. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was used as a cutoff for significance, and descriptive statistics such as frequencies and percentages were presented using tables and charts.

Among those who read leaflets, 87.6% reported that they either read them always or often. Also, 93% (n = 278) said that they would look for other sources of drug information if leaflets were unavailable, and 64.2% (n = 192) said that they used the Internet to look for information about the medication. Pearson’s chi-square test showed no significant association between participants’ reading of the patient information leaflet and whether the medication was dispensed for themselves (P = 0.84) or for their spouse or another relative (P = 0.18). However, a significant association was observed between reading the leaflet and medications dispensed for participants’ children, with a higher proportion of participants reading the leaflet when the medication was intended for their children (95.0%) compared with other groups (83.9%; P = 0.005). Notably, there was no significant association between the most common chronic diseases reported by participants (i.e., hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, chronic back pain, or thyroid disorders) and reading the PIL (P > 0.05). To determine which demographic characteristics were associated with a higher probability of reading leaflets, multiple logistic regression was used. Model fit indices were acceptable, with Nagelkerke’s R2 = 0.16 and an overall model χ2 = 30.5 (P = 0.001). The analysis revealed a significant association between sex and PIL reading, as males were less likely to read PILs compared to females (P = 0.012; OR = 0.41, 95% CI: [0.21–0.82]). Discussion

Key findings

This study provides new insights into the use of patient information leaflets (PILs) in the Jordanian context. We found that 87.2% of participants reported reading PILs. PILs help patients understand how to take their medications as directed and increase awareness of potential side effects. Patients who receive written medication information express greater satisfaction than those who do not.14 According to a survey conducted in another country with a similar design, most respondents (75.4%) reported always reading dispensing information leaflets, and 76.2% considered reading them essential.15

Females and married participants were more likely to read PILs. Females were more likely to read PILs than males in several previous studies.16,17 This may be explained by the lower propensity for risk-taking observed among females.9 Additionally, women tend to be more proactive than men in seeking medicine-related information from multiple sources.17

Governmental hospitals were among the most common locations for obtaining medication prescriptions, likely because medications are accessible and affordable in these settings. Moreover, patients can communicate directly with physicians, ensuring correct medication selection, appropriate dosing, and proper use, as well as the opportunity to inquire about adverse effects.18 In a study conducted in India assessing the availability of 50 essential medicines in public health facilities, 26 medications were available in more than 80% of facilities, while six were found in fewer than 30%.19 These findings align with our results and highlight the critical role of governmental pharmaceutical services in incorporating and improving PILs, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.

We also found that antipyretics, analgesics, and antibiotics were the most commonly prescribed medications. This may indicate inappropriate acute use of antibiotics. Analgesics were also the most frequently prescribed drugs in studies conducted in France and Sri Lanka.20,21 The high use of antibiotics underscores the need to focus on patient awareness regarding antibiotic indications, as misuse affects public health beyond the individual patient. In many instances, antibiotics are improperly used as over-the-counter treatments for upper respiratory tract infections, which are typically self-limiting. Misuse, overuse, and abuse of antibiotics are known risk factors for antimicrobial resistance, adverse effects, and increased healthcare costs. Improving the structure and scientific content of PILs to emphasize appropriate indications, definitions, and resistance risks may help reduce inappropriate antibiotic use. Reading PILs has been shown to positively influence the appropriate use of antibiotics and other medications without a proper indication.22,23

Factors influencing the use of PILs

Polypharmacy was associated with a significantly lower likelihood of reading PILs. Polypharmacy, defined as the concurrent use of five or more medications, is common among older adults.24 Older individuals are more susceptible to chronic conditions and therefore more likely to be prescribed multiple medications. Low health literacy in this population may further reduce their ability to comprehend PILs.25 Consequently, combined educational and behavioral strategies are required to improve medication adherence in older adults. These strategies include medication review, patient education, and regimen simplification. Regular medication reviews conducted by healthcare providers can improve health literacy and lead to better adherence and clinical outcomes.26

Enhancing attitudes toward PILs

Patient information leaflets are essential tools for conveying medication-related information. In this study, the most frequently read sections were “Uses of the drug” and “Side effects.” However, several factors discouraged reading PILs, including reliance on healthcare providers for information, poor font readability, and fear that reading about side effects might induce perceived symptoms. These findings are consistent with previous research conducted in Saudi Arabia.9 Involving healthcare providers in educating patients about the value of PILs, particularly in situations such as polypharmacy, may help integrate PIL use into primary care, especially in low- and middle-income countries where public healthcare facilities are commonly used.

One proposed solution to readability issues is the incorporation of images, which may be particularly beneficial for patients with low literacy.27 Another approach involves transitioning from printed leaflets to digital formats, such as QR codes on medication packaging, although patient acceptance of such technologies varies.28,29 Simplifying language by reducing medical jargon and providing concise explanations is also essential.30 Structuring information with clear headings, bullet points, and visual aids can further improve usability.

Limitations

The study has some limitations. Selection bias is possible because convenience and snowball sampling were conducted through social networks, potentially over-representing younger, better-educated, and more health-literate individuals. Individuals without Internet access or digital skills were less likely to be represented in this study. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and the self-reported measures are subject to recall bias. Another limitation was the inability to statistically assess validity or reliability because the items could not be coded within a common framework (i.e., they were categorical items without a common coding scheme).

Conclusion and recommendations

Our study found a high prevalence of PIL reading in Jordan. Female gender, lower income, and marital status predicted higher odds of reading PILs. Although many participants considered the information provided by physicians sufficient and therefore did not read PILs, small font size and poor readability were important reasons for not reading them. In addition, the high level of antibiotic dispensing highlights the urgent need to incorporate appropriate information on antimicrobial resistance into PILs. Improving font readability, increasing the use of visual elements, using modern methods such as QR codes, excluding irrelevant sections, and educating patients about the importance of reading PILs in special populations (e.g., patients with polypharmacy) could improve PIL format and usability.

Further research is needed to apply and clinically assess the feasibility of new, improved PIL formats. There is a need for interventional studies (e.g., testing redesigned PIL formats, the use of QR codes, and visual aids) to confirm the impact on adherence and clinical outcomes.
