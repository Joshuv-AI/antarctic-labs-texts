Association between sleep duration and thirst in a nationally representative cross-sectional survey
Authors: Inha Hwang, Dana Oh, Jee-Eun Yoon, Jung-A Park, Woo-Jin Lee, Jae Rim Kim, Daeyoung Kim, Kwang Ik Yang, Min Kyung Chu, Chang-Ho Yun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe aimed to examine the association between sleep duration and thirst level, indicator of altered hydration status. We performed a secondary analysis of the 2018 Korean Sleep-Headache Study, a nationally representative cross-sectional survey conducted in the Republic of Korea. Of 2,501 participants who completed the survey, 2,468 were included in the final analysis (age, 47.9 ± 16.4 years; 49.8% male). Participants were categorized into the high- or low-thirst group based on self-reported thirst frequency. Sleep duration was grouped as ≤ 6, > 6–7, > 7–8, and > 8 h. Logistic regression assessed the relationship between sleep duration and high thirst, adjusting for multiple lifestyle and health variables. Short sleep duration (≤ 6 h) was significantly associated with high thirst (adjusted OR 1.69; 95% CI, 1.00–2.86; p = 0.049), compared to the reference group (> 7–8 h). Age-stratified analyses revealed that adults ≥ 50 years showed a significant association between sleep duration of > 6–7 h and high thirst (OR 2.26; 95% CI, 1.23–4.12; p = 0.01). Sensitivity analyses indicated diabetes and habitual snoring as key contributors, and depressive mood consistently predicted high thirst. These findings suggest a complex interplay between sleep, thirst perception, and hydration, potentially mediated by metabolic and sleep-related disorders.Keywords: Organism hydration status, Water, Sleep deprivation, Insufficient sleep syndromeSubject terms: Sleep disorders, Public health, Nutrition

Introduction

Short sleep duration is associated with disruption in multiple homeostatic systems, which are linked to hydration. These systems include parameters such as kidney function1, cardiovascular function2,3, glucose homeostasis4–7, and satiety signals8,9. However, how sleep relates directly to hydration status has received less attention.

Optimal hydration is critical to health and quality of life10. Physiological responses and performance are reduced even when the degree of dehydration is as low as 2% of body weight11. Inadequate hydration has been associated with poor cognitive function and mood12,13, impaired exercise performance, and the development of several diseases14. Chronic dehydration not only increases the risk of urinary tract infection and kidney stones but also contributes to the development of hypertension and stroke, as it causes prolonged vasoconstriction15. Therefore, identification of modifiable risk factors that increase the risk of inadequate hydration status could enhance general health and reduce the risk of dehydration-related chronic diseases.

The specific role of sleep in regulating water balance remains largely unknown. Hormonal regulation of water homeostasis has been postulated. Vasopressin (an antidiuretic hormone) regulates hydration status by promoting water reabsorption from the kidneys, and its release increases during the late sleep period16. Habitual exposure to insufficient sleep may be associated to a decreased effect of vasopressin, possibly disrupting water homeostasis17. Insufficient sleep may also affect hydration status by increasing energy expenditure during the extended awake period18.

Thirst, a basic instinct to drink water, is an essential mechanism involved in hydration homeostasis and an important biomarker (or indicator) of hydration status19–21. An increase in blood osmolality as minimal as 1% is capable of eliciting the sensation of thirst22,23. Studies have shown that thirst sensation is linearly related to body water loss and effectively distinguishes mild hypohydration (–2.0% body mass) from a euhydrated state, and that thirst, along with body mass change, urine specific gravity, and 24-hour urine volume, represents a practical hydration assessment method, supporting its use as a measure of hydration status21,24. However, the mechanism underlying the relationship between sleep duration and thirst in adults from the general population has not been studied. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the relationship between self-reported habitual nocturnal sleep duration and self-reported thirst level in a nationally representative sample of adults using data from the 2018 Korean Sleep-Headache Study25. With an exploratory aim, we also analyzed the relationship between sleep duration and water intake, a hydration-related behavior. We hypothesized that a shorter sleep duration may be associated with an increased prevalence of thirst at the population level.

Materials and methods

Study population and survey methods

This study is a secondary analysis of data from the 2018 Korean Sleep-Headache Study (also known as the Korean Headache-Sleep Study, phase II), a nationally representative cross-sectional survey of community-dwelling adults aged 19 years and older in the Republic of Korea25. The original study aimed to assess the epidemiology of sleep and headache in the general population and to examine the association between headache and sleep problems. A comprehensive description of the study methodology has been published previously25. Key methodological features are summarized below.

The Korean Sleep-Headache Study was conducted in two phases by Gallup Korea: phase I in 2009 (estimated population, 48,580,293; n = 2,836) and phase II in 2018 (estimated population, 51,817,851; adult population aged ≥ 19 years, 42,394,045; n = 2,501), both employing similar sampling, recruitment, and interview procedures. The current analysis utilized data from phase II. Two-stage cluster random sampling proportional to the population distribution was implemented across the 15 administrative divisions of South Korea, excluding Jeju-do. In the first stage, the 15 administrative divisions served as primary sampling units and were assigned sample numbers proportional to their populations. In the second stage, representative basic administrative units, cities (si), counties (gun), and districts (gu), numbering 75, 82, and 69, respectively, were selected with probabilities proportional to cluster size. Participants were subsequently selected by stratified sampling based on age and sex. Trained field personnel from Gallup Korea conducted door-to-door, face-to-face interviews using a structured questionnaire developed by the original investigators. The questionnaire collected information on sociodemographic and anthropometric profiles, health-related behaviors (including water and beverage intake, frequency of physical exercise, alcohol consumption, and smoking status), headache characteristics, sleep timing and duration, sleep quality, snoring, insomnia, daytime sleepiness, mood and anxiety symptoms, and comorbid medical conditions.

The 2018 Korean Sleep-Headache Study enrolled 2,501 participants aged 19 to 92 years (mean age, 47.9 ± 16.4 years; 1,242 men and 1,259 women). For this secondary analysis, the eligibility criteria were as follows. Inclusion criteria were participation in the 2018 Korean Sleep-Headache Study. Exclusion criteria were missing data on any key variables of interest, including sleep duration, thirst frequency, beverage intake, or covariates. Based on these criteria, 33 participants were excluded, resulting in a final analytic sample of 2,468 participants (1,229 men and 1,239 women; mean age, 47.9 ± 16.4 years). The study design was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (approval number: B-1804-484-303). All participants provided written informed consent. The work has been carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Thirst level

Thirst was assessed using a single-item Likert-type question developed for this survey, intended to capture overall subjective thirst frequency in a large-scale population. Thirst level was defined according to the responses to the question, “How often do you feel thirsty?” without distinction between daytime and nighttime occurrences. Participants who answered either “frequently” or “always” were included in the high-thirst group, whereas those who responded as “never,” “infrequently,” or “sometimes” were included in the low-thirst group.

Sleep duration

Sleep duration was determined by responses to the question, “How many hours of sleep did you usually get per night in the past month? Please provide separate responses for weekdays and weekends.” To determine the average sleep duration, a weighted average of sleep durations during weekdays and weekends was calculated using the formula (5 × weekday sleep + 2 × weekend sleep)/7. We categorized sleep duration as ≤ 6, > 6 to ≤ 7, > 7 to ≤ 8, and > 8 h of sleep. According to the Joint Consensus Statement from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society, the recommended daily amount of sleep is 7–8 h, with many studies reporting that sleeping for < 7 h per day is associated with the presence or increased risk of disease compared to sleeping for an average of 7–8 h per day26. Based on this recommendation and evidence, we considered sleep a duration of > 7 to ≤ 8 h as the reference category.

Covariates

Information on sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle habits, and cardiometabolic and mental health was obtained from the study dataset. The amount of daily beverage intake was assessed based on responses to the following survey questions: (1) “How many glasses of any type of beverage (e.g., water, juice, soda, coffee, milk) do you consume per day?” and (2) “How many glasses of water do you consume per day?” Participants were instructed to specify the number of glasses for each question separately. Regarding alcohol consumption and smoking status, we categorized participants into two groups (current vs. never or former). Regular physical exercise was defined as engaging in exercise that induces sweating, with a minimum frequency of three times per week. Hypertension and diabetes status were determined based on participants’ self-reports. Hypertension or diabetes was regarded as present when the participants reported it as a present illness. Body mass index (BMI, kg/m2) was calculated from self-reported height (cm) and body weight (kg). Habitual snoring was defined as snoring at least three nights per week. Participants with a Patient Health Questionnaire-9 depression scale score equal to or above 10 were considered to have depressive mood27.

Statistical analyses

The primary aim of this study was to assess the association between sleep duration and thirst. For group comparisons between participants with high and low thirst, we used a two-tailed Student’s t-test for continuous variables and a chi-squared test for categorical variables. The independent association between the reported sleep duration (≤ 6, > 6 to ≤ 7, > 7 to ≤ 8 [reference group], and > 8 h) and thirst was analyzed using a logistic regression model. The dose-response relationship between sleep duration and thirst was examined using the Cochran–Armitage test for trend and logistic regression analysis. Adjusted covariates included age, sex, occupation, total beverage intake (glass/day), alcohol drinking, cigarette smoking, regular physical exercise, hypertension, diabetes, BMI, habitual snoring, and depressive mood. To examine potential age-related differences, we performed age-stratified analyses by dividing participants into younger (< 50 years) and older (≥ 50 years) groups, based on the median age of 48 years. Additionally, a sensitivity analysis was performed to assess whether the association between sleep duration and thirst was influenced by metabolic and sleep related conditions. We performed the logistic regression models after excluding individuals with diabetes, habitual snoring, and both conditions combined. Multiple linear regression models were used to test the association between the sleep duration category and the amount of beverage intake, including water (glass/day), controlling for confounders. Analysis for the Cochran–Armitage test for trend was performed using the Statistical Analysis System (SAS) statistical software package, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). All other analyses were performed using StataNow/BE18.5 for Windows (StataCorp LLC, College Station, Tx, USA). Results are considered statistically significant at p values < 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of the high- and low-thirst groups

The total size of the study population was 2,468 (males 49.8% and females 50.2%; mean age 47.9 ± 16.4 years). The average sleep duration was 7.3 ± 1.0 h. The high-thirst group consisted of 150 participants (6.1%) and had a significantly shorter sleep duration (7.0 ± 1.1 h) than that of the low-thirst group (7.4 ± 1.0 h; Table 1). The total beverage intake in the high-thirst group was 9.6 ± 6.1 glasses per day, which was higher than that in the low-thirst group (7.0 ± 3.6 glasses per day). The prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, habitual snoring, and depressive mood was higher in the high-thirst group than that in the low-thirst group (Table 1).

Association of thirst with sleep duration

The distribution of thirst levels differed according to sleep duration (Table 2). The prevalence of high thirst was the highest (10.7%) in the group with the shortest sleep duration (≤ 6 h) and the lowest (4.0%) in the group with the longest sleep duration (> 8 h; Fig. 2). Linear trend analysis showed the dose-dependent relationship between sleep duration and thirst level: the shorter the sleep duration, the higher the prevalence of high thirst (Cochran–Armitage test for trend, Z = 3.96, p < 0.001; Fig. 2).

In the primary analysis, compared with the reference sleep duration (> 7 to ≤ 8 h), short sleep duration (≤ 6 h) was associated with a high-thirst state (odds ratio [OR], 1.69; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.00–2.86; p = 0.049). In the groups with moderately short sleep duration (> 6 to ≤ 7 h) and long sleep duration (> 8 h), the association between sleep duration and thirst was not significant (OR, 1.44; 95% CI, 0.95–2.19; p = 0.09; OR, 0.68; 95% CI, 0.39–1.18; p = 0.17; respectively) (Table 3). Logistic regression analysis showed a significant dose-dependent relationship between sleep duration and thirst state after adjusting for any covariates included in the analysis (beta = − 0.31, SE = 0.095, Exp(B) = 0.73, 95% CI for Exp(B) = 0.61–0.88, p = 0.001).

Age-stratified analysis of the association between thirst and sleep duration

When we stratified the analysis by age (Table 4), we observed different patterns between younger (< 50 years, n = 1,340) and older adults (≥ 50 years, n = 1,128). In younger adults, short sleep duration (≤ 6 h) showed a trend toward higher odds of thirst (OR 1.83, 95% CI 0.84–3.98, p = 0.13), while in older adults, moderately short sleep duration (> 6 to ≤ 7 h) was significantly associated with high thirst (OR 2.28, 95% CI 1.23–4.23, p = 0.01). Long sleep duration (> 8 h) showed a trend toward lower odds of high thirst in both age, though these associations did not reach statistical significance. The impact of comorbid conditions differed by age group. Habitual snoring showed a stronger association with high thirst in younger adults (OR 3.21, 95% CI 1.83–5.63, p < 0.001) compared to older adults (OR 1.67, 95% CI 0.97–2.86, p = 0.06), while diabetes was more strongly associated with thirst in older adults (OR 2.47, 95% CI 1.40–4.37, p = 0.002) than in younger adults (OR 2.28, 95% CI 0.34–15.15, p = 0.39). Depressive mood was significantly associated with high thirst in both age groups (younger: OR 3.31, 95% CI 1.62–6.75, p = 0.001; older: OR 3.16, 95% CI 1.73–5.77, p < 0.001).

Sensitivity analyses excluding participants with diabetes and habitual snoring

To examine whether diabetes and habitual snoring influence the observed association between sleep duration and thirst, we conducted a sensitivity analysis (Table 5). After excluding participants with diabetes (n = 2,253), the association between short sleep duration (≤ 6 h) and high thirst was attenuated (OR 1.50, 95% CI 0.81–2.77, p = 0.201), while moderately short sleep duration (> 6 to ≤ 7 h) became significantly associated with high thirst (OR 1.60, 95% CI 1.02–2.49, p = 0.039). When excluding participants with habitual snoring (n = 1,968), the association between short sleep duration and high thirst was further weakened (OR 1.43, 95% CI 0.70–2.89, p = 0.322). After excluding both diabetes and habitual snoring (n = 1,821), the association between short sleep duration and high thirst was substantially reduced (OR 1.09, 95% CI 0.44–2.71, p = 0.857). Longer sleep duration (> 8 h) showed a trend toward a protective effect against high thirst (excluding diabetes: OR 0.52, 95% CI 0.27–1.01, p = 0.054; excluding both diabetes and snoring: OR 0.47, 95% CI 0.21–1.06, p = 0.069). Current smoking remained significantly associated with high thirst after excluding diabetes (OR 2.06, 95% CI 1.25–3.39, p = 0.005) but not after excluding habitual snoring, suggesting a potential interaction between smoking and snoring. Depressive mood consistently showed a strong association with high thirst across all models (OR range: 3.01–3.60, all p < 0.001).

Association between sleep duration and beverage intake

The amount of beverage intake was associated with thirst state (Table 3). The levels of beverage intake (including water) were significantly higher in the high-thirst group than in the low-thirst group (adjusted beta, 2.53; p < 0.001). However, beverage intake was not associated with sleep duration (adjusted beta 0.02, p = 0.85).

Discussion

The findings of this cross-sectional study indicate that short sleep duration is associated with a higher prevalence of thirst at the population level. Adults who slept ≤ 6 h per night had 70% higher odds of experiencing thirst than those who slept > 7 to ≤ 8 h per night. An inverse dose-response pattern was observed between sleep duration and thirst state. These findings contribute to the growing body of evidence suggesting relationships between sleep, hydration levels, and potential health consequences related to dehydration. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the relationship between sleep duration and self-reported thirst state in a nationally representative sample of the general population.

Our age-stratified analyses revealed different patterns between younger and older adults. In younger adults, habitual snoring showed a stronger association with high thirst, while in older adults, moderately short sleep duration (> 6 to ≤ 7 h) and diabetes were more strongly associated with high thirst. These findings align with research suggesting that thirst perception mechanisms change with age28,29. The different patterns in younger versus older adults may reflect age-related physiological changes in both sleep regulation and thirst mechanisms, as well as the increased prevalence of conditions that affect both sleep and thirst with advancing age.

The sensitivity analyses excluding participants with diabetes, habitual snoring, or both conditions provided important insights into the potential mechanisms underlying the sleep-thirst relationship. The substantial attenuation of the association after excluding these conditions suggests that diabetes and habitual snoring may be important mediators or confounders in the relationship between sleep duration and thirst. Diabetes is known to increase thirst sensation due to hyperglycemia-induced osmotic diuresis, while habitual snoring is often associated with mouth breathing and resulting dry mouth upon waking30. These conditions may both independently affect thirst perception and influence sleep quality and duration, potentially explaining part of the observed association in the full sample. The consistent strong association between depressive mood and high thirst across all models suggests an independent relationship between psychological factors and thirst perception. Previous research has linked depression with alterations in various physiological processes, including hydration status and thirst regulation12,31,32. The relationship between depression and thirst warrants further investigation in future studies.

Although research on the direct relationship between sleep and hydration status remains limited, prior studies have established an association between sleep duration and kidney function, which is an essential component of the body’s homeostatic system responsible for regulating hydration levels1,33,34. Notably, a recent cross-sectional study utilizing a US nationally representative survey and Chinese cohort investigated the relationship between sleep duration and hydration biomarkers, specifically urine specific gravity and urine osmolality26; this study reported that short sleep duration (6 h) was associated with higher odds of inadequate hydration than sleep duration of 8 h35. These findings align with our observations regarding the relationship between sleep duration and thirst, which serves as a subjective indicator of hydration status.

While our data suggest a correlation between sleep duration and thirst, we underscore that as a cross-sectional study, our analysis cannot establish causal directionality. Several plausible mechanisms might explain this relationship, with the possibility that either sleep duration affects thirst or thirst affects sleep duration, or that both factors interact in a bidirectional or cyclical manner. In light of previous research, several hypotheses can be proposed. First, we hypothesize that a shortened sleep duration may increase energy expenditure during the extended awake period, thereby increasing the body’s water requirements and ultimately resulting in increased thirst. Although studies examining the interaction between sleep deprivation and thirst are rare, several studies in human and animal models have examined the interaction between sleep deprivation and hunger36,37. There is converging evidence that hyperphagia associated with sleep disturbances is caused by increased energy expenditure due to sleep deprivation36,37. Whether increased energy expenditure leads to an increased water requirement and subsequently results in thirst remains to be determined. Another possible explanation connecting thirst and sleep is the circadian release of vasopressin, an antidiuretic hormone17. During the latter stages of sleep, the secretion of vasopressin increases, thereby aiding in the prevention of nocturnal polyuria and dehydration17. The absence of this late sleep period in those individuals with short sleep duration may interfere with vasopressin release, eventually disrupting hydration homeostasis17. An example in which the same analogue is applied is how short sleep duration affects appetite-regulating hormones and increases appetite. Ghrelin and leptin (both, appetite-regulating hormones) are affected by sleep38. Ghrelin stimulates appetite, while leptin decreases it. Sleep curtailment may cause the ghrelin levels to spike and the leptin levels to decrease, resulting in increased hunger38.

Some previous experimental research findings are incongruent with the outcomes of our study. A recent study reported that neither sleep deprivation nor sleepiness strongly affected thirst, hunger, or preference for foods39. The authors investigated motivation to engage in various behaviors, such as drinking, eating, and performing physical activities. In this study, thirst was defined using a specially developed motivation scale to categorize the answers to the question, “How much would you like to drink a glass of water right now?”39. The outcomes of this study failed to corroborate the notion that a single episode of sleep deprivation is associated with alterations in thirst level or food preference. The authors proposed that drinking and eating can be competitive behaviors with sleeping39. These findings indicate a gap between the feeling of thirst and the motivation to engage in drinking behavior. Although thirst can be one motivator for drinking, the motivation to engage in drinking behavior is influenced by various factors beyond thirst, such as psychological factors, personal preferences, and cultural practices. Moreover, this study was based on data from one night of sleep deprivation and it only evaluated a restricted sample of young adults (123 participants, 61 females and 62 males, mean age 25.3 ± 6.3 years). The outcomes of sleep deprivation may differ between one night of sleep deprivation and habitual short sleep duration. A different experimental investigation conducted recently assessed the influence of controlled dehydration compared to euhydration, as determined by urine specific gravity, on sleep parameters (including sleep duration) through full-night polysomnography40. No significant differences were observed in sleep parameters between the dehydrated and euhydrated states in a previous study40. The limitations of this study include its restricted investigation into the effects of dehydration on sleep duration, without examining the converse relationship of how sleep duration might impact hydration status. The results were again based on data collected during a single night, and sample size was also small.

Limitations

Several methodological limitations warrant consideration when interpreting our findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. While a short sleep duration may affect thirst state, thirst may also lead to reduced sleep duration as discussed above. Our results should be considered preliminary and require confirmation through longitudinal and experimental studies to establish clearer causal pathways. Second, all variables including thirst perception, sleep duration, and beverage intake, were assessed via self-report, which is inherently susceptible to recall bias, social desirability bias, and subjective interpretation. Such measurement error may attenuate or distort the observed associations. Future studies should incorporate objective measurements, such as actigraphy or polysomnography for sleep, and dietary records or 24-hour recalls validated against biomarkers for fluid intake, to minimize self-report bias and strengthen the validity of the findings. Third, related to the broader issue of self-reporting, we used self-reported thirst as a surrogate outcome for hydration status rather than objective laboratory markers (e.g., serum and urine osmolality, sodium concentration, blood urea nitrogen, hematocrit). Although thirst is the primary feedback-controlled mechanism for maintaining hydration homeostasis, water-drinking behavior is also influenced by various psychological, social, and environmental factors10,20. We nonetheless adopted the thirst scale because it provides a practical, inexpensive, and non-technical means of assessing hydration status in large-scale survey research19–21,24. Future studies should validate our findings using objective hydration biomarkers. Fourth, beyond the choice of thirst as a surrogate, the thirst measurement instrument itself had inherent limitations. Thirst was assessed using a single, non-validated item, which may not adequately capture its multidimensional nature. Moreover, the item asked about overall thirst frequency without distinguishing between daytime and nighttime thirst, precluding evaluation of potential circadian variation. Future research should employ validated, multi-item thirst rating questionnaires that differentiate diurnal patterns. Fifth, our study did not sufficiently control for several potential confounding factors. Environmental conditions, including ambient temperature and humidity, can affect both sleep quality and hydration status by altering fluid loss through perspiration and respiration41–43. Dietary habits, especially sodium intake, significantly regulate thirst and fluid balance, with higher salt consumption potentially affecting hydration homeostasis44. Additionally, this survey did not include questions regarding medication use, another important confounder. Various medications, including diuretics, antidepressants, antipsychotics, antihypertensives, and antispasmodics, can significantly alter thirst sensation and fluid regulation. Future studies should incorporate these variables to enhance the accuracy and generalizability of findings by minimizing residual confounding effects.

Despite these limitations, our findings have important implications for understanding the complex interrelationships between sleep, thirst perception, and hydration status. Future research should explore both directions of this relationship—how sleep affects hydration and how hydration status impacts sleep—ideally using objective measures of both sleep and hydration status over extended periods. Interventional studies examining whether sleep improvement strategies affect hydration biomarkers and conversely, whether hydration impacts sleep, would be particularly valuable in establishing causality and potential clinical applications.

Conclusion

We observed association between short sleep duration and increased odds of experiencing thirst at the population level, while sensitivity analysis revealed this association was largely explained by diabetes and habitual snoring. While longer sleep (> 8 h) may be protective, the significance of this effect weakens after adjusting for underlying conditions. Depression remained a strong predictor of thirst across all models, suggesting a key role for mental health in thirst perception. This study advances our understanding of the complex interplay between sleep duration and thirst, with implications for hydration management and associated health risks. Further longitudinal and experimental investigations are necessary to elucidate the causal mechanisms linking sleep duration to thirst and to explore broader clinical and public health implications related to hydration status.
