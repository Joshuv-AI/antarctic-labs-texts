Trajectory-Based Integration of Biomarker Dynamics and Clinical Features for Prognostic Prediction in Hepatocellular Carcinoma: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Cineng Xu, Yun Yang, Dongmei Gou, Xiafei Wei, Qichao Yu, Jianguo Huang, Qingxian Cai, Hongzhong Zhou, Yong Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeDynamic changes in circulating biomarkers may reflect tumor biology and host responses more accurately than static measurements. This study aimed to develop and internally validate a dynamic prognostic model for progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) by integrating longitudinal biomarker trajectories with clinical variables.Patients and MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 379 patients with HCC treated at a single center in China. Latent class mixed models were used to identify circulating biomarker trajectories, which were integrated with clinical variables to construct the Integrated Multi-Biomarker Trajectory (IMBT) model. The model was compared with BCLC stage, a baseline biomarker model, and an AFP trajectory model. Discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility were assessed using C-indices and time-dependent AUCs, calibration plots, calibration slopes and Brier scores, and decision curve analysis (DCA), respectively. Internal subgroup validation was performed in a treatment-defined subset of 327 patients from the same center and source cohort who received PD-(L)1 inhibitor plus molecular targeted therapy. A predefined AFP-negative subgroup analysis was performed using baseline/cycle-0 AFP <20 ng/mL, which was used only to define the subgroup.ResultsAFP, D-dimer, and absolute lymphocyte count (ALC) trajectories independently predicted PFS and OS. Sharp-falling AFP trajectories were associated with favorable outcomes, whereas persistently high or rising AFP and D-dimer trajectories and lower ALC trajectories indicated poorer prognosis. The IMBT model achieved C-indices of 0.741 for PFS and 0.728 for OS. For PFS, the C-index exceeded those of the BCLC, baseline biomarker, and AFP trajectory models by 0.108, 0.124, and 0.039, respectively. The corresponding C-indices were 0.730 and 0.724 in the internal PD-(L)1 plus MTT subgroup. Calibration slopes at 12 and 24 months were 1.176 and 1.509 for PFS and 0.898 and 1.014 for OS, respectively, while DCA demonstrated positive net benefit across clinically relevant thresholds. In AFP-negative patients, the PFS/OS C-indices were 0.647/0.634 in the full cohort and 0.692/0.683 in the PD-(L)1 plus MTT subgroup.ConclusionLongitudinal trajectories of AFP, D-dimer, and ALC provide independent and complementary prognostic information. By integrating dynamic biomarker patterns with clinical features, the IMBT model improved risk stratification compared with conventional staging, baseline biomarker, and AFP-only trajectory models. These findings support further prospective external validation of the model for dynamic prognostic assessment and risk-adapted monitoring in HCC.Keywords: hepatocellular carcinoma, alpha-fetoprotein, D-dimer, absolute lymphocyte count, progression-free survival, overall survival

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) ranks among the leading causes of cancer mortality worldwide, largely owing to late diagnosis and limited eligibility for curative therapies.1 Although treatment options for HCC have expanded to include locoregional, targeted, and immune-based approaches,2–5 resistance and recurrence remain common, and long-term survival remains poor.6,7 The marked heterogeneity of HCC—in tumor biology, liver function, immune microenvironment, and comorbidities—limits the effectiveness of uniform treatment strategies. Prognostic stratification is therefore essential to identify patients most likely to benefit from specific regimens, reduce treatment-related toxicity, and optimize healthcare resources. Traditional staging systems, particularly the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) framework, remain widely used. However, their static, stage-based approach does not adequately reflect the dynamic and heterogeneous course of HCC, constraining their value in precision oncology.8,9

To overcome these limitations, numerous prognostic models have been proposed, incorporating serum biomarkers, imaging, immune signatures, or genomic data. Nomograms that integrate tumor characteristics, alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) scores, and hematological, inflammatory indices and liquid biopsy-based markers—including circulating tumor DNA have improved predictive accuracy relative to conventional staging.10–13 Previous HCC prognostic models provide useful benchmarks. A TACE-HAIC nomogram reported C-indices of 0.717 and 0.724 in the training and validation cohorts, with 1- to 3-year AUCs of 0.776–0.821 and 0.789–0.825, respectively, supported by calibration, DCA, and cut-off-based risk stratification10. A HAIC-specific inflammation-score model showed 2- and 3-year OS AUCs of 0.749 and 0.739 in training and 0.760 and 0.681 in validation cohorts. cfDNA CNV-based models may offer higher discrimination in ICI-treated populations but require specialized sequencing assays. These benchmarks highlight the need for accessible, dynamic models suitable for heterogeneous treatment settings. Despite these advances, prevailing models remain constrained by several key limitations14 Many are derived from homogeneous patient populations receiving uniform treatment, limiting generalizability to real-world practice.15,16 Others depend on costly or technically demanding assays, reducing accessibility.12,13 Most critically, the therapeutic heterogeneity of HCC further complicates prognostic assessment. Locoregional, targeted, immune-based, and combination therapies may each influence circulating biomarkers through changes in tumor burden, liver injury, coagulation activity, and host immunity.17,18 Thus, longitudinal biomarker trajectories may better capture treatment-context-dependent disease dynamics than static baseline values and may improve generalizability across real-world and contemporary systemic therapy populations.

There is therefore an urgent need for dynamic, non-invasive, and clinically accessible biomarkers that enable real-time prognostic assessment in patients with HCC. Serial monitoring of circulating biomarkers has shown clear advantages over baseline values, particularly in dynamic AFP, which improve prognostic accuracy across treatment contexts.19–21 However, AFP alone has limited utility in AFP-negative patients, who constitute ~30% of advanced cases,22 and it reflects tumor biology without accounting for liver function or immune competence. Complementary parameters—including liver function markers (eg, PIVKA-II, ALBI score, prothrombin time, and D-dimer),23–26 as well as immune-related indices such as lymphocyte and neutrophil counts could provide additional prognostic information.27 Such complementary indicators may partially offset the limited prognostic utility of AFP in AFP-negative patients. Nevertheless, the dynamic prognostic relevance of these clinically important biomarkers remains underappreciated, and their integration into time-dependent predictive models has not been systematically investigated.28,29

To address these gaps, we developed a dynamic, blood-based prognostic model for patients with HCC that integrates longitudinal biomarker trajectories with key clinical features. The resulting Integrated Multi-Biomarker Trajectory (IMBT) model effectively stratifies patients by risk, predicts progression-free and overall survival, and demonstrates robust prognostic performance across heterogeneous treatment settings. By capturing the dynamic interplay between tumor biology, liver function, and host immunity, this model provides a practical framework for real-time, individualized risk assessment and may guide adaptive treatment decisions in complex HCC populations.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

This retrospective study selected 771 patients diagnosed with HCC between January 2018 and November 2024. Eligible patients had received molecular targeted therapy (MTT) or immunotherapy combined with or without transarterial interventional therapy. Patients with pre- treatment clinical record, radiological and hematological data and more than 2 cycles of post-treatment data were included. Finally, A total of 379 patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis. A detailed flowchart of patient selection is provided in Figure S1. Inclusion criteria were: (1) pathologically or radiologically confirmed HCC; (2) availability of pre- treatment clinical record, radiological and hematological data and more than 2 cycles of post-treatment data; (3) receipt of treatment during the study period. (4) Age ≥ 18 years. Exclusion criteria included: (1) baseline data missed; (2) Non-primary liver cancer; (3) insufficient longitudinal biomarker data or unavailable outcome follow-up and (4) Unevaluable lesions. This study was conducted in accordance with the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki and reported in line with the STROCSS criteria.30 It was retrospectively registered after data collection but before formal statistical analysis and manuscript submission (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT07204262; October 2, 2025).

Data Collection

Clinical data were retrospectively extracted from electronic medical records using a standardized protocol. Longitudinal hematological and biochemical parameters data were collected at diagnosis and subsequently every 21 days during follow-up visits. Radiological assessments with contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) were performed every 6–12 weeks. Collected variables included demographic information (age, sex), disease stage (BCLC stage, ALBI grade), comorbidities (eg, portal hypertension, hypersplenism, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and decompensated hepatitis B cirrhosis), and 54 hematological and biochemical parameters such as AFP, D-dimer, absolute lymphocyte count (ALC), prothrombin time (PT), and Aspartate Aminotransferase to Alanine Aminotransferase (AST/ALT) ratio. In addition, 16 treatment- or disease-related complications (eg, ascites, portal vein tumor thrombosis, hepatic encephalopathy, hypoproteinemia, infections, gastrointestinal bleeding, and immune-related toxicities) were systematically recorded (Table S1).

Treatment

Patients received various treatment regimens, including MTT (TKI, and anti--VEGF antibody) and immunotherapy (PD-(L)1 inhibitors), transarterial interventional therapies (TACE, and HAIC), or their combinations, based on physician discretion and patient status. Systemic therapies were administered in accordance to the relevant clinical protocols and continued until disease progression, unacceptable toxicity, or patient withdrawal.31–33 All agents were administered at recommended doses and schedules according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the detailed transarterial interventional therapy procedures are provided in Table S2.

Outcome Measures

The primary endpoint was PFS, defined as the time from treatment initiation to radiological disease progression or death from any cause. The secondary endpoint was OS, measured from treatment initiation to death or last follow-up. Tumor response was evaluated every 6–12 weeks with dynamic contrast-enhanced CT or MRI and assessed according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST, version 1.1)34 by two independent radiologists.

Trajectories Identification

Variables with >5% missing data were excluded, while remaining missing values (<5%) were imputed using the mice package.35 Serum AFP values were log-transformed prior to modeling due to skewed distribution. For AFP trajectory modeling, patients were stratified at baseline using the 400 ng/mL cut-off before LCMM fitting because this threshold is commonly used to define high-AFP HCC in prior prognostic studies and separates patients with markedly different AFP ranges, reducing the influence of extreme AFP values on trajectory estimation.21,36,37 To characterize distinct longitudinal trajectories of hematological parameters, several modeling approaches were explored, including Growth Mixture (GM) models (using the flexmix R package), M-beta (Mβ) models (gamlss), Mixed Linear (MLIN) models (nlme), Latent Class Growth Analysis (LCGA), and Latent Class Mixed Models (LCMM) (both using the lcmm package38). Then, models with 2–5 latent classes were fitted, using the single-class solution to provide starting values for higher-class models, and 100 random sets of starting values were tested to avoid convergence to local optima. Model selection was guided by the lowest Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), a minimum class size of ≥5% of the cohort, entropy, and clinical interpretability. Ultimately, the LCMM with the optimal number of classes was selected as the final model, and each patient was assigned to the class with the highest posterior probability (modal assignment). To further evaluate classification reliability, class-specific assigned posterior probabilities were summarized for each selected trajectory model.

Prognostic Model Construction

Following trajectory identification, univariate Cox proportional hazards regression was used to screen potential prognostic factors for OS and PFS with covariates selected based on clinical relevance and statistical significance (p < 0.05). Multivariate Cox regression was then performed to identify independent prognostic factors, and forest plots were generated to visualize hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals. For multivariate modeling, a full Cox model including AFP, ALC, D-dimer trajectories, maximum tumor diameter, BCLC stage, ascites, and age was fitted. Stepwise variable selection in both directions was conducted based on the Akaike information criterion (AIC) to derive the most parsimonious model. Variable contributions to the final model were assessed using likelihood ratio tests, with each variable’s chi-square statistic expressed as a percentage of the total to indicate relative importance. Individual risk scores were calculated from the final model and stratified into low, intermediate, and high-risk groups based on tertiles. Model performance was evaluated using the concordance index (C-index) with 500 bootstrap resamples, and predictive accuracy over time was assessed using time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves at 12, 24, and 36 months, with corresponding area under the curve (AUC) values computed using the timeROC package.39 The robustness of variable selection was assessed by repeating bidirectional AIC-based Cox selection in 500 bootstrap samples and calculating variable selection frequencies. Calibration at 12, 24, and 36 months was evaluated using calibration plots, intercepts, slopes, and Brier scores, while clinical utility was assessed using IPCW-based decision curve analysis across threshold probabilities of 0.05–0.80. In addition, time-dependent NRI and IDI for PFS at 12 and 24 months were calculated to compare the IMBT model with the BCLC-only and AFP trajectory-only models, with censoring handled by IPCW and 95% confidence intervals estimated from 500 bootstrap resamples. Survival differences across risk groups were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the Log rank test. All survival analyses were performed using the survival package (functions: coxph, survfit, Cindex).40

Several sensitivity analyses were then conducted to evaluate the robustness of the findings. To account for potential differences in treatment regimens, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using both unweighted and inverse probability of treatment-weighted (IPTW) Cox models. Stabilized IPTW weights were derived from a multinomial logistic regression including AFP, ALC, and D-dimer trajectories, maximum tumor diameter, BCLC stage, ascites, and age. In a separate sensitivity analysis addressing potential immortal time bias related to the requirement for serial post-treatment biomarker data, we performed a 42-day landmark analysis. Patients who experienced the corresponding endpoint or were censored at or before day 42 were excluded, and survival time was recalculated from the landmark. Model discrimination and risk-group separation were then reassessed in both the full cohort and the PD-(L)1 inhibitor plus MTT subgroup.

Finally, to improve the clinical interpretability of risk stratification, we additionally performed an outcome-oriented optimal cut-off sensitivity analysis for the continuous IMBT risk score. Two cut-off points were identified in the full cohort for PFS using a maximally selected log-rank approach, analogous in purpose to X-tile analysis. To reduce overfitting from very small subgroups, each risk group was required to include at least 15% of the full cohort. Patients were then classified as low-, intermediate-, or high-risk according to these cut-offs, and the same cut-offs were applied without re-estimation to the PD-(L)1 inhibitor plus MTT subgroup as a sensitivity analysis.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.1). Continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test for two-group comparisons or the Kruskal–Wallis test for comparisons across more than two groups, with false discovery rate (FDR) correction applied for multiple testing. Spearman’s rank correlation was used to assess associations between continuous variables.

Results

Patient Baseline Characteristics

We first characterized the clinical profile of the study cohort, which consisted of 379 patients with HCC who had a median follow-up of 13.3 months (95% CI: 12.3–14.4). In total, 2,485 longitudinal biomarker measurements were obtained (Figure S1), enabling the assessment of biomarker dynamics over time. At baseline, ascites was observed in 128 patients (33.8%), portal vein tumor thrombosis (PVTT) in 71 patients (18.7%), and extrahepatic metastasis in 67 patients (17.7%)—features typically associated with advanced disease. Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection was present in 77.9% of patients, representing the predominant underlying etiology. Based on the BCLC staging system, the majority of patients (52.8%) were classified as stage C, while 32.5% were stage A and 14.8% stage B, reflecting a cohort with substantial representation of HCC. Detailed baseline characteristics are provided in Table S3.

Identification of Longitudinal Trajectories

To capture the dynamic changes in biomarkers over time, we first excluded variables with >5% missing data, resulting in the retention of six key biomarkers for trajectory analysis: AFP, D-dimer, ALC, ALBI, PT, and AST/ALT ratio. We then systematically evaluated five modeling approaches, including GM models, Mβ models, MLIN models, LCGA, and LCMM. Model performance was compared using relative entropy, log-likelihood, and the BIC (Table S4). Notably, LCMM showed the best overall performance across these metrics and was therefore selected to construct the trajectory models. In LCMM implementation, models were fitted with 2–5 latent classes, using the single-class solution as starting values, and 100 random sets of initial values were tested to avoid local maxima. The optimal number of trajectories was determined based on the lowest BIC, highest relative entropy, and clinical interpretability, with each class required to include at least 5% of the cohort (Table S5). Application of LCMM revealed distinct longitudinal patterns for each biomarker (Figure 1). For AFP, patients were stratified at baseline into high (>400 ng/mL; n = 132) and low (<400 ng/mL; n = 247) groups. In the AFP-high group, two distinct trajectories were identified: sharp-falling (25.0%, n = 33) and high-stable (75.0%, n = 99). In the AFP-low group, three trajectories emerged: high-rising (7.7%, n = 19), middle-stable (38.9%, n = 96), and low-stable (53.4%, n = 132) (Figure 1A). These patterns may reflect differential tumor behaviors and treatment responses. For D-dimer, three classes were identified: low-stable (71.2%, n = 270), middle-stable (24.0%, n = 91), and high-rising (4.8%, n = 18) (Figure 1B), suggesting varying degrees of hypercoagulability and potential thrombotic risk. For ALC, three trajectories were observed: high-stable (6.6%, n = 25), middle-stable (50.9%, n = 193), and low-stable (42.5%, n = 161) (Figure 1C), which may indicate differences in immune status among patients. For PT, three trajectories were identified: long (16.4%, n = 62), middle (38.5%, n = 146), and short (45.1%, n = 171) (Figure 1D), reflecting varying degrees of liver synthetic function impairment. For ALBI, two groups were identified: high-rising (37.2%, n = 141) and low-falling (62.8%, n = 238) (Figure 1E), potentially indicating different patterns of hepatocellular injury. For AST/ALT ratio, two trajectories were observed: high (11.6%, n = 44) and low (88.4%, n = 335) (Figure 1F), suggesting divergent trends in liver function reserve over time. However, because the ALBI and PT trajectory models showed modest entropy values (0.56 and 0.61, respectively), these survival associations were interpreted as exploratory rather than definitive trajectory-based predictors. Collectively, these trajectory patterns capture the heterogeneity in biomarker dynamics across the HCC cohort, providing a foundation for subsequent prognostic modeling. Association Between Trajectories and Clinical Outcomes

We next evaluated whether the identified biomarker trajectories could stratify patients according to clinical outcomes, beginning with progression-free survival (PFS). PFS differed significantly across AFP trajectory subgroups (Figure 2A, log-rank p < 0.001). Patients in the Sharp-falling and Low-stable groups achieved the longest PFS (285 days, 95% CI: 218–353; and 343 days, 95% CI: 286–401, respectively), whereas the High-stable (203 days, 95% CI: 170–237) and High-rising groups (164 days, 95% CI: 106–221) had the poorest outcomes. Pairwise comparisons revealed that the High-stable group had significantly shorter PFS than Sharp-falling (p = 0.002), Low-stable (p = 0.0004), and Middle-stable group (p = 0.0047). Similarly, the High-rising group demonstrated significantly worse outcomes compared with Sharp-falling group (p = 0.0047), Low-stable (p = 0.0060), and Middle-stable group (p = 0.0074). No significant difference was observed between Sharp-falling and Low-stable group (p = 0.15) or between Low-stable and Middle-stable group (p = 0.74). Collectively, these findings underscore marked PFS heterogeneity driven by AFP dynamics, with persistent elevation or progressive increase identifying the highest-risk patients, while Sharp-falling and Low-stable trajectories are associated with the most favorable outcomes. This suggests that AFP trajectory patterns provide clinically meaningful risk stratification beyond single timepoint measurements.

Kaplan–Meier curves of progression-free survival (PFS) in HCC patients, grouped according to trajectories of (A) AFP, (B) D-dimer, (C) ALC, (D) PT, (E) ALBI, and (F) AST/ALT ratio.

Similarly, D-dimer trajectories provided equally distinct risk stratification (Figure 2B, log-rank p < 0.001). Patients in the High-rising group had a markedly shorter PFS (177 days, 95% CI: 75–279) compared with both Low-stable (280 days, 95% CI: 250–310; p = 0.000017) and Middle-stable groups (269 days, 95% CI: 208–329; p = 0.0035). These findings highlight rising D-dimer as a reliable marker of unfavorable prognosis, potentially reflecting progressive hypercoagulability and disease aggressiveness. ALC trajectories were also significantly associated with outcomes (Figure 2C, log-rank p = 0.0072). The High-stable group achieved the longest PFS (324 days, 95% CI: 229–419), followed by the Middle-stable (290 days, 95% CI: 252–327) and Low-stable groups (244 days, 95% CI: 204–284). Pairwise comparisons revealed significantly inferior PFS in the Low-stable compared with the High-stable group (p = 0.013), with a borderline difference between Low- and Middle-stable trajectories (p = 0.051). These results suggest that preserved lymphocyte counts are associated with improved prognosis, supporting the role of immune competence in HCC outcomes.

Consistent with these findings, additional biomarkers supported these findings. PT trajectories stratified patients into groups with significantly different PFS (Figure 2D, log-rank p < 0.05), with the Short group showing the most favorable outcomes. ALBI grading further differentiated survival (Figure 2E, log-rank p = 0.003), with lower ALBI scores associated with longer PFS. Similarly, lower AST/ALT ratios predicted superior outcomes (Figure 2F, log-rank p = 0.007). Importantly, all trajectory-defined associations for PFS were paralleled by similar differences in OS (Figure S2A–F), strengthening the clinical relevance of our trajectory-based approach. The absence of ascites, PVTT, or extrahepatic metastases was likewise associated with longer PFS and OS (Figure S3A–C). Together, these results demonstrate that longitudinal biomarker trajectories provide robust stratification of HCC patients by clinical outcomes, with distinct patterns associated with significantly different survival probabilities.

Cox Regression Analysis and Prognosis Model Construction

Having established the prognostic value of individual biomarker trajectories, we next sought to integrate these dynamic patterns with established clinical features to construct a comprehensive prognostic model. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors of PFS and OS for model development. In univariate analysis, AFP, ALC, D-dimer, ALBI, PT, and AST/ALT ratio trajectories, together with ascites, age, and maximum tumor diameter, were significantly associated with both PFS and OS (Figure 3A; Figure S4A). Subsequent multivariate analysis confirmed AFP, ALC, and D-dimer trajectories, ascites, age, tumor diameter, and BCLC stage as independent prognostic factors for PFS and OS (Figure 3B; Figure S4B), highlighting the complementary value of combining dynamic biomarker patterns with static clinical parameters.

In multivariate analyses for PFS, compared with the Sharp-falling AFP trajectory, both the High-stable (HR: 5.02, 95% CI: 1.69–14.90; p = 0.004) and High-rising groups (HR: 5.51, 95% CI: 1.59–19.11; p = 0.007) were associated with substantially increased risks of progression, whereas the Middle-stable and Low-stable groups did not differ significantly. These results reinforce that persistently high or elevated AFP levels confer the poorest prognosis, independent of other factors. For ALC, both the Middle-stable (HR: 4.67, 95% CI: 1.42–15.32; p = 0.011) and Low-stable groups (HR: 4.85, 95% CI: 1.45–16.21; p = 0.010) had higher risks relative to the High-stable group, underscoring the protective effect of sustained high ALC. For D-dimer, the High-rising group exhibited an increased hazard (HR: 2.91, 95% CI: 1.47–5.75; p = 0.002) compared with the Low-stable group, while the Middle-stable group showed no significant difference, suggesting that progressively rising D-dimer levels specifically identify high-risk patients. Among clinical variables, advanced BCLC stage (A vs B: HR: 2.95, 95% CI: 1.61–5.41; p < 0.001; A vs C: HR 1.72, 95% CI: 1.07–2.76; p = 0.025), presence of ascites (HR: 1.88, 95% CI: 1.23–2.87; p = 0.004), and larger maximum tumor diameter (HR: 1.01, 95% CI: 1.00–1.01; p < 0.001) was associated with worse outcomes. Although statistically significant, the effect of tumor diameter was modest per unit and should be interpreted over clinically meaningful increments. No significant associations were observed for PT, ALBI, AST/ALT, PVTT, HBsAg, or extrahepatic metastasis in the multivariate model, indicating that their prognostic information is captured by the retained variables. Collectively, these findings validate that biomarker trajectories provide independent prognostic information beyond conventional clinical parameters, setting the stage for integrated model development.

Building upon the identified independent prognostic factors, we developed a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model, the Integrated Multi-Biomarker Trajectory (IMBT) Model, which incorporates dynamic trajectories of AFP, ALC, and D-dimer, alongside key clinical factors including ascites, age, tumor diameter, and BCLC stage. To assess the stability of variable selection, bootstrap variable-stability analysis supported the robustness of the retained IMBT predictors. Within the final candidate set, the selection frequencies across 500 bootstrap resamples were 100.0% for AFP trajectory, 99.8% for maximum tumor diameter, 98.2% for BCLC stage, 96.8% for ALC trajectory, 96.0% for ascites, 88.2% for D-dimer trajectory, and 84.6% for age.

To evaluate robustness across different treatment regimens, pairwise comparisons were performed. Both unweighted and weighted hazard ratios showed no significant differences in model performance across treatments (Table S6A and B). The weighted analysis, using stabilized IPTW to adjust for baseline differences between treatment groups, further confirmed the model’s applicability regardless of treatment modality, although clinical interpretation should still consider potential treatment-specific prognostic effects.

Variable importance analysis demonstrated that AFP dynamics were the predominant determinant of PFS (27.7%), with meaningful contributions from ALC (11.9%), D-dimer (9.5%), and ascites (10.1%) (Figure 4A). This analysis confirms the central role of AFP dynamics while highlighting the complementary value of incorporating additional biomarkers and clinical features. Risk scores derived from the model effectively stratified patients into tertiles with clearly separated survival curves (log-rank p < 0.001). Mean PFS was 321 days (95% CI: 266–376) in the low-risk group, 308 days (95% CI: 261–354) in the intermediate group, and 188 days (95% CI: 159–218) in the high-risk group (Figure 4B). Notably, the high-risk group showed a substantially shorter PFS, with a 41% reduction compared to the low-risk group, underscoring the model’s ability to identify patients at highest risk of disease progression. Model discrimination was robust, with a bootstrap-corrected C-index (500 times) of 0.741 (95% CI: 0.738–0.743) and time-dependent AUCs of 0.804, 0.864, and 0.763 at 12, 24, and 36 months, respectively (Figure 4C).

Prognostic contributions of risk variables and performance of the Cox proportional hazards model in HCC patients. (A) Relative contributions of individual risk variables to PFS in the Cox model. (B) Kaplan–Meier curves for PFS stratified by risk scores derived from the Cox model. (C) Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for PFS at 12, 24, and 36 months.

These results indicate favorable discrimination within the study cohort, with the highest time-dependent AUC observed at 24 months; however, external validation is required before clinical utility can be established. Importantly, consistent findings were obtained for OS (Figure S5A and B; Table S11). Stratification by model-derived risk scores yielded well-separated survival curves (log-rank p < 0.001), with low-risk patients achieving the best outcomes. The model achieved a C-index of 0.728 (95% CI: 0.726–0.730) and time-dependent AUCs of 0.739, 0.777, and 0.841 at 12, 24, and 36 months, respectively (Figure S5C). Beyond discrimination, calibration and decision curve analyses were performed to evaluate agreement between predicted and observed outcomes and the potential clinical utility of the IMBT model for both endpoints. Calibration analyses showed generally reasonable agreement at 12 and 24 months, with slopes of 1.176 and 1.509 for PFS and 0.898 and 1.014 for OS, respectively (Figure S7A and B). DCA demonstrated a positive net benefit of the IMBT model across the evaluated threshold probabilities compared with the treat-all and treat-none strategies (Figure S8A and B). The broadly consistent performance for PFS and OS suggests that the integrated approach may provide prognostic information for both endpoints within the study cohort. Collectively, these results establish the IMBT model as a robust tool for stratifying HCC patients based on their individual risk profiles, with potential applications in personalized treatment decision-making.

Model Comparison

To rigorously evaluate the added value of our integrated approach, we constructed three alternative prognostic models for comparison: (1) a baseline model incorporating static AFP, D-dimer, ALC, and ascites adjusted for BCLC stage, tumor diameter, and age (n = 379); (2) the BCLC staging model (stage only; n = 379); and (3) the AFP-only trajectory model (dynamic AFP trajectories adjusted for BCLC stage, tumor diameter, and age; n = 379). These models were used as comparators to assess the relative performance of the IMBT model. Direct comparison demonstrated that the IMBT model provided the highest prognostic accuracy for PFS, with a C-index of 0.741 (95% CI: 0.738–0.743), significantly exceeding the BCLC model (0.633, 95% CI: 0.630–0.637; p < 0.0001), the baseline model (0.617, 95% CI: 0.615–0.620; p < 0.0001), and the AFP trajectory model (0.702, 95% CI: 0.700–0.705; p < 0.0001) (Figure 5A). This represents a 17.1% improvement over the BCLC staging system and a 5.6% enhancement compared to the AFP-only trajectory model, highlighting the substantial gain achieved through multi-biomarker integration. A similar pattern was observed for OS, with the IMBT model showing higher discrimination than the comparator models within the study cohort (Figure 5B).

Beyond the improvement in discrimination, time-dependent reclassification analyses further demonstrated the incremental prognostic value of the IMBT model. Compared with the BCLC-only model, the NRI was 0.738 and 0.956 and the IDI was 0.220 and 0.242 at 12 and 24 months, respectively. Compared with the AFP trajectory model, the corresponding NRI values were 0.692 and 0.941 and the IDI values were 0.160 and 0.185, respectively. All bootstrap 95% confidence intervals excluded zero (Table S8).

Although the AFP trajectory model outperformed both the BCLC and baseline models, further inclusion of D-dimer and ALC trajectories in the IMBT model yielded additional gains in predictive accuracy. This incremental improvement demonstrates that each biomarker trajectory contributes unique prognostic information, with D-dimer reflecting coagulation status and ALC representing immune competence, thereby capturing complementary aspects of HCC pathophysiology. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that integrating multiple longitudinal biomarkers, rather than relying on AFP alone, provides substantial incremental value and markedly improves risk stratification beyond static baseline measurements or single-marker trajectories. These findings suggest that the IMBT model may provide additional prognostic information in HCC.

Subgroup Analyses of IMBT Model Performance

Exploratory subgroup analyses showed broadly consistent associations between the IMBT risk score and clinical outcomes across AFP status, BCLC stage, PVTT status, ALBI grade, HBV infection status, and major treatment-component subgroups, with no statistically significant interactions detected (Table S9). In the predefined AFP-negative subgroup (baseline AFP <20 ng/mL), 158 of 379 patients in the full cohort and 125 of 319 patients with evaluable baseline AFP in the internal PD-(L)1 inhibitor plus MTT subgroup met the AFP-negative criterion. Among AFP-negative patients in the full cohort, the IMBT model retained prognostic discrimination for both PFS (C-index, 0.647; 95% CI, 0.561–0.733) and OS (C-index, 0.634; 95% CI, 0.540–0.728). Time-dependent AUCs at 12, 24, and 36 months were 0.713, 0.768, and 0.644 for PFS and 0.613, 0.689, and 0.810 for OS, respectively. Model-derived risk groups also showed significant survival separation (PFS log-rank P = 0.002; OS log-rank P = 0.005). Similar findings were observed within the internal PD-(L)1 inhibitor plus MTT subgroup, with C-indices of 0.692 (95% CI, 0.602–0.782) for PFS and 0.683 (95% CI, 0.598–0.768) for OS; risk stratification remained significant for both PFS (log-rank P = 0.001) and OS (log-rank P < 0.001). These findings suggest that the IMBT model provides prognostic information among patients with low baseline AFP, although the 36-month estimates should be interpreted cautiously because of the small number at risk. Across BCLC stages A, B, and C, the C-indices for PFS were 0.715, 0.692, and 0.712, respectively. No significant interaction was observed between the IMBT risk score and BCLC stage (P for interaction = 0.848), indicating no evidence of heterogeneity by BCLC stage within the study cohort.

Validation in the PD-1 + MTT Subgroup

To assess the clinical applicability of our IMBT model in contemporary therapeutic contexts, we performed an internal subgroup validation in 327 patients who received PD-1 inhibitor plus MTT. Notably, this subgroup represents a clinically relevant population receiving modern immunotherapy combinations. The IMBT model demonstrated strong prognostic performance, with C-indices of 0.730 (95% CI, 0.728–0.732) for PFS and 0.724 (95% CI, 0.721–0.726) for OS (Figure 5A and B). These values closely matched those observed in the derivation cohort, indicating consistent discrimination within this treatment-defined subgroup. Kaplan–Meier analysis showed clear risk stratification: low-risk patients had the most favorable outcomes, while high-risk patients experienced rapid progression and shorter survival (log-rank p < 0.0001) (Figure 6A and S6A). This consistent risk stratification across treatment modalities highlights the model’s robustness. Time-dependent ROC analyses confirmed robust discrimination at 12, 24, and 36 months for both PFS (AUCs: 0.805, 0.846, 0.753) (Figure 6B) and OS (AUCs: 0.744, 0.780, 0.849) (Figure S6B). The particularly strong performance at 24 months (AUC: 0.846 for PFS) suggests the model’s utility for medium-term prognosis prediction in immunotherapy-treated patients. PFS and OS showed similar calibration and positive net benefit (Figures S7C–D and S8C–D). Collectively, these findings support the internal consistency of the model within this treatment-defined subgroup but do not establish external generalizability, supporting its potential clinical implementation for risk stratification in HCC patients receiving modern systemic therapies.

42-Day Landmark Sensitivity Analysis

To address potential immortal time bias arising from the requirement for serial post-treatment biomarker measurements, we performed a 42-day landmark sensitivity analysis. After excluding patients who experienced the relevant endpoint or were censored at or before day 42, the IMBT model retained prognostic discrimination. For PFS, risk-group separation remained significant in both the full cohort and the internal PD-(L)1 inhibitor plus MTT subgroup (both log-rank p < 0.0001), with C-indices of 0.717 and 0.742, respectively. Consistent results were observed for OS. Detailed results are provided in Table S10.

Optimal Cut-off–Based IMBT Risk Stratification

To further improve the clinical interpretability of risk stratification, we then performed an optimal cut-off sensitivity analysis for the continuous IMBT risk score. This analysis identified two cut-offs, 24.505 and 65.111, defining low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups. In the full cohort, the three optimal cut-off groups showed clear PFS separation (log-rank p < 0.0001), with median PFS values of 1148, 366, and 216 days for the low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups, respectively. Compared with the low-risk group, the intermediate- and high-risk groups had significantly increased risks of progression (HR, 3.15; 95% CI, 2.05–4.83; and HR, 8.09; 95% CI, 4.99–13.11, respectively). Importantly, the high-risk group also had a higher progression risk than the intermediate-risk group (HR, 2.57; 95% CI, 1.66–3.98), supporting the clinical relevance of distinguishing these two strata. When the same cut-offs were applied to the PD-1/MTT subgroup, risk separation remained significant (log-rank p < 0.0001), with median PFS values of 1059, 324, and 222 days for the low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups, respectively. In this subgroup, high-risk patients also had higher progression risk than intermediate-risk patients (HR, 1.73; 95% CI, 1.08–2.77). These findings support the robustness and clinical interpretability of the IMBT risk strata beyond tertile-based grouping (Figure S9).

Discussion

In this study, we established a trajectory-based prognostic model for patients with HCC that integrates dynamic changes in AFP, D-dimer, and ALC with clinical features. Our findings demonstrate that dynamic biomarker trajectories capture prognostic information beyond static baseline values, providing a more comprehensive reflection of tumor biology, liver function, and host immune competence. The IMBT model consistently outperformed conventional staging, baseline biomarker models, and AFP trajectory alone, highlighting the added value of dynamic monitoring for individualized risk stratification.

Our results extend a growing body of evidence linking dynamic AFP with survival across different treatment modalities and disease stages. Prior studies have shown that AFP decline after resection, TACE, or systemic therapy is associated with improved outcomes.41–45 More recently, trajectory-based approaches have characterized AFP dynamics in patients receiving diverse regimens, including bevacizumab plus immunotherapy and TACE-HAIC-MTT/ICI combinations, demonstrating that AFP trajectories serve as independent prognostic markers that may guide treatment decisions.21,36 Consistent with these observations, we confirmed that an early sharp decline in AFP was associated with favorable outcomes, whereas persistently high or rising AFP trajectories predicted poor prognosis and early treatment resistance. This underscores the prognostic weight of the initial response and the importance of early tumor burden reduction. In contrast, persistently high or rising AFP trajectories identified patients unlikely to benefit from ongoing therapy, suggesting that continuation of ineffective treatment may squander the therapeutic window and increase financial burden. These results support the use of AFP trajectory monitoring as a pragmatic tool to support longitudinal prognostic assessment during treatment.

Although AFP remains the most widely used biomarker in HCC, its sensitivity is limited, particularly in AFP-negative HCC.46,47 Plasma D-dimer reflects fibrin turnover and may rise in HCC through tumor-associated hypercoagulability and vascular invasion, including portal vein tumor thrombosis. It may also increase with bland portal vein thrombosis or hepatic decompensation; therefore, a persistently elevated trajectory should be interpreted as a composite signal of tumor progression, thrombosis, and impaired hepatic reserve rather than evidence of a single causal pathway.48,49 Several studies have demonstrated that elevated D-dimer levels are associated with liver dysfunction and poor survival, and importantly, remain independent predictors of adverse outcomes in AFP-negative HCC, thereby complementing AFP in prognostic assessment.47,50–53 In line with these findings, our study demonstrates that D-dimer trajectories constitute independent prognostic predictors in HCC, with persistently elevated levels associated with shorter PFS and OS. ALC trajectories also proved to be important. A high-stable ALC trajectory may reflect preservation of circulating effector T- and NK-cell reserves, whereas lymphopenia may accompany systemic inflammation and the immunosuppressive HCC microenvironment. Such immune reserve may support responses to immune-checkpoint blockade; however, total ALC cannot define lymphocyte subsets or establish treatment-specific predictive value.54–57 Our results confirm that patients with high-stable ALC trajectories experienced significantly better outcomes, supporting the use of ALC as a simple and accessible surrogate for immune competence in prognostic stratification. These results reinforce the clinical utility of D-dimer and ALC beyond static measurements and suggest that they may complement AFP, particularly in AFP-negative subgroups.

Persistently elevated AST/ALT ratio reflects hepatocellular injury, prolonged PT indicates impaired hepatic synthesis, and higher ALBI denotes reduced hepatic reserve.58–60 Their loss of significance after multivariable adjustment likely reflects overlapping information with clinical stage and other biomarker trajectories, rather than absence of biological relevance. The final model is thus parsimonious and clinically feasible, relying only on routinely available laboratory data.

From a clinical perspective, incorporation of D-dimer and ALC trajectories into AFP-based monitoring provides a multidimensional framework that captures both tumor-driven and host-driven processes. We translated this multidimensional information into clinically interpretable low-, intermediate-, and high-risk strata. Unlike prior models limited to AFP or static hematologic indices, our trajectory-based approach may support precision oncology by guiding risk-adapted management: low-risk patients may be suitable for routine clinical and biomarker monitoring, intermediate-risk patients may benefit from more frequent biomarker reassessment and individualized follow-up, and high-risk patients may warrant closer imaging surveillance, early multidisciplinary review, and consideration of treatment optimization or clinical trial enrollment. By distinguishing different levels of monitoring and management intensity, this approach may facilitate timely intervention for high-risk patients while avoiding unnecessary treatment intensification in low-risk patients, consistent with the evolving paradigm of adaptive cancer care. Nevertheless, these risk strata are prognostic rather than treatment-prescriptive and require prospective validation before clinical implementation.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective single-center design may introduce selection bias and residual confounding. The study was retrospectively registered before formal statistical analysis and manuscript submission. Treatment regimens were heterogeneous and selected according to clinical judgment; therefore, confounding by indication may remain despite IPTW adjustment. Second, the PD-(L)1 plus MTT analysis represented an internal subgroup validation rather than independent external validation, and model performance may therefore be optimistic. Response was assessed using RECIST 1.1 rather than iRECIST; therefore, pseudoprogression may have been misclassified, potentially affecting PFS and model performance in this subgroup. Third, variable selection remained partly data-driven and may have introduced type I error and model optimism despite the high bootstrap selection frequencies. The tertile-based risk thresholds were also cohort-dependent, and the outcome-derived cut-offs likewise require external validation. Fourth, requiring serial post-treatment biomarker measurements may have introduced survivor selection because patients with very early progression or insufficient follow-up were less likely to contribute trajectory data. Although the 42-day landmark sensitivity analysis showed consistent prognostic performance, residual immortal time bias cannot be completely excluded. Fifth, although PIVKA-II is an established biomarker—especially in AFP-negative HCC61 —more than 30% of longitudinal PIVKA-II data were missing, precluding its inclusion in our trajectory modeling. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted as prognostic rather than treatment-prescriptive and are primarily applicable to patients with HCC managed in settings similar to the present cohort. Prospective multicenter external validation is required before clinical implementation.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates the prognostic significance of longitudinal AFP, D-dimer, and ALC trajectories in patients with HCC. By integrating these trajectories with clinical variables, the IMBT model improved prognostic discrimination compared with BCLC staging, baseline biomarker, and AFP-only trajectory models and showed consistent performance in an internal treatment-defined subgroup receiving a PD-(L)1 inhibitor plus MTT. The model also retained prognostic discrimination and risk-stratification ability in AFP-negative patients, suggesting potential complementary value for patients with low baseline AFP. These findings support the further evaluation of trajectory-based monitoring for dynamic risk assessment and risk-adapted follow-up. However, the proposed risk-stratified management considerations are prognostic rather than treatment-prescriptive, and prospective multicenter external validation is required before clinical implementation or use in treatment decision-making.
