Implementation of a mental health promotion intervention in a high-risk pregnancy unit: the “Ombrelles” program
Authors: Rania Ammar, Julie Béland, Annie Janvier, Catherine M Herba, Hélène St-Pierre, Katel Roddier, Lucie Morin, Martin St-André, Sylvana Côté, Isabelle Boucoiran, Ombrelles team
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh-risk pregnancies often result in prolonged hospitalizations, leading to significant emotional and psychological distress for pregnant women and their families. This study aimed to evaluate the implementation of the "Ombrelles" mental health promotion program in a high-risk pregnancy unit in a Canadian tertiary hospital.MethodsA comprehensive needs assessment was conducted using both quantitative and qualitative methods, including a literature review, surveys, semi-structured interviews, and expert committee meetings. The findings were used to develop the "Ombrelles" program, which has three main components: an educational component with educational in-person or virtual workshops, online courses and videos, a structured support component with activities promoting socialization and relaxation and the promotion of a supportive environment. Surveys were then conducted among inpatients and hospital staff to measure the relevance and satisfaction regarding this intervention.ResultsDuring the first two years of rolling out the program, 123 women admitted in the high-risk pregnancy unit and 30 hospital staff were recruited. Results showed proficient resource utilization, high satisfaction (92.2%), and staff recognition of program relevance (55.2%). The "Ombrelles" program effectively met the identified needs, emphasizing a partnership approach between healthcare teams and patients. Areas for improvement were identified, including addressing technical difficulties and avoiding repetition of themes.ConclusionThe "Ombrelles" program effectively addressed the identified needs of pregnant women hospitalized for high-risk pregnancies. It provided essential support, educational resources, and opportunities for socialization. The program's development process emphasized partnership between healthcare teams and patients, ensuring its appropriateness and effectiveness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08378-2.Keywords: High-risk pregnancy, Mental health promotion, Intervention, Structured support, Patient partner, Hospitalization

Introduction

A high-risk pregnancy is defined by a medical condition which brings a risk to the woman or her fetus such as diabetes, multiple pregnancy, or preeclampsia, as well as other conditions [13]. Globally, over 20 million women are estimated to have high-risk pregnancies, and around 260,000 women died from preventable pregnancy and childbirth-related causes in 2023, with mortality concentrated in low-resource settings [24]. High-risk pregnancy induces high levels of stress to the pregnant person and her loved ones, impacting the family, marital, financial, and social spheres [10]. Labeling a woman as having a high-risk pregnancy can lead to a negative perception of the pregnancy and can contribute to emotional vulnerability and mental health difficulties [13]. Women with high-risk pregnancies are monitored more closely, undergo more medical tests, and sometimes must be hospitalized creating some loss of control over their health and pregnancy.

Health Canada reported that approximately 15% of pregnant women undergo at least one hospitalization in the six months preceding their delivery [23]. Women hospitalized in the high-risk pregnancy unit experience physical, social, emotional, and psychological isolation [14] and are at risk for depression’s symptoms [6, 12] and anxiety [19]. Psycho education and relaxation have been demonstrated effective in this population to decrease the risk of depression symptoms [1, 2].

For women, their partners, and the unborn child, prenatal courses hold great potential for health promotion [22]. These courses aim not only to impart knowledge to future parents but also to reduce anxiety associated with the arrival of the new baby and prevent perinatal depression symptoms [25]. Due to the inherent constraints of hospitalization, pregnant women hospitalized in the high-risk pregnancy unit cannot attend regular prenatal courses (Larose [15]), or if they can participate, the prenatal course content may not be adapted to their situation. Indeed, these courses might prioritize addressing common situations and may lack the necessary preparation or resources to effectively manage the unique challenges associated with high-risk pregnancy.

Given the prevalence of admissions for high-risk pregnancies and the limited interventions available to this vulnerable population, we proposed to implement the “Ombrelles” program, a comprehensive mental health promotion program develop with patient partners specifically for women admitted in high-risk pregnancy units.

Aims

Our objective was to develop and implement the program, and to evaluate its implementation, both from the patients’ and the professional perspectives. Specific aims included the evaluation of the uptake of the program, as well as the documentation of the satisfaction of the inpatients and their suggestions for improvement, as well as those of the clinical team.

Contributions

It fills a knowledge gap by introducing a personalized mental health program for high-risk pregnancies, providing insights on its implementation and effectiveness.

Methods

The development of the “Ombrelles” program was conducted in a tertiary mother and child hospital, the CHU Sainte-Justine (Montreal, Canada). This collaborative effort involved interdisciplinary cooperation and partnership with patients. The program development committee consisted of two patient partners, four nurses, an orderly caregiver, a maternal fetal medicine specialist, a resident in gynecology-obstetrics, a representative of volunteers, a spiritual care provider, a librarian, a physiotherapist, a health promotion consultant, a lactation consultant, a neonatologist, a communication consultant and a project manager who was also a nurse consultant.

In order to plan an intervention that leverages existing resources within our institution and addresses the needs of patients with high-risk pregnancy, an iterative planning process was implemented. This process was based on Bartholomew's Intervention Mapping process, a systematic framework for planning health promotion programs through six iterative steps—needs assessment, formulation of change objectives, selection of theory-based methods, program development, implementation planning, and evaluation [5, 7]—and drew inspiration from health promotion strategies outlined in the Ottawa Charter [20].

Needs assessment and program development

For the needs assessment, we used the PRECEDE–PROCEED model, a comprehensive framework that guides the design of health promotion programs by linking assessment of behavioral, environmental, and social determinants (PRECEDE) with program implementation and evaluation (PROCEED), thereby allowing for the most accurate identification of client needs [5, 9, 18]. It has previously been used in a perinatal context, in the planning of complex interventions aimed at reducing distress in perinatal settings (Fontein-Kuipers 2016). In our context, the initial idea for such a program came directly from hospitalized women themselves, who expressed the need for more tailored support and education during their hospitalization for high-risk pregnancy. This patient-identified need served as the foundation for the formal needs assessment.

First, a literature review was conducted. The following keywords were used in the PubMed, Google Scholar and Cinhal databases: INPATIENT AND HIGH-RISK PREGNANCY AND (BEDREST ACTITY RESTRICTION, ANXIETY, DEPRESSION, and POST-TRAUMATIC STRESS DISORDER). To be included, the article had to report interventions or results applicable in a tertiary or quaternary hospital setting and addressed at least one of the key elements targeted by the program, such as coping strategies, anxiety or depression. The reference lists of selected texts were also reviewed.

Complementary quantitative survey and qualitative interview methods were employed to inform the development of the program. On the quantitative side, a survey was conducted in both French and English between June and August 2019 among women admitted to the high-risk pregnancy unit for more than 48 h (N = 20). The content of the survey was developed in consultation with the clinical team of the high-risk pregnancy unit and one patient partner and based on previous work in the institution. On the qualitative side, semi-structured interviews were conducted with four patients who had been hospitalized for more than a week and/or had multiple transitions between the maternity unit and the high-risk pregnancy hospitalization unit. Informed consent was obtained from patients before participating in the survey or interviews. The results and notes were anonymized.

After reviewing the results of the previous steps, the priority needs were recorded in an initial PRECEED model (Fig. 1). To ensure that the information gathered in the initial phases accurately reflected the needs of our patient population, we used a "Brainwriting '' technique, as described by Pineault and Daveluy [18], among the “Ombrelles” program development committee. Each committee member was asked to write down the needs of women hospitalized in the high-risk pregnancy unit. When a person had finished, they were asked to take a list of needs completed by another member of the team and try to complete it. The exercise continued until all ideas were exhausted. Subsequently, in a large group, the PRECEDE–PROCEED model was updated and the themes were ranked by priority needs. Five needs emerged as priority (Table 1): (1) support, (2) socialization, (3) education, (4) reducing stress related to one's health, the health of their baby, and their family situation, and (5) a soothing environment. These needs served as a basis for the development of the mental health promotion program.

Program

In 2020, the “Ombrelles” (English translation: Umbrella) program was implemented. It is available to all persons admitted in the high-risk pregnancy unit and their partners/families. Figure 2 presents the structure of the program and the various strategies that were implemented. More details on each strategy are provided in the appendix. The program included access to the “Toi, Moi, BB” online platform, which uses mindfulness, self-compassion, and cognitive-behavioral therapy techniques, to help women facing stress and anxiety during pregnancy [4].

It also included biweekly workshops which were developed by the program coordinator and the healthcare professionals sitting on the program development committee. A detailed brief was created for each workshop as well as the other components of the program to ensure their sustainability and to standardize the content delivered across time. The workshop facilitators were all employed by the hospital with expertise relevant to the program: nurses, nurse practitioners, neonatologists, nutritionists, lactation consultants, librarians, physiotherapists, spiritual consultants. Since medical constraints may prevent some women from participating in face-to-face workshops (bed rest, isolation for medical reasons, etc.), online participation in the workshops was possible, although face-to-face participation was encouraged.

Evaluation of objectives after program implementation

Pregnant women admitted to the high-risk pregnancy unit at 23 weeks' gestation or more, and whose hospitalization was planned for five days or more, were invited to give their feedback on the program using questionnaires administered between November 2020 and October 2022. Exclusion criteria were age under 14 years old, no understanding of either English or French, admission for medical termination of pregnancy and severe fetal malformations. Women were contacted in person or by phone, by a research assistant to invite them to participate in the research project.

The staff members of the high-risk pregnancy unit, including the workshop facilitators, were surveyed between December 2020 and March 2021.

Participants had the option to complete the questionnaires on paper or online directly though the RedCap electronic data capture tools hosted on the server of the “Université de Montréal” [11].

Measures

The content of the questionnaires was developed with the patient partners involved in the development of the program, based on the objectives, the literature review and the content of the program (Appendix 2). A first questionnaire was completed by the pregnant participants at the time of hospital admission to assess their sociodemographic characteristics. Another questionnaire was used after each participation in a workshop. It comprised closed questions with responses on a 5-point Likert scale, and open-ended questions, asking participants to evaluate the workshops, what they appreciated, disliked and what could be improved. One last questionnaire was administered 6 to 8 weeks postpartum, to evaluate how much each component of the “Ombrelles” program was used.

The questionnaire for workshop facilitators and staff members was similarly developed. It aimed to gather additional insights from a service-provider perspective, facilitating nuanced results and enabling necessary adjustments to the intervention.

Analysis

Categorical and continuous variables are reported using descriptive statistics.

Textual responses to open-ended questions were subjected to thematic descriptive analysis to create a repertoire of the main recurrent themes and to identify avenues for improving the intervention from the perspective of the workshop’s facilitators [21]. Themes and sub-themes were rigorously defined by two independent investigators. Then, two investigators independently coded all the data according to themes. A specialist in mixed methods supervised each step of the analysis and assessed coding reliability. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus.

The use of the program strategies

The workshops were attended by 49% of the study participants, who reported to have attended between 1 and 7 workshops. Regarding the viewing of the videos accessible on the television in the room, 57.7% of the participants watched the explanatory video of the “Ombrelles” program, and 34.3% viewed the relaxation exercise video with an average of two times. Furthermore, 47.8% viewed the maternity ward tour video, and 47.1% watched the neonatology unit tour video. Moreover, 28.5% watched one of the three physiotherapy exercise videos.

Regarding the utilization of the provided educational resources: 57.1% visited the website of the high-risk pregnancy unit, and 16.9% encouraged one or more family members or friends to explore the same website. Moreover, 41.4% read the relaxation exercise materials displayed on the room's wall, and 42.9% made use of the journal provided during their hospitalization with an average of four journal entries. On the other hand, only 5.7% used the “Toi, Moi, Bébé” online intervention, and 4.3% took advantage of the book reservation service. Additionally, 13.5% utilized the electronic device lending service for electronic tablets.

Evaluation of the workshops by participants and facilitators

During the study period, due to the onset of the COVID pandemic, 97% of workshops in which these women participated were conducted live online, while 3% were held in person. Among the evaluated workshops, participants reported attending the following ones: neonatology (33.3%), breastfeeding (18.3%), book club (11.7%), relaxation and stress reduction (8.3%), physiotherapy (8.3%), adapting to hospitalization (10%), group sharing and self-reflection (5%), stages of labor (3.3%), pain management during labor (1.7%).

Most participants (92.2%) expressed satisfaction with their participation in the “Ombrelles” program workshops. Furthermore, 70.5% acknowledged that these workshops helped them to prepare for the arrival of their baby or babies. Additionally, 67.8% felt a sense of empowerment over their situation, 90.3% reported feeling less alone, 69.3% had the opportunity to connect with others in similar situations and 82.2% stated that the interactions during the workshops had a positive impact on them. Overall, 93.6% of women hospitalized in the high-risk pregnancy unit affirmed that the workshops were helpful.

Among the participants, 59 answered the open-ended question "What did you like?". The main themes that emerged from their responses included listening, information quality, Interactions with others, breaking isolation, and well-being. Indeed, the participants appreciated the relevance, and the clarity of the information provided, the quality of workshop planning, and the way facilitators took the time to address their questions and reassure them. The comments also reflect a strong sense of appreciation for the workshop's interactivity, the opportunity to ask questions to the workshop facilitator, and the sharing of experiences with other participants. Several participants highlighted the significance of seeing others who share similar concerns about maternity, to break their isolation and strengthen the sense of community ("I liked to see that we are not alone in this situation and knowing that it went well for most babies and parents before us. It's reassuring").

We received 28 responses to the question "What did you not like or what needs improvement?" that mainly reflect the following themes: low participation, technical issues, more topics, openness to partner participation, interruptions due to medical care, lack of content renewal, and adaptation to the patients. Indeed, some expressed their disappointment regarding the low number of participants and suggested that a more interactive in-person experience would have been more enjoyable. However, they understood that this was not possible at that time due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Technical issues were also reported during virtual meetings. Additionally, some participants expressed the desire to invite their partner to the meeting "even if they are not at the hospital." Some suggested that the content of the workshops should be better adapted to the audience, for instance based on the gestational age of the participants. Finally, the lack of content renewal was highlighted as a problem for women experiencing prolonged hospitalization. Further suggestions for improving the “Ombrelles” program included a desire for richer visual content (e.g. videos), an opportunity to participate in workshops after hospitalization, and the need to provide support for hospitalized women to go outside the unit.

Among the workshop facilitators (n = 29), 74% considered that they had achieved the intended objectives of these workshops. We received 18 responses to the question, "What were the strengths of the workshop (key takeaways/repeatable elements)?". The main themes highlighted in their responses included participation, exchange and sharing, adaptability, and well-being and relaxation. Indeed, workshop facilitators unanimously praised several positive aspects of their sessions, especially the interactivity and sessions based on exchanges and sharing ("A smooth discussion among everyone, humor, beautiful sharing"). They also emphasized their ability to adapt their workshops to the participants' needs (content, language). Finally, the facilitators emphasized that these workshops often serve as a means of promoting well-being and relaxation.

Regarding the question, "What were the weaknesses of the workshop (elements not to replicate and reasons)?", we received 22 responses where the main themes are Technical Issues, Low Number of Participants, Repetitive Content, and Limited Interactions. The shortcomings identified by workshop facilitators mainly highlight technical problems related to the virtual nature of the workshops. The low number of participants in some workshops was a concern highlighted in relation to the resources mobilized ("Low participation rate, two patients present at the workshop when about ten were eligible"). Concerns were also expressed about the repetition of similar questions causing a waste of time for both the participants and the workshops’ facilitators. Finally, some facilitators noted a low level of interaction among some participants, highlighting difficulties in encouraging active participation.

We received 21 responses to the question, "What solutions do you propose?". The themes raised in their responses are Improvement of Connectivity, Promotion of Participation, and Optimization of Organization. Workshop facilitators identified several improvement strategies that have been implemented. Technical solutions have been found to resolve connectivity issues, and a user manual is now provided to guide participants in the connection process. Workshops are now the responsibility of the coordinating nurse of the unit, who organizes the schedule, promotes workshops to the nursing team and hospitalized patients, and cancels the workshop if there is only one participant. Finally, to encourage more active participation, facilitators have proposed asking direct questions and sharing concrete examples from mothers' daily lives.

Evaluation of the implementation by the hospital staff

Among the 30 surveyed hospital staff, 55.2% believed that pregnant patients admitted to the high-risk pregnancy unit did not receive adequate support before this intervention. Moreover, 86.7% hospital staff affirmed the relevance of the “Ombrelles” program for these women. However, four staff members disagreed regarding the relevance of the “Ombrelles” program and identified the lack of information on the program’s content as main limitations.

For the question “What do you like about the “Ombrelles” program?", we received 24 responses. The main themes were breaking isolation, moments of sharing, strengthening bonds, access to information, diversity of activities, occupation and distraction, improvement of mental health, and the well-structured nature. Generally, the hospital staff stated that the program is positive, and well-perceived by the patients. Indeed, the hospital staff appreciate the multidisciplinary nature of the “Ombrelles” program and its ability to break isolation, provide opportunities for sharing, strengthen social bonds, and offer comprehensive care to patients, contributing to their well-being during hospitalization.

Regarding the question "What do you dislike about the “Ombrelles” program (or what needs improvement)?" there were 14 responses mainly related to the workshops. The main themes included technical difficulties, the need for diversification of topics, restrictive schedule, and increased workload for nurses. Proposed areas for improvement included simplifying the connection process, diversifying the topics addressed, managing schedules based on the needs of patients and the availability of workshop facilitators, and better coordination with nursing staff to ensure optimal patient participation.

For “Other comments”, there were 5 responses with the following themes: initial enthusiasm of participants, suggestions for managing redundancies, flexibility, appreciation for the variety of topics.

Discussion

The ‘’Ombrelles’’ program emerged from the meeting of experiential knowledge and the knowledge of health professionals, while respecting the available resources of a tertiary hospital. The “Ombrelles” program was developed in partnership with patients and a multidisciplinary team to reduce isolation and promote the mental health of hospitalized pregnant women. The three components of the program (educational, structured support and supportive environment) allow pregnant women to empower themselves by developing individual skills and provide them with tools to better cope with the difficult situation of hospitalization in the high-risk pregnancy unit. The strength of the program lies in the rigor with which it was developed and the fact that it comprehensively addresses the needs of hospitalized pregnant women. It is further supported by the successful implementation reported here, with high satisfaction from both patients and staff members, achievements of pre-specified objectives (increasing readiness for childbirth, empowerment, and breaking isolation), and overall good uptake of the various components.

As emphasized in the Ottawa Charter [17], several intervention strategies are necessary in health promotion such as building individual skills, creating supportive environments, developing health-supportive policies, strengthening community action, and reorienting health services [3]. It is also necessary to act at various levels (individual, structural). In this sense, even though some patients may not have been able to participate in an educational workshop, they could benefit from some part of the mental health promotion program.

To our knowledge, the Ombrelles program is among the few initiatives specifically designed and implemented within a high-risk pregnancy unit to promote mental health among hospitalized women. Its distinctive contribution lies in its integration of several components to comprehensively answer to the needs of the patients and to promote mental health not only through psychosocial interventions but also through education, socialization and empowerment. Indeed, various psychosocial interventions have been demonstrated effective in promoting mental health during pregnancy and postpartum, in the general population [8], and among adolescents [16]. However, published interventions on mental health promotion program in high-risk pregnancy units have so far had only focus on psychoeducation and relaxation [1, 2] without providing prenatal courses adapted to the unique situation of women admitted in high-risk pregnancy units. Another unique feature of the “Ombrelles” program is that several workshops are facilitated by the unit’s nursing staff, who were trained to deliver the program, thereby embedding the intervention within routine clinical practice and ensuring sustainability. This approach could be adapted to other high-risk pregnancy units, using the comprehensive documentation provided in the appendix. Furthermore, the program adopted a hybrid model (in-person and online) that was rapidly adapted during the COVID-19 pandemic, allowing continued access despite hospital restrictions. Finally, the “Ombrelles” program is explicitly framed as a comprehensive mental health promotion initiative rather than a therapeutic intervention, emphasizing empowerment, coping strategies, and social connection.

The decision to introduce the program during the COVID19 pandemic aimed to provide support to patients and their partners. However, implementing such an intervention requires changes in habits and practices for the patients and healthcare professionals, which may explain the limited utilization of certain aspects of the intervention. Implementing practice changes is always a complex process. The high-risk hospitalization unit primarily focuses on physical perinatal care, and healthcare professionals in this unit had limited experience in mental health promotion interventions. For instance, there were several obstacles to the implantation of the program, including technological literacy of the clinical team, health professional visits and clinical tests taking place at the same time as the workshops, that have been addressed to improve the benefits of the program.

Conclusion

The "Ombrelles" program's development highlighted collaboration among healthcare teams, patients and families, ensuring its suitability and effectiveness. The program successfully met needs prioritized by pregnant women with high-risk pregnancies, offering crucial support, educational materials, and socialization opportunities. Both healthcare teams and patients found it relevant, and participants expressed satisfaction. After three years, the program is now part of the routine care within our high-risk pregnancy unit. The workshops are provided both virtually and in-person, but in-person attendance is favored. The sustainability of the program is ensured by the fact that it relies only on existing resources within our institution, and its management is ensured by the nurse manager of our division. Lessons learned have included the need for continuous reminder and engagement of the clinical team because of high turnover in the team, and the importance of the integration of the program in routine care on the unit for the success of the program.
