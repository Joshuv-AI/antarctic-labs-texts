Circumferential subparaneurial ganglion cyst involving the ulnar nerve at the elbow: illustrative case
Authors: Godard C. W. de Ruiter, Kimberly K. Amrami, Miguel A. Reina, Byung-chul Son, Robert J. Spinner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Subparaneurial cysts represent a rare new variant of intraneural ganglion cysts (IGCs) in which a circumferential cyst is contained within the potential space of the paraneurium, outside the epineurium.1–4 Several cases have been previously reported in the lower limb.3,5–8 The mechanism for their formation and propagation has not been well understood.5–8 Our group recently described the geyser theory, which explains the formation of this type of cyst for so-called extreme cases involving the common peroneal and tibial nerves that extend to the sciatic nerve.4 MRI analysis of 8 cases (4 novel and 4 previously reported cases5–8) showed consistent distribution patterns with circumferential subparaneurial cysts along the course of the involved nerves over long distances and crossover from the parent nerve into the other distal branches at the level of the sciatic bifurcation, with the cyst following the path of least resistance and influenced by pressure and pressure fluxes.3 In these cases, MRI studies demonstrate classic findings in several nerves (sciatic, peroneal, tibial, and sural nerves): an owl-eye sign is seen on axial T2-weighted images (subparaneurial cyst around the epineurium) and the tram-track sign on coronal or sagittal T2-weighted images, in which the subparaneurial cyst distributes around the epineurium. The articular theory explains how, in these cases, cyst arising from the superior tibiofibular or knee joint ascends below the epineurium of the parent peroneal or tibial nerve and crosses over at the sciatic nerve and then ascends in the sciatic or descends in the peroneal or tibial nerves.9,10 The geyser theory elaborates on crossover and explains how subepineurial cyst at the sciatic nerve bifurcation moves from the subepineurial to the subparaneurial compartment.4 Histological analysis at the sciatic bifurcation showed the presence of a consistent fenestration that would explain the potential for cyst to transfer from the subepineurial to the subparaneurial space at this location.4

The goal of the present study was to investigate whether the same explanation would hold for subparaneurial ganglion cysts at other locations. We queried our prospectively maintained database and reviewed > 250 cases of IGCs in the upper limb from > 1100 total cases in the world’s literature11 to find any clear examples of subparaneurial cysts at other locations. We found one previously reported case identified as an IGC of the ulnar nerve at the elbow12 in which we identified unrecognized MRI evidence of a circumferential subparaneurial cyst in the ulnar nerve in the published MR images; the appearance was markedly different from that in the MRI studies available in the 131 reported cases of ulnar IGCs at the elbow region. We hypothesized that this case would have analogous clinicoradiological findings as those described in the lower limb that would support the unified articular and geyser theories: cyst arising from the elbow would ascend in the ulnar nerve and crossover would occur proximally—likely at the level of the medial cord, histological analysis should demonstrate an analogous fenestration. With the assistance of an author of this paper12 (B.C.S.), we obtained full access to the patient’s imaging studies (preoperative radiographic imaging and MRI) and operative photographs, and then, as proof of concept, we performed histological analysis in a cadaver.

Illustrative Case

A 66-year-old man presented with the acute onset of symptoms of ulnar neuropathy for 1 month. Electromyography showed moderate signs of denervation in ulnar nerve innervated muscles. Because of the acute onset, MRI was performed, which showed an intraneural cyst along the ulnar nerve with an elbow joint connection.12 The ganglion was surgically treated by drainage of the cystic part outside the epineurium and transection of the articular branch to the elbow joint (Fig. 1). At the 1-year follow-up, the patient’s symptoms had resolved. There was no clinical recurrence of any cyst. Reinterpretation of available imaging was performed by a fellowship-trained musculoskeletal radiologist (K.K.A.) with much experience with IGCs. The plain radiographs showed severe degenerative disease in the elbow (Fig. 2A). MRI studies showed extensive circumferential subparaneurial cyst along the course of the ulnar nerve extending in the distal arm (to the proximal extent of the elbow MRI), with characteristic owl-eye and tram-track signs (asterisk in Fig. 2B–D).3 A small subepineurial cyst was also observed, as well as a connection (arrowhead in Fig. 2B) to a degenerative elbow joint (+ in Fig. 2D) and small extraneural ganglion cysts. Analysis was performed on histological slides from a previously performed cadaver study of the brachial plexus. Cross-sections that had been obtained at a 2- to 3-mm interval showed openings in the medial side of the epineurium of the ulnar nerve. At the same level of the medial cord, openings were found in the lateral side of the epineurium of the median nerve. Vessels were observed close to these openings.13 The paraneurium at this level surrounded both nerves.

Discussion

Observations

This illustrative case of a subparaneurial cyst in the ulnar nerve at the elbow shares analogous findings to those previously described in peroneal and tibial subparaneurial cysts.3 We hypothesized that for intraneural cyst to be able to transfer from below the epineurium of the ulnar nerve to the subparaneurial compartment, it had to cross over at a more proximal level, like the sciatic bifurcation for cysts involving the peroneal and tibial nerves, which in the case of the ulnar nerve would be the level of the medial cord. Subsequently, the cyst can descend along the subparaneurial compartment giving the characteristic distribution observed in the reinterpretation of this subparaneurial case (Fig. 2). Similar to fenestrations observed at the level of the sciatic bifurcation,4,13 we postulated that similar fenestrations have to exist at the level of the medial cord. For this, we analyzed histological sections from previously studied cadaver specimens (Fig. 3), which indeed showed a fenestration in the epineurium of the ulnar nerve at the level of the medial cord. This fenestration can explain the transfer of cyst from the subepineurial to the subparaneurial compartment. The cyst subsequently quickly distributes in the subparaneurial compartment, because the cyst had ascended under high pressure inside the dense space below the epineurium and burst into the subparaneurial compartment, which has a higher compliance—a phenomenon that we have compared to the eruption of a geyser.4 This leads to filling not only of the subparaneurial space around the ulnar nerve, but also potentially around other distal branches, including the median nerve following the medial cord, because the ulnar nerve and the part of the median nerve that originate from the medial cord at this level share the same paraneurium (Fig 3). At the same time, it leads to deflation of the subepineurial cyst. Interestingly, in the literature we found 1 report14 with cyst extending from the elbow to the infraclavicular region, in which the authors mentioned that the median nerve was also affected,15 supporting our hypothesis for crossover at the level of the medial cord. Limitations

Unfortunately, MRI at a more proximal level for this level was not done. Therefore, we do not have direct proof that the cyst indeed crossed over at the medial cord. Also, we do not have sequential MRI studies prior to surgery showing evolution of the cyst or postoperative imaging showing resolution. Nevertheless, the distribution pattern for this case was quite similar to previous examples with owl-eye and tram-track signs confirming subparaneurial cyst as well as a deflated subepineurial cyst path, which led us to believe that the same sequence of events had occurred in this case.4

Another explanation for the geyser mechanism mentioned above with crossover at the level of the medial cord could be that cyst in this case had crossed to the subparaneurial space at the level of the elbow, but we believe that this explanation is less likely because of the 1) substantial longitudinal proximal extent of circumferential cyst in this case (considering that gravity would favor extreme descent) in comparison with the distal extent (broad arrow in Fig. 2B) and 2) the deflation of the subepineurial portion of the cyst (thin arrow in Fig. 2B). Still, we cannot completely exclude this.

Lessons

IGCs, once thought to be rare curiosities, are now recognized to be more common (with ultrasound and MRI) and joint connected.9 We believe that extreme subparaneurial cysts represent an important, underrecognized part of the intraneural cyst life cycle that must be elucidated. In this illustrative case of a subparaneurial cyst of the ulnar nerve at the elbow, we showed that our recently introduced geyser theory also holds for other locations. Moreover, this case also highlights the importance of proximal coverage with imaging in the case of extensive subparaneurial distribution and the clinical implication of understanding this type of cyst.
