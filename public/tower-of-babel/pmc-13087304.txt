Longitudinal evaluation of neurocognitive outcomes in a cohort with persistent post-COVID olfactory dysfunction
Authors: Tiana M. Saak, Jeremy P. Tervo, Patricia T. Jacobson, Brandon J. Vilarello, Francesco F. Caruana, Liam W. Gallagher, Joseph B. Gary, David A. Gudis, Jeffrey N. Motter, Terry E. Goldberg, Davangere P. Devanand, Jonathan B. Overdevest
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Persistent COVID-19-associated olfactory dysfunction (C19OD), along with other neurologic and cognitive deficits are common features of long COVID. This study aims to evaluate longitudinal trends in neurocognitive performance within a cohort with C19OD. In individuals with perceived C19OD we performed serial psychophysical olfactory and neurocognitive assessments at baseline and follow-up one year later. At baseline evaluation, individuals with C19OD were found to have diminished cognitive functioning compared to normosmic counterparts across several domains, including attention, executive functioning, language, learning and memory, and psychomotor speed. At subsequent one-year follow-up assessment, C19OD participants demonstrated cognitive recovery, with performance comparable to normosmic counterparts. These findings suggest that early associations between C19OD and certain neurocognitive domains may dissipate upon repeated longitudinal evaluation, with partial resolution of cognitive deficits despite persistent C19OD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-39663-5.

Introduction

Cognitive and neurological deficits, including memory impairment, slowed mental processing, and impairment in the sense of smell (olfactory dysfunction; OD), are common findings among individuals diagnosed with long COVID following infection with SARS-CoV-2.1,2 Persistent OD in this setting, or COVID-19-associated olfactory dysfunction (C19OD), may predict the development of long COVID3 and reflects a lasting perturbation of peripheral4 and central chemosensory processing5 in brain regions involved in cognitive processing6. While delayed recovery from COVID-19 alone imparts risk for cognitive and memory functioning,7 preliminary studies suggest that C19OD in the early phase of COVID recovery may be associated with cognitive deficits8. The prevalence of persistent C19OD exists in varying degrees, where most studies suggest that persistent C19OD may be present in at least 5% of individuals,9,10 with up to 30% of individuals self-reporting diminished or altered sense of smell in the years following infection11,12.

Given public concerns about the long-term neurological and neurocognitive sequelae of persistent C19OD,13–15 we sought to clarify whether alterations in olfactory function exacerbate or associate with deficits in neurocognitive performance16. While early reports suggest associations between self-reported olfactory capabilities and cognitive deficits,17,18 this prospective longitudinal cohort study seeks to provide insight into the association between psychophysical testing-confirmed C19OD and performance across specific neurocognitive domains. Towards this objective, we sought to answer two questions: (1) at the time that a patient presents for olfactory and cognitive assessment, is the presence of psychophysical C19OD associated with domain-specific neurocognitive performance impairment, and (2) among those individuals with baseline C19OD how does domain-specific neurocognitive performance evolve in a longitudinal cohort?

Methods

This exploratory prospective study was conducted through the Smell and Taste Center at Columbia University Irving Medical Center under recruitment, consent, and evaluation protocols approved by the institutional review board (AAA5380). During the initial recruitment phase, 120 participants enrolled and completed baseline neurocognitive and chemosensory psychophysical assessment and among these, 54 participants continued with longitudinal monitoring, including follow-up one year after their initial evaluation.

Participant evaluation instruments and batteries

Participation in this study was inclusive of individuals 18 years of age or older who had a confirmed positive SARS-CoV-2 infection via positive PCR or serology to SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid antibody. Exclusion criteria included any perceived or diagnosed pre-existing olfactory dysfunction, any pre-existing neurological or cognitive condition such as TBI or Alzheimer’s disease, other health problems independently associated with olfactory dysfunction (including congenital anosmia, chemical exposure, prior sinonasal surgery). Additionally, to account for individuals whose olfaction may be negatively impacted by rhinologic disease we excluded those who endorsed greater than moderate-severe rhinologic dysfunction on the Sino-nasal Outcome Test (SNOT-22) either with a modified rhinologic subdomain (score of > 21)19,20 or responding to any rhinologic subdomain question with the response the “problem as bad as it can be”, or had an Olfactory Cleft Endoscopy Scale (OCES) score21 of 3 or more22.

Participants completed neurocognitive and olfactory psychophysical assessments at baseline and 1-year follow-up visits, as well as survey instruments evaluating patient demographics, medical history, and perceptions of olfactory impairment. Neurocognitive assessments were selected to evaluate a range of neurological domains over time while reducing practice effects. The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) is a widely used evaluation of global cognitive functioning23. The digit-span test assesses working memory (both forward/backward) and executive functioning (backward only)24,25. The Controlled Oral Word Association Test (COWAT) or Verbal Fluency exam assesses verbal fluency and language skills25,26. The trail-making test (TMT) assesses psychomotor speed (TMT-A) and executive function (TMT-B).27,28 The digit-symbol test is an evaluation of processing speed25. Lastly, the Weschler Memory Scale III for Logical Memory assesses both immediate and delayed recall memory29,30. A composite Z-score was created that included all neurocognitive assessments except the MoCA. The MoCA is already a global neurocognitive assessment, and the composite Z-score across all other assessments provides a more comprehensive index of cognitive functioning.

Olfactory assessment was completed using the validated, extended Sniffin’ Sticks test (Burghart Messtechnik GmBH, Holm, Germany), including odor threshold (T), odor discrimination (D), and odor identification (I) with the calculation of TDI scores and comparison to normative data per established protocols31,32. Based on the results of the Sniffin’ Sticks assessments, participants were stratified according to olfactory status. Psychophysical OD, referred to as “OD” in the patient groups, was defined as any psychophysically validated OD (including hyposmia and anosmia), which corresponds to a TDI score ≤ 30.5.33

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics for macOS, version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for all statistical analyses. Neurocognitive outcome variables and olfactory assessments were assessed for normality using histogram and Q-Q plot visualization. Further, non-parametric tests were performed in conjunction with corresponding parametric tests to ensure that there were no large differences in statistical significance between the two. After ensuring variables met the assumptions required for parametric testing via histogram and Q-Q plot visualization and that there was no difference in outcome between parametric and non-parametric tests in terms of the statistical significance of results, we report the results of appropriate parametric tests, including independent samples t-tests and paired samples t-tests along with corresponding multiple linear regression and repeated measures ANOVA to control for covariates.

Intergroup neurocognitive domain differences: cross-sectional analyses performed at two separate time points

All participants (n = 120) were grouped by olfactory status at baseline based on the presence of psychophysical OD: (1) OD and (2) no OD at their baseline psychophysical olfactory assessment. Intergroup differences in each neurocognitive domain, based on baseline neurocognitive scores, were evaluated using independent t-tests. Multiple linear regression was conducted to assess the statistical significance of intergroup differences at baseline when accounting for covariates of age, sex, highest level of education, and time between initial COVID-19 diagnosis and baseline assessment.

Participants presenting for follow-up (n = 54) were grouped by olfactory status at follow-up based on the presence of psychophysical OD: (1) OD and (2) no OD at their follow-up psychophysical olfactory assessment. Intergroup differences in each neurocognitive domain based on the neurocognitive scores collected at follow-up assessment were evaluated with independent t-tests. Multiple linear regression was conducted to evaluate the statistical significance of intergroup differences at the follow-up assessment, while accounting for covariates of age, sex, highest level of education, and the time between the initial COVID-19 diagnosis and baseline assessment.

Intragroup neurocognitive domain differences: paired longitudinal analysis among study participants

Only participants who completed evaluations at both time points (n = 54) were included in the longitudinal analysis, with C19OD olfactory status established at baseline: (1) OD at baseline (n = 35) and (2) no OD at baseline (n = 19). Intragroup change-score trajectories were compared using paired-samples t-tests, separately by OD status at baseline. Repeated-measures ANOVA was also performed for each OD status across baseline groupings to allow for a paired longitudinal analysis accounting for covariates of age, sex, highest level of education, and time between COVID-19 diagnosis and baseline assessment.

Further, an intergroup, matched-comparison of individuals’ neurocognitive change scores was conducted using an independent-samples t-test to determine whether individual longitudinal changes in neurocognitive scores differed significantly between the two groups. Multiple linear regression was conducted to evaluate the significance of any associations, controlling for covariates of age, sex, education status, and the time between the initial COVID-19 diagnosis and the baseline assessment.

Lastly, to account for potential differences in time to follow-up among participants, an intergroup, matched-comparison of individuals’ neurocognitive change scores per unit time was conducted using an independent-samples t-test 34.

Study population characteristics by Follow-up status

To account for the potential differences among individuals who completed both assessments and those who were lost to follow-up, we stratified participants by follow-up status to perform an intergroup analysis. Our longitudinal cohort (n = 54) and a group comprised of individuals lost to follow-up (n = 66) underwent intergroup analysis for several assessment batteries that either (a) may potentially influence follow-up status such as education status and mental health or (b) were important outcomes of interest, such as olfaction status and neurocognitive scores at baseline using Pearson’s Chi-squared and independent samples t-tests. All statistical tests were two-tailed and performed at α = 0.05 level for inference.

Results

We conducted baseline psychophysical olfactory and neurocognitive assessment on 120 participants, of which 94 (79%) were women and 26 (21%) were men (Table 1). Of these, 54 individuals returned for a follow-up evaluation, including 37 (69%) women and 17 (31%) men. Baseline testing was performed at a mean time from COVID-19 diagnosis of 461 days, and one-year follow-up testing was conducted at a mean time from diagnosis of 833 days, or 372 days following baseline assessment. All patients were over the age of 18 at the time of assessment with a mean age of 44 (SD 16) at the time of enrollment. Education level of participants skewed toward college (baseline, follow-up; 42%, 44%) or advanced degree (48%, 48%). No participants required hospitalization for their initial COVID-19 illness.

At baseline, 38 (32%) of participants were normosmic with a mean TDI of 34.56, while 68 (57%) were hyposmic and 14 (12%) were anosmic, with mean TDI scores of 25.01 and 11.50, respectively. Upon follow-up, 28 (52%) of participants were found to be normosmic with a mean TDI of 35.31. Among those with olfactory dysfunction at follow-up assessment, 24 (44%) were found to be hyposmic, and 2 (4%) were found to be anosmic, with TDI means of 26.35 and 10.50, respectively.

Full cohort intergroup neurocognitive domain differences at two separate time points

Among participants at baseline (n = 120), the no OD group outperformed the OD group on MoCA, digit span backward, verbal fluency, TMT-B, digit symbol, logical memory, and composite Z-score (Fig. 1a & Table S1). There were no significant differences in scores at baseline found between groups on digit span forward or TMT-A. Multiple linear regression showed that differences in MoCA and composite Z-score remained statistically significant when comparing OD versus no OD at baseline, after adjusting for age, sex, highest level of education, and the time between COVID-19 diagnosis and baseline assessment.

At follow-up (n = 54), there was interval resolution of the previously observed significant differences in neurocognitive performance between OD status groups. (Fig. 1b & Table S1). This lack of difference in neurocognitive performance based on OD grouping held after performing multiple linear regression (Table S2).

Subsequently, the longitudinal cohort of n = 54 individuals was compared to the group of n = 66 lost to follow-up. While this longitudinal cohort outperformed the lost to follow-up group on MoCA (p = 0.008) (Table S3) at baseline, there were no other significant differences between these two groups regarding baseline olfactory status, age, education status, BAI, PHQ-9, digit forward, digit backward, TMT-A, TMT-B, language fluency, digit-symbol, or logical memory.

Intragroup evolution of neurocognitive performance: paired longitudinal analysis

Only individuals who completed baseline and follow-up assessments were grouped into (1) OD at baseline; and (2) no OD at baseline for paired analysis. The OD at baseline group showed significant improvement at follow-up assessment on verbal fluency, TMT-A, digit symbol, and logical memory (Fig. 2 & Table S4). There were no significant differences in performance between the two time points on MoCA, digit forward, digit backward, or TMT-B. The no OD at baseline group improved on TMT-A and digit symbol at follow-up but showed no significant differences in performance between the two timepoints on any other neurocognitive assessments. Repeated measures ANOVA demonstrated no significant differences between baseline and follow-up for either group after controlling for age, sex, education status, and time from COVID-19 diagnosis to baseline assessment (Table S5).

A comparison of both groups’ mean change scores between both time points for each cognitive test yielded no significant difference for any neurocognitive assessment (Table S6). Multiple linear regression comparing both groups’ mean change scores while controlling for the above covariates similarly showed no significant difference between the two groups’ change scores for any neurocognitive assessment (Table S7). There were also no significant differences between groups’ mean change scores per unit time for any neurocognitive assessment (Table S8). Further, mean days between baseline and follow-up assessments was not found to be significantly different between groups.

Discussion

In this prospective cohort study, we sought to evaluate the longitudinal impact of persistent C19OD on neurocognitive performance. Limited by loss to follow-up, we completed (1) two separate cross-sectional analyses of domain-specific neurocognitive performance at baseline and one-year time points among the full study cohort (n = 120) and (2) evaluation of paired longitudinal evolution of neurocognitive performance by olfactory status at baseline among individuals completing assessments at both time points (n = 54). In doing so, we sought to answer the questions (1) When a patient shows up at a point in time after their COVID diagnosis, is there an association between their OD status and cognitive impairment? and (2) Is there a change over time in cognitive outcomes in those who initially presented with psychophysical OD versus those who did not? While individuals who returned for their longitudinal evaluation may impose a bias not observed in those only presenting for baseline assessment, we found no difference in factors that may potentially impact an individual’s ability or desire to present for follow-up, such as socioeconomic status, level of anxiety or stress regarding health or OD, and overall mental health. When evaluating neurocognitive outcome measures, however, baseline MoCA scores were significantly different comparing those who completed both assessments and those who were lost to follow-up. This observed difference was not clinically significant, however, and there were no significant differences between these two groups in olfaction status or any of the other neurocognitive assessments. Thus, we concluded that there was no strong indication that those who completed both assessments were significantly unique from those who were lost to follow-up, and the paired intragroup analysis is sufficient to produce results that are likely representative of the overall population, though there are limitations in the paired analysis of MoCA assessments. Any results produced from analyses relying on the MoCA assessment alone should be interpreted with caution.

When examining the data trends of the other neurocognitive outcome tests in our battery, which represent detailed assessments of specific cognitive domains, we found that cognitive function is impacted among those with long-COVID and is initially more pronounced among those with C19OD. This neurocognitive deficit dissipated during the interval of subsequent follow-up despite relative persistence of OD. These findings offer a message of optimism with a delayed improvement in cognitive functioning.

At the baseline evaluation, the strongest intergroup associations for OD and no-OD groups were with global cognition, executive function, and a neurocognitive composite Z-score (Figure S1). Given the prevalence of neurologic complaints as part of the long-COVID symptom constellation,35,36 poor performance on global cognitive tests is not surprising. Individuals with OD may more commonly have other lingering neurologic deficits, which may be particularly noticeable upon evaluating executive function and psychomotor speed at a more granular level. This association appears to have a temporal component, as the relationship between C19OD and neurocognition resolves only at a follow-up time point nearly three years after initial diagnosis. Indeed, those with C19OD at baseline seem to perform worse across several domains that improve during the follow-up interval, as evidenced by the evolution of scores among those with OD at baseline versus those without. Despite this improvement, however, neither the mean change score nor the mean change score per time between these two groups was found to be significant for any neurocognitive assessment, suggesting that the magnitude of this improvement may not be substantial when compared to those with no OD at baseline.

There is a global effect on cognition associated with C19OD during the initial evaluation window, including effects on attention, executive function, language, learning & memory, and psychomotor speed (Figure S1). Many of these domains are also implicated in “brain fog” – a phenomenon often described by patients as cognitive impairment, including difficulty concentrating, confusion, cognitive slowing, forgetfulness, word-finding difficulty, short-term memory loss, and mental fatigue. In a meta-analysis including more than 40,000 long COVID patients, van der Feltz-Cornelis, et al. (2024) found the prevalence of brain fog among long COVID patients to be 23%, using reports of concentration difficulties, irritability, memory loss, and/or confusion37. These symptoms can be attributed to neurocognitive domains of attention, learning & memory, and executive function – domains all shown to be associated with olfactory status at baseline.

Episodic memory appears to be associated with olfactory decline, particularly with odor identification tasks, amidst an overall impairment in global cognition in an aging population38–40. Indeed, performance on logical memory was significantly poorer among those with OD at baseline, and this group experienced a significant longitudinal improvement in logical memory score. In the study, we used an extended olfactory battery to define OD, including identification, threshold, and discrimination domains, while also comprehensively evaluating neurocognition. While some authors suggest that the molecular underpinnings of Alzheimer’s disease and long COVID may be linked, there are also clear differences in pathogenesis, which may allow for recovery of neurocognitive status in C19OD41.

Douaud et al. (2022) describe associations of SARS-CoV-2 infection with structural brain changes, most notably including reduction in global brain size, and reduction of gray matter in the orbitofrontal cortex and the parahippocampal gyrus5. These structural brain changes may impact global cognitive functioning and demonstrate a preferential impact on structures associated with olfaction, reward, and decision-making,42 as well as structures associated with memory and contextual associations43. While the neurocognitive findings reported in our study appear to resolve within two years of initial COVID-19 diagnosis, olfactory deficits demonstrate a more limited recovery, suggesting the structural brain changes associated with SARS-CoV-2 infection may reflect a larger impact on olfaction than neurocognition. Indeed, before the emergence of C19OD, Dintica, et al. (2019) reported MRI findings consistent with smaller volumes of the olfactory bulbs, hippocampus, as well as entorhinal, fusiform, and middle temporal cortices among patients with impaired olfaction44.

A discussion of the clinical significance of these findings is warranted, particularly given that many of the differences between OD and no OD groups at baseline are small and the longitudinal improvements seen in the OD group across several of the domains are small, as well. While there is limited literature regarding minimal clinically important difference (MCID) values for the neurocognitive assessments utilized in this study, there are reports including normative data. Based on previous reports and normative data, we conclude that there is likely clinically meaningful longitudinal improvement in logical memory45 and verbal fluency46 among those who presented with OD at the baseline assessment. However, the other assessments that also showed statistically significant improvement, such as digit symbol47 and TMT-A,48 likely did not confer a clinically meaningful change. Further work in larger cohorts with defined MCID values for the neurocognitive assessments is warranted to further elucidate the clinical significance of the findings reported in this study.

The nature of post-pandemic societal recovery and a study design that emphasized recruitment of individuals with persistent OD (> 3 months) resulted in a larger C19OD than the comparative non-OD cohort. The larger OD cohort also reflects a success in recruiting individuals with persistent OD compared to normative controls, particularly as altruistic study participation waned as the acute phase of the pandemic subsided. This enrichment accounts for the discrepancy in sample sizes between the OD and no OD groups and explains the high prevalence of OD in our study population. Indeed, at the follow-up evaluation, a large portion of this cohort returning for evaluation demonstrated persistent OD on their psychophysical testing, consistent with other studies on olfactory recovery in C19OD.10,12,49 Thus, this study population likely reflects a higher prevalence of persistent C19OD than that observed in the general population.

This study’s strengths include the longitudinal assessment of neurocognition and OD using validated psychophysical olfactory and neurocognitive assessments to encompass a variety of smell modalities and neurocognitive domains to demonstrate an association between these variables. Additionally, the non-elderly age of our population (mean age of 44) minimizes the risk of age-related cognitive and/or olfactory decline as a confounder. Limitations include a small sample size for subgroup analyses and loss to follow-up, which are unlikely to significantly affect the observed trends reported in the present study, except for the MoCA assessment, which may weaken any results or conclusions relevant to this global cognitive assessment, commonly used as a screening tool. The sample size and loss to follow-up may have been insufficient to detect additional associations. While we were unable to complete a formal power analysis as part of this initial exploratory prospective study design, the initial data reporting in this manuscript may inform the study design for future, larger studies examining longitudinal C19OD and neurocognitive outcomes. While all participants did not complete their follow-up at exactly one year, we accounted for this in the analysis of time from baseline to follow-up assessment, showing that the mean time to follow-up was not statistically significant by OD status. Further, we control for the time from diagnosis to baseline assessment in the regression models and in the repeated-measures ANOVA. Paired with the mean score change per unit time analysis, it is unlikely that variation in time to follow-up significantly impacted the results reported in this manuscript. Further, it is important to note that participants complete the same neurocognitive assessments at baseline and follow-up testing. While we do not expect significant learning to occur with a full year between testing time points, participants may be better primed for the neurocognitive assessments at follow-up testing, potentially exaggerating apparent recovery. Additionally, we performed multiple statistical tests in this exploratory study, without the use of correction for multiple tests. Lastly, it is important to recognize that while the extended Sniffin’ Sticks evaluation and resultant TDI score is one way to assess a patient’s quantitative sense of smell, it does not always reflect their lived experience50.

Conclusions

In this exploratory longitudinal cohort study, individuals afflicted with persistent C19OD were found to display signs of impaired cognitive functioning during the early phase of long-COVID compared to normosmic counterparts, and these relationships persisted when controlling for covariates of age, sex, education status, and time between COVID-19 diagnosis and baseline assessment. During the interval to follow-up assessment, there is amelioration of these cognitive deficits despite persistence of OD. This research indicates that while poor performance across several neurocognitive domains is an important feature of long-COVID, particularly accentuated in those with C19OD, subsequent longitudinal evaluation demonstrates cognitive resolution. The variability in neurocognitive performance, the persistence of OD in the long-COVID population, and the limitations of this exploratory study suggests a need for ongoing evaluation of this population.
