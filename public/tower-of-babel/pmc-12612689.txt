Body Mass Index and Hypoglossal Nerve Stimulation Outcomes: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Coleton Evans, Benjamin Winters, Sandeep Rao, Sean Kelso, Peter Wickwire
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThis systematic review and meta‐analysis evaluated how body mass index (BMI) influences surgical outcomes of hypoglossal nerve stimulation (HGNS) for obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), specifically comparing patients with BMI ≥ 32 kg/m2 to those with BMI < 32 kg/m2.Data SourcesPubMed, Embase, and Scopus were searched from January 2014 to April 2025 for prospective and retrospective cohort studies or case–control studies reporting HGNS outcomes stratified by BMI.Review MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines, data were extracted on study design, demographics, baseline apnea–hypopnea index (AHI), and postoperative outcomes. Surgical success was defined using Sher or modified Sher criteria. Random‐effects meta‐analysis was performed using the DerSimonian and Laird method. Fixed‐effect inverse‐variance weights were first calculated, and between‐study heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran's Q statistic and expressed as the I 2 statistic. Study quality was assessed with the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale.ResultsSeven studies (n = 1572) were included in the qualitative synthesis; four were included in the meta‐analysis. Qualitative findings were mixed, with some studies reporting poorer outcomes at higher BMI and others showing no difference. The pooled odds ratio for treatment success in the BMI ≥ 32 kg/m2 group versus < 32 kg/m2 was 0.87 (95% CI: 0.68–1.11). Between‐study heterogeneity was low (Q = 4.11, df = 3, p = 0.25; I 2 = 27%), indicating relatively consistent effect estimates across studies.ConclusionElevated BMI was associated with a nonsignificant trend toward lower HGNS surgical success, but outcomes were broadly consistent across studies. Many patients with BMI ≥ 32 kg/m2 achieved meaningful objective and subjective improvements, challenging rigid BMI‐based eligibility criteria and supporting individualized candidacy assessment.Keywords: apnea–hypopnea index, body mass index, hypoglossal nerve stimulation, obstructive sleep apnea

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a common abnormal breathing disorder defined by recurrent episodes of upper airway obstruction during sleep, leading to intermittent hypoxemia and arousals. Affecting an estimated 9%–38% of adults, OSA's substantial burden includes impaired quality of life and excessive daytime sleepiness [1]. It is also strongly associated with serious cardiovascular and metabolic consequences, including hypertension, arrhythmias, heart failure, stroke, and diabetes [2]. Epidemiologic data indicate that OSA can increase the risk of heart failure by over 140%, stroke by ~60%, coronary heart disease by ~30% [3]. Untreated OSA contributes to higher mortality and significant public health costs, with undiagnosed cases in the US costing ~$150 billion annually from healthcare utilization, lost productivity, and accidents [4, 5].

Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy is the first‐line treatment for OSA, demonstrated to improve daytime alertness, cognitive function, mood, cardiovascular outcomes [6, 7]. Many individuals, however, find chronic CPAP therapy uncomfortable or intolerable, leading to suboptimal usage. Approximately one‐half of patients discontinue CPAP within the first year of therapy and long‐term compliance rates are around 34% [8]. Limitations of traditional therapies highlight a treatment gap, motivating the search for novel therapies.

Hypoglossal nerve stimulation (HGNS) has emerged as an innovative surgical therapy for moderate‐to‐severe OSA, specifically targeting CPAP‐intolerant patients. HGNS involves implanting a neurostimulator that delivers timed electrical pulses to the hypoglossal nerve, contracting tongue musculature to maintain upper airway patency during sleep. The pivotal stimulation therapy for apnea reduction (STAR) trial reported a ~68% reduction in the apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) after HGNS implantation, alongside improved oxygenation and sleep quality [9]. The ADHERE registry and other cohort studies similarly observed AHI reductions of 70%–80% [10]. Patient adherence to HGNS is generally high and quality‐of‐life metrics often show significant improvement, making it a compelling option for those who fail CPAP therapy [11, 12].

Despite the promise of HGNS, not all patients experience equal benefit, and body habitus has been identified as a key factor influencing outcomes. Obesity is a well‐established risk factor for OSA severity, and increased patient BMI has been associated with a higher probability of palatal collapse [13, 14]. The STAR trial's BMI cutoff of ≤ 32 kg/m2 reflected concerns about efficacy in patients with higher BMI, although guidelines have been cautiously expanded by Medicare (35 kg/m2) and the US FDA (40 kg/m2).

To address the need to clarify BMI's influence on HGNS postsurgical outcomes, this systematic review and meta‐analysis synthesizes current evidence on HGNS efficacy and therapeutic success across BMI categories. Focusing on the 32 kg/m2 threshold from the STAR trial, this review aims to determine if elevated BMI is associated with reduced HGNS responsiveness.

Methods

This systematic review followed PRISMA guidelines to evaluate whether HGNS surgical outcomes differ in patients with OSA based on a BMI threshold of 32 kg/m2 [15]. This study aimed to determine if patients with BMI ≥ 32 kg/m2 experience less optimal surgical outcomes compared to those with BMI < 32 kg/m2.

A comprehensive search of PubMed, Embase, and Scopus (January 1, 2014, to April 23, 2025) was conducted on April 23, 2025. The search strategy, developed with a medical librarian and executed through two independent reviews, used controlled vocabulary and free‐text keywords combining variations of “body mass index” and “hypoglossal nerve stimulation” or “upper airway stimulation”.

Studies were included if they reported on adult patients (≥ 18 years) undergoing HGNS for OSA and presented outcomes stratified by BMI, specifically including patients with BMI ≥ 32 kg/m2. Eligible designs included prospective and retrospective cohort studies and case–control studies. Studies were excluded if they lacked full‐text availability such as abstracts from conference proceedings, did not report BMI‐specific outcomes, involved pediatric populations, or assessed non‐HGNS interventions.

Data on study design, demographics, baseline AHI, and postoperative AHI were extracted using a standardized template to ensure consistency. Emphasis was placed on outcome stratification by BMI category (≤ 32 vs. > 32 kg/m2). Surgical success was defined using the Sher criteria (postoperative AHI < 20 events/h and a ≥ 50% reduction in baseline AHI) or a modified Sher criteria (postoperative AHI < 15 events/h and a ≥ 50% reduction in baseline AHI).

The methodological quality of included studies was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale for nonrandomized studies [16]. Three reviewers independently evaluated each study; discrepancies were resolved by consensus. The overall quality of the seven included studies was high, with scores ranging from seven to nine out of a possible nine stars. Most studies demonstrated strong methodology in patient selection and outcome assessment. Minor limitations were noted in the comparability domain for some studies, primarily related to a lack of adjustment for potential confounders beyond baseline AHI. No studies were excluded based on their quality scores.

A random‐effects meta‐analysis using the DerSimonian and Laird method was performed to estimate a pooled odds ratio (OR) for treatment success in patients with a BMI ≥ 32 kg/m2 compared to those with a BMI of < 32 kg/m2 [17]. For each study, ORs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were converted to log ORs and variances; when only CIs were available, variance was derived from the width of the interval. Fixed‐effect inverse‐variance weights were calculated, and between‐study heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran's Q statistic and expressed as the I 2 statistic. The between‐study variance was computed and incorporated into random‐effects weights. A pooled log OR was obtained as a weighted average and exponentiated to yield the final OR and 95% CI. All analyses were performed in Python using NumPy, pandas, and matplotlib.

Results

The initial search yielded 1031 records. After removing 266 duplicates, 765 unique studies remained for screening. Twenty full‐text articles were reviewed for eligibility, of which 13 were excluded (11 abstracts only, 2 without outcomes for BMI ≥ 32 kg/m2). Ultimately, seven studies met all inclusion criteria and were included in the final qualitative synthesis (Figure 1) [18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24].

PRISMA study flow diagram.

The seven included studies, published between 2018 and 2024, comprised 1572 patients who underwent HGNS. Six were retrospective cohort studies; one was a prospective analysis from the ADHERE registry [24]. The settings for five of the included studies were at a single institution, with Sarber et al. notably conducted by a single surgeon. Huntley et al. used the patient populations from two tertiary care academic hospitals, and Suurna et al. was conducted utilizing the ADHERE registry. Study sample sizes ranged from 14 to 1019 participants. Most cohorts were predominantly male, with representation ranging from 67.2% to 94.4%. Mean patient age across studies ranged from 52.9 to 63 years and most studies reported a mean BMI between 28 and 30 kg/m2. Preoperative AHI values were generally in the moderate to severe OSA range, with means between 25 and 44 events/h (Table 1).

Boroosan et al. included 40 patients, but the number with BMI ≥ 32 kg/m2 was not directly reported. However, the upper BMI quartile boundary was 31.6 kg/m2, suggesting that approximately 8–10 participants may have exceeded the threshold. Corr et al. reported three patients with BMI ≥ 32 kg/m2 out of 14 total. Huntley et al. included 153 participants, 40 of whom had an elevated BMI. Patel et al. noted that 12 of 76 patients (16%) fell in the BMI 32–35 kg/m2 range. Renslo et al. stratified 253 patients into BMI‐based cohorts: 33 (13%) with a BMI range of 18.5–25 kg/m2 (BMI18), 174 (68.8%) within the 25–32 kg/m2 range (BMI25), and 46 (18.2%) with a BMI above 32 kg/m2 (BMI32). Sarber et al. included 18 patients, 12 of whom had BMI > 32 kg/m2. Suurna et al.'s BMI subanalysis cohort consisted of 535 patients, with 97 having BMI 32–35 kg/m2 (Table 1).

While not all the studies explicitly stated their inclusion criteria for specific demographics of OSA, Corr et al., Patel et al., and Surrna et al. specified that eligible patients had a diagnosis of moderate to severe OSA (AHI 15–65 events/h). Patel et al. and Corr et al. were the only studies that incorporated an exclusion criterion of BMI > 35 kg/m2. Notably, Sarber et al. identified findings in patients who underwent HGNS but did not meet all FDA guidelines. Patients were included if they did not meet one or more of the FDA criteria for implantation: an AHI > 15 or ≤ 65 events/h, BMI < 32 kg/m2, CAI ≤ 25% of the total AHI, and lack of complete concentric collapse on DISE.

Surgical success was measured with either the Sher criteria or a modified version. The original Sher criteria define surgical success as achieving a final AHI of fewer than 20 events/h and a reduction in the baseline AHI by greater than or equal to 50%. Patel et al. and Renslo et al. were the only studies to incorporate a modified Sher criteria to measure HGNS success. The modified criteria are defined by achieving a final AHI of fewer than 15 events/h while maintaining the ≥ 50% reduction from baseline.

Postoperative AHI improvements and HGNS surgical success were consistently observed across studies, though the degree of response varied with BMI. Most studies showed a decrease in AHI from the moderate–severe range to mild levels, with surgical success rates ranging from approximately 67%–95%, depending on study design and success definition (Table 2).

Study results.

Several studies analyzed outcomes in relation to elevated BMI (≥ 32 kg/m2). Boroosan et al. reported that while patients with BMI > 30 did not have statistically worse outcomes, those above 32 kg/m2 had significantly increased risk of treatment failure (p = 0.041). Corr et al. similarly found that no patients with BMI > 32 kg/m2 achieved an AHI below 20 events/h and reported a significant negative correlation between BMI and AHI reduction (r = 0.67, p = 0.018). Huntley et al. reported high success rates in both BMI subgroups with no significant difference between groups (p = 0.345). Patel et al. found that BMI 32–35 kg/m2 was associated with significantly lower odds of treatment success (OR = 0.25, 95% CI [0.07–0.90]), a finding echoed when using preimplantation BMI. When analyzed as a continuous variable, BMI was associated with a decreased likelihood of responding to HGNS, with each 1‐unit increase in BMI corresponding to an estimated 17% reduction in the odds of treatment response (OR = 0.83; 95% CI [0.68–1.01]; data not shown).

Renslo et al.'s analysis stratified BMI into three groups and found no significant differences in success between groups overall. However, those with BMI > 35 kg/m2 had significantly worse outcomes, with only one of six meeting success criteria. Renslo et al. also analyzed BMI at multiple time points throughout the treatment process and found that BMI measured at the time of the second postoperative sleep study was the only time point significantly associated with treatment success and a greater reduction in AHI (R = 0.25, p = 0.0052). Sarber et al. included a high proportion of participants with BMI > 32 kg/m2 and reported substantial improvements in AHI, with a surgical success rate of 83.3% and 66.7% of patients achieving complete control of OSA (AHI < 5). Suurna et al.'s large cohort showed noninferior outcomes between BMI < 32 and 32–35 kg/m2 groups, though the higher BMI group had a lower success rate (59.8% vs. 72.2%, p = 0.02) and higher adverse event rates. Suurna et al. also assessed postoperative therapy adherence and found a significant difference between BMI cohorts. Patients with a BMI < 32 kg/m2 used therapy for a mean of 5.8 h per day, while those in the elevated BMI group averaged 5.2 h per day (p = 0.028).

Four studies were included in the meta‐analysis, selected from seven eligible papers based on the criterion of having a study population greater than 50 patients. Study‐level ORs ranged from 0.26 to 1.04. Suurna et al. reported an OR of 0.83 (95% CI: 0.58–1.18) and contributed the greatest weight (30.30%) to the analysis. Huntley et al. and Renslo et al. reported ORs of 0.97 (95% CI: 0.58–1.63) and 1.04 (95% CI: 0.63–1.71), respectively. Patel et al., the only study to report a statistically significant result, found an OR of 0.26 (95% CI: 0.07–0.92), suggesting decreased treatment success in patients with BMI > 32 kg/m2, though it contributed the smallest weight (2.36%) due to its limited sample size.

Using the DerSimonian and Laird random‐effects model, the pooled OR for treatment success associated with BMI > 32 kg/m2 was 0.87 (95% CI: 0.68–1.11) [25]. While this reflects a trend toward reduced odds of success in the higher BMI group, the finding did not reach statistical significance, as the CI includes one. Between‐study heterogeneity was low (Q = 4.11, df = 3, p = 0.25; I 2 = 27%), indicating that most of the variability in effect sizes is likely due to sampling error rather than true differences between studies. This suggests the available data, though limited, are relatively consistent in the direction and magnitude of effect (Figure 2).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta‐analysis evaluated HGNS outcomes in patients with elevated BMI across seven studies. To our knowledge, this is the first meta‐analysis specifically examining the HGNS outcomes in relation to the BMI cutoff of 32 kg/m2 used by many US insurers to determine patient eligibility. Qualitative synthesis revealed mixed findings: several studies reported worse outcomes or higher failure rates among patients with BMI > 32 kg/m2, while others found comparable results. The pooled meta‐analysis indicated a trend toward reduced odds of success for higher‐BMI patients (pooled OR = 0.87), though not statistically significant. Between‐study heterogeneity was low (I 2 = 27%), suggesting variation largely due to chance rather than population or methodology differences. Overall, higher BMI may modestly reduce HGNS efficacy in some contexts, but BMI > 32 kg/m2 is not uniformly predictive of failure.

Notably, not all studies used the same surgical success criteria. Patel et al. and Renslo et al. applied a stricter definition: postoperative AHI to be below 15 events/h versus < 20 in other studies. This had minimal impact: in Renslo et al., outcomes did not differ significantly by BMI group, though BMI > 35 kg/m2 showed markedly poorer results (mean AHI 30 events/h). In Patel et al., the looser threshold would have reclassified only one additional patient in the BMI 32–35 group as a responder. Thus, stricter criteria did not meaningfully change interpretations.

The Sher criteria is commonly used to define surgical success in the treatment of OSA. Numerous studies have found an association between BMI and a higher AHI, with one demonstrating a 6.2% increase in AHI for every 1‐point increase in BMI [26, 27, 28]. While the first component—a 50% reduction in baseline AHI—applies across AHI ranges, the second component—postoperative AHI of < 20—may be less suitable for severe OSA, especially in high‐BMI patients, making it harder to classify them as “successes” despite substantial improvement.

Many of the “nonresponders” demonstrated noninferior improvements in subjective measures, a potential reflection of clinical benefits. Suurna et al. found that among patients with BMI > 32 kg/m2, 86.3% were rated as improved on the Clinical Global Impression Index (clinicians' assessments of patient progress), versus a 59.8% success rate by Sher criteria. Similarly, satisfaction rates demonstrated that 96% of patients in the elevated BMI group were satisfied with their therapy response. Sarber et al. reported surgical success rates up to 91.7% in “off‐label” high‐BMI patients.

These findings underscore the importance of incorporating subjective outcome measures alongside traditional success criteria, a point further supported by studies that assessed changes in daytime sleepiness using the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS). This patient questionnaire was administered in Huntley et al., Renslo et al., Sarber et al., and Suurna et al. Importantly, the change in ESS scores did not differ significantly between patients with BMI > 32 kg/m2 and those with BMI < 32 kg/m2. Together, these subjective outcomes add nuance to our understanding of treatment response and highlight the importance of considering more than just AHI when selecting candidates for HGNS.

Weight loss is a cornerstone of OSA management in patients with elevated BMI. As with most conditions, conservative measures should be pursued before more invasive interventions. Because obesity is a reversible risk factor for OSA, evidence suggests that losing more than 10 kg can resolve OSA in over half of patients with mild disease while also improving cardiometabolic health [29]. Recently, glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs) have emerged as an effective pharmacologic tool for weight reduction, producing clinically meaningful decreases in body weight and BMI and lowering many cardiovascular risk factors linked to OSA [30]. These agents have also been associated with improvements in sleep‐disordered breathing, with some studies reporting a mean reduction in AHI of approximately 15 events/h compared with placebo [31]. This evolving landscape supports a multimodal approach to OSA treatment, whether to bring a patient's BMI within insurance thresholds for surgical candidacy or to reduce perioperative risk, where pharmacologic weight loss strategies such as GLP‐1 RAs can be integrated with HGNS to enhance outcomes and quality of life.

CPAP remains the gold standard for OSA treatment, with well‐documented benefits including reductions in disease severity, daytime sleepiness, blood pressure, motor vehicle accidents, and improvements in quality of life [32]. However, concerns have been raised about its association with weight gain. Evidence suggests that CPAP therapy may lead to increases in BMI and body weight over time, particularly, among patients with suboptimal adherence [33]. A recent meta‐analysis reported that BMI significantly increased following CPAP use, especially in those using the device for ≤ 5 h per night, whereas longer use appeared to mitigate this effect [34]. This is, particularly, relevant to the population pursuing HGNS, as they are often CPAP‐intolerant or nonadherent—precisely the group most vulnerable to CPAP‐associated weight gain. These findings raise important questions about BMI trends in OSA management and have implications for HGNS candidacy and long‐term outcomes.

This is, particularly, important in the context of current FDA and insurance guidelines, which still limit access to HGNS for many patients with elevated BMI. In addition to other patient selection criteria, the FDA recommendations for HGNS therapy are for patients to have a BMI less than 40 kg/m2, which was expanded in 2023. This demonstrates limited relevance because many US commercial insurances require a BMI < 32 kg/m2, although Medicare and European coverage extends to patients with BMI < 35 kg/m2 [35].

This study demonstrates that patients with a BMI > 32 kg/m2 can achieve surgical outcomes that are noninferior to those with a BMI < 32 kg/m2. This finding challenges the current insurance restrictions that limit access to HGNS based on BMI cutoffs. For patients who are unable to achieve adequate OSA control with CPAP or other therapies—often due to intolerance or nonadherence—these limitations may exclude a population that could otherwise benefit from HGNS. These results highlight the need for more individualized approaches to patient selection that prioritize clinical response and patient‐centered outcomes over rigid BMI thresholds.

This study analyzed the effects of BMI on surgical outcomes in HGNS; despite these insights, there are several limitations that warrant consideration. As mentioned above, the use of the threshold in the Sher criteria may pose a limitation for studies specifically evaluating HGNS outcomes in patients with elevated BMI. Most patients included across studies were male and of similar age (50–60s), which may limit generalizability to younger populations or to females, who are underrepresented in existing HGNS studies. Additionally, the proportion of patients with BMI ≥ 32 kg/m2 was relatively small in several studies, and few performed dedicated subgroup analysis, which limits the strength of conclusions about elevated BMI populations. Furthermore, only four studies met criteria for inclusion in the meta‐analysis, which may limit statistical power.

Future studies should examine other factors that may moderate the effect of BMI on HGNS success, such as fat distribution, upper airway anatomy, or comorbidities like diabetes or heart disease. Additionally, studies that assess clinical outcomes and quality of life measures in patients with elevated BMI who have received HGNS may be relevant to help physicians decide appropriate candidates for therapy. Finally, longitudinal studies assessing long‐term adherence and sustainability of treatment benefit in patients with elevated BMI could offer insights into optimizing patient selection and postoperative support strategies.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta‐analysis found a non‐significant trend toward lower odds of achieving surgical success in patients with BMI exceeding 32 kg/m2. However, many patients demonstrated improvement across objective and subjective measures, suggesting that HGNS may offer valuable clinical outcomes for appropriately selected patients with elevated BMI. These findings challenge the rigid application of BMI‐based restrictions, supporting an individualized approach to HGNS candidacy. Further research is warranted to explore long‐term outcomes and the role of additional patient characteristics in optimizing treatment selection.
