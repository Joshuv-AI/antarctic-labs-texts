I
Introduction

Gravitational waves (GWs) carry energy and momentum and, once averaged over scales long compared with their wavelength, act as an effective source for the background geometry. In general relativity this idea is captured by the Isaacson high-frequency expansion, in which the quadratic part of the metric perturbation is interpreted as an effective stress tensor
[
4
,
5
]
. The same separation between rapidly varying wave degrees of freedom and slowly varying background fields is useful in modified gravity, but the averaging problem becomes more subtle because the gravitational action can contain additional propagating degrees of freedom and time-dependent effective gravitational couplings
[
9
]
.

The class of
f(R,G)
theories is particularly interesting because it combines the Ricci scalar with the four-dimensional Gauss–Bonnet invariant. For a generic nonlinear function of
R
and
G

f(R)
gravity, and perturbative stability can depend sensitively on the background and on the rank of the Hessian of
f

[
2
,
1
]

f(R,G)
gravity is not appropriate without specifying a dynamically controlled subclass.

II
The model and its degenerate sector

We begin from

S=\frac{M_{\rm Pl}^{2}}{2}\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\,f(R,G).

(II.1)

For the locked family (
I.1
), define

\phi\equiv F_{X}.

(II.2)

Assuming local invertibility of
F_{X}(X)
, let
X=X(\phi)
and define the Legendre potential

V(\phi)=\phi X(\phi)-F[X(\phi)].

(II.3)

The action becomes

S=\frac{M_{\rm Pl}^{2}}{2}\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\left[\phi R+\frac{\phi}{M^{2}}G-V(\phi)\right].

(II.4)

Variation with respect to
\phi
gives the constraint

R+\frac{G}{M^{2}}-V_{,\phi}=0.

(II.5)

The full metric field equations obtained by varying (
II.4
) are given in Appendix
A
.

The degeneracy is structural rather than accidental. Since

f_{R}=\phi,\qquad f_{G}=\frac{\phi}{M^{2}},

(II.6)

there is only one independent auxiliary scalar. This avoids introducing a spurious second scalar in a rank-one Hessian system. The distinction between Hessian degeneracy and global ghost freedom is important:
\Delta_{f}=0
is a necessary structural property of the present reduction, not by itself a no-ghost theorem. Generic
f(R,G)
theories can possess ghost modes on anisotropic backgrounds, while the number of scalar modes changes on FLRW backgrounds
[
2
]
.

The Gauss–Bonnet coupling in (
II.4
) is a standard scalar–Gauss–Bonnet interaction. In four dimensions a constant coefficient multiplying
G
is topological
[
7
,
8
]
, but the present coefficient
\phi/M^{2}
is dynamical and therefore contributes to the field equations.

III
Horndeski mapping and background

III.1
Horndeski representation

We use the standard generalized Horndeski convention

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{2}

\displaystyle=G_{2}(\phi,X),

(III.1)

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{3}

\displaystyle=-G_{3}(\phi,X)\Box\phi,

(III.2)

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{4}

\displaystyle=G_{4}(\phi,X)R+G_{4X}\left[(\Box\phi)^{2}-(\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}\phi)^{2}\right],

(III.3)

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{5}

\displaystyle=G_{5}(\phi,X)G_{\mu\nu}\nabla^{\mu}\nabla^{\nu}\phi-\frac{1}{6}G_{5X}\left[(\Box\phi)^{3}-3\Box\phi(\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}\phi)^{2}+2(\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}\phi)^{3}\right],

(III.4)

with
X=-\nabla_{\mu}\phi\nabla^{\mu}\phi/2

[
6
,
3
]
.

The scalar–Gauss–Bonnet term is represented, up to a boundary term, by the logarithmic
G_{5}
function. With the normalization in (
II.4
),

G_{2}=-\frac{M_{\rm Pl}^{2}}{2}V(\phi),\quad G_{3}=0,\quad G_{4}=\frac{M_{\rm Pl}^{2}}{2}\phi,\quad G_{5}=-\frac{2M_{\rm Pl}^{2}}{M^{2}}\ln X.

(III.5)

This is the noncanonical Horndeski representation of the scalar–Gauss–Bonnet coupling; the logarithm is not an additional physical degree of freedom but a representation of the Gauss–Bonnet interaction within the generalized Horndeski basis.

No assumption that
G_{2}
contain a canonical kinetic term is required. The Horndeski formulas are formulated for arbitrary
G_{i}(\phi,X)
and therefore apply to (
III.5
) whenever the background is regular and the scalar gradient is timelike, so that the unitary-gauge scalar perturbation variable is well defined.

III.2
Background equations

For a spatially flat FLRW background,

ds^{2}=-dt^{2}+a^{2}(t)d{\bf x}^{2},\qquad H=\frac{\dot{a}}{a},

(III.6)

the scalar equation is

V_{,\phi}=12H^{2}+6\dot{H}+\frac{24H^{2}(H^{2}+\dot{H})}{M^{2}}.

(III.7)

The Friedmann equation can be written as

3H^{2}\phi=\frac{1}{2}V-3H\dot{\phi}-\frac{12H^{3}\dot{\phi}}{M^{2}}.

(III.8)

(M^{2}+4H^{2})\ddot{\phi}+(8H\dot{H}-HM^{2}-4H^{3})\dot{\phi}+2M^{2}\dot{H}\phi=0,

(III.9)

This equation follows from the spatial equation together with the
00
constraint and scalar equation; its full derivation is given in Appendix
A
.
A second background equation follows from the spatial Einstein equation and is not needed in its full form below; it provides the remaining consistency condition.

III.3
Horndeski coefficients

For the present model the tensor coefficients are

\mathcal{G}_{T}=M_{\rm Pl}^{2}\left(\phi+\frac{4H\dot{\phi}}{M^{2}}\right),\qquad\mathcal{F}_{T}=M_{\rm Pl}^{2}\left(\phi+\frac{4\ddot{\phi}}{M^{2}}\right),

(III.10)

and

c_{T}^{2}=\frac{\mathcal{F}_{T}}{\mathcal{G}_{T}}=\frac{\phi+4\ddot{\phi}/M^{2}}{\phi+4H\dot{\phi}/M^{2}}.

(III.11)

The remaining quantities entering the scalar constraints and the cubic action are

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=-\frac{2M_{\rm Pl}^{2}\dot{\phi}}{M^{2}},

(III.12)

\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle=M_{\rm Pl}^{2}\left(\phi+\frac{12H\dot{\phi}}{M^{2}}\right),

(III.13)

\displaystyle\Theta

\displaystyle=\frac{M_{\rm Pl}^{2}}{2M^{2}}D,

(III.14)

\displaystyle\Sigma

\displaystyle=-\frac{3M_{\rm Pl}^{2}H}{M^{2}}\left(HM^{2}\phi+M^{2}\dot{\phi}+8H^{2}\dot{\phi}\right),

(III.15)

where

D=2HM^{2}\phi+M^{2}\dot{\phi}+12H^{2}\dot{\phi}.

(III.16)

The scalar kinetic coefficient is

\mathcal{G}_{S}=\frac{3M_{\rm Pl}^{2}\dot{\phi}^{2}(M^{2}+4H^{2})^{2}(M^{2}\phi+4H\dot{\phi})}{M^{2}D^{2}}.

(III.17)

The derivation of (
III.17
) is given in Appendix
B
.

The scalar gradient coefficient is

\mathcal{F}_{S}=\frac{1}{a}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{a\mathcal{G}_{T}^{2}}{\Theta}\right)-\mathcal{F}_{T},\qquad c_{S}^{2}=\frac{\mathcal{F}_{S}}{\mathcal{G}_{S}}.

(III.18)

Stability of the cosmological perturbations requires, within this perturbative description,

\mathcal{G}_{T}>0,\qquad\mathcal{F}_{T}>0,\qquad\mathcal{G}_{S}>0,\qquad\mathcal{F}_{S}>0.

(III.19)

III.4
Exact rolling de Sitter solution

An exact analytic background useful for testing the scalar channel is

H=H_{0},\qquad\phi(t)=\phi_{0}+\Phi e^{H_{0}t}.

(III.20)

Then

\dot{\phi}=H_{0}(\phi-\phi_{0}),\qquad\ddot{\phi}=H_{0}\dot{\phi}.

(III.21)

The scalar equation integrates to

V(\phi)=\left(12H_{0}^{2}+\frac{24H_{0}^{4}}{M^{2}}\right)\phi+V_{0},

(III.22)

while the Friedmann equation fixes

V_{0}=-6H_{0}^{2}\phi_{0}-\frac{24H_{0}^{4}}{M^{2}}\phi_{0}.

(III.23)

The spatial equation is satisfied because

\ddot{\phi}-H_{0}\dot{\phi}=0
and

\ddot{\xi}-H_{0}\dot{\xi}=0
for
\xi=\phi/M^{2}
.

On this solution,

c_{T}^{2}=1.

(III.24)

Moreover, direct substitution into (
III.18
) gives

\mathcal{F}_{S}=\mathcal{G}_{S},\qquad c_{S}^{2}=1.

(III.25)

The result is a useful analytic control case rather than a claim that all cosmological solutions of the theory are luminal.

IV
Tensor sector and effective backreaction

IV.1
Quadratic action

The tensor perturbation is transverse and traceless,

\partial_{i}h_{ij}=0,\qquad h_{ii}=0.

(IV.1)

The quadratic action is

S_{T}^{(2)}=\frac{1}{8}\int dt\,d^{3}x\,a^{3}\left[\mathcal{G}_{T}\dot{h}_{ij}\dot{h}_{ij}-\frac{\mathcal{F}_{T}}{a^{2}}\partial_{k}h_{ij}\partial_{k}h_{ij}\right].

(IV.2)

The tensor equation is

\ddot{h}_{ij}+\left(3H+\frac{\dot{\mathcal{G}}_{T}}{\mathcal{G}_{T}}\right)\dot{h}_{ij}-c_{T}^{2}\frac{\nabla^{2}h_{ij}}{a^{2}}=0.

(IV.3)

In the WKB regime,

h_{ij}=A_{ij}(t)e^{i\theta/\epsilon},\qquad\epsilon\sim\frac{\lambda}{L}\ll 1,

(IV.4)

and the leading dispersion relation is

\omega^{2}=c_{T}^{2}\frac{k^{2}}{a^{2}}.

(IV.5)

The canonical amplitude scales as

A_{k}\propto\frac{1}{a\sqrt{\mathcal{G}_{T}c_{T}}}=\frac{1}{a(\mathcal{G}_{T}\mathcal{F}_{T})^{1/4}}.

(IV.6)

IV.2
Effective GW stress tensor

Averaging the quadratic tensor action over several wavelengths gives

\rho_{\rm GW}=\frac{M_{\rm Pl}^{2}}{4}\left(\phi+\frac{4H\dot{\phi}}{M^{2}}\right)\langle\dot{h}_{ij}\dot{h}_{ij}\rangle+O(H/\omega),

(IV.7)

and for an isotropic stochastic ensemble,

p_{\rm GW}=\frac{1}{3}\rho_{\rm GW}+O(H/\omega).

(IV.8)

The leading equation of state is therefore radiation-like even when
c_{T}\neq 1
. The modification appears instead in the normalization and transport of the energy density.

The averaged continuity equation is

\dot{\rho}_{\rm GW}+4H\rho_{\rm GW}=\frac{\dot{c}_{T}}{c_{T}}\rho_{\rm GW}+O(H/\omega).

(IV.9)

The right-hand side represents exchange with the slowly varying scalar–gravity sector. It is not a violation of the Bianchi identity.

IV.3
Bianchi interpretation

At second order the averaged field equations can be split schematically as

E_{\mu\nu}^{(2)}=-t_{\mu\nu}^{\rm GW}+T_{\mu\nu}^{\rm bg,(2)}.

(IV.10)

The separation is not unique; only the total averaged second-order equation is covariantly conserved. In the FLRW frame,

\nabla^{\mu}t_{\mu 0}^{\rm GW}=-\frac{\dot{c}_{T}}{c_{T}}\rho_{\rm GW}+O(H/\omega),

(IV.11)

and consequently

\nabla^{\mu}T_{\mu 0}^{\rm bg,(2)}=+\frac{\dot{c}_{T}}{c_{T}}\rho_{\rm GW}+O(H/\omega).

(IV.12)

This makes explicit the bookkeeping meaning of the apparent energy exchange.

IV.4
Quadratic curvature sources

For a pure TT high-frequency wave on FLRW, the leading scalar average of the Gauss–Bonnet invariant vanishes,

\langle G^{(2)}\rangle_{\rm HF}=0,

(IV.13)

whereas

\langle R^{(2)}\rangle_{\rm HF}=\frac{1}{4}\left(1-\frac{1}{c_{T}^{2}}\right)\langle\dot{h}_{ij}\dot{h}_{ij}\rangle+O(H/\omega).

(IV.14)

These statements concern the leading geometric-optics contribution; subleading background-curvature terms are not claimed to vanish identically.

V.2
Induced scalar source

The reduced scalar equation is

\frac{1}{a^{3}\mathcal{G}_{S}}\frac{d}{dt}\left(a^{3}\mathcal{G}_{S}\dot{\mathcal{R}}^{(2)}\right)-c_{S}^{2}\frac{\nabla^{2}\mathcal{R}^{(2)}}{a^{2}}=\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{R}}^{\rm GW}.

(V.12)

For an isotropic high-frequency TT ensemble, the leading source has the schematic but controlled form

\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{R}}^{\rm GW}=C_{1}(t)\langle\dot{h}_{ij}^{2}\rangle+C_{2}(t)\frac{\langle\partial_{k}h_{ij}\partial_{k}h_{ij}\rangle}{a^{2}}+O(H/\omega),

(V.13)

where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are determined by the reduced cubic action. On the rolling de Sitter solution,
c_{T}^{2}=1
, and the leading source reduces to

\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{R}}^{\rm GW}=\frac{1}{8}\langle\dot{h}_{ij}^{2}\rangle\left[1+O(H/\omega)\right].

(V.14)

The nonzero value of (
V.14

c_{T}^{2}=1
is one of the principal results of this work. The scalar channel is therefore not equivalent to the tensor-speed modification.

VI
Scalar backreaction

VI.1
Green function

For a Fourier mode
\mathcal{R}_{\bf k}
, define

{\cal D}_{\mathcal{R},k}=\frac{1}{a^{3}\mathcal{G}_{S}}\frac{d}{dt}\left(a^{3}\mathcal{G}_{S}\frac{d}{dt}\right)+c_{S}^{2}\frac{k^{2}}{a^{2}}.

(VI.1)

Let
u_{k}
and
v_{k}
be two independent homogeneous solutions. Their Wronskian satisfies

W_{k}(t)\equiv u_{k}\dot{v}_{k}-\dot{u}_{k}v_{k}=\frac{W_{k,0}}{a^{3}\mathcal{G}_{S}}.

(VI.2)

The retarded Green function is

G_{\mathcal{R}}(k;t,t^{\prime})=\Theta(t-t^{\prime})\frac{u_{k}(t^{\prime})v_{k}(t)-v_{k}(t^{\prime})u_{k}(t)}{W_{k}(t^{\prime})}.

(VI.3)

The derivation of the retarded Green function is given in Appendix
F
.

Consequently,

\mathcal{R}_{\bf k}^{(2)}(t)=\int^{t}dt^{\prime}\,G_{\mathcal{R}}(k;t,t^{\prime})\,S_{\mathcal{R}}({\bf k},t^{\prime}).

(VI.4)

VI.2
Tensor-tensor convolution

For a stochastic tensor field,

\mathcal{R}_{\bf k}^{(2)}=\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}K_{\mathcal{R}}({\bf k},{\bf q};t)h_{ij}({\bf q})h_{ij}({\bf k-q}),

(VI.5)

where

K_{\mathcal{R}}({\bf k},{\bf q};t)=\int^{t}dt^{\prime}\,G_{\mathcal{R}}(k;t,t^{\prime})K_{S}({\bf k},{\bf q};t^{\prime}).

(VI.6)

The scalar power spectrum is then of the schematic convolution form

P_{\mathcal{R}^{(2)}}(k)=\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}|K_{\mathcal{R}}({\bf k},{\bf q})|^{2}P_{h}(q)P_{h}(|{\bf k-q}|){\cal P}_{\rm pol}({\bf k},{\bf q}),

(VI.7)

with
{\cal P}_{\rm pol}
the polarization contraction.

VI.3
Sub-horizon and super-horizon limits

For a sub-horizon scalar mode,

\omega_{S}^{2}=c_{S}^{2}\frac{k^{2}}{a^{2}}\gg H^{2},

(VI.8)

the retarded response gives

\mathcal{R}_{\bf k}^{(2)}\sim\frac{{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{R}}^{\rm GW}}_{\mathbf{k}}}{\omega_{S}^{2}}\left[1+O(H/\omega_{S})\right].

(VI.9)

For a super-horizon mode,

\mathcal{R}^{(2)}(t)=C_{1}+C_{2}\int^{t}\frac{dt^{\prime}}{a^{3}\mathcal{G}_{S}}+\int^{t}\frac{dt^{\prime}}{a^{3}\mathcal{G}_{S}}\int^{t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime\prime}\,a^{3}\mathcal{G}_{S}\,\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{R}}^{\rm GW}(t^{\prime\prime}).

(VI.10)

The homogeneous constant mode is not a new energy density; the physically relevant induced background response is obtained from the sourced derivative and the second-order background constraint.

VI.4
Comparison with the direct tensor response

The direct tensor contribution to the Friedmann constraint scales as

\left|\frac{\delta H}{H}\right|_{\rm tensor}\sim\frac{\rho_{\rm GW}}{M_{\rm Pl}^{2}\phi H^{2}}\sim\frac{Q_{T}}{4\phi}\frac{\langle\dot{h}^{2}\rangle}{H^{2}},

(VI.11)

away from a constraint degeneracy.

The finite-wavenumber scalar response is instead determined directly from the sourced scalar equation. In the sub-horizon WKB regime,

\mathcal{R}_{\bf k}^{(2)}\sim\frac{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{R},\bf k}^{\rm GW}}{\omega_{S}^{2}}\left[1+O(H/\omega_{S})\right].

(VI.12)

This quantity is a finite-
k
perturbative response and is not, by itself, a homogeneous shift of the Hubble parameter. A quantity such as
\delta H_{\rm scalar}
can be defined only after specifying a zero-mode projection and an averaging prescription. We therefore do not assign a universal coefficient relating the scalar response to the direct homogeneous tensor backreaction.

The direct homogeneous tensor contribution remains well defined. From the background constraint,

\delta H_{T}^{(2)}=\frac{\rho_{\rm GW}}{M_{\rm Pl}^{2}F_{1,H}}=\frac{Q_{T}}{4F_{1,H}}\langle\dot{h}_{ij}\dot{h}_{ij}\rangle,

(VI.13)

where
F_{1,H}=\partial F_{1}/\partial H
is evaluated on the background. No universal scalar-to-tensor homogeneous ratio is claimed.

VII
Discussion and conclusions

We have studied gravitational-wave backreaction in the Hessian-degenerate sector

f(R,G)=F(R+G/M^{2})
. The central structural observation is that the rank-one Hessian permits a one-scalar Legendre representation,

S=\frac{M_{\rm Pl}^{2}}{2}\int\sqrt{-g}\left[\phi R+\phi G/M^{2}-V(\phi)\right],

which is equivalent to a scalar–Gauss–Bonnet Horndeski theory.

The tensor sector has modified kinetic and gradient coefficients. Consequently, the effective GW energy density is modified even when the leading pressure remains radiation-like. The corresponding continuity equation contains an exchange term controlled by the time variation of the tensor propagation speed.

The second key result is the existence of a genuine scalar channel sourced by the cubic scalar–tensor–tensor interaction. This source is not reducible to a measure of the tensor-speed deviation. The rolling de Sitter solution provides a particularly clean demonstration: both
c_{T}
and
c_{S}
equal unity, but the induced scalar source remains nonzero.

The scalar response is controlled by the inverse scalar WKB operator, giving
\mathcal{R}_{\bf k}^{(2)}\sim\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{R},\bf k}^{\rm GW}/\omega_{S}^{2}
at finite wavenumber. The direct tensor contribution, by contrast, enters the homogeneous background constraint through the averaged GW energy density. These are distinct response channels, and a universal ratio between them cannot be assigned without specifying how the finite-
k
scalar perturbation is projected onto the homogeneous sector.

The comparison with general relativity is instructive. In GR there is no additional Horndeski scalar channel of this type, while in ordinary scalar–tensor and scalar–Gauss–Bonnet theories scalar–tensor–tensor couplings are well known in the general perturbation formalism. The new point here is their organization and backreaction interpretation inside the specifically degenerate
f(R,G)
sector.

Several qualifications are essential. The condition
\Delta_{f}=0
does not establish global ghost freedom. Stability must be checked on the background through the kinetic and gradient coefficients. The analysis is also restricted to the high-frequency regime and does not constitute a full nonlinear evolution of the coupled tensor, scalar, and background sectors. Finally, the analytic rolling de Sitter solution is a control background, not a universal cosmological history.

VIII
Scope and limitations

The analysis is restricted to the Hessian-degenerate sector
f(R,G)=F(R+G/M^{2})
, for which the Legendre transformation reduces the theory to a single-scalar scalar–Gauss–Bonnet subclass of Horndeski gravity. The degeneracy condition
\Delta_{f}=0
should not be interpreted as a proof of global ghost freedom; stability is assessed through the tensor and scalar quadratic kinetic and gradient coefficients within the cosmological background considered. The Horndeski representation and the perturbative coefficients employed in the analysis assume a regular timelike scalar background for which unitary gauge is well defined and the standard Horndeski perturbation formalism applies. The gravitational-wave backreaction calculation is performed in the high-frequency/geometric-optics regime,
\omega/H\gg 1
, and retains the leading tensor contribution together with the leading induced scalar channel. The explicit analytic background test is based on a rolling de Sitter solution and therefore does not establish the same scalar-source coefficient for every possible cosmological history. The conversion of the finite-
k
scalar response into a homogeneous Hubble shift is prescription-dependent. No complete nonlinear evolution of the coupled gravitational-wave, scalar, and background system is performed. In particular, the behavior near
\mathcal{G}_{S}=0
,
\mathcal{F}_{S}=0
, or other strong-coupling/degenerate surfaces requires a separate analysis. Extending the calculation to general time-dependent backgrounds, solving the full stochastic convolution without the high-frequency approximation, and confronting the resulting backreaction with observationally relevant gravitational-wave spectra remain open problems.
