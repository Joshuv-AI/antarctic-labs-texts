Incidence and Risk Factors for Persistent Perineal Sinus After Proctectomy in Crohn Disease
Authors: Wout De Coster, André J.L. D’Hoore, Kim Vandeuren, Leen Van Langenhoven, Steffen Fieuws, Albert Wolthuis, Gabriele Bislenghi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:This retrospective cohort study aimed to determine the incidence of persistent perineal sinus (PPS) after proctectomy for Crohn disease (CD) and to identify associated risk factors.Summary Background Data:Proctectomy remains necessary in a subset of patients with CD, yet impaired perineal wound healing and PPS continue to represent major sources of postoperative morbidity.Methods:All patients undergoing proctectomy for CD between 1993 and 2023 at a tertiary referral center were included. The primary outcome was development of PPS, defined as a perineal wound remaining unhealed for ≥6 months after proctectomy. Associated risk factors were analyzed using regression analyses.Results:Among 139 patients, 66.20% were women, and PPS occurred in 26. Healing was 81.30% (95% CI, 74.40%–87.30%) at 6 months and 93.50% (95% CI, 88.60%–96.80%) at 12 months; median healing time was 1.80 months (IQR, 0.90–4.60).Patients operated after 2011 (n=113, 81.30%) were significantly older, more ASA III, and active smokers, with longer disease duration, lower CRP, and higher number of advanced therapies.PPS was associated with younger age (OR 0.79; P=0.014) and omentoplasty (OR 2.60; P=0.036). After correction for year of surgery, only older age independently predicted faster healing (OR 0.90; 95% CI, 0.83–0.97; P=0.005), while ≥3 therapy lines showed a nonsignificant protective effect (OR 0.36; 95% CI, 0.12–1.07; P=0.067).Conclusion:Although PPS remains frequent after proctectomy for Crohn disease, most patients ultimately achieve perineal healing. Older age at surgery is the only independent predictor of faster healing. Improved control of systemic inflammation may reduce the risk of PPS.

Proctectomy in patients with Crohn disease (CD) is most commonly indicated for refractory proctitis and severe perianal disease (pCD). Despite the expanding armamentarium of medical therapies, the need for proctectomy in this selected patient population has not substantially declined, with reported rates remaining stable at ∼12%–20%.1,2 Moreover, long-term success in restoring intestinal continuity after diversion in CD–particularly in patients with pCD–remains limited, with durable reversal achieved in only a minority of cases.3

Proctectomy for CD is associated with considerable postoperative morbidity, reflecting both the technical complexity of the procedure and the underlying disease biology. Complications range from minor events such as urinary tract infections to severe pelvic sepsis.4,5 While the widespread adoption of minimally invasive techniques has reduced overall morbidity and has accelerated postoperative recovery,6,7 perineal wound healing remains a major unresolved challenge and frequently represents the principal determinant of delayed recovery following proctectomy.

Perineal wound dehiscence may progress to the formation of a persistent perineal sinus (PPS), reported in ∼25%–56% of patients after proctectomy for CD.8 PPS is typically defined as a perineal wound that remains unhealed for ≥6 months postoperatively and is commonly associated with chronic discharge, pain, and bleeding, leading to a substantial deterioration in quality of life. In addition, PPS is difficult to manage, often requiring prolonged wound care, repeated surgical or interventional procedures, and extended follow-up, thereby imposing a significant burden on health care resources.

Impaired wound healing is a well-recognized feature of CD, driven by chronic inflammation, nutritional impairment, and local septic conditions. In the setting of proctectomy, these factors may converge to increase the risk of perineal wound failure and PPS formation. Nevertheless, the true incidence of PPS and its determinants remain incompletely characterized. Only a limited number of studies have specifically addressed this complication. Reported risk factors–including patient-, disease-, and surgery-related characteristics such as sex, hypoalbuminemia, active perineal sepsis, fecal diversion, and fecal contamination–have shown substantial heterogeneity and inconsistent associations across studies.8–10 Importantly, it remains unclear which of these factors are potentially modifiable, limiting opportunities for targeted preoperative optimization.

Therefore, this study aims to quantify the incidence of PPS and healing time after proctectomy for CD and to identify associated risk factors.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design and Patient Selection

This retrospective observational cohort study included patients with CD who underwent proctectomy between 1993 and 2023 at the University Hospitals Leuven, Belgium, a tertiary referral center for inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). Patients without a confirmed diagnosis of CD were excluded. No minimum postoperative follow-up duration was required.

Demographic, clinical, and surgical data were collected through structured review of electronic health records. Indications for proctectomy were: pCD (involvement of the perianal region, including abscesses, fistulae, sinuses), proctocolitis (endoscopically confirmed), dysplasia or malignancy (histologically confirmed), and other indications.

All procedures were performed by 5 colorectal surgeons (ADH, AW, and GB) certified by the European Board of Surgical Qualification (EBSQ). The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University Hospitals Leuven (S69878).

Outcomes and Definitions

Diagnosis of CD was based on clinical, endoscopic, radiologic, and histologic findings.

The primary outcome was the development of PPS, defined as a perineal wound remaining unhealed for ≥6 months after proctectomy, based on clinical assessment by the treating surgeon. No standardized definition of healing was used and no routine cross-sectional imaging was performed to confirm clinical healing.

Postoperative follow-up was scheduled according to routine clinical practice, with an initial outpatient visit 30 days after hospital discharge and subsequent visits adapted according to clinical findings and ongoing IBD-related care. In cases of impaired perineal wound healing, management typically consisted of wound drainage and packing with intensified follow-up visits.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics, procedural details, and outcomes were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were reported as number of observations, mean and standard deviation or median with interquartile range (Q1–Q3) in case of serious deviations from the normal distribution. Distributions have been checked visually with histograms. Categorical variables were reported with frequencies and percentages.

Time to complete perineal wound healing was visualized using the cumulative distribution function. Potential risk factors for PPS were evaluated for each factor separately, using a univariable logistic regression model, correcting for the year of the surgery. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) from these models were reported. Linearity assumptions for continuous variables were assessed using restricted cubic splines. Due to the limited number of events, no multivariable logistic models were considered (beyond the correction for year of surgery). Time to perineal wound healing (in months) was further analyzed using linear regression. As time to healing approximately followed a log-normal distribution, logarithmic transformation was applied before analysis. Back-transformed (exponentiated) estimates from these models are to be interpreted as ratios, that is, multiplicative effects on the time to healing. Firstly, for each risk factor separately, a model was fitted correcting for the effect of year of surgery. Secondly, a multivariable model was constructed using a predefined set of 6 clinically relevant variables (≥3 lines of biological therapy, the logarithm of C-reactive protein level, diversion before surgery, perineal disease as indication, close rectal dissection, and omentoplasty) and year of surgery. In addition, variables significant in the per-factor analysis (age at surgery and year of surgery) were added into the multivariable model.

Given the presence of missing values for some considered risk factors, all models were fitted using a multiple imputation procedure. Multivariate imputation was performed using the fully conditional specification (FCS) approach,11 using all risk factors and the time to healing in the imputation. Results obtained on 50 imputed datasets were combined using Rubin’s rule.

A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. The data were analyzed using SAS software (version 9.4). No adjustments for multiplicity were made, so results must be interpreted with care.

RESULTS

Patient and Disease Characteristics

Overall, 139 patients were included, of whom 92 (66.2%) were women. The mean age at surgery was 45.9 years (SD ±13.7), and the mean body mass index (BMI) was 23.3 (SD ±4.4). Thirty-eight patients (27.3%) were active smokers. The median preoperative C-reactive protein (CRP) level was 11.5 mg/L (IQR 3.7–26.3), and the median preoperative serum albumin level was 40.8 g/L (IQR 37.1–44.0, n=81). (Supplementary Table 1, Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/SLA/F934)

Extraintestinal manifestations were present in 59 patients (43.4%), and the majority (128 patients, 92.1%) had undergone previous CD-related surgery. Perineal disease was the indication for proctectomy in 52 patients (37.4%). The mean interval between diagnosis of CD and proctectomy was 21.6 years (SD ±10.9). A diverting ostomy before proctectomy was present in 52.5% of cases, with a median interval of 1.9 years (IQR 0.9–6.3) between intestinal diversion and proctectomy. Notably, 36.6% of patients had received ≥3 lines of advanced medical therapy before undergoing proctectomy. Infliximab was used in 82.0% of patients, adalimumab in 63.3%, vedolizumab in 33.1%, and ustekinumab in 25.9%.

Surgical Characteristics

Minimally invasive surgery was performed in 53 patients (38.7%). Ninety-three percent of proctectomies were intersphincteric and performed according to total mesorectal excision (TME) principles (73.9%). Omentoplasty was performed in 47 patients (34.3%), and myocutaneous flap closure in 6 patients (4.4%) (Supplementary Table 1, Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/SLA/F934). There was no difference in baseline characteristics between omentoplasty and no omentoplasty groups (Supplementary Table 2, Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/SLA/F934). Wounds were closed primarily in 97.1%, and pelvic drainage was placed in 86.1% at the end of the operation. An increasing number of proctectomies per year was observed over time (Fig. 1).

Subgroup analyses showed that patients operated after 2011 (n=113, 81.30%) tended to be older (46.8 vs. 41.8 y, P=0.090), more often classified as ASA III (37.5% vs. 16.7%, P=0.002) and more frequently active smokers (31% vs. 11.5%, P=0.09). They had a longer mean interval between diagnosis and surgery (22.8 vs. 16.7 y, P=0.010), a higher median number of advanced therapies (2.0 vs. 1.5, P<0.001) and lower median preoperative CRP levels (10.0 vs. 21.4 mg/L, P=0.003). After 2011 patients more frequently underwent surgery according to TME principles (78.7% vs. 53.8%, P=0.014) (Supplementary Table 3, Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/SLA/F971).

Persistent Perineal Sinus and Healing Time

The median follow-up was 29.7 months (IQR 10.3–78.1). PPS occurred in 26 patients. A formal evaluation of the trend of PPS over time was performed. Despite the decreasing trend, there was no evidence for a change in PPS rate over time (P>0.05) (Fig. 1).

The median time to complete perineal healing was 1.8 months (IQR 0.9–4.6). The estimated cumulative incidence for healing at 3, 6 and 12 months were 64.0% (95% CI, 56.1%–71.9%), 81.3% (95% CI, 74.4%–87.3%) and 93.5% (95% CI, 88.6%–96.8%), respectively (Fig. 2).

The management of nonhealing perineal wounds included initial treatment with sequential curettages (66.7%) and antibiotics (63.9%). Additional interventions included vacuum-assisted therapy (19.4%) and surgical closure with myocutaneous flaps (11.1%).

Risk Factors

Persistent Perineal Sinus

At univariable logistic regression analysis adjusted for year of surgery, older age at surgery was associated with a lower risk of PPS (OR 0.79; 95% CI, 0.66–0.96; P=0.014). Preoperative exposure to ≥3 lines of advanced therapy was also associated with a reduced risk of PPS, although this did not reach statistical significance (OR 0.36; 95% CI, 0.12–1.07; P=0.067). In contrast, omentoplasty was associated with a higher risk of PPS (OR 2.61; 95% CI, 1.07–6.38; P=0.036). No significant associations were observed for sex, CRP, surgical indication, preoperative albumin, or prior intestinal diversion. (Supplementary Table 4a, Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/SLA/F971, Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/SLA/F934).

Healing Time

At univariable linear regression analysis (corrected for year of surgery) on the log-transform of healing time in months, only older age at surgery (OR 0.903; 95% CI, 0.842–0.968; P=0.005) was significantly associated with faster healing. All other analyzed variables showed no association with time to healing (Supplementary Table 4b, Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/SLA/F971).

At multivariable linear regression analysis, the only independent predictive factor for faster healing was older age at surgery (OR 0.896; 95% CI, 0.831-0.967; P=0.005). Preoperative exposure to ≥3 advanced therapies did not reach statistical significance as an independent predictive risk factor (OR 0.692; 95% CI, 0.445–1.076; P=0.102) (Supplementary Table 4c, Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/SLA/F971).

DISCUSSION

In this retrospective cohort study including 139 patients undergoing proctectomy for CD, the incidence of PPS was 18.7%, with perineal wound healing achieved in 81.3% of patients at 6 months, increasing to 93.5% at 12 months. These healing rates are higher than those previously reported in the literature.12 Development of PPS was associated with younger age (OR 0.79; P=0.014) and omentoplasty (OR 2.60; P=0.036) on univariable analysis, while older age at surgery was the only independent factor associated with faster healing.

A recent meta-analysis including 4 retrospective cohort studies comprising 333 patients reported pooled complete perineal healing rates of 65% at 6 months and 70% at 12 months.12 Similarly, a large multicenter Swedish cohort by Berghög et al13 demonstrated healing rates of 72% and 79% at the same time points. The more favorable healing outcomes observed in the present study may be explained by differences in patient selection, particularly with respect to the prevalence and severity of pCD. Perianal CD has consistently been identified as a major risk factor for impaired perineal wound healing and PPS formation, with multicenter data showing significantly lower healing rates after proctectomy for fistulizing perianal disease compared with proctitis alone (OR 0.32).9,13 In contrast, the lower prevalence of pCD (37.5%) in the present cohort may have limited exposure to severe perineal sepsis, potentially contributing to the more favorable healing rates observed (Supplementary Fig. 2, Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/SLA/F934).

Given the long inclusion period, a subgroup analysis was performed according to era of surgery, using 2011 as a cutoff to reflect the broader availability of advanced therapies for CD. Patients operated after 2011 had significantly lower preoperative CRP levels (10.0 vs. 21.4 mg/L, P=0.003), suggesting improved systemic inflammatory control at the time of surgery. This likely reflects both increased use and cumulative exposure to advanced therapies (2.0 vs. 1.5, P<0.001), as well as a longer interval between diagnosis and surgery (22.8 vs. 16.7 y, P=0.010). At the same time, these patients appeared clinically more complex, as reflected by a higher proportion of ASA III patients and active smokers. The increased use of TME in the later era likely reflects evolving surgical practice, in line with emerging evidence supporting TME in proctectomy for CD.14

Preoperative exposure to ≥3 lines of advanced therapy was associated with a reduced risk of PPS, although this did not reach statistical significance (OR 0.36; 95% CI, 0.12–1.07; P=0.067). Together with the observed decreasing trend of PPS over time, these findings suggest that systemic inflammatory burden may be a more relevant determinant of perineal wound healing than local disease characteristics alone, as no association was observed between indication for surgery and PPS or healing time. These observations are consistent with those of a recent two-center study including 103 patients undergoing proctectomy for pCD, in which systemic inflammation emerged as a key driver of impaired healing.9 In this context, advanced medical therapies may exert a protective effect by improving systemic and luminal disease control, despite their theoretical negative impact on tissue repair. In the present study, the vast majority of patients (89.5%) had received advanced therapies, and those exposed to ≥3 lines of treatment demonstrated more favorable healing outcomes. This likely reflects improved control of chronic inflammation in patients with long-standing disease rather than a direct pharmacologic effect on wound healing.

The role of intestinal diversion before proctectomy remains controversial.3,15–18 In this cohort, the presence of a diverting ostomy was not associated with a reduced risk of PPS nor with improved healing time. This may be explained by the relatively short interval between diversion and proctectomy (median ∼22 mo). Previous studies have demonstrated that longer intervals between diversion and proctectomy are associated with improved perineal healing, with patients achieving wound closure at 6 months having significantly longer diversion periods.9 These findings suggest that diversion alone may be insufficient, and that prolonged control of local and systemic inflammation before definitive surgery may be required to optimize outcomes.

Younger age at surgery was associated with an increased risk of PPS and delayed healing, likely reflecting a more aggressive disease phenotype and a higher likelihood of complex surgical scenarios. Similar associations have been reported in previous multivariable analyses,19,20 suggesting that younger patients represent a subgroup at higher risk for impaired wound healing.

Regarding the surgical technique, omentoplasty was the only operative factor associated with an increased risk of PPS. Although counterintuitive, this finding is most likely explained by confounding by indication, as omentoplasty is typically performed in patients with unfavorable local conditions or more extensive pelvic dissection.21 Notably, subgroup analysis (Supplementary Table 2, Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/SLA/F934) did not demonstrate significant differences between patients undergoing omentoplasty and those who did not, although residual confounding cannot be excluded. A recent study including 219 patients similarly failed to demonstrate a benefit of omentoplasty, despite its use in higher-risk patients characterized by active smoking, pCD, and extrasphincteric dissection.22 Furthermore, recent evidence highlights the importance of flap viability; indocyanine green fluorescence imaging has shown that ischemic omental tissue is common and that resection of nonviable segments may reduce perineal wound complications.23 These findings suggest that nonviable tissue may exacerbate, rather than mitigate, perineal wound problems.

The impact of mesorectal inflammation and the extent of dissection on perineal healing remains debated. While a study including 54 patients reported higher healing rates at 6 months following close rectal dissection compared with TME (P=0.014),14 no significant difference between dissection techniques was observed in the present study.

This study benefits from its single-center design within a tertiary IBD referral center, ensuring consistent surgical expertise and multidisciplinary management. Inclusion of only histopathologically confirmed CD enhances cohort homogeneity. However, limitations include the retrospective design, missing data, absence of standardized follow-up and wound assessment, and the long inclusion period spanning more than 3 decades, during which surgical techniques, perioperative care, and medical management evolved. While reflecting real-world practice, these factors limit causal inference.

CONCLUSIONS

PPS remains a frequent complication after proctectomy for CD, although more than 90% of patients ultimately achieve perineal healing within 1 year. Older age at surgery is the only independent predictor of faster healing. Improved control of systemic inflammation may reduce the risk of PPS. Identification and optimization of modifiable factors, particularly systemic inflammatory burden, may provide opportunities to improve outcomes in this challenging patient population.
