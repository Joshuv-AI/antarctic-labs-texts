Future promise, current clinical ambiguity: a systematic review of machine learning algorithm outputs predicting risk of cardiovascular disease
Authors: Susan P Phillips, Han Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo examine whether the outputs of machine learning algorithms designed to predict risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) address known deficiencies of the Framingham Risk Score (FRS) and improve risk estimates.MethodsFor this critical review, Medline, Embase and IEEE were searched from inception to 1 January 2025. Included were studies describing machine learning algorithms designed to specifically compare output of cardiovascular risk assessment with the FRS. Commentaries, letters, unpublished work or non-peer-reviewed papers were excluded.Following Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, two reviewers screened titles and abstracts independently, then populated a purpose-built data extraction form. A subsequent qualitative thematic analysis focused on algorithms’ strengths, added value, potential harms, unintended consequences and equity implications.The main outcome assessed was whether, among healthy adults, the algorithm improved CVD risk prediction relative to the FRS.ResultsOf 707 studies retrieved, 29 met inclusion criteria. 23 reported improved predictive ability relative to the FRS. Most datasets and/or medical records used included sociodemographic predictors of CVD not included among FRS inputs. Some added costly diagnostic tests like CT angiography to FRS screening indicators. When they were defined, inputs and outcomes such as hypertension or myocardial infarction did not always adhere to FRS values. Statistical significance was generally taken as a proxy for clinical significance. Some algorithms overestimated the number at risk compared with the FRS without discussing whether that larger proportion might be at risk of overdiagnosis rather than CVD, while a few decreased the proportion found to be at risk.ConclusionsUse of artificial intelligence to improve accuracy of risk assessment for CVD demonstrates the technological capacity to merge known sociodemographic predictors with biologic variables and examine non-linear interactions among these. Still needed to achieve patient benefit is clinical insight, adherence to screening principles and cost–benefit assessment of inputs selected.

Introduction

Using an individual’s age, high-density lipoprotein, total cholesterol, systolic blood pressure, diabetes and smoking status the Framingham Risk Score (FRS) estimates the 10-year risk of developing cardiovascular disease (CVD). Precision of this widely accepted tool is known to vary across sociodemographic groups. Risk is often overestimated among white men but underestimated for women, socioeconomically deprived populations and racialised groups.1 2 Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) can analyse large datasets of diverse populations with varied individual and social traits. Applying ML to such datasets might, therefore, better identify antecedents of CVD and, particularly, non-linear interactions among these, yielding more accurate CVD risk prediction for all. This systematic review asks whether the potential of ML algorithms designed to increase accuracy of CVD risk assessment relative to the FRS is being met. It expands on the work of Liu et al’s systematic review of studies that used electronic health records as data sources and accepted, as outcome comparators, a range of risk scoring tools.3

CVD accounts for 32% of all deaths globally.4 Many known risk factors for heart disease can be identified with minimal diagnostic intervention or expense. Screening using the low tech, low cost FRS requires few resources and has been associated with an improvement in CVD outcomes.5 The FRS, however, has known limitations. It does not account for the impacts of interactions among risk factors, complexities such as whether there is a combined effect of hypertension and lipid levels that differs by race or sex.6 The primarily white population used to develop the FRS may underpin some of its shortcomings.7 Risk overestimates, often as great as 100%, are also well documented.8 Known strong predictors of CVD, such as lower socioeconomic status (SES), are not incorporated into FRS scoring.9 Are there ways to modify risk assessment and improve its predictive value?

ML, a form of AI, enables appraisal of complex, non-linear interactions among the many data points available in large datasets. The promise of ML’s data-driven computation lies in its ability to include a wide variety of known and unknown risk factors yet produce a parsimonious output of minimal complexity and high predictability.10 ML algorithms are constrained, nevertheless, by limits in the breadth, diversity and accuracy of information in datasets used. Vague or inconsistent definitions of variables such as hypertension, misclassification of categories (who is white, who is Black), missing data (if a medical record does not document smoking can non-smoker be assumed), even spelling errors in data may all skew output.

The aim of this critical review is to identify and appraise outputs of ML algorithms designed to increase accuracy of CVD risk assessments relative to the FRS. The review specifically examines whether these outputs address and correct for known sociodemographic biases or gaps inherent in the FRS tool.11

Methods

This review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines12 and follows the previously published protocol.11

Search strategy

A university Health Sciences librarian with expertise in systematic review searches assisted in developing the search strategy. Searches were conducted across three electronic databases: MEDLINE, EMBASE and IEEE. Several preliminary searches were performed to refine and optimise the search terms, maximise sensitivity and ensure comprehensive retrieval of relevant and eligible studies. The final search strategies were tested to ensure they captured five previously identified key papers.

The search strategy included MeSH index terms and keywords related to ML, CVD and risk prediction. No date or study design restrictions were imposed. Searches were limited to studies published in the English language.

All retrieved records were uploaded into Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia), a web-based systematic review management platform that facilitates screening, data extraction and collaboration among reviewers. The detailed search strategy is included in an online supplemental file and described in the published protocol.11

Information sources

We searched the following databases from their inception to the end of 2024: MEDLINE (OVID interface, 1948 onwards), EMBASE (OVID interface, 1980 onwards) and IEEE (1988 onwards). We also checked reference lists of included papers for relevant studies meeting inclusion criteria and not identified in the search.

We included studies if they (1) described a specific algorithm or ML approach; (2) produced a risk assessment output for one or more of the following CVD outcomes: coronary artery disease, heart failure, cardiac arrhythmia (ie, atrial fibrillation), stroke; (3) provided a comparative performance analysis with the FRS and (4) included participants (≥18 years) from a general population of healthy adults with no history of CVD. Although studies that examined a population with CVD risk factors (eg, obesity) were included, those studies restricted to populations with specific diseases or conditions (eg, rheumatoid arthritis, HIV, metabolic disorders) or with a history of CVD were excluded. Papers were also excluded if they: (1) evaluated algorithms designed for diagnosis rather than risk prediction of CVD; (2) modelled the risk of developing a cardiovascular risk factor rather than a CVD; (3) were conference presentations or abstracts with no associated peer-reviewed paper or; (4) were commentaries, letters, editorials or unpublished work that had not undergone peer review or did not describe a specific ML algorithm.

Outcomes

The endpoints necessary for inclusion were any of coronary artery disease, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, stroke and other major adverse cardiovascular events. When these endpoints were not explicitly reported, surrogate outcomes such as all-cause mortality were considered, as long as the algorithm was developed for CVD risk estimation. Neither the outcomes, themselves, nor their specific medical precursors of risk were the primary focus of the analysis.

Selection process

Two reviewers independently screened all titles and abstracts using the predefined eligibility criteria. This was done within Covidence. Disagreements were resolved via discussion and consensus. Where eligibility was unclear study authors were contacted for more information and clarification.

Two reviewers independently assessed full texts of all papers deemed potentially eligible. Disagreements were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached.

Data extraction

A standardised data extraction form was developed and piloted prior to use. The form documented the following information: purpose of the ML algorithm; comparison of the algorithm’s performance with FRS; specifics of algorithm development (including funding, competing interests, ethics approval and involvement of community stakeholders); sociodemographic variables included in training data and any related potential sources of bias (eg, race, gender, social strata); consent/privacy concerns; definition of CVD risk; algorithm output (including global CVD risk, risk of stroke, heart failure, arrhythmia or coronary artery disease); intended end user of the algorithm; reported clinical utility. We explicitly checked whether ML algorithms included race, sex and socioeconomic indicators to address sociodemographic gaps in the FRS and examine diversity of the population studied.

Dual data extraction was conducted independently by two reviewers. Disagreements in data extraction and risk assessment were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias for each included study was assessed using a modified Prediction Risk Of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST).13 The PROBAST framework evaluates risk of bias across four domains: participants, predictors, outcome and analysis. This tool was incorporated into the data extract form and described in the published protocol.11

Data synthesis and critical review

Following data extraction, two reviewers conducted a detailed qualitative synthesis. A thematic analysis was performed to explore patterns, relationships and trends across included studies. The synthesis focused on added value of ML algorithms relative to the FRS, potential benefits/harms and unintended consequences, equity implications and risks of bias, rather than on computational design, statistical methodology or weighting of medical risk factors.

Results

Data sources, when described, included pre-existing longitudinal surveys such as the UK Biobank, purpose collected datasets, or medical records. Chinese datasets (n=7), Framingham (n=6) and the UK Biobank (n=4) made up more than half the data used. One study39 included a simulated dataset. A few studies from small geographic regions noted homogeneity of the population as a limitation. No paper explored possible shortcomings inherent in the data, such as inaccuracy arising from self-reporting, entry errors, or AI misinterpretations of medical record information. Across studies, missing information was handled by either imputing values, excluding subjects, or by doing nothing. Many studies used available variables beyond the standard FRS parameters. Twenty-eight included added clinical findings, biomarkers, or imaging results. Seven papers added investigations not commonly available among a population to be screened, tests such as CT angiography, DNA methylation or genomic testing.19 22 26 33 37 38 40 A few studies explored whether some FRS parameters could be excluded or replaced by other generally available inputs (eg, ECG) without diminishing predictive accuracy.15 27

Of particular importance was whether studies addressed deficiencies of the FRS attributable to its exclusion of known sociodemographic predictors of CVD. Nine papers added such sociodemographic factors as race or SES. The impact of these traits, when reported, was inconsistent. Almost all underlying datasets included sex as a variable although not all studies reported sex-specific risk predicting abilities of their algorithms.

Three studies23 25 31 explicitly used elements of the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) checklist for reporting multivariable prediction models regarding individual prognosis.41

Potential bias in studies was assessed using the modified PROBAST checklist (see online supplemental table 2). Approximately 50% of studies used datasets with inherent biases such as parameters that were subjective, undefined or defined differently than those used in the FRS, populations with pre-existing CVD, or data with age limitations. Race was explicitly included in six studies and a measure of SES (such as education, income, or deprivation score) was part of datasets used by six algorithms. Few did sample size calculations although almost all appeared to have adequate sample sizes. Most, but not all studies included all enrolled participants in analyses. Twenty-one of 28 studies used FRS input variables. Although the FRS provides a 10 year risk estimation, 11 of the 29 reviewed papers shortened this timeframe while one lengthened it but also recalibrated the FRS accordingly, and two had varying time frames. Missing data were clearly handled appropriately in five studies.

Seven studies or their authors were funded and/or conducted by industries linked to added input parameters.

Most claimed greater predictive value than the FRS (23 of 29), generally doing so when at least one subset within their calculations yielded an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC) or a C-statistic that was greater than that found for the FRS. Statistical strength or significance was almost always taken as a proxy for clinical benefit. A few demonstrated clinical benefit, finding that ML techniques, themselves, might decrease false positives,34 that using fewer parameters than does the FRS could yield equally reliable predictive values,27 29 or that including, for example, lipoprotein metabolism measures35 improved output accuracy.

Discussion

Overall, this review demonstrates modest statistical strength of ML algorithms designed to predict the risk of CVD relative to the FRS. ML’s computational ability to use large datasets, incorporate more characteristics than does the FRS, and examine interconnections among multiple variables appears to be responsible for this strength. Even when parameters included were primarily those used in the FRS, interactions and non-linear effects may have yielded algorithms that improved on FRS’s linear model. Nevertheless, claims made in 23 of the 29 studies that ML improved on the FRS often lack clinical validity. With the exception of 4 of 29 papers, although technical ability and statistical significance were demonstrated these were either not, or only vaguely coupled with clear clinical understanding or evidence of practical merit.42 Our aim was not to identify clinical parameters that would better predict CVD risk than do those in the FRS. We did, however, note that among variables added to studies there were no specific clinical factors commonly available in a general adult population (ie, those for whom screening is intended) that consistently improved risk prediction. Perhaps this reinforces that it is ML’s non-linear design that produces statistical superiority. Also possible is that the variety of added parameters across the 29 papers makes identifying a few of overall benefit difficult.

Including sociodemographics to improve CVD risk prediction

Only research designed to enhance the predictive value of the FRS was included in this review. A shortcoming of the FRS is that with the exception of sex it does not address known sociodemographic determinants of CVD risk such as SES and race.9 Only two of the nine studies that explicitly included either race or SES assessed or commented on their impact.16 23 Several authors did attempt to correct for the racial homogeneity of the Framingham Health Study by studying a non-white although also somewhat homogeneous population, for example, Uyghur or Indian.25 29 30 Almost all datasets included sex as a variable, however few disaggregated results by sex or noted whether their algorithm performed differently for men and women. Inattention to the impact of sociodemographic characteristics underscores a missed opportunity to correct for known FRS shortcomings or to determine whether interactions between, for example, SES and clinical characteristics such as lipid levels might alter CVD risk and should, therefore, be included in a risk prediction tool.

Adding tests not generally available in the population to be screened

As van Smeden et al43 have advised, the utility of an ML prediction model depends on how well data used represent the target population. A number of the ML models examined whether incorporating diagnostic tests such as CT angiography,22 26 retinal imaging,37 biomarkers of DNA methylation19 40 or whole genome testing,14 measures not generally available or warranted among the target screening population, might improve predictive value. The purpose of these frequently industry sponsored studies appeared to be validation, uptake and, perhaps, marketing of those tests. Adding these investigations to the risk prediction algorithm diminished representation and generalisability, and would increase screening costs dramatically. Those in low resourced settings would be particularly disadvantaged, augmenting SES inequities, and negating a real strength of the FRS—its reliance on available, inexpensive, low tech measures.

Design weaknesses

A key inclusion criterion for this systematic review was comparison of outcomes with the FRS. Although all papers reviewed met this criterion many introduced biases by not adhering to FRS definitions of measures, or using surrogate outcomes, raising questions about misclassification and the credibility of comparisons with and claims of greater precision than the FRS. Ambiguity or heterogeneity of terminology used to define CVD outcomes, in particular, has been noted by others as a problem encountered in datasets.3 Some studies shortened the 10 year predictive time frame while others redefined or failed to define hypertension, elevated body mass index (BMI), myocardial infarction (MI), etc. One study44 that was excluded because its outcome was subclinical atherosclerosis identified via CT angiogram, defined increased risk as a BMI >22 and a systolic blood pressure >120, both of which fall within universally accepted normal ranges. Missing values were frequently imputed while proportions as high as 75% of datasets were sometimes excluded for unexplained reasons.36

One otherwise strong study counted each entry in a patient’s record separately meaning a single patient’s data might be in the dataset multiple times while another might be included only once.28 Those papers using pre-existing datasets rather than medical records often had to rely on self-reporting of CVD. A few studies used surrogate outcomes such as imaging of coronary artery atherosclerosis to replace clinically diagnosed CVD.

Clinical significance was rarely mentioned (see online supplemental table 1). CVD prediction models generally assessed their performance by testing for statistical significance of improvement in the AUC (ROC-AUC) or the concordance statistic (c-statistic). Almost all papers reported moderate to large improvements in risk prediction compared with the FRS and implied that these findings were clinically meaningful. With one exception,35 no studies considered whether increasing the number who were identified as at risk would lead to an increase in false rather than true positives.

Risk of overdiagnosis

Although most studies reported sensitivities and specificities of algorithm output, utility of these was limited by the following: input data were often subdivided and sensitivities and specificities ranged significantly across subsets such as age or sex; sometimes multiple training datasets or computational techniques were used with calculations made for each; the comparable sensitivity and specificity for FRS were occasionally not reported. We, therefore, can only offer a qualitative assessment of how ML, particularly when decoupled from clinical oversight, at times precipitated diagnosis creep and overdiagnosis.45 Some studies lowered the threshold at which risk was assumed while others implied that identifying a greater number of individuals as at risk indicated superiority of the algorithm compared with the FRS.38 It is, however, also possible that this greater number found to be at risk was, in reality, an increase in false positives rather than in true risk. The clinical impact would then be to do more rather than to do better, that is, to expand the pool of individuals treated in the name of preventing CVD. Overdiagnosing risk will lead to unnecessary interventions, with associated costs, harms and patient anxiety. Few studies examined the inverse, that is, whether the algorithm more accurately ruled out CVD risk by better identifying true negatives. One noteworthy early study35 did find that including a measure of lipoprotein metabolism reduced overdiagnosis of risk by 25% compared with the FRS. Subsequent researchers were not guided by this approach despite the evidence that a major limitation of the FRS, itself, is the number of individuals identified as at risk who do not go on to develop CVD.46

Limitations

A meta-analysis was not possible as the included research examined different populations, varying interventions, and did not define or measure outcomes in the same way or at the same time intervals.47

This review did not address problems arising from limits to the completeness, variety, integrity or veracity of information in the datasets, themselves.48 Such limitations may have produced biases arising from self-reporting, and, particularly when medical records were used, in reliability of data entered. We note that some had higher ROC-AUCs for women than men while others demonstrated the reverse. This could arise from gaps or inaccuracies in the datasets (eg, as documented by others, minimising of one sex’s concerns relative to the other).49

Our aim was not to evaluate or compare the computational methods used. As ML techniques evolve those used in the studies reviewed will likely be replaced by others with greater methodological strength. We know that ML has the capacity to address key shortcomings of the FRS by including social circumstances and interactions among these. The question addressed in this review is whether, at present, ML is using this capacity. We leave commenting on the computational methods used to those with particular technological expertise.

We do not want to suggest that the FRS is the only CVD prediction tool used at present. We chose it for comparison as it existed well before any ML models would have been developed. The FRS also appears to be the most frequently used CVD risk assessment tool and the one most commonly cited as the standard to which AI algorithms were compared.

Conclusions

Despite common claims that their algorithms improved on the FRS, authors often accepted statistical significance as improvement. Several used study populations already identified as ‘at risk’ (such as those who had previously had a CT angiogram) rather than adhering to the principle of universality that is a fundamental concept of screening. Further, biases within studies arose from expanded (relative to FRS) altered, missing or surrogate definitions of inputs and outcomes, the use of homogeneous populations, conflicts of interest, a failure to determine whether increased identification of individuals at risk reflected true risk or overdiagnosis, extensive use of imputation, or unexplained exclusion of significant proportions of datasets. Although most datasets would have allowed for it, few studies explicitly addressed two key limitations of the FRS—its inability to identify non-linear relationships among risk factors and the variation in its predictive value across sociodemographic characteristics. An unwritten assumption appeared to be that identifying more individuals at risk is better. This could result in overdiagnosis of risk with its concomitant costs and harms of unneeded medications, tests and patient anxiety.

Algorithm development and output to date might be summarised as ‘missed opportunities’. ML algorithms likely can improve CVD risk prediction when coupled with attention to social determinants of health, clinical significance, and parsimony/cost effectiveness, (eg, avoiding costly screening tests if they have limited added value). A 2026 statement from the American Heart Association50 will be useful in designing and assessing future ML models to ensure that they combine technical expertise with clinical insight and methodological rigour to correct for the deficiencies identified in this review.
