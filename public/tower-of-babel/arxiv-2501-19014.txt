Entanglement Entropy and Cauchy-Hadamard Renormalization
Benoit Estienne and Jiasheng Lin

Abstract
This note presents a purely geometric construction of the so-called twist-field correlation functions in Conformal Field Theory (CFT), derived from conical singularities . This approach provides a purely mathematical interpretation of the seminal results in physics by Cardy and Calabrese on the entanglement entropy of quantum systems. Specifically, we begin by defining CFT partition functions on surfaces with conical singularities, using a “Cauchy-Hadamard renormalization” of the Polyakov anomaly integral. Next, we demonstrate that for a branched cover f : Σ d ⟶ Σ f:\Sigma_{d}\longrightarrow\Sigma with d d sheets, where the cover inherits the pullback of a smooth metric from the base, a specific ratio of partition functions on the cover to the base transforms under conformal changes of the base metric in the same way as a correlation function of CFT primary fields with specific conformal weights. We also provide a discussion of the physical background and motivation for entanglement entropy, focusing on path integrals and the replica trick, which serves as an introduction to these ideas for a mathematical audience.



Introduction

An (Euclidean) Conformal Field Theory (CFT) is a theory which, in one way, emerges as the
scaling limit
of a lattice statistical mechanics model with
critical parameters
(at phase transition), as the lattice spacing goes to zero. Due to its conformal covariance properties, such a theory can, in two dimensions, naturally be defined on a surface with its conformal structure (
=
complex structure for oriented surfaces).
Broadly speaking and for the purposes of this paper, the data of such a theory consists of assigning to each (closed) Riemann surface
\Sigma
with metric
g
a number
\mathcal{Z}(\Sigma,g)
(the partition function) and a collection of correlation functions defined on
\Sigma^{n}_{\neq}
(subspace of the
n
-fold product
\Sigma^{n}
with non-coincident points), which satisfy well-defined transformation rules under conformal changes of the metric
g
(see definition
2.1
). In comparison to the original lattice problem, the number
\mathcal{Z}(\Sigma,g)
corresponds to the normalization constant for the Gibbs measure, while the functions on
\Sigma^{n}_{\neq}
describe the scaling limit of the probabilistic correlation functions of
n
local observables of the spin configurations under the Gibbs measure.

It has long been recognized in the physics literature that the presence of
conical singularities
in the underlying metric alters the conformal covariance properties of the partition function
\mathcal{Z}(\Sigma,g)
in a way that mimics the behavior of correlation functions. This paper aims to provide a rigorous framework for these observations, with a particular emphasis on partition functions on branched coverings, motivated by considerations of entanglement entropy (which we introduce in Section
3
). We remark that a CFT in the sense of the previous paragraph makes sense,
a priori
, only for smooth metrics and smooth conformal changes. The first task of this paper thus lies in defining in a simple and natural manner the number
\mathcal{Z}(\Sigma,\widetilde{g})
when the metric
\widetilde{g}
admits finitely many isolated
conical singularities
. Then, applying this definition to metrics coming from pull-backs of ramified holomorphic maps, we observe that certain ratios of these numbers behave exactly like what is expected of correlation functions on the target surface where the variables are the critical values. Conical singularities is also a relatively well-studied subject mathematically. They are one of the simplest types of singularities that can appear on a Riemann surface. The main motivations include spectral geometry (hearing the shape of a drum)

Future work.

In the present version of the work we have focused on the simplest case dealing only with partition functions to illustrate our (already simple) methods. It is clear that the same arguments could apply with minor modifications to obtain analogous results for Segal’s amplitudes on surfaces with boundary (see
[
16
]
section 2.6) when the metric is “flat-at-the-boundary”, and for the case of general boundaries with possible presence of “corners” (i.e. “polygons”) by including the boundary term in the anomaly, as considered for example by

In this section we explain in detail the so-called
entanglement entropies
of quantum systems and a technique for computing them using

, called the

in the physics community
[

,

,
33
,
42
]
. This leads us to consider precisely the ratios of the form shown in (

Partial Trace and Entanglement

In this subsection we discuss some generalities on describing entanglement between quantum systems. We start with a generic quantum system associated to a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Recall that a
(quantum) statistical ensemble
(or
statistical state
) on
\mathcal{H}
is represented by a nonnegative trace class operator
\rho
on
\mathcal{H}
with
\ttr_{\mathcal{H}}(\rho)=1
. This is usually called a
density operator
. Denote by
\mathcal{J}_{1}(\mathcal{H})
,
\mathcal{J}_{\infty}(\mathcal{H})
and
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
the trace class, compact and bounded operators on
\mathcal{H}
.

Now suppose the quantum system can be decomposed into two subsystems
A
and
B
. In other words, assume that the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is a tensor product:

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}.

(3.1)

In order to describe the relation of the subsystems to the whole system, a useful operation is called the
partial trace
. This corresponds to taking trace “over one component” and one is left with an operator acting on the “remainder component”. It could be defined rigorously as follows.
For each
\rho\in\mathcal{J}_{1}(\mathcal{H})
, consider the linear functional

C\longmapsto\ttr_{\mathcal{H}}(\rho(C\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{B}))

(3.2)

for
C\in\mathcal{J}_{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
. We have

\big|\ttr_{\mathcal{H}}\big(\rho(C\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{B})\big)\big|\leqslant\big\lVert\rho\big\rVert_{\mathcal{J}_{1}(\mathcal{H})}\big\lVert C\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{B}\big\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})}=\big\lVert\rho\big\rVert_{\mathcal{J}_{1}(\mathcal{H})}\big\lVert C\big\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{A})}.

(3.3)

Thus (
3.3
) defines a bounded linear functional on
\mathcal{J}_{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
with norm
\left\lVert\rho\right\rVert_{\mathcal{J}_{1}(\mathcal{H})}
. Since
\mathcal{J}_{1}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
is the Schatten dual of the ideal
\mathcal{J}_{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
we obtain a unique representative
\ttr_{B}(\rho)\in\mathcal{J}_{1}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
be a tensor product as in (
3.1
). Then the
partial trace over
\mathcal{H}_{B}
is the linear map

\left.\begin{array}[]{rcl}\ttr_{B}:\mathcal{J}_{1}(\mathcal{H})&\longrightarrow&\mathcal{J}_{1}(\mathcal{H}_{A}),\\
\rho&\longmapsto&\ttr_{B}(\rho),\end{array}\right.

(3.4)

such that
\ttr_{B}(\rho)
is the unique operator on
\mathcal{H}_{A}
satisfying

\ttr_{\mathcal{H}_{A}}\big(\ttr_{B}(\rho)C\big)=\ttr_{\mathcal{H}}\big(\rho(C\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{B})\big)

(3.5)

for all bounded operators
C\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
.

Remark 3.1
.

For more details see Simon
[
39
]
pages 269-270, and also
[
36
]
theorem 3.2. In particular, from (
3.3
) one can see that the partial trace is continuous with respect to the
\mathcal{J}_{1}(\mathcal{H})
- and
\mathcal{J}_{1}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
-norms (trace norms), and by taking
C=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
,
\psi\in\mathcal{H}_{A}
, in (
3.5
) one could also see that
\ttr_{B}(\rho)
is nonnegative if
\rho
is.

Remark 3.2
.

Restricted to rank-one operators, we have

\ttr_{B}\big(|\varphi_{A}\otimes\varphi_{B}\rangle\langle\psi_{A}\otimes\psi_{B}|\big)=\langle\psi_{B}|\varphi_{B}\rangle|\varphi_{A}\rangle\langle\psi_{A}|,

(3.6)

with
|\varphi_{A}\rangle
,
|\psi_{A}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{A}
,
|\varphi_{B}\rangle
,
|\psi_{B}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{B}
. Here the notation
|w\rangle\langle v|
for
w
,
v\in\mathcal{H}
denotes the operator mapping any
u\in\mathcal{H}
to
\left\langle v,u\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}w
. See also example
3.1
.

Definition 3.2
.

Suppose
\rho
is a density operator over
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
. Then the
reduced density operator over
A
is defined by

\rho_{A}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\ttr_{B}(\rho).

(3.7)

It follows from remark
3.1
that
\rho_{A}
is a density operator over
\mathcal{H}_{A}
.

Definition 3.3
.

Suppose
\rho
is a density operator over
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
. We define

\displaystyle\textrm{{the entanglement entropy (over }}A\textrm{{)}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{A}

\displaystyle\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}-\ttr_{\mathcal{H}_{A}}(\rho_{A}\log\rho_{A}),

(3.8)

\displaystyle\textrm{{the }}n\textrm{{-th R\'{e}nyi entropy (over }}A\textrm{{)}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{(n)}_{A}

\displaystyle\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\frac{1}{1-n}\log\ttr_{\mathcal{H}_{A}}(\rho_{A}^{n}),\quad\textrm{for }n>1.

(3.9)

Remark 3.3
.

In general the quantities
\mathcal{S}_{A}
and
\mathcal{S}_{A}^{(n)}
defined above do not equal
\mathcal{S}_{B}
and
\mathcal{S}_{B}^{(n)}
, the corresponding quantities defined the other way round by first taking partial trace over
A
. However,
\mathcal{S}_{A}=\mathcal{S}_{B}
and
\mathcal{S}_{A}^{(n)}=\mathcal{S}_{B}^{(n)}
do hold if
\rho
is a
pure state
, namely a rank-one projector written as
\rho=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
for some
|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}
. Therefore, for pure states it makes sense to define
the
entanglement and Rényi entropies
\mathcal{S}:=\mathcal{S}_{A}=\mathcal{S}_{B}
and
\mathcal{S}^{(n)}:=\mathcal{S}_{A}^{(n)}=\mathcal{S}_{B}^{(n)}
. In the general case, one could then consider another quantity called the
mutual information
, defined as

\mathcal{I}_{A,B}^{(n)}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\mathcal{S}_{A}^{(n)}+\mathcal{S}_{B}^{(n)}-\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{H}}^{(n)},

(3.10)

where
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{H}}^{(n)}
is defined the same way as (
3.9
) by just removing the subscript
A
.

Remark 3.4
.

If
\rho
is trace class, then
\rho_{A}
is trace class and the complex power
\rho_{A}^{z}
is well-defined for
\mathfrak{Re}(z)>1
. If moreover
\mathcal{S}_{A}<\infty
, one then has the relation

\mathcal{S}_{A}=\lim_{z\to 1^{+}}\mathcal{S}_{A}^{(z)}=-\lim_{z\to 1^{+}}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\ttr_{\mathcal{H}_{A}}(\rho_{A}^{z}).

(3.11)

However, for continuum QFTs the tensor product decomposition
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
is generally not well-defined, and the only approach available which produces reasonable answers seems to be employing the so-called
replica
interpretation to be explained in the next subsection, or the equivalent approach by
twist fields
(both avoid defining the tensor product). These concerns the quantities
\ttr_{\mathcal{H}_{A}}(\rho_{A}^{n})
for positive integral
n
and is also what we will focus on for this paper.

Example 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}=:\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
, in this order. Consider the standard basis
e_{1}\otimes e_{1}
,
e_{1}\otimes e_{2}
,
e_{2}\otimes e_{1}
and
e_{2}\otimes e_{2}
(in this order) of
\mathcal{H}
where
e_{1}
,
e_{2}
are standard base vectors of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Then for
\rho\in\End(\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2})
written

\rho=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}a_{11}&a_{12}&a_{13}&a_{14}\\
a_{21}&a_{22}&a_{23}&a_{24}\\
a_{31}&a_{32}&a_{33}&a_{34}\\
a_{41}&a_{42}&a_{43}&a_{44}\end{array}\right),

(3.12)

we have

\ttr_{B}(\rho)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{11}+a_{22}&a_{13}+a_{24}\\
a_{31}+a_{42}&a_{33}+a_{44}\end{array}\right),\quad\quad\ttr_{A}(\rho)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{11}+a_{33}&a_{12}+a_{34}\\
a_{21}+a_{43}&a_{22}+a_{44}\end{array}\right).

(3.13)

Also, if
\rho=\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B}
is a Kronecker product, we have
\ttr_{B}(\rho)=\ttr(\rho_{B})\rho_{A}
,
\ttr_{A}(\rho)=\ttr(\rho_{A})\rho_{B}
.

Path Integrals and Replica

Now let us describe the geometric framework where the quantities
\mathcal{S}_{A}
and
\mathcal{S}_{A}^{(n)}
get represented by
path integrals
, as presented in
[
8
]
. Here we shall describe the pictures heuristically and
not attempt at any rigor
. For this paper we focus on
1+1
dimensional field theories where the space is either the real line or a circle with a specific perimeter (as a Riemannian manifold), denoted generally by
X
, and space-time a 2-dimensional Riemannian surface with or without boundary denoted
\Sigma
(for example,
\Sigma=X\times[0,T]
or
X\times\mathbb{R}
). Each field theory comes with specified
field configuration spaces
over space and space-time, denoted
\conf(X)
and
\conf(\Sigma)
, as well as over their sub-regions. Typically
\conf(X)=\mathrm{Map}(X,\mathscr{V})
, the space of
maps
from
X
to a
spin value space
/
target space

\mathscr{V}
. Such configuration spaces should allow

1.

restriction of a configuration onto a subregion
A\subset X
(or
\Sigma
), heuristically a map

\left.\begin{array}[]{rcl}\conf(X)&\longrightarrow&\conf(A),\\
\phi&\longmapsto&\phi|_{A}.\end{array}\right.

(3.14)

In other words one is allowed to
localize
the field;

2.

each configuration
\phi\in\conf(X)
to be recovered from the pair
(\phi|_{A},\phi|_{A^{c}})
of its restrictions onto
A
and
A^{c}=X\setminus A
, and moreover any pair of configurations on complementary subregions should combine into a global configuration; this is to say heuristically,

\conf(X)\cong\conf(A)\times\conf(A^{c}).

(3.15)

Remark 3.5
.

The above statements are rigorous in the case
X=\Lambda
is a discrete lattice, and
\conf(\Lambda)=\mathrm{Map}(\Lambda,\mathscr{V})
. Here “subregions” correspond to subsets of lattice sites.

Remark 3.6
.

Suppose
\Sigma=X\times[0,T]
. Then
\conf(\Sigma)
could be considered as the space of “paths” through which a configuration over
X
evolves across the time interval
[0,T]
. Indeed, if we have taken
\conf(\Sigma)
to be
\mathrm{Map}(\Sigma,\mathscr{V})
, then we would have simply
\mathrm{Map}(X\times[0,T],\mathscr{V})\cong\mathrm{Map}([0,T],\mathrm{Map}(X,\mathscr{V}))
. The integral of (
3.18
) below is thus an integral over a space of “paths”.

We now describe heuristically the Hilbert space and the time evolution. For the Hilbert space one takes

\mathcal{H}_{X}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}L^{2}(\conf(X),\mathcal{L}),

(3.16)

where
\mathcal{L}
denotes the non-existent
Lebesgue measure
on the configuration space
\conf(X)
. Under the path-integral formalism (Euclidean
3
3

3

See the first paragraph of subsection
3.3
.
) time evolution across
\Sigma=X\times[0,T]
, namely over time
T
, is represented by the integral operator

\left.\begin{array}[]{rcl}U_{T}:\mathcal{H}_{X}&\longrightarrow&\mathcal{H}_{X},\\
F&\longmapsto&(U_{T}F)(\psi)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\displaystyle\int_{\conf(X)}\mathcal{A}_{T}(\psi,\varphi)F(\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}(\varphi),\end{array}\right.

(3.17)

with the integral kernel

\mathcal{A}_{T}(\psi,\varphi)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\int_{\left\{\phi\in\conf(\Sigma)\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \begin{subarray}{c}\phi|_{X\times\{0\}}=\varphi,\\
\phi|_{X\times\{T\}}=\psi\end{subarray}\right\}}\textrm{e}^{-S_{\mathrm{EQFT}}(\phi)}\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}(\phi),

(3.18)

where now we integrate against the still non-existent Lebesgue measure on
\conf(\Sigma)
(with the indicated boundary conditions), and where

is the

(“E” stands for “Euclidean”) of the specific theory at hand.

The above recipe for time evolution does not involve the fact that

is

Now denote by

(composition)

(trace)

\Sigma

\partial_{\mathrm{ter}}\Sigma

where the number

\mathcal{Z}(\check{\Sigma})\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\int_{\conf(\check{\Sigma})}\textrm{e}^{-S_{\mathrm{EQFT}}(\phi)}\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}(\phi)

Remark 3.7

Comparing (

Now we try to incorporate considerations of subregions

\mathcal{H}_{X}\overset{\mathrm{heu}}{\cong}L^{2}\big(\conf(A)\times\conf(A^{c}),\mathcal{L}_{\conf(A)}\otimes\mathcal{L}_{\conf(A^{c})}\big)\overset{\mathrm{heu}}{\cong}\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A^{c}}.

We shall assume that this has been done in the obvious manner.

Finally we arrive at an interpretation of a quantity of the form

) and (
3.21
). The evolution operators defined in this way is said to be in
imaginary time
. For actual Lorentzian QFT one must use
\exp(\mathrm{i}S_{\mathrm{QFT}})
instead of
\exp(-S_{\mathrm{EQFT}})
, where the action
S_{\mathrm{QFT}}
is also written in terms of the Lorentzian metric.

Next, which imaginary-time evolution operators are trace class and nonnegative, serving as candidates for a density operator? In this regard it would be instructive to introduce the third axiom of Segal’s:

(adjoint)

let
\Sigma^{*}
denote the same space-time as
\Sigma
but with the identification of the initial and terminal boundaries
reversed
, then

U_{\Sigma^{*}}=U_{\Sigma}^{\dagger},

(3.25)

where
\dagger
denotes the operator adjoint.

From this, together with the two previous axioms, one could see that Euclidean evolution across a cobordism naturally corresponds to
Hilbert-Schmidt
operators. Indeed,

\ttr_{\mathcal{H}}\big(U_{\Sigma}^{\dagger}U_{\Sigma}\big)=\ttr_{\mathcal{H}}\big(U_{\Sigma^{*}\circ\Sigma}\big)=\mathcal{Z}\big((\Sigma^{*}\circ\Sigma)^{\vee}\big)\quad(<\infty),

(3.26)

where
(\Sigma^{*}\circ\Sigma)^{\vee}
is the “double” of
\Sigma
. This “shows” that
U_{\Sigma}
is Hilbert-Schmidt. Immediately from the same expression one also sees that a cobordism of the form
\Sigma^{*}\circ\Sigma
naturally gives a nonnegative trace class operator which is what we wanted. Such a cobordism has, in other words, a
reflection symmetry
exchanging its initial and terminal boundaries, and whose invariant set is exactly isomorphic to a copy of
X
as a Riemannian submanifold.

Remark 3.8
.

A somewhat technical requirement is that the boundaries of
\Sigma
be geodesic so that its double would be smooth.

Remark 3.9
.

For certain models the (ordinary) Segal axioms have been fully and rigorously constructed. See, for example, the work
[
24
]
of the second author and also
[
17
]
. In these cases
U_{\Sigma}
is rigorously shown to be Hilbert-Schmidt. The main difficulty lies in defining (
3.18
) and (
3.21
) rigorously, in which case it implies
\mathcal{Z}(\check{\Sigma})<\infty
, and also in showing the generic composition axiom out of these definitions.

Now we give some examples.

Example 3.2
.

Assume that one is given a
Hamiltonian operator

H
acting on
\mathcal{H}_{X}
, and let
\beta>0
. Then the so-called
thermal state
(or
Gibbs state
) at
inverse temperature

\beta
is given by the density operator

\rho_{\beta}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\frac{\textrm{e}^{-\beta H}}{\ttr_{\mathcal{H}}(\textrm{e}^{-\beta H})}.

(3.27)

The operator
\textrm{e}^{-\beta H}
gives the evolution over imaginary time
\mathrm{i}\beta
and could be represented by a path integral (
3.18
) over the cylinder
\Sigma_{\beta}:=X\times[0,\beta]
with a specific action related to
H
. Now we denote by
\check{\Sigma}_{n,\beta}
the closed surface obtained by gluing
n
copies of
\check{\Sigma}_{\beta}\setminus A
cyclicly along the slit
A
, as explained at the end of the last subsection. Then the replica trick yields

\ttr_{A}\big(\ttr_{A^{c}}(\rho_{\beta})^{n}\big)=\frac{\mathcal{Z}(\check{\Sigma}_{n,\beta})}{\mathcal{Z}(\check{\Sigma}_{\beta})^{n}}.

(3.28)

We remark again that
\check{\Sigma}_{n,\beta}
comes equipped with the “replica metric” which is induced from
\Sigma_{\beta}
.

Example 3.3
.

Here we consider cobordisms which are disconnected,
\Sigma=\Sigma_{1}\sqcup\Sigma_{2}
, with
\partial\Sigma_{1}\cong\partial\Sigma_{2}\cong X
. We mark
\partial\Sigma_{1}
as initial and
\partial\Sigma_{2}
as terminal. In this case

\conf(\Sigma)=\conf(\Sigma_{1})\times\conf(\Sigma_{2}),\quad\quad\mathcal{L}_{\conf(\Sigma)}=\mathcal{L}_{\conf(\Sigma_{1})}\otimes\mathcal{L}_{\conf(\Sigma_{2})},

(3.29)

and assume (a very weak form of)
locality
of the action (no interaction between disjoint space-times), that is,

S_{\Sigma}(\phi)=S_{\Sigma_{1}}(\phi_{1})+S_{\Sigma_{2}}(\phi_{2}),

(3.30)

with
\phi=(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})
, then we have formally

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}(\psi,\varphi)

\displaystyle=\iint_{\left\{\begin{subarray}{c}\phi_{1}\in\conf(\Sigma_{1}),\\
\phi_{1}|_{\partial\Sigma_{1}}=\varphi\end{subarray}\right\}\times\left\{\begin{subarray}{c}\phi_{2}\in\conf(\Sigma_{2}),\\
\phi_{2}|_{\partial\Sigma_{2}}=\psi\end{subarray}\right\}}\textrm{e}^{-S_{\Sigma_{1}}(\phi_{1})}\textrm{e}^{-S_{\Sigma_{2}}(\phi_{2})}\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}_{\conf(\Sigma_{1})}(\phi_{1})\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}_{\conf(\Sigma_{2})}(\phi_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma_{1}}(\varphi)\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma_{2}}(\psi),

(3.31)

where
\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma_{1}}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma_{2}}
are the amplitudes associated to
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
individually, defined by the same formula (
3.18
) in the case of having just one boundary component (sometimes (
3.31
) itself is taken as an axiom, see
[
16
]
page 766). We see that in this case
U_{\Sigma}
is a rank-one operator. Written more suggestively,

U_{\Sigma}=\big|\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma_{2}}\big\rangle\big\langle\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma_{1}}\big|.

(3.32)

We normalize by putting
\rho_{\Sigma}:=U_{\Sigma}/\ttr(U_{\Sigma})
, then following the replica trick we have

\ttr_{A}\big(\ttr_{A^{c}}(\rho_{\Sigma})^{n}\big)=\frac{\mathcal{Z}(\check{\Sigma}_{n})}{\mathcal{Z}(\check{\Sigma})^{n}},

(3.33)

where
\check{\Sigma}
is the closed surface obtained by gluing
\Sigma_{1}
with
\Sigma_{2}
along
X
, and
\check{\Sigma}_{n}
by gluing
n
copies of
\check{\Sigma}\setminus A
along the slit
A
, equipped with the induced metric. In particular, if
\Sigma_{1}=\Sigma_{2}=\mathbb{D}
, the unit disk, equipped with a flat-at-the-boundary metric (namely it could be written
|z|^{-2}|\mathrm{d}z|^{2}
in some complex coordinate on an annulus around
\partial\mathbb{D}
), then
\rho_{\Sigma}
defined as above is metric independent (provided flat at the boundary) and represents (projection onto) the
vacuum state
, usually written
|0\rangle\langle 0|
(see
[
16
]
page 768).

Geometric Lemmas

To begin with, we record some basic conformal relations that will be used repeatedly throughout the paper. These relations are local in nature as they concern only what happens in an arbitrarily small neighborhood around each point. Namely, let
\Sigma
be a smooth
surface
and denote by
\mathrm{d}V_{g}
,
\nabla_{g}
,
K_{g}
,
\Delta_{g}
and
\nabla_{v}^{g}
respectively the volume (area) form, gradient, Gauss curvature (half the scalar curvature), Laplacian and the Levi-Civita covariant derivative in the direction
v\in T_{z}\Sigma
under the Riemannian metric
g
, and let
h\in C^{\infty}(\Sigma)
, then we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}V_{\textrm{e}^{2h}g}

\displaystyle=\textrm{e}^{2h}\mathrm{d}V_{g},

(4.1)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\textrm{e}^{2h}g}

\displaystyle=\textrm{e}^{-2h}\nabla_{g},

(4.2)

\displaystyle K_{\textrm{e}^{2h}g}

\displaystyle=\textrm{e}^{-2h}\left(-\Delta_{g}h+K_{g}\right),

(4.3)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\textrm{e}^{2h}g}

\displaystyle=\textrm{e}^{-2h}\Delta_{g}.

(4.4)

\displaystyle\nabla_{v}^{\textrm{e}^{2h}g}X

\displaystyle=\nabla_{v}^{g}X+(vh)X+(Xh)v-\left\langle v,X\right\rangle_{g}\nabla_{g}h.

(4.5)

The relation (
4.3
) is also called
Liouville’s equation
.

Technical Remarks on Conical Singularities

The principal local regularity result around a conical singularity in our sense (definition
2.5
) is the following obtained by Troyanov
[
41
]
.

Lemma 4.1

(
[
41
]
proposition 3.2)
.

Let
\Sigma
be a closed Riemann surface and
\widetilde{g}
a generalized conformal metric on
\Sigma
with a conical singularity at
z_{0}\in\Sigma
of exponent
\gamma
. Then there exists a complex coordinate
w
defined on a neighborhood
U\ni z_{0}
, with
w(z_{0})=0
, such that

\widetilde{g}|_{U\setminus z_{0}}=|w|^{2\gamma}\textrm{e}^{2\varphi_{U,w}}|\mathrm{d}w|^{2},

(4.6)

such that
\varphi_{U,w}
satisfies the conditions of definition
2.5
as well as

\varphi_{U,w}(z)=\varphi_{U,w}(z_{0})+\mathcal{O}(|w|^{2\gamma+2}),\quad\textrm{and}\quad\partial_{z}\varphi_{U,w},\penalty\ \partial_{\overline{z}}\varphi_{U,w}=\mathcal{O}(|w|^{2\gamma+1}).

(4.7)

In particular,
\partial_{r}\varphi_{U,w}=\mathcal{O}(|w|^{2\gamma+1})
where
r
is the radial coordinate
r(z)=|w(z)|
under
|\mathrm{d}w|^{2}
.
\Box

Troyanov showed further that a neighborhood (still denoted
U
) of the conical singularity could in fact be equivalently and more intrinsically described by a set of
polar coordinates
. That is, there is a map

h:[0,\varrho]\times\mathbb{S}_{\Theta}^{1}\longrightarrow U,

(4.8)

where
\mathbb{S}_{\Theta}^{1}
denotes the Riemannian circle with perimeter
\Theta=2\pi(\gamma+1)
, such that

1.

h(r,\theta)=z_{0}
iff
r=0
;

2.

h|_{(0,\varrho]\times\mathbb{S}^{1}_{\Theta}}
is a locally bi-Lipschitz homeomorphism onto
U\setminus z_{0}
;

3.

we have

h^{*}(\widetilde{g}|_{U\setminus z_{0}})=\mathrm{d}r^{2}+\omega(r,\theta)^{2}\mathrm{d}\theta^{2},

(4.9)

where
\omega:(0,\varrho]\times\mathbb{S}_{\Theta}^{1}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a function such that
0<c_{1}\leqslant\omega(r,\theta)\leqslant c_{2}
for some constants
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
for all
(r,\theta)
and
\lim_{r\to 0}\omega(r,\theta)/r=1
for all
\theta\in\mathbb{S}_{\Theta}^{1}
.

By the last requirement we have

\Theta(r)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\omega(r,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\asymp r\Theta=2\pi r(\gamma+1),\quad\quad r\to 0,

(4.10)

and thus
2\pi(\gamma+1)
is sometimes called the
cone angle
. Moreover, the regularity of the function
\omega
as well as
h
itself could be deduced from the regularity of the curvature of
\widetilde{g}
on
U\setminus z_{0}
(
[
41
]
theorem 4.1). There are certain recent works in the literature which begin naturally with this latter point of view (
[
2
]
for example). But we stick to definition
2.5
throughout this article although utilization of polar coordinates may (or may not) aide with certain proofs.

Remark 4.1
.

In the literature people have considered more restrictive classes of allowable conical metrics by assuming more regularity on the regular metric potential (see
[
21
]
section 2.1). The principle is to respect the correct scaling property under dilations centered at the cone points. Especially for the conical metrics with
constant curvature
, the corresponding regular metric potentials (against coordinate metrics) are shown to be
dilation analytic
. This translates, in our notations, roughly into saying that sufficiently near
z_{0}
,
\varphi_{U,w}
would be a real analytic function of
|w|^{\gamma+1}
, extendable over a neighborhood of zero, in each direction respectively (this is clearly an enhancement of (
4.7
)). However, due to the simplicity of our method we only need a very rough scaling property that is already deducible from lemma
4.1
, which we treat in the section below.

Dilation Properties at the Cone Point

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\Sigma
be a closed Riemann surface and
\widetilde{g}
a generalized conformal metric on
\Sigma
with a conical singularity at
z_{0}\in\Sigma
of exponent
\gamma
. Then

\widetilde{r}(z)=\frac{\textrm{e}^{\varphi(z_{0})}}{\gamma+1}r(z)^{\gamma+1}+\mathcal{O}\left(r^{3\gamma+3}\right),\quad\quad z\to z_{0},

(4.11)

where
\widetilde{r}(z):=d_{\widetilde{g}}(z,z_{0})
,
r(z):=|w(z)|
,
z\neq z_{0}
,
\varphi:=\varphi_{U,w}
as in lemma
4.1
and
w
is the complex coordinate from lemma
4.1
. In particular, we see that
\widetilde{r}(z)\asymp r(z)^{\gamma+1}
as
z\to z_{0}
.

Proof.

By (
4.7
) we have

0<\textrm{e}^{\varphi(z_{0})}-\alpha r(z)^{2\gamma+2}\leqslant\textrm{e}^{\varphi(z)}\leqslant\textrm{e}^{\varphi(z_{0})}+\alpha r(z)^{2\gamma+2}

(4.12)

for some
\alpha>0
, in some smaller neighborhood
U_{1}\subset U
. Now fix
z\in U_{1}
, let
c(s)=w^{-1}(sw(z)/|w(z)|)
be defined for
s\in[0,r(z)]
(the unit speed geodesic under
|\mathrm{d}w|^{2}
). Then
\widetilde{r}(z)
is majorized by the length of
c
under
\widetilde{g}
, namely

\widetilde{r}(z)\leqslant\int_{0}^{r}|c^{\prime}(s)|_{\widetilde{g}}\,\mathrm{d}s=\int_{0}^{r}s^{\gamma}\textrm{e}^{\varphi(c(s))}\,\mathrm{d}s\leqslant\frac{\textrm{e}^{\varphi(z_{0})}r^{\gamma+1}}{\gamma+1}+\alpha\frac{r^{3\gamma+3}}{3\gamma+3},

(4.13)

by (
4.12
). On the other hand, the length under
\widetilde{g}
of any curve inside
U_{1}
is bounded below by its length under the metric

g_{1}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}r^{2\gamma}\big(\textrm{e}^{\varphi(z_{0})}-\alpha r^{2\gamma+2}\big)^{2}\cdot|\mathrm{d}w|^{2}=f(r)^{2}\cdot|\mathrm{d}w|^{2}.

(4.14)

defined on
U_{1}
. By (
4.5
) we know that
c
would now reparametrize into a geodesic under
g_{1}
(minimizing since
g_{1}
is radial with respect to the geodesic coordinates of
g
). Therefore we obtain

\widetilde{r}(z)\geqslant\int_{0}^{r}|c^{\prime}(s)|_{g_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}s=\frac{\textrm{e}^{\varphi(z_{0})}r^{\gamma+1}}{\gamma+1}-\frac{\alpha r^{3\gamma+3}}{3\gamma+3}.

(4.15)

This gives the result.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

In particular, (
4.13
) also shows
\widetilde{r}(z)<\infty
, which is not necessarily true
a priori
.

From this lemma it follows immediately the next two corollaries.

Corollary 4.3
.

With the same set-up and notation as lemma
4.2
, we have

r(z)=(\gamma+1)^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}\textrm{e}^{-\frac{\varphi(z_{0})}{\gamma+1}}\widetilde{r}(z)^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}+\mathcal{O}(\widetilde{r}^{2+\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}),

(4.16)

as
z\to z_{0}
.

Proof.

This follows from a simple order analysis using Newton’s binomial formula.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

Pick
h\in C^{\infty}(\Sigma)
and denote by
\widetilde{r}_{h}(z)
the distance from
z
to
z_{0}
under the scaled metric

\widetilde{g}_{h}|_{U}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}d_{g}(\bullet,z_{0})^{2\gamma}\textrm{e}^{2(\varphi+h)}\cdot g|_{U}.

(4.17)

Denote by
\delta(\varepsilon)
and
\delta_{h}(\varepsilon)
metric radii under
g
of the
\varepsilon
-metric disks under
\widetilde{g}
and
\widetilde{g}_{h}
respectively centered at
z_{0}
. Then we have

\frac{\delta(\varepsilon)}{\delta_{h}(\varepsilon)}\longrightarrow\exp\left(\frac{h(z_{0})}{\gamma+1}\right),

(4.18)

as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
.
\Box

Log-divergent Integrals

In this section we collect some basic computations of “toy integrals” involving functions with log-divergent singularities, which will nevertheless play a fundamental role in the proofs of the main results of this paper.

Throughout this section, we assume
(\Sigma,g)
is a Riemannian surface with smooth metric
g
, let
z_{0}\in\Sigma
and let
\sigma\in C^{\infty}(\Sigma\setminus\{z_{0}\})
such that in a neighbourhood
U
of
z_{0}
,

\sigma(z)=\gamma\log d_{g}(z,z_{0})+\varphi(z)

(4.19)

for some
\gamma>-1
, where
\varphi
is a function satisfying the requirements for the metric potential in definition
2.5
as well as (
4.7
) with
|\mathrm{d}w|^{2}
replaced by
g
. Formula (
4.27
), however, needs much weaker assumptions. We start by recalling the following basic fact.

Lemma 4.5
.

In the set-up as above, the function
\Delta_{g}\log d_{g}(\bullet,z_{0})
is smooth and uniformly bounded near but not coincident with
z_{0}
.

Proof.

See appendix
A
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

A related classical result says that the perimeter
\ell_{g}(\partial B_{r}(z_{0}))
of a geodesic circle centered at
z_{0}\in\Sigma
of radius
r
has an asymptotics

\ell_{g}(\partial B_{r}(z_{0}))=2\pi r-\frac{\pi}{3}r^{3}K_{g}(z_{0})+o(r^{3}),\quad r\to 0^{+}.

(4.20)

Here as above,
K_{g}(z_{0})
is the Gauss curvature of
g
at
z_{0}
. See, for example,
[
14
]
page 296.

Lemma 4.6
.

In the set-up as above, let
\delta>0
. Then we have

\int_{\Sigma\setminus B_{\delta}(z_{0},g)}|\nabla_{g}\sigma|_{g}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}V_{g}=-2\pi\gamma^{2}\log(\delta)-2\pi\gamma\varphi(z_{0})-\int_{\Sigma\setminus B_{\delta}}\sigma\Delta_{g}\sigma\,\mathrm{d}V_{g}+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2\min\{\gamma,0\}+2}\log\delta)

(4.21)

as
\delta\to 0^{+}
, where
B_{\delta}(z_{0},g)
denotes the geodesic disk around
z_{0}
of radius
\delta
under the metric
g
.

Proof.

Denote
r(z):=d_{g}(z,z_{0})
. Integrating by parts (Green-Stokes formula) we have

LHS

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B_{\delta}}\sigma(-\partial_{r}\sigma)\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{g}-\int_{\Sigma\setminus B_{\delta}}\sigma\Delta_{g}\sigma\,\mathrm{d}V_{g}

\displaystyle=-\gamma^{2}\underbrace{\int_{\partial B_{\delta}}\log r(\partial_{r}\log r)\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{g}}_{A}-\gamma\underbrace{\int_{\partial B_{\delta}}\varphi(\partial_{r}\log r)\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{g}}_{B}-\gamma\underbrace{\int_{\partial B_{\delta}}\log r(\partial_{r}\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{g}}_{C}

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\underbrace{\int_{\partial B_{\delta}}\varphi(\partial_{r}\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{g}}_{D}-\int_{\Sigma\setminus B_{\delta}}\sigma\Delta_{g}\sigma\,\mathrm{d}V_{g}.

By the assumptions (
4.7
) on
\varphi
and by (
4.20
), we have

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\log\delta\cdot\frac{1}{\delta}(2\pi\delta+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{3}))=2\pi\log\delta+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}\log\delta),

(4.22)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{\partial B_{\delta}}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{g}=2\pi\varphi(z_{0})+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}),

(4.23)

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\log\delta\int_{\partial B_{\delta}}\partial_{r}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{g}=\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2\gamma+2}\log\delta),

(4.24)

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2\gamma+2}).

(4.25)

Adding them all up, we obtain the result. Note
2\gamma+2
may be small but is positive, so
\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2\gamma+2}\log\delta)=o(1)
.
∎

Corollary 4.7
.

In the set-up as above, let
\widetilde{g}
be a generalised conformal metric representing

with

, written in the form (

is a smooth conformal metric on

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(z_{0},\widetilde{g})}|\nabla_{g}\sigma|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}V_{g}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\pi\gamma^{2}}{\gamma+1}\big[\log(\varepsilon)+\log(\gamma+1)-\varphi(z_{0})\big]-2\pi\gamma\varphi(z_{0})-\int_{\Sigma\setminus z_{0}}\sigma\Delta_{g}\sigma\,\mathrm{d}V_{g}+o(1)

(4.26)

as
\varepsilon\to 0
, where
B_{\varepsilon}(z_{0},\widetilde{g})
denotes the geodesic ball around
z_{0}
of radius
\varepsilon
under the metric
\widetilde{g}
.

Proof.

This is because
B_{\varepsilon}(z_{0},\widetilde{g})
has radius
\delta(\varepsilon)\sim((\gamma+1)\varepsilon\textrm{e}^{-\varphi(z_{0})})^{1/(\gamma+1)}
under the metric

Lemma 4.8
.

In the set-up as lemma
4.6
, pick further
h\in C^{\infty}(\Sigma)
. Then we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\int_{\Sigma\setminus B_{\delta}(z_{0},g)}\big(\left\langle\nabla_{g}h,\nabla_{g}\sigma\right\rangle_{g}+(\Delta_{g}h)\sigma\big)\,\mathrm{d}V_{g}

\displaystyle=0,

(4.27)

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\int_{\Sigma\setminus B_{\delta}(z_{0},g)}\big(\left\langle\nabla_{g}h,\nabla_{g}\sigma\right\rangle_{g}+h(\Delta_{g}\sigma)\big)\,\mathrm{d}V_{g}

\displaystyle=-2\pi\gamma h(z_{0}).

(4.28)

Here for (

\varphi
be bounded over

in the expression (
4.19
) for

(\left\langle\nabla_{g}h,\nabla_{g}\sigma\right\rangle_{g}+h(\Delta_{g}\sigma))\mathrm{d}V_{g}

\big(\langle\nabla_{\textrm{e}^{2\varphi}g}h,\nabla_{\textrm{e}^{2\varphi}g}\sigma\rangle_{\textrm{e}^{2\varphi}g}+h(\Delta_{\textrm{e}^{2\varphi}g}\sigma)\big)\mathrm{d}V_{\textrm{e}^{2\varphi}g}=\big(\left\langle\nabla_{g}h,\nabla_{g}\sigma\right\rangle_{g}+h(\Delta_{g}\sigma)\big)\mathrm{d}V_{g}

for any

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\Sigma\setminus B_{\delta}(z_{0},g)}\big(\langle\nabla_{\widetilde{g}}h,\nabla_{\widetilde{g}}\sigma\rangle_{\widetilde{g}}+h(\Delta_{\widetilde{g}}\sigma)\big)\,\mathrm{d}V_{\widetilde{g}}=-2\pi\gamma h(z_{0}),

(4.30)

where

is as defined in corollary

Proof.

We apply Green-Stokes. The first integral boils down to

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{\delta}}(-\partial_{r}h)\sigma\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{g}

\displaystyle=\gamma\int_{\partial B_{\delta}}(-\partial_{r}h)\log r\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{g}+\int_{\partial B_{\delta}}(-\partial_{r}h)\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{g}=\mathcal{O}(\delta\log\delta)+\mathcal{O}(\delta),

and the second to

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{\delta}}h(-\partial_{r}\sigma)\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{g}

\displaystyle=-\gamma\int_{\partial B_{\delta}}h\cdot\frac{1}{r}\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{g}+\int_{\partial B_{\delta}}h(-\partial_{r}\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{g}

\displaystyle=-2\pi\gamma h(z_{0})+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2\min\{\gamma,0\}+2}),

as

, by (

∎

The following lemma is not used in the main proof, but is important regarding remark

Lemma 4.9
.

Under the assumptions at the beginning of this section, let
0<\delta_{1}(\varepsilon)<\delta_{2}(\varepsilon)
be two positive functions of
\varepsilon
such that
\delta_{i}(\varepsilon)\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}

,

\delta_{2}/\delta_{1}\to Q>0

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{B_{\delta_{2}(\varepsilon)}(z_{0},g)\setminus B_{\delta_{1}(\varepsilon)}(z_{0},g)}|\nabla_{g}\sigma|_{g}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}V_{g}=2\pi\gamma^{2}\log Q.

Proof.

We abbreviate the disks
B_{\delta_{i}(\varepsilon)}(z_{0},g)
as
B(\delta_{i})
,
i=1
,
2
. Again integrating by parts we have,

\int_{B(\delta_{2})\setminus B(\delta_{1})}|\nabla_{g}\sigma|_{g}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}V_{g}=-\int_{B(\delta_{2})\setminus B(\delta_{1})}\sigma\Delta_{g}\sigma\,\mathrm{d}V_{g}+\int_{\partial B(\delta_{2})}\sigma(\partial_{r}\sigma)\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{g}-\int_{\partial B(\delta_{1})}\sigma(\partial_{r}\sigma)\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{g}.

(4.32)

Since
\sigma
is locally integrable and
\Delta_{g}\sigma
is bounded on
\Sigma\setminus\{z_{0}\}
, we have

\int_{B(\delta_{2})\setminus B(\delta_{1})}\sigma\Delta_{g}\sigma\,\mathrm{d}V_{g}\quad\xlongrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\quad 0.

(4.33)

Next by (
4.19
) and (
4.7
),

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B(\delta_{2})}\varphi(\partial_{r}\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{g},\penalty\ \int_{\partial B(\delta_{1})}\varphi(\partial_{r}\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{g}=\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2\gamma+2})

\displaystyle\xlongrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\quad 0,

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B(\delta_{2})}\varphi(\partial_{r}\log r)\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{g},\penalty\ \int_{\partial B(\delta_{1})}\varphi(\partial_{r}\log r)\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{g}

\displaystyle\xlongrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\quad 2\pi\varphi(z_{0}).

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B(\delta_{2})}\log r(\partial_{r}\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{g},\penalty\ \int_{\partial B(\delta_{1})}\log r(\partial_{r}\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{g}=\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2\gamma+2}\log\delta)

\displaystyle\xlongrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\quad 0.

Therefore the only thing left is

\int_{\partial B(\delta_{2})}\frac{\log r}{r}\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{g}-\int_{\partial B(\delta_{1})}\frac{\log r}{r}\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{g}\sim 2\pi\log\Big(\frac{\delta_{2}}{\delta_{1}}\Big)\quad\xlongrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\quad 2\pi\log Q.

(4.34)

Adding up all the above, we obtain the result.
∎

Regularized Curvature and Anomaly

In this subsection we note another kind of regularized anomaly which concerns smooth (bounded) scaling of a singular metric, rather than singular scaling of a smooth metric which was in some sense what we did above. This quantity will also play a role in the main result proposition
6.1
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\Sigma
be a closed Riemann surface and
\widetilde{g}
a generalized conformal metric representing
D=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\gamma_{j}z_{j}

Proof.

Choose a smooth background conformal metric

and write
\widetilde{g}=\textrm{e}^{2\sigma}g
,
\sigma\in C^{\infty}(\Sigma\setminus\supp D)
. As in lemma
4.8

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma\setminus\supp D}(|\nabla_{\widetilde{g}}h|_{\widetilde{g}}^{2}+2K_{\widetilde{g}}h)\,\mathrm{d}V_{\widetilde{g}}=\int_{\Sigma\setminus\supp D}(|\nabla_{g}h|_{g}^{2}+2(-\Delta_{g}\sigma+K_{g})h)\,\mathrm{d}V_{g}<\infty,

(5.7)

∎

Remark 5.1
.

As a by-product, we also see that the RHS of (

), which is expressed in terms of the background smooth metric

, is independent of

.

Definition 5.1
.

In the situation as lemma

, we call (

) the

of

against
\widetilde{g}

Remark 5.2
.

The distribution (or current, more precisely) on
\Sigma
which we denote by

Comparison with Cut-off Methods

There are existing methods in the literature that essentially define the same quantity as (
2.25
), but instead of removing the balls

entirely, they introduce a regularized metric inside. Such ideas are found in the incomplete notes of Zamolodchikov et al.
[
43

(page 86), as well as in Eskin, Kontsevich, and Zorich
[
15
]
, section 3.6. The cut-off method used in the latter is more mathematically rigorous, being smooth, and it avoids generating additional singularities along each
\partial B_{\varepsilon}
for
\nabla\sigma
. In this subsection, we note that our definition
2.8
yields the same result as in
[
15
]

[
15
]

For each fixed
\delta>\delta^{\prime}>0
we consider a smooth function
\sreg_{\delta,\delta^{\prime}}(r)
such that

\sreg_{\delta,\delta^{\prime}}(r)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\log r&\textrm{for }r\geqslant\delta,\\
\log\delta&\textrm{for }0\leqslant r\leqslant\delta^{\prime},\end{array}\right.

(5.9)

as well as

\log\delta^{\prime}\leqslant\sreg_{\delta,\delta^{\prime}}(r)\leqslant\log\delta,\quad\quad\textrm{for }0\leqslant r\leqslant\delta.

(5.10)

and

\left|\partial_{r}\sreg_{\delta,\delta^{\prime}}(r)\right|\leqslant C\delta^{\prime-1},\quad\quad\textrm{for }0\leqslant r\leqslant\delta,

(5.11)

for some constant

independent of
\delta,\delta^{\prime}
.
Such a function can be constructed using a smooth cut-off function

for which
f(t)\equiv 0
for

,
0<f(t)<1
for

,

for

, and posing

\sreg_{\delta,\delta^{\prime}}(r)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\log\delta+f\Big(\frac{r-\delta^{\prime}}{\delta-\delta^{\prime}}\Big)\log\frac{r}{\delta}.

(5.12)

Now given a closed Riemann surface
\Sigma
and
\widetilde{g}
a generalized conformal metric representing

with
\gamma_{j}>-1
, as well as a smooth background conformal metric

such that around each singularity

holds (

), and the regular metric potentials

satisfy the Troyanov conditions (

), we introduce the regularized smooth metric

(for

\exp\big(2\gamma_{j}\cdot\sreg_{\delta_{j},\delta^{\prime}_{j}}(r_{j})\big)\textrm{e}^{2\varphi_{j}}g&\textrm{on }U_{j},\end{array}\right.

(5.13)

where

, the

Lemma 5.3
.

In the setting as above, write
g_{\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}}=\textrm{e}^{2\sigma_{\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}}}g
now with
\sigma_{\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}}\in C^{\infty}(\Sigma)

Since

on

Remark 5.3
.

This lemma specifically explains why no boundary terms are included in the anomaly formula from definition

\Sigma

Remark 5.4

Imaginably, one could allow even more flexibility in the choice of the cut-off function

to gain even better convergence in (

. Then the surface
\check{\Sigma}_{d}
is topologically still
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
but equipped with the pull-back of the Fubini-Study metric under a holomorphic map
f_{d}:\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\longrightarrow\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
which ramifies exactly at
0
and
z_{0}
both with order
d
. Such a map could be given by (but not related to final result)

f_{d}(z)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\frac{z_{0}\big(\frac{z}{z-z_{0}}\big)^{d}}{\big(\frac{z}{z-z_{0}}\big)^{d}-1}=\frac{z_{0}z^{d}}{z^{d}-(z-z_{0})^{d}}.

(6.13)

Now we apply a smooth scaling
\textrm{e}^{2h}
with
h=\log(L/2\pi)
on the equator, while making
\textrm{e}^{2h}g_{\mathrm{FS}}
flat in a thin neighborhood of the equator, in accordance with the requirement for vacuum state explained in example
3.3
. The latter is always possible for Fubini-Study since
4(1+|z|^{2})^{-2}=|z|^{-2}+\mathcal{O}((1-|z|)^{2})
. Then by proposition
6.3
, lemma
2.1
(and remark
6.3
) we obtain

\displaystyle\ttr_{\mathcal{H}_{A}}\big(\ttr_{A^{c}}(\rho)^{d}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{Z}(\check{\Sigma}_{d})}{\mathcal{Z}(\check{\Sigma})^{d}}=C\textrm{e}^{-h(0)\Delta_{d}}\textrm{e}^{-h(z_{0})\Delta_{d}}\sin\Big(\frac{1}{2}d_{\mathrm{FS}}(0,z_{0})\Big)^{-2\Delta_{d}}

\displaystyle=C\Big(\frac{L}{\pi}\sin\frac{\pi\ell}{L}\Big)^{-2\Delta_{d}},

with
\Delta_{d}=\frac{c}{12}(d-\frac{1}{d})
, giving us the result.
∎

Comments on Relation to Literature

Remark 6.4

(comparison with a result of V. Kalvin
[
21
]
)
.

We note here that V. Kalvin has obtained a closely related result in
[
21
]
corollary 1.3(1) for the
\zeta
-determinant of the Friederichs Laplacian under the conical metric with zero “boundary condition” at the cone points. As far as the Polyakov formula is concerned, the
\zeta
-determinant is no different from a general CFT partition function in the sense of definition
2.1
, except for a constant. So our proposition
6.1
coincides with the case of his result when the two divisors coincide. If they don’t coincide, one could readily derive the corresponding result out of our method following lemma
6.2
. However, in that case, the constants (last term in
[
21
]
eqn. (1.8)) would be different, as our lemma
6.2
only agrees with
[
21
]
eqn. (1.1) upto the integral term and the term involving the regular metric potential. Supposedly, the extra constants in
[
21
]
come from the specific Friederichs extension.

On a deeper level, the proof of
[
21
]
proceeds also by removing disks but then using the BFK decomposition formula. One key point is to show that the blow-up order of
\log\detz\Delta_{D_{\varepsilon}(z_{j})}
of the Friederichs Dirichlet Laplacian
\Delta_{D_{\varepsilon}(z_{j})}
on the shrinking disks
D_{\varepsilon}(z_{j})
centered at the cone points with the singular metric, added up, matches exactly with the blow-up order of the
\log\detz
of the Dirichlet Laplacian on their complement, as the disks shrink (as the contributions from D-to-N maps cancel). In this regard we keenly observe that the cancellation mechanism as shown in
[
21
]
eqn. (2.28) is
different
from ours. The radii of Kalvin’s disks were measured under the background smooth metric. For us, on the other hand, it is important to align the rate of shrinking with the singular metric itself (i.e. measure the disks using the singular metric) in two respects. First to obtain the invariance of the definition of the renormalized partition function as in lemma
5.1
, and secondly to obtain the correct scaling dimensions in the final result. A more detailed understanding of the relation of the two methods seems desirable, especially considering that the BFK formula is closely related to Segal’s gluing axioms for QFT/CFT
[
24
,
17
]
, and that the correlation functions are (conjecturally) the “limits” of propagators associated to the complements of the shrinking disks (
[
23
]
section 3).

Remark 6.5

(relation to Aldana, Kirsten and Rowlett
[
2
]

.

As the authors have themselves remarked in relation to
[
21
]
, they also obtained the same result as above for the
\zeta
-determinant (
[
2

eqn. (1.5)). Their method is based on asymptotic analysis of the heat trace for which related results were obtained earlier
[
25

. For the latter, they employed a blow-up (geometric) technique and the cone contribution term (main interest of this article) seems to ultimately come from computations done to an infinite exact cone which has a longer history (see their appendix A for further references). Last but not least, we note that

(0,2\pi)

6.3
).

Proof of lemma
4.5
.

We denote by
\nabla_{v}
the Levi-Civita covariant derivative in the direction
v
. Since we are concerned with only one metric
g
we omit it from the notations. Take the geodesic polar coordinates
(r,\theta)
around
z_{0}
. Then

g=\mathrm{d}r^{2}+\omega^{2}(r)\mathrm{d}\theta^{2}
where
\omega(r)=|\partial_{\theta}|
. From the expression of
\Delta
in polar coordinates we have

\displaystyle\Delta\log r=\frac{1}{\omega(r)}\partial_{r}\left(\omega(r)\partial_{r}\log r\right)=-\frac{1}{r^{2}}+\frac{\partial_{r}\omega(r)}{r\,\omega(r)}.

Either noting the fact that
\partial_{\theta}
is a Jacobi field along
\theta=\textrm{const}
connecting
z_{0}
to
z
(
[
13
]
page 115), or otherwise, we have a Taylor expansion

|\partial_{\theta}|=r+\mathcal{O}(r^{3}),\quad r\to 0.

(A.1)

This gives

\frac{\partial_{r}|\partial_{\theta}|}{|\partial_{\theta}|}=\frac{1}{r}+\mathcal{O}(r),\quad r\to 0,

(A.2)

yielding the result.
∎

Lemma A.1
.

Let
(\Sigma,g)
be a Riemannian surface with smooth metric
g
, and fix
z_{0}\in\Sigma
. Denote
r(z):=d_{g}(z,z_{0})
. Then for any
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(U)
where the neighborhood
U
lies within the injectivity radius of
z_{0}
,

\int_{\Sigma}\log r(-\Delta_{g}\psi)\,\mathrm{d}V_{g}=\int_{\Sigma\setminus z_{0}}\psi(-\Delta_{g}\log r)\,\mathrm{d}V_{g}-2\pi\psi(z_{0}).

(A.3)

Proof.

Pick the metric disk
B_{\varepsilon}(z_{0})
with radius
\varepsilon>0
at
z_{0}
. Then Green’s formula gives

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(z_{0})}\big[\log r(\Delta_{g}\psi)-\psi(\Delta_{g}\log r)\big]\,\mathrm{d}V_{g}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\partial B_{\varepsilon}}\big[\log r(\partial_{r}\psi)-\psi(\partial_{r}\log r)\big]\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{g}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon\log\varepsilon)+\psi(z_{0})\frac{1}{\varepsilon}2\pi\varepsilon+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon),

as
\varepsilon\to 0
, since
\psi
is smooth and (
4.20
).
∎

Remark A.1
.

More precisely we have the equality of currents
\Delta_{g}\log r\,\mathrm{d}V_{g}=\Delta_{g}\log r|_{U\setminus z_{0}}\mathrm{d}V_{g}+2\pi\delta_{z_{0}}
on

Lemma A.2
.

Fix
r(z):=d_{g}(z,z_{0})
and now let
g_{1}=\textrm{e}^{2h}g
,
h\in C^{\infty}(\Sigma)
, and
r_{1}(z):=d_{g_{1}}(z,z_{0})
. Then

\Delta_{g_{1}}\log r-\Delta_{g_{1}}\log r_{1}\in L^{1}(U,g_{1}).

(A.4)

Namely, as a distribution, it agrees with an integrable function when paired with
\mathrm{d}V_{g_{1}}
.

Proof.

The point is that the current
\Delta_{g_{1}}\log r\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{1}}

\Delta_{g}\log r\,\mathrm{d}V_{g}
, because of their definitions and conformal covariances (

\Delta_{g_{1}}\log r|_{U\setminus z_{0}}-\Delta_{g_{1}}\log r_{1}|_{U\setminus z_{0}}
which is in fact
L^{\infty}(U,g_{1})
.
∎
