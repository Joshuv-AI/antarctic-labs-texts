Observed Maternal Positive Affect Responsivity Prospectively Predicts Lower Internalizing Problems in Toddlers of Mothers With Depression: A Dynamic Systems Analysis
Authors: Julianne M Griffith, Benjamin Harrington, Lindsay Taraban, Erika E Forbes, Judith K Morgan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractToddlers of mothers with depression are at risk for wide-ranging negative outcomes across development, including early emerging internalizing and externalizing problems. These children may be especially sensitive to individual differences in (positive) affective parenting, with implications for enduring psychosocial outcomes. Maternal attunement to child positive emotions may be an important protective factor buffering against risk for psychopathology in vulnerable youth. Illuminating the role of such subtle, moment-to-moment parenting behaviors in attenuating risk across early development is critical to promoting child enduring wellbeing. Thus, this study used state space grid (SSG) analyses to evaluate associations between three indices of maternal positive emotional attunement (duration of shared positive affect, latency of return to shared positive affect, and maternal positive affect responsivity) and child prospective internalizing and externalizing problems in dyads with (n = 38) and without (n = 62) current maternal depression. Mother and toddler positive affect was coded in 5s epochs during a 3-minute play task, and mothers reported on toddler internalizing problems at baseline and one year later. Findings revealed a significant interaction between maternal depression and maternal positive affect responsivity in predicting prospective child internalizing (β=-0.27, padj=0.03). Greater maternal positive affect responsivity predicted lower child internalizing problems one year later among mothers with depression (β=-0.28, padj=0.04) but not those without depression (β=0.14, padj=0.24). Results suggest that maternal positive affect responsivity buffers against future internalizing problems among toddlers of mothers with depression and suggest that maternal attunement to child positive affect may be an important target for preventive intervention.

Maternal depression is a well-established risk factor for child transdiagnostic psychopathology, with evidence implicating maternal depression in risk for both internalizing and externalizing problems across development (Goodman et al., 2011). Importantly, emotional and behavioral difficulties among children of mothers with depression are likely to onset early in development, with potentially cascading effects across the lifespan (Goodman et al., 2011; Weissman et al., 1987, 2016). It is important to note, however, that maternal depression is not deterministic, and there is considerable variability in emotional and behavioral outcomes among offspring of mothers with depression–including good mental health (Collishaw et al., 2016; Padaigaitė-Gulbinienė et al., 2024; Reuben & Shaw, 2015). Indeed, some evidence suggests that positive parenting behaviors may play an important role in buffering against offspring risk for emotional problems in families affected by maternal depression (Burkhouse & Kujawa, 2023; Morgan et al., 2022; Natsuaki et al., 2010; Padaigaitė-Gulbinienė et al., 2024; Silva-Rodrigues et al., 2022). Given epidemiological evidence indicating high rates of depression among mothers (Ertel et al., 2011; Shorey et al., 2018), identifying modifiable parenting behaviors that might attenuate associations between maternal depression and offspring emotional dysfunction is therefore a notable public health priority.

Maternal attunement to children’s positive emotions—observed as maternal maintenance of shared positive affect, latency of return to shared positive affect, and sequential matching of children’s positive affect (“maternal positive affect responsivity”)—has been found to promote emotional and behavioral regulation, as well as the development of social competencies among children as young as infancy and toddlerhood (Feldman, 2007; Harrist & Waugh, 2002). Specifically, maternal attunement during the earliest stages of life is believed to shape fundamental physiological systems underlying diverse socioemotional capabilities including emotion regulation, social bonding, and response to stress, with downstream implications for offspring enduring mental health (Feldman, 2015). As children grow and mature, maternal positive emotional attunement, and particularly maternal positive affect responsivity, continues to serve as a powerful socializing influence, scaffolding children’s effective regulation of positive emotions and potentially buffering against risk for the development of psychopathology (Fredrick et al., 2019; Gentzler & Root, 2019; Kochanska & Aksan, 1995; Yi et al., 2016). Mothers who respond to their children’s joy and enthusiasm in equal measure, and engage their children in sustained positive affectivity, may provide children with foundational skills in flexibly mobilizing positive emotions to enhance resilience.

Maternal positive emotional attunement during toddlerhood, specifically, may be especially valuable as this developmental stage is characterized by increasing child autonomy and emotional lability in the context of relatively immature emotion regulation capabilities (Denham, 1998). As toddlers seek to practice emerging skills in managing their own increasingly complex emotions, the mother-child relationship assumes new importance as a key site for the provision of co-regulatory support. Positive emotional exchanges, in particular, represent critical sites of emotional development, wherein (with maternal support) children acquire foundational social skills, emotional competencies, and the capacity for effective physiological regulation (Feldman, 2012). Moreover, difficulties in emotional and behavioral regulation observed during the toddler years have been found to portend persistent socioemotional difficulties, predicting internalizing and externalizing symptoms into the early childhood period (Briggs-Gowan et al., 2006; Mathiesen et al., 2009). For this reason, maternal scaffolding of toddler emotional competencies via displays of positive emotional attunement may be critical to promoting enduring socioemotional wellbeing (Harrist & Waugh, 2002; Noroña-Zhou & Tung, 2021).

Young children of mothers with depression may be well-positioned to experience benefits of positive, attuned parenting. That is, these children, who are known to be at heightened risk for wide-ranging psychosocial difficulties (Goodman et al., 2011) may have the most to gain from engaged and responsive maternal behaviors. Indeed, systematic reviews demonstrate that positive parenting behaviors, broadly defined, promote positive outcomes among offspring of mothers with depression (Silva-Rodrigues et al., 2022), and some preliminary empirical work finds that associations between positive parenting and favorable outcomes may be enhanced among at-risk offspring relative to their low-risk peers (Kujawa et al., 2015). Such effects of positive parenting behaviors have been observed across timescales and units of analysis. Adaptive parenting (including maternal positive emotional attunement) has been found to associate in-the-moment with strengthened physiological and neural coregulation (Hendrix et al., 2018; Morgan et al., 2023), and over time with greater offspring neural sensitivity to reward (Kujawa et al., 2015; Morgan et al., 2022), improvements in child psychological functioning (Swartz et al., 2018), and resilience to the development of transdiagnostic psychopathology (Brennan et al., 2003). Together, this body of findings suggests that maternal positive emotional attunement may function as a buffer, attenuating well-established associations between maternal depression and disruptions in offspring psychosocial development.

Maternal Attunement: The Importance of a Multidimensional Approach

Moreover, theory would suggest that different dimensions of maternal attunement may associate with relatively unique emotional benefits, and recent empirical work has supported relatively unique associations between different facets of maternal attunement and maternal depression (e.g., Morrow et al., 2021; Vanwoerden et al., 2024), as well as child outcomes (e.g., Griffith & Hankin, 2025; Moran et al., 2019; Nelis et al., 2019; Raval et al., 2019; Shin et al., 2023; Yi et al., 2016). Theories of affective coregulation/synchrony, for example, propose that the extent to which mothers contemporaneously mirror their children’s displays of positive affect promotes children’s physiological capacities for self-regulation and enhances attachment, with implications for the development of adaptive emotion regulation (Feldman, 2007; Leclère et al., 2014). Further, maternal mirroring of children’s positive emotions can also sustain children’s positive emotions beyond the emotion-eliciting cue or event, as mothers’ microsocial affective behaviors reinforce similar behaviors in their offspring (Hatfield et al., 1993). Such contemporaneous affective state matching would be observed as a greater duration of shared positive affect within an interaction. In contrast, from a dynamic systems perspective, dyads’ ability to move freely in and out of shared positive affective states, observed as shorter latencies of return to shared positive affect, may index dyadic socioemotional flexibility, which is hypothesized to promote offsprings’ ability to adaptively respond to a host of novel psychosocial challenges and demands (Hollenstein, 2015; Hollenstein et al., 2013).

Theories of emotion socialization propose that maternal positive affect responsivity may play a particularly important role in upregulating children’s experience of positive emotions and promoting the acquisition of adaptive intrapersonal positive emotion regulation skills across development (Eisenberg et al., 1998; Gentzler & Root, 2019; Morris et al., 2007). When mothers dynamically respond to children’s spontaneous displays of positive affect with their own interest, enthusiasm, and joy, they reinforce the value of positive emotional experience within the family, teaching children that such emotions are acceptable, appreciated, and worthy of display.

Maternal Depression as a Moderator

These different facets of maternal positive emotional attunement may be especially impactful in families affected by maternal depression, as depression associates with broadband impairments in positive emotional experience (Pizzagalli, 2014; Vanderlind et al., 2020), as well as more specific impairments in maternal positive emotion socialization behaviors (Schmitt et al., 2025; Morrow et al., 2021; Granat et al., 2017). In an environment of potential positive emotional scarcity, mothers’ ability to capitalize on children’s self-initiated positive affective displays may be critical to reducing risk for the intergenerational transmission of positive emotional deficits. Indeed, some past research demonstrates that it may be the interaction of maternal depression and specific maternal positive affect socialization behaviors that is influential to child emotional outcomes (Hooper et al., 2015; Morgan et al., 2022). For example, work by Hooper and colleagues (2015) using a latent profile approach demonstrated that unique configurations of maternal depression, maternal emotionality, and maternal emotional expression predicted concurrent preschooler internalizing and externalizing problems. Of note, this research has been cross-sectional in nature and predominantly focused on older children, and the ways in which different indices of maternal positive emotional attunement interplay with maternal depression to predict change in child internalizing and externalizing problems across toddlerhood is not well understood. This is an important gap, as toddlerhood is a sensitive period of development during which foundational emotional competencies are developing, and positively valenced mother-child interactions represent a key site of emotion socialization and development (Feldman, 2007; Harrist & Waugh, 2002).

A Dynamic Systems Approach

The preponderance of research evaluating interactive associations between maternal depression and positive parenting behaviors as they relate to child emotional and behavioral outcomes has evaluated positive parenting in relatively broad terms using global measures (e.g., parenting warmth, support, authoritativeness, etc.; see Silva-Rodrigues et al., 2022). Dynamic systems theories of development, and related methodologies such as state space grids (SSGs), offer a useful framework for representing the role of moment-to-moment dyadic emotional processes in shaping risk and resilience among vulnerable youth. Specifically, these theories propose that the parent-child dyad constitutes a dynamic system characterized by “attractor” states, or recurrent patterns of coordinated affect to which the dyad frequently returns (Butler, 2011; Lewis, 2000). “Attractors” can be empirically measured using SSGs, a sophisticated statistical method for assessing both the content and dynamic structure of parent-child interactions as they unfold across time within an interaction (Lamay et al., 2004). For a dyad high in maternal positive emotional attunement, shared positive affect would be expected to function as an attractor. That is, highly attuned dyads would be expected to spend a greater proportion of an interaction in shared positive affect and demonstrate a shorter latency to return to shared positive affect. Moreover, highly attuned mothers would be expected to display heightened responsivity to moments of child positive affect, observed as a higher conditional probability of mothers entering into shared positive affect with their child after their child displays positive affectivity. Put another way, attuned mothers are expected to follow their children’s positive affective lead, reciprocating their positive emotions, sustaining shared positive emotionality, and repairing quickly when ruptures to shared positive emotionality occur.

Thus, moving beyond broad, global measures of parenting to understand the ways in which these fine-grained indices of mothers’ in-vivo engagement their toddler-aged children may buffer against risk for psychopathology among offspring of mothers with depression is warranted in order to refine theories of intergenerational risk and identify behaviorally-specific, tractable targets for family-based interventions.

The Present Study

The aim of the present study was to examine prospective associations between maternal positive emotional attunement and toddler internalizing and externalizing problems in families with and without maternal depression. Specifically, we tested differential facets of maternal attunement—observed during a 3-minute face-to-face play task—as predictors of toddler internalizing and externalizing symptoms one year later, and how this differed based on mothers’ current depression status. Rooted in existing theory and research (see Feldman, 2007, 2015) and informed by dynamic systems frameworks (Butler, 2011; Lewis, 2000), we examined three distinct elements of maternal positive emotional attunement: (1) proportion of the mother-child interaction spent in shared positive affect, (2) latency to return to shared positive affect, and (3) maternal responsivity to child positive affect. Building on previous work demonstrating the protective power of positive parenting among school-aged children of mothers with depression (Silva-Rodrigues et al., 2022; Kujawa et al., 2015), we hypothesized that each element of maternal attunement would interact with maternal depression to predict child outcomes, such that greater proportion of shared positive affect, shorter latency of return to shared positive affect, and higher maternal positive affect responsivity would predict lower child internalizing and externalizing scores at one-year follow-up, with larger effect sizes in dyads with (versus without) current maternal depression.

Methods

Participants

Participants included 100 mothers (mean age = 33.37 years, SD = 4.54 years, range = 22 to 45 years; 38% depressed) and their 1- to 3-year-old children (mean age = 24.97 months, SD = 10.43 months, range = 10 to 42 months; 54% girls) drawn from a larger study (N = 131 dyads) focusing on longitudinal changes in child neural response to reward-related stimuli. Mothers were recruited between 2018 and 2023 in and around Pittsburgh, PA. Dyads were included in the present study if they completed relevant questionnaire measures at Year 1 (baseline) and Year 2, as well as the videorecorded parent-child play task at the Year 1 visit. All children were the biological children of participating mothers, and were typically developing, without neurological problems. Mothers in the depression group were included if they met criteria for a current depressive disorder, as described in detail below. Mothers were excluded if they met criteria for bipolar disorder or psychotic disorder. Mothers in the non-depressed group had no lifetime history of psychiatric disorder. In terms of race, 2% of children identified as Asian/Asian American, 15% as Black/African American, 70% as white/European American, and 11% as multiracial or of another racial background.1 9% of children identified as Hispanic or Latine/o. Most mothers were married (73%) or living with a partner (9%); 12% percent of mothers reported being single parents. Regarding maternal education, 73% of mothers attained a bachelor’s degree or higher. Participants included in the present analyses differed in sex (X2[1] = 5.91, p=.015) and maternal depression status (X2[1] = 6.74, p=.009) compared to participants with missing data such that girls and dyads without a maternal history of depression were relatively over-represented in the present sample. Included participants did not differ from excluded participants based on child age.

Procedure

At baseline, mothers completed informed consent for their own and their child’s participation and were interviewed on their current and prior history of depression and other psychiatric illnesses. Following the interview, they completed questionnaire measures regarding their child’s internalizing and externalizing problems. On a subsequent visit (M = 40.76 days later; SD = 31.94 days, Range = 0–128 days)2, mothers engaged in a series of interactive activities in the lab with their child. Following a 10-min warm-up period of floor-play, mothers and their children were seated at a small table and engaged in a series of tasks together, including a 3-minute face-to-face play task. During this task, mothers and their children were seated facing one another and engaged in face-to-face play (2 min using puppets; 1 min toy-free). Previous work has found that relative to periods of free play, structured play contexts solicit more variable mother and child (affective) behaviors, and patterns of mother-child affective engagement with one another during structured play correlates more strongly with toddler psychosocial outcomes (Krijnen et al., 2023). The interaction was video recorded from multiple angles to ensure visualization of both the mother’s and child’s face throughout the interaction, and the video recordings were used for affective coding. Approximately 1 year after their initial baseline visit, mothers provided follow-up questionnaires assessing child internalizing and externalizing problems.

Measures

Maternal Depression

Maternal current and lifetime history of depression was assessed using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV (SCID-IV; First, 2015). Interviews were conducted by Bachelors’ and Masters’ level trained interviewers, and 22.1% (n = 29) of interviews were double coded by a licensed clinical psychologist to ensure reliability (Kappa = 0.90 for current major depressive episode; Kappa = 1.00 for current dysthymia). Maternal depression was additionally assessed on the day of the mother-child interaction visit using parent self-report on the Center for Epidemiological Studies-Depression scale (CES-D; Radloff, 1977). Mothers were asked to rate the frequency with which they have experienced a number of depressive symptoms (e.g., depressed mood, sleep difficulties, feelings of worthlessness) over the past week using a 4-point Likert scale (0 = rarely or none of the time [< 1 day]; 3 = most or all of the time [5–7 days]). Scores of 16 and above are interpreted to indicate a high likelihood of clinical depression (Vilagut et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2011). Internal consistency for the CES-D in this sample was good (α = 0.80).

Mothers were classified as depressed if they met criteria for current Major Depressive Disorder, Dysthymia, or Depressive Disorder NOS on the SCID-IV and/or had elevated scores (i.e., ≥ 16) on the CES-D. This relatively inclusive operationalization of depression is consistent with taxometric work demonstrating that depression is distributed dimensionally at the latent level (e.g., Hankin et al., 2005; Liu, 2016), as well as meta-analytic findings showing that maternal depression correlates similarly with child internalizing and externalizing regardless of assessment modality (Goodman et al., 2011). A total of 38 mothers in the present sample met criteria for depression based on one or more of these criteria.3 The remaining 62 mothers were characterized as non-depressed, as they did not meet current or lifetime criteria for depression or any other psychiatric illness on the SCID-IV, and had a score lower than 16 on the CES-D.

Child Internalizing and Externalizing Problems

Child internalizing and externalizing problems were assessed using maternal report on the Child Behavior Checklist for ages 1.5-5 (CBCL; Achenbach & Rescorla, 2000) at baseline and 1 year follow-up. The CBCL comprises 99 items assessing varying emotional and behavioral problems. For each item, mothers were asked to rate the extent to which a statement described their child over the preceding two months using a 3-point scale (0 = not true [as far as you know] to 2 = very true or often true). The internalizing problems subscale includes 36 items assessing varying mood and anxiety difficulties including “shows panic for no good reason,” “upset by new people or situations,” and “worries.” The externalizing problems subscale includes 24 items assessing varying behavioral and attentional difficulties including “can’t concentrate,” “can’t stand waiting; wants everything now,” and “uncooperative.” Raw scores were used for the purposes of the present analyses to preserve the full range of the scale and maximize variability captured. The CBCL is a widely used measure of child internalizing and externalizing and demonstrates strong psychometric properties including validity and reliability (Achenbach, 2018). Internal consistency for each subscale was good across timepoints in the present sample (α = 0.82-0.90).

Affective Coding

Independent observers naïve to study hypotheses and mother depression status coded mother and child affect in 5-s epochs or ‘moments’ for the 3-min face to face play task (36 total epochs). Five-second epochs were chosen to maximize observation of contingent behavior within mother-child dyads as prior work has demonstrated that lagged effects are more significant in this window (Beebe et al., 2010). Mothers and children were coded separately by trained coders on the same moment. Positive affect (present or absent) was coded for each mother and child based on an adapted version of the AFFEX coding system (Izard et al., 1983; Morgan et al., 2020, 2023). Specifically, positive affect was coded dichotomously as the presence or absence of vocal (e.g., ‘yay!’, lilting voices), facial (e.g., smiling, brightening of eyes), and bodily (e.g., clapping hands) expressions of happy/joyful affect. Coders were trained to 80% agreement by a primary coder who had graduate-level training in the AFFEX coding system and periodically retrained to prevent coder drift. Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) reliability calculations conducted in R (ten Hove et al., 2024) indicated that reliability for both parent and child codes was excellent (child ICC = 0.96; mother IC = 0.96).

Dyadic Positive Affect Indices

Indices of dyadic positive affect processes were calculated from affective coding data using SSG analysis implemented in GridWare version 1.15 (Lamay et al., 2004). SSGs represent multivariate affective timeseries data using a 2-D grid based on dynamic systems theory, with mother positive affect (present, absent) on one axis and child positive affect (present, absent) on the other. Each epoch is included as a node on the grid, with consecutive nodes connected with directional arrows (see Fig. 1). For the purposes of the present analyses, data were extracted to calculate (1) proportion of the interaction spent in shared positive affect (i.e., number of nodes located in the shared positive affect quadrant of the grid divided by the total number of nodes comprising the interaction), (2) latency of return to shared positive affect (i.e., the number of nodes between successive instances of visits to the shared positive affect quadrant), and (3) maternal positive affect responsivity (i.e., the conditional probability that mothers join their child in positive affect [enter the shared positive affect quadrant] following an instance of child positive affect). Twenty-nine participants demonstrated shared positive affect on ≤1 occasion and were therefore excluded from analyses evaluating latency of return to positive affect; the sample size for these analyses was n = 71 (38% depressed). Fourteen children did not demonstrate positive affect at any point during the interaction and were therefore excluded from analyses evaluating maternal positive affect responsivity; the sample size for these analyses was n = 86 (36% depressed). Mothers excluded from analysis due to fewer than two instances of shared positive affect (in the case of latency analyses) and mothers excluded due to lack of child positive affect (in the case of responsivity analyses) did not differ from included mothers based on child age, sex, internalizing problems, or externalizing problems, nor did they differ based on maternal depression status or maternal continuous symptoms of depression as assessed using the CES-D (all ps>0.05).

Data Analytic Plan

Hypotheses were tested using linear regressions implemented using R (R Core Team, 2023), with standardized effect sizes generated using the ‘lm.beta’ package (Behrendt, 2025). Prior to analyses, interaction terms were calculated by multiplying dichotomous maternal depression status (0 = never depressed, 1 = currently depressed) by each mean-centered dyadic positive affect index. To evaluate change over time and account for rank-order stability in child internalizing and externalizing, baseline child internalizing and externalizing scores were included in as covariates in models of one-year internalizing and externalizing outcomes, respectively. Child age in month was also included as a covariate in all analyses to account for potential developmental confounds. Benjamini-Hochberg (1995) false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons.

Maternal Depression and Child Internalizing and Externalizing Problems

Children of mothers with depression had higher baseline internalizing problems (t=-2.49, df = 51.61, p=.016, Cohen’s d=-0.58), as well as T2 internalizing (t=-2.28, df = 52.17, p=.027, Cohen’s d=-0.53) and externalizing problems (t=-2.53, df = 98, p=.013, Cohen’s d=-0.52) relative to children of mothers without depression (see Table 1). Two children of mothers with depression scored in the clinical range (T-score ≥ 64) for internalizing problems at baseline and one-year follow up. One child of a mother with depression scored in the clinical range for externalizing problems at baseline; no children scored in the clinical range for externalizing problems at one-year follow up. All children of never-depressed mothers were in the typical range for both internalizing and externalizing problems at both timepoints.

Discussion

The identification of factors that may buffer against risk for emotional and behavioral problems in children of mothers with depression is a pressing public health concern. Using a dynamic systems approach (Lamay et al., 2004), the present study evaluated the extent to which maternal positive emotional attunement associates with resilience in terms of both internalizing and externalizing outcomes across one-year among children of mothers with clinical depression. Findings indicate that maternal positive affect responsivity—the extent to which mothers follow their children’s positive affective lead—during face-to-face play is associated with reduced risk for internalizing problems in toddlers of mothers with depression. Interestingly, neither the amount of time that dyads spent in displays of shared positive affect nor the speed at which dyads repaired ruptures to shared positive affect associated with internalizing or externalizing problems over time. Together, results suggest the importance of maternal sensitivity to toddler positive emotional cues, specifically that mothers’ ability to capitalize on their child’s positive affect, rather than the dyad’s total amount of positive affect, per se, may be particularly important for understanding risk and resilience to internalizing problems among children of mother affected by depression.

Toddlers of mothers with depression were significantly higher in both internalizing and externalizing problems compared to their low-risk peers at both baseline and one-year follow-up. This aligns with a wealth of previous research implicating exposure to maternal depression in risk for wide-ranging emotional and behavioral difficulties (see Goodman et al., 2011). As highlighted in the present work, disproportionate risk for psychiatric symptoms can be observed as early as toddlerhood among these vulnerable youth, and these differences are likely to forecast enduring vulnerability to psychopathology (Briggs-Gowan et al., 2006; Mathiesen et al., 2009).

Importantly, maternal depression is not deterministic, and the present findings suggest that positive parenting may relate to psychosocial resilience among young children of mothers with depression. Specifically, the present work finds that toddlers of currently depressed mothers may be particularly sensitive to variations in maternal positive affect responsivity, such that they demonstrate significantly elevated risk for prospective internalizing problems when maternal positive affect responsivity is low and lower risk of prospective internalizing problems (even relative to toddlers of mothers without depression) when maternal positive affect responsivity is high. These findings add to a rich body of previous research indicating heterogeneity in outcomes amongst offspring of mothers with clinical depression (Collishaw et al., 2016; Padaigaitė-Gulbinienė et al., 2024; Reuben & Shaw, 2015), and point to maternal positive affect responsivity as one behaviorally-specific, potentially tractable target for interventions aimed to reducing risk for emotional problems in these children during toddlerhood.

Of note, duration of shared positive affect and latency to return to positive affect were not found to predict toddler internalizing outcomes in the present work. Thus, associations between maternal positive emotional attunement and toddler outcomes were specifically linked to the extent to which mothers were able to dynamically reciprocate their toddlers’ positive affect in real-time (i.e., maternal positive affect responsivity), rather than the dyads’ general levels of mutual positive affectivity. As toddlers normatively seek opportunities for autonomy (Denham, 1998), one explanation may be that mothers’ ability to responsively coordinate their affective expression to follow their children’s cues serves an important co-regulatory function, reinforcing contextually appropriate pleasure and enthusiasm, and scaffolding children’s developing capacity for effective emotion regulation. Consistent with past research on maternal “enhancing” of child positive affect during other developmental stages (e.g., adolescence; Gentzler & Root, 2019; Fredrick et al., 2019; Kochanska & Aksan, 1995; Yi et al., 2016), this in-the-moment attuned and responsive feedback from mothers may reduce risk for internalizing by enhancing children’s ability to flexibly recruit and sustain positive mood, thereby buffering against inherited vulnerabilities to depression and related emotional difficulties (Goodman et al., 2011).

Indeed, this pattern of findings also aligns with theories of emotion socialization (Eisenberg et al., 1998; Morris et al., 2007), which suggest that maternal reciprocity of children’s self-initiated positive emotions reinforces the value of such emotional displays, with downstream implications for children’s trait positive affect and intrapersonal positive affect upregulation skills. That this was specifically observed among dyads affected by maternal depression points to the potential for such positive affect reciprocity to associate with enhanced emotional functioning in contexts characterized by reduced parental positive emotion displays (Pizzagalli, 2014; Vanderlind et al., 2020). Put another way, when mothers’ own positive emotional expression is more limited due to their experience of depression, their joyful and engaged responses to their children’s positive emotions may be experienced by offspring as especially reinforcing. Although outside the scope of the present observational research, which cannot speak to specific mechanistic pathways or causal relations, future work is needed to probe processes linking maternal positive affect responsivity to child internalizing outcomes.

Contrary to hypotheses, indices of observed maternal positive emotional attunement did not prospectively associate with toddler externalizing problems in the present sample. This may suggest that maternal attunement to child positive emotions is less salient to the development of externalizing problems relative to internalizing problems during the toddler years. Alternatively, it may be the case that associations between maternal positive emotional attunement and child externalizing are complex and qualified by other individual difference factors outside the scope of the present investigation. For example, in past work conducted in children ages 7–12, Yi and colleagues (2016) found that maternal encouraging responses to children’s positive emotions were associated with lower externalizing only amongst children with more difficulties in self-regulation. Moreover, past research suggests that the type and context of child positive affective expression may be particularly relevant to externalizing, with high-intensity positive affect associating with risk rather than resilience to externalizing problems (Dollar et al., 2023; Stifter et al., 2008; Yu et al., 2025). Future work is needed to examine the role of positive affect intensity and parent-child interactional context in moderating associations between maternal positive emotional attunement and toddler externalizing problems among families affected by maternal depression.

Mothers in the depressed group included individuals diagnosed with a depressive disorder using a structured clinical interview, as well as mother self-reporting threshold depressive symptoms using a validated questionnaire measure, given limited evidence for differences in psychosocial outcomes among offsprings of mothers with diagnostic versus dimensionally-assessed depression (see Goodman et al., 2011). Results of sensitivity analyses indicate that patterns of findings generally replicate when maternal depression is defined more strictly according to clinical interview measures alone, although interactive effects of maternal depression and maternal positive affect responsiveness were reduced to trend-level significance after correcting for multiple tests. Minor differences in findings may be explained by reductions in power, and future work in larger, better-powered samples of mothers assessed using both diagnostic instruments and self-report questionnaire measures is needed to ascertain the extent to which measurement effects may influence patterns of associations over time.

This study benefits from several strengths including the inclusion of a clinical sample of mothers with depression, multimethod longitudinal design including fine-grained behavioral observation timeseries measures of mother and child affect, and state space grid analyses, permitting insight into the ways in which the structure of mother-child interactions during toddlerhood contributes to symptom development over time. Findings should also be interpreted in light of several limitations. Specifically, the present study focused exclusively on mothers. While consistent with previous literature in this area (see Goodman et al., 2011), research is needed to examine the extent to which paternal depression may (or may not) similarly sensitize children to individual differences in positive affective parenting. Other features of the study sample, including its limited size, relatively high level of parental education, and limited racial and ethnic diversity limit generalizability of findings. In particular, past research indicates that emotional socialization processes—and particularly maternal engagement with child positive emotions—may meaningfully vary across cultures (Friedlmeier et al., 2011), and research is needed in more diverse samples to ascertain the extent to which results generalize across different racial, ethnic, and cultural contexts. The present sample size, particularly of mothers with depression, was also modest, especially for analyses in which dyads with limited positive emotion were excluded. Thus, it is possible that null findings may reflect Type II error, rather than the lack of a true association. Future work should aim to replicate findings in larger samples of families affected by maternal depression.

Limitations of the present design also suggest important directions for future research. Levels of internalizing and externalizing problems were relatively low in the present sample. This provides important insight into processes shaping risk and resilience before the onset of clinical levels of symptoms; however, future work may wish to target more clinically acute populations of young children and/or implement more extended longitudinal designs to chart the interplay of parental depression and positive emotional attunement on children’s emotional and behavioral trajectories across early development. Additionally, the present work used mother-report of child outcomes, which may have been influenced by mothers’ own symptoms of depression. Future work including multi-informant or observational assessments of child psychosocial functioning are needed to enhance confidence in findings. Finally, the structured play task used in the present work was relatively short in duration, and it is possible that longer task durations would allow for a greater likelihood of capturing meaningful variability in mother and toddler positive affect dynamics.

It is also important to note that caregiving does not occur within a vacuum; a confluence of factors, ranging from family support, co-parent availability, and community resources may function to both facilitate maternal attunement, as well as moderate effects of maternal attunement on young children’s emotional outcomes in families affected by maternal depression. Future work is needed to account for social and contextual factors that may shape dyadic functioning during this sensitive period of development. Moreover, parent-child relationships are bidirectional and transactional in nature (Pardini et al., 2008), and it is possible that children with a lower propensity for the development of internalizing problems elicit more positive affect responsivity from mothers, particularly those affected by depression. Future work is needed to explore the possibility of bidirectional relations.

Using dynamic systems analyses, the present work suggests that maternal positive affective responsivity associates with lower internalizing problems in young children of mothers affected by clinical depression. Clinically, results suggest that interventions aimed at reducing risk for internalizing symptoms among offspring of depressed mothers may wish to target to maternal sensitivity to and reciprocity of children’s positive emotions, rather than merely promoting broadband increases in maternal positive affectivity, per se. That is, even among dyads who struggle to sustain high levels of shared positive affectivity, mothers with depression may help to promote children’s wellbeing by following children’s positive affective lead, attuning to children’s dynamic positive affective expression. Attending to the structure, rather than merely the content, of parent-child interactions is an essential step toward identifying malleable targets for behavioral intervention in these at-risk families.
