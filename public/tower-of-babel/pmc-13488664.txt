Feasibility, Safety, and Effectiveness of Ultrasound-Guided Acupotomy Treatment for Degenerative Cervical Radiculopathy: A Pilot Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Sooran Cho, Sookwang An, Taewook Lee, Hyeri Jung, Jihun Kim, Byoung-Kab Kang, Yoona Oh, Kun Hyung Kim, Gi Young Yang, Eunseok Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo assess the feasibility, safety, and effectiveness of ultrasound-guided acupotomy (UA) compared with conventional physical therapy (CPT) in patients with degenerative cervical radiculopathy (CR).Patients and MethodsThirty-two participants with clinically and radiologically confirmed CR were randomized to receive UA or CPT (interferential current therapy+microwave diathermy). Interventions were delivered twice weekly for 3 weeks, followed by an 8-week follow-up with assessments at weeks 4, 7, and 11. The outcomes included the visual analog scale (VAS) for neck and arm pain, painDETECT, Neck Disability Index (NDI), Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21), clinically important differences (CID), EuroQol 5-dimension 5-level questionnaire (EQ-5D-5L), and Patient Global Impression of Change. Adverse events (AEs) were assessed at every visit.ResultsThe overall treatment adherence rate was 96.9%, with 100% treatment completion in the UA group. Acupotomy-related AEs occurred in 5 of the 96 treatment sessions (5.21%), all of which were mild and self-limiting. The UA group showed preliminary evidence of greater improvements in VAS scores for arm and neck pain or discomfort, painDETECT, NDI, CID, and PGIC compared with the CPT group at all time points. At week 4, adjusted mean differences favored UA by 25.7 mm for neck and 18.7 mm for arm VAS. For the DASS-21, between-group differences were observed for depression and stress at week 4. The UA group showed preliminary evidence of greater improvements in EQ-5D-5L scores at weeks 4 and 7 compared with the CPT group.ConclusionThis pilot study suggests that UA is a feasible and safe treatment option for degenerative CR, with preliminary effects on pain intensity, functional disability, neuropathic pain characteristics, psychological distress, and health-related quality of life, compared to CPT. These findings warrant confirmatory studies with larger sample sizes.

Introduction

Cervical radiculopathy (CR) occurs when cervical nerve roots are compressed by protruding discs, osteophytic growths, or cervical malalignments.1 The primary pathology is a neural lesion, commonly caused by cervical disc herniation or degenerative spondylosis, leading to restricted mobility, neck pain, periscapular pain, radicular arm pain, and sensory disturbance.2 Unlike nonspecific neck pain or myofascial pain, degenerative CR is characterized by cervical nerve root compression or irritation with corresponding radicular arm pain, sensory disturbance, numbness, or weakness.3,4 It should also be distinguished from cervical myelopathy, which is characterized by degenerative structural changes of the cervical spine leading to progressive spinal canal narrowing, chronic spinal cord compression, and resultant spinal cord dysfunction.5 CR has a prevalence of 1.21–5.8 per 1,000 individuals.6 Despite low prevalence, CR markedly impair daily functioning and quality of life.7 In Korea, CR-related medical expenditures exceeded 500 billion KRW in 2022, increasing with population aging.8

Management strategies include surgical and non-operative treatments. Surgery may be required for progressive neurological deficits or structural instability; however, its invasiveness, cost, and perioperative complications limit its use as first-line treatment. Guideline favoring conservative care have increased reliance on nonoperative therapies.9 The criteria for the conservative management remain unestablished, treatment includes pharmacological treatment, physical therapy, and injection procedures.10

In Korean clinical settings, conservative management includes various physical therapy modalities: superficial heat therapy, microwave diathermy (MWD), Interferential Current Therapy (ICT), transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation, laser therapy, intermittent cervical traction, and trigger-point interventions. ICT and MWD are the common noninvasive modalities for managing CR-related pain and dysfunction.11 Korean Medicine treatments, including acupuncture, electroacupuncture, cupping, and moxibustion, are extensively used, with studies reporting improvements in pain intensity, neurological symptoms, and functional disability.12–14

Acupotomy, a modern acupuncture modality, combines classical principles with microinvasive knife-edge manipulation, using a needle-knife to release adhesions, contractures, or scar tissue around the nerve root.15,16 Although direct mechanistic evidence in patients with degenerative CR remains limited, recent studies using cervical spondylosis and cervical spondylotic radiculopathy animal models suggest that needle-knife or needle-scalpel therapy may modulate inflammatory, apoptotic, and disc degeneration-related pathways.17,18 Nevertheless, these preclinical findings should be interpreted cautiously because they do not directly confirm the mechanisms underlying UA in patients with degenerative CR. However, conventional acupotomy relies solely on tactile feedback, posing risks when approaching deep structures. Ultrasound-guided acupotomy (UA) addresses these limitations by enabling real-time visualization of target tissues, potentially improving procedural safety and targeting accuracy.19

Although previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have suggested potential benefits of acupotomy for CR or cervical spondylotic radiculopathy, relevant studies are limited by small sample sizes, heterogeneous treatment protocols and outcome measures, and insufficient methodological rigor.20–22 Moreover, many prior studies have used blind acupotomy techniques without imaging guidance, which may compromise procedural accuracy, reproducibility, and safety in the cervical region.20,21 In studies where acupotomy was combined with other interventions, such as nerve block therapy, the specific contribution of acupotomy itself is difficult to isolate.20 Although ultrasound-guided injection acupotomy has been evaluated in a randomized trial, comparative analyses of UA and clinically relevant active conservative treatments remain scarce.23

Therefore, we designed a pilot randomized controlled trial (RCT) comparing UA with ICT+MWD to evaluate its feasibility, safety, preliminary clinical effectiveness in patients with degenerative CR. These findings provide a basis for future large-scale confirmatory RCT design.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

This single-center, two-arm, pilot RCT was designed to evaluate the feasibility, safety, and effectiveness of UA treatment compared with conventional physical therapy (CPT), consisting of ICT+MWD, in patients with degenerative CR. Patients diagnosed with CR, as identified by the International Classification of Diseases (M50.1), were recruited from the Pusan National University Korean Medicine Hospital between May and November 2024. Participants were aged 30–75, with CR signs such as pain, discomfort, numbness, abnormal sensation, and muscle weakness in the cervical and upper extremities. Pain or discomfort was measured by a visual analog scale (VAS) score, and participants with VAS score of 30 mm or more were included in the study. Participants with symptoms that lasted for ≥3 months were selected only when degenerative changes found on cervical magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), such as intervertebral disc herniation, stenosis, and spondylosis, were confirmed. The diagnosis was based on physical examination, imaging evaluation, medical history assessment, and clinical symptoms by certified Korean Medicine Doctors (KMDs). Participants with a history of cervical surgery, spinal cord disease, spondylitis, fractures, tumors, or other diseases that could cause neuropathic symptoms in the upper extremities were excluded. Patients unsuitable for acupotomy due to impaired hemostasis were excluded. Those who received injection-based interventions, such as epidural injection or nerve block, within the last 2 months, and those who were judged by research clinicians to be ineligible for the study, were not included. The detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Before the study, those who understood its purpose and procedure were enrolled only when they voluntarily agreed to participate and signed a written informed consent form.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Pusan National University Korean Medicine Hospital (PNUKHIRB 2024–04-002) and adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. This prospective clinical study was registered with and approved by the Clinical Research Information Service (KCT0009454; https://cris.nih.go.kr), where the trial protocol is also available.

Sample Size Calculation

This pilot study was designed to evaluate the feasibility, safety, and provide preliminary evidence of the effectiveness of UA treatment. We assumed that UA treatment would show a small-to-medium standardized effect size compared to CPT (ICT+MWD) in degenerative CR. Therefore, 32 participants (16 in the UA group and 16 in the CPT group) were estimated, based on the recommended value of approximately 15 people per group (d=0.5).24 Because this was a pilot RCT, the sample size was not determined based on a formal power calculation for definitive efficacy testing. Therefore, the clinical effectiveness findings should be interpreted as exploratory and as preliminary estimates for planning future adequately powered trials.

Randomization and Blinding

Randomization of the clinical trials was performed by an independent statistical expert to eliminate assignment bias and prevent exposure of researchers and participants. A statistic expert applied stratified block randomization with gender as a stratifying factor using SAS® 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC) to allocate 16 participants to each group at the same probability, and the generated randomization table and block size were kept by a statistician independent of the study and protected from being disclosed. After obtaining consent, researchers sequentially assigned the screening numbers to the participants, and unique numbers were randomly assigned to those who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. For allocation concealment, a sealed opaque envelope with random assignments was stored in a cabinet with a lock. The open envelope was stored separately after recording the date of opening and the researcher’s signature. Blinding of participants and practitioners to group allocation was not feasible because of the inherent differences between the interventions. Although outcome assessors were not blinded, most patient-reported outcomes were obtained using structured self-report questionnaires, with research staff providing only procedural guidance or clarification without influencing participants’ responses.

Interventions

Participants in both groups received the assigned interventions twice per week for 3 weeks, for six treatment sessions. In the UA group, the patient was placed in the lateral decubitus position. The cervical level was confirmed through a transverse or longitudinal scan, and UA was performed primarily using an in-plane technique with a transverse scan. For UA procedures on the facet joints, an out-of-plane technique using a longitudinal scan was additionally applied at the operator’s discretion when required. Acupotomy needles measuring 0.6 × 50mm or 0.5 x 50mm (Dongbang Acupuncture Inc., Korea) were used. The acupotomy targets consisted of fixed points for the main treatment and auxiliary points for the supplementary treatment. Fixed points were targeted to the cervical facet joints (Ex-B2, deep Hyeopcheok), posterior tubercles, and surrounding fibrotic soft tissues at the level of the CR lesions, which were determined by KMDs. Auxiliary points were trigger and tendor points in painful areas, such as the GB-21 (upper trapezius muscle), SI-14 (levator scapulae muscle), Ex-B2 at the cervicothoracic junction, and GV-14 and Ashi points. The practitioner performed manual techniques, including dissection, excision, and strong stimulation, to release adhesions, fibrosis, and fibrotic tissues. Specifically, for the posterior facet joint targets, the acupotomy needle was advanced under real-time ultrasound guidance toward the facet joint capsule, avoiding crossing the anterior border of the articular pillar to prevent inadvertent advancement into the perineural space. In addition, the procedure was performed targeting the cervical facet joint, which appears rounded on ultrasound, avoiding the waist of the articular pillar where the medial branch runs. For the posterior tubercle targets, a bony contact approach was adopted to prevent the tip of UA from passing over the posterior tubercle and damaging the nerve root (Figure 1). The procedures were performed by a KMD with ≥10 years of experience in acupotomy. CPT is consistent with ICT and MWD (deep heat therapy). For the ICT, four pads were attached to the cervical spine and upper extremities, considering the affected level and symptomatic area of radiculopathy. The therapy was applied in mixed mode (0–360Hz) for 15 min at an intensity that was comfortable for the patient. The MWD was applied to the cervical area in the prone position, adjusting the distance and intensity of the heat source to prevent burns, and applied for 10 min. During the study period, participants were not permitted to receive additional treatments, including invasive procedures, pharmacological treatments, or manual therapies, other than the assigned study interventions.

Outcome Measurements

Feasibility Outcomes

Feasibility outcomes, the primary outcomes of this pilot trial, included the total study duration, defined as the time from the first screening visit to final study completion, the recruitment period, attrition rate, and protocol adherence. The attrition rate was defined as the proportion of participants who discontinued participation during the intervention period before completion. Protocol adherence was evaluated using treatment compliance and data completeness. Treatment compliance was assessed to determine adherence to the prescribed intervention schedule. Data completeness was quantified as the proportion of completed outcome assessments relative to the total number of assessments specified in the study schedule.

Safety Outcomes

Safety assessments were conducted at every visit to identify and manage potential risks associated with the intervention. All adverse events (AEs) were recorded and detailed assessments included the date of onset, date of resolution, severity, causal relationship with the intervention, and any required treatments. The severity and causality of each AE were evaluated based on predefined criteria. The severity of the AEs was classified according to the three-level grading system described by Spilker et al.

Clinical Outcomes

Clinical outcomes included pain intensity (VAS), neuropathic pain identification (painDETECT), functional disability (Neck Disability Index; NDI), clinical relevance (The Patient Global Impression of Change; PGIC and Clinically Important Difference; CID), psychological measures (Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21; DASS-21), and quality-of-life measures (EuroQol 5-dimension 5-level questionnaire; EQ-5D-5L). These clinical outcome measures were collected at baseline, weeks 4 (1-week post-intervention), weeks 7 (4 weeks post-intervention), and weeks 11 (8 weeks post-intervention), except for CID and PGIC. PGIC was assessed only after treatment at weeks 4 and 11. CID was defined as the proportion of patients attaining a VAS score decrease greater than the minimum threshold of 10 mm25 or with a percentage ≥30% (moderate CID) and ≥50% (substantial improvement).26 For each outcome measure, between-group differences in the mean change from baseline to weeks 4, 7, and 11 were evaluated. The VAS score measures the intensity of a patient’s pain or discomfort and ranges from 0 (no pain) to 100 (extreme pain).27 VAS scores for arm and neck pain were measured independently. Neuropathic pain characteristics were evaluated using the painDETECT questionnaire, which has been validated in Koreans to ensure linguistic and cultural appropriateness.28 The DASS-21 is a 21-item shortening tool that measures depression, anxiety, and stress, and each sub-score is calculated according to standardized scoring guidelines.29 The EQ-5D-5L index and EQ-VAS were used to assess health-related quality of life more comprehensively.

Statistical Methods

All statistical analyses were performed by an independent statistical expert using SAS® 9.4 versions (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, North Carolina, USA). In the two-sided test, the statistical significance level was set at p<0.05. The full analysis set (FAS) and per-protocol (PP) groups were defined as analysis groups. Missing values were processed using the last-observation-delivered forward method. The FAS included participants who underwent at least one evaluation after randomization and was used as the primary analysis dataset. The PP group included participants who completed clinical trials without major protocol violations and who received at least five interventions (≥80% compliance). The feasibility and safety outcomes included all AEs recorded during the test period and were reported as descriptive statistics. Demographic and baseline characteristics were compared between the two groups and presented as means and standard deviations. Continuous variables were analyzed using the independent sample t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. To evaluate the clinical outcome indicators, an Analysis of Covariance was applied with the baseline score as a covariate and group assignment as a fixed factor. Repeated-measures Analysis of Variance was used to evaluate the group × time interaction effects.

Results

Recruitment and Baseline Characteristics

Thirty-eight individuals were screened for eligibility for the recruitment period between May 30, 2024, and November 14, 2024. During screening, six participants were excluded: four did not meet the inclusion criteria (no cervical radiculopathy on imaging, n=2; similar symptoms linked to other spinal/peripheral nerve disorders, n=1; symptom severity below the threshold, n=1), one met the exclusion criteria (elevated liver enzymes on blood tests, n=1), and one declined to participate. Thirty-two participants were randomized to either the UA group (n=16) or the CPT group (n=16), with one participant in the CPT group withdrawing consent and subsequently dropping out (Figure 2). Feasibility Outcomes

The attrition and protocol adherence rates were 3.1% and 96.9%, respectively. The adherence rates were 100% and 93.8% in the UA and CPT groups, respectively. All randomized participants provided at least one post-randomization data point and were included in the FAS. One participant in the CPT group was excluded from the PP analysis (Figure 2). Data completeness was 96.9% (124/128), with missing assessments arising from one visit violation and three withdrawals of consent.

Safety Outcomes

During the study period, 12 AEs occurred in eight participants (UA group: five participants with nine AEs; CPT group: three participants with three AEs). No significant between-group differences were observed in the incidence and no AE-related discontinuations occurred in either group. In the UA group, five AEs related to acupotomy occurred across 96 treatment sessions (5.21%) (Table 2): post-treatment pain, n=2; local stiffness, n=3; all were mild and resolved on average within 4 days without sequelae. AEs unrelated to the intervention in the UA group included common cold-like symptoms and trauma-related neck pain. In the CPT group, no AEs related to the intervention were reported; AEs unrelated to the intervention included common cold-like symptoms, muscle spasms after awakening, and knee osteoarthritis. Clinical Outcomes

At weeks 4, 7, and 11, the patients in the UA group reported significantly lower VAS scores for arm and neck pain or discomfort than those in the CPT group. The mean differences in the VAS score for neck pain or discomfort were 25.7 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 8.5–43.0; p=0.0048) at weeks 4, 25.3 (95% CI, 7.6–43.0; p=0.0066) at weeks 7 and 24.4 (95% CI, 8.1–40.7; p=0.0047) at weeks 11. The mean differences in the VAS score for arm pain or discomfort were 18.7 (95% CI, 3.2–34.2; p=0.0196), 23.7 (95% CI, 7.6–39.8; p=0.0053) at weeks 7, and 22.7 (95% CI, 5.5–40.0; p=0.0115) at weeks 11 (Table 3). Changes over time in clinical outcome measures.

For the DASS-21, the depression and the stress scores showed a significant between-group difference at week 4. No significant between-group differences were observed for anxiety scores at any time point. Within the UA group, significant within-group improvements were observed for depression at week 4, anxiety at week 11, and stress at all time points. No significant within-group improvements were observed in the CPT group for any DASS-21 domain at any time point. Changes in the EQ-5D-5L index scores from baseline demonstrated statistically significant between-group differences at weeks 4 and 7, whereas no statistically significant difference between the groups was observed at week 11 (Supplementary Figure 1).

For the CID outcomes, the UA group demonstrated significantly higher responder rates for ≥10 mm, ≥30%, and ≥50% reductions in both arm and neck pain across all time points compared with the CPT group (Supplementary Figure 2). A higher proportion of patients in the UA group reported their change as “minimally improved,” “much improved,” or “very much improved” compared to those in the CPT group at weeks 4 (93.75% vs, 46.67%) and 11 (87.50% vs, 53.33%) (Supplementary Figure 3).

Discussion

During study period, high level of treatment adherence to the UA treatment (100%) and low overall attrition rate (3.1%) was maintained, suggesting that the intervention and study protocol are feasible. All AEs related to UA were mild and resolved on average within 4 days without sequelae (5.21%). No severe AEs were reported. UA treatment was found to be feasible and demonstrated an acceptable safety profile in this pilot study. For clinical effectiveness, the UA group showed greater improvements than the CPT group at all time points for VAS scores for neck and arm pain or discomfort, painDETECT, and NDI, providing preliminary evidence for the potential clinical benefits of UA in degenerative CR.

This study used an active control design with ICT+MWD as the comparator intervention. ICT and MWD have known short-term pain relief effects,30,31 reflected in the significant within-group improvements in VAS at all time points compared to baseline. Although both groups showed within-group improvements from baseline, the between-group differences favored the UA group at all time points. In chronic degenerative conditions such as CR, treatment outcomes are often complicated by spontaneous improvement without formal treatment and regression to the mean over time, whereby initial gains tend to diminish during follow-up.32 However, in this study, these between-group differences were maintained throughout the follow-up, a pattern observed not only in neck pain but also in arm pain. Although the relatively short follow-up period of 8 weeks should be considered, these findings suggest that UA may have beneficial effects on radicular pain.

The between-group difference in painDETECT scores progressively increased over time, suggesting a sustained rather than transient treatment effect. At all time points, the CPT group showed no significant within-group changes in painDETECT scores, whereas the UA group demonstrated significant improvements from baseline. CR involves a mixed pain phenotype with both nociceptive and neuropathic components, arising from mechanical compression and chemical irritation of the cervical nerve root.33 The reduction in painDETECT scores in the UA group suggests not only decreased pain intensity but also a potential shift in pain quality, implying a beneficial effect on the neuropathic component of pain beyond short-term nociceptive modulation in this population.

Psychological distress, including stress, anxiety, and depression, has been reported to be associated with neck pain and functional limitations.34,35 In our trial, the stress score on DASS-21 improved in the UA group, although the between-group differences were not significant. The repeated-measures analysis showed no significant group × time interaction for anxiety or stress. Therefore, while psychological factors may interact with musculoskeletal pain, the current findings do not provide clear evidence that UA exerted a distinct effect on psychological distress in this study population.

The procedural rationale of ultrasound-guided cervical acupotomy in this study differs from that of conventional periradicular injection and medial branch block techniques. In periradicular injection, the needle tip is intentionally positioned between the nerve root and the posterior tubercle to access the extraforaminal perineural space.36 In contrast, the acupotomy intervention in this trial utilized bony contact with the posterior tubercle as a depth reference during needle advancement. As for the facet joint approach, medial branch block is designed to target the medial branch nerve located at the waist of the articular pillar.37,38 In the present study, the acupotomy needle was directed toward the facet joint capsule, with needle-knife dissection applied to the capsular and pericapsular soft tissues demonstrating thickening or fibrotic change. Rather than targeting the nerve, acupotomy addressed the surrounding connective tissue structures, employing longitudinal and transverse blade manipulation intended to release fibrotic adhesions — a core principle of acupotomy distinct from fluid-based infiltration of conventional injection techniques.39 Real-time ultrasound guidance was intentionally incorporated into the study protocol to execute this tissue-targeted manipulation safely and precisely in the cervical region, enabling direct visualization of bony landmarks, capsular structures, and adjacent neurovascular anatomy throughout the procedure, thereby minimizing reliance on surface anatomical landmarks. This structurally targeted, image-guided approach represents a mechanistically distinct intervention strategy for patients with CR. Recent cervical animal model studies provide preliminary biological plausibility for such tissue-level effects, demonstrating that needle-knife or needle-scalpel therapy reduces spinal cord inflammatory infiltration and neuronal apoptosis via the IRE1α–XBP1 signaling pathway in a rat model of cervical spondylotic radiculopathy17 and nucleus pulposus cell apoptosis is inhibited and histologically confirmed disc degeneration is attenuated through the SDF-1/CXCR4 axis in a rat degenerative cervical intervertebral disc model.18 However, as these preclinical findings cannot directly confirm the mechanisms underlying UA in patients with degenerative CR, additional tissue-level analyses are needed.

Studies between 2006 and 202520–23,40,41 suggested that acupotomy may alleviate pain in cervical spondylosis and radiculopathy, but these investigations primarily relied on primarily pain-focused outcomes and rarely incorporated comprehensive outcome measures. Additionally, prior interventions used blind acupotomy techniques, which meta-analyses42 associated with variability in precision and safety. The present study addressed these limitations by incorporating ultrasound guidance to improve procedural accuracy and adopting a multidimensional outcome framework encompassing neuropathic pain characteristics, functional disability, psychological distress, and health-related quality of life.

This pilot trial has several limitations. First, the small sample size limits statistical power to detect between-group differences; as this trial was not powered for definitive efficacy testing, all findings should be interpreted as exploratory. Second, the single center may restrict generalizability. Third, blinding of participants and practitioners was not feasible owing to the distinct nature of the interventions, and outcome assessors were not blinded to group allocation. Participants’ awareness of group allocation may have introduced expectation and performance bias, which should be considered when interpreting the self-reported outcomes, and a placebo effect cannot be excluded. Fourth, the 8-week follow-up period is relatively short for a chronic degenerative condition, and it is not clear whether the observed clinical benefits are sustained beyond the observation period. Fifth, outcome assessments were largely patient-reported. Nevertheless, this study had several strengths. Among the few RCTs evaluating UA for degenerative CR, this study contributes to improving procedural accuracy and safety. Participant adherence was high with no discontinuation, showing protocol feasibility. No serious AEs occurred, supporting the safety of UA. The active control group enabled relevant comparison, and the multidimensional framework allowed broader evaluation of pain, neuropathic features, and function.

Conclusion

This pilot RCT provide preliminary evidence indicating that UA is a safe and well-tolerated intervention with potential clinical benefits in pain intensity, neuropathic pain, functional disability, and health-related quality of life. Although limited by its pilot design, small sample size, and short follow-up period, the protocol and findings provide a foundation for future large-scale confirmatory clinical trials.
