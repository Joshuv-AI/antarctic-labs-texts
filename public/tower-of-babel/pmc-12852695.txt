Gestational physical exercise prevents early-life behavioral impairments in the offspring of a rat model of attention deficit hyperactivity disorder
Authors: Andrea Tosta, Ariene S. Fonseca, Debora Jardim Messeder, Priscila Siqueira, Sérgio T. Ferreira, Mychael V. Lourenco, Pablo Pandolfo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a developmental condition characterized by symptoms of inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity. Spontaneously hypertensive rats (SHRs) comprise an ADHD rodent model that exhibits behavioral impairments recapitulating phenotypes observed in humans. SHRs further develop dopaminergic hypofunction in frontostriatal circuits and an imbalance in dopamine and norepinephrine systems. Maternal physical exercise, such as swimming, during pregnancy was shown to enhance offspring angiogenesis and neurogenesis, and to improve memory in rodents. Herein we determined the impact of gestational swimming on behavioral and dopaminergic parameters in childhood (1–2 week-old rats) and adolescence (4–5 week-old rats) of SHR and Wistar Kyoto rats (WKYs), used as a control group. The results showed that gestational swimming prevented neurodevelopmental impairments in behavior, assessed by the righting reflex and olfactory recognition tests, in the offspring. Furthermore, during adolescence, SHRs from exercised dams exhibited reduced novelty-seeking behavior, an important behavioral trait during this developmental period. Finally, D2R and DAT mRNA content was reduced in the frontal cortex of adolescent SHRs whose mothers were exercised. Results indicate that gestational exercise could be an effective preventative strategy against ADHD-associated behavioral and molecular phenotypes in the offspring.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33693-1.

Introduction

Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a neuropsychiatric condition characterized by symptoms of inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity, usually developing throughout childhood and adolescence1. Imbalances in dopaminergic transmission in cortical and subcortical pathways contribute to the motor and cognitive impairments associated with ADHD2. The prefrontal cortex is essential for executive functions, integrating sensory, motor, and emotional responses3. The ability to adjust behaviors through appropriate inhibitory control underlies the risk-taking and novelty-seeking behaviors often observed in ADHD4,5. Additionally, individuals with ADHD face difficulties in sensory and motor integration, leading to deficiencies in fine and gross motor skills, gait regularity, and primitive reflexes, which coincide with cortical neurodevelopmental delays6–10. While considerable advances have been made in understanding the pathophysiology of ADHD, there is still a lack of complete knowledge of its risk factors and options for symptom management.

ADHD modeling in rodents has proven to be challenging. Significant advancements in understanding the neurobiological and behavioral characteristics associated with ADHD have been made through genetic and pharmacological models11. Although each model has its own set of advantages and disadvantages, spontaneously hypertensive rats (SHRs), when compared to Wistar Kyoto rats (WKYs), have been widely used to model and study ADHD pathophysiology. Research on these animals as potential ADHD models began in the 1980 s, led by Sagvolden and his colleagues, who investigated various pharmacological, genetic, and behavioral approaches12–14. Our studies confirmed the behavioral traits exhibited by the SHR strain, including inattention15, impulsivity16, and hyperactivity17. Additionally, we have noted impairments in different cognitive domains associated with ADHD, such as increased risk-taking behavior18, deficits in working17 and spatial19 memories, poorer performance in the object recognition20, and decision-making21 tasks, heightened sensitivity to psychoactive substances like alcohol22 and cannabinoids23,24, as well as sensory impairments25. Moreover, similar to ADHD, these animals exhibit dopaminergic hypofunction within frontostriatal circuits characterized by increased activity of dopamine transporters (DAT) and heightened dopamine uptake15. SHRs respond positively to methylphenidate, a drug that inhibits dopamine transporters and is used as a treatment for human ADHD26.

Regular physical exercise has been shown to improve memory, to promote angiogenesis, neurogenesis, and neurotransmitter release, and to confer neuroprotection against injuries27–29. Exercise also increases the synthesis, release, and availability of dopamine in the brain30–32. In adolescent SHR, physical exercise has been shown to improve behavior and rescue reduced dopamine D2 receptor (D2R) expression25,33–37. Mild-intensity gestational exercise has been considered safe for both mothers and children38,39. Gestational exercise promotes muscle strength and endurance and alleviates pregnancy-related anxiety and depressive states40. Gestational exercise has also been reported to trigger benefits in the offspring, such as increased hippocampal BDNF expression, neurogenesis, and improved learning in rodents41–45. We previously reported that the offspring of SHR mothers who swam during pregnancy exhibited improved nociceptive reflexes and increased hippocampal BDNF levels25. Here we tested the effects of swimming during pregnancy on the offspring of an ADHD animal model, particularly focusing on sensorimotor reflexes, locomotor activity, emotional behaviors, and dopaminergic parameters in the frontal cortex Fig. 1.

Results

Swimming during pregnancy decreased the weight of WKY and SHR offspring in PND 07

Weighing of the offspring at PND 7 and PND 14 showed a significant effect on weight over time (Fig. 2). A three-way ANOVA analysis was performed for PND 7 and showed a significant effect for the strain factor [F (1,84) = 89.1, p < 0.001, n = 9–15] and the strain x treatment interaction [F (1,84) = 19.2, p < 0.001, n = 9–15]. Multiple comparisons showed that the WKY SWIM group had lower body weight than the WKY SED group at P7 (p < 0.001). Conversely, the offspring of the SHR SWIM group had a higher body mass compared to the sedentary group WKY (p < 0.001), regardless of sex. At PND 14, differences between strains persisted [F (1,77) = 100.7, p < 0.001, n = 8–14], and WKY animals continued to have greater body mass than SHRs (p < 0.001).

Gestational swimming prevented neonatal neurodevelopmental delays in SHR offspring

For the righting reflex, a three-way ANOVA showed a significant difference in the strain x treatment x sex interaction at PND 7 [F (1,80) = 4.5, p = 0.036, n = 9–15] (Fig. 3). Multiple comparisons revealed that SHRs showed developmental impairment, i.e., they had significantly higher latency to righting than WKY animals, independently of sex (p = 0.002). Gestational swimming decreased righting latency in SHR (p < 0.01) (Fig. 3A). On PND 14, analyses revealed a significant interaction between strain x treatment [F (1, 78) = 6.3, p = 0.014, n = 8–14]. SHR offspring also had impairments in the righting reflex compared with the WKY animals (p < 0.001), which was prevented in SHR born from exercised mothers (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3B).

In the negative geotaxis test, PND 7 showed an effect of strain [F (1,81) = 20.1, p < 0.001, n = 9–15]. SHR reached the maximum allowed time of 60 s, showing impaired motor coordination compared to WKY (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3C). On PND 14, this difference between strains persisted [F (1,78) = 71.1, p < 0.001, n = 8–14], and SHR showed impaired motor coordination compared to WKY (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3D). Gestational swimming had no effect on the motor coordination reflex of the offspring, regardless of strain and PND (Fig. 3C and D).

In the olfactory recognition test on PND 7, three-way ANOVA revealed a significant effect of strain [F (1,80) = 62.8, p < 0.001, n = 9–15]. SHRs took significantly longer to detect the maternal odor than WKY animals (p < 0 0.001) (Fig. 3E). On PND 14, a three-way ANOVA revealed a significant strain x treatment x sex interaction [F (1, 77) = 8.3, p = 0.005, n = 8–14]. SHR displayed decreased olfactory recognition compared to WKY (p < 0.001). Swimming during gestation caused a significant positive effect on sensory development in the offspring, with the SHR SWIM group displaying better olfactory discrimination performance than the SHR SED group (p = 0.004).

Swimming during pregnancy did not alter the anxiety-related behavior of adolescent WKY or SHR offspring in the elevated plus maze (EPM)

On PND 30, the EPM was performed to measure anxiety-like behavior. Three-way ANOVA revealed no significant effects in the percentage of entries and time spent in open arms or in the number of entries into enclosed arms (Fig. 4).

Multiple comparisons showed that SHRs explored objects longer than WKYs (p < 0.001). Both adolescent WKY and SHR offspring showed SWIM group decreased time spent exploring objects (p = 0.0002) (Fig. 6).

Gestational swimming decreased dopamine D2 receptor mRNA in the SHR frontal cortex

To investigate whether gestational swimming influenced the dopaminergic system, mRNA expression was analyzed in the frontal cortex for TH (Fig. 7A), DAT (Fig. 7B), and D2R (Fig. 7C). No changes in TH, COMT, and MAO-A mRNA expression were detected comparing male and female WKY or SHR rats, whether sedentary or exercised. Regarding DAT mRNA content, a three-way ANOVA revealed a significant effect of treatment [F (1,28) = 4.9, p = 0.035, n = 3–5]. A trend of increase (p = 0.07) was found between SHR and WKY sedentary subjects (Fig. 7B). Swimming during pregnancy decreased DAT mRNA compared to sedentary rats (p = 0.035), regardless of sex. Regarding D2R expression, the analysis showed a significant strain x treatment interaction [F (1,31) = 6,0, p = 0.02, n = 3–5]. SHRs showed higher D2R expression than WKY animals (p = 0.001). Gestational swimming decreased D2R expression in SHR (p = 0.003), regardless of sex (Fig. 7C).

Swimming during pregnancy does not affect levels of proteins involved in dopaminergic neurotransmission in the frontal cortex (FC)

In addition, we performed immunodetection using Western blotting to determine protein levels of TH (Figs. 8A and B), DAT (Figs. 8C and D), and D2R (Figs. 8E and F) in PFC. Three-way ANOVA showed no significant differences in the protein levels examined.

Discussion

The present study investigated the effects of gestational swimming on behavioral and dopaminergic parameters in the offspring of WKY and SHR rats, an animal model of ADHD. SHR exhibited neurodevelopmental delay and novelty-seeking behavior in the adolescence phase, and increased expression of DAT and D2R in the PFC. In the SHRs, maternal gestational swimming improved the motor reflexes of the offspring on postnatal days 7 and 14 and decreased novelty-seeking and D2R expression in the adolescence phase.

Physical exercise significantly improves the quality of life, and it is generally accepted that regular physical exercise is critical in improving brain structure and function. Exercise has pleiotropic effects: it is a common source of stress signaling mechanisms, and contributes to weight loss while regulating various hormones, neurotrophic factors, and neurotransmitter release46,47. It also promotes neuronal maturation and improves learning and memory36,40,48. Moreover, regular physical activity was shown to improve impaired social behavior in children with ADHD and reduce hyperactivity49. However, the possible behavioral and neurobiological consequences to the offspring of pregnant women diagnosed with ADHD who practice regular physical exercise are still unknown.

Regular physical exercise is recommended for women during pregnancy. Engaging in exercise during this period can help reduce maternal weight gain, prevent gestational diabetes, enhance blood circulation, and positively influence fetal development50. Research involving rodents has identified gestational swimming as a suitable option due to its safe and controlled intensity levels, especially when compared to voluntary wheel running or motorized treadmills51. Maintaining the water temperature between 30 and 32 °C ensures that the animals can float comfortably, benefiting both the mother and the offspring41–43,52,53. However, it is crucial to note that forced swimming in rats, particularly when they are subjected to water at 25 °C, as seen in the Porsolt test, can lead to increases in stress-related hormone levels54. This highlights the need for caution in interpreting the results, yet it suggests that controlled gestational exercise may yield significant benefits for the brain health of the offspring. Our recent findings demonstrate that gestational swimming increases BDNF levels in a sex-dependent manner in SHR offspring25.

Few studies have explored sensorimotor reflexes in animal models of ADHD. Ferguson et al. (2003) found that SHRs exhibited slower righting reflexes (PNDs 4–6) and negative geotaxis (PNDs 7–9), and they also had lower weights compared to Wistar Kyoto (WKY) and Sprague-Dawley rats. The authors linked these differences to maturational delays seen in individuals with ADHD55. The results of the present study further corroborate these discrepancies between WKY and SHR at both PND 7 and PND 14, reinforcing the hypothesis of a neurodevelopmental delay. Gestational swimming impacted weight gain on PND 7, resulting in decreased weight for WKY animals and increased weight for SHRs. In the righting reflex test, gestational swimming demonstrated beneficial effects for SHRs at both tested ages. Postnatal ontogeny studies are necessary to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the developmental improvements induced by gestational swimming, including the levels of trophic factors such as BDNF.

Differences between the strains were also observed in the olfactory recognition sensory reflex test. Both male and female SHR rats exhibited sensory impairments at PNDs 7 and 14. All SHR rats tested at PND 7 were unable to orient themselves towards the maternal odor. By PND 14, the SHR animals demonstrated some ability to perform the olfactory recognition task; however, their performance was notably inferior to that of the WKY strain. This study is the first to directly show that gestational swimming improves neurodevelopmental aspects in an animal model of ADHD. Previous study utilizing the same exercise protocol has also demonstrated improved olfactory discrimination in a model of neonatal hypoxia-ischemia, a condition recognized as a risk factor for the development of ADHD.

Naïve SHR rats present evident hyperactivity15,21. Nonetheless, previous findings from our group and many others indicate that interventions such as handling and environmental enrichment influence locomotion in SHR animals19,22,25,55–58. In the current study, the locomotor profile of SHR could be attributed to handling, as all rats were handled during lactation and adolescence. However, we note that handling did not affect the latency to the first entry, central locomotion, or habituation memory of SHR in the OF task. In addition to factors such as handling and environment, maternal care plays a significant role in shaping some behavioral characteristics of SHRs. Previous research has demonstrated that SHR mothers engage more actively with their pups compared to WKY mothers, showing increased licking and spending more extended periods in their presence59,60. These patterns of maternal care could lead to epigenetic changes that affect behaviors throughout life61,62. Furthermore, studies indicate that the level of maternal care can influence the maturation and functionality of impaired dopaminergic pathways associated with ADHD63. However, the impact of gestational swimming on the caregiving behaviors of SHR mothers remains unclear, highlighting the need for further investigation.

Adolescence is marked by behavioral traits such as novelty-seeking, risk-taking, and heightened social interaction, all of which facilitate the development of skills essential for maturity and independence64,65. The maturation of the frontal cortex and executive function abilities renders this period particularly vulnerable to psychiatric disorders and substance abuse66–69. In the present study, SHRs exhibited pronounced risk-taking and novelty-seeking behaviors, coupled with impaired habituation memory. Notably, SHR offspring whose mothers were exercised showed a reduced novelty-seeking behavior. Our findings indicated that swimming during pregnancy reduced the expression of DAT and D2R receptor mRNA in the frontal cortex of SHR offspring. This normalization of D2R expression, along with a tendency towards reduced DAT, could facilitate the regulation of dopaminergic transmission, thereby increasing dopamine availability in the synaptic cleft and improving certain ADHD-related behavioral parameters. Mechanistically, it is plausible that correction of dopaminergic tonus in the frontal cortex is essential for the observed effects, though future experiments are required to confirm this hypothesis. Furthermore, gestational exercise has been shown to improve hippocampal neurogenesis and BDNF levels40,44. Therefore, other brain regions may work in concert with the prefrontal cortex to orchestrate the behavioral benefits induced by gestational exercise in SHRs.

Of note, no changes in the mRNA expression of enzymes controlling dopamine metabolism, including TH, COMT, and MAO-A, were found. While monoamine oxidase B (MAO-B) is greatly relevant to brain dopamine metabolism, notably in astrocytes, MAO-A mRNA expression given it is primarily neuronal, notably in axon terminals70. Early evidence supports that MAO-A expression is higher in younger rats, when ADHD pathogenesis develops, and is the major enzyme metabolizing dopamine at physiological rates in the frontal cortex71,72. Furthermore, MAO-A genetic variants are thought to modulate behavioral manifestations in ADHD73.

In conclusion, current results showed that maternal swimming during pregnancy prevented neonatal development impairments, improved behavioral aspects during adolescence, and normalized the mRNA expression of dopamine D2 receptors in the frontal cortex of an animal model of ADHD. The behavioral effects observed would be linked to other molecular targets in the prefrontal cortex, such as the adenosinergic and endocannabinoid neuromodulation systems17,74. Since physical exercise increases levels of trophic factors and myokines in areas affected by ADHD28,75, investigating the roles of BDNF and irisin in the prefrontal cortex could provide insight into the beneficial effects associated with exercise during pregnancy. The findings support future clinical trials aimed at investigating whether maternal gestational swimming holds potential to be used as a lifestyle-preventative approach for ADHD-associated behaviors.

Methods

Experimental subjects

Male and female isogenic rats were used for mating at approximately 90 days old (WKY and SHR). They were obtained from the Multidisciplinary Center for Biological Research (CEMIB) animal facility at the State University of Campinas (UNICAMP, Brazil). UNICAMP acquired the NTac: WKY and NTac: SHR strains from TACONIC (Germantown, USA). The animals were bred and housed in the sectoral vivarium at the Department of Neurobiology (Federal University, Brazil). Rats were maintained with standard food and water ad libitum and kept in rooms with 12-hour light/dark cycles at 24 °C ± 2 °C. During mating, rats were housed in Plexiglass boxes (42 × 34 × 18 cm): two females and one male per box. Pregnant rats were kept isolated on the twentieth day of gestation until parturition. The WKY rats weighed between 320 and 380 g, while the SHR between 200 and 280 g. The number of pups per litter was standardized according to a previous report25,45, in which 7–8 mothers were used. After weaning, six adolescent rats were housed per cage and separated by sex and strain. Each WKY rat weighed between 35 and 50 g, while each SHR rat weighed between 25 and 40 g. All procedures were performed according to Brazilian legislation after approval of the Federal Fluminense University IACUC under protocol n° 782, following the National Institutes of Health Guide for the care and use of laboratory animals.

Experimental design

Rats were subjected to the swimming protocol from gestational day 0 (GD0) to GD20. Swimming was performed 5 days/week except Saturday and Sunday, and each session lasted 20 min. Offspring were divided in eight experimental groups: WKY SED male (n = 8–9), WKY SED female (n = 7–8), WKY SWIM male (n = 14–15), WKY SWIM female (n = 12–14), SHR SED male (n = 10–13), SHR SED female (n = 8–11), SHR SWIN male (n = 8–12), and SHR SWIN female (n = 8–13). On postnatal days (PND) 7 and 14, neurodevelopmental tests were performed to assess development and sensorimotor reflexes. On PND 21, the animals were weaned and housed according to sex and strain. Behavioral tasks were carried out during adolescence on consecutive days: elevated plus maze (EPM) (PND 30), open field (OF) (PND 31–32), and novelty seeking (PND 33). Rats were moved to the experimental room 1 h before the procedures. Behavioral tests were conducted in a closed, silent room with low-intensity indirect lighting. Equipment and objects were cleaned with a 10% ethanol solution between experiments. Animal behavior was recorded and quantified using AnyMaze®. Twenty-four hours after the last behavioral experiment (PND 34), the frontal cortex was collected for biochemical analysis (Fig. 1). Variables analyzed in this study included strain differences (WKY and SHR), exercise (swimming or sedentary), and sex. This manuscript complies with the general ARRIVE guidelines for experimental design and reporting.

Swimming exercise

Gestational swimming was performed as previously reported25. Swimming was performed daily, starting at GD0, the day of pregnancy confirmation, until GD20. Female animals were split into sedentary (WKY SED, and SHR SED) or exercised groups (WKY SWIM, and SHR SWIM), with 4 pregnant rats per group. Animals in the WKY SWIM and SHR SWIM groups were individually exposed to a 50 × 30 cm plastic tank with 35 cm-high water heated to a temperature of approximately 30–32 °C for 20 min/5 times per week. Control rats were exposed to a tank filled with water at a depth of approximately 1 cm for 5 min. Pregnant rats were weighed every week for pregnancy control.

Neurological reflex testing

ADHD affects cognitive function and the ability to respond appropriately to sensory stimuli. It also impacts motor skills, resulting in deficits in both fine and gross movements. Sensorimotor reflex tests were performed on PND 7 and PND 14 to determine the effects of gestational swimming on neurodevelopment44,55.

Righting reflex

The righting reflex relies on head position information gathered by the utricular and saccular maculae, which help direct the animal’s movement toward the righting reflex55,76. The animal was positioned in a supine stance and restrained for a duration of 5 s. To achieve this, the rat was held on its back, with one hand supporting the lower half and the other hand supporting the upper half. After 5 s, the latency required for the rat to return to its original prone position was measured. The total time taken for the animal to right itself, up to a maximum of 60 s, was recorded.

Negative geotaxis

Negative geotaxis also requires vestibular and motor integration, allowing the assessment of proprioceptive aspects and motor development77. The animal’s ability to orient its body upward in an angular plane improves throughout childhood. The rat was placed on a 30° inclined plane; the apparatus was made of polypropylene (20 × 30 cm). The rat was placed in the apparatus and allowed to freely explore the plane for 2 min. Then, the rat was restrained in the center and oriented with its head pointing downward. The latency for the rat to rotate its body 180° was measured for a maximum of 60 s. Thus, the task was completed after 60 s, and any animals that failed to right themselves within this period were recorded as having taken 60 s.

Olfactory recognition

The olfactory recognition (OR) test was employed to evaluate an animal’s ability to process a familiar odor and navigate toward its source78,79. The OR test serves as a sensory reflex assessment that measures the latency of the animal to orient itself toward this maternal odor. The test was performed in a transparent, rectangular acrylic box (42 × 34 × 18 cm) containing clean sawdust on one side and sawdust from the original cage impregnated with the odor of the mother on the other side. The rat was placed in the center of the enclosure, and its ability to orient to the original sawdust was assessed in a maximum time of 180 s.

Behavioral tasking in adolescence

Behavioral tasks were performed to examine the effects of gestational swimming in the adolescent phase in offspring. These tasks were designed based on findings from previous studies17,18,21,23,58. The first task utilized (PND 30) was the EPM, chosen for its sensitivity to handling effects and prior exposure to other tasks80–82. The initial exposure to the OF arena (PND31), the avoidance-approach paradigm applied to the central area, allows for the assessment of emotional responses in addition to motor activity. Total locomotion typically decreases upon re-exposure to a familiar environment, reflecting the animal’s ability to learn from spatial cues (PND 32). Once the animal has become familiar with the context, objects are introduced to evaluate novelty-seeking behavior (PND 33).

EPM

The elevated plus maze (EPM) operates acts as a conflict test within an approach-avoidance paradigm for a potentially aversive environment83. This task aligns with the innate behaviors of rodents, which inherently avoid open and elevated unprotected spaces. The EPM is an effective tool for assessing defensive behaviors and anxiety-related responses. EPM test was performed in a gray Formica-coated apparatus, in which two open arms (50 × 10 cm) and two closed arms (50 × 10 × 45 cm) are connected by a central platform (10 × 10 cm) at a height of approximately 50 cm. Rats were placed on the central platform with their heads facing one of the open arms and observed for 5 min58,84. Once 90% of the animal’s body has left the center platform, indicating a four-paw exit, an entry into either the open or closed arm is recorded. The percentage of time spent in the open arms and entries was recorded as an indicator of avoidance or anxiety-like behavior. The total number of entries into the closed arms was used to assess locomotor activity.

OF

The open field (OF) test was initially developed by Hall in 1934, who investigated the relationship between defecation and urination measures and the emotional states of rats85. During the initial exposure to the open field, assessments were made of locomotion and avoidance/anxiety behaviors. Comparing locomotion in a subsequent OF session to that of the prior session has been utilized as an indicator of habituation memory86. The OF test was performed in a wooden arena (60 × 60 × 60 cm) whose walls were covered with impermeable white Formica. Rats were placed in the central area, and their locomotor activity was recorded for 10 min on days 1 and 2. On the first day of OF, locomotor activity was assessed in response to a novel environment, and the second day of the task assessed habituation memory, a context that is considered familiar86,87.

Novelty seeking

Novelty seeking is a behavioral trait observed in individuals with ADHD as well as in SHR rats88. This tendency for novelty-seeking behavior is often linked to an increased susceptibility to drug abuse89. On the fourth day of behavioral testing, the motivation of the animals to explore new objects in a familiar environment was evaluated. The animals were placed back in the center of the OF arena, where two identical objects (6 × 6 × 4 cm) were placed equidistant from each other at a distance of 20 cm. The time spent by the animals interacting with these objects was recorded over a duration of five minutes, protocol adapted from Parkitna et al., 201390.

Western blotting

Twenty-four hours after the behavioral battery, the animals were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane, euthanized by decapitation, and the frontal cortex was removed for biochemical analyses. Cortical samples were stored at −80 °C until homogenization in RIPA buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Halt Cocktail, Thermo Fisher). Samples were resolved in pre-cast 4–20% polyacrylamide gels (BioRad®) with Tris/glycine/SDS buffer at 100 V at room temperature. The gel (30 µg total protein/lane) was electroblotted onto Hybond ECL nitrocellulose using 25 mM Tris, 192 mM glycine, 20% methanol (v/v), pH 8.3, at 350 mA for 60 min at 4 °C. Membranes were incubated in 3% BSA in TBS-T (blocking buffer) for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were then incubated under agitation with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer: anti-DAT, anti-THtotal, anti-D2R (1:1,000), or anti-β-actin monoclonal antibody (1:2,000) (Supplementary Table 1) overnight at 4 °C. After incubation with secondary anti-rat or anti-mouse IgGs (1:10,000) for 2 h, the membranes were developed for in an Odyssey imaging apparatus. The images obtained were analyzed using NIH Image J.

RNA extraction and quantitative RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cortical samples using Trizol® (Invitrogen), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, tissue was homogenized in 1 mL Trizol, centrifuged, and the clear aqueous (upper) phase containing total RNA was collected. Samples were then precipitated in 4 volumes of ice-cold isopropanol and washed 3 times in ice-cold ethanol 75% in DEPC water. The RNA pellet was air dried and eluted in nuclease-free water. RNA concentration and purity were determined with Nanodrop at 260/280 nm and 260/230 nm. cDNA synthesis was performed with 1 µg of total RNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, CA). Gene expression was quantitatively determined performed on a 7500 Applied Biosystems real-time PCR system (Foster City, CA) with the Power SYBR Green system and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). β-actin (actb) was used as an endogenous reference gene for data normalization. Primer sequences used for qPCR are described in Supplementary Table 2.

Statistical analyses

All data were analyzed using the Shapiro-Wilk normality test, which indicated that the behavioral data followed a normal distribution. All values were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). For behavioral and biochemical data, three-way ANOVA was used, with treatment, strain, and sex as independent variables. The habituation data were a three-way ANOVA with repeated measures, comparing day 1 with day 2. Following significant ANOVA, multiple post hoc comparisons were performed using the Tukey correction method. Significance was set at p ≤ 0.05. Outliers were identified by ROUT test (Q = 5%). Statistical analyses were fully performed using JASP software (Jeffreys’s Amazing Statistics Program, Amsterdam), and graphs were generated using Prism 8.0 software (Graphpad®). Full statistics data are presented as Supplementary Information (Supplementary Statistics Data).
