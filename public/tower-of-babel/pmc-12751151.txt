Prevalence and correlates of hyperbilirubinemia among people living with HIV (PLHIV) receiving atazanavir boosted with ritonavir (ATV/r)
Authors: Hameedreza Farrokhi, Elnaz Shahmohamadi, Ava Pashaei, Hamid Emadi Koochak, Sahar Nooralioghli Parikhani, Masoumeh Fathi Amrollah, Pegah Mirzapour, Seyed Ali Dehghan Manshadi, SeyedAhmad SeyedAlinaghi, Daniel Hackett
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOne of the recommended protease inhibitor treatments for HIV is Atazanavir boosted with Ritonavir (ATV/r). However, hyperbilirubinemia is a well-recognized adverse effect of this therapy.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study analyzed 121 clinical records of people living with HIV (PLHIV) receiving Atazanavir/Ritonavir treatment at the Iranian Research Center for HIV/AIDS between 2014 and 2020. Study variables were extracted from the digital and physical records at the clinic and recorded using a structured data extraction form developed by the research team. For qualitative variables, results are presented as numbers and percentages, and for quantitative variables, results are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). The authors evaluated the association between demographics, clinical characteristics and laboratory profile with hyperbilirubinemia through univariate and multivariate logistic regression.ResultsHyperbilirubinemia was present in over 85% of the patients, with more than half exhibiting grade III or higher indirect hyperbilirubinemia. No significant associations were found between demographics, ART regimens, or laboratory profiles and hyperbilirubinemia (p > 0.05).ConclusionsThere was a high prevalence of indirect hyperbilirubinemia, with a significant proportion experiencing grade 3–4 elevations. Although no statistically significant associations were observed, it would be valuable to investigate potential genetic predispositions that may make certain individuals more susceptible to developing high-grade hyperbilirubinemia on ATV/r.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04489-4.

Background

HIV and viral proteins can directly contribute to the development of comorbidities, including neurological and neuropsychiatric complications, cardiovascular and metabolic diseases, premature aging, and malignancies. However, given the persistent high prevalence of these comorbidities despite viral suppression, it is likely that antiretroviral therapy (ART), or certain ART agents, also contribute to the development and perpetuation of comorbidities in people living with HIV (PLHIV). These comorbidities often involve vascular activation, injury, and dysfunction [1]. For adult patients initiating ART, the standard recommendation is a combination of two Nucleoside Analog Reverse-Transcriptase Inhibitors (NRTIs) with either a Non-Nucleoside Reverse-Transcriptase Inhibitor (NNRTI) or a Protease Inhibitor (PI) or an Integrase Strand Transfer Inhibitors (INSTI). A commonly prescribed regimen is Atazanavir boosted with Ritonavir (ATV/r), administered once daily [2].

Atazanavir inhibits the formation of mature viruses by blocking the viral proteins Gag and Gag-pol in infected cells. When co-administered with a low dose of Ritonavir, Atazanavir is quickly absorbed, reaching peak plasma levels within three hours, and achieving maximum concentration between four to eight days post-administration [3]. The ATV/r combination exhibits superior clinical antiviral effectiveness and reduced resistance compared to Atazanavir alone. Furthermore, ATV/r is associated with minimal short-term toxicity, primarily presenting as benign hyperbilirubinemia, mainly benign bilirubin elevation, and poses a lower risk of long-term adverse effects such as hyperlipidemia and insulin resistance compared to other PIs [2]. Other reported adverse effects, although less common, include clinically apparent jaundice and nephrolithiasis. Another study found that protease inhibitors induce hyperbilirubinemia without other evidence of liver injury. They are associated with increases in serum unconjugated and total bilirubin and can cause clinically apparent jaundice in up to 10% of patients [4].

Currently, the WHO recommends TDF + 3TC (or FTC) + DTG as the preferred first-line regimen for adults and adolescents, with TDF + 3TC + EFV as an alternative first-line option. For children, ABC + 3TC + DTG is recommended as the first-line regimen, while ABC + 3TC + LPV/r (or TAF + 3TC [or FTC] + DTG) are considered alternative first-line options. Protease inhibitors (PIs), including ATV/r, remain among the preferred second-line ART regimens for adults, adolescents, children, and infants, and may be used in specific circumstances as part of first-line regimens [5]. However, ATV/r and other PIs are no longer routinely prescribed as first-line therapies. Given the availability of more modern ART regimens, this study aimed to investigate one of the reasons for this shift and to provide a cautionary case study on ART-related adverse effects.

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved PIs include atazanavir (ATV), atazanavir/cobicistat (ATV/c), darunavir (DRV), darunavir/cobicistat (DRV/c), fosamprenavir (FPV), indinavir (IDV), lopinavir/ritonavir (LPV/r), nelfinavir (NFV), ritonavir (RTV), saquinavir (SQV), and tipranavir (TPV). Among these, hyperbilirubinemia has been reported more frequently with ATV/r compared to other PIs. Fosamprenavir has been associated with hepatic failure and, in rare cases, clinically significant hyperbilirubinemia [6]. Indinavir is also well known to frequently cause hyperbilirubinemia in patients [7]. In a randomized controlled trial, ritonavir-boosted lopinavir was reported to cause hyperbilirubinemia in 1 of 431 patients [8]. For PIs other than ATV, hyperlipidemia remains the most common adverse effect, whereas for ATV, hyperbilirubinemia is the predominant adverse event of concern. Atazanavir was approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2003 for the treatment of HIV-1 in adults and has since been extensively prescribed in the USA, Europe, and worldwide [3, 9].

The study by Novoa demonstrated that the MDR1 genotype predicts ATV-induced hyperbilirubinemia. Furthermore, both genotype and bilirubin levels were independently associated with plasma ATV concentrations. The MDR1 genotype appears to influence P-gp expression, and the altered activity of this efflux pump subsequently affects ATV plasma levels [10]. Similarly, the study by Ribaudo reported that the UGT1A1*28 genotype was associated with an increased likelihood of hyperbilirubinemia in a large prospective clinical trial. It was also linked to an increased likelihood of atazanavir/ritonavir discontinuation among Hispanic participants, but not among White or Black participants [8]. Although ATV-related hyperbilirubinemia is generally clinically benign, it remains the most common reason for treatment discontinuation, primarily due to cosmetic concerns and the potential for stigmatization, which may ultimately compromise adherence [5].

Considering the critical role of ART and its potential side effects in PLHIV, this study aimed to provide a descriptive prevalence analysis of hyperbilirubinemia among ATV/r recipients, highlighting its contextual and historical significance in PLHIV receiving ATV/r as part of their treatment regimen.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective study analyzed the clinical records of PLHIV treated with Atazanavir/Ritonavir (ATV/r) at the Iranian Research Center for HIV/AIDS (IRCHA) in Tehran, Iran. IRCHA is affiliated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences (TUMS) and represents the first academic research center in Iran dedicated to both basic and community-based HIV/AIDS research. During the study period, the center followed the World Health Organization (WHO) Option B regimen according to national guidelines, initiating ART for patients regardless of CD4 count [11].

The study was performed as a census; therefore, all patient records of individuals receiving ATV/r were reviewed in chronological order based on their record identifiers.

Study participants and data collection

The study population included PLHIV who received Atazanavir/Ritonavir (ATV/r) between 2014 and 2020. Clinical records of these participants were reviewed to extract relevant data for the study.

Study variables were extracted from the digital and physical medical records at the center and recorded using a structured data extraction form developed by the research team. The form included the following variables: identification code, age, gender, total and indirect bilirubin, CD4 count, liver enzyme levels, hepatitis B and C serology, duration of drug use, type of treatment regimen, and regimens before and after drug use (if any).

Bilirubin and liver enzyme tests were categorized into five grades according to the AIDS Clinical Trials Group classification (Table 1) [12, 13], using the highest normal reference value for each laboratory parameter as the reference point: Grade 0: normal; Grade 1: mild; Grade 2: moderate; Grade 3: severe; and Grade 4: extreme. Grades are calculated by dividing the lab values by ULN (Upper Limit of Normal).

The study excluded cases in which the bilirubin test was performed either more than 7 days after treatment cessation or within 7 days of treatment initiation. Additionally, patients who received ATV/r as post-exposure prophylaxis were not included in the study.

For each patient, the laboratory value used was the latest available value either before discontinuation (if the exclusion criteria were not met) or the only available value within the acceptable time frame.

All data used in this study were obtained from patients’ physical records at IRCHA, as very little data had been digitized at the time. Therefore, all information was gathered from paper records available at IRCHA.

Ethical consideration

In this study, we did not intervene with patient treatment or directly access their medical records. Data were collected solely by reviewing medical records, and during the data compilation phase, patient confidentiality was strictly maintained by using unique identifiers instead of names. This ensured that patient medical information remained secure, upholding the ethical principles of confidentiality and privacy. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the ethics committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences (IR.TUMS.IKHC.REC.1398.005).

Data analysis

After extracting and recording the data, Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS version 28.0, IBM) was used for data analysis. For qualitative variables, the results are presented as numbers and percentages, and for quantitative variables, the results are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). In this study, the authors evaluated the association between demographic (gender and age), clinical characteristics (treatment duration, HCV + and ART regimen) and laboratory profile (CD4 count) with hyperbilirubinemia through univariate and multivariate logistic regression. The variables with p < 0.20 in the univariate analysis were entered into the multivariate model. P-values less than 0.05 were considered significant in the final model.

Data were collected in IRCHA with minimal missing data. Given that there was very little missing data in the dataset, the missing data were determined using code “99” without replacement in the variables.

Results

A total of 121 patient records were reviewed, none of which met the exclusion criteria; therefore, all cases were included in the final analysis. Among these patients, 74 (61.2%) were male. Sixteen patients (13.3%) had a CD4 count below 200 cells/mm³. The most frequently prescribed antiretroviral regimen was ATV/r in combination with Tenofovir and Emtricitabine (Truvada), administered to 71 patients (58.7%), followed by Lamivudine with Zidovudine in 26 patients (21.5%). Tenofovir with Lamivudine was prescribed for 10 patients (8.3%), Abacavir with Lamivudine for 11 patients (9.1%). Dolutegravir, Raltegravir, and Lopinavir/Ritonavir (Kaletra) were each prescribed for one patient (0.8%). Elevated indirect bilirubin was observed in 104 patients (86.0%), while increased total bilirubin was detected in 103 patients (85.1%). Severe total hyperbilirubinemia (Grade ≥ III) was recorded in 52 patients (43.0%), and severe indirect hyperbilirubinemia of similar grade was observed in 61 patients (50.4%). With respect to hepatic enzymes, the most frequent abnormalities involved ALT and AST, each elevated in 14 patients (11.6%) (Table 2).

Discussion

Unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia is the most common laboratory abnormality associated with ATV/r therapy and may result in jaundice and treatment discontinuation [14]. Therefore, it is important to emphasize that even clinically benign adverse effects can impact adherence and should always be taken into consideration. It has been suggested that avoiding UGT1A1 inhibitors could be a consideration in future ARV drug development, given the substantial burden of this adverse effect. In this study, we evaluated the prevalence and correlates of hyperbilirubinemia in PLHIV who received ATV/r as part of their treatment regimen. Notably, a significant proportion of participants, 43% overall and 50.4% for indirect bilirubin, experienced grade 3 or 4 hyperbilirubinemia. In the CASTLE randomized trial, over a 96-week period, 44% of patients receiving atazanavir/ritonavir experienced grade 3–4 hyperbilirubinemia at some point. Importantly, less than 1% of patients discontinued atazanavir due to hyperbilirubinemia-related issues, including jaundice or scleral icterus [15]. In our study, however, 15.7% of patients discontinued ATV/r because of jaundice, which is substantially higher than reported in other studies (5.0%) [16, 17]. This discrepancy may be explained by several factors, including differences in drug availability, socioeconomic conditions, health system factors, and cultural perceptions of jaundice, which can strongly influence patient and physician decisions regarding continuation of therapy. These findings underscore the importance of considering the local context, including patient perceptions, regional healthcare practices, and access to alternative antiretroviral therapies, in shaping treatment outcomes [18].

Unlike our findings, which showed no significant differences in total or indirect bilirubin levels between genders, a cohort study involving 2,404 PLHIV with a hyperbilirubinemia prevalence similar to ours identified female gender and the use of NNRTIs as protective factors against hyperbilirubinemia [19]. Additionally, another study examining the relationship between atazanavir plasma concentrations and hyperbilirubinemia in PLHIV reported higher plasma drug levels in females, with hyperbilirubinemia levels above 1.3 mg/dL being a risk factor for treatment discontinuation in this group [10].

There was no significant association between ART regimens and hyperbilirubinemia. Findings from Laprise et al. showed that Zidovudine use was identified as a risk factor for hyperbilirubinemia among atazanavir recipients [20].

Hyperbilirubinemia associated with ATV does not appear to impact hepatic function. No significant association was observed between hyperbilirubinemia and elevations in liver transaminases compared with patients without hyperbilirubinemia [21, 22]. The majority of antiretroviral regimens are considered safe for individuals coinfected with HIV and HCV, including those with cirrhosis [23, 24]. Similarly, this study investigated the relationship between bilirubin and hepatitis C serology and found no statistically significant association, likely due to the small sample size.

The primary symptomatic adverse events linked to the use of ATV/r include scleral icterus and jaundice, resulting in discontinuation in less than 1% of participants in clinical trials [14]. In one study, the protease inhibitors Indinavir (IDV) and Lopinavir (LPV) were associated with kidney stone formation. Atazanavir is generally well tolerated and widely used with increased doses of ritonavir, but it has been associated with crystalluria and nephrolithiasis; discontinuation of treatment prevented new kidney stone formation, whereas continued treatment left patients at risk for recurrence. The higher creatinine (Cr), albumin, and alanine transaminase (ALT) activity in the Dolutegravir group suggests that Dolutegravir impairs the kidneys’ ability to excrete creatinine from the body [25]. In our research, 15.7% of patients discontinued ATV/r due to jaundice, a rate higher than that reported in the AIDS Clinical Trials Group A5257 study (5.0%) [16] and the REMAIN study (4.8%) [17]. These findings highlight the broader considerations that clinicians must take into account when selecting ART regimens, beyond the direct toxicity of ATV/r [26].

A study investigated the effects of a Dolutegravir- and protease inhibitor-based regimen on markers of renal and hepatic function in HIV patients receiving ART. The study found significant differences (p ≤ 0.05) in serum levels of direct bilirubin (DB), total bilirubin (TB), creatinine, and microalbumin (MA) between patients treated with Dolutegravir (DTG) plus PIs and the control group. The Dolutegravir-based regimen demonstrated relatively lower mean values for DB, TB, and MA, but higher mean values for creatinine, albumin, and ALT compared with the protease inhibitor-based regimen and the control group [26].

In general, when hyperbilirubinemia occurs, the recommended management strategy is to perform appropriate laboratory evaluations, including measurement of total and indirect bilirubin and other liver function tests. Potential causes of hyperbilirubinemia should then be assessed, and if no other underlying issue is identified, the condition may be attributed to ATV-induced hyperbilirubinemia. In such cases, ATV is discontinued and replaced with an alternative antiretroviral agent, and the patient is monitored until bilirubin levels return to normal. Given the prevalence of hyperbilirubinemia among individuals receiving Atazanavir, some sources emphasize the importance of informing patients about this possible adverse effect and recommend regular monitoring. Overall, ATV-induced hyperbilirubinemia is generally benign and does not indicate liver injury. Clinical improvement is usually observed following the change in medication [17, 27].

Future research on the pharmacogenomic (e.g., UGT1A1 gene) and pharmacokinetic aspects of ATV/r-associated hyperbilirubinemia is needed to support the development of improved ARV drugs. One of the major limitations of protease inhibitors (PIs) in general has been their adverse effects. Although ATV/r (and other PIs) has been deprecated as a first-line ARV in recent guidelines, it continues to serve as an alternative or second-line option in certain cases. It is therefore important to highlight the challenges in selecting appropriate regimens, considering all factors including benign (but undesirable) adverse effects, costs, and availability, which, although they may seem less critical, can ultimately become major determinants in treatment decisions. Given the prevalence and clinical impact of hyperbilirubinemia in our local setting, it is essential to inform patients about this potential adverse effect and the need for regular monitoring. Although ATV/r is no longer recommended as first-line therapy in updated guidelines [28], it remains a valuable alternative in specific contexts, particularly where other regimens are limited or unavailable. Our findings provide a descriptive account of ATV/r-associated toxicity in a real-world clinical setting, offering insights for both local clinical practice and future antiretroviral drug development [29].

Limitations and strengths

This study has limitations stemming from a small sample size, making it challenging to claim representativeness for the broader population of patients with HIV infection undergoing ART. Consequently, the study was likely underpowered to detect potential associations between hyperbilirubinemia and patient characteristics, ART regimens, or laboratory parameters, which should be considered when interpreting these findings. Additionally, a significant portion of the study population was using combination regimens, introducing potential biases that should be considered when evaluating the effects of ATV/r. Another limitation is that only the latest laboratory value before discontinuation was used, which may have introduced variability and should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, the study offers a valuable opportunity to evaluate the overall trends of hyperbilirubinemia and its clinical impact in patients treated with ATV/r within a tertiary center specializing in the treatment of HIV patients.

Although studies have shown that genetic predisposition, particularly UGT1A1 polymorphisms, affects bilirubin levels, our study could not assess this due to the unavailability of the relevant data. Furthermore, the higher rate of treatment discontinuation due to jaundice observed in our study, compared with rates reported in high-income settings, may be influenced by regional differences in the availability of alternative modern ART regimens, such as INSTIs.

Since the time this study was conducted, guidelines have been revised, and several antiretroviral regimens (including boosted ATV variants) are no longer recommended as initial therapy due to higher pill burdens, increased adverse effects, or lower barriers to resistance compared with other recommended regimens [28]. The study provides valuable local insights into the prevalence and clinical impact of hyperbilirubinemia, highlighting challenges related to treatment adherence and regimen selection within our HIV care center.

Additionally, multiple testing corrections, such as Bonferroni adjustments, were not applied to the analyses; therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

The authors reported a high prevalence of indirect hyperbilirubinemia, with a significant proportion experiencing grade 3–4 elevations. Logistic regression results showed wide confidence intervals and non-significant findings. Though no statistically significant associations were found, this study was likely underpowered to detect associations between hyperbilirubinemia and patients’ demographics, ART regimens, or laboratory profiles due to the limited sample size. While the underlying biological mechanisms remain unclear, identifying patient-level factors influencing hyperbilirubinemia could help improve individualized ART management in similar clinical contexts.

Furthermore, this typically benign complication may serve as a useful marker for monitoring patient adherence and treatment response during routine evaluations. This study provides a historical safety analysis of ATV/r, highlighting its relevance as a cautionary case for future ARV development. Although ATV/r is no longer recommended as a first-line regimen globally, the findings emphasize the ongoing importance of monitoring drug-related toxicities and ensuring equitable access to safer, modern ART options in resource-limited settings.
