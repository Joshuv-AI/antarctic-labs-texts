Equilibria in Large Position-Optimization Games
Rafael Frongillo, Melody Hsu, Mary Monroe, Anish Thilagar

Abstract
We propose a general class of symmetric games called position-optimization games. Given a probability distribution Q Q over a set of targets 𝒴 \mathcal{Y} , the n n players each choose a position in a space 𝒳 \mathcal{X} . A player’s utility is the Q Q -mass of targets they are closest to under some proximity measure, with ties broken evenly. Our model captures Hotelling games and forecasting competitions, among other applications. We show that for sufficiently large n n , both pure and symmetric mixed Nash equilibria exist, and moreover are extreme : all players play on a finite set of psuedo-targets 𝒳 ∗ ⊆ 𝒳 \mathcal{X}^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{X} . We further show that both pure and symmetric mixed equilibria converge to the distribution P P on 𝒳 ∗ \mathcal{X}^{*} induced by Q Q , and bound the convergence rate in n n . The generality of our model allows us to extend and strengthen previous work in Hotelling games, and prove entirely new results in forecasting competitions and other applications.

For example, in a Hotelling game, retailers will follow the distribution of consumer preferences over positions when they set up shop; in forecasting competitions, forecaster reports will mimic the underlying distribution over outcomes.

Despite using similar techniques, the above results all appear in disjoint models—sometimes only loosely related, as in Hotelling versus forecasting—with no clear technical connection between them.
Moreover, several important questions remain unanswered.
Most glaringly, there is no existing work on general equilibria of forecasting competitions for any finite value of

In this paper, we give general equilibrium results and convergence rates for position-optimization games for large

For sufficiently large

As discussed further in §

We also extend previous asymptotic results of the original Hotelling game to non-differentiable (specifically, finite-support) consumer distributions, and strengthen the assumptions and results for finite-location Hotelling games.
We suspect that our work implies novel results for other unexplored instances of this general game as well.

1.1
Motivating examples

Position-optimization games arise across many domains, including network science and voting theory.
We outline several below, and later reference these settings to illustrate our general results.

1.

Forecasting competitions.

In forecasting competitions, forecasters compete to make the best predictions of
m
random events.
One example is the machine learning competition platform Kaggle, where forecasters are provided with the same set of training data and compete to develop the best prediction models.
These competitions typically score each forecaster according to a strictly proper scoring rule, which is known to be truthful in isolation.
However, others have observed that the winner-takes-all nature of the competition causes strategic behavior in the simplest one-event case
(
6
)
.
This tension has motivated extensive game-theoretic study of such competitions
(
4
;
15
;
8
)
.
Under the position-optimization game framework, we observe strategic non-truthful reporting, even when agents share a common-knowledge belief
Q
.

Formally, we model the random events as a vector
Y\in\{0,1\}^{m}
with distribution
Q\in\Delta\left(\{0,1\}^{m}\right)
.
Each forecaster
i
submits a vector of marginals for each event
x_{i}\in[0,1]^{m}=:\mathcal{X}
.
The winner is the forecaster whose prediction has the highest accuracy as judged e.g. by the Brier scoring rule, which corresponds to the
x_{i}
closest to
Y
in
\ell_{2}
-distance (ties broken at random).
We visualize this model of forecasting competitions for
m=3
in Figure
3
.

2.

Finite-location Hotelling games.

Hotelling games are a well-studied class of spatial games involving a set of

and

Retailers choose where to set up their store locations in some space, in order to attract the largest mass of consumers under some distribution.
Consider the finite-location model introduced by
10

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a compact subset of a Euclidean space with distance measure
d
.
Then retailers choose locations
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})

\mathcal{X}=\{1,\ldots,k\}\subset\mathcal{S}

Consumers in
\mathcal{S}
are distributed according to an absolutely continuous distribution

\mathcal{S}

Each retailer’s utility is then the share of customers who patronize their shops, given each customer will visit the location that is preferred most in
\bm{x}

.
We give a visualization of this game in Figure

Classic Hotelling games.

The classic Hotelling game allows retailers to choose any location on the unit interval, unlike the finite-location version.
Consumers follow some distribution on the same interval.

In one-round discrete Voronoi games

1.2
Related work

The majority of related work studies variations of the classic Hotelling game, where firms compete for consumers by location (and not on price), with the firm capturing the set of consumers closest to them.

Their results show that pure equilibria exist, and the empirical distribution in pure equilibria converges to the true underlying mass of first-place consumer rankings over the finite set of locations.
However, they do not provide rates of convergence in

The authors also assume preferences are strict, whereas we only require that consumers have a unique first preference.

A follow-up paper

Given this fact, all further work in the area either restricts to two-player settings with specific instantiations of

Setting

2.1
Model

Let
\mathcal{X}
be an arbitrary action set; we call
x\in\mathcal{X}
a
position
.
Let
\mathcal{Y}
be an arbitrary target set; we call
y\in\mathcal{Y}
a
target
.
We place no assumptions of finiteness on
\mathcal{X}
or
\mathcal{Y}
, nor do we assume that they coincide or are subsets of the same space.
Let
d:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
be a
proximity function
over positions and targets.
Let
x^{*}(y)=\arg\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}d(x,y)
be that minimizer, which we refer to as a
pseudo-target
.
Let
\mathcal{X}^{*}=\bigcup_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}x^{*}(y)
be the set of all pseudo-targets for each
y\in\mathcal{Y}
.
Define
\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
to be the set of all mass distributions over
\mathcal{Y}
, and let
Q\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
be a distribution over targets.
We impose the following conditions on
Q
and
\mathcal{X}^{*}
:

Condition 1
.

Q(\{y:|x^{*}(y)|>1\})=0
and
|\mathcal{X}^{*}|<\infty
.

The first part of Condition
1
, consistent with
10
, allows us to ignore ties; relaxing this assumption would require a more intricate analysis.
The second part, requiring that the set of pseudo-targets is finite, is more fundamental to our approach and thus appears more challenging to relax; see §
6
for discussion.

Definition 1
.

A
position-optimization game

\mathcal{G}_{n}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y},d,Q)
is a game on
n
agents satisfying Condition
1
.
The pure strategy of each agent
i\in[n]
is a position
x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
; let
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
be the vector of
n
players’ positions.
Let
X_{\min}(\bm{x},y)=\arg\min_{x_{i}\in\bm{x}}d(x_{i},y)
be the set of player positions that minimize proximity to target
y
.
Let

\pi_{i}(x_{i},\bm{x}_{-i},y)=\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{x_{i}\in X_{\min}(\bm{x},y)}}{|X_{\min}(\bm{x},y)|}

(1)

represent the share of target
y
that agent
i
receives by playing location
x_{i}
, where target
y
randomizes uniformly over its minimum proximity set
X_{\min}(\bm{x},y)
.
Then agent
i
’s utility in the game is

u_{i}(x_{i},\bm{x}_{-i})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y\sim Q}\pi_{i}(x_{i},\bm{x}_{-i},y).

(2)

That is, the objective of each of agent is to choose a location
x_{i}
that minimizes the proximity function
d
to
y\in\mathcal{Y}
subject to the distribution
Q
over targets and to the locations of the other players
\bm{x}_{-i}
.

We can observe that
\mathcal{G}_{n}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y},d,Q)
is a symmetric game, since for any permutation
\pi
over the
[n]
and for all
i\in[n]
,
u_{\pi(i)}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i},\ldots,x_{n})=u_{i}(x_{\pi(1)},\ldots,x_{\pi(i)},\ldots,x_{\pi(n)})
.

Mixed strategies.

We will also consider mixed strategies, where each agent
i
chooses a strategy
\sigma_{i}\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})
.
A mixed strategy profile for all players is given by
\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n})
.
Let
\bm{\sigma}_{-i}=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{i-1},\sigma_{i+1},\dots,\sigma_{n})
indicate the strategy of all players other than
i
.
Under strategy profile
\sigma
, agent
i
receives expected utility

u_{i}(\sigma_{i},\bm{\sigma}_{-i})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{i}\sim\sigma_{i},x_{-i}\sim\bm{\sigma}_{-i}}\left[u_{i}(x_{i},\bm{x}_{-i})\right].

(3)

For any subset
I\subseteq\mathcal{X}
,
\sigma_{i}(I)
denotes the probability that player
i
chooses a reported position
x_{i}\in I
.
In an abuse of notation, for a single position
x\in\mathcal{X}
we let
\sigma(x)
denote
\sigma(\{x\})
.
We use the shorthand
u_{i}(x_{i},\bm{\sigma}_{-i})=u_{i}(\mathbbm{1}[x_{i}],\bm{\sigma}_{-i})
to be the utility of agent
i
when they play the pure strategy of always reporting position
x_{i}
.

We can now define several notions of Nash equilibria over the game
\mathcal{G}_{n}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y},d,Q)
.

Definition 2

(Pure Nash equilibrium)
.

A
pure Nash equilibrium
is a set of positions
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
such that for all
i\in[n]
and all
x_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
,
u_{i}(x_{i}^{\prime},\bm{x}_{-i})\leq u_{i}(x_{i},\bm{x}_{-i})
.

Definition 3

(Mixed Nash equilibrium)
.

A
mixed Nash equilibrium
is a strategy profile
\bm{\sigma}\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})^{n}
such that for all
i\in[n]
and all
\sigma_{i}^{\prime}\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})^{n}
,
u_{i}(\sigma_{i}^{\prime},\bm{\sigma}_{-i})\leq u_{i}(\sigma_{i},\bm{\sigma}_{-i})
.

Definition 4

(Symmetric Mixed Nash equilibrium)
.

A
symmetric mixed Nash equilibrium
is a single mixed strategy
\sigma\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})
such that
\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma,\sigma,\ldots,\sigma)
is a mixed Nash equilibrium.

We define strategy profiles as
extreme
when agents play positions exclusively in
\mathcal{X}^{*}
:

Definition 5
.

A pure strategy profile
\bm{x}
is
extreme
if for all
i\in[n]
,
x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}^{*}
.
A mixed strategy profile
\bm{\sigma}
is extreme if for all
i\in[n]
,
\supp(\sigma_{i})\subseteq\mathcal{X}^{*}
.

We will later find in both pure and symmetric mixed settings that for large enough
n
, all equilibria are extreme.
It is thus useful for us to compare each equilibrium’s distribution over
\mathcal{X}^{*}
to
P
, the projection of
Q
onto
\mathcal{X}^{*}
.
Formally, for an instance of
\mathcal{G}_{n}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y},d,Q)
, let

P(x)=Q(\{y\in\mathcal{Y}:x^{*}(y)=x\})

be the total mass of targets
y\in\mathcal{Y}
for which the pseudo-target
x
minimizes proximity.
In other words,
P
projects the distribution
Q
over targets onto the set of minimizers
\mathcal{X}^{*}
, so that we can relate the distribution of agent strategies over
\mathcal{X}^{*}
back to
Q
.
We also denote
p_{0}=\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}^{*}}P(x)
as the pseudo-target with the smallest mass relative to
P
.

2.2
Motivating example models

We can now outline how the model for
\mathcal{G}_{n}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y},d,Q)
maps to the forecasting and Hotelling games introduced in §
1
; for the others, we refer the reader to Appendix

.

1.

Forecasting competitions.

The
n
strategic agents are forecasters.

\mathcal{Y}:=\{0,1\}^{m}
, i.e. each target
y
is a specific instantiation of the random event vector
Y
with distribution
Q
;
\mathcal{X}:=[0,1]^{m}
, so each position corresponds to a forecast over the random event vector
Y
; and
d(x,y):=\lVert x-y\rVert_{2}
.
It follows that
\mathcal{X}^{*}=\mathcal{Y}
with
x^{*}(y)=y
; and since

.
That is, the set of pseudo-targets is the set of vertices (or event vectors) on the hypercube, and so the distribution over
\mathcal{Y}

\mathcal{X}^{*}=\mathcal{Y}
.
Since

The ex-ante utility of an agent is then

u_{i}(x_{i},\bm{x}_{-i})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y\sim Q}\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\lVert x_{i}-y\rVert_{2}\leq\max_{j\neq i}\lVert x_{i}-y\rVert_{2}}}{\left|\arg\min_{j\in[n]}\lVert x_{j}-y\rVert_{2}\right|}.

2.

Finite-location Hotelling games.

The
n
strategic agents are retailers, playing in space
(\mathcal{S},d).

\mathcal{X}:=\{1,\ldots,k\}\subset\mathcal{S}
is a finite set of locations retailers can choose from.
The target set
\mathcal{Y}
is the (potentially non-finite) set of consumers, with corresponding distribution
\lambda
over
\mathcal{S}
.
The distance function is simply
d(x,y)
.
Each consumer

can be mapped to a (potentially non-strict) ranking
\pi_{y}
over
\mathcal{X}
induced by

.
The pseudo-target set for consumer

is
x^{*}(y)=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:\pi_{y}(x)=1\}
, so that
\mathcal{X}^{*}=\bigcup_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\{x\in\mathcal{X}:\pi_{y}(x)=1\}
is finite.
Since the distribution
\lambda

\lambda(y:|x^{*}(y)|>1)=0
; therefore, Condition
1
is satisfied.
Then
P(x)=\lambda(\{y\in\mathcal{Y}:\pi_{y}(1)=x\})
, i.e.
P(x)
is the mass of consumers who prefer location

first.
Let
k_{\bm{x}}(x)
be the number of agents in location profile
\bm{x}
.
The utility of each retailer is

u_{i}(x_{i},\bm{x}_{-i})=\frac{\lambda(\{y\in\mathcal{Y}:\pi_{y}(x_{i})\leq\pi_{y}(x_{j})\;\forall j\neq i\})}{k_{\bm{x}}(x_{i})}.

One could also map the more general setting of

\mathcal{X}
and

Here, each consumer is mapped explicitly to some ranking

.

Classic Hotelling games.

Again, the
n
strategic agents are retailers, now playing in space

.

represents a discrete consumer distribution over

, and the target set
\supp(Q)=:\mathcal{Y}
is the set of consumers.
The distance function is

, so that

.

For each

3
Pure Nash Equilibria

In this section, we study pure Nash equilibria, where agent strategies over positions are deterministic.
Throughout,
k_{\bm{x}}(x)
denotes the number of agents on position
x\in\mathcal{X}
for strategy profile
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}.

3.1
Initial observations

We begin by making some observations about pure strategy profiles which will be useful later on to characterize equilibria.
First, our game is constant-sum, since

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[n]}u_{i}(x_{i},\bm{x}_{-i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y\sim Q}\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{x_{i}\in X_{\min}(\bm{x},y)}}{|X_{\min}(\bm{x},y)|}=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y\sim Q}\frac{1}{|X_{\min}(\bm{x},y)|}\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathbbm{1}_{x_{i}\in X_{\min}(\bm{x},y)}=1.

Observation 1
.

For any strategy profile
\bm{x}
,
\sum_{i\in[n]}u_{i}(x_{i},\bm{x}_{-i})=1
.

As a result, there exists an agent with utility at most
\frac{1}{n}
: otherwise,
u_{i}(x_{i},\bm{x}_{-i})>\frac{1}{n}
for all agents
i
, and
\sum_{i\in[n]}u_{i}(x_{i},\bm{x}_{-i})>\sum_{i\in[n]}\frac{1}{n}=1
.
Symmetrically, there exists an agent with utility at least
\frac{1}{n}
.

Observation 2
.

Under any strategy profile
\bm{x}
, there exists an agent
i
such that
u_{i}(x_{i},\bm{x}_{-i})\leq\frac{1}{n},
and an agent
j
such that
u_{j}(x_{j},\bm{x}_{-j})\geq\frac{1}{n}.

We can now relate the utility an agent gains by choosing a pseudo-target
x
(whether as a strategy or deviation) to
P(x)
and
k_{\bm{x}}(x)
.
Intuitively, an agent playing some pseudo-target
x\in\mathcal{X}^{*}
is always in the minimum set
X_{\min}
for any of the targets
y
associated with
x
, so that their utility share is at least
\frac{P(x)}{k_{\bm{x}}{x}}.

Using the same logic, if an agent then deviates to some
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}^{*}
, their new utility will be at least
\frac{P(x)}{k_{\bm{x}}{x}+1}
.

Definition 6
.

We say a strategy profile
\bm{x}

covers
\mathcal{X}^{*}
if
k_{\bm{x}}(x)\geq 1
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}^{*}
.

Lemma 1
.

Consider a strategy profile
\bm{x}
where for some agent
i
,
x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}^{*}.

Then

u_{i}(x_{i},\bm{x}_{-i})\geq\frac{P(x_{i})}{k_{\bm{x}}(x_{i})}
, and equality holds if
\bm{x}
covers
\mathcal{X}^{*}
.
For any deviation
x^{\prime}\neq x_{i}
,

Theorem 1

3.3
Equilibrium convergence and rates

Consider the setting where

By Theorems

Definition 7

Let the empirical distribution,

induced by equilibrium profile
\bm{x}

That is,
\hat{P}_{\bm{x}}(x)=\frac{k_{\bm{x}}(x)}{n}

\bm{x}

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}^{*}}\hat{P}_{\bm{x}}(x)=1

Now we have the tools to prove our main wisdom result.
We first show that in equilibrium, all pseudo-targets are covered by a number of agents as a function of a more general lower bound on

(Lemma

This allows us to tightly bound the utility of any player in equilibrium in Lemma

to

.
We leave proofs of the Lemmas to Appendix

Lemma 5
.

Assume

.

\bm{x}
, for all

, the number of agents on

.

Lemma 6
.

Assume

.

\bm{x}
, the win probability of any player must satisfy

.

Assume

Consider any equilibrium
\bm{x}

every

x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}^{*}
is covered by some agent, someone else is always closer in proximity to
y
and
i
gains no utility.
Meanwhile, if
\mathcal{X}^{*}
is not covered, we crudely upper bound
i
’s utility by assuming they
always
win
y
.
We thus have,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\pi_{i}(x_{i},\bm{x}_{-i},y)\mathbbm{1}_{E_{3}}]\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbbm{1}_{E_{3}\setminus C}]\leq 1-\Pr[C].

(8)

Lemma
11
shows
1-\Pr[C]\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}
for some lower bound on
n
which is a function of
p_{0}
.
The proof follows standard coupon-collector arguments, using the lower bound on
\sigma_{x}
above.
The same logic shows the extremeness of
\sigma
(Proposition
3
), where now we consider
x\notin\mathcal{X}^{*}
, and note that
x
has utility 0 on
C
.
Thus
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\pi_{i}(x,\bm{x}_{-i},y)]\leq 1-\Pr[C]\leq\tfrac{1}{n^{2}}<\tfrac{1}{n}
, meaning
x\notin\supp(\sigma)
by Observation
3
.

Returning to the upper bound on
\sigma_{x}
, we now combine eq. (
8
) with eq. (
6
), giving

\displaystyle u_{i}(x,\bm{\sigma}_{-i})\leq\frac{p_{x}}{n\sigma_{x}}\left(1-\sigma_{x}^{n}-(1-\sigma_{x})^{n}\right)+\frac{1}{n}\sigma_{x}^{n-1}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}.

(9)

We can thus proceed using a similar argument as the lower bound.
Specifically, in equilibrium, we know
u_{i}(x,\bm{\sigma}_{-i})=\frac{1}{n},
allowing us to lower bound the right-hand side of eq. (
9
) by
\frac{1}{n}
to recover a relationship between
\sigma_{x}
and
p_{x}
(Lemma
13
).
Unfortunately, the resulting inverse relation is not as nice: monotonicity breaks when
\sigma_{x}
is too large, intuitively because agents can always achieve utility
\frac{1}{n}
by all playing
x
; with the addition of utility conditioned on
E_{3}
, a player can secure utility above
\frac{1}{n}
.
In light of this technical challenge, we are able to prove the upper bound in Proposition
4
through careful casework.

An example when
|\mathcal{X}^{*}|=2
.

As an illustrative example, we depict our analysis in the fundamental forecasting setting where forecasters submit predictions for a single binary-outcome event
x
, i.e., when
m=1
(and hence
|\mathcal{X}^{*}|=2
); concretely, forecasters’ symmetric equilibrium strategy
\sigma_{x}
is the weight they assign to the pure strategy of reporting
x=1
.
We consider the case where
n
satisfies the bound in Proposition

, so that equilibria are extreme.
In this setting, we are able to pin down the symmetric mixed equilibrium exactly.
In particular, the utility is given by eq. (
6
) as an equality, since the expected utility conditioned on

is 0; i.e.,

u_{i}(x,\bm{\sigma}_{-i})=\frac{p_{x}}{n\sigma_{x}}(1-\sigma_{x}^{n}-(1-\sigma_{x})^{n})+\frac{1}{n}\sigma_{x}^{n-1}.

When the expression for

is exact, the strategy
\sigma_{x}
satisfies
\sigma_{x}=G^{-1}(p_{x})
.
Meanwhile, for
p_{x}<\frac{1}{n}
and

, the only equilibria are to play

and
\sigma_{x}=1
, respectively, by Lemma
10
(which automatically applies when
p_{x}<\frac{1}{n}
as
1-p_{x}>1-\frac{1}{n}
).

,

places

on the outcome of

that is more likely under

.
The striking emergence of non-truthful behavior even under our stylized position-game model suggests that this example encapsulates the core insight of strategic behavior in more complex settings.

5
Applications

In this section, we aim to illustrate how the results for the general abstract game
\mathcal{G}_{n}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y},d,Q)
apply to each of the specific games introduced in §
1
, and what the results imply in each of their different contexts.

5.1
Forecasting competitions

We begin by outlining results for forecasting competitions.
As a reminder, forecasters submit predictions
x_{i}\in[0,1]^{m}
over a random vector
Y\in\{0,1\}^{m}
of
m
binary events with distribution
Q
, which is common-knowledge.

\mathcal{Y}:=\{0,1\}^{m}
is the set of event vector realizations, with
\mathcal{X}^{*}=\mathcal{Y}
, so that a strategy profile is extreme if forecasters only submit deterministic marginal predictions of 0 or 1 for each event.
Let
P
be the projection of
Q
onto
\mathcal{X}^{*}
.
Note that
p_{0}\leq 2^{-m},
so that
n\geq\frac{2}{p_{0}}
implies
n\geq 2^{m+1}
.

Corollary 1
.

(Forecasting)
If
n>\frac{1}{p_{0}}
, then every pure equilibrium
\bm{x}
is extreme (for each forecaster
i
,
\bm{x}_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
) and covers
\mathcal{Y}
(for each
y\in\{0,1\}^{m}
, there exists a forecaster
i
such that
\bm{x}_{i}=y
).
If
n\geq\frac{2}{p_{0}}
, then there exists an extreme, pure Nash equilibrium such that for all
y\in\{0,1\}^{m}
,
k_{\bm{x}}(y)\geq 2
, and if
n>\frac{2}{p_{0}}
, then this condition holds for all pure Nash equilibria.
Moreover, if
n>c\geq\frac{2}{p_{0}}
, for any pure equilibrium
\bm{x}
,

D_{\text{KL}}(\hat{P}_{\bm{x}}\;\|\;P)\leq\log\left(\frac{\lfloor cn\rfloor+1}{\lfloor cn\rfloor}\right),

where
\hat{P}_{\bm{x}}
is the empirical distribution on
\{0,1\}^{m}
induced by
\bm{x}
.

Symmetric mixed Nash equilibria exist for any
n>1
.
If
n>\max\left\{43,8\left(\frac{4}{p_{0}}\log\frac{1}{p_{0}}\right)\right\}
, any symmetric mixed Nash equilibrium
\sigma
is extreme, and for all
y\in\{0,1\}^{m}
, we have
|\sigma(y)-Q(y)|\leq\frac{1}{n}
.

In other words, when the number of forecasters is large relative to the number of events, forecasters will not submit their true predictions about
Y
under their belief distribution
Q
.
Instead, forecasters concentrate their strategies on the potential
outcomes
of the random events.
When agents employ pure strategies, the resulting equilibrium profile will then mirror the underlying distribution
Q
over
Y
, so that while individually forecasters do not submit reports drawn from
Q
, collectively we have a wisdom-of-the-crowds effect: the empirical distribution of reports will closely resemble
Q
.

Since many forecasting competitions are one-shot with little context to predict the behavior of other players, symmetric mixed equilibria are reasonable in modeling participant behavior.
In light of our results, we expect in large
n
regimes that forecasters will sample an outcome from the underlying event distribution, and submit a fully extreme predictions that assign probability 1 to that outcome.
Interestingly, the winner of the competition is not someone who submits a well-calibrated forecast, but instead gets lucky and beats out others by sampling the correct outcome.
From an outsider’s point of view, then, there is little insight to gain about the underlying event probabilities from the winner’s (or any individual’s) reports.
Instead, one can only empirically reconstruct the underlying distribution by observing enough reports from different forecasters that they can be treated as samples from
Q
.
Conversely, our results imply that extreme forecasts are not necessarily the work of lazy or uninformed participants making wild guesses; rather, they may reflect sophisticated strategies designed to maximize win probability by sampling from a highly accurate distribution.

5.2
Finite-location Hotelling games

Now consider the finite-location Hotelling game, with retailers choosing locations in
\{1,2,\ldots,k\}\subset\mathcal{S}
and the set of consumers
\mathcal{Y}
following a distribution
Q
over
\mathcal{S}
.
Here the pseudo-target set
\mathcal{X}^{*}
corresponds to the set of locations that are ranked first by at least one consumer (i.e.,
x^{*}(y)=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:\pi_{y}(x)=1
), and
P
is the projection of
Q
onto
\mathcal{X}^{*}
.
Our results translate as follows:

Corollary 2
.

(Finite-location Hotelling)
If
n>\frac{1}{p_{0}}
, then every pure equilibrium is extreme (for each retailer
i
, there exists some consumer
y\in\mathcal{Y}
such that
\pi_{y}(\bm{x}_{i})=1
) and covers the set of first-ranked locations
\mathcal{X}^{*}
(for each
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, there exists a retailer
i
such that
\pi_{y}(\bm{x}_{i})=1
).
If
n\geq\frac{2}{p_{0}}
, then there exists an extreme, pure Nash equilibrium such that for all
x\in\mathcal{X}^{*}
,
k_{\bm{x}}(x)\geq 2
, and if
n>\frac{2}{p_{0}},
then this condition holds for all pure Nash equilibria.
Moreover, for
n>c\geq\frac{2}{p_{0}}
,

D_{\text{KL}}(\hat{P}_{\bm{x}}\;\|\;P)\leq\log\left(\frac{\lfloor cn\rfloor+1}{\lfloor cn\rfloor}\right),

where
\hat{P}_{\bm{x}}
is the empirical distribution of retailer locations induced by an equilibrium strategy profile
\bm{x}
.

Symmetric mixed Nash equilibria exist for any
n>1
.
If
n>\max\left\{43,8\left(\frac{4}{p_{0}}\log\frac{1}{p_{0}}\right)\right\}
, any symmetric mixed Nash equilibrium is extreme, and for each first-ranked position
x\in\mathcal{X}^{*}
, we have
|\sigma(x)-P(x)|\leq\frac{1}{n}.

That is, when the number of retailers is large relative to the underlying distribution over consumer preferences, they will concentrate on the most-preferred set of locations.
With sufficient competition, then, retailers hyper-specialize over the consumers’ preferences.
Our conclusions mirror the results shown in
9
and
10
for the finite-location Hotelling game, though the convergence rates are novel.

5.3
Classic Hotelling games

In the classic Hotelling game, retailers choose locations in
\mathcal{X}:=[0,1]
and consumers follow a distribution
Q
with finite support over
[0,1]
.
Then
\mathcal{X}^{*}=\supp(Q)
corresponds to the set of consumer locations.
Our result then implies the following.

Corollary 3
.

(Classic Hotelling)
If
n>\frac{1}{p_{0}}
, then every pure equilibrium is extreme (for each retailer
i
,
\bm{x}_{i}\in\supp(Q)
) and covers the consumers (for each
y\in\supp(Q)
, there exists a retailer
i
such that
\bm{x}_{i}=y
).
If
n\geq\frac{2}{p_{0}}
, then there exists an extreme, pure Nash equilibrium such that for all
y\in\supp(Q)
,
k_{\bm{x}}(y)\geq 2
, and if
n>\frac{2}{p_{0}},
this condition holds for all pure Nash equilibria.
Moreover, for
n>c\geq\frac{2}{p_{0}}
,

D_{\text{KL}}(\hat{P}_{\bm{x}}\;\|\;P)\leq\log\left(\frac{\lfloor cn\rfloor+1}{\lfloor cn\rfloor}\right),

where
\hat{P}_{\bm{x}}
is the empirical distribution of retailer locations induced by an equilibrium strategy profile
\bm{x}
.

Symmetric mixed Nash equilibria exist for any
n>1
.
If
n>\max\left\{43,8\left(\frac{4}{p_{0}}\log\frac{1}{p_{0}}\right)\right\}
, any symmetric mixed Nash equilibrium
\sigma
covers the consumers, and for each consumer location
y\in\supp(Q)
, we have
|\sigma(y)-P(y)|\leq\frac{1}{n}.

Our theorem, when applied to the classic Hotelling game, provides a discrete analogue to the results of
11
. Whereas they prove convergence to the consumer distribution under the assumption that its CDF is twice differentiable, we prove convergence in the case where the distribution is discrete.

6
Discussion

In this paper, we present the position-optimization game: players select positions in a common space, with the goal of capturing a set of targets according to some proximity function.
We fully characterize pure and symmetric mixed equilibria for a large number of players.
Furthermore, we show that equilibria converge in
n
to the projection of the target distribution onto the position space, and characterize convergence rates.
These results have broad implications for games encompassed by our model.
In particular, we are the first to provide convergence rates; the first to fully characterize equilibria in traditional forecasting competitions; and the first to derive large-
n
results for the classic Hotelling game with a discrete consumer distribution.

Convergence in all settings.

Our results are restricted to settings satisfying Condition
1
to prevent ties and keep
\mathcal{X}^{*}
finite for analytical tractability.
Interestingly,
11
studied the classic Hotelling game without these assumptions, yet they arrived at the same conclusion, namely that the equilibria converge to the distribution induced by
Q
.
Given the lack of any work that shows a deviation from this pattern, we conjecture that this convergence holds for all position-optimization games in much greater generality, even without Condition
1
.
E.g., for the classic Hotelling game, this would imply that retailer locations converge in equilibria to the underlying consumer distribution
for any class of consumer CDFs
.

Small
n
regimes.

We think of this paper as characterizing equilibria for
large
values of
n
, where the number of players overwhelms any underlying structure of the game.
A natural next step is to analyze equilibrium behavior for smaller values of
n
.
For
medium
values of
n
, we conjecture that the structure of the game becomes much more important.
For example, in forecasting competitions, the geometry of the metric space
[0,1]^{m}
coupled with the
\ell_{2}
distance means that forecasters may be able to capture more win probability by playing in the interior of the hypercube.
Can we characterize at what point over
n
forecasters depart from the vertices, and whether they would remain close to them or move quickly toward less extreme points?

1.

Spatial voting.

The
n
strategic agents are candidates.

\mathcal{Y}
is a finite set of
m
voters.

\mathcal{X}
is a set of potential candidates’ ideological positions; for example,
\mathcal{X}:=[0,1]^{2}
could represent an ideological spectrum over two different issues.
Each candidate chooses a position
x\in\mathcal{X}
.
Then
d:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
corresponds to a function that maps each position and voter to some cost.
We assume there is a single most-preferred position
x
for each voter
y
such that
d(x,y)=0
, with
d(x,y)<d(x^{\prime},y)
for all
x^{\prime}\neq x
.
The pseudo-target for voter
y
is the position
x
such that

, so that
\mathcal{X}^{*}=\bigcup_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\arg\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}d(x,y)
.

\mathcal{X}^{*}
is finite because
\mathcal{Y}
is finite, so Condition
1
is satisfied.

The winning candidate is selected according to plurality rule: the candidate who is ranked first relative to
d
for the most voters wins (with randomized tie-breaking).

Q(y):=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Y}|}
is thus the amount of influence of voter
y
from each candidate’s perspective.
Then

P(x)=\frac{\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathbbm{1}_{x=\arg\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}d(x,y)}}{|\mathcal{Y}|},

i.e.
P(x)
is the percentage of voters who most prefer position
x
.
It follows that the utility of each candidate
i
is

u_{i}(x_{i},\bm{x}_{-i})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Y}|k_{\bm{x}}(x_{i})}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathbbm{1}_{d(x_{i},y)\leq d(x_{j},y)\;\forall j\neq i}.

Discrete Voronoi games.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an undirected, vertex-weighted graph.
There are
n
strategic influencers who want to attract users on the vertices, with position set
\mathcal{X}:=V
.
Targets correspond to users, with
\mathcal{Y}:=V
, and the proximity function is
d(x,y)
for
d
the shortest path between
x
and
y
.
The distribution over targets
Q
is the weight of vertices.

x^{*}(y)=y
, i.e. the pseudo-target of a vertex is itself, so
P(x)=Q(x)
.
Condition
1
is satisfied since
|x^{*}(y)|=1
for all
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, and
\mathcal{X}^{*}=\mathcal{Y}
.
Then, the utility of
i
is

u_{i}(x,\bm{x}_{-i})=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}Q(y)\left[\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{d(x,y)\leq d(x^{\prime},y)\;\forall x\neq x^{\prime}}}{|\arg\min_{j\neq i}d(x_{j},y)|}\right].

(Note our model easily extends to settings where edges are weighted;
G
is a hypergraph; or
\mathcal{X}
is a richer space, with

.)

Lemma 7

We compute the utility conditioned on the events

1.

Appendix D
Additional application results omitted from §
5

Here, we apply our main results to additional settings captured by our model.

D.1
Spatial voting

In the spatial voting setting, the pseudo-target set
\mathcal{X}^{*}
is the set of optimal ideologies for the voters in
\mathcal{Y}
(that is,
d(x^{*}(y),y)=0
).
We can thus refer to each
x\in\mathcal{X}^{*}
as a voter’s “ideal position.”
It follows a strategy profile is extreme if candidates only choose ideological positions that correspond to some voter’s ideal position.

Corollary 4
.

(Spatial voting)
Consider the plurality rule with randomized tie-breaking.
If
n\geq\frac{2}{p_{0}}
, a pure Nash equilibrium exists that is extreme; moreover, the equilibrium covers the set of voters’ ideal positions, i.e. for each voter
y
, at least one candidate chooses position
\arg\min_{x^{\prime}}d(x^{\prime},y)
.
If
n>\frac{1}{p_{0}}
, then all pure equilibria are extreme and cover all voters’ ideal positions.
Moreover, for
n>c\geq 2^{m}
, the KL-divergence between the empirical distribution of candidate positions and the true distribution of voters’ ideal positions is upper bounded by
\log\left(\frac{\lfloor cn\rfloor+1}{\lfloor cn\rfloor}\right).

If
n>\max\left\{43,8\left(\frac{4}{p_{0}}\log\frac{1}{p_{0}}\right)\right\}
, any symmetric mixed Nash equilibrium
\sigma
is extreme, and for each ideal position
x\in\mathcal{X}^{*}
,
|\sigma(x)-P(x)|\leq\frac{1}{n}.

In other words, as the number of candidates grows much larger than the number of voters, those candidates’ projected ideological positions will mimic the underlying distribution of voters’ ideal positions.
Instead of ideological convergence to the median voter, this result implies that candidates will commit to matching individual voters’ ideologies to capture their votes.
However, it is important to note that in most traditional political elections the number of voters is in fact much larger than the number of candidates; our results do not hold in these settings.

D.2
Discrete Voronoi games

In a discrete Voronoi game, each pseudo-target
x\in\mathcal{X}^{*}
corresponds exactly to a user
v\in V
.
A strategy profile is extreme if influencers only place themselves on vertices.

Corollary 5
.

(Discrete Voronoi games)
If
n\geq\frac{2}{p_{0}}
, a pure Nash equilibrium exists that is extreme; moreover, the equilibrium covers the set of users, i.e. for each user
v\in V
, at least one influencer chooses position
v
.
If
n>\frac{1}{p_{0}}
, then all pure equilibria are extreme and cover all users.
Moreover, for
n>c\geq 2^{m}
, the KL-divergence between the empirical distribution of influencers and the true distribution of users over the vertices is upper bounded by
\log\left(\frac{\lfloor cn\rfloor+1}{\lfloor cn\rfloor}\right).

If
n>\max\left\{43,8\left(\frac{4}{p_{0}}\log\frac{1}{p_{0}}\right)\right\},
any symmetric mixed Nash equilibrium
\sigma
is extreme, and for each user
v\in V
,
|\sigma(x)-P(x)|\leq\frac{1}{n}.

That is, when the number of influencers in a network grows much larger than the number of users, they will strategically spread out in the network according to the underlying distribution of users in an attempt to maximize their corresponding Voronoi cell.
