Exploration and practice of home care mode for elderly with stroke disability based on ICF theory
Authors: Chen Zhi, Zhou Chunna, Chen Yuanyuan, Zheng Chunxi, Han Saiyue, He Ningning, Hong Bo, Cao Minfei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Most existing studies on family care for older adults with stroke-related disability focus mainly on caregiving skills training, with limited integration of patients’ functional status and rehabilitation needs into health promotion services. A total of 104 mildly to moderately disabled older adults with stroke and their primary caregivers were recruited from one medical and health care group between September 2023 and October 2024. Participants were randomly assigned to a control group (n = 52 families) or an intervention group (n = 52 families) using a computer-generated random sequence. The control group received routine inpatient care and discharge follow-up. The intervention group received an ICF theory–based home care program. Outcomes were assessed 2 weeks before discharge (discharge preparation) and 4 weeks after discharge (at home). The primary outcome was caregiver burden, and secondary outcomes were patients’ quality of life, caregiving competence, and family functioning. After the intervention, the caregiver burden score was 74.31 ± 7.00 in the control group and 63.54 ± 10.41 in the intervention group (p < 0.001). The caregiving competence score was 50.84 ± 5.85 in the control group and 61.02 ± 7.73 in the intervention group (p < 0.001). The patients’ quality of life score was 68.21 ± 9.38 in the control group and 78.54 ± 8.77 in the intervention group (p < 0.001). The family functioning (Family APGAR score) was 12.88 ± 2.49 in the control group and 14.06 ± 2.62 in the intervention group (p = 0.021). Significant group × time interaction effects were observed across all outcomes. An ICF-based home care program for mildly to moderately disabled older adults after stroke can effectively reduce caregiver burden, improve caregiving competence, and enhance patients’ quality of life and family functioning.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57414-4.

Introduction

Stroke is the second leading cause of death worldwide after ischaemic heart disease. The estimated global cost of stroke is over US$721 billion (0.66% of the global GDP)1. Internationally, stroke is also one of the three main causes of disability in people aged 65 years and older2. Among surviving stroke patients, approximately 50% have some degree of disability, and more than one-third require assistance with activities of daily living3.

As the number of disabled individuals continues to increase, the demand for long-term care also grows. The International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) conceptualizes disability as limitations in body functions and structures, activity, and participation, influenced by environmental and personal factors. After stroke, older adults may experience dysfunction in speech, swallowing, and limb movement4, resulting in reduced independence and restricted social participation.

Although various care programs for disabled older adults have been developed, many interventions focus primarily on caregiving skills training and equipment use and fail to integrate patients’ functional status and rehabilitation needs into a structured home-based rehabilitation plan5,6. Stroke-related disability differs from general disability because caregivers often require disease-specific knowledge and rehabilitation guidance, and the cost of learning appropriate caregiving strategies may be higher. Therefore, there remains a need for an ICF-guided home care program that links functional assessment results with individualized rehabilitation exercises, activity participation goals, and feasible environmental modifications.

Guided by ICF theory, this study aimed to construct and implement a home care program for older adults with stroke-related disability. Based on rehabilitation assessment, the program was designed at four levels: body functions and structures, rehabilitation exercises, activity and participation, and environmental modification. Furthermore, this program was tested in families of older adults with stroke to provide a theoretical basis and practical guidance for optimizing home care for this population.

Methods

Study design

From September 2023 to October 2024, a total of 104 families of older adults with stroke-related disability participated in this study at our hospital. Eligible patients and their primary caregivers were randomly allocated to an intervention group (n = 52 families) or a control group (n = 52 families). This study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of Xiangshan First People’s Hospital Medical and Health Group (2023-Z-12). The trial was registered at the National Medical Research Registration and Filing Information System (MR-33-23-028202). The trial registration record is publicly available in the Chinese Clinical Trial (ChiCTR2600125905,2026-6-1). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Participants

Participants were recruited from the inpatient neurology and rehabilitation units of a tertiary general hospital in China.

The inclusion criteria for patients were as follows: (1) meeting the World Health Organization diagnostic criteria for stroke and confirmed by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography; (2) age ≥ 60 years; and (3) assessed as having mild to moderate disability according to the National Standard for the Standardized Assessment of the Abilities of the Elderly.

The exclusion criteria for patients included: (1) history of Alzheimer’s disease or major mental illness; (2) incomplete demographic data; or (3) significant cognitive impairment. Cognitive function was screened using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE).

The inclusion criteria for caregivers were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) a relative or primary support person of the stroke-disabled older adult; (3) one of at least two caregivers; and (4) the individual providing care for the longest time. The exclusion criteria included poor communication ability or learning disability.

Experienced clinical nurses screened patients and referred eligible participants to the research staff. Trained researchers then provided detailed study information. Written informed consent was obtained prior to enrollment. After baseline assessment and consent, participants were randomized (1:1 ratio) to the intervention group or the control group. Because of the nature of the intervention, participants and care providers could not be blinded; however, outcome assessors were blinded to group allocation.

Randomization and blinding

A randomization list was generated using a computer-generated random sequence by researchers not involved in recruitment or assessment. Following baseline assessment, participants were randomly assigned to either the intervention group or the control group. The allocation sequence was stored in sealed, opaque envelopes, which were opened only after enrollment. Outcome assessors and data analysts were blinded to group allocation. The random sequence was generated by an independent researcher. Allocation was concealed using sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes, which were opened by a coordinator after baseline assessment. A CONSORT participant flow diagram is provided in Fig. 1.

Intervention description

The control group received conventional inpatient care, discharge preparation services, and scheduled follow-up visits after discharge. During hospitalization, rehabilitation therapists, physicians, and nursing staff provided education regarding disease management and rehabilitation. Prior to discharge, caregivers received key care instructions based on the patient’s condition. Telephone follow-up was conducted within 7 days after discharge, and a second follow-up/home visit was conducted 4 weeks after discharge. To ensure standardized delivery, all intervention providers received unified training prior to implementation and followed a structured home-care manual and checklist. Adherence to the home program was monitored during each follow-up contact by reviewing the caregiver checklist and confirming completion of prescribed rehabilitation activities and environmental modifications. Any deviations were recorded and addressed through additional guidance.

The intervention group received an ICF theory–based home care program for older adults with stroke-related disability. Patients underwent rehabilitation evaluation prior to discharge, and caregivers received structured training. Establishment of a home care implementation team

The team consisted of a rehabilitation nurse, neurology nurse, neurologist, geriatric nurse, and rehabilitation therapist. The neurologist was responsible for diagnosis and eligibility screening. Rehabilitation nurses and therapists conducted rehabilitation assessment and caregiver training. Graduate students supported program development and data collection/analysis. All team members received standardized training before implementation to ensure consistent delivery of the intervention.

Construction of the home care plan

We reviewed books and guidelines on care for older adults with stroke-related disability from 2013 to 2023 and searched relevant literature in major Chinese and English databases. Search terms included ((Foster Home Care[MeSH Terms]) OR (Home Nursing[MeSH Terms]) OR (Adult Foster Care[title/abstract]) OR (Kinship Care[title/abstract]) OR (Nonprofessional Home Care[title/abstract])) AND ((Stroke[MeSH Terms]) OR (Cerebrovascular Disorders[title/abstract]) OR (Intracranial Embolism and Thrombosis[title/abstract]) OR (Disabilities[title/abstract])). Implementation of the home care plan

Before discharge, the care team evaluated patients in the rehabilitation therapy room and provided individualized home care guidance. First, rehabilitation physicians and therapists performed functional assessment. Second, specialist nurses delivered daily-life guidance based on functional assessment results. Nurses and therapists provided individualized education according to functional level and activities of daily living (ADL). For example, in eating-related training, caregivers were instructed on safe feeding methods based on swallowing function and adaptive tools based on hand function (e.g., spill-proof spoon, shallow cup). Health education was delivered for ≥ 30 min, three times per week during hospitalization. Caregivers received a caregiver handbook developed by the research team. The researcher completed the handbook with patient information, functional ratings, and checklist items to guide home care tasks after discharge. Intervention delivery was standardized using a caregiver handbook and a home-visit checklist. Adherence to home activities was monitored through caregiver self-report in the handbook and confirmed during each follow-up/home visit.

Within 1 week after discharge, the team conducted a home visit to assess the home environment and proposed feasible environmental modifications according to the patient’s functional level (e.g., installing handrails, toilet seat cushions, training on assistive devices). A second home visit at 4 weeks after discharge assessed modification effectiveness and adjusted the rehabilitation plan. Subsequently, biweekly follow-up visits were scheduled at the rehabilitation outpatient clinic. If clinic visits were inconvenient, patients could access internet-based nursing services for home assessment and further guidance. Adherence was monitored using caregiver checklists and follow-up verification, and high compliance was observed across participants.

Outcome measures

This study assessed outcomes at two time points: baseline (2 weeks before discharge) and post-intervention (4 weeks after discharge). The Zarit Caregiver Burden Interview (ZBI) was predefined as the primary outcome to support the sample size calculation. The primary outcome was caregiver burden (ZBI). Secondary outcomes were patients’ quality of life (WHOQOL–BREF), caregiving competence (FCTI), and family functioning (Family APGAR). Caregiver burden (ZBI) was selected as the primary outcome because it represents the most direct and clinically relevant indicator of caregiving stress in families managing stroke-related disability. All questionnaires were completed in person during hospitalization and during home follow-up.

Caregiver burden: The Zarit Caregiver Burden Interview (ZBI)12 was used to examine caregiver burden before and after the intervention. The scale contains 22 items and includes two dimensions plus four individual items. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale. Higher scores indicate greater caregiver burden. The Chinese version has demonstrated good reliability and validity (Cronbach’s α = 0.863).

Caregiver competence: The Family Caregiver Task Inventory (FCTI)13 was used to assess caregiving competence. The scale covers five domains: adapting to the caregiving role, providing assistance with personal care, dealing with emotions, evaluating family and community resources, and adjusting personal life. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale. Total scores range from 0 to 50, with higher scores indicating better caregiving competence. The scale showed good reliability in this study (Cronbach’s α = 0.930).

Quality of life: Patients’ quality of life was assessed via the World Health Organization Quality of Life Scale (WHOQOL–BREF)14. The scale includes 26 items covering four domains. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, and the total score was calculated according to the scoring manual. Higher scores indicate better quality of life. The scale has demonstrated good reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.863).

Family functioning: Family functioning was assessed using the Family APGAR index15. The scale includes five items (adaptation, partnership, growth, affection, and resolve). Each item is scored on a 1–5 scale, yielding a total score ranging from 5 to 25. Higher scores indicate better family functioning. In this study, the internal consistency coefficient of the scale was 0.89.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (Version 22.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA; https://www.ibm.com/spss). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation. To evaluate the effectiveness of the intervention over time, repeated-measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA) was conducted with group (intervention vs. control) as the between-subject factor and time (baseline and post-intervention) as the within-subject factor. The group × time interaction effect was considered the primary indicator of intervention effectiveness.

When significant interaction effects were identified, post hoc comparisons were conducted. Effect sizes were reported as partial η2, and 95% confidence intervals were calculated where appropriate.

Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed according to the intention-to-treat principle. There were no missing outcome data; therefore, no imputation was required.

Comparison of family care index, quality of life, caregiving burden, and caregiving capacity

No significant between-group differences were observed at baseline (all p > 0.05), indicating good comparability between groups.

After the intervention, significant group × time interaction effects were observed for all outcome variables, including Family Care Index (F = 5.495, p = 0.021), Quality of Life (F = 33.655, p < 0.001), Caregiving Burden (F = 39.584, p < 0.001), and Caregiving Capacity (F = 58.706, p < 0.001). These findings indicate that the intervention produced significantly greater improvements over time compared with the control group.

These results indicate that the intervention had a significant and comprehensive positive effect on improving family care, enhancing caregivers’quality of life and caregiving capacity, and reducing caregiving burden (Table 3).

The effect size analysis showed that before the intervention, the effect sizes for Family Care Index, Quality of Life, Caregiving Burden, and Caregiving Capacity were small (Partial η2=0.006–0.027; Cohen’s f = 0.076–0.165), indicating negligible to weak group differences at baseline.

These findings suggest that the intervention exerted not only statistically significant but also practically meaningful effects, particularly in improving quality of life and caregiving capacity and reducing caregiving burden. Discussion

This study evaluated the effectiveness of an ICF-based home care program for older adults with stroke-related disability. The findings demonstrated significant improvements in caregiver burden, caregiving competence, quality of life, and family functioning, with moderate to large effect sizes observed across most outcomes.

Alleviating the burden of caregivers

Our findings showed that the implementation of this ICF-based home care program effectively reduced caregiver burden, which is consistent with previous studies16,17.

Owing to the particularities of stroke-related disability, long-term caregiving is more demanding and time-consuming than other types of care, and it has a substantial impact on caregivers’ daily life and well-being. In addition to physical demands, caregiving is often accompanied by psychological stress, uncertainty about recovery, and fear of complications, which may increase caregiver burden18–20.

Our care plan integrates caregiving skills training with structured rehabilitation guidance. By linking functional assessment results to home-based rehabilitation goals, caregivers can better understand patients’ progress and expected recovery trajectory, which may enhance confidence and reduce psychological stress. Previous studies have shown that many caregiver programs focus mainly on caregiving skills training or provide general rehabilitation advice21. In contrast, our program provides individualized and function-oriented guidance at discharge and during home follow-up, enabling caregivers to deliver more targeted care and evidence-based home rehabilitation.

Improving the care ability of caregivers

The ICF-based home care program improved the caregiving competence of family caregivers, which is consistent with the results of Wei Wenjing22,23. Our program was developed based on each patient’s functional status, making the intervention content more intuitive and easier for caregivers to follow.

In particular, caregivers could use functional evaluation results to anticipate which activities of daily living (ADL) patients could complete independently or with assistance. This may reduce trial-and-error during caregiving and improve training efficiency. Moreover, observing functional improvement during the intervention may further increase caregiving confidence and reinforce skill acquisition, ultimately improving caregiving competence.

Enhancing the quality of life of stroke-disabled older adults

The implementation of our care program improved patients’ quality of life. Quality of life reflects not only physical function but also psychological well-being, social participation, and overall satisfaction with daily life24. Pan Liping25 also reported that home care could improve older adults’ quality of life.

In this study, individualized home care plans were developed based on functional assessment, allowing caregivers to provide structured rehabilitation training and daily-life guidance according to the care manual. This approach may promote functional recovery and independence, thereby improving quality of life. Additionally, environmental modification may reduce barriers to mobility and self-care, which is an important contributor to quality of life among home-bound older adults17.

However, although the ICF framework emphasizes psychological function and social participation, the present intervention mainly focused on physical function, activities of daily living, and environmental modification, with limited content targeting mental health or social reintegration. This may partly explain why our intervention primarily improved functional and caregiving-related outcomes rather than broader psychosocial outcomes. Future interventions should incorporate structured psychological support and social participation strategies.

Furthermore, digital health services may provide additional resources and continuous support for patients at home, which could further enhance quality of life. Therefore, the findings of this study should be interpreted with caution, as the observed benefits may primarily reflect improvements in physical and functional domains rather than the full spectrum of ICF components.

Enhancing family functioning and family support

Studies have shown that better family functioning is associated with stronger long-term understanding and support, which can reduce patients’ psychological burden and facilitate positive adjustment after stroke. In the present study, patients who completed the ICF-based home care program reported higher levels of family support.

This improvement may be explained by two mechanisms. First, enhanced caregiving competence enables caregivers to provide more targeted and effective support, leading to improved family interactions. Second, the program aims to increase patients’ independence and participation in daily family activities, which may improve patients’ sense of value and belonging and strengthen family cohesion.

Notably, the intervention team did not include several professionals commonly involved in comprehensive stroke rehabilitation, such as physical therapists, occupational therapists, speech and language therapists, and psychologists. This limitation may have restricted the breadth of intervention components, particularly for swallowing/speech rehabilitation and psychosocial well-being. Future home care models should incorporate multidisciplinary collaboration to better address the full range of ICF domains. This ICF-based home care program is feasible for implementation in community and hospital–home transition settings. The model may serve as a practical framework for developing structured discharge-to-home rehabilitation support for older adults with mild to moderate stroke-related disability.

Conclusion

This study developed and implemented an ICF-based home care program for mildly to moderately disabled older adults after stroke and evaluated its effects in a randomized controlled trial. The findings demonstrated that, compared with routine care and standard discharge follow-up, the ICF-guided home care intervention significantly reduced caregiver burden and improved caregiving competence. In addition, older adults in the intervention group showed better quality of life and improved family functioning after discharge.

These results indicate that an ICF-based framework can be feasibly applied to guide home-based rehabilitation and caregiving support for stroke-disabled older adults. By linking functional assessment results with structured rehabilitation training, activity participation goals, and feasible environmental modification, this program provides caregivers with more targeted and actionable guidance, thereby improving caregiving outcomes and patient well-being. Overall, this study provides evidence that a standardized, function-oriented home care model may serve as an effective and practical approach to strengthen transitional care and community-based rehabilitation for older adults with stroke-related disability.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be considered. First, the care program was developed through expert consultation, and most experts were recruited from economically developed coastal regions, which may introduce geographical bias and limit generalizability. Second, the caregiver manual mainly uses text and images, which may be less applicable to caregivers in rural areas or those with lower health literacy. Future work should incorporate videos, animations, or digital modules to improve accessibility and usability.

In addition, although the intervention was guided by the ICF framework, it focused primarily on physical function and daily activities, while psychological support and social participation strategies were not included. Future programs should integrate interventions targeting mental health and social reintegration to better address the full range of ICF domains. Finally, this was a short-term intervention, and further studies with longer follow-up and broader multidisciplinary collaboration are needed. The relatively short follow-up period may limit the assessment of long-term sustainability of the intervention effects. Full implementation of this model also requires coordinated health system support integrating medical institutions, social welfare resources, and long-term care services.
