Unifying nomenclature describing the individuals with Fontan circulation
Authors: Ashish H. Shah, David Renaud, Sivasubramanian Ramakrishnan, Miriam Michel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLanguage shapes clinical care, research interpretation, and patient identity, particularly in chronic and heterogeneous conditions. Individuals living with a Fontan circulation represent a growing and diverse population unified by a surgically created physiology rather than a single anatomical diagnosis. Despite this, terminology used in clinical practice and research remains inconsistent, variably defining individuals by surgical procedure, anatomy, or loosely applied disease labels. Such variability complicates communication, limits comparability across studies, and risks reducing person-centered care. In this review, we examine the historical evolution and current use of terminology describing individuals with a Fontan circulation, highlight the clinical and research implications of inconsistent language, and delineate distinctions between anatomical substrates and shared Fontan physiology. We propose adoption of standardized, person-first nomenclature, specifically “individuals (or patients) with a Fontan circulation” with precise anatomical descriptors used where relevant. Unified terminology may enhance clarity, data harmonization, and patient-centered outcomes across the Fontan community.

INTRODUCTION

Language shapes care. Over the past decades, the shift from a disease-centered to a patient-centered care has been increasingly discussed, also in the field of cardiovascular disease. Only recently, a scientific statement on this topic was published by the American Heart Association.[1] Patient-centered care recognizes that the terminology used to describe individuals living with a condition should reflect respect, personhood, and shared decision-making, rather than reducing a person to their disease.[2] The importance of patient-first language and terminology becomes, especially critical in the context of chronic and lifelong conditions, including congenital disease, as language affects identity, self-perception, stigma, and long-term engagement in care. Especially in rare and heterogeneous conditions, consistent and person-first terminology is essential to ensure clear communication and reduce misinterpretation across subgroups. One example is the group of children and adults born with a complex congenital heart disease, such as a single ventricle heart disease, palliated by a Fontan circulation. This group of individuals is very heterogeneous, rendering uniform terminology particularly important since even subgroups/comorbidities among these patients are frequently poorly defined or inconsistently labeled (e.g., Fontan-associated liver disease.[3]

As a first step toward a uniform and defined language in this particular group of individuals, we review prevailing labels of this special patient group, highlight their clinical and research consequences, and propose a patient-first, standardized nomenclature to improve communication, facilitate the collection of data with guaranteed comparability, and ultimately advance outcomes across the Fontan community.

THE FONTAN PROCEDURE AND SINGLE-VENTRICLE POPULATION

The Fontan procedure is a palliative surgical strategy developed for complex congenital cardiac defects with only one functional ventricle present.[4] Since its first use in 1968, this staged surgery has transformed survival for children born with single-ventricle heart disease, allowing the majority to reach adulthood.[5] Today, an estimated 70,000 individuals worldwide live with a Fontan circulation – a unique physiology where systemic venous blood is routed passively to the lungs in the absence of a subpulmonary ventricle. These persons born with primarily a congenital cardiac defect eventually experience progressive multisystem issues (e.g. liver fibrosis, skeletal muscle wasting, protein-losing enteropathy, limited exercise capacity, and blunted exercise-augmented hemodynamics), thought mostly to be secondary to the Fontan circulation’s elevated venous pressure and low cardiac output.[6]

Fontan circulation represents a surgically created physiological state rather than a uniform anatomical diagnosis. It is applied across a heterogeneous spectrum of congenital heart substrates, including congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries, double-outlet right ventricle, and atrioventricular valve straddling, many of which do not constitute “single-ventricle congenital heart disease” at baseline. Accordingly, the term single-ventricle disease should not be used synonymously with Fontan circulation. Fontan physiology, instead, reflects a convergence at the level of circulatory configuration, characterized by passive pulmonary blood flow and the absence of a subpulmonary pump, irrespective of the underlying cardiac anatomy. Explicitly distinguishing anatomical substrates from the shared Fontan circulatory state is essential for conceptual clarity, mechanistic interpretation, and the development of physiology-targeted research and therapies.

Since its inception, terminology for this growing population has varied widely. Early literature often referred to them simply as “patients surviving the Fontan operation”, reflecting the experimental nature of the procedure. Many articles focused on the procedure rather than the patient. Then came the “Fontan patients,” defining individuals by their surgical palliation.[7] Even recent studies and editorials freely use this label, for example, discussing cohorts of “adult Fontan patients” when reporting clinical outcomes.[78] Others use person-first descriptors such as “patients with a Fontan circulation”[9] or “individuals with a Fontan circulation.”[10] In parallel, the underlying condition is described as “single ventricle” congenital heart disease, emphasizing that these individuals were born with one effective ventricle only. Actually, the nomenclature surrounding the “functionally univentricular heart” has been debated for decades.[1112] In fact, Jacobs and Anderson were the first to propose a formal nomenclature for the “functionally univentricular heart,”[13] with the subsequent work of Frescura and Thiene providing a detailed anatomical exposition.[12] Different centers and authors have historically lumped or split various single-ventricle malformations into inconsistent categories.[1114] Table 1 summarizes the terminology used in the published literature to name individuals with a Fontan circulation.

This lack of unified language extends not only to how we describe individuals with a Fontan circulation but also to the terminology and precise definitions of their complications. A recent publication has described “consensus for unified medical language in Fontan care;”[14] this description focused primarily on Fontan-associated conditions. For conditions directly or indirectly associated with the Fontan circulation, avoid broad, potentially alarming labels, such as “neurodevelopmental issues.” Instead, specify the actual findings, for example, “mild delay in spatial cognition” or “executive-function differences.” This precision serves multiple purposes: it provides families with accurate, actionable information; it reduces unnecessary anxiety about vague, catastrophic-sounding, fear-mongering diagnoses; and it frames these findings as specific medical observations rather than stigmatizing labels.

TOWARDS A UNIFIED NOMENCLATURE

In light of these inconsistencies, experts are calling for standardized, person-centric terminology. A 2019 American Heart Association statement describes individuals with a Fontan circulation as “children and adults with Fontan circulation” rather than “Fontan patients.”[6] Similarly, a 2020 international position paper refers to “people with a Fontan circulation,”[6] signaling a shift toward patient-first language.

Beyond sensitivity toward patients and their families, consistency in nomenclature has practical benefits. The Fontan Outcomes Network and other groups emphasize that unifying a common language is a critical step to improve research collaboration, clinical care, data sharing, and compare outcomes across studies.[6] Going forward, experts advocate describing these patients by their underlying condition, for example, “patients with single-ventricle heart disease or with Fontan circulation.” This phrasing acknowledges that the Fontan is a man-made circulation and not a disease itself and places the focus on the person and their congenital diagnosis. Importantly, it also provides a unifying umbrella term for a heterogeneous group. While no absolute consensus has emerged yet, there is growing agreement that adopting consistent, respectful terminology for those individuals as well as for their associated conditions and complications will benefit collaboration among cardiologists, congenital heart surgeons, and researchers. By speaking the same language, the community may be able to compare studies better, educate families, and ultimately advance care for those living with a Fontan circulation. Ultimately, a uniform language for this patient group will provide a solid foundation for clearly defined and consistently applied subgroup and comorbidity classifications.

The proposed terminology is intended to provide a conceptual framework that may inform patient-centered language development across different languages and cultural contexts, rather than to prescribe universal linguistic equivalents.

CONCLUSIONS

Individuals palliated to a Fontan circulation constitute a heterogeneous population unified by a shared, surgically created physiology rather than a single anatomical substrate. Inconsistent terminology has contributed to ambiguity in clinical communication, research interpretation, and patient understanding. We therefore advocate for the consistent use of the term “individuals (or patients) with a Fontan circulation,” which is person-first and physiologically accurate, and avoids defining individuals by a surgical procedure alone. Where appropriate, the underlying anatomy should be described precisely using established terms such as “functionally univentricular heart.” Adoption of a unified, accurate nomenclature is essential to improve communication, research comparability, and patient-centered care across the Fontan community.
