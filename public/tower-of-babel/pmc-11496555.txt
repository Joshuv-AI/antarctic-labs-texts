GC–MS analysis, molecular docking, and pharmacokinetic studies on Dalbergia sissoo barks extracts for compounds with anti-diabetic potential
Authors: Deepanshi Vijh, Promila Gupta
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetes is a metabolic condition defined by abnormal blood sugar levels. Targeting starch-hydrolyzing enzymes and Dipeptidyl Peptidase 4 (DPP-4) expressed on the surface of numerous cells is one of the key strategies to lower the risk of Type-2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Dalbergia sissoo Roxb. bark (DSB) extracts have been reported to have anti-diabetic properties. This study intended to scientifically validate use of alcoholic and hydro-alcoholic extracts of DSB for T2DM by conducting preliminary phytochemical investigations, characterising potential phytochemicals using Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy and Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis followed by comprehensive in-silico analysis. A qualitative phytochemical evaluation indicated the presence of alkaloids, phenolics, glycosides, conjugated acids and flavonoids. Ethanolic extracts showed highest total phenolic content (TPC) (127.072 ± 14.08031 μg GAE/g dry extract) and total flavonoid content (106.911 ± 5.84516 μg QE /g dry extract). Further FT-IR spectroscopy also revealed typical band values associated with phenol, alcohol, alkene, alkane and conjugated acid functional groups. The GC–MS analysis identified 139 compounds, 18 of which had anti-diabetic potential. In-silico ADMET analysis of potential compounds revealed 15 compounds that followed Lipinski’s rule and demonstrated drug-like properties, as well as good oral bioavailability. Molecular docking was utilised to analyse their potential to interact with three targets: α-amylase, α-glucosidase, and DPP-4, which are crucial in managing diabetes-related problems. Molecular Docking analysis and membrane permeability test utilising the PerMM platform revealed that compounds in the extracts, such as Soyasapogenol B and Corydine, had better interactions and permeability across the plasma membrane than standard drugs in use. Molecular dynamics simulations also showed that selected compounds remained stable upon interaction with α-amylase. Overall, using the in-silico approaches it was predicted that DSB extracts contain potential phytochemicals with diverse anti-diabetic properties. It further needs to be investigated for possible development as formulation or drug of choice for treating T2DM.Keywords: Glucose uptake, Type-2 diabetes mellitus, α-Amylase, α-Glucosidase, DPP-4 inhibitors, Drug discovery and developmentSubject terms: Biotechnology, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery, Plant sciences

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is a group of chronic metabolic diseases characterized by high blood sugar level (hyperglycaemia) over a prolonged period of time1. It is caused by dysregulation of insulin-mediated signalling pathways, resulting in decreased insulin secretion, insulin resistance (IR) or a combination of both. It is proving to be a global epidemic causing highest mortality after cardiovascular diseases and cancer. According to recent reports of International Diabetes Federation (IDF), about 629 million adults in the age group of 20–79 years, have diabetes mellitus. The total number of people living with diabetes is estimated to rise to 783 million by 2045 based on the developing trend2.

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), known as non-insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus, is the main category which accounts for about 90% of diabetes cases around the world. It is characterized by gradual decrease in insulin secretion of β cells or resistance to insulin2. It is caused due to factors like age, dietary habits, sedentary lifestyle, genetic predisposition, hormonal disorders like Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS), environment and behavioural changes3. Individuals suffering from this chronic disease are more susceptible to renal damage, eyes damage, myocardial disease, stroke, bone fracture risks, and mortality due to other function failure4,5.

A number of hypoglycaemic agents are available in the market with different mechanisms to regulate blood sugar levels. Currently, insulin sensitizers and secretagogues are the most often used for diabetes treatments; however, the rise of disaccharide digesting enzyme inhibitors may represent the most potent strategy of postprandial hyperglycaemia management6. Despite the global availability of various anti-hyperglycaemic agents like biguanides, meglitinides, insulin analogs, bromocriptine and oral drugs like metformin, acarbose, repaglinide, sitagliptin; diabetes and its secondary complications continue to be a major concern. Moreover, these drugs have adverse side effects because of which plant-derived drugs are becoming popular in managing T2DM7.

Bioactives from many plants and their parts such as flavonoids, terpenoids, carotenoids, glycosides, and alkaloids, have been reported to show versatile anti-diabetic effects for a long time prior to their isolation and study8. Their anti-diabetic effects are mediated by a decrease in insulin resistance, an increase in insulin secretion, improved hepatic glycogen production, and anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties9. Many native plants are utilised to treat diabetes and to manufacture relevant pharmaceuticals10. Metformin, the most extensively used hypoglycaemic medicine, was found in the medicinal herb Galega officinalis using traditional ways of investigation11.

Dalbergia sissoo Roxb. is an important rosewood species, commercially exploited for its high-quality timber besides its significant role in traditional medicine for antileprotic, anthelmintic, anti-cancer, antinoceceptive, anti-microbial, anti-inflammatory, and anti-diabetic properties of its various extract12–15. Its bark extract was used to treat dyspepsia, anal problems, diarrhoea and skin issues like leucoderma, heartwood extract was utilised as an antipyretic. Its oil was applied topically to heal ulcers and skin ailments15. Flowers were utilised to purify blood, and strengthen the immune system. The wood functioned as a cooling agent and an antileprotic. The plants’ aerial parts were employed as an expectorant and in treatment of spasms. The leaves’ extract has been used as a jaundice remedy, analgesic, antipyretic, antioxidant, antidiabetic, and anticancer agent16.Number of compounds have been isolated from various parts of Dalbergia sissoo Roxb and have been explored for various pharmacological activities13,17.The plant leaves include a variety of chemical components in their ethanolic extracts, such as oligosaccharides, phenols, isoflavone glucoside, caviunin 7-O-[β-d-apiofuranosyl-(1→6)-β-d-glucopyranoside, isoflavanoids, flavanols, neoflavanoids, terpenoids and diterpenoids like lupeol, betulin and phytol18. GC-MS analysis of the seed oil showed the presence of phospholipids, neutral lipids, glycolipids, and fatty acids such as, oleic, stearic palmitic, and linoleic acids. The extracts from root bark contain biochanin A, 7-hydroxy-6-methoxyflavone, rotenoid, and dehydroamorphigenin, along with chalcone (2, 3-dimethoxy-4′,-dimethylallyloxy-2′-hydroxychalcone), isoflavone(7-hydroxy-5- hydroxy-4′-methoxyisoflavone), and other compounds. Two isoflavone glycosides, identified as caviunin 7-O-gentiobioside and isocaviunin 7-O-gentiobioside, have been extracted from Dalbergia sissoo’s mature pods and showed antinoceceptive potential13. The stem bark contains flavonoids, 4-phenylchromene, dalbergenone cinnamylphenols,, dalbergin, and methyl dalbergin, as well as dalbergichromene16. The green branches of the aerial parts of Dalbergia sissoo were used to isolate 13 fatty acids, irisolidone, biochanin-A, muningin, tectirigenin, prunetin, genistein, sissotrin, prunetin-4-O-galactoside, norartocarpetin, β-amyrin, β-sitosterol, and stigmasterol12,19. Aqueous and alcoholic extracts of Dalbergia sissoo bark (DSB) exhibited anti-diabetic properties in streptozotocin-nicotinamide20 and alloxan-induced type 2 diabetic rats21. However, information regarding phytochemicals responsible for hypoglycaemic activity, their mechanism of action and bioaccessibility is lacking. This work attempts to screen and characterize phytochemicals in DSB using FT-IR and GC-MS analysis along with in-silico assessment of their interactions with common anti-diabetic targets i.e. α-amylase, α-glucosidase, and DPP-4 using molecular docking and molecular dynamic studies. However, the level of precision and accuracy of the predictions of anti-diabetic potential of the compounds suggested still depends on in-vitro or in-vivo experimental evidences.

Materials and methods

Plant sample collection and extraction

DSB was collected from the university campus (Guru Gobind Singh Indraprastha University, New Delhi) during the year 2023 and was verified by Dr. Promila Gupta, botanist and Professor, University School of Biotechnology (USBT), Guru Gobind Singh Indraprastha University. A voucher number (USBT-DV-1/22) was deposited to the herbarium collection of ARL-316, USBT. This study complies with the local national regulations for the use of plants in experimental studies. The collected, washed and dried bark (50 g) was ground into a coarse powder with an automatic grinder. The samples were extracted with 100% methanol, 100% ethanol, 50% methanol and 50% ethanol in 1:10 w/v ratio and the mixtures were kept on orbital shaker in a sealed bottle, shaken and stirred continuously for 24 h at 37 °C. Extraction was repeated three times and pooled to maximise the yield for each sample. The sample-solvent combinations were filtered using Whatman qualitative filter paper Grade 1 before the solvent was evaporated in rotavapour at 40 °C for methanolic (BME) and hydro-methanolic (BHME) extracts and at 60 °C for ethanolic (BEE) and hydro-ethanolic extracts (BHEE). The extracts were preserved in a refrigerator (− 4 °C) upto 4–8 weeks till further analysis.

Preliminary phytochemical investigation

The preliminary qualitative analysis of phytochemicals in four extracts was performed using chromophore reagents. For this, 2 mg/mL concentration of extracts dissolved in their respective solvents were used, following the previously reported procedures for confirmation and qualitative identification of : Steroids, Flavonoids, Phenolics, Tannins, Triterpenoids, Glycosides, Saponins and Anthraquinones22–25.

Estimation of total phenolic content

The Folin–Ciocalteu method was slightly modified to determine the Total Phenolic Contents (TPC) of the various bark extracts26. 60 μL of each extract (1 mg/mL) was mixed with 2 mL of the Folin–Ciocalteu reagent (1:10) in water, followed by 1 mL of saturated sodium carbonate solution (1 M) in the reaction mixture. The final reaction mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature and absorbance was measured at 765 nm with a UV–vis spectrophotometer. A calibration curve was used to assess the total phenolics present in the various extracts. Gallic Acid standard curve was produced at varied concentrations (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and, 10 μg/mL). The TPC was reported as μg Gallic Acid Equivalent (GAE)/g dry weight of respective bark extracts.

Estimation of total flavonoids content

The total flavonoid content (TFC) in the various extracts was quantified using the aluminium chloride (AlCl3) colorimetric method with a slight modification27,28. In brief, 75 μL of each extract (1 mg/mL) was mixed with 100 μL of 10% AlCl3 solution, followed by addition of 100 μL 1 M potassium acetate solution after 5 min of incubation at room temperature. Final volume of reaction mixture was raised to 4 mL with distilled water. The final reaction mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min and absorbance was read at 415 nm. To measure the total amount of flavonoids, a calibration curve was created by measuring the absorbance of quercetin as a standard at different concentrations (0, 2.5, 5, 7.5, 10, and 12.5 μg/mL). TFC was reported as μg quercetin equivalent (QE)/g dry weight of respective bark extract.

FT-IR spectroscopic analysis

FT-IR spectroscopy was performed for all four extracts mixed with dried potassium bromide (KBr) salt using mortar and pestle. The mixture of KBr salt with respective extract was compressed into the form of a thin salt pellet. The pellet was loaded onto an FT-IR Affinity-1S spectroscope (Shimadzu, Japan) for further spectral measurement and scanned in the range of 400 to 4000 cm–1 with a resolution of 4 cm–129.

GC–MS analysis

The Shimadzu GC–MS-QP2010 was utilized to perform GC–MS analysis. The GC was equipped with a DB-5 (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm) capillary column. Initially, the device was maintained at a temperature of 100 °C for 2.0 min. At the rate of 10 °C per minute, the temperature was increased to 300 °C and maintained for 18 min. The injection port temperature and carrier gas flow rate were set at 260 °C and 1.21 mL/min, respectively. The samples were injected in split mode at 10:1. The MS scan range was chosen from 40 to 650 (m/z)30. The fragmentation patterns of mass spectra were compared with those stored in the Wiley8 and NIST14s Library MS database (analysed 27 January 2023). The percentage of each compound was computed using the relative peak area of each compound in the chromatogram. The integration concept was used in the ChemStation integrator algorithms (analysed 27 January 2023).

Screening of bioactive constituents from BME, BEE, BHME, BHEE

The reported compounds from all four extracts were pooled and their bioactivities were searched from literature followed by identification of potential anti-diabetic compounds for further screening. The drug-likeness approach (Lipinski’s rule of five) and computational toxicity estimation methods were used to screen selected compounds. Drug likeness overcomes ADME (Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Excretion) constraints while maintaining an oral bioavailability score greater than 0.50. An online programme known as Swiss ADME was utilised to study the drug-likeness features of selected compounds31. For toxicity estimation of these small molecules, a webserver called PROTOX-II was used32. In order to do this calculation, compound’s SMILES from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/, accessed on 19 March 2023) database were utilized.

Molecular docking

Protein preparation

The crystal structures of α-amylase (PDB ID: 2QV4), α-glucosidase (PDB ID: 5NN8) and DPP-4 (PDB ID: 1X70) proteins with the standard ligands acarbose and sitagliptin, respectively were downloaded in .pdb format from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) (https://www.rcsb.org/ (accessed on 11 January 2024)). The PDB is a worldwide archive used to retrieve the biological macromolecules 3D structures33. The receptors were prepared by removing water molecules, heteroatoms and default ligands. To the modified receptors, polar hydrogen atoms and kollman charges were added by AutoDock and then receptors were converted into PDBQT for further docking.

Ligand preparation

The 3D structures of the selected ligands were downloaded in .sdf format from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/ (accessed on 10 January 2024)). PubChem is publicly accessible repository for chemical substances and their associated activities against biological assays34. The downloaded structures were then converted into. pdbqt format using OPEN BABEL. Open Babel is a chemical toolbox used in interconversion of chemical structures in different formats used during computational modelling35.

Molecular docking

On the basis of already present co-crystallized ligand in the .pdb file, the inhibitor binding site was defined using Discovery Studio 2021. The grid parameters and the coordinate of origin (x, y and z) were set to include all the amino acid residues at the active site. The spacing between grid points was maintained at 0.375 Å. All the optimized ligands were docked to the active site of the respective proteins using AutoDock Vina36. Throughout this in-silico investigation, the docking exhaustiveness was set at 20 to guarantee Vina produced the best possible results in an acceptable time span. The protein–ligand complexes were visualized using Discovery Studio Visualizer (Discovery Studio Visualizer v.21.1.0.20298).

Membrane permeability

Soyasapogenol B, Lauroscholtzine, and Corydine in different DSB extracts were chosen to test their ability to pass the plasma membrane using PerMM tools (https://permm.phar.umich.edu/). Environmental factors were set depending on the cell’s physiological state, including pH 7.4 and temperature of 310 K. The samples were entered as PDB files. The chemicals’ passage through the plasma membrane was modelled in 3D. The energy transfer value demonstrated a compound’s ability to permeate the cell membrane, which can be used to predict its availability and toxicity in the therapeutic range, together with other parameters assessed.

Molecular dynamic simulation

The compound with best binding energies and H-bond interactions with all protein targets during molecular docking studies were selected for MD simulations using GROMACS 2020.1 software37. CHARMM27 as an all-atom force field38 was selected and topology of proteins and ligands was generated by SwissParam server39. The complex was solved using the TIP3P water model, neutralized with Na+ and Cl− ions, and equilibrated using a canonical [number of particles (N), system volume (V), and temperature (T), NVT] and isobaric-isothermic ensemble for 100 picoseconds (ps). The final MD run of protein–ligand complexes for 50 ns (ns) was carried out in the NPT group at 300 K temperature and 1 bar pressure. The trajectory files of the simulation were loaded using the VMD analysis tool40. The extracted files were used to calculate the root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuations (RMSF) for protein and ligand, Radius of gyration (RoG) of ligand in the complex to understand the proper dynamic changes of the protein–ligand complex at molecular level. The g_mmpbsa package used in GROMACS was employed to calculate the binding free energy (BE) of ligand-bound complexes. The BE was estimated for the known standard and selected phytochemicals that were bound to the target protein’s active site. The BE of the test complexes was calculated using MD simulation trajectories for 50 ns41.

Results

Preliminary phytochemical investigation

Present study explored different types of phytoconstituents that demonstrate anti-diabetic properties of DSB. Preliminary qualitative phytochemical analysis of all four extracts [methanolic (BME), ethanolic (BEE), hydro-methanolic (BHME) and hydro-ethanolic (BHEE)] revealed the relative presence of secondary metabolites such as phenolic, terpenoids, saponins, flavonoids, anthraquinones and cardiac glycosides as presented in Table 1. These compounds have medicinal importance in many pathological conditions such as diabetes mellitus, diabetes retinopathy and inflammation42. It has been reported that flavonoids and phenolics present in the plants may be commercially useful in pharmaceutical industries43. Also recent studies support the efficacy of flavonoids against diabetes mellitus by affecting insulin signalling44. Several terpenoids have been described as anti-diabetic agents and are under various phases of pre-clinical and clinical assessment because they can inhibit enzymes responsible for insulin resistance and glucose metabolism45,46.

Total phenolic and flavonoid content in various bark extracts

Polyphenols and flavonoids are mainly responsible for the antioxidant potential of plants. As a result, total phenolic content in an extract reflects the antioxidant effects of plant extracts. The total phenolic content of bark was expressed in terms of GAE using the standard curve equation (y = 18.172x + 0.016) with a correlation coefficient (R2) of 0.9911 (​Fig. 1a). BHEE revealed maximum content of polyphenols (127.072 ± 14.08031 μg GAE/g dry extract). In studied extracts, the decreasing trend of phenolic concentration was as follows: BHEE > BEE > BHME > BME (Fig. 1b).

FT-IR analysis of DSB

Phenolic compounds like chlorogenic acid, gallic acid, and caffeic acid are aromatic carboxylic acids found in various fruits and medicinal plants. These phytochemicals possess antioxidant potential and have gained a lot of attention because of their ability to reduce glucose levels and ability to treat diabetes mellitus by regulation of various cellular and molecular pathways47. This could be further demonstrated by the functional groups of active components present in the extracts. The FT-IR spectra of BME and BEE follow a similar trend, however BHME and BHEE show distinct trend from the alcoholic extracts (Fig. 2). The strong and broad bands observed between 3550–3200 cm−1 in all the extracts are related to the stretching vibration of hydroxyl groups (OH phenolic compounds)48. The OH group plays an important role in antioxidant, anti-bacterial, and anti-diabetic activities49. The absorption bands between 3300–2500 cm−1 and 3000–2840 cm−1 presented the O–H stretch and C-H stretch, which corresponded to carboxylic acids and alkanes, respectively. These bands are related to the vibration of CH3, CH2 groups and flavonoid components48. There is no band in the range 1720–1706 cm−1 for BHME and BHEE extracts, while BME and BEE have peaks in the range due to C=O stretching vibration50. The presence of amines/alkenes was determined by peak at 1649.89 cm−1. All the extracts have strong peaks in the region 1620–1610 cm−1 which can be due to C=C stretching corresponding to the presence of α, β-unsaturated ketone groups. These α, β-unsaturated ketones are reported to have anti-inflammatory action51. The band at 1450.47 cm−1 was attributed to the C–C stretching vibration. The bands in the range 1310–1250 cm−1 revealed the presence of C-O band esters. The band at 702.09 cm−1 can be assigned to alkene due to C=C bending.

GC–MS and ADMET analysis of compounds with anti-diabetic potential from DSB.

Chemical structure and composition analysis of the extracts can help in identification of multiple biological potential of different extracts of medicinal plants52. Nonetheless, to our knowledge, there are not many reports on GC–MS based plant metabolite characterization to reveal the presence of various bioactive compounds with anti-diabetic potential in alcoholic extracts of DSB. Therefore, the GC–MS analysis was performed in a systematic manner. Many peaks were observed in the extracts of bark and, each peak of the chromatogram designated the bioactive compounds that were recorded by relating their molecular weight, retention time, and molecular formula with those of the known compounds proposed by the Wiley8 and NIST14s library. The highest number of compounds (67) was observed in BME followed by 63 compounds in BEE, 47 compounds in BHME and 41 compounds in BHEE. A total of 139 compounds were identified and 10 compounds were found to be common in all the four extracts as represented by Venn diagram (Fig. 3). Out of these 139 compounds, 18 compounds were reported to have anti-diabetic potential53–55.

GC–MS chromatograms of the extracts of DSB (a) BME, (b) BEE, (c) BHME, (d) BHEE (e) Venn diagram showing the compounds common to all the extracts [High resolution images (a-d) created using GCMSsolution v. 4.11 (Shimadzu Corporation); and Venn diagram (e) created by freely available tool (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/)].

The absorption, distribution, metabolism, and elimination (ADME) parameters and toxicity prediction of these compounds were estimated using online servers SwissADME and ProTox 2, to identify promising compounds with a better drug-likeness and low toxicity. In drug development and research, potential drug candidates with unfavourable (ADME) parameters do not go for clinical trials56. Compounds satisfying Lipinski’s rule of five regarding molecular weight (≤ 500 KDa), molecular refractivity (40–130), number of H-bond donors (≤ 5), number of H-bond acceptors (≤ 10), and the octanol–water partition coefficient (LogP ≤ 5), are tabulated in Table 2 and compounds with no violations or a minimum of a single violation were subsequently selected. Two or more violations indicate that candidates may not be suitable as oral drug57. Poorly absorbed but highly potent compounds were selected because the absorption can be enhanced with future research on better delivery methods. On the Basis of Lipinski’s rule, the compounds which exhibit good oral absorption and bioavailability scores (0.55 or above) were anticipated to be orally absorbed58. Further, drug-likeness properties of selected compounds were examined using predicted Median Lethal Dose (LD50) values in mg/Kg by ProTox2 server. Most of the 18 potential compounds observed for ADME parameters among the identified library of 139 compounds showed good bioavailability scores except Isorhoifolin which has bioavailability score of 0.17. While the compounds like Dehydroergosterol and Docosanoic acid were not selected due to their poor LD50 value.

Molecular docking analysis

Human lysosomal acid-α-glucosidase enzyme

α-Glucosidase is an important enzyme in human body which plays crucial role in carbohydrate metabolism59. Molecular docking of 15 selected compounds was studied against human lysosomal acid-alpha-glucosidase enzyme (PDB ID: 5NN8) by AutoDock Vina compared to standard acarbose. Molecular Interaction analysis showed that acarbose shows hydrogen bonding with the residues Trp376, Arg411, Asp406, Ser679, Arg375, Leu677, and Ser379 (Fig. 4). Analysis of docking results showed that the binding affinity (kcal/mol) of compounds like Beta-amyron and Soyasapogenol B are -13 kcal/mol and − 7.8 kcal/mol respectively which is less or more negative than -7.7 kcal/mol of acarbose as shown in Table 3. A lower or more negative value of the binding affinity indicates a stronger binding interaction between the compound and the enzyme. Table 4 revealed that these compounds inhibit the alpha-glucosidase through hydrogen bonding.

Human pancreatic α-amylase

Α-amylases are found in several living creatures, including plants, animals, bacteria, and fungus. In humans they are found in salivary glands, and the pancreas. Pancreas secretes them continually into the small intestine during process of digestion. They catalyse the hydrolysis of polysaccharides into monosaccharides, diaccharides, and oligosaccharides, which are subsequently transformed into glucose supply for the body’s energy60. Asp197, Glu233, and Asp300 are important residues in α-amylase active sites, along with other key residues such as Gly306, Tyr62 Phe298, Phe265 and Phe 295, Arg337 and Arg195, His101, His201, Trp203, Trp284, Trp58, Trp59, and Trp299, Asn298 and Ala30760–62.

Table 4 shows the molecular interactions between selected compounds and the α-amylase receptor on the catalytic residues. Among these compounds Soyasapogenol B showed conventional hydrogen bond with Asp300, Glu233 and His299 while Lauroscholtzine shows H-bond interactions with Asp197, His201, Glu233, and Thr163 (Fig. 4). Also the binding affinity of Soyasapogenol B is more negative than Acarbose as shown in Table 3.

Dipeptidyl-peptidase 4 (DPP-4)

Dipeptidyl-peptidase 4 (DPP-4) is a serine exopeptidase (belongs to the S9B family) whose main activity is to cleave X-proline dipeptides from the N-terminus of polypeptides such as growth factors, neuropeptides, and incretin hormones (one of the most important regulators of postprandial insulin secretion). Inhibition of DPP-4 presents a new prospective treatment approach for T2DM as monotherapy and is presently available for medicinal purposes60.

The molecular docking of selected compounds and the standard sitagliptin was performed in the active sites of DPP-4 (PDB ID 1X70). The binding affinity of Soyasapogenol B with DPP-4 was − 8.6 kcal/mol, which was slightly higher than that of the standard sitagliptin (− 8.9 kcal/mol) as shown in Table 3. The DPP-4 active site (α/β-hydrolase domain) has important amino acid residues in the range 39 to 51 and 501 to 70663 (Fig. 5).

Membrane permeability test

The membrane permeability test demonstrated that Soyasapogenol B, Lauroscholtzine, and Corydine had greater ability to enter the plasma membrane than Acarbose and Sitagliptin as revealed by their conformational changes as they enter the cell membrane (Fig. 6b). Each molecule constantly adjusted its position to match the cell plasma membrane’s hydrophilic and hydrophobic characteristics. During movement, the non-polar side of the compound rotates and sinks into the lipid acyl chain region, while the more polar side moves towards the membrane boundaries64. Energy transfer values revealed that Corydine and Soyasapogenol B had higher negative values at the centre of the phospholipid membrane (Fig. 6a) than Acarbose. Soyasapogenol B and Corydine had a lower value, indicating that they may easily pass through acyl chain area.

Molecular dynamic simulation studies

The stability of the protein–ligand interaction and the protein structure after binding to the ligand were investigated using molecular dynamic simulations. The molecular dynamics modelling findings revealed that the 2QV4-Soyasapogenol B and 2QV4-Corydine complexes were stable, with RMSD values below 3 Å. The RMSD controls the protein’s flexibility, equilibration, and the average distance between the protein atoms. The resulting plot (Fig. 7a) illustrates the RMSD, indicating that the complexes had stabilised. The system stability is defined by low RMSD values followed by few fluctuations.65. The 2QV4-Soyasapogenol B complex was more stable than 2QV4-Corydine, which exhibited a larger RMSD value.

Molecular dynamics simulation results of 2QV4-Acarbose, 2QV4-Soyasapogenol B, and 2QV4-Corydine complexes (a) RMSD complexes (b) Radius of gyration (c) RMSF (d) Δ binding energy (kJ/mol).

The radius of gyration (Rg) measures variations in the compactness of the ligand–protein complex. Rg also regulates the stable folding and unfolding of proteins and their complexes. A high Rg indicates a low compactness of the protein–ligand complex. During MD modelling, stable protein folding is determined by keeping a relatively constant value of Rg; if Rg fluctuates over time, the protein is unfolding66.The protein and protein–ligand complex had an average Rg of 23.5 Å, ranging from 23.0 to 23.8 Å (Fig. 7b). The results showed consistent Rg values, which indicates high stability due to the complex’s same compactness tendency.

Root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) was used to estimate the average movement of atoms at a given temperature and pressure. A low RMSF number indicates a stable system, whereas a high RMSF indicates greater system flexibility during simulated paths66. Figure 7c depicts the flexibility of residues in proteins and complexes, as well as their fluctuation. The majority of amino acids exhibited RMSF values less than 4 Å. However, the 2QV4-Soyasapogenol B system displayed more flexible residues at residue number 106–109 and at 306 in comparison to protein and protein–ligand systems. In general, different protein regions showed varying levels of flexibility with very small deviations suggesting overall stability during simulation.

Discussion

Dalbergia sissoo is a commercially important rosewood species endemic to northern India and southern Iran. It is valued for its high-quality timber and plays a significant role in traditional medicine for a variety of skin ailments, stomach infections and metabolic conditions. Due to the commercial processing for its timber use, there is accumulation of wood waste besides from other parts like leaves. With the rise in the usage of herbal medicines around the world, this study focused on drug discovery and probable development of the alcoholic and hydro-alcoholic extracts of bark and its bioactive compounds as safe and effective prospective anti-diabetic drugs. Attempt has been made to use both experimental and computational methodologies to identify and select bioactive molecules with anti-diabetic properties. The selected compounds were evaluated for inhibitory ability against α-amylase, α-glucosidase, and DPP-4 enzymes using in-silico techniques.

As natural products are more complex than synthetic molecules, pharmaceuticals based on natural product structures have greater diversity and occupy a larger chemical space than drugs of synthetic origin67. Compounds discovered using GC–MS analysis from DSB extracts, including Soyasapogenol B, Beta-amyrone, corydine, Lauroscholtzine, and Hinokine, have been claimed to possess anti-diabetic potential, albeit their target and action mechanisms have not been investigated yet54,55,68. It is worth noting that present findings suggest that these compounds support Lipinski’s rule of five. Hence, drugs developed employing them may have considerable advantages over the synthetic drugs like acarbose. Interestingly, acarbose breached three out of the five rules of Lipinski’s RO569.

Reducing postprandial blood sugar is an effectual and appealing technique for managing T2DM70. Many natural compounds derived from plants have the potential to be medicinal agents; therefore, rapid screening of these compounds via molecular docking can swiftly address this demand. Docking describes protein–ligand interactions through a computational method of molecular recognition71. The potential anti-diabetic effects of DSB extracts and their phytochemicals, were investigated for their inhibitory potential against α-amylase, α-glucosidase, and DPP-4 as targets. A relative safety of the bioactives has been demonstrated by toxicity screening, as well as considerable inhibitory potential in comparison to acarbose. Non-toxicity, effective inhibition of target enzymes, and greater membrane permeability validate anti-diabetic properties of their phytochemicals or bioactive molecule reservoirs.

Our docking results indicate that the majority of the bioactive compounds in bark extracts, such as Soyasapogenol B, Corydine, and Lauroscholtzine, can compete with the substrate for the enzyme’s active site in the same way that acarbose and sitagliptin as standard drugs inhibits α-amylase and DPP-4, respectively72,73. This tendency is further verified by the current study’s findings, which show that typical medicines and bioactive compounds occupy similar binding sites and assume similar binding poses (Fig. 4,5). Aside from the binding pose/binding site occupied, all bioactive compounds were bound to the enzymes near the catalytic site domain where conventional inhibitors bind (Fig. 4). DSB extracts may inhibit α-amylase, α-glucosidase, and DPP-4 by a competitive mechanism, similar to acarbose74.

In contrast, Beta-amyrone, Lupeol, and Lup-20(29)-en-3-one, have a higher binding affinity than acarbose for target enzymes (Table 3). However, these did not compete with acarbose for the active site because they attached to the proteins at distinct locations. This shows that they are likely to contribute to the overall inhibitory impact of total bark extracts through the non-competitive method of inhibition. The study found that most potential compounds in bark extracts inhibited α-amylase, α-glucosidase, DPP-4, or both through competitive or non-competitive inhibitory mechanisms. As per literature survey, this study is significant in being the first in-silico study on the inhibitory potential of bioactives on the targets of T2DM from DSB extracts. These extracts contain phytochemicals that inhibit α-amylase, α-glucosidase, and DPP-4, making them potential therapeutic targets for T2DM. This study found that the extracts and phytochemicals studied could be a better prospective therapeutic agent with superior efficacy than acarbose besides having strong inhibitory potential against α-amylase, α-glucosidase, and DPP-4 with distinct anti-diabetic mechanisms.

Present investigation also identified the best docking poses for bioactive chemicals and their binding locations on α-amylase, α-glucosidase, and DPP-4 (Fig. 4,5). This involves a variety of bonding interactions, including typical hydrogen, carbon-hydrogen, pi-sigma, pi-pi, pi-alkyl, and so on. According to the literature, the pi–pi interaction for stabilizing a structural complex is comparable to the strength of a hydrogen bond in an excited state75. In the ground state, the loss of the pi-pi contact does not impact the active-site conformation, but results in a 20 to 30 fold reduction in chemical activity rate76. However, hydrophobic or van der Waals interactions may increase ligand affinity for the target protein77. As a result, this study assessed the binding affinity of ligand and protein complexes by measuring binding energy, H-bonds, pi-pi interactions, and van der Waals interactions. Figure 4 demonstrates that the extract’s bioactive components bind to critical binding residues of α-amylase, including ASP300, ASP197, and GLU23360. Aside from these critical amino acid residues, ARG195, HIS299, and GLY306 established hydrogen bonds, as illustrated in Table 4. In contrast, α-glucosidase interacts with the binding pocket of the following amino acid residues: ASP406, ARG375, SER679, TRP376, and ARG375, whereas DPP-4 interacts with amino acid residues in the range 39 to 51 and 501 to 706 at its active site, which play an important role in phytochemical binding (Table 4).

Corydine exhibited the lowest binding energy, yet it interacted with all three targets’ identified essential catalytic site amino acid residues through a traditional hydrogen bond, pi–pi interaction, and van der Waals interaction. Interestingly, acarbose interacts with critical amino acid residues involved in catalysis at the active region of α-amylase and α-glucosidase (Fig. 4). Selected compounds may inhibit α-amylase and α-glucosidase in a similar way as acarbose does.

The prediction of membrane permeability is required for the development and optimization of delivery of novel medications. PerMM server examination of plasma membrane permeability found that Soyasapogenol B and Corydine have low energy transfer values, implying that these two chemicals can easily penetrate the lipid acyl chain area of the lipid bilayer. As a result, no viable delivery mechanisms, such as liposomes or nanoparticles, are required to administer these two therapeutic candidate molecules.

The stability of these two compounds with α-amylase was assessed using molecular dynamic simulations. The RMSD value indicates the conformational stability of the protein–ligand complex. A complex is stable if its RMSD value is less than 3 Å78.The α-amylase-Soyasapogenol B and α-amylase-corydine complexes have RMSD values below 3 Å, indicating their stability during the simulation. The binding free energies of the complexes determined using MM-PBSA approach support these findings (Fig. 7d). Molecular dynamic binding energy is influenced by the value of potential energy and solvation energy of complexes, ligands, and proteins. The more negative the value, the more stable the protein–ligand interaction41. The radius of gyration (Rg), and the RMSF values were used to assess the protein’s conformational stability following ligand binding. The radius of gyration indicates protein structural compactness. A high Rg value suggests that the protein conformation changes due to the loss of bonds between residues. In contrast, a small Rg value indicates that there are interactions between residues that make the protein stable79. The Rg of all complexes in this study were stable, indicating that there was no significant conformational change in the proteins after binding to the ligands. The RMSF value was also utilised to determine the conformational stability of the protein. Residues having an RMSF value between 1 and 3 Å are stable80.

Conclusion

The findings of this study, which included GC–MS analysis and predictions on the basis of in-silico explorations, are notable as they reveal that bark extracts of D. sissoo contain bioactive chemicals that may be effective in the treatment of diabetes. These findings serve as a foundation for evaluating bark’s potential as a plant-based medicine in diabetes treatment. Nonetheless, more research is required to validate the in-silico explorations and to determine the bioaccessibility and probable mechanism(s) of bioactives in the crude extracts responsible for T2DM alleviation. The tremendous potential of these plant extracts and selected compounds implicated in treating diabetes necessitates further pharmacokinetic studies.
