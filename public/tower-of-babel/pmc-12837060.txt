Anesthetic management for open reduction and internal fixation of the mandibular fracture in a patient with congenital pulmonary hypoplasia: a case report
Authors: Duo Yang, Renzhe Lin, Sen Li, Zitian Luo, Guangwei Hu, Huankai Zhang, Longsheng Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCongenital pulmonary hypoplasia is an exceptionally rare developmental disorder that is usually diagnosed in neonates and seldom encountered in adult.Consequently, evidence-based perioperative strategies for adult CPH patients undergoing non-thoracic surgery remain virtually absent. We present a case of an adult with CPH who required general anesthesia for open reduction and internal fixation of the mandibular fracture, and we describe a comprehensive perioperative management plan centered on lung-protective ventilation strategy and multimodal analgesia.Case presentationThis report presents the case of a 46-year-old man with incidentally diagnosed congenital left pulmonary hypoplasia who underwent open reduction and internal fixation of a mandibular fracture. After multidisciplinary evaluation of cardiopulmonary and airway status, surgery was performed under general anesthesia with lung-protective ventilation strategy, multimodal analgesia and restrictive fluid management. Postoperative treatment included intravenous dexmedetomidine-sufentanil continuous analgesia and vigilant respiratory monitoring. The patient was discharged on post-operative day 3 without pulmonary complications.An individualized post-discharge surveillance schedule was arranged that included evaluations at one week, one month, and three months, and no pulmonary complications or other adverse events were identified during the entire three-month follow-up period.At eighteen months after discharge the patient returned for review, chest CT and colour Doppler echocardiography demonstrated stable cardiopulmonary status with no signs of residual complications.ConclusionsThis case demonstrates that adults with asymptomatic CPH can safely undergo general anesthesia when a meticulous, multidisciplinary pathway is followed. Individualized lung-protective ventilation strategy, restrictive fluid management, and multimodal analgesia effectively averted intra-operative barotrauma, volutrauma and postoperative pulmonary complications.

Background

CPH is a rare congenital disorder characterized by defective development of lung parenchyma that may result in severe restriction of pulmonary function [1, 2]. Owing to its extremely low incidence, the condition is usually identified in the pediatric population.The majority of affected neonates succumb to respiratory insufficiency shortly after birth [3]. Consequently, the diagnosis is seldom first established in adulthood, and published data on the peri-operative anesthetic management of adult patients with CPH remain scarce. These individuals carry a markedly elevated anesthetic risk whenever they require surgical intervention, making their management particularly challenging. Perioperative lung-protective ventilation strategy and multimodal analgesia have been widely adopted and shown to accelerate recovery in diverse surgical populations [4].Nevertheless, their use in adults with CPH has not yet been documented in the international literature.We present the peri-operative course of an adult with CPH who underwent open reduction and internal fixation of the mandibular fracture, analyze the specific hazards and requirements inherent to anesthesia and surgery in this setting, and review the available literature to provide clinical guidance for the treatment of similarly afflicted patients.

Case presentation

The patient is a 46-year-old man (height 175 cm, weight 78.5 kg) who presented to our hospital with mandibular pain three hours after accidental trauma. reported no associated headache, dizziness, or loss of consciousness and was admitted with the diagnosis of mandibular fracture.

The patient had been previously healthy and reported no history of hypertension, coronary artery disease, or diabetes mellitus. He denied any drug allergies and had no history of tobacco or alcohol use.The patient denied any history of recurrent respiratory tract infections, chronic cough, haemoptysis, wheezing, exertional dyspnoea, chest pain, or previous hospitalisations for pulmonary reasons. There was no history of tuberculosis, bronchial asthma, or prior thoracic surgery. He had never received long-term oxygen therapy or bronchodilators. On physical examination, temperature was 36.5 °C, BP 161/100 mmHg, HR 96 beats per minute, and respiratory rate 20 beats per minute. Stomatognathic examination revealed severe trismus (inter-incisal distance < 1 finger-breadth), deviated opening pattern, and markedly disturbed dental occlusion.Airway evaluation revealed severely limited mouth opening, full cervical spine mobility, patent bilateral nares, and inability to perform Mallampati classification due to trismus.Laboratory analyses including complete blood count, hepatic, renal function panels, coagulation profile and arterial blood gas were within normal limits. Pre-operative 12-lead electrocardiography showed sinus rhythm, incomplete right bundle-branch block, and clockwise rotation at a HR of 91 beats per minute. Maxillofacial CT confirmed a fracture of the mandibular body(Fig. 1).High-resolution CT of the chest revealed marked hypoplasia of the left lung(Figs. 2, 3 and 4).The hemithorax was small, contained multiple cystic lucencies, and demonstrated virtually no normal aerated parenchyma.The left main-stem bronchus was patent. The mediastinum and heart were deviated to the left. The right lung showed compensatory over-inflation, remained clear, and contained a single 3 mm subpleural nodule with smooth margins.Tracheal and right-sided bronchial lumina were widely patent without stenosis.Pulmonary function testing demonstrated mild to moderate restrictive ventilatory impairment. Forced vital capacity was 3.24 L (71.3% of predicted), forced expiratory volume in one second was 2.55 L (68.95% of predicted), and the FEV1/FVC ratio was 78.76% (96.8% of predicted).Colour Doppler echocardiography showed normal atrial and ventricular morphology with no chamber enlargement, normal pulmonary artery pressure, and an ejection fraction of 65%.The patient reported excellent health, tolerance of heavy daily physical labor, and absence of exertional dyspnea, angina, or hemoptysis.Preoperative diagnoses were mandibular fracture and CPH. Elective open reduction and internal fixation of the mandibular fracture under general anesthesia delivered via nasotracheal intubation was scheduled.Preoperative anesthetic assessment classified the patient as American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status III.A multidisciplinary team meeting that included specialists from oral surgery, anesthesiology, thoracic surgery, and respiratory medicine was held before the operation. The experts agreed that, although the patient has a rare congenital disorder with compensatory enlargement of the right lung, the left lung showed no evidence of infection and the patient could tolerate general anesthesia with tracheal intubation. Intraoperative lung-protective ventilation strategy and restrictive fluid management were recommended, and comprehensive perioperative analgesia was deemed essential.

After admission to the operating room, standard monitoring of vital signs was established. BP was 140/90 mmHg, heart rate 80 beats per minute, and respiratory rate 20 beats per minute. A peripheral intravenous line was secured in an upper extremity and Ringer’s solution was infused. Oxygen was delivered via face mask. Two drops of ephedrine solution were instilled into the left nostril at a five-minute interval. Rapid-sequence induction was performed with sufentanil 25 µg, propofol 160 mg, and rocuronium 50 mg. After adequate preoxygenation with positive-pressure ventilation, nasotracheal intubation through the left nostril was accomplished uneventfully under vision provided by a Disposcope endoscope [5] (Jiangsu Taiyuan Medical Equipment Technology Co., Ltd., Taiyuan, China), and the tracheal tube was connected to the anesthesia machine for controlled mechanical ventilation.Anaesthesia was maintained with 2% sevoflurane combined with continuous intravenous infusions of dexmedetomidine(Infusion rate:40 µg/h), propofol(Infusion rate:4 ~ 10 mg/kg/h) and remifentanil (Infusion rate:0.1 ~ 0.2 µg/kg/min).Lung-protective ventilation strategy was applied throughout the procedure using a tidal volume of 6 mL/kg, respiratory rate 14 ~ 16 beats per minute, positive end-expiratory pressure 3 cm H2O, fresh gas flow 2 L/min and inspired oxygen fraction 50%. Lung recruitment manoeuvres were performed manually at intervals and PETCO2 was maintained between 35 ~ 45 mmHg.Anesthetic agents were precisely adjusted according to hemodynamic responses and BIS, which were kept between 40 and 60 to guarantee appropriate anesthetic depth. Intravenous fluids were administered as Ringer’s solution at 1 ~ 2 mL/kg/h in accordance with a restrictive hydration strategy.Combined warming with a fluid warmer and a medical temperature control blanket(3 M Bair Hugger Patient Warming System, ) was used throughout the procedure to keep core temperature above 36 °C. BP and HR were maintained within 20% of baseline values. Intraoperative arterial blood gas analysis showed that pH, PaO2, and PaCO2 were all within the normal range.Before skin closure the surgeon infiltrated the incision with 10 mL of 0.2% ropivacaine. At the end of surgery the patient was transferred to the postanesthesia care unit.In the postanesthesia care unit the patient regained full consciousness with stable vital signs and adequate spontaneous ventilation. After thorough suctioning the trachea was extubated and an intravenous analgesia pump was connected. The intravenous analgesic pump was prepared by adding sufentanil 150 µg, dexmedetomidine 100 µg, and tropisetron 5 mg to normal saline for a total volume of 100 mL, delivered at 2 mL/h. After tracheal extubation the patient was monitored for thirty minutes and discharged to the ward when a modified Aldrete score of at least 9 was achieved and vital signs remained stable.The combined duration of anesthesia and surgery was approximately 120 min. Intraoperative infusion of Ringer’s solution totaled 1000 mL and estimated blood loss was 20 mL. Urine output could not be recorded because an indwelling urinary catheter was not placed.Postoperatively, the patient was assisted to sit on the edge of the bed within 2 h of extubation and performed 5 supervised deep inspirations every 8 h while awake. He took his first short corridor walk on the day of surgery.Throughout the postoperative inpatient period the patient remained free of pulmonary complications such as infection, pneumothorax, atelectasis, or respiratory failure, or other adverse events, and the VAS for pain never exceeded 3. The patient was discharged on the third postoperative day.An individualized post-discharge surveillance schedule was arranged that included evaluations at one week, one month, and three months, and no pulmonary complications or other adverse events were identified during the entire three-month follow-up period.At eighteen months after discharge the patient returned for review, chest CT(Figs. 2, 3 and 4) and colour Doppler echocardiography demonstrated stable cardiopulmonary status with no signs of residual complications.Written informed consent was obtained from the patient and his family for the publication of this case report. Institutional review board exemption was obtained.

Discussion and conclusion

This report describes in detail the anesthetic management of a patient with CPH who underwent open reduction and internal fixation of the mandibular fracture.Perioperative anesthetic and surgical management strategies for such cases remain under exploratory. CPH is a rare developmental anomaly characterized by impaired formation of pulmonary vasculature or bronchi due to inherent defects in primordial mesenchymal or parenchymal tissue.CPH in adults lacks specific clinical manifestations. Patients may present with recurrent pulmonary infection accompanied by productive cough or complain of dyspnea and chest tightness, yet the disorder can also remain clinically silent. Because most clinicians are unfamiliar with this entity, it is frequently misdiagnosed as pulmonary tuberculosis or atelectasis.Pulmonary hypoplasia usually develops secondary to other congenital anomalies such as diaphragmatic hernia, cardiac malformations or genitourinary defects. The isolated form is uncommon and generally regarded as idiopathic. In the present patient the hypoplastic lung was not associated with any additional congenital disorder. Although most cases of unilateral involvement are identified during childhood, a small proportion remain asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic and are discovered incidentally on thoracic imaging in adulthood. Studies indicate that up to 50% of neonates with unilateral pulmonary hypoplasia die during the newborn period [6].As the child grows, the healthy lung expands and progressively occupies the thoracic space on the hypoplastic side, shifting the mediastinum toward that side. Because the contralateral lung provides compensatory respiratory capacity, patients with unilateral pulmonary hypoplasia often have few or only subtle symptoms at first-an intermittent cough or mild respiratory infection. The condition is encountered most frequently on the left side, as seen in the present patient. The right lung’s three lobes allow more effective compensation, which may account for the better survival observed in left-sided pulmonary hypoplasia.Owing to financial constraints and his self-reported good health, the patient had never undergone any screening for pulmonary disease.During the preoperative work-up for this mandibular fracture, the patient underwent chest computed tomography rather than plain radiography to rule out possible rib fractures, and the imaging incidentally revealed that he had CPH.Had this injury not required surgical treatment, the CPH might have remained undetected.Because the patient’s pre-operative BP was 161/100 mmHg and he also had unilateral CPH with mild to moderate restrictive ventilatory impairment on pulmonary function testing, we assigned an ASA physical status III. The anesthetic and surgical risk was therefore judged to be high and required close peri-operative monitoring.

Patients with CPH carry elevated anesthetic risk throughout the perioperative period, particularly in relation to airway control and the choice of ventilatory strategy. Reduced pulmonary reserve decreases tolerance of volatile agents and mechanical ventilation, thereby increasing the likelihood of postoperative pulmonary complications.The importance of a lung-protective ventilatory strategy during the perioperative period is widely recognized, especially for reducing pulmonary complications [7]. In this case the strategy included a tidal volume of 6 mL/kg, respiratory rate 14 ~ 16 beats per minute, positive end-expiratory pressure 3 cm H2O, fresh gas flow 2 L/min, inspired oxygen fraction 50% and periodic lung recruitment maneuvers. These interventions decrease the risk of alveolar overdistension and collapse, thereby minimizing the potential for lung injury.Prolonged low tidal volume ventilation may lead to carbon dioxide retention. Studies indicate that permissive hypercapnia attenuates ischemia reperfusion injury and oxidative stress, increases cardiac output, and reduces intrapulmonary shunt, thereby conferring lung protection. Recent reports consider it safe when the rise in arterial carbon dioxide is less than 10 mmHg per hour, the partial pressure remains below 65 mmHg, and pH stays above 7.20. In the present case end-tidal carbon dioxide was maintained between 35 ~ 45 mmHg, probably because the increased respiratory rate compensated for the small tidal volume.A PEEP of 3 cm H2O was applied during mechanical ventilation to prevent alveolar collapse and improve oxygenation while avoiding circulatory depression and lung injury that could result from excessive airway pressure.Because few published studies have addressed lung-protective ventilatory strategy in patients with CPH, an individualized approach is required that integrates all relevant variables to achieve optimal protection. Continuous monitoring of vital signs and pulmonary mechanics is essential during positive end expiratory pressure application. Data guiding the selection of an appropriate level of PEEP in this population remain scarce, and further investigations are needed to provide evidence based recommendations.

Multimodal analgesia was provided by local anesthetic infiltration of the surgical site combined with continuous intravenous opioid infusion, lowering postoperative pain scores and reducing both opioid consumption and the incidence of pulmonary complications. This balanced approach minimizes dose dependence on any single agent and thereby decreases the risk of adverse effects related to excessive medication.Throughout the postoperative inpatient stay the patient’s the VAS for pain never exceeded 3, demonstrating the efficacy and importance of multimodal analgesia. Adequate balanced analgesia contributes to lower incidence of postoperative pulmonary complications and facilitates rapid recovery.Quality of recovery is a key indicator of anaesthetic efficacy. In this patient no pulmonary complications were identified during hospital stay or during assessments up to three months after discharge. Chest CT and colour Doppler echocardiography performed eighteen months later revealed stable cardiopulmonary findings without residual sequelae, confirming that the integrated anaesthetic approach is feasible. Future investigations should clarify the long-term benefits and optimal application of lung-protective ventilation and multimodal analgesia in individuals with CPH.

Because this disorder is exceptionally rare and relevant reports in the international literature are scarce, we obtained comprehensive preoperative assessments including pulmonary function tests and colour Doppler echocardiography and then convened a multidisciplinary team to formulate precise, efficient and individualized care. This approach enhanced therapeutic efficacy and facilitated rapid postoperative recovery, demonstrating the practical value of multidisciplinary consultation in perioperative decision making for rare diseases.Recent international reports of CPH in adults are sparse and originate almost exclusively from thoracic surgery. Ramesh and colleagues described a 72-year-old man with absence of the right lung, pulmonary artery agenesis, and bronchial atresia who underwent right pneumonectomy for recurrent aspergilloma and experienced complete symptom resolution at one year [8].Qiu and colleagues reported a 64-year-old patient with buccal carcinoma and left pulmonary hypoplasia who, because of a 30-year history of bronchiectasis and insufficient contralateral reserve, declined resection and elected for definitive chemoradiation without subsequent respiratory failure, illustrating that conservative management remains viable when pulmonary function is extremely limited [9].Gondal and colleagues reviewed the course of a 66-year-old man with hypoplastic right lung and honeycombing who was hospitalized fifty-six times for pneumonia over ten years and ultimately underwent right pneumonectomy after comprehensive evaluation [10]. These cases demonstrate that chronic hyperinflation and recurrent infection of the unaffected lung are typical in adults with congenital hypoplasia, and that thorough preoperative assessment of airway and pulmonary reserve is mandatory before any surgical intervention, whether pulmonary resection or an alternative procedure.

Comprehensive preoperative evaluation and multidisciplinary discussion were followed by intraoperative protective ventilation, multimodal analgesia and an individualised anaesthetic plan, complemented by meticulous postoperative nursing.These measures prevented pulmonary complications and allowed rapid recovery, with discharge on the third postoperative day.Long-term follow-up demonstrated stable cardiopulmonary function, confirming the efficacy of our integrated strategy.Carefully tailored perioperative anesthetic management in patients with CPH allows anesthesiologists to deliver safer and more effective care that facilitates rapid recovery. This case further supports the efficacy of lung protective ventilation strategy and multimodal analgesia in reducing pulmonary complications and improving outcome.

Limitations

This report details the anaesthetic management of a patient with CPH who underwent open reduction and internal fixation of a mandibular fracture. As with all case reports, the findings cannot be generalised and causality cannot be inferred. Nevertheless, these observations may enhance understanding of perioperative care in perioperative anesthetic management in patients with CPH and add to the limited literature on anaesthetic management of this rare condition.
