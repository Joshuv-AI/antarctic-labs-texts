SolarMagix: An integrated multi-station photovoltaic and weather dataset for solar energy forecasting, fault detection, and anomaly analysis
Authors: Andrej Daskalov, Marija Dimitrova, Tijana Atanasovska, Petre Lameski, Biljana Risteska Stojkoska, Eftim Zdravevski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper investigates the fusion of photovoltaic (PV) system performance data with weather data to create a robust dataset for energy production forecasting and anomaly detection. The study integrates data from multiple PV stations across various manufacturers and locations, addressing challenges in data quality, timestamp alignment, and inconsistent granularity. The resulting dataset underwent rigorous cleaning and aggregation, providing both aggregated and consolidated views, as well as raw telemetry data, for a wide variety of downstream use cases. The refined dataset includes 13,193 rows of station-level data and 88,285 rows of inverter-level data, reflecting energy production and weather parameters. The dataset also includes granular, unfiltered data from the photovoltaic stations, comprising > 1.2 million rows of combined telemetry data. The integration of energy production data with weather data provides a comprehensive foundation for future machine learning applications to improve the efficiency and reliability of PV systems. This dataset is a valuable contribution to the growing body of work focused on optimising solar energy generation through intelligent data integration.Keywords: Photovoltaic power generation, Multivariate time series, Weather-aware photovoltaic dataset, Data quality and validation

Background

With the increasing prevalence of photovoltaic (PV) power plants and other renewable energy sources, these systems are gaining greater significance in overall electricity production within the power distribution network. They are essential to advancing society toward renewable, clean, and environmentally conscious energy sources. However, when utilised without robust monitoring and control, such systems often incur substantial losses, primarily due to an unprepared distribution grid, suboptimal weather conditions, and equipment failures and malfunctions.

By optimising PV power plant operations and enabling early fault detection, it is possible to extend effective operating time, increase overall energy production, and reduce the need for grid compensation from other sources. Using a modern production forecasting model, deviations from normal system performance can be detected. Such a model would generalise across a larger number of devices at different locations. As predictors, the model would use readings from real stations and weather data.

The objective of the paper is to investigate the integration of structured time-series PV data from multiple sources with weather data to create a dataset for further research. This dataset could be used to train models for production forecasting and anomaly detection.

Data Description

The final hourly dataset is structured using a dimensional model, with dedicated fact tables with combined PV + weather data, available at station and inverter aggregation levels. This denormalised modelling approach allows for efficient querying and analysis of the relationship between PV performance and weather conditions. In addition to the dimensional model, we provide the silver and gold layer tables used to derive the fact tables. This includes granular and hourly views into both vendor-specific and combined data. Finally, we provide metadata tables, such as data_status, which provide descriptions of the data quality tags we set throughout the granular tables. We also provide mapping tables and a description table for the categorical inverter states.

Denormalisation of the data was implemented to facilitate machine learning applications and analytical processes. By integrating energy and weather data into unified tables, the complexity of join operations is minimised, resulting in faster query execution and more efficient model training. This denormalised architecture enhances data accessibility for time-series analysis, anomaly detection, and forecasting, thereby improving both computational performance and usability for analysts and machine learning models. Additionally, incorporating location information directly into the station dimension was a deliberate design decision, given that most stations possess unique location data, and creating a separate location dimension would introduce unnecessary joins.

Alongside the final hourly fact and dimension tables, the dataset also provides a set of canonical granular tables that capture higher-resolution inverter telemetry, data quality checks, and the intermediate enrichment steps used to construct the hourly PV plus weather facts. These tables enable reproducibility of the aggregation pipeline, support alternative feature engineering, and allow analysts to trace anomalies in the hourly outputs back to the underlying measurements. These granular tables include per-vendor data at the original granularity (fusionsolar_energy_production and solis_energy_production), combined PV telemetry coerced to a coarser granularity for consistency (energy_production), and hourly-aggregated tables (energy_production_hourly_station and energy_production_hourly_inverter), aggregated by station and inverter, respectively. These hourly aggregations are then joined with the sourced weather data to form the final dataset.

Dataset usage

Researchers may utilise this dataset with all existing data analysis and processing environments (e.g., Python, R, or SQL-based warehouses). By providing the data in an open, plain-text format, the dataset remains accessible and usable with any tool. Users would begin by loading the relevant files into their tool of choice, performing any necessary joins between tables, using the relationships described in Table 1. Then, after the data is loaded and prepared, standard cleanup and filtering can be applied, followed by the relevant research task. A practical use case involves joining the refined telemetry with station metadata to obtain geographic context, enabling comparative correlation analyses between localized atmospheric parameters and system performance across different manufacturer tiers. For diagnostic studies, manufacturer-specific status codes provided in the telemetry can be decoded via the included mapping tables to facilitate status-aware performance modeling. Furthermore, the structured nature of the data supports the development of machine learning pipelines for tasks such as multi-station energy production forecasting and anomaly detection.

Data quality summary

Ensuring high-quality data is fundamental for conducting robust analyses and achieving reliable model performance [1]. This paper introduces an aligned dataset that is predominantly clean and suitable for exploratory purposes, while acknowledging the presence of residual data quality issues. We present statistical analyses of the temporal alignment between energy production and weather measurements, examine missing data across various dimensions, and assess the overall coverage to evaluate how effectively the dataset represents photovoltaic (PV) system operation under the observed environmental conditions.

Although standard, rule-based data quality checks are applied, they are not sufficient to capture all forms of anomalous behaviour. In particular, there are periods with extended sequences of irregular values that remain undetected by static rules. As a result, the dataset should not be regarded as fully cleaned. For downstream model training, additional quality control is recommended, including the use of out-of-distribution detection or related anomaly identification methods to flag problematic segments before further preprocessing or learning.

To address such data quality issues, several studies have proposed advanced statistical and machine learning frameworks for data refinement. For instance, monthly linear fitting has been shown to correct measurement errors more effectively than traditional annual fitting, which often erroneously rejects valid operational data due to inherent seasonal deviations [2]. Other works incorporate statistical approaches such as Seasonal+Trend+Loess (STL) decomposition, the 3-sigma criterion, and box diagrams in order to isolate outliers from normal signal fluctuations [3]. Furthermore, some approaches utilise signal processing techniques like wavelet transforms and Kalman filtering, often integrated with moving average models, to mitigate high-frequency noise and enhance reliability in low-quality data [4]. Finally, modern data fusion approaches may include Natural Language Processing (NLP) techniques to reconcile production time series data with maintenance logs, providing the necessary context to distinguish between sensor communication failures and actual outages [5].

Outages and underperformance

To determine the extent of underperformance in the dataset, we devised calculations to estimate the percentage of possible outages per station. This is done by identifying hours when the irradiance exceeds a predefined threshold, but the PV production is below a minimal epsilon value. For this statistic and subsequent analyses, the global tilted irradiance is utilised as the selected irradiance metric. This value represents a time-averaged measurement over the previous hour, accounting for both tilt and azimuth considerations. To calculate these values accurately, we needed to determine the appropriate threshold and epsilon values.

Based on a sensitivity analysis of the power versus irradiance relationship and on IEC-based rooftop studies [6,7] that commonly retain data within roughly 50–1200 W/m², we set the minimum usable irradiance threshold to 150 W/m2. Readings above this irradiance threshold value should be well within the bounds of usable irradiance for energy production.

In terms of metrics for night-time or non-irradiance hours, the weather data source clearly distinguishes between irradiance and non-irradiance hours, with non-irradiance hours recorded exactly as zero. This is a consequence of both the data's hourly granularity and data cleanup on the source side. With this in mind, selecting values equal to zero is sufficient to calculate any non-irradiance statistic accurately.

Sensor noise

Selecting an appropriately small epsilon value for energy production is critical because it establishes a tolerance threshold below which production is deemed negligible, accounting for sensor noise. This epsilon is particularly important since sensor equipment frequently reports some level of production even during periods without irradiance. Therefore, it is essential to filter out these insignificant values. To ascertain the epsilon parameter, a data-driven methodology is employed. The mean production values during periods of negligible irradiance were averaged and used as the reference for calculating epsilon. The average production during non-irradiance hours was 1.1793 kWh. This value exceeded our initial expectations, as we anticipated it would be below 1 kWh. To investigate the source of this discrepancy, we also analysed the data by source (Table 3). The results indicate that the increased sensor noise originates from source 1.

Weather PV alignment

To ensure proper alignment of the dataset after PV-weather data fusion, we developed a statistic to measure the alignment percentage by station. This is achieved by identifying hours when irradiance exceeds a predefined threshold, but PV production remains below a minimal epsilon value. This check helps detect potential misalignments or system outages. Specifically, if irradiance is high (indicating favourable weather conditions for energy generation) but PV output is extremely low, it may suggest that the system is not functioning correctly, possibly due to technical issues, data errors, or outages. A high percentage of misalignment could also point to errors in data alignment and fusion, which is valuable information. By flagging these instances, the check helps ensure that the dataset accurately reflects energy production relative to the available sunlight, supporting better insights and more reliable machine learning models.

As previously noted, the minimum threshold irradiance value employed for calculating irradiance hours statistics was set at 150 W/m².

Seasonality

The dataset demonstrates pronounced seasonality, with a predominant proportion of data points recorded during the Spring season, accounting for 60.35% of the total entries. Row coverage is concentrated in spring due to commissioning/ingestion timing; therefore, the dataset is not season-balanced. The Fall season follows, representing 14.83% of the data, indicative of reduced energy production as daylight hours diminish. Winter data comprises 16.97% of the dataset, consistent with the expected decrease in solar radiation during colder months.

Summer, despite being a peak solar production period, shows the lowest percentage at 7.85%. This can be attributed to many inverters in the dataset beginning data ingestion much later, resulting in missing data during this season. This seasonal trend highlights the natural variations in energy production, influenced by both weather and the timing of data collection.

Instantaneous column discrepancies

To provide a broader view of the data, we included some instantaneous readings alongside the cumulative counters we mostly focused on. These columns include AC and DC power as well as inverter efficiency. These columns occasionally deviate from the domain logic that defines them, exhibiting inconsistent values. This may suggest either misreadings in the source or the manufacturers providing aggregated values without documenting them as such. In any case, the values provided for the columns in question are provided as such and not otherwise cleaned or additionally processed. Should researchers using this dataset choose to include these columns, they should perform some preprocessing or checks before any downstream use.

Experimental Design, Materials, and Methods

Data ingestion and warehousing

In order to facilitate the ingestion, storage and processing of the dataset, a robust data warehouse for PV plants was developed. It is designed to ingest and process data from various source systems, clean it and handle errors gracefully, while applying necessary domain knowledge. The warehouse was implemented using Python scripts, SQL procedures, and Apache Airflow as the orchestrator.

The data is organised in a medallion data architecture, with bronze, silver and gold layers. The high-level architecture is represented in Fig. 6. The medallion architecture, a multi-tier refinement framework introduced by Databricks [18], was chosen for its support of transparency, auditability, and reproducibility across our data preparation and processing stages. This structure systematically transforms data into progressively refined states, aligning with the foundational data engineering principles established by Strengholt [19]. Mohna et al. provide a comprehensive review of the medallion architecture and its applications across the data engineering industry [20].

High-level overview of medallion architecture.

Data is ingested incrementally daily. It first enters a temporary staging zone in batches, with scheduling designed to respect source rate limits. Metadata tables record load status, future scheduled batches, and informative metadata for quality tagging. The system features robust retry mechanisms, so that if part of the data fails to load, it is retried on the next load. All data is ingested in UTC to maintain consistency across stations and eliminate time zone discrepancies.

Source data

PV data sources

Source data lands in the warehouse via REST APIs in JSON format. Readings from inverters are identified by a composite key (inverter ID and timestamp) and include sensor readings (voltage, power, temperature, etc.) and cumulative energy counters, namely daily and device lifetime totals.

Weather data

For accurate analysis and modelling of PV plant performance, incorporating weather information is essential. PV energy production depends directly on factors such as solar irradiance, ambient temperature, wind speed and direction, and cloud cover. Without this data, models lack contextual information about the environment and its influence on outcomes, making both production forecasting and anomaly detection less accurate and prone to overfitting temporal patterns.

Source and characteristics of the weather data

The meteorological component of this dataset was obtained through the Open-Meteo API [21], which served as an interface to access the fifth-generation atmospheric reanalysis datasets ERA5 and ERA5-Land, produced by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) via the Copernicus Climate Change Service [22]. Data retrieval was conducted via the /v1/archive endpoint, which performs complex interpolation of raw GRIB-formatted reanalysis records into a tabular format. The resulting meteorological dataset provides a consistent time series at spatial resolutions of 0.25° (∼31 km) for atmospheric variables and 0.1° (∼11 km) for land-surface parameters, offering the most accurate historical estimates available for the study region. Similar to the photovoltaic systems’ data, weather data are ingested into the data warehouse via REST APIs in JSON format. It is important to note that the dataset does not incorporate on-site weather measurements. The specific columns extracted are detailed in Table 7. To ensure technical accuracy, all variable definitions adhere to the official specifications provided by the Open-Meteo API documentation.

Bronze layer

Upon successful ingestion, the bronze layer is where the data first lands. The bronze layer’s role is to preserve data as closely as possible to the source format, while ensuring consistency and schema. The data is coerced into a schema with well-defined types as soon as it is loaded into the bronze layer.

Since some PV sources occasionally exhibited late-arriving data, robust handling was required. This is achieved by fetching overlapping periods of data to check whether any previously missing data is present or whether any source-side corrections have been applied. Apart from late-arriving data handling, the bronze layer also handles general deduplication rules, ensuring uniqueness using a composite identity comprised of source, device/station ID, and UTC timestamp.

Time‑Zone alignment

The bronze layer ensures that temporal information in the data is maintained in a consistent UTC time zone across all tables in the system, thereby guaranteeing consistency and proper data alignment downstream. FusionSolar transmits data in UTC (by design or default behaviour), while Solis expects the time zone to be provided as a request parameter. Neither source used daylight saving time (DST), so no special handling was required. All database tables were designed in UTC for consistency, leaving any time‑zone conversions to client-side views (visualisations or applications). If a future source does not support specifying a time zone or relies on DST, time‑zone normalisation should be performed immediately after ingestion.

Open‑Meteo provides timestamps in UTC and does not apply daylight saving time, which eliminates potential DST complications.

Silver layer

Typically, in a medallion data architecture, the silver layer is where most transformations, data quality checks, and tagging are performed. The data is usually kept at the same granularity as the source, allowing it to be traced back to previous layers while ensuring quality. The silver layer serves as a single source of truth in a data warehouse, providing a solid foundation for downstream aggregations and analytics.

Data quality tagging

Ensuring data quality is essential for constructing a reliable dataset used in downstream applications. To this end, we propose a comprehensive data quality framework that addresses raw data issues, incorporates cleaning procedures, and implements validation steps to verify data plausibility, thereby ensuring its readiness for subsequent processing and analysis.

Geocoding station locations

To integrate weather data with PV station data, station locations had to be represented in coordinate form (latitude and longitude). One issue was that some stations do not provide their location in coordinate form. Although stations provided addresses, a geocoding service was necessary to convert them into precise geographic coordinates to enable use with 3rd-party weather services and further spatial analysis.

This process enabled accurate, consistent geolocation of all stations, which is then used as an input parameter when querying the OpenMeteo API. This process ensured that each station was accurately and consistently geolocated, with the resulting coordinates stored directly in the station table.

By embedding location attributes within the station data, the system maintains a direct association between performance and weather conditions, ensuring precise spatio-temporal alignment essential for forecasting and analytical purposes.

While the majority of addresses were geocoded accurately, some were either improperly formatted or incomplete. In future work, leveraging a language model (LLM) could further refine these addresses, improving data consistency and geocoding accuracy.

The gold layer provides the consumer-facing data marts, often aggregated, denormalised and optimised for querying. The gold layer in this particular data warehouse consolidates data from the previous layer, combining PV sources into a unified schema and integrating weather data accordingly.

Challenges in integrating data from different sources

Inconsistent granularities

Although vendor specifications prescribe a 5‑minute data granularity, the reality is more nuanced, even among devices from the same manufacturer. FusionSolar data is spaced mostly at exact 5‑minute intervals (except during faults or outages). However, Solis data readings were less regular: intervals were inconsistent, timestamps across inverters were not synchronised (even within the same station), and some devices had consistently coarser granularity (likely due to different logger hardware).

Two solutions were available to normalise granularity: (i) downsample to a coarser level at which intervals are globally consistent across devices, or (ii) impute missing values using a mathematical function or a model. Because data accuracy is paramount in this domain, option (i) was the only acceptable solution. The smallest coarser granularity at which intervals were consistent in most cases was approximately 10 min.

The solution we implemented was to create fixed 10-minute interval bins, which we use to join our readings. The strategy for choosing the bin is to use a simple rounding method. Since multiple readings from the same device tend to fall into a single bin, the cumulative energy production within that bin is summed, producing a single total energy production value per bin per device.

Different granularities between sources

Because Solis data was normalised to 10‑minute intervals, the other source (FusionSolar) needed to be aligned accordingly. Rounding was unnecessary for FusionSolar due to its inherent consistency and vendor‑side cleaning, so only aggregation to 10‑minute intervals was required (again, applied per device).

As a result of normalising the data into 10-minute intervals, the data points experience natural shifts. This solution also prevents collisions between sources, as the source identifier is also included in the hash key calculation.

For hashing functions, MD5 and SHA‑1 were considered. The criteria included collision resistance, storage efficiency, and performance. For the devices and stations tables, MD5 was selected due to the relatively small number of entries. Considering the current number of devices (66) and projecting to 10 times that (660), the probability of a collision using MD5 is on the order of 1 in 100 trillion, more than sufficient for these tables. The performance readings table currently contains around 600,000 rows; the probability of a collision with MD5 is about 1 in 100 million. This is still very low, but given years of daily ingestion and potential growth in devices and manufacturers, the probability could range from 1 in 10 million to 1 in 1 million. While still small, SHA‑1 may be preferable here to further reduce collision risk at the cost of additional storage. Keys are stored in hexadecimal representation: 32 characters for MD5 and 40 for SHA‑1.

Dimensional models

To support efficient querying and prepare the dataset for analytics and ML use cases, the cleaned, aggregated tables from the gold layer are joined and consolidated into the final fact and dimension structures described in Dataset model. At this stage, production and weather data are fused at a common hourly granularity, and aggregations are performed both at inverter and station levels. These denormalised representations integrate key identifiers, contextual weather variables, and location attributes to provide efficient, analysis-ready datasets.

For this dataset, hourly alignment was chosen for matching weather data to production data for several reasons. First, OpenMeteo’s historical weather data are available at an hourly level, making this the most logical temporal granularity for alignment. Second, although inverter data is available at finer (5- or 10minute) intervals, aggregating them to hourly intervals suppresses shortterm variability and noise that could otherwise degrade model quality. The result is a more stable, betteraligned dataset.

The existing hourly aggregates serve as the basis for joining with weather data: energy parameters, such as average active power or total energy produced per hour, are aligned with the relevant weather indicators (temperature, irradiance, cloud cover, etc.). This supports correlation analyses and the construction of models that explicitly account for the influence of weather conditions on PV system performance.

To ensure accurate data fusion, corrections were necessary regarding the hourly aggregation strategies. Specifically, the hourly aggregate columns in the weather data (see Table 7) utilise a preceding-hour aggregation approach, whereas the PV data is aggregated using an hourly floor convention, where a timestamp t represents the interval from t:00 to t:59. To address this discrepancy, the preceding-hour aggregate columns in the weather data were shifted backwards by one hour for each station identifier before merging. In detail, for each station, the value recorded at time t was replaced with the value from the subsequent hourly record (i.e., the observation originally labelled at t plus one hour). This station-specific adjustment aligns each weather aggregate with the most relevant PV interval and prevents a systematic one-hour mismatch between environmental conditions and measured production.

Depending on the distance between the plant and the weather station, certain local phenomena can be missed, including cloud coverage, which may negatively impact downstream modelling and training.
