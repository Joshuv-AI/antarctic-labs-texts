Exploring the mechanical and thermal properties of sustainable epoxy composites reinforced with Ethiopian false banana fiber
Authors: Hari Prasadarao Pydi, Guye Bogale Benta, Srikanth Karumuri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This work evaluates the mechanical and thermal properties of epoxy-based nanocomposites reinforced with synthesized banana fibers sourced from West Guji Zone in Ethiopia. In particular, the availability and sustainable nature of false banana fiber make it an alternative option for synthetic fibers in composite manufacturing. Epoxy composites were fabricated from three epoxy resin compositions (i.e., 260 g, 325 g, and 390 g) with three different orientations (0°, 45°, and 90°) of banana fibers. The composite specimens were fabricated by compression and manual molding. The fracture surface morphologies and interface bonding strength between the fiber and epoxy matrix will be analyzed by SEM. From the findings, it is evident that the composite material with fibres oriented at 45° with an epoxy resin of 390 g has excellent mechanical properties. The composite exhibited tensile strength of 160 MPa, compressive strength of 50 MPa, flexural strength of 625 MPa, and impact energy absorption of 8.5 J. The SEM micrograph showed that there is an excellent interaction between the fibre and the matrix, with minimal voids and bonded surface, which indicates an efficient transfer of stresses from fibre to matrix. From the TGA plot, the weight loss of 25.15% between 655 °C and 703 °C shows that the composite is thermally stable. These results have demonstrated that False banana fiber reinforced epoxy composites can be considered viable and environmental friendly substitutes for synthetic fiber composites through the demonstration of their mechanical and thermal properties and also provides another contribution towards the development of green material technologies by utilizing the potential of agricultural waste fibers.

Introduction

Composite materials are commonly employed in various industries due to their impressive mechanical and physical properties. Fibres are incorporated into epoxy resins for composite material systems to increase their toughness, rigidity, and durability. There are two main components in any composite material system, namely, the reinforcement component and the matrix component1,2. These fibers do have certain disadvantages, though, such as reduced stiffness, significant moisture absorption, elevated flammability, and limited tensile strength3,4. Current studies concerning environmentally friendly and sustainable composite materials are related to the creation of natural fiber-reinforced polymers that can be used as replacements for synthetic composite materials. The manufacturing process of readily available environmentally friendly fibers, such as the use of alkali treatment, has been studied to improve the mechanical properties, adhesion between the matrix and fibers, and environmental friendliness of the composites5. The optimisation of stacking sequences in agave and jute fibre-based composites has also shown promising mechanical properties in terms of tensile, flexural, impact, and hardness values. This has demonstrated the potential of agave and jute fibre-based composites in terms of their usage in lightweight structures. The mechanical properties of jute and acacia fibre-based composites have also been demonstrated by researchers6,7. The studies on the individual properties of the natural fibres, like abaca fibres, highlight the mechanical, physical, and thermal properties of the fibres, focusing on the use of the fibres for the fabrication of structural polymer composite materials, as well as the direction for the selection of the materials for the fabrication of the composite materials in the future8. In addition, the utilization of leftover fibres from animals to create composites is evident in research on the development of chicken feather fibre composites. In general, research in this field aims to develop sustainable and efficient composites made from renewable animal and plant fibres while solving problems such as moisture sensitivity and variation in fibre properties9,10.

In recent years, banana fiber composites have attracted a lot of interest because of its advantageous mechanical qualities, affordability, and environmental friendliness. These eco-friendly materials offer a competitive substitute for conventional synthetic composites, which makes them appropriate for a range of uses in many sectors11. By using different fiber treatment and processing techniques, banana fiber-reinforced composites can significantly improve their mechanical performance. Alkali treatment is the most successful of these since it increases surface roughness and eliminates impurities, which strengthens and prolongs the fiber-matrix interfacial bonding and increases the composite’s strength. For example, as compared to clean epoxy, banana fiber-reinforced polyester composites with up to 15% fiber content have demonstrated significant gains in tensile, flexural, and impact strength. In a similar vein, soy-resole composites reinforced with banana peduncle fibers demonstrated the potential of banana fibers to improve mechanical performance in bio-based composite systems by achieving a maximum tensile strength of 43.5 MPa and a flexural strength of 36.2 MPa. In a different investigation, it was demonstrated that adding fly ash as a filler to polyester composites reinforced with banana fibers improved their mechanical qualities while simultaneously decreasing their ability to absorb moisture. Banana fiber composites’ mechanical strength and water resistance are further enhanced by the use of compatibilizers like maleic anhydride-grafted LDPE, which makes them more appropriate for structural and environmental applications12–16. Fiber orientation is another important aspect affecting the mechanical performance of fiber-reinforced composites. Strength and stiffness properties are greatly impacted by variations in fiber orientation. For instance, when compared to other fiber orientations, banana/jute hybrid composites with a (0°/0°) orientation demonstrated up to 37% greater tensile strength, Young’s modulus, impact strength, interlaminar shear strength, and fracture toughness. Likewise, flax and glass fiber hybrid composites aligned at 0° outperformed those with a 90° orientation in terms of mechanical parameters, achieving a maximum tensile strength of 82.71 MPa, flexural strength of 143.99 MPa, and impact strength of 4 kJ/m²17,18. Unidirectional (0/0/0/0) and cross-ply (0/+45°/−45°) arrangements of jute fibers have been shown to increase the flexural strength of jute fiber-reinforced composites. By encouraging effective stress distribution along and across the fiber directions, these alignment patterns greatly increase the material’s resistance to bending loads19. To improve their performance, banana fiber composites have been hybridized with nano additives including nano clay and nano silicon in a number of research. The main cause of this improvement is the hydrogen bonds that are formed between the nanoclay and the fiber–matrix interface. These bonds improve interfacial adhesion and allow for more effective stress transfer throughout the composite20–22. Eggshell powder and banana fiber, which are made from post-consumer waste and agricultural wastes, have been used as environmentally acceptable fillers to strengthen concrete. Furthermore, waste materials like fish and chicken bones (femur and beak) have been used as inexpensive bio-adsorbents to remove Cd2 + ions from aquatic settings, showing promise for use in environmental remediation and sustainable building23,24.

Bhoopathi et al. combined synthetic and plant-based reinforcements. Important characteristics like impact resistance, flexural strength, and tensile strength were evaluated. synthetic fiber-reinforced composites, hemp and glass fiber-reinforced banana fiber epoxy composites have competitive mechanical qualities, according to the findings25.

Throat et al. specifically investigated the biocomposites based on banana fibers’ tensile and flexural properties. Their results proved the possibilities that banana fibers had in improving the properties of bio-composites in terms of mechanical behavior by significantly boosting their tensile and flexural strengths after being incorporated into an epoxy matrix26,27. According to Kannan et al. study, the highest mechanical performance was observed at a tensile strength of 19.91 MPa and flexural strength of 92.76 MPa with a fibre volume fraction of 30 vol%. The epoxy composites containing banana fibers have recorded a remarkable improvement of up to 225% in the flexural strength than the pure epoxy composites28,29. A hybrid epoxy-based composite with alternating layers of glass and banana fibers, improved with 1 weight% charcoal as a filler, was created by Arpitha et al.30. The tensile strength of 82.71 MPa, the flexural strength of 143.99 MPa, and the impact strength of 4 kJ/m² were all achieved by a bamboo/glass fiber hybrid composite with a 0° fiber orientation, according to Ramesh et al. The findings demonstrate the stiffness of bamboo and its effectiveness as a strong natural fiber. Ahmad et al. reported that the tensile strength and flexural strength of epoxy composites strengthened using bamboo fibers were 137 MPa and 170 MPa respectively. On the other hand, they also noted that the high lignin content of bamboo would make it difficult for resins to adhere effectively to it, creating poor interfacing between the fiber and matrix31,32.

The novelty of this study lies in the use of false banana fiber from the West Guji Zone in Ethiopia, which has not been widely explored for epoxy composites. This research presents a new way to use this locally available and biodegradable material as a sustainable alternative to synthetic fibres. The combined effects of various fiber orientations and epoxy resin concentrations in artificial banana fiber-reinforced composites have not yet been examined. In this study, the false banana fibers from Ethiopia’s West Guji Zone and creating composites with three different epoxy resin concentrations (260, 325, and 390 g) and three different fiber orientations (0°, 45°, and 90°) utilizing compression molding and hand lay-up. The study provides new insights into optimizing natural fiber composites for sustainable applications by examining fiber–matrix interfacial bonding and fracture morphology by SEM following NaOH treatment, as well as thermal behavior using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA).

Composite preparation

Using a consistent weight of 260 g of fake banana fibers, banana fiber mats were first cut to 300 × 300 mm². Three distinct epoxy resin concentrations and fiber orientations were then added, as shown in Table 2. For matrix preparation, a hydrodynamic shear mixer was used to completely mix the epoxy resin and epoxy–silica particle mixes at 500–600 RPM for 10 min at room temperature in order to obtain uniform dispersion. The mixed solution was degassed in a pressure furnace to release trapped air. The resin mixture was degassed and then the proper amount of hardener was applied. The banana fiber mats were prepared in a flat mold beforehand, and the resin-hardener mixture was poured evenly and manually using the hand lay-up method. To guarantee correct contact and resin penetration, the mold was then closed and compacted with light hand pressure. To finish the polymerization process, the composites were allowed to cure for 24 h at room temperature34,35.

In particular, three specimens with a medium epoxy concentration of 330 g included fibers aligned at 0°, while three specimens with a lower epoxy concentration of 280 g were constructed with fibers orientated at 90°. Three specimens with the highest epoxy concentration (390 g) and the fiber orientation (45°) made up the final set. Conversely, reduced resin concentrations lead to decreased mechanical integrity and interfacial voids. With a 45° bidirectional fiber orientation and a 390 g epoxy concentration, the experimental results of this study clearly show a significant improvement in the tensile strength of fake banana fiber-reinforced epoxy composites, reaching a maximum value of 160 MPa. Compression test results

The compressive strength for samples S1, S2, and S3 were 50 MPa, 26 MPa, and 18 MPa, respectively, and the corresponding stresses were 0.05, 0.03, and 0.035. The large amount of epoxy (390 g), which increases the stiffness of the resin matrix and encourages bonding between fibres and matrix, and the fibre orientation in two directions at 45° to each other. The fibre orientation is important because it allows fibres to withstand tensile and compressive stresses in many directions. there is an increase in strength due to the high epoxy content. Sample S1 recorded the best compressive strength, as depicted in Fig. 11. This is attributed to the high affinity that exists between the fibres and the matrix. The ability to transfer stress from the matrix to the fibres was made possible through bonding that occurs in Sample S1. Moreover, through the elimination of fiber skewing that often occurs during compression and leads to failure, optimal positioning of fibres at 45° is likely to improve compressive strength. By better positioning the fibres to withstand deformation under load, this configuration reduces strain and increases longevity and resistance to compressive forces. The resistance of the material to failure under compressive stress is further improved through better epoxy matrixing that surrounds the fibers, which has successfully reduced matrix cracking.

The compressive strengths of composites with 330 g epoxy and unidirectional (0°) fiber orientation were measured at 46 MPa, 38 MPa, and 20 MPa, as shown in Fig. 12. These values reflect the relationship between epoxy content and fiber alignment, where the 0° orientation provides unidirectional loading but may limit the material’s ability to resist stresses applied from other directions. For composites with the lowest epoxy concentration (280 g) and a 90° fiber orientation, the corresponding compressive strengths were 42 MPa, 35 MPa, and 20 MPa (Fig. 13). The reduction in compressive strength observed with lower epoxy content can be attributed to the decreased resin bonding capacity, resulting in weaker fiber-matrix interaction and reduced stress transfer. When comparing the three different epoxy concentrations, the highest compressive strength of 50 MPa was found in the 390 g epoxy and 45° fiber orientation combination (Fig. 14). This combination demonstrated superior performance due to the synergistic effects of increased resin content and the 45° bidirectional fiber orientation. The 45° orientation provides an optimal arrangement for resisting both tensile and compressive forces from multiple directions, promoting a more uniform distribution of stresses across the composite.

Flexural test

Figure 15 shows that with increasing epoxy concentrations, the flexural strength values were 625 MPa at the highest and 360.6 MPa at the minimum. Samples S1 and S2 both demonstrated a flexural strength of 234.4 MPa in the case of medium epoxy concentration with unidirectional (0°) fiber orientation, whereas the lowest value noted was 156.3 MPa. The composites with a perpendicular (90°) fiber orientation and a lower resin content had a maximum flexural strength of 615 MPa for sample S2 and a minimum of 390.6 MPa for sample S1. Because of better fiber–matrix adhesion, better resin flow, and ideal load distribution, the overall maximum flexural strength value is achieved at higher epoxy resin concentrations (390 g) and 45° fiber orientation. Effective stress transmission under bending requires enhanced fiber wetting, reduced voids, and improved interfacial bonding, all of which are ensured by higher epoxy content.

Impact test results

As the concentration of epoxy resin increased, the Charpy impact energies for samples S1, S2, and S3 of the false banana fiber (FBF) composite were 4.5 J, 8.5 J, and 7.5 J, respectively, as shown in Figs. 16 and 17. With a lower angle of attack (< 120°) and the highest impact energy (8.5 J), Sample S2 demonstrated improved elasticity and energy absorption as a result of better fiber–matrix bonding at higher epoxy levels. These findings demonstrate that FBF composites’ toughness and impact resistance are enhanced by a larger epoxy concentration.

TGA result

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed to check the thermal stability of the false banana fibre (FBF)-reinforced epoxy composite for different concentrations of epoxy resin,. The composite was prepared using different concentrations of epoxy resin (280 g for lower concentration, 325 g for medium concentration, and 390 g for higher concentration), and the ratio was maintained at 260 g of FBF. For the lower concentration composite, FBF was kept at 90° transverse, and for the medium and higher concentration composites, FBF was kept at 45° bidirectional. The two-stage decomposition process is supported by the TGA curve of the composite material of lower concentration. The weight loss of 18.31% in the first stage, between 100 °C and 220 °C, is due to the decomposition of volatile matter including water. The decomposition of cellulose in the fibres and organic structure of the resin is due to the large weight loss of 39.66% in the second stage, between 220 °C and 572 °C. The final decomposition process occurs between 572 °C and 799 °C, resulting in the formation of ash accompanied by a weight loss of 25.15%. This degradation profile suggests that the composite exhibits a good level of thermal stability, even with a reduced resin concentration. The absence of sharp changes in mass loss indicates that the fiber-matrix bonding and the presence of voids play a role in influencing the overall thermal performance8.

From the TGA curve, it is observed that there is a three-stage breakdown process for the composite with high epoxy concentration (Fig. 19). The breakdown of chemicals and moisture resulted in an 18% weight loss for the composite during the first stage, which ranged from 100 °C to 185 °C. The breakdown of cellulose and organic materials presents in the fiber and resin resulted in a major weight loss of 40.77% during the second stage of the process. This second stage ranged from 185 °C to 655 °C. As the composite is converted to ash in the final stage of the process, which ranges from 655 °C to 703 °C, there is a weight loss of 25.15%. Comparing this result with that of the other composites containing less and medium concentrations of resin, it is observed that there is a slower rate of degradation for this composite. The increased bond between the fibers and the matrix, as well as the better encapsulation of the fibers by the resin, which results in the delayed breakdown of the composite, is the reason for the increased thermal stability of the composite at higher epoxy resin concentrations. The composite can withstand high temperatures even at a constant volume of artificial banana fibers by adding more epoxy resin.

The degradation of the main components in the fake banana fibres may be related to the thermal degradation observed in the TGA curves. The breakdown of hemicellulose, which is one of the components in the fibres and degrades at low temperatures, is responsible for the initial weight loss in the range 100–220 °C. The breakdown of cellulose, which is the main component in the fibres and responsible for this weight loss, is responsible for the large weight loss observed in the range 220–572 °C. The breakdown of lignin, which degrades at high temperatures, is related to the last step in the degradation process, which occurs at temperatures greater than 572 °C. the encasing of the fibres by the resin matrix, particularly for the composite with higher concentrations of epoxy, aids in the shielding of the fibres from heat deterioration, thus delaying the decomposition of the cellulose and lignin. The temperature range for the decomposition of the volatile chemicals was found to range between 100 °C and 185 °C, corresponding to the onset temperature (Tonset) for each composite. The greatest rate of deterioration was recorded within the range of 220 °C to 572 °C, corresponding to the peak temperature (Tmax) for the primary decomposition phase. The higher char yield, or the residue remaining after complete thermal degradation, for the composite with higher concentrations of epoxy was indicative of better thermal stability. The increased char yield and delayed degradation in the higher resin composite suggest improved resistance to thermal degradation, which is crucial for sustainable applications that require high-temperature performance.

SEM analysis

The fiber-matrix interfacial behavior and fracture microstructural characteristics of the false banana fiber-reinforced epoxy composites made with different epoxy resin concentrations and fiber orientations were examined using SEM analysis. SEM picture of the composite sample made with a high epoxy concentration (390 g) and a 45° fiber orientation (Sample 1) is shown in Fig. 20. Strong interfacial bonding and fibers that are firmly entrenched in the matrix are seen in the image, suggesting efficient wetting and stress transfer44. The scanning electron microscope images in Fig. 20 indicate that there is uniform distribution of fibres in the epoxy matrix. Uniform distribution of fibres in the epoxy matrix is critical because it ensures effective transfer of applied stress between the matrix and fibres, thus maximising the mechanical properties of the composite material. Higher tensile, compressive, and flexural strengths of the composite material arise as a result of uniform distribution of fibres in the epoxy matrix. Uniform distribution of fibres in the epoxy matrix maximises the load-bearing capacity of the composite material. However, In some sections of this matrix, it is evident that cavities or spaces were created. The entrapment of air during this hand lay-up process and the subsequent curing stage is likely to be responsible for this. The failure of the resin to penetrate all the fibres during this lay-up process may result in entrapment. This is likely to be a common occurrence in composite material production. The entrapment of air has the potential to reduce the density of this composite material. These spaces may act as points of concentrated stress, thus leading to failure.

The SEM image of a second composite sample (Sample 2) with the same resin concentration (390 g) and 45° orientation is displayed in Fig. 21. Long banana fibers are clearly apparent, but the picture also shows a number of spaces, which could indicate irregularities in the flow and impregnation of the resin during manufacturing.

Fibre pull-out and air entrapment during the curing process are likely to be the reasons for the observed cavities and micro-gaps in Fig. 22. When fibres pull out from the composite material due to insufficient binding, this is known as fibre pull-out. This can be caused by failure to treat fibres adequately, failure to cure the resin adequately, and failure to ensure that fibres are adequately coated with resin. Since fibres are the major contributors to reinforcement in composite materials, fibres pulling out weaken the composite. This weakens the composite and makes it possible for the composite to collapse since there is a high chance of cracks forming. Additionally, Fig. 23 shows tiny gaps at the fiber–matrix contact, which suggests poorer bonding effectiveness and less than ideal adhesion.

Conclusion

The mechanical and thermal properties of epoxy composite reinforced with synthetic banana fibers obtained from West Guji Zone in Ethiopia have been effectively demonstrated in this study. The composite containing 390 g epoxy resin and 45° fiber orientation showed better results compared to all other composite configurations, as per the results. The composite showed outstanding mechanical properties such as 160 MPa tensile strength, 50 MPa compressive strength, 625 MPa flexural strength, and 8.5 J impact energy absorption. The best fiber-matrix interfacial bonding, complete wetting of the matrix, and efficient transfer of stress due to 45° fiber orientation have been responsible for this improved composite performance.

The thermal stability of this composite is further confirmed through TGA analysis, where there is an appreciable weight loss of 25.15% within the temperature range of 655 °C to 703 °C. The overall mechanical integrity of this composite has been further improved through SEM analysis, where there is appreciable interfacial bonding and minimal voids present within the composite.Thus, the significance of this study is further highlighted through its emphasis on the necessity to optimize the resin composition and fiber orientation to achieve optimal properties for different applications.

However, the study has some limitations. Despite the composites showing excellent mechanical and thermal properties, the variability in results—especially with lower resin concentrations and different fiber orientations—indicates the need for further optimization to ensure consistency in large-scale production. The future study will be focused on enhancing the manufacturing process to reduce these irregularities. In addition, the study will be focused on different treatments of fibers to enhance bonding between the fibers and the matrix, thus improving the performance of the composites.Overall, this study enhances the use of agro-waste fibers in composites.
