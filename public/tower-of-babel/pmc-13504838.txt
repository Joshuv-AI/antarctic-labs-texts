LARS2 reprograms mitochondrial metabolism and epigenetically upregulates MHC-I to boost antitumor immunity in nasopharyngeal carcinoma
Authors: Mei Yang, Yongzhen Mo, Daixi Ren, Shun Liu, Qijia Yan, Lei Shi, Pan Chen, Bo Xiang, Rongfang He, Songqing Fan, Ming Zhou, Ming Tan, Guiyuan Li, Xuyu Zu, Zhaoyang Zeng, Wei Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is considered a typical “hot” tumor, yet its immune evasion mechanisms remain poorly defined, and immunotherapy efficacy is suboptimal. Attenuation of intrinsic tumor cell immunogenicity to evade T cell recognition and cytotoxicity represents a major route of tumor immune evasion, whose mechanistic basis is largely unelucidated.MethodsMultiplex immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry were used to assess correlations between mitochondrial leucyl-transfer RNA synthetase 2 (LARS2) expression, CD8+ T cell infiltration and immunotherapy response in NPC tissues. In vitro CD8+ T cell co-cultures, immunodeficient nude mice and immunocompetent murine models were used to explore LARS2-dependent CD8+-mediated antitumor immunity. Blue native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (BN-PAGE), extracellular flux and other mitochondrial functional assays were performed to characterize the roles of LARS2 and leucine in electron transport chain (ETC) translation and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) activity. Chromatin immunoprecipitation-quantitative PCR, ELISA and flow cytometry analyzed epigenetic modifications, major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) expression and antigen presentation. Leucine supplementation combined with anti-programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) therapy was tested in mouse models to evaluate in vivo therapeutic efficacy against NPC immune escape.ResultsWe demonstrated that LARS2, a gene mapped to the 3p21 chromosomal region, was significantly downregulated in NPC, and its expression is positively correlated with patient prognosis and response to immunotherapy. Through in vitro and in vivo experiments, we demonstrated that restoration of LARS2 expression accelerates the translation of mitochondrial ETC subunits, enhances OXPHOS, and upregulates MHC-I expression via an epigenetic mechanism. These effects work together to enhance tumor antigen presentation and augment CD8+ T cell-mediated cytotoxicity. Furthermore, we demonstrated that a high-leucine diet increases LARS2 expression and improves the effectiveness of anti-PD-1 immunotherapy.ConclusionsThis study uncovers a novel mechanism by which the tumor suppressor LARS2 inhibits immune evasion in NPC through upregulation of MHC-I, and highlights a high-leucine diet as a promising strategy to sensitize tumors to immunotherapy.

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is a prevalent head and neck malignancy endemic to Southeast Asia and southern China.1 Radiotherapy is the cornerstone of its clinical treatment, with the 5-year survival rate exceeding 90% for patients diagnosed at early stages.2 3 However, due to the tumor’s occult location and subtle early symptoms, more than 75% of patients present with metastatic disease at the time of diagnosis. Over the past two decades, treatment strategies, including neoadjuvant chemotherapy and concurrent chemoradiotherapy, have substantially improved patient outcomes.4 Nevertheless, conventional therapeutic approaches have plateaued, leaving limited room for further improvements in efficacy. NPC is classified as a typical “hot tumor” due to extensive lymphocyte infiltration within the tumor tissue, and the tumor immune microenvironment plays a pivotal role in its pathogenesis and progression.5 Multiple clinical trials6 7 have demonstrated that immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) yields remarkable therapeutic effects in recurrent, metastatic, and refractory NPC after first-line treatment failure, offering a vital treatment option for these patients. Despite these advances, ICB therapy is hampered by a suboptimal overall response rate and frequent drug resistance, and only a small subset of patients achieves durable long-term survival benefits. Accordingly, deciphering novel mechanisms underlying immune evasion and immunotherapy resistance and identifying actionable targets are critical to improving immunotherapy efficacy for NPC.

The excessive activation of oncogenes or the inactivation of tumor suppressor genes drives tumor initiation and progression.8 9 The short arm of human chromosome 3, particularly the 3p21 region, shows a high frequency of loss of heterozygosity across multiple malignancies, suggesting the presence of critical tumor suppressor genes within this locus.10–12 Since the 1990s, our group and others have identified several tumor suppressor genes in the 3p21 region. These genes are markedly downregulated in malignant tumors, and re-expressing them in tumor cells greatly reduces malignant behaviors, such as proliferation, invasion, and migration.13–16 However, although mitochondrial leucyl-transfer RNA synthetase 2 (LARS2)—located at chromosome 3p21.3—is also significantly downregulated in NPC17 and correlated with poor prognosis in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), restoring LARS2 expression in NPC cells did not initially result in any obvious changes in malignant phenotypes in vitro. This phenomenon—where clinical significance is evident but in vitro function is negligible—prompts us to consider that the tumor microenvironment might play a critical role in its regulatory mechanisms.

Eukaryotic mitochondria contain an independent genome (~16.6 kb of circular double-stranded DNA, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)) along with the machinery for gene transcription and protein translation. However, this genome encodes only 13 proteins in the electron transport chain (ETC) complex.18 The remaining proteins required for mitochondrial transcription and translation are encoded by the nuclear genome, translated in the cytoplasm, and then imported into mitochondria to carry out their functions.19 LARS2, located on the short arm of chromosome 3 in the nucleus, is transcribed and then translated in the cytoplasm, and it primarily localizes to mitochondria.20 Within mitochondria, LARS2 catalyzes the formation of leucyl-tRNA from leucine (Leu) and its corresponding transfer RNA (tRNALeu), providing essential substrates for the translation of mitochondria-encoded ETC core subunits, and thereby regulating oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and mitochondrial homeostasis.21 Previous studies have primarily linked LARS2 to hereditary disorders, such as Perrault syndrome,22 neurodegenerative diseases including Parkinson’s disease23 and Alzheimer’s disease,24 as well as metabolic disorders like type 2 diabetes.25 Its roles in tumor initiation and progression remain poorly characterized and are controversial.

Recent studies have revealed that LARS2-high B cells contribute to immune evasion through mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming in colorectal cancer.26 Nevertheless, the overall functions of LARS2 in tumors remain largely uncharacterized. In head and neck cancers, particularly NPC, critical questions about its functional mechanisms and its role in immune evasion and immunotherapy resistance remain unaddressed. Here, we demonstrate that loss of LARS2 expression impairs the antigen-presenting capacity of NPC cells, rendering tumor cells resistant to CD8+ cytotoxic T cell (CTL)-mediated killing and immunotherapy. Restoring LARS2 expression reverses this immune evasion phenotype by recovering tumor antigen presentation, thereby enabling CTL-mediated tumor suppression. A high-leucine diet also upregulates LARS2 expression, enhances tumor antigen presentation through mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming, and improves the efficacy of anti-programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) immunotherapy. Collectively, this study uncovers a novel mechanism by which LARS2 regulates immune evasion in NPC and proposes a leucine-based strategy to sensitize tumors to immunotherapy. Our findings provide new molecular targets and mechanistic insights to advance the development of immunotherapies for NPC.

Results

LARS2 is markedly downregulated in NPC and inversely correlated with tumor immune evasion

Analysis of multiple genome-wide expression profiling microarray datasets revealed that LARS2 is significantly downregulated in HNSCC, including NPC27 (figure 1A,B). Moreover, patients with low LARS2 expression exhibited poorer prognosis (figure 1C), supporting its potential role as a tumor suppressor. To investigate its function, we generated an LARS2 overexpression plasmid and designed a small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting LARS2, achieving efficient overexpression or knockdown in NPC cells (online supplemental figure S1A and B). Interestingly, LARS2 did not directly affect tumor cell proliferation (online supplemental figure S1C) or apoptosis (online supplemental figure S1D), suggesting it does not function as a classical tumor suppressor. Further analysis of immune cell infiltration using Cell-type Identification By Estimating Relative Subsets Of RNA Transcripts (CIBERSORT) and Tumor IMmune Estimation Resource (TIMER) 2.028 on The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset revealed a significant positive correlation between LARS2 expression and CD8+ T cell infiltration in the tumor microenvironment (online supplemental figure S1E and F).

We collected paraffin-embedded tissue specimens from 39 treatment-naïve patients with NPC who received PD-1/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) monoclonal antibody therapy. Multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) staining was performed on 10 of these samples. The results revealed that NPC tissues with high LARS2 expression exhibited increased CD8+ T cell infiltration and elevated interferon (IFN)-γ levels, whereas no significant difference in PD-L1 expression was observed (figure 1D). We further used immunohistochemistry (IHC) to assess the correlation between LARS2 expression and immunotherapy response. Patients who responded to immunotherapy had higher LARS2 and PD-L1 expression and greater CD8+ T cell infiltration (figure 1E). LARS2 expression was significantly positively correlated with CD8+ T cell density (figure 1F), whereas no significant correlation was found between LARS2 levels and PD-L1 Combined Positive Score (CPS) (online supplemental figure S1G). Moreover, patients with high LARS2 expression achieved a higher immunotherapy response rate (75% vs 36.84%) than those with low LARS2 expression (figure 1G). Survival analysis demonstrated that patients with high LARS2 expression had longer progression-free survival (PFS) (figure 1H). Univariate Cox regression confirmed that LARS2 expression was significantly associated with PFS among patients with NPC receiving immunotherapy (online supplemental figure S1H). Given the limited sample size (n=39) and only 13 survival endpoint events, multivariate Cox regression was not performed due to statistical constraints. We further evaluated the distribution of potential confounders, including PD-L1 expression, Epstein-Barr virus copy number, and clinical stage, between the LARS2-high and LARS2-low groups (online supplemental table 2). Finally, we assessed the loss status at the LARS2 locus in these NPC specimens. Heterozygous loss of LARS2 was observed in approximately 35.71% of samples, confirming that deletion of the chromosomal segment harboring LARS2 is a major cause of its downregulation (online supplemental figure S1I).

LARS2 suppresses tumor growth in a CD8+ T cell-dependent manner

To explore whether LARS2 exerts its tumor-suppressive function in a CD8+ T cell-dependent manner. We first determined the major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) genotypes of NPC cell lines HK1 and 5–8F (HLA-A*11:02 and HLA-A*11:01, respectively) and recruited healthy volunteers whose MHC-I genotypes matched those of these cell lines. Peripheral blood was collected from the volunteers, and CD3+ T cells and dendritic cells (DCs) were isolated. DCs were pulsed with HK1 or 5–8F cell lysates to complete antigen presentation and activate T cells. Subsequently, LARS2 was overexpressed or knocked down in HK1 and 5–8F cells, respectively, and these tumor cells were co-cultured with the activated T cells for 12 hours to assess tumor cell survival and CD8+ T cell-mediated cytotoxicity (online supplemental figure S2A). Unlike NPC cells that were merely overexpressed or knocked down LARS2 (online supplemental figure S1C and D), co-culture with LARS2-overexpressing NPC cells resulted in decreased tumor cell survival and significantly increased apoptosis, whereas LARS2 knockdown enhanced tumor cell survival and reduced apoptosis (figure 2A,B, online supplemental figure S2B and C). Flow cytometry analysis of T cells from the co-culture revealed that the LARS2-overexpressing group exhibited higher proportions of IFN-γ+/tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α+ and IFN-γ+/granzyme B (GZMB)+ CD8+ T cells, while LARS2 knockdown reduced the fraction of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells (figure 2C,D, online supplemental figure S2D). Consistent results were obtained from quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) and ELISA analyses of cytotoxic cytokine expression and secretion by CD8+ T cells (online supplemental figure S2E and F).

We further validated the co-culture findings in vivo by subcutaneously implanting HK1 cells with LARS2 overexpression or knockdown into nude mice to establish xenografts. One group received tail vein injections of saline, while another received T cells that had been antigen-presented and activated in vitro (online supplemental figure S2A). In the saline-injected group, LARS2 manipulation had no significant effect on tumor growth or size. In contrast, following T cell transfer, LARS2-overexpressing NPC cells exhibited markedly suppressed tumor growth, whereas LARS2-knockdown tumors grew faster and were larger (figure 2E,F). Tumor tissues were harvested at the end of the experiment. IHC staining revealed increased CD8+ T cell infiltration in the LARS2-overexpressing group (figure 2G). Single-cell suspensions analyzed by flow cytometry showed higher proportions of IFN-γ+/TNF-α+ and IFN-γ+/GZMB+ CD8+ T cells in LARS2-overexpressing tumors, with the opposite trend in LARS2-knockdown tumors (figure 2H, online supplemental figure S3A and B). ELISA analysis of xenograft tissues confirmed increased levels of cytotoxic cytokines IFN-γ, TNF-α, and GZMB in the LARS2-overexpressing group (online supplemental figure S3C). To further determine whether CD8+ T cells act as the key effector cells, we inoculated the murine head and neck squamous cell carcinoma cell line mouse oral cancer 1 (MOC1) into immunocompetent C57BL/6J mice to establish a syngeneic orthotopic tumor model. Lars2 overexpression markedly suppressed tumor growth. Notably, depletion of CD8+ T cells using anti-CD8 neutralizing antibodies abolished the tumor-suppressive effect of Lars2 in these mice (figure 2I, online supplemental figure S3D). Collectively, these results demonstrate that LARS2 suppresses NPC tumor growth in a CD8+ T cell-dependent manner, suggesting that its tumor-suppressive role is due to enhanced antitumor immunity.

LARS2 promotes tumor antigen presentation by increasing MHC-I expression

To investigate the tumor-suppressive mechanism of LARS2, we analyzed the GSE102349 dataset,29 which contains whole-genome expression microarray data from 113 NPC clinical samples. Samples were ranked by LARS2 expression, and the top and bottom 15% were subjected to Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA). In addition to pathways related to classical mitochondrial aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase functions, such as aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, OXPHOS, and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, the antigen processing and presentation (APP) pathway was also activated in LARS2-high samples, which mediates the presentation of tumor antigen peptides and activating DCs and CD8+ T cells to elicit strong antitumor protection30 (online supplemental figure S4A). Analysis of the TCGA database, along with our clinical cohort, further revealed that LARS2 expression is positively correlated with MHC-I molecules, including human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-A, HLA-B, and HLA-C (online supplemental figure S4B and C). Since CD8+ T cell recognition and killing of tumor cells depend on MHC-I-mediated antigen presentation,31 and immune evasion often involves MHC-I downregulation,30 we examined whether LARS2 regulates antigen presentation. The overexpression of LARS2 in NPC cells markedly augmented HLA-ABC expression at both the messenger RNA (mRNA) level (online supplemental figure S4D) and the protein level (figure 3A). More prominently, it increased the surface abundance of HLA-ABC (figure 3B). Conversely, knockdown of LARS2 resulted in the opposite effect. Similarly, LARS2 modulated the expression of most components of the MHC-I antigen processing and presentation machinery (APM) at the mRNA level (online supplemental figure S4E). These results suggest that LARS2 promotes tumor antigen presentation by upregulating MHC-I and other APM components, thereby facilitating CD8+ T cell-mediated tumor suppression. To test this, we simultaneously overexpressed LARS2 and knocked down β2-microglobulin (B2M), a crucial component of MHC-I, in HK1 cells. Subcutaneous tumor formation and adoptive T cell transfer experiments in nude mice (online supplemental figure S2A) showed that B2M knockdown reversed the antitumor immune effects induced by LARS2 overexpression (figure 3C,D).

To determine whether MHC-I upregulation reflects enhanced antigen presentation in tumor cells, we generated a stable MOC1 cell line expressing OVA+GFP+Luc+ using a lentiviral vector (online supplemental figure S5A and B) and overexpressed mouse Lars2 in these MOC1-OVA cells (online supplemental figure S5C). Lars2 overexpression led to increased expression of the mouse MHC-I molecule H2-K1 (online supplemental figure S5D) and enhanced surface presentation of the H-2Kb-bound OVA-derived peptide SIINFEKL (figure 3E). DCs activation is a critical step in antigen presentation and the initiation of adaptive immune responses.32 33 We co-cultured bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) from C57BL/6-Tg (TcraTcrb) 1100Mjb/J (OT-I) mice with Lars2-overexpressing MOC1-OVA cells, using IFN-γ stimulation as a positive control (online supplemental figure S5E). Compared with empty plasmid controls, Lars2-overexpressing MOC1-OVA cells more effectively activated DCs (figure 3F, online supplemental figure S5F and G). These activated DCs were subsequently co-cultured with naïve OT-I CD8+ T cells (online supplemental figure S5H). DCs activated by Lars2-expressing MOC1-OVA cells induced stronger CD8+ T cell activation and enhanced antigen cross-presentation compared with controls (figure 3G, online supplemental figure S5I and J). Finally, activated OT-I CD8+ T cells were co-cultured with Lars2-overexpressing Luc+OVA+ MOC1 cells, and tumor cell survival was assessed by luciferase activity. As the effector-to-tumor (E:T) ratio increased, tumor cell survival decreased, with Lars2-overexpressing cells showing significantly lower survival than controls at the same E:T ratio (figure 3H). Concurrently, the proportion of IFN-γ+ and TNF-α+ OT-I CD8+ T cells was markedly increased (figure 3I, online supplemental figure S5K).

To further validate our co-culture findings in vivo, we established a syngeneic orthotopic tumor model by injecting Luc+OVA+ MOC1 cells into the buccal region of immunocompetent C57BL/6J mice. Compared with control cells transfected with an empty plasmid, overexpression of Lars2 significantly suppressed tumor growth. This antitumor effect was reversed when B2m was simultaneously knocked down in Luc+OVA+ MOC1 cells, confirming the dependence on MHC-I-mediated antigen presentation (figure 3J, online supplemental figure S5L). Flow cytometry analysis of tumor-infiltrating antigen-presenting cells (APCs) revealed that Lars2 overexpression enhanced the presentation of the SIINFEKL peptide by DCs and macrophages (figure 3K, online supplemental figure S5M). Moreover, the proportion of IFN-γ+ and TNF-α+ CD8+ T cells within the tumor increased (figure 3L, online supplemental figure S5N), indicating improved T cell-mediated cytotoxicity. These results demonstrate that restoring LARS2 expression upregulates MHC-I on tumor cells, promotes tumor antigen presentation, activates DCs and CD8+ T cells, and suppresses tumor growth through CTL-mediated cytotoxicity.

LARS2 facilitates tumor antigen presentation by boosting mitochondrial OXPHOS

We overexpressed or knocked down LARS2 in NPC cells (online supplemental figure S6A), and immunofluorescence confirmed its mitochondrial localization (figure 4A, online supplemental figure S6B). LARS2 overexpression increased the protein levels of ETC core subunits I, III, IV, and V, whereas LARS2 knockdown reduced their levels (figure 4B), confirming that LARS2 modulates ETC protein translation. Seahorse metabolic analysis further revealed that LARS2 overexpression significantly enhanced oxygen consumption rate (OCR) (figure 4C, online supplemental figure S6C), increased the intracellular nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+)/reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) ratio (figure 4D), elevated adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production (figure 4E), and strengthened mitochondrial membrane potential (figure 4F). Conversely, LARS2 knockdown produced opposite effects. Notably, extracellular acidification rate (ECAR), reflecting glycolysis (online supplemental figure S6D), as well as the expression (online supplemental figure S6E) and localization of key glycolytic enzymes (eg, HK2) (online supplemental figure S6F) were unaffected. These findings suggest that LARS2 promotes ETC protein translation, thereby increasing OXPHOS levels without altering glycolysis.

The TAP1/TAP2 transporter mediates the translocation of antigenic peptides into the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), a critical step in antigen presentation that depends on ATP.34 To examine the role of mitochondrial ATP in this process, we induced mtDNA depletion in NPC cells using ethidium bromide (EtBr) (online supplemental figure S7A), which significantly reduced mitochondrial ATP production (online supplemental figure S7B). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed a markedly decreased localization of TAP1 at the ER under these conditions (online supplemental figure S7C). In contrast, LARS2 overexpression significantly enhanced TAP1 colocalization with the ER (figure 5A). We further synthesized Cy5.5-labeled SIINFEKL peptide (OVA antigenic epitope) in vitro and confirmed its efficient internalization into HK1 and 5–8F cells by live-cell fluorescence microscopy (online supplemental figure S7D). LARS2 overexpression promoted colocalization of SIINFEKL with TAP1 and the ER (figure 5B). Conversely, treatment with trifluoromethoxy carbonylcyanide phenylhydrazone (FCCP), which inhibits OXPHOS-driven ATP synthesis (online supplemental figure S7E), markedly reduced the colocalization of SIINFEKL, TAP1, and the ER (figure 5B). Similar results were observed in MOC1 cells (figure 5C, online supplemental figure S7F). Flow cytometry analysis of SIINFEKL surface presentation on MOC1 cells showed that EtBr treatment significantly decreased peptide abundance (online supplemental figure S7G). LARS2 overexpression increased surface SIINFEKL presentation, an effect reversed by FCCP treatment (figure 5D). These results demonstrate that LARS2 enhances APP by boosting OXPHOS, thereby supplying the ATP required for TAP1/TAP2-mediated transport of antigenic peptides to the ER.

LARS2 enhances MHC-I expression via upregulating H3K27ac

The ETC is functionally coupled with the TCA cycle during OXPHOS,35 with acetyl-CoA serving as the initial metabolite entering the TCA cycle. In NPC cells, LARS2 overexpression elevated intracellular acetyl-CoA levels, whereas LARS2 knockdown produced the opposite effect (figure 6A). Co-treatment of LARS2-overexpressing cells with rotenone and antimycin A, inhibitors of ETC complexes I and III, respectively, reversed the LARS2-induced increase in acetyl-CoA (online supplemental figure S8A and B). Acetyl-CoA functions not only as a TCA substrate but also as a donor for protein acetylation.36 LARS2 overexpression in NPC cells increased global protein acetylation, whereas LARS2 knockdown reduced acetylation levels (figure 6B). Given the critical role of histone acetylation in epigenetic regulation of gene transcription,37 we examined common histone acetylation marks and found that LARS2 most significantly affected histone H3 lysine 27 acetylation (H3K27ac) (figure 6C), a modification associated with active gene expression.38 Analysis of chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) seq data from NPC cells in the Cistrome DB database revealed significant H3K27ac enrichment at the promoter regions of HLA-A, HLA-B, and HLA-C (online supplemental figure S8C), suggesting a potential role for H3K27ac in regulating these genes. ChIP-qPCR further confirmed the presence of distinct H3K27ac modifications at the regulatory regions of HLA-A, HLA-B, and HLA-C. LARS2 overexpression enhanced H3K27ac enrichment at these promoters (figure 6D, online supplemental figure S8D), whereas LARS2 knockdown reduced H3K27ac levels (figure 6E).

To further confirm that LARS2 epigenetically regulates MHC-I expression through acetyl-CoA, we treated cells overexpressing LARS2 with acetyl-CoA synthesis inhibitors ACSS2-IN-2 or SB 204990, which target the two primary intracellular acetyl-CoA production pathways (online supplemental figure S8A). Inhibition of acetyl-CoA synthesis reversed the LARS2-induced increases in H3K27ac and HLA-ABC levels (figure 6F, online supplemental figure S8E and F). The experiment also revealed that acetyl-CoA inhibitors mildly downregulated LARS2 expression in 5–8F cells, whereas the same treatment had no apparent effect in HK1 cells, indicating a differential response between the cell lines. Furthermore, knocking down LARS2 while supplementing cells with acetate—a precursor for acetyl-CoA converted intracellularly via ACSS2—restored the reductions in H3K27ac and HLA-ABC caused by LARS2 knockdown (figure 6G, online supplemental figure S8G). Notably, co-treatment with the pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase inhibitor dichloroacetate during LARS2 knockdown failed to rescue H3K27ac and HLA-ABC levels (online supplemental figure S8H), indicating that ETC dysfunction induced by LARS2 loss impairs the TCA cycle, which is the primary cause of reduced acetyl-CoA production. These results demonstrate that LARS2 enhances ETC function, promotes TCA cycle–derived acetyl-CoA synthesis, and upregulates H3K27ac modifications, thereby increasing MHC-I expression through an epigenetic mechanism.

Leucine supplementation augments CD8+ T cell-mediated tumor killing by enhancing the LARS2/MHC-I pathway

ARSs act as sensors for their cognate amino acids and participate in intracellular signaling.39 Treatment of NPC cells with increasing concentrations of leucine revealed, via immunoblotting, that higher leucine levels significantly elevated total intracellular LARS2 protein (figure 7A), with a particularly pronounced increase in mitochondrial LARS2 (figure 7B). This treatment also enhanced translation of ETC proteins (online supplemental figure S9A), increased OXPHOS activity (measured by OCR) (figure 7C), elevated the intracellular NAD+/NADH ratio (figure 7D), boosted ATP production (online supplemental figure S9B), and raised acetyl-CoA levels (figure 7E). These findings suggest that high-leucine treatment not only upregulates LARS2 expression but also provides a key amino acid component for leucine-rich ETC protein complexes, thereby promoting ETC protein translation and enhancing OXPHOS. We further investigated the effects of leucine supplementation on H3K27ac modifications and MHC-I expression. Leucine treatment significantly increased H3K27ac levels and HLA-ABC expression (figure 7F), including enhanced HLA-ABC membrane localization (online supplemental figure S9C). Co-treatment with the leucine transporter inhibitor 2-amino-2-norbornanecarboxylic acid (BCH), which blocks leucine uptake, abolished leucine-induced increases in LARS2, HLA-ABC expression, and H3K27ac modification (online supplemental figure S9D).

To assess the impact of leucine on tumor antigen presentation and CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity, we first confirmed in MOC1-OVA cells that leucine treatment upregulates LARS2 and MHC-I expression (online supplemental figure S9E) and enhances presentation of the H-2Kb-bound OVA-derived SIINFEKL peptide (figure 7G). Co-culture of OT-I mouse-derived immature BMDCs with leucine-treated MOC1-OVA cells significantly promoted DC maturation compared with untreated controls (figure 7H, online supplemental figure S9F). This, in turn, enhanced cross-presentation by DCs and increased activation of OT-I CD8+ T cells (figure 7I, online supplemental figure S9G). Activated OT-I CD8+ T cells were then co-cultured with leucine-treated or untreated MOC1-OVA cells to evaluate tumor cell killing. The proportion of IFN-γ+ and TNF-α+ CD8+ T cells increased gradually with higher leucine concentrations (figure 7J, online supplemental figure S9H). At the same E:T ratio, leucine-treated MOC1-OVA cells exhibited significantly lower survival than untreated cells (figure 7K). Importantly, co-treatment with the leucine transporter inhibitor BCH, which blocks tumor cell leucine uptake, abolished this effect (online supplemental figure S9I). Similarly, leucine pretreatment of NPC cells enhanced CD8+ T cell-mediated killing in a concentration-dependent manner (figure 7L, online supplemental figure S9J and K), accompanied by increased tumor cell apoptosis, an effect reversed by BCH (online supplemental figure S9L). These in vitro results indicate that leucine elevates LARS2 expression, promotes MHC-I-mediated antigen presentation, and facilitates CTL-mediated tumor cell killing. This suggests that leucine may reverse immune evasion in NPC cells—particularly those with low LARS2 levels—and potentially enhance the effectiveness of immunotherapy. To test this hypothesis, we performed subsequent in vivo experiments.

High-leucine diet enhances antitumor immunity and improves response to immune-checkpoint blockade in vivo

NPC cells were implanted subcutaneously into nude mice, with one group receiving a normal diet and another on a high-leucine diet (1.5% (w/v) leucine added to drinking water). In these immunodeficient mice, leucine supplementation alone did not influence tumor growth (online supplemental figure S10A–C). Next, B2M was knocked down in NPC cells to block MHC-I assembly. These B2M-knockdown and control (scramble siRNA) cells were then implanted subcutaneously into nude mice, with half of each group fed a normal diet and the other half a high-leucine diet. Seven days later, tumor antigen-presented and activated T cells were injected intravenously via the tail vein, and tumor growth was monitored over time. Following T cell infusion, the high-leucine diet significantly suppressed tumor growth in control tumors, whereas B2M knockdown abolished the tumor-suppressive effect of leucine supplementation (figure 8A–C). We established a syngeneic orthotopic tumor model in immunocompetent mice using Luc+ OVA+ MOC1 cells. A high-leucine diet markedly retarded tumor growth (figure 8D, online supplemental figure S10D), robustly increased the proportion of intratumoral CD8+ T cells among CD45+ immune cells and reduced the proportion of CD4+ T cells (figure 8E, online supplemental figure S10E). This diet also increased the proportions of IFN-γ+ and TNF-α+ CD8+ T cells (figure 8F, online supplemental figure S10F) but had no obvious effects on other immune cells or cancer-associated fibroblasts (online supplemental figure S10G). Specific depletion of CD8+ T cells using anti-CD8 neutralizing antibodies (figure 8G) completely abrogated the antitumor effect of the high-leucine diet (figure 8H, online supplemental figure S10H), indicating that leucine-mediated tumor suppression relies on CD8+ T cell-driven antitumor immune responses. To further validate the requirement for the Lars2-MHC-I axis in this process in vivo, we generated tumor-bearing mice using cells with specific knockdown of Lars2 or B2m, and subsequently administered dietary intervention. The results showed that knockdown of either Lars2 or B2m largely abolished the growth-inhibitory effect of the high-leucine diet (figure 8I, online supplemental figure S10I), demonstrating that the antitumor activity of leucine is highly dependent on endogenous Lars2 expression and on tumor antigen presentation. Collectively, our in vivo findings are consistent with in vitro observations. Leucine upregulates LARS2 expression to sustain MHC-I-mediated antigen presentation, thereby suppressing tumor growth in a CD8+ T cell-dependent manner.

In a clinical immunotherapy study with 40 patients with NPC, gene expression profiles were generated from biopsy tissues (GEO dataset: GSE224450).40 Analysis of the correlation between MHC-I and antigen-presentation-related gene expression and the efficacy of ICB (anti-PD-1/cytotoxic T-lymphocytes-associated protein 4) therapy revealed that patients with higher MHC-I expression had better treatment outcomes (online supplemental figure S11A). To determine if a high-leucine diet can improve ICB effectiveness in vivo, NPC cells were transplanted subcutaneously into nude mice. Half of the mice were given a normal diet, while the other half received a high-leucine diet. Seven days after transplantation, half of the mice in each diet group received anti-PD-1 (αPD-1) via intraperitoneal injection at 100 µg every 3 days for 2 weeks, while the remaining mice received isotype IgG. Meanwhile, all mice received tail vein injections of tumor-specific CTLs labeled with the live-cell dye DIR. T cell distribution was monitored at multiple time points after injection using in vivo fluorescence imaging. Both high-leucine diet and αPD-1 treatment increased T cell accumulation in tumors, with the combination therapy further enhancing CTL infiltration (online supplemental figure S11B). These findings indicate that a high-leucine diet can potentiate the antitumor efficacy of αPD-1 immunotherapy.

Xenograft tumors were harvested from mice, with half fixed and embedded in paraffin for IHC staining, and the other half processed into single-cell suspensions for flow cytometry analysis. In the high-leucine diet group, tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells showed increased proportions of IFN-γ+/TNF-α+ and IFN-γ+/GZMB+ subsets. Combination therapy with αPD-1 and a high-leucine diet further enhanced the proportion of these cytotoxic CD8+ T cells (figure 8M, online supplemental figure S11C). ELISA analysis of tumor tissue lysates showed corresponding increases in cytokine secretion, including IFN-γ, TNF-α, and GZMB (online supplemental figure S11D). IHC staining analysis revealed that the high-leucine diet upregulated LARS2 and HLA-ABC expression in tumor tissues and increased CD8+ T cell infiltration (online supplemental figure S11E). To further validate these findings, a syngeneic orthotopic tumor model using Luc+OVA+MOC1 cells was established in immunocompetent mice. The high-leucine diet similarly suppressed tumor growth and enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of αPD-1 (figure 8N, online supplemental figure S11F). Analysis of excised tumors revealed that the combination of a high-leucine diet and αPD-1 produced the highest proportions of IFN-γ+ and TNF-α+ CD8+ T cells within tumor tissues (online supplemental figure S11G). IHC staining confirmed that the high-leucine diet upregulates Lars2 and MHC-I expression in tumor tissues and promotes CD8+ T cell infiltration (online supplemental figure S11H). These results demonstrate that a high-leucine diet restores LARS2 expression and enhances the antitumor efficacy of ICB therapy.

Discussion

Genomic instability is a driving force in tumor initiation and progression. NPC has a lower frequency of point mutations than other malignancies.41 Its genomic instability is predominantly manifested as deletions, amplifications, or rearrangements of chromosomal fragments. Deletion of the short arm of chromosome 3 is one of the most prevalent molecular events in NPC.10 In addition, certain regions within 3p21 are strongly linked to disease onset in familial NPC.12 These findings suggest the presence of susceptibility or tumor-suppressor genes within this chromosomal segment. Over the past three decades, several critical tumor-suppressor and susceptibility genes have been identified in this region, including RASSF1A, LTF, CACNA2D2, and BLU.14–16 LARS2, located on chromosome 3p21.3, is also significantly downregulated in NPC.17 We performed loss of heterozygosity (LOH) analysis of LARS2 exons 4 and 8 at the 3p21 locus using clinical NPC specimens. The results indicated that LOH at 3p21 may contribute to reduced LARS2 expression in NPC. Meanwhile, other regulatory mechanisms, such as DNA methylation and post-transcriptional modulation, may also jointly govern its expression levels. Notably, restoring LARS2 expression in NPC cell lines did not substantially alter cell proliferation or apoptosis. Here, we report for the first time that LARS2 exerts its tumor-suppressive functions exclusively within the tumor immune microenvironment.

Most existing studies on immune evasion and immunotherapy resistance in NPC have focused primarily on regulatory mechanisms such as PD-L1 expression and T cell exhaustion. Far less attention has been paid to the role of reduced tumor immunogenicity in immune tolerance in NPC. Genomic instability in tumor cells can give rise to neoantigens via point mutations or fusion proteins derived from genomic rearrangements, which serve as the basis for immune cell recognition and tumor cell elimination.42 The recognition and killing of tumor cells by CD8+ T cells depend on MHC-I expression on the tumor cell surface and effective antigen presentation.43 The MHC-I complex consists of a three-domain heavy chain (α), a β2M light chain, and a peptide derived from a tumor antigen.44 The MHC-I APM is essential for MHC-I assembly and peptide loading.45 For an effective antitumor response, tumor antigens must first be captured and cross-presented by DCs to activate CD8+ T cells. Subsequently, tumor cells must maintain intact MHC-I antigen presentation to be recognized and eliminated by activated CD8+ T cells.30 Although ICB can block inhibitory signals that suppress T cell activation and achieve clinical responses, their efficacy is limited to a subset of patients.46 This suggests that additional mechanisms—such as the downregulation of MHC-I expression by tumor cells—facilitate immune evasion by reducing tumor immunogenicity, thereby preventing CD8+ T cell recognition and killing.47 48 Enhancing intrinsic tumor cell MHC-I expression and antigen presentation has therefore emerged as a promising strategy to improve immunotherapy outcomes.49 This study demonstrates that LARS2 enhances tumor cell metabolism by promoting the translation of mitochondrial respiratory chain proteins and epigenetically upregulates MHC-I expression. This dual action facilitates antigen presentation, activates DCs and CD8+ T cells, and increases tumor cell vulnerability to CTL-mediated killing, ultimately inhibiting tumor growth.

ARSs are responsible for covalently attaching each amino acid to its cognate tRNA, a critical step in intracellular protein synthesis. Based on their cellular localization, ARSs are classified into cytoplasmic ARSs (“ARS1”) and mitochondrial ARSs (“ARS2”). In humans, 37 ARSs have been identified, of which 19 are localized to mitochondria.50 51 LARS2, the mitochondrial leucyl-tRNA synthetase, facilitates the translation of mtDNA-encoded ETC complex proteins by charging tRNAs with leucine. These ETC proteins are essential for OXPHOS.20 Our analysis of amino acid usage revealed that leucine accounts for more than 14% of residues in all mitochondrial-encoded ETC subunits, which is significantly higher than the average of 7% (online supplemental table 5). This suggests that LARS2 may serve as a rate-limiting factor in the translation of ETC proteins. Downregulation of LARS2 impairs ETC function, reduces OXPHOS levels, and drives metabolic reprogramming in tumor cells, partially explaining the Warburg effect—a shift toward glycolysis—observed in NPC.52 Conversely, restoring LARS2 expression enhances ETC function and OXPHOS. Previous studies have demonstrated that aberrant enhancement of OXPHOS in tumor cells is accompanied by the acquisition of stem-like phenotypes53 and correlates with treatment response.54 55 We further examined well-recognized stemness markers associated with NPC.56 The results revealed that LARS2-mediated upregulation of OXPHOS did not alter the stemness characteristics of NPC cells (online supplemental figure S6G), suggesting that this metabolic reprogramming is independent of stemness-related regulatory pathways.

An increasing body of evidence indicates that cellular metabolism is closely intertwined with epigenetic regulation.57 Mitochondria act as the central metabolic hub, integrating the metabolism of carbohydrates, lipids, and amino acids through the TCA cycle. Beyond their role in ATP production, mitochondrial intermediate metabolites—such as citrate, α-ketoglutarate, succinate, and fumarate—can regulate tumor epigenetics through multiple pathways.58 For example, succinate has been shown to enhance MHC-I APP by inhibiting histone demethylases and modulating tumor epigenetic states, thereby increasing transcription of MHC-APP-related genes. This process boosts tumor immunogenicity, promotes the activation and infiltration of tumor-reactive CD8+ T cells, and enhances antitumor immunity.59 As the initial metabolite entering the TCA cycle, acetyl-CoA also serves as an indispensable donor for histone acetylation, and thus plays a central role in epigenetic transcriptional regulation. In this study, we demonstrate that LARS2 enhances mitochondrial metabolism and increases acetyl-CoA production, thereby promoting H3K27ac. This epigenetic modification, in turn, upregulates MHC-I expression. H3K27ac is a global marker of chromatin activation. Our data, together with findings from other models, demonstrate that this modification is markedly enriched in the promoters of genes related to MHC-I antigen presentation, making these genes highly susceptible to fluctuations in histone acetylation levels.60 61 This partly explains why LARS2 primarily regulates the MHC-I antigen-presentation pathway in our system. Nevertheless, we cannot rule out the possibility that LARS2-driven metabolic reprogramming may alter the expression of other genes through H3K27ac.

The primary APCs in humans include DCs, macrophages, and B lymphocytes, while tumor cells can also act as non-professional APCs. MHC-I–mediated antigen presentation involves several coordinated steps: intracellular antigens are first broken down by proteasomes, and the resulting peptides are transported into the ER via TAP1/TAP2 transporters, where they are loaded onto MHC-I molecules. The peptide-MHC-I complexes are then transported through the Golgi apparatus to the plasma membrane, where they are presented to DCs and CD8+ T cells.62 The TAP transporter, a heterodimer of TAP1 and TAP2, relies on ATP to perform its function.34 Mitochondria, as the main site of cellular energy metabolism and ATP production, are therefore critical for this process. Prior studies have shown that TAP1/TAP2 expression is downregulated in advanced breast cancer, facilitating immune evasion,63 and that lung cancer cells can reduce surface peptide-MHC-I complexes by decreasing chromatin accessibility at the TAP2 promoter, protecting tumor cells from CD8+ T cell-mediated killing.64 Although these findings highlight the importance of TAP transporters in antigen presentation, the mechanism by which cellular energy metabolism regulates their function remains unclear. In this study, we demonstrate that restoring LARS2 expression in NPC enhances mitochondrial ATP production, promotes the colocalization of antigenic peptides and TAP transporters within the ER, and increases surface levels of antigen peptide–MHC-I complexes. This not only boosts tumor cell immunogenicity but also suppresses immune evasion and sensitizes tumors to immunotherapy.

Finally, we explored strategies to effectively upregulate LARS2 expression to make tumors more sensitive to immunotherapy. Previous studies have shown that tumor cells sustain uncontrolled growth by consuming large amounts of amino acids from the tumor microenvironment, thereby suppressing immune cell function and promoting tumor progression.65 66 This has led to the concept of amino acid-restricted dietary therapy as a potential adjuvant in cancer treatment. For example, methionine restriction inhibits tumor growth and enhances antitumor immunity by increasing the infiltration and cytotoxicity of CD8+ T cells in colorectal cancer mouse models,67 while dietary leucine restriction can counteract B cell-mediated immune suppression in the colorectal cancer microenvironment.26 However, the potential antitumor effects of amino acid supplementation have been largely overlooked. In this study, we discovered a novel role for leucine: rather than restricting amino acids, dietary leucine supplementation simultaneously serves as a translational substrate for leucine-rich mitochondrial ETC complexes and feedback-regulates LARS2 expression. This dual effect synergistically promotes ETC complex translation, restores mitochondrial function, enhances MHC-I expression, improves tumor antigen presentation, and increases CD8+ T cell-mediated killing of NPC cells, ultimately suppressing tumor growth. Therefore, leucine supplementation represents a promising strategy to enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy in NPC. Notably, as a multifunctional nutrient, leucine exerts pleiotropic effects on systemic metabolism and immune regulation. In our animal models, however, among the immune populations examined, the most prominent proportional changes were observed in tumor-infiltrating CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, whereas other immune and stromal cell subsets exhibited no notable alterations. These findings suggest that in our experimental system, dietary leucine preferentially modulates CD8+ T cell-mediated antitumor immunity through the tumor-intrinsic LARS2-MHC-I axis. Even so, potential systemic impacts on the host cannot be fully ruled out during dietary leucine intervention.

In summary, this study reveals the critical role of LARS2 in regulating immune evasion in NPC and elucidates its underlying mechanisms. LARS2, an immune microenvironment-dependent tumor suppressor, is downregulated in NPC, leading to epigenetic suppression of MHC-I expression, reduced tumor antigen presentation, and enhanced immune evasion and resistance to immunotherapy. Conversely, restoring LARS2 expression or providing a high-leucine diet promotes the translation of ETC-related proteins, remodels mitochondrial metabolism, increases intracellular acetyl-CoA levels, and boosts H3K27ac-mediated MHC-I expression. These changes collectively enhance tumor antigen presentation and CD8+ T cell-mediated antitumor immunity (online supplemental figure S12). This study preliminarily proposes a new strategy of leucine supplementation to enhance immunotherapy response in NPC. Meanwhile, a combined assessment of LARS2 and MHC-I expression, together with plasma leucine levels, may serve as a candidate biomarker for predicting immunotherapy response. These findings provide novel insights and a theoretical basis for immune subtyping and prognostic evaluation of NPC. Nevertheless, the present study has certain limitations. The clinical utility of the proposed intervention strategy and the potential biomarkers remain to be validated in larger prospective clinical cohorts.

Materials and methods

Patient samples

A total of 39 NPC tissue samples were analyzed to assess LARS2 and CD8A expression using mIF and IHC techniques (online supplemental table 1). All clinical specimens were obtained from Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (Changsha, China). The diagnosis of each specimen was confirmed by histopathological examination.

Cell culture, plasmids, and cell transfection

NPC lines HK1 and 5–8F were used in this study, while the HNSCC cell line MOC1 was employed for antigen presentation experiments. Cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Gibco, Cat# C11995500BT, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, Cat# 10270106, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (BI, Cat# 03–03101B, Israel) at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. All cell lines were confirmed to be mycoplasma-free prior to experimentation.

To overexpress LARS2, the full-length LARS2 gene was amplified using complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesized from RNA isolated from 5 to 8F cells as the PCR template. The resulting coding sequence was cloned into the BamHI and ApaI sites of the pCMV3-C-FLAG vector to generate the LARS2 overexpression construct, which was verified by sequencing. NPC cells were transfected with either the LARS2 overexpression plasmid or an empty control plasmid using Neofect transfection reagent (Neofect Biotech, China). siRNAs targeting LARS2 and B2M, along with a scramble control, were purchased from RiboBio (Guangzhou, China) and transfected using Hiperfect (Qiagen, Germany). The sequences of all siRNAs used are provided in online supplemental table 3.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were measured using a spectrophotometer (BioTek, USA). cDNA was synthesized from the extracted RNA using the HiScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, Cat# R323-01, China). qRT-PCR was performed with Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Cat# Q511-02, China) on a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA). β-actin was used as the internal control, and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2ˆ(-ΔΔCT) method. All primer sequences are listed in online supplemental table 3.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Epizyme Biomedical, Cat# PC101, USA) to extract total protein. Protein concentrations were determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 23227, USA). Equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) (Servicebio, Cat# G2177, China) and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Cat# IPVH00010, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 2 hours and then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies. After washing three times with phosphate buffered saline with Tween 20 (PBST) for 10 min each, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1–2 hours. Chemiluminescent signals were detected using a chemiluminescence imaging system (Servicebio, Cat# G2020, China). All antibodies used are listed in online supplemental table 4.

Mitochondrial isolation and blue native PAGE

NPC cells were collected by trypsinization and washed twice with ice-cold phosphate buffered saline (PBS). Cells were resuspended in mitochondrial isolation buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with protease inhibitors, incubated on ice for 10–15 min, and then homogenized. The homogenate was centrifuged at 4°C, 1,000×g for 10 min. The supernatant containing the mitochondrial fraction was further centrifuged at 11,000×g for 10 min at 4°C to separate mitochondria from other cytoplasmic components. The supernatant was collected as the cytoplasmic fraction, while the pellet represented the isolated mitochondria. Mitochondrial pellets were resuspended in BN buffer (1 M aminocaproic acid, 50 mM Bis-Tris, pH 7.0) to a final protein concentration of 10 mg/mL. Digitonin was added at a digitonin/protein ratio of 8 g/g (5% final concentration), and samples were incubated on ice for 20 min to solubilize mitochondrial proteins. The mixture was centrifuged at 4°C, 13,000×g for 30 min. One-third volume of BN loading buffer (5% Serva Blue G, 1 M aminocaproic acid) was added to the supernatant containing solubilized proteins. Approximately 80 µg of mitochondrial proteins were separated on 1.0 mm, 10-well NativePAGE 3–12% Bis-Tris protein gels (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# BN1001BOX, USA) at 4°C using dark blue cathode buffer (50 mM Tricine, 15 mM Bis-Tris, pH 7.0, 0.02% Serva Blue G) and anode buffer (50 mM Bis-Tris, pH 7.0). Electrophoresis was performed at 100 V until the sample reached one-third of the gel (~30 min). The dark blue cathode buffer was then replaced with light blue cathode buffer (0.002% Serva Blue G), and electrophoresis continued at a constant current of 15 mA until the dye front reached the bottom (~1 hour). Following separation, proteins were transferred onto a PVDF membrane at 100 V for 1 hour and subjected to western blot analysis.

Seahorse measurements

OCR and ECAR were measured using the Seahorse XF HS Mini Analyzer (Agilent, USA). Cells were seeded at pre-optimized densities into XF HS Mini cell culture microplates and equilibrated for 1 hour in serum-free Seahorse XF Basal Medium at 37°C in a CO₂-free incubator. For OCR assays, cells were sequentially treated with oligomycin (1.5 µM), FCCP (1 µM), and rotenone/antimycin A (0.5 µM each). For ECAR assays, cells were sequentially treated with glucose (10 mM), oligomycin (1 µM), and 2-deoxyglucose (2-DG, 50 mM). All conditions were performed in triplicate. After the assay, cells were washed with ice-cold PBS, and proteins were extracted using 25 µL of 1×RIPA buffer for normalization. Data were analyzed and visualized using Wave software (V.2.3.0, Seahorse Bioscience, Agilent Technologies).

Immunofluorescence staining

To assess the intracellular localization of Cy5.5-labeled SIINFEKL peptide, cells were seeded onto glass-bottom dishes and cultured to confluence. The culture medium was then replaced with medium containing 2.5 µM Cy5.5-labeled SIINFEKL peptide, followed by incubation at 37°C in the dark for 2 hours. After incubation, the medium was removed and replaced with fresh culture medium, and the cells were stained with Hoechst 33342 (1:1,000; Abmole, USA) at 37°C for 10 min. Fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica, Germany).

For co-localization analysis of the Cy5.5-labeled SIINFEKL peptide with TAP1 and the ER, cells were incubated with 2.5 µM Cy5.5-labeled SIINFEKL peptide at 37°C in the dark for 2 hours. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized using a saponin-based permeabilization buffer (Beyotime, China), and blocked in 5% bovine serum albumin for 1 hour at room temperature. Subsequently, cells were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After washing, they were incubated with appropriate fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Invitrogen, Cat# D1306, USA) for 10 min. Fluorescence images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica, Germany). Antibodies used are listed in online supplemental table 4.

mIF staining

The mIF assay was conducted using the Enhanced Five-Label Six-Color Multiplex Fluorescent Staining Kit (AiFang, Cat# AFIHC036, China), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Tyramide signal amplification technology was used to detect target proteins. After each round of staining, non-covalently bound primary antibody–secondary antibody–HRP complexes were removed by heat-induced stripping. Subsequent rounds of staining were performed using different primary antibodies and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, followed by incubation with the corresponding tyramide fluorophore substrates. This iterative process enabled multiplex detection of LARS2, CD8A, and IFN-γ. Slides were counterstained with DAPI-containing mounting medium. Multispectral images of stained sections were acquired using the Pannoramic SCAN II imaging system (3DHISTECH, Hungary) with appropriate fluorescence filters. For quantitative analysis, we selected intact tumor regions without necrosis or staining artifacts. Single fluorescence channels were viewed using SlideViewer (3DHISTECH, Hungary). Appropriate intensity thresholds were applied to remove background noise and identify positive signals. Single-channel images of the same fields were exported to ImageJ. Using DAPI-labeled nuclei as references, we quantified fluorescence intensity in each channel and calculated the mean fluorescence intensity for statistical analysis.

IHC and H&E staining

IHC staining was performed using the Elivision Plus Polymer HRP (Mouse/Rabbit) Immunohistochemistry Kit (Maxim, Cat# Kit-9902, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Tissue sections were dewaxed, subjected to EDTA-based high-temperature antigen retrieval, and treated with an endogenous peroxidase inhibitor. Samples were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After three washes, sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 hour. Staining was assessed based on the proportion of positive cells and staining intensity. Representative tumor images were acquired at 400× magnification. Positive cell percentage was scored as: 0 (none), 1 (≤10%), 2 (10–50%), 3 (50–80%), 4 (>80%). Staining intensity was graded as 0 (negative), 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), or 3 (strong). The final immunoreactive score (IRS) was the product of the two scores, with a total range of 0–12. Only viable, necrosis-free tumor regions were analyzed to exclude non-specific staining. All evaluations were completed by two experienced pathologists. Using the median LARS2 IRS as the cut-off value, 39 NPC samples were stratified into high-expression and low-expression groups. PD-L1 expression was evaluated using the clinical CPS system. Representative images were captured with an Olympus BX51 fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan). Antibodies used are listed in online supplemental table 4.

For H&E staining, paraffin-embedded tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin to visualize nuclei and eosin to stain cytoplasm.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation-quantitative PCR

Approximately 1.0×10⁷ NPC cells were cross-linked with 1% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 10 min, and the reaction was quenched with 0.125 M glycine for 5 min. Cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS containing protease inhibitors (Beyotime, China). Cell lysis buffer was added, and samples were sonicated to shear chromatin into 200–1000 bp DNA fragments. The lysates were incubated overnight at 4°C with 50 µL of protein A/G magnetic beads and 5 µg of primary antibody using a DNA rotary incubator. Protein–DNA complexes were eluted, treated with proteinase K to remove proteins, and the DNA was purified. Enriched DNA fragments were analyzed by qPCR and PCR gel electrophoresis. Primers used for DNA precipitation PCR analysis are provided in online supplemental table 3.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Acetyl-Coenzyme A (Sangon, D751001-0048, China) and the cytokines TNF-α (ZCIBIO, Cat# ZC-M6765, China), GZMB (ZCIBIO, Cat# ZC-33834, China), and IFN-γ (ZCIBIO, Cat# ZC-32271, China) were measured using commercially available ELISA kits. Absorbance values were recorded using a SpectraMax M multifunctional microplate reader (Molecular Devices, China).

Flow cytometry

Apoptosis was assessed using the Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (Invitrogen, Cat# BMS500FI, USA) and analyzed by flow cytometry. To evaluate intracellular cytokines, cells were treated with brefeldin A (BFA; Selleck, Cat# S7046, USA) to inhibit cytokine secretion. After permeabilization, cells were incubated with specific cytokine antibodies. Fluorescence compensation was performed using unstained and single-stained control samples. Flow cytometry was conducted on a DxP Athena flow cytometer (Cytek Biosciences, USA), and data were analyzed using FlowJo software (RRID:SCR_008520). Antibodies used are listed in online supplemental table 4.

Co-culture of OT-I cells and MOC1-OVA cells

Bone marrow cells were harvested from the femur and tibia of C57BL/6-Tg (TcraTcrb) 1100Mjb/J (OT-I) mice. Cells were flushed from the bones, collected, and treated with red blood cell lysis buffer at room temperature for 3 min. The cell suspension was filtered through a 70 µm strainer to remove aggregates. Cells were then resuspended in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) medium supplemented with 10% FBS and cultured for 6–8 days with 20 ng/mL mouse granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) (PeproTech, Cat# 315–03, USA) and 10 ng/mL mouse IL-4 (PeproTech, Cat# 214–14, USA) to generate immature dendritic cells (DCs). Immature DCs were co-cultured with MOC1-OVA cells at a 1:1 ratio for 24 hours to induce DC activation and maturation. Flow cytometry was used to assess DC activation. Cells treated with IFN-γ for 24 hours served as a positive control.

CD8+ T cells were isolated from the spleens of OT-I mice. Spleens were homogenized, filtered through a 70 µm strainer, and treated with red blood cell lysis buffer at room temperature for 5 min. Cells were then resuspended in RPMI medium supplemented with 10% FBS to obtain naïve CD8+ T cells. Naïve CD8+ T cells were co-cultured with MOC1-OVA–matured DCs for 48 hours, after which T cell activation was assessed by flow cytometry. To generate activated, antigen-specific CD8+ T cells, cells were cultured in RPMI medium containing 20 ng/mL mouse IL-2 and stimulated with 1 µg/mL OVA₂₅₇–₂₆₄ peptide (GenScript, Cat# RP10611CN) for 3 days. Activated CD8+ T cells were then co-cultured with MOC1-OVA cells, and their cytotoxic capacity was evaluated by flow cytometry.

MOC1 cells were infected with lentivirus expressing ovalbumin (OVA), luciferase (Luc), and GFP (Shanghai Genechem Co., Ltd, China) to generate OVA-expressing tumor cells. Infected cells were selected using HitransG P virus infection reagent and screened with 2 µg/mL puromycin (MCE, USA) for 3–4 days. OVA expression was confirmed by RT-qPCR. The resulting MOC1-OVA cells were then co-cultured with OT-I mouse-derived BMDCs and CD8+ T cells. During co-culture with OT-I CD8+ T cells, intracellular luciferase activity was measured using Luciferase Assay Reagent (Vazyme, China) to assess tumor cell survival.

Generation of human DCs and tumor-specific T cells

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from healthy donor blood using the Ficoll density gradient method (Cytiva, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

To DCs, isolated PBMCs were cultured in 10% FBS-1640 medium supplemented with 20 ng/mL IL-4 (PeproTech, Cat# AF-200-04, USA) and 40 ng/mL GM-CSF (PeproTech, Cat# 300–03, USA) for 5 days. Subsequently, 25 ng/mL IFN-γ (Sino Biological, Cat# 11725-HNAE, China) was added, and cells were cultured for an additional 24 hours. To prime DCs for tumor-specific antigen presentation, tumor cell lysates were co-cultured with the DCs for 24 hours, generating DCs capable of presenting tumor-specific antigens and inducing cell-mediated immune responses.

For T cell preparation, in vitro expansion was carried out over 8 days using CD3/CD28 magnetic beads (Miltenyi, Cat# 130–091-441, Germany) in the presence of 15 ng/mL IL-2, 5 ng/mL IL-7, and 10 ng/mL IL-15 (Sino Biological, China). Expanded T cells were then co-cultured with the prepared DCs at a 5:1 T cell-to-DC ratio in IL-2-supplemented medium to generate tumor-specific CTLs. Fresh medium and cytokines were replenished every 2 days. Volunteers were selected to ensure their T cells shared the same HLA-A type as the target tumor cell line. These tumor-specific CTLs were subsequently used for in vitro co-culture experiments and for adoptive T cell therapy in animal models.

Animal experiments

BALB/c nude mice (4-week-old females) and C57BL/6J mice (6-week-old females) were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Central South University (Changsha, China) and maintained in a specific pathogen-free barrier facility.

For the BALB/c nude mouse xenograft model, 5×10⁶ transfected human NPC HK1 cells were injected subcutaneously into the right axilla of each mouse. Once tumors became palpable, tumor-antigen-presenting primary T cells were administered via the tail vein. Human T cells were pre-labeled with the deep-red live-cell fluorescent dye DiR (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and their distribution was monitored using a small-animal in vivo imaging system (Bruker, USA). Tumor size was measured every 3–4 days using calipers, and volume was calculated as ½ × (length) × (width)². Mice were euthanized when tumors reached a maximum dimension of 2 cm.

For the C57BL/6J syngeneic orthotopic tumor model, 2×10⁶ Luc+OVA+MOC1 cells were injected into the buccal region of each mouse. Visible tumors developed by day 7 post-injection. To evaluate the synergistic therapeutic effect of leucine supplementation and anti-PD-1 antibody therapy, mice were given drinking water supplemented with 1.5% (w/v) L-leucine (Sigma, Cat# L8912, Germany), alongside intraperitoneal injection of 100 µg anti-PD-1 antibody (Selleck, Cat# A2122, China). For CD8+ T cell depletion, 150 µg CD8 neutralizing antibody (Selleck, Cat# A2102, China) was administered starting 3 days prior to tumor engraftment, and successful depletion was confirmed by flow cytometry. All antibody treatments were delivered intraperitoneally every 3 days for 2 weeks. Tumor growth was monitored via intraperitoneal injection of luciferin substrate (Genomeditech, Cat# 040621, China); bioluminescent signal intensity and spatial distribution were quantified using a small-animal in vivo imaging system (IVIS Spectrum, USA).

At the end of the experiment, mice were euthanized, and tumor tissues were collected. Portions of the tumors were processed into single-cell suspensions for ELISA and flow cytometry, while the remaining tissues were paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and subjected to H&E and IHC staining. The animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Central South University (Number: APU-2025-0043).

Statistical analysis

All continuous variables are presented as mean±SD. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism V.9.4.1. Comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired Student’s t-test, while comparisons among three or more groups were analyzed by one-way or two-way analysis of variance with appropriate multiple comparison tests. Survival differences were assessed using Kaplan-Meier curves and the log-rank test. A p value<0.05 was considered statistically significant.
