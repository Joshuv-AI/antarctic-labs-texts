Metal-insulator transition at low densities in a two-dimensional low-bandgap semiconductor
Authors: Ali Al Mejamai, Thiti Taychatanapat, Johan Félisaz, Oldrich Cicvarek, Kenji Watanabe, Takashi Taniguchi, Zdeněk Sofer, Oleg V. Yazyev, Ahmet Avsar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metal–insulator transitions in low band-gap systems emerge from the interplay of interactions, disorder, and band structure on comparable energy scales. Platinum diselenide, with its thickness-dependent electronic structure, provides an ideal platform to explore this regime, as the five-layer limit lies close to the semimetallic boundary while retaining a small bandgap (~0.1 eV). Here we show that hexagonal boron nitride-encapsulated five-layer PtSe2 devices with embedded ultra-flat, pre-patterned platinum contacts exhibit high electronic quality, with mobilities up to 1,640 cm2V−1s−1 and contact resistances as low as 1.31 kΩ·μm at 1.5 K. These characteristics enable a gate-tunable metal-insulator transition at carrier densities down to 2.75 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻². Transport in this regime is dominated by electron–electron interactions, as supported by finite-temperature scaling. Below 50 K, however, an additional disorder-related energy scale emerges, consistent with percolation. These results demonstrate that disorder remains relevant even in high-mobility devices, challenging a purely interaction-driven transition in two-dimensional systems.

Introduction

Phase transitions in condensed-matter systems are fundamental for understanding the collective dynamics of quantum many-body systems. In two-dimensional (2D) materials, metal-insulator transitions (MITs) provide a particularly compelling platform for exploring these phenomena, extending beyond single-electron models to encompass complex many-body interactions1,2. In this context, the zero-temperature quantum phase transition (QPT) model emphasizes the role of strong carrier-carrier interactions in driving MITs, while the percolation model focuses on how spatial disorder disrupts conductive pathways. Both models contribute to our understanding of MITs, but the interplay between these mechanisms in 2D materials remains not fully understood.

Discrepancies in observed transitions in 2D semiconductors highlight the complexities involved, compounded by sample-to-sample variations and inconsistencies. For instance, some studies on MoS2 suggest that interaction-driven MITs are predominant3,4, while others attribute MITs in MoS2 transistors to percolation mechanisms5,6. These discrepancies underscore the challenges in understanding MITs in 2D systems and have led to debates about the feasibility of observing quantum phases like the Wigner crystal (WC) in 2D systems due to inherent disorder7. To investigate MIT phenomena and mechanisms effectively in 2D systems, it is crucial to fabricate high-quality devices.

Recent advancements in high-quality monolayer WSe2 devices8, enabled by innovative “charge-transfer contacts” and superior crystal growth techniques, have demonstrated MITs at exceptionally low carrier densities ( ~ 1.6 ×1011 cm−2). These studies report that the observed transitions are consistent with a QPT, leading to the formation of a WC in the insulating phase. Conversely, theoretical models suggest that Coulomb disorder in 2D systems might prevent the stabilization of a WC, potentially resulting in a localized Anderson insulator instead7. This theoretical perspective underscores the complexity of disorder’s role in 2D materials and highlights the subtle interplay between disorder and interactions in MITs, which continues to be an area of active investigation in both experimental and theoretical studies.

In this context, platinum diselenide (PtSe2), a relatively unexplored transition metal dichalcogenide (TMD)9, emerges as a promising candidate for studying MITs. In comparison to other layered TMDs, this material is characterized by a rather strong coupling between the electronic states of individual monolayer counterparts, which results in strong dependence of its electronic properties on thickness accompanied by strong electron-electron interactions and spin-orbit coupling9–13. Notably, PtSe2 undergoes a thickness-dependent transition from semimetal for the bulk material to semiconductor with a relatively large gap for its monolayer. Theoretical predictions indicate the potential for high field-effect mobilities14; however, experimental results with directly evaporated contacts have frequently reported much lower mobilities15,16. Nevertheless, with the fabrication of high-quality devices, PtSe2 offers the potential for studying MITs in detail.

In this study, we experimentally characterize high-quality, hexagonal boron nitride (hBN)-encapsulated PtSe2 devices with embedded, ultra-flat, pre-patterned Pt contacts. We focus on 4-layer (4 L) and 5-layer (5 L) PtSe2 field-effect transistors (FETs). Among the semiconducting forms, 5 L and 4 L PtSe2 exhibit the smallest bandgaps, approximately 0.1 eV and 0.26 eV, respectively. The formation of transparent ohmic contacts is observed at backgate voltages above 7.5 V down to ~ 1.5 K, with contact resistances as low as ≈ 1.31 kΩ µm, and high 2-terminal field-effect mobilities up to ~ 650 cm2V−1s−1 and 4-terminal mobilities up to ~ 1640 cm2V−1s−1. These devices enable the observation of a gate-tuned MIT in 5 L at ultralow carrier densities ( ~ 2.75 × 1010 cm−2), and in 4 L at slightly higher densities ( ~ 5.88 × 1010 cm−2). At these densities, a large interaction parameter of rS ~ 20.6 is obtained, indicating strong carrier-carrier interactions at higher temperatures ( > 50 K), consistent with an interaction-dominated MIT, which is characterized using finite-temperature scaling. However, at temperatures below 50 K, charge transport enters the charge-impurity-scattering regime, inducing disorder and suggesting a crossover to a percolation-driven MIT. Our findings underscore the critical interplay between disorder and interactions in shaping MITs in 2D materials.

Results

Our device architecture is depicted schematically in Fig. 1a. Fabrication begins with the mechanical exfoliation and identification of ~ 30 nm thick bottom hBN flakes on doped Si wafers with a 300 nm SiO2 layer, which serves as the backgate (Fig. 1b). Standard e-beam lithography is then used to define a Hall-bar contact geometry, followed by etching the exposed regions by ~ 15 nm using deep reactive ion etching. Ti/Pt (2/14 nm) electrodes are deposited by e-beam evaporation to form the pre-patterned contacts. Afterwards, the device undergoes an initial annealing step under high-vacuum conditions ( ~ 1 × 10−6 mbar) at 340 °C for 6 h to remove fabrication-related residues. Next, a bilayer of top hBN and PtSe2-each picked-up from SiO2 substrates using the polycarbonate-based dry transfer method17-is transferred onto the pre-patterned Pt contacts. By embedding the Pt contacts within the bottom hBN dielectric, a nearly flat surface is achieved for PtSe2 deposition, improving the interface quality between hBN and PtSe2 and preventing strain, thus enhancing overall device performance. To complete the device, 2 nm Ti/100 nm Au contacts are formed over the unexposed Pt contact regions using additional e-beam lithography and evaporation. As the thickness of PtSe2 decreases below six layers, its band structure transitions from semimetallic to semiconducting, with the bandgap increasing from 105 meV in the 5-layer (5 L) structure to 1.85 eV in the monolayer, as confirmed by our hybrid functional DFT calculations (Fig. 1e). For our study, we intentionally selected 4 L and 5 L flakes as they exhibit the smallest band gaps just below the semimetallic response. The thinnest flakes tend to exhibit larger Schottky barriers and lower mobilities due to their higher band gaps, whereas 4 L and 5 L flakes provide higher mobility, reduced defect density, and lower contact resistance, making them ideal candidates for investigating gate-voltage-driven MIT. Since the electronic properties of PtSe2 are highly dependent on layer thickness, here we used both AFM and Raman spectroscopy to precisely characterize the number of layers. An example AFM height measurement of 3 L, 4 L, and 5 L PtSe2 is shown in Fig. 1c, alongside corresponding Raman spectra in Fig. 1d. As the thickness decreases to 3 layers, the Eg peak shifts from 175 cm−1 in the bulk to 180.2 cm−1, while the ratio of the intensity of Eg to the intensity of A1g increases from 1.6 (bulk) to 9.8 (3 L) (Fig. 1d - inset). These monotonic shifts show the effectiveness of Raman spectroscopy for identifying PtSe2 flakes of semiconducting thickness. Utilizing both characterization techniques, we fabricated devices based on 4 L and 5 L PtSe2 flakes for this study. Unless otherwise stated, the results presented here are from a representative 5 L PtSe2 device, shown optically in Fig. 1b.

The capacitance is calculated as Ci = εrε0 / (tSiO2 + thBN) where εr ~ 3.9 is the dielectric constant of SiO2 and hBN, and (tSiO2 + thBN) ~ 330 nm is the combined thickness of the dielectric layers. The two-terminal electron field-effect mobilities of ~ 650 cm2V−1s−1 and ~ 412 cm2V−1s−1 were obtained for the 5 L and 4 L devices, respectively, representing the highest values recorded for PtSe2-based FETs at these thicknesses13,15. We note that the Au-top-contacted test sample on SiO2 (Supplementary Note 1) shows significantly lower mobilities, underscoring the importance of contact quality and hBN encapsulation on device performance.

We further examine the mobility of devices by probing in a four-terminal (4-t) geometry; Fig. 2e conveys the evolution of the 4-t and 2-t mobility of the 5 L device as a function of temperature. The highest 4-t mobility is found to be ~ 1640 cm2V−1s−1 at T = 1.5 K for the 5 L device and ~ 1480 cm2V−1s−1 at T = 20 K for the 4 L device (Fig. 2e inset). As the temperature reduces from 300 K to ~ 100 K, there is a steep increase in the mobility in both measurement geometries, which delineates that mobility is limited by phonon scattering. The temperature dependence in this regime follows the power law, µ ~ T-γ, where γ depends on electron-phonon scattering and is extracted to be ~ 0.9-0.97 and ~ 1.25 for 5 L and 4 L devices, respectively. Calculations for MoS222, suggest that γ ~ 1, as observed in the 5 L device, is indicative of scattering by acoustic phonons, while γ > 1, observed in the 4 L device, corresponds to scattering by optical phonons. Additionally, the γ values obtained for our PtSe2 devices are comparable to those reported for high-quality, fully encapsulated MoS219 and WSe28, reinforcing the quality of our samples. The mobility continues to increase more gradually as the temperature is lowered further until ~ 50 K, where it begins to saturate before decreasing as the temperature reaches 1.5 K. This behavior suggests that mobility is increasingly limited by charge impurity scattering at lower temperatures. We will later discuss how the onset of charge-impurity–dominated scattering introduces disorder, leading to a crossover from an interaction-dominated transport regime to percolation-like transport.

We next investigate the temperature-dependent charge transport characteristics of the 5 L device. Figure 3a presents the conductivity as function of VBG on a semilog-scale, measured over the temperature range 1.5 – 300 K under a constant VSD = 0.1 V. Firstly, for gate voltages between −8 V to −15 V, we observe that the device exhibits p-type semiconducting behavior. This is expected given the small size of the bandgap, allowing for the gate voltage to tune the Fermi level into both the conduction and valence bands. Secondly, we detect the crossing-over of all conductivity curves acquired at different temperatures around VBG = 7 V; this is suggestive of a metal-insulator transition. Below this point, the device performs as a classical semiconductor with conductivity decreasing as the temperature is lowered. The carrier density at which the MIT occurs is referred to as the critical carrier density nC. In Fig. 3c, the conductivity versus the temperature is displayed, we determine VC = 3 V, which from the capacitance model n = Ci(VBG – VTH)/e; corresponds to nC = 2.75 × 1010 cm−2 (Supplementary Note 3)26–31. This demonstrates an ultralow critical carrier density in 5 L PtSe2. Similar VC was extracted for measurements conducted under zero DC bias and a small AC excitation of 1 mV (Supplementary Fig. 6), indicating no clear influence of potential heating effects on the determination of VC32. Theoretical work has shown that nC is related to mobility and that high mobility due to sample cleanliness results in low nC7. Accordingly, we attribute the low nC observed to the high-quality of our samples, demonstrated by the high mobilities achieved and transparency of the contacts. Additionally, the 4 L PtSe2 device also exhibited a MIT at low carrier densities, with nC found to be 5.88 × 1010 cm−2 (Supplementary Fig. 7), the difference in nC compared to the 5 L PtSe2 suggests that narrowing of the bandgap facilitates achieving lower density MITs. At such low critical densities, carrier interactions are expected to drive the MIT and the strength of the Coulomb interaction between carriers can be characterized by the Wigner-Seitz radius, rS = m*/(m0aBε√πn) where m* = 0.21m0 is the effective mass of PtSe214 and aB is the Bohr radius. We find for the 5 L device rS ~ 20.8 at nC, comparable to high-quality 2D electron gas systems33, placing the system firmly in the strongly interacting regime (rS » 1). This strong interaction regime is accompanied by a low Fermi temperature TF = EF/kB ≈ 3.6 K, where EF = πℏ2n/ m*. Furthermore, we characterize the metallic phase as Fermi liquid-like (Supplementary Fig. 8), further indicating the strongly correlated nature of our system8.

We now turn our attention to the mechanism underlying the observed MIT. In 2D systems where strong carrier-carrier interactions are present, critical-like transport behavior can emerge near a density separating metallic and insulating regimes. Such behavior is often analyzed using scaling approaches formulated in the context of quantum phase transitions. In the present work, this scaling analysis is employed as a phenomenological characterization of finite-temperature critical-like transport, rather than as evidence of access to an asymptotic zero-temperature quantum critical regime.

When the temperature is rescaled appropriately for the metallic and insulating regimes, the renormalized conductivity collapses into two distinct branches. This phenomenon is illustrated in Fig. 4a, where data scaling from 50 to 300 K shows a clear bifurcation of the conductivity into an upper branch for the metallic phase and a lower branch for the insulating phase, near the transition. The successful scaling of the conductivity over a wide temperature range is consistent with interaction-dominated transport near the transition, reflecting the important role of strong carrier–carrier interactions in this regime. However, we note that scaling behavior alone does not uniquely determine the microscopic mechanism, as scaling-like behavior may also arise from other effects6. Moreover, the scaling collapse is observed over the temperature range 50–300 K, well above the estimated Fermi temperature at the critical density, and therefore limits the interpretation of the data within a zero-temperature QPT framework. However, when applying the scaling analysis across the full temperature range (1.5 K to 300 K), the data below 50 K does not conform well to the expected scaling (Supplementary Fig. 9). Similar deviations have been reported in MoTe2/WSe2 moiré superlattices35 and have been ascribed to the presence of disorder in the sample creating charge inhomogeneity. Interestingly, the temperature range at which the scaling fails, 1.5 to 50 K, coincides with the transition from the phonon-limited regime to the charge impurity scattering regime in the charge transport, as conveyed in Fig. 2e. This correlation suggests that the disorder introduced by charge impurities disrupts the role of strongly correlated electrons in driving the MIT, obscuring any potential quantum critical behavior and preventing the observation of a zero-temperature QPT. The validity of the scaling analysis can be verified by extracting the critical exponents. In Fig. 4b, T0 is fitted to the power law T0(δn)∝ | δn |zv, yielding zI vI ≈ 1.10 ± 0.19 and zM vM ≈ 1.01 ± 0.04, for the insulating and metallic sides, respectively. These values are consistent with those reported for WSe227 and Si-MOSFETs36,37. Additionally, the power laws σC(nC, T) ∝ Tx and σ0(δn, T = 0) ∝ δnµ are used to extract the exponents µ and x, as shown in Fig. 4c, d. The fittings yield values of x ≈ 1.25 ± 0.02 and µ ≈ 1.30 ± 0.05, and from standard scaling arguments, we obtain µ = (zv) ≈ 1.32 ± 0.14, which is in agreement with the value of µ obtained from the fitting in Fig. 4c. A value of µ ~ 1-1.5 is also typically associated with low-disorder, strongly interacting systems26. These consistencies further corroborate our observation of interaction-dominated transport and, together with the presence of ES-VRH demonstrated in Fig. 3b and high rS, provide strong evidence for the significant role of strongly correlated electrons in PtSe2.

Finally, we examine the nature of the MIT below 50 K. It follows, in systems undergoing a MIT driven by Coulomb disorder, a percolation-based MIT is expected as the carrier density is modulated, where the conductivity typically follows the relation, σ (n) = A(n – nCP)δ, with δ being the percolation exponent which is ≈ 1.33 for 2D systems and nCP the percolation threshold density. Fitting the percolation model to the data at 50 K, as displayed in Fig. 5a, yields δ = 1.65, which is in line with values reported for MoS25. In fact, we find that as the temperature is reduced towards 1.5 K, δ converges towards 4/3, and this relation is depicted in Fig.5a (inset), reaching 1.47 at T = 1.5 K. The trend indicates that percolation behavior becomes increasingly dominant as the temperature is lowered, suggesting a rise in disorder within the system. Similar converging behavior toward 1.33 as the temperature is reduced has also been observed in n-GaAs systems38. This observation directly correlates with the drastic increase in the effective charge trap density (Ntr) as the temperature falls below 50 K, as shown in Fig. 5a (inset). The effective charge-trap density, discerned from the subthreshold swing39 via the expression SS = (kBT/e) ln (10) (1 + (e2Ntr/Ci)), rises from 5.88 × 1012 cm−2eV−1 at T = 50 K to 7.20 × 1012 cm−2eV−1 at T = 1.5 K. This behavior coincides with the observed enhancement in charge impurity-scattering limiting the mobility as temperature falls below 50 K, as shown in Fig. 2e. Discussion

The fabrication of high-quality low band-gap PtSe2 devices has enabled the observation of a metal-insulator transition at very low carrier densities. As the system tends towards a charge-impurity scattering-dominated transport regime below 50 K, the failure of screening at low carrier densities gives rise to enhanced Coulomb disorder. This leads to a breakdown of finite-temperature scaling and a departure from the interaction-dominated transport regime. In disordered 2D systems, long-range Coulomb scattering introduces spatial inhomogeneities in the potential landscape (“puddles”) which disrupt coherent charge transport and give rise to a classical, percolation-driven transition40,41. The emergence of percolative behavior below 50 K, characterized by the extracted temperature-dependent percolation exponent δ, reflects a fundamental shift in the dominant mechanism governing charge transport in PtSe2, where charge impurity scattering begins to dominate, gradually overshadowing the electron-electron interactions at higher temperatures. This may explain the observed increase in Ntr as temperature is lowered, and charge-impurity scattering strengthens. The MIT can thus be delineated by two distinct regimes: a high temperature, low-disorder, interaction-dominated transport regime and a low temperature, high-disorder percolation-like regime. Although strong electron-electron interactions persist across both regimes, disorder ultimately dominates the MIT mechanism at lower temperatures, consistent with theoretical predictions7. This dual behavior of the MIT underscores the complex interplay between electronic interactions and disorder in determining the phase behavior of 2D materials. We further verified that these conclusions are reproduced under zero DC bias conditions (Supplementary Fig. 10), confirming that the observed dual character of the MIT is intrinsic to PtSe2 and not an artifact of finite-bias heating.

PtSe2 not only exhibits gate-voltage-driven MIT but also thickness-dependent and temperature-dependent transitions. Notably, the material demonstrates a crossover at a thickness of 6 L, where the MIT behavior transitions (Fig. 1e). Additionally, it shows a pronounced temperature-dependent conductance in the OFF state, as evidenced by the OFF current increasing by two orders of magnitude with rising temperature (Fig. 3a). The salience of 5 L PtSe2 is underlined by its critical carrier density being almost an order of magnitude lower than other TMD systems, as illustrated in Fig. 5b. This low nC value approaches those seen in ultrahigh-mobility GaAs MOSFETs and SiGe/Si/SiGe quantum wells, while the increase in nC for identically fabricated 4 L PtSe2 devices indicates a dependence on band gap. This underscores the advantage of multilayer PtSe2’s narrower bandgap in accessing the low-density, strongly interacting regime where quantum phase transitions emerge—conditions that are challenging to reach in wider-bandgap TMDs such as MoS2 or WSe2.

Together, these characteristics establish PtSe2 as a versatile material system, enabling controlled manipulation of transition mechanisms through variations in temperature, carrier density, and disorder. Furthermore, our achieved high mobilities and low contact resistance in PtSe2, along with advancements in its large-scale growth42, emphasize its promise for future electronic device applications.

Methods

Growth of crystals

For the synthesis of PtSe2, platinum powder (99.99%, SurePure, USA) and selenium granules (99.9999%, 2–4 mm, Wuhan Xinrong New Materials Co., USA) were used. The synthesis was carried out using a self-flux growth method in a quartz glass ampoule. Platinum and selenium, in amounts sufficient to produce 3 g of PtSe2, along with 2 at.% excess selenium to ensure stoichiometric balance, were placed in the ampoule (dimensions: 100 × 25 mm, 3 mm wall thickness). The ampoule was sealed under high vacuum (below 1 × 10⁻³ Pa, achieved using an oil diffusion pump and liquid nitrogen cold trap) and sealed with an oxygen-hydrogen welding torch. The ampoule was placed inside a corundum protective crucible (wall thickness >5 mm) within a muffle furnace equipped with SiC heating elements. The furnace was first heated to 800 °C at a rate of 1 °C/min. After maintaining this temperature for 25 h, the temperature was raised to 1280 °C over 4 h, then reduced to 1230 °C over 120 min, followed by a further reduction to 1000 °C over the next 120 min. The sample was left to cool to room temperature overnight. After cooling, the ampoule was opened in an argon-filled glovebox, and crystals were exfoliated from the resulting bulk material.

BN single crystals were obtained by using the temperature gradient method under high pressure and high temperature. Typical growth conditions were 3 GPa and 1500 ◦C for 120 h. A solvent of the Ba–BN system was used to obtain high-purity crystals. The source of BN crystals was heat treated so as to reduce oxygen impurities at 2000 ◦C under a nitrogen atmosphere. The recovered BN crystals were treated with strong acid (hot aqua regia) to remove residual solvent and washed with diluted water to supply them for the exfoliation process.

Sample preparation

Bottom hBN flakes ( ~ 30 nm) were exfoliated onto heavily doped 300 nm Si/SiO2 substrates, which served as global back gates. A bilayer PMMA resist was first spin-coated to produce an undercut profile for lithography. Hall-bar contacts were defined by electron-beam lithography, and the exposed hBN was etched by ~15 nm using CHF3/O2 deep reactive ion etching (40/4 sccm, 30 W). Ti/Pt (2/14 nm) pre-patterned electrodes were deposited by electron-beam evaporation, after which the substrates were annealed at 340 °C for 6 h in high vacuum ( ~ 1 × 10−6 mbar). A top hBN/PtSe2 heterostructure prepared by polycarbonate-based dry transfer was then laminated onto the pre-patterned contacts. Ti/Au (2/100 nm) metal leads were subsequently added in a second lithography step, followed by a final anneal under identical conditions. The wafers used are SSP PRIME GRADE Si wafers sourced from NOVA Electronic Materials LLC and have a custom resistivity specification ranging from 0.005 to 0.02 Ωcm. These wafers are heavily doped and highly conductive, suitable for FET devices.

Measurement

Cryogenic measurements were performed in a closed-cycle Oxford Instruments cryomagnetic system with a base temperature of ~ 1.5 K. During two-terminal charge transport measurements, drain currents were measured using a Keithley Sourcemeter 2450, and a Keithley Sourcemeter 2400 was used to apply gate bias through the SiO2 gate dielectric. Four-terminal charge transport measurements were performed by using a lock-in amplifier (Stanford Research SR830) at very low-frequency ( ~ 13 Hz).

First-principles calculations

Density functional theory calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP)43 utilizing plane-wave basis set and the projector-augmented wave (PAW) method44,45. Band structure calculations utilized the HSE06 hybrid exchange-correlation functional46. An energy cutoff of 250 eV, and a 9 × 9 × 1 uniform sampling of the Brillouin zone were used in the self-consistent field procedure. Periodic images of PtSe2 multilayers were separated by a 20 Å thick vacuum region. The atomic coordinates of the optimized computational models are provided as a separate Supplementary Data file.
