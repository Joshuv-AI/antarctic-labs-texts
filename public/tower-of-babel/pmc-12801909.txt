Correlation of admission sodium levels with 28-Day and 1-year mortality in STEMI patients undergoing emergency PCI
Authors: Qingqing Ruan, Zengyong Qiao, Xiumei Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the association between serum sodium levels within 24 hours of admission and short-term (28-day) and medium-term (1-year) all-cause mortality in patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) following emergency percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI).MethodsWe conducted a single-center retrospective cohort study including 532 patients with STEMI who underwent emergency PCI from January 2020 to January 2022. Patients were stratified into two groups based on admission serum sodium levels: <145 mmol/L (n=476) and ≥145 mmol/L (n=56). Restricted cubic spline models analyzed the non-linear relationship, and multivariable logistic regression assessed prognostic impact. ResultsAll-cause mortality rates were 2.2% (12/532) at 28 days and 2.8% (15/532) at 1 year. Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a U-shaped association between serum sodium and mortality (P < 0.001), with an inflection point at 143 mmol/L. Subgroup analyses confirmed stability across age, gender, and comorbidities. ConclusionsAdmission hypernatremia (≥145 mmol/L) is significantly associated with increased 28-day and 1-year mortality risks in STEMI patients post-emergency PCI. Early sodium management may improve outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05420-1.

Introduction

Acute ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) is a critical cardiovascular condition causing myocardial injury, where early diagnosis combined with reperfusion therapy significantly improves prognosis [1]. Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), as the preferred treatment strategy, can considerably reduce in-hospital mortality and major adverse cardiovascular events; however, the long-term prognosis of patients after PCI remains dependent on various factors [2, 3].

The association between electrolyte disturbances, particularly sodium imbalance, and cardiovascular outcomes has garnered increasing attention in recent years. Sodium is an essential ion maintaining osmotic pressure and cellular electrophysiological stability [4, 5]. Abnormal sodium levels may influence pathophysiological processes in STEMI through myocardial excitability and neurohumoral mechanisms. Hyponatremia (sodium < 135 mmol/L) demonstrates a significant association with in-hospital mortality in STEMI patients according to existing evidence [6, 7]. Conversely, hypernatremia (sodium ≥ 145 mmol/L, particularly in ICU settings) is also associated with elevated mortality risk [8, 9]. However, current research has predominantly focused on hyponatremia, with limited studies quantifying the effects of hypernatremia in STEMI [10, 11]. Furthermore, there are currently no large-scale clinical studies establishing this association with long-term mortality in post-emergency PCI patients [5].

This retrospective cohort study investigates the association between serum sodium levels within 24 h of hospital admission and all-cause mortality at 28 days and 1 year post-discharge in STEMI patients undergoing emergency PCI. The research aims to provide theoretical evidence for early intervention in dysnatremia (sodium imbalance), while also informing strategies to optimize comprehensive management of STEMI patients.

Materials and methods

Study population

This single-center retrospective cohort study analyzed 532 anonymized STEMI cases treated at Fengxian Central Hospital, Shanghai (Ethics Approval No. 2024-KY-26-02) between January 2020 and January 2022. Eligible patients met al.l the following criteria: (1) persistent chest pain >20 min; (2) ST-segment elevation in ≥ 2 contiguous leads with dynamic evolution of cardiac biomarkers per 2023 ESC STEMI guidelines [12]; (3) emergency PCI within 12 h of symptom onset; (4) serum sodium measurement within 24 h of admission. Exclusion criteria comprised: incomplete baseline data, non-STEMI acute coronary syndrome, non-emergency PCI management, and severe electrolyte disturbances (e.g., end-stage renal disease, decompensated cirrhosis).

Data acquisition

Clinical data of STEMI patients undergoing emergency PCI were collected through a structured electronic medical record system. Demographic characteristics included sex, age, and body mass index (BMI), while medical history encompassed coronary atherosclerosis, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and smoking history. Analyzed laboratory indicators comprised liver function tests, lipid profiles, renal function parameters, myocardial injury markers, inflammatory markers, and metabolic parameters. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was specifically assessed in cardiac function evaluation. The primary endpoints were all-cause mortality at 28-day and 1-year post-discharge for assessing short- and medium-term clinical outcomes.

Endpoints

The aim of this study was to assess all-cause mortality in STEMI patients post-emergency PCI. The primary endpoint was evaluated at two time points: 28-day and 1-year post-discharge. Endpoint events included all-cause mortality encompassing both cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular deaths.

Statistics

Data analysis was performed using R (version 4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and Free Statistics software version 1.9. The Shapiro-Wilk test assessed normality of continuous variables. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± SD, while non-normally distributed data as median (interquartile range). Cox proportional hazards regression models (unadjusted and multivariable-adjusted) were used to evaluate associations between serum sodium levels and mortality. Dose-response relationships were analyzed using adjusted restricted cubic spline plots with three knots. Stratified subgroup analyses were conducted by age, sex, BMI, and comorbidities. Interaction effects were assessed using likelihood ratio tests comparing models with and without interaction terms. Two-sided p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 532 STEMI patients (mean age 58.5 ± 13.2 years) underwent emergency PCI. The all-cause mortality rates were 2.2% (12/532) at 28 days and 2.8% (15/532) at 1 year. Based on admission sodium levels, patients were stratified into hypernatremia (serum sodium > 145 mmol/L) and normonatremia (135–145 mmol/L) groups. The hypernatremia group demonstrated significantly lower systolic blood pressure (P < 0.001), diastolic blood pressure (P = 0.001), and heart rate (P = 0.013). This group also exhibited elevated levels of aspartate aminotransferase (AST; P = 0.013), D-dimer (P < 0.001), and C-reactive protein (CRP; P = 0.029), along with significantly reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR; P = 0.001). Association between admission sodium and all-cause mortality in ST-elevation myocardial infarction

Survival curve analysis (Fig. 1) reveals a significant U-shaped nonlinear association between admission sodium levels and all-cause mortality in STEMI patients at 28 days and 1 year (P < 0.001). Threshold effect analysis (Table 2) indicates that when admission sodium is < 143 mmol/L, the odds of mortality decrease by 45% (OR = 0.545, 95% CI: 0.371–0.801) and by 40% (OR = 0.604, 95% CI: 0.438–0.834) at 28 days and 1 year, respectively. Conversely, when admission sodium is ≥ 143 mmol/L, the odds increase by 32% (OR = 1.316, 95% CI: 1.058–1.637) and by 26% (OR = 1.258, 95% CI: 1.024–1.546). Univariate regression (Table 3) identified ten risk factors including smoking history, AST, and eGFR (P < 0.05). Multivariate analysis (Table 4) confirmed that for every 1-mmol/L increase in admission sodium, the odds of mortality at 28 days and 1 year increased by 22% (OR = 1.220, 95% CI: 1.090–1.360) and 18% (OR = 1.182, 95% CI: 1.068–1.309), respectively. The high sodium group (≥ 145 mmol/L) was significantly associated with 1-year mortality risk (OR = 3.305, 95% CI: 1.328–8.223), while the 28-day risk showed borderline significance (OR = 2.742, 95% CI: 0.982–7.660). Sensitivity analysis and trend tests supported the robustness of the results (1-year P = 0.010).

Subgroup analysis

The selected variables for the subgroup analysis were driven by the following principles. Firstly, age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and smoking have been shown in multiple studies to be important factors affecting the prognosis of patients with STEMI [13–16]. Furthermore, current studies have demonstrated the obesity paradox in the STEMI population, i.e., overweight or mildly obese patients may have better outcomes compared to those with normal weight [17]. Considering these research findings, this study incorporated the aforementioned variables into the subgroup analysis to assess their potential influence on the prognosis of STEMI patients in detail. Standards for overweight and obesity among Asian adults divide the population into two subgroups based on BMI: <24 and ≥ 24 kg/m² [18].

Subgroup analysis (Figs. 2 and 3) revealed that the association between high admission sodium levels and all-cause mortality at 28 days and 1 year remained consistent across various subgroups, including age, sex, history of hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and smoking status. This association was significant in both unadjusted and adjusted models, with the odds ratios demonstrating a significant association between high admission sodium levels and mortality (P < 0.001). However, the relationship between high admission sodium levels and all-cause mortality showed statistically significant differences across BMI groups, particularly between the BMI < 24 group and the BMI ≥ 24 group (P < 0.05). This suggests that BMI may be an important effect modifier in the relationship between admission sodium levels and all-cause mortality.

Propensity score matching analysis

Given imbalanced baseline characteristics, we performed propensity score-matched analysis to assess the predictive value of admission sodium for 1-year mortality in STEMI patients post-PCI (Fig. 4). The ROC curve demonstrated the sensitivity-specificity tradeoff, with an AUC of 0.670 (95% CI: 0.598–0.742). The optimal cutoff (Youden index = 0.1) yielded 38.2% sensitivity and 89.3% specificity. Figure 5 displays standardized mean differences across adjustment methods (unmatched, matched, and inverse probability weighting), with the x-axis showing SMD values and y-axis listing covariates. After matching, although the association did not reach statistical significance (P > 0.05), we observed a trend toward increased mortality risk with higher sodium levels.

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study demonstrated a U-shaped association between admission serum sodium levels and all-cause mortality in STEMI patients undergoing emergency PCI, with a nadir of risk at 143 mmol/L identified via restricted cubic spline analysis. Multivariate adjustment revealed that both hyponatremia and hypernatremia were independently associated with increased 28-day and 1-year mortality risks, suggesting that dysnatremia is independently associated with adverse outcomes following primary PCI. These findings highlight the clinical utility of incorporating early sodium assessment into risk stratification protocols for this population.

Hypernatremia is a common electrolyte disorder encountered in clinical practice. The pathophysiology includes dehydration, renal failure, and sodium overload. It elevates cardiovascular risk by promoting water-sodium retention, blood volume expansion, and blood pressure increase [19]. Cohort studies indicate that increased dietary sodium intake is positively associated with cardiovascular events (a 6% risk increase per 1 g/day increment). Furthermore, a J-shaped association exists between serum sodium levels and cardiovascular events, with the lowest risk observed between 141 and 143 mmol/L [20–22]. Elevated sodium load can exacerbate heart failure progression and significantly increase mortality risk in specific populations such as patients with diabetes mellitus or COVID-19 [23–25]. This study is the first to establish the independent prognostic value of admission hypernatremia in STEMI patients following emergency PCI, providing critical evidence for clinical risk assessment.

STEMI, as the most critical clinical type of acute coronary syndrome, presents significant challenges in improving long-term prognosis for cardiovascular disease prevention and treatment [26–28]. Although emergency PCI has significantly reduced acute-phase mortality; however, the all-cause mortality rate remains elevated between 30 days and 1 year post-procedure [26–28]. Serum sodium levels, serving as biomarkers of volume status and neuroendocrine activation, demonstrate controversial associations with STEMI prognosis: while some studies link hyponatremia to adverse outcomes [29, 30], others report no significant associations [31], potentially due to study heterogeneity [31]. Our multicenter cohort analysis revealed that among STEMI patients undergoing emergency PCI, those with serum sodium levels ≥ 143 mmol/L at 24 h post-PCI exhibited 2.3-fold and 1.8-fold increased risks of all-cause mortality at 28 days and 1 year, respectively. After multivariable adjustment, serum sodium ≥ 145 mmol/L was identified as an independent mortality predictor. We propose integrating dynamic sodium monitoring into STEMI prognostic assessments and establishing 143 mmol/L as a clinical intervention threshold for peri-procedural electrolyte management.

This study revealed a U-shaped nonlinear relationship between serum sodium levels and all-cause mortality at 28 days and 1 year post-STEMI. Effective interventions can increase serum sodium levels. Each 1 mmol/L increment in serum sodium was associated with 46% and 40% lower risks of 28-day and 1-year mortality when levels were < 143 mmol/L. Conversely, in patients with serum sodium ≥ 143 mmol/L, each 1 mmol/L increase corresponded to 32% and 26% higher mortality risks at 28 days and 1 year, respectively. These findings suggest both hyponatremia and hypernatremia may adversely affect clinical outcomes.Based on these results, we recommend implementing sodium-level-specific management strategies. For patients with serum sodium < 143 mmol/L, intensive monitoring and hyponatremia correction through fluid replacement should be considered. Those with serum sodium ≥ 143 mmol/L, particularly with persistent hypernatremia, require comprehensive evaluation for potential dehydration, renal dysfunction, or other etiologies, coupled with targeted interventions to mitigate hypernatremia-related risks.

The prognostic impact of hypernatremia involves multisystem mechanisms: (1) Sodium-water imbalance increases cardiac workload through renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) activation [32, 33]; (2) Cardiovascular stress elevates myocardial oxygen demand and exacerbates metabolic derangements [34, 35]; (3) The inflammation-electrolyte crosstalk establishes a pro-inflammatory positive feedback loop via NOD-, LRR- and pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome formation [36]; (4) Myocardial remodeling promotes fibrotic progression and ventricular dysfunction through Ca2+/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII) pathway activation [37–41]. These interconnected pathways collectively constitute the pathophysiological basis for adverse outcomes.

The result of the subgroup analysis shows that there was a difference in the relationship of serum sodium and all-cause mortality based on BMI. Especially in individuals with a BMI < 24 kg/m², the relationship of hypernatremia and all-cause mortality was stronger. As per the findings, patients with low BMI may be more sensitive to sodium levels which, in turn, may lead to sodium-related adverse effects. As a result, stricter serum sodium monitoring and might be necessary for STEMI patients with a low BMI. In particular, the treatment of these patients should involve closer scrutiny of changes in serum sodium with appropriate correction to avoid having an adverse impact on patient prognosis due to abnormal sodium levels. Conversely, future research could look into sodium management protocols by BMI and examine their value in improving survival rates in low BMI patients. This will help in tailoring individual treatment strategies. Furthermore, it may also help to improve the long-term outcome of patients with STEMI.

The main innovation of this study is demonstrated in the following aspects: First, it reveals a U-shaped nonlinear association between serum sodium levels and all-cause mortality at 28 days and 1 year in STEMI patients undergoing emergency PCI. The analytical approach enabled systematic investigation of hypernatremia, which was identified as an independent prognostic factor in this population. The clinical intervention threshold was established at 143 mmol/L, with an independent risk cutoff ≥ 145 mmol/L, providing quantifiable evidence for electrolyte management during the acute phase of PCI. Notably, the analysis provides novel evidence that BMI modulates the prognostic impact of sodium dysregulation, supporting a BMI-stratified management approach. These findings advance theoretical frameworks for developing personalized sodium monitoring protocols, particularly for patients with BMI < 24 kg/m².

Limitations of this study

This study has several limitations. Initially, we only used sodium levels at admission, not sodium levels during hospitalization and therapeutic interventions occurring during the hospitalization that potentially impacts electrolyte balance. This could mean we overlooked the dynamic prognostic value of sodium fluctuations. Moreover, not collecting data on important confounding factors like fluid resuscitation strategies and the use of diuretics and rennin-angiotensin system inhibitors may lead to residual confounding bias. The third factor that limits the generalizability of the results and the statistical power for rare events is the fact that they are from one medical center. In the future multicenter prospective studies should include continuous sodium metrics and treatment datat o further elucidate these links.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a U-shaped association between admission serum sodium levels and mortality risk in STEMI patients undergoing emergency PCI, with the lowest mortality observed at 143 mmol/L. Multivariable analysis confirmed hypernatremia (≥ 145 mmol/L) as an independent predictor of increased 28-day and 1-year all-cause mortality. These findings underscore the prognostic significance of early sodium assessment, particularly in low-BMI patients, and support integrating sodium monitoring into risk stratification protocols with 143 mmol/L as a potential intervention threshold. Limitations include the single-center design and lack of longitudinal sodium-level tracking. Future multicenter studies should validate these findings and explore mechanisms linking dysnatremia to adverse outcomes in acute coronary syndromes.
