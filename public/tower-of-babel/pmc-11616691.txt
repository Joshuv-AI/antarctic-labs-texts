Fish-inspired dynamic charging for ultrafast self-protective solar-thermal energy storage
Authors: Xiaoxiang Li, Jingyi Zhang, Yizhe Liu, Yangzhe Xu, Yixuan Xie, Ting Hu, Benwei Fu, Chengyi Song, Wen Shang, Peng Tao, Tao Deng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Solar-thermal energy storage (STES) within solid-liquid phase change materials (PCMs) has emerged as an attractive solution to overcome intermittency of renewable energy. However, current storage systems usually suffer from slow charging rates, sacrificed storage capacity, and overheating tendency. Inspired by the thermoregulation behavior of Cyprinid fish, here, we present a quick-responsive, ultrafast, large-capacity, overheating-protective STES strategy. We fabricate a liquid-infused solar-absorbing foam charger that can rapidly advance the receding solid-liquid charging interface to efficiently store solar-thermal energy as latent heat and spontaneously float upward to cease the charging process upon overheating. This bioinspired dynamic charging is adaptable to a variety of PCMs, unlocking the potential for safe and efficient utilization of renewable thermal energy.

INTRODUCTION

Solar-thermal conversion has emerged as a vital technology to power carbon-neutral sustainable development of human society because of its high energy conversion efficiency and increasing global heating consumption need (1–4). Latent heat solar-thermal energy storage (STES) offers a promising cost-effective solution to overcome intermittency of solar irradiation and provide stable heating supply owing to narrow heat storage/release temperature range, high energy density, and abundance of heat storage phase change materials (PCMs) (5–8). Like electric battery technology (9, 10), the desired STES systems should have large latent heat storage capacity and high storage efficiency and can be rapidly charged in large volume without causing safety concerns (11–16). Neat PCMs including both organic paraffin wax (PW) and inorganic salts, however, suffer from poor solar absorptance and low thermal conductivity (k), which severely limit the harvesting performances of current STES systems (17–19).

To accelerate the solar-thermal harvesting process, solar-absorbing high-k fillers such as expanded graphite (20, 21), carbon nanotubes (CNTs) (22, 23), graphene (24), and MXene (25, 26) have been compounded with PCMs, but only moderate enhancement of effective k and charging rates was achieved because of the inevitable interfacial thermal resistance between fillers and PCM matrix (27). Although improved k has been achieved through incorporating three-dimensional (3D) graphite foam (28, 29), CNT sponge (30), hybrid graphite-CNT foam (31), carbonized biomass (32), hybrid silver flake and CNT (33), and metal-organic frameworks (34, 35), these high-k fillers usually sacrifice the latent heat storage capacity and seriously weaken the cost advantage (36). Moreover, the charging rates quickly decay with heat-transfer distances, which prevent the employment of the k-enhancement strategy in large-volume PCM systems (37). Under high-flux irradiation, the converted solar-thermal energy could not be timely stored as the desired latent heat, and the accumulated heat could cause overheating and other safety issues.

Over billions of years of natural selection, biological species have evolved distinct thermal regulation strategies and provided ample inspiration for improving the STES performances of engineering systems (37–40). One intriguing feature of biological species is that they can intelligently regulate STES as needed (41). For instance, the cold-blooded Cyprinus carpio fish can rely on the dark scales to quickly warm up their body during sun basking and can dynamically regulate their body temperature by controlling the floating depths (42). By contrast, most often, the engineering STES systems are bulky and static, although intensive research efforts have been devoted to improving their performances (43–46). To date, it remains a formidable challenge to explore biomimetic fast-responsive, rapid-charging, large-capacity, dynamically controllable STES within PCMs.

Here, inspired by the intelligent thermoregulation behavior of fish, we present a strategy for ultrafast, overheating-protective STES within PCMs by using a flexible liquid-infused polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS)–graphite (LPG) foam with tunable density as the movable charger (Fig. 1). The vertical macropores and connected micropores within the LPG foam not only efficiently capture incident solar photons achieving a high solar absorptance of ~97% but also can be infused with ~150 wt % of liquids such as water to tune the effective density of the foam charger. Under direct solar illumination (0.2 W/cm2), the flexible LPG foam, driven by gravity, can adhere to the surface of the solid PCMs, steadily advance the receding solid-liquid charging interface with a consistent high speed of ~0.66 mm/min, and store solar-thermal energy as the desired latent heat with a high efficiency of ~92.9% while fully retaining the latent heat storage capacity of original PCMs. When charging under concentrated solar illumination, the gravity-driven sinking of LPG foam enables ultrafast charging without safety concerns. When there is occurrence of excessive heating over the boiling point of the infused liquids, the LPG foam automatically floats upward (fig. S1), which, in turn, connects the microslide switch and triggers emergent stop of the charging process. Such bioinspired dynamic sinking and floating-based charging offers a widely applicable strategy for ultrafast, large-capacity, smartly controllable STES within a variety of PCMs.

RESULTS

Fabrication and characterization of LPG foam charger

Sun-basking is a unique thermoregulation behavior for fish to attain higher body temperatures that enables faster growth (Fig. 2A) (42). The cold-blooded fish such as Carassius auratus typically have evolved dark fish scales around the dorsal fin, which could directly absorb sunlight and increase body temperature (Fig. 2B). Those dark fish scales are composed of micro/nanostructured surface features (fig. S2) (47, 48), which could enhance absorption of solar photons and achieve efficient solar-thermal conversion (Fig. 2C). To regulate body temperature, fish adjust their depth in rivers to tune the amount of captured solar energy (fig. S3). Swim bladders, which are commonly filled with nitrogen and oxygen gases and act as a sort of floater, are crucial for fish to balance their density and control their floating depth in rivers (49–51). For example, swim bladders with a low density of ~0.139 g/cm3 can act like a balloon and lift weights several times of its body (Fig. 2D).

Inspired by the ecological depth-control and sun-basking behavior of Cyprinid fish, we fabricate density-variable LPG foams as the charger for dynamic STES within PCMs. As schemed in Fig. 2E, the LPG foam with hierarchical pores was prepared by using water-soluble ground salts and vertically aligned needles as dual templates. The ground salts could restrict the flow of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) precursor and help retain the preset shape of the composites before curing (fig. S4). The ground salts can be easily washed away to produce numerous pores inside the composites. To explore the suitable particle size of ground salts, two different salts with an average diameter of ~500 and ~50 μm were chosen for fabricating the LPG foam (fig. S5). Compared with the composites templated with large-size salts (500 μm), the one templated with small-size ground salts (~50 μm) have lower transmission of incident solar light (fig. S6). Through varying the content of nano-graphite within the porous composites, an optimum loading of 5 wt % was identified to achieve an average solar absorption of ~93% (fig. S7). In addition, the thickness of the foam charger could influence the charging performance. To achieve an optimal balance between high solar absorption and good mechanical flexibility, a thickness of ~1 mm was selected for subsequent experiments (figs. S8 and S9).

We press needle arrays into uncured PDMS precursor and salt mixture to further introduce vertically aligned macropores (fig. S10). The hierarchical pores simultaneously improve solar absorptance and mechanical flexibility of the LPG foam as evidenced by its dark appearance and deformability (Fig. 2F). Scanning electron microscopy observation indicates the coexistence of vertical macropores with an average diameter of ~240 to 360 μm and interconnected micropores with an average diameter of ~30 to 60 μm within the foam (Fig. 2G and fig. S11). The vertical macropores could further enhance the capture of solar photons due to the multiple-scattering and reabsorption effect (52, 53), resulting in high solar absorption of ~97% (Fig. 2H). Under direct solar illumination with a power density of 0.1 and 0.2 W/cm2 for 5 min, such PDMS-graphite (PG) foam could efficiently convert incident solar photons into heat and the whole composite was uniformly heated up from room temperature to 78.5° and 115.2°C, respectively (fig. S12). The hierarchical porous structure retained its integrity when the PG foam was heated at 300°C for more than 30 min (fig. S13).

In the meantime, the porous PG foam can be infused with more than 150 wt % of water due to the strong capillary force provided by the interconnected micropores (Fig. 2I). Through heating up the LPG foam, the liquid water expands and the effective density of the LPG foam gradually decreases. When the heating temperature rises to 100°C, the contained water boils generating numerous bubbles. The resultant density of the LPG foam quickly drops and becomes lower than that of melted PW (0.8 g/cm3), which enables spontaneous upward floating of the LPG foam within the melted PCM (Fig. 2J).

Dynamic solar-thermal energy charging performances

We compared the static and dynamic STES performances by directly shedding solar illumination onto PW, which was loaded with dispersed nano-graphite particles, a 3D copper foam, and the movable LPG foam under the same power density of 0.2 W/cm2 (Fig. 3A). Time-sequential infrared images in Fig. 3B show that, although nano-graphite particles could convert incident sunlight into heat and induce melting of PW in initial 10 min, further charging was limited by the low-k of PW composite (~0.3 W/m K). As a result, the converted solar-thermal energy was accumulated at the top portion of PW and less than 50% of PW was melted after charging for 50 min (Fig. 3C). Compared with discrete nano-graphite particles, the high-k copper foam could effectively improve heat transfer (table S1) and led to more uniform temperature distribution in the charged sample. However, it took ~20 min to warm up the whole composite before initiating the melting of PW (Fig. 3D). In addition, the latent heat storage enthalpy of the PCM system is reduced from 140 to 70.3 J/g due to the heavy loading (~50 wt %) of the copper foam (Fig. 3E).

Under the dynamic charging mode, the LPG foam could continuously advance the solid-liquid phase change interface and avoid long-distance thermal diffusion in low-k PCMs. Under the same illumination conditions, all the PW was melted by the movable LPG foam achieving uniform temperature distribution (temperature difference < 5°C) (Fig. 3C). Both experimental measurement and simulation results indicate that the dynamic charging has rapid thermal response and consistent fast charging rates and can retain the original latent heat of pure PW (Fig. 3, D and E). Benefiting from the rapid charging rate and large latent heat storage capacity, the calculated average phase-change STES efficiency reached 92.9% (table S2). The amount of solar-thermal energy stored as desired latent heat increased from 159 and 212 J for the PW loaded with static nano-graphite particles and the static copper foam to 350 J in the dynamic charging system (Fig. 3F).

Rapid scalable harvesting of solar-thermal energy

Because of the density difference between solid and liquid state, PW and many other PCMs usually form surface shrinkage voids after solidification (54). The uneven surface would restrict direct tight contact between the rigid solar-thermal charger and the PCM, and the low-k air gap limits the charging process in early stage (Fig. 4A). Different from conventional rigid carbon foam, the LPG foam is soft and deformable (Fig. 4B and fig. S14) and can accommodate itself to uneven surfaces to form close contact (Fig. 4C and fig. S15). After solar illumination (0.2 W/cm2) for 5 min, Fig. 4D presents that the poor contact between the rigid carbon foam and PW restricts STES within PW and leads to a high temperature of 74.3°C in the center of the top surface, which is much higher than the PCM charged by the flexible LPG foam (56.1°C). Time-sequential temperature monitoring shows that the temperature of the middle portion of the carbon foam rises to more than 70°C within 10 s, whereas the edge of the rigid foam that is in direct contact with the PW is only heated up by less than 5°C (fig. S16). In comparison, the flexible LPG foam shows a much smaller temperature difference (<10°C) between the center and the edge during the initial 5 min (Fig. 4E). Benefiting from the intimate contact, the weight of PW melted by the flexible LPG foam is 375% more than that melted by the rigid carbon foam in the initial charging period (Fig. 4F). Figure 4G presents that the flexible LPG foam can consistently advance the charging interface during repeated melting/solidification for 50 cycles. Such self-adaptive feature of flexible LPG foam enables rapid thermal response during repeated charging of PCMs with uneven surfaces.

Like fish that can freely sink to the bottom of the river, the LPG foam could also continuously track the receding solid-liquid charging interface within bulk PCMs (Fig. 4H). Theoretical simulation shows that the dynamic STES system had consistent high charging rates and instant thermal response when the volume of charged PCM was enlarged by ~30 times (fig. S17). The infrared images in Fig. 4I present that accelerated charging of bulk PCM was realized not only within PW but also in other PCMs with different phase change temperatures such as stearic acid (SA) and erythritol (ET). After solar illumination with a power density of ~0.25 and ~0.5 W/cm2 for 180 and 190 min, all the PCMs (50 g of SA and 80 g of ET) were fully melted by the LPG foam, whereas only a small portion of SA (~17.5 g) and ET (~27.9 g) were melted by the fixed rigid carbon foam. The dynamic sinking of the LPG foam within these PCMs achieves nearly constant charging rates over long distances and uniform temperature distribution in the charged PCMs (fig. S18). The temperature fluctuation of PW charged by flexible LPG foam is only ~7.5°C, which is much smaller than the one charged by rigid carbon foam (35°C) (Fig. 4J). The averaged solar-thermal charging rates and the corresponding stored latent heat within different PCMs are more than doubled (Fig. 4, K and L). In addition, the dynamic charging system retained ~100% of the latent heat storage capacity of the original large-volume PCMs (Fig. 4M).

Ultrafast self-protective charging

Besides the diving movement, fish can freely swim upward to the water surface to capture sunlight during the day time (Fig. 5A). To mimic the floating behavior enabled by the swim bladder, we introduced low-density vapor bubbles by heating the LPG foam above the boiling point of the infused liquid. The LPG foam is sensitive to the surrounding temperature change, which could quickly stimulate density change and control its up-and-down motion within the melted PCMs (movie S1). Alternatively, after receiving concentrated solar illumination (1 W/cm2) for 20 s, the LPG foam was intensively heated up generating numerous bubbles (Fig. 5B). The generated bubbles lift the fish-shaped LPG away from the bottom and drive it through the melted PW to the air/PCM interface. Through repeatedly turning on/off the incident concentrated sunlight, the gravity-driven downward sinking and bubble-driven upward floating movement of the LPG foam can be dynamically controlled (movie S2).

The bubble-driven movement offers a facile way to control the movement of LPG foam through concentrated solar light, enabling motions such as blooming like a flower or flipping letters (Fig. 5C). Under charging illumination of 0.2 W/cm2, the temperature of the LPG foam is lower than the boiling point of the infused water, and there is no bubble generation (Fig. 5D). Under this circumstance, the LPG foam, with a larger density than PW, can continuously sink along with the moving charging interface to fully melt PW. After concentrating the illumination intensity to 1 W/cm2, the water inside the LPG foam was violently vaporized, which helped generate buoyant bubbles and lift the foam charger to the surface within 40 s. Such floating of the LPG foam enables timely stop of the solar-thermal charging process within tens of seconds (fig. S19). Figure 5E presents that the infused water can sustain the repeated sinking and floating of the LPG foam within melted PW for more than 50 cycles. When the density-modulation liquid is exhausted, it can be readily reloaded by simply immersing the PG foam within the liquid. The reloaded LPG foam has demonstrated consistent gravity-driven dynamic charging and up-floating charger recycling behaviors.

The temperature-sensitive automatic floating of the LPG foam can prevent potential overheating of the charged PCMs when undergoing ultrafast charging under high-flux solar illumination (Fig. 5F). Figure 5G presents that, when the solar flux increases from 0.2 to 0.4 W/cm2, there is no obvious increase of the static charging rate due to limited heat transfer within the low-k PW. By contrast, the dynamic charging rate approximately linearly increases from ~0.66 to ~1.02 and ~1.31 mm/min with the increasing solar illumination power density, proving its capability for achieving ultrafast charging under high-flux solar illumination.

To realize automatic overheating protection, we designed a microslide switch, which has no current flow during normal dynamic charging processes due to its open circuit state (fig. S20). Once the temperature of LPG foam exceeds the boiling point of the infused liquid, the LPG foam floats to the surface of PCMs, closes the circuit, and triggers the alarm system (fig. S21). Under such circumstances, the abnormal charging process is quickly reset to avoid the overheating of PCMs. Figure 5H shows that the flexible LPG foam can adapt to different charging power density and realize programmable, fast-responsive, rapid direct STES within PCMs without occurring overheating. Specifically, under low-flux solar illumination (0.2 W/cm2) for the initial 10 min, the flexible LPG foam shows a rapid thermal response. In the subsequent ultrafast charging under the solar flux of 0.4 W/cm2, the charging interface rapidly advances until all the PW was melted. When further prolonging the illumination duration or switching to high-flux illumination (1 W/cm2), excessive heating induces automatic floating of LPG foam and triggers the alarming system to reset the charging process. Benefiting from the automatic alarming protection, the overheating degree (ΔTo), which is defined as the temperature difference between the maximum heating temperature of the PCM (Tmax) and the melting point (Tm), was only 18.3°C for the dynamic charging system, while the ΔTo for the fixed charging system reached 89.4°C within 2 min.

Figure 5I presents that, compared with the fixed charging system, the ΔTo for the dynamically charged PW was notably reduced under different solar intensities. In particular, the bioinspired dynamic system could automatically trigger overheating protection when the charging solar density was higher than 0.6 W/cm2. Such automatic floating limited the ΔTo within 20°C and prevented overheating-induced safety issues such as severe volatilization, oxidation, and even burning of organic PCMs (fig. S22). In comparison with fixed charging of PCM composites, the fish-inspired dynamic charging system minimizes the sensible heating of melted PCM and fillers due to suppressed overheating, thereby maximizing latent heat storage (supplementary text, note 3) and achieving unprecedented charging rates under high-flux solar illumination without causing safety issues (Fig. 5J).

By infusing the LPG foam with other density-modulation liquids (n-hexanol), fast dynamic charging with smart thermal protection has also been realized in other PCM systems such as sugar alcohols (fig. S23 and table S4). Different boiling points of those alternative density-modulation liquids would enable fine tuning of the thermal protection temperature for the ultrafast dynamic charging systems. Owing to the overheating protection by the dynamic charging process, the charged PCMs were rapidly and uniformly heated to temperatures only slightly higher than their melting points. The small overheating degree is beneficial for avoiding potential degradation of organic PCMs (55, 56). No thermal/photoinduced denaturation and decomposition of PW, SA, and ET was observed during the repeated charging/discharging cycles (figs. S24 and S25). After completing one cycle of charging, the LPG foam charger can be readily recycled through shedding concentrated solar illumination that triggers the upward floating of the foam charger. The floated LPG foam charger can be manually picked up or magnetically fixed at the top surface, supporting efficient continuous charge-discharge cycles (figs. S26 and S27). With the help of external thermal insulation, the charged PCMs can retain their melted state during storage. The liquid retention duration could last for ~5 min for the uninsulated PCM system loaded with tens of grams of PW, and the retention duration could be tripled after thermally insulating the charged system with melamine sponges (fig. S28). To release the stored thermal energy, the charged PCMs can be readily coupled with a metal spiral tube–based heat exchanger. By regulating the flow rate of the heat-exchange fluids, the heat-releasing rate from the charged PCMs can be tuned to provide stable supply of hot water and thermal energy for domestic use. For the charged system loaded with 20 g of PW pumping through room-temperature water, the latent heat–releasing duration decreased from ~22 to ~10 min when the flow rate increased from 1 to 2.6 ml/min (fig. S29). The bioinspired dynamic charging strategy is, therefore, anticipated to be applicable for intelligent latent heat STES storage within a variety of PCMs, offering a reliable and efficient thermal energy supply.

DISCUSSION

Inspired by the thermoregulation behavior of fish, we reported a dynamic charging strategy for direct, ultrafast, large-capacity, safe STES within various PCMs by using multifunctional LPG foam that simultaneously has high solar absorptance (~97%), mechanical flexibility, and large adsorption capacity of density-modulation liquids (~150 wt %) as the charger. Driven by gravity, the flexible solar-thermal LPG foam can form intimate contact with solid PCMs, instantaneously induce melting, and continuously advance the solid/liquid charging interface. The dynamic charging avoids the long-distance heat transfer from the melted liquid phase to the nonmelted solid phase, thereby speeding up the charging process and achieving high-efficiency uniform STES within large-volume PCMs. The temperature-sensitive automatic floating of LPG foam not only enables recycling of the charger and timely stop of the charging process, but also avoids potential overheating issues when the PCMs undergo ultrafast charging under high-flux solar illumination. Such bioinspired dynamic charging strategy offers a promising solution for high-performance intelligent harvesting of abundant solar-thermal energy within a variety of PCMs.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

Nano-graphite was purchased from XFNANO Industrial Corporation (Jiangsu, China). PDMS was purchased from Zhantu Chemical Industrial Corporation (Jiangsu, China). NaCl, n-hexane, acetone, and n-hexanol were provided by Guoyao Industrial Corporation (Shanghai, China). PW and SA were purchased from Aladdin Reagent (Shanghai, China). ET was provided by Macklin (Shanghai, China). The typical fish specimen (J4101) was brought from Yangrui Education Corporation (Jiangsu, China).

Methods

Fabrication of LPG foam

The LPG foam was fabricated by a dual-template method. In a typical experiment, NaCl salts were manually ground for 5 min to obtain fine NaCl powders and were further sieved by a 100-mesh sifter. To prepare PG composites, first, 10 g of ground NaCl powders, 2 g of PDMS, 0.2 g of curing agent, 0.1 g of nano-graphite, and 15 ml of n-hexane were uniformly mixed. After evaporating the n-hexane from the mixture, the PG composite slurry was poured into a plastic petri dish. A tightly aligned needle template was then pressed onto the uncured composite to introduce vertical holes. The PG composite together with the needle template was cured by heating within an oven at 80°C for 24 hours. The LPG foam was obtained after washing out NaCl salts with water and loading with density-modulation liquid (water for charging of PW and SA). For dynamic charging in ET, the PG composite was prepared by similar procedures. The raw materials include 10 g of ground NaCl powders, 2 g of PDMS, 0.2 g of curing agent, 2 g of MnO2 powders, 0.1 g of nano-graphite, and 15 ml of n-hexane. After removing the NaCl salts, the LPG foam was lastly obtained after loading the PG foam with n-hexanol that has a higher boiling point than water.

Characterization and property measurement

The micromorphology of PG foam was detected by a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FEG Quanta 250). The phase change behavior of PW, SA, ET, and other PCM composites was characterized by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC 2500, TA Instruments Co. Ltd.) with a heating and cooling rate of 10°C/min under inert nitrogen flow (20 ml/min). Thermal stability of PG foam was characterized by a thermogravimetric analyzer (SDT-Q600, TA Instruments Co. Ltd.). Optical absorption spectra were detected by an ultraviolet–visible–near infrared spectrometer (PerkinElmer, Lambda 950, USA) equipped with an integrating sphere. The real-time temperature distribution of different samples was measured by an infrared camera (FLIR T640, FLIR Systems Inc.). Stress-strain curves of LPG foam and rigid carbon foam were measured by a dynamic mechanical analyzer (DMA Q850, TA Instruments Co. Ltd.) with a speed of 1 mm/min at room temperature. 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were recorded on a 400-MHz NMR spectrometer (Avance III, Bruker). The 1H NMR chemical shifts were reported in parts per million (ppm) relative to the residual protons signal in the deuterated solvent [CDCl3 at 7.26 ppm and (CD3)2SO at 2.5 ppm]. The infrared spectra of PCMs were analyzed using a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Nicolet 6700, Thermo Fisher Scientific) over the wavenumber range of 400 to 4000 cm−1. The density of the LPG foam was measured by immersing the foam within melted PW, which was sealed in an evacuated Erlenmeyer flask and was then put in an oven with a series of preset temperatures (60°, 70°, 80°, 90°, 100°, and 110°C). After the whole system was uniformly heated to the preset temperature, the height of PW in the capillary was recorded at least three times every 5 min.

Fixed and dynamic solar-thermal charging experiment

To compare the fixed and dynamic solar-thermal charging performance, 2.5 g of PW loaded with 5 wt % of nano-graphite, 50 wt % of copper foam, and the LPG foam (0.03 g) were placed within a cuvette (10 mm by 10 mm by 45 mm), respectively. A solar simulator (TRM-PD, Jinzhou Sunshine Technology Co. Ltd.) was used to generate sunlight with tunable power density (0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, 1, and 1.2 W/cm2). The power density of sunlight was measured by an optical power densitometer (CEL-NP2000, Zhongjiao Technology Co. Ltd.).

Scalable STES within different PCMs

To investigate the charging behavior of PCMs with shrinkage cavities, two different foams (a rigid carbon foam and a flexible LPG foam) were placed on the top surface of PW and were directly illuminated by a solar simulator with a power density of 0.2 W/cm2 for 5 min. To explore STES within large-volume PCMs, the rigid carbon foam and the flexible LPG foam with the same diameter of ~35 mm were used as the fixed and dynamic charger to charge solar-thermal energy within bulk PCMs including PW (50 g), SA (50 g), and ET (80 g) under a power density of ~0.2, ~0.25, and ~ 0.5 W/cm2, respectively.

Controllable movement of LPG foam

The sinking and floating behavior of LPG foam was investigated by recording the movement under different solar illumination power intensity. Under normal illumination (~0.2 W/cm2), the LPG foam sank along with the receding charging interface. Under concentrated sunlight (≥0.8 W/cm2), the density-modulation liquids within LPG foam underwent liquid-gas phase transition continuously generating vapor bubbles to propel the floating motion of the LPG foam. After turning off the solar simulator, the LPG foam gradually sank to the bottom driven by gravity. Through repeatedly turning on/off the solar simulator, the dynamic floating and sinking behavior of the LPG foam was recorded by a video recorder.

Thermal energy release from the charged PCM

A copper spiral tube (outer diameter, 2 mm; wall thickness, 0.5 mm) was used as a heat exchanger and coupled with the storage system to extract the stored thermal energy. Cold heat transfer fluids (water, ~24°C) were continuously pumped through the heat exchanger. The flow rate of the heat transfer fluid (1, 1.7, and 2.6 ml/min) was controlled by a peristaltic pump (BT300-2J, Longer Pump). The real-time temperature variation of charged PW (20 g) and heat transfer fluids was measured by thermocouples connected with a data collector (34972A LXI Data Acquisition/Switch Unit, Agilent).
