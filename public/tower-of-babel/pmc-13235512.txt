Acral lentiginous melanoma metastases to the vitreous masquerading as uveitis: A case report
Authors: Rohini Chahal, Pranati Ahuja, Rozhna Al-Zangana, Zoya Arain, Patricia Chévez-Barrios, Maria De la Garza Bravo, Kelly L Larkin, Dan S Gombos, Amina Lateef Chaudhry, Nagham Al-Zubidi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeCutaneous melanoma is the most common malignancy to present with vitreous metastasis. This report describes a case of vitreous metastasis from acral lentiginous melanoma masquerading as intermediate uveitis in a patient undergoing checkpoint inhibitor (CPI) therapy.ObservationsThis is an 86-year-old Caucasian female with Stage III Acral Lentiginous Melanoma of the left heel who was initiated on nivolumab and relatlimab along with intralesional talimogene laherparepvec (TVEC). Eight months later she developed blurry vision and floaters. She was diagnosed elsewhere with unilateral acute intermediate uveitis, leading to discontinuation of immunotherapy and initiation of topical difluprednate. Extensive infectious and autoimmune work-up including MRI of the brain and orbits was negative. Failing to improve, she presented to our hospital. Exam of the right eye showed best corrected visual acuity 20/50, intraocular pressure of 16 mmHg, keratic precipitates, 1+ anterior cells, and 2+ large amelanotic vitreous cells arranged in veils. The left eye was quiet. Vitreous biopsy revealed malignant melanoma cells, confirming metastatic melanoma. The patient underwent a therapeutic pars plana vitrectomy, intravitreal melphalan injection, and ocular radiotherapy, with resolution of vitreous metastasis.Conclusions and ImportanceSeveral atypical, red flags in this patient may serve as clinical indicators; history of malignancy with locoregional progression, timing of immune CPI therapy to symptom onset, failure to treatment response, and large white, amelanotic vitreous cells arranged in veils. This case emphasizes the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis in patients with atypical, or treatment resistant uveitis, especially in individuals with known underlying malignancy.Keywords: Masquerading uveitis, Acral lentiginous melanoma, Vitreous metastasis

Introduction

Cutaneous melanoma is a malignancy arising from the melanocytes of the skin, accounting for nearly 90% of skin cancer mortality.1 While cutaneous melanoma frequently metastasizes to visceral organs and the brain, ocular involvement remains exceedingly rare.2 Less than 5% of metastases to the eye and orbit are due to cutaneous melanoma. Nevertheless, among malignancies that metastasize to the vitreous, cutaneous melanoma is the most common primary tumor.

Acral lentiginous melanoma is a rare melanoma that accounts for less than 5% of all melanoma diagnoses, primarily affects non-White populations and is known for its poor prognosis,3 with the lowest five- and ten-year survival rates of melanoma subtypes. This subtype typically invades acral sites such as palms, soles, and the nail unit. Additionally, treatment strategies for cutaneous melanoma with ocular metastasis have historically shown poor or variable outcomes, underscoring the need for more robust and standardized treatment regimens.

This report presents an unusual manifestation of metastatic acral lentiginous melanoma as vitreous seeding, masquerading as intermediate uveitis, resulting in clinical improvement following a unique treatment strategy.

Case description

This is an 86-year-old Caucasian female with a primary Stage III Acral Lentiginous Melanoma, status post wide local excision (WLE) with negative sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB).

The patient first noticed a non-pigmented lesion on the left heel. Initial depth of invasion was 2.5 mm (mm), expanding over 6 months to a depth of invasion of 5.8 mm. After WLE and SLNB, the patient developed Pseudomonas wound infection which delayed the start of combination immunotherapy with nivolumab and relatlimab. The primary tumor was found to be PRAME and MART-1 positive, while the SLNB histological analysis was negative for melanoma with 0/10 involved lymph nodes. The patient developed multiple in-transit metastases in the left lower leg during this 3-month treatment delay. Eventually immunotherapy was initiated with nivolumab and relatlimab every four weeks, and she completed nine cycles. Despite immunotherapy, she had locoregional progression of melanoma, and was initiated on intralesional talimogene laherparepvec (T-VEC) injections in the left leg, left heel, and left pelvic area.

This patient's ocular history was significant for non-exudative macular degeneration in the right eye, exudative macular degeneration with active choroidal neovascular membrane treated with intravitreal aflibercept and lamellar hole in the left eye, and glaucoma managed with dorzolamide/timolol and latanoprost eye drops in both eyes. Ocular symptoms of blurry vision and increased floaters in the right eye, started around the eighth cycle of combination immunotherapy. She endorsed chronic mild floaters prior to immunotherapy but felt as though the floaters had worsened, and felt as if she was looking through a "smudged lens” in the right eye. She was seen by a local eye care provider, and was diagnosed with intermediate uveitis in the right eye. Extensive infectious and autoimmune work-up including MRI brain and orbits was negative. Uveitis was deemed likely secondary to nivolumab. Immunotherapy was discontinued and treatment was initiated with topical difluprednate to the right eye. She continued intralesional (T-VEC) injections for regional metastasis and began ipilimumab approximately one month after discontinuing nivolumab and relatlimab. She completed six cycles of T-VEC, consisting of four injections each cycle given two weeks apart, and two cycles of ipilimumab, administered three weeks apart.

The patient presented to our ocular oncology service for treatment resistant intermediate uveitis, just prior to beginning ipilimumab immunotherapy. Examination showed best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) of 20/50 in the right eye, and 20/40 in the left eye. Intraocular pressure was 18 mmHg in the right eye and 16 mmHg in the left eye. The right eye showed medium keratic precipitates (KP's), 1+ anterior chamber cells, 2+ large amelanotic vitreous cells arranged in veils, pseudophakia, macular drusen and peripheral paving retinal degeneration (Fig. 1). The left eye was quiet and pseudophakic, with macular drusen, inactive choroidal neovascular membrane, epiretinal membrane, lamellar hole and peripheral paving retinal degeneration (Fig. 1).

Ultra-wide field fundus photographs at time of ocular presentation. Right fundus shows large, amelanotic, vitreous cells arranged in veils with obscuration of retinal details. The left fundus shows clear media, posterior vitreous detachment, macular drusen and inactive choroidal neovascular membrane along with peripheral paving stone retinal degeneration.

The differential diagnosis for intermediate uveitis included idiopathic, autoimmune, infectious, drug-induced, and masquerade etiologies. Infectious and autoimmune causes were excluded by extensive serologic workup. Drug-induced uveitis is a diagnosis of exclusion, prompting further workup. The presence of atypical large amelanotic vitreous cells arranged in veils, lack of response to local ocular therapy, history of malignancy, and prolonged timing of immune checkpoint inhibitor (CPI) therapy to symptom onset, raised concern for a masquerade or metastatic process. Given these findings, a diagnostic vitrectomy was pursued, and topical corticosteroids were discontinued to optimize vitreous yield. The vitreous biopsy revealed malignant melanoma cells, confirming metastatic melanoma. The biopsy pathology determined BRAF negative, KIT negative, CDKN2A and CDKN2B positive inactivating mutations, and NRAS and TNFAIP3 positive missense mutations.

An ultrasound biomicroscopy (UBM) excluded choroidal or ciliary body involvement but demonstrated a residual vitreous skirt. The patient subsequently underwent a second complete pars plana vitrectomy (PPV), along with intravitreal melphalan injection (29 mcg), and cryotherapy to the vitrectomy ports. Subsequently, she underwent 3500 cGy external beam radiation (to the right globe, and continued systemic adjuvant therapy with ipilimumab, binimetinib (MEK inhibitor) and intralesional T-VEC injections. Follow-up examination showed resolution of vitreous veils on fundus photography (Fig. 2). The patient died five months after the ocular diagnosis.

Discussion

Melanoma is the fifth most common malignancy in both males and females in the United States.4 Melanoma metastasizes to distant organs via hematogenous spread or lymphatic spread.5 Cutaneous melanoma is the most common malignancy reported to present with vitreous metastasis.2 Ocular metastases from cutaneous melanoma are uncommon and more frequently involve intraocular rather than extraocular structures, with uveal involvement reported in up to 75% of cases2,6, 7, 8. Metastasis confined to the vitreous without clinical involvement of other intraocular structures is exceptionally rare. It has been proposed that clumps of pigmented or nonpigmented cells in the vitreous or on the lens surface may suggest metastatic melanoma, aligning with this patient's presentation with large, amelanotic cells in the vitreous.2,6,9 The median age at diagnosis of ocular metastasis for patients across different studies typically falls in the seventh decade of life or later.7,10

It is important to recognize unusual uveitis and masquerade syndromes which warrant further evaluation with diagnostic vitrectomy. This patient was initially diagnosed at an outside facility as intermediate uveitis secondary to CPIs. However, she presented to our hospital with features atypical for a uveitis diagnosis, such as unilateral presentation, extensive vitreous involvement with large, amelanotic cells arranged in veils, a history of known malignancy, and a prolonged timing of immune CPI therapy to symptom onset. The index of suspicion was very high for masquerade syndrome, which warrants diagnostic vitrectomy for cytopathological diagnosis. It is important to note that drug-induced uveitis is a diagnosis of exclusion.

Ocular immunotherapy-related adverse events (IRAEs) have been shown to cause uveitis.11 The American Society of Clinical Oncology reports a median onset at 5 weeks, with a range from 1 to 72 weeks of ocular IRAEs from CPI therapy.12,13 Our patient presented with ocular symptoms 32 weeks after the start of nivolumab-relatlimab combination therapy, which falls within the time range in which ocular adverse events can occur.13 Despite falling within the possible range, it is highly unlikely that this was CPI-induced uveitis given presentation long after the median time to onset of 5 weeks. Thus, it is possible that the time of symptom onset may provide additional clues in differentiating vitreous metastasis from drug-induced uveitis. The third-party ophthalmologist believed the “uveitis” presentation was a complication of immunotherapy.

Intermediate uveitis typically presents with vitreous cells and/or diffuse, homogenous vitreous haze known as vitritis.14 This patient presented with large, amelanotic vitreous cells arranged in veils which is more indicative of masquerade or malignancy etiologies, especially given underlying negative workup.2,7,10,15 Although uveitis is typically synonymous with an “inflammatory” eye, including pain, photophobia, and red eye, many cases of intermediate uveitis do not present with these symptoms. It is therefore important not to exclude uveitis solely in the absence of classic inflammatory symptoms; however, in this context, the atypical vitreous morphology with underlying cancer progression while on CPI warranted heightened suspicion for a masquerade syndrome.

In patients with a known history of malignancy, new ocular findings may raise concern for primary ocular malignancy or metastasis. Cancer patients are at an increased risk of developing second primary malignancies, underscoring the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis.16 A rare possibility was primary uveal melanoma with vitreous spread. Primary uveal melanoma is the most common eye cancer in adults.17 The most common presenting symptoms are blurry vision, photopsia, and floaters. While uveal melanoma is usually straightforward, it only rarely presents with features resembling uveitis due to secondary inflammation, in which case it may be considered a form of masquerade uveitis.18 Lemaître et al. describe melanoma of the ciliary body, incidentally discovered with associated findings of vitreous seeding and slightly increased intraocular pressure.19 Vitreous biopsy and other imaging modalities such as UBM help to differentiate between metastatic disease and primary malignancy.

The eye's immune privilege combined with prolonged survival due to immunotherapy may contribute to the development of intraocular metastasis from cutaneous melanoma. This patient's only ocular surgical history included bilateral cataract surgery, nearly 10 years prior to onset of ocular metastasis. Cataract surgery can cause temporary disruption of the blood-retinal barrier (BRB) due to inflammation, often leading to subclinical leakage which typically resolves.20 It is unlikely that cataract surgery from 10 years ago predisposed to vitreous metastasis in our patient.

Acral lentiginous melanoma is the rarest of four subtypes of cutaneous malignant melanoma that arises on the hands and feet.3 It has a greater incidence in non-White populations compared to the other subtypes and is not associated with ultraviolet-radiation mutations. This subtype carries one of the worst prognoses because it is often advanced at time of presentation, leading to a high rate of morbidity and mortality. This case is unique in demonstrating that acral lentiginous melanoma may metastasize to the vitreous and masquerade as uveitis, expanding the spectrum of its metastatic behavior.

Our patient underwent a diagnostic vitrectomy to establish a cytopathological diagnosis, as uveitis work-up for infectious and autoimmune etiology, including MRI brain and orbits, was negative. Following the tissue diagnosis of cutaneous melanoma metastasis to the vitreous, a UBM was done to assess for uveal involvement, that showed peripheral vitreous skirt and residual vitreous cells. She underwent a second complete vitrectomy to reduce vitreous disease burden, with intravitreal 29 mcg melphalan injection, and cryotherapy to the surgical ports. She subsequently underwent 35 cGy external beam radiation to the right globe and was continued on systemic ipilimumab, binimetinib and intralesional T-VEC injections. The treatment for ocular metastases from cutaneous melanoma has been described as largely palliative and tailored to the extent of ocular and systemic involvement.2,7,8,21 This may include external beam radiation (typically 30–40 cGy) and intravitreal melphalan for local control, while systemic therapies, especially immune CPIs and BRAF/MEK inhibitors in mutation-positive cases, may reduce the need for enucleation or radiation.22 This multimodal approach can achieve regression of intraocular metastases in select patients. Surgical excision and enucleation are reserved for refractory cases or those with severe pain or poor visual potential. Reported clinical outcomes are generally poor, with median survival after ocular metastasis ranging from 8 to 24 months, and visual prognosis is often limited due to complications such as neovascular glaucoma and retinal detachment.2,7,8,15

The use of intravitreal melphalan in this case is novel, as it is a form of chemotherapy that is typically used to treat retinoblastomas.23 It is also more commonly used as FDA-approved treatment of hepatic metastases secondary to primary uveal melanoma.24 Although the genetics of primary uveal melanoma and cutaneous melanoma are different, intravitreal melphalan has been used to improve patient outcomes in both diseases. A multicenter, retrospective study conducted by Francis et al. describes the use of intravitreal melphalan in three study eyes with cutaneous metastatic melanoma in the eye, yielding mixed results but indicating possible favorable responses with increased dosage.2 A smaller multicenter retrospective study by Ashkenazy et al. also showed some degree of success with melphalan for ocular metastasis in cutaneous melanoma; due to access limitation for melphalan, in one patient, intravitreal methotrexate was even trialed, but the efficacy of the latter remains unknown with limited clinical data.10

In our patient, a combination of these treatment modalities resulted in resolution of the metastatic vitreous cells. A limitation of this case is the inability to attribute the observed clinical improvement to specific treatment modalities; however, the resolution of the vitreous veils was most likely attributable to the complete vitrectomy. Future studies are needed to better delineate the relative contributions and effectiveness of individual treatments in similar cases. Nonetheless, this case indicates that a multimodal treatment approach may be utilized in similar cases to result in better clinical outcomes than those previously reported for ocular metastases secondary to cutaneous melanoma. This case study illustrates the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion when evaluating cutaneous melanoma patients who present with new ocular findings, as these symptoms may signal rare but significant metastatic spread. Early recognition and timely diagnosis are crucial and require close interdisciplinary coordination between oncology and ophthalmology teams to ensure optimal patient management and outcomes. This case underscores the need for vigilance and collaboration while also expanding the known spectrum of acral lentiginous melanoma behavior, demonstrating its potential for unexpected metastatic presentations beyond traditional patterns, and providing a potential framework for treatment.
