Protocol to study ex vivo T cell priming by conventional dendritic cells from the mouse spleen
Authors: Mitra Ashayeripanah, Jose A. Villadangos
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryConventional dendritic cells (cDC) are professional antigen-presenting cells able to prime naive T cells. Here, we present a protocol for ex vivo T cell priming by murine splenic cDC. We describe the steps of injecting fluorescently labeled antigens to mice, purifying antigen-bearing cDC, and priming antigen-specific T cells ex vivo. This protocol is suitable for studying the T cell priming function of cDC in various murine models and helps factor in the effect of the microenvironment on cDC ability to uptake and process antigens.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Ashayeripanah et al.1

Before you begin

Conventional dendritic cells (cDC) are professional antigen presenting cells capable of priming naïve T cells. Two subsets of cDC exist in mice, cDC1 and cDC2, which perform partially non-overlapping functions in antigen capture and presentation, and T cell priming. cDC1 are able to efferocytose dying cells and to present antigens contained in these cells via MHC class I molecules (cross-presentation) and via MHC class II. Both cDC1 and cDC2 can capture other forms of antigen such as soluble proteins, proteins bound to artificial beads or ligands for receptors expressed on the surface of cDC1 and/or cDC2. Generally, only the cDC1 can cross-present these antigens. Both cDC1 and cDC2 can also present them via MHC II molecules, but for an equal amount of antigen capture, cDC2 are generally more efficient than cDC1 at MHC II presentation. As a corollary, priming of CD8+ T cells is performed almost exclusively by cDC1 while CD4+ T cell priming can be carried out by both cDC1 and cDC2 or, in some circumstances, only by cDC2.2,3,4,5,6,7,8,9 The antigen-presenting capacity of both cDC1 and cDC2 is further tuned in vivo by local tissue factors that induce so-called Spatio-Temporal Adaptations (STAs) on cDC during the final stages of their development. STAs can vary over time and among anatomical locations in response to infection and/or inflammatory stimuli.10 To characterize the ability of cDC1 and cDC2 with different STAs to present a specific form of antigen to CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, these cells can be isolated from their tissue of residence (e.g., the spleen), loaded in vitro with antigen, and incubated with antigen-specific T cells followed by measurements of T cell activation or proliferation. However, a limitation of this approach is that purified cDC may not display in vitro the same antigen-presenting capabilities they have in vivo under the influence of the tissue factors that induce STAs. To overcome this, fluorescently-labeled antigen can be injected to mice and, after relevant intervals, cDC that captured the antigen in vivo can be isolated. The cells can then be incubated ex vivo with antigen-specific CD8+ and CD4+ T cells to measure antigen presentation via MHC I and MHC II molecules, respectively. Our recent work highlighted the benefit of this ex vivo priming protocol as we observed cDC of mice that recovered from systemic severe inflammation functioned normally in vitro but had undergone a paralysis STA in vivo that impaired their capacity for T cell priming in vivo and ex vivo.1

The step-by-step protocol below describes optimal doses of antigens for injection to mice; optimized intervals between administration of the antigen and spleen harvest; how to isolate, enrich and fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)-sort the antigen-bearing cDC; co-culture of these cDC with antigen-specific T cells ex vivo; and measurement of T cell priming. The model antigen used here is ovalbumin (OVA) and three forms of this antigen including soluble, cell-associated, and bead-associated were studied.

Media and solutions should be prepared according to the materials and equipment section prior to experiments. Storage instructions are provided in the footnotes of the recipes.

Expected outcomes

This protocol is designed to examine the capacity of murine splenic cDC1 and cDC2 to prime antigen-specific T cells ex vivo. Following uptake and processing of the antigen by cDC in vivo, antigen-bearing cDC are purified and cocultured with antigen-specific T cells ex vivo. As shown in Figure 3, T cells proliferation is characterized by the dilution of CTV. While the rightmost peak represents un-proliferated OT-cells, each subsequent peak represents one cell division. Notably, the capability of cDC to prime T cells varies depending on the microenvironment in which the antigen is captured and processed. Limitations

In the current protocol, ex vivo OT-cells priming index is used as surrogate for T cell priming capacity of cDC. However, the in situ localization of cDC, the in situ recruitment of T cells to the antigen-bearing cDC, and the effect of other regulatory cell types in vivo are not factored in. To evaluate the T cell priming function of cDC, additional assays, such as in vivo T cell priming experiments, may be required. Furthermore, the process of tissue digestion (more specifically collagenase treatment), incubation with nycodenz, and Ab-mediated positive selection via CD11c microbeads (if applied) may modify the surface expression of some cDC molecules, although we do not observe changes in expression of the molecules listed in this protocol.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Sub-optimal coating of Streptavidin Fluoresbrite YG microspheres with OVA-biotin.

Potential solution

Perform titration of the ratios between OVA-biotin and beads. In order to check the saturation of streptavidin binding sites on the beads, perform secondary staining with a fluorescently-labeled biotin Ab. This fluorescence signal must be read on a channel different from that of FITC to avoid overlap with the beads intrinsic signal. The optimized titration of OVA-biotin to beads is the one in which all the streptavidin binding sites of the beads are occupied, thus there will be no positivity respective to the secondary fluorescent biotin labeling.

Problem 2

No layering is formed in nycodenz after gradient centrifugation.

Potential solution

Mix the nycodenz stock very well before using. Maintain the cold-chain for nycodenz by keeping on ice at all times and using 4°C centrifugation. Do not use thawed and re-frozen nycodenz stock. If the problem persists, prepare a new batch of nycodenz.

Problem 3

Low yield of enriched cDC per spleen.

Potential solution

Efficient digestion is the critical step in improving the yield of cDC. Make sure each spleen is very well chopped to become paste-like with no visible fragment left. If digesting multiple spleens with collagenase III/DNase I, split the samples in two or more batches for the digestion to ensure effective manual resuspension with transfer pipet for each sample. For example, to process 12 spleens, chop and digest the first 6 spleens, wash with RPMI 2% FBS, reconstitute the cell pellets in small volume (e.g., 500 μL) of FBS, and place on ice. Then, continue with the next 6 spleens.

Problem 4

No OT-cells priming detected.

Potential solution

Increase the number of cDC per well in the coculture of cDC-OT cells. Check the viability of FACS-sorted cDC by staining with a viability dye other than FVD eF780. If the sorting is causing huge cell death in cDC, seek advice to optimize your FACS-sorting to yield viable, healthy and functional cDC.

Problem 5

Under proliferation or over proliferation of OT-cells.

Potential solution

Adjust the number of cDC per well (10 × 103 to 25 × 103) for optimized OT-cells proliferation. Harvest the cells at the timing that allows optimized OT-cells proliferation. In general, OT-I cells start the proliferation cycle faster than OT-II cells. Consider shorter incubation period of 60 h for OT-I but longer incubation period of 70 h for OT-II. In our hands, the viability of activated OT-I cells drops quickly after 70 h.
