Global dataset for realized thermal and aridity niche limits for terrestrial vertebrates
Authors: Matthew Watson, Jeremy Kerr
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Climate changes are altering temperature and aridity regimes, posing serious challenges for many species globally. Particularly, environmental changes that create conditions that push species beyond there realized thermal and aridity niche limits are especially likely to contribute to extinction risk. Yet, responses to climate change vary interspecifically for geographical range shifts, the timing of biological activities, trends in body size, and population growth differences. Estimates of thermal and aridity niche limits help predict and understand such variation. For such studies that address how species respond to climate change over broad geographical areas, we created a dataset of realized niche limits for 33,941 species of terrestrial vertebrates for (amphibians, birds, mammals, reptiles). Specifically, these datasets include monthly lower and upper realized thermal and aridity niche limits, along with yearly average estimates of niche limits. These datasets will be curated to expand taxonomic coverage of realized niche limits and facilitate evaluation of impacts of climate change on biodiversity.

Background & Summary

Where species are found reflects the combinations of abiotic and biotic conditions that enable them to maintain positive population growth rates1,2. Climate change poses a significant risk to global biodiversity by imposing novel environmental conditions on species, which may be beyond tolerable levels3. A vital question for conservation in this era of rapid global change is when will species encounter tipping points that contribute to population declines and eventual local extinction. A key element of climate change is the imposition of increasingly severe and frequent weather extremes4, driving many species to show signs of population decline and elevated extinction risk5,6.

Environmental changes and extreme events that create conditions that exceed species’ realized thermal and moisture niche limits appear especially likely to contribute to extinction risk7. Many valuable niche concepts exist. Among them, the realized niche concept is a simple reflection of the combination of environmental conditions and biotic interactions that enable a species to maintain populations characterized by population growth rates ≥ 01,8. Realized niche boundaries often vary from lab-based measurements of physiological tolerances9,10, and are certainly imperfect representations of species’ true tolerances of environmental extremes. Yet, field and macroecological observations of biotic responses to climate change are broadly consistent with the critical premise that environmental conditions observed in nature relate to where species are found and their prospects for survival11–13. Further, accounting for species niche limits has been found to improve predictions of species responses to climate change3,11,14.

Emerging evidence suggests that realized niche limits and consequent susceptibility to changing incidences and severity of extreme weather should also reflect seasonal differences in species’ tolerances15,16. Recently proposed metrics that address how environmental changes interact with species’ potential seasonal differences in susceptibility to changing temperature and precipitation regimes include the thermal position index (TPI) and aridity position index (API)17,18. These metrics, which are species-specific, help predict population change and local extinction risk among insects and vertebrate species at global scales19,20. In addition, body size differences among vertebrate species globally relate to the extent that climate changes impose conditions that approach or exceed the boundaries of their realized niches, as indexed using TPI and API18. Land use changes can also increase species’ exposures to conditions that are more extreme relative to those found within structurally complex habitats, a trend that could benefit - but more often harms - species21. Such TPI and API data for vertebrate species, whose geographical ranges can be estimated nearly comprehensively, can help inform assessments of species’ past, present, or future vulnerabilities to environmental change.

Here we provide a dataset containing monthly and yearly average lower and upper realized thermal and aridity niche limits for all extant terrestrial birds, mammals, reptiles, and amphibians with known geographical ranges. These data provide a means of exploring and predicting species’ positions within their realized niche spaces, which can be used to inform predictions of species distributions and population dynamics1,19. Additionally, realized niche limits can be valuable in determining species’ vulnerabilities to climate change and whether shifting mean conditions or extreme events might accentuate their vulnerabilities or create potential benefits. These data also enable calculation of TPI and API, which relate to species extinction-colonization dynamics and shifting body sizes in relation to climate change18,19,22.

Thermal and Aridity Position Indices

The Thermal Position Index19 and Aridity Position Index are scaled metrics that determines the environmental position of a species relative to their thermal and aridity realized niche limits, respectively. Each index is based on observations of where species are found (or believed to be found, based on expert knowledge) and the limits of both temperature and aridity that prevail in those areas. Previous studies have shown that measures of fitness decline when species’ experience conditions that approach or exceed their realized niche limits11,12,23. When species experience conditions that approach or exceed their environmental realized niche limits, they must commit more time and energy to behaviours that reduce their exposure to temperature or arid extremes24,25. This can cause increased physiological stress and consequent populations declines26–29. Further, heat stress and dehydration associated with such extreme conditions can lead to mass mortality events30–32. Since realized niche limits are species-specific, responses to similar environmental conditions should vary between species. TPI and API calculations are species-specific, generating estimates of proximity to realized niche limits, which can inform evaluations of climate change-related risks and to help understand which species may be vulnerable to population and geographical range changes.

To address the challenge of measuring species niche limits, we provide a dataset for calculating TPI and API that covers a broad taxonomic spectrum of terrestrial vertebrates, including 11036 bird, 5654 mammal, 9284 reptile, and 7959 amphibian species. To this end, we provide monthly estimates of species thermal and aridity tolerances, along with yearly average lower and upper thermal and aridity realized niche limits. Monthly realized niche limits provide the benefit of being able to account for variable seasonal adaptation species utilize that may alter niche limits over the course of a year. This addresses a limitation of many experimentally-observed thermal tolerance metrics, which provide single measurements of lower and upper thermal limits and can miss important seasonal variation34.

This dataset does not attempt to recreate such direct physiological measurements, though TPI and API could be calculated directly from them for species where physiological limits were known. Instead, TPI and API measurements were created for use at broad (macroecological) spatial extents18–20. However, recent work on butterflies has shown that realized niche limits derived at macroecological scales (~5 km2) can be used to predict occupancy dynamics at microclimatic scales (~0.0025 m2)22. This demonstrates that realized niche limits derived at broad spatial scales can be effectively applied to high resolution climate data and provide meaningful insights into species occupancy dynamics. This is a necessary consideration since many species are heavily dependent on microclimates to buffer against broader climatic pressures35.

Previous studies have found thermal range limits generally reflect species thermal tolerance limits36. However, species from higher latitudes have been found to exhibit warmer thermal tolerances than would be expected based on their warm range boundaries10. This highlights that upper thermal realized niche limits may sometimes underestimate species’ capacity to persist in warming areas, especially for temperate and polar species. However, changes in temperature and aridity that exceed species historical conditions are likely to reduce fitness37. Nevertheless, we include analyses in the Technical Validation section that test the relationship between our derived realized niche limits and available estimates of physiological tolerances. We also generate realized niche estimates from species observation records and site level temperature records using North American Breeding Bird Survey data38 to further test estimates generated using range maps and historical climate data. Ultimately, our goal is to provide a practical data source to improve predictions of species responses to climate change and aid studies focused on species demographic, occupancy, and distribution dynamics in relation to environmental conditions.

Methods

To calculate TPI, we created a dataset containing historical monthly upper and lower thermal niche limits for terrestrial birds, mammals, reptiles, and amphibians. We accessed monthly minimum and maximum global temperature raster maps from the WorldClim database39 (https://www.worldclim.org/data/monthlywth.html). We selected datasets at the highest spatial resolution of 2.5 minutes (~21 km2 at the equator) and downloaded all months for the years 1961 to 1975. We used this time-period to represent historical thermal conditions species would have experienced prior to rapid increases in global temperatures due to climate change40. Although species have the capacity to adapt to changing environmental conditions, rates of current climate warming generally exceed historic rates of niche evolution41. As a result, we assume that species thermal niche limits would likely not evolve in pace with climate change and therefore continue to reflect thermal conditions prior to rapid climate change. By determining thermal niche limits of species prior to rapid changes in environmental conditions we aim to avoid potential overestimation of species upper thermal niche limits. Finally, setting a baseline period for determining upper and lower thermal niche limits permits tests of how climate change alters the thermal and aridity position of species spatially and temporally, which has enabled such shifts to be tested successfully against observed biological responses18.

Once we extracted and organized the climate data, we assembled expert-informed range maps for birds, mammals, reptiles, and amphibians. We first accessed range maps for all terrestrial mammal, reptile, and amphibian species from the International Union for the Conservation of Nature (IUCN: https://www.iucnredlist.org/resources/spatial-data-download)42. Once collected, we loaded all range maps into ArcGIS Pro43 along with a shapefile matching the spatial distribution of WorldClim temperature data (See Data Records). Using the shapefile as the inclusion boundary we used the Clip function to limit species ranges to the spatial extent of available temperature data. Following this process, we extracted each individual species range map for downstream processing of climate data.

After processing mammal, reptile, and amphibian range maps, we obtained range maps for all bird species from BirdLife International44 (https://datazone.birdlife.org/contact-us/request-our-data). We first processed all bird range maps in the same manner as described above. After the initial step, we needed to apply additional processing to bird range maps due to the prevalence of migration. BirdLife range maps are composed of multiple range maps constituting seasonal location. Using this information, we were able to use ArcGIS Pro to split the range maps for birds into resident maps (known to occur year-round), and seasonal maps (breeding season, non-breeding season, and passage). After separating the range maps into seasonal maps, we recombined these maps in four specific combinations to generate new range maps with the following classifications: Breeding (Resident and Breeding range maps), Non Breeding (Resident and Non Breeding range maps), Migration Non Breeding (Resident, Non Breeding, and Passage range maps), and Migration Breeding (Resident, Breeding, and Passage range maps) using the Merge function in ArcGIS Pro.

For the final step of processing, we appended a hemisphere classification to each bird species range map. We used biogeographic classifications from the IUCN Red List to bin each species to either a Northern or Southern hemisphere distribution42. Species with any record of occurring within the Palearctic or Nearctic regions were assigned to Northern hemisphere distribution, while species that did not occur in these regions were classed as Southern. We applied this final step considering that timing of migrations in birds differs between the Northern and Southern hemisphere and as such would impact which of our seasonal range maps we used for specific monthly temperature extractions45.

Using the processed range maps for terrestrial mammals, reptiles, and amphibians, we extracted temperature data from the WorldClim raster files. All following processes were completed in the R environment46. We loaded WorldClim monthly minimum and monthly maximum temperature rasters using the terra package47 along with each species range map into the R environment. Species range maps were used to define the spatial boundaries from where we would extract the absolute maximum and minimum temperature value from WorldClim raster maps. We extracted absolute monthly maximum and minimum temperatures from each species range for each month from the years outlined above (1961–1975). We then calculated lower and upper thermal tolerance limits for each month by calculating the mean of all minimum and all maximum temperatures. Finally, we calculated the mean for minimum and maximum thermal tolerance limits across all months to establish one value for average yearly minimum and average yearly maximum thermal limit for each species.

Again, we utilized the same methods for calculating thermal niche limits in birds as we did for mammal, reptiles, and amphibians, with steps detailed below. Mainly, we selected range maps to be used for determining monthly tolerance limits based on the month and geographical distribution of bird species45,48. Non-Breeding and Breeding range maps were simply assigned to the months commonly associated with non-breeding and breeding periods for each hemisphere. Breeding Migration and Non-Breeding Migration Range maps were assigned to months that are typically associated with mass migratory events for birds, based on hemisphere of occurrence48. At the start of the migratory period when birds are leaving their non-breeding ranges, we assumed there would be individuals distributed across their resident and non-breeding ranges along with their migratory route. However, there would be very few individuals likely to occur within the full extent of their breeding range, so this section of range map was excluded. The following month we assumed individuals would have begun arriving on their breeding grounds but that some individuals would still be in the process of migration49,50. We therefore assigned the month after the initiation of migration to the Breeding Migration range map. We followed the same process but in reverse for when birds were migrating from their breeding ranges to their non-breeding ranges. Once range maps were assigned their appropriate months for temperature extraction, we followed the same processing steps as we did for amphibians, mammals and reptiles.

To generate aridity realized niche limit values, we used monthly precipitation and potential evapotranspiration data51 (10.7923/G43J3B0R) to calculate historical monthly AI. We then followed an identical workflow as was used for generating thermal niche limits using the AI data. Due to extremely low potential evapotranspiration values in some areas, it is possible to generate disproportionately large AI values. To account for this, we identified any potential outlier upper AI values. Outlier values were identified as exceeding the third quartile of range wide AI values plus 1.5 times the interquartile range. Any AI value that exceeds this limit was replaced with a value matching the third quartile plus 1.5 times the interquartile range of range wide AI. Once we completed the generation of all thermal and aridity niche limits for terrestrial vertebrates, we concatenated all values into a single datafile. We then generated a second datafile that contained a single upper and lower thermal and aridity niche limit for each species. These values were calculated as the yearly average lower and upper limit for all months.

The scripts containing all calculations and data generation outlined above can be accessed from the Code Availability section. All original data sources required to generate both temperature and aridity realized niche limits following the scripts available at (https://github.com/MacroEcoMatt/TPI_API), are listed here: Range Maps (https://www.iucnredlist.org/resources/spatial-data-download, https://datazone.birdlife.org/contact-us/request-our-data), minimum and maximum monthly temperature rasters (https://www.worldclim.org/data/monthlywth.html), and minimum and maximum monthly precipitation and potential evapotranspiration rasters (https://www.climatologylab.org/terraclimate.html).

Data Records

Thermal and aridity realized niche limit datasets can be downloaded in two comma-separated values (CSV) file from figshare52 (10.6084/m9.figshare.28990130.v2). The downloaded files are in spreadsheet format, with one containing lower and upper thermal tolerance limits for birds, mammals, reptiles, and amphibians for each month (Month_Limits.csv), the other containing the averaged lower and upper thermal limit over all months for each species (Yearly_Average.csv). Within the Monthly_Limits.csv file each species contains 12 rows, one pertaining to each month of the year, with month being represented in two columns. Column MonthNumber contains the numeric code for each month, while MonthName contains the three letter abbreviation for each month. Both Month_Limits.csv and Yearly_Average.csv contain columns TMin and TMax, which contain the lower and upper thermal niche limit value, and AMin and AMax which contain the lower and upper aridity niche limit value, respectively.

Both files contain taxonomic information from the species to class level for each species included in the dataset. Additionally, a column is included with the taxonomic binomial (Genus species). An additional file is provided to aid in cross comparing taxonomic names due to the prevalence of species synonyms. The file Synonym_List.csv provides a comma-separated values (CSV) file with two columns. The first column contains the heading “Synonym” which lists species binomials known to be synonyms of accepted species binomials used by the IUCN Red List. The second column contains species binomials used by the IUCN Red List to cross compare with synonymous binomials. Finally, both niche limit datasets (Month_Limits.csv and Yearly_Average.csv) contain columns linking to the data sources (range maps, temperature data, precipitation, and potential evapotranspiration data) used in generating the niche limits.

Technical Validation

Experimentally derived measures of physiological thermal tolerances can provide detailed measurements of species thermal neutral zones (TNZ) or critical thermal limits (CT). These are valuable measurements and provide detailed insight into the thermal conditions that illicit specific physiological responses from species53,54. However, variations in humidity and inconsistent methodologies can make comparisons between studies of physiological thermal tolerances challenging54–57. Physiological studies also tend to acclimate subjects which can mask potential seasonal or shorter-term variation in thermal tolerance58, though some studies do include seasonal variation in analyses59,60. Finally, another major challenges of experimentally deriving TNZ or CT is that it is time consuming and logistically difficult, particularly for rare and large species57,58,61.

In contrast to experimentally derived tolerances, we generated realized thermal and aridity niche limits from species range limits and historical climate data, to be used to generate estimates of species climatic position (TPI/API). These values have the benefit of being specific to each species, which allows for relative comparisons of temperature change experienced between species based on their respective realized niche limits. Additionally, environmental realized niche limits provided in the dataset can be generated for many species, relatively quickly, and cost effectively. However, it is important to establish the reliability of measurements that are generated through historical climatic data derived from species ranges34. Here we compare our environmentally derived lower and upper thermal limits against a dataset of experimentally derived lower (LTNZ) and upper (UTNZ) thermal neutral zone values for a total of 494 bird and mammal species, critical thermal maximum (CTmax) for 336 reptile and amphibian species, critical thermal minimum (CTmin) for 189 reptile species, and lower median lethal temperature 50 (LT50) for 29 amphibian species62. We use thermal tolerances derived from physiological studies to serve as a reference measurement against which we compared the lower and upper thermal realized niche limits derived from our dataset.

For each species present in both the reference dataset and in our dataset, we extracted the minimum lower thermal niche limit (LNL) and maximum upper thermal niche limit (UNL), as well as LTNZ and UTNZ for birds and mammals, and CTmin or LT50 and CTmax for reptiles and amphibians. We then ran separate phylogenetic linear regression models (PLM) for each taxonomic class using the phylolm (2.6.5) package63 (Table 2) to determine if species environmental niche limits were positively related to species physiological derived thermal tolerances (TNZ, CT, LT50). All models controlled for phylogenetic nonindependence following a Pagel’s Lambda model of the error term using phylogenetic covariance matrices derived from available phylogenies (Amphibian64, Aves65, Mammalia66, Reptilia67). We compared these models with models that were fit using a Brownian Motion model error term. We continued with all models fit under a Pagel’s Lambda model based on consistently lower model AIC values. In addition, we repeated these analyses using mean LNL and UNL and compared model results, finding no significant difference in results. All PLMs revealed significant positive relationships between both lower and upper thermal tolerance estimates and realized thermal niche limits (Figs. 2, 3). Our results are consistent with the findings of previous studies that demonstrated a positive relationship between experimentally derived thermal tolerances and macroecological niche limits9,10. Generally, species’ cold range edges are typically determined by species cold tolerance (metabolic constraints)68, which indicates that values derived for species lower thermal niche limits (LNL) should be well below species LTNZ. This is in fact what we find in our analysis of LNL, with these estimates being consistently below LTNZ values for all birds and mammals. For amphibians and reptiles LNL values generally tracked with species LT50 and CTmin, though many LNL values fell below the physiologically derived critical limits. We additionally found a positive relationship between species’ upper thermal niche limits (UNL) and their UTNZ in birds and mammals (Fig. 3). For amphibians and reptiles, we observed that UNL were well predicted by species critical thermal maximum (CTmax), with many species UNL falling below species CTmax (Fig. 3). In other words, amphibians and reptiles generally occupy environments with temperatures below their upper CT limits, likely due to reductions in performance and fitness associated with those temperatures61.

It is important to note that despite all estimates showing significant positive trends, we find relatively weak R2 estimates across all groups of terrestrial vertebrates (Tables 2 & 3). This is not surprising given the variety of both biotic and abiotic factors that contribute to determining species range boundaries and realized niche limits, particularly for species warm range limits10,70. We do not suggest that realized niche limits are interchangeable with physiological thermal tolerance limits. Instead, we note the significant, positive relationship between species physiological limits and realized niche limits estimated using TPI and API methodology. Species sometimes occupy environments with thermal conditions beyond those predicted by physiological limits, likely reflecting the interplay of behavioural strategies and microclimate availability25,61,71. We also tested estimates of realized niche limits using range maps and historical monthly average temperature data against species observations with locally recorded temperature data. To meet requirements for generating realized niche limits, such data had to have broad temporal and geographic coverage, and preferably contain observations dating back to the 1960s to enable observation of conditions prior to rapid climate changes. For these purposes, we used observation data of three well-sampled bird species (Corvus ossifragus, Cyanocitta cristata, Poecile carolinensis) from the North American Breeding Bird Survey (BBS) datasets38. We selected these three species based on the abundance of observational data and because their range limits are entirely within areas covered through BBS monitoring. To ensure sufficient point observations to generate niche limit estimates, we collected all observations between the years 1968–1999 for the months of May, June, and July. In total we retained a total of: 999 C. ossifragus, 5230 (C. cristata), and 2220 (P. carolinensis) observations. We then extracted the maximum and minimum recorded temperatures at locales where each species was observed for every month and year in the dataset.

We then compared all maximum and minimum temperature values from the BBS dataset with values generated from our range map method for each species-by-month grouping. We used Welch’s t-test to determine if estimates for each species’ monthly upper and lower thermal realized niche limits differed between methods. This test was selected to account for unequal variances in temperatures between the two methods. For upper thermal realized niche limits, we found seven out of nine comparisons showed no significant difference between the two methods (Table 4). We found that our method using range maps generated significantly hotter upper thermal niche limit estimates in June for P. carolinensis (T = 2.67, p = 0.030; Fig. 4a) and July for C. cristata (T = 3.21, p = 0.017; Fig. 4a). Our findings reveal that using range maps generate comparable estimates of upper thermal realized niche limits to those generated using species observations. However, estimates of lower thermal realized niche limits are lower than those generated from point-based species observations. One reason for these discrepancies is likely that BBS weather data are only collected during active surveys. In contrast, range map methods use regional monthly minimum and maximum temperatures derived through spatial interpolations of weather station recordings39. Given that surveys are also typically conducted between dawn and noon, the hottest and coldest temperatures at survey sites are not likely to be recorded. This is likely further impacted by the limited latitudinal coverage of BBS sites: BBS sites generally had overall higher minimum and lower maximum latitudes (Table 5). Estimates are generally consistent for upper thermal limit estimates, whether from BBS observations or range map data. However, generating niche limits from point-based observation data poses logistical challenges. The first such challenge is that collecting spatially and temporally consistent observation records for all species is infeasible. Additionally, the likelihood of false absences among point-based surveys is high, especially for cryptic and rare species74. Further, existing point-based observations tend to be strongly biased geographically toward areas near larger human populations and accessible areas among wealthier nations, leading to large under sampled regions75, including many global biodiversity hotspots.

Nevertheless, there are biases associated with species range maps that affect realized niche measurements provided here. Range maps are prone to both omission and especially to commission errors76, which can lead to errors like false absences common to point-based observations. Additionally, species range maps describe the limit of species distributions and provide no information about variation in species abundance or occupancy within those boundaries77. This could potentially lead to the inclusion of climate data from regions of species’ ranges that they do not actually inhabit. We highlight these points so that users of the provided estimates of realized niche limits can be aware of the associated assumptions being made. Particularly, temperature and aridity sampling assume that species occupy the entire area of their range. Secondly, we assume that range maps encompass the entirety of each species’ distribution. Because species ranges are shifting due to many forms of environmental change68,78, species’ current geographical ranges can differ from their historical ranges, but the use of those historical ranges is nevertheless integral to observations of realized niche limits. Range maps will need to be reassessed as climate change continues. As updated range maps become available, new tests of how species are adapting (or not) to climate change will be necessary, but comparisons against historical baselines will remain, in our view, vital.

Previous work comparing species- environment associations using Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF) observation records and species range maps found high correlations between the two methods79. Such results offer further support for using either range maps or species observations to generate species niche estimates, at least for well-sampled species. The taxonomic and geographical coverage of estimated realized niche limits derived by using recently-published range data for the world’s terrestrial vertebrates also helps address the paucity of point-based observations for rare and cryptic species. Further, a growing body of research that has used these realized niche limits, or estimates generated through the same methodology, show that detection and attribution of biological responses to global change can succeed using these data18,19,21,22.

Usage Notes

The datafiles were generated in a format that is intended to provide an easy way to incorporate thermal and aridity niche limits into research and conservation contexts. These data are not intended to be as measurements of experimentally-derived physiological thermal tolerances. Instead, they provide measures of species’ environmental niche limits to measure relative impacts of changes in temperature or aridity across species’ ranges. These data can used in combination with the provided climatic positioning equation (TPI/API) to converting raw environmental temperature or aridity index data into species-specific indices that can then be used to investigate a variety of ecological questions. It is important to consider that these niche limits were generated using species range maps and global climate datasets at a spatial resolution of 2.5 minutes (~21 km2), and datasets created here are intended to be used across broad geographical areas. Additional consideration of microclimatic variability can be included to provide additional, potential biological insights, especially among small-bodied organisms that frequently depend on microclimate buffering35,61.

Studies on the impacts of temperature and climate change on species range shifts, population trends, and phenological shifts are excellent candidates for the integration of the provided thermal and aridity niche limit data along with TPI and API calculations. Research articles can be found that provide examples of the use of these niche limit data18, and niche limits generated following the same methods for taxa such as bumblebee19 and butterfly22 species.
