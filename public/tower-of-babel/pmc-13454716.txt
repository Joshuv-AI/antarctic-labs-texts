Hypocalcaemic cardiomyopathy with recurrent heart failure: a natural rechallenge case report with 30-month follow-up
Authors: Naidong Pang, Rou-Mu Hu, Lin Xu, XingPeng Liu, Yanjiang Wang, Massimo Mapelli, Shahrukh Hashmani, Andrea Freixa-Benavente, Deepti Ranganathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypocalcaemic cardiomyopathy is a rare but potentially reversible cause of heart failure (HF), yet it remains under-recognized in clinical practice. Evidence supporting a direct causal relationship is limited, particularly with regard to relapse and long-term follow-up.Case summaryA 71-year-old woman with post-thyroidectomy hypoparathyroidism presented with new-onset HF characterized by progressive exertional dyspnoea, orthopnoea, and paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea. Echocardiography revealed left ventricular and left atrial dilatation with a reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (34%) and severe functional mitral regurgitation. Electrocardiography showed sinus rhythm with prolonged corrected QT interval and T-wave inversion. Coronary angiography excluded significant coronary disease, and no alternative causes of HF were identified. Laboratory testing demonstrated severe hypocalcaemia (1.18 mmol/L) and suppressed parathyroid hormone levels. Despite guideline-directed HF therapy, symptoms failed to improve. Intravenous and oral calcium supplementation, together with calcitriol, led to rapid symptomatic and functional recovery. Two months later, discontinuation of calcium supplementation resulted in recurrent hypocalcaemia and relapse of HF, despite ongoing guideline-directed HF therapy, and again resolved after calcium repletion. During 30 months of follow-up, adherence to supplementation maintained calcium homeostasis and was associated with preserved cardiac function, with no further HF recurrence.DiscussionThis case illustrates reversible and recurrent hypocalcaemic cardiomyopathy and highlights the importance of calcium homeostasis. The reproducible dechallenge–rechallenge pattern observed on two occasions provides clinically relevant evidence supporting a causal relationship between hypocalcaemia and myocardial dysfunction. Long-term biochemical monitoring and sustained adherence to calcium and vitamin D supplementation are important to prevent recurrence in patients with hypoparathyroidism.Keywords: Hypocalcaemia, Cardiomyopathy, Heart failure, Hypoparathyroidism, Calcium supplementation, Case report

Introduction

Hypocalcaemia is a common biochemical abnormality, most frequently associated with hypoparathyroidism, vitamin D deficiency, chronic kidney disease, or certain medications.1 Although neuromuscular manifestations are well recognized, cardiac involvement is uncommon and may be overlooked.2 In rare instances, severe hypocalcaemia can result in dilated cardiomyopathy and heart failure (HF), with reported recovery following correction of calcium levels—a condition referred to as hypocalcaemic cardiomyopathy.3

However, current knowledge is largely derived from isolated case reports with limited longitudinal follow-up.3–5 Evidence supporting a direct causal relationship between hypocalcaemia and HF remains largely observational, and documentation of recurrent cardiac dysfunction associated with fluctuations in serum calcium is particularly scarce.

Here, we report a patient with post-thyroidectomy hypoparathyroidism who experienced two distinct episodes of HF temporally associated with hypocalcaemia, both resolving after calcium repletion. The dynamic course and detailed long-term follow-up in this case offer a clinically illustrative example of the reversible yet potentially recurrent nature of hypocalcaemic cardiomyopathy.

Case presentation

A 71-year-old woman presented with a 20-day history of progressive exertional dyspnoea, orthopnoea, and paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea. Eight months earlier, she had undergone total thyroidectomy for papillary thyroid carcinoma and subsequently developed postsurgical hypoparathyroidism with persistent hypocalcaemia. Although calcium carbonate and calcitriol were prescribed, adherence was poor and supplementation was discontinued without supervision.

Months later, she experienced episodic tetany. Transthoracic echocardiography at a tertiary hospital revealed left ventricular (LV) and left atrial (LA) dilatation, LV ejection fraction (LVEF) 37%, and severe functional mitral regurgitation. She was diagnosed with HF with reduced ejection fraction and treated with guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) and loop diuretics, but symptoms persisted, prompting referral. On admission, blood pressure was 94/60 mmHg. Laboratory testing showed elevated B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP 443 pg/mL; reference range <100 pg/mL), markedly reduced serum calcium (1.18 mmol/L; reference range 2.11–2.52 mmol/L), elevated serum phosphate (3.20 mmol/L; reference range 0.85–1.51 mmol/L), and suppressed parathyroid hormone (PTH 1.7 pg/mL; reference range 18.5–88.0 pg/mL). Thyroid function, cardiac troponin I, and other myocardial enzymes were within normal limits.

Electrocardiography demonstrated sinus rhythm with prolonged corrected QT interval (QTc 651 ms) and T-wave inversion. Echocardiography confirmed severe global LV systolic dysfunction (LVEF 34%), LV/LA dilatation, moderate-to-severe functional mitral regurgitation, and diffusely reduced global longitudinal strain (−9.2%). Coronary angiography excluded flow-limiting coronary disease. Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) demonstrated LV dilatation and reduced LV systolic function. Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) imaging showed patchy intramural enhancement involving the basal septal, inferior, and lateral LV walls, without a typical subendocardial or transmural ischaemic distribution, consistent with a non-ischaemic cardiomyopathic pattern. Alternative causes of acute or subacute HF were excluded after comprehensive evaluation, including infection, pulmonary embolism, renal failure, autoimmune disease, infiltrative cardiomyopathy, and thyroid dysfunction. Intravenous calcium gluconate was initiated, along with oral supplementation of calcium carbonate and calcitriol. With correction of hypocalcaemia, symptoms improved rapidly. The BNP level decreased, LV dimensions normalized, LVEF improved to 50%, mitral regurgitation regressed to mild, and QTc shortened. These findings supported a diagnosis of hypocalcaemic cardiomyopathy.

After discharge, cardiac function further improved (LVEF 56%) under continued supplementation. Two months later, despite continued GDMT, the patient became non-adherent to calcium therapy. Three weeks thereafter, HF recurred with serum calcium 1.39 mmol/L (reference range 2.11–2.52 mmol/L), BNP 371 pg/mL (reference range <100 pg/mL), LV dilatation, severe mitral regurgitation, and LVEF 37%. Cardiac function did not improve despite continued GDMT until calcium supplementation was resumed, after which recovery again occurred. Initiation of calcium repletion led to normalization of serum calcium, recovery of cardiac function, and resolution of HF. Subsequent discontinuation of supplementation resulted in recurrent hypocalcaemia and HF relapse, which again resolved after calcium repletion. Sustained supplementation maintained normocalcaemia with preserved cardiac function and no further HF recurrence during long-term follow-up.

During 30 months of follow-up, including 26 months after the second episode, the patient continued long-term oral calcium supplementation with periodic intravenous calcium administration and regular follow-up visits. Serum calcium levels remained within the normal range with good adherence to therapy, and no further recurrence of HF was observed. Cardiac structure and function were preserved at the last evaluation, and the patient remained asymptomatic with full return to normal daily activities.

Discussion

This case adds to the existing knowledge of hypocalcaemic cardiomyopathy by documenting a repeated dechallenge–rechallenge pattern. While prior reports have described recovery of LV function after calcium correction, recurrence of HF associated with withdrawal of supplementation has been less clearly documented.3–5 In our patient, two distinct episodes of LV systolic dysfunction occurred in parallel with hypocalcaemia and resolved after calcium repletion. Cardiac function remained stable during 30 months of follow-up once calcium homeostasis was maintained. Although causality cannot be definitively established from a single case, the temporal sequence, reproducibility, and sustained stability after repletion strengthen the likelihood of a direct relationship between hypocalcaemia and myocardial dysfunction.

Recent reports have further emphasized hypocalcaemic cardiomyopathy as a reversible cause of dilated cardiomyopathy and HF. Ejjebli et al. described a case of reversible hypocalcaemic dilated cardiomyopathy associated with secondary hypoparathyroidism decompensated after COVID-19 infection, highlighting that metabolic vulnerability may become clinically apparent after an intercurrent illness.6 Mapelli et al. reported a case with comprehensive multimodality evaluation, including CMR tissue characterization and endomyocardial biopsy, highlighting the value of excluding myocarditis and other cardiomyopathic processes.4 Dang et al. recently described a post-thyroidectomy patient initially misdiagnosed with alcoholic cardiomyopathy, in whom calcium and calcitriol supplementation led to normalization of QTc and substantial recovery of LVEF.5 These cases underscore the importance of early recognition, calcium correction, and careful aetiological assessment. Compared with these reports, the present case further documents recurrence after discontinuation of calcium supplementation and recovery after re-treatment during the 30-month follow-up. Electrocardiographic abnormalities observed in this case are consistent with known electrophysiological effects of hypocalcaemia. Prolongation of the QT interval reflects delayed ventricular repolarization, as reduced extracellular calcium prolongs phase 2 of the ventricular action potential by delaying L-type calcium channel inactivation.7,8 The associated T-wave inversion may relate to heterogeneous repolarization across myocardial layers.9,10 Importantly, normalization of QT duration after calcium correction paralleled recovery of systolic function, further supporting a direct physiological effect.

The mechanisms underlying hypocalcaemia-induced myocardial dysfunction are likely multifactorial. Reduced intracellular calcium availability impairs actin–myosin interaction and cross-bridge cycling, resulting in diminished contractility.7,11,12 In addition, the absence of parathyroid hormone may further compromise myocardial performance.13 In the present case, the marked recovery of LV function after calcium repletion supports reversible contractile dysfunction as the predominant mechanism. However, acute-phase CMR also demonstrated patchy intramural LGE involving the basal septal, inferior, and lateral LV walls, without a typical subendocardial or transmural ischaemic distribution, supporting a non-ischaemic myocardial fibrosis or injury pattern rather than an ischaemic scar.

The relationship between hypocalcaemia and LGE should be interpreted cautiously. Late gadolinium enhancement is a key CMR marker of myocardial tissue abnormality and its distribution helps distinguish ischaemic from non-ischaemic injury patterns.14 In a recent CMR-based report of hypocalcaemic cardiomyopathy, Mapelli et al. observed increased native T1/T2 values and patchy intramyocardial LGE, used endomyocardial biopsy to exclude active myocarditis, and showed reverse remodelling on follow-up CMR; importantly, interventricular septal LGE persisted after several months despite complete recovery of biventricular systolic function.4 This observation suggests that prolonged hypocalcaemia may be accompanied by chronic myocardial abnormalities. Thus, severe, prolonged, or recurrent hypocalcaemia may theoretically contribute not only to reversible contractile dysfunction but also to myocardial stress, adverse remodelling, and focal fibrosis.14 In our patient, however, follow-up CMR and endomyocardial biopsy were not performed; therefore, whether the acute-phase LGE was directly caused by hypocalcaemia or reflected pre-existing non-specific fibrosis or other unrecognized myocardial injury remains uncertain.

Beyond pathophysiology, this case highlights an often underrecognized clinical dimension: adherence. Recurrence occurred after discontinuation of calcium supplementation despite ongoing guideline-directed HF therapy, and cardiac function did not improve until calcium replacement was resumed. This sequence supports hypocalcaemia as the principal reversible contributor to myocardial dysfunction in this patient. These findings suggest that hypocalcaemic cardiomyopathy may be not only reversible but also preventable through sustained biochemical monitoring and long-term supplementation in patients with hypoparathyroidism or postsurgical hypocalcaemia.

However, while ESC guidelines emphasize careful monitoring and correction of electrolyte disturbances—particularly in acute HF, such as abnormalities of sodium, potassium, and magnesium—specific recommendations for hypocalcaemia-related HF are not provided.15 Sporadic reports of hypocalcaemic cardiomyopathy suggest that this rare metabolic derangement warrants individual consideration in the management of HF.

Taken together, this report illustrates hypocalcaemic cardiomyopathy as a dynamic and potentially recurrent condition closely linked to calcium homeostasis. By documenting reproducible deterioration and recovery with extended follow-up, it provides clinically relevant evidence supporting hypocalcaemia as a reversible contributor to myocardial dysfunction.
