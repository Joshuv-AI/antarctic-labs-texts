Testing the cosmic distance duality relation using model-independent approach Preprint: APS/123-QED
Shubham Barua, Sujit K. Dalui, Rikiya Okazaki, Shantanu Desai

Abstract
In this work, we test the cosmic distance duality relation (CDDR) using the arbitrary redshift pivot Padé-(2,1) expansion methodology developed in Ref. Fazzari et al. 2025 . This approach allows us to constrain cosmographic parameters and test CDDR at a particular redshift. Further, it does not rely on data reconstructions or extrapolations of the cosmographic parameters to higher redshifts. We employ observational data from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument Baryon Acoustic Oscillation dataset, cosmic chronometers, and Type Ia supernovae from the Pantheon Plus and Dark Energy Survey Year 5 compilations. We find no significant deviations from the standard CDDR relation, with constraints reaching sub-percent precision across most redshifts in the range 0 ≲ z ≲ 1 0\lesssim z\lesssim 1 .



I
Introduction

The cosmic distance duality relation (CDDR)
Etherington 1933
is one of the fundamental tenets in cosmology. It is based on three fundamental assumptions
Bassett and Kunz 2004
: how the metric theory of gravity describes space-time, propagation of photons along null geodesics and photon number conservation. It does not assume any information about the expansion history of the universe and hence, is agnostic to the underlying cosmological model. The CDDR establishes a relation between two distance measures for an astronomical object - the luminosity distance (
D_{L}(z)
) and the angular diameter distance (
D_{A}(z)
). Mathematically, the relation is given by
D_{L}(z)=(1+z)^{2}D_{A}(z)
. Due to its fundamental nature, testing this relation implies testing the foundations of modern cosmology itself
Avgoustidis et al. 2010
;
Gonçalves et al. 2012
. Any violation of CDDR would point to a whole bunch of new Physics scenarios as recently reviewed in
Zhang et al. 2025
.

With the advent of precision cosmology, various tensions have come under scrutiny in the
\Lambda
CDM framework: the most prominent being the Hubble tension
Bernal et al. 2016
;
Bethapudi and Desai 2017
;
Vagnozzi 2020
;
Vagnozzi 2023
;
Zhao et al. 2022
;
James et al. 2022
;
Di Valentino et al. 2021
;
Verde et al. 2019
;
Abdalla et al. 2022
;
Scherer et al. 2025
;
Poulin et al. 2024
.
The Hubble tension refers to the discrepancy in measurements of the present expansion rate of the universe (
H_{0}
) between the CMB data and low-redshift probes and with statistical significance greater then
5\sigma
.
Studies indicate that CDDR violation may signal inconsistencies in distance calibrations among various cosmological probes
Efstathiou 2020
;
Teixeira et al. 2025
, which could partially underlie the observed tensions. Hence, assessing the validity of the CDDR is crucial in the context of current cosmological discrepancies.
In light of these tensions, it has become important to consider model-independent approaches to analyze cosmological data
Chakraborty et al. 2021
;
Mehrabi and Rezaei 2021
;
Naik et al. 2023
;
Cozzumbo et al. 2025
;
Ormondroyd et al. 2025a
;
Ormondroyd et al. 2025b
;
Berti et al. 2025
. Various such techniques exist in the literature, such as using Artificial Neural Networks (ANN)
Goh et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2021
;
Holanda et al. 2022
;
Qi et al. 2023
;
Abedin et al. 2025
, cosmography
Busti et al. 2015
;
Tucker et al. 2005
;
Dunsby and Luongo 2016a
, Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) methods
Seikel et al. 2012
;
Wang 2020
;
Colgáin and Sheikh-Jabbari 2021
;
Zhao et al. 2024
;
Velázquez et al. 2024
;
Mukherjee et al. 2024
;
Singirikonda and Desai 2020
and many more
Shah et al. 2024a
;
Shah et al. 2024b
;
Shah et al. 2023
;
Mukherjee et al. 2024
. Over the years, lots of combinations of cosmological probes and methodologies have been utilized to test CDDR. (See
Liu et al. 2021
;
Li et al. 2025
;
Qi et al. 2025
;
Holanda et al. 2010
;
Khedekar and Chakraborti 2011
;
Ma and Corasaniti 2018
;
Holanda et al. 2017
;
More et al. 2016
;
Zhou et al. 2021
;
Qi et al. 2019
;
Bora and Desai 2021
;
Mukherjee and Mukherjee 2021
;
Xu et al. 2022
;
Yang et al. 2019
;
Alfano and Luongo 2025
;
Zheng et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
da Silva et al. 2020
;
Gonçalves et al. 2020
;
Santos-da-Costa et al. 2015
;
Holanda et al. 2012
;
Gonçalves et al. 2012
;
Holanda et al. 2019
for a non-exhaustive list). In most of the works which test CDDR using cosmography
Yang et al. 2024
;
Jesus et al. 2025
, the constraints on the cosmographic parameters are obtained at redshift
z=0
. Then, considering a parameterization for
\eta(z)=\frac{D_{L}(z)}{D_{A}(z)(1+z)^{2}}
, the CDDR relation is tested over a wide range of redshifts. This process has the limitation that this test depends on extrapolation of cosmographic parameters. Ref.
Fazzari et al. 2025
(henceforth F25) introduced generalized Padé-(2,1) expansions around arbitrary pivot redshifts. This has the advantage of constraining the cosmographic parameters at any fiducial redshift.

In this work, we use the Padé-(2,1) expansions introduced in F25 to test CDDR at the pivot redshifts. This approach is advantageous because it does not rely on any extrapolation or reconstruction techniques or specific parameterizations. This paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
we introduce the datasets considered and the outline our methodology, in Sec.
III
we present our results and finally, in Sec.
IV
we summarize our findings.

II
Methodology

In this section we describe the datasets employed and the methodology adopted to test the CDDR.

•

Baryon Acoustic Oscillations
(BAO): We use the recent Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) data release 2
Abdul Karim et al. 2025
. The measurements we consider are
D_{V}/r_{d}
(for BGS),
D_{M}/r_{d}
,
D_{H}/r_{d}
and the correlations between them from different tracers for seven redshift bins - 0.295, 0.51, 0.71, 0.93, 1.32, 1.48, 2.33.
D_{M}(z)
is the transverse comoving distance,
D_{H}(z)
is the Hubble distance and
D_{V}(z)
is the angle averaged distance, all normalized to the sound horizon at the drag epoch (
r_{d}
). Since cosmographic methods like Padé and Taylor expansions are accurate only at low redshifts, they do not change early universe physics. Hence, in our analyses we consider a Planck-derived Gaussian prior on
r_{d}
taken to be
\mathcal{N}(147.09,0.26)
Mpc
Planck Collaboration et al. 2020
.

•

Cosmic Chronometers
(CC): Cosmic Chronometers directly measure the Hubble expansion rate
H(z)
by using massive, passively evolving galaxies as cosmic clocks
Jimenez and Loeb 2002
;
Borghi et al. 2022
. Motivated by the concerns raised in Ref.
Ahlström Kjerrgren and Mörtsell 2023
, we restrict our analysis to a subset of 15 data points
Moresco et al. 2012
;
Moresco 2015
;
Moresco et al. 2016
for which full covariance information, including correlations and systematics, is available
Moresco et al. 2018
;
Moresco et al. 2020
.

•

Supernovae
(SNe): We use PantheonPlus (PP)
Scolnic et al. 2022
and Dark Energy Survey (DES) Y5 SNe
DES Collaboration et al. 2024
samples in this work. PP consists of 1701 light curves of 1550 distinct Type Ia SNe. We remove SNe for
z\lesssim 0.01
due to strong peculiar velocity dependence
Brout et al. 2022
. DESY5 consists of 1829 Type Ia SNe (1635 in the range
0.1<z<1.13
and 194 external low-redshift sample spanning
0.025<z<0.1
). When using PP, we impose a uniform prior on the peak absolute magnitude:
M_{B}\in\mathcal{U}(-21,-18)

Chen et al. 2024
. For DESY5,
M_{B}
is marginalized over so no prior is required in this case.

Cosmography
Busti et al. 2015
;
Dunsby and Luongo 2016b
;
Tucker et al. 2005
describes the expansion history of the universe in a model-independent way by expressing cosmological observables in terms of derivatives of the scale factor or the expansion rate. This is done by carrying out a Taylor expansion of the cosmological observables about redshift
z=0
. Observational data are then used to constrain the cosmographic parameters
Visser 2004
at the present epoch: Hubble constant
\left(H_{0}\equiv\left.\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\right|_{z=0}\right)
, the deceleration parameter
\left(q_{0}\equiv-\left.\frac{1}{H_{0}^{2}}\frac{\ddot{a}}{a}\right|_{z=0}\right)
, the jerk parameter
\left(j_{0}\equiv\left.\frac{1}{H_{0}^{3}}\frac{\dddot{a}}{a}\right|_{z=0}\right)
and the snap parameter
\left(s_{0}=\left.\frac{1}{H_{0}^{4}}\frac{\ddddot{a}}{a}\right|_{z=0}\right)
. The limitation of Taylor expanding observables is evident. It diverges at higher redshifts
(z\gtrsim 1)
, which limits its applicability given that most datasets probe redshifts in that region
Aviles et al. 2012
.

To overcome this, many other series expansion strategies have been proposed
Vitagliano et al. 2010
;
Bamba et al. 2012
, the most popular being the Padé approximants
Capozziello et al. 2020
;
Gruber and Luongo 2014
;
Aviles et al. 2014
;
Wei et al. 2014
. Following the methodology of
1
, we use the Padé-(2,1) expansion as it provides the best compromise between accuracy, stability and the number of kinematic parameters
Capozziello and Ruchika 2019
;
Hu and Wang 2022
;
Yu et al. 2025
.
1
derived expressions for
H^{(2,1)}(z)
and
D_{L}^{(2,1)}(z)
expanded about an arbitrary pivot redshift
z_{0}
. This allows one to overcome the limitations of the traditional Padé approximants by being able to constrain cosmographic parameters at any particular redshift value directly and improve the precision of fitting cosmological data at higher redshifts. Using these expressions (presented below), we constrain the cosmographic parameters over the redshift range
0\leq z\leq 1
, with a step size of 0.1. For this purpose, we utilize different combinations of datasets (described above) specifically, BAO
+r_{d}
, BAO
+r_{d}+
SNe, BAO
+r_{d}+
SNe
+
CC, and BAO
+r_{d}
and SNe, where SNe is taken to be both PP and DESY5.

The cosmographic expansions for
H^{(2,1)}(z)
and
D_{L}^{(2,1)}(z)
are as follows
Fazzari et al. 2025
:

f^{(2,1)}(z)=a_{0}+(a_{1}-a_{0}b_{1})x+(a_{2}-a_{1}b_{1}+a_{0}b_{1}^{2})x^{2}+(-a_{2}b_{1}+a_{1}b_{1}^{2}-a_{0}b_{1}^{3})x^{3}+\mathcal{O}(x^{4}),

(1)

where the Padé parameters
(a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},b_{1})
can be fixed by comparing to the Taylor expansions of
H(z)
and
D_{L}(z)
which are given by

\displaystyle H^{\text{Tay}}(z)

\displaystyle=c_{0}+c_{1}x+c_{2}x^{2}+c_{3}x^{3}+\mathcal{O}(x^{4}),\text{where}

(2)

\displaystyle c_{0}

\displaystyle=H(z_{0})

(3)

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle=H(z_{0})A

(4)

\displaystyle c_{2}

\displaystyle=H(z_{0})B

(5)

\displaystyle c_{3}

\displaystyle=H(z_{0})C\text{ and}

(6)

\displaystyle D_{L}^{\text{Tay}}(z)

\displaystyle=c_{0}+c_{1}x+c_{2}x^{2}+c_{3}x^{3}+\mathcal{O}(x^{4}),\text{where}

(7)

\displaystyle c_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+z_{0}}{H(z_{0})}\left[z_{0}+\frac{A}{2}z_{0}^{2}+\frac{A^{2}-B}{3}z_{0}^{3}\right]

(8)

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{H(z_{0})}\left[1+2z_{0}+\frac{A}{2}z_{0}^{2}+\frac{A^{2}-B}{3}z_{0}^{3}\right]

(9)

\displaystyle c_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2H(z_{0})}\left[2-A(1+z_{0})\right]

(10)

\displaystyle c_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6H(z_{0})}\left[2(1+z_{0})(A^{2}-B)-3A\right].

(11)

In Eqns.
2
-
11
,
A=\frac{1+q(z_{0})}{1+z_{0}}
,
B=\frac{j(z_{0})-q(z_{0})^{2}}{2(1+z_{0})^{2}}
and
C=\frac{3q(z_{0})^{3}+3q(z_{0})^{2}-(4q(z_{0})+3)j(z_{0})-s(z_{0})}{6(1+z_{0})^{3}}
. Then, we fix the Padé parameters in Eqn.
1
using
a_{0}=c_{0}
,
a_{1}=c_{1}+c_{0}b_{1}
,
a_{2}=c_{2}+c_{1}b_{1}
and
b_{1}=-\frac{c_{3}}{c_{2}}
. Finally, using these Padé parameters, we can get the Padé-(2,1) expressions for
H^{(2,1)}(z)
and
D_{L}^{(2,1)}(z)
using

f^{(2,1)}(z)=\frac{a_{0}+a_{1}x+a_{2}x^{2}}{1+b_{1}x}

(12)

To test CDDR, we use the relation

\eta(z=z_{0})=\frac{D_{L}(z_{0})}{(1+z_{0})^{2}D_{A}(z_{0})},

(13)

where,
D_{L}(z)
is the luminosity distance at redshift
z
and
D_{A}(z)
is the angular diameter distance, given by

\displaystyle D_{L}(z)

\displaystyle=c(1+z)\int_{0}^{z}\frac{dz^{\prime}}{H(z^{\prime})}

(14)

\displaystyle D_{A}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{D_{M}(z)}{1+z}.

(15)

D_{M}(z)
,
D_{V}(z)
, and
D_{H}(z)
are given by

\displaystyle D_{M}(z)

\displaystyle=c\int_{0}^{z}\frac{dz^{\prime}}{H(z^{\prime})}

(16)

\displaystyle D_{H}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{H(z)}

(17)

\displaystyle D_{V}(z)

\displaystyle=[zD_{M}(z)^{2}D_{H}(z)]^{1/3}.

(18)

Finally, we state the relation between
M_{B}
and
D_{L}(z)
for Type Ia SNe:

m(z)=5\log_{10}\left[\frac{D_{L}(z)}{\text{Mpc}}\right]+25+M_{B},

(19)

where
m(z)
denotes the SNe apparent magnitude.

We use
Cobaya
sampler
Torrado and Lewis 2021
to perform Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling
Lewis and Bridle 2002
;
Lewis 2013
in order to constrain the cosmographic parameters at the pivot redshift
z_{0}
. We consider the chains to have converged for the Gelman-Rubin criterion of
R-1\lesssim 0.01

Gelman and Rubin 1992
. We use
GetDist

Lewis 2025
for analysis and visualization of the Bayesian posteriors. To test CDDR, we postprocess the chains and compute
\eta(z_{0})
values. We then calculate the median and the 68% credible intervals. In this work, we consider a flat universe (Eqn.
16
).

IV
Conclusions

In this work, we test the cosmic distance duality relation (CDDR) by applying the methodology of
1
to a combination of CC, DESI BAO, and SNe data from the PP and DESY5 compilations. Specifically, we employ the generalized Padé-(2,1) expansion about arbitrary pivot redshifts to obtain model-independent constraints on the CDDR parameter
\eta(z)
(cf. Eqn.
13
). This approach allows us to avoid assuming any specific parameterization of the cosmographic parameters. Instead, we directly determine the values of the cosmographic parameters and
\eta(z)
at a chosen pivot redshift. Moreover, our method does not require dataset reconstructions, which can introduce additional uncertainties, noise, or dependence on binning choices.

We find that the deviations in
\eta(z_{0})
are within
2\sigma
for all dataset combinations except for the “new dataset” and DESI
+r_{d}+
SNe
+
CC at the redshifts 0.5, 0.6, 0.7 and 0.8. The likely cause of this is due to the sub-percent precision of the error bars which can show large deviations even if the central value is not too far from the expected one.

Further, we see a decreasing trend in the central values of
\eta(z_{0})
in dataset combinations where DESI BAO data has been included. However, the deviations are still within
2\sigma
. It will be interesting to see what happens at higher redshifts when more data is available.

Overall, this work demonstrates the application of the arbitrary-pivot Padé-(2,1) expansion introduced in
1
, showing that
\eta(z)
can be constrained at sub-percent accuracy.
