Mechanism of blueberry anthocyanins inhibit glycation of bovine serum albumin-fructose/methylglyoxal revealed by LC ETD MS/MS
Authors: Dengle Duan, Siran Zhang, Ruanbing Zhang, Hui Wang, Yujuan Xu, Qin Wang, Yuanshan Yu, Wenhua Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn order to investigate the inhibitory mechanism of blueberry anthocyanins (BAs) on glycation, bovine serum albumin-fructose (BSA-Fru) and BSA-methylglyoxal (BSA-MGO) models induced by ultrasound-microwave were implemented. BAs exhibited concentration-dependent inhibition of glycation, reducing advanced glycation end products (AGEs) formation by approximately 60% in the BSA-Fru model and 32.58% in the BSA-MGO model at 1.0 mg/mL. Notably, BAs significantly increased the molecular weight of glycated BSA and maintain BSA secondary structure by mitigating the decrease in α-helix content. LC-ETD-MS/MS analysis revealed that BAs reduced glycation sites from 23 to 18 in BSA-Fru and from 11 to 6 in BSA-MGO, with key sites including K75 and K88 being protected. Additionally, BAs increased the molecular weight of glycated BSA and mitigated the decrease in α-helix content. These findings suggest that BAs is potential effective glycation inhibitors and could be used in food processing to inhibit the formation of AGEs.Keywords: Blueberry anthocyanins, Glycation inhibition, BSA-fructose, BSA-methylglyoxal, Glycation sites

Introduction

Maillard reaction is widely implied in food processing, such as baking, toasting, roasting, and frying (Anwar et al., 2021). By reacting carbonyl groups in reducing sugars with free amino groups in proteins, it produces the flavors, colors, and aromas behind some of the most beloved food products (Yang et al., 2018). However, this process yields harmful byproducts such as fructosamine, carboxy methyl lysine (CML) (L. Zhang et al., 2020), and advanced glycation end products (AGEs). These substances can inflict severe physiological damage to proteins and trigger a myriad of complications, including chronic diseases (Ravichandran et al., 2021). AGEs, in particular, accumulate in body tissues and lead to serious oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis, contributing to conditions like diabetes, Alzheimer's disease, cardiovascular disease, inflammation, and kidney failure (Rungratanawanich et al., 2021). This makes the inhibition of glycation and AGEs formation a compelling area of study.

Various compounds such as aminoguanidine (Ahmad et al., 2021), amlodipine (Maaa & Ynsa, 2020), and pyridoxamine (Mestry et al., 2018) have shown promise as potential glycation inhibitors. However, due to their toxicities and side effects, their development remains largely experimental. Therefore, the pursuit continues for natural, efficient, and non-toxic compounds to mitigate glycation. In this respect, polyphenols, abundantly found in nature, have drawn considerable interest. Several studies have demonstrated that natural polyphenols efficiently attenuate protein glycation without adverse effects and toxicities. Curcumin, for instance, has been found to counteract glucose-induced bovine serum albumin (BSA) glycation and AGEs formation (Fu et al., 2022), while catechin has reportedly hindered AGEs release by altering the conformation of glycated BSA (Q. Wu et al., 2021). Nelumbo nucifera leaf extracts, quercetin, and dragon fruit polyphenolics are among other natural compounds with notable effects on inhibiting glycation and AGEs formation (Ravichandran et al., 2021; L. Zhang, Lu, et al., 2018; L. Zhang, Zhang, et al., 2018). Moreover, vitexin is recognized as a promising glycation inhibitor that can safeguard the structure of BSA, sequester methylglyoxal (MGO), and modify glycation sites induced by interaction with BSA in BSA-fructose (BSA-Fru) and BSA-methylglyoxal (BSA-MGO) models (Ni et al., 2021). Accordingly, in order to study the different glycation stages, the BSA-Fru model is used to represent the entire glycation process, and the BSA-MGO model to illustrate the intermediate glycation stage.

Blueberry anthocyanins (BAs), one of the most abundant flavonoids, have demonstrated their ability to limit AGEs formation, subsequently preventing the browning and hardening of proteins (R. Wang et al., 2021). Recognized for their minimal adverse effects on normal cells and the body (Huang et al., 2020), BAs have been the subject of extensive research probing their nutritional and health benefits, with a primary emphasis on their antioxidant properties (S. Yang et al., 2021). Nevertheless, a comprehensive understanding of the effects of BAs on the structure and glycation sites of proteins remains underexplored (Cerezo et al., 2020).

The determination of protein structures typically employs some techniques, including sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR), circular dichroism spectroscopy (CD), ultraviolet-visible, and fluorescence spectroscopy. Despite these techniques offering insight into the overall protein structure, they may not fully elucidate intricate intraprotein changes and specific modifications following the introduction of glycation inhibitors. However, advanced detection strategies such as orbitrap tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) coupled with electron transfer dissociation (ETD) and liquid chromatography (LC) have bridged this gap. Consequently, LC ETD MS/MS has emerged as an integral tool for precision structural characterization and peptide sequencing at the molecular level.

Traditional glycation techniques involving room temperature for a week or constant water bath temperatures for several hours often struggle with precision control. The ultrasound-microwave induced glycation method alleviates this issue, presenting a more manageable, time-efficient, and user-friendly approach. This system simulates thermal processing conditions (e.g., baking, frying) commonly used in the food industry, enabling accelerated glycation under controlled conditions for mechanistically relevant studies. Validation under conventional heating is recommended to confirm translational relevance. This study centers around the inhibitory influence of BAs on glycation, specifically pinpointing alterations in glycated peptides and glycation sites of BSA. Fluorescence and ultraviolet spectroscopy were employed to detect glycation product content, while SDS-PAGE and matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) were used to analyze changes in glycated BSA's molecular weight. Furthermore, CD spectroscopy was employed to detail modifications in BSA's secondary structure, while LC ETD MS/MS was used explicitly for the identification of glycated peptides and glycation sites within the glycated BSA. The findings indicate that BAs possess a significant inhibitory effect on glycation, thereby suggesting their potential as glycation inhibitors in the food industry, notably in the processing of high-protein and high-carbohydrate foods.

Materials and methods

Materials

BAs was extracted from blueberry by ultrasound-microwave. Briefly, blueberry was juiced with 60% ethanol aqueous solution with solid-liquid ratio of 1:2 (g/mL). Then it was extracted at 50 °C for 25 min by ultrasound-microwave processing with power of 500 W and 100 W, respectively. After centrifuged at 4000 ×g for 10 min, it was filtered, freeze-dried (purity >95%), and finally stored at 4 °C until use. Blueberry was purchased from Dandong City, Liaoning Province, China. Bovine Serum Albumin was purchased from Beijing Lanjieke Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Fructose, methylglyoxal (40% aqueous solution), 5,5’-Dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB), Fluorescamine, Nitrotetrazolium Blue chloride (NBT) and Girard-T reagents were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All kits were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). All other reagents used were of analytical grade and purchased from Solarbio Chemical Co. (Shanghai, China).

Preparation of glycated BSA

The glycation model was established according to the method reported previously with some modifications (J. Peng et al., 2023). BSA-Fru model was prepared in BSA (20 mg/mL) and Fructose (100 mg/mL) with 100 mM, pH 7.4 PBS buffer solution. In brief, BSA solution was incubated with fructose solution at a proportion of 1:1 (v/v) and BAs at various concentrations (1, 0.5, 0.25 and 0.125 mg/mL). The blank group and control group without fructose or BAs were prepared in parallel to analyze the effect of BAs. After microwave irradiation (500 W) for 60 min with ultrasound (400 W) at 50 °C by ultrasound-microwave cooperation workstation (Atpio Instrument Manufacturing Co., Nanjing, China), reaction solutions were cooled in ice-bath immediately to stop the reaction. Finally, they were kept at 4 °C for further analysis. Similarly, the BSA-MGO model was produced by incubating BSA (1 mg/mL) with methylglyoxal (5 mM).

SDS-PAGE

SDS-PAGE was carried out on a Mini-protean Tetra Cell using 12% separating gel and 5% stacking gel. Firstly, samples (0.5 mg/mL) were mixed with loading buffer at a ratio of 4:1 (v/v) and boiled for 6 min. After centrifugation at 10000 ×g for 2 min, 25 μL of each sample and 3 μL of Marker were loaded on the gel lanes for electrophoresis. After the electrophoresis for 50 min, the gel sheets were stained with 0.1% Coomassie Blue R-250 for shaking 60 min, and decolored with distilled water (L. Zhang, Lu, et al., 2018).

MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry

Molecular weight analysis of BSA was conducted on a Ultraflex Extreme MALDI-TOF mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics, Germany) in positive ion mode (L. Zhang, Lu, et al., 2018). Moreover, 4-Chloro-α-cyanocinnamic acid (CHCA) was used as the matrix. Calibration Mixture 1 was used as calibration standard with mass error less than 1 ppm. All spectra were acquired in the mass range of 40,000–100,000 m/z. Each spectrum was collected with 500 laser shots.

Circular dichroism spectroscopy

Each sample was prepared using 100 mM PBS buffer (pH 7.4). The BRIGHTTIME Chirascan spectropolarimete was used to obtain the circular dichroism spectra of the samples. The scan range was 195–250 nm. The scan speed was 60 nm/min. The path length of the quartz cuvette was 1 mm. The spectra represent the average of three repeated scans of each sample (Y. Hu et al., 2019).

BSA digestion

Digestion of each sample was performed according to the FASP procedure. Briefly, the detergent, DTT and other low-molecular-weight components were removed by using 200 μL of UA buffer (8 M Urea, 150 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0) with repeated ultrafiltration (Microcon units, 10 kD) and facilitated by centrifugation. Then, 100 μL of 0.05 M iodoacetamide in UA buffer was added to block reduced cysteine residues. After incubated for 20 min at room temperature in darkness, they were orderly washed with 100 μL of UA buffer and 25 mM NH4HCO3 for three times. Then the protein suspension was digested with 3 μg of trypsin (Promega) in 40 μL of 25 mM NH4HCO3 overnight at 37 °C. Finally, the peptides were collected and processed forward.

Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry

LC-MS were performed on a Orbitrap Fusion™ Lumos™ Tribrid™ Mass Spectrometer that was coupled to Easy nLC (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The peptide mixture was loaded onto a the C18-reversed phase column (15 cm long, 75 μm inner diameter, 5 μm resin) in buffer A (0.1% Formic acid in HPLC-grade water) and separated with a linear gradient of buffer B (0.1% Formic acid in 84% acetonitrile) at a flow rate of 250 mL/min controlled by IntelliFlow technology over 60 min. MS data was acquired using a data-dependent top10 method, dynamically choosing the most abundant precursor ions from the survey scan (300–1800 m/z) for ETD fragmentation. Determination of the target value is based on predictive Automatic Gain Control (pAGC). Dynamic exclusion duration was 20 s. Survey scans were acquired at a resolution of 70,000 at m/z 200 and resolution for ETD spectra was set to 17,500 at m/z 200. Normalized collision energy was 27 eV and the underfill ratio, which specifies the minimum percentage of the target value likely to be reached at maximum fill time, defined as 0.1%.

Statistical analysis

All physical and chemical measurements were repeated three times and the results were showed as mean ± standard deviation. Origin 2022b was used for data analysis. The variance, significance difference and correlation were analyzed by SPSS 20.0 (P < 0.05).

Results and discussion

Effect of BAs on glycation oxidation

Protein oxidation during glycation can be identified through the depletion of protein thiols and the escalation of protein carbonylation (Sarmah & Roy, 2022). The influence of blueberry anthocyanins (BAs) on glycation oxidation is depicted in Fig. 1. As shown in Fig. 1A, the thiol group content of bovine serum albumin (BSA) witnessed a reduction by 29.5% and 6.0% post-glycation in the BSA-Fructose (BSA-Fru) and BSA-Methylglyoxal (BSA-MGO) models, respectively. However, in comparison to glycated BSA in the absence of BAs, the samples glycated with BAs demonstrated a significant enhancement in thiol group content in both models, suggesting that BAs remarkably hinder BSA oxidation. Notably, with the incremental concentration of BAs, the thiol group content of glycated BSA in both models amplified, reaching a peak value at a BAs concentration of 1.0 mg/mL.

Effect of BAs on the free thiol group (A), protein carbonyl group (B), fructosamine (C), α-dicarbonyl compounds (D), CML (E) and AGEs (F) of BSA samples, respectively. Means with different letters (a-d/a-e) in the bars are significantly different (P < 0.05).

Similarly, as indicated in Fig. 1B, the carbonyl content of BSA escalated by 11.7- and 9.4-folds following glycation in the BSA-Fru and BSA-MGO models, respectively. In contrast to glycation without BAs, glycated samples containing BAs displayed a significant reduction in carbonyl content across both models, indicating that BAs prominently inhibit BSA oxidation. Correspondingly, as BAs concentration increased, the carbonyl content of glycated BSA diminished, recording the lowest value at a BAs concentration of 1.0 mg/mL. It can be inferred that BAs mitigate the depletion of the thiol group and the generation of carbonyls in glycated proteins in a concentration-dependent manner, corroborating previous findings (Q. Zhang et al., 2021). During glycation, the generation of reactive oxygen species prompts the oxidation of amino acid residues in the protein into carbonyl derivatives, subsequently weakening the protein's oxidative defense by reducing thiol groups (Khan et al., 2022). Additionally, the oxidation of thiol groups triggers conformational alterations in the protein structure, subsequently forming disulfide bonds and generating an elevated level of free radicals. Thus, the results underscore that BAs could potentially serve as effective agents in mitigating oxidative damage to proteins.

Effect of BAs on glycation products

In the early glycation stage, fructosamine, a typical stable Amadori product, is formed during the reaction between a protein's free amino group and a reducing sugar's carbonyl group (L. Zhang et al., 2020). The inhibition rate of fructosamine is shown in Fig. 1C. Compared to glycated BSA, the fructosamine levels of BSA glycated with BAs saw a maximum reduction of 15.02% in the BSA-Fru model and 76.54% in the BSA-MGO model. This evidence suggests that exhibit the inhibitory effects of BAs in the BSA-MGO model is greater than that in the BSA-Fru model. Fructosamine arises from a thermodynamically unstable Schiff base, and BAs stabilize the reaction system, thereby suppressing the Schiff base's transformation during glycation's early stage (Mestry et al., 2018). Consequently, BSA glycation inhibition follows a positive correlation to BAs concentration.

In glycation's intermediate stage, α-dicarbonyl compounds, precursors of AGEs, were generated. The effect of BAs on inhibiting these compounds is shown in Fig. 1D. In the BSA-Fru model, when BAs concentrations ranged from 0.125 to 1.0 mg/mL, α-dicarbonyl compounds were substantially inhibited, reaching an maximum inhibition rate close to 60% at 1.0 mg/mL. In the BSA-MGO model, the maximum inhibition value was 14% within the same concentration range. Furthermore, as the BAs concentration increased, their capacity to scavenge α-dicarbonyl compounds amplified. The findings imply that BAs possess superior α-dicarbonyl trapping capability in glycation's intermediate stage. Amadori products undergo dehydration and rearrangement to form α-dicarbonyl compounds through oxidation reactions (D. Zhang et al., 2021). Suppression of superoxide and hydroxyl radicals could alleviate oxidative stress and reduce α-dicarbonyl compounds generation (W. Zhao et al., 2022). BAs effectively prevented protein oxidation by scavenging these radicals, thereby inhibiting the formation of α-dicarbonyl compounds.

In glycation's final stage, complex reactions such as rearrangement, oxidation, and dehydration occur, culminating in the production of detrimental AGEs (Liu et al., 2022), among which CML is a significant non-fluorescent AGE. The effect of BAs on CML inhibition in BSA-Fru and BSA-MGO models is presented in Fig. 1E. The CML inhibition rate escalated with increasing BAs concentration, reaching a maximum value of 89.65% at 1.0 mg/mL of BAs. A similar trend was observed in the BSA-MGO model, suggesting BAs possess significant (p < 0.05) protective effects against CML formation.

Moreover, the influence of BAs on AGEs inhibition is shown in Fig. 1F. When BAs concentrations ranged from 0.125 to 1.0 mg/mL, the AGEs inhibition rate rose from 26.75% to 60% in the BSA-Fru model, while in the BSA-MGO model, the AGEs inhibition rate of glycated BSA varied from 16.71% - 32.58% with the same BAs concentrations. A BAs concentration of 1.0 mg/mL achieved maximum inhibition, whereas a concentration of 0.125 mg/mL yielded the lowest values. These results showed that the AGEs inhibition rate in BSA-Fru model was more pronounced than that in BSA-MGO model. These results showed that the AGEs inhibition rate in BSA-Fru model was more pronounced than that in BSA-MGO model. Indicate a more pronounced AGEs inhibition rate in the BSA-Fru model than in the BSA-MGO model, suggesting that BAs effectively inhibit the glycation process's end stage, aligning with the CML content results (Fig. 1E). Nonetheless, compared to the BSA-Fru model, BAs' AGEs inhibition in the BSA-MGO model was marginally weaker, implying that MGO may have stronger binding activity with BSA (Ni et al., 2021).

Protein glycation is concurrent with oxidative reactions. Free radicals, generated from these oxidative reactions, lead to oxidative damage and serve as reactive intermediates accelerating protein modification and AGEs formation (Akagawa, 2021). In addition, α-dicarbonyl compounds are more reactive than reducing sugars as they can react with protein's amino groups, leading to protein cross-links and subsequent AGEs formation (Eggen & Glomb, 2021). It's hypothesized that BAs exhibit potent antioxidant activity, inhibiting the interaction of dicarbonyl compounds with proteins and thus reducing AGEs formation. It's evident from the findings that BAs possess a significant ability to break down AGEs' cross-linking structure.

The inhibition of glycation is strongly associated with antioxidant activity. However, the antioxidant activity of BAs may not solely account for their glycation inhibitory action. Other mechanisms of glycation inhibition have been proposed, such as disrupting the cross-linking structures of AGEs and hindering the formation of late-stage Amadori products (Lee et al., 2022). Additionally, the same BAs concentration in different models yields distinct inhibitory effects on glycation products in early, intermediate, and end stages. This differential effect may stem from the irreversible reaction between MGO and amino acids, specifically lysine (K) and arginine (R), leading to a decreased BAs inhibition rate for glycation products in the intermediate and end stages. Furthermore, the BAs inhibited the glycoxidation of BSA by scavenging free radicals (J. Peng et al., 2023), while fructose could undergo irreversible reactions with BSA by forming covalent bonds, and exhibited stronger free radical scavenging capacity. Hence, the concentration or dosage of these natural, non-toxic bioactive ingredients should be increased in food processing applications. Furthermore, microencapsulation techniques, including encapsulating multiple distinct cores within a continuous wall material coating, warrant further research for preserving BAs activity. Multiple coatings could facilitate slow BAs release, enabling them to function at each glycation stage.

Molecular weight analysis

The influence of glycation and BAs on the molecular weight of BSA in BSA-Fru and BSA-MGO models was examined using SDS-PAGE. Fig. 2A shows a distinct band at around 66 kDa for the BSA samples, which aligns with the BSA molecular weight (66.4 kDa). Noticeably, significant differences were observed between BSA and glycated BSA with BAs in the electrophoretogram's mobilities. Compared to the BSA-Fru model, bands of glycated BSA were markedly shifted upwards in the BSA-MGO model, indicating an increase in BSA's molecular weight. These results suggest the formation of BSA cross-linking induced by glycation.

To more accurately determine the changes in molecular weight, MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry analysis was employed (Zhong et al., 2021). As illustrated in Fig. 2B, the molecular weight of BSA was about 66.17 kDa, which significantly increased to 66.22 kDa post-glycation in the BSA-Fru model. In contrast, the molecular weight dramatically increased to 67.06 kDa in the BSA-MGO model (as shown in Fig. 2C). In the presence of 1.0 mg/mL and 0.5 mg/mL of BAs, the molecular weight of glycated BSA increased to 66.37 kDa and 66.28 kDa (BSA-Fru) and 68.64 kDa and 68.16 kDa (BSA-MGO), respectively. Notable changes were observed in the molecular weight of glycated BSA with BAs compared to glycated BSA without BAs. This is because BAs attach to BSA and protect it from glycation, a finding consistent with the SDS-PAGE analysis results (R. Wang et al., 2021). The observed molecular weight increase is attributed to non-covalent binding between BAs and BSA. This interpretation is supported by the absence of characteristic covalent modification shifts (e.g., +162 Da for glycation), and is consistent with subsequent analyses showing structural stabilization (Section 3.4), which is more consistent with non-covalent association than with disruptive covalent modification. Furthermore, the increase of molecular weight in BSA-MGO is more than that in BSA-Fru. These results demonstrated that the BSA molecular weight increased due to fructose and methylglyoxal attachment induced by glycation (Ni et al., 2021; J.-W. Wu et al., 2009). Therefore, Circular Dichroism (CD) spectroscopy was employed to confirm whether BSA underwent glycation modification.

Secondary structure analysis

CD spectroscopy was used to examine the changes in secondary structure of BSA samples (Miles et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 3A, the α-helix structure decreased from 66.7% to 44.3% after glycation in the BSA-Fru model, while β-sheet content increased from 4.5% to 9.7%, and β-turn content rose from 1.5% to 22.4%. These results suggest that BSA folds after glycation, leading to a transformation from α-helix to β-sheet and β-turn. The two negative peaks at 208 nm and 222 nm, which represent the absorption peak of the α-helix (J. Zhao et al., 2022), shifted upwards, indicating a decrease in α-helix content. Contrarily, prior research reported an increase in α-helix content after glycation (W. Zhao et al., 2022). After treated with 1 mg/mL of BAs, the α-helix content of glycated BSA was 50.7% in the BSA-Fru model, with BAs causing a slight shift of the glycated BSA molecule towards the α-helix. However, in the BSA-MGO model, a different trend was observed (as shown in Fig. 3B); compared to BSA, glycation reduced the α-helical content from 51.3% to 26.5%, with an increase in random coil structures. Furthermore, the damage to the α-helix of BSA in the BSA-MGO model was even more significant than that in the BSA-Fru model.

The secondary structure of proteins is determined by the sequence of amino acids and the interactions between different molecules (Xu et al., 2021). Glycation can induce the protein unfolding, resulting in the transformation of protein structure from α-helix to β-sheet (Y. M. Hu et al., 2021). The results suggested that fructose-induced glycation disrupts these interactions, resulting in alterations in BSA's secondary structure. The increase in disulfide bridge formation and intermolecular forces may primarily contribute to the rise of β-sheet and β-turn structures in the protein. These changes could be related to varying glycation conditions, including pH, reaction time, and temperature. Moreover, BAs significantly inhibit the decrease of α-helix content, helping maintain BSA's structure stability (J. Peng et al., 2023). The exact mechanisms by which BAs impact BSA glycation by fructose and methylglyoxal remain unclear. Therefore, LC-MS/MS analysis was conducted to confirm the glycation modification of BSA.

Identification of glycated peptides and glycation sites

To better illustrate the mechanism of glycation inhibition of BSA by BAs, the glycated peptides and glycation sites were identified by LC-MS/MS. The peptide sequences of BSA were accurately analyzed by matching the c-series ions and z-series ions obtained by the ETD MS/MS fragmentation ions in the database Maxquant and Proteome Discover. The glycated peptides were mapped by detecting the mass shift. Theoretically, if a peptide was modified by one fructose molecule, m/z peaks with 1, 2, 3, and 4 charges will display a mass shift of 162.0528 Da, 81.0264 Da, 54.0176 Da, and 40.5132 Da, respectively. If modified by two fructose molecules, these values will double.

As detailed in Table 1 and depicted in Fig. 4, the peptide sequences and glycated sites of BSA with and without BAs were identified. Peptides glycated by fructose were easily discernible from the mass increase resulting from glycation. For example, in the BSA-Fru model, the unglycated peptide 123–138 with m/z of 951.43932+ was observed to have its glycated form show a mass shift of 81.0248 Da, confirming modification by one fructose molecule at the K130 site. Similarly, glycated forms of other peptides were identified, with mass shifts indicating single fructose glycation. In addition, the m/z of 757.58764+ had an increase mass of 121.5389 with the unglycated form (636.04874+) in peptide 118–138, demonstrating that the peptide was simultaneously glycated by two molecules of fructose.

Mechanism of BAs inhibit ultrasound-microwave induced glycation in BSA-Fru and BSA-MGO models

In this study, the Bovine Serum Albumin-Fructose (BSA-Fru) and Bovine Serum Albumin-Methylglyoxal (BSA-MGO) models are employed to represent distinct phases of the glycation process. The BSA-Fru model replicates the full glycation procedure, while the BSA-MGO model symbolizes an intermediate phase with less MGO content and partial glycation of BSA. In the BSA-MGO model, the concentration of BSA is set to be 1 mg/mL. BAs significantly inhibited glycation in both models, an effect closely linked to their antioxidant properties. Studies have shown that BAs reduced oxidative stress by mitigating thiol loss and carbonyl formation, thereby suppressing glycation intermediate production.

Known for their strong antioxidant activity due to their phenolic hydroxyl group, BAs have been shown to impact glycation processes. Glycation involves oxidation of protein's amino acid residues to carbonyl groups, incurring a loss of the protein's thiol group (Khan et al., 2022). However, BAs could reverse the deletion of thiol group and the generation of carbonyls in glycated proteins. When BAs are present, there is a less pronounced decrease in thiol and carbonyl groups of BSA following glycation in both BSA-Fru and BSA-MGO models. BAs also protect proteins from oxidative damage by scavenging superoxide and hydroxyl radicals (L. Zhang et al., 2023; Q. Zhang et al., 2021), which are generated through disulfide bond formation during glycation. Such antioxidant action mitigates oxidative stress and impedes the development of glycated intermediates, including fructosamine and α-dicarbonyl compounds. This activity is reflected in a decrease in the levels of these compounds. In addition, BAs obstruct the formation of glycated intermediates leading to a notable reduction in AGEs and CML. The potency of glycation inhibition in BSA seems to be enhanced with the increase in BAs concentration. The differential inhibition rates between the two models can be attributed to the distinct glycation mechanisms involved. In the BSA-Fru model, glycation proceeds via the Maillard reaction with reversible intermediates, whereas in the BSA-MGO model, MGO directly forms irreversible adducts with lysine and arginine residues, limiting the protective effect of BAs. When comparing glycated BSA without BAs to that with BAs, it is observed that the molecular weight of the latter is slightly elevated. This increase is attributed to the binding between BAs and BSA, which not only inhibits the glycation of BSA but also stabilizes the glycated BSA molecule. This interaction is reflected in a decrease in β-sheet and β-turn content, leading to an increase in the α-helix content in BSA's secondary structure (X. Peng, Hu, et al., 2023; Y. Zhang, Zhang, et al., 2023).

Furthermore, LC ETD MS/MS analysis reveals a reduction in the number of glycated sites from 23 in the BSA-Fru model (without BAs) to 18 when BAs are present at a concentration of 1.0 mg/mL. In the BSA-MGO model, the number of glycated sites decreases from 11 (without BAs) to 6. Also, the locations of glycation sites differ in glycated BSA samples containing BAs. This suggests that the binding between BAs and BSA may obstruct certain BSA glycation sites. This binding also appears to influence the glycation reactivity of lysine and arginine residues due to changes in BSA's conformational structure and microenvironment within the BSA-Fru and BSA-MGO models. Specifically, residues such as K75 and K88 (domain I) are involved in ligand binding; their protection by BAs likely prevents local unfolding. K130 near Sudlow site I and K256 (domain II) are also shielded. Although direct binding data are lacking, we hypothesize that BAs bind non-covalently to Sudlow sites I/II, creating steric hindrance that reduces accessibility of these lysine residues to glycation agents. Collectively, these factors contribute to the observed glycation inhibition of BSA by BAs.

Conclusion

In this investigation, the role of BAs in inhibiting BSA glycation across different models was meticulously examined, and the underlying mechanism was elucidated via high-resolution mass spectrometry. BAs demonstrated a potent inhibitory effect on BSA glycation, showing an inhibition efficacy positively correlated with their concentration. Glycation typically induces the unfolding of BSA and an increase in molecular weight. However, these structural alterations were mitigated when BAs were bound to BSA. Importantly, the binding of BAs not only altered but also reduced the number of glycated sites on BSA. These insights significantly enhance our understanding of the mechanism through which BAs inhibit protein glycation, positioning BAs as potential anti-glycation agents. Future research efforts should aim to characterize the interaction between BSA and BAs more comprehensively, and further investigate the influence of this interaction on the glycation-inhibiting properties of BAs.
