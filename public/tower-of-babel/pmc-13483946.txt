Numerical simulation of productivity evaluation for multi-cluster fracturing in horizontal wells considering non-uniform proppant placement within fractures
Authors: Yixuan Wang, Jiexin Li, Yanchao Li, Kun Dai, Xudong Zhao, Jingyun Huang, Yuanxin Song, Guchang Zhang, Tiankui Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNon-uniform proppant placement during horizontal well hydraulic fracturing significantly impairs fracture conductivity and well productivity. Most existing models oversimplify conductivity distribution and lack integration between proppant transport and production prediction. This study presents a novel integrated numerical framework that fully couples fracture propagation, proppant transport, and post‑fracturing production. Unlike previous approaches that treat conductivity as uniform or decouple the physical processes, our model incorporates a dynamic conductivity evolution submodel that updates fracture conductivity in real time based on local closure stress and proppant concentration, enabling a more realistic representation of proppant placement effects. The model is validated against the commercial reservoir simulator ECLIPSE, with a cumulative production mismatch of less than 8% over 300 days, confirming its reliability. A two‑way coupled approach then systematically evaluates the impact of key pumping parameters: sand injection method, proppant size, injection rate, fluid viscosity, sand concentration, and perforation design. Results demonstrate that parameter sensitivity strongly depends on reservoir permeability. In ultra‑low permeability reservoirs (1 × 10⁻⁶ µm²), propped fracture area is the dominant productivity control, favoring constant‑concentration sand addition with finer proppant (70/140 mesh), lower injection rates, and lower viscosities to maximize fracture length. In higher permeability reservoirs (1 × 10⁻³ µm²), fracture conductivity becomes critical, with optimal performance achieved using constant‑concentration addition of coarser proppant (30/50 mesh), higher injection rates, and higher viscosities. Increasing sand ratio improves production in both reservoir types, though with diminishing returns. These findings provide clear, permeability‑specific guidelines for optimizing fracturing design and enhancing well performance.Keywords: Hydraulic fracturing, Non-uniform proppant placement, Fracture conductivity, Numerical simulation, Productivity predictionSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

For the extraction of unconventional reservoir resources, the commonly used technique is horizontal well hydraulic fracturing1,2. The fractures created after hydraulic fracturing of horizontal wells not only affect the productivity of the horizontal wells themselves but also influence water injection efficiency and economic benefits3,4. During hydraulic fracturing, proppants are added to ensure that the fractures maintain a certain degree of conductivity5,6. Achieving a uniform distribution of proppants within the fracture during operation is highly challenging. In most cases, the proppants inside the post-fracture fractures end up being distributed unevenly, leading to reduced effective fracture surface area, near-well fracture closure, and other issues that impair production enhancement7. Currently, the impact of non-uniform proppant distribution on well performance remains poorly understood. Most productivity prediction models simplify the distribution of fracture conductivity, seriously compromising the accuracy of post-fracturing productivity forecasts8,9.

Proppant fracture conductivity is a key parameter influencing hydraulic fracturing effectiveness. Current research primarily falls into two categories: laboratory experiments and mathematical modeling. Mathematical models for permeability and conductivity often rely on the C–K equation10. Cooke et al. conducted the first study on conductivity in fractures with high proppant concentrations, examining the effects of fracture geometry, proppant type, and fracturing fluid viscosity11. Parker and McDaniel used laboratory tests to investigate how filter cakes formed under varying proppant concentrations affect fracture conductivity12. Makurat et al. demonstrated experimentally that stress and deformation influence fracture permeability, highlighting the role of fracture surface characteristics and stress state in controlling fluid flow13. Fredd et al. experimentally analyzed conductivity changes under different fracturing conditions, emphasizing the importance of fracture surface roughness and displacement at low proppant concentrations14. Bortolan et al. studied the relationship between partially propped fracture conductivity and well productivity, though their work considered only proppant compression effects while neglecting mechanisms such as embedment, dissolution, and diffusion15. Zhang et al. employed a coupled discrete element and computational fluid dynamics approach to simulate proppant embedment and conductivity evolution after fracturing16. Cao et al. focused on factors including proppant size, closure pressure, and fracture surface characteristics, finding that larger proppant size and higher concentration enhance conductivity, whereas high closure pressure significantly reduces it17. Guo et al. investigated the relationship between proppant breakage rate and conductivity through theoretical and experimental analysis of breakage behavior under varying closure pressures18.

Research on quantitative models for fracture conductivity reveals a range of approaches, each with distinct limitations. Early work by Darin and Huitt applied the Carman-Kozeny (C-K) equation to partially propped fractures, indicating conductivity an order of magnitude higher than fully propped fractures19. However, this model’s assumption of uniform single-layer proppant distribution deviates from field conditions. Experimental studies by Wen et al., using apparatus like the FCES-100, yielded analytical expressions linking conductivity to effective stress and time20. While valuable, these empirical correlations lack a mechanistic explanation of how individual factors influence conductivity. Later models incorporated more complex physics but faced practical challenges. Lee et al.’s permeability characterization, which accounts for stress-induced chemical processes, is hampered by numerous difficult-to-obtain parameters and computational complexity, making integration with production simulations cumbersome21. Zhang et al. proposed a novel theoretical framework based on population balance equations, integrating proppant properties, fracture design, and formation mechanics22. Conversely, Barree et al. developed a statistical model from experimental data that is broadly applicable for evaluating various proppant types but overlooks critical mechanisms like proppant embedment23. Recent efforts focus on specific mechanistic insights. Chen et al. modeled proppant embedment as an elastoplastic process, providing a valuable formula, though it omits other geochemical mechanisms24. Teng et al. introduced an analytical model derived from a modified Brinkman equation, offering improved accuracy across flow regimes by considering both fracture width and proppant-pack properties25. Nevertheless, its reliance on idealized assumptions regarding these parameters restricts its utility in complex field settings. In summary, while existing models have progressively incorporated more physical processes—from empirical stress-time relationships to embedment and complex flow dynamics—a persistent gap remains for a comprehensive, mechanistically robust, and practically applicable model that integrates key factors without excessive parameterization or idealized simplifications.

Various numerical simulation approaches have been developed to study productivity in fractured reservoirs. Raymond et al. applied a similarity transformation to derive a mathematical model for vertically fractured oil wells, incorporating the effects of fracture conductivity, formation damage, and incomplete near-wellbore fracture fillings26. For modeling multiphase flow in complex fracture systems, Jong-Gyun utilized the finite element method to solve discrete fracture models27, while S. H. Lee developed a hierarchical model for multiscale fracture flow, though its solution process is computationally intensive28. Al-Salem et al. employed finite difference simulations to evaluate tight oil reservoir development, demonstrating that optimized well placement could achieve a stable 20-year commercial production period29. To enhance computational efficiency, Ghaderi et al. proposed an Embedded Discrete Fracture Model (EDFM), which calculates fracture-matrix transfer functions on a coarse grid30. The EDFM handles complex fracture networks more efficiently than traditional constrained Delaunay methods, but can demand significant memory resources for highly intricate systems. In summary, while significant advancements have been made—ranging from analytical transformations and various numerical discretization methods to efficient hybrid models like EDFM—a key challenge remains in developing a robust, computationally efficient, and comprehensive simulation framework that fully integrates dynamic geomechanical effects, complex fracture network geometries, and multi-phase flow physics to reliably predict long-term productivity in fractured reservoirs.

Despite these advances, a critical knowledge gap persists: existing models for productivity evaluation either assume uniform fracture conductivity, neglect the dynamic evolution of conductivity after proppant placement, or decouple fracture propagation from proppant transport and production. Consequently, they cannot quantify how non-uniform proppant distribution—induced by different pumping schedules—translates into spatially varying conductivity and ultimately impacts long-term well performance. To bridge this gap, this study develops an engineering-scale proppant transport model using the Euler–Euler method, fully coupled with a fracture propagation solver and a post-fracturing production model. The model employs a multiphysics solver to handle the stress-seepage coupling equations and incorporates local mesh refinement for accurately resolving proppant concentration within fractures. A key novelty is the conductivity‑evolution submodel embedded into the fracture elements, which updates conductivity in real time based on local closure stress and proppant concentration, thereby enabling the first integrated framework that can dynamically simulate the entire process from fracturing to production. This integrated simulation framework offers a robust numerical tool for optimizing fracturing design and enhancing well performance in heterogeneous reservoirs, and is uniquely capable of addressing questions—such as how sand‑addition methods affect productivity through conductivity distribution patterns—that existing decoupled models cannot answer.

Numerical scheme

The numerical simulations of fracture propagation and proppant transport presented in this paper are conducted using the numerical model published by Chen et al.32. This model is designed for hydraulic fracturing in unconventional reservoirs and addresses non‑uniform multi‑fracture initiation and proppant migration during multi‑cluster staged fracturing of horizontal wells. Specifically, Chen et al.’s model solves the coupled system of stress and fluid flow (Eq. 13) to predict fracture geometry, and employs an Euler–Euler approach to simulate proppant transport. The original contribution of the present work lies in three aspects: (i) we develop a conductivity‑evolution submodel that converts the normalized proppant concentration from Chen et al.’s model into a physically meaningful fracture conductivity distribution (Section “3.2“); (ii) we embed this conductivity field into a production model, thereby achieving a fully integrated framework that couples fracture propagation, proppant transport, and post‑fracturing production for the first time; (iii) we use this framework to conduct a systematic parametric study under two reservoir permeabilities, yielding permeability‑specific optimization guidelines. The solution procedure is illustrated in Fig. 1. The production simulation adopts a single-phase flow assumption, which is valid for the early production stage of unconventional reservoirs.

Flowchart for calculating the initial conductivity.

Relevant studies26 have shown that hydraulic fractures are not complex structures characterized by random branching; rather, they predominantly consist of parallel planar domains. Consequently, subsequent research has generally disregarded the opening of natural fractures and the entry of proppants. Using the developed numerical model for proppant transport, we calculated the distribution of fracture widths for each fracture cluster as well as the normalized concentration distribution of proppants within the fractures. After the fracturing operation is completed, the closure stress acting on the fracture walls causes unpropped fracture segments to close. The initial fracture conductivity of the propped fracture segments primarily depends on several key factors: the distribution of proppants, the physical properties of the proppants themselves, the closure stress in the formation, and the matrix Young’s modulus, totaling two parameters. By post-processing the simulated normalized proppant concentration data, we obtained proppant concentration units(kg·m-2) consistent with the field fracturing design. We then embedded these proppant concentration values and their corresponding coordinates into the fracture elements using interpolation functions, thereby dynamically integrating the fracture propagation and proppant transport simulation results with the initial fracture conditions of the horizontal well fracturing production model. This approach enabled us to calculate the distribution of initial fracture conductivities for each fracture cluster.

By conducting secondary development on the established horizontal well fracturing production model, we have integrated the propped fracture and fracture conductivity calculation models developed in the preceding section into the production model, thereby exploring the mechanism by which the distribution of fracture conductivity under different fracturing operation parameters affects production capacity.

To ensure that the numerical results are independent of the spatial discretization, a mesh sensitivity analysis was performed for the COMSOL Multiphysics model. Three mesh resolutions were tested, with the characteristic element size along the fracture path set to 1.0 m (coarse), 0.5 m (medium), and 0.25 m (fine), corresponding to total element counts of approximately 80,000, 150,000, and 280,000, respectively. The time-stepping and convergence criteria were kept identical across all three meshes. The medium mesh shows excellent convergence, with all metrics agreeing with the fine mesh to within 0.6%. Considering that the medium mesh requires approximately 40% of the CPU time of the fine mesh, it was adopted for all simulations in this study. This analysis confirms that the numerical results presented in Sect. 5 are effectively grid-independent.

Model validation

The finite element numerical analysis method can efficiently solve systems of multi‑field coupled equations. However, the accuracy of its numerical solutions still requires verification. The validation in this section focuses on the production module of our integrated framework, i.e., the stress‑seepage coupling solver for post‑fracturing flow. The fracture propagation and proppant transport submodels, which are based on Chen et al.32, have been separately validated in their original work: the propagation module was verified against PKN/KGD analytical solutions, and the transport module was compared with laboratory slot‑flow experiments reported in32,33. For the production module, we adopt a cross‑verification strategy using the industry‑standard reservoir simulator ECLIPSE (Schlumberger). While experimental data for a fully coupled fracturing‑production process are not available at this scale, ECLIPSE provides a reliable benchmark because its flow solver has been validated against numerous analytical and field cases. Comparison under identical model parameters allows us to assess the correctness of our finite‑element discretization, boundary condition implementation, and numerical coupling scheme. The small discrepancies observed (less than 8% in cumulative production) actually reflect the additional physics (stress‑seepage coupling) incorporated in COMSOL, which ECLIPSE with its conventional approach does not capture — this is consistent with theoretical expectations and highlights the advantage of our coupled model.

To validate the accuracy of the productivity calculation model for multi‑cluster fracturing in horizontal wells, we compare the reservoir production simulation results for horizontal wells with the numerical solutions34 obtained from simulations using classical reservoir models. Production

Figure 4 shows a comparison of production over 300 days for the two models under two different conditions. As can be seen, the COMSOL model yields slightly lower production than the results calculated by the ECLIPSE model; this phenomenon is even more pronounced in the multi-cluster fracture model. This may be because, compared to VISAGE’s unidirectional call of ECLIPSE’s pore-pressure calculation results, the COMSOL model implements a bidirectional coupling between the flow field and the stress field. The reduction in matrix porosity and permeability caused by stress attenuation leads to a slight decrease in production. Overall, the production predictions from the two models show good agreement, indicating that the horizontal well fracturing model built using COMSOL has high accuracy in estimating reservoir productivity. Quantitatively, the cumulative oil production after 300 days from the COMSOL model is 2.1% lower for the single‑cluster case and 7.6% lower for the five‑cluster case compared to ECLIPSE, which is considered acceptable given the fully coupled stress‑seepage effects.”

Results and discussion

A unified numerical simulation is conducted for multi-cluster fracturing, covering fracture propagation, proppant transport, and post-fracturing production under different pumping procedures. The proppant transport component of the unified numerical simulation method is based on the mathematical equations described in Sect. 2 and uses MATLAB to solve the numerical model. The proppant concentration distribution data from the simulation results are then imported into a fracturing productivity numerical model that incorporates a submodel for calculating fracture conductivity, enabling the computation of the initial fracture conductivity distribution. For horizontal well staged multi-cluster fracturing in unconventional oil and gas reservoirs, typically 3–6 clusters per stage are employed, and the fracturing fluid is predominantly slickwater. Therefore, the baseline case for multi-cluster fracturing adopts a constant concentration to add sand, with 5 perforation clusters, a perforation density of 10 holes per cluster, and a perforation diameter of 12 mm. The total simulation time is 10 min, during which the preflush is injected for 4 min, followed by injection of a proppant with a sand concentration of 10% and a density of 2600 kg/m³, consisting of 70/140 mesh proppant (particle size 120 μm).

The effect of the sand addition method

This section compares and analyzes the fracture characteristics resulting from different sand-addition methods, and optimizes the sand-addition method based on post-fracturing production as the objective. The Figs. 6 shows the injection procedures and bottomhole pressure calculation results for the three sand-addition methods. The pre-flush injection time is identical for all three methods, and the total sand volume added is also the same. For the stepwise concentration sand addition, the sand ratio increases stepwise from 0.05 to 0.15; in the slug-style sand addition, the sand concentration is 0.15.

The injection procedures and bottomhole pressures of different sanding methods.

Figure 7 shows cloud maps of proppant concentration distributions resulting from three different sand-addition methods. Although the fracture morphologies generated by the different sand-addition methods are similar, the distribution patterns of proppant within the fractures differ significantly. In the stepwise continuous sand addition method, the proppant concentration along the fracture length generally exhibits a monotonically decreasing trend, with a small region near the fracture tip showing an increase in dimensionless proppant concentration. In the slug-based sand addition method, the proppant concentration within the fracture displays three distinct peaks, with sharp drops in concentration occurring between these peaks, corresponding to the gaps between injection stages.

The distribution of proppant concentration of different sanding methods.

The fracture area, proppant contact area, and fracture proppant ratio for the three sand-addition methods are shown in Fig. 8. The fracture areas of the three sand-addition methods are essentially identical. Among them, the plug-type sand addition yields the largest fracture proppant area and the highest proppant ratio. In contrast, the stepwise concentration sand addition results in a lower sand concentration during the early stage of injection, leading to insufficient proppant support at the fracture edges; consequently, this method exhibits the smallest fracture proppant area and the lowest proppant ratio. The fracture proppant area and proppant ratio for the constant-concentration sand addition fall between those of the other two sand-addition methods.

The fracture parameters of different sanding methods.

Figure 9 shows the distribution cloud map of fracture conductivity under different sand-addition methods. For the constant-concentration sand addition method, the conductivity is relatively uniformly distributed along the entire fracture length. In the stepwise increasing-concentration sand addition method, the conductivity may be higher near the wellbore end of the fracture and gradually decrease along the fracture length. Although the conductivity near the wellbore end is high, the conductivity at the fracture tip is relatively low, resulting in an overall “wedge-shaped” distribution. In the slug-based sand addition method, the conductivity distribution exhibits multiple peaks that correspond to the peaks in proppant concentration. While the conductivity in these peak regions is relatively high, the conductivity between the peaks is lower, which may limit the continuity of oil and gas flow within the fracture.

The distribution of conductivity of different sanding methods.

In a reservoir with a permeability of 1 × 10− 6 µm2, the 10-year production levels for the three sand-addition methods were 763 tons, 743 tons, and 673 tons, respectively. In a reservoir with a permeability of 1 × 10− 3 µm2, the 5-year production levels for the three sand-addition methods were 1784 tons, 1788 tons, and 1647 tons, respectively. In both target reservoirs, the production profiles exhibited a pattern of: constant concentration ≈, stepwise increase > slug injection (Fig. 10). The discontinuous distribution of fracture conductivity in slug-injection fractures severely impairs the continuity of fluid flow within the fractures; thus, although these fractures have the largest propped area, their stimulation effect is the weakest. By contrast, fractures with constant-concentration conductivity feature a uniform distribution of fracture conductivity and a large propped area, resulting in the best stimulation performance. Stepwise-increase fractures, while sacrificing some propped area, enhance the fracture conductivity at the central region (approximately 1 D·cm), yielding a stimulation effect second only to that of constant-concentration sand addition.

The effect of proppant particle size

The particle size of proppants has almost no effect on the propagation pattern of fractures; therefore, this subsection will no longer repeatedly show contour maps of fracture widths. Instead, Fig. 11 shows contour maps of proppant concentration distributions for different proppant particle sizes. As the particle size decreases, proppant particles become easier to be carried and transported by the fluid to the far end of the fracture, increasing the distance that proppants travel within the fracture. Consequently, the proppants are distributed more uniformly throughout the fracture, and the proppant concentration near the fracture center gradually decreases. When the proppant particle size increases, the proppants travel shorter distances within the fracture, primarily concentrating in the near-wellbore region of the fracture.

The distribution of proppant concentration of different particle size.

Figure 12 shows fracture parameters for different proppant particle sizes. The fracture areas for the three particle sizes are roughly the same, but the propped fracture areas differ significantly. When the particle size is reduced from 450 μm to 300 μm, the propped fracture area increases by 55%; and when it is reduced further to 120 μm, the fracture area increases by 168%. As the particle size decreases, the proportion of the fracture that is effectively propped increases significantly, leading to improved propping performance.

The fracture parameters of different particle sizes.

Figure 13 shows the distribution of fracture conductivity under different proppant particle sizes. Since the sand addition method involves stepwise concentration increases, the conductivity of all three particle sizes decreases monotonically along the fracture length. When using 70/140 mesh proppant, fracture conductivity is the lowest, but the proppant distribution is the most uniform, and the fracture support area is the largest. With 40/70 mesh proppant, conductivity is moderate, and the distribution is relatively uniform. When using 30/50 mesh proppant, fracture conductivity is the highest, the decline in conductivity along the fracture length is the steepest, and the fracture support area is the smallest. The maximum conductivities for the three particle sizes are approximately 1.2 D·cm, 12 D·cm, and 32 D·cm, respectively.

The distribution of conductivity of different particle sizes.

In a reservoir with a permeability of 1 × 10− 6 µm2, the 10-year production levels for the three proppant particle sizes were 752 tons, 708 tons, and 591 tons, respectively. The enhanced production effects were as follows: 70/140 mesh > 40/70 mesh > 30/50 mesh. In reservoirs with extremely low permeability, the dimensionless fracture conductivity of fractures has already reached above 100. Increasing the particle size of proppants to enhance fracture conductivity no longer yields significant production gains, and a reduction in the fracture support area actually weakens the production enhancement effect (Fig. 14).

The productivity of different particle sizes.

In reservoirs with a permeability of 1 × 10− 3 µm2, the 5-year production volumes for the three sand-addition methods were 2,002 tons, 1,949 tons, and 1,679 tons, respectively. The enhanced production effects were in the order: 30/50 mesh > 40/70 mesh > 70/140 mesh. In reservoirs with relatively higher permeability, the demand for fracture conductivity increases to enable efficient oil and gas development. In such cases, fractures propped with 70/140 mesh support agents exhibit an excessively low dimensionless fracture conductivity, resulting in the poorest production enhancement effect. By contrast, the fracture conductivities of 30/50 mesh and 40/70 mesh are of the same order of magnitude; both offer advantages in terms of high fracture conductivity and large fracture half-length, leading to comparable overall production enhancement effects, with 30/50 mesh slightly outperforming 40/70 mesh.

The effect of injection sand ratio

Similar to the particle size of proppants, changing the injection sand ratio has essentially no effect on the fracture propagation pattern; therefore, this section will not repeat the presentation of cloud maps showing the fracture width distribution. Increasing the injection sand ratio means that the total amount of proppant injected into the fracture rises. The Fig. 15 illustrates the distribution patterns of proppants at different sand ratios. The distribution trends of proppants at various sand ratios are basically identical: the concentration decreases monotonically along the fracture length, and the overall proppant concentration increases significantly as the injection sand ratio is raised.

The distribution of proppant concentration of different sand concentrations.

Figure 16 shows a summary of fracture parameters under different sand-injection ratios. Since only the sand-injection parameters were varied, the fracture area remained essentially unchanged. As the sand-injection ratio increased, the fracture edges received better support, leading to a slight increase in the supported area and a corresponding rise in the fracture’s support ratio.

The fracture parameters of different sand concentrations.

Figure 17 shows how the distribution of fracture conductivity changes as the sand injection ratio increases. Figure 22 compares the fracture-enhanced production effects under different injection rates in two types of reservoirs with permeabilities of 1 × 10− 6 µm2 and 1 × 10− 3 µm2, respectively. As clearly shown in the figure, the fracture-enhanced production effects at different injection rates are strikingly opposite in these two reservoir types. When the permeability is 1 × 10− 6 µm2, the production levels corresponding to the four injection rates are 757 tons, 743 tons, 724 tons, and 715 tons, respectively. Production decreases as the injection rate increases. At this point, the fracture’s conductivity has already exceeded 100, and further increasing the injection rate no longer provides any meaningful benefit in terms of enhanced production due to the high fracture conductivity achieved. Therefore, using a lower injection rate to create longer fractures can yield higher production. On the other hand, when the permeability is 1 × 10− 3 µm2, the production levels corresponding to the four injection rates are 1719 tons, 1752 tons, 1776 tons, and 1802 tons, respectively. Production increases as the injection rate rises. When the reservoir permeability improves by three orders of magnitude, the matrix’s demand for fracture conductivity also increases significantly. In this scenario, the higher fracture conductivity obtained through a higher injection rate can offset the production loss caused by the reduced fracture length, thus leading to even greater overall production. In summary, for reservoirs with extremely low permeability(1 × 10− 6 µm2), it is recommended to use a low injection rate to create longer fractures; whereas for reservoirs with relatively higher permeability(1 × 10− 3 µm2), it is advisable to employ a higher injection rate to enhance fracture conductivity (Fig. 22).

Conclusions

This study develops an integrated numerical simulation framework that couples fracture propagation, proppant transport, and post-fracturing production performance. By employing a two-way coupled computational approach, the model captures the dynamic evolution of fracture conductivity and systematically examines the impact of key pumping parameters—such as sand injection mode, proppant size, flow rate, fracturing fluid viscosity, sand ratio, and perforation design—on fracture geometry, proppant distribution, and resultant conductivity. The primary findings are summarized as follows:

Constant-concentration sand addition achieved the most uniform fracture conductivity distribution and provided superior proppant support at the far-wellbore region in both reservoir types. While slug-style addition yielded the largest propped area, its discontinuous conductivity pattern led to the lowest production. Stepwise incremental addition offered intermediate performance by enhancing center-conductivity at the expense of edge support.

In higher-permeability reservoirs, 30/50 mesh proppant performed optimally, providing an order-of-magnitude higher conductivity. A careful balance is required, as finer proppant increases area but significantly reduces conductivity.
