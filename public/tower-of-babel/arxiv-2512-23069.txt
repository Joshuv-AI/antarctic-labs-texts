Robustness of OLS to sample removals: Theoretical analysis and implications
Eyar Azar, Michael J. Feldman, Boaz Nadler

Abstract
For learned models to be trustworthy, it is essential to verify their robustness to perturbations in the training data. Classical approaches involve uncertainty quantification via confidence intervals and bootstrap methods. In contrast, recent work proposes a more stringent form of robustness: stability to the removal of any subset of k k samples from the training set. In this paper, we present a theoretical study of this criterion for ordinary least squares (OLS). Our contributions are as follows: (1) Given n n i.i.d. training samples from a general misspecified model, we prove that with high probability, OLS is robust to the removal of any k ≪ n k\ll n samples. (2) For data of dimension p p , OLS can withstand up to k ≪ n ​ p / log ⁡ n {k\ll\sqrt{np}/\log n} sample removals while remaining robust and achieving the same error rate as OLS applied to the full dataset. Conversely, if k k is proportional to n n , OLS is provably non-robust. (3) We revisit prior analyses that found several econometric datasets to be highly non-robust to sample removals. While this appears to contradict our results in (1), we demonstrate that the sensitivity is due to either heavy-tailed responses or correlated samples. Empirically, this sensitivity is considerably attenuated by classical robust methods, such as linear regression with a Huber loss.

Introduction

Assessing the robustness of learned models and their predictions is essential for the widespread adoption of artificial intelligence systems. The standard supervised learning workflow involves training a model on a labeled dataset, evaluating its performance, and deploying it to make predictions on new unlabeled samples. A key question is whether we can trust the learned model—for example, to remain stable under minor data perturbations. To address this, a variety of tools have been developed to assess the reliability of learned models and quantify their uncertainty. These include cross-validation, bootstrap methods, confidence intervals, outlier detection, and conformal prediction
(
11
;
22
;
2
)
.

The stability of a model is closely related to how subsets of the training data affect its learned parameters.
Classical works quantified the effect of individual samples using the influence function
(
17
;
8
)
, while the bootstrap assesses stability under the removal of random subsets of samples.
In contrast, contemporary studies have emphasized the importance of evaluating how specifically selected subsets of training samples collectively affect a learned model
(
24
;
4
;
21
)
. Clearly, the reliability of a learned model is called into question if removing a handful of training samples drastically changes the fitted parameters. For instance,
6
showed that in several econometrics datasets, removing even a small fraction of the data (in some cases, less than 1%), significantly alters the learned model.

In response, a stringent notion of robustness was recently proposed: stability to the removal of
any
subset of
k
samples from the training data
(
6
;
9
)
. Identifying the subset of
k
training samples whose removal most alters a model is known as the most influential subset selection (MISS) problem
(
4
;
20
)
. The naive brute-force approach to finding this subset is to retrain the model on all subsets of the data of size
n-k
. Clearly, this is computationally infeasible for even moderately sized datasets once
k
exceeds
3
or
4
samples. Indeed, various problems related to the robustness of OLS to sample removals are computationally intractable

(
28
;
31
)
. Several recent works—including
6
,

25
,

13
,

28
,

20
,

32
and
21
—developed and analyzed approximate methods to solve the MISS problem, and, more generally, to assess and certify the robustness to sample removals. This emerging area of research is termed
robustness auditing
.

This form of robustness raises two fundamental questions:
under the standard statistical learning paradigm, where the training samples
({\bm{x}}_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,({\bm{x}}_{n},y_{n})
are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) from a probability distribution
P({\bm{x}},y)
, when is a learned model provably robust to sample removals?
Conversely, when is a model non-robust purely due to the inherent randomness of the training data, even under the i.i.d. sample assumption and in the absence of outliers?
In this paper, we study these questions within the context of ordinary least squares (OLS), one of the most widely used tools in data science.

Contributions.

We analyze the robustness of OLS in a general setting where the distribution
P({\bm{x}},y)
of the data is arbitrary, subject to mild regularity conditions.
In this case, the optimal predictor of
y
given
{\bm{x}}
under squared loss,
\mathbb{E}[y|{\bm{x}}]
, is generally nonlinear in
{\bm{x}}
, implying that the linear model is misspecified. Let
\widehat{\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}}
denote the OLS coefficients computed on the full training data, and
\widehat{\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}}_{\mathcal{S}}
those computed on a subset
{\mathcal{S}}
of size
|{\mathcal{S}}|\geq n-k
. Under mild conditions on
P({\bm{x}},y)
, we prove that
\widehat{\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}}_{\mathcal{S}}
concentrates around
\widehat{\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}}

uniformly
over all subsets
{\mathcal{S}}
, provided that
k\ll n
.
We present asymptotic results of the form

\displaystyle\max_{{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq[n]:|{\mathcal{S}}|\geq n-k}\|\widehat{\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}}-\widehat{\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}}_{\mathcal{S}}\|\xrightarrow{\,p\,}0\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty,

(1.1)

as well as non-asymptotic concentration inequalities.
In particular, when
k\ll n
, OLS is provably robust irrespective of the data dimension
p
; that is, robustness holds even when
p\asymp n
, a regime in which OLS is inconsistent.
Furthermore, for
{k\ll\sqrt{np}/\log n}
, OLS achieves the same error rate as on the full dataset. That is, the error in (
1.1
) is comparable to
\|\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}-\widehat{\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}}\|
, where

\bm{\beta}

is the best linear predictor.

Under the standard Gaussian linear model,
{\bm{y}}=X\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}+{\bm{\varepsilon}}
with
X
multivariate Gaussian, we derive sharper non-asymptotic bounds with explicit constants.
As outlined in
Table
1
,
depending on
n
,
p
, and
k
, OLS exhibits distinct robustness and consistency properties. In particular, it is provably non-robust when
k
is proportional to
n
.
The analysis under the general model uses standard concentration results and union bounds. In contrast, the sharper bounds for the Gaussian linear model are derived by (to our knowledge) a novel application of Gaussian comparison inequalities.
Specifically, union bounds are overly conservative as subsets of
n-k
samples are highly correlated when
k\ll n
. This technique may be useful for analyzing the robustness of regression methods beyond OLS.

Our theoretical results have several implications for robustness auditing.
If the removal of a handful of samples significantly affects the fitted coefficients, the
source of this
non-robustness should be carefully
investigated. In particular, it
may stem from one or more of the following factors:
(1) the covariance matrix of the data is ill-conditioned, (2) training samples are not all i.i.d., or (3) the data is heavy-tailed. In other words, even under model misspecification, the probability of
\widehat{\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}}
exhibiting extreme sensitivity to sample removals, as observed in several recent studies, is exponentially small if the data is “well-behaved”.

To complement our theoretical analysis, we empirically investigate the robustness of OLS to sample removals in two widely cited econometric datasets: the Cash Transfers data
(
3
)
and the Nightlights data
(
26
)
.
Prior robustness audits of these datasets, including
6
and
32
, identified small subsets of samples whose removal reversed the sign of the estimated treatment effects.

At first glance, these empirical findings appear to contradict our theoretical results. However, we show that the observed non-robustness is driven by either heavy-tailed responses or by correlated samples. In the Cash Transfers data, it is attributable to the heavy-tailed distribution of the response variable
y
.
Specifically, many of the samples identified for removal by the AMIP algorithm (Approximate Maximum Influence Perturbation;
6
) have response values far exceeding the mean of
y
over the full dataset—often by 5 or 10 standard deviations, and in some cases, by more than 20.
This key pattern, which elucidates the lack of robustness, is not
discussed in
6
or
32
.
In the Nightlights data, the removed samples are concentrated in only a few countries and years, and are therefore highly correlated.

Next, we show empirically that this non-robustness is substantially mitigated by applying classical robust regression techniques.
In particular, fitting a linear model under the Huber loss, the estimated coefficients are considerably more stable to sample removals.
For the Cash Transfers data, roughly two to six times as many samples must be removed to reverse the sign of the estimated treatment effect. Moreover, the robust estimates
closely align with the original OLS estimates
in
3
, which seems to reaffirm their findings.
Similarly, for the Nightlights dataset, compared to OLS, Huber regression
requires over 50% more removals
to reverse the study’s conclusions.
In summary, the interpretation of a robustness audit—that a study’s conclusion may be overturned by removing a handful of samples—requires careful assessment.

While this work focuses on OLS, it suggests several potential research directions. A natural question is whether the robustness guarantees established here can be extended to other regression methods, such as ridge or kernel regression. Of particular interest are methods that use robust loss functions, which are designed to limit the influence of outliers or heavy-tailed data. Sparse linear regression raises another question: how stable are the selected variables under sample removals? We leave these questions for future research.

Problem Setting

Consider a regression problem with training data
({\bm{x}}_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,({\bm{x}}_{n},y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{p+1}
and
n\geq p
. Let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
contain
{\bm{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{n}
as its rows and
{\bm{y}}\coloneqq(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})^{\top}
be the
n
response values. Assuming
X
has full column rank, the OLS estimator is

\displaystyle\widehat{\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}}\coloneqq\big(X^{\top}X\big)^{-1}X^{\top}{\bm{y}}.

(2.1)

For
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq[n]
of size
|{\mathcal{S}}|\geq p
, let
X_{\mathcal{S}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|{\mathcal{S}}|\times p}
be the submatrix of
X
consisting of the rows indexed by
{\mathcal{S}}
, and define
{\bm{y}}_{\mathcal{S}}
analogously.
Assuming
X_{\mathcal{S}}
has full column rank, the OLS estimator computed on the subset

(X_{\mathcal{S}},{\bm{y}}_{\mathcal{S}})

of the training data is

\displaystyle\widehat{\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}}_{\mathcal{S}}\coloneqq\big(X_{\mathcal{S}}^{\top}X_{\mathcal{S}}\big)^{-1}X_{\mathcal{S}}^{\top}{\bm{y}}_{\mathcal{S}}.

(2.2)

As in
32
, the robustness of
\widehat{\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}}
to
k
sample removals in a fixed direction
{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
is
measured by the following quantity:

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}({\bm{v}})\coloneqq\max_{{\mathcal{S}}\subset[n],|{\mathcal{S}}|=n-k}\langle\widehat{\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}}-\widehat{\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}}_{\mathcal{S}},{\bm{v}}\rangle,

(2.3)

where
\langle{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}\rangle
denotes the inner product of the vectors
{\bm{u}}
and
{\bm{v}}
.
For example,
\Delta_{k}({\bm{e}}_{1})
quantifies the sensitivity of the first regression coefficient to
k
removals. If

\Delta_{k}(\text{sign}(\widehat{\beta}_{1}){\bm{e}}_{1})>|\widehat{\beta}_{1}|
,
then there exists a subset of
k
samples whose removal changes the sign of
\widehat{\beta}_{1}
.
This may have important implications:
if
x_{1}
(the first coordinate of
{\bm{x}}
) is a treatment indicator in a study, then
\beta_{1}

represents the treatment effect. If a small number of sample removals reverses the sign of
\widehat{\beta}_{1}
, the estimated treatment effect is unreliable and trust in the model is questionable.

As discussed in Section
1
, exact evaluation of
\Delta_{k}({\bm{v}})
is computationally infeasible, and
various methods have been developed to approximate it.
In contrast, our work focuses on a complementary aspect of robustness: we investigate conditions under which
\Delta_{k}({\bm{v}})
is either provably close to zero, or bounded away from zero, even when the data are i.i.d. from a given distribution. Specifically, we study the robustness of OLS to sample removals under two generative models: (1) a general setting where the joint distribution of
{\bm{x}}
and
y
is arbitrary, subject to mild conditions, in which case the linear model is potentially misspecified, and (2) a standard Gaussian linear model.

We now formally describe these two models.
For completeness, a precise definition of sub-Gaussian random variables is provided below.

Model 1
.

Let
P({\bm{x}},y)
be a probability distribution over
\mathbb{R}^{p+1}
such that

(i)

{\bm{x}}
is sub-Gaussian, zero mean, and has positive-definite covariance
\Sigma\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}{\bm{x}}^{\top}]
.

(ii)

y
is sub-Gaussian.

As is well known, the optimal predictor of
y
given
{\bm{x}}
under squared loss is the conditional mean

\mathbb{E}[y|{\bm{x}}]
. Under Model
1
,
this function is in general non-linear in
{\bm{x}}
and challenging to accurately estimate, particularly in high dimensions
where
p\gg 1
.
A standard approach is to fit a linear model, which, although potentially misspecified, often has low variance and may perform well in practice.
Under squared loss, the quantity of interest is the vector of coefficients of the best linear predictor:

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}\coloneqq\argmin_{{\bm{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\mathbb{E}\left[(y-{\bm{b}}^{\top}\hskip-0.80002pt{\bm{x}})^{2}\right]=\Sigma^{-1}\mathbb{E}[y{\bm{x}}].

(2.4)

Model 2
.

Let
y=\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}^{\top}\hskip-0.80002pt{\bm{x}}+\varepsilon
, where
\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is deterministic and

(i)

{\bm{x}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
where
\Sigma
is positive definite.

(ii)

\varepsilon
is sub-Gaussian, mean zero, and independent of
{\bm{x}}
.

Properties of OLS have been extensively studied in both classical and high-dimensional regimes. M-estimators—including OLS and regression with Huber’s loss—are consistent
under Model
2

when
p/n\rightarrow 0

(
29
)
. In contrast, in high-dimensional settings where
p
is comparable to
n
, such estimators are inconsistent
(
23
;
12
)
. Recent works, including
10
and
18
, derive exact asymptotic prediction errors for OLS and ridge regression.
To our knowledge, however, the robustness of OLS to sample removals has not been systematically studied.

Remark 2.1
.

Model
2
is a particular instance of Model
1
. Hence, whenever OLS is robust under Model
1
, it is also robust under Model
2
.

Remark 2.2
.

The assumption that
{\bm{x}}
is zero-mean
is standard in theoretical statistics and machine learning, as it simplifies the analysis.
Our results extend to
{\bm{x}}
of the form
(1,\widetilde{\bm{x}})
, where
\widetilde{\bm{x}}
is sub-Gaussian, zero mean, and has positive-definite covariance. In this case, the OLS estimator defined in (
2.1
) includes a constant intercept.
The generalization of our analysis to the case where
{\bm{x}}
has an arbitrary mean is left for future work.

Definitions and Notations.

We denote the spectral norm of a
positive-definite matrix
\Sigma
by
\|\Sigma\|
and its condition number by
\kappa(\Sigma)\coloneqq\lambda_{1}(\Sigma)/\lambda_{p}(\Sigma)
. The sub-Gaussian and sub-exponential norms of a real-valued random variable

\|z\|_{\psi_{2}}
and
\|z\|_{\psi_{1}}
, are defined as

\displaystyle\|z\|_{\psi_{\alpha}}\coloneqq\inf\big\{t>0:\mathbb{E}[\exp(|z|^{\alpha}/t^{\alpha})]\leq e\big\},

(2.5)

for
\alpha=1,2
.
For a random vector
{\bm{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, these norms are defined as

\|{\bm{z}}\|_{\psi_{\alpha}}\coloneqq\sup_{{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{S}^{p-1}}\|{\bm{v}}^{\top}{\bm{z}}\|_{\psi_{\alpha}},\quad\alpha=1,2.

We say that
{\bm{z}}
is sub-Gaussian if
\|{\bm{z}}\|_{\psi_{2}}<\infty
and sub-exponential if
\|{\bm{z}}\|_{\psi_{1}}<\infty
. The choice of the constant
e
in (
2.5
) is arbitrary; for instance,
33
uses the value 2. We adopt
e
so that for deterministic scalars
a\in\mathbb{R}
,
\|a\|_{\psi_{1}}=|a|
. See Sections 2 and 3 of
33
for equivalent definitions in terms of the decay of tail probabilities.

Robustness Guarantees for Model

This section establishes the asymptotic robustness of OLS to sample removals as

Formally, for our asymptotic results, we consider a sequence of regression problems indexed by the sample size

If

Theorem

4.1
Heavy-tailed Responses and Non-robustness
of OLS

The empirical findings presented above seem to contradict our theoretical results on the robustness of OLS to
k\ll n
removals.
As we now discuss, this seeming discrepancy is due to the heavy-tailed distribution
of the response.
Columns 7 and 8 of Table
2
report the mean
\mu_{y}
and standard deviation
\sigma_{y}
of the response
y
across the six datasets.
For each household type (poor/non-poor) the means are similar across the three time periods, with consumption in non-poor households roughly 50 pesos higher than in poor ones.
Crucially, columns 9 and 10 show that each dataset contains a non-negligible number of samples whose response
y
deviates by at least 5 or even 10 standard deviations from the mean. In simple terms, the response
y
is quite heavy-tailed.

Next, we examine the response values of the few samples selected for removal by
1-Greedy
.
As shown in column 11 of Table
2
, these samples have
extremely large

y
-values, with many exceeding
\mu_{y}
by more than 5 standard deviations.
Moreover, column 12 shows that the maximal selected
y
value is between 13 and 33 standard deviations above
\mu_{y}
!
It is thus unsurprising that these subsets of samples have an outsized effect on the fitted model.
We remark that in
dataset 2
—where only 5 removals suffice to reverse the sign of
\widehat{\beta}_{1}
—the estimated treatment effect computed on the full data
was to begin with not statistically significant, even at the 10% level (Table 1 of
3
).

4.2
Robustness under the Huber Loss

For datasets with a heavy-tailed response, a standard approach is to fit a regression model using a robust loss function, such as Huber or
L_{1}
, rather than ordinary least squares.
In this section, we study the robustness of this approach to sample removals in the six Cash Transfers datasets.

Given a training set
({\bm{x}}_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,({\bm{x}}_{n},y_{n})
, assuming a linear model, this method
solves the following optimization problem:

\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{Huber}}\coloneqq\argmin_{{\bm{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{\tau}({\bm{b}}^{\top}\hskip-0.80002pt{\bm{x}}_{i}-y_{i})

(4.1)

where
h_{\tau}
is the Huber loss function, given by

\displaystyle h_{\tau}(z)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{z^{2}}{2}&|z|\leq\tau,\\
\tau\Big(|z|-\frac{\tau}{2}\Big)&|z|>\tau.\end{cases}

(4.2)

Here,
\tau>0
controls the transition from squared loss to absolute loss.

For each of the six datasets, we assessed their robustness as follows: (1) compute
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{Huber}}
, the solution of (
4.1
) on the full data with
\tau=1
;
(2) run
1-Greedy
with the Huber loss to identify a subset of samples whose removal reverses the sign of
\widehat{\beta}_{1}^{\text{Huber}}
.

As shown in Table
3
,
across all datasets,
\widehat{\beta}_{1}
under Huber regression has the same sign as under OLS, though typically with slightly smaller magnitude. Importantly, with Huber regression, the subsets identified by
1-Greedy
are considerably larger than for OLS. For instance, in dataset 6, reversing the sign of
\widehat{\beta}_{1}
requires removing 162 samples, compared with just 26 for OLS.
The only exception is the second dataset, where 5 removals suffice to flip the sign of
\widehat{\beta}_{1}
.
However, as noted by
3
, this coefficient is not statistically significant to begin with.

In summary, assuming the
1-Greedy
approximation is reasonably accurate, Huber regression is substantially more robust than OLS to sample removals. A theoretical investigation of the properties of such estimators under sample removals is an interesting direction for future work.

Proof of Theorem

Recall that our goal is to bound

uniformly over all subsets
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq[n]

. To this end, we first derive a bound for a fixed subset
{\mathcal{S}}
. By the definitions of
\widehat{\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}}
and
\widehat{\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}}_{\mathcal{S}}
, given in (
2.1
) and (

\displaystyle\widehat{\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}}-\widehat{\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}}_{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle=\big(X^{\top}\hskip-0.80002ptX\big)^{-1}X^{\top}{\bm{y}}-\big(X_{\mathcal{S}}^{\top}X_{\mathcal{S}}\big)^{-1}X_{\mathcal{S}}^{\top}{\bm{y}}_{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle=\big(X^{\top}\hskip-0.80002ptX\big)^{-1}\big(X^{\top}{\bm{y}}-X^{\top}\hskip-0.80002ptX\big(X_{\mathcal{S}}^{\top}X_{\mathcal{S}}\big)^{-1}X_{\mathcal{S}}^{\top}{\bm{y}}_{\mathcal{S}}\big).

(A.4)

Inserting the identities
X^{\top}\hskip-0.80002ptX=X_{\mathcal{S}}^{\top}X_{\mathcal{S}}+X_{{\mathcal{S}}^{c}}^{\top}X_{{\mathcal{S}}^{c}}
,
X^{\top}{\bm{y}}=X_{\mathcal{S}}^{\top}{\bm{y}}_{\mathcal{S}}+X_{{\mathcal{S}}^{c}}^{\top}{\bm{y}}_{{\mathcal{S}}^{c}}
, and
X=Z\Sigma^{1/2}
into (
A.4

\displaystyle\widehat{\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}}-\widehat{\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}}_{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle=\big(X^{\top}\hskip-0.80002ptX\big)^{-1}\Big(X_{{\mathcal{S}}^{c}}^{\top}{\bm{y}}_{{\mathcal{S}}^{c}}-X_{{\mathcal{S}}^{c}}^{\top}X_{{\mathcal{S}}^{c}}\big(X_{\mathcal{S}}^{\top}X_{\mathcal{S}}\big)^{-1}X_{\mathcal{S}}^{\top}{\bm{y}}_{\mathcal{S}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\Sigma^{-1/2}\big(Z^{\top}\hskip-0.80002ptZ\big)^{-1}Z_{{\mathcal{S}}^{c}}^{\top}\Big({\bm{y}}_{{\mathcal{S}}^{c}}-Z_{{\mathcal{S}}^{c}}\big(Z_{\mathcal{S}}^{\top}Z_{\mathcal{S}}\big)^{-1}Z_{\mathcal{S}}^{\top}{\bm{y}}_{\mathcal{S}}\Big).

(A.5)

By the triangle inequality and the identity
\|Z^{\top}\hskip-0.80002ptZ\|=\sigma_{p}^{2}(Z)

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}}-\widehat{\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}}_{\mathcal{S}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\|\frac{\sigma_{1}(Z_{{\mathcal{S}}^{c}})}{\sigma_{p}^{2}(Z)}\Big(\|{\bm{y}}_{{\mathcal{S}}^{c}}\|+\big\|Z_{{\mathcal{S}}^{c}}\big(Z_{\mathcal{S}}^{\top}Z_{\mathcal{S}}\big)^{-1}Z_{\mathcal{S}}^{\top}{\bm{y}}_{\mathcal{S}}\big\|\Big)

(A.6)

where
{\bm{v}}_{\mathcal{S}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|{\mathcal{S}}^{c}|}

We now bound each term on the right-hand side of (
A.2

A.1

(A.8)

t_{3}>0

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\|{\bm{y}}_{{\mathcal{S}}^{c}}\|^{2}\geq\|y\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}\Big(1+\frac{kt_{3}}{|{\mathcal{S}}^{c}|}\Big)|{\mathcal{S}}^{c}|\bigg)\leq e^{-c|{\mathcal{S}}^{c}|-ckt_{3}}.

(A.11)

Next, we bound
\|{\bm{v}}_{\mathcal{S}}\|

is independent of

and
{\bm{y}}_{\mathcal{S}}

A.1

A.11

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\|{\bm{v}}_{\mathcal{S}}\|^{2}\geq(1+\omega^{2})(1+t_{3})k\Big)\leq e^{-ckt_{3}}.

(A.12)

Now, we bound the second term on the right-hand side of (

A.10
), (
A.12
),

\sqrt{1+\omega^{2}}\leq 1+\omega

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{1}(Z_{\mathcal{S}})\|{\bm{v}}_{\mathcal{S}}\|\|{\bm{y}}_{\mathcal{S}}\|}{\sigma_{p}^{2}(Z_{\mathcal{S}})}

Combining (
A.2

A.7
), (
A.11

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}}-\widehat{\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}}_{\mathcal{S}}\|\leq

\displaystyle\,C(1+\omega)\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\|\|y\|_{\psi_{2}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{k}+C\omega^{2}(\sqrt{p}+t_{1})}{\big(\sqrt{n}-C\omega^{2}(\sqrt{p}+t_{2})\big)^{2}}

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\binom{n}{\ell}\leq k\Big(\frac{en}{k}\Big)^{k},

(A.15)

which holds for

) holds uniformly in
{\mathcal{S}}

(A.17)

The theorem follows from (

Proof of Theorem

.

Let
\gamma\coloneqq p/n
and
\Omega
be the inverse sample covariance matrix of
Z
:

\displaystyle\Omega\coloneqq\bigg(\frac{1}{n}Z^{\top}\hskip-0.80002ptZ\bigg)^{-1}.

(A.18)

Substituting
X=Z\Sigma^{1/2}
into (
2.1
) yields

\widehat{\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}}=\Sigma^{-1/2}\big(Z^{\top}\hskip-0.80002ptZ\big)^{-1}\Sigma^{-1/2}X^{\top}{\bm{y}}=\frac{1}{n}\Sigma^{-1/2}\Omega\Sigma^{-1/2}X^{\top}{\bm{y}}.

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}}-\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}\|

\displaystyle\leq\bigg\|\frac{1}{n}\Sigma^{-1/2}\Omega\Sigma^{-1/2}X^{\top}{\bm{y}}-\frac{1}{n}\Sigma^{-1}X^{\top}{\bm{y}}\bigg\|+\bigg\|\frac{1}{n}\Sigma^{-1}X^{\top}{\bm{y}}-\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}\bigg\|.

The first term on the right-hand side can be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\bigg\|\frac{1}{n}\Sigma^{-1/2}\Omega\Sigma^{-1/2}X^{\top}{\bm{y}}-\frac{1}{n}\Sigma^{-1}X^{\top}{\bm{y}}\bigg\|

\displaystyle~=\bigg\|\frac{1}{n}\Sigma^{-1/2}(\Omega-I_{p})\Sigma^{-1/2}X^{\top}{\bm{y}}\bigg\|

\displaystyle~\leq\sqrt{\kappa(\Sigma)}\big\|\Omega-I_{p}\big\|\left\|\frac{1}{n}\Sigma^{-1}X^{\top}{\bm{y}}\right\|

\displaystyle~\leq\sqrt{\kappa(\Sigma)}\big\|\Omega-I_{p}\big\|\bigg(\bigg\|\frac{1}{n}\Sigma^{-1}X^{\top}{\bm{y}}-\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}\bigg\|+\|\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}\|\bigg).

This leads to the following bound on
\|\widehat{\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}}-\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}\|
:

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}}-\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}\|

\displaystyle\leq\big(1+\sqrt{\kappa(\Sigma)}\big\|\Omega-I_{p}\big\|\big)\left\|\frac{1}{n}\Sigma^{-1}X^{\top}{\bm{y}}-\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}\right\|+\sqrt{\kappa(\Sigma)}\big\|\Omega-I_{p}\big\|\|\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}\|.

(A.20)

By Lemma
A.1
for
t_{1},t_{2}>0
, such that
C\omega^{2}(\sqrt{\gamma}+t_{2})<1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\|\Omega-I_{p}\|\geq\frac{\big(1+C\omega^{2}(\sqrt{\gamma}+t_{1})\big)^{2}-1}{\big(1-C\omega^{2}(\sqrt{\gamma}+t_{2})\big)^{2}}\bigg)\leq e^{-nt_{1}^{2}}+e^{-nt_{2}^{2}}.

(A.21)

Note that if
\|A-I_{p}\|\leq\delta<1
, then
A
is invertible and
\|A^{-1}-I_{p}\|\leq\delta/(1-\delta)
. Using this fact together with (4.22) in
33
, one obtains

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\|\Omega-I_{p}\|\geq\frac{C\omega^{2}(\sqrt{\gamma}+t_{2})}{1-C\omega^{2}(\sqrt{\gamma}+t_{2})}\bigg)\leq 2e^{-nt_{2}^{2}}.

(A.22)

To bound the second term on the right-hand side of (
A.19
), notice that
X^{\top}{\bm{y}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}{\bm{x}}_{i}
is a sum of i.i.d. sub-exponential vectors. For any fixed
{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
, applying the two-sided analog of Lemma
A.3
yields, for
t_{3}>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\bigg|\frac{1}{n}{\bm{v}}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X^{\top}{\bm{y}}-{\bm{v}}^{\top}\hskip-0.80002pt\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}\bigg|\geq\big\|\Sigma^{-1}(y{\bm{x}}-\mathbb{E}[y{\bm{x}}])\big\|_{\psi_{1}}\cdot t_{3}\bigg)\leq 2e^{-cnt_{3}^{2}}.

(A.23)

By Corollary 4.2.13 of
33
, there exists a
1/2
-net
\mathcal{N}
covering
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
with cardinality at most

|\mathcal{N}|\leq 5^{p}.

Then, using a union bound over
\mathcal{N}
and the inequality

\displaystyle\bigg\|\frac{1}{n}\Sigma^{-1}X^{\top}{\bm{y}}-\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}\bigg\|\leq 2\max_{{\bm{v}}\in\mathcal{N}}\Big(\frac{1}{n}{\bm{v}}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X^{\top}{\bm{y}}-{\bm{v}}^{\top}\hskip-0.80002pt\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}\Big)

(Exercise 4.4.2 in
33
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\bigg\|\frac{1}{n}\Sigma^{-1}X^{\top}{\bm{y}}-\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}\bigg\|\geq 2\big\|\Sigma^{-1}(y{\bm{x}}-\mathbb{E}[y{\bm{x}}])\big\|_{\psi_{1}}\cdot t_{3}\bigg)\leq 2e^{\log(5)p-cnt_{3}^{2}}.

(A.24)

Thus, by (
A.20
)–(
A.22
) and (
A.24
), with probability at least
1-e^{-nt_{1}^{2}}-3e^{-nt_{2}^{2}}-2e^{\log(5)p-cnt_{3}^{2}}
,

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}}-\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}\|\leq

\displaystyle~2\bigg(1+\frac{C\sqrt{\kappa(\Sigma)}\omega^{2}(\sqrt{\gamma}+t_{2})}{1-C\omega^{2}(\sqrt{\gamma}+t_{2})}\bigg)\big\|\Sigma^{-1}(y{\bm{x}}-\mathbb{E}[y{\bm{x}}])\big\|_{\psi_{1}}\cdot t_{3}

\displaystyle~+\sqrt{\kappa(\Sigma)}\bigg(\frac{\big(1+C\omega^{2}(\sqrt{\gamma}+t_{1})\big)^{2}-1}{\big(1-C\omega^{2}(\sqrt{\gamma}+t_{2})\big)^{2}}\bigg)\|\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}\|.

(A.25)

Let
\delta>0
be a constant such that
C(\omega^{2}\sqrt{\gamma}+\delta)\leq 1/2
. To conclude the proof, set

\displaystyle t_{1}\coloneqq\sqrt{\gamma}\hskip 1.49994ptt,

\displaystyle t_{2}\coloneqq\frac{\delta}{\omega^{2}},

\displaystyle t_{3}\coloneqq\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{c}\log(5)}\hskip 1.49994pt(1+t),

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}}-\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}\|\leq

\displaystyle~+C\sqrt{\kappa(\Sigma)}\omega^{2}(1+\omega^{2})\|\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$\bm{\beta}$}\|\big(\sqrt{\gamma}(1+t)+\gamma(1+t)^{2}\big).

(A.26)

The theorem follows from (

and

denotes the standard normal density. For each
x\in\mathbb{R}
, define

A_{x}:=\{z\in\mathbb{R}:xz\leq t\}=\begin{cases}(-\infty,t/x]&x>0\\
[t/x,\infty)&x<0\\
\mathbb{R}&x=0\end{cases}.

Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[z^{2}\mathbbm{1}(\varepsilon z\leq t)\big]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{A_{x}}z^{2}\,\phi(z)\,dz\,dF_{\varepsilon}(x).

Recall that for the standard normal density,

\int_{-\infty}^{x}z^{2}\phi(z)\,dz\leq\int_{-\infty}^{x}\phi(z)\,dz\quad\text{for }x>0,\quad\int_{x}^{\infty}z^{2}\phi(z)\,dz\leq\int_{x}^{\infty}\phi(z)\,dz\quad\text{for }x\leq 0.

Thus,

\int_{A_{x}}z^{2}\phi(z)dz\leq\int_{A_{x}}\phi(z)dz.

It follows that

\mathbb{E}\big[z^{2}\mathbbm{1}(\varepsilon z\leq t)\big]\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{A_{x}}\phi(z)\,dz\,dF_{\varepsilon}(x)=\mathbb{P}(\varepsilon z\leq t),

completing the proof.
∎
