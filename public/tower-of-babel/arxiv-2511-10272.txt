Bidirectional Bounded-Suboptimal Heuristic Search with Consistent Heuristics
Shahaf S. Shperberg, Natalie Morad, Lior Siag, Ariel Felner, Dor Atzmon

Abstract
Recent advancements in bidirectional heuristic search have yielded significant theoretical insights and novel algorithms. While most previous work has concentrated on optimal search methods, this paper focuses on bounded-suboptimal bidirectional search, where a bound on the suboptimality of the solution cost is specified. We build upon the state-of-the-art optimal bidirectional search algorithm, BAE*, designed for consistent heuristics, and introduce several variants of BAE* specifically tailored for the bounded-suboptimal context. Through experimental evaluation, we compare the performance of these new variants against other bounded-suboptimal bidirectional algorithms as well as the standard weighted A* algorithm. Our results demonstrate that each algorithm excels under distinct conditions, highlighting the strengths and weaknesses of each approach.

Weighted BAE*

We now introduce
WBAE
∗
, a BSS variant of

∗
that integrates the WA* idea of inflating

Recall the

\mbox{$b_{D}$}(n)=\mbox{$g_{D}$}(n)+\mbox{$h_{D}$}(n)+\mbox{$d_{D}$}(n)

In WA*, the

We define the

\mbox{$b_{W_{D}}$}(n)=\mbox{$g_{D}$}(n)+W\cdot\mbox{$h_{D}$}(n)+\lambda\cdot\mbox{$d_{D}$}(n)

Here,

, fully incorporating

WBAE

terminates once a state

is found on both open lists and the cost of the path from

to

is
\leq LB_{\mathtt{WB}}

LB_{\mathtt{WB}}={(\mbox{$b_{W}Min_{F}$}+\mbox{$b_{W}Min_{B}$})}/{2}

and

is the minimal

Open

.

Notably, BAE* guarantees optimality only when given consistent heuristics, due to the nature of the error-correction term

. Consequently, WBAE* requires heuristic consistency to ensure bounded suboptimality. Throughout the remainder of the paper, we therefore assume that the given heuristic

4.2
The Role of
\lambda

In this section, we discuss the role of
\lambda
and provide guidelines for selecting an appropriate value for it.

In heuristic search, algorithms must perform two parallel tasks.
The first task is finding and refining solutions, which corresponds to lowering the upper bound (
U
).
The second task is proving the (sub)optimality of solutions, corresponding to increasing the lower bound (
LB
).
Search algorithms can only terminate when
U\leq LB
, making both tasks essential.
However, the approaches for tackling each task often contradict one another.
For example, the DVCBS algorithm
(
29
)
excels at increasing the lower bound but often finds solutions more slowly than other algorithms, whereas Greedy Best-First Search (GBFS)
(
10
)
—which prioritizes nodes based solely on their
h
-values—is good at finding solutions, but is not concerned with proving optimality.

We next show that while incorporating the heuristic error
d
supports the task of proving solution suboptimality (the second task), it can hinder the process of finding a solution (the first task).
Given two nodes,
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
, in direction
D
with
\mbox{$g_{D}$}(n_{1})+W\cdot\mbox{$h_{D}$}(n_{1})=\mbox{$g_{D}$}(n_{2})+W\cdot\mbox{$h_{D}$}(n_{2})
,
WBAE
∗
with
\lambda>0
delays the expansion of the node with the higher heuristic error
d
.
By definition,
d(n)=\mbox{$g_{D}$}(n)-\mbox{$h_{\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.6pt}}{D}}$}(n)
.
Due to admissibility,
\mbox{$g_{D}$}(n)\geq\mbox{$h_{\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.6pt}}{D}}$}(n)\geq 0
.
Thus, for every node
n
,
d(n)\leq\mbox{$g_{D}$}(n)
.
Consequently, nodes with higher
g
-values tend to have higher
d
values, and their expansion is often delayed by
WBAE
∗
.
Expanding nodes with high
g
-values is essential for finding solutions.
Therefore,
WBAE
∗
is expected to be less efficient than WBiA
∗
in finding solutions.
Nonetheless,
d
offers a more accurate estimate of the lower bound on solution cost, making a larger
\lambda
more effective for establishing the (sub)optimality of solutions.

Due to the trade-off between increasing
LB
and decreasing
U
, different approaches are required based on the specific challenge of each task.
At one extreme, if the initial heuristic yields a sufficiently high lower bound to guarantee (sub)optimality upon finding a solution, the focus should be on finding a solution—favoring a small (or zero)
\lambda
, especially when valid solutions are rare.
Conversely, when many (sub)optimal solutions exist but proving their quality requires extensive search, the emphasis should be on raising
LB
, implying a large
\lambda
.
In general, we expect the task of increasing
LB
to be dominant when: (i) the heuristic is weak, significantly underestimating the shortest paths from many states, and (ii)
W
is small, as the effort to prove suboptimality increases.
Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis on the role of
\lambda
:
Larger values of
\lambda
are more effective when the heuristic is weak and the suboptimality bound
W
is small. Smaller values of
\lambda
are preferable when the heuristic is more accurate or when
W
is large.

5
Obtaining Stronger Lower Bounds

In this section, we examine two methods to tighten lower bounds throughout the search, potentially reducing the effort needed by BSS algorithms to return a suboptimal solution.

5.1
Alternative Termination Criteria

As reviewed in the background section, algorithms in the WA* family typically terminate based on the minimum values of their priority, which multiply the heuristic estimation
h
(but not the cost
g
) by
W
.
For example, WBiA
∗
terminates when
U\leq\max(\mbox{$pr_{W}Min_{F}$},\mbox{$pr_{W}Min_{B}$})
, where
U
is the cost of the incumbent solution, and
pr_{W}Min_{F}
,
pr_{W}Min_{B}
are the minimal
pr_{W}
-values in the open lists.

Nonetheless,
fMin_{F}
and
fMin_{B}
are still lower bounds on
C^{*}
, the cost of the shortest path between
start
and
goal
.
Consequently,
W\cdot\max(\mbox{$fMin_{F}$},\mbox{$fMin_{B}$})\leq W\cdot C^{*}
, and therefore
U\leq W\cdot\max(\mbox{$fMin_{F}$},\mbox{$fMin_{B}$})
is an alternative termination condition that still maintains the bounded-suboptimality guarantees.
Moreover, let
n
be the node with the minimal
f
-value in direction
D
.
Thus,

\displaystyle W\cdot\mbox{$fMin_{D}$}

\displaystyle=W\cdot\mbox{$g_{D}$}(n)+W\cdot\mbox{$h_{D}$}(n)

\displaystyle\geq\mbox{$g_{D}$}(n)+W\cdot\mbox{$h_{D}$}(n)

\displaystyle\geq\mbox{$pr_{W_{D}}$}(n)\geq\mbox{$pr_{W}Min_{D}$}

This means that the alternative lower bound (denoted
ALB
) is tighter than the original lower bound, which can result in fewer expansions during the search.

The above
ALB
was defined based on
fMin
.
Nonetheless, a similar
ALB
can be defined for all algorithms in the WA* family by taking the bounds of their corresponding optimal variant and multiplying them by
W
.
For
WBAE
∗
,
ALB
will therefore halt the search when
U\leq W\cdot LB_{B}
.

Nevertheless, using
ALB
incurs extra overhead as it maintains both priorities (e.g.,
pr_{W}Min_{D}
and
fMin_{D}
for WBiA
∗
, or
b_{W}Min_{D}
and
bMin_{D}
for
WBAE
∗
).
This might require storing two open lists for each frontier.
We analyze the improvement resulting from
ALB
, as well as the computational overhead, in our empirical evaluation below.

demonstrated how edge cost information can tighten lower bounds on
C^{*}
throughout the search.
They assumed the algorithm knows the
greatest common denominator
(GCD) of all edge costs,
\iota
, and used it to redefine
BAE
∗
’s termination condition:

LB_{B}=\left\lceil\tfrac{{(\mbox{$bMin_{F}$}+\mbox{$bMin_{B}$})}/{2}}{\iota}\right\rceil\cdot\iota

and showed that this new definition is a lower bound on
C^{*}
, based on the idea that solutions can only increase in GCD increments.
Thus, if a lower bound suggests a solution not divisible by
\iota
, it can be rounded up to the nearest
\iota
increment.
For example, if
\iota=3
and the lower-bound
LB=14
,
\lceil 14/3\rceil\cdot 3=15
can be used as a tighter lower bound.
A similar approach could apply to A*’s lower bound, but typically,
\mbox{$fMin$}=g(n)+h(n)
is already divisible by
\iota
since
g(n)
is always divisible by
\iota
, and
h(n)
is often as well.

This alteration to the lower bound formula can also be applied to the BSS variants of
BAE
∗
as follows:

LB_{\mathtt{WB}}=\left\lceil\tfrac{{(\mbox{$b_{W}Min_{F}$}+\mbox{$b_{W}Min_{B}$})}/{2}}{\iota\cdot W}\right\rceil\cdot\iota\cdot W

Let
n_{f}
and
n_{b}
be the nodes with the minimal
b
-value in
Open
F
and
Open
B
, respectively, at some arbitrary iteration during the search.
This formulation of
LB_{\mathtt{WB}}
still yields bounded solutions, since:

\displaystyle\left\lceil\tfrac{(\mbox{$b_{W}Min_{F}$}+\mbox{$b_{W}Min_{B}$})/{2}}{\iota\cdot W}\right\rceil\cdot\iota\cdot W

\displaystyle\leq\left\lceil\tfrac{(\mbox{$b_{W_{F}}$}(n_{f})+\mbox{$b_{W_{F}}$}(n_{B}))/{2}}{\iota\cdot W}\right\rceil\cdot\iota\cdot W

\displaystyle\leq\left\lceil\tfrac{(W\cdot{(\mbox{$b_{F}$}(n_{f})+\mbox{$b_{F}$}(n_{B}))}/{2}}{\iota\cdot W}\right\rceil\cdot\iota\cdot W

\displaystyle=\left\lceil\tfrac{W\cdot(\mbox{$bMin_{F}$}+\mbox{$bMin_{B}$})/{2}}{\iota\cdot W}\right\rceil\cdot\iota\cdot W

\displaystyle=\left\lceil\tfrac{(\mbox{$bMin_{F}$}+\mbox{$bMin_{B}$})/{2}}{\iota}\right\rceil\cdot\iota\cdot W\leq C^{*}\cdot W

Similar adjustments can be applied to all WA*-family algorithms.
Notably, for
W>1
, this revised formulation can benefit WBiA
∗
(and also WA*), as
\mbox{$fMin$}=g(n)+W\cdot h(n)
is not guaranteed to be divisible by
W
.
Moreover, unlike ALB, which requires maintaining multiple open lists that incur additional computational overhead, the GCD improvement adds no computational cost.

Moreover, neither WMM nor WBS

(similar to

We integrated the core concept of Weighted A* (WA*) into

∗
outperforms related algorithms in many cases. We also provided practical guidelines for using
WBAE
∗
and selecting

Appendix A: Bounded suboptilmality of WBiA
∗

We prove below that WBiA
∗
is guaranteed to return bounded suboptimal algorithms. The proof is a direct generalization of the proof of WA*.

Theorem 4
.

WBiA
∗
is bounded-suboptimal.

Proof.

We need to prove that when a solution is returned with cost
U\leq LB_{W}
then it holds that
LB_{W}\leq W\cdot C^{*}
.
Without loss of generality, assume that the
\max
term is
pr_{W}Min_{F}
.
Let
x
be the node for which
\mbox{$pr_{W_{F}}$}(x)=\mbox{$pr_{W}Min_{F}$}
and let
y
be the node for which
\mbox{$f_{F}$}(y)=\mbox{$g_{F}$}(y)+\mbox{$h_{F}$}(y)=\mbox{$fMin_{F}$}
, where
fMin_{F}
is the minimal
f
-value in
Open
F
.

Given an admissible heuristic, it holds that
\mbox{$f_{F}$}(y)\leq C^{*}
.
Note that the open lists are sorted according to
pr_{W_{D}}
so
\mbox{$pr_{W_{F}}$}(x)\leq\mbox{$pr_{W_{F}}$}(y)
.
As a result, we get:

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle\leq\mbox{$pr_{W}Min_{F}$}

\displaystyle=\mbox{$g_{F}$}(x)+W\cdot\mbox{$h_{F}$}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\mbox{$g_{F}$}(y)+W\cdot\mbox{$h_{F}$}(y)

\displaystyle\leq W\cdot\mbox{$g_{F}$}(y)+W\cdot\mbox{$h_{F}$}(y)

\displaystyle=W\cdot\mbox{$f_{F}$}(y)\leq W\cdot C^{*}

Thus, WBiA
∗
returns solutions that are bounded by
W\cdot C^{*}
, making it a BSS algorithm.
∎

Appendix B: Absence of Node Re-expansion with Consistent Heuristics

Theorem 3
.

Given consistent heuristics
WBAE
∗
finds a bounded-suboptimal solution even if never re-expand nodes.

Proof.

To prove this lemma, it suffices to show that every node is expanded with a bounded-suboptimal path. Specifically, we need to demonstrate two conditions:

(i)

Whenever a node
u
is expanded in the forward direction (
F
), it holds that
\mbox{$g_{F}$}(u)\leq W\cdot c(start,u)
.

(ii)

Similarly, whenever a node
u
is expanded in the backward direction (
B
), it holds that
\mbox{$g_{B}$}(u)\leq W\cdot c(u,goal)
.

We will prove condition (i); the proof for condition (ii) is symmetrical.

Assume, for the sake of contradiction, that
WBAE
∗
expands a node
u
such that
\mbox{$g_{F}$}(u)>W\cdot c(start,u)
. Further, assume that
u
is the first node expanded in this manner. Note that
u\neq start
, since the start node is expanded with an optimal cost of zero.

Given that
u
is the first node expanded with an unbounded suboptimal path cost, there must exist another node
u^{\prime}\in\textsc{Open\textsubscript{F}}
that lies on the optimal path from
start
to
u
, satisfying
\mbox{$g_{F}$}(u^{\prime})+c(u^{\prime},u)\leq W\cdot c(start,u)
. Since
u^{\prime}
is on the optimal path, we have
c(start,u)=c(start,u^{\prime})+c(u^{\prime},u)
. Now, because
\mbox{$g_{F}$}(u)>W\cdot c(start,u)
, it follows that:

\mbox{$g_{F}$}(u)>W\cdot c(start,u^{\prime})+W\cdot c(u^{\prime},u).

Since
\mbox{$g_{F}$}(u^{\prime})\leq W\cdot c(start,u^{\prime})
, we have:

\mbox{$g_{F}$}(u)>\mbox{$g_{F}$}(u^{\prime})+W\cdot c(u^{\prime},u).

Additionally, since
c(u^{\prime},u)\geq 0
, we know
\mbox{$g_{F}$}(u^{\prime})<\mbox{$g_{F}$}(u)
.

Thus, we can evaluate the
b_{F}
values:

\mbox{$b_{W_{F}}$}(u^{\prime})=\mbox{$g_{F}$}(u^{\prime})+W\cdot\mbox{$h_{F}$}(u^{\prime})+\lambda(\mbox{$g_{F}$}(u^{\prime})-\mbox{$h_{B}$}(u^{\prime})).

Substituting the known inequalities, we get:

\displaystyle\mbox{$b_{W_{F}}$}(u^{\prime})<

\displaystyle\mbox{$g_{F}$}(u)-W\cdot c(u^{\prime},u)+W\cdot\mbox{$h_{F}$}(u^{\prime})

\displaystyle+\lambda(\mbox{$g_{F}$}(u)-W\cdot c(u^{\prime},u)-\mbox{$h_{B}$}(u^{\prime})).

Using the consistency of the heuristics,
\mbox{$h_{F}$}(u^{\prime})\leq c(u^{\prime},u)+\mbox{$h_{F}$}(u)
and
\mbox{$h_{B}$}(u)\leq c(u^{\prime},u)+\mbox{$h_{B}$}(u^{\prime})
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mbox{$b_{W_{F}}$}(u^{\prime})<

\displaystyle\mbox{$g_{F}$}(u)+W\cdot\mbox{$h_{F}$}(u)

\displaystyle+\lambda(\mbox{$g_{F}$}(u)-\mbox{$h_{B}$}(u)-(W-1)c(u^{\prime},u)).

Since
W\geq 1
and
c(u^{\prime},u)\geq 0
, this simplifies to:

\mbox{$b_{W_{F}}$}(u^{\prime})<\mbox{$g_{F}$}(u)+W\cdot\mbox{$h_{F}$}(u)+\lambda(\mbox{$g_{F}$}(u)-\mbox{$h_{B}$}(u))=\mbox{$b_{W_{F}}$}(u).

This contradicts the assumption that
u
is expanded before
u^{\prime}
.

Consequently, every path discovered during the search is guaranteed to be bounded-suboptimal. Since the algorithm terminates only once a path is found whose cost is greater or equal to the lower bound, it must return a path of cost that does not exceed
W\cdot C^{*}
.
∎
