The Tractability Border of Reachability in Simple Vector Addition Systems with States
Dmitry Chistikov Wojciech Czerwiński Filip Mazowiecki Łukasz Orlikowski Henry Sinclair-Banks Karol Węgrzycki

Abstract
Vector Addition Systems with States (VASS), equivalent to Petri nets, are a well-established model of concurrency. A d d -VASS can be seen as directed graph whose edges are labelled by d d -dimensional integer vectors. While following a path, the values of d d nonnegative integer counters are updated according to the integer labels. The central algorithmic challenge in VASS is the reachability problem: is there a run from a given starting node and counter values to a given target node and counter values? When the input is encoded in binary, reachability is computationally intractable: even in dimension one, it is NP -hard. In this paper, we comprehensively characterise the tractability border of the problem when the input is encoded in unary. For our main result, we prove that reachability is NP -hard in unary encoded 3-VASS, even when structure is heavily restricted to be a simple linear path scheme. This improves upon a recent result of Czerwiński and Orlikowski (2022), in both the number of counters and expressiveness of the considered model, as well as answers open questions of Englert, Lazić, and Totzke (2016) and Leroux (2021). The underlying graph structure of a simple linear path scheme (SLPS) is just a path with self-loops at each node. We also study the exceedingly weak model of computation that is SPLS with counter updates in { − 1 , 0 , + 1 } \{-1,0,+1\} . Here, we show that reachability is NP -hard when the dimension is bounded by 𝒪 ⁡ ( α ⁡ ( k ) ) \mathcal{O}(\alpha(k)) , where α \alpha is the inverse Ackermann function and k k bounds the size of the SLPS. We complement our result by presenting a polynomial-time algorithm that decides reachability in 2-SLPS when the initial and target configurations are specified in binary. To achieve this, we show that reachability in such instances is well-structured: all loops, except perhaps for a constant number, are taken either polynomially many times or almost maximally. This extends the main result of Englert, Lazić, and Totzke (2016) who showed the problem is in NL when the initial and target configurations are specified in unary.

Introduction

The reachability problem has always been of core interest in computer science.
The halting problem, which is undecidable, is an instance of reachability from some starting configuration in a Turing machine to some accepting configuration.
Based on a seminal (now folklore) result by Minsky
[
44
]
, reachability is undecidable in a much simpler model: two-counter machines.
This model can be seen as a finite automaton with two nonnegative valued integer counters in which transitions can increment, decrement, or test the counter for zero (i.e. the transition can only be taken if a specified counter has zero value).
It is natural to consider a model without zero tests: Vector Addition Systems with States (VASS).
VASS are a fundamental model of computation that has been studied since the 1970s
[
31
,
27
,
29
]
.
They are equivalent to Petri nets, a popular and simple model of concurrency with
vast applications
[
54
,
53
,
10
,
5
,
9
]
.

Concretely, a
d
-dimensional VASS (
d
-VASS) is a directed graph with edges labelled by vectors from
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
Vertices of this graph are called states and edges are called transitions.
A configuration of a VASS is a state
p
and a vector
{\bf v}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, denoted
p({\bf v})
.
Notice that vectors in the configurations are always nonnegative, but this is not necessarily true for the transition labels.
This is because vectors labelling the transitions correspond to updates applied to counters which can only take nonnegative values.
Precisely, suppose there is a transition from
p
to
q
labelled by
{\bf u}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, then one can take this transition from the configuration
p({\bf v})
to reach the configuration
q({\bf v+u})
so long as
{\bf v}+{\bf u}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
.
The reachability problem asks for a given VASS, initial configuration, and target configuration, whether there is a sequence of transitions from the initial to the target configuration.
The dimension of the VASS can be fixed or variable; when it is fixed, it is specified (i.e. reachability in
d
-VASS vis-à-vis in VASS).

The complexity of reachability in VASS has a great history and literature.
Early on, the problem was proved to be
EXPSPACE
-hard by Lipton in 1976
[
41
]
and decidable by Mayr in 1981
[
43
]
.
More recently, the problem has amassed plenty of attention and its complexity was finally settled: reachability in VASS is
Ackermann
-complete
[
36
,
37
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
40
]
.
Finer improvements to the upper and lower bounds are an area of active research
[
32
,
13
]
.

Reachability in fixed dimension VASS has also been studied earnestly.
When the dimension is fixed, the encoding of the VASS matters.
Integer vectors that are transition labels can be encoded in binary (“
binary VASS
”) or in unary (“
unary VASS
”).
Reachability is

-complete for both unary 1-VASS
[

]
and unary 2-VASS

On the other hand, reachability is

Determining the complexity of reachability in 3-VASS, with either unary or binary encoding, is an intriguing and long-standing problem
(see, e.g.,
[
7
,
6
,
18

35
]

[
29
]
.
In this paper, we present the first non-trivial lower bound for reachability in 3-VASS: we show that reachability in unary 3-VASS is
NP
-hard.
In fact, our lower bound holds for an extremely restricted kind of VASS that we will detail shortly.

Due to both its wide applicability and high complexity, reachability has been studied for numerous variants and subclasses of VASS (for a survey on the matter, see
[
49
]
).
Flat VASS are a well-studied subclass of VASS that do not contain any nested cycles
[
12
,
38

39
,
35
]

[
34
]

Each state in a flat VASS belongs to at most one cycle; the underlying graph structure can be seen as a directed acyclic graph with disjoint cycles attached to each of the nodes.
Our paper focuses on reachability in flat VASS.
Regardless of the dimension, and even when encoded in binary, reachability in flat VASS is in
NP

[
26
,
15
]
.
Already in dimension one, reachability in binary flat 1-VASS is
NP
-hard
[
28
]
.
The complexity of reachability in unary flat

The only known upper bounds below

are for dimensions

,
24
]

Negatively resolving questions in

,

]
, reachability in unary flat VASS was shown to be

-hard for dimension

Dimension

We close this line of research, showing that reachability in unary flat 3-VASS is

Perhaps surprisingly, we prove hardness by first restricting our systems even further, as follows.

Linear Path Schemes (LPS) are flat VASS that, informally speaking, do not have branching.
More precisely, an LPS is a flat VASS whose underlying graph structure is a simple path with disjoint cycles attached to the nodes.
In other words, LPS have the form
\alpha_{1}\,\beta_{1}^{\,*}\,\alpha_{2}\,\cdots\,\alpha_{n-1}\,\beta_{n}^{\,*}\,\alpha_{n}

LPS are a basic and well-known subclass of VASS; a flat VASS can be seen as a union of (possibly exponentially many) LPS

6
]

To go one step further, we consider an exceedingly weak model of computation: LPS with cycles of length at most one; these innocuous VASS are known as Simple Linear Path Schemes (SLPS)

Prior to our work, it was not known whether reachability in unary

-LPS was

Overview of Results: Contributions and Techniques

Our first main contribution is the following theorem, on (unary flat) 3-VASS.
This result answers open questions on reachability in 3-LPS
[
24
, Section 6]
and
[
35
, Section 5]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Reachability in unary 3-SLPS is
NP
-complete.

To show
NP
-hardness, we employ a combination of newly developed and known techniques.
Given an instance of 3-CNF SAT
\varphi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
, we associate, to the
i
-th Boolean variable
x_{i}
, the
i
-th prime number
p_{i}
.
An assignment
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{n}
corresponds to an integer
v\in\mathbb{N}
so that
x_{i}
is true if
v\equiv 1\bmod p_{i}
and
x_{i}
is false if
v\equiv 0\bmod p_{i}
.
By the Chinese remainder theorem, for every
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{n}
there is a
v\in\mathbb{N}
that
encodes
it:
v\equiv a_{i}\bmod p_{i}
for each
i
.
Some
v\in\mathbb{N}
do not correspond to a valid assignment; e.g., if
v\equiv 2\bmod p_{i}
.
Whilst this encoding is well known, non-deterministic branching is not available in LPS (and SLPS).
So, to ensure validity, we cannot directly “say” that
v\equiv 1\bmod p_{i}
or
v\equiv 0\bmod p_{i}
for every
i
.
Because of this, our construction fits everything into a conjunction (logical
and
) of checks, which together ensure that a “guessed” number
v\in\mathbb{N}
encodes to a valid assignment, and moreover this assignment satisfies
\varphi
.

As it turns out, it suffices to test that
v\in\mathbb{N}
is not divisible by an appropriately chosen small integer
q
,
for polynomially many values of
q
.
This sequence of tests is expressed as a unary 1-SLPS extended with
non-divisibility assertions
(
Lemma

3.1
).
Such assertions can, in turn, be realised by a novel family of unary 2-SLPS extended with zero tests (one 2-SLPS for each non-divisor
q\geq 2
).
The formal description is in
Figure

4
, and
3.2
asserts the correctness.
By combining the 3-CNF SAT implementation with this family, we now have a unary 2-SLPS with zero tests for which reachability coincides with the satisfiability of
\varphi
.
Importantly, the zero tests in the 2-SLPS are heavily restricted: they only lie on transitions between the self-loops, so there are at most polynomially many of them.

Now these zero tests also need to be implemented. To this end, we review the “controlling counter” technique, originally developed in
[
18
, Section 4]
.
Intuitively, this technique takes a VASS with zero tests as input, and outputs a VASS without zero tests.
The new VASS uses an additional counter to simulate a polynomial number of zero tests on the other counters.
In this paper, we provide a new black-box version of the “controlling counter” lemma, which we believe is more amenable and has wider applicability compared to the ones in the literature.
With this new
Lemma

2.2
, the proof of
Theorem

1.1
is completed by applying it to the unary 2-SLPS with zero tests which we constructed above.

Next, we consider what is perhaps one of the weakest models of computation involving multiple counters.
These are VASS with constant updates in
\{-u,-u+1,\ldots,u-1,u\}
, where
u\geq 1
is a constant independent of the size of the VASS.
If
u=1
, i.e. updates can only belong to
\{-1,0,1\}
, we say the VASS is unitary.
In general, one can construct a polynomial size
unitary
VASS that is equivalent to a
unary
VASS: for example, a transition with update
\mathsf{x}

+=

5
can be split into five successive transitions each with the update
\mathsf{x}

+=

1
.
Such a construction does not work for simple LPS, because a single-transition cycle cannot be split into several transitions.
It is not at all clear if there exists a constant dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}
for which reachability in unitary
d
-SLPS is
NP
-hard.
For our second main contribution, we achieve one of the closest results one could hope for.

Theorem 1.2
.

Reachability in unitary
\alpha(k)
-SLPS is
NP
-complete.

Here,
k
denotes the size of the reachability instance and
\alpha:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
is the inverse of the Ackermann function

\mathit{Ack}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
.
Thus, the restriction to
\alpha(k)
-SLPS permits the dimension of the SLPS to grow, but only very slowly relative to the size.

We use the same approach to obtain
NP
-hardness: reduction from 3-CNF SAT.
We first reuse
Lemma

3.1
since the aforementioned 1-SLPS with non-divisibility assertions is unitary.
However, we must work harder to implement non-divisibility.
We construct a unitary 5-SLPS to test whether
\mathsf{x}
(the primary counter maintaining the guessed value of the assignment) is not divisible by
q
.
Although it is more intricate than its unary counterpart, the overall idea remains: add a non-zero remainder to
\mathsf{x}
and then test divisibility by

, before restoring the original counter values.
(The construction is presented in
Figure

13

Accordingly, we obtain a unitary 5-SLPS with a polynomial number of zero tests for which reachability coincides with 3-CNF satisfiability.

There is a major difference in how we simulate zero tests in unitary SLPS.
For this, we use technique known as “multiplication triples”
[
18
, Section 3]
which was initially proposed in
[
14

Here, three counters with initial values
\mathsf{b}=B
,
\mathsf{c}=2C
, and

The challenge for us to overcome is how to initialise a triplet of counters with the right values: we need the quantity

to be at least exponential and

to be polynomial.
To do this, we manipulate some ideas, in particular, from
[
32
]
, that were used to show that reachability in VASS is

-hard.
We build a unitary
d
-SLPS of size
\mathcal{O}(A(d))
i
i

i

For our purposes, we define a fast-growing function

, see

.

which, from an initial configuration with counter values

By setting

Once this is achieved, the simulation only requires replacing a zero test with a constant number of transitions and self-loops, which are indeed unitary (

To complement our first two results and to trace the tractability border for reachability in unary
d
-SLPS from the other side (upper bounds), we study an extension of reachability in unary 2-SLPS where the initial and target configurations are encoded in binary.
Recall that reachability is
NL
-complete in unary 2-VASS
[
24

and

-complete in binary 2-VASS
[

In these papers, the encoding of vectors in the VASS transitions, the initial configuration, and the target configuration are treated in the same way.
A recent line of research focuses on VASS in which the encoding of VASS is treated differently to the encoding of source and target

21

Namely, the VASS itself is fixed and only the initial and target configurations form an instance of the problem.
The complexity of the reachability problem in such instances is currently unknown but conjectured to be in

In our result, the VASS is not fixed but, in a similar spirit, the initial and target counter values are encoded differently.

Theorem 1.3
.

Reachability in unary 2-SLPS is in
P
even if the initial and target configurations are encoded in binary.

The proof builds on the result when the initial and target configurations are encoded in unary
[
24
]
and introduces new techniques.
Observe that, in an SLPS
\alpha_{1}\,\beta_{1}^{\,*}\,\alpha_{2}\,\cdots\,\alpha_{n-1}\,\beta_{n}^{\,*}\,\alpha_{n}
, a run is uniquely defined by a vector
(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, i.e. how many times each cycle is performed. We divide the proof into two parts, both interesting on their own.

Figure 1:
The figure presents the schematic view of the structure of
reachability runs for unary 2-SLPS with binary-encoded

s({\bf x})
and
t({\bf y})
. The grey parts of the run (little curves)
are of polynomial length. The first four cycles (straight arrows highlighted in red) are taken

(straight arrows highlighted in blue) can terminate at arbitrary points.

First, in Theorem
4.16
we show that if there is a reachability witness then there is one of a
specific shape, where “almost” every
m_{i}
is either small (polynomial in the size of the input SLPS) or almost as big as possible, i.e. such that the cycle ends not far (polynomially close) from one
of the principal axes. We write “almost” as we allow at most
\mathcal{O}(1)
of
the
m_{i}
’s to be arbitrary (this seems to be unavoidable but is easy to handle nevertheless).
Note that in particular this means that cycles for almost all big
m_{i}
’s are
neither positive nor negative. They are so-called
mixed vectors
, i.e. they strictly increase exactly one coordinate and strictly decrease the other
one.
We refer the reader to
Figure

1
for a schematic overview of the characterisation.

Second, we show that such runs can be found using a dynamic programming algorithm. The main idea is simple: we maintain a subset of reachable configurations and perform consecutive cycles.
Given our above characterisation of runs, whenever we prolong the configurations with the next cycle, we either move only within a polynomial ball of previously reachable configurations (if
m_{i}
is small), or move from one of the axes to the other (if
m_{i}
is big). The issue is that in the latter case, the set of reachable configurations grows multiplicatively by a polynomial factor; thus a naive implementation would result in an exponential algorithm.

Here, we rely on a new idea that allows us to maintain only a small subset
of reachable configurations. We make two big steps at once instead of one.
Recall that, for big steps, most cycles are mixed. Intuitively, a pair of mixed vector updates,
performed as much as possible, multiplies one of the coordinates by a
rational number and does not change the other much.
For example, starting
from
(n,0)
and performing
(-2,+1)
followed by
(+5,-1)
results roughly in

(0,\frac{n}{2})
followed by
(\frac{5}{2}n,0)
, thus multiplying the value by

\frac{5}{2}
. Such a pair can express a vector parallel to the axis, e.g. in
this case
(-2,1)+(5,-1)=(3,0)
. In this case we can forget configurations
which differ by a multiple of
3
on the first coordinate, assuming that performing
(-2,+1)

followed by
(+5,-1)
would not result in a counter dropping below zero.
Intuitively, we
observe that the drop below zero occurs only if the quotient of the values in
the first coordinate is larger than
\frac{5}{2}
, which means that there are
only polynomially many regions that cannot reach one another. Altogether this
allows us to maintain a small number of configurations, as thanks to the first
part it suffices to consider only configurations polynomially close to the
axes (see
Figure

1
).

We want to highlight the following observation, that most complexity upper bounds for VASS and related models are strongly dependent on the length of the shortest run witnessing reachability.
For example, this is the case in the two above-mentioned results for 2-VASS
[
7
,
24
]
, where the shortest reachability witnesses are of polynomial and exponential size, respectively, which immediately yields the
NL
and
PSPACE
upper bounds. This is also the case for the

[
37
]

Our work, among few others, offers techniques going beyond length bounds of the shortest reachability witnesses.
Under

Finally and making for a secondary contribution, we study a subclass of simple linear path schemes called
ultraflat VASS

[

Answering an open question of
[

We approach the lower bound in much the same way as for unary 3-SLPS; for a more detailed discussion, see

Further Related Work

As mentioned above, reachability in flat VASS can be decided in
NP
, even though the shortest reachability witnesses could be exponentially long; this follows from the fact that reachability can be expressed as an integer linear program
[
26
]
.
Another example is
NP

[
28
]
.
The proof idea is to find a positive cycle followed by a negative cycle, which allows transitions to be reordered freely in the middle part of the run.

In linear path schemes, the reachability problem has also been studied with continuous semantics.
In a continuous VASS
[
8
]
, the update of a transition can be multiplied by any real number in
(0,1]
.
Very recently, it was proven that reachability in continuous LPS is in
P

[
3
, Theorem 13]
and reachability in continuous 2-LPS with zero tests is
NP
-hard
[
3
, Theorem 21]
.
Differently from our paper (aside from the continuous semantics), in these results transitions in VASS and LPS are encoded in binary.

Preliminaries

Let
\mathbb{Z}
be the set of integers.
Let
\mathbb{N}
be the set of natural numbers, that is, the set of nonnegative integers.
As usual,
\lvert S\rvert
denotes the cardinality of a set
S
.
We use
\textsf{poly}(n)
to denote
n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
.
We denote integer intervals as
[x,y]=\{z\in\mathbb{Z}:x\leq z\leq y\}
.
We use boldface to denote vectors and we specify a vector by listing its coordinates in a tuple.
We index coordinates of vectors using square brackets:
{\bf v}=({\bf v}[1],\ldots,{\bf v}[d])
, where

is the dimension of the vector.
For two

{\bf u}

{\bf u}[i]\leq{\bf v}[i]

if additionally
{\bf u}\neq{\bf v}
.
A vector

is nonnegative if
{\bf v}\geq(0,\ldots,0)
.
The norm

, of a

We overload this notation for sets of vectors

.

Vector addition systems with states.

A
d
-dimensional Vector Addition System with States
(

-VASS)
\mathcal{V}=(Q,T)
consists of a non-empty finite set of

Q
and a non-empty finite set of

A

Given two configurations

A

A

The

.
The

\mathit{eff}(\pi)={\bf v}_{\ell}-{\bf v}_{0}
.
Given such a run

, we can also write
q_{0}({\bf v}_{0})\xrightarrow{\pi}q_{\ell}({\bf v}_{\ell})
;

.
We can also write

to

in a VASS
\mathcal{V}
.
It is often the case that the VASS

.
Furthermore, when the states

The reachability problem.

An instance

Zero tests.

We will also use counter programs (or VASS) that are enriched with zero tests.
A VASS with zero tests can zero test the counter on special zero-testing transitions; equivalently, a counter program can zero test a counter
\mathsf{x}

\mathsf{x}

A zero-testing transition does not update the counter value and can only be taken if indeed the value of
\mathsf{x}
is zero.
VASS with zero tests, more commonly known as

Formally, a

We will now define the semantics; let

Then

is a run if, for every

is “

In words, the transition

can only be taken from a configuration

if all of the counters indexed by the components of

that are “

\mathit{size}(\mathcal{Z})\coloneqq\lvert Q\rvert+\lvert Z\rvert+\sum_{(p,{\bf x},q)}\lVert{\bf x}\rVert.

In this paper, we always use zero tests in restricted ways.

3
Hardness of Reachability in Linear Path Schemes

In this section, we detail the two main
NP

The first is when the SLPS has unary encoding and three dimensions.
The second is when the SLPS has unitary encoding and at least inverse Ackermann dimensions.

Both lower bounds are obtained by reducing from SAT.
In both reductions, we use an encoding of Boolean vectors by integers sometimes known as the Chinese remainder encoding
(see, e.g., Stockmeyer and Meyer
[
51
, proof of Theorem 6.1]
).
Let
\varphi
be an input formula in 3-CNF with variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
and clauses
C_{1},\ldots,C_{m}

We choose positive integers
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
that are pairwise coprime, for example the first

An integer

encodes an assignment to
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
if and only if
v\bmod p_{i}
is either 0 or 1 for all

; namely, this assignment has

x_{i}\text{ is true }\text{if }v\equiv 1\bmod p_{i}\text{\quad and \quad}x_{i}\text{ is false }\text{if }v\equiv 0\bmod p_{i}.

By the Chinese remainder theorem, for each assignment vector
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, there exists a nonnegative integer
v
that encodes the assignment
x_{i}=a_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
Note that this does not rule out the fact that there exist integers

that do not correspond to assignments: if, for example,
v\equiv 2\bmod p_{i}
for some
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, then
v
does not correspond to an assignment.

First, we provide

we test whether

Conjunction, on the other hand, is straightforward: one can compose two (simple) linear path schemes by joining the end of the first with the start of the second.

3.2
Constant Updates and Inverse Ackermann Dimensions

Typically, the choice of updates (the numbers occurring on the transitions) of a VASS, or their encoding, does not influence expressivity of the model even though that choice is likely to influence its size.
However, if we restrict our focus to VASS subclasses, this may not be true; in this section we consider a situation in which expressivity is influenced by the choice of numbers on the transitions.

A VASS has
constant updates
if all counter updates on all transitions belong to
\{-u,-u+1,\ldots,u-1,u\}
for some constant
u\in\mathbb{N}
that is independent of the size of the VASS.
We say that a VASS is
unitary
if that constant is one (so all updates belong to
\{-1,0,1\}
).
In general, a VASS can be simulated by a VASS with constant updates (in fact by a unitary VASS) of the same dimension.
The construction is rather simple, for example, one can replace a transition with an update of
+5
with five transitions with updates of
+1
.
The size of the resulting unitary VASS is equal to the size of the original VASS times the greatest absolute value of any of its updates.
Thus, a unary VASS can be simulated by a polynomial size unitary VASS of the same dimension.
The same cannot be said for
simple
linear path schemes; the aforementioned construction does not respect the structural requirement that the cycles can only consist of one transition.

We show that the choice of the number to fix the updates does not affect the expressivity of SLPSs.
The following lemma (proved in
Section

C.3
) allows us to focus on
unitary SLPS, noting that we may need to increase the dimension by a constant
factor if we require constant updates of magnitude greater than 1.

Lemma 3.3
.

A
d
-SLPS with constant updates in
\{-u,-u+1,\ldots,u-1,u\}
can be simulated by a unitary
(d\cdot u)
-SLPS.

Unitary SLPS is an
exceedingly weak
model of computation.
Prior to this work, it was not at all clear whether reachability for unitary SLPS is
NP
-complete when the dimension is not fixed, however one can obtain this lower bound by combining
Theorem

1.1
with
Lemma

3.3
.
Besides, we will present a much stronger result: reachability in unitary SLPS with inverse Ackermann dimensions is
NP
-complete.

To be as clear as possible, one can view the reachability problem in inverse Ackermann dimension unitary SLPS as follows.
The input is:

The question is: does there exist a run in
\mathcal{V}
from

to

The number

be an arbitrary natural number.

4
Decomposing Runs in Unary 2-SLPS Between Binary-Encoded Initial and Target Configurations

In this section, we will present a structural result about the shape of the runs that are sufficient to consider as witnesses of reachability in unary 2-SLPS where the initial and target configurations are encoded in binary.
Intuitively speaking, a cycle can be exhausted if the cycle has a negative effect on (at least) one counter.
We will argue that cycles are either iterated a “small” number of times or are “nearly exhausted”, except for a constant number of cycles that can be taken many times.
We will then leverage our structural result about runs to obtain a polynomial time algorithm for reachability in unary 2-SLPS with binary encoded initial and target configurations in

We will now introduce some bounds that will be convenient to use throughout the rest of this section.

Definition 4.7

(Essential subpaths)

Let

A.1
A Simpler Proof of Hardness of Reachability in Unary Flat 3-VASS

Here, we present a relatively simple argument for why reachability in flat unary 3-VASS is

-hard.
Indeed, simple linear path schemes are a restricted variant of flat VASS, so this is subsumed by
Theorem

.
Nevertheless, we outline the proof as this paper marks the conclusion of the line of research concerning the complexity of reachability in flat VASS.
The main high-level difference between linear path schemes and flat VASS is that non-deterministic branching is not a feature of linear path schemes; this simpler argument leverages non-deterministic branching.
Here, we focus our attention to a reduction from 3-CNF SAT.
Consider an instance

with
n

and
m

.
We will create an instance of reachability in unary flat 2-VASS with zero tests that do not belong to the cycles.

\varphi
.
We call the primary counter

and its ancillary counter
\mathsf{y}
.

We associate, to the

j
-th prime

.
For each variable

, let

be the set of indices of clauses containing the positive literal

and

be the set of indices of clauses containing the negative literal

.
On the counter
\mathsf{x}
, we shall “guess” a value corresponding to an assignment in a nonstandard way.
Initially
\mathsf{x}=1
.
Now, suppose

is set to true, we multiply the value of
\mathsf{x}
by all of the primes

such that

.
Similarly, if

is set to false, then we will multiply the value of
\mathsf{x}
by all of the primes

such that

Each multiplication of

by a number

is realised by first transferring the value of

to

by a loop

LOOP:

-=

A.2
The Controlling Counter Technique

Context.

In essence, the
controlling counter technique
of Czerwiński and Orlikowski
[
18
]
is a technique for simulating a fixed number of zero tests in a VASS. The technique uses just one additional counter; the magnitude of the updates that the counter receives is proportional to the number of zero tests that need to be simulated. Therefore, a polynomial size unary
(d+1)
-VASS can be used to simulate a unary
d
-VASS that uses a polynomial number of zero tests.

To this end, the original lemma supporting the controlling counter technique
[
18
, Lemma 10]
offers a “hindsight” view for verifying that zero tests were faithfully simulated.
That is, so long as a run in a given
(d+1)
-VASS has some distinguished properties, then one can guarantee at certain points in the run, some of the first
d
counters must have had value zero.

However, we instead offer a “foresight” view.
If a given
d
-VASS uses a fixed and small number of zero tests, then there exists a
(d+1)
-VASS that can implicitly simulate the zero tests.
This view is more amenable; for an instance of reachability in a
d
-VASS with a small number of zero tests, we can produce an equivalent instance of reachability in a
(d+1)
-VASS (without zero tests).

Our assumptions and notation deviate somewhat from
[
18
, Lemma 10]
.
Namely, we assume that a transition in a
d
-VASS can only zero test one counter at a time.
Such an assumption can be made without loss of generality: a transition with more than one zero test can be split into at most
d
transitions that zero test just one counter each.
We also show that our construction preserves, in some way, the structure of the graph underlying the VASS; we can apply the controlling counter technique to linear path schemes with zero tests and obtain a linear path scheme.

Intuition.

Before we present the construction of
\mathcal{V}
and provide a full proof, we will explain the core idea behind the controlling counter techniques via a basic example.
This example has been presented similarly before
[
18
, Example 9]
.

Consider a scenario in which we need to perform exactly three zero tests on a given counter
\mathsf{x}
in a run
\rho
.
We shall assume, for simplicity, that the initial value of
\mathsf{x}
is zero.
We shall cut the run into four about the three moments in which
\mathsf{x}
is zero-tested.
Call these runs
\rho_{1}
,
\rho_{2}
,
\rho_{3}
and
\rho_{4}
; so
\rho=\rho_{1}\,\rho_{2}\,\rho_{3}\,\rho_{4}
.
Let us denote by
x_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
the effect of
\rho_{i}
on
\mathsf{x}
, for each
i\in\{1,2,3,4\}
.
The first zero test asserts that
x_{1}=0
, the second asserts
x_{1}+x_{2}=0
, and the third asserts
x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}=0
.
Given the semantics of VASS, we also know that
\mathsf{x}\geq 0
at all times, so
x_{1},x_{1}+x_{2},x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}\geq 0
must hold (it is possible for
x_{2}
and
x_{3}
to be negative).
Thus, to check whether
x_{1}
,
x_{1}+x_{2}
, and
x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}
are equal to zero, it suffices to check whether their sum equals zero.
In other words, we only need to check whether
3x_{1}+2x_{2}+x_{3}=0
.

To actually perform this check, we add one additional counter
\mathsf{c}
, which we call
the controlling counter
.
The role of
\mathsf{c}
is to maintain the value
3x_{1}+2x_{2}+x_{3}
, so an instance of reachability can assert that at the end of a run
\mathsf{c}=3x_{1}+2x_{2}+x_{3}=0
.
Initially, set
\mathsf{c}
to have zero value.
In the first part of the run
\rho_{1}
,
\mathsf{c}
receives updates that are three times the updates that
\mathsf{x}
receives; after
\rho_{1}
we know that
\mathsf{c}=3x_{1}
holds.
Similarly, in the second part of the run
\rho_{2}
,
\mathsf{c}
receives updates that are two times the updates that
\mathsf{x}
receives; after
\rho_{2}
, we know that
\mathsf{c}=3x_{1}+2x_{2}
holds.
Then, in the third part of the run
\rho_{3}
,
\mathsf{c}
receives updates that are equal to the updates that
\mathsf{x}
receives; after
\rho_{3}
, we know that
\mathsf{c}=3x_{1}+2x_{2}+x_{3}
holds.
For the remainder of the run after
\rho_{3}
, so throughout
\rho_{4}
,
\mathsf{c}
is not updated.
Therefore, we can just add one counter to simulate the three zero tests on
\mathsf{x}
, so long as
\mathsf{c}=0
is asserted in the target configuration of the reachability instance.

Observe that this technique can be generalised to handle
m
zero tests.
In the section of the run in which
\mathsf{x}
still has
m
many zero tests to be performed, the controlling counter
\mathsf{c}
receives updates that are
m
times the updates that
\mathsf{x}
receives.
Furthermore, this technique can be extended to handle zero tests over multiple counters; the values are just independently added to
c
, and
c
is checked for being zero at the end of the run.
Concretely speaking, in a

C.7
The Ackermann Function and its Inverse

First, let us define a family of
fast-growing
functions
G_{i}
which we will show are comparable to the fast-growing functions defining the Ackermann function.
First, we let
G_{1}(n)=2n
, and the subsequent functions are defined, for
i>1
, inductively by composition,

G_{i+1}(n)\coloneqq\underbrace{G_{i}(G_{i}(\cdots G_{i}}_{\lfloor n/12\rfloor\text{ times}}(1)\cdots)).

We let
A(n)=G_{(n-14)/4}(3)
; this function grows at the same asymptotic rate as the Ackermann function.
In particular, as we show in Claim
C.1
, the inverse of the function
A
is bounded by a constant times the inverse Ackermann function.

As they appear in
[
18
, Section 2]
, the fast-growing functions defining the Ackermann function are as follows:
F_{1}(n)\coloneqq 2n
, and

F_{i+1}(n)\coloneqq\underbrace{F_{i}(F_{i}(\cdots F_{i}}_{n\text{ times}}(1)\cdots)).

Accordingly,
\mathit{Ack}(n)=F_{n}(n)
is the
Ackermann function
and recall that
\alpha(n)=\mathit{Ack}^{-1}(n)
is the
inverse Ackermann function
.
Note that these functions differ slightly from the original defined by Ackermann
[
1
]
, however, for most applications the differences do not matter, this is discussed in
[
48
, Section 4]
, precise details can be found in
[
46
,
42
]
.

Claim C.1
.

There is a constant
c\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
the following inequality holds:

A^{-1}(n)\leq c\cdot\alpha(n).

Proof.

Firstly, it is easy to show by induction that for any
n\geq 3
we have
F_{i}(n)\geq F_{i-1}(n+1)
, therefore
F_{2n}(3)\geq F_{n}(n+3)\geq F_{n}(n)=\mathit{Ack}(n)
.
Furthermore, it is also easy to prove
G_{12i}(n)\geq F_{i}(n)
by induction.
Then for any
n\geq 1
,

A(110n)=G_{(110n-14)/4}(3)\geq G_{96n/4}(3)=G_{24n}(3)\geq F_{2n}(3)\geq\mathit{Ack}(n).

Thus
A^{-1}(n)\leq 110\alpha(n)
, which concludes the proof with
c=110
.
∎

The idea for the first counter of

is that, since we are ignoring the nonnegativity condition of the first counter, we only care for the final counter value to differ from the initial counter value by precisely
{\bf t}[1]-{\bf s}[1]\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Therefore, we only need to determine if
\mathcal{A}
accepts a word such that the number of ‘
a
’s read minus the number of ‘
b
’s read is equal to
{\bf t}[1]-{\bf t}[2]
.
For a set of words
W
over an alphabet
\Sigma
, the
Parikh image

\psi(W)\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\Sigma}
is the set

\psi(W)\coloneqq\{{\bf v}\in\mathbb{N}^{\Sigma}:\text{some $w\in W$
contains, for each $\sigma\in\Sigma$, exactly ${\bf v}[\sigma]$ occurrences of $\sigma$}\}.

With this definition in hand, we need to check, in polynomial time, if there exists a vector
{\bf v}\in\psi(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}))
such that
{\bf v}[a]-{\bf v}[b]={\bf t}[1]-{\bf s}[1]
and for which
{\bf v}[a],{\bf v}[b]\leq P+Q
.
The bound
{\bf v}[a],{\bf v}[b]\leq P+Q
, that we require, is used to inform us that the length of the path
\pi
in
\mathcal{A}
such that
q_{0}({\bf s}[2])\xrightarrow{\pi}q_{f}({\bf t}[2])
is bounded above by
P+Q
.
Now, since
{\bf s}[1]\geq P+Q
, we therefore know that the corresponding run in
\mathcal{V}
that witnesses reachability from
{\bf s}
to
{\bf t}
remains nonnegative.

The main result of
[
4
, Section 4]
gives an algorithm that constructs, for a given one-counter automaton over a fixed alphabet, a non-deterministic finite automaton (NFA) whose language has the same Parikh image.
Instead of invoking this result, we invoke several lemmas that give us a semilinear representation of this Parikh image, for our automaton
\mathcal{A}
over
\Sigma=\{a,b\}
.
Using the terminology of that paper,
\mathcal{A}
is a simple OCA.
We omit the definitions of semilinear and linear sets; as
|\Sigma|=2
, all vectors in these sets will be two-dimensional (think
\mathbb{N}^{2}
).
By
[
4
, Lemma 15]
, we obtain a representation of the semilinear set
\psi(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}))
(“semilinear representation”) that has polynomial size.
More precisely, the number of linear sets in the union, the number of basis vectors, and the number of period vectors, as well as all of the components in said vectors, are all polynomially bounded.
By using
[
4
, Lemma 16]
, this description can be computed in polynomial time.
Since we care to observe a vector
{\bf v}\in\psi(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}))
such that
{\bf v}[a]-{\bf v}[b]={\bf t}[1]-{\bf s}[1]
, we can define the (semi)linear set
R=\{{\bf v}\in\mathbb{N}^{\Sigma}:{\bf v}[a]-{\bf v}[b]={\bf t}[1]-{\bf s}[1]\}
.
We next use the handy fact that the intersection of two semilinear sets with polynomial descriptions is itself semilinear and has a polynomial description too
[
11
, Theorem 6]
.
Importantly for our purposes, when we measure the description size here, the maximum component of each vector in the semilinear representation (in the notation of
[
11
]
: the
norm
) is bounded from above by a polynomial.
Therefore, if
\psi(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}))\cap R\neq\emptyset
, then there exists
{\bf v}\in\psi(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}))\cap R
such that
{\bf v}
is polynomially bounded (on both components
{\bf v}[a]
and
{\bf v}[b]
).

Now we know that if there exists
{\bf v}\in\psi(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}))
such that
{\bf v}[a]-{\bf v}[b]={\bf t}[1]-{\bf s}[1]
, then there exists such a
{\bf v}
that is polynomially bounded and since
\Sigma
is fixed, there are at most polynomially many such vectors
{\bf v}
.
Finally, it remains to enumerate every possible polynomially bounded vector
{\bf v}
such that
{\bf v}[a]-{\bf v}[b]={\bf t}[1]-{\bf s}[1]
and, by
[
4
, Lemma 17]
, determine whether
{\bf v}\in\psi(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}))
in polynomial time.

Finally, we remark that the integer
Q
depends on the polynomial bound on
{\bf v}\in\psi(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}))\cap R
(if
\psi(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}))\cap R\neq\emptyset
).
This bound itself depends on the size of semilinear description of
\psi(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}))
(as discussed above, this is polynomial in the size of
\mathcal{A}
; recall that the size of
\mathcal{A}
is itself polynomial in the size of
\mathcal{V}
); the size of the semilinear description of
R
; and the polynomial bounds obtained when intersecting two semilinear sets with polynomial-size descriptions.
∎
