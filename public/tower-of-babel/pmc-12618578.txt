Characteristics and drivers of spatial–temporal coupling of carbon emission performance and ecological resilience in the Yellow River Basin
Authors: Qingyuan Xiong, Zhenwen Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This paper investigates the spatial and temporal evolution of the coordination between carbon emission performance (CEP) and ecological resilience (ER) in the Yellow River Basin, considering China’s active urbanization transformation and commitment to the “dual-carbon” target. We establish a comprehensive evaluation system using the super-efficiency SBM model and the entropy weight TOPSIS method. Additionally, we analyze the dynamic evolution and driving factors of CEP and ER in the region through Gaussian kernel density estimation and geographic detector. The findings reveal that: CEP in the Yellow River Basin fluctuates during 2011–2021, with a slight overall change, while ER shows a consistent increase. Notably, CEP decreases notably in the upstream, moderately in the middle reaches, and marginally in the downstream. Conversely, ER improves in the upstream but declines overall, with slight enhancements in the middle reaches and little change downstream. The coupling coordination degree of CEP and ER among prefecture-level cities in the Yellow River Basin exhibits fluctuating patterns, with modest increases in the middle and upper reaches and slight decreases in the lower reaches. Overall, the coordination degree is disordered, with regional disparities. High-value points were notable in the downstream in 2011, peaked in the midstream in 2017, but declined overall by 2021.The dynamic evolution of the coupling coordination degree of carbon emissions and ecological resilience reflects the complex interplay between urbanization, environmental policies, and geographic factors in the Yellow River Basin.

Introduction

In recent years, scholars both domestically and internationally have conducted multidimensional research on carbon emission performance (CEP) and ecological resilience (ER) in the Yellow River Basin, covering aspects such as accounting methods, spatiotemporal characteristics, influencing factors, and the coupling relationship between the two1–3.

In CEP research, evaluation primarily relies on non-parametric efficiency measurement models4. Among these, the super-efficiency SBM model, which can simultaneously handle undesirable outputs and break through the 1.0 efficiency value limit, has become a mainstream method5,6, effectively addressing the limitations of traditional DEA models in distinguishing efficient decision-making units. Meanwhile, the logarithmic mean divisia index (LMDI) method is widely used to decompose changes in carbon emissions7 into scale, structure, and technology effects due to its residual-free decomposition property. The STIRPAT model provides an extensible empirical framework for analyzing socio-economic driving factors8. Existing research has effectively revealed the spatiotemporal evolution patterns and regional disparities of carbon emissions in the Yellow River Basin. Studies generally indicate that economic scale, industrial structure, energy intensity, and technological level are key factors influencing CEP9. Emission intensity is higher in the energy-rich upper reaches, while total emissions are more prominent in the economically developed lower reaches, showing an overall trend of fluctuation with a gradual deceleration10,11.In ER research, measurement is generally based on the “resistance-recovery-adaptability” theoretical framework. A comprehensive evaluation of regional ER levels is achieved by constructing a comprehensive indicator system and combining objective weighting methods (such as the entropy weight method) with multi-criteria decision-making methods12,13. Additionally, spatial analysis based on landscape pattern indices and ecosystem service valuation provide important supplements for ER quantification14,15. Existing studies show that climate change, land use change, water resource development intensity, and frequent natural disasters are the main factors influencing ER16–19. The ecological resilience of different sections within the basin exhibits significant spatial heterogeneity, with clear differences in resilience levels between the ecologically fragile upper reaches and the human activity-intensive middle and lower reaches.In studies on the coupling relationship between CEP and ER, the Coupling Coordination Degree (CCD) model, geographically and temporally weighted regression (GTWR)20–22, and bivariate spatial autocorrelation analysis23 have become mainstream methods for revealing the spatiotemporal dynamics of their synergistic/trade-off relationships24. Research finds apparent interaction and spatial dependency characteristics between CEP and ER. High coordination areas are mostly distributed in urban agglomerations with higher development levels and better ecological protection, while low coordination areas are concentrated in ecologically sensitive or energy-dependent regions. Economic expansion, technological progress, environmental regulations, and ecological restoration projects are the main drivers promoting the coordinated development of both. However, the synergistic mechanisms of multiple factors and the differentiated coupling pathways across the upper, middle, and lower reaches of the basin require further in-depth analysis.

From the perspectives of resource use efficiency, ecosystem services and economic system feedback, and policy and market mechanisms, there exists a close coupling relationship between enhancing CEP and strengthening ER25–27. As shown in Fig. 1, this relationship exhibits significant spatial heterogeneity across different sections of the Yellow River Basin. In the ecologically sensitive upper reaches, which primarily serve water conservation and ecological barrier functions, high CEP signifies intensive energy and resource use, which can effectively alleviate ecological pressure and support stable ER maintenance. In the middle reaches, an area of intertwined energy and agriculture, high ER, by ensuring the supply and quality of water and soil resources, facilitates the green transformation of agricultural and energy industries, thereby improving CEP. In the highly urbanized lower reaches, high CEP helps mitigate the impact of environmental pollution on ecosystems, while sound ecosystem services can enhance cities’ capacity to cope with climate risks, forming a positive feedback loop. Within the dynamic coupling of ecosystem services and the economic system, the ecological restoration capacity of the upper reaches sustains the basin’s ecological security and CEP sustainability. The stable supply of ecosystem services in the middle reaches helps reduce the environmental costs of industrial and agricultural production. The lower reaches support economic system operation by mitigating heat island effects and regulating rainfall runoff. Policies and market mechanisms need to be implemented differentially according to section characteristics: key ecological functional areas in the upper reaches require strengthened ecological compensation and carbon sink incentives; the middle reach should focus on implementing energy consumption control and paid use of water and soil resources; the lower reach can fully utilize market instruments like carbon trading and green finance to promote industrial ecological transformation and urban ecological construction. Cross-provincial ecological compensation and coordinated emission reduction mechanisms within the basin provide a critical entry point for multi-level policy coupling.Fig. 1The research framework.

Nevertheless, current research still has significant shortcomings: most studies focus on a single-dimensional analysis of either CEP or ER, failing to construct a comprehensive evaluation system that integrates economic, social, and environmental dimensions. Methodologically, the application of models like super-efficiency SBM and entropy-weighted TOPSIS for the synergistic evaluation of CEP and ER at the basin scale is still insufficient. Analyses of the spatiotemporal dynamics of their coupling coordination relationship often rely on traditional CCD models, lacking integration with methods like kernel density estimation to deeply reveal distribution dynamics and evolution trends. Furthermore, analyses of driving mechanisms are mostly limited to identifying the impact of single factors, lacking systematic investigation into the interaction of multiple factors using tools like the geographical detector.

Based on this, this paper makes important contributions in the following aspects:

Firstly, utilizing the super-efficiency SBM model and the entropy-weighted TOPSIS method, this paper constructs a comprehensive evaluation indicator system for CEP and ER in the Yellow River Basin (YRB) region encompassing economic, social, and environmental dimensions. It accurately identifies the asynchronous evolution characteristics of CEP and ER in the Yellow River Basin from 2011 to 2021, characterized by “fluctuating and stagnant performance alongside steadily improving resilience,” and discovers significant spatial heterogeneity in their changes, deepening the understanding of the complex relationship between ecological and economic systems in different sections of the basin. Secondly, this paper employs a coupling coordination degree model combined with a Gaussian kernel density function to systematically analyze the spatiotemporal coupling coordination relationship between urbanization and ecological restoration. By quantitatively assessing their coupling coordination degree, it reveals the dynamic evolution characteristics and regional differences, clearly delineating the typical fact of the CEP-ER coupling coordination relationship being “generally low, regionally differentiated, and temporally fluctuating.” Finally, from the perspectives of dynamic evolution and spatial interaction, this paper reveals the underlying multi-dimensional driving mechanism behind the synergistic development of CEP and ER. The spatiotemporal evolution of the coupling coordination degree is not only a reflection of environmental policy effectiveness but also the result of the complex interplay of urbanization processes, resource endowment constraints, and regional development strategies. This provides an important scientific basis for establishing differentiated and refined coordinated basin management policies.

Materials and data

Study area

The Yellow River Basin (31° 20′ –40° 48′ N, 97° 51′–115° 41′ E) spans nine provincial-level regions from Qinghai to Shandong as shown in Fig. 2, with a drainage area of approximately 752,400 square kilometers. It serves as a crucial ecological barrier and a key energy and agricultural base in China. The basin not only carries multiple national strategic functions but also exhibits significant gradient disparities between its upper, middle, and lower reaches: the upper reaches are characterized by water conservation functions and ecological fragility; the middle reaches represent a zone of energy richness coupled with coordinated soil and water erosion management; while the lower reaches face intense tensions between high urbanization and ecological conservation. Simultaneously, the Yellow River Basin is both a major carbon emission-intensive zone and a focal area for ecological restoration. The coupling process between CEP (carbon emission performance) and ER (ecological resilience) here is profoundly influenced by energy structure, ecological policies, and regional functional differentiation, making it a typical and multi-layered geographical vehicle for studying the synergistic mechanism of “carbon emission–ecological resilience.” Therefore, this study selects the Yellow River Basin as a case to reveal the spatial patterns and driving mechanisms of the CEP-ER coupling relationship from the perspective of segmental synergy.Fig. 2Study area. Dataset sources

The PM2.5 data utilized in this study originates from the annual average concentration data of global PM2.5 provided by Columbia University’s Center for Socio-Economic Data and Applications. Additional data sources include the China Urban Statistical Yearbook, the Statistical Yearbook of Prefectural Municipalities, and the China Energy Statistical Yearbook. Due to data limitations, a refined selection process was undertaken, resulting in the inclusion of 81 cities within the Yellow River Basin spanning the years 2011 to 2021. Missing data were addressed through interpolation and neighborhood value matching techniques.

Initially, the paper computed the CEP and ER within the Yellow River Basin from 2011 to 2021 using the highly efficient SBM model and entropy-weighted TOPSIS. Following this, the coupling coordination degree of CEP and ER was assessed utilizing a coupling coordination degree model. It specifically utilizes capital, labor, and energy as input factors, GDP as desired outputs, and carbon emissions as non-desired outputs to measure carbon emission performance. Calculation of ER

The construction of the ecological resilience indicator system can be examined from three dimensions: resilience, recovery capacity, and adaptability21. However, the traditional understanding and assessment of these three dimensions, while revealing some characteristics of ecosystems to a certain extent, fail to fully reflect the complex interaction processes between external disturbances and internal system elements. The introduction of systems theory provides a new perspective and method for in-depth analysis of ecological resilience, helping to grasp the dynamic changes and internal mechanisms of ecosystems more comprehensively and accurately.

Resilience from a systems theory perspective: From a systems theory viewpoint, resilience is no longer merely confined to the speed at which an ecosystem recovers to its original state. An ecosystem is a complex dynamic system composed of numerous interconnected and interacting elements, including biological communities, abiotic environments, and various ecological processes between them. External disturbances, such as natural disasters and human activities, disrupt the original equilibrium state of the ecosystem, triggering the reorganization and adjustment of internal elements. In this case, resilience manifests as the ability of the ecosystem, after being disturbed, to rebuild a stable structure and restore key functions through synergistic interactions and feedback regulation mechanisms among internal elements. This ability depends not only on the system’s initial state and element characteristics but is also closely related to the type, intensity, and duration of the disturbance.

Recovery capacity from a systems theory perspective: Recovery capacity gains richer connotations within the systems theory framework. It is not only the ability of an ecosystem to withstand external pressures, resist damage, and maintain basic structure but also involves its self-repair and reconstruction capacity after disturbance, and the dynamic relationship between this capacity and the system’s internal structure and function. An ecosystem with good recovery capacity has tightly coupled relationships and flexible regulatory mechanisms among its internal elements. When external disturbances act on the system, it can reduce the overall negative impact by reconfiguring internal elements and adjusting functions.

Adaptability from a systems theory perspective: Adaptability is one of the most critical characteristics of ecosystems from a systems theory viewpoint. It reflects the ability of an ecosystem to proactively adjust its own structure and function according to environmental changes, embodying the system’s intelligence and self-evolution potential. In a constantly changing environment, ecosystems need to continuously perceive external signals and, through information transmission and synergistic interactions among internal elements, make corresponding adjustments and responses.

In summary, to assess ecological resilience more comprehensively and accurately, this paper constructs a systems theory-based comprehensive evaluation framework. This framework should fully consider the interaction process between external disturbances and internal system elements, organically combining the three dimensions of resilience, recovery capacity, and adaptability. Results

Analysis of carbon emission performance and the development of ecological resilience

To precisely analyze the spatiotemporal evolution patterns of Carbon Emission Performance (CEP) and Ecological Resilience (ER) in the Yellow River Basin (YRB), this study conducted a detailed analysis of the average CEP and ER indicator values for 81 prefecture-level cities in the YRB from 2011 to 2021 (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Mean and standard deviation of carbon emission performance and ecological resilience in the Yellow River Basin, 2011–2021.

Mean and standard deviation of carbon emission performance and ecological resilience in the Yellow River Basin, 2011–2021.

From the overall data distribution, the averages of CEP and ER show significant differences, and they exhibit distinctly different yet clearly patterned characteristics during their evolution.

Between 2011 and 2021, the Carbon Emission Performance (CEP) in the Yellow River Basin exhibited a fluctuating evolution trend, which was closely related to the intensity of policy regulation, industrial development pathways, and energy structure transformation processes at different stages within the basin. Specifically: The slight increase in CEP from 2011 to 2012 may be associated with the preliminary industrial lightweighting and energy cleaning transformations in downstream regions such as Shandong and Henan, driven by regional strategies like the “Development Plan for the Efficient Ecological Economic Zone of the Yellow River Delta.” The decline in CEP from 2012 to 2015 reflects the continued dominance of traditional high-carbon industries such as coal power and chemicals in energy-rich areas of the middle and upper reaches (e.g., Inner Mongolia and Shanxi). Although the national “Air Pollution Prevention and Control Action Plan” was implemented, the rigidity of the industrial structure in the short term kept carbon emission intensity high. The recovery of CEP from 2015 to 2017 was closely related to the preparation and introduction of the “Outline of the Ecological Protection and High-Quality Development Plan for the Yellow River Basin” and the vigorous promotion of “coal-to-gas conversion” and circular economy pilots in key cities such as Xi’an and Zhengzhou, indicating that systematic environmental governance policies began to take effect. The renewed fluctuation of CEP from 2017 to 2021 reveals that the coordinated emission reduction mechanisms among different provinces within the basin remain imperfect. For example, the slow progress of renewable energy substitution in western provinces such as Ningxia and Gansu, coupled with the growing energy demand driven accelerated urbanization, partially offset the emission reduction achievements in the central and eastern regions. This fluctuating trajectory demonstrates that improving CEP in the Yellow River Basin not only relies on short-term policy interventions but also requires the establishment of cross-regional, multi-level industry-energy-policy synergy mechanisms to overcome the dependence on high-carbon development pathways and achieve stable optimization of carbon emission performance.

In stark contrast to the fluctuating trend of CEP, the Ecological Resilience (ER) of the Yellow River Basin showed a continuous and systematically robust growth trend from 2011 to 2021. From a systems perspective, this steady improvement profoundly reflects the synergistic enhancement of regional ecosystems across the dimensions of “resistance, recovery, and adaptability.”

In terms of resistance, the sustained rise in ER indicates an overall enhanced buffering capacity of the basin’s ecosystem against external pressures. In the recovery dimension, the year-by-year growth of ER signifies improved speed and efficiency of the system returning to a stable state after disturbances, likely attributable to the implementation of a series of ecological restoration projects in the Yellow River Basin, which strengthened internal structural integration and functional feedback mechanisms. Regarding adaptability, the stable growth rate of ER also reflects the gradual improvement of the ecosystem’s ability to adapt to and learn from long-term environmental changes. By adjusting species composition, structural configuration, and material cycling pathways, the system has achieved functional maintenance and optimization in a changing environment.

However, in contrast to the steady enhancement of ER, the improvement of CEP during the same period lagged overall and exhibited significant volatility, indicating that the low-carbon transition capacity of the economic system and the resilience enhancement of the ecosystem have not yet formed effective synergy. Moving forward, greater emphasis must be placed on optimizing the coupling relationship between these systems. By strengthening policy interventions and technological penetration, the positive feedback mechanisms between the economic and ecological subsystems can be enhanced, thereby promoting the Yellow River Basin’s transition toward a high-quality development path characterized by the synergistic improvement of CEP and ER.

Between 2011 and 2021, the spatial pattern of Carbon Emission Performance (CEP) in the Yellow River Basin underwent significant evolution, closely linked to the region’s economic development stage, policy implementation intensity, and functional positioning. In 2011, medium-to-high carbon emission levels were concentrated in the energy-rich upper reaches (e.g., Ordos, Yulin) and the industrial-urban agglomerations in the lower reaches (e.g., Jinan, Zhengzhou), reflecting the fundamental constraints of resource endowment and industrial structure on carbon emissions. By 2017, the distribution of medium-to-high CEP expanded comprehensively across the upper, middle, and lower reaches. On one hand, this was due to the emission reduction pressures faced by the middle reaches (e.g., Shanxi, Shaanxi) amid the continued expansion of energy-intensive and chemical industries; on the other hand, it was also related to the rising total energy consumption driven by economic growth in downstream urban agglomerations, showing a pattern of “areal diffusion with localized intensification.” By 2021, CEP declined across the entire basin, with the most significant reduction observed in the upper reaches. This turning point was directly associated with the deepened implementation of the Yellow River Basin’s ecological protection and high-quality development strategy. As an ecological security barrier, the upper reaches strictly restricted the expansion of high-energy-consuming industries under the “Dual Carbon” goals and vigorously promoted renewable energy alternatives, such as the construction of wind and solar power bases in Inner Mongolia, effectively reducing carbon intensity. The middle reaches, leveraging industrial transformation and upgrading pilot policies, gradually phased out outdated production capacity and optimized the energy structure, achieving preliminary control over carbon emissions. Although the lower reaches experienced a relatively smaller decline due to their large economic scale and strong energy inertia, they maintained a stable downward trend through low-carbon city initiatives and emission reduction actions in key sectors (e.g., Shandong’s Pilot Zone for the Conversion of Old and New Kinetic Energy). However, rapid urbanization and infrastructure construction within the basin continue to introduce new carbon emission sources, particularly in sectors such as transportation and construction, where rising emissions partially offset the improvements in CEP.

The analysis of ER in the YRB from 2011 to 2021 revealed the following patterned evolution trends: Overall, the basin’s ER showed a pattern of gradual reduction, with the number and extent of medium–high resilience regions significantly shrinking.

In 2011, the spatial pattern of Ecological Resilience (ER) in the Yellow River Basin exhibited a gradient characteristic of “upper reaches > lower reaches > middle reaches,” reflecting profound differences in the resistance capacities of ecosystems across various sections based on their inherent conditions. The upper reaches, with their structurally intact and functionally stable ecosystems, demonstrated strong disturbance resistance. In contrast, the middle reaches, characterized by prominent ecological fragility, showed relatively weak inherent capacity to withstand external pressures. Between 2014 and 2021, the evolutionary paths of ER in different sections significantly diverged, highlighting variations in system recovery and adaptability under the combined influence of external pressures and human interventions. The limited growth of ER in the upper reaches (except for Chifeng City) indicated that despite their relatively stable ecological baseline, the systems struggled to effectively adjust their structures to adapt to new environmental constraints under sustained industrialization and urbanization pressures, revealing a notable lack of adaptability. The significant improvement in ER in the middle reaches can be attributed to the systematic implementation of ecological projects such as soil and water conservation and the Grain for Green Program. These initiatives not only accelerated ecosystem recovery but also enhanced long-term adaptability through vegetation restoration and habitat rehabilitation. The lower reaches maintained stable ER levels without significant improvement, suggesting that under intense human disturbance, the system’s recovery capacity has approached saturation, and its adaptability is constrained by limited natural space, resulting in a “low-level resilience equilibrium.” This evolutionary trajectory demonstrates that enhancing ER relies not only on the system’s inherent resistance but also on sustainable ecological interventions to improve recovery efficiency and on optimizing system structures to expand adaptive capacity, thereby achieving a high-resilience pathway synergistic with “resistance-recovery-adaptability.”

From the perspective of socio-ecological system coupling, the divergent evolution of ER in the middle and lower reaches of the Yellow River Basin profoundly reflects the interactive responses between human activities and natural systems under varying intervention intensities. The significant improvement in ER in the middle reaches can be seen as a result of targeted ecological governance projects enhancing system recovery and adaptability. Through systematic measures such as the Grain for Green Program and soil and water conservation, not only was the ecological baseline improved, but the system structure was also optimized, strengthening its buffering capacity and self-regulatory functions in response to external pressures. This exemplifies a positive feedback mechanism of “targeted human intervention—enhanced system functionality.” The lack of significant improvement in ER in the lower reaches indicates that under high-intensity urbanization and industrialization disturbances, the ecosystem’s resistance has been continuously weakened, and its structure has become simplified, leading to reduced recovery capacity and limited space for adaptability adjustments.

Analysis of the degree of coupling coordination

To deeply explore the spatiotemporal evolution patterns of carbon emissions and ecological resilience in 81 prefecture-level cities in the YRB, we systematically analyzed the spatiotemporal characteristics of their coupled development. Mean value of coupled coordination of carbon emission performance and ecological resilience in the Yellow River Basin, 2011–2021.

From the perspective of the basin’s overall evolutionary pattern, the coordination degree between CEP and ER exhibits moderate fluctuations, reflecting that the two systems have not yet established a stable synergistic relationship at the current stage of development and remain in a process of dynamic adjustment and mutual adaptation.

At the regional scale, evolutionary pathways show significant differences: the coupling coordination degree in the middle and upper reaches demonstrates a slight upward trend, indicating that ecological governance measures such as soil and water conservation and vegetation restoration in this region have not only promoted ecological functional recovery but also generated a certain synergistic regulatory effect on carbon emission intensity, preliminarily revealing a positive interaction between ecological and economic systems. In contrast, the coupling coordination degree in the lower reaches shows a slight decline, reflecting that under the context of high urbanization and industrialization, the tension between economic growth and ecological protection remains prominent. Emission reduction and resilience enhancement have not been effectively synchronized, and the development model still faces challenges of systemic transformation.

Temporal analysis indicates that between 2011 and 2020, the coordination degree in the middle and upper reaches was mostly on par with or slightly below the basin average, suggesting that regional development had not yet broken through the overall coordination bottleneck during this period. Between 2020 and 2021, the coordination level significantly surpassed the basin average, likely closely related to the comprehensive implementation of major strategies for Yellow River ecological protection and the release of policy dividends from large-scale renewable energy development in the middle and upper reaches. The lower reaches once led the basin in coordination level between 2011 and 2015, demonstrating short-term effectiveness of early urbanization and industrial greenization attempts. However, from 2015 to 2021, the coordination degree gradually declined and converged with the overall basin level, indicating insufficient sustainability of the original development model and difficulties in continuously balancing economic growth with ecological resilience enhancement under high-intensity development.

Throughout the study period, the basin-wide coupling coordination degree consistently fluctuated within the low range of 0.34–0.45, indicating that the system as a whole remains in a transitional stage from antagonistic to barely coordinated. This result demonstrates that the ecological-economic system of the Yellow River Basin is still deeply entrenched in structural contradictions between development and protection, urgently requiring systematic policy innovation and cross-regional collaborative governance to break through the current low-level equilibrium state and move toward a new stage of high-quality coordinated development.

To thoroughly examine the spatial evolution patterns of the coupling between CEP and ER in the Yellow River Basin from 2011 to 2021, the natural breaks classification method (Fig. 8) was employed for distribution analysis. Overall, the coupling coordination level across the basin exhibited a fluctuating trend of “initial increase followed by decrease,” with significant regional heterogeneity. In 2011, areas with high coordination were primarily concentrated in the lower reaches, with only sporadic distribution in the upper and middle reaches. In terms of system attributes, the upper reaches generally displayed characteristics of “low CEP—high ER—low coordination,” indicating that the advantage of a robust ecological baseline had not yet been translated into synergistic development effectiveness. The middle reaches mostly exhibited “low CEP—low ER—medium to high coordination,” suggesting that the systemic relationship remained in a state of barely synergistic at a relatively low level. The lower reaches, however, demonstrated an overall pattern of “high CEP—high ER—high coordination,” indicating a relatively balanced development between the economic and ecological subsystems during this period. By 2017, the distribution of high-value points in the middle reaches significantly increased, making it an aggregation area for high coordination levels across the entire basin. High-value points in the lower reaches showed a tendency to diffuse toward the middle and upper reaches, reflecting an enhancement of synergistic effects between regions. In 2021, although the number of high-value points across the basin still showed growth compared to 2011, it declined relative to the peak stage in 2017. Some counties and cities in the upper and middle reaches experienced adjustments characterized by alternating rises and falls in coordination levels, while many areas in the lower reaches showed significant downgrades. Overall, a spatial restructuring pattern of “improvement in the middle and upper reaches, contraction in the lower reaches” emerged. This evolutionary pattern is closely related to regional development pathways and the intensity of policy interventions. Although the lower reaches are economically developed, the rapid advancement of industrialization and urbanization has also brought sustained carbon emission pressures. Some areas failed to achieve systemic synergy through effective structural transformation and ecological regulation, leading to a decline in coordination levels. The middle and upper reaches, however, likely benefited from the implementation of national ecological projects, regional transfer payments, and adjustments in industrial access policies. Between 2011 and 2021, the driving factors influencing the coordination between Carbon Emission Performance (CEP) and Ecological Resilience (ER) in the Yellow River Basin and their respective impacts underwent significant evolution. The dominant factors during each period reflected the core contradictions in the synergistic mechanisms of the system at different developmental stages and the focus of policy interventions.

In 2011, ecological environment (X2), industrialization level (X8), and economic development level (X1) emerged as the most critical driving factors (q statistic > 0.4). This pattern indicated that during the early stage of development, regional coordination was highly dependent on ecological baseline conditions, traditional industrial expansion, and macroeconomic growth, reflecting the preliminary coupling characteristics between economic and ecological systems under an extensive development model.

By 2014, the dominant factors shifted to urbanization level (X4), innovation capacity (X6), and population density (X5). Rapid urbanization exacerbated ecological pressures through mechanisms such as land occupation and growing energy consumption, while innovation inputs had not yet fully translated into green technology benefits. Consequently, urbanization, innovation, and population aggregation collectively exerted a compounded constraining effect on coordination in the short term. In 2017, the influence of most factors diminished, with only industrial structure (X3) and temperature (X7) retaining significant impacts. The persistent effect of industrial structure highlighted the delayed progress in industrial green transformation, while the prominence of temperature factors suggested that climate change was increasingly directly interfering with the stability of the basin’s ecological-economic system through extreme weather events and water resource redistribution. By 2021, industrial structure (X3) and urbanization level (X4) once again became core drivers, indicating that after years of development, lagging industrial upgrading and the environmental costs of urbanization remained critical bottlenecks hindering coordination improvement. Meanwhile, the influence of most factors generally intensified, reflecting that systemic synergy had transitioned from single-factor dominance to multi-factor interaction, with coordination mechanisms becoming increasingly complex. The evolution of factor influence reveals that the operational pathway of CEP-ER coordination in the Yellow River Basin has shifted from “scale-driven” to “structure-constrained.” Future efforts must focus on green industrial transformation, developing sustainable urbanization models, and climate-adaptive governance to achieve a breakthrough in systemic synergy.

Driver interaction detection results

The analysis using nine indicators of driving factors for interaction detection reveals a significant nonlinear enhancement in the interaction between ecological environment and urban development in the Yellow River Basin, indicating a strong coupling effect shaping the coordination between CEP and ER (Fig. 10). The interaction between these factors consistently demonstrates a stronger influence than individual factors alone, underscoring the complexity of their interplay.Fig. 10Driver interaction detection results.

Driver interaction detection results.

In 2011, the most robust interaction occurs between ecological environment and urban development (X2 and X3), with a coefficient of 0.99800, while the weakest interaction is observed between transport and population density (X4 and X6), with a coefficient of 0.69340. By 2014, 2017, and 2021, the strongest two-factor interactions shift to urban development level and population density (X3 and X6), this shift was primarily influenced by policy, economic, and technological factors: Firstly, the ecological protection policy system was continuously improved, evolving from initial construction to forming hard constraints. This led urban development to interact more tightly with other factors around ecological protection goals, relatively reducing the importance of interactions between the ecological environment and other factors. Simultaneously, policies related to new urbanization strategies promoted the enhancement of urban development levels, strengthening interactions between urbanization and factors like population density and industrial structure, making it one of the core driving forces for regional development. Secondly, industrial structure accelerated its adjustment: the proportion of traditional high-energy-consuming industries declined, and emerging industries rose. The development of these industries is related to urban infrastructure and innovation environments, enhancing interactions between urban development level and industrial structure. The driving force of economic development shifted towards innovation: innovation promoted industrial upgrading and urban functional enhancement, strengthening interactions between urban development level and factors like innovation and temperature, reducing the urgency of interactions between the ecological environment and other factors. Finally, advances in new energy and energy-saving technologies changed the energy structure, providing more energy choices for urban development, thus enhancing interactions between urbanization and energy structure optimization. The application of ecological restoration and carbon capture technologies improved the ecological environment and enhanced ecosystem stability. This made urban development need to consider the application requirements of these technologies, strengthening interactions between urbanization and the ecological environment. It also prompted the ecological environment and other factors to collaborate more closely in responding to climate change.

Notably, the synergistic impact of urban development level emerges as the predominant force shaping the coupled and coordinated pattern in the Yellow River Basin, with combinations like urban development level and industrial structure, urban development level and population density, and urban development level and temperature exhibiting strong interactions. Conversely, the synergistic influence of ecological environment appears relatively weaker in comparison. This highlights the pivotal role of urban development in driving the coupled coordination between CEP and ER in the Yellow River Basin, while also emphasizing the need for balanced consideration of ecological factors in shaping sustainable development strategies.

Conclusions

Against the backdrop of China’s urban transformation and the “Dual Carbon” goals, this study focuses on the critical issues of Carbon Emission Performance (CEP) and Ecological Resilience (ER) in the Yellow River Basin. It not only provides an in-depth analysis of their theoretical interactions and impact mechanisms but also achieves significant progress in methodological and theoretical exploration, offering broader perspectives and approaches for research in this field. The main conclusions of this study are as follows:The evolution of CEP and ER in the Yellow River Basin exhibits notable regional heterogeneity and phase-specific characteristics. The upper reaches demonstrate an endowment of high ER and low CEP, the middle reaches face intense trade-offs between energy development and ecological protection, while the lower reaches show significant tension between high urbanization and ecological restoration. Overall, ER levels show continuous improvement, whereas CEP enhancement lags relatively, revealing that economic growth and emission reduction goals within the basin have not yet achieved full synergy.The coupling coordination degree between CEP and ER in the Yellow River Basin remains generally in an antagonistic-break-in period, with substantial room for improvement. Its spatial distribution follows a typical pattern of “middle reaches > upper reaches > lower reaches,” and over time, regional disparities show a converging trend, indicating strengthening interactions between subsystems and making coordinated development an inherent requirement.The evolution of their coupling coordination demonstrates obvious spatial equalization and depolarization trends. The coordination center has undergone multiple flattening processes, with peak fluctuations rising and amplitudes narrowing, reflecting gradually shrinking development disparities among regions within the basin and a transition of the coupling coordination relationship from local agglomeration to relative global equilibrium.The key driving factors influencing their coordination relationship exhibit significant temporal sensitivity and spatial heterogeneity. Factors such as vegetation coverage, urbanization rate, industrial structure, and temperature emerge as dominant forces in different periods and sections, and the interactive enhancement effects among these factors are consistently significantly higher than their independent impacts, highlighting that the coupling coordination of CEP and ER is a complex system process nonlinearly driven by multidimensional factors.
