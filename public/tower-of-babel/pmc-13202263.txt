Effectiveness of Proximal Interphalangeal Joint Orthosis and Therapeutic Exercise in the Management of Trigger Finger: A Prospective Case Series
Authors: Cuong Dinh To, Thanh Xuan Vu, Hung Phuoc Do, Khoa Anh Nguyen, Phi Duong Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTrigger finger, or stenosing tenosynovitis, is a common musculoskeletal disorder characterized by pain, stiffness, and locking of the affected digit. This prospective case series evaluated the outcomes of a commercially available proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joint orthosis combined with therapeutic exercise in patients with Green grades I–III trigger finger. Thumbs were excluded.MethodsPatients aged ≥18 years with trigger finger (Green grades I–III) were treated with a PIP joint orthosis worn during sleep and as needed during the day, together with a structured exercise program including flexor tendon gliding and stretching exercises for 6 weeks. Pain intensity (Numeric Pain Rating Scale [NPRS]), Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand (QuickDASH) scores, and Green’s grades were assessed at baseline and after 6 weeks. Treatment success was defined based on initial Green grade.ResultsSeventy-nine patients (107 affected digits) were enrolled, with a mean age of 51.8 ± 9.8 years and a women-to-men ratio of 3.4:1. After 6 weeks of intervention, median NPRS pain scores significantly decreased from 6 to 1 during movement, and QuickDASH scores improved from 25 to 10. The proportion of digits with Green grade III decreased from 8.0% to 2.3%, whereas grade I increased to 71.3%. The overall treatment success rate was 55.2%.ConclusionsIn this prospective case series, the combination of PIP joint orthosis and therapeutic exercise was associated with reduced pain, improved hand function, and decreased triggering severity in patients with Green grades I–III trigger finger. Because of the absence of a control group, these findings demonstrate feasibility and short-term clinical improvement but cannot establish superiority over other conservative treatments.Type of study/level of evidenceTherapeutic IV.Key words: Green classification, Proximal interphalangeal joint orthosis, QuickDASH, Therapeutic exercise, Trigger finger

Trigger finger (TF), or stenosing flexor tenosynovitis, is a common musculoskeletal disorder affecting approximately 2.6% of the general population.1 It occurs most frequently in middle-aged women and is characterized by pain, stiffness, and locking or catching of the affected digit, which can significantly limit daily activities, such as grasping or holding objects, and negatively impact quality of life.2

Current treatment options for TF include both conservative and surgical approaches.3 Among conservative methods, orthotic intervention is considered a safe and effective option with minimal complications, particularly for patients who prefer to avoid or are unsuitable for corticosteroid injection or surgical release.2,4 Orthoses function by reducing friction between the flexor tendons and the pulley system, thereby promoting tendon and sheath recovery. A systematic review by Lunsford et al2 recommended splinting for a period of 6–10 weeks as part of conservative management for TF.

Most previous studies on orthotic treatment have focused on metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint orthoses, with reported success rates ranging from 70% to 92.9% after 6 weeks of treatment.5, 6, 7 Recently, several studies have demonstrated the potential advantages of proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joint orthoses. Teo et al4 compared custom-made MCP joint orthoses with commercially available PIP joint (Oval-8) orthoses and reported that both designs were effective in reducing pain, improving function, and alleviating locking symptoms; however, the PIP joint orthosis produced superior outcomes. The proposed mechanism involves restricting the hook-fist position, which limits excessive gliding of the flexor digitorum superficialis and flexor digitorum profundus tendons, thereby reducing friction across the A1 pulley.4,8 Similarly, Nadar9 demonstrated that continuous PIP joint orthosis use, both day and night for 6 weeks, effectively alleviated pain and improved function and symptoms in patients with TF.

In addition to orthotic management, therapeutic exercise is a widely used conservative treatment modality for TF. Combining orthotic intervention with therapeutic exercise may offer a more comprehensive rehabilitation strategy. Although the efficacy of PIP joint orthoses has been confirmed in several studies, evidence regarding their combination with exercise therapy remains limited. Moreover, the types of orthoses used in previous studies are not readily available in the Vietnamese market; till date, to our knowledge, no domestic studies have evaluated this combined intervention.

The study objective is to evaluate the improvement in pain, Green’s classification (Table 1), upper limb functional score (Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand [QuickDASH]), and treatment success rate in patients with TF treated using a PIP joint orthosis in combination with therapeutic exercise.

Materials and Methods

Study design

This study was a prospective case series.

Study period and setting

The study was conducted from March to October 2025 at University of Medicine and Pharmacy at Ho Chi Minh City, Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam.

Study population

All patients diagnosed with TF involving the A1 pulley who presented during the study period were screened.

Sampling method

A consecutive sampling was used.

Inclusion criteria

The following inclusion criteria were used: aged ≥18 years; TF involving the A1 pulley (Green grades I–III); and provided informed consent.

Exclusion criteria

The following exclusion criteria were used: isolated thumb involvement; fixed flexion contracture of the PIP joint; prior corticosteroid injection or surgical release for TF; or concomitant musculoskeletal disorders affecting the upper limb.

Sample size

All eligible patients during the study period were included.

Study procedure

Patients meeting inclusion criteria underwent baseline assessment of demographics, clinical features, pain intensity (NPRS at rest and during movement), QuickDASH score, and Green’s grading. All participants received the same intervention: a PIP joint orthosis combined with a structured therapeutic exercise program for 6 weeks. Reassessment was performed at the end of the 6-week intervention.

Intervention program

The program comprised the following steps:

1.Patient education: Patients received information on the condition, ergonomic advice, and activity modification.

Patient education: Patients received information on the condition, ergonomic advice, and activity modification.

2.PIP joint orthosis: A commercially available proximal interphalangeal joint orthosis (Fig.) was used to immobilize the PIP joint in full extension (0°), while allowing free motion at the MCP and distal interphalangeal (DIP) joints. Patients were instructed to wear the orthosis during sleep and during the day when triggering symptoms were most frequent.

3.Therapeutic exercise: The program included flexor tendon gliding exercises and stretching exercises. Each exercise was performed for 10 repetitions per session, three times daily. Stretching was held for 20 seconds with a 5-second rest. Tendon gliding exercises consisted of sequential fist positions (straight fist, hook fist, full fist, and straight fist again), each held for 5 seconds with a 5-second rest. Exercises were performed after removing the orthosis and performed gently without increasing pain.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using STATA version 14.0. Qualitative variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages (rounded to the nearest whole number). McNemar test was used for paired categorical comparisons, and Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for continuous variables (P < .05 considered statistically significant).

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Medicine and Pharmacy at Ho Chi Minh City (Approval No. 63, dated October 24, 2023). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians prior to inclusion in the study.

Results

A total of 79 patients with 107 affected digits diagnosed with TF were included in the study. The mean age of participants was 51.76 ± 9.82 years (range, 24–70 years), with the 50- to 59-year-old age group representing the largest proportion (43%). Women predominated, with a men-to-women ratio of 1:3.39. The majority of participants were engaged in occupations requiring repetitive hand and finger movements (73.4%). Regarding the affected side, the right hand was most commonly involved (53.2%). In terms of comorbid conditions, most patients had no associated diseases (88.6%), whereas 11.4% had diabetes mellitus. The majority presented with a single affected digit (68.35%), and only a small proportion (3.8%) had involvement of three digits (Table 2).

The majority of patients had a disease duration of ≤6 months (88.8%). Among the 70 patients who completed reassessment at 2 weeks after intervention, most had received oral nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) (97.1%). The duration of NSAID use was generally short term, with 80.00% of patients using the medication for 2 weeks. In contrast, only 8.6% used it for 4 weeks, and 8.6% used it for 6 weeks. Based on Green’s classification, among the 107 affected digits, the majority were classified as grade I or II, accounting for a combined 92.5% of cases, whereas grade III was observed in only 7.5% (Table 3).

Among the 64 patients (corresponding to 87 affected digits) who completed the full intervention program, the majority of digits were initially classified as grade II according to Green’s classification (47.1%), whereas grade III accounted for 8%. After 2 and 4 weeks of intervention, the proportion of digits with grade III severity gradually decreased to 5.8% and 3.4%, respectively. Following 6 weeks of treatment, Green’s grades showed a marked improvement, with 71.3% of digits classified as grade I, 26.4% as grade II, and only 2.3% remaining as grade III.

Following the intervention, patients demonstrated a significant reduction in pain at rest, with the median Numeric Pain Rating Scale (NPRS) score decreasing from 1 (interquartile range [IQR] 2) at baseline to 0 (IQR 0) after 6 weeks. Similarly, pain during movement showed marked improvement, with the median NPRS score decreasing from 6 (IQR 2) to 1 (IQR 2) after 6 weeks. The median change in pain during movement was –4 (IQR 3) points. Upper limb functional outcomes, which were assessed using the QuickDASH questionnaire, also improved significantly, with the median score decreasing from 25 at baseline to 10 after the intervention, representing a median improvement of –14.3 points. All improvements were statistically significant (P < .001) (Table 4).

Among the 39 digits initially classified as grade I according to Green’s classification, 21 digits (53.8%) achieved successful outcomes after intervention, defined as complete resolution of pain during both rest and movement, or minimal residual pain without functional limitation. For digits initially graded as II or III, 27 out of 48 digits (56.3%) were considered successful, which was characterized by a reduction in pain compared with baseline and an improvement of at least one grade on Green’s classification following intervention.

Discussion

In this study, the mean age of patients was 51.76 years (range, 24–70 years), with the 50- to 59-year-old age group accounting for the largest proportion (43%). These findings are consistent with previous reports on TF. Teo et al4 noted that most patients were middle aged, with the highest prevalence between 50 and 60 years. Similarly, Pataradool and Lertmahandpueti10 reported a mean age of 59 years. In Vietnam, Đào Xuân Thành et al11 also found a comparable mean age of 51.8 years, with the majority of cases occurring between 50 and 70 years.

Several studies have also identified an association between TF and occupations requiring repetitive grasping or hand tool use.12,13 In our study, most patients were manual laborers (65.8%), and those performing repetitive hand or finger movements accounted for 73.42%, which is consistent with prior reports. Đào Xuân Thành et al and Huy et al14 both reported that approximately 70% of TF patients were manual workers. Likewise, Teo et al4 observed a predominance of housewives and manual laborers (65.7%), suggesting that repetitive mechanical strain or chronic microtrauma may underlie disease onset. Furthermore, a study among construction workers showed that frequent gripping (>30 times/day) increased both TF risk and severity nearly sevenfold compared with those performing <10 grips/day.15

Pain during movement decreased by a median of four points on the NPRS, whereas pain at rest showed a minimal reduction from 1 to 0 points. The improvement in movement-related pain is consistent with previous studies evaluating PIP joint orthoses, with or without adjunctive exercise, which have reported clinically meaningful reductions in pain scores over similar follow-up periods.4,9,10 Differences in the magnitude of pain reduction across studies may relate to variations in baseline severity, splint-wearing schedules, and the use of adjunctive treatments such as NSAIDs.

Postintervention analysis revealed a statistically significant reduction in pain during both rest and movement. The median NPRS score at rest decreased from 1 to 0, whereas pain during movement decreased from 6 to 1, with a median improvement of –4 points after 6 weeks.

Compared with the study by Pataradool and Lertmahandpueti, our results were similarly favorable, with a median pain reduction of –4 versus a mean reduction from 5.3 to 1.9 (–3.4) in the cohort from the study by Pataradool and Lertmahandpueti. Studies of Valdes16 and Gil et al17 achieved clinically meaningful improvement, as over 50% of patients achieved a ≥3-point reduction, exceeding the minimal clinically important difference proposed by Copay et al.16, 17, 18

Although Pataradool and Lertmahandpueti used a visual analog scale (VAS) and we used NPRS, the two scales are considered comparable for musculoskeletal pain assessment; however, specific validation for TF remains limited.19,20 Nadar reported a decrease in NPRS from 5.18 to 2.32 (mean –2.9) after 6 weeks, with sustained benefit at 3 months. Our greater pain reduction may be attributable to differences in splinting time, sample inclusion (our study included grade I cases), and NSAID use.

A change of ≥8 points on the QuickDASH is considered clinically meaningful.21 In our study, the median QuickDASH score decreased significantly from 25 to 10 after 6 weeks (median improvement, –14.3 points), reflecting substantial improvement in daily functional ability. Over 60% of patients achieved a clinically significant improvement.

This improvement is comparable to findings from Teo et al, Pataradool and Lertmahandpueti, and Nadar, who reported QuickDASH improvements of 8.81, 29, and 17.2 points, respectively, all exceeding the threshold for clinical relevance. Variations in improvement magnitude across studies may relate to differences in inclusion criteria, splinting duration, adjunctive therapy, and patient adherence.22 Nevertheless, all findings consistently support the role of PIP joint splinting for at least 6 weeks as an effective conservative intervention for Green grades I–III TF.

Valdes16 also demonstrated sustained outcomes after 10 weeks of MCP joint splinting, with 87% of patients showing lasting symptom relief without further intervention. In contrast, Drijkoningen et al23 reported a 55% complete recovery rate after 6 weeks of nighttime splinting, which is comparable to our findings.

Differences in success rates across studies may relate to splint wear duration and definition of treatment success. Our patients wore splints for an average of 11.1 hours/day over 6 weeks, shorter than the 10-week full-time protocol (87% success) of Valdes or 6-week protocol (92.9% success) of Colbourn et al.5 Furthermore, some studies defined success based solely on patient satisfaction or lack of further intervention, whereas ours required objective Green grade improvement.16, 17, 18

Collectively, our findings indicate that PIP joint splinting combined with therapeutic exercise produces significant clinical and functional improvement, with an average success rate of 55.2% after 6 weeks, which is consistent with the midrange of prior reports. The therapeutic mechanism likely involves reducing tendon gliding and A1 pulley friction, allowing tendon rest and recovery while maintaining joint mobility through concurrent exercise.9,10,24 These findings support PIP joint splinting as a valuable noninvasive conservative treatment for TF and provide a basis for future studies with longer follow-up and controlled comparisons.

However, the interpretation of these findings should consider several important limitations. First, all patients received the same combined intervention without a control or comparison group. Therefore, it is not possible to determine the relative contribution of the PIP joint orthosis, therapeutic exercise, or nonspecific factors such as patient education or natural recovery. Second, cross-study comparisons are inherently limited by differences in study design, patient populations, inclusion criteria, and outcome definitions. Third, the follow-up period was limited to 6 weeks, and longer-term outcomes remain unclear.

Taken together, these results suggest that a combined approach using PIP joint orthosis and therapeutic exercise may be a feasible noninvasive option associated with short-term clinical and functional improvement in patients with Green grades I–III TF. Future studies with comparative designs and longer follow-up are needed to better define the role of this intervention relative to other conservative treatment strategies.
