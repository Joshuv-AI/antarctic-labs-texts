Redemption Score: An Evaluation Framework to Rank Image Captions While Redeeming Image Semantics and Language Pragmatics
Ashim Dahal, Ankit GhimireSaydul Akbar MuradNick RahimiSchool of Computing Sciences and Computer EngineeringThe University of Southern Mississippi, Hattiesburg, MS, USA{ashim.dahal, ankit.ghimire, saydulakbar.murad, nick.rahimi}@usm.edu

Abstract
Evaluating image captions requires cohesive assessment of both visual semantics and language pragmatics, which is often not entirely captured by most metrics. We introduce Redemption Score, a novel hybrid framework that ranks image captions by triangulating three complementary signals: (1) Mutual Information Divergence (MID) for global image-text distributional alignment, (2) DINO-based perceptual similarity of cycle-generated images for visual grounding, and (3) BERTScore for contextual text similarity against human references. A calibrated fusion of these signals allows Redemption Score to offer a more holistic assessment. On the Flickr8k benchmark, Redemption Score achieves a Kendall- τ \tau of 56.43, outperforming twelve prior methods and demonstrating superior correlation with human judgments without requiring task-specific training. Our framework provides a more robust and nuanced evaluation by effectively redeeming image semantics and linguistic interpretability indicated by strong transfer of knowledge in the Conceptual Captions and MS COCO datasets.

Introduction

It is most commonly acknowledged that a picture is worth a thousand words, but the pragmatics of image captioning focus on finding the most plausible set of 10-20 words that does the best justification on describing its complexities. This precipitates a unique problem on linguistics deeply rooted to subjectivity. It dictates that two highly plausible captions grounded to the same image may lead to drastic degradation of traditional scoring metrics like BLEU

)

)

Recent advancements on vision-grounded NLP, shared embedding spaces, and strong multimodal systems have dawned new possibilities for vision-grounded language, alongside a proliferation of evaluation metrics to rank image captions. These metrics can be grouped into three broad families:

•

Surface-overlap scores
(BLEU-4
(
Papineni et al. 2002
)
, METEOR
(
Banerjee and Lavie 2005
)
, CIDEr
(
Vedantam et al. 2015
)
and SPICE
(
Anderson et al. 2016
)
): emphasize
n
-gram or scene-graph agreement yet can penalise legitimate paraphrases.

•

Embedding-based or cross-modal measures
(BERTScore
(
Zhang et al. 2019
)
, CLIPScore
(
Hessel et al. 2021
)
and Gaussian-assumed MID
(
Kim et al. 2022
)
): exploit pretrained representations but inherit modality biases and often struggle with fine-grained grounding.

•

Cycle-consistent approaches
(CAMScore)
(
Cui et al. 2025
)
: regenerate an image from the caption and compare it perceptually; sensitive to text-to-image artifacts and rely on a single synthetic perspective.

While each family illuminates part of the quality spectrum, none yields a fully reliable view of factual detail, linguistic nuance, and multi-reference visual grounding.

We introduce a hybrid evaluation framework that combines three complementary signals: (i) global image–text distributional alignment, (ii) perceptual image similarity obtained via cycle generation, and (iii) contextual text similarity against human references. By fusing these perspectives, our metric captures errors that any single signal alone would have otherwise been susceptible to miss. In summary, the key contributions of this paper are listed below:

•

Formulation of a
triangulated
caption–image metric that integrates distributional, perceptual, and linguistic cues within a single scoring function.

•

An efficient calibration procedure that selects fusion weights to maximize Kendall–
\tau
(c) correlation with human judgements with SOTA results.

•

Transfer of metric knowledge from one dataset to another indicating the robust nature of Redemption Score’s capacity to understand image-text relationships.

The formal definition of each component and the fusion rule are detailed in Section
3
.

Related Work

A short summary of current literature is presented on
Table

1
. In order to lighten the dense information, we present its key ideas in the following subsections:

2.1
Historical Metrics

These metrics were based on surface overlap and are best suited and designed to evaluate machine translation and text summarization and hold no information regarding the "image" component of image captioning. Metrics like BLEU, METEOR, CIDEr and SPICE
(
Papineni et al. 2002
;
Banerjee and Lavie 2005
;
Vedantam et al. 2015
;
Anderson et al. 2016
)
fall under this category. The limitations for these metrics include but not limited to penalize re-phrasings and paraphrasing, optimization toward metric idiosyncrasies and lack of visual grounding.

2.2
Embedding and Cross-Modal Metrics

These are the second generation metrics based on multimodal or unimodal embeddings shift and similarities. Unimodal text-only variants include BERTScore, whereas multi-modal variants like CLIPScore, ViLBERTScore, UMIC, MID, and Polos
(
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Hessel et al. 2021
;
Lee et al. 2020
;
Lee et al. 2021
;
Wada et al. 2024
)
metric share a common latent space for texts and images. Although these metrics improve semantic awareness, they still cease to resist biases inherited from encoder, fine-grained object attributes and inherit a persistent modality gap in which spatial relation and numeracy are encoded differently even in the shared vision and language channels.

2.3
Cycle Consistent and Judge Models

These are the most recent works in vision grounded NLP metrics which attempt to circumvent the modality gap in its entirety. Metrics like CAMScore
(
Cui et al. 2025
)
regenerates an image from candidate caption via a text-to-image (T2I) model and compares the synthetic image to original using perceptual and object-level criteria. Likewise, VLM/LLM-as-a-Judge
Chen et al. 2024
frameworks employ the LLM foundation to assess caption quality directly through scalar scoring or pairwise preference. Although these metrics achieve the highest known correlation with human judgements on long and detailed captions, cycle consistency inherits the biases and failures of T2I models, while the LLM-as-a-Judge models introduce heavy computational cost and calibration drift.

2.4
Research Gap and Our Work

Across the three diverse group of metrics, two key gaps are persistent: (i)
inclusive coverage
: no single metric captures global image to text alignment, local visual grounding and linguistic fidelity jointly and (ii)
robustness
: metric often inherit systematic biases from their underlying models and reference corpora leading to misleading ranking in exception cases. To bridge these two gaps in current metrics, we propose Redemption Score; a hybrid triangulated metric that fuses distributional alignment (MID), with perceptual agreement (self-DIstillation with NO labels (DINO) Similarity
(
Caron et al. 2021
)
on cycle-generated images), and contextual text similarity (BERTScore).

3.1
Mutual Information Divergence (MID)

We adapt the Mutual Information Divergence (MID) from
Kim et al. 2022
to capture both image semantics and language pragmatics. Let
g(I)
and
h(\hat{c})
be the
\ell_{2}
-normalized CLIP ViT-L/14
Radford et al. 2021
embeddings of an image
I
and a candidate caption
\hat{c}
. Assuming these embeddings (random variables
X
for images,
Y
for captions) follow multivariate Gaussian distributions with means
\mu_{x},\mu_{y}
and covariances
\Sigma_{x},\Sigma_{y}
, the continuous mutual information is:

I(X;Y)=\tfrac{1}{2}\log\frac{\det(\Sigma_{x})\det(\Sigma_{y})}{\det(\Sigma_{xy})}

(1)

where
\Sigma_{xy}
is the joint covariance.

Kim et al. 2022
define a point-wise mutual information (PMI) for individual pairs
(x,y)
, which adjusts
I(X;Y)
using Mahalanobis distances to reflect how well the pair aligns with the learned distributions. We redefine the PMI normalization on
eq.

9
. The final MID score is the expectation of these PMI values:

\operatorname{MID}(I,\hat{c})=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim p}[\operatorname{PMI}(x,y)]

(2)

The necessary distributional statistics
(\mu_{x},\Sigma_{x},\mu_{y},\Sigma_{y},\Sigma_{xy})
are estimated once from the entire dataset (details in §
4.1
).

3.2
DINO Perceptual Similarity

The perceptual branch leverages the self-supervised

DINO
vision transformer
(
Caron et al. 2021
)
. This reinforces the redemption of image semantics into the Redemption Score.
For every example we consider the original image
I
, its human reference caption
c^{\text{ref}}
, and the model-generated candidate caption
\hat{c}
. Using the public Stable Diffusion 3 checkpoint
(
Rombach et al. 2021
)
, we synthesize two proxy images
\tilde{I}_{\text{ref}}
and
\tilde{I}_{\text{cand}}
.
All images are resized to
224\times 224
and
ImageNet-normalised before encoding.

Feature extraction.

A frozen
ViT-B/8 DINO
encoder

E(\cdot)\in\mathbb{R}^{768}
produces a
[CLS]
embedding
for every image. Embeddings are
\ell_{2}
-normalised:

\hat{e}(X)=E(X)/\lVert E(X)\rVert_{2}
.

Cosine similarities.

We compute two edge scores

\displaystyle s_{1}

\displaystyle=\bigl\langle\hat{e}(I),\,\hat{e}(\tilde{I}_{\mathrm{cand}})\bigr\rangle,

(3)

\displaystyle s_{2}

\displaystyle=\bigl\langle\hat{e}(\tilde{I}_{\mathrm{cand}}),\,\hat{e}(\tilde{I}_{\mathrm{ref}})\bigr\rangle.

(4)

Aggregate perceptual score.

The final Redemption Score receives the mean of these two scores.

\mathrm{DINO_{sim}}\;=\;\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(s_{1}+s_{2}\bigr).

(5)

3.3
BERTScore

We adopt BERTScore
(
Zhang et al. 2019
)
with the
microsoft/deberta-v3-large encoder to account for language pragmatics on the Redemption Score. For each reference caption
c^{\mathrm{ref}}_{i}
we compute:

\displaystyle P_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\hat{c}|}\!\sum_{t\in\hat{c}}\max_{s\in c^{\mathrm{ref}}_{i}}\cos\!\bigl(\mathbf{e}(t),\mathbf{e}(s)\bigr),

(6)

\displaystyle R_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|c^{\mathrm{ref}}_{i}|}\!\sum_{s\in c^{\mathrm{ref}}_{i}}\max_{t\in\hat{c}}\cos\!\bigl(\mathbf{e}(s),\mathbf{e}(t)\bigr),

(7)

where
\mathbf{e}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the contextual embedding
of token
x
produced by the DeBERTa-v3 encoder,
t
ranges over
tokens in the candidate caption
\hat{c}
, and
s
ranges over tokens
in the reference caption
c^{\mathrm{ref}}_{i}
and define the score as the best
F_{1}
over all references:

\mathrm{BERTScore}(\hat{c},R)\;=\;\max_{i}\,\frac{2P_{i}R_{i}}{P_{i}+R_{i}}.

(8)

3.4
Redemption Score Aggregation

Normalization of MID.

First we redefine the PMI on MID Score’s paper
(
Kim et al. 2022
)
to bring it to the range
PMI\in\mathbb{R}^{[0,1]}
:

PMI=\frac{\frac{PMI}{1+|PMI|}+1}{2}

(9)

This normalizes the MID score in
eq.

2
to also be in the range
MID\in\mathbb{R}^{[0,1]}
.

Aggregation.

The final
Redemption Score (RS)
sums the normalized signals
with learnable weights
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
:

\displaystyle RS(I,\hat{c};R)=\alpha\cdot MID+\beta\cdot DINO_{sim}

\displaystyle+\gamma\cdot BERTScore

(10)

where
\alpha+\beta+\gamma=1,
and
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\geq 0
and
R
is the reference caption.

Weight calibration.

The triple
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
is obtained by an exhaustive
grid-search on the simplex
(
\Delta=0.02
step)
that maximizes Kendall–
\tau
correlation with human ratings;
the optimal values are reported in
fig.

2(a)
.
During inference the weights are kept fixed and no additional learning is
performed.

Efficiency Considerations

The largest bottleneck on calculation of Redemption Score lies in the image generation segment for calculation of perceptual similarity using DINO. Stable Diffusion-3 requires 48GiB of VRAM to generate 16 images.

However, with fair assumption that most benchmarking tools are oftentimes used on larger clusters of HPC, we assume this phase wouldn’t be a hindrance for most research labs.

5
Conclusion and Future Works

Conclusion.

In this paper we propose Redemption Score, a scoring metric or evaluation framework to evaluate image captioning multimodal systems. We propose a parameterized metric that maximizes kendall-
\tau
on the Flickr8k dataset and generalizes well on unseen data. Redemption Score triangulates language pragmatics, image semantics and distributional significance of samples to provide a score which closely aligns with human evaluations. Mainly, the Redemption Score: (i) visualizes reference and candidate captions for cyclic comparison against input image, (ii) takes into consideration the contextualized language pragmatics of reference and candidate captions and (iii) emphasizes the gaussian distribution inclusion of candidate caption in context of overall dataset in a multimodal fashion.

Future Works.

As we move forward to further solidifying Redemption Score as a robust metric for multimodal system, our key goals would revolve around four key areas: (i)
Temporal Grounding:
expand Redemption Score from image-text to video-text domain, (ii)
Multilingual Extension:
expand the BERTScore, and Stable Diffusion models on different cultural domains, (iii)
Adaptive
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
selection
: replace grid search with a learnable network that predicts optimal weights for each dataset domain and (iv)
Lightweight Compute
: explore options to distill DINO and MID models and reduce number of images to be generated.

Limitations

Dependence on T2I Models.

Although current SOTA, in rare occurrence when Stable diffusion hallucinates or fails to capture the concept in a caption, the DINO similarity score would get heavily penalized.

Computational Cost.

The compute cost of generating images initially is high. However, once generated, the images can be used for multiple runs via the cached directory.

Bias Inheritance.

CLIP and DINO are subject to some known biases, especially on minority identities and subtle properties. This could lead to some bias inheritance on Redemption Score. However, BERTScore would, to the least, try to contextually focus on the language pragmatics with less bias than the other two components.

Language Coverage.

Currently, we have only tested Redemption Score on English language. The robustness transfer to other commonly spoken language is yet to be studied.
