Psychometric Properties of the General Decision‐Making Style (GDMS‐20) Questionnaire in a Lebanese Arab Adult Sample
Authors: Rebecca Rahme, Ziad Azar, Rabih Hallit, Diana Malaeb, Mohamad El Haj, Fouad Sakr, Mariam Dabbous, Sami El Khatib, Sahar Obeid, Feten Fekih‐Romdhane, Souheil Hallit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDecision‐making styles reflect stable individual tendencies in approaching choices and have been found to relate to health behaviors, clinical decisions, and psychosocial outcomes. The General Decision‐Making Style (GDMS) questionnaire is a widely used instrument that assesses five distinct decision‐making styles. This scale has been translated into many languages but not in Arabic, and has demonstrated robust psychometric properties across multiple cultural contexts. To address the absence of a validated tool for assessing general decision‐making styles in Arabic‐speaking populations, this study aimed to evaluate the validity, reliability and factor structure of the 20‐item version of the GDMS questionnaire.MethodsThis cross‐sectional study conducted between the months of November and December 2024 included 536 Lebanese Arabic speaking participants.ResultsConfirmatory factor analysis results supported the original five factor structure. Internal reliability was satisfactory as evidenced by both McDonald's omega and Cronbach's alpha: Systematic (ω = 0.76/α = 0.76), Intuitive (ω = 0.58/α = 0.58), Dependent (ω = 0.72/α = 0.72), Avoidant (ω = 0.71/α = 0.70) and Spontaneous (ω = 0.67/α = 0.66). Measurement invariance across sex was confirmed, with significant higher mean dependent decision making found in females compared to males whereas higher mean spontaneous decision‐making style was found in males compared to females. No significant difference was found between males and females in terms of systematic, intuitive and avoidant decision‐making styles. Higher systematic, dependent and intuitive styles were significantly associated with better work engagement and wellbeing.ConclusionThis study provides a reliable and valid Arabic version of the 20‐item GDMS for assessing different general decision‐making styles in Arabic‐speaking populations. The scale has potential applications in several targeted applications in healthcare and research.Keywords: decision‐making styles, GDMS questionnaire, Lebanon, reliability, validity, well‐being, work engagement

Introduction

Decision‐making is a process requiring the evaluation of alternative options and selecting a course of action (Jianmin 2024). It is considered a series of thoughts studied by an individual, a group or even an organization to acquire a certain conclusion regarding the roadmap needed to follow for a goal‐oriented objective achievement, while keeping in mind limitations that may be faced (Schoemaker and Russo 2018). This process will sometimes be repeated, highlighting the problem and the essential information to keep in mind before concluding and learning from experience (Schoemaker and Russo 2018). Moreover, decision‐making is an integral part of psychology, which impacts behavior in clinical, educational, organizational, and social settings (Clark et al. 2024). Individual differences in styles of decision‐making have been found to impact problem‐solving, risk‐taking behavior, mental health, and follow‐up of medical and life decisions (Wei et al. 2024). The importance of decision‐making lies in its key role in defining the path to be taken, efficiently utilizing resources, solving problems, and achieving desired goals, while also emphasizing accountability and adaptability. Decision making has been proven to play a critical role in shaping well‐being (Sarmiento et al. 2024; Sari 2022). This association is emphasized by both stress (Sarmiento et al. 2024) and work engagement (Bakker et al. 2012) through their effects on, learning, clinical skills and overall well‐being (Michailidis and Banks 2016). Given its importance and impact on people's lives and wellbeing, decision making has been the subject of studies, and several instruments have been developed to measure the construct.

Measures of Decision Making

In order to assess decision making many scales have been used in the literature. Some examples include the 25‐item (Scott and Bruce 1995) and then the 20‐item (Kolar and Tušak 2022) General Decision Making Style (GDMS) Questionnaire task, the 22‐item Melbourne Decision Making Questionnaire (MDMQ) (Mann et al. 1997), the 45‐item Decision Making Style Inventory (DMI) (Loo 2000), the 87‐item Adult Decision‐Making Competence (A‐DMC) (Bruine de Bruin et al. 2007) and the 43‐items Decision Styles Questionnaire (DSQ) (Leykin and DeRubeis 2010). Many of these scales have several limitations. For instance, the A‐DMC (Bruine de Bruin et al. 2007) measures decision making competence via cognitive task and focuses on normative decision and not individual style preference, additionally being composed of 87 items make it time consuming and not suitable for surveys. The DMI (Loo 2000) covers only three styles (Analytical, intuitive and regret) which may limit its efficacy since it may miss other styles. The DSQ (Leykin and DeRubeis 2010) composed of 43 items evaluates multiple decision styles but due to its length, it is less validated compared to other scales and is more complex to use in surveys. Finally, the MDMQ (Mann et al. 1997) emphasizes mainly maladaptive styles which limit coverage of adaptive and spontaneous styles and also assess the decision‐making response to conflict and not general decision‐making tendencies.

One of the most used measures in the literature is the General Decision‐Making Style (GDMS) questionnaire that has been used to classify decision making into five styles based on the usual way a person approaches this task (Aluja et al. 2024). According to the GDMS questionnaire, there are five distinct styles: systematic, intuitive, dependent, avoidant, and spontaneous (Scott and Bruce 1995). A systematic decision‐making style is considered when the person makes well planned decisions after viewing all facts and options to obtain the best outcome (Scott and Bruce 1995). An intuitive decision‐making style is when a person makes decisions based on their emotions, their intuition, and on their instinct (Scott and Bruce 1995). A dependent decision‐making style relies on other people's advice and insight, where the person is insecure about making decisions based on their sole judgment (Scott and Bruce 1995). An avoidant decision‐making style is used when a person feels distressed making important decisions, so they try to avoid making them as much as possible and only make one when pressured to do so (Scott and Bruce 1995). And finally, a spontaneous decision‐making style is when a person makes decisions without thinking hard or stressing on future outcomes due to rash decisions (Scott and Bruce 1995).

The GDMS is preferred in that unlike other decision‐making instruments, it represents five well‐acknowledged styles: Rational, Intuitive, Dependent, Avoidant, and Spontaneous. Secondly, it is concise (25 items in total) and practical for large questionnaires. Thirdly, its validity has been widely acknowledged in various adult and cross‐cultural populations. Finally, it captures adaptive and non‐adaptive decision‐making preferences. Thus, this study will tackle the GDMS questionnaire since it is a user‐friendly, easily administered, brief, reliable tool that helps assess different decision‐making styles and combinations of styles leading to distinct decision behaviors and patterns and is especially pertinent in health related research and practice since its five styles closely align with treatment adherence and health behaviors (Scott and Bruce 1995).

The GDMS questionnaire was originally created in the English language as a 25‐item questionnaire (Scott and Bruce 1995). Then, another adaptation consisted of 20 items by removing redundant and weak items, and to preserve the structural balance across decision making styles one item per style was removed in the end, while retaining the original five‐factor structure, thus reflecting the same psychometric properties as the original study, and making it more concise and user friendly (Kolar and Tušak 2022). All the items should be answered by either 1, 2 or 3 by the participant. After that, participants are required to add up the numbers in each case to have a total. The case with the highest total will represent which of the five decision‐making styles the participant uses. In light of establishing the structural validity, the GDMS questionnaire has also established a broad applicability by validating and translating the 25‐item GDMS to French (Girard et al. 2016), Chinese (Ding et al. 2020), Slovak (Bavoľár and Orosová 2015), Italian (Gambetti et al. 2008), and Swedish (Thunholm 2004), the 24‐item GDMS in Norwegian (Giske et al. 2013), the 22‐item GDMS in both Spanish (Alacreu‐Crespo et al. 2019) and Russian (Kornilova 2024), the 20‐item GDMS in Slovenian (Kolar and Tušak 2022) and finally a reduced 13‐item GDMS in German (Fischer et al. 2015).

The Present Study

Middle Eastern populations are predisposed to different cognitive loads in comparison with Western population. This is due to many factors like the difference in thinking styles, different cultures, variation in education, linguistic styles, and many more (Hofstede 2001). All of which have a major influence on general decision‐making of each individual (Hofstede 2001). Despite Arabic being the main official national language in 22 countries in the Middle East and North Africa, there is no current Arabic version of the GDMS questionnaire in the literature. Most past studies that evaluated general decision making depended on instruments developed and validated in Western populations. However, these instruments cannot be used in Arabic‐speaking communities due to cultural and contextual differences. Being widely used in research and practical contexts, a validated Arabic‐language format of such a scale in Arabic countries and especially Lebanon does not exist, yet where decision making can be influenced in a meaningful way by culture and social environments. Lebanon provides a distinct sociocultural setting with collectivistic social values, a robust role of family, hierarchical interpersonal relationships (Soubra et al. 2024; Akl 2007), and persistent social stress, which can all influence styles of decision‐making. Additionally, long‐term economic instability, political unpredictability, and frequent social crises have also led to resource constraints and chronic stress, which are known to impair decision‐making (Starcke and Brand 2012; Cherfane et al. 2024). The lack of an existing, validated Arabic tool prevents a thorough study of decision‐making in this population and blocks comparisons based on international information. Therefore, it is important to have an Arabic version compared to using a different version in another language that may alter the cognition process (foreign language effect) (Andrade 2023), taking into account that cultural changes have a major impact on decision making (Yates and de Oliveira 2016). Accordingly, the present study sought to answer the following research questions: (1) Does the Arabic version of the GDMS‐20 replicate the originally proposed five factor structure among Lebanese adults? (2) Does the Arabic GDMS‐20 show satisfactory internal consistency and construct validity? (3) Does the Arabic GDMS‐20 demonstrate measurement invariance across relevant sociodemographic groups, such as sex? (4) Are the GDMS‐20 decision‐making styles significantly associated with work engagement and overall wellbeing, supporting the scale's concurrent validity?

Thus, the objectives of this study are to translate and validate the GDMS questionnaire in Arabic, to assess the validity, the reliability and the factor structure so that it can be used in future practice and research. We hypothesize that the Arabic version of the GDMS will demonstrate good validity, high internal reliability and strong concurrent validity with measures of work engagement and overall wellbeing.

Methodology

Study Design and Participants

The cross‐sectional study was conducted between November 19 and December 13, 2024 (immediately after the war), with a total of 536 participants enrolled using the snowball sampling from multiple Lebanese governorates. Individuals were invited to participate via an online, anonymous, self‐administered questionnaire created on Google Forms. The study focused on Arabic‐speaking adults aged 18 years and older, who were recruited using diverse platforms such as social media networks, medical forums, and academic communities. Participation was voluntary, and no incentives were provided. All participants were briefed about the study's aims and guaranteed confidentiality before providing informed consent. The questionnaire contained a comprehensive description of the study purpose, confidentiality assurances, and the consent form. Eligibility criteria included Arabic‐speaking adults aged 18 years or older, capable of completing the survey in Arabic, and willing to provide informed consent.

Minimal Sample Size Calculation

A minimal sample size of 400 patients was calculated, based on the rule of 20 participants per item (Mundfrom et al. 2005); since the scale was composed of 20 items, at least 400 patients were needed. This criterion was met with a final sample in the current study of 536 participants.

Measures

Data were collected through an online, anonymous, self‐administered questionnaire, which was made available in Arabic. The questionnaire was organized into two sections. The first section collected basic sociodemographic data including age, sex, marital status and level of education.

The General Decision‐Making Style (GDMS) Questionnaire

The GDMS Questionnaire consists of 20 statements designed to assess different decision‐making styles. It encompasses five subscales, evaluating each of the different decision‐making styles: rational, intuitive, dependent, avoidant and spontaneous. Participants assign to each statement one of the following numbers: 1 (This statement is false), 2 (This statement is sometimes true. It depends on the situation), or 3 (This statement is true). Then the participant is asked to fill in the assigned numbers for each of the five tables named A to E, with each table containing 4 statements. Afterward, the summation of the numbers on each table should be done. The table with the greatest sum will indicate the person's dominant decision‐making style (Kolar and Tušak 2022). The GDMS‐20 was tested in a sample of 80 managers of Slovenian sports organizations where it has demonstrated Cronbach's alpha coefficient of 0.686 for the overall scale and between 0.572 (DMS Spontaneous) and 0.814 (DSM Avoidant) for the five subscales (Kolar and Tušak 2022).

The forward‐backward translation method has been implied to translate the GDMS questionnaire. The English version of the questionnaire was translated by an independent Lebanese translator that was not affiliated to the study. Then the Arabic version was back translated to English by a Lebanese psychologist with high English proficiency. The translation team reconciled any literal or context‐specific translations. For an accurate translation, a group of experts consisting of the research team, a psychologist, a psychiatrist, and the two translators compared both the original English version to the new translated Arabic version adjusting all differences. A pilot study was conducted on 30 participants to ensure the clarity of all questions.

The Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (UWES‐9)

The UWES‐9 is a tool used to measure the three core dimensions of work engagement: Vigor (e.g., At my job, I feel strong and vigorous), Dedication (e.g., I am enthusiastic about my job), and Absorption (e.g., I am immersed in my work) (Balducci et al. 2010). For every dimension, three items are assigned making a total of nine items. Responses are then evaluated on a seven‐point frequency scale going from 0 (never), 1 (almost never), 2 (rarely), 3 (sometimes), four (often), 5 (very often), and 6 (always). Greater work engagement is characterized by higher scores. The Cronbach's α value in the current study was 0.95. The UWES‐9 has demonstrated satisfactory reliability and factorial validity across different cultures and occupational contexts. In its original study involving 14,521 employees from 10 countries, the UWES‐9 demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency (α = 0.80) (Schaufeli et al. 2006). In Portuguese and Brazilian workers, alpha value was 0.96 for the total scale (Sinval et al. 2017). Total scale α coefficients of 0.94 in Brazilian workers (Vazquez et al. 2015), 0.92 in Japanese engineers and nurses (Shimazu et al. 2008), 0.88 in Chinese workers (Fong and Ng 2012), 0.82 in Indian workers (Chaudhary et al. 2012) and 0.76 in Nepalese nurses (Panthee et al. 2014) further support its reliability across cultures and occupational groups.

Statistical Analysis

We used data from the total sample to conduct a CFA using the SPSS AMOS version 28 software. Therefore, we assumed a minimum sample of 400 participants needed to have enough statistical power based on a ratio of 20 participants per one item of the scale, which was exceeded in our sample. Parameter estimates were obtained using the maximum likelihood method. Fit indices were the normed model chi‐square (χ2/df), the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), the Tucker‐Lewis Index (TLI), and the Comparative Fit Index (CFI). Values ≤ 5 for χ2/df, and ≤ 0.08 for RMSEA, and 0.90 for CFI and TLI indicate good fit of the model to the data (Byrne 2013). Multivariate normality was not verified at first; therefore, we performed non‐parametric bootstrapping procedure.

To examine sex invariance of the scale, we conducted multi‐group CFA using the total sample (Chen 2007). Measurement invariance was assessed at the configural, metric, and scalar levels (Chen 2007). We accepted ΔCFI ≤ 0.010 and ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015 or ΔSRMR ≤ 0.010 as evidence of invariance (Vadenberg and Lance 2000). Comparison between genders was done using the Mann‐Whitney test.

Composite reliability in both subsamples was assessed using McDonald's ω and Cronbach's α. Values greater than 0.70 reflecting adequate composite reliability. To assess criterion‐related validity, we examined bivariate correlations between the decision‐making scales' scores and work engagement using the Spearman correlation test. Based on Cohen (1992) (Cohen 1988), values ≤ 0.10 were considered weak, ∼ 0.30 were considered moderate, and ∼ 0.50 were considered strong correlations.

Discussion

The current cross‐sectional study involving 536 participants aimed to evaluate the psychometric properties of the Arabic version of the 20‐item GDMS questionnaire. Our results demonstrated satisfactory properties including high internal reliability, adequate construct validity and strong convergent validity with work engagement and overall wellbeing.

Consistent with the analysis used in the original English version, our analysis demonstrated a five‐factor structure representing each of the five distinct decision‐making styles: Systematic, Intuitive, Dependent, Avoidant, and Spontaneous. This five‐factor structure was also observed and confirmed by the Slovenian (Kolar and Tušak 2022) for the 20‐item GDMS and many other versions in Russian (Kornilova 2024), Swedish (Thunholm 2004), Italian (Gambetti et al. 2008), Spanish (Alacreu‐Crespo et al. 2019) for the 25 item GDMS. This implicates that the present study's replication of the 20‐item General Decision‐Making Style questionnaire's original five‐factor structure, offers strong evidence of the Arabic version's construct validity in a Lebanese sample.

In our study, acceptable reliability coefficients were obtained for all five types of decision making, as evidenced by both McDonald's omega and Cronbach's alpha: Systematic (ω = 0.76/α = 0.76), Intuitive (ω = 0.58/α = 0.58), Dependent (ω = 0.72/α = 0.72), Avoidant (ω = 0.71/α = 0.70) and Spontaneous (ω = 0.67/α = 0.66), indicating acceptable internal reliability which was similar to the Slovenian 20‐item GDMS adaptation showed equivalent internal reliability and consistency: Rational (α = 0.74), Intuitive (α = 0.775), Dependent (α = 0.705), Avoidant (α = 0.814), Spontaneous (α = 0.609) but McDonald's omega was not reported in this adaptation. The internal consistency coefficients observed in the current validation study is similar to other GDMS validations in the literature. In fact, in the original scale development, highest internal consistency was observed for the Systemic style with an α = 0.87, and Intuitive and spontaneous styles showing lower reliability with α = 0.62 for both styles (Scott and Bruce 1995). Moreover, this structure has been replicated across multiple other adaptations with Systematic style typically falling within the 0.70–0.85 range, and values for Intuitive and Spontaneous styles frequently ranging between 0.55 and 0.70 (Bavoľár and Orosová 2015; Spicer and Sadler‐Smith 2005). These minor reductions observed in internal consistency compared to other validation studies may be due to cultural and linguistic factors rather than weaknesses in the scale. Finally, by demonstrating acceptable internal validity compared to previous validation studies and a replicated factor structure similarly to other studies in the literature, the present study supports the construct validity and robustness of this scale in this population.

The present study is among the few that considered studying invariance across sex (Alacreu‐Crespo et al. 2019; Kornilova 2024). This psychometric property is a fundamental step, since in the absence of sex invariance, observed differences in score may be attributable to measurement bias or differential item functioning (Vadenberg and Lance 2000). Therefore, demonstrating sex invariance improve construct validity by assuring unbiased and interpretation of group differences (Vadenberg and Lance 2000). For instance, the Slovenian 20‐item adaptation did not test for sex invariance, since their sample only consisted of 80 managers (Kolar and Tušak 2022). This is noteworthy considering different sexes may have different decision‐making styles. When comparing our results between men and women, a significant difference in Systematic, Dependent and Spontaneous decision‐making styles were noted. As a matter of fact, cultural and linguistic differences may explain result variations between studies since decision making styles differ with different cultural norms, variations in individualism, collectivism, and uncertainty avoidance (Dabić et al. 2015). Indeed, Middle Eastern and Lebanese cultures are considered relatively collectivist, stressed by the role of social contexts and the role of family and social considerations in the decision‐making process (Hofstede 2001). Additionally, sex role may also affect decision making style due to socially oriented decision making for man and woman in the Middle East (Baiocco et al. 2009).

In the current study, as previously discussed, decision‐making styles were significantly associated with better work engagement and wellbeing. Participants with Systematic, Intuitive, Avoidant, and Dependent decision‐making styles reported higher levels of work engagement, while the spontaneous decision‐making style reported lower levels of work engagement. These results are consistent with previous studies stating that effective decision‐making is associated with better stress management and well‐being (Santhosh and Vimala 2025). In fact, a study conducted by Flores‐Buils and Mateu‐Pérez (Flores‐Buils and Mateu‐Pérez 2025) that training students and professionals in developing structured decision making skills, helped them manage stress, adapt to challenges and improve their overall mental health. Additionally, it was proven that proactive decision‐making, including planning, is markedly valuable for stress management (Nwaogu and Chan 2022).

Clinical Implications

The Arabic version of the 20‐item GDMS showed good psychometric properties, including satisfactory internal validity in its five domains. Practically, knowledge of patients preferred decision‐making styles could improve the quality of decision‐making especially in a communal society like the Middle Eastern society where family input may affect treatment related decisions. This may be accomplished by enabling healthcare professionals to shape their communication and practices according to their patients' preferences. Moreover, the GDMS has several targeted applications in healthcare and research. For instance, in mental health clinics, this scale may help practitioners to guide and inform therapeutic interventions which is achievable by identifying dysfunctional decision‐making preferences, such as those linked to symptoms of anxiety, indecisiveness, or avoidance. Furthermore, for managing chronic diseases, the GDMS can help in improving treatment compliance, promoting better self‐management practices, and supporting successful lifestyle modifications by ensuring that healthcare services are appropriately matched to the patient's individual cognitive and behavioral preferences. Finally, the Arabic 20‐item GDMS will help researchers make feasible cross‐cultural comparisons regarding decision‐making practices in various clinical and non‐clinical situations in Arabic‐speaking populations.

Limitations

This study presents some limitations that should be acknowledged. Its cross‐sectional design prevents the assessment of temporal stability and test‐retest reliability. Furthermore, although the overall psychometric performance of the Arabic GDMS was acceptable, the reliability values for the Intuitive and Spontaneous decision‐making styles fell below the suggested thresholds for reliability indices (Cronbach's Alpha and McDonald's Omega). For this reason, any interpretation of these two values should be cautious due to the fact that this finding is consistent with previous validation studies, indicating reduced reliability values for both these styles. Additionally, being limited to only Lebanese responders may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other Arabic‐speaking countries, where participants could have different decision‐making style due to cultural variations and different social norms. Moreover, using an online form to collect data lead to a form of selection bias by having a female predominance, along with a majority of responders being single and university students. This pattern may restrict the generalizability of our findings to the Lebanese population due to the unrepresentativeness of the sample. Therefore, the Arabic version of the GDMS questionnaire is highly recommended to be validated in other geographical contexts. Future studies should examine the psychometric properties of the Arabic GDMS‐20 in other Arabic‐speaking countries. Longitudinal studies are also needed to assess test‐retest reliability and the temporal stability of the GDMS‐20. Further research should investigate the sources of the lower reliability of the Intuitive and Spontaneous subscales and determine whether item refinement or culturally adapted wording could improve their measurement without altering the original constructs.

Conclusion

Our study supports the reliability and validity of the Arabic version of the GDMS‐20 questionnaire. This scale plays an important role since it contributes to the international movement toward standardizing the evaluation of the process of decision‐making, by facilitating the application of the GDMS questionnaire among people who speak the Arabic language. Increased awareness of one's decision‐making styles can contribute toward more efficient management of challenges faced by an individual. This could be achieved by identifying the strengths and weaknesses of the styles the individual displays.
