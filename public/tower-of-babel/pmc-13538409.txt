Effects of drought stress and Ca supply on photosynthesis, fluorescence parameters and growth of Glycyrrhiza uralensis in the Hexi Corridor
Authors: Enjun Wang, Pengbin Dong, Na Bai, Hong Gao, Yong Zhang, Zhenzhi Zhao, Xiucun Zeng, Yunchen Zhao, Yajuan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Calcium (Ca) functions as a secondary messenger and contributes to plant drought tolerance. This study examined how Ca supplementation influences photosynthetic performance and growth in Glycyrrhiza uralensis seedlings under drought stress. Drought significantly reduced net photosynthetic rate, stomatal conductance, and transpiration rate. Under severe drought, photosynthesis was primarily constrained by non-stomatal limitations, indicating damage to the photosynthetic apparatus. Ca supplementation partially restored gas exchange and alleviated drought-induced photoinhibition by mitigating the decline in maximum PSII efficiency. Drought also reduced growth and root yield (plant height, basal diameter, crown area, root length), whereas Ca supplementation promoted seedling growth and alleviated drought-related damage, thereby enhancing overall drought adaptability. These findings demonstrate that Ca plays a key role in maintaining photosynthetic function and alleviating photodamage under water-limited conditions. Appropriate Ca supplementation may therefore serve as a practical strategy to improve drought tolerance and support the sustainable cultivation of G. uralensis in arid and semi-arid regions.

Introduction

Drought is a persistent global challenge; in China, approximately half of the national territory is classified as arid or semi-arid1. Drought stress is a major abiotic constraint on plant growth and crop yield in arid and semi-arid regions2,3. Leaf photosynthetic rate is widely used as a physiological indicator of photosynthetic function4. Water deficit typically decreases photosynthetic rate5. Drought reduces photosynthetic capacity through multiple mechanisms, including restricted leaf expansion and diminished photochemical and biochemical activities in chloroplasts6,7. Drought stress reduces leaf area and some chlorophyll fluorescence parameters, inhibits the photosynthetic rate, and forces photosynthetic system II (PSII) to be photodamaged4,8. Wu et al. (2008) reported that drought reduced leaf area and photosynthetic pigment content in Sophora davidii seedlings, lowered photosynthetic efficiency, and ultimately decreased photosynthetic rate9. Similarly, Zhang et al. reported that drought decreased chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll (a + b), and the Chl a/b ratio in Phragmites australis seedlings, resulting in reduced photosynthetic rate3.

In arid and semi-arid environments, water is the primary constraint on plant growth; nevertheless, water also serves as the medium and solvent for many physiological and biochemical processes, whereas nutrients provide the material basis for growth and productivity10. As drought intensifies, nutrient-poor soils in arid ecosystems are likely to experience greater nutrient losses. Because water availability is difficult to modify in these systems, fertilization is often considered a practical strategy to increase the availability of limiting soil nutrients, and it has received increasing attention9. Previous studies have shown that moderate drought stress increases stomatal density, while severe drought reduces stomatal density. Further research found that GTL (GT-2like1) in the plant trihelix transcription family can regulate stomatal movement by regulating the expression of SDD1 (stomatal density and sistribution 1) gene11. SDD1 encodes a subtilisin-like protease and changes stomatal density, transpiration rate and water use efficiency through a negative regulatory mechanism12. When PtaGTL1 was transferred into Arabidopsis thaliana, under water-deficient conditions, GTL increased the expression of SDD1 gene by binding to Ca²⁺-CaM, thereby reducing stomatal density and transpiration rate and thus increasing water use efficiency13. At the same time, the study also found that intracellular calcium ions can regulate water flow through aquaporin (AQP)14. External application of calcium ions can also promote the flow of water between cells. This may be achieved by regulating the activity of AQPs or the expression of genes to increase the flow of water between cells under osmotic stress, thereby reducing water loss from leaves under osmotic stress15. The decrease in chlorophyll content in plants under calcium stress may be due to two reasons. On the one hand, it may be caused by reduced activity of chloroplast pigment synthase, which hinders chloroplast synthesis. On the other hand, it may also be caused by calcium ions disrupting chloroplast function, accelerating chlorophyll decomposition, and damaging chloroplast morphology and structure, thus reducing the content of chlorophyll in plant leaves and affecting the photosynthetic characteristics of plants, leading to a decrease in pn16.

Glycyrrhiza uralensis Fisch. is a species of the genus Glycyrrhiza (Fabaceae) and is widely used in traditional Chinese medicine because of its therapeutic properties17. Its dried roots and rhizomes are commonly used to treat bronchitis, ague, hepatitis, phthisis, and gastric ulcers18. In addition to its medicinal value, G. uralensis provides ecological services such as windbreak, sand fixation, and soil stabilization. Accordingly, it is a suitable species for vegetation restoration and ecological construction in western China19. Wild G. uralensis resources are abundant in the Hexi Corridor and occur mainly in the arid desert steppe at 860–1500 m above mean sea level (AMSL). The species exhibits tolerance to cold, heat, drought, sand burial, high irradiance, and saline–alkali conditions, making it an important (and in some settings irreplaceable) component of desert and arid ecosystems in northwest China20. However, long-term, large-scale harvesting and excavation have degraded G. uralensis stands and depleted natural resources, thereby accelerating desertification in the region21. In recent years, cultivation trials have been conducted to establish production bases, which could both meet market demand and reduce pressure on wild populations while supporting ecological conservation22. The Hexi Corridor is characterized by an arid climate, and annual precipitation is < 200 mm in many areas23. Consequently, water availability is the dominant environmental factor limiting crop growth and development. To enable large-scale cultivation of G. uralensis in this region, it is necessary to clarify its drought tolerance and the mechanisms underlying drought alleviation. Most studies have focused on drought tolerance mechanisms and plant-water relations in G. uralensis20, whereas comparatively few have examined how exogenous calcium influences growth and physiological responses under drought stress.

Accordingly, controlled experiments are needed to evaluate the two key constraints on plant growth in arid and semi-arid regions: water and nutrient availability. Specifically, under drought stress, can exogenous calcium supply alleviate the inhibition of photosynthesis in licorice by increasing stomatal conductance and maintaining the maximum photochemical efficiency (Fv/Fm) of photosystem II (PSII)? Secondly, does exogenous calcium supply improve licorice growth under drought stress in a concentration-dependent manner, with moderate calcium concentrations showing the best promoting effect on aboveground biomass accumulation, while excessively high or low calcium concentrations lead to insignificant effects? Therefore, this study systematically investigated the effects of calcium supply under drought stress on the photosynthetic characteristics, chlorophyll fluorescence parameters, and growth of licorice (G. uralensis), exploring the mechanism by which calcium supply alleviates drought-induced growth limitations, thereby testing the two hypotheses mentioned above. The aim is to provide a scientific basis for the artificial cultivation of licorice in arid and semi-arid regions and support cultivation as a strategy to reduce dependence on wild populations, thus contributing to the protection of wild resources. Furthermore, understanding drought mitigation mechanisms is of great significance for producing high-quality cultivable materials and accelerating the vegetation restoration of licorice in western China.

Materials and methods

Overview of the study area

The study was conducted at Hexi University in Zhangye City, Gansu Province, China (37°28′–39°57′ N, 97°20′–102°12′ E; 1439–1496 m above mean sea level, AMSL). The region has a continental climate with mean annual precipitation of 113–120 mm. Mean annual evaporation is 2000–3500 mm, and the mean annual temperature is 7 °C. The frost period lasts 138–179 days per year, and the annual sunshine duration is approximately 3106 h. The site location is shown in Fig. 1.

Experimental design

One-year-old Glycyrrhiza uralensis seedlings of uniform size (height of about 55 cm) were selected from the Hexi Corridor Medicinal Plant Germplasm Nursery (Zhangye City) and transplanted on 23 April 2022, with five seedlings per plastic pot (22 cm diameter × 20 cm height). Pots were filled with a substrate of sand, local topsoil, and humus (1:2:1, v/v/v). Watering and Ca treatments began when seedlings reached ~ 20 cm in height. All pots were placed under a mobile rain shelter to exclude natural rainfall. Treatments were applied from 17 June to 10 July 2022 (24 d) using a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with five replicates in a factorial arrangement of three watering regimes-control (watered every 2 d), mild drought (7 d), and severe drought (14 d)-and two Ca levels: Ca0 (no Ca) and Ca1 (Ca added). On day 0 (17 June 2022), Ca1 pots received 1,000 mL of 10 mmol L− 1 CaCl₂ solution plus 1,000 mL water per pot, whereas Ca0 pots received 2,000 mL water per pot. Thereafter, irrigation followed the assigned interval; drought treatments were irrigated at 7- or 14-day intervals with 2,000 mL per pot per irrigation. Physiological variables were measured on day 14 after treatment initiation. After measurements, the drought treatment was terminated and all pots were rewatered following the control schedule. All experiments were conducted on a per-pot basis. All seedlings were harvested on 10 July 2022. Treatment schedules and harvest timing are summarized in Table 1.

Gas exchange parameters measurement

On day 14, net photosynthetic rate (Pn), stomatal conductance (gs), transpiration rate (E), intercellular CO₂ concentration (Ci), and relevant environmental variables were measured in each treatment group. Measurements were taken on fully expanded, mature leaves from three plants per treatment using a CRISE-2 portable photosynthesis system at 07:00, 09:00, 11:00, 13:00, 15:00, 17:00, and 19:00 on a single day. Pn-PAR response curves were determined at PAR levels of 2000, 1500, 1000, 500, 200, 100, 50, 20, and 0 µmol m− 2 s− 1 under uniform chamber conditions (30 °C and 345–365 ppm CO₂) in full sunlight. Light compensation point (LCP), maximum net photosynthetic rate (Pmax), light saturation point (LSP), and dark respiration rate (RD) were calculated following Zhang et al.24. Each measurement was replicated three times. Diurnal changes in photosynthetically active radiation (PAR; bars) and air temperature (line) from 07:00 to 19:00 on 11 July 2022 at Hexi University are shown in Fig. 2.

Growth measurements

On day 24 of the experiment, we measured plant height, stem diameter, crown area, and root length of Glycyrrhiza uralensis. Plant height was measured from the soil surface to the highest point of the plant with leaves naturally extended, using a ruler. Stem diameter was measured at the stem base using a vernier caliper. Crown area was determined from measurements taken along two perpendicular canopy axes using a measuring tape. Root length was measured from the root tip to the stem base using a measuring tape. Each treatment included five replicates.

Statistical analyses

All experiments followed a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three or five replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data were analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (two-way ANOVA) to assess the main effects of treatments A and B and their interaction. When the interaction effect was significant, a simple effects analysis was performed, and the least significant difference (LSD) method was used to compare the mean differences between treatment combinations. If the interaction effect was not significant, multiple LSD comparisons were performed between the levels of the main effects of each factor to determine the significance of the differences between treatments25.

Results

Effects of Ca supply on gas exchange parameters under drought stress

Drought stress altered diurnal fluctuation patterns of several physiological parameters in G. uralensis (Fig. 3). Net photosynthetic rate (Pn) exhibited a unimodal diurnal pattern, but peak timing differed among treatments: CK and 7dCa0 peaked at 10:00, whereas the remaining treatments peaked at 13:00 (Fig. 3a). Pn declined significantly (p < 0.05) as drought intensity increased. Under the same water regime, Ca supplementation (7dCa1 and 14dCa1) increased Pn relative to the corresponding non-supplemented treatments (7dCa0 and 14dCa0) (Fig. 3a). Stomatal conductance (gs) generally decreased under drought, except in 14dCa0, where gs remained low and nearly constant throughout the day; the minimum gs occurred in 14dCa0 (Fig. 3b). Compared with 14dCa0, Ca supplementation (14dCa1) increased gs under severe drought (Fig. 3b). In contrast to Pn and gs, intercellular CO₂ concentration (Ci) decreased during the morning and then increased after 11:00 in all treatments except severe drought (14dCa0) (Fig. 3c). In 14dCa0, Ci increased sharply from 07:00 to 11:00, reached a minimum at 13:00, and then increased again until 19:00. Transpiration rate (E) decreased significantly under drought, with the lowest values under severe drought (14dCa0) (Fig. 3d). Under severe drought, Ca supplementation (14dCa1) significantly increased E relative to 14dCa0 (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3d).

Effects of Ca supply on Pn-PAR response curves under drought stress

Drought stress and Ca supply also influenced the Pn-PAR response curves of G. uralensis (Fig. 4). Pn decreased markedly as drought intensity increased, but increased with Ca supplementation at PAR ≥ 500 umol photons m− 2 s− 1. Under the same drought level, Pn was higher in Ca-supplemented treatments (7dCa1 and 14dCa1) than in the corresponding non-supplemented treatments (7dCa0 and 14dCa0). Drought stress and Ca supply both significantly affected the light-response parameters of G. uralensis leaves (p < 0.01). Specifically, Pmax decreased with increasing drought (7dCa0 and 14dCa0) but increased with Ca supplementation under the corresponding water regimes (7dCa1 and 14dCa1) (Table 2). Drought increased LCP and RD (7dCa0 and 14dCa0), whereas Ca supplementation decreased these parameters (7dCa1 and 14dCa1) (Table 2). In contrast, LSP showed the opposite trend: drought reduced LSP, while Ca supplementation increased LSP (Table 2).

Effect of Ca supply on chlorophyll fluorescence of G. uralensis under drought stress

Drought stress and Ca supply significantly affected chlorophyll fluorescence parameters in G. uralensis leaves (p < 0.05; Fig. 5). F0 increased significantly as drought intensity increased (7dCa0 and 14dCa0), with the highest F0 observed under severe drought (14dCa0) (Fig. 5a). Ca supplementation had a significant effect only under severe drought (14dCa1) (p < 0.05). Fm, Fv/Fm, and ΦPSII showed similar diurnal patterns (Figs. 5b-d), decreasing initially and reaching minima between 09:00 and 13:00 across treatments. Relative to the control, these parameters were lower under mild drought (7dCa0), whereas under severe drought (14dCa0) all three decreased significantly (p < 0.05). Under the same drought level, Ca supplementation (7dCa1 and 14dCa1) increased Fm, Fv/Fm, and ΦPSII compared with the corresponding non-supplemented treatments. Notably, under severe drought, Ca supplementation increased Fm, Fv/Fm, and ΦPSII by 8.36%, 10.49%, and 12.77%, respectively (Figs. 5b-d).

Effects of Ca supply on the growth of G. uralensis seedlings under drought stress

Stem diameter, plant height, and crown area of G. uralensis under drought (7dCa0 and 14dCa0) were significantly lower than in the control (Table 3). In contrast, root length was higher under mild drought (7dCa0) than in the control. Under severe drought (14dCa0), growth indices were the lowest. Specifically, compared with the control, severe drought reduced stem diameter, plant height, and crown area by 20%, 8%, and 34%, respectively (Table 3). Ca supplementation (7dCa1 and 14dCa1) increased stem diameter, plant height, crown area, and root length under the corresponding water regimes (7dCa0 and 14dCa0). Relative to mild drought without Ca (7dCa0), 7dCa1 increased these traits by 2%, 11%, 2%, and 4%, respectively. Under severe drought, 14dCa1 increased them by 5%, 0.7%, 33%, and 10%, respectively, compared with 14dCa0 (Table 3).

Drought stress and Ca supply also altered the morphological characteristics of G. uralensis (Fig. 6). Under normal water supply (control), plants exhibited dark green leaves and a well-developed root system, with continuous production of new roots. The root system was characterized by robust taproots and abundant lateral roots (Fig. 6). After 7 days of drought (7dCa0), leaves remained mostly dark green, although some showed slight curling. However, leaf number and lateral root development decreased significantly compared with the control. With Ca supplementation under the same water regime (7dCa1), leaves were greener and more numerous than in the non-supplemented treatment (7dCa0). After 14 days of drought without Ca (14dCa0), leaves turned yellow; many lateral roots withered; the main root was stunted; and lateral roots were sparse or absent (Fig. 6). With Ca supplementation under severe drought (14dCa1), leaf withering was reduced and lateral roots remained visible (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Plants in natural environments are continuously exposed to multiple abiotic stresses, including excess light, temperature extremes, and drought26. Among these, drought is a major constraint on plant growth and yield27. Drought commonly reduces stomatal conductance (gs), thereby limiting CO₂ uptake and decreasing net photosynthetic rate (Pn), transpiration (E), and photosystem reaction-center activity28, ultimately suppressing growth and potentially causing plant death29. Previous studies have shown that the photosynthetic rate and transpiration rate of licorice leaves were highest when the relative soil moisture content was 65%. In the two treatments with a relative soil moisture content higher than 65% and the drought treatment with a relative soil moisture content of 50%, the photosynthetic rate and transpiration rate of licorice leaves decreased, but the decrease was not significant. Only when the relative soil moisture content was 35% under severe drought stress, the net photosynthetic rate and transpiration rate of licorice were significantly lower than those of other treatments, but the decrease in water use efficiency (WUE) was not significant, indicating that licorice can maintain a relatively stable water use efficiency even when photosynthesis is inhibited. Midgley et al. believed that this physiological response could prolong the growing season and defend against fatal drought stress30,31. Calcium (Ca) has been reported to enhance Rubisco activity under abiotic stress, which is associated with higher leaf photosynthetic rates32. Consistent with this, Pn, gs, and E in G. uralensis leaves declined significantly as drought intensity increased (Figs. 3a, b, d), with the strongest reductions under severe drought (14dCa0), suggesting impaired photosynthetic capacity and possible photoinhibition. Unlike Pn, gs, and E, intercellular CO₂ concentration (Ci) followed a distinct diurnal pattern (Fig. 3c). In all treatments except 14dCa0, Ci decreased sharply before 09:00 and then increased gradually until 19:00. Under severe drought (14dCa0), Ci increased from 07:00 to 09:00, decreased thereafter, and rose again from 13:00 to 19:00; Ci was also significantly higher than in the other treatments (Fig. 3c). Elevated Ci implies reduced carboxylation efficiency, indicating that non-stomatal limitations predominated in G. uralensis seedlings under severe drought. Ca is a key regulator of photosynthesis, influencing chlorophyll fluorescence, photosynthetic electron transport, and stomatal movement, among other processes27. In our experiment, Ca supplementation mitigated drought-induced declines in Pn, gs, and E (Figs. 3a, b, d), indicating that Ca can attenuate reductions in photosynthetic performance and alleviate drought-related physiological damage in G. uralensis, consistent with reports in Malus baccata33 and Handeliodendron bodinieri34.Pn-PAR response curves describe the relationship between net photosynthetic rate (Pn) and illumination intensity and are widely used to assess plant photosynthetic capacity35. In this study, a modified hyperbolic model was fitted to the light-response curves to estimate key parameters, including Pmax, RD, LSP, and LCP, which helps elucidate photosynthetic responses of leaves under different treatments. Pmax represents the maximum photosynthetic capacity of leaves36. LCP and LSP are key indicators of plant light-demand characteristics: under unfavorable environmental conditions, LSP and light-saturated photosynthetic rate often decline, whereas LCP increases20. Our Pn-PAR curves (Fig. 4) showed that Pmax and LSP decreased as drought intensity increased in G. uralensis, while LCP exhibited an increasing trend (Table 2). Pmax and LSP were highest in the control and lowest under severe drought (14dCa0), whereas LCP showed the opposite pattern (Table 2). Together, these changes indicate that drought-particularly severe drought (14dCa0)-reduces the photosynthetic capacity of G. uralensis leaves, narrows the range of usable light intensities, and weakens acclimation to the light environment. Under the same water conditions, Ca supplementation (7dCa1 and 14dCa1) increased Pmax and LSP and decreased LCP, suggesting that Ca widens the light-adaptation range and enhances potential photosynthetic capacity under drought stress. RD reflects plant respiration in darkness36. RD generally increases under drought as stress intensifies; however, under corresponding water regimes, Ca supplementation reduced RD in G. uralensis leaves (Table 2), indicating reduced nighttime carbon loss and improved overall photosynthetic performance.

Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters are widely used to evaluate stress-induced damage27. F0 represents the minimum fluorescence yield when PSII reaction centers are fully open37. An increase in F0 suggests irreversible damage or reversible inactivation of PSII reaction centers38. In this experiment, F0 increased significantly (p < 0.05) in non-supplemented drought treatments (7dCa0 and 14dCa0) and rose further with increasing drought intensity (Fig. 5a). Notably, F0 in the severe drought treatment (14dCa0) was 35.85% higher than the control (CK). These results indicate that increasing drought stress compromises PSII reaction-center stability in G. uralensis leaves4, leading to PSII damage or partial inactivation. Under the same water regime, Ca supplementation (7dCa1 and 14dCa1) significantly reduced F0 (Fig. 5a), confirming that drought increased F0, whereas Ca supplementation suppressed this increase. Fm reflects the status of PSII electron transport20, and ΦPSII represents the effective quantum yield of PSII; higher ΦPSII indicates greater light-energy conversion efficiency39. Fv/Fm, an early indicator of stress tolerance, is closely associated with the maximum quantum efficiency of PSII40 and is typically > 0.8 under non-stress conditions but declines under stress20. Consistent with these expectations, increasing drought stress (7dCa0 and 14dCa0) caused Fm, Fv/Fm, and ΦPSII to decline sharply (Figs. 5b-d), while F0 increased significantly. This pattern indicates that water deficit damaged PSII, inhibited photochemical processes, reduced photosynthetic performance, and ultimately suppressed plant growth. In contrast, under drought stress, Ca supplementation (7dCa1 and 14dCa1) resulted in higher Fm, Fv/Fm, and ΦPSII and lower F0, suggesting that Ca helps maintain PSII openness and electron transport and alleviates drought-induced photoinhibition, thereby improving photosynthetic capacity and drought tolerance in G. uralensis. Similar findings have been reported for H. bodinieri seedlings34.

Drought is one of the most important constraints on plant growth and productivity9. It causes significant changes in growth, chlorophyll content, leaf water status, and photosynthetic traits4. Zhang et al. reported that drought significantly reduced stem diameter, total leaf area, and total dry weight in lily4. Our previous work also showed that drought stress not only reduced growth indices (e.g., plant height, stem diameter, and crown area) but also decreased dry matter accumulation in different organs of G. uralensis20. Previous studies have shown that Ca supply can enhance stress tolerance by regulating physiological responses28. In the present study, drought treatments without Ca (7dCa0 and 14dCa0) reduced key growth traits of G. uralensis, including plant height, stem diameter, crown area, and root length (Table 3), and also significantly altered plant morphology (Fig. 6). As drought intensified, leaves gradually turned yellow-green and then yellow; leaf margins curled, followed by wilting and eventual desiccation. In addition, the taproot became stunted and the number of lateral roots declined, leading to root-system desiccation (Fig. 6). These results indicate that drought stress inhibits the growth of G. uralensis in the Hexi Corridor and induces pronounced morphological symptoms. Under the same water regime, Ca supplementation (7dCa1 and 14dCa1) alleviated drought-induced growth inhibition and reduced morphological damage (Fig. 6). This is also consistent with the findings of previous studies: (1) drought inhibits cell elongation and division, leading to reduced plant height, leaf area, and stem diameter; (2) drought accelerates chlorophyll degradation, decreasing leaf chlorophyll content and causing leaf yellowing; (3) drought reduces leaf relative water content (RWC), disrupting leaf water balance; (4) drought induces stomatal closure, thereby lowering the net photosynthetic rate41.

In conclusion, our hypothesis that Ca supplementation improves the drought adaptability of G. uralensis seedlings was partially supported. Ca supplementation promoted growth under drought and mitigated adverse morphological effects, thereby enhancing drought adaptability. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Ca supplementation alleviates drought-induced physiological damage in G. uralensis primarily by maintaining PSII photochemical efficiency, stabilizing photosynthetic electron transport, expanding the light-adaptation range, and reducing nocturnal carbon loss, thereby sustaining higher overall photosynthetic capacity and supporting biomass accumulation under water-limited conditions. Given that non-stomatal limitations predominated under severe drought, the protective role of Ca on carboxylation efficiency and photochemical apparatus appeared particularly critical. Therefore, appropriate Ca supplementation is recommended for G. uralensis seedlings in the Hexi Corridor as a practical strategy to enhance photosynthetic performance and mitigate growth inhibition under drought stress. Future studies should explore the molecular mechanisms underlying Ca-mediated photoprotection and the optimal Ca application rates under field conditions to facilitate the integration of this approach into sustainable cultivation practices for this medicinally important species.
