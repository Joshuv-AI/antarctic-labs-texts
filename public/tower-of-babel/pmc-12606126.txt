A CPEB2/ANGPTL3 feedback loop promotes the progression of podocyte injury in nephrotic syndrome
Authors: Xin Zheng, Shuibao Chen, Yizhong Chen, Yuling Wu, Fan Lin, Zihua Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPodocyte injury is the primary pathology responsible for 70–90% of nephrotic syndrome (NS) in children, a condition closely associated with the aberrant expression of Angiopoietin-like-3 (ANGPTL3). The regulatory mechanism by which ANGPTL3 is upregulated during NS progression remains elusive. In this study, we found that ANGPTL3 can be regulated by Cytoplasmic polyadenylation element binding protein 2 (CPEB2) through translational control, and conversely, CPEB2 can be transcriptionally regulated in response to ANGPTL3 signaling as a feedback loop. Exposure to puromycin aminonucleoside (PAN) or adriamycin (ADR) dose-dependently increased the expression of both CPEB2 and ANGPTL3 in MPC-5 cells. Ectopic overexpression of CPEB2 in MPC-5 cells significantly promoted cell apoptosis and migration, which could be abrogated by knockdown of ANGPTL3. Mechanistically, the regulation of ANGPTL3 by CPEB2 was attributed to translational promotion. CPEB2 could directly bind the 3′ untranslated coding region (UTR) of ANGPTL3 mRNA, thereby promoting the recruitment of ANGPTL3 transcripts to the translation initiation complexes. Consequently, polysomal profiling suggested that the abundance of ANGPTL3 transcripts in actively high-translating polysomes was dramatically increased upon CPEB2 overexpression. On the other hand, ANGPTL3 signaling could increase the mRNA stability of CPEB2, thereby promoting the mRNA expression of CPEB2. Furthermore, the role of CPEB2/ANGPTL3 in podocyte injury was validated in vivo. Depletion of CPEB2 with adeno-associated virus (AAV9)-shCPEB2 significantly decreased the expression of ANGPTL3, and consequently mitigated albuminuria and histopathological injury in ADR-induced NS mice. In conclusion, this study highlighted the CPEB2/ANGPTL3 feedback loop as a novel mechanism contributing to the progression of podocyte injury.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23393-1.Keywords: Nephrotic syndrome, Podocyte injury, CPEB2, ANGPTL3Subject terms: Kidney diseases, Molecular biology

Introduction

Nephrotic syndrome (NS) is a common chronic glomerular disease in children, characterized by massive proteinuria, hypoalbuminemia, systemic edema, and hyperlipidemia1,2. The incidence of NS is about 1.15–16.9 per 100, 000 children, varying by ethnicity and region3. Based on hormone sensitivity, NS could be divided into steroid-sensitive nephrotic syndrome (SSNS) and steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS), accounting for 80–90% and 10–20% of pediatric NS respectively4. Despite ongoing relapses, outcomes of the patients with SSNS remain good, with risk of progression to chronic kidney disease estimated to be less than 5% at 10 years after diagnosis5. In contrast, SRNS is particularly challenging due to its prolonged treatment course and difficulty in management. Approximately 30–40% of the children with SRNS may progress to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) after 10 years, which is a challenging issue in clinical practice6,7. Minimal change disease (MCD) is the main pathology accounting for up to 70–90% of NS in children, which is closely related to loss or altered function of the podocytes (podocytopathy), resulting in impaired glomerular filtration barrier and massive proteinuria8. Therefore. it is imperative to investigate the in-depth molecular mechanisms involved in the progression of podocyte injury and to identify novel therapeutic targets against NS.

Angiopoietin-like-3 (ANGPTL3) is a secretory protein belonging to the angiopoietin-like protein family, which has been shown to play a significant role in the progression of podocyte injury. For instance, ectopic overexpression of ANGPTL3 could upregulate the expression of Nephrin, thereby promoting the motility of podocytes9. In addition, ANGPTL3 also participated in podocyte detachment and apoptosis, leading to loss of podocytes through the activation of and Integrin α3β1/p53 signaling10. Nevertheless, there is still an important scientific question that urgently needs to be clarified. The above studies have suggested that aberrant expression of ANGPTL3 might be indeed involved in the progression of podocyte injury through various pathways. However, the regulatory mechanism of ANGPTL3 expression still remains unclear. Under normal physiological conditions, ANGPTL3 is mainly synthesized and secreted by hepatocytes, while only a small amount of ANGPTL3 is expressed in the kidney11,12. Under pathological conditions, however, it raised our great interest to address how renal ANGPTL3 expression is induced in NS patients.

RNA binding proteins (RBPs) are a class of proteins that can interact with specific RNA sequences to regulate transcription, splicing, stability, transport, and translation. Accumulating evidence has suggested that RBPs may be implicated in the development of various kidney diseases, including tubulointerstitial fibrosis13, acute kidney injury14, polycystic kidney disease15, diabetic kidney disease16 and glomerulonephritis17. Cytoplasmic polyadenylation element binding protein 2 (CPEB2), is a sequence-specific RNA-binding protein that belongs to the cytoplasmic polyadenylation element binding protein (CPEB) family. The CPEB family members could bind the cytoplasmic polyadenylation element (UUUUUAU or UUUAAU) to regulate mRNA stability and translation18. The role of CPEB2 in the development of NS and podocyte injury remains elusive. In this study, we found that ectopic overexpression of CPEB2 may enhance the expression of ANGPTL3 in podocytes. Conversely, activation of ANGPTL3 signaling can also induce the expression of CPEB2 as a feedback loop. Consequently, our findings revealed the significance of the CPEB2/ANGPTL3 regulatory feedback loop in mediating the progression of podocyte injury, which could potentially serve as a novel therapeutic target to halt the relapses of NS.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatic analysis

Candidate genes potentially contributing to podocyte injury were identified using Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets. Two datasets were utilized: GSE124622, an experimental dataset derived from bulk RNA sequencing of human podocytes, and GSE241302, a clinical dataset obtained through single-cell RNA sequencing of kidney tissue from patients with idiopathic membranous nephropathy (IMN). In the GSE124622 dataset19, an in vitro model of podocyte injury was applied with the human podocytes exposed to either puromycin aminonucleoside (PAN) or adriamycin (ADR) as described. We analyzed both the ADR- and PAN-exposed cells, and re-set the criterion for the identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) as follows: Log2 fold change (FC) > 1, and adjusted p-value (Padj) < 0.05. In the GSE241302 dataset20, renal tissue samples were analyzed from the healthy controls and the patients with idiopathic membranous nephropathy, a predominant pathological type of the adult primary nephrotic syndrome. Eleven kidney cell clusters were identified, and DEGs in the podocytes were re-analyzed using the following criteria: Log2 FC > 0.5, and p-value < 0.05. Overlapping DEGs identified in both GSE241302 and GSE124622 (control vs. ADR), as well as GSE124622 (control vs. PAN), were selected for further analysis and experimental validation.

Cell culture and treatment

Conditionally immortalized mouse podocytes (MPC-5) were purchased from Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences. The MPC-5 cells were grown on collagen-coated culture dishes, cultured in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 µg/ mL streptomycin, and 10 U/mL murine interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) at 33 ℃. In order to induce differentiation, the podocytes were maintained under non-permissive conditions in the absence of IFN-γ at 33 ℃ for 10–14 days. After differentiation, MPC-5 cells were exposed to ADR (0.2, 0.5, 1 µM) or PAN (0.2, 0.5, 1 mM) for 24 h to induce podocyte injury.

Cell transfection

Transient transfections were performed with Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The Cpeb2 mouse expression vector (Cat. No. MR225994L4) and the control vector (Cat. No. PS100093) were purchased from OriGene (Rockville, MD, USA). Cpeb2 Mouse siRNA Oligo Duplex (Cat. No. SR416598), Angptl3 Mouse siRNA Oligo Duplex (Cat. No. SR414829) and Eif3d Mouse siRNA Oligo Duplex (Cat. No. SR417145) were purchased from OriGene (Rockville, MD, USA). Negative scrambled siRNAs or negative scrambled plasmids were used as a control.

Cell viability

Cell viability was determined with Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay kit (Cat. No. C0037, Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were incubated with CCK-8 at 37 ℃ for 30 min. Absorbance was determined using a Multiskan Spectrum (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) at 450 nm.

Cell apoptosis

Apoptosis was quantified by flow cytometry using the FITC-Annexin V apoptosis detection kit (Cat no. C1062S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were incubated with FITC-Annexin V (2 µg/mL) and propidium iodide (2 µg/mL) for 10 min in the dark at 4 ℃. A total of 1 × 104 cells were analyzed for each sample with a FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA).

Transwell migration assay

Transwell cell culture inserts (pore size 8 μm; Costar Corporation, Corning, NY) were coated with a type-I collagen. A total of 1 × 104 cells were seeded in the inserts and allowed to migrate for 24 h. Migrated cells were stained with crystal violet (Sigma–Aldrich). Images were visualized using a Leica DMI6000B fluorescence microscopy, and the number of migrated cells was counted with the ImageJ software (v1.54f).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated using Trizol according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR was conducted on a PIKO96 (Thermo, Germany). The primers utilized in this study are detailed in Table 1. Data were analyzed via the ΔΔCq method, with GAPDH serving as the normalization control.

Western blot analysis

Samples were homogenized and lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (50 mM Tris‑HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% SDS, 1% Nonidet P‑40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate). Following centrifugation at 10,000 g for 10 min, the resulting supernatant was collected. Protein concentration was measured using a BCA protein assay kit (Cat no. P0012S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Aliquots of the lysates (40 µg protein) were subjected onto a NuPAGE Bis/Tris gel (Novex, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.). After transblotting, the membrane was incubated with the indicated primary antibodies at 4 ℃ overnight: CPEB2 (1:2000), ANGPTL3 (1:2000), EIF3D (1:1000), NPHS2 (1:1000) and GAPDH (1:5000). The immunoblots were visualized with a ChemiDoc XRS + system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), and the band density was measured with Image J software (v1.54f).

Immunofluorescence staining

The cells were fixed in 3.7% formaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min, followed by permeabilization with 0.2% Triton-X100 for 10 min. The cells were incubated with the primary antibody against ANGPTL3 (1:500, OriGene) or FITC-labeled phalloidin (1:200, Beyotime) at 4 ℃ overnight. The images were captured using a Leica DMI6000B fluorescence microscope and processed with LAS AF LITE image processing software (Leica, Germany).

mRNA stability assay

MPC-5 cells were treated with Actinomycin D (Act D, at 5 µg/mL) for 0–12 h. The amount of normalized CPEB2 mRNA levels versus time was plotted, based on which the half-time of CPEB2 mRNA transcripts was calculated.

Ribonucleoprotein‑immunoprecipitation (RNP‑IP) assays

Briefly, 1 × 107 cells were harvested and lysed in 500 µL polysome extraction buffer on ice for 30 min. The lysates were incubated with the antibody-conjugated beads at 4 ℃ overnight in the presence of excess IP antibodies against EIF3D (1:100) or EIF3G (1:100). The beads containing the immunoprecipitated RNA-protein complex were then treated with proteinase K to remove the proteins. The RNA was purified by Trizol method and analyzed by qPCR.

Luciferase assays

The chimeric firefly luciferase reporter constructs containing the coding region (CR) and 3′ untranslated region (3’ UTR) were obtained from iGeneBio (Guangzhou, China). Luciferase activity was measured using the Dual Luciferase Assay System (Cat. No. RG027, Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Briefly, cDNA purified from MPC-5 cells was used as a template for PCR amplification of the CR and 3′ UTR of ANGPTL3 transcripts (NM_013913.4). Polysomal profiling

In brief, 2 × 107 cells were exposed to cycloheximide (0.1 mg/mL) for 15 min. The cells were harvested by scraping in 1 mL of polysome extraction buffer and then lysed on ice for 30 min. Following centrifugation (12,000 g, 10 min), the resulting supernatant was fractionated through a linear sucrose gradient ranging from 10% to 45%. Eluted fractions were collected using a fraction collector (Brandel, Gaithersburg, MD), and the mRNA abundance in each fraction were measured through qPCR analysis.

Nascent protein synthesis

Nascent protein synthesis was detected by L-azidohomoalanine (AHA)-labeling Click-iT protein analysis detection kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Cat No. C10102/C10276, Thermo).

NS mouse model

The in vivo NS model was generated using 8-week-old C57BL/6 female mice (Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd.). The mice were maintained under standard conditions (25 ± 0.5 ℃, 12:12 dark: light cycles) with access to food and water ad libitum. All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee for Animal Experiments at Fujian provincial hospital (IACUC-FPH-PZ-20240531[0314]). All methods were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and the ARRIVE guidelines.

Endogenous expression of CPEB2 was depleted using the adeno-associated virus (AAV) containing shCPEB2 plasmids, a pool of 3 target-specific shRNAs (Hanbio Biotechnology, China). The sequences were sh-CPEB2#1 (GTGTTCAGGACAGACAACAAT), sh-CPEB2#2 (GCGAG TCGCTTCTCCAATCA), and sh-CPEB2#3 (GAGTTCCATAAGCCATTGGTG). After 7-day-acclimatization, the mice were randomly separated into four groups (n = 5/group): AAV-scramble/Vehicle group, AAV-scramble/ADR group, AAV-shCPEB2/Vehicle group and AAV-shCPEB2/ADR group. For the ADR treatment, ADR (25 mg/kg, dissolved in saline to a final concentration of 5 mg/mL) was intravenously administrated via tail vein injection on day 0, while the vehicle groups were injected with an equivalent volume of saline. For virus infection, 5 × 1011 AAV virus (50 uL) containing the scramble or shCPEB2 plasmids (Hanbio Biotechnology, China) were injected into the renal pelvis on day 0. After 8 weeks of modeling (on day 56), animals were euthanized via intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium at a dose of 100 mg/kg. Spot urine and blood samples were collected for biochemical tests. Urinary levels of creatinine, albumin and ANGPTL3 were determined using the Creatinine Content Assay Kit (Cat. No. BC4915), Albumin Content Assay Kit (Cat. No. BC5835) and Mouse ANGPTL3 ELISA Kit (Cat. No. SEKM-0202), respectively. Serum levels of albumin and total cholesterols were determined using the Albumin Content Assay Kit (Cat. No. BC5835) and Total Cholesterol Content Assay Kit (Cat. No. BC1985). The kidney tissue samples were stored at -80 ℃ until further analysis.

Histological examination

Kidney tissue samples (0.4 cm × 0.3 cm × 0.3 cm) were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and were processed to paraffin Sect. (5 μm) for periodic acid-schiff (PAS) staining to evaluate the morphological changes. The expression of ANGPTL3 in glomerulus was determined by immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining. In brief, paraffin sections were de-paraffinized and rehydrated, followed by antigen retrieval with 1 mM EDTA (pH = 9) buffer at 95 ℃ for 10 min. The sections were incubated with the primary antibodies against ANGPTL3 (1:200, OriGene) or WT1 (1:200, Santa Cruz) at 4 ℃ overnight. TUNEL staining was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Cat. No. C1089, Beyotime). The images were captured by a digital section scanner (Pannoramic MIDI, Hungary). The severity of podocyte injury was determined using a transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Briefly, fresh kidney tissues (1–3 mm3) were fixed in 1% osmium acid, followed by gradient dehydration in ethanol and acetone. Afterwards, the samples were osmotically embedded, polymerized, ultra-thinly sectioned, and visualized under a TEM (Hitachi, HT7700). The TEM images were taken from 5 to 10 glomeruli randomly selected from each mouse, and were analyzed with the ImageJ software (v1.54f).

Statistical analysis

All results are presented as mean ± SD. Data were statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (San Diego, CA, USA). Variables were analyzed either by Student’s t-test for two groups or ANOVA analysis for multiple groups. Statistical significance was assumed when p < 0.05.

Results

Aberrant expression of CPEB2 in podocyte injury

We analyzed the overlapping DEGs from three GEO datasets. As shown, a total of 37 DEGs were identified in injured podocytes, including 11 down-regulated DEGs and 26 up-regulated DEGs (Fig. 1A and B). The detailed information on these DEGs was summarized in Table 3. We chose to focus on CPEB2, because among these DEGs, it was the only RBP that could regulate the expression of target genes through translation control. We then validated the expression of CPEB2 in MPC-5 cells. As shown, the cell viability decreased significantly by ADR treatment in a dose-dependent manner, concomitant with increased expression of CPEB2 (Fig. 1C and D). Notably, knockdown of CPEB2 with siRNAs significantly abolished the elevated expression of CPEB2 by ADR, and consequently alleviated the podocyte injury (Fig. 1E and F). Consistently, PAN treatment basically mirrored the effect of ADR treatment. As shown, PAN treatment dose-dependently decreased the cell viability, and increased the expression of CPEB2 (Fig. 1G and H). Knockdown of CPEB2 significantly abrogated the elevated expression of CPEB2 by PAN, and consequently alleviated the podocyte injury (Fig. 1I and J).

Ectopic overexpression of CPEB2 induced podocyte injury through ANGPTL3

As shown, ectopic overexpression of CPEB2 significantly reduced the cell viability (Fig. 2A). Interestingly, the immunofluorescence staining revealed a significant increase in ANGPTL3 expression following CPEB2 overexpression (Fig. 2B). Notably, knockdown of ANGPTL3 evidently abolished the effect of CPEB2 on cell viability and ANGPTL3 expression (Fig. 2C and D). Moreover, ectopic overexpression of CPEB2 induced significant apoptosis, which was mitigated by ANGPTL3 knockdown (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, the results of Transwell assay suggested that the migratory capacity of MPC-5 cells was substantially enhanced by ectopic overexpression of CPEB2, which was mitigated by ANGPTL3 knockdown (Fig. 2F). Consistently, F-actin staining suggested that ectopic overexpression of CPEB2 evidently induced the formation of lamellipodia, which was mitigated by ANGPTL3 knockdown (Fig. 2G). Collectively, these results suggested that CPEB2-induced podocyte injury was partially dependent on ANGPTL3.

CPEB2 promoted the translation of ANGPTL3 by interacting with the 3’ UTR of ANGPTL3 mRNA transcripts

No significant changes in the mRNA expression of ANGPTL3 were observed following CPEB2 overexpression (Fig. 3A). However, the polysomal profiling suggested that the transcripts of ANGPTL3 in the control MPC-5 cells (vector) were enriched in low-translating fractions 1–5, while those in CPEB2-overexpressing cells were enriched in actively high-translating fractions 6–10 (Fig. 3B). Consequently, the synthesis of ANGPTL3 protein (AHA-ANGPTL3) increased time-dependently in CPEB2-overexpressing cells (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, the RNP-IP assay indicated that ectopic overexpression of CPEB2 remarkably enhanced the association of ANGPTL3 transcripts with the complexes of protein translation initiation, EIF3D (~ 3.4-fold increase) and EIF3G (~ 2.7-fold increase) (Fig. 3D). The results of western blot demonstrated that CPEB2-induced translation of ANGPTL3 was notably abrogated by EIF3D depletion (Fig. 3E). In order to further validate the association of CPEB2/ANGPTL3 mRNA complex, luciferase assay and pulldown assay were performed with the fragments spanning the ANGPTL3 CR (spanning positions 135–1343) or 3′ UTR (spanning positions 1240–1584). As shown, the luciferase activity of 3′ UTR was increased by about 120% upon CPEB2 overexpression, while no significant changes were observed in CR (Fig. 3F). Consistently, the pulldown assay validated that only the 3’ UTR fragment could bind CPEB2, with no detectable bindings observed in the CR fragment (Fig. 3G). Collectively, these results suggested that CPEB2 could enhance the translation of ANGPTL3 by interacting with the 3’ UTR of ANGPTL3 mRNA transcripts.

The CPEB2/ANGPTL3 feedback loop responding to ADR exposure

The above-mentioned results suggested that ANGPTL3 was a direct target gene of CPEB2. We then hypothesized that CPEB2 might also be regulated by ANGPTL3 signaling as well. As shown, ADR treatment significantly increased the protein levels of both CPEB2 and ANGPTL3 in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4A) and time-dependent manner (Fig. 4B). Interestingly, knockdown of ANGPTL3 with siRNAs significantly abrogated the elevated expression of CPEB2 by ADR (Fig. 4C), suggesting that CPEB2 might be a downstream target gene of ANGPTL3 signaling as well. Consistently, changes in the expression of CPEB2 mRNA levels basically mirrored the changes in protein levels. ADR-induced CPEB2 mRNA expression could be abrogated by knockdown of ANGPTL3 (Fig. 4D). Mechanistically, ANGPTL3 might increase the mRNA expression of CPEB2 by enhancing the mRNA stability of CPEB2 transcripts. No significant differences were found in the half-life of CPEB2 mRNA under untreated conditions. However, ADR treatment significantly extended the half-life of CPEB2 mRNA, which was abrogated by knockdown of ANGPTL3 (Fig. 4E). Taken together, these results suggested that CPEB2/ANGPTL3 might serve as a positive feedback loop responding to ADR exposure.

Knockdown of CPEB2 decreased renal ANGPTL3 expression and alleviated ADR-induced podocyte injury in vivo

In order to further elucidate the role of CPEB2 in the podocyte injury, we generated the AAV-shCPEB2 virus to deplete the endogenous expression of CPEB2. Compared to the vehicle group, the urinary albumin excretion rate was significantly elevated in the ADR group, which was evidently attenuated by CPEB2 knockdown (Fig. 5A). Similarly, ADR-induced loss of serum albumin levels was significantly alleviated by CPEB2 knockdown (Fig. 5B). In addition to serum albumin, serum levels of triglycerides and cholesterols were significantly elevated by ADR treatment, which was remarkedly attenuated by CPEB2 knockdown (Fig. 5C and D). Moreover, the histological examination suggested that the severity of ADR-induced podocytopathy was significantly alleviated by CPEB2 knockdown. The TEM images suggested that CPEB2 knockdown induced less diffuse foot process fusion of podocytes compared to the scramble group (Fig. 5E). Additionally, the PAS staining suggested that CPEB2 knockdown induced less morphological damage (glomerular-capsule adhesions) in the glomeruli, while the IHC staining of ANGPTL3 suggested that CPEB2 knockdown induced less ANGPTL3 expression (Fig. 5F). Moreover, in order to further validate the expression of ANGPTL3 in podocytes, co-staining of WT1 (a specific biomarker for podocytes) and ANGPTL3 was performed. As indicated, ADR treatment substantially induced ANGPTL3 expression in podocytes (yellow arrows), which was significantly mitigated by depletion of CPEB2 (Figure S1). Consistently, the elevated urinary ANGPTL3 excretion rate by ADR treatment was significantly mitigated by CPEB2 knockdown (Fig. 5G). Furthermore, TUNEL staining revealed that ADR treatment substantially induced apoptosis in podocytes (TUNEL+), which was significantly mitigated by depletion of CPEB2 (Figure S2). Consequently, the results of western blot indicated that CPEB2 knockdown significantly restored the loss of NPHS2 (podocin) by ADR, one of the podocyte markers (Fig. 5H). Collectively, these results suggested that knockdown of CPEB2 decreased renal ANGPTL3 expression and alleviated ADR-induced podocyte injury in vivo.

Discussion

Nephrotic syndrome is the most prevalent glomerular disease in children. A meta-analysis of 27 studies reported an average incidence of 2.92 per 100, 000 children, with significant variations based on ethnicity. The incidence was notably higher in southeast Asia and east Asia compared to Europe, North America, and Oceania (6.1 vs. 2.15, 2.4, and 1.4 per 100, 000 children, respectively)21. Most NS patients tend to have relapses throughout childhood. Approximately 35–40% of the patients may have a single episode or one to two relapses, while about 55–60% may experience more frequent relapses22. In China, the cumulative incidence of relapse at 1-, 2-, and 5-year intervals was reported as 51.0%, 62.5%, and 66.6%23. Nephrotic syndrome can be classified into steroid-sensitive or steroid-resistant categories based on the clinical response to steroid therapy. While this classification aids in guiding treatment, it provides limited insight into the disease mechanism. Conversely, pathological changes may offer a better understanding of disease progression. The most common pathological type in childhood NS is MCD. The glomeruli appear normal under light microscopy, while the effacement of foot processes is evident under electron microscopy. The etiology of MCD remains unclear, but podocyte injury and glomerular filtration barrier dysfunction may play a central role.

Podocytes are specialized, polarized epithelial cells with interdigitating foot processes and a unique cell-cell junction known as the slit diaphragm. Together with the glomerular basement membrane and fenestrated glomerular endothelium, podocytes form a trilayered structure known as the glomerular filtration barrier. Any disruption in podocyte structure, including damage to the foot processes or the slit diaphragm, can lead to albumin loss in the ultrafiltrate. Podocytes are particularly vulnerable to injury, because these cells are terminally differentiated cells with limited regenerative ability. The precise mechanisms of podocyte effacement remain unclear, however accumulating evidence suggested that podocyte injury can be triggered by various factors, including genetic mutations24 and immune responses25. Additionally, numerous systemic circulating factors have been recently identified that might induce podocyte apoptosis and increase glomerular permeability, including heparanase, haemopexin and angiopoietin-like 3/4 (ANGPTL3/4).

In this study, we chose to focus on ANGPTL3, a secreted protein with multiple functions. Although the major function of ANGPTL3 is involved in neovascularization and lipid metabolism, recent research has posited that dysregulated ANGPTL3 may contribute to the pathogenesis of various kidney diseases, including acute kidney injury26, diabetic nephropathy27, renal cell carcinoma28 and nephrotic syndrome29. Overall, the mechanism of ANGPTL3-induced progression of NS is associated with the cytoskeletal rearrangement of podocytes. Ectopic overexpression of ANGPTL3 in vitro could enhance the motility of podocytes by inducing actin filament (F-actin) rearrangement via PI3K/PKB signaling, thereby leading to podocyte foot process fusion, a hallmark of podocyte injury30. Moreover, ANGPTL3 may interact with podocyte-expressed integrin β3, and disturb the formation of actin bundles and essential polarity through α-actinin-431. In addition to podocytes, ANGPTL3 can also modulate the barrier function of human glomerular endothelial cells through PI3K/PKB and integrin αvβ3 signaling32. Furthermore, some preclinical studies demonstrated that inhibition of ANPTL3 signaling with neutralizing antibodies, chemical drugs and traditional Chinese medicine targeting ANGPTL3 directly or indirectly may offer therapeutic benefits in experimental NS animal models33,34. These findings collectively suggested that ANGPTL3 may serve as a promising therapeutic target against the progression of podocyte injury. However, it remains unclear how the expression of ANGPTL3 is induced in the context of NS, given that both human and murine ANGPTL3 are predominantly expressed in the liver with minimal expression in other tissues and organs.

In general, numerous studies have demonstrated that ANGPTL3 is mainly regulated by post-translational modifications (e.g, cleavage). ANGPTL3 is primarily cleaved at two sites, Arg221 to Ala222 and Arg224 to Thr225, yielding an N-terminal coiled-coil domain (CCD) and a C-terminal fibrinogen-like domain (FLD). In addition to post-translational, however, the potential for other mechanisms to regulate ANGPTL3 during the progression of NS remains unclear. One of the key findings in this study is the post-transcriptional regulation of ANGPTL3 by CPEB2, a member belongs to the CPEB family. All CPEB members share a similar structure with conserved C-terminal regions composed of two RNA recognition motifs and two zinc finger motifs, and a variable regulatory N-terminal region35. By promoting cytoplasmic polyadenylation, the CPEB family has been shown to play a fundamental role in the translational control of mRNAs driving various biological processes, including germ-cell development, cell division, cellular senescence, synaptic plasticity, and learning and memory36. In this study, we identified 37 overlapping DEGs from podocyte injury-related GEO datasets. Notably, the mRNA levels of ANGPTL3 were unchanged or undetectable within these datasets, implying that post-transcriptional mechanisms may be crucial for ANGPTL3 regulation. We chose to focus on CPEB2 because it was the only RBP that may regulate the expression of target genes through translation control. We then experimentally confirmed that both ADR and PAN treatment can induce CPEB2 expression, and knockdown of CPEB2 can evidently mitigate the severity of podocyte injury. Moreover, we also demonstrated that the effect of CPEB2 on podocytes was dependent on ANGPTL3, and the regulation of ANGPTL3 by CPEB2 was mediated by translational control through interacting with ANGPTL3 3’ UTR. More interestingly, the role of CPEB2/ANGPTL3 might serve as a positive feedback loop during the progression of podocyte injury, since knockdown of ANGPTL3 could suppress the CPEB2 expression as well. The regulatory pattern of CPEB2/ANGPTL3 was similar to Hu et al.’s study37, wherein a p53/CPEB2 negative feedback loop was identified to play a critical role in controlling the proliferation and migration of renal cancer cells. Finally, we validated the in vivo role of CPEB2/ANGPTL3 axis in the podocyte injury by using AAV-shCPEB2 virus in NS mice. Concomitant with mitigated indicators of podocyte injury, elevated serum levels of triglycerides and cholesterols were also significantly remarkedly attenuated by CPEB2 knockdown. Given the pivotal role of ANGPTL3 in the maintenance of lipid homeostasis. We proposed that these changes could be considered a consequence of protection of kidney function and pathology via blockade of CPEB2/ANGPTL3 axis.

A few limitations of this study need to be addressed. First, only one mouse podocyte cell line (MPC-5) was used in this study, and thus it remains unclear if the CPEB2/ANGPTL3 feedback loop may mediate the progression of podocyte injury in human podocytes. Second, more in vivo data are needed to consolidate the fundamental role of the CPEB2/ANGPTL3 feedback loop in experimental NS animal models (e.g., podocyte specific CPEB2−/− or ANGPTL3−/− mice).

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study reported a new role of CPEB2 in mediating the progression of podocyte injury via ANGPTL3 signaling. Ectopic overexpression could promote the translation efficiency of ANGPTL3, while ANGPTL3 could promote the mRNA stability of CPEB2 and thus increase CPEB2 expression. Therefore, the CPEB2/ANGPTL3 regulatory feedback loop may serve as a new therapeutic target to halt the relapses of NS.
