Beyond-Labels: Advancing Open-Vocabulary Segmentation With Vision-Language Models
Muhammad Atta ur Rahman

Abstract
Self-supervised learning can resolve numerous image or linguistic processing problems when effectively trained. This study investigated simple yet efficient methods for adapting previously learned foundation models for open-vocabulary semantic segmentation tasks. Our research proposed ”BeyondLabels,” a lightweight transformer-based fusion module that uses a handful of image segmentation data to fuse frozen image representations with language concepts. This strategy allows the model to successfully actualize enormous knowledge from pre-trained models without requiring extensive retraining, making the model data-efficient and scalable. Furthermore, we efficiently captured positional information in images using Fourier embeddings, thus improving the generalization across various image sizes, addressing one of the key limitations of previous methods. Extensive ablation tests were performed to investigate the important components of our proposed method; when tested against the common benchmark PASCAL-5i, it demonstrated superior performance despite being trained on frozen image and language characteristics.

II-A

Semantic Segmentation of Unseen Categories

Teaching a network to segment new, previously unknown categories remains difficult, as most existing approaches only function with a predefined set of categories, requiring the test and training categories to match. Zero-shot semantic segmentation attempts to address this issue by employing word embeddings (semantic representations of category names) to recognize new categories without further training data. There are two primary approaches:

•

Generative approaches
, such as ZS3Net
[

and CaGNet

•

Discriminative algorithms
, such as SPNet
[
30
]
and LSeg
[
8

, map pixels and category names into a shared embedding space, which is then used to forecast the class of each pixel.

II-B

Fourier Embedding for Spatial Encoding

Recently, Fourier embeddings have become appealing alternatives to traditional position encodings in those tasks that require strong spatial representation. Unlike learned positional embeddings, which will struggle hard to generalize outside the training distribution, Fourier-based embeddings provide a continuous and smooth encoding of spatial locations. Recently, it has been the case that Fourier embeddings turn out to be useful in improving generalization for implicit neural representations. For example, NeRF
[
19
]
proposed using Fourier feature mappings to help the positional encoding for 3D reconstruction tasks. Since then, this idea has been applied to image segmentation, where models benefit from the smoothness and periodicity of Fourier-transformed coordinates. In this context, self-supervised learning for Perceiver IO
[
14
]
uses Fourier embeddings to map high-dimensional inputs to a latent representation, with the best performance across various multimodal tasks. Correspondingly, in semantic segmentation, Fourier network representations
[
26
]
are utilized to encode better spatial dependencies, reducing large labeled datasets. Our approach thus replaces conventional learned embeddings with integrated Fourier embeddings of spatial information for smooth generalization across image domains. This goes in line with previous work showing that continuous and structured position encoding is helpful, both for vision and language models.

III
Methodology

This section begins by defining the open-vocabulary semantic segmentation problem. Next, we describe our suggested architecture, ”Beyond-Labels,” which links the previously trained language and image models to accomplish the task.

III-A

Problem Formulation

In this section, we will describe our approach to formulating the data. A training set is provided to us:

\displaystyle\text{Data}_{\text{train}}

\displaystyle=\{(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{U})\mid\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 3},

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times|\mathcal{U}|},

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{S}\}

(1)

Where
\mathcal{X}
is the input picture and
\mathcal{Y}
is the one-hot encoded segmentation masks for
\mathcal{X}
;
\mathcal{U}
represents a unit of observed categories in
\mathcal{X}
, and
\mathcal{S}
represents all training categories in this unit. We aim to train a segmentation model that can separate a test image into significant areas of unexplored categories.

III-B

Image Feature Extraction

The process starts with splitting the input image
\mathcal{X}
as a collection of non-overlapping 2D patches. Each patch is flattened into a vector and put onto an embedding space with dimension ’d’ using a linear projection.
Fourier embeddings
[
27
]
are used to encode positional information rather than standard pre-trained positional embeddings, allowing for improved generalization to images of varied sizes while also maintaining smooth and consistent spatial encoding. A Fourier embedding is calculated for each patch at point (x,y) on the grid using sinusoidal functions. The Fourier embedding is applied to the patch embedding, resulting in a final position-aware embedding
\mathcal{Z}
for each patch.

\displaystyle z_{i}

\displaystyle=x_{i}+f\_emb(x,y)

(2)

Transformer encoder layers process position-aware embeddings,
\mathcal{Z}
and layer normalization, feedforward networks, multi-head self-attention, and residual connections are used by each layer to analyze the embeddings. CLIP
[
20
]
, a self-supervised language model trained on a huge collection of texts or images, is used as an image encoder.

\displaystyle F_{V}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\text{IE}}\left(f\_{\text{emb}}(X_{j})\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{(HW)\times d}

(3)

Where ”d” is the image embedding dimension, p is the patch size, and H = H/p and W = W/p.

Textual Feature Extraction

Semantic categories (
\mathcal{W}
) are encoded using a text encoder to produce text embeddings.

\displaystyle F_{W}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\text{TE}}(W)\in\mathbb{R}^{|W|\times d}

(4)

Where ”d” is the dimension of the word embeddings and ”W” is the number of input categories. We employ prompt templates such as ”a picture of a category within the context” to provide semantic category context. The output embeddings generated by the text encoder are then averaged. The ”Result Section” offers a collection of prompt templates.
We use CLIP

, a self-supervised language model, as a text encoder.

These were then compared with the textual features using cosine similarity, creating a segmentation mask with labels refined by thresholding and a temperature-scaled sigmoid.

III-D

Multi_Model Embedding Fusion

To align the image and textual information, we use MLPs (
\theta
) to align their channel dimensions (
F_{V}
and
F_{W}
). To capture the interactions between the textual and image data, we subsequently pass the combined features through a multimodal embedding fusion module:

[F_{V}^{0},F_{W}^{0}]=\Phi_{\text{Fuse}}(\theta[F_{V},F_{W}])

(5)

The fusion module uses multiple transformer encoder layers to capture image-text relationships. The fused image features (
F_{V}^{0}
) and text features (
F_{W}^{0}
) resemble the originals (
F_{V}
) and (
F_{W}
) in shape, but they are improved and enhanced by constantly focusing on one another’s modality. This enrichment comes because the fusion module iteratively puts more focus on cross-modal interactions, which allow the features of the image and text modalities to enrich each other’s representations. Thus, the fused features become better aligned and may be capable of providing a more holistic representation of the underlying data, therefore boosting performance in further tasks involving either images or text. Such an attention-driven fusion process significantly enhances the model’s capability of capturing fine-grained correlations between the two modalities, which is crucial for achieving high-quality results on tasks like open-vocabulary semantic segmentation. Our approach exploits the complementary strengths of both image and text data to allow more accurate and context-aware embeddings

III-E

The Hierarchical Decoder

The multimodal embedding fusion combines the image and text encoders’ features. These combined features are passed to the decoder for further processing. The decoder gives us the image feature map, which is progressively upsampled to match the resolution of the input image. The upsampled image features are compared with the textual features using cosine similarity to generate the logits
ŷ
. This comparison helps determine how closely each area in the image aligns with the provided text categories. The final result is a segmentation mask, where different regions of the image are assigned labels. Using class-wise thresholding and a sigmoid with a temperature (
\mathcal{\tau}
), final predictions can be made using binary segmentation for each category.

IV-A

Dataset

We evaluated our approach on the PASCAL-5i
[
23
]
dataset, which is designed for few-shot segmentation approaches. It is based on the well-known PASCAL VOC 2012 dataset. The dataset is divided into four folds (i = 0, 1, 2, 3) and contains 20 different object classes. Each fold contained five classes, and the model was trained on the other fifteen classes. This cross-validation design separates the classes used for testing from the classes used for training, ensuring that this is indeed a valid evaluation strategy to determine how well few-shot learning algorithms generalize onto new categories. The support set contains some labeled instances, for example, 1-shot or 5-shot, from each of the five target classes in each fold to use as a reference for segmentation. Given this limited information in the support set, the query set should separate the objects precisely in the unlabeled photos that contain objects from the same five classes. Models trained on 15 classes and tested on the remaining 5 are doing so to cover all classes iteratively, using a technique called ”leave-one-fold-out.” Since this is indeed an exhaustive evaluation framework, it has made PASCAL-5i a standard benchmark for the comparison of few-shot segmentation. We have reported the results of our method in the ablation study and results comparison section.

IV-B

Evaluation Metric

Our key evaluation metric is mean intersection-over-union (mIoU), which averages IoU across all classes within the fold.

\text{mIoU}=\frac{1}{F}\sum_{F=1}^{F}\text{IoU}_{F}

Where
IoU
denotes the intersection over the union of class (
F
), which is the target fold’s class count.

IV-C

Results Comparison

Table 2 compares different models on the segmentation task for all folds in terms of the overall mean IoU, namely, mIoU. SPNet has a representation with an overall mean IoU of 18.3 and folds 2 and 3 performed the weakest, showing the low ability of the network to generalize well against the unseen complexities of classes within few-shot segmentation tasks. ZS3Net performed better with a mIoU of 38.3, trying to generalize across folds in semantic features The OSLSM improves further by achieving a mIoU of 40.8, which is superior to that of ZS3Net and SPNet; however, challenges remain for particular folds such as 3. Our model surpasses all baselines by a mIoU of 41.5, with maximum performance at folds 0 and 1 at 44.6 and 49.4, respectively. This again supports the hypothesis that integrating Fourier embedding with multi-model fusion, together with a decoder, will improve feature representation by paying full consideration to the mutual image and textual information. This indicates that the relative improvement of 3.2 in mIoU over OSLSM and 8.4 over ZS3Net demonstrates the effectiveness of our proposed components in handling complex spatial relationships and unseen class distributions. These results confirm that our approach not only achieves better segmentation accuracy but also ensures more consistent performance across diverse folds.

IV-D

Ablation Study

We investigated the contribution of each core component in the proposed ”Beyond-Labels” framework. Specifically, we investigated the impact of the Fourier Embedding (F_Emb), multi-model embedding fusion module (Fusion), and the use of a decoder instead of traditional positional embedding techniques. Detailed results of this study can be seen in Table 1 for breaking down performance in different configurations.

•

By default, it uses only an upward model that includes the usual positional embedding and upsampling using a decoder. The use of this standard baseline model resulted in minimum performance, by which B_L_0 obtained a mIoU of only 25.7. The large decreases in scores in folds 2 and 3 represent a limitation based on the use of standard position embedding without robust feature fusion in action. Without Fourier embedding, the model cannot capture such high-dimensional and expressive positional information, which is critical for improving segmentation accuracy, especially in challenging folds such as folds 2 and 3.

•

While for B_L_1, incorporation of Fourier embedding improves its performance on all counts with the mIoU increasing to 36.9. This, in turn, indicates that adding frequency-based representation is highly vital for boosting the feature extraction required for improving the generalization of non-visible classes. More important is the model’s capability of adaptation to different complicated spatial scenarios due to improved performances on all folds.

•

The best-performing model, B_L_2, is the full model, including the Fourier embedding, Fusion, and decoder, with a mIoU of 41.5. Additional positional information is introduced by Fourier Embedding that, combined with the fusion module, enables the model to learn richer representations. Further refinement of the upsampling process with the decoder leads to consistent improvements in all the folds. This includes the Fourier embedding module with the Fusion module, which generates high-dimensional and expressive forms of position-based information, thus yielding significant gains in segmentation accuracy. This further facilitates better spatial understanding within the image, boosting the capability for more precise feature representations of the model.

The benefits of Fourier embedding are easily found in all folds in B_L_1 and B_L_2, where traditional embeddings often fall short.

IV-E

Implementation Details

The vision-language model (CLIP
[
20
]
) is frozen throughout the training process. Each of the 8 levels that make up the multi-model embedding fusion module has 4 attention heads. After k convolutional layers, the decoder performs a 2× upsampling operation. We employ the AdamW optimizer with a cosine scheduler to decay the learning rate to maximize training. We use cross-entropy for the loss function and set the temperature factor at (
\mathcal{\tau}
)= 0.08.

IV-F

Conclusion

Our research aims to make advanced vision and language models more helpful for open-vocabulary semantic
segmentation. We introduce ”Beyond-Labels,” a compact and efficient module that links image and language models to do open-vocabulary semantic segmentation, which involves finding and categorizing items in images, even for
previously unknown categories. We tested it on the “PASCAL” dataset and found that it performs extremely well,
exceeding some existing approaches. Despite its simplicity, “Beyond-Labels” delivers excellent results.

IV-G

Future Work

To assess our work’s resilience and enhance generalization across many domains, we will expand it to include more datasets and perform more ablation studies. Through testing on various datasets, we hope to ensure that our method works reliably and successfully in practical situations, capturing a wider range of difficulties and subtleties in semantic segmentation tasks.
