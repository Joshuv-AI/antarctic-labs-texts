Peritoneal Metastasis in Advanced Lung Adenocarcinoma Harboring EGFR Exon 19 Deletion/T790M with STRN::ALK Fusion and EGFR Kinase Domain Duplication: Two Case Reports and a Narrative Literature Summary
Authors: Hao Zhang, Jun Han, Feng Rong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeritoneal metastasis from lung adenocarcinoma is rare and generally associated with poor prognosis. Here, we report two cases of advanced NSCLC with multiple metastases in which the patients developed rare peritoneal metastases during disease progression. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) revealed rare genomic alterations: one patient harbored concurrent EGFR exon 19 deletion and T790M mutation, accompanied by EGFR amplification, TP53 mutation, and STRN::ALK fusion, while the other exhibited EGFR kinase domain duplication and TP53 mutation. Both patients underwent multiple lines of systemic therapy, including targeted treatment guided by NGS results, and achieved survival exceeding six months following the diagnosis of peritoneal metastasis, with ongoing treatment at last follow-up. These observations suggest that more precise molecularly guided targeted therapy may play a crucial role in the clinical management of peritoneal metastasis.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the most prevalent malignancy worldwide, accounting for 12.4% of all cancer cases,1 and is the leading cause of cancer-related mortality. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is the predominant histological subtype, with common metastatic sites including the brain (47%), bone (36%), liver (22%), adrenal glands (15%), pleural cavity (11%), and distant lymph nodes (10%).2

Peritoneal metastasis (PM) typically arises from intra-abdominal malignancies, whereas involvement from extra-abdominal primary tumors, such as lung cancer, is exceptionally rare.3 In a retrospective analysis by Niu et al4 metastatic sites with an incidence of <5% were defined as uncommon; within this context, the peritoneum was identified as the third rarest metastatic site in NSCLC, with an incidence of 0.84%. PM in NSCLC is usually diagnosed at an advanced stage, is associated with poor prognosis, and remains poorly understood in terms of its underlying mechanisms and optimal management strategies.

The identification of predictive biomarkers and the development of targeted therapies against driver genes such as EGFR, ALK, and KRAS has substantially transformed the treatment landscape of NSCLC. However, durable clinical benefit remains limited because acquired resistance inevitably develops in most patients.5 Rare genomic alterations, including EGFR kinase domain duplication (EGFR-KDD), STRN::ALK fusion, and complex EGFR mutation profiles, have only occasionally been reported in patients with peritoneal metastasis, and their clinical significance and resistance mechanisms remain poorly understood.

Here, we report two patients with advanced NSCLC and peritoneal metastasis harboring rare genomic alterations. One patient developed concurrent EGFR exon 19 deletion, T790M mutation, EGFR amplification, and STRN::ALK fusion, whereas the other harbored EGFR-KDD. Both patients survived for more than six months after the diagnosis of peritoneal metastasis and received molecularly guided treatment. These cases provide additional clinical evidence regarding this rare metastatic pattern and support the potential value of repeated molecular profiling in guiding individualized treatment.

Methods

Clinical Data Collection

Clinical data were retrospectively collected from the medical records of two patients with advanced lung adenocarcinoma and peritoneal metastasis treated at Lu’an Hospital of Anhui Medical University. The collected information included clinical history, imaging findings, pathological results, molecular testing results, treatment course, response assessment, and follow-up outcomes. The final follow-up date was March 18,2026.

Diagnostic and Response Assessment

The diagnosis of lung adenocarcinoma was confirmed by histopathological examination and radiological imaging. Peritoneal metastasis was diagnosed based on abdominal imaging findings, ascitic fluid cytology and pathological evidence, and exclusion of other possible primary malignancies when clinically indicated.

Treatment response was evaluated according to RECIST v1.1, based on serial imaging examinations and changes in clinical symptoms.

Molecular Testing and Immunohistochemical Testing

Molecular profiling was performed by NGS using left supraclavicular lymph node tissue from Patient 1 and ascitic fluid cell pellet from Patient 2. Sequencing was conducted on the Illumina NextSeq550/NovaSeq6000 platform with a targeted DNA/RNA panel covering 26 lung cancer-related DNA genes and 19 fusion-related RNA genes. The reference genome was GRCh37/hg19. The average sequencing depth was 3180.16×, and the variant allele frequency threshold was approximately 20%. Gene fusions were detected by DNA- and RNA-based NGS.

Quality-control parameters were as follows: the average sequencing depth was 3180.16×, the proportion of bases with ≥500× coverage was 96.50%, the on-target rate was 99.93%, and the Q30 value was 96.18%. The total RNA input was 952 ng, and 44.874 million valid RNA reads were obtained. Positive and negative controls were within control limits, and the overall quality-control assessment met the predefined criteria.

PD-L1 expression was assessed on the Dako platform using the 22C3 antibody clone and scored by TPS and CPS. PD-L1 expression was negative, with TPS <1% and CPS <1; PD-L1 positivity was defined as TPS ≥1%.

ALK immunohistochemistry was performed using the mouse monoclonal antibody clone 5A4. ALK positivity was defined as cytoplasmic staining in at least one tumor cell. ALK fusion status was further assessed by DNA- and RNA-based NGS when applicable.

Because only two patients were included, no formal statistical analysis was performed. Clinical findings, molecular results, treatment courses, and outcomes were summarized descriptively.

Literature Search

A narrative literature search was conducted using the PubMed database to identify relevant studies on lung adenocarcinoma with peritoneal metastasis and rare genomic alterations. The search terms included “lung adenocarcinoma”, “peritoneal metastasis”, “EGFR kinase domain duplication,” “EGFR-KDD,” “T790M,” “ALK fusion,” and “STRN::ALK”. This search was intended to provide clinical context rather than a systematic review.

Ethical Statement

Institutional approval was required and was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Lu’an Hospital of Anhui Medical University (approval No. 2026LLKS-KY-067).

Case Presentation

Case 1

A 59-year-old female patient presented to our hospital in August 2017 with cough and sputum production. Serum tumor marker evaluation revealed a markedly elevated carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) level of 168.54 ng/mL. Chest computed tomography (CT) and histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of left lung adenocarcinoma. Subsequent PET/CT performed at an outside hospital on September 6, 2017, showed an irregular soft-tissue mass in the left upper lobe with abnormally increased FDG uptake, enlarged lymph nodes of varying sizes in the mediastinum and bilateral hilar regions, marked enlargement of the right adrenal gland, and a round soft-tissue nodule in the right parieto-occipital region, all with increased FDG uptake. Focally increased FDG uptake was also observed in the left femur and right ilium. Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with stage IV left lung adenocarcinoma (T2bN3M1b), with metastases to the mediastinal and bilateral hilar lymph nodes, right adrenal gland, brain, and multiple bone sites.

On September 7, 2017, the patient initiated a chemotherapy regimen consisting of Endostar (30 mg, days 1–7), pemetrexed (0.8 g, day 1), and lobaplatin (50 mg, day 1). Genetic testing performed at an outside hospital on September 9, 2017, identified EGFR exon 19 mutation positivity, with ALK and ROS1 negativity. Subsequently, the patient received long-term tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) therapy with gefitinib (250 mg once daily).

Serial CT scans from July 2022 to May 2023 showed a fluctuating but gradually enlarging left lung mass, measuring approximately 52 mm × 46 mm on July 20, 2022; 57 mm on September 12, 2022; 50 mm on November 9, 2022; 60 mm on January 9, 2023; 48×66 mm on March 11, 2023; and 74 mm on May 15, 2023. A contrast-enhanced chest CT on May 19, 2023, revealed new metastatic lesions involving the second rib, multiple thoracic vertebrae, and adjacent structures. At that time, tumor markers were as follows: CEA 271.21 ng/mL, SCA 0.37 ng/mL, cytokeratin 19 fragment 23.68 ng/mL. In the context of overall disease progression, the treatment response was assessed as progressive disease (PD), with suspected TKI resistance. On May 23, 2023, a whole-body bone scan indicated multiple osseous metastases. A repeat percutaneous lung biopsy on May 25, 2023, was consistent with adenocarcinoma. Immunohistochemical staining showed CK5/6 focal positivity, P40 negativity, CK7 positivity, TTF-1 negativity, Napsin A negativity, and Ki-67 positivity of approximately 20%. Repeat genetic testing continued to indicate EGFR exon 19 deletion. Consequently, the patient was switched to a combination regimen of almonertinib and anlotinib.

On January 1, 2025, a follow-up CT scan revealed a left lung mass measuring approximately 24 mm, multiple hypodense lesions in the liver, and a 1.3cm hypodense nodular shadow in the left kidney. By August 2025, the patient had developed progressive abdominal distension and occasional nausea, without vomiting, abdominal pain, or diarrhea. A chest CT showed a left lung mass (approximately 24 mm) and multiple small mediastinal nodular lesions (approximately 10 mm) (Figure 1A). Ultrasound revealed left supraclavicular lymph node metastasis and a hypoechoic nodule (18×17 mm) in the right liver. A contrast-enhanced abdominopelvic CT on September 1, 2025, demonstrated multiple hepatic metastases, as well as metastatic involvement of lymph nodes in the hepatoduodenal ligament region and retroperitoneum (Figure 1B). Concurrent ascitic fluid cytology was suggestive of adenocarcinoma (Figure 1C). Taken together, these findings indicated the development of hepatic and peritoneal metastases, consistent with further disease progression, and the treatment response was assessed as progressive disease (PD). Case 2

A 58-year-old man was initially diagnosed in April 2024 with invasive lung adenocarcinoma and multiple metastases involving the brain, bone, and lymph nodes. From May 8 to October 22, 2024, the patient received eight cycles of chemotherapy with carboplatin (500 mg, day 1), pemetrexed (0.8 g, day 1), and camrelizumab (200 mg, day 1). This was followed by three cycles of maintenance therapy with pemetrexed (0.8 g, day 1) combined with camrelizumab (200 mg, day 2) on November 21, 2024; December 19, 2024; and January 15, 2025.

A follow-up upper abdominal CT and brain MRI on February 17, 2025, revealed disease progression, and the treatment response was assessed as progressive disease (PD). On February 19, 2025, the patient received one cycle of combination therapy consisting of tislelizumab (200 mg, day 1), Endostar, liposomal paclitaxel (240 mg, day 1), and cisplatin (30 mg, days 1–3), along with concurrent bone-protective treatment. Subsequently, the patient developed worsening gait instability. A brain MRI on March 18, 2025, revealed metastatic lesions involving the bilateral frontoparietal lobes, temporal lobes, and the right hippocampus. On March 20, 2025, the patient initiated whole-brain radiotherapy (WBRT; CTV: entire brain; PGTV: 3 mm extension beyond GTV; PTV: 3 mm extension beyond CTV; 6-MV X-ray IMRT: 95% PTV: 30 Gy/10 fractions, 3 Gy/fraction).

Given intolerance to prior chemotherapy, the patient subsequently received tislelizumab (200 mg) combined with Endostar (15 mg, once daily on days 1–7) on April 2, 2025. Discussion

We report two cases of advanced NSCLC with multiple metastases in which patients developed rare peritoneal metastases and survived for more than six months after the diagnosis of peritoneal metastasis; both cases harbored rare genetic alterations identified by NGS.

Lung cancer remains the malignancy with the highest incidence and mortality rates worldwide. NSCLC accounts for approximately 80% of all lung cancer cases, and the majority of patients are diagnosed at advanced stages.6 With the advent of systemic therapies, including immunotherapy and targeted therapy, both progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) in patients with lung cancer have improved.7 Nevertheless, distant metastasis remains a major cause of treatment failure. While well-established diagnostic and therapeutic strategies are available for common metastatic sites in NSCLC, such as the brain, bone, liver, and adrenal glands, the optimal management of peritoneal metastasis (PM) remains poorly defined.

Peritoneal Metastasis in Lung Cancer

PM most commonly arises from intra-abdominal malignancies, including colorectal, appendiceal, gastric, and ovarian cancers, whereas dissemination from extra-abdominal primary tumors is relatively uncommon. Lung cancer represents one of the most frequent non-abdominal malignancies causing PM.8,9 Autopsy findings indicate that the incidence of peritoneal metastasis from NSCLC ranges from approximately 2.7% to 16%,10 however the clinical detection rate is considerably lower.10,11 Among histological subtypes, adenocarcinoma appears to have a higher propensity for peritoneal dissemination.12 Owing to a lack of specific early clinical manifestations, PM is often misattributed to treatment-related effects, leading to missed optimal treatment opportunities; consequently, it is frequently diagnosed at an advanced stage and is associated with poor prognosis.13 The median overall survival (mOS) following the diagnosis of PM is generally less than three months, often indicating terminal-stage disease.14,15 Notably, Niu et al reported no significant difference in survival between patients with synchronous and metachronous peritoneal metastases.4

Peritoneal metastases are frequently accompanied by ascites, and the development of malignant ascites generally indicates that the patient’s condition has progressed to a point where treatment options are limited. Furthermore, given that the peritoneal cavity does not constitute a favorable microenvironment for lung cancer cell colonization, surviving tumor cells may exhibit enhanced invasive and proliferative capabilities.16

Mechanisms of Peritoneal Metastasis

The mechanisms underlying peritoneal metastasis have been extensively characterized in intra-abdominal malignancies. Tumor cells can induce a pro-inflammatory milieu within the peritoneal cavity, leading to disruption of the protective mesothelial layer and exposure of the extracellular matrix, thereby facilitating tumor cell adhesion. Additionally, cancer cells infiltrating the peritoneum can induce cell contraction via integrins, promoting peritoneal metastasis through multiple mechanisms while potentially impairing the delivery of chemotherapeutic agents.17,18

In contrast, the mechanisms underlying peritoneal metastasis in lung cancer remain incompletely understood. Cao et al16 proposed two potential explanations: First, lung adenocarcinoma is prone to hematogenous dissemination, which represents one of the primary routes for peritoneal metastasis in NSCLC. Second, given the high prevalence of driver gene alterations in lung adenocarcinoma, targeted therapy is commonly employed. Although such treatment prolongs patient survival, it may inadvertently provide tumor cells with an extended timeframe to adapt to the peritoneal microenvironment, thereby increasing the likelihood of peritoneal involvement.

In addition, Patil et al18 reported that NSCLC patients who develop PM frequently present with concurrent pleural effusion. In a case study of lymphangioleiomyomatosis, Takagi et al19 demonstrated that the diaphragm harbors a rich lymphatic network, and that diaphragmatic lesions involving lymphatic channels may serve as a conduit for the translocation of chylous fluid from the thoracic to the peritoneal cavity. Based on these observations, we hypothesize that in patients with malignant pleural effusion, tumor invasion of the diaphragm may enable dissemination via diaphragmatic lymphatic pathways, thereby contributing to the development of peritoneal metastases.

Genetic Alterations and Resistance Mechanisms

The identification of oncogenic driver alterations and the selection of appropriate targeted therapies are crucial for improving the prognosis of patients with advanced NSCLC. However, acquired resistance eventually develops in most patients and limits the long-term benefits of targeted therapy.

The epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) signaling pathway plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of NSCLC, and dysregulation of EGFR is considered one of the most important molecular events underlying lung carcinogenesis.6 Among EGFR mutations, exon 19 in-frame deletions account for approximately 45% of all EGFR alterations, with delE746_A750 being the most common subtype.20 First-generation tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), such as gefitinib used in the present case, initially exhibit remarkable antitumor activity. However, nearly half of patients develop resistance within 9–14 months after treatment initiation, resulting in disease recurrence or progression.21 Acquired resistance mechanisms include secondary point mutations, such as T790M;22 activation of bypass signaling pathways, including HER2 amplification;23 and histological transformation from NSCLC to SCLC.24 Among these, the secondary T790M mutation in exon 20 is the most common mechanism of gefitinib resistance.25 This mutation results in the substitution of threonine with methionine, thereby impairing the efficacy of reversible TKIs.26 In addition, amplification of the EGFR locus may further enhance the resistance effect mediated by T790M.27 In the present case, concurrent EGFR exon 19 deletion, T790M mutation, and EGFR amplification were identified, and disease progression after long-term gefitinib treatment was consistent with these established resistance mechanisms.

EGFR kinase domain duplication (EGFR-KDD) is an extremely rare genomic alteration. A large retrospective study reported a prevalence of only 0.12%. EGFR-KDD usually involves an in-frame tandem duplication spanning exons 18–25, although duplications involving exons 14–26 or 17–25 have also been described.28 These alterations result in duplication of the EGFR kinase domain at the protein level, promoting intramolecular dimerization and constitutive activation of downstream signaling pathways.29 Several reports have indicated that EGFR-KDD may confer resistance to first- and second-generation TKIs. Nevertheless, Qin et al30 described two patients with EGFR-KDD-positive NSCLC who achieved progression-free survival exceeding two years after receiving first-line furmonertinib, suggesting that third-generation EGFR-TKIs may represent a promising therapeutic option for this rare molecular subtype.

TP53 mutation is also common in NSCLC. A study by Shaylin A et al31 identified 264 alterations in the TP53 gene, of which 249 (93.4%) were pathogenic mutations and only 15 were variants of uncertain significance (VUS); these alterations were evenly distributed across the DNA-binding domain. In a meta-analysis by Tammemagi et al32 TP53 mutation was present in 46.8% of cases and occurred more frequently in smoking-associated squamous cell carcinoma (51.2%) and large cell carcinoma (53.7%) than in adenocarcinoma (38.8%). Li et al33 reported TP53 mutations in approximately 45% of lung adenocarcinomas, corroborating this observation. The analysis by Mitsudomi et al34 demonstrated that TP53 mutation is a significant prognostic marker in lung adenocarcinoma, indicating greater invasiveness and shorter survival. TP53 mutations can co-occur with EGFR mutations,35 as observed in the present case. Such co-mutations generally arise before metastasis and are retained during disease progression. Moreover, TP53 alterations have been associated with therapeutic resistance.36

ALK gene fusion represents a distinct molecular subtype and an important driver alteration in NSCLC. However, it is relatively rare in NSCLC, with an incidence of approximately 5%,37 and occurs predominantly in males, never-smokers, and patients with adenocarcinoma.38 Multiple studies have confirmed that ALK fusions are also implicated in TKI resistance. EML4-ALK is the most common ALK fusion variant in NSCLC, whereas the STRN::ALK fusion identified in the present case is rare. Previous evidence suggests that alectinib may have favorable efficacy against STRN::ALK fusions. Su et al39 reported a patient with advanced NSCLC harboring this fusion who achieved a remarkable response lasting over 19 months after alectinib treatment.

Treatment and Prognosis of Peritoneal Metastasis

The development of lung cancer is a multistep process with a highly complex and heterogeneous molecular pathogenesis, which may be driven by diverse genetic factors and molecular alterations.20 The identification of actionable driver alterations and the selection of appropriate targeted therapies are critical for improving outcomes in patients with advanced NSCLC. The advent of NGS has enabled the detection of rare and previously unrecognized oncogenic drivers, thereby facilitating more precise molecular stratification and the implementation of personalized therapeutic strategies.40

Patients with NSCLC and peritoneal metastasis generally have poor clinical outcomes, underscoring the importance of robust prognostic indicators. In a retrospective analysis of 27 patients with PM from NSCLC, Ohta et al12 reported that poor prognosis was associated with worse performance status, concurrent pleural metastasis, and the absence of actionable driver alterations. In another retrospective analysis, Peng et al41 reported that NSCLC patients with rare EGFR exon 19 deletion variants achieved a significantly longer median progression-free survival (mPFS) following TKI therapy compared with those harboring common exon 19 deletions. Consistent with these findings, previous studies have suggested that patients harboring actionable genomic alterations may experience more favorable outcomes.16

Moreover, timely and effective treatment strategies may still confer meaningful survival benefits. In a retrospective cohort of 193 patients with rare metastatic sites in lung cancer, Niu et al4 demonstrated that combined systemic and local therapy was associated with significantly improved survival compared with either systemic therapy alone or best supportive care. This finding is further supported by the report from Griffioen et al, which corroborated the superior prognosis associated with combined systemic and local treatment.42 In the present report, both patients with advanced lung adenocarcinoma underwent multiple lines of systemic therapy, including targeted therapy and chemotherapy. Despite the development of peritoneal metastases—typically associated with a dismal prognosis—both patients survived for more than six months after the diagnosis of peritoneal metastasis, underscoring the clinical importance of effective systemic treatment in this setting.

Traditional drug development is limited by its inability to target a substantial proportion of the human proteome, including transcription factors (TFs) and RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), which have long been considered “undruggable” due to the lack of well-defined binding pockets for small-molecule inhibitors.43 The emergence of proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs), particularly in combination with oligonucleotide-based strategies, has provided a novel approach to targeting proteins that are not amenable to conventional pharmacological inhibition. This strategy offers additional advantages in overcoming drug resistance, addressing tumor recurrence, and expanding the range of actionable targets, including low-abundance or structurally challenging proteins.44 With ongoing technological advances, PROTAC-based approaches may play an increasingly important role in the development of next-generation targeted therapies.

Clinical Implications

Clinically, early diagnosis of peritoneal metastasis in NSCLC is challenging due to the lack of specific symptoms. Common manifestations include nausea, vomiting, anorexia with weight loss, fatigue, abdominal distension, a marked decline in performance status (PS), and, in some patients, evident ascites. These symptoms are often mistaken for treatment-related adverse effects, thereby delaying early diagnosis and contributing to a poor prognosis. As previously mentioned, lung adenocarcinoma and pleural effusion are high-risk factors for peritoneal metastasis, and the development of ascites generally portends a worse prognosis. Therefore, heightened vigilance is warranted for patients presenting with the aforementioned symptoms.

Contrast-enhanced CT is the preferred imaging modality and typically reveals indirect signs such as peritoneal thickening, nodules, omental thickening, or ascites. However, CT has insufficient sensitivity for detecting small tumor implants or early peritoneal disease.13 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (FDG-PET/CT) can improve diagnostic accuracy by identifying increased glucose metabolism in tumor lesions, particularly in patients without extensive disease or with unexplained abdominal symptoms, thereby compensating for the limitations of conventional imaging.45 Nevertheless, FDG-PET/CT also has limited sensitivity for small or hypometabolic lesions. Ascitic fluid cytology is a commonly used adjunctive diagnostic method, but its sensitivity varies widely (50–70%) and is influenced by tumor burden and mucin-secreting tumors.13 In addition, immunohistochemistry (IHC) can effectively distinguish metastatic lung adenocarcinoma in the peritoneum from primary gastrointestinal malignancies; TTF-1 and Napsin-A exhibit persistent high-sensitivity expression in lung adenocarcinoma, whereas gastrointestinal markers such as CK20 and CDX2 remain negative, which is critical for confirming the pulmonary origin of peritoneal lesions.46 In recent years, molecular testing has gradually assumed an important role. NGS can detect targetable genetic alterations, thereby supporting both diagnosis and individualized treatment selection.

Conclusion

In summary, peritoneal metastasis from lung cancer is rare but clinically important and is generally associated with poor prognosis. Patients with advanced lung adenocarcinoma who develop malignant effusion, abdominal symptoms, or atypical disease progression may require closer surveillance for peritoneal involvement. These cases highlight the potential value of NGS in tracking tumor evolution and identifying potentially actionable alterations, particularly rare EGFR alterations or acquired fusion events. However, the evidence remains limited, and no definitive conclusions regarding survival benefit or optimal treatment can be drawn from two cases. Further multicenter case series, registry-based studies, and mechanistic investigations are needed to better define the clinical features, molecular characteristics, prognosis, and treatment strategies of lung adenocarcinoma with peritoneal metastasis. Future translational studies may also explore novel targeted agents or targeted protein degradation approaches for EGFR- or ALK-driven tumors to overcome resistance to conventional tyrosine kinase inhibitors.
