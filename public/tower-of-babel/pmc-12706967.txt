Letter to the editor regarding ‘Liberal or restrictive transfusion for veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation patients: a target trial emulation using the OBLEX study data’
Authors: Tessa A van der Meer, Senta Jorinde Raasveld, Marcella C A Müller, Alexander P J Vlaar, Jimmy Schenk
Published: 2025

With great interest, we have read the paper by Thao and colleagues on the emulated target trial using the OBLEX cohort to compare a liberal to a restrictive red blood cell (RBC) transfusion strategy in veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) [1]. They reported that receiving a liberal transfusion practice (hemoglobin [Hb] ≥ 90 g/L) appeared to have a moderate survival advantage compared to a restrictive transfusion practice (Hb ≤ 70 g/L) for the first two to three days on VA-ECMO. The authors used a target trial emulation and a sequential trial approach to estimate a causal effect, however, we identified several methodological concerns that may hamper the causal interpretation of their results.

Target trial emulation has become increasingly popular to estimate a causal effect in observational cohorts. Credible causal inference requires emulating the target trial’s random assignment by adjusting for key prognostic factors [2]. In their study, the authors did not account for expected (baseline) differences in SOFA-, APACHE IV- or SAPS-II scores or pre-ECMO hemoglobin levels between these groups. As a result, we suspect that the estimated effect might have been subject to confounding by indication, hampering its causal interpretation. This is underlined by the fact that, to be eligible for randomization to the restrictive group, patients needed to receive their first blood transfusion while their hemoglobin level was already below 70 g/L. Especially in the first few days on VA-ECMO, it is very probable that these were the patients with an overall greater illness severity or with a higher (pre-) ECMO bleeding burden. In a sensitivity analysis the authors did adjust for the potential bias introduced by massive transfusion protocol employment prior to ECMO. However, this approach did not adjust for patients who experienced clinically relevant bleeding that did not meet all the criteria for a massive transfusion protocol.

Furthermore, by defining time zero as the time of ECMO initiation, but only assigning treatment strategies after post-baseline transfusion events occurred, the analysis was subject to immortal time bias [3]. Patients that died before transfusion were automatically assigned to the no transfusion group and patients with a hemoglobin level of < 90 g/L at ECMO-initiation could never enter in the liberal group. Given these limitations, we strongly doubt that the two groups are truly comparable for assessing a causal effect.

To estimate a per-protocol effect, the authors censored patients when they deviated from their assigned strategy. Because it was deemed likely that switching of transfusion threshold could be deterioration driven, the authors applied inverse probability of artificial censoring weighting to prevent selection bias. They included the covariates renal replacement therapy, invasive ventilation and severe bleeding, but failed to include other relevant parameters that can indicate clinical deterioration over time such as changes in lactate levels, SOFA scores or vasopressor usage and therefore possibly overestimated the survival advantage in the liberal group.

As a possible explanation for their findings the authors propose that in the early days of VA-ECMO, when left ventricular ejection is often severely impaired and shock is common, the ECMO-flow may not fully compensate the reduced native cardiac output, potentially leading to inadequate oxygen delivery. Based on their results the authors suggest that, at that specific moment in ECMO treatment, transfusions might have a beneficial effect. Secondary evidence for this hypothesis is lacking. To the contrary, a review on blood transfusion strategies in ECMO noted that, although transfusions can increase oxygen delivery (DO₂) by raising hemoglobin concentration, experimental studies have demonstrated that the oxygen consumption (VO₂) remains independent of DO₂ until a critical DO₂ threshold is reached. Consequently, RBC transfusion are rarely required to optimize DO₂ to match VO₂, except in cases of severe hemorrhagic shock or severe anemia which is lower than the 70 g/L for most patients [4].

The possible beneficial effect of a liberal transfusion threshold has not been observed in any other observational cohort of ECMO patients with confirmation of this lack of effect in multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses [5, 6]. In cardiac surgery patients, a population showing overlap to those on VA-ECMO, randomized controlled trials have demonstrated that adopting a restrictive threshold was safe [7, 8]. A true answer to the difference in effect of liberal versus restrictive transfusion should be based on a rigorously executed trial, such as the currently ongoing TREC-RCT (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT06560164), aiming to provide the robust evidence needed for optimizing transfusion strategies [9].

In conclusion, implementing target trial emulation to assess questions in observational data is promising, however, before this results in the advice to change transfusion practices, several preconditions need to be met to conclude a true causal effect. While the authors used advanced methodological methods, the OBLEX database might not have been sufficient enough to successfully emulate the target trial and therefore suffered from unmeasured bias. Given the vulnerability of this patient population, well designed randomized controlled trials are urgently awaited to guide transfusion practice.
