The effectiveness of a cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia based intervention to improve sleep quality in nursing home residents, a cluster randomised controlled trial
Authors: Hilde Lahaye, Filip Haegdorens, Erik Franck, Sebastiaan Engelborghs, Heidi Arnouts, Marianne De Witte, Wouter R Ryckaert, Roel Daneels, Abdesselam Zhiri, Gentiana Kajdomqaj, Sarah Peeters, Kaat Vercammen, Barbara Beyer, Elisabeth Croonenberghs, Katrin Gillis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the effectiveness of a structured, team-delivered intervention based on cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) on sleep and related outcomes in nursing home residents.MethodsA cluster randomised controlled trial with intervention group and control group was conducted in five nursing homes between February 2024 and February 2025. A total of 240 residents were included, with a mean age of 86.8 years and 72.5% being female. Sleep outcomes were assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) for residents who were cognitively intact or mildly impaired, and the Neuropsychiatric Inventory-Nursing Home version (NPI-NH) for residents with significant cognitive or language impairments.ResultsIn the PSQI group (n = 124) there was a significant interaction effect between time and group for the total PSQI score (p = 0.031). No significant between-group difference was observed at follow-up. In the NPI-NH group (n = 116), no significant effect was found for sleep and nighttime behaviour disturbances (p = 0.061). However, residents in the intervention group showed significant improvements across several neuropsychiatric domains, including reductions in delusions, agitation/aggression, depression, disinhibition and aberrant motor behaviour.ConclusionAlthough the intervention did not result in significant between-group differences in sleep outcomes, these findings suggest that a structured, team-delivered CBT-I–based intervention may contribute to improvements in behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD). This underscores the often-overlooked relationship between sleep and BPSD and supports further investigation of team-based sleep interventions in long-term care settings.Trial registrationISRCTN50844606, registered on 9 September 2024.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s41999-026-01519-6.Keywords: Sleep, Dementia, CBT-I, Nursing homes, BPSD, Non-pharmacological interventions

Introduction

Sleep undergoes significant changes with age, particularly among older adults in long-term care facilities. Physiological alterations such as difficulties in initiating and maintaining sleep, reductions in slow-wave and rapid eye movement sleep, and a shift toward lighter sleep stages are well-documented, alongside circadian rhythm changes resulting in a morning chronotype and decreased melatonin secretion [1]. A range of external factors common in institutional settings further compounds these biological changes: nighttime care routines, environmental noise, rigid schedules, limited daytime activity, multimorbidity, chronic pain, and psychosocial factors such as social isolation and symptoms of depression or anxiety [2–5].

Sleep disturbances are highly prevalent as a result: up to 65% of NH residents report poor sleep quality [6], and 20–23% of people with dementia experience sleep disturbances [7, 8]. The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) identifies insomnia as the most common sleep disorder in later life, with incidence rates of 30–48% and defines insomnia as persistent difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep, or non-restorative sleep, at least three nights per week for three months, with significant daytime impairment [9]. Sleep disturbances are particularly concerning in older adults with mild cognitive impairment, where they are associated with accelerated cognitive and functional decline, neuropsychiatric symptoms, and depression [10], and may increase the risk of progression to dementia [11].

Pharmacological treatment options are commonly used and can be effective. However, their use should be carefully balanced against concerns regarding safety and long-term efficacy, including sedation, falls, drug interactions, and dependence [12]. Consequently, multi-component non-pharmacological interventions are increasingly prioritised. Recent evidence, including a Cochrane review [13] and NICE guidance [14], supports their effectiveness, particularly when caregivers are involved [13].

Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is widely recognised as the first-line treatment for chronic insomnia in adults of all ages, including those with comorbidities. It combines behavioural, cognitive, and psychoeducational strategies and is recommended in recent European clinical guidelines [15]. CBT-I has demonstrated effectiveness in managing insomnia among older adults, with the behavioural components showing the strongest empirical support [16, 17]. However, most research has focused on community-dwelling adults aged 65 to 74 years [18], leaving a gap regarding nursing home residents, who often face greater medical and psychosocial challenges and have limited personal agency over contextual factors affecting sleep.

Moreover, over 55% of NH residents live with a neurocognitive disorder [19], limiting their ability to initiate or sustain behavioural sleep strategies without support. Involving formal caregivers may therefore be essential for achieving sustainable improvements. Hypothesising that a structured, team-delivered CBT-I–based intervention would improve sleep quality, this trial aimed to evaluate its effectiveness among NH residents.

Methods

Trial design, setting and trial population

A cluster randomised controlled trial, including intervention and control groups, was conducted in five NHs in Flanders, Belgium. These NHs provide comprehensive residential care and 24-h nursing support to older adults, including individuals with cognitive impairment and all residents had single-occupancy rooms.

Randomisation was done at the NH level, using a computer-generated permuted block design in Microsoft Excel [20], resulting in two NHs allocated to the intervention group (INT) and three to the control group (CTR).

Residents with a very short anticipated stay in the nursing home (e.g., due to expected transfer, discharge, or being in the terminal phase of life) were excluded due to feasibility constraints related to follow-up assessments and to avoid interfering with care priorities in the terminal phase. Recruitment and consent procedures were conducted by NH staff, who approached NH residents who did not meet the exclusion criteria or their proxies. A total of 349 NH residents (or their legal representatives) provided informed consent. A flowchart of participant recruitment, exclusion, and allocation is presented in Fig. 1.

Outcomes and data collection

NH residents were divided into two groups based on cognitive functioning and ability to complete the assessment instruments: those who were cognitively intact or mildly impaired and able to self-report, and those with significant cognitive or language impairments requiring proxy reporting. This classification was guided by Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores (approximately < 19). All measurements were taken at baseline (T0, February 2024) and at follow-up (T1, February 2025) by trained researchers who were blinded to group allocation. To minimise seasonal influences on sleep, both baseline and follow-up assessments were made in February.

Cognitively intact or mildly impaired NH residents

To evaluate perceived sleep quality and related disturbances in the past month, the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) was used for NH residents who were cognitively intact or mildly impaired [21, 22]. The PSQI is a widely validated self-report instrument comprising 19 items grouped into seven components: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication and daytime dysfunction. Each component is scored on a 0–3 scale, yielding a global score ranging from 0 to 21, where higher scores indicate poorer sleep quality. A global PSQI score greater than 5 is commonly used as a threshold for poor subjective sleep quality.

NH residents with significant cognitive or language impairment

For NH residents with significant cognitive or language impairment, sleep behaviour was assessed using the Neuropsychiatric Inventory-Nursing Home version (NPI-NH). This proxy-rated, validated instrument measures 12 neuropsychiatric domains common in people with dementia, including sleep and nighttime behaviour disturbances, and captures associated caregiver distress [23, 24]. Every NH resident had two fixed observers, consisting of a night-shift staff member and a direct caregiver (e.g., nurse, physiotherapist, or occupational therapist), who rated behavioural and mood symptoms during the whole trial.

Baseline demographic data and medication use were recorded. Medication classification followed the Belgian Centre for Pharmacotherapeutic Information system, with a single modification reflecting real-world use. Trazodone was classified in the category of hypnotics, sedatives, and anxiolytics because it is predominantly used for the symptomatic treatment of insomnia and other sleep disturbances. Only regularly prescribed medications (i.e., scheduled, chronic medication recorded in the medication charts) were recorded. The Katz score was used to assess functional status.

Identification of poor sleepers

NH residents were classified as poor sleepers based on PSQI or NPI-NH outcomes, depending on their cognitive status.

For cognitively intact or mildly impaired residents, poor sleep was defined using two specific PSQI items: (1) “During the past month, how would you rate your sleep quality overall?”, with responses of fairly bad or very bad, and/or (2) “During the past month, how often have you had trouble staying awake while driving, eating meals, or engaging in social activity?”, with responses of once or twice a week or three or more times a week. Residents meeting either criterion were classified as poor sleepers.

For residents assessed with the NPI-NH, scores from the nighttime behaviour disturbances, anxiety, and depression domains were summed, given the close interrelationship between sleep problems, anxiety, and depressive symptoms in older adults [25, 26]. Residents with a total score of 12 or higher, or a score of 6 or higher on the sleep and nighttime behaviour disturbance domain, were classified as poor sleepers.

Intervention

The choice of CBT-I as the framework underlying the intervention is grounded in well-established theoretical models of chronic insomnia. Specifically, the intervention draws on Spielman’s 3P model, which conceptualizes insomnia as being maintained by perpetuating behavioural and cognitive factors, such as extended time in bed, irregular sleep schedules, and maladaptive beliefs about sleep, that persist independently of the original precipitating cause [27].

Complementing this behavioural framework, Harvey’s cognitive model of insomnia posits that excessive negatively toned cognitive activity, including heightened arousal, selective attention, and distorted beliefs about the consequences of poor sleep, plays a central role in perpetuating sleep difficulties [28]. CBT-I directly targets both behavioural and cognitive mechanisms, making it a theoretically coherent and particularly well-suited intervention to address the chronic, self-perpetuating nature of insomnia commonly observed in elderly care home residents [29].

Accordingly, the intervention comprised four interrelated components embedded within a team-based care approach, each aligned with core CBT-I principles. Core CBT-I principles, including sleep hygiene, stimulus control, cognitive restructuring, and relaxation, were adapted to the nursing home context and operationalised in daily care practices.

Psychoeducational training

Caregivers received a total of six hours of psychoeducational training and supervision. The training was delivered by a sleep expert (EF) and aimed at enhancing their understanding of sleep and its importance in long-term care settings. In line with CBT-I principles of cognitive restructuring and sleep hygiene education, the training emphasised the importance of sleep for overall well-being and introduced caregivers to non-pharmacological strategies for promoting healthy sleep.

The training was structured around five core modules: (1) an introduction to the relevance of sleep in long-term care settings; (2) the fundamentals of sleep, including its stages and functions; (3) the regulation of the sleep–wake cycle and how it changes with age; (4) the development and maintenance of insomnia, with attention to both predisposing and perpetuating factors; and (5) evidence-based approaches for addressing sleep problems, including practical ways to optimise sleep quality in daily care routines.

Throughout the training, common assumptions about sleep in older adults were explicitly addressed, such as the belief that poor sleep is an inevitable part of aging or that older adults require significantly less sleep. By challenging these beliefs, the training aimed to modify maladaptive cognitions that may contribute to the under recognition of sleep problems or an inadequate response to them [15], consistent with CBT-I cognitive restructuring principles.

To facilitate the translation of knowledge into daily practice, complementary educational resources were made available as handouts, posters, and visual reminders, reinforcing CBT-I-informed sleep hygiene principles.

An audio booth was also installed, offering professional caregivers access to a curated selection of sleep-related podcasts. The content included both activating podcasts, providing educational insights into the science of sleep and its relationship to aging and health, and relaxing podcasts featuring slow-paced narratives and calming soundscapes designed to promote relaxation. Episodes varied in length to accommodate preferences and time constraints, offering a low-threshold, multimedia supplement to the written resources.

Finally, four supervision sessions were organised over a 16-week period. These meetings allowed care teams to discuss challenging sleep-related cases, share practical experiences, and reflect on the application of non-pharmacological interventions, thereby supporting the implementation of CBT-I-informed practices in daily care.

Relaxation training

To support caregiver well-being and promote a calm ward environment, a structured relaxation workshop was implemented. Reducing stress and physiological arousal among caregivers contributes to conditions conducive to residents’ sleep.

In line with CBT-I principles targeting hyperarousal, the workshop focused on body-oriented and sensory strategies. Topics included managing racing thoughts, promoting physical relaxation, breathing awareness, the use of calming sounds (e.g., music), aromatherapy, grounding through foot awareness, and the value of time spent outdoors to stimulate natural light exposure. These techniques were presented as practical tools to help regulate arousal levels during day and evening shifts.

Environmental and organisational adaptation to support sleep–wake regulation

This component aimed to optimise the physical and organisational environment of the ward to support healthy sleep–wake regulation, consistent with CBT-I principles of stimulus control and circadian rhythm alignment.

Six months prior to the start of the intervention, two separate participatory 24-h observations were conducted by two researchers on different days, capturing care routines, environmental conditions, and activity patterns across a full day-night cycle. In addition, light measurements were carried out at night in the living areas using a spectral light meter (BTS256-EF WiFi), to assess exposure to artificial light. The lighting spectrum was measured at eye level (1.2 m) at all possible seating positions of the residents. From these measured spectra, the melanopic equivalent daylight illuminance (melanopic EDI) at the residents’ point of view was calculated in accordance with CIE S026/E:2018 [30]. The melanopic EDI is a quantity used to evaluate the non-image-forming effects of light, such as circadian entrainment and alertness [31].

These observations served as the foundation for structured team-based dialogue, enabling critical reflection on routines, environmental conditions, and underlying care assumptions in relation to sleep–wake regulation.

Individualised non-pharmacological sleep care

Individualised sleep interventions were implemented for NH residents classified as poor sleepers. Interventions were chosen according to need-based care principles [25], and aligned with CBT-I strategies such as sleep hygiene optimisation, stimulus control, and reduction of physiological and cognitive arousal.

The trial contributed to a further refinement of the need-based framework, including the addition of outdoor moments (Supplementary Table 1).

For aromatherapy, chamomile, an essential oil known for inducing relaxation and drowsiness and for enhancing GABA receptor activity, was used. For aromatherapy via massage, anthemis nobilis flower oil was used, diluted to a 17% solution in helianthus annuus seed oil. For inhalation and skin application, a skin roller was used with helianthus annuus seed oil diluted in a combination of helianthus annuus seed oil and simmondsia chinensis seed oil [32].

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed separately for PSQI and NPI-NH.

Demographics, medical diagnoses, and Katz scores at baseline are summarised for control and intervention groups using mean and standard deviation for the numeric variables and percentages for the categorical variables. To compare these values between control and intervention group, linear mixed models were used for the continuous variables and generalised linear mixed models for the categorical variables. A random intercept for NH was included in all models to account for clustering within NHs.

To examine whether the psychometric medication use evolved differently over time between control group and intervention group, a logistic mixed model was used with time, group and the interaction effect between time and group as fixed effects, and with subject and NH as random intercepts. When significant interaction effects were identified, post hoc analyses were performed.

PSQI and NPI-NH outcomes were analysed to assess whether they evolved differently over time between control and intervention group. Mixed models were used with time, group and the interaction effect between time and group as fixed effects, and subject and NH as random intercepts. Where baseline analyses indicated differences between control and intervention group in demographics, medical diagnoses, Katz score or medication use, the corresponding variables were added as covariates. For the NPI-NH outcomes and the total PSQI score linear mixed models were used, and for the categorical PSQI outcomes generalised linear mixed models were used.

Where significant interaction effects were identified, between-group comparisons per time point (control versus intervention), and within-group comparisons across time points (baseline versus follow-up) were performed.

All analyses were done in JMP® version 18.2.1 (JMP Statistical Discovery LLC, Cary, NC, USA) and SAS OnDemand for Academics (SAS Institute Inc. 2025, Cary, NC, USA). Significance level was set at 5%.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The trial was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of Antwerp University Hospital (18 December 2023/B3002023000194). Written informed consent to participate was obtained from all individual participants or their legal guardians.

No statistically significant differences were observed between the control and intervention groups regarding demographics, medical conditions, or functional dependence as assessed by the Katz score. Psychotropic medication use did not evolve differently over time between control and intervention groups and none of the interaction effects between time and group were significant. A significant difference was found in benzodiazepine use at baseline (p = 0.047), with 57.0% of residents in the control group using benzodiazepines compared with 35.6% in the intervention group (Table 2).

NH residents with significant cognitive or language impairment

A total of 116 NH residents were included in the analysis, with 64 in the control group and 52 in the intervention group (INT). Of these residents, 69.8% were female, and the mean age was 87.2 years (SD ± 7.9). A neurodegenerative disorder, defined according to DSM-5 diagnostic criteria, was present in 54.4% of residents and 7.9% had a history of an episode with depression. Functional dependence according to the Katz score showed that 13.8% of residents were fully dependent for transfer and mobility, and 27.9% were fully dependent for toilet use (Table 2).

No statistically significant differences were observed between the control and intervention groups regarding demographics, medical conditions, functional dependence as assessed by the Katz score and psychotropic medication use (Table 2).

Environmental and organisational adaptation to support sleep–wake regulation

Following the 24-h observations, the multidisciplinary team in each NH jointly assessed three core domains: (1) sleep hygiene, with a focus on daytime activation and routine stability; (2) the sensory environment, including light, sound, temperature, and comfort; and (3) the dynamic balance between tension, stimulation and relaxation throughout the day and evening. This process resulted in a collaboratively developed action plan, defining concrete improvements as well as short- and long-term goals to enhance sleep-supportive care on the ward.

Several structural interventions were implemented in the two NHs in the intervention group. In NH A, residents who rarely went outside were scheduled for weekly outdoor visits, and family members were encouraged to ensure at least two outdoor sessions per week. Evening snacks were offered to address potential hunger and promote comfort before bedtime, while meals were organised in smaller groups to reduce overstimulation and support calmer dining experiences. Staff were made aware of unnecessary noise, particularly in the kitchen, and a team agreement was established to minimise auditory disruptions during ongoing activities. In NH B, nighttime light intensity in all corridors was reduced by one-third by decreasing the number of light sources. During evening meals, decaffeinated coffee was offered, intake interviews with new residents and their families included questions regarding sleep habits, and doors that previously produced excessive noise were modified to reduce auditory disturbances.

Despite these adjustments, observations and measurements revealed that lighting levels in the living areas were insufficient to produce meaningful positive effects on residents’ sleep, behaviour, or mood. The maximum average melanopic EDI measured at eye level was 120 lx, with most spaces registering below 100 lx and many even below 50 lx. These levels fall short of recommended values which suggest 250 lx melanopic EDI in the morning and during the day, with ideal morning levels for older adults being even a factor 2–4 higher [31]. Consequently, residents were exposed to inadequate light, indicating a need for further environmental adjustments to optimise sleep-supportive conditions.

Individualised non-pharmacological sleep care

Overall in the PSQI group, 34 residents (27.4%) were classified as poor sleepers, including 18 (40.0%) in the intervention group. In the NPI group, 18 residents (15.5%) were classified as poor sleepers, with 11 (21.2%) in the intervention group. Multidisciplinary analyses of the 29 poor sleepers in the intervention group identified unmet needs in 79.3% of the residents, poor sleep hygiene in 62.1%, a physical cause in 48.3%, and environmental disturbances in 31.0%. A sense of security was found to be the most identified unmet need (62.1%), followed by a sense of continuity (27.6%). In most cases, more than one unmet need was identified. Tailored interventions were applied to the 26 residents in which sleep problems were identified. Aromatherapy (34.5%) and outdoor moments (31.0%) were the most frequently prescribed non-pharmacological treatments (Supplementary Fig. 1).

PSQI group

In the analysis of the PSQI outcomes, benzodiazepine use was included as covariate because a statistically significant difference at baseline was observed between control and intervention groups.

The evolution over time did not differ significantly between the control and intervention groups for any of the subjective sleep parameters: sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, sleep disturbance, and daytime dysfunction (Table 3). However, a significant difference in the evolution of sleep medication use over time was observed between the control and intervention groups (p = 0.032). At follow-up, both the control and intervention groups exhibited a bimodal distribution of sleep medication use. The intermediate categories (1 and 2) were no longer represented in either group, contrasting with the more evenly distributed use at baseline.

Analysis also showed a significant interaction effect between time and group for the total PSQI score (p = 0.031). Post hoc analyses showed no significant difference between the control and intervention groups (p = 0.671) at follow-up. However, the total PSQI score improved in both groups, and the improvement was more pronounced in the intervention group. NPI-NH group

No statistically significant interaction between group and time was observed for nighttime behaviour changes, anxiety, euphoria, sleep, or appetite and eating changes.

In contrast, significant interaction effects were observed for hallucinations (p = 0.001), aberrant motor behaviour and apathy (both p = 0.006), delusions (p = 0.008), depression and disinhibition (both p = 0.016), and agitation/aggression, irritability (p < 0.001), total NPI score, and total emotional distress (all p < 0.001) (Table 4). Specifically, the intervention group showed a significant decrease in delusions over time, with a mean reduction of 0.84 (with 95% CI [0.18,1.52]) from baseline to follow-up (p = 0.013). The control group exhibited a significant increase in hallucinations, with a mean rise of 0.79 (with 95% CI [-1.19, -0.38]) (p < 0.001).

At baseline, the control group scored significantly higher than the intervention group for agitation/aggression (mean difference = 0.96 with 95% CI [-1.82, -0.10], p = 0.029), but this difference was no longer statistically significant at follow-up (p = 0.071). Over time, the control group showed a mean increase of 0.89 (with 95% CI [-1.48, -0.22]) in agitation/aggression (p = 0.004), while the intervention group showed a mean decrease of 0.86 (with 95% CI [0.20, 1.53]) (p = 0.012). Depression significantly decreased over time in the intervention group (mean reduction = 0.77 (with 95% CI [0.38, 1.16]), p < 0.001), whereas apathy significantly increased in the control group (mean increase = 0.87- (with 95% CI [− 1.25, − 0.49]), p < 0.001). Disinhibition decreased significantly in the intervention group (mean reduction = 0.64 (with 95% CI [0.12, 1.17]), p = 0.016). Although irritability did not differ significantly between groups at follow-up (p = 0.062), the control group showed a mean increase of 1.01 (with 95% CI [− 1.34, − 0.67]) over time (p < 0.001). Aberrant motor behaviour decreased significantly in the intervention group (mean reduction = 1.21 (with 95% CI [0.44, 1.98]), p = 0.002).

For the total NPI-NH score, the difference between groups at follow-up was not statistically significant (p = 0.050), yet over time the control group exhibited a mean increase of 6.02 (with 95% CI [− 9.36, − 2.69]) (p < 0.001), while the intervention group showed a mean decrease of 5.66 (with 95% CI [1.96, 9.35]) (p = 0.003). Finally, there was a statistically significant decrease in total emotional distress in the intervention group (mean decrease = 1.59 (with 95% CI [0.22, 2.95]), p = 0.003) over the trial period while there was a statistically significant increase in this outcome in the control group (mean increase = 2.00 (with 95% CI [− 3.23, − 0.77]), p = 0.002) (Table 4 and Fig. 2).

Discussion

This cluster randomised trial investigated the effectiveness of a structured, team-delivered intervention based on CBT-I, combining structural changes and individual-level strategies, to improve sleep quality in NH residents. In this trial, 52 of 240 residents (21.7%) were identified as poor sleepers, most with multifactorial sleep problems, highlighting the complexity of sleep disturbances in this population. This prevalence is lower than reported in previous research; a meta-analysis of 52 studies including 9,308 institutionalised older adults reported a pooled prevalence of poor sleep quality of 65% [6]. A possible reason for this discrepancy is the operationalisation of 'poor sleep'. Whereas most studies in the meta-analysis used the PSQI global score (> 5), our trial applied a stricter operational definition of poor sleep for cognitively intact or mildly impaired residents, identifying poor sleep when residents reported either (1) fairly bad or very bad overall sleep quality, or (2) daytime sleepiness at least once or twice per week. This difference likely contributed to the lower prevalence in our sample, underscoring the influence of measurement criteria on reported prevalence in NH populations.

No statistically significant differences were observed between the intervention and control groups on PSQI scores at T1. This contrasts with research in community-dwelling older adults without severe mental disorders, where cognitive interventions have resulted in significant differences between intervention and control group [33]. A key difference is that the present trial targeted frailer, institutionalised residents, a population underrepresented in previous trials. This discrepancy may also reflect the contextual constraints of the NH environment, where residents have limited personal agency, follow fixed routines, experience nighttime interruptions, and are highly dependent on staff. Such factors complicate the consistent application of CBT-I. These contextual barriers align with qualitative findings, highlighting the impact of noise, light exposure, unfamiliar surroundings, and resident-staff relationships on sleep [34].

Beyond these contextual barriers, several intervention-specific factors may have further contributed to the absence of significant between-group differences. The intervention did not include sleep restriction therapy, one of the most evidence-based CBT-I components for improving sleep continuity and efficiency [17, 35], and the absence of trained therapists limited the delivery of cognitive restructuring strategies [10]. Additionally, the relatively low prevalence of poor sleepers at baseline (27.4% in the PSQI group) and the small intervention group (n = 45) may have limited statistical power to detect modest between-group effects.

Although no between-group differences were observed at T0 and T1, a significant difference in change over time was found. Both groups showed improvements in overall sleep quality over time, with a more pronounced improvement in the intervention group. The fact that sleep quality also improved in the control group may reflect the influence of trial participation itself. Completing the PSQI and engaging in conversations about sleep likely increased residents' awareness of their sleep habits, stimulated reflection on factors affecting sleep, and may have prompted behavioural adjustments, even in the absence of a formal intervention.

In the NPI-NH group no significant changes in sleep and nighttime behaviour were observed. This contrasts with the MoNoPol Sleep study, in which the intervention led to a reduction in sleep problems among NH residents with dementia [36]. The absence of a significant intervention effect in the present trial may be explained by several factors. First, as noted above, the strict classification criteria applied in this group may have resulted in an under identification of residents with clinically relevant sleep problems, limiting the potential for detectable improvement. Second, and perhaps more fundamentally, sleep disturbances in residents with severe cognitive impairment are often neurobiological in nature, reflecting progressive degeneration of the suprachiasmatic nucleus and reduced melatonin secretion associated with advancing dementia [37]. Such neurobiological changes may render sleep disturbances less responsive to behavioural interventions alone, suggesting that pharmacological or light-based approaches may need to be combined with behavioural strategies in this population. Finally, in the MoNoPol-Sleep study [36], each NH appointed two dedicated sleep-nurses ensuring consistent implementation, and poor sleepers were classified using the Sleep Disorders Inventory rather than the NPI-NH, which may have allowed for more sensitive identification of sleep problems.

The present findings suggest that targeting sleep through a structured, team-based intervention may have broader effects on behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD). In the NPI-NH group, improvements were observed across several domains, including reductions in delusions, agitation/aggression, depression, disinhibition and aberrant motor behaviour, as well as decreased caregiver emotional distress and lower total NPI-NH scores.

These findings are consistent with evidence linking sleep disturbance to psychotic symptoms. For example, Freeman et al. (2015), in a randomised, assessor-blind trial of CBT for insomnia in patients with delusions and hallucinations, reported that improvements in insomnia were accompanied by reductions in delusions and hallucinations, supporting sleep disturbance as a potential transdiagnostic treatment target [38]. Sleep disturbances often occur early in the course of illness and may even precede diagnosis, highlighting their relevance as an early intervention target [39]. In nursing home populations, psychotic experiences affect up to 22% of residents and contribute substantially to burden [40].

This pattern suggests potential spillover effects of the sleep-focused intervention on neuropsychiatric symptom domains beyond sleep, particularly in relation to psychotic and affective symptoms. More broadly, the observed reductions in depression suggest that sleep-focused interventions may have wider mental health benefits in nursing home residents, consistent with evidence linking insomnia to comorbid psychiatric symptoms [41, 42].

Interpretation of our findings must consider several methodological aspects.

First, some limitations relate to measurement approaches. The NPI-NH was administered only to residents with significant cognitive or language impairments, which may have limited the comprehensiveness of behavioural assessment across the full sample. Including proxy-reported behavioural data for cognitively intact or mildly impaired residents could have provided a more complete picture and enabled comparisons between self- and proxy-reported outcomes within the same cognitive groups.

In addition, sleep in the cognitively impaired group was assessed using a single NPI-NH item, which may have lacked sensitivity to detect subtle changes in sleep behaviour. Although objective measures such as actigraphy could have provided more detailed insights, their use was not feasible within the practical and resource constraints of the nursing home setting. Moreover, emerging evidence suggests that actigraphy may not reliably distinguish individuals with insomnia from good sleepers [43]. Given that subjective improvement is often the primary treatment goal in insomnia, future research should consider integrating both subjective and objective assessments, while balancing their feasibility and clinical relevance [44]. As no gold standard currently exists for assessing sleep problems, a triangulation approach, combining self-report, proxy ratings, and observations by trained blinded assessors, has been recommended to enhance accuracy and ecological validity, particularly in institutional settings [36].

Second, potential sources of bias should be considered. Researcher blinding was implemented for PSQI assessments but not for NPI-NH outcomes, which may have introduced bias in behavioural ratings. Lack of blinding increases the risk of expectancy effects, whereby knowledge of group allocation may unconsciously influence symptom scoring. Consequently, some observed improvements in NPI-NH scores may partly reflect rater expectations rather than true behavioural changes. The use of blinded assessors for behavioural outcomes would strengthen internal validity in future studies.

Third, environmental factors may have influenced the results. A strength of the study is that both measurement points were scheduled in February, minimising potential confounding from seasonal variation in sleep patterns [45]. Objective light measurements provided additional context, although they did not fully capture individual differences in exposure. For example, residents located near windows may have received more daylight than recorded, while overall winter light exposure was likely limited. These considerations highlight the importance of accounting for environmental factors such as light, noise, and daily routines when evaluating sleep interventions in nursing home settings.

Finally, design-related considerations should be acknowledged. Cluster randomisation at the nursing home level resulted in two intervention and three control sites. While including both intervention and control conditions within each facility could have improved environmental comparability, this approach would have introduced a substantial risk of contamination, as care staff may transfer intervention-related practices across units. For behavioural and care-based interventions such as CBT-I-informed approaches, contamination between groups is a well-recognised methodological concern, making cluster-level randomisation at the facility level the preferred design. A random intercept for nursing home was included in the analyses to account for clustering effects. Nevertheless, the unequal distribution of sites across conditions represents a limitation, and future studies with a larger number of facilities should aim for a more balanced allocation.

Taken together, these findings suggest that a structured, team-delivered intervention based on CBT-I holds promise for reducing neuropsychiatric symptoms and caregiver emotional distress in nursing home residents, even in the absence of significant between-group differences in sleep outcomes. Future studies could build on this approach by integrating structural modifications, individualised non-pharmacological care, caregiver training in cognitive and behavioural strategies, and ongoing supervision, to explore whether such interventions can achieve dual benefits for both sleep quality and neuropsychiatric outcomes in this population.

Conclusion

This trial indicates that a structured, team-delivered intervention based on CBT-I did not result in significant improvements in primary sleep outcomes among nursing home residents. Exploratory findings suggest that the intervention may positively influence BPSD. These findings are hypothesis-generating and provide a rationale for future trials specifically designed to examine the effect of sleep-focused interventions on BPSD.
