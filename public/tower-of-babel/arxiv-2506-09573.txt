Probability-One Optimization of Generalized Rayleigh Quotient Sum For Multi-Source Generalized Total Least-Squares
Dominik Friml, Pavel Václavek

Abstract
This paper addresses the global optimization of the sum of the Rayleigh quotient and the generalized Rayleigh quotient on the unit sphere. While various methods have been proposed for this problem, they do not guarantee convergence to the global maximizer. To overcome this limitation, we introduce a probability-one homotopy optimization method that, under certain conditions, guarantees convergence to the global maximizer. The proposed method is analyzed alongside state-of-the-art approaches through numerical experiments, evaluating their performance in terms of convergence speed and ability to reach the global maximizer. Furthermore, we demonstrate how this ties in with the multi-source Bayesian Generalized Total Least-Squares (B-GTLS) problem, illustrating its applicability.

This problem arises in numerous practical applications, e.g. in the downlink of a multi-user MIMO system

II
APPLICATION

The Bayesian Generalized Total Least-Squares (B-GTLS) is a Bayesian approach that efficiently approximates and addresses limitations of existing errors-in-variables (EIV) methods. It enables recursive EIV identification with noise across all variables and provides an approximate posterior distribution of the identified parameters. This method was progressively developed in
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
, with the latest work extending it to EIV identification from multiple data sources.

To formally define the EIV problem solved by B-GTLS, we consider a linear model:

\displaystyle 0=\overline{d}_{i}^{T}\vartheta,

(3)

where
\overline{d}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
is the
i
-th data sample, and
\vartheta=[\tau^{T},\,-1]^{T}
, with
\tau
being the vector of unknown parameters.

The fundamental equation of linear EIV arises when noisy samples are modeled as
d_{i}=\overline{d}_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}
, assuming additive, independent, identically distributed Gaussian noise
\varepsilon
with zero mean and covariance
\Sigma
.

From
[
11
, Lemma 2]
, the likelihood function of B-GTLS is:

\displaystyle\likelihood(\tau|D,\Sigma)\propto\exp\left(-\dfrac{1}{2}\frac{\vartheta^{T}\left(D^{T}D\right)\vartheta}{\vartheta^{T}\Sigma\vartheta}\right),

(4)

where
D\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times n}
is constructed from transposed data vectors
d_{i},\,i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
stacked underneath each other.

Given a normal prior
p(\tau)=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{p},\Sigma_{p})
, the posterior distribution is approximated as:

\displaystyle p(\tau|D,\Sigma)\approx q(\tau|D,\Sigma)p(\tau)=\mathcal{N}\left(\tau^{*},\hat{\Sigma}(\Omega,\tau^{*})\right)p(\tau),

(5)

where
q(\tau|D,\Sigma)
is the Laplace likelihood approximation,
\tau^{*}
is the mode of the likelihood function, and

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}^{-1}(\Omega,\Sigma,\tau^{*})=\frac{2}{\alpha(\Sigma,\tau^{*})}\Omega_{11}+\frac{8\alpha(\Omega,\tau^{*})}{\alpha(\Sigma,\tau^{*})^{3}}\Gamma(\Sigma,\Sigma,\tau^{*})

(6)

\displaystyle-\frac{4}{\alpha(\Sigma,\tau^{*})^{2}}\left({\Gamma(\Omega,\Sigma,\tau^{*})}^{T}+\Gamma(\Omega,\Sigma,\tau^{*})\right)-\frac{2\alpha(\Omega,\tau^{*})}{\alpha(\Sigma,\tau^{*})^{2}}\Sigma_{11}

is the Hessian of the negative log-likelihood evaluated at
\tau^{*}
, with:

\displaystyle\alpha(A,x)

\displaystyle=x^{T}A_{11}x-2a_{12}^{T}x+a_{22},

(7)

\displaystyle\Gamma(A,B,x)

\displaystyle=A_{11}(xx^{T})B_{11}-A_{11}xb_{12}^{T}-a_{12}x^{T}B_{11}+a_{12}b_{12}^{T}.

While effective for standard EIV problems, challenges arise when handling data from multiple sources. In such cases, the joint likelihood is given by:

\displaystyle\likelihood(\tau|D_{1},\Sigma_{1},D_{2},\Sigma_{2})=\likelihood(\tau|D_{1},\Sigma_{1})\likelihood(\tau|D_{2},\Sigma_{2}).

(8)

If
\tau

[

]
, but this may introduce errors in the general case. Computing a Laplace approximation of (

\displaystyle\tau^{*}:=\operatorname*{argmax}_{\tau}\quad\ln\left(\likelihood(\tau|D_{1},\Sigma_{1},D_{2},\Sigma_{2})\right).

(9)

Since the objective function in (
9

\vartheta

, and impose
\|x\|=1

1

Defining
\xi=Rx^{*}
, where

is the solution of (
1

\displaystyle\tau^{*}=-\frac{\xi_{12}}{\xi_{22}}.

(10)

The posterior distribution is then approximated as:

\displaystyle p(\tau|D_{1},\Sigma_{1},D_{2},\Sigma_{2})\approx\mathcal{N}\left(\mu_{\tau},\Sigma_{\tau}\right),

(11)

where:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\tau}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\hat{\Sigma}^{-1}(\Omega_{1},\Sigma_{1},\tau^{*})+\hat{\Sigma}^{-1}(\Omega_{2},\Sigma_{2},\tau^{*})+\Sigma_{p}^{-1},

(12)

\displaystyle\mu_{\tau}

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{\tau}\left(\hat{\Sigma}^{-1}(\Omega_{1},\Sigma_{1},\tau^{*})\tau^{*}+\hat{\Sigma}^{-1}(\Omega_{2},\Sigma_{2},\tau^{*})\tau^{*}+\Sigma_{p}^{-1}\mu_{p}\right).

III
RIEMANNIAN ANALYSIS

The constrained optimization problem can be reformulated as an unconstrained Riemannian optimization problem. Considering the unit sphere
\mathcal{S}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{q}\mid\|x\|=1\}
as a submanifold of the Euclidean space
\mathcal{M}:=\mathbb{R}^{q}
, let
f:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the smooth Riemannian objective function:

\displaystyle f(x):=\frac{\phi_{b}(x)}{\phi_{w}(x)}+{\phi_{d}(x)},

(13)

where
\phi_{b}(x):=x^{T}Bx
,
\phi_{w}(x):=x^{T}Wx
, and
\phi_{d}(x):=x^{T}Dx
.

To efficiently solve this Riemannian optimization problem, we employ the Riemannian Trust-Region (RTR) method, which utilizes both first- and second-order information of
f(x)
, ensuring local superlinear convergence. Before deriving the RTR method, key geometric components are introduced.

Following
[
12
]
, the Riemannian metric on
\mathcal{S}
is
\langle a,b\rangle_{x}=a^{T}b
restricted to the tangent space
\mathcal{T}_{x}\mathcal{S}:=\{z\mid z=P_{x}y,\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{q}\}
, where
P_{x}=I_{q}-xx^{T}
is the orthogonal projection onto the tangent space. The projection onto the normal space at
x
is
P_{x}^{\bot}=xx^{T}
. Furthermore, let us define the matrix

\displaystyle E(x):=B-\frac{\phi_{b}(x)}{\phi_{w}(x)}W+\phi_{w}(x)D,

(14)

importance of which will be explained later, euclidean gradient and euclidean Hessian of
f(x)

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(x)=\frac{2}{\phi_{w}(x)}E\left(x\right)x,

(15)

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(x)=\frac{2E\left(x\right)}{\phi_{w}(x)}+

\displaystyle\frac{8\phi_{b}(x)Wxx^{T}W}{\phi_{w}(x)^{3}}-\frac{4\left({\mathrm{Bxx}}^{T}W-{\mathrm{Wxx}}^{T}B\right)}{{\phi^{2}_{w}(x)}},

(16)

respectively. This allows for derivation of Riemannian gradient
[
12
]

\displaystyle\mathrm{grad}\;f(x)=P_{x}f^{\prime}(x)=f^{\prime}(x)-2\phi_{d}(x)x

(17)

and Riemannian Hessian
[
13
]

\displaystyle\mathrm{Hess}\;f(x)[h]=P_{x}f^{\prime\prime}(x)h+A_{x}\left(h,P_{x}^{\bot}f^{\prime}(x)\right),

(18)

where
A_{x}(h,P_{x}^{\bot}f^{\prime}(x))=-hx^{T}P_{x}^{\bot}f^{\prime}(x)
is Weingarten map for unit sphere
\mathcal{S}^{q-1}
.

With the prerequisites established, we introduce the Riemannian Trust-Region (RTR) method for solving (
1
). RTR is chosen for its robust global convergence properties and capability to handle negative curvature, ensuring reliable convergence to a stationary point from any initialization.

RTR iteratively minimizes a local quadratic model

\displaystyle m_{x}(h):=f\left(x\right)+\langle h,\mathrm{grad}\;f(x)\rangle_{x}+\tfrac{1}{2}\langle h,\mathrm{Hess}\;f(x)[h]\rangle_{x},

(19)

approximating objective function
f(x)
within a region around the current point. Radius of the region is trust-region radius
\Delta_{\textrm{TR}}\in[\Delta_{\textrm{TR, min}},\Delta_{\textrm{TR, max}}]
, defined in
\tangentspace
.

Trust-region subproblems are usually a minimizing problems
[
14
,
15
]
. In our case, given
m_{x}(h)
and Riemannian metric on
\mathcal{S}
, the trust-region subproblem is expressed as

\displaystyle\begin{split}h^{*}:=\operatorname*{argmin}_{h\in\tangentspace}&\quad h^{T}g_{x}+\tfrac{1}{2}h^{T}H_{x}(h)\\
\textrm{s.t.}&\quad\left\lVert h\right\rVert<\Delta_{\textrm{TR}},\end{split}

(20)

where
g_{x}:=-\mathrm{grad}\;f(x)
,
H_{x}(h):=-\mathrm{Hess}\;f(x)[h]
.

The (approximate) solution
h^{*}
in
\tangentspace
is then retracted

\displaystyle\xi\leftarrow\frac{x+h^{*}}{\|x+h^{*}\|},

(21)

generating a candidate point
\xi\in\mathcal{S}
.

Using the candidate, the agreement between the model and the actual improvement is evaluated via agreement parameter

\displaystyle\rho:=\frac{f(x)-f(\xi)}{m_{x}(0)-m_{x}(h^{*})}.

(22)

The trust-region radius is updated based on this parameter. Finally, if the parameter is smaller than threshold
\underline{\rho}>0
, the model is deemed inaccurate and the candidate is rejected. The complete algorithm is summarized in Algorithm
1
, where
i_{\textrm{max}}
denotes the maximum number of iterations and

is the initial point.

IV
PROPOSED METHOD

While RTR guarantees global convergence, it does not guarantee convergence to the global maximizer. In this section, we introduce our proposed method, which builds on the Probability-One Homotopy Optimization
[
20
,
21
]
. This method enables reliable convergence to the optimum by constructing a homotopy
h:\mathcal{S}\times[0,1]\rightarrow\real
between a trivial function
g(x)
with a known (or trivial) solution
x^{*}_{g}
and the optimized function
f(x)
:

\displaystyle h(x,\lambda):=\lambda f(x)+(\lambda-1)g(x).

(23)

As homotopy parameter
\lambda\in[0,1]
varies from 0 to 1, the function transitions from the trivial function
h(x,0)=g(x)
to the target function
h(x,1)=f(x)
. During this transformation, the solutions corresponding to each intermediate value of
\lambda
form a continuous solution trajectory that connects the trivial solution
h(x^{*}_{g},0)
to the solution
h(\hat{x},1)
of the target function.

The homotopy optimization (HOM) algorithm
[
21
]
operates by incrementally solving the optimization problem

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{x}:=\operatorname*{argmax}_{x}&\quad h(x,\lambda)\\
\textrm{s.t.}&\quad\left\lVert x\right\rVert=1,\end{split}

(24)

while progressively increasing the homotopy parameter
\lambda
. This procedure traces the solution trajectory along the homotopy, ultimately converging to the optimum of
f(x)
. Notably,
\lambda
need not evolve linearly from 0 to 1; instead, a homotopy schedule
\lambda(k)
can control its progression. This schedule applies a non-linear transformation of
\lambda
as a function of iteration count, improving convergence with fewer iterations.

Throughout the homotopy optimization process, (
24
) is always initialized near the previous solution, making RTR well-suited due to its superlinear local convergence. Furthermore, if certain conditions hold, the HOM method reaches
h(\hat{x},1)
with probability one
[
22
,
23
]
. These conditions are discussed in the next section. Failing to meet them does not imply failure but introduces a nonzero probability of tracking failure.

Let us conjecture for (
1
), that the global maximizer
x^{*}
is predominantly influenced by either
g_{1}(x):=\frac{\phi_{b}(x)}{\phi_{w}(x)}
or
g_{2}(x):=\phi_{d}(x)
. Consequently, a homotopy where the dominant term serves as a trivial function establishes a connection between its trivial solution
h(x^{*}_{g},0)
and the global optimizer
h(x^{*},1)
. However, since the dominant term is not explicitly identifiable, tracking both solution trajectories corresponding to each term guarantees convergence to the global optimum.

Substitution of individual candidates into (
23
) results in two homotopies

\displaystyle h_{1}(x,\lambda):=\frac{\phi_{b}(x)}{\phi_{w}(x)}+\lambda{\phi_{d}(x)},

(25)

\displaystyle h_{2}(x,\lambda):=\lambda\frac{\phi_{b}(x)}{\phi_{w}(x)}+{\phi_{d}(x)},

(26)

with trivial solutions
x^{*}_{g1}
and
x^{*}_{g2}
, respectively, as maximizing
h_{1}(x,0)
corresponds to computing the eigenvector of the largest eigenvalue of the matrix pencil
(B,W)
, while maximizing
h_{2}(x,0)
corresponds to computing the eigenvector of the largest eigenvalue of
D
.

Tracking both solution trajectories is achieved by running two parallel HOM algorithms, with each homotopy initialized with its respective trivial solution. The final solution is selected as the one yielding the highest function value, leading to Algorithm
3
.

V
Probability-One Conditions

Certain conditions, outlined in
[
20
]
and later extended to Riemannian manifolds in
[
24
]
, ensure that the solution trajectory from
h(x^{*}_{g},0)
to
h(\hat{x},1)
is followed with probability one.

Based on
[
24
, Theorem 3.1]
, those conditions are:

\displaystyle\textrm{1}\big)\textrm{rank}\left(\mathrm{Hess}\;h(x,\lambda)\right)=q,\quad\forall(x,\lambda)\in\mathcal{S}\times[0,1],

\displaystyle\textrm{2}\big)\left\lVert\mathrm{Hess}\,h(x,\lambda)^{-1}\!\!\!\left[\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\mathrm{grad}\,h(x,\lambda)\right]\right\rVert\!<\!L,\forall(x,\lambda)\in\mathcal{S}\times[0,1];

where
L>0
is radius of ball on manifold
\mathcal{S}
.

The first condition ensures the solution trajectory remains parameterizable by
\lambda
, while the second maintains the optimization domain within a region where the Riemannian Hessian is full rank.

Verifying these conditions is generally challenging. The similarity between
h(x,\lambda)
and
f(x)
implies via (
18
) that a full-rank Hessian requires at least one of
B
,
W
, or
D
to be full rank, while the remaining terms must contribute independent directions. If this holds, the Hessian remains full rank for all
(x,\lambda)
, ensuring condition two.

In many practical applications, such as B-GTLS,
B
and
D
are typically full rank and linearly independent, which strongly suggests that the condition holds.

If the conjecture in the previous section holds, then satisfying these conditions guarantees that Algorithm
3
(pHOM) attains the global maximizer of (
1
) with probability one.

VIII
CONCLUSIONS

In this work, a probability-one homotopy and Riemannian trust-region optimization-based algorithm, Algorithm
3
(pHOM), is introduced for the global optimization of (
1
). Additionally, state-of-the-art methods are reviewed for comparison. Numerical analysis demonstrates that pHOM achieves 100% convergence to the global optimum across all tested cases, whereas existing methods fail to guarantee such reliability. Although the probability-one conditions do not always hold, and adversarial cases could theoretically prevent global convergence, we were unable to generate such an instance through extensive randomized dataset testing, suggesting strong practical robustness. The algorithm further exhibits scalability and computational efficiency, benefiting from the sustained superlinear convergence of the Riemannian trust-region method throughout the entire optimization process.

This work advances existing research in globally solving (
1
) and extends the method’s applicability by demonstrating its integration within the multi-source Bayesian Generalized Total Least-Squares framework. Future research will focus on further improving computational efficiency, particularly for large-scale problems.
