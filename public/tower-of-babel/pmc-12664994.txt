FABP3 and FABP4 promote lipid peroxidation injury during static cold storage of donor heart: Insights from multi-omics and therapeutic targeting
Authors: Yu Feng, Yongbu Peng, Jincheng Hou, Zihao Wang, Junlin Lai, Tixiusi Xiong, Jiawei Shi, Yixuan Wang, Wai Yen Yim, Yuqi Chen, Nianguo Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo clarify the mechanism of myocardial injury associated with lipid peroxidation and the role of fatty acid binding proteins FABP3/4 during Static cold storage (SCS) of donor hearts.MethodsMulti-omics analysis included mouse myocardial metabolomics and transcriptomics, proteomics of cardiac preservation solution in SCS injury. Lipid peroxidation was detected by malondialdehyde, transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and fluorescent probes. Lipid peroxidation antagonist MitoQ was examined by TEM. FABP3/4 were analyzed by combining the proteomic data of first perfusion serum from human donor hearts with those of mouse myocardial SCS solution. Overexpressing FABP3/4 were evaluated by detecting ROS and apoptosis via flow cytometry. snRNA-seq revealed the cellular landscape of SCS injury in pig cardiomyocytes.ResultsMulti-omics analysis of the SCS injury model revealed significant changes in lipid metabolism. Disturbed lipid metabolism and peroxidation were demonstrated in mouse SCS injury model and cell model. MitoQ attenuated lipid peroxidation and antagonized cardiac SCS injury. FABP3/4 were released during SCS and promoted injury in cardiomyocytes. In SCS solution, FABP3/4 expression were improved in both short-term and long-term SCS. In myocardial tissue, FABP3 expression decreased during short-term SCS and then gradually increased after long-term SCS. FABP4 expression were first upregulated and then decreased, remaining stable during long-term SCS. Pig cardiomyocytes showed low FABP3 and high FABP4 expression during short-term SCS.ConclusionDisturbed lipid metabolism and peroxidation occur during SCS of hearts. FABP3/4 promote lipid uptake and aggravate SCS damage. FABP3/4 should be further investigated as potential therapeutic targets for SCS injury during heart transplantation.Keywords: FABP3, FABP4, Static cold storage, Heart transplantation, Lipid peroxidation, Lipid metabolism

Introduction

Heart transplantation is an effective surgical treatment for end-stage heart failure and the number of procedures is increasing every year [1,2]. Recent reports have showed that the global amount of heart transplants in 2023 is 10,121 with a 13 % increase compared to 2022. The median survival of modern-day patients has increased to 12.5 years, with a median survival of 14.8 years for those who survived the first 12 months [1,3]. Consequently, it is critical to address the process of donor heart protection. Static cold storage (SCS) is still considered the gold standard for donor heart protection [4]. During SCS, myocardial tissue is in an ischemic state, and metabolites accumulate, which leads to inflammation and tissue damage after reperfusion [5]. SCS affects glucose metabolism, which decreases glycolysis and increases gluconeogenesis due to decreasing glucokinase and phosphofructokinase activity [6]. Additionally, protein metabolism changes significantly during SCS [7].

Apart from the metabolic reprogramming of glucose and proteins, the metabolic reprogramming of lipids and lipid peroxidation are of equal interest. Lipid metabolism is significantly altered in pathways related to mitochondrial β-oxidation of long-chain fatty acids and oxidation of branched-chain fatty acids [8]. Lipid peroxidation is the process by which oxygen radicals, peroxyl radicals and hydroxyl radicals obtain electrons from lipids to produce redox-active intermediates [9]. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are oxygen molecules that have lost electrons. Most intracellular ROS are derived from superoxide radicals, which are mainly formed by NADPH oxidase, xanthine oxidase, and the mitochondrial electron transport chain [[9], [10], [11]].

Fatty acid binding proteins (FABPs), a family of intracellular lipid-binding proteins with a molecular weight of 14–15 kDa, regulate lipid transport and metabolism in cells associated with energy metabolism and inflammatory pathways [12]. FABPs assist in the transport of fatty acids to specific organelles and provide an energy substrate for lipid oxidation in mitochondria and peroxisomes [13]. Similarly, FABPs regulate transcription factors that participate in the regulation of signaling, transport and plasma membrane synthesis [14,15]. FABP3, also known as heart FABP, is highly expressed mainly in the heart, brain, and skeletal muscle. Clinical studies have suggested that FABP3 is rapidly released during myocardial injury, and that elevated circulating levels of FABP3 are associated with myocardial infarction [16,17]. FABP4, also known as adipocyte-type FABP, is mainly distributed in white adipocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells [18]. It is involved in the process of fat storage and lipid droplet formation, promoting fatty acid storage and triglyceride synthesis in adipocytes [19,20]. In addition, FABP4 is relevant to inflammation and immune regulation, and participates in chronic inflammatory responses by regulating inflammatory factors such as NF-α and IL-6 in macrophages [21,22].

In this study, we explored the potential markers of SCS-induced injury in transplanted hearts and hypothesized that FABP3 and FABP4 play a vital role during cardiac SCS. By using human perfusate proteomics, mouse myocardial metabolomics, transcriptomics, and single-nuclei RNA sequencing data from pig heart sample, as well as DCFH-DA and Bodi-Py fluorescence staining, we found that disturbed lipid metabolism and peroxidation occur during SCS of hearts. Lipophilic antioxidants MitoQ could attenuate this peroxidation and antagonize cardiac SCS injury. And the changes were particularly obvious for FABP3 and FABP4 releasing in this condition, which lead to promote lipid uptake and aggravate SCS damage. Thus, we proposed that FABP3 and FABP4 can be regarded as therapeutic targets for SCS-induced injury with the aim to enhance the efficiency of heart transplantation.

Materials and methods

Animals

C57BL/6 J mice (20–25 g, male), and Sprague–Dawley rats (up to 3 days old) were supplied by Biont Biological Technology Company, Hubei, China. All animals were housed nonspecific-pathogen-free animal facilities. The animal experimental procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee on Animal Care of Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology [2023] 4113. C57BL/6 J mice were used for mouse heart SCS-induced injury model building. Sprague–Dawley rats were used to extract primary cardiomyocytes, which were stained with fluorescent DCFH-DA and Bodi-Py.

Ethical guidelines for human heart transplantation

All donor hearts were allocated by the China Organ Transplant Response System according to Chinese laws, which has committed towards the use of voluntary donation after brain death for heart transplantation. Our study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology [2023] 0291 and performed in accordance with the national program for deceased organ donation in China (national protocol for China Category I). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients, and we ensured that our research complied with the Declarations of Helsinki and Istanbul.

Establishment of mouse heart SCS-induced injury model

Male wild-type C57BL/6 J mice were randomly assigned to the SCS groups with cold ischemic time 0, 3, 6, 9, 12 h. Anesthesia was induced with 1 % pentobarbital sodium. Connective tissues overlying the abdominal aorta and inferior vena cava were bluntly dissected. Heparinized saline was injected into the inferior vena cava. After 1 min of systemic heparinization, the abdominal aorta was transected. Bilateral thoracotomy was rapidly performed along the subcostal margin to the sternoclavicular joints. The thymus and pericardium were dissected, and the aorta was isolated and transected. The heart was excised immediately and immersed in ice-cold histidine-tryptophan-ketoglutarate (HTK) solution. The components of 1000 ml HTK include Sodium chloride, Potassium chloride, Potassium hydrogen 2-ketoglutarate, Magnesium chloride, Histidine-HCl-H2O, Histidine, Tryptophan, Mannitol Calcium chloride-2H2O and ddH2O with a specific concentration for each substance (pH 7.02–7.20 at 25 °C, pH 7.4–7.45 at 4 °C, osmolality: 310 mosmol/kg). Retrograde perfusion of ice-cold HTK solution was performed and the heart achieved a softened texture. The heart was rinsed twice with HTK solution and preserved in 2 ml of 4 °C HTK solution with above mentioned SCS time. After preservation, the heart was briefly re-perfused with HTK solution. The perfusate was collected and combined with preservation solution. The myocardium was incised, blotted dry, and divided into specimens. Mixtures were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for subsequent omics analyses and validation experiments.

Extraction of Sprague–Dawley rat primary cells

First, heart and cardiac tissues were isolated and minced. For enzymatic digestion, after brief sedimentation to allow tissue fragments to settle, the supernatant containing released erythrocytes and cellular debris was discarded. Fresh digestion solution was added, and the mixture was gently mixed. The final supernatant was collected with ice-cold termination buffer to halt enzymatic activity. The cell suspension was filtered through a 100-μm mesh to remove residual tissue aggregates and centrifuged. After centrifugation, the pellet was resuspended in culture medium and seeded into 60 mm culture dishes at a density equivalent to two hearts per dish. Cells were subjected to differential adhesion in an incubator at 37 °C, 5 % CO₂ for 90–120 min. The appropriate amounts of cells were inoculated into 0.1 % gelatin-coated culture dishes. After overnight culture, floating cells were aspirated, and fresh complete medium supplemented with bromodeoxyuridine was added to suppress fibroblast proliferation.

Serum proteomic sequencing for the first perfusion of human donor heart

Specimens were collected in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and with the approval of the Ethics Committee of Tongji Medical College of Huazhong University of Science and Technology. All blood samples were collected after obtaining informed consent from patients. The control group specimens were obtained from the preoperative serum of patients who underwent heart transplantation in our hospital (n = 3). The experimental group (n = 5) consisted of patients in the control group whose aorta was opened during heart transplantation to extract the first perfusion serum from the donor heart (n = 3), and combined with two cases of non-self control perfusion serum. The samples were collected and stored in liquid nitrogen, and sent for proteomic sequencing. Data were analyzed using the R language, version 4.05, with differential protein screening criteria of FC > 1.2 and P < 0.05 for basic information on first perfusion fluid proteomics of human donor heart specimens. [2023] 0291.

Cell model of SCS

Cells were cultured to 60–80 % confluency, and then the medium was aspirated. Cells were rinsed with HTK solution (4 °C) twice, and then 1.5 ml HTK solution (4 °C) was added. Cell culture dishes were sealed with sealing film, and incubated at 4 °C to mimic SCS for 0, 3, 6, 9, 12 h.

Transmission electron microscopy

The apical heart tissue was excised and immediately fixed with 2.5 % glutaraldehyde (2 ml) at 4 °C for 24 h, and then post-fixed with osmium tetroxide. Dehydration was performed using a graded ethanol series for 20 min at each concentration, and repeated twice. For infiltration, samples were sequentially incubated at 37 °C with acetone÷epoxy resin mixtures (2:1 for 12 h; 1:1 for 12 h) and pure epoxy resin for an additional 12 h. The infiltrated tissues were then embedded in fresh epoxy resin within embedding molds and polymerized at 60 °C for 48 h. Ultrathin sections were cut using an ultramicrotome. Finally, sections were double-stained with 2 % uranyl acetate in saturated aqueous solution and lead citrate as dye liquid, and then air-dried.

Malondialdehyde detection

MDA Assay Kit from Shanghai Biyuntian Biotechnology Co was used. Following the kit's instructions, prepared the MDA assay working solution and configured 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50 μM standard dilutions by stepwise dilution method. The reaction system was configured by protein lysate/tissue soak, standard sample, sample to be measured and MDA working solution, which was mixed completely and then heated at 100 °C for 15 min. The mixture was cooled to precipitation and turned light red, after which it was centrifuged at 1000 ×g for 10 min. Supernatant (200 μl) was to transferred to a 96-well plate, and absorbance was measured at 532 nm using an enzyme meter. For liquid samples (e.g. plasma, serum and urine), the concentration of malondialdehyde was calculated directly from the standard curve. For solid samples (e.g. cells and tissues), the concentration of malondialdehyde was calculated after lysis, and the malondialdehyde content was normalized to malondialdehyde concentration/sample mass or protein content.

Quantitative real-time fluorescence polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Tissue samples were homogenized with Trizol reagent. After homogenization, chloroform (200 μl) was added, and the mixture was vortexed and incubated. The aqueous phase was transferred to a new RNase-free tube. An equal volume of isopropanol (1:1 ratio) was added, and the mixture was inverted several times. After centrifugation at 5000 ×g for 5 min at 4 °C, a white RNA pellet adhered to the tube wall. RNA concentration was quantified using a spectrophotometer. Reverse transcription was performed according to the selected cDNA synthesis kit. Quantitative PCR was performed using an ABI real-time PCR system with validated primer pairs and cycling conditions as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. The designed primers were shown in the following Table 1.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The mouse heart SCS injury model soaking fluid was used for sample preparation. Standard solutions were prepared via serial dilution. Wash buffer was prepared by diluting the stock solution with deionized water at a ratio of 1:24. Biotinylated antibody and horseradish peroxidase-conjugated working solutions were prepared. ELISA kits were used. The plate was gently agitated, sealed with adhesive film to minimize evaporation, and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Next, biotinylated antibody working solution (100 μl) and substrate solution (90 μl) were added to each well. The plate was incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 15–30 min to allow chromogenic development. The reaction was terminated by adding stop solution (50 μl) to each well. Optical density at 450 nm was measured within 5 min by a microplate reader. A standard curve was generated, and sample concentrations were calculated by interpolating optical density values against the standard curve.

Immunohistochemical staining

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were baked at 60 °C for 30–60 min to enhance adhesion. Slices were dewaxed in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed using EDTA buffer under high-temperature conditions. After cooling to room temperature, excess moisture was removed, and tissue boundaries were outlined. Sections were rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and blocked with 3 % bovine serum albumin for 20 min. Primary antibodies, diluted in appropriate buffer, were applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Following primary antibody incubation, sections were treated with horseradish-peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 30 min. The addition of diaminobenzidine substrate was optimized to prevent overstaining. For nuclear counterstaining, sections were incubated in hematoxylin for 3 min, differentiated in 1 % acid alcohol for 5–10 s, and rinsed again with ddH2O. Dehydration was achieved through a reverse ethanol gradient, followed by xylene clearing. Slides were air-dried and mounted with a non-aqueous medium for microscopic analysis.

Proteomics of cardiac preservation solution of SCS models of mouse donor hearts

SCS fluid obtained from the mouse SCS heart model after 0, 3, 6, 9, and 12 h was sent to Wuhan Medical Laboratory, BGI for proteomic sequencing. The data were analyzed using R language version 4.05. The top 100 proteins were screened and cluster grouped using the impulse DE2 package, and the GO and KEGG databases were used to analyze the data.

Myocardial transcriptomics of SCS models of mouse donor hearts

Tissue RNA was extracted from the mouse heart SCS model after 0, 3, 6, 9, and 12 h. The extracted RNA was sent to Wuhan Medical Laboratory, BGI for transcriptome sequencing. The proposed chronological analysis was performed in R language, version 4.05. The impulse DE2 package was used with a screening condition of P < 0.05. The GO and KEGG databases were used for enrichment analyses.

Flow cytometry

The flow cytometer used in this experiment was made by BD Medical Company America. Cells were dissociated using 0.25 % trypsin until visible detachment of adherent cells was observed. Digestion was terminated by adding an equal volume of complete medium, followed by centrifugation. The dye solution was prepared according to a certain proportion. For dye incubation, supernatant was discarded, and cells were resuspended in fluorescent probe solution (200 μl /35 mm culture area) and incubated at 37 °C. Unbound dye was removed by PBS, and cells were resuspended in PBS. A 40 μm tissue sieve or metal strainer was used to filter larger cell clusters into a flow-through tube. Stained cells were immediately analyzed through a flow cytometer equipped with appropriate laser and filter configurations.

Western blot

Mouse myocardial tissue proteins were extracted, and their concentrations were determined by BCA method. Protein denaturation was conducted, and Tris-MOPS-SDS electrophoresis, membrane transfer, and TBST solutions as well as concentrated and separator gels were prepared. SDS-PAGE electrophoresis and membrane transfer were conducted, after which PVDF membranes were sealed in 5 % skimmed milk powder and placed on a shaker at room temperature for approximately 60 min. Membranes were cut and incubated with primary and secondary antibodies. Developing solution (solution A: solution B = 1:1) was used, and a chemiluminescence instrument collected the signals.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were prepared and fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde. For intracellular antigen detection, cells were permeabilized with 0.1 % Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min. Nonspecific binding was blocked with 5 % goat serum. Cells were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, and unbound primary antibodies were removed with PBS. CST-derived secondary antibodies were diluted 1:1000 in PBS, applied to cells (approximately 100 μl /dish), and incubated in dark for 45 min. Excess secondary antibodies were rinsed off with PBS. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 3 min. Cells were mounted with antifade medium to minimize fluorescence quenching. For fluorescent staining of probes, the steps before formaldehyde fixation were the same as above, and the subsequent staining procedure was the same as that for flow cytometry.

Plasmid extraction

Overnight bacterial cultures were centrifuged. Supernatants were discarded, and cell pellets were resuspended in Buffer P1 (500 μl). Bacterial lysis was initiated by adding Buffer P2 (500 μl). The lysate was incubated for 3–5 min until a clear, viscous solution formed. Neutralization was achieved by adding Buffer E3 (500 μl) and precipitating genomic DNA/proteins. The supernatant was filtered through an Endo-Remover FM column via centrifugation. Isopropanol was added to precipitate plasmid DNA. The solution was mixed by inversion and loaded onto a pre-equilibrated adsorption column. After loading and centrifugation, contaminants were removed by Buffer PW (750 μl). Plasmid DNA was eluted by applying Endo-Free Buffer EB (100–200 μl) directly to the center of the column matrix. The eluted plasmid DNA was quantified by a spectrophotometer (A260/A280 ratio) and stored at −20 °C.

Molecular cloning

The pLKO.1 TRC cloning vector, restriction endonuclease sites and recognition sequences were prepared. Fragments were extracted for amplification in the cDNA library. RNA of the desired tissue was extracted, and PCR was performed with the designed primers to amplify desired genes. DNA recombinase transferred the target gene into the plasmid, which completed transfection. Bacteria were screened, including the process of Escherichia coli activation, inoculation, selection, bacterial amplification, plasmid extraction and virus synthesis. Finally, cells were infected and incubated at 37 °C for 20–24 h. Afterward, the medium was replaced with complete medium containing puromycin.

Oil red staining

H9C2 cells overexpressing FABP3 and FABP4 were incubated with control H9C2 cells in 35 mm dishes overnight, and then the cell culture medium was replaced with fresh normal medium and palmitate-overloaded medium, respectively. Cells were incubated in 5 % CO2 at 37 °C for 24 h, and then fixed with paraformaldehyde. They were then soaked in 60 % isopropyl alcohol for 1–2 min and in hot oil red solution for 10 min. Subsequently, they were rinsed twice and observed under a microscope.

Single-nuclei RNA sequencing data processing and analysis

Single-nuclei RNA sequencing data from pig heart samples subjected to cold ischemia were retrieved from our previous study (cold cardioplegia + HTK group) [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2095927325002476#ak005] and processed using CellBender, a machine learning algorithm designed to mitigate technical artifacts and delineate biological heterogeneity [37592183]. Raw count matrices from CellBender-processed h5 files were aggregated into a Seurat object [29,608,179; 37,231,261]. Further contamination from ambient RNA was addressed using decontX [32138770], and doublet detection was performed with scDblFinder [PMC9204188], retaining singlets for downstream analysis. Quality control (QC) filtering excluded cells with mitochondrial transcript proportions >1 %, low (<800) or high (>6000) gene counts, and genes annotated as mitochondrial, ribosomal, pseudogenes, immunoglobulin-related, RIKEN cDNA sequences, or lacking Entrez IDs. The filtered dataset was normalized, and variable features were identified prior to batch correction using Harmony integration across samples. Dimensionality reduction via PCA and UMAP visualization, combined with graph-based clustering (resolution 0.3), identified transcriptionally distinct cell populations. Annotated cell type labels were assigned to clusters based on canonical markers (e.g., TNNT2 for CMs, COL1A1 for fibroblasts, PECAM1 for endothelial cells). Subsequent analysis focused on cardiomyocyte (CM) subpopulations and dynamic gene expression patterns during cold ischemia. CMs were manually subset and subjected to iterative sub-clustering via UMAP visualization, with interactive cell selection using Seurat's CellSelector to refine populations. Differential expression analysis was performed using TDEsEq. [38622747], a pseudotime-aware method, to identify temporally regulated genes across ischemic timepoints (0 h, 4 h, 8 h). TDEseq parameters included linear modeling (fit.model = “lm”), significance thresholding at 5 % FDR, and batch correction for biological replicates (sample.var. = “rep”). Key lipid metabolism regulators (FABP3, FABP4) were visualized via pseudotime-split expression plots, while global transcriptional trajectories were summarized using hierarchical clustering and heatmap visualization (ComplexHeatmap). To identify genes differentially expressed between early (4 h) and baseline (0 h) as well as late (8 h) and early (4 h) cold ischemia stages in cardiomyocytes, a pairwise comparison was performed using the MAST (Model-based Analysis of Single-cell Transcriptomics) framework within Seurat [26653891]. Cells were grouped by cold ischemia time (cit), and differential expression was assessed using FindMarkers with default MAST parameters, which accounts for zero-inflated transcriptomic data. Significance thresholds were set at adjusted p-value <0.05 and absolute log₂ fold change >0.15. Key lipid metabolism genes (FABP3, FABP4) were highlighted in EnhancedVolcano plots for visual prioritization.

Pathway enrichment analysis

Functional interpretation of temporally dynamic genes in cardiomyocytes was performed using clusterProfiler (v4.6.0) [34557778]. Significant genes (FDR < 0.05) identified by TDEseq were mapped to Entrez IDs via human orthologs (org.Hs.eg.db v3.16.0) to leverage pathway databases. Enrichment analysis was conducted for KEGG pathways and Gene Ontology Biological Processes (GO-BP) using Fisher's exact test with Benjamini-Hochberg correction. KEGG results were filtered to exclude broad “Human Diseases” categories, while GO-BP terms were simplified (semantic similarity cutoff = 0.7) to reduce redundancy. Dot plots visualizing top pathways (KEGG: top 10; GO-BP: top 12) were generated to highlight pattern-specific functional associations, with gene ratios and adjusted p-values annotated. Analyses were restricted to human orthologs due to limited pig-specific pathway databases, a common approach in cross-species functional inference.

Statistics

All the data were analyzed via GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, Inc., CA, USA). All the experiments were performed in triplicate, and the data are presented as frequencies (n) with percentages (%) for categorical variables and as means with standard deviations (means ± SDs) for continuous variables. Differences in baseline characteristics among groups were assessed with chi-square or Fisher exact tests for categorical variables and with two-sided Student's t–tests for continuous variables with normal distributions. For continuous variables that were not normally distributed, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied. Post-transplantation survival analysis was conducted via the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups were evaluated via the log-rank test. P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Myocardial multi-omics analysis of the mouse heart SCS injury model suggests significant changes in lipid metabolism

The generation of SCS injury models of mouse donor hearts and bioinformatics analyses were briefly shown in this flowchart (Fig. 1A). The myocardial metabolomics of the mouse heart during SCS showed consistently upregulated metabolites in the pathways related to mitochondrial β-oxidation of long-chain fatty acids and oxidation of branched-chain fatty acids. This suggested that the pathway was blocked by tissue hypoxia, which leads to the accumulation of metabolic intermediates. Accordingly, downregulated metabolites were enriched in carnitine metabolism, suggesting that carnitine, which is associated with lipid transport, was depleted during SCS of mouse hearts (Fig. 1B).

For myocardial transcriptomics, mRNA was extracted from mouse heart for high-throughput sequencing. According to their trends over time, transcript levels were categorized into four groups: transiently decreased (⁎down), transiently increased (⁎up), continuously decreased (down), and continuously increased (up) (Fig. 1C). The persistently elevated group could be clustered into the pathway related to acylglycerol homeostasis and lipid storage, suggesting that lipid metabolic homeostasis is affected during SCS (Fig. 1D).

The proteomics of cardiac preservation solution were divided into ascending and descending groups according to the change in content with SCS time (Fig. 1E). The blank control HTK solution did not contain the above-mentioned proteins. The proteins in the descending group reached the peak release value within 3 h of SCS and responded rapidly to SCS. Moreover, the descending group could be clustered into reactive oxygen stress, glutathione metabolism and triglyceride catabolism using the GO database (Fig. 1F). In addition, the TOP100 heatmap of proteomics in mouse cardiac preservation solution showed that FABPH and MYC could be vital factors during SCS, which will be described in more detail for the following results (Fig. 1G).

Disturbed lipid metabolism in the SCS injury model of mouse donor hearts and lipid peroxidation damage in the cell model

Changes in transcript levels of key genes of the lipid metabolism pathway were examined by qRT-PCR. The expression of SLC27A1 (FATP), which assists in the transport of lipids from extracellular to intracellular, gradually increased with SCS time, with a peak at 9 h, and then gradually decreased. The expression of ACC1, which is related to lipid synthesis and homeostasis [23], was transiently upregulated, with a peak at 9 h. Furthermore, the expression of PPARα and PPARγ, which are key proteins in lipid metabolism and transport [24], was also transiently upregulated. Additionally, the key proteins of lipid metabolism and transport, e.g. CPT1, CD36, MCD, and ACC2 [25,26], were also upregulated, suggesting that the lipid transport and metabolism pathways were activated at the transcriptional level during SCS (Fig. 2A).

Transmission electron microscopy was used to assess the structure of the left ventricle of cold preserved mouse donor hearts and revealed lipid droplets around the mitochondria after 12 h of SCS (Fig. 2B). This indicates that SCS promotes lipid deposition. The content of malondialdehyde in the SCS solution and mouse heart tissue increased with preservation time, which suggests increased lipid peroxidation in heart tissue (Fig. 2C–D). In rat primary cardiomyocytes, ROS levels (measured by DCFH-DA) increased significantly after 6 h of SCS and started to decrease after 9 h (Fig. 2E).

We hypothesized that the increased ROS levels might oxidize the accumulated lipids. Therefore, we examined lipid peroxidation levels in primary and H9C2 cells during SCS. In primary cardiomyocytes, lipid peroxidation appeared after 3 h of SCS, peaked at 6 to 9 h, and decreased by 12 h. Non-peroxidized lipids decreased at 12 h, and the other changes were not significant. The violin plot illustrated the ratio of fluorescence intensities between Bodipy 510 and Bodipy 581 to normalize the results here (Fig. 2F). Lipophilic antioxidants attenuate lipid peroxidation and antagonize cardiac SCS injury

MitoQ, as the lipid peroxidation antagonist [27], attenuated lipid peroxidation and antagonized cardiac SCS injury. For the rat primary cardiomyocyte SCS injury model, we added MitoQ to HTK preservation solution. The intracellularly generated ROS rise was stimulated by SCS, and the ROS removal efficiency increased with increasing MitoQ concentrations, which was shown in the violin plot based on the ratio of fluorescence intensity in DCFH to the count of cells in Bright. Besides, control group here was the group without SCS, while the 0 group of MitoQ-treated was the group with SCS but without the addition of MitoQ. Thus, the generation of ROS in the group without SCS is less than that in the group with the SCS but without the addition of MitoQ (Fig. 3A). MitoQ significantly inhibited increasing lipid peroxidation levels during SCS. The anti-lipid peroxidation effects were also highly associated with MitoQ concentration. The violin plot illustrated the ratio of fluorescence intensities between Bodipy 510 and Bodipy 581. The level of lipid peroxidation in control group was less than that of 0 MitoQ-treated group, while the level of non-peroxidized lipid was higher than that of 0 MitoQ-treated group. Obviously, these results were attributable to the SCS (Fig. 3B). MitoQ-treated rat primary cardiomyocytes retained more neutral lipids than untreated cardiomyocytes, which was shown in the violin plot based on the ratio of fluorescence intensity in Bodipy 493/503 to the count of cells in Bright to normalize the readout to number of cells. Furthermore, the content of neutral lipid in control group was higher than that of 0 MitoQ-treated group, also due to the SCS causing an increase in the consumption of neutral lipids (Fig. 3C). The increasing lipid peroxidation levels in H9C2 cells after 12 h of SCS were inhibited by MitoQ, and the depletion of non-peroxidized lipids decreased (Fig. 3D). CCK8 experiments demonstrated that MitoQ delayed the decreasing cell activity during SCS (Fig. 3E–F). The addition of MitoQ to the H9C2 cell line and primary rat myocardial cells inhibited increases in ROS, decreased peroxidative lipid synthesis and neutral lipid depletion, and inhibited lipid peroxidation during SCS. At the same time, CCK8 indicted that MitoQ could delay the decrease in cell activity. This indicates that lipid peroxidation plays an important role in SCS injury, and that inhibition of lipid peroxidation can diminish the cardiomyocyte injury.

Discussion

Heart transplantation is an effective surgical treatment for end-stage heart failure. However, it is limited by the scarcity of donors. SCS is still considered the gold standard for donor heart protection [29], and exploring new therapeutic targets in SCS injury is still a vital aspect for transplant surgeons. Our multi-omics bioinformatics analyses of myocardial metabolomics and transcriptomics and proteomics of cardiac preservation solution in a mouse heart SCS injury model showed significantly altered lipid metabolism. Moreover, mouse donor hearts exhibited disturbed lipid metabolism, and the cell model exhibited lipid peroxidation damage during SCS. Moreover, proteomics of human perfusion serum revealed FABP3 and FABP4 as key factors during SCS, which could promote SCS injury in cardiomyocytes. Proteomic sequencing of mouse heart preservation solution showed that both FABP3 and FABP4 proteins were released during SCS. FABP3 peaked after 3 h of preservation, followed by a decrease, suggesting potential degradation of the proteins released during SCS. Apart from the result according to SCS solution, in myocardial tissue, FABP3 transcript and protein levels showed a tendency to decrease to low levels at 3 h, and then gradually increased to the highest levels at 9 h. Further, FABP4 transcript and protein levels were first upregulated to high levels at 3 h and then decreased to stable levels around 9 h. Pig cardiomyocyte snRNA-seq identified FABP3 and FABP4 as pivotal genes with low and high expression at 4 h, respectively.

At present, there is no recognized standard model for cardiac SCS. In our research, we used 4 °C as the general SCS condition for the mouse heart SCS injury model, which simulates SCS for human heart transplantation. Although the metabolic rate at 0–4 °C is 10 % with a preservation time of 4–6 h, 4 °C is still considered the equilibrium between metabolic decreases and preservation time, and this method is relatively simple and easy to realize [30]. Thus, we used HTK solution at 4 °C to perfuse C57BL/6 J mouse hearts and continuously preserved them at 4 °C. Currently, the three most common solutions for cardiac preservation are Histidine-Tryptophan-Ketoglutarate (HTK), University of Wisconsin (UW), and Celsior solution [30]. In a study of adenosine injection prior to cardiac arrest, the investigators used a rat ectopic heart transplant model with Lewis rats as donors. The hearts were perfused with a cold, low-potassium “extracellular” solution (Celsior) and stored at 4 °C for 6 h. However, Celsior solution could not achieve immediate cardiac arrest, which may lead to depletion of cellular energy reserves and impaired myocardial function [31]. Of note, some studies have also used a model of applying a magnetic field for in vitro supercooling preservation of rat hearts at −3 °C. The hemodynamic and metabolic indexes of such methods may be superior to the conventional 4 °C preservation method [32]. This method was also used for 12 h with a porcine heart preservation model at −3 °C [33]. However, this method has limitations that are relatively complex compared with the general method of SCS. Moreover, the hemodynamic situation after reperfusion by this method is still unclear and the clinical application is not yet widespread. Overall, in this research, we used the general feasible method to establish the mouse heart SCS injury model by using 4 °C HTK solution.

ROS play an important role in lipid peroxidation. Under physiological conditions, ROS are normal by-products of cellular metabolism. When ischemia-reperfusion injury occurs, mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation is inhibited, and damage to the oxidative respiratory chain causes massive ATP depletion with large amounts of ROS production [34]. Cell or organelle membranes contain a large number of polyunsaturated fatty acids, which are susceptible to ROS attack. This leads to “lipid peroxidation”, which directly destroys the phospholipid bilayer of cell membranes and can also act as a cell death signal inducing programmed cell death [9,35].

For lipid storage, triglycerides, cholesterol, and phospholipids are the primary components of lipoproteins. Under physiological conditions, fatty acids are the preferred source of energy intake for the heart. The heart obtains fatty acids primarily from celiac particles, very low-density lipoproteins, and albumin-associated non-esterified fatty acids [36]. When hearts undergo pathologic conditions or changes in these metabolic pathways and factors, cardiac lipid storage will be affected by the altered primary energy substrate, and the heart will utilize glucose rather than oxidized fatty acids [36,37]. In this situation, elevated levels of triglycerides and plasma fatty acids lead the absorbance and storage of lipid droplets in the heart. Intramyocardial lipids that exceed storage and oxidative capacity may cause an imbalance between fatty acid uptake and oxidation which leads to cellular lipid accumulation and lipotoxic cardiomyopathies [38]. Therefore, a good balance between lipid uptake, storage, and use is essential for heart homeostasis. We hypothesize that FABP3 and FABP4 play an important role in this process.

FABP3 is a cardiac injury marker with high specificity that is rapidly released during myocardial injury, and elevated FABP3 circulating levels are associated with congestive heart failure and infarction [16,17]. In a mouse infarction model, FABP3 was upregulated in the infarct zone, and FABP3 knockdown could reduce myocardial necrosis and remodeling during infarction [39]. Moreover, FABP3 is involved in fatty acid uptake, transport, and metabolism [40]. It assists in the uptake and translocation of long-chain fatty acids, promotes mitochondrial β-oxidation for energy production, and supports the high energy demand in heart tissue [41,42]. In addition, FABP3 regulates genes related to fatty acid oxidation through PPARα, a pathway that promotes fatty acid oxidation to regulate lipid metabolism [42,43]. FABP4 has been identified as a therapeutic target and marker for ischemic stroke [44], and promotes atherosclerosis and chronic cardiovascular disease by inhibiting myocardial function [20,45]. FABP4 affects adipogenesis and inflammatory responses through PPARγ and NF-κB pathways [46,47]. By contrast, the PPARγ pathway promotes lipid storage for the purpose of regulating lipid metabolism [48], which also differs from FABP3 in the regulation of lipid metabolism as mentioned above. In addition to fat storage, lipid droplet formation, and immune regulation, FABP4 modulates insulin signaling pathways in adipocytes and participates in metabolic syndrome [49,50].

In our study, FABP3 and FABP4 showed meaningful results both in SCS solution and myocardial tissue. According to the time distribution in our study, 0 h, 3–6 h and 8–12 h SCS can respectively represent normal, short-term, and long-term SCS injury of myocardium. In myocardial tissue, FABP3 is more abundant than FABP4, and FABP3 is involved in myocardial injury and considered a myocardial injury marker. Therefore, when the heart is in the compensatory phase of cold ischemia during SCS, FABP3 can be rapidly released into the bloodstream, which explains why FABP3 levels in myocardial tissues decrease during the initial phase of SCS, whereas SCS fluids show higher levels. With the prolongation of SCS and further aggravated ischemic injury of myocardial tissue, the compensatory phase gradually evolves into a non-compensatory phase. This phenomenon is corroborated by persistently elevated FABP3 in myocardial tissue. In addition, because FABP4 is mainly involved in lipid storage, it would be first elevated to cope with the changes in lipid metabolism during SCS. Then, with SCS time and aggravated cold ischemic injury, it exceeds the ability of FABP4 for lipid storage and metabolism. We hypothesize that the cells secreting FABP4 are damaged during this period. Consequently, the content of FABP4 in myocardial tissue decreased. This also explains the result that FABP4 levels initially increase and then decrease in myocardial tissue.

For the limitation of this study, a more integral relationship among FABP3/FABP4, lipid peroxidation and heart transplantation need to be demonstrated in detail. Thus, further refinement of cell and animal experiments and more in-depth explorations such as ischemia-reperfusion models are needed to elucidate the underlying mechanism. Additionally, clinical cases were limited, and thus the sample size must be expanded in future studies.

To summarize, in this study, we investigated cellular mechanisms and protein release during SCS of donor hearts through bioinformatics analysis, animal experiments, in vitro cellular experiments and clinical specimen testing. The main findings include the release of the lipid metabolism-related proteins FABP3 and FABP4, the presence of oxidative stress, lipid metabolism disorders, and lipid peroxidation damage during SCS of donor hearts. Furthermore, the lipophilic antioxidant MitoQ attenuates lipid peroxidation and counteracts SCS injury in cardiomyocytes. Nonetheless, FABP3 and FABP4 promote lipid uptake and induce SCS injury. Lipid peroxidation may be a critical event during cardiac SCS, and lipid peroxidation-related targets such as FABP3 and FABP4 may become potential markers of cardiac SCS injury. Future studies should aim to attenuate cellular SCS injury by inhibiting lipid peroxidation.
