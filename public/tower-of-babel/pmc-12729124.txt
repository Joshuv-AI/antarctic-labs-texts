Cost-minimization analysis of postoperative antibiotic use in high horseshoe anal fistula: evidence from a randomized controlled trial in China
Authors: Xue Li, Yicheng Cheng, Pengyi Xu, Zijian Wei, Chen Li, CongCong Zhi, Xin Li, Ye Yuan, Lihua Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the total societal costs of postoperative antibiotic use compared to non-use in patients undergoing surgery for high horseshoe anal fistula (HHAF), based on a randomized controlled trial.MethodsWe conducted a single-blinded randomized controlled trial in 90 patients with HHAF treated using a standardized loose combined cutting seton (LCCS) procedure at the Department of Colorectal Surgery, China-Japan Friendship Hospital, between January and October 2023. Participants were randomly assigned to either a postoperative Antibiotic group or a No Antibiotic groups. The primary outcome was total societal cost. Clinical outcomes, including wound healing time and early recurrence, were assessed as secondary measures A societal cost-minimization analysis (CMA) was performed, encompassing direct medical costs, outpatient visit costs, and indirect costs due to lost productivity. Patient follow-up was completed by December 2024.ResultsNinety patients were randomized equally (45 per group) with comparable baseline data. The Antibiotic group showed significantly higher hospitalization costs (¥13,202 ± 3,054 vs. ¥9,322 ± 1,205; p < 0.001) and longer stays (12.5 ± 5.8 vs. 7.9 ± 2.4 days; p < 0.001), without differences in wound healing (p = 0.490). Regression confirmed that both incision number (β = 1735.18, p = 0.020) and antibiotic use (β = 3287.06, p < 0.001) independently increased cost, with a significant interaction (β = 3641.00, p = 0.010). Antibiotic use also led to more outpatient visits (5.25 vs. 2.75; p = 0.013) and higher total societal costs (¥9,802 vs. ¥7,931; p = 0.014). Sensitivity analyses under multiple scenarios consistently confirmed higher overall costs in the Antibiotic group.ConclusionRoutine postoperative antibiotics showed no significant additional clinical benefit in terms of wound healing or recurrence in HHAF patients treated with LCCS, yet significantly increase societal costs and postoperative burdens. A selective, evidence-based approach to antibiotic use should be adopted in the surgical management of complex anal fistulas.Trial registrationThis study was retrospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400093477) on December 5, 2024, after the enrollment of participants began in January 2023. The retrospective registration is in accordance with the journal’s editorial policy for studies involving healthcare interventions in human participants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13561-025-00697-6.

Introduction

High horseshoe anal fistula (HHAF) is a complex subtype of anorectal fistula characterized by deep intersphincteric extensions and a high risk of recurrence [1]. The loose combined cutting seton (LCCS) technique has been increasingly employed as a sphincter-preserving approach, providing effective drainage while allowing controlled division of the fistula tract [2]. A targeted literature search of PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus (2020–2025) revealed that, despite advances in surgical techniques, the role of postoperative antibiotics in HHAF management remains controversial. A systematic review by de Jonge et al. (Lancet Infect Dis, 2020) found no significant benefit of continuing postoperative antibiotic prophylaxis in clean-contaminated surgeries, while guidelines from Emory Healthcare (2023) and ASHP (2020) similarly recommend restricting prophylaxis to within 24 h due to limited efficacy and potential antimicrobial resistance risks [3–5]. These findings highlight the ongoing uncertainty surrounding routine antibiotic use after anorectal surgery and form the rationale for our randomized controlled trial.

Routine postoperative antibiotic administration is often based on habit rather than demonstrated necessity [6]. Retrospective studies have reported inconsistent findings: some have observed a reduction in local infection rates with prophylactic antibiotics, whereas others found no significant impact on recurrence or wound healing [7]. Moreover, antibiotics are associated with well-known adverse effects, including gastrointestinal disturbances, allergic reactions, and promotion of antimicrobial resistance [8]. These potential harms, coupled with uncertain clinical benefits, warrant a careful re-evaluation of routine antibiotic use. From a health economics standpoint, the costs of antibiotic use extend beyond the price of the drugs themselves to include managing adverse events, prolonged nursing care, and productivity losses due to recovery time [9].

Despite growing interest in value-based surgery, few studies have systematically evaluated the cost-effectiveness of routine postoperative antibiotics from a comprehensive societal perspective. In this context, a cost-minimization analysis (CMA) is appropriate when clinical outcomes are equivalent, allowing a clear comparison of economic burdens [10]. This randomized controlled trial (RCT) was designed to determine whether omitting postoperative antibiotics in HHAF patients undergoing LCCS surgery can yield substantial cost savings without compromising clinical outcomes. By integrating direct medical, outpatient, and indirect costs, our study provides evidence to inform evidence-based decision-making regarding perioperative antibiotic stewardship in proctologic surgery.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a single-center RCT conducted at the China-Japan Friendship Hospital from January to December 2023. The study was approved by the hospital’s Ethics Committee (Approval No. 2022-KY-121-2). Patient outcomes were monitored through follow-up visits from January to December 2024. Due to administrative delays in early enrollment, the trial was retrospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400093477) on December 5, 2024.

Participants and interventions

A total of 90 patients aged 18–70 years with a confirmed diagnosis of HHAF were prospectively enrolled [11]– [12]. All patients were immunocompetent and eligible for LCCS surgery. Exclusion criteria included immunosuppression, severe comorbidities, or prior anal fistula surgery [13]– [14]. Participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio using a computer-generated sequence to receive either postoperative antibiotics (Antibiotic group) or no antibiotics (control group). The success of the randomization procedure was confirmed by comparing baseline characteristics between the two groups, which showed no significant differences in key prognostic factors, including the number of surgical incisions (Table 1). All surgeries were performed by the same surgical team using a standardized LCCS procedure.

Baseline characteristics

Surgical technique: loose combined cutting Seton (LCCS)

All patients underwent the LCCS surgical technique for fistula management. The procedure was as follows: a probe was inserted through the external opening to trace the fistula tract and identify the internal opening near the dentate line. A radial incision was made from the external opening to the internal opening at the dentate line, completely incising the overlying skin and subcutaneous tissue. A medium-curved hemostat was then advanced from the internal opening to the apex of the fistula tract. Under digital guidance via the rectal lumen, the tip of the hemostat was made to penetrate the rectal wall, creating an internal exit. Four size-10 surgical sutures, tied together at one end, were introduced into the rectal cavity, grasped with the hemostat, and pulled through the fistula tract to establish a “solid thread-dragging” seton fixation. Adjacent fistula tracts or secondary openings were managed with a “hollow thread-dragging” technique using a size-10 suture to facilitate drainage (Fig. 1) [14].Fig. 1Surgical Procedure of Loose Combined Cutting Seton (LCCS). Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the total societal cost, defined as the sum of direct medical costs (hospitalization, medications, laboratory tests, nursing care), outpatient costs (clinic visits, dressing changes), and indirect costs (productivity loss during recovery) [15]. Secondary outcomes included wound healing time, length of hospital stay, number of postoperative outpatient visits, fistula recurrence rates, and any adverse drug reactions.Wound healing time was defined as the duration from surgery to complete epithelialization of the external wound, without discharge or residual cavity, as assessed by direct clinical examination. All follow-up evaluations were conducted weekly by the same attending surgeon until full healing was achieved.

Economic evaluation

We performed a cost-minimization analysis under the assumption of equivalent clinical outcomes between groups. Costs were categorized as direct medical (e.g., medications, laboratory tests, nursing care), outpatient (clinic visits, dressing changes), or indirect (lost productivity).

Direct medical costs (hospitalization, medications, laboratory tests, nursing care) were obtained from the hospital’s financial billing system. Outpatient visit costs and dressing-change expenses were extracted from electronic health records. Indirect costs related to productivity loss were derived from patient-reported questionnaires (PRQs) using a human-capital approach, based on the number of missed workdays multiplied by the national average daily wage of ¥300 according to official statistics. It should be noted that the total societal cost was derived from itemized cost components (direct medical, outpatient, and indirect costs) rather than from the gross hospital billing totals. This approach was used to maintain comparability between cost domains and avoid double-counting across categories. Patients were also asked in the PRQs to report any additional medical visits or treatments received at external facilities (e.g., local clinics or general practitioners). None of the participants reported seeking further professional medical care outside the study center during the follow-up period. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by varying adverse event rates and labor cost assumptions to test the robustness of the cost estimates [15].

Statistical analysis

Mann-Whitney U test was applied for comparisons of continuous variables, and the chi-square test for categorical variables. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0. Outlier exclusion was applied only to a few patients with exceptionally high lost labor or care costs unrelated to study interventions, as these values (exceeding ± 3 SD from the mean) markedly inflated the mean estimates.

Results

Baseline characteristics were comparable between the Antibiotic and No Antibiotic groups (each n = 45). There were no significant differences in age (39.2 ± 10.5 vs. 40.1 ± 9.8 years, p = 0.560), gender distribution (male/female: 28/17 vs. 30/15, p = 0.790), BMI (23.6 ± 2.7 vs. 24.0 ± 3.1 kg/m², p = 0.620), or the proportions of patients who smoked, or had diabetes or hypertension. In addition, the distribution of incision numbers (≤ 2 vs. ≥ 3) was similar between groups (24.4% vs. 22.2% for ≤ 2 incisions, p = 1.000). These findings indicate that randomization achieved good baseline balance across all key characteristics (Table 1).

In the overall randomized cohort (n = 90), patients in the Antibiotic group had significantly higher total hospitalization costs (¥13,202.37 ± 3,053.59 vs. ¥11,321.70 ± 1,204.69; p < 0.001) and longer hospital stays (12.53 ± 5.84 days vs. 7.89 ± 2.41 days; p < 0.001) compared with those in the No-Antibiotic group. Healing time showed no significant difference between the two groups (2.33 ± 0.53 vs. 2.41 ± 0.55; p = 0.490)(Table 2).

Overall, the mean total cost per patient was significantly greater in the Antibiotic group (¥9,802.12 ± 3,464.22) compared to the No Antibiotic groups (¥7,931.00 ± 2,151.31; p = 0.014). Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the cost findings (Table 9; Fig. 4). Under various adjusted scenarios, the Antibiotic group consistently incurred higher total costs than the No Antibiotic groups. For example, even when adverse event management costs were hypothetically reduced by half or when all outpatient costs were excluded, total costs in the Antibiotic group remained greater than in the control group. In a scenario with a 30% increase in productivity loss costs, the Antibiotic group still had higher overall expenses. These results reinforce that routine antibiotic use lacks economic justification when clinical outcomes are equivalent. Figure 5 presents the results of the one-way sensitivity analysis in the form of a tornado diagram. The diagram illustrates the impact of varying each parameter by ± 30% on the total societal cost. The parameters are ordered by their influence on the total cost, with labor cost having the greatest impact, followed by nursing care cost and outpatient cost. These results demonstrate that the base-case conclusions remain robust across all tested scenarios, confirming that variations in the parameters do not significantly alter the overall cost-minimization outcome.

Discussion

Our results collectively suggest a lack of significant clinical benefit and an unfavorable economic profile for routine postoperative antibiotic use in patients with high horseshoe anal fistula (HHAF) undergoing Loose Combined Cutting Seton (LCCS) surgery. These findings are corroborated by a systematic review and meta-analysis by de Jonge et al., which integrated evidence across surgical specialties and reported no significant reduction in surgical site infection risk with extended antibiotic prophylaxis [16]. This perspective is further supported by clinical guidelines from the American Society of Health-System Pharmacists, which generally advise against prolonged postoperative antibiotics in clean-contaminated procedures [6]. Additionally, a randomized controlled trial by Nasasra et al. in a related clinical context—incision and drainage of perianal abscess—found no significant effect of antibiotic therapy on subsequent fistula formation [7]. By contributing Level I evidence specifically within the HHAF population, our study reinforces the view that adjunctive antibiotic therapy does not confer meaningful clinical improvement when effective surgical source control is achieved via LCCS. Moreover, our cost-minimization analysis extends this paradigm by providing an economic rationale, demonstrating that omitting postoperative antibiotics significantly reduces societal costs without compromising clinical outcomes [17]– [18]. Together, these data substantiate a shift toward selective, evidence-based antimicrobial stewardship in proctologic surgery, optimizing healthcare value while maintaining patient safety.

Healing time was included as a clinically and economically relevant secondary outcome reflecting wound recovery and resource utilization. Although the study was powered for cost outcomes rather than small differences in healing time, the nearly identical values between groups (p = 0.490 in the overall cohort) indicate no clinically meaningful effect of antibiotics on wound healing.

The LCCS procedure inherently provides effective drainage and controlled tract division, thereby minimizing postoperative infection risk through surgical source control [19]. Continuous evacuation of secretions and abscess contents prevents fluid accumulation and bacterial overgrowth. A uniform operative technique and consistent postoperative wound management likely equalized infection risk across both groups, as evidenced by the absence of any deep surgical infections in either arm of the trial.

Conversely, systemic antibiotic administration can introduce adverse effects that may hinder recovery. Patients in the Antibiotic group experienced higher rates of antibiotic-associated complications such as gastrointestinal disturbances and drug-induced fevers, which required additional interventions and shifted the postoperative burden from infection-related issues to drug-related complications [20]– [21]. These adverse events contributed to longer hospital stays and greater resource utilization in the Antibiotic group.

From a societal cost-minimization perspective, the no-antibiotic strategy proved clearly superior. With equivalent clinical outcomes between groups, omitting antibiotics reduced the total cost burden by approximately 19% in our study [18]. While the cost of the antibiotic medications was relatively minor, the management of antibiotic-related adverse events and the increased nursing care needs generated substantial expenses. Additionally, patients receiving antibiotics required more post-discharge clinic visits, which increased outpatient costs.

These findings are consistent with principles of antimicrobial stewardship, which advocate for avoiding unnecessary antibiotic use to curb resistance, minimize patient harm, and reduce healthcare costs [22]– [23]. A statewide quality improvement program in the U.S. has shown that targeted interventions can significantly reduce inappropriate antibiotic prescribing in non-indicated conditions such as asymptomatic bacteriuria, rein-forcing the importance of stewardship initiatives across surgical and non-surgical set-tings [24]. Our study underscores that routine postoperative antibiotic prophylaxis has no economic justification in the absence of a demonstrated clinical benefit.

From a health economics viewpoint, our trial illustrates a clear cost-minimization scenario: given equivalent outcomes, the less costly strategy (withholding antibiotics) is preferable. Total societal cost in the No Antibiotic group was roughly 19% lower than in the Antibiotic group. Antibiotic use drove costs up through multiple pathways. Direct drug costs accounted for only a small part of the difference, but treating antibiotic-related complications was significantly costly. We observed greater nursing care requirements in the Antibiotic group due to the extra time needed for drug administration and monitoring of side effects. Patients who received antibiotics also had more frequent follow-up clinic visits—likely for wound checks or management of side effects—which increased outpatient expenses. Importantly, indirect costs from lost productivity were substantially higher for the Antibiotic group, as longer hospital stays and recovery times translated into more missed work days. Many of these expenses are often underappreciated in clinical decision-making.

The cost patterns we observed resonate with broader goals of antimicrobial stewardship and value-based care. Stewardship programs emphasize the avoidance of unnecessary antibiotics not only to prevent resistance but also to minimize avoidable harm and expenditure. In our study, each component of the excess cost attributable to antibiotics represents a potentially preventable expense if antibiotics are reserved only for clear, evidence-based indications. Even under optimistic assumptions (best-case scenarios), the Antibiotic group remained more costly, reinforcing that routine prophylactic antibiotics lack economic justification when they do not improve outcomes.

This trial introduces several notable innovations. First, we integrated a comprehensive cost minimization analysis (CMA) within an RCT framework, focusing on cost differentials under equivalent efficacy [18]. Second, we adopted a societal perspective, encompassing direct medical, outpatient, and indirect productivity costs 18. Third, our 3D waterfall visualization decomposed incremental cost contributions, enabling stakeholders to identify primary drivers of expense. Lastly, extensive sensitivity analyses confirmed the stability of our economic conclusions across varied scenarios [20]. Together, these elements offer a rigorous template for coupling clinical and economic evaluation in surgical research.

Limitations include the single center design, which may restrict generalizability; multicenter trials are warranted. All cost data were derived from a single hospital, specifically China-Japan Friendship Hospital. This may limit the generalizability of the findings. However, none of the participants reported seeking additional professional care outside the study center during the follow-up period. Despite this, the limitation of relying on a single hospital for cost data remains, and future studies with multi-center data would help improve the external validity of these findings. The follow up period was limited to early outcomes, necessitating longer surveillance to evaluate late recurrence and antibiotic resistance. Some economic data relied on patient self report, introducing potential recall bias; future work could leverage real time cost tracking to enhance accuracy. While our study was primarily powered for cost comparisons, it was not specifically powered for clinical equivalence of secondary outcomes, such as wound healing time. Therefore, although no significant differences in healing were observed, we cannot definitively rule out very small, non-clinically meaningful differences. Future larger trials specifically designed for non-inferiority could further clarify this aspect.

Future research directions include exploring the implementation of multicenter RCTs with extended follow-up durations to evaluate long-term outcomes of selective antibiotic use. It would also be valuable to perform cost-utility analyses (e.g., calculating quality-adjusted life years QALYs and disability-adjusted life years DALYs) to further quantify the value of avoiding unnecessary antibiotics. Investigations into biomarkers or wound microbiome profiles may help identify subgroups of patients who could benefit from targeted antibiotic therapy. In addition, ongoing large-scale trials in related anorectal conditions will continue to inform and refine clinical guidelines for antibiotic use in surgery.

Conclusion

Routine postoperative antibiotic use in HHAF surgery provides no additional clinical benefit while substantially increasing societal costs. In our trial, clinical outcomes were equivalent between the antibiotic and No Antibiotic groups, but the no-antibiotic strategy incurred significantly lower costs related to adverse events, nursing care, and lost productivity. These findings support adopting a more selective, evidence-based approach to postoperative antibiotic administration.

By integrating a cost-minimization framework and advanced visualization methods, this study offers a practical model for economic evaluation in surgical care. The results support value-driven clinical decision-making and align with global antimicrobial stewardship efforts. Future studies should explore wider adoption of these approaches, assess long-term impacts, and investigate individualized perioperative strategies to further improve patient outcomes and resource efficiency.
