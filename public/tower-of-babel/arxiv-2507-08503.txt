Bounds on the game isolation number and exact values for paths and cycles
Csilla Bujtás, Tanja Dravec, Michael A. Henning, Sandi Klavžar

Abstract
The isolation game is played on a graph G G by two players who take turns playing a vertex such that if X X is the set of already played vertices, then a vertex can be selected only if it dominates a vertex from a nontrivial component of G ∖ N G ​ [ X ] G\setminus N_{G}[X] , where N G ​ [ X ] N_{G}[X] is the set of vertices in X X or adjacent to a vertex in X X . Dominator wishes to finish the game with the minimum number of played vertices, while Staller has the opposite goal. The game isolation number ι g ​ ( G ) \iota_{\rm g}(G) is the number of moves in the Dominator-start game where both players play optimally. If Staller starts the game the invariant is denoted by ι g ′ ​ ( G ) \iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G) . In this paper, ι g ​ ( C n ) \iota_{\rm g}(C_{n}) , ι g ​ ( P n ) \iota_{\rm g}(P_{n}) , ι g ′ ​ ( C n ) \iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(C_{n}) , and ι g ′ ​ ( P n ) \iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(P_{n}) are determined for all n n . It is proved that there are only two graphs that attain equality in the upper bound ι g ​ ( G ) ≤ 1 2 ​ | V ⁡ ( G ) | \iota_{\rm g}(G)\leq\frac{1}{2}|V(G)| , and that there are precisely eleven graphs which attain equality in the upper bound ι g ′ ​ ( G ) ≤ 1 2 ​ | V ⁡ ( G ) | \iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G)\leq\frac{1}{2}|V(G)| . For trees T T of order at least three it is proved that ι g ​ ( T ) ≤ 5 11 ​ | V ⁡ ( T ) | \iota_{\rm g}(T)\leq\frac{5}{11}|V(T)| . A new infinite family of graphs G G is also constructed for which ι g ​ ( G ) = ι g ′ ​ ( G ) = 3 7 ​ | V ⁡ ( G ) | \iota_{\rm g}(G)=\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G)=\frac{3}{7}|V(G)| holds.

More recently, the authors in
[

introduced a game counterpart of the isolation number following the ideas of the classical domination game
[

. The
isolation game
is played on a graph
G
by Dominator and Staller, who take turns selecting/playing a vertex from
G
while obeying the rule that if
X
is the set of already played vertices, then a vertex can be selected only if it dominates a vertex that belongs to a nontrivial component of the graph
G-N_{G}[X]
. Such a vertex

is

, and a move selecting

. The game ends when no playable vertex exists. When the game ends, the set of vertices selected forms an isolating set of

. As in the domination game,
Dominator wishes to finish the game by playing the fewest number of vertices, while Staller wishes to delay the process as much as possible. Thus, Dominator seeks to minimize the size of the chosen set while Staller tries to make it as large as possible. If Dominator starts the game, we speak of a
D-game
, otherwise it is an
S-game
. The
game isolation number
,
\iota_{\rm g}(G)
, and the
Staller-start game isolation number
,
\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G)
, are the number of vertices selected in the D-game and the S-game, respectively, provided that both players play optimally.

As mentioned above, the isolation number of a graph was introduced in a more general frame, the same holds for the isolation game from
[
7
]
. A few results, such as the Continuation Principle and the fact that for each graph

[
7
]
for the general situation, while a special focus has been given on the isolation game. It was proved that

is at most half the order for any graph

and conjectured that the actual sharp upper bound is the ceiling of three-sevenths of the order. The isolation number of paths

was also determined for the cases when
n\equiv i\bmod 5
,
i\in\{1,2,3\}
.

In Section

[

The result for cycles reads as follows.

Theorem 2.3
.

For every
n\geq 4
,

\iota_{\rm g}(C_{n})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2\left\lceil\frac{n}{5}\right\rceil;&\text{if~}n\equiv 0\bmod 5,\\
\\
2\left\lceil\frac{n}{5}\right\rceil-1;&\text{otherwise}.\\
\end{array}\right.

\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(C_{n})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2\left\lfloor\frac{n}{5}\right\rfloor+1;&\text{if~}n\equiv 4\bmod 5,\\
\\
2\left\lfloor\frac{n}{5}\right\rfloor;&\text{otherwise}.\\
\end{array}\right.

Proof.

For
C_{4}
and
C_{5}
, the formulas can be checked directly. In the continuation, we may assume
n\geq 6
and prove lower and upper bounds on the invariants by observing strategies for Staller and Dominator, respectively in the subsequent two claims.

Claim A
.
For every
n\geq 6
,

\min\left\{2\left\lceil\frac{n}{5}\right\rceil,2\left\lceil\frac{n-4}{5}\right\rceil+1\right\}\leq\iota_{\rm g}(C_{n})

(1)

and

\min\left\{2\left\lceil\frac{n-3}{5}\right\rceil,2\left\lceil\frac{n-4}{5}\right\rceil+1\right\}\leq\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(C_{n}).

(2)

Proof.

We consider the following strategy of Staller. In each of her moves, she plays a (playable) vertex that is a neighbor of an already played vertex. Observe that it is always possible when the game is not over. By a run
B_{i}
we mean a maximal sequence of played vertices along the cycle. Let
B_{1},\dots,B_{\ell}
be the runs obtained when the game is over. According to Staller’s strategy, she never increases the number of runs except in the first move in the S-game. Hence, the number of Dominator’s moves is an upper bound on
\ell
and
\ell-1
in the D-game and S-game, respectively.
Between any two consecutive runs there are at most three unplayed vertices. Let
t=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}|B_{i}|
, which is exactly the number of played vertices during the game.

We first prove (
1
) and assume that a D-game is played on
C_{n}
. If
t
is even, Staller finishes the game and the number of runs can be estimated as
\ell\leq t/2
. Therefore,

n\leq t+3\,\frac{t}{2}=5\,\frac{t}{2}

and, as
\frac{t}{2}
is an integer, we have
\frac{t}{2}\geq\left\lceil\frac{n}{5}\right\rceil
. Hence
we may conclude
2\left\lceil\frac{n}{5}\right\rceil\leq t
when
t
is even. If
t
is odd, then Dominator plays
\frac{t+1}{2}
vertices and
\ell\leq\frac{t+1}{2}
. This implies

n\leq t+3\,\frac{t+1}{2}=5\,\frac{t-1}{2}+4.

Since
\frac{t-1}{2}
is an integer, we get

2\left\lceil\frac{n-4}{5}\right\rceil+1\leq t

for the case of
t
is odd. Putting together the cases with odd and even
t
, we obtain inequality (
1
).

For the
S
-game, we can prove (
2
) in an analogous way. Here, the first move of Staller creates a new run, but later she does not increase the number of runs. Hence, the number of Dominator’s moves is an upper bound on
\ell-1
. If
t
, the number of played vertices, is an even number, then
\ell\leq\frac{t}{2}+1
and

n\leq t+3\left(\frac{t}{2}+1\right)=5\,\frac{t}{2}+3.

Therefore,
2\left\lceil\frac{n-3}{5}\right\rceil\leq t
holds if
t
is even. If
t
is odd, then Staller finishes the game, and we get

n\leq t+3\left(\frac{t-1}{2}+1\right)=5\,\frac{t-1}{2}+4.

and conclude

2\left\lceil\frac{n-4}{5}\right\rceil+1\leq t.
From the odd and even cases, we get (
2
) as stated.
(
\Box
)

Claim B
. For every
n\geq 6
, the following inequalities hold.

(a)

If
n\not\equiv 0\bmod{5}
, then
\iota_{\rm g}(C_{n})\leq 2\left\lceil\frac{n}{5}\right\rceil-1
.
If
n\equiv 0\bmod{5}
, then
\iota_{\rm g}(C_{n})\leq 2\left\lceil\frac{n}{5}\right\rceil
.

(b)

If
n\not\equiv 4\bmod{5}
, then
\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(C_{n})\leq 2\left\lfloor\frac{n}{5}\right\rfloor
. If
n\equiv 4\bmod{5}
, then
\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(C_{n})\leq 2\left\lfloor\frac{n}{5}\right\rfloor+1
.

Proof.

To prove the upper bounds stated in the claim, we consider the following strategy of Dominator. If it is not the first move in the game, then Dominator chooses a vertex
v_{i}
such that
v_{i-4}
is a played vertex and
v_{i-3},v_{i-2},v_{i-1}
are all unplayed. If the game is not over, then he is able to follow this strategy. We further note that if there is a playable vertex
v_{j}
in
C_{n}
(at any moment in the game), then there is a sequence of at least four consecutive playable vertices that includes
v_{j}
. Consequently, if
C_{n}
contains at most seven playable vertices before a move of Dominator, then these vertices form a sequence, and Dominator can choose one of them to finish the game with this move. If the number of playable vertices is eight, then Dominator can follow his strategy, which ensures that the next move played by Staller will finish the game. For both the D-game and S-game, we count the playable and unplayable vertices after each move in
C_{n}
to prove the upper bounds. Let
u_{i}
denote the number of vertices made unplayable by the
i^{\rm th}
move in the game.

(a) Assume that a D-game is played. The first move of Dominator makes exactly one vertex (the played vertex itself) unplayable,
u_{1}=1
. Then, for Staller’s move, we have
u_{2}\geq 1
. As Dominator’s strategy ensures, his every later move makes at least four vertices unplayable and hence
u_{2j-1}\geq 4
for every
j\geq 2
. For Staller’s moves, we have
u_{2j}\geq 1
. In general, there are

U_{2i}=\sum_{s=1}^{2i}u_{s}\geq 2+5(i-1)=5i-3

unplayable vertices after
2i
moves in the game.

•

If
n=5k
, then
U_{2k-2}\geq 5k-8
and at most eight playable vertices remain before Dominator’s move. He can make sure that the game finishes with the
(2k)^{\rm th}
move or sooner. Therefore,
\iota_{\rm g}(C_{n})\leq 2k=2\left\lceil\frac{n}{5}\right\rceil
as stated.

•

If
n=5k+r
with
r\in\{1,\dots,4\}
, then
U_{2k}\geq 5k-3
implies that at most
r+3\leq 7

\iota_{\rm g}(C_{n})\leq 2k+1=2\left\lceil\frac{n}{5}\right\rceil-1
.

(b) In an S-game on
C_{n}
Dominator’s strategy implies
u_{1}=1
,
u_{2}\geq 4
, and in general,
u_{2j-1}\geq 1

u_{2j}\geq 4

2i-1

5i-4
.

•

If
n=5k+4

U_{2k-1}\geq 5k-4
. Thus, at most eight playable vertices remain and the game finishes with the
(2k+1)^{\rm st}
move or sooner. We conclude that
\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(C_{n})\leq 2k+1=2\left\lfloor\frac{n}{5}\right\rfloor+1
.

•

If
n=5k+r

r\in\{0,\dots,3\}
, then
U_{2k-1}\geq 5k-4
implies that the number of playable vertices is at most
4+r\leq 7
. It follows that Dominator can finish the game with his next move and
\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(C_{n})\leq 2k=2\left\lfloor\frac{n}{5}\right\rfloor
.
(
\Box
)

To complete the proof of the theorem, we compare the lower and upper bounds in Claims A and B.

If

2\left\lceil\frac{n}{5}\right\rceil=2k<2k+1=2\left\lceil\frac{n-4}{5}\right\rceil+1,

2\left\lceil\frac{n}{5}\right\rceil=2k+2>2k+1=2\left\lceil\frac{n-4}{5}\right\rceil+1,

2k+1\leq\iota_{\rm g}(C_{n})
. On the other hand, Claim B(a) establishes
\iota_{\rm g}(C_{n})\leq 2\left\lceil\frac{n}{5}\right\rceil-1=2k+1
. We may therefore infer
\iota_{\rm g}(C_{n})=2\left\lceil\frac{n}{5}\right\rceil-1
.

For
\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(C_{n})
, we first consider the case of
n=5k+4
and observe

2\left\lceil\frac{n-3}{5}\right\rceil=2k+2>2k+1=2\left\lceil\frac{n-4}{5}\right\rceil+1.

Inequality (
2
) then yields
2k+1\leq\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(C_{n})
which matches the upper bound in Claim B(b) and proves
\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(C_{n})=2\left\lfloor\frac{n}{5}\right\rfloor+1
.
If
n=5k+r
with
r\in\{0,\dots,3\}
, then

2\left\lceil\frac{n-3}{5}\right\rceil=2k<2k+1=2\left\lceil\frac{n-4}{5}\right\rceil+1

and (

) gives
2k\leq\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(C_{n})
. By Claim B(b) we know that
\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(C_{n})\leq 2k=2\left\lfloor\frac{n}{5}\right\rfloor
.

2.1

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
n\geq 6
. If
n\equiv 0\bmod 5
, or
n\equiv 4\bmod 5
, then

\iota_{\rm g}(P_{n})=\left\lceil\frac{2n}{5}\right\rceil-1\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(P_{n})=\left\lfloor\frac{2n+2}{5}\right\rfloor.

Proof.

We first prove that the lower bound

\left\lceil\frac{2n}{5}\right\rceil-1\leq\iota_{\rm g}(P_{n})
in Theorem
2.1
is tight when
n=5k
or
n=5k+4
. Consider a D-game on
P_{n}\colon v_{1}\dots v_{n}
when Dominator applies the strategy described in the proof of Theorem
2.3
, Claim B. More exactly, Dominator plays
v_{3}
as his first move, and later he chooses to play a vertex
v_{i}
such that
v_{i-4}
is played and
v_{i-3},v_{i-2},v_{i-1},v_{i}
are all unplayed. If there is no such vertex
v_{i}
, then either the game is over or the last three vertices of the path are unplayed, and the only edge which is not isolated is
v_{n-1}v_{n}
. Let
t
denote the number of moves in this game.

Let
u_{j}
denote the number of vertices made unplayable by the
j^{\rm th}
move of the game and let
U_{j}=\sum_{s=1}^{j}u_{s}
be the total number of unplayable vertices after the
j^{\rm th}
move. By Dominator’s strategy,
u_{1}\geq 3
, and
u_{2j-1}\geq 4
for every
j\geq 2
if
t>2j-1
. Therefore,
U_{2j}\geq 5j-1
holds if
t>2j
.

If
n=5k
, then
U_{2k-2}\geq 5k-6
. Hence, after
2k-2
moves in the game, at most six vertices are playable. Then these six vertices must be consecutive on the path, and Dominator can finish the game with his next move. It gives

\iota_{\rm g}(P_{n})\leq t\leq 2k-1=\left\lceil\frac{2n}{5}\right\rceil-1

and proves the formula for
n=5k
. If
n=5k+4
, then we can give a similar reasoning. In this case,
U_{2k}\geq 5k-1
that is, at most five vertices are playable after
2k
moves. These are consecutive vertices, and Dominator can finish the game with his next move. We may conclude

\iota_{\rm g}(P_{n})\leq t\leq 2k+1=\left\lceil\frac{2n}{5}\right\rceil-1

and equality follows by Theorem
2.1
.

Consider now an S-game on
P_{n}
. To prove a lower bound on
\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(P_{n})
, we suppose that Staller plays according to the strategy described in
the proof of Theorem
2.3
, Claim A. Her first move is
v_{1}
, and later she always plays a vertex next to an already played vertex. By this strategy, if
\ell
is the number of runs (maximal sequences of consecutive played vertices) at the end of the game, then Dominator played at least
\ell-1
vertices. Recall also that there are at most three unplayed vertices between two consecutive runs. By Staller’s strategy,
v_{1}
belongs to a run. At the other end of the path, at most two unplayed vertices may remain. Again, let
t
denote the number of moves in the game.

If
t
is even, then Dominator played
t/2
vertices and

n\leq t+3\ell-1\leq t+3\left(\frac{t}{2}+1\right)-1=5\,\frac{t}{2}+2.

Since
\frac{t}{2}
is an integer, it is equivalent to
2\left\lceil\frac{n-2}{5}\right\rceil\leq t
.
If
t
is odd, then Dominator played
(t-1)/2
vertices and

n\leq t+3\ell-1\leq t+3\left(\frac{t-1}{2}+1\right)-1=5\,\frac{t-1}{2}+3

follows. Hence we get
2\left\lceil\frac{n-3}{5}\right\rceil+1\leq t
. From the two cases, we derive

m=\min\left\{2\left\lceil\frac{n-2}{5}\right\rceil,2\left\lceil\frac{n-3}{5}\right\rceil+1\right\}\leq t\leq\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(P_{n}).

(3)

Finally, we compare (

) with the upper bound
\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(P_{n})\leq\left\lfloor\frac{2n+2}{5}\right\rfloor

.
If
n=5k
, then

, and it equals the upper bound. If

, then

, which matches the upper bound again. We conclude that
\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(P_{n})=\left\lfloor\frac{2n+2}{5}\right\rfloor

3
Graphs that attain the 1/2-bound

Brešar et al.
[
7
, Theorem 3.2]
proved that the game isolation number of a graph is at most one-half its order. We characterize the graphs achieving equality in this upper bound on the game isolation number. For this purpose, we follow the proof technique employed in
[
7
]
, with the modification that we adopt a greedy strategy for Dominator.

For completeness, we include the key strategy of the proof in
[
7
]
. During the course of the game, when
S
is the set of selected vertices, a vertex
v
is considered
marked
if
v\in N_{G}[S]
or if all neighbors of
v
are in
N_{G}[S]
. Note that a marked vertex may or may not be playable and a vertex is not playable if and only if its entire closed neighborhood is marked.

Theorem 3.1
.

If
G
is a connected graph of order
n
, then
\iota_{\rm g}(G)\leq\frac{1}{2}n
, with equality if and only if
G\in\{K_{2},C_{6}\}
.

Proof.

Let
G
be a connected graph of order
n
. If
n=1
, then
\iota_{\rm g}(G)=0
, while if
n=2
, then
G=K_{2}
and
\iota_{\rm g}(G)=1=\frac{1}{2}n
. Hence we may assume that
n\geq 3
, for otherwise the desired result is immediate. Following the proof strategy employed in
[
7
]
, we weight the vertices in the graph
G
as follows. At the beginning all vertices are unmarked and each vertex has weight
1
. During the game, when a vertex is marked its weight drops to
0
. The
weight

{\rm w}(G)
of
G
is the sum of weights of all vertices of
G
. Thus at the beginning of the game
{\rm w}(G)=n
and when the game ends the weight is zero. Let
M
be the set of marked vertices in some stage of the game.

We adopt a refinement of Dominator’s strategy as given in
[
7
]
in that Dominator now adopts a greedy strategy and plays a vertex that results in a maximum weight decrease. At the start of the game, Dominator therefore plays a vertex of maximum degree
\Delta(G)
, thereby marking at least
\Delta(G)+1
vertices and decreasing the weight by at least
\Delta(G)+1
on his first move. On each of his subsequent moves, if there exists a component
C
of
G-M
of order at least
3
, then Dominator can always decrease the weight by at least
3
by playing a vertex of degree at least
2
in
C
. Every move of Staller decreases the weight of

by at least
1
since she must dominate at least one (unmarked) vertex in
G-M
.

In the final stage of the game when every component in
G-M
is a
K_{2}
-component, every move decreases the weight by at least
2
. By playing a vertex in a
K_{2}
-component of
G-M
, we note that Staller can play a move that decreases the weight by exactly
2
. However according to Dominator’s greedy strategy, he plays a marked vertex that is adjacent to the maximum number of
K_{2}
-components in
G-M
, thereby decreasing the weight by at least
2
.

In summary, Dominator’s greedy strategy implies all his moves decrease the weight by at least
3
, except possibly in the final stage of the game when every component in
G-M
is a
K_{2}
-component, in which case all his subsequent moves decrease the weight by at least
2
and all subsequent moves of Staller decrease the weight by exactly
2
. Moreover if at least one component of
G-M
is of order at least
3
, then Staller’s move decreases the weight of
G
by at least
1
. Hence every move of Dominator decreases the weight by at least
2
, while each of Dominator’s move together with Staller’s reply to his move decrease the weight by at least
4
. Therefore on average every played move decreases the weight by at least
2
, implying that the game finishes after at most
\frac{1}{2}n
moves.

By our earlier assumption, recall that
n\geq 3
, and so
\Delta(G)\geq 2
. Suppose that
\Delta(G)\geq 3
. In this case, Dominator’s first move decreases the weight by at least
4
. Since Staller’s move decreases the weight by at least
1
, after the first two moves are played at least five vertices are marked. Thereafter, Dominator’s greedy strategy guarantees that at most
\frac{1}{2}(n-5)
additional moves are needed to finish the game. Hence in this case the game finishes after at most
2+\frac{1}{2}(n-5)=\frac{1}{2}(n-1)
moves, and so
\iota_{\rm g}(G)<\frac{1}{2}n
.

Hence we may assume that
\Delta(G)=2
. Thus,
G
is a path
P_{n}
or a cycle
C_{n}
. Suppose that
G=P_{n}
(and

). Then by Theorem
2.5
, we have
\iota_{\rm g}(G)\leq\frac{2}{5}(n+1)<\frac{1}{2}n
. Hence if
G=P_{n}
and
n\geq 3
, then
\iota_{\rm g}(G)<\frac{1}{2}n
.

Suppose finally that
G=C_{n}
. If
n\in\{3,4\}
, then
\iota_{\rm g}(G)=1<\frac{1}{2}n
. If
n=5
, then
\iota_{\rm g}(G)=2<\frac{1}{2}n
. If
n=6
, then
\iota_{\rm g}(G)=3=\frac{1}{2}n
. Hence we may assume that

. By Theorem
2.3
, we now infer the following. If

, then
\iota_{\rm g}(C_{n})=\frac{2}{5}n<\frac{1}{2}n
. If
n\equiv 1\bmod{5}
, then
\iota_{\rm g}(C_{n})=\frac{2}{5}(n+4)-1<\frac{1}{2}n
. If
n\equiv 2\bmod{5}
, then
\iota_{\rm g}(C_{n})=\frac{2}{5}(n+3)-1<\frac{1}{2}n
. If
n\equiv 3\bmod{5}
, then
\iota_{\rm g}(C_{n})=\frac{2}{5}(n+2)-1<\frac{1}{2}n
. If
n\equiv 4\bmod{5}
, then
\iota_{\rm g}(C_{n})=\frac{2}{5}(n+1)-1<\frac{1}{2}n
. In all cases, when

are the only graphs that satisfy
\iota_{\rm g}(C_{n})=\frac{1}{2}n

Theorem 3.2
.

If
G
is a connected graph of order
n
, then
\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G)\leq\frac{1}{2}n
, with equality if and only if
G\in{\cal F}
.

Proof.

Let
G
be a connected graph of order
n
. We first prove the desired upper bound.

Claim 1
.

\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G)\leq\frac{1}{2}n
.

Proof.

If
n=1
, then
\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G)=0
, hence assume in the rest that
n\geq 2
. As in the proof of Theorem
3.1
, we show that if Dominator employs his greedy strategy, then the game finishes after at most
\frac{1}{2}n
moves. Also, let
M
be the set of marked vertices in some stage of the game. At the start of the game, Staller plays a vertex that marks at least
\delta(G)+1
vertices. Therefore her first move decreases the weight by at least
2
. Thereafter, as shown in the proof of Theorem
3.1
, Staller’s subsequent moves decrease the weight by at least
1
. Moreover in the final stage of the game when every component in
G-M
is a
K_{2}
-component, every move of Staller decreases the weight by at least
2
. As shown in the proof of Theorem
3.1
, Dominator has a strategy that guarantees that all his moves decrease the weight by at least
3
, except possibly in the final stage of the game when every component in
G-M
is a
K_{2}
-component, in which case all his subsequent moves decrease the weight by at least
2
.

If Dominator’s first move decreases the weight by exactly
2
, then since he follows the greedy strategy, all moves played in the game decrease the weight by exactly
2
, implying that the game finishes after
\frac{1}{2}n
moves. Hence we may assume that the first move of Dominator decreases the weight by at least
3
, for otherwise the desired upper bound follows. Let
i+1
be the first move of Dominator that decreases the weight by exactly
2
, where
i\geq 1
. Thus, all moves after Dominator plays his
(i+1)
th move decrease the weight by exactly
2
. The first move of Staller decreases the weight by at least
2
, while all her other moves played before Dominator’s
(i+1)
th move decrease the weight by at least
1
. Hence the first
2i+1
moves played in the game (that is, all moves played immediately before Dominator plays his
(i+1)
th move) decrease the total weight by at least
2+i+3i=2(2i+1)
. Thus on average every played move decreases the weight by at least
2
, implying that the game finishes after at most
\frac{1}{2}n
moves. This establishes the desired upper bound, namely
\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G)\leq\frac{1}{2}n
.
(
\Box
)

Suppose next that
\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G)=\frac{1}{2}n
. In particular,
n\geq 2
is even. If

, then it is straightforward to verify that in this case

. Hence we may assume that in the rest of the proof

. We proceed further with a series of claims establishing some structural properties of the graph
G
.

Claim 2
.

Staller’s first move decreases the weight by exactly
2
.

Proof.

Suppose that Staller’s first move decreases the weight by at least
3
. As observed earlier, on average every move played after the first move of Staller decreases the weight by at least
2
, implying that the game finishes after at most
1+\frac{1}{2}(n-3)<\frac{1}{2}n
moves, a contradiction.
(
\Box
)

By Claim
2
, Staller’s first move decreases the weight by exactly
2
, implying that the first move of Staller is a vertex of degree
1
. Let
u
be the vertex played by Staller on her first move, and let
v
be the (unique) neighbor of

in
G
. Thus at this opening stage of the game, we have
M=\{u,v\}
.

Claim 3
.

Every move of Dominator decreases the weight by
2
or
3
.

Proof.

Suppose that after the first move of Staller, every component of
G-M
is a
K_{2}
-component. By assumption

, and so

contains

. Dominator now plays the vertex
v
as his first move, thereby marking all vertices, and so

, a contradiction. Hence, at least one component of

has order at least

. The first move of Dominator therefore decreases the weight by at least

Suppose that the first move of Dominator decreases the weight by at least

(
\Box
)

4
The isolation game played in trees

As a consequence of Theorem
3.1
, we have the following upper bound on the game isolation number of a tree.

Corollary 4.1
.

If
T
is a tree of order
n
, then
\iota_{\rm g}(T)\leq\frac{1}{2}n
, with equality if and only if
T=K_{2}
.

In this section, we improve the
\frac{1}{2}n
-upper bound in Corollary
4.1
to a
\frac{5}{11}n
-upper bound when
T
is a tree of order
n\geq 3
. For this purpose, we use a proof technique from
[
14
]
used to establish upper bounds on the isolation number of a graph. The technique uses an
isolation residual graph
defined as follows.

Given a graph
G
and a subset
S\subseteq V(G)
, the
isolation residual graph
denoted by
G_{S}^{\iota}
is obtained as follows. Its vertex set is
V(G)
. Further:

(a)

A vertex in
G-N[S]
with a neighbor in
G-N[S]
is colored
white
.

(b)

A vertex in
N[S]
with a white neighbor is colored
blue
.

(c)

All other vertices are colored
red
.

(d)

The edge set of
G_{S}^{\iota}
consists of all edges of
G
that are incident with at least one white vertex.

As remarked in
[
14
]
, the white vertices still need to be isolated, the blue vertices might be useful to do this isolating, and the red vertices are no longer relevant. We denote the set of red vertices by
R
, the set of blue vertices by
B
, and the set of white vertices by
W
. We observe that these sets form a (weak) partition of
V(G)
. We shall need the following elementary properties of a residual graph.

Observation 4.2
.

The following holds in the isolation residual graph
G_{S}^{\iota}
.

(a)

Every vertex of
S
is colored red.

(b)

Every red vertex is isolated, but no white nor blue vertex is isolated.

(c)

The degree of a white vertex in
G_{S}^{\iota}
equals its degree in
G
.

(d)

The set
S
is an isolating set in
G
if and only if there is no white vertex.

We are now in a position to prove the following result on the game isolation number of a tree.

Theorem 4.3
.

If
T
is a tree of order
n\geq 3
, then
\iota_{\rm g}(T)\leq\frac{5}{11}n
.

Proof.

For a set
S\subseteq V(T)
, consider the isolation residual graph
T_{S}^{\iota}
and the sets of white, blue, and red vertices in
T_{S}^{\iota}
. We define the weight function

{\rm w}(T_{S}^{\iota})=5|W|+3|B|.

Equivalently, we associate every white, blue, and red vertex with a weight of
5
,
3
, and
0
, respectively, and then consider the sum of the weights in
T_{S}^{\iota}
.

When the game starts,
S=\emptyset
and
{\rm w}(T_{\emptyset}^{\iota})=5n
, while
{\rm w}(T_{S}^{\iota})=0
holds when the game is over. Further, if
v
is a playable vertex in the isolation residual graph
T_{S}^{\iota}
, then
{\rm w}(T_{S}^{\iota})\geq{\rm w}(T_{S\cup\{v\}}^{\iota})
and we use the notation
\xi(v)={\rm w}(T_{S}^{\iota})-{\rm w}(T_{S\cup\{v\}})
. We will show that Dominator can ensure that
{\rm w}(T_{S}^{\iota})
decreases by at least
22
in every two consecutive moves of Dominator and Staller. To prove this, we consider three stages in the D-game. In the graph
T_{S}^{\iota}[W]
, induced by the white vertices of
T_{S}^{\iota}
, a
big component
is a component of order at least
4
. If a component is not big, it is called
small
. If
T
is a tree, a small component of
T_{S}^{\iota}[W]
is either a
P_{2}
- or a
P_{3}
-subgraph.

Stage
1
.

A move of Dominator belongs to Stage
1
if
T_{S}^{\iota}[W]
contains a big component before this move. A move of Staller belongs to Stage
1
, if so does Dominator’s previous move.

We first state that every legal move decreases
{\rm w}(T_{S}^{\iota})
by at least
5
. Indeed, if a white vertex
v
is played, it becomes red and
\xi(v)\geq 5
. If a blue vertex
u
is played that has a white neighbor
u^{\prime}
, then
u
becomes red and
u^{\prime}
becomes blue or red, which gives
\xi(u)\geq 3+(5-3)=5
. In particular, each move of Staller decreases the weight of
T_{S}^{\iota}
by at least
5
.

Suppose that Dominator plays in
T_{S}^{\iota}
in Stage
1
. The induced subgraph
T_{S}^{\iota}[W]
then contains a component
T_{i}
on at least four vertices. If
T_{i}
is a star
K_{1,k}
for a
k\geq 3
, then Dominator chooses the center
v
. Since all vertices in the star become red,
\xi(v)\geq 5(k+1)\geq 20
. If
T_{i}
is not a star, it contains a diametrical path
v_{1}\dots v_{d}
with
d\geq 4
. Then
P\colon v_{1}v_{2}v_{3}v_{4}
is a
4
-path such that
v_{1}
is a leaf in
T_{i}
and if
v_{2}
has a neighbor
v^{\prime}\notin V(P)
, then
v^{\prime}
is a leaf in
T_{i}
.
Dominator now plays
v_{3}
. With this move
v_{1}
,
v_{2}
,
v_{3}
(more generally,
N_{T_{i}}[v_{2}]
) become red and
v_{4}
is colored blue or red. Consequently,
\xi(v_{3})\geq 3\times 5+(5-3)=17
. This shows that Dominator can always decrease
{\rm w}(T_{S}^{\iota})
by at least
17
, when the move belongs to Stage
1
. We may conclude that Dominator has a strategy to ensure that the weight decreases by at least
17+5=22
in every two consecutive moves in Stage
1
.

Before discussing Stage
2
, we introduce a new vertex type in the isolation residual graph
T_{S}^{\iota}
. We say that a vertex
u
is
light blue
, and belongs to
B^{\ell}
, if it became blue during Stage
2
. Light blue vertices are associated with a weight of
1
; that is, the new weight function we use in Stages
2
and
3
is

{\rm w}(T_{S}^{\iota})=5|W|+3|B\setminus B^{\ell}|+|B^{\ell}|.

Note that all light blue vertices are of degree
1
. Further, we say that a component
T_{i}
of
T_{S}^{\iota}[W]
is
special
, if it is a
P_{2}
-component and each adjacent blue (or light blue) vertex is of degree
1
in
T_{S}^{\iota}
.

Stage 2.

A move of Dominator belongs to Stage
2
, if the subgraph
T_{S}^{\iota}[W]
in
T_{S}^{\iota}
before this move consists only of small components and not all of them are special. A move of Staller belongs to Stage
2
, if so does Dominator’s previous move.

We first prove that every move
v
of Staller decreases
{\rm w}(T_{S}^{\iota})
by at least
7
in Stage
2
. If
v
is a white vertex, then an entire
P_{2}
- or
P_{3}
-component of
G[W]
becomes red and
\xi(v)\geq 2\times 5=10
. The same is true if
v\in B
and a white neighbor
v^{\prime}
belongs to a
P_{2}
-component. Assume
v\in B
and that a neighbor
v^{\prime}
of
v
is from a
P_{3}
-component of
T_{S}^{\iota}[W]
. We remark that in this case
v\in B\setminus B^{\ell}
because there were no components in
T_{S}^{\iota}[W]
on more than three vertices at the beginning of Stage
2
, and so
v
was not white at that time.
If
v^{\prime}
is the central vertex of the
P_{3}
-component, then
v^{\prime}
and the entire
P_{3}
-component becomes red with this move and we have
\xi(v)\geq 3+3\times 5=18
. If
v^{\prime}
is a leaf in the
P_{3}
-component, then
v
becomes red and
v^{\prime}
becomes light blue with this move. Therefore,
\xi(v)\geq 3+(5-1)=7
, completing the proof of the statement.

Consider now Dominator’s move. If a
P_{3}
-component is present in
T_{S}^{\iota}[W]
, he can choose a vertex
v
from such a component
T_{i}
. All three white vertices of
T_{i}
become red, which results in
\xi(v)\geq 3\times 5=15
. If there is no
P_{3}
-component, then a non-special
P_{2}
-component exists. Dominator may choose a blue vertex
u
that has neighbors
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
from two different
P_{2}
-components. Playing
u
, both components and
u
itself become red and
\xi(u)\geq 4\times 5+3=23
. We may conclude that Dominator is always able to decrease
{\rm w}(T_{S}^{\iota})
by at least
15
with a move in Stage
2
and therefore, every two consecutive moves together decrease the weight of the isolation residual graph by at least
7+15=22
.

Stage
3
.

Stage
3
starts when all components of
T_{S}^{\iota}[W]
are special and ends when the game is over.

Each move of a player removes a
P_{2}
-component from
T_{S}^{\iota}
by recoloring both vertices with red. Further, by Observation
4.2
(c) and the condition
n\geq 3
in the theorem, each special component is adjacent to a blue vertex of degree
1
, which also becomes red together with the component. We infer that
{\rm w}(T_{S}^{\iota})
decreases by at least
2\times 5+1=11
with each move played in Stage
3
, no matter whether the move is played by Dominator or by Staller.

By the analysis of the stages of the game when Dominator follows the described strategy, we obtained that, on average, each move decreases
{\rm w}(T_{S}^{\iota})
by at least
11
. Let
t
denote the number of vertices played in the game. As the game started with
{\rm w}(T_{\emptyset}^{\iota})=5n
and finished with
{\rm w}(T_{S}^{\iota})=0
, we conclude
11t\leq 5n
and then,
\iota_{\rm g}(T)\leq t\leq\frac{5}{11}n
follows. ∎

5
On the
3/7
-bound

In
[
7
]
the following conjecture is posed.

Conjecture 5.1
.

For any graph
G
with no
K_{2}
-components,

\iota_{\rm g}(G)\leq\left\lceil\frac{3}{7}n(G)\right\rceil\text{ and }\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(G)\leq\left\lceil\frac{3}{7}n(G)\right\rceil.

If the conjecture holds true, then it is sharp as the following example from
[
7
]
demonstrates. Start with an arbitrary graph

Proposition 5.2
.

If
G
is a graph, then

\iota_{\rm g}(\widehat{G})=\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(\widehat{G})=\frac{3}{7}n(\widehat{G})\,.

Proof.

For a vertex
v\in V(G)
, let
G_{v}
be the subgraph of
\widehat{G}
induced by
\{v\}\cup V(T_{v})\cup V(T_{v}^{\prime})
, see Fig.
2
again.

Consider the following strategy of Dominator that guarantees that not more than three vertices are played in each
G_{v}
. Assume that the last move of Staller is a vertex from
G_{v}
, for some
v\in V(G)
. If there are vertices of
G_{v}
which remain playable, then Dominator plays a vertex of
G_{v}
such that no more than three vertices from
G_{v}
will be played when the game finishes. He can achieve this goal since if Staller played
v
, then Dominator selects a vertex from
T_{v}
or a vertex from
T_{v}^{\prime}
, and if Staller selects some vertex from
V(T_{v})\cup V(T_{v}^{\prime})
, then Dominator replies by playing
v
if possible, otherwise a vertex from the triangle where no vertex has been played yet. Note that Dominator can also follow this strategy for his first move in the D-game, that is, he selects some vertex
v\in V(G)\subseteq V(\widehat{G})
. On the other hand, if no vertex of
G_{v}
remains playable after the last move of Staller, Dominator applies analogous strategy in some
G_{u}
,
u\neq v
, where there still exist playable vertices. Note that this strategy of Dominator can be applied no matter whether the D-game or the S-game is played. We can conclude that
\iota_{\rm g}(\widehat{G})\leq\frac{3}{7}n(\widehat{G})
as well as
\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(\widehat{G})\leq\frac{3}{7}n(\widehat{G})
.

Consider now the following strategy of Staller. Her goal is that in each
G_{v}
, the vertex
v
is selected during the game, either by Dominator or by Staller. So assume that the last move of Dominator is a vertex from
G_{v}
, for some
v\in V(G)
, and that no other vertex of
G_{v}
has yet been played. If Dominator selected
v
, then Staller replies by selecting a neighbor of
v
in
T_{v}
. On the other hand, if Dominator selected a vertex different from
v
, then Staller selects
v
. In either case, this strategy of Staller guarantees that by the end of the game, three vertices from
G_{v}
are selected. In addition, if Staller is forced to play first on some
G_{v}
, then she selects
v
, thus forcing again that three vertices from
G_{v}
will be played. We can conclude that
\iota_{\rm g}(\widehat{G})\geq\frac{3}{7}n(\widehat{G})
as well as
\iota_{\rm g}^{\prime}(\widehat{G})\geq\frac{3}{7}n(\widehat{G})
, and we are done. ∎
