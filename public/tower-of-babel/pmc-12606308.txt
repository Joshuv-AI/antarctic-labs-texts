Machine learning prediction of osteoarthritis risk from volatile organic compound exposure using SHAP interpretation in US adults
Authors: Shanbin Zheng, Jiaqing Zhu, Xun Cao, Zhiyuan Chen, Chao Zhang, Tianwei Xia, Jirong Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractExposure to volatile organic compounds (VOCs) is widespread and has been implicated in the pathogenesis of various chronic diseases. However, the specific relationship between VOC exposure and the risk of osteoarthritis (OA) remains poorly characterized. This study aimed to investigate the associations between a broad spectrum of VOC metabolites and OA risk, and to identify the most influential VOC metabolites. We analyzed data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2011–2018, comprising 3683 US adults. OA status was self-reported. Exposure levels to 17 VOCs were assessed using their urinary metabolites. After data splitting (70% training, 30% testing), multiple machine learning models were trained and evaluated. The optimal model was interpreted using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to identify key predictors and elucidate their dose-response relationships with OA risk. The Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) model demonstrated the best predictive performance (AUC = 0.755). SHAP interpretation revealed that besides age, specific VOC metabolites were among the top predictors of OA. N-Acetyl-S-(3,4-dihydroxybutyl)-l-cysteine (DHBMA, a metabolite of 1,3-butadiene) and N-Acetyl-S-(3-hydroxypropyl-2-methyl)-l-cysteine (HMPMA, a metabolite of crotonaldehyde) were identified as novel and significant risk factors. Further analysis delineated non-linear, dose-response relationships between these VOCs and OA risk. Subgroup analyses suggested that the associations were consistent across different demographics. In summary, this study developed a machine learning model based on VOC exposure that effectively predicts osteoarthritis risk. LDA model achieved robust performance, with SHAP interpretation identifying DHBMA and HMPMA as novel and significant risk factors, in addition to known demographic predictors. Subgroup analyses further confirmed the consistent and non-linear association of these VOC metabolites with OA across diverse populations. These findings underscore the value of integrating environmental exposure data into OA risk prediction and support its potential for targeted prevention strategies in high-risk groups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23050-7.Keywords: Metabolite of volatile organic compound, Osteoarthritis, Machine learning, Shapley additive explanationsSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Medical research

Introduction

In recent years, exposure to environmental pollution has been recognized as an important risk factor for the development of many diseases, and its harmful effects on human health cannot be ignored1. It is estimated that nearly 7 million people die prematurely each year due to air pollution2. Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) are ubiquitous in a wide variety of environments, and they account for a large proportion of environmental pollutants. VOCs originate from natural emissions from living organisms as well as from anthropogenic activities, with the latter making a significant contribution3,4. VOCs in daily life typically come from automobile emissions, tobacco smoke, chemical industry emissions, paints, and other sources containing organic solvents, with annual emissions being significant. Chronic exposure can lead to a range of health problems, including hypertension5, obesity and diabetes6, respiratory disease7, kidney disease8, osteoporosis9 and cancer10. Because of the volatility of VOCs, direct detection of VOC concentrations in breath and blood is challenging. Similarly, the accuracy of urinary VOC measurements can be compromised by losses during collection11. Because mVOCs are more stable and less volatile than their parent compounds, urine VOC samples have higher stability and longer biological half-lives than blood12. The mVOCs are more prone to inducing inflammation in the bloodstream and in vivo, impacting the systemic immune-inflammatory index (SII ) and Systemic Inflammatory Response Index (SIRI) index levels13. And urine also has the advantage of non-invasive sampling, among other things, so the selection of urine VOC concentrations can help to assess the specific impact of VOC metabolites levels on human health14.

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a widespread disease of articular cartilage involving a complex process of inflammation and metabolism, and its prevalence in the population is increasing every year15. It severely affects the quality of life of millions of people worldwide, resulting in pain, dysfunction, and decreased mobility16 and placing a heavy burden on the healthcare system. Between 2013 and 2015, OA resulted in approximately 180 million lost workdays and has also become a significant cause of disability in the U.S.17 In a previous study, Lee et al. found18 that there was an association between exposure to phenol and parabens and the risk of OA in 7114 U.S. adults. Another cross-sectional study19 showed that phthalate metabolites were associated with the incidence of OA risk. Accumulating evidence emphasizes the positive association of VOC metabolites with obesity and oxidative stress (OS)20–22, both of which are known risk factors for OA23. In addition VOCs may play an integral role in biological aging and increase susceptibility to OA. Recent studies have shown that exposure to VOCs is associated with an increased risk of OA in middle-aged and elderly Americans. The mediating effect of aging contributes to the association between co-exposure to VOCs and the risk of OA3. Although previous studies have examined the relationship between VOC metabolites and OA using traditional statistical methods24, and machine learning models have been developed for OA prediction utilizing other data types such as imaging25, to our knowledge, this is the first study to integrate VOC exposure profiles with machine learning algorithms for the purpose of constructing a predictive model for OA risk.

In this study, we acknowledge that relationships between multiple VOC components and demographic covariates may be complex, and that traditional linear statistical and predictive models can be affected by interactions among predictors and by multicollinearity. High-dimensional feature sets combined with limited sample size can also increase the risk of overfitting for many types of models. Therefore, we compare a range of linear and nonlinear predictive approaches using rigorous cross‑validation and model‑selection procedures to identify the best‑performing algorithm for our data26. Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) is a game-theoretic approach that helps to explain the output of any machine learning model. It quantifies the marginal contribution of each feature to a single prediction, thereby providing insights into the model’s decision-making process26–28. Therefore, the aim of this study was to use 17 urinary volatile organic compound metabolite parameters and demographic features, based on the NHANES database. demographic characteristics, to construct an accurate OA incidence risk prediction model using multiple ML and SHAP interpretable machine learning algorithms, and to identify the VOC components of the model that have high values of contribution to OA incidence risk.

Methods

Study population

Publicly available data for NHANES from 2011 to 2018 were obtained from the National Center for Health Statistics (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.htm), part of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, with a total of 39,156 participants collected and combined. To ensure the representativeness of the data and the robustness of the results, metabolites of VOCs(mVOCs)with only similar values, missing values, or below the lower limit of detection (LLOD) in urine were excluded from more than one-third of the total number of participants, ultimately leaving 17 mVOCs to be used in the analysis. The final number of people included in this study was 3683, as shown in Fig. 1.

Flowchart of the study design.

Assessment of urine mVOCs

In this study, 17 mVOCs were analyzed. Table S1 lists detailed information on these mVOCs, including their full names, parent VOCs, and the proportion of 3683 participants who met or exceeded the detection limit. Quantitative analysis of mVOCs in urine was performed using ultraperformance liquid chromatography-electrospray ionization tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-ESI/MSMS)29. Detailed information about the laboratory methods and quality control has been described in previous articles30. For analytes that were detected below the lower limit of detection, the Estimated Fill Value was used. This value is calculated as the lower limit of detection divided by the square root of 2 (LLOD/sqrt31). To minimize the effect of urine dilution on measurement results, we use urinary creatinine to calibrate the level of mVOCs in urine.

Definition of OA

The diagnosis of OA as the primary outcome variable in this study was based on self-reported data from the NHANES dataset (specifically the “Medical Conditions” section). Participants were first asked, “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you have arthritis? If the answer was ‘yes’, participants were then asked ‘What type of arthritis?’ Response options included “rheumatoid arthritis,” “osteoarthritis,” “psoriatic arthritis,” “other ”, “refused” and “don’t know”. Participants who reported having “osteoarthritis” were categorized into the OA group, while those who reported having other types of arthritis or answered equivocally were excluded from the OA analysis32. The interview process was conducted by a trained interviewer in the participant’s home, using a computer-assisted personal interview system. This structured approach significantly reduced the potential for bias and improved the reliability of the questionnaire.

Covariates

The demographic questionnaire collected information on age, gender (male/female), race/ethnicity (Mexican American, Other Hispanic, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Other Race - Including Multi-Racial), Family Poverty Income Ratio (PIR), Marital Status, and Educational Attainment (Less than 9th grade, 9th-11th grade (Includes 12th grade with no diploma), High school graduate/GED or equivalent, Some college or AA degree, College graduate or above). Data on hypertension, diabetes, alcohol use, and smoking status were obtained from health questionnaires. BMI data for each participant were extracted from the examination records and calculated by dividing their weight by the square of their height. Diagnoses of diabetes and hypertension were based on questionnaire data. PIR was categorized into 3 classes: ≤1.30, 1.31 to 3.50, and > 3.5033,34 Smoking status was categorized into smokers, nonsmokers, and undetermined based on whether the individual had smoked 100 or more cigarettes. According to the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism (NIAAA), drinking status is categorized as heavy drinkers and light or moderate drinkers, with heavy drinking defined as drinking more than four standard drinks for men or three standard drinks for women on any given day35. The NIAAA also classifies individuals as heavy drinkers or light or moderate drinkers. Three categories were developed based on the cut-points from the guidelines36: Insufficient (those performing some regular activity, but not reaching the minimum standards of the guidelines), Moderate (those performing 500–1000 MET-minutes of activity per week), and High (those performing N1000 MET-minutes of activity per week). By controlling for covariates, a more comprehensive assessment of the investigated relationships was ensured.

Statistical analysis

Baseline analysis and logistic regression

In the baseline analysis, the wilcoxon rank sum test and pearson’s Chi-squared test were used to compare differences between the two cohorts with and without osteoarthritis. We also performed a log10 transformation of the concentrations of mVOCs, on the basis of which univariate and multivariate logistic regression models were used to assess the association between mVOCs and the incidence of osteoarthritis disease. In the regression models, model 1 was not adjusted for any variables, model 2 was adjusted for age, gender, race, marital status, PIR and education level, and model 3 was further adjusted for smoking, alcohol consumption, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, BMI and waist circumference based on model 2. Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine the correlation between mVOCs. Strongly correlated compounds were visualized using hierarchical clustering heat maps.

Feature selection approach

To ensure a robust and reliable feature set, we utilized both Boruta and LASSO algorithms for feature selection. Boruta, a Random Forest-based method, was employed to capture features with complex, nonlinear interactions. Simultaneously, we applied LASSO regression to exploit its capability to introduce sparsity and manage multicollinearity effectively. By intersecting the features identified by both methods, we isolated a core set of predictors. This combined approach ensures that the final selected features are consistently important across different methodological paradigms, thus enhancing the overall robustness and generalizability of our model.

Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO)

Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO)

LASSO regression improves model refinement by applying a penalty function that compresses select regression coefficients, constraining the sum of their absolute values below a predetermined threshold37,38. We implemented LASSO regression using the glmnet package in R, with family = “binomial” specified for our binary outcome data.

(2)Boruta Algorithm36:

Boruta Algorithm36:

Boruta is a Random Forest-based feature selection method that assesses feature importance by comparing original variables against artificially generated “shadow variables”39. The algorithm iteratively evaluates each variable’s significance relative to its shadow counterpart, continuing for 500 iterations or until stability is achieved. Feature importance results were extracted using the attStats function and processed with a customized adjustdata function40 .

Model development

Based on the training set, a total of eight ML methods were used for model construction which include Logistic Regression (LR), Naive Bayes (NB), Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), Ridge Regression (Ridge), AdaBoost (Ada), Gradient Boosting Classifier (GBC), Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM). These eight ML-based models were used to predict OA caused by exposure to mVOCs. Each of the eight models has its own characteristics, and each ML model was subjected to hyper-parameter tuning and quintuple cross-validation to ensure optimal model performance and reliability. In this study, the models were evaluated using the Accuracy, AUC, Recall, Precision(Prec.), F1, Kappa, MCC, and Brier metrics. The model with the best final results was elucidated using the SHAP algorithm, which assigns a corresponding imputation value (SHAP value) to each variable. These SHAP values provide a quantitative measure of the impact of each feature on prediction accuracy. To visualize the contribution of each feature to the model, we generated a SHAP summary plot41.

All statistical analyses in this study were performed in R software 4.4.2 and Python 3.9.0 environments. Again, a two-tailed test was executed and a p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The process of data processing and analysis is shown in Fig. 1.

Correlation between mVOCs and risk of developing OA

The logistic regression analysis revealed significant associations between mVOCs and OA risk through three progressively adjusted models, as detailed in Table 2.

In the unadjusted Model 1, several mVOCs showed significant associations: AMCC (OR 1.27, 95% CI 1.09–1.47, P = 0.002); CEMA (OR 1.35, 95% CI 1.16–1.57, P < 0.001); BPMA (OR 0.83, 95% CI 0.74–0.93, P = 0.002); DHBMA (OR 1.57, 95% CI 1.29–1.90, P < 0.001); HMPMA (OR 1.30, 95% CI 1.13–1.49, P < 0.001); GAMA (OR 1.28, 95% CI 1.00-1.65, P = 0.050). After adjusting for age, sex, race, marital status, PIR, and education (Model 2), additional significant associations emerged: AAMA (OR 1.34, P = 0.001); 34MHA (OR 1.17, P = 0.024); SBMA (OR 1.18, P = 0.032); CYMA (OR 1.15, P = 0.002); 3HPMA (OR 1.20, P = 0.025); MA (OR 1.34, P = 0.003). The fully adjusted Model 3 (further controlling for smoking, alcohol, diabetes, hypertension, BMI and waist circumference) showed robust associations with: AAMA (OR 1.28, P = 0.009); AMCC (OR 1.20, P = 0.042); CEMA (OR 1.30, P = 0.004); CYMA (OR 1.19, P = 0.001); DHBMA (OR 1.37, P = 0.005); GAMA (OR 1.48, P = 0.008); 3HPMA (OR 1.23, P = 0.016); MA (OR 1.26, P = 0.025); MHBMA3 (OR 1.28, P = 0.002); HMPMA (OR 1.36, P < 0.001).

Notably, several associations showed dose-response patterns across models as more covariates were introduced, while others like BPMA lost significance after full adjustment. The most consistent strong associations were observed for CEMA, DHBMA, HMPMA and GAMA across all models. These findings suggest that specific mVOCs may serve as independent risk factors for OA development.

Correlation between mVOCs

The heatmap analysis (Fig. 2) revealed significant co-expression patterns among mVOCs, with strong correlations (r ≥ 0.6) observed between MA, PGA, and 2MHA; CYMA, GAMA, and AAMA; as well as CYMA, 3HPMA, MHBMA3, HMPMA, and AMCC. Additionally, 3HPMA was highly correlated with CEMA, while 3HPMA, MHBMA3, and HMPMA formed another related cluster, and MA correlated strongly with PGA. Statistically significant correlations of lower magnitude (r < 0.6) were also observed. Hierarchical clustering suggested the presence of two distinct biomarker expression profiles, possibly reflecting different exposure sources, metabolic states, or population subgroups. The clear separation between positive and negative correlation clusters further indicated potential co-expression modules, providing insights into their biological roles.

Model variable selection

Our study included 3683 participants randomly divided into training (n = 2578) and testing (n = 1105) cohorts at a 7:3 ratio. Feature selection was performed using two complementary approaches: (1) the Boruta algorithm (an enhanced Random Forest method) identified 26 key features including the mVOCs 2MHA, 34MHA, AAMA, AMCC, ATCA, CEMA, CYMA, DHBMA, GAMA, 3HPMA, MA, MHBMA3, PGA, and HMPMA, along with demographic/clinical variables Age, Alcohol consumption, BMI, Diabetes status, Education level, Gender, Hypertension status, Race, Waist circumference, and PIR (Fig. 3A); (2) LASSO regression with L1 regularization selected Waist circumference, Hypertension, Gender, Education, DHBMA, HMPMA, Diabetes, BMI, GAMA, PIR, Race, Age, Metabolic equivalents, SBMA, Marital status, and Cigarette use (Fig. 3B, C). The intersection of both methods yielded an 12-feature consensus set (Waist circumference, Hypertension, Gender, Education, DHBMA, HMPMA, Diabetes, BMI, GAMA, PIR, Race and Age) that was used for final model construction (Fig. 3D), ensuring robust variable selection through this dual-algorithm approach.

To optimize model performance, we implemented a 10-fold stratified cross-validation framework with nested hyperparameter tuning. The inner loop employed Bayesian optimization (via the optuna library) over 50 iterations to search the parameter space, ensuring efficient convergence while avoiding overfitting. All tuning was performed within the cross-validation folds to prevent data leakage. Model selection was based on the average validation score (AUC-ROC) across folds, with final evaluation on a held-out test set. Notably, the LDA model has the strongest AUC performance (AUC, 0.75) in the test set of these 8 ML models, which is significantly higher than the other 7 models. Visualization of feature importance

We performed a SHAP analysis to assess the importance of each feature variable in the LDA model and its contribution in the model prediction, and these results are shown in Fig. 6. Figure 6a visually displays this ranking, with each point representing a sample. The color gradient from blue to red indicates the magnitude of the sample’s feature values. The vertical axis shows the importance ranking of the features, while also illustrating the correlation and distribution of each feature value with the SHAP value. Specifically, among the continuous variables, age, BMI, DHBMA, HMPMA, and GAMA have a positive contribution to the prediction results. Figure 6b shows the hierarchical importance of features in the LDA model. The vertical axis ranks features in descending order of importance, while the horizontal axis represents the average SHAP value. The analysis results show that age, gender, hypertension, BMI, and race are the top five features ranked by importance, indicating that they have a critical influence on the presence of OA. To comprehensively interpret the model’s behavior, we employed SHAP multi-decision plots to visualize population-level feature contributions, alongside SHAP waterfall charts for LDA to dissect individual predictions. This dual approach revealed both consistent global patterns and sample-specific exceptions. The SHAP multi-decision plot (Fig. 6c) highlights how feature values collectively drive predictions across subgroups, while the LDA-specific waterfall chart (Fig. 6d) decomposes exact contribution steps for critical samples, ensuring both interpretability and granularity. To better understand the model’s decision-making process at the individual level, we conducted a detailed interpretability analysis on two representative samples (Fig. 6e). Subgroup analysis

To explore whether the associations between mVOCs and OA risk were modified by other demographic and health factors, we conducted comprehensive subgroup analyses for the two most significant VOC metabolites, DHBMA and HMPMA. The results are summarized in Fig. 7.

Subgroup analysis forest plot of two key VOC metabolites (DHBMA, HMPMA) associated with osteoarthritis risk.

For DHBMA, significant positive associations with osteoarthritis risk were observed in several subgroups: men (OR 1.52, 95% CI 1.08–2.14), those with high physical activity level (OR 1.68, 95% CI 1.17–2.41), non-diabetes (OR 1.88, 95% CI 1.08–3.26), pre-diabetes (OR 1.36, 95% CI 1.06–1.75), and hypertension groups (OR 1.48, 95% CI 1.07–2.05). However, no significant interaction effects were detected for any of the examined variables (all P interaction > 0.05).

For HMPMA, significant associations were found in both men (OR 1.41, 95% CI 1.10–1.81) and women (OR 1.32, 95% CI 1.06–1.66), insufficient physical activity group (OR 1.30, 95% CI 1.02–1.67), non-diabetes (OR 1.72, 95% CI 1.15–2.56), pre-diabetes (OR 1.28, 95% CI 1.06–1.54), both hypertension (OR 1.30, 95% CI 1.02–1.64) and non-hypertension groups (OR 1.29, 95% CI 1.03–1.62), and cigarette smokers (OR 1.40, 95% CI 1.10–1.79).

A significant interaction was identified for alcohol consumption on the association between HMPMA and OA (P for interaction = 0.007). The association was particularly strong and significant among heavy drinkers (OR 3.51, 95% CI 1.61–7.62) and in the uncertainty group for alcohol consumption (OR 1.61, 95% CI 1.25–2.09), but absent in those with no or light alcohol intake. In summary, while the associations of DHBMA and HMPMA with OA risk were persistent across many population strata, a significant effect modification was only found for alcohol consumption regarding HMPMA exposure.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a machine learning model using demographic and VOC metabolite data from the NHANES 2011–2018 to predict OA risk in U.S. adults. Conditional logistic regression identified AAMA, AMCC, CEMA, CYMA, DHBMA, 3HPMA, MA, MHBMA, and HMPMA as significantly positively associated with OA risk. Among eight machine learning algorithms tested, the LDA model demonstrated the strongest predictive performance, achieving a cross-validated AUC of 0.755, indicating good discriminatory ability. SHAP analysis revealed that age was the most influential predictor, consistent with previous reports44–46⁠, followed by hypertension, gender, and BMI, which also aligned with established risk factors47,48. Among VOC metabolites, DHBMA and HMPMA contributed substantially to the model, highlighting their potential role in OA pathogenesis. Our subgroup analyses yielded one particularly noteworthy finding: the significant interaction between alcohol consumption and HMPMA exposure on OA risk. The remarkably strong association observed among heavy drinkers suggests a potential synergistic effect. This synergism may be biologically plausible, as both alcohol metabolism and certain mVOCs like crotonaldehyde (the parent compound of HMPMA) can induce substantial oxidative stress and deplete glutathione, a key cellular antioxidant. This combined insult may overwhelm the joint’s defensive capacity, accelerating cartilage degradation49,50. This finding highlights a critical population at risk—individuals with high VOC exposure who are also heavy consumers of alcohol—and suggests that public health interventions targeting OA prevention could be particularly beneficial for this group. For other factors including gender, hypertension, and diabetes, the consistent positive associations across strata, despite non-significant interactions, reinforce DHBMA and HMPMA as robust and independent risk factors for OA, whose effects are pervasive across diverse demographic and health statuses.

During OA, a chronic low-grade inflammatory state persists across articular cartilage, synovium, and other joint tissues, promoting cartilage degradation and structural joint damage. This inflammatory microenvironment may also impair chondrocyte metabolic activity and matrix synthesis, further exacerbating OA pathology51. Supporting a potential link between environmental exposures and inflammation, Hu et al. reported a significant association between mVOCs and elevated systemic inflammation in a large-scale study of 7,007 participants13⁠.

Exposure to VOCs has been linked to oxidative stress (OS)52. OS arises from an imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and clearance. Excess ROS promote chondrocyte apoptosis, inflammation, and suppression of cartilage matrix synthesis53. Furthermore, ROS activate redox-sensitive transcription factors such as NF-κB via phosphorylation of its inhibitor IkB. Once activated, NF-κB translocates to the nucleus and regulates—alone or in concert with other factors (e.g., AP-1, HIF-1α, PPAR-γ, Nrf-2)—the expression of inflammatory mediators54,55. Growing evidence associates specific VOC metabolites with OS. For instance, urinary DHBMA, a metabolite of 1,3-butadiene (BD), correlates with elevated oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction56. Experimental studies indicate that BD induces OS and DNA damage in murine bronchial and lung tissues57. Similarly, crotonaldehyde—a hazardous air pollutant metabolized to HMPMA—triggers oxidative stress58 and inflammation59, and has been shown to cause mitochondrial-mediated OS and inflammation in rats60. In a human biomonitoring study, crotonaldehyde, acrolein, isoprene, and benzene were identified as major contributors to oxidative damage biomarkers (8-OHdG, 8-OHG, diY)61. Acrolein, a biomarker for 3HPMA and CEMA, promotes cellular degranulation and ROS production62. It undergoes metabolic conversion to acrylic acid via methyl ester formation, which subsequently conjugates with glutathione to yield 3HPMA and CEMA63. Additionally, MA—a metabolite of styrene or ethylbenzene—has been implicated in osteoarthritis related to urinary VOC exposure3. Consistent with these findings, Ogbodo et al. reported elevated leukocyte and platelet counts alongside reduced hemoglobin levels in spray-painting workers exposed to ethylbenzene and styrene64.

Hou et al. showed65 that cellular senescence plays an important role in the pathology of OA, with chondrocytes exhibiting a variety of senescence-related phenotypes. Aging is also closely related to oxidative stress and inflammation as we mentioned earlier66. A prospective twin study showed that residential traffic exposure was negatively associated with telomere length at birth and accelerated telomere shortening in the first two decades of life67. Since traffic pollution is one of the major sources of VOCs, we can assume that urinary VOC metabolite concentrations plays a crucial role in aging, which may be be closely related to the fact that VOCs exposure causes inflammation and OS. Shi et al. showed68 that personal exposure to VOCs was significantly and positively correlated with DNA mPhenoAge acceleration. Shorter estimated telomere lengths were observed in men with higher VOC exposure69. It has been previously shown3 that aging has an important moderating role in the association between co-exposure to VOCs and risk of OA.

Our study offers novel insights for environmental health research by enabling personalized OA risk prediction in populations exposed to VOCs. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a cross-sectional analysis, this study cannot assess long-term dynamics in VOC metabolite levels or establish causal relationships between VOC exposure and OA development. Second, OA diagnoses were self-reported via questionnaire in NHANES, without imaging confirmation or clinical detail on severity or joint involvement. Although self-reported OA generally shows good agreement with medical records70, recall bias and cognitive limitations may affect diagnostic accuracy and subsequent model performance. Third, only 17 VOC metabolites were included; future studies should incorporate a broader range of metabolites to better characterize exposure - disease relationships. Fourth, all VOC measurements were taken at a single time point; repeated measurements would improve exposure assessment and reduce misclassification. Fifth, although we adjusted for major confounders, residual or unmeasured confounding may still influence the results. Sixth, although the NHANES sample is drawn from multiple U.S. regions, it remains nationally representative only of the U.S. population and may not be generalizable to other countries or ethnicities. Finally, including variables without established causal links in machine learning models may affect ecological inference. Therefore, further external validation and causal investigations are essential to confirm the clinical applicability and robustness of our model.

Future research should prioritize large-scale prospective cohorts with regular follow-ups to establish causal inferences—using methods such as Mendelian randomization—and to develop predictive models based on longitudinal data. Such approaches would improve long-term prognosis assessment of VOC-related adverse health outcomes and enhance the robustness of the findings. Furthermore, mechanistic studies based on epidemiological results are needed to elucidate the biological pathways through which VOCs and their metabolites influence OA pathogenesis. Additionally, the model developed in this study requires external validation across diverse populations using large-scale environmental exposure databases from other regions (e.g., Europe and Asia). Multicenter validation will help verify the model’s generalizability and ensure its predictive accuracy and clinical utility across different demographic settings.

Conclusion

Based on the findings of this study, the LDA model exhibited the strongest predictive capability for OA risk, achieving an AUC of 0.7551. SHAP-based interpretation identified both demographic factors and specific VOC metabolites as major contributors to the model, with DHBMA and HMPMA emerging as novel and significant risk factors for OA. Further analyses revealed non-linear, dose-response relationships between these metabolites and OA risk, and subgroup analyses consistently supported these associations across diverse demographic strata. These results underscore the value of incorporating environmental exposure indices, particularly VOC metabolites, into machine learning models for improved OA risk assessment. This approach not only enhances early identification of high-risk populations but also provides a scientific basis for developing personalized prevention and intervention strategies aimed at mitigating OA risk associated with environmental VOC exposure.
