Beyond scope of practice: a prescribing autonomy framework to support early-career prescribers
Authors: William Swain, Michelle Styles, Ellen Schafheutle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The introduction of pharmacist independent prescribing at the point of registration represents a profound shift in UK pharmacy practice. From 2026, newly qualified pharmacist independent prescribers will begin their careers with prescribing responsibilities but without the experiential consolidation or structured supervision pathways that underpin early prescribing in other professions. Existing governance mechanisms—particularly the concept of scope of practice—were designed for experienced, often specialist, prescribers and therefore fail to capture the dynamic decision-support needs of novice practitioners working across varied clinical contexts. Competency frameworks also provide limited insight into how prescribers negotiate uncertainty, collaborate with colleagues or calibrate clinical oversight in day-to-day practice. This commentary argues that these developments in the UK require an alternative approach to describing, governing and supporting prescribing practice, particularly at transition points. The Prescribing Autonomy Framework (PAF) proposes a context-sensitive framework that categorises prescribing decision-making into three levels of autonomy—low, medium and high—based on the degree of collaboration, protocol use, clinical oversight and digital decision-support required. By focusing on how prescribing decisions are reached rather than solely on what prescribers are authorised to prescribe, the framework offers a structured vocabulary for framing competence, informing support needs and surfacing risks. It also intersects with concepts of clinical complexity, accountability and risk, with the potential to support richer reflection and governance than existing approaches in the UK. While developed in response to changes in UK pharmacy training, the framework may have broader applicability. It offers a mechanism for supporting early-career prescribers across professions, guiding transitions into new areas of practice and strengthening healthcare governance. As prescribing roles expand internationally, the PAF aims to provide a scalable and adaptable approach to supporting safe, contextualised development of prescribing practice. The framework is presented as a theoretical governance lens and has not yet undergone empirical testing or formal validation.

Introduction

Prescribing roles across UK healthcare have expanded substantially and now include multiple professional groups, including nurses and pharmacists [1]. Until recently, independent prescribing by professionals other than doctors and dentists was associated with advanced practice and required a postgraduate qualification [2]. Following regulatory standards published in 2021, from 2026 all newly qualified pharmacists will register as independent prescribers after completing a five-year pathway comprising a four-year Master of Pharmacy degree and a Foundation Training Year in practice [3].

Pharmacy is the first UK profession to introduce independent prescribing at the point of initial registration, without a prior period of post-qualification consolidation. This contrasts with medicine, where doctors prescribe under supervision during a provisionally registered year before obtaining full registration [4]. Pharmacist prescribers will therefore enter practice with independent prescribing responsibilities from the outset. However, governance and supervision frameworks were largely designed for practitioners who qualified as prescribers some time after registration, and structured support for novice prescribers at entry to the register is not yet consistently embedded across settings. Literature also highlights that pharmacist autonomy in clinical decision-making is shaped by organisational context, particularly in decentralised models of care where responsibility, risk and decision-making are distributed across systems [5].

Regulatory bodies require clinicians to prescribe within their scope of competence and qualifications [6]. Scope of practice typically defines the clinical areas, patient groups or medicines a prescriber may manage safely and reinforces shared organisational and individual responsibility for safe activity [7]. When pharmacist prescribing was a postgraduate qualification, most candidates had substantial experience and clearly defined roles, and scope functioned effectively as a governance mechanism. Evidence suggests that prescribing confidence and willingness to assume responsibility increase with experience, yet pharmacist prescribers may also encounter organisational and supervisory barriers when expanding scope [8–12]. For prescribers who qualify at registration, this experiential grounding is not yet present, and the potential mismatch between training expectations and governance frameworks becomes more visible. Clearer articulation of how prescribing decisions are supported, supervised and developed in early practice is therefore required.

Why a prescribing ‘scope of practice’ is less useful in early practice

Prescribing training and assessment in the initial education and training of pharmacists differs substantially from historical models of post-registration prescribing training in the UK. Prescribing capabilities are taught and assessed over a continuum of five years culminating in demonstration of prescribing learning outcomes during the foundation training year, overseen by a Designated Prescribing Practitioner (DPP) [3]. Focusing on a narrow therapeutic area (often referred to as a prescribing scope of practice) to demonstrate prescribing capability during training may support early consolidation, but it risks anchoring the application of prescribing skills to specific clinical domains and may inadvertently restrict future workforce flexibility [13]. In England, foundation trainee pharmacists must identify a nominated prescribing area to provide a defined context for demonstrating prescribing capability [14]. This represents a pragmatic attempt to balance boundary-setting with flexibility during training. However, while a nominated area provides focus for assessment, it does not articulate how prescribing decisions are reached, how uncertainty is negotiated, or how much decision-support is required in different situations. The remaining gap is therefore not one of defining what prescribers may do, but of calibrating the level of collaboration, clinical oversight, protocol use and digital decision-support required within those boundaries.

Competency frameworks remain central to contemporary health professions education and regulation, providing structured articulation of expected capabilities and alignment between training and assessment [15]. In parallel, models such as Entrustable Professional Activities (EPAs), widely used in medical education and emerging in other disciplines, focus on decisions about supervision and readiness for practice.

Competency frameworks such as the Royal Pharmaceutical Society (RPS)’s prescribing competency framework outline the competencies underpinning safe prescribing [16]. Yet competency frameworks are not designed to capture the specificity of decision-support needs. Trainee prescribers are often required to evidence competence against all descriptors within a framework, which risks conflating minimum adequacy with genuine readiness [17–19]. Competence assessments provide only a snapshot of performance and may not reliably predict future performance or performance in different contexts [20]. Staged models such as Dreyfus describe developmental progression from novice to expert, but these models assume linearity and stability that cannot necessarily be applied to prescribing-related decision-making [21]. Prescribing decisions are sensitive to context, patient complexity, experience, team structures and the availability of supervisory support. The conceptual language of novice-to-expert is easily misinterpreted and may be unhelpful in determining what support a practitioner requires in specific situations.

Neither scope of practice nor competency frameworks describe the nature of support a recently qualified prescriber, particularly one who has also only recently registered as a pharmacist, may need to reach safe prescribing decisions. They describe what prescribers may do, but not how they do it or how much collaboration, protocol use, clinical oversight or digital decision-support is required. For early-career prescribers, this absence of context becomes problematic. Prescribers qualifying at registration are likely to arrive in the workforce with strong theoretical preparation but limited lived experience of negotiating diagnostic ambiguity, managing complex multimorbidity, integrating psychosocial factors or safety netting. They will encounter rapidly shifting demands across practice settings and will require a governance framework that adapts to complexity, enables reflection on decision-support needs and helps both prescribers and organisations to articulate expectations clearly.

Conceptual foundations

Professional autonomy in healthcare has been conceptualised in multiple ways. It has often been associated with discretionary authority and independent clinical decision-making within defined professional boundaries. More recent pharmacy scholarship has moved beyond independence-based interpretations. Forsyth et al. conceptualise pharmacist autonomy as “independence within interdependence”, emphasising that professional agency is exercised within collaborative systems rather than outside them [22].

Rushworth and colleagues extend this position philosophically by framing the pharmacist as a clinician whose autonomy is neither technical independence nor isolated decision-making, but devolved agency enacted within legitimate governance structures [23]. In this view, autonomy is enabled by structured multidisciplinary pathways, collective professional consent and clearly articulated roles. Collaboration, rather than diminishing autonomy, becomes a precondition for its safe and legitimate exercise.

The prescribing autonomy framework

The PAF does not seek to redefine professional autonomy at this professional or sociological level. Instead, it adopts a deliberately bounded and operational use of the term to describe the degree of decision-support reasonably required within a specific prescribing decision. The framework therefore operates at the micro-level of prescribing decision architecture rather than at the macro-level of professional identity or workforce design. Within this framework, “high autonomy” denotes reduced reliance on immediate decision-support in a given clinical context. The PAF does not imply detachment from collaborative practice, collective governance or interprofessional accountability; it instead operationalises these concepts. In this way, the framework is conceptually aligned with relational and clinician-based accounts of autonomy while narrowing its analytical focus to how prescribing decisions are supported in practice.

Graded approaches to autonomy are also used in other safety–critical industries, such as aviation and automotive engineering, where levels of autonomy describe the extent of human oversight required for safe performance [24]. These systems enable nuanced governance, staged development and clarity around expectations. Applied to prescribing practice, a graded autonomy lens allows decision-support requirements to be made explicit, particularly in contexts where clinicians are transitioning into new or expanded responsibilities.

The Prescribing Autonomy Framework (PAF) offers an alternative to the traditional scope-of-practice paradigm by shifting attention from what a prescriber is authorised to do to how a specific prescribing decision is supported. It recognises prescribing as a situated activity shaped by the degree of decision support available and required within a particular context. Table 1 summarises illustrative characteristics associated with each autonomy level. These descriptors operate along sliding scales and are intended to guide contextual judgement rather than function as fixed thresholds. Autonomy levels reflect the overall pattern of decision-support required and should not be determined by any single dimension in isolation.

For the purposes of this framework, within Table 1, “collaboration” refers to the involvement of colleagues in shaping prescribing decisions through shared discussion, advice or co-reasoning. “Clinical oversight” refers to the frequency and intensity of supervisory review, authorisation or retrospective scrutiny associated with a prescribing decision. “Protocols” refer to locally agreed clinical pathways that structure prescribing decisions. Protocols commonly include defined inclusion or exclusion criteria and decision algorithms and may require specific training to be used safely. Digital decision-support refers to the use of digital tools that contribute to prescribing decision-making, including clinical knowledge resources, guidelines, calculators and algorithmic systems. This includes AI-enabled (artificial intelligence) tools, which may differ from traditional resources in their generative or probabilistic outputs.

The autonomy descriptors focus on prescribing decisions rather than on practitioners. A single consultation may therefore involve multiple decision points operating at different autonomy levels. The framework is not intended to classify practitioners’ overall level of professional autonomy, but to prompt reflection on the degree of support required at meaningful prescribing junctures. This distinction matters: a prescriber may demonstrate high autonomy in routine tasks but require more oversight when engaging with unfamiliar clinical presentations or complex cases. The framework captures this variability and positions autonomy as fluid, context-dependent and sensitive to both the clinical environment and the practitioner’s evolving experience. It builds on collaborative clinical reasoning literature, which highlights how clinical decisions are often co-constructed with supervisors, colleagues and the wider context of care [25]. The framework is therefore aligned with contemporary understandings of clinical reasoning as a dynamic, relational and contextual process rather than a solely individual cognitive activity.

The PAF is distinct from models of staged competence or Entrustable Professional Activities (EPAs). Unlike EPA-based systems that describe readiness to take on specific professional activities with varying levels of supervision, autonomy levels in this framework describe the nature of decision support required within prescribing itself. The framework’s flexibility allows it to be applied across settings, professions and levels of experience. It is not an assessment tool but a developmental and governance tool that enables practitioners and organisations to articulate expectations clearly. High autonomy in this framework is not defined simply by the absence of decision-support. It reflects the level of support that would reasonably be required for safe prescribing in that specific clinical context. Avoiding appropriate support in high-complexity situations would indicate a governance concern rather than elevated autonomy.

Intersections with competence, complexity, accountability and risk

Competence is commonly framed as a threshold judgement [17], yet it is enacted differently across contexts [18, 26]. The PAF distinguishes competence from autonomy, positioning autonomy as a contextual descriptor of the decision-support required within a given prescribing activity. Clinical complexity spans biomedical, psychological and social dimensions of care [27], and considering complexity alongside autonomy may enable more precise calibration of decision-support without presuming a fixed relationship between the two. Legal accountability remains with the individual prescriber [28]; however, as prescribing is often collaborative, articulating autonomy levels provides a structured language for explaining how decisions were supported. Autonomy levels are not risk assessments, but when considered alongside complexity they may surface governance signals. For example, sustained high autonomy in high-complexity situations or persistent low autonomy in routine work may prompt more in-depth reflection and risk mitigation.

Application to practice

In practice, the PAF may guide structured discussions between prescribers and supervisors about support needs. Autonomy levels within this framework are not intended to be self-declared in isolation. Determination of the level of decision-support required is best understood as a structured, supervised and context-sensitive judgement, developed through dialogue between prescriber and organisation. The framework operates within existing regulatory standards and patient consent processes and does not replace individual accountability for prescribing decisions. For newly qualified prescribers, autonomy mapping may be used at induction to clarify expectations and establish escalation pathways. Illustrative example of autonomy mapping in practice

The PAF adds an additional dimension to professional development. The RPS guidance on expanding prescribing scope of practice outlines how pharmacists may develop and evidence competence when entering new clinical areas [29]. However, neither framework explicitly differentiates the level of decision-support at which prescribing within that expanded scope is expected to be exercised. A prescriber may therefore broaden their scope yet practise at different levels of autonomy depending on context. When developing a new area of practice, the intended level of autonomy consequently shapes the scale of preparation required. Prescribing within a well-supported environment may require consolidation of transferable skills, whereas preparing to practise safely with less immediate decision-support may demand greater depth of knowledge, experience and clinical judgement.

The framework may also enhance governance and quality assurance. By articulating levels of autonomy used in decision-making, teams can review prescribing incidents or near misses from a new perspective. When developing new areas of practice, the intended level of autonomy at which a prescriber plans to operate can also shape the scale and complexity of learning required. Future development and evaluation

The PAF is conceptual and not a validated assessment or risk tool. Its application depends on professional judgement and shared interpretation, which may vary across teams. Used rigidly, autonomy indicators risk becoming fixed labels rather than reflective prompts.

It should therefore be regarded as a governance lens requiring empirical evaluation before wider adoption. Future work should pilot the framework across diverse settings, examine inter-rater consistency in autonomy mapping discussions, and assess its impact on supervision and risk identification. Mixed-method evaluation will be needed to determine reliability, acceptability and practical utility at scale.

Conclusion

Pharmacy’s transition to early-career prescribing presents a challenge to governance mechanisms based on traditional scope of practice. Such mechanisms may not capture the complexity of prescribing practice or the decision-support needs of prescribers qualifying at registration. The PAF offers a structured, context-sensitive alternative that describes how prescribing decisions are reached and supported. It aims to complement existing frameworks, enhance reflective practice and support governance by articulating levels of collaboration, clinical oversight, protocol use and digital decision-support. The framework has relevance not only for newly qualified pharmacist prescribers but also for transitions across scopes of practice and for other health professions. As prescribing roles expand internationally, the framework offers a pragmatic and adaptable approach that may support prescriber development and early-career practice, and contribute to safer prescribing.
