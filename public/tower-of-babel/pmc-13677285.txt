Standardized Clinical Photography for Improving Dermatological Treatment Adherence (Scoping Review): Evidence Landscape and Research Gaps
Authors: Naisu Cao, Yi Hu, Xiaqing Wang, Siqi Wu, Mengjie Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTreatment adherence remains suboptimal across chronic dermatological conditions, contributing to poor clinical outcomes and increased healthcare burden. Despite the established role of standardized clinical photography in clinical documentation, its specific application as an adherence-promoting intervention remains poorly characterized. This scoping review aimed to systematically map the literature on photographic feedback in dermatology, identify research gaps, and propose methodological recommendations for future randomized controlled trials (RCTs). We conducted a scoping review according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR). Systematic searches of PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science, supplemented by Europe PMC, ClinicalTrials.gov, WHO ICTRP, and Google Scholar (first 200 records), were performed from database inception to December 31, 2025 (searches executed September 20–27, 2026). Of 1181 identified records, 18 studies met the eligibility criteria: five provided direct evidence, five indirect evidence, and eight methodological evidence. Direct evidence comprised three observational studies in hair loss disorders encompassing 3,218 patients (93% of participants derived from a single retrospective cohort), one virtual feasibility study in atopic dermatitis, and one remote photographic wound-monitoring study in keloids; the remaining five prespecified conditions (psoriasis, acne, vitiligo, rosacea, and melasma) yielded zero direct evidence, although vitiligo has an established methodological photography literature. Of the five direct-evidence studies, four measured motivation, satisfaction, task completion, or image-upload compliance rather than treatment adherence per se. Indirect evidence from smartphone applications exists for psoriasis and atopic dermatitis, but these bundle photography with other digital functions. We propose the “early difference refutation” design to address reverse causality in future RCTs. High-quality RCTs are urgently needed to determine whether photographic feedback alone can improve dermatological treatment adherence.Keywords: adherence, clinical photography, dermatology, scoping review, photographic feedback, visual feedback

Introduction

Chronic skin diseases affect millions across all age groups and socioeconomic strata, with psoriasis, atopic dermatitis, acne vulgaris, and vitiligo imposing substantial physical, psychological, and economic burdens.1,2 Treatment adherence remains critically poor:3 in a specialist psoriasis clinic survey, 39% of patients reported non-adherence to prescribed regimens;4 less than 30% of acne patients maintain consistent adherence over one year;5 adherence to topical therapy in atopic dermatitis is similarly poor;6 and 95% of patients applying topical treatments underdose.7 These findings highlight a pervasive adherence crisis undermining treatment efficacy and prolonging disease duration.

Standardized clinical photography has emerged as a promising, low-cost adjunct to enhance adherence through visual feedback. Serial comparison of standardized images enables objective visualization of therapeutic progress otherwise imperceptible to patients, potentially reinforcing motivation and strengthening the therapeutic alliance. Methodological advances in positioning, illumination, and image acquisition have established reliable serial documentation across diverse conditions, while smartphone technology has reduced implementation barriers.

However, visual feedback is not invariably beneficial. During the therapeutic latency period—typically weeks 2–6 for many dermatologic treatments—early objective imaging may reveal no perceptible improvement or even transient worsening, such as minoxidil-induced telogen shedding in androgenetic alopecia. Without structured pre-counseling, the absence of visible early gain can demotivate patients and precipitate premature treatment discontinuation. The adherence value of serial photography therefore likely depends on accompanying communication that establishes realistic temporal expectations; this dual-edged psychological dynamic informed the trial design considerations proposed later in this review.

However, causal interpretation is complicated by reverse causality: patients responding well may return more frequently for photographs, while non-adherent patients may be lost to follow-up. Lee et al8 explicitly noted this limitation in their retrospective alopecia cohort, where improved follow-up rates after photographic introduction could not be definitively attributed to the intervention itself.

Despite decades of clinical use, standardized photography has rarely been investigated as an independent adherence intervention. The dermatological literature contains thousands of studies on adherence and extensive methodological work on clinical photography, yet these two domains have never intersected to position photography as a behavioral adherence tool. This oversight, rather than a null finding, deserves documentation.

We therefore conducted this scoping review with three objectives: (1) map existing literature on standardized photographic feedback for dermatological adherence; (2) identify evidence distribution across specific conditions; and (3) propose the “early difference refutation” framework to address reverse causality in future RCTs.

Methods

Protocol and Registration

This scoping review was conducted according to PRISMA-ScR9 and Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guidance,10 adopting the Arksey and O’Malley framework;11 the study flow is reported following PRISMA 2020.12 The study was not prospectively registered.

Eligibility Criteria

The PCC framework was used to define eligibility.

Population: Patients of any age with dermatological conditions, including hair loss disorders (androgenetic alopecia and alopecia areata), acne vulgaris, psoriasis, atopic dermatitis, vitiligo, rosacea, melasma, and keloids.

Concept: Standardized clinical photography, photographic feedback, or photographic assessment as an intervention or evaluation tool, with at least one adherence-related outcome.

Context: Outpatient dermatology clinics, telemedicine, or mobile health applications.

Study types: RCTs, non-randomized trials, cohort studies, cross-sectional studies, feasibility studies, and reviews.

Exclusion criteria: Case reports, editorials, commentaries, animal studies, and non-English publications. Methodological, measurement, and technical validation literature relevant to standardized photography in dermatology was retained for evidence mapping but analyzed separately from intervention studies.

Information Sources

Systematic searches were conducted in PubMed (MEDLINE), Embase, the Cochrane Library (CENTRAL), Web of Science, and Europe PMC from database inception to December 31, 2025, supplemented by the trial registries ClinicalTrials.gov and WHO ICTRP and by Google Scholar (the first 200 records of approximately 2,070 retrieved). All searches were executed on September 20–27, 2026, and were restricted to English-language records. Reference lists of included studies were manually screened (citation searching). Full search strategies and stage-by-stage record counts are provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Search Strategy

The core search string was: ((“photographic feedback” OR “photo feedback” OR “visual feedback” OR “standardized photography” OR “clinical photography” OR “photographic assessment” OR “photographic documentation”) AND (“treatment adherence” OR “medication adherence” OR “compliance” OR “persistence” OR “concordance”) AND (“dermatology” OR “skin disease” OR “cutaneous disease”)). An expanded string captured digital health interventions incorporating photography. Full strategies are provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Selection Process

Records were imported, deduplicated, and screened by two authors, with AI assistance (Kimi, Moonshot AI) used for initial classification; all screening decisions at both the title/abstract and full-text stages were independently reviewed by both authors and re-checked by a third author, and any discrepancies were resolved by discussion. Reasons for exclusion were recorded. Citation searching (backward and forward reference tracking of included studies and relevant reviews) was prespecified as a complementary strategy because methodological and measurement literature in this field often lacks adherence-related terms in titles and abstracts.

Data Extraction

Data were extracted by one author using a standardized form including: author, year, country, design, condition, sample size, photographic intervention description, adherence outcomes, key findings, and limitations; all extracted data were verified against the source publications by a second author and re-checked by a third author.

Synthesis methods

Given anticipated scarcity and heterogeneity, a narrative synthesis was conducted without meta-analysis. Results were summarized by condition, categorizing studies by photographic component type (clinic-based, smartphone, or digital platform) and adherence outcome nature (objective versus subjective). Given the scoping design, formal risk-of-bias assessment was not mandatory; where informative, design-appropriate tools (RoB 2 for RCTs, NOS for cohort studies, JBI checklists for other designs) were applied and are summarized in Table 1, while feasibility and instrument-validation studies without comparative outcomes were not formally scored.

Results

Search Results and Study Selection

The searches yielded 1,181 records: 1,032 from databases (PubMed, 336; Embase, 392; Cochrane CENTRAL, 42; Web of Science, 39; Europe PMC, 23; Google Scholar, first 200 records) and 149 from trial registries (ClinicalTrials.gov, 101; WHO ICTRP, 48). After removal of 168 duplicates, 1,013 records were screened by title and abstract and 779 were excluded, leaving 234 reports for full-text assessment. Of these, 218 were excluded (non-adherence outcomes, 160; non-photographic intervention, 24; outside the prespecified population scope, 13; editorials, comments, or case reports, 12; non-dermatological condition, 6; methodology or technical validation only, 2; trial withdrawn, 1), two duplicate reports were merged into their primary studies, and one unpublished trial registration was retained for tracking. Eight studies were included through database and registry searching and ten through citation searching, yielding 18 studies in the formal evidence set (Figure 1). Five studies provided direct evidence of photographic feedback as an adherence intervention—three in hair loss disorders, one in atopic dermatitis, and one in keloids (Table 1). Five studies provided indirect evidence from digital health interventions that incorporated photography as a secondary or supportive feature. The remaining eight comprised methodological, assessment-tool, or consensus literature. No randomized controlled trial (RCT) specifically tested standardized photographic feedback as an independent adherence intervention in any dermatologic condition (Table 2). Five ongoing registered studies and one unpublished trial registration were identified (ClinicalTrials.gov/WHO ICTRP) and are listed as potentially relevant upcoming evidence. Four additional studies fell outside the predefined population scope—two in melanoma and skin self-examination research, one in work-related skin disease, and one in chronic low back pain—and were therefore excluded from the formal evidence set; they are discussed separately as cross-disciplinary supportive literature.

Evidence Mapping by Dermatologic Condition

Androgenetic Alopecia and Alopecia Areata

Direct evidence: Three observational studies met criteria, encompassing 3,218 patients in total. Lee et al8 found that introducing serial scalp photography increased 1-year follow-up rates from 13.5% to 35.8% in androgenetic alopecia (AGA) and 16.3% to 50.0% in alopecia areata (AA) (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] for loss to follow-up: 0.51 and 0.41), though the retrospective design precludes causal inference. Pathoulas et al13 reported that 98.3% of 119 hair loss patients felt photographs increased motivation, though this was a single-center convenience sample without a comparator. Park and Lee14 found phototrichogram education improved satisfaction in 100 AGA patients, though adherence was not explicitly measured.

Indirect evidence: Rose et al17 introduced a structured photography protocol, improving technically adequate images from 32% to 66%; methodological barriers to reliable capture remain substantial.

Evidence gap: No RCTs have isolated photographic feedback from other care components, and transferability to other conditions is unknown.

Psoriasis

Direct evidence: None.

Indirect evidence: Domogalla et al18 evaluated a psoriasis app enabling patients to upload photographs of their skin for monitoring in 107 randomized patients; the primary endpoint was mental health, not adherence. Rijsbergen et al19 validated a mobile e-diary in 256 dermatology trial participants in the Netherlands, in which patients photographed each applied medication dose to objectively document adherence (median treatment adherence 98%); the tool addressed measurement rather than intervention.

Evidence gap: A complete absence of direct evidence; indirect studies cannot disentangle photography’s contribution from reminders, education, and remote clinician contact.

Acne Vulgaris

Direct evidence: None.

Related literature (outside the formal evidence set): Hampton et al35 reported favorable reception of a photograph comparison feature in a skin self-monitoring app used across multiple conditions including acne, but no adherence data were collected. Wyatt et al36 found positive patient attitudes toward secure medical photography apps, though neither condition-specific nor adherence-focused. Neither constitutes adherence evidence in acne.

Evidence gap: Despite being one of the most common dermatologic conditions with documented adherence challenges, no direct evidence links photographic feedback to improved adherence.

Atopic Dermatitis

Direct evidence: Ali et al15 conducted a siteless, virtual, reward-based feasibility study in which 55 adults with atopic dermatitis photographed target lesions weekly by smartphone over 8 weeks and completed treatment-use questionnaires; more than 90% of all study tasks were completed and retention was 96% (53/55), although the weekly personalized genetic lifestyle reports used as rewards likely contributed to engagement, and treatment adherence itself was not a comparative endpoint.

Indirect evidence: Marquez-Grap et al20 (n=28) used a mobile app for remote atopic dermatitis (AD) monitoring with 97.6% photographic task completion, but the primary objective was concordance assessment, not adherence improvement. Zvulunov et al21 (n=58) reported an mHealth app incorporating AI-assisted severity evaluation improved Scoring Atopic Dermatitis (SCORAD), but medication adherence was not reported. Ali et al27 demonstrated reliable photo-based remote rating (intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC] 0.90–0.96) in a proof-of-concept study. Related literature: Tier et al6 reviewed AD topical treatment adherence but did not discuss photographic feedback.

Evidence gap: Direct evidence is limited to a single uncontrolled virtual feasibility study demonstrating high task completion and retention with weekly photographic monitoring; whether photographic feedback improves adherence to atopic dermatitis treatment itself remains untested.

Vitiligo

Direct evidence: None.

Methodological evidence: van Geel et al24 systematically reviewed image analysis systems for measuring vitiligo lesion surface area, finding two-dimensional photo-based analysis robust and three-dimensional photography promising. Batchelor et al25 validated the Vitiligo Noticeability Scale using standardized pre- and post-treatment photographs. Thomas et al26 used baseline digital photographs to anchor patient-reported Vitiligo Noticeability Scale outcome assessment in the HI-Light trial. Ezzedine et al,28 on behalf of the Vitiligo Global Issues Consensus Conference (VGICC), established consensus definitions of repigmentation and maintenance of repigmentation incorporating photograph-illustrated descriptors and minimal assessment tools. van Geel et al29 proposed a core set of international recommendations for standardizing serial photography in vitiligo assessment and monitoring.

Evidence gap: No study has repurposed this established photographic infrastructure as an adherence intervention.

Rosacea, Melasma, and Keloids

Direct evidence: Suemitsu et al16 reported the OGOG Project, a nationwide, multi-institutional remote wound-management initiative to prevent keloid and hypertrophic scar formation after cesarean section in Japan (206 cases): patients photographed their wounds at 1, 3, and 6 months postoperatively and uploaded the images to a cloud platform (162, 102, and 45 uploads, respectively); all images were of sufficient quality for evaluation, and structured capture instructions, photographic examples, and scheduled upload timelines were used to support compliance, although long-term upload adherence was suboptimal. The measured outcome was adherence to scheduled image uploads for post-cesarean wound monitoring rather than adherence to a dermatologic treatment regimen, and the report is available only as a conference abstract; this classification as direct evidence should therefore be interpreted with caution. No direct evidence was identified for rosacea or melasma.

Related literature (outside the formal evidence set): Reviews of melasma treatment emphasize adherence and patient education as determinants of outcomes but do not discuss photographic feedback or visual monitoring.37 No literature of any kind addressing photographic feedback was identified for rosacea.

Evidence gap: A complete evidence void persists for rosacea and melasma; for keloids, direct evidence is limited to a single conference-abstract implementation report.

Summary of the Evidence Landscape

Across the eight prespecified dermatologic conditions, direct evidence was identified for three domains: hair loss disorders (three observational studies encompassing 3,218 patients, although a single retrospective cohort contributed 93% of these participants, n=2,999), atopic dermatitis (one virtual feasibility study), and keloids (one remote photographic wound-monitoring study). The remaining five conditions—psoriasis, acne, vitiligo, rosacea, and melasma—returned zero direct evidence. Indirect evidence from digital health RCTs was sparse and bundled photography with other features, precluding isolation of its specific contribution. The core finding is the profound scarcity of evidence: our systematic search identified no RCT that has tested standardized photographic feedback as an independent adherence intervention in any dermatologic condition (Table 2).

Study Characteristics and Risk of Bias

Among the five direct-evidence studies, the study by Lee et al8 carries selection bias risks; Pathoulas et al13 relied on self-reported motivation without a comparator; Park and Lee14 was a single-center study with no comparator; Ali et al15 was an uncontrolled feasibility study in which reward-based engagement may have inflated retention; and Suemitsu et al16 is available only as a conference abstract and reported declining image-upload rates over time. Among indirect evidence, Domogalla et al18 randomized patients but measured mental health rather than adherence. Zvulunov et al21 employed rigorous randomization but did not report adherence outcomes. No study employed a design capable of refuting reverse causality. The proposed “early difference refutation” framework would require demonstrating an adherence benefit before the earliest plausible biological treatment response, thereby excluding the possibility that adherence differences merely reflect perceived efficacy. It should also be noted that four of the five direct-evidence studies were published as letters or conference abstracts, and only Lee et al8 reported a behavioral adherence proxy (follow-up rates); Pathoulas et al13 assessed self-reported motivation, Park and Lee14 assessed satisfaction, and Ali et al15 and Suemitsu et al16 reported study-task completion, retention, and image-upload adherence rather than comparative treatment-adherence effects. Discussion

This scoping review mapped the literature on standardized clinical photography as an adherence intervention in dermatology. While clinical photography is widely adopted for documentation, its potential as a standalone behavioral adherence tool has been almost entirely neglected. The primary contribution is not the synthesis of a large evidence base, but the documentation of its absence.

Principal Findings

Direct Evidence is Extremely Scarce Globally

Only five studies with direct evidence were identified—three observational studies in hair loss disorders, one virtual feasibility study in atopic dermatitis, and one remote photographic wound-monitoring study in keloids. Five conditions—psoriasis, acne, vitiligo, rosacea, and melasma—had no direct evidence whatsoever. Indirect evidence from bundled digital health interventions cannot disentangle photography’s specific contribution. The choice of scoping review methodology was deliberate: the near-total absence of direct evidence precluded quantitative synthesis, and scoping review design enabled wider capture of indirect evidence to map this intersection of two disconnected literatures.

Cross-Disciplinary Supportive Evidence

Four studies fell outside the predefined population scope yet provide relevant context. In skin self-examination research, a pilot RCT showed that a smartphone app delivering patients’ own total-body photography images increased self-examination rates from 58% to 83% at 6 months, with no incremental benefit from added reminders or accountability partners,30 and a video-based intervention improved skin self-examination prevalence in older men;31 in work-related skin diseases, a feasibility study reported high usability of and adherence to a photo-based self-management application.32 Outside dermatology, a randomized trial in chronic low back pain found that visual feedback–based clinical monitoring significantly improved exercise adherence.33 These studies address screening behaviors or non-dermatologic conditions rather than adherence to dermatologic treatment and were therefore excluded from the formal evidence set; nevertheless, they corroborate the behavioral plausibility of visual feedback as an adherence-promoting mechanism across clinical fields. Notably, even the landmark app-based adherence RCT in psoriasis34 included no photographic component—its app relied on daily reminders and electronic dispenser monitoring—and its adherence benefit was significant only at week 4 (65% vs 38%, P=0.004), attenuating at weeks 8 and 26; photography has been absent even from landmark adherence research. Notably, photography-supported adherence evidence does exist in melanoma skin self-examination but fell outside the prespecified population scope of this review; adapting such visual-feedback interventions from screening behaviors to dermatologic treatment adherence represents a concrete future research direction.

Why is Evidence so Scarce?

The Scarcity Likely Stems from Three Factors

Historically, clinical photography has been conceptualized exclusively as an endpoint measurement tool, with its purpose anchored in outcome assessment and documentation rather than behavioral intervention. Technologically, standardized photography required dedicated equipment and expertise until smartphone proliferation democratized access, yet the research community has not exploited this for adherence research. Conceptually, dermatological literature contains extensive methodological discussions on photography but virtually no discourse on its psychological and behavioral impact, reflecting a tendency to separate “measurement” from “intervention”.

The Reverse Causality Problem and the “Early Difference Refutation” Framework

A fundamental challenge is reverse causality: patients responding well may be more motivated to return for photographs, while non-adherent patients drop out. Without randomization, observed associations remain causally uninterpretable.

We propose the “early difference refutation” framework: the framework rests on temporal ordering. The latency to visible effect depends on the treatment modality rather than the diagnosis alone: topical corticosteroids in atopic dermatitis or psoriasis may show visible effects within days to two weeks, whereas minoxidil in alopecia, repigmentation therapy in vitiligo, or biologic agents typically require 8–12 weeks or longer. The early adherence assessment window must therefore be calibrated to the expected onset of the specific treatment under study. For treatments with delayed onset, an adherence benefit detectable within the pre-visible-effect window (for example, at 4–6 weeks for delayed-onset treatments), before any objective therapeutic effect is visible—would argue against reverse causality and favor a genuine intervention effect; its absence would suggest confounding by treatment response. This framework borrows from pharmacoepidemiological approaches exploiting temporal ordering. The framework also accommodates a potential negative feedback loop: if early imaging during the latency window reveals no visible change, patient motivation may decline unless visual feedback is coupled with structured counseling (Figure 2).

Potential Mechanisms

Four interconnected mechanisms are supported by theoretical and cross-disciplinary evidence. First, visual evidence and objective self-monitoring: photographs provide tangible evidence of baseline and incremental changes, revealing subtle improvements invisible to casual observation. Second, patient engagement and therapeutic alliance: being photographed and reviewing images transforms patients from passive recipients to active participants, enhancing psychological investment. Third, habit formation and behavioral rhythm: regular photography sessions serve as temporal anchors structuring treatment routines. Fourth, enhanced patient-physician communication: photographs provide a shared visual language reducing information asymmetry.

Clinical and Research Implications

Despite limited evidence, clinical implications are substantial. Standardized photography is low-cost and scalable; smartphones produce clinically adequate images with simple protocols and minimal additional clinic time. Treatments with delayed onset of visible effect may benefit most, as photographic feedback may serve as a motivational bridge during the critical early months when patients most commonly discontinue treatment. Realizing this potential will also require validated, low-cost smartphone standardization protocols, rigorous image privacy governance, and scripted clinical communication frameworks so that clinicians can deliver visual feedback without fostering unrealistic expectations during early treatment phases.

Research priorities include: (1) RCTs in alopecia, vitiligo, atopic dermatitis, and psoriasis testing photography as an independent intervention, incorporating the early difference refutation framework; (2) AI-assisted image quantification to complement qualitative feedback with objective numerical data; (3) cross-cultural and cross-phototype validation of standardized protocols;38 and (4) long-term follow-up and cost-effectiveness studies.

Strengths and Limitations

This is the first systematic attempt to map photographic feedback as an adherence intervention across common dermatological conditions. The review followed PRISMA-ScR guidelines and searched eight disease domains. Limitations include the near-total absence of direct evidence, inability to disentangle photographic contributions from bundled digital interventions, restriction to English-language literature, and the observational design of nearly all direct-evidence studies. Additionally, the majority of included studies (10 of 18) were identified through citation searching rather than database searching, because the search strings required adherence-related keywords that much of the methodological and measurement literature does not contain; complete search strategies and stage-by-stage record counts are provided in Supplementary Material 1. Data extraction was performed by one author and verified by a second and a third author rather than independently in duplicate. In addition, Google Scholar was searched through a mirror interface with screening limited to the first 200 records, which may limit the reproducibility of that source.

Conclusions

Standardized clinical photography has substantial potential to improve dermatological treatment adherence, particularly for chronic conditions with delayed therapeutic onset. However, the evidence base is inadequate. Direct evidence is limited to five studies—three observational studies in hair loss disorders, one virtual feasibility study in atopic dermatitis, and one remote wound-monitoring study in keloids—and our search identified no RCT testing it as a standalone adherence intervention. Large, common disease domains remain entirely unstudied.

This review has identified a significant research gap. We call upon the dermatology research community to prioritize high-quality RCTs incorporating the “early difference refutation” framework. The documentation of this gap identifies a low-cost, scalable, and theoretically grounded intervention that has been overlooked despite decades of clinical use.
