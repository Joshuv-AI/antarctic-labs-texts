Abstract
Multidimensional quaternion arrays (often referred to as “quaternion tensors”) and their decompositions have recently gained increasing attention in various fields such as color and polarimetric imaging or video processing. Despite this growing interest, the theoretical development of quaternion tensors remains limited. This paper introduces a novel multilinear framework for quaternion arrays, which extends the classical tensor analysis to multidimensional quaternion data in a rigorous manner. Specifically, we propose a new definition of quaternion tensors as ℍ ​ ℝ \mathbb{H}\mathbb{R} -multilinear forms, addressing the challenges posed by the non-commutativity of quaternion multiplication. Within this framework, we establish the Tucker decomposition for quaternion tensors and develop a quaternion Canonical Polyadic Decomposition (Q-CPD). We thoroughly investigate the properties of the Q-CPD, including trivial ambiguities, complex equivalent models, and sufficient conditions for uniqueness. Additionally, we present two algorithms for computing the Q-CPD and demonstrate their effectiveness through numerical experiments. Our results provide a solid theoretical foundation for further research on quaternion tensor decompositions and offer new computational tools for practitioners working with quaternion multiway data.

Introduction

Quaternions are famous in computer science by their ability to efficiently encode three-dimensional (3D) rotations (which is crucial for e.g., computer graphics
[
30
]
).
In addition, recent years have seen an increased interest in quaternion arrays, where quaternions are used to encode as a single scalar a 3D or 4D real vector information.
Important examples include RGB color images
[

For a recent review on the usefulness of quaternion representations in data science, signal and image processing, see
[

[

34
]

Quaternion multiway arrays arise naturally when 3D or 4D vector data is collected across two or more diversities, such as time, space, wavelength, database index, etc.
Over the last decades, an important research effort has been devoted to the study of quaternion vectors and matrices, see e.g.,
[
38
]
for an introduction to quaternion linear algebra.
On the other hand, the study of quaternion higher-order multiway arrays (i.e., with 3 dimensions or more) remains in its infancy.
In particular, the notion of
quaternion tensor
– as a multilinear mathematical object – remains to be properly established, despite being commonly employed as a synonym for multidimensional arrays of quaternions.
Several tensor-like tools for quaternion multiway arrays have been recently proposed
[
27
,
22
,
26
]
and used in color imaging applications
[
37
,
23
]
.
These works essentially build on generalization of the tensor singular
value decomposition (t-SVD)
[
18
,
17
]
to the quaternion domain.
In fact, while in the real and complex cases it is known
[
13
]
that such decompositions can be interpreted as a specific multilinear tensor decomposition (such as the Canonical Polyadic decomposition), the lack of a multilinear definition of quaternion tensors makes it an interesting open question.

This paper deals with the study of quaternion multidimensional arrays from a multilinear point of view to meaningfully define the notion of
quaternion tensors
.
The main difficulty lies in handling the non-commutativity of quaternion multiplication, which complicates the analysis and prevents straightforward extensions of the usual definitions from the real and complex cases.
It builds on our preliminary results in
[
15
]
, where only partial results were presented.
The contributions of this paper are threefold:
(i)
we propose a coordinate-free definition of quaternion tensors as
\mathbb{H}\mathbb{R}
-multilinear forms and identify a quaternion multiway array with the representation of a quaternion tensor in some fixed product basis;
(ii)
we introduce the notion of quaternion Tucker format,
n
-mode products, change-of-basis and characterize their properties;
(iii)
we develop a complete theory for quaternion Canonical Polyadic Decomposition (Q-CPD), including characterization of trivial ambiguities, equivalent complex-valued models and establish several sufficient uniqueness conditions.
Finally, two practical algorithms for computing the Q-CPD are presented and their performance is validated through numerical experiments.
We believe the results of this paper can be of interest to practitioners dealing with quaternion multiway arrays, by providing a general framework for the development of new quaternion tensor decompositions and relevant models for applications that require uniqueness guarantees.
In addition, the theoretical study presented in this paper relies extensively on the necessary distinction between
left
and
right
linear independence of quaternion vectors – due to quaternion non-commutativity – leading us to introduce several new results on left and right ranks and Kruskal ranks of quaternion matrices, which may be of independent interest for researchers in quaternion linear algebra.

2.1
Quaternions

Let
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{C}
denote the usual real and complex field, respectively.
The set of quaternions
\mathbb{H}
defines a 4-dimensional normed division algebra over the real numbers
\mathbb{R}
.
It has canonical basis
\{1,\bm{i},\bm{j},\bm{k}\}
, where
\bm{i},\bm{j},\bm{k}
are imaginary units such that

\bm{i}^{2}=\bm{j}^{2}=\bm{k}^{2}=\bm{i}\bm{j}\bm{k}=-1,\quad\bm{i}\bm{j}=-\bm{j}\bm{i},\bm{i}\bm{j}=\bm{k}\>.

(1)

Any quaternion
q\in\mathbb{H}
can be written as

q=q_{a}+\bm{i}q_{b}+\bm{j}q_{c}+\bm{k}q_{d}\>,

(2)

where
q_{a},q_{b},q_{c},q_{d}\in\mathbb{R}
are called the components of
q
.
The real part of
q
is
\mathrm{Re}\,q=q_{a}
whereas its imaginary part is
\mathrm{Im}\,q=\bm{i}q_{b}+\bm{j}q_{c}+\bm{k}q_{d}
.
A quaternion
q
is said to be purely imaginary (or simply, pure) if
\mathrm{Re}\,q=0
.
The quaternion conjugate of
q
is denoted by
\overline{q}=\mathrm{Re}\,q-\mathrm{Im}\,q
.
The modulus of
q
is
|q|=\sqrt{q\overline{q}}=\sqrt{\overline{q}q}=\sqrt{q_{a}^{2}+q_{b}^{2}+q_{c}^{2}+q_{d}^{2}}
.
Any non-zero quaternion
q
has an inverse
q^{-1}=\overline{q}/|q|^{2}
.
Just like complex numbers, a quaternion can be written in polar form as
q=|q|\exp(\bm{\mu}_{q}\varphi_{q})
, where its axis
\bm{\mu}_{q}
is a pure unit quaternion (i.e., such that
\bm{\mu}_{q}^{2}=-1)
) and where
\varphi_{q}\in\mathbb{R}
is the angle of
q
.

One of the specific features of quaternions is the non-commutativity of the quaternion product.
Indeed, for
p,q\in\mathbb{H}
, one has
pq\neq qp
in general since imaginary units do not commute.
Nonetheless, standard operations such as conjugation and inversion distribute well over products of two quaternions: one has
\overline{(pq)}=\overline{q}\>\overline{p}
and
(pq)^{-1}=q^{-1}p^{-1}
.

Given a pure unit quaternion
\bm{\mu}
(i.e., such that
\mathrm{Re}\,\bm{\mu}=0
and
|\bm{\mu}|=1
), one can define the set
\mathbb{C}_{\bm{\mu}}=\mathbb{R}\oplus\bm{\mu}\mathbb{R}
which is a
complex subfield
of
\mathbb{H}
.
In particular, any quaternion
q
can be written as a combination of two complex numbers in
\mathbb{C}_{\bm{\mu}}
, a procedure known as the
Cayley-Dickson decomposition
of
q
.
For
\bm{\mu}=\bm{i}
, it reads

q=q_{1}+q_{2}\bm{j},\quad q_{1},q_{2}\in\mathbb{C}_{\bm{i}}.

(3)

The decomposition (
3
) permits to establish a useful complex matrix representation of quaternions arrays
[
38
]
, known as the complex matrix adjoint.
This will be investigated in detail in
Section

2.2.2
.

2.2.1
Quaternion matrix operations

Quaternion vectors and matrices can be defined as 1-D and 2-D arrays with quaternion entries.
The
i
-th entry of quaternion vector
\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{H}^{N}
is given by
(\mathbf{q})_{n}=q_{n}\in\mathbb{H}
, while for a quaternion matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{M\times N}
, its
(m,n)
-th entry reads
(\mathbf{A})_{mn}=a_{mn}\in\mathbb{H}
.
The columns of
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{M\times N}
are denoted by
\mathbf{a}_{1},\mathbf{a}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{N}\in\mathbb{H}^{M}
.
The transpose of
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{M\times N}
is denoted by
\mathbf{A}^{\sf T}\in\mathbb{H}^{N\times M}
and its conjugate-transpose is
\mathbf{A}^{\sf H}:=\overline{(\mathbf{A}^{\sf T})}=(\overline{\mathbf{A}})^{\sf T}\in\mathbb{H}^{N\times M}
.
Finally, we denote by
\mathbf{I}_{M}
the identity matrix in
\mathbb{H}^{M\times M}
, which is the same as in
\mathbb{C}^{M\times M}
and
\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
.

Standard operations of linear algebra translate to the quaternion domain provided that operations take into account the non-commutativity of the quaternion product.
Consider
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{M\times N}
and
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{H}^{N\times P}
. Then it is possible to define the direct and reverse quaternion matrix products as

direct quaternion matrix product

\displaystyle(\mathbf{A}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\mathbf{B})_{mp}:=\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{mn}b_{np},

(4)

reverse quaternion matrix product

\displaystyle(\mathbf{A}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\mathbf{B})_{mp}:=\sum_{n=1}^{N}b_{np}a_{mn}.

(5)

The direct or reverse denomination simply refers to the ordering of the two factors in the expression of the matrix product entries: direct refers to the left-to-right direction, while reverse means right-to-left direction.
Note that, in many publications, the direct matrix product is often implicitly used
[
38
]
; however this distinction will appear crucial to our construction of quaternion tensors (see
Section

3
).

Due to quaternion non-commutativity, for two matrices with compatible sizes,
\mathbf{A}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\mathbf{B}\neq\mathbf{A}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\mathbf{B}
in general; however, if one of the matrices has real-valued entries (say e.g.,
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times P}
), then
\mathbf{A}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{A}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\mathbf{B}
and we can drop the subscript to write
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}
if there is no risk of confusion from the context.

Following
[
29
]
1
1

1

In
[
29
]
, the authors refer to direct and reverse quaternion matrix products as “left” and “right”, respectively. To avoid confusion with left and right quaternion vector spaces introduced in the next section, we chose this alternate denomination of quaternion matrix products.
, the direct and reverse quaternion matrix products satisfy the following properties:

transposition

\displaystyle(\mathbf{A}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\mathbf{B})^{\sf T}=\mathbf{B}^{\sf T}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\mathbf{A}^{\sf T}

and

\displaystyle(\mathbf{A}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\mathbf{B})^{\sf T}=\mathbf{B}^{\sf T}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\mathbf{A}^{\sf T},

conjugation

\displaystyle\overline{(\mathbf{A}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\mathbf{B})}=\overline{\mathbf{A}}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\overline{\mathbf{B}}

and

\displaystyle\overline{(\mathbf{A}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\mathbf{B})}=\overline{\mathbf{A}}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\overline{\mathbf{B}},

conjugate-transposition

\displaystyle(\mathbf{A}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\mathbf{B})^{\sf H}=\mathbf{B}^{\sf H}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\mathbf{A}^{\sf H}

and

\displaystyle(\mathbf{A}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\mathbf{B})^{\sf H}=\mathbf{B}^{\sf H}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\mathbf{A}^{\sf H}.

Proofs of these properties are readily obtained from the direct and reverse quaternion matrix product definitions.

The same need to distinguish between direct and reverse product applies to Kronecker and Khatri-Rao products, which are two standard matrix products encountered in tensor algebra.
Given two quaternion matrices
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{M\times N}
and
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{H}^{P\times Q}
, their direct and reverse Kronecker product are defined as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\mathop{\boxtimes}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\mathbf{B}:=\begin{bmatrix}a_{11}\mathbf{B}&\ldots&a_{1N}\mathbf{B}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{M1}\mathbf{B}&\ldots&a_{MN}\mathbf{B}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{H}^{(MP)\times(NQ)},

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\mathop{\boxtimes}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\mathbf{B}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{B}a_{11}&\ldots&\mathbf{B}a_{1N}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{B}a_{M1}&\ldots&\mathbf{B}a_{MN}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{H}^{(MP)\times(NQ)}.

Similarly, given two matrices
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{M\times P}
and
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{H}^{N\times P}
, their direct and reverse Khatri-Rao product (column-wise Kronecker product) are defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\mathop{\odot}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\mathbf{B}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathop{\boxtimes}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\mathbf{b}_{1}&\mathbf{a}_{2}\mathop{\boxtimes}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\mathbf{b}_{2}&\ldots\mathbf{a}_{P}\mathop{\boxtimes}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\mathbf{b}_{P}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{H}^{(MN)\times P},

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\mathop{\odot}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\mathbf{B}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathop{\boxtimes}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\mathbf{b}_{1}&\mathbf{a}_{2}\mathop{\boxtimes}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\mathbf{b}_{2}&\ldots\mathbf{a}_{P}\mathop{\boxtimes}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\mathbf{b}_{P}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{H}^{(MN)\times P}.

Finally, the Hadamard product between two quaternion matrices
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{H}^{M\times N}
is defined as

(\mathbf{A}\boxdot\mathbf{B})_{mn}=a_{mn}b_{mn}\;.

(6)

which is naturally non-commutative, unlike its real and complex counterparts. Therefore, we do not distinguish between direct and reverse product since it simply corresponds to exchanging the positions of the two matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
.

Finally, note that similarly to the quaternion matrix product case, the distinction between direct and reverse operations becomes irrelevant for Kronecker and Khatri-Rao products as long as one of the matrices is real-valued. In this case, we usually drop the subscript if there is no risk of confusion within the context.
More properties on direct and reverse matrix operations can be found in
[
29
]
.

2.2.2
Complex adjoints for quaternion matrix products

The notion of complex adjoint of a quaternion matrix is a key tool in quaternion linear algebra
[
38
]
.
In particular, it permits to establish the properties of quaternion matrices (rank, inversion, eigen-decompositions, etc.) from their complex adjoint counterpart.
The definition of the complex adjoint is implicitly related to the choice of quaternion matrix multiplication, i.e., direct (
4
) or reverse (
5
).
Therefore in full generality one needs to distinguish between
direct
and
reverse
adjoints.
Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{M\times N}
and consider its Cayley-Dickson decomposition
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}_{1}+\mathbf{A}_{2}\bm{j}
, where
\mathbf{A}_{1},\mathbf{A}_{2}
are matrices in
\mathbb{C}^{2M\times 2N}_{\bm{i}}
.
The
direct
complex adjoint
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{A})
and
reverse
complex adjoint
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}(\mathbf{A})
enable an isomorphism between quaternion matrices (equipped with the corresponding matrix product) and complex matrices with block structure, such that

\displaystyle\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{A})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{1}&\mathbf{A}_{2}\\
-\overline{\mathbf{A}}_{2}&\overline{\mathbf{A}}_{1}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{2M\times 2N}_{\bm{i}},

(7)

\displaystyle\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}(\mathbf{A})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{1}&-\overline{\mathbf{A}}_{2}\\
\mathbf{A}_{2}&\overline{\mathbf{A}}_{1}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{2M\times 2N}_{\bm{i}}.

(8)

The only difference – yet crucial – between (
7
) and (
8
) resides in the placement of anti-diagonal blocks.

Proposition

1
below summarizes important properties of these complex adjoints.

Proposition 1
(Properties of the complex adjoint).

Let
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
be two quaternion matrices with arbitrary dimensions.
The direct (
7
) and reverse (
8
) complex adjoint satisfy the following properties:

•

\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{I}_{M})=\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}(\mathbf{I}_{M})=\mathbf{I}_{2M}
;

•

\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B})=\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{A})+\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{B})
and
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B})=\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}(\mathbf{A})+\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}(\mathbf{B})
if
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
have the same size;

•

\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{A}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\mathbf{B})=\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{A})\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{B})
and
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}(\mathbf{A}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\mathbf{B})=\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}(\mathbf{A})\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}(\mathbf{B})
if one can form the matrix product between
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
;

•

\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{A}^{\sf H})=\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}^{\sf H}(\mathbf{A})
and
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}(\mathbf{A}^{\sf H})=\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}^{\sf H}(\mathbf{A})
;

•

\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{A})=\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}^{\top}(\mathbf{A}^{\top})
;

The proof of these results follows by direct calculations. For additional properties, we refer the reader to
[
38
]
, where the direct complex adjoint is considered - similar results hold for the reverse adjoint by simple adaption of the proofs therein.

Remark 2
.

The standard definitions (
7
)–(
8
) of complex adjoints of a quaternion matrix can be related to the horizontal stack of the adjoint of each of its columns.
Let
\mathbf{A}=[\mathbf{a}_{1}\>\mathbf{a}_{2}\>\ldots\>\mathbf{a}_{N}]\in\mathbb{H}^{M\times N}
, and introduce the direct column-wise complex adjoint

\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}^{\pi}(\mathbf{A})=\begin{bmatrix}\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{a}_{1})\mid\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{a}_{2})\mid\ldots\mid\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{a}_{N})\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{2M\times 2N}_{\bm{i}}.

(9)

From the definition of the direct complex adjoint for a quaternion vector, it is easy to see that there exists a permutation matrix
\bm{\Pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times 2N}
such that
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{A})=\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}^{\pi}(\mathbf{A})\bm{\Pi}
.
Equation (
9
) also defines a uniform partition of
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}^{\pi}(\mathbf{A})
in
N
submatrices
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{a}_{n})\in\mathbb{C}_{\bm{i}}^{2M\times 2}
.
The same definition and properties holds for the columnwise reverse complex adjoint, which we denote by
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}^{\pi}(\mathbf{A})
.
This will be useful later on for the study of tensor decompositions of quaternion multiway arrays.

2.2.3
Quaternion vector spaces

The set of quaternions
\mathbb{H}
is non-commutative and therefore it is not a field.
As a result, since vector spaces are defined over a field (e.g.,
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
), it is not possible to define the notion of vector space over
\mathbb{H}
,
per se
.
Strictly speaking, one has to consider the notion of left- or right-module, which can be viewed as an extension of vector spaces where scalar multiplication is performed over a non-commutative ring.
However, in the case of quaternions, it is common practice
[
28
]
to call left (resp. right)
\mathbb{H}
-modules left (resp. right) quaternion vector spaces, with little abuse.
In particular, since
\mathbb{H}
is a division algebra, quaternion vector spaces exhibit the usual properties of vector spaces (dimension, basis, linear maps, etc. ) up to the necessary distinction between left and right linearity.

For completeness, we give the definition of a left quaternion vector space below. The definition for a right vector space follows by direct adaption.

Definition 3
(Left quaternion vector space
[
16
]
).

A left vector space
\mathcal{H}
over
\mathbb{H}
is an additive abelian group in which the operation of scalar multiplication by elements of
\mathbb{H}
is defined.
Scalar multiplication is assumed to obey the following laws for all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{H}
and
q,p\in\mathbb{H}
:

•

q(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y})=q\mathbf{x}+q\mathbf{y}
;

•

(q+p)\mathbf{x}=q\mathbf{x}+p\mathbf{x}
;

•

(qp)\mathbf{x}=q(p\mathbf{x})
;

•

1\cdot\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}
, where
1\in\mathbb{H}
.

A subscript
L
or
R
permits to distinguish between left and right quaternion vector spaces.
Two important examples are:
\mathbb{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm L}^{M}
, the left quaternion vector space of vectors in
\mathbb{H}^{M}
and
\mathbb{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm R}^{M}
, the right quaternion vector space of vectors in
\mathbb{H}^{M}
.

2.2.4
The rank and Kruskal rank of quaternion matrices

In this section we review the
rank
of a quaternion matrix and extend the definition of the Kruskal rank, or simply
\krank
-rank, to quaternion matrices.
In the real and complex cases, the rank of a matrix refers to the maximum number of
linearly independent
columns (or rows) in the matrix.
When extending this notion to quaternion matrices, non-commutativity of quaternion multiplication calls once again for a distinction between left and right linear independence.
The rank of a quaternion matrix
\mathbf{A}
is usually defined to be the maximum number of
right
linearly independent columns
[
38
]
.
However, as it will appear clearly in the sequel, the theory presented in this paper calls for a distinction between the
left
and
right
ranks (and thus,
\krank
-ranks) of a quaternion matrix.

Definition 4
(Left and right columns spans).

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{M\times N}
and denote by
\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{N}\in\mathbb{H}^{M}
its columns. Then, the span of left-linear combination of columns of
\mathbf{A}
is denoted as

\lspan(\mathbf{A}):=\left\{\sum_{n=0}^{N}x_{n}\mathbf{a}_{n}\mid x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{H}\right\},

(10)

and the span of right-linear combinations of columns of
\mathbf{A}
is given by

\rspan(\mathbf{A}):=\left\{\sum_{n=0}^{N}\mathbf{a}_{n}x_{n}\mid x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{H}\right\}.

(11)

From these definitions, one observes that
\lspan(\mathbf{A})
defines a left vector space over the columns of
\mathbf{A}
, while
\rspan(\mathbf{A})
defines a right vector space over the same columns.

The following definition of left and right ranks of a quaternion matrix follows naturally.

Definition 5
(Left and right ranks of a quaternion matrix).

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{M\times N}
. The left rank of
\mathbf{A}
is defined as
\lrank\mathbf{A}=\dim\lspan(\mathbf{A})
while the right rank is given by
\rrank\mathbf{A}=\dim\rspan(\mathbf{A})
.

Remark 6
.

For a real or complex matrix
\mathbf{A}
, we drop the subscripts and keep the unambiguous notations
\Span(\mathbf{A})
to denote the span of its columns and its rank by
\rank\mathbf{A}
.

As in the usual real and complex cases, the dimension of the left (resp. right) column span encodes the maximal number of left (resp. right) linearly independent vectors.
For quaternion matrices, the left and right ranks are in general different; see e.g.,
[
38
, Example 7.3]
.
Hence the distinction is critical.
Nonetheless, the left and right ranks satisfy several important properties similar to their real and complex counterparts.
In particular, for a quaternion matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{M\times N}
, the rank cannot exceed its dimensions, i.e.,
\lrank\mathbf{A}\leq\min(M,N)
and
\rrank\mathbf{A}\leq\min(M,N)
.
The matrix
\mathbf{A}
is said to be full left rank if
\lrank\mathbf{A}=\min(M,N)
and full right rank if
\rrank\mathbf{A}=\min(M,N)
.

The following proposition relates the left and right ranks of a quaternion matrix to those of its direct and reverse complex adjoints.

Proposition 7
(Left and right ranks from complex adjoints).

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{M\times N}
be a quaternion matrix.
Then the left and right ranks of
\mathbf{A}
can be obtained from direct and reverse complex adjoints as

1.

\rank{\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)}=2\rrank\mathbf{A}
;

2.

\rank{\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)}=2\lrank\mathbf{A}
.

Proof.

We only prove the first statement; the second follows from direct adaptation of the proof.
Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{M\times N}
and suppose that
\rrank\mathbf{A}=r\leq\min(M,N)
.

Let us first prove that
\rank\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{A})\geq 2r
.
Since
\rrank\mathbf{A}=r
, this implies that there exist
r
columns
\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{r}
of
\mathbf{A}
which are right linearly independent.
Denote by
\mathbf{A}_{r}=[\mathbf{a}_{1}\cdots\mathbf{a}_{r}]\in\mathbb{H}^{M\times r}
this submatrix of
\mathbf{A}
.
Now let
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{C}_{\bm{i}}^{2r}
such that
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{A}_{r})\bm{\beta}=0
.
Partition
\bm{\beta}=[\bm{\beta}_{1}^{\top}\>-\overline{\bm{\beta}_{2}}^{\top}]^{\top}
with
\bm{\beta}_{1},\bm{\beta}_{2}\in\mathbb{C}_{\bm{i}}^{r}
.
The complex adjoint structure of
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{A}_{r})
implies that
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{A}_{r})\bm{\beta}^{\perp}=0
as well, where
\bm{\beta}^{\perp}:=[\bm{\beta}^{\top}_{2}\>\overline{\bm{\beta}_{1}}^{\top}]^{\top}
.
Therefore, posing
\bm{\alpha}=\bm{\beta}_{1}+\bm{\beta}_{2}\bm{j}
and observing that
[\bm{\beta}\>\bm{\beta}^{\perp}]=\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\bm{\alpha})
, one has
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{A}_{r})\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\bm{\alpha})=0.

Using the properties of the direct adjoint
we thus have
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{A}_{r}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\bm{\alpha})=0
and then
\mathbf{A}_{r}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\bm{\alpha}=0.

Since we have supposed that the columns of
\mathbf{A}_{r}
were linearly independent, we necessarily have
\bm{\alpha}=0
hence
\bm{\beta}=0
. Therefore the
2r
columns of
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{A}_{r})
are linearly independent over
\mathbb{C}_{\bm{i}}
. Since
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{A}_{r})
is a submatrix of
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{A})
we have
\rank\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{A})\geq 2r
.

We now prove that
\rank\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{A})\leq 2r
.
Since
\rrank\mathbf{A}=r
, upon proper permutations of columns, one can factorize
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}_{r}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}[\mathbf{I}_{r}\>\mathbf{B}]
, where
\mathbf{A}_{r}=[\mathbf{a}_{1}\cdots\mathbf{a}_{r}]\in\mathbb{H}^{M\times r}
has linearly independent columns and where
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{H}^{r\times(N-r)}
encodes the right-linear combinations that yields the remaining
N-r
columns of
\mathbf{A}
.
Applying the direct complex adjoint to this factorization, one gets

\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{A})=\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{A}_{r})\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}([\mathbf{I}_{r}\>\mathbf{B}])
.
This shows that
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{A})
can always be factorized as a product of a
2M\times 2r
matrix by a
2r\times 2N
matrix, hence
\rank\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{A})\leq 2r
.
Bringing the two parts together yields
\rank\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{A})=2r=2\rrank\mathbf{A}
.

The definition of the left and and right Kruskal ranks follows naturally from that of the left and right ranks of a quaternion matrix.

Definition 8
(Left and right Kruskal ranks of a quaternion matrix).

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{M\times N}
be a quaternion matrix. Then:

•

the left Kruskal rank of
\mathbf{A}
is denoted by
\lkrank(\mathbf{A})
. It is the maximal number
r
such that any set of
r
columns of
\mathbf{A}
are
left
linearly independent.

•

the right Kruskal rank of
\mathbf{A}
is denoted by
\rkrank(\mathbf{A})
. It is the maximal number
r
such that any set of
r
columns of
\mathbf{A}
are
right
linearly independent.

For a real or complex matrix
\mathbf{A}
, we simply write
\krank(\mathbf{A})
to denote its usual Kruskal rank. For quaternion matrices, we use the shorthand
\lkrank
-rank and
\rkrank
-rank to mean the left and right Kruskal ranks, respectively.

It is possible to relate the
\lkrank
-rank and
\rkrank
-rank of a quaternion matrix to the
\kprank
-rank of its direct and reverse column-wise complex adjoints (see definitions in
Remark

2
).
Let us first recall the definition of the
\kprank
-rank of a partitioned matrix.

Definition 9
(
\kprank
-rank of a partitioned matrix
[
8
]
).

Let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{F}^{M\times N}
be a real or complex partitioned matrix (not necessarily uniformly).
Then the
\kprank
-rank of
\mathbf{X}
, denoted by
\kprank(\mathbf{X})
, is the maximal number
r
such that any set of
r
submatrices of
\mathbf{X}
yields a set of linearly independent columns.

The following lemma establishes the relation between the
\lkrank
-rank and
\rkrank
-rank of a quaternion matrix and the
\kprank
-rank of its column-wise complex adjoints.

Lemma 10
(
\kprank
-rank of column-wise adjoint matrices).

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{M\times N}
and let
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}^{\pi}(\mathbf{A})\in\mathbb{C}_{\bm{i}}^{2M\times 2N}
and
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}^{\pi}(\mathbf{A})\in\mathbb{C}_{\bm{i}}^{2M\times 2N}
be its column-wise direct complex adjoint and reverse complex adjoint, respectively, with the uniform partition introduced in
Remark

2
.
Then
\rkrank(\mathbf{A})=\kprank(\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}^{\pi}(\mathbf{A}))
and
\lkrank(\mathbf{A})=\kprank(\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}^{\pi}(\mathbf{A}))
.

Proof.

We start by proving the result for the
\rkrank
-rank of
\mathbf{A}
.
Let us assume that
\rkrank(\mathbf{A})=c
.
According to the definition of the
\rkrank
-rank, any subset of
c
columns of
\mathbf{A}
forms a submatrix
\mathbf{A}_{c}
of
\mathbf{A}
such that
\rrank\mathbf{A}_{c}=c
.
Using
Proposition

7
, we obtain
\rank{\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{A}_{c})}=2c
.
This is equivalent to saying that any set of direct adjoint matrices associated with the
c
columns of
\mathbf{A}_{c}
yields a set of linearly independent columns of
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}^{\pi}(\mathbf{A})
, which, by Definition
9
implies
\kprank(\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}^{\pi}(\mathbf{A}))\geq c=\rkrank(\mathbf{A})
.

Using once again the definition of the
\rkrank
-rank, there exists at least a subset of
c+1
columns of
\mathbf{A}
, such that the matrix
{\mathbf{A}_{c+1}}
formed by these
c+1
columns is right rank deficient, i.e.,
\rrank{\mathbf{A}_{c+1}}<c+1
, implying
\rank{\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{A}_{c+1})}<2(c+1)
. Consequently, as
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{A})=\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}^{\pi}(\mathbf{A})\bm{\Pi}
, there exists a set of
c+1
submatrices of
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}^{\pi}(\mathbf{A})
that yields a set of linearly dependent columns, thereby completing the proof.
The second result can be proved in an analogous way, by replacing
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{A})
by
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}(\mathbf{A})
and right ranks by left ranks.

This result will be essential to the study of uniqueness conditions for the quaternion Canonical Polyadic Decomposition, see further in
Section

4
.

3
Quaternion tensors: construction and properties

In the standard real and complex cases, three alternative definitions of
tensors
are possible
[
21
]
: tensors as objects obeying certain transformation rules, tensors as multilinear maps or tensors as elements of tensor product spaces.
While the first definition has historical importance, it has now been superseded by the two other definitions
[
5
]
.
Over the past few years, there has been an increasing interest in studying multidimensional arrays of quaternions
[
27
,
22
,
26
,
37
,
23
]
; while the term
quaternion tensor
is often used, a proper algebraic, coordinate-free definition remains to be established.

In this paper, we choose to construct quaternion tensors as quaternion-valued multilinear forms.
Our definition of a quaternion tensor can be viewed as an instance of
[
21
, Definition 3.3]
specified to quaternion-valued forms satisfying sufficient multilinearity properties.
More precisely, we identify a tensor of order
D
with a quaternion-valued multilinear form
f:\mathcal{S}_{1}\times\mathcal{S}_{2}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{S}_{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}
where
\{\mathcal{S}_{d}\}_{d=1}^{D}
are some carefully chosen vector spaces.
This definition enables a natural generalization of the notion of tensors to the quaternion domain, while revealing the crucial caveats associated to the non-commutativity of quaternion multiplication.
In what follows,
Section

3.1
discusses the issue of quaternion multilinearity,
Section

3.2
establishes the definition of quaternion tensors as multilinear forms.

Section

3.3
derives elementary transformations for quaternion-valued tensors, which are direct consequences of
Definition

13
below.
Finally,
Section

3.4
introduces the Tucker format of a quaternion tensor.

3.1
The quaternion multilinearity issue

The definition of quaternion multilinearity is cumbersome due to the non-commutativity of quaternion multiplication.
To illustrate this, considering the three-dimensional case is sufficient.
Let
\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2},\mathcal{S}_{3}
be three left quaternion vector spaces and let
f:\mathcal{S}_{1}\times\mathcal{S}_{2}\times\mathcal{S}_{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}
be a multilinear form.
By definition,
f
is
left
-linear in each one of its arguments, i.e., for any
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{H}
and for any
\mathbf{x}_{d},\mathbf{y}_{d}\in\mathcal{S}_{d}
with
d=1,2,3
, one has

\displaystyle f(\alpha\mathbf{x}_{1}+\beta\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{3})=\alpha f(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{3})+\beta f(\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{3}),

(12)

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x}_{1},\alpha\mathbf{x}_{2}+\beta\mathbf{y}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{3})=\alpha f(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{3})+\beta f(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{3}),

(13)

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\alpha\mathbf{x}_{3}+\beta\mathbf{y}_{3})=\alpha f(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{3})+\beta f(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{y}_{3}).

(14)

However, when one has to evaluate quantities such as
f(\alpha\mathbf{x}_{1},\beta\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{3})
, one is faced with a disturbing issue: in which order should linearity properties (
12
) – (
14
) be applied?
Starting with (
12
) and then (
13
) gives
f(\alpha\mathbf{x}_{1},\beta\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{3})=\alpha\beta f(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{3})
, while the reversed order yields
f(\alpha\mathbf{x}_{1},\beta\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{3})=\beta\alpha f(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{3})
.
Since the product of two quaternions is non-commutative,
\alpha\beta\neq\beta\alpha
in general and thus
\beta\alpha f(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{3})\neq\alpha\beta f(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{3})
.
Any other choice of variables leads to similar contradictions.
In other terms, without specifying a somewhat arbitrary ordering of the variables
\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}
and
\mathbf{x}_{3}
(or equivalently, of quaternion vector spaces
\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2}
and
\mathcal{S}_{3}
), the definition of quaternion multilinearity is unpractical as it is.

The issue is not limited to left quaternion vector spaces.
Considering
\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2},\mathcal{S}_{3}
to be right quaternion vector spaces leads to the same conclusions.
Mixing left and right quaternion vector spaces does not solve the issue either.
To see this, suppose without loss of generality that
\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2}
are left quaternion vector spaces, while
\mathcal{S}_{3}
is a right quaternion vector space.
Quaternion multilinearity between the first and last variables is unambiguous: indeed, for any
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{H}
, one has
f(\alpha\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{3}\beta)=\alpha f(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{3}\beta)=f(\alpha\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{3})\beta=\alpha f(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{3})\beta
.
However, the first two modes suffer from the previous issue since for any
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{H}
,
f(\alpha\mathbf{x}_{1},\beta\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{3})
can be equal to
\alpha\beta f(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{3})
or
\beta\alpha f(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{3})
, depending on the order in which linearity properties are applied.

In the next section, we circumvent the issue of general quaternion multilinearity by introducing a special class of multilinear quaternion-valued forms.
By carefully specifying the nature of the vector spaces
\{\mathcal{S}_{d}\}
, we can recover fundamental multilinearity properties, similar to those in the real and complex cases. This class forms the foundation for the definition of quaternion tensors in Section
3.2
and the establishment of their properties.

3.2
Quaternion tensors as
\mathbb{H}\mathbb{R}
-multilinear forms

In order to extend relevant multilinearity-like properties to the quaternion linear form
f:\mathcal{S}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{S}_{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}
, it is necessary to restrict the nature of the different vector spaces
\{\mathcal{S}_{d}\}
defining the domain of
f
.

Definition 11
(
\mathbb{H}\mathbb{R}
-multilinear form).

Let
D\geq 3
be an integer and let the vector spaces
\{\mathcal{S}_{d}\}
be such that:
\mathcal{S}_{1}
is a
left
quaternion vector space;
\mathcal{S}_{d}
,
1<d<D
are real vector spaces; and such that
\mathcal{S}_{D}
is a
right
quaternion vector space.
Then
f:\mathcal{S}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{S}_{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}
is said to be
\mathbb{H}\mathbb{R}
-multilinear if

•

f
is left-quaternion linear in its first argument, i.e.,

\forall\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{H},\forall\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{1}\in\mathcal{S}_{1},f(\alpha\mathbf{x}_{1}+\beta\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots)=\alpha f(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots)+\beta f(\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots);

(15)

•

f
is real-linear in its
d
-th argument,
1<d<D
, i.e.,

\begin{split}\forall\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R},\forall\mathbf{x}_{d},\mathbf{y}_{d}\in\mathcal{S}_{d},&\\
f(\ldots,\alpha\mathbf{x}_{d}+\beta\mathbf{y}_{d},\ldots)&=f(\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{d}\alpha+\mathbf{y}_{d}\beta,\ldots)\\
&=\alpha f(\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{d},\ldots)+\beta f(\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{d},\ldots)\\
&=f(\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{d},\ldots)\alpha+f(\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{d},\ldots)\beta;\end{split}

(16)

•

f
is right-quaternion linear in its last argument, i.e., :

\forall\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{H},\forall\mathbf{x}_{D},\mathbf{y}_{D}\in\mathcal{S}_{D},f(\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{D}\alpha+\mathbf{y}_{D}\beta)=f(\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{D})\alpha+f(\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{D})\beta.

(17)

Remark 12
.

Several remarks are in order.
The term
\mathbb{H}\mathbb{R}
-multilinearity refers to the fact that conditions (
15
)–(
17
) mix between real and quaternion linearity properties.
In addition, note that if a function is
\mathbb{H}\mathbb{R}
-multilinear, by definition it is also
\mathbb{R}
-multilinear.
Finally, it is worth noting that the ordering of vector spaces in Definition
11
is completely arbitrary, and can be permuted if necessary. The only restriction lies in considering exactly one quaternion left vector space, one quaternion right vector space, and
D-2
real-vector spaces for an arbitrary number of dimensions
D\geq 3
.

In the sequel, we restrict our attention to finite dimensional vector spaces
\{\mathcal{S}_{d}\}
with respective dimension
\dim\mathcal{S}_{d}=N_{d}
.
Therefore, without loss of generality, we can assume that
\mathcal{S}_{1}=\mathbb{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm L}^{N_{1}}
,
\mathcal{S}_{d}=\mathbb{R}^{N_{d}}
for
1<d<D
and
\mathcal{S}_{D}=\mathbb{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm R}^{N_{D}}
.
The next definition defines quaternion tensors as
\mathbb{H}\mathbb{R}
-multilinear forms.

Definition 13
(Quaternion tensor).

Let
f:\mathbb{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm L}^{N_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{N_{2}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{N_{D-1}}\times\mathbb{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm R}^{N_{D}}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}
be a
\mathbb{H}\mathbb{R}
-multilinear form.
Let
\{\mathbf{e}^{(d)}_{i_{d}}\}_{i_{d}=1:N_{d}}
denote a basis of the vector space
\mathcal{S}_{d}
for
1\leq d\leq D
.
Then the quaternion tensor of order
D
associated to
f
is the
D
-dimensional quaternion array
\bm{\mathcal{T}}\in\mathbb{H}^{N_{1}\times\ldots\times N_{D}}
defined by its entries as

\mathcal{T}_{i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{D}}=f\left(\mathbf{e}^{(1)}_{i_{1}},\mathbf{e}^{(2)}_{i_{2}},\ldots,\mathbf{e}^{(D)}_{i_{D}}\right)

(18)

i.e.,
\bm{\mathcal{T}}
is the representation of
f
in the Cartesian product basis
\bigtimes_{d=1}^{D}\{\mathbf{e}^{(d)}_{i_{d}}\}_{i_{d}=1:N_{d}}
.

Remark 14
.

Following standard practice in multilinear algebra, we identify a tensor with its multidimensional array coordinate representation in a given basis.

Definition

13
suggests that different choices of bases for
\{\mathcal{S}_{d}\}
lead to different multidimensional quaternion arrays representations of the same
\mathbb{H}\mathbb{R}
-multilinear form
f
.
Therefore a natural question is how these representations are related to one another.
Let
f:\mathbb{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm L}^{N_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{N_{2}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{N_{D-1}}\times\mathbb{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm R}^{N_{D}}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}
be a
\mathbb{H}\mathbb{R}
-multilinear form and consider two quaternion multidimensional array representations:

\displaystyle\text{``new'' representation $\bm{\mathcal{T}}\in\mathbb{H}^{N_{1}\times\ldots\times N_{D}}$ in the basis $\bigtimes_{d=1}^{D}\{\mathbf{e}^{(d)}_{i_{d}}\}_{i_{d}=1:N_{d}}$},

(19)

\displaystyle\text{``old'' representation $\bm{\mathcal{\tilde{T}}}\in\mathbb{H}^{N_{1}\times\ldots\times N_{D}}$ in the basis $\bigtimes_{d=1}^{D}\{\tilde{\mathbf{e}}^{(d)}_{j_{d}}\}_{j_{d}=1:N_{d}}$}.

(20)

Expressing “new” basis vectors as a function of “old” ones one gets

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}_{i_{1}}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{N_{1}}a^{(1)}_{i_{1}j_{1}}\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{j_{1}}^{(1)},

\displaystyle a^{(1)}_{i_{1}j_{1}}\in\mathbb{H},\quad

\displaystyle i_{1}=1,\ldots,N_{1}

(21)

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}_{i_{d}}^{(d)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j_{d}=1}^{N_{d}}a^{(d)}_{i_{d}j_{d}}\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{j_{d}}^{(d)},

\displaystyle a^{(d)}_{i_{d}j_{d}}\in\mathbb{R},\quad

\displaystyle i_{d}=1,\ldots,N_{d}\text{ and }1<d<D

(22)

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}_{i_{D}}^{(D)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j_{D}=1}^{N_{D}}\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{j_{D}}^{(d)}a^{(D)}_{i_{D}j_{D}},

\displaystyle a^{(D)}_{i_{D}j_{D}}\in\mathbb{H},\quad

\displaystyle i_{D}=1,\ldots,N_{D},

(23)

on account of the nature of the vector spaces
\{\mathcal{S}_{d}\}
.
Plugging expressions (
21
) – (
23
) into the definition (
18
) of
\bm{\mathcal{T}}
, one obtains the fundamental change-of-basis relation for quaternion tensors relating the entries of the tensor
\bm{\mathcal{T}}
to that of
\bm{\mathcal{\tilde{T}}}
:

\mathcal{T}_{i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{D}}=\sum_{j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{D}=1}^{N_{1},N_{2},\ldots,N_{D}}a_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{(1)}a_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{(2)}\ldots a_{i_{D-1}j_{D-1}}^{(D-1)}\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{j_{1}j_{2}\ldots j_{D}}a_{i_{D}j_{D}}^{(D)}.

(24)

On the one hand, remark the particular position of terms
a_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{(1)}
and
a_{i_{D}j_{D}}^{(D)}
, to the left and to the right of
\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{j_{1}j_{2}\ldots j_{D}}
, respectively, due to the left (resp. right) linear nature of the associated quaternion vector spaces.
On the other hand, the position of central terms
a_{i_{d}j_{d}}^{(d)}
,
1<d<D
, with respect to
\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{j_{1}j_{2}\ldots j_{D}}
has no importance since they are real-valued.

3.3
Transformations rules for quaternion tensors

The change-of-basis equation (
24
) is the first example of transformation rules for quaternion tensors. Such transformations rely on the
\mathbb{H}\mathbb{R}
-multilinear form representation of quaternion tensors (
Definition

13
) and the linearity properties of the underlying vector spaces.
Extending the change-of-basis rule to arbitrary linear transformations in each variable leads to the definition of the
n
-mode product, which needs to be carefully specified for quaternion tensors due to
\mathbb{H}\mathbb{R}
-multilinearity.
From now on, we fix a basis for the
D
vector spaces
\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{D}
and identify tensors with
D
-dimensional array of numbers.

Definition 15
(
n
-mode product for quaternion tensors).

Let
\bm{\mathcal{T}}\in\mathbb{H}^{N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{D}}
be a quaternion tensor of order
D
.
The
n
-mode-product operation corresponds to multiplying the
n
-mode fibers of
\bm{\mathcal{T}}
by a matrix
\mathbf{U}
of appropriate size and domain, depending on the considered mode.
Explicitly,

•

the 1-mode product is defined as the direct quaternion matrix product by
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{H}^{J\times N_{1}}
such that

\left(\bm{\mathcal{T}}\times^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm\rhd}_{1}\mathbf{U}\right)_{ji_{2}\ldots i_{D}}=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{N_{1}}u_{ji_{1}}\mathcal{T}_{i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{D}},

(25)

•

for
1<d<D
, the
d
-mode product is defined as the real matrix product by
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times N_{d}}
such that

\left(\bm{\mathcal{T}}\times_{d}\mathbf{U}\right)_{i_{1}\ldots j\ldots i_{D}}=\sum_{i_{d}=1}^{N_{d}}\mathcal{T}_{i_{1}\ldots i_{d}\ldots i_{D}}u_{ji_{d}},

(26)

•

the
D
-mode product is defined as the reverse quaternion matrix product by
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{H}^{J\times N_{D}}
such that

\left(\bm{\mathcal{T}}\times^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm\lhd}_{D}\mathbf{U}\right)_{i_{1}\ldots i_{D_{1}}j}=\sum_{i_{D}=1}^{N_{D}}\mathcal{T}_{i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{D}}u_{ji_{D}}.

(27)

Just like for quaternion matrix multiplication, symbols
{\rhd}
and
{\lhd}
indicate the type of quaternion matrix multiplication involved in mode-products when relevant.
In addition, one can express
n
-mode products in terms of unfoldings of quaternion tensors and quaternion matrix multiplication operations.
Following standard practice, we denote by
\mathbf{T}_{(d)}\in\mathbb{H}^{N_{d}\times\prod_{i\neq d}N_{i}}
the
d
-th mode unfolding of a quaternion tensor
\bm{\mathcal{T}}\in\mathbb{H}^{N_{1}\times\cdots N_{D}}
and use the indexing convention of
[
19
]
.
The
n
-mode product operations relate to matrix unfoldings as

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}=\bm{\mathcal{T}}\times^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm\rhd}_{1}\mathbf{U}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\mathbf{T}_{(1)}^{\prime}=\mathbf{U}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\mathbf{T}_{(1)},

(28)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}=\bm{\mathcal{T}}\times_{d}\mathbf{U}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\mathbf{T}_{(d)}^{\prime}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{T}_{(d)},

(29)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}=\bm{\mathcal{T}}\times^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm\lhd}_{D}\mathbf{U}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\mathbf{T}_{(D)}^{\prime}=\mathbf{U}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\mathbf{T}_{(D)},

(30)

i.e., the matrix unfoldings inherit the direct and reverse order of the
n
-mode product operation.

Proposition

16
and
Proposition

17
show that the
definition

15
of
n
-modes product permits to preserve the classical properties of successive
n
-mode products: (i) commutativity between distinct modes and (ii) matrix composition between identical modes.
These desirable properties are directly inherited from the definition a quaternion tensor as a
\mathbb{H}\mathbb{R}
-multilinear form.

Proposition 16
(Commutativity).

Let
\bm{\mathcal{T}}\in\mathbb{H}^{N_{1}\times N_{2}\times\cdots\times N_{D}}
be a quaternion tensor of order
D
.
Let
\mathbf{U}_{1}\in\mathbb{H}^{J_{1}\times N_{1}}
and
\mathbf{U}_{D}\in\mathbb{H}^{J_{D}\times N_{D}}
be arbitrary quaternion matrices, and let
\mathbf{U}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{J_{d}\times N_{d}}
and
\mathbf{U}_{d^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}^{J_{d^{\prime}}\times N_{d^{\prime}}}
be arbitrary real matrices with
d\neq d^{\prime},1<d,d^{\prime}<D
.
The following properties are satisfied:

\displaystyle\left(\bm{\mathcal{T}}\times^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm\rhd}_{1}\mathbf{U}_{1}\right)\times^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm\lhd}_{D}\mathbf{U}_{D}

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{\mathcal{T}}\times^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm\lhd}_{D}\mathbf{U}_{D}\right)\times^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm\rhd}_{1}\mathbf{U}_{1},

(31)

\displaystyle\left(\bm{\mathcal{T}}\times^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm\rhd}_{1}\mathbf{U}_{1}\right)\times_{d}\mathbf{U}_{d}

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{\mathcal{T}}\times_{d}\mathbf{U}_{d}\right)\times^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm\rhd}_{1}\mathbf{U}_{1},

(32)

\displaystyle\left(\bm{\mathcal{T}}\times_{d}\mathbf{U}_{d}\right)\times^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm\lhd}_{D}\mathbf{U}_{D}

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{\mathcal{T}}\times^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm\lhd}_{D}\mathbf{U}_{D}\right)\times_{d}\mathbf{U}_{d},

(33)

\displaystyle\left(\bm{\mathcal{T}}\times_{d}\mathbf{U}_{d}\right)\times_{d^{\prime}}\mathbf{U}_{d^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{\mathcal{T}}\times_{d^{\prime}}\mathbf{U}_{d^{\prime}}\right)\times_{d}\mathbf{U}_{d}.

(34)

Proposition 17
(Composition).

Let
\bm{\mathcal{T}}\in\mathbb{H}^{N_{1}\times N_{2}\times\cdots\times N_{D}}
be a quaternion tensor of order
D
.
Let
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{V}
be matrices with adequate dimensions and values.
The following properties are satisfied:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{T}}\times^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm\rhd}_{1}\mathbf{U}\times^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm\rhd}_{1}\mathbf{V}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathcal{T}}\times^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm\rhd}_{1}(\mathbf{V}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\mathbf{U}),

(35)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{T}}\times^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm\lhd}_{D}\mathbf{U}\times^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm\lhd}_{D}\mathbf{V}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathcal{T}}\times^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm\lhd}_{D}(\mathbf{V}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\mathbf{U}),

(36)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{T}}\times_{d}\mathbf{U}\times_{d}\mathbf{V}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathcal{T}}\times_{d}(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{U}),\quad 1<d<D.

(37)

Proofs of
Proposition

16
and
Proposition

17
are found by direct calculations.

Remark 18
(Change-of-basis).

The change of basis equation (
24
) can be rewritten as successive
n
-mode products. Let
\mathbf{A}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{H}^{N_{1}\times N_{1}},\mathbf{A}^{(2)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{2}\times N_{2}},\ldots,\mathbf{A}^{(D-1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{D-1}\times N_{D-1}},\mathbf{A}^{(D)}\in\mathbb{H}^{N_{D}\times N_{D}}
the change-of-basis matrices. Then the change-of-basis equation can be rewritten as

\bm{\mathcal{T}}=\bm{\mathcal{\tilde{T}}}\times^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm\rhd}_{1}\mathbf{A}^{(1)}\times_{2}\mathbf{A}^{(2)}\cdots\times_{D-1}\mathbf{A}^{(D-1)}\times^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm\lhd}_{D}\mathbf{A}^{(D)},

(38)

where
n
-mode products can be performed in any order according to
Proposition

16
.

3.4
Tucker format of quaternion tensors

Without loss of generality, in the remainder of the paper we focus on the case of third order tensors.
The next definition defines the Tucker format for quaternion tensors.

Definition 19
(Tucker format).

Let
\bm{\mathcal{T}}\in\mathbb{H}^{N_{1}\times N_{2}\times N_{3}}
be a third-order quaternion tensor. The tensor
\bm{\mathcal{T}}
is said to be expressed in Tucker format if there exists a core tensor
\bm{\mathcal{S}}\in\mathbb{H}^{F_{1}\times F_{2}\times F_{3}}
and matrices
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{N_{1}\times F_{1}},\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{2}\times F_{2}}
and
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{H}^{N_{3}\times F_{3}}
such that

\bm{\mathcal{T}}=\bm{\mathcal{S}}\times^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm\rhd}_{1}\mathbf{A}\times_{2}\mathbf{B}\times^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm\lhd}_{3}\mathbf{C}.

(39)

The Tucker format of
\bm{\mathcal{T}}
is written in short-hand notation as
\bm{\mathcal{T}}=\llbracket\bm{\mathcal{S}};\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{C}\rrbracket
.

Direct calculations show that the entries of a tensor
\bm{\mathcal{T}}=\llbracket\bm{\mathcal{S}};\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{C}\rrbracket
can be written in terms of the entries of the core tensor
\bm{\mathcal{S}}
and factor matrices
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{C}
as

\mathcal{T}_{i_{1}i_{2}i_{3}}=\sum_{f_{1}=1}^{F_{1}}\sum_{f_{2}=1}^{F_{2}}\sum_{f_{3}=1}^{F_{3}}A_{i_{1}f_{1}}B_{i_{2}f_{2}}S_{f_{1}f_{2}f_{3}}C_{i_{3}f_{3}},

(40)

for
i_{1}=1,\ldots,N_{1},i_{2}=1,\ldots,N_{2}
and
i_{3}=1,\ldots,N_{3}
. Note that due to quaternion non-commutativity, elements in (
40
) do not commute except for the entries of
\mathbf{B}
.
For tensors written in Tucker format it is also possible to derive expressions of their mode-unfoldings.
Following the unfolding convention of Kolda and Bader
[
19
]
, if
\bm{\mathcal{T}}=\llbracket\bm{\mathcal{S}};\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{C}\rrbracket
then its matrix unfoldings along the first and last mode read

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}_{(1)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\mathbf{S}_{(1)}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\left(\mathbf{C}\mathop{\boxtimes}\mathbf{B}\right)^{\top},

(41)

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}_{(3)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{C}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\mathbf{S}_{(3)}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\left(\mathbf{B}\mathop{\boxtimes}\mathbf{A}\right)^{\top}.

(42)

However, due to quaternion non-commutativity, the unfolding along the central mode can only be expressed in a simple way when the core tensor is real-valued, i.e.,

\mathbf{T}_{(2)}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{S}_{(2)}\left(\mathbf{C}\mathop{\boxtimes}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\mathbf{A}\right)^{\top},\quad\text{when }\bm{\mathcal{S}}\text{ is real-valued.}

(43)

4
Quaternion Canonical Polyadic Decomposition

4.1
Definition

If in (
39
) the tensor
\bm{\mathcal{S}}
is diagonal, it enables the derivation of the quaternion equivalent of a well-known tensor model, the Canonical Polyadic Decomposition (CPD). By absorbing the diagonal entries of
\bm{\mathcal{S}}
in the factor matrices
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{C}
, we define the Quaternion Canonical Polyadic Decomposition (Q-CPD) as follows.

Definition 20
(Quaternion Canonical Polyadic Decomposition (Q-CPD)).

The Q-CPD of a third-order quaternion tensor
\bm{\mathcal{T}}\in\mathbb{H}^{N_{1}\times N_{2}\times N_{3}}
is given element-wise by :

\mathcal{T}_{i_{1}i_{2}i_{3}}=\sum_{f=1}^{F}A_{i_{1}f}B_{i_{2}f}C_{i_{3}f},

(44)

for
i_{1}=1,\ldots,N_{1},i_{2}=1,\ldots,N_{2}
and
i_{3}=1,\ldots,N_{3}
. The smallest value of
F
for which equality (
44
) holds exactly is called the rank of
\bm{\mathcal{T}}
.
By expressing the elements of the decomposition in matrix format, the Q-CPD of
\bm{\mathcal{T}}
can be succinctly expressed as
\bm{\mathcal{T}}=\llbracket\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{C}\rrbracket
, where
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{N_{1}\times F},\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{2}\times F}
,
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{H}^{N_{3}\times F}.

Remark 21
.

Contrary to quaternion matrices, the rank of a quaternion tensor is unambiguous as defined and thus the left or right distinction does not apply.

Notation for the Q-CPD
\bm{\mathcal{T}}=\llbracket\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{C}\rrbracket
refers to the fact that the Q-CPD can be interpreted as a Tucker model in which the core tensor can always be chosen to be the identity tensor. In particular, this enables a direct derivation of the unfoldings of the model; see Eqs. (
45
)-(
47
) below.
Moreover, similarly to the Tucker model, elements in (
44
) do not commute except for the entries of
\mathbf{B}
. First, let us analyze the trivial ambiguities of the Q-CPD.

Let
\bm{\mathcal{T}}=\llbracket\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{C}\rrbracket
follow a CPD of rank
F
.
The first trivial ambiguity corresponds to an arbitrary joint permutation of the columns of the factor matrices, i.e.,
\bm{\mathcal{T}}=\llbracket\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{C}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathbf{A}\bm{\Pi},\mathbf{B}\bm{\Pi},\mathbf{C}\bm{\Pi}\rrbracket
with
\bm{\Pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{F\times F}
an arbitrary permutation matrix.
The second trivial ambiguity relates to scaling between factors: unlike the real and complex CPD, one has to distinguish between
real
and
quaternion
scaling ambiguities due to factor matrices taking values in different spaces.
Define three diagonal scaling matrices
\bm{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{A}},\bm{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{C}}\in\mathbb{H}^{F\times F}
and
\bm{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{F\times F}
(with non-zero diagonal entries).
Direct calculations show that
\bm{\mathcal{T}}=\llbracket\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{C}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathbf{A}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\bm{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{A}},\mathbf{B}\bm{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{B}},\mathbf{C}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\bm{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{C}}\rrbracket
holds for arbitrary factor matrices
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{C}
if and only if
(\bm{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{A}}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\bm{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{C}})\bm{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{B}}=\mathbf{I}_{F}
, where
\mathbf{I}_{F}
is the
F\times F
identity matrix.
By exploiting the polar form of scaling coefficients further interpretation is possible.

Proposition 22
(Interpretation of scaling ambiguities).

Consider three diagonal matrices
\bm{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{A}}=\diag(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{F})\in\mathbb{H}^{F\times F}
,
\bm{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{B}}=\diag(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{F})\in\mathbb{R}^{F\times F}
and
\bm{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{C}}=\diag(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{F})\in\mathbb{H}^{F\times F}
.
Write quaternion entries in polar form such that
\alpha_{f}=|\alpha_{f}|\exp(\bm{\mu}_{\alpha_{f}}\varphi_{\alpha_{f}})
and
\gamma_{f}=|\gamma_{f}|\exp(\bm{\mu}_{\gamma_{f}}\varphi_{\gamma_{f}})
where
|\cdot|
denotes the modulus and
(\bm{\mu},\varphi)
the axis and angle of the quaternion, respectively.
Then, the scaling condition
(\bm{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{A}}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\bm{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{C}})\bm{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{B}}=\mathbf{I}_{F}
can be equivalently interpreted as the combination of two ambiguities:

•

a modulus scaling ambiguity:
|\alpha_{f}|\,|\beta_{f}|\,|\gamma_{f}|=1
,
f=1,\ldots,F

•

a angular scaling ambiguity: for
f=1,\ldots,F
, if
\beta_{f}
is positive, it implies there is a choice of
\bm{\mu}_{\alpha_{f}},\varphi_{\alpha_{f}}
and
\bm{\mu}_{\gamma_{f}},\varphi_{\gamma_{f}}
, such that
\bm{\mu}_{\alpha_{f}}=\bm{\mu}_{\gamma_{f}}
and
\varphi_{\alpha_{f}}+\varphi_{\gamma_{f}}=0
.
In the case
\beta_{f}
is negative, it implies there is a choice of
\bm{\mu}_{\alpha_{f}},\varphi_{\alpha_{f}}
and
\bm{\mu}_{\gamma_{f}},\varphi_{\gamma_{f}}
, such that
\bm{\mu}_{\alpha_{f}}=\bm{\mu}_{\gamma_{f}}
and
\varphi_{\alpha_{f}}+\varphi_{\gamma_{f}}=\pi
.

i.e., moduli must multiply to 1 and arguments of quaternion factors compensate each other according to the sign of
\beta_{f}
.

Proof.

Starting from
(\bm{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{A}}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\bm{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{C}})\bm{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{B}}=\mathbf{I}_{F}
we get
\alpha_{f}\beta_{f}\gamma_{f}=1
for every
f=1,\ldots,F
.
Using polar forms, the latter reads
|\alpha_{f}|\exp(\bm{\mu}_{\alpha_{f}}\varphi_{\alpha_{f}})\beta_{f}|\gamma_{f}|\exp(\bm{\mu}_{\gamma_{f}}\varphi_{\gamma_{f}})=1
.
Taking the modulus of this expression one gets
|\alpha_{f}|\,|\beta_{f}|\,|\gamma_{f}|=1
.
For the argument, we have
\exp(\bm{\mu}_{\alpha_{f}}\varphi_{\alpha_{f}})\exp(\bm{\mu}_{\gamma_{f}}\varphi_{\gamma_{f}})=\frac{\beta_{f}}{|\beta_{f}|}
. By considering separately the cases
\beta_{f}>0
and
\beta_{f}<0
one obtains the desired property.

These ambiguities play a crucial role to define the uniqueness of the Q-CPD; see
section

4.3
for more details.
Finally, note that handling the Q-CPD trivial ambiguities does not pose any challenge as long as non-commutativity of quaternion multiplication is taken into account.

The matrix unfoldings of the quaternion CPD model follow directly from the unfoldings (
41
) – (
43
) of the Tucker model.
As already mentioned, the Q-CPD can be viewed as a special case of the Tucker model with a core identity tensor; therefore the unfoldings of
\bm{\mathcal{T}}=\llbracket\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{C}\rrbracket
read

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}_{(1)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\left(\mathbf{C}\mathop{\odot}\mathbf{B}\right)^{\top},

(45)

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}_{(2)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}\left(\mathbf{C}\mathop{\odot}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\mathbf{A}\right)^{\top},

(46)

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}_{(3)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{C}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\left(\mathbf{B}\mathop{\odot}\mathbf{A}\right)^{\top}.

(47)

Once again, these matrix unfoldings are similar to those of the real and complex cases (see, e.g.,
[
19
]
) yet matrix and Khatri-Rao products have direct or reverse orderings due to non-commutativity of the quaternion product.
Moreover, these unfoldings are essential to the practical computation of the factor matrices
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{C}
; see
Section

5
for further details.

To conclude this section, note that it is also possible to derive mathematical expressions for the matrix slices of the Q-CPD (
44
).
The frontal slices
\mathbf{T}_{::i_{3}}\in\mathbb{H}^{N_{1}\times N_{2}}
, lateral slices
\mathbf{T}_{:i_{2}:}\in\mathbb{H}^{N_{1}\times N_{3}}
and horizontal slices
\mathbf{T}_{i_{1}::}\in\mathbb{H}^{N_{2}\times N_{3}}
of
\bm{\mathcal{T}}=\llbracket\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{C}\rrbracket
can be written in terms of factor matrices as

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}_{i_{1}::}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}\left(\mathbf{D}^{(i_{1})}_{\mathbf{A}}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\mathbf{C}^{\sf T}\right),\quad\mathbf{D}^{(i_{1})}_{\mathbf{A}}:=\diag(\mathbf{A}_{i_{1}:})\in\mathbb{H}^{F\times F},\quad i_{1}=1,\ldots,N_{1},

(48)

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}_{:i_{2}:}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}^{(i_{2})}_{\mathbf{B}}\right)\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\mathbf{C}^{\sf T},\quad\mathbf{D}^{(i_{2})}_{\mathbf{B}}:=\diag(\mathbf{B}_{i_{2}:})\in\mathbb{R}^{F\times F},\quad i_{2}=1,\ldots,N_{2},

(49)

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}_{::i_{3}}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{A}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\mathbf{D}^{(i_{3})}_{\mathbf{C}}\right)\mathbf{B}^{\sf T},\quad\mathbf{D}^{(i_{3})}_{\mathbf{C}}:=\diag(\mathbf{C}_{i_{3}:})\in\mathbb{H}^{F\times F},\quad i_{3}=1,\ldots,N_{3}.

(50)

These expressions can be deduced from the definition (
44
) of the Q-CPD.
They will be useful in the next section to study equivalent models of the Q-CPD.

4.2

In this subsection, we propose different ways to rewrite the

Two main approaches are investigated.
The first one models complex adjoints of lateral slices of
\bm{\mathcal{T}}

The former will allow us to establish uniqueness properties of the Q-CPD in

The latter will be useful in

Complex adjoint representation

Consider a third-order tensor
\bm{\mathcal{T}}\in\mathbb{H}^{N_{1}\times N_{2}\times N_{3}}

\bm{\mathcal{T}}=\llbracket\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{C}\rrbracket

Recall that the lateral slices of

(51)

For the structured rank-(2,2,1) decomposition (

), the situation is more restrictive with the help of the following lemma.

Lemma 24
.

Let
\mathbf{p},\tilde{\mathbf{p}}\in\mathbb{H}^{M}
and
\mathbf{q},\tilde{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathbb{H}^{N}
.
Then
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{p})\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}^{\top}(\mathbf{q})=\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\tilde{\mathbf{p}})\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}^{\top}(\tilde{\mathbf{q}})
if and only if there exists a non-zero quaternion
r\in\mathbb{H}
such that
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}=\mathbf{p}r
and
\tilde{\mathbf{q}}=r^{-1}\mathbf{q}
.

Proof.

Using complex adjoint properties, one has
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{p})\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}^{\top}(\mathbf{q})=\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{p})\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{q}^{\top})=\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{p}\mathbf{q}^{\top})
. Thus
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{p})\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}^{\top}(\mathbf{q})=\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\tilde{\mathbf{p}})\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}^{\top}(\tilde{\mathbf{q}})
if and only if
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{p}\mathbf{q}^{\top})=\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\tilde{\mathbf{p}}\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{\top})
. By the properties of the complex adjoint, this is equivalent to saying that
\mathbf{p}\mathbf{q}^{\top}=\tilde{\mathbf{p}}\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{\top}
.
The equality is satisfied if and only there exists a non-zero
r\in\mathbb{H}
such that
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}=\mathbf{p}r
and
\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{\top}=r^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{\top}
. Since
r
is a scalar, this equivalently means that
\tilde{\mathbf{q}}=r^{-1}\mathbf{q}
and it concludes the proof.

Applying
Lemma

24
to the decomposition (
52
), one obtains that
\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{a}_{f})\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}^{\top}(\mathbf{c}_{f})=\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{a}_{f})\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{A}}(\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}(\mathbf{c}_{f})\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{C}})^{\top}
if and only if there exists a non-zero scalar
r_{f}\in\mathbb{H}
such that
\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{A}}=\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(r_{f})
and
\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}=\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(r_{f}^{-1})
, or equivalently,
\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{C}}=\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}^{\top}(r_{f}^{-1})=\chi_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}(r_{f}^{-1})
.
In other terms, the ambiguities of the rank-(2,2,1) factors are directly inherited from those of the quaternion factors of the Q-CPD.

4.3
Uniqueness of the Q-CPD

As explained in

Definition 26
(Uniqueness of the Q-CPD).

Let

F

\bm{\Pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{F\times F}

\bm{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{A}},\bm{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{C}}

\bm{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{B}}

(\bm{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{A}}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\bm{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{C}})\bm{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{B}}=\mathbf{I}_{F}

Proposition 27

Let

Building on this result,

Theorem 28

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{N_{1}\times F},\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{2}\times F}

5
Algorithms for computing the quaternion CPD

Given an arbitrary tensor
\bm{\mathcal{T}}\in\mathbb{H}^{N_{1}\times N_{2}\times N_{3}}
, the Q-CPD approximation problem of rank-
F
can be formulated as the following optimization problem

(\hat{\mathbf{A}},\hat{\mathbf{B}},\hat{\mathbf{C}})=\mathop{\operator@font arg\,min}_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{C}}\left\|\bm{\mathcal{T}}-\llbracket\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{C}\rrbracket\right\|_{F}^{2}.

(63)

To solve (
63
), we introduce two algorithms that operate in the quaternion domain and the complex domain, respectively.
Both algorithms rely on the well known Alternating Least Squares procedure (ALS).
As we will show, they yield equivalent update rules; however subtle differences arise in their practical implementation, as discussed in
Section

6
.

5.1
Computing the Q-CPD in
\mathbb{H}
: the Q-ALS algorithm

The Q-ALS algorithm extends the ALS algorithm to the Q-CPD.
It resorts to the matrix unfoldings of the Q-CPD given in equations (
45
)-(
47
).
Thereby, at each iteration the factor matrices are updated as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\mathop{\operator@font arg\,min}_{\mathbf{A}}\;\;\lVert\mathbf{T}_{(1)}-\mathbf{A}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{C}\mathop{\odot}\mathbf{B})^{\top}\rVert^{2}_{F},

(64)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\mathop{\operator@font arg\,min}_{\mathbf{B}}\;\;\lVert\mathbf{T}_{(2)}-\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{C}\mathop{\odot}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\mathbf{A})^{\top}\rVert^{2}_{F}\ \ \text{s.t.}\ \ \mathbf{B}\text{ has real-valued entries},

(65)

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\mathop{\operator@font arg\,min}_{\mathbf{C}}\;\;\lVert\mathbf{T}_{(3)}-\mathbf{C}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}(\mathbf{B}\mathop{\odot}\mathbf{A})^{\top}\rVert^{2}_{F},

(66)

where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
denotes the Frobenius norm of a matrix.
We thus have to solve three different kinds of quaternion matrix least squares subproblems:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\operator@font arg\,min}_{\mathbf{X}}\lVert\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{X}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\mathbf{N}\rVert^{2}_{F},

(67)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{X}}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\operator@font arg\,min}_{\mathbf{X}}\lVert\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{N}\rVert^{2}_{F}\ \ \text{s.t.}\ \ \mathbf{X}\text{ has real-valued entries},

(68)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\operator@font arg\,min}_{\mathbf{X}}\lVert\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{X}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\mathbf{N}\rVert^{2}_{F}.

(69)

The solution to (
67
) can be found in
[
36
]
and is given by
\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}=\mathbf{M}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\mathbf{N}^{\sf H}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\left(\mathbf{N}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\mathbf{N}^{\sf H}\right)^{-1}\>.

(Note that in this section, quaternion matrix inverses are always defined with respect to the direct quaternion matrix product and can be computed through inversion of the corresponding complex adjoint, see
[
38
, Theorem 4.2]
.)
The second subproblem is a constrained optimization problem since
\mathbf{X}
is constrained to belong to the set of real-valued matrices. The optimal solution can be found in
[
11
, Appendix B]
and is given by

\hat{\mathbf{X}}=\mathop{\operator@font Re}\left\{\mathbf{M}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\mathbf{N}^{\sf H}\right\}\mathop{\operator@font Re}\left\{\left(\mathbf{N}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\mathbf{N}^{\sf H}\right)\right\}^{-1}\>.

Eventually, in order to solve (
69
), we resort to the properties of direct and reverse quaternion matrix products:
\lVert\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{X}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\mathbf{N}\rVert^{2}_{F}=\lVert\mathbf{M}^{\sf T}-\mathbf{N}^{\sf T}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\mathbf{X}^{\sf T}\rVert^{2}_{F}
so that the solution is given by:
\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}=\mathbf{M}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\mathbf{N}^{\sf H}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\left[\left(\overline{\mathbf{N}}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\mathbf{N}^{\sf T}\right)^{-1}\right]^{\sf T}\>.

As a consequence, the updates computed during one iteration of the Q-ALS algorithm are given by:

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\mathbf{T}_{(1)}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\overline{\mathbf{C}\mathop{\odot}\mathbf{B}})\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}\left((\mathbf{C}\mathop{\odot}\mathbf{B})^{\sf T}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\overline{\mathbf{C}\mathop{\odot}\mathbf{B}})\right)^{-1}

(70)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\mathop{\operator@font Re}\left\{\mathbf{T}_{(2)}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\overline{\mathbf{C}\mathop{\odot}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\mathbf{A}})\right\}\mathop{\operator@font Re}\left\{(\mathbf{C}\mathop{\odot}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\mathbf{A})^{\sf T}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\overline{\mathbf{C}\mathop{\odot}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\mathbf{A}})\right\}^{-1}

(71)

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\mathbf{T}_{(3)}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}(\overline{\mathbf{B}\mathop{\odot}\mathbf{A}})\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\lhd}\left(\left((\mathbf{B}\mathop{\odot}\mathbf{A})^{\sf H}\cdot_{\scriptscriptstyle\rhd}(\mathbf{B}\mathop{\odot}\mathbf{A})\right)^{-1}\right)^{\sf T}.

(72)

The Q-ALS updates resemble those of the usual ALS updates for computing the real and complex CPD. Therefore we anticipate that existing convergence results for ALS
[
32
]
can be adapted to the quaternion setting, exploiting recent advances in quaternion optimization
[
12
,
35
,
36
]
.
However, a precise characterization of such properties requires a dedicated study, which is left for future work.

Numerical simulations

Figure 1:
Median value of the cost function and median NMSE on the factor matrices with respect to the SNR.

The purpose of the following numerical simulations is twofold. We thus want to :
i)
verify that the Q-CPD can be computed effectively ;
ii)
assess the performances of the proposed algorithms in terms of convergence speed and accuracy in a simple but classical scenario.
To this purpose, we consider here the Q-CPD decomposition of 500 third order tensors built according to the Q-CPD model (

and
N_{1}=N_{2}=N_{3}=10
.
The entries of the three factor matrices were drawn randomly according to a normal distribution and are different for each tensor.
Before computing the Q-CPD, an additive white Gaussian noise was applied to each tensor.
The noise powers were set in order to obtain the desired Signal to Noise Ratio (SNR).
More complex scenarios involving correlated factors, higher ranks, higher order tensors and further comparison between the algorithms deserve a specific study and are not in the scope of this paper.
All simulations were performed using
Matlab
environment. The Q-ALS algorithm has been programmed using the QTFM toolbox
4
4

4

https://nl.mathworks.com/matlabcentral/fileexchange/76158-quaternion-toolbox-for-matlab

Both Q-ALS and C-ALS algorithms were initialized with the same (random) values and were stopped when the relative deviation between two consecutive values of the cost function fell under
10^{-8}
with a maximum of 500 iterations.
At the end of each run, after removing the scaling and permutation ambiguities, the relative root mean-squared errors between the actual and estimated factor matrices were computed for both algorithms.
Eventually, the median values of these errors and of the relative cost function were calculated over the 500 tensors realizations.
This scenario was repeated for different SNR. Results are plotted on Figure

.

Three main conclusions can be drawn.
First, as expected, the curves corresponding to the two algorithms overlap perfectly.
Second, the errors decrease linearly with the noise power. This behavior is similar to the standard CPD algorithms used for real or complex tensors. Third, the error values are small enough to consider that Q-CPD decomposition can actually be computed, which validates the possibility of using this new tensor model in practical applications.
In order to illustrate the practical interest of C-ALS, we also computed for each algorithm the average running time, running time per iteration and number of iterations.
The average running times for C-ALS and Q-ALS were about

and

These observations hold for all the considered SNR.
Eventually the same experiments were run for

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we rigorously extended the notion of tensors to quaternion algebra. The key insight is that a quaternion tensor can be understood as a peculiar type of multilinear form, an
\mathbb{H}\mathbb{R}
-multilinear form, which preserves the fundamental multilinearity properties expected of such mathematical objects.
Building on this foundation, we extended the Tucker and Canonical Polyadic Decompositions (CPD) to quaternion tensors. In particular, we showed the equivalence of the quaternion CPD (Q-CPD) to a specific complex-valued rank-
(2,2,1)
decomposition and established sufficient conditions for its uniqueness. These conditions rely on a careful handling of left and right linear independence in the quaternion domain, through the notion of left and right ranks, and left and right Kruskal ranks which are defined herein.
Additionally, we derived two ALS-based algorithms for computing the Q-CPD, one operating in the quaternion domain and the other in the complex domain, demonstrating that the new decomposition can be computed efficiently.
Future work will focus on further evaluation of the Q-CPD algorithms and on exploring their potential applications in data analysis and related fields.
