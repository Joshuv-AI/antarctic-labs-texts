Patterns of intimate partner violence after abortion seeking in Nepal (2019–2024): a prospective cohort study
Authors: Rachel Murro, Nadia G. Diamond-Smith, Mahesh C. Puri, Anupama Ale Magar, Diana Greene Foster
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundWomen in Nepal commonly experience intimate partner violence. This study aims to understand how abortion access, a key indicator of reproductive autonomy, may be linked to partner violence in this setting.MethodsBetween April 2019 and December 2020, pregnant women seeking an abortion at 22 facilities across all provinces in Nepal were recruited into a prospective cohort study. The primary exposure was pregnancy outcome (gave birth vs. did not give birth). The outcome, intimate partner violence, was measured at baseline and then every 12 months using indicators of emotional, physical, economic, and sexual violence. Sociodemographic, health, and care-seeking characteristics were collected at baseline. Mixed effects multivariable logistic regression models, adjusted via propensity score weighting, were used to assess the association between birth and subsequent intimate partner violence via risk differences (RDs).FindingsA total of 1742 pregnant women were followed for a median duration of 62 months. At baseline, 15% reported experiencing emotional violence in the past year, 12% physical, seven percent economic, and nine percent sexual violence. Weighted models showed no significant change in violence risk from baseline among individuals who gave birth (five-year RD = 0.02; 95% CI −0.09 to 0.14). In contrast, overall violence declined among those who did not give birth (one-year RD = −0.05; 95% CI −0.08 to −0.03, five-year RD = −0.08; 95% CI −0.16 to −0.01). This group also experienced declines in physical and economic violence specifically. In comparing the two groups, women who did not give birth experienced less violence overall than those who gave birth, particularly in years two (RD = 0.05; 95% CI 0.00 to 0.10), three (RD = 0.07; 95% CI 0.01 to 0.13), four (RD = 0.07; 95% CI 0.01 to 0.13), and five (RD = 0.10; 95% CI 0.03 to 0.16) after abortion seeking.InterpretationCompared to those who were able to end their pregnancy, women who gave birth were at higher risk of partner violence, even several years after abortion seeking. Abortion access may offer protection against intimate partner violence compared to carrying an unwanted pregnancy to term.FundingThis study was supported by the National Institutes of Health and the David and Lucile Packard Foundation.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

On October 21, 2025, we searched PubMed for English-language studies using the terms (“abortion”) AND (“partner violence” OR “domestic violence”), yielding 499 results. Most relevant studies fell into several categories: prevalence of intimate partner violence among abortion seekers, integration of partner violence screening or services into abortion care, coerced abortion in relation to partner violence, and associational studies. The largest category was associational studies, primarily cross-sectional analyses linking partner violence to unintended pregnancy, elective abortion, and miscarriage, along with several studies examining possible causal pathways. One study by Thakuri et al. examined this association in Nepal. Among the remaining articles, only Roberts et al. in the United States compared abortion seekers who received care with those denied an abortion, finding that those denied experienced disproportionately higher violence. Additional studies used econometric methods to show that abortion restrictions in the United States and Mexico increase risk of partner violence. While it is well established across contexts that abortion seekers disproportionately experience intimate partner violence, the extent to which abortion denial exacerbates this risk remains unstudied in this region.

Added value of this study

This study is the first in the Southeast Asia region seeking to understand the effect of receiving or being denied an abortion on intimate partner violence. Using a prospective longitudinal design with an appropriate comparator group for drawing causal claims, we present estimates quantifying this effect. Our extended follow-up time of five years allows us to isolate effects beyond the time frame of most cohort studies. Our findings demonstrate that access to abortion care protects women in Nepal from facing subsequent intimate partner violence, even five years after abortion seeking.

Implications of all the available evidence

An estimated 27% of women worldwide have experienced violence from a partner, likely an underestimate. Because intimate partner violence is closely linked with unintended pregnancy, individuals seeking abortions are already at elevated risk. Findings from The Nepal Turnaway Study indicate that obtaining a wanted abortion is associated with reductions in violence, including physical and economic violence. For some forms of violence, particularly physical violence, these reductions persist even five years later. These results align with research from the U.S. and highlight how reproductive autonomy can benefit populations already at heightened risk of violence. Improving access to safe, patient-centered, high-quality abortion care not only safeguards individuals’ reproductive health and rights, but may also protect against intimate partner violence.

Introduction

Across Southeast Asia, 33% of women report experiencing violence from an intimate partner within their lifetime.1 While violence can be perpetrated by many actors, romantic partners are most likely to commit physical violence against women. In 2022, The Lancet Regional Health—Southeast Asia published an editorial calling for action to reduce intimate partner violence (IPV) in the region, specifically highlighting a 44% increase in sexual assault cases in Nepal between 2017 and 2020.2 According to nationally-representative data from 2022, 17% of women in Nepal report having experienced physical, sexual, or emotional violence from an intimate partner in the past year, and 29% have experienced controlling behaviors.3

Reproductive autonomy, including the ability to obtain a wanted abortion, may be directly linked to partner violence through several mechanisms. Heise's 1998 socioecological framework describes how factors across multiple levels—individual, microsystem, exosystem, and macrosystem—contribute to violence against women.4 Under this framework, IPV is predicted by microsystem factors such as male dominance, household control, alcohol use, and marital conflict, as well as exosystem factors like socioeconomic status and social isolation. The mechanisms through which reproductive autonomy might influence these proximate determinants have not been formally explored.

In Southeast Asia, evidence linking IPV to reproductive autonomy is varied. Studies report inconsistent associations between IPV and sexual autonomy (e.g., condom negotiation and ability to refuse sex),5 joint contraceptive decision-making,5,6 and unmet need for contraception.7 However, IPV risk has been positively associated with certain indicators that may act as proxies for constrained reproductive freedom within this context, including both sterilization8 and infertility9 in Nepal.

Abortion was legalized in Nepal in 2002 to address the high prevalence of maternal mortality and morbidity associated with unsafe abortion practices.10 The current law permits abortion up to 12 weeks upon request, up to 28 weeks in the case of rape or incest, and at any gestation if the woman's physical or mental health is at risk or the fetus is incompatible with life.10,11 Efforts to reduce barriers to care following legalization included nation-wide certification of public, private, and non-profit facilities and policies to provide universal free abortion care.12 Still, assessments over the last decade find that abortion access is limited for much of the population. As of 2018, fewer than half of facilities in Nepal that provide delivery care—and therefore are eligible to offer abortion services because they meet provider training and equipment requirements—had performed a procedural abortion in the past three months. In that same period, just over one third had provided medication abortion.13 A recent systematic review found that a complex system of economic, geographic, legal, sociocultural, and health systems obstacles prevent many women from obtaining abortion care.14

People who have abortions in Nepal, as in many other countries, are more likely to experience intimate partner violence than their counterparts.15 However, it is unclear whether this risk may be exacerbated when women are unable to obtain a wanted abortion. In the US, women who received abortions experienced a reduction in physical IPV, while those who were denied did not.16 This protective effect has been attributed to the ability to leave an abusive partner following an abortion. In a country like Nepal, however, where most women who seek abortions are married, and married women often lack independent financial power, most cannot leave abusive relationships.17,18 Still, one cohort study in Nepal found that women who became pregnant or gave birth faced an increased risk of subsequent IPV compared to those who did not.19 In nationally-representative demographic and health survey data, having more children in a household is associated with greater IPV risk.20 Increasing parity may be reflective of an inability to prevent unwanted pregnancy or childbirth, suggesting that abortion access could protect women from violence. However, a longitudinal study design with a target population of abortion seekers is necessary to investigate this research question. This paper will examine how inability to access abortion, a key marker of constrained reproductive autonomy, shapes IPV in this setting.

Methods

The Nepal Turnaway Study is a prospective cohort study of women who sought abortions at 22 randomly selected public, private, and non-profit facilities across the seven provinces of Nepal. Eligible participants were at least 15 years old, lived in Nepal, were pregnant and seeking an abortion. Participants were recruited between April 2019 and December 2020 and completed baseline surveys prior to receiving or being denied abortion care. After the first month of recruitment, eligibility was restricted to those with self-reported last menstrual period (LMP) of ten weeks prior or unknown date of LMP to increase the number of participants unable to obtain an abortion. Follow-up interviews were conducted six weeks after baseline, and then every six months for five years. Longitudinal data collection concluded in December 2025. A detailed description of the data source can be found in the Nepal Turnaway Study Cohort Profile, available online as a preprint.21

The primary exposure, pregnancy outcome, was ascertained during a follow-up interview. This typically occurred at the first follow-up survey (six weeks after baseline) but was less commonly ascertained at a subsequent interview when the respondent was unavailable at six weeks or was still pregnant but seeking an abortion. Participants were asked to self-report whether they had obtained an abortion and whether they were still pregnant. The range of possible experiences included: (1) obtaining an abortion at the recruitment clinic; (2) being denied care at the recruitment clinic but obtaining an abortion elsewhere or at a later date; (3) being denied an abortion and subsequently miscarrying; or (4) being denied an abortion and subsequently giving birth. Those who reported obtaining an abortion at any point and that they were no longer pregnant were categorized into the “No Birth” group. Participants denied an abortion who subsequently gave birth were classified in the “Gave Birth” group. The “No Birth” group also included a small number of individuals who reported either a miscarriage or that they were no longer pregnant without specifying how the pregnancy ended.

Study outcomes, indicators of intimate partner violence, were measured at recruitment (baseline) and then annually for five years. Participants were asked whether they had experienced different types of violence from a partner within the last year across 17 survey statements covering emotional (i.e., threatened to hurt you or harm you or someone close to you), sexual (i.e., forced you to perform sexual acts you did not want to), physical (i.e., punched you with his fist or with something that could hurt you) and economic violence (i.e., taken your earnings/property against your will). A full list of the 17 items can be found in Supplementary Data. Emotional, physical, and sexual violence statements were adapted from the DHS Domestic Violence Module22 and economic violence statements were taken from the Nepal 2011 Men and Gender Survey.23 Less than 0.5% of respondents (maximum 72 respondents at any one round) answered “Don't Know” or refused to answer questions in the violence module; these cases were recoded to “No”. Only respondents who were currently married at each survey (95–97% across all surveys) received the intimate partner violence questions; all unmarried individuals were recoded to “No”. Partner violence was assessed as a binary variable in all analyses, indicating whether an individual responded “Yes” to at least one statement. We examined measures of the four individual types of violence, as well as an overall indicator of any violence. Due to technical issues in the data collection software, the sexual violence module (three statements) was not asked at the 12-month survey and was only asked to approximately one third of participants at the 24-month survey. For this reason, the overall indicator of “any violence” used in analyses excludes sexual violence.

Sociodemographic and health characteristics, along with pregnancy-related and care seeking factors were collected at baseline via patient self-report.

Statistical analysis

Sociodemographic characteristics and intimate partner violence indicators were summarized for the analytical sample at baseline using percentages for categorical variables. Differences in baseline characteristic distributions between pregnancy outcome groups were assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs) as described below.

We used propensity score weighting to adjust for baseline confounding in analyses examining the association between pregnancy outcome, time and violence. To generate weights, propensity scores were estimated via a multivariable logistic regression model predicting exposure (Gave Birth vs. No Birth) based on the following baseline variables: sociodemographic characteristics (age, marital status, education, employment, parity, income), pregnancy characteristics (gestational age, abortion seeking due to fetal anomaly or sex selection, pregnancy intendedness), clinic characteristics (distance from participant home, public/private), medical history (anxiety/depression, chronic pain, overall health), and measures of empowerment, intimate partner violence and husband drinking behaviors. These predictors of pregnancy outcome were determined a priori based on expert knowledge from the study team. Overlap weights were derived from these propensity scores. Overlap weights are similar to commonly used “inverse probability of treatment” weights, which create a pseudo-population in which baseline characteristics are balanced across exposure groups at baseline, allowing for estimation of causal effects.24 We examined differences in baseline violence and sociodemographic characteristics by pregnancy outcome, both before and after applying balancing weights. Standardized mean differences were used to assess covariate balance, with a cutoff of 0.1 used to indicate sufficient balance after applying balancing weights.25 A detailed description of weight development and diagnostics can be found in the Cohort Profile.21 These weights were then applied in the outcome models, as described below.

To estimate the association between pregnancy outcome and subsequent intimate partner violence, we used Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) in a series of mixed effects logistic regression models containing time and pregnancy outcome as exposures, and partner violence as the outcome. Mixed effects models accommodate repeated measures; random intercepts were utilized for each individual across all models. Clustering by recruitment facility was accounted for in all models by using robust standard errors. Each model was performed twice, first unadjusted, and then adjusted using the propensity score-derived weights described above to balance characteristics at baseline. Weights were employed as probability weights at the person-level. Models differed only in the outcome of interest—any intimate partner violence, any emotional violence, any physical violence, any economic violence, and any sexual violence. Time was measured as a categorical variable where survey responses were either classified as happening at baseline or by the year after baseline. An interaction between the time term and pregnancy outcome was employed in each model to account for possible differences in the effect of time on partner violence between those who gave birth vs. did not give birth.

Descriptive and statistical analyses were conducted using Stata SE version 17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX). For all longitudinal models, marginal estimates were calculated using potential outcomes estimation to strengthen causal inference via the post-estimation margins command in Stata. We estimated (1) predicted probabilities of violence at each time point, for both the Gave Birth and No Birth groups; (2) within-group contrasts comparing each time point with baseline; and (3) between-group contrasts comparing the Gave Birth and No Birth groups at each time point.

We conducted sensitivity analyses to check for model misspecification and alternative exposure definitions, including the exclusion of presumed miscarriages and separating individuals who received abortion at recruitment from those who obtained abortion later (both belong to the No Birth group in main analyses). We also evaluated potential bias induced by the study design and missing data, including the coding of participants missing partner violence or pregnancy outcome data, loss to follow-up, and administrative censoring. For all sensitivity checks, we accounted for design effects (clustering) to isolate specific effects of analysis decisions.

Ethics approval

Ethical review and approval was provided by The University of California, San Francisco Human Research Protection Program (18-25863, initial approval on April 15, 2019) and the Ethics Committee of the Nepal Health Research Council (704/2018, initial approval on December 26, 2018). Informed written consent was obtained from all participants prior to study participation. A thumbprint was obtained for women unable to sign. In the case of minors under 18 years of age, participants provided assent for participation and interviewers obtained consent from one biological parent.

The funders had no role in study design, data collection and analysis, decision to publish, or preparation of the manuscript.

Results

Baseline findings and sample description

Of a total of 8849 individuals screened, 1916 were eligible to participate and 1832 consented and completed a baseline survey (additional data in the Cohort Profile). Of these, 1742 women were retained in the cohort long enough to report their pregnancy outcome and comprise the sample for this analysis. Most of these individuals (1467) received an abortion or reported being no longer pregnant, while 275 gave birth.

Just over one-fifth of participants (n = 354) reported experiencing violence from a partner in the year prior to abortion seeking, with 15% (n = 265), 12% (n = 213), seven percent (n = 124), and nine percent (n = 151) reporting emotional, physical, economic, and sexual violence, respectively. The two study groups differed in violence reported at baseline: 24% of those who gave birth, compared with 20% of those who did not, reported violence at the time of abortion seeking (SMD = 0.14) (Table 1). Baseline differences were also observed for economic (12% vs. 6%; SMD = 0.22), emotional (19% vs. 15%; SMD = 0.13), and sexual violence (11% vs. 8%; SMD = 0.10), but were smaller for physical violence (14% vs. 12%; SMD = 0.04). After applying balancing weights, violence at baseline was well balanced between groups (all SMDs <0.001).

Violence risk over time, by pregnancy outcome

The median duration of follow-up was 62 months (interquartile range = 51–64). Table 2 shows results from weighted mixed effects models, specifically changes in IPV risk over time with each follow-up point compared to baseline. Among those who gave birth, no changes in violence risk reached levels of statistical significance. In contrast, those who did not give birth demonstrated lower risk of violence at years one (RD = −0.05 [−0.08 to −0.03]) and five (RD = −0.08 [−0.16 to −0.01]). They also consistently reported lower risk of physical violence at all time points, compared to baseline. The decreased risk in physical violence for this group ranged from three to six percentage points in the five years after abortion seeking, with the greatest observed reductions occurring during years one (RD = −0.05 [−0.07 to −0.03]), two (RD = −0.06 [−0.09 to −0.03]), four (RD = −0.06 [−0.10 to −0.03]), and five (RD = −0.06 [−0.10 to −0.02]). We also observed a statistically significant decline from baseline for economic violence in the first (RD = −0.05 [−0.07 to −0.02]), fourth (RD = −0.05 [−0.08 to −0.01]), and fifth (RD = −0.04 [−0.08 to 0.00]) years after baseline. Bias assessment and sensitivity analyses

Findings were robust to alternative model specifications, including different functional forms for time, alternative methods of adjustment to accommodate facility-level clustering, and inclusion of calendar time in models. Results were also unchanged in sensitivity analyses varying treatment of the small number of participants with uncertain pregnancy outcomes and missing IPV data (particularly unmarried women). In exploratory analyses separating the No Birth group into those who received an abortion at the time of recruitment and those who were initially denied but later received an abortion, the two groups followed similar trajectories. See Supplementary Data for further details.

Five percent of participants (90/1832) were excluded from analyses due to unknown pregnancy outcome, 11% (191/1742) were lost to follow-up before completing their 60-month survey, and 27% (478/1742) were administratively censored prior to the final 60-month survey. Neither loss to follow-up nor administrative censoring was associated with baseline intimate partner violence, although administrative censoring was more common among those who gave birth due to inherent aspects of the study design. Maximum likelihood models accounting for a broad range of relevant baseline indicators were used to accommodate differential follow-up times; findings can be considered reasonably unbiased to this source of selection bias under the assumption of “missing at random” (see Supplementary Data for details).26

Discussion

In this study, abortion seekers in Nepal who ultimately gave birth reported higher prevalence of all types of intimate partner violence in the five years following, compared to those who received an abortion and did not give birth. A protective effect of abortion was observed, particularly for physical violence, whereby women who did not give birth observed decreases in violence, while those who gave birth did not.

Women who were denied their abortions and gave birth were already more disadvantaged prior to abortion seeking, reporting higher prevalence of emotional, economic, and to a lesser extent physical and sexual violence in the past year compared to their counterparts who did not give birth. This aligns with findings from a previously published baseline analysis of this cohort that found that women who were generally more disadvantaged tended to be denied abortion care.17 It's important to consider that when abortion access is constrained, it disproportionately harms women with less social and economic power, including those who are in abusive relationships.

After abortion seeking, we found that the prevalence of IPV was greater among those who gave birth, with the greatest differences observed in years three, four and five. A prior longitudinal study of newly married couples in Nepal found an increased risk of subsequent emotional, physical, economic, and sexual violence for those who became pregnant, and an even greater (in magnitude) risk for those who gave birth.19 The findings from this study provide further evidence to support the theory that increasing number of births is associated with all types of violence risk in this setting.20 It also provides additional information, suggesting that it may be that an increase in unwanted fertility that contributes to IPV risk.

We did not directly examine mechanisms linking abortion denial to IPV in this study. Still, it is notable that nearly all participants were married at baseline and remained married throughout follow-up, and neither baseline marital status nor changes in marital status were associated with giving birth. While it is possible that some women were able to separate physically from their husbands without a divorce, the association in this context likely cannot be attributed to women's ability to “untether” from abusive partners, as has been suggested in western countries.16 The increasing magnitude of differences over time also distinguishes these results from those in the US. Our findings suggest that a more complex, long-term process over the course of years following abortion seeking shapes violence risk.

One possibility is that achieving one's reproductive goals through accessing a wanted abortion may allow women to pursue education and employment opportunities, which in turn protect against intimate partner violence, even years later. Diamond-Smith et al., in their analysis of this same cohort, found that those who obtained an abortion were subsequently more likely to be in school.27 Thus, limiting or spacing childbearing through abortion may foster greater freedoms in this setting. Across contexts, women forced to raise a child they didn't plan for often face obstacles continuing work outside the house,28 and the link between education and reduction in IPV has been well established.18

Abortion access may also be linked to intimate partner violence through social connection and mental health. Social isolation, which is closely tied to stress, anxiety, and depression, is a strong predictor of intimate partner violence risk.4,18 As with education and employment, giving birth may prevent women from maintaining social connections outside of the home due to increased parenting responsibilities. This may be more pronounced for those who did not want to have another child; women whose pregnancies are unwanted or mistimed are more likely to suffer from new or exacerbated mental health conditions.29 Notably, however, Diamond-Smith found no overall difference in connections to family and friends based on pregnancy outcome in this cohort, suggesting that pathways distinct from social support may be responsible for linking abortion access, mental health, and intimate partner violence.27

Another explanation for our findings may be that the added household stress of having an additional child, particularly one that was not desired, could lead to marital conflicts that escalate to violence. In resource-poor areas, access to contraception improves women's earnings and overall household economic status.30 Abortion access may function similarly. In Nepal, one of the strongest predictors of intimate partner violence is financial stress, which is also closely tied to other key violence risk factors, such as male drinking habits and marital conflicts.31 In previously published analyses of the Nepal Turnaway Study, the most common reasons for seeking abortion were having enough children already, being unable to afford another child, or already having a young child.17 These findings together suggest that abortion access is a key component of reproductive autonomy, with potential to improve both household economic status and women's wellbeing.

The longitudinal design of the Nepal Turnaway Study allows examination of time trends in intimate partner violence following abortion seeking. Prior research has found that physical, emotional, and sexual violence may rise during pregnancy and the postpartum period, though bearing a son may also be protective in contexts with strong son preference.31 During the first year after abortion seeking, we observed no increases in IPV, nor any statistical differences in IPV by birth. However, because women reported violence over the past year rather than specifically during pregnancy, experiences of pregnancy-related violence may have been obscured by large reporting windows. Notably, differences between groups emerged primarily in years two through five. During this time, IPV declined among those who did not give birth but increased slightly (not statistically) or stayed the same among those who did. This delayed effect may reflect the financial strain of raising a child in a resource-limited setting, for example the burden of private school fees for primary education. On the other hand, if abortion access allows women to pursue or continue her own schooling or employment, the resulting improvement in her status and individual income may also be delayed. Other stressors exacerbated by unwanted childbearing—marital conflict, husband's alcohol use, and mental health challenges—may accumulate over time, resulting in greater violence risk in later years. These findings underscore the value of extended follow-up, as important consequences of restricted reproductive autonomy may emerge beyond the observation period of most cohort studies.

This study has several limitations, including missing data. We assumed that participants who declined to report intimate partner violence, as well as those not asked the violence modules because they were unmarried, did not experience violence. This group comprised less than five percent of participants, and sensitivity analyses among unmarried women suggested this assumption did not meaningfully alter results. However, our findings may not generalize to settings where a larger proportion of pregnant women are unmarried or where greater stigma around intimate partner violence leads to higher non-response rates.

Just under five percent of participants recruited were lost after baseline and excluded from analyses due to unknown pregnancy outcomes. While a small proportion of the overall sample, these participants differed from those who were included and their exclusion may induce some level of selection bias. Additionally, eleven percent of participants were lost to follow-up after their pregnancy outcome was ascertained and 27% were administratively censored around 48 or 54 months of follow-up. Loss to follow-up was nondifferential with respect to pregnancy outcome or baseline violence, while administrative censoring was correlated with pregnancy outcome due to study design. While the models used in this analysis are fairly robust to differing follow-up times, it is possible that unmeasured predictors of censoring induced selection bias.

Due to technical issues, data was not collected on sexual intimate partner violence at certain time points, preventing us from fully assessing this type of violence, particularly in the first year postpartum when some research suggests sexual violence risk is increased. Because of this missing data, findings regarding “any violence” should be interpreted strictly as any physical, emotional, or economic violence. Additionally, we did not collect information on violence occurring specifically during pregnancy or in the immediate postpartum period. The period for which violence was reported lasted a year, potentially obscuring more discrete trends and leaving room for errors in recall. Underreporting may have been present due to the sensitive nature of these topics, although tools validated in this setting were used to assess violence and prevalence reported in this study was generally in line with that reported in nationally representative surveys. Lastly, unmeasured and unknown sources of confounding may have biased associations.

Future research should explore the acute effects of abortion access on violence risk by collecting more granular data on violence reported during pregnancy or postpartum. This could directly inform public health interventions designed to care for individuals at high risk for violence during the antenatal period. Additional work is also needed to clarify whether abortion access impacts specific types of violence more than others. This research raises questions about the mechanisms by which abortion access, and reproductive autonomy broadly, may offer protection against violence. Qualitative research could be used to investigate pathways around empowerment, mental health, and relationship stress, shedding light on how reproductive autonomy may be leveraged to improve people's overall health and wellbeing in this setting.

We identified a specific protective effect of abortion access on subsequent intimate partner violence. Those who received their abortion experienced reductions in physical and economic violence, beginning one year after abortion seeking and in some cases continuing for the next five years, while those who were denied abortions did not. This protective effect has notable implications given that women experiencing an unintended pregnancy are disproportionately more likely to already be experiencing intimate partner violence.32 In Nepal, as in the US,16 women in abusive relationships may experience abortion access as a pathway out of abuse. On the other hand, those who were unable to access abortion were more likely to experience emotional, physical, and sexual violence in the years after abortion seeking. Denial of abortion care, in addition to being a violation of human rights and autonomy, has consequences for women's health and wellbeing even five years later.
