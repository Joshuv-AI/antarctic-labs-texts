Pri-MCCD: A Real-World Multimodal Dataset for Analyzing Classroom Climate in Primary School Lessons
Authors: Weigang Lu, Ying Chen, Xiaofang Wang, Cunling Bian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnlike experienced teachers who intuitively perceive classroom dynamics, artificial intelligence still struggles to develop comparable perceptual abilities due to the complex, evolving nature of real classroom climates. Capturing this shared emotional and cognitive atmosphere is essential for understanding teaching effectiveness and student development, and especially challenging in primary school settings, where students’ expressions are often nonverbal and volatile. Existing datasets are typically task-specific, single-modal, and collected in controlled environments, limiting their ability to reflect authentic multimodal interactions. Annotations also tend to be shallow and lack labels that represent the broader classroom climate. To address these limitations, we present Pri-MCCD, a multimodal classroom climate dataset built from real-world primary school lessons. It integrates visual and auditory features like body posture and acoustic cues. The dataset contains fifteen 40-minute classes across subjects, and is designed to support research in classroom diagnostics, affect-aware systems, and learning environment analysis.Subject terms: Education, Human behaviour

Background & Summary

Artificial intelligence is profoundly transforming modern society, yet for it to be truly integrated into highly social contexts like education, it must be capable of perceiving the complex interplay of human emotions and interactions1. The classroom, as one of the most intricate social environments2, is not merely a space for knowledge transmission but a dynamic system co-constructed by teachers and students3,4. At the core of this system lies the classroom climate, the collective emotional and cognitive state of the group5. A positive climate is strongly linked to teaching effectiveness, student motivation, and academic achievement, making its automated recognition a critical step for developing truly responsive and socially intelligent educational AI6,7. Analyzing classroom climate is fundamentally different from traditional perception tasks. Unlike object recognition, it is a subjective, continuously evolving phenomenon that emerges from the interactions of multiple participants8. It resembles complex psychosocial constructs like “collective cohesion” or “team emotional tone” rather than an individual’s emotional state9. This presents a profound challenge: AI must shift from an individual-centric paradigm to a group-level, context-aware estimation of social states. This requires systems to move beyond detecting “who is speaking” or “who is smiling”, and toward understanding the collective emotional tone of the class as a whole, and how this shared climate evolves in response to pedagogical and social dynamics10. For primary education, it is a critical stage in which children gradually develop their emotional regulation, social skills, and behavioral habits. The classroom climate during this period has a profound impact on students’ learning motivation, self-awareness, and interpersonal development11. Young students tend to experience frequent emotional fluctuations and are more susceptible to the influence of teacher attitudes, classroom climate, and peer interactions12,13. A positive classroom climate is thus essential for fostering their sense of psychological safety. Compared to older students, primary school teachers face greater emotional regulation demands in classroom management. If artificial intelligence systems can effectively perceive classroom climate, they hold promise for reducing teacher workload while enhancing instructional quality.

The complexity of modeling classroom climate manifests on several interconnected levels. From a perceptual standpoint, the climate is shaped by a multifaceted array of multimodal signals, including fleeting facial expressions, body gestures, and the prosodic features of speech14,15. Yet the meaning of these signals is not absolute and depends heavily on context7,16,17. For example, a high level of classroom noise might signify disruptive behavior in a structured class like mathematics, but collaborative engagement during a hands-on art project. This contextual sensitivity means that any robust model must be able to interpret signals based on the specific goals and norms of the learning environment. Furthermore, classroom climate exhibits a strong sense of holism and irreducibility18. It cannot be accurately inferred by simply aggregating the states of individual students. The collective dynamic often produces patterns and outcomes greater than the sum of its parts, a phenomenon that challenges simple bottom-up modeling approaches19. Modeling classroom dynamics in primary settings presents unique challenges. Children’s behaviors are less stable, more reactive, and less synchronized, which increases the complexity of temporal modeling and group-level emotion recognition20. Moreover, primary classrooms often involve diverse and flexible forms of interaction, such as educational games, group reading, and music-based activities, making multimodal data collection and annotation design significantly more demanding21. These multifaceted challenges, spanning multimodal representation, contextual interpretation, and holistic modeling22, underscore an urgent need for new data resources. High-quality, multimodal, group-level, and contextually grounded datasets are essential to advance methodologies and ensure that educational AI develops true ecological validity, moving beyond mere technical feasibility.

However, many existing datasets used in affect recognition and behavior analysis are collected in highly controlled laboratory environments23,24. These datasets often rely on presenting participants with carefully selected images, audio clips, or videos to elicit emotional responses. While such methods offer high signal-to-noise ratios and strong experimental repeatability, the emotions they evoke tend to be idealized, simplified, and overly explicit. These kinds of responses are rarely observed in real-world classroom settings, which limits the representativeness and applicability of lab-generated data to authentic educational contexts. In contrast, emotional and social dynamics in real classrooms are far more subtle and complex25. Changes in classroom climate rarely manifest as sudden, intense emotional outbursts. Instead, they are reflected in nuanced variations across multiple dimensions, including group interactions, non-verbal cues, prosodic features of speech, and the progression of classroom activities. These patterns are highly dependent on contextual factors such as teaching content, instructional pace, teacher-student relationships, and cultural norms26. As a result, data collected in laboratory settings often fail to generalize to the dynamic, socially embedded nature of actual classrooms, limiting their effectiveness in the development of educational evaluation. Moreover, existing real-world classroom datasets often suffer from limited scope. Many focus on a single aspect of classroom climate, such as emotion detection or behavioral analysis, without offering a holistic view of the classroom as a multimodal, socially constructed, and context-sensitive environment. For instance, some datasets emphasize individual student emotions while overlooking teacher-student interactions27, while others focus on observable behaviors or speaking turns but lack insight into the underlying emotional and cognitive processes28,29. In addition, many datasets are constrained to a single modality, relying solely on visual inputs (e.g., images or video frames) or auditory signals (e.g., raw speech or acoustic features)30,31. While such unimodal datasets can provide useful insights for targeted tasks, they fall short in capturing the complex, dynamic nature of classroom climate, an environment shaped by continuous, multimodal interactions. Relying on a single modality often misses important cross-modal cues, such as the alignment between a teacher’s vocal tone and facial expression. Classroom climate is inherently multimodal, involving the interplay of sound, visuals, and embodied behaviors. Ignoring this integration risks an incomplete or even misleading interpretation of classroom dynamics. The absence of comprehensive datasets that fuse emotional, behavioral, linguistic, and interactive signals across modalities remains a major bottleneck in effectively modeling and sensing real-world classroom climates.

To address the lack of datasets for multimodal evaluation of classroom climate in real-world settings, we introduce a Primary school Multimodal Classroom Climate Dataset(Pri-MCCD). Collected from 15 unscripted lessons using in-class recording equipment, the dataset provides synchronized audio and video covering key signals such as teacher-student interactions, body posture and acoustic features. By incorporating annotations from multiple observer perspectives, the dataset enhances the reliability and richness of classroom climate representation, enabling more nuanced and accurate modeling. Overall, it serves as a foundational benchmark for developing and evaluating classroom analysis models. Beyond supporting robust climate recognition, the dataset facilitates fine-grained investigations into the micro-level dynamics of teaching and learning, such as emotional shifts, engagement patterns, and teacher-student interaction styles. This contributes to the advancement of more interpretable, adaptable, and context-aware AI systems for real-world educational environments.

Methods

Ethics statements

In accordance with ethical research standards, informed consent was obtained from school principals, teachers and parents prior to data collection, along with verbal assent from the participating students. Access to the raw, unedited data is strictly limited to research purposes. Specifically, participants were informed that videos and audio recordings containing clearly identifiable facial features and voices would be shared in an online repository for research use. They were also made aware that their participation was entirely voluntary and that they could withdraw from the study at any time without any negative consequences. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Scientific Ethics Committee of Ocean University of China, under approval number OUC-HM-2025-047. All procedures were conducted in compliance with the ethical guidelines and regulations approved by the relevant institutional review board. To protect participant privacy while preserving data integrity, additional measures were taken, such as removing video segments that contained students’ real names.

Participants

The data for this study were collected from a public primary school located in an urban area of China. A total of 13 teachers and approximately 546 students participated in the study. The school enrolls students primarily from nearby neighborhoods, resulting in a relatively homogeneous socioeconomic background among the student population. Classes of the same grade follow a parallel structure, ensuring comparable teaching content and academic levels across different classrooms. The participating teachers were between 26 and 35 years old, with varying degrees of teaching experience and naturally diverse instructional styles. The student participants ranged from grades 3 to 6, with ages between 9 and 14. They demonstrated strong classroom engagement and verbal expression abilities. The dataset covers five major subjects, including Chinese, English, music, and art, capturing a wide range of instructional content and classroom interaction patterns. This diversity provides a rich contextual foundation and behavioral cues for modeling classroom climate.

Acoustic Features: To extract acoustic features from classroom audio, we first used the MoviePy toolkit to separate the audio track from the original instructional videos and convert it into standard WAV format. Once prepared, the openSMILE toolkit was applied to extract the IS09 Emotion Challenge feature set32. The IS09 feature set consists of 384 statistical descriptors capturing pitch (F0), energy, spectral characteristics, and Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs), along with their first- and second-order derivatives to reflect temporal dynamics. Features are computed at the segment level using statistical functionals (e.g., mean, standard deviation, skewness, kurtosis) over each 5-second clip. Acoustic features were extracted from the full classroom audio recordings without isolating human speech from environmental sounds. Consequently, the extracted descriptors characterize the overall classroom acoustic environment rather than exclusively speech-derived properties. This design choice preserves the ecological validity of the recordings, allowing the dataset to reflect natural multimodal classroom conditions.

Data collection procedure

The data collection process consists of four main stages: Setup & Onboarding, Baseline Recording, In-Class Recording, and Post-lesson Annotation, as summarized in Table 1. These stages were designed to ensure a structured and comprehensive approach to capturing, contextualizing, and annotating classroom cliamtes, providing a solid foundation for subsequent analysis.

Setup & Onboarding. During each data collection session, two researchers are present in the classroom. To minimize observer effects, they avoid interacting with teachers or students once the lesson begins. Their role is strictly limited to operating and monitoring the recording equipment. Before the students arrive, the research team conducts a comprehensive equipment check. This includes adjusting the camera angle to ensure optimal coverage of both the teacher’s activity zone and the student seating area, performing sound checks on the microphone to prevent issues such as clipping or low volume, and verifying time synchronization across all devices to ensure data consistency. Teachers receive a brief explanation of the research objectives and the data collection procedures. They are explicitly instructed to carry out the lesson as usual, without making any special adjustments for the recording. The research team emphasizes that the primary goal is to capture the natural dynamics of the classroom environment as authentically as possible.

Baseline Recording. We conduct a baseline recording to establish a reference point for non-interactive or neutral classroom states, which is essential for distinguishing meaningful variations in behavior, affect, and interaction throughout the lesson. Silent reading is selected as the baseline activity because it typically involves minimal verbal exchange and consistent body posture, reducing variability and external stimuli. This allows researchers to isolate changes in classroom dynamics that arise from instructional activities, rather than general classroom noise or spontaneous behavior. The teacher instructs students to engage in two minutes of silent reading, during which the researchers initiate the recording at the onset of the activity.

Teachers and students conducted the class as usual, and the recording proceeded continuously and without interruption. This setup allowed the natural ebb and flow of classroom climate to be captured over time, including transitions between different instructional activities (e.g., from lecture to group discussion). In addition, the classroom seating arrangements varied across sessions based on the teacher’s preferences, including traditional row-based layouts as well as alternative configurations such as U-shaped or saddle-shaped distributions, reflecting authentic pedagogical practices.

Post-lesson Annotation. After the class concluded, we segmented the full-length video into 5-second clips to facilitate subsequent analysis and processing. We then invited two experienced expert annotators to label the video clips based on a set of pre-agreed annotation guidelines aimed at categorizing classroom climate. To enhance the scientific rigor and validity of the annotation process, several additional measures were implemented. First, both experts underwent standardized training to ensure consistent understanding of the labeling criteria. Second, we adopted a double-coding strategy, where each clip was independently annotated by both experts. This allowed us to calculate inter-rater agreement metrics, with the final Cohen’s Kappa score reaching 0.821, indicating a high level of consistency. For clips where disagreements arose, a secondary round of discussion was conducted to reach a final consensus. All annotated results were then stored in a structured database, ensuring efficient retrieval, systematic analysis, and effective integration into model training workflows.

Annotation Protocol

In this study, we aim to model the classroom climate by developing a theoretically grounded and practically feasible multi-dimensional annotation framework. This framework is designed to capture the dynamic interplay of teacher-student interactions and collective emotional states in real classroom settings, allowing for a deeper understanding of the cognitive and affective dimensions embedded in classroom social contexts.

To ensure the scientific rigor and validity of the annotation system, we draw upon well-established frameworks from educational psychology and classroom observation research, with particular reference to the Classroom Assessment Scoring System (CLASS)33, which has been widely validated as a reliable and effective framework across diverse educational settings34,35. Developed by the University of Virginia, CLASS is a widely adopted tool for assessing the quality of classroom interactions in K-12 education. It emphasizes the structured observation of teacher-student exchanges to evaluate the overall learning environment. The CLASS system organizes classroom dynamics into three core domains: Emotional Support, Classroom Organization, and Instructional Support, each comprising several sub-dimensions, and uses a 7-point scale to assess the quality of interactions.

Emotional Support. In the domain of Emotional Support, we focus on both the overall emotional tone of the classroom and the quality of teacher-student emotional connections. To capture this, we define two sub-dimensions: Collective Student Emotion and Teacher-Student Emotional Rapport. The first sub-dimension assesses the group-level emotional expressions of students within a given time segment, such as signs of engagement, smiling, laughter, or, conversely, visible signs of boredom, fatigue, or anxiety. The second sub-dimension evaluates whether the teacher demonstrates care, respect, and emotional sensitivity, such as using a warm tone, encouraging student participation, and responding empathetically to students’ emotional cues. This annotation design is informed by Self-Determination Theory36 and Attachment Theory37, both of which emphasize that meeting students’ psychological needs for autonomy, relatedness, and security is critical for sustaining positive classroom climate and effective learning.

Classroom Organization. The dimension of classroom organization primarily focuses on whether teaching activities are conducted in an orderly manner, whether the pacing is appropriate, and whether students are able to remain engaged throughout the lesson. Within this dimension, we introduce two sub-indicators: clarity of instructional flow and student behavioral engagement. These indicators are designed to assess the naturalness of transitions between classroom activities, the clarity of the teacher’s instructions, and the extent to which students maintain attention during different phases of the lesson. For example, in a classroom where the instructional flow is clear and directions are well-articulated, students are more likely to complete tasks smoothly and transition seamlessly from one activity to the next, demonstrating a high level of engagement. In contrast, when the flow is disorganized or instructions are ambiguous, students may become distracted, engage in side conversations, or display off-task behaviors, potentially disrupting the overall classroom environment. This framework draws on behaviorist learning theory38, which emphasizes the influence of external stimuli on learning behaviors, as well as cognitive load theory39, which highlights the impact of instructional structure on learning efficiency. By integrating these perspectives, this dimension aims to uncover how classroom organization affects both teacher-student interactions and the overall quality of student engagement.

Instructional Support. In the dimension of instructional support, we focus on the quality of teacher-student interaction and the opportunities students have to express their ideas, with an emphasis on whether the classroom environment encourages higher-order thinking and individual expression. To capture these aspects, we define two sub-dimensions: interaction quality and student voice. The first assesses whether teachers promote student thinking through questioning, responding, and guiding. The second evaluates whether students are given sufficient opportunities to articulate their thoughts and perspectives. For instance, when a teacher poses open-ended questions, facilitates meaningful peer discussions, and provides constructive feedback, it often leads to richer interactions and greater cognitive engagement among students. In contrast, in classrooms dominated by lecture-based instruction with limited space for student input, learners may become passive recipients of information, showing reduced motivation to participate or express their ideas. This dimension is closely aligned with Vygotsky’s sociocultural theory40, which posits that learning occurs through social interaction. According to this theory, a teacher’s supportive actions can foster students’ cognitive growth within their “zone of proximal development,” ultimately enhancing the depth and autonomy of learning.

Building on this foundation, we adapt the CLASS framework to align with the characteristics of our multimodal dataset, captured through video and audio. Specifically, while retaining the three high-level domains of CLASS, the adaptation concerns the temporal unit of analysis and operational decision rules, as our annotations are conducted on segmented 5-second clips rather than extended observation cycles used in the standard CLASS protocol. The 5-second duration was selected to balance interactional coherence and temporal precision for multimodal modeling. When multiple behavioral states occur within a segment, annotators apply a peak-dominance principle, labeling the clip according to the most interactionally salient and affectively dominant state observable within the window. Consistent with the CLASS framework, we employed a 7-point rating scale. Annotators first assigned a score from 1 to 7 based strictly on observable multimodal evidence within the 5-second segment, without inference beyond visible or audible cues. For dataset release and computational usability, these scores were subsequently collapsed into three discrete labels using predefined thresholds: scores of 1–2 were mapped to Negative, 3–5 to Neutral, and 6–7 to Positive. This mapping follows conventional interpretations of the CLASS scale, in which lower scores reflect strained or low-quality interactions, mid-range scores reflect routine or moderate interaction quality, and higher scores indicate emotionally supportive and cognitively engaging classroom environments.

Annotation decisions were grounded in observable behavioral and acoustic indicators, including facial expressions, collective student affect, teacher tone and prosody, clarity of instructional transitions, turn-taking patterns, and visible engagement or disengagement signals. To distinguish between Neutral and Positive ratings, annotators applied explicit operational decision criteria. A segment was labeled Positive when multiple converging cues indicated active engagement and supportive interaction, such as coordinated student attention combined with warm or encouraging teacher feedback. A segment was labeled Neutral when classroom activity appeared procedural, emotionally moderate, or task-focused without strong affective intensity or cognitive stimulation. A segment was labeled Negative when visible or audible cues indicated disengagement, disorder, emotional distance, or strained interaction, such as scolding tone, off-task behavior, chaotic transitions, or dismissive responses. Data Records

The dataset, named Pri-MCCD41, comprises a total of 4,357 video segments, each approximately 5 seconds in duration. Among these segments, 731 are annotated as Negative, 2,114 as Neutral, and 1,512 as Positive. Each video sample has a spatial resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels, and the corresponding audio is recorded in stereo at a sampling rate of 44.1 kHz. In total, the dataset contains approximately 363 minutes of classroom recordings. Pri-MCCD were collected from 15 distinct lessons delivered by 13 teachers, involving approximately 546 students. The recorded lessons span multiple subject areas, including Visual Arts, Music, Practical Skills, Chinese Language, and English, thereby ensuring diversity across both artistic and language-based instructional contexts.

All samples are organized under a root directory, which is divided into three subdirectories corresponding to the climate categories: negative, neutral, and positive. Each subdirectory contains the video clips in MP4 format and the corresponding audio files in WAV format. Each segment is assigned a unique four-digit numerical identifier and follows a category-specific prefix: neg for negative, neu for neutral, and pos for positive. The filename consists of the prefix, the four-digit identifier, and an emotion label suffix: _0 for negative, _1 for neutral, and _2 for positive. For example, neu0023_1.mp4 and neu0023_1.wav denote the same neutral segment. This dual encoding strategy, combining directory-level categorization with both prefix and suffix label indicators, allows samples to be retrieved either by filename parsing, by folder structure, or through a combination of both. The four-digit identifier ensures global uniqueness across the dataset, preventing sample duplication and minimizing the risk of label ambiguity during data processing. Acoustic features were extracted from each WAV file using the openSMILE toolkit with the IS09_Emotion configuration. The resulting features are stored as individual NumPy (.npy) files prefixed with IS09 and followed by the same identifier and label suffix as the corresponding MP4 and WAV files (e.g., IS09neu0023_1.npy). Each file contains a 384-dimensional array representing the acoustic characteristics of the corresponding 5-second segment. This consistent naming convention establishes a strict one-to-one correspondence among video, audio, and feature files, ensuring reliable cross-modal alignment and systematic dataset organization.

The diversity in subject matter, instructional timing, and classroom spatial configurations enhances the ecological validity and representativeness of the dataset. Specifically, the inclusion of both artistic (e.g., Music, Visual Arts) and linguistic (e.g., Chinese, English) subjects captures a broad spectrum of classroom interaction styles, ranging from expressive, student-centered participation to structured, teacher-led instruction. The recordings span both morning and afternoon sessions, allowing the dataset to reflect natural fluctuations in student energy levels, engagement, and classroom dynamics throughout the school day. Additionally, the variety of classroom seating arrangements, including traditional row seating, U-shaped layouts, circular formations, and collaborative group clusters mirrors real-world pedagogical practices and supports the analysis of spatial influences on teaching strategies and student behavior. Together, these dimensions create a rich and realistic dataset that supports fine-grained research on classroom climate, multimodal teacher-student interaction, and the development of intelligent learning analytics systems capable of understanding complex educational environments. Technical Validation

The validation analyses reported in this section are conducted at the aggregated dataset level. They are intended to assess annotation reliability and discriminative validity of the labeled segments, rather than to draw discipline-specific or subject-level conclusions. As classroom interaction patterns may vary across instructional contexts, the present validation focuses on overall dataset-level consistency.

Quality Validation

All recorded videos undergo manual visual inspection to ensure that the primary subjects (teachers and students) are clearly visible and unobstructed in the footage. The audio tracks are also reviewed to identify and exclude segments with obvious noise interference or missing clips. Sessions with severe issues, such as corrupted files, persistent background humming, or significant audio-video desynchronization, are removed from the final dataset. Ultimately, the dataset retains 13 complete and valid classroom recordings.

All annotations are independently performed by two experts with backgrounds in education or psychology. For any disagreements in labeling, the annotators engage in focused discussions and negotiations to reach consensus, ensuring both consistency and subjective validity of the labels. Based on a three-class labeling scheme (positive, neutral, negative), the inter-rater agreement is high, with a Cohen’s Kappa value of 0.821, providing strong support for the reliability and robustness of the data.

Multimodal Discriminative Validation

To assess whether the annotated climate labels contain meaningful multimodal signal, we trained a baseline multimodal classifier based on42 using visual and acoustic features. For the visual modality, spatial features were extracted using ResNet43, and head pose information was obtained using MTCNN44. For the audio modality, IS09_Emotion acoustic features were incorporated. Using these multimodal inputs, the classifier achieved an accuracy of 0.7538, precision of 0.7350, recall of 0.7670, and F1-score of 0.7443 on the test set. Performance substantially above chance level indicates that the annotated labels exhibit separable patterns in multimodal feature space. These results support the discriminative validity of the dataset and demonstrate its suitability for multimodal modeling.

Acoustic Feature Validation of Climate Labels

To further examine whether the annotated climate categories exhibit systematic variation in acoustic space, we conducted statistical analyses on representative IS09 features, shown in Fig. 3. Specifically, we evaluated Mean Pitch (F0 Mean), Energy Standard Deviation (Energy StdDev), and Pitch Variation (F0 StdDev) across the three labeled categories. All analyses included Negative, Neutral, and Positive groups to ensure complete representation of the annotated climate types. Distributional comparisons and one-way ANOVA tests were performed across the full dataset. Mean Pitch (F0 Mean) showed significant variation among the three categories (p < 0.001). Energy StdDev also exhibited statistically significant differences (p < 0.001), whereas Pitch Variation (F0 StdDev) did not show significant differences (p = 0.893). Although the distribution of samples across climate types is not fully balanced, all categories were incorporated into the statistical comparisons and visualizations.

Collectively, these results provide evidence that the labeling scheme captures measurable and internally consistent signal, while also indicating that classroom climate cannot be fully characterized by single acoustic indicators.

Usage Notes

Potential applications

The dataset provides a multimodal resource for studying authentic classroom climate and affective dynamics in real-world educational settings. Our dataset can be used to evaluate the effectiveness of multimodal fusion in automated classroom climate recognition and to validate the performance of multimodal models. Researchers can also apply data mining and machine learning techniques to build models of classroom engagement and emotional states based on multimodal behavioral features, providing real-time feedback and instructional support for teachers. The synchronized video, audio, and standardized IS09 acoustic features enable benchmarking and methodological validation of multimodal models under ecologically valid classroom conditions. The dataset enables quantitative analysis of interactional dynamics associated with classroom climate, such as the density of teacher-student exchanges, the proportion of student-initiated speech, response latency following teacher prompts, and the distribution of speaking time between teachers and students. These measurable behavioral indicators can be examined in relation to annotated climate categories to explore how interaction structure and participation patterns correspond to different classroom climate states. For example, researchers may investigate whether higher proportions of student-initiated contributions or more balanced turn-taking patterns are associated with segments labeled as Positive climate. Beyond model development, the dataset may serve as a pilot benchmark for methodological research and cross-cultural comparative studies in educational affect computing.

Limitations

These data are entirely derived from primary school classrooms in one specific location, and as such, the observed interaction patterns and classroom norms may reflect specific cultural characteristics. The dataset is therefore intended as a methodologically grounded and ecologically valid resource rather than a statistically representative sample of all educational contexts. Future research should aim to expand investigations across more diverse cultural contexts and cover a broader range of educational stages to enhance the generalizability and representativeness of the findings. In addition, while the dataset encompasses multiple classroom contexts, it is not designed to represent the full spectrum of subject-specific instructional variability. Consequently, the present validation focuses on overall multimodal consistency rather than discipline-specific effects.
