MRP8‐Cre Transgenic Mice Are Resistant to Weight Gain and Diet‐Induced Obesity Independently of Neutrophils
Authors: Stephan Forisch, Fatemeh Soltani, Laia Salvat Costa, Beatriz M Cardoso, Yaiza Moreno Galiano, Cedric Duval, Robert A S Ariëns, Navdeep S Chandel, Mari T Kaartinen, Stefanie K Wculek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObesity is a major risk factor for metabolic syndrome, cancer, diabetes, and other diseases. The occurrence of comorbidities is associated with systemic and adipose tissue inflammation, which is marked by the presence of macrophages and neutrophils in adipose tissue. The role of neutrophils in driving adipose tissue inflammation and pathology is understudied and may hold therapeutic promise. MRP8Cre transgenic mice are frequently used to investigate neutrophil functions. A recent report demonstrates that the insertion of the MRP8Cre transgene resulted in whole‐body deletion of Ap1s1 and Serpine1, the latter encoding the pro‐obesogenic factor plasminogen activator inhibitor‐1 (PAI‐1). Here, we show that MRP8Cre transgenic mice have lower body weight than their littermate controls on a standard chow diet. Furthermore, they are resistant to weight gain, adipose tissue hypertrophy, systemic deregulation of glucose metabolism, infiltration of macrophages into AT, and liver steatosis upon feeding of obesogenic, high‐fat diets (HFD). Mechanistically, this was independent of neutrophils or bone marrow‐derived cells, sex, age of the mice, the duration of the HFD feeding, treatment start, housing facility, or the original source of the mice. The expression of Sepine1/PAI‐1 and Ap1s1 is globally reduced across metabolic organs of MRP8Cre mice irrespective of diet, thus phenocopying previous reports of the obesity‐protective effect of PAI‐1 depletion in mice. Our findings demonstrate the limited applicability of MRP8Cre mice for obesity or metabolic studies due to the observed off‐target effects and highlight the need for respective controls when using transgenic mice, such as Cre‐only controls or use of independent Cre lines for studies involving the Cre/loxP system.Keywords: neutrophils, obesity, PAI‐1, transgenic mouse models

Introduction

Obesity is a major risk factor for type 2 diabetes, cancers, cardiovascular and thrombotic diseases. The etiology of the comorbidities has underlying contributions from chronic, systemic low‐grade inflammation, which originates from adipose tissue (AT) dysfunction during weight gain [1]. AT inflammation upon obesity arises from several factors, including adipocyte stress and death, mechanical stress on cells from surrounding fibrotic extracellular matrix (ECM), and dysregulation of fatty acid metabolism [2]. The inflammation hampers the metabolic state and mitochondrial function of the AT, adipogenesis and lipogenesis, which in turn causes ectopic lipid accumulation in the metabolic organs, such as the liver andskeletal muscle [2, 3]. Monocytes are recruited to the AT via locally released danger signals and chemokines such as CCL2, where they differentiate into AT macrophages, which are the key promoters of AT inflammation. The presence of macrophages in AT contributes to AT insulin resistance and subsequently to other downstream obesity‐associated pathologies [2, 4]. Macrophage infiltration into AT is promoted by the presence of fibrin deposits and pro‐coagulatory and antifibrinolytic molecules such as Factor XIII‐A (FXIII‐A) transglutaminase and PAI‐1 in AT [5, 6]. The process, referred to as thromboinflammation, aggravates the obesity‐related metabolic dysfunction and promotes weight gain [7, 8].

Neutrophils play a significant role in thrombotic disorders and AT inflammation, and elevated neutrophil numbers correlate with increased inflammation and worsening health [9, 10, 11]. Upon weight gain, neutrophils infiltrate AT within days, and initially drive AT inflammation via promoting pro‐inflammatory macrophage recruitment, which further aggravates AT dysfunction and inflammation [12]. Furthermore, neutrophil‐derived reactive oxygen species and neutrophil extracellular traps contribute to obesity‐related inflammation [13, 14]. In line with this, reducing neutrophil abundance [15, 16], preventing neutrophil extracellular trap release [17] or inhibiting neutrophil elastase activity [18] improves the health status of obese mice fed a high‐fat diet (HFD). Hence, a better understanding of the activities and biology of neutrophils as promoters of obesity‐related pathologies holds great therapeutic promise.

Mouse models of obesity and transgenic mice represent invaluable tools for functional studies of the role of neutrophils in obesity, including neutrophil‐specific gene deletion via the Cre‐loxP system using Lyz2Cre [19], Ms4a3Cre [20], Ly6gCre [21], or MRP8Cre [22] mice. Lyz2Cre and Ms4a3Cre driver lines delete genes in neutrophils and monocytes, which is suboptimal due to the known pathology‐promoting role of monocyte‐derived macrophages in obesity [2]. The Ly6gCre line is specific for neutrophils, but deletes very late in their development, which often only allows partial gene deletions in neutrophils that may be insufficient, especially during slowly progressing chronic disease processes such as obesity. In contrast, the MRP8Cre line permits efficient gene deletion and is largely specific to neutrophils [23], which makes it well suited to study the relevance of neutrophils in obesity.

The transgenic MRP8Cre mouse line was generated by the random genome integration of an MRP8‐Cre‐ires‐eGFP cassette that mediates expression of the Cre recombinase and eGFP by the human S100A8 (myeloid‐related protein 8, MRP8) promoter [22]. The MRP8Cre mouse helped advance our knowledge on neutrophil biology, but limitations of this research tool have emerged [24, 25] as for other Cre lines [26]. Firstly, homozygosity for the MRP8Cre transgene is lethal in mice, likely due to the insertion of the MRP8‐Cre‐ires‐eGFP cassette into chromosome 5, which causes complete deletion of the Serpine1 (Plasmin Inhibitor Activator‐1, PAI‐1) gene and partial deletion of Ap1s1 (Adaptor Related Protein Complex 1 Subunit Sigma 1) in the mouse genome [25]. Moreover, a recent report demonstrated that mice carrying the MRP8Cre transgene develop arthritis, induced by anticartilage antibody, which was attributed to the lack of Serpine1/PAI‐1 expression in neutrophils and macrophages upon LPS stimulation [24].

PAI‐1 inhibits fibrinolysis as part of the hemostatic process to sustain blood clots, and its function is strongly linked to mediation of thromboinflammation and exacerbation of obesity‐related pathologies, diabetes, and metabolic syndrome [7, 8, 27]. Systemic PAI‐1 levels are elevated in obesity in patients and mice via increased expression in AT and liver [28, 29, 30]. Interference with PAI‐1 in mice, either genetically or pharmacologically, leads to resistance to weight gain upon overnutrition or genetic predisposition. PAI‐1 impairment also protects from AT inflammation and obesity‐related metabolic dysfunctions [6, 31, 32, 33, 34]. In human subjects, heterozygosity for SERPINE1/PAI‐1 correlates with elevated lifespan and lower incidence of diabetes [35]. Currently, PAI‐1 inhibitors are being developed for clinical applications in cancer, thrombosis, and age‐related conditions (including obesity and diabetes) [36].

In this study, we report that MRP8Cre transgenic mice have a decreased body weight, are resistant to diet‐induced obesity, AT inflammation, liver steatosis, and the development of disturbances in diabetes/glucose metabolism. Expression of Ap1s1 and Serpine1 is significantly decreased in metabolic organs of MRP8Cre transgenic mice fed with chow or an obesogenic diet, which likely causes the observed protection against diet‐induced obesity. Our results emphasize the necessity for Cre‐controls in research using gene editing via the Cre‐loxP system in mice.

Results

MRP8Cre Transgenic Mice Have a Reduced Body Weight and Are Protected From HFD‐Induced Weight Gain Independent of Housing, Floxed Alleles or Bone Marrow‐Derived Cells

We observed a decreased body weight of 8‐week‐old wildtype and heterozygous MRP8Cre male and female littermates on a standard chow diet (Figure 1A). Additionally, to mimic an experimental scenario involving a Cre driver line for gene deletion, the Wculek laboratory used MRP8Cre mice to eliminate a mitochondrial bioenergetics‐related factor in neutrophils, the gene Uqcrq [37]. Chow‐fed male and female MRP8Cre mice carrying Uqcrqf/f alleles (MRP8Cre Uqcrqf/f ) also had a lower body weight than their littermate Uqcrqf/f controls at 8 weeks of age, which was maintained over time (Figure 1B,C). Tibia length was equal or only slightly changed between experimental groups, suggesting largely unaltered growth of mice (Figure S1A). Next, to model diet‐induced obesity, wildtype (MRP8+/+ ) and heterozygous MRP8Cre littermates as well as Uqcrqf/f and MRP8Cre Uqcrqf/f littermates were placed on a 60% kCal from fat HFD, and their weight was monitored over time. Male and female MRP8Cre mice showed a drastically reduced weight gain irrespective of the presence or absence of floxed alleles compared with controls, which developed the expected increase in body mass (Figure 1D,E). Again, tibia length was largely equal or only minimally altered between experimental groups (Figure S1B). To dissect underlying causes of the observed weight gain protection of MRP8Cre mice, we first interrogated the specific contribution of bone marrow‐derived cells, mainly neutrophils and monocytes, as the MRP8Cre transgene is globally inserted in all cells of MRP8Cre mice. Male wildtype mice were lethally irradiated to ablate bone marrow cells and grafted with bone marrow from either control Uqcrqf/f or MRP8Cre Uqcrqf/f littermates, which resulted in circulating neutrophil chimerism of about 90% (Figure 1F,G). Notably, MRP8Cre transgene presence exclusively in bone marrow‐derived cells did not reduce weight gain of mice upon HFD feeding (Figure 1H). This observation shows that factors other than neutrophils or bone marrow‐derived cells underlie the weight‐gain protection of HFD‐fed MRP8Cre mice. To further confirm this notion, we used Ms4a3Cre mice to target gene expression in neutrophils [20]. Male mice carrying the Ms4a3Cre together with our model floxed allele Uqcrqf/f (Ms4a3Cre Uqcrqf/f mice) were not protected from HFD‐induced weight gain either when compared with their littermates (Figure 1I; Figure S1C). In line with this, the abundance of neutrophils across tissues, their transcriptome, expression of maturation‐associated proteins, respiratory burst capacity, and reactive oxygen species production was unaltered in chow‐fed and/or HFD‐fed control Uqcrqf/f versus MRP8Cre Uqcrqf/f littermates (Figure 1J; Figure S1D–I).

Secondly, we investigated a potential influence of other environmental or experimental factors on the decreased body weight and HFD‐induced weight gain of MRP8Cre mice, including the model floxed allele, age of starting HFD feeding, housing environment, and the origin of the MRP8Cre mice. Therefore, similar studies were independently established by the Kaartinen group that bred MRP8Cre mice with the floxed allele F13a1f/f (MRP8Cre F13a1f/f ) with the initial aim to investigate the role of fibrin crosslinking enzyme FXIII‐A (F13a1) in neutrophils [5]. Chow‐fed 20‐week‐old male MRP8Cre and MRP8Cre F13a1f/f mice exhibited a decreased body weight compared with their F13a1f/f controls (Figure 1K). Of note, full‐body F13a1‐deficient (F13a1 −/−) mice display features of metabolically healthy obesity upon obesogenic diet feeding and gain a comparable amount of weight as wildtype controls [5]. Next, male MRP8Cre , MRP8Cre F13a1f/f , and control F13a1f/f mice were fed a 60% HFD from 4 weeks of age onward. In line with our previous observations, the HFD‐fed control mice showed a significant increase in body weight, but MRP8Cre and MRP8Cre F13a1f/f mice were largely protected from HFD‐induced weight gain (Figure 1L). A similar pattern was observed in female MRP8Cre mice, with differences showing after 10 weeks on HFD (data not shown). Again, general growth and body size of MRP8Cre mice remained unaltered compared with mice of other groups, as body length was similar (Figure S1J).

Overall, two independent laboratories observed that MRP8Cre transgene heterozygous mice display a lower body weight on standard chow diets than their control littermates and are resistant to weight gain on HFD. This phenotype was not influenced by MRP8Cre transgene presence in bone marrow‐derived cells/neutrophils, presence of floxed alleles, onset of HFD feeding, housing conditions, or source of MRP8Cre mice, suggesting that the protection against HFD‐induced obesity results from the global MRP8Cre transgene insertion in those mice.

MRP8Cre Transgenic Mice Exhibit an Improved Global Metabolic Health Despite Equal Food Intake

Next, we analyzed parameters of systemic metabolic health, the pathophysiology of obesity, and the development of metabolic syndrome in control (MRP8+/+ or Uqcrqf/f ), MRP8Cre , and/or MRP8Cre Uqcrqf/f mice on a chow diet and after HFD feeding. Chow‐fed 16 (males) or 22 (females) week‐old Uqcrqf/f and MRP8Cre Uqcrqf/f littermates exhibited no changes in lean mass; especially, MRP8Cre Uqcrqf/f females had a slightly decreased fat mass, and both genders exhibited a trend towards accelerated clearing of injected high glucose in a glucose tolerance test (GTT) (Figure 2A,B). After HFD feeding, male and female MRP8Cre heterozygous littermate mice had a significantly reduced fat mass by about 50% and only minimally unaffected lean mass (Figure 2C; Figure S2A), which suggests largely unaltered growth between experimental groups and a specific reduction in fat levels in MRP8Cre mice. This was accompanied by significantly decreased plasma cholesterol and glucose concentrations in males and improved clearance of injected high glucose levels (GTT) in MRP8Cre Uqcrqf/f versus control littermates of both sexes (Figure 2D,E).

Of note, chow‐ and HFD‐fed MRP8Cre (and MRP8Cre Uqcrqf/f ) mice consumed equal or elevated amounts of drink and food as controls (Figure 2F,G; Figure S2B–E), in line with their comparable plasma triglyceride concentrations after HFD feeding (Figure 2E). They exhibited similar voluntary movement activity, but especially female MRP8Cre transgene‐carrying mice showed an elevated energy expenditure compared with controls (Figure 2H,I; Figure S2B–E). This is likely contributing to the resistance of MRP8Cre mice to HFD‐induced weight gain.

Overall, the lower weight of MRP8Cre transgenic mice on a chow diet and their reduced weight gain upon HFD feeding, specifically affecting fat mass, are not explained by alterations in food consumption and result in ameliorated systemic glucose metabolism.

Reduced Fat Deposition Is Accompanied by Improved WAT and Liver Health in HFD‐fed MRP8Cre Transgenic Mice

Excess calories are stored in white adipose tissue (WAT), mainly epididymal/perigonadal (eWAT/pgWAT) and inguinal/subcutaneous WAT (iWAT) fat pads, that become hypertrophic and inflamed upon obesity and, at more advanced stages, fat is deposited in the liver causing steatosis. WAT‐associated inflammation is characterized by cytokine/chemokine production and elevated macrophage infiltration, which form crown‐like structures to engulf dying adipocytes [1, 2, 4]. On a chow diet, the fat pads of MRP8Cre transgenic mice had a mildly reduced weight compared with littermate controls, which was more pronounced upon age‐related fat accumulation (Figure 3A; Figure S3A). However, the eWAT/pgWAT and iWAT fat pads were notably smaller in male and female HFD‐fed MRP8Cre and MRP8Cre Uqcrqf/f mice compared with control littermate mice (despite a low n number in one control group), with significantly smaller adipocytes in size in eWAT and iWAT (Figure 3B–E; Figure S3B,C). Furthermore, F4/80+ and CD64+ macrophage presence and crown‐like structure formation were also reduced in eWAT and iWAT of male HFD‐fed MRP8Cre mice, in concert with decreased expression of the monocyte‐attracting chemokine Ccl2 (Figure 3D,F; Figure S4A–G). Yet, the expression of other inflammatory parameters in eWAT/iWAT, WAT neutrophil infiltration, or systemic/circulating monocyte and neutrophil numbers remained unaffected (Figures S1H and S4F,J). HFD‐fed female MRP8Cre Uqcrqf/f versus littermate Uqcrqf/f mice did not exhibit altered macrophage presence in pgWAT (Figure 3F; Figure S4B,E). Furthermore, work in the Kaartinen laboratory confirmed that the elevated macrophage infiltration into eWAT and iWAT upon HFD feeding of control F13a1f/f mice was also not observed in male MRP8Cre F13a1f/f mice by analyzing expression of the macrophage‐characteristic gene Adgre1 that encodes F4/80 (Figure S4K). Given the pronounced protection of MRP8Cre transgene‐carrying mice from overnutrition‐induced morphological alterations of the WAT, but only mild changes in inflammation that predominantly affect macrophage presence, we further explored parameters of WAT health in those mice. First, insulin‐sensitive genes and genes characteristic of insulin (receptor) signaling were more highly expressed in eWAT of male HFD‐fed MRP8Cre and MRP8Cre Uqcrqf/f mice versus control mice/littermates (Figure 3G), suggesting improved insulin sensitivity of WAT. Similarly, Adipoq (encoding adiponectin) expression, but not that of Lep (encoding leptin), was elevated in eWAT of male MRP8Cre mice on a HFD (Figure 3H), which further indicates ameliorated metabolic health of the WAT of HFD‐fed MRP8Cre transgenic mice. Finally, despite a mildly altered expression of genes involved in thermogenesis, male HFD‐fed MRP8Cre and MRP8Cre Uqcrqf/f littermates exhibited a reduced expression of the central WAT beiging regulator Dio2 and undetectable expression of the thermogenesis factor UCP1 across genotypes in the iWAT (Figure S5A,B), a tissue with a known role in adaptive thermogenesis [38]. Overall, those data suggest similar inflammatory gene expression in WAT, despite decreased WAT macrophage infiltration and ameliorated WAT insulin sensitivity, adipocyte function, and improved metabolic health in HFD‐fed MRP8Cre transgenic mice compared with controls, with no overt signs of WAT beiging.

Next, we investigated indicators of steatosis and observed unaltered liver weights in chow‐fed MRP8Cre versus MRP8+/+ and MRP8Cre Uqcrqf/f versus Uqcrqf/f littermates (Figure 3A; Figure S3A). Yet, livers of foremost male MRP8Cre and MRP8Cre Uqcrqf/f mice after HFD feeding remained lighter than those of their littermates (Figure 3B; Figure S3B), despite a low n number in one control group. Indeed, male MRP8Cre Uqcrqf/f mice showed significantly decreased lipid accumulation in the liver and reduced concentrations of the lipid damage‐associated protein alanine aminotransferase (ALT) in plasma (Figure 3I–K). Female MRP8Cre transgenic mice only showed very mildly ameliorated liver health parameters, likely due to the known protection of females against HFD‐induced liver pathologies (Figure 3J,K). The weight of nonmetabolic organs such as the lung did not seem to be overtly affected in MRP8Cre mice irrespective of sex or diet (Figure 3A,B; Figure S3A,B).

In summary, we found that mice carrying the MRP8Cre transgene are largely resistant to HFD‐induced adipose tissue hypertrophy, likely due to improved AT physiology, including insulin signaling, which also elevates their liver health.

Expression of Serpine1 (PAI‐1) Is Decreased Across Metabolic Organs of MRP8Cre Transgenic Mice Upon HFD Feeding

The insertion of the MRP8Cre transgene into the mouse genome causes a heterozygous, global deletion of Serpine1 (PAI‐1) and Ap1s1 [25] (Figure 4A), which mimics SERPINE1 heterozygous loss of function in human subjects that have a decreased prevalence of diabetes [35]. We queried the CZ CELLxGENE Discover platform [39] for expression of SERPINE1 across distinct cell types in human liver and adipose tissue and found it to be ubiquitously expressed without clear tissue or cell tropism (Figure S5C,D). Hence, we evaluated the expression of Serpine1 and Ap1s1 in MRP8Cre mice in fat depots. Firstly, as expected [28, 29, 30], we observed that Serpine1 expression is significantly upregulated in iWAT of control F13a1f/f mice upon HFD feeding compared with control diet‐fed F13a1f/f mice, although this was not observed in eWAT. Ap1s1 was also upregulated in the iWAT and eWAT of control F13a1f/f mice upon HFD feeding (Figure 4B,C). Notably, we found a significantly reduced expression (by more than 50%) of Serpine1 and Ap1s1 in fat pads of chow‐ and HFD‐fed MRP8Cre F13a1f/f versus control F13a1f/f mice (Figure 4B,C). In line, Serpine1 and Ap1s1 expression was also significantly decreased in eWAT, iWAT and liver from chow‐ and HFD‐fed male and female MRP8Cre and MRP8Cre Uqcrqf/f mice compared with control/littermate Uqcrqf/f mice (Figure 4D–G).

PAI‐1/Serpine1 impairment was shown to decrease adiposity, reduce weight gain and obesity‐related pathologies in mice and humans [6, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35]. However, pro‐obesogenic effects of Serpine1 loss in mice were also reported [34]. The genetic background of mice seems to account for those contradictory findings [40], which will need to be considered in translational studies of PAI‐1/Serpine1 in metabolic diseases. Notably, PAI‐1/Serpine1 loss in adipocytes improves adipocyte insulin sensitivity and glucose uptake, and PAI‐1/Serpine1‐deficient adipocytes are resistant to TNFα‐induced dedifferentiation and insulin signaling inhibition [41]. Hence, the significant decrease of Serpine1 expression across metabolic organs of HFD‐fed MRP8Cre transgenic mice is likely contributing to their resistance to the gain of fat mass, AT hypertrophy and deterioration, macrophage infiltration into AT, and associated systemic metabolic syndrome. On the other hand, homozygous deficiency of AP1S1/Ap1s1 is embryonically lethal in mice and humans [42, 43], which hampers studies of its function. However, permissive pathogenic variants of AP1S1 are associated with a rare autosomal recessive neurocutaneous disorder called IDEDNIK that can cause diarrhea, liver disease, and mental and physical retardation [44]. Interestingly, the complete loss of the related isoform Ap1s2 in mice was implicated in impaired adipocyte differentiation, but does not affect systemic glucose homeostasis [42]. Hence, Ap1s1 loss and/or the simultaneous loss with Serpine1 may additionally influence the WAT, liver and systemic metabolic health of MRP8Cre transgenic mice.

Discussion

In this study, we report that MRP8Cre transgenic mice have an intrinsic resistance to weight gain on normal diets, which is aggravated upon overnutrition‐induced metabolic stress. We observed reduced expression of Serpine1/PAI‐1 and Ap1s1 in their fat depots and other metabolic organs, which is likely explained by the heterozygous loss of both genes due to MRP8Cre transgene integration [25]. MRP8Cre mice were protected from weight gain, AT macrophage infiltration, liver steatosis, and diabetes upon HFD feeding, which phenocopies PAI‐1 impairment in obese mice [6, 31, 32, 33, 34] and PAI‐1 heterozygosity in human subjects [35]. Notably, the improved metabolic health of MRP8Cre transgenic mice seems to be largely equal in both sexes and independent of floxed alleles, housing conditions, onset of HFD feeding, and, especially, neutrophils. The decreased global expression of Serpine1 in chow‐ and HFD‐fed male and female MRP8Cre transgenic mice seems plausible to account for their improved metabolic health. However, additional factors may influence or promote the intrinsic (metabolic) phenotypes of MRP8Cre/+ mice, including the decreased expression of Ap1s1 [42], off‐target effects of Cre or eGFP expression, alterations in tertiary DNA structure due to transgene integration, and other unknown variables, which remain to be defined. Importantly, Serpine1/PAI‐1 and Ap1s1 play crucial roles beyond obesity or metabolic syndrome, including inflammatory diseases, cancer, cerebral infarction, T cell responses [35, 45, 46], and other rare diseases such as IDEDNIK, respectively [44, 47, 48]. Hence, we recommend caution when using the MRP8Cre/+ transgenic mouse model in experimental research across different fields and diseases, which is highlighted by their recently described susceptibility to arthritis [24].

In conclusion, our study advocates for the careful evaluation of MRP8Cre transgenic mice for studies of metabolism, thrombosis, or any context where Serpine1/PAI‐1 or Ap1s1 may be involved. On a broader scale, our findings emphasize the importance of including appropriate controls in experiments using genetic mouse models, foremost the necessity of Cre‐only controls and the validation of experimental results in independent Cre lines by studies relying on the mouse Cre‐loxP system.

Materials and Methods

Mice

C57BL/6N wildtype, B6.Cg‐Tg(S100A8‐cre.‐EGFP)1Ilw (MRP8Cre kind gift from A. Hidalgo [CNIC, Madrid, Spain] used by the Wculek lab or obtained from The Jackson Laboratory [stock #021614] use by the Kaartinen lab), Uqcrq1.1Ncdl (Uqcrqf/f ) [37], Ms4a3em2(cre)Fgnx (Ms4a3Cre kind gift from F. Ginhoux; Gustave Roussy, Villejuif, France) [20] and FXIII‐Aflox/flox (F13a1fl/fl ) [49] mice were kept in specific pathogen‐free conditions with 12 hour light–dark cycle and ad libitum access to food and water at the animal facility of the Parc Científic de Barcelona (registry number B9900044) or in the Animal Facility of McGill University. All Uqcrqf/f and MRP8Cre/+ Uqcrqf/f mice, as well as all used MRP8+/+ and MRP8Cre/+ mice in experiments, were always littermates. Additionally, we included age‐ and sex‐matched MRP8Cre/+ mice in several analyses using Uqcrqf/f and MRP8Cre/+ Uqcrqf/f littermates or F13a1f/f mice.

Diet‐Induced Obesity, Metabolic Assessments and Mouse Procedures

Eight‐week‐old male and female mice received 60% kCal from fat HFD (D12492i, ResearchDiets Inc.) for 9–10 (males) or 15 (females) weeks at IRB Barcelona or standard chow diet (U8100G10R, SAFE Diets). Four‐week‐old male and female mice received 60% kCal from fat HFD (TD.06414, Inotiv Inc) for 16 weeks, and control mice received nutrition‐matched 10% kCal from fat control diet (TD.08806, Inotiv Inc) at McGill University.

For Glucose tolerance tests, fasted mice were injected intraperitoneally with 1 g/kg glucose. Blood glucose concentration was determined using an Accu‐Chek Instant (Roche). Body composition was assessed using a minispec LF50 Whole Body Composition Analyzer (Bruker). Metabolic phenotyping was performed using a Promethion Core (Sable Systems). For bone marrow transplantation, male recipient C57BL/6N mice received two lethal X‐ray radiation doses of 5.5 Gy (SMART 200, RX‐YLON International) before intravenous injection of 20 × 106 donor bone marrow cells from Uqcrqfl/fl and MRP8ΔUqcrq mice.

Tissue Dissociation for Cell Isolation

Bone marrow was isolated from the tibia and femurs by cutting open the epiphyses and flushing the bone marrow with ice‐cold PBS. Blood was collected in EDTA K3 microvette tubes (Sarstedt). Red blood cells were lysed with red blood cell lysing buffer Hybri‐Max (R7757, Sigma‐Aldrich), and cells were filtered through a 70 µM cell strainer.

Mice were perfused with HBSS (11560456, Gibco). eWAT and pgWAT were minced and incubated for 20 min in HBSS (Gibco) with 0.5% BSA (A7030, Merck) and 2 mg/mL Collagenase II (LS004176, Worthington) at 37°C and shaking at 250 rpm. Digested tissue was filtered through a 100 µM cell strainer. Red blood cells were lysed using red blood cell lysing buffer Hybri‐Max and cells filtered through a 40 µM cell strainer.

Oxidative Burst

Bone marrow cells were isolated as stated above and incubated with biotinylated anti‐Ly6G antibody in MACS buffer (130‐091‐221, Miltenyi Biotec) for 20 min on ice. Cells were washed and incubated with Streptavidin Microbeads (130‐048‐102, Miltenyi Biotec) for 15 min on ice. Cells were washed and positively labelled cells separated using an LS column (130‐042‐401, Miltenyi Biotec) as per the manufacturer's instructions. The neutrophil‐enriched fraction (>95% purity) was resuspended in Seahorse media (103576‐100, Agilent) supplemented with 10 mM glucose (15384895, ThermoFisher), 1 mM pyruvate (11530396, ThermoFisher), and 2 mM glutamine (11574466, ThermoFisher). Seahorse plates (102340‐100, Agilent) were coated with 22.4 µg/mL Cell‐Tak (354240, Corning), and 1.5 × 106 neutrophils were plated in each well. The plate was centrifuged at 50×g for 30 s without brake to attach floating neutrophils to the bottom of each well. After a 45 min incubation at 37°C, the plate was placed into a Seahorse XFe24 (Agilent) and the assay started. To induce oxidative burst, neutrophils were stimulated with 500 nM PMA (P1585, Sigma‐Aldrich) through a Seahorse well plate port.

Flow Cytometry

Single cell suspensions were incubated for 20 min at 4°C in PBS supplemented with 2% FBS (A5256801, ThermoFisher) and 2.5 mM EDTA (A3145.1000, ITW Reagents) containing Fc Shield (70‐0161‐M001, Cytek Biosciences) and a mix of fluorochrome‐conjugated antibodies. Dead cells were stained with 0.1 µg/µL DAPI (D1306, ThermoFisher) before acquisition on a FACSAria Fusion or FACSymphony A3 (BD Biosciences). A representative gating strategy is shown in Figure S6. Antibodies used for flow cytometry are shown in Table S1.

To measure cellular ROS levels, antibody‐stained neutrophils were incubated with 5 µM CellROX Deep Red (C10422, ThermoFisher) at 37°C for 30 min in phenol red‐free RPMI1640 supplemented with 2 mM glutamine, 100 U/mL Pen/Strep (11548876, ThermoFisher), 100 µM sodium pyruvate, and 10 mM HEPES (12509079, ThermoFisher). Cells were washed with RPMI1640 and acquired as described above.

Blood and Clinical Biochemistry

Cardiac blood was analyzed using a Veterinary Blood Cell Counter BC‐5000 VET (Mindray). Plasma analysis was performed using an MNCHIP PointCare V3 analyzer (RAL SA).

Tissue Processing, Embedding, and Staining

Tissues were fixed in 10% formalin, processed and embedded in paraffin or incubated in 30% sucrose overnight before embedding in OCT‐Tissue‐Tek medium (4583, Sakura). For macrophage and UCP1 protein visualization, paraffin sections were stained with rabbit anti‐F4/80 (1:750) or rabbit anti‐UCP1, followed by anti‐rabbit HRP. Antigen–antibody‐HRP complexes were visualized with 3‐3′‐diaminobenzidine. For the visualization of intratissue lipid droplets, cryosections were incubated in a 0.416% (w/v) Oil Red O solution (O0625, Sigma‐Aldrich), washed, and counterstained with hematoxylin. Brightfield images were acquired using a NanoZoomer‐2.0 HT C9600 digital scanner (Hamamatsu) with a 20X objective.

RNA Extraction and cDNA Synthesis

For experiments at IRB Barcelona, tissues were homogenized in TRIzol (15596018, ThermoFisher) using a Precellys 24 Homogenizer (Bertin). RNA obtained by chloroform‐induced separation was purified using the RNeasy Mini Kit (74106, Qiagen) following the manufacturer's protocol. cDNA was synthesized using the High‐Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (10186954, Applied Biosystems) as per the manufacturer's instructions. For experiments at McGill University, tissues were homogenized in QIAzol (79306, Qiagen) buffer, and RNA was purified using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen), following the manufacturer's protocol. cDNA was synthesized using the High‐Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems).

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

For experiments at IRB Barcelona, GoTaq qPCR Systems (A6002, Promega) were used as the master mix for determining relative gene expression. Actb was used as an internal control for normalization using the 2−ΔΔCt method. All primer sequences are shown in Table S1. For experiments at McGill University, TaqMan Gene Expression Assays were used for determining relative gene expression of Serpine1, Ap1s1 and Adgre1 (Mm00802529_m1). TATA‐box binding protein (Tbp) was used as the internal control, and relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Bulk mRNA Sequencing

Bone marrow neutrophils were prepurified using magnetic microbeads as stated above. Neutrophils (DAPI− CD11bhi GR1hi) were isolated using fluorescence‐activated cell sorting and cell pellets frozen at −80°C until RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The concentration of total RNA extractions was quantified with the Nanodrop One (ThermoFisher), and RNA integrity was assessed with the RNA ScreenTape assay of the TapeStation 4200 platform (Agilent). Libraries for RNA‐Seq were prepared at the IRB Barcelona Advanced Genomic Technologies core facility. Briefly, mRNA was isolated from 200 ng of total RNA and used to generate dual‐indexed cDNA libraries with the Illumina stranded mRNA ligation kit (Illumina) and UD Indexes Set A (Illumina). Thirteen cycles of PCR amplification were applied to all libraries. Libraries were quantified using the Qubit dsDNA HS assay (Invitrogen) and quality controlled with the TapeStation D5000 assay (Agilent). An equimolar pool was prepared with the eleven libraries and submitted for paired‐end 150 nt sequencing on a NovaSeq6000 S4 (Illumina). More than 191 Gbp of reads were produced, with a minimum of 52 million paired‐end reads per sample.

Bioinformatic Analysis

Analysis of bulk RNA‐seq data was performed in R (v4.1.3 & v4.6.0) using the Bioconductor package. Reads were aligned with STAR (v2.7.10a) [50]. Differential expression analysis was performed using Limma (v3.64.3) [51]. A gene was considered differentially expressed when both an empirical Bayes moderated F‐test and at least one pairwise moderated t‐test passed a Benjamini‐Hochberg corrected q‐value < 0.05 and exhibited a Log2FoldChange ≥1 in that contrast.

Normalized gene expression data of SERPINE1 in human tissues was acquired through the online portal of the CZ CELLxGENE Discover platform by filtering for SERPINE1 as the gene of interest in the corresponding human tissues [39]. Gene expression data of aggregated cell types were manually curated to yield a final list of definitive specific cell types.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 10) as indicated in figure legends. Briefly, comparison of two groups was performed using a two‐tailed unpaired t‐test, comparison of three or four groups was performed using one‐way ANOVA, and comparison of longitudinal data was performed using two‐way ANOVA. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean. All experiments were repeated at least twice, and data were pooled. All data points represent biological replicates. Differences are indicated as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.
