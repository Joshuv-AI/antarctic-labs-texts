Three novel Myxidium species (Myxozoa: Myxosporea) from fishes in river and small channels across Terengganu, Malaysia
Authors: Nadhirah Syafiqah Suhaimi, Csaba Székely, Gábor Cech, Boglárka Sellyei, Muhammad Hafiz Borkhanuddin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Previously, only a single Myxidium species, Myxidium cf. notopterum from Notopterus notopterus, and two unidentified species from Tor tambroides and Barbonymus gonionotus had been reported in Malaysia. In the present study, three additional species, Myxidium acutum n. sp., Myxidium truncatum n. sp., and Myxidium orbiculare n. sp. were described from the gallbladders of freshwater fishes, Labiobarbus leptocheilus, Trichopodus pectoralis and Channa gachua, respectively, in Malaysia. Myxidium acutum n. sp. possessed elongated, fusiform spores with tapered ends and 6–7 valvular striations, measuring 12.5 ± 0.6 μm in length, 5.1 ± 0.4 μm in width, and 4.3 ± 0.4 μm in thickness. The polar capsules were equal and slightly pyriform, measuring 3.3 ± 0.3 μm × 2.7 ± 0.2 μm, each containing a tubule with 4–5 coils. Myxidium truncatum n. sp. exhibited elongated, fusiform spores with truncated ends and five valvular striations, measuring 11.7 ± 0.6 μm × 4.4 ± 0.3 μm × 3.8 ± 0.3 μm. Its pyriform polar capsules measured 3.8 ± 0.3 μm × 2.7 ± 0.2 μm with four coils. Myxidium orbiculare n. sp. had ellipsoidal, slightly concave spores with truncated ends and seven valvular striations, measuring 13.4 ± 0.4 μm × 6.5 ± 0.4 μm × 5.5 ± 0.3 μm, with rounded polar capsules bearing papilla-like tips (4.1 ± 0.3 μm × 3.3 ± 0.2 μm), each containing a tubule with four coils. Morphological, molecular, and phylogenetic analyses supported their recognition as new species and extended the known diversity and geographic distribution of Myxidium in Malaysia.

Introduction

Myxidium Bütschli, 1882 is the second largest myxozoan genus with nearly 300 described species (Eiras et al., 2011; Ponzetto et al., 2026). Most of them are coelozoic, primarily infecting fish gallbladders, urinary bladders or renal tubules, with few records from amphibians, reptiles, and birds (Bartholomew et al., 2008; Eiras et al., 2011; Hartigan et al., 2012; Espinoza et al., 2017). Unlike Zschokkella, which has nearly spherical polar capsules clustered on one side of the spore body, Myxidium spores are distinguished by spindle-shaped, straight, crescent, or sigmoid bodies with pointed ends, two pyriform polar capsules at opposite ends, smooth or striated shell valves, and a bisecting suture line (Lom and Dyková, 2006; Heiniger and Adlard, 2014).

In Malaysia, records remain scarce, with only Myxidium cf. notopterum from Notopterus notopterus (Borkhanuddin et al., 2020) and two comprehensively not described species from Tor tambroides (Borkhanuddin, 2013) and Barbonymus gonionotus (Suhaimi et al., 2025a). Previously, most Myxidium species have been detected among cyprinids, while none were known in Anabantiformes. Here, we describe three new freshwater Myxidium species that parasitize the gallbladders of fish from the order Cypriniformes (a single species) and Anabantiformes (2 species) based on morphology, morphometrics and 18S rDNA sequence analysis.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and microscopic study

A total of thirty-nine fish specimens representing three species; Labiobarbus leptocheilus (N = 15), Trichopodus pectoralis (N = 12) and Channa gachua (N = 12) were obtained from a local fish market in Kuala Terengganu, Terengganu, Malaysia. These fish originated from the river of Sungai Nerus in Kuala Nerus and from small channels across Kuala Terengganu. The procurement was conducted at two-week intervals over two periods: July to August 2023 and September to November 2024.

Live specimens were transported in oxygenated plastic bags to the Marine Science Biodiversity Laboratory of the Faculty of Science and Marine Environment at Universiti Malaysia Terengganu. Upon arrival, the fish were maintained in aerated tanks until autopsy and fed with commercial fish diet once daily. Taxonomic identification of the fish specimens was based on their morphological characteristics. In the laboratory, the fish were euthanized by neural pithing, dissected, and major organs, such as the fins, gills, skin, intestine, stomach, kidney, gallbladder, and muscles, were thoroughly examined to detect plasmodia of myxosporean parasites. Initial inspections were conducted with naked eye, followed by detailed observations using an Olympus SZX7 dissecting microscope (Olympus Corporation, Japan) and an Olympus CX33 biological microscope (Olympus Corporation, Japan) to confirm the presence of the plasmodia.

Free-floating spores and plasmodium were isolated from the gallbladders and examined on glass slides using the biological microscope. After microscopic assessment, the remaining plasmodium and spores were divided into two and preserved in 90% ethanol for molecular analyses and in 10% neutral buffered formalin for morphological analysis. All fixed specimens were subsequently transported to the Fish Pathology and Parasitology Laboratory at the HUN-REN Veterinary Medical Research Institute (Budapest, Hungary) for further investigation, including high-magnification examinations (100× objective) and photographic documentation with an Olympus BX53 light microscope equipped with an Olympus DP74 digital camera (Olympus Corporation, Japan). Morphometric analysis of formalin-fixed spores was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of Lom and Arthur (1989) using ImageJ software. All measurements are reported as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), with the range provided in parentheses and expressed in micrometers (μm). Line drawings of Myxidium spores were prepared from original digital images using Inkscape (Free Software Foundation, Inc., MA, USA).

The pellets including spores were rinsed twice with 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.5) to eliminate any residual ethanol. Phylogenetic analyses

The obtained 18S rDNA sequences were visualized, corrected if necessary based on the chromatogram, and assembled using Geneious Prime v11.1 (Bio-matters; Auckland, New Zealand; https://www.geneious.com/) (Kearse et al., 2012). Phylogenetic analysis was conducted using the three sequences (from each fish host individuals) generated in this study, together with 41 related sequences retrieved from the GenBank database, using a threshold of ≥80% identity and ≥80% query coverage. Ceratomyxa shasta (AF001579) was designated as the outgroup. Sequences with lower identity (<80%) or poor support were excluded. Multiple sequence alignment was performed using the ClustalW algorithm (Thompson et al., 1994) implemented in MEGA X (Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis, 64-bit version; https://www.megasoftware.net/) (Kumar et al., 2018). Phylogenetic tree was reconstructed using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method with 1000 bootstrap replicates in MEGA X, and Bayesian Inference (BI) conducted in MrBayes v3.2.443 (Ronquist et al., 2012). Gaps were treated using partial deletion with 75% site coverage cut-off for ML. The best-fit mucleotide substitution model (GTR + G + I) was selected based on the Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) executed in jModelTest 2.1.10 v2016030344 (Darriba et al., 2012). For Bayesian analysis, Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulations were run for 1,000,000 generations, with the initial 25% discarded as burn-in. A consensus tree was generated from the remaining sampled topologies. Clades were considered well supported when bootstrap values (BS) were ≥ 70% for ML and posterior probabilities (PP) were ≥ 0.90 for BI analysis. The resulting tree topologies were visualized in MEGA X and FigTree v. 1.4.445 (Rambaut, 2018), and further refined graphically using Inkscape software. Pairwise genetic distances were calculated in MEGA X using p-distance model, accounting for both transitions and transversions.

Results

Description of novel Myxidium species

Myxidium acutum n. sp. (Fig. 1A–D)

Spore: Fixed dispersal spores, coelozoic, elongate and fusiform in both frontal and sutural views, with tapered ends, 12.7 ± 0.5 (12.0–13.9) μm long, 5.1 ± 0.4 (4.5–5.9) μm wide, 4.4 ± 0.3 (3.5–4.9) μm thick (n = 16). Polar capsule: Two, equal, slightly pyriform polar capsules, positioned at opposite ends of the spore and oriented outward in opposite directions, 3.3 ± 0.3 (2.6–3.7) μm long, 2.7 ± 0.2 (2.3–3.1) μm wide. Distance between two polar capsules, 5.0 ± 0.5 (4.1–6.3) μm. Polar tubule: Coiled four to five times, positioned perpendicularly to capsule axis. Sutural line: Straight. Valvular striation: Six to seven longitudinal striations (Fig. 1B). Sporoplasm: Binucleate, filling the spore body cavity between the two polar capsules. Class: Myxosporea Bütschli, 1881

Order: Bivalvulida Sgulman, 1959.

Family: Myxidiidae Thélohan, 1892

Genus: Myxidium Bütschli, 1882

Host: Thin-lips barb Labiobarbus leptocheilus Valenciennes, 1842 (Order: Cypriniformes). Average length between 7.0 and 13.0 cm.

Locality: Sungai Nerus, Kuala Nerus, Terengganu, Malaysia.

Prevalence: 6.67% (1/15).

Type material: Phototypes were deposited in the parasitological collection of the Zoological Department, Hungarian Natural History Museum, Budapest, Coll. No. HNMPCC-HNHM-PAR-72089.

Etymology: The name Myxidium acutum n. sp. is derived from the pointed ends of the spore.

Molecular data: Partial 18S rDNA sequence of M. acutum n. sp., consisting of 1,862 base pairs was deposited in GenBank under the accession number PZ519830. Pairwise distance estimation of M. acutum n. sp. indicated the highest similarity of 99.4% to Zschokkella sp. (KM401441), which infects the gallbladder of Labeo rohita in Myanmar (Table 3). Phylogenetic analyses revealed two main distinct lineages: A–B (Fig. 2). Clade A comprised majority of myxosporean species, including most Myxidium spp., together with several freshwater and marine Zschokkella spp. In contrast, Clade B contained a small number of Myxidium and Zschokkella species, predominantly originating from freshwater habitats, with only two species reported from marine environments. Remarks: Based on spore morphology, this species is classified as a new member of the genus Myxidium. The morphology and morphometrics of M. acutum n. sp. do not match any previously described Myxidium spp. (Table 4). However, we found a sequence of Zschokkella sp. (KM401441; 2057 base pairs) deposited in GenBank sharing 99.4% similarity to M. acutum n. sp. Unfortunately, the morphology and morphometric measurements of these spores have not been published for comparison. Remarkably, the host species also differ, infecting L. leptocheilus and Labeo rohita from Myanmar. Discussion

Several scientific reports have been published on the myxozoans of Malaysia in previous years; however, only a single Myxidium species, Myxidium cf. notopterum has been described from this country (Borkhanuddin et al., 2020). This study aimed to broaden the survey by examining additional host species such as L. leptocheilus, T. pectoralis, and C. gachua, to identify potential new species. In this study, three novel Myxidium species were described namely Myxidium acutum n. sp. from L. leptocheilus, Myxidium truncatum n. sp. from T. pectoralis, and Myxidium orbiculare n. sp. from C. gachua in Malaysia. These species were characterized based on morphological features and analyses of the 18S rDNA gene sequences. All three species exhibited similar tissue tropism, as they were detected in the gallbladder of their respective hosts.

Based on BLASTn analysis, the Myxidium sample collected from gallbladder of L. leptocheilus in this study showed 99.4% sequence similarity to Zschokkella sp. (KM401441; 2,057 base pairs) previously reported from the gallbladder of L. rohita. However, no morphological and morphometric were available for that species, as it lacked a valid description at the time of publication. This situation is consistent with findings from recent study on Zschokkella chezhachei, in which sequence data were available in GenBank but no accompanying morphological information existed to support the species identification evidence (Suhaimi et al., 2025b). Consequently, Z. chezhachei was described as a new taxon based on combined molecular and morphological. The spores of M. acutum n. sp. exhibited typical Myxidium morphology such as fusiform or sigmoid shape with pointed ends and pyriform polar capsules located at each pole (Lom and Dyková, 2006), in which differed clearly from the characteristics of Zschokkella, whose spores are typically subspherical to spherical with nearly spherical polar capsules positioned on one side of the spore. These morphological distinctions ruled out the possibility of synonymy, supporting its classification as a new species. The previous misidentification of Myxidium as Zschokkella may have resulted from low-resolution microscopic images or insufficient taxonomic expertise. Phylogenetic analysis further showed that M. acutum n. sp. clustered with Zschokkella sp. (KM401441) within a well-supported monophyletic clade of Myxidium species, suggesting that Zschokkella sp. (KM401441) likely represents a member of the Myxidium genus. Since the similarity was 99.4% and the sequence of Zschokkella sp. (KM401441) is long enough (2,057 bp) to include conservative and variable regions for comparison both samples may represent the same species. This could be explained by M. acutum n. sp. being developing in both L. leptocheilus or L. rohita. Given that both hosts are members of the family Cyprinidae and relatively closely related, such host sharing would be possible. Therefore, additional sampling of L. rohita in Myanmar to obtain specimens for morphological and morphometric comparison, is necessary to confirm this assumption.

Morphologically, M. acutum n. sp. was compared with several previously described Myxidium species, including Myxidium rhodei Léger, 1905; Myxidium eminentis Ishizaki, 1957; Myxidium melanostigmum Noble, 1966; Myxidium bouixi Siau, 1971; Myxidium iwamotoi Moser, Noble et Lee, 1976; Myxidium profundum Zubtschenko, 1981; Myxidium baueri Kovaljova et Gaevskaya, 1982; and Myxidium pantanalense Ponzetto, Adriano et Maia, 2026. Although these species share generally similar spore morphology, elongated, fusiform spores with pointed ends, they differ in morphometric characteristics. Furthermore, molecular data are currently unavailable for several of these taxa, which limits comprehensive genetic comparisons.

This study represent the first investigation of myxozoan parasites in T. pectoralis, leading to the identification M. truncatum n. sp. detected in the gallbladder. Morphological and morphometric comparisons with previously described Myxidium spp. revealed significant differences. Its morphology was most similar to M. djolonensis from Cameroon, sharing the rare feature of a fusiform body with truncated ends, in contrast to the pointed ends typical of most Myxidium spp. Despite their resemblance, they differed in spore dimensions, geographical distributions and host species. The phylogenetic and pairwise distance analyses showed that M. truncatum n. sp. was closely related to Zschokkella sp. (MT840090) from E. suratensis and Myxosporea (KP030767) from Synodontis sp. However, due to the lack of available morphological and morphometric data for these latter two species, direct comparison was not possible. Further sampling of E. suratensis and Synodontis sp. is recommended to enable full species descriptions of Zschokkella sp., and Myxosporea and to clarify their taxonomic relationships.

Since M. truncatum n. sp. exhibited the typical morphological characteristics and clustered within the lineage in Clade B (Fig. 2), it was confirmed to belong to genus Myxidium. However, the phylogenetic analysis of the closely related species revealed certain anomalies, such as the inclusion of a small group consisting of eight marine Zschokkella spp. and two Myxidium spp. within a predominantly freshwater Myxidium lineages, A and B, respectively. Similar clustering of marine Zschokkella species within the freshwater clades have been reported and discussed previously (Fiala and Bartošová, 2010; Yemmen et al., 2013; Rocha et al., 2013; Heiniger and Adlard, 2014; Azizi et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2020). According to Fiala and Bartošová (2010), this is because a secondary lineage of marine-origin myxosporeans re-entered marine environments after adapting to freshwater habitats. The taxonomic boundaries between Myxidium and Zschokkella remain poorly defined, largely due to similarities in their morphology, as defined by Lom and Dyková (2006), which has often resulted in inconsistent species assignments. Molecular approaches have therefore been increasingly applied to clarify the ambiguous distinction between these two genera (Fiala, 2006; Fiala and Bartošová, 2010). The disposition of the marine M. grauri n. sp. and M. sharmai n. sp. (Gupta et al., 2025) within the freshwater clade (B) also supports a subsequent marine-to-freshwater transition. Additionally, this clade includes Myxidium species that infect variety of fish hosts across Malaysia, Kenya, India and China, highlighting their wide geographic and host diversity.

Only a few studies have investigated myxozoan parasites infecting the genus Channa. Most records originate from India and China (Chen and Ma, 1998; Gupta et al., 2021; Eiras et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023); and only a single Myxidium species, Myxidium islampurium Sarkar, Mazumber et Pramanik, 1985 has been described from Channa marulius. In this study, an additional Myxidium species was identified from C. gachua. Both plasmodia and dispersed spores of M. orbiculare n. sp. were detected in the gallbladder of the host. This species exhibited a unique morphological feature, such as concave spore shape/form with slightly truncated ends, which has not been previously reported in other Myxidium species, supporting its recognition as a novel species. In comparison, M. islampurium from C. marulis displays a fusiform spore shape/form with tapered ends (Eiras et al., 2011). Unfortunately, the absence of M. islampurium sequences in GenBank prevented molecular comparison. Molecular analyses revealed that M. orbiculare n. sp. shared its highest similarity (91.0%) to M. chuatsi, a parasite of Siniperca chuatsi, which also exhibits a concave spore shape/form but with tapered ends. Furthermore, it confirmed the species’ separation, which occupies a basal position within a subclade that includes Myxidium spp. capable of infecting a variety of hosts (Rhinogobius similis, S. chuatsi, Labeo horie, Cranoglanis bouderius, L. leptocheilus, L. rohita, Culter alburnus, Myxocyprinus asiaticus from Order Gobiiformes, Centrarchiformes, Cypriniformes and Siluriformes) from China, Kenya, Malaysia and Myanmar. The obvious separation of M. orbiculare n. sp. probably reflects the absence of available Myxidium sequences from Channa species in current databases. Including M. islampurium sequence data could presumably place both species close together in the phylogenetic tree, given their shared host genus. Notably, this represents the first record of a Myxidium species infecting C. gachua. The distinctive morphological characters observed, particularly the appearance of spore ends, may reflect host specificity, suggesting that the truncated form may be an exclusive feature for Myxidium of Channa from Malaysia. Further surveys of Channa species across Southeast Asia could help clarify host-parasite coevolution, reveal the diversity of myxozoans in this genus, and better characterize the morphological features of Malaysian Myxidium.

In conclusion, this study provides strong morphological and molecular evidence supporting the description of Myxidium acutum n. sp., Myxidium truncatum n. sp., and Myxidium orbiculare n. sp. as new species. These findings expand current knowledge of Myxidium diversity in Malaysia, and highlight the need for a broader investigations of myxozoan parasites infecting native and introduced fish fauna in the region, as they may represent unexplored reservoirs of myxozoan biodiversity. Furthermore, this study represents the first record of Myxidium species and myxozoan parasites infecting L. leptocheilus and T. pectoralis, and the second report of a myxozoan species from C. gachua.
