Finite element analysis of force-multiplying bridge structure applied in screw internal fixation for distal radius type C fractures
Authors: Xie Huo, Yixuan Cao, Sun Ke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo investigate the differences in biomechanical performance between the Bailey bridge screw fixation system derived from truss units and the conventional volar locking plate-screw fixation system in the treatment of type C distal radius fractures, and to explore a simpler and minimally invasive internal fixation configuration. Based on the radial CT data of a healthy female volunteer, a finite element model of type C distal radius fracture was established, and the plate-screw group (PS group) and the Bailey bridge screw group (FMB group) were assembled respectively, with 2.7 mm screws adopted in both groups. Material parameters, boundary conditions and four physiological loading conditions (axial, rotational, palmar flexion, dorsiflexion) were set uniformly. The mesh size of 1 mm was determined through mesh convergence analysis, and the deformation scale factor of 1.0 and the color scale range of 0 ~ 0.66 mm were unified. The displacement amplitudes of the two groups of models were compared at the levels of the overall structure, internal fixation devices and articular surface. The maximum displacement amplitudes of the PS group under axial, palmar flexion and dorsiflexion conditions were 0.1636, 0.3760 and 0.0915 mm, respectively, with a reduction of 32.7, 37.7 and 84.9% compared with the FMB group, showing better rigidity. The FMB group exhibited advantages only under the rotational condition, with a maximum displacement amplitude of 0.2308 mm, a 65.0% reduction compared with the PS group. In terms of displacement distribution, the PS group showed a characteristic of local concentration, with high-displacement areas located at the contact area between the plate and the fracture end, the distal screw holes of the plate, and the dorsal and ulnar margins of the articular surface; the FMB group presented uniform displacement distribution at the fracture end, each screw and the entire articular surface without obvious concentrated areas. At the level of internal fixation devices, the displacement of the plate in the PS group was concentrated at the distal screw holes, while the displacement of the truss screws in the FMB group was evenly distributed. Moreover, the FMB group achieved fixation performance comparable to that of the PS group with less material. The conventional plate-screw fixation system has higher overall rigidity under multi-directional loads, and is suitable for patients with type C distal radius fractures who require rigid fixation and early weight-bearing. The Bailey bridge screw fixation system realizes load distribution by virtue of the truss structure, which has the advantage of minimal invasiveness and excellent stability under rotational condition, making it suitable for patients pursuing minimally invasive treatment and requiring early rotational functional exercise. This study provides a new option for the individualized internal fixation treatment of type C distal radius fractures, and also offers a new idea for the interdisciplinary integrated design of engineering structural science and orthopedics.Keywords: Distal radius type C fracture, Finite element analysis, Force-multiplying bridge structure, Truss element, Internal fixation systemSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

At present, the clinically common open reduction and internal fixation method for distal radius fractures is locked plate-screw fixation. This surgical approach is typically performed via a volar incision, which results in a relatively large wound and requires the transection of the pronator teres muscle. In addition to open reduction, closed reduction and limited open reduction are also available for the reduction of distal radius fractures, both of which can achieve satisfactory reduction outcomes. Therefore, the development of a more minimally invasive internal fixation method has become a current research focus in this field.

The BaileyBridge structure is an innovative configuration designed based on mechanical optimization, which consists essentially of prefabricated truss units, high-strength connectors and load-bearing panels. It constructs a force transmission system by virtue of the geometric stability of triangles1. Its key characteristic lies in realizing load distribution through a rational structural layout, which uniformly transmits external forces to all stress-bearing components. Meanwhile, it relies on the mechanical amplification effect to significantly enhance the overall load-bearing capacity with less material consumption. In the engineering field, this structure has been widely applied in scenarios such as wartime bridge construction and the establishment of emergency access for disaster relief due to its advantages of convenient assembly and adjustable span, demonstrating excellent mechanical stability and adaptability.

Currently, volar locked plate-screw fixation is the most commonly used internal fixation method for the surgical treatment of distal radius fractures in clinical practice. From a biomechanical perspective, this plate-screw fixation is an eccentric fixation method, which often leads to uneven mechanical distribution of the fixation structure and consequently causes stress concentration at the screw-bone interface or the local fracture site2. Such stress concentration is prone to induce complications including fixation loosening, screw fracture and delayed fracture union. The introduction of the BaileyBridge structure into fracture screw fixation enables more minimally invasive surgical procedures owing to its simple structure. Furthermore, its structural characteristics of load distribution and stress optimization can enhance the overall stability of the fixation system to achieve a better fixation effect3, provide a more physiologically compatible mechanical environment for the fracture site, and reduce the risk of fatigue failure of internal fixation devices. Since the majority of type C distal radius fractures require surgical treatment in clinical practice, this study selects type C distal radius fractures as the research model to provide a novel surgical fixation scheme for the treatment of this fracture type.

Experimental materials and methods

Experimental materials

A 40-year-old healthy female volunteer, standing 160 cm tall, with no history of forearm trauma or other medical disorders, was recruited for this study. Computed tomography (CT) data of the volunteer’s radius were obtained and stored in DICOM format. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of The Fourth People’s Hospital of Longgang District, Shenzhen, with the approval number: 202,307. The volunteer had signed an informed consent form and participated in the study voluntarily. All experimental procedures were strictly performed in accordance with the principles and norms formulated by the Medical Ethics Committee of our hospital. The experiment was conducted at The Fourth People’s Hospital of Longgang District, Shenzhen from February to August 2025.

Experimental methods

Establishment of the finite element model of type C distal radius fracture

A complete forearm of the volunteer was scanned layer by layer with thin-slice computed tomography (CT). The scanning parameters were set as follows: tube voltage of 120 kVp, automatically adjusted tube current, slice thickness of 0.625 mm, voxel size of 0.4 × 0.4 mm, and field of view (FOV) of 150–180 mm4. The complete DICOM data of the radius were acquired and then imported into Mimics 21.0 software. The threshold value for bone tissue was set to − 250 to 3000 HU5, with an appropriate grayscale value assigned to the data. Operations including threshold segmentation, region growing and mask editing were performed to fill the defects; the radius was independently segmented via region growing again and the cavities within were filled. A three-dimensional (3D) model was generated from the segmented mask, and after verifying the integrity of all bony landmarks of the radius, the model was smoothed and exported in STP format.

The STP format model was imported into Geomagic Wrap 2017 software, and a solid model of the radial cortical bone was constructed through a series of operations including 3D surface subdivision, denoising, feature removal, grid construction and surface fitting. A solid model of the cancellous bone was obtained by offsetting the cortical bone model, which was then saved separately in STP format. The constructed cortical and cancellous bone models were further imported into SolidWorks 2021, and assembled by the origin matching command, followed by saving in part format.

Referring to the typical characteristics of AO type C distal radius fracture, the fracture line originates from the volar cortex of the distal radius, extends obliquely, penetrates and involves the radiocarpal joint surface, forming a four-part fracture6. The model was reconstructed in accordance with the above characteristics, as shown in Fig. 1a.

Design

A Type C fracture model (intra-articular four-part fracture) was constructed based on the distal radius fracture model. A BaileyBridge structure was formed by using 6 screws with the articular surface as the bridge deck, as shown in Fig. 1c1; the state after the 6 screws were implanted into the distal radius is presented in Fig. 1c.

Group assembly

In this experiment, a plate-screw group and a BaileyBridge group were set up for comparative analysis. The plates were provided by Xiamen Greatcare Medical Technology Co., Ltd., and 2.7 mm screws were used in both groups. The assembled plate-screw group is shown in Fig. 1b, with the plate and screws presented in Fig. 1b1; the assembled BaileyBridge group is shown in Fig. 1c. The BaileyBridge screw construct was formed by taking the articular surface as the bridge deck and the ulnar and radial sides as the two abutments, using screws of the same specification as those in the plate-screw group (6 screws in total), as shown in Fig. 1c1. The assemblies of the two groups were constructed separately using SolidWorks 2021 (as shown in Fig. 1b and c) and then saved as xt files.

Material assignment and mesh generation

The above two groups of models were imported separately into the finite element analysis software ANSYS 17.0 to establish a static analysis module. A bonded contact was set for the screw-plate interfaces due to the threaded locking connection between screws and locking plates.

Tetrahedral meshing with 4 nodes per element was performed on the models9. A mesh convergence analysis was conducted using a global mesh refinement strategy, with three sets of mesh sizes set (2.0 mm, 1.0 mm, 0.5 mm)10. Under the premise of keeping material properties, boundary conditions, loads and contact settings consistent, simulation calculations were completed for each set of meshes. The maximum displacement and maximum von Mises stress were extracted as the core evaluation indices, and the relative errors between adjacent meshes were calculated11. The results showed that when the mesh size was reduced to 1.0 mm, the relative errors of all key indices were less than 5%, the results tended to be stable and met the convergence requirements. Therefore, the mesh size was set to 1 mm. The number of nodes and elements for each group of models are presented in Table 2.

Loading conditions and boundary conditions

Loading conditions were formulated based on the common mechanical loads acting on the distal radius during daily activities (Fig. 2). Therefore, four directional load simulation analyses were conducted on the two groups of models, namely axial, dorsiflexion, palmar flexion and rotation. The loading parameters were determined in accordance with the relevant references13.

Main outcome measures

To ensure the rigor and comparability of the biomechanical comparisons between groups, a deformation scale factor of 1.0 (actual physical deformation ratio) was uniformly set for all models in this study15. The color scale range of 0–0.66 mm was consistently adopted for the displacement nephograms under all working conditions based on the range of the results, enabling intuitive visual comparison and quantitative analysis of displacement amplitudes. After the four types of loads in different directions were applied to the two groups of models, the displacement distribution and maximum displacement of the internal fixation models were mainly observed for each group, respectively.

Model validation

The construction process of the type C distal radius fracture model, the setting of bone tissue thresholds, and the assembly method of internal fixation in this study all followed the classic approaches for finite element analysis of distal radius fractures16. The material parameters of cortical bone, cancellous bone and internal fixation devices, as well as the contact and loading condition parameters, were directly cited from well-recognized literature data in the field of orthopaedic biomechanics16, which were fully consistent with the parameter system adopted by Zhang et al.16 in their finite element research on fracture internal fixation.

Meanwhile, the core biomechanical trends of this study were compared with those of similar studies on internal fixation for distal radius fractures. Prasil et al.17 confirmed that locked plate-screw fixation exhibits a significant stiffness advantage under bending loads, yet its torsional resistance is greatly affected by screw arrangement. This finding is highly consistent with the mechanical performance trend of the plate-screw group in the present study, which further verifies the rationality of the established model and ensures that the simulation results can effectively reflect the actual biomechanical characteristics of the two internal fixation methods.

Results

Analysis of displacement characteristics of the two internal fixation models

Plate-screw group (PS group)

As shown in Fig. 3, the displacement distribution of the PS group exhibited a local concentration characteristic: the high-displacement areas were mainly concentrated at the contact region between the plate and the fracture site and around the screw holes at the proximal end of the plate, while the displacement in the radial diaphysis region was uniform and maintained at an extremely low level. This distribution characteristic reflects the “load concentration” mechanical mechanism of the locked plate18: as the core load-bearing structure, the plate bears the vast majority of external loads through its high stiffness, concentrating stress and deformation on the plate itself to minimize the overall micromovement at the fracture site, which is consistent with the clinical therapeutic principle of rigid internal fixation. However, it also confirms the potential risk of local stress concentration in eccentric fixation as mentioned in the introduction.

BaileyBridge group (FMB group)

As shown in Fig. 3, the displacement distribution of the FMB group presented a uniform dispersion characteristic: displacement was evenly distributed along the screw-bone interface at the fracture site and around each screw, with no obvious local high-displacement areas. This distribution characteristic stems from its truss-type screw arrangement configuration: multiple screws form a spatial triangular truss structure, which uniformly distributes and transfers external loads to the surrounding bone tissue, avoiding local deformation concentration caused by a single supporting structure and achieving the core mechanical advantage of “load sharing”19. This not only provides a more uniform mechanical environment for the fracture site but also lays a biomechanical foundation for the implementation of minimally invasive surgical procedures.

Axial loading simulates the mechanical environment of daily axial weight‑bearing of the limb. The results in Table 3 show that the displacement amplitude of the PS group was approximately 32.7% lower than that of the FMB group, suggesting that the conventional locking plate‑screw system provides higher overall stiffness under axial compression loading, restricts axial micromotion at the fracture site more effectively, and offers a more stable axial mechanical environment for fracture healing. The displacement amplitudes of both groups were within the acceptable micromotion range for indirect fracture healing (< 2 mm), and both could meet the basic fixation requirements for axial weight‑bearing.

Rotational loading simulates the mechanical environment during wrist torsion (e.g., towel wringing). The results in Table 3 show that the displacement amplitude of the FMB group was 65.0% significantly lower than that of the PS group, indicating that the BaileyBridge screw fixation system derived from truss units presents a distinct stability advantage under torsional loading. This finding is highly consistent with the load‑sharing design feature of the BaileyBridge structure: the spatial truss‑like screw arrangement disperses torsional loads in multiple directions and avoids the mechanical weakness of single‑plane support. In contrast, the conventional volar locking plate is an eccentric fixation structure that cannot effectively resist the tendency of rotational displacement under torsional loading, resulting in a significantly higher displacement amplitude.

Palmar flexion loading simulates the mechanical environment during wrist palmar flexion. The results in Table 3 show that the displacement amplitude of the PS group was approximately 37.7% lower than that of the FMB group, suggesting that the volar locking plate‑screw system exhibits an obvious stiffness advantage under palmar flexion loading. Under palmar flexion, the plate directly attaches to the volar cortex of the distal radius and directly counteracts the displacement effect of the palmar flexion moment on the fracture site, which conforms to its design philosophy of rigid internal fixation. In comparison, the BaileyBridge system lacks an integrally connected plate structure and has relatively insufficient resistance to palmar flexion moment, leading to a higher displacement amplitude.

Dorsiflexion loading simulates the mechanical environment during wrist dorsiflexion. The results in Table 3 show that the displacement amplitude of the PS group was approximately 84.9% lower than that of the FMB group, demonstrating the absolute advantage of the conventional plate‑screw system under dorsiflexion loading. The locking plate connects multiple screws into a mechanical integrity via its overall structure, forming an efficient anti‑dorsiflexion stiffness system that effectively restricts dorsal displacement of the fracture site. Conversely, the independent screw truss configuration of the BaileyBridge system provides relatively weak resistance to dorsiflexion moment, resulting in a significantly higher displacement amplitude under the dorsiflexion condition.

Overall, the two internal fixation systems exhibited differentiated biomechanical advantages: the PS group had smaller displacement amplitudes and higher stiffness under axial, palmar flexion and dorsiflexion conditions, making it more suitable for clinical scenarios requiring multi-directional rigid fixation and early weight-bearing; the FMB group showed lower displacement amplitudes only under rotational conditions, and at the same time had the mechanical characteristics of uniform displacement distribution and load sharing, which was in line with the therapeutic needs of minimally invasive internal fixation, providing a new option for the individualized treatment of distal radius type C fractures.

A separate analysis of the displacement amplitudes of the internal fixation devices themselves was conducted, as shown in Fig. 4. Horizontal comparison showed that the FMB group was still insufficient in resisting palmar flexion and dorsiflexion, while the PS group was slightly inferior in rotation and palmar flexion compared with the other two conditions; vertical comparison of the two internal fixation materials showed that the PS group had a certain advantage, but the FMB group achieved such results with less material, which was quite remarkable.

Articular surface displacement

As shown in Fig. 5, the high-displacement areas of the articular surface in the PS group were concentrated at the dorsal and ulnar margins, with minimal displacement observed at the volar articular surface. This is consistent with the mechanical transmission law of eccentric fixation with a volar plate, indicating a risk of micromotion at the dorsal articular surface. In the FMB group, the displacement of the articular surface was uniformly distributed across the entire articular surface without obvious localized high-displacement areas, which reflects the “load sharing” characteristic of the truss configuration. This feature can prevent excessive collapse of the local articular surface and provide a more uniform mechanical environment for the repair of articular cartilage.

Discussion

The finite element model of this study was qualitatively verified by literature parameter calibration and biomechanical trend comparison. The model construction and simulation process were in line with the norms of orthopaedic finite element research, and the simulation results had reliable clinical reference value.

As a complex intra-articular fracture involving the articular surface, the core clinical treatment goal of distal radius type C fractures is to achieve accurate reduction of the articular surface, provide stable fixation to promote fracture healing, and minimize complications and preserve wrist joint function. At present, the mainstream clinical volar approach locking plate-screw fixation can achieve rigid fixation, but has the problems of large incision, need to dissect the pronator teres, and easy stress concentration caused by eccentric fixation; minimally invasive internal fixation technology has become an important research direction for the treatment of such fractures due to its advantages of less trauma and fast recovery. This study compared the biomechanical performance of the Force-Multiplying Bridge screw fixation system derived from truss units with the conventional plate-screw fixation system through finite element analysis, providing an objective biomechanical basis for the individualized treatment of distal radius type C fractures.

The Force-Multiplying Bridge is often used in temporary emergency or rapid erection scenarios in the fields of bridge engineering and structural mechanics. After the structure is formed, it can bear a load several times that of its own material20, so it is theoretically reasonable to apply this structure to fracture internal fixation. Due to the unevenness of the articular surface compared with the flatness of the bridge deck, the entire upper support surface of the Force-Multiplying Bridge was designed into a slope shape to fit the slope-shaped articular surface of the radius in this experiment, as shown in Fig. 1c1. This experiment was also fully verified: performance comparable to conventional plate internal fixation was obtained with very little material.

The displacement analysis of the internal fixation devices themselves further supplemented the mechanical differences between the two internal fixation systems: the displacement of the plate in the PS group was concentrated at the distal screw holes and the corresponding area of the fracture end, reflecting the characteristic of “overall load-bearing and local concentration”, which also explained the potential risk of distal screw fracture and plate fatigue of the locking plate in clinical practice—local displacement concentration is easy to cause stress concentration at the screw-bone interface21, which is prone to complications such as fixation loosening under long-term mechanical load; the displacement of the truss screws in the FMB group was evenly distributed on each screw with no obvious high-displacement concentration areas, which fully reflected the advantage of “load sharing” of the truss structure, can effectively reduce the mechanical load of a single screw, reduce the risk of screw fatigue failure, and at the same time, its configuration without an integral plate provides a structural basis for more minimally invasive surgical operations, conforming to the research direction of minimally invasive surgery in modern medicine.

The displacement characteristics of the articular surface are directly related to the quality of fracture healing and the recovery of wrist joint function, and are key evaluation indicators for the treatment of distal radius type C fractures. This study found that the high-displacement areas of the articular surface in the PS group were concentrated at the dorsal and ulnar margins, indicating that the mechanical transmission characteristics of volar plate eccentric fixation may lead to the risk of micromotion at the dorsal articular surface. Long-term micromotion may affect the repair of articular surface cartilage and increase the risk of post-traumatic arthritis; the displacement of the articular surface in the FMB group was evenly distributed on the entire articular surface with no obvious local high-displacement areas. This characteristic is closely related to the load sharing advantage of the Force-Multiplying Bridge structure, which can avoid excessive collapse of the local articular surface, provide a more uniform mechanical environment for the repair of articular surface cartilage, be more in line with the physiological mechanical conditions required for fracture healing, and provide biomechanical support for reducing long-term complications.

Conclusions

With an ultra-simplified design using only 6 screws, the Force-Multiplying Bridge structural internal fixation system achieves optimized load distribution through a triangular truss configuration, reaching the overall stability comparable to that of the conventional plate-screw system. This innovative design provides a simpler internal fixation configuration for the internal fixation treatment of complex intra-articular fractures of the distal radius, thus laying a theoretical foundation for more minimally invasive surgical methods.

In summary, the plate-screw fixation system has higher stiffness under axial, palmar flexion and dorsiflexion conditions, and is suitable for patients with distal radius type C fractures who require rigid fixation and early weight-bearing; the Force-Multiplying Bridge screw fixation system has the advantages of minimal invasiveness, load sharing and excellent stability under rotational conditions, and is suitable for patients pursuing minimally invasive treatment and requiring early rotational functional exercise. This study provides a new idea for the interdisciplinary design of engineering structural science, and also provides an experimental basis for the further development of minimally invasive internal fixation technology for distal radius fractures.
