“Being seen, heard, witnessed and acknowledged”: a reflexive thematic analysis of peer support following termination for medical reasons among women in Great Britain
Authors: Heather Robinson, Ellie Vowles, Paul Marshall, Fiona Lobban, Áine Aventin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTermination for medical reasons (TFMR) is associated with profound distress, grief and emotional trauma, as well as stigma and isolation. While peer support has been reported to reduce isolation and validate emotions in broader contexts, the distinct ethical and emotional complexities surrounding TFMR mean these findings cannot be assumed to transfer directly. TFMR‑specific evidence is therefore needed.AimTo explore women’s perspectives on peer support following termination for medical reasons (TFMR) in order to make policy and practice recommendations to improve support.MethodsThis study was a reflexive thematic analysis, conducted using qualitative data from 6 facilitated online focus groups with 28 women in total, aged 18 or older who had experienced TFMR in Great Britain within the past 10 years.ResultsFive key themes were developed: 1) Blindsided and isolated with a silenced grief; 2) They just “get it” - the unique value of TFMR-specific peer support; 3) Navigating the abyss: support that hinges on luck; 4) Shared journey, differing and evolving support needs; and 5) The other edge of the sword: when others’ stories are heavy.ConclusionsTFMR is a profoundly distressing experience, and women’s accounts highlight significant gaps in the availability and consistency of support. Peer support represents a potentially effective and low‑cost intervention, but access is currently dependent on chance rather than a clear pathway. Strengthening collaboration between statutory and voluntary sectors could enable the development of varied, TFMR‑specific peer support options that reflect the differing needs of those affected. Further research is needed to understand how peer support is experienced across diverse groups and how it can be tailored to ensure equitable, sensitive, and sustainable provision.

Background

The loss of a baby during pregnancy is a distressing event for women and their families [1]. For some expectant parents, their baby loss follows a diagnosis of a fetal anomaly, sometimes incompatible with life, or posing risks to the mother’s health, leading to the often heart wrenching decision to end their pregnancy [2]. Also referred to as termination for medical reasons (TFMR), this experience affects 4.6 per 1,000 births across Europe [3] and is associated with extreme distress, grief and emotional trauma [4–9] as well as stigma and isolation [10–11]. Women undergoing TFMR may receive medical management, typically using medication to induce labour, or surgical management to remove pregnancy tissue, under anaesthetic. For later-gestation TFMR, an injection is given to ensure no signs of life at birth [12]. Evidence suggests that women who have experienced TFMR require tailored, specific support, which is currently not widely available, and often find support aimed at other types of baby loss inappropriate [13]. For this reason, women who experience TFMR can often feel isolated, and may struggle to access effective support.

In statutory services, care pathways vary internationally reflecting differences in laws, services and attitudes towards TFMR. In the UK, TFMR support sits between maternity, bereavement and mental health care and is guided by the NHS Bereavement Care Pathway and NHS Bereavement Care Standards (Sands & Department of Health and Social Care, 2020). Current practice typically includes access to bereavement midwives and signposting or referral to other NHS (such as specific bereavement support or trauma services) or third sector services for peer support. Bereavement midwives can talk women through support options when they are not in a position to decide what to access themselves or feel anxious about engaging in support. Yet, despite these structured care pathways, many parents report gaps in continued care and emotional validation following TFMR [13].

Peer support involves connecting with others who have shared lived experience experience either in a formal setting (such as NHS or third sector facilitated groups), or informally (such as through privately organised online groups). Peer support has been reported to reduce feelings of isolation and provide emotional validation in broader pregnancy loss research [14]. However, the distinct ethical and emotional complexities that surround TFMR mean that findings from studies examining broader loss may not transfer directly, underscoring the need for TFMR-specific research in this area. TFMR is a particularly stigmatised type of loss, where peer support has been highlighted as a key need to allow people to talk openly about their experiences, in a safe space, where they feel understood [15]. TFMR-specific peer support is now offered by a range of voluntary sector organisations in the UK (e.g. Antenatal Results and Choices (ARC), TFMR Mamas, Stillbirth and Neonatal Death Society (SANDS), The Worst Girl Gang) and other high-income countries, but as far as we are aware, there is currently no research into women’s experiences and perceptions of this type of support following TFMR.

This paper makes a timely and important addition to the existing literature by reporting women’s perceptions of peer support following TFMR. Given that TFMR is well established as a highly distressing experience requiring tailored support, and that peer support, though increasingly available, remains under‑examined in this context, this paper addresses a critical gap. The findings offer an evidence base to inform policy and practice recommendations designed to ensure that support meaningfully meets the needs of women affected by TFMR through the sharing of lived experience.

Aim

The aim of this paper is to explore women’s perspectives on peer support following termination for medical reasons (TFMR) in order to make policy and practice recommendations to improve support.

Methodology and methods

Ethics approval and key considerations

This project was reviewed and approved by Lancaster University Faculty of Health and Medicine Research Ethics Committee (FHMREC; FHM-2024-4773-RECR-1). Data were collected in accordance with General Data Protection Regulations [16].

It is important to balance the ethics of managing an inherently distressing topic, with the ethical implications of not conducting research in this area where it is very much needed. Careful consideration was given to the potential for participant distress and strategies for mitigating this. Participants were prepared for the sensitive nature of the topics covered through the participant information sheet (PIS) and a verbal briefing before each group. They were also offered a Teams meeting prior to groups; provided with resources to contact in the event of distress (in the PIS) and informed of focus group ground rules via email and verbally prior to focus groups. These included expectations for open and respectful communication and for maintaining the confidentiality of group discussions.

We explicitly acknowledged the potential for distress at the start of each group, emphasising that distress could arise for researchers or participants not because anyone was upsetting anyone else, but that the topics discussed are inherently upsetting. Our intention was not to supress emotions that may come up, but to protect participants and researchers from extreme distress that could result in serious risk to self or others. To aid transparency and increase feelings of psychological safety within the group, HR acknowledged her lived experience at the beginning of the focus group. A screen break was incorporated into the session for the comfort of participants, and a second facilitator looked out for signs of participant distress with the option to speak to individuals separately from the main group if needed.

To support HR in leading the focus groups, a second facilitator was present at all sessions. FL, a clinical psychologist by background, was available to provide additional support to facilitators as required. After each group, facilitators had a debrief session and ensured protected time for self-care before continuing with further tasks. HR had access pre-established self-care strategies and private psychological support as needed. All facilitators also had access to the Division of Health Research Mental Health and Well-being Officer, if required.

Design and setting

This is a qualitative study using reflexive thematic analysis of qualitative data from online focus groups with women aged 18 or above who had experienced TFMR in Great Britain (GB; England, Scotland and Wales) within the last 10 years. Time since participants’ TFMR was limited to promote accuracy of recall of relevant experiences. The decision to focus on GB, rather than the UK, was based on the change in laws permitting TFMR in Northern Ireland being relatively recent [17], which may impact participants’ perceptions of, and access to, peer support. To be as inclusive as possible, we did not impose a minimum amount of time between TFMR and study participation, we worked under the assumption that if someone volunteered to participate and give fully informed consent then they felt ready and able to talk about their experiences.

Focus groups were conducted online and facilitated by two researchers, one of whom has lived experience of TFMR. Focus groups were chosen due to the sensitive and stigmatised nature of TFMR. The hope was that participants may feel more able to speak openly in a group where others have experienced something similar and they can respond to each other offering insights that may not be captured through individual interviews.

Philosophical perspective

Adopting a critical realist ontology alongside a relativist epistemology allowed us to treat the phenomenon under investigation - women’s perceptions and experiences of peer support following TFMR - as real while recognising that our knowledge of the phenomena is always interpretive. This pairing provides a coherent route to impact and a strong philosophical fit for reflexive thematic analysis [18].

Positionality statement

HR is a female Lecturer in Mental Health with substantial research expertise in peer support for mental health challenges and lived experience of tfmr. ÁA, is a female Senior Lecturer in Sexual and Reproductive Health with long-standing professional and personal experience supporting families bereaved through pregnancy loss. FL is a Professor of Clinical Psychology with specialist knowledge in peer support and extensive experience working with sensitive topics and vulnerable groups. PM is a male researcher who has conducted extensive research into experiences of peer support including for early pregnancy loss and mental health difficulties. EV is a female doctoral student with qualitative and digital design expertise. Together, these differing positions and areas of expertise enriched our engagement with participants and enhanced analytic depth.

Participant recruitment and Patient and Public Involvement (PPI)

We used purposive sampling to recruit participants through social media and relevant third sector organisations, as well as by word of mouth. Recruitment materials were designed by the first author and Principal Investigator (HR), who has lived experience of TFMR. Three staff members from ARC with over 40 years’ experience in supporting people following TFMR reviewed the study participant-facing material and provided feedback on language and processes. Specifically, they commented on the study advert, PIS, recruitment email, consent and registration, and debrief. Recruitment materials were promoted through third sector organisations’ social media accounts and contact lists. A project-specific email was set up to which participants sent expressions of interest and were then contacted with further information by the first author (HR) or research assistant (EV). We received 65 expressions of interest, which resulted in the PIS being sent out. Thirty-nine women subsequently registered for a focus group. We did not collect data on why 11 women chose not to take part after registering. Prior to participation, we encouraged people to reflect on how they would feel talking about their experiences and emphasised that participation was entirely voluntary, and they could withdraw at any point. The fact that these women withdrew demonstrates that they felt able to do so. Written informed consent was provided by all participants in the final sample via the survey platform Qualtrics following reading a written information sheet and prior to data collection.

Data generation

Demographic and obstetric data were collected via Qualtrics prior to the focus groups. Six focus groups were held consisting of 3–7 participants in each. Group membership was determined by participants’ preferences for date and time of groups. Groups were facilitated by the first author (HR) and one other co-author (FL, PM or ÁA), conducted on Microsoft Teams, and recorded with captions on to capture the transcription. Participants were given the option to have their camera on or off, though we encouraged those that felt comfortable to have their camera on as this can aid cohesion of the group and can help with identification of participant distress. All participants chose to leave their cameras on. Groups lasted approximately 1.5–2 hours. To aid transparency and increase feelings of psychological safety within the group, the first author (HR) acknowledged her lived experience at the beginning of each group and emphasised that the focus was on the experience of group members. Participant discussion was prompted using a topic guide developed by the research team, based on the study research aims (see Table 1 below). As an acknowledgement of their time and contribution, participants were offered a gift voucher of £30.

Data analysis

Transcripts from the recordings were cleaned and anonymised by an external professional transcriber, who signed a confidentiality agreement before accessing the data.

Data were analysed by the first author (HR) in NVivo 14 [19], following guidance for reflexive thematic analysis (RTA; [20–22]). RTA was deemed suitable for this study because it positions the researcher as an active influence on the analytic process. This aligns with the first author’s lived experience, which is seen as an analytic asset supporting a more nuanced, sensitive interpretation of the data, rather than a source of bias.

HR began by familiarising herself with the data through multiple readings of anonymised transcripts, noting thoughts, impressions and areas of analytic interest within the data. Coding was conducted inductively, with coding labels applied to segments of data to capture meaning. HR then generated initial themes by identifying patterns across codes and their relation to the dataset. Themes were iteratively developed, reviewed and refined in relation to both the coded data and the dataset, before being named in consultation with co‑authors.

Rigour and trustworthiness

Steps taken to promote rigour and trustworthiness were intentionally aligned with our ontological and epistemological commitments [22]. Maintaining a reflexive diary strengthened confirmability and supported ongoing consideration of how positionality shaped perception and interpretation throughout the study. To enhance credibility and dependability and deepen reflection, co author ÁA independently coded and documented preliminary themes and interpretive insights from an early focus group transcript, which were then compared and collaboratively discussed with HR. Research team debriefing further enabled us to draw on the team’s diverse personal and professional perspectives, supporting more nuanced meaning making and enhancing analytic transparency. Rigour was additionally strengthened through iterative thematic development, involving repeated, in-depth engagement with the data and regular cycles of written and verbal feedback across the research team. The transferability of findings is supported by detailed descriptions of study setting, participants, researcher positionality and methodologies.

Reflexivity

Reflexive practice was consistently undertaken by the first author (HR) throughout the study to ensure that her lived experience of TFMR informed, but did not dominate, the analytic process: 1) HR kept a reflective journal to document emotional responses, assumptions, and points of resonance with participants’ accounts. This provided a space to recognise where her own experiences might shape interpretation and to bring these reflections to discussion with the wider team; 2) Immediately after each focus group a reflexive session took place in which HR and the group co-facilitator (FL, PM or ÁA) reflected on what was discussed and feelings that had arisen. Moments where HR felt particularly connected or disconnected from participants narratives were discussed, helping to distinguish her own experiences from the meanings participants attributed to theirs; 3) HR and co-author PM had regular discussions to review, challenge and refine aspects of the developing analysis as well as consider how HR’s positionality was shaping theme development; and 4) All authors met during data analysis to discuss progress, issues, and methodological decisions.

Author reflections

We anticipated that recruitment could be a challenge, given the sensitive nature of the topic and emotional demands of taking part in focus groups. However, recruitment was fast and relatively easy, reflecting participants’ desires to talk about their experiences. The majority (n = 26) had accessed peer support, though the perspectives of two participants who had not, were also included.

Before data collection, we were mindful that participants may be hesitant to openly discuss their experiences due to the stigmatised nature of TFMR, however, in practice, the level of participant engagement within all groups was high – participants engaged in articulate, reflective accounts of their views and experiences, generating rich, in-depth data. Openness within groups was likely facilitated by the first author’s initial declaration of lived experience, a decision which required careful consideration when planning the study to balance transparency with the need to focus on participant’s experiences rather than those of the first author. Some women became tearful and were offered a break to allow space for these natural emotions. No participants experienced extreme distress.

Group facilitators responded to the high engagement of participants by allowing conversations to flow naturally between them and only intervening to prompt discussion in line with topic guide or to invite participants to speak when necessary, allowing participants to shape the direction and depth of conversations. Participants spoke openly and expressed gratitude for the opportunity to talk about their TFMR, their loss and their babies, reflecting the therapeutic benefit of taking part in the focus groups, despite this not being an aim of the study.

Some of the things that participants shared within the groups was difficult to witness, and may be difficult to read, but is of profound importance to acknowledge and to work to improve the experiences of women in the future.

Participant Demographics

All women had a termination due to fetal anomaly diagnosis (n = 28), with the most common timepoint being 21–24 weeks gestation (n = 11). This aligns with when many congenital anomalies are first identified during routine fetal anomaly screening at 18–22 weeks’ gestation [23]. Most women were 1–5 years post‑TFMR (n = 16) and over half of the sample had other loss experiences (n = 17), including previous TFMR (n = 3), highlighting the prevalence and recurrent nature of pregnancy loss.

Participants’ narratives provided insight into the types of peer support they had engaged with. Women described accessing a wide range of support, from informal activities such as reading others’ stories on social media, listening to podcasts, and participating in online forums or group chats, to more formal options including online or face-to face support groups facilitated by third sector organisations or the NHS. Engagement varied considerably across participants, with some connecting one-to-one with peers and others taking part in group based support, and with differing levels of frequency and involvement.

Thematic overview

Five key themes were developed: 1) Blindsided and isolated with a silenced grief; 2) They just “get it” - the unique value of TFMR-specific peer support; 3) Navigating the abyss: support that hinges on luck; 4) Shared journey, differing and evolving support needs; and 5) The other edge of the sword: when others’ stories are heavy.

Figure 1 presents a conceptual model illustrating the interplay between themes. The analysis begins by outlining participants’ motivations for seeking TFMR‑specific peer support, rooted in feeling blindsided, stigmatsied, misunderstood, unsupported and isolated (Theme 1). It then highlights the unique value of TFMR‑specific spaces, where participants feel validated, experience freedom to share their experience and supported by others whose experiences are relatable (Theme 2). However, access to peer support often hinges on luck – where participants live (postcode) and whether they have an engaged bereavement midwife - shaping the extent to which they are left self‑searching while struggling, relying on whatever resources they are able to find (Theme 3). Once engaged, participants have differing and evolving support needs across the TFMR journey; with strong preferences for support after diagnosis and differing needs after loss including flexible, ongoing signposting, and enduring peer friendships (Theme 4). Finally, the analysis highlights the other edge of the sword within peer support – the emotional weight of others’ stories which can make joining/staying in peer spaces difficult (Theme 5).

Theme 1: Blindsided and isolated with a silenced grief

Blindsided

Participants described feeling completely “blindsided” (FG 1) and emotionally unprepared when TFMR was raised as a potential path for their pregnancy; news which “came like a ton of bricks” (FG 1) sending participants “reeling” (FG 1). This metaphor conveys not only the suddenness of the conversation around TFMR but also the crushing emotional force with which it landed. Participants had not heard of TFMR or paid little attention to it as they never believed it would happen to them:

“I lived in ignorant bliss before our 20 week scan so I had no idea any of this stuff went on until I was living it.” (FG 4).

“I lived in ignorant bliss before our 20 week scan so I had no idea any of this stuff went on until I was living it.” (FG 4).

Silence and stigma

Participants reported never having met anyone who had been through a TFMR, which further contributed to feelings of “isolation and feeling totally alone” (FG 4) like they were “the only person this had ever happened to” (FG 2). This may reflect a genuine lack of contact with others who had experienced TFMR or may be because TFMR is not discussed as openly as other forms of loss because “It’s not part of the dialogue around pregnancy, it’s not part of an ecology of conversation that anybody has in this country” (FG 1), suggesting that others with similar experiences may choose not to disclose them.

The lack of open conversation about TFMR – both in terms of participants sharing their experience and within wider society - was attributed to TFMR being seen as “controversial” (FG 4), “the biggest taboo on the planet” (FG 3) and surrounded by “stigma” (FG 5) due to the “decision” that was made:

“…almost the darkest form of it is when you have to make that decision yourself. And I think because of a lot of the religious confusion and beliefs around that… people don’t want to talk about it.” (FG 1).

“…almost the darkest form of it is when you have to make that decision yourself. And I think because of a lot of the religious confusion and beliefs around that… people don’t want to talk about it.” (FG 1).

For a participant, this stigma was self-perceived, preventing her from talking about her TFMR and seeking support, thus prolonging her sense of isolation:

“I’m in that like sort of isolated…because I’d made that decision, …I didn’t feel like mine [loss] was valid…still to this day, like six years down the line, I don’t feel like I’ve actually spoken properly and like resonated with anybody.” (FG 4).

“I’m in that like sort of isolated…because I’d made that decision, …I didn’t feel like mine [loss] was valid…still to this day, like six years down the line, I don’t feel like I’ve actually spoken properly and like resonated with anybody.” (FG 4).

However, participants who were further on from their loss acknowledged that TFMR is becoming more visible through the increase in number and size of charities that support TFMR and the rise in online conversations on the subject that “definitely weren’t around when [they were] in the trenches” (FG 4). The language used by this participant frames TFMR as a battle endured with little external support, highlighting the profound isolation that shaped this participant’s experience.

The weight of others

Participants felt anxious about disclosing their TFMR outside the TFMR community due to the unpredictability of others’ responses “because you don’t know your audience until you’ve told them” (FG 1). There was a risk of being judged, and participants felt “terrified” (FG 2) of this:

“you’re taking a risk with every single part of what you’re disclosing at every time.” (FG 1).

“you’re taking a risk with every single part of what you’re disclosing at every time.” (FG 1).

Deciding whether to disclose TFMR was cognitively challenging and psychologically burdensome. Participants described having to weigh up the likely response from others, their mental capacity to deal with that (unknown) response, and the repercussions of not disclosing the truth – increased isolation, limited support and guilt:

“it’s that torment of, like, “What do I do like? I don’t have the energy, but I want people to know the truth so they can help me, but I don’t have the energy to do that” (FG 3).

“it’s that torment of, like, “What do I do like? I don’t have the energy, but I want people to know the truth so they can help me, but I don’t have the energy to do that” (FG 3).

Not only did participants fear judgement about the decision they had made, but they also worried about distressing others by disclosing their TFMR, and having to manage others’ distress on top of their own:

“when people hear what decision we made, they can’t help thinking, “What would I do? What would I have done in that situation?” and then you’re taking them on a journey that they may find very, very uncomfortable” (FG 1).

“when people hear what decision we made, they can’t help thinking, “What would I do? What would I have done in that situation?” and then you’re taking them on a journey that they may find very, very uncomfortable” (FG 1).

Concerns around disclosure perpetuated feelings of isolation, as participants who stayed silent about the reality of their experiences were not connected with peer support:

“it’s only through being open and honest with other people that I’ve found other people that have experienced similar.” (FG 3).

“it’s only through being open and honest with other people that I’ve found other people that have experienced similar.” (FG 3).

Others don’t understand and can’t support you

Feelings of isolation were further compounded by a broad lack of understanding from others; people may not talk about TFMR because they do not understand it, equally in not talking about TFMR their lack of understanding is reinforced:

“The problem with TFMR is that if people don’t understand it, it can become a deniable loss because they can hide behind the fact that they don’t know what it really means, and therefore, if you don’t know what it means, it means it didn’t really happen” (FG 1).

“The problem with TFMR is that if people don’t understand it, it can become a deniable loss because they can hide behind the fact that they don’t know what it really means, and therefore, if you don’t know what it means, it means it didn’t really happen” (FG 1).

This invalidates grief and therefore the possibility of a socially recognised bereavement. Unfortunately, this was an idea echoed by some professionals:

“my GP didn’t actually know what a TFMR was… he just wrote on my notes “miscarriage”” (FG 2).

“my GP didn’t actually know what a TFMR was… he just wrote on my notes “miscarriage”” (FG 2).

Acknowledging the reality of TFMR was important to participants. They described how the clinical language of “termination” obscures the lived experience of labour, birth, and loss, and takes the idiosyncrasies, compassion and emotion out of the experience. This sanitisation of an experience felt so viscerally compounds misunderstanding and isolation:

“I don’t think they realised that I gave birth to my daughter and I held her while she was alive and I watched her pass away in my arms, and they dressed her in a little outfit and put her in a crib… I don’t think anyone realises that: because they say termination, you don’t imagine a little baby, you just imagine bleeding.” (FG 1).

“I don’t think they realised that I gave birth to my daughter and I held her while she was alive and I watched her pass away in my arms, and they dressed her in a little outfit and put her in a crib… I don’t think anyone realises that: because they say termination, you don’t imagine a little baby, you just imagine bleeding.” (FG 1).

Participants acknowledged that although their partners had also lost their baby and shared some of the same distress, the father’s experience of TFMR was very different from their own. As a result, participants felt that their partners could not fully understand the deeper emotional and physical intricacies of the experience or its aftermath from their perspective and wanted to connect with other mothers:

“we have experienced this differently. I say we’ve both experienced the same grief journey, but we’re going down the river in different vessels, and I can’t understand everything he’s going through and he can’t understand everything I’m going through.” (FG 3).

“we have experienced this differently. I say we’ve both experienced the same grief journey, but we’re going down the river in different vessels, and I can’t understand everything he’s going through and he can’t understand everything I’m going through.” (FG 3).

Emotional support that was offered by family and friends was often experienced as well-meaning but unhelpful. Not acknowledging one’s painful situation or offering “toxic positivity”(FG 1), for example “look for the silver linings and everything happens for a reason and variations thereof” (FG 1), were experienced as a form of bypassing the lived reality of participants’ experiences and thus invalidated their feelings:

“the less you say, the sooner you’ll mend…like the less you talk about it, the less likely you are to think about it and we can all have a nice dinner, you know? And I think that’s very, invalidating because it totally goes against your sense of reality, then you feel like, “God, did I imagine this whole thing?” …and that’s completely disorientating.” (FG 1).

“the less you say, the sooner you’ll mend…like the less you talk about it, the less likely you are to think about it and we can all have a nice dinner, you know? And I think that’s very, invalidating because it totally goes against your sense of reality, then you feel like, “God, did I imagine this whole thing?” …and that’s completely disorientating.” (FG 1).

Participants described family and friends as not wanting to understand their experience, being “unwilling to sit with the discomfort of it” (FG 1) and unable to provide the support they needed. Perhaps because family and friends did not want, or need, to know the reality of TFMR, whereas these participants “had to learn all this: [they] didn’t have a choice” (FG 1). Or perhaps because it is“hard for them [family and friends]” (FG 2) to engage and provide emotional support following loss because they are “emotionally attached to you [the mother]” (FG 2). The lack of emotional support from one’s usual social networks is critical and reinforces feelings of isolation within them:

“when I’m with my family, who maybe haven’t had such a traumatic event happen to them, I frequently feel like…the ghost at the feast or something…sat in the corner with a gloomy face on …because I felt so different to them.” (FG 1).

“when I’m with my family, who maybe haven’t had such a traumatic event happen to them, I frequently feel like…the ghost at the feast or something…sat in the corner with a gloomy face on …because I felt so different to them.” (FG 1).

Out of place in broader loss spaces

Participants described being isolated from broader loss spaces, noting that TFMR was not supported by all baby loss charities and, in some cases, was excluded. Several participants did not feel they fit into broader loss spaces because of the decision, feeling uncomfortable talking about the decision in spaces where people had experienced loss that was not their “choice”. One participant felt they were not deserving of that space because they made a choice that others did not have:

“I had chosen this for my baby and I didn’t deserve to be in that group, because how could I relate to people? I felt like how could I stand there and say, “I’ve lost my baby,” to people who had no choice over it and I had a choice” (FG 3).

“I had chosen this for my baby and I didn’t deserve to be in that group, because how could I relate to people? I felt like how could I stand there and say, “I’ve lost my baby,” to people who had no choice over it and I had a choice” (FG 3).

Participants feared judgement in these spaces, were hesitant to disclose their full story not knowing how others would react, and that their experience was invalidated. Broader loss spaces also exposed them to other ways pregnancy could go wrong in the future, increasing anxiety and reducing hope:

“I think especially when it’s not specific to TFMR, I think it does just give you a lot more to worry about, because you’re thrust into this baby loss community, and all of a sudden you know every possible way a pregnancy can go wrong.” (FG 2).

“I think especially when it’s not specific to TFMR, I think it does just give you a lot more to worry about, because you’re thrust into this baby loss community, and all of a sudden you know every possible way a pregnancy can go wrong.” (FG 2).

There was an acknowledgment that nobody was specifically doing anything unsupportive within the groups, but that this was how participants felt, perhaps reflecting internalised stigma. Interestingly, knowing someone else in the group had experienced TFMR increased feelings of safety, highlighting the value of TFMR-specific peer connection for this group:

“my bereavement midwife told me that the [charity] coordinator had had a TFMR, and I think if I didn’t know that going into it, I wouldn’t have gone to the meeting, but I knew that there was like an ally there already.” (FG 2).

“my bereavement midwife told me that the [charity] coordinator had had a TFMR, and I think if I didn’t know that going into it, I wouldn’t have gone to the meeting, but I knew that there was like an ally there already.” (FG 2).

Theme 2: They just “get it” - the unique value of TFMR-specific peer support

Validation

In TFMR-specific peer support spaces participants anticipated a level of understanding and validation due to shared lived experience, alleviating anxiety associated with deciding whether to share the details and reality of one’s experience:

“peer support sort of alleviated that anxiety about other people’s responses, and it’s not just like other people’s judgement, it’s…other people’s grief, other people’s sadness…other people panicking about what to say” (FG 4).

“peer support sort of alleviated that anxiety about other people’s responses, and it’s not just like other people’s judgement, it’s…other people’s grief, other people’s sadness…other people panicking about what to say” (FG 4).

Peer spaces offered “solidarity in just how rubbish it was” (FG 2), normalising emotions that participants often felt were inappropriate or unacceptable. Having their feelings validated was a powerful and essential aspect of peer support-something participants felt could only come from those who understood their reality on a visceral level:

“within a peer-to-peer framework, I think you’ve got the chances for being seen, heard, witnessed and acknowledged and not dismissed. And that validation is completely- it’s invaluable” (FG 1).

“within a peer-to-peer framework, I think you’ve got the chances for being seen, heard, witnessed and acknowledged and not dismissed. And that validation is completely- it’s invaluable” (FG 1).

Freedom

Participants described the freedom they felt in these spaces - speaking openly, without minimising, sanitising, or protecting others, bypassing the additional cognitive burden of weighting up others’ potential reactions. Participants felt free to express thoughts they would censor elsewhere, particularly the “ugly” (FG 6) emotions they felt and the real, detailed experience of TFMR. Participants also felt free to ask uncensored questions to an audience they implicitly trusted, without fear of judgement:

“… the horrific thoughts that had gone through my head, that I now know lots of people have, like wishing for a miscarriage, that at the time I hated myself for thinking that, but I prayed, literally, was praying to God that somebody would take this decision out of my hands… And I think being able to talk to somebody who got it was absolutely vital.” (FG 2).

“… the horrific thoughts that had gone through my head, that I now know lots of people have, like wishing for a miscarriage, that at the time I hated myself for thinking that, but I prayed, literally, was praying to God that somebody would take this decision out of my hands… And I think being able to talk to somebody who got it was absolutely vital.” (FG 2).

Feeling free and supported to talk about their baby was an important aspect of peer support for participants. Talking to others outside the TFMR community about their babies felt uncomfortable because “they forget” (FG 3) - “No one says his name” (FG 1). This was painful because it was important to participants that their babies were remembered, and peer support gives participants a space where they are:

“I think that’s something that that TFMR community gives as well; it’s like a chance to actually speak about your baby.” (FG 3).

“I think that’s something that that TFMR community gives as well; it’s like a chance to actually speak about your baby.” (FG 3).

Relatability

Participants described experiencing a strong sense of relatability and shared understanding with peers who had also experienced TFMR. These peers were seen as uniquely able to understand their emotions and experiences and offer a level of empathy that felt unavailable elsewhere. This shared understanding didn’t always need to be spoken aloud-participants described an immediate, instinctive sense of comfort with others who “get it” (FG 6), even in silence:

“And the comfort I found in speaking to other women who got it…until you’ve physically been through it, no one else gets it…I could be on Teams with these other mums, who I didn’t have to speak to them necessarily, but I just knew they would get it…You don’t necessarily need anything more than that”. (FG 2)

“And the comfort I found in speaking to other women who got it…until you’ve physically been through it, no one else gets it…I could be on Teams with these other mums, who I didn’t have to speak to them necessarily, but I just knew they would get it…You don’t necessarily need anything more than that”. (FG 2)

Knowing that others had experienced similar emotions and situations, and made the same decision to TFMR, was an important part of feeling comfortable and finding a sense of relief and community in peer spaces: “you felt at home” (FG 4). Participants did not feel the need to explain or justify their emotions or experiences, and felt unlikely to be judged for their decision, because everyone had made the same choice:

“if you meet someone who’s been through a TFMR, you know that when you talk about it… they’ve had to go through that same sort of decision-making process” (FG 5).

“if you meet someone who’s been through a TFMR, you know that when you talk about it… they’ve had to go through that same sort of decision-making process” (FG 5).

Despite the unique and valuable support peers could offer, participants noted that shared lived experience alone was not always enough – there needed to be a genuine human connection too:

“even if you’ve both been through the same experience, there might be situations where you just don’t gel with somebody.” (FG 4).

“even if you’ve both been through the same experience, there might be situations where you just don’t gel with somebody.” (FG 4).

Although other mothers had potentially similar physical and emotional experiences, there was acknowledgement that the TFMR experience was “very, very different for everybody” (FG 6). This variation created different potential touchpoints for shared experience, and participants noted that having certain key aspects in common with a peer-those that felt personally important to them-made that peer more relatable and, in turn, made the peer relationship feel more supportive. The potential commonalities raised by participants were country of residence, fetal diagnosis, gestation at the time of TFMR, whether they had a living child, and the time since their TFMR:

“just talking to people who specifically have been through TFMR, especially for a condition that’s really similar to or the same as the diagnosis that I’ve had for my baby in the past, that was really helpful and just made you feel less like a total alien.” (FG 4).

“just talking to people who specifically have been through TFMR, especially for a condition that’s really similar to or the same as the diagnosis that I’ve had for my baby in the past, that was really helpful and just made you feel less like a total alien.” (FG 4).

Theme 3: Navigating the abyss: Support that hinges on luck

Support based on luck

Participants recognised that whether they found each other within “the abyss” (FG 3) was based on luck:“I think actually we’ve been quite lucky in the care that we got” (FG 3). Perceived luck was related to how engaged their bereavement midwife was and where they lived: “it feels very much hit and miss, depending on who your healthcare providers are and what your midwife is like” (FG 5), which determined whether they received guided support or were left to self‑search. For those left to navigate alone, the support they accessed depended entirely on what they happened to come across:

“hearing that very first few minutes where we all went round and said that none of us got any support to start with just broke my heart, that we all felt that lonely at that time, and we’re all here yet, you know, we’re all in the abyss, we’re all around. We could have supported each other, but none of us got that, so I’m sorry that none of you got all of that” (FG 3).

“hearing that very first few minutes where we all went round and said that none of us got any support to start with just broke my heart, that we all felt that lonely at that time, and we’re all here yet, you know, we’re all in the abyss, we’re all around. We could have supported each other, but none of us got that, so I’m sorry that none of you got all of that” (FG 3).

Bereavement midwives as gatekeepers to support

Bereavement midwives can play a key role in signposting and support. Participants described contrasting experiences when bereavement midwife involvement was present, absent, or discontinued. There was agreement that being given lots of written information at hospital was overwhelming and, without this active support to go through it, was not helpful:

“we weren’t…in the headspace to be looking through those booklets and be actively trying to find out what to do. But then I had…the check-up afterwards with my bereavement midwife, and she made it a lot easier….she’d gone through the groups and said, “This is the TFMR support group, this is a really good one,”…she was the most useful with signposting” (FG 2).

“we weren’t…in the headspace to be looking through those booklets and be actively trying to find out what to do. But then I had…the check-up afterwards with my bereavement midwife, and she made it a lot easier….she’d gone through the groups and said, “This is the TFMR support group, this is a really good one,”…she was the most useful with signposting” (FG 2).

The link between bereavement midwives and peer support spaces is important as there is little/no continuity of care from hospital once participants return home, so the hand-over to relevant health service providers or third sector organisations is crucial:

“there was…nothing aside from a bereavement midwife at the hospital, and then obviously, once you’ve delivered and dealing with that there’s nothing to be seen, they just disappear, don’t they?” (FG 6).

“there was…nothing aside from a bereavement midwife at the hospital, and then obviously, once you’ve delivered and dealing with that there’s nothing to be seen, they just disappear, don’t they?” (FG 6).

Those without bereavement midwife contact had little/no support from hospital, further emphasising the key role bereavement midwives play in participants’ care:

“I missed her call once, and that was the only support, the only opportunity that we had ever had. We didn’t have anything. Nobody else reached out to us, it was just that one missed call.” (FG 4).

“I missed her call once, and that was the only support, the only opportunity that we had ever had. We didn’t have anything. Nobody else reached out to us, it was just that one missed call.” (FG 4).

The postcode lottery of access to support

Participants highlighted that their experience of access to peer support was dependent on where they lived:

“it really is a lottery and depending on where you are, you either have everything or nothing, or very little.” (FG 5).

“it really is a lottery and depending on where you are, you either have everything or nothing, or very little.” (FG 5).

For some charities, the support they offered was constrained to the local area due to commissioning through local Integrated Care Boards (ICBs) or local authorities to work only within specific postcodes or primarily delivering face-to-face support making wider coverage impractical. This meant that for some participants certain charities were not available to them, either through hospital signposting or self-access, leaving them feeling frustrated, hopeless, and isolated:

“we got rejected by [charity] because they don’t operate with our hospital, and there just seems to be nowhere to turn to.” (FG 3).

“we got rejected by [charity] because they don’t operate with our hospital, and there just seems to be nowhere to turn to.” (FG 3).

Participants highlighted that there was lack of in-person support local to them and that this meant they missed out on face-to-face support. A participant specifically linked this to the increased effort to travel further at a time when she was already struggling:

“…recommended a [charity] group, but the nearest one was like Manchester … getting up every day was an effort at the time.” (FG 6).

“…recommended a [charity] group, but the nearest one was like Manchester … getting up every day was an effort at the time.” (FG 6).

Participants who had had their TFMR through the NHS noted that there was significant geographical variation in the information, signposting and support they received. Some participants felt well supported, while others received very little or no support, highlighting potential disparities in hospital resources and staff training to support participants following TFMR:

“everyone’s got such a different experience of being in the hospital or not having their support after the hospital.” (FG 2).

“everyone’s got such a different experience of being in the hospital or not having their support after the hospital.” (FG 2).

A participant who had their TFMR in a non-NHS facility got no support afterwards, amplifying isolation and the need to self-search for support:

“I kind of felt like that’s probably why my aftercare was sh*t, because I kind of left the NHS and they didn’t really- I had to chase everything.” (FG 6).

“I kind of felt like that’s probably why my aftercare was sh*t, because I kind of left the NHS and they didn’t really- I had to chase everything.” (FG 6).

Self-searching while struggling

Participants described “desperately” (FG 4) seeking TFMR-specific support themselves, often because they were not signposted to support from hospital or they were signposted to broader loss spaces. This involved “trawling the internet” (FG 5) at a time when they needed guidance the most and so was cognitively and emotionally draining:

“I was desperately searching, and then you end up in this Google wormhole that people tell you is dangerous and you shouldn’t do, but you’re literally just sort of wading against the tide, trying to find someone that can possibly understand what you’ve been through.” (FG 3).

“I was desperately searching, and then you end up in this Google wormhole that people tell you is dangerous and you shouldn’t do, but you’re literally just sort of wading against the tide, trying to find someone that can possibly understand what you’ve been through.” (FG 3).

Participants noted that they were not armed with the right information about terminology to search for TFMR support and some were not aware of the support offered by charities, highlighting the need for active signposting and support from hospital:

“You’re not given the right terminology in order to go and seek the support that you need.” (FG 1).

“You’re not given the right terminology in order to go and seek the support that you need.” (FG 1).

However, once participants found a TFMR-specific charity, or charity with a focus on TFMR, they were signposted to other useful charities, further emphasising the need for better, more supportive signposting to the peer support that is out there but is not always easy to find:

“…that was [charity], then they signposted to like [charity], that kind of came about when I was searching, and that one was great, because then that was highlighting [charity]” (FG 4).

“…that was [charity], then they signposted to like [charity], that kind of came about when I was searching, and that one was great, because then that was highlighting [charity]” (FG 4).

Theme 4: Shared journey, differing and evolving support needs

On their own terms:Peer support preferences after diagnosis

Some participants felt strongly that they did not want to connect with peers while they were deciding whether to have a TFMR or continue with the pregnancy, fearing outside influence on a decision that ultimately, they had to be able to live with, knowing it was right for them and their family:

“I was very protective of that time…I felt like I was on another planet, really, and I just wanted no sort of influence from anywhere…wanted to know myself that when I’d made that decision, it was truly what I’d decided, and not because of any other influence.” (FG 4).

“I was very protective of that time…I felt like I was on another planet, really, and I just wanted no sort of influence from anywhere…wanted to know myself that when I’d made that decision, it was truly what I’d decided, and not because of any other influence.” (FG 4).

At this stage, some participants did want to know that there was support out there that they could access further down the line, reducing feelings of isolation but protecting a time that felt very private and personal:

“between the diagnosis and the termination itself, [I] definitely went online and found all the resources that I spoke about earlier, I think that was in anticipation of a future me, and I needed to create like a little nest of potential peer support that I could then, you know, be depressed with afterwards” (FG 4).

“between the diagnosis and the termination itself, [I] definitely went online and found all the resources that I spoke about earlier, I think that was in anticipation of a future me, and I needed to create like a little nest of potential peer support that I could then, you know, be depressed with afterwards” (FG 4).

Others recognised that they may have benefitted from being signposted to peer support immediately after getting potentially bad news to reduce shock, process medical information and validate their decision to end their pregnancy:

“I think in hindsight what would have been like gold standard would have been at that point where we were told that we would have to make a decision on whether to carry on or to end the pregnancy, and we were obviously left then to go away and make that decision for a few days. I feel like then would have been like the best time to have introduced me to someone or something that had peer support…where you can speak to specifically a person who has experienced this.” (FG 3).

“I think in hindsight what would have been like gold standard would have been at that point where we were told that we would have to make a decision on whether to carry on or to end the pregnancy, and we were obviously left then to go away and make that decision for a few days. I feel like then would have been like the best time to have introduced me to someone or something that had peer support…where you can speak to specifically a person who has experienced this.” (FG 3).

However, other participants felt that having information at this stage would be unhelpful as it may not be accurate due to variability in hospital processes and care, and could cause unnecessary worry:

“you’d probably end up with an awful lot of information that wasn’t particularly accurate, and…potentially then worrying about something that you might not need to worry about because it might not be relevant in your area.” (FG 4).

“you’d probably end up with an awful lot of information that wasn’t particularly accurate, and…potentially then worrying about something that you might not need to worry about because it might not be relevant in your area.” (FG 4).

After the decision to have a TFMR, participants talked about a period of waiting in which they would have liked peer support to understand what would happen in hospital so that they felt prepared and knew what to expect:

“Nobody told me what to expect…they didn’t tell me there was…a chance my baby could have been born alive, and she was. And that was hard and that was horrible, and nobody explained that…peer support, that guidance, would have really made a difference for me.” (FG 5).

“Nobody told me what to expect…they didn’t tell me there was…a chance my baby could have been born alive, and she was. And that was hard and that was horrible, and nobody explained that…peer support, that guidance, would have really made a difference for me.” (FG 5).

This knowledge provided crucial foresight, which is uniquely important in TFMR compared to other types of baby loss. Following diagnosis and the ‘decision’ to TFMR, participants had “no idea what’s coming” (FG 4), they were not equipped with information about what the experience would be like — physically or emotionally — due to the wider societal silence surrounding TFMR and lack of experience-based understanding from those around them. Peers were uniquely placed to provide information and support on the “unknown unknowns” of the TFMR experience - those aspects of pregnancy, birth, loss, and beyond that participants could not anticipate or even know to ask about: “you don’t know what you don’t know until you get into a situation where you never know to ask a question” (FG 3). Peers have already navigated this journey themselves, and so can highlight the practical and emotional realities that remain unknown until they are lived, pre-normalising the significant psychological impact of TFMR and prompting preparedness:

“if we’d have had someone who’d…told us how big the emotions are going to be, it might not have been as terrifying to have those… really horrible big emotions in the first few weeks. Because all of that… hit us out of the blue…we weren’t expecting it” (FG 5).

“if we’d have had someone who’d…told us how big the emotions are going to be, it might not have been as terrifying to have those… really horrible big emotions in the first few weeks. Because all of that… hit us out of the blue…we weren’t expecting it” (FG 5).

Valued by all but needed differently: Peer support preferences after loss

Some participants took “comfort in reading [or listening to] other people’s stories and feeling less alone” (FG 2), others preferred face-to-face or online support where they could interact with peers, and this preference could vary over time, within and between individuals:

“sometimes I felt face-to-face was what I wanted, and sometimes it felt like I wanted to be online and anonymous” (FG 2).

“sometimes I felt face-to-face was what I wanted, and sometimes it felt like I wanted to be online and anonymous” (FG 2).

However, in-person groups were described as “few and far between” (FG 1), often leading participants to seek peer support online, which offered accessibility and anonymity but could feel overwhelming in large, diverse spaces:

“[I get] quite lost even in that world, because it was such a big, broad place of everybody had their own specific [experience]” (FG 6).

“[I get] quite lost even in that world, because it was such a big, broad place of everybody had their own specific [experience]” (FG 6).

There was agreement that the earlier days/months following TFMR were particularly hard. For some, they felt an immediate need to connect with peers during this time:

“I think that’s a very personal choice…when you need peer support…for me it was the day after…it was just such a shock event, that I felt like I needed support and I reached out to peers” (FG 4).

“I think that’s a very personal choice…when you need peer support…for me it was the day after…it was just such a shock event, that I felt like I needed support and I reached out to peers” (FG 4).

For others, engaging in any type of peer support during this time felt “overwhelming” (FG 4), but they wanted it more as time went on and emotional stability increased, capacity to engage grew and others returned to “some sort of normality” while their “life was just like on hold” (FG 4). Therefore, signposting support in hospitals during “that fog of grief” without any follow-up was futile for some. Participants expressed the need to be “followed up in the months afterwards, once feelings have settled down” (FG 4):

“there could be signs everywhere in the hospital, but if I wasn’t ready then to contact anyone, they were useless to me then. But then I wanted to know about it afterwards…I really wanted it post-birth…I had to look quite hard afterwards.” (FG 2).

“there could be signs everywhere in the hospital, but if I wasn’t ready then to contact anyone, they were useless to me then. But then I wanted to know about it afterwards…I really wanted it post-birth…I had to look quite hard afterwards.” (FG 2).

Being able to access peer support “at your fingertips” (FG 6), “literally 24/7” (FG 3) was highlighted as valuable throughout all stages following TFMR. Immediate and accessible support was particularly important because they could not predict what might trigger them or when it would happen. These triggers were often things they felt unable to express openly-such as distress caused by someone else’s pregnancy, which is typically seen as a joyful event:

“I saw a pregnant woman and she was moaning about her pregnancy and it really triggered something, I can literally put that on the message and get that…response from my peers…living in the moment. Whereas I found the…care that’s being offered maybe … “Oh, we’re here for you, nine to five, Monday to Friday” … my feelings weren’t working like that” (FG 3).

“I saw a pregnant woman and she was moaning about her pregnancy and it really triggered something, I can literally put that on the message and get that…response from my peers…living in the moment. Whereas I found the…care that’s being offered maybe … “Oh, we’re here for you, nine to five, Monday to Friday” … my feelings weren’t working like that” (FG 3).

As well as being immediate and accessible, peer support can provide continuous support, not offered through hospitals or charities. Participants described support offered through these organisations as either nonexistent or offered only at “a point in time” (FG 6) rather than on an ongoing basis, often ending long before they felt ready for it to stop:

“I started mine a couple of months after I gave birth and then it was fortnightly. But it finished just before what should have been my daughter’s due date….I feel like it finished just as I was about to face the most difficult day.” (FG 5).

“I started mine a couple of months after I gave birth and then it was fortnightly. But it finished just before what should have been my daughter’s due date….I feel like it finished just as I was about to face the most difficult day.” (FG 5).

Peer support offers a unique opportunity for continuity - friendships made through organisations that facilitate peer connection can last a lifetime and that is how long support following TFMR may be needed:

“I got two really good friends out of the [charity] group, and we still talk to each other, we remember each other’s dates, we talk about our children as if they’re alive, and we can just message each other when we wake up one day in that cloud of grief for whatever reason and just be like, “This is a really sh*t day”….I’ve got those friends that have come out of it, which allow me a place to hang those feelings whenever I need to, and I think that was the huge benefit of it for me” (FG 1).

“I got two really good friends out of the [charity] group, and we still talk to each other, we remember each other’s dates, we talk about our children as if they’re alive, and we can just message each other when we wake up one day in that cloud of grief for whatever reason and just be like, “This is a really sh*t day”….I’ve got those friends that have come out of it, which allow me a place to hang those feelings whenever I need to, and I think that was the huge benefit of it for me” (FG 1).

Theme 5: The other edge of the sword: When others’ stories feel heavy

Opening oneself up to the “very volatile emotional landscape[with]... little pitfalls and unexpected revelations with[in] groups” (FG 1) was a key challenge for participants, “especially at the beginning when everything’s a bit more raw” (FG 5) and there was a need to focus on, and process, one’s own experiences and emotions without exposure to others’. Participants highlighted the fluidity of their own, and others', pregnancy/loss experiences as a challenge in peer support groups, with people coming and going due to pregnancy and/or loss, leading to the realisation that pregnancy loss can happen multiple times. As with broader loss spaces, exposure to the various ways pregnancy could go wrong diminished hope for future pregnancies:

“I found it really hard to see someone going through it for a second time, and that stark reality of, “Sh*t, like, lightning really does strike twice. This could really happen again…I was like, “I do not need to see you navigate this while I am still actively trying to conceive and in the IVF cycle,” which is really, really hard.” (FG 1).

“I found it really hard to see someone going through it for a second time, and that stark reality of, “Sh*t, like, lightning really does strike twice. This could really happen again…I was like, “I do not need to see you navigate this while I am still actively trying to conceive and in the IVF cycle,” which is really, really hard.” (FG 1).

A participant, who was pregnant at the time of the focus groups, talked about the need to isolate herself from peer support groups to alleviate distress and protect her wellbeing during her current pregnancy, but highlighted the internal conflict this caused in cutting off the spaces in which she remembered her baby:

“I’m now five months pregnant again, and I have found that I’ve had to mute absolutely everything on Instagram, on Facebook, I’ve had to remove myself from mailing lists, because I cannot deal with it…. which has been really, really hard to also still remember my daughter.” (FG 2).

“I’m now five months pregnant again, and I have found that I’ve had to mute absolutely everything on Instagram, on Facebook, I’ve had to remove myself from mailing lists, because I cannot deal with it…. which has been really, really hard to also still remember my daughter.” (FG 2).

Participants found it overwhelming to be in a space where so many people were sharing stories of distress. A participant described their increased vulnerability to receiving potentially distressing content by linking a TFMR WhatsApp community to their mobile device. While this made support readily accessible, having control over exposure was highlighted as important, so that it comes when one is prepared for it:

“I can’t imagine being at work and getting “Ping, ping, ping, ping,” come through, and it knocking you, say if someone’s having a really tough day… I mute it, so then I still check it multiple times a day, but it’s not chucked at me at the wrong time” (FG 6).

“I can’t imagine being at work and getting “Ping, ping, ping, ping,” come through, and it knocking you, say if someone’s having a really tough day… I mute it, so then I still check it multiple times a day, but it’s not chucked at me at the wrong time” (FG 6).

Peer support spaces could reveal others’ experiences and opportunities that prompted refection on, and sometimes regret about, their own choices:

“you don’t know about the thing you didn’t do, because you didn’t do it, and I think it’s another layer of “What ifs” I think in that sense… Now, over a year later, there’s a big part of me that wishes I had gone through a labour and delivery, because that way, I would have got to have seen her.” (FG 1).

“you don’t know about the thing you didn’t do, because you didn’t do it, and I think it’s another layer of “What ifs” I think in that sense… Now, over a year later, there’s a big part of me that wishes I had gone through a labour and delivery, because that way, I would have got to have seen her.” (FG 1).

The variation in TFMR experience within TFMR specific peer spaces created uncertainty regarding potential judgement. A participant highlighted fear of judgement related to a “grey” diagnosis, where there is a fetal anomaly diagnosis but the fatality of which is unknown. For these participants, connecting with others who share the same diagnosis - or at least a similarly uncertain one - may be particularly important for them to feel safe:

“I also, within the TFMR community, I found myself not telling people fully because of our diagnosis: because ours was a grey diagnosis, I did feel like I potentially couldn’t be fully myself sometimes, because, again, I don’t want to be judged on why we TFMR’d as well.” (FG 2).

Discussion

This study highlights women’s perceptions of peer support and their lived experiences following TFMR. Findings show the profound isolation, misunderstanding and stigma women experience following TFMR and the unique value of TFMR-specific peer support for validation, normalisation and connection. However, access to such support was often dependent on luck, with postcode variation and professional gatekeeping shaping whether women found help or were left searching for support alone. Findings show that women’s needs changed over time, and while peer spaces were valuable, they could also be emotionally demanding. To our knowledge, this is the first study to explore perceptions of peer support following TFMR, providing crucial insights and clear clinical implications.

The lack of awareness and open conversation around TFMR highlighted in this study reflects a wider societal focus on hopeful, positive narratives around pregnancy, shaped by cultural expectations to reproduce. Such narratives obscure the prevalence and reality of baby loss and leave individuals feeling isolated and alone should it happen to them. Previous research has shown that clinical language in relation to pregnancy loss can actively contribute to grief and trauma [24]. Language related to ‘choice’ and ‘decision’ may further exacerbate the problem - framing decisions within clinical and policy discourse as matters of individual ‘choice’ obscures the structural, emotional, and moral pressures shaping decision-making, while simultaneously positioning individuals as responsible for outcomes that do not feel freely chosen.

The unique stigma associated with the perceived ‘choice’ involved in TFMR [25] further prevents open conversations, both within and beyond women’s usual social networks, impeding social support [26] and increasing the need for TFMR‑specific peer connection. Our findings highlight that existing generic baby‑loss services do not adequately meet the needs of those who experience TFMR; indeed, the presence of such services may mask an unmet need for TFMR‑specific support. This aligns with calls from others for tailored TFMR peer spaces [13]. Our analysis illustrates the distinct value these dedicated spaces provide.

There are several reasons why peer support is important for people who have experienced TFMR. Firstly, peer support for mental health and wellbeing is reported to reduce isolation, enhance coping, and increase validation through shared experience [27]. Our findings indicate that these mechanisms are also central to TFMR-specific peer support. Additionally, TFMR involves moral complexity and stigmatised decision making under significant distress, the intensity of which may create a particularly strong bond within peer support groups, offering support not available elsewhere.

Secondly, inequity in access to bereavement care has been previously reported [28]. The current study highlights that inequity extends beyond formal services: whether women ever reach peer support also depends on luck, shaped by the engagement of individual bereavement midwives and postcode‑based variation in service provision. This adds a new dimension to existing evidence by showing that structural inequities influence not only healthcare provided through statutory services but also access to community‑based peer support.

Finally, consistent with broader baby loss literature [29], our findings show that continued support and shared understanding remain invaluable as the years go on post‑TFMR. Our findings show the significant variation in needs between individuals and over time, demonstrating the need for flexible and adaptable support pathways.

It is important to recognise that while peer support may be associated with financially low costs, there may be hidden personal and psychological costs that need to be considered. For example, the needs of peer supporters require careful consideration and management to avoid burnout [30]. Voluntary, Community and Social Enterprise (VCSE) organisations providing peer support initiatives are often underfunded [31], making small and sustained funding streams critical for supporting and maintaining community-based support.

Strengths and limitations

This is a timely and important study with clear implications for practice. Recruitment was relatively easy, focus groups were well attended, and participants were highly engaged, possibly reflecting a desire from woman to share their experiences in order to improve services. The first author shared her lived experience with participants from the outset, with the aim of creating a space where women felt safe to share their views. The analysis was led by the first author and supported by the other authors, ensuring a range of perspectives, including clinical and lived expertise.

However, given the design we may not have captured the perspectives of those unwilling or unable to take part in focus group research - voices that may similarly be missing from peer support spaces. All participants had experienced TFMR for fetal anomaly within Great Britain, therefore the views of women who had experienced TFMR for maternal health reasons and/or outside GB, were not captured. The sample was also limited to mainly white, educated, married women.

The sample was predominantly white and middle-class, therefore it is important that future research explore the perceptions of a more socio-economically diverse population, using a range of methods to facilitate engagement. It is also important to explore peer support following TFMR for fathers, those who go on to have a subsequent pregnancy, and from the perspective of peer supporters themselves, as each group may have distinct support needs. Further research could also explore different models of delivering peer support, including innovative approaches such as a Library of Lived Experience, which have shown promise in broader mental health [32], and may offer a valuable way of providing accessible peer support for those affected by TFMR.

Clinical implications

This study has several clear and important clinical implications (Table 3). Findings show that generic baby-loss services alone offer insufficient support for TFMR and may obscure unmet TFMR-specific needs. Access to specialised TFMR support often occurs by chance, and continued engagement depends on the availability of evolving and varied support options. Therefore, it is crucial that peer support for TFMR is specialised, specific and standardised across regions, with bereavement midwife training strengthened to ensure consistent pathways to support and a flexible, diverse support offer that can meet evolving needs over time. This may be particularly important during subsequent pregnancies and for those facing uncertain or ‘grey’ diagnoses. Findings further highlight the importance of sensitive, non-judgmental language in all aspects of TFMR support, including broader public health and workplace training initiatives aimed at increasing societal understanding and improving support for women when returning to work following TFMR. The predominantly white, middle-class sample reflects the broader pattern of who is most likely to access formal peer-support spaces [33]. This highlights an important gap: those outside more privileged social positions may have fewer opportunities to engage with peer support. Ensuring that peer support spaces are accessible and inclusive is crucial.

Clinical implications

Conclusion

TFMR is a prevalent and highly distressing experience. Effective, and relatively inexpensive, support could be available through peer support offering validation, normalisation and connection; however, there is currently no standarised pathway to access it. This is not to suggest that peer support should be solely responsible for supporting families following TFMR. Rather, that greater formalisation and standardisation is needed to ensure that peer support access is equitable and that it is delivered safely and sustainably. The statutory and voluntary sectors could be enabled to develop more peer support offers, delivered in different forms to meet variation in need across all those effected by TFMR. More research is needed to understand how different forms of peer support are experienced and how it could be tailored to meet the needs of diverse groups, including, but not limited to, fathers and other non-birthing partners. Such findings would inform the development and evaluation of much needed evidence-based peer support interventions for those bereaved by TFMR.
