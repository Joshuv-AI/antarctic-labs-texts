Effect of simulated bur radius compensation on full-contour CAD crown morphology and trueness across tooth types. An in- silico study
Authors: Nabeel S Martani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo systematically investigate and quantify the influence of bur radius compensation size on the design parameters of full-contour computer-aided design (CAD) crowns. The study analyzes the deterministic trends in morphological characteristics, surface area, volume, and geometric trueness across varying tooth anatomies to provide data-driven guidelines for CAD protocols. This study utilized a deterministic computer simulation to generate idealized virtual crown designs for three distinct anatomical tooth types (molar [#3], premolar [#5], and central incisor [#8]). Because the computational algorithm yields identical outputs from identical inputs with zero variance, a sample size calculation was not applicable; instead, single standardized designs were analyzed. The crowns were processed with seven varying bur radius settings: a 0.00 mm baseline (representing an ideal design with no bur constraints) and six test radii ranging from 0.10 mm to 1.50 mm. The resulting standard tessellation language (STL) files were analyzed using a custom Python algorithm (Eyleen Code) by utilizing Trimesh and NumPy libraries, to calculate trend geometric deviation metrics Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) and Hausdorff Distance (H.D) relative to the baseline. The simulation data revealed that increasing the bur radius from 0.10 mm to 1.50 mm resulted in progressive morphological changes across all tooth types. A marked trend of volumetric loss was observed; the molar volume decreased from a baseline of 160.60 mm3 down to 135.13 mm3 at the maximum radius. Conversely, surface area and roughness metrics showed a consistent upward trend. Geometric trueness deteriorated substantially with larger radii. While maximum localized deviations (H.D) peaked between the 0.75 mm and 1.00 mm settings, overall morphological deviation (RMSE) continued to climb, peaking at the 1.25 mm to 1.50 mm settings depending on the tooth type. Specifically, the RMSE for the central incisor rose from 0.049 at the minimal compensation to a peak of 0.184, indicating substantial volumetric and geometric distortion compared to the ideal baseline design. The selection of bur radius size directly dictates the morphological reliability of dental CAD. The data demonstrate that a minimal radius (0.10 mm) yields the highest trueness to the ideal baseline, whereas larger settings cause substantial volumetric reduction and geometric deviation, particularly in posterior teeth. Introduction

Prosthetic dentistry has been dramatically advanced by the incorporation of computer-aided design and computer-aided manufacturing (CAD/CAM) systems. The integration of digital workflows has increased the precision, efficiency, and predictability of dental restorations (Miyazaki et al. 2009). Unlike traditional manual methods, these digital protocols enable the reproducible creation of dental prostheses, significantly improving the consistency of crown and bridge fabrication (Pjetursson et al. 2007).

The digitalization process typically begins with the acquisition of the patient's oral anatomy via intraoral or laboratory scanners, followed by the creation of a definitive design model, the CAD file, which serves as the ideal blueprint for the final prosthesis. This blueprint is then physically produced through subtractive manufacturing, primarily high-speed milling (Davidowitz and Kotick 2011). While CAD/CAM systems offer remarkable theoretical accuracy, the translation from a perfect digital design to a physical restoration is inherently subject to manufacturing constraints. One of the most significant yet often overlooked technical limitations is the physical geometry of the cutting tool, specifically the bur radius size (Liu et al. 2025).

As milling machines utilize spherical-tipped burs to remove material, they are physically incapable of perfectly replicating sharp internal angles or the deep, narrow fissures often present in digital CAD models. This limitation necessitates a compromise between the cutting tool radius and anatomical detail, often resulting in deviations between the intended digital morphology and the actual manufactured outcome (Abduo et al. 2014). Furthermore, the selection of the bur radius involves a trade-off: while a smaller radius achieves higher accuracy for complex features, it dramatically increases machining time and accelerates tool wear, impacting laboratory efficiency and cost (Van Noort 2012).

The challenge of manufacturing is further complicated by the variable anatomical complexity across the dentition. Posterior teeth, such as molars, exhibit intricate occlusal surfaces with deep fissures and sharp cuspal inclines, presenting a significantly greater challenge than the smoother morphologies of incisors or premolars (Li et al. 2020). This variation is functionally dictated; the complex ridge and groove system of a molar is essential for masticatory efficiency, whereas anterior teeth prioritize smooth contours for esthetics and guidance. These inherent anatomical differences interact directly with the geometric limitations of the milling tool (Li et al. 2020). For instance, a larger bur radius may adequately reproduce the broad facial surface of an incisor but would fail to mill the narrow grooves of a molar's occlusal table. Understanding this interaction is critical, as compromised morphology can lead to poor fit, suboptimal occlusal function, and ultimately, prosthetic failure (Al Hamad et al. 2021).

While the mechanical limitations of physical milling, such as tool vibration and wear, are well-documented, the specific influence of virtual CAD software compensation algorithms on crown and prosthesis morphology remains under-explored. Current literature often conflates these software-driven design modifications with physical machining errors. Consequently, there is a lack of systematic, quantitative data that isolates the effect of the "simulated" bur radius compensation from the cumulative errors of the actual manufacturing process. Establishing this baseline is essential for optimizing digital workflows before physical production begins.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to systematically investigate the effects of simulated bur radius size and tooth anatomy on the morphological characteristics and geometric trueness of CAD crown designs. This study tested two primary hypotheses: (1) that increasing the simulated bur radius would result in a reduction in crown volume and geometric trueness; and (2) that the pattern and magnitude of these morphological deviations would vary significantly across different tooth anatomies (molar, premolar, and central incisor) due to their distinct geometric features. By focusing exclusively on the computational phase, this investigation distinguishes intrinsic software-based simulation constraints from the variables of physical manufacturing.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and master model creation

Intraoral digital impressions of the maxillary and mandibular arches, including bite registration, were obtained from a fully dentate subject using a TRIOS 3 intraoral scanner (3Shape, Copenhagen, Denmark). All procedures followed the manufacturer's standardized scanning protocol, and patient data were fully anonymized from archived case prior to data export. The scans were exported in 0.3oxz format and imported into the Dental System CAD software (3Shape, Copenhagen, Denmark).

Virtual tooth preparation

To isolate the variable of anatomical complexity, the "Virtual Preparation" module was utilized to generate idealized crown preparations on three specific teeth: a maxillary central incisor (#8), a maxillary first premolar (#5), and a maxillary first molar (#3). These teeth were selected to represent increasing levels of geometric intricacy, from the smooth, broad surfaces of the anterior dentition to the complex fissure systems of the posterior dentition. The "Model Builder" module was subsequently used to fabricate the digital master model (Fig. 1).

Virtual crown preparations generated on the digital master model for the molar (#3), premolar (#5), and central incisor (#8)

Crown design and parameter standardization

A standard full-contour crown design order was created for each of the three prepared teeth. To ensure consistency, a specific "die interface" parameter set was established and maintained as a constant across all experimental groups: (Cement gap: 0.04 mm, Extra cement gap: 0.07 mm, Distance to margin line: 0.80 mm, Smooth distance: 0.20 mm) as shown in (Fig. 2).

Standardized die interface settings maintained constant throughout the study (Cement space: 0.04 mm; Extra cement space: 0.07 mm; Distance to margin: 0.8 mm)

Simulated bur radius protocols

This study employed a deterministic computational simulation to evaluate the effect of tool size on crown morphology. The CAD software’s native CAM calculation engine was used to simulate the path of the milling tool. This process utilizes a morphological closing operation where the software generates an offset surface equivalent to the tool radius to prevent the bur from gouging the intended geometry. Consequently, design features smaller than the tool diameter (e.g., deep occlusal fissures) are automatically bridged or smoothed.

To quantify this effect, the master designs were processed under seven distinct simulated bur radius conditions (n = 21 total unique designs):

Control Group (0.00 mm): A theoretical baseline design with no bur radius compensation, representing the ideal anatomical morphology.

Computational mesh analysis

Automated geometric analysis was performed using a custom-developed Python script (Elyeen Code) utilizing the Trimesh version 3.9 and NumPy scientific computing libraries. The script imported the binary STL files and executed the following comparative algorithms relative to the 0.00 mm control group:

Geometric Trueness: Calculated using the point-to-surface Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) after global registration via the Iterative Closest Point (ICP) algorithm (capped at 100 iterations). This metric quantifies the average deviation of the test mesh vertices from the reference surface.

Validation of the python algorithm

The validation of the custom Python metrology algorithm was conducted using a two-step protocol. First, to establish a computational baseline and confirm the absence of algorithmic noise, a zero-deviation control test was implemented. Comparing a single baseline mesh directly against itself yielded an expected absolute zero deviation. This initial test confirmed the script's foundational stability and correct data parsing when handling the intricacies of complex, high-density dental geometries, as shown in Fig. 4.

Visual plot and mesh comparison showing the color-coded deviation maps for the central incisor, premolar, and molar

Second, to rigorously validate the algorithm's accuracy in quantifying true geometric deviations, a benchmarking protocol was performed against an established, open-source 3D point cloud processing software (CloudCompare, version 2.12). To test non-zero deviation parameters, the baseline central incisor mesh (0.00 mm) and a morphologically altered test mesh (1.50 mm simulated bur radius) were registered using Iterative Closest Point (ICP) alignment capped at 100 iterations, followed by point-to-surface distance computations in both environments. The Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) calculated by the custom Python script matched the mathematically derived RMSE from the validated CloudCompare software (0.1719 mm). This confirms the algorithmic reliability and geometric accuracy of the custom metrology pipeline.

Sample size justification

This study utilized a deterministic computer simulation model. Unlike physical milling, where stochastic variables (e.g., tool wear, machine vibration, material heterogeneity) introduce variance requiring large sample sizes, the CAD software's virtual compensation algorithm functions deterministically; identical input parameters yield identical output meshes with zero variance (Variance = 0). Consequently, a sample size of n = 1 per experimental condition was sufficient to fully characterize the algorithmic behavior. Traditional power analysis and inferential statistics (p-values) were therefore not applicable to this study design.

Surface roughness and geometric complexity

Surface roughness metrics increased consistently with larger bur radii. The molar group exhibited the highest absolute roughness values, rising from 2.234 μm at baseline to 2.753 μm at the 1.50 mm radius, corresponding to a 23.23% increase. The premolar group reached a peak roughness of 2.501 μm, representing a 21.17% increase from its 2.064 μm baseline.

Geometric complexity (a dimensionless ratio) generally increased with bur size. For the premolar, complexity peaked at 101,603 at the 0.50 mm radius from a baseline of 95,168 (a 6.34% increase) before stabilizing. The central incisor showed a gradual increase, peaking at 97,139 from a baseline of 93,260 (a 3.99% increase).

Geometric trueness analysis

The geometric integrity of the models was evaluated by comparing various bur radii against a baseline control (0.00 mm). Trueness was quantified using Hausdorff Distance (H.D) representing the maximum localized error, Mean Squared Error (MSE), and Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE). The results indicate a clear correlation between increased bur radius and a reduction in geometric accuracy.

Discussion

This study systematically investigated the impact of simulated bur radius settings on the morphological and geometric trueness of full-contour CAD crown designs. The results demonstrate that increasing the bur radius substantially compromises the ability to reproduce accurate anatomical morphology. Specifically, larger radii were associated with progressive volumetric loss and increased geometric deviation from the ideal design. This aligns with established principles of subtractive manufacturing, where tool accessibility and geometry directly dictate the reliability of the milled restoration (Bosch et al. 2014; Padrós et al. 2020).

Mechanisms of deviation

The observed morphological changes are primarily driven by the software's native "bur radius compensation" algorithms. As the simulated tool radius increases, the software calculates a larger offset path to prevent the tool from binding. On the intaglio (internal) surface, this compensation effectively enlarges the internal cavity to accommodate the larger tool, resulting in the observed reduction in overall crown volume. This volume reduction is not a random error but a calculated adjustment to ensure the physical crown can fully seat on the abutment. Simultaneously, on the occlusal surface, features smaller than the tool diameter, such as deep central fissures or sharp marginal ridges, are automatically smoothed or bridged. This "filtering" effect explains the increase in surface roughness and loss of geometric complexity observed in the molar and premolar groups.

Clinical contextualization

To place these findings in a clinical context, it is necessary to reference established acceptability thresholds. While the clinically acceptable marginal gap is widely cited between 50 and 120 μm (McLean and von Fraunhofer 1971), internal fit tolerance is generally more permissible, often ranging up to 200 μm to accommodate luting cement. Our simulation showed that increasing the bur radius to 1.25 mm caused localized deviations (Hausdorff Distance) exceeding 0.45 mm in the molar fissures. Unlike marginal gaps, which expose cement to dissolution, these internal deviations primarily risk 'bottoming out' the restoration, where the crown binds on the occlusal preparation before fully seating at the margin. This confirms that while larger burs may theoretically 'mill' a crown, they introduce geometric errors that would necessitate significant chairside adjustment to achieve marginal seal.

Comparison with existing literature

Our findings validate work by Bosch et al. 2014, who reported that larger milling burs failed to reproduce fine fissures, leading to increased internal gaps. Similarly, Kirsch et al. 2017 demonstrated that smaller diameter burs improve morphological trueness, particularly in areas with sharp anatomical features. Avrahami et al. (2025) and Kim et al. (2016) confirmed that milling protocols utilizing multiple small-diameter burs (versus single large-diameter strategies) result in superior restoration fit and adaptation. A comparative in vitro study by Al-Ramadan et al. (2024) further identified bur diameter as a primary determinant of restoration fit, concluding that larger tools introduce substantial geometric inaccuracies.

However, the literature is not unanimous regarding the clinical significance of these deviations. Research by Zimmermann et al. (2018) highlights the impact of milling parameters on restoration accuracy, noting that while marginal adaptation may remain clinically acceptable across various settings, different CAM strategies produce statistically significant discrepancies in the axial and occlusal internal fit of partial crowns. Braian et al. (2018) also noted that advanced tool-path strategies can partly compensate for larger burs, though they cannot eliminate geometric errors entirely. Furthermore, Riccitiello et al. (2018) argued that while minor internal discrepancies exist, they are often clinically acceptable as they are compensated for by the luting agent. Our data suggests that while "acceptable" fit might be achievable, the morphological reliability of the occlusal surface is definitely compromised by larger burs.

Clinical implications

The practical implications of these findings highlight a critical trade-off in digital dentistry. While selecting a smaller bur radius (e.g., 0.50 mm or smaller) enhances detail reproduction and trueness, particularly for anatomically intricate posterior teeth, it inevitably increases milling time, tool wear, and the risk of chipping in brittle ceramics. Conversely, larger burs offer efficiency but at the cost of geometric accuracy. It is important to note that milling protocols typically employ a hybrid strategy, utilizing larger burs for bulk removal (roughing) and smaller burs for fine details (finishing). Our study simulated a restricted single-radius environment to isolate the variables; however, in practice, the use of a small finishing bur would likely mitigate some of the gross deviations observed here. Practitioners and laboratory technicians must therefore tailor their milling strategies: prioritizing small-radius protocols for complex molar restorations while potentially utilizing larger radii for anatomically simpler anterior teeth.

Limitations

This computational study has several inherent limitations. First, using a single patient dataset and a single CAD software (3Shape Dental System) restricts the findings to specific algorithmic behaviors, which may not fully generalize to other anatomical variations or CAD platforms. Second, the 0.00 mm bur radius baseline is a theoretical geometric ideal impossible to achieve in physical subtractive manufacturing. Furthermore, the calculated geometric trueness metrics may be slightly influenced by the exported STL mesh resolution (triangulation) and mathematical approximations within the Iterative Closest Point (ICP) registration algorithm. Finally, as a deterministic in-silico simulation, this study isolates software logic but cannot account for the stochastic variables of physical manufacturing, such as machine vibration, kinematics, or bur wear. Consequently, the results represent theoretical software limits rather than final clinical outcomes, highlighting the need for future physical validations.
