A global prevalence of electronic nicotine delivery systems (ENDS) use among students: a systematic review and meta-analysis of 4,189,145 subjects
Authors: Muayad Saud Albadrani, Muhammad Abubaker Tobaiqi, Mohammed A. Muaddi, Heba M. Eltahir, Esra Seddiq Abdoh, Atallah Mohammad Aljohani, Emad Ali Albadawi, Naweed SyedKhaleel Alzaman, Mekky M. Abouzied, Hammad Ali Fadlalmola
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe use of electronic nicotine delivery systems (ENDS), including e-cigarettes, vape pens, hookah pens, e-cigars, and e-pipes, has grown in popularity worldwide, particularly among young individuals. However, these products are not without risks, as their emissions may contain harmful substances such as diacetyl (a flavoring agent linked to specific pulmonary problems in high concentrations), tobacco-specific nitrosamines, and volatile organic compounds with unknown long-term effects on lung health.ObjectiveThis systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to assess the prevalence of ENDS use among school and college students on a global scale.MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science databases. Two researchers independently selected and extracted data from the identified studies. The pooled prevalence of ENDS use among school or college students was calculated using a random-effect meta-analysis.ResultsThe systematic review encompassed 146 studies with 4,189,145 subjects from 53 countries and six continents. The overall current prevalence of ENDS use was 10.2%, indicating a significant presence of this behavior among students. Moreover, ENDS current use was higher among males (10.2%) than females (7.5%). In comparison, the lifetime prevalence was 22.0%, indicating that a substantial portion of students have tried these products but do not continue to use them regularly.ConclusionOur meta-analysis reveals a significant prevalence of ENDS use among school and college students globally, with higher rates observed among males than females. The lifetime prevalence of ENDS indicates that many students have tried or are currently using these products. The current prevalence of ENDS use was 10.2%, with notable variations across different continents.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20858-2.

Introduction

The use of electronic nicotine delivery systems (ENDS), including e-cigarettes and vaporizers, has grown significantly in popularity worldwide, particularly among young individuals. These devices were introduced to the United States market in 2007 as alternatives to traditional cigarettes, offering a method to vaporize a solution of nicotine and liquid flavors instead of burning tobacco [1, 2]. ENDS encompass a variety of products such as vape pens, e-pens, e-pipes, and e-cigars, all classified under this category [3].

Despite their popularity, ENDS are not risk-free because, in addition to nicotine, their emissions may contain harmful substances such as diacetyl (a flavoring agent linked to specific pulmonary problems in high concentrations), tobacco-specific nitrosamines, and volatile organic compounds with unknown long-term effects on lung health [4, 5]. In addition to various theories, ENDS use may cause lung cancer [6]. Another study showed that ENDS may increase respiratory and cardiovascular diseases [7].

The prevalence of ENDS use is increasing worldwide, according to previous studies, especially among the youth. On the other hand, conventional tobacco smoking is decreasing gradually due to the shift of consumers from cigarettes to electronic ones [8]. The reason for that shift in the consumers may be due to the branding campaign from the e-cigarette companies of the benefits of using e-cigarettes instead of usual tobacco [9]. People around the world are using higher non-disposable generations of e-cigarettes, as between 2014 and 2016, youth in the United States were more likely to use advanced-generation such as vape pens and mods than disposable devices [10]. However, it is essential to consider the possibility of a causal link between the rise in ENDS use and the decline in traditional cigarette consumption. This crossing of the curves raises significant questions for those who support the theory of a dominant gateway effect, where e-cigarette use leads to traditional cigarette smoking. Conversely, there is an emerging hypothesis that the competition between ENDS and traditional cigarettes may contribute to the decline in cigarette smoking. This inverse effect of competition suggests that as more individuals choose ENDS, the demand for traditional cigarettes decreases. Both hypotheses warrant further investigation into the complex dynamics between ENDS and traditional cigarette use.

At the early start, when e-cigarettes invaded the markets, there were no specific regulations or restrictions for marketing them. Nowadays, there are specific rules that prohibit smoking and the use of ENDS in indoor areas such as restaurants, cafes, and working spaces, except for smoke-free areas, along with the laws that prevent selling ENDS to underage in the 50 states in the USA. However, the regulatory environments vary significantly across different countries, which might influence ENDS usage patterns. For instance, some countries have stringent regulations that limit the sale and advertisement of ENDS, while others have more lenient policies. In the European Union, the Tobacco Products Directive (TPD) regulates the sale and marketing of ENDS, including restrictions on nicotine content and advertising. In contrast, countries like Japan have banned the sale of nicotine-containing ENDS altogether, while others, such as the United Kingdom, have adopted a more supportive stance, promoting ENDS as a harm-reduction tool for smokers. These varying regulatory environments can significantly impact the prevalence and patterns of ENDS use and public perceptions of their safety and benefits.

Our work builds upon the recent systematic review by Kim et al. [8], which provided a comprehensive overview of e-cigarette use among young people. However, our systematic review and meta-analysis aim to assess the global prevalence of ENDS use among school and college students. Focusing specifically on the student population and providing a more detailed analysis of gender, country, and continental differences, we aim to shed new light on the patterns and trends of ENDS use among students, thereby contributing to the current state of knowledge. By synthesizing data from multiple studies, we seek to provide a comprehensive overview of the current and lifetime prevalence of ENDS use. Our findings will contribute to understanding ENDS use patterns among students and inform public health initiatives and regulatory measures to address this emerging issue.

Additionally, data from the European School Survey Project on Alcohol and Other Drugs (ESPAD) indicate a decline in cigarette use among adolescents from 1995 to 2019 but a rise in e-cigarette use in recent years [11]. The Health Behaviour in School-aged Children (HBSC) study and the Global Youth Tobacco Survey (GYTS) also report similar trends, highlighting the increasing prevalence of e-cigarette use among youth globally [11].

This systematic review and meta-analysis focuses exclusively on the prevalence of ENDS use among students and does not examine traditional cigarette consumption. While ENDS and traditional cigarettes both deliver nicotine, this analysis is limited to ENDS use among school and college students. By synthesizing data from multiple studies, we seek to provide a comprehensive overview of the current and lifetime prevalence of ENDS use, with a focus on differences by gender, country, and continent. Our findings will contribute to the understanding of ENDS use patterns among students and inform public health initiatives and regulatory measures to address this emerging issue.

Methods

Study design

The study was designed according to the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [12, 13].

Literature search and eligibility criteria

Using this search strategy, we searched PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane Central, and Web of Science in February 2023: (((Student OR Students OR "young adult" OR "College-aged") AND Prevalence) AND ("Electronic Nicotine Delivery Systems" OR Vaping OR "Electronic cigarettes" OR "E-cigarettes" OR "E-Cigs" OR "Electronic Cigarette" OR "E-Cigarettes" OR "E-Cigarette" OR "E Cigs" OR "E-Cig" OR Vapes OR Vape OR "e-hookahs" OR "Electronic hookah" OR "vape pens" OR "ENDS" OR "electronic nicotine delivery" OR vaporize* OR "e liquid*" OR "e-liquid*" OR EVALI)). The criteria of the included articles were 1) Population: students in school or college using electronic cigarettes 2) outcome: prevalence of ENDS use 3) study design: all types of study designs (cross-sectional, cohort, case–control, and interventional articles) 4) studies written in English. Exclusion criteria included qualitative papers and papers that were published as review studies, editorial comments, presentations, or studies that did not evaluate any of our outcomes.

Study selection and data extraction

Data extraction was performed through an offline data extraction sheet. The extracted data included the following: study ID, country, age, the prevalence in males and females, study design, current prevalence, lifetime prevalence, and the number of subjects. Two researchers independently extracted data using a standardized data extraction form. Any disagreements between the reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus. If necessary, a third reviewer was consulted to reach a final decision.

Risk of bias assessment

Two authors independently assessed the methodological quality of the observational studies included using the National Institutes of Health's (NIH) tool [14]. This tool consists of several questions that assess the risk of different sources of bias and confounders. The reviewers' opinion is classified as "good", "fair", or "poor" according to scores obtained during the assessment. In addition, Egger's test and funnel plot method was used to evaluate the risk of publication bias.

Moreover, we revealed all the information we collected on ENDS use prevalence among students worldwide, partitioned by gender, country, and continent. We analyzed categorical outcomes as pooled proportions with 95% confidence intervals (CI). The random effect model was applied using the DerSimonian-Laird method. We investigated the statistical heterogeneity between studies using the I2 statistics chi-squared test, with p < 0.1 considered heterogeneous and I2 ≥ 50% suggestive of high heterogeneity. Also, we did cumulative analyses for our primary outcomes. We conducted statistical analyses using the Comprehensive Meta-Analysis Software (CMA).

Results

Study selection

We found 9619 studies after searching four databases. When we screened the titles and abstracts of the 6681 studies after removing duplicates, we found that 6277 had no relevance to our study. A total of 404 studies underwent full-text screening, and only 146 were considered for inclusion in our systematic review. Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis study aims to determine the global prevalence of ENDS use among school students and college students. We found that the current prevalence in men within total men is higher than in women within total women, as the results were 10.2% and 7.5%, respectively. We also found that the current prevalence in men within the total population was higher than in women, as the results were 10.9% and 8.1%, respectively. The lifetime prevalence in men is also higher than in women: 27.7% vs 23.7%. Also, there was a continuous rise in the prevalence of ENDS use among students.

ENDS use in men has been increasing since the early start of the marketing campaign in social media. The continuous increase in the prevalence of ENDS use may be attributed to the branding that is less harmful than tobacco, the good taste of e-cigarettes compared to traditional cigarettes, and the marketing for e-cigarettes to increase energy for men [8, 15, 16]. It is important to consider the possibility of a causal link between the rise in ENDS use and the decline in traditional cigarette consumption. This crossing of the curves raises significant questions for those who support the theory of a dominant gateway effect, where e-cigarette use leads to traditional cigarette smoking. Conversely, there is an emerging hypothesis that the competition between ENDS and traditional cigarettes may contribute to the decline in cigarette smoking. This inverse effect of competition suggests that as more individuals choose ENDS, the demand for traditional cigarettes decreases. Both hypotheses warrant further investigation to understand the complex dynamics between ENDS and traditional cigarette use.

Some European countries reported low lifetime prevalence, such as Iceland 7.8% [17], Ireland 3.7% [18], and Serbia 10.1% [19]. On the other hand, most studies in Europe reported a high lifetime prevalence, as Kinouani et al. reported in France, with 40% lifetime prevalence [20]. In addition, Gorini et al. reported a high lifetime prevalence in Italy, with 43% of the population [21].

The use of ENDS, including e-cigarettes, presents a complex issue with both potential benefits and significant risks. On the one hand, ENDS are often promoted as a harm reduction tool for smokers, offering a less harmful alternative to traditional cigarettes by eliminating the combustion process and reducing exposure to harmful tar and chemicals [17]. This can be particularly beneficial for current smokers looking to quit or reduce their tobacco use. On the other hand, there are substantial concerns regarding the use of ENDS among never smokers, especially young people, due to the risk of nicotine addiction and potential gateway effects leading to traditional cigarette smoking. Additionally, the long-term health effects of ENDS are still not fully understood, with studies indicating possible respiratory and cardiovascular risks. Therefore, while ENDS may serve as a valuable tool for harm reduction among smokers, it is crucial to implement careful regulation and monitoring to prevent misuse and protect public health, particularly among vulnerable populations [17].

One of the key strengths of our study is its focus on assessing the prevalence of ENDS use among students in educational settings, including both schools and universities. This focus allows us to capture a critical demographic that is often targeted by ENDS marketing campaigns. Additionally, our study includes a large sample size, enhancing the generalizability of our findings. This comprehensive approach enables us to offer a detailed analysis of gender, country, and continental differences in ENDS use, contributing significantly to the current state of knowledge and informing public health strategies and regulatory measures aimed at reducing ENDS use among young people. Our study included cross-sectional and cohort studies, as well as an examination of the prevalence of ENDS worldwide and their lifetime and current use, which are among the many advantages. Also, to the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to assess ENDS use prevalence among a specific category: students who have an education, whether in school or college. We included a large number of studies with a high sample size. This study also has some limitations. Firstly, the reliance on self-reported data in many of the included studies may introduce biases such as recall bias and social desirability bias, potentially leading to underreporting or overreporting of ENDS use. Secondly, the predominance of cross-sectional studies in our analysis limits our ability to infer causality. Cross-sectional studies provide a snapshot of ENDS use at a single point in time, making it challenging to determine temporal relationships or causal links between variables. Longitudinal studies are needed to better understand the dynamics of ENDS use over time and to establish causal relationships. Additionally, the high heterogeneity observed among the included studies may impact the generalizability of our findings.

The high heterogeneity (I2 > 99%) observed among the included studies suggests substantial variation that warrants further exploration. Several potential sources of heterogeneity could contribute to these findings. Firstly, differences in study design, such as cross-sectional versus cohort studies, may introduce variability in the reported prevalence rates. Cross-sectional studies provide a snapshot at a single point in time, while cohort studies follow subjects over a period, potentially capturing changes in behavior. Secondly, population characteristics, including age, gender, socioeconomic status, and cultural factors, can influence ENDS usage patterns and contribute to heterogeneity. For instance, studies conducted in regions with different regulatory environments or cultural attitudes toward ENDS use may report varying prevalence rates. Lastly, data collection methods, such as self-reported surveys versus objective measurements, can also impact the results. Self-reported data may be subject to recall bias or social desirability bias, leading to underreporting or overreporting of ENDS use. Future research should aim to standardize study designs and data collection methods to reduce heterogeneity and provide more comparable results.

It is important to note that this study does not explore the potential causal relationship between the rise in ENDS use and the decline in traditional cigarette consumption. Some studies suggest that the increase in ENDS use may be associated with a decrease in traditional cigarette smoking, potentially due to competition between the two products. This hypothesis, along with the theory of a gateway effect from e-cigarettes to traditional cigarettes, warrants further investigation. Additionally, while this study focuses on the prevalence and risks associated with ENDS use, it is essential to consider the broader risk–benefit balance. ENDS are often promoted as a harm reduction tool for smokers, offering a less harmful alternative to traditional cigarettes. However, the long-term health effects of ENDS are still not fully understood, and their use among never smokers, particularly young people, raises significant public health concerns.

Additional research is needed in this area to examine the reasons for the continuously rising prevalence and to find out different modalities that may help smokers stop or put limits on conventional cigarette use due to the high prevalence of ENDS use among existing users [17]. Due to the findings in our study of the rise in ENDS use in continental Europe and compared to other continents, we suggested doing additional in-depth research to pinpoint ENDS use, conduct surveys, and adopt legislation to outlaw ENDS in this continent.

Conclusion

Our meta-analysis reveals a significant prevalence of ENDS use among school and college students globally, with notable variations across different continents. The overall current prevalence of ENDS use was 10.2%, with higher rates observed among males (10.2%) compared to females (7.5%). The lifetime prevalence was 22.0%, indicating substantial experimentation among students.
