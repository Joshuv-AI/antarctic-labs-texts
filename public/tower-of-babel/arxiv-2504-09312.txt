Testing Juntas and Junta Subclasses with Relative Error
Xi Chen, William Pires, Toniann Pitassi, Rocco A. Servedio

Abstract
This papers considers the junta testing problem in a recently introduced “relative error” variant of the standard Boolean function property testing model. In relative-error testing we measure the distance from f f to g g , where f , g : { 0 , 1 } n → { 0 , 1 } f,g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\} , by the ratio of | f − 1 ​ ( 1 ) ​ △ ​ g − 1 ​ ( 1 ) | |f^{-1}(1)\hskip 1.70709pt\triangle\hskip 1.70709ptg^{-1}(1)| (the number of inputs on which f f and g g disagree) to | f − 1 ​ ( 1 ) | |f^{-1}(1)| (the number of satisfying assignments of f f ), and we give the testing algorithm both black-box access to f f and also access to independent uniform samples from f − 1 ​ ( 1 ) f^{-1}(1) . [ CDH+25a ] observed that the class of k k -juntas is poly ⁡ ( 2 k , 1 / ε ) \mathrm{poly}(2^{k},1/\varepsilon) -query testable in the relative-error model, and asked whether poly ⁡ ( k , 1 / ε ) \mathrm{poly}(k,1/\varepsilon) queries is achievable. We answer this question affirmatively by giving a O ~ ​ ( k / ε ) \tilde{O}(k/\varepsilon) -query algorithm, matching the optimal complexity achieved in the less challenging standard model. Moreover, as our main result, we show that any subclass of k k -juntas that is closed under permuting variables is relative-error testable with a similar complexity. This gives highly efficient relative-error testing algorithms for a number of well-studied function classes, including size- k k decision trees, size- k k branching programs, and size- k k Boolean formulas.

1.3
Techniques

In this subsection we focus our discussion on the more general and challenging problem of testing subclasses of juntas (
Theorem

2
).
(An overview of
Theorem

Our approach builds on techniques that were used to give efficient algorithms for testing juntas and subclasses of juntas in the
distribution-free
testing model.
We recall that in the distribution-free testing model, as defined by
[
GGR98
]
and subsequently studied by many authors
[
HK07
,
HK04
,
GS09
,
DR11
,
CX16
,
LCS+19
,
Bsh19
,
Bel19
,
CP22
,
CFP24
]
,
there is an unknown and arbitrary “background distribution”
{\cal D}
over
\{0,1\}^{n}
, and the distance between two functions
f
and
g
is measured by
\operatorname{{\mathbf{Pr}}}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim{\cal D}}[f(\boldsymbol{x})\neq g(\boldsymbol{x})].
In this model the testing algorithm can both make black-box queries to the unknown
f
that is being tested, and can also draw i.i.d. samples from the distribution
{\cal D}
.

Remark 4

(On distribution-free testing versus relative-error testing.)
.

We stress that while a relative-error testing algorithim gets access to independent uniform samples from
f^{-1}(1)
, akin to how a distribution-free tester gets access to independent samples from
{\cal D}
, relative-error testing does
not
correspond to the special case of distribution-free testing in which the “background distribution”
{\cal D}
is uniform over
f^{-1}(1)
. This is because in the distribution-free model, the distance
\operatorname{{\mathbf{Pr}}}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim{\cal D}}[f(\boldsymbol{x})\neq g(\boldsymbol{x})]
between two functions
f,g
is measured vis-a-vis the unknown distribution
{\cal D}
which the testing algorithm can draw samples from. This is quite different from the way distance is measured in the relative-error setting; in particular, the relative distance
\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(f,g)
is
not
equal to
\smash{\operatorname{{\mathbf{Pr}}}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim{\cal U}_{f^{-1}(1)}}[f(\boldsymbol{x})\neq g(\boldsymbol{x})]}
. Indeed, a function
g
can have large relative distance from
f
because
g(x)=1
on many points
x
for which
f(x)=0
, but all such points have zero weight under the distribution
{\cal U}_{f^{-1}(1)}
. This issue necessitates a number of changes to the techniques used to give prior distribution-free testers, as described below.

Improving on
[
LCS+19
]
,
[
Bsh19
]
gave a distribution-free testing algorithm that uses

\tilde{O}(k/\varepsilon)

queries and samples, and soon thereafter
[
Bsh20
]
, extending and strengthening the learning-based approach of
[
DLM+07
]
, gave an efficient distribution-free tester for any subclass of
k
-juntas.
At a high level our approach is similar to that of
[
Bsh20
]
, but there are several significant differences (most significant for the last two phases of our algorithm), which we describe in the high-level sketch of our approach given below.

•

Like
[
Bsh20
]
, the first two phases of our main
Junta-Subclass-Tester
algorithm (
Algorithm

1
) checks that certain restrictions of the function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
that is being tested are close to literals in a suitable sense. This is done in our algorithm as follows: in the first phase (“Partition
[n]
and find relevant blocks”), the algorithm randomly partitions the
n
input variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
into
O(k^{2})
disjoint blocks, and performs a binary search procedure over blocks to find (i) a collection of “relevant blocks,” and (ii) for each “relevant block,” a string (denoted
v^{\ell}
) that “witnesses” that the block is relevant.
(The whole algorithm rejects if this phase identifies more than
k
such blocks.)
Next, the second phase (“Test the relevant sets”) uses a uniform-distribution 1-junta testing algorithm
UniformJunta
due to
[
Bla08
]

(see
Theorem

12
in
Section

A.2
) to check that certain restrictions of
f
(denoted
f^{\ell}
, and defined using the witness strings

) are suitably close to literals under the uniform distribution.

At a high level these phases of our algorithm are similar to
[
Bsh20
]
, but the differences between the relative-error framework and the distribution-free framework mean we must carry out these phases in a somewhat different way.

[
Bsh20
]
creates restrictions and uses witness strings which, roughly speaking, fix
variables which ‘‘appear to be irrelevant’’ to 0.
3

3

[
Bsh20

under restrictions that fix variables to 0.

This approach works in the distribution-free context, since roughly speaking,
the distance between two functions can only come from examples
x
that are “likely to be drawn under
{\cal D}
.” In contrast, in the relative-error setting,
a function
g
can be very far from

has many satisfying assignments

, and such points will never be drawn when we sample from

•

In the third phase (“Trimming
Approx
”), our algorithm creates a set
of candidate functions which are possible “approximators” (in a suitable sense) for the unknown function
f
.
Recall from
Table

1
that
{\cal C}(k)^{*}\subset{\cal C}(k)
is the subset of
{\cal C}(k)
consisting of the functions in
{\cal C}(k)
whose set of relevant variables is contained in
\{1,\dots,k\}
;
our algorithm carries out the third phase by “trimming” an initial set

which
consists of a specially constructed set of juntas that each have small relative distance to some function in
{\cal C}(k)^{*}
.
This “trimming” is done by checking, for each candidate function
g
in
Approx
, whether randomly sampled positive examples of

also correspond (after a suitable remapping of variables) to positive examples of the candidate function
g
; if this does not hold for one of the sampled positive examples, then the candidate
g
is discarded. (The algorithm rejects if all candidates in
Approx
are discarded.)

In its fourth and final phase (“Find
k

f
”), our algorithm determines whether the trimmed approximator set contains a suitable approximator for
f
. This is done by (i) identifying the function
{\mathfrak{g}}
in
the trimmed approximator set which has the fewest satisfying assignments,
and (ii) verifying that

{\mathfrak{g}}
is indeed close to
f
in a suitable sense (under
a remapping of the variables). If this is the case then our algorithm accepts, and otherwise it rejects.

The most significant differences between our algorithm and that of
[
Bsh20
]
are in Phases 3 and 4.

[
Bsh20
]
starts by checking that
f
is close to a particular subfunction of
f
, denoted
F
, which sets all variables in “irrelevant” blocks of
f
to 0 and sets all variables in each “relevant” block of
f
to the same bit.

[
Bsh20
]
then checks
F
against a set of candidates, each of which belongs to the subclass
{\cal C}(k)
.
Because the
[
Bsh20
]
(distribution-free) notion of two functions
h,h^{\prime}
being
\varepsilon

h(\boldsymbol{x})\neq h^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x})
with probability at least
\varepsilon

\boldsymbol{x}\sim{\cal D}
, it is enough to simply sample independent draws from
{\cal D}
and reject if
f
and
F
disagree on any of them. Similarly for the second check of
F
against the candidates, if
F
and a candidate disagree on a sampled point

then the candidate is eliminated.

In contrast, in our relative-error setting, since we can only draw samples from
f^{-1}(1)
, after we trim the approximator set in Phase 3, it could still be possible that
f
is far in relative distance from some surviving
g
in the trimmed set, which could happen if
g^{-1}(1)\cap f^{-1}(0)
is large.

To deal with this, our approach is as follows: we do not use an analogue of
[
Bsh20
]
’s function
F
, but rather we consider
only
the function
{\mathfrak{g}}
in the trimmed set
that has the
fewest

In the yes-case, when
f\in\mathcal{C}(k)
, we must have
|{\mathfrak{g}}^{-1}(1)|\approx|f^{-1}(1)|
, and since
{\mathfrak{g}}(\boldsymbol{x})=1
for
\boldsymbol{x}\sim f^{-1}(1)
with high probability, it must also be the case that
f(\boldsymbol{x})=1
for
\boldsymbol{x}\sim{\mathfrak{g}}^{-1}(1)
with high probability. And in the no case, when
\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(f,h)
is large for every
h\in\mathcal{C}(k)
, we cannot have both
{\mathfrak{g}}(\boldsymbol{x})=1
for
\boldsymbol{x}\sim f^{-1}(1)
with high probability and also
f(\boldsymbol{x})=1
for
\boldsymbol{x}\sim{\mathfrak{g}}^{-1}(1)
with high probability. For this would imply that
\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(f,{\mathfrak{g}})
is small, but since
{\mathfrak{g}}
is very close in relative distance to some function in
\mathcal{C}(k)
, a simple triangle inequality
implies that
f
has small relative distance to some function in
{\cal C}(k)
, which contradicts the fact that we are in the no-case.

1.4
Future work: relative-error testing of classes of functions that are approximated by juntas?

A natural goal for future work is to obtain positive results for testing classes of functions that do not correspond exactly to subclasses of juntas. In particular, in the standard uniform-distribution model, the techniques of
[
DLM+07
]
go beyond just testing subclasses of juntas and allow testing classes of functions which can be
approximated
to high accuracy by juntas under the uniform distribution. Several classes of interest are of this sort, including: size-
s

s
-term DNF formulas,
s
-sparse polynomials over
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, and others; each of these can be approximated, but not exactly computed, by juntas. Can classes such as these be tested efficiently in the relative-error setting?

A challenge which immediately arises is that while these classes are well-approximated by juntas under the uniform distribution, this is not the case under relative error. For example, for any constant
s
it is easy to give an
s
-term DNF formula which cannot be well-approximated in relative error by any
O_{s}(1)
-junta. On the positive side, though, for both the class
{\cal C}=
conjunctions and the class
{\cal C}=
decision lists, functions in
{\cal C}
are approximable by
O(1)
-juntas under the uniform distribution and cannot in general be well-approximated by juntas under relative error, but
[
CPPS25
]
gave efficient testing algorithms for each of these classes. Each of the two testers of
[
CPPS25
]
was carefully specialized to the particular class at hand; it would be very interesting, though perhaps quite challenging, to give a general extension of
Theorem

2
to classes of functions that are only uniform-distribution approximable by juntas. A first goal in this direction is to either give an
O_{s,\varepsilon}(1)
-query algorithm for testing the class of
s
-term DNF, or alternatively to prove an
\omega_{n}(1)
-query lower bound for constant
\varepsilon
.

Testing Subclasses of Juntas

Recall that a class
\mathcal{C}(k)
of Boolean functions from
\{0,1\}^{n}
to
\{0,1\}
is a
subclass of
k
-juntas
if (i) every
f\in\mathcal{C}(k)
is a
k
-junta, and (ii)
\mathcal{C}(k)
is closed under permuting variables (that is, if
f(x)\in\mathcal{C}(k)
then for any permutation
\pi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[n]\to[n]
we have that
f_{\pi}(x)=f(\pi(x))
is also in
\mathcal{C}(k)
).
Throughout this section,
\mathcal{C}(k)
denotes a fixed and arbitrary subclass of
k
-juntas. (For example,
\mathcal{C}(k)
might be the class of decision trees over
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
with at most
k
nodes).
We further recall that
\mathcal{C}(k)^{*}\subset\mathcal{C}(k)
is the subset of
\mathcal{C}(k)
consisting of the functions in
\mathcal{C}(k)
that do not depend on variables
x_{k+1},\dots,x_{n}
. (Continuing the example from above,
\mathcal{C}(k)^{*}
would be the class of size-
k
decision trees over variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
.)

2.1
Notation

Let
h\leq k
be a positive integer.
Given an injective map
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[h]\rightarrow[n]
and an assignment
y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, we write
\sigma^{-1}(y)
to denote the string

y\in\{0,1\}^{h}

with
y_{i}=x_{\sigma(i)}
for each

.
Given a

\sigma(z)
to denote
the string
u\in\{0,1\}^{S}
, where
S=\{\sigma(i)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}i\in[h]\}
is the image set of
\sigma
and
u_{\sigma(i)}=z_{i}
for each

.
Given a function
g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\{0,1\}^{h}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
over

h
variables and an injective map
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[h]\rightarrow[n]
,
we write
g_{\sigma}
to denote the Boolean function over

\{0,1\}^{n}
with

g_{\sigma}(x)=g(\sigma^{-1}(x))
(note that
g_{\sigma}
is an
h
-junta and hence also a
k
-junta).
We will always use

etc. to denote a Boolean function over
\{0,1\}^{n}
and
g,g^{\prime},{\mathfrak{g}},
etc. to denote a Boolean function over
\{0,1\}^{h}
for some
h\leq k
.
With this notation, note that
\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(f,g_{\sigma})
and
\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(g_{\sigma},f)
are well defined, since
f
and
g_{\sigma}
are Boolean functions on
\{0,1\}^{n}
.

Given a positive integer
h\leq k
and
0<\kappa\leq 1/2
, we now
define a collection of functions over
\{0,1\}^{h}
called

\textsc{Approx}(h,\kappa)
. The idea is that
\textsc{Approx}(h,\kappa)
contains functions over

h
variables that approximate functions in
\mathcal{C}(k)
with
error parameter
\kappa
, in a suitable sense that we define as follows:

For each function
f\in\mathcal{C}(k)^{*}
, let
f^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
be the function defined as follows:

f^{\prime}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\text{arg}\max_{b\in\{0,1\}}\left\{\mathop{{\mathbf{Pr}}\/}_{\boldsymbol{w}\sim\{0,1\}^{{n-h}}}\big[f(x_{[h]}\circ\boldsymbol{w})=b\big]\right\}.

(1)

Clearly
f^{\prime}
is an
h
-junta that only depends on the first
h

variables.
Let
g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\{0,1\}^{h}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
be the function
with
g(z)=f^{\prime}(z\circ 0^{n-h})
.
The function
g
is in
\textsc{Approx}(h,\kappa)
iff
\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(f,f^{\prime})\leq\kappa
.

Note that a testing algorithm can construct the set
\textsc{Approx}(h,\kappa)
“on its own” without making any queries.
At a high level, the reason we need to introduce
\textsc{Approx}(h,\kappa)
, instead
of working with
\mathcal{C}(k)^{*}
directly, is because the algorithm may only be able to
identify, implicitly, an
h
-subset of the
k
relevant variables.

The fact below follows directly from the definition of
\textsc{Approx}(h,\kappa)
:

Fact 5
.

|\textsc{Approx}(h,\kappa)|\leq|\mathcal{C}(k)^{*}|
.

The next lemma shows that if
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
is close to
some
g\in\textsc{Approx}(h,\kappa)
in relative distance under some
injective map
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[h]\rightarrow[n]
,
then it must be close to
\mathcal{C}(k)
in relative distance as well.

Lemma 6
.

If
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
satisfies

\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(f,g_{\sigma})\leq 1
for some function
g\in\textsc{Approx}(h,\kappa)
and injective map
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[h]\rightarrow[n]
,
then we have
\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(f,\mathcal{C}(k))\leq\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(f,g_{\sigma})+4\kappa
.

Proof.

By the construction of
\textsc{Approx}(h,\kappa)
, given that
g\in\textsc{Approx}(h,\kappa)
, there exists a function
f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(k)
that satisfies
\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(f^{\prime},g_{\sigma})\leq\kappa
.
Using
Lemma

10
(in
Section

A.2
) which shows that relative distance satisfies approximate symmetry, we have
that
\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(g_{\sigma},f^{\prime})\leq 2\kappa
.
The lemma follows by bounding
\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(f,f^{\prime})
using
the triangle inequality (
Lemma

11
in
Section

A.2
).

2.2
Overview of the Main Algorithm

We start with an overview of our main
algorithm,
Algorithm

1
, for testing an arbitrary subclass
\mathcal{C}(k)
of
k
-juntas that is closed under permutations.
At a very high level, the approach is to perform “implicit learning” by searching a candidate set of hypotheses to see whether it contains a hypothesis that is consistent with the algorithm’s data.

Let
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
be the function that is being tested.

Algorithm

1
starts by drawing a partition of
variables
[n]
into
r=O(k^{2})
many blocks
\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{X}_{r}

uniformly at random.
For the case when
f\in\mathcal{C}(k)
, because
f
only depends on

k
variables, a quick calculation shows that most likely
every block
\boldsymbol{X}_{\ell}
contains at most one relevant variable
of
f
. For the overview we will assume that this is the case when
f\in\mathcal{C}(k)
.

In Phase 1,
Algorithm

1
tries to find as many
relevant
blocks
\boldsymbol{X}_{\ell}
of
f
as possible under a query complexity budget. Here intuitively we say
\boldsymbol{X}_{\ell}
is a relevant block of
f

if the algorithm has found a string
v^{\ell}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
such that

f^{\ell}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\{0,1\}^{\boldsymbol{X}_{\ell}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, defined as

{f^{\ell}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=f\left(v^{\ell}_{\overline{\boldsymbol{X}_{\ell}}}\circ x\right)},

(2)

is not a constant function.
To search for a new relevant block,
let
\{\smash{\boldsymbol{X}_{\ell}}\}_{\smash{\ell\in\boldsymbol{I}}}
denote the set of relevant blocks found so far and
\boldsymbol{X}
be their union (with
\smash{\boldsymbol{I}=\emptyset}
and
\boldsymbol{X}=\emptyset
initially).
Phase 1 draws a uniform satisfying assignment
\boldsymbol{u}\sim f^{-1}(1)
and a random
\boldsymbol{w}\sim\smash{\{0,1\}^{\overline{\boldsymbol{X}}}}
; if
f(\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{X}}\circ\boldsymbol{w})=0
, then a binary search over blocks can be run
on
\boldsymbol{u}
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{X}}\circ\boldsymbol{w}
, using
O(\log r)=O(\log k)
many queries, to find a new relevant block
\boldsymbol{X}^{\ell}
together with an accompanying
v^{\ell}
(which is used to define
f^{\ell}
).
If Phase 1 finds too many relevant blocks (i.e.,
|\boldsymbol{I}|
becomes larger than
k
), then
Algorithm

1
rejects; on the other hand, if it fails to make progress after
T_{2}=O(\log k)\cdot T_{1}

many rounds, where

T_{1}=O\left(\frac{\log|\mathcal{C}(k)^{*}|}{\varepsilon}\right),

then
Algorithm

1
stops searching and moves on to Phase 2. The latter helps ensure that the number of queries used in Phase 2 stays within the budget.

In Phase 2,
Algorithm

1
checks if every
f^{\ell}
,
\ell\in\boldsymbol{I}
, is
(1/30)
-close to a literal under the uniform distribution (i.e.,
x_{i}
or
\overline{x_{i}}
for some variable
i\in\boldsymbol{X}_{\ell}
),
and rejects if any of them is not.

When
Algorithm

1
reaches Phase 3, one may assume that the relevant blocks found satisfy the following two conditions (recall
\boldsymbol{X}
is the union of relevant blocks
\boldsymbol{X}_{\ell}
,
\ell\in\boldsymbol{I}
, found in Phase 1):

1.

First, we have

\mathop{{\mathbf{Pr}}\/}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{u}\sim f^{-1}(1)\\
\boldsymbol{w}\sim\{0,1\}^{\overline{\boldsymbol{X}}}\end{subarray}}\big[f(\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{X}}\circ\boldsymbol{w})=0\big]\leq\kappa/4,

(3)

where
\kappa
is chosen to be
1/20T_{1}
,
since otherwise it is unlikely for Phase 2 to fail to find a new
relevant block after
T_{2}=O(\log k)\cdot T_{1}
repetitions before moving to Phase 3.

2.

Second, for every
\ell\in\boldsymbol{I}
,
f^{\ell}
is
(1/30)
-close to a literal
x_{\tau(\ell)}
or
\overline{x_{\tau(\ell)}}
for some
\tau(\ell)\in\boldsymbol{X}_{\ell}
;
since otherwise Phase 2 would reject with high probability. When this is the case,
\tau(\ell)\in\boldsymbol{X}_{\ell}
for each
\ell\in\boldsymbol{I}
is well defined and unique.

To give some intuition for Phase 3 and 4, let’s consider
the case when
f\in\mathcal{C}(k)
.
Let
h=|\boldsymbol{I}|
and let
\ell_{1}<\ldots<\ell_{h}
be indices of blocks in
\boldsymbol{I}
.
Let
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[h]\rightarrow[n]
be the injective map
with
\sigma(i)=\tau(\ell_{i})
for each
i\in[h]
,
let
S=\{\tau(\ell_{i})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}i\in[h]\}
, and let
\mathsf{G}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\{0,1\}^{h}\rightarrow\{0,1\}

be the following function:

\mathsf{G}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\text{arg}\max_{b\in\{0,1\}}\left\{\mathop{{\mathbf{Pr}}\/}_{\boldsymbol{w}\sim\{0,1\}^{\overline{S}}}\big[f(\sigma(x)\circ\boldsymbol{w})=b\big]\right\}.

Lemma

15
shows that
Equation

3
implies

\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(f,\mathsf{G}_{\sigma})\leq\kappa
and thus, we have
\mathsf{G}\in\textsc{Approx}(h,\kappa)

by the construction of
\textsc{Approx}(h,\kappa)
.
On the other hand, if
f
is
\varepsilon
-far from every function

\mathcal{C}(k)

The goal of Phase 3 and 4 is then to distinguish the following two cases: (i) there exists a

( note that there is a big gap between the

and

To gain some intuition, pretend for a moment that
\textsc{Approx}(h,\kappa)
is a collection
of functions over
\{0,1\}^{n}
, and the two cases we need
to distinguish are simply either (1) there exists a function
\mathsf{G}\in

\textsc{Approx}(h,\kappa)
such that
\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(f,\mathsf{G})\leq\kappa

.
This could be done easily as follows:

1.

Draw
1/(20\kappa)
satisfying assignments
\boldsymbol{u}\sim f^{-1}(1)

and remove every

with

{\mathfrak{g}}
be the function left with the smallest

{\mathfrak{g}}^{-1}(1)

If we are in case (1), then with probability at least

19/20
the function
\mathsf{G}

On the other hand, in both cases (1) and (2), by a union bound every
g
that survives

\frac{|f^{-1}(1)\setminus g^{-1}(1)|}{|f^{-1}(1)|}{\leq\varepsilon/500}.

In particular the function
{\mathfrak{g}}
passed down to the next phase must satisfy this condition in both cases.
For case (1), one can use the choice of
{\mathfrak{g}}
(i.e.,
|{\mathfrak{g}}^{-1}(1)|

) to show that

\displaystyle\frac{|{\mathfrak{g}}^{-1}(1)\setminus f^{-1}(1)|}{|{\mathfrak{g}}^{-1}(1)|}{\leq\varepsilon/400}.

(4)

For case (2), using
\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(f,{\mathfrak{g}})\geq\varepsilon/2
,

\varepsilon/7
.

2.

Draw

A.2
Standard-model property testing and relative-error property testing

We briefly review the “standard” model for testing Boolean functions
[
Gol17
]
. A standard-model testing algorithm for a class
{\cal C}
of
n
-variable Boolean functions has oracle access
\mathrm{MQ}(f)
to an unknown and arbitrary function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
.
The requirement on the algorithm is that it must output “yes” with high probability (say at least 2/3; this can be amplified using standard techniques) if
f\in{\cal C}
, and must output “no” with high probability (again, say at least 2/3) if
f
disagrees with every function
g\in{\cal C}
on at least
\varepsilon 2^{n}
inputs in
\{0,1\}^{n}
.

As defined and studied in
[
CDH+25a
,
CDH+25b
,
CPPS25
]
, a
relative-error
testing algorithm for
{\cal C}
has oracle access to
\mathrm{MQ}(f)
and also has access to a “random satisfying assignment” oracle which, when called, returns a uniform random element
\boldsymbol{x}\sim f^{-1}(1)
.
An
\varepsilon
-relative-error testing algorithm for
{\cal C}
must output “yes” with high probability (say at least 2/3; again this can be easily amplified) if
f\in{\cal C}
and must output “no” with high probability (again, say at least 2/3) if
\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(f,{\cal C})\geq\varepsilon
, where

\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(f,{\cal C})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\min_{g\in{\cal C}}\hskip 1.42271pt\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(f,g)\ \quad\text{and}\ \quad\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(f,g)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}={\frac{|f^{-1}(1)\ \triangle\ g^{-1}(1)|}{|f^{-1}(1)|}}.

Thus, in the relative-error setting, the distance from
f
to
{\cal C}
is measured “relative to the sparsity of
f
.”

The following easy observation says that relative distance doesn’t change when we apply a permutation:

Observation 9
.

If
\pi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[n]\to[n]
is a permutation, then
\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(f,g)=\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(f_{\pi},g_{\pi})
.

We will need the following two easy lemmas about relative distance:

Lemma 10

(Approximate symmetry of relative distance)
.

Let
f,g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
be functions such that
\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(f,g)\leq\varepsilon
where
\varepsilon\leq 1/2
. Then
\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(g,f)\leq 2\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Since
\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(f,g)\leq\varepsilon
we have that

\frac{|f^{-1}(1)\triangle g^{-1}(1)|}{|f^{-1}(1)|}\leq\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{2}.

which means we must have that
|g^{-1}(1)|\geq\frac{|f^{-1}(1)|}{2}
. Hence

\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(g,f)=\frac{|f^{-1}(1)\triangle g^{-1}(1)|}{|g^{-1}(1)|}\leq\frac{|f^{-1}(1)\triangle g^{-1}(1)|}{2|f^{-1}(1)|}\leq 2\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(f,g)\leq 2\varepsilon.

∎

Lemma 11

(Approximate triangle inequality for relative distance)
.

Let
f,g,h\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
be such that
\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(f,g)\leq\varepsilon
and
\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(g,h)\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}
. Then
\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(f,h)\leq\varepsilon+(1+\varepsilon)\varepsilon^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(f,g)\leq\varepsilon
we have that

{\frac{|f^{-1}(1)\ \triangle\ g^{-1}(1)|}{|f^{-1}(1)|}}\leq\varepsilon,

which means we must have
|g^{-1}(1)|\leq(1+\varepsilon)|f^{-1}(1)|
. Hence

\displaystyle\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(f,h)

\displaystyle={\frac{|f^{-1}(1)\ \triangle\ h^{-1}(1)|}{|f^{-1}(1)|}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|f^{-1}(1)|}\left|\{z\>\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\>f(z)\neq h(z)\}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|f^{-1}(1)|}\left|\{z\>\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\>f(z)\neq g(z)\lor g(z)\neq h(z)\}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|f^{-1}(1)|}\left|\{z\>\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\>f(z)\neq g(z)\}\right|+\frac{1}{|f^{-1}(1)|}\left|\{z\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}g(z)\neq h(z)\}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(f,g)+(1+\varepsilon)\frac{1}{|g^{-1}(1)|}\left|\{z\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}g(z)\neq h(z)\}\right|

\displaystyle=\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(f,g)+(1+\varepsilon)\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(g,h)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon+(1+{\varepsilon})\varepsilon^{\prime}.

∎

\mathop{{\mathbf{Pr}}\/}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{u}\sim f^{-1}(1)\\

and let

be a subset of at most
k

Then we have

\mathop{{\mathbf{Pr}}\/}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{u}\sim f^{-1}(1)\\
\boldsymbol{w}\sim\{0,1\}^{\overline{X}},\\
\boldsymbol{y}\sim\{0,1\}^{X\setminus J}\end{subarray}}\big[f(\boldsymbol{u}_{X}\circ\boldsymbol{w})\neq f(\boldsymbol{u}_{J}\circ\boldsymbol{y}\circ\boldsymbol{w})\big]\geq\varepsilon/5.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{u}\sim f^{-1}(1)
,
\boldsymbol{w}\sim\{0,1\}^{\overline{X}}
and
\boldsymbol{y}\sim\{0,1\}^{X\setminus J}
as above. Then the following inequality completes the proof (where the last line uses the assumption of the Lemma, and
Lemma

25
.

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathbf{Pr}}\/}\big[f(\boldsymbol{u}_{X}\circ\boldsymbol{w})\neq f(\boldsymbol{u}_{J}\circ\boldsymbol{y}\circ\boldsymbol{w})\big]

\displaystyle\geq\mathop{{\mathbf{Pr}}\/}\big[f(\boldsymbol{u})=f(\boldsymbol{u}_{X}\circ\boldsymbol{w})\land f(\boldsymbol{u})\neq f(\boldsymbol{u}_{J}\circ\boldsymbol{y}\circ\boldsymbol{w})\big]

The tester and an overview

The junta testing algorithm, called

, is presented in
Algorithm

4
.
We bound its sample and query complexity in
Section

.
We show in
Section

that

accepts with

when

-junta, and show in

when

is
\varepsilon
-far from every
k
-junta in relative distance.

Before the formal proof, in this subsection we give an overview of the algorithm
and its analysis.
Since we are concerned with the entire class of
k
-juntas rather than a subclass, there is no need to do the kind of “implicit learning” that was used in
Algorithm

1
; this lets us achieve a much better query complexity than would follow from a naive application of
Algorithm

1
.
Looking ahead, compared to
Algorithm

1
,
the savings comes from the fact that no trimming is needed in
Algorithm

4
.
Instead,
at a high level
the oranization of our

4

is set up so as to follow the distribution-free junta testing algorithm and proof of correctness from
[

]
quite closely,
but there are some differences which we describe at the end of this subsection.

Let
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
be the input function that is being tested.
In Phase A,
Algorithm

4
starts by drawing a partition of

[n]
into
r=O(k^{2})
many blocks

In Phase B,

tries to find as many

\boldsymbol{X}_{\ell}

if we have found a string

such that

{f^{\ell}(x)=f\left(x\circ v^{\ell}_{\overline{\boldsymbol{X}_{\ell}}}\right)},

is not a constant function.
Let
\{\smash{\boldsymbol{X}_{\ell}}\}_{\smash{\ell\in I}}

be their union.
Phase B draws

\boldsymbol{u}\sim f^{-1}(1)

\boldsymbol{w}

over
\smash{\{0,1\}^{\overline{\boldsymbol{X}}}}

rejects; on the other hand, if it fails to make progress by finding a new relevant block after

.

In Phase C

checks whether every function

,

, is close to a literal under the uniform distribution (i.e.,

or

for some variable

),
and rejects if any of them is not.

When
Algorithm

4
reaches Phase D, one may assume that the relevant blocks found satisfy the following two conditions:

1.

First, we have

\mathop{{\mathbf{Pr}}\/}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{u}\sim f^{-1}(1)\\
\boldsymbol{w}\sim\{0,1\}^{\overline{\boldsymbol{X}}}\end{subarray}}\big[f(\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{X}}\circ\boldsymbol{w})\neq f(\boldsymbol{u})\big]\leq\varepsilon/20;

(13)

since otherwise it is unlikely for Phase B to fail to find a new

repetitions before moving to Phase C.

2.

Second, every

is close to a literal
x_{\tau(\ell)}
or
\overline{x_{\tau(\ell)}}
for some unique
\tau(\ell)\in\boldsymbol{X}_{\ell}
;
since otherwise Phase C rejects with high probability.

Let

be the set of indices
\{\tau(\ell)\}_{\ell\in I}
, with

. Then by

, we have

\mathop{{\mathbf{Pr}}\/}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{u}\sim f^{-1}(1)\\
\boldsymbol{w}\sim\{0,1\}^{\overline{X}},\\
\boldsymbol{y}\sim\{0,1\}^{X\setminus J}\end{subarray}}\big[f(\boldsymbol{u}_{X}\circ\boldsymbol{w})\neq f(\boldsymbol{u}_{J}\circ\boldsymbol{y}\circ\boldsymbol{w})\big]\geq\varepsilon/5

(14)

when

is
\varepsilon
-far from every
k
-junta in relative distance.
While

and
\boldsymbol{w}

to sample

.
The challenge here is that
Algorithm

4

does not know

(even though it is well defined given that
every

is close to a literal) and it would require too many queries
to explicitly identify any

given that each block is likely to contain

many variables (recall that we wants to avoid

We explain how the subroutine works in
Section

C.4
;

Algorithm

4
rejects in Phase D if

f(\boldsymbol{u}_{X}\circ\boldsymbol{w})\neq f(\boldsymbol{u}_{J}\circ\boldsymbol{y}\circ\boldsymbol{w})

(15)

and it accepts if this does not happen after
M=O(1/\varepsilon)
rounds.

For the case when

is a
k
-junta, as will become clear in
the analysis in

,
Algorithm

4
always accepts if the partition is such
that every block
\boldsymbol{X}_{\ell}
contains at most one relevant variable of

. Given that we draw

blocks, this happens with high probability by a standard birthday paradox analysis.
For the case when every

-junta is

-far from

(which follows from

Finally, we briefly discuss how our algorithm and (mostly) its analysis differ from
[
Bsh19
]
. Each of our algorithm’s phases is quite similar to the corresponding phase of the
[
Bsh19
]
algorithm. However, in Phase B,

[
Bsh19
]
picks a restriction
f^{\prime}
of
f
in which every variable outside the “relevant blocks” is set to
0
. Since
[
Bsh19
]
works in the distribution free setting it’s easy to check whether
f
and
f^{\prime}
are close, by simply sampling
\boldsymbol{u}\sim\mathcal{D}
and checking if
f(\boldsymbol{u})=f^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{u})
. Finally, in Phase D
[
Bsh19
]
checks if
f^{\prime}
is close to a specific
k
-junta under the distribution
\mathcal{D}
. In the no case of the distribution-free setting, since
f
is far from every
k
-junta and
f^{\prime}
is close to
f
,
f^{\prime}
must fail this final check with high probability.

In contrast, in our relative-error setting it is not easy to check if
f
is relative-error close to a restriction like
f^{\prime}
. In the relative-error setting we can only get samples from
f^{-1}(1)
, and it is not clear how to use such samples to detect that

is far from
f^{\prime}
. This is because it could be the case that
\mathrm{rel}\text{-}\mathrm{dist}(f,f^{\prime})
is large, but for
\boldsymbol{u}\sim f^{-1}(1)
we always have

.

To deal with this issue, we do not use a restriction of

, but rather we set variables in the “irrelevant blocks” uniformly at random in both Phase B and Phase D. We do not get that
f
is close to some function
f^{\prime}
by the end of Phase B; instead, the correctness of our algorithm crucially relies on
Lemma

26
, which does not have an analogue in the analysis of
[
Bsh19
]
.

C.2
Sample and query complexity of
Algorithm

We first note that Phase A does not make any samples or queries.
In Phase B, we repeat lines 4–8 until either

.
In one iteration of the loop, if the “if” condition on line 5 is true, then lines 6–7 are executed which makes

In Phase C, lines 11–12 are executed at most
