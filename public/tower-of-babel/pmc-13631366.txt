Vascular calcification: pathogenic mechanisms, signaling crosstalk and translational therapeutics
Authors: Rui Hua, Bao Qiao, Xuehao Liu, Bingchen Jiang, Jinlei Wu, Chuanxin Zhang, Han Liu, Bailu Wang, Shujian Wei, Yuguo Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVascular calcification (VC) is a multifactorial pathological process characterized by ectopic deposition of hydroxyapatite in the arterial wall, which is closely associated with increased cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. This review provides a comprehensive synthesis of current insights into the pathogenesis of VC, emphasizing its nature as a dynamically regulated process driven by intricate crosstalk among diverse molecular mechanisms, signaling cascades, and pathological pathways. Under specific pathological stimuli, this intricate regulatory network disrupts the homeostatic balance between pro-calcific and anti-calcific mechanisms. Key drivers of VC include the phenotypic transformation of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT), inflammation, oxidative stress, endoplasmic reticulum stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and various forms of programmed cell death. The transcription factor RUNX2 serves as a central regulator, integrating signals from pathways such as WNT/β-catenin, BMP/Smad, PI3K/AKT, MAPK, AMPK, NF-κB, Notch, and Rho/ROCK. Furthermore, emerging evidence highlights the critical regulatory roles of epigenetic mechanisms, including posttranslational modifications (PTMs) and noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs, miRNAs, circRNAs). Given the complexity of VC, we critically evaluate the efficacy and safety of various pharmacological interventions from clinical trials, including bisphosphonates (BPs), calcimimetics, phosphate binders, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitor (DPP4i), SNF472, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), statins, proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 inhibitors (PCSK9i), sodium thiosulfate, and vitamins K and D, while also discussing potential endogenous molecules and natural compounds. A deep understanding of these interconnected molecular networks is essential for developing precision therapies to mitigate VC and VC-related cardiovascular complications.Subject terms: Cardiology, Physiology

Introduction

Vascular calcification (VC) is a progressive and highly regulated pathological process characterized by the abnormal deposition and ectopic calcification of hydroxyapatite crystals in the extracellular matrix (ECM) of arterial walls, specifically in the intima or media.1,2 VC significantly predisposes the at-risk population as a key risk factor and independent predictor of cardiovascular events.3,4 The high prevalence and all-cause mortality of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) among the elderly population reflect its age-related correlation.5,6 VC was previously regarded as a passive pathological process occurring after calcium and phosphorus deposition in the vascular wall; however, accumulating evidence has demonstrated that it primarily exhibits an active, adaptive pathophysiological process induced by specific conditions.7 The prevalence of VC varies significantly across populations and disease states. Ankle-brachial index data show an overall adult VC prevalence of 0.5%, with a higher incidence in men (3:2 ratio).8 Notably, VC is most prevalent in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), affecting 17% of newly diagnosed patients9 and reaching 41.5% in those receiving oral antidiabetics.10,11

VC is classified into three main types based on anatomical location: intimal, medial, and adventitial calcification. Intimal calcification in coronary arteries is associated with atherosclerosis; medial calcification in elastic arteries involves elastin degradation and hydroxyapatite deposition; peripheral arteries (e.g., femoral/tibial) exhibit chronic kidney disease (CKD)-related medial calcification via metabolic disorders.12 Morphologically, VC progresses from microcalcification (0.5–15 μm) to punctate (15 μm–1 mm), linear (1–3 mm), and diffuse (≥3 mm) stages, with advanced stages correlating with plaque stability or obstruction. These classifications reflect distinct cellular mechanisms and clinical implications. In terms of hemodynamics, VC stiffens vulnerable arterial walls, diminishes vascular compliance, and induces fluctuations in systolic-diastolic blood pressure, ultimately increasing cardiac workload and impairing tissue perfusion, even as the cause of undermining cardiovascular function.13 In addition, VC worsens vascular stenosis and exacerbates flow stasis and reduces blood flow velocity, which significantly formulates a feedback loop that exacerbates cardiovascular vulnerability, thereby intensifying VC in a vicious cycle14.

VC is driven by a multilayered regulatory cascade that encompasses aberrant intracellular signaling, dysregulated molecular networks, epigenetics, intercellular communication, and metabolic reprogramming (Fig. 1). Core signaling pathways, including the wingless/integrated (WNT)/β-catenin, bone morphogenetic protein (BMP), phosphatidylinositol-3 kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/AKT), mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), adenosine 5‘-monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK), nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB), Notch and Rho/Rho-associated coiled-coil containing protein kinase (Rho/ROCK) signaling pathways, are closely involved in the development and progression of VC. Oxidative stress, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, and calcium-phosphate imbalance further exacerbate this process by promoting mitochondrial dysfunction and inflammatory cytokine secretion-mediated mineralization.15,16 Epigenetic regulators, such as noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs) and posttranslational modifications (PTMs), add another layer of complexity by regulating gene expression and chromatin accessibility.17,18 Clinically, these mechanisms underscore the necessity to develop targeted therapies, with emerging strategies focusing on disrupting pro-calcific signaling pathways. This review synthesizes current insights into the molecular pathogenesis of VC, explores signaling crosstalk, and evaluates translational opportunities to address this unmet clinical challenge.

Pathophysiological processes of vascular calcification and its associated diseases. Multiple complex mechanisms are involved in the formation and development of VC, including inflammation, oxidative stress, epigenetics, phenotypic transformation of VSMCs, EndMT, calcium-phosphorus imbalance, programmed cell death, endoplasmic reticulum stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and extracellular vesicles. VC leads to thickening, hardening, and decreased elasticity of the vessel wall, which in turn leads to luminal narrowing. A variety of diseases are strongly associated with VC, including cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, cerebrovascular disease, peripheral vascular disease, and diabetes mellitus. Cellular and molecular mechanisms of vascular calcification

VC is not passive calcium deposition but an active, cell‑driven pathological process governed by coordinated cellular responses and molecular regulators and represents an actively regulated program characterized by vascular wall remodeling, aberrant cellular phenotypic transformation, and microenvironmental disturbance. Key events include phenotypic transformation of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT), fibroblast activation, ECM remodeling, oxidative stress, ER stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammation, and programmed cell death, all of which are tightly modulated by critical molecular regulators. This chapter focuses on the core cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying these processes.

Cellular phenotypic remodeling and dysfunction

Phenotypic transformation of VSMCs

VSMCs, the primary cells in the arterial media, display remarkable plasticity and can adopt diverse phenotypes that are crucial for vascular homeostasis and pathological processes (Fig. 2).19

The MYOCD coactivator and SRF transcription factor bind to CArG-rich cis-regulatory elements within the promoter and enhancer regions of contractile genes, forming the CArG/SRF/MYOCD transcriptional complex that upregulates the expression of canonical VSMC contractile markers, including α-SMA, SM22α, SM-MHC, and CNN1. This process is regulated by PTMs, including H3Ac, H4Ac, and histone H3K4me2, which promote the assembly of this transcriptional complex and drive the selective activation of contractile gene expression. Disruption of this complex results in the downregulation of contractile gene expression. Under pathological conditions, differentiated contractile VSMCs undergo phenotypic transformation to generate a spectrum of functionally heterogeneous VSMC subtypes. This transformation occurs via two core pathways: (1) the direct conversion pathway, in which contractile VSMCs directly undergo dedifferentiation into distinct functional phenotypes (e.g., osteochondrogenic-like VSMCs, adipocyte-like VSMCs); and (2) the indirect intermediate pathway, in which contractile VSMCs first transform to a dedifferentiated intermediate state (e.g., SEM cells, mesenchymal-like VSMCs, Lgals3+ VSMCs) before further transformation into downstream effector phenotypes. Under physiological conditions, healthy blood vessels are composed of “differentiated” contractile VSMCs, which are located in the ECM rich in collagen and elastin. These cells exhibit low proliferation activity and minimal organelles20 and express markers such as α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), smooth muscle 22α (SM22α), and smooth muscle myosin heavy chain (SM-MHC) to maintain vascular tone and structural integrity.21 This contractile phenotype, which is the primary phenotype for VSMCs to perform their normal physiological functions, is regulated by multiple mechanisms. Among them, the serum response factor (SRF)/myocardin (MYOCD) complex, which binds to the CC(A/T-rich)6GG cis-regulating element (CArG) in the promoter region of contractile genes, plays a core regulatory role in maintaining the expression of contractile genes.22 VSMC-specific factors, such as the long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) CARMN, can stabilize MYOCD to preserve contractile function, while phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) enhances SRF/CArG binding.23,24 Actin dynamics further regulate this process through the RhoA/ROCK signaling pathway, promoting the translocation of MYOCD-related transcription factors (MRTFs) into the nucleus.24,25 In addition, GATA-binding protein (GATA) 6 is a zinc finger transcription factor expressed in VSMCs that can promote the transcriptional activity of contractile genes, although it is not essential.26 Knockdown of GATA-6 alone can reduce the abundance of contractile proteins in VSMCs to 60% below the baseline level.27

However, when exposed to pathological cell-matrix interactions, contractile VSMCs undergo a phenotypic transformation into “dedifferentiated” synthetic VSMCs (noncontractile VSMCs). These synthetic VSMCs exhibit enhanced proliferation and migration capabilities, downregulate the expression of contractile markers,28 and upregulate the expression of the markers osteopontin (OPN) and CD68. Meanwhile, these dedifferentiated synthetic VSMCs produce extracellular vesicles (EVs) that secrete lipids, RNA, and proteins and induce the upregulation of various osteogenic and chondrogenic markers, thereby promoting VC.28,29 In addition, they also possess vascular repair functions, which is further confirmed by the upregulated ECM remodeling proteins, including collagen I and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs),30,31 which contribute to tissue remodeling.

The phenotypic transformation of VSMCs toward an osteochondrogenic phenotype is a key step in VC, driven by the dysregulation of osteogenic and anti-osteogenic signaling pathways. Osteochondrogenic-like VSMCs drive VC by upregulating RUNX2 and releasing calcium phosphate-rich vesicles as nucleation sites for hydroxyapatite crystals.32 Notably, this transformation is not limited to the expression of osteogenic markers, including RUNX2, alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and Msh homeobox 2 (MSX2) but also includes chondrogenic characteristics, namely, the upregulated expression of chondrocyte-specific markers, including type II collagen, type X collagen, and SRY-box transcription factor 9 (SOX9).33,34 The coexpression of these mixed osteogenic and chondrogenic markers confirms that the phenotypic transformation of VSMCs follows the trajectory of osteochondral tissue differentiation rather than a linear, single lineage. While osteoblast-like and chondrocyte-like VSMCs share common markers such as ALP and OPN, chondrogenic differentiation is distinctively characterized by the formation of cartilaginous matrices and dependence on SOX9.33,34 Notably, chondrocytes are not always detectable in calcified blood vessels. The process of VC also involves an intramembranous ossification-like pattern, where VSMCs directly undergo calcification and form osteoid cells.35 This complexity explains why chondrogenic markers are preferentially detected in calcified blood vessels: VSMCs first transform into a chondrocyte-like phenotype, which is morphologically similar to hypertrophic chondrocytes in the growth plate. These cells are embedded in cartilaginous tissue and then transform into osteochondrogenic-like VSMCs, ultimately completing mineralization.

In addition, VSMCs can also transform into other phenotypes, contributing to the formation of VC. Mesenchymal-like VSMCs may originate from damaged contractile VSMCs and perivascular SCA1+ stem cells, which possess stem cell properties and migrate to vascular injury sites to promote neointimal hyperplasia and injury repair.36 Additionally, two VSMC phenotypes with stem cell characteristics have been identified in atherosclerosis: the “pioneer” cell phenotype and SEM cells, composed of stem cells, endothelial cells (ECs) and monocyte characteristics, which are regulated by the pluripotency genes Klf4 and Oct4. Their abnormal activation promotes late-stage atherosclerotic lesion progression.37,38 Interestingly, although fibroblast-like VSMCs exacerbate arterial fibrosis and sclerosis, they may exert a protective effect in atherosclerosis through transcription factor (TCF) 21,39 preventing vascular rupture. Hypercholesterolemia can induce the formation of macrophage-like VSMCs, which show limited phagocytic capacity and are less efficient than professional macrophages.40 Although these cells express galectin-3 (Lgals3)41,42 and proinflammatory cytokines, they lack bona fide antigen-presenting capacity. This functional defect is consistent with their limited phagocytic capacity, indicating that they may represent a transitional state rather than an independent lineage.37,43 During atherosclerosis, nearly half of the foam cells originate from macrophage-like VSMCs rather than monocytes/macrophages,38 thus playing a key role in the development of atherosclerotic plaques. Under adipogenic stimulation, VSMCs transform into adipocyte-like VSMCs and express lipid regulators (FASN and SREBP1) in atherosclerotic plaques,44 leading to lipid accumulation.45,46 In conclusion, these phenotypic transformations under metabolic or inflammatory stress highlight how VSMCs adapt to drive vascular diseases, such as atherosclerosis, by altering ECM structure and promoting lipid accumulation as well as inflammatory and calcific processes.

Endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition

Healthy ECs not only form a continuous physical barrier in the cardiovascular system but also play a crucial role in maintaining vascular tone, angiogenesis, and hemostasis. In fact, similar to VSMCs, ECs are also involved in the formation of VC, and this consistency may be attributed to their close proximity to each other in arteries. In VC associated with highly BMP‑activated diseases, ECs can directly give rise to osteoprogenitor cells.47

Upon exposure to pathological stimuli, ECs can undergo a similar dedifferentiation process to transform into mesenchymal cells (MCs), namely, EndMT. This is a dynamic process in which ECs lose tight junctions and their characteristic marker (vascular endothelial cadherin)48 while simultaneously exhibiting enhanced motility, increased secretion of ECM proteins, and mesenchymal-like proliferation, migration, and collagen synthesis.48,49 This process leads to the generation of cells with osteogenic potential, which can contribute to VC progression. Lineage tracing has shown that labeled ECs in calcified blood vessels express osteogenic markers, which further confirms their osteogenic role.50 Therefore, EndMT may be an intermediate process between VC and endothelial dysfunction, playing an important role in the early stage of VC. Evidence indicates that BMPs activate EndMT. In particular, BMP2 overexpression promotes EndMT by inducing serine protease activity, driving the migration of endothelial-derived MCs to calcification-prone vascular walls and their transformation into osteoblast-like cells.51,52 BMPs are inhibited by matrix Gla protein (MGP), and MGP deficiency can lead to progressive VC and EC abnormalities.51 Inflammatory factor-mediated downregulation of BMP receptor (BMPR) 2 reduces c-JUN N-terminal kinase (JNK) signaling but amplifies BMP9-induced osteogenic differentiation,52 suggesting that the BMPR2/JNK signaling pathway is an important route for regulating inflammation-induced EndMT and promoting VC. In addition, transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) signaling, via small mothers against decapentaplegic (Smad)-dependent and Smad-independent pathways, activates transcription factors (Snail, Twist1) to suppress endothelial markers and enhance mesenchymal traits.53 While hyperglycemia activates Snail to drive ECs toward chondrogenic phenotypes,54 Notch and advanced glycation end products (AGEs)/receptor for AGEs (RAGE) signaling collaborate with TGF-β to promote EndMT in valvular calcification.55,56 Exosomes rich in stromal proteins vimentin and N-cadherin, isolated from hemodialysis (HD) patients, can induce EndMT in ECs and promote the significant upregulation of BMP2, BMPR2, BMP4, BMP9, and RUNX2, which confirms the role of exosomes in EndMT.57

In summary, upon exposure to pathological stimuli such as hyperphosphatemia, ECs undergo EndMT, transforming into osteoblast-like cells, promoting ECM calcium deposition, and inducing VC.58 This process is particularly important in intimal calcification, which contributes to the identification of strategies and implementation of targeted therapies for delaying or preventing the progression of intimal calcification.

Fibroblast activation

Fibroblast proliferation within the vascular wall is another critical process in VC. Fibroblasts, normally quiescent in healthy vascular tissue, become activated and proliferate in response to injury or pathological stimuli.59 This activation is driven by growth factors such as platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) and fibroblast growth factor (FGF), which stimulate cell cycle progression and the synthesis of ECM components. Activated fibroblasts synthesize and secrete matrix proteins such as collagen types I and III, fibronectin, and proteoglycans. These proteins not only provide a structural scaffold for cell adhesion and growth but also interact with mineral ions to promote the deposition of hydroxyapatite crystals, facilitate ECM remodeling, and drive the phenotypic transformation of VSMCs.60 In addition, the ECM proteins secreted by activated fibroblasts can also promote the phenotypic transformation of VSMCs, forming a synergistic effect with ECs and VSMCs to accelerate VC.

Key molecular regulatory networks

Endoplasmic reticulum stress

ER stress drives VC through integrated activation of three unfolded protein response (UPR) pathways: protein kinase R-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK)/eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2α (eIF2α)/activating transcription factor (ATF) 4, inositol-requiring enzyme 1α (IRE1α)/X-box binding protein 1 (XBP1) and ATF6. These pathways disrupt protein folding homeostasis, triggering UPR-mediated transcriptional reprogramming that promotes osteochondrogenic transformation of VSMCs, apoptotic cell death and matrix vesicle (MV) release.61,62

The PERK/eIF2α/ATF4 axis phosphorylates eIF2α to transiently reduce global protein synthesis while selectively upregulating ATF4. Once activated, ATF4 drives two critical pro-calcific cascades: it induces C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP), which shifts the apoptotic balance by downregulating anti-apoptotic B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL-2) and upregulating pro-apoptotic Bim, leading to caspase-12-mediated apoptosis and the release of calcification-nucleating apoptotic bodies. In addition, ATF4 directly upregulates RUNX2, promoting osteogenic marker expression (BMP2, OPN) and downregulating contractile markers (SM22α).63

In the IRE1α/XBP1 pathway, the enzyme IRE1α becomes activated and splices XBP1u (inactive, unspliced form) to XBP1s (active, spliced variant). This active XBP1s then translocates to the nucleus, where it acts as a transcription factor to trigger the production of ER chaperones that refold misfolded proteins and restore ER homeostasis. This active XBP1s also induces autophagy regulators such as Beclin-1, which promotes the removal of damaged cellular components to alleviate stress.64 However, prolonged IRE1α activation also triggers JNK phosphorylation and promotes the secretion of inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin (IL)-6 and IL-8. It also induces MMP-mediated ECM degradation that facilitates hydroxyapatite deposition.65

The ATF6 pathway further amplifies VC by translocating ATF6 to the Golgi apparatus, where it is cleaved by site-1 protease and site-2 protease. This cleavage releases the active form of ATF6, which not only upregulates XBP1 but also induces components of the ER-associated degradation (ERAD) system. These ERAD components further accelerate the degradation of misfolded proteins while promoting the osteochondrogenic transformation of VSMCs.66,67 These UPR axes intricately regulate VSMC phenotypic transformation, enhance apoptotic vesicle release, and remodel the ECM, creating a pro-mineralization microenvironment.

Mitochondrial dysfunction

Mitochondrial dysfunction, characterized by excessive fission and impaired mitophagy, disrupts structural integrity and respiratory chain function, exacerbating oxidative stress.68 This process is initiated by nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate hydrogen (NADPH) oxidase (NOX)-derived reactive oxygen species (ROS), particularly from NOX1/NOX5 complexes containing p22phox, p47phox, and p67phox subunits. ROS induce mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) damage, promote dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1)-dependent mitochondrial fragmentation, and trigger mitochondrial permeability transition pore opening, disrupting ATP/ADP balance and triggering cytochrome c release to activate caspase-9/caspase-3 apoptotic pathways (Fig. 3).69 In addition, ROS activate NF-κB and JNK signaling through tumor necrosis factor (TNF) receptor-associated factor 2 (TRAF2), amplifying inflammatory cytokine secretion and MMP-mediated ECM degradation.

Regulation of endoplasmic reticulum stress, oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction in vascular calcification. Oxidative stress, ER stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction are core interconnected drivers of VC. NOX is a major membrane-bound source of ROS. Pathological stimuli trigger the assembly and activation of the NOX complex, consisting of membrane subunits NOX and p22phox, cytosolic regulatory subunits p47phox, p67phox, p40phox, and the GTP-bound small GTPase Rac. Activated NOX catalyzes ROS generation using NADPH as an electron donor. The ER and mitochondria are the primary intracellular sources of ROS. Elevated intracellular ROS trigger ER stress and activation of the UPR, which is mediated by three canonical branches: (1) the ATF6 pathway; (2) the IRE1 pathway; and (3) the PERK pathway. ATF4 further upregulates CHOP, a key mediator of pro-apoptotic signaling. ER stress also promotes Ca²⁺ release from the ER lumen, which is taken up by mitochondria to further stimulate mitochondrial ROS production, forming a vicious cycle between ER stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. Mitochondrial ROS drive multiple aspects of mitochondrial dysfunction, including mtDNA damage, functional abnormalities of the ETC, ATP/ADP ratio imbalance, dysregulated mitochondrial fusion/fission, and opening of the mPTP. mPTP opening triggers cytochrome c release into the cytoplasm, which sequentially activates caspase-9 and caspase-3, ultimately initiating apoptosis. Moreover, mitochondrial dysfunction leads to the depletion of endogenous antioxidants, which further exacerbates ROS accumulation and oxidative stress. Calcium and phosphate overload in VSMCs intensifies mitochondrial damage, driving VC. Moreover, iron overload exacerbates this injury through Fenton reactions, including mitochondrial permeability transition pore opening, membrane depolarization, and lipid peroxidation, which synergize with ferroptosis to promote calcification.70 Iron dysregulation impairs electron transport chain function, while mitochondrial-derived ROS destabilize iron regulatory proteins, creating a vicious cycle that amplifies oxidative damage.70

Oxidative stress

Oxidative stress is derived from an imbalance between ROS overproduction and insufficient antioxidant defenses, directly contributing to phenotypic transformation of VSMCs and calcium phosphate deposition (Fig. 3).67 NOX is a membrane protein that plays a key driving role in oxidative stress. It can reduce molecular oxygen to superoxide anions through an NADPH-dependent single-electron reduction process, serving as a major source of ROS in the body. NOX proteins themselves exhibit little catalytic activity and require association with multiple subunits, including p22phox, p40phox, p47phox, and p67phox, to form a complex for functional activation. In addition to catalyzing ROS formation and indirectly regulating the phenotypic transformation of VSMCs, studies have demonstrated that NOX1 drives the transformation of VSMCs to the synthetic phenotype by enhancing cell migration and proliferation,71,72 whereas NOX4 generally supports the maintenance of the contractile phenotype.72 These roles may vary depending on the specific pathological microenvironment.

Elevated ROS levels activate pro-calcific signaling pathways, including NF-κB and BMP2/RUNX2, which drive phenotypic transformation of VSMCs.73 Mitochondrial dysfunction exacerbates oxidative damage by impairing respiratory chain complexes. This not only enhances ROS generation but also reduces ATP production, accelerating the release of MVs and the nucleation of hydroxyapatite crystals.69,74 In addition, the interplay between oxidative stress and disrupted iron homeostasis further aggravates lipid peroxidation and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) inactivation of ferroptosis in VC.75 These mechanisms highlight oxidative stress as a central mediator of VC progression through multiple redox-dependent molecular crosstalk.

Inflammation

Sustained chronic inflammation can damage the vascular wall. Injured vascular ECs or VSMCs release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), recruit inflammatory cells to the vascular wall, and initiate inflammatory cascades to release a large number of inflammatory factors (ILs and TNF-α),76 thereby regulating VC (Fig. 4). Additionally, it promotes the formation of VC through a combination of effects, including altering the ECM microenvironment, inducing oxidative stress, and disrupting calcium-phosphate metabolic balance. These integrated processes highlight inflammation as a key target for VC treatment.

Inflammation-induced signaling pathways in vascular calcification. Proinflammatory cytokines, including ILs and TNF-α, are central drivers of VC. They activate multiple interconnected pro-calcific signaling cascades upon binding to their respective membrane receptors. Both ILs and TNF-α activate the canonical NF-κB pathway: receptor ligation triggers assembly of the IKK complex, which phosphorylates IκB to release the p50/p65/c-Rel transcription factor complex into the nucleus, driving transcription of pro-calcific and pro-inflammatory genes. Meanwhile, both cytokines promote phosphorylation and activation of NOX complex subunits, mediating NADPH-dependent ROS production to accelerate VC. For ILR signaling, ILs trigger GEF-mediated RAC1 activation. Activated RAC1 further amplifies NF-κB signaling, promotes NOX complex assembly, and activates the RAS/RAF/MEK1/2/ERK1/2 cascade, which upregulates the transcription factor c-FOS to drive pro-calcific gene expression. For TNFR signaling, TNF-α activates two additional signaling branches: the TAK1-MKK3/6-p38 MAPK axis, which further enhances NF-κB activation, and the cAMP-PKA signaling axis, which directly promotes VC progression. Interleukins

Multiple ILs play pleiotropic roles in VC through intricate interactions with inflammatory and osteogenic signaling pathways. Pro-calcific ILs include IL-1β, which activates the NF-κB/p53/p21 axis to induce VSMC senescence and phenotypic transformation. This process is associated with the upregulation of BMP2 and RUNX2 and the suppression of the contractile markers SM22α and α-SMA.77 In atherosclerotic calcification, RAC1, a Rho family GTPase, collaborates with IL-1β signaling to upregulate RUNX2 and ALP expression.78 Moreover, RAC1-mediated NOX activation generates ROS, further contributing to the process of VC.79 Similarly, IL-6 binds to soluble IL-6 receptor to activate the p53/p21 pathway in senescence-associated VC.80 Moreover, elevated serum levels of IL-6 correlate with increased cardiovascular mortality in HD patients.81 TNF-α promotes VC by inducing extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK)/activator protein-1 (AP-1)/cellular proto-oncogene FOS (c-FOS)-mediated IL-6 expression. It also further upregulates IL-1β and NOX to enhance ROS generation and downstream signaling in CKD models.82 IL-18 enhances the transient receptor potential melastatin 7 (TRPM7)/ERK1/2 signaling pathway, upregulating osteogenic markers and calcium deposition in VSMCs.83 In addition, IL-29 promotes VC through Janus kinase (JAK) 2/signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) 3-mediated BMP2 upregulation in human calcified arteries.84

Conversely, some ILs play a protective role. IL-24 suppresses the WNT/β-catenin pathway and phenotypic transformation. This process is particularly effective in iron-overloaded environments, where it attenuates ferroptosis-associated calcification.85 IL-10 and IL-37, as protective cytokines, inhibit NLRP3 inflammasome activation and osteogenic differentiation.86,87 IL-4 exhibits biphasic effects: short-term IL-4-mediated STAT6 activation increases osteoprotegerin (OPG) production, but long-term exposure downregulates OPG and promotes phenotypic transformation of VSMCs.88

Tumor necrosis factor-α

TNF-α, a proinflammatory cytokine with cytotoxic effects, mediates systemic inflammation and contributes to VC through multiple signaling pathways. TNF-α promotes the phenotypic transformation of VSMCs by inhibiting TGF-β, thereby upregulating osteogenic markers and calcium deposition. Beyond canonical ERK and NF-κB activation, TNF-α regulates VC through cAMP-dependent mechanisms. TNF-α increases intracellular cAMP levels to enhance DNA binding of osteoblast-specific factor (Osf2), AP-1, and CREB to drive phenotypic transformation of VSMCs in vitro.89 In inflammatory microenvironments, M1-type macrophages secrete TNF-α, which induces carbonic anhydrase I/II expression in VSMCs to facilitate calcium carbonate formation.90 This macrophage-VSMC interaction is critical for hydroxyapatite nucleation, distinguishing M1-driven calcification from M0/M2 phenotypes. Overall, TNF-α and ILs collaborate to orchestrate chronic inflammatory responses in VC. These findings underscore the complexity of inflammatory networks in VC pathogenesis and highlight TNF-α as a critical therapeutic target.

Programmed cell death

Programmed cell death, including autophagy, ferroptosis, apoptosis, and pyroptosis, constitutes a key regulatory network of VC. In recent years, crosstalk regulation among different modes of programmed cell death has become a research focus. These complex crosstalk effects regulate the occurrence and development of VC through multiple pathways, providing possibilities for exploring new therapeutic targets.

Autophagy

Autophagy plays a dual regulatory role in VC. Under physiological conditions, autophagy regulates VSMC phenotypic plasticity, preventing the transition from a contractile state to an osteochondrogenic phenotype characterized by upregulated markers, such as RUNX2, ALP and MV release.91 Elevated phosphate levels induce autophagy to counteract oxidative stress and apoptosis in VSMCs, thereby reducing calcifying MV secretion and hydroxyapatite deposition.92 Autophagy also regulates intracellular calcium flux by degrading dysfunctional mitochondria via mitophagy, thereby preventing mitochondrial calcium overload, permeability transition pore opening and subsequent apoptosis.93 Furthermore, MVs, which are abundant in phosphatidylethanolamine and lysosomal proteins, facilitate calcium phosphate nucleation by providing a membrane-bound microenvironment conducive to hydroxyapatite formation.94 Impaired mitophagy accumulates calcium-loaded mitochondria-derived vesicles, while enhancing BCL-2/adenovirus E1B 19 kDa protein-interacting protein 3-mediated mitophagy attenuates calcification.93 In AGE‑induced human VSMCs, autophagy and apoptosis are activated, and the calcification process is upregulated. Notably, inhibition of autophagy at this stage attenuates both calcification and apoptosis, demonstrating the crosstalk between autophagy and apoptosis as well as the pro‑calcifying role of autophagy.95 These findings underscore autophagy as a central regulatory pathway in VC, balancing cellular stress responses and mineral homeostasis.

Ferroptosis

Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent programmed cell death mechanism, is driven by mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress, where palmitic acid-induced lipotoxicity exacerbates mtDNA damage, leading to cytoplasmic DNA leakage.96 The released mtDNA activates the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (CGAS)/stimulator of interferon (INF) genes 1 (STING1) signaling pathway, initiating autophagy-dependent ferroptosis characterized by nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4)-mediated degradation of ferritin heavy chain and subsequent iron overload.96,97 This iron accumulation amplifies lipid peroxidation via the Fenton reaction, triggering VSMC death and osteochondrogenic transformation. In addition, mitochondrial dysfunction reduces ATP production, elevates ROS and impairs membrane potential, further destabilizing cellular homeostasis.96 Notably, activation of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) α counteracts these effects by restoring mitochondrial integrity, suppressing CGAS/STING1 signaling, and inhibiting ferritinophagy, thereby mitigating ferroptosis and calcification.98

Apoptosis

Apoptosis is a genetically regulated and ordered form of programmed cell death that aims to eliminate damaged, senescent, or abnormal cells in the body to maintain tissue homeostasis and physiological balance. Apoptosis occurs prior to VC, and under pathological stimuli, VSMCs undergo apoptosis and generate apoptotic bodies with a structure similar to MVs, which form calcification nuclei to promote VC. In contrast, inhibition of apoptosis attenuates VC, which confirms the critical role of apoptosis in VC.99,100 Further studies have revealed that apoptotic bodies derived from VSMCs accumulate calcium through their intact membranes. When these bodies expose phosphatidylserine, potential calcium-binding sites and membrane surfaces suitable for hydroxyapatite deposition are formed. In the early stage, phagocytes can recognize and phagocytose apoptotic bodies within calcified nodules. However, as apoptotic bodies accumulate and the phagocytic efficiency of phagocytes within the nodules decreases, apoptotic bodies promote the formation of VC.101 Furthermore, AGE-induced VC and apoptosis were suppressed upon inhibition of autophagy, suggesting a possible interaction between autophagy and apoptosis in VC.95 The antioxidant mitoquinone (MitoQ) can inhibit oxidative stress by regulating the Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1)/nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor (Nrf2) pathway and reduce the expression of the pro-apoptotic factor Bax, thereby inhibiting the apoptosis of VSMCs and exerting a role in suppressing VC.102 It is certain that apoptosis plays a critical role in VC, and inhibiting apoptosis can alleviate VC. However, there are relatively few studies on the regulation of VC by EC apoptosis.

Pyroptosis

Pyroptosis is an inflammatory form of programmed cell death that can trigger intense inflammatory responses and regulate cellular phenotypic transformation, whose core mechanism relies on the activation of inflammasomes (such as NLRP3) and the synergistic effect of downstream effector molecules.103 In the process of β-glycerophosphate (β-GP)-induced calcification of mouse VSMCs, the expression of pyroptosis-related proteins (cleaved caspase-1, GSDMD-N, and IL-1β) is upregulated, accompanied by pyroptotic cell death, indicating that pyroptosis is involved in this process.104 Further studies have revealed that NLRP3 activates the pyroptotic pathway, while irisin can inhibit this process to alleviate VC. As a key hub for regulating pyroptosis, the activation, inhibition, and interactions of the NLRP3 inflammasome are crucial in the regulation of VC. Trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), a metabolite of intestinal flora, promotes the phenotypic differentiation of VSMCs by activating the NLRP3 and NF-κB signaling pathways.105 In contrast, the inhibitory effect of puerarin and sinomenine can reduce the production of Caspase-1 and IL-1β, thereby alleviating VC.106,107 These findings emphasize the critical role of NLRP3 and pyroptosis in VC. Under a high-glucose environment, NLRP3 can be activated, with increased release of caspase-1 and IL-1β, thereby promoting VC.108 When the concentration of ROS rises, thioredoxin (Trx)-interacting protein (TXNIP), a protein associated with insulin resistance, dissociates from Trx and then binds to the leucine-rich repeat domain of NLRP3, which in turn activates NLRP3. This leads to increased release of caspase-1 and IL-1β, inducing pyroptosis; however, TXNIP deficiency inhibits this process,109 which indicates the interaction mechanism between oxidative stress and pyroptosis in a high-glucose environment.

Important regulators of vascular calcification

Alkaline phosphatase

ALP, first discovered in 1923, is a ubiquitous membrane-bound glycoprotein that rapidly hydrolyzes hexose phosphates to inorganic phosphate.110 In humans, four major ALP isozymes have been identified, among which tissue-nonspecific ALP (TNAP) is the most prevalent isozyme, predominantly expressed in the liver, kidney and bone.111 Among these, bone-type TNAP acts as a core pro-mineralizing enzyme in VC. It not only hydrolyzes inorganic pyrophosphate (PPi), a key mineralization inhibitor in the vascular wall, to relieve its inhibitory effect on hydroxyapatite crystal formation but also hydrolyzes phosphomonoesters to generate inorganic phosphate.112 These two processes synergistically drive MV-mediated nucleation and deposition of hydroxyapatite crystals. Furthermore, TNAP modulates the pro-calcific microenvironment of the vascular wall and regulates the mineralization function and release of MVs, thereby mediating VC progression.113 These properties underscore TNAP as a critical biomarker of VC, and elevated TNAP levels correlate with an increased risk of VC.114 Preclinical studies support its therapeutic potential, and inhibition of TNAP activity mitigates VC progression in CKD mouse models,115 highlighting TNAP targeting as a promising strategy for VC intervention.

Krüppel-like factor 4

Krüppel-like factor 4 (KLF4), a Sp1/Krüppel-like zinc-finger transcription factor,116 is induced by vascular injury, inflammatory factors and oxidative stress and acts as a transcriptional target of BMP2/4/6 and TGF-β1.117 Structurally, it contains a C-terminal DNA-binding domain and an N-terminal transcription regulatory domain that regulate gene expression.118 KLF4 promotes phenotypic transformation of VSMCs by inhibiting contractile markers (via inhibition of the CArG/SRF/MYOCD pathway).119 It is negatively regulated by microRNAs (miRNAs), such as miR-135a and miR-92b-3p, which reduce KLF4 expression levels to attenuate VC.120,121 In addition, KLF4 interacts with RUNX2 to amplify homocysteine-induced RUNX2 activity, accelerating VC progression.122

Matrix gla protein

MGP is a potent vitamin K-dependent inhibitor of VC that is synthesized by VSMCs, chondrocytes, and endothelial cells to exert its anti-calcific activity.123 Vitamin K serves as an essential cofactor for γ-glutamyl carboxylase, which mediates the posttranslational γ-carboxylation of glutamate (Glu) residues in MGP to form calcium-binding γ-carboxyglutamic acid (Gla), thereby activating its anti-calcific function.124 This modification is not only a prerequisite for MGP to acquire calcium-binding capacity and exert anti-calcific effects but also a common essential step for the biological function of all vitamin K-dependent proteins. Accordingly, vitamin K is currently being investigated as a potential therapeutic strategy for VC. In addition, phosphorylation is another important posttranslational modification that regulates MGP function by further enhancing its stability and inhibitory activity.125 Through different combinations, these two modifications form multiple modified phenotypes of MGP, which exhibit varying inhibitory effects on VC. Among them, the fully carboxylated and phosphorylated MGP (pcMGP) displays the highest calcification-inhibitory activity owing to its strongest calcium-binding capacity.126

In specific patient populations, including those with T2DM, CKD, vitamin K deficiency or impaired utilization directly reduces γ-carboxylation and functional MGP levels, thereby promoting VC.127,128 Therefore, the measurement of functional and nonfunctional MGP can serve as a reliable biomarker for reflecting systemic vitamin K status, cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk and the severity of VC.129

Msh homeobox 2

MSX2, the vertebrate homolog of the Drosophila muscle segment homeobox gene, contains a highly conserved homeodomain composed of ~60 amino acids. MSX2 is induced by BMP2, and this induction is synergistically enhanced by Notch signaling. Notably, through this process, MSX2 can indirectly regulate RUNX2 activity and upregulate WNT ligands and promote β-catenin nuclear translocation to enhance osteogenic gene expression to orchestrate phenotypic transformation and subsequent VC processes in VSMCs.130,131 Targeted reduction of MSX1 and MSX2 expression in the aortas of low-density lipoprotein (LDL) receptor (LDLR)-deficient mice fed a diabetogenic diet effectively reduces aortic VC, accompanied by decreased expression of multiple WNT ligands, further validating the pro-VC role of MSX2.132 Additionally, MSX2-expressing cells establish a pro-VC microenvironment via paracrine WNT signaling to regulate VC progression.132 Collectively, MSX2 serves as a critical intermediate factor in pro-VC signaling, particularly by modulating the WNT signaling pathway.

Osterix

Osx, a transcription factor first identified in 2002, is essential for osteoblast differentiation and bone formation, with high specificity to osteogenic cells.133 Structurally, Osx contains a C-terminal DNA-binding domain with three C2H2-type zinc fingers for binding GC-rich promoters and an N-terminal proline-rich region that represses WNT signaling.134 It integrates multiple signaling pathways; specifically, BMP2 upregulates Osx via the PI3K/AKT and Smad signaling pathways,135 while the MAPK pathway independently enhances Osx expression, regardless of upstream RUNX2 activity.136

RUNX2

RUNX2, a transcription factor initially identified for its role in bone development, is a central regulator of VC by driving the phenotypic transformation of VSMCs and VC. Genetically, the RUNX2 gene spans 223 kb on human chromosome 6p21.1,137 encoding two isoforms (RUNX2-I and RUNX2-II) via alternative promoters.138 Structurally, the protein contains a runt homology domain for DNA binding to osteoblast-specific elements (OSEs),139 three transcriptional activation domains (AD1-AD3)140 and regulatory motifs for nuclear localization and matrix remodeling.141

In VC, RUNX2 expression positively correlates with disease severity, as demonstrated by knockdown models that suppress phenotypic transformation of VSMCs and hydroxyapatite deposition.142 As a central regulator, RUNX2 integrates signaling pathways, including WNT/β-catenin, BMP/Smad, and PI3K/AKT, which regulate its expression and activity (Fig. 5). Inflammatory and oxidative stress states, such as phosphate overload, usually amplify RUNX2-driven VC. Elevated phosphate induces VSMCs to secrete IL-6 and monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 (MCP1), which in turn activates JAK/STAT signaling and regulates RUNX2 expression.143 Notably, activation of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway suppresses calcification while sustaining inflammation, whereas inhibition of this pathway results in increased calcium deposition and RUNX2 expression. This suggests an endogenous protective feedback mechanism, offering a novel perspective for understanding the relationship between inflammation and VC. PTMs such as acetylation, phosphorylation, and ubiquitination also regulate RUNX2 stability and function. BMP2-induced phosphorylation enhances RUNX2 transcriptional activity, while the Smad ubiquitination regulatory factor 1 (Smurf1)-mediated ubiquitin‒proteasome pathway promotes its degradation.144

Role of RUNX2 in vascular calcification. Upon stimulation, some regulatory factors, such as Wnt3α, Wnt5α, TNF-α, ILs, BMPs, and growth factors, activate key signaling pathways. These signaling pathways, including the Wnt, NF-κB, BMP, and PI3K/AKT pathways, coregulate RUNX2 expression and phenotypic transformation of VSMCs in VC. Wnt3α activates the canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway, while Wnt5α activates noncanonical pathways, such as Wnt/Ror2. TNF-α, ILs, and other related factors are capable of activating the NF-κB signaling pathway. Subsequently, this activation leads to the release of NF-κB dimers from the inhibitory IκB complex, followed by nuclear translocation. BMP regulates VC primarily through the BMP/Smad signaling pathway. Activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway involves the PI3K-mediated phosphorylation of PIP2 to PIP3, leading to AKT activation. Elevated RUNX2 expression drives the transformation of contractile to osteochondrogenic-like phenotypes. In addition, during the formation of atherosclerotic plaques and VC, VSMCs can transition to other phenotypic states, including mesenchymal-like, fibroblast-like, macrophage-like, and adipocyte-like states. Foam cells may originate from VSMCs or macrophages, although it remains unclear whether VSMC-derived foam cells require a macrophage-like phenotype for foam cell formation. Epigenetic regulation in vascular calcification

PTMs and ncRNAs, such as lncRNAs, miRNAs, and circular RNAs (circRNAs), play crucial roles in VC by modulating the structure or function of specific transcription factors.

Posttranslational modifications

PTMs of proteins refer to chemical modifications occurring after translation, regulating protein function and stability through the addition of functional groups, proteolytic cleavage of protein subunits, or degradation of entire proteins. These modifications play a critical role in VC, participating in its formation by regulating key osteogenic factors and influencing signaling pathway functions. Currently, well-studied PTMs include ubiquitination, acetylation, phosphorylation, methylation, O-GlcNAcylation, SUMOylation, carbonylation, and lactylation, collectively forming a complex and interconnected regulatory network.

Ubiquitination, a key PTM, precisely regulates protein degradation through the E1-E2-E3 enzyme cascade, participating in the regulatory network of VC.145 The E3 ubiquitin ligases Smurf1 and neuronal precursor cell-expressed developmentally downregulated 4 (Nedd4) inhibit phenotypic transformation of VSMCs by targeting the degradation of the osteogenic factor RUNX2.146 However, ER stress-induced tribble homolog 3 (TRIB3) promotes autoubiquitination of Smurf1, relieving its inhibition of RUNX2 and forming a pro-VC positive feedback loop.147 During metabolic disorders, AGEs accelerate ubiquitination-mediated degradation of AGER1 by regulating FBXW7 expression, exacerbating AGE accumulation and activating the NF-κB pathway.148 In the WNT pathway, loss of IκKβ reduces β-catenin ubiquitination, leading to its nuclear accumulation and activation of RUNX2,149 while XBP1u and BRCA1/BRCA2-containing complex subunit 36 (BRCC36) finely tune signaling intensity by regulating β-catenin ubiquitination.62,150 Upstream osteogenic factors MSX1/2 activate MDM2 transcription, enhancing MDM2-mediated ubiquitin-dependent degradation of HDAC1 to lift its inhibition on RUNX2.151

Carbonylation is the process by which the amino or imino group in the side chain of an amino acid residue is converted to an aldehyde group or keto group in the presence of an oxygen radical. 4-Hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), a byproduct of lipid peroxidation, induces carbonylation of RUNX2 at Lys176. This modification reduces RUNX2 degradation via the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway without affecting its transcriptional activity, thereby increasing RUNX2 protein levels and promoting VC.152 The mitochondrial enzyme aldehyde dehydrogenase 2 (ALDH2) detoxifies lipid peroxidation products, and its activation and overexpression reduce 4-HNE levels and attenuate VC.15,16 Thus, ALDH2 agonists may represent a therapeutic strategy for VC, although effective agonists remain unidentified, and their therapeutic efficacy requires further validation. Notably, the ALDH2 rs671 variant, prevalent in East Asian populations, impairs ALDH2 activity. Cellular studies have confirmed that reduced or absent ALDH2 activity leads to 4-HNE accumulation and increased RUNX2 expression in VSMCs, suggesting that carbonylation may underlie the genetic predisposition to VC in East Asians.16,152 Further exploration of other mechanisms by which carbonylation regulates VC is warranted.

Acetylation, a common protein modification, is mediated by lysine acetyltransferases (KATs), also known as histone acetyltransferases (HATs), and histone deacetylases (HDACs), which influence nucleosomes by neutralizing charges and altering protein conformations.153 The sirtuin family (SIRT1-7), classified as class III HDACs, utilizes nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD⁺) to remove acetyl groups from lysine residues154 and exerts multiple protective effects. SIRT1 deacetylates the RUNX2 protein or targets the WNT signaling pathway to inhibit VC and regulates ER stress by suppressing deacetylation of PERK at K889.155 SIRT3 inhibits phenotypic transformation of VSMCs by regulating acetylation of NFATc1 in the context of CML-induced VC.156 Meanwhile, capsaicin-induced SIRT6 upregulation suppresses VC by promoting deacetylation-mediated degradation of HIF-1α.157 Ganoderma lucidum spore powder (GLSP) alleviates VC by upregulating SIRT7 to promote deacetylation of Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1) and activate Nrf2-mediated antioxidation.158 In addition, HDAC1, 3, 4, and 6 suppress VC by deacetylating RUNX2 to promote its degradation.159–162 However, ERK/MAPK signaling activated by BMP2 and FGF2 recruits p300, a HAT, and promotes RUNX2 acetylation via peptidyl-prolyl cis-trans isomerase NIMA-interacting 1 (PIN1)-mediated proline isomerization to enhance its stability.144,163,164 Concurrently, p300-mediated acetylation of β-catenin activates the WNT/β-catenin pathway to modulate VC, a process inhibited by the binding of SIRT1 to p300 and influenced by the microenvironment.165,166

Phosphorylation involves the transfer of phosphate groups from ATP/GTP to serine, threonine and tyrosine residues by protein kinases, inducing conformational changes in proteins to regulate their structure and function.167 As a major regulator of VC, RUNX2 is phosphorylated at multiple sites, and phosphorylation at different sites differentially regulates RUNX2 stability, subcellular localization and transcriptional activity, thereby modulating phenotypic differentiation of VSMCs and VC progression.168 The growth hormone-releasing hormone (GHRH) agonist MR409 inhibits VC induced by RUNX2 and ALP by activating the cAMP/protein kinase A (PKA) pathway, suppressing NOX activity to reduce ROS production, and blocking NF-κB phosphorylation.169 Moreover, the crosstalk between phosphorylation and other PTMs is critical for VC regulation. RUNX2 phosphorylation and PIN1-mediated proline isomerization influence each other. In the absence of proline isomerization, phosphorylated RUNX2 is more prone to ubiquitin-mediated degradation rather than acetylation.170 CML promotes NFATc1 acetylation by downregulating SIRT3 while antagonizing focal adhesion kinase (FAK)-induced NFATc1 phosphorylation, coordinately regulating VC.156 Notably, a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs-5 (ADAMTS5) is significantly downregulated during VC. Its overexpression inhibits phenotypic transformation of VSMCs and VC by reducing versican levels in arterial ECM and suppressing integrin β1 and FAK phosphorylation, highlighting the therapeutic potential of ADAMTS5 as a negative regulator of phosphorylation signaling.171

O-GlcNAcylation, an intracellular glycosylation dynamically regulated by O-GlcNAc transferase (OGT) and O-GlcNAcase, modulates VC through crosstalk with phosphorylation and metabolic signals. Dysregulation of this dynamic balance is closely associated with cardiovascular complications in T2DM.172 In diabetic mice, deletion of stromal interaction molecule 1 (STIM1) leads to significantly upregulated OGT activity, increasing intracellular O-GlcNAcylation levels to enhance RUNX2 expression and promote phenotypic transformation of VSMCs.173 Intriguingly, glucose concentration exerts inconsistent effects on RUNX2 expression; specifically, moderate glucose levels enhance its expression via O-GlcNAcylation, whereas high glucose suppresses it.174 O-GlcNAcylation of AKT at Thr430 and Thr479 promotes AKT phosphorylation, exemplifying the complex regulatory relationship between O-GlcNAcylation and phosphorylation pathways.175 Additionally, OGT drives VC progression via RUNX2-independent pathways. In CKD-related VC, OGT activates the WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway by regulating β-catenin, increasing the transcriptional activity of target genes by 2.3-fold to drive VC progression.176

SUMOylation, a reversible PTM, precisely regulates protein function by forming an isopeptide bond between small ubiquitin-like modifiers (SUMO) and the ε-NH₂ group of lysine residues in target proteins. Unlike ubiquitination, SUMOylation employs a single E2 conjugating enzyme, ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme 9 (Ubc9), and typically does not induce protein degradation.177 The AMPKα1 signaling pathway and SUMOylation exhibit intricate cross-regulation. Activated AMPKα1 phosphorylates protein inhibitor of activated STAT (PIAS) 1 at Ser510, significantly enhancing its activity as a SUMO E3 ligase for RUNX2. This process promotes RUNX2 SUMOylation, which accelerates its nuclear export and degradation, thereby inhibiting the phenotypic transformation of VSMCs.178 Additionally, lncRNAs play a critical role in regulating SUMOylation. For instance, lncRNA SNHG1 enhances the SUMOylation of the transcription factor basic helix-loop-helix family member e40 (Bhlhe40) by facilitating the interaction between the SUMO E3 ligase PIAS3 and Bhlhe40. SUMOylated Bhlhe40 exhibits enhanced nuclear translocation, thereby suppressing the transcription of osteogenic genes and effectively blocking phenotypic transformation of VSMCs.179

Methylation, defined as the enzymatic transfer of methyl groups from active methyl donors (e.g., S-adenosylmethionine, SAM) to nucleic acids or proteins, regulates VC by dynamically modulating osteogenic gene expression and signaling pathway activity through DNA, RNA, and protein methylation.180 DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) methylate cytosine residues in CpG islands. Studies have demonstrated that hypomethylation of the RUNX2 promoter is associated with enhanced phenotypic transformation of mouse MSCs.181 In contrast, the D DNMT inhibitor 5-aza-2’-deoxycytidine (5-aza-dC) promotes high phosphorus-induced VC by upregulating ALP expression and reducing DNA methylation levels at the ALP promoter region.182 Methyltransferase-like (METTL) 3 knockdown in bone marrow MSCs (BMSCs) increases bone loss in mice183; similarly, METTL14 is also involved in the regulation of VC.184 Melatonin treatment of endothelial cells upregulates WTAP expression and promotes m6A methylation of miR-302d-5p, thereby modulating the WNT signaling pathway and inhibiting VC.185 Furthermore, protein arginine methyltransferase 3 (PRMT3) promotes CKD-induced VC by methylating HIF-1α.186

Lactylation, defined as the covalent attachment of lactate to lysine residues to form lactyl groups on proteins, is primarily catalyzed by nuclear acetyltransferases.187 Its regulatory proteins—“Readers” (e.g., Brg1188), “Writers” (e.g., p300187), and “Erasers” (e.g., HDAC1/2/3/8,189,190 SIRT1/2/3191,192) —share extensive regulatory mechanisms with other PTMs, thus warranting further investigation into the potential competitive or inhibitory relationships between lactate secretion and other protein modifications. In hyperglycemia-induced VC models, global lactylation levels in VSMCs are significantly elevated, with notably upregulated lactylation at histone H3 lysine 18 (H3K18). This modification activates CHI3L1 gene expression, which drives the IL-13/IL-13Ra2/JAK1/STAT3 signaling pathway to promote RUNX2 expression.193 The orphan nuclear receptor NR4A3 enhances glycolysis and lactate production to facilitate VC, thereby promoting histone acetylation.194 Notably, in calcified valves, elevated lactate drives LUM-mediated H3 histone lactylation, upregulating RUNX2 expression to accelerate pathological progression.195 The natural product andrographolide exhibits anti-VC potential by targeting lactylation. Mechanistically, it inhibits the key glycolytic enzyme PFK-1 to reduce lactate accumulation in VSMCs, thereby decreasing p300-catalyzed H3 histone lactylation.196

The development of VC relies on the dynamic imbalance and crosstalk of PTMs, including ubiquitination, acetylation, phosphorylation, carbonylation, O-GlcNAcylation, methylation, and lactylation. These modifications form the core epigenetic basis of VC by dynamically regulating the stability of osteogenic transcription factors, the activity of signaling pathways, and the response to metabolic stress. Multiple PTMs exhibit remarkable crosstalk in VC, including sharing modifying enzymes, competing for modification sites, and integrating cross-signals to form a precise regulatory network. Therapeutic interventions should focus on hub nodes of the modification cascade to achieve effective global regulation of the network and exert therapeutic effects. Future research could leverage single-cell modification omics to resolve cellular heterogeneity and develop combined intervention strategies targeting multiple modifications. Notably, race-specific mechanisms should be considered to facilitate the development of precision therapy for VC.

Noncoding RNAs in vascular calcification

Long noncoding RNAs

LncRNAs exert multiple roles in VC through diverse mechanisms. Pro-calcific lncRNAs, such as H19, promote phenotypic transformation of VSMCs by sponging miR-106a-5p and miR-103-3p to upregulate RUNX2 expression.197,198 FAS-AS1 exacerbates hyperphosphatemia-induced VC by promoting oxidative stress.199 MALAT1 promotes the calcification of human VSMCs by sponging miR-30c to regulate RUNX2 expression,200 highlighting the complex crosstalk between lncRNAs and miRNAs in VC. Conversely, protective lncRNAs (e.g., CARMN) maintain VSMC contractility by interacting with cardiac myosin,23 and SNHG29 inhibits the α-Klotho/FGFR1/FGF23 axis by downregulating miR-200b-3p.201 SNHG1 stabilizes Bhlhe40 mRNA to suppress phenotypic transformation of VSMCs,179 whereas ANCR attenuates VC by activating autophagy.202

MicroRNAs

MiRNAs also significantly regulate VC by intricately modulating the phenotypic transformation of VSMCs. Pro-calcific miRNAs, including miR-130b-5p, enhance VC by promoting p300-mediated RUNX2 acetylation,203 while endothelial exosomal miR-670-3p accelerates VC through insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) signaling.204 In a diabetic milieu, high glucose-induced miR-125b-5p inhibits SIRT7 in myeloid cells, thereby promoting VC.205 Conversely, protective miRNAs mitigate VC through multiple mechanisms. MiR-145, miR-378, and miR-302b act as critical negative regulators of VC by suppressing the BMP/Smad signaling pathway.17,18 Triptolide upregulates miR-204 to suppress BMP2 and RUNX2 expression.206 Exosomal miR-204-5p transferred to VSMCs alleviates VC by targeting RUNX2.207 Additionally, curcumin (CCM) exerts protective effects via exosomal miR-92b-3p/KLF4,121 and melatonin attenuates VC through paracrine miR-204/miR-211.208 Interestingly, in young and healthy blood vessels, miR-542-3p inhibits BMP7 expression, thereby maintaining the contractile phenotype of VSMCs. However, during aging, miR-542-3p levels in VSMCs are significantly decreased, leading to upregulated BMP7 expression and subsequent promotion of VC.209 These findings highlight miRNAs as potential diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets, providing novel strategies for gene therapy of VC.

CircRNAs

CircRNAs have recently emerged as novel regulators of VC through miRNA sponging mechanisms. Under high glucose conditions, EC-derived EVs are enriched with circ_0008362. Upon being transferred to VSMCs, circ_0008362 not only upregulates RUNX2 expression by sponging miR-1251-5p but also directly interacts with RUNX2, thereby exacerbating VC.210 In addition, transcriptomic analysis identified circ_TOP1 as a pro-calcific regulator that mediates the phenotypic transformation of VSMCs by targeting PTBP1.211 In addition, circRNAs have been implicated in calcific aortic valve disease (CAVD). Among them, circ_RIC3 can act as a sponge for miR-204-5p to regulate the expression of dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP4), thereby promoting the progression of CAVD. Protective circRNAs include circ_HIPK3, which reduces ALP activity and RUNX2 expression while upregulating contractile markers SM22α and α-SMA by sponging miR-106a-5p.212 Circ_ST6GAL1 acts as a sponge for miR-130b-5p, thereby inhibiting p300-mediated RUNX2 acetylation and attenuating VC.203

Collectively, these studies reveal the bidirectional regulatory properties of ncRNAs (lncRNAs, miRNAs, and circRNAs) in VC, which exert their effects through multiple mechanisms, including posttranscriptional regulation (e.g., miRNA sponging), interaction with PTMs, and intervention in signaling pathways. Although the key functions of multiple ncRNAs in VC have been identified, their specific regulatory roles in different tissues, cell subsets, and disease contexts require further exploration. As emerging epigenetic regulators in VC, circRNAs hold great potential for the development of diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic strategies, but additional research is needed to elucidate their complex crosstalk with miRNAs and lncRNAs. Future studies should focus on developing novel ncRNAs-targeted intervention strategies while systematically evaluating their safety and efficacy in clinical translation.

Signaling pathways and crosstalk in vascular calcification

As discussed earlier, VC is driven by a spectrum of cellular and molecular abnormalities, including phenotypic remodeling, dysregulation of key calcification regulators, stress responses, and inflammatory and programmed cell death. These processes are dynamically controlled by a network of signaling pathways that collectively determine the onset and progression of VC. Multiple canonical cascades mediate the development of VC, including WNT/β‑catenin, BMP/Smad, PI3K/AKT, AMPK, Notch, MAPK, NF‑κB, and Rho/ROCK. These pathways interact with core transcription factors such as RUNX2 to form a comprehensive regulatory network that regulates all stages of VC progression. In this section, we systematically outline the functions of these pathways, their crosstalk mechanisms, and their potential value as therapeutic targets.

Wingless/integrated signaling pathway

The WNT signaling pathway, initially discovered in 1982, comprises 19 secreted glycoproteins ligands and two major branches (canonical and noncanonical) with three subtypes, including the canonical (β-catenin-dependent) pathway, which is pivotal to the pathogenesis of VC.213 This pathway can initiate an osteogenic programme in VSMCs by activating RUNX2, which in turn upregulates the expression of calcification markers, ultimately promoting the phenotypic transformation of VSMCs and ectopic mineralization.214

Crosstalk in the WNT signaling pathway

WNT3a, a canonical WNT ligand, activates the β-catenin-dependent pathway by binding to Frizzled receptors and LDLR-related protein 5/6 (LRP5/6) coreceptors, activating Dishevelled (Dvl) proteins to inhibit the activity of glycogen synthase kinase 3-β (GSK3-β) and stabilizing cytoplasmic β-catenin, which then translocates to the nucleus to regulate the transcription of osteogenic genes.215 In hyperphosphatemic conditions, WNT3a-induced nuclear accumulation of β-catenin induces RUNX2 upregulation, promoting the osteogenic phenotypic transformation of VSMCs and calcium deposition.214 AGEs activate the WNT3a/β-catenin pathway in VSMCs, which directly contributes to the development of VC in patients with T2DM.216–218 Exposure to titanium dioxide nanoparticles (TiO₂-NPs) suppresses WNT3a signaling, reduces RUNX2 and Osx expression, induces apoptosis, and suppresses osteoblast differentiation, although their role in VSMCs remains unclear.219,220

WNT5a, primarily associated with noncanonical WNT signaling,221 and promotes VC through macrophage-derived inflammation and activation of the chondrogenic regulator SOX9.222,223 It has been identified as an independent risk factor for VC in end-stage renal disease (ESRD).224 Clinical samples have shown that the expression of WNT5a and receptor tyrosine kinase-like orphan receptor 2 (Ror2) is significantly higher in advanced human atherosclerotic plaques than in early lesions, and they are predominantly expressed in macrophages and foam cells within plaques, suggesting that WNT5a signaling may exert a direct role in atherosclerosis.225 Interestingly, recent studies on human adipose-derived MSCs have highlighted that IL-6 exacerbates VC through the STAT3-dependent WNT5a/Ror2 signaling pathway,226 which may be attributed to inflammation or upstream regulatory factors. Oxidized high-density lipoprotein (ox-HDL) induces WNT5a-mediated osteochondrogenic transformation of VSMCs by upregulating WNT5a expression, which is counteracted by adiponectin.227 Additionally, WNT5a regulates VSMC proliferation and phenotypic transformation through PKC signaling, thus intersecting with the regulatory network of the canonical WNT pathway.228,229 Although recent studies have implicated WNT5a in the pathogenesis of VC, its precise regulatory mechanisms and therapeutic targets remain to be further elucidated.

The WNT pathway crosstalks with multiple signaling pathways to regulate VC. For instance, WNT/β-catenin and PPARγ exhibit reciprocal inhibition. Specifically, activated WNT signaling sequesters β-catenin, which translocates to the nucleus to inhibit PPARγ expression, thereby impeding PPARγ-mediated adipogenesis and promoting the phenotypic transformation of VSMCs,230,231 while PPARγ agonists downregulate WNT/β-catenin signaling by promoting β-catenin degradation.232 In addition, the WNT and BMP signaling pathways collaborate to drive the osteogenic program in VSMCs. BMP2 induces WNT signaling via MSX2 in aortic valve calcification, thereby enhancing β-catenin nuclear translocation.233 Similarly, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-induced osteochondrogenic transformation of VSMCs requires WNT3a and BMP4 coexpression, forming a feedforward loop that amplifies VSMC mineralization.234 Furthermore, BMP phosphorylates Smad proteins, which then interact with β-catenin to coactivate osteogenic genes such as RUNX2.235,236 The PI3K/AKT and WNT/β-catenin crosstalk further underscores the complexity of the signaling network regulating VC. PI3K/AKT phosphorylates GSK3-β at Ser9 and inactivates it, stabilizing β-catenin and augmenting WNT signaling.237,238 Inhibition of PI3K reduces β-catenin levels and osteogenic markers in VSMCs,239 although the regulatory effect of this interaction remains controversial in VC.240 The above signaling pathways highlight WNT signaling as a critical contributor to the pathogenesis of VC, necessitating multipathway strategies to mitigate its contributions to pathological vascular mineralization.

Bone morphogenetic protein signaling pathway

The BMP family, a subgroup of TGF-β superfamily proteins, plays a pivotal role in the development of bone and cartilage and has increasingly been recognized as a key regulator in the pathogenesis of VC.241,242 BMP2, in particular, accelerates VC in murine models such as ApoE−/− mice by downregulating VSMC contractile markers (SM22α and α-SMA) and upregulating osteogenic-related genes.216,243 BMPs exert their effects by binding to type I receptors (ALK2, ALK3, ALK6; also known as BMPR1A, BMPR1B, and ACVR1, respectively) and type II receptors, which activate the kinase activity of type I receptors to phosphorylate Smad1/5/8. Phosphorylated Smad1/5/8 forms a complex with Smad4 that translocates to the nucleus to regulate osteogenic gene transcription, mediating phenotypic transformation of VSMCs and VC progression. Among these interactions, BMP2 binding to BMPR1A is particularly critical for this phenotypic transformation.244

Crosstalk in the BMP signaling pathway

BMPR1A, a type I serine/threonine kinase receptor, binds to BMP2 to activate the Smad1/5/8 signaling pathway, driving the phenotypic transformation of VSMCs and upregulating osteogenic markers such as RUNX2 and Osx.245 BMPR1A expression is closely associated with inflammatory processes. IL-1β induces EndMT through BMPR1A/BMPR2,246 while IL-6 enhances BMP2 signaling by promoting the translocation of BMPR1A to the cell surface, thereby exacerbating the osteogenic phenotypic transformation of BMSCs,247 thus highlighting BMPR1A as a promising therapeutic target for VC. Several endogenous regulators also modulate the BMP/Smad signaling pathway to participate in VC regulation. MGP not only directly inhibits the nucleation and growth of VC but also neutralizes the activity of BMP2 and BMP4 to block the BMP/Smad signaling pathway, thereby suppressing phenotypic transformation of VSMCs and vascular hydroxyapatite deposition.126,248 Interestingly, no basal upregulation of BMP/Smad signaling was detected in the aortas of MGP-deficient mice under normal conditions.

Multiple factors regulate VC progression by modulating the BMP signaling pathway. BMP2 serves as a central mediator in VC progression through the BMP/Smad1/5/8 signaling pathway. Erythropoietin (EPO) enhances BMP2 expression via the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway, thereby exacerbating VC by promoting the BMP2-mediated phenotypic transformation of VSMCs.249 Inhibitors including dorsomorphin homolog 1 (DMH1), a highly selective small-molecule inhibitor, directly abrogate BMP receptor activity, whereas upper zone-associated protein/Gla-rich protein (Ucma/GRP) indirectly represses the BMP signaling pathway by binding to BMP2.250,251 Both molecules attenuate SMAD phosphorylation and phenotypic transformation of VSMCs. In addition, ncRNAs also play crucial roles in regulating BMP2-mediated VC. MiR-145 and miR-37817,18 further attenuate BMP2-mediated calcification. Furthermore, recent studies have found that NONO, an RNA/DNA-binding paraspeckle protein, directly binds to the promoter region of the BMP2 gene via its C-terminal domain, thereby inhibiting BMP2 transcription and suppressing VC progression.252 This finding identifies NONO as a novel negative regulator of BMP2, which provides a promising therapeutic target for the prevention and treatment of VC.

Beyond BMP2, other members of the BMP family contribute to VC progression through distinct molecular mechanisms. Activation of BMP4 is negatively correlated with decreased KLF2 expression in calcified vascular lesions, whereas overexpression of KLF2 attenuates VC by inhibiting BMP/Smad1/5 signaling.253 Similarly, BMP6 synergizes with ox-LDL to induce osteogenic differentiation in endothelial cells via autocrine signaling, activating Smad1/5 phosphorylation and upregulating bone matrix proteins.254 In human coronary artery VSMCs derived from patients with Kawasaki disease, BMP6 is significantly upregulated, which is closely associated with the pathological process of VC.255 In contrast, exogenous administration of BMP7 attenuates VC in CKD models by reducing BMP2 and RUNX2 expression in aortic tissues.256 Notably, exogenous BMP7 fails to reverse established VC in CKD murine models.257 Furthermore, BMP9 has also been shown to promote osteoblast differentiation, yet its role and mechanism in VC remain to be further investigated.258 Overall, these findings highlight the diverse roles of BMP family members in linking oxidative stress, inflammation, and osteogenic signaling during VC progression.

The BMP signaling pathway interacts with multiple signaling networks to regulate VC. PI3K/AKT signaling acts upstream of BMP/Smad, promoting BMP-induced RUNX2 transcriptional activity.259 Nesfatin-1 further bridges these pathways by inhibiting the E3 ligase SYTL4, stabilizing BMP2, and enhancing Smad1/5/9 and HDAC4 phosphorylation to activate RUNX2 and MSX2.260 Additionally, the MAPK signaling pathway, particularly the ERK1/2 subtype, intersects with the BMP/Smad pathway at the level of Smad proteins. Specifically, ERK1/2 phosphorylates the linker region of Smad1/5, which impairs their nuclear translocation and thereby attenuates BMP/Smad-mediated osteogenic signaling. Growth factors such as epidermal growth factor (EGF), FGF and IGF leverage this mechanism to inhibit BMP-mediated osteogenesis.261 These interactions highlight the complex regulatory landscape of BMP signaling in VC.

Phosphatidylinositol-3 kinase/protein kinase B signaling pathway

PI3K, a lipid kinase composed of catalytic (p110) and regulatory (p85) subunits, is activated by G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), RTKs, IGF-1 receptor, and B-cell receptors. It phosphorylates the 3’-OH group of the inositol ring in phosphatidylinositol lipids, thereby facilitating the conversion of phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) into the second messenger phosphatidylinositol-3,4,5-trisphosphate (PIP3). Subsequently, PIP3 recruits AKT and phosphoinositide-dependent kinase 1 (PDK1) to the plasma membrane, where PDK1 phosphorylates AKT at the Thr308 residue, leading to partial activation of AKT signaling. Full activation of AKT requires additional phosphorylation at the Ser473 residue by the mammalian target of rapamycin complex 2 (mTORC2). PTEN, a key negative regulator of the PI3K/AKT pathway, counteracts this process by dephosphorylating PIP3 to PIP2.262 This pathway regulates fundamental cellular processes, including proliferation, growth, differentiation, and apoptosis, and emerging evidence has further highlighted its critical regulatory role in the pathogenesis of VC.

The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway crosstalks with multiple other signaling networks to coordinately regulate VC progression. GSK3-β, a critical downstream effector of the PI3K/AKT pathway, regulates RUNX2 activity during VC progression. Upon PI3K activation, AKT phosphorylates GSK3-β at the Ser9 residue, leading to its inactivation, which reduces the ability of GSK3-β to phosphorylate RUNX2, thereby preventing RUNX2 degradation and stabilizing its protein level, ultimately driving the phenotypic transformation of VSMCs.263,264 GSK3-β also bridges PI3K/AKT and WNT signaling: AGEs induce phenotypic transformation of VSMCs via the PI3K/AKT/GSK3-β axis, a process that can be inhibited by the PI3K inhibitor LY294002 and potentiated by the GSK3-β inhibitor TWS119.265 In addition to GSK3-β, another critical downstream effector of the PI3K/AKT pathway is mTOR, a highly conserved serine/threonine protein kinase that influences VC by modulating the phenotypic transformation of VSMCs. Although the direct regulatory mechanism of mTOR in VSMC calcification remains to be fully elucidated, relevant studies in BMSCs have provided preliminary clues for understanding its pro-osteogenic function. For instance, kaempferol activates the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway in BMSCs, upregulating ALP activity and SOX2 to drive osteogenesis. However, miR-124-3p suppresses this axis, attenuating mTOR-mediated osteogenic signaling.266

In addition, the forkhead box O (FOXO) transcription factor family of transcription factors is another key set of downstream targets of the PI3K/AKT pathway.267 In humans, the FOXO family consists of four major members: FOXO1, FOXO3, FOXO4, and FOXO6. Among these, FOXO1 and FOXO3 exert inhibitory effects on VC by promoting the ubiquitination and subsequent degradation of RUNX2. In the context of PTEN deficiency, the loss of PTEN-mediated negative regulation leads to sustained AKT activation, which in turn phosphorylates FOXO1/3 and promotes their cytoplasmic retention and impairs the nuclear translocation of FOXO1/3 and their ability to induce RUNX2 ubiquitination, thereby resulting in the accumulation and upregulation of the RUNX2 protein.264 Notably, PI3K/AKT-mediated phosphorylation of FOXO4 abrogates its inhibitory effect on MYOCD activity, thereby promoting the maintenance of contractile VSMCs and inhibiting their phenotypic transformation.268 Additionally, FOXO proteins exert intrinsic antioxidant effects, indirectly regulating VC.269 To date, no direct regulatory role of FOXO6 in VC has been reported in the literature.

The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway acts as an upstream regulator of both the WNT and BMP/Smad pathways, orchestrating regulatory effects on osteogenic processes in multiple cell types. During osteoblast differentiation, the WNT pathway initiates this process, while BMP/Smad signaling drives subsequent differentiation and maturation of osteoblasts.270,271 As an upstream regulator, PI3K/AKT may prime the phenotypic transformation of VSMCs by coregulating these downstream pathways, indicating its potential critical role in the early stages of VC pathogenesis. PI3K/AKT also intersects with the MAPK and Notch signaling pathways to regulate VC. The PI3K/AKT and MAPK signaling pathways exhibit extensive functional crosstalk, as activated Ras-GTP can simultaneously trigger both signaling cascades in multiple cell types.272,273 However, the specific regulatory mode of their interaction in VC remains poorly defined, representing a key underexplored area in VC pathogenesis. In addition, the Notch signaling pathway modulates PI3K/AKT activity by repressing PTEN expression, a regulatory crosstalk well described in cancer.274 However, whether this interaction influences VC progression remains unclear, presenting promising potential for future research. These interconnected signaling networks highlight the complexity of PI3K/AKT-mediated regulation in VC and underscore the critical need to delineate these crosstalk mechanisms to advance the development of targeted therapeutic strategies for VC.

The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway plays a context-dependent dual role in VC. In the diabetic environment, leptin accelerates phenotypic transformation of VSMCs via PI3K/AKT, correlating with VC severity.275 In CKD, the uremic toxin indoxyl sulfate (IS) drives phenotypic transformation of VSMCs through PI3K/AKT signaling, contributing to the high prevalence of VC in CKD patients.276 Osteogenic factors such as isocoumarin A (isolated from rhubarb) have also been reported to regulate osteogenic differentiation. It promotes BMP/RUNX2 expression in a PI3K/AKT-dependent manner in MC3T3-E1 preosteoblasts.277 Vascular peroxidase 1-mediated VC in rats is also PI3K/AKT dependent, which can be blocked by the PI3K inhibitor LY294002276.278

Conversely, PI3K/AKT signaling can mitigate VC under specific conditions. The receptor tyrosine kinase Axl protects against VC in vitro through PI3K/AKT activation.279 Oxidative stress reduction through selenite inhibits PI3K/AKT-mediated phenotypic transformation of VSMCs.67 Globular adiponectin (gAd) suppresses the phenotypic transformation of VSMCs by inhibiting both the PI3K/AKT and WNT/β-catenin signaling pathways.280 These findings highlight the complexity of PI3K/AKT signaling in VC. This context-dependent duality underscores the critical need for precision in developing PI3K/AKT-targeted therapies for VC, with careful consideration of disease stage, underlying etiology, and cell type-specific effects.

Mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling pathway

The MAPK pathway consists of evolutionarily conserved serine/threonine kinases, including four major subfamilies: ERK, p38 MAPK, JNK, and big mitogen-activated protein kinase 1 (BMK1/ERK5). It exerts effects via a sequential phosphorylation cascade involving MAPK kinase kinases (MKKK), MAPK kinases (MKK), and MAPK, regulating cell proliferation, differentiation, stress responses and inflammation. Emerging evidence highlights its pivotal role in VC.

Crosstalk in the MAPK signaling pathway

As a highly homologous MAPK family member, ERK1/2 plays pivotal roles in VC.281–283 Uremic toxin p-cresyl sulfate (PCS), a key driver of VC in CKD, amplifies phenotypic transformation of VSMCs by phosphorylating ERK1/2, JNK and p38 MAPK, thereby activating NF-κB to upregulate osteogenic markers.284 Oxidative stress mediators, including vascular peroxidase 1 (VPO1) and ox-LDL, promote ERK1/2 phosphorylation, exacerbating ox-LDL-induced VC.278 Additionally, ox-LDL stimulates human coronary artery ECs to upregulate BMP2 via TLR2/4-mediated ERK1/2 and NF-κB activation, indirectly promoting VC.285 Conversely, pharmacological inhibition of ERK1/2 by agents such as captopril or PD98059 attenuates VC by restoring contractile markers and counteracting phenotypic transformation of VSMCs.286,287

The p38 MAPK pathway plays a critical dual role in VC. The pro-calcific factor Nε-carboxymethyl-lysine (CML) induces ROS-dependent p38 MAPK activation, promoting calcium deposition and osteogenic marker upregulation in VSMC calcification models.288 The inflammatory cytokine IL-18 amplifies VC via p38 MAPK-dependent signaling in high-fat diet-fed CKD rats.289 Conversely, endothelial-derived 5-methoxytryptophan (5-MTP) reduces atherosclerotic lesions and calcification by blocking p38 MAPK activation.290 FGF21 downregulates VSMC osteogenic markers via p38 MAPK inhibition, which is reversed by the p38 inhibitor SB203580.291

The JNK subfamily, a member of the MAPK family, is encoded by three distinct genes: JNK1 and JNK2 are ubiquitously expressed in various tissues, whereas JNK3 expression is restricted to the brain, heart and testis.292 PTH exacerbates VC in CKD by activating ER stress via the PERK/CHOP and IRE1/JNK signaling pathways, which subsequently promotes the phenotypic transformation and apoptosis of VSMCs.293 Uremic toxin IS also induces phenotypic transformation of VSMCs via the JNK/Pit-1 signaling axis, and inhibition of JNK significantly attenuates IS-induced calcium deposition.294

BMK1, also known as ERK5, is activated by growth factors and oxidative stress, regulating cell survival and differentiation. In rat aortic VSMCs, hydrogen peroxide-induced oxidative stress activates BMK1 in a Src-dependent manner, protecting cells from oxidative damage-induced death and implying a potential VC regulatory role.295 Since abnormal VSMC apoptosis promotes VC, BMK1 may exert anti-calcific effects, but further studies are needed to confirm its precise function.

Adenosine 5‘-monophosphate-activated protein kinase signaling pathway

AMPK is a serine/threonine kinase with a complex heterotrimeric structure composed of one α catalytic subunit and two regulatory subunits (β and γ). As a fundamental regulator of cellular energy homeostasis in mammals, the AMPK signaling pathway senses changes in intracellular AMP/ATP ratios to maintain metabolic balance, sensing intracellular energy levels to maintain metabolic balance. It orchestrates multiple critical cellular processes, including protein, lipid and carbohydrate metabolism, autophagy and mitochondrial integrity.296

Crosstalk in the AMPK signaling pathway

The AMPK pathway exerts a dual regulatory role on RUNX2. During osteogenesis, AMPK signaling can induce RUNX2 expression to promote osteoblast differentiation.297 In contrast, in VSMCs undergoing phenotypic transformation, AMPK activation downregulates RUNX2 expression. Specifically, metformin activates AMPK signaling to stimulate endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), thereby increasing nitric oxide (NO) production and ultimately inhibiting phenotypic transformation of VSMCs.298 Similarly, in diabetes mellitus (DM)-induced VC, ubiquitin-specific protease 10 (USP10), a deubiquitinating enzyme, attenuates CML-induced VC by promoting AMPK activation. Collectively, these findings indicate that AMPK modulates RUNX2 activity. Whereas it exerts a pro-osteogenic effect in osteoblasts, it functions as a suppressor of VSMC phenotypic transformation and VC.299

AMPK also regulates autophagy in the pathogenesis of VC. Proautophagic activation of AMPK attenuates VC. For instance, melatonin reduces the phenotypic transformation of VSMCs and VC by upregulating AMPK and Unc-51 Like Autophagy Activating Kinase 1 (ULK1), thereby inhibiting mTOR signaling.300 Aldosterone promotes high phosphate-induced phenotypic transformation of VSMCs and VC by suppressing AMPK-dependent autophagy, which provides a new explanation for the high correlation between aldosterone and VC under different pathological conditions. AGEs accelerate VC by inhibiting autophagy via the AMPK/mTOR axis, an effect that can be reversed by autophagy activators (e.g., rapamycin) or AMPK agonists (e.g., AICAR).301 These findings collectively confirm that AMPK-dependent autophagy serves as a key protective mechanism against VC and that dysregulation of this pathway contributes to pathological mineralization. Therefore, AMPK regulates VC primarily by enhancing autophagy, which is also the main role of the AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway in VC.

AMPK can also alleviate ER stress-driven VC.302 Current studies have shown that death-associated protein kinase 3 (DAPK3) inactivates the AMPK signaling pathway and promotes the expression of ER stress-associated proteins such as CHOP and GRP78. In contrast, pretreatment with the AMPK inhibitor compound C abrogated the DAPK3 shRNA-mediated inhibition of ER stress in VSMCs.303 In addition, β-hydroxybutyrate (BHB), a ketone body, alleviates ER stress-mediated apoptosis and VC by activating the AMPK/Nrf2 pathway.304 These findings establish a functional link between AMPK and ER stress in VC: AMPK activation counteracts ER stress, while DAPK3-mediated AMPK inhibition exacerbates ER stress-driven vascular mineralization.

AMPK signaling also regulates mitochondrial dynamics in VC. Melatonin and irisin mitigate VC by activating AMPK, which in turn reduces the expression of Drp1, a key mediator of mitochondrial fission.305 The mitochondrial-derived peptide MOTS-c suppresses VC in rats by activating AMPK, which underscores the protective role of AMPK-dependent mitochondrial regulation in VC.306 Ubiquitin-specific protease 10 (USP10) mediates the ubiquitination of AMPKα and increases the phosphorylation level of AMPKα at Thr172, thereby attenuating VC formation in T2DM models307; thus, USP10 represents a novel therapeutic target for diabetic VC.

The AMPK pathway engages in intricate crosstalk with the WNT/β-catenin, PI3K/AKT, and receptor activator of NF-κB ligand (RANKL) signaling networks and dynamically influences VC through context-dependent mechanisms. AMPK phosphorylates β-catenin at Ser552, which stabilizes β-catenin and enhances β-catenin/TCF-mediated transcriptional activity.308 Conversely, GSK3-β, a downstream molecule of WNT signaling, forms a complex with AMPKα and β subunits to inhibit AMPK function,309 thereby establishing a reciprocal regulatory loop between AMPK and WNT/β-catenin signaling. In aortic valve calcification, metformin attenuates the calcification of valvular interstitial cells (VICs) through AMPK-dependent activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway,310,311 suggesting a cooperative role between AMPK and PI3K/AKT signaling in this process. In addition, AMPK regulates RANKL signaling to suppress macrophage infiltration and osteoclastogenesis, thereby inhibiting vascular mineralization.168

Nuclear factor kappa-B signaling pathway

NF-κB is a family of transcription factors pivotal in orchestrating inflammatory responses, immune signaling, apoptosis, and stress adaptation. Characterized by a conserved Rel homology domain, the NF-κB family comprises five members: NF-κB1 (p50), NF-κB2 (p52), RelA (p65), RelB, and c-Rel. NF-κB is ubiquitously expressed across animal cell types. NF-κB is ubiquitously expressed in mammalian cells. It integrates diverse extracellular stimuli, including cytokines, oxidative stress, ox-LDL, and microbial antigens, via canonical and noncanonical pathways that differ in activation mechanisms and downstream targets.312

The canonical NF-κB pathway is rapidly activated by proinflammatory signals. Activation involves IKK-mediated phosphorylation and degradation of IκB, release of NF-κB dimers, and their nuclear translocation, release of NF-κB dimers, and nuclear translocation. IKK is composed of two catalytic subunits, IKKα (IKK1), IKKβ (IKK2) and the regulatory subunit IKKγ (NEMO), which are crucial for the activation of the canonical NF-κB signaling pathway.313 Although NF-κB signaling is generally pro-calcific in VC, IKK2/NF-κB activation in VSMCs reduces apoptosis and MV release, thereby protecting against CKD-associated VC.314 Several regulators modulate NF-κB to affect VC. Polypeptide N-acetylgalactosamine transferase 3 (GALNT3) increases O-GalNAcylation of TNFR1, blocks NF-κB activation, and attenuates oxidative stress and apoptosis in VSMCs, thereby mitigating VC.100 The gut bacterium Prevotella copri accelerates CKD-associated VC via lipopolysaccharide-induced NF-κB/NLRP3 activation.315 Zinc sulfate (ZnSO4) mitigates VC by upregulating zinc finger protein (TNF-α-induced protein) through ZnR/GPR39, which inhibits NF-κB signaling.316 The lncRNA H19, upregulated in aortic tissues of rats with CKD, binds miR-138 to activate TLR3/NF-κB signaling and promote VC.317 TLR4 also drives VC via NF-κB,318 while IS induces phenotypic transformation of VSMCs through ROS/NF-κB signaling,319 an effect inhibited by Ginkgo biloba extract (EGb761).320

The noncanonical NF-κB signaling pathway is mainly activated by specific ligands, including lymphotoxin β (LTβ), CD40L, BAFF, and RANKL, distinguishing it from the canonical pathway, which responds to classic proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1.321 Activation of the noncanonical pathway relies on the accumulation and activation of NF-κB-inducing kinase (NIK). TNF-like weak inducer of apoptosis (TWEAK) binding to its receptor Fn14 promotes VC by activating both canonical and noncanonical NF-κB pathways, with the canonical pathway playing a dominant role.321,322 RANKL can activate the noncanonical NF-κB/p52 signaling pathway in ECs, stimulating the secretion of paracrine factors that in turn induce phenotypic transformation of adjacent VSMCs and promote VC.323 In addition, pharmacological inhibition of the noncanonical pathway by panax notoginseng water extract (PNWE) reduces BMP2 expression in ECs, further supporting a role for noncanonical NF-κB signaling in VC pathogenesis.324

NF-κB signaling also acts as a central integrator of pro-calcific signals. Genetic evidence highlights its pro-calcific role: knockdown of p65 reduces MMP9 expression, thereby lowering BMP2 levels in VSMCs and inhibiting VC progression.325 The inflammasome component NLRP3 further accelerates VC by activating NF-κB.105 Pro-calcific mediators such as serum and glucocorticoid-inducible kinases (SGK1/3) and short-chain fatty acid butyrate exacerbate VC through NF-κB-dependent mechanisms,326–328 whereas the endothelial protective factor 5-methoxytryptophan (5-MTP) attenuates VC by blocking p38 MAPK and inhibiting NF-κB transactivation.329 In simulated microgravity, the OPG/RANKL/RANK axis regulates phenotypic transformation of VSMCs and calcification by engaging NF-κB signaling.330 The PI3K/AKT pathway acts as an upstream regulator of NF-κB during VC progression.276 In addition, NF-κB interacts with WNT signaling in a context-dependent manner, underscoring the dynamic balance between pro- and anti-inflammatory signals in VC, although the precise underlying mechanisms remain unclear.

Notch signaling pathway

The Notch signaling pathway, characterized by its evolutionary conservation across species, mediates a distinct mode of cell‒cell communication. It is a unique protease-dependent signaling pathway composed of four core components: Notch receptors (Notch1/2/3/4), Notch ligands (δ-like ligands, DLL1/3/4; Jagged ligands, JAG1/2), CSL-DNA-binding proteins, and downstream target genes (e.g., Hes family and MYC). Unlike other signaling pathways that rely on secreted ligands and intracellular signal amplification cascades, Notch signaling is initiated by direct interactions between transmembrane ligands on one cell and Notch receptors on an adjacent cell. This interaction triggers a three-step proteolytic cleavage process (S1, S2, and S3 cleavages), leading to the release of the Notch intracellular domain (NICD). The NICD then translocates to the nucleus, binds to the CSL DNA-binding protein and induces transcriptional activation of downstream target genes.331

The Notch signaling pathway operates through a tightly regulated proteolytic cascade in VC. Initially, synthesized as an inactive monopeptide in the ER, Notch undergoes sequential cleavage events for maturation: first, it is cleaved by furin protease in the Golgi apparatus (S1 cleavage), generating a heterodimeric receptor that is translocated to the cell surface. Ligand binding (DLL1/3/4, JAG1/2) induces conformational changes in the Notch receptor, triggering ADAM metalloproteinase-mediated S2 cleavage,332,333 followed by γ-secretase-dependent S3 cleavage to release the active NICD.334 The NICD subsequently translocates to the nucleus, forms a complex with CSL transcription factors and regulates the expression of downstream target genes.331,335 NICD also cooperates with Smad1 to enhance BMP2-induced MSX2 expression, promoting phenotypic transformation of VSMCs in VC.130,336 In contrast, WNT16 attenuates TGF-β-induced phenotypic transformation of VSMCs via Notch signaling,337 indicating a protective role of WNT16 in VC progression.

Notch3, one of the four Notch receptors, is a transmembrane protein critical for vascular homeostasis. In macrophages, the uremic toxin IS activates Notch signaling via the transporter OATP2B1, upregulating DLL4 expression and driving pro-inflammatory macrophage activation in VC-associated vascular inflammation.338 In VSMCs, hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) activates Notch3 via the c-Met/AKT pathway to promote phenotypic transformation of VSMCs.339 In contrast, IS inhibits Notch3 signaling to dysregulate VSMC fate, upregulate osteogenic markers and accelerate VC, which emphasizes the protective role of Notch3 against VC.340 Collectively, these findings establish Notch3 as a key modulator of VSMC plasticity, inflammation, and calcification, with targeted manipulation of its signaling axes offering promising therapeutic strategies for VC intervention.

Rho/Rho-associated coiled-coil containing protein kinase signaling pathway

ROCK, a typical serine/threonine protein kinase belonging to the AGC kinase family, has two isoforms: ROCK I (p160 ROCK, ROKβ) and ROCK II (Rho kinase, ROKα). These two isoforms share similar structures, with ~65% homology in their amino acid sequences. Rho belongs to the small GTP-binding protein family; as a member of this family, the Rho subfamily primarily comprises Rho (A, B, and C), Rac (1, 2, and 3), and Cdc42. Rho family proteins regulate multiple physiological processes, including cytoskeletal dynamics, cellular morphology, membrane trafficking, and cell migration, which is dependent on their GTP-bound active state and GDP-bound inactive state.341

The Rho/ROCK signaling pathway shows context-dependent effects in VC. In VSMCs, pharmacological inhibition of ROCK using Y-27632 increases osteogenic ALP activity and accelerates VC.342 This effect is associated with reduced fetuin-A uptake, suggesting that RhoA/ROCK acts as a negative regulator of VC, possibly by maintaining contractile VSMCs and inhibiting osteogenic transformation. Conversely, in VICs, ox-LDL-derived lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) promotes phenotypic transformation of VSMCs via LPA receptor 1 (LPAR1)-mediated activation of the RhoA/ROCK/NF-κB signaling pathway. In this process, RhoA/ROCK phosphorylates IKKα, driving p65 phosphorylation at Ser536 and initiating an osteogenic programme that is resistant to canonical IκB inhibitors.343 This cell type-specific difference arises from distinct transcriptional modes: Rho/ROCK suppresses calcification in VSMCs, whereas it initiates inflammation-driven mineralization in VICs.

Stimulus-dependent mechanisms further contribute to the role of Rho/ROCK in VC. In CKD models, WNT3a attenuates VC by inhibiting the Rho/ROCK/JNK signaling pathway, indicating protective crosstalk between the WNT and Rho/ROCK pathways.344 Conversely, pro-calcific stimuli such as oxysterol 7-ketocholesterol exacerbate phosphate-induced VC via Rho/ROCK activation, and ROCK inhibition partially reverses this pro-calcific effect.345 Similarly, high-glucose conditions upregulate osteogenic ALP activity via Rho/ROCK in VSMCs by downregulating BMP-binding endothelial cell precursor-derived regulator (BMPER) expression,346 highlighting metabolic stress as a key driver of pro-calcific Rho/ROCK activation.

Pathogenic processes of vascular calcification

VC represents an active pathological process characterized by aberrant mineral deposition within the vascular wall. As highlighted in the previous sections, VC is driven by integrated cellular phenotypic remodeling, dysregulated molecular networks, and aberrant activation of multiple signaling pathways. VC is highly prevalent in chronic disease states, including T2DM and CKD, where it serves as a severe comorbidity that accelerates disease progression. Pathologically, VC develops through initiation and progression phases, which collectively promote vascular wall remodeling, impair hemodynamic stability, and increase the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) and all‑cause mortality. However, under distinct pathological conditions, the action modes and regulatory networks of the various mechanisms regulating VC differ depending on the inducing stimuli and the characteristics of the local microenvironment.

Initiation of vascular calcification

The core mechanism underlying VC is the dysregulation of local pro-calcific–anti-calcific homeostasis and the formation of calcification nuclei. Under physiological conditions, the vascular wall maintains a homeostatic balancing pro‑calcific and anti‑calcific processes.347 The anti‑calcific system is centered on inhibitors such as MGP and fetuin‑A. Specifically, MGP exerts its calcium deposition‑inhibiting activity through vitamin K‑dependent γ‑carboxylation, while phosphorylation enhances its stability.348 In contrast, fetuin-A preserves vascular anti‑calcific homeostasis by specifically chelating free calcium and phosphate ions to prevent the formation of supersaturated calcium‑phosphate complexes.349 The pro‑calcific system, by comparison, is expressed at low levels and remains restrained under physiological conditions. Under pathological stimuli, including inflammation, metabolic disorders, and aging, this homeostasis is disrupted. On the one hand, fetuin-A loses its chelating capacity, MGP fails to exert its inhibitory function due to insufficient γ-carboxylation, and dysregulated calcium-phosphate transport leads to local calcium-phosphate supersaturation.350 On the other hand, key osteogenic regulators, including RUNX2, BMP2, and ALP, are activated, resulting in markedly enhanced pro-calcific signaling, ultimately triggering VC. This homeostatic imbalance represents the critical pathological basis for the initial development of VC.

In addition to the homeostatic imbalance, EVs play a pivotal nucleation role in VC initiation. Apoptotic or activated VSMCs and ECs can release EVs, such as exosomes and MVs. These vesicles contain pro-calcific factors including sortilin and annexins that directly initiate mineral nucleation, as well as calcification inhibitors such as MGP, which are involved in regulating the local calcification balance.351,352 Under pathological stimuli, the release of EVs is increased, and their cargo composition is altered, thereby enhancing their nucleating activity. Oxidative stress and inflammatory responses can further promote EV release and activate their nucleating function by inducing abnormal cellular activation. Meanwhile, EV membranes are abundant in negatively charged phosphatidylserine, a feature that enables them to adsorb calcium and phosphate ions to form initial calcium-phosphate complexes. Moreover, the confined intraluminal microenvironment of EVs provides favorable conditions for the nucleation and growth of calcium phosphate nanocrystals, thus rendering vesicle-mediated mineralization a dual process occurring both at the membrane surface and within the vesicle lumen.352–354 By disrupting the local balance between calcification inhibitors and promoters and furnishing a multifunctional structural scaffold for mineral deposition, EVs facilitate the formation of amorphous calcium‑phosphate complexes and microprecipitates. This marks the initiation of VC and ultimately compromises the stability of atherosclerotic plaques.355

Progression of vascular calcification

ECM remodeling serves as the fundamental basis for calcium-phosphate deposition. Pathological stimuli activate matrix-degrading enzymes, including MMPs and cathepsins.356 MMPs primarily degrade collagen and elastic fibers, while cathepsins mainly breakdown proteoglycans; together, these enzymes breakdown elastic fibers and collagen within the vascular wall to reduce the elasticity and mechanical strength of the vascular wall while inducing VSMCs to secrete abundant mineralization-related matrix components, such as type I collagen, osteopontin, and bone sialoprotein. These components can specifically bind calcium-phosphate crystals via surface active sites. Type I ollagen fragments act as a scaffold to anchor nascent calcium phosphate complexes and prevent their dissolution, thereby facilitating the formation of hydroxyapatite crystals.30,31,356 Degradation of elastic fibers also releases fibronectin and IGF-1, which stimulate phenotypic transformation of VSMCs and promote the progression of VC.268 Meanwhile, structural disruption of the ECM exposes more cryptic calcium‑binding sites, exacerbating local calcium and phosphate ion accumulation. It also provides structural pathways for the free diffusion of EVs within the vascular wall, indirectly enhancing the efficiency of calcification spread and promoting the progression of VC.357

Beyond ECM remodeling, persistent local imbalance in calcium-phosphate metabolism further drives VC progression. This imbalance leads to persistent deposition of calcium and phosphate ions, enabling the gradual mineral transformation of initial calcium-phosphate complexes into mature hydroxyapatite crystals.358 Crystal size increases continuously with increased calcium‑phosphate supply, while calcification inhibitors remain functionally impaired and fail to effectively restrain crystal growth and deposition. Under the influence of inflammation and oxidative stress, the activities of calcium and phosphate transporters in vascular wall cells are reduced, resulting in diminished intracellular uptake and excretion of calcium and phosphate ions.73 This further exacerbates local metabolic imbalance and accelerates the progression of VC.

Additionally, EVs play a critical role in the spread of calcification during VC progression. EVs carrying mature hydroxyapatite crystals can not only mediate the transmission of pro‑calcific signals to adjacent vascular wall cells through direct cell‑to‑cell contact or diffusion in the ECM but also directly transport these crystals to target cells, leading to the spread of local lesions and the development of diffuse calcification.359,360 Meanwhile, EVs can fuse via their membranes to form larger vesicular structures, accelerating crystal aggregation and fusion and promoting the formation of calcified nodules. Inflammation, oxidative stress, and other pathological stimuli upregulate the expression of adhesion molecules on the EV surface, enhance the binding affinity of EVs to target cells, and further accelerate the progression and spread of VC.352,356

Vascular calcification in different pathologic conditions

Atherosclerosis

VC is a pathological manifestation of atherosclerotic progression and a marker of cardiovascular aging, and its occurrence is associated with genetic susceptibility. Importantly, although VC and atherosclerosis share some overlapping risk factors and pathogenic mechanisms, they can also occur independently or coexist. Under atherosclerotic conditions, vascular endothelial injury and dysfunction, as well as the persistent activation of chronic inflammatory signals, lead to impaired cellular barrier and increased permeability.361 This disruption enables oxidized LDL, calcium, and phosphate ions to penetrate into the vascular wall, promoting the release of inflammatory factors and inhibiting the synthesis of calcification inhibitors. ox-LDL can be internalized through scavenger receptors on the surface of macrophages, driving the transformation of macrophages into foam cells that massively release inflammatory factors and MMPs. Ox-LDL mediates ECM degradation in vulnerable plaque areas, promotes the mineral transformation and maturation of amorphous calcium-phosphate precipitates into hydroxyapatite crystals, and simultaneously induces the phenotypic transformation of VSMCs,362 EC apoptosis, and EV-mediated calcification spread.363 In addition, DNA damage, epigenetic modifications, cellular senescence, and programmed cell death can promote atherosclerosis and VC by inducing oxidative stress, impairing DNA repair, and amplifying proinflammatory signals through senescence-associated secretory phenotypes (SASPs).175,364–366

During the pathological process of atherosclerosis, VC predominantly occurs in the vascular intima. With the progression of atherosclerosis, VC thickens the fibrous cap of sclerotic plaques in the early stages, thereby enhancing plaque stability to a certain extent. However, as plaques continue to progress, VC gradually worsens, evolving from initial punctate and linear calcification to plaque-like and diffuse calcification, which may lead to plaque instability in advanced stages, increase the risk of rupture and increase the risk of MACE.367

Diabetes mellitus

DM is an important systemic risk factor for VC, and the incidence of VC in patients with DM is significantly higher than that in nondiabetic populations, which is closely related to the abnormal regulation of multiple metabolic pathways, including a hyperglycemic microenvironment, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and chronic inflammation, and typically presents as mixed and diffuse calcified lesions that can extensively affect large- and medium-sized vessels throughout the body.368 Persistent hyperglycemia-induced oxidative stress accelerates the degradation of elastin in the vascular wall. Elastin degradation products provide nucleation sites for mineral deposition within the vascular wall and initiate the calcification process.369 Furthermore, a hyperglycemic environment directly induces phenotypic transformation of VSMCs and promotes the expression of RUNX2 and BMP2.175 This is mainly attributed to the accumulation of AGEs, which bind to RAGE and activate the downstream NF‑κB signaling pathway. This cascade promotes the release of proinflammatory cytokines, further amplifying inflammation and oxidative stress370 and accelerating phenotypic transformation of VSMCs and matrix calcification. Meanwhile, EVs act as carriers of calcifying signals, mediate the propagation of pro-calcific signals within the vascular wall and expand the area of mineralization.

Under normal physiological conditions, insulin inhibits VC by stimulating NO release from ECs and suppressing lipoprotein oxidation.371 However, in the setting of insulin resistance (IR), the vasculoprotective effects of insulin are markedly attenuated, exacerbating glucotoxicity and dyslipidemia and significantly increasing the risk of atherosclerosis and VC.372 DM patients are often accompanied by severe dyslipidemia, which aggravates cellular injury and oxidative stress, promotes the osteochondrogenic transformation of VSMCs and accelerates calcium-phosphate crystal deposition.373,374 In contrast, HDL exerts an inhibitory effect on VC.375 Therefore, targeting metabolic abnormalities (e.g., hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia) in patients with DM may provide potential therapeutic strategies for the prevention and treatment of VC.

Chronic kidney disease

CKD promotes medial VC through complex systemic and local mechanisms, in which systemic dysregulation of calcium-phosphate metabolism is the core driver, and vitamin metabolic disorder also plays an important auxiliary role. Specifically, disturbed calcium‑phosphate homeostasis induces osteochondrogenic transformation of VSMCs and promotes hydroxyapatite deposition.376 Persistent hyperphosphatemia not only provides sufficient substrate for calcific crystal formation but also induces phenotypic transformation of VSMCs, inhibits the expression of calcification inhibitors in VSMCs, and promotes the release of numerous mineralization-competent EVs. These EVs mediate diffuse propagation of calcification, expand the mineralized area, and aggravate VC.377

In addition, vitamin K deficiency leads to dysfunction of calcification inhibitors such as MGP, which can no longer effectively chelate calcium‑phosphate complexes or inhibit crystal growth, thereby triggering the development of VC.378 Meanwhile, insufficient production of active vitamin D in CKD can induce secondary hyperparathyroidism (SHPT) and disordered bone metabolism, promoting massive release of calcium and phosphate from bone tissue into the circulation, thereby elevating circulating calcium‑phosphate levels and indirectly accelerating VC.379

Thus, CKD‑induced VC can extensively affect large and medium‑sized vessels throughout the body. Vascular wall stiffening and reduced compliance occur early in the course, with the characteristics of rapid progression and diffuse distribution that are positively correlated with the decline of renal function. Vascular wall stiffening and reduced compliance occur early in the course of CKD, even in the early stages of renal insufficiency. With disease progression, the severity of VC increases continuously, significantly elevating the risks of heart failure, hypertension and MACE.380,381 VC therefore represents one of the important predictors of cardiovascular mortality in patients with CKD, and the severity of VC is closely related to the prognosis of patients with ESRD.

Bone-vascular axis theory

A growing body of evidence indicates a close association between osteoporosis and VC, leading to the proposal of the bone‑vascular axis and calcification paradox concepts. Specifically, this phenomenon describes the coexistence of VC and osteoporosis, characterized by increased ectopic calcium deposition in the vascular wall accompanied by reduced bone mineral density or abnormal bone remodeling.382 Epidemiological data demonstrate that patients with CKD have a higher risk of both osteoporosis and VC. Notably, ESRD patients with reduced bone mineral density at different sites exhibit a significantly elevated risk of 5-year all-cause mortality.383 Clinical studies have demonstrated that osteoporosis is significantly associated with abdominal aortic calcification in postmenopausal women, independent of age and traditional cardiovascular risk factors. This association may be related to accelerated VC and impaired vascular protective pathways caused by decreased estrogen levels.384 Additionally, CKD patients are at increased risk of CAC, which is closely associated with enhanced oxidative stress.383,385,386 This paradox arises from dysregulated crosstalk between bone and vascular cells (predominantly the FGF23-Klotho-PTH axis), as well as abnormal molecular signaling networks, which ultimately disrupts the homeostatic balance between bone metabolism and vascular stability.387 Abnormal bone metabolism is mainly characterized by impaired osteoblast function and excessive osteoclast activation, leading to systemic calcium loss and disrupted calcium-phosphate homeostasis, accompanied by the release of osteogenic factors into the circulation. These factors act on the vascular wall, inhibit the activity of calcification inhibitors, induce phenotypic transformation of VSMCs and promote calcium phosphate crystal deposition, thereby accelerating VC.388,389 Of note, VC is essentially a process of vascular ectopic mineralization that may reversely impair normal bone metabolism, establishing a vicious cycle that further elevates patient mortality.382

Clinical management of this paradox must account for the reciprocal interactions between bone disease and vascular lesions, requiring a therapeutic balance between the two. This is a challenge shared by clinicians and researchers. Importantly, dysregulation of the bone-vascular axis is an independent risk factor for increased cardiovascular mortality, so clinical treatment should avoid single-target therapy that may exacerbate the other lesion. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of VC should integrate osteoporosis and related factors to elucidate its true pathogenesis and identify clinically relevant therapeutic targets. In the clinical management of patients with severe VC, targeted evaluation of bone status and renal function is essential.

Potential therapeutic strategies and pharmacological interventions

Despite significant advancements in our understanding of VC, effective clinical interventions remain limited. This chapter focuses on therapeutic agents for VC evaluated in clinical studies, primarily randomized controlled trials (RCTs), summarizing their efficacy and safety profiles (Table 1). These agents include bisphosphonates (BPs), calcimimetics, DPP-4 inhibitors (DPP-4i), phosphate binders, proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 inhibitors (PCSK9i), SNF472, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), STS, statins, vitamin D, and vitamin K. Additionally, certain antidiabetic agents, calcium channel blockers (CCB), mTOR inhibitors (e.g., rapamycin, sapanisertib) and denosumab have demonstrated anti-VC effects, although robust evidence remains scarce. Endogenous small molecules, biologicals, inhibitors, natural compounds, and phytochemicals have also shown anti-VC effects in preclinical models, but their clinical translation may be constrained by interindividual response variability and warrants further investigation (Fig. 6).

Potential therapeutic drugs for vascular calcification for different signaling pathways. Vascular calcification (VC) is a complex pathological process mediated by a network of interconnected signaling pathways. A wide range of potential therapeutic targets based on this regulatory network have been identified through extensive preclinical research. However, most of these targets and corresponding agents remain in the preclinical stage, and large-scale clinical trials are urgently needed to validate their clinical efficacy, long-term safety, and tolerability in VC patients. Bisphosphonates

BPs, classic drugs for treating bone metabolic disorders, have drawn increasing attention for their potential applications in anti-VC. These drugs exert anti-VC effects by interfering with intracellular signaling pathways involved in the phenotypic transformation of VSMCs, inhibiting the expression of specific osteogenic factors, and preventing the formation of hydroxyapatite crystals by inhibiting osteoclast activity. Clinical studies have shown that etidronate exerts anti-VC activity across multiple diseases. In 108 patients at high risk of atherosclerosis, etidronate effectively reduced the volume of abdominal aortic atherosclerotic plaques, with enhanced efficacy when combined with atorvastatin.390 In patients with arterial calcification due to deficiency of CD73, etidronate only delayed the progression of lower extremity VC, although the study had a small sample size and failed to show significant VC reversal.391 In adult patients with pseudoxanthoma elasticum (PXE), etidronate sodium compared with placebo attenuated VC progression and subretinal angiogenesis, but vigilant monitoring for hyperphosphataemia is required.392 Notably, alendronate, another BP, has shown disappointing results in clinical practice. A secondary analysis of the SALTIRE2 RCT demonstrated that alendronate failed to significantly slow down the progression of coronary or aortic calcification in patients with aortic stenosis complicated by osteoporosis.393 Moreover, a pilot RCT showed that alendronate could not significantly alter the course of VC in patients with CKD stages 3 and 4.394 These differences highlight that etidronate has limited therapeutic potential in VC compared to alendronate, although the underlying reasons, such as differences in drug targets, dosage, treatment duration, or study populations, require further clarification. Meanwhile, it may act predominantly on early-stage VC rather than established calcified lesions.

Calcimimetics

Calcimimetics, primarily including cinacalcet, etelcalcetide, and evocalcet, are currently among the most widely and deeply studied classes of drugs in the field of VC treatment. These agents exert anti-VC effects by activating class C G-protein-coupled calcium-sensing receptors (CaSR), which in turn inhibits the secretion of parathyroid hormone (PTH), reduces serum calcium and phosphorus levels, decreases the calcium-phosphorus product, and thereby inhibits the phenotypic transformation of VSMCs. Despite their clear mechanism of action, the efficacy of different calcimimetics varies across clinical and experimental studies, and their efficacy and safety still require in-depth exploration.

As a first-generation calcimimetic, cinacalcet regulates VC through multiple pathways: downregulating RUNX2, attenuating NF-κB-mediated inflammation, reducing serum PTH, calcium, and phosphorus levels, and upregulating the activity of calcification inhibitors.395,396 Multiple clinical trials have demonstrated its complex therapeutic effects in patients with VC. In the ADVANCE trial involving 360 HD patients with SHPT, cinacalcet combined with low-dose active vitamin D effectively reduced serum PTH, calcium, and phosphorus levels, although the CAC score (CACS) only showed a trend of delayed progression without statistical significance.395 In the EVOLVE trial including 3883 HD patients with moderate to severe SHPT, a 64-month follow-up showed that compared with placebo, cinacalcet did not significantly reduce the risk of death or the incidence of MACE but exhibited certain benefits presumably related to its regulatory effects on nonatherosclerotic processes. It is noteworthy that cinacalcet is associated with a significant increase in the incidence of hypocalcemia and gastrointestinal adverse reactions.397 Similarly, a small RCT involving HD patients with severe SHPT found no between-group differences in VC progression,398 while long-term use of cinacalcet in this population stabilized CACS over 12 months, with rapid PTH and FGF23 rebound upon discontinuation,396 indicating limitations in the sustainability of its efficacy.

Compared with cinacalcet, etelcalcetide has demonstrated distinct advantages in some studies. An RCT involving 326 HD patients showed that etelcalcetide was superior to the vitamin D receptor activator maxacalcitol in reducing serum calcification propensity.399 During 12 weeks of treatment, it significantly decreased serum calciprotein particle and FGF23 levels, suggesting its potential to intervene in the VC process through multiple pathways.400

In summary, current studies suggest that the efficacy of cinacalcet and etelcalcetide in treating VC is relatively limited, and careful consideration of adverse effects such as hypocalcemia and gastrointestinal discomfort is necessary during clinical application. These disparities in efficacy may stem from drug-specific properties, patient population heterogeneity, and varying stages of VC. Furthermore, promising results from evocalcet in animal models offer new directions for the development of novel calcimimetics.401 Future research should focus on expanding the indications of calcimimetics or designing more targeted agents with fewer side effects to optimize clinical therapeutic strategies for VC.

Dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitor

Dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors (DPP4i), such as gemigliptin, are primarily used in patients with DM. They exhibit anti-VC effects by inhibiting the PI3K/AKT signaling pathways and NF-κB-mediated inflammation and reducing oxidative stress, thereby suppressing the RUNX2-driven phenotypic transformation of VSMCs. A recent study involving patients with diabetic nephropathy showed that 6 months of treatment with gemigliptin reduced urinary kidney injury biomarkers (NGAL, KIM-1) and serum ALP. However, gemigliptin did not improve estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) or CACS, suggesting a limited impact on established calcification.402 The discrepancy between the reduction in kidney injury biomarkers and the lack of improvement in eGFR/CACS may be closely related to the pathological stage of VC, and during the early inflammation-dominant stage of VC, DPP-4i can block VC initiation through multitarget intervention, while in the mature calcification stage, the irreversibility of mineral deposition limits the drug’s therapeutic effect.

Notably, although short-term gemigliptin treatment failed to improve eGFR and CACS, the decrease in urinary kidney injury biomarkers suggests a renal protective effect, which may indirectly delay VC progression by improving renal function, alleviating calcium-phosphorus metabolism disorders, and reducing inflammatory responses. This potential indirect anti-VC effect requires validation with longer observation periods or larger sample sizes, and further research is needed to clarify the optimal VC stage for DPP-4i efficacy.

Phosphate binder

Hyperphosphatemia is a key driver of VC progression, and phosphate binders alleviate VC primarily by binding dietary phosphate in the intestine, reducing its absorption, and thereby lowering serum phosphate levels. In HD patients with ≥1 VC risk factor, non-calcium-based phosphate binders such as lanthanum carbonate failed to significantly delay CAC progression or reduce the incidence of cardiovascular events over 2 years compared with calcium carbonate.403,404 This finding suggests limitations of lanthanum carbonate in high-risk populations, although further subgroup analyses are needed to confirm these limitations. Conversely, sevelamer carbonate demonstrated superior efficacy in patients with CKD by reducing serum phosphate, calcium-phosphate product, FGF23, and inflammatory marker (IL-8, IFN-γ, TNFα) levels relative to calcium acetate.405 This difference may be attributed to its polymer structure, which adsorbs intestinal endotoxins and reduces the activation of chronic inflammation.

Notably, the iron-based phosphate binder sucroferric oxyhydroxide showed no significant difference from lanthanum carbonate in retarding calcification progression. However, a strict phosphorus control strategy (serum phosphorus target 3.5–4.5 mg/dl) reduced the change rate of CACS by 32% compared with standard control (5.0–6.0 mg/dl) in maintenance HD patients. This effect was more pronounced in the elderly subgroup (65–74 years), indicating that high-risk populations require stricter phosphorus management targets.406 In contrast, magnesium hydroxide, also a magnesium supplement, failed to slow the progression of VC in patients with CKD,407 suggesting that magnesium alone is of limited value as an anti-VC agent. Overall, these findings emphasize the therapeutic target of phosphate binder in mineral metabolism and inflammation.

Available evidence suggests that an ideal phosphate binder should concurrently possess potent phosphorus control, anti-inflammatory, and vascular protective properties. The advantages of sevelamer carbonate may be associated with its comprehensive mechanism of action, and multiacting noncalcium phosphate binders are prioritized for high-risk patients. Future research should focus on developing phosphate binders with more potent and targeted effects to further optimize VC therapeutic strategies.

Proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 inhibitors

PCSK9i, such as evolocumab and alirocumab, as novel lipid-lowering agents, may exert potential anti-VC effects through lipid regulation and anti-inflammatory and antioxidant actions beyond their classical lipid-lowering properties. An RCT involving 968 patients with coronary artery disease (CAD) showed that evolocumab can significantly retard the progression of coronary atherosclerosis and reduce plaque volume by potently lowering LDL-C levels. However, intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) imaging revealed no significant differences in calcified volume, fibrous volume, fibrofatty volume, or necrotic core volume of atherosclerotic plaques,408 indicating that the anti-VC potential of evolocumab is mainly dependent on its LDL-C-lowering effect and the regression of total atheroma volume, rather than the direct regulation of calcified plaque volume. Another clinical study using coronary positron emission tomography (PET) and coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) found that 18 months of evolocumab treatment can further improve coronary plaque composition and effectively inhibit microcalcification activity in CAD patients.409 This finding highlights its potential therapeutic value in targeting microcalcifications.

In conclusion, the specific molecular mechanism of PCSK9 inhibitors in regulating VC remains unclear. Future studies should explore long-term outcomes and combination therapies to determine their full potential in cardiovascular protection.

SNF472

SNF472, an intravenous formulation of inositol hexakisphosphate (IP6), is an investigational agent with promising anti-calcification activity. It inhibits hydroxyapatite crystal formation via competitive calcium chelation, thereby blocking pathological mineral deposition in VSMCs and the ECM.410 This mechanism is supported by in vitro studies demonstrating that IP6 potently reduces hydroxyapatite nucleation in serum samples from HD patients. Phase I trials confirmed its safety and tolerability in both healthy volunteers and HD patients, with single-dose administration achieving sustained hydroxyapatite growth inhibition.411 In the Phase IIb CaLIPSO trial, SNF472 significantly reduced CAC and aortic valve calcification progression in HD patients.412–414 Notably, SNF472 is metabolized to water and carbon dioxide via a renal-independent pathway, enabling its use in patients with ESRD without drug accumulation.415 Accordingly, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has granted SNF472 orphan drug designation for ESRD-related peripheral artery disease and calciphylaxis. The clinical development of SNF472 has expanded beyond VC to a broader spectrum of calcification disorders. The completed phase III CALCIPHYX trial aimed to validate its efficacy in calciphylaxis wound healing, while the primary endpoint of complete wound healing was not met. The treatment showed promising improvements in pain reduction and quality of life, consistent with phase II findings.416–418

Although SNF472 has demonstrated efficacy in delaying CAC progression in clinical trials of HD patients, its dose-dependent anti-calcific effects and dose-toxicity relationship remain incompletely characterized, and long-term data are required to rule out off-target effects at high doses. Additionally, its efficacy in VC of different etiologies warrants further investigation in large-scale studies. Nevertheless, SNF472 represents one of the most promising agents for VC to date, with the potential to redefine clinical prevention and treatment strategies for VC and related calcification disorders.

Sodium-dependent glucose transporter 2 inhibitors

SGLT2i demonstrate promising anti-calcific effects via multitargeted mechanisms. Canagliflozin attenuates VC by inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated inflammation and promoting AMPK-dependent autophagy, thereby mitigating phenotypic transformation of VSMCs.419,420 Dapagliflozin confers vascular protection by reducing ATF6-dependent ER stress and oxidative stress, which downregulates the expression of thioredoxin domain-containing protein 5 (TXNDC5). This in turn promotes ubiquitination-mediated degradation of RUNX2, reducing its protein stability and osteogenic activity.421 Empagliflozin ameliorates phosphate-induced phenotypic transformation of mouse VSMCs and VC progression by activating the AMPK-regulated Nrf2/HO-1 anti-inflammatory and antioxidant pathways.422 It also exerts antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects in preclinical models of T2DM to delay VC, further highlighting its therapeutic potential.423 Of note, a completed randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase II clinical trial was designed to evaluate the efficacy of dapagliflozin in attenuating CAC and epicardial fat volume in patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS) and T2DM. The study found that 12 months of dapagliflozin treatment did not significantly alter the progression of CACS, and there was no statistically significant difference in CACS changes compared with the placebo group.424

Sodium thiosulfate

STS, an antidote with anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, has been shown to inhibit VC progression in clinical studies and meta-analyses.425,426 However, long-term systemic administration is associated with notable safety concerns, including hypocalcemia, dysregulation of calcium-phosphate homeostasis, bone toxicity, and metabolic acidosis. To address these limitations, the development of hydroxyapatite crystal-binding peptide (ESTP)-modified nanodrug delivery systems has emerged as a promising strategy to improve targeted delivery and reduce off-target toxicity.427

Statins

Statins, as cornerstone medications for CVD treatment, have long been investigated for anti-VC potential, given their pleiotropic effects, including lipid lowering, endothelial protection, and anti-inflammatory activity. Preclinical studies have supported this potential, showing that statins can regulate osteogenic transcription factors such as RUNX2 and inhibit macrophage-mediated pro-calcification pathways. However, accumulating clinical evidence over recent years has revealed substantial contradictions, demonstrating that the role of statins in VC progression is far more complex than initially hypothesized.428–430 The multinational PARADIGM study showed that while statin therapy slowed the progression of total atherosclerotic plaque volume, it was accompanied by a significant increase in calcified plaque volume.431 This finding has been interpreted as a marker of atherosclerotic lesion stabilization, and inducing plaque calcification may be one of the mechanisms by which statins reduce the risk of MACE. Future large-scale clinical trials are warranted using multimodal imaging techniques to further characterize changes in plaque calcification composition, calcification activity, and high-risk plaque features. Stratified analyses based on VC stage are also needed to clarify the divergent effects of statins across different phases of VC development.

Vitamin D

The role of vitamin D in VC remains highly controversial, as both its deficiency and excess have been linked to accelerated pathological mineralization in patients with ESRD.432 This duality arises from its central role in calcium–phosphorus homeostasis: vitamin D increases intestinal calcium absorption and modulates renal phosphate excretion via interactions with FGF23 and PTH, thereby indirectly influencing the phenotypic transformation of VSMCs.433,434 In HD patients, cholecalciferol supplementation significantly increases serum 25(OH)D and fetuin-A without significantly altering circulating FGF23 or OPG levels.435,436 Similarly, in patients with CKD and SHPT, 8 weeks of treatment with calcitriol or paricalcitol showed no between-group differences in CAC progression or valvular calcification severity.437 These findings suggest that vitamin D supplementation may upregulate systemic calcification inhibitors such as fetuin-A but does not directly inhibit VC progression. Current clinical practice recommends low-dose 25(OH)D supplementation to avoid exacerbating VC in patients with ESRD, although the optimal therapeutic threshold remains undefined.438

Vitamin K

Vitamin K deficiency is closely associated with increased CVD risk, particularly in patients with CKD. Its anti-VC effects are primarily mediated by enhancing the activity of vitamin K-dependent calcification inhibitors and downregulating components of the innate immune system.378 Numerous clinical trials have explored the potential of vitamin K supplementation for preventing, delaying, or reversing VC, but the outcomes have been inconsistent. A trial in community-dwelling older adults showed that participants receiving vitamin K1 (phylloquinone) had a 6% slower rate of CAC progression than those receiving multivitamins alone.439 However, a 12-month study found that while phylloquinone supplementation reduced circulating dephospho-uncarboxylated MGP (dp-ucMGP) levels by 86%, there were no significant between-group differences in absolute or relative CAC progression.440 This inconsistency is further evident across populations: combined supplementation with vitamin K2 (menaquinone) and native vitamin D has beneficial effects on VC in pediatric HD patients,441 whereas in adult populations, similar supplementation inhibits pro-VC factors but does not significantly delay VC progression.442,443

Despite extensive clinical research, current evidence does not support the use of vitamin K supplements as a reliable method for preventing or reversing VC.

Despite extensive clinical investigation, current evidence does not support the use of vitamin K supplements as a reliable intervention for preventing or reversing VC. Future studies should focus on evaluating higher doses, longer treatment durations, alternative administration routes, and the use of vitamin K as an adjuvant therapy in combination with other anti-VC agents.

Other potential treatments

Therapeutic research for VC has expanded beyond traditional targets, with a wide range of cross-indication drugs, endogenous molecules, and natural compounds showing therapeutic potential via diverse mechanisms. However, these strategies vary widely in their mechanisms of action, clinical translation feasibility, and clinical application scenarios, requiring systematic evaluation based on mechanistic complementarity and the hierarchy of clinical evidence.

Cross-indication drugs

Multiple drugs approved for other conditions have demonstrated preclinical efficacy against VC. The glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) analog liraglutide combats VC by modulating the PI3K/AKT and MAPK pathways,444,445 while metformin attenuates VC in rodent models via activation of AMPK signaling.446 Azelnidipine, a CCB, inhibits phenotypic transformation of VSMCs by suppressing Notch-dependent MSX2 signaling, thereby preventing pathological mineralization.447 In addition, mTOR inhibitors, including rapamycin and sapanisertib, indirectly inhibit VC via multipathway modulation in preclinical models, although they are primarily developed for oncology and transplant immunosuppression indications.448,449 Denosumab, a monoclonal antibody for osteoporosis, specifically inhibits RANKL and thus has theoretical inhibitory effects on VC.450

The current studies of these cross-indication agents are mostly limited to in vitro cellular experiments and in vivo animal models. High-quality clinical evidence supporting their routine use for VC treatment is lacking, and further large-scale clinical trials are required to validate their efficacy and safety in patients with VC.

Endogenous small molecules, biologics and inhibitors

Endogenous small molecules and biological agents have opened new directions for VC treatment by enabling precise targeting of key signaling pathways. As an NAD⁺-dependent deacetylase, SIRT1 inhibits WNT/β-catenin signaling when activated, reducing VC development in both animal and ex vivo human models.451,452 Resveratrol, a natural SIRT1 activator, suppresses phenotypic transformation of VSMCs and VC via this pathway while also inhibiting NF-κB signaling and enhancing endogenous antioxidant defenses.453,454 To address the low oral bioavailability of resveratrol, synthetic SIRT-activating compounds (such as resVida, Longevinex R, SRT501)455,456 and non-resveratrol-derived SIRT1 activators (such as SRT1720, SRT2379 and SRT2104) have been developed, with preclinical studies showing improved potency and bioavailability.457–459 Furthermore, the development and application of analogs of the calcification inhibitor MGP represent a potential therapeutic strategy for VC.460

For targeted pathway inhibition, Dickkopf-related protein 1 (DKK1) blocks the WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway,461 while LDN193189 and Noggin antagonize the BMP signaling pathway, both of which function by reducing RUNX2 expression.243,462 Inhibitors of the PI3K/AKT, MAPK, NF-κB, and Notch pathways have also validated anti-VC effects in preclinical models,239,249,265,283,445,463–465 but balancing therapeutic efficacy and toxicity remains a major barrier to clinical translation. Notably, the ROCK inhibitor Y-27632 exhibits paradoxical effects: in a high-glucose environment, it can both inhibit phenotypic transformation of VSMCs and promote VC by upregulating ALP activity, indicating the cell-specific and environment-dependent nature of VC regulation.342,346 This highlights the need for future studies to deeply explore the refined mechanisms of drug action and avoid potential risks of single-pathway intervention.

Natural compounds and phytochemicals

Natural compounds and phytochemicals provide a rich library of potential drug precursors for VC treatment. Capsaicin reduces the activity of osteogenesis-related proteins by inhibiting WNT/β-catenin signaling and RUNX2 transcriptional activity,466 while paeonoside (PASI) regulates the BMP2 and WNT3a pathways in osteogenic lineage cells.467 PNWE and rosmarinic acid target the NF-κB and WNT/β-catenin pathways, with rosmarinic acid additionally activating Nrf2 to enhance antioxidant defense.324,468 Aloe emodin (AE) suppresses the expression of osteogenic markers and attenuates calcium-induced VC in a concentration-dependent manner.469 Puerarin, gallic acid, ellagic acid, and baicalein inhibit pro-calcific BMP signaling; among them, puerarin also regulates PI3K/AKT and NF-κB signaling.106,463,470–472 Epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG) and icariin modulate ERK and p38 MAPK signaling, respectively, to inhibit VC.473,474 CCM exerts anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects to regulate VC progression,475,476 whereas policosanol modulates metabolic pathways to attenuate phosphate-induced VC via AMPK signaling.477 Although natural compounds exhibit promising therapeutic potential and may enable long-term VC modulation via dietary guidance, current research remains largely limited to preclinical studies. Further drug development should prioritize high-quality clinical trials to validate their efficacy, safety, and optimal dosing regimens in human VC.

Overall, potential therapeutic strategies for VC are characterized by multitarget mechanisms, and concurrent intervention of interconnected signaling axes such as BMP, MAPK, and NF-κB may exert synergistic anti-calcific effects. Importantly, most therapeutic targets for VC share signaling pathways with the skeletal system, necessitating careful evaluation of concurrent effects on bone metabolism during drug development. While multiple agents, including BPs, calcimimetics, DPP-4i, phosphate binders, PCSK9i, SGLT2i, statins, vitamin D, and vitamin K, theoretically inhibit VC and have shown efficacy in cellular and animal studies, clinical trial results have been largely inconsistent and unsatisfactory, precluding their use as first-line therapies for VC. SNF472, by contrast, has demonstrated consistent efficacy in delaying CAC progression and a favorable safety profile in phase II clinical trials, representing one of the most promising agents for VC. Cross-indication agents, endogenous molecules, and natural compounds also show preclinical therapeutic potential, but their clinical development is challenged by off-target effects, narrow therapeutic windows, and barriers to clinical translation. Future research should prioritize the development of targeted drug delivery systems (e.g., ESTP-modified nanotherapeutics) to precisely modulate pro-calcific signaling pathways in diseased vascular tissue while minimizing interference with normal physiological functions, thereby improving the therapeutic index and safety of anti-VC agents.

Conclusions and perspectives

VC is now recognized as a highly regulated and active pathological process driven by an imbalance between pro-calcific stimuli and endogenous anti-calcific defense mechanisms, rather than a passive degenerative consequence of aging or ectopic calcium deposition. Although phenotypic transformation of VSMCs represents a central event in VC pathogenesis, it is by no means the sole contributor. EndMT, ECM remodeling, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, ER stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and multiple forms of programmed cell death (including autophagy, apoptosis, pyroptosis and ferroptosis) collectively participate in the initiation and progression of vascular mineralization. Importantly, these mechanisms do not operate independently. Instead, they are integrated through key molecular hubs, particularly RUNX2, which coordinates multiple signaling pathways, including WNT/β-catenin, BMP/Smad, PI3K/AKT, MAPK, NF-κB, AMPK and Rho/ROCK. The extensive crosstalk among these pathways establishes a highly interconnected regulatory network that ultimately drives vascular cells toward osteogenic and chondrogenic phenotypes and accelerates mineral deposition. Based on this conceptual framework, we propose that VC should be regarded as a “network disease”. Future therapeutic strategies should therefore focus on targeting critical regulatory nodes within this network or simultaneously modulating multiple interacting pathways to restore the equilibrium between pro-calcific and anti-calcific mechanisms.

Despite substantial advances in elucidating the molecular basis of VC, translating experimental discoveries into clinical benefit remains challenging. Three major obstacles warrant particular attention. First, VC exhibits marked heterogeneity. Atherosclerosis-associated intimal calcification, CKD-related medial calcification, DM-associated diffuse calcification, and calcific valvular disease share certain molecular signatures but differ considerably in their initiating stimuli, cellular composition, disease trajectories, and imaging characteristics. Future studies should prioritize the identification of subtype-specific drivers and biomarkers to facilitate patient stratification and enable precision medicine approaches. Second, current experimental models possess significant limitations. Most mechanistic studies rely on simplified in vitro culture systems and young or genetically modified animal models, which fail to recapitulate the complex interplay among aging, metabolic disturbances, chronic inflammation, and long-term environmental exposures that characterize human disease. Consequently, rigorous validation is required before translating preclinical findings into clinical applications. Prospective longitudinal cohort studies integrating high-resolution imaging modalities, circulating biomarkers, and histopathological analyses are urgently needed to establish causal relationships and identify intervention targets with genuine clinical relevance. Third, the challenge of maintaining the bone-vascular axis remains unresolved. Vascular mineral metabolism and skeletal mineral metabolism share multiple regulatory pathways, including BMP, WNT and RANKL signaling. Therapeutic interventions designed to suppress pathological VC may inadvertently impair physiological bone remodeling, thereby increasing the risk of osteoporosis and fractures. Accordingly, future drug development should systematically evaluate skeletal safety and prioritize the design of novel agents or targeted delivery strategies capable of selectively inhibiting ectopic vascular mineralization while preserving normal bone homeostasis.

Emerging technologies offer unprecedented opportunities to overcome these barriers. Single-cell sequencing, spatial transcriptomics and integrative multi-omics analyses have begun to uncover previously unrecognized cellular subpopulations and dynamic intercellular communication networks within calcified vascular lesions. These approaches may enable precise identification of the cellular lineages and molecular axes that drive disease progression. Meanwhile, studies of epigenetic regulation, including ncRNAs (lncRNAs, miRNAs and circRNAs) and PTMs such as phosphorylation, acetylation, ubiquitination, lactylation, carbonylation, SUMOylation, methylation and O-GlcNAcylation, are evolving from single-molecule investigations toward multilayered integrative analyses. Future efforts should focus on constructing tissue-specific regulatory network maps and identifying reliable biomarkers applicable to early detection, disease staging, and dynamic monitoring. Moreover, the development of safe and effective anti-calcification therapies remains an unmet clinical need. Numerous candidate agents have demonstrated promising efficacy in preclinical models but have failed to consistently translate into meaningful patient benefit. Among the agents currently undergoing clinical evaluation, SNF472, which competitively chelates calcium ions and selectively inhibits the nucleation and growth of hydroxyapatite crystals, has shown the most encouraging effects in slowing the progression of calcification while maintaining a favorable safety profile. It has also received U.S. FDA designation for the treatment of ESRD-related peripheral artery disease and calciphylaxis. Nevertheless, its definitive clinical value requires confirmation through larger RCT with extended follow-up to determine whether delaying calcification progression ultimately translates into reductions in cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality. In addition, drug repurposing strategies involving SGLT2i, metformin, and denosumab; endogenous bioactive molecules such as MGP analogs and SIRT1 activators; natural compounds including capsaicin, resveratrol, and curcumin; and combination therapies all warrant further investigation. Novel technologies, such as targeted nanodelivery systems using hydroxyapatite-binding peptide-modified EVs and tissue-specific drug delivery platforms, may enhance lesion selectivity while minimizing off-target effects and therefore hold considerable therapeutic promise.

In conclusion, VC is an active pathological process orchestrated by intricate interactions among diverse cellular and molecular mechanisms. Future research should move beyond reductionist approaches centered on individual molecules toward systems-level investigations that integrate disease context, patient characteristics, and inter-organ communication. A deeper understanding of signaling pathway crosstalk, the evolution of cellular heterogeneity, and the dynamic epigenetic regulatory landscape will be essential for overcoming current translational barriers. Such advances are expected to accelerate the implementation of precision medicine strategies and ultimately facilitate the prevention, attenuation, and potentially even partial reversal of VC and its associated cardiovascular complications.
