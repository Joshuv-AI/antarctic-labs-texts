Determinants of ethical care provision among nurses working in public hospitals of Wolaita Zone, South Ethiopia
Authors: Muluemebet Dejene, Tilahun Saol, Tesfaye Yitna Chichiabellu, Nefsu Awoke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEthical care provision among nurses refers to the provision of care that is in line with ethical principles, which include autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice. Ethical care is fundamental to effective and safe care. Violations of ethics can result in negative outcomes for patients and erode trust in healthcare providers. There are gaps between accepted ethical principles and ethical care provision in Ethiopia. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the ethical care provision and its determinants among nurses.MethodsAn institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 411 nurses in public hospitals of the Wolaita Zone, South Ethiopia, from August 1 to 30, 2023. The study comprised two components: a self-administered survey (n = 411) and an embedded overt observational component. Of the total participants, 354 nurses were selected for observation, with 322 successfully completing the component. A simple random sampling method was used to select study participants from selected hospitals. The data were entered into EpiData version 4.6, and analysis was done using SPSS version 25. Bivariate and multivariate logistic regression were used to identify associated factors. Significance was considered at a P-value < 0.05 in the multivariate analysis.ResultsThe prevalence of self-reported good ethical care provision was 64.5%, while observed practice was 59.9%. Nurses who had good knowledge of the ethical care principles (AOR 5.23; 95% CI 2.27, 12.03), nurses who had good communication skills (AOR 2.32; 95% CI 1.07, 5.00), nurses who were satisfied with their jobs (AOR 9.53; 95% CI 4.54, 20.02), nurses who had training in the nursing code of ethics (AOR 5.68; 95% CI 2.56, 12.59), and nurses who had favorable attitudes (AOR 4.42; 95% CI 2.09, 9.40) were significantly associated with ethical care provision.ConclusionThe ethical care provision in this study was suboptimal. Knowledge, attitude, communication skills, training, and job satisfaction were the predictors of ethical care provision. Therefore, providing in-service training for nurses to improve their skills in the ethical care provision and communication was recommended to maximize the ethical care provision. In addition, nurses should engage in continuous professional development to enhance their knowledge and skills in ethical care.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

Ethical care provision in nursing involves adhering to rigorous professional standards while fundamentally respecting patient rights [1–3]. These ethical frameworks serve as essential guides for daily clinical practice. Since the International Council of Nurses (ICN) established the inaugural global code of ethics in 1953 [4], numerous nations have developed or adapted national codes to govern professional conduct; prominent examples include the frameworks established by the American Nurses Association [5] and the Canadian Nurses Association [6]. Ethiopia has similarly adopted these standards to direct the professional ethics of its nursing practitioners [7].

Upholding patient dignity requires a steadfast commitment to autonomy, ensuring that patients are actively involved in decisions regarding their care and daily activities. Furthermore, maintaining confidentiality and privacy remains a cornerstone of ethical practice [8]. In contemporary healthcare environments, ethical literacy is indispensable for nurses to fulfill their professional obligations, navigate complex clinical scenarios, and ensure the delivery of safe, legal, and appropriate care [9, 10]. An ethically conscious caregiver is characterized by a commitment to professional accountability and a profound respect for the patient [11]. Ultimately, high-quality ethical care is defined by the patient’s sense of both physical and psychological security [9].

The prevalence of ethical care provision varies significantly across developing nations. For instance, research in Nepal’s Pokhara region indicated that 76.4% of nurses demonstrated adequate ethical practice [9]. However, studies conducted in Ethiopia among healthcare professionals suggest a lower adherence to these standards, with the magnitude of ethical practice according to the code of ethics ranging from 32.68% to 46.7% [12–14].

Despite the fundamental role of nurses in delivering ethical patient care, ethical violations persist within healthcare settings. These breaches not only compromise physical and emotional well-being but also erode trust in healthcare providers and adversely affect patient outcomes [15–17]. Furthermore, unethical conduct tarnishes the professional reputation of individual nurses and the nursing profession as a whole, while potentially leading to legal and disciplinary consequences. Consequently, identifying the determinants of these violations is essential for developing targeted strategies to promote consistent ethical care provision [18–21].

Existing literature identifies a multifaceted range of factors contributing to unethical nursing practices. These include individual-level challenges, such as insufficient ethical training, limited awareness, and low job satisfaction, alongside systemic barriers like staffing shortages, excessive workloads, and inadequate resource allocation. Furthermore, organizational constraints—including a lack of clear clinical guidelines, ineffective supervisory support, and the absence of functional institutional ethics committees—significantly impede the delivery of ethical care. These challenges are often exacerbated by poor provider-patient communication and institutional cultures that fail to prioritize patient-centeredness and autonomy [11, 22–25].

There is, therefore, a need for nurses to embrace ethical care, which contributes to high-quality patient care. Studies related to ethics in the health profession have been conducted in various countries in the world, including Ethiopia [9, 12–14, 26]. There remains a need for focused research on the nursing workforce, given their unique role in direct patient care. Therefore, this study aimed to assess ethical care provision and its determinants among nurses working in public hospitals of the Wolaita Zone.

Methods

Study area and period

The study was conducted in the selected public hospitals of the Wolaita Zone. There are 8 public hospitals (Wolaita Sodo University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital and 7 primary hospitals) and 68 health centers in the Wolaita Zone. According to the data obtained from the human resources departments of different public healthcare facilities, currently, there are 3790 healthcare workers (HCWs) in Wolaita Zone; among these, 2156 are nurses [27, 28]. The study was conducted from August 1 to August 30, 2023.

Study design

This was an institution-based cross-sectional study with two components: a self-administered survey among 411 nurses and an embedded overt observational component among a subsample of eligible nurses.

Source population

All nurses who were working in public hospitals of the Wolaita Zone.

Study population

All nurses who were working in selected public hospitals of the Wolaita Zone.

Inclusion criteria

Nurses who had been working for more than six months before the data collection period.

Exclusion criteria

Nurses who did not participate in direct patient care (head nurses and matrons).

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

Depending on the study objective, sample size was calculated by using a single population proportion formula and a double population proportion formula. The highest sample size was obtained from the double population proportion formula calculated using OpenEpi version 7.2 software with the assumptions below and predictor variables from previous studies (Table 1). Based on the given assumption and the result from a previous study, the highest calculated sample size, 411, was the final sample size for this study.

Sampling procedure

From eight public hospitals in the Wolaita Zone, four public hospitals—Wolaita Sodo University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Boditi Primary Hospital, Bele Primary Hospital, and Bitena Primary Hospital—were selected by simple random sampling. The study population consisted of nursing professionals involved in active delivery of care to admitted patients working in medical, surgical, obstetrics and gynecology, intensive care unit (ICU), recovery, pediatrics, dialysis, oncology, and emergency wards in selected public hospitals in the Wolaita Zone. The number of nurses who were involved in the direct care of patients selected from the four hospitals was 505; among them, 411 study participants were selected by the simple random technique, the lottery method. For the observational component, a sub-sample of 354 nurses was selected. Of the 354 nurses selected for observation, 322 observations were successfully completed, resulting in a 91.0% completion rate. The remaining 32 nurses from emergency department were excluded since it’s very busy and crowded, which makes the observational study challenging. In addition, the nature of some of the patients (for example, unconscious and critical patients) makes it difficult to apply ethical principles such as obtaining permission for care (informed consent).

Data collection tools

Data were collected by using structured, self-administered, and pretested questionnaires. The questionnaire was adapted from a previous study conducted in Ethiopia among health care professionals [14, 26] (Supplementary file 1). The questionnaire has two parts. Part I, the self-administered questionnaire, consists of six sections as follows:

Section I: Socio-Demographic Information: This section included six items gathering socio-demographic data from participants. Section II: Ethical Care Provision: This section assessed nurses’ self-reported ethical care provision using 14 items. The questions were based on the seven ethical principles of nursing (Autonomy, Beneficence, Justice, Nonmaleficence, Confidentiality, Fidelity, and Veracity) [29]. Responses were measured on a 5-point Likert scale: Always (5), Very often [4), Sometimes (3), Rarely (2), and Never (1). This scale was adapted from a previous study [14]. Ethical care provision was categorized according to Bloom’s cut-off [30] and a previous study [14] as “good” if the score was ≥ 75% of the maximum possible score and “poor” if < 75%. Section III: Knowledge of Ethical Care Principles: This section assessed nurses’ knowledge of ethical care principles using 11 yes/no questions. Participants scoring ≥ 75% were classified as having “good knowledge,” while those scoring < 75% were classified as having “poor knowledge.” The response was categorized according to modified Bloom’s cut-off [31] for binary distinction and previous study [14]. Section IV: Attitude Towards Ethical Care Provision: This section evaluated nurses’ attitudes towards ethical care using 13 items with a 5-point Likert scale response: (1) Strongly disagree, (2) Disagree, (3) Neutral, (4) Agree, (5) Strongly agree. Participants scoring ≥ 75% (≥ 48.75 points) were categorized as having a “favorable attitude,” and those scoring < 75% were categorized as having an “unfavorable attitude” [26]. The response was categorized according to modified Bloom’s cut-off [31] for binary distinction and previous study [14]. Section V: Communication Skills: This section assessed nurses’ communication skills using 14 standardized items with a 5-point Likert scale response: (1) Strongly disagree, (2) Disagree, (3) Neutral, (4) Agree, and (5) Strongly agree [32]. Participants scoring ≥ 60% (≥ 39 points) were grouped as having “good communication skills,” while those scoring < 60% were grouped as having “poor communication skills” [20]. Section VI: Profession and Organization Characteristics: This section gathered data on 17 items related to nurses’ professional characteristics and the organizational context [14]. Job Satisfaction was assessed using a single dichotomous item: “Satisfaction with your job” with response options of “satisfied” and “dissatisfied.” The internal consistency reliability, such as Cronbach’s alpha, was not estimated since the variable was measured using a single-item dichotomous response.

Part II: Observational checklist and procedure

A 10-item observational checklist adapted from previous studies conducted in Ghana and Ethiopia [7, 33], was used to assess the ethical care provision through overt non-participatory observation. The observational outcome was derived by scoring each of the 10 items as ‘Yes’ (performed) or ‘No’ (not performed). Based on the scoring criteria of previous literature [33], nursing practice was categorized as ‘adequate’ if the nurse correctly performed more than 60% of the items (a score of 7 or higher) and ‘inadequate’ if they performed 60% or fewer (a score of 6 or lower).

The observational component was conducted to supplement and triangulate the findings from the self-reported survey The data collectors obtained informed voluntary verbal and signed consent from each respondent prior to data collection. Each participating nurse was observed exactly once during a single shift. To capture a diverse range of clinical activities, observations were distributed across morning, afternoon, and night shifts. During each session, which lasted approximately 1 hour the observer positioned themselves discreetly within the ward to witness nurse-patient interactions without interfering with care. To minimize the Hawthorne effect, observers did not provide details of the study procedures.

The nurses were supposed to have provided good ethical care if they met the following standards while being observed by data collectors. He/she displayed the following qualities: a neat and clean uniform, work-appropriate clothing, short hair and nails, clean hands, an appropriate facial expression, clear nursing diagnoses recorded in the registration book, timely administration of prescribed medications, and consideration, punctuality, and application of the nursing process for each client. Observations of study participants determined whether they provided unethical care if they displayed any of the following: inappropriate hair, nails, and hands; the inappropriate language used while delivering nursing care; inappropriate facial expressions; unclear nursing diagnoses recorded in the registration book; a poorly organized nursing care plan; and untimely administration of prescribed medication [7, 33].

Data collectors

Five data collectors with a minimum of a B.Sc. in Nursing were involved in data collection of the self-administered questionnaire and observational checklist. Their activity was supervised by four supervisors with an MSc qualification in the health field. To minimize the observer bias in the observational study, detailed training was given for the data collectors

Data quality control

Two days of training were provided to data collectors and supervisors on the study’s objectives and the contents of the questionnaire. The questionnaire was pretested with 5% of the study population at Gasuba Primary Hospital. Based on the pretest findings, the questionnaire was evaluated for clarity, simplicity, understandability, and coherence of questions. Supervisors reviewed each questionnaire daily to ensure completeness, accuracy, and clarity. The reliability of the tool was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, which yielded a value of 0.897 for the dependent variable.

Data processing and analysis procedures

Collected data was checked for its completeness, cleaned for missed values, and coded. The coded data was entered into EpiData version 4.6 and then exported into SPSS window version 25.0 for analysis. Descriptive statistics were carried out and summarized with texts, tables, and figures. A binary logistic regression model was used to assess the association between the outcome variables and the predictor variables. Variables with a p-value less than 0.25 in bivariate analysis were fitted into the multivariate analysis for controlling confounders and to identify the independent predictors of the outcome variables. In multivariable analysis, the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was used to assess model fitness. The model indicated an adequate fit to the data (Chi-square = 3.888, df = 8, p = 0.867). Before including two independent variables in the multivariable logistic regression, the multicollinearity between them was checked using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and one of the independent variables was dropped if the VIF was > 10. Adjusted odds ratios and P-values were calculated. Finally, variables with a p-value < = 0.05 at a 95% confidence interval were declared statistically significant.

Ethical consideration

Ethical clearance and approval were obtained from the Ethical Review Committee of Wolaita Sodo University College of Health Science and Medicine (Ref. No. CRCSD4/1993/2011). Official letters of cooperation were sent to all public hospitals participating in this study, and formal permission was secured from each to conduct the research. All methods were carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. Informed written consent was acquired from all participants after they received a detailed explanation of the study’s objectives and nature. Participants were informed of the right to withdraw or to interrupt their participation at any time without penalty or loss of benefits. Confidentiality was maintained throughout the research process by assigning code identification to participants instead of using their names.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of nurses

In this study, 411 nurses participated in a self-administered survey with a response rate of 100%. For the observational component, 354 nurses were sampled from the survey participants, of whom 322 completed observations, yielding a response rate of 91%. Among the study participants, 278 (67.6%) were females, and 200 (48.7%) of them were between 20 and 30 years old with a mean ± SD of 32.14 ± 5.42 years. The majority of the respondents had a first degree (266, 64.7%), and 147 (35.8%) of them had 5–10 years of work experience with a mean ± SD of 7.80 ± 4.9 years. Among the study respondents, 163 (39.7%) had a monthly income of 5000–7000 ETB. Among nurses, 83 (20.2%) of them were from the medical ward (Table 2).

The self-reported ethical care provision indicated that the majority of respondents, 265 (64.5%), had good ethical care provision, while the observational study showed that the level of ethical care provision of nurses was 59.9% (Fig. 1).

Individual factors

The study found that 304 (74.0%) of study participants were happy with their current profession. The primary source of information for professional ethics was college/university 254 (61.8%). One-third of the study participants, 164 (39.9%), had been accused of unethical health care issues. Truth-telling was the most common ethical dilemma, at 133 (32.4%). Among nurses who perceived unethical health care provision, the most common reason reported was workload, 157 (38.2%). In terms of knowledge, attitude, and communication skills, the study found that 224 (54.5%) of nurses had good knowledge of ethical care principles, 247 (60.1%) had a favorable attitude towards ethical care provision, and 69.1% had good communication skills. The study found that 262 (63.7%) of nurses were satisfied with their job, and only 236 (57.4%) had training in the nursing code of ethics (Table 3).

Factors associated with ethical care provision among nurses

Out of 19 independent variables, twelve were selected as candidate variables for multiple logistic regression analysis based on a bivariate analysis with a p-value of less than 0.25, following a multicollinearity test. In the multivariate analysis, five variables demonstrated a significant association (p-value < 0.05) with the ethical care provision: nurses’ knowledge of ethical care principles, communication skills, job satisfaction, training on the nursing code of ethics, and attitudes towards nursing ethics.

Nurses with good knowledge of the ethical care principles were 5.23 times more likely to provide good ethical care (AOR 5.23; 95% CI 2.27, 12.03) compared to those with poor knowledge. Additionally, nurses with good communication skills were 2.32 times more likely to provide good ethical care (AOR 2.32; 95% CI 1.07, 5.00) compared to those with poor communication skills. Those who reported being satisfied with their jobs were 9.53 times more likely to provide good ethical care (AOR 9.53; 95% CI 4.54, 20.02) compared to nurses who were dissatisfied with their jobs. Furthermore, Nurses who received training in the nursing code of ethics were 5.68 times more likely to provide good ethical care (AOR 5.68; 95% CI 2.56, 12.59) compared to those who did not receive training. Finally, nurses with favorable attitudes towards ethical care provision were 4.42 times more likely to provide good ethical care (AOR: 4.42; 95% CI 2.09, 9.40) compared to those with unfavorable attitudes (see Table 5).

Discussion

This study investigated the prevalence of ethical care provision and its associated factors among nurses in public hospitals within the Wolaita Zone. The findings revealed that 64.5% (95% CI: 59.6–69.1) of nurses reported providing ethical care. According to an observational study, the magnitude of ethical care provision was 59.9%. In this study, 164 (39.9%) of the study participants reported having been accused of unethical care provision.

The prevalence of ethical care provision observed in this study was higher than that reported in Bale Zone (45.6%) [12] and Gurage Zone (53%) [7]. This discrepancy may be attributed to variations in healthcare facility levels and the specific populations studied. For instance, the Bale Zone study was limited to a single hospital and included all healthcare professionals, whereas our research was conducted across multiple hospitals and specifically focused on nurses.

Conversely, our finding was lower than the studies reported at Zagazig University, Egypt (97.8%) [34], University hospital in Mansoura, Egypt (75%) [33], in Ghana (98%) [22], and in Pokhara, Nepal (76.4%) [9]. This disparity can be explained by variations in socioeconomic, cultural, and organizational factors, along with differences in leadership quality, the presence of role models, and levels of management support across different regions. Additionally, differences in study units could contribute; for example, the Zagazig study focused exclusively on nurses in hemodialysis units [34], while our participants were drawn from all working units across the hospitals, potentially capturing a broader range of ethical challenges.

Regarding specific determinants, nurses with good communication skills were found to be 2.32 times more likely to provide good ethical care compared to those with poor communication skills. This finding aligns with a study conducted in Iran [35]. This is likely due to the ability of proficient communicators to effectively convey health information, treatment options, and potential risks, thereby respecting patient autonomy and fostering ethical decision-making [36, 37]. Good communication skills also enable nurses to discuss ethical concerns, address conflicts, and compassionately provide accurate information, ultimately promoting higher standards of ethical care provision [38]. Therefore, developing stronger communication skills among nurses could enhance their capacity for ethical practice and lead to improved patient outcomes.

Nurses who had received training in the nursing code of ethics were 5.68 times more likely to provide good ethical care compared to nurses who had not received training. This result is in line with the studies conducted in the Gurage Zone [7], Addis Ababa [39], and Indonesia [29]. This suggests that nurses with such training possess a deeper understanding of the ethical issues they face and are better equipped to apply ethical principles in their work [40]. To improve their ability to address ethical challenges in healthcare, nurses should engage in continuous education on moral principles, legal frameworks, and professional requirements. Training programs can equip nurses with the skills necessary to utilize ethical decision-making models, engage in reflective practice, and effectively manage moral quandaries, ensuring they deliver excellent, patient-centered care in line with evolving ethical standards [41].

Ethical care provision was significantly associated with nurses’ knowledge of core ethical principles. A robust understanding of these principles is fundamental for nurses to fulfill their professional obligations of beneficence and non-maleficence in clinical settings [42]. In this study, nurses with high levels of ethical knowledge were 5.23 times more likely to provide ethical care than those with poor knowledge levels. This finding is consistent with research conducted in the Central Gondar Zone [14] and in Addis Ababa [39]. This positive association likely stems from the fact that knowledgeable nurses are better equipped to identify complex ethical dilemmas and apply appropriate moral frameworks to guide their decision-making [40, 43]. Consequently, institutionalizing continuous professional development and ensuring nurses remain current with ethical best practices are essential for maintaining high standards of healthcare delivery [44].

Furthermore, maintaining favorable attitudes is critical for upholding the pillars of bioethics—autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice—as well as professional standards regarding human dignity, advocacy, and trust [45]. Our analysis revealed that nurses with favorable attitudes were 4.42 times more likely to provide ethical care compared to those with unfavorable attitudes. This finding aligns with research conducted in Iran [35], which suggested that positive ethical attitudes strengthen a practitioner’s commitment to moral care. This association may be explained by the predictive influence of attitude on behavior; nurses with a positive ethical outlook are more inclined to translate their moral convictions into constructive clinical actions [38]. Similar results have been reported in the Central Gondar Zone [14], a nationwide Ethiopian survey across 24 health facilities [13], and studies from Addis Ababa [39] and Iran [46].

Our findings demonstrate a significant association between job satisfaction and adherence to ethical care principles. In the multivariable model, job satisfaction emerged as an independent predictor; specifically, nurses satisfied with their roles were 9.53 times more likely to provide high-quality ethical care than their dissatisfied counterparts. However, the magnitude of this association should be interpreted with caution due to the wide confidence interval and the inherent limitations of the cross-sectional study design. This finding aligns with previous research conducted in Gondar [14], in Addis Ababa (39], and a nationwide assessment across 24 Ethiopian health facilities [13]. This association may be explained by the fact that job satisfaction fosters a greater commitment to professional standards. Satisfied nurses are more likely to exert maximum effort in patient care and are less susceptible to the stressors and burnout that often compromise ethical decision-making. Furthermore, job satisfaction often reflects effective management practices, which serve as positive reinforcement for adhering to professional codes of conduct [46]. Nurses working in supportive environments—characterized by transformative leadership, adequate resource allocation, and a positive organizational culture—report higher levels of dedication and improved teamwork. These systemic factors collectively reduce the risk of burnout and empower healthcare professionals to uphold rigorous ethical standards, even in challenging clinical situations [47].

Strength and limitation of the study

This study employed an institution-based cross-sectional study with two components: a self-administered survey and an embedded overt observational component to assess the ethical care provision among nurses, who are the primary actors in the healthcare setting and are present 24 h a day/7 days a week.

The study may have had the following limitations: First, there is social desirability bias since the study assessed self-reporting practices; there might be overreported behavior, which was minimized by explaining the purpose of the research. Second, even though there is a strong positive correlation between ethical care delivery and job satisfaction, the large confidence interval suggests that the data should be interpreted cautiously because of this imprecision, most likely due to a small number of nurses in one of the job satisfaction categories.

Because the observation was used to collect data for the observational study, the Hawthorne effect may have occurred. The observer may have influenced the nurses’ behaviors, as this approach assumes that the nurses are aware of being observed. Additionally, the observer’s interpretation of the definitions and the actual circumstances could affect the reliability of the data collected. Additionally, nurses working in the emergency department were omitted from the observation study due to the difficulty of applying ethical principles such as obtaining permission for care (informed consent). As a result, the study’s findings should be interpreted with caution. Because a single observation per nurse was used to measure ethical care provision, we recommend that the study adopt a longitudinal observational approach with multiple observations per participant to better understand ethical care provision in the future.

Implication for practice

This study helps healthcare settings by emphasizing the importance of training nurses about ethical principles, patient communication skills, and the nursing code of ethics—all of which are fundamental to preserving the standard of care given to patients. Training can improve nurses’ knowledge and attitudes to improve ethical care delivery, which ultimately results in patients receiving respectful care. The organizations need to act on the underlying causes of nurses’ dissatisfaction, which can result in moral distress and ethical violations.

Conclusion

It was found that nurses working in public hospitals in the Wolaita Zone provided suboptimal ethical care. The ethical care provision was strongly associated with knowledge of the ethical care principles, attitude toward it, job satisfaction, nurses’ communication skills, and ethics training. In order to maximize the ethical care provision, it was suggested that nurses receive in-service training to enhance their communication and ethical caregiving abilities. In order to improve their expertise in providing ethical care, nurses should also participate in ongoing professional development.
