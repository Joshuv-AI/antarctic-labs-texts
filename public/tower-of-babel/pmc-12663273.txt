Self-sensing capabilities in steel fiber-reinforced concrete for shrinkage resistance and structural health monitoring
Authors: Masroor Ahmad, Zahoor Hussain, Muhammad Akbar, Abasal Hussain, Zhibin Lin, Majed Alzara, Ahmed M. Yosri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Shrinkage-induced cracking compromises concrete durability, while structural health monitoring typically requires external sensors. This study demonstrates that steel fiber–reinforced concrete (SFRC) can simultaneously mitigate shrinkage and enable intrinsic self-sensing. Sixteen beam specimens (C30 and C35) were cast with 50 mm and 60 mm steel fibers (SF) at dosages of 0.19%, 0.26%, 0.32%, and 0.38%. Shrinkage strain and fractional change in resistance (FCR) were monitored for 180 days and 72 h, respectively. Results shows that 60 mm SF at 0.38% reduced 180-day shrinkage strain by up to 38%. C30 beams with 50 mm SF exhibited peak FCR values of 181%, whereas C35 beams with 60 mm SF reached 128%. A strong positive correlation (R² = 0.90–0.98) was observed between FCR and shrinkage strain, confirming reliable self-sensing capability. The FCR rate of change decreased from 0.998%/h to 0.303%/h with higher SF dosage and length, indicating improved early-age stability. These findings confirm that optimized SFRC mixtures offer dual functionality which are effective in shrinkage control and real-time health monitoring, making them ideal for applications like industrial floors and tunnel linings where crack resistance and self-diagnosis are critical.

Introduction

Shrinkage-induced cracking remains a persistent challenge in concrete infrastructure, compromising serviceability, durability, and long-term structural performance1. One of the main reason for concrete cracking is due to the volumetric changes that occur during curing and drying. This happens as the cementitious material begins to shrink due to chemical reactions and autogenous effects in the early age2,3. Shrinkage arise from moisture loss and autogenous volume changes during hydration, particularly in low water-to-binder (w/b) system below 0.44–6. Shrinkage deformation produces internal forces in concrete, which in turn create stress within the concrete, leading to the formation of micro cracks that affect the structural integrity7. Also, these internal stresses produced by hydration process, moisture loss, and thermal contraction, which collectively induce tensile forces that drastically reduce ductility and resilience during the initial curing period8,9.

Some of the approaches that researchers have adopted in their research work, such as using steel fiber-reinforced concrete (SFRC) to reduce the challenges of shrinkage deformation in concrete matrix. Some studies have reported that steel fiber (SF) reinforcement contribution to shrinkage strain was negligible, while others have highlighted the positive role of SF on the reduction of shrinkage of SFRC10. Optimizing essential parameters like curing conditions and relative humidity can significantly impact shrinkage deformation11. Moreover, at curing age of 170 days, the autogenous and drying shrinkage reduced to about 3.1% and 7.2%, respectively12. Additionally, it has been found in the previous study that when 0.38% by volume of SF is used, it give extraordinary results for reducing early-age shrinkage deformation13. In order to minimize shrinkage, SF could be beneficial when added to high strength concrete (HSC). These findings from previous studies demonstrate that SF have a major role in influencing the shrinkage behavior of SFRC.

Despite the contribution of SF in controlling shrinkage, it also offers additional benefits including self-monitoring behavior by reducing electrical stress and enhancing mechanical properties14. Crack monitoring in SFRC is more effective because of the conductive properties of SF, which create the network of electrical conductivity within the material15. However, the electrical resistance of material is affected by temperature, moisture content and humidity16. Among these raw materials, SF have been used in this study because it showed convincing performance in self-monitoring of SFRC, it has great conductive nature, cost friendly and easy availability. According to a recent study, the major electro-physical property that influence the conductivity of SFRC is electrical resistance17. Conductivity of concrete is assessed to detect variation in the electrical conductivity of SF as the material undergoes mechanical deformation18. A number of parameters, including the quantity of SF, their alignment with respect to the direction of electrical current flow, and the water to cement ratio in SFRC, affect how much the electrical characteristics fluctuate19. Higher SF dosage enhances conductivity by forming a percolation network, reducing electrical resistance20–22. The practical implementation of self-sensing concrete has also been made possible by studies on the long-term serviceability and durability criteria23. Yu et al. (2025) showed that fiber length governs self-sensing repeatability in SFRC24. Recent studies compared the environmental impacts of SSCS and conventional polyester foil strain gauge (SG) sensor throughout the cradle to gate and cradle to grave processes, with a particular focus on the sensors themselves25.

As summarized in Table 1, while shrinkage mitigation and self-sensing capabilities of SFRC have been studied separately, no work has systematically correlated fractional change in resistance (FCR) with time-dependent shrinkage strain across varying fiber lengths, dosages, and matrix strengths. This gap is critical for real-world deployment, where dual functionality of shrinkage resistance and intrinsic health monitoring is needed. To address this gap, we present an integrated experimental study on sixteen SFRC beam specimens systematically examining the dual role of SF in shrinkage control and self-sensing response over 180 days. We evaluate two concrete strength (C30 and C35), two SF lengths (50 mm and 60 mm), and four dosages (0.19%, 0.26%, 0.32%, and 0.38%) monitoring both shrinkage strain and FCR evaluation during the early period (First 72 h). Furthermore, a quantitative FCR-shrinkage strain has been established, revealing how SF parameter influence mechanical and self-sensing performance within a single experimental framework. This work advances the design of multifunctional concrete systems aligned with the growing demand for smart, durable, and resilient infrastructure.

Experimental program

Material and mixture proportion

Two concrete mixes with target compressive strengths of 30 MPa (C30) and 35 MPa (C35) were prepared. The base mix proportions (kg/m³) were: cement = 393, fly ash = 44, water = 218 (C30), 196(C35), fine aggregate (0–5 mm) = 872 (C30), 883 (C35), and coarse aggregate (5–10 mm) = 872 (C30), 883 (C35). The gradation curve of aggregates is illustrated in Fig. 1. Chemical composition of raw materials is presented in Table 2. A polycarboxylate water reducing superplasticizer (Sika® ViscoCrete®−3301) was added at 1.5% by mass of cement (5.9 kg/m³). The physical properties of superplasticizer are presented in Table 3. As illustrated in Fig. 2, two types of steel fibers (SF) were used: SF50 (50 mm length, 0.5 mm diameter) and SF60 (60 mm length, 0.75 mm diameter), both with a tensile strength of 1100 MPa. SF were incorporated at dosages of 0.19%, 0.26%, 0.32%, and 0.38% based on the SF density of 7850 kg/m3 in addition to the base mix. One of the fundamentals of SF is that they act as internal sensors, which help to monitor the internal cracks and stress produced in concrete during self-sensing tests. Specimen preparation

Concrete mixing was carried out in a forced mixer. First, dry mixing of coarse and fine aggregates was carried for two minutes, followed by the addition of cement and fly ash and further dry mixing for two minute. A premixed solution of water and superplasticizer (1.5% by weight of cement) was slowly added, and wet mixing was continued for two minutes to achieve a homogeneous SFRC mixture. Uniform fiber dispersion is critical for consistent shrinkage control and electrical percolation. SF were gradually introduced at specified dosage into the rotating dry mix over two minutes to ensure uniform dispersion and prevent balling. All 16 mix designs were fabricated in triplicate making it total of 48 beam specimens. For analysis, one representative specimen per mix was selected based on consistent shrinkage and FCR trends across replicates. Visual inspection and preliminary data screening confirmed no outliers, ensuring the selected specimens reflect typical behavior. After mixing, the fresh SFRC was cast into non-absorbent plastic molds into 48 beam specimens, each measuring 100 mm × 100 mm × 400 mm. The top surface of each specimen was finished smoothly to ensure consistent electrode placement for self-sensing tests. Specimens were then covered with plastic sheets and left undisturbed in the molds. After 24 h, the specimens were carefully demolded and immediately transferred to a controlled drying room maintained at 22 ± 2 °C temperature and 55 ± 5% relative humidity, where they were stored upright with 200 mm spacing between them. All subsequent experiments including shrinkage deformation monitoring and self-sensing measurements were conducted in this environment over a period of 180 days. Shrinkage test

The shrinkage test is carried out in accordance with ISO 1920-8:200929,30. For shrinkage measurement, beams were mounted vertically in a shrinkage meter with 200 mm spacing between them to prevent interference. Dial gauges with a precision of 0.001 mm were positioned on the top surface of each beam specimen to monitor shrinkage deformation, as shown in Fig. 4. The shrinkage deformation was closely monitored and noted for 3 days, 90 days, and 180 days to capture early-age and long-term behavior. Specific conditions are selected to intimate the actual conditions that concrete structures can be subjected to, give a better estimate of how the steel fibers would behave with concrete in the future.

Self-sensing test

The Intelligent data collecting system (IMC) plays a crucial role in the self-sensing test, allowing real-time monitoring of beam specimens. It analyzes changes in electrical behaviors, such as resistance, that change in response to applied voltage. Electrical resistance measurements were performed using the four-probe method. Electrodes made of copper wires embedded in conductive graphite glue were attached at the surface of the specimen as shown in Fig. 5. Direct current (50 Hz) is intended to flow through outer electrodes A and D, which are 190 mm apart, while voltage is measured by inner electrodes B and C, which are 100 mm apart. A 12 V DC power supply and 5 kΩ fixed resistor (Rf) were included in the measurement. Results and discussion

Workability analysis of SFRC

The fresh-state properties of the steel fiber-reinforced concrete (SFRC) mixtures including slump, slump loss over time, bleeding, and pressure bleeding were evaluated in accordance with the Standard Test Method for Performance of Freshly Mixed Ordinary Concrete (GB/T 50080–2016). Slump was measured immediately after mixing, and slump loss was recorded at 30 and 60 min to assess workability retention. Bleeding was determined as the total volume of bleed water per unit area over 180 min, while pressure bleeding was measured using a standardized pressure bleeding apparatus under a pressure of 3.5 MPa for 15 min, as specified in the standard.

Table 6 compares the results of slump flow, slump loss over time, bleeding rate, and pressure bleeding rate of SFRC in its fresh state in this experiment. It can be seen from the Table 3 that as the SF content increases, the slump of the fresh concrete gradually decreases. Compared to C30SF15-60, the slump of C30SF20-60, C30SF25-60, and C30SF30-60 decreased by 13.16%, 18.42%, and 34.21%, respectively as shown in Fig. 7. Meanwhile, with the increase of fiber content, the slump loss of fresh concrete increases over time, while the bleeding rate and pressure bleeding rate decrease to a certain extent. For instance, compared to C30 under the condition of constant water reducing agent dosage, when the strength grade of the concrete matrix is increased from C30 to C35, the slump of fresh concrete decreases slightly. In addition, there is a certain degree of reduction in the bleeding rate and pressure bleeding rate. Comparing the effects of 60 mm SF and 50 mm SF on the workability of fresh concrete, it can be seen that 50 mm SF with the same dosage reduces the slump of concrete more than 60 mm SF. However, in terms of slump loss, bleeding rate, and pressure bleeding rate, the differences in the effects of 60 mm SF and 50 mm SF on fresh concrete are relatively small.

Shrinkage behavior of SFRC

Shrinkage strain was monitored over 180 days across all mixes as shown in Table 7; Fig. 8a,b and Fig. 9a,b. In C30 concrete (w/b = 0.54), 3-day strain ranged from 105µε − 130µε with 50 mm SF but dropped to 27µε − 60µε with 60 mm SF. By 90 days, the highest strain recorded was 615µε, occurred in C30-25SF-50, while 60 mm SF at the same dosage reduced it to 310µε. At 180 days, 50 mm SF yielded up to 755µε, whereas 60 mm SF limited strain to 500µε, leading to a 34% reduction as shown in Fig. 8a,b. In denser C35 concrete (w/b = 0.45), baseline shrinkage was lower: 3-day strain was 10µε − 40µε with 50 mm SF and 77µε − 100µε with 60 mm SF. By 180 days, both SF types produced strains below 500µε (380µε – 400µε for 50 mm, and 420µε – 470µε for 60 mm) as shown in Fig. 9a,b. Residual shrinkage strain (RSS) and shrinkage rate further clarify these trends. In C30-25SF-50, RSS reached 550µε (3–90 days), reflecting fiber clumping at high dosage as shown in Fig. 9a,b. In contrast, 60 mm fibers formed uniform networks, lowering RSS. In Fig. 10, the shrinkage rate also declined more sharply in mixes with 60 mm SF, for instance, reducing from 1.33 µε/day to 1.11 µε/day in C30 with 50 mm SF leading to a 17% drop versus a steeper reduction with 60 mm SF indicating more effective internal restraint.

These results reveal that 60 mm SF consistently outperform 50 mm SF, but the mechanism depends on matrix density. In porous C30, longer SF compensate for weak fiber-matrix bonding by spanning micro cracks and redistributing stress reducing both early and long-term shrinkage. This behavior aligns with fiber pull-out theory, where increased fiber length enhances interfacial bond area and energy dissipation during crack bridging31,32. In dense C35, the same fibers initially introduce mild restraint (elevating 3-day strain slightly), but enhance crack control as the matrix hardens, a phenomenon consistent with findings on internal stress redistribution in low-porosity systems33. Critically, moderate dosages (20–25 kg/m³) of 60 mm fibers deliver optimal shrinkage mitigation without excessive workability loss. At 30 kg/m³, diminishing returns occur, and in C30-25SF-50, excessive short SF actually increase RSS due to poor dispersion26,34. This supports the concept of a percolation threshold which states that, below a critical fiber concentration (~ 0.5% by volume), short SF fail to form a continuous reinforcement skeleton, leaving the matrix vulnerable to sustained capillary tension20,35. Longer SF, by contrast, bridge larger inter-fiber gaps even in open matrices stabilizing microstructure and limiting volumetric contraction.

Phase wise approach of shrinkage strain

Early-age shrinkage behavior of SFRC has been explored for 72 h. The shrinkage strain-time curve has been divided into three phases: initial, primary, and secondary as shown in Table 8. The initial phase ends at turning point 1 (Tp1), and the primary phase ends at turning point 2 (Tp2), which marks the start of the secondary phase that ends at the final point (Fp), which has the maximum shrinkage strain.

The initial phase of the shrinkage strain ranges from 0 to 10 h. There is a rapid increase in shrinkage strain as the moisture present in the beam begins to evaporate, which leads the concrete to volumetric contraction. For instance, in the shrinkage strain of beam specimens C30-15SF-50 and C35-15SF-60 as shown in Fig. 11, the shrinkage strain is rapidly increases in the initial phase, such as Tp1 = 77 µε and Tp1 = 42.5 µε, respectively. A similar trend has been observed for all the remaining beam specimens, as shown in Table 4.

Fig. 11Shrinkage strain in initial phase (0–10) hours of different strength concretes and dosage of SF.

Shrinkage strain in initial phase (0–10) hours of different strength concretes and dosage of SF.

The role of SF is very crucial here as it holds the concrete matric together and help in reducing shrinkage strain. However, shorter steel fiber (50 mm) tends to be less effective in the initial phase, which results in higher shrinkage strain, while longer steel fibers (60 mm) offer better resistance against micro-cracks in the concrete and provide better control over shrinkage. Longer SF better disperse internal stresses, stop cracks from spreading, and create a stronger bond with the matrix, which improves shrinkage control. These factors can be linked to the crack-bridging mechanism and fiber-matrix interaction32. The initial phase sets the trend for the rest of the shrinkage behavior depending on length and dosages, which gives better resistance.

The primary phase starts at turning point 1 (Tp1) and ends at turning point 1 (Tp2). The duration of the primary phase is 10–50 h as it loses more moisture over time. The rate of shrinkage strain in the primary phase slows down, but increases steadily. For instance, in beam specimens C30-30SF-50 from 45 µε and ends at 77.5 µε. Similarly, in beam specimen C35-30SF-60, shrinkage strain starts from 42.5 µε and ends at 53 µε as shown in Fig. 12. The increase in shrinkage strain for both beam specimens has been found is 72% and 24%, respectively. The increase in shrinkage strain in C35-30SF-60 beam specimen, which has longer steel fiber is more stable than the beam specimen with shorter steel fiber (50 mm). The steel fibers support the concrete matrix as it settles, leading to a more controlled and stable strain increase through this period.

Shrinkage strain in primary phase (10–50) hours of different strength concretes and dosage of SF.

The turning point (Tp2) marks the start of the secondary phase, which ends at the final point (Fp). In the secondary phase, the rate of shrinkage strain further slows down, levelling off as it reaches to a more stable state around the 72-hour mark. For instance, in beam specimen C30-30SF-60, the shrinkage strain ends up at about 55 µε, while C30-15SF-50 beam specimen has a final shrinkage strain of 123 µε as shown in Fig. 13. The increase in shrinkage strain for both specimens is 19% and 23%, respectively, which indicates a stability and slower rate of increase in shrinkage strain towards the end of the secondary phase. This slower rate of shrinkage strain shows how steel fiber holds the concrete matrix together under low moisture conditions. The longer steel fiber in C30-30SF-60 beam specimen helps spread out the internal stresses, reducing the concrete overall deformation.

Recent research on SFRC carried by demonstrated that higher SF dosages decrease shrinkage and micro cracking, but it has mostly concentrated on shorter SF. Recent studies have comprehensively investigated the role of SF on the shrinkage behavior of concrete. Similarly, investigation into ultra-high performance concrete (UHPC) has demonstrated that SF significantly minimize drying shrinkage, particularly during the initial curing age by increasing interfacial bonding36. Unlike earlier research, which often shows aggregate shrinkage over significant time interval, our phase-wise approach provides satisfactory temporal solution, revealing key moments when SF reinforcement is most effective. Future research will extend monitoring beyond 72 h, perform ANOVA analysis for statistical significance, and incorporate environmental factors such as temperature and humidity in order to further validate these finding.

Based on the above analysis, it has been concluded that the secondary phase shows the best performance in controlling shrinkage, representing long-term stability. The steel fiber allows for an even redistribution of internal stresses, which minimizes the localized tension points that can lead to cracks, making the concrete more stable and resilient. The Steel fiber dosage and length also have a huge impact on controlling shrinkage as higher dosage and longer steel fiber effectively lock the structure, resisting deformation, making SF a highly effective solution for controlling shrinkage in concrete, even under dry conditions where shrinkage is more severe. Overall, a more durable, shrinkage-resistant SFRC with enhanced structural strength is produced by employing longer and higher SF dosages.

In Fig. 14b,d, it has been observed that steel fiber dosage also has a great effect on both FCR and shrinkage strain. For the beam specimen having 0.19% steel fiber, as shown in Fig. 14b, we see relatively high FCR peaking at 64%, whereas shrinkage strain rises to around 67.5µε by the end of 72 h. Linear regression analysis of FCR versus shrinkage strain over 72h confirms a strong positive correlation, with R² values ranging from 0.90 to 0.98 across key mix designs as shown in Fig. 14a,b These high coefficients confirm that FCR reliably tracks volumetric deformation in SFRC. In case of a concrete beam having 0.38% steel fiber as shown in Fig. 14b, exhibits a much lower FCR ending at 37.73%, with a shrinkage strain of 55µε. This reduction in FCR shows that higher steel fiber content brings stability to concrete beams, making the material resistant and less sensitive to shrinkage strain. It also shows that higher steel fiber content has better control over the shrinkage strain itself, resulting in a slightly lower shrinkage strain as compared to a concrete beam having 0.19% steel fiber as shown in Fig. 14a. The trend of a strong positive correlation between FCR and shrinkage can be seen in all concrete beams.

While prior studies have examined electrical resistance changes in SFRC, these have largely focused on mechanically induced damage such as loading, unloading, or crack propagation under external stress. For instance, Hussain et al.15 demonstrated FCR-based crack monitoring in SFRC beams under bending15. Others have explored self-sensing using synthetic fibers37,38, which differ fundamentally from steel fibers in conductivity and bond behavior. Critically, none of these works investigates the intrinsic coupling between FCR and free shrinkage strain, which is volumetric deformation occurring without external load, driven solely by moisture loss and autogenous effects during early-age curing. Our study fills this gap by establishing, for the first time, a time-resolved, quantitative relationship between FCR and free shrinkage strain in unrestrained SFRC beams over 72 h. We further reveal how this relationship is modulated by concrete matrix density, in porous C30, longer fibers (60 mm) stabilize the conductive network and reduce FCR, whereas in dense C35, the same fibers enhance FCR by enabling percolation where shorter fibers fail. This dual behavior, opposite FCR trends governed by microstructure, has not been reported before and is critical for designing reliable self-sensing concrete systems.

Influence of concrete strength on FCR

The influence of SF length on FCR exhibits contrasting trends in C30 and C35 concrete matrices, which can be attributed to the interplay between fiber network formation and the underlying microstructure of the cementitious matrix. In Fig. 15a, C30 concrete, which has a relatively higher water-to-binder ratio (w/b ≈ 0.54) and a more porous microstructure, the inclusion of 50 mm steel fibers at a dosage of 0.19% results in a high FCR of 181%, indicating significant electrical instability due to poor fiber connectivity and susceptibility to micro-cracking. In contrast, 60 mm fibers at the same dosage establish a more continuous conductive network, reducing FCR to 37% and demonstrating enhanced signal stability. This trend reflects the ability of longer SF to bridge larger inter-fiber gaps in a more open matrix, thereby improving electrical percolation28,39. In Fig. 15b, C35 concrete, however, the denser and less porous matrix (w/b ≈ 0.45) restricts SF mobility during casting and limits the formation of conductive pathways at low SF dosages. Here, 50 mm SF remain largely isolated, yielding a very low FCR of 17.16%, which suggests insufficient electrical connectivity. Conversely, 60 mm fibers span the compact microstructure more effectively, enabling percolation even at 0.19% and increasing FCR to 64%. This behavior aligns with findings by Ding et al. (2024), who reported that longer steel fibers enhance crack control and create more reliable conductive pathways within the concrete matrix24,40. Thus, the opposite FCR trends in C30 and C35 are not contradictory but rather highlight how fiber length interacts differently with matrix porosity. In porous systems, longer fibers stabilize an otherwise fragmented network (reducing FCR), whereas in dense systems, they enable network formation where shorter fibers fail (increasing FCR)41.

The observed behavior is governed by three interconnected mechanistic principles: (1) fiber pull-out theory, which explains how longer fibers enhance crack-bridging through increased interfacial bond area and energy dissipation42, (2) percolation threshold theory, which clarifies why 60-mm fibers reduce FCR in porous C30 (stabilizing a fragmented network) but increase FCR in dense C35 (enabling conductive pathway formation where shorter fibers fail)43, and (3) piezo resistive response to volumetric strain, where shrinkage-induced micro cracking alters inter-fiber spacing, modulating electrical resistance27. Collectively, these theories provide a coherent mechanistic framework that explains how steel fibers simultaneously mitigate shrinkage and enable reliable self-sensing particularly the contrasting FCR behavior observed in porous C30 versus dense C35 matrices.

Practical implications

The findings of this experimental investigation are directly applicable to the implementation of SFRC as a dual-purpose material in real-world infrastructure. The property of shrinkage mitigation and reliability of self-sensing capability in SFRC can be implemented to reduce the use of embedded sensors or external crack monitoring devices thus reducing the long-term maintenance cost. Since the tested dosages and SF length are commercially available and compatible with conventional batching and casting techniques, it can be easily implemented in industry flooring, tunnel segment, and infrastructure where crack control and self-monitoring are crucial. Moreover, the designed methodology used in this study not only enhance the durability performance of SFRC through stress redistribution, but it also aligns well with the construction requirement by controlling shrinkage-induced micro cracks.

Conclusions

This study presents a detailed experimental investigation about the effects of steel fibers (SF) on shrinkage behavior and self-sensing properties of steel fiber-reinforced concrete (SFRC) containing 50 mm and 60 mm SF with four dosages such as 0.19%, 0.26%, 0.32%, and 0.38%. Shrinkage strain curve has been developed over 72-hour time period. This study aims to develop a relationship between fractional change in resistance (FCR) and shrinkage strain. After detailed analysis, following conclusions have been drawn:

60 mm SF consistently outperform 50 mm SF, yet their mechanism differs by matrix. In porous C30 concrete, they stabilize a fragmented conductive network, reducing FCR from 181% to ~ 38% and 180-days shrinkage strain by up to 38%, while in dense C35 concrete, they enable percolation where shorter fibers fail, increasing FCR from 17% to 128%, while maintaining low shrinkage.A moderate dosage of 20–25 kg/m³ of 60 mm SF optimally balances shrinkage control, workability, and FCR stability avoiding diminishing returns at 30 kg/m³.Significantly, we establish a strong, quantitative correlation (R² = 0.90–0.98) between FCR and free shrinkage strain in unrestrained beams over 72 h, validating SFRC’s self-sensing capability under real-world curing conditions.These insights provide a mechanistic foundation for designing multifunctional SFRC systems that intrinsically resist cracking and self-diagnose deformation.
