Coaching Teachers to Implement Equity and Social and Emotional Learning Focused Curricula in the Classroom: Insights from a Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Meredith P Franco, Sandra Rouiller, Catherine P Bradshaw, Elise T Pas, Toshna Pandey, Chelsea A Kaihoi, Jessika H Bottiani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTeacher coaching has frequently been used to support the implementation of social and emotional learning (SEL) interventions in classrooms. Though research on effective SEL implementation coaching strategies is growing, less is known about coaching to support equity-focused SEL. The present study sought to address this gap through an examination of coaching provided in a randomized controlled trial in which schools were assigned to either (a) an active control condition, where they received teacher coaching supports for the implementation of the SEL curriculum, Second Step or (b) an intervention condition where they also received an equity-focused set of lessons as an augmentation to Second Step, plus coaching supports for the implementation of both. The current study aimed to assess fidelity to the coaching models, examine coaching acceptability, and explore teacher engagement in coaching among 106 coached teachers across both conditions. Results indicated that coaching in both conditions was implemented as designed and that intervention condition coaching was distinct from coaching solely focused on SEL. Across both conditions, teacher demographic characteristics were associated with coaching participation, and teachers had positive experiences working with coaches. We conclude with a discussion of implications and recommendations for coaching to optimize implementation of equity-focused SEL.

Coaching as a means of supporting behavior change has been examined across disciplines (e.g., Kurz et al., 2017), with a specific focus on improving teachers’ instructional effectiveness emerging in the field of education. Though there is causal evidence supporting the positive effects of coaching programs on teachers’ instructional practices (Kraft et al., 2018), much of this literature examined coached teachers’ delivery of academic content rather than other forms of education, such as social and emotional learning (SEL; Glover et al., 2023). There is emerging literature on coaching to promote implementation of SEL interventions (Ialongo et al., 2019) and specific teacher practices such as classroom management (Fabiano et al., 2018; Owens et al., 2020; Reinke et al., 2008), bullying prevention (Pas et al., 2019), and culturally responsive practices (Bradshaw et al., 2018). The acceptability of coaching to teachers and how to promote or increase teacher engagement is an important area of inquiry, as teacher buy-in has been identified in the literature as a barrier to the scaling of coaching (Kraft et al., 2018). There is research to suggest teacher coaching that is focused on culturally responsive teaching, which on average took less than 5 hours of teacher time per school year, can be well received (Pas et al., 2016) and effective (Bradshaw et al., 2018). Prior studies also show specific coaching activities can impact SEL implementation dosage and quality, with the working relations between coach and teacher amplifying this impact (Johnson et al., 2018). Yet, there has been less research on specific aspects of the coaching processes (e.g., type of interactions, emotional responses) that can promote the implementation of equity-focused SEL. Therefore, in this study, we compared the coaching of teachers to implement equity-focused SEL programming to the coaching of teachers to implement a standard SEL intervention. We did so via secondary data analysis using data from a broader school-based, active comparison randomized controlled trial. We assessed coaching dosage and teachers’ perceived acceptability in two coaching conditions: (a) an active control condition that received teacher coaching supports for the implementation of a traditional SEL curriculum or (b) an intervention condition that received an equity-focused set of lessons as an augmentation of the traditional SEL curriculum and coaching supports for the implementation of both. We also explored the teacher characteristics associated with engagement in coaching across conditions.

A focus on coaching

The field of implementation science highlights the importance of coaching as a vehicle for supporting quality implementation of school-based practices (Lyon et al., 2024). School-based coaching as a form of professional development can reduce teacher distress and increase their sense of felt support (Downer et al., 2024). Such supports may be particularly important when adopting a practice that is novel or perhaps more sensitive, as SEL and equity-focused practices might be perceived by some educators (Bottiani et al., 2024). Few pre-service programs adequately cover how to teach SEL or integrate an equity lens into SEL teaching, and thus many educators need more in-service professional development and coaching on these topics (Matschiner, 2023; Schonert-Reichl et al., 2017). School-based coaching is an excellent resource as it is a form of professional development that goes beyond one-time, in-service presentations, which can be problematic and give educators a false sense of cultural competency (Ladson-Billings & Dixson, 2021).

Coaching teachers to implement SEL interventions

As defined by the Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning (CASEL, 2023), SEL is the process of developing core competencies in the following areas: self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills, and responsible decision-making. Fostering these skills during critical developmental periods in childhood and adolescence supports positive youth development in areas that are vital for students’ school, work, and life success (CASEL, 2023). In a recent meta-analysis, students who participated in universal school-based SEL programming demonstrated improved academic achievement, emotional skills, prosocial and civic behaviors, and reduced internalizing and externalizing behaviors (Cipriano et al., 2023). By and large, research suggests that SEL curricula are more effective when delivered by classroom teachers rather than by other school staff such as counselors or instructional assistants (Cipriano et al., 2023). However, inadequate training may cause variability in implementation, and outcomes, of SEL programs by teachers (Buchanan et al., 2009; Durlak 2017). A seminal review found that SEL program implementation fidelity was linked with program outcomes (Domitrovich & Greenberg, 2000); programs implemented with low fidelity fail to produce intended effects (see also Kraft et al., 2018). Aspects of implementation fidelity, such as quality of engagement, have been linked with improved student behavior, academic success, and teacher-student interaction (Kraft et al., 2018). Ongoing coaching support is important for improving teachers’ implementation fidelity of SEL interventions (Doyle et al., 2023; Skaar et al., 2016).

Coaching teachers to implement equity-focused SEL

In addition to the five competencies of traditional SEL outlined by CASEL (2023), there has been recent recognition that SEL programming can be integrated with equity-focused programming. Jagers and colleagues (2021, 2024), for example, introduced the concept of transformative SEL, that is, the teaching of a subset of SEL competencies that can help to promote school communities in which young people have equal opportunities to thrive. These competencies were identified within the broader five CASEL competencies focused on identity, agency, belonging, collaborative problem-solving, and curiosity. To effectively address equity in conjunction with an SEL curriculum, program implementers should receive training in both SEL and equity and ideally deliver that program in the context of other culturally responsive practices (Huizar et al., 2025; Lim et al., 2024). Experts in the transformative SEL field have recommended that teachers adapt their own instructional practices of teaching traditional SEL to align more with the justice-orientation of transformative SEL (Rosario-Ramos et al., 2021). However, given the already high levels of stress and burnout among teachers (Reinke et al., 2025) and emotional labor of engaging with topics of bias in the classroom (e.g., Duncan, 2019; Sleeter, 2016) many teachers would likely benefit from personalized coaching on the implementation of SEL in combination with equity-focused programming. For example, teachers may avoid conversations about racism and other forms of group-based discrimination in fear of being labeled as racist (Tropp & Rucinski, 2022) and require individualized consultation about how to approach these conversations with students (see Williams & Cholewa, 2025).

One promising approach to improving teachers’ receptiveness to equity-focused professional development is school-based equity coaching (Blaisdell, 2018; Fallon et al., 2024). Teacher coaching has frequently been used to support the implementation of SEL classroom-based interventions (Pas et al., 2023), yet coaching the implementation of equity-focused SEL programming is less understood. There is some evidence that intensive, yearlong teacher coaching support to implement culturally responsive SEL programming can help mitigate teachers’ discriminatory beliefs toward racially minoritized students (Mellom et al., 2019), but less is understood about brief coaching of equity-focused SEL implementation. Given these potential challenges in addressing bias in the classroom, the needs of teachers in terms of coaching activities and the perceptions of the coaching relationship may differ from when teachers are coached for SEL implementation without the equity focus.

The current study

The present study aimed to address gaps by conducting a secondary data analysis of coaching processes within a randomized controlled trial (RCT) in which schools were assigned to receive teacher coaching supports for (a) the implementation of the SEL curriculum, Second Step (Frey et al., 2000) by itself (“SEL Only”) as an active control condition or (b) the implementation an equity-focused supplementary set of lessons as an augmentation to Second Step and coaching supports for the implementation of both (“SEL+Equity”), the intervention condition. We sought to explore whether coaching on equity-related topics in the intervention condition would inhibit teachers from engaging in coaching activities in terms of quantity (i.e., frequency and duration of teacher-coach sessions) and quality (i.e., teachers’ emotional reactions and topics covered during teacher-coach sessions). We selected this design because an active control randomized design allows for rigorous testing of the value added of one intervention over and above the effect of the other (see Zabor et al., 2020). Though our focus in the current study is not on programmatic effects (see Bottiani et al., 2025 for main effects), this active comparison design is particularly helpful for examining questions related to coaching and implementation, as both conditions received coaching supports, which allowed for direct comparisons and a larger sample of coached teachers.

Specifically, in the current study, Aim 1 assessed dosage for both coaching models (i.e., different coaching model across conditions). Coaching dosage was measured via the amount of coaching delivered in minutes, including the frequency, duration, and activities of teacher-coach sessions (Collins et al., 2025). Above and beyond the structural differences in each coaching model, we hypothesized that teachers randomized into the SEL Only (active control) condition would have shorter sessions (as measured in minutes) given teachers’ prior exposure to professional development on this particular SEL program by the participating school district. Within the SEL+Equity (intervention) condition, we hypothesized that more time would be spent on cross-condition coaching activities due to the novel focus on equity-related topics. Aim 2 examined teachers’ perceived coaching acceptability across conditions, measured as coach ratings of teacher affect and interpersonal reactions during the coaching sessions. Teachers’ perceived acceptability was also considered in the context of their active participation in the coaching process (Collins et al., 2025). Despite the voluntary nature of the study, we hypothesized that teachers randomized into the SEL +Equity condition would be more likely to have instances of emotional reactivity (e.g., stress, discomfort, crying) to coaching as documented by the coach (Tropp & Rucinski, 2022). Lastly, Aim 3 explored the teacher characteristics associated with engagement in coaching across conditions.

We hypothesized that teachers’ occupational health (i.e., lower levels of stress and burnout) and coaching readiness would be positively associated with coaching engagement, as measured by total minutes of coach interaction, across conditions. Prior research has consistently demonstrated that teacher stress and burnout negatively impact engagement with professional development, including coaching (e.g., Salmela-Aro et al., 2019). Additionally, for the SEL+Equity condition, we posited that teachers who identified as a race other than White (i.e., Asian, Black or African American, Latine or Hispanic, multiracial) would spend more time interfacing with coaches based on prior coaching engagement literature (see Nagourney, 2023). This trend may be due to the complexities of processing personal and professional lived experiences and navigating the delivery of the student-facing equity lessons as a person of color (Kaufman et al., 2024; Tropp & Rucinski, 2022). Together, these results were intended to inform future efforts to support teachers in their integration and joint adoption of SEL and equity-focused programming.

Method

Study design

The research team recruited three cohorts over three consecutive years, starting each fall from 2021 to 2023. The study was approved by the university’s Institutional Review Board (IRB) and by the participating school district’s IRB. All research activities adhered to the ethical guidelines and regulations set forth by both IRBs. With the assistance of the school district partners, the research team held informational sessions and obtained signed consent from a school administrator (e.g., principal), confirming their commitment to participate in the study for the school year. Teacher recruitment occurred after school participation was secured, and teachers were informed in writing that their school would be randomly assigned to one of the two (active control or intervention) conditions. The research team held teacher recruitment informational sessions and obtained signed consent forms or contact information from those teachers who were interested in participating in the study. A total of 131 teachers submitted signed consent forms at baseline, though only 106 teachers were included in the sample of this study; see “Participants” section below for details on eligibility criteria for inclusion in the current secondary analysis.

Procedure

Following each fall baseline data collection for the three cohorts, and to ensure balance across the two conditions before random assignment, the research team used a propensity score-based approach to conduct school-level randomization, which included information on school-level demographics. The research team informed each school of their randomly assigned conditions (SEL Only active control, or SEL+Equity intervention) every fall after collecting baseline data. All teachers provided written consent for their participation, and all teacher self-reported data collection occurred online via Qualtrics. All participation was voluntary.

Participants

The sample included 106 4th − 8th grade teachers across 20 schools over three consecutive school years, starting in Fall 2021 and ending in Spring 2024. Given that this study’s aims center on coaching implementation outcomes (e.g., acceptability), we focused on a subsample of 106 teachers who actively engaged in one of the two RCT conditions (see section on attrition). We identified the subsample of actively engaged teachers by selecting those who, at a minimum, engaged in at least 15 minutes of direct coach contact. The cutoff value of greater than or equal to 15 minutes was used as the minimum viable coaching duration, based on the extant middle school teacher coaching literature (e.g., Ellington et al., 2017) and to the research team’s extensive prior experience in school-based coaching initiatives.

Of the 20 schools, there were 11 elementary schools and 9 middle schools. Teachers were mostly female, White (both over 80%), and experienced (i.e., averaging 11 years of teaching experience). Based on teachers’ self-report of their race and ethnicity, participating teachers demonstrated a more racially and ethnically representative population compared to the national teacher workforce (U.S. Department of Education, National Center for Education Statistics, 2020). No significant demographic differences were found between teachers at the intervention and active control schools. Additional details on the teachers and demographic composition of the schools are provided in Table 1 and the CONSORT is in Figure 1. Briefly, schools served a racially and ethnically diverse student population (i.e., 41% White, 25% Black or African American, and 24% Hispanic or Latine students) and half of students qualified for free or reduced-price meals. Baseline equivalence confirmed no significant differences in demographic characteristics between the two study conditions.

Factors related to coaching attrition

We conducted a series of ad hoc analyses to understand the characteristics of the 25 teachers who initially enrolled in the broader RCT study but did not engage in coaching following randomization and thus were excluded from the current study. Of these 25 teachers, the only statistically significant commonality was teachers’ school type. Specifically, elementary school teachers opted out of coaching (i.e., <15 minutes of coach contact) at a higher-than-expected frequency compared to middle school teachers (χ2 = 7.67, p < .01). Teachers within four schools opted out of coaching for various reasons, such as administrator opt-out due to staff overwhelm during the post-pandemic school year (resulting in the loss of 14 teachers). Though not statistically significant, teachers who were randomly assigned into the SEL Only active control condition were overrepresented among those who did not participate in coaching activities for this reason. Notably, neither teachers’ self-reported demographics (i.e., race, sex, age), RCT cohort, baseline mental wellness (i.e., stress, burnout), nor baseline levels of racial discomfort were significantly associated with attrition to coaching.

Coaches

Over the course of three years, five project coaches were assigned to a participating school at some point; all coaches had prior coaching experience and were exposed to SEL and equity topics through their graduate-level training. Through this study, coaches were explicitly trained in motivational interviewing, the programs offered in the RCT, and this study’s specific teacher coaching model. Of the five coaches, three were doctoral level psychologists (i.e., clinical or school psychologists) and two were master’s level educational specialists. Three of the coaches held some form of clinical mental health licensure (e.g., licensed clinical psychologist, licensed clinical social worker) and two of the coaches held state professional teaching licenses. Four coaches were female, one coach was male, and across the group, individuals self-identified as African American, Hispanic, White, and mixed race. The demographic information of the coaches is intentionally reported in aggregate to remain de-identifiable. All coaches were trained and supervised by a project co-investigator with expertise in Motivational Interviewing (Miller & Rollnick, 2002).

Coaching procedures

Coaches followed a distinct coaching model designed for each condition, outlined below, though there were some common elements. Coaching interactions were guided by motivational interviewing and the relationships between the teacher and coach were non-evaluative in nature (Miller & Rollnick, 2002, 2023). Most interactions between the teacher and coach were in-person, though resources for teachers were shared through virtual communication (e.g., e-mails, video calls).

SEL Only coaching model

The RCT active control condition, referred to as the “SEL Only” coaching model, provided a structured approach to supporting educators in implementing the Second Step program effectively. This occurred both by working with a school-based SEL lead and offering time-limited 1-on-1 coaching supports to teachers. Upon condition assignment and coach assignment, the process began with an initial meeting between the coach and the schools’ designated SEL lead. The SEL lead position was often the school counseling department chair or a school administrator. During the SEL lead meetings, the coaches built rapport with the SEL lead, introduced their role as the Second Step supports coach, outlined the coaching model’s structure, and addressed any questions or concerns. An additional goal of this SEL meeting was for the coach to gain perspective and insight as to how Second Step was implemented in the school and what specific, unique needs there were overall with SEL implementation in each school. Together, the coaches and SEL leads planned the next steps for implementation with teacher participants.

The next step was to conduct the Second Step Implementation Needs Assessment Interview with teachers. During teachers’ needs assessment interview, the coach focused on building rapport and developing a collaborative relationship with teachers. The coach also gathered detailed responses from teachers about their experience with their school’s implementation of Second Step, including successes and challenges of effective school-wide implementation within teachers’ homeroom classes. During this meeting, teachers were encouraged to speak candidly about their implementation of Second Step lessons. They were also presented with a menu of teacher supports that the coach was available to provide throughout their participation in the project. Examples of activities included: classroom observations of Second Step lessons and feedback, resource sharing (e.g., one pagers, tips and tricks), or a consultee-centered consultation to support skill development. All support was optional and it was up to the teacher to select what would best suit their needs.

The coach then held a second meeting with the SEL lead to discuss the themes that arose during teachers’ needs assessments, without divulging teacher-specific information, to inform how to best provide additional teacher supports. This aggregated feedback included both positive and challenging aspects of Second Step implementation at the school-wide and individual classroom levels as reported by the participating teachers. The SEL lead then provided their immediate reactions to the data, and offered recommendations related to implementation in light of their knowledge about the school’s climate and general staff attitudes toward SEL. Together, the SEL lead and coach selected a topic and date for the school’s hour-long professional development session and tailored the content to the school’s SEL-related needs. The professional development session was led by the project coach, hosted at a mutually agreed upon time, and made available to all study participants at the school.

The SEL+Equity coaching model

Educators in the SEL+Equity condition received 1-on-1 coaching through the structured R-CITY model, direct work with the implementation (in this case, Equity) lead and group training (Bottiani et al., 2024). Individualized coaching was aimed at helping teachers implement culturally sustaining practices while fostering their “equity stamina,” a phrase the local district used to help teachers recognize that equity-informed teaching practices, though at times difficult, were vital professional skills that could be improved through consistent practice. In a parallel format to the SEL Only condition, the SEL+Equity coach also met with the school’s implementation lead; in this case, an Equity lead rather than SEL lead. The Equity lead was a school-based staff member who had volunteered to engage with the district’s equity office in addition to their primary school-based duties. The coaching process followed five distinct steps: (1) assisting teachers in reconnecting with their core values, (2) developing teacher-formulated goals, (3) aligning evidence-based strategies with these goals using an organized strategy mapping resource, (4) creating actionable plans for applying the strategies, and (5) observing classroom implementation and providing constructive feedback. The process was designed by drawing upon other stage-based problem solving coaching and consultation models, with a particular leveraging of the Classroom Check-Up (CCU, Reinke et al., 2008).

An important distinction of the SEL+Equity condition coaching compared to the SEL Only condition was the greater degree of 1-on-1 teacher support provided by the coach to facilitate the effective implementation of six teacher-led equity-focused SEL lessons. Measures

Coach contact logs to assess dosage and acceptability

After each contact with a teacher, the coach completed a coach contact log, which documented the specific activities and time spent with the teacher during coaching for all 5-minute or longer contacts and activities. The total number of minutes for each teacher was aggregated and minutes spent in each specific activity were also summed. This measure has been used for estimating dosage in prior studies (e.g., see Bradshaw et al., 2018; Debnam et al., 2024; Pas et al., 2016). In addition, coaches completed other indicators of their implementation, as well as perceptions of the coaching session and teacher engagement; specifically, we assessed coaching acceptability using the coach rating of teachers’ emotional reactions, which were tallied across all coaching contacts for the current study. Coaches were presented with a checklist and the prompt “Check all of the following that occurred or were evident to you during your visit: positive affect, anger or hostility, sadness or depression, health or mental health problem, personal problem (e.g., financial, family), work-related problem (e.g., boss, colleagues), cried or became emotional during the session, expressed concerns about COVID or its impacts, expressed concerns about discussing race or racism, was withdrawn during the meeting, did not maintain focus, and none of these apply.”

Teacher self-report measures

Teachers self-reported their demographic information as a part of the RCT’s broader set of self-report measures. See Table 1 for complete descriptives statistics. Specially, 101 of the 106 teachers provided information on years of teaching experience, grade(s) currently taught, race, ethnicity, and gender. Multiple self-report measures were also completed at the baseline, prior to randomization to condition. These included teachers’ self-reported Coaching Readiness, General Teaching Self-Efficacy, Job Stress, Maslach Burnout, and Racial Identity. See details below. Participants responded on a 6-point scale, from Strongly Disagree to Strongly Agree. Certain items were rescaled, such that a higher score indicated more agreement. The Cronbach alphas (α) reported below are from the current sample.

Self-reported Job Stress (Hurrell & McLaney, 1988) included items such as I am unable to cope with the stress of my job on a daily basis (5 items; α = .88). The Maslach Burnout Inventory (Maslach et al., 1997) included items such as I feel emotionally drained from my work (4 items; α = .93). The General Teaching Self-Efficacy (Hoy & Woolfolk, 1993) scale included items such as I can manage almost any student behavior problem (3 items; α = .79). The Coaching Readiness scale included items such as I am eager to use new strategies in my classroom instruction (2 items; α = .68). Finally, the Racial Identity scale included items such as I think about how my life is affected by my race/ethnicity (4 items; α = .86).

Analyses

Descriptive statistics by condition

Coaching dosage and in-session teacher reactions were examined using a series of descriptive statistics to address Aims 1 and 2, respectively. A chi-square test of independence was performed in SPSS. The chi-square statistic (χ2) and the phi coefficient (Φ) were calculated to assess the strength and direction of the association between the study condition and coach-reported teacher emotional reactions. Adjusted standardized residuals were examined to identify specific differences between groups (Sharpe, 2015).

Linear regression

A multiple regression analysis was performed using robust standard errors clustered by school to account for the nested data structure. The analyses were conducted to examine the impact of teacher baseline characteristics on the total minutes of coaching received (Aim 3). The analyses aimed to identify which factors significantly predict the amount of coaching provided to teachers. The regression model included both fixed and random effects. Fixed effects estimates were calculated for each independent variable, along with robust standard errors, t-values, and 95% confidence intervals. Random effects were assessed to determine the within-level and between-school variance. The significance of each predictor was evaluated based on the t-values and confidence intervals. The intercept estimate provided the baseline level of coaching minutes, while the estimates for each predictor indicated their impact on coaching minutes. Ad hoc analyses were conducted to examine moderation (i.e., through inclusion of interaction terms) to explore the ways in which teachers’ self-reported race may affect the association between study condition and minutes engaged in teacher coaching. We examined the interaction between teacher race (i.e., White vs. not White) and total minutes engaged in coaching activities across study participation.

Results

Descriptive statistics by condition

Coaching Dosage

Teachers in the SEL+Equity condition received significantly more coaching compared to the SEL Only group, which is aligned with the design of the coaching interventions. There were significant differences in both the frequency of coach contacts and duration in minutes of coaching. Specifically, teachers in the SEL+Equity group had a higher mean number of coaching contacts (M = 9.86) compared to teachers in the SEL Only group (M = 3.53). The SEL+Equity group had a higher mean coaching duration in minutes (M = 186.27) in contrast to the teachers in the SEL Only group (M = 53.43). See Table 3 for additional details.

Coaching Acceptability

When examining significant differences in teachers’ emotional reactions observed during coaching between the two conditions, the SEL+Equity group exhibited a higher frequency of positive affect (M = 15.6) compared to the SEL Only condition (M = 7.8), with a chi-square value of χ2 (1, N = 106) = 7.51, p < .01, and a phi coefficient of Φ = 0.24. Very few teachers were rated by the coaches as exhibiting anger or hostility during coaching sessions in either condition. Discussion

The present study sought to examine key differences in coaching across two different conditions of SEL implementation – one when implemented alone as an active control, and the other when SEL was combined with equity-focused lessons. The results revealed several key findings regarding the dosage, acceptability, and factors influencing teacher engagement across conditions. The distinctiveness of equity-focused coaching was evident in the increased coaching duration, unique topics of discussion (e.g., conversations regarding teachers’ hesitancy to discuss race or racism with students), and teachers’ overall receptiveness (i.e., positive affect) during coaching sessions. This study provides a unique contribution providing a quantitative analysis on the characteristics of equity-focused coaching, in tandem with future publications that will further explore the qualitative, practice-based implications of introducing equity lesson content to students (see Franco et al., 2025) as well as the primary outcomes of the broader school-based RCT (see Bottiani et al., 2025). As designed, the SEL+Equity condition included substantially more coaching compared to the SEL Only condition, with teachers receiving an average of over three hours of coaching versus an average of under one hour, respectively. The program differentiation is important in the context of the broader RCT, as it ensured that the SEL+Equity condition was appropriately distinct, maintaining its unique elements, from the SEL Only condition.

Moreover, the study demonstrates the utility and acceptability of school-based equity coaching as a tool for working with teachers to support the implementation of SEL and equity curricular materials. Teacher coaching took place during a distinctive period in the United States’ sociopolitical history, spanning Fall 2021 through Spring 2024. Equity-focused coaching was closely aligned with district priorities, and many teachers’ commitments to antiracist pedagogy were reinvigorated as the COVID-19 pandemic illuminated longstanding racial inequities across the nation. Over time, as the national sociopolitical climate shifted, schools in the partner district were not immune to the backlash that emerged in response to what some perceived as an overemphasis on equity-related initiatives within schools. In spite of potential sociopolitical concerns being raised about SEL and equity, the findings of this study suggest that coaches reported a positive reception to coaching on equity-related topics. In fact, coaches reported that teachers in the SEL+Equity condition exhibited more positive emotional reactions during coaching sessions, with 98% showing positive affect sometime during coaching interactions compared to 76% in the SEL Only condition. The discussion of equity-related topics such as group-based discrimination (i.e., racism) and COVID-19 related inequities did not deter or disengage teachers from engaging in coaching activities.

Middle school teachers engaged in more minutes of coaching relative to their peers. Moreover, when examining coaching attrition, elementary school teachers opted out of coaching (i.e., <15 minutes of coach contact) at a higher-than-expected frequency compared to middle school teachers. This is likely attributable to the greater schedule flexibility for middle school teachers, including longer blocks of planning time, and possibly the greater relevance of the curricula content for secondary aged students. Future research should examine best practices for coaching implementation in elementary school settings given the challenges of attrition and engagement at the elementary-level in this study. As previously noted, the SEL+Equity condition presented new content to teachers warranting a more intensive coaching model. However, above and beyond the by-design differences in condition, teachers of color (although a very small subsample; n = 17) engaged in significantly more minutes of coaching, which led to ad hoc explorations of an interaction between teacher race and study condition. The association between teacher race and coaching minutes was significantly moderated by study condition (depicted in Figure 2), with teachers of color assigned to the SEL+Equity condition engaging in more coaching compared to White teachers assigned to the SEL+Equity condition and when compared to teachers of color assigned to the SEL Only condition. This interaction may be explained by a host of factors, such as teachers of color potentially being more open to participating in the equity coaching process, or White teachers engaging with equity-focused topics at a more surface level, shaped by their degree of self-awareness and racial identity development. This is in line with extant research about the added demands on professionals of color when engaging in equity related professional development efforts (Wang et al., 2024). For example, teachers of color may be expected to lead or engage more deeply in school- or classroom-based discussions about sensitive equity-related topics. Additionally, there may be greater challenges and risks for teachers of color related to having equity-based discussions with their students. The increased coaching engagement may be reflective of teachers’ need to find a safe space to get coaching and discuss sensitive topics, which may not be otherwise offered.

Limitations

Despite the promising findings, our study has some limitations. This study focused on the teacher interactions and coach processes that occurred in the context of a broader RCT designed to test an SEL program as compared to the SEL+Equity focused lessons (see Bottiani et al., 2025). While the randomized active control design was helpful in contrasting the two coaching conditions, it limited our ability to directly report on the primary outcomes of teacher and student behavior change over time. Additionally, data for this implementation analysis were collected and analyzed during the trial period, prior to completion of the fourth cohort. Future studies should further explore the effects of coaching dosage and model implementation on intervention outcome effects. We acknowledge the limitation of coaches’ awareness of condition and how that may have impacted their ratings. It was not possible to double blind the teachers and coaches to condition, as the coaching models do vary by condition; as such coaches needed to know which schools were randomly assigned to which condition in order to provide the relevant coaching supports. This is a research design limitation inherent to school-based coaching work; in this study, coaches were intentionally employed by the research team, rather than employees of the school district. Nevertheless, the coaches in this study (and coaches working with any school system) are likely to hold varying beliefs about the value of exploring and advancing work related to equity, and vary in their own past training experiences, sociodemographic background, racial-ethnic socialization, and professional self-efficacy. Data were collected in real-time and generally measure objective aspects of coaching (e.g., time spent) and were designed to minimize bias by coach knowledge of the condition.

The voluntary nature of the study may have introduced selection bias, as schools and teachers who opted to participate might have been more motivated or receptive to coaching and the equity focus of the study. Further, there were teachers who consented and did not engage in the coaching; this could have further exacerbated selection bias. To the research team’s knowledge, most participants hoped to be assigned to the SEL+Equity condition to gain access to equity coaching and student-facing equity lessons, so those randomly assigned to the SEL Only active control condition may have experienced greater disengagement resulting in the notable differences in coach-reported teacher affect across conditions. This additional context may also explain why a handful of teachers randomly assigned to the active control condition exhibited anger or hostility during at least one coaching session. Additionally, the small sample size of teachers of color limits our ability to make group-specific claims and limits the generalizability of our findings. We also adjusted standard errors to account for the clustering of classrooms within schools, rather than leveraging a true multilevel approach due to the relatively small number of classrooms and schools. Future studies with a larger sample size may allow for a true multilevel approach and exploration of factors at both the classroom and school level. Future studies should aim to include larger and more diverse samples to validate and extend our results. We only had coaching data on teachers who actively engaged in the coaching process. Therefore, we know little about how teachers who did not engage in coaching would have responded to the coaching process, as they were excluded from the analyses. Finally, acceptability was measured as the coach rating of affect and teacher time engaged in the coaching; direct responses from teachers may have provided additional perspective. Coach measures were used as they completed a measure right after coaching sessions; such ratings were seen as a burden to teachers. Other research has examined an end-of-year measure of teacher-reported alliance, which could be a useful tool in future research (e.g., Johnson et al., 2016).

Conclusions

These findings underscore the acceptability and feasibility of school-based coaching on equity and SEL topics to create more supportive and inclusive educational environments. By addressing both SEL and equity, schools can better support teachers and students, ultimately contributing to a more just and equitable education system. Future initiatives should explore strategies to maintain fidelity and effectiveness of equity coaching at scale, such as leveraging technology, refining implementation models, and providing sustained support for educators. Developing scalable equity coaching frameworks that retain the core benefits observed in efficacy trials will be essential for ensuring long-term success in educational settings
