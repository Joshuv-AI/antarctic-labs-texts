Does the material of the external ocular prosthesis matter? Clinical outcomes after enucleation with orbital implantation
Authors: Weronika Pociej-Marciak, Joanna Kosałka-Węgiel, Izabella Karska-Basta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe influence of ocular prosthesis material and patient-related factors on clinical outcomes following enucleation with orbital implantation remains insufficiently characterized. This study evaluated clinical outcomes in relation to external ocular prosthesis material, age, and gender.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 58 patients who underwent enucleation with orbital implantation. Acrylic prostheses were used in 19 patients (32,6%), while 39 (67,4%) used glass prostheses. Subjective evaluation was based on a 4-item questionnaire assessing patient-reported postoperative outcomes. Objective assessment used a 4-point scale to evaluate upper eyelid sulcus symmetry, color matching, prosthetic motility, and eyelid position and was conducted by investigators.ResultsPatients using glass prostheses demonstrated better objective clinical outcomes than those using acrylic prostheses in the unadjusted analysis (p = 0.038). However, this association was no longer significant after adjustment for age, gender, and time since enucleation (p = 0.058). Objective clinical outcomes were rated lower than patients’ subjective assessments (p = 0.004). Older patients reported higher subjective ratings than younger individuals (p = 0.001), and their subjective assessments exceeded objective evaluations (p < 0.001). In the adjusted analysis, younger age (18–49 years) remained the only independent predictor of lower subjective assessment scores (p = 0.002).ConclusionsBoth glass and acrylic ocular prostheses were rated favorably by patients. Prosthesis material was not an independent predictor of objective or subjective outcomes after adjustment for age, gender, and time since enucleation. Patients tended to rate outcomes more favorably than clinicians, particularly in older age groups.Keywords: Ocular prosthesis, Orbital implant, Uveal melanoma

Introduction

Enucleation of the eyeball has a long history, with evidence of ocular removal dating back to ancient civilizations [1, 2]. Today, this procedure remains necessary in selected cases and is often performed with simultaneous orbital implant placement, followed by rehabilitation with an external ocular prosthesis.

Ocular prostheses may be fabricated from different materials. Glass prostheses, made of cryolite glass, are smooth, scratch-resistant, and resistant to protein accumulation on their surface. An additional advantage of glass is its excellent wettability. In contrast, acrylic prostheses, made of polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA), are more impact-resistant, cost-effective, and better tolerated in colder conditions. The choice of prosthesis material may influence clinical outcomes, cosmetic appearance, and patient comfort, while management of post-enucleation socket syndrome remains an important component of care [3–5]. Despite the widespread use of prostheses made of different materials, their impact on clinical outcomes remains insufficiently investigated.

The reports to date mainly concern the mobility of the artificial eye depending on the orbital implant used. [6–8]. Furthermore, previous studies have investigated patient concerns related to cryolite glass and PMMA ocular prostheses, as well as the response of the anophthalmic socket to cryolite glass prosthetic eyes [9, 10]. However, to the best of our knowledge, no studies have evaluated clinical outcomes following enucleation with orbital implantation according to the material of the ocular prosthesis used.

The aim of this study was to assess the clinical outcomes of enucleation with orbital implant placement in relation to the material of the external ocular prosthesis (cryolite glass or PMMA acrylic), patient age, and gender.

Materials and methods

This study included patients who had undergone enucleation with orbital implant placement for uveal melanoma and presented for follow-up at the Department of Ophthalmology, Division of Ophthalmology and Ocular Oncology, Jagiellonian University Medical College (Kraków, Poland), between May 2015 and January 2017. The diagnosis of uveal melanoma was histopathologically confirmed after enucleation in all patients. All patients had undergone enucleation with placement of a 20 mm Guthoff orbital implant. Inclusion criteria were as follows: age over 18 years, a history of enucleation with orbital implant placement for uveal melanoma, the presence of a contralateral eye, and written informed consent to participate. Exclusion criteria included the presence of concomitant malignancy with a remission period of less than 12 months, and inability to complete the questionnaire or undergo photographic documentation due to psychosomatic conditions. A clinical psychologist was part of the research team. The study was approved by the Jagiellonian University Bioethics Committee (No. 122.6120.5.2015, Date 29 Jan 2015).

The subjective and objective clinical outcomes were evaluated using methods previously described in our extended publication [4].

Subjective assessment of clinical outcome

Subjective assessment was conducted through a structured questionnaire comprising 4 questions, each rated on a 4-point scale:

Overall satisfaction: “Are you satisfied with the cosmetic effect of the ocular prosthesis?”

Color matching: “Does the color of the prosthetic eye match the color of the healthy eye?”

Motility: “Is the motility of the prosthetic eye similar to that of the healthy eye?” (subjective assessment of motility)

Motility: “Is the motility of the prosthetic eye similar to that of the healthy eye?” (subjective assessment of motility)

Fit of the prosthetic eye: “Does the prosthetic eye slip out of place during daily activities?”

Fit of the prosthetic eye: “Does the prosthetic eye slip out of place during daily activities?”

The first 3 questions were rated on a 4-point scale as follows: 1—not at all; 2—partly; 3—significantly; and 4—highly. The fourth questions used a different scale: 1—very often; 2—quite often; 3—sometimes; and 4—not at all. To calculate the overall subjective clinical assessment score, the responses to questions a-d were summed and the total divided by 4.

Objective assessment of clinical outcome

Objective evaluation was based on standardized photographs of the healthy and prosthetic eyes taken in 5 directions of gaze: primary (straight ahead), right, left, up, and down. Photographs were acquired with the patient’s head immobilized, using a Nikon 2700 camera with an 18–105 mm VR lens (Tokyo, Japan) mounted on a tripod. A scale ruler was positioned adjacent to the patient’s eye and photographed to enable pixel-to-millimeter conversion. Symmetry of the upper eyelid sulci, color matching between the painted iris of the prosthetic eye and the iris of the healthy eye, and motility comparison between the prosthetic and healthy eye, and eyelid position were assessed from the photographs.

Symmetry of the upper eyelid sulci was assessed from the frontal gaze photograph using a 4-point scale: 1—asymmetrical; 2—deepening of more than half the sulcus; 3—deepening of less than half the sulcus; and 4—symmetrical.

Color matching between the prosthetic and healthy eye was also assessed from the frontal view using a 4-point scale, with 1 denoting no similarity; 2, slight similarity; 3, significant similarity; and 4, high similarity. Color evaluation was performed by 3 independent investigators under standardized lighting and monitor display settings. To reduce the impact of outlier ratings, the highest and lowest scores were discarded, and the remaining intermediate score was used as the final assessment.

Motility of the prosthetic and healthy eye was measured in both horizontal and vertical directions using the ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland, and the Laboratory for Optical and Computational Instrumentation, University of Wisconsin, Wisconsin, United States). Pupil deviation was measured in 4 gaze directions: temporal, nasal, superior, and inferior, and expressed in millimeters. For horizontal movement (temporal and nasal), 0–0.75 mm was classified as no movement (1); 0.76–1.5 mm, as slight (2); 1.51–2.25 mm, as significant (3); and ≥2.26 mm, as high (4). For vertical movement (up and down): 0–0.5 mm was classified as no movement (1); 0.51–1.25 mm, as slight (2); 1.26–2.00 mm, as significant (3); and ≥2.01 mm, as high (4). To account for possible distortions due to varying gaze angles, the ratio of prosthetic eye motility to the motility of the healthy eye was calculated for standardization. Eyelid position was assessed from the frontal gaze image using a 4-point scale: 1—abnormal; 2—partially abnormal; 3—mostly normal; and 4—normal. Assessment focused on the presence of ptosis or upper eyelid retraction, and lower eyelid ectropion or laxity. Evaluations were conducted by 3 independent investigators under standardized lighting and monitor display settings. The highest and lowest scores were excluded, and the middle value was taken as the final assessment.

The overall objective assessment score was calculated by summing the scores for the 4 evaluated features, including sulcus symmetry, color matching, prosthesis motility, and eyelid position, and dividing the sum by 4.

The ocular prosthesis (glass or acrylic) was selected by the patient after consultation and discussion of the characteristics, advantages, and limitations of both prosthesis materials. Patients were not assigned to a specific prosthesis by the investigators.

The investigators who assessed the photographs and performed the measurements were blinded to patient-specific data, including the presence of an orbital implant and the material of ocular prosthesis.

Statistical analysis

Qualitative variables were reported as absolute numbers and percentages. Quantitative variables were expressed as the mean and standard deviation or, in the case of non-normally distributed data, as the median with the first and third quartiles.

Contingency tables were used to evaluate associations between qualitative variables. The Pearson chi-squared test was applied when expected frequencies exceeded 5 in at least 80% of the cells. When this assumption was not met, the Fisher test was used for 2 × 2 tables, and the Fisher–Freeman–Halton test for larger tables. For comparisons between independent groups, the Mann–Whitney U test was used for interval-level variables, and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used for ordinal variables. For paired comparisons, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for both interval and ordinal variables.

The Spearman rho correlation coefficient was used to assess the strength and direction of associations between interval variables. The loess regression curve was applied for graphical presentation of relationships between variables measured on at least an ordinal scale.

For statistical analysis, patients were categorized into three age groups: 18–49 years, 50–74 years, and 75 years or older.

Linear regression analysis was performed to identify factors influencing the overall objective clinical assessment and the overall subjective clinical assessment.

A p value of less than 0.05 was considered significant. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 24 for Windows (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Overall subjective assessment was positively correlated with time since prosthesis fitting (Rho=0.23, p=0.012) (Fig. 2).

Relationship between overall subjective clinical assessment and time since current prosthesis fitting

No significant correlations were observed between time since prosthesis fitting and objective assessment (Rho=− 0.02, p=0.878) or time since enucleation and overall subjective (Rho=0.07, p=0.434) or overall objective assessment (Rho=0.13, p=0.343).

In the multivariable analysis, prosthesis material was not associated with subjective assessment (B = 0.021, p = 0.876). Similarly, gender (B = − 0.102, p = 0.400), time since enucleation (B = 0.012, p = 0.642), objective cosmetic assessment (B = 0.230, p = 0.149), and age 75–90 years (B = 0.033, p = 0.845) were not significant predictors. In contrast, patients aged 18–49 years reported significantly lower subjective assessment scores than the reference age group (B = − 0.472, 95% CI: − 0.768 to − 0.175, p = 0.002).

In the multivariable analysis, neither gender, age, nor time since enucleation were significantly associated with objective clinical assessment (all p > 0.05). Prosthesis material was also not an independent predictor of objective clinical outcome after adjustment (B = − 0.224, 95% CI: − 0.456 to 0.008, p = 0.058), although the effect size and direction remained similar to those observed in the unadjusted model.

Discussion

The present study evaluated the influence of prosthesis material and patient-related factors on clinical outcomes following enucleation with orbital implantation. To our knowledge, this study represents one of the most comprehensive analyses of clinical outcomes in Polish patients following enucleation with orbital implantation.

Our study reveals four findings that may have important clinical implications:

Both glass and acrylic external ocular prostheses were associated with favourable clinical outcomes after enucleation with orbital implantation.

Patients reported high satisfaction, with no significant differences between prosthesis materials.

Older patients provided higher subjective ratings than younger ones and their subjective scores exceeded objective evaluations.

The objective assessment of color matching was significantly higher in patients with glass prostheses compared to those with acrylic prostheses. One possible explanation for this finding may be differences in prosthesis fabrication methods, as acrylic prostheses can be either custom-made or selected from prefabricated stock prostheses [11]. The first method involves custom fabrication. According to Baino et al., this approach should be considered the preferred method for prosthesis selection [3]. The second method involves stock or prefabricated prostheses, where the most suitable prosthesis is selected from a range of premanufactured options. Patients with cryolite glass eye prostheses typically receive a personalized prosthesis based on orbital changes [12]. Therefore, the higher degree of individualization of glass prostheses may contribute to improved colour matching [9].

In our study, objective prosthesis motility was greater in downward gaze among patients with glass prostheses compared to those with acrylic prostheses. However, this difference was not observed when motility was analysed as a ratio to the companion eye, suggesting that overall motility is comparable between glass and acrylic prostheses. This is important information in the context of previous observations demonstrating that ocular prosthetic motility has an impact on patient satisfaction and appearance [7].

In both groups, subjective assessments of color matching were significantly higher than objective assessments. Additionally, blue-eyed patients rated prosthesis color significantly higher than the investigators, whereas no significant difference was found between subjective and objective color assessments in brown-eyed patients. One possible explanation is the lower contrast between the pupil and iris in brown eyes compared with blue eyes, which may reduce perceptual sensitivity to color mismatches. Although a different investigator-developed grading system was used in the present study, previous studies have emphasized the value of structured objective assessment of ocular prostheses and highlighted the importance of evaluating individual components of prosthesis performance separately [13, 14]. This is particularly relevant in our study, where the overall difference between groups was predominantly related to colour matching rather than eyelid position or motility.

The overall subjective clinical assessment was significantly higher than the objective assessment in the acrylic prosthesis group. This observation may be related to differences in glass and acrylic prostheses fabrication method and is interesting in the context of the results of the study by Rokohol et al., who underline that many concerns were significantly higher in users of PMMA prostheses than in users of cryolite glass prostheses [9]. Given the generally high patient satisfaction irrespective of prosthesis material, these findings should be interpreted with caution regarding their clinical relevance.

In patients aged 50 years or older, the overall subjective assessment of the clinical effect was significantly higher than the objective assessment. This is in line with previous research suggesting that older individuals exhibit greater acceptance of bodily changes and lower appearance-related expectations, often through the use of cognitive strategies that reduce the importance of conforming to social standards of physical attractiveness [15]. This may reflect psychological adaptation and changing expectations with age.

Gender was not significantly associated with either the overall subjective or objective clinical outcome in either prosthesis group. However, both women and men rated their postoperative appearance higher than the investigators. Although previous studies on body image satisfaction suggest that women tend to be more dissatisfied with their appearance than men, this trend was not observed in our study [15]. This may be attributed to the specific patient population—individuals undergoing enucleation for uveal melanoma—for whom disease treatment likely took precedence over cosmetic concerns.

As time since prosthesis fitting increased, the overall subjective assessment of the clinical effect also improved significantly. This is consistent with findings from previous studies, which indicate that adaptation to the prosthesis is a gradual process that enhances over time [16–18]. Moreover, initial concerns in post-enucleation patients significantly decrease two years after the surgery [19].

A strength of this study is the relatively homogeneous patient population, as all participants underwent enucleation for uveal melanoma and received the same type and size of orbital implant. In addition, objective outcomes were assessed using standardized photographic documentation and quantitative measurement methods.

This study also has several limitations. First, its single-center design may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the relatively small sample size, particularly in the acrylic prosthesis group, may have reduced the statistical power of the analyses. Furthermore, the use of a single orbital implant type precluded comparisons between different implant designs. The study groups differed in age and, to some extent, in time since enucleation. In addition, prosthesis was chosen by the patients rather than assigned randomly. Therefore, the observed differences in clinical outcomes may have been influenced by patient-related factors and selection bias in addition to prosthesis material. We did not collect certain potentially relevant clinical and patient-reported variables, including comorbidities and previous treatments for uveal melanoma, such as brachytherapy, which may affect prosthesis fitting and long-term outcomes. Although evaluations were performed independently by three observers and the final score was based on the intermediate rating after exclusion of the highest and lowest scores, individual observer ratings were not retained. Consequently, formal assessment of inter-observer agreement was not possible, which should be considered a limitation of the study. In addition, prosthesis weight was not recorded and therefore its potential influence on prosthesis motility could not be evaluated. Finally, owing to the cross-sectional design of the study, the observed associations should not be interpreted as evidence of causal relationships. Some of the reported findings may reflect unmeasured confounding factors.

Conclusion

In summary, both glass and acrylic external ocular prostheses were associated with favourable outcomes following enucleation with orbital implantation. Patients generally rated outcomes more favourably than clinicians, particularly at older age. In addition, subjective satisfaction increased with time since prosthesis fitting, suggesting a role of long-term adaptation in perceived clinical success.
