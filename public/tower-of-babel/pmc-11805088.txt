Central Incisor with Pulp Canal Obliteration and Apical Periodontitis Managed with a 3D Printed-Guided Endodontic Therapy: A Case Report
Authors: Abdulaziz Abdulwahed
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis case report discusses a case of a maxillary central incisor with pulp canal obliteration (PCO), managed by guided endodontic access. A preoperative periapical (PA) radiograph and cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) displayed an incidental finding of a periapical radiolucency (PARL), indicating a PA lesion of endodontic origin on tooth #11. An intraoral three-dimensional (3D) scan was obtained, and its data and CBCT images were processed using planning software. A digital model of the guide was designed and 3D printed. The guide was placed on the tooth surface, and the tooth was accessed until the desired length was reached and the canal was located. Non-surgical root canal treatment was conducted, and the canal was obturated with gutta-percha and bioceramic sealer. A final restoration was placed with flowable composite along with the placement of a full ceramic crown. A 1-year recall showed complete healing of the apical area.

INTRODUCTION

Pulp canal obliteration (PCO) (or calcific metamorphosis) poses a significant challenge for practitioners performing root canal treatment. It is defined as radiographic evidence of increased dentin production primarily in response to trauma. The result of this process is a calcified canal, which does not necessarily indicate diseased pulp. The term pulp canal calcification may also be used to refer to this condition.[1] The most common etiology of PCO is dental trauma, especially luxation injuries.[23] However, other factors might play a role in this condition, such as the pulpal reaction to dental caries,[4] restorations,[5] vital pulp therapy,[6] as a response to orthodontic forces,[7] or a long-term secondary dentin apposition seen in elderly patients.[89] One of the main consequences of PCO is the probability of pulpal necrosis (PN), which constitutes 1–16.5% of the teeth,[10] and it is estimated that up to 27% of these cases will undergo apical changes or symptoms.[211] Perforations are common in cases of PCO where repair is indicated if the tooth is restorable and the prognosis is favorable. Multiple case reports discussed this potential complication and its management once it occurs.[1213]

Guided endodontic access provides safety in terms of accessing teeth in these types of cases and facilitates the procedure by navigating the bur toward the correct angle with high accuracy and with the estimation of the length of the preparation beforehand, thus resulting in time reduction and high efficiency.[14] This novel technique was first introduced by Krastl et al. in 2016, which uses computer-aided design and computer-aided manufacturing (CAD/CAM) to produce a stent with a sleeve that acts as a guide for an operating bur during penetration and to reach the patent part of the canal. This technique involved the use of cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) to locate the patent part of the canal and a 3D intraoral scan to record the relationship of the tooth with other teeth in the dental arch and to support the superimposition process of these two images. The data are then sent to a 3D printer to synthesize a model that can be used in the patient’s mouth.[15]

CASE REPORT

A 41-year-old woman was referred to an endodontic clinic in a private practice in Riyadh, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. The prosthodontist detected an incidental finding of periapical radiolucency (PARL) at the apex of the root in tooth #11 [Figure 1]. The patient denies any issues with her medical history and does not have any allergies, according to her knowledge. The patient reported a history of dental trauma to the maxillary anterior teeth when she was a child, without specifying a certain age. The patient presented asymptomatic before and during the endodontic examination, and the tooth was transilluminated, and a faint crack was detected on the labial surface of #11. The sensibility examination was conducted with no response to both cold test and electric pulp test (EPT). Both percussion and palpation revealed no pain or discomfort. Periodontal probing depths were within normal limits, and the mobility was physiological. Intraoral periapical (PA) radiographs of #11 were taken, followed by a CBCT exposure (Orthophos S 3D, Dentsply Sirona, Bensheim, Germany).

Preoperative periapical radiograph of #11 showing pulp canal obliteration with narrowing of the canal to the middle third of the root. Periodontal ligament (PDL) widening is seen at the apex of #11

Radiographic interpretation was performed using radiographic software (Sidexis 4, Dentsply Sirona, Bensheim, Germany) and revealed PCO roughly up to the middle third of the root with a 1 × 3 mm PARL at the root apex [Figures 2 and 3]. The CBCT examination session was saved as digital imaging and communications in medicine (DICOM) extension, and the clinical diagnosis reached for #11 was pulpal necrosis (PN) with asymptomatic apical periodontitis (AAP). A consent form was obtained from the patient after establishing the diagnosis, and discussing the proposed treatment plan.

a and b: Sagittal view of CBCT image showing a small periapical radiolucency (PARL) at the apex of root #11. A measurement of the estimated depth of calcification was taken, and it was shown to be around 9 mm

An axial view of #11 showing pulp chamber calcification in the coronal third of the root

A 3D intraoral scan (TRIOS 3, 3Shape, Copenhagen, Denmark) was taken for maxillary and mandibular arches and saved as a stereolithography (STL) file. The STL and DICOM files were added and then superimposed to each other using planning software (Blue Sky Plan, Blue Sky Bio®, LLC, Libertyville, IL, USA) [Figure 4a and b]. The location of the canal was obtained, and a virtual straight path was placed on the CBCT image of the tooth to determine the angulation and estimated depth. In addition, the virtual sleeve was created and customized to fit with the access bur of choice. Subsequently, a digital guide was constructed, including all six anterior teeth and two premolars from each side. The information was sent to the 3D printer (Asiga MAX, Asiga, Alexandria NSW, Australia) for fabrication [Figure 4c and d].

(a and b) Length and angular orientation on Blue Sky Plan software. (c and d) Digital design of the access guide

An initial access was made with a size #1 round diamond bur (Diaswiss FG, Geneva, Switzerland) on a high-speed handpiece with water coolant, and then, a Munce bur (CJM Engineering, Santa Barbara, CA, USA) was attached to a latch-joint slow speed handpiece with water coolant, and the bur was entered into the opening while the guide was firmly in place. A PA radiograph was taken during the troughing process to ensure that the angulation was correct. Then, sizes #10 and #15 stainless steel hand files (Mani, Matsutani Seisakusho Co., Takanezawa, Japan) were negotiated within the canal, and a glide path was established in conjunction with an apex locator (iPex, NSK, Nakanishi, Japan).

A PA radiograph was taken to confirm the working length (WL). Subsequently, rotary endodontic files (ProTaper Gold Dentsply Sirona, Ballaigues, Switzerland) attached to a portable endodontic micromotor (E-connect, Eighteenth Medical Technology, Changzhou, China) were used for cleaning and shaping up to size F2. Irrigation with 6 ml of 5.25% sodium hypochlorite, followed by 2 ml of 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) with activation, then a final irrigation of sodium hypochlorite with activation. The obturation technique of choice was a continuous-wave, warm vertical compaction, with a gutta-percha matching cone (ProTaper Gold Dentsply Sirona, Ballaigues, Switzerland), and a bioceramic sealer (TotalFill® BC Sealer HiFlow™, FKG Dentaire SA, La-Chaux-de-Fonds, Switzerland); then, a final restoration of a flowable composite was placed (3M Filtek, 3M ESPE, Saint Paul, MN, USA) [Figure 5]. Finally, the patient was referred back to her prosthodontist for a treatment plan regarding the crack on the tooth’s surface. A follow-up radiograph was taken 1 year after the root canal treatment, with signs of complete resolution of the radiolucency indicating complete healing and that the final crown was in place [Figure 6a and b].

DISCUSSION

Cases with PCO account for 75% of perforations during access cavities and during locating calcified canals.[16] The pathogenesis of PCO has been discussed in multiple studies and showed various rationales including the uncontrolled sympathetic reaction at the time of neurovascular damage to the dental pulp as a result of traumatic injuries. The probable subsequent loss of parasympathetic control may lead to the excessive secretion by odontoblasts therefore narrowing or occluding the canal with secondary dentin.[17] This description is in agreement with other studies.[1819]

However, another explanation provided by Torneck et al.[20] suggests that tertiary dentin apposition is involved which has a highly irregular pattern. Therefore, guided endodontic access as one of the applications of 3D printing provides a practical solution in minimizing the probability of complications.[14] A case report published in 2021 discussed a lateral perforation on a maxillary central incisor that occurred due to failed endodontic access on the calcified tooth. The case was managed with a treatment plan involving a 3D scan of a plaster model previously obtained by silicone impression.[21] In this case report, the 3D scan was completed directly in the patient’s mouth and it seemed that both methods were acceptable in obtaining successful results.

A variation in the design of the stent was discussed in a study published by Torres et al. in 2023. The design used a sleeveless guide with rails located on each side of the tooth. These rails aid in the positioning of the handpiece with the desired angulation. Many advantages could be drawn from this design especially if used in posterior teeth or used in patients with limited mouth opening.[22] However, in this case report, a sleeve design was used and showed no interference using the handpiece with the patient’s mouth, and the location of the sleeve is fairly clear of obstruction as it was in the anterior region.

The rationale behind the safe application of guided access is its accuracy in reaching the exact position of the root canal. Several investigations discussed the accuracy of this device and concluded with deviations of the apical position by around 0.7 mm and an angular deviation of only 1.5°.[22] The disadvantages of guided endodontic access are limited to cost, in which devices are required to collect sufficient data, and the availability of materials aiding in the fabrication of guides. In addition, additional steps are necessary, including CBCT acquisition, 3D scanning, and 3D printing to reach the final product.[23]

CONCLUSION

This case report illustrates the significance of guided endodontic access, as it provides a safe and rapid entry to the pulp canal system, in which the predictable outcomes outweigh the time-consuming and relatively high-cost planning before treatment.
