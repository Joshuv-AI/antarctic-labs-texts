Optimal Best-Arm Identification under Fixed Confidence with Multiple Optima
Lan V. Truong, Senior Member, IEEE

Abstract
We study best-arm identification in stochastic multi-armed bandits under the fixed-confidence setting, focusing on instances with multiple optimal arms. Unlike prior work that addresses the unknown-number-of-optimal-arms case, we consider the setting where the number of optimal arms is known in advance. We derive a new information-theoretic lower bound on the expected sample complexity that leverages this structural knowledge and is strictly tighter than previous bounds. Building on the Track-and-Stop algorithm, we propose a modified, tie-aware stopping rule and prove that it achieves asymptotic instance-optimality, matching the new lower bound. Our results provide the first formal guarantee of optimality for Track-and-Stop in multi-optimal settings with known cardinality, offering both theoretical insights and practical guidance for efficiently identifying any optimal arm.



I
Introduction

The multi-armed bandit (MAB) problem provides a fundamental framework for sequential decision-making under uncertainty, where a learner interacts with a set of arms corresponding to unknown reward distributions. The objective is to efficiently balance exploration and exploitation in order to make statistically reliable decisions.

In the best-arm identification (BAI) problem under the fixed-confidence setting, the goal is to identify an arm with the highest (or nearly highest) expected reward with a prescribed confidence level, using as few samples as possible. This formulation arises in various applications such as clinical trials, A/B testing, hyperparameter optimization, and recommendation systems.

Most existing works on BAI assume the existence of a unique best arm. However, in many real-world scenarios, multiple optimal arms may exist, each achieving the same maximal expected reward. Degenne and Koolen
[
1
]
studied this case and derived a fundamental lower bound together with the sticky Track-and-Stop algorithm, which attains asymptotic sample complexity matching this bound when the number of optimal arms is
unknown
. Despite these advances, the optimal strategy and the fundamental performance limits when the number of optimal arms is
known
a priori remain unexplored.

I-A

Motivation

The best-arm identification problem is central to sequential decision-making, where the objective is to identify an optimal arm using as few samples as possible while ensuring a fixed level of confidence. This problem arises in a wide range of domains, including clinical trials, A/B testing, and hyperparameter tuning. While the case of a unique best arm has been extensively studied, many practical applications naturally involve multiple arms achieving the same maximal expected reward. This motivates the need to understand the optimal sample complexity in this more general multi-optimal scenario.

Among existing algorithms for fixed-confidence BAI, the
Track-and-Stop
algorithm stands out for its principled foundation based on likelihood ratio statistics and its strong empirical performance. Although it is widely regarded as near-optimal in the single-optimum case, a complete theoretical justification of its optimality when multiple optimal arms exist has remained elusive.

Degenne and Koolen
[
1
]
addressed the setting where the number of optimal arms is unknown, establishing a lower bound and proposing the sticky Track-and-Stop algorithm, which asymptotically achieves this bound. In contrast, this paper focuses on the complementary setting where the number of optimal arms is known in advance. Under this assumption, we derive a new information-theoretic lower bound that characterizes the minimal achievable sample complexity, which is strictly tighter than the bound in
[
1
]
.

Furthermore, we propose a modified version of the Track-and-Stop algorithm and prove that it achieves
instance-optimal sample complexity
in the fixed-confidence regime. These results provide the first formal characterization of the optimal sample complexity when multiple optimal arms exist and their number is known, thereby completing the theoretical picture and offering new insights into the design of exploration strategies in stochastic bandit problems.

I-B

Related Work

Best-arm identification (BAI) in stochastic multi-armed bandits has been a central focus in the literature for over a decade, particularly under fixed-confidence and fixed-budget settings. In the fixed-confidence framework, the goal is to identify an arm with the highest expected reward with probability at least
1-\delta
, while minimizing the expected number of samples
[
2
,
3
]
. The fixed-budget setting, by contrast, fixes the total sampling budget and seeks to minimize the probability of misidentification
[
4
]
.

Early work introduced elimination-based strategies and stopping rules that guarantee PAC (Probably Approximately Correct) bounds
[
2
]
, later complemented by information-theoretic approaches establishing matching lower and upper bounds under the assumption of a unique best arm
[
3
]
. Such analyses leverage Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergences to characterize instance difficulty, and algorithms like Track-and-Stop
[
3
]
and lil’UCB
[
5
]
exploit these insights for near-optimal performance. Other PAC-based algorithms, including LUCB
[
6
]
, adapt confidence bounds to efficiently balance exploration, but rely on uniqueness of the best arm and may over-sample tied arms.

In practice, multiple arms often share the maximal expected reward, e.g., in clinical trials, recommendation systems, or A/B testing. Standard algorithms may waste samples distinguishing statistically equivalent arms, motivating approaches that exploit ties. Prior work has addressed related problems, such as top-k arm identification and best-subset selection
[
4
,
7
,
8
]
, which aim to identify all optimal arms but often incur unnecessary sample complexity when the goal is to find any optimal arm. Degenne and Koolen
[
1
]
explicitly considered multiple optimal arms, deriving a lower bound and proposing the sticky Track-and-Stop algorithm, which achieves asymptotically optimal sample complexity when the number of optimal arms is unknown.

From a theoretical standpoint, classical lower bounds assume a unique best arm
[
3
]
; handling multiple optima requires refined bounds that characterize minimal sampling effort across tied arms. Beyond fixed-confidence BAI, related challenges arise in fixed-budget BAI
[
4
,
7
]
, combinatorial bandits
[
7
]
, and contextual bandits
[
9
]
, where tie-aware strategies improve both efficiency and fairness
[
10
]
.

In summary, while most BAI studies focus on unique optima, recent work highlights the importance of efficiently handling multiple optimal arms. Our paper contributes by deriving a new lower bound for the case where the number of optimal arms is known and demonstrating that a modified Track-and-Stop algorithm achieves instance-optimal sample complexity, closing a key theoretical gap between the known- and unknown-cardinality settings.

\mathrm{kl}(x,y)=x\log\frac{x}{y}+(1-x)\log\frac{1-x}{1-y},

(2)

with the conventions

For any differentiable function

\dot{f}(x)=\frac{df(x)}{dx},\qquad\ddot{f}(x)=\frac{d^{2}f(x)}{dx^{2}}.

(3)

\Sigma_{K}=\left\{w\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K}:\sum_{i=1}^{K}w_{i}=1\right\}.

(4)

II
One-parameter exponential family

The exponential family encompasses a broad and significant class of probability distributions, including many widely used models in statistics and machine learning, such as the Bernoulli, Poisson, Gaussian (with known variance), and exponential distributions. A particularly important subclass is the one-parameter exponential family, where each distribution is characterized by a single real-valued parameter.

A probability distribution
P_{\mu}
belongs to the one-parameter exponential family if its density (or mass) function can be written in the form:

\displaystyle p_{\mu}(x)=h(x)\exp\left(\theta(\mu)x-A(\theta(\mu))\right),

(5)

where
h(x)
is a non-negative function, and

•

\mu=\mathbb{E}[X]
denotes the mean of the distribution,

•

\theta(\mu)
is the natural (canonical) parameter, assumed to be a bijective function of
\mu
,

•

A(\theta)
is the log-partition function, which is convex and differentiable, and ensures normalization:

\displaystyle\int h(x)\exp\left(\theta x-A(\theta)\right)dx=1,

•

d(\mu,\lambda):=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{\mu}\|P_{\lambda})
denotes the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between two members of the family.

The KL divergence between
P_{\mu}
and
P_{\lambda}
admits the following closed-form expression:

\displaystyle d(\mu,\lambda)=A(\theta(\lambda))-A(\theta(\mu))-(\theta(\lambda)-\theta(\mu))\mu.

(6)

Then, the following results can be proved.

Lemma 1
.

For the one-parameter exponential family, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{d\theta(\mu)}{d\mu}=\frac{1}{\ddot{A}(\theta(\mu))}>0,\qquad\forall\mu.

(7)

Proof.

The mean of the distribution is given by:

\displaystyle\mu(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[X]=\dot{A}(\theta).

(8)

Since the function
A(\theta)
is strictly convex, its derivative
\dot{A}(\theta)
, which maps
\theta\mapsto\mu
, is strictly increasing.

From calculus, if a function
f
is strictly increasing and differentiable, then its inverse
f^{-1}
is differentiable, and

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dy}f^{-1}(y)=\frac{1}{\dot{f}(f^{-1}(y)}.

(9)

Applying this to
f(\theta)=\mu(\theta)=\dot{A}(\theta)
, whose inverse is
\theta(\mu)
, we get:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\mu}\theta(\mu)=\frac{1}{\ddot{A}(\theta(\mu))}.

(10)

Because
A(\theta)
is the log-partition function of an exponential family, it is strictly convex, and hence:

\displaystyle\ddot{A}(\theta)>0,\qquad\forall\theta.

(11)

Therefore:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\mu}\theta(\mu)=\frac{1}{\ddot{A}(\theta(\mu))}>0,\qquad\forall\mu.

(12)

∎

The following result can be shown in Appendix
A
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
(w_{1},w_{2},\cdots,w_{M},w_{a})
be a tuple of positive numbers. For common exponential family distributions such as Gaussian with known variance, Bernoulli, and Poisson, it holds that

\displaystyle f(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\cdots,\lambda_{M},\lambda_{a})=\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}d(\mu_{i},\lambda_{i})+w_{a}d(\mu_{a},\lambda_{a})

is jointly convex in
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\cdots,\lambda_{M},\lambda_{a})
, where
d(\mu,\lambda)
is the KL divergence between two distributions from the exponential family defined in (
6
).

III
Problem Setup

We consider the stochastic multi-armed bandit (MAB) problem in the fixed-confidence best-arm identification setting. Let
\mathcal{A}=\{1,2,\dots,K\}
be a finite set of
K
arms. Each arm
a\in\mathcal{A}
is associated with an unknown distribution
\nu_{a}
belonging to a known exponential family, with mean reward
\mu_{a}=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\nu_{a}}[X]
.

At each round
t\in\mathbb{N}
, the learner selects an arm
A_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
and observes an independent sample
X_{t}\sim\nu_{A_{t}}
. Based on the history of arm pulls and rewards, i.e.,
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma(A_{1},X_{1},A_{2},X_{2},\cdots,A_{t},X_{t})
, the learner chooses a stopping time
\tau
and outputs a recommendation
\hat{a}_{\tau}\in\mathcal{A}
.

Define the set of optimal arms as

\mathcal{A}^{\star}:=\left\{a\in\mathcal{A}\,:\,\mu_{a}=\mu^{\star}\right\},\quad\text{where}\quad\mu^{\star}:=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mu_{a}.

The goal is to identify any one arm in
\mathcal{A}^{\star}
with high probability while minimizing the expected sample complexity.

Fixed-Confidence Guarantee.

Given a confidence parameter
\delta\in(0,1)
, a
(\delta,\mathcal{A}^{\star})
-PAC (Probably Approximately Correct) algorithm is one that ensures

\mathbb{P}(\hat{a}_{\tau}\in\mathcal{A}^{\star})\geq 1-\delta.

Sample Complexity

The performance of an algorithm is measured by its sample complexity, i.e., the expected number of samples used before stopping:

\mathbb{E}[\tau].

Our aim is to design algorithms that satisfy the PAC criterion and achieve the minimal possible sample complexity, especially in the presence of multiple optimal arms.

Challenge with Multiple Optima

When
|\mathcal{A}^{\star}|>1
, standard algorithms may over-sample to distinguish between arms that are equally optimal. The key challenge is to avoid unnecessary comparisons among optimal arms and stop as soon as any one of them is confidently identified.

IV
General Lower Bound

Let
N_{a}(t)=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mathbf{1}_{\{A_{s}=a\}}
be the number of draws of arm
a
between the instants
1
and
t
, and
N_{a}=N_{a}(\tau)
be the total number of draws of arm
a
.
First, we recall the following lemma.

Lemma 3
.

[
11
, Lemma 1]

Let
\nu
and
\nu^{\prime}
be two bandit models with
K
arms such that for all
a
, the distributions
\nu_{a}
and
\nu^{\prime}_{a}
are mutually absolutely continuous. For any almost-surely finite stopping time
\sigma
with respect to
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
,

\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[N_{a}(\sigma)]D_{\text{KL}}(\nu_{a},\nu^{\prime}_{a})\geq\sup_{\mathcal{E}\in\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}}\text{kl}(\mathbb{P}_{\nu}(\mathcal{E}),\mathbb{P}_{\nu^{\prime}}(\mathcal{E})).

(13)

Now, denote by

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\big\{\mu=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\cdots,\mu_{K}):\mbox{exactly $M$ optimal arms}\big\}.

(14)

Definition 4
.

Let
a\in\{1,2,\cdots,K\}\setminus\{1,2,\cdots,M\}
. For any tuple
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{M})\in[0,1]^{M}
such that
0\leq\sum_{i=1}^{M}\alpha_{i}\leq 1
, define

\displaystyle I_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{M}}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\cdots,\nu_{M},\nu_{a})

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\alpha_{i}d\bigg(\nu_{i},\sum_{i=1}^{M}\alpha_{i}\nu_{i}+\bigg(1-\sum_{i=1}^{M}\alpha_{i}\bigg)\nu_{a}\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad+\bigg(1-\sum_{i=1}^{M}\alpha_{i}\bigg)d\bigg(\nu_{a},\sum_{i=1}^{M}\alpha_{i}\nu_{i}+\bigg(1-\sum_{i=1}^{M}\alpha_{i}\bigg)\nu_{a}\bigg),

\displaystyle\qquad\forall(\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\cdots,\nu_{M},\nu_{a})\in\mathbb{R}^{M+1}.

(15)

Then, the following can be proved.

Theorem 5
.

Consider a bandit
\mu=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\cdots,\mu_{K})\in\mathcal{S}
such that the set of optimal arms is
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\cdots,\mu_{M}
.
Let

\displaystyle\text{Alt}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{a\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\{1,2,\cdots,M\}}\big\{\lambda\in\mathcal{S}:(\lambda_{a}>\lambda_{1})\wedge(\lambda_{a}>\lambda_{2})

\displaystyle\qquad\wedge\cdots\wedge(\lambda_{a}>\lambda_{M})\}.

(16)

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
. Then, for any
\delta
-PAC strategy, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\tau]\geq T^{*}(\mu)\text{kl}(1-\delta,\delta),

(17)

where

\displaystyle T^{*}(\mu)^{-1}=\sup_{w\in\Sigma_{K}}\inf_{\lambda\in\text{Alt}(\mu)}\bigg(\sum_{a=1}^{K}w_{a}d(\mu_{a},\lambda_{a})\bigg)

(18)

\displaystyle=\sup_{w\in\Sigma_{K}}\min_{a\notin[M]}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}+w_{a}\bigg)

\displaystyle\times I_{\frac{w_{1}}{\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}+w_{a}},\frac{w_{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}+w_{a}},\cdots,\frac{w_{M}}{\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}+w_{a}}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\cdots,\mu_{M},\mu_{a}).

(19)

Remark 6
.

Some remarks are in order.

•

The case
M=K
is trivial when
M
is known in advance, as any arm can be selected. Hence, in this work we assume
K>M
. Moreover, Theorem
5
recovers the sample complexity result in
[
3
, Lemma 3]
when
M=1
.

•

As
\text{kl}(1-\delta,\delta)\sim\log(1/\delta)
when
\delta
goes to zero. Theorem
5
provides an asymptotic lower bound

\displaystyle\liminf_{\delta\to 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\tau]}{\log(1/\delta)}\geq T^{*}(\mu).

(20)

A non-asymptotic version can be obtained by using the inequality
\text{kl}(1-\delta,\delta)\geq\log(1/(2.4\delta))
that holds for any
\delta\in(0,1)

[
3
]
.

Proof.

For any
\lambda\in\text{Alt}(\mu)
, there exists
a\notin[M]
such that
\lambda_{a}>\lambda_{i}
for all
i\in[M]
. Hence,
[M]
is not the set of optimal arms under
\lambda
. Thus, introducing the event
\mathcal{E}=\{\hat{a}_{\tau}\in[M]\}\in\mathcal{F}_{\tau}
, any
\delta
-PAC algorithm satisfies
\mathbb{P}_{\mu}(\mathcal{E})>1-\delta
and
\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{E})\leq\delta
.

By Lemma
3
, we have

\displaystyle\forall\lambda\in\text{Alt}(\mu):\sum_{a=1}^{K}d(\mu_{a},\lambda_{a})\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[N_{a}(\tau)]\geq\text{kl}(1-\delta,\delta).

(21)

Then, it holds that

\displaystyle\text{kl}(1-\delta,\delta)\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\tau]\sup_{w\in\Sigma_{K}}\inf_{\lambda\in\text{Alt}(\mu)}\bigg(\sum_{a=1}^{K}w_{a}d(\mu_{a},\lambda_{a})\bigg),

(22)

or

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\tau]\geq\bigg[\sup_{w\in\Sigma_{K}}\inf_{\lambda\in\text{Alt}(\mu)}\bigg(\sum_{a=1}^{K}w_{a}d(\mu_{a},\lambda_{a})\bigg)\bigg]^{-1}\text{kl}(1-\delta,\delta).

(23)

Now, we estimate
\inf_{\lambda\in\text{Alt}(\mu)}\big(\sum_{a=1}^{K}w_{a}d(\mu_{a},\lambda_{a})\big)
for a fixed
w\in\Sigma_{K}
. Observe that

\displaystyle\inf_{\lambda\in\text{Alt}(\mu)}\bigg(\sum_{a=1}^{K}w_{a}d(\mu_{a},\lambda_{a})\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad=\min_{a\notin[M]}\inf_{\lambda:\lambda_{a}\geq\lambda_{1},\lambda_{a}\geq\lambda_{2},\cdots,\lambda_{a}\geq\lambda_{M}}\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}d(\mu_{i},\lambda_{i})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+w_{a}d(\mu_{a},\lambda_{a}).

(24)

Next, we minimize
f(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\cdots,\lambda_{M},\lambda_{a})=\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}d(\mu_{i},\lambda_{i})+w_{a}d(\mu_{a},\lambda_{a})
under constraints
\lambda_{a}\geq\lambda_{1},\lambda_{a}\geq\lambda_{2},\cdots,\lambda_{a}\geq\lambda_{M}
. This is a convex optimization problem (see Lemma
2
) that can solved analytically. First, we assume that
w_{i}>0,\forall i\in[M]
.

The Lagrangian is

\displaystyle L(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\cdots,\lambda_{M},\lambda_{a},\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\cdots,\beta_{M})

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}d(\mu_{i},\lambda_{i})+w_{a}d(\mu_{a},\lambda_{a})-\sum_{i=1}^{M}\beta_{i}(\lambda_{a}-\lambda_{i}),

(25)

where
\beta_{i}\geq 0
for all
i\in[M]
.

For a one-parameter exponential family with density

p_{\mu}(x)=h(x)\exp\left(\theta(\mu)x-A(\theta(\mu))\right),

where
\mu=\dot{A}(\theta)
, the KL divergence between
P_{\mu}
and
P_{\lambda}
is given by:

d(\mu,\lambda)=A(\theta(\lambda))-A(\theta(\mu))-(\theta(\lambda)-\theta(\mu))\mu.

Hence, we have

\displaystyle L(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\cdots,\lambda_{M},\lambda_{a},\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\cdots,\beta_{M})

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\big[A(\theta(\lambda_{i}))-A(\theta(\mu_{i}))-(\theta(\lambda_{i})-\theta(\mu_{i}))\mu_{i}\big]

\displaystyle\quad\qquad+w_{a}\big[A(\theta(\lambda_{a}))-A(\theta(\mu_{a}))-(\theta(\lambda_{a})-\theta(\mu_{a}))\mu_{a}\big]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\sum_{i=1}^{M}\beta_{i}(\lambda_{a}-\lambda_{i}).

(26)

Then, the KKT conditions are

\displaystyle 0=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\lambda_{i}}=w_{i}(\lambda_{i}-\mu_{i})\frac{d\theta(\lambda_{i})}{d\lambda_{i}}+\beta_{i},\qquad\forall i\in[M],

(27)

\displaystyle 0=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\lambda_{a}}=w_{a}(\lambda_{a}-\mu_{a})\frac{d\theta(\lambda_{a})}{d\lambda_{a}}-\sum_{i=1}^{M}\beta_{i},

(28)

\displaystyle\beta_{i}(\lambda_{a}-\lambda_{i})=0,\qquad\forall i\in[M],

(29)

\displaystyle\lambda_{a}\geq\lambda_{i},\qquad\forall i\in[M].

(30)

If

for all

. Consequently, by (

for all

\displaystyle f(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{M},\lambda_{a})=\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}d(\mu_{i},\lambda_{i})+w_{a}d(\mu_{a},\lambda_{a})=0.

\displaystyle=\text{argmax}_{w\in\Sigma_{K}}\min_{a\notin[M]}\inf_{\lambda:\lambda_{a}\geq\lambda_{1},\lambda_{a}\geq\lambda_{2},\cdots,\lambda_{a}\geq\lambda_{M}}

\displaystyle\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}d(\mu_{i},\lambda_{i})+w_{a}d(\mu_{a},\lambda_{a}),

(40)

V
The Track-and-Stop Strategy

V-A

Sampling Rule: Tracking the Optimal Proportions

We adopt the same sampling rules as described in
[
3
, Section 3]
. The core idea is to match the optimal sampling proportions
w^{*}(\mu)
by tracking the plug-in estimate
w^{*}(\hat{\mu}(t))
. However, in bandit settings, relying directly on plug-in estimates can be risky: poor initial estimates may cause some arms to be prematurely discarded, thus preventing the collection of new observations that would correct earlier mistakes. In fact, naive plug-in tracking may fail to identify the best arm reliably.

To mitigate this issue, a simple yet effective solution is to enforce sufficient exploration across all arms to ensure fast convergence of the empirical means
\hat{\mu}(t)
.

One such strategy, termed
C-Tracking
, modifies the optimization step by projecting
w^{*}(\mu)
onto a truncated simplex to ensure every arm is sampled at least minimally. For each
\varepsilon\in(0,1/K]
, define the truncated simplex:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{K}^{\varepsilon}=\left\{(w_{1},\dots,w_{K})\in[\varepsilon,1]^{K}:\sum_{a=1}^{K}w_{a}=1\right\}.

(41)

Let
w^{\varepsilon}(\mu)
denote the
L^{\infty}
-projection of
w^{*}(\mu)
onto
\Sigma_{K}^{\varepsilon}
, and choose
\varepsilon_{t}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{K^{2}+t}}
. Then the sampling rule is given by

\displaystyle A_{t+1}\in\arg\max_{1\leq a\leq K}\sum_{s=0}^{t}w_{a}^{\varepsilon_{s}}(\hat{\mu}(s))-N_{a}(t).

(42)

An alternative, often more practical rule is
D-Tracking
, which targets
w^{*}(\hat{\mu}(t))
directly and introduces forced exploration only when necessary. Let

\displaystyle U_{t}=\left\{a\in\{1,\dots,K\}:N_{a}(t)<\sqrt{t}-\frac{K}{2}\right\}.

(43)

Then the sampling rule is defined as

\displaystyle A_{t+1}\in\begin{cases}\arg\min_{a\in U_{t}}N_{a}(t)&\text{if }U_{t}\neq\emptyset,\\
\arg\max_{1\leq a\leq K}\left[tw^{*}_{a}(\hat{\mu}(t))-N_{a}(t)\right]&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(44)

Both sampling rules are guaranteed to ensure that the empirical sampling proportions converge to the optimal allocation, as established in
[
3
, Section 3.1]
.

For simplicity, we only provide a proof for Bernoulli bandits (see

\displaystyle T_{a;b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{M}}=\inf\{t\in\mathbb{N}:Z_{a;b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{M}}(t)>\beta(t,\delta)\}.

Now, for each tuple
(b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{M})
such that
\{b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{M}\}\neq[M]
, there exists

\mu_{a^{*}_{b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{M}}}\geq\max_{1\leq i\leq M}\mu_{b_{i}}.

In addition, from (
95
) we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\big(\tau<\infty,\hat{a}_{\tau}\notin[M]\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sum_{b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{M}}\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\big(T_{a^{*}_{b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{M}};b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{M}}<\infty\big).

(96)

Now, it is sufficient to show that if

\beta(t,\delta)=\log\left[K^{M}\left(\frac{4}{M+1}\right)^{(M+1)/2}t^{(M+1)/2}\frac{1}{\delta}\right]

and

\mu_{a}\geq\mu_{b_{1}}\wedge\mu_{a}\geq\mu_{b_{2}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mu_{a}\geq\mu_{b_{M}},

then

\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\big(T_{a;b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{M}}<\infty\big)\leq\frac{\delta}{K^{M}}.

For such a tuple of arms, observe that on the event
\{T_{a;b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{M}}=t\}
, time
t
is the first moment when
Z_{a;b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{M}}
exceeds the threshold
\beta(t,\delta)
, which implies by definition that

\displaystyle 1\leq e^{-\beta(t,\delta)}

\displaystyle\times\frac{\max_{\mu^{\prime}_{a}\leq\mu^{\prime}_{b_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mu^{\prime}_{a}\leq\mu^{\prime}_{b_{M}}}p_{\mu^{\prime}_{a}}(\underline{X}_{N_{a}(t)}^{a})\prod_{i=1}^{M}p_{\mu^{\prime}_{b_{i}}}(\underline{X}_{N_{b_{i}}(t)}^{b_{i}})}{\max_{\mu^{\prime}_{a}\geq\mu^{\prime}_{b_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mu^{\prime}_{a}\geq\mu^{\prime}_{b_{M}}}p_{\mu^{\prime}_{a}}(\underline{X}_{N_{a}(t)}^{a})\prod_{i=1}^{M}p_{\mu^{\prime}_{b_{i}}}(\underline{X}_{N_{b_{i}}(t)}^{b_{i}})}\Bigg].

(97)

It thus holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\big(T_{a;b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{M}}<\infty\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\big(T_{a;b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{M}}=t\big)=\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{(T_{a;b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{M}}=t)}\right]

(98)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}e^{-\beta(t,\delta)}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\Bigg[\mathbbm{1}_{(T_{a;b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{M}}=t)}

\displaystyle\quad\frac{\max_{\mu^{\prime}_{a}\leq\mu^{\prime}_{b_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mu^{\prime}_{a}\leq\mu^{\prime}_{b_{M}}}p_{\mu^{\prime}_{a}}(\underline{X}_{N_{a}(t)}^{a})\prod_{i=1}^{M}p_{\mu^{\prime}_{b_{i}}}(\underline{X}_{N_{b_{i}}(t)}^{b_{i}})}{\max_{\mu^{\prime}_{a}\geq\mu^{\prime}_{b_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mu^{\prime}_{a}\geq\mu^{\prime}_{b_{M}}}p_{\mu^{\prime}_{a}}(\underline{X}_{N_{a}(t)}^{a})\prod_{i=1}^{M}p_{\mu^{\prime}_{b_{i}}}(\underline{X}_{N_{b_{i}}(t)}^{b_{i}})}\Bigg]

(99)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}e^{-\beta(t,\delta)}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\Bigg[\mathbbm{1}_{(T_{a;b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{M}}=t)}

\displaystyle\quad\frac{\max_{\mu^{\prime}_{a}\leq\mu^{\prime}_{b_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mu^{\prime}_{a}\leq\mu^{\prime}_{b_{M}}}p_{\mu^{\prime}_{a}}(\underline{X}_{N_{a}(t)}^{a})\prod_{i=1}^{M}p_{\mu^{\prime}_{b_{i}}}(\underline{X}_{N_{b_{i}}(t)}^{b_{i}})}{p_{\mu_{a}}(\underline{X}_{N_{a}(t)}^{a})\prod_{i=1}^{M}p_{\mu_{b_{i}}}(\underline{X}_{N_{b_{i}}(t)}^{b_{i}})}\Bigg].

(100)

Expanding the expectation into an integral over the sample space
\{0,1\}^{t}
, we get

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}e^{-\beta(t,\delta)}\int_{\{0,1\}^{t}}\mathbbm{1}_{(T_{a;b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{M}}=t)}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{t})

\displaystyle\quad\times\max_{\mu^{\prime}_{a}\leq\mu^{\prime}_{b_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mu^{\prime}_{a}\leq\mu^{\prime}_{b_{M}}}p_{\mu^{\prime}_{a}}(\underline{x}_{N_{a}(t)}^{a})\prod_{i=1}^{M}p_{\mu^{\prime}_{b_{i}}}(\underline{x}_{N_{b_{i}}(t)}^{b_{i}})

\displaystyle\qquad\prod_{i\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\{a,b_{1},\cdots,b_{M}\}}p_{\mu_{i}}(\underline{x}_{N_{i}(t)}^{i})dx_{1}dx_{2}\cdots dx_{t}.

(101)

Now, by using
[
3
, Lemma 11]
, we have

\displaystyle\max_{\mu^{\prime}_{a}\leq\mu^{\prime}_{b_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mu^{\prime}_{a}\leq\mu^{\prime}_{b_{M}}}p_{\mu^{\prime}_{a}}(\underline{x}_{N_{a}(t)}^{a})\prod_{i=1}^{M}p_{\mu^{\prime}_{b_{i}}}(\underline{x}_{N_{b_{i}}(t)}^{b_{i}})

\displaystyle\quad\leq 2\sqrt{N_{a}(t)}\prod_{i=1}^{M}(2\sqrt{N_{b_{i}}(t)})\,\text{kt}(\underline{x}_{N_{a}(t)}^{a})\prod_{i=1}^{M}\text{kt}(\underline{x}_{N_{b_{i}}(t)}^{b_{i}})

\displaystyle\quad\leq 2^{M+1}\left(\frac{t}{M+1}\right)^{\frac{M+1}{2}}\text{kt}(\underline{x}_{N_{a}(t)}^{a})\prod_{i=1}^{M}\text{kt}(\underline{x}_{N_{b_{i}}(t)}^{b_{i}}),

(102)

where
\text{kt}(\cdot)
is the Krichevsky–Trofimov distribution (see
[
13
]
). Here, we use the fact that
\nu_{1}\nu_{2}\cdots\nu_{M+1}\leq(\frac{\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}+\cdots+\nu_{M+1}}{M+1})^{M+1}
for all
\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\cdots,\nu_{M+1}\geq 0
in (

).

Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\big(T_{a;b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{M}}<\infty\big)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}2^{M+1}\left(\frac{t}{M+1}\right)^{\frac{M+1}{2}}e^{-\beta(t,\delta)}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\int_{\{0,1\}^{t}}\mathbbm{1}_{(T_{a;b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{M}}=t)}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{t})

\displaystyle\qquad\times\text{kt}(\underline{x}_{N_{a}(t)}^{a})\prod_{i=1}^{M}\text{kt}(\underline{x}_{N_{b_{i}}(t)}^{b_{i}})

\displaystyle\qquad\times\prod_{i\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\{a,b_{1},\cdots,b_{M}\}}p_{\mu_{i}}(\underline{x}_{N_{i}(t)}^{i})\,dx_{1}\cdots dx_{t}.

(103)

Defining
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
as the probability measure induced by the product of KT distributions for arms

and the true distributions for all other arms, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\big(T_{a;b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{M}}<\infty\big)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}2^{M+1}\left(\frac{t}{M+1}\right)^{\frac{M+1}{2}}e^{-\beta(t,\delta)}\tilde{\mathbb{P}}\big(T_{a;b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{M}}=t\big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta}{K^{M}}\tilde{\mathbb{P}}\big(T_{a;b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{M}}<\infty\big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta}{K^{M}}.

(104)

Sample Complexity Analysis

In this section, we analyze the sample complexity of the modified C-Tracking and D-Tracking algorithms introduced in Section
V
. We begin by establishing the following result.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\alpha>0
and
c_{1},c_{2}>0
be such that

such that

Based on Lemma

VII-A

Almost-sure Upper Bound

Theorem 8
.

Let
\mu
denote a bandit model from an exponential family. Define

\displaystyle C=K^{M}\left(\frac{4}{M+1}\right)^{(M+1)/2},\quad\alpha=\frac{M+1}{2},

Consider applying Chernoff’s stopping rule with confidence parameter

\beta(t,\delta)=\log\left(\frac{Ct^{\alpha}}{\delta}\right),

in conjunction with either the C-Tracking or D-Tracking sampling rule. Then, almost surely,

\limsup_{\delta\to 0}\frac{\tau}{\log(1/\delta)}\leq T^{*}(\mu),

where
\tau
denotes the stopping time, and
T^{*}(\mu)
is defined in Theorem
5

Proof.

For sufficiently large
t
, i.e.,
t\geq t_{0}
, it holds that

\displaystyle(\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)\geq\hat{\mu}_{a}(t))\vee(\hat{\mu}_{2}(t)\geq\hat{\mu}_{a}(t))\wedge\cdots\wedge(\hat{\mu}_{M}(t)\geq\hat{\mu}_{a}(t))

(105)

for all
a\notin[M]
.

In addition, by using the C-Tracking and D-Tracking sampling rule, for
t_{1}
sufficiently large (
t_{1}>t_{0}
), for any
t\geq t_{1}
and any
a\notin[M]
, we also have

\displaystyle\max_{a\notin[M]}S_{a;1,2,\cdots,M}(t)

\displaystyle\quad=\max_{a\notin[M]}\frac{N_{a}(t)}{t}\big[d(\hat{\mu}_{a}(t),\hat{\mu}_{a;1,2,\cdots,M}(t)+\zeta(t))

\displaystyle\qquad-d(\hat{\mu}_{a}(t),\hat{\mu}_{a;1,2,\cdots,M}(t))\big]

\displaystyle\qquad-\min_{i\in[M]}\frac{N_{i}(t)}{t}d(\hat{\mu}_{i}(t),\hat{\mu}_{a;1,2,\cdots,M}(t))

\displaystyle\quad\leq-\frac{1}{2}\min_{a\notin[M]}\min_{i\in[M]}w_{i}^{*}d\bigg(\mu_{i},\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}^{*}\mu_{i}+w_{a}^{*}\mu_{a}}{\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}^{*}+w_{a}^{*}}\bigg)<0.

(106)

Now for
t>t_{1}
, observe that

\displaystyle Z(t)

\displaystyle\geq\min_{a\notin[M]}Z_{a;1,\ldots,M}(t)

\displaystyle=\min_{a\notin[M]}\sum_{i=1}^{M}N_{i}(t)\,d\left(\hat{\mu}_{i}(t),\hat{\mu}_{a;1,\ldots,M}(t)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+N_{a}(t)\,d\left(\hat{\mu}_{a}(t),\hat{\mu}_{a;1,\ldots,M}(t)\right)

\displaystyle=t\min_{a\notin[M]}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{N_{i}(t)}{t}+\frac{N_{a}(t)}{t}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times I_{\frac{N_{1}(t)/t}{\sum_{i=1}^{M}N_{i}(t)/t+N_{a}(t)/t},\ldots,\frac{N_{M}(t)/t}{\sum_{i=1}^{M}N_{i}(t)/t+N_{a}(t)/t}}

\displaystyle\qquad\left(\hat{\mu}_{1}(t),\ldots,\hat{\mu}_{M}(t),\hat{\mu}_{a}(t)\right).

(107)

For
a\geq M+1

\displaystyle(w,\lambda)\mapsto\left(\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}+w_{a}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\times I_{\frac{w_{1}}{\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}+w_{a}},\ldots,\frac{w_{M}}{\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}+w_{a}}}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{M},\lambda_{a})

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{N_{i}(t)}{t}+\frac{N_{a}(t)}{t}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\times I_{\frac{N_{1}(t)/t}{\sum_{i=1}^{M}N_{i}(t)/t+N_{a}(t)/t},\ldots,\frac{N_{M}(t)/t}{\sum_{i=1}^{M}N_{i}(t)/t+N_{a}(t)/t}}

\displaystyle\qquad\left(\hat{\mu}_{1}(t),\ldots,\hat{\mu}_{M}(t),\hat{\mu}_{a}(t)\right)

\displaystyle\quad\geq\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}^{*}+w_{a}^{*}}{1+\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\qquad\times I_{\frac{w_{1}^{*}}{\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}^{*}+w_{a}^{*}},\ldots,\frac{w_{M}^{*}}{\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}^{*}+w_{a}^{*}}}(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{M},\mu_{a}).

(108)

Hence, for
t\geq t_{2}
,

\displaystyle Z(t)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{t}{1+\varepsilon}\cdot\min_{a\notin[M]}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}^{*}+w_{a}^{*}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\times I_{\frac{w_{1}^{*}}{\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}^{*}+w_{a}^{*}},\ldots,\frac{w_{M}^{*}}{\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}^{*}+w_{a}^{*}}}(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{M},\mu_{a})

\displaystyle=\frac{t}{(1+\varepsilon)T^{*}(\mu)}.

(109)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle=\inf\{t\in\mathbb{N}:Z(t)\geq\beta(t,\delta)\}

\displaystyle\leq t_{2}\vee\inf\left\{t\in\mathbb{N}:\frac{t}{1+\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{1}{T^{*}(\mu)}\geq\log\left(\frac{Ct^{\alpha}}{\delta}\right)\right\},

(110)

where

C=K^{M}\left(\frac{4}{M+1}\right)^{(M+1)/2},\quad\alpha=\frac{M+1}{2}.

In the case where
M
is fixed (not a function of
\delta

7

as

\limsup_{\delta\to 0}\frac{\tau}{\log(1/\delta)}\leq(1+\varepsilon)T^{*}(\mu).

Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
concludes the proof.

∎

VII-B

Asymptotic Optimality in Expectation

Theorem 9
.

Let
\mu
be a bandit model from an exponential family. Define

\displaystyle C=K^{M}\left(\frac{4}{M+1}\right)^{(M+1)/2},\quad\alpha=\frac{M+1}{2},

Using Chernoff’s stopping rule with

\displaystyle\beta(t,\delta)=\log\left(\frac{Ct^{\alpha}}{\delta}\right),

and the sampling rule C-Tracking or D-Tracking, we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{\delta\to 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\tau_{\delta}]}{\log(1/\delta)}\leq T^{*}(\mu),

(111)

where
T^{*}(\mu)
is defined in Theorem
5
.

Remark 10
.

Some remarks are in order.

•

Consider the special Gaussian bandit
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
where
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}=\cdots=\mu_{M}=\Delta
and
\mu_{M+1}=\mu_{M+2}=\cdots=\mu_{K}=0
for some
\Delta>0
. Then, for any
a\notin[M]
we have

\displaystyle I_{\frac{w_{1}}{\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}+w_{a}},\cdots,\frac{w_{M}}{\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}+w_{a}}}(\mu_{1},\cdots,\mu_{M},\mu_{a})

\displaystyle\quad=\bigg[\frac{\beta_{a}(w)(1-\beta_{a}(w))^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Delta^{2}+\frac{(1-\beta_{a}(w))\beta_{a}^{2}(w)}{2\sigma^{2}}\Delta^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{\beta_{a}(w)(1-\beta_{a}(w))}{2\sigma^{2}}\Delta^{2},

(112)

where

\displaystyle\beta_{a}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}+w_{a}}.

(113)

Hence, by (
19
) it holds that

\displaystyle T^{*}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{\sup_{w\in\Sigma_{K}}\min_{a\notin[M]}\beta_{a}(w)(1-\beta_{a}(w))\Delta^{2}}

\displaystyle=\Theta\bigg(\frac{1}{\Delta^{2}}\bigg).

(114)

This implies that the expected sample complexity diverges to infinity as
\Delta\to 0
for
\Delta>0
. When
\Delta=0
and
M=K
is known, any arm can be selected as mentioned above, and hence the sample complexity is finite (in fact, zero).

•

For each bandit instance
\lambda\in\mathcal{S}
, let
i^{*}(\lambda)
denote the set of all optimal arms. Further, for each
i\in\{1,2,\dots,M\}
, define

\displaystyle\neg i:=\{\lambda\in\mathcal{S}:i\notin i^{*}(\lambda)\}=\bigcup_{a\neq i}\{\lambda\in\mathcal{S}:\lambda_{a}>\lambda_{i}\}.

It is straightforward to verify that
\neg i\supset\text{Alt}(\mu)
for all
i
. Therefore, by
[
1
, Theorem 1]
and (
18
), we have

\displaystyle T^{*}(\mu)\leq D(\mu)^{-1},

where

\displaystyle D(\mu)=\max_{i\in i^{*}(\mu)}\max_{w\in\Sigma_{K}}\inf_{\lambda\in\neg i}\sum_{a=1}^{K}w_{a}\,d(\mu_{a},\lambda_{a}).

(115)

Here,
D(\mu)^{-1}
corresponds to the sample complexity of the same bandit problem in the unknown-
M
setting
[
1
]
. This highlights that knowing
M
can improve the sample complexity. Technically, the stopping time based on the generalized log-likelihood ratio (GLLR) statistic
Z_{a;b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{M}}(t)
in (
45
) is only well-defined when
M
is known (see Section
V
).

Proof.

Following a similar strategy as in
[
3
, Theorem 14]
, we simplify notation by assuming that the bandit means satisfy

\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}=\cdots=\mu_{M}>\mu_{M+1}\geq\mu_{M+2}\geq\cdots\geq\mu_{K}.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. By the continuity of
w^{*}(\mu)
, there exists
\xi=\xi(\varepsilon)\leq\frac{\mu_{M}-\mu_{M+1}}{4}
such that the hypercube

\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}:=[\mu_{1}-\xi,\mu_{1}+\xi]\times\cdots\times[\mu_{K}-\xi,\mu_{K}+\xi]

satisfies, for all

,

In particular, if
\hat{\mu}(t)\in\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
, then the top-
M
empirical arms are exactly

.

Let
T\in\mathbb{N}
, define

, and introduce the event

\mathcal{E}_{T}:=\bigcap_{t=h(T)}^{T}\{\hat{\mu}(t)\in\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}\}.

In addition, by Lemma
[
3

Hence, by using the C-Tracking and D-Tracking sampling rule, for any sufficient small

\displaystyle\sup_{a\notin[M]}S_{a;1,2,\cdots,M}(t)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{i=1}^{M}N_{i}(t)d(\hat{\mu}_{i}(t),\hat{\mu}_{a;1,\dots,M}(t))\big]

\displaystyle:=\min_{a\notin[M]}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{M}w^{\prime}_{i}+w^{\prime}_{a}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\times I_{\left\{\frac{w^{\prime}_{i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{M}w^{\prime}_{j}+w^{\prime}_{a}}\right\}_{i=1}^{M}}(\mu^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\mu^{\prime}_{M},\mu^{\prime}_{a}).

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{T}+\sum_{t=\sqrt{T}}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\{tC^{*}_{\varepsilon}(\mu)\leq\beta(T,\delta)\}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{T}+\frac{\beta(T,\delta)}{C^{*}_{\varepsilon}(\mu)}.

(125)

\mathcal{E}_{T}\subset\{\tau_{\delta}\leq T\},

[

\eta>0

\displaystyle T_{0}(\delta)

\displaystyle\quad\leq C(\eta)+\inf\left\{T\in\mathbb{N}:\frac{1}{C^{*}_{\varepsilon}(\mu)}\log\left(\frac{CT^{\alpha}}{\delta}\right)\leq\frac{T}{1+\eta}\right\}

and thus,

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\tau]}{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\leq T^{*}(\mu).

∎

VIII
Conclusion

In this work, we revisited the problem of best-arm identification in stochastic multi-armed bandits under the fixed-confidence setting, focusing on the general case where multiple optimal arms may exist. Unlike prior studies, which primarily addressed the scenario with an unknown number of optimal arms, we investigated the setting where this number is known in advance.

Our main contribution lies in deriving a new information-theoretic lower bound that characterizes the minimal achievable sample complexity when the number of optimal arms is known. Building on this insight, we established that a modified Track-and-Stop algorithm—equipped with an appropriate stopping rule based on generalized log-likelihood ratio statistics—achieves this bound asymptotically. This result provides the first instance-optimal guarantee for Track-and-Stop in multi-optimal settings and demonstrates that knowledge of the number of optimal arms can fundamentally reduce sample complexity.

Overall, our findings extend the theoretical foundations of best-arm identification and reinforce the role of likelihood ratio-based methods as principled and optimal strategies for sequential decision-making under uncertainty. Future research directions include generalizing these results to structured bandit models, such as combinatorial or contextual settings, and developing adaptive algorithms that leverage structural information while preserving optimality guarantees.

Gaussian Distribution (Known Variance):

Let
X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
. The natural parameter is
\theta(\lambda)=\lambda/\sigma^{2}
, and the log-partition function is
A(\theta)=\frac{\sigma^{2}\theta^{2}}{2}
.

The KL divergence is:

d(\mu,\lambda)=\frac{(\mu-\lambda)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}.

This is a quadratic function in
\lambda
, and hence convex. In fact,

\frac{d^{2}}{d\lambda^{2}}d(\mu,\lambda)=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}>0.

•

Bernoulli Distribution:

Let
X\sim\text{Bernoulli}(\lambda)
with
\lambda\in(0,1)
. The KL divergence is:

d(\mu,\lambda)=\mu\log\left(\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\right)+(1-\mu)\log\left(\frac{1-\mu}{1-\lambda}\right).

Taking the second derivative with respect to
\lambda
, we obtain:

\frac{d^{2}}{d\lambda^{2}}d(\mu,\lambda)=\frac{\mu}{\lambda^{2}}+\frac{1-\mu}{(1-\lambda)^{2}}.

Since
\lambda\in(0,1)
and
\mu\in(0,1)
, this expression is strictly positive. Thus,
d(\mu,\lambda)
is convex in
\lambda
.

•

Poisson Distribution:

Let
X\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda)
with
\lambda>0
. The KL divergence is:

d(\mu,\lambda)=\lambda-\mu+\mu\log\left(\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\right).

Taking the first and second derivatives:

\frac{d}{d\lambda}d(\mu,\lambda)=1-\frac{\mu}{\lambda},\quad\frac{d^{2}}{d\lambda^{2}}d(\mu,\lambda)=\frac{\mu}{\lambda^{2}}>0.

Hence,
d(\mu,\lambda)
is convex in
\lambda
for Poisson distributions.

In all three cases—Gaussian, Bernoulli, and Poisson—the KL divergence
d(\mu,\lambda)
is convex in the second argument
\lambda
, which is a useful property in many optimization and information-theoretic contexts.

Therefore,
\{d(\mu_{i},\lambda_{i})\}_{i=1}^{M}
and
d(\mu_{a},\lambda_{a})
are convex in their respective arguments.

Now, define

\displaystyle f(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\cdots,\lambda_{M},\lambda_{a})=\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}d(\mu_{i},\lambda_{i})+w_{a}d(\mu_{a},\lambda_{a}).

(130)

Since each term
d(\mu_{i},\lambda_{i})
is convex in
\lambda_{i}
, and since
w_{1},w_{2},\cdots,w_{M},w_{a}>0
, their weighted sum is jointly convex in
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\cdots,\lambda_{M},\lambda_{a})
. This is a standard result from convex analysis.
