Analysis of the risk factors for contralateral refracture after hip fracture surgery in elderly individuals: a retrospective study
Authors: Ming Chen, Yanliang Li, Yudie Yang, Wei Zhuang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe risk factors for contralateral hip refracture after primary hip fracture are not fully understood; therefore, this study compared the clinical characteristics of patients with first and second hip fractures and explored and analyzed the risk factors for contralateral refracture after hip fracture in elderly individuals to provide a reference for the clinical prevention of postoperative refracture of hip fracture in elderly individuals.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted on 458 elderly patients with hip fractures who underwent surgical treatment and were discharged from our hospital from March 2016 to March 2019. The clinical data of the patients were analyzed retrospectively. Patients were divided into a case group (postoperative refracture) and a control group (no postoperative refracture) based on whether they experienced refracture within five years after surgery. The clinical data of the two groups were compared and analyzed via univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) regression analysis to identify the risk factors for refractures after hip fracture surgery in elderly patients.ResultsSixty-one patients experienced refracture, with an incidence rate of 13.3%. Age ≥ 80.5 years, female sex, poor knee joint function, FRAX score ≥ 15.5, anemia, visual impairment, osteoporosis, and Alzheimer’s disease (AD) were identified as risk factors for refracture after hip fracture surgery in elderly individuals (P < 0.05).ConclusionElderly patients with hip fractures are susceptible to refracture after surgery because of factors such as advanced age, female sex, high FRAX score, poor knee joint function, anemia, osteoporosis, visual impairment, and AD. Targeted interventions should be implemented based on the above risk factors.

Introduction

Hip fractures in older adults due to osteoporosis are known as the last fracture of life, with a very high mortality rate and a severe medical burden for patients and society. Previous studies have shown that the one-year mortality rate for hip fracture patients is 22% [1]. The incidence rate of contralateral hip fracture within ten years after hip fracture is 10–15% [2]; 1.2–9% of patients experience contralateral refracture within one year [3], and the incidence of complications, socioeconomic costs, and mortality associated with the second contralateral fracture is significantly greater than that associated with the first fracture. Therefore, preventing refracture after hip fracture is crucial. Targeted preventive interventions can help reduce the incidence of hip refracture, improve the quality of life, and alleviate the socioeconomic burden. However, effective assessment methods for evaluating the risk of hip refracture are currently lacking. The FRAX score is used to predict the probability of hip fracture and major osteoporotic fractures within the next ten years and has been found to have some predictive power for the refracture of the hip [4]. A study showed that poor knee joint function increases the risk of falls, significantly affecting the incidence of hip fractures [5], and post-hip fracture surgery can also lead to knee pain and decreased function [6], potentially causing refracture. Therefore, the primary purpose of this study was to investigate the impact of the FRAX score, knee joint function, and other factors, such as sex, age, anemia, visual impairment, chronic kidney disease (CKD), osteoporosis, cerebral infarction, and AD, on the risk of contralateral refracture after hip fracture.

Methods

Collection method

A retrospective analysis of the clinical data of 458 elderly patients with hip fractures who underwent surgical treatment and were discharged from our hospital from March 2016 to March 2019 was conducted. The patients’ ages ranged from 65 to 90 years, with 95 patients aged 80 years or older and 363 patients under 80 years old; there were 132 males and 326 females.

Experimental methods

A retrospective analysis of the clinical data of the patients was performed, and the incidence rate of refracture within five years of surgery was statistically analyzed. Patients were divided into a case group (in which refracture occurred) and a control group (in which no refracture occurred) based on whether they experienced refracture within five years after surgery. The clinical data of the two groups were compared and analyzed. Univariate analysis was used to screen for variables statistically significant for subsequent contralateral hip fracture. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve assessment was performed for the FRAX score and age, followed by multivariate logistic regression analysis to establish independent risk factors. The analysis indicators included age, sex, FRAX score, Lysholm knee score, hemoglobin, CKD, visual impairment, osteoporosis, cerebral infarction, and AD.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was performed via SPSS 26.0 software. The Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test was used to assess normality for continuous variables. Normally distributed data are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SDs) and were analyzed via independent Student’s t-tests; nonnormally distributed data are presented as the medians and interquartile ranges and were analyzed via Mann‒Whitney U tests. Categorical data are presented as numbers and percentages and analyzed via chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. A two-tailed P-value less than 0.05 indicated a significant difference.

Results

There were 458 patients (Figs. 1), 121 males and 276 females. Sixty-one (13.3%) patients (ten males and 51 females) experienced refracture of the hip joint within five years after surgery. Statistical analysis of the two groups revealed significant differences in sex, age, FRAX score, Lysholm knee score, hemoglobin, visual impairment, osteoporosis, cerebral infarction, and AD between the refracture and nonfracture groups (P < 0.05). CKD was not statistically significant (Table 1).

We plotted the ROC curve to assess the discriminative ability of the FRAX score and We calculated the area under the curve (AUC) to be 0.8608 (95% CI, 0.8000–0.9217) (Fig. 2A), indicating that the predictive model has discriminative solid power. The cutoff value was 15.5 (specificity 0.912, sensitivity 0.672). Additionally, to further clarify the discriminative ability of age, we plotted the ROC curve for age (Fig. 2B), with an AUC of 0.8165 (95% CI, 0.7489–0.8841), and the cutoff value was 80.5 (specificity 0.899, sensitivity 0.639). We defined the group with FRAX scores above the cutoff value of 15.5 as the high-risk group and those above 80.5 as the elderly group. Anemia was categorized based on the diagnostic criteria (males < 120, females < 110) as the anemia group. Variables with P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were added to the multivariate logistic regression analysis, which revealed advanced age (OR, 5.997, 95% CI, 2.270–15.846, P < 0.001), high-risk FRAX score (OR, 8.952, 95% CI, 3.303–24.266, P < 0.001), female sex (OR, 2.646, 95% CI, 1.002–6.992, P = 0.05), poor knee score (OR, 0.089, 95% CI, 0.016–0.499, P = 0.006), anemia (OR, 6.048, 95% CI, 2.280–16.041, P < 0.001), visual impairment (OR, 4.504, 95% CI, 1.405–14.439, P = 0.011), cerebral infarction (OR, 1.708, 95% CI, 0.647–4.509, P = 0.28), AD (OR, 3.691, 95% CI, 1.096–12.433, P = 0.035), and osteoporosis (OR, 2.714, 95% CI, 1.037–7.102, P = 0.042) were independent risk factors for refracture (Table 2).

Discussion

This study utilized univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis combined with ROC curve analysis to identify independent risk factors for refracture after hip fracture surgery in elderly individuals, excluding the interaction between these factors and validating the predictive capabilities of the FRAX score and age, as well as determining the optimal threshold values for these predictors. The results revealed significant differences in age, sex, FRAX score, Lysholm knee score, anemia, visual impairment, osteoporosis, cerebral infarction, and AD between the case and control groups (P < 0.05). Further multivariate logistic regression analysis confirmed that age ≥ 80.5 years, female sex, poor knee function, FRAX score ≥ 15.5, anemia, visual impairment, osteoporosis, and AD were risk factors for refracture after hip fracture surgery in the elderly (P < 0.05).

The treatment guidelines for osteoporosis are based on dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry T-scores, fracture history, and FRAX scores. The FRAX score has been effectively used to predict the likelihood of hip and other major osteoporotic fractures. However, there is limited research on its predictive value for subsequent refractures. Our findings suggest that the FRAX score effectively assesses the risk of contralateral hip fracture in patients. Lott corroborated our findings, indicating that patients with FRAX scores above 15.5 are in the high-risk group, whereas those below 15.5 have a relatively lower risk of refracturing [4]. Therefore, patients identified as high risk by the FRAX score should adopt more proactive preventive measures to reduce the incidence and mortality associated with a second hip fracture.

Advanced age is not only a risk factor for initial hip fracture but also for refracture. This finding aligns with the findings of previous studies [7, 8], suggesting that older patients tend to have poorer physical function, reduced motor coordination, and lower muscle density [9, 10], making them more prone to refracture due to falls.

Numerous studies have indicated that women are more susceptible to refracture than men are [7, 8], potentially because of increased bone resorption and decreased bone density following menopause, possibly related to women’s longer life expectancy [11].

The Lysholm knee score is widely applied in various knee conditions, offering a comprehensive assessment of knee function, simple operation, and a direct reflection of patient perceptions and functional status. Our study categorized knee function into four levels based on the Lysholm score: exceptional, acceptable, fair, and poor. We found that poor knee function affects patients’ balance, increasing the risk of falls. Recent research has supported this finding [12], indicating that some patients experience post-hip fracture knee pain (PHFKP), which slows walking speed and increases the risk of refracture. Additionally, reduced mobility and bone loss due to poor knee function increase the likelihood of refracture [13].

A prospective study revealed that anemic patients have a greater risk of hip fracture [14], suggesting that decreased hemoglobin may be a marker of bone fragility progression. Anemia is often associated with osteoporosis and sarcopenia in elderly individuals [15], and the loss of muscle mass and function increases the risk of falls and subsequent fragility fractures [16]. Moreover, Quaranta et al. demonstrated that patients with high Hb levels after hip fracture have a relatively low risk of readmission [17].

AD is characterized by memory and cognitive decline due to degenerative changes in the central nervous system. Most AD patients exhibit balance disorders and gait abnormalities, increasing the risk of falls [18]. Tzeng reported results consistent with our findings [19]. Another study revealed a link between cognitive decline and the risk of hip fracture [20].

Patients with visual impairment often have poor physical balance, reduced coordination, impaired motor function, and unstable gait, making them prone to falls and fractures [21]. Ouyang et al. reported that eye diseases are independent risk factors affecting falls in older adults and that eye diseases such as cataracts and glaucoma may contribute to the increased risk of falls by affecting walking speed and lower limb muscle strength [22]. Visual impairment, poor mobility, and AD all increase the risk of refracture by increasing the risk of falls.

According to the World Health Organization criteria, patients are classified as having osteoporosis if the T-score is ≤ − 2.5. Osteoporosis patients have abnormal bone tissue structure and altered bone metabolism, leading to changes in the biomechanics of the hip joint and a greater risk of fractures. Notably, while most identified risk factors (such as sex and comorbidities) are unchangeable, osteoporosis can be improved. Yanzhu reported that the risk of refracture in patients receiving alendronate after hip fracture was significantly reduced [23]. Therefore, the early use of anti-osteoporosis medication for patients with a first hip fracture is an effective method to prevent recurrent hip fractures. Notably, in our study, patients with osteoporosis were treated with conventional calcium and calcitriol, and 20 experienced refractures. The specific effects of different anti-osteoporotic drugs on the incidence of subsequent hip refractures is a question worthy of further study.

When a patient has a hip fracture for the first time, we should not only identify the relevant risk factors for refracture in time but also take corresponding measures to prevent refracture, and it is necessary to reduce the risk of falling first. To avoid the risk of falls, a clinical assessment to identify medical conditions (such as postural hypotension, syncope, and arrhythmia) needs to be undertaken, as do basic investigations (i.e., blood pressure measurements, a 12-lead ECG, and a review of current medications). Similarly, early detection, diagnosis, and timely treatment of visual impairment, AD, and anemia may reduce the risk of falls; if someone can take care of elderly daily life, the risk of falls can also be reduced. Second, routine blood tests can assess bone health status to evaluate calcium or vitamin D deficiency. Secondary prevention of osteoporotic fracture is recommended in elderly patients with confirmed osteoporosis and a high risk of refracture, with the initial use of anabolic drugs (such as teriparatide, abaloparatide, and romosozumab) followed by anticatabolic medications (i.e., oral or intravenous bisphosphonates or denosumab) [24].

However, this study has several limitations. First, the impact of bone metabolism and other test indicators was not explored. Second, the study population was entirely Asian, which may limit the applicability of the findings to different ethnicities. Third, the sample size of patients included in this study was relatively small. Therefore, large-sample, multicenter clinical studies are needed to address these limitations, refine the research on contralateral hip fractures after hip fracture surgery, and proactively identify high-risk groups early to prevent secondary fractures. Fourth we did not consider the effects of different surgical modalities in this study, although a study reported no significant differences between surgical modalities in the treatment of patients [25]; another reported that patients treated with an Intertan nail had better outcomes and lower rates of Hb loss and blood transfusions [26]. Fifth, as we included patients from the whole hospital from March 2016 to March 2019, our hospital did not have a high screening rate for osteoporosis patients, and some of the bone mineral density data needed to be included, which may be why there were fewer osteoporosis patients. In the future, we will further study osteoporosis and treatment for hip refracture.

Conclusion

Our study revealed that advanced age, female sex, poor knee function, high FRAX score, anemia, osteoporosis, visual impairment, and AD were risk factors for refracture after hip fracture surgery in elderly patients. We also calculated that the likelihood of refracture is more significant when the age is ≥ 80.5 years and the FRAX score is ≥ 15.5. Therefore, for high-risk patients with a second hip fracture, targeted interventions such as fall prevention, medication treatment, and active treatment of underlying diseases should be implemented to reduce the incidence and premature mortality rates among this vulnerable population.
