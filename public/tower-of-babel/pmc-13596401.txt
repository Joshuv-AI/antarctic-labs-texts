Formulation and Evaluation of a Nigella sativa Oil-Based Emulsion as a Potential Hand Sanitizer Against Methicillin-Susceptible and Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus
Authors: Tamba Buffa, Abdulai Kanu, Sahr E Gbomor, Amidu G Torto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHand hygiene is key to controlling healthcare-associated infections. Staphylococcus aureus is a critical pathogen in wound infections and infection prevention is compromised by methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) because of the ability of this pathogen to resist antimicrobial agents. In this study, the efficacy of Nigella sativa oil-based emulsion against clinical S. aureus strains separated from wound samples, including methicillin-resistant strains, was studied.MethodsThis in vitro experimental study examined the properties of crude N. sativa oil, its preliminary oil emulsions, and the final emulsions made by mixing with either distilled water or carbomer 940 gel. The identification of wound strains was performed using culturing and different tests like Gram staining, catalase, coagulase, and performance of the phenotypic test for methicillin-resistant strains using cefoxitin disk diffusion. The results of antibacterial activity testing were obtained by using agar well diffusion. The obtained results were compared to those of broth-dilution MIC. The characterization included physicochemical parameters such as quality of oil, size of droplets in emulsion, viscosity of gel, and pH. All results were analyzed statistically and no duplicates were included in the analyses.ResultsThe tested 13 S. aureus strains are listed in the table containing the results of the antibacterial efficacy comparison. Undiluted N. sativa oil is suggested to have shown antibacterial activity against 11 (84.6%) of the S. aureus isolates, with the measured inhibition zones ranging from 24 to 38 mm. On the basis of the susceptibility data presented in the paper and CLSI interpretation standard, three strains from the total number of tested strains belong to MRSA. Of these three strains, two strains are susceptible to undiluted N. sativa oil. The average inhibition zone when using N. sativa oil was recorded at 27.5 ± 13.0 mm, although the inhibition ring average in the case of cefoxitin usage made 21.5 ± 8.1 mm, although the statistical difference in results was insignificant (paired t test, P = 0.100). The active solution of the emulsion at the concentration of 50% produced inhibition effects on seven studied S. aureus strains. As for other concentrations, the lowest one producing measurable effects on all studied S. aureus strains made 10% w/w with water, or 5% w/w with carbomer gel. As for the carbomer gel S1 studied in terms of viscosity, it was shown that its viscosity amounted to 2050 poise and the pH level was 7.6. The initial studies of the emulsion based on N. sativa showed droplet diameters equal to 0.25–0.29 µm.ConclusionThe conducted research shows that it is worthwhile to study different formulations on the basis of N. sativa. Nonetheless, the research has several limitations, such as lack of in vivo studies and lack of any time stability testing.

Introduction

Hand hygiene is a vital factor in reducing infectious agents in healthcare facilities. While alcohol-based hand sanitizers are often convenient since they permit quick usage, continuous application may result in dry skin. This obstacle has led to looking into other formulations that can provide similar antimicrobial characteristics.1–3 The chosen pathogenic microorganism was Staphylococcus aureus because it is one of the most frequent causes of skin and wound infections and can be present in health facilities. The organism may be showed as methicillin-susceptible S. aureus (MSSA) or methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA), the latter being an important antimicrobial-resistant strain. Evidence from Sierra Leone indicates the considerable burden of methicillin-resistance of S. aureus-related wound pathogens, demonstrating the significance of evaluating an antimicrobial formulation in two forms.4,5 Nigella sativa has fixed and volatile oils that are capable of acting as antimicrobial agents. Recent investigation confirms that this oil has a significant impact on Staphylococcus aureus responsible for antimicrobial resistance.6

Carbomer was chosen as the gelling polymer because carbomer polymers are frequently used as rheology modifier substances in topical pharmaceutical formulations and cosmetics and are capable of reaching high viscosity levels at low concentrations.7 The soybean-oil formulations were used as formulation development experiments to study the behavior of oil phase combined with ethanol, aqueous phase, surfactant/emulsifying agents, and neutralizing agents prior to the incorporation of N. sativa oil into the formulation. Consequently, soybean formulations served only as the developmental and not the experimental formulations. The formulation was made with a sufficiently high oil phase to assess the potential of the incorporation of N. sativa oil into a practically workable emulsion that would then be turned into a viscous topical formulation. The final formulation was chosen based on the physicochemical data collected in the course of the experiment namely the possibility to obtain a fine emulsion and the physicochemical properties of carbomer gel regarding its viscosity and pH.8

The main significance of the current study is the investigation of a locally obtained formulation comprising N. sativa oil for its efficacy against clinical S. aureus strain isolates and the comparison of two types of formulations, namely, water-diluted and carbomer gel diluted formulations. We hypothesize that N. sativa oil retains its antibacterial potency after emulsification and that incorporation into a carbomer gel will facilitate the optimal formulation properties. The aims of the study included: (1) the characterization of the physicochemical properties of N. sativa oil; (2) formulation of N. sativa oil emulsions; (3) measurement of droplet size, pH and viscosity; (4) conducting in vitro bacteriological studies of the emulsions against clinical S. aureus strains including MSSA and MRSA; (5) comparing the activity of water-diluted and carbomer gel diluted formulations and (6) determining the MIC of the crude oil in broth dilution assays.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

The study was experimental in vitro formulation and bacteriological study. The major independent variables included N. sativa oil/formulation concentration and formulation vehicle (water or carbomer gel). Dependent variables included inhibition zone diameter, broth MIC, droplet size, pH and viscosity, among other physicochemical parameters of oil including its composition.

Study Sites and Sample Collection

Swabs from infected wounds were collected from trauma units of Connaught Hospital (Sierra Leone is a very significant referral hospital) and 34 Military Hospital. Sampling was done with the aid of sterile dry cotton swabs before cleaning the wounds. The swabs were placed in their respective containers and taken to the microbiology laboratory for analysis. For social data, there was the use of structured questionnaires. Bacterial Isolation and Identification

To obtain the specimens, they were placed on Mannitol Salt Agar and incubated at 37°C for 24 hours. The presumptive S. aureus samples were analyzed based on Gram staining and catalase tests. The identification was confirmed using the sliding and tube coagulase tests based on the appropriate bacteriological methods.9 The identification of methicillin was carried out through the cefoxitin disk diffusion process as according to CLSI guidelines for determination of sensitivity.10–12 The revised dataset for strains was classified using the results obtained from cefoxitin testing as follows: strains which fall within the resistant levels are referred to as MRSA, while the rest are considered as MSSA. It is important to note that as mecA/mecC testing was not available in the data provided to us, the classification is noted in the revised dataset as being phenotypic.

Carbomer Gel Preparation

Carbomer 940 gels were made with the application of TEA in distilled water. The formulation marked as S1 contained an equal concentration of 1.25% w/w of TEA and carbomer and was chosen for further work as the one with the highest viscosity while remaining stable. The reason for it being particularly suitable for topical gel is that it produces high viscosity of the gel and can be neutralized.7

Droplet-Size, Viscosity and pH Measurements

In the original research paper, geometric mean droplet sizes were obtained by microscopic analysis, while viscosity and pH of carbomer formulations were also measured. In the rewritten paper, however, these measurements were not classified as stability studies since the presented records do not confirm the duration of storage of formulations, frequency of observation, or repeat measurements throughout the observation period.

Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing

As for the antimicrobial susceptibility test, standardized bacterial suspensions made from fresh cultures were used to inoculate Mueller–Hinton agar for agar well diffusion. The study materials state that the 0.5 McFarland standard was used. Test formulations were applied to wells, and the inhibition zones were measured in millimeters after the incubation period. The original versions did not report the number of technical replicates for the tests; therefore, no repeat measurements were inserted in the new version. It follows that the figures are reported as isolated observations, while statistical analysis was expressed as exploratory. Cefoxitin disk results were kept as the phenotypic reference for classification of methicillin resistance. The provided MIC table based on agar dilution was removed from the rewritten manuscript because it contains only the presence and absence symbols without sufficient quantitative concentration series and definition of the endpoint. Thus, keeping the numerical MIC based on agar dilution equal to 20 mg/mL would suggest a level of methodological certainty that is not supported by the records of the experiment. Meanwhile, the information from broth-dilution MIC observations is retained and reported.

Data Analysis

Descriptive statistics are displayed in the form of mean ± standard deviation only for the numerical measurements where observations were made on multiple isolates. An exploratory paired t test was used to analyze inhibition-zone measurements of cefoxitin and N. sativa in the above mentioned thirteen isolates. Since the source dataset does not include any technical replicate experiments, the results were not depicted in respect of replicate precision. Friedman tests were conducted to measure the effect of concentration in the changes seen in inhibition zones in seven isolates. Statistical significance of P <0.05 was employed in the analyses, however caution needs to be exercised in interpreting the results because of small sample sizes and clinical isolates being analyzed instead of true technical replicates. Analyses were conducted through Python/scipy for verification of results.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical permission for the study was granted by the Sierra Leone Ethics and Scientific Review Committee (SLESRC). The study took place between the dates mentioned in the ethical approval letter. Participants provided informed consent. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki.13

Since the original records lack a complete quantitative agar dilution series, there is no reason for retaining previously reported 20 mg/mL MIC in agar as a definitive one. Differences in broth and agar dilution can arise from factors like dispersing hydrophobic oil in the test medium, contact of oil and bacteria, composition of the medium, and endpoint reading but the available records do not allow attributing it to one basis. Discussion

This research determined if N. sativa oil could be included in an emulsion-based topical preparation that should demonstrate measurable antibacterial action toward SVS bacteria. The principal finding of the study shows that crude N. sativa oil was evidenced to provide measurable action against most of the 13 isolates used in the numerical dataset; the results of the formulation experiment demonstrate that the greater the dilution of oil, the lesser the antibacterial action. Thus, we conclude that the results are significant for screening the preparation, but can be interpreted without implications referring to the data that point at the efficient use of the hand sanitizer in a clinical practice. The key experiments were presented in Tables 10–15 and Figures 1–3. The decision to estimate S. aureus was justified by the importance of this organism for infection in wounds and by its relation to the problem of antibiotic resistance. Local studies in Sierra Leone demonstrated that there is a high level of methicillin resistance showing the importance of MSSA and MRSA included in the paper.4,5,14 This particular study has deliberately focused on S. aureus rather than broad spectrum organism panel thus making generalization more limited.Figure 3Geometric mean droplet diameters recorded for preliminary soybean-oil and N. sativa-oil emulsions.A box plot showing droplet-size distribution for soybean pre-emulsions and Nigella pre-emulsions.

Geometric mean droplet diameters recorded for preliminary soybean-oil and N. sativa-oil emulsions.

The N. sativa has potential to possess antibacterial effects due to the presence of certain bioactive compounds in both its fixed and volatile oils, such as thymoquinone.6 Confirmation of the relationship between the activity in question and the compound cannot be claimed because the study did not measure any thymoquinone or conduct fractional analysis. The data pertaining to physicochemical properties give different aspect of formulation perspective. The experimental emulsions of N. sativa showed submicron droplet sizes in the first experiments, and the carbomer gel tested had very high viscosity and the near neutral pH value. Carbomer is commonly used in topical preparations since it is capable of producing good viscosity even at low concentrations.7 The pH value above 7.6 cannot be claimed to be equivalent to the skin surface pH because it is already known that pH of human skin is generally slightly acidic.8 The pH may be acceptable for preliminary formulations screenings but not necessarily for skin compatibility tests. The relevant data can be found in Tables 7–9 and illustrated for droplet size in Figure 3.

The presence of higher mean inhibition zones due to increasing emulsion concentrations is in agreement with concentration-dependent antibacterial characteristics. The gel-diluted formulation produced significant inhibition at 5% w/w when tested against the seven isolates in comparison to 10% w/w for the water-diluted formulation. This may imply formulation properties affecting dispersity, viscosity, retention in agar wells or oil droplet-bacteria cell interaction. Since the study has not tested the vehicle-only ethanol control or matched formulation-control, it is not possible to distinguish the contribution of ethanol, TEA, oleic acid and other excipients. The summary tables and statistics reflecting isolation-specific data are presented in Tables 13–15 and Figure 2. Comparing with cefoxitin, the findings indicate that the mean zone for N. sativa oil is bigger; however, the statistical test indicates that the difference is insignificant. It is important to point out that cefoxitin and crude oil belong to different test systems and cannot be treated as equal therapeutic comparators. Thus, the modified interpretation recognizes cefoxitin mainly as a phenotypic reference rather than a standard for the evaluation of how good a sanitizer is.

Despite the results being revealing, there are important limitations. All of the data presented do not include technical replicates, therefore, the statistical analysis is based on available observations at the isolate level instead of relying on the fabricated triplicate data. Moreover, the current study also did not include molecular confirmation of MRSA, formal time-based stability tests, organoleptic testing, skin irritation tests or broad-spectrum antimicrobial studies. Thus, due to the limitations mentioned above, the product should be regarded as in vitro product, rather than validated hand sanitizer.

Conclusion

The analysis shows that N. sativa oil and its emulsion formulations provided measurable in vitro antibacterial efficacy on clinical S. aureus isolates, including some phenotypically MRSA isolates. The carbomer-gel formulation was characterized by the measurable inhibition at lesser concentration, than the water dilution formulation. Thus, despite the findings made, no claims can be made about the effectiveness of the findings and there should be further research involving standardized replicate-based antimicrobial testing, molecular MRSA confirmation, matched vehicle controls, organoleptic testing and testing of the product degree of safety.
