Epidemiological characteristics of brucellosis in non-occupational population in Zhejiang Province, China, 2018–2024: epidemic trend and infection analysis
Authors: Xuguang Shi, Yong Yang, Ying Liu, Jiangping Ren, Rong Zhang, Song Guo, Huimin Ma, Jimin Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo clarify the epidemiological characteristics of brucellosis in non-occupational populations in Zhejiang Province during 2018–2024, provide evidence for formulating targeted prevention and control strategies, and respond to the core requirement of “human-animal-environment” collaborative prevention and control under the One Health framework.MethodsData on brucellosis cases among non‑occupational populations in Zhejiang Province were obtained from the Chinese Disease Prevention and Control Information System. Individual case investigations were also carried out for the corresponding cases. After standardized collation and validation, descriptive epidemiological analyses were performed with R 4.3.1 software.ResultsA total of 499 non-occupational brucellosis cases were reported in Zhejiang Province during 2018–2024, accounting for 43.96% (499/1137) of the total brucellosis cases in the province over the same period. The proportion of non-occupational cases showed a significant year-on-year upward trend (Trend χ² test = 15.53, P < 0.001). Among the 499 cases, 317 were male and 182 were female, with a male-to-female ratio of 1.74:1. The age of patients ranged from 1 to 91 years, with a median age of 54 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 43–64 years); the median age of males was 53 years (IQR: 42–63 year) and females 55 years (IQR: 43.75–66 years). The main transmission routes were direct contact (55.31%, 276/499) and digestive tract transmission (17.43%, 87/499). The predominant clinical manifestations included fever, excessive sweating, myalgia/arthralgia and fatigue. The median interval from onset to confirmed diagnosis was 19 days (IQR: 10–43 days), indicating a certain degree of diagnostic delay.ConclusionThe proportion of non-occupational brucellosis cases in Zhejiang Province has increased year by year, making this population a key group that cannot be ignored in local brucellosis prevention and control. To effectively curb this trend, efforts should be made to further strengthen the popularization of brucellosis prevention and control knowledge among the general public and prioritize specialized training on brucellosis diagnostic capabilities for medical workers. The One Health framework provides a critical theoretical and practical basis for cross-sectoral interventions integrating human health, animal epidemic prevention, and environmental governance.Keywords: Brucellosis, Non-occupational population, Epidemiological characteristics, One Health, Prevention and control, China

Introduction

Brucellosis, also known as Mediterranean fever, or Malta fever, is a typical zoonotic infectious disease caused by Brucella species. The Brucella species currently includes 6 species and 19 biovars, among which four are pathogenic to humans(B. abortus, B. melitensis, B. suis and B. canis). It is prevalent worldwide and has become one of the important public health issues seriously threatening public health security and population health [1]. From the perspective of host range, this disease mainly infects common livestock such as cattle, pigs, sheep, and dogs. These infected animals are not only the main reservoir hosts of the disease but also the key sources for transmitting pathogens to humans [2]. Human infection occurs through diverse pathways, including direct contact with infected animals, indirect contact with pathogen-contaminated environments, and ingestion of unpasteurized milk and dairy products, raw/undercooked livestock meat, and sheep placenta. The diversity of transmission pathways significantly increases the risk of human infection [3].

Brucellosis causes direct damage to animal health, substantial economic losses to animal husbandry, and severe threats to human well-being: patients present with prolonged fever, joint pain, fatigue, and hepatosplenomegaly, and some develop chronic infections or lifelong disabilities [4, 5].

Although the hazards of brucellosis have been widely recognized, as a long-neglected zoonotic disease, its prevention and control still face severe challenges in many countries and regions around the world [6, 7].

In recent years, China’s brucellosis epidemic has shown a continuous upward trend with altered epidemiological characteristics: the traditional “northern high-incidence” pattern has evolved into a “north strong and south increasing” diffusion trend, with rising incidence in southern provinces including Hunan, Hubei, and Guangdong [8–10]. Notably, transmission has expanded from traditional pastoral areas to agricultural areas, semi-agricultural/semi-pastoral areas, and urban centers, and the infected population has expanded from occupational groups (herdsmen, veterinarians, slaughterhouse workers) to non-occupational populations, expanding the scope and difficulty of prevention and control [11].

Non-occupational populations face high infection risks due to limited brucellosis awareness and protective measures, such as contact with livestock, consumption of unquarantined livestock products. However, most existing studies focus on occupational populations, with systematic research on non-occupational brucellosis remaining scarce (mostly sporadic case reports or small-sample surveys) [12]. The epidemic characteristics, risk factors, and transmission pathways of brucellosis in non-occupational populations remain incompletely understood. This limits the development of targeted prevention and control strategies and cannot meet the needs of comprehensive brucellosis control under the current diversified epidemic situation. Therefore, systematic research on the infection status of non-occupational populations is urgently needed to fill existing research gaps and address key issues in brucellosis prevention and control.

As an economic core and major importer of livestock products in the Yangtze River Delta, Zhejiang Province has active cross-regional livestock trade, frequent population mobility, and diverse dietary habits among residents. Therefore, studying the infection status and risk characteristics of non-occupational populations in Zhejiang Province is of great public health significance. One Health is a systematic concept that emphasizes the interdependency among human, animal and environmental health, and calls for interdisciplinary and cross-sectoral collaboration to tackle public health challenges. As a typical zoonotic disease, brucellosis establishes a complete transmission cycle involving animal reservoirs, human exposure and environmental pollution, which fully embodies the practical value of the One Health philosophy. Thus, only through a holistic ‘human–animal–environment’ chain-oriented strategy with multi-dimensional coordinated prevention and control can the transmission risks be effectively contained. Based on this, this study collected brucellosis case data of non-occupational populations in Zhejiang Province from 2018 to 2024 to systematically analyze their infection characteristics, exposure patterns, and epidemic rules. The aim is to address the weaknesses in the research field of brucellosis in non-occupational populations, provide a scientific basis for formulating precise and effective prevention and control strategies and intervention measures for this group, and discuss comprehensive brucellosis prevention and control strategies in combination with the One Health concept, thereby providing references for coping with the complex brucellosis epidemic in China.

Materials and methods

Data source and definition

This retrospective study extracted data on human brucellosis cases reported in Zhejiang Province from 2018 to 2024 from the China Information System for Disease Control and Prevention (CISDCP)—a national mandatory surveillance system with standardized case reporting protocols, regular quality control (including duplicate case checks, missing data supplementation, and field verification), and provincial/municipal/county-level review to ensure reporting completeness and data reliability. Case information was obtained from standardized individual questionnaire surveys for brucellosis, covering basic demographic characteristics, exposure history, clinical symptoms, diagnostic procedures, and laboratory test results.

Definition of non-occupational population: Individuals who do not engage in occupational brucellosis exposure-risk activities, including livestock (mainly cattle and sheep) breeding, slaughtering, trading, milking, and livestock product processing. This definition excludes individuals whose primary occupation involves livestock-related work; farmers included in the study engage in crop cultivation only (no regular livestock breeding/slaughtering).

Research indicators and analysis methods

Descriptive epidemiological analysis was used to characterize the incidence overview, demographic/regional/temporal distribution, infection sources, transmission pathways, clinical symptoms, and diagnostic status of confirmed non-occupational brucellosis patients. Key indicators included case counts, constituent ratios, median age, median onset-to-diagnosis interval, and misdiagnosis rate.

Diagnostic criteria

Case diagnosis followed the Diagnostic Criteria for Brucellosis (WS269-2007, updated to WS269-2019 in 2019). No significant changes were made to the core diagnostic criteria between the two versions: both define serologically positive cases as a serum agglutination test (SAT) titer of 1:100 + + or higher, or a titer of 1:50 + + or higher for more than 1 year. Confirmed cases include serologically positive cases with typical clinical symptoms and epidemiological exposure history, and etiologically confirmed cases with Brucella isolated by culture (Individuals infected with B. melitensis and B. abortus).

Statistical analysis

All case data were double-entered and logically verified using the Epidata 3.1 database to ensure data quality. Statistical analyses were performed with Microsoft Excel 2023 and R 4.3.1 (packages: epiDisplay and stats).

The normality of continuous data was determined using the Kolmogorov‑Smirnov test. Data were normally distributed if P > 0.05; otherwise, data were non-normally distributed. Data conforming to a normal distribution with homogeneous variance were expressed as “mean ± standard deviation (x ± s)”; data not conforming to a normal distribution were expressed as “median (interquartile range) [M (P25, P75)]”. Trend analyses were conducted using the trend chi-square test. P < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

Overall overview

A total of 1137 brucellosis cases were reported in Zhejiang Province from 2018 to 2024, with 107, 108, 123, 178, 200, 195, and 226 cases reported annually. There were 499 non-occupational brucellosis patients, accounting for 43.88%. The number of reported non-occupational brucellosis cases per year was 30, 44, 49, 72, 89, 113, and 102 respectively, accounting for 28.04%, 40.74%, 39.84%, 40.45%, 44.5%, 57.95%, and 45.13% of the total brucellosis cases, showing an overall increasing trend (trend χ² test = 15.53, P < 0.001).

Epidemiological characteristics

Population characteristics

Among the 499 non-occupational brucellosis patients, 317 were males (63.53%) and 182 were females (36.47%), with a male-to-female ratio of 1.74:1. A total of 394 cases were reported among the middle-aged and elderly population aged 40 years and above, accounting for 78.96% of all cases (Table 1). The age distributions of the overall population, males, and females all violated normality assumptions, as indicated by K-S tests (all p < 0.05).The age ranged from 1 to 91 years, with a median age of 54 years (IQR: 43–64, range: 1–91). The median age of males was 53 years (IQR: 42–63, range: 1–91), and that of females was 55 years (IQR: 43.75-66, range: 1–82). The main occupations were farmers (141, 28.26%), followed by workers (87, 17.44%) and commercial service personnel (64, 12.83%) (Table 2).

Regional distribution

Non-occupational brucellosis cases were reported in all prefecture-level cities except Zhoushan. Jiaxing reported the highest number of cases (105), followed by Hangzhou (70), Huzhou (67), Wenzhou (56), Ningbo (49), Shaoxing (47), Jinhua (41), Lishui (27), Quzhou (20), and Taizhou (17).

From 2018 to 2024, 81 counties (cities, districts) reported non-occupational cases, with the number of affected counties showing a fluctuating upward trend (22 in 2018, 27 in 2019, 26 in 2020, 42 in 2021, 37 in 2022, 41 in 2023, 44 in 2024). Tongxiang reported the highest number of cases (78), accounting for 15.63% of the total incidence in the province, followed by Nanxun District (56 cases), Yuhang District (15 cases), Dongyang City (14 cases), Fenghua District, Linping District, Rui’an City, and Shangyu District (12 cases each) (Fig. 1).

Non-occupational cases were reported every month of the year, with a clear spring-summer peak (Mar–Aug). June had the highest number of reported cases (70), accounting for 14.03% of all cases, followed by May (69 cases) and April (66 cases). Clinical characteristics and diagnostic status

The main clinical symptoms were fever, hyperhidrosis, myalgia/arthralgia and fatigue, accounting for 88.18% (440/499), 59.52% (297/499), 73.55% (367/499), and 69.94% (349/499), respectively. Patients with hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, and lymphadenopathy accounted for 4.01% (20/499), 8.62% (43/499), and 5.21% (26/499), respectively. The median interval from onset to confirmed diagnosis was 19 days, with the longest diagnosis time of 394 days and the shortest of 1 day (IQR: 10–43 days, K-S test P < 0.05). A total of 81 cases were misdiagnosed as other diseases, accounting for 16.23%. The misdiagnosed diseases mainly included upper respiratory tract infection, osteoarthrosis, hematological diseases, digestive system diseases, and fever of unknown origin.

Diagnostic institutions were mainly county-level hospitals (199, 39.88%,), followed by municipal hospitals (162, 32.46%), provincial hospitals (101, 20.24%), other hospitals (20, 4.01%), and CDCs (17, 3.41%).

Discussion

During 2018–2024, non-occupational cases of brucellosis in Zhejiang Province accounted for 43.96% of total cases, which was higher than that in Fujian Province (38.2%) [13] and Jinan City (20.21%) [14], with a marked year-on-year increase. This trend may be attributed to the following factors: (1) Zhejiang Province serves as an important hub for livestock and poultry trading and slaughtering. Its processed products supply not only the province itself but also surrounding areas including Shanghai, with a large trade volume, diverse sources, and insufficient supervision in trading links. (2) Dietary preferences also vary across regions. Residents in Tongxiang, Nanxun, and other areas favor fresh and raw mutton as well as lamb. During food preparation, meat may not be thoroughly cooked. In addition, consumers exhibit low awareness of protective measures and lack appropriate precautions when handling raw meat or visiting slaughter facilities. (3) On-site slaughtering facilities are rudimentary, and the harmless disposal of sewage and waste is insufficient.(4) Accelerated urban-rural integration has increased contact opportunities between non-occupational populations and livestock, raw meat products, and contaminated environments, thereby elevating the exposure risk.(5) The surveillance system for brucellosis has been further improved, and the diagnostic capacity of medical institutions has been continuously enhanced, reducing missed diagnoses and misdiagnoses of non-occupational cases, thereby leading to an increase in the proportion of such cases. Collectively, these factors have significantly elevated both the transmission risk and detection rate of brucellosis among non-occupational populations, making this disease a key concern for brucellosis prevention and control in Zhejiang Province.

The male-to-female ratio of non-occupational brucellosis patients was 1.74:1, which was significantly lower than the gender ratio of the total brucellosis population in the province (2.81:1) [15]. Non-occupational exposure risks are more evenly distributed by gender, reflecting the universal susceptibility of the general population to brucellosis.

All populations are susceptible to brucella. The median age of 54 years (53 years for males, 55 years for females) indicates that middle-aged and elderly populations are the main non-occupational affected groups. This phenomenon is multifactorial, stemming from the interplay of exposure patterns, preventive practices and age-related immune senescence. Compared with younger cohorts, middle-aged and older adults exhibit greater involvement in household purchase of live animals, and meat and dairy processing, thereby elevating the probability of direct contact with infected animals and their excreta. Compounded by insufficient awareness of brucellosis prophylaxis and inadequate utilization of personal protective equipment, their vulnerability is further exacerbated. Finally, impaired immune function renders this population more susceptible to Brucella infection under conditions of equivalent exposure.

The spring-summer incidence peak is a distinctive seasonal characteristic of non-occupational brucellosis in Zhejiang, which is closely related to the winter mutton consumption peak (a key exposure period) and the 2–6 week incubation period of brucellosis. Winter is the peak season for lamb consumption in Zhejiang, with increased non-occupational exposure to raw meat, slaughtering environments, and contaminated livestock products; the spring-summer peak is a direct reflection of this time-lagged exposure.

Brucella can infect humans through multiple transmission routes, including direct contact with skin and mucous membranes, ingestion, and inhalation. Outbreaks of brucellosis among non-occupational populations have been reported in several regions of China. For example, outbreaks caused by the consumption of unpasteurized goat milk were documented in Guangdong Province (2015) and Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region (2016) [16, 17]. However, systematic studies on the infection routes among this population at the whole regional level remain limited, and relevant literature is scarce. In this study, the main transmission pathway of non-occupational populations in Zhejiang Province was direct contact with infected animals or products (55.31%). Non-occupational populations have weak protective awareness and often contact infected livestock or their products without corresponding protection, resulting in infection. Digestive tract transmission (17.43%) was the second most common route, among which consumption of undercooked beef and mutton predominated. This was mainly attributed to consumers’preference for tender meat texture and insufficient heating of beef and mutton in cooking methods such as hot pot. Cases attributed to raw milk consumption accounted for only 2.61%This lower proportion may be related to the local residents’ low acceptance of raw milk, differing from the relatively more acceptable consumption of raw milk in some regions of southern China [17]. Respiratory transmission among non‑occupational populations (12.02%) warrants close attention. Although aerosol transmission has traditionally been regarded as mainly associated with occupationally exposed populations such as slaughterhouse workers [3], this study demonstrates that non-occupational populations are also at risk of infection, which most likely occurs via inhalation of aerosolized bacteria in these contaminated environments.

Human‑to‑human transmission was rare in this study, with only one case of mother‑to‑child transmission and one suspected case of sexual transmission reported, consistent with other report [18]. Overall, no targeted public health interventions are required for human‑to‑human transmission, but clinicians should still pay attention to the identification and monitoring of vertical transmission. 14.83% of cases had unknown transmission routes. Due to the relatively long incubation period, concealed and scattered non‑occupational exposures, and inaccurate recall of exposure histories by patients, the transmission route could not be identified in some brucellosis cases. Such individuals often failed to provide relevant epidemiological information, which imposed more stringent requirements on disease diagnosis.

The main clinical manifestations of these patients included fever, excessive sweating, myalgia, arthralgia and fatigue, which showed no significant difference from those presented in all cases [19]. The median onset-to-diagnosis interval of 19 days (longest 394 days) and 16.23% misdiagnosis rate reflect significant diagnostic delays in non-occupational brucellosis cases in Zhejiang. The main causes are: (1) diversity and nonspecificity of clinical symptoms: fever, joint pain, and fatigue are easily confused with upper respiratory tract infection, osteoarthrosis, and other common diseases; (2) lack of epidemiological history collection: grassroots medical staff rarely inquire about livestock product contact/consumption history in non-occupational patients; (3) low brucellosis awareness: non-occupational patients do not actively report exposure history, and medical staff in southern China (with historically low brucellosis incidence) lack diagnostic vigilance.

The continuous increase in non-occupational brucellosis cases in Zhejiang has three key public health implications: (1) expanded epidemic scope: brucellosis has broken through occupational boundaries and become a public health problem affecting the general population; (2) increased prevention and control difficulty: non-occupational exposure routes are diverse and scattered (no concentrated occupational settings), making targeted intervention challenging; (3) hidden economic and social costs: diagnostic delays and chronic infections lead to increased medical expenses and reduced quality of life for patients, and the spread of brucellosis in urban areas may cause public anxiety.

In this context, the “One Health” concept provides important theoretical guidance and a practical framework for brucellosis prevention and control. The “One Health” concept emphasizes that animal health, human health, and ecological environment health are inseparable, and it is necessary to formulate prevention and control strategies from an overall perspective [20]. Based on the study results, we propose targeted One Health interventions for Zhejiang Province:

Animal health sector (source control): (1) Strengthen cross-provincial livestock quarantine and traceability, strictly inspecting sheep imported into Zhejiang; (2) Promote regular serological testing in key trade/slaughtering hubs (e.g., Tongxiang, Nanxun); (3) Standardize livestock slaughtering practices, improve slaughterhouse environmental sanitation, and implement harmless treatment of sewage and waste.

Human health sector (case detection and intervention): (1) Strengthen brucellosis diagnostic training for grassroots medical staff (especially in southern China), emphasizing the collection of epidemiological exposure history for patients; (2) Establish a rapid diagnosis channel for brucellosis in municipal/county-level hospitals, reducing diagnostic delay; (3) Conduct targeted health education for key non-occupational groups (middle-aged and elderly farmers, commercial service personnel, catering practitioners), focusing on protective measures for raw meat contact and proper cooking of livestock products.

Environmental health sector (transmission chain interruption): (1) Strengthen environmental disinfection of livestock trade markets, slaughterhouses, and farmer’s markets; (2) Regulate the on-site slaughter of livestock in urban and rural areas, ban unlicensed slaughtering; (3) Improve the environmental governance of livestock breeding and slaughtering areas to prevent pathogen contamination of soil, water sources, and air.

Cross-sector collaboration: Establish a joint brucellosis prevention and control mechanism between health, agriculture, market supervision, and environmental protection departments in Zhejiang Province, with regular information sharing and joint law enforcement to address livestock trade, slaughtering, and food safety issues.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. First, this was a retrospective study using routine surveillance data, and incomplete individual exposure histories may introduce information bias. Second, the study relied only on human surveillance data, lacking animal health data (e.g., livestock infection prevalence, vaccination coverage) and molecular epidemiological evidence (e.g., Brucella species and genotype identification). This limits infection source tracing and in-depth analysis of the human–animal transmission chain. Third, potential misclassification of occupational status cannot be excluded. Although non-occupational cases were defined as those without professional livestock work, some individuals may have underreported informal exposures such as household animal breeding. These hidden, infrequent contacts are easily overlooked, leading to misclassification. Future studies with more detailed questionnaires and on-site verification will help reduce such bias. Fourth, the study was limited to non-occupational human brucellosis cases in Zhejiang Province. Due to regional differences in dietary habits, livestock breeding, and trading patterns across southern China, the generalizability of our findings may be restricted. Further multi-center and large-sample studies are needed for external validation.
