Optimal timing of mechanical circulatory support in rapidly progressive fulminant myocarditis: a case report
Authors: Kentaro Matsushita, Tomofumi Nakatsukasa, Takaaki Sato, Atsushi Hirohata, Josip Andelo Borovac, Niklas Schenker, Jose Montero Cabezas, Deepti Ranganathan
Published: 2026

Introduction

Fulminant myocarditis (FM) is a life-threatening condition characterized by rapid progression to cardiogenic shock, malignant arrhythmias, and multiorgan failure. Refractory cardiogenic shock can cause unpredictable circulatory collapse. This situation makes it unclear when to optimally initiate mechanical circulatory support (MCS), despite its widespread use in these patients. Clear clinical criteria for determining the optimal timing of MCS initiation have not been established. The rapid deterioration of FM makes decision-making for MCS initiation challenging, as delayed intervention can lead to fatal outcomes. We report a case of FM with an exceptionally rapid clinical course, in which MCS was initiated in the cardiac catheterization laboratory prior to cardiac arrest, highlighting the challenges of determining the optimal timing of MCS initiation in patients at high risk of imminent deterioration.

Case presentation

A 57-year-old Asian man with no prior history of cardiovascular disease presented to our emergency department with chest pain following flu-like symptoms 5 days earlier. The medical history includes essential hypertension. On admission, his blood pressure was 79/47 mmHg, heart rate 79 beats/min, and oxygen saturation 98% on room air. Electrocardiography showed an escape rhythm with ST-segment elevation in leads I, aVL, and V2, and ST-segment depression in leads II, III, aVF, V1, and V3–6 (Figure 1A). Laboratory findings revealed markedly elevated cardiac biomarkers (troponin I 258 447 pg/mL, creatine kinase (CK) 1827 U/L, CK-MB 270 μg/L), a brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) level of 1256 pg/mL, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) of 2567 U/L and creatinine of 2.44 mg/dL. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated global hypokinesia with a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of approximately 40% (Figure 1B, C, Supplementary material online, Video S1). On contrast-enhanced computed tomography, which was performed to exclude aortic dissection, a region of poor enhancement was observed in the lateral wall area (Figure 1D). Haemodynamics were temporarily stabilized with norepinephrine at 0.06 μg/kg/min. Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) was suspected, and emergent coronary angiography (CAG) was performed, which revealed no significant coronary stenosis. Based on history of flu-like symptoms, electrocardiographic abnormalities, and echocardiographic findings, acute myocarditis was strongly suspected. Endomyocardial biopsy (EMB) was planned to establish a definitive diagnosis and guide subsequent management. However, ventricular tachycardia (VT) occurred frequently, requiring two defibrillation shocks, after which the VT ceased. The mean blood pressure dropped to 55 mmHg, raising concerns about haemodynamic instability. Lactate level measured in the catheterization laboratory was 5 mmol/L. Because FM with further haemodynamic deterioration was strongly suspected, VA-ECMO was therefore initiated before the development of refractory shock or cardiac arrest. EMB was subsequently performed safely, with three samples obtained from the right ventricular septum. Right heart catheterization revealed a pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP) of 24 mmHg, cardiac index (CI) of 1.42 L/min/m2, cardiac power output of 0.4 W, and pulmonary artery pulsatility index of 0.44, indicating severe biventricular dysfunction. Restriction of aortic valve opening was also observed on echocardiography. Following endotracheal intubation, Impella CP was inserted for left ventricular unloading, and combined support with VA-ECMO and Impella CP (ECPELLA) was established. Despite initial haemodynamic stabilization, the patient subsequently developed complete atrioventricular block, followed by pulseless electrical activity and cardiac arrest. Immediate transcutaneous pacing was initiated, followed by temporary pacemaker placement. After admission to the intensive care unit, steroid pulse therapy with intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg/day was initiated before pathological confirmation and continued for 3 days, followed by prednisolone at 1 mg/kg/day, which was subsequently tapered and discontinued. Peak CK reached 3562 U/L, followed by gradual improvement in hepatic enzyme, serum creatinine level and lactate levels. However, despite inotropic support, left ventricular function remained severely impaired on day 7 (LVEF approximately 10%) (Figure 2A–D Supplementary material online, Video S2), and weaning from MCS was considered unlikely. Given the anticipated need for advanced therapies, including left ventricular assist device implantation or heart transplantation, a bridging strategy was planned. To facilitate neurological assessment and informed consent, VA-ECMO was weaned after upgrading to Impella 5.5, confirming stable intracardiac pressures during flow reduction. VA-ECMO was successfully discontinued on day 12. Subsequently, gradual recovery of cardiac function was observed, allowing removal of Impella 5.5 on day 43 and discontinuation of inotropic support on day 104. LVEF improved to approximately 30% (Figure 3A, B Supplementary material online, Video S3). The patient was eventually transferred for rehabilitation. Histopathological examination demonstrated lymphocytic infiltration, confirming the diagnosis of lymphocytic myocarditis (Figure 4). Due to the presence of a preceding infection, it was considered that the patient had FM, raising the possibility of viral myocarditis; however, the cause could not be identified.

Discussion

FM is a life-threatening condition characterized by rapid progression to cardiogenic shock, ventricular arrhythmias, and multiorgan failure.1 Although clinical deterioration is generally reported to occur over several days to weeks,2 predicting fulminant progression at the time of initial presentation remains challenging, often requiring clinicians to make critical decisions under substantial uncertainty. Furthermore, FM is associated with substantial mortality, particularly in patients requiring MCS.3 The patient progressed from ambulatory presentation to cardiac arrest within approximately 4 h, a rarely reported clinical course. The timing of MCS initiation is a key determinant of outcomes in FM.3 This case highlights that determining the optimal timing of MCS initiation is not simply a matter of responding to established refractory cardiogenic shock, but rather of deciding when to intervene by anticipating further haemodynamic deterioration while diagnostic evaluation is still ongoing. Before CAG in this case, although the patient was considered to be in SCAI stage B or C, the clinical findings alone were insufficient to justify immediate VA-ECMO initiation. Therefore, exclusion of ACS was prioritized before escalation to MCS.4 In this case, a decrease in LVEF, a significant rise in CK and CK-MB, and the emergence of sustained VT that was treated with defibrillation during the CAG indicated a high risk of acute exacerbation of FM.5,6 Furthermore, persistent hypotension despite norepinephrine support raised concern for progression to SCAI stage D shock. Therefore, VA-ECMO was considered appropriate.4 Although early EMB was necessary, it was considered that if haemodynamic instability or cardiac arrest occurred during the procedure, it could lead to an increased risk of procedural complications and a delay in the establishment of VA-ECMO, potentially resulting in poor prognosis.7 Proceeding to ICU without circulatory support could delay VA-ECMO if cardiac arrest occurred during ongoing evaluation. Delayed initiation after haemodynamic collapse or cardiac arrest has been associated with worse short- and long-term outcomes.8 This case suggests that determining the timing of MCS initiation in rapidly progressive FM may require integrating evolving evidence of hypoperfusion, electrical instability, and end-organ dysfunction to anticipate impending circulatory collapse during the diagnostic process. In the present case, additional left ventricular unloading with Impella CP was implemented based on haemodynamic assessment, resulting in an ECPELLA strategy that may have contributed to myocardial recovery and avoidance of durable mechanical support or heart transplantation.9,10 After upgrading to Impella 5.5, although transition to durable ventricular assist device support was initially considered because of persistent severe ventricular dysfunction, subsequent clinical evaluation suggested gradual myocardial recovery. These evolving findings supported continued temporary mechanical unloading as a bridge-to-recovery strategy while signs of myocardial recovery were reassessed before transition to durable MCS was considered.
