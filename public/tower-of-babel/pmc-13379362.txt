Introduction

Crisscross heart is an extremely rare congenital heart defect defined as the crossing of the long axis of the atrioventricular (AV) valve. It was first described by Lev and Rowlatt1 in 1961, and the term “crisscross heart” was initially coined by Anderson et al2 in 1972. According to the AV connection, crisscross heart can be categorized into 2 types: concordant and discordant. In the concordant type, the inflowing blood streams from the right atrium (RA) to the right ventricle (RV) and from the left atrium to the left ventricle (LV) intersect. In the discordant type, the inflowing blood streams from the RA to the LV and from the left atrium to the RV intersect.

A few studies have reported on radiofrequency ablation in crisscross heart. However, to our knowledge, no report has described atypical (fast-slow) AV nodal reentrant tachycardia in patients with discordant crisscross heart. We describe a rare case of successful radiofrequency ablation of atypical AV nodal reentrant tachycardia in a patient with discordant crisscross heart.

Case report

A 2-year-old girl was diagnosed with complex congenital heart disease during the fetal period. She was born at 38 weeks of gestation, weighing 3060 g. She had discordant crisscross heart, double-outlet RV, pulmonary atresia, and ventricular septal defect. At 9 months of age, she underwent a systemic-pulmonary shunt followed by a bilateral bidirectional Glenn procedure and atrial septal defect enlargement (Figure 1A–1C).

At 2 years of age, weighing 13 kg, the patient underwent catheter ablation because supraventricular tachycardia was induced in an electrophysiological study before Fontan surgery. Holter rhythm strips showed 2 distinct QRS morphologies, indicating the presence of twin AV nodes. Under general anesthesia, the quadripolar electrode catheters were placed at the left pulmonary artery via the left superior vena cava and at the RV via the aorta and the decapolar electrode catheters were placed at the anterior His bundle (7 o’clock position of the tricuspid annulus) and posterior His bundle (3 o’clock of the mitral annulus) via the femoral vein. The QRS morphology was M-shaped in lead II during sinus rhythm, suggesting conduction mainly via the anterior His bundle, whereas the QRS morphology was rS in lead II during atrial pacing, suggesting conduction mainly via the posterior His bundle (Figure 2). The QRS morphology was the same as that during atrial pacing and tachycardia.

However, during atrial pacing or tachycardia, the QRS complex conducts through the posterior His bundle (forming an rS wave in lead II). The effective refractory periods of the anterior and posterior AV pathways were similar, at 230 and 190 ms, respectively. Ventriculoatrial (VA) conduction exhibited decremental properties, and the anterior AV node was the earliest atrial site during ventricular pacing at 130 ppm. Ventricular overdrive pacing induced a long RP tachycardia (cycle length 272 ms; His-atrial interval 220 ms; atrial-His interval [AH] 52 ms) and the sequence of atrial waves during the tachycardia differed slightly from that observed during ventricular pacing (130 ppm) (Figure 3). The QRS morphology was rS in lead II, and the posterior His-V interval was 29 ms, suggesting the presence of a conducting posterior AV node. Neither antegrade nor retrograde conduction exhibited a distinct AH jump in the AH interval of ≥50 ms in response to a 10-ms decrement in the S1-S2 coupling interval during atrial or ventricular extrastimulation. His refractory premature ventricular contraction did not reset tachycardia, and ventricular overdrive pacing restored the initial V-A-V activation sequence, postpacing interval – tachycardia cycle length = 126 ms, and prolonged the VA interval (stimulus-atrial interval – VA interval = 101 ms). Consequently, on the basis of the above-mentioned electrophysiological findings and the AV and VA intervals, the supraventricular tachycardia was diagnosed as fast-slow AV nodal reentrant tachycardia involving the posterior AV node at the mitral annulus. The earliest atrial activation site was near the posterior AV node (Figure 4C).

Radiofrequency energy was delivered at the earliest atrial activation site during tachycardia using a nonirrigated catheter (NAVISTAR B-curve, Biosense Webster, Irvine, CA; 40 W, 55°C, and total duration 60 seconds) (Figure 4A and 4B). Tachycardia was terminated by VA block (Figure 4C), and the tachycardia could no longer be induced. The antegrade conduction through both AV nodes remain unaffected after ablation. The patient subsequently underwent Fontan surgery, and the postoperative course was uneventful.

Discussion

We report successful radiofrequency ablation of atypical (fast-slow) AV nodal reentrant tachycardia in a child with discordant crisscross heart. There have been only 7 case reports on catheter ablation in crisscross heart,3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 with the accessory pathway observed in 3 cases, tachycardia involving twin AV nodes in 1, and AV nodal reentrant tachycardia in 3. 2 of the 7 patients had discordant AV connections and underwent catheter ablation of the accessory pathway. Their His bundle electrograms were identified at the inferior right-sided mitral annulus in discordant crisscross heart and at the tricuspid annulus in concordant crisscross heart except 1 case with twin AV nodes (one superior and anterior and second posterior and inferior of the common AV annulus). There was no description of the His bundle recording site in 1 case.

In the present case, His bundle electrograms were recorded at the 7 o’clock position of the tricuspid annulus and the posterior His bundle at the 3 o’clock position of the mitral annulus (Figure 4B and 4D). A compact AV node should be present in the morphological RA. If concordant, the His bundle should be sought at the junction of the RA and RV, whereas if discordant, it should be sought at the junction of the RA and LV. Furthermore, twin AV nodes exist regardless of the AV connection, necessitating confirmation via prior Holter monitoring or atrial pacing from multiple sites to check for QRS waveform changes. The AV node location can be identified from the earliest site of atrial excitation during ventricular pacing. The location of the coronary sinus ostium can be beneficial in locating the His potential.

Notably, the dual AV pathways associated with crisscross heart have not yet been fully elucidated. Previous reports indicated that slow-slow AV nodal reentrant tachycardia in situs solitus, concordant crisscross heart was observed in 2 cases whereas fast-slow AV nodal reentrant tachycardia in situs inversus, discordant crisscross heart was observed in 1 case.3,6,8 The slow-slow AV nodal reentrant tachycardia cases were ablated on the earliest retrograde atrial electrogram, which was recorded in the proximal coronary sinus and at the fragmented atrial potentials recorded near the coronary sinus ostium during tachycardia.3 Meanwhile, the fast-slow AV nodal reentrant tachycardia case did not exhibit coronary sinus and was ablated at the low atrioseptal region of the tricuspid annulus. In the present case, the earliest retrograde atrial electrogram during tachycardia was recorded at 3 o’clock in the mitral annulus, near the His bundle electrogram recording site (Figure 4). To date, there have been no reports on fast-slow AV nodal reentrant tachycardia in crisscross heart. However, the location of the slow pathway remains unknown, making anatomical ablation difficult; this remains a challenge for the future.

Conclusion

We successfully performed ablation of fast-slow AV nodal reentrant tachycardia in a 2-year-old girl with discordant crisscross heart and twin AV nodes. Recognition of AV node location is crucial for the diagnosis and treatment of arrhythmias. In cases that are highly likely to involve an AV block, such as AV nodal reentrant tachycardia, accumulating more cases is necessary to ensure safe ablation.
