Synergistic thermosonication combined with germinants and licorice extract improves microbial inactivation, physicochemical quality, and volatile profile stability of ready-to-eat chicken breasts
Authors: Jinxue Hou, Kairan Sheng, Weixuan Guo, Yang Le, Jingxin Sun, Ming Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Ready-to-eat (RTE) chicken breast is widely favored for its convenience, high-quality protein, low-fat content, and compatibility with modern dietary trends [1], [2], [3]. Industrial processing typically involves high-temperature sterilization and vacuum packaging to ensure safety without the need for secondary cooking [4]. However, its high moisture and protein content increase the risk of microbial contamination [5], [6], particularly from heat-resistant Bacillus and Clostridium spp., whose spores can survive under harsh conditions [7], [8], [9], [10] and subsequently germinate under favorable post-processing or refrigerated storage conditions, leading to spoilage and toxin accumulation [11], [12], [13], [14], [15]. Therefore, developing effective strategies to control spore survival and germination is critical for improving the microbial stability and safety of RTE poultry products [5], [7], [16], [17].

Pasteurization and high-temperature sterilization are widely used to inactivate large numbers of microorganisms, including Bacillus and Clostridium, to extend the shelf life of foodstuffs [18]. Nevertheless, these treatments often reduce sensory and nutritional qualities [4], [19]. Thermosonication, an environmentally friendly, non-chemical meat preservation and decontamination technology, has garnered increasing attention for its ability to lower bacterial counts while maintaining food quality [13], [20]. The synergistic interaction of cavitation and thermal effects enhances convective heat transfer and improves heat transfer efficiency, thereby inactivating microorganisms through disruption of their cell membranes [21], [22].

However, thermosonication alone generally exhibits limited efficacy against highly resistant spores, particularly within complex meat matrices [23]. To overcome this limitation, the “germination–inactivation” strategy has emerged as an effective method for spore control [24], [25], [26]. This strategy employs germination, such as L-alanine, to activate spore germination receptors and trigger irreversible germination [27], [28]. During this process, spores undergo rehydration and coat degradation, transforming from a heat-resistant dormant state to a highly stress-sensitive vegetative state [16], [17], [29], [30]. Consequently, their intrinsic resistance is markedly reduced, enabling efficient sterilization under relatively mild conditions [10], [27], [31]. Despite the promise of the “germination–inactivation” strategy, its practical effectiveness is often limited by population heterogeneity and the presence of hyperdormant spores that fail to germinate under conventional induction conditions [30], [32], [33]. These residual spores may survive subsequent processing, thereby posing a potential risk to the long-term storage safety of RTE chicken breast [33], [34], [35]. Therefore, more effective strategies are required to overcome these limitations and ensure complete spore control during processing and storage. In order to address these limitations, we have proposed a novel “germination–inactivation–inhibition” strategy [23], [36]. In this system, germinants may reduce spore resistance by promoting germination, while thermosonication may subsequently induce spore surface swelling and damage to or perforation of the inner membrane. At the same time, bioactive compounds such as glycyrrhizic acid and flavonoids in licorice extract may interact with membrane structures and, as suggested by previous studies [26], [36], may contribute to increased structural damage and nucleic acid leakage during thermosonication, which may subsequently support the inhibition of the growth and reproduction of the surviving spores.

The present study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of the “germination–inactivation–inhibition” strategy in controlling spore contamination in RTE chicken breasts and its impact on key quality attributes, including color, pH, texture, moisture distribution, lipid oxidation, total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N) levels, and volatile profile. We hypothesized that this strategy would provide more effective spore control than conventional treatments while maintaining product quality.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Ready-to-eat chicken breasts were obtained from Nanjing Huangjiaoshou Food Science and Technology Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). The L-alanine (‘A’), D-glucose (G), D-fructose (F), pyridine-2,6-dicarboxylic acid (DPA) and licorice extract were provided by Maclean Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The microbiological media used include reinforced clostridium medium (RCM), reinforced clostridium agar (RCA), and plate count agar, all of which were obtained from Hope Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Qingdao, China). Components for specialized media preparation, including tryptone, agar, yeast extract, sodium thioglycolate, disodium hydrogen phosphate (Na2HPO4) and d-raffinose pentahydrate were purchased from Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Additionally, Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) supplied the boric acid and pH indicators (methyl red and bromocresol green). Other essential analytical reagents, such as 2-thiobarbituric acid (TBA), trichloroacetic acid (TCA), sodium chloride (NaCl), terbium chloride hexahydrate (TbCl3·6H2O) and various inorganic acids (hydrochloric acid, sulfuric acid), were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All reagents were of analytical grade.

Bacterial strain and spore suspension preparation

The Paraclostridium bifermentans strain used in this study was isolated from spoiled RTE chicken breast obtained from an industrial producer (Nanjing Huangjiaoshou Food Science and Technology Co., Ltd., China) and preserved by our research group, following a protocol described in our previous study [26]. The isolate represents a naturally occurring spore-forming bacterium in this meat system. It was identified via 16S rRNA gene sequencing, and the resulting sequence has been registered in the National Center for Biotechnology Information database under accession number ON078501.

The preparation of spores followed the method established by Huang et al. [24]. In brief, an activated culture grown in RCM under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C for 24 h was inoculated onto Duncan–Strong Sporulation Medium (DSSM) agar, containing 15 g tryptone, 4 g yeast extract, 10 g Na2HPO4, 4 g D-raffinose pentahydrate, 1 g sodium thioglycolate and 20 g agar per liter, adjusted to pH 7.5. The plates were incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for seven days to allow sporulation. After incubation, spores were collected by centrifugation at 7,500 × g for 15 min at 4 °C using an Allegra-64R centrifuge (Beckman Coulter, USA) and subsequently washed four times with sterile, ice-cold distilled water. Spore purity was confirmed to exceed 95 % using fluorescence microscopy (Scope.A1, Carl Zeiss, Germany). The final spore suspension remained viable for one month when stored at 4 °C.

Sample treatment and storage

According to the procedure presented in Fig. 1, RTE chicken breasts were inoculated with spore suspension of P. bifermentans. The suspension was prepared at 106 CFU/mL, and the inoculation was performed at a level of 1 mL/100 g. The spore-contaminated chicken breasts were divided into seven distinct treatment groups, with the treatment parameters selected based on our previous optimization study [36]: C, serving as the untreated control; 121, sterilized at 121 °C; 80, subjected to 80 °C sterilization for 60 min; T, treated with thermosonication at 80 °C for 60 min; GT, which was subjected to germination using a germinant mixture (‘A’ GFNa-60), consisting of L-alanine (90 mmol/L), D-glucose (10 mmol/L), D-fructose (10 mmol/L), and NaCl (60 mmol/L), followed by thermosonication at 80 °C for 60 min; TL, supplemented with 6.25 mg/mL licorice extract in combination with thermosonication under the same conditions; and GTL, subjected to the ‘A’ GFNa-60 germination treatment together with 6.25 mg/mL licorice extract and thermosonication at 80 °C for 60 min. The treated RTE chicken breasts were stored at 25 ± 1 °C. This temperature was selected as an accelerated storage condition to evaluate microbial and quality changes [15], [37]. It should be noted that this temperature does not represent standard refrigeration (4 °C), and the results reflect treatment performance under this specific accelerated condition rather than direct predictions of refrigerated shelf life. Samples were taken at 3-day intervals for index determination over a storage period of 9 days, which was selected to capture the progression from early microbial growth to advanced spoilage under these conditions.Fig. 1Schematic workflow of sample preparation and treatment for RTE chicken breast.

Microbiological analysis

Microbial contamination was evaluated according to the method reported by Lei et al. [38]. 10 g of chicken breast sample was aseptically transferred into a sterile stomacher bag containing 90 mL of sterile 0.85 % NaCl solution. The sample was homogenized for 2 min with a tapping homogenizer to form a uniform suspension, which was subsequently subjected to stepwise dilution using sterile saline. Aliquots (1 mL) from appropriate dilutions were plated onto plate count agar to determine total viable counts (TVC) and onto RCA for enumeration of anaerobic Clostridium. For RCA plates, anaerobic incubation was performed in an anaerobic chamber (Bugbox M, Ruskinn, UK) with an atmosphere of 90 % N2, 5 % CO2, and 5 % H2. All plates were incubated upside down at 37 °C for 24 h. Plate count agar plates were cultured in a microbiological incubator (B1-150A, STIK, Shanghai), whereas RCA plates were incubated under the anaerobic conditions described above. No heat shock treatment was applied prior to plating. After incubation, colonies were counted using an automated colony counter (Scan1200, Interscience, France), and the results were reported as log CFU/g.

Lipid and protein oxidation analysis

The content of thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARs) was analyzed according to the procedure reported by Xu et al. [39] with slight adjustments. In brief, a 4 g sample was mixed with 20 mL of 7.5 % TCA and then homogenized in an ice at 10,000 × g for 2 × 15 s. The homogenate underwent centrifugation at 13,000 × g for a duration of 10 min at 4 °C. Following this, 2 mL of the supernatant was reacted with TBA at 95 °C for 30 min, and the absorbance was read at 532 nm. TBARs results were expressed as mg MDA/kg. The determination of TVB-N followed the Chinese standard (GB/T 5009.228–2016). In brief, 4 g of sample was blended thoroughly with 1 g of magnesium oxide, after which the mixture was transferred into a digestion tube. The analysis was carried out using an automatic Kjeldahl nitrogen analyzer (Hanon K9860, Jinan, China). The final results were reported as mg TVB-N/100 g.

pH and color

A calibrated pH meter (FE20, Mettler Toledo, Switzerland) was employed to detect the pH of chicken breast samples following the approach described by Wu et al. [40]. Prior to measurement, the samples were mixed with 0.1 mol/L KCl solution at a 1:9 (w/w) ratio and homogenized for 30 s using a homogenizer. The surface color of the samples was evaluated in terms of L* (lightness), a* (redness), and b* (yellowness) using a portable colorimeter (CR-400, Konica Minolta, Japan) according to the method of Ume Roobab et al. [41]. Before measurement were taken, the instrument was standardized with a white calibration plate (CIEL* = 96.86, CIE a* = −0.15, CIE b* = 1.87).

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) spectroscopy

The distribution of water in chicken breast samples was measured using an LF-NMR analyzer (NiumagPQ001, Niumag, Shanghai). Samples were cut into pieces of about 1 cm × 1 cm × 2 cm, wrapped in plastic film, and placed in NMR tubes with an 18 mm diameter. The instrument was operated under the following settings: resonance frequency of 40 MHz, sampling frequency of 100 kHz, initial delay of 0.08 ms, waiting time of 8000 ms, data radius of 1, analogue gain of 20 dB, digital gain of 3, 1024 data points, 8 scans, 2000 echoes, and half echo time of 0.1 ms [42].

Texture profile analysis (TPA)

Textural characteristics of the samples were evaluated based on the method described by Gao et al. [43] with minor adjustments, using a texture analyzer (TA-XT Plus, Stable Micro Systems, UK) fitted with a P50 cylindrical probe (50 mm diameter). The chicken breast samples were first cut into cubes with dimensions of 1 cm × 1 cm × 1 cm. The trigger force was set to 25 g during the test, with a 5 s pause between each compression. The speeds before, during, and after the test were 2.0 mm/s, 1.0 mm/s, and 5.0 mm/s, respectively, and the compression ratio was kept at 50 %.

E-nose analysis

The volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in the samples were assessed using a PEN3 E-nose system (Airsense Analytics, Germany) following the procedure described by Zhu et al. [44]. The device was equipped with 10 metal oxide sensors (W1C, W1S, W1W, W2S, W2W, W3C, W3S, W5C, W5S, and W6S), with their specifications summarized in Table S1. Approximately 2 g of chicken breast was precisely weighed and allowed to equilibrate in a water bath at 50 °C for 30 min. Measurements were conducted under the following conditions: sensor and sample gas flow at 300 mL/min, air collection time of 120 s, sampling interval of 1 s, zeroing time of 10 s, and sensor cleaning duration of 120 s. To maintain measurement accuracy, the inlet needle was replaced immediately after each analysis.

Headspace-gas chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry analysis (HS-GC-IMS)

To further characterize the VOCs in the samples, we used HS-GC-IMS. This was done using a FlavourSpec®, G.A.S. (Dortmund, Germany), following the method outlined by Zhu et al. [44] with some modifications. For the analysis, 2.0 g of minced chicken breast was put into a 20 mL headspace vial and sealed tightly. The vial was incubated at 60 ℃ for 10 min prior to injection. The chromatographic separation was achieved using an FS-SE-54-CB-1 capillary column (15 m × 0.53 mm, 1 μm film thickness) held at 60 ℃. At the same time, the syringe needle was kept at 85 ℃. Nitrogen (≥ 99.99 % purity) was used as carrier gas, with a programmed flow: 2 mL/min for the first 2 min, rising to 10 mL/min over the next 8 min, then to 50 mL/min within 10 min, and finally reaching 150 mL/min in the last 10 min. The overall analysis lasted 31 min. For retention index calibration, a series of n-ketone standards (C4–C9) were injected. Tentative identification of VOCs was performed by comparing retention indices, drift times, the IMS database, and existing literature.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was repeated three times, and the results are shown as mean ± standard deviation. Data were analyzed using SAS software (version 8.1; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). One-way or two-way ANOVA was utilized where applicable, with two-way ANOVA used to evaluate the effects of treatment, storage time, and their interaction. Prior to analysis, the normality of the data and homogeneity of variance were verified. Duncan's multiple range test was then applied for multiple comparisons, and differences were considered significant at p < 0.05. Data visualization was done using GraphPad Prism 8.0 and Origin 2024. Principal component analysis (PCA) and linear discriminant analysis (LDA) were performed using WinMuster software. The 3D plots, 2D maps, and VOCs fingerprints were generated using LAV software.

Results and discussion

Changes of microbiological under different treatments

Meat spoilage is primarily caused by microbial activity. According to the Chinese Standard (GB 2726–2016), the TVC in cooked meat products should be maintained below 4 log CFU/g. As shown in Fig. 2A, the TVC increased gradually with the prolongation of storage time across all groups; however, substantial differences were observed in both the growth rate and final microbial load. At the start of storage, the TVC levels in all treated groups were considerably less than those in the control group (p < 0.05). As storage time extended, the control and 80 °C treatment groups showed the fastest growth rates, reaching 4.60 and 4.90 log CFU/g, respectively, by day 3. By day 6, the counts in 121 °C, T, and GT groups increased to 5.02–5.36 log CFU/g among these groups. In contrast, the TL and GTL groups remained below the regulatory limit of 4 log CFU/g until day 9, indicating that thermosonication combined multifactor treatment effectively suppressed microbial growth. This pattern was consistent with a significant interaction between treatment and storage time (p < 0.01, Table S3), suggesting that the antimicrobial effects of different treatments varied across the storage period.Fig. 2Changes in TVC (A) and Clostridium counts (B) of RTE chicken breast under different treatments during storage. A comparable trend was noted for Clostridium counts, which increased with storage time but differed significantly among treatments (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2B). In contrast with the control group, initial Clostridium counts significantly reduced in all treated groups (p < 0.05). Specifically, the 121 °C and GTL groups decreased by 2.15 and 2.28 log CFU/g, respectively, whereas the 80 °C treatment showed the weakest reduction (0.4 log CFU/g). By day 6, Clostridium counts in control and 80 °C groups reached 7.31 and 7.96 log CFU/g, respectively. By contrast, the TL and GTL groups showed lower Clostridium counts, reaching 4.28 and 4.42 log CFU/g. These results highlight the synergistic antibacterial effects of thermosonication combined with the bioactive compounds in licorice extract and germinants. Moreover, a significant treatment × storage time interaction (p < 0.01, Table S3) indicated that the effectiveness of different treatments was dependent on storage duration. In summary, while thermal and thermosonication treatments alone have been shown to be effective in initial reduction, only the TL and GTL treatments provided sustained inhibition of Clostridium growth during storage. This difference may be attributed to multiple factors. On the one hand, germination may reduce spore resistance and make spores more susceptible to inactivation, while thermosonication may physically disrupt microbial structures and increase permeability [27], [36]. On the other hand, the polyphenols in licorice extract may interact with membrane structures and further aggravate membrane damage during thermosonication, which is consistent with previous observations that these compounds can affect membrane integrity [26], [36]. Although sterilization at 121 °C effectively reduces the initial microbial load, the relatively faster regrowth observed during storage may be attributed to the germination and outgrowth of heat-activated spores [10], [45]. Accordingly, the present findings should be interpreted as reflecting the relative effectiveness of the treatment under accelerated storage conditions and should not be directly extrapolated to refrigerated shelf life.

Changes of TVB-N during storage

TVB-N is an important indicator reflecting the degree of microbial proliferation and protein degradation during meat storage. It's mainly composed of ammonia and amine compounds produced by endogenous enzymes and spoilage microorganisms during meat storage [3], [38]. According to GB 2707–2016, fresh meat is considered acceptable if its TVB-N value is below 15 mg/100 g. Values between 15 and 20 mg/100 g are considered a critical level, while values above 20 mg/100 g indicate spoilage. During storage, the TVB-N levels in all groups exhibited a significant rise (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3A), and a significant treatment × storage time interaction (p < 0.01, Table S3) resulted in time-dependent differences in TVB-N accumulation among treatments. Notably, the original TVB-N value observed in the 121 °C group was higher than that of the control, which may be related to heat-induced chemical changes, as strong thermal treatment can disrupt myofibrillar structure and promote protein oxidation and aggregation [4], [46], [47]. In addition, high-temperature treatment may also alter nucleotide-related metabolites by lowering IMP and increasing inosine and hypoxanthine [48], [49]. These alterations show that nitrogen-containing compounds may undergo transformation under thermal conditions, thereby contributing to the formation of volatile basic nitrogen compounds [48]. By day 3, the TVB-N concentrations with both the control and 80 °C groups increased sharply to 24.94 and 24.83 mg/100 g, respectively, which exceeded the spoilage threshold. This increase was consistent with the rise in TVC, indicating that TVB-N reflected the progression of spoilage. And the TVB-N of samples treated by thermosonication and its combined treatment (T, GT, TL, GTL) was 11.37 to 16.68 mg/100 g on day 3. In particular, the GTL group still maintained a low level (12.63 mg/100 g) on day 6. This observation might indicate that thermosonication delayed the TVB-N formation, and its combination with germinant or licorice extract may enhance antimicrobial efficacy through synergistic effects [50]. Changes of TBARS during storage

TBARS is commonly used to evaluate the extent of lipid oxidation in meat products, with higher values reflecting greater oxidative deterioration [43], [50]. From Fig. 3B, it can be seen that the TBARS values increased gradually in all treatment groups, indicating that oxidative degradation was in progress. However, compared with the control group, treatment as 121 °C, 80 °C, thermosonication, and combined treatments (GT, TL, GTL) showed significant inhibition of the growth of TBARS value throughout the storage period (p < 0.05). Among these, the TL and GTL groups demonstrated the most pronounced inhibitory effect, which is likely due to the combined action of thermosonication and the antioxidant properties of licorice extract, particularly its bioactive constituents such as glycyrrhizic acid and flavonoids (e.g., liquiritin and liquiritigenin), which have been reported to scavenge free radicals and inhibit lipid peroxidation [26], [51]. In addition, a significant treatment × storage time interaction (p < 0.01, Table S3) indicated that, although storage time played a leading role in lipid oxidation, the inhibitory effects of different treatments were different during the whole storage period.

According to the results listed in Table 2, the L* values of chicken breast meat gradually decreased, the a* value first stabilized and then decreased, whereas the b* value continuously increased during storage. Two-way ANOVA showed that the effects of treatment and storage time differed among color parameters. Among them, no significant interaction was observed for L* value (p > 0.05, Table S3), indicating that the effects of each treatment on lightness were relatively stable during storage. In contrast, significant interactions were observed for both a* and b* values (p < 0.05), demonstrating that treatment effects on redness and yellowness changed over storage time. Further analysis showed that the 121 °C treatment group had the lowest L* value during storage compared with other groups, which may be due to the irreversible denaturation of proteins and pigments caused by extreme heat [4], [46]. Surprisingly, compared to the control group, thermosonication treatment reduced the a* value while increasing the b* value, which is consistent with findings regarding the influence of ultrasound on the coloration of marinated yak meat [55]. The effect of combined treatments (GT, TL, GTL) on color was more complex. During storage, GTL treatment showed no significant impact on lightness or redness values (p > 0.05) but significantly increased yellowness (p < 0.05). This occurrence is likely caused by the relatively mild impact of thermosonication in limiting pigment oxidation, as well as the presence of flavonoids in licorice extract, which are known to inhibit lipid oxidation [51]. Changes of textural profile under different treatments

Texture profile analysis is a critical objective method for evaluating meat quality, replacing subjective sensory evaluations with quantifiable mechanical parameters [6]. Key indicators such as hardness, springiness, cohesiveness, and chewiness are closely associated with consumer perception, directly influencing meat tenderness, juiciness, and overall acceptability [56], [57]. Table 3 indicates that, at the initial stage of storage, the group subjected to 121 °C treatment demonstrated the greatest hardness and chewiness. This finding indicates that high-temperature processing can cause protein denaturation, resulting in a denser structure of the RTE chicken breast and substantial moisture loss. Additionally, this group also had the highest springiness, indicating an enhanced elastic recovery capacity. These alterations reflect the irreversible effects of high-temperature treatment on protein conformation. Among combination treatments, the TL and GTL groups showed the highest level of cohesiveness, which may be related to the synergistic effects of thermosonication and natural additives in stabilizing the muscle microstructure. By day 3 of storage, although the 121 °C group continued to maintain the highest hardness and chewiness compared with day 0, its springiness slightly decreased. It is worth noting that the TL and GTL treatments exhibited superior cohesion without compromising tenderness and maintained moderate chewiness. In summary, the 121 °C treatment increased meat firmness and chewing resistance, which may adversely affect sensory acceptance. In contrast, combined treatments such as TL and GTL improved muscle cohesiveness without significantly affecting springiness (p > 0.05), thus offering a promising strategy for preserving textural quality during storage and processing. However, it should be noted that TPA measurements were conducted only at days 0 and 3. Water distribution profiles under different treatments

LF-NMR was employed to measure the transverse relaxation times (T2) to assess the water distribution and mobility in RTE chicken breast during processing treatments. A longer T2 typically indicates higher water mobility and weaker binding states. As demonstrated in Fig. 4A and 4B, three main peaks were detected, representing bound water (T2b), immobilized water (T21), and free water (T22). The result summarized in Table S2 further show significant differences (p < 0.05) in T2 and associated peak area percentages (P2b, P21, P22) (Fig. 4C) among all groups. Specifically, the T2b values of the 121 °C, T, GT, TL, and GTL groups were considerably smaller in comparison to those measured in the control and 80 °C groups (p < 0.05), suggesting enhanced binding of water to proteins, likely due to conformational alteration or protein cross-linking induced by these treatments. In contrast, there were no significant differences (p > 0.05) in T21 values, while T22 values were significantly prolonged in the T and GT groups (p < 0.05). This could potentially be attributed to ultrasound enhancing myofibrillar protein solubility, disrupting myofibrillar spatial structure, promoting protein release, and exposing hydrophobic groups [42]. Similarly, storage time also influenced water mobility. After 3 days, the T2b and T22 values exhibited a substantial increase than control group, indicating an achievable rise in mobility and water loss over time. The distribution of water proportion revealed that the P2b value was highest in 121 °C group at the initial storage stage, further confirming that high-temperature treatment enhanced protein-water binding. However, the P22 values in the T and GT groups were lowest, indicating a reduction in free water content, which may be attributable to the thermosonication effects. Notably, the TL and GTL groups showed no significant differences in P21 compared to the control (p > 0.05), which may be associated with the presence of licorice extract, whose antioxidant properties could help maintain the integrity of the myofibrillar network under thermal or ultrasonic conditions, thereby contributing to the preservation of water distribution [58], [59]. By day 3, the control group displayed a significant increase in P21 value in (p < 0.05), while P22 values experienced a substantial reduction. Volatile compounds analysis by E-nose

To assess changes in aroma characteristics, the volatile profile of RTE chicken breast under various treatments was detected with an E-nose system (Fig. 5). Among all treatments, the signals from sensor W5S, W1W, W2S, W1S and W2W changed the most, indicating that the primary VOCs include nitrogenous compounds, methyl derivatives, sulfur-containing volatiles, and oxygenated compounds. At the initial stage, W5S, W1W, and W2S showed an increase in the T group, followed by the combined groups (GT, TL, GTL) in relation to the control group. It aligns with the Zhao's study [60], indicating that the ultrasonic treatment improves the overall volatile profile of meat. By day 3, the signals of W5S, W1S, W1W and W2S in the control, 80 °C, and 121 °C groups were higher than those at the initial stage, whereas signals of W3S, W3C, W6S, and W5C decreased in the control group. Interestingly, the T, GT, TL and GTL groups maintained stable sensor responses during storage, suggesting their efficacy in preserving volatile profile stability.Fig. 5Radar plots of the E-nose response data (A, B), PCA plots (C, D) and LDA score plots (E, F) of RTE chicken breast under different treatments.

PCA was used to evaluate the volatile profile of chicken breast samples. As shown in Fig. 5C and 5D, the first two principal components accounted for 98.17 % (PC1: 93.66 %, PC2: 4.51 %) and 98.98 % (PC1: 97.57 %, PC2: 1.40 %) of total variance during storage, respectively. Both values (>85 %) were sufficient to capture the major variability among samples and effectively reflected the volatile characteristics under different treatments [60]. There was obvious separation between the control and treatment group, suggesting that different treatments altered the overall volatile profile of RTE chicken breast. It is worth noting that there is partial overlap among the treatment groups in PCA plot. Therefore, LDA was further used to improve the discrimination between samples. LDA is a multivariate statistical method that can enhance group separation by constructing linear combinations of characteristic variables [61]. As illustrated in Fig. 5E and 5F, the first two discriminant functions explained 90.65 % and 82.68 % of the total variance, indicating that most of the variation among samples was captured. In the LDA plot, the seven groups appeared more separated than in the PCA plot. There was a partial overlap between GT and the T/TL groups, suggesting that the volatile profiles were similar under ultrasound treatment. However, due to the limited specificity of E-nose technology in identifying individual VOCs, further analysis of volatile compounds is warranted using GC-IMS.

Fingerprint spectra were generated to assess the variations of VOCs in RTE chicken breast under different treatment and to allow both visual and quantitative assessment of these differences. In Fig. 6E, rows represent samples with different treatments, columns denote the detected VOCs, and color intensity reflects their relative concentrations. Each compound was labeled as “M” for its monomeric form and “D” for the dimeric form, corresponding to protonated molecular ions ([M + H]⁺) and proton-bound dimer ions ([2 M + H]⁺), respectively. Overall, 51 VOCs were characterized in RTE chicken breast samples, comprising 13 alcohols, 11 ketones, 9 esters, 7 aldehydes, 3 furans and 8 others (Fig. 6E and Table S4). As shown in Fig. 6E, the levels of key compounds such as 3-methyl-1-butanol-M, 3-methyl-1-butanol-D, 1-heptanol, isobutyl acetate, hexyl acetate, 2,3-butanediol and 2-methyl propanal varied across different treatment groups. However, the intensity of butanal-D, butanal-M, cyclohexanone-M, ethyl acetate, 2-acetylthiazole-D and propyl butanoate remained relatively stable. At the initial storage stage, the levels of 4-methyl-2-pentanol-D, heptanol, 3-methylpentanol, 3-methyl-1-butanol-D, pentanal-D, 2-methylpropyl acetate, 2-heptanone-D and cyclohexanone-D decreased in the 121 °C group as opposed to the control group. These findings suggest that high-temperature sterilization causes the loss of VOCs in RTE chicken breast. Moreover, some VOCs, including esters and furans such as propyl acetate-D, 2-ethylfuran-D and 2-ethylfuran, along with alcohols (e.g., 2,3-butanediol) and aldehydes (e.g., 2-methylpropanal), showed an increase by day 3. These compounds may be mainly produced by microbial metabolism or lipid degradation [42]. Meanwhile, VOCs such as alcohols and ketones (e.g., 1-octanol, furaneol, 3-octanol, 2-propanol-D, 2-propanol-M, and 2-propanone) were exclusively detected on day 3, which was attributed to the action of microbial enzymes on proteins in RTE chicken breast, resulting in the production of novel VOCs [62]. Conversely, esters, furans, ketones and terpenes, such as 3-hexenyl acetate, 2-pentylfuran, acetophenone, limonene and 1-octen-3-one, only appeared at day 0, which may be volatilized or metabolized after heat treatment. It should be noted that during the whole storage process, some VOCs in the thermosonication group and the combined groups (GT, TL, GTL), such as 1-heptanol, 4-methyl-2-pentanol-D, heptanol, 3-methylpentanol, 2,3-butanediol, isobutyl acetate, 2-methylpropyl acetate, propyl acetate-D, 2-ethylfuran-D, styrene-D and 2-methyl propanal, remained at a high level or showed an increasing trend. This indicates that thermosonication and its combined treatment contribute to maintaining the volatile profile stability of chicken breast meat. This phenomenon may be attributed to high-intensity heat treatment (such as 121 °C sterilization) accelerating lipid oxidation, promoting aldehyde accumulation associated with off-flavor, while inducing protein oxidation and changing its conformation, thereby reducing the retention ability of VOCs [63]. In contrast, ultrasound can enhance flavor retention by promoting partial protein unfolding and exposing hydrophobic domains, which enhances their interaction with VOCs [64]. Therefore, thermosonication under mild conditions can help suppress excessive oxidation and maintain volatile flavor profile.

Although GTL treatment showed good performance in inhibiting microbial growth and improving the edible quality of RTE chicken breasts compared with conventional 121 °C sterilization, its practical feasibility remains to be evaluated in relation to product value and quality requirements. Despite the specialization of thermosonication equipment, challenges related to equipment availability, processing efficiency, and energy consumption may limit its large-scale application [65]. Furthermore, licorice extract has a good inhibitory effect on microbial growth under laboratory conditions, but its effectiveness in actual meat processing may be affected by product characteristics (e.g. pH value, water activity, fat and protein content), packaging methods (e.g. vacuum or modified atmosphere) and storage conditions (e.g. temperature and humidity) [23]. Therefore, further pilot studies are needed to assess the economic feasibility and scalability of the GTL treatment under practical production conditions.

Conclusions

This study systematically examined the preservation impacts of thermosonication combined with germination induction and licorice extract (GTL) on RTE chicken breasts under the “germination–inactivation–inhibition” strategy, with particular emphasis on its impact on microbial inactivation, physicochemical quality, and volatile profiles. Results indicate that the GTL treatment clearly reduced initial TVC during storage and effectively inhibited the growth of Clostridium and its spores. Additionally, the GTL treatment demonstrated enhanced stability in color parameters, textural properties and moisture distribution, while also delayed the rise in pH and the processes of lipid oxidation and protein degradation. Regarding VOCs, the findings from E-nose and GC-IMS revelated that the GTL treatment preserved or even enhanced the levels of esters, alcohols, aldehydes and furans (e.g., 1-heptanol, isobutyl acetate, 4-methyl-2-pentanol-D, heptanol, 2-methylpropyl acetate, 3-methylpentanol, 2-ethylfuran-D, propyl acetate-D, pentanal-D, and 2-methyl propanal), which are often lost during high-temperature sterilization, thereby contributing to a more stable volatile profile. Therefore, thermosonication combined with germination induction and licorice extract can be considered a promising strategy for delaying quality deterioration and improving edible quality of RTE chicken breast under accelerated storage conditions. However, when it comes to practical application, cost-effectiveness, the challenges of large-scale promotion and regulatory compliance for the use of licorice extract still need to be considered. In China, although licorice is recognized as a medicine and edible substance, its use in RTE meat products should comply with relevant food safety regulations. In addition, the potential sensory impact of licorice extract (e.g., bitterness or aftertaste) was not evaluated in the present study because sensory analysis could not be performed after inoculation with P. bifermentans. Therefore, the influence of licorice extract on consumer sensory acceptance remains to be verified. Furthermore, because a germination-only control group was not included, the independent contribution of germination induction could not be directly evaluated. Future studies should further evaluate consumer sensory acceptance and explore the applicability of this approach to other poultry products (e.g., dark meat, skin-on cuts) as well as other RTE meat products.
