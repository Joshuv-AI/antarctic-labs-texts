Low Risk Is Not Synonymous With No Risk
Authors: Ranjan Banerjee, Jordan D Awerbach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: cardiac magnetic resonance, computed tomography, coronary vessel anomaly, ventricular tachycardia

Most diagnoses of anomalous aortic origin of a coronary artery (AAOCA) are discovered incidentally during cross-sectional chest imaging performed either as part of a chest pain evaluation or for unrelated noncardiac indications. The reported prevalence of AAOCA among patients undergoing cardiac imaging, including echocardiography, computed tomography angiography, magnetic resonance imaging, and invasive coronary angiography, is estimated at 0.7%.1 Once the diagnosis is made, the questions become: 1) Does this explain the patient's symptoms (if any symptoms exist)? And in either case; 2) What do we do about it? As the quality, availability, and utilization of advanced multimodal cardiac imaging continue to increase, incidental diagnoses of AAOCA will become more common, placing greater emphasis on accurate risk assessment.

The case presented by Eisa et al2 in this issue of JACC: Case Reports describes a 43-year-old physically active man who developed exertional symptoms and was found during the recovery phase of exercise stress testing to have frequent premature ventricular contractions that progressed to nonsustained ventricular tachycardia. Subsequent coronary computed tomography angiography demonstrated an anomalous right coronary artery arising from the left sinus with multiple high-risk anatomical features. Surgical unroofing resulted in resolution of symptoms and an excellent clinical outcome.

From a management perspective, the decision in this case appears relatively straightforward. Current guidelines support surgical intervention in patients with AAOCA who have ischemic symptoms or objective evidence of ischemia attributable to the anomalous coronary.3 The presence of ventricular arrhythmias in conjunction with high-risk anatomic features strongly supports surgical correction. In many respects, this case represents the type of patient for whom contemporary risk stratification performs well.

The more provocative questions lie in the negative space. Suppose this patient's AAOCA had been identified incidentally 6 years earlier, before the onset of symptoms that ultimately prompted his evaluation. At that time, he would have been a healthy, active, asymptomatic adult with no previous history of chest pain, syncope, or arrhythmia. He likely would have undergone provocative ischemia testing, which may well have been negative. What would have happened next?

Current risk stratification in AAOCA has become increasingly refined. Beyond simply the sinus of origin and presence of an interarterial course, specific anatomic features are known to be associated with symptoms, ischemia, and sudden cardiac death. Higher-risk features include a long intramural course, a slit-like coronary ostium or proximal coronary narrowing, an acute take-off angle, a high ostial origin, and a thickened intercoronary pillar.4 The 2025 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association/Heart Rhythm Society/International Society for Adult Congenital Heart Disease/Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions Guideline for the Management of Adults With Congenital Heart Disease states that surgical intervention is reasonable in an asymptomatic patient with an anomalous left coronary and high-risk anatomic features.5 Conversely, the benefit of intervention in a patient with an anomalous right coronary artery and high-risk anatomy in the absence of demonstrated myocardial ischemia remains unclear. As a result, many asymptomatic adults in this population are managed conservatively.

Recent efforts have attempted to integrate these findings into unified risk stratification frameworks (Figure 1). Importantly, these frameworks move beyond anatomy alone and acknowledge that management decisions occur along a continuum rather than as a binary choice between surgery and observation. Factors such as age, athletic participation, patient goals and preferences, and a willingness to accept uncertainty may all influence decision-making. As incidental diagnosis of AAOCA becomes more common, these patient-centered considerations are likely to play a greater role in determining management strategies.

Such an approach is supported, at least in part, by observational data suggesting that risk declines with age. In one cohort of 30 patients with AAOCA, of whom 70% were anomalous right coronaries, age >30 was the only factor associated with a lower risk of sudden cardiac death.5 Moreover, in a cohort of 66 middle-aged patients, the presence of AAOCA was not associated with major adverse cardiovascular events compared with matched controls.6 Collectively, these findings have contributed to the perception that beyond early adulthood, asymptomatic individuals, particularly those with anomalous right coronary arteries, are at relatively low risk for adverse events. Yet the present case serves as a real-world reminder that low risk is not synonymous with no risk.

Indeed, this case raises a fundamental question that remains largely unanswered: how should asymptomatic adults who do not meet criteria for surgery be followed longitudinally? Existing guidelines offer relatively little direction, reflecting the limited evidence base that characterizes much of adult congenital heart disease care. As the diagnostic prevalence of AAOCA continues to rise, defining optimal surveillance strategies and determining which testing modalities provide the most meaningful assessment of evolving risk over time will become essential.

Ultimately, the challenge in AAOCA care extends beyond estimating the risk of sudden cardiac death. For many patients, the diagnosis itself may carry significant consequences, including anxiety, uncertainty, employment implications, repeated testing, and restrictions on exercise or athletic participation. These considerations are particularly relevant in otherwise healthy individuals whose anomaly is discovered incidentally and who may never experience symptoms or adverse events. The goal of risk stratification, therefore, is not simply to identify patients who may benefit from intervention, but also to provide patients with the information necessary to make informed decisions that align with their goals of care. Until more robust longitudinal data become available, individualized risk assessment, thoughtful surveillance, and shared decision-making remain the cornerstones of care.
