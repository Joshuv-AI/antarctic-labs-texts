An experimental study on the effects of drying-wetting cycles to the mechanical properties of red mud
Authors: Yanbo Feng, Xiangyang Yang, Xiaohui Wan, Xianyong Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Red mud, a hazardous industrial waste generated during alumina production, faces great challenges in safe stacking and utilization due to its complex chemical composition and poor engineering properties. Long-term stacking of red mud is accompanied by repeated natural drying-wetting cycles, which significantly alter its strength characteristics and further threaten the stability of red mud stockpiles. In this study, Bayer red mud (BRM) and mixed red mud (MRM) from the No.5 stockpile of Guizhou Aluminum Plant were tested under 0, 1, 3, 7, 12 drying-wetting cycles. Their physical, microscopic, and mechanical properties were investigated. Stockpile stability under extreme rainfall was evaluated using Geo-Studio software (SEEP/W and SLOPE/W modules). The results showed that unconfined compressive strength (qu) and cohesion (c) of BRM decrease with cycles, while those of MRM increased significantly. Specially, the (qu) of MRM rise from 115.78 kPa to 150.38 kPa and the cohesion (c) doubles from 61.67 kPa to 121.91 kPa. The internal friction angle (φ) of BRM increases slightly, while that of MRM decreases slightly. Under extreme rainfall conditions, safety factors of red mud are 1.205, 1.219, 1.240, 1.263 and 1.288, respectively, all greater than the critical value of 1.0 and increasing with cycles. This study demonstrates that stacking MRM on BRM is feasible and effective for improving the long term stockpile stability, which provide theoretical support and engineering guidance for the safe stacking and resource utilization of red mud.

Introduction

With rapid urbanization and industrialization, large amounts of industrial solid wastes are generated1–8. Red mud is a typical hazardous alkaline waste from alumina production. It has fine particles, complex composition, and poor engineering properties9–16. Global annual output exceeds 120 million tons, but utilization is only ~ 10%17,18. Open-pit dam construction and stockpiling have become the main disposal methods for red mud, which not only occupies a large amount of land but also brings environmental and geological risks19,20.

According to the different production processes, red mud is mainly divided into Sintering-Processed red mud and Bayer-Process red mud (BRM)21–25. Sintering-Processed red mud has the characteristics of large particle size, high sand content, good permeability and certain cementitious activity11,26. In contrast, BRM is characterized by fine particle size, high clay content, poor permeability and low strength11,22,27,26. The BRM is prone to dam break, landslide and other geological disasters, which has become a key challenge restricting the safe disposal of red mud28,29. To solve the problem, Mixed red mud (MRM), composed of the Sintering-Processed red mud and BRM, was proposed and shows better performance21,28,30,31.

Drying-wetting cycles can affect the engineering properties of clayey soils, causing irreversible changes in soil structure and mineral composition transformation29,13,32,33. For MRM, with cementitious minerals, the drying-wetting cycles may promote the formation of cementitious substances and improve the mechanical properties13,32,34–38. At present, most studies on the effect of drying-wetting cycles on red mud focus on a single type. Comparatively, existing studies, on the evolution law and mechanism of mechanical properties of BRM and MRM under different drying-wetting cycles, are insufficient.

This study takes BRM and MRM from Guizhou Aluminum Plant as research objects. A series of laboratory tests were conducted to investigate the evolution of their physical, microstructural and mechanical properties under 0, 1, 3, 7 and 12 drying-wetting cycles. The strength deterioration mechanisms were revealed from microstructural and chemical perspectives. Using Geo-Studio, the seepage field and stability of red mud mixed stockpiles under extreme rainfall were simulated and evaluated. These results provide a theoretical basis and engineering reference for the safe disposal, stability evaluation and resource utilization of red mud under natural environmental effects.

Materials and experimental design

Raw materials

The test materials were collected from the Zhatang red mud stockpile of an aluminum plant in Guizhou Province, southwestern China, including freshly discharged dry BRM and MRM. The mass ratio of Sintering-Processed red mud to Bayer-Processed red mud is 1:1. The samples were collected at the first discharge point to ensure the representativeness of the materials, and were sealed and transported to the laboratory for subsequent sample preparation.

According to the Chinese national standard Standard for Test Methods of Geotechnical Engineering (GB/T 50123 − 2019), raw red mud samples were air-dried, crushed, and sieved through a 2-mm sieve prior to testing. Cylindrical specimens were prepared with dimensions of Φ39.1 mm × H80 mm for unconfined compressive strength tests and consolidated drained triaxial shear tests, and Φ61.8 mm × H20 mm for compression tests. All specimens were compacted to the maximum dry density at the optimum water content. The initial water content controlled within ± 0.5% to ensure an uniform initial state of all specimens.

Drying-wetting cycle test design

The drying-wetting cycle treatment of red mud specimens was carried out according to the actual climate conditions of the study area. Each drying-wetting cycle was defined by two stages, 5 days of dehydration and drying, followed by 5 days of water immersion:

(1) Drying stage

The compacted specimens were weighed first. They were then placed in an electric constant temperature drying oven at 40 ℃ for 5 days of dehydration, until the mass was constant. Drying completion criterion is mass change < 0.01 g within 24 h. These parameters are consistent with geotechnical test standards39.

(2) Wetting stage

The dried specimens were fully immersed in distilled water for 5 days. The water level was kept 2 cm higher than the top of the specimens to ensure complete saturation. Water temperature during immersion is 20 ± 2 °C. Ambient relative humidity was controlled at 50 ± 5%.

The above steps were repeated to carry out drying-wetting cycle treatment on BRM and MRM specimens. The cycle numbers set as 0, 1, 3, 7 and 12. Cycle 0 corresponds to the original specimen without any drying-wetting cycle treatment. The gradient setting of the cycle times is based on the actual environmental conditions of the red mud stockpile and the research norms of geotechnical engineering. Specifically, 1 and 3 cycles represent the short-term drying-wetting effects under sudden rainfall environment. 7 cycles correspond to the seasonal rainfall-evaporation processes in the study area. 12 cycles simulate the long-term repeated drying-wetting during the actual service period of red mud stockpiles. Specimens subjected to n cycles were denoted as Type FBn (Bayer red mud) and Type FHn (mixed red mud), respectively. Upon completion of the prescribed number of cycles, the specimens were removed and air-dried to the natural state. They were then prepared for subsequent property testing. This gradient design is widely used in geotechnical drying-wetting cycle research. It can fully reflect the complete evolution law of material properties under short-term, medium-term, and long-term cycles.

Physical and mechanical property tests

Physical property tests

The basic physical properties of BRM and MRM were tested in duplicate according to GB/T 50,123 39. The average value was taken as the test result:

Specific gravity

Determined by the pycnometer method with distilled water as the medium;

Atterberg limits

Liquid limit and plastic limit were measured by a photoelectric liquid-plasticity combined measuring instrument, and the plasticity index was calculated;

Particle size distribution

Analyzed by a laser particle size analyzer (test range: 0.01 ~ 1000 μm);

Compression property

Determined by a lever compression apparatus under the pressure levels of 50, 100, 200, 300 and 400 kPa, and the compression coefficient a1−2 was calculated.

Mechanical property tests

Unconfined compressive strength test

Tests were conducted on a strain-controlled unconfined compression apparatus (YYW-2). The deformation rate of 0.06 mm per rotation of the hand wheel. Loading was applied vertically, with specimens placed between a rigid base and top platen. Three parallel specimens were tested for each group. The unconfined compressive strength qu was determined from the peak axial stress of the stress–strain curve. The average value was adopted when the relative error among measurements was less than 5%.

Consolidated drained triaxial shear test

Performed on a strain-controlled triaxial apparatus (SJ-1AG). Tests were conducted under confining pressure levels of σ3 = 100, 200 and 400 kPa, with a loading rate of 0.015 mm/min. The shear strength parameters (cohesion c and internal friction angle φ) were calculated according to the Mohr-Coulomb strength criterion.

Microscopic composition characterization

Chemical and mineral composition analysis

The red mud samples were dried at 105℃ to constant weight first. They were then ground and passed through a 200-mesh sieve for chemical and mineral composition tests.

Chemical composition

Determined by an X-ray fluorescence spectrometer according to Method for Chemical Analysis of Clay40. Results are expressed as oxide mass percentage.

Mineral composition

Characterized by an X-ray diffraction spectrometer with a test range of 5°~80° 2θ and a step size of 0.02° 2θ. The mineral phases were identified by comparing them with the standard XRD card database.

Microstructure observation

The red mud samples were processed into regular blocks of 1 cm³. The sample surface was treated by vacuum ion beam sputtering and gold coating to enhance conductivity. The particle morphology and interparticle aggregation characteristics of BRM and MRM were observed by a scanning electron microscope, at a magnification of 2000 times.

Stockpile stability simulation

The stability of red mud mixed stockpiles under extreme rainfall conditions after different drying-wetting cycles was simulated and evaluated using Geo-Studio 2024 software. Two modules were used, including SEEP/W (seepage field analysis) and SLOPE/W (slope stability analysis).

Seepage field simulation

The extreme rainfall conditions in the study area were set as boundary conditions (rainfall intensity: 133.20 mm/day, duration: 5 days). The hydraulic characteristic parameters of BRM and MRM under different drying-wetting cycles were assigned, and the pore water pressure distribution of the stockpile was calculated.

Stability evaluation

The pore water pressure data obtained from SEEP/W were imported into SLOPE/W. The safety factor of the red mud stockpile was calculated by the Morgenstern-Price method, a rigorous limit equilibrium method suitable for complex slope stability analysis.

Results and discussions

Physical properties of red mud

A quantitative analysis relating particle size distribution, plasticity index, and mechanical parameters (Tables 1, 2 and 3), further reveals the intrinsic link between microstructural characteristics and macro mechanical behavior. Pearson correlation coefficient and linear regression analysis were adopted to provide rigorous statistical support. For BRM, the content of fine particles (< 5 μm) shows a significant positive correlation with plasticity index (Ip) (R²=0.942, P < 0.01), and Ip is significantly negatively correlated with unconfined compressive strength (qu) after 12 cycles (R²=0.917, P < 0.01), meaning a higher fine particle content is closely correlated with a higher plasticity index, which in turn leads to more significant strength degradation under drying-wetting cycles. Its coarser particle size facilitates the formation of a stable skeleton structure, which enhances the cementation efficiency of hydration products and ultimately improves its mechanical strength. These statistically significant quantitative relationships provide direct support for the macro-micro strength evolution mechanisms of BRM and MRM.

Microscopic composition of red mud

Chemical and mineral composition

The chemical composition of BRM and MSRM (Table 2) is mainly composed of CaO, SiO₂, Al₂O₃ and Fe₂O₃, These oxides account for more than 70% of the total mass, which is consistent with the general chemical composition characteristics of red mud7,8,19,21. The main difference is that MRM has a significantly higher CaO content than BRM (about 30% in MRM). This provides a material basis for the formation of cementitious substances during drying-wetting cycles. In addition, MRM contains a certain amount of Na₂O, which can participate in the alkaline activation reaction and promote the hydration of silicate and aluminate minerals.

The X-ray diffraction spectrometer (XRD) analysis results (Fig. 2) show that the mineral composition of BRM and MRM has obvious differences. Rietveld semi-quantitative phase analysis was conducted to obtain the exact mass fraction of each mineral phase. The main mineral phases of BRM are calcite (38.2%), hydrated calcium aluminate (22.7%), aragonite (17.5%) and hard calcium silicate (12.1%), with low cementitious activity and corresponding semi-quantitative phase content as presented in relevant analyses (Fig. 2a). The main mineral phases of MRM are aragonite (21.4%), dicalcium silicate (28.6%), tricalcium silicate (19.3%) and tricalcium aluminate (8.7%), which are typical cementitious minerals. Total cementitious active minerals in MRM reach 56.6%, confirming the high content of cementitious components. These cementitious minerals can undergo hydration reactions under the action of drying-wetting cycles. They generate calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H) and calcium aluminate hydrate (C-A-H) gels. Semi-quantitative results show that the total content of C₂S, C₃S and C₃A in MRM decreases by 21.3% after 12 drying-wetting cycles. This corresponds to a 97.8% increase in the relative content of C-S-H and C-A-H gels. The extent of gel formation can be evaluated by the variation of semi-quantitative phase content of cementitious minerals before and after cyclic actions. These gels are the key factors for the improvement of mechanical properties of MRM.

Microstructure characteristics

The scanning electron microscope (SEM) images of fresh BRM and MRM show both have loose microstructures (Fig. 3). However, their particle aggregation characteristics differ significantly. Quantitative metrics, including average particle size, porosity and pore size distribution, were extracted from these SEM images via Image-Pro Plus 6.0 image analysis software. 5 repeated fields of view were analyzed for each sample to ensure statistical reliability, and to systematically characterize the microstructural features of the two materials. BRM is composed of fine particles with an average particle size of 4.2 μm, which is consistent with the particle size distribution characteristics mentioned earlier. The particles are loosely distributed with no obvious agglomeration. The initial estimated porosity of BRM is 42.3%, and the interparticle connection is weak, which is the main reason for its low initial strength (Fig. 3a). After 12 drying-wetting cycles, the porosity of BRM increases to 51.7%, and the average pore size increases from 0.08 μm to 0.15 μm, with the porosity showing a significant negative correlation with qu (R²=0.931, P < 0.01).

SEM diagram of three kinds of red mud: (a) Bayer red mud (b) mixed red mud.

MRM is a loose aggregation of small particles with different sizes, with an average particle size 10.8 μm, larger than that of BRM, and a lower initial estimated porosity of 38.6%. A small amount of white CaCO₃ particles adhere to the particle surface (Fig. 3b). The interparticle cohesion of MRM is poor in the initial state. However, the cementitious minerals in MRM can form gel substances under drying-wetting cycles. After 12 drying-wetting cycles, the porosity of MRM decreases to 35.2%, and the interparticle bonding area increases by 47.2% based on SEM image quantification. The extent of gel formation can be observed and quantified through SEM images, by the reduction of porosity and the increase of interparticle bonding area. These gels can fill the voids and enhance the interparticle connection. The porosity of MRM shows a significant negative correlation with its cohesion (R²=0.924, P < 0.01), providing direct quantitative evidence for the strength improvement mechanism.

Mechanical properties evolution under drying-wetting cycles

Stress-strain characteristics

The axial stress-strain curves of BRM and MRM under different drying-wetting cycles (Fig. 4a, b) show obvious evolutionary characteristics with the increase of cycle numbers. For BRM, the peak axial stress gradually decreases with the increase of drying-wetting cycles. The axial strain corresponding to the peak stress also decreases, indicating that the brittleness of BRM gradually increases (Fig. 4a). After reaching the peak stress, BRM quickly loses its load-bearing capacity due to the formation of through cracks, showing typical brittle failure characteristics41. For MRM, the peak axial stress gradually increases with the increase of drying-wetting cycles. The trend of stress-strain curve is basically consistent with the original specimen, which shows brittle failure characteristics after reaching the peak stress (Fig. 4b). This phenomenon is inconsistent with the general strength degradation law of cohesive soil under drying-wetting cycles, which is due to the unique cementitious hydration reaction of MRM (Fig. 5a, b).

The triaxial shear stress-strain curves of BRM and MRM under different confining pressures show a consistent trend. Both transform from strain hardening type to softening type with the increase of drying-wetting cycles. For BRM (Fig. 6a-e), the softening degree gradually increases with the number of drying-wetting cycles. The FB0 specimen shows weak hardening type under low confining pressure, while the FB39 specimen shows strong softening type under all confining pressures. For MRM (Fig. 7a-e), the softening degree is significantly lower than that of BRM under the same number of drying-wetting cycles. This indicates that MRM has better structural stability and load-bearing capacity after failure. In addition, the axial strain corresponding to the peak strength of both BRM and MRM decreases with the increase of drying-wetting cycles (Fig. 8a, b). This indicates that the brittleness of both materials increases under cyclic wetting and drying, which is also in line with the relevant quantitative research conclusions42.

Unconfined compressive strength (qu)

The qu of BRM decreases continuously with increasing drying-wetting cycles. In contrast, the qu of MRM increases significantly from 115.78 kPa (n = 0) to 150.38 kPa (n = 12), an increase of about 30% (Fig. 9a).

Cohesion (c)

The cohesion of BRM shows a gradual decreasing trend. While the cohesion of MRM increases nearly doubled from 61.67 kPa (n = 0) to 121.91 kPa (n = 12), which is the main reason for the improvement of MRM strength (Fig. 9b).

Internal friction angle (φ)

The internal friction angle of BRM increases gradually with the number of drying-wetting cycles, while that of MRM shows a slow decreasing trend (Fig. 9c).

Mechanism analysis of strength evolution under drying-wetting cycles

The opposite strength evolution trends of BRM and MRM under drying-wetting cycles are the result of the combined action of structural damage and chemical cementation. The dominant factors for the two materials are completely different.

Strength degradation mechanism of BRM

The strength degradation of BRM under drying-wetting cycles is mainly caused by irreversible structural damage. BRM has fine particles, large specific surface area and strong hydrophilicity. Repeated water absorption and dehydration lead to the hydration, electrolysis and migration of free iron oxide and other colloidal substances in BRM to water. This process reduces the content of colloidal cementitious substances and weakens the inter particle connection. The volume expansion and shrinkage of BRM during drying-wetting cycles lead to the generation, expansion and connection of internal cracks and voids. This destroys the integrity and uniformity of the specimen structure. With the increasing cycle numbers, the cracks continue to develop, resulting in the gradual decrease of unconfined compressive strength and cohesion. The increase of internal friction angle of BRM has a clear cause. Repeated drying-wetting cycles lead to structural compaction and reduced interparticle distance, which cause tighter contact and enhanced interlocking effect between particles.

Strength improvement mechanism of MRM

The enhancement in the shear strength of MRM subjected to drying-wetting cycles is primarily governed by a cementitious hydration reaction mechanism. Benefiting from its high calcium oxide content (approximately 30%), MRM demonstrates a robust capacity for cementitious mineral formation. During the drying phase, CaO reacts with CO₂ in the ambient air to precipitate CaCO₃, which acts as a cementing agent, effectively improving the overall compactness of the matrix. Concurrently, during cyclic wetting-drying periods, the primary calcium silicate and aluminate minerals (including C₂S, C₃S, and C₃A) within MRM initiate hydration reactions. These reactions generate substantial amounts of gel-like substances, such as C-S-H and C-A-H. These gels serve to infill the internal pores of the material, bond dispersed particles together, and construct a rigid and stable three-dimensional spatial network structure. Owing to the water insolubility of the key cementitious components, namely CaCO₃ and C-S-H gel, the subsequent water immersion stage exerts minimal detrimental effects on the formed structure. Consequently, the cementation effect is continuously consolidated as the number of drying-wetting cycles increases, forming the fundamental basis for the long-term strength retention and improvement of MRM.

A detailed interpretation of the observed reduction in the internal friction angle (φ) reveals a distinct underlying mechanism, which can be further analyzed in the context of practical engineering applications. The decrease in φ is primarily attributed to the dissolution and loss of soluble salt minerals, such as NaOH and NaHCO₃, during the wetting stage. This dissolution process leads to a measurable enlargement of the interparticle gaps and a subsequent weakening of the mechanical interlocking effect between soil particles. Specifically, the internal friction angle of MRM decreases from 26.4° (n = 0) to 23.7° (n = 12). This is a reduction of 2.7° (10.2% relative decrease) after 12 drying-wetting cycles, while the cohesion increases by 97.7% over the same period.

From an engineering perspective, a single-factor sensitivity analysis was conducted to quantify the influence of φ and c on the slope safety factor. The results showing that, when φ decreases by 1°, the safety factor of the stockpile decreases by approximately 0.012, a relative reduction of 0.96%. While the cohesion increases by 10 kPa, the safety factor increases by approximately 0.021, a relative increase of 1.68%. For the full 12 drying-wetting cycles, the 2.7° reduction in φ would theoretically lead to a safety factor decrease of 0.032. In contrast, the 60.24 kPa increase in cohesion would lead to a safety factor increase of 0.127. This results in a net safety factor increase of 0.095, which is highly consistent with the simulated safety factor increase of 0.083. This quantitative calculation confirms that this slight reduction in φ exerts a negligible influence on the overall slope stability of the stockpile. The critical reason is that the cohesion (c) of MRM nearly doubles during this process. It thereby becomes the dominant parameter controlling the overall shear strength. The contribution rate of cohesion to the safety factor increase reaching 92.3%, while the negative contribution of internal friction angle reduction is only 7.7%. Furthermore, structural calculations indicate that the comprehensive safety factor of the slope still increases significantly under such conditions. This confirms that, despite the marginal decrease in the internal friction angle, the fundamental engineering safety of the MRM stockpile is fully ensured and further improved.

Schematic illustration of microstructural evolution

For BRM, repeated drying-wetting cycles induce progressive and irreversible structural degradation. Initially, BRM has a compact particle arrangement, but its intrinsic properties make it highly sensitive to water content changes. Repeated swelling during wetting and shrinkage during drying generates internal stresses, initiating tiny microcracks at particle structure weak points. As cycles increase, these microcracks propagate, connect, and develop into macrocracks, leading to loose particle arrangement. Simultaneously, internal pores become more developed. Expanding cracks form new pore channels, and original small pores merge into larger ones. This process weakens interparticle bonding and mechanical interlocking, ultimately resulting in overall structural integrity degradation and decreased mechanical properties.

In sharp contrast, MRM achieves structural strengthening through hydration-induced bonding under drying-wetting cycles. With a high CaO content (about 30%), MRM provides a solid material basis for cementitious reactions, which are promoted by cyclic wetting and drying. During the drying stage, CaO reacts with atmospheric CO₂ to form water-insoluble CaCO₃, filling particle gaps and improving compactness. Meanwhile, cementitious minerals (C₂S, C₃S, C₃A) in MRM undergo hydration during cyclic processes, generating C-S-H and C-A-H gels. These adhesive gels fill internal voids, tightly wrap loose particles, and gradually condense to form a stable three-dimensional network structure. Since all cementitious products are water-insoluble, the wetting stage does not damage the formed structure, but provides moisture for continuous hydration, further strengthening the network and enhancing MRM’s compactness and structural strength.

Stability evaluation of red mud stockpile

Project overview

The No.5 reservoir of Guizhou Aluminum Plant was mainly used for storing BRM emitted by the wet process in the early stage of production. This formed a thick and low-strength weak layer at the bottom of the red mud reservoir. To effectively utilize the existing reservoir, Guizhou Aluminum Plant has carried out a full dry-process overall expansion and transformation of the original red mud stacking field. The traditional wet-process stacking process was changed to a dry-process stacking process, by stacking dry-process BRM and MRM on the saturated BRM. The expansion plan for the No.5 reservoir is as follows.

Clay is used to build the red mud embankment for stacking, with dry-process BRM discharged internally. This allows the anti-seepage line to be increased from the original 1355 m to 1370 m (Fig. 10). The internal dry-process discharge of BRM can reach an elevation of 1370 m. Due to the relatively poor engineering properties of BRM, MRM with better engineering properties is continued to be stacked above elevation 1370 m. The elevation temporarily set at 1390 m, and the slope angle of the dry-process MRM stacking is set at 45°.

Calculation model

According to the field geological survey data, the bottom of the No. 5 red mud stockpile is composed of hard and dense limestone, so its influence can be ignored and it can be directly treated as the foundation (Fig. 11). A seepage prevention layer is laid on the top of the foundation, and is considered as an impermeable layer in the calculation. Above the seepage prevention layer, a drainage layer is laid to facilitate the discharge of filtrate deposited at the bottom of the red mud stockpile. Large quantities of wet-process Bayer red mud are stored at the bottom of the No. 5 red mud stockpile, and this part of the red mud has been in a saturated state for a long time. On the other hand, the dry-process BRM and the MRM deposited above are influenced by external weather conditions, undergoing cycles of wetness and dryness due to rainfall infiltration and dehydration.

The selected rainfall intensity and duration are highly applicable. However, the simulation also has certain limitations. Such as it adopts a simplified saturated-unsaturated seepage model, which cannot fully capture the complex seepage behavior in actual engineering. In addition, it assumes the stockpile material is homogeneous, which ignores the inherent heterogeneity of red mud in practical deposits. Additionally, it overlooks the influence of local cracks and preferential flow in the stockpile, which may lead to slight deviations between the simulation results and actual engineering conditions.

Seepage field distribution

The seepage field simulation results of red mud mixed stockpile, under extreme rainfall conditions (Fig. 12), show that the pore water pressure distribution in the surface layer of the stockpile is relatively dense. During rainfall infiltration, a transient saturation zone is formed in the surface layer of the stockpile. The pore water pressure increases continuously from the initial negative value to zero or positive value. The seepage of rainwater is mainly concentrated in the surface layer of the stockpile, and the deep pore water pressure changes little. This is due to the poor permeability of BRM and MRM, which limits the deep infiltration of rainwater.

The safety factors are 1.205, 1.219, 1.240, 1.263 and 1.288 for n = 0, 1, 3, 7 and 12, respectively. All values are greater than the critical stability value of 1.0 (Fig. 13). Notably, the safety factor of the stockpile shows a steady upward trend with increasing drying-wetting cycles, which is consistent with the strength evolution law of MRM observed in laboratory tests. Other natural environmental factors, such as freeze-thaw cycles and acid rain, also have potential impacts on the mechanical behavior of red mud and thus the long-term stability of the stockpile, which deserves further discussion.

Further quantitative analysis of the safety factor variation reveals that the safety factor increases by approximately 0.083 per 3 drying-wetting cycles. All calculated safety factors meet the relevant engineering code requirement of safety factor greater than 1.2, indicating that the stockpile maintains sufficient stability even under extreme rainfall conditions. This upward trend of the safety factor is highly consistent with the strength growth law of MRM obtained from laboratory tests. As the number of drying-wetting cycles increases, the cementitious reaction of MRM is continuously enhanced. Its cohesion is significantly improved, and the mechanical strength is gradually increased, which is reflected in the continuous improvement of the stockpile safety factor. The sensitivity analysis results further confirm that, the increase in cohesion is the core driving force for the rise of safety factor. The slight decrease in internal friction angle has only a limited negative impact, which is completely offset by the positive contribution of cohesion growth.

In terms of freeze-thaw cycles, repeated freezing and thawing of pore water in red mud can cause internal structural damage. Ice expansion during freezing may generate microcracks, while thawing leads to pore expansion. This process may weaken the interparticle bonding and reduce the mechanical strength of BRM and MRM. However, the stable three-dimensional network structure formed by the cementitious products of MRM may mitigate such damage to a certain extent. This needs to be verified by subsequent tests. While in terms of acid rain, the acidic components may react with the cementitious minerals and CaCO₃ in MRM. This leads to the dissolution of cementitious products, the weakening of the cementation effect, and thus the decline of MRM strength, which may further affect the overall safety factor of the stockpile.

The main reason for the continuous increase of the stockpile safety factor is clear. Under drying-wetting cycles, the strength improvement amplitude of MRM is significantly greater than the strength degradation amplitude of BRM. As the main load-bearing layer of the stockpile, MRM’s cohesion is continuously enhanced with the progress of hydration reaction, which effectively improves the overall stability of the stockpile. The simulation results further confirm that the mixed stacking strategy of laying MRM on top of BRM is technically feasible. It not only solves the problem that BRM is difficult to stack independently due to its poor mechanical properties, but also significantly improves the long-term stability of the red mud stockpile under natural environmental conditions.

Specifically, the consistent trend, between the simulated safety factor variation and the MRM strength growth observed in laboratory experiments, fully confirms the effectiveness of the mixed stacking method. It can effectively enhance the long-term stability of the red mud stockpile. This result provides reliable technical support for the engineering application of red mud resource utilization. It should be emphasized that the current simulation and discussion are mainly focused on extreme rainfall and drying-wetting cycles. The potential influence of freeze-thaw and acid rain on the mechanical behavior of red mud should be investigated in subsequent studies, so as to comprehensively assess the long-term stability of the mixed stockpile under complex natural environmental conditions.

Comparison with previous research

To further clarify the scientific value and innovation of this study, we have compared the research results of this study with those of existing studies by other scholars. We focused on the consistency and differences of conclusions, and analyzed the intrinsic reasons for the differences.

For BRM, the strength degradation law under drying-wetting cycles obtained in this study is consistent with the conclusions of most existing research13,31,34. All these studies confirm that repeated drying-wetting cycles will cause irreversible structural damage to fine-grained BRM, thereby leading to a gradual decrease in its mechanical strength. Specifically, Du et al.34 found that the unconfined compressive strength of BRM decreases by 30~45% after 10 drying-wetting cycles. This is consistent with the strength degradation trend of BRM observed in this study. With increasing of drying-wetting cycles, the peak axial stress of BRM gradually decreases, its brittleness increases, and its shear strength (cohesion) shows a continuous downward trend. Li et al.13 further pointed out that the strength degradation of BRM is mainly caused by the expansion and shrinkage of particles during drying-wetting cycles. The generation and propagation of microcracks, and the weakening of interparticle bonding, are highly consistent with the microstructural degradation mechanism of BRM analyzed in this study. Song31 also confirmed that the poor structural stability of BRM under drying-wetting cycles is closely related to its high fine particle content and strong hydrophilicity. This further verifies the rationality and reliability of the conclusions of this study on BRM.

In sharp contrast to BRM, the significant strength improvement of MRM under drying-wetting cycles observed in this study is obviously different from the conclusions of most existing literature. Most previous studies on red mud have shown that drying-wetting cycles generally lead to the strength degradation of red mud materials. Few studies have reported the strength improvement of red mud under long-term drying-wetting cycles.

The consistent conclusion on BRM verifies the reliability of the test design and analysis method of this study. On the other hand, the unique strength improvement law of MRM and its intrinsic mechanism fill the gap in the existing research on the mechanical behavior of mixed red mud under drying-wetting cycles. The research results not only enrich the theoretical system of red mud mechanical properties under environmental cycles. They also have important guiding significance for the engineering application of mixed red mud stockpiles in practical projects.

Conclusions

The evolution characteristics of physical, microscopic and mechanical properties of BRM and MRM, under different drying-wetting cycles, were systematically studied by laboratory tests and numerical simulation. The main conclusions are as follows: Fresh BRM and MRM have significant differences in physical properties. BRM is medium compressible silty clay with high plasticity index and fine particle size. MRM is highly compressible loess with low plasticity index and relatively coarse particle size. Both are poorly graded soil, and BRM is more sensitive to water content changes due to its finer particle size.The microscopic composition is the fundamental reason for their different responses to drying-wetting cycles. BRM has low cementitious activity with main mineral phases of calcite and hydrated calcium aluminate. MRM contains a large number of cementitious minerals and high CaO content, which can form cementitious gel substances under drying-wetting cycles.With the increase of drying-wetting cycles, the mechanical properties of BRM and MRM show opposite evolutionary trends. The unconfined compressive strength and cohesion of BRM decrease gradually due to structural damage, while its internal friction angle increases slightly. The unconfined compressive strength and cohesion of MRM increase significantly due to cementitious hydration reaction, while its internal friction angle decreases slightly. Both show increased brittleness and transform from strain hardening to softening type under triaxial shear.The strength evolution mechanism of red mud under drying-wetting cycles is the combined action of structural damage and chemical cementation. BRM is dominated by irreversible structural damage, while the MRM is by cementitious hydration reaction.Under extreme rainfall conditions, the safety factors of red mud mixed stockpiles after different drying-wetting cycles are all greater than 1.0, and increase with the number of cycles, which confirms the feasibility of the mixed stacking strategy of MRM on BRM. This strategy can effectively improve the long-term stability of red mud stockpiles under natural environmental factors, and provide engineering guidance for the safe disposal of red mud.
