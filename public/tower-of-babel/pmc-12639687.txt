A comparative immunohistochemical and histomorphometric analysis of human gingiva in smokers and non-smokers with periodontitis : a cross-sectional study
Authors: Saravanan Sampoornam Pape Reddy, Delfin Lovelina Francis, Dibyajyoti Boruah, Akash Kumar Giri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis cross-sectional study aimed to investigate histological, immunohistochemical, and histomorphometric differences in gingival epithelial thickness, vascular elements, and connective tissue in smokers vs. non-smokers with Stage II Grade B generalized periodontitis.MethodsSixty gingival tissue samples [smokers (n = 30) and non-smokers (n = 30)] were harvested from patients undergoing modified Widman flap surgery. All participants were systemically healthy, with probing pocket depth (PPD) ≤ 5 mm and clinical attachment loss of 3–4 mm. Histological analysis was performed using hematoxylin & eosin staining, Masson’s trichrome staining, and immunohistochemical staining with CD34 and CD105. Morphometric evaluations, including epithelial thickness, epithelial cell and nuclear area, collagen fibre density, and microvascular density, were conducted using an image analysis software. Statistical comparisons between groups were analyzed using unpaired t-tests, Pearson correlation and multivariate linear regression with p-values < 0.05 considered significant.ResultsSmokers exhibited significantly increased gingival epithelial thickness (p = 0.001), epithelial cell area (p < 0.001), and nuclear area (p < 0.0001) compared with non-smokers. The collagen fibre density was reduced in smokers (p < 0.0001). Microvascular density (MVD) was did not differ in smokers (p = 0.001), but vascular calibre [CD34, (p = 0.89)] and neovascular potential [CD105, (p = 0.99)] were comparable between groups.ConclusionSmoking induces significant histological and vascular changes in the gingiva, including epithelial thickening, reduced collagen density, and decreased microvascular density, while maintaining the neovascular potential. These findings highlight smoking as a critical modulator of periodontal tissue pathogenesis, and reinforce the importance of smoking cessation in mitigating periodontal disease progression.Graphical Abstract The study identified increased epithelial thickness, decreased microvascular density, and changes to collagen morphology in smokers compared to non-smokers with periodontitis. Nevertheless, potential neovascularization is similar to that of non-smokers, indicating a restoration of wound healing capacity following smoking cessations, as it remains important in periodontal therapy Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07233-2.Keywords: Gingiva, Gingival disease, Histological techniques, Periodontitis, Smoking

Clinical relevance

Background

Smoking is well established that it leads to periodontitis progression and severity. Risk for attachment loss, alveolar bone resorption and tooth loss are greater in smokers than non-smokers. The association between smoking and its role in periodontitis is well established; however, the histopathological and immunohistochemistry changes in the gingiva are still under investigation.

Added value of this study

This study demonstrates histological, histomorphometric, and immunohistochemical assessment of gingival tissues in smokers and non-smokers with stage II grade B generalized periodontitis. Notably, this study demonstrates that even in the presence of significant gingival tissue changes, the neovascular potential of the gingiva among smokers and non-smokers are comparable, thereby providing further evidence of the potential impact of smoking cessation on periodontal tissue healing. These findings can help develop focused therapeutic strategies for the management of periodontal disease in smokers.

Clinical implication

The findings of this study emphasize the significant impact of smoking on periodontal tissues, particularly in terms of epithelial thickening, reduced microvascular density, and altered collagen fibre morphology. However, the preserved neovascular potential in smokers indicates that periodontal healing can still be optimized after smoking cessation. Clinically, this highlights the importance of incorporating smoking cessation programs into periodontal treatment plans to improve therapeutic outcomes.

Introduction

Periodontal diseases are one of the most chronic inflammatory diseases, and their etiopathogenesis multifactorial in nature. The polymicrobial nature of periodontitis has been proven to be the initiating agent, but there are numerous other factors that increase susceptibility to periodontal disease. One such important risk factor that has been investigated for decades is smoking. Studies showed that there is high prevalence of periodontal disease and tooth mortality in smokers. The extent and duration of smoking is directly proportional to disease severity in the periodontal tissues. The risk reduces as the patient quits smoking showing that there is a lesser risk in former smokers than current smokers [1]. Although smoking decreases the clinical presentation of gingival inflammation, there is a strong association between periodontal diseases and smoking [2]. The classical study of gingivitis conducted in smokers vs. non-smokers showed that there is a similar rate of plaque formation in smokers compared to non-smokers [3]. Previous studies showed a rationale for the pathogenesis of periodontal diseases and negative effects of smoking. Furthermore, there are significant changes in the subgingival environment with reduced oxygen tension, leading to proliferation of bacteria in a favourable niche [4]. Hence, smoking triggers an anaerobic subgingival prevalence in the pockets, which leads to increased susceptibility to periodontal diseases. The by-products of tobacco smoke which originate due to oxidation histologically modify the tissues leading to progression of periodontitis [5].

The reduction and masking of clinical inflammation in smokers is attributed to cotinine, proven to exert vasoconstriction on the gingival microvasculature. The reduced intensity of the gingival response is due to the reduction in vascular components or gingival epithelial changes [6]. It is known that tobacco use has a direct and causal relationship with cardiovascular diseases and lung cancers [7]. Ample evidence attributes smoking to higher calculus deposition, more deep pockets, significant attachment loss and radiographic alveolar bone loss [8]. Initial histological studies to assess smoking-induced gingival changes were based on the qualitative changes in vasculature [9]. With advancement of histopathological techniques using immunohistochemistry, quantitative changes can also be evaluated. The present study was therefore aimed at comparisons with of the gingival tissues from periodontitis of smokers and non-smokers; with the intention to define the smoking-related modifications in the thickness of the epithelium, the organization of the connective tissue as well as the nature of vascularity.

Materials and methods

Study design and sampling method

This study aimed to compare histological, immunohistochemical, and histomorphometric changes in the epithelium, connective tissue, and vascular elements of human gingiva in smokers vs. non-smokers with periodontitis. The study was an ex-vivo analytical cross-sectional design conducted over a period of twelve months and was approved by the Institutional Research Board and Institutional Ethical Committee vide Medical Research Cell letter no IEC/13 JAN 2023 dated 13 Jan 2023 and the study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. The study details were explained to each participant and written informed consent was taken from all the participants. Patients indicated for elective periodontal surgery were treated according to their treatment requirements. Sixty tissue samples were harvested from 60 patients who were enrolled in the study by applying following selection criteria. Patients of either gender, aged 25–55 years, diagnosed with stage II grade B periodontitis, systemically healthy, and willing to participate were included. Patients undergoing modified Widman flap procedure, with PPD ≤ 5 mm, CAL of 3–4 mm, smokers (less than 10 cigarettes per day) and those who never smoked in their life time for non-smoker group. The exclusion criteria included patients who underwent oral prophylaxis within six months, history of systemic conditions, use of smokeless tobacco, use of medications including supplements, pregnant and lactating women and patients on systemic hormonal therapy.

Sample and sample size

The calculated sample size for the hypothesis, Ho: π1- π2 = 0 against H1: π1- π2 > 0 with α = 0.05% (5%), power (1-β) as 80%, and π1 = 85% and π2 = 55% based on previously published literature. All hypothesis tests were two-tailed and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Thus, the minimum required sample size required was 28 per group. In this study, 60 tissue samples were analyzed (smokers, n = 30; non-smokers n = 30). Mean epithelial thickness (MET) was chosen as the primary endpoint, as the primary objective of the study is to quantify smoking-mediated epithelial remodeling, whereas other histomorphometric and vascular indices are considered as key secondary tissue-level endpoints. Post-hoc power and effect size calculations were carried out for the primary outcome measure (Cohen’s d ≈ 0.87 and the achieved power for a two-sided t-test with n = 30 per group was 0.91). Patients were selected by convenience sampling; already diagnosed with stage II grade B generalized periodontitis and indicated for periodontal surgery. Group A comprised gingival tissue samples (n = 30) harvested from non-smokers undergoing periodontal surgical procedures, and Group B comprised gingival tissue samples (n = 30) harvested from patients who were smokers. From each patient, one gingival tissue sample was harvested from the surgical site and processed.

Clinical procedures

In the selected patients, detailed case histories were recorded and full-mouth clinical examination was undertaken for each subject by a single examiner (S.S.P). The measured clinical parameters included, pocket probing depth (PPD), clinical attachment level (CAL), plaque index (PlI), gingival index (GI), and periodontal disease index (PDI). Both the groups, consisted patients with periodontitis with or without smoking, underwent phase I periodontal therapy, comprising of oral prophylaxis with subgingival instrumentation and chemical plaque control with 0.2% chlorhexidine gluconate. The patients were placed on maintenance therapy for one month, after which they were re-evaluated and taken up for periodontal flap surgery, as indicated. During modified Widman flap surgery, the marginal gingival tissues excised as a part of the surgery were collected as the required tissue samples. The pathologist was blinded about the groups from which the samples were taken. The processing was performed as per protocol by Rhodes [10] for tissue fixation, Hematoxylin & Eosin staining and Masson’s Trichrome staining [11] as previously described. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) protocol was performed as described by Nakane [12] using Avidin-Biotin-Complex (ABC) method. The stained sections were imaged using digital photomicrography, and morphometry was carried out using an Olympus Microscope with Biowizard 4.2 vers image analyzing software to standardize the histomorphometry. The histological parameters included the thickness of the gingival epithelium (Fig. 1), histomorphometry of gingival epithelial cells and nuclear morphometry (Fig. 2), histomorphometry of collagen fibres, and histomorphometry of vascular elements using CD34 and CD105. Histomorphometry was carried out using CD-34 and CD105 (Fig. 3) immunostained sections with digital photomicrograph. The calibration of the measuring scale of the image analysis software was done using standardized scale as per manufacturer’s instruction. Images were processed using the software provided to obtain sharp microvessel boundary before performing measurements (Fig. 4). After IHC with CD34 and CD105, hotspots were identified where these markers were constitutively expressed in the endothelial cells. High-power fields (400X) of at least five areas were captured as hotspot areas. The branching structures were actually counted as single vessels, except when there was a breach in continuity of the structure. A single microvessel was defined by the presence of brown-immunostained endothelial cells which are separated from adjacent microvessels and other tissues. The microvessel density was calculated in five fields in the hotspots at 400x i.e. area of 0.289 mm². The cross-section of the microvessel was measured as the vascular caliber (VC). In histological sections, a microvessel can be cut in any direction. Hence, it may appear in different patterns such as circular, elliptical, parallel lines, solid bar and also as an irregular structure. When the microvessel was a solid bar, then its thickness was taken as its VC. The percentage of total microvessel area (MVA) was calculated by measuring all MVA in five 400x sections and dividing it by the total area of five hotspots. Microvessel density (MVD in mm2) at 100x magnification was calculated. After applying the microgrid in the software, the area was divided into four equal parts of 100µm2 beneath the epithelium, and the number of microvascular elements stained with CD34 was counted. The vessel lumen area was calculated using a digital cursor, and the software automatically calculated VC in µm2. A protocol similar to that described above for CD34 was used to analyze vascular elements after staining with CD105.

Statistical analysis

The study consisted of two groups with 30 samples per group. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS for Windows version 29.0, Microsoft Office (SPSS, 2023.SPSS Inc, New York). The statistical significance was prior set at p < 0.05. A descriptive analysis was performed to explore demographic characteristic distribution. Mean ± standard deviation (SD) across the two groups was used to present the continuous variables. The qualitative data of sex distribution were studied using the chi-square test, and unpaired t-test was utilized to compare the quantitative data. The statistical significance of clinical variables including PPD (mm), CAL (mm), PlI, GI, PDI Scores and histological variables; Mean Epithelial Thickness [(MET) µm], Epithelial Base Thickness [(EBT) µm], MVD CD34 (Count), Epithelial Cell Area [(ECA) µm2], NA (µm2), Vascular Caliber (VC) with CD34 (µm2), Collagen Fibre Morphometry MTC (µm2), Neovascular Caliber with (NVC) CD105 (µm2) values were analyzed by mean ± SD and by unpaired ‘t’ test by confirming the underlying normality assumption being quantitative parameters with a sample size of 30 per group. Normality was assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk/Mann-Whitney U test. Pearson correlation analysis and multivariate linear regression analyses were carried out to find the potential effect of confounders including age and gender.

Results

Study participant attributes

The mean ± SD age of smokers was 46.3 ± 14.6 and of non-smokers was 32.3 ± 13.6. The average age was significantly higher in smokers compared to non-smokers (p-value < 0.001). The gender distribution differed significantly between the two groups (p-value < 0.001). The entire group (100.0%) of smokers were males and large majority (63.3%) of females formed part of non-smoker group (Supplementary Table 1).

Clinical outcomes

The mean PPD in smokers was 5.6 ± 1.10 and in non-smokers was 5.0 ± 1.01. The mean PPD was significantly higher in smokers than in non-smokers (p = 0.02). The mean CAL in smokers was 3.8 ± 0.8 and in non-smokers was 2.9 ± 0.9; which was significantly higher in smokers (p = 0.0003). The mean PlI in smokers and non-smokers was 1.7 ± 0.7 and 1.6 ± 0.7, respectively and did not differ between smokers and non-smokers (p = 0.45). The mean GI in smokers and non-smokers were 1.8 ± 0.7 and 1.9 ± 0.7 respectively. The mean GI did not differ between smokers and non-smokers (p = 0.72). The mean PDI values in smokers was 1.9 ± 0.8 and in non-smokers was 2.1 ± 0.8 and did not differ between smokers and non-smokers [(p = 0.43) (Fig. 5)].

Histological outcomes

The MET in smokers was 388.5 ± 92.9 μm and non-smokers was 321.9 ± 55.5 μm. The MET was significantly higher in smokers than non-smokers (p < 0.001). The EBT in smokers and non-smokers were 210.9 ± 68.4 μm and 172.7 ± 41.2 μm, respectively and was significantly higher in smokers (p = 0.01). The MVD using CD 34 in smokers was 31.6 ± 15.3 and in non-smokers was 58.7 ± 12.3; and was significantly higher in non-smokers (p < 0.001). The mean ECA in smokers and in non-smokers groups were 7051.3 ± 1586.7 µm2 and 2208.0 ± 776.8 µm2 and was significantly higher in smokers (p < 0.0001). The mean NA in smokers was 1638.0 ± 537.9 µm2 and non-smokers was 567.9 ± 165.3 µm2 and was significantly higher in smokers (p < 0.0001). The mean VC using CD 34 in smokers and non-smokers groups were 7.7 ± 1.9 µm2 and 7.7 ± 1.4 µm2 respectively and did not differ between two groups (p = 0.89). The collagen fibre morphometry using MT staining in smokers and non-smokers groups were 28.0 ± 5.2 µm2 and 40.9 ± 6.4 µm2 respectively and was significantly higher in non-smokers (p < 0.0001). The mean NVC in smokers was 7.1 ± 1.4 µm2 and that in non-smokers was 7.1 ± 1.2 µm2 and did not differ between two groups (p = 0.99) (Fig. 6). In addition, no correlations were observed between age and histological parameters (MET, EBT, MVD, ECA, NA, VC, collagen density, and NVC) in either group (all p > 0.05). This indicates that age-related variability did not contribute to the differences between smokers and non-smokers. To adjust more for potential confounding, multivariate linear regression analyses including smoking status were adjusted for age and gender. After adjustment, smoking was an independent predictor of mean (β = +62.5 μm, p < 0.001) and base (β = +35.2 μm, p = 0.01) epithelial thickness, epithelial area (β = +4820 μm², p < 0.001; adjusted difference in nuclear area: +1070 μm², p < 0.001), adjusted difference in microvascular density: − 25.4 counts (p < 0.001) and total (adjusted difference in collagen fibre area: − 12.6 μm², p < 0.001). In contrast, vascular calibre (CD34) (β = 0.02 μm², p = 0.91) and neovascular calibre (CD105) (β = 0.01 μm², p = 0.95) did not differ significantly (post-adjustment). Detailed regression outputs are presented in Supplementary Table 2. These findings indicate that smoking has an independent influence on gingival histology unrelated to demographic variables. In effort to control for sex, we compared only male smoker (n = 30) vs. non-smoker (n = 11) cases. The findings were that smokers had a significantly thicker MET (p = 0.012), EBT (p = 0.048), ECA (p < 0.001), and NA (p < 0.001) as well as significantly less MVD (p < 0.001) and collagen density (p < 0.001). In contrast, vascular and neovascular calibre did not differ between the groups (p = 0.91 and p = 0.95, respectively) (Supplementary Table 3). These observations verify that the histological changes observed are due to smoking and not to gender. The continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Most variables, including MET, EBT, collagen fibre morphometry, VC (CD34 & CD105), were normally distributed (p > 0.05). However, MVD (CD34) and ECA in smokers and NA in non-smokers deviated significantly from normality (p < 0.05). Accordingly, data on these variables were non-normally distributed and were compared using non-parametric tests (Mann–Whitney U); normally distributed outcomes were compared with independent two-sample t-tests.

Discussion

The current study showed significant histologic, histomorphometric, and immunohistochemical differences in the periodontal tissue of smokers compared with nonsmokers with Stage II Grade B Periodontitis. Smokers had marked thicker epithelium, with larger area of epithelial cells, and nucleus and lower density of collagen fibres and microvessels. However, vascular calibre and neovascular calibre (CD105) were not significantly different between the groups. These results were also significant after controlling for age and gender, and were upheld via male-only sensitivity analyses, providing evidence that these observed alterations were driven by smoking, as opposed to demographic characteristics.

Tobacco smoking is a definitive and known risk factor for periodontal disease which significantly affects the treatment outcomes. It is a well-known fact that smoking is an established behaviour in the population across the world, and there is a proven temporality between periodontal disease and smoking [13]. The association between periodontal health impairment and smoking raises with increased exposure, hence, past smokers displayed an intermediate health status between current and non-smokers. Smoking affects microbial ecology, produces changes in the host immune-inflammatory responses, and produces changes in the periodontal tissues. It has been proven in the medical literature that tobacco smoking and its constituents of smoking does interfere with revascularization potential. Such a histological change induced by smoking definitely affects wound healing, and periodontal tissues are no exemption from this [14]. The combination of systemic factors along with smoking will further enhance the risk of periodontal destruction and can be synergistic, resulting in greater severity of periodontal disease. The oral tissues were subjected to 300 times higher concentrations of nicotine than the plasma. Contrary to the hypothesis of impaired gingival blood flow due smoking, laser Doppler flowmetry studies have shown that in humans, there is no change or increase in gingival blood flow.

With non-surgical therapy, smoking compromises the reduction in PD and CAL gain, with less reduction in periodontal parameters compared with non-smokers [15]. Previous studies have shown that regenerative therapy is less successful in smokers during management of periodontal osseous defects [16]. The past effects of smoking cannot be reversed with cessation of smoking, but at the same time there is strong evidence that after cessation, the amount of periodontal destruction and severity of disease is reduced. In the present study, the mean PD was significantly higher in smokers than in non-smokers, which is in agreement with the classical study by Bergstrom [3] in 1986. There was a statistically significant difference in PD between smokers and non-smokers (p < 0.05). The amount of CAL was significantly higher in the smoking group than in non-smokers, similar to a previous study by Axelsson [1] in 1998 which concluded that smokers exhibited a greater amount of CAL. The Pll scores were comparable and similar to those of the smokers and non-smokers. This is in agreement with the previous studies by Lindeboom et al. 2005 [17], Jayashree and Vandana 2012 [18], Petrovic et al. 2013 [19] where plaque accumulation was found to be similar in smokers and non-smokers. The irreversible destruction of periodontal attachment and bone loss, resulting from past smoking is one of reasons, that the damage cannot be fully reversed by quitting. Nevertheless, cessation of smoking decreases the continued pathologic effect, and the host response and vascular function partially recover; this results in the disease progressing more slowly and not as aggressively. In the present study, the values for PPD and CAL were significantly higher in the smokers, supporting previous findings. This mirrors the smoking-impaired host defence, fibroblast and collagen metabolism, and bone homoeostasis. Plaque scores were similar in smokers and non-smokers, consistent with earlier reports that show that, smoking enhances tissue destruction by influencing neither plaque amount nor the pathogenicity of the plaque or host response to it.

The clinical signs of gingival inflammation by GI in the present study showed that GI was comparable between smokers and non-smokers. This was contrary to Martinez-Canut 1995 [20] where authors found that the GI was significantly reduced in smokers. Petrovic et al. in 2013 [19] in contrary to the present study, concluded that GI scores are increased in smokers compared to non-smokers. PDI was compared between smokers and non-smokers with stage II grade B periodontitis. It has been found that the mean PDI did not differ between smokers and non-smokers, which is in agreement with a previous study Hasan et al. 2024 [21], where the authors concluded that there was no significant difference between the PDI scores. This might be due to the combined nature of the PDI, reflecting both plaque and gingival inflammation. Indeed, in our study plaque amount did not differ most between the groups, and smoking is found to mask clinical characteristics of gingival inflammation by decreasing gingival blood flow, nicotine through vasoconstriction. It would therefore seem that the overall PDI scores were equal and not different in spite of a more severe tissue damage in smokers.

In the present study the MET was significantly higher in smokers compared to non-smokers and the EBT was also significantly higher in smokers compared to non-smokers. However, Kumar et al. 2011 [22] had concluded that there was no significant difference in the MET among smokers and non-smokers, and they also found that MET alone was increased in smokers compared to non-smokers, in agreement with the results of the present study. In 2012 Jayashree and Vandana [18] has found that, there was a significant difference in the midfacial thickness of the gingiva, which was significantly higher in smokers than in non-smokers which is in agreement with the present study. Jarnbring in 2002 [23], Villar and Lima 2003 [24], Gultekin in 2008 [25], Popescu et al. 2013 [26], Prakash et al. 2014 [27] found that thickness of the gingival epithelium increased with smoking. This could be due to the chronic chemical and cytotoxic effects of tobacco derived smoke that leads to compensatory hyperplastic changes in the gingival epithelium. Nicotine and other contents induce the proliferation of keratinocyte and change the turnover of the epithelium in the thickened epithelium. Further, smoking-induced vascular insufficiency leads to a hypoxic niche that favours an adaptive, more keratinized epithelium. These biological mechanisms may account for the greater gingival thickness seen in smokers in both our study and in the aforementioned research.

The ECA measured by histomorphometry was significantly higher in smokers than in non-smokers in the present study. NA measured by histomorphometry was found to be significantly higher in smokers than in non-smokers. This is in agreement with the morphometric measurements of gingival ECA and NA of smokers with diabetes by Raghavendra in 2010 [28], Patel in 2011 [29], and Nandita et al. in 2014 [30]. However all the authors had compared smokers with diabetes and without diabetes; however, in the present study diabetic patients were excluded from the study to reduce confounding. Pejcic A in 2012 [31] found that greater ECA in smokers than in non-smokers, in agreement with the present study. Contrary to the present study, Petrovic et al. 2013 [19] found that there is no significant difference in NA between smokers and non-smokers.

The MVD using CD 34 marker was significantly higher in non-smokers compared to smokers which is similar to Kumar V 2011 [22], Prakash et al. 2014 [27], Seppala in 1997 [32]. Contrary to the present study, Mirbod et al. 2001 [33] reported that MVD using CD 34 were similar in smokers and non-smokers. The VC measured with the CD 34 marker showed that the mean VC did not differ between smokers and non-smokers, which is not in agreement with previous studies [18, 23]. In the present study, collagen fiber morphometry using MTS was significantly higher in non-smokers than in smokers. This is in agreement with the study Seguier et al. [34] in 2000, which concluded that there is a reduction in the collagen density in smokers compared to non-smokers, exhibiting a quantitative reduction of the collagen morphometric measurements in smokers.

The mean NVC using the CD 105 marker did not differ significantly between smokers and non-smokers. No previous studies have compared the neovascular potential of the gingiva in smokers and non-smokers. However, CD 105 is used as a marker to assess the vascularity potential in tumors, as stated in consensus by the American Association of Pathology [14], 1996 and in the study by Minhajat et al. 2006 [35]. This was the first study to assess the neovascularity potential of gingival tissues using the vascular marker CD 105. In the present study, one factor that could have influenced the results was the age distribution of participants. The average age was significantly higher in the smokers group than non-smokers. In this study, although MVD (CD34) was significantly lower in smokers, neovascular capacity, as determined by CD105, did not differ among groups. It should be noted that this is in contrast to some of the previous literature, in which smoking has been reported to decrease CD105 expression, especially in the context of tumor angiogenesis models [36, 37]. A proposed explanation is related to the unique biology of gingival tissue, in which the local inflammatory mediators strongly stimulate endothelial proliferation, perhaps overriding the anti-proliferative effects of exposure to tobacco. Furthermore, the low level of smoking within our study group (< 10 cigarettes/day) might not have been a strong stimulus to down-regulate CD105. Accordingly, while the baseline vascularity is affected by smokers, the maintained neovascular potential implies that the gingival tissue does indeed harbour the potential for vascular remodeling and healing following smoking cessation.

Study limitations

The limitations of the present study were the unequal gender distribution, as it was very difficult to equally compare the effect of gender distribution in studies related to smoking in India due to non-reporting by females because of cultural stigma. The entire group (100.0%) of smokers was male and a large majority (63.3%) of females were in non-smoker group. The demographic composition was not equal that all the smokers were male and were also much older than the non-smoker group, who were mostly female. This is a reflection of the social construct in India that men tend to smoke more and women are likely to underreport smoking because of social stigma. These discrepancies might have introduced confounding effects, especially with age-related vascular alterations and gender differences in periodontal tissues. Future research should include stratified sampling or matching to these demographic characteristics. To consider this demographic imbalance in detail, age-correlations between the histological outcomes were analysed. There were no significant correlations, suggesting differences were unlikely to be entirely related to age. To address this imbalance, regression analyses adjusted for age and gender were performed. Importantly, smoking remained an independent predictor of epithelial thickness, nuclear and cellular areas, reduced collagen density, and diminished microvascular density, even after adjustment. These findings strengthen the robustness of our conclusions, suggesting that the observed alterations are primarily attributable to smoking rather than demographic differences. Nevertheless, larger studies with matched cohorts are warranted to validate these findings. An additional drawback of our study is that over 60% of non-smokers were females. In females, hormone changes such as those occurring during puberty, the menstrual cycle or pregnancy can induce temporary effects on the gingival epithelium, connective tissue and vascularity. Although it is difficult to completely eliminate such confounding factors, we attempted to limit bias by excluding pregnant or lactating women and women with known endocrine disease. Furthermore, the male-only sensitivity analyses showed that, the main histological differences remained in male smokers, indicating that the observed changes are predominantly due to smoking rather than hormonal changes. The present study did not address the quality of smoking. The present study also did not assess the menstrual status of the female participants despite employing a convenience sampling method. The quality of smoking may also vary with different types of smoking, with varying levels of nicotine exposure in the oral tissues. This was conducted as a single centre study and hence lacks generalizability of the results. The concentration of nicotine in the plasma or urine and its correlation with quantitative histological changes could have implied closer reflections of the changes induced by smoking. The effect of passive smoking, including second- and third-hand smoking, in non-smokers could not be assessed and ruled out in the present study. Furthermore, the present study revealed that there is a comparatively equal neovascularity tendency in smokers when compared with non-smokers. Thus, histological, immunohistochemical, and histomorphometric analyses of the gingiva in smokers vs. non-smokers exhibited that, although there are significant changes associated with smoking, the neovascularity potential of the tissues is still preserved and will be beneficial to patients when smoking cessation is advocated.

Conclusions

The role of tobacco smoking on the pathogenesis and outcome of periodontal treatment have been the subject of many studies over the past few decades. Within the constraints of this study, smoking was associated with characteristic histological as well as histomorphometric changes in the gingiva, such as epithelial changes, alterations of cellular characteristics, decrease of collagen, and impaired vascularity. These results highlight the adverse effects of cigarette smoking on periodontal tissues and provide insight into the altered healing response noted clinically. Retention of neovascular potential in the periodontium of former smokers indicates that cessation of smoking may enable at least a partial recovery, emphasizing the necessity of smoking control in the plan for treatment of periodontal disease.
