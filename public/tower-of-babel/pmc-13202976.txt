Distinguishing cancer patients’ mild and severe symptoms in radiotherapy via zero-shot and few-shot large language model-based probabilistic prompts
Authors: Matthew W Chen, Yang Yan, Xinglei Shen, Hao Gao, Zhong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeMonitoring symptom severity during and after radiotherapy (RT) is critical for cancer patient care, however, existing machine learning and deep learning approaches face high retraining costs and lack clinically acceptable explanations. This study aims to investigate effective prompts for LLM-based reasoning and decision-making, distinguishing cancer patients’ mild and severe status during and after radiotherapy across multiple health symptoms.MethodsTo overcome these limitations, we developed a set of few-shot Large Language Model (LLM)-based predictive models operating under an anomaly detection framework (AD-LLM). Our approach leverages six novel, probabilistically designed prompts tailored for AD-LLM to identify and track patients with mild or severe symptoms, enabling accurate prediction despite limited patient-specific data.ResultsExperiments on prostate cancer patients monitoring bowel pain, depression, and sexual dysfunction problems demonstrated AD-LLM’s effectiveness in classifying symptom severity levels using these structured prompts. We evaluate the performance of different prompt strategies using AUC, AUCPR, Precision, Recall, and F1 scores, with confusion matrices and ROC/PR curves visualized to offer insights into LLM reasoning. The reasoning examples of LLMs are also provided to explain and understand their decision-making process.ConclusionThis method provides a promising and explainable solution for real-time symptom monitoring in RT settings, potentially improving timely intervention and patient outcomes.Keywords: Few-shot learning, Probabilistic prompt design, Meta Llama-3.1-8B, OpenAI GPT-4o, Health symptoms, Radiation therapy

Introduction

Radiotherapy is a cornerstone of cancer treatment [1], however, its effectiveness is counterbalanced by the risk of treatment-associated toxicities (or side effects) [2] (as illustrated in Fig. 1). These toxicities, ranging from acute reactions like skin burns and fatigue to chronic issues like fibrosis or organ dysfunction, significantly impact patient safety, quality-of-life (QoL), and treatment tolerance [5]. Accurately monitoring these symptoms is therefore fundamental and essential [6]. Without precise tracking, severe toxicities may be missed or underestimated, leading to preventable suffering, treatment interruptions compromising efficacy, and potentially life-threatening complications. Effective and accurate symptom monitoring is the bedrock for timely supportive care interventions and maintaining the delicate therapeutic balance between tumor control and patient well-being [7]. Early detection allows for timely intervention (e.g., symptom management, supportive care, treatment breaks/modifications), preventing severe complications and improving daily life during treatment.

An overview of expanded prostate cancer index composite for clinical practice (EPIC-CP questionnaire) [3, 4], assessing how the treatments affect a patient’s quality of life, focusing on aspects like urinary, sexual, and bowel function

Accurate toxicity tracking is also indispensable for advancing personalized cancer care in radiotherapy [8]. Understanding individual patient responses, both during and after treatment, provides critical data and evidence. This data informs real-time treatment adjustments (e.g., dose modification), refines future planning protocols to spare healthy tissues, validates predictive models of toxicity risk, and ultimately improves the therapeutic ratio for subsequent patients. However, achieving accurate tracking faces significant hurdles, including under-reporting by patients, potential underestimation or inconsistent grading by clinicians, challenges in capturing long-term effects, and the burden of systematic data collection. Overcoming these barriers is essential to realize truly personalized radiotherapy.

Key Challenges: Existing machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) models [9–13] for tracking radiotherapy patients face significant hurdles due to high training costs and poor retraining scalability. Training sophisticated models, especially deep neural networks (DNNs) [14], demands substantial computational resources and large, high-quality datasets. This becomes particularly problematic for rare conditions like pediatric cancers, where patient numbers are inherently limited. The scarcity of data increases the risk of overfitting and hinders the development of reliable and generalizable models [15]. Furthermore, keeping models current as new clinical data emerges is computationally expensive and logistically complex, making them difficult to adapt and deploy widely across diverse healthcare settings. This lack of efficient retraining scalability prevents models from evolving with clinical practice.

A critical barrier to clinical adoption is the lack of reliable explanations for model predictions. Most advanced ML/DL techniques operate as black boxes [16–20], providing outputs without transparently revealing the reasoning behind their decisions. In high-stakes healthcare applications like monitoring cancer treatment toxicity, clinicians require interpretable predictions to understand the contributing factors, assess plausibility, and build trust. The absence of robust and clinically meaningful explanations hinders model validation, complicates integration into clinical workflows, and raises safety concerns, as clinicians cannot reliably verify or act upon opaque model suggestions.

Our Motivation: Thanks to the rapid development of large language models (LLMs) [21–24], they offer a transformative potential to address the limitations of traditional ML/DL in radiotherapy toxicity tracking. First, LLMs excel in few-shot learning, enabling reliable predictions even with sparse pediatric/rare-cancer data. Their pretraining on vast biomedical literature allows them to understand complex clinical patterns without task-specific retraining. Updates can be achieved efficiently via lightweight fine-tuning (e.g., LoRA [25] and Swift [26]) rather than full-model retraining, slashing computational costs. Second, LLMs generate natural language explanations for predictions. This bridges the black-box gap by highlighting contributing factors (clinical/dosimetric variables), citing relevant guidelines or literature, and aligning reasoning with clinical intuition. Last but not the least, LLMs can fuse structured data (such as dose metrics, demographic data, and text-based clinical notes) with unstructured notes (such as clinician narratives, patient-reported outcomes) and imaging reports, enabling holistic toxicity assessment without custom model architectures. To address these challenges encountered in clinical practice, we leverage the prediction and reasoning ability of LLMs such as Meta Llama and OpenAI ChatGPT to enhance prediction results as well as provide reasons of decision-making.

Our Contributions: Our primary contribution lies in the novel design of six distinct predictive prompts specifically tailored for leveraging Large Language Models for Anomaly Detection (AD-LLM) [27–30] to monitor symptom severity in cancer patients undergoing and recovering from radiotherapy. We systematically developed and evaluated prompts based on Zero-Shot AD-LLM (requiring no examples), Few-Shot AD-LLM (using a small mild and severe example set), Normal-Shot AD-LLM (using only mild symptomatic examples), Anomaly-Shot AD-LLM (using only severe symptomatic examples), and Probabilistic One-Shot AD-LLM (incorporating uncertainty or probabilistic outputs from one-shot example). This comprehensive framework addresses the challenge of applying LLMs effectively under varying data availability constraints inherent in clinical monitoring.

We applied this prompt framework to the critical real-world problem of continuously monitoring both mild and severe symptoms throughout the radiotherapy treatment trajectory and into the survivorship phase. This represents a significant contribution by demonstrating the practical utility of prompt-engineered AD-LLM for fine-grained, longitudinal health monitoring in oncology. Our approach directly tackles the class imbalance between periods of wellness (majority) and symptomatic periods (minority, especially severe symptoms), providing a scalable method for early detection of adverse events without requiring extensive labeled datasets or model fine-tuning for each specific symptom or severity level.

Our core innovation lies not in the prompts themselves but in the novel framework that adapts LLMs to a one-class anomaly detection setting for a complex clinical task. Traditional ML/DL approaches for PRO symptom tracking typically require large, balanced datasets for supervised binary or multiclass classification. Our work fundamentally re-frames this as a few-shot anomaly detection problem, where severe symptoms are treated as anomalies against a backdrop of mild or normal states. This is a significant methodological shift. To our knowledge, this represents the first application of LLMs configured explicitly for one-class classification in PRO-based radiotherapy toxicity monitoring, moving beyond using LLMs as mere classifiers or information retrievers to function as contextual anomaly detectors with inherent explanatory reasoning.

Related work

ML/DL for PRO analysis

Previous literature revealed that cancer patients undergoing radiotherapy commonly experience physical and psychological symptom distress. Tang et al. [31] have evaluated the change in symptom distress in cancer patients after radiotherapy and its predisposing factors. A recent study by Jagsi et al. [32] is to identify patients whose symptoms are underrecognized during treatment with breast radiotherapy. Recent studies have focused on employing ML/DL approaches to address the challenges of patient-reported outcomes. Yan et al. [33, 34] explore various machine learning techniques to predict patient outcomes related to health status such as pain levels and sleep discomfort using PRO datasets from a cancer photon/proton therapy center. Specifically, they deploy six advanced machine learning classifiers – Random Forest (RF) [35], XGBoost [36], Gradient Boosting (GB) [37], Support Vector Machine (SVM) [38], Multi-Layer Perceptron with Bagging (MLP-Bagging) [39], and Logistic Regression (LR) [40] – to tackle a multi-class imbalance classification problem across various prevalent cancer types. Furthermore, motivated by the issue of extreme class imbalance regarding mild and severe symptoms, Yan et al. [41] investigate a set of unsupervised anomaly detection approaches to classify patients with mild/intermediate and severe outcomes using patient-reported outcomes (PROs) datasets. Among them, the histogram-based outlier score (HBOS) method [42] has demonstrated a superior performance for the minority class, representing cancer patients with severe health status. It’s worth noting that all these ML/DL methods require large training data for training the models, however, our proposed prompt strategies do not require any training data but only provide a few exemplars in our case.

LLM for imbalance classification

A wide range of recent studies have explored leveraging the knowledge graph and few-shot capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) to address class imbalance inherent in PRO analysis. A primary focus has been on prompt engineering techniques without extensive fine-tuning. Studies investigate methods like Chain-of-Thought prompting [43], providing explicit instructions to prioritize minority classes, weighting examples within prompts, or crafting diverse synthetic examples of the minority class for in-context learning [44]. These approaches aim to guide the LLM’s reasoning towards recognizing rare patterns by exploiting its pre-trained understanding and ability to learn from limited examples presented in the prompt. Additionally, zero-shot classification [45] using carefully designed prompts is evaluated, often showing robustness compared to traditional models on highly imbalanced data, particularly when classes are semantically distinct, though performance can vary significantly based on prompt design and model choice.

Beyond prompting, studies also examine fine-tuning LLMs [46] specifically for imbalanced tasks. This involves techniques like minority-class oversampling (text augmentation) before fine-tuning, employing cost-sensitive loss functions [47] that penalize misclassifying minority instances more heavily, and transfer learning from related tasks. Furthermore, hybrid approaches are prominent, where LLMs act as feature extractors (generating embeddings from text inputs) or data generators (synthesizing realistic minority class samples) to enhance traditional imbalanced learning algorithms like SVMs [38], Random Forests [35], or specialized cost-sensitive classifiers [48]. These hybrids often demonstrate strong performance by combining the semantic understanding of LLMs with the efficiency and optimization of conventional methods tailored for imbalance, though challenges remain around computational cost, potential for generating low-quality synthetic data, and overfitting on very small minority sets. Overall, research indicates LLMs offer promising avenues, especially through prompting and hybrid systems, but optimal strategies are task-dependent.

Proposed method

In this section, we will elaborate on our proposed prompts to guide LLMs under anomaly detection settings to distinguish mild and severe symptom of cancer patients undergoing and recovering from radiotherapy.

Zero-shot AD-LLM prompts

We implement two distinct experimental settings using the AD-LLM framework [27] based on Meta’s Llama 3.1 8B Instruct model. The first, termed Llama-3.1-8B Instruct Mild, is primed solely on the characteristics of mild symptoms (treating them as the normal reference category as AD-I), and the second setting is Llama-3.1-8B Instruct Mild+Severe, where both mild and severe symptoms are described in the prompts (treating them as the normal and anomaly reference categories as AD-II). The prompt template setting for “Zero-shot LLM-based using patients with mild symptoms solely” is presented in Table 1. Based on this prompt template, we also design another prompt for “Zero-shot LLM-based using patients with mild and severe symptoms” by feeding LLM with both mild and severe symptoms guidance. Firstly, we give a clear definition of the PRO task. We collaborate with a radiation oncologist to carefully design scenarios of severe symptoms to ensure that the LLMs can understand anomaly definition in text-based PRO reports. Secondly, a strict scoring mechanism description is given to LLM for determining the anomaly score. Thirdly, step-by-step reasoning with Chain-of-Thought (CoT) [43] encourages LLMs to sequentially produce the reason and anomaly score in a structural and logical way. Lastly, a clear response format ensures the LLM can provide the answers in a correct JSON format.

This prompt template guides an LLM to act as a senior clinical expert specializing in prostate cancer symptom monitoring, specifically tasked with identifying clinically significant bowel dysfunction in patient self-reports. It strictly focuses only on bowel-related symptoms (e.g., frequency changes, urgency, pain, consistency) while explicitly disregarding unrelated urinary, hormonal, or sexual concerns. The core objective is anomaly or severity detection for medical monitoring systems.

The template defines clear labeling criteria: Assign Label 1 (“anomalous”) if bowel complaints are persistent, recurring, described as a “moderate” or “big” problem, or disruptive to daily life. Assign Label 0 for absent, vague, minor (“no problem”, “small problem”), or unrelated symptoms. It includes illustrative examples of both labels with reasoning and associated anomaly_score values (a float between 0 and 1). Scoring guidelines link the anomaly_score to confidence levels:>0.75 (strong confidence in significance), (mild/moderate concern), and <0.4 (less confidence in normal/irrelevant symptoms).

The prompt emphasizes strict interpretation: Label 1 should only be assigned if bowel distress is explicitly supported by the patient’s language, without inference. The required output is a JSON object containing a brief “reason” (1–3 sentences) explaining the logic and the calculated “anomaly_score”. This structure provides the LLM with clear classification rules, examples, and output format constraints for consistent few-shot learning.

Probabilistic one-shot AD-LLM prompts

The probability-based prompt template summarized in Table 3 configures an LLM (GPT-4o) as a clinical assistant for anomaly detection in bowel symptoms among prostate cancer patients undergoing radiotherapy. Unlike the previous few-shot approach, this uses a one-shot methodology anchored to predefined examples of normal/mild symptoms. The core task is to distinguish expected, manageable radiotherapy side effects from potentially concerning anomalies warranting clinical attention. Context emphasizes that most patients experience mild, self-resolving bowel changes, with only a small subset developing severe issues.

LLM prompt template used for one-shot AD-LLM (GPT-4o) with mild symptoms solely on pain/urgency of bowel dataset

The template provides explicit clinical definitions: Normal symptoms include slight frequency increases, occasional urgency without accidents, or mild discomfort described as “manageable” or “no big issue”. Anomalies involve severe indicators like rectal pain, incontinence, daily disruption, bleeding, or psychological distress. Critically, the LLM operates under an AD-I protocol, meaning it only references descriptions of normal symptoms (e.g., “Occasional urgency, but no accidents”) and cannot directly simulate severe patterns. Judgment relies solely on comparing patient narratives against these mild benchmarks.

Decision rules enforce a probabilistic chain of thought: (1) Analyze bowel-specific content while ignoring unrelated domains. (2) Compare tone/symptoms against provided “normal” examples. (3) Assign a conservative anomaly_score in three tiers: 0.0–0.3 (clearly mild, not anomalous); 0.3–0.7 (borderline); 0.7–1.0 (likely anomaly). Uncertain cases default to “not anomalous”. The corresponding output must be strict JSON with escaped quotes, followed by a summary paragraph.

Finally, the prompt includes a concrete output example demonstrating low-risk classification (e.g., anomaly_score: 0.22 for “occasional urgency without distress”). This structure prioritizes safety by constraining the LLM’s reference data to normal symptom patterns and requiring conservative, probability-weighted scoring tied to clinical severity indicators.

Evaluation metrics

The Few-Shot Prompt operates on a rule-based scoring system guided by an explicit and pre-defined criteria. The anomaly score or severity score is calculated by the LLM based on the presence and severity of keywords and phrases within the patient’s text. The inference is made by comparing the patient’s narrative against a set of binary classification rules: descriptions of “moderate problem” or “big problem”, terms indicating persistence (“persistent”, “recurring”, “frequent”), and evidence of life disruption directly lead to a high anomaly score (>0.75). Conversely, the absence of these markers or the presence of minimizing language (“no problem”, “small problem”) results in a low score (<0.4). The provided examples of Label 1 and Label 0 outputs serve as anchors, guiding the LLM on how to apply these rules to generate a reasoned score between 0 and 1.

In contrast, the One-Shot Prompt utilizes a probabilistic, similarity-based scoring approach under an Anomaly Detection by Comparison (AD-I) protocol. The calculation of the anomaly score is based on the semantic similarity between the patient’s narrative and a provided set of “Known Descriptions of Normal Symptoms.” The LLM is instructed to judge responses solely against these examples of mild, expected outcomes. The inference is made through a comparative chain of thought: if the patient’s tone and described symptoms closely align with the known normal examples (e.g., “manageable,” “occasional … but no accidents”), the score is pushed toward the lower range (). A higher score () is assigned only if the narrative describes features starkly different from the normal set, such as pain, loss of control, or severe disruption, which are defined as anomalous. This method explicitly forbids the LLM from referencing severe patterns, making its inferences solely from a foundation of what is known to be normal.

The performance of the different prompt strategies was evaluated against expert-annotated ground truth labels for clinically significant bowel symptoms. Experiments

PRO data

The PRO dataset was collected from 01/2021 to 11/2024 at KUMC with a required training policy. The responsible conduct of research and human subjects protection training is required of research personnel, as outlined in the policy, and provided via the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative (CITI). No identifying information will be included with the data when it is downloaded. The data has been de-identified. The prostate PRO dataset consists of 22 instruments measuring urinary, bowel, sexual, and vitality/hormonal health, featuring three target measurements: (1) rectal pain/urgency of bowel (assessed via EPIC-CP/IPSS [49, 50] for Health-Related Quality of Life (HRQoL)), (2) sexual function, and (3) depression feeling (both capturing prostate-specific symptoms). Responses were classified as mild symptoms (“No problem”, “Very small problem”, “Small problem”) or severe symptoms (“Moderate problem”, “Big problem”). Following exclusion of records with missing PRO data, the final cohort includes: 10,356 mild vs. 586 severe cases for rectal pain/urgency; 8444 mild vs. 1998 severe cases for sexual dysfunction; and 10,266 mild vs. 665 severe cases for depression.

Baseline methods

Our baselines encompassed six LLM-based Anomaly Detection (AD-LLM) methods mainly divided into three categories: One zero-shot, Three few-shot, and Two probabilistic one-shot approaches: (1) Zero-shot AD-LLM: We designed prompts using only patients with mild symptoms to predict severe PRO symptoms (Zero-Shot Mild). Additionally, we designed prompts using both patients with mild and severe symptoms (Zero-Shot Mixed). Both utilized Meta Llama-3.1-8B. (2) Few-shot AD-LLM: We extended the zero-shot prompts by providing sets of five examples. Three variations were evaluated: i) Five patients with mild symptoms only (Few-Shot Mild Only); ii) Five patients with severe symptoms only (Few-Shot Severe Only); iii) A combined set of five mild and five severe patients (Few-Shot Mixed). (3) Probabilistic One-shot AD-LLM: Building on the zero-shot prompts, we investigated the impact of providing a single additional example: either one cancer patient with mild symptoms (One-Shot Mild), or one with severe symptoms (One-Shot Severe), or strategically supplementing the prompt with two patients representing high and low confidence levels for severe symptoms (Two-Shot Probability-Based). All experiments were conducted on a Windows workstation equipped with a 3.10 GHz Intel Xeon w5-2445 processor, 32 GB RAM, and dual NVLink-connected NVIDIA RTX A4500 GPUs (40 GB VRAM), running Meta Llama-3.1-8B and GPT-4o.

Experimental settings

All experiments were conducted with a fixed random seed (42), applied consistently across Python, NumPy, PyTorch (CUDA), and Hugging Face Transformers. Deterministic execution was enforced by disabling cuDNN benchmarking and enabling deterministic CUDA operations. For Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, inference used greedy decoding with temperature set to 0, no top-p or top-k sampling, no presence or frequency penalties, and a maximum generation length of 512 tokens (4096 for extended prompts). This configuration ensures deterministic and fully reproducible outputs. For GPT-4o, inference was performed using the OpenAI API with deterministic decoding (temperature = 0). As GPT-4o does not expose internal random seed control, exact reproducibility cannot be strictly guaranteed; however, deterministic decoding yielded stable outputs across repeated runs in practice.

First, the performance indicates a high variation by different health statuses. Specifically, the performance of bowel pain predicted by designed prompts such as probabilistic one-shot AD-LLMs consistently achieves the highest scores (e.g., AUC often > 0.9, F1 often > 0.8), indicating these models are most effective for this task. Result regarding depression shows moderate performance (AUC typically , F1 ~ 0.64), suggesting moderate effectiveness with room for improvement. Result regarding sexual dysfunction is the most challenging, with generally low AUCPR (<0.35), Precision (<0.32), and F1 score (<0.46), despite sometimes high Recall. This indicates models struggle to identify true positives accurately for this condition, often generating many false positives (low precision) while frequently over-flagging cases, leading to extremely high recall but poor precision.

Second, the proposed Probabilistic One-shot AD-LLM approach generally delivers the best overall performance, particularly for patients with Bowel Pain. It achieves the highest AUC and F1 scores for Bowel Pain across both Llama 3.1 variants and GPT-4o. GPT-4o Mild + Severe achieves the highest AUC (0.9416), while GPT-4o Mild attains the highest F1-score (0.8262), indicating complementary strengths in discrimination and balanced classification. While less dominant on the other tasks, it remains highly competitive.

Based on the performance metrics reported in Table 4, the GPT-4o model, particularly the “Mild + Severe” variant, demonstrated superior performance for the Bowel Pain category, achieving the highest scores across key metrics. It attained a near-state-of-the-art AUC of 0.9416 with CI and an AUCPR of 0.8780 with CI , indicating exceptional ability to distinguish clinically significant bowel symptoms from normal ones. Its high precision (0.9092) and recall (0.7506) yield an F1-score of 0.8222, reflecting a strong trade-off favoring fewer false positives. This suggests that large, advanced models like GPT-4o, when provided with examples of both mild and severe symptoms, are highly effective for this specific medical anomaly detection task.

In contrast, performance varied significantly across symptom categories and was notably weaker for Depressed Feeling and Sexual Function. For these categories, no model configuration achieved an AUCPR above ~0.61 or an F1-score above ~0.67, with many models scoring significantly lower. This indicates a much more challenging detection environment, likely due to the subjective and nuanced language used in patient reports for these issues. The Probabilistic One-shot AD-LLM exhibits a distinct behavior: for bowel pain, it balances precision and recall effectively, whereas for sexual dysfunction, it aggressively flags anomalies, achieving extremely high recall at the expense of very low precision. Overall, the results underscore that the effectiveness of an AD-LLM prompt strategy is highly dependent on the specific clinical domain to which it is applied.

Lastly, GPT-4o (specifically the Mild + Severe variant in Probabilistic One-shot) sets the benchmark for Bowel Pain. Llama 3.1 Mild in Probabilistic One-shot also performs exceptionally well on Bowel Pain. For Depressed Feeling, GPT-4o Mild achieves the highest F1 score (0.6738). For Sexual Function, the best F1 score (0.4501) is achieved by GPT-4o Mild, though overall performance remains poor. Including “Severe” examples alongside “Mild” (Mild + Severe) does not yield consistent improvements. Its effect varies significantly depending on the specific approach (Zero-shot, Few-shot, etc.) and health condition. For example, it helps GPT-4o in Probabilistic One-shot for Bowel Pain, but sometimes slightly hurts Llama 3.1 in other paradigms or conditions. Particularly evident in Sexual Function, many models exhibit very high Recall (e.g., >0.8 or even > 0.99) but extremely low Precision (<0.32). This suggests a strategy of flagging many instances as anomalies to catch most true ones, but at the cost of many false alarms. Balancing this trade-off remains a significant challenge for this condition.

The comparison in terms of confusion matrix evaluates the confusion matrices of six distinct AD-LLM approaches (illustrated in Fig. 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8) applied to prostate cancer patients across three key symptoms: bowel pain, sexual function, and depression feeling. The methods include zero-shot (Fig. 3), few-shot (Fig. 4), anomaly-shot (Fig. 6), normal-shot (Fig. 5), and probabilistic one-shot using both Llama 3.1-8B (Fig. 7) and GPT-4o (Fig. 8). However, they struggle significantly with severe outcomes (Class 1), showing extremely low diagonal values and very high false positives. Consequently, many patients experiencing severe symptoms are incorrectly classified as mild/intermediate. However, the other baseline methods either suffer high true positive rates of Class 1 but low true positive rates of Class 0 or high true positive rates of Class 0 but low true positive rates of Class 1.

ROC/PR curves and AUC/AUCPR comparison

All six AD-LLM methods in Fig. 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8 are evaluated using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves and Precision-Recall (PR) curves. Each figure prominently displays these curves alongside confusion matrices. The performance is measured by the Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) and the Area Under the PR Curve (AUCPR), providing complementary views: AUC assesses the model’s ability to rank anomalies correctly across thresholds, while AUCPR is crucial for evaluating performance on imbalanced datasets by focusing on precision and recall of the positive (anomalous or severe) class.

The ROC/PR curves (as seen in curve separation and implied AUC/AUCPR) vary considerably across the three symptom domains (Bowel Pain, Sexual Function, Depression Feeling), suggesting that detecting anomalies is symptom-specific. Including only “Mild” anomalies versus combining “Mild+Severe” anomalies (columns within each symptom row) demonstrably changes the difficulty of the task, reflected in the shape and position of the ROC/PR curves and the resulting AUC/AUCPR values. Severe cases in Bowel Pain, Sexual Function, Depression Feeling likely alter the data distribution and class imbalance.

Configurations yielding ROC curves closer to the top-left corner indicate better overall ranking ability (higher implied AUC). Similarly, PR curves closer to the top-right corner indicate better precision-recall trade-offs (higher implied AUCPR), which is particularly important for the likely imbalanced anomaly detection task. The side-by-side presentation across methods, symptoms, and severity within each figure, and the consistent structure across all figures, facilitates identifying the proposed probabilistic one-Shot LLM-based AD method including severe cases (Mild+Severe) generally produces the best-performing curves (highest implied AUC/AUCPR). Also, the Probabilistic One-Shot with GPT-4o (Fig. 8) outperforms Probabilistic One-Shot with Llama 3.1-8B (Fig. 7).

Conclusion

This work investigated six few-shot Large Language Model (LLM)-based approaches utilizing probabilistic prompts to enhance the prediction of mild and severe symptoms in cancer patients during and after radiotherapy. By leveraging probabilistically designed prompts, the proposed methods achieved accurate predictions despite limited patient-specific data and provided reliable explanations as part of model outputs. Experiments conducted on a prostate cancer patient-reported outcome (PRO) dataset, measuring urinary, bowel, sexual, and vitality/hormonal health, enabled a comprehensive comparison of prompt strategies. Performance was evaluated using AUC, AUCPR, Precision, Recall, and F1 scores, with confusion matrices and ROC/PR curves visualized to offer insights into LLM reasoning.

The results demonstrated that the Probabilistic One-shot AD-LLM approach generally delivered the best overall performance. However, performance varied significantly across health domains: while results for bowel pain were promising, the approach struggled with depression and sexual function. To bridge this performance gap, future work will explore probabilistic Few-shot AD-LLM techniques, transfer learning, or domain adaptation strategies.
