Stoner Transition at Finite Temperature in a 2D Isotropic Fermi Liquid
R. David Mayrhofer, Megan Schoenzeit, Andrey V. Chubukov

Abstract
We present the results of a mean-field analysis of the temperature evolution of a ferromagnetic Stoner transition in a two-dimensional (2D) system with an isotropic dispersion ε k ∝ k 2 ​ α \varepsilon_{k}\propto k^{2\alpha} , which for α > 1 \alpha>1 models flat dispersions in various multi-layer graphene systems in a displacement field. This study is an extension to a finite T T of previous studies at T = 0 T=0 , which found both first-order and second-order Stoner transitions, depending on the value of α \alpha and special behavior at α = 1 \alpha=1 and α = 2 \alpha=2 . We find that the Stoner transition at a finite T T displays new features not seen at T = 0 T=0 . The most interesting one is the reentrant behavior, where the ordered state emerges as temperature is increased. This behavior develops at α > 1.4 \alpha>1.4 and the range where it holds increases with α \alpha . We conjecture that the reentrant behavior is the fundamental feature of the Stoner transition in 2D, not sensitive to the details of the electronic structure.

II
Mean-Field Equations

Our point of departure is a Hamiltonian for spin 1/2 fermions within one valley with the dispersion
\varepsilon_{k}
and short range interaction
g
:

\displaystyle H=\sum_{\mathbf{k},\alpha}\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\alpha}c_{\mathbf{k},\alpha}+\frac{g}{2V}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q},\alpha,\beta}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},\alpha}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q},\beta}c_{\mathbf{p},\beta}c_{\mathbf{k},\alpha}.

(1)

We assume that the dispersion is isotropic and takes the form,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{k}=c\left(\frac{k^{2}}{4\pi}\right)^{\alpha}.

(2)

The corresponding density of states at the Fermi level
\nu_{F,\alpha}
is

\displaystyle\nu_{F,\alpha}=\frac{\varepsilon_{F}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-1}}{\alpha c^{1/\alpha}}.

(3)

The dimensionless coupling
\lambda
is the product of
g
and the density of states:

\displaystyle\lambda=g\nu_{F,\alpha}.

(4)

As we said, we will analyze the type of transition into a ferromagnetic state and the topology of the phase diagram within the mean-field (ladder) approximation. It has been known for quite some time
Kanamori 1963
that this approximation under-estimates the strength of fluctuations which tend to eliminate a Stoner instability or at least move it to larger
\lambda
. On the other hand, the results of the
T=0
mean-field analysis on the development of spin and valley polarization in 2D are largely consistent with the data for multi-layer graphene sheets (Bernal bilayer graphene, rhombohedral trilayer graphene, rhombohedral pentalayer graphene) and AlAs (see more on this in Ref.
Raines and Chubukov 2025
).
We take this as a justification to extend mean-field analysis to finite
T
.

Within mean-field, the ferromagnetic order parameter is determined self-consistently as

\displaystyle\Delta=\frac{g}{2V}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\alpha,\beta}\langle c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\alpha}\sigma_{\alpha\beta}^{z}c_{\mathbf{k},\beta}\rangle=\frac{g}{2V}\left(N_{\uparrow}-N_{\downarrow}\right),

(5)

where
V
is the area of the system.
This self-consistent equation relates the spin order parameter to spin dependent energy shift of the bands in the ordered state, induced by the same order parameter. The system must also obey particle conservation, which is the condition that the number of particles,

\displaystyle N=\sum_{\mathbf{k},\alpha,\beta}\langle c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\alpha}\delta_{\alpha\beta}c_{\mathbf{k},\beta}\rangle,

(6)

or, equivalently, particle density
n=N/V
does not change upon spin polarization. This equation determines the temperature dependent chemical potential, which in the normal state we call
\mu(T)
and in the ordered state
{\bar{\mu}}(T)
. The particle density can be easily evaluated and equals

\displaystyle n=2\alpha\epsilon_{F}\nu_{F,\alpha}.

(7)

Using (
5
) and (
6
), we can relate
\Delta
to the polarization of the system,
\zeta=(N_{\uparrow}-N_{\downarrow})/N
:

\displaystyle\Delta=\zeta\alpha\lambda\varepsilon_{F}.

(8)

Lastly, we note the true ground state is a minimum of the free energy of the system. If the system undergoes a second order phase transition, it is essentially guaranteed that once the self-consistent ladder equation (
5
) develops a non-zero solution, this solution minimizes the free energy. However, if the transition is first order,
one must explicitly compare the free energies of the system at finite
\Delta
and at
\Delta=0
.
To do this, we need the expression for the free energy. Within the ladder approximation, it is

\displaystyle F_{ord}=\Omega_{ord}+\bar{\mu}N=\frac{V\Delta^{2}}{g}+\bar{\mu}N-T\sum_{k,\sigma}\log\left(1+e^{\left(\bar{\mu}-\varepsilon_{k,\sigma}\right)/T}\right),

(9)

where
\Omega_{ord}
is the grand potential in the ordered state and
\varepsilon_{k,\sigma}=\varepsilon_{k}\pm\Delta
. The free energy in the normal state is obtained from Eq. (
9
) by setting
\Delta=0
and
{\bar{\mu}}=\mu
.

III
Ordered States

Here, we obtain the order parameter, the chemical potential, and the free energy at different values of
\alpha
and detect the phase boundary between the ordered and normal states.

First, assume that the transition is second order. The order parameter susceptibility then diverges precisely at the transition point. Within the ladder approximation, the susceptibility in the normal state is

\displaystyle\chi=\frac{\chi_{0}}{1-g\Pi_{\alpha}(T)},

(10)

where
\Pi_{\alpha}(T)
is the static particle-hole bubble at zero-momentum transfer. The susceptibility diverges at
g\Pi_{\alpha}(T)=1
. The polarization
\Pi_{\alpha}(T)
at a finite temperature can be obtained analytically:

\displaystyle\Pi_{\alpha}(T)

\displaystyle=-T\sum_{n}\int\frac{d^{2}k}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}G(\mathbf{k},\omega_{n})^{2}=-\nu_{F,\alpha}\varepsilon_{F}^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}d\varepsilon_{k}\varepsilon_{k}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-1}n_{F}^{\prime}(\varepsilon_{k}-\mu)

\displaystyle=-\nu_{F,\alpha}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\left(\frac{T}{\varepsilon_{F}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-1}\text{Li}_{\frac{1}{\alpha}-1}\left(-e^{\mu/T}\right),

(11)

where
G(\mathbf{k},\omega_{n})=(i\omega_{n}-\varepsilon_{k}-\mu)^{-1}
is the fermionic Green’s function, and Li
{}_{s}(z)
is the polylogarithm (the Jonquiere’s function). Similarly, the order parameter
\Delta
, the chemical potential
{\bar{\mu}}
and the free energy are also expressed via polylogarithms:

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle=\frac{g}{2V}\sum_{\beta}\int\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}n_{F}(\varepsilon_{k,\beta}-\bar{\mu})\sigma_{\beta\beta}^{z}=\frac{\lambda}{2}\varepsilon_{F}^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\sum_{\beta}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}d\varepsilon_{k}\varepsilon_{k}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-1}n_{F}(\varepsilon_{k,\beta}-\bar{\mu})\sigma_{\beta\beta}^{z}

(12)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda\varepsilon_{F}}{2}\left(\frac{T}{\varepsilon_{F}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\left[\text{Li}_{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\left(-e^{\left(\bar{\mu}+\Delta\right)/T}\right)-\text{Li}_{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\left(-e^{\left(\bar{\mu}-\Delta\right)/T}\right)\right],

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle=2\alpha\nu_{F,\alpha}\varepsilon_{F}=-\nu_{F,\alpha}\varepsilon_{F}\left(\frac{T}{\varepsilon_{F}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\left[\text{Li}_{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\left(-e^{\left(\bar{\mu}+\Delta\right)/T}\right)+\text{Li}_{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\left(-e^{\left(\bar{\mu}-\Delta\right)/T}\right)\right],

(13)

\displaystyle\frac{F_{ord}}{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta^{2}}{2\alpha\varepsilon_{F}\lambda}+\bar{\mu}+\frac{\varepsilon_{F}}{2\alpha}\left(\frac{T}{\varepsilon_{F}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}+1}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\left[\text{Li}_{1+\frac{1}{\alpha}}\left(-e^{\left(\bar{\mu}+\Delta\right)/T}\right)+\text{Li}_{1+\frac{1}{\alpha}}\left(-e^{\left(\bar{\mu}-\Delta\right)/T}\right)\right].

(14)

One can extract the low temperature behavior of the polarization bubble and other variables by expanding the polylogarithm in powers of temperature. For
z\gg 1
,

\displaystyle\text{Li}_{s}\left(-e^{z}\right)=-2\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\eta(2k)\frac{z^{s-2k}}{\Gamma\left(s-2k+1\right)}+\mathcal{O}\left(e^{-z}\right),

(15)

\displaystyle\eta(k)=-2\pi^{k-1}\sin\left(\frac{\pi k}{2}\right)\left(1-2^{k-1}\right)\Gamma(1-k)\zeta(1-k),

(16)

where
\zeta(k)
is the Riemann zeta function
Wood 1992
and
\eta(0)=1/2
,
\eta(2)=\pi^{2}/12
. The
k=0
term corresponds to the
T=0

k=1
term gives the

correction. For the chemical potential in the normal state this gives (see Appendix A for details)

\displaystyle\frac{\mu(T)}{\varepsilon_{F}}=1+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}\frac{\left(\alpha-1\right)}{\alpha}\left(\frac{T}{\varepsilon_{F}}\right)^{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(T^{4}\right).

(17)

Inserting this into Eq. (
11
), we find a similar looking formula

\displaystyle\Pi_{\alpha}(T)=\nu_{F,\alpha}\left(1+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}\frac{\left(\alpha-1\right)}{\alpha}\left(\frac{T}{\varepsilon_{F}}\right)^{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(T^{4}\right)\right).

(18)

Note that for
\alpha>1
, the coefficient for the
T^{2}
term is positive. This indicates that the susceptibility in Eq. (
10
) diverges at a smaller
\lambda
at finite temperature than at
T=0
, signaling the possibility of reentrant behavior. We plot the full polarization for

IV
Conclusions

In this paper, we have analyzed the Stoner transition in a 2D isotropic system with dispersion
\varepsilon_{k}\propto k^{2\alpha}
at finite temperature. We demonstrated that the nature of the order parameter as well as the topology of the phase diagram depend strongly on the value of
\alpha
. For
\alpha<1
, the finite temperature behavior is the same as at
T=0
. That is, the transition is second order throughout the phase diagram. For
\alpha=1
, the parabolic dispersion, the phase transition is also second order at any finite temperatures. We recover the previous result at
T=0
that the onset of the ordered phase coincides with the divergence of the susceptibility, despite the order parameter evolving discontinuously.

In the range
1<\alpha<2
, the transition is second order at higher temperatures and becomes first order at low temperatures. For
\alpha\gtrsim 1.4
, the phase boundary exhibits reentrant behavior, with the transitions from the normal state to the ordered state and then back to the normal state occurring as temperature is increased. The reentrant behavior becomes more prevalent as
\alpha
is increased. For
\alpha\geq 2
, the transition is second order at low at and high temperatures and first order at intermediate temperatures. The intermediate range shrinks as
\alpha
increases, and vanishes for
\alpha\sim 2.15
. For larger
\alpha
, the transition is second order throughout the phase diagram. In the limit
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
, when the fermionic dispersion is essentially flat at small
k
, the phase diagram displays finger-type extension to the smallest
\lambda
.

We hope that our results, particularly the one about the reentrant behavior, will be relevant for non-twisted graphene multilayers. In the simplest tight binding model, the low energy band structure takes the form
\varepsilon_{k}\sim k^{n}
, where
n
is the number of graphene layers. The effective interaction
\lambda
in these systems can be tuned via the density of states by modifying particle number. That said, further work would be needed to determine the full phase diagram for an experimentally relevant two valley system. In addition, including trigonal warping terms in the dispersion will most likely affect the polarization of the isospins, as has already been determined at zero temperature for bilayer graphene
Mayrhofer and Chubukov 2025
.

Appendix B
Calculation of Order Parameter and Phase Boundaries

B.1
\alpha=1

We present here the calculation of the order parameter for
\alpha=1
. We first consider
\lambda\sim\lambda_{c}(T)
, which will yield Eq. (
22
) in the main text. We note that for
\alpha=1
the self-consistent equations are simplified since
\text{Li}_{1}(z)=-\log(1-z)
, and we can write

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta}{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{T}{2}\log\left(\frac{1+e^{\left(\bar{\mu}+\Delta\right)/T}}{1+e^{\left(\bar{\mu}-\Delta\right)/T}}\right),

(37)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{F}

\displaystyle=\frac{T}{2}\log\left(\left(1+e^{\left(\bar{\mu}+\Delta\right)/T}\right)\left(1+e^{\left(\bar{\mu}-\Delta\right)/T}\right)\right).

(38)

To begin, we expand Eq. (
38
) to order
\Delta^{2}
,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{F}=T\log\left(1+e^{\frac{\bar{\mu}}{T}}\right)+\frac{e^{\frac{\bar{\mu}}{T}}\Delta^{2}}{2T\left(1+e^{\frac{\bar{\mu}}{T}}\right)}.

(39)

Then, we can solve for
\bar{\mu}
up to order
\Delta^{2}
,

\displaystyle\bar{\mu}=T\log\left(e^{\frac{\varepsilon_{F}}{T}}-1\right)-\frac{e^{-\frac{\varepsilon_{F}}{T}}}{2T}\Delta^{2}.

(40)

We can see here that the corrections to the chemical potential due to a finite
\Delta
are suppressed by a factor
e^{-\frac{\varepsilon_{F}}{T}}
. We can then safely set
\Delta=0
in
\bar{\mu}
when determining
\Delta
at low temperatures in the vicinity of the quantum critical point. Expanding Eq. (
37
) to order
\Delta^{3}
, we find

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta}{\lambda}=\frac{\Delta e^{\frac{\bar{\mu}}{T}}}{1+e^{\frac{\bar{\mu}}{T}}}\left(1-\frac{e^{\frac{\bar{\mu}}{T}}-1}{6\left(e^{\frac{\bar{\mu}}{T}}+1\right)^{2}}\frac{\Delta^{2}}{T^{2}}\right).

(41)

Inserting the expression for
\bar{\mu}
we obtained above and solving for
\Delta^{2}
, we find

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}=\frac{6T^{2}e^{\frac{\varepsilon_{F}}{T}}}{\left(1-2e^{-\frac{\varepsilon_{F}}{T}}\right)}\frac{\lambda-\lambda_{c}(T)}{\lambda}\sim 6T^{2}e^{\frac{\varepsilon_{F}}{T}}\left(\lambda-\lambda_{c}(T)\right),

(42)

where
\lambda_{c}(T)=(1-e^{-\frac{\varepsilon_{F}}{T}})^{-1}
, and we have assumed
T\ll\varepsilon_{F}
,
\lambda\sim\lambda_{c}(T)
, and kept only terms leading order
e^{\frac{\varepsilon_{F}}{T}}
.

We now present the calculation for
\Delta
close to its saturated value, i.e. the expression in Eq. (
23
). We can add and subtract Eq. (
37
) and (
38
) to obtain two simpler equations for
\Delta
and
\bar{\mu}
,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{F}+\frac{\Delta}{\lambda}

\displaystyle=T\log\left(1+e^{(\bar{\mu}+\Delta)/T}\right),

(43)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{F}-\frac{\Delta}{\lambda}

\displaystyle=T\log\left(1+e^{(\bar{\mu}-\Delta)/T}\right).

(44)

Solving the first equation for
\bar{\mu}
, we find

\displaystyle\bar{\mu}=T\log\left(e^{\left(\varepsilon_{F}+\frac{\Delta}{\lambda}\right)/T}-1\right)-\Delta.

(45)

Inserting this into the second equation, we find

\displaystyle e^{(\varepsilon_{F}-\frac{\Delta}{\lambda})/T}-e^{\left(\varepsilon_{F}+\Delta\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}-2\right)\right)/T}+e^{-\frac{2\Delta}{T}}=1.

(46)

Here, we will make the ansatz that
\Delta/\lambda=\varepsilon_{F}-\delta
, and we will assume that we are in some vicinity of the critical point
\epsilon=\lambda-1>0
. The above equation is then written as

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=e^{\delta/T}-e^{\left(\delta-2\varepsilon_{F}\epsilon+2\delta\epsilon\right)/T}+e^{-2\left(\varepsilon_{F}-\delta\right)/T}.

(47)

Assuming that
\delta\ll\varepsilon_{F}
, we can then keep leading order terms in
\delta
to obtain

\displaystyle\delta=-T\log\left(1-e^{-\frac{2\varepsilon_{F}\epsilon}{T}}-e^{-\frac{2\varepsilon_{F}}{T}}\right)\implies\frac{\Delta}{\lambda}=\varepsilon_{F}+T\log\left(1-e^{-\frac{2\varepsilon_{F}\left(\lambda-1\right)}{T}}-e^{-\frac{2\varepsilon_{F}}{T}}\right).

(48)

For
\lambda<2
and
T\ll\varepsilon_{F}
, we can see
e^{-\frac{2\varepsilon_{F}\left(\lambda-1\right)}{T}}\gg e^{-\frac{2\varepsilon_{F}}{T}}
, so we can neglect the
e^{-\frac{2\varepsilon_{F}}{T}}
term in the logarithm when in the vicinity of the quantum critical point. Therefore, we arrive at the expression in Eq. (
23
) in the main text.

B.2
1<\alpha<2

We next present the calculations of the results for
1<\alpha<2
. To start, we add and subtract Eq. (
12
) and (
13
) to obtain two new equations,

\displaystyle\alpha+\frac{\Delta}{\lambda\varepsilon_{F}}=-\left(\frac{T}{\varepsilon_{F}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\text{Li}_{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\left(-e^{\left(\bar{\mu}+\Delta\right)/T}\right),

(49)

\displaystyle\alpha-\frac{\Delta}{\lambda\varepsilon_{F}}=-\left(\frac{T}{\varepsilon_{F}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\text{Li}_{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\left(-e^{\left(\bar{\mu}-\Delta\right)/T}\right).

(50)

To do this, we will first solve for the chemical potential of the system by examining Eq. (
49
). We can expand the polylogarithm in this equation by using the series expansion in Eq. (
15
). Solving for the chemical potential up to order
T^{2}
, we find

\displaystyle\frac{\bar{\mu}}{\varepsilon_{F}}=\left(1+\frac{\Delta}{\alpha\lambda\varepsilon_{F}}\right)^{\alpha}-\frac{\Delta}{\varepsilon_{F}}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}\left(1+\frac{\Delta}{\alpha\lambda\varepsilon_{F}}\right)^{-\alpha}\left(\frac{T}{\varepsilon_{F}}\right)^{2}.

(51)

We can then solve Eq. (
50
). At
T=0
, we will assume and later verify that
\Delta>\bar{\mu}
, so
e^{(\bar{\mu}-\Delta)/T}
is exponentially small at low temperatures. For any
s>0

\text{Li}_{s}(z)\sim z
for
z\ll 1
. The corrections to the polylogarithm are then exponentially small. We neglect these exponential corrections, as these will always be smaller than a power law correction at low temperatures. Therefore, we can safely set the right hand side of Eq. (
50
) to zero. We can then easily see that
\Delta=\alpha\lambda\varepsilon_{F}+\mathcal{O}\left(e^{\frac{\bar{\mu}-\alpha\lambda}{T}}\right)
and that the chemical potential can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{\bar{\mu}(T)}{\varepsilon_{F}}=2^{\alpha}-\alpha\lambda+\frac{\left(\alpha-1\right)\pi^{2}}{3\alpha}\frac{1}{2^{1+\alpha}}\left(\frac{T}{\varepsilon_{F}}\right)^{2}.

(52)

At
T=0
,
\bar{\mu}-\Delta=\varepsilon_{F}\left(2^{\alpha}-2\alpha\lambda\right)
. In the ordered state, for
1<\alpha<2
, we can see that
\bar{\mu}-\Delta<0
, so our initial assumption is justified. We can then insert these expressions for
\bar{\mu}
and
\Delta
into the free energy Eq. (
14
). To determine where the onset of the ordered state occurs, we must subtract off the normal state free energy, i.e. Eq. (
14
) with
\Delta=0
. Doing so and expanding to order
T^{2}
, we obtain for the free energy

\displaystyle\frac{\delta F}{N}=\frac{F_{ord}-F_{N}}{N}=\frac{\alpha\varepsilon_{F}}{2}\left(\frac{2\left(2^{\alpha}-1\right)}{\alpha\left(1+\alpha\right)}-\lambda\right)+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6\alpha}\left(1-2^{-\alpha}\right)\frac{T^{2}}{\varepsilon_{F}},

(53)

where
F_{N}
is the free energy in the normal state. Setting this equal to zero, we can solve for the critical
\lambda
to obtain

\displaystyle\lambda_{c}=\frac{2\left(2^{\alpha}-1\right)}{\alpha\left(1+\alpha\right)}+\frac{\left(1-2^{-\alpha}\right)}{3\alpha^{2}}\pi^{2}\left(\frac{T}{\varepsilon_{F}}\right)^{2}.

(54)

B.3
\alpha=2

We present the calculation of the critical interaction strength for
\alpha=2
. To start, we make the ansatz

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta}{\lambda}=2\varepsilon_{F}-A\sqrt{\varepsilon_{F}T}.

(55)

In addition, we assume that
\lambda=1
, since this is the location of the phase transition at
T=0
. The critical
\lambda
will vary with temperature, however we will show that the corrections to the order parameter from the temperature dependence of
\lambda
are subleading. We also note that, since
\bar{\mu}+\Delta>0
at low temperatures, the expansion in Eq. (
51
) is still valid. Then, using this ansatz for
\Delta
and Eq. (
51
), we write

\displaystyle\bar{\mu}-\Delta=\frac{A^{2}}{4}T.

(56)

Inserting this into Eq. (
50
) to obtain an equation for
A
,

\displaystyle A=-\sqrt{\pi}\text{Li}_{\frac{1}{2}}\left(-e^{\frac{A^{2}}{4}}\right),

(57)

where we have used
\Gamma(1/2)=\sqrt{\pi}
. Numerically solving this equation, we obtain
A\sim 1.4525
. To determine the critical interaction, we substitute these results into the free energy for the system in Eq. (
14
), and expand to leading order in temperature to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\delta F}{N\varepsilon_{F}}=1-\lambda-\left(1-\lambda\right)\frac{A^{2}T}{4\varepsilon_{F}}+\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{A^{3}}{6}+\sqrt{\pi}\text{Li}_{\frac{3}{2}}\left(-e^{\frac{A^{2}}{4}}\right)\right)\left(\frac{T}{\varepsilon_{F}}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(T^{2}\right).

(58)

We write
\lambda=1-\epsilon
and solve for
\lambda
such that the free energy in the ordered and normal states are equal. Setting
\delta F=0
and expanding in small
\epsilon
, we determine that, to leading order in temperature

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{A^{3}}{6}+\sqrt{\pi}\text{Li}_{\frac{3}{2}}\left(-e^{\frac{A^{2}}{4}}\right)\right)\left(\frac{T}{\varepsilon_{F}}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\sim 0.377\left(\frac{T}{\varepsilon_{F}}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}

(59)

\displaystyle\implies\lambda_{c}

\displaystyle=1-0.377\left(\frac{T}{\varepsilon_{F}}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}.

(60)

We note that in the initial ansatz for
\Delta
, we neglected the temperature dependence in
\lambda_{c}
. We can see that this assumption is justified, as the leading order correction in
\bar{\mu}-\Delta
is linear in
T
, whereas corrections arising from the temperature dependance of
\lambda_{c}
are proportional to
T^{\frac{3}{2}}
.

B.4
\alpha\rightarrow\infty

We analyze the critical coupling in the limit
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
, and show how to obtain the minimum value of
\lambda_{c}(T)
as written in Eq. (
29
). Since the transition is second order, it is sufficient to calculate the maximum value of polarization bubble as a function of
T
. However, to calculate
\Pi_{\alpha}(T)
, we first must determine the chemical potential of the system at large
\alpha
. To do so, we begin by considering equation for conservation of number of particles in the normal state,

\displaystyle 2\alpha\nu_{F,\alpha}\varepsilon_{F}

\displaystyle=2\nu_{F,\alpha}\varepsilon_{F}^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}d\varepsilon_{k}\varepsilon_{k}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-1}n_{F}\left(\varepsilon_{k}-\mu\right),

(61)

\displaystyle\implies\alpha

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{T}{\varepsilon_{F}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}I,\quad I=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dx\frac{x^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-1}}{1+e^{x-\frac{\mu}{T}}},

(62)

where we have written
x=\varepsilon_{k}/T
. We evaluate the integral in
I
by parts to obtain

\displaystyle I=\frac{\alpha x^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{1+e^{x-\frac{\mu}{T}}}\Big\rvert_{0}^{\infty}+\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha x^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{4\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{\left(x-\mu/T\right)}{2}\right)}.

(63)

We note that, for any
\alpha>0
, the first term will evaluate to zero. To evaluate the second term, we can then expand in large
\alpha
,

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha+\log(x)}{4\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{\left(x-\mu/T\right)}{2}\right)}+\mathcal{O}\left(\alpha^{-1}\right)

(64)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{1+e^{-\frac{\mu}{T}}}+\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\log(x)}{4\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{\left(x-\mu/T\right)}{2}\right)}+\mathcal{O}\left(\alpha^{-1}\right).

(65)

We note that
\cosh(x)^{2}
is peaked around
x=0
, so to logarithmic accuracy, we can write
I
as

\displaystyle I=\frac{\alpha}{1+e^{-\frac{\mu}{T}}}+\frac{\log\left(\frac{\mu}{T}\right)}{1+e^{-\frac{\mu}{T}}}.

(66)

Substituting this result into Eq. (
62
) and expanding
T^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}
to leading order in
\alpha
, we obtain

\displaystyle\alpha=\left(1+\frac{\log\frac{T}{\varepsilon_{F}}}{\alpha}\right)\left(\alpha+\log\left(\frac{\mu}{T}\right)\right)\frac{1}{1+e^{-\frac{\mu}{T}}}.

(67)

Neglecting the term proportion to
\alpha^{-1}
, we find for the chemical potential

\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{T}=\log\alpha-\log\left(\log\left(\frac{\mu}{\varepsilon_{F}}\right)\right).

(68)

We can then iteratively solve for
\mu
to leading order. Doing so, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{T}=\log\alpha-\log\left(\log\left(\frac{T}{\varepsilon_{F}}\log\alpha\right)\right).

(69)

Now, we can directly calculate the polarization bubble,

\displaystyle\Pi_{\alpha}(T)=-\nu_{F,\alpha}\varepsilon_{F}^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}d\varepsilon_{k}\varepsilon_{k}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-1}n_{F}^{\prime}(\varepsilon_{k}-\mu).

(70)

We proceed in a manner similar to what we did to calculate the chemical potential. We rescale
\varepsilon_{k}
by
T
, integrate by parts, and then expand
x^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}
to leading order in
\alpha
. Doing so, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\Pi_{\alpha}(T)}{\nu_{F,\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon_{F}}{4T}\left(1+\frac{\log\frac{T}{\varepsilon_{F}}}{\alpha}\right)\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dx\frac{\alpha+\log(x)}{\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{x-\mu/T}{2}\right)\coth\left(\frac{x-\mu/T}{2}\right)}

(71)

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon_{F}}{4T}\left(1+\frac{\log\frac{T}{\varepsilon_{F}}}{\alpha}\right)\left(\alpha\sech^{2}\left(\frac{\mu}{2T}\right)+\int\limits_{-\mu/T}^{\infty}dx\frac{\log(x+\frac{\mu}{T})}{\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)\coth\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)}\right).

(72)

We can expand log in powers of
x
, so
\log\left(x+\frac{\mu}{T}\right)\sim\log\left(\frac{\mu}{T}\right)+\frac{Tx}{\mu}
. However, we can quickly see that the term with
\log\left(\frac{\mu}{T}\right)
is exponentially suppressed by a factor
e^{-\frac{\mu}{T}}\sim\alpha^{-1}
, since the integrand is odd over
x
. We neglect this term, and instead include only the next order term with the lower bound set to
-\infty
, since
\mu/T\sim\log\alpha
. We can therefore write the above expression for
\Pi_{\alpha}(T)
as

\displaystyle\frac{\Pi_{\alpha}(T)}{\nu_{F,\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon_{F}}{4T}\left(1+\frac{\log\frac{T}{\varepsilon_{F}}}{\alpha}\right)\left(\alpha\sech^{2}\left(\frac{\mu}{2T}\right)+\frac{T}{\mu}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\frac{x}{\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)\coth\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)}\right)

(73)

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon_{F}}{4T}\left(1+\frac{\log\frac{T}{\varepsilon_{F}}}{\alpha}\right)\left(\alpha\sech^{2}\left(\frac{\mu}{2T}\right)+\frac{4T}{\mu}\right).

(74)

Now, we can insert the expression we had obtained for
\mu
into this expression to obtain, to subleading order in
\alpha

\displaystyle\frac{\Pi_{\alpha}(T)}{\nu_{F,\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon_{F}}{T}\left(\log\left(\frac{T}{\varepsilon_{F}}\log\alpha\right)+\frac{1}{\log\alpha}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\alpha^{-1}\right).

(75)

We can locate the temperature at which the polarization bubble is at a maximum by taking
\frac{\partial\Pi_{\alpha}(T)}{\partial T}=0
. Doing this, we find,

\displaystyle\frac{T_{m}}{\varepsilon_{F}}=\frac{e}{\log\alpha}\left(1-\frac{1}{\log\alpha}\right).

(76)

Reinserting this expression for the temperature into the polarization bubble, we obtain for the maximum value of the polarization bubble,

\displaystyle\frac{\Pi_{\alpha}(T_{m})}{\nu_{F,\alpha}}=\frac{\log\alpha+1}{e}.

(77)

We can then write
\text{min}\left(\lambda_{c}(T)\right)
to order
1/(\log\alpha)^{2}
as

\displaystyle\text{min}\left(\lambda_{c}(T)\right)=\frac{\nu_{F,\alpha}}{\Pi_{\alpha}(T_{m})}=\frac{e}{\log\alpha}\left(1-\frac{1}{\log\alpha}\right).
