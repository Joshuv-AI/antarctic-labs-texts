The burden and histopathological characteristics of early-onset colorectal cancer: a retrospective cross-sectional study at Ethiopia’s largest tertiary hospital (2019–2024)
Authors: Ahmed Abdela, Solomon Genet, Abdisa Tufa Bedada, Dinksira Bekele, Wajana Lako Labisso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe global rise in early-onset colorectal cancer (EOCRC) is a growing concern, particularly in low-resource settings. However, comprehensive data from Ethiopia are limited.AimThis study aimed to describe the clinicopathological profile and proportional hospital-based representation of EOCRC at Ethiopia’s largest tertiary referral center.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cross-sectional analysis of 630 histologically confirmed colorectal cancer (CRC) cases diagnosed at Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital between January 2019 and September 2024. EOCRC was defined as a diagnosis before the age of 50 years and late-onset CRC (LOCRC) as a diagnosis at the age of 50 years or older. Data on demographics, tumor location, histology, differentiation grade, and TNM stage were also extracted. Descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, and binary logistic regression were used for the analysis.ResultsOf the 630 CRC cases, 292 (46.3%) were EOCRC (mean age 36.1 years, SD 7.9). Rectal tumors were significantly more common in EOCRC (31.2% vs. 22.5%, p = 0.012), as were poorly differentiated/undifferentiated tumors (21.9% vs. 13.0%, p = 0.003). Metastasis at diagnosis was less frequent in the EOCRC group (27.4% vs. 66.3%, p < 0.001). Multivariable analysis identified rectal location (adjusted odds ratio (AOR) = 1.60, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.11–2.30) and poor differentiation (AOR = 1.94, 95% CI 1.25–3.00) as independent predictors of EOCRC, while metastasis was inversely associated (AOR = 0.20, 95% CI 0.14–0.29).ConclusionIn this hospital-based sample, EOCRC constitutes a substantial proportion of CRC cases, with distinct clinicopathological features, including rectal predilection and aggressive histology. Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most commonly diagnosed cancer and the second leading cause of cancer death worldwide, with a growing burden in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [1]. While traditionally considered a disease of older adults, a concerning rise in the incidence of early-onset colorectal cancer (EOCRC), typically defined as diagnosis before age 50, has been observed globally over the past two decades [2, 3]. This trend is especially alarming in sub-Saharan Africa, where healthcare systems are strained by infectious diseases and the rising burden of non-communicable diseases [4].

In some high-income countries, notably the USA and Canada, the rise in EOCRC has led to updated screening guidelines recommending initiation of average-risk screening at 45 years of age [5]. However, in Ethiopia and similar settings, population-level screening programs are absent. Public awareness of CRC symptoms, particularly among younger individuals and primary healthcare providers remains critically low [6]. This often results in prolonged patient intervals and diagnostic delays, leading to advanced-stage disease presentation.

EOCRC has been proposed as a potentially distinct clinicobiological entity, although prospective validation in African populations is lacking. EOCRC often presents with a higher prevalence in the distal colon and rectum, more aggressive histopathological subtypes, and a potentially more advanced stage at diagnosis compared to late-onset CRC (LOCRC) [7, 8]. These characteristics complicate therapeutic management and contribute to a significant loss of productive life years.

Robust epidemiological data on CRC in Ethiopia are scarce. The country lacks a population-based national cancer registry; however, a subnational registry in Addis Ababa has identified CRC as the leading cancer in the capital [9]. Existing evidence remains fragmented and is derived from small-scale, single-center studies. Limited studies from sub-Saharan Africa suggest a potentially higher hospital-based proportion of young-onset CRC [10, 11]. However, no large-scale study from Ethiopia has systematically compared EOCRC versus LOCRC in a tertiary care setting. Consequently, the clinical and pathological profiles of EOCRC in Ethiopia are poorly defined.

This study aimed to provide a comprehensive proportional hospital-based representation, tumor location, and histopathological characteristics of EOCRC at Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital (TASH), Ethiopia’s largest oncology referral center. By comparing EOCRC and LOCRC cases, we aimed to inform clinical practice and guide future research priorities for this vulnerable population.

Methods

Study setting and design

We conducted an institution-based, retrospective, cross-sectional study at the Pathology Department of Tikur Anbessa Specialized Teaching Hospital (TASH) in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. TASH is Ethiopia's largest referral teaching hospital, serving as the primary oncology center with an estimated 60,000 cancer patient visits per year. The pathology Department provides comprehensive cancer diagnostic services to the hospital and receives referrals from across the country.

This study aimed to describe the proportional hospital-based representation, tumor location, and histopathological characteristics of early onset colorectal cancer (EOCRC) compared to late‑onset CRC (LOCRC) at TASH. The secondary aim was to identify factors independently associated with EOCRC.

This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross‑sectional studies.

Study population and eligibility

We included all patients with a confirmed histological diagnosis of primary colorectal adenocarcinoma at TASH between January 2019 and September 2024. Patients with complete medical and pathological records containing information on age, sex, tumor location, histology, grade, and stage were included. We excluded cases with incomplete demographic information, duplicate entries, insufficient pathological details, or non-adenocarcinoma histologies (e.g., lymphoma and gastrointestinal stromal tumor). Of 687 initially identified CRC cases, 630 met the inclusion criteria.

Cohort definitions

Early-onset colorectal cancer (EOCRC): Diagnosis at age < 50 years. This threshold is consistent with international consensus definitions adopted by the American Cancer Society, the World Health Organization, and major colorectal cancer guidelines [3, 5], and no conflicting national Ethiopian guideline exists for this cutoff.

Late-onset colorectal cancer (LOCRC): Diagnosis at age ≥ 50 years.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS Version 27. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Age was normally distributed (p > 0.05) and is presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between EOCRC and LOCRC groups were performed using the independent samples t‑test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi‑square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Univariable analyses were conducted for all demographic, tumor location, histopathological, and staging variables. Variables with a p-value < 0.10 in univariable analysis were entered into a multivariable binary logistic regression model to identify factors independently associated with EOCRC. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). All tests were two-tailed; p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics and Research Committee of the Pathology Department, College of Health Sciences, Addis Ababa University (Ref No. SOM/PTHL/068/2017). The study utilized only secondary data and the principles of confidentiality and privacy were strictly maintained. The data were accessed for research purposes between October 1, 2024, and December 15, 2024. The authors did not have access to information that could identify individual participants at any point during this study, as all data were fully anonymized prior to analysis. The requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized patient data.

Results

Descriptive results

A total of 630 CRC cases met inclusion criteria. The mean age at diagnosis was 50.3 years (SD 15.8; range 16–87), with a median of 50 years. EOCRC accounted for 46.3% (292/630) of cases, with a mean age of 36.1 years (SD 7.9). LOCRC constituted 53.7% (338/630), with a mean age of 62.6 years (SD 9.3). Clinicopathological characteristics by age group

Regarding tumor location, the rectum was the most frequent tumor site overall (26.5%, 167/630) and was significantly more common in patients with EOCRC (31.2% vs. 22.5%, p = 0.012). Multiple/overlapping sites were the second most common category (16.8%), followed by the sigmoid colon (14.8%). Left-sided tumors (rectum, sigmoid, descending colon) accounted for 55.6% of all cases (Table 1).

Conventional adenocarcinoma was the most prevalent histological subtype (67.3%). Mucinous and signet-ring cell carcinomas were numerically more common in EOCRC (24.7% vs 20.1%), though not statistically significant (p = 0.421). EOCRC had a significantly higher proportion of poorly differentiated/undifferentiated tumors (21.9% vs 13.0%, p = 0.003) (Table 1).

Most patients presented with advanced-stage disease (stages III/IV: 71.1%). LOCRC had a significantly higher proportion of stage IV disease at diagnosis (39.6% vs 18.8%, p < 0.001) and metastasis (66.3% vs 27.4%, p < 0.001) (Table 1). Metastasis occurred most commonly in the liver (42.1%), peritoneum (28.6%), and lung (15.8%).

Statistical analysis results

Multivariable logistic regression identified rectal location (AOR = 1.60, 95% CI 1.11–2.30) and poorly/undifferentiated histology (AOR = 1.94, 95% CI 1.25–3.00) as independent associated factors for EOCRC in this hospital-based sample. Metastasis at diagnosis was inversely associated with EOCRC (AOR = 0.20, 95% CI 0.14–0.29). Sex and aggressive histology (mucinous/signet-ring) were not independently associated with EOCRC (Table 2).

Discussion

This hospital-based study of 630 CRC patients at Ethiopia’s largest tertiary referral center revealed that EOCRC constitutes a substantial proportion (46.3%) of diagnosed cases. This proportion is higher than the 10–20% typically reported in Western population-based studies [7, 8], but must be interpreted in light of Ethiopia’s demographic structure, where approximately 60% of the population is aged less than 25 years and life expectancy at birth is 67.3 years [12]. In such a young population, a higher proportional representation of EOCRC among diagnosed cancers is expected, even without an increased age-specific risk of CRC. Therefore, our findings describe an important clinical reality for tertiary care providers rather than a population-level epidemiological shift.

A direct comparison of our findings with those of other African studies reveals both similarities and important contextual differences. A Nigerian study by Holowatyj et al. [10] reported that 42.1% of CRC cases occurred in patients aged less than 50 years, closely matching our 46.3% proportion. Similarly, a multicenter Egyptian study by Rashad et al. [13] reported that EOCRC comprised 38.7% of CRC cases, with rectal tumors (34.2%) and poorly differentiated histology (19.7%) being significantly more common in younger patients, which closely parallels our observations. Similarly, a recent population-based cancer registry study from Addis Ababa Gizaw et al. (2026) documented CRC as one of the leading cancers in the capital, although age-specific EOCRC proportions were lower in the population-based setting (approximately 28%) than in our hospital-based series, reflecting the referral bias inherent to tertiary care [9]. Across sub-Saharan Africa, hospital-based EOCRC proportions range from 35 to 55%, consistently exceeding Western population-based estimates of 10–20% [7, 8], likely driven by the region’s young demographic structure and selective referral of younger patients to tertiary centers.

Recent large‑scale population‑based studies from high‑income countries continue to confirm that EOCRC accounts for 10–20% of CRC diagnoses and is often associated with left‑sided tumors and advanced stage at presentation [14–16]. However, these cohorts were derived from populations with established screening programs and older demographic structures. In contrast, our hospital‑based findings from Ethiopia, where approximately 60% of the population is under age 25, demonstrate a substantially higher proportional burden (46.3%) and, paradoxically, a lower metastatic rate at diagnosis in EOCRC (27.4% vs. 66.3% in LOCRC). This highlights the critical influence of demographic context and referral bias on observed EOCRC proportions, a nuance that is often overlooked in global comparisons. To our knowledge, this is one of the largest histopathologically characterized EOCRC cohorts from sub-Saharan Africa, providing essential baseline data for a region where population‑based screening is absent and CRC epidemiology remains poorly defined.

The predominance of rectal tumors in patients with EOCRC (31.2%) aligns with global studies reporting a distal predilection in younger cohorts [17]. This anatomical trend may influence symptomatology, with rectal cancers more likely to cause tenesmus, rectal bleeding, or altered bowel habit symptoms, which may prompt earlier medical consultation in younger, health-aware individuals. This could partly explain the paradoxical finding of lower metastatic burden at diagnosis in patients with EOCRC despite more aggressive histology. Alternatively, biological differences in tumor progression between younger and older adults may exist, which requires molecular investigation.

Notably, the frequency of mucinous and signet-ring histologies in our EOCRC cohort (24.7%) aligns with the 22–28% range reported in other African case series [10, 11], suggesting that aggressive histologic subtypes may be disproportionately represented among younger African CRC patients compared to Western cohorts, where such histologies account for 10–15% of EOCRC cases [18].

Multivariate analysis confirmed rectal location and poor differentiation as independent predictors of EOCRC, reinforcing the distinct clinicopathological profile of CRC in younger patients. The strong inverse association with metastasis at diagnosis may reflect a combination of lead-time bias (younger patients seeking care earlier for symptoms), different biological behaviors, or possibly a higher threshold for biopsy or imaging confirmation of metastasis in older patients with comorbidities. This finding is broadly consistent with international data: US population-based series (SEER) have reported that EOCRC patients are more likely to present with localized or regional disease than older patients [8, 16]. In contrast, some African and South Asian series have reported higher rates of advanced-stage disease at presentation among young patients, attributed to delayed healthcare-seeking and limited diagnostic access [19]. The lower metastatic rate in our EOCRC cohort may therefore reflect a combination of symptom-driven earlier presentation (rectal bleeding prompting earlier consultation in younger adults) and possible under detection of distant metastases due to limited imaging availability for older patients with comorbidities in our setting.

Notably, most patients presented with advanced-stage disease (stages III/IV: 71.1%), underscoring systemic delays in diagnosis due to limited awareness, cultural stigma, and diagnostic constraints in the primary care. Although patients with EOCRC presented with less metastatic disease than those with LOCRC, they were still largely diagnosed at locally advanced stages, highlighting a critical need for improved early detection pathways.

The lower prevalence of stage IV disease in EOCRC contrasts with that in some Western studies, where EOCRC often presents at advanced stages [19]. This discrepancy may be attributable to differences in healthcare-seeking behavior, symptom interpretation, or access to tertiary referrals. In our setting, younger adults may be more likely to persist in seeking care for symptoms like rectal bleeding, whereas older adults may attribute such symptoms to benign causes.

At Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital, the standard of care for non-metastatic CRC involves surgical resection (either open or laparoscopic when feasible), followed by adjuvant chemotherapy with FOLFOX or capecitabine-based regimens for stage III and high-risk stage II disease. A cost-effectiveness modeling study from the South African public health sector supports the use of these regimens, particularly 3 months of CAPOX, as a viable strategy in resource-limited African settings [14]. For locally advanced rectal cancer, neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (45–50.4 Gy with concurrent capecitabine) is recommended, although radiotherapy access remains limited owing to equipment constraints. A recent study from Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital reported that only 24.2% of patients with locally advanced rectal cancer received radiotherapy, with a median waiting time of 10 months, highlighting the substantial barriers to timely neoadjuvant treatment [15]. Metastatic disease is managed with palliative chemotherapy (FOLFOX or FOLFIRI), with bevacizumab available only for patients with private insurance or out-of-pocket payments. Notably, due to the absence of a national screening program and limited public awareness, most patients present with symptomatic, advanced-stage disease, as reflected in our finding that 71.1% presented with stage III/IV disease. The high proportion of patients with EOCRC presenting with stage III disease (53.4%) rather than stage IV (18.8%) suggests that younger patients may be diagnosed at a potentially curative stage, reinforcing the importance of timely referral pathways.

Our study had several limitations. As a single-center, retrospective analysis of referred cases, selection bias is likely: TASH receives more complex cases, and patients with early-stage or unresected disease may be underrepresented. The lack of population-based denominators precludes calculation of incidence rates. We excluded cases with incomplete pathological data, which may have disproportionately affected advanced/unresectable cancers. Molecular data (e.g., microsatellite instability, Lynch syndrome testing, mismatch repair (MMR) protein status, and RAS/BRAF mutations) were unavailable, limiting the exploration of genetic underpinnings. Data on family history, lifestyle factors, and comorbidities, HIV status and long-term outcomes were not systematically recorded. Management details (surgical resection rates, neoadjuvant therapy, and adjuvant chemotherapy) could not be reliably extracted because of incomplete documentation. Finally, the use of clinical/radiological staging in unresected cases may introduce misclassification, although all cases were reviewed in a multidisciplinary setting. Future prospective multicenter studies incorporating family history, HIV testing, molecular profiling, and standardized treatment protocols are urgently needed.

Given the high proportion of rectal tumors and aggressive histology in EOCRC, awareness campaigns should prioritize symptoms such as rectal bleeding and persistent bowel habit changes among younger adults and primary care providers. Because population-wide screening is currently infeasible, diagnostic pathways for symptomatic patients including timely endoscopy and histopathology require strengthening as an immediate priority. Concurrently, selective screening should target high-risk individuals with a family history of CRC or genetic syndromes. Finally, sustained investment in cancer registration is essential to generate the population-based data needed to refine these strategies over time.

Conclusion

This hospital-based study highlights that EOCRC represents a substantial clinical burden at Ethiopia’s largest tertiary referral center, characterized by rectal predilection and aggressive histology. Although the high proportion of EOCRC is influenced by Ethiopia’s young population structure, the distinct clinicopathological profile calls for heightened clinical suspicion in younger adults. These findings underscore the need for targeted awareness initiatives, strengthened diagnostic capacity, and prospective multicenter studies incorporating molecular and lifestyle data to guide prevention and early detection strategies in resource-limited settings.
