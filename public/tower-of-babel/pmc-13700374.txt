ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe developed a novel, community pharmacy-based, pharmacist-driven atrial fibrillation (AF) stroke prevention model involving pharmacist prescription of oral anticoagulants (OAC) in Alberta, Canada. Generalizability to healthcare systems outside Canada is unknown.ObjectiveWe recruited 5 California pharmacies for a proof-of-concept pilot study aimed at evaluating the prevalence of “actionable” AF and the feasibility, safety, and barriers to implementing a pharmacist OAC prescription program.MethodsBefore implementation, pharmacist and physician stakeholders were surveyed to identify potential barriers. Pharmacists identified adults aged ≥60 years with a CHA2DS2-VASc score of ≥2 (men) or ≥3 (women), and “actionable” AF, defined as (1) untreated, (2) sub-optimally treated, or (3) inappropriately treated. Participants were enrolled in a 3-month intervention in which pharmacists optimized OAC. Participant satisfaction was assessed at 3 months. Safety endpoints were assessed at 6, 9, and 12 months. Pharmacist-reported implementation barriers were assessed at 1 and 3 months.Results“Actionable” AF prevalence ranged from 14% to 41%. A total of 14 participants were enrolled. At baseline, 50% of participants were untreated, whereas 50% were sub-optimally treated. At 3 months, 91% of participants were on guideline-concordant OAC therapy. There were no major bleeding events and no prescription overrides. Participants reported high satisfaction with pharmacist services. Perceived barriers reflected reported the following implementation challenges: (1) lack of time, (2) insufficient access to medical records, and (3) adequate reimbursement.ConclusionCommunity-based, pharmacist-driven OAC prescription offers an opportunity to effectively close gaps in stroke risk reduction therapy for patients with AF. Scalability in the United States will depend on policy and infrastructure changes that address implementation challenges.Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT04907825

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common heart rhythm disorder worldwide and the leading cause of stroke in older adults.1,2 AF-related strokes are more severe and are associated with greater long-term morbidity and mortality.3 The human costs are compounded by the economic burden of treating stroke, which has reached $50 billion annually in the United States.2 Despite robust evidence that oral anticoagulation (OAC) therapy is safe and effective for stroke prevention, the availability of simple stroke risk stratification schemes4,5 and guidelines identifying which patients would benefit from OAC therapy,3 with less than two-thirds of eligible patients with AF receiving an OAC prescription.6 This highlights the need for innovative strategies to improve stroke prevention therapy delivery.

Hundreds of studies have demonstrated the positive impact of pharmacists on guideline-directed targets and cardiovascular disease outcomes.7, 8, 9, 10 The first pharmacist-led anticoagulation clinic was established at San Francisco General Hospital in 197411 with positive preliminary evaluations leading to nationwide expansion.12 Today, over 3000 such clinics operate across the US,13 improving outcomes and patient satisfaction compared with other models of care.14, 15, 16 However, these services are largely confined to hospital and clinic settings, limiting their reach. Expanding clinical services to community pharmacies presents a unique opportunity to close care gaps in a venue that is both accessible and convenient. Of the US population, 89% lives within a 5-mile radius of a pharmacy,17 and older Americans make an average of 13–16 visits to pharmacies per year18; outpacing visits to physician offices by a factor of 3.19

In the Program for the Identification of Actionable Atrial Fibrillation Study, 80 participants from 27 community pharmacies in Alberta, Canada, were randomized to either a pharmacist intervention, in which pharmacists used electronic health records (EHR) to prescribe guideline-based OAC, or an enhanced usual care group, in which the pharmacist notified the participant’s primary care provider (PCP) that OAC therapy was suboptimal.18 At 3 months, guideline-concordant OAC use was significantly higher in the pharmacist intervention group (92.3%) compared with the enhanced usual care group (56.1%) (P < .001), with an absolute increase of 34% and a number needed to treat of 3.18

Generalizability to healthcare systems outside Canada remains uncertain, particularly the US, which has a fragmented, mixed public-private healthcare system. Thus, a feasibility study assessing potential barriers was a necessary precondition before undertaking a prospective, open-label, cluster-randomized controlled trial in the US. We chose to perform this study in California (CA), which, including Alberta, is among the most progressive US states for pharmacy practice. In 2013, CA Senate Bill 493 recognized pharmacists as healthcare providers, allowing them to perform patient assessments, order and interpret laboratory results, and manage drug therapy under collaborative practice agreements (CPAs)—making California an ideal place to pilot this innovative model.20

Methods

Study design and oversight

The APOTHECARY AF Study was a single-arm pilot designed to assess the feasibility and potential barriers to community-based, pharmacist-led management of OAC therapy for stroke prevention. The 12-month study included a “pre-implementation phase” to assess stakeholder-perceived barriers, a 3-month “active intervention phase” during which pharmacists optimized OAC therapy for enrolled participants, and a 9-month “passive follow-up phase” during which the study team evaluated safety endpoints. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Cedars-Sinai Medical Center and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the CONSORT guidelines. Reliance agreements were executed with participating pharmacy sites that adhered to the approved protocol. All participants provided written informed consent.

Stakeholder perceived barriers to pharmacist OAC prescribing

Before study implementation, members of the California Pharmacists Association (CPhA) were invited to participate in a web-based, cross-sectional survey designed to assess (1) knowledge of stroke prevention therapy in AF using 5 case scenarios with gold-standard decisions based on the 2019 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association/Heart Rhythm Society AF guidelines and (2) potential barriers to pharmacist-managed OAC (quantified on a 5-point scale, with 1 indicating “least important” and 5 indicating “most important”). The survey was distributed via email on 4 separate occasions over 1 month.

A physician focus group was also convened to assess physician attitudes toward physician–pharmacist collaboration. Physicians were recruited via informal email invitations and provided verbal consent to participate. Semi-qualitative interviews were conducted virtually via a secure communication platform.

Study setting

The study was conducted in community and outpatient hospital pharmacies across 3 Southern California counties—Los Angeles, Orange, and San Bernardino. Pharmacies were selected based on their ability to access EHR, dedicate staff to clinical research activities, and establish CPAs with local providers.

Pharmacist training

Participating pharmacists completed a comprehensive web-based training program consisting of 6 modules covering AF epidemiology, stroke risk stratification, OAC indications and dosing, bleeding risk assessment, informed consent, screening and enrollment processes, other research procedures, and tips for integrating research into existing practice. The online training, which took approximately 2.5 hours to complete, was designed and narrated by a cardiac electrophysiologist and a clinical research pharmacist. Competency was assessed at the end of the training using the same case scenarios as the CPhA survey. Additional training was provided through in-person or virtual meetings with the study team.

Study population

From January 2023 to August 2025, participating pharmacists screened their clientele to identify individuals aged more than 60 years with a CHA2DS2-VASc score of ≥3 for women or ≥2 for men,3 and “actionable” AF, defined as either (1) untreated (no OAC prescription), (2) sub-optimally treated (over- or underdosed OAC), or (3) inappropriately treated (wrong drug).

Patients with valvular heart disease (mitral stenosis, prosthetic valve, or valve repair), recent history of major bleeding (defined as overt bleeding at a critical site; a bleed requiring hospitalization; or any bleed with ≥2 g/dL drop in hemoglobin or requiring transfusion of ≥2 units packed cells), end-stage renal disease, foreshortened life expectancy, cognitive dysfunction, major surgery in the prior 30 days, history of alcohol abuse, and uncontrolled hypertension (defined as systolic blood pressure of >160 mm Hg) were excluded. Patients were also excluded if they required OAC for other conditions (deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, percutaneous coronary intervention, or left ventricular thrombus), were on dual antiplatelet therapy, were on an aspirin dose of >100 mg and unwilling to decrease it, were pregnant, or were unable to read or comprehend instructions in English or Spanish.

Study intervention and assessments

Eligible participants were identified in 3 ways as follows: (1) self-referral in response to advertisements, (2) prospectively at the time of OAC or antiarrhythmic prescription fill, or (3) retrospectively during a monthly review of all OAC prescriptions filled by the pharmacy (Supplemental Figure 1).

Consented participants’ PCPs were notified of study enrollment via faxed letter. A customized, California Board of Pharmacy-approved, CPA template agreement outlining OAC management was also faxed and signed by the participant’s PCP before pharmacist intervention. Baseline assessments included weight and blood pressure measurements, a review of demographic characteristics, recent laboratory data (complete blood count, basic metabolic panel, and liver function tests), medical history, and current medications. Pharmacists provided tailored AF education using American Heart Association materials and initiated or adjusted OAC therapy. Drug choice was determined by cost and insurance formulary, with direct OACs (DOACs) preferred over warfarin. All OAC therapy changes were communicated to participants’ PCPs via faxed letter.

Follow-up visits at 1 and 3 months were conducted either in-person or virtually to evaluate safety and adherence. An OAC monitoring checklist (Supplemental Figure 2) was reviewed along with new medical problems, laboratory data, and acute care utilization (urgent care or emergency department visits and/or hospitalization). If required, OAC therapy was optimized, and PCPs were notified. Participants received $25 per pharmacist visit to offset the costs of generic drugs and transportation to pharmacies.

At 3 months, PCPs were notified of the study’s end via a final faxed letter to ensure transition of care. Participants completed a confidential, web-based “Exit Survey” that assessed satisfaction with pharmacist services and shared decision-making (quantified on a 4-point Likert scale, with 1 indicating “strongly agree” and 4 indicating “strongly disagree”).

Participants were passively followed for an additional 9 months to assess adherence to OAC therapy, OAC therapy appropriateness, and safety endpoints. This was achieved by automated texts sent to participants asking them to self-report adverse events and acute care utilization at 6, 9, and 12 months, and reviewing dispensing and medical records.

Participating pharmacists were surveyed at 1 and 3 months post-implementation to assess comfort with recruitment, screening or enrollment times, and barriers to study implementation (quantified on a 5-point scale, with 1 indicating “never experienced” and 5 indicating “always experienced”). Pharmacists received $25 for completing the surveys, as well as a $350 “reimbursement” payment for each participant who completed the protocol.

Study outcomes

Outcome measures include the prevalence of “actionable” AF, stakeholder-perceived barriers to pharmacist OAC prescribing, pharmacist-reported barriers to implementation, OAC therapy adherence (defined as the proportion of prescription days covered [PDC] ≥80%), frequency of safety events (ie, minor or major bleeding, stroke, transient ischemic attack, systemic embolism, acute coronary syndrome, and all-cause mortality), acute care utilization, PCP prescription overrides, and patient-reported satisfaction with pharmacist services.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed from December 2025 through February 2026. A convenience sample (n = 20) was selected for this feasibility pilot study. The prevalence of “actionable” AF was calculated as the number of individuals with “actionable” AF divided by the total number of individuals screened, multiplied by 100 (%). Median and interquartile range (IQR) values were derived from the Likert scale used to assess perceived barriers to pharmacist OAC prescribing, barriers to implementation, and satisfaction with pharmacist services. Focus group audio recordings were analyzed with discussions undergoing thematic analysis and qualitative coding. Descriptive statistics were used to examine the relative frequencies of responses for undertreated AF, adverse events, acute care utilization, and PCP prescription overrides.

Results

Stakeholder perceived barriers to pharmacist OAC prescribing

A total of 57 CPhA pharmacists completed the web-based survey over 2 months. Correct responses to case-based scenarios ranged from 14% to 95%. The primary perceived barriers to pharmacist-OAC management were as follows: insufficient reimbursement (median, 5 [IQR, 3–5]), limited access to medical records (median, 4 [IQR, 3–5]), inadequate support from management (median, 3 [IQR, 3–5]), and lack of time (median, 4 [IQR, 3–5]) (Figure 1, Supplemental Table 1). Of respondents, 84% supported pharmacist OAC prescribing if barriers were addressed. Four internists participating in our physician focus group felt shared care with pharmacists would be the future of health care delivery and were comfortable collaborating with adequately trained pharmacists. Physicians did not believe shared care with pharmacists would result in poorer quality of care or affect satisfaction with physicians. Physicians preferred communicating via bi-directional EHR messaging systems over fax; however, limited EHR permissions (ie, read-only) meant fax was used to communicate changes to OAC therapy.

Study site and participant characteristics

Five pharmacies, representing community pharmacy (n = 2) and outpatient hospital (n = 3) practice settings, and large (n = 3, Los Angeles, CA) and medium urban areas (n = 2, Palm Springs, CA, and Santa Ana, CA) participated. Independently owned community pharmacies and outpatient hospital pharmacies all had access to either Cerner or EPIC EHR systems, although the degree of access (read vs read-write) varied. The mean time to site activation was 6 weeks. One site was dropped due to unexpected closure. Notably, participating pharmacists who completed our structured online training program answered case-based scenarios with 80% to 100% accuracy.

From January 2023 to August 2025, 4197 patients with AF were screened across all sites, with 927 meeting inclusion criteria, yielding an overall “actionable” AF prevalence of 22% (range, 14%–41%). Notably, 850 patients were excluded. A history of major bleeding, end-stage renal disease, and dual antiplatelet therapy were the primary drivers, suggesting relative contraindications may underlie the significant proportion of people who are not anticoagulated. A total of 14 patients were enrolled in the 12-month intervention (Figure 2). Participant clinical and safety outcomes

Safety outcomes

There were no serious treatment-related adverse events. Epistaxis, bruising, and trace hematuria were reported by 3 participants during the active intervention phase; all resolved, and all remained on their prescribed OAC therapy (Supplemental Table 2). During passive follow-up, 2 participants elected to undergo Watchman procedures; 1 later discontinued OAC therapy, whereas the other remained on OAC due to a post-op pulmonary embolism. There was 1 participant death, adjudicated by physician monitors, and determined to be unrelated to study participation.

Acute care utilization was not affected by study participation. Three participants reported emergency department (ED) visits (for a fall, upper-extremity weakness, and dysphagia), and 5 reported hospitalizations (for a fall, cholecystitis, ESRD, post-op pulmonary embolism, and community-acquired pneumonia). There were no PCP overrides of pharmacist prescriptions throughout the study.

Patient satisfaction

Participants reported high satisfaction with pharmacist services: 91% reported that care exceeded their expectations and 9% reported that it met expectations. The median overall satisfaction score was 1.0 (IQR, 0). The median satisfaction score for each survey item is summarized in Supplemental Table 3.

Barriers to implementation

The primary barriers to implementation were lack of time (median, 4 [IQR, 3–4.5]) and insufficient access to medical records (median, 4 [IQR, 2.5–4]) (Figure 3, Supplemental Table 4). These concerns mirrored perceived barriers reported by CPhA pharmacists and remained unchanged over time (1-month vs 3-month surveys). Of participating pharmacists, 78% expressed willingness to continue study activities. Notably, “insufficient support from management” and “lack of reimbursement” were less often reported as barriers, given the CPhA survey informed site selection criteria and a “reimbursement” stipend.Figure 3Pharmacist-reported barriers to implementation. Discussion

Over the past 2 decades, anticoagulation rates in patients with AF have improved only modestly despite the introduction of DOACs and physician-targeted strategies to reduce clinical inertia and modify risk perception. Currently, an estimated 30% of patients with AF in the US are not anticoagulated,6 whereas another 20% are suboptimally treated.21 Here, we observe an “actionable” AF prevalence of 22% (range, 14%–41%), highlighting the need for alternative strategies to close gaps in stroke prevention therapy.

This feasibility pilot study builds on prior work in Alberta, Canada,18 demonstrating trained community pharmacists’ ability to correctly identify and treat “actionable” patients with AF at risk for stroke. Increased rates of guideline-concordant OAC prescription use, coupled with high adherence, low adverse events, and high patient satisfaction, suggest this model could be a viable strategy.

Implementation of the protocol revealed several key learnings. First, although pharmacists’ scope of practice has continued to evolve, there remains a basic lack of understanding of pharmacists’ skill set among patients (and sometimes providers), leading to reluctance to receive pharmacist-provided care. Building partnerships with local providers, clinics, and health systems can establish direct referral pathways that may allay patient concerns about receiving clinical pharmacy services.

Second, although participating sites had EHR access, permissions varied by site and were sometimes limited to “read-only” access. These restrictions, coupled with data fragmentation across multiple EHR systems, hampered pharmacists’ ability to determine eligibility, order labs and medications, and communicate with providers. Transitioning from siloed pharmacy systems to interconnected platforms with full, read–write EHR interoperability will be essential to improving access to care in community pharmacy settings.

Finally, despite site selection criteria favoring sites with management support and dedicated time for clinical research, a lack of time due to competing priorities was the most commonly cited barrier to implementation. This likely contributed to lower-than-expected enrollment and suggests that although pharmacists are well positioned to assume a greater role in managing chronic disease, many, such as PCPs, are overburdened by their current workload. Reimbursement for clinical services may be the missing piece, as increased revenue would enable better staffing levels. Although progress has been made with many state Medicaid programs and commercial, value-based insurance plans, pharmacists still lack universal federal provider status under Medicare Part B, which limits direct fee-for-service billing and results in patchy, program-specific reimbursement. Advocacy continues in this area, but success will likely hinge on support from provider (professional) organizations, which to date has been lacking.

Study limitations include the absence of a control group, a low response rate to the CPhA survey, and a small convenience sample that introduces potential selection bias. Extensive exclusion criteria, implemented to assuage provider concerns for patient safety, resulted in a high screen-failure rate and may limit the study’s generalizability. Additionally, California’s progressive pharmacy practice permissions restrict the applicability of findings to states with similar policies.22,23 Finally, although participant financial incentives may have influenced the intervention’s outcomes, data indicate they have only modest effects on medication adherence,24 which remained high (from months 3 to 12) without further payments.

The success of a future cluster-randomized controlled trial depends on identifying pharmacy sites with the infrastructure required to conduct study activities, including read-write EHR access, adequate staffing, and established relationships with local providers. The use of e-prescribing protocols with clinical enrichments and/or artificial intelligence platforms that interface with EHR systems and accelerate screening procedures would enhance efficiency.

Finally, although “true” community pharmacy sites are the ideal venue for improving healthcare accessibility, current conditions suggest that health system-owned or affiliated outpatient pharmacies, or those within value-based, clinically integrated networks, are best suited for this type of intervention.

Conclusion

A community-based, pharmacist-driven OAC prescription model is feasible with appropriate infrastructure and offers a significant opportunity to close gaps in stroke prevention for patients with AF. Scalability will require substantive changes to US healthcare policy and infrastructure, including standardized pharmacist–provider collaborative practice permissions, expanded EHR access, and improved reimbursement pathways.
