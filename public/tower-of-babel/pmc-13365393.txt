Risk-adapted therapy guided by human papillomavirus (HPV) circulating tumor DNA in HPV-positive oropharyngeal cancer (ReACT 1.0): an exploratory phase II trial

ABSTRACT
Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) has an expanding role in oncology. It is unknown whether on-treatment ctDNA can be used to personalize radiation dose. This phase 2 exploratory clinical trial enrolled 102 patients with human papillomavirus (HPV)-positive oropharyngeal carcinoma (OPC). Intermediate-risk patients (T4 disease or >10 pack-year smokers with pre-treatment HPV ctDNA scores >200) were reclassified as low-risk if HPV ctDNA cleared by >95% mid-treatment. All clinically low-risk and reclassified patients received de-intensified (chemo)radiation. The primary outcome was progression-free survival (PFS) among de-escalated patients; secondary outcomes included tolerability, distant metastatic-free, and overall survival. Eighty-nine patients received de-escalated treatment (60 low-risk, 29 reclassified). Two-year PFS among de-escalated patients was 93% (95%CI, 87-99). Post-hoc analyses suggested that intermediate-risk patients reclassified as low-risk and treated with de-escalation had distinct HPV ctDNA dynamics and favorable outcomes. HPV ctDNA is an emerging biomarker that might improve risk stratification for patients with intermediate-risk HPV-positive OPC. Registration number: NCT04900623.

Introduction

Circulating tumor (ct)DNA represents an accessible source for detecting and tracking tumor genetic biomarkers1 that is currently used to guide treatment decisions in solid tumors in the adjuvant setting following surgery2,3. However, to our knowledge, there are no prospective studies across oncology that use ctDNA profiling to personalize radiotherapy dose or chemoradiation intensity. Concurrent chemoradiation with cisplatin remains the standard non-surgical treatment option for oropharyngeal carcinoma (OPC) patients, yielding favorable outcomes despite toxicity and functional concerns4,5. Tumor-tissue modified viral (TTMV)-HPV DNA is a commercially available assay that detects five common high-risk HPV genotypes. Circulating TTMV-HPV DNA is frequently detectable in patients with HPV-associated OPC of the tonsil and base of tongue and is associated with nodal status at diagnosis6 and with rapid post-op clearance kinetics given its short half-life7. Chera and colleagues previously suggested that pre-treatment HPV DNA values greater than 200 copies/mL and clearing HPV DNA by 95% or more (from baseline) mid-treatment identifies a group of patients with favorable outcomes8,9. These results fueled strong interest in exploring the feasibility of TTMV-HPV DNA in selecting patients for de-intensified curative-intent therapy.

Numerous clinical trials have investigated de-intensification strategies in OPC aimed at preserving oncologic outcomes while mitigating treatment-related morbidity, including approaches that reduce radiation volume or dose, reduce or eliminate cisplatin, and incorporate upfront surgery or chemoselection4,5,10–14. These important trials have relied on clinical risk factors such as staging and smoking history, but few have explored biomarkers beyond p16 immunohistochemistry (IHC) to guide treatment decision-making. Recently, tumor hypoxia imaging has been explored to select patients for de-escalated chemoradiotherapy with encouraging preliminary results15.

We present an exploratory clinical trial using real-time TTMV-HPV DNA to select patients with HPV-positive OPC for risk-adapted, de-escalated non-surgical treatment. Herein we find that TTMV-HPV DNA profile has a limited role in predicting recurrence among clinically low-risk patients but may help to guide de-intensification in clinically intermediate-risk patients. These data suggest that TTMV-HPV DNA profiling has potential utility for refining clinical risk groups in future HPV-positive OPC therapeutic trials.

Results

Administrative summary

Between July 2021 and April 2025, 143 patients consented and screened to participate (Fig. 1A-B). Forty-one patients were ineligible for de-escalation at the time of screening, most commonly for having an undetectable or indeterminate pre-treatment TTMV-HPV DNA score (15, 10%), while 13 of 143 (9%) had T4 disease and/or were smokers but had pre-treatment TTMV-HPV DNA scores <200. These patients were treated with standard chemoradiation. One hundred and two patients enrolled and were potentially eligible for de-escalation (60 were low-risk and 29 were reclassified to low-risk; 13 were intermediate-risk). TTMV-HPV DNA dynamics

Median pre-treatment TTMV-HPV16 DNA score was 794 (interquartile range [IQR], [193–2686]) for the entire study population. Of the 60 patients initially assigned as low-risk to receive de-intensified therapy, 14 (23%) had a smoking history of 1–10 pack-years and 11 (18%) had N2 disease, with a median pre-treatment HPV DNA score of 326 (IQR, [114–1551]). Among the 29 patients initially assigned as intermediate-risk who were reclassified based on HPV DNA clearance to low-risk and de-escalated, 18 (62%) had a >10 pack-year smoking history and 14 (48%) had T4 disease, with a median pre-treatment HPV DNA score of 2281 (IQR, [896-7800]). Among the 13 intermediate-risk patients, 8 (62%) had a >10 pack-year smoking history, 5 (38%) had T4 disease, and their median pre-treatment HPV DNA score was 1461 (IQR, [524–15,560]). Efficacy and response

Among the de-escalated population of 89 that included both low-risk and initially intermediate-risk patients who were reclassified to low-risk based on >95% HPV DNA clearance the 2-year PFS was 93% (95% CI, 87–99) meeting our primary endpoint at the third IA (Fig. 4A) at a median follow-up of 24.7 months (range, 5.1–51+). Five PFS events were observed among the de-escalated cohort, three in initially low-risk patients with one experiencing distant relapse, one local recurrence (suspected second primary), and the other with synchronous local and distant recurrence (Supplementary Table 2). The other two patients with recurrence had clinically intermediate-risk disease and were reclassified to low-risk based on HPV DNA clearance, one experienced distant recurrence and the other synchronous local and distant relapse. All of these 5 patients cleared their HPV DNA mid-treatment by >95%, and 3 of the five events were identified first by newly detectable TTMV-HPV DNA during surveillance (lead time, range: 21–161 days). One salvage neck surgery occurred 7 months into follow-up and was negative for recurrence. When considering only the 60 initially low-risk patients, 2-year PFS was 93% (95% CI, 86–100) (Fig. 4B) and among the 29 intermediate risk patients reclassified to low-risk by HPV DNA clearance, 2-year PFS was 92% (95% CI, 83–100) (Fig. 4C). Post-hoc analysis demonstrated a 2-year PFS of 86% (95% CI, 72–100) when pooling those patients who received standard 70 Gy platinum-based chemoradiation with T4 and/or >10 pack-year smokers who either had detectable HPV DNA scores of <200 at baseline or those who failed to clear their HPV DNA score by >95% from baseline (n = 26). Comparatively, those who received de-escalated chemoradiation with T4 disease and/or >10 pack-year smoking histories who exhibited HPV DNA scores equal or above 200 at baseline and cleared by mid-treatment had a 2-year PFS of 92% (83–100) (n = 29). Two participants in the de-escalated cohort died during follow-up from disease progression. Two-year DMFS and OS was 95% (95% CI, 90–100) and 100% in the de-escalated group and 100% for both in the standard treatment group, respectively.

Safety and tolerability

Supplementary Table 3 summarizes common AEs experienced by the overall population. The most common treatment-related AEs (TRAEs) attributed to radiation across both the de-escalated and standard therapy groups were dysgeusia, oral mucositis, and xerostomia. Rates of grade 3–4 TRAEs attributed to radiation were as expected, most commonly dysphagia (15, 17%) and oral mucositis (12, 13%) in the de-escalated group. Anemia, thrombocytopenia, and nausea were the most reported TRAEs attributed to chemotherapy. Overall grade 3-4 TRAEs were limited, the most common being vomiting and neutropenia (each 4, 4%) in the de-escalated group and anemia (4, 31%) in the standard treatment group. Percutaneous endoscopic gastrotomy tube dependence 3-months post-treatment was overall low (8% in the de-escalated group).

Next-generation sequencing

Next-generation exome sequencing (using any available commercial assay) was performed on recurrent tumor tissue samples if sufficient tissue was available after pathologic review (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Discussion

ReACT 1.0 represents a clinical trial investigating the use of ctDNA, or more specifically TTMV-HPV DNA, to risk stratify locally advanced head and neck cancer patients and guide real-time deintensification of chemoradiotherapy. The study is one of few that has offered deintensification to higher-risk patients with HPV-driven disease who have T4 tumors or a greater than 10 pack-year smoking history. The trial met its primary endpoint, such that 2-year PFS was 93% with a lower CI bound exceeding 80% among all de-escalated patients, which included both the lower risk patients but also the intermediate risk patients who were reclassified to lower risk based on >95% HPV DNA clearance mid-treatment—the latter comprising 33% of the total de-escalated group. These data suggest that HPV DNA could be a biomarker to risk-stratify clinically intermediate-risk patients with HPV-driven oropharyngeal cancer, but further validation is needed.

As an initial trial in head and neck cancer to evaluate real-time, on-treatment HPV DNA dynamics, it was important to interrogate how scores changed and patterns of clearance over time among risk groups. Our data suggest differences in pre-treatment HPV DNA scores by clinical risk group and that scores rapidly increased in a shorter amount of time among clinically high-risk patients—which has been shown in other tumors16. It was also notable that among the 7 patients (7%) with a rising HPV DNA mid-treatment, none developed recurrence in follow-up. By the end of treatment, 2 patients (2%) had failed to clear their HPV DNA and only one recurred after remaining persistently DNA positive at 3-month follow-up and beyond. Similar to other cancers, HPV DNA clearance at 3-months post-treatment remains a landmark timepoint that is prognostic and correlates with recurrence-free survival17–19. In the future it will be important to understand any overlap with high-risk HPV DNA dynamics and additional biomarkers. Next-generation exome sequencing was later performed on 5 of 6 recurrent tumor samples across the study, with two patients exhibiting hotspot mutations in PIK3CA. Several somatic mutations (e.g., KRAS, TP53, and SMAD4) including alterations in the PI3K pathway have been linked with high-risk ctDNA changes and poor outcomes in other cancers20. We obtained paired simulation/planning and on-treatment MRI of the neck among a subset of patients on study, and will explore radiomic correlates of HPV DNA dynamics in a future analysis.

Many trials have aimed to de-intensify therapy for favorable risk patients with HPV-driven oropharyngeal cancer to minimize toxicity and preserve outcomes. Studies have modified chemotherapy dose and type, explored upfront transoral surgery to attenuate post-op therapy, evaluated lower radiation doses, and used upfront systemic therapy to select responders for less definitive treatment11–14. NRG Oncology RTOG 1016 randomized patients with HPV-driven oropharyngeal cancer to radiation (70 Gy in 35 fractions) with cisplatin vs. cetuximab, and included both smokers and patients with T3-4 tumors—representing 38% and 12% of the study population, respectively4. The 5-year OS estimate for smokers and those with T4 disease in the cisplatin-RT group of RTOG 1016 was 76–81%, while the 2-year PFS of 85% for the entire cisplatin-RT arm was numerically lower than that observed in our smaller de-escalated group who received lower dose RT. The De-ESCALaTE HPV trial enrolled a European population but used similar eligibility criteria to RTOG 1016. The standard arm patients in this trial received RT (70 Gy, 35 fractions) with higher dose cisplatin5. Only 17% of the trial population had T4 disease and 46% had previously smoked (median pack-years: 8). Among the T4 or N3 patients (n = 58), 2-year OS for the cisplatin-RT arm was 93% (2-year PFS was not reported) which was numerically comparable to our de-escalated group (2-year OS: 100%) that included similar patients. ECOG 1308 explored induction chemotherapy with cetuximab prior to response-adapted cetuximab-RT and included higher-risk smokers or those with T4 tumors12. Again, 2-year PFS among those with T4 or N2c disease or >10 pack-year smoking history was comparatively lower at 71% in the induction group that received ≤54 Gy RT. Several other smaller trials enrolled T4/smokers and showed comparable results to our study using various de-intensification platforms such as induction chemotherapy with response-adapted cisplatin-RT to 45 Gy, paclitaxel-RT to 54–60 Gy, and carboplatin-RT to 56 Gy21–25 or induction chemoimmunotherapy26. Taken together, the 2-year PFS in ReACT 1.0 compares favorably to the landscape of previously reported de-intensification trials that included similar patients while using a non-invasive ctDNA biomarker to help risk stratify patients. We acknowledge that further validation and longer-term follow-up is needed, and that cross trial comparisons must be interpreted with caution.

It is important to consider the 60 low-risk patients on trial who received lower dose RT with or without chemotherapy at any detectable HPV16 DNA score. Two-year PFS was 93% for this group which is on par with many of the trials above, however, the recently reported results of NRG HN005 warrant attention27. That study enrolled patients with T1-2N1, T3N0-1 (AJCC 8th edition) HPV-driven disease with a ≤10 pack-year smoking history and randomized them to 70 Gy RT with 2-cycles of bolus cisplatin vs. two other arms exploring lower dose RT with cisplatin or nivolumab. The 2-year PFS for the standard 70 Gy cisplatin-RT arm was 98% (95% CI, 95–100) with no locoregional failures reported. These exceptional results set a high bar for future de-escalation studies, and while the low-risk cohort in ReACT 1.0 was inferior without biomarker selection, 15% of our low-risk patients had N2 disease and our RT schedule did not use accelerated fractionation. The preliminary NRG HN005 results certainly give pause when considering future de-intensification regimens, and our data suggest that HPV DNA clearance may be limited as a predictor of recurrence among clinically low-risk patients.

Few trials before ReACT 1.0 have incorporated a real-time biomarker to guide treatment decisions for an HPV-driven oropharyngeal cancer population. The only published study to our knowledge is MSKCC 30 ROC, which de-escalated patients with hypoxia-negative tumors as determined by 18F-fluoromisonidazole (FMISO)-PET to receive 30 Gy RT with cisplatin15,28. Two-year PFS was 94% for the 30 Gy cohort but this study did not include patients with T4 tumors, irradiated patients after the primary tumor had been excised, and did not count early salvage neck dissections as failure events. Additionally, FMISO-PET imaging will likely require specialized training before integration into clinical practice29. An ongoing study at UNC/UF uses smoking and TP53 mutational status to de-intensify select patients (NCT03077243), while other completed and ongoing trials assess CT or PET response mid-treatment to guide de-escalation30 (NCT03215719, NCT03416153). But now that HPV DNA assays have become clinically available as a tool to assess disease status, they represent an important potential biomarker to explore across de-intensification trials. As evidenced by robust participation from our community network sites in this study, HPV DNA assays are both widely available and readily integrated into general oncology practice.

Roughly 10% of patients in our trial had undetectable or indeterminate TTMV-HPV DNA results pre-treatment which often correlates with lower disease burden8,9,31. It is unclear if HPV DNA undetectability with untreated HPV-driven disease portends a worse prognosis, as has been observed in cervical cancer where viral integration negatively impacts outcomes32. Building on work by Chera and colleagues we defined a favorable HPV DNA clearance profile in the present study as having a higher baseline score (>200) and >95% clearance by week 4 of treatment8— recognizing that such cutoffs can be arbitrary and need further validation. Other ongoing trials are exploring HPV DNA as a decision-making biomarker for de-escalation in HPV-driven oropharyngeal cancer. UF HN004 (NCT05268614) enrolls low-risk patients (non-T4, minimal or remote smokers) to receive lower dose RT (50–60 Gy) based on HPV subtype and score range, reserving the lowest dose of RT for those patients with HPV DNA scores of 100 or greater. ReACT 1.0 remains the only risk-adaptive de-intensification study to date that offers higher risk patients de-intensified therapy based on HPV DNA clearance metrics.

It is difficult to interpret the toxicity data reported in this trial since most patients received de-escalated therapy and reported outcomes may reflect our institutional practice patterns. However, rates of grade 3–4 treatment-related adverse events were as expected and <20% for all de-escalated patients which is comparable to the results of NRG HN00214. Three-month rates of percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) tube dependence were low at ≤10%.

It is important to acknowledge the limitations of the study, though it represents a prospective clinical trial. This study has a modest sample size and only a subset of patients were de-escalated based on HPV DNA clearance, it was conducted at a single institution and regional network sites in New England and was limited in terms of diversity given the epidemiology of the disease under study. Further, smoking status reporting is subjective, and a cutoff of 20 pack-years may be more predictive33. Notably, the median follow-up time was 2 years (and late recurrences can occur in this disease), and a limited number of events were observed given the overall favorable outcomes reported in this disease population. Longer-term toxicity data and patient-reported quality of life metrics are not yet available but will be useful to further clarify the value of a ctDNA-guided biomarker approach to therapeutic modification. Finally, ctDNA clearance metrics may certainly differ with other assays under development, and the results of this trial may not be generalizable to patients with low TTMV-HPV DNA scores (<200) and non-HPV16 genotypes. Next-generation sequencing assays may offer greater sensitivity for detection and disease clearance.

ReACT 1.0 explored risk-adapted de-intensified chemoradiation using HPV DNA metrics among a higher risk HPV-driven oropharyngeal cancer population receiving non-surgical treatment. We show that on-treatment, real-time HPV DNA testing to guide therapy is feasible and that previously reported HPV DNA cutoffs along with clinical risk factors (T4/smoking status) may be valuable together in deciding whether an intermediate risk patient is appropriate for stepwise de-intensified treatment. However, outside of a clinical trial, concurrent platinum-based chemoradiation to 70 Gy remains the standard of care. Future de-escalation trials for HPV-driven oropharyngeal cancer should continue to explore how to best leverage emerging biomarkers including HPV DNA and functional imaging to improve patient outcomes.

Methods

Study oversight

The study protocol (Supplementary file) was approved by the institutional review board at Dana-Farber Cancer Institute (DF/HCC# 21–191), and the trial is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04900623). The trial was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Conference on Harmonization Good Clinical Practice guidelines.

Participants

Patients were recruited from Dana-Farber Brigham Cancer Center and two community-based regional network sites. Patients age 22 and older with pathologically confirmed, newly diagnosed oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma were eligible if they had stage I, II, or III disease (2017 American Joint Committee on Cancer, 8th edition), HPV tumor genotype 16 (by p16 IHC and HPV RNA in situ hybridization or PCR testing), detectable pre-treatment TTMV-HPV16 DNA (NavDx®; Naveris, Waltham, MA) drawn within 28 days of starting therapy, European Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0–2 with adequate organ and marrow function, and were planned to receive non-surgical curative-intent treatment.

Patients with T0 unknown primary tumors and those who underwent excisional or surgical biopsy at diagnosis were eligible if there was gross residual disease on imaging and/or clinical exam. Patients with undetectable pre-treatment TTMV-HPV DNA scores, non-HPV16 genotypes (18, 31, 33, and 35), and those with N3 or clinically fixed nodal disease were excluded. All patients underwent pre-treatment neck imaging with contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and positron emission tomography (PET)-CT for initial staging and were evaluated by a multidisciplinary team.

Study design and treatment

This phase 2, non-randomized, open-label, single-center exploratory clinical trial assigned patients to one of two clinical risk cohorts prior to starting treatment. The low-risk arm included patients with clinical T0-3, N0-2 disease with a smoking history of 10 pack-years or less and any detectable TTMV-HPV DNA score pre-treatment. Patients were initially assigned to the intermediate-risk arm if they had T4 disease or a >10 pack-year smoking history and demonstrated a pre-treatment HPV DNA score >200. Those patients with intermediate-risk clinical features with pre-treatment scores of 200 or less were excluded. Intermediate-risk patients were re-classified as low-risk if their HPV DNA cleared by >95% of their individual pre-treatment score between weeks 4 and 5 on treatment.

All low-risk patients were assigned to de-intensified chemoradiation with intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) to 54–60 Gray (Gy) in 30 fractions targeting the primary site and/or gross nodal disease. Elective radiation treatment volumes received a prescription dose of 30–54 Gy (48 Gy in 30 fractions preferred). De-escalated radiation alone (54–66 Gy) was permitted for T0-2 N0-1 patients. In the de-escalated low-risk group, patients preferentially received up to two bolus or five weekly doses of cisplatin (not exceeding 200 mg/m2) but could receive five doses of weekly carboplatin AUC 1.5 with paclitaxel (45 mg/m2) if cisplatin-ineligible. Patients who were initially intermediate-risk and re-classified to low-risk were de-escalated as above utilizing the same radiation plan. Intermediate-risk patients who did not clear their HPV DNA by >95% during treatment received standard dose chemoradiation to 70 Gy in 35 fractions (elective doses 56–63 Gy) with up to 300 mg/m2 of cisplatin or 7 weeks of weekly carboplatin with paclitaxel.

Assessments

Adverse events (AEs) to assess treatment safety were recorded using Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0. Patients underwent imaging assessments (contrast-enhanced CT or MRI of the neck with PET-CT) at baseline and 10-12 weeks following the completion of therapy. Imaging beyond first restaging was at the discretion of the treating investigators, but patients were followed in clinic with flexible endoscopic exams per national guidelines every 3–4 months during years 1–2 and every 4–6 months during years 3–5. TTMV-HPV DNA was collected at each surveillance visit and any newly detectable score triggered diagnostic imaging and/or biopsy, as clinically indicated. At first disease progression, patients were followed for documentation of survival every 3 months up to 5-years.

Endpoints and statistical design

The primary endpoint was 2-year progression-free survival (PFS) among all patients receiving de-intensified treatment, including both low-risk and intermediate-risk patients who were reclassified to low-risk based on HPV DNA clearance. Progression was defined as the first of disease recurrence (locoregional or distant), death, or was censored at last follow-up. Survival was calculated from the date of final treatment. Secondary endpoints included tolerability (3- and 6-month feeding tube dependence rates), best overall response rate (ORR by RECIST 1.134), overall survival (OS), distant metastatic-free survival (DMFS), and patient-reported quality of life outcomes (PROs). PROs were collected using the FACT H&N questionnaire. Exploratory biomarker data were collected by offering MRI-guided radiation simulation paired with repeat neck MRI between weeks 4 and 5 on treatment to correlate radiomic parameters and HPV DNA scores. PRO and exploratory biomarker analyses will be reported separately.

Our primary objective was to rule out a 2-year PFS ≤ 80% among the entire de-escalated treatment population as compared to historical outcomes (3-year OS: 70.8% in RTOG 012935 and 5-year OS: 76.4% in RTOG 10164 for intermediate risk patients) given that we included clinically low-risk patients and those who had T4 or N2 disease and a >10 pack-year smoking history. Based on our center patient demographics and our prior data14, we estimated that 70% of accrued patients would be eligible for de-escalation. We employed a group sequential testing design targeting a power of 0.85 and significance level of 0.05. After accruing 40 low-risk patients each having >6 months of follow-up, interim analysis (IA) was planned. IAs to assess for non-inferiority (NI) then occurred every 4 months thereafter with Bootstrap calibration and alpha spending36. If the 95% confidence interval (CI) lower bound exceeded the pre-defined threshold of 0.8 at any IA, then NI was declared. Under these assumptions we set a maximum of 100 low-risk and 145 total patients enrolled to the trial, with 2-year PFS reported with a 95% CI.

Response rate was summarized as a proportion with a corresponding 95% CI. The distribution of OS and DMFS was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. Two-sided p-values are reported. Data as of October 20, 2025, were analyzed using R (v4.4.0) and GraphPad Prism v10 (Boston, MA).

Post hoc analyses

We analyzed the association between the time interval elapsed from pre-baseline (first) and pre-treatment measurements (“days in between”) and the log-transformed change in HPV DNA score stratified by risk arms. We fitted a series of linear regression models to examine factors associated with the log difference. First, we explored a simple model that assessed days as the sole predictor. Second, we developed a multivariable model including relevant clinical covariates (risk arm, HPV DNA score, smoking history, T stage, N stage, and overall stage). Lastly, to determine potential effect modification, we compared additive and interaction models via ANOVA, testing whether adding days × risk arm interaction improved model fit, meaning that the effects of time differed across different risk arms.
