A case of rapidly progressive cervical primary spinal epidural lymphoma

INTRODUCTION

Primary spinal epidural lymphomas (PSELs) are rare, accounting for only 0.1–6.5% of all extranodal lymphomas[5] and 9% of all primary spinal epidural tumors.[1] The thoracic spine is the most common site of involvement,[7] with only rare reports of cervical lesions. Here, a 74-year-old male presented with the acute onset of quadriparesis attributed to a magnetic resonance (MR)-documented cervical epidural diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL). Following appropriate C2-C4 surgical resection, the patient was effectively treated with additional chemotherapy and radiation.

CASE STUDY

History

A 74-year-old male was first diagnosed with ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL). However, he next acutely developed quadriparesis. The manual muscle test (MMT) at the initial consultation, left upper extremity was 1–2/5 and left lower was 4/5, and right upper and lower was 4/5. He also developed sensory disturbance below the neck, left-dominant. His Japanese Orthopaedic Association score for cervical myelopathy was five out of 17.

CT and MR findings

Radiographs and computed tomography (CT) showed OPLL from C2 to C4 with associated spinal canal stenosis. The MR, however, revealed a mass posterior to the spinal cord from C3 to C5 that enhanced with contrast. On axial T2-weighted images, the most severe stenosis was at the C4/5 level; the tumor further extended through the intervertebral foramen to the posterior epidural space at the C4/5 level [Figure 1]. A preoperative whole-body CT scan did not document an obvious primary lesion.

Surgery

Six days after presentation, he underwent a partial laminectomy of C2 and complete laminectomies of C3–4. At surgery, the tumor invaded the posterior soft tissues and bone. Upon removal of the ligamentum flavum, a whitish mass was seen directly over the dura, but the tumor was not adherent to the dura and showed no signs of infiltrating the dura [Figures 2 and 3]. A macroscopic gross total resection was achieved. The MR imaging obtained 7 days after the operation documented gross total tumor excision and adequate cord decompression [Figure 4].

Pathology documented DLBCL

Histopathological examination with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining showed proliferation of large atypical lymphocytes. Immunohistochemistry was positive for CD20, confirming the diagnosis of DLBCL [Figure 5]. For staging, a fluorodeoxyglucose-positron emission tomography (FDGPET) scan revealed abnormal uptake in the pituitary gland, colon, and prostate; these findings were later correlated with a pituitary adenoma, a colonic polyp, and primary prostate cancer. The tumor was diagnosed as primary B-cell cervical epidural lymphoma for which the patient subsequently received three cycles of polatuzumab vedotin plus rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, and prednisolone (Pola-RCHP) therapy combined with local radiotherapy (40 Gy in 20 fractions).

Outcome

Three months postoperatively, the patient’s quadriparesis gradually improved and MMT was 5/5 in all upper and lower extremities. Improvements in his sensory levels were observed in all four limbs, including the left upper limb, which had been the most severely affected. He could walk independently and sufficiently perform multiple activities of daily living.

DISCUSSION

PSEL is a rare disease, especially in the cervical spine. One study reported that the incidence was highest in the thoracic spine (69%), followed by the lumbar (27%) and cervical (4%) spine. Furthermore, extranodal infiltration of lymphoma into the epidural space is believed to occur through the intervertebral foramina, invading paraspinal soft tissues such as paraspinal nerve ganglia and epidural lymphatic tissues, without destroying the bony structures. Most cases of spinal epidural lymphoma are discovered at Stage I or II; only 5.7% of patients present with B-symptoms such as weight loss, night sweats, and fever.[5] Once neurological compression occurs, establishing the diagnosis and determining whether surgical decompression and/or chemotherapy/radiation are needed is critical may be warranted.

Efficacy of chemotherapy/radiation therapy for DLBCL

Bhardwaj et al. reported on treatment strategies for primary spinal DLBCL of the thoracic spine, noting that a combination of surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy yielded favorable outcomes.[2] A cohort study by Xiong et al. also demonstrated the efficacy of this multimodal approach for spinal DLBCL.[7]

Role of decompressive surgery

The role of decompressive surgery is debated, with some reports opposing it for spinal lymphoma due to high postoperative mortality rates.[6] Bhardwaj et al., based on a review of previous case reports, demonstrated the effectiveness of decompressive surgery for thoracic spinal lymphoma.[2] Similarly, Chang et al. reported that surgical decompression, including tumor biopsy, improved neurological outcomes in patients with lymphoma-associated spinal cord compression.[3] Thus, decompressive surgery has emerged as a primary recommendation for treating these lesions. In addition, authors like Cugati et al. suggest adopting additional treatment protocols that include chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy.[3] In our case, a combination of surgical intervention, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy for cervical epidural lymphoma resulted in a favorable outcome, including neurological recovery [Table 1].
