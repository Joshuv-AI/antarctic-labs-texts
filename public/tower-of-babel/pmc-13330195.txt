Proteolysis-targeting chimera (PROTAC) in cancer: design principles and applications on “undruggable” targets
Authors: Xun Lu, Jianliang Qin, Shuilin Dong, Yue Yuan, Zhanguo Zhang, Bixiang Zhang, Xiaoping Chen, Xiaoli Gan, Huifang Liang, Furong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTargeted cancer therapies can enhance in vivo efficacy and decrease side effects by changing the distribution and exposure of specific biomolecules in tissues. Nevertheless, there are many cancer target proteins that cannot be targeted by traditional drugs. Some new technologies and drug design strategies have been applied to overcome these “undruggable” targets, among which the most well-known and classic technology is proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs). In order to design a better PROTAC structure for targeting “undruggable” targets, we elaborated in detail on the design of protein of interest (POI) ligands, E3 ligase ligands and linkers in PROTAC structures, the predicting of PROTAC ternary complex structures, and the advantages and disadvantages of using carriers in PROTAC delivery systems. In addition, this article also reviews the current research status of PROTAC targeting “undruggable” targets, such as kirsten rat sarcoma (KRAS), epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), c-Myc and p53. The challenges faced by PROTACs and the possible solutions were discussed, and the possibility of PROTAC broadening the range of drug therapeutic targets, especially the “undruggable” target, was prospected.Keywords: Proteolysis-targeting chimera (PROTAC), Applications on “undruggable” targets, Design principles

Introduction

Identifying the defects of oncogenic or non-oncogenic drugs is one of the main goals of cancer research [1, 2]. To date, traditional research on small-molecule drugs targeting intracellular targets mainly focused on the development of high-affinity inhibitors, which either target active sites of enzymes or allosteric sites of enzymes to disable the function of protein of interest (POI). Although this has been a highly efficient method, it cannot act on those potential undruggable targets [3–5]. Proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) bring degradation functions to the target, eliminate the need for active sites, and redefine “undruggable” targets as “undrugged” [6–8]. Professor Cruise of Yale University and his colleagues first proposed the concept of PROTAC in 2001 [9]. After years of development, PROTAC technology has now become an advanced method for new drug development, at present, many PROTAC molecules have advanced to the clinical development stage [10–12]. PROTACs have significant advantages, such as reducing systemic drug exposure, targeting specific degradation, and targeting targets that were traditionally classified as “undruggable” [13–15]. Unlike the “occupancy-driven” mode of conventional small-molecule inhibitors, PROTACs adopt an “event-driven” mechanism. By facilitating the complete degradation of the target protein, PROTACs thereby eliminate both its non-enzymatic and enzymatic functions. Meanwhile, the protein degradation induced by PROTAC is a catalytic process, because PROTAC dissociates after facilitating the polyubiquitination of POI, which enables PROTAC to function at a very low dose. In contrast to the mechanism of conventional small molecule inhibitors that compete with active sites and occupy pharmacological action models, PROTACs induce the degradation of target proteins through an iterative mode of action. Therefore, it is not easily affected by the increased expression and mutation of the target protein. Compared with traditional small molecule inhibitors, PROTACs have significant advantages [16].

Many proteins play a significant role in the occurrence and development of cancer and are regarded as important therapeutic targets [17, 18]. However, these proteins usually lack targetable pockets (allosteric sites or orthosteric), and therefore they are regarded as “undruggable” by conventional small-molecule inhibitors [19, 20]. PROTACs can utilize low-affinity small-molecule ligands or oligonucleotides as protein decoys to overcoming the reliance on well-defined targetable sites, thereby offering an opportunity to degrade “undruggable” proteins [21–24]. At present, the main targets of PROTAC targeting undruggable reported in the literature are kirsten rat sarcoma (KRAS), epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), c-Myc and p53 [15, 25–27]. In this article, we reviewed the design methodology of PROTAC, mainly POIs, E3 ligases and linkers in PROTAC ligand structure. At the same time, we also reviewed the application of PROTACs on some “undruggable” targets and the current research progress. In addition, the challenges faced by PROTACs and the possible solutions were discussed, and the possibility of PROTAC broadening the range of drug therapeutic targets, especially the “undruggable” target, was prospected.

Basic principles of PROTAC design

Structure of PROTACs

PROTAC mainly targets the degradation of proteins via the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS). The “event-driven” mechanism of PROTACs enables it to target proteins that were traditionally classified as “undruggable” [28]. In recent years, artificial intelligence-aided drug design has promoted the development of PROTAC drugs. An online database named PROTAC-DB can display relevant structural information in real time. The PROTAC-DB database included 9380 PROTACs, 1493 ligands of POIs, 2771 linkers and 126 ligands of E3 ligases so far [29]. PROTACs mainly consist of three parts: an E3 ubiquitin ligase ligand, a POI ligand, and a linker that connects these two parts. The ligand is typically small molecule or peptide that have high affinity for their respective targets. The linker’s role is to tightly bind the ligand of E3 ligase and POI, facilitating the formation of the POI-PROTAC-E3 ternary complex, thereby triggering the ubiquitination of POI, which is then degraded by the UPS [8, 9, 30, 31]. The process of ubiquitination is mediated by a cascade of three enzymes: E1 activating enzyme, E2 conjugating enzyme, and E3 ligase [24, 32–34]. PROTAC precisely utilizes this UPS mechanism to recruit E3 ligase to the target protein, catalyzing the ubiquitination of POI in an “event-driven” manner, and ultimately promoting its degradation by the proteasome (Fig. 1) [35–38]. Ideally, PROTACs can target mutant and/or wild-type proteins, and can be extended from druggable to ligandable proteins, as the ligands of POI can be selected from its regulators and inhibitors, while the activity of POI is not inhibited [39].

Ligands of POIs in PROTACs

PROTACs have successfully degraded a variety of POIs, including kinases, nuclear receptors, epigenetic proteins, transcription factors and others [40–43] (Table 1). Many kinase degraders such as PI3K [44], FAK [45], MET [46], and EGFR [47] have been reported. Donovan KA et al. identified 172 degradable kinases and developed a map of the kinase that can be degraded, providing resources for accelerating the development of degradants [48]. The development of PROTACs targeting nuclear receptors, such as ER and AR, has also progressed significantly. Notably, two oral-active PROTACs, ARV-110 and ARV-471, whose are undergoing clinical trials [49]. Zhang et al. have summarized PROTACs targeting epigenetic proteins, such as BET, EZH2, HDAC [40]. PROTACs targeting transcription factors mainly include STAT3 and c-Myc [50, 51]. One of the biggest advantages of PROTAC is its capacity to degrade traditionally “undruggable” targets lacking suitable drug-binding sites, of which the most studied are KRAS, EGFR, c-Myc, and p53, which will be discussed in detail below.

Ligands of E3 ligases in PROTACs

At present, the human genome contains over 600 types of E3 ligases, a subset of can be utilized for PROTACs [62–65]. The most used of these are CRBN, VHL, MDM2 and cIAP (Table 2). Obviously, E3 ligases, as a key component of PROTAC, greatly limit the development of PROTAC technology [66–68]. Therefore, new E3 ligases need to be designed to alleviate concerns about the already explored E3 ligases and expand the therapeutic potential of PROTAC. Liu et al. developed a user-friendly and flexible website, which aimed to help the researchers quickly identify E3 ligases with targeted protein degradation (TPD) activity for specific targets. It will significantly improve the ability of PROTAC researchers to quickly discover new E3 ligases that target TPD activity for specific targets [69]. To address the limitations of endogenous E3 ligase dependence, Teng et al. developed tumor-selective PROTAC drugs [50]. They developed the ClickRNA-PROTAC system, which expressed the fusion protein of E3 ubiquitin ligase SIAH1 and SNAPTag by transfection mRNA and used bio-orthogonal click chemistry to recruit the POI. ClickRNA-PROTAC could degrade multiple proteins, such as NFκB, BRD4 and KRAS by replacing different warheads. By using tumor-specific mRNA-responsive translation strategies, ClickRNA-PROTAC achieved selective degradation of POI in tumor cells.

E3 ligase has a great influence on the selectivity and activity of PROTAC. The commonly used E3 ligases in PROTAC are CRBN and VHL, which are only highly expressed in a minority of tumors, thereby limiting the efficacy and application scope of PROTAC drugs [69–71]. In addition, PROTAC drugs lacking tumor-specific E3 ligase may cause toxic and side effects [10], so the following aspects should be considered in the PROTAC design [51, 69, 72]: (1) subcellular localization, expression levels and physiological roles of E3 ligase. (2) chemical ligandability characterizing the availability of binders for the E3 ligase. (3) the expression pattern of E3 ligase at the overall and single-cell levels in tumor and normal samples. (4) the protein-protein interaction (PPI) of E3 ligase that further characterize its potential protein targets. (5) other key factors that must be taken into account in PROTAC design include the functional essentiality, structure availability and PPI interface of E3 ligases.

Linkers in PROTACs

The composition, flexibility and length of the linkers in PROTACs have a significant impact on the physicochemical characteristics and biological activities of the molecules such as metabolic stability, cell permeability and solubility [79–87]. Currently, the commonly used linkers mainly include Alkyl chains, PEG, Triazole and Cycloalkane [88, 89] (Table 3). In the past, when designing PROTACs linker, the following four aspects were mainly considered: (1) Adjust the length of the linker to achieve the best configuration for a specific PROTAC [90]. (2) Balance the hydrophilicity and hydrophobicity of the PROTAC by changing the type of linker [16]. (3) Increase the stability of PROTAC by adjusting the flexibility of the linker and its site [45, 91]. (4) Design and synthesize different linkers to enrich the types of PROTAC. Linker is the “intelligent bridge” of PROTAC. Its design needs to strike a fine balance among length, rigidity and hydrophilicity to maximize the formation efficiency of the ternary complex. At present, in PROTAC development, linker optimization still highly relies on empirical screening, but computational simulations (such as molecular dynamics) are gradually becoming important tools for assisting design. In recent years, methods for designing PROTAC linkers using artificial intelligence (AI) technology have emerged, all of which are based on the use of existing knowledge [92–95]. Its main processes include data collection, feature extraction, selection of appropriate machine learning models, model training, validation, and deployment [94]. Firstly, collect a dataset of relevant information on known PROTAC linkers, including their efficacy, structure, stability and cell permeability. Then use this dataset to train and validate the machine learning model. Next, extract the relevant physicochemical features from the linker structures, which may include the kinds of bonds and atoms, electrical distribution and molecular weight, etc. Subsequently, select the appropriate machine learning model, such as Transformer Neural Network [94], Deep Neural Network [96] or recurrent Neural Network [92], and conduct training using the known experimental data and extracted features. By optimizing the underlying algorithm and adjusting the model parameters, the prediction accuracy and general ability are improved. The predictive performance of the training model was assessed using an independent test dataset. Finally, the trained model was used to predict the new type of PROTAC linker (Fig. 2). The shift from previous experience to AI technology is expected to enhance the design efficiency of PROTAC linkers. Murakami et al. first constructed a prediction model for cell membrane permeability based on PROTAC-DB 3.0, which demonstrated high predictive performance. By integrating this prediction model into the generative model, a PROTACs linker with higher predictive cell membrane permeability was successfully designed, taking into account the similarity of PROTAC [97]. Furthermore, Alessandra Salerno et al. recently discovered that embedding ferrocene into linker as a “molecular hinge” can promote tight conformation, enhance cell permeability, and reduce exudation responsibility [98]. These methods can provide references for the subsequent design of linker.

Prediction of the structure of PROTAC ternary complexes

Optimization of a PROTAC is mostly guided by medicinal chemistry intuition to improve cell permeability and degradation [81, 109, 110]. Structure based optimization can be effective in improving the ternary complex stability and half-life, which in turn results in greater degradation potency, but solving the crystal structures of ternary complexes is challenging [85, 101, 111–114]. In the absence of crystal structure, the computational method for predicting ternary complexes could offer a model for structure-based optimization. PROTAC delivery systems

The effective transformation of PROTACs from the laboratory to clinical application not only depends on ligand optimization but also on the delivery system. PROTACs possess unique physicochemical features: high molecular weight, considerable polar surface area and inherent hydrophobicity, which often lead to poor solubility and limited permeability of the cell membrane. These characteristics collectively hinder their bioavailability and effective access to intracellular targets. Therefore, in order to improve these problems, developing an effective drug delivery system is of vital importance to PROTAC research. At present, the carriers of the reported PROTAC delivery systems mainly include prodrugs, nanoparticles, protein-based delivery systems and nucleic acid-based delivery systems (Fig. 3). PROTACs targeting the “undruggable” protein

“Undruggable” proteins generally refer to those that cannot be targeted by traditional small-molecule inhibitors because of the following factors: (1) absence of ligand-binding pockets, (2) highly specific conserved active sites within protein families, (3) function based on PPIs, (4) the variability of tertiary docking structure hinders the binding of traditional small molecule inhibitors [133]. Consequently, it has become difficult to design and synthesize drugs that target these proteins using traditional methods [19], but they have great clinical significance. PROTACs targeting KRAS

KRAS is a critical member of the RAS family [162]. Meanwhile, it is also one of the most common oncogenic mutant genes in tumors and is implicated in approximately 85% of RAS-driven cancers [1]. KRAS is a major oncogene involved in cell proliferation and survival [2, 163]. KRAS mutations are very common in cancer [26, 164, 165]. The KRAS mutation subtypes mainly include G12D, G12V, G12C, G13D, G12R, G12A or KRAS wild-type amplification [166]. KRAS mutations are distributed differently in various human tumors. Among them, the two most frequently mutation types in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) and colon cancer (CRC) are KRASG12D and KRASG12V, as shown in Fig. 4. For nearly 40 years, due to the smooth surface of the KRAS protein, the lack of a suitable drug binding bag, and the extremely high binding affinity of KRAS with a large amount of cytoplasmic GTP, it has long been regarded as an undruggable target [3, 167–169]. However, with the development of organic chemistry, bioengineering and related fields, the possibility of directly targeting KRAS has been provided.

Sotorasib and adagrasib are two KRAS inhibitors that have been approved by the FDA [171, 172]. However, up to now, clinically approved inhibitors have shown heterogenous patient responses and drug resistance [173–175]. Through the analysis of relapsed patients, it was found that drug resistance included KRAS mutations that reduced drug binding or maintained activity (On) status, genomic KRAS amplification, as well as mutations and/or amplification of RAS signals in upstream and downstream components of the RAS pathway [176]. To overcome these problems, PROTAC is expected to become the next-generation strategy for addressing resistance to KRAS inhibitors. The design of PROTAC for degrading KRAS mainly uses a linker, one end is connected to a KRAS inhibitor, the other end is connected to an E3 ubiquitination ligase composed mainly of VHL or CRBN [177, 178]. The linker consists mainly of 5 to 15 carbon atoms or other atoms [89]. The linker components of PROTACs can affect the permeability of their cell membranes, thereby influencing the efficiency of KRAS degradation [141]. Zhou et al. found that the linker part of PROTAC is vital for regulating degradation activity, target selectivity and physicochemical properties [140]. After systematically modifying the composition and length of the linker, they observed that even a minor modification to just one atom in the linker part of ZZ151 would lead to a significant change in the formation of the ternary complex, thus greatly influencing the degradation activity. At present, in the reported studies, the types of PROTAC targeting KRAS can be roughly divided into the following types: KRASG12C degrader, KRASG12D degrader and Pan-KRAS degrader.

At present, the main research on PROTAC targeting KRASG12C includes: Yang et al. developed YF135, the first reversible-covalent PROTAC, which was designed based on the scaffold of MRTX849 linkage VHL ligand [61]. YF135 reversibly induced the quick and continuous degradation of endogenous KRASG12C via recruiting the proteasome degradation mechanism mediated by VHL. Li’s group reported the development of KP-14, which is based on KRASG12C−IN−3 (a KRASG12C inhibitor) and pomalidomide as KRASG12C warhead degraders. By binding to KRASG12C and recruiting the E3 ligase CRBN, it achieves quick and continuous degradation of KRASG12C [138]. Michael J et al. reported LC-2, the first endogenous KRASG12C degrader, which was designed by binding MRTX849 to the ligand of VHL E3 ligase [60]. LC-2 covalently combined with KRASG12C through MRTX849, recruiting E3 ligase VHL to rapidly and continuously degrade KRASG12C, thereby inhibiting MAPK signaling in both heterozygous and homozygous KRASG12C cell lines. Yang et al. discovered that YN14 as an efficient degrader against KRASG12C which was induced cell migration, cell apoptosis, G2 cell cycle arrest and tumor growth in pancreatic cancer [139]. This study was the first report confirming the anti-tumor activity of KRASG12C PROTAC in vivo. In MIA PaCa-2 cells, YN14 showed anti-cancer effect with DC50 and IC50 values decreased to nanomolar level, accompanied by Dmax > 95%. In the MIA PaCa-2 xenograft model with a well-tolerated dose schedule, YN14 causes tumor regression, and the tumor growth inhibition rate exceeds 100%. In addition, Sajid Khan et al. found that DT2216 (a clinical-stage BCL-XL PROTAC) can enhance the therapeutic effect of sotorasib on CRC patients with KRASG12C mutated. The combination therapy of DT2216 can significantly improve the anti-tumor efficacy of sotorasib in vivo and in vitro and is expected to further conduct clinical trials [142].

The main research on PROTAC targeting KRASG12D includes: Zhou et al. found that PROTAC linked to MRTX1133 analogues and VHL ligands could target the degradation of KRASG12D. Representative compound 8o indicates that it induced the degradation of KRASG12D in a VHL- and proteasome-dependent manner, thereby inhibiting the growth of various KRASG12D mutant cancer cells [141]. Jeon et al. reported that CH091138(6) selectively degrades endogenous and exogenous KRASG12D, which leads to KRASG12D selectivity by mediating the interaction between KRASG12D and VHL [136]. It inhibited the proliferation of AsPC-1 cells and the growth of colon cancer patient-derived organoids carrying KRASG12D. Ji et al. investigated a highly efficient and selective KRASG12D degrader, RP03707, which could effectively inhibit the growth of tumor cells in multiple KRASG12D cell lines and also demonstrated durable PK/PD effects and excellent therapeutic effects in the KRASG12D tumor mouse CDX model [137].

The main research on PROTAC targeting Pan-KRAS includes: Popow et al. reported the development of bifunctional PROTAC capable of targeting the remaining oncogenic KRAS mutations and found that ACBI3 could selectively degrade most oncogenic KRAS mutations [135]. Starting from the non-covalent ligand of KRAS and the E3 ligase VHL, the chemical optimization guided by co-crystal structures of VHL: PROTAC: KRAS ternary complexes led to the selective degradation of 13 of the 17 most common mutants by a small molecule, thereby inhibiting the proliferation of KRAS mutant cell lines, which are associated with the occurrence of many tumors. Zhou et al. found that ZZ151 displayed potent anti-proliferation activity against extensive cancer cells driven by KRAS mutation [140].

Due to the high incidence of KRAS tumor mutations, there are many targets that currently have no drugs that can effectively inhibit them. The above-mentioned research provides a better approach to solve this problem, and the experimental results show that in experimental models, KRAS degradation is more effective than inhibition in inducing tumor regression. In addition, some drugs have already entered clinical trials. The specific details are as follows: ARV-806 is a selective PROTAC KRASG12D degrader. Preclinical data of pancreatic cancer and colorectal cancer indicate that the inhibitory effect of ARV-806 on cell proliferation is 25 times higher than that of KRASG12D inhibitors in the ON and OFF forms and other KRASG12D degraders at the clinical stage. After a single intravenous dose of ARV-806, KRAS was observed to degrade by 90% within 7 days in vivo. Accordingly, low doses of ARV-806 every week or every two weeks produced a robust efficacy response of a 30% reduction in tumor volume in pancreatic and colorectal cell line-derived xenograft models and a patient-derived xenograft model of lung cancer [179]. Based on these favorable results, ARV-806 is currently undergoing a phase 1 clinical trial in patients with KRASG12D-mutated advanced solid tumors, who have previously received standard treatment for their disease and have KRASG12D mutation detected in tumor tissues or through circulating tumor DNA. Patients eligible for phase 2 have advanced PDAC, ≥ 1 prior line of systemic therapy, and KRASG12D mutation confirmed by molecular or next-generation sequencing of tumor tissue. In both phases, patients must have eastern cooperative oncology group performance status of 0 or 1, adequate organ function, and no prior treatment with a KRASG12D or a KRASG12C targeting therapy, including pan-KRAS inhibitors or degraders. In phase 1, patients will receive escalating intravenous doses of ARV-806 weekly or every 2 weeks in 28-day cycles to evaluate its safety, pharmacokinetics, tolerability and preliminary antitumor activity. The primary endpoint of phase 1 is dose-limiting toxicity during the first cycle and adverse events to determine the maximum tolerated dose (MTD) or maximum administered dose if MTD not reached and to select recommended phase 2 dose(s) (RP2Ds) for cohort expansion. In phase 2, patients will receive ARV-806 at one or more doses selected from phase 1 to evaluate its anti-tumor activity and support further RP2D optimization [180]. Clinical efficacy data have not yet been disclosed. Clinical trial information: NCT07023731.

ASP3082 is a selective PROTAC KRASG12D degrader that eliminates mutant proteins through VHL-mediated E3 ubiquitin ligase [181, 182]. ASP3082 is currently undergoing a phase 1 clinical trial in patients with metastatic or locally advanced unresectable solid tumors with KRASG12D mutation. It was granted fast track designation by the FDA in February 2023 for the treatment of pancreatic adenocarcinoma [183]. This phase 1 clinical study aims to evaluate the pharmacodynamics, pharmacokinetics, safety and anti-tumor activity of ASP3082 in patients with previously treated advanced solid tumors containing the KRAS p.G12D variant. The primary objective is to evaluate dose-limiting toxic effects and adverse events (the primary endpoint), thereby determining the phase 2 dose. ASP3082 was administered intravenously once a week, with a dose ranging from 10 to 800 mg. A total of 203 patients were enrolled, among whom 76 patients received a dose of 600 mg of ASP3082 and were ultimately selected for the phase 2 dose All patients experienced adverse events during the treatment period, and 42% of the patients experienced grade 3 or above adverse events. 93% of the patients experienced treatment-related adverse events, among which the most common were transient infusion-related reactions (80%) and nausea (30%). Two patients discontinued treatment due to adverse events. Among the 45 NSCLC patients who received a dose of 600 mg, 36% achieved a partial response (95% confidence interval [CI], 22–51). Median progression-free survival (mPFS) was 8.3 months (95% CI, 4.1- not estimable), and the 12-month overall survival (OS) rate was estimated at 59% (95% CI, 40–74). Among the 21 patients with metastatic PDAC, all received 600 mg of ASP3082 as a second-line or third-line treatment (67% of the patients used it as the third-line treatment). The median OS was 10.3 months (95% CI, 4.2–13.0), mPFS was 3.0 months (95% CI, 1.4–6.9) and 24% had a response (95% CI, 8–47). ASP3082 has demonstrated anti-tumor activity in patients with advanced KRAS p.G12D-mutated NSCLC or PDAC who had previously received treatment, and the situation of discontinuation of treatment due to adverse reactions was relatively rare [184].

ASP4396, a novel protein degrader, targets KRASG12D-mutated protein for degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome system. ASP4396 is under clinical development by Astellas Pharma and currently in a phase 1 trial in patients with locally advanced (unresectable) or metastatic solid tumors harboring KRASG12D mutation, with a phase transition success rate (PTSR) of 70% for progression to phase 2 [185]. Patients who have previously received treatment for KRASG12D will be excluded. This Phase 1 of ASP4396 is a multicenter, open-label, dose-expansion and dose-escalation study. Clinical efficacy data have not yet been disclosed. However, since the targets of ASP4396 and ASP3082 are the same (KRASG12D), and the mechanisms are similar (both are PROTAC degraders), only the E3 ligase is different. Moreover, ASP3082 has achieved breakthrough efficacy in pancreatic cancer. As a result, Astellas has terminated the development of ASP4396 and is concentrating more resources on ASP3082.

PROTACs targeting EGFR

EGFR actively participates in the regulation of various mechanisms related to cancer progression, including angiogenesis, migration and differentiation [186–188]. Unfortunately, the continuous emergence of drug resistance mechanisms and new mutations has become a bottleneck for EGFR-based treatments, thereby limiting the efficacy of drugs and making EGFR “undruggable” [186, 189]. Currently, the commonly used EGFR inhibitors only block kinase activity, but the EGFR protein itself still exists in the cells and may continue to promote tumor progression through non-kinase-dependent mechanisms [190, 191]. However, PROTACs can degrade disease-related proteins from cells rather than merely inhibit them, thereby eliminating any potential scaffolding function [192, 193]. They act in an iterative manner, inducing the degradation of the target in sub-stoichiometric quantities, which can reduce the dosage compared with their own traditional inhibitor, thereby influencing side effects [13, 193]. The development of PROTACs targeting EGFR mutants can provide the possibility to overcome these situations [39, 145, 194]. PROTACs are expected to inhibit EGFR and avoid EGFR inhibitor resistance that occurs in tumors.

At present, the research status of using PROTAC technology to target EGFR and solve its drug resistance in tumors is as follows: A recent study by Zheng et al. showed that DP-C-1, a CRBD ligand-based PROTAC, degraded EGFR in a time- and dose-dependent manner in SW1990 cells [152]. DP-C-1 can simultaneously degrade PARP and EGFR in cancer cells by utilizing a three-functional natural amino acid as a star-shaped core linker, linking two independent inhibitors (PARP inhibitor and EGFR inhibitor) and the E3 ligand. Li et al. reported a PROTAC degrader targeting EGFR, named P-G. This degrader can induce the degradation of EGFR in NSCLC cells with EGFR activation mutations and in cells that have developed resistance to tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), thereby preventing the development of drug resistance [151]. Zhang et al. designed and synthesized PROTACs 10 and PROTACs 2, finding that they could induce EGFR degradation in HCC827 cells, with DC50 values of 34.8 and 45.2 nM, respectively [153]. He et al. designed and synthesized 16c and found that it had a good inhibitory effect on both PC9 cells and H1975 cells. The results of Western blotting indicated that PROTAC 16c could target and degrade EGFRdel19 in PC9 cells [154]. In addition, the most frequent targeted resistance mechanism is the EGFR mutation. To overcome this resistance, HJM-561, C6, 6 h, CP17, P3 and HJM-17 were designed and synthesized. They are PROTACs targeting EGFR, selectively degrading EGFR mutants and thereby overcoming drug resistance in tumors [143, 145, 147, 155, 156, 159]. PROTACs 13b, 9ea, 14o and 1q could significantly inhibit the proliferation of tumor cells [144, 148–150]. They can degrade EGFRL858R/T790M through the ubiquitin-proteasome system in a time- and dose-dependent manner, but have no significant effect on EGFRWT.

It is well known that traditional PROTACs has problems such as poor cell permeability, off-target effect and intrinsic “hook effect”. To solve these problems, some new delivery systems have been adopted. For example, Zhang et al. reported an intracellular prepared targeted chimeric nano-PROTACs model, which has a central radial degradation network and can achieve effective dose-dependent protein degradation in tumors [195]. It can degrade EGFR in a wide range in vitro in a dose-dependent manner, with a degradation rate of 95%. This indicated that it has significant promise in overcoming the key drawbacks of traditional PROTACs, particularly the “hook effect” and off-target side effects. Vartak et al. prepared a PROTACs (EPRO and BPRO) nano-liposome formulation for degrading EGFR and BRD4. By using an improved hydration technique, the encapsulation of two highly lipophilic molecules was achieved in the EGFR-targeting nano-liposomal carriers (T-BEPRO). The tumor growth inhibition rate (TGI) of T-BEPRO in tumor-bearing mice is as high as 77.6%, demonstrating a lasting potential for tumor suppression [146]. Vartak et al. developed antibody-based PROTAC (ABTAC), which can effectively degrade EGFR that drives cancer proliferation and progression [157]. Fang et al. reported a type of nano-PROTACs based on silica (SiPROTACs), which has multiple ligands on its surface and can target and degrade the EGFR. In PC-9 and HCC-827 cell lines, SiPROTACs were able to initiate EGFR degradation at a concentration as low as 50 nM, and their efficiency was significantly higher than that of traditional PROTACs [158]. Wang et al. demonstrated the development of a versatile split-and-mix liposomal PROTAC based on liposome self-assembly (containing DSPE-PEG2000-EGFR ligand and DSPE-PEG2000-E3 ligand) for EGFR degradation [196]. Compared with traditional PROTACs, it can achieve efficient EGFR degradation through the lysosome-autophagy pathway and the E3-dependent mechanism. These can provide references for improving the degradation efficiency of PROTAC in tumors in the future. The chemical structural formulas of each representative compound are shown in Fig. 6.

BG-60366 is designed to degrade mutant EGFR, which is a common cause for NSCLC. BG-60366 is currently in the clinical development stage and is undergoing Phase 1 trials in patients carrying EGFR-activating mutations (who have previously received standard EGFR-TKI treatment). Stage 1a requires progression of previous third-generation EGFR-TKI and is not suitable for standard treatment (such as platinum-based chemotherapy). The Phase 1a safety expansion cohort requires the recording of EGFR resistance mutations (especially C797S). Clinical efficacy data have not yet been disclosed [NCT06685718].

The design of CFT8919 is aimed at exerting a potent and selective inhibitory effect on EGFR that carries the oncogenic L858R mutation. CFT8919 demonstrated activity both in vivo and in vitro in L858R-driven NSCLC models and maintained full activity against additional mutations resistant to EGFR inhibitors, including L858R-T790M, L858R-C797S and L858R-T790M-C797S in preclinical studies [197]. CFT8919 is currently in the clinical development stage and is undergoing Phase 1 trials in patients with locally advanced or metastatic NSCLC carrying EGFR mutations. Only patients with EGFR L858R mutation were included, and Del19 or other mutation types were not included. The focus is on monitoring the mutations of T790M and C797S. This study includes dose escalation, cohort expansion and dose expansion. The main objective is to evaluate its safety and tolerability as well as its anti-tumor activity in different cohorts. The secondary objective is to assess the pharmacokinetic parameters [NCT06641609]. Clinical efficacy data have not yet been disclosed.

HJ-004-02 is the first PROTAC targeting EGFR mutations that has entered clinical development and is orally bioavailable. It aims to induce selective ubiquitination and proteasome degradation of mutant EGFR. Currently, this drug is undergoing a Phase 1 clinical trial in patients with advanced non-squamous NSCLC who have EGFR mutations. The study enrolls approximately 36 patients and aims to evaluate the safety, pharmacokinetic (PK) characteristics, tolerability and anti-tumor activity of HJ-004-02, with an estimated enrollment of approximately 36 patients. The patients participating in the trial include those who experienced disease progression during previous standard treatments (such as EGFR inhibitors), and a 28-day treatment cycle is used to determine the optimal and tolerable dose [47]. Although no clinical efficacy data have been released yet, this method provides the first opportunity to assess whether targeting EGFR degradation can overcome the current resistance mechanisms faced by EGFR-targeted therapies.

HSK40118 is undergoing Phase 1 trials in patients with locally advanced or metastatic NSCLC who carry activating EGFR mutations. This study consists of two phases: phase 1a is the dose escalation stage, and phase 1b is the dose expansion stage. The enrolled patients must meet the following conditions: phase 1a: they have received at least one EGFR-TKI treatment before, including first-generation, second-generation, and third-generation EGFR-TKIs; phase 1a / phase 1b: they have received third-generation EGFR-TKI treatment before[NCT06050980]. Clinical efficacy data have not yet been disclosed.

PROTACs targeting c-Myc

The c-Myc protein is an oncogenic transcription factor that is considered as an effective target for cancer treatment because it is implicated in up to 70% of human cancers and has a great influence on tumorigenesis and treatment resistance [1, 198–202]. However, the c-Myc protein has been considered a traditionally “undruggable” target due to several practical and conceptual difficulties in targeting it, including its inherently disordered structure, localization to the nucleus, lacking of a defining pocket for small molecule binding, and potential “targeted” toxicity to normal tissues [15, 161, 203]. For that reason, wang et al. identified an aptamer specific for c-Myc (MA9C1) by establishing an efficient, high-throughput aptamer screening strategy called microwell-SELEX. Using aptamer MA9C1 as ligand, a multifunctional aptamer based proteolytic targeting chimera (PROTAC) was developed for proteolytic c-Myc (ProMyc). ProMyc could not only degrade c-Myc through the UPS, but also reduce Max protein and synergically inhibit the transcriptional activity of c-Myc in HCT116 cells [161]. Saraswat et al. prepared ARV-NP by nano-precipitation method. It has obvious cytotoxic, apoptotic and anti-clonal effects on pancreatic cancer cells, and significantly down-regulates the target protein c-Myc [128].

In addition, there are many studies on the degradation of c-Myc using oligonucleotide PROTAC. Li et al. developed a conjugate termed TNA-E-box-pomalidomide (TEP), which utilizes a threose nucleic acid (TNA) aptamer to bind the c-Myc/Max heterodimer. This aptamer was attached to an E-box DNA sequence, forming a bivalent conjugate with high affinity and stability that specifically degrades endogenous c-Myc/Max and simultaneously exerts a strong anti-tumor effect on breast cancer [160]. Ai et al. reported a strategy for c-Myc degradation in hepatocellular carcinoma using oligonucleotide-based PROTACs (MP-17 and MP-16), which conjugate the VH032 ligand to a c-Myc-targeting DNA sequence [58]. These findings affirm the potential feasibility of oligonucleotide PROTAC targeting c-Myc in cancer treatment. The chemical structural formulas of each representative compound are shown in Fig. 7.

PROTACs targeting p53

p53 is one of the genes with the highest mutation frequency in human cancers and is present in a majority (> 50%) of human tumors [204]. Mutations in p53 not only impair its anti-tumor activity, but also affect the carcinogenic properties of the mutated p53 protein [205–210] Therefore, p53 is widely considered the “undruggable” [211].

In view of the lack of small molecule drug active sites that make these mutants less effective therapeutically, in recent studies, a selective and effective PROTAC was developed to target p53-R175H, a common hotspot mutant with dominant-negative and carcinogenic activities. This study focuses on the design of a high-performance DNA aptamer to improve the specificity and affinity of p53-R175H utilizing a new iterative molecular docking guided post-SELEX strategy. Then, using the aptamer as the binder of PROTAC, a selective p53-R175H degrader was developed and known as dp53m (Fig. 8). dp53m had a great anti-tumor effect on p53-R175H-driven cancer cells both in vivo and in vitro, and is non-toxic. In addition, dp53m also enhanced the sensitivity of cancer cells expressing p53-R175H to cisplatin [59]. Thus, these studies suggest that dp53m is expected to become a potential therapeutic drug in p53-R175H-driven cancers.

Conclusions and perspectives

The event-driven, catalytic mechanism of PROTACs allows for sustained target degradation at low doses, reduced risk of resistance, and the potential to eliminate both non-enzymatic and enzymatic function of target proteins [212, 213]. Despite these advancements, PROTAC technology still faces limitations: these relatively high-molecular-weight (> 800 Da) compounds have potential low bioavailability, poor cell permeability, and a low blood-brain barrier diffusion coefficient [214, 215]. These limitations are largely due to the complex interactions among the three components of PROTACs—linkers, POIs and E3 ligase ligands, which require precise optimization to achieve desired drug-like qualities. However, recent advancements in structural design offer hope for overcoming these obstacles. By modifying the structure of PROTACs, such as optimizing the length and composition of the linker can change cellular permeability, metabolic stability, and solubility [7, 88, 216–218]. The recently disclosed structures of ARV-471 and ARV-110 suggest that increasing rigidity and shortening length is a practical linker design approach. Exploring new E3 ligases in addition to the commonly used CRBN and VHL can improve the stability and effectiveness of PROTACs, or developing new POIs can degrade “undruggable” targets that lack suitable drug-binding sites. Additionally, the application of computational methods like MEGA PROTAC has shown promise in improving the screening and design processes [219]. Cancer dependency map is drawn by screening out genes that inhibit the growth of cancer cell with functional loss can provide many potential targets for treatment [220]. Among these targets, 395 non-enzyme targets usually do not have key functional active sites that can be targeted by occupancy-driven small-molecule inhibitors [221, 222]. Therefore, PROTACs provide a novel approach to directly degrade these “undruggable” proteins through the UPS. As research progresses, the continuous optimization of PROTAC structures and the expansion of targetable proteins will undoubtedly bring new breakthroughs in cancer treatment and other therapeutic areas.
