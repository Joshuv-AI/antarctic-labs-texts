Procalcitonin, C-reactive protein, and white blood cell count levels in end-stage cancer patients: A retrospective study on inflammatory markers and their prognostic value
Authors: Qihao Zhou, Xuefen Lu, Liping Qian, Chaochao Yu, Jingru Xie, Dexia Kong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Procalcitonin (PCT), C-reactive protein (CRP), and white blood cell count (WBC) are commonly recognized as inflammatory markers. Some studies showed that these markers were also related to some cancers. This study aimed to investigate whether these markers were exhibited aberrations in end-stage cancer patients and to assess their correlation with infection and prognosis. The study underscores the potential of inflammatory markers as prognostic indicators in end-stage cancer, proposing that routine monitoring of these markers could substantially enhance patient management and improve outcomes. The medical records of 116 cancer patients were retrospectively reviewed. Data from end-stage cancer patients were collected during the week preceding their death. The control group consisted of cancer patients with a performance status score of 3 to 4 and clinical stage of III to IV. Normally distributed continuous data (mean ± SD) were analyzed using t-test. Non-normally distributed data (median [M] with interquartile range [P25, P75]) were analyzed using Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Numerical data were subjected to Chi-square test. Logistic regression analysis was employed to assess the correlation between inflammatory markers and patient prognosis. There were significant differences between the 2 groups for PCT, WBC, CRP, platelet count, and body mass index. However, subgroup analysis indicated that there were no significant differences in PCT, CRP, and WBC levels between infected and noninfected patients within the deceased group. It is worth noting that elevated levels of PCT (OR = 7.13, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.68–30.36, P = .008) and WBC (OR = 2.47, 95% CI = 1.13–1.13, P = .024) were ultimately identified as independent risk factors associated with poor prognosis in cancer patients, as demonstrated by further logistic regression analysis. In the subgroup analysis of noninfected patients, the univariate analysis revealed that both PCT (OR = 11.612, 95% CI = 1.217–110.776, P = .033) and WBC (OR = 3.077, 95% CI = 1.004–9.427, P = .049) had a statistically significant difference in outcomes between the alive group and the deceased group. The PCT, WBC, and CRP levels of end-stage cancer patients were significantly elevated, regardless of infection. An increase in PCT and WBC was associated with an increased risk of death. These findings suggest that monitoring PCT and WBC levels in end-stage cancer patients may provide valuable prognostic information, aiding in clinical decision-making.

1. Introduction

The development of cancer is a complex process. Worldwide, millions of new cancer cases occurred, with approximately 16% attributable to infectious agents. This proportion, however, is still on the rise.[1,2] Various clinical and experimental studies have revealed that a majority of cancer-related deaths are associated with chronic infection.[3] Likewise, certain inflammatory disorders arising from nonetiologies can potentially trigger the process of carcinogenesis.[1,3] Consequently, inflammation is frequently associated with the initiation and advancement of cancer. In addition, a multitude of environmentally induced carcinogenic factors and elements of risk are frequently correlated with various manifestations of chronic inflammatory states.[4]

Procalcitonin (PCT) consists of 116 amino acids and represents the precursor to the peptide calcitonin. It is regarded as a diagnostic marker for septic patients and a marker for systemic inflammation.[5] C-reactive protein (CRP) is a nonspecific acute phase protein those is secreted in response to various cytokines.[3] Traditionally, it has been used as a marker for infection, especially sepsis, and for cardiovascular events.[6,7] White blood cell (WBC) is the primary effector of the immune system protecting the body against infection and resolving tissue injury.[8] As is well known, PCT, CRP, and WBC are all associated with inflammation. However, they are also closely associated with cancer. It was found that cancer patients had higher serum PCT levels than non-cancer patients.[9] Studies showed elevated CRP in ovarian, lung, endometrial, and urothelial cancers, linked to poor prognosis.[10–13] Some studies associated lung, breast, and kidney cancers with higher WBC.[8,14] However, there is a lack of pertinent literature documenting the variations of these indicators in specific patients with end-stage tumors.

Unlike prior literary reports, this study centered its focus on patients with end-stage cancer, with the objectives of examining whether inflammatory markers exhibited abnormalities in this patient population and assessing their correlation with infection and prognosis. Furthermore, the study aimed to elucidate whether these alterations could serve as predictive indicators of disease progression in the clinical management of such patients.

2. Methods

2.1. Patients

We conducted a retrospective study of cancer patients who died in the Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital Nanxun and Wangjiangshan District from 2022 to 2023, and collected data on their serum levels of biomarkers such as procalcitonin (PCT), WBC count, hemoglobin (Hb), platelet count (PLT), and C-reactive protein (CRP) during the week preceding their death.

The eligibility criteria were as follows:

(a) Patients who had been diagnosed with cancer through pathological examination.

(b) For patients in the experimental group, relevant laboratory test results had to be available within 1 week prior to their death.

(c) Given the extreme frailty of the patients in the experimental group, who are in the advanced stages of their tumor, the control group was comprised of individuals required to have an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status (PS) score ranging from 3 to 4, and to be at clinical stages III to IV of their cancer. Additionally, they had to be still alive 1 month after the collection of the relevant indicators.

Additionally, they had to be still alive 1 month after the collection of the relevant indicators.

The exclusion criteria were as follows:

(a) Unclear cancer diagnosis.

This study was approved by the ethics committee of Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital (QT2024018), and was conducted according to the ethical standards of our institutional research committee and the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. All participants or the next of kin of deceased patients provided written informed consent to the study.

2.2. Patient characteristics and clinical data

We collected patients’ data including sex, age, pathologic diagnosis, body mass index (BMI), and treatment status at the time of PCT assessment. The objective data included complete blood count, serum biochemistry panels, CRP, and PCT levels. A total peripheral blood cell analysis was conducted using an automated hematology analyzer (BC-7500, Mindray). The CRP levels were determined through a latex-enhanced immunoturbidimetric assay utilizing the same automated hematology analyzer (BC-7500, Mindray). The serum PCT concentrations were measured in serum samples employing a Wan200 + automatic chemiluminescence analyzer (Xiamen Youmaike Medical Instrument Co., Ltd.), with a detection limit of 0.05 ng/mL. We categorized the PCT concentrations into 5 groups: < .05 ng/mL, 0.05 to 1 ng/mL, 1.001 to 2 ng/mL, 2.001 to 10 ng/mL, and > 10 ng/mL. We categorized the CRP concentrations into 4 groups: < 10 mg/L, 10 to 50 mg/L, 50.001 to 100 mg/L, and > 100 mg/L. We categorized the WBC concentrations into 4 groups: < 4 × 109/L, 4 to 10 × 109/L, 10.001 to 20 × 109/L, and > 20 × 109/L.

2.3. Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (SPSS Inc, Chicago). Continuous data that follow a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± SD and analyzed using the t-test. For data that did not conform to the normal distribution, the median (interquartile range) M (P25, P75) was reported, and Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed. Numerical data were expressed as percents (%) and subjected to Chi-square test. Since logistic regression is capable of identifying independent risk factors, it was utilized to evaluate the correlation between PCT, along with other indicators, and the prognosis of patients. The results were reported as an odds ratio with a 95% confidence interval (CI). A value of P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

There were no statistically significant differences in general data including age, gender, pathological types, treatment, and hemoglobin between the groups (P > .05).

3.2. Comparison of infection status between deceased and alive group

In the deceased group, there were 18 patients who had clear signs of infection when they were tested in the laboratory, including lung infections, abdominal or pelvic infections, and urinary system infections. In alive group there were 26 patients. The PCT, WBC, and CRP of the 2 groups of patients was presented in Tables 2, 3, and 4 respectively. Among the deceased group, there was no statistically significant difference in PCT and CRP levels between infected and noninfected individuals (P > .05). No statistically significant difference in WBC was found between infected and noninfected individuals in both the deceased and alive groups (P > .05). In the alive group, the levels of PCT and CRP showed statistically significant differences between infected and noninfected individuals (P < .05). This indicates that, in clinical practice, alterations in PCT, CRP, and WBC levels among end-stage patients may not solely result from infection. However, when considering changes in PCT and CRP levels in end-stage patients, infection factors remain a primary consideration.

3.3. Comparative analysis of factors affecting outcome events between the deceased and alive group

We conducted a logistic regression analysis on the clinical characteristic variables in Table 1, which revealed statistically significant differences between the deceased and alive groups (Table 5). The Omnibus test and goodness of fit test indicated that the model was statistically significant and exhibited good fit to the data. The results showed that PCT (OR = 7.13, 95% CI = 1.68–30.36, P = .008) and WBC (OR = 2.47, 95% CI = 1.13–1.13, P = .024) had a statistically significant difference in outcomes between the alive group and the deceased group, with an increase in PCT and WBC being associated with an increased risk of death. No statistically significant difference was found in BMI (OR = 0.91, 95% CI = 0.80–1.04, P = .164), PLT (OR = 0.10, 95% CI = 0.99–1.00, P = .294), and CRP (OR = 1.53, 95% CI = 0.89–2.64, P = .125) levels between the 2 groups. The findings indicate that in clinical practice, an elevation in PCT and WBC levels necessitates careful consideration of whether the patient’s cancer has advanced.

4. Discussion

This was a retrospective study. In this study, we collected the data of end-stage cancer patients during the week preceding their death. The control group was selected from the cancer patients with 3 to 4 PS and III-IV clinical stages. We found statistically significant differences between the 2 groups for PCT (Z = −6.55, P < .001), WBC (Z = −5.30, P < .001), CRP (Z = −4.52, P < .001), PLT (Z = −2.33, P = .020), and BMI (t = −2.04, P = .043).

Numerous clinical and experimental investigations had demonstrated that persistent infections were linked to the majority of cancer-related fatalities.[3] In addition to the direct carcinogenic mechanisms linked to the infectious agents, a persistent inflammatory environment – a consequence of the body’s inability to clear the infection – also contributed to the development of these cancers.[1,3] As we know, PCT, CRP, and WBC are all factors related to inflammation.

PCT is an inactive precursor of calcitonin and is composed of 116 amino acids. It is considered a marker for systemic inflammation and an important diagnostic marker of septic patients.[5] The level of PCT was also found high in severe COVID-19 cases,[15] Streptococcus pneumonia patients,[16] and acute respiratory distress syndrome in patients undergoing cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass.[17] Procalcitonin is normally secreted by the thyroid parafollicular cells, and its serum concentration in healthy people is very low, usually < 0.05 ng/mL. PCT detectable in the plasma during infection is not produced by C-cells of the thyroid but rather by the neuroendocrine cells in the lungs or intestine.[5] Noninfectious causes such as surgery, shock, burn injury, trauma, chronic kidney disease and liver cirrhosis all can induce procalcitonin production.[5,18] Thus, PCT can be raised in certain neoplastic situations without an infectious process. This is especially true for tumorous disorders such as medullary thyroid cancer and SCLC, where PCT is secreted by the tumor cells.[19]

A review brought attention to the connection between PCT and cancer. It was discovered that, in comparison to non-cancer patients, serum PCT levels were higher in cancer patients.[9] Kajikawa et al found serum PCT in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) was elevated regardless of the presence of infection. Higher PCT levels are associated with poor PS and shorter OS in NSCLC. They speculated that poorer general conditions or tumor progression caused high PCT levels.[20] Furthermore, our study of end-stage cancer patients has confirmed that this association is not limited to NSCLC, but is also observed in other types of terminal-stage cancers. The reason for this was still unclear. Some thought tumor cells and surrounding cells release proinflammatory molecules such as tumor necrosis factor–α (TNF-α), cytokines interleuin-6 (IL-6), and interleukin-1β, creating an inflammatory tumor microenvironment in cancer.[21] Secretion of PCT is activated in diverse parenchymal tissues in response to systemic inflammation which is mediated by TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β.[20] As we know solid tumors often occur hypoxic conditions. Hypoxic conditions with increased pro-inflammatory cytokines may lead to increased levels of PCT, tumor progression and metastases, and deteriorated general condition, all of which likely lead to poor prognosis.[20]

CRP is an acute inflammatory protein that has been traditionally utilized as a marker of infection and cardiovascular events.[7] Similarly to serum PCT, it has been used as a serum marker of infection and sepsis in cancer patients.[6] WBC is the primary effector of the immune system protecting the body against infection and resolving tissue injury.[8] In addition to their roles in inflammation, both CRP and WBC have been linked to tumor biology. Elevated levels of these biomarkers have been associated with various aspects of tumor development and progression. Studies had indicated that elevated CRP levels were observed in certain types of cancer, including ovarian, lung, endometrial, and urothelial cancers. These increased CRP levels were associated with a poor prognosis.[10–13] Some studies showed that cancers of the lung, breast, and kidney were associated with higher WBC.[8,14]

Infection in cancer patients is a poor prognostic factor. However, was the difference observed in PCT, CRP, and WBC levels between the 2 groups due to infection? We conducted subgroup analysis to evaluate the correlation between infection and biomarker levels in the deceased and alive patient groups. The PCT levels showed no significant difference between infected and noninfected patients (χ² = 3.30, P = .533), similarly, CRP (χ² = 4.06, P = .232) and WBC (χ² = 2.04, P = .588) levels also did not exhibit statistical significance. That meant, there was no significant correlation between elevated PCT, CRP, and WBC levels and the presence of infection in the deceased patient group. Certainly, there were differences in PCT and CRP levels between infected and noninfected patients within the alive group, which aligns with clinical practice. Therefore, we speculate that the increase levels of PCT, CRP, and WBC in the deceased group patients may be associated with tumor progression and the severity of the disease.

There is a strong association between cancer cells and platelets, as the former stimulate platelet multiplication, while the latter aid in cancer cell invasion and distant metastasis.[14] Studies have shown that thrombocytosis is a poor prognostic factor for lung cancer patients.[14,22] In our study, we observed statistically significant differences in platelets between the 2 groups. Furthermore, there was a statistically significant difference in BMI between the 2 groups, which could be attributed to the poor nutritional status often seen in patients with end-stage cancers.

In our study, the outcomes for the 2 patient groups were different. Patients in the deceased group passed away within a week, whereas those in the control group were still alive after 1 month. In comparing the 2 groups, we found significant differences in BMI PCT, WBC, PLT, and CRP levels. Further logistic regression analysis revealed that elevated PCT (OR = 7.13, 95% CI = 1.68–30.36, P = .008) and WBC (OR = 2.47, 95% CI = 1.13–1.13, P = .024) were the ultimate independent risk factors for poor prognosis in these patients. In the subgroup analysis of noninfected patients, the univariate analysis revealed that the p-values for the clinical characteristic variables, including treatment, BMI, PCT, WBC, and CRP were < .05. Further multivariate analysis revealed that, among noninfected patients, both PCT (OR = 11.612, 95% CI = 1.217–110.776, P = .033) and WBC (OR = 3.077, 95% CI = 1.004–9.427, P = .049) were significantly associated with differential outcomes between the alive group and the deceased group. Similarly, in noninfected patients, elevated levels of both PCT and WBC were identified as significant independent risk factors for a poor prognosis. Notably, the P-value for WBC, though significant, was very close to the conventional cutoff of 0.05, suggesting a weaker association compared to PCT.

In a study by Song et al on incident cancer cases in the UK Biobank, it was shown that increases in the total WBC were associated with a higher overall risk of cancer, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.05 (95% CI = 1.03–1.06) per quartile increase.[8] Building upon this evidence, our research further confirms that an increased WBC serves as a definitive and independent predictor of poor prognosis in cancer patients. In the study by Chen et al, which employed multivariable Cox regression analysis, pretreatment PCT levels were identified as an independent predictor of progression-free survival in patients with extensive-stage SCLC receiving first-line chemotherapy.[19] Consistent with the findings of Kajikawa et al, the multivariate analysis identified the PCT level as an independent prognostic factor for NSCLC (HR 3.951, P = .010).[20] Our research provides further confirmation that PCT (OR = 7.13, CI = 1.68–30.36, P = .008) serves not only as an independent risk factor for lung cancer, but also plays a significant role in tumor progression.

Although studies on PCT and other indicators exist in cancer, to our knowledge, our study is the first to explore the differences in PCT and other related indicators between end-stage cancer patients who died within 1 week and those who survived during the same period. To avoid bias in patient selection for enrollment, considering the physical condition and tumor staging of the experimental group, our control group of cancer patients was chosen based on an ECOG PS score of 3 to 4 and clinical stages III–IV. We confirmed that these patients were still alive 1 month after the collection of indicators. While it is impossible to predict with certainty whether cancer patients will succumb to their illness within a week, this retrospective study offers valuable insights into the role of inflammatory markers, particularly elevated PCT and WBC, as risk factors for patient mortality, irrespective of infection status. These findings have the potential to transform current practices in the monitoring and management of cancer patients in their end stages, advocating for the inclusion of PCT and WBC monitoring as part of routine care plans.

Our study also has several limitations. First, being a retrospective study based on the medical records of a small cohort from a single institution, it inherently limits the generalizability of our results to other settings or populations. Secondly, the retrospective nature of the clinical tests, which rely on medical records for data collection, may give rise to missing, incomplete data, or potential errors. Moreover, we were unable to secure test results for the deceased patients dating back 2 weeks, 3 weeks, or even 1 month prior to their passing. Thirdly, our study failed to consider potential confounding factors that could impact the outcomes, including concomitant medications and individual differences in metabolism. Therefore, future research should incorporate multi-center studies and utilize larger sample sizes to further validate and corroborate the research findings. Additionally, large-scale prospective studies are needed to explore the biological mechanisms underlying the observed associations.

5. Conclusion

The PCT, WBC, and CRP levels of end-stage cancer patients were significantly elevated, regardless of infection. PCT and WBC were identified as significant independent risk factors associated with a poor prognosis in cancer patients, including those without infections. Elevated levels of both PCT and WBC were linked to an increased risk of death. This study highlights the potential of inflammatory markers as prognostic tools in end-stage cancer, suggesting that routine monitoring could significantly improve patient management and outcomes.
