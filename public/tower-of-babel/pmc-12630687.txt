Reconfigurable chalcogenide integrated nonlinear photonics
Authors: Di Xia, Liyang Luo, Linyi Wang, Xin Zhao, Zelin Yang, Jiayue Wu, Qi-Fan Yang, Zhaohui Li, Bin Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Nonlinear optics has extended the reach of integrated photonics to narrow-linewidth lasers, broadband light sources, and highly efficient wavelength converters. However, the dispersion characteristics of these nonlinear photonic devices, which determine the phase-matching conditions of various nonlinear optical processes, remain challenging to modify post-fabrication. Here, we present integrated GeSbS microresonators with dispersion profiles that can be arbitrarily reconfigured. Using intracavity backscattering, we generate standing wave patterns that can imprint or erase Bragg gratings along the microresonator via photosensitivity. The dispersion is synthesized from bidirectional resonances strongly coupled through the Bragg gratings. The tunable dispersion enables the adjustment of parametric oscillator sidebands, the spectral flattening of Kerr microcombs, and the deterministic generation of Brillouin lasers. Our approach allows for arbitrary and dynamic dispersion engineering in integrated photonics, paving the way for scalable and reconfigurable nonlinear devices that can be reprogrammed for multiple applications.

Introduction

The efficacy of numerous nonlinear optical processes fundamentally relies on the fulfilment of energy and momentum conservation, referred to as the phase-matching condition1. Achieving phase matching is often hindered by dispersion, which is typically compensated by the material’s birefringence in free-space optics. In integrated photonics, the tight confinement of the optical field introduces a geometric degree of freedom, enabling tailored dispersion properties. This capability, combined with advancements in ultralow-loss waveguide technology and high-quality-factor (high-Q) optical microresonators, has facilitated the realization of integrated optical parametric oscillators (OPOs)2,3, optical frequency combs4,5, frequency converters6–8, and quantum light sources9. Meanwhile, photonic integrated circuits with proper structure design harness the stimulated Brillouin scattering (SBS) processes to enable diverse applications in microwave photonics, signal processing and sensing10,11.

Modifying the microresonator’s geometry tunes the dispersion parameters Dn, thereby influencing all modes. Recently, dispersion engineering has been extended to individual modes, such as by coupling modes propagating in opposite directions12 or across coupled microresonators13–17. With sufficiently strong coupling, near-degenerate modes can hybridize, resulting in modified resonant frequencies. However, current implementations of individual-mode-level dispersion engineering face limited flexibility. For instance, microresonators engraved with multi-periodic gratings allow arbitrary dispersion engineering but cannot be adjusted post-fabrication12, rendering these devices monofunctional and susceptible to fabrication imperfections. On the other hand, dispersion can be reconfigured in coupled systems, but the degrees of freedom are limited by the number of resonators17. Achieving arbitrarily reconfigurable dispersion without introducing additional losses remains a challenge compared to free-space optical systems.

Chalcogenide glasses (ChGs) are emerging nonlinear optical materials18 with large nonlinear refractive indices (n2 > 10−18 m2/W) and photoelastic coefficients (p11 ≈ p12 ≈ 0.238). Their transparency window can be engineered to cover visible to the far-infrared regime by tailoring the material composition19–21. Moreover, by leveraging the photosensitivity and high Brillouin gain of ChGs, the Bragg gratings were internally written to selectively enhance or inhibit SBS effects in integrated ChG waveguides22,23. Recent progress in low-loss integrated ChG waveguide technology has realized Q exceeding 5 million24–26, enabling applications including broadband nonlinear photonics27, efficient acousto-optic modulation28, highly sensitive photoacoustic detection29, and high-speed photonic computing30.

In this work, we exploit the photosensitivity of ChGs to realize arbitrarily reconfigurable dispersion of integrated ChG microresonators. Such devices provide a versatile platform for widely tunable OPOs, spectrally uniform Kerr microcombs, and highly compact Brillouin lasers.

Results

Principle

We fabricate the microresonator core from Ge25Sb15S60 (GeSbS) following the procedure detailed in Methods31 (Fig. 1a). The molecular network of GeSbS contains homopolar and dangling bonds32, which can transform into heteropolar bonds upon optical illumination33 (Fig. 1b). We leverage this effect to imprint refractive index gratings within the microresonator. The device supports two degenerate travelling-wave modes propagating clockwise (cw) and counterclockwise (ccw). As illustrated in Fig. 1c, a laser coupled into the cw mode can be backscattered into the ccw mode via inherent inhomogeneities in the microresonator. The resulting interference between these counter-propagating waves generates periodic intensity modulations, preferentially reducing the refractive index at intensity maxima. This process establishes a Bragg grating that progressively enhances the coupling between the counter-propagating modes. When the coupling strength J is sufficiently large, the cw and ccw modes hybridize into non-degenerate standing-wave modes separated by 2J. These standing-wave modes align their intensity maxima with the refractive index variations of the Bragg grating. Specifically, the blue-shifted mode localizes in the laser-modified region (with additional refractive index reduction), while the red-shifted mode remains in the unmodified region (with only the overall change).

Pumping the blue-shifted mode continuously amplifies the index contrast of the Bragg grating, thereby enhancing mode splitting through an “imprinting” mechanism. During the “initialization” process, the resonance blue-shifts until distinct mode splitting appears (the presence of the “smeared-out” effect), whereas in the “imprinting” stage, the red-shifted mode remains nearly stationary (Fig. S7 in SI). Conversely, pumping the red-shifted mode induces additional Bragg gratings between the original ones, causing destructive interference with the initial reflections and reducing mode splitting via an “erasing” mechanism. Moreover, once the grating-induced refractive index contrast is sufficiently reduced during erasure, continued pumping drives the system back to the initial stage of splitting (Stage i in Fig. 1c). Notably, other modes within the mode family remain unaffected by this Bragg grating; their frequencies can also be tuned using the same process. This capability ultimately enables the creation of a mode family with arbitrarily set frequencies.

Experimentally, we send 40 mW of optical power into the microresonator to imprint the gratings (Fig. 1d). After maintaining the laser in the mode for 6 minutes, a pair of split modes emerges from the originally degenerate modes. We then continuously adjust the laser frequency to remain in the blue-detuned standing-wave mode, increasing the mode splitting to approximately 17 GHz. This splitting is sustained even after the laser is removed. Subsequently, the splitting is gradually erased by tuning the laser into the red-detuned standing-wave mode, the full dynamics and transmission spectra are provided and discussed in Fig. S7 of SI. Completely erasing the grating via the red-shifted mode pumping proves challenging. Decreasing grating index contrast brings the blue- and red-shifted resonances closer together. In this regime, a high-power laser scan across the resonance may unintentionally enhance one of the grating components, increasing the splitting width. To overcome this, we implement an approach to fully erase the grating using a thermal annealing process, see Fig. S9 in SI. It’s noted that thermal annealing is not the primary reconfigurability approach here, as it may increase power or temperature requirements in later cycles, limiting the total number of reprogrammings. Since both laser exposure and thermal annealing reduce the refractive index, subsequent tuning will require increased optical power or higher temperatures only if the prior modification has reached the saturation level. Otherwise, reprogramming can proceed under the same power or temperature.

The mode splitting as a function of time under fixed optical power is illustrated in Fig. 1e, with no degradation observed in the microresonator’s intrinsic Q factor. Reconfigurability of the device is further demonstrated by tuning the splitting from 1 GHz to 5 GHz over 80 cycles (Fig. S8 in SI). The reprogramming of resonance splitting distributions is governed by the refractive index contrast between the blue- and red-shifted resonances of each mode—greater contrast enables higher programmability. A splitting resonance with a large splitting width (127 GHz) exhibits only minor performance degradation (Fig. S10 in SI), serving as equivalent evidence of the device’s robust reprogramming capability.

It’s noted that managing the interplay between nonlinearity and the photosensitivity effects of ChGs is a critical aspect35, we exploit the photosensitive saturation effect to control these two kinds of processes (Fig. S3 in SI)36,37. We first imprint the desired grating using high pump power, inducing a resonance shift beyond the saturation level observed at lower powers. Subsequent low-power exposure no longer alters the refractive index, enabling operation in a regime dominated by nonlinear effects with photosensitivity effectively suppressed (Fig. S4 in SI). Moreover, photosensitivity saturation exhibits inherent power-dependent37, enabling further microresonator modification upon exposure to higher optical power. Critically, during grating inscription, efficiently generated nonlinear sidebands, particularly in over-coupled microresonators, can imprint unintended secondary gratings. To mitigate this effect, we can implement a dual-coupler microring38: target gratings are imprinted via coupler-1 (optimized for reduced sideband coupling), while nonlinear operations employ reduced pump power via coupler-2. The pump power and corresponding intracavity power density for imprinting grating or realizing nonlinear processes are listed in Supplementary Materials (Table S1).

Wavelength-accurate OPOs

Recently, Kerr microresonator OPOs have been demonstrated via dispersion engineering through waveguide geometry and radius optimization39–41. Additionally, photonic-crystal ring resonators (PhCR) have enabled OPOs through mode splitting, overcoming the constraints of traditional dispersion engineering42–44. However, these approaches lack post-fabrication reconfigurability of the operational modes45. We reconfigure a GeSbS microresonator with a radius of 100 μm and a free spectral range (FSR) of 200.3 GHz to achieve tunable and wavelength-accurate OPOs through Kerr-induced four-wave mixing. The device exhibits a negative D2/2π = − 3.1 MHz around 1556 nm, indicative of normal group velocity dispersion (GVD), which typically inhibits the initiation of parametric oscillations. We adjust the dispersion by red-shifting the central mode to overcome this limitation, thereby inducing local anomalous dispersion that facilitates parametric gain (Fig. 2a). Gratings are imprinted using approximately 40 mW of on-chip power, inducing mode splitting that causes a frequency shift in the pump mode. After red-shifting the mode by 1.2 GHz (Fig. 2b), we turn off the imprinting laser and then pump the red-shifted mode with 12 mW of on-chip power. Subsequently, we perform an erasing process to reduce the shift, thereby tuning the wavelength of the sidebands back to 30 FSRs from the pump. The experimentally measured dependence of the OPO wavelengths on the magnitude of the redshift shows good agreement with theoretical predictions (Fig. 2d). The management strategy for controlling nonlinearity and photosensitivity in GeSbS microresonators also demonstrates excellent stability in nonlinear processes (Fig. S5 in SI). Furthermore, a high conversion efficiency (>30%) and output power (>10 mW) of the sidebands are achieved in a microresonator with enhanced resonator-waveguide coupling efficiency (Fig. S14 in SI).

However, power-uniform multi-wavelength laser sources are desired for sensing and communication applications. Such flat spectra theoretically occur in microresonators with vanishing GVD across a broad spectrum12, specifically when higher-order dispersion coefficients Dn = 0 for n ≥ 2. These conditions cannot be achieved by modifying the microresonator’s cross-sectional geometry.

To address this, we imprint multiple grating patterns to eliminate the GVD in the telecommunication C-band. By sending a 40 mW imprinting laser into 29 modes, we sequentially tune their frequencies by introducing mode splitting. The illumination time is precisely controlled, such that the integrated dispersions of the blue-shifted modes are aligned to a straight line (Fig. 3c). It is noted that the intracavity refractive index variation during the imprinting of multiple grating patterns can significantly exceed the saturation level observed at 23 mW pump power, thereby ensuring stable splitting distribution and resonance positions for microcomb operation. Pumping the blue-shifted 0th mode with 23 mW generates a soliton microcomb with a flat spectrum spanning 55 nm, from 1543 nm to 1598 nm (Fig. 3d). The appearance of a soliton step during the pump laser scan across a cavity resonance, together with low-intensity-noise behavior (inserts in Fig. 3d), indicates the transition to a coherent soliton state46–48. The experimental results agree well with numerical simulations based on the Lugiato-Lefever equations accounting for bidirectional modal coupling (Fig. S2 in SI), further corroboration of the generation of a coherent pulse49.

Compact stimulated Brillouin lasers

Finally, we reconfigure a micron-scale GeSbS microresonator to realize stimulated Brillouin lasers (SBL).The structure of microresonators must be meticulously engineered to satisfy the phase-matching conditions among identical or distinct mode families, a capability demonstrated across various photonic platforms including chalcogenide glasses50,51, silicon52, silicon nitride53, and thin-film lithium niobate54. Recently, PhCRs have enabled the frequency-matching condition for SBL through mode splitting55, while their performance depends on precise fabrication and lacks post-fabrication reconfigurability.

Given the 7.4 GHz backward Brillouin shift of GeSbS within the telecommunication C-band56, a microresonator with an approximate radius of 4.4 mm is typically required to support resonances for both pump and Stokes wavelengths simultaneously. However, to achieve compactness, we employ a microresonator with a radius of 20 μm. By setting the power of the imprinting laser to approximately 15 mW (the intracavity power density remained high at 538 MW/cm2), we tune the mode splitting to align with the Brillouin shift (Fig. 4a). Pumping the blue-shifted mode induces SBL in the red-shifted mode. Since the standing-wave modes comprise both cw and ccw components, SBL is observed in both propagation directions (Fig. 4b). Additionally, we detect sidebands arising from four-wave mixing between the reflected pump and Stokes waves in the bus waveguide. By measuring the Stokes power as a function of pump power, we determine a lasing threshold of 460 μW (Fig. 4c), which is lower than previously reported thresholds for on-chip SBLs fabricated from ChGs24. This notably low threshold is attributed to the significantly reduced mode volume, despite a relatively modest loaded Q factor of 5 × 105. To confirm the coherence of the SBL, we measure the electrical beat note between the pump and the SBL (Fig. 4d), revealing a linewidth of 667 Hz. The individual linewidths of the pump and SBL are presented in Fig. 4e, with the intrinsic linewidth of the SBL determined to be 81 Hz. Discussion

In summary, we have demonstrated nanophotonic devices with arbitrarily reconfigurable dispersion that remains nonvolatile over extended periods. Their benefits to integrated photonics can be considered from two key perspectives. First, reconfigurability provides multifunctionality and precise control, which is crucial as the complexity of integrated photonic circuits increases. As circuit size scales up, fabrication yield becomes a critical challenge, particularly for nonlinear optical devices where dispersion is highly sensitive to minute fabrication-induced variations. Our approach enables each device to be reconfigured to perform in predefined or synchronized modes, allowing the synergetic operation of hundreds of nonlinear devices in large-scale communication, computation, and quantum circuits. Second, the ability to arbitrarily tailor dispersion opens possibilities beyond the capabilities of conventional devices. For instance, the spectrally engineered microcombs we achieved exhibit flatness and spectral width comparable to non-resonant electro-optic frequency combs59, but with broader bandwidth, higher repetition rates, and no microwave power consumption.

It should be noted that the multifunctionality and reconfigurability of these devices are fundamentally rooted in the versatile nonlinear optical properties of ChGs. Fortunately, ChGs are highly compatible with other established photonic platforms. Low-temperature thermal deposition techniques (below 300 °C) have enabled the wafer-scale fabrication of Ge25Sb15S60 films with high laser damage thresholds on silicon and LiNbO3 substrates, thus eliminating the need for wafer bonding28,60,61. Future work is poised to the fabrication of silicon nitride-ChG heterogeneous integrated devices to leverage the photosensitivity of ChGs in combination with low-loss silicon photonics. In this direction, a thin ChG film is deposited onto a silicon nitride integrated circuit without requiring any etching of the ChG layer, opening avenues for reconfigurable functionalities in silicon photonics. Consequently, the large Kerr nonlinearity and Brillouin gain of ChGs, combined with their reconfigurability and dispersion control, offer tremendous potential for accelerating the integration of nonlinear optics in the photonic industry.

Methods

Silicon-based Chalcogenide glass (ChG) photonic chip fabrication

The GeSbS thin film is deposited on a 3-μm-thick thermal oxide substrate via thermal deposition method. We use electron beam lithography (EBL) to define the microresonator and waveguide patterns. The corresponding coated photoresist is ARP-6200. Then, an improved etching process is performed to transfer the patterns to the GeSbS layer60. Finally, the devices are clad with two kinds of cladding materials. The 2-μm-thick Benzocyclobutene (BCB) polymer is deposited on the chip as a cladding. It’s noted that the soft cladding of BCB can lead to acoustic leakage and reduced SBS gain62. Therefore, a 3-μm-thick stiff SiO2 cladding is chosen and deposited via the inductively coupled plasma-chemical vapor deposition (ICP-CVD) method to enhance the confinement of acoustic modes and improve the SBS efficiency.

Inprinting grating and reconfigurable splitting width modulation

Our method uses two continuous tunable lasers (Fig. S6 in SI), one as the pump laser is amplified and launched into the GeSbS microresonator with high power (on-chip power 40 mW), and the other as the probe laser is used to characterize the resonance mode structures based on Mach-Zehnder interferometer optical sampling technique. During the imprinting grating process, the pump laser is locked to the cavity mode by the Pound-Drever-Hall (PDH) approach, ensuring the pump wavelength can be automatic tuned to compensate for the resonance shift. Once the mode splitting can be observed, the PDH error signal can appear two locking point, enabling selective excitation of either the blue-shifted or red-shifted resonance. The frequency of RF source is set to 2 GHz, lager than the loaded resonance linewidth ( ~200 MHz), ensuring a steep error signal across the resonance. Once the mode splitting can be observed, the PDH error signal can appear two locking point, enabling selective excitation of either the blue-shifted or red-shifted resonance (Fig. S6 in SI). We note that the intrinsic backward scattering of the microresonator of the chip will induce bidirectional propagation modes, forming an interference between cw and ccw modes. We measure the transmission spectrum every 1 minute using a probe laser with a low power of around 0.02 mW.

To demonstrate the reconfigurable property in our GeSbS microresonators, the splitting width control is performed with over 80 steps (Fig. S8 in SI). During this process, the on-chip pump power is further increased to around 60 mW to ensure sufficient index contrast of the Bragg grating for effective control over multiple splitting cycles.

Experimental setup for nonlinear processes

We pump the target resonance via a C-band continuous-wave tunable laser (Toptica CTL 1550), which can be amplified through an erbium-doped fiber amplifier (EDFA) (Amonics, AEDFA-33-B-FA). The output signals are divided into three parts to monitor the optical spectrum by an optical spectrum analyzer (YOKOGAWA AQ6370D), the intensity noise or beat note signal by an electrical spectrum analyzer (Agilent N9030A), and the power trace of pump light by an oscilloscope (Keysight, DSOS404A), respectively. The reflected signal can be detected using a fiber circulator in the input port. Furthermore, we use the delayed self-heterodyne Method to measure the frequency noise level of SBL. We use a tunable narrowband bandpass filter (EXFO-XTM-50) to filter out the SBL and divide it into two paths with a 5050 coupler. SBL passes through a 100 MHz acoustic optical modulator (AOM) and a 100 m optical fiber. Finally, we obtain the beatnote of the two optical signals through a balanced detector. The fundamental linewidth can be given by the white-frequency-noise floor times π. Since the coupling between pump amplitude noise and Brillouin frequency noise64,65, the Brillouin laser inherits the pump’s low-frequency noise spectrum shape at low frequency offset range. We note that the fiber-delayed self-heterodyne measurement technique is vulnerable to ambient acoustic and vibrational perturbations coupled into the Mach-Zehnder interferometer, reducing its accuracy at low offset frequencies. A more reliable approach would involve heterodyne beat note detection between the Brillouin laser and a frequency-stabilized reference laser66, which was not available during this study. Consequently, Fig. 4e displays only the data above 104 Hz, which accurately reflect the laser noise characteristics.
