Public awareness and acceptance of malaria vaccine in Sub-Saharan Africa: a systematic review
Authors: Efosa Ekhator, Victor Godwin Essien, Oluwatosin Esther Afolabi, Moses Ikpomwonsa Okundaye, Temitope Adewonbi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe African continent continues to experience a high prevalence of malaria more than three years after the licensure of the first malaria vaccine. This study aimed to review relevant literature on malaria vaccine awareness and acceptance among the public in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA).MethodsThe Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) Statement of 2020 was used for reporting this systematic review. Related publications were searched from nine public databases. The search was restricted to studies published between January 2021 and March 2025, using the Population, Exposure, Outcome (PEO) framework to assess the eligibility. The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Tools were used for critical appraisal, and relevant data were extracted into Microsoft Excel 2016, where they were synthesized.ResultsA total of 33 studies were included in this review, predominantly from West Africa and of cross-sectional design. Malaria vaccine awareness ranged from 7.26% in the Democratic Republic of Congo to 95.6% in Ghana, with healthcare workers as the main information source. Acceptance levels also varied widely, from 10.3% to 100%, and were generally higher in Nigeria and Ghana. Beyond acceptance, participants from both countries were also willing to recommend the vaccine to others. There was scant evidence on the acceptance of malaria vaccines among the vaccine types. Education and occupation were consistently associated with greater awareness and acceptance, while factors such as age, gender, and income showed inconsistent or limited influence. In three African nations (Uganda, Ghana, and Nigeria), higher education was associated with a greater likelihood of vaccine awareness. Employed people also had higher awareness and acceptance of the vaccines in SSA. Moreover, the awareness of the vaccine was associated with its acceptance.ConclusionMalaria vaccine awareness and acceptance in SSA vary widely across regions, with education, occupation, and healthcare workers playing key roles in shaping positive uptake. Hence, more concrete steps are needed to increase regional awareness and acceptance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25196-5.Keywords: Malaria, Vaccines, Acceptance, Awareness, Africa, Caregivers, Children

Background

Malaria is an infectious disease caused by Plasmodium parasites and transmitted to humans by infected female Anopheles mosquitoes during a blood meal. Malaria has continued to plague the lives of humans all over the world [1]. The burden is particularly high in Africa, where environmental, economic, and behavioural factors contribute to the disease [2]. Presently, Africa houses the highest population of malaria-infected people in the world, being responsible for as much as 94% of cases and 95% of deaths [3]. Two sub-Saharan African (SSA) countries (Nigeria and the Democratic Republic of Congo) combined accounted for 39% of global malaria cases in 2023 [3]. Moreover, SSA is a major source of malaria export to some European and North American countries [4, 5]. With an already struggling healthcare system in Africa [6], the high malaria burden increases healthcare service usage [7], thus reducing attention to other diseases. Pregnant women and children are at the highest risk for the infection [8, 9].

The role of adequate prevention in curbing the malaria epidemic in Africa has been well-stressed in various policy statements [10, 11]. Years of painstaking research led to the development and licensing of the first malaria vaccine, called RTS, S/AS01 (or Mosquirix), in 2021, and another, called R21/Matrix-M, was licensed in 2023 [12]. These vaccines have continued to be rolled out into routine immunisation in various countries in SSA, starting with Cameroon in 2021 [13]. As of April 2025, about 20 countries in the African continent have established malaria vaccination programs, and more are expected [14].

As with other immunisation programs, the role of public awareness, knowledge, and acceptability of the vaccine in the success of such intervention is pivotal [15]. This may be an issue in SSA, considering the widespread health illiteracy [16]. Furthermore, cultural and religious factors associated with the spread of myths surrounding vaccination can hamper the programs’ success [17], as seen with the COVID-19 vaccine on the continent [18]. The result is the promotion of malaria vaccine hesitancy in Africa [19]. Low vaccine literacy promotes vaccine hesitance as people can generally decide whether to take an intervention when they have previously heard of it and refuse to accept what they have no knowledge of, as evident during the COVID-19 pandemic [20].

Furthermore, sociodemographic variables including age, sex, and socio-economic status (education, income, and occupation) are among the most common variables assessed for possible association with vaccine awareness and acceptance [21, 22]. Determining the relationship between these variables and vaccine acceptance can help health actors determine which population requires attention, particularly in terms of advocacy.

Previous studies have sought to review the awareness and acceptability of malaria vaccination in SSA. However, some of these studies did not adopt a systematic review methodology [23, 24], and others were non-specific to SSA [25, 26]. Moreover, there was paucity of studies that systematically reviewed the specific evidence on malaria vaccine awareness and acceptability from the time of the licensing of the first malaria vaccine to date. Previous reviews had included studies conducted and published before the first malaria vaccine was approved [25, 26], which can reduce the applicability of the review findings to the present-day scenario.

This paper aims to systematically review the studies assessing the public awareness and acceptance of malaria vaccines from 2021 to March 2025. The findings of this study could assist various national governments in SSA in adequately planning and executing their malaria vaccination outreaches. Similarly, international bodies, including the Global Alliance for Vaccines and Immunisation (GAVI) and the World Health Organisation (WHO), can use this information to assist their malaria vaccination efforts in SSA. Finally, for researchers, the methodology used in his study can provide a framework for future studies into vaccine awareness and acceptance in SSA and other regions of the world.

Methods

Study Design

The study adopted a systematic review methodology. This design was chosen given the existence of diverse primary studies on public awareness and acceptance of malaria vaccines in SSA, and the need to bring the evidence together [27] to provide information for regulators and researchers alike to guide malaria control and eradication plans. The protocol for the review was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under registration number CRD420251011467 and was assigned the link https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420251011467. The systematic review was reported following the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) Statement of 2020 [28].

Information sources

This research relied on information available on nine research databases, including PubMed, EBSCOHOST (All Databases), African Index Medicus, Directory of Open Access Journals, Google Scholar, African Journals Online (AJoL), ScienceDirect, Cochrane Library, and Research4Life. The review relied on the published information, and the study authors were only contacted when the full text of studies could not be retrieved from the internet. Moreover, registries were not searched for this review.

Search strategy

The search strategy used a combination of subject headings, keywords, Boolean operators, and truncations (to widen the search) and was run by the guarantor. The search was limited to studies published in English between 1 st January 2021 and 31 st March 2025. The search strategy was run on different dates in April 2025 and yielded 2,828 records (see Additional file 1). To ensure the inclusion of all relevant studies, the references of the included studies and similar systematic, scoping, or rapid reviews were searched for potentially-eligible studies. Furthermore, the studies that cited the included studies were also searched on Google Scholar.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were designed using the PEO (Population, Exposure, and Outcome) framework. Studies where the population was sub-Saharan Africans, exposure was malaria vaccination, and the outcome(s) was/were awareness and/or acceptability were included. This review included only peer-reviewed full-text primary research conducted both in hospital and community environments. Both qualitative and quantitative studies were eligible for this review. Only those studies published in English language were considered to avoid translation difficulties. Furthermore, only those studies published between 2021 and 2025 were included and reported the collection of ethical clearance and/or participant consent.

Exclusion criteria

This review excluded research conducted on healthcare workers and policy actors. It also excluded pre-print articles, unpublished theses and dissertations, conference proceedings, review papers, and government reports. Government reports were excluded because of the risk of manipulation of official statistics for political gains [29]. Studies that did not report collecting ethical approval and seeking the participants’ consent were also excluded. The above exclusion was necessary to ensure that the review’s findings reflected the true perceptions of the participants in individual studies, because when people participate in a study against their will, they may not respond to questions truthfully. All grey literature were also excluded because of the difficulties in assessing their risk of bias.

Selection process

Zotero reference manager was used in all stages of the selection process, including screening for duplicates, screening of records, retrieval of full-text articles, and eligibility assessment. The selection/screening process was done following the PRISMA guidelines. Duplicates were identified and removed using Zotero. The title and abstract of each record were screened independently by three reviewers. The results of each reviewer’s screening were presented at a group discussion, and disagreements were resolved by voting. After screening, the corresponding author sought the full-text of all potentially-eligible reports, contacting authors whose reports were not publicly available. The reports that had their full texts retrieved were independently screened by three reviewers for eligibility in line with the earlier-mentioned criteria. A vote settled potential disagreements. The reason(s) for the exclusion of any study at this stage was/were noted.

Study records

Data management

The data from the included studies were managed using Zotero reference manager version 7.0.13. The library was exported as a Zotero RDF file after each stage in the screening process.

Data collection process

Two reviewers independently collected the data from the included papers into a standardised data collection form prepared with Microsoft Excel version 2016. Conflicts in data extraction were resolved by seeking the opinion of other reviewers. In the absence of a consensus, the final decisions were made by the corresponding author.

Data items

Data on the first author’s surname, year of publication, title of research, location the research was conducted, study design, sample characteristics (study population, sample size, age [mean/range/median], gender), method of data collection, awareness of malaria vaccine, acceptance of malaria vaccine, and factors affecting malaria vaccine awareness and acceptance were extracted from each study (see Additional file 2). Studies that lacked any of the aforementioned data items were excluded at this stage.

Outcome and prioritization

Main outcomes

Awareness of Malaria Vaccination: Awareness was considered as “having generalised or diffuse knowledge about the existence of something” [30].

Acceptability of Malaria Vaccination: Acceptability was viewed as “the extent to which people delivering or receiving a healthcare intervention consider it to be appropriate, based on anticipated or experienced cognitive and emotional responses to the intervention” [31].

Additional outcomes

Factors influencing malaria vaccine awareness and acceptance. Age, sex, educational status, economic status, and occupation were of interest.

Risk of bias in individual studies

The risk of bias in individual studies was assessed at the study level to determine the methodological quality using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Tools, depending on the study types [32] (see Additional file 3 for the detailed results of the critical appraisal). Two reviewers independently worked on the critical appraisal, and the study ratings were compared with the final decision made by the two reviewers. The results of the critical appraisal did not influence study eligibility. However, it was used to adequately tailor the discussion of findings to ensure that studies with high ratings were more buttressed.

Data synthesis

The data synthesis was done by the corresponding author, and audited by two other reviewers. Majority votes resolved disagreements. A narrative synthesis using themes in line with the method described by Pugh et al. [33] was used in this review. The four elements in the synthesis were: (1) developing a theory, (2) developing a preliminary synthesis, (3) exploring relationships within and between studies, and (4) assessing the robustness of the synthesis. However, no theory was developed for this review because of its exploratory nature and wide scope [33]. The thematic analysis was done manually using colour coding. Findings on similar issues were highlighted with a colour code in the table of study characteristics. From the coding, four key themes arose from the included papers, and they were: (1) awareness of malaria vaccine, (2) acceptance of malaria vaccine, (3) factors associated with malaria vaccine awareness, and (4) factors associated with malaria vaccine acceptance. The themes were subdivided into other sub-themes as deemed appropriate by the reviewers.

Results

Study selection

The search strategy yielded 2,828 records across all databases, and 8 records were identified by other methods. Most of the records were from ScienceDirect (n = 872), followed by EBSCOHOST (n = 825), Google Scholar (n = 500), Research4Life (n = 257), DOAJ (n = 223), AJOL (n = 100), Cochrane Library (n = 28), PubMed (n = 22), and AIM (n = 1). Although Google Scholar search initially resulted in 17,800 studies, only the first 500 were considered and exported to the reference manager in view of some previous recommendations of 200 [34, 35]. Moreover, the first 500 records from Google Scholar were sorted based on relevance, and beyond the 200th record, the vast majority of search results had no affiliation with the topic in focus. The summary of the selection/screening process is shown on the PRISMA flowchart in Fig. 1. 557 of these records were removed because they were duplicates according to Zotero. The remaining 2,271 records had their topic and abstracts screened, and 2,218 were excluded, leaving behind 53 reports. Fifty of these records had their full-texts retrieved, while 3 could not be retrieved. By screening the full texts of each report, 19 studies were excluded because they did not fulfil the inclusion criteria. The major reasons for the exclusion of studies were outcome not measured (n = 9), wrong population (n = 4), and review study design (n = 3). Other reasons were no ethical approval (n = 1), unpublished thesis (n = 1), and year of publication (n = 1). At the end of the article screening, 31 studies were considered eligible. Citation searching of the included research identified 8 more records, out of which 6 had their full texts retrieved, and only 2 met the inclusion criteria. In total, 33 studies were included in this systematic review.

Study characteristics

Most of the studies were published in 2024 (n = 10), followed by 2025 and 2023 (n = 8 each), 2022 (n = 4), and 2021 (n = 3). Most of the studies were from Nigeria (n = 11) and Ghana (n = 8), compared to Kenya and Ethiopia (n = 2 each), Mali, Burundi, Cameroon, Sierra Leone, Tanzania, Uganda, and the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) (n = 1 each). This imbalance in the country of the included study limited the generalisability of the review findings. Furthermore, three of the studies were transnational and conducted in Guinea and Sierra Leone (n = 1), Uganda, Ghana, and Nigeria (n = 1), Ghana, Kenya, and Malawi (n = 1). The vast majority of the studies featured a cross-sectional design (n = 25), and others were qualitative (n = 3), cohort (n = 2), and mixed method designs (n = 3).

The participants in the studies included caregivers (parents and guardians, n = 26), mothers (n = 3), adults (n = 3), and women (n = 1). In most studies, the participants were predominantly female, except for some that had predominance of males [23, 36, 37] and one study of equal sex distribution [38]. The sample size across all included studies was 18,975.

Awareness of malaria vaccines

The awareness of malaria vaccines was considered in 24 of the included studies (see Table 1). In a study conducted in three African countries, including Uganda, Ghana, and Nigeria, the awareness of malaria vaccine stood at 58.3%, Uganda had the highest awareness rate of 67.6% followed by Nigeria (55.7%) and Ghana (55.6%) [23]. Overall, the awareness of malaria vaccines in SSA ranged from 7.26% in a large study (n = 1612) from Bukavu, DRC [37] to 95.6% in a smaller study (n = 405) from the Kassena-Nankana districts of Ghana [39].

In East Africa, the public awareness ranged from 8.77% to 67.6%. It was highest in Uganda, 67.6% [23], followed by Ethiopia (from South West), 18% [40], Tanzania (Dar es Salaam), 14.7% [41], and Burundi (Muyinga, Gitega, Kiganda, Rwibaga, and Buja Nord health districts), 12.3% [42]. Another Ethiopian study (from North West) reported the least awareness of 8.77% among caregivers [43]. Moreover, in a cohort study from Kenya, Hoyt et al. [44] reported that prior to the interview, some caregivers had either not heard about the vaccine or had heard of it but were not confident in their understanding of the information.

In West Africa, the awareness ranged from 23.7% to 82% both in Nigeria [45, 46]. Two national studies on 347 and 399 caregivers reported awareness of 30.3% [36] and 72.9% [47], respectively, in Nigeria. On the state level, the 72.1% awareness in Enugu State [48] was followed by 56% in Kaduna State [49], 28.5% in Kaduna State [50], and the least awareness was the 23.7% in Niger State [46]. Furthermore, studies indicated a greater malaria vaccine awareness of 40.3% in Northern Nigeria [15], compared to the 26% in the South Western States of Ogun, Ondo, Oyo, and Osun [51]. Moreover, a study from Enugu State, Nigeria [38] indicated that all respondents were aware that malaria vaccines were being developed, but did not report on whether the participants were aware that a vaccine was already available.

Studies from Ghana indicated the highest malaria vaccine awareness among those in the Kassena-Nankana East and West districts, 95.6% [39], and this was followed by 81%, 57.1% and 44% awareness from Cape Coast metropolis [52], Kassena-Nankana East Municipality [53], and nationally [54], respectively. Moreover, 92.5% of caregivers were aware of the RTS, S malaria vaccines after their introduction in seven selected districts of Ghana [55]. Studies from the Foumbot District of Cameroon and Gulu, Uganda reported 26.68% [56] and 62% [57] awareness among caregivers.

Sources of information on malaria vaccines

The sources of information regarding malaria vaccines were evaluated in 12 studies (see Table 1). In many of the studies, healthcare workers/health facilities were the dominant sources of information [39, 41, 48, 50, 51, 54]. Other major sources of information in Nigeria were friends [46] and the internet [47]. Facebook was the major information channel in a study from Ethiopia [40], while radio and village health teams were the major information sources in a Ugandan study [57]. Furthermore, health care workers were not major sources of information on malaria vaccines in Ethiopia, with only 15.15% of the aware individuals reportedly getting their information from healthcare workers [40]. In addition, other major information sources indicated in the reviewed studies included traditional media (radio/television/posters/billboards/pamphlets), social media, and relatives [40, 41, 45–47, 50, 51, 56].

General acceptance of malaria vaccines

The acceptance of malaria vaccines was discussed in 28 studies (see Table 2). The acceptance of malaria vaccines among the study participants ranged from 10.3% [58] to 100% [38], both in Nigeria. Online national polls among caregivers from Nigeria suggested high malaria vaccine acceptance rates of 85.2% [47] and 78.4% [36] in two small studies on 399 and 347 participants, respectively. In another national study among 1,413 parents of under-5 children, only 10.3% were not against taking the malaria vaccine [58]. The general acceptance of malaria vaccine in Nigeria was highest among those from Enugu State, 100% [38], followed by the 94.0% in Kaduna [50]. Moreover, a study conducted among caregivers of under-five children in four South West States of Nigeria (Ogun, Ondo, Oyo, and Osun) indicated that 90.0% of the participants accepted the vaccine [51].

Studies on acceptance of malaria vaccine

The willingness to vaccinate oneself was 83.1% in Enugu State, Nigeria [48] and 46.53% in Bukavu, Democratic Republic of Congo [37]. Before the formal rollout of the malaria vaccine in Nigeria, two studies suggested that 85.2% [47] and 91.9% [15] of respondents would be willing to take the vaccine when introduced/available in the country.

Studies from other countries in West Africa also suggest high acceptance rates. In Gulu City, Uganda, 91.4% of caregivers accepted the malaria vaccine [57], while 95.8%, 90%, and 62.8% accepted the same in a national study in Ghana [55], North West, Ethiopia [43], and Bungoma County, Kenya [59], respectively. In Sierra Leone, 90% of the respondents were willing to accept traditional malaria vaccines and transmission-blocking vaccines (TBV) in Bo [60]. Explaining the mechanism of TBV action could lead to better vaccine acceptance among community members [60]. Furthermore, some qualitative studies also indicate wide acceptance of malaria vaccines in Ghana, Kenya, Malawi, and Mali [61–63].

Acceptance of malaria vaccine for children/wards

The acceptance of malaria vaccines for children was a subject in 12 studies. Overall, the acceptance was highest (97.3%) in Niger State, Nigeria [46] and lowest (32.3%) in Woliata Sodo town, Ethiopia [40]. In other locations, the willingness to vaccinate children was 92.9% in Enugu State, Nigeria [48], 92.8% in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania [41], 90.6% in Burundi [42], 82.16% in Foumbot, Cameroon [56], 81% and 76% in Sierra Leone and Guinea respectively [64], 67.9% in Kaduna State, Nigeria [49], 65.5% [65] and 59% both from Ghana [54], and 52.60% in Congo [37]. The acceptance of the vaccine for self and children was also 81.1% in Enugu State, Nigeria [48]. Moreover, a study from Anambra State, Nigeria, indicated that 76% of the studied caregivers were willing to consent to malaria vaccine trials on their children [45].

Recommendation of malaria vaccine for others

In some cases, the acceptance of the malaria vaccine led to the recommendation of the vaccine for others, and this was a subject in five studies. In Nigeria, almost all participants (99.6%) in Enugu State were willing to recommend a malaria vaccine for others [38], while this value was 71% in Kaduna State, Nigeria [49], and 88.6% in Kassena-Nankana East Municipality, Ghana [53]. However, the fear of side effects was a barrier against caregivers’ recommendations of the vaccine to others in Ghana [61]. Other than recommending the vaccine for others, in one study from Burundi, 95% of the caregivers were willing to lead mobilisation efforts to improve the vaccine uptake among others [42], while 91.4% in Kassena-Nankana East Municipality, Ghana, recommended universal coverage of the malaria vaccine in childhood [53]. In addition, 74.8% of those who knew about the vaccine in one study suggested that it should be added to the National Program on Immunisation [49].

Comparison of acceptance rate between malaria vaccine types

Four studies assessed the differences in the malaria vaccine acceptance rate, with three of the studies from Ghana. In Ghana, parents showed more acceptance of the R21/Matrix-M Malaria vaccine (47.3%) compared to the earlier RTS S/AS01 vaccine [54]. Another Ghanaian study also indicated that caregivers who accepted malaria vaccines preferred the R21/Matrix-M vaccine compared to the RTS, S/AS01 vaccine, while those who were hesitant preferred the RTS, S/AS01 compared to the R21/Matrix‐M vaccine [65]. Moreover, an in-depth qualitative interview conducted among primary caregivers by Darkwa et al. [56] in Ghana indicates a preference for the RTS, S/AS01 vaccine compared to other malaria prevention methods, and the major reason was the purported better efficacy of the vaccine, mobility, and lower side effects. Furthermore, Alagbe et al. [46] noted that about one-third of mothers in Niger State would prefer an oral vaccine.

Relationship between awareness and acceptance

Eleven studies assessed the relationship between malaria vaccine awareness and acceptance. In Uganda, good knowledge led to good acceptance among the respondents [57]. This was also the case in the studies from Burundi [42], Ghana [39], and Ethiopia [40]. Furthermore, in-depth interviews conducted in Kenya suggested that a lack of awareness reduces uptake of the malaria vaccine [44]. Another study from Tanzania also reported that lack of information was the major reason for the hesitancy [41]. Moreover, from Northern Nigeria, those aware of the vaccine were more than 6 times likely to accept the same [15]. In other studies, those with knowledge of the vaccine were three times more likely to accept it in Ethiopia [40], Guinea, and Sierra Leone [64]. Awareness of malaria vaccine also increased the likelihood of vaccinating oneself and children in Enugu State, Nigeria [48]. However, in another study, awareness had no relationship with the willingness to take the vaccine in Niger State, Nigeria [46]. In addition, caregivers in Anambra State, Nigeria, perceived that poor awareness could potentially impact vaccine acceptance [45].

Factors associated with malaria vaccine awareness

The sociodemographic factors, including age, sex, educational status, and occupational status, associated with malaria vaccine awareness were assessed in 4 of the included studies (see Table 3).

Age

In Ghana, young age was associated with greater malaria vaccine awareness [23]. Another study from Ghana also indicated that awareness was greater among those aged 28–37 years compared to those aged 38–47 and 18–27 years [53].

Sex

In Uganda and Nigeria, male respondents had a greater likelihood of malaria vaccine awareness compared to their female counterparts [23].

Educational status

Four studies assessed the role of educational status on malaria vaccine awareness. The awareness was significantly higher among those with graduate/professional degrees in three African countries of Nigeria, Ghana, and Uganda [23]. Furthermore, those with a tertiary level of education also had greater awareness in Ghana [53]. Moreover, in Nigeria, those with tertiary education were five times more likely to be aware of the malaria vaccine compared to those with primary education, as indicated by the crude odds ratio [51]. Surprisingly, in a study from Northern Nigeria, participants with secondary and tertiary levels of education as their highest formal education level had lower odds of malaria vaccine awareness compared to those with no formal education [15].

Occupation

In Northern Nigeria, caregivers who were employed were three times more likely to be aware of the malaria vaccine compared to those who were unemployed [15].

Factors affecting malaria vaccine acceptance

The sociodemographic factors influencing the rate of malaria vaccine acceptance, including age, sex, educational status, income status, and occupation, were assessed in 17 of the included studies.

Age

Age as a factor in malaria vaccine acceptance was considered in 10 studies. In three studies from Nigeria, younger maternal age was associated with a greater likelihood for malaria vaccine acceptance in the adjusted [15, 48] and unadjusted models [46]. On the contrary, in Kenya, caregivers of higher age groups (25 years and above) were more likely to vaccinate their under-five children compared to those aged 18–24 years [37]. Still in Kenya, caregivers had a greater odds to accept malaria vaccines for their children aged 24 months and below, both in the chi-square test and the logistic regression model [59]. On the contrary, a Ghanaian study indicated that having young children and a youngest child aged 1 − 3 years was an independent predictor of malaria vaccine hesitancy [54]. Caregivers’ age was not associated with malaria vaccine acceptance in 6 studies from Uganda [57], Kenya [59], Nigeria [50], Ghana [54], and Burundi [42]. Furthermore, there was no significant difference between age and reasons for malaria vaccine rejection in Nigeria [58].

Sex

Twelve (12) included studies assessed the role of sex in malaria vaccine acceptance. Sex (caregiver’s and child sex) was not associated with malaria vaccine acceptability in Nigeria [15, 46, 48, 50], Ethiopia [40], Uganda [57], Kenya [59], Ghana [54], Burundi [42], and DRC [37]. There was also no significant difference between sex and reasons for malaria vaccine rejection in a study from Nigeria [58]. Only one study among 600 participants from Foumbot, Cameroon, indicated that female caregivers were more likely to accept the vaccine compared to their male counterparts in both the crude and adjusted models [56].

Educational status

The role of educational status in malaria vaccine acceptance was determined in 13 studies. In studies from Nigeria [47, 58], Sierra Leone [64], Cameroon [56], and Ghana [39], higher education was associated with a greater likelihood for malaria vaccine acceptance. Furthermore, in some studies, higher education was associated with a greater likelihood of acceptance of the vaccine in the crude model, but the adjusted model suggested no significant association in Nigeria [15, 46] and Uganda [57]. In DRC, educational status was not associated with acceptance of vaccine for self, but caregivers with medium education were more likely to accept the vaccine for their under-five children compared to those with low education, while the relationship between high education compared to low education was not significant [37]. In addition, some studies found no association between educational status and malaria vaccine acceptance in Kenya [59], Ghana [54], Nigeria [50], and Burundi [42].

Income status

Ten (10) studies evaluated the role of income status on malaria vaccine acceptance. Most studies indicated that high wealth was associated with a greater likelihood of malaria vaccine acceptance in Nigeria [46, 47, 58] and DRC [37]. On the contrary, in Guinea, those belonging to high wealth households were less willing to vaccinate, compared to those in low wealth households [64]. Furthermore, a Nigerian study indicated that people belonging to the low socio-economic class had five times greater odds of accepting malaria vaccines for themselves and their babies compared to those in the high socio-economic category [48]. Income was not associated with acceptance/willingness to accept malaria vaccine in Kenya [59], Sierra Leone [64], Cameroon [56], and Ghana [54].

Occupation

The role of occupation in malaria vaccine acceptance was assessed in 10 studies. Employed individuals had twice the odds of accepting the vaccine compared to those who were unemployed in the unadjusted multinomial regression in Nigeria [15]. The odds for malaria vaccine acceptance were higher among farmers in Burundi [42], healthcare workers in Ghana [54, 65], and self-employed and civil servants in Uganda [57]. In the Ugandan study, the adjusted model showed no significant association [57]. In DRC, non-student/non-healthcare worker professions were less likely to accept the vaccine for themselves, but more likely to accept the vaccine for their under-five children compared to those who were healthcare workers by profession in the adjusted model [37]. Occupation was not associated with the malaria vaccine acceptance in some studies [39, 48, 56, 59].

Discussion

In SSA, at least 33 studies have reported on the public awareness and/or acceptance of malaria vaccines. Although most of these studies were from the endemic zones of West Africa (particularly Nigeria and Ghana), the present study provides valuable insights into the general outlook in SSA. The awareness of malaria vaccines among the public in SSA ranged from 7.26% in DRC to 95.6% in Ghana. This indicates a wide geographical difference in malaria vaccine awareness, suggesting that the level of awareness in one region is not applicable to other regions. The low rates in DRC likely resulted from continuous years of conflict, considering that the study was conducted in a post-conflict setting [37]. Previous studies have established that periods of conflict were associated with low awareness and acceptance of vaccines [67, 68], because governments and health workers may be too busy with the conflict/treating the wounded that they neglect vaccination awareness efforts. On the contrary, the last major conflict in Ghana (the Konkomba–Nanumba Conflict) occurred more than 20 years ago in 1994 [69].

The major source of information in the studies were largely healthcare workers. Healthcare workers were well-positioned to provide information on the malaria vaccine, considering their continuous contact with patients [70], and they were often better informed than other workers. A larger systematic review also explained the importance of healthcare workers in vaccine awareness [71]. Moreover, from some regions, the major information sources were traditional media (radio/television/posters/billboards/pamphlets), social media, and relatives in malaria vaccine awareness. Specifically, in an Ethiopian study [40], only 15.1% of the participants reported health workers as their source of malaria vaccine information. This may be due to the greater strain on healthcare workers in Ethiopia, which limits the quality of services offered by health professionals. To put into perspective, Ethiopia has a health professional density of 1.23 health professionals per 1,000 population [72], compared to the 1.83 health professionals per 1,000 population in Nigeria [73], both of which fall below the WHO recommendation of 4·45 per 1000 people [74, 75]. These discrepancies could also reflect the differences in the source of health information in various parts of SSA.

The acceptance of malaria vaccines was in the range of 10.3% to 100%, with the vast majority of the studies indicating above 50% acceptance rates. The vaccine acceptance for self and children was highest among the caregivers in Nigeria. This may be because of the endemic nature of the disease in the region [76], which was naturally expected to promote the desirability of preventive interventions. Specifically, Nigeria, the country with the highest malaria vaccine acceptance rate, is hyper-endemic for malaria [77, 78]. On the contrary, an Ethiopian study indicated that caregivers had a low rate of vaccine acceptance for their children. This was possibly because of the low awareness of the vaccine in the country, as evident in this review. In addition, malaria vaccines were yet to be formally rolled out in Ethiopia as of August 2025 [79].

In this review, Ghanaian and Nigerian caregivers were willing to recommend the vaccine for caregivers and national immunisation programs. As such, they can be actively engaged as malaria vaccine ambassadors and testifiers in their community to recruit other caregivers to vaccinate their children. As established in a previous research, community engagement is pivotal in the acceptance of any vaccine [80]. The participants that accepted the vaccine preferred the R21/Matrix-M compared to Mosquirix. This is very good for malaria vaccination efforts, considering that the former could confer greater protection at a lower manufacturing cost [81]. However, considering that most studies comparing the vaccine preference with respect to vaccine types were conducted in Ghana, the generalisability of the above should be avoided.

The included papers provided unanimous evidence that participants will accept malaria vaccines if aware of their existence. This finding was also stressed in previous reviews on COVID-19 and Polio vaccine [20, 82]. The reason could be because awareness is a prerequisite for making informed decisions about vaccine acceptance. Hence, greater awareness of malaria vaccine is crucial for the successful acceptance of the same.

In terms of the factors affecting the awareness of the malaria vaccines, the role of age was not clear in the papers, as two studies from Ghana had indicated that younger age and middle age were associated with greater awareness of the vaccine compared to older age. This may be because younger adults were more exposed to technologies, including the internet, which increases their health literacy [83] about vaccines. Health awareness groups in SSA should thus include younger adults in their malaria vaccination campaigns. Regarding sex, males had a higher awareness of malaria vaccine in only one study from Ghana, with more than 90% of the included studies reporting on this theme indicating no significant association. Hence, the findings in Ghana could be a research artefact, although the reason for this was unclear.

In three African nations (Uganda, Ghana, and Nigeria), higher education was associated with a greater likelihood of vaccine awareness, although one study in Nigeria reported the contrary. The higher awareness among those with more education may be because these individuals were more likely to understand health information than those without education. Furthermore, education also breeds social communication [84], which the uneducated cannot relate to. Moreover, employed caregivers also had higher awareness of the vaccine, which can also be attributed to their greater social communication. Employed people can gain access to health information in their workplace, while the unemployed cannot. These different demographic groups with high awareness need to be highly involved in malaria vaccine awareness outreach.

The studies had many notable revelations regarding the factors affecting vaccine acceptance. The age pattern in the acceptance was not clear because younger caregivers had greater malaria vaccine acceptance in Nigeria, but not in Kenya. This is probably due to the greater burden of the disease in Nigeria compared to Kenya [3], which increases the chances of young Nigerians ever experiencing more serious outcomes compared to their counterparts in Kenya, who may not have fully realised/experienced the dangers of the disease. Furthermore, in Kenya and Ghana, having young children was associated with a greater likelihood of malaria vaccine acceptance and hesitancy, respectively. The above is worrisome considering that the greater burden of malaria among Ghanaians compared to Kenyans was expected to increase the likelihood of vaccine desirability and acceptance for caregivers of younger children in Ghana. However, differences in past experiences with vaccination and the perception of increased danger of vaccines when administered to younger children in Ghana could have played a role. A recent study on routine immunisation in Ghana had indicated that children of younger age (12–17 months) were less likely to be fully immunised compared to their older (18–23 months) counterparts [85].

In more than 90% of the included studies, the caregivers’/children’s sex was not associated with the acceptance of the malaria vaccine. The only deviation was a study from Cameroon, where female caregivers were more likely to accept the vaccines than males [56]. The reason for the discrepancy was not clear. Still, it is possible that the lower viewing of televisions (the dominant malaria vaccine information source in the study) and involvement of Cameroonian men in child health/antenatal care visitations compared to their female counterparts [86] could make men miss potential health information regarding malaria vaccination that could trigger their acceptance. In perspective, the National Institute of Statistics of Cameroon estimated that 61.4% and 47.3% of Cameroonian men and women, respectively, participate in the labour market [87]. However, similar scenarios also occur in many countries in SSA [88, 89]. Hence, more detailed research is still required to fully grasp the reason for the gender difference in malaria vaccine acceptance in Cameroon.

The studies consistently indicated that higher education was associated with a greater likelihood of vaccine acceptance. The above may be because those with low education were more likely to believe vaccination myths, as seen with the COVID-19 vaccine [90]. The role of income status in malaria vaccine acceptance in SSA is also unclear, as the studies reported contradicting findings. However, most of them (particularly those from Nigeria and DRC) indicated a positive/direct relationship. The reverse was the case among participants in Guinea, possibly because rich people may consider themselves less vulnerable to malaria since they can afford the cost of treatment and prevention compared to poor people [64]. On the contrary, the hyper-endemicity of the disease in Nigeria could make everyone, including the rich, perceive vulnerability. Finally, among other occupations, farmers, healthcare workers, and self-employed/civil servants were more likely to accept the vaccines in Burundi, Ghana, and Uganda. The reasons stated for the greater malaria vaccine awareness among the employed, as reported earlier in this study, could also explain this occurrence.

This study had numerous strengths. First, the review used only studies published since 2021 to ensure the findings indicated current trends. Secondly, the study used multiple databases and citation searching to ensure wider article coverage. Furthermore, more than one reviewer participated in each stage of the review to reduce the author’s bias. The study did not also consider uptake rates as acceptance because people can take vaccines they don’t accept and vice versa. Despite these strengths, the study was limited by the lack of access to larger scientific databases like Scopus and Web of Science. However, this was compensated for by searching other databases and citation searching. Moreover, the inclusion of studies whose data were collected before the first malaria vaccine licensure in 2021 could make the findings not exclusive to the post-vaccine introduction period. The rarity of nationally-representative surveys was also a limitation, as most data came from convenience or purposive sampling, and it may be inappropriate to consider state/district trends as national trends. The wide heterogeneity across populations and measures precluded meta-analysis, and as such, the pooled awareness and acceptance of malaria vaccines in SSA were not determined. Finally, more than 50% of the included studies were from Nigeria and Ghana, with none from North Africa, reducing the generalisability of the review findings to the entire African continent. Hence, researchers from African regions where data paucity persists are encouraged to focus on research on malaria vaccine awareness and acceptance in their region.

Conclusion

In conclusion, there were large geographical and population disparities in the awareness and acceptance of malaria vaccines among the public in SSA. Awareness and acceptance rates were as high as 95.6% and 100% respectively in some areas, while others had levels below 50%. Moreover, age, higher educational status and being employed were associated with vaccine awareness. For the acceptance, no clear relationship with age, sex and income status was found, but acceptance was more among those with higher education and certain occupation types. In conclusion, this study provides valuable insights into the awareness and acceptance of malaria vaccines in SSA, and the onus now lies on governments, researchers, and health workers to make good use of the presented findings. Governments can help by increasing their funding and support for various malaria vaccination campaigns. Researchers can fill necessary research gaps to identify malaria vaccine awareness and acceptance patterns unique to their domains. Moreover, health workers across SSA should be trained on malaria vaccination, considering they were the dominant information source in this review.
