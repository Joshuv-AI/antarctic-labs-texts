Doxorubicin exposure leads to cardiac fibroblast dysregulation and worsens fibrotic remodeling in the pathological heart☆
Authors: Sukriti Bagchi, Amy Cai, Alina S. Bilal, Javier Gamez-Luque, Manuel Rosa-Garrido, Bethany Patton, Mrinalini Kala, Christopher C. Glembotski, Erik A. Blackwood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Advances in cancer treatments have enabled long-term patient survival at increasing rates, however these therapies often have chronic adverse side effects of which cardiovascular disease is a major cause for concern. Specifically, patients treated with the anthracycline, doxorubicin (DOX), face significantly higher rates of delayed-onset heart failure (HF), clinically referred to as chronic DOX-induced cardiotoxicity (DIC), typically years or even decades following treatment despite showing no observable signs of acute cardiotoxicity. In patients, even low-dose DOX is known to induce cardiac fibrosis which increases the risk for HF, thus, implicating the cardiac fibroblast (CFB) as having a prominent role in chronic DIC. To determine possible roles for CFB in DIC we developed in vitro and in vivo models that tested the immediate and delayed effects of DOX administered at concentrations that mimic those used clinically. While, at these levels, DOX had very little effect on CFB viability in vitro, it did increase markers of CFB activation and dramatically altered the CFB transcriptome. These effects persisted even when CFB were subsequently treated with the canonical differentiation stimulus, transforming growth factor-β (TGFβ), consistent with a cellular memory of prior DOX exposure. In our in vivo model in mice, clinically relevant doses of DOX had essentially no effect on cardiac structure and function, however, prior exposure to DOX exacerbated cardiac dysfunction and structural remodeling in response to a subsequent stressor in the form of chronic angiotensin II/phenylephrine (ANGII/PE) infusion mimicking the effects of a common ailment (i.e. hypertension) DIC patients often face later in life. At the molecular level, DOX increased markers of CFB activation and cardiac fibrosis in mouse hearts in response to ANGII/PE that was coordinate with the severity of cardiac dysfunction. In summary, our mouse model mimicked the delayed deleterious effects of DOX on cardiac structure and function, suggesting that despite exhibiting no effect on CFB viability, DOX promotes maladaptive cardiac remodeling through an exaggerated response to a delayed stimulus that ultimately results in an exacerbated progression into HF.

Introduction

Doxorubicin (DOX) is a highly effective anti-cancer therapy but poses significant risk for cardiotoxicity, as high cumulative exposures can cause heart failure (HF) [1], [2], [3], [4]. In efforts to mitigate this, cancer treatment regimens now limit lifetime DOX exposure to no more than 450 mg/m2 and cardioprotective strategies such as co-administration of the iron chelator dexrazoxane have been developed to prevent damage to the heart during administration [5], [6], [7]. Despite this, patients can still develop delayed-onset HF (i.e. chronic DOX-induced cardiotoxicity, or chronic DIC) in the years following treatment which has proven intractable with standard HF therapies and is associated with a poor prognosis [8], [9], [10]. Further, clinical investigations have demonstrated a high prevalence of subclinical dysfunction, namely diastolic dysfunction, in patients with chronic DIC even when treated with very low cumulative exposure of DOX [11], [12], [13].

Preclinical studies have been undertaken in past decades to understand the mechanistic basis of cardiotoxicity in chronic DIC, but these have mainly focused on the cardiomyocyte as the primary effector cell type of the heart. Studies also commonly utilize high doses to rapidly elicit overt cardiotoxicity both in vitro and in vivo, which identified potential mechanisms underlying toxicity such as mitochondrial dysfunction, ROS generation, and DNA damage [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]. However, such model systems are not necessarily clinically relevant as they do not adequately mimic the concentrations of DOX exposure and time post-treatment experienced by patients; thus, the exact mechanisms contributing to chronic DIC are still unclear. As an example, many in vitro studies utilize a supraphysiological treatment concentration of 1 μM, which exceeds steady state DOX levels in tissues and plasma and may even surpass peak DOX levels in the heart [19], [20], [21], [22]. Pharmacologic modeling estimated peak DOX levels in the cardiac interstitium following a standard infusion in the clinical setting to be <0.1μM [21]. Moreover, since DOX is rapidly eliminated in the initial hours following administration, then more slowly eliminated over the timescale of days, cardiotoxicity may continue progressing at even lower physiological concentrations (0.01–0.05 μM). The mechanisms of cellular toxicity that occur at a lower concentration are of particular interest for chronic DIC which can occur even in patients with low cumulative exposures (<120 mg/m2) [8], [12].

Among studies of chronic DIC in patients and animal models, cardiac fibrosis has been identified as a consequence of DOX exposure that may contribute to disease progression [23], [24], [25], [26], [27]. Cardiac fibroblasts (CFB) are the main producers of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins that play an integral role in maintaining the structural scaffolding of the heart, as well as coordinating remodeling during stress or injury. While CFB promote wound-healing in adaptive contexts, they can also promote pathological fibrotic remodeling when their activity is unconstrained or dysregulated [28], [29]. In the case of chronic DIC, there is currently a paucity of mechanistic studies specifically interrogating the effects of DOX on CFB and their overall contribution to the structural remodeling observed in chronic DIC.

The goal of this study was to investigate the effects of clinically relevant concentrations of DOX on CFB in vitro and using a novel mouse model of chronic DIC that appropriately mimics the delayed onset effects of DIC observed in patients. To accomplish this, treatment paradigms were established where primary CFB or mice were challenged either (1) acutely with DOX or (2) chronically with a secondary stressor after an initial period of DOX exposure where there were no observable signs of toxicity. A broad range of CFB characteristics were comprehensively assessed to determine both the effects of DOX on CFB and their contribution to structural remodeling of the heart during chronic DIC progression.

Methods

Laboratory animals

The research reported in this article has been reviewed and approved by the University of Arizona Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and conforms to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the National Research Council. All animals were fed ad libitum for all experimental purposes and kept on a traditional 12-h light/dark cycle. For animal studies, wild-type (WT) 8–12-week old male C57B/6 J mice were used (The Jackson Laboratory; Bar Harbor, ME). Because of the high variability in animal models of DOX-induced cardiotoxicity (DIC), doses were selected based on expected clinical outcome (minimal morbidity and mortality after treatment completion). In the literature, this is thought to be approximately 10 mg/kg DOX cumulatively in mice [30]. Thus, to develop a low-dose DOX treatment paradigm, mice were randomized into three groups (n = 6–7) as follows: normal saline (vehicle), 1.5 mg/kg DOX, or 3 mg/kg DOX (MedChemExpress, HY-15142) administered intraperitoneally (IP) once per week for 4 weeks resulting in a cumulative dose of 6 mg/kg or 12 mg/kg. 4 weeks after the last DOX administration at the study endpoint, echocardiographic analysis was performed, as well as gravimetric analysis and preservation of cardiac tissue.

To model chronic DIC in the context of cardiac pathology, mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 9–10): with or without DOX administration, then subsequent challenge with or without angiotensin II plus phenylephrine (ANGII/PE). Mice were treated either with vehicle or 1.5 mg/kg DOX administered IP once per week for 4 weeks (=6 mg/kg cumulative dose). 4 weeks after the last vehicle or DOX administration, mini-osmotic pumps (Alzet, model 2004) were implanted containing either saline or ANGII/PE to allow continuous infusion for 28 days for either. ANGII (Calbiochem cat# 05-23-0101) was provided at a rate of 1.5 μg/g/day and PE (Sigma cat#P6126) were provided at a rate of 50 μg/g/day [31]. To insert minipumps, mice were anesthetized with 2–3% isoflurane and minipumps were implanted subcutaneously along the right flank. For the DOX dose response experiment, numbers of animals used was based on a predictive power analysis to achieve 5% error and 80% power based on the typical effect size seen in mouse models of DIC. For chronic DIC plus ANGII/PE, the numbers of animals used were determined practically, based on previous experiments designed to determine approximate magnitude of changes in the measured parameters after ANGII/PE. For all experimentation, mice were randomized in a 1:1 ratio and assigned a coded number independent of the blinded investigator.

Echocardiography

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed using an ultrasound imaging system (Vevo 3100 System, Fujifilm VisualSonics, Toronto, Ontario, Canada) as described [32]. Parameters were assessed for ventricular structure (i.e. chamber volume, wall thicknesses), systolic function (ejection fraction), diastolic function (Pulsed-wave, color, and tissue Doppler imaging at the level of the mitral annulus, E/e’), and LV remodeling (LV inner diameter during diastole, LV mass, and their ratio).

Hydroxyproline assay

Approximately 10 mg of snap frozen LV tissue was homogenized in 100 μL of ultrapure H2O and hydrolyzed with equal volume 12 N HCl at 95 °C overnight and debris was removed by centrifuging at 10,000 ×g for 3 min. Hydroxyproline content was assessed according to kit instructions (Sigma, cat#MAK569). In brief, hydrolyzed samples were desiccated overnight at 60 °C in a dry incubator. Hydroxyproline was oxidized by Chloramine T and a reaction was performed with 4-(dimethylamino) benzaldehyde (DMAB) which results in a colorimetric product proportional to the amount of hydroxyproline in the tissue sample. Hydroxyproline content was determined by measuring absorption at 560 nm and using a standard curve with known amounts to calculate tissue hydroxyproline.

Cultured neonatal rat cardiac fibroblasts (CFB) and experimental design

Neonatal rat CFB were isolated from 1 to 3 day-old Sprague-Dawley rats via enzymatic digestion of dissected ventricles using a neonatal cardiac myocyte isolation system (Worthington Biochemical Corp, cat# LK003300). CFB were then purified from myocytes using a 2-layer Percoll density gradient. Briefly, isolated cells were counted and centrifuged at 250 ×g for 5 min in an Eppendorf 5810R using a swinging bucket rotor. Forty to sixty million cells were then resuspended in 2 mL of 1 × ADS buffer containing phenol red (116 mM NaCl, 18 mM HEPES, 845 μM NaHPO4, 5.55 mM Glucose, 5.37 mM KCl, 831 μM MgSO4, 0.002% Phenol Red, pH 7.35 ± 0.5). Stock Percoll was prepared by combining 9 parts of Percoll (Cytiva, cat# 17–0891-02) with 1 part of clear 10 × ADS (without phenol red). The stock Percoll was used to make the Percoll for the clear top (density = 1.059 g/mL; 1 part Percoll stock added to 1.2 parts clear 1 × ADS) and red bottom (density = 1.082 g/mL; 1 part Percoll stock added to 0.54 parts red 1 × ADS) layers. The gradient consisted of 4 mL top Percoll pipetted first in a 15 mL conical tube, then layering 3 mL of bottom Percoll gently underneath. Counted, isolated cells (in 2 mL red 1 × ADS buffer) were layered on the top then centrifuged at 1500 ×g for 30 min with no deceleration brake at room temperature. Isolated CFB concentrated in the layer located between the middle clear ADS layer and the upper red ADS layer. CFB were carefully collected and washed twice with 50 mL of 1 × ADS, then resuspended in maintenance medium and seeded onto 15cm2 dishes. Maintenance medium consisted of Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM):F12 (Gibco, cat# 11330-032), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and antibiotics (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin, PS). One day following isolation, the culture medium was replaced with fresh 10% media and cells were monitored over the following days for passaging at 90% confluency. For all experiments, P1-P2 CFB were plated at an optimal density previously determined for each assay. Treatment with DOX was performed the following day. DOX treatment was done in DMEM:F12 with 2% FBS and PS for 24 h, and TGFβ treatment (10 ng/mL) was done in DMEM:F12 with 1% FBS and PS for up to 96 h.

MTT assay

CFB were plated in a 96-well plate at a density of 10,000 cells per well, then subjected to 24 h DOX treatment (10 nM-25 μM). Viability was determined using MTT. In brief, MTT (Roche, cat# 11465007001) was added into the culture medium, then incubated at 37 °C for 90 min. Media was aspirated and DMSO was added to solubilize incorporated MTT reagent. After total solubilization of purple crystals, absorbance was measured at 570 nm.

Live/dead imaging

CFB were plated in 6-well plates at a density of 150,000 cells per well in biological triplicate, then treated with DOX (10 nM-1 μM) for 24 h. For live/dead cell labelling, 1 mL of media was removed from the 2 mL working volume. 1 μL each of 1 mg/mL Calcein-AM in DMSO (Invitrogen, cat# 65–0853-39) and 1 mg/mL propidium iodide (PI; Invitrogen – cat # P3566) were spiked into the 1 mL of media remaining in each well. CFB were promptly imaged on an Echo Revolve M256 microscope using the 20× objective. Fluorescent images were acquired from Texas red (TXR), and GFP channels from four randomly selected fields in each well (total = 12 images per treatment condition). ImageJ was used to quantify the number of cells in each single-channel image. Cell bodies of Calcein-AM+ live cells or rounded PI+ cells were distinguished by enhancing the brightness and contrast of the image and using a signal threshold to isolate individual cells. ImageJ was then used to count the number of cell bodies on thresholded images. At least 500 cells were quantified in each treatment condition, and data were presented as % dead cells (PI+) of total cells (sum of Calcein-AM+ and PI+).

Flow cytometry

4 Million CFB were plated into 10cm2 dishes and treated with DOX (10 nM-1 μM). After 24 h, cells were harvested with trypsin and washed twice with FACS buffer (0.1% FBS in PBS). CFB were then counted, and after centrifugation, were resuspended in Annexin-V binding buffer (BioLegend, cat# 422201) at a concentration of 1 million cells/mL for subsequent staining. For each 100,000 cells, 10uL Annexin-V (BioLegend, cat# 640906) and 5uL PI (Invitrogen, cat # P3566) was added and incubated for 15 min at room temperature, then analyzed within 1 h. Data acquisition was obtained using the BD FACS Canto II (BD Biosciences) and data analysis was performed on FlowJo (version 10).

BrdU incorporation

BrdU incorporation corresponds to DNA replication and was used as a readout for CFB proliferation. Cells were seeded at a density of 20,000 cells per well in a 96-well plate. BrdU was allowed to incorporate for a 24 h period after which cells were fixed and stained according to manufacturer's instructions (Qiagen, cat# QIA58). Briefly, cells were fixed then dried for subsequent antibody-based detection of BrdU incorporated into cells, and signal amplification with peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody. Addition of 3′,5,5′-Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate resulted in the formation of a blue solution corresponding to primary and secondary antibody-bound BrdU. The reaction was allowed to proceed for 30 min, after which a stop solution was added and absorbance at 450 nm was measured.

Immunocytofluorescence

CFB were plated for immunocytofluorescence (ICF) at 100,000 cells/well which was previously determined as optimal plating densities on glass cover slips pre-coated with fibronectin (Sigma cat# F2006). At the experimental endpoint, CFB were subjected to fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde then stained with the activated fibroblast marker, alpha smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) at a dilution of 1:1000 (Abcam, cat# ab7817) and DAPI to mark nuclei. For each condition, at least 6 images were collected at 10× magnification for a minimum of 1000 cells/condition using a Leica Thunder microscope. myoFB were scored via the following stringent criteria: (1) α-SMA positivity; (2) presence of α-SMA stress fibers across the cell body; and (3) characteristic cellular hypertrophy. Percent myoFB activation was quantified based on the number of myoFB+ cells normalized to DAPI.

Immunoblotting

CFB cultures were lysed with VC lysis buffer made from 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), 1% Triton X-100, protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche Diagnostics), and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Roche Diagnostics). Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 15,000 ×g for 1 min, and the protein concentration was determined using DC protein assay (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). Samples comprising 20 μg of protein were mixed with Laemmli sample buffer with β-ME and SDS to aid protein reduction, then boiled for 5 min at 95 °C. Protein samples were then subjected to SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) on a 12% gel with 18-wells, followed by transfer onto PVDF membranes for immunoblotting. The following antibodies were used in subsequent experiments: vinculin (1:20,000, sc-73,614), p-SMAD2/3 (1:500, CST—8828S), t-SMAD2/3 (1:2000, CST—8685S), p-ERK1/2 (1:2000, CST—9101S), t-ERK1/2 (1:2000, CST—9102S).

Seahorse assay for cellular bioenergetics

CFB were plated on a CellTak precoated 96-well seahorse plate from Agilent. To adsorb wells with CellTak (Corning, cat# 354240), the reagent was prepared in a 0.1 M Sodium Bicarbonate solution (pH = 8). 20 μL of CellTak containing 0.39 μg was added to each well and incubated at room temperature for 20 min to adsorb, then washed 3× with sterile molecular grade H2O and allowed to dry completely. 10–20,000 CFB were plated into each well, omitting each of the 4 corner wells of the 96-well plate for blank measurement. Following DOX or TGFβ treatment, cells were washed three times with seahorse base media (Agilent, cat# 103575–100) prepared according to the table below for the appropriate stress test, then kept in a final volume of 175 μL of base media in a non-CO2 incubator for 1 h to equilibrate pH. During this time, 25 μL of each mitochondrial stressor compound or glycolysis compound were added to quadrants A, B, and C of a hydrated flux pack prepared the day prior with calibrant solution at 8×, 9×, or 10× the final concentration respectively. Base media components and final concentrations of each stressor compound for mitochondrial and glycolysis stress tests are presented in Table S1.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from CFB or mouse hearts as previously described using a Quick-RNA MiniPrep kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA cat# R1055) or RNeasy Mini kit (Qiagen, cat# 71106), respectively. cDNA synthesis was performed using SuperScript III First-Strand Synthesis System (Thermo Fisher, cat# 18080051). qRT-PCR was performed using Maxima SYBR Green/ROX qPCR Master Mix in a StepOnePlus RT-PCR System (Thermo Fisher, cat# K0223). Primer sequences for targeted transcripts are listed in Table S2.

RNA-sequencing, differential gene expression, and gProfiler categorical analysis

RNA was isolated from CFB using a Quick-RNA MiniPrep kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA cat# R1055) for sequencing. RNA quality and integrity was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis, Nanodrop spectrophotometry, and Agilent 2100 analysis. Samples were processed for mRNA sequencing by Novogene Corporation, consisting of rRNA depletion, poly(A) enrichment, and cDNA library preparation via reverse transcription. Libraries were sequenced using an Illumina platform. To identify differentially expressed genes, paired-end 150 bp reads were demultiplexed and aligned to the reference genome (hg38) using STAR. Gene-level counts were then obtained and imported into DESeq2 for differential expression analysis. The DESeq() function was run with default parameters to compute p-values and Benjamini–Hochberg–adjusted p-values. Genes with an adjusted p-value <0.05 and an absolute fold change ≥1.5 were used as cutoffs for significantly differentially expressed genes. Functional enrichment analyses were performed using Gene Ontology categories—Biological Process (GO:BP) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases. Data visualization was carried out in R using the clusterProfiler package.

Cell proliferation with longitudinal imaging

To assess proliferation by longitudinal imaging of cultured cells, CFB were plated in 6-well plates at a density of 150,000 cells per well in triplicate, then placed on an incubator-compatible Olympus Microscope for longitudinal imaging every 4H using fixed-point imaging and analysis of CFB confluence and cell number using the corresponding Olympus software. Cell proliferation was monitoring throughout the duration of the experiment, composed of 24 h DOX treatment (10 nM) followed by culturing in fresh culture medium (DMEM:F12, 1% FBS and PS) without DOX.

Gel contraction assay

CFB were pre-treated with DOX or vehicle for 24 h at P1 in 15cm2 dishes. CFB were dissociated with trypsin and counted, then resuspended at a concentration of 312,000 cells/mL in serum-free DMEM:F12 with PS. 800 μL of CFB were aliquoted into a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube to which 240 μL of Collagen (R&D Systems, cat# 3440–100-01) was added and mixed thoroughly, carefully avoiding bubbles. NaOH (12 μL) was rapidly added and mixed, then 500 μL of the total mixture was plated into a well of a 24-well plate. TGFβ (10 ng/mL final concentration) was promptly added and mixed into the remaining volume before the second 500 μL was plated into another well. This process was repeated for each vehicle or DOX pretreatment condition. Gels were allowed to solidify for 20 min at room temperature. After polymerization, once gels were solid, 1 mL of DMEM:F12 with 1% FBS and PS was added (±TGFβ). Finally, gels were dissociated from the edge of the well by inserting an autoclaved 200 μL tip between the gel and the edge of the well and dragging the tip around the circumference of the well. CFB in collagen disks were cultured and monitored for contraction using a brightfield imager up to 96H, at which point maximum contraction was achieved.

Migration assay

CFB were pre-treated with DOX or vehicle for 24 h at P1 in 15cm2 dishes. Following dissociation with trypsin, P2 CFB were plated in a dense monolayer (1 million cells per well in a 6-well plate) in maintenance medium and allowed to recover overnight. The following day, media was aspirated and a p200 pipette tip was used to create a vertical scratch down the center of each well. Cells were washed once with PBS to remove any loosely adherent clumps, then DMEM/F12, with 1% FBS and PS was replaced, and cells were monitored with longitudinal fixed-point imaging every 15-min on an incubator-compatible Olympus Microscope. Three fields per well were chosen that contained the scratched region for longitudinal imaging to determine % closure of scratched area over time, indicating CFB migration.

Statistics

Cell culture experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates for each condition. For experiments involving phenotypic characterization (i.e. ICF and gel contraction), results were confirmed across at least three independent replicates. Statistical comparisons in DOX dose-response experiments were performed using a one-way ANOVA with Dunnett's multiple comparisons test post-hoc. Two-group comparisons were performed using Student's unpaired t-test. Multi-group comparisons with two variables were performed using a two-way ANOVA with Tukey's multiple comparisons test post-hoc, or Fisher's post-hoc analysis when appropriate. Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 7.0e. mRNA and protein expression levels were normalized to either β-Actin transcript and vinculin protein respectively. In some cases, when appropriate, a linear transformation of data was performed to set the result of control group (usually vehicle) to 1, by dividing each group with the average obtained for their control group. Data are represented as mean with all error bars indicating ± SEM. *, # signifies statistical significance with respective p-values detailed in the corresponding figure legend for data compared to its respective control group also denoted in the corresponding figure legend.

Results

CFB retain viability after low-dose DOX treatment

To determine the potentially maladaptive effects of DOX on the cellular viability, cultured CFB were subjected to various doses of acute DOX for 24 h (24 h) and viability was determined using MTT assays. DOX concentrations varied from low but clinically relevant (10-100 nM), to supraphysiologic and not clinically relevant (1-25 μM). As shown in Fig. 1A, CFB viability was not affected at clinically relevant concentrations of DOX (e.g. 10-100 nM), though it significantly decreased at higher concentrations (≥500 nM DOX). Surprisingly, even at the highest DOX concentration examined, there was only a 20–25% reduction in viability implying CFB are relatively resilient to DOX. To further assess potentially masked effects on viability, CFB were subjected to a live/dead assay using the fluorescent labels calcein-AM and propidium iodide, which showed a similar response to DOX as the MTT (Fig. 1B; Fig. S1A). Finally, these results were corroborated using flow cytometry which confirmed that the percent of cell death was low at all DOX doses (Fig. 1C; Fig. S1BD). These results demonstrate that clinically relevant low concentrations of DOX do not overtly affect CFB viability.

CFB are minimally affected by acute low-dose DOX treatment

Focusing further on the effects of 10 nM DOX as that potentially encompasses a broader patient population exposed to even mild concentrations of DOX, a comprehensive panel of assays was used to characterize the effects of acute DOX on various CFB phenotypes, including proliferation, morphology, differentiation signaling, and bioenergetics (Fig. 2A). To determine the effects of acute DOX on CFB proliferation, BrdU incorporation was assessed throughout the treatment paradigm which showed that 10 nM DOX did not affect proliferation (Fig. 2B). Next, CFB morphology was analyzed as a readout for CFB activation. When activated, CFB differentiate into myofibroblasts (myoFB) which are the pathologic cell type responsible for secreting massive quantities of ECM proteins and have a distinct morphology that includes increased (1) expression of α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), (2) cellular hypertrophy, and (3) presence of actin forming stress fibers [33], [34]. Thus, immunocytofluorescence (ICF) staining for α-SMA was performed to quantify the effects of DOX on CFB activation. Interestingly, 10 nM DOX was sufficient to significantly increase CFB activation (Fig. 2C, D), although only to a modest extent when compared to the expected increase in CFB activation with a canonical differentiation stimulus (e.g. transforming growth factor-β; TGFβ). Still, in light of this, canonical signaling pathways relevant for CFB activation were assessed in the form of the SMAD and ERK signal cascades. As shown in Fig. 2E-G, 10 nM DOX did not have a robust effect on these signaling pathways, although there was a slight increase in ERK signaling, as evidenced by increased p-ERK1/2 levels. Lastly, DOX is known to impair bioenergetics in multiple cell types of the heart and CFB differentiation into myoFB is tightly linked to an altered metabolic profile [35], [36]. Accordingly, the effect of DOX on CFB bioenergetics was examined using a Seahorse assay to measure mitochondrial respiration and glycolysis in response to stressors of either oxidative phosphorylation or glycolysis, respectively. While 10 nM DOX did not affect CFB mitochondrial respiration (Fig. 2H, I) or basal glycolysis (Fig. 2J, K), maximum glycolysis was significantly reduced by DOX. While additional studies are required, this may signify that DOX promotes a state of metabolic inflexibility as it relates to using glucose as a primary substrate for CFB metabolism. Overall, acute low-dose DOX minimally effects the functional characteristics of CFB.

Acute low-dose DOX alters the CFB transcriptome

Despite only marginally affecting phenotypes associated with CFB function, DOX may be genetically modifying CFB in a more pronounced manner observable at the transcript level. Accordingly, the expression of a panel of fibrosis-related genes (e.g. Postn, Fn1, Col1a1, and Col3a1) was examined by RT-qPCR after acute treatment of 10 nM DOX. Among these genes, only Postn showed a significant increase (1.4-fold change) with no significant change in Fn1, Col1a1, or Col3a1 expression (Fig. 3A). To more broadly assess the effects of DOX on the CFB transcriptome, bulk mRNA-sequencing was performed on CFB following acute DOX treatment. As shown in the heatmap in Fig. 3B, acute DOX significantly altered the CFB transcriptome comprised of 708 differentially expressed genes (DEGs; p < 0.05, FDR < 0.05, log2 FC > 0.5) the majority of which were uniquely downregulated. DEGs were further analyzed for categorical enrichment of GO Biological Processes (Fig. 3C) or KEGG Pathway terms (Fig. 3D). Identified terms broadly represented DNA damage responses, and cell cycle or cell division processes in the nucleus, supporting the notion that acute treatment of 10 nM DOX may induce cell cycle dysfunction in CFB. Interestingly, despite identifying several fibrosis-related genes as being differentially expressed with DOX (e.g. Sertad4), terms related to fibrosis or extracellular matrix were not identified as being significantly enriched in either GO or KEGG categories which aligns with our data demonstrating a minimal effect on CFB phenotypes in response to acute DOX (Fig. 2). First, the effects of DOX pretreatment on CFB proliferation were assessed via BrdU incorporation. Following this chronic treatment paradigm, prior DOX exposure had a slight but significant effect in decreasing CFB proliferation independent of TGFβ treatment (Fig. 4B), an effect which was not seen in the acute treatment paradigm (Fig. 2B). These mild effects on CFB proliferation were further corroborated by assessing overall cell number after extended time in culture following prior DOX exposure (Fig. 4C). However, CFB migration, as determined via a scratch assay, was not altered by prior DOX exposure indicating that DOX does not impair CFB migration towards sites of injury (Fig. S2A). Next, to determine whether prior exposure to DOX affects CFB activation in response to TGFβ, ICF for α-SMA was used to visualize CFB morphology and quantify percent differentiation into myoFB. Here, while TGFβ modestly increased differentiation of CFB into myoFB as expected, this effect was exacerbated with prior DOX exposure alone, and compounded with subsequent TGFβ treatment (Fig. 4D, E). As a defining characteristic of myoFB is their contractile ability, a gel contraction assay was performed on CFB subjected to the chronic treatment paradigm. Coordinate with the effects on CFB activation assessed by ICF for α-SMA, prior DOX exposure exacerbated percent contraction compared to vehicle with subsequent TGFβ treatment (Fig. 4F, G). This finding was replicated across several independent experiments, though quantified changes in area for DOX alone did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 4G).

At the molecular level, while TGFβ had the expected effect of increasing both SMAD and ERK signaling, prior exposure to DOX increased CFB responsiveness to TGFβ as evidenced by augmented levels of phosphorylated SMAD and ERK (Fig. 4H, I). While these effects were most pronounced after 2 h, a timepoint chosen to focus on short-term signaling during CFB activation, increased ERK signaling with DOX persisted after 48 h of TGFβ treatment (Fig. S2BD). Overall, these results are consistent with prior DOX exposure promoting CFB hyperactivation in response to TGFβ and may also reveal altered terminal differentiation of CFB due to persistent ERK signaling.

Lastly, the effects of DOX pretreatment on mitochondrial respiration and glycolysis were also assessed in CFB subjected to the chronic treatment paradigm. Here, while TGFβ increased both mitochondrial respiration and glycolysis as expected, prior exposure to DOX attenuated this response and was most noticeable when examining maximal respiratory and glycolytic capacity (Fig. S2EH). This was particularly interesting given that acute DOX treatment had no effect on mitochondrial respiration (Fig. 2H, I), further implicating a delayed effect of prior DOX exposure altering CFB responsiveness to TGFβ.

While TGFβ treatment alone had the most dramatic effect on increasing Postn expression, intriguingly, this was exacerbated with prior exposure to DOX (Fig. 5A). To further elucidate how prior exposure to DOX in the chronic treatment paradigm affects the CFB transcriptome, bulk mRNA-sequencing was performed. As shown in Fig. 5E, hierarchical clustering of DEGs revealed that the effects of prior DOX exposure on CFB gene expression persisted throughout the chronic treatment paradigm in a manner that resembled CFB activation. Moreover, TGFβ treatment following prior exposure to DOX significantly remodeled the CFB transcriptome when compared to TGFβ treatment alone indicating a delayed effect of DOX capable of altering CFB responsiveness to subsequent TGFβ treatment. Categorical analyses of DEGs were performed to determine the GO Biological Processes (Fig. 5F) or KEGG Pathway terms (Fig. 5G) that were enriched with prior exposure to DOX in the context of TGFβ treatment of which the major identified terms represented cell cycle and cell division processes in the nucleus. Given the similarity of these enriched terms as those identified in Fig. 3, this potentially reflects persistent dysfunction and DNA damage following acute DOX treatment. Similar terms were also found from DEGs identified when comparing conditions without TGFβ treatment suggesting that prior exposure to DOX was the primary contributor to these changes (Fig. S3A, B). Altogether, these results are indicative of the durability of acute DOX exposure in mediating CFB transcriptome remodeling such that the signature pathways affected at the mRNA level were maintained throughout the chronic treatment paradigm and promoted a state of hyperreactivity to subsequent TGFβ treatment. These data also suggest the provocative notion that prior exposure to DOX promotes persistent DNA damage and dysregulated cell cycle processes that could mechanistically contribute to the hyperactivation of CFB observed in response to TGFβ.

Prior exposure to low-dose DOX exacerbates pathological cardiac remodeling, in vivo

Based on the findings observed in primary cultured CFB, it was critical to determine whether prior exposure to DOX alters CFB in a model of chronic DIC, in vivo. However, current preclinical models of chronic DIC typically use supraphysiological doses of DOX, i.e. ≥20 mg/kg administered either all at once or distributed over weekly administrations. While this rapidly elicits cardiotoxicity, it also results in significant mortality and does not recapitulate the clinical scenario of chronic DIC where the time course of disease progression in patients is delayed and occurs over the course of months to years typically in the presence of a secondary stressor. In efforts to develop a mouse model that better recapitulates the clinical presentation of chronic DIC, a dose response of DOX was performed where DOX was administered at doses of 1.5 or 3 mg/kg, once per week for 4 weeks (6 or 12 mg/kg total) corresponding to low- and moderate-level DOX exposures, respectively (Fig. S4A). Throughout the course of this experiment, the mice were monitored longitudinally for signs of toxicity (e.g. body weight loss) and echocardiography was performed to assess cardiac structure and function at baseline, one-week post-treatment and four-weeks post-treatment. As shown in Fig. S4B-E, neither dose of DOX affected body weight or cardiac structure and function. These results were expected given that the doses of DOX and experimental paradigm were intentionally designed to mimic the clinical scenario where patients would have no observable signs of DIC after completing a cancer treatment regimen. At the molecular level, expression of a panel of fibrosis-related genes in left ventricle (LV) tissue extracts were not significantly altered, however a trend towards increased expression was observed (Fig. S4F).

Focusing further on the effects of the 1.5 mg/kg DOX treatment regimen as that potentially represents a broader patient population exposed to even mild concentrations of DOX, mice were subjected to a chronic DIC treatment paradigm that implemented a subsequent neurohumoral stress with angiotensin II and phenylephrine (ANGII/PE) infusion via osmotic minipump (Fig. 6A). At the study endpoint, while prior DOX exposure did not affect body weight or systolic function of the heart (Fig. 6B, D), it exacerbated markers of diastolic function, global longitudinal strain, and structural remodeling of the heart following ANGII/PE infusion (Fig. 6C, E-I). Discussion

Chronic cardiotoxicity after use of DOX in cancer treatment continues to be highly prevalent, even with exposure levels that have traditionally been considered low-risk. Clinical and preclinical research indicate that fibrotic remodeling in the heart and diastolic dysfunction are common features in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity (DIC), which implicates CFB in DIC pathophysiology. However, neither CFB nor the chronic consequence of low exposure levels have been a focus in the majority of DIC studies, thus the precise time course and mechanisms underlying cellular toxicity that promote progressive dysfunction are unclear. This study investigated the immediate effects of low-dose DOX on CFB biology and the delayed effects of DOX during CFB differentiation by TGFβ to understand how dysregulated CFB activity may contribute to chronic DIC. Finally, a mouse model of low-dose DOX treatment was designed and validated where the long-term effects of DOX on fibrotic remodeling were assessed both with treatment alone as well as in the context of subsequent pathology.

The results showed that CFB retain their viability at nM levels of DOX which represents clinically relevant, steady-state physiological concentrations during which cells in the heart may be continuously exposed to DOX. At the lowest tested concentration, DOX had no immediate impact on CFB proliferation and minimal impact on signaling and metabolism. Interestingly, CFB continued to develop functional impairments after DOX was removed from the culture medium demonstrating that even low levels of exposure can result in prolonged CFB dysfunction. These findings support the notion that DOX can induce delayed dysfunction in proliferation, bioenergetics, and intracellular signaling which likely impairs CFB differentiation responses to stressful or injurious stimuli. Phenotypic and molecular changes were consistent with altered or accelerated differentiation and hyperactivation by TGFβ as a delayed consequence of DOX exposure in CFB. Transcriptomic analysis revealed persistent and widespread chromosome and cell cycle dysfunction, which is likely a response to DNA damage due to DOX intercalation in DNA, the canonical mechanism by which DOX induces killing of cancer cells [14], [39], [40]. It's believed that this points to a potential mechanism of action whereby acute treatment with DOX is eliciting a prolonged effect even in its absence that renders CFB more responsive to subsequent stimuli for activation. Two likely explanations are (1) DOX alters the metabolic profile of CFB allowing for the accumulation of Kreb's cycle intermediates (e.g. alpha-ketoglutarate) which has demonstrated potential for remodeling the CFB epigenome into a primed fibrogenic state [35], [36]; and (2) DOX is promoting a mild state of cellular senescence which has been documented to augment the fibrotic response in the heart [37]. In summation, it appears that while acute low-dose DOX minimally impacts CFB viability and function, DOX exposure triggers widespread dysfunction of nuclear processes. This may continue to accumulate and propagate further dysfunction, i.e. proliferation and metabolism, which were found to be delayed consequences of DOX.

The in vivo relevance of low-dose DOX has been infrequently studied and the potential longitudinal pathogenesis that occurs is unknown in this scenario. Moreover, current models that use higher doses of DOX do not recapitulate the chronic DIC in the clinical setting, where cardiotoxicity is not present immediately after treatment, but rather develops months to years after treatment has ended typically in the presence of other cardiovascular risk factors. In this study, a low-dose DOX treatment paradigm was validated as a clinically relevant in vivo model. This treatment paradigm was used to model chronic DIC where mice were treated with a 6 mg/kg cumulative dose of DOX followed by a recovery period and then a subsequent 4 weeks of ANGII/PE infusion, a well characterized model for pathological fibrotic remodeling after neurohumoral stress. The results strongly suggest that, mechanistically, low-dose DOX exacerbates progressive cardiac dysfunction and delayed-onset HF in the context of a secondary stress. Taken together with the in vitro findings from cultured CFB, even low levels of DOX exposure confer persistent alterations in CFB. Therapeutically, this suggests that targeting the pathological processes induced by DOX that promote CFB activation may be effective in mitigating chronic DIC even in patients with lower cumulative exposures.

CFB differentiation is not a singular process and CFB fates can be variable, resulting in profound heterogeneity of this cell type in the heart [29], [38], [41]. Therefore, it is possible that CFB hyperactivation by DOX may not occur through conventional pathways, especially in the unique context of sustained DNA damage by DOX. While canonical activation and differentiation into myoFB is well characterized as an important role of CFB in the heart, DOX has been shown to alter CFB phenotypes i.e. transdifferentiation into a pro-inflammatory phenotype [26], [42]. Further, myoFB can follow divergent paths such as undergoing apoptotic clearance or reverting to a quiescent state [29], [37], [38]. Especially in cardiac pathology, myoFB may continue a differentiation path towards that of a matrifibrocyte lineage, becoming chondrocyte-like and resistant to apoptosis, or they may progress along a continuum of senescence after which they lie dormant until becoming reactivated to induce a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) [18], [37]. The results presented in this study indicate lasting changes in CFB in response to low-dose DOX such as reduced proliferation, metabolism, and altered ERK signaling, each of which could reflect alternate cell fates. Thus, in future studies it would be important to assess whether there are distinct markers of alternative cell fates in CFB after DOX, and if so, what changes by DOX enable transition towards other differentiation trajectories.

The findings from this study point to several possible mechanisms by which cell fate can be altered. One possibility is through altered signaling, where notably in this study ERK signaling was elevated immediately after DOX treatment and remained aberrantly elevated after 48 h of TGFβ treatment. ERK is appreciated as a master regulator of crucial cell processes such as metabolism, growth, survival, and differentiation [43], and could therefore mediate several of the delayed changes that were observed in this study. Another possibility is through epigenetic modulation of genes involved in CFB differentiation that may constitute a cellular memory which is a particularly relevant considering the delayed effects of DOX exposure in patients. It is becoming appreciated that even after decades, the heart can retain memory of prior DOX exposure that, in response to additional DOX infusions, a patient can have an increased risk of adverse events and cardiotoxicity even without prior detectible signs of damage [4]. This clinical phenomenon strongly suggests the presence of cellular memory in the form of a latent but durable change that is conditionally exposed by appropriate triggers. Moreover, such a latent change in CFB could be revealed as activation and differentiation during chronic DIC pathophysiology, given that CFB differentiation into myoFB necessitates histone remodeling at genomic loci of fibroblast activation genes [36], [44], [45]. Thus, epigenetic changes could be induced by DOX at gene loci that are conditionally regulated only during CFB activation. Furthermore, dysregulated bioenergetics could alter the flux of Krebs cycle metabolites into the nucleus that serve as the substrate for epigenetic changes, a process that is especially important during CFB activation [35], [36]. Since both epigenetic changes as well as dysregulated metabolism could arise from sustained DNA damage, and could correspond to altered intracellular signaling, further investigation would be required to clarify the causal interplay of these characteristics within the overall mechanism of chronic DIC.

Finally, it is important to note the limitations of this study. First, cultured CFB are notorious for becoming activated when placed on very stiff tissue-culture grade plastic which could exacerbate CFB activation in response to DOX, in vitro. Because of this, validating hypothesized mechanisms from cultured CFB studies in mouse models of chronic DIC will be essential and is the intent of the newly developed mouse model of chronic DIC. Another limitation of cultured CFB studies of chronic DIC is the difficulty of modeling the precise pharmacokinetics of DOX metabolism in the body and the time course of disease in patients. In these studies, low concentrations would best represent the steady-state levels of DOX following infusion, but the dynamic and phasic clearance of DOX cannot be adequately mimicked. And, while this investigation focuses on CFB due to the importance of this cell type in fibrotic remodeling in the heart and diastolic dysfunction, DOX is known to affect cardiac myocytes and other cell types in the heart such as endothelial or immune cells [16], [46]. It is currently unknown to what extent the pathology is driven by interplay of dysregulated feedback between different cell types. Further mechanistic studies are required to specifically assess cell-specific contributions to chronic DIC pathogenesis and determine whether targeted CFB interventions alone can represent viable therapeutic approaches. Lastly, it's critical to note that the models described here are preclinical. While fibrosis has been well-described in DOX-treated patients via the use of cardiac MRI [23], [24] and mechanisms described in this study (e.g. DNA damage and repair mechanisms) have been associated with DIC in childhood cancer survivors [47], much is left to be explored in more clinically relevant experimental model systems. Currently, the use of human iPSC-derived cardiac organoids has been adopted as a promising strategy to model chronic DIC and associated fibrotic remodeling [48], [49], [50], [51], [52].

In conclusion, we demonstrate that while acute treatment with a clinically relevant low-dose of DOX has only minor effects on the CFB phenotype, it alters the transcriptome activating DNA damage, cell cycle, and senescent pathways in a manner that persists and renders CFB more susceptible to a secondary stress (i.e. TGFβ). We also present a novel mouse model of chronic DIC in which prior exposure to DOX exacerbates cardiac dysfunction and fibrotic remodeling in response to neurohumoral stress.
