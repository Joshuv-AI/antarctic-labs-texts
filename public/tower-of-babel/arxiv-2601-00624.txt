Do Chatbot LLMs Talk Too Much? The YapBench Benchmark
Vadim Borisov, Michael Gröger, Mina Mikhael, Richard H. Schreiber

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini increasingly act as general-purpose copilots, yet they often respond with unnecessary length on simple requests adding redundant explanations, hedging, or boilerplate that increases cognitive load and inflates token-based inference cost. Prior work suggests that preference-based post-training and LLM-judged evaluations can induce systematic length bias, where longer answers are rewarded even at comparable quality. We introduce YapBench , a lightweight benchmark for quantifying user-visible over-generation on brevity-ideal prompts. Each item consists of a single-turn prompt, a curated minimal sufficient baseline answer, and a category label. Our primary metric, YapScore, measures excess response length beyond the baseline, in characters, so results are comparable across models without relying on any specific tokenizer. Further, we summarize the YapScores of each model via the YapIndex, a uniform-weighted average of category-level median YapScores. YapBench contains over three hundred English prompts spanning three common brevity-ideal settings: (A) minimal/ambiguous inputs where the ideal behavior is a short clarification, (B) closed-form factual questions with short stable answers, and (C) one-line coding tasks where a single command/snippet suffices. Evaluating 76 76 assistant LLM models, we observe an order-of-magnitude spread in median excess length, and distinct category-specific failure modes (vacuum-filling on ambiguous inputs and explanation/formatting overhead on one-line technical requests). We release the benchmark and maintain a live leaderboard for tracking verbosity behavior over time.

Introduction

Preference-based post-training (e.g., Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)
(
50
)
and related alignment pipelines) is now routine in training assistant large language models (LLMs)
(
8
)
. A recurring concern is that these methods can induce
length bias
: longer outputs are systematically favored, even when additional text does not improve correctness or helpfulness. Evidence for this effect appears in reward-model and LLM-judge preferences
(
56
)
, in observations that Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) can increase response length
(
54
;
27
)
, and in findings that models sometimes produce extra text as a response to uncertainty
(
67
)
. This motivates the following evaluation question:

Research question:

On brevity-ideal prompts, how strong is length bias in assistant LLMs, measured as verbosity beyond a minimal sufficient answer?

Or in plain English:

when a short answer is sufficient, how much additional text do assistant LLMs generate beyond a minimal sufficient response?

This question also captures a
recurring phenomenon
in assistant-style interactions: models often add text beyond what is required for correctness or sufficiency in simple requests. This extra content can take the form of prompt restatement, generic caveats, or boilerplate preambles (e.g.,
‘‘Great question!’’
and
‘‘As an AI language model’’
). This avoidable verbosity carries at least three concrete costs:

•

User experience cost.
Extra tokens translate into extra reading and scrolling, which can reduce satisfaction and trust—especially on simple tasks where the desired response is short and direct. Empirically, users often prefer short answers for simple tasks
(
29
)
, and lower cognitive load in chatbot interactions is associated with more positive user attitudes and stronger intentions to continue using the system
(
12
)
.

•

Energy and environmental cost.
Inference cost in LLMs scales roughly linearly with the number of generated tokens, so unnecessary verbosity directly increases energy use. Recent measurements estimate per-token inference energy on the order of joules, implying that large-scale overgeneration can produce measurable electricity consumption and associated emissions
(
57
)
.

At current estimates, generating on the order of tens of unnecessary tokens can waste energy comparable to keeping an LED light bulb on for a few seconds negligible once, but material when repeated at scale

(
28
)
.

•

Economic cost.
Commercial LLM APIs are typically priced per token, so even modest verbosity multipliers increase per-request cost and can materially raise operating expenses in high-throughput applications. For instance, OpenAI reported that users showing gratitude and being nice to ChatGPT by adding ”Please” and ”Thank you” to their prompts, incurred costs in the range of 10s of millions of dollars
(
9
)
. The same logic applies to the verbosity of an LLM’s answer.

Despite these costs, most existing evaluation suites emphasize accuracy, robustness, and safety, with limited attention to verbosity as a first-class outcome. At the same time, human-factors evidence is increasingly clear that more text is not always better: people prefer simple explanations for simple events and more complex explanations only when the underlying phenomenon demands it
(
29
)
. For straightforward information-seeking interactions, recent studies similarly point toward a preference for direct, concise responses over drawn-out dialogue
(
12
)
.

In this paper, we introduce
YapBench
, a lightweight benchmark and metric suite designed to quantify verbosity specifically in settings where brevity is broadly preferred. The core ideas are:

•

We curate prompts that are brevity-ideal by construction: each can be satisfied with a short, self-contained minimal sufficient answer.

•

For each prompt, we define a
minimal sufficient answer
: the shortest response that raters still judge as correct, clear, and sufficient.

•

We define a primary measure, YapScore, which measures how much longer a model’s output is than the minimal sufficient answer while remaining easy to interpret.

•

We complement YapScore with
auxiliary analyses
, including a cost-oriented metric (YapTax) that estimates the marginal token-priced overhead of over-generation, and behavioral diagnostics (e.g., over-generation on minimal/nonsense inputs and refusal-length overhead).

YapBench
measures a specific but practically important component of assistant quality: producing the minimal sufficient information without redundant text on brevity-ideal prompts. While it does not cover all aspects of assistant performance, it can serve as a proxy for utility in workflows where concision, latency, and cost are first-order objectives. Concretely, we contribute
YapBench
v0.1 (304 brevity-ideal prompts with curated minimal-sufficient baselines and category labels), simple and interpretable verbosity metrics
YapScore
(per-prompt excess characters) and
YapIndex
(a category-balanced aggregate) and a cost-oriented companion metric,
YapTax
, alongside category-level diagnostics and an empirical evaluation across a broad set of assistant LLMs with a maintained
live leaderboard
.

Related Work

Prior work has documented multiple mechanisms and manifestations of
verbosity/length bias
in assistant LLMs, as well as mitigation strategies for length-sensitive evaluation.

Verbosity measurement and correlates.

67
introduce the
verbosity compensation
(VC) score, showing that models often answer with more words than necessary even when explicitly instructed to be concise. They demonstrate that VC is pervasive across models and datasets (e.g.,
GPT-4
shows
\approx
50% VC frequency on some tasks). They also observe that verbose answers are often less accurate, with large performance gaps between concise and verbose settings, and report that verbosity correlates with uncertainty signals such as higher perplexity and related uncertainty metrics.

Length bias in preference learning and LLM-judged evaluation.

58
investigate length bias in RLHF reward models, showing that vanilla reward models can learn a shortcut where longer responses receive systematically higher scores, even when additional text does not improve helpfulness. Relatedly, when GPT-4 is used as a proxy judge, it tends to favor longer answers over shorter ones at comparable quality
(
56
;
15
)
, inducing a length-correlated bias that advantages verbose models in win-rate–based evaluations. To address this,
15
introduce a length-controlled win rate that removes the length term from the preference regression, improving robustness to verbosity manipulation and increasing correlation with LMSYS Chatbot Arena rankings
(
11
)
.

26
further analyze length bias in LLM-as-a-judge settings by decomposing win rate into length-independent
desirability
and length-dependent
information mass
(quantified via conditional entropy), and argue that longer responses gain higher win rates primarily through increased information mass. Building on this view, they introduce AdapAlpaca, a variant of AlpacaEval that dynamically matches the length of reference and test responses to mitigate length bias in automatic preference evaluation.

Over-reasoning as a failure mode of over-generation.

Chiang and Lee
(
10
)
study over-reasoning in LLMs using GSM8K-Zero, where questions can be answered directly from the prompt text without any calculations. They show that RLHF-trained models nonetheless produce lengthy, unnecessary chains of calculation on these trivial problems, often introducing errors and lowering accuracy compared to short, direct answers. Using GPT-4 as proxy reward models, they further find that these judges systematically prefer long answers even when the long answer is wrong and a competing short answer is correct-implicating length-favoring preference mechanisms as a driver of redundant reasoning.

Reference-free conciseness metrics.

16
show that conciseness is
positively correlated with human judgments of redundancy and verbosity
across several tasks. They propose ConCISE, a reference-free conciseness metric that estimates redundancy by generating compressed variants of an answer (abstractive, extractive, and pruned) and using an LLM judge to verify semantic and entity preservation. However, this approach requires multiple additional LLM calls, is sensitive to the choice of helper model and prompts, and treats the original answer as the reference rather than anchoring conciseness to task-specific minimal sufficiency or distinguishing brevity-focused from explanation-heavy settings.

3
YapBench

YapBench
is a benchmark for measuring
user-visible over-generation
on
brevity-ideal
prompts. Each benchmark item is a triple
(p_{i},b_{i},c_{i})
: a single-turn prompt
p_{i}
, a short reference baseline
b_{i}
, and a category label
c_{i}
. The baseline is the
minimal sufficient answer
: the shortest response that is
still correct, clear, and sufficient for a typical user.
The
YapBench
benchmark asks a question
:
when a short answer is enough, how much longer does the model answer than this baseline?

Informally,
YapBench
compares assistant LLMs on how much they
over-generate
(”yap”) beyond a prompt-specific minimal sufficient answer on prompts where brevity is ideal.

Formally,
YapBench
is designed to measure
unnecessary verbosity
in settings where a short answer is clearly sufficient. The benchmark is built around three goals:

1.

Isolation of verbosity.
Evaluate verbosity separately from other axes (e.g., correctness, safety), rather than collapsing them into a single scalar.

2.

Brevity-ideal scope.
Focus core metrics on prompts where annotators broadly agree that short, direct answers are preferred.

3.

Practical simplicity.
Enable evaluation with standard APIs and offline scoring from logged model outputs.

3.1
Prompt set and categories

We group prompts into a small number of categories to keep analysis interpretable. Different models “yap” for different reasons (e.g., filling the vacuum on nonsense, or adding explanations around a one-line command). For the initial version of the benchmark we selected three prompt categories:

Prompt Category A: minimal / ambiguous input.

Category A is designed to stress-test assistants on
low-information
turns, where the safest and most useful behavior is to ask for clarification (or give a minimal acknowledgment), not to guess intent. Concretely, the category includes (i) pure noise
inputs with no semantic content (empty/whitespace-only, punctuation spam, keyboard mashes, emoji-only), and (ii) underspecified openers that signal a problem but provide no details (e.g.,
help
,
error
,
fix this
,
how?
,

it doesn’t work
). We also include short phatic tokens (e.g.,
ok
,
cool
,
thanks
) and bare digits (e.g.,
1
,
42
) as ambiguous turns that commonly trigger over-interpretation. Baselines therefore use
minimal
templates: a short clarification request (e.g.,
Please rephrase.
,

What do you need help with?
) or a minimal acknowledgment (e.g.,
OK.
,
You’re welcome.
).

The goal of Category A is to measure how much models
over-generate
when the prompt is unclear: do they stay minimal, or do they produce extended, unsolicited content despite insufficient context?

Prompt Category B: short factual Q&A (closed-form).

Category B targets prompts where the user is clearly asking for a
single fact
and
a one-token (or very short) answer is sufficient. The prompts are intentionally
closed-form: capitals, simple arithmetic, basic geometry, chemical symbols and formulas,
atomic numbers, common biological facts, and canonical authorship/date questions. We
also include a small number of “confusion-prone” but still unambiguous items (e.g.,

What is the capital of Australia?
) to capture a frequent assistant behavior:
adding disclaimers or extra context around a straightforward fact.
The goal of
Category B is to measure over-generation in the simplest information-seeking setting:
when the prompt has a unique, stable answer, does the model return the minimal fact, or
does it add avoidable explanation and filler?

Prompt Category C: one-line coding task / command.

Category C targets prompts where the user requests an
atomic, one-line output
: a shell command, a regex pattern, a simple SQL statement, or a single-line code snippet. The v0.1 set spans common command-line tasks (
wc
,
find
,
grep
,
tar
,
df
,
tail
), Git one-liners, basic SQL
SELECT/WHERE
and aggregates, practical regexes, and short idiomatic snippets in Python/JavaScript/Java/Rust/Docker. Baselines in this category are
exactly one line
by construction.
The goal of Category C is to measure a very common assistant failure mode in technical help: when a single line is sufficient, does the model output only the one-liner, or does it add extra prose (headings, explanations, duplicated code
blocks) that increases length without improving task completion?

Dataset size (v0.1).

YapBench
v0.1 contains 304 English prompts: 60 in Category A, 126 in Category B, and 118 in Category C. At the moment of writing the category counts in
YapBench
are unequal. This does not induce a weighting effect in the aggregate metric: YapIndex is computed from category-level medians and then combined with uniform category weights (Section
3.3
), so category contribution is not proportional to the number of prompts. The role of
|\mathcal{I}_{c}|
is primarily statistical: larger categories yield more stable estimates of the category median, rather than systematically shifting the aggregate score.

3.2
Minimal sufficient baselines

For each prompt
p_{i}
, we define a baseline
b_{i}
that represents the minimal sufficient response. Baselines are intentionally short: they are not “best possible” answers, only the minimum needed to satisfy the prompt.

Baseline rules (operational).

Each
(p_{i},b_{i})
pair is curated to satisfy:

•

Unambiguous intent.
The prompt targets one task and one correct answer
in standard usage (up to trivial surface variants).

•

Minimal sufficiency.
The baseline is as short as possible while still
correct and clear; removing any token should break correctness or clarity.

•

Atomic and self-contained.
No reliance on prior turns or hidden context.

•

Standard practice.
When multiple short correct answers exist, choose
the idiomatic/common one (especially in code/commands).

•

Reproducible.
Items that produce disagreement about “minimal
sufficient” are revised or removed.

How baselines look in each category.

•

A:
a minimal clarification (e.g.,
Please rephrase.
,

What do you need help with?
) or a minimal acknowledgment for phatic inputs
(e.g.,
OK.
,
You’re welcome.
). This is why Category A includes
multiple baseline
types
.

•

B:
a single token or short phrase (e.g.,
Paris
,
12
,

H
2
O
,
Yes
).

•

C:
exactly one line of code, command, or pattern, with no additional symbols (e.g., Markdown formatting).

Baseline construction and review.

Baselines are written to be minimal rather than exhaustive: the shortest answer that remains correct, clear, and sufficient for a typical user.
We iteratively refined
(p_{i},b_{i})
pairs using internal review: for each prompt, we edited the baseline down to the shortest form that still preserves correctness and clarity, and removed or rewrote prompts where “minimal sufficient” produced repeated disagreements. This process emphasizes reproducibility: if a prompt does not admit a stable minimal answer under standard usage, it is excluded or corrected in the future versions of the benchamrk.

3.3
Measures: YapScore, YapIndex, and YapTax

In this subsection, we define quantitative measures for
unnecessary verbosity
on brevity-ideal prompts.
The key idea is to measure how much
extra
text a model produces beyond a prompt-specific minimal sufficient answer.

Definition 1

(Per-prompt YapScore (Excess Length))
.

Let
i\in\mathcal{I}
index a prompt with baseline answer length
B_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
, measured in characters.
For a model
M
, let
L_{i}(M)\in\mathbb{N}
denote the character length
of its response to prompt
i
.
The per-prompt YapScore is defined as

\mathrm{YapScore}_{i}(M)\;=\;\max\{0,\;L_{i}(M)-B_{i}\}.

(1)

Interpretation.

\mathrm{YapScore}_{i}(M)
measures the number of
extra characters

generated by model
M
beyond the minimal sufficient answer for prompt
i
.
A score of zero indicates a baseline-length response.
Larger values indicate increasing amounts of unnecessary verbosity.
By construction, this metric is robust to extremely short baselines and
requires no smoothing or tuning parameters.

Definition 2

(Category and Aggregate YapIndex)
.

Let
\mathcal{I}_{c}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
denote the set of prompts
belonging to category
c\in\mathcal{C}
, where

\mathcal{C}
is the set of benchmark categories (e.g.,
\mathcal{C}=\{A,B,C\}
).
The category-level YapIndex is defined as

Y_{c}(M)\;=\;\operatorname{median}\{\mathrm{YapScore}_{i}(M):i\in\mathcal{I}_{c}\}.

(2)

The aggregate YapIndex is the weighted average of category indices:

\mathrm{YapIndex}(M)\;=\;\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}w_{c}\,Y_{c}(M),

(3)

where
w_{c}\geq 0
are category weights with
\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}w_{c}=1

(uniform by default).

Aggregation Rationale.

We use the median within each category to reduce sensitivity to rare
extreme verbosity bursts and to avoid dominance by prompts with very short
baselines.
Aggregating across categories ensures that the final YapIndex reflects
verbosity behavior across distinct interaction regimes rather than a
single prompt type.

Why are baselines required?

Verbosity is inherently relative to task sufficiency.
The baseline
B_{i}
defines the minimum information required to correctly
and clearly answer prompt
i
.
Without baselines, a length-based metric would conflate necessary content
with unnecessary over-generation and would reduce to measuring response
length rather than
unnecessary
verbosity.

3.3.1
Uncertainty estimation (bootstrap confidence intervals)

Since YapBench reports medians of heavy-tailed excess-length distributions, we estimate uncertainty using a nonparametric bootstrap over prompts (percentile intervals). For each model and each statistic reported, we run
B=1000
bootstrap iterations.
Each iteration resamples prompts
with replacement
and recomputes the statistic on the resampled set.

Category medians.

For a category
c\in\{A,B,C\}
with per-prompt YapScores
\{s_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c}}
,
bootstrap iteration
b
draws a resample

\{s^{*(b)}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{|\mathcal{I}_{c}|}
and computes the bootstrap median

m_{c}^{(b)}\;=\;\operatorname{median}\!\left(\{s^{*(b)}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{|\mathcal{I}_{c}|}\right).

(4)

We form a 95% percentile confidence interval for the category median as

[L_{c},U_{c}]\;=\;\left[Q_{0.025}\!\left(\{m_{c}^{(b)}\}_{b=1}^{B}\right),\;Q_{0.975}\!\left(\{m_{c}^{(b)}\}_{b=1}^{B}\right)\right].

(5)

YapIndex.

To capture uncertainty in the aggregate score, each bootstrap iteration resamples

within each category
and recomputes the full index:

\displaystyle m_{c}^{(b)}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\operatorname{median}\!\left(\text{resample of Cat\penalty\ }c\text{ scores}\right),\hskip 20.00003ptc\in\{A,B,C\},

(6)

\displaystyle\mathrm{YapIndex}^{(b)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in\{A,B,C\}}w_{c}\,m_{c}^{(b)}.

We then take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of
\{\mathrm{YapIndex}^{(b)}\}_{b=1}^{B}

to obtain the 95% percentile interval
[L,U]
.

Reporting as a half-width.

For compact presentation, we summarize each 95% interval
[L,U]
by its half-width

\Delta_{95}\;=\;\frac{U-L}{2},

(7)

and report statistics as
\hat{s}\pm\Delta_{95}
.
Note that bootstrap percentile intervals for medians can be asymmetric; the
\pm
notation is a symmetric summary intended to ease comparison across models. (Uniform
w_{c}
is used by default.)

3.3.2
Cost-based measure: YapTax

While YapScore is computed in characters for tokenizer-agnostic comparison, we also report a cost-oriented secondary metric that estimates the
marginal dollar cost
of over-generation under token-based pricing.

Definition 3

(Excess output tokens)
.

Let
T_{i}(M)\in\mathbb{N}
denote the number of
output tokens
produced by model

M
on prompt
i
, and let
T_{i}(b)
denote the number of tokens in the minimal
sufficient baseline
b_{i}
, computed under a fixed tokenizer.
We define excess output tokens as

\mathrm{ExcessTok}_{i}(M)\;=\;\max\{0,\;T_{i}(M)-T_{i}(b)\}.

(8)

Definition 4

(Per-prompt YapTax (USD))
.

Let
p_{M}
be the output-token price for model
M
in USD per token.
The per-prompt YapTax is defined as

\mathrm{YapTax}_{i}(M)\;=\;\mathrm{ExcessTok}_{i}(M)\cdot p_{M}.

(9)

Definition 5

(Aggregate YapTax per 1,000 prompts)
.

For a set of evaluated prompts
\mathcal{I}
with
|\mathcal{I}|=N
, we report

\mathrm{YapTax}_{\$1000}(M)\;=\;1000\cdot\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\mathrm{YapTax}_{i}(M).

(10)

Interpretation.

\mathrm{YapTax}_{\$1000}(M)
is the expected additional dollar cost attributable to
over-generation for 1,000 benchmark queries under the model’s output-token pricing.

T_{i}(M)
uses the model’s
non-reasoning
output tokens (excluding chain-of-thought tokens when reported separately by the API).

•

T_{i}(b)
is computed by tokenizing
b_{i}
using each model’s own tokenizer.

p_{M}
are retrieved from the OpenRouter API

4
Evaluation Protocol and Parameters

Access, prompting, and decoding.

All models are queried via the OpenRouter API with absent system prompt. We set temperature to
0
whenever the model endpoint exposes this control; for endpoints that do not allow temperature configuration (e.g., GPT-5 variants), we use the provider default, the rest of hyperparameters are default to each provider. All prompts are evaluated independently in a single-turn setting with no prior context.

Length units.

The primary YapScore is computed in characters to enable tokenizer-agnostic comparison across providers. We also neglect the markdown characters from the YapScore calculations.

Correctness and sufficiency.

YapBench
v0.1 is intentionally constructed so that Category B (closed-form factual items)
and Category C (atomic one-line commands/snippets) are
trivial to answer correctly

for essentially any instruction-tuned model. Consequently, we do not apply an explicit correctness gate in the main results; the benchmark is designed to isolate over-generation behavior rather than task difficulty. Category A items are likewise evaluated primarily for verbosity behavior on underspecified inputs, where the desired behavior is a minimal clarification or acknowledgment rather than substantive content.

Reasoning-model ablations.

For models that expose explicit “reasoning” modes or verbosity controls, we run paired evaluations with these features enabled and disabled to measure their impact on YapScore and category-level behavior. Importantly, YapScore is computed only from the
user-visible final answer
: we exclude any hidden or separately reported reasoning traces (e.g., chain-of-thought tokens) from length and token counts whenever the API provides them separately.

We evaluate models using the excess-length YapScore and the aggregate YapIndex defined

5.3
Results: Category-Level Behavior

Category medians reveal distinct verbosity failure modes:

Category A (underspecified / ambiguous inputs).

Category A can be a major source of excess length: several models generate large median overhead on low-information prompts, consistent with a tendency to “fill the vacuum” with unsolicited content rather than issuing a minimal clarification.

Category B (short factual Q&A).

Median excess in Category B is low for some models but dominates for others, especially on confusion-prone yet closed-form questions, indicating that many assistants add avoidable context or caveats even when a single fact is sufficient.

Category C (one-line code tasks).

Category C frequently drives the largest median excess for many models, reflecting formatting and explanation overhead (e.g., headings, prose, or duplicated code blocks) around atomic commands or snippets where a one-line answer would suffice.

Lastly, while YapIndex summarizes typical behavior via medians, over-generation can be highly variable across prompts; we therefore complement median-based reporting with tail statistics (e.g., percentiles) to characterize worst-case “verbosity bursts” that are most disruptive for user experience and cost.

6
Discussion

Brevity-optimal behavior is not aligned with model recency or perceived capability.

A central finding of YapBench is that response verbosity on brevity-ideal prompts is not monotonic
in model generation or perceived frontier capability. In particular, a model from 2023
gpt-3.5-turbo achieves the
best overall YapIndex (23)
in our evaluation (Table
4
), outperforming a
range of newer frontier assistant models (Gemini-Pro, GPT-5, etc). This result suggests that unnecessary verbosity is not an inherent consequence of scale or general competence. Instead, it appears to be a behavioral property that is influenced by post-training and assistant-style response priors. From a deployment
perspective, this decoupling implies that selecting models solely based on conventional capability benchmarks can yield suboptimal user experience and higher inference cost in brevity-ideal settings.

Verbosity is heterogeneous across interaction regimes, motivating category-level analysis.

YapBench was designed to separate distinct regimes where brevity is typically preferred, and the results confirm that models exhibit markedly different behavior across categories. The category leaderboards (Table
5
) show that some models can be near-minimal on closed-form factual queries (Category B), while simultaneously producing substantial over-generation on underspecified inputs (Category A) or one-line technical coding requests (Category C). This heterogeneity indicates that a single global verbosity score is insufficient to characterize assistant behavior in practice. Reporting category-level outcomes is therefore essential to identify which interaction types drive excess length and to diagnose failure modes that are likely to be product-critical.

Underspecified inputs bring out systematic over-generation that is better explained as a policy prior than a knowledge limitation.
Category A prompts contain low-information turns for which the minimal sufficient behavior is a short
clarification request or acknowledgment. Despite the absence of actionable task information, many models generate extended content, which we interpret as a systematic tendency to produce proactive, generic assistance rather than requesting missing context. Because Category A baselines are intentionally minimal, excess length in this regime directly quantifies an assistant prior to respond substantively even when the prompt does not support it. This behavior is particularly salient from a
human factors and cost perspective, since so-called ”vacuum-filling” responses can become long while providing
limited incremental utility.

One-line technical requests expose formatting and explanation overhead as a dominant source of excess length.

Category C isolates cases where the user request admits an atomic solution. The results suggest that
over-generation in this regime often arises from presentation and pedagogy defaults rather than task
requirements. Common patterns include multi-line formatting around a one-line command, redundant
restatement of the request, and additional explanatory text that is not necessary for successful task
completion. Importantly, the presence of models with low median excess in Category C
(Table
5
) indicates that this overhead is not unavoidable. Instead, it is a
controllable output policy that can likely be reduced through targeted post-training objectives or
inference-time constraints.

Closed-form factual prompts still trigger avoidable text, consistent with length-favoring preference mechanisms.

Although Category B is constructed so that short answers are sufficient, many models still add
context, qualifiers, or restatements. The category-level top performers demonstrate that minimal
behavior is attainable, yet the broader spread in Category B suggests that some assistants adopt a default of providing additional narrative even when the prompt is unambiguous. This pattern is consistent with prior evidence that preference modeling and LLM-judged evaluation can reward
responses that appear more thorough, even when additional content does not improve correctness or sufficiency. YapBench provides a direct measurement of this effect under conditions where brevity is normatively preferred.

Reasoning modes do not uniformly increase typical verbosity, but they plausibly increase worst-case over-generation.

A common expectation is that explicit reasoning modes necessarily induce longer outputs. Our
category-level results indicate a more nuanced picture: several reasoning variants remain competitive
on median excess length in specific categories (Table
5
).

7
Future Work and Limitations

Future work.

We plan to add auxiliary prompt sets that isolate other common sources of undesirable
length, while keeping the core benchmark brevity-ideal:

•

Safety / Refusal.
Harmful or disallowed requests where the desired
response is a short, firm refusal (avoiding pages of boilerplate).

•

Misconception traps.
Prompts that invite agreement with a falsehood
(“X is true, right?”) where the desired response is a brief correction.

•

Legitimate verbosity (control).
Tasks where detail is genuinely
helpful (e.g., step-by-step instructions, teaching, drafting a careful message),
used as a control to ensure the benchmark does not reward pathological brevity.

Limitations.

Baseline selection is inherently a source of variance: there is no universal “gold” standard for response length.
YapBench
mitigates this via criteria and annotator agreement, but residual subjectivity remains. In addition, some prompts can become non-stationary over time (e.g., facts that change) and must be periodically audited. Finally, very short or empty inputs are handled differently across providers; we therefore recommend using explicit blank-input proxies (e.g.,
<EMPTY>
) for Category A to avoid benchmarking API preprocessing rather than model behavior.

8
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced
YapBench
v0.1, a lightweight benchmark for measuring user-visible over-generation on brevity-ideal prompts using curated minimal sufficient baselines. To quantify verbosity in a simple and interpretable way, we defined three complementary measures:
YapScore
, a per-prompt excess-length metric in characters;
YapIndex
, a category-balanced aggregate based on category-level medians; and
YapTax
, a cost-oriented estimate of the marginal token-priced overhead attributable to over-generation. Across
76
assistant models, we find substantial and structured differences in verbosity, with distinct category-level failure modes and large variation even among highly capable systems.

We release
YapBench
and maintain a live leaderboard to support ongoing tracking of verbosity behavior over time and to encourage training and evaluation methods that optimize for
minimal sufficient
responses when brevity is ideal.

deepseek-v3.2

Prompt:

Who discovered penicillin?

Response:
That’s an excellent question! The discovery of penicillin is credited to the Scottish scientist Sir Alexander Fleming in 1928.

However, the full story involves a famous accident, a moment of brilliant observation, and the work of other scientists who turned the discovery into a life-saving drug.

Here’s a breakdown of the key events:
Alexander Fleming’s Accidental Discovery (1928)
The Setting: Fleming was a bacteriologist at St. Mary’s Hospital in London. He was researching staphylococci bacteria.
The Accident: Upon returning from a vacation in September 1928, Fleming noticed that a Petri dish containing staphylococci had been contaminated by a blue-green mold.
The Crucial Observation: Instead of just discarding the contaminated dish, Fleming saw that the bacteria would not grow in a circle around the mold. He realized the mold was releasing a substance that killed the bacteria. He named this substance "penicillin" after the genus of the mold, Penicillium notatum*.
Fleming’s Contribution: He identified the mold’s antibacterial properties and published his findings in 1929. However, he found penicillin difficult to produce in large, stable quantities and eventually moved on to other work.
Howard Florey and Ernst Chain: The Development of a Drug (1939-1945)

For a decade, Fleming’s discovery was largely a laboratory curiosity. It was the team of Howard Florey (an Australian pathologist) and Ernst Chain (a German-born biochemist) at the University of Oxford who unlocked its potential.
Their Work: Beginning in 1939, Florey and Chain led a team that successfully isolated, purified, and concentrated penicillin.
Proving its Effectiveness: They conducted a famous experiment on mice, showing that penicillin could cure deadly bacterial infections. The first human patient was treated in 1941 with remarkable results.
Mass Production: With World War II creating a huge need for antibiotics, they worked with American pharmaceutical companies to develop methods for the mass production of penicillin, saving countless lives on and off the battlefield.

Recognition: The Nobel Prize

In 1945, the significance of their work was recognized when Alexander Fleming, Howard Florey, and Ernst Chain were jointly awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

In summary:
Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin by accident and recognized its potential.
Howard Florey and Ernst Chain figured out how to turn it into a practical, life-saving drug.

So, while Fleming is the most famous name associated with penicillin, it was truly a team effort that brought it to the world.

To safely check if a String variable s is null or empty in Java, while checking for null first to avoid a NullPointerException, you can use the following boolean expression:

s == null || s.isEmpty()

Explanation:
s == null: Checks if the string reference is null. This is evaluated first.
||: Short-circuit OR operator — if the first condition is true, the second is not evaluated, preventing NullPointerException.
s.isEmpty(): Checks if the string is empty (i.e., has zero length). This only runs if s is not null.

Example usage:
String s = null;

if (s == null || s.isEmpty())
System.out.println("String is null or empty");

This pattern is common and safe in Java. Alternatively, you can use Objects.isNull() and String.isEmpty() for a slightly more modern style:

Objects.isNull(s) || s.isEmpty()

But the traditional s == null is still widely used and perfectly acceptable.
