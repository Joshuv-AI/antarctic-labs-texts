Abstract

We investigate the variation in the total number of points in a random
p\times p
square in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
at which the
p
-adic valuation of a given polynomial in two variables is greater than
1
. We establish that this quantity follows a Poisson distribution as
p\rightarrow\infty
under a certain conjecture. We also relate this conjecture to certain uniform distribution properties of a vector-valued sequence.

Introduction

The
p
-adic valuation of integer sequences is a fundamental concept in number theory, providing insights into the divisibility properties of integers with respect to a prime number
p
. The
p
-adic valuation

\nu_{p}(n)
of an integer
n
is defined as the largest exponent
e
such that
p^{e}
divides
n
for a prime number
p
.
Historically, the concept of
p
-adic valuation stems from the work of Hensel
[
6
]
, who introduced
p
-adic numbers in 1897. This development allowed mathematicians to study numbers and their divisibility in a local sense, focusing on individual primes. Over time, the theory of
p
-adic numbers and valuations was expanded by E. Artin, Grothendieck, and others, making it a crucial tool in areas such as Diophantine equations and algebraic geometry.

One of the first and important results in the study of
p
-adic valuations is Legendre’s formula
[
7
]
for the
p
-adic valuation of
n!
. For a prime number
p
, the formula is given by:

\nu_{p}(n!)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{p^{k}}\right\rfloor,

where
\lfloor x\rfloor
is the greatest integer less than or equal to
x
. This sums the number of multiples of
p,p^{2},p^{3},\dots
less than or equal to
n
, counting the number of times
p
divides
n!
. This formula has important applications in number theory and combinatorics, particularly in understanding binomial coefficients and their divisibility by primes.

The work presented by Byrnes et al.
[
3
]
studies the
2
-adic valuation of
n^{2}-a
. It is shown that
\nu_{2}(n^{2}-a)
has a simple closed form (see
[
2
,
4
]
for a detailed discussion of the definition of a closed form) when
a\not\equiv 4,7\mod 8
. For these two remaining cases, the valuation cannot be described in a simple form. However, it can be fully described in a rule-based framework called the
valuation tree
.

These examples form part of a general project, with several notable contributions made by Victor Moll
[
3
,
8
]
and his coauthors to describe the
p
-adic valuations
\nu_{p}(x_{n})
,
for any given sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of integers. In the work
[
10
]
by the second author, the valuations
\nu_{p}(f(m,n))
of a two-dimensional sequence
\{f(m,n)\}_{m,n\in\mathbb{N}}
of integers are taken up,
where
f(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}[x,y]
is any polynomial in two variables with integer coefficients. It is fully described by generalizing the notion of the valuation tree.

In this work, we wish to understand the distribution of the
p
-adic valuations
\nu_{p}(f(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{k}))
as
m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{k}
vary over
\mathbb{N}
for a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},x_{2},

\ldots,

x_{k}]
. Let us first discuss the case
k=1
of a polynomial in one variable.

Let
f(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
have degree
d
and non-zero discriminant. First, we note that points
n
where
f
has non-zero valuation
\nu_{p}(f(n))>0
follow a periodic pattern with period
p
, as
\nu_{p}(f(x))>0\Leftrightarrow\nu_{p}(f(x+p))>0
. Hence, every interval of length
p
, in particular the intervals
\{kp,kp+1,\ldots,kp+p-1\}
, will contain the same number of points of non-zero valuation and this number is the cardinality of the solution set of
f(x)=0
over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

The situation changes when we consider the points of valuation
\nu_{p}(f(n))>1
. In the first place, the pattern is still periodic with period
p^{2}
. However, not all intervals of length
p
contain the same number of points where
\nu_{p}(f(n))>1
. In particular, at least
p-d
intervals
\{kp,kp+1,\ldots,kp+p-1\}
with
k=0,1,\ldots,p-1
do not contain any points where
\nu_{p}(f(n))>1
. This is because, by Corollary
1
, the total number of points where
\nu_{p}(f(n))>1
in the set
\{0,1,\ldots,p^{2}-1\}
is at most
d
.
Hence, as
p\rightarrow\infty
, the total number of points
n
of valuation
\nu_{p}(f(n))>1
in an interval
\{kp,kp+1,\ldots,kp+p-1\}
chosen uniformly at random from
k=0,1,\ldots,p-1
is zero with probability tending to
1
.

Theorem 1
.

Let

be a polynomial with no solutions to the set of equations

\lambda=1

We note that the condition on the polynomial

mentioned in Theorem

is satisfied by the polynomials mentioned in Figures

To obtain a sufficient condition on

Preliminaries

We start by studying the cardinality of the set
\{x:\nu_{p}(g(x))>1,0\leq x<p^{2}\}
for a given polynomial
g(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
p
be a prime number, let
g(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
, and let
r\in\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}
not be a common solution to equations
g(r)\equiv 0\pmod{p}\text{ and }g^{\prime}(r)\equiv 0\pmod{p}
. Then the cardinality of the set
\{k\in\mathbb{F}_{p}:\nu_{p}(g(r+kp))>1\}
is at most
1
.

Proof.

We note that

g(r+kp)\equiv g(r)+kpg^{\prime}(r)\pmod{p^{2}}.

(5)

If
g(r)\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}
, then by (
5
), there are no solutions to
g(r+kp)\equiv 0\pmod{p^{2}}
.
On the other hand, if
g(r)\equiv 0\pmod{p}
and
g^{\prime}(r)\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}
, then (
5
) provides a unique solution to
g(r+kp)\equiv 0\pmod{p^{2}}
, where
k\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
. ∎

Corollary 1
.

Let
g(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
have degree
d
and non-zero discriminant
\Delta
. Then the cardinality of the set
S=\{x:\nu_{p}(g(x))>1,\ 0\leq x<p^{2}\}
is at most
d
for sufficiently large prime numbers
p
.

Proof.

First, we note that for
p
sufficiently large, we have
\Delta\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}
, as
\Delta
is a non-zero integer (since it is a polynomial in the coefficients of
g
). Hence, there are no repeated roots of
g
in any algebraically closed extension of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. As a result, there are no solutions to
g(r)\equiv 0\pmod{p}
and
g^{\prime}(r)\equiv 0\pmod{p}
in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

Thus, the conditions of Proposition
1
hold for all
r\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Now, from the proof of Proposition
1
, it is clear that there are solutions to
g(r+kp)\equiv 0\pmod{p^{2}}
only if
r
is a root of
g(r)\equiv 0\pmod{p}
. Hence, there are at most
d
such roots
r\in\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}
to consider, and at most one translate
r+kp
belongs to the set
S
. Thus, the cardinality of
S
is at most
d
.
∎

f(r,s)+kpf_{x}(r,s)+lpf_{y}(r,s)\equiv 0\pmod{p^{2}}.

3
Indicator random variables for valuation greater than
1

We shall assume for the rest of this work that
p
is a prime number and that
f
satisfies the condition in Theorem
1
, namely that there are no solutions to the set of equations
f(x,y)\equiv 0\pmod{p},f_{x}(x,y)\equiv 0\pmod{p},
and
f_{y}(x,y)\equiv 0\pmod{p}
for all sufficiently large prime numbers
p
.

For each
(x,y)\in C_{f}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
, define a random variable
Y_{x,y}:\mathbb{F}_{p}\times\mathbb{F}_{p}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
as

Y_{x,y}(k,l)=\begin{cases}1,&\nu_{p}(f(x+kp,y+lp))>1,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

In other words,
Y_{x,y}
is an indicator function on the translates of
(x,y)
where
f
has
p
-adic valuation greater than 1. For all points
(x,y)\in C_{f}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
, at least one of
f_{x}(x,y)
or
f_{y}(x,y)
is non-zero in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
by the assumption on
f
. Hence, using Proposition
2
, we infer that the probability mass function of
Y_{x,y}
is

P(Y_{x,y}=i)=\begin{cases}1/p,&i=1,\\
(p-1)/p,&i=0.\end{cases}

(14)

Note that
Y_{x,y}
has the following properties:

(i)

The expectation
E[Y_{x,y}]=1/p.

(ii)

The variance
\operatorname{Var}(Y_{x,y})=\frac{p-1}{p^{2}}
.

(iii)

The
r^{\mathrm{th}}
moment about the origin
\mu_{r}^{\prime}=1/p
.

(iv)

By Chebyshev’s inequality

P(|Y_{x,y}-1/p|\geq\epsilon)\leq\dfrac{p-1}{\epsilon^{2}p^{2}},

for any
\epsilon>0
.

For any pair of points
(x,y)
and
(r,s)\in C_{f}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
that satisfy the conditions of Proposition
3
, the joint probability mass function of
Y_{x,y},Y_{r,s}
is

P(Y_{x,y}=i,Y_{r,s}=j)=\begin{cases}1/p^{2},&i=j=1,\\
(p-1)/p^{2},&i=1,j=0\text{ or }i=0,j=1,\\
(p-1)^{2}/p^{2},&i=j=0.\end{cases}

Clearly
P(Y_{x,y}=i,Y_{r,s}=j)=P(Y_{x,y}=i)P(Y_{r,s}=j)
for all
i
and
j
, as (
14
) holds for both points. Hence,
Y_{x,y}
and
Y_{r,s}
are independent random variables.

Similarly, the joint probability mass function for
Y_{x,y},Y_{r,s}
where the pair of points satisfy the conditions of Proposition
4
is

P(Y_{x,y}=i,Y_{r,s}=j)=\begin{cases}0,&i=j=1,\\
1/p,&i=1,j=0\text{ or }i=0,j=1,\\
(p^{2}-2p)/p^{2},&i=j=0.\end{cases}

in the case
r=2
. In the other case
r=1
, we have

P(Y_{x,y}=i,Y_{r,s}=j)=\begin{cases}1/p,&i=j=1,\\
0,&i=1,j=0\text{ or }i=0,j=1,\\
(p^{2}-p)/p^{2},&i=j=0.\end{cases}

Clearly, neither of these cases describe independent random variables. However, the correlation coefficient is
-1/(p-1)
for the case
r=2
and
1
for the case
r=1
. Hence, the random variables are close to being uncorrelated when
r=2
, but display a linear relationship when
r=1
.

For ease of notation, we now fix any order on
C_{f}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
for the rest of this article and denote by
Y_{i}
the random variable corresponding to the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
point.

Recall from Section
1
that
m_{k}(f,p)
denotes the number of element-wise distinct
k
-tuples of points on
C_{f}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
whose associated matrix
B_{k}(\mathbf{P})
, given by (
2
) has rank
2
with the first two columns of
B_{k}(\mathbf{P})
being linearly independent. Let
o_{k}(f,p)
denote the number of element-wise distinct
k
-tuples where
B_{k}(\mathbf{P})
has rank
1
with at least one among the first two columns non-zero.

Proposition 5
.

For
k\geq 1
, we have

\sum_{l_{1}\neq l_{2}\neq\cdots\neq l_{k}}E[Y_{l_{1}}Y_{l_{2}}\cdots Y_{l_{k}}]=m_{k}(f,p)/p^{2}+o_{k}(f,p)/p.

(15)

Proof.

For any element-wise distinct tuple
l_{1},l_{2},\ldots,l_{k}
, it is easy to see that
P(Y_{l_{1}}=Y_{l_{2}}=\cdots=Y_{l_{k}}=1)=n/p^{2}
where
n
is the number of solutions
(i,j)
of the equation

B_{k}(\mathbf{P})\begin{pmatrix}i\\
j\\
1\end{pmatrix}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{pmatrix}\pmod{p}.

(16)

In the case where the rank of
B_{k}(\mathbf{P})
is
2
and the first two columns are linearly independent, there is a unique solution of (
16
) and hence
n=1
. In the case where the rank of
B_{k}(\mathbf{P})
is
1
with at least one among the first two columns non-zero, there are
n=p
distinct solutions of (
16
). The case where the rank of
B_{k}(\mathbf{P})
is
0
is ruled out by the condition on
f
in Theorem
1
.

The remaining cases are those where the rank of
B_{k}(\mathbf{P})
is either
3
, or
2
with the first two columns linearly dependent, or
1
with the first two columns zero. It can be seen that there are no solutions of (
16
) in either of these remaining cases.

Since
E[Y_{l_{1}}Y_{l_{2}}\cdots Y_{l_{k}}]=P(Y_{l_{1}}=Y_{l_{2}}=\cdots=Y_{l_{k}}=1)
, equation (
15
) follows.
∎

Corollary 4
.

For
k\geq 1
, assuming that Conjecture
1
holds, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{l_{1}\neq l_{2}\neq\cdots\neq l_{k}}E[Y_{l_{1}}Y_{l_{2}}\cdots Y_{l_{k}}]=1

Proof.

Recall that
m_{1}(f,p)=0
trivially and
o_{1}(f,p)=m
. Hence, by the Hasse-Weil bounds
[
11
]
, we get
m_{1}(f,p)/p^{2}\rightarrow 0
and
o_{1}(f,p)/p\rightarrow 1
as

. Using this in equation (

), we get the required result for

.

Also, for

, we note that

is bounded by a constant depending on

. This is because, for

, we can bound

by the number of solutions of the following system of

\displaystyle f(x,y)\equiv f(r,s)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{p};

\displaystyle f_{x}(x,y)f_{y}(r,s)-f_{x}(r,s)f_{y}(x,y)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{p};

\displaystyle f_{x}(x,y)f(r,s)-f_{x}(r,s)f(x,y)

and Bezout’s theorem gives a bound on this number depending only on

and independent of

. For the case

, there are more equations than there are unknowns and, in general, there are no solutions. In either case,

∎

4
Uniform distribution results

We start by describing the idea of uniform distribution of the sequence
\mathcal{F}_{p}
(defined in (
4
)) in
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{3}
, namely that for every set
E\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{3}
, the proportion of points of
\mathcal{F}_{p}
that lie in
E
is approximated by the proportion of
|E|
to
|\mathbb{F}_{p}^{3}|
for
p
sufficiently large, viz.

\frac{|\mathcal{F}_{p}\cap E|}{m}\approx\frac{|E|}{p^{3}},\quad p\rightarrow\infty.

(17)

We shall use several measures to quantify the deviation from uniform distribution, also called discrepancy in the literature (see
[
5
]
for an excellent exposition) and define precise versions of uniform distribution.

At the outset, we describe the version of uniform distribution that provably implies Conjecture
1
. For that, we give the following definition of discrepancy.

\mathcal{D}_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{p})=\sup_{E\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{3}}\left|\frac{|\mathcal{F}_{p}\cap E|}{|E|}\frac{p^{3}}{m}-1\right|.

(18)

Using this, we formulate the following conjecture, which implies the uniform distribution of
\mathcal{F}_{p}
as described in (
17
).

Conjecture 2
.

For
p\rightarrow\infty
, we have
\mathcal{D}_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{p})\rightarrow 0
.

We now give the proof of Conjecture
1
assuming this version of uniform distribution.

Proposition 6
.

Conjecture
2
implies Conjecture
1
.

Proof.

We start by noting that Conjecture
2
can be applied to any independent choice of
k
vectors from
\mathcal{F}_{p}
. More precisely, Conjecture
2
implies

\sup_{E\subseteq(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{3})^{k}}\left|\frac{|\mathcal{F}_{p}^{k^{\prime}}\cap E|}{|E|}\frac{p^{3k}}{m(m-1)\ldots(m-k+1)}-1\right|\rightarrow 0,

(19)

as
p\rightarrow\infty
where
\mathcal{F}_{p}^{k^{\prime}}
denotes the element-wise distinct
k
-tuples of elements of
\mathcal{F}_{p}
.

Applying (
19
) to
E=\{(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k})\in(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{3})^{k}|\ \textit{associated matrix has rank }2

and first two columns linearly independent
\}
gives

\left|\frac{m_{k}(f,p)}{|E|}\frac{p^{3k}}{m(m-1)\cdots(m-k+1)}-1\right|\rightarrow 0.

Now
|E|=p^{3}(p^{3}-p)(p^{2}-2)(p^{2}-3)\cdots(p^{2}-k+1)
and
m/p\rightarrow 1
as
p\rightarrow\infty
by the Hasse-Weil bounds
[
11
]
. Hence, we get (as
p\rightarrow\infty
)

\left|\frac{m_{k}(f,p)}{p^{2}}-1\right|\rightarrow 0,

which is the same as (
3

∎

For comparison with Conjecture
2

3

\mathcal{F}_{p}
scaled down by a factor of

in each component, which we denote

\Delta_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{p})=\sup_{E\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{3}}\left|\frac{|\mathcal{F}_{p}\cap E|}{m}-\frac{|E|}{p^{3}}\right|.

(20)

We now state the following conjecture, which is another version of uniform distribution as described in (
17
).

Conjecture 3
.

For
p\rightarrow\infty
, we have
\Delta_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{p})\rightarrow 0
.

It can be seen that Conjecture
2
implies Conjecture
3
, as
\Delta_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{p})\leq\mathcal{D}_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{p})
. It is also easy to see that Conjecture
3
gives

as

. However, it is not yet clear whether this suffices to prove Conjecture
1
in its entirety.

On the other hand, we note the connection of Conjecture

with exponential sums by recalling the Erdös-Turan-Koksma inequality (Theorem 1.21,
[

]
).

Proposition 7
.

[

There exists a constant

such that, for any positive integer

, the following bound holds

\Delta_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{p})\leq C\left(\frac{1}{L}+\frac{1}{m}\sum_{0<|\mathbf{a}|\leq L}\frac{1}{r(\mathbf{a})}\left|\sum_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{F}_{p}}e_{p}(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{v})\right|\right),

,

and

.

We finally state the following conjecture on exponential sums which appears to be obtainable along the same lines as Bombieri
[

.

Conjecture 4

, the following bound holds

Proof of Theorem

We start by noting that the random variable
X(k,l)
defined in (
1
), representing the total number of points in
C_{f}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
with valuation greater than
1
in a random
p\times p
square, has the following expression.

X(k,l)=\sum_{(x,y)\in C_{f}(\mathbb{F}_{p})}Y_{x,y}(k,l)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}Y_{i}(k,l).

Recall that
m=|C_{f}(\mathbb{F}_{p})|
.

By the properties of the random variables
Y_{x,y}
discussed in Section
3
, we have

\displaystyle E[X]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}E[Y_{i}]=m/p.

In general for
k\geq 1

\displaystyle E[X^{k}]

\displaystyle=E\big[\big(\sum_{l=1}^{m}Y_{l}\big)^{k}\big]=E\big[\big(\sum_{l_{1},l_{2},\cdots,l_{k}=1}^{m}Y_{l_{1}}Y_{l_{2}}\ldots Y_{l_{k}}\big)\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}S(k,i)\sum_{l_{1}\neq l_{2}\neq\cdots\neq l_{i}}E[Y_{l_{1}}Y_{l_{2}}\ldots Y_{l_{i}}],

(21)

where
l_{i}\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}
and
S(k,i)
is the Stirling number of the second kind. This is because for each element-wise distinct tuple
(l_{1},l_{2},\ldots,l_{i})
in the last sum, the contribution from each product, where only the variables
Y_{l_{1}},Y_{l_{2}},\ldots,Y_{l_{i}}
appear, is the value
E[Y_{l_{1}}Y_{l_{2}}\ldots Y_{l_{i}}]
. Since the positions where these variables appear, form a partition of
\{1,2,\cdots,k\}
into
i
parts, we get
S(k,i)
many such products for each
(l_{1},l_{2},\ldots,l_{i})
.

Thus, from (
21
) and Corollary
4
(assuming Conjecture
1
as in the theorem), we get the following limit for the
k^{\mathrm{th}}
moment
E[X^{k}]
:

\lim_{p\to\infty}E[X^{k}]=\sum_{i=1}^{k}S(k,i),

which is indeed the
k^{\mathrm{th}}
moment of the Poisson distribution with parameter
\lambda=1
(see
[
9
]
). In other words, the moment generating function of
X
,
M_{X}(t)
, converges to

\lim_{p\to\infty}M_{X}(t)=\exp(e^{t}-1).

Hence,
X
converges in distribution to a Poisson random variable with parameter
\lambda=1
as
p\rightarrow\infty
.
