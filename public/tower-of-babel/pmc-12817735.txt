Benefits of hip arthroscopy surgery for soccer players: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Wenbo Zhao, Zihan Zhou, Yang Li, Changquan Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe complex and dynamic biomechanics of soccer significantly increase the susceptibility of the hip joint to injury. While hip arthroscopy is a valuable diagnostic and therapeutic modality that facilitates an expedited return to play for athletes, the specific extent of its clinical benefit for soccer players requires further evaluation.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted. Embase, Pubmed, ScienceDirect, and Wiley Online were selected as search databases. Case series related to “soccer” and “hip arthroscopy” were retrieved. After screening, Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) tools was used to assess the risk of bias. The differences before and after treatment in modified Hip Harris Score (mHHS) scores, Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores, Activities of Daily Living (ADL) scores, and Return to Play (RTP) rates were evaluated.ResultsThis study included 12 case series studies into quantitative analysis, with a total of 361 soccer players. Among them, eight with high-quality and four with moderate quality. The meta-analysis results showed that after receiving hip arthroscopy, patients’ mHHS scores increased (MD = 28.49, 95%CI [19.23; 37.75], Z = 6.03, P < 0.0001), VAS scores decreased (MD=-4.91, 95%CI [-7.02; -2.80], Z=-4.57, P < 0.0001), and ADL scores increased (MD = 23.59, 95%CI [18.04; 29.14], Z = 8.33, P < 0.0001). Soccer players who underwent hip arthroscopy surgery had an RTP of Proportion = 0.910, 95%CI [0.822; 0.974], a high-level performance RTP ratio of Proportion = 0.71, 95%CI [0.40; 0.90], and a mean return to play time of 6.34, 95%CI [4.50; 8.18] months.ConclusionAmong soccer players with hip injuries, hip arthroscopy surgery is associated with improved hip function, reduced pain levels, enhanced ability to perform daily activities, and a high return-to-sport rate.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-09401-5.

Many competitive sports demand complex and dynamic hip joint activities, including short-distance running, twisting, kicking, and other high-stress maneuvers. These repetitive and intense movements subject the hip joint to substantial mechanical stress, resulting in a high incidence of hip injuries among athletes [1]. The most common injuries include intra-articular conditions such as femoroacetabular impingement syndrome (FAI). Additionally, extra-articular conditions such as rectus femoris injuries, rectus femoris tendinitis, and symptomatic osteitis pubis are also prevalent [2, 3]. Athletes with hip injuries frequently present with severe pain, particularly during hip joint extension, flexion, internal rotation, or abduction. The pain, which may be localized to the anterior, lateral, or posterior hip, often limits the range of motion and can significantly impair athletic performance. In severe cases, it may result in disability. Without accurate diagnosis and timely intervention, these conditions may lead to chronic dysfunction and, in some cases, premature retirement from competitive sports [4, 5]. A retrospective epidemiological study by Gerhardt MB et al. [6] surveyed 95 elite soccer players and found that 75% of male players and 50% of female players had radiological hip abnormalities. Johnson et al.‘s research [7] also suggested that adolescents participating in high-level soccer during bone immaturity may experience changes in the morphology of the proximal femur, which increases the risk of hip pain. These studies suggest that hip abnormalities are prevalent among soccer players but are easily overlooked in the early asymptomatic stage.

Hip arthroscopy is a key treatment modality for hip injuries, offering advantages such as minimal invasiveness and rapid recovery, making it particularly suitable for managing hip joint injuries in athletes [8]. The treatment approach should be tailored to the specific characteristics of the sports injury. Detailed assessment of the acetabular morphology, degree of degeneration and hyperplasia, and the presence of labral cysts guides the surgical plan, which may include femoral head-neck reconstruction and acetabular labrum repair [9]. A multicenter randomized controlled trial by Palmer AJR et al. [10] demonstrated that, compared to physical therapy, hip arthroscopy surgery resulted in greater improvements in hip function scores and daily living indices in patients with FAI. Hip arthroscopy is particularly well-suited for athletes as it can effectively shorten recovery time and help them return to play [11].

Although existing systematic review and meta-analysis [12] has demonstrated that hip arthroscopy surgery benefits patients with FAI from all spectrums, such as basketball, rugby, American football, hockey, still there is a lack of specific evaluation focused on individual sport, particularly soccer. To address this gap, we conducted this meta-analysis to evaluate the benefits of hip arthroscopy surgery for hip injuries in soccer players.

Materials and methods

Protocol

The process of this systematic review and meta-analysis follows the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta Analyses) guidelines [13]. This review was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251163044). The protocol is available at the PROSPERO website.

Databases and search strategy

We selected Embase, Pubmed, Science Direct, and Wiley online as the main search databases, and supplemented them with Google Scholar. The search strategy used free-text terms: “hip arthroscopy”, “hip arthroscopic surgery”, “arthroscopic resection”, “soccer”, “football”, “elite soccer”, “soccer athletes”, “association football”. Studies published before July 15, 2025 were eligible.

Study inclusion criteria

All included studies were case series studies. Single-case studies were excluded due to the small sample size. Meeting notes, investigations, evidence summaries, and letters that do not provide data were also excluded. The participants should be soccer players. Studies on hockey, American football, Australian football, rugby, basketball, baseball, tennis, and golf will be excluded. All study subjects should undergo hip arthroscopy surgery, with their disease diagnosis types of FAI, osteitis pubis, or acetabular ossification. Research should report detailed surgical procedures, postoperative follow-up, and detailed preoperative and postoperative outcome data.

Screening of the studies

After performing the literature search, the results were imported into Zotero software (v6.0) to identify and exclude duplicates based on titles, authors, and publication years. Two researchers independently screened the titles and abstracts according to the inclusion criteria. In cases of disagreement, the inclusion or exclusion of specific studies was resolved through discussion. Once the preliminary selection was finalized, the full texts of the selected studies were retrieved from online sources and reviewed in detail. Studies that did not report relevant data or outcome measures of interest were excluded during this phase.

Quality evaluation

The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) tool for case series studies [14] was used to assess the quality of the included studies. This tool consists of 10 items evaluating aspects such as sampling, patient characteristics, data collection, and statistical analysis. Each item is rated as “yes,” “no,” “unclear or not applicable.” Studies in which all items are rated as “yes” are considered to have a low risk of bias and high quality. If any item is rated as “no,” the study is deemed to have a high risk of bias and low quality. If any item is rated as “unclear” or “not applicable,” the study is considered to have a moderate risk of bias and moderate quality. The reviewers independently assessed the risk of bias using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist for case series. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Data extraction

Two researchers independently extracted literature data, including information on authors, publication dates, number of participant cases, participant age, gender ratio, diagnosis, surgical characteristics, follow-up time, outcomes. After completing the data extraction, the two researchers cross checked each other’s results and discussed the differences that arose before finalizing.

Outcomes

Outcomes related to the effectiveness of hip arthroscopy were collected, including pre- and postoperative mHHS, VAS, and ADL scores, overall return to play (RTP) rate, high-level RTP rate, and the average time to return to competition. A high-level RTP rate refers to RTP with high-level performance, which is defined by athletes playing at a level of soccer at least as high as, or even higher than that before surgery.

Effect size pooling

The effect sizes of mHHS score, VAS score, and ADL score were reported as mean difference (MD) and 95% confidence interval (CI) between postoperative and preoperative levels. The data included in the studies were pooled using the “meta” package’s meta-analysis function in R language. RTP ratio and high-level RTP ratio were reported based on proportions by using the “metaprop” function of the “meta” package. The average time for returning to the competition was reported by using raw or untransformed mean (MRAW) and its 95% CI. If the heterogeneity between studies is statistically significant, the random effects model’s Dersimonian-Laird method would be used for calculation. The pooling results were displayed in a forest plot. Subgroup analysis was carried out to explore the source of heterogeneity.

Influence analysis

The “InfluenceAnalysis” function of the “dmetar” package was used to conduct influence analysis on the pooling results.

Publication bias assessment

The “funnel” function of the “meta” package was used to assess publication bias for the pooled results, and the findings were presented in a funnel plot.”

Statistical analysis

Data statistics were completed by using R language (version 4.4.1) under Rstudio (version 2024.04.2 + 764, Posit Software, PBC). Unless specified, P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Literature search and selection results

Figure 1 shows the study selection process. Initially, a total of 519 articles were identified, and after deduplication and screening, 11 articles were included for quantitative analysis. We also initially retrieved 78 articles from Google Scholar, and after exclusion, one article was included for quantitative analysis. A total of 12 articles [15–26] were included in the final analysis. In the screening process, we excluded all studies with non-soccer subjects. In Karlsson et al.‘s study [27], high-level athletes who received hip arthroscopy due to FAI were followed up for up to 10 years, but it was excluded as the study included non-soccer athletes. Owens JS et al. [28] reported the benefits of hip arthroscopy for basketball players with FAI patients but it was excluded as the study subjects were not soccer players. Funasaki H et al. [29] reported a case of a single soccer athlete receiving hip arthroscopy in their study, but it was excluded due to the small sample size. In the study by Clapp IM et al. [30], a comparison was made between the effects of hip arthroscopy on professional and non-professional athletes. However, this study was excluded due to due to its non-case series design and lack of useful outcome data. Although Menge TJ et al. [31] reported the therapeutic effect of hip arthroscopy on FAI patients in football athletes, we found after reading the full text that this type of football is American football, not soccer, and therefore it was excluded.

Effect size pooling results

Comparison of mHHS scores before and after hip arthroscopy surgery for soccer players

Eight studies [16, 19, 21–26] reported the comparison of mHHS scores of patients before and after surgery, and there was statistical heterogeneity between the studies (I2 = 91.6%, P < 0.0001). Using a random effects model, meta-analysis showed that the mHHS scores of patients who received hip arthroscopy improved (MD = 28.49, 95%CI [19.23; 37.75], Z = 6.03, P < 0.0001), as shown in Fig. 2A and B for details.

The MD of pre- and postoperative mHHS scores of soccer athletes. A Forest map. B Drapery diagram. MD > 0 indicates a result in favor of postoperative scores. MD, mean difference. FAI, Femoroacetabular impingement

We divided the eight studies into two subgroups, FAI/Non-FAI, based on the disease diagnosis type, and there was no significant difference between the subgroups (P = 0.62), indicating that disease type is not a source of heterogeneity (Fig. 2A).

Comparison of VAS scores before and after hip arthroscopy surgery for soccer players

Six studies [16, 20, 23–26] reported the comparison of VAS scores of patients before and after surgery, and there was statistical heterogeneity between the studies (I2 = 97.8%, P < 0.0001). Using a random effects model, meta-analysis showed that the VAS scores of patients who received hip arthroscopy were significantly reduced (MD=-4.91, 95%CI [-7.02; -2.80], Z=-4.57, P < 0.0001), as shown in Fig. 3 for details.

A total of 11 articles [15–21, 23–26] reported the RTP rate after surgery, and there was no statistical heterogeneity among the articles (I2 = 49.6%, P = 0.03). Meta analysis showed that the RTP of soccer players after hip arthroscopy surgery was (Proportion = 0.910, 95%CI [0.822; 0.974]), as shown in Fig. 5A for details. Discussion

In this study, 12 case series studies were included, involving 361 soccer athletes who underwent hip arthroscopy. The results demonstrated significant improvements in the mHHS and ADL scores, accompanied by a significant reduction in VAS pain scores following surgery. These findings indicate that hip arthroscopy can markedly enhance hip joint function, alleviate pain, and improve the daily quality of life in soccer players.

Hip arthroscopy is primarily indicated for structural intra-articular pathology such as femoroacetabular impingement (FAI), labral tears, acetabular rim fractures or ossifications (Os acetabuli), and symptomatic osteitis pubis that does not respond to conservative treatment. These conditions are prevalent among soccer players due to frequent pivoting, sprinting, cutting, and high-load rotational movements. Arthroscopy provides several advantages, including minimally invasive access, the ability to address both bony morphology and soft-tissue pathology in the same procedure, reduced postoperative pain, and an earlier return to sport compared with open surgery [31].

Despite these benefits, athletes with extensive chondral damage, substantial deformities requiring large osteoplasty, or coexisting extra-articular pathology may require a longer rehabilitation period and have less predictable return-to-play outcomes. As Beck et al. [32] demonstrated, morphological variations such as the size and location of cam deformity significantly influence intra-articular damage patterns, which in turn may affect postoperative recovery following hip arthroscopy. Additionally, Bolia et al. [33] noted that differences in sport-specific loading patterns may contribute to variability in functional recovery and return-to-sport timelines.

Hip arthroscopy has demonstrated surgical efficacy in several clinical studies. In the study by Mercurio et al. [34], patients who underwent hip arthroscopy for labral repair and femoral osteochondroplasty were followed for at least five years, and the findings showed significant symptom improvement and high postoperative satisfaction. Similarly, Filan et al. [35] reported that among 386 athletes, hip arthroscopy resulted in reductions in groin and hip pain scores, with additional improvement in activity-related limping.

There are currently some meta-analyses on hip arthroscopy for athletes from all spectrums, but there is no meta-analysis specifically for soccer players. In Minkara et al.‘s study [12], 1,911 athletes were included, and after hip arthroscopy treatment, the overall mHHS score increased by 24.43, 95%CI [16.62, 29.25] compared to before treatment, ADL score increased by 23.65, 95%CI [18.51, 28.79] compared to before treatment, while the pain score decreased by -4.48, 95%CI [-5.05, -3.92]. This is very close to the results of this study, where mHHS was 28.50 [19.23; 37.75], ADL score was 23.59 [18.04; 29.14], and VAS score was − 4.91 [-7.02; -2.80]. However, the study [12] included a mixed population of athletes from various sports, such as basketball, field hockey, American football, and baseball. In contrast, this study focused exclusively on soccer players. This distinction highlights that soccer players with hip injuries experience the same significant benefits from hip arthroscopy as athletes from other sports. Moreover, due to the narrower focus, more homogeneous study population, and reduced heterogeneity in this analysis, the findings are more reliable and specific to soccer players.

In addition to team-based cutting sports, studies have also examined return-to-sport patterns in athletics, including sprinters, jumpers, and endurance runners. Findings from the study [33] indicate that athletes in running-dominant sports may show comparable or slightly prolonged RTP timelines due to repetitive high-load hip demands. Integrating these results, soccer players—which require both cutting mechanics and endurance running—demonstrate RTP patterns similar to those of athletes in both cutting and athletics-related sports.

Soccer players often face limited athletic careers due to age constraints, making rapid recovery after surgery critical for returning to high-level competition. In this study, 11 articles reported the return-to-play (RTP) rate of athletes following hip arthroscopy. The overall RTP rate for soccer players was 0.910 (95% CI [0.822, 0.974]), slightly higher than the 88.9% reported in reference [33], which included athletes from various competitive sports. Notably, this study provided a novel report on the RTP rate at a high-performance level, with a proportion of 0.71 (95% CI [0.40, 0.90]) in soccer. This indicates that 71% of the athletes were able to return to their original high level of performance after surgery. These findings suggest that while hip arthroscopy effectively repairs hip joint damage and improves daily functional abilities, it may not fully mitigate the decline in athletic performance or prevent the career-shortening effects associated with hip injuries. This underscores the importance of early and regular screening for hip joint variations in soccer players. Proactive participation in hip arthroscopy evaluations and timely correction of abnormalities can help prevent the progression of injuries, preserve athletic performance, and extend players’ careers.

In this study, the average return-to-play time reported across eight studies was 6.34 months (95% CI [4.50, 8.18]), which is consistent with the findings of study [33]. The rehabilitation process following hip arthroscopy depends on the surgical approach and the patient’s individualized condition. Voight et al. [36] reported that for large-scale femoral head-neck or labral repair/reconstruction requiring the use of hip abduction braces, a longer period was needed for patients to return to play.

Although the findings of this study indicate that hip arthroscopy can benefit athletes with hip injuries, the success of the procedure is influenced by multiple factors. Rafols et al. [37] reported that, although no significant differences in clinical outcomes were observed between patients with labral valgus and those with labral tear following hip arthroscopy, appropriate management of this anatomical variation during surgery is critical for optimal results.

Our study demonstrated a high-level RTP of 71% in soccer players. Consistent with the observations in the study [33], this suggests that although a majority of athletes successfully resume high-level participation, a notable subset may still struggle to fully regain their preinjury competitive capacity.

Return to sport is further affected by factors such as age, pain severity, developmental abnormalities, and degenerative changes. As noted by Matache et al. [38], expert consensus remains lacking in key areas, including the need for postoperative hip bracing and the appropriate timing of RTP. Marom [39] also emphasized that reported RTP rates may be misleading, as many athletes return to participation without fully recovering their preinjury performance level. Therefore, the role of hip arthroscopy in soccer athletes requires further research and long-term evaluation.

In this study, all 12 included studies were case series. Influence analysis revealed that although the results from Ganal et al. [24] deviated from those of other studies, excluding this study did not significantly alter the overall pooled results, indicating that the findings of our meta-analysis are stable. However, the exclusive reliance on case series without control groups introduces potential implementation bias, contributing to the significant heterogeneity observed among the included studies. Although a multicentre randomized controlled trial by Palmer et al. [10] provided higher-level evidence for the treatment of FAI, the study population consisted of mixed-sport participants rather than focusing specifically on soccer players, and the number of available RCTs remains small. Consequently, there remains a lack of high-quality RCTs evaluating the benefits of hip arthroscopy specifically for soccer players.

In addition, the number of participants included in this meta-analysis was still too small. Moreover, this study did not evaluate some important indicators, such as the degree of change in a-angle, the incidence of surgical complications, the number of times the surgery participated in competitions, and the length of soccer career. Therefore, further in-depth exploration and analysis are still needed for research on this topic.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis included 361 soccer athletes from 12 articles. The results showed that after hip arthroscopy surgery, the hip function of soccer players improved, pain levels decreased, the ability to perform daily activities improved, and the return-to-play rate was high. However, only half of the participants achieved the pre-surgery exercise performance levels. Given the relatively small sample size and the fact that the included studies were case series, further investigation into this topic is warranted through larger randomized controlled trials.
