Intertwining operators beyond the Stark Effect
Luca Fanelli, Xiaoyan Su, Ying Wang, Junyong Zhang, Jiqiang Zheng

Abstract
. The main mathematical manifestation of the Stark effect in quantum mechanics is the shift and the formation of clusters of eigenvalues when a spherical Hamiltonian is perturbed by lower order terms. Understanding this mechanism turned out to be fundamental in the description of the large-time asymptotics of the associated Schrödinger groups and can be responsible for the lack of dispersion [ 14 , 16 , 17 ] . Recently, Miao, Su, and Zheng introduced in [ 31 ] a family of spectrally projected intertwining operators , reminiscent of the Kato’s wave operators, in the case of constant perturbations on the sphere (inverse-square potential), and also proved their boundedness in L p L^{p} . Our aim is to establish a general framework in which some suitable intertwining operators can be defined also for non constant spherical perturbations in space dimensions 2 and higher. In addition, we investigate the mapping properties between L p L^{p} -spaces of these operators. In 2D, we prove a complete result, for the Schrödinger Hamiltonian with a (fixed) magnetic potential an electric potential, both scaling critical, allowing us to prove dispersive estimates, uniform resolvent estimates, and L p L^{p} -bounds of Bochner–Riesz means. In higher dimensions, apart from recovering the example of inverse-square potential, we can conjecture a complete result in presence of some symmetries (zonal potentials), and open some interesting spectral problems concerning the asymptotics of eigenfunctions. Key Words: intertwining operator, electromagnetic field, Stark effect, uniform resolvent estimate, Bochner–Riesz means. AMS Classification: 35P25, 35A23, 35Q40.



1.
Introduction

In 1913, Johannes Stark observed a peculiar phenomenon now known as the Stark effect: under the influence of a non-constant external electric field, the energy levels of an atom shift and split into multiple lines, clustering into specific formations
[
34
]
.
This effect is analogous to the mechanism observed under a static external magnetic field, as first identified by Pieter Zeeman in 1896
[
46
]
.
Mathematical evidence of the above effect can be obtained by analyzing the spectrum of the spherical Schrödinger Hamiltonian,
-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
when perturbed by a lower-order external force.
Due to the significant experimental and theoretical implications, extensive literature has been devoted to the study of the spectrum of operators of the form
-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}+a(\theta)
, where
a:\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
This type of perturbation typically results in the formation of eigenvalue clusters around the unperturbed energy levels
j(j+d-2)
, with
j\in\mathbb{N}
, together with a uniform shift, provided that the potential is non-constant and satisfies certain symmetry requirements.
Some pioneering works, such as those by D. Gurarie
[
24
]
and by L. Thomas and S. Wassell
[
37
]
, have established this clustering behavior, with extensive references therein. When
d=2
(corresponding to a one-dimensional spherical problem), this clustering effect is nearly universal, requiring only minimal regularity conditions on the potentials (see
[
16
, Lemma 2.1]
for detailed asymptotics of eigenvalues and eigenfunctions.)

In the past decade, beginning with
[
14
]
, a deep connection has emerged between these spectral properties and the large-time asymptotics of scaling-invariant Schrödinger groups.
Broadly, the structure and separation of high-energy clusters, along with the asymptotic profiles of the associated eigenfunctions, are crucial in analyzing the high-energy band in the time-decay properties of these Schrödinger groups in the full space, as showed in several recent papers (see e.g.
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
22
,
23
,
32
]
).
A canonical model within this context is the Schrödinger Hamiltonian with an inverse-square potential
-\Delta+\frac{a}{|x|^{2}}
,
a
being a constant.
Restricting this model to
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
results in a constant perturbation of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on the sphere,
-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}+a
.
The large-time behavior of the propagator
\cramped{e^{it(-\Delta+\frac{a}{|x|^{2}})}}
, including decay and Strichartz estimates, is now well understood, particularly for dimensions
d=2
and
3
.
In this case, the spectral problem is straightforward as the spectrum only sees a uniform shift, without cluster formation.
Specifically, the spectrum of
-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}+a
is given by the numbers
j(j+d-2)+a
, with
j\in\mathbb{N}
, and the eigenfunctions are spherical harmonics, with shifted index.
This leads to a direct one-to-one correspondence between the unperturbed and perturbed eigenvalues, preserving their order and allowing the definition of some operators that map spectral projections before perturbation to their perturbed counterparts.
Miao, Su, and Zheng in
[
31
]
introduced such operators, which exhibit an intertwining property that allows to represent the perturbed functional calculus in terms of the unperturbed one.
This concept aligns with the classic wave operators introduced by T. Kato,
[
28
]
, whose
L^{p}
-boundedness properties have been extensively studied, due to their applications in dispersive estimates and scattering theory, in
[
9
,
11
,
12
,
39
,
40
,
41
,
42
,
43
,
44
,
45
]
and many other papers.

The objective of this manuscript is to establish a rigorous mathematical foundation for defining similar intertwining operators as those in
[
31
]
also in presence of cluster formations.
Extending this analysis beyond the Stark effect needs a precise combinatorial analysis of the energy levels and their associated eigenfunctions after the splitting. Once this structure is understood, we propose a general methodology to prove the
L^{p}
-boundedness of these operators.
This involves a novel discrete multiplier result with variable coefficients (Lemma
2.3
below) and detailed decay estimates of oscillatory integrals.

1.1.
Mathematical framework

Let us consider an electromagnetic Schrödinger operator on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 2
, of the form

\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},a}\coloneq\left(-i\nabla+\frac{\mathbf{A}(\theta)}{|x|}\right)^{2}+\frac{a(\theta)}{|x|^{2}},

(1.1)

where
\theta=\frac{x}{|x|}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
,
a\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{R})
and
\mathbf{A}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a transversal vector field,

\mathbf{A}(\theta)\cdot\theta=0\text{ for all }\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}.

(1.2)

The differential operator
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},a}
acts on complex-valued functions
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\})
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},a}f=-\Delta f+\frac{|\mathbf{A}(\theta)|^{2}+a(\theta)-i\operatorname{div}_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}{\mathbf{A}(\theta)}}{|x|^{2}}-2i\frac{\mathbf{A}(\theta)}{|x|}\cdot\nabla f,

where
\operatorname{div}_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}{\mathbf{A}(\theta)}
denotes the Riemannian divergence of
{\mathbf{A}}
on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
endowed with the standard metric.

In spherical coordinates
x=r\theta
,
r=|x|
,
\theta=\frac{x}{|x|}
,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},a}
can be written as

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},a}=-\partial_{r}^{2}-\frac{d-1}{r}\partial_{r}+\frac{L_{\mathbf{A},a}}{r^{2}},\end{split}

(1.3)

where the spherical operator
L_{\mathbf{A},a}
is defined by

\displaystyle L_{\mathbf{A},a}=(-i\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}+\mathbf{A})^{2}+a(\theta),

(1.4)

where
\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
is the spherical gradient on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
By standard spectral theoretical arguments, we see that the spectrum of
L_{\mathbf{A},a}
is purely discrete, and consists of a diverging sequence of real eigenvalues

\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{A},a)\uparrow+\infty
where each eigenvalue is repeated according to its finite multiplicity (see e.g.
[
21
]
).

Under the condition that

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A},a)\geq-\Big(\frac{d-2}{2}\Big)^{2},

(1.5)

the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},a}
can be understood as the Friedrichs’ extension of the natural quadratic form on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})

[
28
, Theorem VI.2.1]
, with the form domain

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},a})=\bigg\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{C}):\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigg\lvert\nabla f+i\frac{\mathbf{A}(\theta)}{|x|}f\bigg\lvert^{2}+\frac{a(\theta)}{|x|^{2}}|f|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x<\infty\bigg\}.

As explained above, the goal of this manuscript is to introduce some intertwining operators which enable us to reduce the functional calculus associated to
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},a}
to the one for the electric-free operator
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},0}
. Once such an object is available, its mapping properties between
L^{p}
-spaces will permit us to obtain relevant information about the large-time asymptotics of the Schrödinger group
e^{it\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},a}}
, as well as the resolvent of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},a}
, by inheriting these properties from
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},0}
.

When
d=2
, a fundamental example of a magnetic field in the above class is the so called
Aharonov–Bohm
potential (hereafter
\AB
), defined by

\AB(x):=\frac{\mathbf{A}(\theta)}{|x|},\qquad\mathbf{A}(\theta)=\alpha\left(-\frac{x_{2}}{|x|},\frac{x_{1}}{|x|}\right),\quad\alpha\in\mathbb{R}.

(Aharonov–Bohm)

In Aharonov and Bohm’s paper
[
1
]
, the phenomenon of scattering in regions in which the electromagnetic field is absent was predicted within the framework of Schrödinger dynamics. This phenomenon was later
incontrovertibly confirmed by experiments of Tonomura et al.
[
33
]
. The
\AB
model serves as a 2D idealization of a 3D model involving an infinitely long and thin solenoid carrying an electric current, which confines a magnetic field within its interior. Recently, experimental evidence of 2D quasiparticles, known as
anyons
, which naturally carry on an
\AB
-type magnetic field
[
2
]
, has reinforced the significance of this 2D model. Notably, the
\AB
Schrödinger Hamiltonian
\mathcal{L}_{\AB,0}
retains the same scaling invariance as the free operator
\mathcal{L}_{0,0}
, highlighting the scaling-critical nature of this perturbation.

In the context of zero-order perturbations, the canonical example in any dimension is the inverse-square potential operator
\mathcal{L}_{0,a}
where
\cramped{a\geq-\frac{(d-2)^{2}}{4}}
is a constant. Also in this case, we have that
\mathcal{L}_{0,a}
scales as
\mathcal{L}_{0,0}
.

Extensive research on such operators has focused on the validity of some families of estimates that characterize the Schrödinger equation as the prototypical dispersive PDE. Burq, Planchon, Stalker, and Tahvildar-Zadeh proved in
[
5
,
6
]
that the standard Strichartz estimates are valid in the presence of zero-order perturbations with the same scaling as the inverse-square. Later, in
[
14
,
16
]
it was shown that the decay estimate

\|e^{it\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},a}}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim|t|^{-1},

(1.6)

which implies Strichartz estimates by standard results, holds generally in 2D. In 3D, this estimate holds for the defocusing inverse-square potential case
a\geq 0
, but fails for the focusing case

-(d-2)^{2}/4\leq a<0

[
14
]
. The proofs in
[
14
,
16
]
rely on a detailed analysis of the Stark and Zeeman effects post-perturbation. These results have been later extended to other dispersive operators (see
[
19
,
20
,
23
,
32
]
and references therein).

This paper aims to address the spectral structure of operators
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},a}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},0}
for fixed
\mathbf{A}
, by developing a rigorous framework which may also hold in presence of cluster formations for defining intertwining operators under specific spectral conditions. Establishing such operators and bounding them in
L^{p}
-spaces will require careful spectral analysis, as well as some novel harmonic analytical tools which will come into play in the sequel.

1.2.
Spectral framework

The free spherical operator
L_{0,0}=-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
has purely discrete spectrum, given by the eigenvalues
\eta_{j}^{2}
where
\eta_{j}\coloneq\sqrt{j(j+d-2)}\geq 0
, each one with multiplicity
m_{j}\coloneq\frac{(d+j-1)!}{(d-1)!j!}=O(j^{d-2})
. Moreover, the usual spherical harmonics are the corresponding eigenfunctions. Having compact inverse, the operators
L_{\mathbf{A},0}
and
L_{\mathbf{A},a}
also have purely discrete spectrum with eigenvalues accumulating at infinity (see e.g.
[
14
]
).
The expected spectral picture for
L_{\mathbf{A},a}
after the Stark–Zeeman effect suggests to assume from now on that the following conditions hold for
L_{\mathbf{A},0}
and
L_{\mathbf{A},a}
:

Assumption 1.1
.

(1)

Asymptotics of clusters.
There exist
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and a constant
0<C_{\mathbf{A}}<1/2
, only depending on
\mathbf{A}
, such that for any
j\geq\ell
, the free eigenvalues
\eta_{j}^{2}=j(j+d-2)
split into clusters of eigenvalues of the operators
L_{\mathbf{A},0}
and
L_{\mathbf{A},a}
, each with exactly
m_{j}\coloneq\frac{(d+j-1)!}{(d-1)!j!}
eigenvalues (with possible repetitions), denoted by
\mu_{jk}^{2}
and
\nu_{jk}^{2}
,
k=1,\dots,m_{j}
, respectively, with the following localization property:

\displaystyle\eta_{j}+C_{\mathbf{A}}-\frac{1}{2}<\mu_{j1}\leq\mu_{j2}\leq\dots\leq\mu_{jm_{j}}\leq\eta_{j}+C_{\mathbf{A}}+\frac{1}{2},

\displaystyle\eta_{j}+C_{\mathbf{A}}-\frac{1}{2}<\nu_{j1}\leq\nu_{j2}\leq\dots\leq\nu_{jm_{j}}\leq\eta_{j}+C_{\mathbf{A}}+\frac{1}{2}.

The corresponding normalized eigenfunctions are denoted by
e_{jk}
and
\phi_{jk}

(k=1,\dots,m_{j})
, respectively, i.e. for all
j\geq\ell,
and all
1\leq k\leq m_{j}
,

L_{\mathbf{A},0}e_{jk}(\theta)=\mu_{jk}^{2}e_{jk}(\theta),\quad\text{and}\quad L_{\mathbf{A},a}\phi_{jk}(\theta)=\nu_{jk}^{2}\phi_{jk}(\theta).

(2)

Simultaneous completion.
There exists
\ell_{0}
(possibly zero) depending on
\ell
, a set
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1,2,\dots,\ell_{0}}
of normalized eigenfunctions
of
L_{\mathbf{A},0}
, and a set
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1,2,\dots,\ell_{0}}
of normalized eigenfunctions
of
L_{\mathbf{A},a}
,
with corresponding eigenvalues
\mu_{i}^{2},\nu_{i}^{2}\in[-\frac{(d-2)^{2}}{4},\eta_{\ell}+C_{\mathbf{A}}-\frac{1}{2}]
respectively, such that

\{e_{i}\}_{i=1,2,\dots,\ell_{0}}\cup\{e_{jk}\}_{j\geq\ell,k}
and
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1,2,\dots,\ell_{0}}\cup\{\phi_{jk}\}_{j\geq\ell,k}
are both bases of
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
.

In the above, we allow
\mu_{i}^{2},\nu_{i}^{2}<0
for
i<\ell_{0}
for notational convenience.

Remark 1.2
.

Assumption
1.1
(
1
) is describing the typical asymptotic spectral picture after the occurrence of the Stark–Zeeman effect. First we notice a shift of the
\eta_{j}^{2}
’s. This is due to the presence of
\mathbf{A}
and
a
, but asymptotically the dominant order of the shift is given by
\mathbf{A}
(see e.g.
[
16
, Lemma 2.1]
), and this is the reason why
C_{\mathbf{A}}
does not depend on
a
. Then the free eigenvalues split in a cluster of new eigenvalues, and the total multiplicity remains equal to
m_{j}
. This last fact is generic in dimension 2, and it’s known to occur under some suitable symmetry assumptions in higher dimensions.
Among the examples of potentials satisfying (
1
), we list:

•

for any
d\geq 2
, the inverse-square potential, namely
L_{\mathbf{A},a}
, with
a
constant;

•

in
d=2
, provided
\mathbf{A}\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
a\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{R})
, see Section
5
for details;

•

in
d\geq 3
, provided
\mathbf{A}=0
and
a
enjoys
zonal
symmetry
[
24
,
37
]
.

Remark 1.3
.

Assumption
1.1
(
2
) is about the low frequency part of the spectra: under this assumption, we can always complete the above families of eigenfunctions to orthonormal bases of
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
, by adding
exactly
the same number of low frequencies
j\leq\ell_{0}
in the two cases
L_{\mathbf{A},0}
and
L_{\mathbf{A},a}
. This is crucial in order to be able to give the definition of intertwining operators in Section
1.10
below. Although (
2
) could seem to be easily satisfied, we did not find in the literature any result along these lines. Hence we needed to perform a suitable analysis in order to give examples of potentials which fully satisfy Assumption
1.1
, see Section
5
.

From now on, we denote the index sets by

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\ell}\coloneq\{(j,k):j\geq\ell,\ 1\leq k\leq m_{j}\},\quad\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\perp}\coloneq\{1,\dots,\ell_{0}\},

so that
\{e_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\perp}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}
and
\{\phi_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in{\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\perp}}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}
are two bases of
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
; we will also write
\sum_{\alpha}
instead of
\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\perp}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}
and
\sum_{j,k}
to mean
\sum_{j=\ell}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{m_{j}}
when the intent is clear.

We now aim to introduce the spectral measures associated to
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},0}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},a}
. We first notice that under Assumption
1.1
,
we have the following two orthogonal decompositions of
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
:

\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})=\bigoplus_{\mathclap{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\perp}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}}\spn\{e_{\alpha}\}=\bigoplus_{\mathclap{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\perp}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}}\spn\{\phi_{\alpha}\}.

Setting

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}=\{f(r)e_{\alpha}(\theta)\mid f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+};r^{d-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r)\},\quad\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\perp}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\ell},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha}=\{f(r)\phi_{\alpha}(\theta)\mid f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+};r^{d-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r)\},\quad\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\perp}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\ell},

we get that

L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\perp}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\perp}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha}
.
In other words, any function
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
can be written as

f(x)=\sum_{\mathclap{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\perp}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}}f_{\alpha}(r)e_{\alpha}(\theta)=\sum_{\mathclap{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\perp}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}}\widetilde{f}_{\alpha}(r)\phi_{\alpha}(\theta)

where

f_{\alpha}(r)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}f(r,\theta)\bar{e}_{\alpha}(\theta)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta
,
and

\widetilde{f}_{\alpha}(r)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}f(r,\theta)\bar{\phi}_{\alpha}(\theta)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta
.

We now define the following projection operators to each subspace:

\begin{split}\mathbb{H}_{\alpha}&\coloneq\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},0}|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha}}=-\partial_{r}^{2}-\frac{d-1}{r}\partial_{r}+\frac{\mu_{\alpha}^{2}}{r^{2}},\quad\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\perp}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\ell},\\
\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}_{\alpha}&\coloneq\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},a}|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha}}=-\partial_{r}^{2}-\frac{d-1}{r}\partial_{r}+\frac{\nu_{\alpha}^{2}}{r^{2}},\quad\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\perp}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\ell},.\end{split}

It is well-known that

\mathbb{H}_{\alpha}
and
\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}_{\alpha}
formally have eigenfunctions

\displaystyle E_{\alpha}(r,\lambda)=(r\lambda)^{-\frac{d-2}{2}}J_{\widetilde{\mu}_{\alpha}}(\lambda r);\quad\widetilde{E}_{\alpha}(r,\lambda)=(r\lambda)^{-\frac{d-2}{2}}J_{\widetilde{\nu}_{\alpha}}(\lambda r),

where the
J_{\nu}
are Bessel functions of the first kind of order
\nu
, and

\widetilde{\mu}_{\alpha}=\sqrt{\mu_{\alpha}^{2}+\Big(\frac{d-2}{2}\Big)^{2}},\quad\widetilde{\nu}_{\alpha}=\sqrt{\nu_{\alpha}^{2}+\Big(\frac{d-2}{2}\Big)^{2}}.

Their corresponding eigenvalue is
\lambda^{2}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{\alpha}E_{\alpha}(r,\lambda)=\lambda^{2}E_{\alpha}(r,\lambda),\quad\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}_{\alpha}\widetilde{E}_{\alpha}(r,\lambda)=\lambda^{2}\widetilde{E}_{\alpha}(r,\lambda).

(1.7)

For a radial function
f(r)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+},r^{d-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r)
, we then define its Hankel transform of order
\nu\geq 0
as

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{\nu}f(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{\infty}(r\lambda)^{-\frac{d-2}{2}}J_{\nu}(r\lambda)f(r)r^{d-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r.

(1.8)

It is easy to see that

\mathscr{H}_{\nu}\mathscr{H}_{\nu}=I,\qquad\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+},r^{d-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r)}=\|\mathscr{H}_{\nu}f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+},\lambda^{d-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda)}.

Furthermore, Hankel transforms diagonalize the differential operators
\mathbb{H}_{\alpha}
and
\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}_{\alpha}
:

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{\widetilde{\mu}_{\alpha}}(\mathbb{H}_{\alpha}f)(\lambda)=\lambda^{2}\mathscr{H}_{\widetilde{\mu}_{\alpha}}f(\lambda),\quad\mathscr{H}_{\widetilde{\nu}_{\alpha}}(\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}_{\alpha}f)(\lambda)=\lambda^{2}\mathscr{H}_{\widetilde{\nu}_{\alpha}}f(\lambda).

(1.9)

Therefore, the spectral measures associated to
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},a}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},0}
are given by

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}E_{\lambda}(\mathbf{A},0)f(r,\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathclap{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\perp}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}}\mathscr{H}_{\widetilde{\mu}_{\alpha}}f_{\alpha}(\lambda)e_{\alpha}(\theta),\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}E_{\lambda}(\mathbf{A},a)f(r,\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathclap{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\perp}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}}\mathscr{H}_{\widetilde{\nu}_{\alpha}}\widetilde{f}_{\alpha}(\lambda)\phi_{\alpha}(\theta).

1.3.
Intertwining Operators

We are now ready to introduce the core of this manuscript.
We start by defining the following families of linear angular operators:

\displaystyle W^{\theta}_{\alpha}:\spn\{e_{\alpha}(\theta)\}

\displaystyle\to\spn\{\phi_{\alpha}(\theta)\}

\displaystyle W_{\alpha}^{\theta}(e_{\alpha}(\theta))

\displaystyle=\phi_{\alpha}(\theta).

(1.10)

The dual operators
\{W_{\alpha}^{\theta,*}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\perp}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}
are given by

\displaystyle W^{\theta,*}_{\alpha}:\spn\{\phi_{\alpha}(\theta)\}

\displaystyle\to\spn\{e_{\alpha}(\theta)\}

\displaystyle W_{\alpha}^{\theta,*}(\phi_{\alpha}(\theta))

\displaystyle=e_{\alpha}(\theta).

(1.11)

Remark 1.4
.

Notice that the above operators in are not uniquely defined: indeed, definition (
1.10
) depends on the way we order the eigenfunctions
e_{\alpha},\varphi_{\alpha}
, when the corresponding eigenvalues are repeated (see Assumption
1.1
above). Nevertheless, we are able to prove that any of these choices of the order leads to the definition of intertwining operators with suitable boundedness properties in
L^{p}
.

For each fixed
\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\perp}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\ell}
,
W_{\alpha}^{\theta}
and
W_{\alpha}^{\theta,*}
are unitary operators on
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
since both
e_{\alpha}(\theta)
and
\phi_{\alpha}(\theta)
are normalized.

Next, we define a family of radial operators
\{W_{\alpha}^{r}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\perp}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}
on
L^{2}(r^{d-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r)
as follows:

W_{\alpha}^{r}\coloneq\mathscr{H}_{{\widetilde{\nu}_{\alpha}}}\mathscr{H}_{\widetilde{\mu}_{\alpha}}.

(1.12)

As for the dual operators
W^{r,*}_{\alpha}
, those are given by

W_{\alpha}^{r,*}=\mathscr{H}_{\widetilde{\mu}_{\alpha}}\mathscr{H}_{\widetilde{\nu}_{\alpha}}.

(1.13)

Thanks to the properties of the Hankel transform, we see that for each fixed
j
,
W_{\alpha}^{r}
and
W_{\alpha}^{r,*}
are unitary operators on
L^{2}(r^{d-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r)
.

We can finally give the main definition of this manuscript.

Definition 1.5

(Intertwining operators)
.

Given
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, with expansions

\displaystyle f(x)=\sum_{\mathclap{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\perp}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}}f_{\alpha}(r)e_{\alpha}(\theta)=\ \sum_{\mathclap{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\perp}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}}\widetilde{f}_{\alpha}(r)\phi_{\alpha}(\theta),

we define the intertwining operators
W
,
W^{*}
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by

\displaystyle Wf(x)

\displaystyle\coloneq\sum_{\mathclap{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\perp}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}}W^{r}_{\alpha}f_{\alpha}(r)W^{\theta}_{\alpha}(e_{\alpha}(\theta))=\sum_{\mathclap{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\perp}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}}\mathscr{H}_{{\widetilde{\nu}_{\alpha}}}\mathscr{H}_{\widetilde{\mu}_{\alpha}}f_{\alpha}(r)\phi_{\alpha}(\theta)\,

(1.14)

with its dual operator
W^{*}
formally given by

\displaystyle W^{*}f(x)

\displaystyle\coloneq\sum_{\mathclap{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\perp}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}}W^{r,*}_{\alpha}\widetilde{f}_{\alpha}(r)W^{\theta,*}_{\alpha}(\phi_{\alpha}(\theta))=\sum_{\mathclap{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\perp}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}}\mathscr{H}_{{\widetilde{\mu}_{\alpha}}}\mathscr{H}_{\widetilde{\nu}_{\alpha}}f_{\alpha}(r)e_{\alpha}(\theta).

(1.15)

In order to check the duality, notice that,
for any
f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
one has

\displaystyle\langle Wf,g\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}\langle\mathscr{H}_{\widetilde{\nu}_{\alpha}}\mathscr{H}_{\widetilde{\mu}_{\alpha}}f_{\alpha},\widetilde{g}_{\alpha}\rangle_{\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha}}=\sum_{\alpha}\langle f_{\alpha},\mathscr{H}_{\widetilde{\mu}_{\alpha}}\mathscr{H}_{\widetilde{\nu}_{\alpha}}\widetilde{g}_{\alpha}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}}=\langle f,W^{*}g\rangle.

As the Hankel transform is unitary, and both
\{e_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\perp}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}
and
\{\phi_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\perp}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}
are bases, it is easy to see that
W
is also unitary, i.e.

\displaystyle WW^{*}=W^{*}W=I.

(1.16)

We now show that the above operator enjoy the above mentioned intertwining property.

Lemma 1.6

(Intertwining property)
.

The operator
W
defined in (
1.14
) satisfies the following intertwining property in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
: for any bounded Borel-measurable function
F
,

F(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},a})=WF(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},0})W^{*}.

(1.17)

Proof.

By (
1.16
), it is sufficient to show that
F(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},a})W=WF(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},0})
. By the definition, we have for the left-hand side

\displaystyle F(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},a})Wf(r,\theta)

\displaystyle=F\big(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},a}\big)\Big(\sum_{\alpha}\mathscr{H}_{{\widetilde{\nu}_{\alpha}}}\mathscr{H}_{\widetilde{\mu}_{\alpha}}f_{\alpha}(r)\phi_{\alpha}(\theta)\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}F(\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}_{\alpha})\mathscr{H}_{{\widetilde{\nu}_{\alpha}}}\mathscr{H}_{\widetilde{\mu}_{\alpha}}f_{\alpha}(r)\phi_{\alpha}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}\mathscr{H}_{\widetilde{\nu}_{\alpha}}\mathscr{H}_{\widetilde{\nu}_{\alpha}}F(\mathbb{H}_{\alpha})\mathscr{H}_{\widetilde{\nu}_{\alpha}}\mathscr{H}_{\widetilde{\mu}_{\alpha}}f_{\alpha}(r)\phi_{\alpha}(\theta))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}\mathscr{H}_{{\widetilde{\nu}_{\alpha}}}\left[F(\lambda^{2})(\mathscr{H}_{\widetilde{\mu}_{\alpha}}f_{\alpha})(\lambda)\right](r)\phi_{\alpha}(\theta),

while the right-hand side is

\displaystyle WF(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},0})f(r,\theta)

\displaystyle=W\bigg(\sum_{\alpha}F(\mathbb{H}_{\alpha})f_{\alpha}(r)e_{\alpha}(\theta)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}\mathscr{H}_{\widetilde{\nu}_{\alpha}}\mathscr{H}_{\widetilde{\mu}_{\alpha}}\left[F(\mathbb{H}_{\alpha})f_{\alpha}(\cdot)\right](r)\phi_{\alpha}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}\mathscr{H}_{{\widetilde{\nu}_{\alpha}}}\left[F(\lambda^{2})\left(\mathscr{H}_{\widetilde{\mu}_{\alpha}}f_{\alpha}\right)(\lambda)\right](r)\phi_{\alpha}(\theta).

The proof is hence complete.
∎

2.
Main results

We showed in the previous section that the intertwining operators defined in (
1.14
) and (
1.15
) are bounded in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
However, to get the
L^{p}
-boundedness of those operators for
p\neq 2
, we need stronger spectral conditions than those in Assumption
1.1
on the spherical operators
L_{\mathbf{A},0}
and
L_{\mathbf{A},a}
. Roughly speaking, we need to control the asymptotic size of the separation between the eigenvalues
\mu_{jk}^{2},\nu_{jk}^{2}
. Notice that
\mathbf{A}
is fixed in the two cases; in fact, we believe that the intertwining operators between
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A}_{1},0}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A}_{2},0}
are not bounded in
L^{p}
for any nontrivial
\mathbf{A}_{1},\mathbf{A}_{2}
, as their eigenvalues
\mu_{jk}^{2},\nu_{jk}^{2}
(appropriately paired up) will not be close enough to each other as
j
goes to infinity (see the details in the sequel).

2.1.
Discrete mulitiplier bases

In order to state our main results, we start by some preliminary notations and definitions.
For any doubly indexed sequence
\{\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}
, we define its average sequence
\{\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{j}\}_{j\geq\ell}
by

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{j}=\frac{1}{m_{j}}\sum_{k=1}^{m_{j}}\mathcal{C}_{jk}

(2.1)

Then we define the forward finite difference operator with respect to
j
acting on sequences
\{\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{j}\}_{j\geq\ell}
by

\mathfrak{D}\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{j}\coloneq\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{j+1}-\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{j},\quad\mathfrak{D}^{N}\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{j}\coloneq\mathfrak{D}^{N-1}(\mathfrak{D}\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{j}).

Definition 2.1
.

We call a basis
\{\psi_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\perp}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
a
discrete multiplier basis
and write
\{\psi_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\perp}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}\in\mathcal{DMB}
if whenever
\{\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}
is a sequence of complex numbers
satisfying

\displaystyle\sup_{j\geq\ell}\left|\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{j}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim 1,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\sup_{L\in\mathbb{N}\colon 2^{L}\geq\ell}2^{(N-1)L}\sum_{j=2^{L}}^{2^{L+1}}\left|\mathfrak{D}^{N}\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{j}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim 1,\quad N=1,2,\dots,\left\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\right\rfloor,

(2.3)

we have for all
1<p<\infty
the following inequality,

\displaystyle\bigg\|\sum_{j=\ell}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{m_{j}}\mathcal{C}_{jk}F_{jk}\psi_{jk}(\theta)\bigg\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}\lesssim\bigg\|\sum_{j=\ell}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{m_{j}}F_{jk}\psi_{jk}(\theta)\bigg\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}.

(2.4)

Remark 2.2
.

Let
\{\psi_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha}
be the eigenfunctions of
L_{\mathbf{A},a}
. When
{\mathbf{A}}=0
and
a(\theta)=0
,
\{\psi_{\alpha}\}
are the spherical harmonics on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, and
\{\psi_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{DMB}
by the results in
[
3
]
. More generally, for any
\mathbf{A}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1};\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
a\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1};\mathbb{R})
,
\{\psi_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{DMB}
by
[
35
, Theorem 5.3.1]
.

We now state a new fundamental tool for our main results.

Lemma 2.3

(Variable coefficients discrete multiplier theorem)
.

Let
\{\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}(\theta)\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}
be a sequence of bounded functions belonging to
C^{d-1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
and satisfying the following conditions:

\displaystyle\sup_{j,\theta}|\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(\theta)|<C,\quad\sup_{j,\theta}\left|\partial_{\theta}^{d-1}\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(\theta)\right|<C,

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta,2^{L}\geq\ell}2^{(N-1)L}\sum_{j=2^{L}}^{2^{L+1}}\left|\mathfrak{D}^{N}\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(\theta)\right|<C,

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta,2^{L}\geq\ell}2^{(N-1)L}\sum_{j=2^{L}}^{2^{L+1}}\left|\mathfrak{D}^{N}\partial_{\theta}^{d-1}\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(\theta)\right|<C,

for
N=1,2,\dots,\left\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\right\rfloor
.
If
\{\psi_{\alpha}\}\in\mathcal{DMB}
, then for every
1<p<\infty
,

\displaystyle\bigg\|\sum_{j=\ell}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{m_{j}}\mathcal{C}_{jk}(\theta)F_{jk}\psi_{jk}(\theta)\bigg\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}\lesssim\bigg\|\sum_{j=\ell}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{m_{j}}F_{jk}\psi_{jk}(\theta)\bigg\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}.

Proof.

Let
\theta=\theta(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{d-1})\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
be the standard parameterization of the sphere, and abusively write
\mathcal{C}_{jk}(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{d-1}):=\mathcal{C}_{jk}(\theta(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{d-1}))
. By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, we can write

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{jk}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}_{jk}(0,\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{d-1})+\int_{0}^{\theta_{1}}(\partial_{\theta}\mathcal{C}_{jk}\partial_{\theta_{1}}\theta)(\theta_{1}^{\prime},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{d-1})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta_{1}^{\prime}

We can repeat this again for the first term
\mathcal{C}_{jk}(0,\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{d-1})
to get

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{jk}(0,0,\theta_{3},\dots,\theta_{d-1})+\int_{0}^{\theta_{2}}\partial_{\theta}\mathcal{C}_{jk}(0,\theta_{2}^{\prime},\theta_{3},\dots,\theta_{d-1})\partial_{\theta_{2}}\theta(0,\theta_{2}^{\prime},\theta_{3},\dots,\theta_{d-1})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta_{2}^{\prime},

and for the integral term
\int_{0}^{\theta_{1}}(\partial_{\theta}\mathcal{C}_{jk}\partial_{\theta_{1}}\theta)(\theta_{1}^{\prime},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{d-1})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta_{1}^{\prime}
to get

\int_{0}^{\theta_{1}}(\partial_{\theta}\mathcal{C}_{jk}\partial_{\theta_{1}}\theta)(\theta_{1}^{\prime},0,\theta_{3},\dots,\theta_{d-1})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta_{1}^{\prime}\\
+\int_{0}^{\theta_{2}}\int_{0}^{\theta_{1}}(\partial_{\theta}^{2}\mathcal{C}_{jk}\partial_{\theta_{1}}\theta\partial_{\theta_{2}}\theta+\partial_{\theta}\mathcal{C}_{jk}\partial_{\theta_{1}}\partial_{\theta_{2}}\theta)(\theta_{1}^{\prime},\theta_{2}^{\prime},\theta_{3},\dots,\theta_{d-1})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta_{1}^{\prime}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta_{2}^{\prime}.

We can inductively repeat this until we obtain terms of the form

\displaystyle c\int_{0}^{\theta_{i_{1}}}\dots\int_{0}^{\theta_{i_{N}}}G(\theta^{\prime}_{i_{1}},\dots,\theta^{\prime}_{i_{N}})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime}_{i_{1}}\dots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime}_{i_{N}}

where
c
is a combinatorial constant,
0\leq M\leq N\leq d-1
, and
G(\theta^{\prime}_{i_{1}},\dots,\theta^{\prime}_{i_{N}})
is the product of
\partial^{M}_{\theta}\mathcal{C}_{jk}
and various derivatives of the parameterisation
\theta
, all evaluated at the point

(0,\dots,0,\theta^{\prime}_{i_{1}},0,\dots,0,\theta_{i_{N}}^{\prime},0,\dots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1},

i.e. the point whose
i_{k}^{\text{th}}
component is equal to
\theta_{i_{k}}^{\prime}
, and all other components equal to 0. A precise enumeration could be given with the Faà di Bruno formula, but the important point is that every term depends on the coordinates
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{d-1}
only in the integration limits. Thus, every integrand
G(\theta^{\prime}_{i_{1}},\dots,\theta^{\prime}_{i_{N}})
can be treated as a constant coefficient multiplier, as they satisfy (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) for each fixed choice of
\theta^{\prime}_{i_{1}},\dots,\theta^{\prime}_{i_{N}}
. Hence, by applying Definition
2.1
, we have that for any
1<p<\infty
,

\displaystyle\Big\|\sum_{j,k}\mathcal{C}_{jk}(\theta)F_{jk}\psi_{jk}(\theta)\Big\|_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\lesssim\Big\|\sum_{j,k}\mathcal{C}_{jk}(0,\dots,0)F_{jk}\psi_{jk}(\theta)\Big\|_{L^{p}}\\
+\!\!\sum_{M,N,i_{1},\dots,i_{N}}\int_{0}^{\pi}\!\cdots\!\int_{0}^{\pi}\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Big\|\sum_{j,k}G(\theta^{\prime}_{i_{1}},\dots,\theta^{\prime}_{i_{N}})F_{jk}\psi_{jk}(\theta)\Big\|_{L^{p}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime}_{i_{1}}\dots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime}_{i_{N}}\end{multlined}

\displaystyle\lesssim\bigg\|\sum_{j,k}F_{jk}\psi_{jk}(\theta)\bigg\|_{L^{p}},

which was what we wanted.
∎

2.2.
Proper perturbations

Definition 2.4

(Proper perturbation)
.

Assume that
L_{\mathbf{A},a}
and
L_{\mathbf{A},0}
satisfy Assumption
1.1
. We call
L_{\mathbf{A},a}
is a
proper perturbation
of
L_{\mathbf{A},0}
if

(1)

There exists a constant
C_{a}
that depends only on
\mathbf{A}
and
a
such that for all
j\geq\ell
,

\left|\nu_{jk}^{2}-\mu_{jk}^{2}-C_{a}\right|\leq\frac{C}{j}

for all
1\leq k\leq m_{j}
, where
C
is a constant independent of
j
and
k
.

(2)

The corresponding eigenfunctions
\phi_{jk}
and
e_{jk}

j\geq\ell
can be written

\phi_{jk}(\theta)=e_{jk}(\theta)(1+R_{jk}(\theta)),

where
R_{jk}
satisfies with
N=\big\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\big\rfloor+1
the following estimates:

(a)

\sup_{\theta}|\overline{R}_{j}(\theta)|=O(\frac{1}{j})
as
j\to\infty
, where
\overline{R}_{j}=\frac{1}{m_{j}}\sum_{k=1}^{m_{j}}R_{jk}
,

(b)

\sup_{2^{L}\geq\ell,\theta}2^{(N-1)L}\sum_{j=2^{L}}^{2^{L+1}}|\mathfrak{D}^{N}\overline{R}_{j}(\theta)|\lesssim\ell^{-1}
,

(c)

\sup_{2^{L}\geq\ell,\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}|\partial_{\theta}^{d-1}\overline{R}_{j}(\theta)|\lesssim\ell^{-1}
,

(d)

\sup_{2^{L}\geq\ell,\theta}2^{(N-1)L}\sum_{j=2^{L}}^{2^{L+1}}|\mathfrak{D}^{N}\partial_{\theta}^{d-1}\overline{R}_{j}(\theta)|\lesssim\ell^{-1}
.

Remark 2.5
.

In some cases, in order to estimate the derivatives of the reminder terms, we can further decompose
R_{jk}=\sum_{m=1}^{d}R_{jk}^{m}
with the
m^{\text{th}}
reminder term
R_{jk}^{m}
satisfying

\displaystyle\|R_{jk}^{m}\|_{L^{\infty}}=O\Big(\frac{1}{j^{m}}\Big).

For
m\leq d-1
, we can control the derivatives using the explicit formula. For
R_{jk}^{d}
, we can use triangle inequality and the fact that

\sum_{jk}\frac{1}{j^{d}}\lesssim\sum_{j}\frac{1}{j^{2}}<\infty

to get the boundedness in
L^{p}
. We will see this in Section
5.2
.

Remark 2.6
.

When
d=2
,
\mathbf{A}\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
and
a\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
,
L_{\mathbf{A},a}
is a proper perturbation of
L_{\mathbf{A},0}
by the results in
[
16
]
.

Remark 2.7
.

An important question that we will not address in the present paper is the following:
for a fixed
\mathbf{A}
, what is the minimal regularity of
a(\theta)
such that
L_{\mathbf{A},a}
is a proper perturbation of
L_{\mathbf{A},0}
?

Lemma 2.8
.

Assume that
\{e_{\alpha}\}\in\mathcal{DMB}
. If
L_{\mathbf{A},a}
is a proper perturbation of
L_{\mathbf{A},0}
, then
\{\phi_{\alpha}\}\in\mathcal{DMB}
. That is,
whenever
\{\mathcal{C}_{jk}\}_{(j,k)\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}
satisfies the estimates (
2.2
) and (
2.3
),
the perturbed eigenfunctions
\{\phi_{jk}\}
satisfy

\displaystyle\bigg\|\sum_{j=\ell}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{m_{j}}\mathcal{C}_{jk}F_{jk}\phi_{jk}(\theta)\bigg\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}\lesssim\bigg\|\sum_{j=\ell}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{m_{j}}F_{jk}\phi_{jk}(\theta)\bigg\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}.

(2.5)

Proof.

From
\phi_{jk}=e_{jk}(\theta)(1+R_{jk}(\theta))
, triangle inequality and
\{e_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{DMB}
, we apply Lemma
2.3
with
\mathcal{C}_{jk}R_{jk}
as the variable coefficients to get

\bigg\|\sum_{j,k}\mathcal{C}_{jk}F_{jk}\phi_{jk}\bigg\|_{L^{p}}\lesssim\bigg\|\sum_{j,k}\mathcal{C}_{jk}F_{jk}e_{jk}\bigg\|_{L^{p}}+\bigg\|\sum_{j,k}\mathcal{C}_{jk}R_{jk}F_{jk}e_{jk}\bigg\|_{L^{p}}\lesssim\bigg\|\sum_{j,k}F_{jk}e_{jk}\bigg\|_{L^{p}}.

To finish, we write
e_{jk}=\phi_{jk}-e_{jk}R_{jk}
and use the fact that
R_{jk}
is small for
j\gg 1
,

\displaystyle\bigg\|\sum_{j,k}F_{jk}e_{jk}\bigg\|_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg\|\sum_{j,k}F_{jk}\phi_{jk}\bigg\|_{L^{p}}+\frac{C}{\ell}\bigg\|\sum_{j,k}F_{jk}e_{jk}\bigg\|_{L^{p}},

so the second term on the right hand side can be absorbed into the left side. So the result is proven.
∎

2.3.
Main result

We are now ready to state our main result. From now on, we let
n(d):=\frac{d-2}{2}
.

Theorem 2.9

(
L^{p}
-boundedness of
W
and
W^{*}
)
.

Let
d\geq 2
, and
L_{\mathbf{A},0}
,
L_{\mathbf{A},a}
satisfy Assumption
1.1
. Assume that the eigenfunctions of
L_{\mathbf{A},0}
,
\{e_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{DMB}
and
L_{{\mathbf{A},a}}
is a proper perturbation of
L_{{\mathbf{A},0}}
. Then

W
defined in (
1.14
) is bounded on
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for
p\in(1,\infty)
satisfying

\frac{n(d)-\widetilde{\nu}_{1}}{d}<\frac{1}{p}<\frac{d-n(d)+\widetilde{\mu}_{1}}{d},

and
W^{*}
defined in (
1.15
) is bounded on
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for
p\in(1,\infty)
satisfying

\frac{n(d)-\widetilde{\mu}_{1}}{d}<\frac{1}{p}<\frac{d-n(d)+\widetilde{\nu}_{1}}{d},

and
\widetilde{\mu}_{1}=\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A},0)+n(d)^{2}}
,
\widetilde{\nu}_{1}=\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A},a)+n(d)^{2}}
. In particular, if
\widetilde{\mu}_{1},\widetilde{\nu}_{1}\geq n(d)
, then
W
and
W^{*}
are bounded on
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all
1<p<\infty
.

Remark 2.10
.

Note that the restriction on
p
depends on the angular operators
L_{\mathbf{A},0},L_{\mathbf{A},a}
only through their first eigenvalues.

Remark 2.11
.

Owing to our use of the theory of singular integrals and the discrete multiplier theorem, we are unable to prove the endpoint cases
p=1,\infty
with this method.

Remark 2.12
.

For
d\geq 3
, Theorem
2.9
includes the inverse square potential which is given by
\mathbf{A}=0
and
a
constant. For more general potentials, our result shows that to study
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},a}
it suffices to understand
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},0}
and the perturbation theory for the angular component.

2.4.
The two-dimensional case

In dimension
d=2
, the spectral assumptions in our main theorem are generically satisfied, which leads to the following quite complete result. Let us define
A,\widetilde{a}
, and
\widetilde{A}
by

\begin{gathered}A(\theta)=\mathbf{A}(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\cdot(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta),\\
\widetilde{a}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}a(\theta)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta,\qquad\widetilde{A}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}A(\theta)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta.\end{gathered}

(2.6)

Theorem 2.13
.

Let
a\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{R})
,
\mathbf{A}\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{R}^{2})
satisfying (
1.2
) and (
1.5
) hold. If
\widetilde{A}\notin\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}
or
\widetilde{A}\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}
and
a(\theta)
is symmetric across
\theta=\pi
(i.e.
a(\pi-\theta)=a(\pi+\theta)
for all
\theta\in[0,\pi]
),
W
and
W^{*}
defined in (
1.14
) and (
1.15
) are
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
-bounded for all
1<p<\infty
.

Theorem
2.13
will be proven in Section
5
using Theorem
2.9
. We now state some of the consequences of Theorem
2.13
.

For the wave propagator, the authors in
[
19
]
has proved the following estimate for the electric-free operator,

\bigg\|\frac{\sin(t\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},0}})}{\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},0}}}\varphi(\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},0}})f\bigg\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C(1+|t|)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

(2.7)

where
\text{supp}\;\varphi\subset[1/2,2].
Interpolating this with trivial
L^{2}
bound, we have for any
1\leq p\leq 2

\bigg\lVert\frac{\sin(t\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},0}})}{\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},0}}}\varphi\big(\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},0}}\big)f\bigg\rVert_{L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C(1+|t|)^{-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{p^{\prime}})}\|f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

where
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}=1
. This together with Theorem
2.13
and the intertwining property (
1.17
) gives the corresponding result for
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},a}
:

Corollary 2.14

(Dispersive estimates of wave propagator)
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{A}(\theta)
and
a(\theta)
satisfy the assumptions of Theorem
2.13
. Let
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})
with

0\leq\varphi\leq 1
and
\text{supp}\;\varphi\subset[1/2,2]
. Then, for
1<p\leq 2
,

\bigg\lVert\frac{\sin(t\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},a}})}{\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},a}}}\varphi(\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},a}})f\bigg\rVert_{L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C(1+|t|)^{-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{p^{\prime}})}\|f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

(2.8)

Recently, Fanelli–Zhang–Zheng
[
20
]
extended the uniform resolvent estimates for Laplacian to the pure magnetic potential. They proved that for all
p
and
q
satisfying

\frac{2}{3}\leq\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}<1,\ \frac{3}{4}<\frac{1}{p}\leq 1,\ \text{and}\ 0\leq\frac{1}{q}<\frac{1}{4},

(2.9)

the following resolvent estimate is valid,

\left\|(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},0}-z)^{-1}f\right\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C|z|^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}-1}\|f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},\quad\text{ for all }\;z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}^{+}.

(2.10)

An immediate consequence of Theorem
2.13
and (
2.10
) is

Corollary 2.15

(Uniform resolvent estimate)
.

Assume that
\mathbf{A}(\theta)
and
a(\theta)
satisfy the assumptions of Theorem
2.13
. Let
(p,q)
satisfy (
2.9
) and
1<p,q<\infty
. Then, we have

\left\|(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{A},a}-z)^{-1}f\right\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C|z|^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}-1}\|f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},\quad\text{ for all }\;z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}^{+}.

(2.11)

The Bochner–Riesz means of order
\delta
associated with the any self-adjoint operator

Appendix A

For any function

with the expansion

Define the operator

by

(A.1)

Then, we rewrite

\displaystyle W_{\ell}f(r,\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k}\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{jk}(s)\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{\widetilde{\nu}_{jk}}(r\lambda)J_{\widetilde{\mu}_{jk}}(s\lambda)\lambda^{d-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\lambda}\right)s\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}se_{jk}(\theta)

\displaystyle a_{jk}

as

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{j,k,2}

\displaystyle=2\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}+1-\frac{d}{p}+\widetilde{\mu}_{jk}}\frac{\sin(-\pi b_{jk})}{\pi}\left[\frac{1}{1-(\frac{s}{r})^{2}}-a_{jk}b_{jk}\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)^{-2}\ln\!\Big({1-\Big(\frac{s}{r}\Big)^{2}}\Big)+E_{jk}^{+}\right]\chi_{+}

\displaystyle\quad+2\left(\frac{r}{s}\right)^{{\frac{d}{p}-\frac{d}{2}+1+\widetilde{\nu}_{jk}}}\frac{\sin(\pi b_{jk})}{\pi}\left[\frac{1}{1-(\frac{r}{s})^{2}}+a_{jk}b_{jk}\left(\frac{r}{s}\right)^{-2}\ln\!\Big({1-\Big(\frac{r}{s}\Big)^{2}}\Big)+E_{jk}^{-}\right]\chi_{-}

\displaystyle=:\widetilde{K}_{j,k,2}^{1}(r,s)+\widetilde{K}_{j,k,2}^{2}(r,s)+\widetilde{K}_{j,k,2}^{3}(r,s)

(A.21)

with

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{j,k,2}^{1}(r,s)

\displaystyle=2\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}+1-\frac{d}{p}+\widetilde{\mu}_{jk}}\frac{\sin(-\pi b_{jk})}{\pi}\frac{1}{1-\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)^{2}}\chi_{+}

\displaystyle\quad+2\left(\frac{r}{s}\right)^{{\frac{d}{p}-\frac{d}{2}+1+\widetilde{\nu}_{jk}}}\frac{\sin(\pi b_{jk})}{\pi}\frac{1}{1-(\frac{r}{s})^{2}}\chi_{-};

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{j,k,2}^{2}(r,s)

\displaystyle=-2a_{jk}b_{jk}\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}-1-\frac{d}{p}+\widetilde{\mu}_{jk}}\frac{\sin(-\pi b_{jk})}{\pi}\ln\!\Big({1-\Big(\frac{s}{r}\Big)^{2}}\Big)\chi_{+}

\displaystyle\quad+2a_{jk}b_{jk}\left(\frac{r}{s}\right)^{{\frac{d}{p}-\frac{d}{2}-1+\widetilde{\nu}_{jk}}}\frac{\sin(\pi b_{jk})}{\pi}\ln\!\Big({1-\Big(\frac{r}{s}\Big)^{2}}\Big)\chi_{-};

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{j,k,2}^{3}(r,s)

\displaystyle=2\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)^{\!\frac{d}{2}+1-\frac{d}{p}+\widetilde{\mu}_{jk}}\frac{\sin(-\pi b_{jk})}{\pi}E_{jk}^{+}(r,s)

\displaystyle\quad+2\left(\frac{r}{s}\right)^{\!{\frac{d}{p}-\frac{d}{2}+1+\widetilde{\nu}_{jk}}}\frac{\sin(\pi b_{jk})}{\pi}E_{jk}^{-}(r,s).

We denote the corresponding operators
\widetilde{W}_{\ell,2}^{1},\widetilde{W}_{\ell,2}^{2}
and
\widetilde{W}_{\ell,2}^{3}
by

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}_{\ell,2}^{i}f(r,\theta)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{j,k}\widetilde{K}_{j,k,2}^{i}(r,s)f_{jk}(s)e_{jk}(\theta)\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}{s},\qquad i=1,2,3.

Estimate for the first term
\widetilde{W}_{\ell,2}^{1}
.

Step 1:
Estimate for the approximate kernels:
Let us define the approximate kernels by

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{j,k,2}^{1,\text{ap}}(r,s)

\displaystyle=2\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}+1-\frac{d}{p}+\widetilde{\mu}_{\ell,1}}\frac{\sin(-\pi b_{jk})}{\pi}\frac{1}{1-\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)^{2}}\chi_{+}

\displaystyle\quad+2\left(\frac{r}{s}\right)^{\frac{d}{p}-\frac{d}{2}+1+\widetilde{\nu}_{\ell,1}}\frac{\sin\pi b_{jk}}{\pi}\frac{1}{1-(\frac{r}{s})^{2}}\chi_{-},

and the corresponding operator
\widetilde{W}_{\ell,2}^{1,\text{ap}}f(r,\theta)
by

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}_{\ell,2}^{1,\text{ap}}f(r,\theta)\

\displaystyle\coloneq\sum_{j,k}\int_{0}^{\infty}\widetilde{K}_{j,k,2}^{1,\text{ap}}(r,s)f_{jk}(s)\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}{s}e_{jk}(\theta)

\displaystyle=2\int_{\frac{r}{2}}^{r}\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}+1-\frac{d}{p}+\widetilde{\mu}_{\ell,1}}\frac{1}{1-(\frac{s}{r})^{2}}\sum_{j,k}\frac{\sin(-\pi b_{jk})}{\pi}f_{jk}(s)e_{jk}(\theta)\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}{s}

\displaystyle\quad+2\int_{r}^{2r}\left(\frac{r}{s}\right)^{\frac{d}{p}-\frac{d}{2}+1+\widetilde{\nu}_{\ell,1}}\frac{1}{1-(\frac{r}{s})^{2}}\sum_{j,k}\frac{\sin\pi b_{jk}}{\pi}f_{jk}(s)e_{jk}(\theta)\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}{s}.

Using Lemma
3.3
, we have

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{W}_{\ell,2}^{1,\text{ap}}f(r,\theta)\|_{L^{p}(\frac{dr}{r})}\lesssim\bigg\|\sum_{j,k}\sin\pi b_{jk}f_{jk}(r)e_{jk}(\theta)\bigg\|_{L^{p}(\frac{dr}{r})}.

We can easily check that the sequence
\{\sin\pi b_{jk}\}
satisfies the condition (
2.2
) and (
2.3
). Hence

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{W}_{\ell,2}^{1,\text{ap}}f(r,\theta)\|_{L^{p}(\frac{dr}{r}\cdot d\theta)}\lesssim\bigg\|\sum_{j,k}f_{jk}(r)e_{jk}(\theta)\bigg\|_{L^{p}(\frac{dr}{r}\cdot d\theta)}.

(A.22)

Step 2:
Estimate for the error term
. Define the kernels of the error terms

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{j,k,2}^{1,\text{err}}(r,s)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{K}_{j,k,2}^{1}(r,s)-\widetilde{K}_{j,k,2}^{1,\text{ap}}(r,s)

\displaystyle=2\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}+1-\frac{d}{p}+\widetilde{\mu}_{\ell,1}}\frac{\sin(-\pi b_{jk})}{\pi}\frac{1}{1-\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)^{2}}\left[\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)^{{\widetilde{\mu}_{jk}-\widetilde{\mu}_{\ell,1}}}-1\right]\chi_{+}

\displaystyle\quad+2\left(\frac{r}{s}\right)^{\frac{d}{p}-\frac{d}{2}+1+\widetilde{\nu}_{\ell,1}}\frac{\sin(\pi b_{jk})}{\pi}\frac{1}{1-(\frac{r}{s})^{2}}\left[\left(\frac{r}{s}\right)^{{\widetilde{\nu}_{jk}-\widetilde{\nu}_{\ell,1}}}-1\right]\chi_{-},

and the associated operator

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}_{\ell,2}^{1,\text{err}}f(r,\theta)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{j,k}\widetilde{K}_{j,k,2}^{1,\text{err}}(r,s)f_{jk}(s)e_{jk}(\theta)\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}{s}.

By the same argument as in
[
31
, Lemma 4.3, Lemma 4.5]
, we have

\displaystyle|\overline{\widetilde{K}}_{j2}^{1,\text{err}}(r,s)|\lesssim 1,\quad\sup_{L\in\mathbb{N}:2^{L}\geq\ell}2^{(N-1)L}\sum_{j=2^{L}}^{2^{L+1}}|\mathfrak{D}^{N}\overline{\widetilde{K}}_{j,2}^{1,\text{err}}(r,s)|\lesssim 1.

Hence

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{W}_{\ell,2}^{1,\text{err}}f(r,\theta)\|_{L^{p}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta)}\leq\int_{r/2}^{r}\Big\|\sum_{j,k}f_{jk}(s)e_{jk}(\theta)\Big\|_{L^{p}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta)}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}{s}.

Furthermore, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{W}_{\ell,2}^{1,\text{err}}f(r,\theta)\|_{L^{p}(\frac{dr}{r}\cdot\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta)}\leq\|f(r,\theta)\|_{L^{p}(\frac{dr}{r}\cdot\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta)}.

(A.23)

Combining this with (
A.22
), we get the
L^{p}
-boundedness of
\widetilde{W}_{\ell,2}^{1}
.

Estimate for the second term
\widetilde{W}_{\ell,2}^{2}
. Direct computation gives

\displaystyle|\overline{\widetilde{K}_{j,2}}(r,s)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\left|\ln\!\Big({1-\Big(\frac{s}{r}\Big)^{2}}\Big)\right|\chi_{+}+\left|\ln\!\Big({1-\Big(\frac{r}{s}\Big)^{2}}\Big)\right|\chi_{-},

\displaystyle\sup_{L}2^{(N-1)L}\sum_{j=2^{L}}^{2^{L+1}}|\mathfrak{D}^{N}\overline{\widetilde{K}_{j,2}^{2}}(r,s)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\left|\ln\!\Big({1-\Big(\frac{s}{r}\Big)^{2}}\Big)\right|\chi_{+}+\left|\ln\!\Big({1-\Big(\frac{r}{s}\Big)^{2}}\Big)\right|\chi_{-}.

Since
\{e_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}^{\perp}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}\in\mathcal{DMB}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{W}_{\ell,2}^{2}f(r,\theta)\|_{L^{p}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta)}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{r/2}^{r}\left|\ln\!\Big({1-\Big(\frac{s}{r}\Big)^{2}}\Big)\right|\bigg\lVert\sum_{j,k}f_{jk}(s)e_{jk}(\theta)\bigg\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta)}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}{s}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{r}^{2r}\left|\ln\!\Big({1-\Big(\frac{r}{s}\Big)^{2}}\Big)\right|\bigg\lVert\sum_{j,k}f_{jk}(s)e_{jk}(\theta)\bigg\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta)}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}{s}.

Hence, by Young’s inequality, we get

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{W}_{\ell,2}^{2}f(r,\theta)\|_{L^{p}(\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}{r}\cdot\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\theta})}\lesssim\|f(r,\theta)\|_{L^{p}(\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}{r}\cdot\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\theta})}.

(A.24)

Estimate for the third term
\widetilde{W}_{\ell,2}^{3}
. Recall that
\widetilde{K}_{j,k,2}^{3}
is given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{j,k,2}^{3}(r,s)

\displaystyle=2\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)^{\!\frac{d}{2}+1-\frac{d}{p}+\widetilde{\mu}_{jk}}\frac{\sin(-\pi b_{jk})}{\pi}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}E_{j,k,n}^{+}\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)^{2n}\chi_{+}

\displaystyle\quad+2\left(\frac{r}{s}\right)^{\frac{d}{p}-\frac{d}{2}+1+\widetilde{\nu}_{jk}}\frac{\sin\pi b_{jk}}{\pi}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}E_{j,k,n}^{-}\left(\frac{r}{s}\right)^{2n}\chi_{-},

where

\displaystyle E_{j,k,n}^{+}=A_{j,k,n}^{+}-1+\frac{a_{jk}b_{jk}}{n+1},\ 1\leq k\leq m_{j};

(A.25)

\displaystyle E_{j,k,n}^{-}=A_{j,k,n}^{-}-1-\frac{a_{jk}b_{jk}}{n+1},\ 1\leq k\leq m_{j}.

(A.26)

Follow the same argument as in
[
31
, Lemma 4.6]
, we can prove that

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{N}E_{j,k,n}^{+}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}j^{N}}\bigg|\lesssim\frac{1}{j^{N}}\frac{1}{n+1},

(A.27)

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{N}E_{j,k,n}^{-}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}j^{N}}\bigg|\lesssim\frac{1}{j^{N}}\frac{1}{n+1}.

(A.28)

These will lead to that
\widetilde{K}_{j,2}^{3}(r,s)
will satisfies the conditions (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) with an upper bound
|\ln\left(1-(\frac{s}{r})^{2}\right)|
for fixed
r,s
, hence

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{W}_{\ell,2}^{3}f(r,\theta)\|_{L^{p}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta)}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{r/2}^{r}\Big|\ln\!\Big({1-\Big(\frac{s}{r}\Big)^{2}}\Big)\Big|\Big\|\sum_{j,k}f_{jk}(s)e_{jk}(\theta)\Big\|_{L^{p}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta)}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}{s}

\displaystyle+\int_{r}^{2r}\Big|\ln\!\Big({1-\Big(\frac{r}{s}\Big)^{2}}\Big)\Big|\Big\|\sum_{j,k}f_{jk}(s)e_{jk}(\theta)\Big\|_{L^{p}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta)}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}{s}.

Log-Young’s inequality again gives

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{W}_{\ell,2}^{3}f(r,\theta)\|_{L^{p}(\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}{r}\cdot\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\theta})}\lesssim\|f(r,\theta)\|_{L^{p}(\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}{r}\cdot\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\theta})}.

(A.29)

Combining (
A.23
), (
A.24
) and (
A.29
), we have proved that

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{W}_{\ell,2}f(r,\theta)\|_{L^{p}(\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}{r}\cdot\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\theta})}\lesssim\|f(r,\theta)\|_{L^{p}(\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}{r}\cdot\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\theta})}.

(A.30)

This inequality together with
(
A.14
) and (
A.17
) implies that
\widetilde{W}
is bounded in
L^{p}(\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}{r}\cdot\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\theta})
for
1<p<\infty
. Hence, we conclude the proof Proposition
4.1
.
