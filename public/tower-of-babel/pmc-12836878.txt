Overexpression of OsPLGG1b enhances rice tolerance to high-light and high-temperature stress through modulating photorespiration
Authors: Lili Cui, Jieyu Qiu, Ganting Li, Xiangze Chen, Xiyao Wu, Xiangyu Zhang, Yingheng Wang, Qiuhua Cai, Qiang Lin, Xinxiang Peng, Guohui Zhu, Jianfu Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Photorespiration, essential for photosynthetic organisms, is a highly compartmentalized pathway that relies on efficient transport of intermediates between organelles, facilitated by numerous transporters. OsPLGG1b, a glycolate/glycerate transporter localized to the chloroplast outer envelope membrane in rice, has an unclear physiological function. This study demonstrates that OsPLGG1b performs a non-redundant role distinct from OsPLGG1a. While overexpression of OsPLGG1b negatively impacted rice growth and yield under optimal conditions, likely due to disrupted carbon flux, it significantly enhanced tolerance to high-light and high-temperature (HLHT) and drought stresses. Mechanistically, OsPLGG1b-overexpressing lines exhibited elevated photorespiratory flux under HLHT stress, evidenced by increased photorespiration rates and accumulation of photorespiratory intermediates. Transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses revealed enhanced expression of photorespiratory and stress-responsive genes, as well as accumulation of protective metabolites, such as amino acids and organic acids. These adaptations enabled higher photosynthetic efficiency and photosystem II activity under HLHT stress. Collectively, these findings highlight OsPLGG1b as a promising target for improving crop resilience to environmental stress.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07927-3.Keywords: Abiotic stress, High light, High temperature, Photorespiration, Photosynthetic efficiency, OsPLGG1

Introduction

Photorespiration is initiated by the oxygenase activity of ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (Rubisco), which catalyzes the oxygenation of RuBP and O2 to produce one molecule of 2-phosphoglycolate (2-PG) and one molecule of 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PGA). Notably, 2-PG acts as a potent inhibitor of Calvin-Benson cycle enzymes [1, 2]. Within chloroplasts, 2-PG is dephosphorylated to glycolate by phosphoglycolate phosphatase. Glycolate is then exported from the chloroplasts and ultimately converted back to 3-PGA through a series of enzymatic reactions across multiple organelles [3, 4]. The resulting 3-PGA re-enters the Calvin-Benson cycle for RuBP regeneration. This recycling process serves dual critical physiological roles: it mitigates the toxic effects of 2-PG accumulation and recovers fixed carbon.

As a highly compartmentalized metabolic pathway, photorespiration involves chloroplasts, peroxisomes, mitochondria, and the cytosol. This spatial complexity necessitates efficient inter-organellar transport of photorespiratory intermediates, a process facilitated by numerous transporters [5]. The plastid glycolate/glycerate transporter 1 (PLGG1) was the first transporter identified as a core component of the photorespiratory pathway in Arabidopsis, mediating the export of glycolate from chloroplasts and the import of glycerate back into chloroplasts [6, 7]. The rice genome encodes two homologous PLGG1 isoforms, OsPLGG1a and OsPLGG1b. These isoforms share high amino acid sequence identity and both demonstrate functional glycolate/glycerate transport activity in vitro [8, 9]. However, a striking functional divergence is observed: osplgg1a mutants exhibit severe growth defects under normal light and ambient CO2 conditions, whereas osplgg1b mutants display no discernible phenotypic alterations [9]. This phenotypic disparity highlights a significant gap in our understanding — despite possessing biochemical transport capability, the in planta physiological role of OsPLGG1b remains unresolved.

Accumulating evidence establishes photorespiration as a critical adaptive mechanism enabling plants to cope with abiotic stresses. Under conditions such as high light, elevated temperature, or drought, increased photorespiratory flux helps mitigate reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation by dissipating excess reductants and energy, thereby enhancing stress tolerance [10–12]. Given its role as a transporter modulating photorespiratory carbon flux, a key question arises: Is OsPLGG1b involved in regulating photorespiration and, consequently, plant tolerance under environmental stress? To address this question, we generated OsPLGG1b-overexpressing rice plants and systematically evaluated their photosynthetic performance and stress resilience. Our findings reveal critical roles for OsPLGG1b in plant adaptation to environmental stresses, particularly high-light and high-temperature conditions, mediated through the regulation of photorespiratory flux.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Zhonghua 11 rice (Oryza sativa L., referred to as wild type, WT) was employed to generate overexpressing or mutant plants. osplgg1a and osplgg1b (osplgg1b-3 and osplgg1b-5) were generated as previously reported [9]. Rice seeds were germinated in the dark at 28 °C for 2–3 days until their roots grew. Germinated seedlings were initially cultured in Kimura B complete nutrient solution under hydroponic conditions. Afterward, the seedlings were transferred to soil and grown in a greenhouse with a temperature of 25–30°C, Photosynthetic Photon Flux Density (PPFD) of 800–1000 μmol m−2 s−1, and a photoperiod of 14 h light/10 h dark. Various treatments are described in the corresponding figure legends.

Stress treatment

The rice seedlings were cultivated under standard growth conditions prior to being subjected to stress treatments. The standard growth conditions were as follows: temperature of 25–30°C, Photosynthetic Photon Flux Density (PPFD) of 800–1000 μmol m⁻2 s⁻1, and a photoperiod of 14 h light/10 h dark. In this study, two specific high-light and high-temperature (HLHT) stress regimes were employed as previously reported [13]: (1) Severe HLHT condition: 1,800 μmol m−2 s−1 light intensity and 45–50°C temperature. The light intensity at the plant canopy level was measured using a LI-185B light meter (LI-COR) with a cosine-corrected quantum sensor to ensure accurate light delivery; (2) Mild HLHT condition: 1,200 μmol m−2 s−1 light intensity and 40 °C temperature. The specified light intensities of 1800 and 1200 μmol m⁻2 s⁻1 were achieved using high-power plant growth lamps (GRUFO-KY-150w), supplemented by additional light sources within the growth chamber. Light intensity was similarly measured and adjusted to achieve the desired value at the plant canopy. Detailed information regarding the treatment durations and specific periods is provided in the corresponding figure legends.

The experimental stages and treatment durations were carefully chosen to capture key physiological responses at different growth stages of rice seedlings. For consistency, the sampling times and treatment durations were aligned where possible, as outlined below: HLHT phenotyping was conducted at the 3-leaf stage to assess initial stress responses. For polyethylene glycol (PEG)-mimic drought stress, two-leaf stage OsPLGG1b-OE and osplgg1b plants cultured under normal conditions were subjected to 25% (w/v) PEG 6000 solution for 5 days, followed by 7 days of recovery on standard growth medium. Soil drought stress (Fig. S2) was initiated at the 3-leaf stage to simulate field drought conditions. Photosynthesis measurements (Fig. 6) were taken at the 5-leaf stage, when photosynthetic capacity is more established. Gene expression analysis (Fig. 1A) was performed at the 4-leaf stage to capture intermediate molecular responses. Transcriptomics (HLHT, 4 h) focused on early molecular responses, whereas physiological assays (HLHT, 7 days) assessed longer-term effects of stress exposure.

Construction of the OsPLGG1b-OE and the osplgg1a/OsPLGG1b-OE plants

For the overexpression of OsPLGG1b, the full-length cDNA sequence of OsPLGG1b (Os10g42780) was amplified by RT-PCR, then the sequence was cloned into the pYL-OX expression vector under the control of the ubiquitin promoter. The constructed vectors were introduced into ZH11 and osplgg1a mutants by Agrobacterium-mediated transformation using strain EHA105, generating OsPLGG1b-OE and osplgg1a/OsPLGG1b-OE plants, respectively. Homozygous lines were obtained through hygromycin-resistance screening. The primers used for vector construction are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated with a MiniBEST Plant RNA Extraction Kit (TaKaRa, Tokyo, Japan). The isolated RNA was then treated with DNase Ⅰ and used as a template for first strand cDNA synthesis using a PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (TaKaRa, Dalian, China). qRT-PCR was performed with an AceQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China), with three or four biological repeats. Rice Actin2 was used as an internal reference gene to quantify cDNA abundance. Relative expression levels were calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primers used for qRT-PCR are listed in Table S1.

Gas exchange measurements and chloroplast fluorescence analysis

Gas exchange characteristics including photosynthetic rate (Pn) was analyzed in situ using the portable Pn system LI-6800 (LI-COR, America). Plants grown under normal natural conditions or high light and high temperature conditions. The youngest fully expanded leaves of different plants were used to assess the photosynthetic parameters. Measurements were carried out in clear and cloudless weather between 9 am to 5 pm. In the leaf chamber, the photon flux density (PFD) of the built-in light source was set to 1200 μmol−1 m−2 s−1, the CO2 concentration was 400 μmol mol−1, and the temperature inside was 30 ℃. The leaf to be measured was acclimated for 3–5 min and the data were recorded after the system has stabilized. Photorespiration rate was estimated by subtracting the value of Pn at 21% O2 from that at 2% O2 according to Yeo et al. [14].

Chlorophyll fluorescence was measured using the MINI-PAM portable chlorophyll fluorometer (WALZ, Germany). Leaves were dark adapted for at least 20 min before measurement. The minimum fluorescence (Fo) was first measured with the measuring light (ML) open, followed by a very short duration of saturation pulse (SL) to obtain the maximum fluorescence (Fm). For rapid light curves, leaves were illuminated for 20 s at progressively increasing light intensities. After each illumination gradient, the fluorescence data were recorded after applying a SL. Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters, including the effective quantum yield of PSII (Y(Ⅱ)), the relative electron transport rate (ETR), the photochemical fluorescence quenching (qP) and the non-photochemical fluorescence quenching (NPQ), were calculated by the chlorophyll fluorometer program. Enzyme activity assays

Leaf tissues were harvested at specified time points (for detailed information on treatment durations and sampling periods, refer to the corresponding figure legends) and ground to a fine powder in liquid nitrogen. The powdered tissue was then homogenized in 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 8.0), containing 10% (v/v) glycerol and 0.5 mM EDTA·Na₂. After thorough mixing, the homogenate was centrifuged and the supernatant was collected as the enzyme extract. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was assayed by monitoring the inhibition of the photochemical reduction of nitro-blue tetrazolium (NBT) according to the method of Giannopolitis and Ries [15]. Glycolate oxidase (GLO) activity was determined via a coupled reaction that quantified the H2O2 produced, monitored at 520 nm [16]. Briefly, 1 mL reaction mixture contained 30 mM phosphate buffer (pH 8.0), 3 mM 4-amino-antipyrine, 2.5 mM phenol, 0.1 mM flavin mononucleotide (FMN), 5 mM glycolate, and 5 U horseradish peroxidase. The reaction was initiated by adding 50 µl enzyme extract.

Transcriptome sequencing analysis

Leaves from three-leaf-stage wild-type and OsPLGG1b-OE1 plants, both under normal conditions and after 4 h of HLHT stress (1,800 µmol m−2 s−1 light intensity, 45–50°C) were sampled for RNA sequencing. Three biological replicates were used for each sample. The collected samples were forwarded to Wuhan Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). RNA-seq analysis was performed using Beijing Genomics Institute (BGI, Beijing, China) platform. Fragments were assigned to genes by feature counts and count programs, and gene expression abundance was represented by the fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped fragments (FPKM). Significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified (Fold Change ≥ 1, adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05). Heatmap analysis of the transcriptional levels of these genes was performed using TBtools software [17]. FPKM values were log2-transformed and normalized through row-scaling to standardize the expression patterns of individual genes across all samples. All data related to gene expression profiles are shown in Supplementary Dataset S1.

Metabolite measurements

Metabolite levels were measured in leaves from three-leaf-stage wild-type and OsPLGG1b-OE1 plants, both under normal conditions and after 4 h of HLHT stress (1,800 µmol m⁻2 s⁻1 light intensity, 45–50°C). The same plant material used for transcriptome sequencing was utilized for metabolite analysis. The samples were promptly frozen in liquid nitrogen and sent to Wuhan Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) for targeted metabolomics assays using UPLC-MS/MS. Each sample was represented by three biological replicates. Metabolites were studied by hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA), and heatmaps were generated with the Complex Heatmap package in R software.

As described by Rahman et al. [18], the quantification of oxidized and reduced glutathione (GSSG and GSH) was performed through the chromogenic reaction of GSH with 5,5’-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) at an absorbance wavelength of 412 nm.

Statistical analysis

Statistical differences were calculated using Tukey’s test based on the one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and two-tailed Student’s t-test in Statistical Product and Service Solutions (SPSS, version 22, America). P values smaller than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Except for the heatmaps, which were created using TBtools software [17], all other graphs were generated by GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0, America). Details and numbers of biological replicates are described in the respective figure legends.

Results

OsPLGG1b fails to complement the osplgg1a mutant phenotype

Rice possesses two PLGG1 isoforms, OsPLGG1a and OsPLGG1b. While osplgg1a mutants exhibit severe growth defects, including leaf chlorosis and stunting, osplgg1b mutants display normal growth [9]. To assess potential functional redundancy between OsPLGG1a and OsPLGG1b, we introduced the OsPLGG1b gene, driven by the ubiquitin promoter, into the osplgg1a mutant background, generating osplgg1a/OsPLGG1b-OE transgenic plants. Quantitative RT-PCR analysis confirmed elevated OsPLGG1b transcript levels in two independent homozygous transgenic lines (Fig. 1A). Remarkably, these overexpression lines retained the characteristic osplgg1a mutant phenotype, exhibiting severe growth stunting (Fig. 1B, C). These genetic data demonstrate that, despite their shared biochemical transport capacity, OsPLGG1a and OsPLGG1b play non-redundant roles in rice development.

Effects of OsPLGG1b overexpression on rice agronomic traits

To elucidate the physiological function of OsPLGG1b, we generated OsPLGG1b-overexpressing lines (OsPLGG1b-OE) in the ZH11 background using the ubiquitin promoter. Homozygous transgenic lines were obtained through hygromycin selection. Quantitative RT-PCR analysis revealed that OsPLGG1b transcript levels were significantly upregulated by 14.6-fold and 54-fold in the OsPLGG1b-OE1 and OsPLGG1b-OE2 lines, respectively, compared to the wild type (Fig. 2A). Additionally, we utilized two previously characterized osplgg1b knockout mutants generated via CRISPR/Cas9, which exhibited a complete loss of OsPLGG1b transcripts (Supplementary Fig. S1) [9].

Both the OsPLGG1b-OE lines and the osplgg1b mutants displayed normal growth at the seedling stage (Fig. 2B). To evaluate their agronomic performance, plants were transferred to pots for further growth (Fig. 2C). At the heading stage, no significant differences in net photosynthetic rate were observed among the wild type, osplgg1b mutants, and OsPLGG1b-OE lines (Fig. 2D). However, agronomic traits analyses revealed that the osplgg1b mutants exhibited significantly reduced plant height, effective tiller number, panicle length, and seed setting rates compared to the wild type. These deficits collectively resulted in decreased aboveground dry weight and grain yield per plant in the mutants (Fig. 2E-K). Strikingly, overexpression of OsPLGG1b also negatively impacted plant growth and grain productivity. This effect was particularly pronounced in the OsPLGG1b-OE2 line, which displayed agronomic parameters very similar to those of the osplgg1b mutants (Fig. 2E-K). These findings indicate that both the loss of OsPLGG1b function and its extreme overexpression (as seen in OsPLGG1b-OE2) detrimentally affect rice growth and yield.

Overexpression of OsPLGG1b enhances rice tolerance to abiotic stresses

Photorespiration plays a critical role in plant adaptation to abiotic stresses, particularly under high light and drought conditions [10, 19]. To assess the stress response of OsPLGG1b-OE and osplgg1b plants, we subjected seedlings at the three-leaf stage to combined high-light and high-temperature (HLHT) stress. Compared to control conditions (800 µmol m−2 s−1 light intensity, 25 °C), both severe HLHT (1,800 µmol m−2 s−1 light intensity, 45–50°C) and mild HLHT (1,200 µmol m−2 s−1 light intensity, 40 °C) induced pronounced growth inhibition across all three genotypes (Fig. 3A-C). Strikingly, while severe leaf tip wilting, even lethal under severe HLHT, was evident in both wild-type and osplgg1b plants, the OsPLGG1b-OE lines maintained relatively vigorous growth and displayed significantly reduced wilting symptoms (Fig. 3A, B). This observation indicates that overexpression of OsPLGG1b enhances tolerance to HLHT stress.

We evaluated tolerance to polyethylene glycol (PEG)-mimicking drought stress by exposing two-leaf stage OsPLGG1b-OE and osplgg1b plants to a 25% (w/v) PEG 6000 solution for five days in hydroponics, followed by recovery in standard growth medium. Following the removal of PEG stress, the OsPLGG1b-OE lines exhibited a significantly higher survival rate compared to wild-type plants. Conversely, the osplgg1b mutants showed increased sensitivity to drought stress, resulting in a significantly lower survival rate than the wild type (Fig. 3D, E). This trend was consistent with observations from direct dehydration treatments (Supplementary Fig. S2), further validating the role of OsPLGG1b in drought stress response. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that overexpression of OsPLGG1b significantly improves rice resilience to both HLHT and drought stress.

Overexpression of OsPLGG1b improves the antioxidant system under HLHT stress

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the enhanced HLHT tolerance conferred by OsPLGG1b overexpression, we performed RNA sequencing on leaves of three-leaf-stage OsPLGG1b-OE1 and wild-type plant under normal conditions and after 4 h of severe HLHT stress (1,800 µmol m−2 s−1 light intensity, 45–50°C). Three independent biological replicates were analyzed for each condition.

Transcriptome analysis identified 394 differentially expressed genes (DEGs; Log2 (Fold Change) ≥ 1, adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05) in OsPLGG1b-OE1 compared to the wild type under HLHT stress, whereas only 83 DEGs were detected under normal conditions (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Dataset S1B, C). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of HLHT-induced DEGs revealed significant enrichment in biological processes including diterpenoid metabolism, regulation of salicylic acid biosynthesis, carbohydrate derivative catabolism, and response to oxidative stress (Fig. 4B). Notably, HLHT triggered extensive transcriptomic reprogramming, inducing 2,541 DEGs in the wild type and 2,635 DEGs in OsPLGG1b-OE1 when comparing stressed versus unstressed plants (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Dataset S1D, E).

Examination of specific gene categories showed that high-light responsive markers, such as Early Light-Induced Protein (ELIP) genes [20], were strongly upregulated by HLHT (Fig. 4C). In contrast, photosynthesis-associated genes, particularly those encoding light-harvesting complex (LHC), were downregulated in both genotypes under HLHT stress (Fig. 4C; Supplementary Fig. S3; Supplementary Dataset S1F). Conversely, genes encoding heat shock proteins (HSPs) and antioxidant enzymes (e.g., SODA and FSD) were significantly induced by HLHT, with the highest induction levels consistently observed in OsPLGG1b-OE1 plants (Fig. 4C; Supplementary Dataset S1F), potentially contributing to their enhanced stress tolerance. Consistent with these transcriptional changes, superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was elevated in both OsPLGG1b-OE and wild-type leaves following HLHT treatment, with relative higher in OsPLGG1b-OE plants compared to the wild type (Fig. 4D). The MDA content (Fig. 4E) and H2O2 levels (Fig. 4F) were also measured to assess oxidative stress responses in both OsPLGG1b-OE and wild-type plants under HLHT conditions. Additionally, OsPLGG1b-OE plants exhibited improved glutathione redox homeostasis under HLHT stress, characterized by higher levels of reduced glutathione (GSH) and a significantly elevated GSH/GSSG ratio compared to the wild type (Fig. 4G). These findings demonstrate that OsPLGG1b overexpression enhances the antioxidant machinery, promoting redox homeostasis and improving stress tolerance under HLHT conditions.

Overexpression of OsPLGG1b promotes accumulation of amino acids and organic acids under HLHT stress

To investigate the metabolic changes associated with OsPLGG1b overexpression, we conducted targeted metabolomic analysis using UPLC-MS/MS. This approach enabled the quantitative profiling of 60 metabolites, encompassing nucleotides and derivatives, amino acids, organic acids and derivatives, carbohydrates, and phosphorylated sugars (Supplementary Table S2). Hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) of these metabolites is presented as a heatmap (Fig. 5A).

Under HLHT stress, the abundance of most amino acids, including serine, glutamate, glutamine, and alanine, were significantly elevated. Notably, this accumulation was most pronounced in OsPLGG1b-OE1 leaves (Fig. 5B). Given that serine is a core photorespiratory intermediate and glutamate is an integral component of the photorespiratory nitrogen cycle [4], this pattern suggests an increased photorespiratory flux in OsPLGG1b-OE1 plants under HLHT stress. Additionally, we observed increased abundance of organic acids, particularly key tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediates such as citrate, α-ketoglutarate, and succinate. The highest levels of these metabolites were detected in HLHT-treated OsPLGG1b-OE1 plants (Fig. 5C), highlighting their critical roles in energy metabolism during HLHT stress.

Overexpression of OsPLGG1b enhances photorespiration and improves photosynthetic efficiency under HLHT stress

To evaluate the impact of OsPLGG1b overexpression on photosynthetic performance under stress, five-leaf-stage OsPLGG1b-OE and wild-type plants were subjected to mild HLHT stress (1,200 µmol m−2 s−1 light intensity, 40 °C) for 7 days. Under normal growth conditions, no significant differences were observed between OsPLGG1b-OE and wild-type plants in net photosynthetic rate (Pn) and photorespiratory rate (Fig. 6A, B). However, HLHT treatment significantly reduced Pn while increasing photorespiratory rate in both genotypes (Fig. 6A, B). This observation aligns with transcriptomic data, which revealed downregulation of photosynthesis-associated genes (e.g., LHC genes) and upregulation of photorespiratory genes (e.g., GLO1) under HLHT stress (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Fig. S3; Supplementary Dataset S1F). Notably, under HLHT conditions, OsPLGG1b-OE plants maintained significantly higher Pn and photorespiratory rate compared to the wild type (Fig. 6A, B). Consistent with the elevated photorespiratory flux indicated by the increased photorespiratory rate, glycolate oxidase (GLO) activity was induced by HLHT treatment and was significantly higher in OsPLGG1b-OE plants than in the wild type (Fig. 6C). GLO catalyzes the oxidation of glycolate, the substrate transported by OsPLGG1b [9, 21].

Chlorophyll fluorescence analysis showed that, under normal conditions, OsPLGG1b-OE lines exhibited values of the effective quantum yield of PSII (Y(II)) comparable to the wild type. However, they showed slight decreases in photochemical fluorescence quenching (qP) and electron transport rate (ETR), coupled with a significant reduction in non-photochemical fluorescence quenching (NPQ) (Fig. 6D-G). HLHT stress substantially decreased PSII activity in the wild type, but its impact on OsPLGG1b-OE lines was relatively less severe. Notably, under HLHT stress, OsPLGG1b-OE plants maintained higher ETR and qP values than the wild type (Fig. 6E, F). These findings collectively demonstrate that OsPLGG1b overexpression enhances photosynthetic efficiency under HLHT stress by sustaining higher PSII activity and photorespiratory flux.

Discussion

Expression level of OsPLGG1b modulates rice growth and agronomic traits

Both OsPLGG1a and OsPLGG1b function as glycolate/glycerate transporters in rice. However, osplgg1a and osplgg1b loss-of-function mutants exhibit distinct growth phenotypes [8, 9]. Our findings further demonstrate that overexpression of OsPLGG1b fails to rescue the growth inhibition phenotype of osplgg1a mutants (Fig. 1). This lack of complementation aligns with their reported subcellular localization: OsPLGG1a resides in the chloroplast inner envelope membrane, while OsPLGG1b is localized to the outer envelope membrane [9]. This distinct compartmentalization strongly suggests non-redundant physiological functions, raising the critical question: What is the specific physiological role of the outer membrane-localized transporter OsPLGG1b?

Our results reveal that constitutive overexpression of OsPLGG1b negatively impacts rice growth and productivity, leading to reduced aboveground biomass and grain yield (Fig. 2). Similar detrimental effects have been observed in Arabidopsis overexpressing AtLrgB/PLGG1 and tomato overexpressing SlLrgB/SlPLGG1 [22, 23]. However, this contrasts with overexpression studies of other photorespiratory genes, such as phosphoglycolate phosphatase 1 (PGLP1), glycine decarboxylase H-protein (GDC-H) and serine hydroxymethyltransferase 1 (SHMT1), which typically enhance plant photosynthesis, biomass or yields [1, 24–26].

The negative correlation between OsPLGG1b overexpression and growth may stem from imbalances in carbon flux and carbohydrate metabolism induced by constitutive high-level expression. Supporting this notion, constitutive overexpression of GDC-H in tobacco under the 35S promoter reduced carbohydrate content and impaired early growth [26]. Similarly, rice GLO1 overexpression lines with moderate (60–100%) increases in GLO activity showed improved growth, whereas lines with very high (150–210%) activity increases exhibited severe growth retardation [27]. In this study, the OsPLGG1b-OE1 line (14.6-fold overexpression) displayed less severe negative impacts than the OsPLGG1b-OE2 line (54-fold overexpression) (Fig. 2), highlighting the importance of expression level. Future studies might benefit from using stress-inducible promoters to fine-tune OsPLGG1b expression, potentially mitigating the negative effects of constitutive overexpression while retaining its beneficial roles under stress conditions.

Overexpression of OsPLGG1b enhances rice stress tolerance

Crucially, our findings demonstrate that OsPLGG1b overexpression significantly enhances rice tolerance to abiotic stresses, including HLHT and drought stress (Fig. 3). This enhanced tolerance aligns with observations for other photorespiratory pathway components, for instance, overexpression of Arabidopsis PGLP1 alleviates 2-PG-mediated inhibition of the Calvin-Benson cycle, thereby improving stress tolerance [1, 28]. Similarly, rice GLO1 overexpression enhances photosynthetic efficiency under high light and temperature stress [27]. This enhanced tolerance likely results from increased photorespiratory flux, evidenced by elevated photorespiration rates, higher glycolate oxidase activity, and accumulation of photorespiratory intermediates (e.g., serine) in OsPLGG1b-OE lines under HLHT stress (Figs. 5, 6).

Photorespiration serves as a critical component of abiotic stress responses. By consuming excess reductants generated by the chloroplast electron transport chain, it mitigates reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation under stress conditions [10, 19, 29]. Additionally, abiotic stresses often induce stomatal closure, reducing intercellular CO2 concentration and consequently limiting carbon fixation [12, 30]. The increased photorespiratory flux facilitated by OsPLGG1b overexpression may help alleviate this limitation. By accelerating the photorespiratory cycle, it promotes the release of CO2 within the pathway, potentially mitigating CO2 limitation in the Calvin cycle and helping maintain essential metabolic activity under stress [10, 31]. Although stomata typically open under HLHT stress for transpirational cooling, photorespiration, enhanced by OsPLGG1b overexpression, may still help alleviate metabolic constraints by mitigating oxidative stress and stabilizing cellular processes, even when CO₂ limitation is less pronounced. Consistent with this, the metabolomic data revealed higher accumulation of organic acids and amino acids in OsPLGG1b-OE plants under HLHT stress (Fig. 5). These accumulated intermediates may further contribute to stress tolerance: amino acids can stabilize proteins and membranes and act as ROS scavengers, while organic acids, particularly tricarboxylic acid cycle intermediates, are crucial for energy production and metabolic adaptation during stress [32–34]. The detrimental effects of OsPLGG1b overexpression may stem from feedback inhibition on OsPLGG1a, disrupting the balance between these isoforms and limiting the potential benefits of OsPLGG1b overexpression, particularly under stress conditions.

In summary, OsPLGG1b, localized to the chloroplast outer envelope membrane, cooperates with the inner envelope protein OsPLGG1a to transport glycolate and glycerate. Our findings indicate that overexpression of OsPLGG1b promotes glycolate export from chloroplasts, thereby accelerating photorespiratory carbon flux. While constitutive high-level expression can negatively impact growth under optimal conditions, this enhanced flux significantly improves rice adaptability to abiotic stresses, particularly HLHT and drought, by modulating redox balance, mitigating CO2 limitation, and facilitating metabolic adjustments. This study highlights the critical role of OsPLGG1b in improving plant resilience to abiotic stress and its potential as a target for crop improvement.
