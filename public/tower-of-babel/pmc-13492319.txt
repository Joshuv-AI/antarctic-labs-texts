Introduction

Radiofrequency ablation (RFA) has proven efficacy and safety in atrial and ventricular arrhythmia. However, close proximity to coronary artery (CA) can impair the ability to ablate at the best site and reduces the efficacy of the procedure. We report a first-in-human application of a technique to mitigate CA injury while ensuring procedural efficacy during RFA.

Case report

A 71-year-old woman was seen in the primary care physician clinic for palpitations. Electrocardiogram (ECG) was concerning for ventricular trigeminy. A 3-day Holter study showed 32% premature ventricular complex (PVC) burden in singles, couplets, non-sustained ventricular tachycardia (VT) runs, and asymptomatic episodes of self-terminating sustained monomorphic VT. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed normal RV size and function. Late gadolinium enhancement was abnormal with extensive fatty infiltration of mid-to-distal LV epicardium and mid myocardium (Figure 1A). Genetic analysis results showed pathogenic DSP, Exon 23, c.4044_4047del (p.Ser1349Argfs∗2), heterozygous mutation. This mutation is pathogenic for left-dominant arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy.1,2 Family history was negative for any significant known heart disease. In the electrophysiology laboratory she had frequent PVCs of right bundle branch block morphology with inferior axis. WebsterTM Decapolar catheter (D-F curve, 7F) was positioned in coronary sinus (CS) with distal electrode at 12 o’clock position (CS-anterior interventricular vein junction [great cardiac vein: (GCV)]). Since earliest ventricular electrogram (EGM) during clinical PVC was seen on the distal electrode of the CS catheter, activation mapping using 3.5-mm tip, 8F, F curve THERMOCOOL® SMARTTOUCH irrigated Biosense ablation catheter was performed in this region. No sheath was used to position ablation catheter in CS/GCV. Local activation was 52 ms pre-PVC QRS with ablation catheter tip positioned at 12 o’clock position in GCV and QS pattern on the distal unipolar EGM (Figure 1B). None of the proximal sites in GCV showed EGMs as early or earlier. Hemodynamically stable, self-terminating VT was induced with programmed stimulation from RV mid septum with 2 extrastimuli (drive cycle length 500 ms; S2 250 ms; S3 240 ms). Morphology of induced VT was similar to clinical PVC (Figure 1C). The earliest activation site was 2.5 mm from major CA on multiple views (Figure 2A and 2B) during coronary angiography. This was measured at the point in the cardiac cycle where the electrode was in the closest proximity to the left circumflex CA in the right anterior oblique and left anterior oblique projections. Distance was measured from the center of the tip of ablation catheter to outer margin of CA when fully opacified with contrast. Twenty milliliter of VisipaqueTM (iodixanol) contrast diluted in a 2:1 ratio with heparinized saline solution was injected into left circumflex artery slowly, while RFA was performed at this site for 10 seconds using a power of 15 W. PVCs were initially abolished but recurred 5 minutes later at a significantly reduced frequency. When ablation was re-attempted at 15 W without concomitant coronary angiogram (CAg) patient complained of chest pain. Repeat ablation for 20 seconds during concurrent CAg did not result in chest pain and reduced PVC load by >75% with only infrequent PVCs observed. VT was no more inducible. A total of 60 seconds of ablation was performed at this site (Figure 2C). Post-ablation in GCV, activation mapping was continued in outflow tracts and left ventricle (LV). Endocardially, the earliest local EGM 22 ms pre-PVC QRS onset was seen at a site opposite to the location of CS 3,4 electrode. (Figure 1D). RF ablation at this site (Figure 2C and 2D) did not further reduce the PVC burden beyond what had already been achieved with ablation in GCV. No additional ablation was performed. Acutely and during 45-minute waiting period, no ECG changes suggestive of myocardial ischemia or injury were observed. PVCs were infrequent and VT was not inducible at the end of the case on and off isoproterenol with programmed stimulation, compared to induction of VT without isoproterenol prior to ablation. Repeat CAg did not show any changes in CA compared to pre-ablation.

Post-ablation event monitor confirmed complete elimination of PVC (Figure 1E). No recurrence of PVC/VT was seen at 8 months follow-up and patient received implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD).

In this method we initially confirm the proximity of the CA to the site of origin of ventricular arrhythmia using multiple fluoroscopic views by injecting contrast diluted in normal saline. If the distance of CA from site of interest for ablation is between 2.5 mm and 5 mm, contrast injection is initiated just before ablation and continued slowly during the ablation. Ablation is performed in repeated applications of 20–30 seconds, with power of ≤20 W and the temperature less than 39°C. This method has not been used if the distance is <2.5 mm. We do not have the exact value of contact force at the successful ablation site in the GCV. Emphasis is placed on the EGMs at the ablation site, detailed activation map, manual review of bipolar and unipolar EGMs at multiple sites within the CS, and the directionality of contact force vector, which should be pointing towards the epicardial surface during ablation.

Since this case, we have applied the method in 5 additional cases, 2 involving focal epicardial VT and 3 epicardial monomorphic PVC. In these cases, the site of origin was 2.5–5 mm from the CA. In both VT cases and 1 PVC case there was complete elimination with ablation from CS/GCV. In the remaining 2 PVC cases there was >75% reduction in PVC load with epicardial ablation. We did not measure serum troponin post procedure before discharge, as none of the patients reported chest pain or other symptoms post ablation.

Discussion

CA injury is among the rarest complications of RFA. Roberts-Thomson et al3 reported 0.09% of patients undergoing ablation experiencing CA injury. However, inability to ablate secondary to proximity to CA can reduce procedural success. In their study, Stavrakis et al4 suggested RFA within <5 mm of a significant CA should be carefully considered. They also demonstrated that the risk of injuring a CA, as a function of the distance from the CA, represents a continuum, rather than an all-or-none phenomenon. Haines et al5 similarly showed that temperature decreases through tissue in a hyperbolic fashion and the likelihood of thermal injury to CAs decreases as distance from the catheter tip increases. The risk is negligible for distances >5 mm.

In “heat sink effect,” coronary blood flow within the vessel provides protective feature by preventing substantive heating of the vascular endothelium.6,7 This protective effect is further enhanced by increased diameter and increased flow in the vessel. Hindricks et al8 proposed that convective cooling might be the reason for paucity of reported CA complications. It is important to note that tissue–temperature model is independent of the convective cooling phenomenon. Although the “heat sink effect” gives the protective effect, “shadow effect” can reduce the efficacy of the same radiofrequency lesion. The shadow effect needs to be weighed against the heat sink effect to maximize the likelihood that RF lesion is effective.6 In our case, shadow effect might have contributed to the initial recurrence of PVCs 5 minutes after the first ablation, although this phenomenon may be more relevant to sites in intimate contact with CA. Scharf et al9 reported a case where right CA balloon occlusion was used to avoid heat convection during RFA of an accessory pathway. The case demonstrates the shadow effect resulting in failure to ablate accessory pathway when ballon occlusion was not done in prior RF application.

Thyer et al10 in 2006 demonstrated on excised ovine hearts, that intracoronary irrigation with chilled saline may protect the CA endothelium from heat damage during epicardial RFA. Our case is a first reported clinical application of intracoronary fluid delivery, specifically diluted iodinated contrast, to achieve convective coronary protection during RFA in a living human patient. In our case, we demonstrate a safe method of RFA when the site of interest is 2.5–5 mm from one of the major CA. In all 6 cases we have used diluted contrast (as mentioned previously) and have paused after 20 seconds before repeating another ablation at the same site, with a maximum total ablation duration of 120 seconds. A detailed activation map was constructed with manual review of local bipolar and unipolar EGMs before determining the optimal ablation site. If electroanatomic mapping is used, after the initial CAg confirms the distance of the ablation site from the CA, intra-coronary infusion during ablation serves an exclusively thermo-protective purpose rather than an imaging one. Normal saline, preferably chilled, would fulfill the same protective function with meaningful advantages: lower viscosity yielding higher flow rates and superior convective heat dissipation, no nephrotoxic risk, and lower cost. Contrast infusion during ablation could be used in cases without electroanatomic mapping. A prospective comparative evaluation of chilled saline vs diluted iodinated contrast is needed in future studies.

Chest pain when RFA was done without concomitant CAg could be secondary to heating of the sensitive pericardial tissue. Absence of the pain with CA cooling underlines the competition between the local heating due to RF delivery and cooling due to injection of the room temperature solution into the CA.

Increase rate of flow in CA during angiogram and replacement of blood by cooler contrast are both plausible reasons for safety of RFA at sites closer to CA.

Our patient had a non-missense (truncating) mutation in desmoplakin which is classically associated with extensive LV gadolinium enhancement with near normal RV performance in left-dominant arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy.11 Ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death occur in the absence of overt LV dysfunction prompting ICD implantation. To our knowledge, this is the first-in-human application of a technique demonstrating the safety and efficacy of RFA close to CA.

Conclusion

Ventricular arrhythmias arising 2.5–5 mm from a major CA can be safely ablated during simultaneous intracoronary fluid delivery. Diluted iodinated contrast was used in this series; chilled saline may be preferable when electroanatomical mapping is available, and prospective comparison is warranted.
