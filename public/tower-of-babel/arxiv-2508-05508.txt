Auto-Eval Judge: Towards a General Agentic Framework for Task Completion Evaluation
Roshita Bhonsle, Rishav Dutta, Sneha Vavilapalli, Harsh Seth, Abubakarr Jaye, Yapei Chang, Mukund Rungta, Emmanuel Aboah Boateng, Sadid Hasan, Ehi Nosakhare, Soundar Srinivasan

Abstract
The increasing adoption of foundation models as agents across diverse domains necessitates a robust evaluation framework. Current methods, such as LLM-as-a-Judge, focus only on final outputs, overlooking the step-by-step reasoning that drives agentic decision-making. Meanwhile, existing Agent-as-a-Judge systems, where one agent evaluates another’s task completion, are typically designed for narrow, domain-specific settings. To address this gap, we propose a generalizable, modular framework for evaluating agent task completion independent of the task domain. The framework emulates human-like evaluation by decomposing tasks into sub-tasks and validating each step using available information, such as the agent’s output and reasoning. Each module contributes to a specific aspect of the evaluation process, and their outputs are aggregated to produce a final verdict on task completion. We validate our framework by evaluating the Magentic-One Actor Agent on two benchmarks, GAIA and BigCodeBench. Our Judge Agent predicts task success with closer agreement to human evaluations, achieving 4.76% and 10.52% higher alignment accuracy, respectively, compared to the GPT-4o based LLM-as-a-Judge baseline. This demonstrates the potential of our proposed general-purpose evaluation framework. * * footnotetext: Equal Contribution 1 1 1 Code will be made available on GitHub.

Introduction

Problem and Motivation

We propose a general-purpose, scalable, and modular Agent-as-a-Judge evaluation framework designed to assess agentic task performance with minimal human oversight, while maintaining robustness across diverse domains and datasets.

Human evaluation continues to serve as the de facto standard for determining task success in agentic systems. However, it is inherently limited by its high cost, subjectivity, and lack of scalability posing a critical obstacle to widespread deployment of autonomous agents in real-world settings.

Recent advances have explored leveraging large language models (LLMs) and autonomous agents as evaluators. Notable examples include the LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm
[
7
]
and Meta’s Agent-as-a-Judge framework
[
8
]
. While promising, these approaches exhibit key limitations: (1) they operate primarily on final outputs and ignore intermediate reasoning traces; (2) they often rely on post hoc human adjudication for ground-truth validation; and (3) they are narrowly scoped, often bound to specific task domains or evaluation protocols.

The following definitions will be used consistently throughout this paper:

•

Actor
: the agent responsible for executing a task through sequential reasoning and interaction.

•

Judge
: the agent tasked with evaluating the
Actor
’s performance by analyzing both its intermediate reasoning states and final output artifacts.

To address these shortcomings, we formalize the evaluation setup by introducing two core agentic roles and framing our contributions around two central research questions:

Research Questions

•

RQ1
: How can the task completion ability of Agentic Systems be evaluated across a wide range of domains?

•

RQ2
: How can the quality and human-alignment of
Judge
, tasked with evaluating these agentic systems, be improved?

By addressing these questions, we aim to establish a general evaluation framework that reduces human dependency while maintaining semantic rigor and cross-domain applicability.

Contributions

The key contributions of this work are:

•

We present a domain-agnostic decomposition of the agent evaluation task, enabling step-wise reasoning over task completion without presupposing task-specific heuristics.

•

We release an open-source, modular, and extensible evaluation framework equipped with interface-consistent modules that can be independently substituted or enhanced. A concrete instantiation demonstrates its compatibility and generalization across multiple domains.

•

Empirical results show that our implementation consistently outperforms existing baselines, achieving stronger alignment with human evaluations (see
Figure 1
).

Proposed Approach

Our framework is inspired by how expert human evaluators decompose complex tasks into logical subgoals and assess correctness at each step via evidence-based reasoning.

The
Judge
agent architecture incorporates multiple LLM-based and agentic modules that collectively verify both intermediate reasoning steps and final outputs of the
Actor
agent. For our experiments, we instantiate the
Actor
using the Magentic-One agent, a general-purpose agent capable of multi-step task execution. Architectural and implementation details are provided in Section 3.

We evaluate our framework on two benchmark datasets, GAIA and BigCodeBench, which focus on text-based tasks. Evaluation quality is assessed by comparing the
Judge
’s verdicts against human annotations to measure alignment and reliability.

Related Work

Recent research has increasingly focused on evaluating large language models (LLMs) and autonomous agents by leveraging other LLMs or agents as evaluators, aiming to supplement or even replace human evaluation.

LLM-as-a-Judge:

Several studies have shown that LLMs can serve as effective judges. For example, MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena
[
7
]
use human-aligned LLMs to score open-ended responses, showing high agreement with human evaluations. Compass-Judger-1
[
1
]
trains an open-source judge using a mix of public and self-collected feedback data for scoring and pairwise comparisons. Prometheus
[
4
]
further improves evaluation fidelity by training on a diverse feedback dataset, achieving results comparable to GPT-4.

While effective, these methods focus on static, text-based tasks. Our work builds on them by expanding evaluation to more complex, step-driven agentic behaviors.

Multi-LLM as Judge Evaluation:

Multi-LLM setups simulate human-like debate to enhance evaluation quality. ChatEval
[
2
]
assigns roles to LLMs for collaborative judging, while AutoArena
[
6
]
uses peer debate and committee voting to select top responses. Though limited to chat tasks, these frameworks motivate our own
Judge
design, aimed at evaluating broader workflows.

Evaluating Agentic Systems:

Agent-as-a-Judge
[
8
]
introduces the idea of agent judging agents. However, it is constrained to DevAI dataset which contains ML tasks introduced through this paper. Moreover, this work needs a set of soft and hard requirements for each task as an input in order to perform evaluations, which hugely limits the extensibility of this method towards other datasets of same domain. Our work removes this requirement by automatically generating a set of checklist criteria agnostic to domain or dataset.

Step-by-Step Evaluation:

Since Agentic Systems often operate in sequences, evaluation should track not just final outputs but intermediate steps. LLM-Reasoners
[
3
]
proposes AutoRace to assess reasoning chains like Chain of Thought and Tree of Thought for LLMs. We adopt a similar philosophy, our
Judge
agent evaluates the completeness and correctness of each step in the
Actor
agent’s process, enabling more accurate and transparent assessments.

Method

Figure 2
. gives an overview of the proposed
Judge
system. The remainder of this section describes the functional modules of the
Judge
framework in detail.

3.1
Criteria Generator

Given a task description, the Criteria Generator module generates a concise list of checklist questions which must be met by the
Actor
Agent to successfully complete the task. The generated checklist aims to verify whether the agent has satisfied the “explicit” requirements stated in the task, while minimizing assumptions.

It follows detailed prompting guidelines to ensure that each checklist question is strictly aligned with the task. The questions are designed to be binary ("yes" or "no") in nature and focus on a single requirement at a time. This approach simplifies evaluation by avoiding complex, nested questions that would otherwise require additional reasoning or multiple steps.

To further enhance relevance, an LLM-based filtering step is applied post-generation to remove questions that are either redundant or loosely connected to the task goal.

shows a comprehensive overview of the data flow for the implementation of this module.

3.2
Artifact Content Parser

This module is responsible for structuring and accurately retrieving relevant information ("proofs") from the log file generated by the
Actor
.
The first step is handled by the
Indexer
, followed by the
Retriever
.

This entire pipeline is inspired by the Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) approach.
Figure 4
describes the overall flow.

Figure 4:
Architecture overview for Artifact Content Parser. The blue box represent input to the module and the green box depicts output.

Indexer:

It handles the efficient organization and structuring of log files (with current focus on a single log file) which is generally over 2000 lines.
It operates in two key steps:

1.

Chunking
:
The Indexer begins with chunking. There are two key motivations for this step. First, lengthy log files generated by the
Actor
may exceed the context window of the language model. Second, even if the entire content of the log fits within the context window, chunking improves efficiency by reducing irrelevant information and optimizing latency and token usage.

We use 300-token chunks, as this configuration performs best in combination with a cross-encoder during retrieval.

2.

Summarization
: Each chunk is summarized using an LLM to serve as a content index, which is important given the presence of mixed formats such as code, HTML tags, and plain text in the log. This improves performance significantly compared to using the raw chunks directly.

The resulting summaries are passed to the next component, the
Retriever
.

The
Retriever
is responsible for identifying and extracting the relevant proofs from the log that align with specific checklist questions. This involves two steps:

1.

Chunk Retrieval
: Cross-encoder (ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2) uses the checklist question against each generated summaries to identify the most relevant chunks. The relevant chunks here are chosen based on a set threshold.

2.

Snippet Extraction
: For each relevant chunk, an LLM is employed to extract the precise snippet from the relevant chunks that serves as "proof" against each checklist question. These extracted snippets against each checklist question are passed to the subsequent module, the Criteria Check Composer.

3.3
Criteria Check Composer (C3)

This module functions as the final integrative module responsible for synthesizing all upstream outputs into a coherent verification strategy. For each checklist query, C3 constructs an execution plan by referencing the relevant
Actor
logs and using LLM-based reasoning alongside optionally using any externally provisioned computational assets such as structured knowledge bases, tools (like web search, file explorer, computer terminal, etc.).

These auxiliary resources may encompass annotator-derived procedural traces (e.g., intermediate steps recorded during dataset curation), canonical task solutions, predefined lookup tables, or any dataset-embedded metadata intended to support interpretability or correctness checking.

To architect the verification pipeline, C3 leverages modular perceptual analyzers specific to each input modality or source. These submodules facilitate the decomposition, classification, and semantic parsing of input signals, enabling the system to instantiate a dynamic, decision tree based action graph. This graph encodes conditional verification pathways, allowing for fine-grained alignment between evidence sources and checklist criteria.
Figure 5
provides a schematic representation of the system’s architecture.

Figure 5:
Architecture overview for Criteria Check Composer. The blue boxes represent inputs to the module (with dashed arrow as an optional input) and the green box depicts output. The dark gray box is the middle highlights the implicit/explicit components constituting this module

Checklist Question Perception.

This component implements a hierarchical multi-stage classification pipeline over checklist-style queries, leveraging large language model (LLM) inference at each stage. At the first level, each question is classified into one of two primary semantic categories:
\mathbf{factual}
or
\mathbf{logical}
. A query is labeled as
\mathbf{factual}
if its resolution necessitates incorporation of external world knowledge or previously acquired domain understanding that is not inferable from the execution trace or log artifacts alone. Conversely, a
\mathbf{logical}
query is one whose truth value or correctness can be determined strictly from the internal artifacts of task execution (e.g., function outputs, logs, or traces), potentially requiring auxiliary symbolic tools (e.g., code interpreters, calculators) or embedded logical reasoning mechanisms.

Subsequently, questions categorized as
\mathbf{logical}
are further disambiguated into subtypes:
\mathbf{reasoning}
or
\mathbf{coding}
. The
\mathbf{reasoning}
class encapsulates tasks requiring abstract inference, deductive logic, or multi-step cognitive operations over the observed artifacts, while the
\mathbf{coding}
class pertains to instances requiring programmatic analysis, code generation, or interpretation. Class labels are deliberately designed to be interpretable yet exhaustive within the operational scope of our task framework. Each classification stage is independently parameterized and executed through discrete LLM invocations to maintain modularity and compositionality.

Proof Perception.

This sub-module conducts an automated verification pass to determine whether the candidate proofs associated with each checklist item are semantically sufficient for downstream evaluation. If the verification yields inconclusive or incomplete justification, the module triggers an expansion of the contextual retrieval window to incorporate broader evidence. This decision process is implemented via an LLM-based inference mechanism.

Conditioned on the inferred verification type, the system dispatches the corresponding evaluation task to either a specialized language model or a general-purpose LLM-driven agentic pipeline. As detailed in
Figure 6
, verification tasks categorized as
\mathbf{factual}
or
\mathbf{coding}
are processed through a modular multi-agent architecture, Magentic-One. This framework comprises a planning agent (Orchestrator), a code generation agent, a retrieval/surfing agent, and a code execution environment. The planner synthesizes a verification trajectory which is subsequently executed against the retrieved artifacts. Conversely,
\mathbf{reasoning}
-type tasks are verified via a single-step LLM inference, as they predominantly involve abstract or logical inference chains that do not benefit from procedural decomposition.

Task Perception.

Rather than constituting a discrete module, task perception functions as an overarching conceptual layer within the C3 pipeline. Its role is to inform verification planning by coupling checklist-based reasoning with a nuanced, semantic interpretation of the task description. To operationalize this, each LLM-driven sub-step is explicitly conditioned on the task under evaluation, embedding task-level awareness into the examination of both proofs and checklist items. This integration ensures that verification is contextually grounded and sensitive to the specificities of the task at hand.

Verdict Generator

Finally, this module reasons with all the available information and outputs of previous modules to come to a verdict of whether the task was successfully completed by the
Actor
. For example: Given a task, list of checklist questions with their proofs that pertain to each checklist question, and the output from each checklist from C3, the verdict reasoner gives a single "Yes" or "No" as the answer.

Data source

Our approach currently leverages

4.1.2
Data splits

Since our focus is purely on evaluation, the dataset is sampled as follows:

•

From GAIA, 42 out of 53 Level-1 tasks were selected, excluding those with multimodal attachments. A balanced distribution of
Actor
runs was ensured across these tasks where 21 tasks were completed successfully and 21 were not.

•

From BigCodeBench, 38 tasks were selected, excluding those with incorrect test-cases (which would have made the evaluation ambiguious). 28 tasks were completed successfully and 10 were not.

4.2
Evaluation Method

For each dataset, three types of evaluations were considered:

1.

Human Evaluation:

•

If ground truth was available in the dataset, it was used to guide the evaluation.

•

If ground truth was not available, evaluators assessed correctness based on available evaluation methods, such as test cases.

•

In the absence of both, annotators manually verified task completion through direct inspection.

2.

LLM as a Judge Verdict:

•

A binary verdict (
Yes
or
No
) generated by GPT-4o-based LLM on the provided task description and final output.

3.

Judge Verdict:

•

A binary verdict (
Yes
or
No
) produced by our framework, given the task description and the corresponding log file.

To measure evaluation quality, human alignment was calculated by constructing a confusion matrix between the human evaluation and the verdicts generated by each method. Human alignment with the LLM as a Judge serves as the baseline for evaluation. The human alignment score for the
Judge
verdict is compared against this baseline to assess improvements.

4.3
Baselines

Due to the lack of prior research for a general agent as a judge across domains, there are no agentic baselines to consider, we implement LLM-as-a-Judge as the baseline. This choice stems from the hypothesis that agentic judge, which incorporates intermediate reasoning steps, will outperform a LLM-based judge that relies solely on final outputs.
To address the context window limitations of LLMs in LLM-as-a-Judge, we pass only the final output of the
Actor
to the LLM-as-a-Judge, instead of verbose log files. By focusing solely on the final output, we effectively mitigate issues caused by the excessive length of detailed logs.

5.1
Error Analysis

•

Some evaluation tasks demand more than just a binary check. For example, given the task -

"Of the authors (First M. Last) that worked on the paper ’sPie Menus or Linear Menus, Which Is Better?’ in 2015, what was the title of the first paper authored by the one that had authored prior papers?"

our existing judge framework bases its evaluations on evidence extracted from the actor agent’s execution logs. In this instance, the judge assigns positive verdict essentially trusting
the validity of those logs, even though the actor’s answer does not match the ground truth. To preserve a clear distinction from the Actor Agent, our current framework prevents the judge from solving the tasks directly. However, the example highlights that it is imperative for a judge system to perform the task itself to validate answers concretely.

•

The Artifact Content Parser has mainly two failure modes. First, it occasionally injects opinions into its output; although undesirable, this behavior does not really impair the Criteria Check Composer (C3) module’s ability to verify claims. Second, the parser sometimes extracts proof snippets directly from the Actor Plan. In these instances, C3 interprets these proofs as established evidence of that ‘action’ being performed and consequently generates incorrect verdict.

•

In some tasks that involve “role-playing” examples like:

“You are Van Helsing, a renowned vampire hunter. A Count of Moldova, La»õcu IV, son of Costea, has tasked you with investigating the village of »òirnea in neighboring Wallachia. The Count’s advisors have reported that a vampire was spotted crossing the border near the village, and would like you to investigate it.”

the Content Generator module often confuses the fictional role-play directives with the actor agent’s actual behavior. As a result, it may pose verification questions that assume in-world actions rather than monitoring what the agent genuinely executed. For example:

–

Did the AI agent travel to the village of

S

,

irnea as instructed?

–

Did the AI agent observe the vampire jumping from rooftop to rooftop?

–

Did the AI agent attempt to chase the vampire back to its home?

Because these queries do not focus on the agent’s logged operations, the system cannot accurately verify whether the agent performed them.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced a modular, domain-agnostic framework for evaluating agent task completion by assessing not just final outcomes, but the reasoning steps behind them. Our Judge Agent, evaluated on GAIA and BigCodeBench, achieved 4.76% and 10.52% higher alignment with human judgments compared to the GPT-4o LLM-as-a-Judge baseline.

These results highlight the strength of step-wise evaluation in capturing task completion in a more robust manner, offering a scalable foundation for evaluating increasingly capable agentic systems.

However, the current framework is not without limitations. It does not yet support the evaluation of multi-modal tasks, datasets, or domains. The Criteria Generator is currently limited to generating checklist questions for text-based tasks and does not handle tasks involving files or attachments. Likewise, the Artifact Content Parser can process only a single log file and does not account for multiple outputs or additional artifacts produced during task execution.

To address these gaps, we plan to extend the framework with an Environment Explorer module, integrated into the Criteria Check Composer, to support inspection of file-based outputs and more complex evaluation scenarios.
