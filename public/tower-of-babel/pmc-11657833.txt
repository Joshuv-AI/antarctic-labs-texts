Active bodies - active minds? A deep dive into the voluntary sports programs for individuals with psychiatric disorders in Switzerland: participants view
Authors: Carina Baumberger, Frank Wieber, Carole Pauli, Daniela Loosli, Birgit Kleim, Nikolai Kiselev
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe positive influence of physical activities on a person’s physical and mental health has been proven many times. These findings were implemented in the regular exercise therapy offered within psychiatric hospitals in Switzerland. However, after leaving a psychiatric hospital, there are insufficient opportunities for individuals with psychiatric disorders to engage in sports. Therefore, voluntary sports groups for this specific population have been offered in Switzerland by the disability sports systems since 2015. Still, the perspective of individuals with psychiatric disorders as well as perceived barriers and facilitators for participating in these groups have neither been assessed nor included in the design of sports programs.MethodsThis study consists of 15 semi-structured interviews (N = 15) lasting up to one hour each with individuals with psychiatric disorders who participated in voluntary sports groups for this specific population in Switzerland. The results were analyzed employing Thematic Analysis using Nvivo.ResultsInterviewees named offer-specific (disability-related), emotional (social exchange), and rational motives (health) for their participation in voluntary sports groups for individuals with psychiatric disorders. In addition, intrapersonal (psychological/physical deficits), sociocultural (stigmatization), and structural (unsuitable location) factors were mentioned as possible inhibitors in the context of participation in voluntary sports groups for individuals with psychiatric disorders. Furthermore, participants experienced predominantly positive outcomes from participation.ConclusionIndividuals with psychiatric disorders benefit socially, physically, and psychologically from participating in voluntary sports groups for this specific population. However, participation is associated with barriers that need to be minimized in the future. Additionally, a large-scale survey is necessary to validate the results among the population.Keywords: Psychiatric disorders, Physical activity, Sports, Mental health, Qualitative study

Background

In contrast to the general population, individuals with psychiatric disorders (IPD) exhibit poorer mental and physical health [1]. In a study by Vancampfort et al. [2], IPD engaged in significantly less physical activity than the average population. Furthermore, it has been shown that psychiatric disorders can influence various lifestyle factors of a person, leading to poor nutrition, insufficient sleep, and reduced physical activity. These lifestyle factors, in turn, negatively impact health [3]. Additionally, psychiatric disorders not only affect the physical and mental health of those afflicted but also influence the social life of IPD [4–6]. In summary, psychiatric disorders decrease the quality of life for affected individuals and impact all areas of life [7].

At the same time, the positive influence of exercise and sports on the physical, mental, and social health of IPD is well-documented [1, 4–6, 8–17]. This influence of sports on a person’s physical, mental, and social health can be found across all age groups and is independent of an individual’s condition [4, 18].

The knowledge of the positive influence of sports on a person’s holistic health has now become established in the therapy offerings of most psychiatric hospitals, clinics and institutions in Switzerland [19]. Many psychiatric facilities - both for inpatient and outpatient treatment - offer a wide range of exercise and sports programs. Despite the positive experiences in exercise and sports programs during their stay in psychiatric hospital, IPD often do not continue their physical activities after discharge [4]. There are several reasons for the insufficient physical activity level after leaving the hospital (e.g., lack of physical activities offers adapted to the needs of IPD, stigmatization, effects of medication, disorder typical motivational challenges, irregular participation, reduced physical fitness, weight gain, lack adequate social support, limited financial means, etc.) [4, 20–29]. However, pursuing physical activity after leaving a psychiatric hospital is crucial for IPD’s recovery and rehabilitation process, as well as relapse prevention [30].

Besides participating in regular sports clubs in Switzerland, IPD can join a sports club for individuals with disabilities (SCID). The Swiss disabled sports system is managed by three primary overarching organizations: PluSport Schweiz, Procap Sport Schweiz, and Rollstuhlsport Schweiz (Wheelchair sports). Collectively, they provide approximately 750 regular sports programs through nearly 150 sports clubs in Switzerland, catering to about 20,000 members. Furthermore, they organize over 160 sports camps and “active vacations” annually. In the past years, there has been a notable increase in IPD attended to dedicated sports groups [23, 31]. For example, the number of active IPD participants only in PluSport clubs rose in five years from under 250 to almost 600 in 2019 in around 85 clubs in Switzerland. The number of specialized sports groups only for this population (PSYSG) grew from 2 to 20 during the same period. Considering the growing trend and the fact that every second person who obtains disability insurance in Switzerland receives it due to a psychiatric disorder [32], it might be reasonably assumed that the number of sports groups and camps for IPD in Switzerland will experience a significant increase in the coming years.

However, SCID also presents barriers due to the lack of specific sports offers for IPD. For example, structural and sociocultural challenges inhibit access to disability sports, as joining a disability sports club is associated with self-stigmatization, and the person with a psychiatric disorder must accept themselves as “disabled” [4].

Even though there are several PSYSG available outside of psychiatric facilities in Switzerland and new groups are arising, they have been designed according to the traditional guidelines and recommendations of adult or disability sports (e.g., in the framework of J + S and esa programs (J + S = Youth + Sports promotion program; esa = adult sports)). Moreover, various barriers have been reported to assess these groups [20]. At the same time, it is questionable whether the knowledge and expertise from traditional guidelines and recommendations for adult or disability sports (with a focus on, e.g., individuals with cognitive or physical impairments) can be applied to PSYSG as the differences between subgroups (regarding the needs and resources of participants) are obvious.

The existing studies on the IPD, focusing on physical activity, primarily come from the clinical setting or have been conducted in laboratory settings. It is also uncertain how these findings can be transferred to voluntary sports in everyday settings. Furthermore, there is almost no literature on the motivation of IPD to participate and the supporting factors (abilities, opportunities, resources) that might improve participation in a voluntary sports group. However, this information is essential for the functionality of these exercise and sports groups. It is also very desirable to ensure the quality and safety of these sports programs in the framework of the trainer’s education.

Methods

The present qualitative study aims to understand better the perspective of participants in a voluntary sports group for individuals with psychiatric disorders. The study will focus on the participants’ motivation, abilities, desires, challenges, and outcomes to provide insight into their experiences.

Based on the fact that there were only a few studies or other comparable publications analyzing the point of view of participants of PSYSG, a cross-sectional, qualitative design was applied using in-depth semi-structured interviews.

Setting

In Switzerland, PSYSG are provided by PluSport – the umbrella organization for disabled sports [30]. Participants of these PSYSG are usually individuals with a history of a severe psychiatric disorder (e.g., schizophrenia, severe depression, post-traumatic stress disorder, etc.). With rare exceptions, participants are in stable condition and are not undergoing inpatient treatment. Participation in PSYSG requires a membership after a short trial period. For these reasons, almost every participant of PSYSG is registered in the PluSport data bank, allowing them to be reached directly.

Participants & recruitment

Based on previous research in the field and expert literature, our study aimed to conduct interviews with 15 participants from various PSYSG [1, 5, 10, 33–42]. The participants were informed about the study via email and in the group WhatsApp chats of their PSYSG and were asked to contact the interviewer directly if interested in participating. The recruitment period extended from February 21, 2022, to April 19, 2022, during which 19 participants initially expressed their willingness to participate in the interviews. Using the Maximum Variation Sampling approach, the study participants were selected from the pool of interested individuals to obtain a diverse sample of 15 participants.

The study participants had to meet the following inclusion and exclusion criteria: They had to be at least 16 years old, participate in a voluntary physical activity and sports program for individuals with psychiatric disorders, suffer from a psychiatric disorder, and have sufficient German language proficiency.

Interviewer

All interviews were conducted in (Swiss-)German by a psychology master’s student from the University of Zurich (first author). The interviewer had prior interviewing experience and was additionally trained in practicing open-ended interviewing.

Process

After obtaining informed consent, 13 interviews were conducted in Swiss-German and 2 interviews were conducted in Standard German. All interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim. Prior to delving into the study’s topic, the participants’ sociodemographic information was gathered. The interviews were conducted between the end of February and the end of April 2022, and due to the pandemic, some interviews were conducted via Zoom based on participants’ preferences.

A few months after the interviews, each participant received a small gift from PluSport in the form of a branded swimming towel worth CHF 20 (approximately USD 20) as a token of appreciation for their participation. The participants were not informed about the gift beforehand, and there were no other monetary compensations or incentives for their participation.

Analysis

The applied thematic analysis method, described by Guest et al. [34], was used in this study. Initially, the research team reviewed the dataset to identify central themes in the data. Next, an MSc in Psychology student developed codes and grouped them into broader concepts under the supervision of a senior researcher. The research team then reviewed and agreed upon the coding framework. The final coding was applied to the data, with any ambiguous or uncertain cases being discussed with a senior researcher. To eliminate potential bias, a research team member who was not involved in the initial coding phase coded 20% (n = 3) of the dataset using the final coding framework. The interrater reliability was excellent at k = 0.88.

NVivo 12 software (Version 12, QSR International) was used for coding. It is important to note that each statement could contain information relating to several themes, and therefore, it was possible to assign one statement to multiple codes.

Results

Demographics

Interviews with the 15 participants (N = 15) from 10 PSYSG from 7 SCID lasted up to one hour. The age range of participants was 18 to 68 years, with a median age of 33. The study included a total of 5 women and 10 men. All of them participated in a PSYSG for at least one year.

Of the 15 study participants, 14 had a mental disorder, such as schizophrenia (n = 5), depression (n = 3), personality disorder (n = 4), trauma-related disorder (n = 1), and autism spectrum disorder (n = 1). One participant had both an anxiety disorder and depression (n = 1).

Thirteen of the study participants were active in sports groups that focused on a specific sport (n = 13), while two participants attended multiple sports groups and practiced two (n = 1) or three (n = 1) different sports. The distribution of sports practiced by the participants was as follows: Polysport (7), Climbing (4), Football (3), Volleyball (2), Nordic Walking (2).

Program-specific reasons

The data analysis revealed two main offer-specific reasons for participating in a PSYSG: the preference for a specific sport (n = 5) and the team-sport characteristic (n = 5). For example, one participant expressed their passion for their preferred sport by saying, “It was always a childhood dream of mine to do climbing. It’s really something cool and fantastic” (IP 1). Five participants (n = 5) who chose team sports expressed their desire to participate in group activities and play sports with others because they see themselves as team players or want to strengthen their ability through participation in the sports group. One of them said, “I really enjoy doing sports with other people because it’s just not fun alone. Generally, I’m a person who likes to exercise with other people” (IP 8).

Emotional reasons

In regards to emotional reasons, the interviewees particularly highlighted the following: social exchange with others (n = 9), enjoyment of sports and physical activity (n = 7), and the opportunity for self-determination and participation in a sports program (n = 5) were the most commonly mentioned reasons by all participants, as explained by the following quotes: “You’re there voluntarily as a participant so you can try out something for yourself” (IP 4) and “It’s always about the same people. And social exchange is something very important to me. You can exchange ideas and ask how the day went (…). It’s always very interesting to hear how people experienced their day” (IP 8). Four participants (n = 4) also mentioned participating in a PSYSG due to the sense of community and belonging. Three individuals (n = 3) said they chose this sports group because they enjoy being in nature: “It’s about being outside, meeting other people, being in nature, and yes, just walking and being on the move” (IP 3). Furthermore, some participants (n = 3) mentioned that they would participate in the sports program to gain new experiences and to understand themselves better, as evidenced in the following quote: “You experience very exciting things. And I think that’s the case with all disabled sports, that you experience all sorts of things. It’s not always just about the sport itself” (IP 11).

Rational-related reasons

Thirteen of the 15 participants (n = 13) reported participating in a PSYSG due to a rational motive. Eleven out of the 15 participants (n = 11) cited physical activity and health as their reason for joining a PSYSG: “There have been situations where I have been really fit and have had muscle soreness (…) just sitting is much easier, and now it’s like I can barely hold myself up, everything is too weak. So it’s physical, to have the body working properly. That’s really my main motivation. To be physically fit and to feel better because of it” (IP 1).

In addition, some participants stated that they chose the PSYSG because they were already familiar with the sports group, for example, through an inpatient stay (n = 6): “I don’t remember if it was (…) I know I was in the day clinic and we had gymnastics once a week. Also, ball sports and I realized that it was something for me (…) If I can chase a ball, then that’s somehow good” (IP 1).

Three participants (n = 3) also reported that participation in sports would make it easier to deal with their mental disorder and that they could use sports as a coping strategy in relation to their mental disorder, as shown in the following quote: “… and yes, it’s just (…) I just have to. I have a bit more energy than before, yes, abstinence and such, and that takes a lot of energy or I have a lot of energy that needs to be released somewhere” (IP 13).

Reasons mentioned rarely

Two persons (n = 2) reported participating in PSYSG because the program is specifically offered for IPD, and the individuals share the same background. Two additional participants (n = 2) reported that they had always enjoyed sports and that was why they participated in this sports group: “Sports is one of the best inventions of humanity because it promotes health in all areas (…), and some people say ‘Sports is murder’; but I say: Without sports, I’m dead.” So I don’t know how I couldn’t do sports” (IP 6). Further, two participants (n = 2) reported that participating in a PSYSG allows the daily structure and that’s why they attend the sports group: “Oh, I don’t know…Why? Yes, the community, being together, doing something together (…) yes, structure, so (…) yes, to be honest, I haven’t really thought about it” (IP 15).

Other reasons that were only mentioned individually were the following: the appropriate level of sports offered (in comparison to the higher level in the mass sports clubs) (n = 1); participation because it is closely connected to the living institution (n = 1); participation because animals can be taken to the sports group(n = 1); general interest in sports(n = 1); stepping stone or opportunity for other activities and functions in the future (n = 1). Finally, one person (n = 1) described participating in the sports program to improve their physical appearance.

Intrapersonal challenges in ability and personality

The responses of intrapersonal challenges were categorized as hindering personality, physical deficits, and psychological deficits.

Personality traits: Personality traits such as stubbornness and introversion, etc., were grouped under obstructive personality traits and reported as a challenge by the participants (n = 5), as can be seen in the following quote: “Yes, of course, it is a challenge. I am rather introverted and like to have my peace” (IP 1).

Physical deficits: Physical deficits that may lead to irregular participation in the sports program were reported by 11 participants (n = 11). Most often, physical disabilities (negative physical aspects) such as obesity, significant physical complaints, reconvalescence (e.g., healing of scars) and injuries were mentioned as an inhibitor to regular participation (n = 8): “I had a sugar shock with quite high blood sugar 5 or 6 years ago and had to go to hospital afterwards (…) during that time I didn’t go to training for about half a year because I just wasn’t fit” (IP 5).

Furthermore, 5 people (n = 5) reported a reduced level of fitness, which could make it challenging to participate in a PSYSG. One participant said: “Or just that people might (…), if they had a hard day, not want to participate in the training because it would be too much for them” (IP 2).

Other physical deficits were evident in IPD: motor slowdown (n = 1) and the lack of necessary skills to perform the sport (n = 1).

Psychological deficits: All but one participant (n = 14) emphasized the psychological deficits in their mental state as a factor that would prevent them from participating in a PSYSG. In this context, 9 participants (n = 9) explained that their current mental state might hinder them from participating regularly. One participant described the mental state challenge in relation to the severity of the mental disorder as follows: “If it is a group with people who have mental problems (…) it is difficult for them to do it regularly because they are somehow in a hole or because they somehow (…) have sleeping problems and can’t do it anymore. And yes, stories like that” (IP 2).

Furthermore, the lack of motivation was reported as a barrier (n = 5). However, none of the participants described the lack of motivation specifically or in detail. They stayed by general descriptions like the following quote illustrates: “Well, either because of medical reasons or (…) I don’t know, maybe they are also unmotivated” (IP 14).

The analysis also revealed psychological deficits in cognitive processes (n = 4) such as inhibition, lack of concentration, anxiety before or during the sports lesson, as well as forgetfulness or cognitive ambivalence, as the following quote is meant to illustrate: “But I don’t want to, mentally (…) or for example: Today it looks like quite a few participants are coming, and then I already have the ulterior thought again: yes, maybe the training will be canceled, yeeeah (laughs). Actually, I don’t want to, but I want to because I have to, because my mind says I have to. But my mind, my other part, it doesn’t want to. It shirks whenever it can” (IP 1).

Structural barriers

All participants reported structural barriers and challenges.

“Corona-virus”: However, there was only one barrier mentioned by everyone (n = 15) – the “Corona-Virus” – and the related measures significantly influenced the training-associated behavior: “Well, my experience is that you’ve somehow become lazier, at least I have. Somehow, and it’s just that it takes away your motivation because, for example, you’re told that you can only participate with masks on. So playing football in masks when you’re sweaty anyway is rather inappropriate. And that didn’t suit me either, so I thought I’d rather stay at home” (IP4).

Lack of time: In addition, some participants stated that the lack of available time was a significant challenge to participating in the sports program (n = 10). One participant said: “Yes, if I have time and nothing comes in my way (…) so lately I’ve always had something, once they said they were coming to change the windows and I had to be present in the flat (…) There’s always been something going on and I haven’t always been able to go to sports. Or I’ve had a dentist appointment too. If it’s not that kind of thing, I go regularly” (IP 10).

Time of training:Four persons (n = 4) reported that the inconvenient time of training or the poor accessibility of the training location were challenges, as the following quote shows: “Climbing is okay, but the way is too far for me. You know, I live in *** and then I would have to go to Uster. And that’s not exactly on the way (…) But if climbing was nearby, it would certainly not be a problem and I would certainly take part from time to time, but for me, I just find it very tedious to drive to Uster first and yes” (IP 10).

Lack of knowledge: Another barrier mentioned was the lack of or insufficient knowledge about the sports programs (n = 3): “Some [IPD] use them [sport offers] to some extent, others or most of them don’t know anything about them. (…) Yes, good. Maybe that’s also a problem, that the information doesn’t always get through very well, or you could theoretically get the information yourself on the homepage, but yes, maybe (…)” (IP 2).

Structural barriers mentioned rarely: Finally, other structural reasons mentioned rarely were bad weather conditions and the influence of the season (n = 2) [e.g., early darkness in winter] as well as administrative challenges (n = 1).

Sociocultural barriers

Overall, the participants rarely mentioned the sociocultural barriers, such as the lack of social support (n = 1) and stigmatization (n = 2). For example, one participant said: “I don’t like to use the word ‘disability’ so much, that’s why I always say ‘impairments’, because it doesn’t sound as condemning as ‘you are so disabled’. You often hear that from young people [“disabled” – in German “behindert” – is a swearword for, e.g., generally an incompetent person or a person who failed to achieve expectations; even meaning “disabled” the word does actually not imply a real connection to a disability of this person]. But I have to say, we are not disabled, we have an impairment (…) from the outside you might think: “She is completely normal”, but when you get to know the person better, you realise that there is something behind it” (IP 3).

Negative outcomes

Of the 15 participants, 14 reported one or more negative outcomes, as presented in Table 3.

Negative intrapersonal outcomes

Within the negative intrapersonal outcomes related to psychological aspects were mentioned only three times (n = 3) amd were reöated to negative emotions such as disappointment and bad mood (n = 2) and additional stress (from participation) (n = 1). For example, one participant said: “But actually, after the sport I only have stress until I am dressed, somehow it then stresses me out and I just want to go home” (IP 1).

At the same time few intrapersonal outcomes on the physical level have been reported as well. The analysis showed that physical intolerances (n = 2), such as sun intolerance, as well as pain (n = 2) and injuries (n = 1) were negative physical outcomes of participation in a sports group. One participant said: “[…] I just have the problem that I absolutely can’t stand the heat. And if you have to walk a bit under the stinging sun, I don’t think that’s so good and it doesn’t have to be” (IP 11).

Negative intrapersonal outcomes

Within the interpersonal negative outcomes, three subcategories of negative outcomes were identified. Eight participants (n = 8) reported that they had already experienced conflict in or with the group. One participant said, “Well I have to say, yes exactly, once on Monday a new person came to the training and he was very rude in the sport. So, when he came to the training, it went “bang” and you kind of flew away. That was also the reason why I stopped attending the Monday group” (IP 9).

Furthermore, participants stated that they were overburdened with sports in the group (n = 6), as can be seen in the following quotation: “Yes. Well, in the group, but if I’m honest, I tend to do sports alone. So I’m more of a person who does something alone. Maybe that’s also because I have Asperger’s syndrome and that’s why I do a lot of things on my own. Group sports are less my thing. It’s also a bit difficult and I’ve also noticed in the sports group that now and then it’s difficult for me to do what is required of me, let’s just say (…)”. (IP 4).

Other participants (n = 3) reported that they found the irregular attendance of other members disturbing. For example, one participant said, “Except when nobody comes. Me and two Coaches. Yes, that has happened. That is already little and as I said, the more people, so the better, it is more fun and you are more motivated, but with 2–4 people it is a bit more difficult” (IP 13).

One person (n = 1) said he no longer participated in a sports program because of harassment from third parties.

Negative structural outcomes

Four interviewees (n = 4) described negative structural outcomes. One person (n = 1) stated that he did not like the sports facility, and two others (n = 2) noted that the sports program did not meet their expectations, which is illustrated by the following quote: “Not at the moment, because I’m studying now anyway and I don’t think I’ll go back, but not because of the people, because for me it’s more like purely school sport, which I used to do at school, and yes, I think it’s more for those who don’t exercise much, for older adults who sit a lot at work and don’t do any other sport, but I’ve been doing fitness, i.e., weight training, muscle building, for about 18 months, and that’s why yes.”(IP 4).

Positive outcomes

Positive intrapersonal outcomes

Within the positive intrapersonal outcomes, the following three physical aspects (n = 13) were mentioned most frequently by the participants: Feeling fit and healthy (n = 7), sore muscles as a sign of physical activity (n = 7), and tiredness and exhaustion (n = 7). One participant said: “I feel fitter somehow, you know those days when you get up and think: I feel bad, I feel weak somehow and so. And since I’ve been doing sport, I don’t have that anymore, I think” (IP 4). Another participant reported: “Yes, it has changed over the years, a certain exhaustion. But now it’s a good tiredness” (IP 7). The connection between sport and tiredness/exhaustion was described by one interviewee as follows: “You do notice that you are tired after training. Especially if the training was very intensive and good, then I have to say that I am tired now and that was a good training” (IP 8).

Further physical outcomes mentioned rarely were the improvement of expertise in the specific sport (n = 2), the prevention of injury and illness through sporting activity (n = 1), and the influence of sport on physical appearance (n = 1).

Besides the intrapersonal physical aspects, intrapersonal psychological aspects were frequently (n = 13) described as well: 60% of respondents (n = 9) stated that they would experience subjective well-being due to participating in the sports program, as evidenced by inner balance, satisfaction, and symptom reduction. One participant said: “Yes, I already have the feeling that I feel better. I have the feeling that I need sport as a balance (…). So in my free time, when there is no sport at all and I can’t get up, then I already have the feeling that I am missing something (…) It is very important for balance, yes, but I think that most people feel that way, that if they just bum around, they don’t feel so good as a result” (IP 2). Another participant reported: “I just feel better too, you know. It has a positive impact. Especially for someone like me who suffers from chronic depression, it just does something to your head that makes you feel good. You just notice it and you feel better when you’ve done sport” (IP 10).

Furthermore, almost half of the participants (n = 7) reported a brightening of their mood during and after the training, as can be seen in the following quote: “Well, yes, the mood is certainly also an issue, that the mood also lifts (…) yes, when I have a bad day, I notice it even faster, yes, that it is certainly better for a moment. (…) Even during the climbing itself, yes, well, I mean, climbing is quite extreme anyway, and in terms of adrenaline, I already notice the spike (…) but yes, of course that also falls again, it is not constant, but I would say that I notice relatively quickly when something changes (…) if I can get involved” (IP 7).

Another positive psychological outcome mentioned was the fun and enjoyment during and after the sport (n = 5). One participant said: “But as I said, people come once or twice and then think it’s nice and good and fun, then they’ll surely come again” (IP 13).

Interviewees (n = 4) also reported that they could distract themselves from problems and thoughts through sporting activity: “Well, it takes me out of the whole world of thoughts for a moment, yes” (IP 7). Another participant said: “You’re just distracted from your own problems that are buzzing around and sometimes it’s quite good if you can gain a bit of distance from one or the other” (IP 11).

Furthermore, sport was perceived as an essential part of life (n = 2) and an instrument for relaxation and to calm down (n = 2). Further outcomes reported were an improvement in concentration (n = 1) and body awareness (n = 1) as well as an improvement in vegetative symptoms, e.g., sleep and appetite (n = 1) due to sporting activation.

Positive interpersonal outcomes

Extended social contact (n = 7) was most frequently mentioned as an interpersonal social outcome. Participants explained that they would strengthen existing relationships through the sports group, make new friends, and pursue joint activities outside the sports program. One participant said: “Friendships have been formed through the sports group. Friendships have also deepened a lot in the last months and years (…) But for me it has no connection with football (…) Sure it’s training, it has a connection there. But it doesn’t really have a connection with the training system itself, you know what I mean? It developed in passing” (IP 9).

Six people (n = 6) mentioned the social exchange within the group as a positive outcome. One participant said: “Sure, the sport, but also the social exchange, because these are people with a mental illness and they understand you better. Of course, the sport is also very important, but also that you can regularly exchange ideas. I think that’s very important” (IP 4).

Furthermore, five people (n = 5) had learned to show commitment through sport. One participant explained the connection between sporting activity and commitment as follows: “But that also encourages you, or when they run after you, or and then I also try, when we now have a relay race as a warm-up, then I also try to give everything, because when I see how the others run, that’s like a little incentive” (IP 1).

Also, the participants reported a positive group cohesion (n = 4) and that they had learned to accept the other people and to build mutual understanding (n = 4). For example, one participant said: “For example, in climbing, you come, you warm up together, you go into climbing groups, you motivate each other, you take a break together. For me, it’s more the cohesion that plays a role, an important role, and not the sport” (IP 4).

Additionally, three persons (n = 3) stated that they were able to find a social connection through the sports group. One person said: “Yes, fully, yes. It’s important to me (…) that you somehow (…) just with me it’s like this, that in my circle of colleagues they all have children and kids and therefore don’t have so much time to do something regularly during the week. And that’s why it’s kind of cool that you still have a connection” (IP 2).

Other interpersonal positive outcomes mentioned were willingness to compromise (n = 2), social identity (n = 1) and group dynamics (n = 1).

Positive structural outcomes

Three individuals (n = 3) reported structural – such as giving / supporting the dayli structure - outcomes related to the offer and the program design.

Neutral outcomes

It needs be mentioned that one person (n = 1) reported having no perceived outcomes (neither negative nor positive).

Characteristics of the coaches

To analyze the personal attributes of PSYSG coaches from the point of view of IPD, we employed Zeuner and Hummel’s competence model [44], which encompasses four key domains: professional expertise, methodological expertise, self-expertise, and social skills. This model enjoys widespread adoption within the Swiss sports education framework. Furthermore, we tried to identify of unfavorable traits, or “negative competencies,” that would be incongruent with the role of a PSYSG coach from the point of view of IPD. Positive characteristics of a coach

All participants (n = 15) talked at least about one positive characteristic of a coach. According to Zeuner and Hummel’s competence model, the answers were allocated to the specific competencies.

Methodological competence

13 out of 15 participants (n = 13) stated that it is essential that the coach of a PSYSG had methodological competence. A coach should be able to motivate and encourage the participants, but at the same time, he or she should also be able to make it fun. They are responsible for the program design and the structure and sequence of the sports lessons: “I would say that a good coach is someone who can encourage, but at the same time does not push too hard, so (…) yes, just finds a bit of a balance” (IP 7).

Social competence

Furthermore, 11 participants (n = 11) were of the opinion that social competence should be an essential characteristic of a coach. According to the participants, coaches of a PSYSG must have an understanding for IPD and be able to recognize the needs of IPD. In this category, the adequate handling of conflict situations was also described. One participant said: “and of course the coach is also a contact person if someone has a problem with another person or teammate. And that person who has the problem can also approach the coach and say that they have a problem and would like to look at it with the coach. (…) and that he is of course able, if there is a discussion or someone gets angry, to calm that person down and also mediate between the persons” (IP 8).

Self-competence

Furthermore, the importance of self-competence of coaches of a PSYSG was emphasized (n = 7). Here, the positive attitude of the trainer and the importance of fair play were mentioned in particular. One participant said: “So I think quite clearly, constantly, that the coach must always come. Then punctuality, honesty, openness, and the willingness to respond to the other persons. That is certainly what makes a good coach. And then they should also have a lot of understanding, empathy, that is certainly good, and they should also be physically fit, yes” (IP 15).

Professional competence

One third of the interviewees (n = 5) mentioned professional competence as a positive characteristic of a coach. In particular, knowledge of skills, assumption of responsibility, and level of training were mentioned, which is illustrated by the following quotation: “Yes. And for example, especially for us in the group, it is important that the coach is able to master and communicate the safety and the technique well” (IP 7).

Negative characteristics of a coach

13 of the 15 persons (n = 13) additionally gave information about the negative characteristics of a coach from their point of view.

The most frequent negative competence of a coach of a PSYSG was a lack of social competence in the sense of disinterest in the participants (n = 6) of a PSYSG: “A bad coach: I don’t care who comes and I don’t care what they want (…) yes, disinterest, yes simply indifference” (IP 3).

Moreover, the participants named negative self-competences, such as lack of openness and stubbornness (n = 5) as a negative characteristic of a coach. Two persons (n = 2) each named poor emotion regulation and insufficient motivation as negative characteristics of a coach. Other negative characteristics mentioned were laziness (n = 1), too much need to talk (n = 1), and no separation from private life (n = 1).

Furthermore, the participants reported negative methodological competencies, such as faulty handling of the participants (n = 3), destructiveness (n = 1), wrong program (n = 1), no encouragement of the participants (n = 1) as well as the inability to motivate the participants (n = 1).

Participation in a regular sports club as a personal option/alternative

Around half of the participants (n = 7) stated that they would see a regular sports club as a possible option for physical activities in addition to physical activities in a PSYSG. Four of these seven people (n = 4) were already active in a regular sports club during the survey period. Three participants (n = 3) stated that they would like to extend their sports participation in a regular sports club and that this was currently in the planning stage. For example, one participant said: “Yes. That is an issue. Now, I’m already on it, so, I’m already looking for a football club. Sure. I met a colleague in therapy. He’s also a football coach and he said come over when Corona is over. That’s so far now. I’ll get in touch with him and ask” (IP 13).

In comparison, the other eight persons (n = 8) stated they could not imagine playing sports in a regular sports club in the current period.

Experiences in a regular sports club in the past

The past experiences in regular sports clubs might be divided into two subcategories: negative related to challenges and barriers, and positive.

Challenges and barriers in regular sports clubs

66% of all participants (n = 10) reported possible or known barriers to joining a regular sports club. A total of 7 challenges and barriers were mentioned, whereby multiple answers were also possible here.

The financial aspect (n = 3), the reduced physical fitness (n = 3), the high-performance requirements and regulations (n = 1), instability due to mental illness (n = 1), low familiality (n = 1), as well as fear of being perceived differently by other participants (n = 1) were mentioned. Furthermore, one person stated that they prefer to do sport alone and, therefore, do not want to change to a regular sports club (n = 1). These barriers are illustrated by the following quotes: “I used to be, but (…) it just wasn’t financially feasible and that’s why I’m with PluSport now” (IP 6). Another participant said, “(…) It was a physical overload. Sure. You have normal people who are, let’s say, physically less limited than I am, but not in terms of the degree of disability but more in terms of fitness. Of course, you noticed that” (IP 9).

Positive experiences with regular sports clubs

Furthermore, the participants (n = 8) reported positive experiences in a regular sports club, as will be highlighted in the following quotations as examples: “Well. My impairment was actually never an issue or a reason whether I join there or not. Maybe if I want to take part in competitions at some point, then in any case maybe a para-sport, but otherwise it’s not an issue for me, I just do it because I enjoy it and I’m not yet in a competitive mood, I’ll say that now, that’s why yes” (IP 4). Another participant said: “But (…) you were received very friendly and well and there were not any comments like “he’s just fat”, but more “cool that you’re here”. And I get so many compliments like “you like more, you do more” and “you can tell you are doing something” (IP 9).

Discussion

The present study is a unique exploration of the world of voluntary sports programs for individuals with psychiatric disorders (IPD) in Switzerland. What sets this research apart is that, for the first time, we gained access to the invaluable perspective of IPD who participate in sports groups specifically designed for their needs (PSYSG). The study delved deep into their motivations, needs, resources, and the formidable barriers and challenges they face in engaging in these sports programs.

The following subchapters discuss the results with a focus on the research questions as well as the results.

Reasons for the participation

Three motives for participating in a PSYSG could be identified depending on the reported motives: program-specific, emotional, and rational. For program-specific reasons, the participants named both the sporting activity in a team and the sport itself (also due to the importance of sport and exercise for health) as the main reason for participating in a PSYSG. The feeling of community and the shared experience in team sports as well as in life (related to the psychiatric disorder) can have a particularly motivating and unifying effect. These findings align closely with previous publications in the field [4, 5, 8, 45]. Additionally, the most frequently mentioned emotional reasons for participation, such as social exchange and a sense of belonging and community, indicate forming a social identity within the group, which helps participants both feel less isolated (what is often experienced by IPD) and focus less on their impairments [6, 46–48]. The last assumption might be confirmed by the fact that disability or participating in disability sports was mentioned only once. In sum, these outcomes might be seen as reinforcers for renewed participation, according to the theory of basic needs [49].

Furthermore, the adapted sporting level between the sports groups was mentioned as a motivation for participation, highlighting the need for tailored group structures to meet diverse physical and psychological needs and is in line with Häusermann & Kiselev’s [4] publication.

Furthermore, as emphasized by Chekroud et al. [9], Hölter [8], and others, fun is also essential for motivation, according to which sporting activities release neurotransmitters that subsequently bring about a sense of well-being and an elevated mood [24, 50]. The results of the present study could confirm this finding, although the underlying motives of fun were not specified in more detail in our research. Therefore, it is unclear whether the fun is rooted in social exchange or practicing the sport.

Moreover, the participants appreciated that the sporting group activities took place outdoors in nature and that certain groups were very flexible about taking pets along if this was possible due to the type of sport. Overall the topics of nature and pets might be associated with.

Symptom reduction was also mentioned as a motive. The participants see sports activation as a kind of coping strategy, which can be confirmed by the literature below [10, 12, 13, 16, 51–53].

Barriers and challenges to participation

Intrapersonal challenges and barriers

In terms of intrapersonal challenges and barriers, IPDs’ mental and physical deficits (incl. reduced physical performance such as lack of energy and drive as a result of the illness) were cited as the most common barriers to participation in a PSYSG. This is broadly consistent with the findings of this target group in studies conducted in inpatient and outpatient settings [25, 28, 54–56]. Also reported as a physical barrier by the IPD was a motor slowdown, although the influence of medication use was not mentioned explicitly by participants [24, 25]. The results listed can be interpreted in terms of the theory of basic needs [49], according to which the need to experience competence cannot be satisfied due to psychological and physical deficits, which leads to a reduction in self-efficacy expectations [57, 58], which subsequently causes the target behavior to be performed with less probability. Likewise, previous negative experiences with sports groups can reduce self-efficacy expectations and prevent participation [1]. The lack of motivation as a barrier is in line with the results of previous studies in the inpatient setting [25, 28, 56], according to which IPD show reduced motivation compared to the average population.

Furthermore, personality traits such as introversion, stubbornness, and being a loner were cited as barriers to participation in a PSYSG. These findings can again be attributed to the increased social isolation of IPD [4, 6]. As IPD often have only a small network of relationships, joining a sports group is associated with uncertainty and may be experienced as challenging, manifesting in irregular sports group participation.

Interpersonal barriers

The group constellation was reported to be sometimes challenging for the participants. Even with similarities, some participants feel less socially integrated due to their differences, which negatively affects their motivation. Therefore, focusing on and highlighting similarities and reducing the communication of differences might be crucial for creating a social identity and should be considered when designing offers for PSYSG. However, given the target population being individuals with psychiatric disorders, it is also essential to acknowledge that differences in abilities, communication styles, and levels of engagement may be unavoidable. These differences can arise from the diverse manifestations of psychiatric conditions, medication side effects, or varying levels of physical and mental health. Coaches should be equipped with effective strategies to manage and mediate these differences, promoting inclusivity without diminishing individual needs.

Furthermore, (self-) stigmatization was mentioned as a sociocultural barrier. Häusermann & Kiselev [4] reported that participation in a sports group for IPD requires participants to accept themselves as “impaired”, which can be perceived as stressful by the participants. From this point of view, the organization offering PSYSG in Switzerland should consider using their names without mentioning the part related to a disability (for example, not like “name – organization for people with disabilities” but just “name”).

Structural barriers

In addition to administrative barriers and lack of knowledge about sporting opportunities, situational barriers such as the time of year and the weather were also mentioned by participants as structural barriers. However, participants in the walking and football groups mainly reported these situational barriers. According to Häusermann & Kiselev [4], the coaches of PSYSG are particularly challenged to motivate participants to participate despite these situational challenges. Furthermore, participants reported that the time and place of the sports group were inappropriate, which is also in line with the results of current research [59]. The findings suggest that IPD access to sports should be further facilitated, for example, by expanding and developing the range of sports groups available to IPD in the same region [23].

Finally, highly likely related to the previous aspect, the lack of time was indicated as a possible structural barrier. This result is exciting in view of the fact that almost all IPD who participated in the study were only employed to a limited extent (mainly for a few hours a day) and thus had enough time besides work. According to the participants’ statements, it is not only the lack of time that is the reason for canceling the training but rather the fact that alternative actions would be preferred. However, this pattern is not only likely to occur with IPD, but also with mentally healthy people. The attractiveness of alternative actions and their prioritization is quite understandable in certain situations, especially if the alternative actions are given a higher value situationally [59]. But, some of the reported alternatives do not seem to be either more attractive (dentist, visit of craftsman, etc.) or unable to be postponed. From this point of view, it remains unexplained why IPD prefer the less attractive opportunities to sports.

In summary, the results suggest that IPD’s structural and psychological deficits are a major barrier to participation, but this is not surprising given the specificity of the target group. How these challenges can best be reduced is part of further research.

Outcomes as a result of participation

The reported outcomes of IPD through participation in a PSYSG could be divided into the categories of intrapersonal, interpersonal, and structural, following Darcy et al. [43]. Overall, many more positive than negative outcomes were mentioned. It follows that IPD can benefit from the PSYSG groups and that this sporting activity in the group has a positive effect on their social, physical, and mental health [8].

Intrapersonal-physical effects

The outcome of feeling fit and healthy as a result of sporting activity is in line with previous research findings that sporting activity positively impacts a person’s physical health [4, 8, 14].

The reported exhaustion, fatigue and muscle soreness after sport were considered positive by the participants, as these effects would lead them to experience themselves as competent and would confirm to them that they had achieved something themselves through their behavior (basic needs theory according to Ryan & Deci [49]). The reported effects of sporting activity on physical appearance, as well as the improvement of professional competencies, are also in line with the results of the literature [8], according to which IPD can better perceive and reassess their abilities and competencies through sport [6]. Through the perceived physical effects, IPD experience themselves as competent, increasing their self-efficacy expectation and the likelihood of participating again [49].

Injuries, pain, and intolerance caused by sports were mentioned as negative intrapersonal-physical factors. Experiencing a physical injury limits the ability to do sport – what is obvious - but it also might reduce the self-efficacy expectation “I am physically able to do sport” in the long term or create a new routine self-perception of being unable to do sports lasting even after the injury. This reduces motivation to continue or relaunch participating in a sports program.

Intrapersonal-psychological effects

The participants reported that sport had reduced their psychological symptoms, resulting in a subjective feeling of well-being. As a result, the participants were able to concentrate better, felt calmer and more relaxed, and perceived their bodies better. An improvement in vegetative symptoms was also achieved. These results are consistent with the findings of studies in clinical settings [28, 60], according to which physical activity can lead to a reduction in the psychological symptoms of IPD and subsequently to an increase in psychological well-being.

Participants further explained that they could distract themselves from problems and thoughts through sport. IPD often suffer from thought circles or ruminating as a result of their disorder and are thus limited in their cognitive abilities. Through sporting activity, they are able to better structure, control and regulate their thoughts, which can lead to a decrease in brooding tendencies [4, 61].

Furthermore, IPD often have insufficient daily structure due to their limited employment. Through regular participation in a sports group, the sports group is perceived as a supportive and sustaining structure, but this was only described by a few participants. It, therefore, seems as if the maintenance of structure through the sports group is not perceived as a primary goal of the participants, although precisely this function of the sports group is, among other things, decisive for the health process of IPD [11].

As for negative effects on the psychological level, the participants explained that participation would cause them additional stress and that participation could also afflict with negative emotions such as disappointment and frustration. These findings are described in the literature as challenging factors, that lead to people no longer participating in sports programs [28, 62, 63].

Structural effects

The positive structural outcomes mentioned by the participants primarily concern the offer and the program of the PSYSG. On the one hand, the sports facility was mentioned as structurally negative, as it was already somewhat older. This negative structural outcome also represents a situationally stable challenge that is difficult to influence [4]. Furthermore, some participants stated that they did not like the sports program or that the offer did not meet their requirements. One participant also described that she associated the program with previous negative experiences and would, therefore, not continue to attend this sports group. The sports program - coded here as a negative outcome - corresponds in the literature to coaches’ lack of methodological competence [44]. In this case, the negative outcome concerns a coach’s ability to design a program that is suitable for as many participants as possible, which can be achieved, for example, through co-determination of the participants in the program. Through co-determination, the persons’ experience of autonomy and social integration in the group are promoted, which increases the motivation to participate again. At the same time, it needs to be stated that it is also impossible to meet everyone’s wishes.

Interpersonal effects

As a positive interpersonal outcome, the participants stated that they would find social connections through participation in a PSYSG, that they could exchange ideas within the group and that they had made new friends or deepened old relationships. This result can be confirmed in the literature [6, 8] and already discussed above as one of the possible reasons for the participation.

The participants also explained negative social effects that had arisen in the context of participation in a PSYSG. The participants reported that they experienced the irregular participation of the other members and the group constellation as challenging, which also led to conflicts within the group. These results are in line with previous research showing that the group constellation can be a negative factor, as participants within a group can have different disturbance patterns and differences in physical, psychological and communicative abilities [4, 43]. Due to irregular attendance and the group constellation, the formation of a social identity of the group can be made more difficult. As a result, members feel less socially included, which in turn reduces the motivation to participate again.

In addition, one person reported that she had been harassed by a third person and, therefore, no longer participated in the training. According to the literature, people with impairments are often victims of violence and harassment [64].

Perspectives on coaches

The analysis of the reported characteristics of coaches is in line with the classification of the action competence model according to Zeuner & Hummel [44].

Professional competence

Regarding professional competence, participants mentioned in particular knowledge about a sport, skills and abilities, and the assumption of responsibility.

Methodological competence

Methodological competence was mentioned the most by the participants. In particular, the motivation and encouragement of participants were mentioned [65]. Furthermore, the participants emphasized having fun, which is in line with the methodological competence of creating a non-competitive atmosphere [4]. At the same time, the participants described that they would like to be adequately supported. This is in line with the recommendations of Cronin et al. [65], according to which ambition should be conveyed to the participants, which, however, is in contrast to the recommendation of creating a non-competitive atmosphere by Häusermann & Kiselev [4]. The reported responsibility of the coach for the structure and running of a sports group is consistent with previous research findings [66]. Other findings mentioned were the avoidance of destructive and commanding behavior, as well as poor program design and insufficient encouragement and motivation of participants, which is largely in line with Häusermann & Kiselev’s [4] recommendations.

Self-competence

The importance of the self-competence of PSYSG coaches was emphasized. Here, the positive attitude of the trainer and the importance of fair play and honesty were mentioned in particular. One person also reported that the coach must be constant and punctual. The participants noted the following negative self-competences of coaches: lack of openness and stubbornness, laziness, no separation from private life, poor emotional regulation, and insufficient motivation. Therefore, coaches should be able to perceive and assess themselves, regulate their emotions, and organize themselves [44, 66].

Social competence

According to the participants, PSYSG coaches must understand the participants and be able to recognise the needs of IPD and deal adequately with conflict situations. Furthermore, the disinterest of the coach in the participants should be avoided. A good coach should have the skills, abilities and attitude to behave adequately in social situations and to display social behavior when dealing with people [66] This builds a relationship of trust, which in turn has a reinforcing effect on regular participation in the sports program [4, 67, 68].

In summary, coaches act as role models and reinforcers for participants in a PSYSG [59]. According to the data analysis, they should have the following characteristics: be able acting adequately to expected behavior in social situations, they should also be open-minded and interested in the participants and show understanding for IPD. Furthermore, from the point of view of the study participants, a good coach is also characterized by high self-motivation and fairness. Interviews seem to be out of the opinion that participants of PSYSG often look to the coach as a role model. Through constant participation, a relationship of trust can be built. This means the coaches can better support the participants. All these aspects have also been reported previously in the literature on sports and disabled sports [4]. This literature also suggests that the sports program should have a balance between participant support and a non-competitive atmosphere. On the one hand, this can promote the participants’ experience of competence, and on the other hand, it strengthens the sense of belonging and community. Both, in turn, might have a positive effect on regular participation in the sports program.

Are regular sports clubs an alternative?

Around half of the participants considered joining a regular sports club as a possible option. Almost a quarter of respondents were active in a regular sports club at the time of the survey, with another three currently planning to join a regular sports club. These findings are consistent with the literature that IPD can increase interest in sport and exercise and improve fitness in a protected setting (PluSport groups). Afterwards, a transfer to a regular sports club can be aspired to. The sports group continues to be seen as a “safe base” to which one can return at any time and thus contributes greatly to the rehabilitation and inclusion of IPD [4, 69].

Two people also stated that they practice an individual sport (strength training) in addition to voluntary exercise and sports groups. This corresponds to the statements of Häusermann & Kiselev [4], according to which individual sports and exercise can be perfect for those affected, but this also carries the risk that if the focus is solely on individual sports, the sporting activities can decrease in the longer term or be abandoned altogether, since no social group identity is formed by practicing an individual sport. This means that the motivation for a person to become active in sport again depends on intrapersonal factors, such as the expectation of consequences and the development of the goal intention. However, if the goal intention [58] is not sufficiently high or if other barriers and challenges prevent the execution of the goal, the goal behavior is less likely to be executed.

Furthermore, some sports can be performed as individual and group sports (e.g., bouldering, climbing). This allows IPD to benefit from both the advantages in the group (social inclusion) and the advantages in individual sports (experience of autonomy), which in turn increases the motivation to perform the target behavior again [4].

Experiences in a regular sports club in the past

The present study participants reported negative and positive experiences in a regular sports club. Among the challenges, the financial aspect and the lack of or insufficient physical fitness were mentioned most often. One person was also afraid of being perceived as different by the other participants and would not join a regular sports club. This result corresponds to the challenges described by Kiselev and Loosli [70], according to which participation in a regular sports club would offer a good opportunity for IPD to be included in society, but this is not possible due to challenges within the persons concerned themselves, but also due to external challenges through the regular sports club [4]. In addition to the literature [20], the following challenges were also mentioned in the context of a regular sports club: One person reported that they perceived the regular sports club as less familiar than PluSport and, therefore, did not feel adequately taken care of. Another challenge is the stricter rules and regulations in a regular sports club, particularly related to regular participation, which might be challenging for someone with a specific psychiatric disorder. This also can again be justified by the fact that in a regular sports club, more emphasis is placed on the performance factor than on the fun factor. However, as can be seen from the explanations on the characteristics of a coach (research question 4), IPD want a balanced program in which both performance and fun are to be promoted.

Limitations

Due to the pandemic-related situation as well as the mental health background of the target group, appointments were repeatedly postponed because appointments were missed or not attended. With regard to the psychiatric background of the study participants, a certain difficulty in this area was to be expected, but nevertheless, the survey period was extended by one month. Furthermore, the sample consisted of only 15 persons. Again, further studies with larger samples are indicated. A difference between the data collection by Zoom and on-site data collection could be observed. We saw that the participants were much more open and willing to talk during the on-site data collection than in the interviews conducted by Zoom. It is therefore recommended that future studies conduct all interviews on-site.

The interviewed participants were all IPD who regularly participated in a sports program. Therefore, only limited statements can be made about what challenges and barriers prevail among persons in connection with first-time participation in a PSYSG or those who quit the PSYSG due to challenges and barriers. However, this knowledge would be necessary in connection with the transition from hospitalsport to a PSYSG.

An additional limitation of our study might be the fact that we did not explicitly assess whether participants were utilizing psychotherapy or counseling interventions during their participation in PSYSG. This factor might be protective to prevent or alleviate their psychiatric symptoms and support them in holding on to sports programs. However, given the nature of the participants and the severity of their disorders, it is reasonable to assume that all of them were under the supervision of a psychotherapist or psychiatrist during the time of the study. While this assumption aligns with typical treatment protocols for individuals with severe psychiatric disorders, we did not formally evaluate this aspect and, therefore, cannot confirm it based on our data. This dimension would be particularly interesting for further understanding and reflecting on our results, as it could provide deeper insights into the participants’ overall mental health management and its impact on their engagement in sports activities. Future research should consider incorporating this factor to analyze protective interventions alongside voluntary sports programs comprehensively.

Furthermore, no relationship might be established between the results and the type of mental impairment or the gender of the participants based on the fact the personal and interview data was accessed, recorded and stored separately to ensure participants anonymity. Therefore, the individual results may only occur depending on certain disorders or gender roles.

Conclusions

In summary, it can be said that the present results are beneficial for the understanding of IPD as well as for the knowledge of the adequate design of a PSYSG. Nevertheless, there are still many open questions that the present study could not answer. Research in this area is not yet complete and should be pursued further so that, on the one hand, the present results can be validated and, on the other hand, future interventions and measures for PSYSG can be derived.

Data availability

The interview guidelines in German are available upon reasonable request from the corresponding author. According to the informed consent, we promised to the participants that the interview transcripts will be storage according the data protection requirements by PluSport and will not be published/uploaded anywhere to the open access. This was important due to the fact that we interviewed a very sensitive (and depending on disorder very distrustful) group – individuals with psychiatric disorders. To ensure the interviewees speak freely, it was necessary to accept this obligation. And for these reasons, we are strongly reluctant to publish the transcripts in any open access data banks. However, we are happy to share the transcripts (in German with editor and/or reviewers) under condition that they will not publish it as open access. In addition to contacting the corresponding author, the following organization can be requested for data access: upon reasonable request at PluSport, Umbrella Organization of Swiss Disabled Sports, Chrisbaumstrasse 6, 8604 Volketswil, Switzerland [mailbox@plusport.ch].
