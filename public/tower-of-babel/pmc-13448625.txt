Childhood cancer predisposition study: a prospective registry and biorepository protocol
Authors: Melissa R Perrino, Suzanne P MacFarland, Luke Maese, Jennie Vagher, Junne Kamihara, Surya Rednam, Philip J Lupo, Garrett M Brodeur, Joshua Schiffman, Lisa Diller, Lauren Desrosiers-Battu, Sharon E Plon, Kim E Nichols, Samuel L Volchenboum, David Malkin, Anita Villani, Christopher C Porter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCancer is the most common cause of disease-related mortality in children, highlighting the urgent need for improved care in this population. It is estimated that >15% of children diagnosed with cancer harbour a germline pathogenic variant in a cancer predisposition gene which confers an increased risk to develop cancer. There are over 100 genes associated with cancer predisposition syndromes (CPSs) and greater availability and acceptability of genetic testing in the last decade has facilitated their recognition in childhood. However, individually, each of these CPSs is rare, impeding robust research and advancement of clinical care. Once a specific CPS is diagnosed, current recommendations for clinical care are based primarily on expert consensus with a ’one size fits all’ approach to management. Detailed knowledge of genotype-phenotype associations and the impact of genetic modifiers is lacking, and due to strong ascertainment bias of children already diagnosed with cancer predominantly being tested for a CPS, the true cancer risk is likely overestimated.Methods and analysesThe Childhood Cancer Predisposition Study (CCPS) is a multi-centre registry and biorepository for children and adolescents aged 0–21 years with a clinical or molecularly confirmed CPS and their family members (NCT04511806). The study objectives are to characterise the natural history of each CPS, correlate the natural history phenotype with CPS genotype, evaluate the efficacy of standard surveillance strategies and allow future investigation into the feasibility and effectiveness of novel surveillance strategies. The principal study question is whether adherence to recommended tumour surveillance guidelines is associated with earlier detection of tumours and/or improved survival. We will also address what barriers exist that prevent adherence to surveillance guidelines. Data collected from primary subjects includes detail about the CPS, genomic data, cancer history and family cancer history, as well as information about tumour surveillance. Required biospecimen collection includes germline DNA samples, with optional collection of serial blood and stool samples. Planned enrolment is 1050 primary subjects.Ethics and disseminationCCPS was reviewed and approved by a central IRB (WCG IRB 2020P002450) and the Research Ethics Board at The Hospital for Sick Children in Toronto (1000072286). Written informed consent is obtained from all participants. Data and specimens are available to qualified investigators by request to address specific research questions through a standardised research application process. In addition, de-identified data are available through the Pediatric Cancer Data Commons for exploration. Analysed data will be disseminated in peer-reviewed publications and at conferences including meetings inclusive of patient and family advocacy groups.Trial registration numberNCT04511806.

Introduction

Over 100 cancer predisposition genes have been identified and associated with a wide array of childhood and adult-onset cancers. Although early large-scale genomic sequencing studies detected germline pathogenic variants in ~10% of paediatric cancer patients,1–4 there is general agreement that the true burden of genetic predisposition to cancer is likely much higher.5–8 Indeed, variants in regulatory gene regions, epigenetic changes and polygenic interactions are generally not assessed by traditional sequencing and bioinformatics approaches, and still more cancer predisposition genes likely remain to be identified. Thus, as more information is gained, it is likely that an even greater proportion of paediatric cancer patients will benefit from advances in the understanding of hereditary cancer.

There is a tremendous opportunity to optimise outcomes for children with underlying cancer predisposition syndromes (CPSs) through personalised approaches to clinical care.9 Improved cancer-related survival in this young population can be achieved primarily through early tumour detection using effective surveillance, and this should be associated with a significant increase in long-term survival (and life-years gained). Decreasing cancer-related and therapy-related morbidity is an equally important aim. Sparing paediatric patients from aggressive therapies needed to control advanced-stage disease will decrease the individual, familial, and societal burden of late effects by minimising, or eliminating, the need for genotoxic chemo- and/or radiotherapy. Reduction in initial exposure to these agents can decrease subsequent tumour risk and enable optimised treatment of later tumours by avoiding maximal dose thresholds of chemo- and radiotherapy.10–17 Given the lifelong cancer risk, it is critical to establish the most effective, risk-tailored and minimally invasive surveillance approaches, in order to decrease burden and enhance compliance.

Several challenges have impeded successful research and systematic approaches to the care of children affected with a CPS. First, until the recent emergence of the era of genomic medicine, genetic testing of paediatric patients was not widely adopted, due to several reasons, including cost and unclear benefit for the child.18–23 Thus, progress was impeded by the cyclical inability to prove benefit without systematic identification and research. Second, individual CPS remain relatively rare, and the knowledge base established to date has been informed by epidemiological studies that, due to ascertainment bias, likely represent those with the most penetrant diseases. This has limited our understanding of the true cancer risks, ages of highest tumour risk, genotype-phenotype correlations and impact of genetic modifiers, which together lead to ‘one size fits all’ management plans and little stratification of care for many of these patients. Third, until recently, there has been little consistency in the management and surveillance practices for children with a CPS across centres and health systems, precluding robust evaluation of various approaches. In 2017, the first series of consensus recommendations were proposed to guide surveillance practices of paediatric patients with CPS, with these manuscripts recently updated.24–44 These recommendations, while largely based on expert opinion, emphasised a need for more robust evidence supporting the efficacy of surveillance and data to inform the most sensitive and specific surveillance approaches.9 Individual studies with an individual CPS focus have demonstrated benefit of active surveillance for children,45–47 but larger, more comprehensive studies encompassing the multitude of CPS remain limited. Fourth, despite the recognised need for research in children with a CPS and the potential to inform cancer prevention and treatment for the general population,48 the study of individuals at high risk for cancer, but who have not yet developed cancer, has yet to be prioritised by major paediatric cooperative groups in the USA or elsewhere. As a result, the ability to perform enhanced surveillance or cancer prevention trials in this population has been limited, whereas promising risk reduction strategies for adult-onset tumours such as hereditary breast cancer have been successful.49–52 Finally, centralised collection of tissue, including paediatric tumours from patients with a CPS is lacking, restricting our understanding of potential differences in pathogenesis of germline-driven cancers compared with their sporadic counterparts, and thus limiting novel approaches to cancer care in the paediatric CPS population.

To address these challenges and opportunities, and serve as a platform for future studies in children with CPS, the Childhood Cancer Predisposition Study (CCPS) was developed by the Consortium for Childhood Cancer Predisposition, which includes seven large academic centres with established paediatric cancer predisposition programmes (box 1).8

Methods and analysis

Objectives

CCPS is designed as a multi-centre, longitudinal, observational study that collects clinical and biological data and specimens from children with a CPS and their relatives (figure 1). The principal study question is whether adherence to recommended tumour surveillance guidelines is associated with earlier detection of tumours and/or improved survival. We will also address what barriers exist that prevent adherence to surveillance guidelines. The study objectives are to (1) characterise the natural history of disease in children with a CPS through assessment of overall and cancer-free survival, genotype-phenotype correlations, and cancer biology and therapy outcomes; (2) evaluate standardised surveillance strategies’ impact and effectiveness through imaging and biochemical diagnostic accuracy; type and timing of cancer identified; impact on morbidity, mortality and quality of life; and common barriers to completing recommended surveillance; and (3) collect samples to evaluate the feasibility, impact and effectiveness of detection and quantification of circulating tumour DNA in the peripheral blood of subjects with specific CPSs as a novel surveillance strategy in future studies.

Study schema primary subjects are enrolled, after which affected parents or adult siblings and unaffected parents can enrol. Primary subjects or others in the primary family unit can invite participation of other family members who can enrol remotely. Longitudinal follow-up occurs for all subjects at least annually. Eligibility

Children and adolescents (age 0–21 years) with a CPS, with or without a history of cancer, are being recruited and enrolled as primary subjects. As this study is investigating CPS that may be inherited within families, family units are offered enrolment, including CPS-affected parents, CPS-affected adult siblings and unaffected parents and siblings. CPS-affected siblings under age 21 are offered enrolment as a primary subject and are linked to biologically related primary subjects through de-identified mechanisms. Other adult family members (with documented or obligate CPS) are also eligible to enrol as affected family members.

Primary subjects must meet the following criteria to be eligible for enrolment: (1) be less than 21 years of age at the time of enrolment and (2) have a diagnosis of a CPS through clinical laboratory testing demonstrating a pathogenic or likely pathogenic germline variant, well-established clinical diagnostic criteria and/or a high clinical suspicion of a specific CPS with clinical laboratory testing demonstrating a variant of uncertain significance, as determined by the treating physician. Participation in other registries does not preclude participation in CCPS. Individuals with a strong personal or family history of cancer without a genetic or clinical diagnosis of a specific CPS are not eligible for enrolment. Eligible CPS diagnoses are detailed in the online supplemental table 1).

Enrollment

Enrollment began in spring 2021 and will continue through spring 2028. Potential participants are recruited from participating institutions’ clinics providing care for children with a CPS, which may include paediatric oncology, haematology or genetics clinics, at the time of their routinely scheduled clinical visits. Participating centres are spread across the USA, concentrated in the east, with one centre in Ontario, Canada. Each centre has a dedicated programme for patients with cancer predisposition. Patient populations are served from the local geographic catchment of each institution but also from remote distances if local sub-specialty care options are not available and are covered by a mix of public and private insurance for care. Primary subjects must be a patient at a participating site clinic to be recruited. Family members of a primary subject need not be cared for in one of these clinics as they are provided information about the study from the primary subject or the parents in the form of an enrolment link, which connects them with study personnel to request further information and/or enrol directly.

Subjects may be enrolled at any time after being diagnosed with a CPS and may participate for the duration of the study, regardless of remaining a patient at the enrolling site. As the study is designed as a longitudinal, observational study of individuals with relatively rare syndromes, the duration of the study is undefined and will continue as long as funding remains available, currently secured until 2028.

Procedures involved

Recently published consensus surveillance recommendations are used as the basis to create minimum surveillance protocols for consortium centres to prescribe to enrolled participants, and otherwise routine clinical care is not limited by this study and is provided per the managing clinical care team.24–42 An optional questionnaire about tumour surveillance is offered for self-reporting.

Data are collected at local institutions and de-identified data are exported quarterly to a central REDCap database. Data collection instruments for primary subjects (online supplemental figure) were designed to collect baseline information at enrolment (table 1), including details about the CPS such as affected gene, type of variant, inheritance and recommended surveillance regimen, cancer history details (if applicable, and with pathology report source documentation), and family cancer history. Updates and tumour surveillance results are collected at routine clinical visits (table 2). Data abstraction is carried out by clinical research associates via thorough medical record review. Genetic testing reports are centrally reviewed by a senior genetic counsellor, and detailed variant data are annotated on specific case report forms, including the ClinGen allele registry ID and ClinVar and ClinGen variant curation expert panel classification of pathogenicity (if available). Electronic surveys about the family’s experience with the diagnosis of a CPS and tumour surveillance including the psychosocial burden are sent annually, and surveys about the reasons or barriers to timely tumour surveillance are sent when recommended studies are delayed or missed (table 3). The study data dictionary was developed in collaboration with the National Cancer Institute (NCI) Thesaurus (NCI Thesaurus Code C177281), which maintains a thesaurus that is a widely recognised standard for biomedical coding and reference. On discharge from the participating institution due to age or other circumstances, primary subjects will have the option to continue participation via annual electronic self-reporting surveys distributed automatically by the local REDCap database.

Survey questions

Data collection from adult family members or family unit participants not cared for at local institutions are collected by direct input (self-report) from the participant, including their personal medical and family history, with a focus on cancer (online supplemental table 2). Participants will also directly upload relevant genetic testing and pathology reports. These data will be collected via annual electronic surveys distributed by the local REDCap database, and linked to primary subjects’ pedigrees with a unique pedigree identifier.

Currently, the CCPS does not include any study intervention or investigational agent, and clinical care is performed at the discretion and recommendations of the primary care team. It is anticipated that additional specific research questions will be formulated and addressed in the future using the population, data and/or biorepository tissue of the CCPS cohort. Sub-studies with or without an intervention or investigational agent will require independent protocols that will undergo regulatory review and oversight.

Required biospecimen collection includes germline DNA samples of the ‘Primary Family Unit’, with optional collection from primary subjects of serial samples of blood, stool, leftover tumour tissue and samples that may be used to generate induced pluripotent stem cells. All peripheral blood samples are collected during clinically scheduled blood draws. The CCPS establishes a biorepository, designed as a hub-and-spoke structure with a central repository for prospective collection of some tissues, linked to local biorepositories at participating institutions. Information about inventory of tumour specimens already banked locally will be available in the database, along with reference to existing genomic studies of the tumour, such that investigators may identify and request such tissue or data for specific studies, subject to approval of the CCPS Scientific Committee. Samples are collected and shipped to the central database for processing and storage. Germline DNA is collected from blood mononuclear cells, saliva or buccal swab or skin-derived fibroblasts from primary subjects. For primary subjects who consent to optional blood collection, samples are collected at least annually, ideally at the time of routine clinical blood draws. Blood is processed to store plasma and viable cells for future substudies which could include cell-free DNA analyses as a tumour surveillance strategy and/or induction of pluripotent stem cells. Stool samples will be processed for future analyses of the microbiome with correlation with tumour development, for example.

Data analysis plan

Descriptive analyses of the cohort, assessment of the effectiveness of tumour surveillance in the cohort and an evaluation of the perceived barriers to tumour surveillance experienced by families of children with childhood cancer predispositions will be conducted after the study has been open for 5 years and periodically thereafter. Additional comparative statistical analyses will be detailed in sub-study protocols.

To address the efficacy of recommended tumour surveillance strategies, we will evaluate three main outcomes: (1) the detection rate and diagnostic accuracy of screening investigations; (2) incidental findings and burden of additional investigations; and (3) adverse events related to surveillance procedures. Pre-symptomatic tumour detection will be reported as the proportion of asymptomatic individuals with a screen-detected tumour in each CPS cohort during the follow-up period. Both malignant and benign tumours (eg, haemangioblastoma in Von Hippel Lindau syndrome, paraganglioma in hereditary paraganglioma/pheochromocytoma syndrome) will be captured. The evaluation of diagnostic accuracy will be based on an assessment of sensitivity and specificity of surveillance tests, through classification of surveillance tests as true positives, true negatives, false positives and false negatives. Importantly, false negative tests will be distinguished from true negative tests with subsequent ‘interval cancers’. Distinct adaptations to screening strategies would be implemented in response to these outcomes: tests with low sensitivity (high false negative rate) would suggest the need for a different modality, whereas high interval cancer rates would suggest the need for a shorter screening interval. The definition of an ‘interval cancer’ will be adapted from published guidelines,53 and a false negative will be defined when signs suspicious for malignancy are retrospectively seen on a surveillance test in a subject who is later diagnosed with cancer. Although centralised radiologic review of surveillance scans is beyond the scope of this proposal, we will collect source documents (radiology and pathology reports) and radiographic imaging. Consortium site co-investigators will follow standard guides for the classification of surveillance tests, and local radiologists will be engaged for review of potential false negative scans. An evaluation of incidental findings and adverse events related to surveillance procedures will be critical to comprehensively assess the impact of surveillance protocols on the paediatric population. Incidental findings will be categorised as those findings not related to the underlying condition. We will also capture the incidence of additional investigations, including extra imaging tests and biopsies related to false positive surveillance tests. Finally, we will document the proportion of participants requiring anaesthesia for surveillance tests and the incidence of adverse events related to surveillance procedures, including those requiring additional clinical interventions and/or hospital admission.

Ethics and dissemination

Informed consent

Informed consent/assent is obtained prior to the collection of research data. Assent and consent forms are Institutional Review Board/Research Ethics Board (IRB/REB)-approved, and assent and consent are obtained per IRB/REB/local ethics committee policies and guidelines. An example consent form is provided in the online supplemental information). Participating institutions in the USA rely on a central IRB (WCG IRB 2020P002450) for human subjects protection oversight, and the REB at the Hospital for Sick Children in Toronto oversees the study there (1000072286). The participant or parent/legal guardian may withdraw consent at any time throughout the course of the study. A signed copy of the informed consent document is given to the participants’ parent/legal guardian. For children under the age of majority or capacity (as locally defined) at the time of parental consent, once the child reaches the age of majority or capacity, informed consent is re-obtained by the primary subject directly. Families are approached about participation in the study after the diagnosis of a cancer predisposition has been disclosed in the context of appropriate genetic counselling and other support, as guided by standard institutional practices.

Informed consent includes permission to (1) access and provide clinical data centrally for CCPS-related research, including family history, (2) collect biospecimens for future research, (3) be contacted for future research (optional), (4) be contacted for return of medically significant and actionable research results (if permitted at the institution of enrolment) and (5) store de-identified data in a manner such that it can be efficiently shared with participating institutions, other consortia and public databases.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not directly involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Data sharing

Qualified investigators will be able to request data and biological samples using a project proposal form available on the website of the Consortium for Childhood Cancer Predisposition (childhoodcancerpredisposition.org). Identifying information will not be stored in the CCPS Database but will be maintained locally, available to CCPS investigators if needed to address specific research questions. All sub-studies using CCPS data or biospecimens through the project proposal form must undergo scientific and regulatory review by the CCPS Scientific Committee and the sub-study investigator’s institutional IRB/REB prior to the release of data or biospecimens.

To ensure long-term access to the data, de-identified data will be exported to the Pediatric Cancer Data Commons (PCDC), an international effort to collect and harmonise clinical research data from children with cancer from around the world into a single unified platform for research. The PCDC data portal allows investigators to explore data from multiple cancer types and trials. Registered investigators may access data in the PCDC for exploration, including determination of subject numbers, specimen availability and hypothesis generation. Line level data may be requested for more detailed analyses. The inclusion of data from children with CPS will allow linkage of pre-cancer history to data from cancer registries, clinical trial participation and survivorship via the US National Cancer Institute’s (NCI) Childhood Cancer Data Initiative Participant Index (CPI). The NCI will serve as an honest broker linking CCPS identifiers with a subject’s CPI.

Findings from the study will be presented at national and international conferences and published in peer-reviewed journals, regardless of the outcome of this study, adhering to personal data privacy regulations.

Discussion

Outcomes in children with cancer have improved dramatically over the last several decades, primarily due to collaborative cooperative national and international groups. However, these cooperative groups do not have an existing mechanism to prospectively enrol children who have not had cancer, even those at the greatest risk due to underlying genetic predispositions. To address this population, the Consortium for Childhood Cancer Predisposition was established and launched the CCPS.

While CCPS is intended to be broadly inclusive of children with any cancer predisposition syndrome, there are practical barriers that may limit participation of some subjects and its impact. The investigators direct clinics or programmes for children with cancer predispositions in which the majority of subjects are enrolled or offered enrolment, but at many of the centres there are multidisciplinary clinics for children with multisystem disorders (eg, neurofibromatosis) who may never have contact with the investigators and the opportunity to enrol, which may introduce selection bias. In addition, CPS are often identified soon after the diagnosis of cancer in a child, a sensitive time when a family may not be interested in considering CCPS. Further, the participants are limited to those in the catchment areas of large, tertiary paediatric centres and may not accurately represent a broader population. Lastly, while funding to initiate the CCPS has been generous, the sustainability of the study may be at risk without diversification of support.

Nonetheless, CCPS has unique strengths that ensure lasting impact. It is a multi-centre registry and biorepository for children with any CPS and it includes children who have, and have not, developed cancer. This aggregated and detailed registry and biorepository aims to open the door for the study of rare CPS that demand multi-institutional interaction to yield high participant numbers. Answers to the principal study question about whether adherence to recommended tumour surveillance guidelines is associated with earlier detection of tumours and/or improved survival will help guide the development of future, data-supported, recommendations. The study also allows for the opportunity to ask unique research questions across genetic syndromes, including patient-reported outcomes. Data and samples are being collected in anticipation of future studies, for example, peripheral blood collected through this study, and others, will allow for future studies of circulating tumour DNA as potential platforms to complement, or even replace, current surveillance strategies. Also, there is considerable interest in genotype-phenotype correlations, as well as epigenetic changes that could result in cancer predisposition but not be detected by routine DNA sequencing methods. These, and other, investigations hold opportunity to better understand our ability to detect asymptomatic disease in high-risk patients and identify the evolution of initial mutational burden that is driving tumourigenesis. In addition, access to tumour samples represents an opportunity for future studies enriched for CPS-related cancers.

Data from CCPS will be widely available through the PCDC and qualified investigators will be able to request data and specimens directly from the Consortium for Childhood Cancer Predisposition. Moreover, study participants will optionally provide consent for future contact about additional studies for which they may be eligible. The data and patient reports within this repository provide an invaluable resource for future studies to have at-hand information from rare populations with paired biologic and clinical data. Moreover, as knowledge of germline and cancer genomics increase, the CCPS can expand eligibility to study new CPS. It is also the goal as funding and resources allow, to expand participating institutions and methods for recruitment to provide study access to as many eligible participants as feasible. The individuals and families affected by CPS have shown dedication to research and deepening knowledge of diagnosis, surveillance, and management of cancer in this population and the CCPS will serve as a registry and biorepository to help advance these goals.
