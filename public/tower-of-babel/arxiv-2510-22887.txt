Doubling and the two-dimensional critical valued Lagrangian phase
Arunima Bhattacharya, Ravi Shankar, and Jeremy Wall

Abstract
. In this paper, we establish interior Hessian and gradient estimates for the two-dimensional Lagrangian mean curvature equation when the phase changes signs, provided the gradient of the phase vanishes along its zero set. At the critical phase in two dimensions, the Jacobi inequality degenerates, preventing the use of higher-dimensional methods to obtain Hessian estimates. To address this difficulty, we introduce a modified doubling technique that applies to degenerate Jacobi inequalities and yields interior estimates.



1.
Introduction

We study the two-dimensional Lagrangian mean curvature equation

(1.1)

\arctan\lambda_{1}+\arctan\lambda_{2}=\Theta(x)

where
\Theta
has bounded second derivatives. Here,
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
are the eigenvalues of the Hessian
D^{2}u
, and the function
\Theta
is called the Lagrangian phase or angle of the Lagrangian submanifold
L=(x,Du(x))\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
. As shown by Harvey-Lawson
[
HL82
, Proposition 2.17]
,
\Theta
acts as the potential of the mean curvature vector of
(x,Du(x))
:

\vec{H}=J\nabla_{g}\Theta,

where
g=I_{2}+(D^{2}u)^{2}
is the induced metric on
L
, and
J
is the almost complex structure on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Thus, equation (
1.1
) is the potential equation for prescribed
Lagrangian mean curvature
. When the Lagrangian phase is constant,
u
solves the
special Lagrangian equation
of Harvey-Lawson
[
HL82
]
. In this case,
H=0
, and
L
is a volume-minimizing Lagrangian submanifold.

An equivalent form of (
1.1
) can be written using the symmetric polynomials:

(1.2)

\tan(\Theta)=\frac{\sigma_{1}}{1-\sigma_{2}}

where
\sigma_{1}(D^{2}u)=\Delta u=\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}
and
\sigma_{2}(D^{2}u)=\det D^{2}u=\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}
.

Our main results are the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
u
be a smooth solution of (
1.1
) in
B_{R}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
-\pi<\Theta<\pi
,
D\Theta=0
on the level set
\{\Theta=0\}
and
\Theta\in C^{2}(B_{R}(0))
. Then the Hessian of
u
admits the bound

|D^{2}u(0)|\leq C(\|u\|_{C^{1}(B_{R}(0))},\|\Theta\|_{C^{2}(B_{R}(0))},R).

Remark 1.1
.

Combining this with the gradient estimate in Lemma
5.1
, we obtain

|D^{2}u(0)|\leq C(\mathrm{osc}_{B_{R}(0)}u,\|\Theta\|_{C^{2}(B_{R}(0))},R).

Remark 1.2
.

A special case of Theorem
1.1
shows that if
u
is a smooth solution of (
1.1
) in
B_{R}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
0\leq|\Theta|<\pi
, and
\Theta\in C^{2}(B_{R}(0))
, then the Hessian of
u
admits the bound

|D^{2}u(0)|\leq C\!\left(\|u\|_{C^{1}(B_{R}(0))},\|\Theta\|_{C^{2}(B_{R}(0))},R\right).

Remark 1.3
.

If
0\leq\Theta<\pi
is nonnegative and
C^{2}(B_{R}(0))
, we find that there exists a solution
u\in C^{3}(\Omega)\cap C^{0}(\bar{\Omega})
to the Dirichlet problem

\begin{cases}\arctan\lambda_{1}+\arctan\lambda_{2}=\Theta(x)\text{ in }\Omega\\
u=\phi\text{ on }\partial\Omega\end{cases}

where
\phi\in C^{0}(\partial\Omega)
. Here
\Omega
is a uniformly convex bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}.
This follows from classical solvability for positive phases
\Theta\geq\delta
in
[
Bha24
]
, combined with the above interior Hessian estimate, the interior gradient estimate in
[
BMS22
]
, and the interior
C^{2,\alpha}
estimate in
[
BSWY25
, Section 7.1]
.

The range of the Lagrangian phase determines the concavity of the arctangent operator in (
1.1
). In dimension
n
, the phase is hypercritical whenever
|\Theta|\geq(n-1)\pi/2
. In this case, the potential
u
is convex, and the arctangent operator is concave. The phase is supercritical whenever
|\Theta|\geq(n-2)\pi/2+\delta
for some fixed
\delta>0
. In this case, the eigenvalues could have opposite signs, but the smaller magnitude eigenvalue is bounded by a constant depending on
\delta
as shown by Yuan
[
Yua06
]
. In the supercritical range, the arctangent operator can be modified to a concave operator by exponentiating it, see
[
CPW17
]
and
[
CW19
]
. The phase is critical and supercritical whenever
|\Theta|\geq(n-2)\pi/2
. The level set
\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\sum\arctan\lambda_{j}=\Theta\}
is convex only when
|\Theta|\geq(n-2)\pi/2

[
Yua06
]
. In the subcritical case, i.e., when
|\Theta|<(n-2)\frac{\pi}{2}
, counterexamples to Hessian estimates exist, and the Dirichlet boundary value problem is not classically solvable with smooth boundary data as shown by Nadirashvili-Vlăduţ
[
NV10
]
, Wang-Yuan
[
WY13
]
, and Mooney-Savin
[
MS23
]
.

Hessian and gradient estimates for the special Lagrangian equation in two dimensions with critical and supercritical phase were first proven by Warren-Yuan
[
WY09
]
. In higher dimensions, Hessian estimates have been shown for the special Lagrangian equation by Warren-Yuan
[
WY10
]
, Wang-Yuan
[
WY14
]
, Li
[
Li19
]
, and most recently, using the doubling method by Shankar
[
Sha24
]
. Hessian estimates for the Lagrangian mean curvature equation with critical and supercritical phase
\Theta\in C^{1,1}
was shown by Bhattacharya
[
Bha21
,
Bha22
]
. See also Lu
[
Lu23
]
. Hessian estimates were later extended by Zhou
[
Zho25
]
to a supercritical Lipschitz continuous phase. Hessian estimates for critical and supercritical Lipschitz continuous phase were shown by Ding
[
Din24
]
using geometric measure theoretic tools.

The difficulty in proving Hessian estimates for the Lagrangian mean curvature equation in two dimensions comes from the fact that when
\Theta
is zero, the function
u
becomes harmonic. Harmonic functions, while known to be regular, do not satisfy a Jacobi inequality and hence, the Jacobi inequality must degenerate as
\Theta
approaches zero. This degeneracy prevents us from applying the direct integral techniques used to prove Hessian estimates in higher dimensions
n\geq 3
. A similar degeneration of the Jacobi inequality occurs for the
\sigma_{2}=1
equation in dimension four
[
SY25
]
. We would like to point out that while the Jacobi inequality given in
[
Bha22
, (2.2)]
remains valid in the supercritical case, once the phase touches the critical value, the constant
c(2)
is no longer purely dimension-dependent. This phenomenon is specific to dimension two only.

A second difficulty is that when
\Theta
crosses the critical value, both eigenvalues may change sign. Previous works avoided this by assuming either
\Theta>0
or
\Theta<0
, where the estimates applied separately. Pointwise, however, one can reduce to the symmetric opposite case since the potential
u
also satisfies

\arctan(-\lambda_{1})+\arctan(-\lambda_{2})=-\Theta(x).

The pointwise calculations are shown invariant under this sign change, so a symmetry argument takes care of negative phase values. The condition that
D\Theta=0
on the level set
\{\Theta=0\}
allows us to use the pointwise interpolation inequality
[
Hör83
, Lemma 7.7.2]
. This assumption on
\Theta
means that the function
g(x)=\max\{0,\Theta(x)\}
(or
g(x)=\max\{0,-\Theta(x)\}
) will be a nonnegative
C^{1,1}
function for which the interpolation inequality still holds.

The doubling method of Shankar-Yuan
[
SY25
]
, used to obtain Hessian estimates for the
\sigma_{2}
equation in dimension four, fails for (
1.1
) because the entries of the linearized
\sigma_{2}
operator behave like
O(|D^{2}u|)
near degeneracy, whereas the arctangent operator behaves like
O(\frac{1}{1+\lambda_{n}^{2}})
, so it shrinks. The good terms are only
O(1)
, while the bad terms are
O(\frac{1}{1+\lambda_{n}^{2}}/\rho^{2})
, creating a delicate balance between the smallness of the operator and the largeness of
1/\rho
. Unlike in the
\sigma_{2}
case, where abundant good terms compensated in the degenerate region, the setting of (
1.1
) requires a finer analysis of the degeneracy. The cutoff in the test function requires adjusting to a higher rate of decay to the boundary.

An important ingredient in the proof is an Alexandrov Theorem that holds for convex functions (see
[
EG92
]
), and was extended by Chaudhuri-Trudinger
[
CT05
]
to functions which are
k
-convex where
k\geq n/2
, and
n
is the dimension. We recall that a function
u
is
k
-convex if
\sigma_{j}\geq 0
for all
1\leq j\leq k
, where
\sigma_{j}
is the
j
-th symmetric polynomial of the eigenvalues of
D^{2}u
. However, in two dimensions, the potential
u
is only
1
-convex (subharmonic) for positive phase, which does not satisfy the above conditions. In order to prove an Alexandrov Theorem for viscosity solutions of (
1.1
), we develop a new scale-invariant, interior gradient estimate and an integral estimate for the volume form
V=\sqrt{\det g}
. We note that this bound of the volume form does not require the assumption that the gradient of the phase vanishes on its zero set.

Lastly, to extend the regularity provided by the Alexandrov Theorem, we make use of a recent result by Fan
[
Fan25
]
which extends Savin’s
[
Sav07
]
perturbation theory to second order, fully nonlinear equations with variable right-hand side. As in
[
SY25
]
, this shows that the regularity at a point
y_{0}
can be extended to regularity over a small ball
B_{r}(y_{0})
. The doubling inequality then extends this regularity to a larger ball containing the singular point, contradicting the assumed irregularity.

Organization

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce notation and recall several useful equations. In Section
3
, we establish the degenerate Jacobi inequality, which is then applied in Section
4
to prove a doubling property for the Hessian. In Section
5
, we develop an Alexandrov-type Theorem by deriving both a scale-invariant gradient estimate and an integral estimate for the volume. In Section
6
, we present the proof of Theorem
1.1
. The paper concludes with an Appendix containing additional computations.

2.
Preliminaries

For the convenience of the readers, we recall some preliminary results. We first introduce some notation that will be used in this paper. The induced Riemannian metric on the Lagrangian submanifold
L=(x,Du(x))\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
is given by

g=I_{2}+(D^{2}u)^{2}.

We denote

\displaystyle\partial_{i}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\text{ , }\partial_{ij}=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}\text{ , }u_{i}=\partial_{i}u\text{ , }u_{ij}=\partial_{ij}u.

Note that for the functions defined below, the subscripts on the left do not represent partial derivatives

\displaystyle h_{ijk}=\sqrt{g^{ii}}\sqrt{g^{jj}}\sqrt{g^{kk}}u_{ijk},\quad g^{ii}=\frac{1}{1+\lambda_{i}^{2}}.

Here,
(g^{ij})
is the inverse of the matrix
g
, and
h_{ijk}
denotes the second fundamental form when the Hessian of
u
is diagonalized.
The volume form, gradient, and inner product with respect to the metric
g
are given by

\displaystyle dv_{g}=\sqrt{\det g}dx

\displaystyle=Vdx\text{ , }\qquad\nabla_{g}v=g^{ij}v_{i}L_{j}

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{g}v,\nabla_{g}w\rangle_{g}

\displaystyle=g^{ij}v_{i}w_{j}\text{ , }\quad|\nabla_{g}v|^{2}=\langle\nabla_{g}v,\nabla_{g}v\rangle_{g}.

The Laplace-Beltrami operator on the non-minimal submanifold
L=(x,Du(x))
is given by:

\Delta_{g}=g^{ij}\partial_{ij}-g^{jp}u_{pq}(\partial_{q}\Theta)\partial_{j}.

On taking the gradient of both sides of the Lagrangian mean curvature equation (
1.1
), we get another useful equation

(2.1)

\sum_{a,b=1}^{2}g^{ab}u_{jab}=\partial_{j}\Theta.

The details for the two above equations can be found in
[
Bha21
]
.

3.
A degenerate Jacobi inequality

In this section, we prove a new Jacobi-type inequality for the slope of the gradient graph
(x,Du(x))
in two dimensions, i.e., we show that a certain function of the slope of the gradient graph
(x,Du(x))
is almost strongly subharmonic.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
u
be a smooth solution of (
1.1
) in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Suppose that the Hessian
D^{2}u
is diagonalized at the point
x_{0}
. Then at
x_{0}
,
b=\log\sqrt{\det g}
satisfies

(3.1)

\displaystyle\Delta_{g}b

\displaystyle\overset{x_{0}}{=}\sum_{a,b,c=1}^{2}(1+\lambda_{b}\lambda_{c})h_{abc}^{2}

(3.2)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{2}g^{ii}\lambda_{i}\partial_{ii}\Theta-\sum_{i=1}^{2}g^{ii}\lambda_{i}(\partial_{i}\Theta)\partial_{i}b.

The proof follows from the computations in
[
Yua02
, Lemma 2.1]
and
[
BW24
, Proposition 3.1]
.

Next, we have the following degenerate Jacobi inequality which was inspired by
[
WY09
]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
u
be a smooth solution of (
1.1
) in
B_{1}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}

-\pi<\Theta<\pi

C^{2}(B_{1}(0))

D\Theta=0

\{\Theta=0\}

D^{2}u

x_{0}

(3.3)

\Delta_{g}b\geq\frac{\sin|\Theta(x_{0})|}{4}|\nabla_{g}b|^{2}-C(\|D^{2}\Theta\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1}(0))})

whenever
0\leq|\Theta(x_{0})|<\frac{\pi}{2}
. When

(3.4)

Proof.

We prove this in four cases, based on the value of

Case 1.
Suppose that

3.1
) from Proposition
3.1
. We will use (
2.1
) in the form of

(3.5)

h_{11i}+h_{22i}=\sqrt{g^{ii}}\Theta_{x_{i}}

for
i=1,2
in order to write everything in terms of
h_{112}
and
h_{122}

\displaystyle\sum_{a,b,c=1}^{2}(1+\lambda_{b}\lambda_{c})h_{abc}^{2}

\displaystyle=h^{2}_{111}(1+\lambda_{1}^{2})+h_{112}^{2}(3+(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})^{2}-\lambda_{2}^{2})

\displaystyle\quad+h_{222}^{2}(1+\lambda_{2}^{2})+h_{122}^{2}(3+(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})^{2}-\lambda_{1}^{2})

\displaystyle=(\sqrt{g^{11}}\Theta_{x_{1}}-h_{122})^{2}(1+\lambda_{1}^{2})+h_{112}^{2}(3+(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})^{2}-\lambda_{2}^{2})

\displaystyle\quad+(\sqrt{g^{22}}\Theta_{x_{2}}-h_{112})^{2}(1+\lambda_{2}^{2})+h_{122}^{2}(3+(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})^{2}-\lambda_{1}^{2})

\displaystyle=(h_{112}^{2}+h_{122}^{2})(4+(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})^{2})+(g^{11}\Theta_{x_{1}}^{2}-2\sqrt{g^{11}}\Theta_{x_{1}}h_{122})(1+\lambda_{1}^{2})

\displaystyle\quad+(g^{22}\Theta_{x_{2}}^{2}-2\sqrt{g^{22}}\Theta_{x_{2}}h_{112})(1+\lambda_{2}^{2}).

Now, the right-most term of (
3.2
) becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}g^{ii}\lambda_{i}(\partial_{i}\Theta)\partial_{i}b

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}g^{ii}g^{jj}\Theta_{x_{i}}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}u_{jji}=\sum_{i,j}^{2}\sqrt{g^{ii}}\Theta_{x_{i}}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}h_{jji}

\displaystyle=g^{11}\Theta_{x_{1}}^{2}\lambda_{1}^{2}-\sqrt{g^{11}}\Theta_{x_{1}}h_{122}\lambda_{1}(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})

\displaystyle\quad+g^{22}\Theta_{x_{2}}^{2}\lambda_{2}^{2}-\sqrt{g^{22}}\Theta_{x_{2}}h_{112}\lambda_{2}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})

where the last lines follow from (
3.5
). Combining these together, we get

\displaystyle\Delta_{g}b

\displaystyle=(h_{112}^{2}+h_{122}^{2})(4+(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})^{2})+(g^{11}\Theta_{x_{1}}^{2}-2\sqrt{g^{11}}\Theta_{x_{1}}h_{122})(1+\lambda_{1}^{2})

\displaystyle\quad+(g^{22}\Theta_{x_{2}}^{2}-2\sqrt{g^{22}}\Theta_{x_{2}}h_{112})(1+\lambda_{2}^{2})

\displaystyle\quad-g^{11}\Theta_{x_{1}}^{2}\lambda_{1}^{2}+\sqrt{g^{11}}\Theta_{x_{1}}h_{122}\lambda_{1}(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})

\displaystyle\quad-g^{22}\Theta_{x_{2}}^{2}\lambda_{2}^{2}+\sqrt{g^{22}}\Theta_{x_{2}}h_{112}\lambda_{2}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})

\displaystyle\quad+g^{11}\lambda_{1}\Theta_{x_{1}x_{1}}+g^{22}\lambda_{2}\Theta_{x_{2}x_{2}}

\displaystyle=(h_{112}^{2}+h_{122}^{2})(4+(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})^{2})

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{g^{11}}\Theta_{x_{1}}h_{122}(-2(1+\lambda_{1}^{2})+\lambda_{1}(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}))

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{g^{22}}\Theta_{x_{2}}h_{112}(-2(1+\lambda_{2}^{2})+\lambda_{2}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}))

\displaystyle\quad+g^{11}\Theta_{x_{1}}^{2}+g^{22}\Theta_{x_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+g^{11}\lambda_{1}\Theta_{x_{1}x_{1}}+g^{22}\lambda_{2}\Theta_{x_{2}x_{2}}.

The gradient of
b
is then given by

\displaystyle|\nabla_{g}b|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{2}g^{ii}(\partial_{i}b)^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{2}g^{ii}\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a,b=1}^{2}g^{ab}\partial_{i}g_{ab}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{2}g^{ii}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{2}g^{jj}\lambda_{j}u_{jji}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=g^{11}(g^{11}\lambda_{1}u_{111}+g^{22}\lambda_{2}u_{122})^{2}+g^{22}(g^{11}\lambda_{1}u_{112}+g^{22}\lambda_{2}u_{222})^{2}

\displaystyle=g^{11}(g^{22}u_{122}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})+\lambda_{1}\Theta_{x_{1}})^{2}+g^{22}(g^{11}u_{112}(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})+\lambda_{2}\Theta_{x_{2}})^{2}

\displaystyle=(h_{112}^{2}+h_{122}^{2})(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})^{2}+g^{11}\lambda_{1}^{2}\Theta_{x_{1}}^{2}+g^{22}\lambda_{2}^{2}\Theta_{x_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+2h_{122}\lambda_{1}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})\sqrt{g^{11}}\Theta_{x_{1}}+2h_{112}\lambda_{2}(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})\sqrt{g^{22}}\Theta_{x_{2}}.

Altogether, we get

\displaystyle\Delta_{g}b-\epsilon|\nabla_{g}b|^{2}

\displaystyle=(h_{112}^{2}+h_{122}^{2})(4+(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})^{2}-\epsilon(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})^{2})

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{g^{11}}\Theta_{x_{1}}h_{122}(-2(1+\lambda_{1}^{2})+\lambda_{1}(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})-2\epsilon\lambda_{1}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}))

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{g^{22}}\Theta_{x_{2}}h_{112}(-2(1+\lambda_{2}^{2})+\lambda_{2}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})-2\epsilon\lambda_{2}(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}))

\displaystyle\quad+g^{11}\Theta_{x_{1}}^{2}(1-\epsilon\lambda_{1}^{2})+g^{22}\Theta_{x_{2}}^{2}(1-\epsilon\lambda_{2}^{2})

\displaystyle\quad+g^{11}\lambda_{1}\Theta_{x_{1}x_{1}}+g^{22}\lambda_{2}\Theta_{x_{2}x_{2}}

(3.6)

\displaystyle=(h_{112}^{2}+h_{122}^{2})(4+(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})^{2}-\epsilon(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})^{2})

(3.7)

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{g^{11}}\Theta_{x_{1}}h_{122}(-(1-2\epsilon)\lambda_{1}^{2}-(1+2\epsilon)\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}-2)

(3.8)

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{g^{22}}\Theta_{x_{2}}h_{112}(-(1-2\epsilon)\lambda_{2}^{2}-(1+2\epsilon)\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}-2)

(3.9)

\displaystyle\quad+g^{11}\Theta_{x_{1}}^{2}(1-\epsilon\lambda_{1}^{2})+g^{22}\Theta_{x_{2}}^{2}(1-\epsilon\lambda_{2}^{2})

\displaystyle\quad+g^{11}\lambda_{1}\Theta_{x_{1}x_{1}}+g^{22}\lambda_{2}\Theta_{x_{2}x_{2}}.

From here we apply Young’s inequality to (
3.7
) and (
3.8
) so that they combine into (
3.6
) and (
3.9
).

Using the alternate form of equation (
1.2
) given by

0=\cos\Theta\Delta u+\sin\Theta(\det D^{2}u-1)=\cos\Theta\sigma_{1}+\sin\Theta(\sigma_{2}-1),

we rewrite the coefficient of
(h_{112}^{2}+h_{122}^{2})
as a quadratic polynomial in
\sigma_{1}
. We note that
\sigma_{1}=\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}\geq 0
and
\sigma_{2}=\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\leq 0
from the assumption that
0<\Theta(x_{0})\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
.

Applying Young’s inequality to (
3.7
), we get

\displaystyle\sqrt{g^{11}}\Theta_{x_{1}}h_{122}

\displaystyle(-\lambda_{1}^{2}+2\epsilon\lambda_{1}^{2}-\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}-2\epsilon\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}-2)\geq

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{g^{11}\Theta_{x_{1}}^{2}\lambda_{1}^{2}}{2\eta}-\frac{h_{122}^{2}\lambda_{1}^{2}\eta}{2}-\epsilon g^{11}\Theta_{x_{1}}^{2}\lambda_{1}^{2}-\epsilon h_{122}^{2}\lambda_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{g^{11}\Theta_{x_{1}}^{2}\lambda_{1}^{2}}{2\eta}-\frac{h_{122}^{2}\lambda_{2}^{2}\eta}{2}-\epsilon g^{11}\Theta_{x_{1}}^{2}\lambda_{1}^{2}-\epsilon h_{122}^{2}\lambda_{2}^{2}-g^{11}\Theta_{x_{1}}^{2}-h_{122}^{2}.

Applying Young’s inequality again, (
3.8
) combines with (
3.6
) and (

\displaystyle\Delta_{g}b-\epsilon|\nabla_{g}b|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq(h_{112}^{2}+h_{122}^{2})\bigg((1-2\epsilon-\eta/2)\sigma_{1}^{2}-\cot\Theta(\eta+6\epsilon)\sigma_{1}+(3+\eta+6\epsilon)\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad-g^{11}\Theta_{x_{1}}^{2}(1+\lambda_{1}^{2}(2\epsilon+1/\eta))-g^{22}\Theta_{x_{1}}^{2}(1+\lambda_{2}^{2}(2\epsilon+1/\eta))

\displaystyle\quad+g^{11}\lambda_{1}\Theta_{x_{1}x_{1}}+g^{22}\lambda_{2}\Theta_{x_{2}x_{2}}.

For the coefficient on the mean curvature terms to be positive it suffices to pick

such that

\displaystyle(1-2\epsilon-\eta/2)

\displaystyle\geq 0

(3.10)

\displaystyle\cot^{2}\Theta(\eta+6\epsilon)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4(1-2\epsilon-\eta/2)(3+\eta+6\epsilon)

where the second line comes from the quadratic formula of
a\sigma_{1}^{2}+b\sigma_{1}+c
and forcing

We choose

\displaystyle 4(1-\sin\Theta)(3+\frac{5}{2}\sin\Theta)-(5/4)^{2}\cos^{2}\Theta

\displaystyle=4(3-\frac{1}{2}\sin\Theta-\frac{5}{2}\sin^{2}\Theta)-\frac{25}{4}\cos^{2}\Theta

\displaystyle=12-2\sin\Theta-10\sin^{2}\Theta-\frac{25}{4}\cos^{2}\Theta

\displaystyle=12-\frac{25}{4}-2\sin\Theta-\frac{15}{4}\sin^{2}\Theta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}(23-8\sin\Theta-15\sin^{2}\Theta)\geq 0

We get

(3.11)

\Delta_{g}b-\frac{\sin\Theta}{4}|\nabla_{g}b|^{2}\geq-|D\Theta|^{2}(3/2+\csc\Theta)+g^{11}\lambda_{1}\Theta_{x_{1}x_{1}}+g^{22}\lambda_{2}\Theta_{x_{2}x_{2}}.

As

on
\{\Theta=0\}
, we can apply the pointwise interpolation inequality
[

, Lemma 7.7.2]
to

, which is still

5.
Alexandrov-type Theorem

We seek to prove the following theorem, which is inspired by
[

]
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
u_{k}
be a sequence of smooth solutions to (
1.1
) for some
\Theta_{k}
on
B_{R}(0)
. Suppose that

converges uniformly to

1.1
) on
B_{R}(0)

\Theta_{k}
satisfy
\|D\Theta_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R}(0))},\|D^{2}\Theta_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R}(0))}<K_{2}
and
\Theta_{k}\to\Theta
in

for
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Then
u
is twice differentiable almost everywhere in
B_{R}(0)
, i.e., for any
x\in B_{R}(0)
, there exists a quadratic polynomial
Q
such that

\sup_{y\in B_{r}(x)}|u(y)-Q(y)|=o(r^{2}).

To prove this, it suffices to show that for the viscosity solution
u
, we get

(5.1)

\displaystyle\sup_{x,y\in\Omega:x\neq y}d_{x,y}^{n+1}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{|x-y|}

\displaystyle\leq C(n)\int_{\Omega}|u|dx

(5.2)

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0}\fint_{B_{r}(x)}|Du(y)-Du(x)|dy

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{a.e. }x\in B_{R}(0)

(5.3)

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0}\fint_{B_{r}(x)}|D^{2}u(y)-D^{2}(x)|dy

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{a.e. }x\in B_{R}(0)

(5.4)

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{1}{r^{n}}\|[D^{2}u]_{s}\|(B_{r}(x))

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{a.e. }x\in B_{R}(0).

The first line states that
u
is locally Lipschitz. For more general operators, we actually need that

, where

is any linear function, satisfies (

); however, in this case, if
u
solves (
1.1
), then so does

and (

) will hold true for

is differentiable almost everywhere with the weak gradient

. The third and fourth lines state the almost everywhere existence of

as a Radon measure and the Lebesgue decomposition of that measure. If the four conditions above are satisfied, the proof of Theorem

follows exactly as in
[
SY25
]
,
[
EG92
]
, or
[
CT05
]
.

In order to prove (

5.1.

This follows by adopting a similar strategy to

Lemma 5.1
.

Let

\displaystyle\rho_{4}(\Theta(x))\sigma_{1}dx=\int_{B_{2R}(0)}\chi(x/R)\rho_{4}(\Theta(x))\text{div}Du\;dx

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{R}\int_{B_{2R}(0)}\rho_{4}(\Theta(x))\langle D\chi,Du\rangle dx

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{B_{2R}(0)}\chi(x/R)\rho_{4}^{\prime}(\Theta)\langle D\Theta,Du\rangle dx

\displaystyle\leq 4|B_{R}|\left(\frac{1}{R}+\frac{8}{\pi}\|D\Theta\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{2R}(0))}\right)\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{2R}(0))}.

To bound the integral with
\sigma_{2}
, we can use the inductive argument from
[
WY14
]
and the divergence structure of
\sigma_{k}(D^{2}u)
:

\displaystyle k\sigma_{k}(D^{2}u)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}\frac{\partial\sigma_{k}}{\partial u_{ij}}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\left(\frac{\partial\sigma_{k}}{\partial u_{ij}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=\text{div}(L_{\sigma_{k}}Du),

where
L_{\sigma_{k}}
is the matrix given by
\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial\sigma_{k}}{\partial u_{ij}}\right)
. We get

\displaystyle\int_{B_{2R}(0)}\chi(x/R)

\displaystyle\rho_{3}(\Theta(x))(-\sigma_{2})dx=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{2R}(0)}\chi(x/R)\rho_{3}(\Theta(x))\text{ div}(L_{\sigma_{2}}Du)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{2R}(0)}\langle D(\chi(x/R)\rho_{3}(\Theta(x))),L_{\sigma_{2}}Du\rangle dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{2R}(0)}\partial_{1}\big[\chi(x/R)\rho_{3}(\Theta(x))\big]\left[\partial_{2}(u_{1}u_{2})-\partial_{1}(u_{2}^{2})\right]dx

\displaystyle\;+\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{2R}(0)}\partial_{2}\big[\chi(x/R)\rho_{3}(\Theta(x))\big]\left[\partial_{1}(u_{1}u_{2})-\partial_{2}(u_{1}^{2})\right]dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{2R}(0)}\partial_{1}^{2}\big[\chi(x/R)\rho_{3}(\Theta(x))\big]u_{2}^{2}\;dx

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{2R}(0)}\partial_{2}^{2}\big[\chi(x/R)\rho_{3}(\Theta(x))\big]u_{1}^{2}\;dx

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{2R}(0)}2\partial_{12}^{2}\big[\chi(x/R)\rho_{3}(\Theta(x))\big]u_{1}u_{2}\;dx

\displaystyle\leq 4|B_{R}|\left(\frac{2}{R^{2}}+\frac{48}{\pi^{2}}\|D\Theta\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{2R}(0))}^{2}+\frac{4}{\pi}\|D^{2}\Theta\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{2R}(0))}\right)\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{2R}(0))}^{2}

where the third line uses the divergence structure of
L_{\sigma_{2}}Du
and integration by parts. To see this, observe that

\sigma_{2}=\det\begin{bmatrix}u_{11}&u_{12}\\
u_{21}&u_{22}\end{bmatrix}=u_{11}u_{22}-u_{12}u_{21}

from which we get

L_{\sigma_{2}}=\begin{bmatrix}u_{22}&-u_{21}\\
-u_{12}&u_{11}\end{bmatrix}.

Altogether this becomes

\displaystyle L_{\sigma_{2}}Du

\displaystyle=(u_{22}u_{1}-u_{21}u_{2},-u_{12}u_{1}+u_{11}u_{2})

\displaystyle=(\partial_{2}(u_{2}u_{1})-2u_{2}u_{21},\partial_{1}(u_{1}u_{2})-2u_{12}u_{1})

\displaystyle=(\partial_{2}(u_{2}u_{1})-\partial_{1}(u_{2}^{2}),\partial_{1}(u_{1}u_{2})-\partial_{2}(u_{1}^{2})).

The bound on the volume element is then

\frac{1}{|B_{R}|}\int_{B_{R}(0)}Vdx\leq 12C_{1}\left(1+C_{2}\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{2R}(0))}^{2}+C_{3}\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{2R}(0))}\right).

where

C_{2}=\frac{2}{R^{2}}+\frac{48}{\pi^{2}}\|D\Theta\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(B_{2R}(0))}+\frac{4}{\pi}\|D^{2}\Theta\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{2R}(0))}\quad\text{and}\quad C_{3}=\frac{1}{R}+\frac{8}{\pi}\|D\Theta\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{2R}(0))}.

∎

Proof of (

)

We now prove that

(5.12)

To bound the right-hand side, we use the gradient estimate:

By combining (

Proof of (
5.3

We now prove that

.

\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(B_{R}(0))

(5.15)

\int_{B_{R}(0)}u_{k}\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}dx=\int_{B_{R}(0)}\phi\frac{\partial^{2}u_{k}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}dx.

Again since

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{B_{R}(0)}u_{k}\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}dx=\int_{B_{R}(0)}\lim_{k\to\infty}u_{k}\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}dx=\int_{B_{R}(0)}u\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}dx.

The right-hand side is trickier since we don’t have a point-wise bound on the second derivatives. However, we do have the following pointwise

Furthermore, using the volume bound (

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}(0)}V_{k}dx

\displaystyle\leq C(R,\|D\Theta_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{2R(0)})},\|D^{2}\Theta_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{2R(0)})})(\|Du_{k}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(B_{2R(0)})}+\|Du_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{2R(0)})}+1)

\displaystyle\leq C(R,K_{2})(\max_{k\leq M}\{(\mathrm{osc}_{B_{2R(0)}}(u_{k}-u))^{4}\}+(\mathrm{osc}_{B_{2R(0)}}u)^{4}+2)

where the last line is independent of

for all

6.
Proof of the Main Theorem

Proof.

Our proof follows in four steps.

Step 1.

We claim that
|D^{2}u(0)|
is bounded above by
\|u\|_{C^{1}(B_{R}(0))}
. Otherwise, there exists a sequence of smooth solutions to (
1.1
) with variable
\Theta_{k}\in C^{1,1}
on
B_{R}(0)
such that
\|u_{k}\|_{C^{1}(B_{r_{1}}(0))}\leq K_{1}
for
r_{1}<R
, but
|D^{2}u_{k}(0)|\to\infty
. We note that the gradient and Hessian of
\Theta_{k}
are uniformly bounded above by some constant
K_{2}
. By Arzelà-Ascoli, we can extract a subsequence, still denoted
u_{k}
, which converges uniformly over a smaller ball
B_{r_{1}}(0)
and
\Theta_{k}\to\Theta
in
C^{1,\alpha}
for
0<\alpha<1
. By the closedness of viscosity solutions
[
CC95
]
,
u_{k}
converges uniformly to a continuous viscosity solution, which we denote by
u
.

Step 2.

By the Alexandrov-type Theorem
5.1
, we have that
u
is second differentiable almost everywhere on
B_{r_{1}}(0)
. Fix such a point
y\in B_{r_{2}}(0)
where
r_{2}<r_{1}/2
and let
Q(x)
be the quadratic such that
\sup_{x\in B_{r_{2}}(y)}|u(x)-Q(x)|=o(r_{2}^{2})
.

Step 3.

We now apply Fan’s
[
Fan25
]
extension of Savin’s
[
Sav07
]
perturbation theorem to
v_{k}=u_{k}-Q
. We rescale the functions
v_{k}
to

\bar{v}_{k}(\bar{x})=\frac{1}{r_{2}^{2}}v_{k}(r_{2}\bar{x}+y)\quad\text{ for }\bar{x}\in B_{1}(0).

It follows that

\displaystyle\|\bar{v}_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1}(0))}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|u_{k}(r_{2}\bar{x}+y)-u(r_{2}\bar{x}+y)\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1}(0))}}{r_{2}^{2}}+\frac{\|u(r_{2}\bar{x}+y)-Q(r_{2}\bar{x}+y)\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1}(0))}}{r_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\|u_{k}-u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r_{2}}(y))}}{r_{2}^{2}}+\frac{\|u-Q\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r_{2}}(y))}}{r_{2}^{2}}

(6.1)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{o(k)}{r_{2}^{2}k}+\frac{o(r_{2}^{2})}{r_{2}^{2}}.

We also have that
\bar{v}_{k}
solves

G(D^{2}\bar{w}(\bar{x}))=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left[\arctan\lambda_{i}(D^{2}\bar{w}+D^{2}Q)-\arctan\lambda_{i}(D^{2}Q)\right]=f_{k}(\bar{x})

where

We note that as

is a quadratic,
D^{2}Q
is a constant matrix. We then see that
G
satisfies the hypotheses of
[
Fan25
, Theorem 1.7]
, namely, 1)
G
is elliptic and furthermore, uniformly elliptic near the origin, 2)
G(0)=0
, and 3)
G
is
C^{2}
near the origin.

We show that for
k
large enough and
r_{2}
small enough,

\|\bar{v}_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1}(0))}\leq c_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad\|f_{k}\|_{C^{0,\alpha}(B_{1}(0))}\leq c_{1}

for some

depending on

.
We first choose
r_{2}
small enough and
k
large enough so that (
6.1
) is smaller than
c_{1}
. We then see that

\displaystyle\|f_{k}\|_{C^{0,\alpha}(B_{1}(0))}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\bar{x},\bar{x}^{\prime}\in B_{1}(0):\bar{x}\neq\bar{x}^{\prime}}\frac{|f(\bar{x})-f(\bar{x}^{\prime})|}{|\bar{x}-\bar{x}^{\prime}|^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\bar{x},\bar{x}^{\prime}\in B_{1}(0):\bar{x}\neq\bar{x}^{\prime}}\frac{|\Theta_{k}(r_{2}\bar{x}+y)-\Theta_{k}(r_{2}\bar{x}^{\prime}+y)|}{|\bar{x}-\bar{x}^{\prime}|^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{x,x^{\prime}\in B_{r_{2}}(y):x\neq x^{\prime}}\frac{|\Theta_{k}(x)-\Theta_{k}(x^{\prime})|}{|x-x^{\prime}|^{\alpha}}r_{2}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq\|\Theta_{k}\|_{C^{0,\alpha}(B_{r_{1}}(0))}r_{2}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq(\|\Theta_{k}-\Theta\|_{C^{0,\alpha}(B_{r_{1}}(0))}+\|\Theta\|_{C^{0,\alpha}(B_{r_{1}}(0))})r_{2}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\|\Theta\|_{C^{0,\alpha}(B_{r_{1}}(0))})r_{2}^{\alpha}

where in the last line we choose

.

We then get
\bar{v}_{k}\in C^{2,\alpha}(B_{1/2}(0))
with

which is equivalent to

(6.3)

\|u_{k}\|_{C^{2,\alpha}(B_{r_{2}/2}(y))}\leq C(n,Q,\alpha,r_{2}).

Step 4.

We now apply the doubling inequality in Lemma
4.1

\displaystyle|D^{2}u_{k}(0)|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{B_{r_{2}}(y)}|D^{2}u_{k}|

which contradicts the blowup assumption. This concludes the proof.

In this case, we are taking
x=1/|\lambda_{2}|
, so we will consider
0\leq x\leq 1
. We take

Observe that

and

for

4.12
)

We similarly take
y=1/|\lambda_{1}|
, and thus
0\leq y\leq 1
. Let
0<p\leq 1
. We show that

6.1
for some
C(p)
to be chosen. We see that
f(0)=0
and choose

We then calculate

\displaystyle=\frac{pCy^{p-1}}{(1+y^{2})^{\frac{p}{2}}}-\frac{pCy^{p+1}}{(1+y^{2})^{\frac{p}{2}+1}}-\frac{1}{1+y^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{pCy^{p}}{(1+y^{2})^{\frac{p}{2}}}\frac{1}{y(1+y^{2})}-\frac{1}{1+y^{2}}

Lastly, we get that

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\bar{b}}\leq\frac{C}{\rho^{2}\bar{b}^{1-q}}.
