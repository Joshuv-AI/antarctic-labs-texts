Numerical analysis and simulation of lateral memristive devices: Schottky, ohmic, and multi-dimensional electrode models
Dilara Abdel, Maxime Herda, Martin Ziegler, Claire Chainais-Hillairet, Benjamin Spetzler, Patricio Farrell

Abstract
In this paper, we present the numerical analysis and simulations of a multi-dimensional memristive device model. Memristive devices and memtransistors based on two-dimensional (2D) materials have demonstrated promising potential for neuromorphic computing and next-generation memory technologies. Our charge transport model describes the drift-diffusion of electrons, holes, and ionic defects self-consistently in an electric field. We incorporate two types of boundary models: ohmic and Schottky contacts. The coupled drift-diffusion partial differential equations are discretized using a physics-preserving Voronoi finite volume method. It relies on an implicit time-stepping scheme and the excess chemical potential flux approximation. We demonstrate that the fully discrete nonlinear scheme is unconditionally stable, preserving the free-energy structure of the continuous system and ensuring the non-negativity of carrier densities. Novel discrete entropy-dissipation inequalities for both boundary condition types in multiple dimensions allow us to prove the existence of discrete solutions. We perform multi-dimensional simulations to understand the impact of electrode configurations and device geometries, focusing on the hysteresis behavior in lateral 2D memristive devices. Three electrode configurations – side, top, and mixed contacts – are compared numerically for different geometries and boundary conditions. These simulations reveal the conditions under which a simplified one-dimensional electrode geometry can well represent the three electrode configurations. This work lays the foundations for developing accurate, efficient simulation tools for 2D memristive devices and memtransistors, offering tools and guidelines for their design and optimization in future applications.

Modeling charge transport in memristive devices

2.1
Charge transport model

Let
\mathbf{\Omega}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\{1,2,3\}
, be an open, connected, and bounded spatial domain representing the TMDC material layer.
The TMDC layer is typically placed on a substrate and laterally sandwiched between two electrode contacts, as shown in
Figure

2.1
.
The common electrode configurations include side, top, and mixed contacts, with the interface between the electrodes and the TMDC layer denoted by
\mathbf{\Gamma}^{C}
.
We consider the transport of three carriers: electrons (
\alpha=\text{n}
), holes (
\alpha=\text{p}
), and ionic defects (
\alpha=\text{a}
).
Our approach closely follows the charge transport model for TMDC-based memristive devices formulated in
[
43
]
.
The set of unknowns consists of
(\varphi_{\text{n}},\varphi_{\text{p}},\varphi_{\text{a}},\psi)
, where
\varphi_{\alpha}
represents the quasi Fermi potential of carrier species
\alpha\in\{\text{n},\text{p},\text{a}\}
, and
\psi
is the electrostatic potential.

:
Illustration of a three-dimensional geometry of a memristive device for a
(a)
side,
(b)
top, and
(c)
mixed contact with indicated substrate and TMDC layer
\mathbf{\Omega}
.
Furthermore, the contact boundary
\mathbf{\Gamma}^{C}
, defined as the interfaces of the contacts (gold) and the TMDC material (blue), are highlighted in red for each configuration.

For
t\geq 0
the electron, hole, and ionic defect densities
n_{\text{n}},n_{\text{p}},n_{\text{a}}
satisfy the continuity equations

\displaystyle z_{\alpha}q\partial_{t}n_{\alpha}+\nabla\cdot\mathbf{j}_{\alpha}=0,\quad{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbf{\Omega},\ t\geq 0,\quad\text{for}\;\alpha\in\{\text{n},\text{p},\text{a}\},

(2.1a)

which are self-consistently coupled via the electrostatic potential
\psi
to the nonlinear Poisson equation

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(\varepsilon_{s}\nabla\psi)=\!\!\!\!\sum_{\alpha\in\{\text{n},\text{p},\text{a}\}}\!\!\!\!z_{\alpha}qn_{\alpha}+z_{C}qC({\mathbf{x}}),~\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{\Omega},\ t\geq 0.

(2.1b)

The quantity
z_{\alpha}
is the charge number of the moving charge carrier species
\alpha\in\{\text{n},\text{p},\text{a}\}
,
q
the positive elementary charge and
\mathbf{j}_{\alpha}
the carrier dependent current density.
For electrons and holes, we assume
z_{\text{n}}=-1
and
z_{\text{p}}=1
.
For the ionic defects, we have
z_{\text{a}}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Moreover, we define
\varepsilon_{s}=\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{r}
as the dielectric permittivity of the TMDC material given as a product of the vacuum permittivity
\varepsilon_{0}
and the relative permittivity
\varepsilon_{r}
.
Furthermore,
C\in L^{\infty}(\mathbf{\Omega})
denotes one type of background charge density with a respective sign
z_{C}\in\{-1,1\}
.
For all carriers, we assume no reaction or production rates in (
2.1a
).
The current densities contributing to the continuity equations (
2.1a
) are defined as

\mathbf{j}_{\alpha}=-z_{\alpha}^{2}q\mu_{\alpha}n_{\alpha}\nabla\varphi_{\alpha},\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{\Omega},\ t\geq 0,~\quad\text{for}\;\alpha\in\{\text{n},\text{p},\text{a}\},

(2.1c)

where
\mu_{\text{n}},\mu_{\text{p}},\mu_{\text{a}}
are the carrier-dependent mobilities.
We have formulated the model equations based on the quasi Fermi potentials, the electrostatic potential, and the charge carrier densities.
A state equation connects these quantities

n_{\alpha}=N_{\alpha}\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}\Bigl(\eta_{\alpha}(\varphi_{\alpha},\psi)\Bigr),\quad\eta_{\alpha}=z_{\alpha}\frac{q(\varphi_{\alpha}-\psi)+E_{\alpha}}{k_{B}T},\quad\alpha\in\{\text{n},\text{p},\text{a}\}.

(2.1d)

Here,
N_{\text{n}},N_{\text{p}}
represent the effective densities of states of the conduction and valence bands, respectively, while
N_{\text{a}}
denotes the saturation limit for the ionic defect concentration.
The function
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
is referred to as statistics function, and its argument
\eta_{\alpha}
is called the dimensionless chemical potential.
The dimensionless chemical potential depends on the intrinsic energy level
E_{\alpha}
, the temperature
T
, and the Boltzmann constant
k_{B}
.
For electrons and holes, we also call
E_{\text{n}},E_{\text{p}}
the intrinsic band edge energies of the conduction and valence bands, respectively.
Lastly, the choice of statistics functions
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
in (
2.1d
) depends on the carrier species
\alpha\in\{\text{n},\text{p},\text{a}\}
.
For inorganic three-dimensional semiconductor devices, the Fermi-Dirac integral of order
1/2
is typically used for electrons and holes

F_{1/2}(\eta)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\xi^{1/2}}{\exp(\xi-\eta)+1}\>\mathrm{d}\xi,\quad\eta\in\mathbb{R},

(2.2)

i.e.,
\mathcal{F}_{\text{n}}=\mathcal{F}_{\text{p}}=F_{1/2}
.
Contrarily, for the ionic defects
\alpha=\text{a}
, we choose the Fermi-Dirac integral of order
-1
,

\displaystyle F_{-1}(\eta)=\frac{1}{\exp(-\eta)+1},\quad\eta\in\mathbb{R},

(2.3)

i.e.,
\mathcal{F}_{\text{a}}=F_{-1}
, which limits the ionic defect accumulation to a maximum defect density
[
79
,
52
]
.
Generally, for the upcoming mathematical analysis of the model, we assume for
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in\{\text{n},\text{p},\text{a}\}
,

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{F}_{\text{n}},\mathcal{F}_{\text{p}}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow(0,\infty)\text{ are }C^{1}\text{- diffeomorphisms};\\[5.0pt]
&0<\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{\prime}(\eta)\leq\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(\eta)\leq\exp(\eta),\quad\eta\in\mathbb{R},\ \alpha\in\{\text{n},\text{p}\}.\end{aligned}\right.

(H1)

and

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{F}_{\text{a}}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow(0,1)\text{ is a }C^{1}\text{- diffeomorphism};\\[5.0pt]
&0<\mathcal{F}_{\text{a}}^{\prime}(\eta)\leq\mathcal{F}_{\text{a}}(\eta)\leq\exp(\eta),\quad\eta\in\mathbb{R}.\end{aligned}\right.

(H2)

Indeed, the introduced statistics functions (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) satisfy these hypotheses
[
52
]
.
Moreover, direct implications for
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in\{\text{n},\text{p},\text{a}\}
, are the inequalities

\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{-1})^{\prime}\bigl(\frac{n_{\alpha}}{N_{\alpha}}\bigr)=\left(\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{\alpha}\left(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{-1}\bigl(\frac{n_{\alpha}}{N_{\alpha}}\bigr)\right)\right)^{-1}\geq\frac{1}{n},\quad~\log\bigl(\frac{n_{\alpha}}{N_{\alpha}}\bigr)\leq\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{-1}\bigl(\frac{n_{\alpha}}{N_{\alpha}}\bigr).

(2.4)

We can formulate a generalized Einstein relation for
\alpha\in\{\text{n},\text{p},\text{a}\}
,

D_{\alpha}\bigl(\frac{n_{\alpha}}{N_{\alpha}}\bigr)=\mu_{\alpha}U_{T}g_{\alpha}\bigl(\frac{n_{\alpha}}{N_{\alpha}}\bigr),\quad g_{\alpha}\bigl(\frac{n_{\alpha}}{N_{\alpha}}\bigr)=\frac{n_{\alpha}}{N_{\alpha}}\left(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{-1}\right)^{\prime}\bigl(\frac{n_{\alpha}}{N_{\alpha}}\bigr),

(2.5)

where
U_{T}
denotes the thermal voltage and the quantity
g_{\alpha}
refers to the nonlinear diffusion enhancement
[
79
]
.
With the generalized Einstein relation and (
2.1d
) we can rewrite the current densities (
2.1c
) in a drift-diffusion form with a density-dependent diffusion

\displaystyle\mathbf{j}_{\alpha}=-z_{\alpha}q\Bigl(D_{\alpha}\bigl(\frac{n_{\alpha}}{N_{\alpha}}\bigr)\nabla n_{\alpha}+z_{\alpha}\mu_{\alpha}n_{\alpha}\nabla\psi\Bigr).

(2.6)

Finally, the system (
2.1
) is supplied with initial conditions for
t=0

\displaystyle\varphi_{\text{n}}(\mathbf{x},0)=\varphi_{\text{n}}^{0}(\mathbf{x}),~\quad\varphi_{\text{p}}(\mathbf{x},0)=\varphi_{\text{p}}^{0}(\mathbf{x}),~\quad\varphi_{\text{a}}(\mathbf{x},0)=\varphi_{\text{a}}^{0}(\mathbf{x}),\quad\mathbf{x}\in\boldsymbol{\Omega},

(2.7)

where we assume
\varphi_{\text{n}}^{0},\varphi_{\text{p}}^{0},\varphi_{\text{a}}^{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbf{\Omega})
.
We define the initial densities via
n_{\alpha}^{0}(\mathbf{x})=N_{\alpha}\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(\eta_{\alpha}(\varphi_{\alpha}^{0},\psi(\mathbf{x},0)))
for
\alpha\in\{\text{n},\text{p},\text{a}\}
.

2.1.1
Boundary conditions

We divide the outer boundary of the device geometry
\mathbf{\Omega}
into two parts:
\mathbf{\Gamma}^{C}
and
\mathbf{\Gamma}^{N}
.
Here,
\mathbf{\Gamma}^{C}
denotes the interface between the semiconductor and the electrodes, as highlighted in red in
Figure

2.1
, while
\mathbf{\Gamma}^{N}
refers to the remaining boundaries.
We assume that
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{C}
and
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{N}
are closed subsets of
\partial\boldsymbol{\Omega}
with
\partial\mathbf{\Omega}=\mathbf{\Gamma}^{C}\cup\mathbf{\Gamma}^{N}
.
For the ionic point defects all boundaries represent physical barriers described by an isolating Neumann boundary condition

\displaystyle\mathbf{j}_{\text{a}}(\mathbf{x},t)\cdot{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(\mathbf{x})=0,\quad\mathbf{x}\in\partial\mathbf{\Omega},\ t\geq 0,

(2.8a)

where
\boldsymbol{\nu}
is the outward pointing unit normal to
\partial\mathbf{\Omega}
.
At the isolating Neumann boundary we impose for the other species zero flux conditions

\displaystyle\nabla\psi(\mathbf{x},t)\cdot{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(\mathbf{x})=~\mathbf{j}_{\text{n}}(\mathbf{x},t)\cdot{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(\mathbf{x})=~\mathbf{j}_{\text{p}}(\mathbf{x},t)\cdot{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(\mathbf{x})=~0,\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{\Gamma}^{N},\ t\geq 0.

(2.8b)

1. Schottky boundary model.

A Schottky model is commonly assumed at the metal-semiconductor contact
\mathbf{\Gamma}^{C}
for memristive devices.
The Schottky boundary conditions describe thermionic emission of electrons and holes via

\displaystyle\mathbf{j}_{\text{n}}(\mathbf{x},t)\cdot{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=z_{\text{n}}qv_{\text{n}}(n_{\mathrm{n}}(\mathbf{x},t)-n_{\mathrm{n},0}),\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{\Gamma}^{C},\ t\geq 0,

(2.9a)

\displaystyle~\mathbf{j}_{\text{p}}(\mathbf{x},t)\cdot{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=z_{\text{p}}qv_{\text{p}}(n_{\text{p}}(\mathbf{x},t)-n_{\text{p},0}),\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{\Gamma}^{C},\ t\geq 0.

(2.9b)

Here,
v_{\text{n}},v_{\text{p}}\geq 0
are the electron and hole recombination velocities, respectively.
The equilibrium carrier densities
n_{\text{n},0}
, and
n_{\text{p},0}
at the contacts are given by
n_{\alpha,0}:=N_{\alpha}\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(\eta_{\alpha}(\varphi_{0},\psi_{0}))
for
\alpha\in\{\text{n},\text{p}\}
, where
\varphi_{0}
is the constant quasi Fermi potential at equilibrium, and
\psi_{0}=-(\phi_{0}-E_{\text{n}})/q
denotes the intrinsic electrostatic potential barrier.
In the expression for
\psi_{0}
, we have
\phi_{0}=\phi_{0}(\mathbf{x})>0
as the intrinsic Schottky energy barrier constant.
For the electrostatic potential at the metal-semiconductor interface
\mathbf{\Gamma}^{C}
, we apply the Dirichlet condition

\displaystyle\psi(\mathbf{x},t)=\psi_{0}(\mathbf{x})+V(\mathbf{x},t)~,\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{\Gamma}^{C},\ t\geq 0,

(2.9c)

where
V
is a time-dependent voltage applied at the contact.

2. Ohmic boundary model.

As we will see in the simulations, for specific scenarios, we can replace the Schottky contact boundary model with an ohmic contact boundary model

\displaystyle\psi(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=\psi_{0}(\mathbf{x})+V(\mathbf{x},t)~,\quad

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{\Gamma}^{C},\ t\geq 0,

(2.10a)

\displaystyle~\varphi_{\text{n}}(\mathbf{x},t)=\varphi_{\text{p}}(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=V(\mathbf{x},t),\quad~

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{\Gamma}^{C},\ t\geq 0,

(2.10b)

where
V
denotes again a time-dependent applied voltage.
In other words, the Robin boundary conditions for electrons and holes (
2.9a
), (
2.9b
) are replaced by a Dirichlet condition.

3. Time-constant applied voltage for Schottky and ohmic contact boundary models.

In order to avoid unnecessary technicalities in the exposition we make the additional assumption of a time-constant applied voltage, i.e.,
V(\mathbf{x},t)=\overline{V}(\mathbf{x})
,
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{\Gamma}^{C}

Typically,

is constant along each electrode.
The proofs are valid for both contact boundary models, the Schottky boundary conditions (
2.9
) and the ohmic contact boundary conditions (

).
Our stability and existence results can be extended to time-varying applied voltages under some mild assumptions on the boundary data.
We refer to Remark
3.5

This extension is particularly relevant for modeling pulse measurements

43
]
, where devices are operated by applying repetitively short constant-voltage pulses.

2.2
Entropy functions and continuous entropy-dissipation inequality

A well-established tool for the structural analysis of PDE models and their discrete counterparts is the entropy method
[
77
]
.
Typically, the entropy function
\Phi_{\alpha}
of a carrier
\alpha\in\{\text{n},\text{p},\text{a}\}
is defined as the anti-derivative of the inverse of the statistics function

\Phi_{\alpha}^{\prime}(x)=\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{-1}(x),\quad x\geq 0.

(2.17)

Since the statistics function is strictly increasing (due to (
H1
) and (
H2
)),
\Phi_{\alpha}
is strictly convex.
By (
2.17
) the entropy function
\Phi_{\alpha}
is only uniquely defined up to a constant.
Thus, for the ionic defects we assume that the integration constant to uniquely determine
\Phi_{\text{a}}
via (
2.17
) is chosen in such a way that
\Phi_{\text{a}}
is non-negative and vanishes at only one point.
Moreover, for electrons and holes
\alpha=\text{n},\text{p}
we introduce a relative entropy function to deal with the boundary conditions.
We have

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}(x,y)=\Phi_{\alpha}(x)-\Phi_{\alpha}(y)-\Phi_{\alpha}^{\prime}(y)(x-y),\quad x,y\geq 0,

(2.18)

which is non-negative due to the convexity of
\Phi_{\alpha}
.
For example, in case of a Boltzmann approximation, i.e.
\mathcal{F}(\eta)=\exp(\eta)
, we have

\displaystyle\Phi(x)=x\log(x)-x+1,\quad x\geq 0,

and, in case of the Fermi-Dirac integral of order
-1
, i.e.,
\mathcal{F}(\eta)=(\exp(-\eta)+1)^{-1}
, we have

\displaystyle\Phi(x)=x\log(x)+(1-x)\log(1-x)+\log(2),\quad x\geq 0.

With this, we can formulate a
total relative entropy

\mathbb{E}
with respect to the boundary values
\psi^{D},\varphi^{D}\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbf{\Omega})
.
The total relative entropy is adapted from
[
76
]
.
For both types of boundary conditions, Schottky (
2.15
) and ohmic (
2.16
), we define for
t\geq 0

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}(t)=&\;\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\int_{\mathbf{\Omega}}|\nabla(\psi-\hat{\psi}^{D})|^{2}\,d\mathbf{x}~+\int_{\mathbf{\Omega}}\Phi_{\text{a}}(n_{\text{a}})\,d\mathbf{x}\\
~&+\delta_{\text{n}}\int_{\mathbf{\Omega}}H_{\text{n}}(n_{\text{n}},n_{\text{n}}^{D})\,d\mathbf{x}~+\delta_{\text{n}}\delta_{\text{p}}\int_{\mathbf{\Omega}}H_{\text{p}}(n_{\text{p}},n_{\text{p}}^{D})\,d\mathbf{x},\end{split}

(2.19)

where we set
\hat{\psi}^{D}=\psi^{D}+\overline{V}
for the Schottky and
\hat{\psi}^{D}=\psi^{D}
for the ohmic boundary conditions.
The densities
n_{\text{n}}^{D}
,
n_{\text{p}}^{D}
can be calculated by inserting
\varphi^{D},\psi^{D}
into the dimensionless state equation (
2.13f
).
Since we assume
\Phi_{\text{a}}\geq 0
the second term is non-negative as well which implies that the total entropy is non-negative.

For each boundary condition, we define the dissipation functionals separately.
The associated dissipation
\mathbb{D}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{SC}}}
for the Schottky boundary conditions
(SC)
(
2.15
) reads

\begin{split}\mathbb{D}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{SC}}}(t)=&\frac{\delta_{\text{n}}}{2\nu}\int_{\mathbf{\Omega}}n_{\text{n}}|\nabla\varphi_{\text{n}}|^{2}\,d\mathbf{x}~+\frac{\delta_{\text{n}}\delta_{\text{p}}}{2\nu}\int_{\mathbf{\Omega}}n_{\text{p}}|\nabla\varphi_{\text{p}}|^{2}\,d\mathbf{x}\\
~&+\frac{z_{\text{a}}^{2}}{2}\int_{\mathbf{\Omega}}n_{\text{a}}|\nabla\varphi_{\text{a}}|^{2}\,d\mathbf{x}\\
~&+\frac{\delta_{\text{n}}}{\nu}\int_{\mathbf{\Gamma}^{C}}v_{\text{n}}\bigl(\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\text{n}}(n_{\text{n}})-\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\text{n}}(n_{\text{n}}^{D})\bigr)\left(n_{\text{n}}-n_{\text{n}}^{D}\right)\,d\gamma\\
~&+\frac{\delta_{\text{n}}\delta_{\text{p}}}{\nu}\int_{\mathbf{\Gamma}^{C}}v_{\text{p}}\bigl(\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\text{p}}(n_{\text{p}})-\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\text{p}}(n_{\text{p}}^{D})\bigr)\left(n_{\text{p}}-n_{\text{p}}^{D}\right)\,d\gamma.\end{split}

(2.20)

The boundary integrals in the last two terms are non-negative due to the monotonicity of the inverse of statistics function
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{-1}
.
Hence, the overall dissipation for the Schottky contact boundary model is non-negative as well.
For ohmic boundary conditions
(OC)
(
2.16
), the non-negative dissipation for
t\geq 0
is defined as

\begin{split}\mathbb{D}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{OC}}}(t)=&\frac{\delta_{\text{n}}}{2\nu}\int_{\mathbf{\Omega}}n_{\text{n}}|\nabla\varphi_{\text{n}}|^{2}\,d\mathbf{x}~+\frac{\delta_{\text{n}}\delta_{\text{p}}}{2\nu}\int_{\mathbf{\Omega}}n_{\text{p}}|\nabla\varphi_{\text{p}}|^{2}\,d\mathbf{x}\\
~&+\frac{z_{\text{a}}^{2}}{2}\int_{\mathbf{\Omega}}n_{\text{a}}|\nabla\varphi_{\text{a}}|^{2}\,d\mathbf{x}.\end{split}

(2.21)

With the entropy and dissipation formulations, we can establish a continuous entropy-dissipation inequality, a crucial
a priori
estimate to investigate the charge transport model.
In particular, a discrete variant of this theorem helps us to prove the existence of discrete solutions.

Theorem 2.1
.

(Continuous entropy-dissipation inequality)
Consider a smooth solution to the model (
2.13
), with initial conditions (
2.7
) and boundary conditions (
2.14
) and (
2.15
) (or (
2.16
)).
Then, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there is a constant
c_{\varepsilon,\mathbf{\Omega}}>0
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\mathbb{E}(t)+\mathbb{D}(t)\leq c_{\varepsilon,\mathbf{\Omega}}+\varepsilon\mathbb{E}(t),\quad t\geq 0,

(2.22)

where the entropy is defined in (
2.19
) and the dissipation
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{D}_{\text{SC}}
in (
2.20
) or
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{D}_{\text{OC}}
in
(
2.21
).
The constant
c_{\varepsilon,\mathbf{\Omega}}
depends only on
\varepsilon
, the measure of
\mathbf{\Omega}
, the boundary data via the norms
\|\varphi^{D}\|_{W^{1,\infty}}
and
\|\psi^{D}\|_{W^{1,\infty}}
,
z_{\text{a}}^{2}
, and the dimensionless parameters
\delta_{\text{n}}
,
\delta_{\text{p}}
and
\nu
.
Under the assumption of constant boundary data, i.e.,
\nabla\varphi^{D}=\nabla\psi^{D}=\mathbf{0}
, the right-hand side of the inequality vanishes.

\square

The proof follows similarly to the proof of
[
76
, Theorem 3.3]
and is therefore omitted here.

\sigma

0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

(3.3)

Due to small parameters, such as the rescaled Debye length
\lambda
, the stiffness in drift-diffusion models favors fully implicit time discretization methods.
Among these, the implicit Euler method offers robust discretization and preserves asymptotic properties
[
82
,
83
]
.
Consequently, it is the method of choice in this work.
For the finite volume discretization of the model, we proceed as follows:
We integrate the bulk equations (
2.13
) over each control volume
K\in\mathcal{T}
, apply the divergence theorem to the current densities, and break down the surface integrals into a sum of integrals over the faces
\sigma\in\mathcal{E}_{K}
.
This yields for
m\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\nu z_{\text{n}}m_{K}\frac{n_{\text{n},K}^{m}-n_{\text{n},K}^{m-1}}{\tau^{m}}+\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{E}_{K}}J_{\text{n},K,\sigma}^{m}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle K\in\mathcal{T},

(3.4a)

\displaystyle~\nu z_{\text{p}}m_{K}\frac{n_{\text{p},K}^{m}-n_{\text{p},K}^{m-1}}{\tau^{m}}+\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{E}_{K}}J_{\text{p},K,\sigma}^{m}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle K\in\mathcal{T},

(3.4b)

\displaystyle~z_{\text{a}}m_{K}\frac{n_{\text{a},K}^{m}-n_{\text{a},K}^{m-1}}{\tau^{m}}+\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{E}_{K}}J_{\text{a},K,\sigma}^{m}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle K\in\mathcal{T},

(3.4c)

which are coupled to the discrete Poisson equation which reads for
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
K\in\mathcal{T}

\begin{split}-\lambda^{2}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{E}_{K}}\tau_{\sigma}D_{K,\sigma}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{m}~=&~\delta_{\text{n}}m_{K}\Bigl(z_{\text{n}}n_{\text{n},K}^{m}+\delta_{\text{p}}(z_{\text{p}}n_{\text{p},K}^{m}+z_{C}C_{K})\Bigr)\\
&+m_{K}z_{\text{a}}n_{\text{a},K}^{m}.\end{split}

(3.4d)

We assume for
\alpha\in\{\text{n},\text{p},\text{a}\}
that the discrete current densities
J_{\alpha,K,\sigma}^{m}
are locally conservative and consistent approximations of
\int_{\sigma}\,\mathbf{j}_{\alpha}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}_{K,\sigma}\,dS
,
where
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{K,\sigma}
is the outward-pointing unit normal to the control volume
K
on the face
\sigma
.
We mean with locally conservative that for
\sigma=K|L
the flux approximation shall satisfy

J_{\alpha,K,\sigma}^{m}+J_{\alpha,L,\sigma}^{m}=0,\quad\alpha\in\{\text{n},\text{p},\text{a}\}.

(3.5)

The finite difference operator
D_{K,\sigma}
, defined in (
3.3
), is also locally conservative.
Choosing the discrete current densities
J_{\alpha,K,\sigma}^{m}
is delicate, as an incorrect choice can cause instability or violate thermodynamic principles.
In the following, we use the excess chemical potential approximation
[
84
]
as two-point flux approximation (TPFA) scheme for
J_{\alpha,K,\sigma}^{m}
which was numerically analyzed in
[
70
,
85
,
76
]
and compared in
[
71
,
86
]
.
This flux discretization scheme reads for
\alpha\in\{\text{n},\text{p},\text{a}\}

J_{\alpha,K,\sigma}^{m}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\!\!\!-z_{\alpha}\tau_{\sigma}\Bigl(B\left(-Q_{\alpha,K,\sigma}^{m}\right)n_{\alpha,L}^{m}-B\left(Q_{\alpha,K,\sigma}^{m}\right)n_{\alpha,K}^{m}\Bigr),&\text{if }\sigma=K|L,\\[6.02773pt]
~\!\!\!\!\!-z_{\alpha}\tau_{\sigma}\Bigl(B\left(-Q_{\alpha,K,\sigma}^{m}\right)n_{\alpha,\sigma}^{m}-B\left(Q_{\alpha,K,\sigma}^{m}\right)n_{\alpha,K}^{m}\Bigr),&\text{if }\sigma\in\mathcal{E}^{C},\alpha\neq\text{a},\\[6.02773pt]
~\!\!\!0,&\text{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

(3.6a)

where the quantity
Q_{\alpha,K,\sigma}^{m}
is defined as

Q_{\alpha,K,\sigma}^{m}=D_{K,\sigma}\left(z_{\alpha}{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{\alpha}^{m}-\log{\boldsymbol{n}}_{\alpha}^{m}\right).

(3.6b)

In the previous formula, the logarithm is applied componentwise.
Moreover, the function
B
denotes the Bernoulli function

B(x)=\frac{x}{\exp(x)-1},\;\text{for}\;x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\quad\text{and}\quad B(0)=1.

(3.7)

We now address the discretization of the boundary conditions.
Notably, the no-flux boundary conditions for the rest of the domain boundary have already been implicitly incorporated through the last conditions in (
3.3
) and (
3.6a
).
For the ionic defects, the no-flux boundary condition (
2.14b
) and the thermodynamic consistency of the flux discretization scheme (
3.6
), allows the ionic defect boundary unknown to be expressed as

\displaystyle\varphi_{\text{a},\sigma}^{m}:=\varphi_{\text{a},K}^{m},\quad\text{for all }\sigma\in\mathcal{E}^{C}{\text{ s.t. }\sigma\subset\overline{K}}.

(3.8)

Note that the previous formula is not necessary to define the scheme as the no-flux boundary conditions are implicitly contained in the third condition in (
3.6a
).
In case of ohmic boundary conditions (
2.16
), the boundary unknowns are set to the given Dirichlet functions

\varphi_{\text{n},\sigma}^{m}=\varphi_{\text{p},\sigma}^{m}:=\varphi_{\sigma}^{D},\quad\psi_{\sigma}^{m}:=\psi_{\sigma}^{D},\quad\text{for all }\sigma\in\mathcal{E}^{C},

(3.9)

where the discrete boundary values
\varphi_{\sigma}^{D}
,
\psi_{\sigma}^{D}
are defined in (
3.1b
).
For Schottky contacts (
2.15
), we analogously define the discrete electric potential unknown at the boundary as

\psi_{\sigma}^{m}:=\tilde{\psi}_{\sigma}^{D}=\psi_{\sigma}^{D}+\overline{V},\quad\text{for all }\sigma\in\mathcal{E}^{C}.

(3.10)

Furthermore, for Schottky contacts, the discrete approximation of
\int_{\sigma}\,\mathbf{j}_{\alpha}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}_{K,\sigma}\,dS
for
\sigma\in\mathcal{E}^{C}
and
\alpha\in\{\text{n},\text{p}\}
is given by

J_{\alpha,K,\sigma}^{m}=z_{\alpha}v_{\alpha}m_{\sigma}(n_{\alpha,\sigma}^{m}-n_{\alpha,\sigma}^{D}),\quad\text{for all }\sigma\in\mathcal{E}^{C},

(3.11)

where we incorporate the condition (
2.15b
).

Higher-order methods in space have been investigated in related contexts, for example, in
[
87
,
88
]
, where hybrid finite volume methods were applied to simpler drift-diffusion type models.

3.1.3
Elimination of boundary values

Rather than solving for the unknowns at the cell centers
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\text{n}}^{m}
,
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\text{p}}^{m}
,
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\text{a}}^{m}
,
\boldsymbol{\psi}^{m}
and on the boundary faces
(\varphi_{\text{n},\sigma}^{m})_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}^{C}}
,
(\varphi_{\text{p},\sigma}^{m})_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}^{C}}
,
(\varphi_{\text{a},\sigma}^{m})_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}^{C}}
,
(\psi_{\sigma}^{m})_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}^{C}}
, the boundary unknowns can be expressed in terms of neighboring cell unknowns.
The expression (
3.8
), (
3.9
) and (
3.10
) allow to eliminate the unknowns trivially.
In the case of Schottky contacts, the boundary densities
(n_{\text{n},\sigma}^{m})_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}^{C}}
and
(n_{\text{p},\sigma}^{m})_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}^{C}}
are characterized implicitly not only by the discrete flux expression (
3.6a
) but also by the discrete Schottky boundary condition (
3.11
).
Setting both expressions equal, yields for all
\sigma\in\mathcal{E}^{C}
and
\alpha\in\{\text{n},\text{p}\}

-z_{\alpha}\tau_{\sigma}\Bigl(B\left(-Q_{\alpha,K,\sigma}^{m}\right)n_{\alpha,\sigma}^{m}-B\left(Q_{\alpha,K,\sigma}^{m}\right)n_{\alpha,K}^{m}\Bigr)=z_{\alpha}v_{\alpha}m_{\sigma}(n_{\alpha,\sigma}^{m}-n_{\alpha,\sigma}^{D}).

(3.12)

In the next lemma we show that this equation has a unique solution which defines the remaining boundary unknowns.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\alpha\in\{\text{n},\text{p}\}
.
Moreover, let
K\in\mathcal{T}
and
\sigma\in\mathcal{E}^{C}
, where
\mathcal{E}^{C}\cap\overline{K}\neq\emptyset
.
We define

Q_{\alpha,K,\sigma}^{m}(s)=z_{\alpha}D_{K,\sigma}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{m}+\left(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{-1}(s)-\log(s)\right)-\left(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{-1}(n_{\alpha,K}^{m})-\log(n_{\alpha,K}^{m})\right).

The function

\begin{split}Z_{\alpha,K}^{m}(s)=&-\tau_{\sigma}\Bigl(B\bigl(-Q_{\alpha,K,\sigma}^{m}(s)\bigr)s-B\bigl(Q_{\alpha,K,\sigma}^{m}(s)\bigr)n_{\alpha,K}^{m}\Bigr)\\
&-v_{\alpha}m_{\sigma}s+v_{\alpha}m_{\sigma}n_{\alpha,\sigma}^{D}\end{split}

has a unique and positive zero that we denote by
n_{\alpha,\sigma}^{m}
.

Proof.

The claim is established by demonstrating the following:
i) As
s\rightarrow+\infty
, we have
Z_{\alpha,K}^{m}(s)\rightarrow-\infty

ii)
Z_{\alpha,K}^{m}
approaches a non-negative limit as
s\rightarrow 0

iii)
Z_{\alpha,K}^{m}
is strictly decreasing.
First, we prove i).
Due to a non-dimensionalized version of the second inequality in (
2.4
), we can estimate

Q_{\alpha,K,\sigma}^{m}\geq z_{\alpha}D_{K,\sigma}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{m}+\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{-1}(n_{\alpha,K}^{m})-\log(n_{\alpha,K}^{m}).

Thus, there exist constants
\underline{B}_{-},\overline{B}_{+}>0
independent of
s
such that

\displaystyle B\bigl(-Q_{\alpha,K,\sigma}^{m}(s)\bigr)\geq\underline{B}_{-},\quad B\bigl(Q_{\alpha,K,\sigma}^{m}(s)\bigr)\leq\overline{B}_{+},\quad\text{for all }s\geq 0,

since
x\mapsto B(-x)
is non-negative and increasing and the Bernoulli function
B
is non-negative and decreasing.
It follows that

\displaystyle Z_{\alpha,K}^{m}(s)\leq-\tau_{\sigma}\underline{B}_{-}s-v_{\alpha}m_{\sigma}s+\tau_{\sigma}\overline{B}_{+}n_{\alpha,K}^{m}+v_{\alpha}m_{\sigma}n_{\alpha,\sigma}^{D},

which tends to
-\infty
as
s\rightarrow\infty
.
For ii), we know that for
s\in(0,1]
, the function
s\mapsto\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{-1}(s)-\log(s)
is bounded from above and below via
0\leq\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{-1}(s)-\log(s)\leq\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{-1}(1)
since
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{-1}
is monotonically increasing.
Hence, there also exist constants
\overline{B}_{-},\underline{B}_{+}>0
independent of
s
such that

\displaystyle B\bigl(-Q_{\alpha,K,\sigma}^{m}(s)\bigr)\leq\overline{B}_{-},\quad B\bigl(Q_{\alpha,K,\sigma}^{m}(s)\bigr)\geq\underline{B}_{+},\quad\text{for all }s\in(0,1].

Consequently, we have

\displaystyle\liminf_{s\rightarrow 0}Z_{\alpha,K}^{m}(s)\geq\tau_{\sigma}\underline{B}_{+}n_{\alpha,K}^{m}+v_{\alpha}m_{\sigma}n_{\alpha,\sigma}^{D}>0.

Lastly, to prove iii), we calculate the derivative of
Z_{\alpha,K}^{m}
which is

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}Z_{\alpha,K}^{m}}{\text{d}s}=

\displaystyle-\tau_{\sigma}B\bigl(-Q_{\alpha,K,\sigma}^{m}(s)\bigr)+\tau_{\sigma}\frac{\text{d}Q_{\alpha,K,\sigma}^{m}}{\text{d}s}B^{\prime}\bigl(-Q_{\alpha,K,\sigma}^{m}(s)\bigr)s

\displaystyle+\tau_{\sigma}\frac{\text{d}Q_{\alpha,K,\sigma}^{m}}{\text{d}s}B^{\prime}\bigl(Q_{\alpha,K,\sigma}^{m}(s)\bigr)n_{\alpha,K}^{m}~-v_{\alpha}m_{\sigma}.

Due to a non-dimensionalized version of the first inequality in (
2.4
), we have

\text{d}Q_{\alpha,K,\sigma}^{m}/\text{d}s=(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{-1})^{\prime}(s)-1/s\geq 0
.
Moreover,
B\geq 0
,
B^{\prime}\leq 0
,
s\geq 0
,
v_{\alpha}m_{\sigma}>0
, and, hence,
\text{d}Z_{\alpha,K}^{m}/\text{d}s<0
which proves that the derivative of
Z_{\alpha,K}^{m}
is strictly decreasing.
∎

The equations (
3.8
), (
3.9
), (
3.10
), and
Lemma

3.1
ensure that all boundary unknowns
(\varphi_{\text{n},\sigma}^{m})_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}^{C}}
,
(\varphi_{\text{p},\sigma}^{m})_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}^{C}}
,
(\varphi_{\text{a},\sigma}^{m})_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}^{C}}
,
(\psi_{\sigma}^{m})_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}^{C}}
can be uniquely expressed as functions of the cell center unknowns for both contact boundary models.
As a result, these boundary unknowns can be eliminated from the system.
Thus, the discretization scheme (
3.4
) and (
3.6
) reduces to a nonlinear system of equations at each time step, where the unknowns are the quasi Fermi potentials and the electrostatic potential evaluated exclusively at the cell centers.

3.2
Existence of a discrete solution

A crucial step in proving the existence of a discrete solution is establishing the entropy-dissipation inequality, which depends on the discrete entropy
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}}^{m}
and dissipation
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{T}}^{m}
.
Both are discrete analogues to the continuous counterparts introduced in
Section

2.2
.
Following the formulation in (
2.19
), the non-negative discrete entropy for
m\in\mathbb{N}
is defined as

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}}^{m}=\,&\,\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{E}}\tau_{\sigma}\left(D_{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{\psi}^{m}-\boldsymbol{\hat{\psi}}^{D})\right)^{2}~+\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}m_{K}\Phi_{\text{a}}(n_{\text{a},K}^{m})\\
~&+\delta_{\text{n}}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}m_{K}H_{\text{n}}(n_{\text{n},K}^{m},n_{\text{n},K}^{D})~+\delta_{\text{n}}\delta_{\text{p}}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}m_{K}H_{\text{p}}(n_{\text{p},K}^{m},n_{\text{p},K}^{D}),\end{split}

(3.13)

where
\boldsymbol{\hat{\psi}}^{D}=\boldsymbol{\psi}^{D}+\overline{\mathbf{V}}
for the Schottky boundary model and
\boldsymbol{\hat{\psi}}^{D}=\boldsymbol{\psi}^{D}
for the ohmic boundary conditions.
Furthermore, we have
\Phi_{\text{a}}^{\prime}=\mathcal{F}_{\text{a}}^{-1}
(see (
2.17
)) and
H_{\alpha}(x,y)=\Phi_{\alpha}(x)-\Phi_{\alpha}(y)-\Phi_{\alpha}^{\prime}(y)(x-y)
for
\alpha\in\{\text{n},\text{p}\}
(see (
2.18
)).
The non-negative dissipation rate for
m\in\mathbb{N}
for the Schottky boundary model is defined similarly to (
2.20
) for
m\in\mathbb{N}

\begin{split}\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{T},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{SC}}}^{m}=&\;\frac{\delta_{\text{n}}}{2\nu}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{E}}\tau_{\sigma}\overline{n}^{m}_{\text{n},\sigma}(D_{\sigma}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\text{n}}^{m})^{2}~+\frac{\delta_{\text{n}}\delta_{\text{p}}}{2\nu}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{E}}\tau_{\sigma}\overline{n}^{m}_{\text{p},\sigma}(D_{\sigma}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\text{p}}^{m})^{2}\\
~&+\frac{z_{\text{a}}^{2}}{2}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{E}}\tau_{\sigma}\overline{n}^{m}_{\text{a},\sigma}(D_{\sigma}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\text{a}}^{m})^{2}\\
~&+\frac{\delta_{\text{n}}}{\nu}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{E}^{C}}v_{\text{n}}m_{\sigma}\bigl(\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\text{n}}(n_{\text{n},\sigma}^{m})-\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\text{n}}(n_{\text{n},\sigma}^{D})\bigr)\left(n_{\text{n},\sigma}^{m}-n_{\text{n},\sigma}^{D}\right)\\
~&+\frac{\delta_{\text{n}}\delta_{\text{p}}}{\nu}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{E}^{C}}v_{\text{p}}m_{\sigma}\bigl(\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\text{p}}(n_{\text{p},\sigma}^{m})-\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\text{p}}(n_{\text{p},\sigma}^{D})\bigr)\left(n_{\text{p},\sigma}^{m}-n_{\text{p},\sigma}^{D}\right),\end{split}

(3.14)

and for the Dirichlet boundary model we have the discrete counterpart of (
2.21
) for
m\in\mathbb{N}

\begin{split}\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{T},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{OC}}}^{m}=&\;\frac{\delta_{\text{n}}}{2\nu}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{E}}\tau_{\sigma}\overline{n}^{m}_{\text{n},\sigma}(D_{\sigma}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\text{n}}^{m})^{2}~+\frac{\delta_{\text{n}}\delta_{\text{p}}}{2\nu}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{E}}\tau_{\sigma}\overline{n}^{m}_{\text{p},\sigma}(D_{\sigma}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\text{p}}^{m})^{2}\\
~&+\frac{z_{\text{a}}^{2}}{2}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{E}}\tau_{\sigma}\overline{n}^{m}_{\text{a},\sigma}(D_{\sigma}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\text{a}}^{m})^{2}.\end{split}

(3.15)

In particular, the contact boundary terms over
\sigma\in\mathcal{E}^{C}
in (
3.14
) are non-negative due to the monotonicity of the inverse of statistics function.
The entropy-dissipation inequality reads as follows and can be proven similarly to
[
76
, Theorem 4.4]
.

Theorem 3.2
.

(Discrete entropy-dissipation inequality)
For any solution to the finite volume scheme (
3.4
), (
3.6
) one has the following entropy-dissipation inequality: For any
\varepsilon>0
, there is a constant
c_{\varepsilon,\mathbf{\Omega}}>0
such that for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
, one has

\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}}^{m}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}}^{m-1}}{\tau^{m}}+\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{T}}^{m}\leq c_{\varepsilon,\mathbf{\Omega}}+\varepsilon\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}}^{m},

(3.16)

where the entropy
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}}^{m}
is defined in (
3.13
) and the dissipation
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{T}}^{m}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{T},\text{SC}}^{m}}
in (
3.14
) (or
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{T}}^{m}=\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{T},\text{OC}}^{m}
in
(
3.15
)).
The constant
c_{\varepsilon,\mathbf{\Omega}}
depends solely on
\varepsilon
, the measure of
\mathbf{\Omega}
, the mesh regularity, the boundary data via the norms
\|\varphi^{D}\|_{W^{1,\infty}}
and
\|\psi^{D}\|_{W^{1,\infty}}
, as well as on
z_{\text{a}}^{2}
and the dimensionless parameters
\delta_{\text{n}}
,
\delta_{\text{p}}
and
\nu
.
If
\nabla\varphi^{D}=\nabla\psi^{D}=\mathbf{0}
, then the right-hand side of (
3.16
) vanishes.

\square

Theorem

3.4
, establishes the existence of discrete solutions.
The proof is divided into lemmas, each using different arguments, as shown in

Most of the necessary auxiliary results are provided in

.
However, to prove for the Schottky contact boundary model the
a priori estimates
on the electron and hole quasi Fermi potentials in

we are in need of another lemma which guarantees the boundedness of the boundary potentials
\varphi_{\text{n},\sigma}^{m}
,
\varphi_{\text{p},\sigma}^{m}

Particularly in the proof of
Lemma

A.2
, we set
a=z_{\alpha}(\varphi_{\alpha,\sigma}^{m}-\psi_{\sigma}^{m})
and
b=z_{\alpha}(\varphi_{\alpha,\sigma}^{D}-\psi_{\sigma}^{D})

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\alpha\in\{\text{n},\text{p}\}
and
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
.
Assume that there exists
\underline{b},\overline{b}\in\mathbb{R}
and
M\geq 0
, such that

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\underline{b}\leq b\leq\overline{b},\\[5.0pt]
&(a-b)\left(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(a)-\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(b)\right)\leq M.\end{aligned}\right.

Then,

\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\alpha}\Bigl(\bigl(-\frac{\sqrt{M}}{2}+\sqrt{\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(\underline{b})}\bigr)^{2}\Bigr)\leq a\leq\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\alpha}\Bigl(\bigl(\frac{\sqrt{M}}{2}+\sqrt{\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(\overline{b})}\bigr)^{2}\Bigr).

Proof.

We start by estimating

\displaystyle M\geq(a-b)\left(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(a)-\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(b)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{a}^{b}1\,ds\right)\left(\int_{a}^{b}\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(s)\,ds\right)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\int_{a}^{b}\sqrt{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(s)}\,ds\right)^{2},

which is due to the Hölder inequality.
The next estimate follows from the assumption (
H1
)

\displaystyle\left(\int_{a}^{b}\sqrt{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(s)}\,ds\right)^{2}=4\left(\int_{a}^{b}\frac{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(s)}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(s)}}\,ds\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq 4\left(\int_{a}^{b}\frac{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(s)}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(s)}}\,ds\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=4\left(\sqrt{\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(b)}-\sqrt{\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(a)}\right)^{2},

where the last equality can be proven by a substitution
u=\sqrt{\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(s)}
.
By combining both estimates, we receive

\displaystyle M\geq 4\left(\sqrt{\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(b)}-\sqrt{\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(a)}\right)^{2},

which proves the claim after rearranging terms and using
\underline{b}\leq b\leq\overline{b}
.
∎

:

3.4

Theorem 3.4
.

(Existence of discrete solution)
For all time steps
m\geq 1
, the implicit-in-time finite volume scheme (
3.4
), (
3.6
) for the TMDC-based memristor charge transport model (
2.13
) supplied with initial (
2.7
), and boundary conditions (
2.14
) and (
2.15
) (or (
2.16
)) has at least one solution

(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\text{n}}^{m},\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\text{p}}^{m},\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\text{a}}^{m},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{\theta}

with
\theta=4\textrm{Card}(\mathcal{T})
.
Moreover, this solution satisfies the following
L^{\infty}
bounds.
There exists
M_{B}>0
such that

\displaystyle-M_{B}\leq\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\text{n}}^{m},\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\text{p}}^{m},\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\text{a}}^{m},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{m}\leq M_{B},\quad\text{for all}\;m\geq 1,

holds componentwise.
The constant
M_{B}
depends on the data (including the non-dimensionalized parameters and the Dirichlet functions) as well as on the temporal and spatial mesh.

Proof.

The proof approach follows the methodology outlined in
[
76
, Theorem 5.5]
, adapted to our setting.
Assuming the existence of a solution at time step
m-1
, we aim to prove the existence of a solution at time step
m
.
To achieve this, we define the vector of unknowns
\mathbf{X}
, stated in (
A.1
).
Using
Lemma

A.4
and

3.1
, the discrete electric potential and the boundary quasi Fermi potentials for electrons and holes are expressed uniquely in terms of
the quasi Fermi potentials of electrons, holes and vacancies
.
By that, we define the vector of unknowns
\mathbf{X}

), which is expressed in terms of quasi Fermi potentials.

Next, we introduce a continuous vector field

with

such that
\mathbf{P}_{m}(\mathbf{X})=\mathbf{0}
is equivalent to the continuity equations (
3.4a
)–(
3.4c
).
That is,
\mathbf{X}
solves the continuity equations if and only if

.

To prove the existence of such
\mathbf{X}
, we first consider
a regularized version of
\mathbf{P}_{m}

and apply

Lemma

A.5
.
Uniform boundedness in the regularization parameter is then shown using

A.1
,
Lemma

A.2
, and
Lemma

A.3
.
For the Schottky boundary model, the proof of

A.2

3.3
.
Finally, by passing to the limit with respect to the regularization parameter, we demonstrate the existence of solutions for the original vector field

Remark 3.5
.

(Time-dependent boundary conditions)
Let us informally comment on the extension of our results to time-dependent applied voltages
\overline{V}(\mathbf{x},t)
.
Define
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}}^{m,n}
as the entropy (
3.13
) at time
t^{m}
taken with respect to the boundary data at time
t^{n}
.
Then, with the same computations as above, the entropy-dissipation inequality becomes

\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}}^{m,m}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}}^{m-1,m}}{\tau^{m}}+\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{T}}^{m,m}\leq c_{\varepsilon,\mathbf{\Omega}}+\varepsilon\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}}^{m,m}.

This inequality is sufficient to extend Theorem
3.4
by the same proof, as it yields a control of
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}}^{m,m}
and
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{T}}^{m,m}
. Thus, one can get existence of a discrete solution. However, the stability of this solution is not granted as
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}}^{m,m}
and
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{T}}^{m,m}
may not be uniformly bounded with respect to
m
on finite time intervals. More precisely, in order to obtain

\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}}^{m,m}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}}^{m-1,m-1}}{\tau^{m}}+\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{T}}^{m,m}\leq c_{\varepsilon,\mathbf{\Omega}}+\varepsilon\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}}^{m,m},

one needs additional regularity assumptions on
\overline{V}
, which are needed to control new remainder terms coming from the estimate of
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}}^{m-1,m}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}}^{m-1,m-1}
.

Remark 3.6

(Cell centers on boundary)
.

The hypothesis
d_{\sigma}>0
for all edges
\sigma
can be relaxed to cover a wider class of meshes.
In particular, consider boundary cells with centers located on
\partial\Omega
.
By letting
d_{\sigma}\rightarrow 0
, for
\sigma\subset\partial\Omega
in the scheme (
3.4
), (
3.6
), one obtains a limit scheme.
This limit process could be justified rigorously using the compactness properties provided by the uniform bounds from the entropy-dissipation estimate.
Up to redefining appropriately the boundary data (
3.1
), the boundedness of the entropy implies that
\psi_{K}^{m}-\psi^{D}_{\sigma}\rightarrow 0
as
d_{\sigma}\rightarrow 0
, since
\tau_{\sigma}\rightarrow\infty
. Starting from this, one can repeat the elimination process of Section
3.1.3
, to find that
n_{\alpha,K}^{m}-n_{\alpha,\sigma}^{m}\rightarrow 0
as
d_{\sigma}\rightarrow 0
.
In particular, if
\textbf{x}_{K}\in\mathcal{E}^{C}
, the Schottky boundary flux (
3.11
) simply becomes

J_{\alpha,K,\sigma}^{m}=z_{\alpha}v_{\alpha}m_{\sigma}(n_{\alpha,K}^{m}-n_{\alpha,\sigma}^{D}),\quad\text{for}\;\sigma\in\mathcal{E}^{C}.

Device simulations

In this section, we perform numerical experiments to analyze the different electrode models and configurations for their application in memtransistor and memristive device simulations.
We introduce the simulation parameters and geometry in
Section

4.1
.
Then, in
Section

4.2

The nonlinear discrete system is solved with a damped Newton method, using analytical Jacobians obtained via automatic differentiation in the programming language Julia.
With the previous time step as an initial guess, the solver converges within a few iterations.
Linear systems are handled by the sparse direct solver

5
Conclusion

In this work, we numerically investigated a vacancy-assisted drift-diffusion charge transport model for 2D TMDC-based memristive devices with various electrode models.
First, we developed a novel, physics-preserving, implicit-in-time finite volume scheme for the numerical discretization of the model equations.
We demonstrated the unconditional stability, structure preservation, and existence of discrete solutions in multiple dimensions using the entropy method, thereby ensuring the mathematical robustness of the numerical method.
Building on this, we extended the results from
[
76
]
to incorporate the Schottky boundary model, addressing it for the first time in the mathematical literature.

Next, we applied the model to explore various contact configurations through a series of numerical simulations. The results provide insights into the conditions under which simplified one-dimensional models are adequate approximations, and when a multi-dimensional approach is required to accurately capture the complexity of charge transport in these systems.
For small electrode lengths, regardless of the memristive layer thickness, we observed that 1D simplifications reliably approximate complex multi-dimensional simulations.
Additionally, we found that the mixed and top contact configurations yield similar results across different electrode and memristive layer thicknesses, as most of the current flows through the top contact region in both cases.
Our findings lay a solid foundation for further refining the modeling techniques for TMDC-based memristive devices and memtransistors.
