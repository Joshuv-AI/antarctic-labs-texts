B B -Stirling numbers associated to potential polynomials
José A. Adell, Beáta Bényi

Abstract
. We introduce the B B -Stirling numbers of the first and second kind, which are the coefficients of the potential polynomials when we express them in terms of the monomials and the falling factorials, respectively. These numbers include, as particular cases, the partial and complete Bell polynomials, the degenerate and probabilistic Stirling numbers, and the S S -restricted Stirling numbers, among others. Special attention is devoted to the computation of such numbers. On the one hand, a recursive formula is provided. On the other, we can compute Stirling numbers of one kind in terms of the other, with the help of the classical Stirling numbers.

05A18, 05A19, 60E05.

1.
Introduction

Let
{\mathbb{N}}
be the set of positive integers and
{\mathbb{N}}_{0}={\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
. Throughout this paper, we assume that
j,k,m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
,
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
with
|z|\leq r
, for some
r>0
, where
r
may change from line to line.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
be the set of functions
A(z)
which are analytic at
z=0
and let
{\mathcal{B}}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
be the set of functions
B(z)
such that
B(0)=1
. If
A(z)\in{\mathcal{A}}
and
B(z)\in{\mathcal{B}}
, we consider the sequence of polynomials
(P_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
whose generating function is given by

(1)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{P}_{n}(x)\frac{z^{n}}{n!}=A(z)\left(B(z)\right)^{x}.

If
A(0)\not=0
and

\displaystyle B(z)=\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}H_{i}\frac{z^{i}}{i!}\right),\qquad H_{1}\not=0,

then
(P_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
is called a
Sheffer polynomial sequence
(see Sheffer
[
18
]
, Roman
[
17
]
, Wang and Wang
[
20
]
, and Marcellán et al.
[
16
]
for equivalent definitions).

Given
B(z)\in{\mathcal{B}}
, the sequence
(P_{n}(B;x))_{n\geq 0}
defined by

(2)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P_{n}(B;x)\frac{z^{n}}{n!}=\left(B(z)\right)^{x},

is known in the literature as the sequence of
potential polynomials
(see, for instance, Comtet
[
10
, Sec.3.5]
, Howard
[
12
]
, Cenkci
[
9
]
, and Wang
[
19
]
). If

\displaystyle A(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}A_{n}\frac{z^{n}}{n!},

then we have from (
1
), (
2
), and (
11
) and (
12
) below

\displaystyle{P}_{n}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}A_{k}P_{n-k}(B;x).

For this reason, we will restrict our attention to potential polynomials. To describe such polynomials, we introduce the sequences
(s_{B}(n,k))_{n\geq k}
and
(S_{B}(n,k))_{n\geq k}
respectively defined as

(3)

\displaystyle\frac{(\log B(z))^{k}}{k!}=\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}s_{B}(n,k)\frac{z^{n}}{n!},

and

(4)

\displaystyle\frac{(B(z)-1)^{k}}{k!}=\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}S_{B}(n,k)\frac{z^{n}}{n!}.

We call such numbers the
Stirling numbers of the first and second kind associated to
B(z)
, respectively, or for short, the
B
-Stirling numbers.

In first place, note that
B
-Stirling numbers extend the classical Stirling numbers of the first and second kind, respectively denoted by
s(n,k)
and
S(n,k)
, in the following sense. Suppose that

(5)

\displaystyle I(z)=1+z.

Then, we have from (
3
) and (
4
)

(6)

\displaystyle s_{I}(n,k)=s(n,k)\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad S_{I}(n,k)=\delta_{n,k}.

In this case, the potential polynomials are the falling factorials, that is,

(7)

\displaystyle P_{n}(I;x)=(x)_{n}:=x(x-1)\cdots(x-n+1),\qquad((x)_{0}=1).

Similarly, for

(8)

\displaystyle E(z)=e^{z},

we have

(9)

\displaystyle s_{E}(n,k)=\delta_{n,k}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad S_{E}(n,k)=S(n,k),

as well as

(10)

\displaystyle P_{n}(E;x)=x^{n}.

In second place, it will be shown in
Section

4
that many sequences of known numbers are obtained as particular cases of
B
-Stirling numbers for an appropriate choice of the function
B(z)\in{\mathcal{B}}
. For instance, partial and complete Bell polynomials (cf. Comtet
[
10
]
, Yang
[
21
]
, and Wang and Wang
[
20
]
), the degenerate Stirling numbers of both kinds introduced by Kim and Kim
[
13
,
14
]
, the probabilistic Stirling numbers of both kinds introduced by the authors
[
4
,
1
,
2
]
, and the
S
-restricted Stirling numbers considered by the second author
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
.

The paper is organized as follows. In
Section

2
, we show that potential polynomials are closely connected with
B
-Stirling numbers. Indeed, the
B
-Stirling numbers of the first kind (resp. second kind) are the coefficients of the potential polynomials when expressed in terms of the monomials
x^{k}
(resp. the falling factorials
(x)_{k}
). As a consequence, the
B
-Stirling numbers of the first kind (resp. second kind) are obtained by differentiation (resp. computation of forward differences) at the origin of the potential polynomials. A recursive formula to compute both kinds of such numbers is also provided.

In
Section

3
, we introduce two inner operations in the set
{\mathcal{B}}
and describe the behaviour of the corresponding
B
-Stirling numbers and potential polynomials under these two operations. One important consequence, from a computational point of view, is the following. In dealing with particular examples, one kind of the
B
-Stirling numbers
s_{B}(n,k)
or
S_{B}(n,k)
is easier to compute than the other. Under such circumstances, we directly compute the easiest one, say
s_{B}(n,k)
, and then compute the other, say
S_{B}(n,k)
, in terms of
s_{B}(n,k)
and the classical Stirling numbers.

2.
B
-Stirling numbers

The
B
-Stirling numbers allow us to describe the potential polynomials (and also the Sheffer polynomials) in closed form, as shown in the following result.

Theorem 1
.

Let
B(z)\in{\mathcal{B}}
. Then,

\displaystyle P_{n}(B;x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}s_{B}(n,k)x^{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}S_{B}(n,k)(x)_{k}.

Proof.

By (
2
) and (
3
) we have

\displaystyle(B(z))^{x}

\displaystyle=\exp{(x\log B(z))}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}x^{k}\frac{(\log B(z))^{k}}{k!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}x^{k}\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}s_{B}(n,k)\frac{z^{n}}{n!}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}\sum_{k=0}^{n}s_{B}(n,k)x^{k},

which shows the first identity. On the other hand, suppose that
|B(z)-1|<1
. By (
2
), (
4
), and the binomial expansion, we obtain

\displaystyle(B(z)-1+1)^{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(x)_{k}\frac{(B(z)-1)^{k}}{k!}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(x)_{k}\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}S_{B}(n,k)\frac{z^{n}}{n!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}\sum_{k=0}^{n}S_{B}(n,k)(x)_{k},

thus showing the second identity and completing the proof.
∎

Remark 2
.

Applying
Theorem

1
to
I(z)=1+z
, we get from (
6
) and (
7
) the well known identity

\displaystyle P_{n}(I;x)=(x)_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}s(n,k)x^{k}.

Similarly, for
E(z)=e^{z}
, we have from (
9
) and (
10
)

\displaystyle P_{n}(E;x)=x^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}S(n,k)(x)_{k}.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be the set of complex sequences
\mathbb{U}=(U_{n})_{n\geq 0}
having a finite generating function

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}U_{n}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}.

If
\mathbb{U},\mathbb{V}\in\mathcal{H}
, we define their binomial convolution
\mathbb{U}\times\mathbb{V}=((U\times V)_{n})_{n\geq 0}
as

(11)

\displaystyle(U\times V)_{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{j}U_{j}V_{n-j}.

It is well known (see, for instance,
[
3
]
) that

(12)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(U\times V)_{n}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}U_{n}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}V_{n}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}.

The following result shows that
B
-Stirling numbers can be recursively computed.

Theorem 3
.

Let
B(z)\in{\mathcal{B}}
. For any
n\geq k\geq 1
, we have

(13)

\displaystyle s_{B}(n,k)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=k-1}^{n-1}s_{B}(j,k-1)s_{B}(n-j,1),

and

(14)

\displaystyle S_{B}(n,k)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=k-1}^{n-1}\binom{n}{j}S_{B}(j,k-1)S_{B}(n-j,1).

In particular,

(15)

\displaystyle s_{B}(k,k)=[s_{B}(1,1)]^{k}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad S_{B}(k,k)=\left[S_{B}(1,1)\right]^{k}.

Proof.

Starting form the identity

\displaystyle\frac{(\log B(z))^{k}}{k!}=\frac{1}{k}\frac{(\log B(z))^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\log B(z),

formula (
13
) follows from (
3
), (
11
), and (
12
), since

(16)

\displaystyle s_{B}(n,k)=S_{B}(n,k)=0,\quad n<k.

The proof of (
14
) is similar starting from the identity

\displaystyle\frac{(B(z)-1)^{k}}{k!}=\frac{1}{k}\frac{(B(z)-1)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}(B(z)-1).

Finally, we have from (
13
)

\displaystyle s_{B}(k,k)=s_{B}(k-1,k-1)s_{B}(1,1).

Thus, the first equality in (
15
) follows by induction on
k
. The second one is shown in a similar way.
∎

Recall that the
r
th forward differences of a function
f:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
are recursively defined as

\displaystyle\Delta^{0}f(x)=f(x),\quad\Delta^{1}f(x)=f(x+1)-f(x),\quad\Delta^{r}f(z)=\Delta^{1}(\Delta^{r-1}f)(x),\quad r\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Equivalently,

(17)

\displaystyle\Delta^{r}f(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{r}\binom{r}{j}(-1)^{r-j}f(x+j),\qquad r\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}.

It is well known that if
p_{n}(x)
is a polynomial of degree
n
, then

(18)

\displaystyle\Delta^{r}p_{n}(x)=0,\qquad r>n.

The
B
-Stirling numbers of the first and second kind are related, respectively, to the derivatives and the forward differences of the potential polynomials
P_{n}(B,x)
. In a certain sense,
Theorem

4
is the inverse of
Theorem

1
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
B(z)\in{\mathcal{B}}
and let
r\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
,
0\leq r\leq n
. Then,

(19)

\displaystyle\frac{P_{n}^{(r)}(B;x)}{r!}=\sum_{k=r}^{n}\binom{k}{r}s_{B}(n,k)x^{k-r}=\sum_{j=r}^{n}\binom{n}{j}s_{B}(j,r)P_{n-j}(B;x),

and

(20)

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta^{r}P_{n}(B;x)}{r!}=\sum_{k=r}^{n}\binom{k}{r}S_{B}(n,k)(x)_{k-r}=\sum_{j=r}^{n}\binom{n}{j}S_{B}(j,r)P_{n-j}(B;x).

In particular,

(21)

\displaystyle\frac{P_{n}^{(r)}(B;0)}{r!}=s_{B}(n,r),\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\frac{\Delta^{r}P_{n}(B;0)}{r!}=S_{B}(n,r).

Proof.

The first equality in (
19
) readily follows from
Theorem

1
. By differentiating
r
times with respect to
x
in (
2
) we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n=r}^{\infty}\frac{P_{n}^{(r)}(B;x)}{r!}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}=\frac{(\log B(z))^{r}}{r!}B(z)^{x}.

Hence, the second equality in (
19
) follows from (
11
), (
12
), and (
16
).

On the other hand, it is easily checked by induction on
r
that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r!}\Delta^{r}(x)_{k}=\binom{k}{r}(x)_{k-r},\qquad r=0,1,\ldots,k.

Thus, the first equality in (
20
) follows by applying the operator
{\Delta^{r}}/{r!}
to the second equality in
Theorem

1
and taking into account (
18
). Applying again the operator
{\Delta^{r}}/{r!}
to both sides of (
2
) and recalling (
18
), we get

\displaystyle\sum_{n=r}^{\infty}\frac{\Delta^{r}P_{n}(B;x)}{r!}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}=\frac{(B(z)-1)^{r}}{r!}B(z)^{x}.

Therefore, the second equality in (
20
) follows from (
11
), (
12
), and (
16
). Finally, the two identities in (
21
) follow from (
19
) and (
20
), respectively.
∎

Remark 5
.

As in
Remark

2
, consider
E(z)=e^{z}
. In this case
P_{n}(E;x)=x^{n}
and
S_{E}(n,k)=S(n,k)
, as noted in (

). Hence, (
17
) and the second equality in (
21

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r!}\sum_{j=0}^{r}\binom{r}{j}(-1)^{r-j}j^{n}=S(n,r),\qquad r\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}.

3.
Inner operations in the set
{\mathcal{B}}

Given
B(z),C(z)\in{\mathcal{B}}
, we consider the inner operation in
{\mathcal{B}}
defined as

(22)

\displaystyle B\circ C(z)=B(C(z)-1).

This operation is not commutative in general. The identity element is
I(z)=1+z
, i.e.,

(23)

\displaystyle B\circ I(z)=I\circ B(z)=B(z),\qquad B(z)\in{\mathcal{B}}.

The behaviour of the corresponding Stirling numbers with respect to this operation is described in the following result.

Theorem 6
.

Let
B(z),C(z)\in{\mathcal{B}}
. Then,

(24)

\displaystyle s_{B\circ C}(n,k)=\sum_{j=k}^{n}S_{C}(n,j)s_{B}(j,k),

and

(25)

\displaystyle S_{B\circ C}(n,k)=\sum_{j=k}^{n}S_{C}(n,j)S_{B}(j,k).

As a consequence,

(26)

\displaystyle s_{C}(n,k)=\sum_{j=k}^{n}S_{C}(n,j)s(j,k).

Proof.

By (
3
), (
4
), and (
22
), we see that

\displaystyle\frac{(\log B\circ C(z))^{k}}{k!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}s_{B}(j,k)\frac{(C(z)-1)^{j}}{j!}=\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}s_{B}(j,k)\sum_{n=j}^{\infty}S_{C}(n,j)\frac{z^{n}}{n!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}\sum_{j=k}^{n}S_{C}(n,j)s_{B}(j,k),

thus showing (
24
). Similarly,

\displaystyle\frac{(B\circ C(z)-1)^{k}}{k!}=\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}S_{B}(j,k)\frac{(C(z)-1)^{j}}{j!}=\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}\sum_{j=k}^{n}S_{C}(n,j)S_{B}(j,k),

which shows (
25
). Finally, (
26
) follows by choosing
B=I
in (
24
) and recalling (
6
). The proof is complete.
∎

Remark 7
.

In matrix notation,
Theorem

6
can be simply written as

\displaystyle\mathrm{\textbf{s}}_{B\circ C}=\mathrm{\textbf{S}}_{C}\mathrm{\textbf{s}}_{B},\quad\mathrm{\textbf{S}}_{B\circ C}=\mathrm{\textbf{S}}_{C}\mathrm{\textbf{S}}_{B},\quad\mathrm{\textbf{s}}_{C}=\mathrm{\textbf{S}}_{C}\mathrm{\textbf{s}},

where
s
stands for the matrix
\mathrm{\textbf{s}}=(s(j,k))
,
j,k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
.

Another interesting inner operation in
{\mathcal{B}}
is defined as

(27)

\displaystyle B\diamond C(z)=B(\log C(z)),\qquad B(z),C(z)\in{\mathcal{B}}.

This non-commutative operation has identity element
E(z)=e^{z}
, that is,

\displaystyle B\diamond E(z)=E\diamond B(z)=B(z),\qquad B(z)\in{\mathcal{B}}.

An analogous result to
Theorem

6
for this inner operation is the following.

Theorem 8
.

Let
B(z),C(z)\in{\mathcal{B}}
. Then,

(28)

\displaystyle s_{B\diamond C}(n,k)=\sum_{j=k}^{n}s_{C}(n,j)s_{B}(j,k),

and

(29)

\displaystyle S_{B\diamond C}(n,k)=\sum_{j=k}^{n}s_{C}(n,j)S_{B}(j,k).

As a consequence,

(30)

\displaystyle S_{C}(n,k)=\sum_{j=k}^{n}s_{C}(n,j)S(j,k).

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of
Theorem

6
and therefore we omit it.
∎

Let
B(z)\in{\mathcal{B}}
. Formulas (
26
) and (
30
) are important for computations. In fact, dealing with particular examples, one kind of the
B
-Stirling numbers
s_{B}(n,k)
or
S_{B}(n,k)
is easier to compute than the other. In such a case, the strategy consists of directly computing the easiest one and then use (
26
) or (
30
) to compute the other by using the classical Stirling numbers.

Remark 9
.

Choosing
C=E
in (
26
) and
C=I
in (
30
) and recalling (
6
) and (
9
), we obtain the well known formula

\displaystyle\delta_{nk}=\sum_{j=k}^{n}S(n,j)s(j,k)=\sum_{j=k}^{n}s(n,j)S(j,k).

The behaviour of the potential polynomials under the two operations considered above is specified in the following result.

Theorem 10
.

Let
B(z),C(z)\in{\mathcal{B}}
. Then,

(31)

\displaystyle P_{n}(B\circ C;x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}S_{C}(n,k)P_{k}(B;x),

and

(32)

\displaystyle P_{n}(B\diamond C;x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}s_{C}(n,k)P_{k}(B;x).

Proof.

By (
2
), (
4
), and (
22
), we have

\displaystyle(B\circ C(z))^{x}

\displaystyle=(B(C(z)-1))^{x}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}P_{k}(B;x)\frac{(C(z)-1)^{k}}{k!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}P_{k}(B;x)\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}S_{C}(n,k)\frac{z^{n}}{n!}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}\sum_{k=0}^{n}S_{C}(n,k)P_{k}(B;x),

thus showing (
31
). In a similar way,

\displaystyle(B\diamond C(z))^{x}

\displaystyle=(B(\log C(z)))^{x}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}P_{k}(B;x)\frac{(\log C(z))^{k}}{k!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}P_{k}(B;x)\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}s_{C}(n,k)\frac{z^{n}}{n!}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}\sum_{k=0}^{n}s_{C}(n,k)P_{k}(B;x),

which shows (
32
) and completes the proof.
∎

4.
Examples

To illustrate the results of the previous sections, we consider the following examples.

4.1.
Example 1. Partial and complete Bell polynomials

An important spacial case of the inner operation defined in (
22
) occurs when
B(z)=E(z)=e^{z}
. More precisely, we give the following result.

Theorem 11
.

Let
E\circ C(z)=\exp{(C(z)-1)}
, where
C(z)\in{\mathcal{B}}
. Then,

(33)

\displaystyle s_{E\circ C}(n,k)=S_{C}(n,k),\qquad S_{E\circ C}(n,k)=\sum_{j=k}^{n}S_{C}(n,j)S(j,k).

In addition,

(34)

\displaystyle P_{n}(E\circ C;x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}S_{C}(n,k)x^{k}.

Proof.

The identities in (
33
) follow by choosing
B(z)=E(z)
in (
24
) and (
25
) and taking into account (
9
). Formula (
34
) follows by setting
B(z)=E(z)
in (
31
) and applying (
10
). The proof is complete.
∎

Theorem

11
is a reformulation of the well known partial and complete Bell polynomials. Actually, recall that (see, for instance, Comtet
[
10
]
, or Wang and Wang
[
20
]
) the partial Bell polynomials are defined by

(35)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k!}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}x_{m}\frac{z^{m}}{m!}\right)^{k}=\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}B_{n,k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-k+1})\frac{z^{n}}{n!},

whereas the complete Bell polynomials are given by

(36)

\displaystyle\exp\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}x_{m}\frac{z^{m}}{m!}\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}B_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\frac{z^{n}}{n!}.

Define
C(z)\in{\mathcal{B}}
as

\displaystyle C(z)=1+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}x_{m}\frac{z^{m}}{m!}.

Comparing the first identity in (
33
) with (
35
), we see that

\displaystyle s_{E\circ C}(n,k)=S_{C}(n,k)=B_{n,k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-k+1}).

Similarly, comparing (
34
) with (
36
), we obtain the well known relation

\displaystyle B_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=P_{n}(E\circ C;1)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}S_{C}(n,k)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}B_{n,k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-k+1}).

In other words, the partial Bell polynomials are the
E\circ C
-Stirling numbers of the first kind, whereas the complete Bell polynomials coincide with the potential polynomials associated to
E\circ C
evaluated at
1
.

4.2.
Example 2. Degenerate Stirling numbers

Let
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}
. Denote by

(37)

\displaystyle(x)_{n,\lambda}=x(x-\lambda)\cdots(x-(n-1)\lambda)=\left(\frac{x}{\lambda}\right)_{n}\lambda^{n},\quad n\in{\mathbb{N}},\quad(x)_{0,\lambda}=1.

Recently, Kim and Kim
[
14
]
defined the degenerate Stirling numbers of the first and second kind as

(38)

\displaystyle(x)_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}S_{1,\lambda}(n,k)(x)_{k,\lambda}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad(x)_{n,\lambda}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\lambda}(x)_{k},

respectively, and proved different results for these numbers.

Let us describe such numbers using the tools developed above. In first place, we define the function
B_{\lambda}(z)\in{\mathcal{B}}
as

(39)

\displaystyle B_{\lambda}(z)=(1+\lambda z)^{1/\lambda}.

As noted in
[
14
]
,
B_{\lambda}(z)\rightarrow E(z)=e^{z}
, as
\lambda\rightarrow 0
. In the following result, we compute the
B_{\lambda}
-Stirling numbers and the potential polynomials associated to
B_{\lambda}(z)
.

Theorem 12
.

Let
B_{\lambda}(z)
be as in (
39
). Then,

(40)

\displaystyle s_{B_{\lambda}}(n,k)=\lambda^{n-k}s(n,k),

and

(41)

\displaystyle S_{B_{\lambda}}(n,k)=\sum_{j=k}^{n}\lambda^{n-j}s(n,j)S(j,k)=\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}(-1)^{k-j}(j)_{n,k}.

In addition,

(42)

\displaystyle P_{n}(B_{\lambda};x)=(x)_{n,\lambda}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\lambda^{n-k}s(n,k)x^{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}S_{B_{\lambda}}(n,k)(x)_{k}.

Proof.

From (
39
), we directly compute
s_{B_{\lambda}}(n,k)
as follows

\displaystyle\frac{(\log B_{\lambda}(z))^{k}}{k!}=\frac{1}{\lambda^{k}}\frac{(\log(1+\lambda z))^{k}}{k!}=\frac{1}{\lambda^{k}}\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}s(n,k)\frac{(\lambda z)^{n}}{n!},

which, by (
3
), shows (
40
). The first equality in (
41
) directly follows from (
30
) and (
40
).

On the other hand, we have from (
37
), (
39
), and the binomial expansion

\displaystyle(B_{\lambda}(z))^{x}=(1+\lambda z)^{x/\lambda}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{x}{\lambda}\right)_{n}\lambda^{n}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(x)_{n,\lambda}\frac{z^{n}}{n!},

thus showing the first equality in (
42
). The other two equalities in (
42
) follow from
Theorem

1
and (
40
).
Finally, we get from (
17
), the second identity in (
21
), and the first equality in (
42
)

\displaystyle S_{B_{\lambda}}(n,k)=\frac{1}{k!}\Delta^{k}P_{n}(B_{\lambda};0)=\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}(-1)^{k-j}(j)_{n,k}.

This shows the second equality in (
41
) and completes the proof.
∎

Comparing the second identity in (
38
) with (
42
), we see that

\displaystyle\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\lambda}=S_{B_{\lambda}}(n,k).

In other words, the degenerate Stirling numbers of the second kind coincide with the
B_{\lambda}
-Stirling numbers of the second kind.

Define the function
C_{\lambda}(z)\in{\mathcal{B}}
as

(43)

\displaystyle C_{\lambda}(z)=\exp\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}((1+z)^{\lambda}-1)\right).

Note that
C_{\lambda}(z)\rightarrow I(z)=1+z
, as
\lambda\rightarrow 0
. The
C_{\lambda}
-Stirling numbers, as well as the potential polynomials
P_{n}(C_{\lambda};x)
are not easy to compute in closed form. However, it is easily checked that the functions
B_{\lambda}(z)
and
C_{\lambda}(z)
satisfy the following relations

(44)

\displaystyle C_{\lambda}\circ B_{\lambda}(z)=E(z)=e^{z}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad B_{\lambda}\diamond C_{\lambda}(z)=I(z)=1+z.

This allows us to relate the
C_{\lambda}
-Stirling numbers and the potential polynomials
P_{n}(C_{\lambda};x)
with those associated to
B_{\lambda}(z)
.
We restrict our attention to the numbers
s_{C_{\lambda}}(n,k)
.

Theorem 13
.

Let
B_{\lambda}(z)
and
C_{\lambda}(z)
be as in (
39
) and (
43
), respectively. Then,

(45)

\displaystyle\delta_{n,k}=\sum_{j=k}^{n}S_{B_{\lambda}}(n,j)s_{C_{\lambda}}(j,k),

(46)

\displaystyle s(n,k)=\sum_{j=k}^{n}s_{C_{\lambda}}(n,j)\lambda^{j-k}s(j,k),

and

(47)

\displaystyle(x)_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}s_{C_{\lambda}}(n,k)(x)_{k,\lambda}.

Proof.

Formula (
45
) follows from the first identity in (
44
), by applying (
9
) and (
24
). Formulas (
46
) and (
47
) follow from the second identity in (
44
). Specifically, (
46
) follows from (
6
), (
28
), and (
40
). To show (
47
), we have from (
7
), (
32
), and the first equality in (
42
)

\displaystyle(x)_{n}=P_{n}(I;x)=P_{n}(B_{\lambda}\diamond C_{\lambda};x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}s_{C_{\lambda}}(n,k)P_{k}(B_{\lambda};x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}s_{C_{\lambda}}(n,k)(x)_{k,\lambda}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Comparing (
38
) with (
47
), we see that

\displaystyle S_{1,\lambda}(n,k)=s_{C_{\lambda}}(n,k),

that is, the degenerate Stirling numbers of the first kind coincide with the
C_{\lambda}
-Stirling numbers of the first kind.

4.3.
Example 3. Probabilistic Stirling numbers

Suppose that
B(z)\in{\mathcal{B}}
is given by

(48)

\displaystyle B(z)={\mathbb{E}}e^{zY},

where
{\mathbb{E}}
stands for mathematical expectation and
Y
is a complex-valued random variable having a finite moment generating function in a neighbourhood of the origin, i.e.,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}e^{r|Y|}<\infty,\quad\mbox{for some}\quad 0<r<\infty.

The authors introduced in
[
2
]
(see also
[
4
]
) the probabilistic Stirling numbers of the first and second kind, respectively denoted by
s_{Y}(n,k)
and
S_{Y}(n,k)
, associated to
Y
and defined as

(49)

\displaystyle s_{Y}(n,k)=(-1)^{n-k}s_{B}(n,k)\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad S_{Y}(n,k)=S_{B}(n,k).

Explicit expressions of such numbers for different choices of
Y
, as well as applications in analytic number theory and probability theory can be found in
[
1
,
4
,
5
,
15
]
.

Here, we focus our attention on the probabilistic interpretation of certain formulas given in the previous sections, whenever
B(z)
has the form given in (
48
). To this end, let
(Y_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be a sequence of independent copies of the random variable
Y
and set

(50)

\displaystyle W_{m}=Y_{1}+\cdots+Y_{m},\quad m\in{\mathbb{N}}\qquad(W_{0}=0).

In first place, the values of the potential polynomials
P_{n}(B;x)
at nonnegative integers are in fact the moments of
W_{m}
. Specifically,

(51)

\displaystyle P_{n}(B;m)={\mathbb{E}}W_{m}^{n}.

Actually, it follows from (
48
) and (
50
) that

\displaystyle(B(z))^{m}=\left({\mathbb{E}}e^{zY}\right)^{m}={\mathbb{E}}e^{zW_{m}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{E}}W_{m}^{n}\frac{z^{n}}{n!},

which, by virtue of (
2
), shows (
51
).

In second place,
Theorem

1
, (
49
), and (
51
) give us

\displaystyle P_{n}(B;m)={\mathbb{E}}W_{m}^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}S_{Y}(n,k)(m)_{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}s_{Y}(n,k)m^{k}.

In other words, the moments of
W_{m}
can be computed in terms of the probabilistic Stirling numbers. Viceversa, we have from (
17
), the second identity in (
21
), and (
51
)

\displaystyle S_{Y}(n,r)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r!}\Delta^{r}P_{n}(B;0)=\frac{1}{r!}\sum_{j=0}^{r}\binom{r}{j}(-1)^{r-j}P_{n}(B;j)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r!}\sum_{j=0}^{r}\binom{r}{j}(-1)^{r-j}{\mathbb{E}}W_{j}^{n}.

This means that the probabilistic Stirling numbers of the second kind can be computed in terms of the moments of the random variables
W_{j}
.

4.4.
Example 4.
S
-restricted Stirling numbers

S
-restricted Stirling numbers of the first and second kind
(denoted by
\begin{bmatrix}n\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{S}
and
\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{S}
, respectively) were introduced and studied in
[
6
]
and
[
8
]
. Given a set
S\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
,
\begin{bmatrix}n\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{S}
is the number of permutations of
[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
with
k
cycles such that the size of each cycle is contained in
S
, whereas
\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{S}
gives the number of partitions of the set
[n]
having exactly
k
blocks with the additional restriction that the size of each block is contained in
S
.
If
S=\{k_{1},k_{2},\ldots\}
, the exponential generating functions are given by

\displaystyle\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}\begin{bmatrix}n\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{S}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}=\frac{1}{k!}\left(\sum_{i\geq 1}\frac{z^{k_{i}}}{k_{i}}\right)^{k}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{S}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}=\frac{1}{k!}\left(\sum_{i\geq 1}\frac{z^{k_{i}}}{k_{i}!}\right)^{k}.

Clearly,
S
-restricted Stirling numbers of the second kind are special cases of
B
-Stirling numbers of the second kind with

\displaystyle B^{(S)}(z)=1+\sum_{i\geq 1}\frac{z^{k_{i}}}{k_{i}!}.

For instance, by choosing
B(z)=\cosh z
, we obtain by the
B
-Stirling numbers of the second kind the number of set partitions of
[n]
into
k
non-empty blocks with
even
sizes.

The classes of set partitions with the restriction that a block can have
at most
or
at least
a certain number of elements have drawn the attention of many researchers. The counting sequences, the so called
restricted Stirling numbers
and
associated Stirling numbers
are well studied in the combinatorial literature (see, for instance,
[
6
]
for references).

Let us define the functions

\displaystyle R^{(\leq m)}(z)=1+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{z^{i}}{i!}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad R^{(\geq m)}(z)=1+\sum_{i=m}^{\infty}\frac{z^{i}}{i!}.

Then
R^{(\leq m)}
-Stirling numbers coincide with the restricted Stirling numbers and
R^{(\geq m)}
-Stirling numbers with the associated Stirling numbers (for further details, see
[
6
]
).

Similarly, let us define

\displaystyle P^{(\leq m)}(z)=1+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{z^{i}}{i}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad P^{(\geq m)}(z)=1+\sum_{i=m}^{\infty}\frac{z^{i}}{i}.

Then,
P^{(\leq m)}
-Stirling numbers of the second kind count permutations of
[n]
into
k
cycles such that each cycle is of length
at most

m
and
P^{(\geq m)}
-Stirling numbers those with cycle-length
at least

m
. In particular, derangements (permutations without fix points) are counted by
P^{(\geq 2)}
-Stirling numbers of the second kind, the number of involutions is given by
B
-Stirling numbers of the second kind with
B(z)=1+z+z^{2}/2
and
k
-pairings are enumerated by
B
-Stirling numbers with
B(z)=1+z^{2}/2
. For further details and special cases see
[
8
]
and
[
11
]
.

Setting
B(z)=1/(1-z)
,
B
-Stirling numbers of the second kind coincide with Lah numbers. Lah numbers enumerate partitions of a set with
n
elements into
k
non-empty ordered lists. An introductory study of
S
-restricted Lah numbers, which are defined in the same spirit as the above mentioned
S
-restricted Stirling numbers, is given in
[
7
]
.
