Precision resuscitation beyond neutral trials in cardiac arrest research
Authors: Tae Nyoung Chung
Published: 2026

Cardiac arrest care has advanced in large part through standardization. High-quality chest compressions, early defibrillation, structured post–cardiac arrest care, and multimodal neuroprognostication provide a shared framework for managing a complex, time-sensitive condition. Standardized protocols have improved consistency across resuscitation practice and should remain its foundation.

At the same time, several influential randomized trials across the cardiac arrest care continuum have reported neutral patient-centered outcomes. In the TTM2 trial, hypothermia at 33 °C did not reduce mortality or improve functional outcomes compared with targeted normothermia and early fever treatment [1]. In the BOX trial, neither a higher mean arterial pressure target nor a restrictive oxygen target improved clinical outcomes [2,3]. In the TELSTAR trial, rhythmic and periodic electroencephalographic activity was suppressed, but neurological outcomes were unchanged [4]. More recently, the AUGMENT-VA trial showed that manual pressure augmentation during defibrillation reduced transthoracic impedance but did not improve survival [5]. Although these trials examined different phases of resuscitation and different therapeutic targets, none demonstrated superiority in a broad trial population.

These findings should be regarded as applicable to the populations studied. Revisiting these trials is not an attempt to recast neutral studies as positive on the basis of exploratory subgroup findings. Rather, the repeated pattern of neutral results raises a different question: should future trials continue to apply the same intervention or fixed target to clinically heterogeneous populations, or should trial designs account more directly for the biological heterogeneity of cardiac arrest?

Cardiac arrest is defined by a shared final event, but it is not a single disease. Etiology, no-flow and low-flow duration, initial rhythm, prearrest physiological reserve, reperfusion injury, and extracranial organ dysfunction vary substantially across patients. Post–cardiac arrest brain injury is similarly heterogeneous; its severity and dominant mechanisms may differ according to cerebral perfusion, oxygen delivery, selective anatomical vulnerability, inflammation, cerebral edema, and electroencephalographic evolution. Traditional variables, including witnessed status, arrest location, and initial rhythm, remain clinically important, but they may not adequately capture the biological state that determines responsiveness to a specific treatment [6].

Most conventional randomized trials estimate an average treatment effect. This estimate is useful for determining whether an intervention should be applied broadly, but the average effect does not necessarily apply to every patient. An intervention may benefit one subgroup, have little effect in another, and cause harm in a third. When these effects are pooled, the overall result may be neutral. This variation is known as heterogeneity of treatment effect. However, heterogeneity should not be invoked only as a post hoc explanation for a neutral trial. Potential effect modifiers should be selected a priori and justified by clinical or biological plausibility.

A recent viewpoint by Lileikyte et al. [7] in JACC: Advances offers a timely discussion of this problem. The authors argue that future cardiac arrest trials should be designed to accommodate heterogeneity of treatment effect instead of treating it solely as a secondary analytical concern. They present conventional subgroup analysis, risk-based analysis, and effect-based estimation as complementary approaches. They also discuss adaptive and platform trial designs, which may allow prespecified patient strata to be evaluated more efficiently. The central message is direct: future trials should ask not only whether a treatment works on average, but also which patients are more or less likely to benefit.

This approach is not merely a call to create many small subgroups or to apply complex machine learning models without a clear clinical purpose. Prognostic phenotypes and treatment-responsive phenotypes are distinct. A high biomarker level, diffuse cerebral edema, or a malignant electroencephalographic pattern may identify patients with a poor prognosis, but these findings support precision treatment only when they also indicate a differential response to a specific intervention. The practical goal should therefore be to identify an actionable phenotype—one that can be detected within the therapeutic window, relates to the proposed treatment mechanism, and can reasonably guide trial enrollment or treatment assignment.

Future trials should also establish whether the intended biological target is present, whether treatment can be delivered within a relevant therapeutic window, and whether the intervention actually modifies the target. The TELSTAR trial provides an instructive example [4]. Aggressive antiseizure treatment largely suppressed rhythmic and periodic electroencephalographic activity, but neurological outcomes did not improve. The disappearance of this abnormal activity does not necessarily indicate recovery of cerebral function; the background may remain suppressed or low-voltage because of severe underlying brain injury. Thus, in at least some patients, these patterns may reflect the severity of underlying brain injury rather than a modifiable driver of further damage. The AUGMENT-VA trial offers a different lesson [5]. Transthoracic impedance was reduced, but survival was unchanged. However, per-protocol compliance was only 23.6%, meaning that most patients assigned to the intervention did not receive it as planned. The trial therefore tested not only whether the intervention was effective, but also whether it could be delivered reliably during real-world resuscitation. These studies illustrate why patient-centered outcomes should be interpreted alongside target relevance, target engagement, and implementation fidelity. The International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation scientific statement on post–cardiac arrest brain injury similarly emphasized the need to match treatment with the appropriate patient, timing, route, dose, and duration, and to confirm biological response when possible [8].

Precision resuscitation should not be framed as an alternative to protocolized care. Cardiac arrest management requires rapid decision-making, low cognitive load, and coordinated team performance. Standardized universal core interventions must therefore be preserved. Current resuscitation guidelines continue to emphasize standardized core components of post–cardiac arrest care [9]. Precision approaches should instead refine protocols in areas supported by sufficient evidence, allowing treatment pathways to branch according to physiology or phenotype without delaying essential care.

Emergency medicine has an important role in this transition. Initial rhythm, response to defibrillation, serial end-tidal carbon dioxide measurements, recurrent arrest, early features of shock, and immediate findings after return of spontaneous circulation are generated in the prehospital setting or emergency department. When the therapeutic window is short, phenotyping that begins only after intensive care unit admission may occur too late to guide treatment. Future studies will therefore need to combine early phenotyping and rapid enrollment while preserving feasibility, reproducibility, and treatment speed.

Neutral trials do not indicate a failure of cardiac arrest research. Rather, they have clarified the limits of routinely applying the tested strategies across heterogeneous patient populations. The next step is not to search retrospectively for a positive interpretation, but to ask prospectively which patients are most likely to respond to a given intervention. The approach proposed by Lileikyte et al. [7] provides a useful direction for future cardiac arrest trials. Planning for heterogeneity before a trial begins, rather than considering it only after the results are known, may help make resuscitation trials more precise and clinically informative.
