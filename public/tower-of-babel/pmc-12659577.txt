Histopathological spectrum and short-term outcome of rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis in children: a retrospective analysis from a tertiary care center
Authors: Irshad Ali Bajeer, Sabeeta Khatri, Habib Qaiser, Seema Hashmi, Muhammed Mubarak, Ali Asghar Lanewala
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionRapidly progressive glomerulonephritis (RPGN) is a medical emergency in children. Introduction

Rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis (RPGN) is often used synonymously with Crescentic glomerulonephritis (CrGN) and which is considered a medical emergency [1]. It is defined as rapid decline in kidney function over days to weeks [2].

According to a systematic review worldwide incidence of primary GN in children is 0.1 / 100,000 / year [3]. We Lanewala et al. in 2009 reported 5.2% prevalence of CrGN in a large cohort of 801 pediatric kidney biopsies at our single center [4]. As per NAPRTCS 2014 registry data CrGN was the underlying etiology in 1.7% of all kidney transplant recipients [5].

The chief presenting features of RPGN are hypertension, oliguria, hematuria, variable grade of proteinuria and progressive kidney dysfunction [6]. The definitive diagnosis of CrGN is established through kidney biopsy, which shows crescent formation within glomeruli. A crescent is the extracapillary proliferation of cells originating from parietal epithelium in response to inflammatory mediators released from damaged capillary walls and the glomerular basement membrane [7]. To define CrGN, a crescent must cover 50% of the glomerular circumference. Greater involvement (>80%) is associated with significant deterioration of kidney functions and ultimate poor outcome [8, 9]. The percentage of glomeruli involved with crescents has been debated and lower percentages with clinical context of severe disease may prompt pediatric nephrologists to treat [10]. Prognosis also depends on the type of crescent whether its cellular, fibro-cellular or fibrous [11]. Underlying etiology in CrGN is variable and extends from primary glomerular to systemic diseases with kidney involvement [12]. Based on immunofluorescence study, CrGN is divided into immune-complex, pauci immune and linear staining patterns [13].

Protocol for the management of CrGN has two phases. Induction phase includes administration of high dose methylprednisolone (MP) and IV cyclophosphamide (CYC) pulses, while maintenance phase consists of low dose steroids and azathioprine (AZA) or mycophenolate mofitil (MMF) [14]. CYCLOPS trial showed similar efficacy between oral and intravenous CYC with the latter having reduced dose and toxicity profile [15]. Another trial CYCAZAREM concluded that early introduction of AZA in the treatment protocol has similar outcome as that of CYC [16]. In fulminant crescentic GN, plasma exchange and intravenous immunoglobulin are advised [17].

Majority of the data on CrGN is derived from adult literature and very few studies are conducted in the pediatric population and those studies have either retrospective design or case series [18]. Only prospective study conducted by Dewan et al. has administered oral CYC and concluded that CrGN has poor outcome due to delayed referral of disease [19]. This study aims to determine the clinicopathological spectrum of RPGN, and short-term outcome of crescentic GN in pediatric population.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at the outpatient and emergency departments of pediatric nephrology, Sindh institute of urology and transplantation Karachi, from January, 2019 to December, 2022. Institutional ethical review committee approved the study and consent from individual patient/parents was waived due to the retrospective design.

All children aged 1–18 years with clinical presentation of RPGN, kidney biopsy results demonstrating at least one crescent and minimum follow-up of 24 months were included. Those with acute tubular necrosis, tubulointerstitial nephritis and thrombotic microangiopathy were excluded.

A detailed proforma was filled to document the age, gender, onset of swelling, hematuria, oliguria, cola colored urine and systemic features like rash, joint involvement, photophobia, seizures, psychosis, hemoptysis and hematochezia. Physical examination findings were recorded for rash, hypertension and edema. Investigations including complete blood count, urea, creatinine, hematuria, proteinuria, complement levels C3 and C4 were noted. Anti neutrophilic cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) profile and anti GBM antibodies were documented for kidney biopsy findings like pauci-immune immunofluorescence (IMF) and linear deposits along GBM respectively.

Real time ultrasound guided kidney biopsy was performed and the biopsy tissue was sent to the histopathology department with clinical information and investigations. The sample was processed for light microscopy and stained with periodic acidic schiff, silver and hematoxylin and eosin. Immunofluorescence studies were conducted on fresh tissue, staining for IgG, IgA, IgM, C3 and C1q. The patterns and intensity of IMF were recorded. The facility of electron microscopy was not available in our institute during the study time frame. One of the co-authors (MM), a specialist in kidney histopathology assessed details including total glomeruli, percentage of crescents, and type of crescents (cellular, fibrocellular and fibrous), degree of acute tubular necrosis and tubular atrophy. Mayo 2016 recommendations were used to classify crescents as cellular, fibrocellular and fibrous [20]. The histopathological description of crescent is shown in the Fig. 1.

Fibrocellular crescent comprising of admixture of fibrous tissue and cells in the Bowman’s space. (Periodic acid−Schiff stain, ×400). C. The background parenchyma showing a mild degree of tubular atrophy and interstitial inflammation. (Periodic acid−Schiff stain, ×200)

Once the diagnosis of RPGN was confirmed, induction phase was initiated with MP pulses until renal biopsy confirmed presence of crescentic GN, the induction phase continued for 4 to 6 months, consisting of 3 to 5 doses of intravenous MP, followed by daily oral prednisolone and monthly CYC pulse therapy.

Either AZA or MMF along with low-dose, alternate-day steroids were used in the maintenance phase for variable duration according to the underlying etiology. Plasma exchange was considered for ANCA associated vasculitis with severe disease at presentation [14]. Children with advanced kidney failure were started on kidney support therapy and stabilized prior to the kidney biopsy. Hypertension was managed with Amlodipine and if required Enalpril was added after documenting normal potassium level. Other supportive care measures were considered like fluid restriction, diuretic use, antibiotics, packed cell transfusion, and plasmapheresis as per the clinical need of individual patient.

Children were followed up every 2 weeks in the initial phase, then every 4 and 12 weeks. At each follow-up visit blood pressure, urinary dipstick for proteinuria and hematuria, complete blood count, serum creatinine and albumin were monitored. Outcomes were measured at 6, 12, 18 and 24 months in terms of complete remission(CR), partial remission(PR), chronic kidney diseases(CKD), end stage kidney disease (ESKD) and dialysis dependency or death.

RPGN was defined as progressive rise in the serum creatinine level over days to weeks, active urinary sediment on dipstick, normal kidneys on ultrasonography [21]. Participants were labelled as being in complete remission if the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) normalized, no active urinary sediment, and proteinuria was absent on urinary dipstick report. Partial remission was described as 50% improvement in eGFR and reduction in proteinuria with or without active urinary sediment. In children with persistently declined eGFR less than 60 ml/min/1.73 for 3 months, CKD was diagnosed. ESKD was determined if patient required kidney support therapy in the form of dialysis or kidney transplant or eGFR < 15 ml/min/1.73m2. The relapse of the disease was managed initially with pulse MP therapy only and in case of no response IV CYC was considered if the cumulative dose remained within non-toxic range.

All the data was entered into SPSS Version 26. The quantitative variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean with standard deviation, otherwise median with interquartile ranges were applied. Qualitative variables were expressed as percentages or range. T-test and chi-square were used for continuous and categorical variables respectively. P-value < 0.05 was considered as significant.

The most common etiology was immune complex MPGN (33%), followed by pauci-immune ANCA negative glomerulonephritis (27%), lupus nephritis (20%), C3 dominant MPGN (10%), MPO positive ANCA associated vasculitis (AAV) (7%) and IgA nephropathy (3%). MPGN was categorized on the basis of immunofluorescence due to non-availability of EM and further division of dense deposit disease and C3 glomerulonephritis was not made. At presentation, all children had microscopic hematuria, while gross hematuria was seen in 9 (30%) and oliguria in 16 (53.3%). Edema was present in 20 (66.7%), mostly mild 15 (50%), with 5 (16.6%) having moderate to severe swelling and seizures occurred in 7 (23.3%) of cases. Only one adolescent girl with MPO positive AAV required plasmapheresis at presentation. She showed no improvement in renal functions despite MP, CYC and plasmapheresis. A repeat biopsy at 3 months revealed complete glomerulosclerosis. Consequently, she was classified as having chronic kidney disease. All the children with lupus nephritis had no systemic features of lupus and were additionally administered hydroxychloroquine and angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors.

Children with delayed presentation (greater than 10 days from onset of symptoms) had relatively more incidence of chronic kidney disease (4 versus 1, p-value 0.36), fibrous or fibro cellular crescents (6 versus 3, p-value 0.29) and relapse of disease (3 versus 1, p-value 0.35). Residual hypertension was documented in 5 (17%) including three in stage II and two in elevated category. Relapse of disease occurred in four children (13%) and two of them progressed to chronic kidney disease. At last follow-up, the outcome was CR, PR, CKD and ESKD in 16 (53%), 9 (30%), 2 (7%) and 3 (10%) cases respectively. All the children with CR had normal eGFR, no proteinuria and occasional microscopic hematuria. Children attaining PR status continued to have microscopic hematuria, low grade proteinuria with serum albumin more than 3 G/dl and normal eGFR. No deaths were reported in the study participant. The most common adverse effect of CYC was reversible leukopenia in 7 (23.3%) and one child (3.3%) developed sepsis requiring hospitalization. AZA related leukopenia was documented in one child with IC-MPGN and was switched to MMF.

Discussion

RPGN is considered as serious clinical syndrome with diverse underlying etiologies, varied presentations and scarce information. This retrospective cohort of 30 cases with RPGN over period of 3–4 years represent a significant disease burden at a single public health tertiary specialized centre of a developing country.

The mean age at presentation in our cohort was 11 (± 2.5) years, which is slightly higher than the 8.5 ± 3.1 years reported by mosaad et al. [22]. The variation in the age can be explained by differences in the underlying etiologies of CrGN. Majority of our cohort had hypertension at presentation 26 (86%) which is higher than reported by Ozlu et al. 62.2% [23]. The requirement of kidney support therapy was observed in 8 (26%) children which is almost similar to 30% reported by Rianthavorn et al. [24].

The majority of cases with CrGN showed immune complex glomerulonephritis 17 (57%) of which 10 (59%) were MPGN, six (35%) were associated with lupus nephritis and one (6%) had IgA nephropathy. Mean follow up was 29 months ± 17.4 with 16 (53%) having complete renal recovery and 5 (17%) progressed to chronic kidney disease and ESKD.

A recently conducted systematic review of observational studies, concluded that the most common etiology of CrGN in children is immune complex GN with pooled proportion of 76%. Though the specific diseases had a significant contribution of post infectious glomerulonephritis [25]. The frequency of etiology of CrGN in our cohort is similar to this review and other published studies with immune complex glomerulonephritis being the most common [26]. The underlying pathogenesis of immune complex glomerulonephritis varies and includes membranoproliferative GN, lupus nephritis, post infectious GN, IgA nephropathy and Henoch Schonlein purpura.

Pauci immune GN in our study constitutes 33.3% of the total, while other studies have accounted for 4.6% to 8.1% [19, 27, 28]. A study by Sinha et al. had shown a predominance of pauci immune GN which may have reflected a decrease in post infectious GN in their study but in general data on pauci immune GN in children is sparse [29]. The application of IMF and ELISA has contributed significantly to the detection of ANCA and in specification of microscopic polyangiitis, renal limited vasculitis and eosinophilic polyangiitis with granulomatosis. In our study majority of the pauci immune GN were ANCA negative (80%) which is in contrast to previous studies where ANCA positivity has been seen [30, 31]. A study by Minz et al. in adults had shown a ANCA negativity of 43% [32].

Maintenance therapy duration with AZA or MMF depends on the underlying etiology. There has been an increasing advocacy for avoidance of CYC due to risk of infertility and infections with role of rituximab, calcineurin inhibitors and MMF being explored [33, 34]. We have continued with monthly CYC pulses due to compliance issues.

Overall, we observed complete remission and partial remission in 16 (53%) and 9 (30%) patients respectively. While, 3 (10%) patients progressed to end stage kidney disease and 2 (6.6%) CKD stage IV. Early treatment with immunosuppression has been stressed as an important contributor to favorable prognosis [14]. Sinha et al. have also demonstrated improved outcomes with early institution of dialysis/plasmapheresis and immunosuppression [29]. Even though previous studies have shown a worse prognosis in patients with increased number of crescents we did not find a similar association which can be possibly due to the small sample size. A study by Kayki et al. had shown a positive correlation with complete and partial response in patients with less than 50% crescents [27]. Our cohort underwent kidney biopsy within 4 days of presentation and it took median 6 days to receive IV CYC. Such timely interventions are critical in the management of crescentic GN and we were able to avoid unusual delays.

This study has several limitations. Being a retrospective, single center study so, the results may not be generalized to the general population. Due to small sample size this study is not powered enough which affects its external validity. Electron microscopy was not available for the duration of the study, and it would have helped in further delineating the C3 dominant MPGN.

It is difficult to compare our study with other centers due to the small number of patients in most of the studies and the variable demographics. There is also a lack uniformity as far as treatment is concerned with an increasing deviation from the use of cyclophosphamide.

Conclusion

In this cohort of children with crescentic glomerulonephritis, the most frequent histopathological diagnoses were immune complex membranoproliferative GN and pauci-immune GN, followed by lupus nephritis, C3-dominant MPGN, ANCA-associated vasculitis, and IgA nephropathy. Clinically, hypertension, hematuria, and edema were common, with over one-fourth presenting with severe kidney dysfunction requiring dialysis. All patients were treated with an induction regimen of methylprednisolone and cyclophosphamide, followed by maintenance with azathioprine or mycophenolate mofetil plus low-dose alternate-day steroids. Complete remission was achieved in half of the cohort, while the remainder had partial remission or progressed to CKD or ESKD. Early diagnosis and prompt immunosuppressive therapy may improve short-term outcomes, but variability in etiology and late presentation remain challenges. Further multicenter prospective studies are warranted to define optimal treatment strategies in pediatric crescentic GN.
