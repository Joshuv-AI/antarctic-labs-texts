Evaluating Empowerment in Adolescents with a Disability and/or Chronic Condition from Neurology and Orthopedic Clinics: A Mixed-Methods Study
Authors: Kennedy Austin, Kathryn Selby, Alice Virani, Colin Ross, Harpreet Chhina, Anthony Cooper, Alison M Elliott
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate empowerment and quality of life in adolescents (10-19 years of age) with chronic conditions and/or disabilities from neurology and orthopedic clinics and identify areas to improve their care and empowerment.Study designWe utilized a mixed-methods design with an explanatory sequential approach. Participants were recruited from neurology and orthopedic clinics at BC Children's Hospital and completed a demographic survey and two validated online tools: The Gothenburg Young Person's Empowerment Scale (GYPES) and Pediatric Quality of Life (PedsQL) 4.0 self-report. Semi-structured interviews were conducted to gain a deeper understanding of adolescents' feelings of empowerment. Linear regression analysis was performed to identify demographic variables associated with decreased empowerment and quality of life. Interviews were analyzed using an interpretive description framework.ResultsForty-eight participants completed the survey. The mean reported GYPES and PedsQL scores were 58.15 ± 8.46 and 61.57 ± 22.32, respectively. Theoretical sufficiency was reached after 14 interviews. Four environmental factors leading to feelings promoting empowerment were identified: (1) shared decision-making enabled advocacy and independence, (2) education (particularly involving genetics) increased knowledge and understanding, (3) sharing stories led to empowering others, and (4) participation in physical activities promoted a sense of belonging.ConclusionsThis study was the first to use the GYPES in adolescents with diverse disabilities and/or chronic conditions from neurology and orthopedic clinics. Adolescent patients felt empowered when they were actively involved in their health care and informed about the genetics of their diagnosis. Participants also wanted opportunities to empower others going through similar experiences. This study emphasizes the need for access to genetic counseling and opportunities for shared expression to facilitate empowerment.Keywords: adolescents, disability, chronic condition, empowerment

The term disability includes physical, cognitive, sensory, and emotional limitations that may result from disease, injury, or congenital conditions.1 Chronic health conditions typically encompass those requiring continuous treatment and limiting daily functioning lasting for at least 1 year.2 Individuals with disabilities and/or chronic health conditions face social and environmental barriers that can hinder participation and may lead to discrimination.3,4 These barriers stem from social bias and ableism present in the early stages of child development, making timely interventions to reduce these biases imperative to promoting a more inclusive society for individuals with a disability.5

Population growth and continuous medical advancements have led to an increase in the number of individuals, including those within the growing adolescent population, who are living with a disability.6 Adolescence, defined by the World Health Organization as those aged 10-19 years,7 is a period that includes biological and social changes alongside identity formation.8,9 It is important to recognize the distinct needs of adolescents experiencing chronic health conditions, as they have reported problems with psychological adjustment and show greater risks for depression, anxiety, and delays in psychosocial development.10,11 However, health care systems currently face barriers to supporting this group, including a lack of experienced health care professionals (HCPs), lack of resources, and discrimination, emphasizing the need for continuous research to help improve the lives of adolescents with a disability.6

One health-related outcome that has not been extensively studied in adolescents with various disabilities and/or chronic conditions (hereafter referred to as “disabilities/chronic conditions”) is empowerment.12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 Patient empowerment is an ongoing process where patients are encouraged to be involved in their health care and are given the resources needed to make informed health decisions.12 It is also important to recognize that individuals have different needs, and HCPs must provide support to accommodate different empowerment processes.13 Previous studies have suggested that patient empowerment leads to an improvement in quality of life (QOL) and could result in eventual long-term health benefits.14,15 In adolescents, patient empowerment has been observed as being positively associated with improved therapeutic adherence in individuals with diabetes16 and cystic fibrosis.17 Efforts to empower adolescent patients through education and interventions to increase their knowledge, skills, and self-management abilities have also been described in the literature; however, these avenues require further exploration.18

This study utilized a mixed-methods approach to gain an in-depth understanding of how adolescents with a disability/chronic condition (those lasting at least 1 year limiting daily functions and requiring ongoing medical treatment related to neurologic and/or orthopedic disorders)2,19 feel empowered in their health. We used an explanatory sequential research design where quantitative data was first collected and analyzed and was then used to purposively select participants for the qualitative phase of the study.20 For the quantitative phase, 2 validated online tools were used to evaluate empowerment and QOL; The Gothenburg Young Persons Empowerment Scale (GYPES)21 and The Pediatric Quality of Life (PedsQL)22 4.0 self-report. Using these tools and semistructured interviews, we aimed to identify concerns related to disability and to address areas in which to improve adolescent patient care.

Methods

Ethics approval was granted by the UBC Research Ethics Board on May 31, 2024 (H23-03736).

Recruitment

Eligible participants included adolescent patients (aged 10-19 years) followed in the pediatric neurology or orthopedic clinics at BC Children's Hospital (Vancouver, British Columbia, Canada) with a chronic condition/disability who were cognitively able to complete an online survey and interview. We defined chronic conditions/disabilities as those limiting daily functioning and/or requiring ongoing medical interventions for at least 1 year.2 We aimed to recruit ∼50 participants for the quantitative portion, as this allows for both statistical and logistical analysis. Participant sampling for the qualitative phase was guided by the concept of information power, where we expected theoretical sufficiency would be reached by ∼15 interviews.23,24

Participants were recruited from June 2024 to April 2025 during in-person clinic visits, in the hospital wards, and through posters including details about the study. Consent forms were administered to parents of adolescents under the age of 19 (adolescent completed an assent form), whereas those aged 19 years were able to consent to participate in the study. Participants received an honorarium of a $10 Starbucks gift card for completing the quantitative phase, and an additional $20 Starbucks gift card for completing the qualitative phase.

Data Collection

Participants completed a demographic survey and 2 validated online tools (GYPES and PedsQL 4.0 self-report) via research electronic data capture (REDCap)25 housed at BC Children's Hospital Research Institute. The GYPES measures empowerment through 5 domains; knowledge and understanding, identity, personal control, shared decision-making, and enabling others.21 The PedsQL 4.0 self-report measures QOL through 4 dimensions of health: physical, emotional, social, and school functioning.22 Both tools use a Likert scale. The GYPES contains 15 items, and the PedsQL 4.0 contains 23 items. The demographic survey (included in Appendix 1, available at www.jpeds.com) surveyed a variety of demographic variables, including current age, age at diagnosis, clinic, diagnosis/medical condition, sex, gender, ethnic background, among others. Participants also were asked if they would like to be contacted for an interview or future research studies and whether they wanted to be informed of the results of this study.

Semi-structured interviews with open-ended questions about disability and empowerment (eg, “Can you tell me about a time where you felt empowered in managing your condition?”) were conducted with a subset of participants who completed the online survey and agreed to be contacted for an interview. Interview participants were purposively selected from the quantitative phase to ensure that a diverse group of individuals with various conditions, ages, ethnicities, sex, gender, and reported levels of empowerment and QOL were represented. A full copy of the interview guideline is included in Appendix 2 (available at www.jpeds.com). Interviews were completed in person or via Zoom (based on the preference of the participant) and were audio recorded. Medical translators were available for participants who spoke languages other than English.

Data Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the reported GYPES and PedsQL 4.0 values, and univariate linear regression analysis was performed to identify potential associations between demographic characteristics and empowerment (GYPES) or QOL (PedsQL 4.0) scores. A P value of less than .05 was considered statistically significant, and all estimates were reported with 95% confidence intervals. All statistical analysis were performed using R version 4.4.3.26

We followed a pragmatic research design, which focuses on generating practical outcomes,27 and a methodologic framework called interpretive description, which aims to capture individual experiences while also identifying common themes within a population to generative evidence-based knowledge that can inform future practice.28 Our qualitative findings were analyzed using phronetic iterative analysis, in which data are collected and analyzed simultaneously, and both emerging themes and previously established theories are used to interpret the findings.29 This analysis involves both inductive and deductive coding. Interviews were transcribed, and themes relating to disability and empowerment were generated separately by 2 independent coders. To resolve any coding discrepancies between the 2 coders, a third coder was consulted. These themes related to barriers to empowerment in order to generate evidence-based knowledge to help inform future clinical practice.

Quantitative and qualitative results were analyzed separately, then integrated through the process of data triangulation, which increases the validity and reliability of our findings and allows for a more comprehensive understanding of our research topic.20

A total of 67 individuals consented to being a part of the study and were sent surveys. Of this, 48 surveys were completed and included in data analysis, with a response rate of 72%. Participants represented a diverse range of ages (mean = 13.98 years), sex, gender, ethnicities, and medical conditions (Table I).

Inferential Statistics

Univariate linear regression analysis identified significant associations between demographic predictors and GYPES and PedsQL 4.0 scores (Tables III and IV, respectively). Participants recruited from the orthopedic clinic had significantly greater “personal control” empowerment domain scores and QOL scores across all 4 health dimensions compared to those from the neurology clinic. In addition, individuals who indicated that they would like to be contacted for future research studies reported significantly higher total and “enabling others” empowerment domain scores and lower “emotional functioning” QOL scores compared with those who did not want to be contacted. Participants who indicated that they would like to be contacted about the results of our study had significantly higher total “knowledge and understanding” and “enabling others” empowerment domain scores and lower “physical,” “emotional,” “social,” and total functioning QOL scores compared with those who did not.

Qualitative

Interview Participants

Of the 48 participants who completed the survey, interviews were completed with 14 individuals and ranged from 17 minutes to 1 hour in length (average length of 31 minutes). This sample appropriately reflected the diversity of adolescents observed in the quantitative phase of our study; a full breakdown of interview participant demographic characteristics is included in Appendix 3 (available at www.jpeds.com).

Facilitating Empowerment

Primary coding analysis initially identified more than 60 codes emerging from interview transcripts. Secondary coding analysis grouped relevant themes together and excluded themes unrelated to empowerment. Throughout the interviews, adolescents expressed varying perceptions of empowerment, with many sharing that when they were unable to feel physically empowered and in control of their body, they would focus on feeling emotionally empowered, as represented in the quote, “But like emotionally I do feel empowered and OK about my disability. I think sometimes it's also like a mental thing to not let it stop you.” [ID5, 19, Neurology].

We identified four4 external factors (influenced by the outer environment) supporting internal factors (feelings experienced by participants) which allowed adolescents to accept their diagnosis and feel confident and empowered in managing their health. These themes are shown in the Figure and are explained further below.

(1) Enabling Shared Decision-Making Among Patients, Parents, and HCPs

External factors leading to internal feelings promoting acceptance and emotional empowerment in participants.

When asked for examples of when they felt empowered, participants shared that they appreciated being involved in decisions about their health. This sense of control was important to them, with 1 participant explaining, “No one knows exactly how you feel, except for you” [ID5, 19, Neurology]. Another stated, “I feel like because of my […] powerful advocacy skills, I feel like that gives me more control over my decisions medically and in daily life with my recovery.” [ID2, 16, Neurology].

Adolescents also wanted independence from their parents and felt empowered when they were able to make autonomous decisions. One individual explained the importance of being able to make his own choices, “Being able to choose what I want to do and not really having to tell my parents like they know that I'll be fine. I think that was probably empowering for me.” [ID8, 18, Orthopedics].

(2) Providing Sufficient Education Around Topics Such as Treatment, Management, and Genetics

(2) Providing Sufficient Education Around Topics Such as Treatment, Management, and Genetics

Participants also shared that being educated about their diagnosis impacted how they felt about it and how they made decisions. One individual stated, “If I didn't have much knowledge about [my treatment], if I didn't really know anything I wouldn't have gone through so many things like so many procedures […] being knowledgeable about it is definitely a big part about how you feel about it” [ID3, 16, Orthopedics].

This desire for knowledge was especially seen in those with genetic or potentially genetic conditions who wanted to learn more about the genetic aspects of their diagnosis to help improve their QOL and understand inheritance patterns, with some stating that they would benefit from speaking with a genetic counselor. For example, when asked what could help them feel more empowered, one participant said, “I would love to [find a genetic cause for my condition] just because if there's a way to like…somehow fix that, then that would be the best thing or to know like is it gonna be passed down if I choose to have children.” [ID11, 16, Neurology].

(3) Promoting Opportunities for Patients to Share Their Stories and Express Their Feelings

(3) Promoting Opportunities for Patients to Share Their Stories and Express Their Feelings

Many adolescents wanted more opportunities to share their stories to help build confidence and enable others, as well as hearing other stories to feel connected. One participant explained that hearing others share their stories, “Helps me feel like I'm not alone in my journey with muscular dystrophy.” [ID16, 16, Neurology]. Others wanted to hear stories to help improve their own QOL: “I'm just wondering about how others view their life and how maybe it could make it easier for me?” [ID1, 16, Neurology]. Some individuals wanted to share their stories to help improve care for future generations and felt empowered being a part of this process, as explained by the quote, “With my experience, it could be better in the future? I think to take all of this and to do this research and everything…if it improves the situation for other people then it would be really good, I want that for the next generations.” [ID11, 16, Neurology].

(4)Encouraging Participation in Modified Physical Activities

(4)Encouraging Participation in Modified Physical Activities

Another way in which adolescents described feeling empowered was when they were included in physical activities with their peers and developed a strong sense of belonging. For example, one participant shared that he was able to play basketball with his friends while sitting down when he was unable to walk. He explained, “I think I have been able to keep like a positive attitude throughout all this because I do enjoy just even watching [sports] if I have to, or sometimes people find a way for me to even include myself.” [ID3, 16, Orthopedics].

Restricting Empowerment

Participants also shared what factors led to them feeling disempowered. This included a lack of legal control over decisions where they did not feel autonomous as their parents had the final say in signing consent forms. It was also difficult for adolescents to make decisions, accept their diagnosis, and feel comfortable explaining their disability to their peers when they did not feel educated enough to do so. Some adolescents with progressive neurologic disorders also felt disempowered and fearful of their future as they could not predict or control how their disorder would progress. This is explained by one participant saying, “I'm excited to grow up, be able to do stuff on my own. But lots of stuff I don't think I'm going to be able to do by myself, […] because I just don't know what's going to happen to me yet.” [ID56, 14, Neurology].

Participants also felt frustrated and disempowered when they were excluded from groups due to physical limitations, or bullied by peers for being different, “Kids would make fun of me for having a wheelchair, or like, my arms not bending straight, which is not really fun […] those were just moments where I didn't feel empowered because like I was questioning like whether I could or couldn't do something” [ID22, 13, Neurology].

Lastly, some individuals expressed that they did not feel supported by the health care system and often felt overlooked and unimportant when they were not seen as a priority in clinic or were experiencing long wait times and difficulties scheduling appointments. Some participants shared that they did not feel prioritized in the clinic because they “looked less disabled” than other children. This was also identified in school settings, where accommodations were not being made for them because their disabilities did not appear as physically severe as others, leaving individuals feeling like they were being pushed to the side by their teachers who did not know how to engage with them.

Integration

Our quantitative findings identified that patients with orthopedic disorders reported higher QOL and “personal control” empowerment scores compared to patients with neurological disorders. This aligns with our qualitative findings, where those with neurological disorders expressed more challenges related to empowerment and described a sense of a lack of control over the potential progression of their disorders. In addition, participants who wanted to be contacted for future research studies and informed about the results of our study reported higher total and “enabling others” empowerment scores, while having significantly lower “emotional functioning” QOL scores. This was supported in interviews, where some individuals wanted more opportunities to speak about their experiences because they felt confident and wanted to help others, in spite of facing emotional challenges. Interview participants also wanted to be involved in future studies to help improve their own health and QOL, and some felt empowered to help enable future generations with these opportunities.

Discussion

Our study was the first to use a validated tool (GYPES) to evaluate empowerment in adolescents with a diverse range of neurologic and/or orthopedic disabilities/chronic conditions. Participants in our study included adolescents aged 10-19 years with a diverse range of ethnicities, gender identities, levels of empowerment, and QOL. The total mean GYPES score in our study (58.15 ± 8.46) was similar to comparable studies evaluating empowerment in adolescents with congenital heart disease,30 type 1 diabetes,31 and cystic fibrosis.17

The mean PedsQL 4.0 score (61.57 ± 22.32) reported in our study was lower than those typically reported in healthy children (80.0)32 and fell between those previously reported in studies measuring QOL in children with neurologic and orthopedic conditions, with lower scores typically observed in neurologic compared with orthopedic studies.33, 34, 35, 36 The significantly higher QOL scores reported in patients with orthopedic disabilities compared to patients with neurological disorders in our study may be due to the nature of many progressive neurologic disorders that were included (ie, muscular dystrophy, spinal muscular atrophy). These findings further emphasize support needed for children with neurological disorders to help improve their QOL.

Our qualitative findings suggest that participants felt empowered when they were actively contributing to decisions in their health care and felt educated enough to make these decisions confidently. Some adolescents felt disempowered by a lack of control over their future and not knowing how their health condition would progress. Similar themes have been identified in children with chronic kidney disease, who wanted to be more involved in their health and expressed that limited knowledge as well as fear and uncertainty about their future contributed to feelings of disempowerment.37

Importantly, many adolescents in our study with genetic or potentially genetic conditions wanted to gain a better understanding of the genetic aspects of their disorder by talking with a genetic counselor, with some participants seeking a confirmed genetic diagnosis. In past research, adolescents participating in the 100 000 Genomes project were motivated to find a confirmed genetic diagnosis. However, many were uninformed about what it meant to have genome sequencing,38 similar to some participants expressing their lack of knowledge around genetic concepts. This highlights the importance of appropriate information sharing with adolescents, which is especially relevant in cases in which genetics are involved and where individuals may benefit from learning more about their diagnosis from a genetic counselor, though the process of genetic counseling within this population needs to be further explored.39

We also identified the value of providing adolescents the opportunity to make autonomous decisions independently from their parents to feel empowered. Barriers limiting active participation in this population have been identified including undervalued opinions and insufficient power needed to make decisions, resulting in adolescents feeling excluded from their health process and disempowered.40,41 Previously, adolescents with long-term health conditions have expressed their desire for a more collaborative approach in their health care where they are well-informed and able to share opinions and concerns with parents and HCPs.42 This aligns with findings from our study, signifying the need for opportunities in which adolescent patients can be involved in their health care to feel empowered and confident in managing their health.

Both our quantitative and qualitative findings recognized the importance of enabling others in their health process, with some adolescents wanting to gain confidence and feeling empowered through sharing their stories and hearing from others experiencing similar challenges. A past study identified that including adolescents in research projects facilitated empowerment as they were helping to create a better future for themselves and for others,43 which was identified in our study as well. Similarly, another study found that adolescents with type 1 diabetes felt empowered when they shared their stories to help influence peers in a positive manner and advocate for change within society.44 These findings underscore the need for opportunities where adolescents can express their feelings and share their journey with others to feel empowered and included in the process of advocating for future change.

Including adolescent voices in research is especially important in marginalized populations (such as those with disabilities), as these voices have been overlooked in the past.45 This population has reported many positive outcomes stemming from being involved as research partners including increased independence, confidence, and empowerment. Adolescents have indicated that involvement in research allows them the chance to make meaningful changes and help others in similar situations,46 which was also described by participants in our study. These opportunities also improve the quality and relevance of research in this population, further highlighting the significance of including adolescents as partners in research.47

Some participants in our study felt a sense of belonging, contributing to their empowerment when they were included in physical activities with both their disabled and nondisabled peers. Aligned with our findings, participating in sports with peers has been shown to promote social acceptance and self-efficacy in this population, meaning they felt confident in their abilities to complete a task.48 Our study also identified barriers to empowerment in adolescents such as feeling excluded from activities and a lack of disability awareness from peers. This has been identified in literature where adolescent participation in physical activities has been restricted by negative attitudes from peers and limited opportunities for engaging in these activities.49,50

More extreme cases of exclusion leading to disempowerment were also identified by some participants in our study such as bullying and discrimination. Interestingly, increased participation in extracurricular activities has been found to reduce the likelihood of bullying within this population, signifying the importance of these opportunities.51 Previous research has also highlighted the negative impact of discrimination in adolescents, which is especially seen in those with chronic illnesses.52 This may stem from a lack of knowledge about disabilities from non-disabled adolescents, as research examining their perspectives identified that most felt uncomfortable interacting with their peers who had genetic conditions.53 However, they also noted that they wanted to learn more about genetic conditions and thought that increased social interaction with their peers with genetic condition would help improve their negative attitudes. These results suggest to help reduce bias present in non-disabled adolescents, education, awareness, and social interaction with individuals with a disability are needed.

The strengths of our study include the diversity of our sample (ie, various neurological and/or orthopedic conditions, ages, sex, gender, and ethnicities) ensuring that diverse perspectives and experiences were captured. The mixed-methods approach also enhanced the validity and reliability of our results. In addition, we used 2 validated tools to measure empowerment and QOL specifically tailored to this population that have been previously tested in a subset of similarly affected cohorts. Limitations include a relatively low sample size for quantitative research (n = 48) meaning that we were unable to perform multivariate analysis, limiting our statistical findings. This also resulted in some categories having low sample sizes (n < 5), which were excluded from our analysis due to the instability of some P values. The diversity of our sample was also limited as despite the research team having access to a translator, all participants spoke English, which may influence the applicability of our studies results to other populations. It is also important to note that our study focused on those with the cognitive capacity to complete a survey and interview, meaning that our findings cannot necessarily be generalized to those with significant cognitive disabilities.

We have identified areas in which we can promote empowerment and improve patient well-being in an adolescent population with diverse neurological and/or orthopedic disabilities/chronic conditions. Our findings suggest that adolescents need to be active participants in the health care decision making process to feel empowered. This can be achieved through collaborative care and joint decision-making, education, opportunities for self-expression and helping others, in an inclusive environment, all of which promote self-confidence and allow adolescents to be seen and heard in managing their health. We encourage access to genetic counseling for adolescents to understand the genetic basis of their diagnosis, and inclusive environments and opportunities for adolescents to speak about their experiences and be active participants in care decisions. Future studies should include use of the GYPES in chronic conditions not included in our study (eg, intellectual disability) as well as longitudinal studies measuring empowerment in adolescent populations over time and as they transition into adulthood. Collectively, these findings highlight ways to improve adolescent patient care and empowerment both as inpatients and outpatients in adolescents with a disability, providing strategies to help them build confidence in managing their health.
