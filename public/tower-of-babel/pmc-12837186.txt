Factors associated with developing and surviving brain metastases in patients with breast cancer: a multi-center experience
Authors: Ahmad Alhalabi, Theresa Abdo, Ali Hijazi, Mohamed Mohanna, Rami Tfayli, María Herrán, Saad Sabbagh, Saffet Guleryuz, Razan Mohanna, Rashid Mahrous, Kaylee Sarna, Surabhi Ranjan, Christopher W Fleming, Zeina Nahleh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposePatients with breast cancer and brain metastasis (BCBM) have poor survival outcomes. We aim to explore predictors of survival in patients with BCBM including the Breast Graded Prognostic Assessment -GPA tool and its variables and review the impact of emerging targeted therapies.Patients and methodsThe Cleveland Clinic database was retrospectively reviewed to identify patients diagnosed with BCBM between 2010 and 2020 at multiple treatment centers in Ohio and Florida. Data on demographic, clinical, and treatment variables were collected, including BC surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, endocrine therapy, HER2-targeted therapy, immunotherapy, PARP inhibitors, CDK4/6 inhibitors, tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), BM characteristics (number, size, location), extracranial metastasis (ECM), and BM treatments. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to calculate median overall survival (mOS), and univariate and multivariate Cox regression models were adopted to identify significant survival predictors.ResultsN = 318 evaluable patients with BCBM were included. Patients were aged 46–60 years (42.5%), female (99.7%), of white race (79.6%), and non–Hispanic (97.2%). Among BC subtypes, the triple positive subgroup had the longest mOS, while the triple negative subgroup had the shortest. Having one or less ECM was linked to longer survival compared to ≥ 2 ECM. Patients receiving HER2-targeted therapy, PARP inhibitors, and TKIs had significantly longer mOS than those who did not receive targeted agents. Additionally, patients who underwent BM surgery had significantly longer mOS. Patients treated with Stereotactic Radiosurgery (SRS) such gamma knife radiosurgery had the longest mOS among the radiotherapy subgroups.ConclusionBCBM is a heterogeneous population with variable outcomes. This analysis suggests that several factors influence the development and outcome after BCBM with HR+/HER2- subgroup having the longest median time to develop BM compared to other subgroups. Survival after BCBM is impacted by age, performance status and overall disease burden, as well as the use of novel targeted therapies suggesting the feasibility to overcome the historical blood brain barrier to systemic treatment benefit from chemotherapy. Brain surgery and Radiation modalities also play an important role in improving long term outcome. These results support updating current prognostic models, such as the Breast-GPA, by integrating treatment exposures, shifting from static baseline scores to dynamic, treatment-informed prognostication.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) remains the most diagnosed cancer among women in the United States constituting over 30% of all cancers [1]. From 2010 to 2019, BC incidence increased by 0.5% annually, while mortality rates dropped by 1.3% due to advancements in BC therapy including immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) and targeted therapies for all BC subtypes, including triple negative (TN) BC [2–5]. However, this also led to an increase in the incidence of brain metastasis (BM) which still confers poor survival outcomes [6, 7]. Brain metastasis may occur in up to 20%-40% of patients with BC [8] and may have led to highly variable survival rates due to various factors, including but not limited to tumor subtypes [8]. However, patients with TN disease continue to have a disproportionately short median OS of less than 5 months [8].

Treatment options for patients with breast cancer that has metastasized to the brain (BCBM) include surgery, whole brain radiotherapy (WBRT), stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS), including gamma knife radiosurgery (GKRS), and systemic chemotherapy including agents such as capecitabine, which remains a category 2B option for patients with BCBM [9–11]. Systemic chemotherapies have shown varying degrees of CNS efficacy; however, when combined with novel targeted therapies/agents, improved survival outcomes could be attained [12, 13]. Recently, targeted therapies such as monoclonal antibodies, tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), PARP inhibitors, CDK4/6 inhibitors, and ICIs have shown promise in the prevention and treatment of BCBM [14] and offers an emphasis on the importance of the multimodal approach that needs to be adopted for the management of patients with BCBM [15, 16].

Given the highly variable survival rates among patients with brain metastases, Sperduto et al. developed the Graded Prognostic Assessment (GPA) index to aid in survival prediction and treatment decision-making. The GPA encompasses a range of solid tumors, including lung cancer, melanoma, breast cancer, renal cell carcinoma, and gastrointestinal cancers. Specifically, the GPA for breast cancer (Breast-GPA) incorporates four key variables: Breast receptor subtype, age, Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS), and the presence of extracranial metastases [17, 18]. Recent studies have proposed additional prognostic indicators such as brain metastasis velocity (BMV) and sarcopenia although these have not yet been integrated into standard GPA scoring systems [19, 20].

In this study, we examined the Breast-GPA tool and its variables alongside selected therapies, with the goal of expanding our understanding of factors that might influence survival outcomes in patients with BCBM.

Methods

Patient population

We retrospectively reviewed the electronic health records of patients from the Cleveland Clinic database across all Enterprise between 2010 and 2020. Patients with BC confirmed by pathology (biopsy or surgical specimens) and a BM confirmed by CT/MRI were consecutively included in the study. Patients were excluded from the study if brain lesions were not confirmed to be of breast origin. A total of n = 318 patients were included in the analysis. It is important to note that some patients may have been enrolled in clinical trials during their treatment course; however, this information was not collected at that time.

Demographic data was collected on age, sex, race, ethnicity, marital status, and insurance status. Clinical and pathologic data were collected on ECOG score, Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS), tumor laterality, histology, size, surgical margins, stage, grade, HR status, HER2 receptor status. BM specific information was collected including BM number, size, and location, extracranial metastases (ECM) and treatment modalities including BM surgery and radiotherapy (WBRT and SRS/GKRS). Information about treatment modalities were collected including chemotherapy, breast surgery and radiotherapy, ET (tamoxifen, anastrozole, letrozole, fulvestrant, elacestrant, exemestane), HER2-targeted therapy (trastuzumab, pertuzumab, ado–trastuzumab emtansine, fam–trastuzumab deruxtecan), immunotherapy/antibody drug conjugates (ADCs) (pembrolizumab, atezolizumab, sacituzumab), PARP inhibitors (olaparib, talazolaparib), CDK 4/6 inhibitors (palbociclib, ribociclib, abemaciclib), and TKIs (lapatinib, tucatinib, alpelisib, neratnib). A distinction was made between treatments received after initial BC diagnosis and those received after disease progression or interval BM development. The latter treatments of BM were of interest and are identified with the word “late”; for instance: “late chemotherapy” to distinguish them from initial treatment provided to patients with BC and without BM.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics like counts/frequencies/percentages were conducted on the whole dataset. The Kaplan Meier method with log rank tests was used to compute (Median Overall Survival) mOS in months with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Univariate Cox regression was performed to determine significant variables to include in multivariate Cox regression. Multivariate Cox regression was performed with backwards elimination of 0.05 to identify significant predictors of survival. Overall survival (OS) was calculated from the time of diagnosis of brain metastases secondary to breast cancer (time zero) until the date of death or last follow-up, based on patients’ vital status (alive or deceased). SAS version 9.4 and R 4.2.3 were used to analyze the data.

Results

Descriptives

We included n = 318 evaluable patients with BCBM diagnosed between 2010 and 2020, and with complete diagnostic and treatment data for this analysis. The median age was 54 years (IQR: 18.0; Q1: 44.0, Q3: 62.0). Most patients were female (99.7%), white (79.6%), non-Hispanic (97.2%), married (53.5%), and many patients had private insurance (43.1%). Based on BC characteristics, the following histological criteria were identified among the cohort of patients with BCBM: invasive ductal carcinoma (81.7%), grade III (68.5%), stage III at initial diagnosis (33.6%), HR(+) (56.9%), and HER2(-) (67.9%). In terms of treatments received, a greater proportion of patients received chemotherapy (86.5%) most of them being neo–adjuvant (66.3%), breast radiotherapy (62.9%), and breast surgery (92.5%) most of them being modified radical mastectomy (MRM) (72.8%). 50.6% of patients received ET, 29.9% received HER2-targeted therapy, 11% received immunotherapy, 8.5% received PARP inhibitors, 27.4% received CDK 4/6 inhibitors, 19.8% received TKIs. After recurrence, 34.3% received late ET, 25.8% received late HER2-targeted therapy, 43.4% received late chemotherapy with 43.7% receiving capecitabine. Based on BM local treatments, 14.5% underwent BM resection, and 85.2% of patients received at least one form of BM radiotherapy. The overall time to developing BM from initial diagnosis was 31.1 months (p = 0.0002). Of all the BC subgroups, the HR+/HER2- group had the longest median time to develop BM of 40.3 months (p = 0.0016). Overall survival analysis and univariate regression

Several factors influenced the overall survival and predictability of progression in patients with breast cancer and brain metastases. These factors can be categorized into demographic and clinical factors, tumor characteristics, surgical and systemic therapy, and local brain treatment.

Demographics and clinical factors

Age was found to be an important factor as patients older than 60 years old had the shortest mOS at 4.2 months (95% CI, 2.73–7.29), compared to the longest mOS in patients younger than 45 years at 11.7 months (7.56–17.31) (p = 0.0013). Compared to patients ≤ 45 years, 46–60-year-old patients had 1.25 times (95% CI, 0.94–1.68, p = 0.1279), and patients ˃60 years of age had 1.76 times (1.29–2.41, p = 0.0004) higher risk of death.

As expected, Performance Status (KP) was also found to be an important variable. Patients with better KPS (80–100) had longer mOS at 12.2 months (9.17–17.31) than patients with worse KPS (30–70) at 3.6 months (2.63–5.32) (p < 0.0001). A higher KPS score (80–100) had a lower risk of death compared to those with lower KPS score (30–70) [0.53(0.42–0.69), p < 0.0001].

Tumor Characteristics played a significant role in defining outcome in patiets with BCBM. Based on BC subgroup, the HR(+)/HER2(+) subgroup had the best mOS at 19.8 months (11.04–25.27) followed by the HR(-)/HER2(+) subgroup at 13.5 months (9.10–20.17), then the HR(+)/HER2(-) subgroup at 6.1 months (3.65–8.87), with the Triple negative HR(-)/HER2(-) (TNBC) subgroup having the worst mOS at 5.3 months (3.25–7.52) (p = 0.0005). Compared to the HR(+)/HER2(+) subgroup, all other subgroups had higher risk of death with the TNBC group having almost double the mortality risk: HR(-)/HER2(-) [1.94(1.37–2.75), p = 0.0002], HR(-)/HER2(+) [1.25(0.82–1.91), p = 0.3075), and HR(+)/HER2(-) [1.78(1.27–2.50), p = 0.0009].

Disease Burden was also evaluated and was a determining factor as well. Patients having ≥ 2 ECM had significantly worse mOS at 6.8 months (4.2–8.9) compared to patients with ≤ 1 ECM at 12.5 months (7.4–18.6) (p < 0.0001). Compared to patients with ≤ 1 ECM, those with ≥ 2 ECM were 1.69 times more likely to die (1.32–2.17, p < 0.0001).

Furthermore, the surgical and systemic therapies impact was reviewed and found to be significant as well. Patients receiving Modified Radical Mastectomy MRM had longer mOS at 9.1 months (5.95–11.53) than patients receiving breast conserving surgery (BCS) at 6 months (2.89–8.41) (p = 0.0404). Patients who received HER2-targeted therapy had significantly longer mOS at 17.9 months (11.53–22.54) compared to those not receiving HER2-targeted therapy 5.9 months (3.81–7.75) (p = 0.0001). Patients receiving HER2-targeted therapy had a protective effect compared to patients who did not receive HER2-targeted therapy [0.60(0.47–0.78), p = 0.0001]. Patients receiving TKIs had a significantly longer mOS at 25.2 months (13.73–33.05) compared to those not receiving TKIs at 6 months (4.17–7.75) (p < 0.0001). TKIs [0.53(0.39–0.71), p < 0.0001]. Patients receiving late ET had a significantly longer mOS at 11.8 months (7.03–17.74) compared to those not receiving late ET at 7 months (4.17–9.10) (p = 0.0116). late ET [0.73(0.56–0.93), p = 0.0121]. Patients receiving late HER2-targeted therapy had a significantly longer mOS at 20 months [Range 15.08–27.17 months, 0.57(0.44–0.75), p < 0.0001, compared to those who did not at 5.5 months (3.91–7.52) (p = 0.0116). Patients receiving capecitabine had a significantly longer mOS at 11.9 months (9.1–18.4) compared to those receiving other chemotherapy agents at 5.3 months (3.6–7.7) (p = 0.0035). Also, capecitabine [0.70(0.56–0.89), p = 0.0037], had a lower risk of death compared to their counterparts who did not receive these treatments.

Local Brain metastasis treatment played an important role in the outcome as well. Patients receiving BM surgery had significantly longer mOS at 27.2 months (13.7–34.3) compared to those who did not receive surgery at 6.1 months (4.2–8.4) (p < 0.0001). A higher number of BM correlated with worse survival. Compared to patients having a single BM, patients having 2–8 and > 9 BM were 1.40 times (1.03–1.90, p = 0.0317) and 2.33 times (1.74–3.13, p < 0.0001) more likely to die. Among the various BM radiotherapies received, patients receiving SRS achieved the highest mOS at 19.2 months (11.4–24.5) followed by patients receiving SRS + WBRT at 15.3 months (8.9–20.8). Patients receiving no radiotherapy or WBRT alone had the worst mOS at 1.5 months (0.8–2.2) and 3.8 months (2.8–5.3), respectively (p < 0.0001). Compared to patients receiving no RT, patients receiving SRS had a 71% lower risk of death [0.29(0.20–0.42), p < 0.0001], patients receiving SRS + WBRT had a 61% lower risk of death [0.39(0.26–0.58), p < 0.0001], and patients receiving WBRT had a 30% lower risk of death [0.70(0.49–1.00, p = 0.0469].

Other variables that were not found to be of significant association with survival include grade, stage, late chemotherapy, and BM size. Discussion

In this retrospective analysis, we identified several factors associated with mOS in patients with BCBM including age, performance status, BC subtype as well as the number of ECM, number of BM, and location of BM. Both systemic therapies including the use of HER2-targeted therapy, TKIs, ET and capecitabine as well as local therapies with, BM surgery, and BM radiotherapy played an important role in influencing outcome.

These findings are consistent with validated diagnosis-specific tools that estimate survival in patients with brain metastases from solid tumors, including the Graded Prognostic Assessment Index (GPA) [22, 23]. The GPA has a specific version for breast cancer, Breast-GPA. It provides a median overall survival estimate from the time of brain metastasis diagnosis and has demonstrated consistent prognostic value across large national cohorts [17, 18, 23]. The Breast-GPA incorporates age, KPS, receptor subtype, number of brain metastases, and extracranial metastases [24]. Beyond prognosis, it offers an eligibility quotient that estimates the probability of 12-month survival, helping guide clinical trial inclusion and treatment planning [18]. While the Breast-GPA is helpful for initial stratification, our study suggests that certain therapies such as HER2-targeted therapies and capecitabine may be additional predictors of survival. This highlights the potential value of dynamic prognostic models that account for treatment exposure over time to provide more clinically relevant survival estimates.

This analysis showed mOS significantly decreased with older age, with patients > 60 years having the worst survival. Numerous studies support these findings highlighting worse survival with increasing age in BCBM patients [25–27]. Moreover, many previous reports have similarly endorsed the use of KPS as a tool to assess prognosis of BCBM, identifying that patients with longer survival have KPS scores ≥ 70 [28, 29], which supports our results indicating KPS as an important prognostic tool with KPS of 80–100 conferring improved survival compared to lower values.

When evaluating the impact of the type of breast surgery upon diagnosis on BCBM prognosis, data is scarce. However, a retrospective study suggested a longer mOS with modified radical mastectomy (MRM) (7.5 months) versus breast-conserving surgery (BCS) (3.5 months), though not statistically significant [30]. In our analysis patients who underwent MRM had a statistically significant longer mOS (9.1 months) after developing BCBM compared to BCS (6 months). However, this warrants further evaluation, as it could not be readily explained by the stage at presentation (33% had stage III).

In this analysis, survival in BCBM based on BC subtype is also consistent with prior studies showing that the triple positive group achieved the best and the TN group achieved the worst survival [25, 31–34]. One study found the best survival rate in HR(+)/HER2(+) at 20 months and the worst in HR(-)/HER2(-) at 5 months, which is consistent with our findings [26].

When assessing the findings related to the disease burden, our date is supported by a similar trend in other reports suggesting that mOS is associated with the number of ECM: ≤ 1 ECM (12 months), 2 ECM (9 months), and 3 ECM (4 months) versus 12.52 months for ≤ 1 ECM and 6.72 months for ≥ 2 ECM in our study [26]. Furthermore, a systematic review of six research studies found that a higher number of brain lesions was associated with worse survival which supports our findings of decreased survival with more BM [35].

The findings in this report are also consistent with the literature on the significant impact of local brain treatment on outcome. We highlighted a survival benefit for BM resection with a mOS of 27.7 months compared to 6.1 months in patients not undergoing surgery. This finding is supported by the literature indicating a longer survival after BCBM resection at around 28 months [36] and could be related to disease burden and performance since those who undergo BM resection typically have fewer lesions and can tolerate surgery, contributing to better survival.

Historically, patients with BM were treated with WBRT or surgical resection. However, concerns about WBRT-induced neurocognitive decline led to SRS/GKRS emerging as an alternative for limited BM [37]. Our analysis showed a promising survival benefit for SRS alone or SRS + WBRT, with no significant differences between the two arms (19.2 vs. 15.3 months). This aligns with a meta-analysis that found no significant survival difference [38–40], supporting the use of SRS alone in BCBM. However, it is important to note that the benefit derived from SRS could also be due to the limited toxicity associated with this technique compared to WBRT. The beneficial effects are, therefore, likely associated with a better quality of life; however, there could be a potential higher risk of distant brain recurrence compared to WBRT in selected cases [39, 40].

The type of systemic therapy plays an important role in BCBM outcome. Capecitabine is used across all breast cancer subtypes, but its role varies by molecular profile [41]. In HER2-positive disease, it appears to be most effective when combined with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), such as lapatinib or tucatinib. In TNBC, capecitabine remains one of the few active systemic agents with demonstrated CNS activity [16]. In hormone receptor–positive/HER2-negative (HR+/HER2–) disease, it is typically reserved for later lines of therapy following progression on endocrine and targeted treatments [41]. In this analysis, patients who received capecitabine had an improved overall survival compared to those who did not receive it, including the patients with triple-positive (TP) disease and HER2-positive disease. This aligns with prior reports demonstrating that capecitabine is used across all subtypes with varying degrees of efficacy [41].

HER2-targeted therapy was associated with improved survival in our BCBM cohort, nearly quadrupling survival after BCBM (20 vs. 5.5 months). These findings align with the evidence behind improved outcomes in neoadjuvant, adjuvant, and metastatic HER2 + BC [42, 43]. For patients with HER2 + BCBM, HER2-targeted treatment with monoclonal antibodies likely prolongs survival by controlling extracranial disease, as the antibodies’ size likely impedes crossing the BBB [44]. As such, the CLEOPATRA trial demonstrated a delay in the CNS progression with pertuzumab compared to placebo (median 15.0 vs. 11.9 months) [45]. However, more recent studies have reported both improved PFS and intracranial response in HER2 positive BCBM patients treated with novel agents like the ADCs, including Trastuzumab Deruxtecan [45, 46] as well as TKIS. This was apparent in the DESTINY-Breast 03 trial (2022) that demonstrated superiority of Trastuzumab derutexcan in patinets with HER-2 positive metastatic disease [47] and shifting it to be the standard of care in the second line [48]. In the phase 3b/4 DESTINY- Breast 12 study, Trastuzumab Derutexcan in HER2 positive metastatic breast cancer with or without brain metastases demonstrated an intracranial objective response rate of 71.7% with 12-month CNS progression-free survival rates of approximately 59% rendering it a highly active systemic option of HER 2-positive BCBM [49]. Similarly, patients with brain metastases showed a significantly longer median progression free survival of 15 months compared to 3 months with trastuzumab emtansine [47].

TKIs significantly improved survival in our cohort, increasing it nearly fivefold (25.2 vs. 6 months), consistent with multiple studies on their role in BCBM management [50]. In an updated analysis of the HER2CLIMB trial, tucatinib with trastuzumab and capecitabine improved overall survival while reducing the risk of new brain lesions (21.6 vs. 12.5 months) [51]. Another TKI, lapatinib, in combination with capecitabine, was found to have an objective CNS response in 66% of patients and a median time to progression of 5.5 months [52]. Additionally, the phase III NALA trial demonstrated improved progression-free survival with neratinib and capecitabine compared to lapatinib and capecitabine [53]. These findings underscore the importance of combination therapy with BBB-crossing potential, particularly TKIs, in treating and potentially preventing BCBM in high-risk patients for future research considerations.

Endocrine therapy continues to be a cornerstone of treatment for patients with BCBM and hormone receptor (HR) positive tumors. The addition of CDK4/6 inhibitors have become the standard of care in combination with Endocrine Therapy (ET) for HR+/HER2 − locally advanced and metastatic BC, but few studies have included patients with BCBM [54]. One of the CDK4/6 inhibitors, abemaciclib, has shown potential to cross the blood brain barrier (BBB) [55], with a phase II study of abemaciclib in heavily pretreated HER2 + BCBM patients indicating an intracranial clinical benefit rate of 24% despite not meeting the primary endpoint an intracranial objective response rate of 5.2% [56]. The intracranial clinical benefit rate in that study was assessed using the Response Assessment in Neuro-Oncology for Brain metastasis and Leptomeninges. This includes either complete response, partial response or stable disease for more than 6 months [56]. In addition to ET, ADCS have also shown promise in endocrine-resistant HR+/HER2- tumors with sacituzumab govitecan showing significant improvement to mOS (14.4 months) when compared with traditional chemotherapy (11.2 months) in the phase 3 TROPiCS-02 trial. This therapy may be used in the 2nd line setting for unresectable or metastatic Hormone Receptor positive/HER2-negative disease or beyond the second line [57]. In the subsequent Destiny breast 04 trial, Trastuzumab deruxtecan in patients with HER2-Low metastatic or unresectable breast cancer showed a longer median progression free survival of 10.1 months compared to 5.4 months for patients using physician’s choice of chemotherapy [58]. Unfortunately, it does not report CNS-specific outcomes.

Other promising novel agents include PARP inhibitors, which are FDA approved for germline BRCA-positive BC patients. The OlympiaAD trial in 2017 demonstrated improved mOS in BRCA positive metastatic BC patients treated with agents such as Olaparib (19.3 months) in comparison to traditional chemotherapy (17.3 months) particularly in HER-2 negative, Hormone Receptor-positive and Triple negative disease [59]. However, their role and efficacy in BCBM remains under investigation by clinical trials. For example, a recent phase I trial highlighted the efficacy and safety of veliparib plus WBRT for patients with BCBM, with a further phase II trial undergoing [60].

This analysis has several strengths. It provides a detailed analysis on the benefit of various targeted novel therapies in BCBM and suggests new dynamic variables and treatment modalities to be incorporated to prognostication tools. This is one of the few studies to provide a detailed real-world analysis of the survival benefit associated with various targeted therapies in patients with breast cancer brain metastases (BCBM). It captures modern treatment patterns across a range of clinical scenarios and reflects outcomes in a post-trial, routine-care setting. Importantly, our findings extend beyond the scope of existing baseline prognostic models, such as the Breast-GPA. These results underscore the potential value of evolving current models into dynamic, treatment-informed frameworks that account for the impact of therapy received throughout the disease course, rather than relying solely on prognostication from day one.

The main limitation of this study is its retrospective nature, which restricts control over known and unknown confounding variables. An example is the association between surgery/SRS and improved overall survival, which remains valid based on our multivariate regression; however, it may be subject to some patient selection bias, as patients selected for these treatments must be initially medically fit and able to tolerate the procedure. Additionally, the relatively small sample size hindered multivariate Cox regression and subgroup analyses. Moreover, the focus on hospitals within the same enterprise with similar practices limits the generalizability of the findings regarding these agents. It is worth noting that our cohort’s study period spanned from 2010 to 2020. This could also limit the assessment of emerging modern therapies previously discussed in the manuscript, including trastuzumab deruxtecan, tucatinib, and sacituzumab govitecan. As such, our cohort likely underrepresents the survival improvements now achievable for patients with BCBM with post-2020 treatments. However, the data that provided holds a valuable historical benchmark for survival outcomes and treatment options before the widespread adoption of modern therapies.

Conclusion

BCBM is a heterogeneous population with variable outcomes. This analysis suggests that several factors influence the development and outcome after BCBM with HR+/HER2- subgroup having the longest median time to develop BM compared to other subgroups. Our results support updating current prognostic models, such as the Breast-GPA, by integrating treatment exposures, shifting from static baseline scores to dynamic, treatment-informed prognostication.
