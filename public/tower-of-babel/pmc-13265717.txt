Biomechanical assessment of manual handling to enhance workplace safety based on gait and center of mass analysis
Authors: Amit M Adhaye, Dhananjay A Jolhe, Lushank Shambharkar, Nandagopal Kailiappan, Ashutosh D Bagde, Irshad Qureshi, Pratik Phansopkar, K Kamakshi Priya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractManual material handling (MMH) in informal sectors is a significant contributor to work-related musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs), often resulting from unstable load carriage and poor ergonomic practices. This study investigates the biomechanical impact of traditional load-carrying techniques - on the shoulder (CoS), on the back (CoB) - in comparison with a newly designed ergonomic backpack (CoBp), focusing on gait stability, center of mass (COM) dynamics, and lower limb kinematics. Fifteen healthy male participants (mean age: 23.4 ± 1.6 years) performed gait trials under seven load conditions, while spatiotemporal, kinematic, and electromyographic (EMG) data were collected using inertial sensors and wireless surface EMG. Results revealed that the CoBp configuration significantly reduced COM displacement in both horizontal and vertical directions, with a 33% decrease in lateral COM excursion compared to CoB_20 (from 55.2 mm to 37.1 mm) and a 20% reduction in COM height (from 0.91 m to 0.73 m). Additionally, cadence increased from 107.2 steps/min in CoS_20 to 113.1 steps/min in CoBp_20, approaching the normal walking cadence of 111.0 steps/min. Double support phase, a key indicator of stability, was minimized in CoBp_20 (12.4%) compared to CoB_20 (18.2%). Joint kinematics showed reduced range of motion (ROM) at the hip and knee under CoBp, while EMG data indicated a more balanced muscle activation pattern with less asymmetry between the left and right lower limbs. These findings highlight that the proposed backpack design enhances dynamic stability, improves load symmetry, and minimizes lower limb strain during loaded gait. The study establishes CoBp as a biomechanically superior alternative for MMH, with implications for ergonomic interventions across occupational settings.Keywords: Center of mass, Gait analysis, EMG, Carrying on backpack evaluationSubject terms: Engineering, Mechanical engineering

Introduction

Work-related musculoskeletal disorders (WMSDs) pose a major concern in manual material handling (MMH) tasks, driven by improper postures, excessive loads, and repetitive movements1. These disorders, which impact millions of workers globally, are the second most common cause of disability worldwide, contributing significantly to both health-related costs5 and decreased productivity, as reported in numerous studies2. The relationship between WMSDs and worker productivity has been extensively investigated, with research by Manjunath et al.3, and Ray et al.4 underscoring the detrimental effects of WMSDs on workplace efficiency and overall well-being. Waters et al.6 specifically highlighted how load carriage affects the cervical spine, while Sanders and McCormick7 focused on the influence of load size, shape, and coupling mechanisms on stress accumulation in the musculoskeletal system. Moreover, the studies conducted by Harman et al.8, Knapik et al.9,10, and Legg et al.11 have shed light on the biomechanical implications of different load-carrying methods, such as backpack, anterior, and shoulder carrying12. However, these research focuses on special case of material handling (i.e. military backpack handling) and considers specific group of gait parameters along with muscle groups. The military backpack handling has differences in terms of posture and load carried with respect to task under consideration. The present study tries to evaluate newly developed backpack for the material handling activity. The research issues in the present work emanates from the unique and widespread nature of the MMH activity chosen for the study. The relevance of material handling postures and activities to WMSD development is particularly evident in sectors such as agriculture, construction, logistics, and the food supply chain. These industries heavily rely on manual labor, with workers frequently exposed to biomechanical strain due to awkward postures, extended handling durations, and load instability. Recognizing this, several researchers have explored the use of assistive technologies, including upper-body13–15 and lower-extremity exoskeletons16–18, as potential interventions to mitigate WMSD risks. Back-support exoskeletons have gained attention for their ability to redistribute spinal loads and reduce back pain, improving both ergonomic conditions and worker safety during manual handling tasks19. The backpack for the experimentation in the current study were developed considering the most prevalent body regions prone to musculoskeletal disorders as identified in Adhaye and Jolhe20 for the similar task.

Gait analysis helps identify potential deficiencies in movement patterns and highlights areas of the body that are subjected to excessive loading, leading to a higher risk of injury. Researchers like Whittle21 and Kaufman & Sutherland22 have established frameworks for identifying normal and abnormal gait patterns, serving as a basis for assessing deviations that may result in musculoskeletal injuries to workers. Gait pattern, characterized by multiple gait parameters23, is affected by many factors, such as walking speed24–26, weight being handled27–29, load and fatigue30, hip contact forces31,32, carrying methods33,34 with each playing a role in the biomechanics of movement. For instance, increased anterior pelvic tilt (APT)35, often caused by load-induced postural changes, can lead to hip joint flexion, knee hyperextension, and plantar flexion of the feet36. Such adaptations, including lumbar lordosis35, which is essential for maintaining sagittal balance37, are closely linked to gait abnormalities including tight hip flexors, weak gluteal muscles, and poor postural habits38,39 and the risk of musculoskeletal strain. Recent advances, including gait pattern recognition and machine-learning–based approaches, have improved the ability to detect subtle gait variations across different populations and task conditions, reinforcing the relevance of gait analysis in both clinical and occupational biomechanics40,41. The present study examines the emergence of the aforementioned deviations and establishes criteria for assessing the risk associated with manual material handling.

The metabolic cost of walking, another important consideration in MMH tasks, is directly related to the movement of the body’s center of mass (COM). When gait kinematics alter the path of the COM, energy expenditure can increase significantly, compromising the worker’s endurance and efficiency42–44. Postural adaptations that shift the COM anteriorly or posteriorly can further destabilize gait, resulting in energy inefficiencies and increased biomechanical burdens. For instance, a wider base of support during walking may lead to greater side-to-side COM excursions, which not only impacts energy efficiency but also raises the risk of musculoskeletal injuries33. Accurately measuring the COM provides valuable insights into worker stability and balance during material handling, enabling the identification of risky postures and movements that could lead to accidents or injury. Gait stability needs to be evaluated based on the three types of equilibrium viz., stable, unstable and neutral. Stable equilibrium occurs when an object is placed in such a fashion that an effort to disturb it would require its centre of gravity (COG) to be raised42–44. Therefore, the first criteria to assess stability is the height of centre of mass (COM) above the base of support. The COM represents the point where the body’s mass is concentrated, and any shifts or deviations from the optimal position can compromise stability and increase the risk of falls or injuries. Precise measurement and analysis of the COM enable researchers to identify specific actions or postures that may lead to an increased risk of accidents or musculoskeletal disorders. Another important terminology in stability assessment is base of support (BoS). The BoS includes area enclosed by the part of a body in contact with the supporting surface.

Given the substantial biomechanical demands associated with MMH tasks, researchers have increasingly focused on optimizing load-carrying strategies to reduce WMSD risks. In our earlier study20, we have identified five major risk factors for the same task, as (A) footwear type, (B) method of carrying the load, (C) load handled, (D) slope of the walking surface, and (E) speed of walking were responsible for developing MSDs in workers. The effects of individual factors on gait parameters and factor optimization demonstrated the key parameters and need of backpack interventions to minimize the MSDs in workers45. The present study examines the effects of two key factors viz., load carrying method and load handled on gait patterns during specific MMH tasks. By employing a comprehensive biomechanical assessment, including lower extremity gait analysis, center of mass (COM) measurement, joint kinematics, and electromyography (EMG) assessments of target muscle groups, this study aims to evaluate the efficacy of backpack design as a potential intervention to enhance gait safety and reduce injury risks. Specifically, the research compares the impact of carrying loads using a backpack (with a handle) against more traditional carrying methods, such as on the back (CoB) and on the shoulder (CoS). The study’s findings offer new insights into how different load-carrying methods influence postural stability, muscle engagement, and overall worker safety.

In the following sections, we provide a detailed description of the study’s methodologies, findings, and their potential implications for improving manual handling practices in industries where MMH tasks are prevalent.

Method and experimental procedure

Fifteen healthy male subjects (coded as S1 to S15) involved in material handling other than warehouse with the demographic data shown in Table 1 were chosen for the study. As stated earlier, two methods of carrying the load i.e., CoB and CoS are adopted by workers at the warehouse. The third carrying method i.e., carrying on backpack (CoBp) was introduced as an interventional measure. In this study, CoBp was compared against CoB and CoS to evaluate the efficacy of specially designed backpack in minimizing the risk of handling. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee at Datta Meghe Institute of Higher Education and Research Wardha, India, under reference number DMIMS(DU)/IEC/2023/1077, dated 01/07/2023. Further, the written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study, along with permission for the publication of the study findings. All participants were informed of the study’s purpose, procedures, potential risks, and their right to withdraw at any point. The participants were allowed to get comfortable in treadmill walking over a week (daily 15–30 min per subject). The Vega 201 model treadmill with computerized control manufactured and distributed by Recorders & Medicare Systems (RMS) Pvt. Ltd. India was used for trials.

In current study, the trials were aimed to examine quantitatively the effect of various carrying methods and loads. The subjects were asked to walk on a treadmill running at zero slope and constant speed. The set of trials includes total seven tasks (1) normal walking, (2) CoB with 10 kg (CoB_10), (3) CoB with 20 kg (CoB_20), (4) CoS with 10 kg (CoS_10), (5) CoS with 20 kg (CoS_20), (6) CoBp with 10 kg (CoBp_10), and (7) CoBp with 20 kg (CoBp_20). Lower extremity kinematics including spatiotemporal parameters such as cadence (Y1), ⁒ double support (Y2), average gait cycle duration (Y3), pelvic symmetry ⁒ (Y4), and various joint angles such as pelvic tilt (Y5), pelvic obliquity (Y6), pelvic rotation (Y7), hip flexion-extension (Y8), hip adduction-abduction (Y9), hip rotation (Y10), knee flexion-extension (Y11), knee adduction-abduction (Y12), ankle dorsiflexion-plantarflexion (Y13), ankle eversion-inversion (Y14), and ankle rotation (Y15) were captured using 7- inertial sensors-based iSen systems. Electromyography (EMG) profiles of lower extremity muscles i.e., quadriceps, hamstrings, ankle dorsiflexors and gastrocnemius were recorded using wireless EMG system (Delsys system, USA).

The newly designed backpack (CoBp) was developed with the objective of improving load distribution and minimizing asymmetric loading during walking. Unlike conventional backpack (CoB) and side-carrying (CoS) configurations, the CoBp design promotes a more centralized and balanced positioning of the carried load relative to the wearer’s trunk. The design concept emphasizes redistribution of load closer to the body’s center of mass and reduction of lateral and posterior load offsets, which are known to influence gait stability and musculoskeletal loading.

The external geometry and functional layout of the backpack were finalized following iterative ergonomic considerations and are protected under a registered industrial design. Detailed construction features and internal mechanisms are the subject of an ongoing patent application and are therefore not disclosed in this manuscript. However, the design principles outlined above are sufficient to support biomechanical interpretation of the experimental findings and to differentiate the CoBp configuration from conventional load-carrying methods evaluated in this study.

Data collection

The lower extremity kinematics, i.e., 15 gait parameters were captured using sensors-based gait measurement system (iSen model, STT systems, San Sebastián, Spain). iSen provides specific protocol for placing the sensors on body landmarks. Though iSen assumes that the transformation between the sensor local reference frame and the body segment reference frame remains constant throughout the recording, we adopted the following guidelines provided by the original equipment manufacturer (OEM) i.e., STT systems. (i) Choose a landmark such that the relative motion between sensor and bone is minimized. (ii) The landmark to be chosen on –(a) flat, harder regions, (b) regions with a smaller muscular mass, and (c) regions where skin-folds throughout a joint range of motion have a smaller impact.

The seven inertial sensors, ELECTRON, FAX, GEO, HELIO, INDIE, JAVA and KARENAI, were mounted respectively on the body landmarks - sacrum, right thigh, left thigh, right leg, left leg, right foot and left foot, using straps as per the lower body gait protocol specified for iSen software. The sensor placement as per the protocol36,37 is depicted in Fig. 1 (a).

Sensor placement on body regions.

iSen software provides the provision for global calibration, which ensures that all the sensors are set at the proper positions. For global calibration, iSen uses a global reference frame which is an external reference frame common to all sensors. It is typically determined by the vertical axis (accelerometer output while sensors are still) and a certain heading. During the experiment, the IMU sensors were arranged with arrow head pointing vertically upward. Because of the movement of subjects, drifts in the sensors are likely to take place. This drift was detected and eliminated by two ways- (i) by performing global calibration at the start of each trial, and (ii) by comparing the silhouette video (which is obtained from the sensor data) with normal video of walking. The iSen software has capability to identify the initial support and toe-off events in the recording which determines the gait cycle. The accuracy of iSen system is among the best of its kind: Pitch/roll < 0.5 degrees (Root Mean Square (RMS)) and Heading < 2.0 degrees (RMS).

It should be noted that trials for task 1 i.e., normal walking consisting of walking without load at self-paced speed. The participants were asked to walk with self-pace on the treadmill to measured their normal walking speed. The normal walking speed of 15 participants recorded as 0.98 ± 0.059 m/s. Based on the mean value the constant speed of treadmill is selected as 0.98 m/s during remaining six trials. Multiple trials under each seven tasks were performed by the 15 workers. Each trial was recorded for 30 s giving 20 gait cycles approximately. The iSen software produced the reports for each trial individually. The trials captured the following data for each subject: (1) average values of four spatio-temporal gait parameters (Y1, to Y4) (2) values of the 11 joint angles (Y5 to Y15) and EMG profiles for the four muscles as normalized time series, and (3) values of ROM of the 11 joint angles. The normalized time series data of 11 joint angles for 15 participants in seven trials were exported and tabulated in spreadsheet. The average of 20 normalized time series (for 20 gait cycle in each trial) is obtained for inter-task comparisons. Further, these averaged normalized time series data for seven trials were plotted for 15 subjects separately. Wireless surface electrodes were placed on the dominant (right) leg over the following muscle groups: quadriceps, hamstrings, ankle dorsiflexors, and gastrocnemius, in accordance with standard anatomical landmarks, as shown in Fig. 1 (b). Prior to electrode placement, the skin was shaved, lightly abraded, and cleansed with alcohol to minimize skin–electrode impedance. All EMG signals were visually inspected to ensure signal quality before further analysis. The EMG signals recorded during the trials consisted of bipolar waveforms containing both positive and negative values, which are not directly suitable for quantitative comparison. Therefore, full-wave rectification was applied by taking the absolute value of the signal. The gait signals were sampled at 100 Hz. A fourth-order low-pass Butterworth filter with a cutoff frequency of 3 Hz was applied to remove high-frequency noise while preserving relevant gait dynamics. The cutoff frequency was selected based on previous gait biomechanics studies indicating that the dominant frequency components of walking typically occur below 5 Hz. Additionally, FFT analysis was performed during preliminary signal inspection to confirm that the primary spectral energy of the recorded signals was concentrated below 3 Hz, supporting the chosen filtering parameters. For normalization, the root mean square (RMS) value of the EMG signal was computed and used to normalize the maximum signal amplitude across all trials. This normalization procedure reduced inter-subject variability and enabled reliable inter-subject and inter-task comparisons.

Data analysis

The one-way ANOVA and Tukey HSD (Honestly Significant Difference) post hoc test are used for evaluating the difference between the tasks. The significance was achieved with p < 0.05. The ANOVA test tells whether there is a difference in the means of three or more sets of data, but does not specify the pair(s) of data that causes the difference. Tukey’s HSD test specifies the pair(s) of data that causes the difference in the means identified from an ANOVA Test. A repeated-measures ANOVA with Bonferroni-adjusted post-hoc tests was used to compare COM height and distance across carrying methods. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Time-normalized kinematic and electromyographic waveforms (0–100% of the gait cycle) were analyzed using Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM). A one-way repeated-measures SPM analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to examine condition-dependent differences across the gait cycle. When significant main effects were detected, post-hoc paired SPM t-tests were performed. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Methodology of stability assessment

The location of centre of the base of support (CBoS) was obtained based on the guidelines by Hamilton et al.43; Alamoudi et al.33; Krkeljas and Moss46. Figure 2 depicts CBoS for various positions of feet obtained by adopting these guidelines. Another criterion for stability of the human body is the distance between the line of gravity (LOG) and the CBoS. For both the criteria, lesser the height (or distance) more is the stability. The LOG is a vertical line passing through the COG/COM and perpendicular to ground when the object in stable equilibrium. In unstable equilibrium or during motion, the centre of mass (COM) is taken as the starting point for LOG.

Guidelines for locating the centre of base of support (CBoS)33,43.

In this study, we draw a vertical line L1 through CBoS to measure the horizontal excursion of COM. Drawing the LOG for an unbalanced posture requires an understanding of the misalignments and compensations present in the body. The normal walking comprises of adjusting the body position for the equilibrium consciously or unconsciously43. The excursion of COM from vertical line L1 varies linearly with the load handled. An object retains its equilibrium only as long as its LOG falls within its BoS. When the LOG falls outside the BoS, then a reaction is needed in order to stay balanced.

In (a), (b), (c), and (f) the weight is supported by the feet; in (d) the weight is supported by the forehead and hands during a headstand; in (e) the weight is supported by the hands and feet while the body is in a squat position. Circled crosses indicate point of intersection of LOG with BoS.

In this study, the location of COM for 15 subjects in three postures were obtained using a validated technique adapted from Ismail47. The images were captured using a high-resolution digital camera mounted on a fixed tripod positioned perpendicular to the plane of motion at a distance of 0.6 m. The camera captured images at a resolution of 1280 × 1024 pixels and a frame rate of 60 FPS. Prior to data collection, spatial calibration was performed using a cross-calibration wand of known dimension placed within the field of view. Reflective marker points were placed on predefined anatomical landmarks corresponding to major body segments, following standard biomechanical conventions. The captured images were processed using Maxtraq 2D software, involving grayscale conversion, thresholding, and segmentation to isolate the subject from the background. The center of mass was then computed based on pixel intensity distribution and segment weighting as described by Ismail (2016). The method of COM calculation involves use of image processing to (a) locate all joint points and lines connecting them, (b) the product of reference COM ⁒ for each body segment and respective length locates the COM of each body segment (Xi, Yi), (c) the mass of each segment (mi) from total mass of subject is calculated using the standard mass of segments by Clauser et al.48 and Hari Krishnan et al.33. (d) Finally, the summation of product of mass and COM of each segment divided by total mass of subject gives the location of COM, as shown in Eq. 1.

Results

The experimentation discussed in previous section evaluates the effect of backpack as an interventional measure to minimize risk. The study was primarily focused on the variations in spatio-temporal, kinematic and EMG gait parameters in view of analyzing the gait stability.

The generalised observations based on the stability analysis are: The height of COM from ground (h) is inversely proportional to the stability of body. A one-way repeated-measures ANOVA revealed a significant effect of condition on center of mass height, F(2, 28) = 11.32, p = 0.0002, η²ₚ = 0.45. Bonferroni-adjusted post-hoc comparisons showed that COM height was significantly reduced in the CoBp condition compared with CoS (p < 0.001), while no significant differences were observed between CoS and CoB (p = 0.336) or between CoBp and CoB (p = 0.061), as illustrated in Fig. 4. It is observed from figure, that ‘h’ is least for CoBp. The mean h during CoS is higher than other carrying methods. The h is higher at toe off as compared to midstance. This signifies that stability at midstance is higher than toe off. In a few cases, the h for CoB is lower than CoBp. These suggests that workers are excessively bending forward due to weight on their back. This excessive bending for long time generates stresses in the lower back. According to existing literature, working with the hands positioned above shoulder level poses a greater risk compared to tasks performed with the hands below shoulder height. Among the evaluated configurations, only the CoBp setup maintains hand positioning consistently below shoulder level.

The horizontal distance (d) between the COM and L1, representing anterior-posterior deviation, was also assessed (Fig. 5). Similarly, The repeated-measures ANOVA revealed no significant main effect of condition on the distance between the center of mass and the line of gravity, F(2, 28) = 1.29, p = 0.291, η²ₚ = 0.08. However, Bonferroni-adjusted post-hoc analyses indicated that the distance was significantly smaller in the CoS condition compared with both CoBp (p < 0.001) and CoB (p < 0.001), while no significant difference was observed between CoBp and CoB (p = 0.057) (Fig. 5). Results showed that CoB had the largest average d, indicating greater instability, while CoS demonstrated the smallest d, likely due to lateral compensation. However, the apparent sagittal plane stability in CoS is misleading, as the COM in this case shifts significantly toward the shoulder bearing the load. This highlights the necessity of incorporating coronal plane analysis for a comprehensive understanding of stability in asymmetrical load conditions like CoS. The distance d is larger in midstance than that of toe off. This signifies the instability and the tendency of roll-on to next gait phase resulting in forward travel of body.

The statistically significant reductions in both COM height and horizontal displacement indicate a more centrally aligned load–body configuration when using the backpack carrying (CoBp) method. From a biomechanical perspective, such reductions are associated with improved postural stability and reduced moment demands on the lower extremities during manual material handling tasks.

The two phases in gait cycle i.e., stance and swing are characterized by foot strike (initial contact) and foot-off (toe-off) events. The gait of an individual can be assessed considering three major categories of parameters, viz., spatio-temporal (cadence, double support ⁒, etc.,), kinematic (various joint angles), and EMG profiles of selected muscles. The following subsections discuss about the spatio-temporal assessment, kinematic assessment and EMG assessment.

With respect to CoBp, pelvic symmetry % increase significantly in CoS (p < 0.001). These findings imply that the CoBp method promotes a gait profile more consistent with natural, unloaded walking, especially in terms of cadence and pelvic symmetry. Conversely, CoS and CoB introduce asymmetries and compensations that may increase musculoskeletal strain over time.

Kinematic assessment

Kinematic gait parameters describe the extent, speed, and direction of movement of joints or body segments. The excessive movement of the joints at pelvis, hip, knee and ankle in various planes disturbs the normal gait. Joint range of motion (ROM) characteristics of the pelvis, hip, knee, and ankle were evaluated to assess the biomechanical effects of different load-carrying configurations. Time-normalized kinematic and electromyographic waveforms (0–100% gait cycle) were analyzed using one-way repeated-measures Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM). The analysis identified condition-dependent differences in selected lower-limb kinematic and EMG variables, while no significant differences were observed for the remaining variables. The SPM repeated-measures ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of load-carrying condition on knee flexion during the stance phase of gait (p < 0.05, Fig. 6g). Post-hoc SPM comparisons indicated that the CoBp condition exhibited greater knee flexion than the CoS condition during mid-stance, whereas no significant differences were observed between CoBp and CoB.

A significant effect of condition was also observed for ankle dorsiflexion during late stance (p < 0.05; Fig. 6i). The SPM analysis demonstrated altered dorsiflexion patterns across conditions, suggesting condition-specific modulation of ankle mechanics during the propulsion phase of gait. No statistically significant SPM clusters were detected for pelvic or hip kinematic variables, indicating that these segments were comparatively unaffected by the load-carrying configurations examined.

Figure 6 illustrates the ensemble-averaged pelvic, hip, knee, and ankle kinematic waveforms across load-carrying conditions in comparison with normative reference bands.

Pelvic tilt waveforms (Fig. 6a) for normal walking and CoBp largely remained within the normative range, whereas CoS and CoB demonstrated reduced pelvic tilt excursions across the gait cycle. Although these patterns suggest altered trunk–pelvis coordination under asymmetric and posterior load conditions, SPM analysis did not reveal statistically significant differences in pelvic tilt ROM across conditions (p > 0.05). Pelvic obliquity (Fig. 6b) and pelvic rotation (Fig. 6c) exhibited greater qualitative deviations under CoS and CoB conditions; however, these variations likewise did not reach statistical significance based on SPM inference.

Hip kinematic waveforms showed limited condition-dependent modulation. Hip rotation (Fig. 6d) and hip flexion–extension (Fig. 6e) trajectories generally followed normative patterns, with minor deviations observed during loading response and terminal stance–initial swing transitions. Hip adduction–abduction (Fig. 6f) exhibited minimal deviations across conditions, with CoBp showing the closest alignment to normative behavior. No significant SPM clusters were identified for hip ROM variables across the gait cycle (p > 0.05).

In contrast, knee flexion–extension waveforms (Fig. 6g) demonstrated statistically significant condition-related differences, with SPM identifying significant clusters primarily during the stance and pre-swing phases (p < 0.05). These differences were characterized by increased knee flexion under loaded conditions, particularly CoBp and CoS. Knee adduction–abduction patterns (Fig. 6h) showed qualitative deviations during mid-stance and mid-swing; however, these did not reach statistical significance.

At the ankle, plantarflexion–dorsiflexion ROM (Fig. 6i) exhibited significant SPM clusters during late stance and terminal swing (p < 0.05), indicating altered push-off mechanics under different load configurations. CoB showed increased plantarflexion, whereas CoS demonstrated reduced plantarflexion relative to normative patterns. Ankle frontal and transverse plane motions did not show statistically significant differences across conditions.

Overall, the SPM-based analysis indicates that load-related kinematic adaptations are predominantly expressed in sagittal-plane knee and ankle mechanics, whereas pelvic and hip kinematics remain comparatively preserved across load-carrying configurations.

Discussion

In this study, we explored a comprehensive approach to assess the biomechanical factors involved in manual handling, specifically focusing on backpack usage. In current study, we utilize COM measurement as a crucial parameter in evaluating the balance of postures during manual handling activities. A significant portion of metabolic energy is expended in moving the body’s center of mass (COM)49,50. The increase in energy expenditure (above baseline) while walking is typically due to: (1) changes in joint angles which disturbs path of the COM51, (2) postural adaptations leading to anterior-posterior excursion in COM46. (3) bigger BoS leading to large side-to-side excursions of the COM33. The results of the present study reveal that, the horizontal and vertical excursion of COM is largest in CoB and CoS respectively. The CoS requires largest BoS. It suggests that CoB and CoS tend to expend larger energy than CoBp. This discussion demonstrates that, the CoBp configuration minimized both (vertical as well as horizontal) components of COM motion, supporting its ergonomic advantage.

Range-of-Motion principle states that less ROM is most effective for low-effort (force and speed) and high-accuracy movements, while greater ROM favours maximum efforts related to speed and overall force production52. Thus, the lower ROM values of joint angles are preferable. The results revealed in the present study suggest significant difference between normal walking and CoBp. The differences between normal walking and CoB/CoS are found to be less prominent. Overall, the kinematic analysis supports the efficacy of the CoBp method in distributing load more symmetrically and minimizing risky joint movement, offering an ergonomically advantageous strategy for manual load carriage. The joint ROM for the flexion - extension angles are more and raising with the task increasing load, whereas the pelvic joint ROM is less and deviating slightly. As the load increases across tasks, the range of motion (ROM) for flexion-extension angles at major joints (hip, knee, ankle) increases, while the ROM at the pelvic joints remains comparatively lower and shows only slight deviations. This pattern suggests that the body compensates for the increased mechanical demands by amplifying movement in the lower limb joints, particularly in the sagittal plane, to maintain balance and forward propulsion52. However, the restricted pelvic ROM may limit the body’s ability to effectively distribute kinetic energy, leading to inefficient load transfer and increased muscular strain in the lower extremities53. Over time, such compensatory strategies can result in fatigue, reduced gait efficiency, and heightened risk of overuse injuries especially in the lumbar spine and knee joints due to repetitive overextension.

The present study employed Statistical Parametric Mapping to examine the effects of different load-carrying configurations on lower-limb kinematics and muscle activation during gait. The principal findings indicate that the backpack-based configuration (CoBp) induces distinct biomechanical adaptations at the knee, ankle, and plantar-flexor musculature, while proximal joint kinematics and lower-limb muscles remain largely unaffected. The observed increase in knee flexion during stance under the CoBp condition suggests a more compliant lower-limb strategy during weight acceptance, which may facilitate improved shock absorption when carrying external loads53. This adaptation contrasts with the CoS configuration, where reduced knee flexion may reflect a stiffer gait pattern and potentially higher joint loading.

Similarly, the condition-dependent modulation of ankle dorsiflexion during late stance highlights the role of the ankle in compensating for altered load distribution52. Enhanced dorsiflexion during propulsion may contribute to improved forward progression and mechanical efficiency when load is distributed closer to the body’s center of mass, as in the CoBp configuration. The significant increase in gastrocnemius activation during late stance under the CoBp condition provides further evidence of enhanced push-off mechanics54. The gastrocnemius plays a critical role in plantar-flexion and forward propulsion, and its increased activation suggests that the CoBp configuration promotes a more effective utilization of the ankle plantar-flexors during gait. In contrast, the reduced gastrocnemius activation observed in the CoB condition may indicate a compensatory strategy involving greater reliance on trunk musculature and altered posture, potentially increasing spinal loading demands54. Importantly, the absence of significant SPM effects in pelvic and hip kinematics, as well as in lower-limb muscles, suggests that the biomechanical adaptations associated with the CoBp configuration are localized rather than global. This localized adjustment may be advantageous, as it avoids widespread compensatory strategies that could elevate the risk of musculoskeletal strain. Overall, the SPM findings provide quantitative statistical support for the biomechanical advantages of the CoBp design, particularly in terms of knee compliance, ankle propulsion, and plantar-flexor engagement. These adaptations are consistent with improved gait efficiency and may contribute to a reduced risk of fatigue and musculoskeletal disorders during load carriage55.

Theoretical design optimization can be used for optimizing backpack design based on the findings from the computational analysis (REBA, stability, kinematic, EMG analysis). This framework could involve principles of biomechanics, ergonomics, and materials science. For instance, we could propose specific design parameters such as strap width, padding distribution, material flexibility, etc., and explain how these parameters can be adjusted to optimize load distribution and minimize stress on the shoulders and back. Using computational simulations, one could demonstrate how different design parameters impact the loads exerted on the shoulder and back. The practitioner could simulate various scenarios, such as adjusting strap angles, redistributing weight within the backpack, or modifying the backpack’s structure, and observe the resulting stress distributions. This iterative process could show how small design changes can lead to improvements in comfort and ergonomics56.

While the sample size of 15 may limit statistical power, care was taken to ensure participant diversity in key anthropometric and biomechanical traits, as much as possible within the scope of this phase. The study focused on gait, postural stability, and center-of-mass–related biomechanical parameters, which provide indirect indicators of musculoskeletal loading rather than direct measures of musculoskeletal disorder (MSD) risk or injury outcomes. Further, the experimental tasks were performed under controlled laboratory conditions, which may not fully capture the complexity, variability, and cumulative effects of real-world manual sustainable material handling activities57. The short-term assessment design also limits the ability to infer long-term adaptations, fatigue effects, or injury prevention outcomes associated with prolonged use of the proposed backpack design. Future studies incorporating larger and more diverse samples, field-based assessments, and longitudinal designs are therefore warranted to strengthen the evidence base and validate the practical implications of these findings. The findings serve as a foundation for further investigation, and future studies with larger, more diverse populations are planned to validate and expand upon these initial results. Further, we fully acknowledge that manual material handling in real-world settings involves complex movements, variable terrains, and spontaneous directional changes that a treadmill cannot fully replicate. This highlights the need for future studies involving overground walking and dynamic handling tasks to further enhance the ecological validity of the findings.

Finally, the principles and methodologies developed for optimizing backpack design could be applied to other contexts beyond warehouse sack carrying. For example, we could explore applications in outdoor recreation, military backpack, medical equipment, or even everyday bag carrying. By highlighting the versatility and broader implications of the approach, the researcher could demonstrate the potential impact of the work beyond the specific domain studied in the thesis.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive biomechanical assessment of manual material handling (MMH) tasks, with a particular focus on backpack usage as an ergonomic intervention. By evaluating the influence of key factors such as load handled, carrying method, and walking speed on lower extremity gait patterns and stability, we have gained valuable insights into how different load-carrying techniques affect worker safety and efficiency.

This study demonstrated that the stability offered by the backpack carrying method (CoBp) is significantly greater than that of both carrying on the back (CoB) and carrying on the shoulder (CoS). The gait analysis revealed that CoB and CoS are associated with increased range of motion (ROM) and higher forces exerted on specific body regions, notably the lumbar spine and hip joints. These factors elevate the risk of musculoskeletal injuries, especially when loads are carried for extended periods or under suboptimal conditions. The use of a specially designed backpack, as tested in this study for carrying grain bags, was found to reduce these biomechanical risks by lowering the body’s center of mass and minimizing deviations in gait kinematics. This suggests that the backpack design offers an effective solution for mitigating the physical strain associated with MMH tasks.

This has significant implications for industries that rely heavily on manual labor, such as agriculture, logistics, and manufacturing, where workers are routinely exposed to biomechanical stresses. The collective findings of this study contribute substantially to the fields of biomechanics and ergonomics, offering actionable insights for researchers, occupational health practitioners, and workplace safety engineers.
