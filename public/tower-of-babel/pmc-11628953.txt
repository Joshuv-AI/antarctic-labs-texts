Utilisation and trends of laboratory testing and point-of-care testing in primary care in Germany: an analysis of claims data
Authors: Robby Markwart, Josephine Storch, Jutta Bleidorn, Antje Freytag
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT

Introduction

General practitioners (GPs) serve as the early interface between patients and the healthcare system, emphasising health education, preventive measures and early diagnostic and therapeutic interventions.1 GPs employ laboratory tests for the identification and monitoring of diseases as well as for assessing treatment effectiveness.2,4 Many diagnostic and therapeutic decisions in primary care are based on laboratory test results, highlighting their importance for informed clinical decision-making.5,7

In Germany and many other countries, patients have their sample (eg, blood) taken in the GP practice and GPs can send the patient samples to external laboratories for analyses, referred to as ‘initiated laboratory services’ (ILS). ILS test results are usually available after 12 to 48 hours. Alternatively, GPs can perform laboratory tests within the GP practice, referred to as point-of-care testing. Point-of-care tests (POCTs) are designed to be user-friendly, do not require extensive laboratory equipment and typically provide results in under 15 min.8 9 In contrast to ILS, the results of POCTs are readily available, thereby, supporting decision-making at the point-of-care. Technology advancements resulted in an increasing number of POCTs available for GPs10 and diagnostic accuracy of many POCTs being not inferior to standard laboratory tests.11 The great majority of German GPs anticipate a rising significance of POC testing in the years ahead as they are useful diagnostic tools in primary care.12

Although laboratory testing plays a crucial role in primary care, little is known on its utilisation in German GP practices. This study aims to investigate the current use and time trends of laboratory testing in German GP practices with a particular focus on the role of POC testing. We further examined whether particular characteristic of GPs and patients was associated with an increased use of POC testing instead of ILS.

Methods

This study was conducted and reported following the A Consensus German Reporting Standard for Secondary Data Analyses13 and Reporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected health Data14 recommendations.

Study design and data source

We conducted a retrospective consecutive cross-sectional study through an analysis of complete outpatient healthcare claims data (2017–2022) from GPs in Thuringia, a federal state in Germany. To pay outpatient doctors offering care to patients with statutory health insurance (SHI) in Germany, 17 regional Associations of Statutory Health Insurance Physicians gather claims data as per §295 Social Code Book V. These data include details of SHI physicians and patients, along with the medical services reimbursed. As of 2023, SHI covered 91% and 88% of the Thuringian and total German population, respectively.15 For this study, the Thuringian Association of Statutory Health Insurance Physicians provided pseudonymised claims data of reimbursed laboratory services in line with §75 Social Code Book V. The Thuringian Ministry of Labor, Social Affairs, Health, Women and Family authorised the transfer and analysis of this data for this study.

For this study, we analysed laboratory claims data from Thuringian GPs, distinguishing between (1) ILS (ie, samples sent to external laboratories) and (2) POCTs (ie, laboratory tests conducted within GP practices). Eligible were all GPs with at least one laboratory analysis in 2017–2022. The dataset comprised the following data: name and fee schedule item of the laboratory procedure, laboratory type (ILS vs self-performed as POCT), GP practice type (solo practice, group practice, ambulatory healthcare centre, other), community type of practice location (administratively independent city, urban district, rural district with densely populated areas, rural district; as defined by the Federal Institute for Research on Building, Urban Affairs and Spatial Development16), GP employment status (employed in GP practice, self-employed, other), GPs’ sex (female, male), GPs’ age, GPs’ work experience as an SHI-physician as well as patients’ sex and age. Unique pseudonymised identifiers were used to identify individual GPs, GP practices and patients.

Statistical analyses

Data cleaning of raw data was performed with ‘SAS’ V.9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina). We analysed (1) the total number laboratory analyses per year and (2) the number of laboratory analyses per patient and year. For this, we summarised fee schedule items covering the same laboratory analyses. Individual laboratory analyses were identified by the schedule items from chapter 32 of the Uniform Value Scale, which contains all laboratory analyses that are reimbursed by SHI. A specific item within the dataset, distinctly identified whether a laboratory analysis was performed by GPs within the practice (ie, POCT) or was an ILS. Individual patients were identified by unique combinations of age, gender and the pseudonymised health insurance numbers. ‘R’ (V.4.2.2) and R Studio (V.2022-12-03) were used for statistical analysis and data visualisation.17 18

In order to analyse whether GP and patient characteristics are associated with POCT use instead of ILS in 2022, we conducted univariate and multivariate two-level logistic regression analysis using the R package ‘Survey’.19 We defined individual GPs as clusters, because we assume that the odds of the outcome variable might differ from one cluster to another. The binomial dependent variable was the use of POCT versus ILS among laboratory analyses that can be performed either as POCT or ILS. The independent a priori defined variables included GP practice type, GP employment status, community type of practice location, GPs’ sex and working experience as an SHI physician as well as patients’ sex and age.

Patient and public involvement

During this study, we discussed laboratory and point-of-care testing in GP practices with an advisory board of patients and public representatives established at our institute. The perspectives of patients and public representatives were included in the discussion of the findings of this study.

Results

Characteristics of included GPs and GP practices

A total of 2061 GPs from 1575 GP practices were included in this study, and their characteristics are summarised in table 1. More than half of the included GPs were women (60.9%) and GPs had a median age of 57 years (IQR: 47–65). The majority of GPs were self-employed (57.1%) and 40.3% were employed in practices. Most of the GP practices surveyed were solo practices (70.3%), with group practices and ambulatory healthcare centres representing 13.8% and 14.3% of all practices, respectively. In both solo and group practices, the great majority of GPs were self-employed (75.1% and 79.2%, respectively), while in ambulatory healthcare centres, almost all GPs were employed (94.2%) (online supplemental sTable 1). The majority of GP practices were located in rural districts (54.2%) and rural districts with densely populated areas (17.8%). In contrast, 13.1% of all GP practices were located in cities with a population exceeding 100 000. In the dataset, 1 762 558 patients were included with a median age of 58 years (IQR: 39–72) and 53.5% of them being women.

Utilisation of laboratory analyses and POCTs in GP practices

In 2022, 1601 GPs performed 16.1 million laboratory analyses, with 14.9 million (92.5%) being ILS and 1.2 million (7.5%) being POCTs conducted within GP practices. The mean number of laboratory tests per patient was 15.5 in 2022. Among those, 14.3 laboratory tests per patient were ILS and 1.2 tests per patient were POCTs within the GP practice.

In the whole study period (2017–2022), the most common laboratory analyses included glucose, creatinine, blood cell count, HbA1/HbA1c and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), which accounted for one-third of all laboratory analyses in GP practices (table 2). Of the POCT analyses performed by GPs, six POCTs accounted for more than 95% of all POCT analyses: glucose (36.3%), urine test strip (30.9%), prothrombin time/international normalised ratio (INR) (8.8%), HbA1/HbA1c (7.8%), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (7.3%) and microalbuminuria (5.4%).

Between 2017 and 2020, the total number of laboratory analyses, which include both ILS and POCTs conducted in GP practices, decreased by −17.4% from 18.5 to 15.3 million (figure 1A). There was a slight increase of 4.3% from 2020 to 2022. However, the annual number of laboratory tests performed per patient continuously decreased in the study period from 19.1 in 2017 to 15.5 in 2022 (−18.8%) (figure 1B).

There was a profound and consistent reduction in the total number of POCTs carried out in GP practices from 2017 to 2022 (−31.5%) (figure 1A), along with a drop in the proportion of POCT analyses among all laboratory analyses, which decreased from 9.8% in 2017 to 7.7% in 2022. The mean number of POCTs per patient and year decreased from 1.9 to 1.2 in the study period 2017 and 2022 (−36.0%) (figure 1B).

Glucose and HbA1/HbA1c

Measurements of glucose and HbA1/HbA1c are important diagnostic markers for diabetic syndromes and were among the most frequently performed laboratory analyses in GP practices (table 2). From 2017 to 2022, glucose analyses (ILS and POCTs) declined by −23.6% (figure 2A). POCT glucose analyses within GP practices exhibited a pronounced steady decline (−38.1%) throughout the study period and in 2022, 35.0% of all glucose measurements were performed as POCTs. In contrast, the number of laboratory analyses for HbA1/HbA1c remained relatively constant between 2017 and 2022 (figure 2B). However, there was a shift towards HbA1/HbA1c POC testing indicated by an increase of POCT analyses by 26.7%. In 2022, 14.8% of HbA1/HbA1c analyses were carried out as POCTs within GP practices.

Association of GP characteristics and the use of POCTs within the GP practice

We performed univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses to investigate whether GP and patient characteristics are associated with increased odds of laboratory analyses being performed as POCTs or as ILS.

Community type of GP practice location (eg, rural vs urban districts), GP employment status and GPs’ sex were not associated with higher or lower odds of POCT analyses (table 3). There was a 34% higher odds that GPs in group practices perform laboratory analyses as POCTs compared with their peers in solo practices (OR 1.34, 95%CI 1.10 to 1.62,), which was confirmed in the multivariate regression analysis (adjusted OR: 1.37, 95% CI 1.14 to 1.66).

At the patient level, female patients exhibited slightly lower odds for laboratory analyses performed as POCTs (adjusted OR=0.93, 95% CI 0.90 to 0.96) (table 3). Furthermore, there was a markedly higher chance of laboratory analyses conducted as POCTs instead of ILS in older patient groups (≥40 years) compared with young adult patients (18–39 years) (aOR 40–59 years: 1.19 (95% CI 1.14 to 1.24); aOR 60–79 years: 1.48 (95% CI 1.40 to 1.57); aOR >79 years: 1.43 (95% CI 1.34 to 1.54)).

Discussion

In this study, we analysed complete SHI laboratory claims data of 1575 GP practices in Thuringia, a federal state in central Germany. The most frequently employed laboratory tests, either ILS or POCTs, include glucose, creatinine, blood cell count, HbA1/HbA1c, TSH and liver enzymes (gamma-GT, ALAT). The most common POCTs performed in GP practices were glucose, urine test strips, prothrombin time/INR, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, HbA1/HbA1c and microalbuminuria. In 2022, POC testing within GP practices accounted for 7.5% of all laboratory tests and the remaining 92.5% tests were ILS. Between 2017 and 2022, the mean number of laboratory tests per patient steadily declined by 18.8%. A particular pronounced decline was observed in the use of POCTs. In 2022 GPs conducted 36% less POCTs within the GP practice compared with 2017.

Time trend of laboratory use in German GP practices

Similar to other countries, such as the United Kingdom,20 laboratory use in German outpatient practices increased markedly from 2000 to 2015.21 However, our data from 2017 to 2022 show that this upward trend has ceased and a decrease in laboratory use was observed in GP practices, which can be attributed to multiple factors. In 2017, the German National Association of Statutory Health Insurance Physicians implemented a laboratory reform with the aim of reducing laboratory use in outpatient practices, which the governing bodies perceived as too high. Outpatient physicians, including GPs, now receive a financial bonus when they use less laboratory testing, including both ILS and POC testing.

In addition, GPs may have rationalised their utilisation of laboratory tests by adopting diagnostics recommendations from evidence-based guidelines. Studies suggest that 21% of laboratory tests may be unnecessary,22 indicating the potential for optimisation of laboratory use. Effective strategies for promoting rational laboratory use include training of GPs, incorporating evidence-based guidelines as well as individualised feedback reports in combination with education of GPs.23

Moreover, the COVID-19 pandemic probably contributed to the lower utilisation of laboratory testing (except for SARS-CoV-2 testing) in German GP practices due to fewer patient visits during the pandemic. However, the downward trend already started prior to the pandemic and continued in 2022, indicating that the more rational use of laboratory testing is largely independent of the pandemic.

Point-of-care testing within GP practices

POC testing in GP practices has a great potential to support clinical decision-making of GPs and the number of POCTs available for GPs is steadily increasing.10 24 However, our data show that less than 8% of all laboratory analyses were carried out as POCT analyses, and POCT use significantly decreased between 2017 and 2022. In addition to concerns regarding diagnostic accuracy, German GPs reported high costs and inadequate reimbursement as main limitations for implementing POCTs.12 In fact, between 2017 and 2022 reimbursement for POCTs remained unchanged for GPs providing care to SHI patients.25 Consequently, performing POCTs may not be economical from a GP perspective. Additionally, German GPs’ relatively low utilisation of POCTs also may be due to their high workload, with an average consultation time of 7–9 min,26 27 which leaves little time for additional diagnostics measures, such as POCTs. Furthermore, Germany has a wide availability of external laboratories, which offer fast services for GP practices, including for those located in rural areas.28 German GPs reported that results from initiated laboratory analyses are typically available in less than 18 hours.12 In line with this, our study indicates that the likelihood of using POCT instead of ILS does not differ between GP practices in rural areas and those in urban areas.

Our analysis shows that frequently employed POCTs include tests for common chronic conditions in GP practices, such as diabetes, renal dysfunction and coagulopathies. The purchase and use of POCTs for these clinical indications are more likely to be economical from a GP perspective due to the relatively high number of patients with repeated need for testing. In line with this, our study shows that GPs in group practices more likely use POC testing instead of external laboratory service compared with their colleagues in solo practices. In group practices, two or more GPs share practice staff and equipment, making the purchase and use of POCTs more lucrative due to shared costs and a higher number of patients.

Glucose analyses were the most commonly performed laboratory tests in GP practices, but their use decreased by 24% between 2017 and 2022. Conversely, the number of HbA1c analyses remained unaltered. This is at least partly explained by updated clinical guidelines, which recommend HbA1c measurements in lieu of glucose analyses for the monitoring of diabetes.29

There are notable differences in the utilisation of POCTs between German GPs and their counterparts in other countries.30 31 For example, in contrast to GPs from Australia, Belgium, the Netherlands, the UK and the USA, German GPs do not use urine pregnancy testing or β-human chorionic gonadotropin. These analyses are typically conducted by gynaecologists in Germany. Similarly, compared with GPs from the USA, German GPs rarely use rapid tests for group A streptococci.31 However, those tests are common in outpatient paediatrician practices. German GPs also seldom utilise rapid testing for influenza12 due to the lack of reimbursement for patients with SHI (88% of the German population). It is noteworthy that between 2020 and 2022, German GPs frequently used rapid antigen tests for SARS-CoV-2.12 However, as these tests were reimbursed not by health insurances but by governmental authorities, they are not included in our dataset.

Interestingly, we found that GPs more likely decide for POCT testing rather than ILS, when they employ laboratory testing for older patients compared with young adults. One explanation may be that GPs see a greater need for rapid test results to make immediate clinical decisions in older patients than in younger ones.

Strengths and limitations

From our knowledge, our study is the first in-depth analysis of laboratory use in German GP practices, including both ILS and POC testing within GP practices. We analysed over 95 million laboratory analyses using complete SHI claims data from over 1.7 million patients and 1575 GP practices in Thuringia. Our study has some limitations. First, our analyses are limited to laboratory tests covered by SHI. However, SHI covers 88% of the German population15 and based on anecdotal evidence from our GP network, GPs do rarely employ laboratory tests that are not covered by SHI, with the exception of SARS-CoV-2 POCTs.12 Second, our data only included GPs from Thuringia, a federal state in central Germany, which may limit the generalisability of our results. However, when compared with official statistics,32 33 Thuringian GPs appear to be reasonably representative for the total German GP population (online supplemental sTable 2). The Thuringian GP population is similar to the overall GP population in terms of average age (57 vs 55 years), distribution of practice types and GP density per 100 000 population (63.5 vs 63.9). On the other hand, GPs in Thuringia were more likely to be women (61% vs 49 and less likely to be self-employed (57% vs 75%)).

Conclusion

German GPs regularly employ laboratory testing in their routine patient management, primarily utilising external laboratory service, while POC testing within the GP practice plays a relatively minor role. The notable decline in the use of laboratory testing by GPs, particularly POC testing, is attributed to contextual factors, including regulations from healthcare authorities, reimbursement conditions and increasingly scarce time resources in GP practices. In order to ensure optimal patient care, healthcare authorities should review and potentially adapt regulations to guarantee sufficient and economical access to laboratory testing for physicians and patients.
