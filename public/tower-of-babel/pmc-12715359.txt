Structured Clinical Tools for Periodic Health Assessment of Older Adults in Primary Care: A Scoping Review
Authors: Marie‐Pier Villemure, Benoît Cossette, Pierre‐Vincent Breault‐Ruel, Alexandre Camirand, Catherine Hudon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsDelivering preventive care to older adults presents challenges due to the lack of consensus on screening items, particularly regarding geriatric syndromes. Furthermore, chronological age often differs from biological age, which requires primary care clinicians (PCCs) to assess each patient's aging profile and tailor preventive interventions accordingly. Periodic Health Assessments (PHAs) offer an opportunity to administer preventive care. While several authors have developed Structured Clinical Tools (SCTs) to assist clinicians during PHAs, no SCT is widely used across clinicians. The study aims to identify existing SCTs for the PHA of older adults and evaluate their content, formats, and practical usability.MethodsWe conducted a scoping review following the 6‐step methodology recommended by Arksey and O'Malley (2005) and Levac (2010). We searched in PubMed, CINAHL, and Ageline, as well as in the grey literature, using keywords related to four main concepts: older adults, primary care, periodic health assessment, and SCT. We retrieved SCTs published in French and English between 2000 and 2024. Data were screened and charted by two independent reviewers. For step six (consultation), we gathered opinions of 10 PCCs on the practical usability of different SCTs.ResultsAmong the 8029 identified publications, we retrieved 16 distinct SCTs. Design objectives and conception processes of these SCTs varied. They were presented in various formats, including questionnaires, tables, schemas, checklists, acronyms, and mnemonics. While all SCTs addressed geriatric syndromes, only two adapted their recommendations to patients' aging profiles. Consulted PCCs emphasized that an ideal SCT should be integrated into an electronic health record, accommodate various aging profiles, and rely on evidence‐based medicine.ConclusionsA reliable and user‐friendly SCT for the PHA of older adults would facilitate the delivery of preventive care. However, no published SCTs to date meet the expectations of PCCs. Further research is necessary to develop one.Keywords: aged, preventive health care, primary health care, surveys and questionnaires

Why Does This Matter?

Tailoring preventive care interventions to aging profiles is critical to address the diverse health needs of older adults. Current structured clinical tools fail to account for the variability in aging, limiting their applicability and effectiveness. Developing tools that integrate evidence‐based recommendations with personalized approaches can enhance care delivery, improve outcomes, and support primary care clinicians in addressing the unique challenges of geriatric medicine.

Introduction

Primary Care and Older Adults

Primary care is essential for access to the health care system and serves as its entry point. Primary care clinicians (PCCs), including general practitioners and nurses, face challenges, particularly the pressure to manage high patient volumes [1]. Caring for vulnerable populations such as older adults with complex needs and multimorbidity increases this challenge [2]. Additionally, ageing trajectories vary widely among older adults; individuals of the same chronological age can differ widely in biological age [3]. An increasing number of older adults are experiencing successful ageing, maintaining high levels of functioning into old age [4], making it difficult for PCCs to tailor recommendations precisely to each ageing trajectory.

Older adults are generally underrepresented in clinical trials [5], which limits the evidence available to guide PCCs in their care [6]. Furthermore, PCCs need to address not only diseases, but also geriatric syndromes—multifactorial conditions that are common in older adults [7]. Since their first definition in 1965 [8], over 20 geriatric syndromes have been described [9], but the relative clinical importance of each syndrome remains unclear [10], leaving PCCs uncertain about screening priorities. This complexity calls for a person‐centred care framework that guides the adaptation of preventive strategies to each individual's ageing and functional profile [11], in line with the principles of inclusion and global anti‐ageism initiatives.

Periodic Health Assessments

Preventive care, defined as interventions to detect, prevent, or slow the progression of disease [12], is an essential part of PCCs' responsibilities. For older adults, evidence on the impact of preventive care on delaying disability and functional decline is mixed, with most studies supporting its benefits [13]. Periodic health assessments (PHAs) are commonly used to provide preventive care, incorporating age‐specific health measures into routine visits [14]. PHAs are designed to be conducted opportunistically in patients of all ages, often during visits scheduled for other health concerns [14].

For older adults, however, preventive care needs vary significantly according to ageing profiles, whether individuals present a robust profile or one of frailty [15]. While recommendations for disease screening are often age‐based, the optimal starting point for detecting geriatric syndromes is less well defined, as it depends on factors beyond age alone. This creates a shortfall in preventive care between the time when individuals benefit from disease screening and the moment when the detection of geriatric syndromes typically begins.

Alongside the PHA model in primary care, previous work has also examined Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA) and its role in this setting [16, 17]. CGA is a multidimensional, multidisciplinary diagnostic and therapeutic process that primarily targets older adults at the frail and pre‐frail end of the robustness spectrum, with a strong (though not exclusive) emphasis on geriatric syndromes. While essential, CGA tends to overlook the needs of robust older adults [4] and does not easily fit into primary care practice given its comprehensiveness [18] and the time pressures faced by primary care clinicians [19, 20].

Many structured clinical tools (SCTs) have been developed to facilitate the delivery of PHAs by PCCs, often in the form of checklists or tables. Studies show that SCTs can save time [21] and improve the quality of care [22]. SCTs are widely used in paediatric [23], adolescent [24], and adult under 65 years old [25]. While SCTs for older adult PHAs are available [26], their content and format vary widely, with each designed according to available resources and priorities. SCTs specific to older adults are not systematically implemented in practice and generally do not consider the variability in ageing profiles.

Methods

Study Design

Building on this observation, we argue that a single SCT for adults over 65 cannot address the full range of preventive care needs in this heterogeneous population. Ideally, an SCT would tailor interventions to each patient's ageing trajectory and functional profile (Figure 1).

To explore how existing SCTs address this complexity, we conducted a scoping review to identify and describe available tools for older adult PHAs, and to examine their content, format, and practical applicability. Scoping reviews aim to systematically map literature on topics that are poorly defined or need clarification [27]. Throughout this review, we remained mindful of the importance of individualizing preventive care for older adults based on their ageing trajectory.

We followed the 6‐step methodology recommended by Arksey and O'Malley [28], completed by Levac [29], Peters [30], and Westphaln [31]. Additionally, we used the PRISMA‐ScR framework to ensure transparency in the reporting process (Table S1) [32].

Search Strategy

We developed a search strategy in collaboration with a research librarian (Table S2). We searched relevant publications in PubMed/MEDLINE, CINAHL and Ageline databases, as well as geriatric and primary care textbooks and grey literature [33] (Supporting Information S3). We also used a snowball sampling method [34]. The search terms covered four main core concepts: older adults, primary care, periodic health assessment, and structured clinical tools. We searched for publications in English and French, between January 1, 2000 and January 1, 2024.

Eligibility Criteria

The titles and abstracts of eligible publications were screened by two independent researchers (MPV, PVBR), both with a medical background. Zotero software was used to sort the publications. They were included if they presented one or more SCTs for PHA of older adults (65 years and older) in primary care. All types of publications were included, except for letters, commentaries and editorials. The SCTs had to address a multidimensional assessment of global health. Exclusion criteria were: (1) publications not specific to a population of older adults, (2) publications about populations not living in the community or about specific contexts, diseases and syndromes (including frailty and functional decline), (3) publications not specific to primary care, (4) SCTs in the form of a measurement instrument, index or score, (5) SCTs created before 2000 or not updated since then. Measurement instruments were excluded because they do not provide a holistic and multidimensional view of the patient, which is fundamental to primary care.

We included publications irrespective of the availability of their development process (e.g., websites of official organizations). Scoping reviews, by design, do not evaluate the scientific quality of data sources [35], making quality appraisal optional. Our objective was to preserve the exploratory nature of the study, enabling a comprehensive overview of the various formats of SCTs available. Emphasis was placed on reviewing grey literature.

A full‐text review was independently conducted for all relevant publications. Reviewers met to pool their findings and reach a consensus. In case of disagreement, the opinion of a third reviewer (AC) was sought.

Data Collection and Extraction

We classified the results according to the type of SCT (questionnaire, acronym, table, checklist, etc.), design process, target population (if available), consideration of different ageing profiles, data on the time of administration of the SCT, and inclusion of subsequent recommendations of care in the case of abnormal screening.

Extraction tables were designed and completed independently by the two researchers who did the initial publication screening (MPV, PVBR). Each structured clinical tool was analyzed according to the above criteria, depending on data availability.

We summarized the information from the scoping review in two main tables. To provide an overview of the SCTs included in the review, we presented key characteristics for each tool, such as target population, purpose, and implementation context in a first table. A second table outlines the specific elements included in each SCT to establish the breadth and variation between tools and to facilitate comparisons of their content. These tables are accompanied by a narrative description highlighting notable patterns, gaps and unique components identified within the structured clinical tools.

Consultation Exercise

Stage 6 involved consulting field experts to identify knowledge gaps in the literature and explore dissemination strategies, based on Arksey and O'Malley [28] and Levac [29]. This step is now widely regarded as essential [31], despite limited methodological guidance that allows researchers discretion in its implementation. Buss et al. [36] recommend documenting each step, justifying decisions, and adhering to ethical standards. It is important to note that this consultation method differs from formal qualitative research and is not intended as a substitute.

We consulted general practitioners, primary care nurses, and nurse practitioners (PCCs) working in primary care settings with a focus on older adults. PCCs were recruited through purposive sampling from primary care facilities in the province of Quebec, social media, and professional networking at conferences to ensure diversity of rural, urban, and managerial perspectives. Upon realizing that early‐career clinicians were more easily recruited, we made a deliberate effort to include professionals with greater experience.

The consultation process consisted of individual interviews that were conducted to review the clinical tools from step 4 and assess their usability in practice. Key themes from the interviews are presented in Table S4. Content analysis [37] was applied to analyze the results, with transcriptions coded, grouped in a Microsoft Word document, and synthesized by theme. Informed and written consent was obtained from all participants. The protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Université de Sherbrooke, Canada (Reference number 2022‐3496). Informed consent was obtained from each participant during the expert consultation step. The protocol for this scoping review has been registered with the Open Science Framework.

Results

Selecting Relevant Publications

After removing duplicates, the search identified 8029 publications (Table S5). These publications underwent an initial screening based on titles and abstracts, resulting in 406 retained for full‐text review. Subsequently, the full‐text publications were evaluated against the eligibility criteria, leading to the exclusion of 298 publications for one or more of the following reasons: lack of relevance, focus on a specific population, context, or condition, or other factors (Figure 2 and Table S6). Ultimately, 108 publications met the inclusion criteria and were retained for further analysis. Among these, 44 publications referred to 16 distinct SCTs and were included in the final review.

Charting, Collating, Summarizing, and Reporting the Data

The types of publications related to SCTs included seven development studies [38, 39, 40, 43, 47, 53, 54], three reports from health organizations [40, 41, 49, 50], two were expert reports [45, 48] and two retrieved from health organization websites [51, 52]. Finally, one SCT was produced as part of a clinical trial [42] and one as part of a pilot study to adapt and implement geriatric assessment in primary care [44].

The design objectives were explicit in all SCTs except the Geriatric Periodic Health Assessment [51]. A comprehensive and systematic conception process was reported for six of the SCTs [38, 39, 41, 43, 46, 47]. The population was well defined in most publications, with nine using chronological age as the basis for their definition. In contrast, six publications referred to the population as “geriatric patients” without providing further specification. Four SCTs were used in subsequent validation or efficacy studies [38, 41, 49, 50, 51]; this information was partially available for two other SCTs [42, 43].

SCTs were presented in a variety of formats: four were questionnaires (either reported or self‐reported) [41, 42, 43, 48], four were tables [38, 39, 44, 45], two were schematics [53, 54], two were mixed [40, 51], one was a checklist [47], one was an acronym [46], and one was a mnemonic [49, 50]. None of the SCTs was presented as an algorithm or clinical decision support tool. Among the SCTs, two were available for use on a mobile application [41, 45], but none were explicitly available for an electronic medical record (EMR).

All SCTs included screening for geriatric syndromes. The most frequently mentioned geriatric syndromes were falls/mobility (16/16 SCTs), cognitive disorders (15/16), depression (14/16), visual impairment (12/16) and hearing impairment (11/16). Five SCTs proposed the use of rapid geriatric assessment in primary care [38, 44, 45, 47, 48]. A minority of SCTs consider the medical aspects of older adults' health, such as reviewing pharmacological profiles (5/16) and vaccination status (5/16), or screening for metabolic, cardiovascular, or oncological diseases (7/16) (Figure 3).

Clinical element categories included in Structured Clinical Tools.

Only two SCTs adapted their recommendations to different ageing profiles or to the functional level of each patient [39, 40]. In Flaherty's Health Maintenance Clinical Glidepaths, the authors recommended adapting interventions according to four categories: robust, frail, moderately demented, and end of life [39]. The Hong Kong Reference Framework suggested three categories: independent without known chronic disease, independent with chronic disease, and older adults with disabilities [40]. In both SCTs, there was no objective guidance on how to categorize a patient's ageing profile, recommending that PCCs rely on their clinical judgment and experience for individualized categorization.

Five of the SCTs have measured and published data on the time required for their use in clinical practice [43, 44, 46, 47, 53]. The administration time for each of these SCTs ranged from 8 min [46] to 31 min [44].

Eight SCTs gave PCCs follow‐up recommendations for care in the event of an abnormal screening (Table 1). For example, in the case of a positive screen for falls, the Brief Assessment Tool [38] recommends on the elements to be investigated next such as review of medications, referral to a balance exercise program, etc. Five SCTs gave comprehensive recommendations for care in the event of an abnormal screening for all items screened [38, 40, 41, 49, 50, 53], and three gave partial recommendations or only for a few items [48, 51, 52].

Consultation Exercise

Demographic and practice characteristics of clinicians participating in the consultation exercise are presented in Table S7. The experts' consultation highlighted numerous factors to consider in the conceptualization of an SCT, including both its content and format (Figure 4). PCCs emphasized the need for SCTs to be flexible, practical, and capable of accommodating diverse clinician roles and patient needs. Experts valued tools that allow for annotation and customization across multiple visits and supported options that assist both novice and experienced clinicians in urban and rural settings. Concerns about over‐medicalization were raised, with a preference for SCTs that balance comprehensiveness with clinical judgement. Seamless integration with EHR was considered essential, with interactive elements such as check boxes and free text fields preferred for ease of use. Paper‐based options were also suggested for specific cases (e.g., home‐based medical care). The potential for SCTs to disrupt the doctor‐patient dynamic was noted, particularly if patients felt uncomfortable with the technology; simpler “one‐pager” formats were preferred for their efficiency and ability to maintain clinical focus.

Expert consultation (step 6): Summary of discussions with primary care clinicians.

Experts emphasized the importance of early education and ongoing training about PHA of older adults. PCCs perceive SCTs as flexible cognitive aids that complement ‐rather than replace‐ their personal clinical judgment. These findings provide a basis for identifying what makes certain SCTs easily applicable by primary care clinicians, aligning with the aims of this review.

Discussion

Summary of Evidence

This scoping review mapped the essential characteristics of SCTs for geriatric care, focusing on tool format, content, and adaptability to primary care settings. Findings revealed that SCTs predominantly focused on assessing geriatric syndromes. Formats ranged from tables and checklists to mnemonics and digital applications, but standardized integration into clinical practices were often lacking, making consistent use across care settings difficult. Consultations with experts highlighted the need for SCTs to be flexible, practical, and adaptable to different clinician roles and patient needs. Experts preferred tools that are integrated to HER and that balance comprehensiveness with clinical judgment.

Strengths and Limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the different SCTs for PHA of older adults. It is highly relevant in the current clinical context, with limited primary care access in many areas and the increasing demographic proportion of older adults. In addition, the study incorporates a consultation exercise and adheres to the PRISMA‐ScR reporting guideline.

Although a systematic appraisal of data quality was not performed, we upheld a methodologically robust approach, placing significant emphasis on the comprehensive review of grey literature.

Some limitations should be noted. A publication bias may have occurred if some PHAs were unavailable, such as templates created by PCCs but not disseminated. Geographic and temporal limitations are present, as European and North American publications are overrepresented, and tools published before 2000 or in languages other than French or English were excluded. In step 6, a selection bias was observed, as most participants were non‐randomly recruited early‐career professionals with a specific interest in caring for older adults.

Significance of Findings

Content Variability

This scoping review highlighted the wide variability in the content of SCTs for PHAs of older adults. While some recommend a comprehensive assessment model [51], others favor a targeted approach [45, 46]. This divergence may stem from some PHAs being designed by primary care clinicians [38, 44] and others by geriatric specialists [39]: patients seen in geriatrics typically have more complex profiles than those in primary care [60]. This scoping review highlights that SCTs primarily focus on the detection of geriatric syndromes, with little emphasis on providing guidance regarding the role of disease screenings (e.g., cancer, metabolic diseases, osteoporosis). Some older adults may still be eligible for these screenings [61, 62]; overlooking this aspect, while prioritizing geriatric syndrome screening in robust older adults, could be perceived as a form of discrimination.

Inclusion of the Variability of Ageing Profiles

Most SCTs in our review treat older adults as a homogeneous group, defined by an arbitrary age threshold, despite the heterogeneity of ageing profiles. While various tools exist for stratifying ageing and screening for frailty, few are designed for streamlined application in primary care settings [63, 64]. We highlight a gap in the existing resources available to PCCs to provide primary care that is both individualized and efficient.

The two SCTs that suggested individualizing PHAs did so by recommending that clinicians subjectively estimate patients' profiles based on their judgment and clinical experience [39, 41]. This approach poses several challenges, including potential fluctuations in functional status and overlap between categories [39]. Furthermore, some PCCs may lack sufficient confidence to accurately assess patients' functional status and ageing profiles. Finally, PCCs tend to display an optimistic bias when estimating a patient's ageing profile [65]. A promising solution involves implementing clinical decision support systems that extract health record data to provide individualized prognostic indices for older adults [66].

Practical Usability of Results

One major barrier to using PHA‐structured tools in primary care is practical usability. The time required to administer an SCT is an important factor to consider when assessing its usability for PCCs. Given the shortage of PCCs in many regions, SCTs for primary care need to be concise. Few SCTs report their completion time; where noted, times range from 8 [46] to 31 min [44], a substantial portion of the average 15‐ to 25‐min PCC consultation [19, 20], which typically covers active issues, curative care, and preventive care, addressing an average of six health concerns per visit [20].

The ideal administration time for a screening tool varies depending on appointment length and patient needs, but a PHA tool should balance thoroughness with efficiency, allowing sufficient time to address both preventive care and active health concerns. One possible solution to enhance efficiency could be the use of computerized clinical decision support systems; however, none of the SCTs in our review employed this approach, despite a growing body of literature highlighting its benefits [67, 68].

Consistency With Current Scientific Evidence

Most reviewed publications recommend focusing on identifying geriatric syndromes, yet primary care screening practices sometimes differ from guidelines, especially regarding cognitive impairment, which is predominantly recommended in geriatric PHAs but not universally endorsed by clinical standards [69]. Systematic screening for geriatric syndromes based on age risks promoting ageism by overlooking healthy older adults and underdiagnosing frail younger patients.

There is extensive literature on the Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA) [70], especially in secondary and tertiary care, where it primarily targets geriatric syndrome identification. However, shortening CGA alone doesn't make it suitable for primary care, as it lacks certain key elements for this context. Primary care practitioners handle a broader patient range, managing both general and age‐specific conditions, unlike secondary care, which often treats patients with more advanced frailty [60]. Few SCTs provide comprehensive recommendations for this broader scope, likely due to evolving evidence that complicates guideline consistency.

Clinical Relevance of SCTs for PHA in Primary Care

Some authors have addressed the relevance of preventive interventions based on impact on clinical outcomes. Among the identified SCTs, the “Brief Assessment Tool” has tested tool effectiveness, showing no significant impact of their PHA on functional decline [38]. Prior studies reported positive impacts of PHAs on quality of life [71] and marginal benefits for hospitalization rates [16], disability [72], and healthcare costs [73]. However, the specific elements assessed in these PHAs are unavailable, limiting the replicability of results. Effectiveness also hinges on factors like population type (frail vs. robust), resource availability to address identified deficits, and intervention time‐to‐benefit [74, 75], which are often overlooked in studies [16]. The literature on individual intervention effectiveness remains limited and controversial [76].

Thus, even in the absence of definitive data demonstrating favorable effectiveness, SCTs for PHAs of older adults in primary care should not be disregarded. Instead, it should prompt careful consideration of their content, objectives, usability, and target population.

Lastly, preventive care for older adults is a shared responsibility that should involve not only PCCs but also health professionals with specialized expertise, community members, and local organizations, consistent with social geriatrics' emphasis on community collaboration in elder care [77].

Implications for Future Research

Future research should aim to identify the key elements of content and format that make SCTs both effective and feasible in primary care. Efforts should be directed toward developing rapid, individualized assessment strategies based on biological ageing profiles (e.g., robust, frail), using objective criteria such as frailty indices rather than relying solely on chronological age. Geriatric training remains essential to support the thoughtful and context‐sensitive use of these tools across diverse clinical situations. An ideal SCT would offer a structured framework to guide patient‐centered care [11] while still allowing room for clinician judgement.

In addition, the future development of SCTs for older adults should consider integrating social determinants of health—such as isolation, caregiver burden, and socioeconomic vulnerability—while also ensuring continued access to appropriate disease‐specific prevention strategies, as emphasized in the work of the World Health Organisation [53]. While we fully agree that equity and fairness must remain central to fair healthcare practices, it is equally important to design tools that support clinical decisions tailored to individual needs. These elements are essential to fully support person‐centred care within primary care settings.

Conclusions and Implications

This review of SCTs for PHA of older adults in primary care reveals a crucial gap: while many tools address specific geriatric syndromes, none combine comprehensiveness, practicality, and time‐efficiency for primary care. This study highlights the need for SCTs that address the diversity of ageing experiences without imposing ageist assumptions. With an increasing focus on screening in geriatrics, there is a risk of a cynical approach to prevention ‐ targeting older adults as a homogeneous group characterized only by decline. Instead, SCTs for PHA should respect individual needs, acknowledging both resilience and vulnerability.
