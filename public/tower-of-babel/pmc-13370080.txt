Histopathological analysis of oral epithelial dysplasia in pericancerous epithelium of lingual squamous cell carcinoma of dogs and cats
Authors: Tomoo KUDO, Naoyuki AIHARA, Tetsuo OHMACHI, Junichi KAMIIE, Yuuichi SOENO
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOral epithelial dysplasia (OED) is a precancerous lesion of oral squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) in humans, characterized by decreased cytokeratin (CK) 13 expression, de novo CK17 expression, and increased Ki-67 expression. This study investigated the histopathological and immunohistochemical features of pericancerous epithelium (PCE) in lingual SCCs of dogs (n=14) and cats (n=8). PCEs were classified as high-grade or low-grade OED based on the human World Health Organization criteria. High-grade OED exhibited decreased CK13 expression, increased CK17 expression, and Ki-67 expression, resembling human high-grade OED. Morphological analysis revealed sharply demarcated lesions with drop-shaped rete processes and irregular epithelial stratification. These findings suggest that OED in canine and feline PCE shares key characteristics with human high-grade OED, supporting the presence of field cancerization in the oral mucosa of domestic animals.Keywords: comparative pathology, lingual squamous cell carcinoma, oral epithelial dysplasia, pericancerous epithelium, reactive epithelial atypia

INTRODUCTION

Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is the most common neoplasm in feline oral cancer and the second most common neoplasm in canine oral cancer [35]. Oral SCCs in dogs and cats are primarily localized in the gingiva (especially in dogs) and tongue (particularly in cats) [36]. Malignant neoplasms of the tongue in dogs and cats are mainly diagnosed as SCC [6]. In humans, oral SCC is the most common oral cancer, primarily arising from the lateral mucosa of the tongue [1, 44]. Moreover, the majority of oral SCCs arise in areas of pre-existing epithelial dysplasia due to prolonged and repeated carcinogenic insults, a concept explained by multistage carcinogenesis and “field carcinogenesis” [9, 64]. In fact, epithelial dysplasia may be observed at the surgical resection margin of oral SCC, and its presence is associated with an increased risk of local recurrence [5, 63].

In the human World Health Organization (WHO) classification of head and neck cancer, oral epithelial dysplasia (OED) is defined as “a spectrum of structural and cytological changes in the epithelium due to the accumulation of genetic alterations” [64]. Diagnostic criteria are established based on abnormal proliferation, maturation and differentiation of epithelial cells [64]. Pathologists have traditionally used a three-tier OED grading system (mild, moderate, and severe) based on these criteria, with high-grade lesions usually exhibiting increased architectural and cytological atypia [46, 64]. To accurately assess the risk of malignant transformation in OED, various grading systems have been proposed, based on the WHO classification to evaluate architectural and cytological features [46]. A review comparing these systems showed that mild dysplasia is classified as low-grade, while moderate and severe dysplasia are categorized as high-grade [46]. In addition to the typical epithelial dysplasia observed in other stratified squamous epithelial mucosa, OED presents distinct histological variations, such as differentiated-type OED [58]. Since accurately assessing the risk of malignant transformation based solely on hematoxylin & eosin (HE) staining is challenging, ongoing research aims to identify highly reliable immunohistochemical markers to enhance diagnostic precision.

In humans, changes in the cytokeratin (CK) profiles during carcinogenesis of the oral mucosa have been investigated [18, 24, 33, 50]. OED and oral SCC exhibit de novo CK17 expression and a loss of CK13 expression, whereas normal mucosa shows only positive staining for CK13 [23, 24, 33, 66]. These changes have been primarily analyzed in the lingual mucosa, a common site for OED and oral SCC [33, 64]. Conversely, a few studies have reported that the gingival epithelium, which is a keratinized stratified squamous epithelium, exhibits a distinct CK expression profile from the non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium of the lingual mucosa, even under normal conditions [31, 49]. Furthermore, the combination of increased Ki-67 expression, a marker of cell proliferation, and changes in the CK profiles has been reported as a useful immunohistochemical marker for distinguishing OED from reactive atypical epithelium [66]. To the best of our knowledge, reports on the expression of CK13 and CK17 in the oral mucosa of domestic animals are very limited [19, 48]. TP53 is a well-known tumor suppressor gene and the most frequently mutated gene in human oral SCC [37, 42, 67]. Numerous studies have investigated the use of p53 immunostaining in OED and oral SCC [27, 40, 52, 55]. In recent years, a correlation between TP53 mutations and p53 immunostaining patterns has been demonstrated [17, 52, 55]. Additionally, a recent study reports that OED cases with abnormal p53 overexpression have a high risk of progressing to SCC [25], highlighting the growing importance of p53 immunostaining in OED diagnosis.

Regenerative epithelial atypia (REA) is observed in regenerative epithelium (RE) of ulcers, and in humans, biopsies of refractory oral ulcers are often performed to exclude oral SCC [10]. The distinction between REA and OED can present a diagnostic challenge in some cases [11]. In ulcer-associated REA, however, it is considered that atypia diminishes with increasing distance from RE [15]. In dogs and cats, lingual SCC is frequently recognized clinically as a refractory ulcer, and many cases present with ulcer formation at the time of diagnosis [4]. Ulceration associated with carcinoma is thought to result from tumor cell invasion into the surrounding tissues [35], and the pericancerous mucosa or affected epithelium may contain RE.

In this study, we hypothesized that OED is distributed around oral SCC in dogs and cats, similar to humans. To investigate this hypothesis, we conducted a morphological analysis of the pericancerous epithelium (PCE) and classified it into high-grade and low-grade OED. Because PCE includes REA in RE, we applied the WHO diagnostic criteria for human OED to conduct a morphological grading of malignancy in order to demonstrate that high-grade OED can be distinguished from RE. Additionally, based on changes in the immunostaining profiles of the lateral mucosa of tongue, a common site of human oral SCC, we clarified the immunostaining patterns of cancer, PCE, and RE in dogs and cats. Furthermore, we analyzed the expression patterns of Ki-67 and p53 in SCC and PCE as indicators of abnormal cell proliferation. By comparing the histopathological findings and immunostaining profiles of PCE and RE, we sought to identify characteristics of OEDs in dogs and cats that resemble high-grade lesions in humans.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

All cases examined in this study were histologically diagnosed as oral SCCs or ulcerated lesions of the tongue between 2010 and 2018 by a veterinary diagnostic service (PATHO LABO Co., Ltd.). In total, 241 canine and 223 feline cases were diagnosed with oral SCC, including 48 cases (20%) in dogs and 69 cases (31%) in cats specifically identified as lingual SCC. Among these, 30 canine and 56 feline cases were diagnosed with lingual SCC through core biopsy. Surgical resection of lingual SCC was performed in 18 canine and 13 feline cases, while clinical information was unavailable for 4 dogs and 5 cats. Finally, 14 canine cases and 8 feline cases were selected for analysis. The degree of differentiation of oral SCC was classified according to the degree of keratinization, following the criteria described in Veterinary Oral and Maxillofacial Pathology, which are derived from the classification system used for human oral SCC [36]. REs were selected from 182 canine and 11 feline cases diagnosed as ulcerative lesions of the tongue lacking histological features of tumors. Finally, 9 cases from each group were randomly selected for analysis as REs. All canine RE cases and 5 feline cases were diagnosed as sublingual nodules/polyps. One feline case was diagnosed as eosinophilic granuloma, while another presented an ulcer with papillomavirus infection. Two feline cases exhibited ulcers of unknown cause. Normal tongue samples were collected from three dogs and three cats through routine necropsies at Azabu University to assess the immunohistochemical phenotypes of normal mucosa. The clinicopathological information of these cases is summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Histopathology and immunohistochemistry

Formalin-fixed tissues from the normal mucosa, REs, and SCCs of the tongue were routinely processed, and 4-μm-thick serial sections were prepared. PCEs were selected from paraffin blocks within the same SCC case, including margin evaluation specimens. In this study, PCEs were defined as the transitional mucosa between the tumor and normal mucosa, and areas with the greatest atypia were selected from multiple sections. Immunohistochemistry was performed using the primary antibodies listed in Table 1. ​The staining intensity and expression patterns of CK13 and CK17 were assessed in the lateral mucosa of the normal tongue, lingual SCCs, PCEs, and REs. Evaluations were conducted based on these expressions by assessing the percentage of positive cells in PCEs and REs [3]. The quantitative assessment followed the grading system reported by Boricic et al. [3], in which the expression of CK13 and CK17 was assessed on a 5-point scale without considering staining intensity, a criterion we also adhered to. CK13 was graded as follows: grade 0 (100% positive cells; except basal cells, which are negative in normal epithelium), grade 1 (>75% positive cells), grade 2 (>50% to ≤75% positive cells), grade 3 (>5% to ≤50% positive cells), grade 4 (≤5% of positive cells). CK17 was graded as follows: grade 0 (without expression), grade 1 (≤5% positive cells), grade 2 (5% to ≤50% positive cells), grade 3 (>50% to ≤75% positive cells), grade 4 (>75% positive cells). Based on the combined pattern of the CK profiles in normal mucosae and SCCs, we classified the mucosal CK expression of PCEs and REs into three groups. Type A was defined by a CK13 and CK17 expression pattern similar to normal mucosa, with diffuse expression of CK13 and negativity for CK17 or a basal/parabasal localization pattern of CK17 expression. Type B showed an inverse distribution of CK13 and CK17 expression throughout the mucosal lesions, corresponding to the CK profiles in lingual SCCs. In other patterns, epithelial tissues within PCEs showed double-positivity for CK13 and CK17. These double-positive cells were observed either in a distribution restricted to the boundary area or in a wide distribution across the lesion. These cases were classified as Type C. Ki-67 expression patterns in PCEs and REs were evaluated in reference to its expression in the lateral mucosa of the normal tongue. Based on previous studies, Ki-67 expression was graded on a three-tier scale as an indicator of proliferative activity in PCEs and REs [20]. In brief, Ki-67 expression was classified into three patterns: normal (grade 0), basal and parabasal (grade 1), and high expression (grade 2), where Ki-67-positive nuclei extend beyond the basal and parabasal layers into the superficial layer. p53 expression was evaluated in the lateral mucosa of the normal tongue, SCCs, PCEs, and REs. In SCCs, it was assessed as either positive or negative. For PCEs and REs, p53 assessment followed a modified p53 grading system for human OED, as described in a previous study [20, 52]. Previous studies classified p53 expression into four categories based on TP53 gene mutation analysis: normal type, basal cell type and parabasal cell type, high expression type, and null type [52]. However, due to the inability to perform TP53 gene mutation analysis in this study, it was not possible to determine whether the p53-negative pattern corresponded to the null type; thus, it was classified as grade 0. Consistent with previous studies, p53 positivity in basal/parabasal cells was defined as the basal/parabasal cell type when p53 was diffusely positive, with more than 50% of cells exhibiting positivity [52]. High expression was defined as diffuse expression of p53 in the superficial layers rather than the parabasal layers. In summary, p53 expression was categorized into three groups: negative or normal (grade 0), basal to parabasal (grade 1), and high expression (grade 2).

Morphological evaluation of PCEs and REs

Two veterinary pathologists (TK and NA) classified OED into three grades (mild, moderate, and severe) using HE-stained specimens of PCE from dogs and cats. This assessment was based on the human WHO classification of head and neck cancer [64]. Mild dysplasia is characterized by cytological atypia confined to the basal third of the epithelium and includes squamous hyperplasia as well as basal and parabasal cell hyperplasia. Moderate dysplasia extends to the middle third of the epithelium, while severe dysplasia involves the upper third, encompassing lesions previously classified as carcinoma in situ [46, 64]. Furthermore, based on a comprehensive review of the malignant transformation risk of OED, canine and feline PCEs were classified using this three-tiered evaluation system, with mild dysplasia OED categorized as low-grade and moderate to severe dysplasia OEDs as high-grade [2]. To investigate whether this binary grade classification correlates with morphological malignancy, we developed a new OED scoring system for the quantitative assessment of histological atypia, based on the WHO classification of human OED [64]. The human WHO diagnostic criteria list a total of 28 histological features for OED evaluation, comprising 16 architectural features and 12 cytological features [64]. In this study, 9 architectural features and 11 cytological features were derived by excluding or integrating items from the 28 criteria listed in the WHO classification and were used as the evaluation criteria for the OED score (shown in Table 2 and Supplementary Table 2). Among the 16 architectural features listed in the WHO classification, 9 features that reflected abnormalities in the structure, differentiation, and proliferation of stratified squamous epithelium were adopted [15]. In addition, “reduced keratinocyte cohesion” and “sharply defined margin to changes”, which are considered indicators of neoplastic transformation of epithelial cells, were also included [15]. Among the 12 cytological features, 11 were selected because they represent general cytological atypia. The remaining feature, single-cell keratinization, which is related to abnormal epithelial differentiation, was evaluated together with two architectural features under the integrated category “abnormal keratinization” [15]. Accordingly, “generalized premature keratinization,” “keratin pearls in rete processes,” and “single-cell keratinization” were integrated and evaluated collectively as “abnormal keratinization” in this study. Likewise, “mitoses high in epithelium” and “mitoses in maturing cells” were categorized as “locations of mitotic figures,” as they assess mitotic figures in maturing cells [15]. Since PCE was selected and evaluated from the areas with the greatest degree of atypia, and the histological characteristics of OED in dogs and cats remain unclear, “multiple different patterns of dysplasia” and “multifocal or skip lesions” were excluded from the evaluation criteria in this study. Two veterinary pathologists (TK and NA) evaluated and graded each feature as absent (0), slight (1), or marked (2). The evaluation of basal cell clustering/nesting was conducted with reference to the assessment method described in the review article on diagnostic criteria for human OED [15, 53, 54]. Atypical mitosis and karyorrhectic cells (mitotic-like figures) were evaluated according to the review by Donovan et al. [8]. In most cases, due to the small tissue area (making it impossible to measure mitotic figures in 2.37 mm2), increased mitotic activity was evaluated by calculating the number of typical mitotic figures per unit area. Furthermore, the average value was set as the cutoff, with cases below the average classified as (1) and those above the average classified as (2). For atypical mitotic figures, the atypical mitosis ratio (the number of atypical mitotic figures/the number of typical mitotic figures/area) was calculated and evaluated [28]. Similarly, the average value was set as the cutoff, with cases below the average classified as (1) and those above the average classified as (2). Based on these characteristics, an OED score (ranging from 0 to 40) was calculated as an index of morphological malignancy. The morphological malignancy of high-grade OED in PCE was compared with that of low-grade OED and REs. Additionally, in each group, the correlation between the OED score and the immunostaining grades of CK13, CK17, Ki-67, and p53 was analyzed.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using EZR software (version 1.64) [21] and the graphical user interface for R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). To evaluate the correlation between variables, Spearman rank correlation was employed. The comparison of variables between each group was performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by pair-wise comparison using the Mann–Whitney U test. A value of P<0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

RESULTS

In SCC cases, no sex or breed predilection was observed, and the animals were middle to old age, with a mean age of 11.6 ± 2.4 years for dogs and 14.4 ± 2.4 years for cats. The majority of canine SCCs (64%) were located in the ventral mucosa of the apex, whereas feline SCCs were primarily located in the ventral mucosa of the tongue body (50%) or the lingual frenum (25%). Similarly, in ulcerated cases, no sex or breed predilection was observed. However, the animals were younger than those with SCC, with a mean age of 10.6 ± 3.1 years for dogs and 9.1 ± 5.8 years for cats. The ulcerated lesions were predominantly located in the lateral-to-ventral mucosa of the tongue body (33% of dogs and 22% of cats) or the lingual frenum (44% of dogs and 56% of cats). The typical histological features of the PCEs and REs in both animals are shown in Figs. 1 and 2. Both PCEs and REs exhibited epithelial thickening accompanied by elongation of the rete processes, with elongation being more pronounced in dogs (93%) than in cats (63%). “Drop-shaped rete processes”, defined as rete processes with a broader base than the apex, were primarily observed in high-grade OED (Figs. 1A and 2A, and Supplementary Fig. 1A and 1C). A disorganized epithelial layer structure, with layers that are difficult to distinguish, was characteristic of high-grade OED but was not observed in low-grade OED (Figs. 1 and 2, and Supplementary Fig. 1). In most RE cases, except for the epithelial tongue region (the leading edge of migrating epithelial cells covering the ulcer surface), disturbances in the stratified epithelial structure were mild, and the distinction between epithelial layers remained clear (Figs. 1C, 1F, 2C and 2F). However, this was not the case in some instances (Supplementary Fig. 1F). Apoptotic cells were observed in canine PCE and RE, as well as in feline PCE. In most cases, only a small number of apoptotic cells were detected; however, moderate to extensive apoptosis was observed in two dogs (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Additionally, in all RE cases, parakeratosis was present in the mucosal epithelium (Figs. 1C and 2C). In PCE cases, parakeratosis was observed in 57% of dogs and 75% of cats (Fig. 2A and 2B); however, hyperorthokeratosis was not detected (Supplementary Table 3). “Altered polarity or disorganization of basal cells” was observed in all cases of PCE and RE (Figs. 1D–F and 2D–F). Basal cell clustering/nesting was also observed in most cases of PCE and RE (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Findings such as abnormal variations in cell and nuclear size, as well as abnormal variations in cell and nuclear shape, which are generally recognized as indicators of cellular atypia, were observed to varying degrees in both PCE and RE. An increased nuclear-to-cytoplasmic (N:C) ratio was commonly observed in dogs (79% of PCEs and 67% of REs), whereas in cats, this characteristic was less common (50% of PCEs and 44% of REs). Mitoses in maturing cells (referred to as locations of mitotic figures), atypical mitotic figures and nuclear hyperchromasia were mainly observed in PCE cases but were rare in RE (Supplementary Table 4). Karyorrhectic cells were observed in only two cases of PCE and one case of RE, with moderate levels detected in one case of high-grade OED (Fig. 1D). Dysplastic lesions characterized by architectural and cytological atypia exhibited an abrupt transition to normal epithelium in high-grade OED, whereas in low-grade OED and RE, they transitioned more gradually into the surrounding mucosa (Fig. 2A–C). In PCE, lymphoplasmacytic infiltration was mainly observed in the subepithelial region; however, this was rare in cats. In RE, lymphoplasmacytic infiltration was rare, whereas neutrophil and macrophage infiltration was more noticeable (Supplementary Table 3). The detailed results are shown in Supplementary Tables 3 and 4. The median OED score for canine PCE was 19 (range, 10–32), while that for canine RE was also 18 (range, 15–25). The median OED score for feline PCE was 12 (range, 6–26), whereas that for feline RE was 12 (range, 7–21). Although there was no significant difference in OED scores between PCEs and REs (P=0.90), high-grade OED lesions exhibited significantly higher scores compared to both low-grade OED and RE (P<0.01, Fig. 3). In both species, high-grade OED lesions tended to have higher scores than low-grade OED and RE, although a statistically significant difference was observed only in dogs (P=0.03, Fig. 3). These results indicate that the OED score is useful for reflecting the grading of dysplasia rather than distinguishing between PCE and RE. No association was found between lesion location and histological atypia.

To investigate whether CK profile changes occur during the mucosal carcinogenesis process in dogs and cats, we first examined CK13 and CK17 expression in normal and cancerous lingual mucosa in these animals. Additionally, we analyzed the distribution of Ki-67-positive nuclei in normal lingual mucosa. In normal lingual mucosa, CK13 was positive in the cytoplasm of all cell layers except the basal layer in 50% of cases (2 of 3 dogs and 1 of 3 cats). In the remaining cases, diffuse CK13 positivity was observed throughout the entire epithelial layer (1 of 3 dogs and 2 of 3 cats; Supplementary Fig. 2A and 2E). On the other hand, CK17 was negative in only one feline case, while the remaining dogs and cats showed CK17 positivity in the basal layer (3 of 3 dogs and 2 of 3 cats; Supplementary Fig. 2B and 2F). In both animals, Ki-67-positive nuclei were scattered among basal and parabasal cells and tended to be found in basal cells in cases with a thin mucosal epithelium (Supplementary Fig. 2C and 2G). In all cases of SCC, loss or decreased expression of CK13 and diffuse expression of CK17 were observed (Supplementary Fig. 3). CK13 was reactive in the keratinized area at the center of the foci, and while most cases showed only weak expression (50% of dogs and 38% of cats), a single feline case exhibited moderate expression. No correlation was observed between CK expression and the degree of differentiation (Supplementary Fig. 3C and 3G). The detailed results are shown in Supplementary Table 5.

To evaluate the usefulness of CK13, CK17, and Ki-67 immunohistochemistry in distinguishing OED from RE in dogs and cats, we analyzed CK expression profiles and cellular proliferation in the OED and RE groups. In canine PCE, decreased CK13 expression was observed in 43% of cases, whereas de novo CK17 expression was detected in 57% of cases (Fig. 4A, 4B, 4E and 4F). In contrast, in feline PCE, decreased CK13 expression was observed in 88% of cases, and all cases exhibited de novo CK17 expression (Fig. 4C, 4D, 4G and 4H). In RE, decreased CK13 expression was observed in 56% of dogs and 67% of cats, while all cases showed de novo CK17 expression. CK17 was mainly expressed in epithelial cells within the mucosa at the ulcer margin, particularly in the region commonly referred to as the epithelial tongue (Supplementary Fig. 4). Increased Ki-67 expression was commonly observed in both PCE (64% of dogs and 75% of cats) and RE (67% in both animals). In both groups, an increased Ki-67 expression in the nuclei of basal and parabasal cells was typically observed (Fig. 4I–L and Supplementary Fig. 4). However, grade 2 expression, characterized by Ki-67-positive nuclei extending beyond the basal and parabasal layers into the superficial layer, was observed exclusively in PCE (Fig. 4J and 4K). A comparison of CK13, CK17, and Ki-67 grades between high-grade and low-grade OED revealed that each marker had a higher grade in high-grade OED. Statistically significant differences were noted in CK13 and Ki-67 grades (Fig. 5). Conversely, while a statistically significant difference in CK13 grades was found only between RE and low-grade OED, no significant differences in CK13 or CK17 grades were observed between RE and high-grade OED (Fig. 5A and 5B). However, a statistically significant difference in Ki-67 grade was detected between RE and high-grade OED (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, across all cases, including both PCE and RE, a statistically significant correlation was observed between CK13 and CK17 grades (r=0.73, P<0.01), indicating an inverse relationship in expression between CK13 and CK17 (Fig. 5D). Next, we compared mucosal CK expression patterns and proliferative activity in PCE and RE. In high-grade OED, all 9 cases were classified as type B and exhibited an expression pattern similar to SCC (Fig. 4B, 4C, 4F and 4G). In contrast, among canine low-grade OED cases, 5 of 8 were classified as type A, displaying an expression pattern similar to normal mucosa (Fig. 4A and 4E), while the remaining 3 cases were classified as type C. All feline low-grade OED cases were classified as type C (Fig. 4D and 4H). For RE, all cases were classified as type C, except for four cases: one canine and three feline cases classified as type B (Supplementary Fig. 4). When comparing CK profile changes and their relationship with cellular proliferation, all PCE cases with Ki-67 grade 2 were classified as type B (Fig. 4J and 4K). In contrast, PCE cases with Ki-67 grade 0 or grade 1 exhibited various CK expression patterns (Fig. 4I and 4L). The detailed results are shown in Table 3.

Finally, we evaluated the significance of p53 expression in mucosal carcinogenesis in dogs and cats. Diffuse p53 expression was observed in 7 SCC cases (29% of dogs and 38% of cats; Supplementary Table 5), whereas p53-positive nuclei were rarely observed in normal mucosa (Supplementary Fig. 2). Increased p53 expression was observed in 32% of PCEs, while 56% of REs showed increased expression (Fig. 6 and Table 3). Grade 2, indicating abnormal p53 expression, was characterized by p53-positive nuclei extending beyond the basal and parabasal layers into the superficial layer and was observed in only two cases of high-grade OED (cases C1 and F2; Fig. 6D and 6F). All other cases with increased p53 expression were classified as grade 1, where expression was restricted to the basal and parabasal layers (Fig. 6B). Comparison of p53 expression between SCC and PCE revealed that p53-positive SCC cases did not exhibit abnormal p53 expression in PCE, where grade 0 or 1 expression was typically observed. Among the two PCE cases showing abnormal p53 expression, one exhibited high p53 expression in both the SCC and PCE regions (case F2; Table 3 and Supplementary Table 5). In contrast, in the other case, p53 was undetectable in the SCC region but showed abnormal expression in the PCE region, demonstrating a clear difference in p53 expression status between SCC and PCE (case C1; Fig. 6E–G).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we demonstrated the presence of epithelial dysplasia in the pericancerous epithelium of lingual SCC in dogs and cats. Some of these lesions exhibited histopathological features consistent with severe OED, which is associated with a higher risk of malignant transformation in humans. Morphological malignancy was evaluated using an OED score based on the human WHO classification [64], revealing that high-grade OED exhibited significantly higher scores than low-grade OED and RE. Notably, drop-shaped rete processes, irregular stratification, and sharply defined margin to changes were characteristic features of high-grade OED. Drop-shaped rete processes, which reflect lateral or downward cell maturation and growth, are thought to result from the reduplication or expansion of basal cells [39, 59]. Additionally, irregular stratification indicates disordered stratification and maturation in the squamous epithelium [15]. Since both features reflect the loss of epithelial organization, they have long been used as diagnostic criteria for OED [43]. In particular, drop-shaped rete processes are considered reproducible and specific histopathological features of OED [26, 59]. In this study, low-grade OED and REs exhibited “altered polarity or disorganization of basal cells” and elongation of rete processes, but drop-shaped rete processes were observed in only three cases of canine RE (33% of canine RE cases, 17% of all RE cases). Unlike those in high-grade OED, these cases retained an easily identifiable stratified epithelial structure with well-organized epithelial layers. Moreover, OED is characterized by a clonal keratinocyte population, resulting in an abrupt transition from normal to atypical epithelium [15]. In contrast, REA tends to gradually taper off with increasing distance from the ulcer, rather than exhibiting a sharply defined border [15]. Consistent with this, high-grade OED in this study exhibited sharply defined lesions, making it easy to distinguish from the surrounding mucosa, whereas epithelial stratification and organization were maintained, with a gradual transition to the surrounding epithelium in low-grade OED and most RE cases. The presence of sharply defined dysplastic lesions with irregular epithelial stratification and drop-shaped rete processes suggests abnormal differentiation, maturation, and proliferation in stratified squamous epithelium. Therefore, these features were considered relevant diagnostic criteria for OED in dogs and cats. Reflecting these abnormalities in differentiation and proliferation, high-grade OED exhibited marked cellular atypia. However, apart from hyperchromasia, no significant differences were observed between OED and RE. This suggests that cellular atypia alone is insufficient to distinguish between OED and RE. Nuclear hyperchromasia is generally considered an indicator of rapid cell growth, cell division, and protein synthesis [13], suggesting increased cellular proliferation in high-grade OED. On the other hand, mitoses in maturing cells, reflecting abnormal proliferation of keratinocytes [15], were frequently observed in PCE. Most mitotic figures were located in the lower two-thirds of the epithelium, near the basal cell layer; however, in one case, they were also observed in the upper one-third. Notably, mitoses in maturing cells were considered a diagnostic feature of OED in dogs and cats.

On the other hand, the PCE analyzed in this study did not exhibit the findings of “altered keratin pattern for oral sub-site”, “verrucous/papillary architecture”, or “extension along salivary ducts”, which are described as diagnostic indicators in the human WHO classification [64]. In humans, hyperorthokeratosis is observed in lesions such as leukoplakia and proliferative verrucous leukoplakia, which are known as pathological changes occurring in both non-keratinized and keratinized stratified squamous epithelium [51]. Leukoplakia is a clinical diagnostic term for a white oral mucosal lesion, defined by the exclusion of other known diseases, and encompasses a range of lesions from hyperplastic changes to severe OED [57]. This condition is the most commonly encountered in clinical practice in the human oral mucosa [62]; however, to the best of our knowledge, leukoplakia has not been reported in the oral cavity of dogs or cats [14, 45]. In this study, abnormal keratinization was commonly observed in dogs and frequently detected in cats. Similarly, parakeratosis was observed at a comparable frequency, indicating abnormalities in the keratinization process of the mucosal epithelium. However, hyperorthokeratosis was not detected, suggesting that this may be a distinct feature from human OED [15]. In the oral mucosa of dogs and cats, gene expression related to keratinocyte differentiation may differ from that in humans in response to tissue reactions.

In humans, high-grade OED has been reported to be associated with a greater risk of malignant transformation [62]. Additionally, OED involvement at resection margins has been linked to a tendency for recurrence [12]. However, in this study, OED formation at the resection margin was observed in only one canine case, and postoperative follow-up was not conducted for this case. Among the five cases that underwent postoperative re-examination, recurrence of lingual SCC was observed in two cases (one dog and one cat). However, neither SCC nor OED was detected at the resection margins (data not shown), making it difficult to clarify the clinical significance of OED. To elucidate the clinical significance of high-grade OED in dogs and cats, it will be necessary to detect and excise OED lesions at an early stage and analyze a larger cohort of cases.

In humans, CK13 expression has been reported in non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelia and urothelium but not in keratinized stratified squamous epithelia such as the skin [29, 34]. Previous studies on CK13 expression in animals are limited, with reports based on paraffin-embedded specimens in dogs and frozen specimens in cats [19, 48]. These studies demonstrated CK13 expression in non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium, while variations were observed among humans, dogs, and cats in the urothelium and skin. Such differences may reflect species-specific patterns of keratin expression, potential antibody cross-reactivity, or methodological variation in antibody selection and immunohistochemical procedures. In the present study, as a preliminary investigation, we examined CK13 expression using tissue microarrays (TMAs) of canine and feline tissues. We consistently observed strong CK13 expression in the oral mucosa of both species; however, it was absent in the epidermis, excluding the basal layer. This finding supports its utility as a marker for non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium (Supplementary Fig. 5). In humans, CK17 is expressed in myoepithelial cells of the mammary and salivary glands, in basal cells of epithelial tissues, and in hair follicles [34, 60]. CK17 expression has also been reported in canine tissues using methods different from ours, but the observed distribution differed substantially from that in humans [48]. In our TMA-based analysis, CK17 expression was detected in myoepithelial cells and ductal basal cells of the mammary and salivary glands, as well as in hair follicles, yielding a pattern similar to that in humans (Supplementary Fig. 5). Although larger-scale validation is needed, it is feasible to conclude that the expression patterns of CK13 and CK17 in normal tissues of dogs and cats closely resembled those in humans.

Abnormal keratin patterns have been reported in cancer, and in recent years, changes in keratin expression have been considered to play a role in tumor formation and prognosis [22, 34]. In human oral SCC, the inverse expression pattern of CK13 and CK17 has been reported [24]. Decreased KRT13 expression has been associated with poor prognosis, while CK17 expression has been linked to enhanced cell proliferation and migration [7, 23, 41]. In this study, we analyzed CK13 and CK17 expression in normal mucosa and lingual SCC of dogs and cats and demonstrated that CK13 and CK17 expression was reversed in cancerous tissues. This result is consistent with previous reports from RNA-seq analysis of canine oral SCC, which showed increased CK17 expression and decreased CK13 expression in cancerous tissues compared to normal tissues [14]. In the lingual cancer tissues of dogs and cats, CK13 expression was observed in keratinized areas and differentiated tumor cells. Similar CK13 expression has also been reported in human oral SCC, particularly in well-differentiated and at the center of cancer foci [24, 41]. Considering CK13 expression in normal mucosa and previous reports in humans, CK expression in the lingual SCC of dogs and cats appears to be a specific response. Therefore, in dogs and cats, as in humans, the inverse expression of CK13 and CK17 may also occur during mucosal carcinogenesis.

In humans, OED is caused by abnormal epithelial proliferation, resulting in disrupted differentiation and maturation [59, 64]. Consequently, the evaluation of the CK profile and proliferative activity using immunostaining has been demonstrated to be useful for its diagnosis [33, 50, 66]. Notably, high-grade OED has been reported to carry a greater risk of malignant transformation into oral cancer compared to low-grade OED, characterized by decreased CK13 expression and increased CK17 and Ki-67 expression [66]. Furthermore, the loss of CK13 expression and the increased expression of Ki-67 have been identified as potential independent diagnostic markers for high-grade OED [66]. In this study, an inverse relationship between CK13 and CK17 expression was also observed in the PCE of dogs and cats. CK13 expression in high-grade OED was significantly decreased compared to low-grade OED, while cell proliferative activity, as indicated by Ki-67, was significantly higher in high-grade OED than in low-grade OED. However, because the number of feline high-grade OED cases was very small, it was not possible to clarify distinct differences between dogs and cats. CK13 is known as a keratinocyte differentiation marker [16], and it has been reported that Krt13 knockout in transgenic mice induces the inhibition of differentiation and excessive proliferation in the lingual mucosal epithelium [56]. In canine and feline OED, keratinocyte differentiation abnormalities, indicated by decreased CK13 expression, may also be associated with increased proliferative activity. CK17 is also known as a stress-responsive keratin and is expressed under stress conditions such as skin wound healing, inflammation, and autoimmune diseases [61]. In the epidermis and oral mucosa at wound edges, CK17 expression has been reported in regenerative epithelium [23, 32]. In canine and feline RE, CK17 expression was observed in regenerative epithelium, making it difficult to distinguish OED from RE based on CK17 expression.

In human oral cancer, TP53 is the most frequently mutated gene and is believed to play a role in the early changes associated with the carcinogenesis of the oral mucosa [52]. Notably, immunohistochemical staining results for p53 in human oral SCC and OED have been reported to correlate with TP53 mutation status [27, 52, 55]. Similarly, increased p53 expression in feline oral SCC has been reported to be associated with TP53 mutations [47]. Even though large-scale TP53 mutation analyses have not yet been conducted in canine oral SCC, it has been suggested that the frequency of TP53 mutations may be lower compared to humans [30]. In this study, p53 overexpression in oral SCC was higher in cats (38%) than in dogs (29%), reflecting the TP53 mutation status reported in previous studies on oral SCC. While p53-positive nuclei were rarely observed in normal mucosa, they were detected in the basal and parabasal cells of RE cases (6 of 9 dogs, 4 of 9 cats). TP53 is known as the “guardian of the genome” and plays a role in cellular stress responses [65]. Renzi et al. previously reported that p53 expression was observed in the hyperplastic basal layer of epithelial components in eosinophilic granuloma and chronic gingivostomatitis in cats [47]. In PCE, unlike in normal mucosa and RE, p53 overexpression was observed in two cases of high-grade OED (one dog and one cat). This abnormal p53 expression occurred locally at high levels in dysplastic epithelial tissue, suggesting lesion formation associated with p53 abnormalities. Furthermore, in one dog of these cases, the p53 expression status differed between SCC and OED, revealing the presence of multiple lesions with distinct genetic backgrounds within the epithelial mucosa. In human OED and oral SCC, nonsense and frameshift mutations in TP53 result in structural alterations of the p53 protein, leading to loss of p53 expression in immunohistochemical staining [17, 38, 52]. Although p53-negative cases were also observed in high-grade OED, the extent to which this correlates with TP53 mutations in dogs and cats remains unclear and requires large-scale p53 mutation analysis.

In summary, this study analyzed the pericancerous epithelium of lingual SCC in dogs and cats and revealed histopathological features similar to those of high-grade OED in humans, suggesting the possibility of field cancerization in the oral mucosa of animals. Additionally, a decrease in CK13 expression and de novo CK17 expression were observed in oral SCC and OED, and immunohistochemical profiles indicated that abnormal differentiation and proliferation of keratinocytes may be involved in OED. However, due to the lack of sufficient follow-up, it was difficult to clarify the clinical significance of high-grade OED.
