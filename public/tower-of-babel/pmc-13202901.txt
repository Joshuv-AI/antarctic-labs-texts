CycleGAN-based image-to-image translation for synthetic contrast enhancement in non-contrast cardiac CT: a ViT-CNN hybrid deep learning approach
Authors: Doyoung Park, Jinsoo Kim, Xucong Ruan, Weien Chow, Lohendran Baskaran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundContrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) is essential for visualizing cardiac structures and stenosis; however, it is unsuitable for patients with contrast agent allergies. To address this, we propose RICAUNet-v2, a ViT-CNN hybrid architecture designed for CycleGAN-based similarity-guided unpaired NCCT-to-CECT translation.MethodsRICAUNet-v2 integrates RICA blocks and Vision Transformer (ViT) layers to capture local and global features. We compared our architecture against UNet, RICAU-Net, MaskGAN, and a supervised pix2pix-RICAUNet-v2 using metrics including Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM), and Fréchet Inception Distance (FID). Statistical significance was assessed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and a blinded reader study conducted by two clinicians evaluated perceptual realism.ResultsCycleGAN-RICAUNet-v2 achieved the lowest FID score (0.0639). Although pix2pix-RICAUNet-v2 attained the best MAE (92.72) and SSIM (0.5380), and MaskGAN achieved the highest PSNR (13.50), the superior FID score of CycleGAN-RICAUNet-v2 indicates better perceptual realism for unpaired translation. Ablation studies confirmed that using three-channel windowed inputs and similarity-guided sampling reduced the FID compared to single-channel input and unpaired cross-patient sampling (FID of 0.0802). Clinical evaluation demonstrated 100% precision and 15% recall, with a Cohen’s kappa of 0.40, suggesting that most misclassifications of real images as synthetic may be due to conservative or expectation bias rather than a lack of realism.ConclusionsRICAUNet-v2 improves perceptual realism in similarity-guided unpaired NCCT-to-CECT translation by integrating ViT and RICA blocks. Although further clinically relevant validation of diagnostic utility is required, the proposed architecture demonstrates potential for contrast-free cardiac CT enhancement.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03446-9.

Background

Patients often undergo cardiac computed tomography (CT) to evaluate the condition of their hearts. Depending on the clinical focus, patients receive either non-contrast computed tomography (NCCT) or contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT). In cardiology, NCCT is typically used to quantify calcification in the coronary arteries, expressed as the coronary artery calcium (CAC) score, also known as the Agatston score. This score is widely recognized as a strong predictor of cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk [1]. Conversely, CECT is commonly employed to visualize more detailed cardiac structures, including the chambers, heart valves, coronary arteries, and the narrowing of the coronary artery lumen, known as stenosis [2, 3]. Although CECT offers greater diagnostic detail than NCCT, it has certain inherent disadvantages. First, CECT requires the administration of a contrast agent to enhance tissue contrast. Second, some patients cannot receive contrast agents due to the risk of adverse effects, such as allergic reactions, which vary depending on the type and dose of the agent [4, 5]. Third, CECT exposes patients to higher levels of radiation compared to NCCT, potentially increasing the risk of radiation-related adverse effects. Nevertheless, the clinical benefits and diagnostic value of CECT generally outweigh these drawbacks.

Artificial intelligence (AI) has advanced significantly and garnered global interest, being applied across various fields. It has also been actively introduced into the medical field to assist healthcare professionals through tasks such as segmentation, detection, and image generation [6–8]. In the domain of image generation, deep learning (DL) techniques are employed to generate target images from random noise vectors or to translate images from one domain to another. In the medical domain, image generation is typically framed as image-to-image (I2I) translation, which involves converting images from one modality to another. The Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) approach is one of the DL methods frequently used for I2I translation in medical applications. Among the various types of GANs, recent studies have applied Cycle-consistent GAN (CycleGAN) to generate synthetic contrast-enhanced computed tomography (sCECT) images from NCCT images, a process also known as NCCT-to-CECT translation [8, 9]. CycleGAN enables I2I translation using unpaired datasets by leveraging two generators and two discriminators. Each generator produces images in the target domain from the source domain, while each discriminator evaluates whether the generated images are real or fake. Consequently, CycleGAN is widely adopted in the medical domain, where obtaining fully paired image datasets across different modalities is challenging. However, using unpaired image datasets still presents certain issues, such as structural misalignment between NCCT and CECT in the pixel level, which leads to poor synthetic contrast generation performance [10]. Thus, most studies have employed fully paired rather than unpaired datasets [8]. In many medical CycleGAN studies, convolutional neural network (CNN)-based architectures such as UNet or ResNet have been used as generators [11–13]. CNNs have demonstrated remarkable performance across various tasks by extracting local features through multiple filters and convolutional operations. However, this local feature extraction can be a limitation for NCCT-to-CECT translation, as the model must capture global contextual transitions between the source and target domains.

Vision Transformer (ViT), first developed in 2020, interprets input images as sequences of patches, effectively bridging the gap between natural language processing and visual understanding by leveraging the Transformer’s global attention mechanism for image recognition [14]. ViT exhibits a strong capability to capture global context and long-range dependencies from the earliest processing layers through its self-attention mechanism. In contrast, CNN progressively enlarges their receptive fields via stacked convolutions and initially focus primarily on local features. ViT has also demonstrated superior transfer learning performance compared to CNNs when pre-trained on large-scale datasets. Fine-tuning a pre-trained ViT often yields strong results even with limited task-specific dat [15]. Moreover, ViT shows greater resilience to domain shifts, outperforming CNN counterparts in unsupervised domain adaptation scenarios [16, 17]. However, despite these advantages, ViT has notable drawbacks. Its most significant limitation is poor performance on small datasets due to a lack of strong inductive biases, making them heavily reliant on large-scale pre-training or extensive data augmentation [18, 19]. Additionally, ViT can struggle to capture local textures and high-frequency details, which are often essential for tasks such as fine-grained medical image analysis. This weakness contrasts with CNN’s inherent ability to detect local patterns through convolutional operations [14].

Building upon our previous study, RICAU-Net [20], which demonstrated outstanding performance in small lesion segmentation by learning the positional information of small and sparse CAC using Residual-Inspired Coordinate Attention (RICA) blocks, we propose a ViT-CNN hybrid architecture called RICAUNet-v2 as the generator for CycleGAN-based similarity-guided unpaired NCCT-to-CECT translation. This approach leverages the strengths of ViT while maintaining the efficient local feature extraction capabilities of CNN. Additionally, since the NCCT-to-CECT dataset is unpaired despite being scanned from the same patients, we designed an image similarity algorithm to identify the most similar NCCT-CECT pairs, thereby optimizing the mapping in the feature space.

Dataset and pre-processing

In this study, we collected cardiac NCCT and CECT datasets from 523 patients. Of these, 15 cases were excluded based on the criteria shown in Fig. 1. The remaining dataset of 508 patients was divided into 274 for training, 94 for validation, and 140 for test.

All cardiac NCCT and CECT scans were single-channel grayscale images with a resolution of 512\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}512 pixels. To optimize the visualization of anatomical structures and tissues in both domains, and to enable accurate image similarity comparisons, we applied three different CT window settings to each image and stacked the resulting images to create three-channel grayscale images. The first channel used the soft tissue window, clipping the Hounsfield Unit (HU) range to [-125, 225] with a window width (WW) of 350 and a window level (WL) of 50. The second channel used the mediastinal window, clipping the HU range to [-200, 300] with a WW of 500 and a WL of 50. The third channel used the bone window, clipping the HU range to [-500, 1500] with a WW of 2000 and a WL of 500. Finally, each three-channel image was normalized to a range of [-1, 1] independently for each channel.

Image similarity algorithm

Although the NCCT and CECT images were acquired from the same patient, differences in slice thickness resulted in a varying number of slices between the two image sets. To optimize feature space mapping during training, pairs of NCCT and CECT images from the same patient were prepared by matching the most similar slices. To facilitate this, an image similarity algorithm was proposed and employed in this study, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The NCCT images served as the reference for identifying the most similar CECT images, since NCCT scans contained fewer slices than CECT scans. For each patient, NCCT and CECT slices were denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X={\left\{{x}_{i}\right\}}_{i=1}^{N}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y={\left\{{y}_{j}\right\}}_{j=1}^{M}$$\end{document}, respectively. For similarity assessment, we computed the Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM) and Mean Absolute Error (MAE) between NCCT and CECT images in each channel, selecting pairs that exhibited the highest mean SSIM and lowest mean MAE simultaneously. If equal similarity values were obtained, the first encountered CECT slice was paired to maintain the sequential order. This approach allowed the algorithm to approximately evaluate not only pixel-level structural similarity but also image similarity across domains of luminance, contrast, and structure. Once an initial pair of non-contrast (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${x}_{i})$$\end{document} and contrast images \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left({y}_{j}\right)$$\end{document} was identified, the algorithm proceeded to search for the next pair starting from the subsequent index (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$j>{j}_{previous}$$\end{document}) of the paired CECT images, thereby maintaining the sequential order of CECT images when pairing with NCCT images and producing the same number of NCCT-CECT pairs as the number of NCCT slices per patient. The pseudocode of the similarity algorithm is shown in Supplementary Algorithm 1.

After running the image similarity algorithm to prepare the dataset, the final dataset comprised 13,003 pairs of NCCT and CECT images for training, 4,311 pairs for validation, and 6,720 pairs for test, respectively.

The overview of the CycleGAN-based approach

As noted in many CycleGAN-based studies, our study also employs two generators and two discriminators. The generators utilize a ViT-CNN hybrid architecture to fully leverage the global and local feature extraction capabilities of both architectures for synthetic contrast generation, while the discriminators use a PatchGAN architecture. Generator and discriminator

RICAUNet-v2 was used as the generator in the CycleGAN for this study. Its architecture is identical to that of RICAU-Net, except that ViT layers are incorporated into the last skip connection, as shown in Fig. 3. The output features of the last encoder block undergo a linear projection to produce embedded patches, which serve as the input tensors for the ViT block. Before the linear projection, the output features of the last encoder block, with shape (B, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{5}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{H}{16}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{W}{16}$$\end{document}), need to be converted into flattened 2D patches of shape (B, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{H \cdot W}{{(16\cdot patch\_dim)}^{2}}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{5}\cdot {patch\_dim}^{2}$$\end{document}) = (B, 1024, 1024) using the patch size of 1, where B represents the batch size, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{5}$$\end{document} denotes the number of channels in the fifth (last) encoder block, H and W are the height and width of the input image, and patch_dim denotes the patch dimension. The patches are then mapped to constant latent D-dimensional vectors through a trainable linear projection to produce patch embeddings of dimension D [14], where D denotes hidden size of 768. To preserve the sequential information of the patches, trainable positional embeddings are added element-wise to the patch embeddings before they are fed into the Transformer encoder. Through these processes, the feature map size feeding into the Transformer encoder has a shape of (B, 1024, 768). In this process, the class token was not utilized in our study. In our architecture, the Transformer encoder comprises 12 Transformer encoder blocks; each block contains 12 Multihead Self-Attention (MSA) and Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) blocks. Layer normalization (LN) and residual connections are applied before and after the MSA and MLP blocks, respectively [14]. The MLP block consists of two fully-connected layers and uses the GELU activation function. In our ViT implementation, we adopted the same hyperparameters as the ViT-Base model from [14]: hidden size D of 768, MLP size of 3072, 12 number of layers, and 12 number of heads. The output features of the ViT block, shaped (B, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{H\cdot W}{{(16 \cdot patch\_dim)}^{2}}$$\end{document}, D), are then reshaped to (B, D, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{H}{16 \cdot patch\_dim}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{W}{16 \cdot patch\_dim}$$\end{document}) before being fed into the decoding path. At the end of the decoding path, a hyperbolic tangent (Tanh) activation is applied to constrain the generated output to the range [-1, 1], matching the range of the normalized input image.

Since our input images were preprocessed to be three-channel grayscale, both L2 and SSIM losses were computed independently for each channel and averaged across channels.

For discriminators, the loss function for classifying the real or fake images is defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${L}_{D}={L}_{GAN}^{Real}+{L}_{GAN}^{Fake},$$\end{document}

Implementation detail and evaluation

All experiments were conducted using a PyTorch container image version 23.06 (2.1.0a0 + 4136153) with Python 3.10.6, utilizing 8 CPU cores (64 GB) from an AMD EPYC 7742 processor and four NVIDIA DGX-A100 GPUs, each equipped with 40 GB of RAM (totaling 160 GB). Since this approach is based on CycleGAN, two generators and the two discriminators were jointly trained from scratch for 100 epochs, with the training process taking approximately 135 h. To train the RICAUNet-v2 generators, pre-trained ViT weights were not used, as most pre-trained ViT weights are trained on ImageNet or ImageNet-21k datasets, which differ significantly from medical domain images [25]. Both generators and discriminators were trained with a batch size of 4 using the Adam optimizer. The initial learning rate was set to 1e-4 for the generators and 1e-6 for the discriminators, maintained constant during the first half of training, then linearly decreased to zero over the remaining epochs.

In this study, three pixel-based metrics: MAE, Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), and SSIM, along with a perceptual metrics: Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) score were employed to evaluate the I2I translation between CECT and sCECT images. Lower values of MAE and FID, and higher values of PSNR and SSIM indicate a better performance in synthetic contrast generation by the model. For comparisons against baseline and state-of-the-art (SOTA) models, the average across the three channels along with the standard deviation (SD) and 95% confidence interval (CI) was reported. For ablation studies, metrics were reported as a simple three-channel average to maintain table readability. Computational efficiency was evaluated using 100 test images. The inference time for each image was measured individually, and the per-slice processing time is reported as the mean ± SD. The normalized input images were denormalized back to their respective window settings (first channel: soft tissue window [-125, 225], second channel: mediastinal window [-200, 300], and third channel: bone window [-500, 1500]) to compute MAE, and PSNR in HU domain. To calculate SSIM, the input images were re-normalized to a range of [0, 1] for each channel to standardize the data range across all three channels. The FID score is an evaluation metric which measures the similarity between groups of generated and real images in the feature space by comparing the features extracted from a pre-trained InceptionV3 network [26]. Although many studies have used the ImageNet pre-trained InceptionV3 network in the medical domain, its relevance to medical imaging is limited [27–29]. In our study, we used the RadImageNet pre-trained InceptionV3 network to compute the FID score. RadImageNet is a large-scale medical imaging database comprising 1.35 million radiologic images from three modalities (CT, MRI, ultrasound) and 11 anatomical regions [30]. The FID was computed using the entire test set, comprising 6,720 real CECT and 6,720 sCECT images. Prior to feature extraction, all images were resized to 299 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} 299 pixels using bilinear interpolation. Feature vectors of 2048 dimensions were then extracted using the RadImageNet pre-trained InceptionV3 backbone. For each set, this resulted in a feature matrix of size 6720 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} 2048.

The FID was calculated according to the standard formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$FID={||{\mu}_{r}-{\mu}_{g}||}^{2}+{T}_{r}({\varSigma}_{r}+{\varSigma}_{g}-{2\left({\varSigma}_{r}{\varSigma}_{g}\right)}^{1/2}),$$\end{document}

where (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mu}_{r}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\varSigma}_{r}$$\end{document}) and (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mu}_{g}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\varSigma}_{g}$$\end{document}) represent the mean and covariance matrices of the feature distributions for real and generated (synthetic) images, respectively and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T}_{r}$$\end{document} denotes the trace operator.

Experiments and results

Quantitative and qualitative evaluation of sCECT

The performance of synthetic contrast generation was evaluated by comparing the sCECTs with their corresponding ground truth CECTs in the dataset, as presented in Table 1. Additionally, to assess the impact of the RICA block and ViT layers in NCCT-to-CECT translation, the vanilla UNet, RICAU-Net, RICAUNet-v2-Small, and supervised pix2pix using RICAUNet-v2 were also trained, validated, and tested as generators under the same environment and hyperparameters as RICAUNet-v2. RICAUNet-v2-Small is the proposed architecture using a smaller ViT with hyperparameters: hidden size D of 256, MLP size of 1024, and 8 number of heads. For pix2pix, all hyperparameters remained the same except that the initial learning rates for both the generator and discriminator were set to 2e-4, and the generator loss function was defined as GAN adversarial loss + 100 * L1 loss, where the GAN adversarial loss is the binary cross-entropy with logits loss. MaskGAN [31] was also adopted for performance comparison. MaskGAN was originally designed for MR-CT unpaired translation and utilized single-channel grayscale images as the input. Therefore, we used the bone window channel [-500, 1500] for MaskGAN training, and the three-windowing processing was applied to the resulting bone window single-channel grayscale output images for performance evaluation. As presented in Table 1, pix2pix-RICAUNet-v2 achieved the highest SSIM of 0.5380 ± 0.1140 (0.5353–0.5407) and the lowest MAE of 92.72 ± 41.17 (91.74–93.71), while MaskGAN obtained the highest PSNR value of 13.50 ± 2.04 (13.45–13.55). However, RICAUNet-v2 with CycleGAN achieved the lowest FID score of 0.0639, an important metric for evaluating the realism of the sCECT group relative to CECT. Although models such as RICAUNet-v2 with pix2pix and MaskGAN demonstrated better performances on pixel-based metrics, FID is often considered a more suitable evaluation metric for unpaired I2I translation because it compares groups of generated images in feature space, reducing bias introduced by pixel-level misalignments inherent in unpaired datasets. This suggests that, although other models may better align with specific pixel values, the sCECT images generated by RICAUNet-v2 with CycleGAN are perceptually more realistic than those produced by the other models.

Statistical analysis using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for MAE further validated these results, demonstrating that the performance of the proposed architecture was significantly different (p < 0.005) from baseline models such as UNet, RICAU-Net, and RICAUNet-v2-Small. Regarding computational efficiency, MaskGAN exhibited the fastest inference time of 4.13 ± 0.54 milliseconds (ms) per slice, whereas RICAUNet-v2 required 32.99 ± 0.16 ms per slice. Despite the longer inference time of the proposed architecture, the inclusion of RICA blocks enabled RICAUNet-v2 and RICAU-Net (FID of 0.0658) to consistently outperform UNet (FID of 0.0681) and MaskGAN (FID of 0.1682) in perceptual quality. The higher SSIM values observed in RICAUNet-v2 with pix2pix and UNet may be attributed to the nature of the unpaired image dataset. Although the proposed image similarity algorithm was used to match the most similar NCCT-CECT image pairs within the same patients, the positional coordinates of tissues, vessels, and bones in NCCT-CECT pairs differ at the pixel level, making the perceptual FID score a more robust evaluation metric for unpaired I2I translation. The per-channel performance of synthetic contrast generation using the three networks (UNet, RICAU-Net, and RICAUNet-v2) was also evaluated and is reported in Supplementary Table S1.

Additionally, it demonstrates that the proposed image similarity algorithm can use NCCT images as references to identify the most similar CECT images from the same patients, thereby constructing a dataset for similarity-guided unpaired NCCT-to-CECT translation. Ablation study

Four ablation studies were conducted to evaluate the design aspects of the proposed architecture, including the input channels and the image similarity algorithm. Table 2 shows that the three-channel grayscale inputs achieved better MAE and PSNR values of 95.36 and 13.31, respectively, compared to the single-channel model. While the single-channel model using the bone window demonstrated superior SSIM and FID scores of 0.5855 and 0.0474, respectively, this discrepancy arises because those values were calculated solely for a single-channel, whereas the three-channel metrics represent the mean across three different CT windows. When comparing pixel-based metrics between the single-channel model and the bone window of the three-channel model, the three-channel model still exhibited better MAE and PSNR values of 151.05 and 17.96, respectively. This outcome demonstrates that utilizing three-channel grayscale images is more effective for NCCT-to-CECT translation than using single-channel images. Furthermore, the effectiveness of the proposed image similarity algorithm was evaluated by comparing it against unpaired cross-patient sampling (NCCT and CECT slices sampled from different patients). The similarity-guided unpaired translation resulted in a significantly lower FID of 0.0639 compared to 0.0802 for the unpaired cross-patient sampling. This suggests that matching the most similar NCCT-CECT pairs within the same patients when the number of slices are different facilitates the model in producing more realistic sCECT images. Additionally, to ensure a fair comparison with the three-channel model, three-windowing processing was applied to the outputs of the single-channel model. The three-channel input model exhibited better FID, MAE, and PSNR values (0.0639, 95.36, and 13.31) compared to the three-windowing processing for the single-channel model (0.0693, 97.93, and 13.13), indicating that providing three-channel grayscale information at the input stage is a more optimal choice. Figure 6 illustrates that sCECT images generated using the three-channel grayscale images exhibit higher contrast around the coronary artery tissues compared to those generated from single-channel images.

Clinical evaluation

Two cardiologists or radiologists with extensive training in cardiac CT interpretation participated in a blinded reader study to evaluate the perceptual realism of sCECT images generated by the proposed architecture. A total of 60 CT images were assessed: 30 real CECT images (positive class) and 30 corresponding sCECT images generated by the proposed CycleGAN-RICAUNet-v2 using paired NCCT scans. The 60 images used for the clinical evaluation are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. The images were randomly shuffled, and the clinicians were informed that the dataset contained both real and synthetic images but were blinded to the proportion of each class.

The aggregated results of the clinical evaluation from the two readers are presented in the confusion matrix shown in Fig. 8. The individual reader performances and corresponding metrics are provided in Supplementary Fig. 2. Both readers exhibited trends consistent with the aggregated results. Figure 8 shows that the accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score were 57.5%, 100%, 15%, and 26.09%, respectively. The precision of 100% signifies that neither clinician misclassified any sCECT images as real CECT images. However, the notably low recall (15%) indicates that only 9 out of 60 real CECT images were correctly identified as real, while the majority (51 out of 60) were misclassified as sCECT images. These findings may be attributed to expectation bias among the readers, who were aware that they were evaluating AI-synthesized CECT images. Consequently, the clinicians might have adopted a stricter decision criterion for real CECT, leading them to classify images with minor artifacts or noise as synthetic.

Inter-rater agreement was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa coefficient (κ). The κ value of 0.40 indicates fair-to-moderate agreement between the two clinicians, suggesting that conservative bias in classification was consistent across both raters. Although the sCECT images were not indistinguishable from the real CECT images under the given high-pressure conditions, the finding that the majority of real CECT images were misclassified as synthetic suggests that the sCECT images exhibit a high degree of perceptual similarity to clinical data, though they do not fully capture the realism.

Discussion

In this study, we proposed a ViT-CNN hybrid architecture called RICAUNet-v2, which serves as the generator for CycleGAN-based similarity-guided unpaired NCCT-to-CECT translation. The proposed architecture was compared against the vanilla UNet, RICAU-Net, a smaller RICAUNet-v2, MaskGAN [31], and a supervised pix2pix implementation using the RICAUNet-v2 generator. The quantitative analysis showed that pix2pix-RICAUNet-v2 achieved the best results in SSIM (0.5380 ± 0.1140 (0.5353–0.5407)) and MAE (92.72 ± 41.17 (91.74–93.71)), and MaskGAN showed the highest PSNR values (13.50 ± 2.04 (13.45–13.55)), while CycleGAN-RICAUNet-v2 achieved the lowest FID score of 0.0639. This discrepancy between the pixel-based metrics and perceptual metric may be attributed to the structural misalignment at the pixel level between NCCT and CECT in the unpaired image dataset. Since FID compares the groups of images in feature space, it reduces the bias introduced by these misalignments, making it a more robust evaluation metric to assess the realism of the sCECT images. Our qualitative analysis supported these findings. The sCECT images generated by RICAUNet-v2 also exhibited more realistic shapes and contrast of cardiac structures, including the descending thoracic aorta, ascending aorta, left ventricle, and CAC in LAD and LCX compared to other models. Furthermore, our ablation studies demonstrated that providing three-channel grayscale information at the input stage is more effective than single-channel inputs, even when the contrast of the latter in enhanced using three-windowing post-processing. We also validated that similarity-guided unpaired NCCT-CECT pairs outperforms unpaired cross-patient sampling NCCT-CECT pairs by providing a much lower FID score (0.0639 vs. 0.0802). This confirms that matching intra-patient slices at slice-selection level without any pixel-wise or voxel alignment is substantial for improving similarity-guided unpaired NCCT-to-CECT translation training. However, minor smoothing artifacts were observed in the myocardial walls of both the three-channel and single-channel images shown in Fig. 6. Although these artifacts were subtle, GAN-based image translation models are inherently prone to over-smoothing or generating hallucinated textures. Therefore, careful clinical interpretation and validation are essential, particularly for clinically sensitive structures. Finally, these quantitative and qualitative analyses validated that the inclusion of RICA blocks and ViT layers effectively improves the generation of synthetic contrast enhancement for unpaired I2I translation by capturing finer local features such as CACs and aortic boundaries, as well as global features like contrast enhancement in sCECT relative to NCCT-CECT pairs. To evaluate the clinical realism of sCECT images, a blinded reader study involving two extensively trained clinicians was conducted using a total of 60 CECT images (30 real and 30 synthetic). Using the real CECT images as the positive class, the aggregated results yielded F1 score of 26.09%, recall of 15%, and precision of 100%. These results indicate that no sCECT images were misclassified as real CECT images. However, the notably low recall indicates that only 9 out of 60 real CECT images were correctly classified as real. The remaining 51 real CECT images were misclassified as synthetic, suggesting expectation bias or conservative bias in classification under high-pressure conditions. This finding is further supported by the inter-rater agreement analysis. A Cohen’s kappa (κ) of 0.40 indicates fair-to-moderate agreement between the two readers, suggesting consistency in classification across both clinicians. Although the notably low recall may be interpreted as problematic, the fact that the majority of real CECT images were misclassified as synthetic suggests that the sCECT images exhibit a high degree of visual similarity to clinical data to a certain extent.

However, this study has four limitations. First, despite the image similarity algorithm, the dataset remains fundamentally unpaired. This leads to unavoidable pixel-wise structural misalignments between NCCT and CECT images, resulting in inaccurate calculations of pixel-based metrics like MAE, SSIM, and PSNR. Second, the generalizability of the proposed architecture was not extensively evaluated across diverse imaging modalities in this study. Given its modality-agnostic design, the proposed architecture could potentially be extended to other contrast-heavy imaging domains, such as abdominal CT imaging or MR-to-CT synthesis, where both small structural details and global contrast enhancement are crucial. However, such adaptation would require retraining on domain-specific datasets, along with careful hyperparameter optimization. Third, while we employed the RadImageNet pre-trained InceptionV3 network to compute the FID score, it is not a gold-standard evaluation metric for I2I translation, as it does not fully assess image-level similarity between the two domains. Lastly, while our clinical evaluation suggests that the sCECT images are perceptually convincing to a certain extent, this study lacks comprehensive assessments of diagnostic utility. In addition to the binary classification of real versus synthetic images, more clinically relevant evaluation metrics such as stenosis detection and lumen diameter measurement would further strengthen the clinical applicability of the proposed architecture and enhance its the practicality for clinical decision-making.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that RICAUNet-v2 is an effective generator for CycleGAN-based similarity-guided unpaired NCCT-to-CECT translation. By leveraging the capabilities of capturing long-range dependency of ViTs along with the efficient local feature extraction capabilities of CNNs, the model generates high-quality synthetic contrast enhancement from NCCT images. The integration of three-channel grayscale multi-windows input preprocessing method and the image similarity algorithm further enhances the translation process, addressing the significant challenge of working with predominantly unpaired real-world medical datasets. By enabling effective domain adaptation without the need for costly paired data, our method provides a potential clinical solution. Although more clinically relevant assessments of diagnostic utility of the proposed architecture is required, it can be particularly beneficial for patients who cannot undergo standard CECT scans due to contrast agent allergies or concerns regarding radiation exposure, potentially reducing the need for additional contrast-enhanced acquisitions in selected clinical scenarios.
