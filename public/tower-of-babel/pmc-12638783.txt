Intelligent prediction of heat capacity of polyethylene glycol polymer
Authors: Jamal I. Al-Nabulsi, Zaid Ajzan Alsalami, J. Deepak, Johar MGM, Anupama Routray, A. Karthikeyan, Harjot Singh Gill, Yashwant Singh Bisht, Ahmad Abumalek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Polyethylene glycol (PEG) exhibits tunable molar heat capacity, making it essential for thermal management and materials processing. Using an experimental databank of 528 observations, this study applies machine learning to develop predictive models based on temperature and molar mass. Data reliability was ensured through Monte Carlo–based outlier detection and correlation analysis, which confirmed temperature as the dominant predictor. Among the algorithms tested, Random Forest delivered the highest performance (R² = 0.9969, AARE = 8.29%), outperforming other methods that showed signs of overfitting. The results demonstrate that Random Forest provides a cost-effective, accurate, and scalable alternative to calorimetric experiments, enabling precise estimation of PEG heat capacity for applications in materials design, energy storage, and biomedical engineering.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25326-4.

Introduction

Polyethylene glycol (PEG) is a widely used polymer known for its unique thermal properties, particularly its molar heat capacity, that plays an essential role in various scientific and industrial uses1–3. Its ability to absorb, store, and release heat efficiently makes it a key material in thermal energy management systems, like phase change materials (PCMs) employed for temperature regulation in cooling and heating applications4–6. PEG’s molar heat capacity is highly dependent on its molecular weight and temperature, allowing researchers and engineers to tailor its properties for specific uses, including pharmaceuticals, biotechnology, and advanced material design7–9. In drug delivery systems, PEG’s heat capacity contributes to the stability of formulations, ensuring that active ingredients remain effective under varying temperature conditions10. Additionally, in polymer-based heat exchangers and biomedical devices, PEG’s predictable thermal behavior enhances performance by maintaining stable temperature conditions11,12. Given its broad applications, accurately modeling and predicting the heat capacity of PEG is essential for optimizing its functionality in different environments13.

Kou et al. investigated heat capacity of PEG polymers with varying molar masses (1000 to 20,000) over a wide temperature spectrum (1.9 to 400 K) utilizing a blend of Physical Property Measurement System (PPMS) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC). Their study provided essential insights into the thermal behavior of PEG, including its standard molar heat capacity, enthalpy, entropy, phase transition properties, and thermal stability, contributing to a more profound comprehension of its role in thermal energy storage applications14. Marcos et al. examined the isobaric heat capacity of polyethylene glycol (PEG200) in nano-enhanced PCMs by dispersing carbon black (CB) nanoparticles with polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) as a surfactant. Their study revealed that while thermal conductivity showed minor changes, the latent heat significantly increased with CB loading, providing valuable insights into the thermal conduct of PEG-based PCMs15. Ahmad et al. explored the impact of PCMs on the heat capacity of PEG-based building wallboards. Their study demonstrated that incorporating PCMs, along with vacuum insulation panels, enhances the observed heat capacity of the structure, improving thermal inertia and energy storage efficiency16. Azizi and Sadrameli investigated the heat capacity of polyethylene glycol (PEG1000) as a phase change material (PCM) for temperature regulation in lithium-ion battery systems. Their study demonstrated that incorporating PEG1000 with aluminum wire mesh plates significantly reduced surface temperature of batteries, enhancing thermal stability and overall performance17.

Despite the widespread use of polyethylene glycol (PEG) polymers, accurately estimating their molar heat capacity remains a considerable challenge due to the intricate relationships between parameters like temperature and molar mass. While direct experimental measurements provide reliable data, they are often expensive and time-intensive, emphasizing the necessity for computational models that can deliver rapid and precise predictions.

Machine learning algorithms have surfaced as a potent instrument for this purpose, offering precise and affordable options to conventional experimental techniques18–20. By leveraging data-driven models, scholars can obtain more profound understanding of the thermal characteristics of PEG, improving its application in energy storage, material processing, and biomedical engineering.

Recent advances in computational modeling have significantly contributed to the prediction of thermal properties in polymers, with methods ranging from empirical correlations to complex molecular dynamics simulations. Traditional approaches, while informative, often require extensive input parameters such as structural descriptors, chemical composition, or experimental calibration, which limit their accessibility and scalability across different systems. For example, models based on group contribution methods or atomistic simulations provide detailed insights but are computationally expensive and data-intensive. In contrast, several recent machine learning studies have attempted to forecast heat capacity and related thermophysical properties of polymers, yet many rely on large sets of molecular descriptors or specialized experimental conditions, which reduce their practical applicability. The present study addresses these challenges by demonstrating that highly accurate predictions of PEG heat capacity can be achieved using only two easily accessible input features—temperature and molar mass—combined with advanced machine learning algorithms such as Random Forest and Ensemble Learning. This streamlined approach not only reduces the complexity and data requirements of the predictive framework but also highlights the potential for rapid, cost-effective, and scalable application in polymer science and engineering.

To address this challenge, the research employs modern machine learning techniques including Decision Trees, Random Forests, AdaBoost, K-Nearest Neighbors, and various ensemble strategies to construct reliable models capable of predicting the molar heat capacity of PEG with high precision. A dataset comprising 528 observations is employed, with correlation analysis conducted to assess the significance of input features. Additionally, a feature importance evaluation is conducted to ascertain the individual impact of each parameter on heat capacity. To assess model performance, various statistical indicators and graphical representations are utilized. Moreover, the application of SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) allows for assessing the relative importance of the input variables, highlighting temperature as the primary driver, with PEG’s molar mass emerging as the next most significant contributor. Background of machine learning methods

In this study, a set of widely used supervised machine learning algorithms including Decision Tree, Random Forest, AdaBoost, and K-Nearest Neighbors were selected due to their proven capability to capture nonlinear relationships, handle mixed input variables, and provide interpretable results in regression problems involving material property prediction21,22. Random Forest and AdaBoost were included for their ensemble learning strengths, which reduce variance and improve generalization, while Decision Tree and KNN were chosen as baseline models to benchmark performance and illustrate potential overfitting risks23,24. This balanced selection allows for both methodological comparison and performance validation against established approaches in the field25,26. Collection of data and assessment metrics

Overview of data acquisition

The dataset employed in this study consists of 528 experimental records of the molar heat capacity of polyethylene glycol (PEG). These data were compiled from four published experimental studies: Kou et al.14, Ahmad et al.16, Azizi & Sadrameli17 and Marcos et al.27. The reported values were obtained directly from tabulated results and digitized from published graphs where necessary, ensuring consistency through cross-checking and validation. The dataset spans a temperature range of approximately 1.9–400 K and covers PEG molar masses from ~ 1,000 g·mol⁻¹ up to 20,000 g·mol⁻¹, thereby capturing a wide range of thermal behavior. Each record includes temperature, PEG molar mass, and corresponding molar heat capacity, standardized into consistent units prior to model development. The full dataset, including preprocessing steps, has been organized in spreadsheet form and is available upon reasonable request to the corresponding author, ensuring transparency and reproducibility of the analysis. These parameters are fundamental in defining the molar heat capacity of PEG polymers, making them indispensable for predictive analysis.

K-fold cross-validation is a technique used to improve machine learning algorithms performance of by assessing them through multiple iterations, ensuring more consistent and reliable outcomes. This method splits the dataset into ‘K’ equal-sized subsets (or folds). In each iteration, a single fold serves as the validation set, while the remaining ‘K-1’ folds are used for training. After completing all K iterations, the outcomes are averaged to yield a more reliable assessment, thereby reducing the risk of bias that may arise from relying on just a single random split of the data into training and testing sets28–30. Furthermore, this technique aids in minimizing overfitting. Figure 5 depicts the k-fold cross-validation approach. In this research, a 5-fold procedure was applied during model training to improve the predictive performance of all machine learning algorithms.

Results and analysis

Prior to developing machine learning algorithms for predicting the molar heat capacity of polyethylene glycol (PEG) polymers, it is essential to guarantee the dependability of the information by addressing outliers. In this study, the MCOD algorithm is employed to detect anomalies within large-scale datasets. The method integrates random sampling with density-based analysis to locate data points that deviate notably from their surrounding neighbors. By applying Monte Carlo sampling, the algorithm works on a smaller subset of the data, which lowers computational demands while preserving accuracy. Thanks to its scalability and efficiency, MCOD is well-suited for high-dimensional problems and real-time applications. However, the method involves balancing accuracy against computational effort, with factors such as sample size and the chosen number of nearest neighbors (k) influencing this trade-off. Despite these constraints, MCOD remains a valuable technique for exploratory analysis and for spotting outliers, especially when quick estimates are sufficient or computational capacity is constrained. In the context of this work, its balance of speed and reliability makes it particularly effective for detecting irregularities in datasets used for predicting PEG’s heat capacity.

The plot indicates that most data points fall within these bounds, reflecting the high quality of the dataset. In this paper, the complete databank was utilized for training machine learning algorithms to ensure a thorough learning process and enhanced generalization to new, unseen data. Through the inclusion of the complete dataset, models are more capable of capturing key patterns and variations, leading to an increase in accurate and dependable estimations for the molar heat capacity of PEG polymers.

Limitations of study

This study has several limitations that inform a balanced interpretation of the results. First, although Random Forest achieved the best generalization performance, signs of overfitting were observed for K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) and Decision Tree (DT) models, underscoring the sensitivity of certain algorithms to data partitioning and hyperparameter choices. Second, the predictors rely on only two input variables including temperature and PEG molar mass. While this minimal feature set enhances practicality and data accessibility, it may not capture additional factors (e.g., pressure, crystallinity, additive content, or phase state) that can influence heat capacity in real-world settings. Third, the current models were trained on a PEG-specific databank; therefore, applicability to other polymers (or PEG formulations outside the training domain) should be considered with caution, especially when extrapolating beyond the temperature or molar-mass ranges represented in the data.

Future works

Building on the findings of the study on the molar heat capacity prediction of polyethylene glycol (PEG) using machine learning, several avenues for future research can be explored to strengthen the generalizability and practical value of the models. The present work employed a comprehensive experimental databank of 528 observations across a range of thermodynamic conditions and molecular weights, with predictive features restricted to temperature and molar mass. Data integrity was safeguarded by Monte Carlo–based outlier detection and correlation analysis, which identified temperature as the dominant factor. Among the algorithms tested, Random Forest achieved the highest predictive accuracy (R² = 0.9969, AARE = 8.29%), while other methods exhibited tendencies toward overfitting.

The methodological framework, which integrates robust preprocessing, multiple machine learning architectures, and SHAP-based feature importance analysis, offers a scalable strategy applicable beyond PEG systems. By ensuring reliable data handling, rigorous model evaluation, and interpretability, this workflow highlights the potential of Random Forest as a cost-effective and accurate surrogate for calorimetric experiments. Such an approach can be extended to predictive modeling in materials design, energy storage, and biomedical engineering, where efficient and precise estimation of thermophysical properties is essential. Predictive modeling techniques have potential applications in evaluating the load-bearing capacity of cable domes47 and ensuring stable fuel supply in marine propulsion systems48. These examples emphasize how reliable models, applied across a wide spectrum of thermodynamic states, can serve similar purposes to heat capacity prediction in organic Rankine cycle fluids. Such strategies may also be adapted to estimate thermophysical parameters under challenging or extreme operating environments, supporting the advancement of next-generation energy conversion technologies and the optimization of heat exchanger efficiency. The same framework can be redirected toward evaluating material responses in specialized scenarios, such as deformation behavior at elevated temperatures49, enhancing therapeutic outcomes in stem cell research50, studying mechanisms of ultrasonic welding51, boosting the effectiveness of supercapacitors52, or engineering collagen-based scaffolds for regenerative medicine53. SHAP-based interpretability offers further value by clarifying the dominant influence of temperature on heat capacity while simultaneously recognizing secondary contributions from pressure, molecular mass, and boiling point. This interpretive capacity provides a foundation for extending machine learning models to forecast additional thermophysical or chemical outcomes. Possible directions include the synthesis of complex compounds54, mechanistic studies of arylgermylation processes55, nalysis of ionic adsorption on clay minerals, and the design of improved nanocatalysts for energy conversion56,57, Ultimately, the use of machine learning can significantly raise predictive reliability in chemical and material systems characterized by intricate, interdependent variables58.

The following recommendations aim to extend these findings for broader applications. By integrating advanced optimization strategies with high-performing algorithms such as Random Forest, researchers can improve the precision of property prediction, reduce reliance on costly calorimetric experiments, and accelerate the design of thermal management solutions. Adopting this workflow enables scalable, cost-effective, and interpretable model development, contributing to advances in materials design and energy technologies, mirroring the success demonstrated in PEG heat capacity prediction.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the potential of data-driven modeling for accurately predicting the molar heat capacity of polyethylene glycol (PEG) using minimal and easily accessible input variables (temperature and molar mass). Among the algorithms evaluated, Random Forest achieved the highest predictive performance (R² = 0.9969, AARE = 8.29%), underscoring its ability to capture nonlinear relationships while avoiding overfitting observed in simpler models such as KNN and Decision Tree. Beyond the technical findings, the results highlight the industrial relevance of predictive modeling for PEG: the framework provides a cost-effective alternative to calorimetric experiments, enabling rapid screening and optimization in applications such as thermal energy storage, drug delivery systems, and polymer-based thermal management devices. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. The dataset size (528 samples) constrains the diversity of operating conditions, and the reliance on only two input variables omits other influential factors such as pressure, crystallinity, or compositional modifications. Furthermore, the models were trained specifically on PEG, and their generalizability to other polymers remains uncertain. Looking forward, future research should focus on expanding the dataset with additional experimental measurements, incorporating broader physicochemical variables, and validating the framework across different polymer systems. Integration of physics-informed learning and transfer-learning techniques could further enhance model robustness and applicability. Such developments will contribute to more versatile predictive tools for polymer thermal property estimation, bridging the gap between laboratory-scale data and industrial-scale applications.
