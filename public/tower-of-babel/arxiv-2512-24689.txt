Small 3-fold blocking sets in PG ⁡ ( 2 , p n ) \mathrm{PG}(2,p^{n})
B. Csajbók, M. R. Kepes, E. M. Robin, B. Sógor, S. Wang, E. Williams

Abstract
A t t -fold blocking set of the finite Desarguesian plane PG ⁡ ( 2 , p n ) \mathrm{PG}(2,p^{n}) , p p prime, is a set of points meeting each line of the plane in at least t t points. The minimum size of such sets is of interest for numerous reasons; however, even the minimum size of nontrivial blocking sets (i.e. 1 1 -fold blocking sets not containing a line) in PG ⁡ ( 2 , p n ) \mathrm{PG}(2,p^{n}) is an open question when n ≥ 5 n\geq 5 is odd. For n > 1 n>1 the conjectured lower bound for this size is ( p n + p n ⁡ ( s − 1 ) / s + 1 ) (p^{n}+p^{n(s-1)/s}+1) , where p n / s p^{n/s} is the size of the largest proper subfield of 𝔽 p n \mathbb{F}_{p^{n}} . Since the union of t t pairwise disjoint nontrivial blocking sets is a t t -fold blocking set, it is conjectured that when p n / s p^{n/s} is large enough w.r.t. t t , then the minimum size of a t t -fold blocking set in PG ⁡ ( 2 , p n ) \mathrm{PG}(2,p^{n}) is t ⁡ ( p n + p n ⁡ ( s − 1 ) / s + 1 ) t(p^{n}+p^{n(s-1)/s}+1) . If n n is even, then the decomposition of the plane into disjoint Baer subplanes gives a t t -fold blocking set of this size. However, for odd n n , the existence of such sets is an unsolved problem in most cases. In this paper, we construct 3 3 -fold blocking sets of conjectured size. These blocking sets are obtained as the disjoint union of three linear blocking sets of Rédei type, and they lie on the same orbit of the projectivity ( x : y : z ) ↦ ( z : x : y ) (x:y:z)\mapsto(z:x:y) .

A set of points
{\mathcal{B}}

{\mathcal{B}}
meets each line of the plane in at least one point. It is easy to see that the smallest blocking sets are lines. The question naturally arises: can we determine the minimum size of

t

to be a point set that has at least
t

t
disjoint nontrivial blocking sets. In this paper, we mainly focus on multiple blocking sets of
\PG(2,q)

-fold blocking sets has many applications, such as the theory of linear error correcting codes
[

If

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we list the main definitions, results and constructions for blocking and multiple blocking sets. We present a construction which explains why we assume in Conjecture
1.1
that the size
p^{n/s}
of the largest subfield of
{\mathbb{F}}_{p^{n}}
is large enough w.r.t.
t
. We will show how the exponent of the smallest nontrivial blocking set is related to the largest subfield of
{\mathbb{F}}_{p^{n}}
, cf. Proposition
2.7

2.12

2.1
Blocking sets and multiple blocking sets containing lines

In
[
2
, Theorem 4.1]
, Ball proved that if
{\mathcal{B}}
is a
t
-fold blocking set that contains no line, then it has at least
tq+\sqrt{tq}+1
points. If
\ell
is a line contained in a
t
-fold blocking set
{\mathcal{B}}
, then
{\mathcal{B}}\setminus\ell
is an affine
(t-1)
-fold blocking set and hence it has size at least
tq-t+1
(cf. Bruen
[
10
, Theorem 2.1]
), thus
|{\mathcal{B}}|\geq(t+1)q-t+2
. For a fixed
t
, this lower bound is smaller than the conjectured (
1.1
) only for small
p^{n/s}
.
For
t=2
this happens only for
q=2^{n}
,
n
prime; and for
t=3
when
q=2^{n}
, or
q=3^{n}
, with
n
prime. We show that in these two cases it is in fact possible to construct blocking sets whose size is smaller than (
1.1
). These blocking sets, however, contain some lines.

Example 2.1
.

The union of three lines in general position in
\PG(2,q)
gives a double blocking set of size
3q
. If
q=2^{n}
,
n
is a prime, then this number is smaller than (
1.1
), which reads as
2(2^{n}+2^{n-1}+1)
.

Similarly, if
q
is even and we take the union of four lines in general position, say
\{X=0\}\cup\{Y=0\}\cup\{Z=0\}\cup\{X+Y+Z=0\}
, then this point set meets each line of the plane in at least three points, except for the lines
\{X=Y\},\{Y=Z\}
and
\{Z=X\}
, which are blocked twice and are concurrent at the point
(1:1:1)
(we are using homogeneous coordinates here). And so,
\{X=0\}\cup\{Y=0\}\cup\{Z=0\}\cup\{X+Y+Z=0\}\cup\{(1:1:1)\}
is a 3-fold blocking set of size
4q-1
, which is smaller than (
1.1
) when
t=3
,
q=2^{n}
and
n
is a prime.

Figure 1:
3
-fold blocking set of size
4q

Moreover, if we take four lines in general position in
\PG(2,q)
with the addition of two suitably chosen points as seen above in Figure
1
, we get a
3
-fold blocking set of size
4q
. When
q=3^{n}
,
n
is a prime, then (
1.1
) reads as
4q+3>4q
.

This construction can be generalized for arbitrary
t
to give
t
-fold blocking sets that are smaller than (
1.1
) when
t
is large enough w.r.t.
p^{n/s}
.
The construction works in an arbitrary projective plane
\Pi_{q}
of order
q
.
First, take
t+1
distinct lines
\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\dots,\ell_{t+1}
in
\Pi_{q}
such that no three of them are incident with the same point.
These lines will then have
\binom{t+1}{2}
mutual intersections. Denote the point set of these intersections by
{\mathcal{M}}
. The union
{\mathcal{B}}:=\ell_{1}\cup\ell_{2}\cup\dots\cup\ell_{t+1}
will contain
(t+1)(q+1)-\binom{t+1}{2}
points.
This point set meets all lines of the plane in at least
t
points, except for those incident with multiple of the
\binom{t+1}{2}
points of
{\mathcal{M}}
. Such lines are either the
\ell_{i}
themselves, or lines meeting
{\mathcal{B}}
in fewer than
t
points. We will call the latter lines exceptional.
For
i=0,1,2,\ldots,t-1
let
n_{i}
denote the number of lines meeting
{\mathcal{M}}
in exactly
i
points.
By double counting the triples
(P,Q,\ell)
, where
P,Q\in{\mathcal{M}}
are distinct points of the exceptional line
\ell
, we obtain:

|{\mathcal{M}}|(|{\mathcal{M}}|-2t+1)=\sum_{i=2}^{t-1}i(i-1)n_{i}.

For an exceptional line
\ell
meeting
{\mathcal{M}}
in
i
points, we want to add
(i-1)
extra points of
\ell
to
{\mathcal{B}}
in order to have
t
points on that line as well.
Hence we must add

\sum_{i=2}^{t-1}(i-1)n_{i}=\sum_{i=3}^{t-1}(i-1)n_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\left(|{\mathcal{M}}|(|{\mathcal{M}}|-2t+1)-\sum_{i=3}^{t-1}i(i-1)n_{i}\right)=

\frac{1}{2}|{\mathcal{M}}|(|{\mathcal{M}}|-2t+1)-\sum_{i=3}^{t-1}(i^{2}+1)n_{i}

points to
{\mathcal{B}}
. In the worst case

n_{3}=n_{4}=\ldots=n_{t-1}=0
.
This results in
\binom{t+1}{2}\left(\binom{t+1}{2}-2t+1\right)/2
exceptional lines, which are all only incident with
2
points of
{\mathcal{M}}
and hence
t-1
points of
{\mathcal{B}}
.
Each of these lines can then be blocked with the addition of a single point, resulting in a
t
-fold blocking set of size

tq+q+1+\left(t^{4}-2t^{3}-5t^{2}+6t\right)/8.

(2.1)

If two exceptional lines meet each other outside of
{\mathcal{B}}
, then they can be blocked by a single point instead of using two points. This means that a more careful analysis could slightly improve the construction above.

Note that extending with points the union of some lines in general position is a standard technique to construct small multiple blocking sets. For some recent constructions of this type see for example
[
13
]
.

2.2
Linear blocking sets

Definition 2.2
.

A blocking set
{\mathcal{B}}
in
\PG(2,q)
is called small if
|{\mathcal{B}}|<3(q+1)/2
.
A
k
-secant of
{\mathcal{B}}
is a line that meets
{\mathcal{B}}
in exactly
k
points.
Blocking sets of size
q+N\leq 2q
in
\PG(2,q)
with an
N
-secant are called
Rédei type blocking sets
. Lines that meet such a blocking set in
N
points are called
Rédei lines
.
A
t
-fold blocking set of
\PG(2,q)
is called small when it has less than
tq+(q+3)/2
points.

In our constructions, we will use disjoint copies of small linear blocking sets of Rédei type. Linear blocking sets are of interest to us since, over prime fields, it has been proved, while over fields of composite order it is conjectured that all small blocking sets are linear.

The theory of linear blocking sets is based on the fact that the lattice of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{s}}
-subspaces of
V={\mathbb{F}}_{q^{s}}^{3}
is isomorphic to
\Pi=\PG(2,q^{s})
. Lines of
\Pi
are set of points defined by nonzero vectors of two-dimensional
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{s}}
-subspaces of
V
.
Let
U
be any
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-subspace of
V
of dimension
(s+1)
, denote by
U^{*}
the set of nonzero vectors of
U
, and let
T
be any two-dimensional
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{s}}
-space. By Grassmann’s identity,
U\cap T
is nontrivial, and hence

{\mathcal{L}}_{U}:=\{\langle\vec{v}\rangle_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{s}}}:\vec{v}\in U^{*}\}

is a blocking set, which is called an
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-linear blocking set
(of rank
(s+1)
).
This argument is due to Lunardon
[
18
]
.

Definition 2.3
.

The weight of the point
P=\langle\vec{v}\rangle_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{s}}}
w.r.t.
{\mathcal{L}}_{U}
is defined to be
w(P):=\dim_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}(U\cap\langle\vec{v}\rangle_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{s}}})
. Similarly, if
\ell
is a line whose points are defined by the nonzero vectors of the
2
-dimensional
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{s}}
-subspace
S
, then the weight of
\ell
w.r.t.
{\mathcal{L}}_{U}
is
w(\ell):=\dim_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}(U\cap S)
.

The following example of a linear blocking set will be crucial for us.

Example 2.4

(The trace construction)
.

If
s
is the smallest prime divisor of
n>1
, then
{\mathbb{F}}_{p^{n/s}}
is the largest subfield of
{\mathbb{F}}_{p^{n}}
. Put
q:=p^{n/s}
.
In
\PG(2,p^{n})=\PG(2,q^{s})
consider the mapping
\Tr_{q^{s}/q}:{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{s}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
defined by
\Tr_{q^{s}/q}(x):=x+x^{q}+\ldots+x^{q^{s-1}}
. Note that this is an
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-linear functional.

We may take the following
(s+1)
-dimensional
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-subspace of
V={\mathbb{F}}_{q^{s}}^{3}
:

U:=\{(x,\Tr_{q^{s}/q}(x),y):x\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{s}},y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\}.

(2.2)

It is well-known that
{\mathcal{L}}_{U}=\{(x:\Tr_{q^{s}/q}(x):y):x\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{s}},y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q},(x,y)\neq(0,0)\}
is a blocking set of size
q^{s}+q^{s-1}+1
in
\PG(2,q^{s})
(cf.
[
5
, pg. 138]
), thus when
s
is the smallest prime divisor of
n
, its size reaches the lower bound in Conjecture
1.1
.

The point
(1:0:0)
is defined by the vectors in
\{(x,0,0):\Tr_{q^{s}/q}(x)=0\}
and hence it has
weight
(s-1)
.
Also,
{\mathcal{L}}_{U}
is a blocking set of Rédei type,
\ell_{\infty}
(the line of equation
Z=0
) is one of its Rédei lines. This means that
{\mathcal{L}}_{U}
consists of the graph of the trace function in the affine plane (points of form
(x:\Tr_{q^{s}/q}(x):1)
), and the directions determined by this graph (points of form
(x:\Tr_{q^{s}/q}(x):0)
).

From now on, we will refer to this construction as the trace construction.
Note that the observations made for Example
2.4
are true if we replace
\Tr_{q^{s}/q}
with any
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-linear function whose kernel has codimension
1
. These are exactly the maps
x\mapsto\alpha\Tr(\beta x)
, where
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{s}}^{*}
. In our constructions, we will use
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{s}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
linear functionals. The following proposition is crucial to our cause.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
W
be any
(s+1)
-dimensional
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-subspace of
V={\mathbb{F}}_{q^{s}}^{3}
such that
{\mathcal{L}}_{W}
is a nontrivial (i.e., it is not a line) blocking set of
\PG(2,q^{s})
with a point
P
of weight
(s-1)
. Then
{\mathcal{L}}_{W}
is projectively equivalent to
{\mathcal{L}}_{U}
defined in Example
2.4
.

Proof.

First, note that lines have weight at most
s
w.r.t.
{\mathcal{L}}_{W}
. Indeed, if there were a line
\ell
of weight at least
s+1
, then, by Grassmann’s identity, each point of
\ell
is a point of
{\mathcal{L}}_{W}
, and hence
{\mathcal{L}}_{W}
is trivial.
If
Q\neq P
was another point of
{\mathcal{L}}_{W}

of weight larger than
1
, then the line
\langle Q,P\rangle
would have weight
s+1
, a contradiction.
Let
\ell
denote a line through
P
and put
S
for the
2
-dimensional
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{s}}
-subspace of
V
whose nonzero vectors define the points of
\ell
. Then either
{\mathcal{L}}_{W}\cap\ell=\{P\}
, or
\dim_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}(S\cap W)=s
. In the latter case, put
w_{1}
to denote the number of points of weight
1
in
\ell\cap{\mathcal{L}}_{W}
. Then counting nonzero vectors of
S\cap W
gives

q^{s}-1=|(S\cap W)^{*}|=w_{1}(q-1)+(q^{s-1}-1)
and hence
w_{1}=q^{s-1}
, thus
|\ell\cap{\mathcal{L}}_{W}|=w_{1}+1=q^{s-1}+1
.

After a suitable projectivity we may assume
P=(1:0:0)
,
O=(0:0:1)\in{\mathcal{L}}_{W}
,
(\infty)=(0:1:0)\notin{\mathcal{L}}_{W}
and that
\ell_{\infty}
is a line meeting
{\mathcal{L}}_{W}

in
q^{s-1}+1
points.
Then
W
is generated by
(0,0,1)
and by
\{(x,f(x),0):x\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{s}}\}
for some
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-linear function
f\colon{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{s}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{s}}
, i.e.

{\mathcal{L}}_{W}=\{(x:f(x):y):x\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{s}},y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q},(x,y)\neq(0,0)\}.

The line
\langle O,P\rangle
is a
(q^{s-1}+1)
-secant of
{\mathcal{L}}_{W}
and hence the kernel of
f
has dimension
(s-1)
over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
. It follows that
f(x)=\alpha\Tr(\beta x)
for some
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{s}}^{*}
. The projectivity
(x:y:z)\mapsto(\beta x:y/\alpha:z)
maps
{\mathcal{L}}_{W}
to
{\mathcal{L}}_{U}
.
∎

So, any
(s+1)
-dimensional
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-subspace of
V={\mathbb{F}}_{q^{s}}^{3}
that satisfies the conditions of the proposition defines a linear blocking set of desired size. Our goal is to find such sets disjoint from each other. In Proposition
2.12
, we show that the disjoint union of three copies of the trace construction is necessarily a minimal
3
-fold blocking set.

Definition 2.6
.

A
t
-fold blocking set
{\mathcal{B}}
is called minimal if it does not contain a smaller
t
-fold blocking set, or equivalently, if each of its points is contained in a
t
-secant of
{\mathcal{B}}
.

If
{\mathcal{B}}
is a small minimal nontrivial blocking set, then Szőnyi proved that there is an integer
1\leq e<n
such that each line meets
{\mathcal{B}}
in
1\pmod{p^{e}}
points, and there is a line meeting
{\mathcal{B}}
not in
1\pmod{p^{\,e+1}}
points, see
[
22
]
.
The integer
e
is called the
exponent
of
{\mathcal{B}}
, and Sziklai proved in
[
21
]
that
e\mid n
.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
{\mathcal{B}}
be the smallest minimal nontrivial blocking set in
\PG(2,p^{n})
,
n>1
, and let
s
denote
the smallest prime divisor of
n
. Then the exponent
e
of
{\mathcal{B}}
is
n/s
.

Proof.

Since
e\mid n
, the statement is trivial when
n
is prime, so assume that
n
is composite.
Szőnyi proved in
[
22
]
the inequality

p^{n}+1+\frac{p^{n}}{p^{e}+2}\;\leq\;|{\mathcal{B}}|.

Since
e<n
and
e\mid n
, we must have
e\leq n/s
.
On the other hand, Example
2.4
shows the existence of minimal nontrivial blocking sets of size

p^{n}+p^{\,n(s-1)/s}+1.

Thus, it remains to show that if
e<n/s
, then

p^{n}+p^{\,n(s-1)/s}+1\;<\;p^{n}+1+\frac{p^{n}}{p^{e}+2}.

The right-hand side is the smallest when
e
is the largest, i.e. when
e=n/s-1
.
Substituting
e=n/s-1
and simplifying gives the inequality

p^{\,n-1}\!\left(2p^{\,1-n/s}-p+1\right)<0,

which is equivalent to
p^{\,1-n/s}<\tfrac{p-1}{2}
.
The left-hand side is maximal when
n/s
is minimal.
Since
n
is composite,
1-n/s\leq-1
, so
p^{\,1-n/s}\leq p^{-1}
, and the inequality

p^{-1}<(p-1)/2
holds for all
p>2
, and also for
p=2
whenever
n/2>s
.

If
s=n/2
, then
n=2s
and
n/2
is the smallest prime divisor of
n
.
This forces
n/2=2
, so
n=4
.
In this case,
{\mathcal{B}}
has the size of a Baer subplane in
\PG(2,16)
and therefore is itself a
Baer subplane
[
8
]
.
Its exponent is
2=n/s
, which completes the proof.
∎

When
t=1
,
s=3
,

, then Conjecture

follows from

]
together with Proposition

.
Indeed, in the case of

, see

.
Polverino and Storme proved in

]

Thus, the conjectured bound (

) holds for

,

,

.

The following lemma is useful when dealing with linear functionals:

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
be a nonzero,
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-linear functional. Then for any

,

\lvert\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}:f(x)=k\}\rvert=q^{h-1}.

∎

In the next result, we summarize the main properties of the trace construction. These are well-known results and they can be proved easily by using Lemma
2.8
and some linear algebra.
We will need this lemma to prove the minimality of our future constructions.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}
denote the blocking set
\{(x:\Tr_{q^{h}/q}(x):y):x\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{h}},y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q},(x,y)\neq(0,0)\}
of
\PG(2,q^{h})
,
h\geq 2
.

(1)

Each line meets
{\mathcal{L}}
in
1
,
(q+1)
, or in
(q^{h-1}+1)
points.

(2)

(1:0:0)
is the the only point of
{\mathcal{L}}
of weight
(h-1)
and it is incident with
(q^{h}-q)
tangent lines to
{\mathcal{L}}
and with
(q+1)
lines meeting
{\mathcal{L}}
in
(q^{h-1}+1)
points. These are the only
(q^{h-1}+1)
-secants of
{\mathcal{L}}
.

(3)

The other points of
{\mathcal{L}}
are of weight
1
and they are incident with
q^{h}-q^{h-1}
tangent lines to
{\mathcal{L}}
. ∎

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}
be a blocking set in
\PG(2,q^{h})
,
h\geq 2
, equivalent to the trace construction,
P
a point not in
{\mathcal{L}}
. Then
P
is incident with at least
q^{h}-q^{h-2}+1>0
tangent lines to
{\mathcal{L}}
.

Proof.

Let

be the number of tangents to
{\mathcal{L}}
incident with
P
. Counting the number of points of
{\mathcal{L}}
on the

lines incident with

gives the following:

(1)

If

is not incident with
(q^{h-1}+1)
-secants of
{\mathcal{L}}
, then:

1\cdot k+(q+1)(q^{h}+1-k)=q^{h}+q^{h-1}+1.

Solving this linear equation for

.

(2)

If

-secant of

, then:

(q^{h-1}+1)\cdot 1+1\cdot k+(q+1)(q^{h}-k).

Solving for

.

(3)

If

is incident with at least two

-secants of

2.9

(2)
,
P\in{\mathcal{L}}
, a contradiction.

The result follows, since

for

.
∎

Proposition 2.11
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{L}}_{2}
be two disjoint blocking sets in
\PG(2,q^{h})
,
h\geq 2
, both projectively equivalent to the trace construction. Then each point of

is incident with at least
(q^{h}-q^{h-1}-q^{h-2})

2
-secants. In particular,
{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\cup{\mathcal{L}}_{2}
is a minimal double blocking set.

Proof.

Since
{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\cap{\mathcal{L}}_{2}=\emptyset
, it is clear that
{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\cup{\mathcal{L}}_{2}
is a 2-fold blocking set.

Take a point
P
from
{\mathcal{L}}_{1}
. By Lemma
2.9
,

is incident with at least

tangent lines to
{\mathcal{L}}_{1}
, and, by Lemma

,

(q^{h}-q^{h-1})+(q^{h}-q^{h-2}+1)-(q^{h}+1)=q^{h}-q^{h-1}-q^{h-2}

and

. Since

is incident with a
2
-secant, and hence it is a minimal double blocking set.
∎

Proposition 2.12
.

Let

be a prime power,

, and

be pairwise disjoint blocking sets in

-fold blocking set.

Again we see that

Take a point

from

. According to Proposition

, there are at least

2
-secants of

,

that are

-secants of

and tangents to

-secants of

. If

, then his number is positive. Again, using the same argument for points of

and

, we see that

-fold blocking set.
∎

3
Double blocking sets

3.1
The upper chromatic number of finite projective planes

Before moving on to the construction of a
2
-fold blocking set (also known as a double blocking set), we provide some motivation as to why the minimum size of double blocking sets matters. We take a look at the proper colorings of finite projective planes and their connection to
2
-fold blocking sets. The results presented in this section are based on the work of Bacsó, Héger and Szőnyi. For further information on the topic, we recommend reading
[
1
]
.

A
hypergraph

\mathcal{H}
consists of an underlying set
X
of vertices and a system of sets on
X
that define the hyperedges of the graph. A coloring assigns each of the vertices a color. A
proper coloring
is a coloring such that each hyperedge has at least two vertices that have the same color.

Definition 3.1
.

The upper chromatic number of
\mathcal{H}
is the maximum number of colors that can be used in a proper coloring. Notation:
\overline{\chi}(\mathcal{H})
.

A finite projective plane
\Pi
is a hypergraph, the underlying set of vertices being the points of the plane, and the hyperedges being defined by the lines. If we take a coloring of the plane with colors
c_{1},\dots,c_{k}
, then the sets
C_{i}
, the set of points colored
c_{i}
, partition the plane’s point set. Moreover, if the coloring is proper, then the union
\bigcup_{|C_{j}|\geq 2}C_{j}
is a double blocking set. Conversely, a double blocking set defines a proper coloring: assign the same color to all points of the double blocking set, and distinct colors to all points outside it.
When the double blocking set is of minimum size (
\tau_{2}
), then these proper colorings are called
trivial colorings
. Such colorings use
v-\tau_{2}+1
colors, where
v
is the number of points of
\Pi
. Thus,

\overline{\chi}(\Pi)\geq v-\tau_{2}+1.

Furthermore, it was shown that if
q>256
is a square,
or
p>29
and
h\geq 3
is odd, then
\overline{\chi}(\PG(2,p^{h}))=v-\tau_{2}+1
, see
[
1
, Theorem 1.12]
. So, the value of
\tau_{2}
is of particular interest.

3.2
Construction of two disjoint blocking sets

As stated before, we aim to construct a
2
-fold blocking set of size
2(q^{h}+q^{h-1}+1)
in
\PG(2,q^{h})
by finding two disjoint blocking sets, each of size
q^{h}+q^{h-1}+1
. Then, their union will be a double blocking set of the desired size.

The following lemma will be useful:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\vec{u},\vec{v},\vec{w}
be three linearly independent vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}^{3}
, and let
f\colon\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
be a nonzero linear functional. Define the subspace

U:=\{x\vec{u}+f(x)\vec{v}+y\vec{w}:x\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}},y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\}.

Then
{\mathcal{L}}_{U}
is a blocking set of size
q^{h}+q^{h-1}+1
.

Proof.

Since
\dim_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}U=h+1
, by Proposition
2.5
, it is sufficient to prove that
{\mathcal{L}}_{U}
is a nontrivial blocking set with a point of weight
h-1
. A line must have weight
(h+1)
to be contained in
{\mathcal{L}}_{U}
, so
{\mathcal{L}}_{U}
is trivial if and only if it is a line.
There exists an
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
such that
f(a)\neq 0
. Then the nontriviality of
{\mathcal{L}}_{U}
follows from the fact that the points
\langle\vec{u}\rangle_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{h}}}
,
\langle a\vec{u}+f(a)\vec{v}\rangle_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{h}}}
and
\langle\vec{w}\rangle_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{h}}}
of
{\mathcal{L}}_{U}
are not collinear, since
\vec{u},\vec{v}
and
\vec{w}
were chosen to be linearly independent over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
.

The point
\langle\vec{u}\rangle_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{h}}}
in
{\mathcal{L}}_{U}
has weight
h-1
, because

U\cap\langle\vec{u}\rangle_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{h}}}=\{x\vec{u}+f(x)\vec{v}+y\vec{w}:x\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}},f(x)=y=0\}

is an
(h-1)
-dimensonal
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-subspace.

And so,
{\mathcal{L}}_{U}
is projective equivalent to the blocking set presented in Example
2.4
, which has size
q^{h}+q^{h-1}+1
.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

Note that because of the
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-linearity of
f
, if
y\not=0
, then one can assume
y=1
. If we have
\vec{u}=(1,0,0),\vec{v}=(0,1,0),\vec{w}=(0,0,1)
, then the points with
y=0
and
y=1
correspond to the ideal and affine points of the blocking set, respectively.

We will search for two disjoint blocking sets of the following form:

{\mathcal{L}}=\{(x:f(x):y):x\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}},y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q},(x,y)\neq(0,0)\},

{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}=\{(y^{\prime}:x^{\prime}:g(x^{\prime})+y^{\prime}\alpha):x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}},y^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q},(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\neq(0,0)\},

where
f,g\colon\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
are nonzero linear functionals, and
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}\setminus{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
is a constant. Note that
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
is also a blocking set of size
q^{h}+q^{h-1}+1
because of Lemma
3.2
(
\vec{u}=(0,1,0),\vec{v}=(0,0,1),\vec{w}=(1,0,\alpha)
).

Theorem 3.4
.

The blocking sets
{\mathcal{L}}
and
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
are disjoint if the following conditions hold:

(1)

g(1)=g(\alpha)=1
,

(2)

f\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\not=0
,

(3)

f\left(\frac{k-1}{k(k-1)\alpha-k^{2}}\right)\not=1
for all
k\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
,
k\not=0,1
.

Proof.

Let
P\in{\mathcal{L}}
,
P^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
, meaning

P=(x:f(x):y),\quad P^{\prime}=(y^{\prime}:x^{\prime}:g(x^{\prime})+y^{\prime}\alpha),

for some
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
,
y,y^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
. Assume that the conditions hold. We want to show
P\not=P^{\prime}
. We will do this by checking case by case based on whether
y
and
y^{\prime}
are
0
or
1
.
We begin by indirectly assuming
P=P^{\prime}
.

1.

y=0
, meaning

(x:f(x):0)=(y^{\prime}:x^{\prime}:g(x^{\prime})+y^{\prime}\alpha).

Then
g(x^{\prime})+y^{\prime}\alpha=0
, but because of
\alpha\not\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, that means
g(x^{\prime})=y^{\prime}=0
, so
P^{\prime}=(0:x^{\prime}:0)=(0:1:0)
. This means
x=0
and
f(x)\not=0
, a contradiction.

2.

y^{\prime}=0
, meaning

(x:f(x):y)=(0:x^{\prime}:g(x^{\prime})).

Therefore
x=0
, so
P=(0:f(0):y)=(0:0:1)
, but then
x^{\prime}=0
and
g(x^{\prime})\not=0
, a contradiction.

3.

y=y^{\prime}=1
, meaning

(x:f(x):1)=(1:x^{\prime}:g(x^{\prime})+\alpha).

We introduce
k=f(x)
and
l=g(x^{\prime})
(note that both
k
and
l
are in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
). Now
P=P^{\prime}
exactly if

x=\frac{1}{l+\alpha}\text{, and }x^{\prime}=k(l+\alpha).

Applying
f
and
g
to these equations, we get

k=f\left(\frac{1}{l+\alpha}\right)

(3.1)

and

l=g(kl+k\alpha)=klg(1)+kg(\alpha)=kl+k.

The second equation can be rearranged as

(k-1)l=-k.

(3.2)

Now we show that, for every
k\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, we obtain a contradiction.

(a)

k=0
: From (
3.2
) we get
l=0
. Substituting this into (
3.1
) yields
0=f\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)
, which contradicts condition
(2)
of the statement.

(b)

k=1
: Substituting into (
3.2
), we obtain
k=0
, a contradiction.

(c)

k\not=0,1
: Dividing (
3.1
) by
k
, we obtain

1=f\left(\frac{1}{k(l+\alpha)}\right)=f\left(\frac{k-1}{k(k-1)\alpha+kl(k-1)}\right)=f\left(\frac{k-1}{k(k-1)\alpha-k^{2}}\right),

which contradicts condition
(3)
of the statement.

∎

Proposition 3.5
.

For any nonzero
f:\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, there exist
\alpha
and
g
such that the conditions in Theorem
3.4
hold.

Proof.

First, we show that there exists an
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}\setminus{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
such that the second and third conditions in Theorem
3.4
hold. Then we choose a suitable
g
.

We denote by
B_{0}
the set of
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
where the second condition does not hold, and by
B_{k}
the set of
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
where the third condition fails for a fixed
k\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
,
k\neq 0,1
.
We count the size of these sets using Lemma
2.8
:

\lvert B_{0}\rvert=\left\lvert\left\{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}:f\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)=0\right\}\right\rvert=\left\lvert\left\{\beta:\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}},f\left(\beta\right)=0\right\}\setminus\{0\}\right\rvert=q^{h-1}-1.

\lvert B_{k}\rvert=\left\lvert\left\{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}\ |\ f\left(\frac{k-1}{k(k-1)\alpha-k^{2}}\right)=1\right\}\right\rvert=\left\lvert\left\{\beta:\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}},f\left(\beta\right)=1\right\}\right\rvert=q^{h-1},

for any
k\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
,
k\not=0,1
. This is because the
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}\setminus\{k/(k-1)\}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}\setminus\{0\}
function
\alpha\mapsto\frac{k-1}{k(k-1)\alpha-k^{2}}
is a bijection for each
k\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\setminus\{0,1\}
.

Therefore,

\left\lvert\left\{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}\setminus{\mathbb{F}}_{q}:f\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\not=0,\,f\left(\frac{k-1}{k(k-1)\alpha-k^{2}}\right)\not=1\ \ \forall k\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\setminus\{0,1\}\right\}\right\rvert=\\
=\left\lvert\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}\setminus\left({\mathbb{F}}_{q}\cup\bigcup_{k\not=1}B_{k}\right)\right\rvert\geq q^{h}-q-(q-1)q^{h-1}+1=q^{h-1}-q+1>0.

We have shown that there always exists an
\alpha
such that the second and third conditions of Theorem
3.4
are met. It remains to find an
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-linear
g\colon\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
such that
g(1)=g(\alpha)=1
. A
g
like this exists, because to obtain an
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{h}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
linear functional, the values can be freely chosen on an
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-linearly independent set, and
\{1,\alpha\}
is such a set because
\alpha\not\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
. If
g(1)=g(\alpha)=1
, then any such
g
is obviously nonzero.
∎

Corollary 3.6
.

For all prime powers
q
, there exists a
2
-fold blocking set of size
2(q^{h}+q^{h-1}+1)
in
\PG(2,q^{h})
,
h\geq 2
.

4
3
-fold blocking sets

4.1
Preliminaries to the main construction

Similarly to what we did in the case of
2
-fold blocking sets, we now construct three disjoint blocking sets of size
q^{h}+q^{h-1}+1
in
\PG(2,q^{h})
, where
h\geq 2
. We will not concern ourselves with the case of
h=2
, since in that case one can just pick three disjoint Baer subplanes from a partition of the point set of
\PG(2,q^{2})
into Baer subplanes, see e.g.
[
3
]
.

Let us consider a blocking set of the form

{\mathcal{L}}:=\{(x:f(x)+y\alpha:y\beta):x\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}},y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q},(x,y)\neq(0,0)\},

where
f:\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
is a nonzero
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-linear functional,
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}\setminus{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
,
\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}\setminus\{0\}
.
The point set
{\mathcal{L}}
is a blocking set of size
q^{h}+q^{h-1}+1
by Lemma
3.2
with
\vec{u}=(1,0,0)
,
\vec{v}=(0,1,0)
, and
\vec{w}=(0,\alpha,\beta)
.

Next, we take the collineation

\varphi:\PG(2,q^{h})\rightarrow\PG(2,q^{h}),\,(x:y:z)\mapsto(z:x:y).

Note that
\varphi
has order
3
. Since
\varphi
is a collineation,
\varphi({\mathcal{L}})
and
\varphi^{2}({\mathcal{L}})
are also blocking sets of size
q^{h}+q^{h-1}+1
. Our aim is to choose
{\mathcal{L}}
such that
{\mathcal{L}}
,
\varphi({\mathcal{L}})
and
\varphi^{2}({\mathcal{L}})
are pairwise disjoint. Notice that if
{\mathcal{L}}\cap\varphi({\mathcal{L}})=\emptyset
, then applying
\varphi
yields
\varphi({\mathcal{L}})\cap\varphi^{2}({\mathcal{L}})=\varphi(\emptyset)=\emptyset
, and applying
\varphi
again, we get
\varphi^{2}({\mathcal{L}})\cap\varphi^{3}({\mathcal{L}})=\varphi^{2}({\mathcal{L}})\cap{\mathcal{L}}=\emptyset
. This means that it suffices to check that
{\mathcal{L}}\cap\varphi({\mathcal{L}})=\emptyset
in order to obtain a
3
-fold blocking set.

Similarly to Theorem
3.4
, we set conditions on
f
,
\alpha
and
\beta
such that if they hold, then
{\mathcal{L}}
and
\varphi({\mathcal{L}})
are disjoint:

Theorem 4.1
.

The blocking sets
{\mathcal{L}}
and
\varphi({\mathcal{L}})
(defined as above) are disjoint if the following conditions hold:

(1)

f\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)\not=1
,

(2)

k\not=f\left(\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\beta}\right)+\left(f\left(\frac{\beta^{2}}{\alpha+k}\right)+k\right)f(\frac{\alpha}{\beta})+kf\left(\frac{\beta^{2}}{\alpha+k}\right)f\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\right)
for all
k\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

Proof.

The proof is analogous to that of Theorem
3.4
. We take
P\in{\mathcal{L}}
and
P^{\prime}\in\varphi({\mathcal{L}})
, meaning

P=(x:f(x)+y\alpha:y\beta),\quad P^{\prime}=(y^{\prime}\beta:x^{\prime}:f(x^{\prime})+y^{\prime}\alpha),

for some
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
,
y,y^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
. We want to show
P\not=P^{\prime}
by indirectly assuming
P=P^{\prime}
. We will find contradictions case by case based on whether
y
and
y^{\prime}
are
0
or
1
.

1.

y^{\prime}=0
, meaning

(x:f(x)+y\alpha:y\beta)=(0:x^{\prime}:f(x^{\prime})).

The first coordinate has to be zero, meaning
x=0
. But then
y\not=0
(because
(0:0:0)
is not a point), so we can take
y=1
, and we get
(0:x^{\prime}:f(x^{\prime}))=(0:\alpha:\beta)
, or equivalently,
\left(0:\frac{x^{\prime}}{f(x^{\prime})}:1\right)=(0:\frac{\alpha}{\beta}:1)
.
It follows that

f\left(\frac{x^{\prime}}{f(x^{\prime})}\right)=f\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)
. Now, the fact that
f
is an
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-linear functional implies
f\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)=1
, which contradicts the first condition.

2.

y=0
and
y^{\prime}=1
, meaning

(x:f(x):0)=(\beta:x^{\prime}:f(x^{\prime})+\alpha).

This is impossible, since
f(x^{\prime})\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
,
\alpha\not\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, and hence
f(x^{\prime})+\alpha\neq 0
.

3.

y=y^{\prime}=1
, meaning

(x:f(x)+\alpha:\beta)=(\beta:x^{\prime}:f(x^{\prime})+\alpha).

We introduce
k=f(x^{\prime})
and
l=f(x)
(note that both
k
and
l
are in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
).
After cross-multiplying the third coordinates by the first and second coordinates, respectively, and solving the equations for
x
and
x^{\prime}
, we can see that
P=P^{\prime}
implies

x=\frac{\beta^{2}}{k+\alpha}\text{, and }x^{\prime}=\frac{(k+\alpha)(l+\alpha)}{\beta}=kl\frac{1}{\beta}+(l+k)\frac{\alpha}{\beta}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\beta}.

Applying
f
to both equations, we obtain:

l=f\left(\frac{\beta^{2}}{k+\alpha}\right)\text{, and }k=klf\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\right)+(l+k)f\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)+f\left(\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\beta}\right).

Substituting
l=f\left(\frac{\beta^{2}}{\alpha+k}\right)
into the second equation yields:

k=f\left(\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\beta}\right)+\left(f\left(\frac{\beta^{2}}{\alpha+k}\right)+k\right)f\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)+kf\left(\frac{\beta^{2}}{\alpha+k}\right)f\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\right).

Since
k\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, this contradicts the second condition of the statement.

∎

Remark 4.2
.

If such a
3
-fold blocking set exists, then of course there exists a
2
-fold blocking set as well, by picking two of the disjoint blocking sets.

The conditions of this theorem are too complicated for a simple counting argument (as in Proposition
3.5
) to work. In the next two subsections, we show the existence of
\alpha,\beta
and
f
for which the conditions of Theorem
4.1
hold with
h\geq 3
.

4.2
The case of
h\geq 4
.

We will use Theorem
4.1
to show that a
3
-fold blocking set of size
3(q^{h}+q^{h-1}+1)
exists in
\PG(2,q^{h})
for
h\geq 4
. Therefore, we want to find an
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}\setminus{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
,
\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}\setminus\{0\}
and an
f\colon\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
nonzero linear functional satisfying the conditions of the theorem.

Take any
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}
such that
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}={\mathbb{F}}_{q}(\alpha)
, and let

m(x)=x^{h}+\lambda_{h-1}x^{h-1}+\dots+\lambda_{1}x+\lambda_{0}

be its minimal polynomial over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
. Put
\beta:=1
and define
f
on the basis
1,\alpha,\dots,\alpha^{h-1}
by:

f\left(\alpha^{j}\right):=\begin{cases}1\quad\text{if}\quad j=0\\
s\quad\text{if}\quad j=2\\
t\quad\text{if}\quad j=h-1\\
0\quad\text{otherwise}\end{cases},

(4.1)

where
s,t\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
will be chosen later. In fact, we aim to show that there exist at least
q-1
choices for
(s,t)
such that the conditions of Theorem
4.1
hold.
For this, we need to calculate
\frac{1}{\alpha+k}
as the linear combination of the
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-basis
1,\alpha,\dots,\alpha^{h-1}
of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{h}}
. This will be summarized in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\alpha\not\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
,
k\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, and
p\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[x]
be a monic polynomial of degree
h>1
for which
p(\alpha)=0
. Then there exist coefficients
a_{1,k},\dots,a_{h-1,k}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
such that

(\alpha-k)\left(\alpha^{h-1}+a_{1,k}\alpha^{h-2}+\dots+a_{h-2,k}\alpha+a_{h-1,k}\right)=-p(k).

In particular, if
m
is the minimal polynomial of
\alpha
, and
k\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, then there exist unique coefficients
c_{1,k},\dots,c_{h-1,k}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
such that

\frac{1}{\alpha+k}=-\frac{1}{m(-k)}\left(\alpha^{h-1}+c_{1,k}\alpha^{h-2}+\dots+c_{h-2,k}\alpha+c_{h-1,k}\right).

Proof.

Consider the polynomial
r_{k}(x)=p(x)-p(k)
. Since
r_{k}(k)=0
, and
r_{k}
is a monic polynomial, there exists a monic polynomial
w_{r}(x)=x^{h-1}+a_{1,k}x^{h-2}+\dots+a_{h-1,k}
of degree
h-1
such that
r_{k}(x)=(x-k)w_{k}(x)
. Substituting
\alpha
and using
p(\alpha)=0
, we obtain our first equation.

For the second equation, we apply the first one with
p=m
. We need
\alpha+k\neq 0
and
m(-k)\not=0
for all
k\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, which holds since
\alpha\not\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
. Substituting
k\mapsto-k
into the first equation and dividing both sides by
-(\alpha+k)\cdot m(-k)
, we get our second equation (with
c_{i,k}:=a_{i,-k}
). The coefficients
c_{i,k}
are unique since
1,\alpha,\dots,\alpha^{h-1}
are
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-linearly independent.
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}\setminus{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
with
\mathbb{F}_{q^{h}}={\mathbb{F}}_{q}(\alpha)
,
\beta:=1
and define
f
as in
4.1
for some
s,t\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
. Then, there exist at least
q-1
pairs
(s,t)
such that the conditions of Theorem
4.1
hold for
(\alpha,1,f)
.

Proof.

Substituting
\beta=1
into the first condition of Theorem
4.1
, we obtain
f(\alpha)\neq 1
, which is true, because we set
f(\alpha)=0
.

Substituting into the second condition of Theorem
4.1
, we obtain

k\not=f(\alpha^{2})+kf\left(\frac{1}{\alpha+k}\right)

(4.2)

for all
k\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
. For
k=0
, this condition is
f(\alpha^{2})\not=0
, meaning
s\not=0
. We now consider all
q^{2}-q
pairs
(s,t)\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\setminus\{0\}\times{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, and count how many pairs fail to satisfy the second condition for some
k\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\setminus\{0\}
.
By Lemma
4.3
, if
m
is the minimal polynomial of
\alpha
, then

\frac{1}{\alpha+k}=-\frac{1}{m(-k)}\left(\alpha^{h-1}+c_{1,k}\alpha^{h-2}+\dots+c_{h-2,k}\alpha+c_{h-1,k}\right)

for some unique
c_{i,k}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, meaning

f\left(\frac{1}{\alpha+k}\right)=\frac{c_{h-1,k}+c_{h-3,k}s+t}{-m(-k)},

from our definition of
f
. Substituting into (
4.2
) and multiplying by
\frac{m(-k)}{k}\neq 0
, we obtain that a pair
(s,t)
fails the second condition for a given
k\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\setminus\{0\}
if only if

-t-c_{h-3,k}s-c_{h-1,k}+\frac{m(-k)}{k}s=m(-k)\Longleftrightarrow t=-m(-k)-c_{h-1,k}-c_{h-3,k}s+\frac{m(-k)}{k}s,

which is a linear equation in
t
, therefore for every
s,k\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\setminus\{0\}
, there is exactly one
t
for which
(s,t)
fails to satisfy the second condition.

It follows that there are at least
(q^{2}-q)-(q-1)^{2}=q-1
pairs
(s,t)
that satisfy both conditions of Theorem
4.1
.
∎

4.3
The case of
h=3

In this case, we use a slightly different construction, because when defining
f
we have only three degrees of freedom instead of at least four.

Again, put
\beta:=1
. We will choose an appropriate
\alpha\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{3}}\setminus{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
and show the existence of an
f
such that the triple
(\alpha,1,f)
satisfies the conditions of Theorem
4.1
. As it turns out, the following lemma will be very useful for our construction:

Lemma 4.5
.

The number of triples
(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{3}
, where the polynomial
p(x)=x^{3}-\lambda_{2}x^{2}-\lambda_{1}x-\lambda_{0}
is irreducible over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, and the polynomial
r(x)=x^{3}+\lambda_{2}x^{2}-(\lambda_{1}-1)x+\lambda_{0}
is reducible over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, is at least

\frac{q(q-1)^{2}}{9}-\frac{2(q-1)}{3}.

Proof.

Fix
r(x)=x^{3}+\lambda_{2}x^{2}-(\lambda_{1}-1)x+\lambda_{0}
, and assume that it is reducible. Since it has degree
3
, it follows that
r
has a root in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
. Let us fix
k\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
as a root. If
k=0
, then
\lambda_{0}=0
, so the polynomial
p(x)=x^{3}-\lambda_{2}x^{2}-\lambda_{1}x-\lambda_{0}
is also not irreducible, so we will assume
k\not=0
.
If
r(k)=0
, then

k^{2}\lambda_{2}-k\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{0}=-k^{3}-k,

(4.3)

so there are exactly
q^{2}
choices for
(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
such that
r(k)=0
. We will give a lower bound on the number of triples
(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
for which
p(x)
is irreducible and
r(k)=0
. This will be achieved by giving an upper bound on all remaining cases, namely those for which
r(k)=0
and
p(x)
is reducible. We will make use of Vieta’s formulas: If
p(x)=x^{3}-\lambda_{2}x^{2}-\lambda_{1}x-\lambda_{0}=(x-r_{1})(x-r_{2})(x-r_{3})
, then
\lambda_{2}=r_{1}+r_{2}+r_{3}
,
-\lambda_{1}=r_{1}r_{2}+r_{2}r_{3}+r_{1}r_{3}
,
\lambda_{0}=r_{1}r_{2}r_{3}
.

1.

p(x)
has one root of multiplicity
3
, meaning

p(x)=(x-r_{1})^{3}\text{, }r_{1}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}.

After substituting Vieta’s formulas into (
4.3
), we obtain
r_{1}^{3}-3kr_{1}^{2}+3r_{1}k^{2}=-k^{3}-k
. This is a cubic equation in
r_{1}
, meaning there are at most
3
solutions, so at most
3
triples
(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
for which
r(k)=0
and
p(x)
has a
3
-fold root in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
.

2.

p(x)
has two distinct roots, one with multiplicity
2
, meaning

p(x)=(x-r_{1})(x-r_{2})^{2}\text{, }r_{1},r_{2}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q},\,r_{1}\neq r_{2}.

Similarly, substitution into (
4.3
) gives
r_{1}(k^{2}+2kr_{2}+r_{2}^{2})=-k^{3}-k-2k^{2}r_{2}-kr_{2}^{2}
, or equivalently,
r_{1}(k+r_{2})^{2}=-k(k+r_{2})^{2}-k
. If
r_{2}=-k
, we get
k=0
, a contradiction. If
r_{2}\not=-k
, we have a linear equation in
r_{1}
, so for each
r_{2}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\setminus\{-k\}
, we have exactly one solution for
r_{1}
. This means that there are at most
(q-1)
triples
(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
, one for every
r_{2}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\setminus\{-k\}
, for which
r(k)=0
and
p(x)
has two distinct roots, one with multiplicity
2
.

3.

p(x)
has three distinct roots, meaning

p(x)=(x-r_{1})(x-r_{2})(x-r_{3})\text{, }r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\text{ are pairwise distinct.}

Similarly to the previous case, substitution into (
4.3
) gives us
r_{3}(k^{2}+kr_{1}+kr_{2}+r_{1}r_{2})=-k(k^{2}+kr_{1}+kr_{2}+r_{1}r_{2})-k
. If
k^{2}+kr_{1}+kr_{2}+r_{1}r_{2}=0
, we again get
k=0
, a contradiction. If
k^{2}+kr_{1}+kr_{2}+r_{1}r_{2}\not=0
, then we get a linear equation in
r_{3}
, thus, for any pair
r_{1},r_{2}
there is at most one
r_{3}
such that this case holds. Since
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
are distinct, and their roles are symmetric, we can choose them in
q\choose 2
ways. In these
q\choose 2
cases, we counted every
\{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}\}
unordered triple 3 times, because we fixed
r_{3}
to be the third, meaning, for this case there are at most
\frac{1}{3}{q\choose 2}=\frac{q^{2}-q}{6}
triples
(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
for which
r(k)=0
and
p(x)
has three pairwise distinct roots.

4.

p(x)
has one root in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
and two conjugate roots in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}
, meaning

p(x)=(x-r_{1})(x-r_{2})(x-r_{2}^{q}),\,r_{1}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q},\,r_{2}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}\setminus{\mathbb{F}}_{q}.

As in the previous case, we can see that for any choice of
r_{2}
(which determines
r_{2}^{q}
) there is at most 1 suitable choice for
r_{1}
. Since
r_{2}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}\setminus{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, we can fix either
r_{2}
or
r_{2}^{q}
, meaning there are
\frac{q^{2}-q}{2}
choices. This means that in this case there are at most
\frac{q^{2}-q}{2}
possible triples
(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
for which
r(k)=0
and
p(x)
has an irreducible factor of degree
2
.

Using these upper bounds, we can get a lower bound on the number of triples where
p(x)
is irreducible and
k
is a root of
r(x)
:

\left\lvert\{p(x)\text{ is irreducible over }{\mathbb{F}}_{q}:r(k)=0\}\right\rvert\geq q^{2}-3-(q-1)-\frac{q^{2}-q}{6}-\frac{q^{2}-q}{2}=\frac{q(q-1)}{3}-2.

Summing over all
k\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\setminus\{0\}
gives us:

\frac{q(q-1)^{2}}{3}-2(q-1)\leq\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}}|\{(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}):r(k)=0,\,p\text{ is irreducible}\}|\leq

3|\{(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}):p\text{ is irreducible},\,r\text{ is reducible}\}|,

where we obtained the last inequality because
r
can have at most
3
roots in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, so each pair
(p,r)
, with
p
irreducible and
r
reducible, is counted at most
3
times. Dividing by
3
gives the inequality of the statement.
∎

Now we are ready to prove our main claim.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
q>10
be a prime power. There exists an
\alpha\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{3}}\setminus{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
and an
f\colon{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{3}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
linear functional such that the triple
(\alpha,1,f)
satisfies the conditions of Theorem
4.1
.

Proof.

First, take an arbitrary
\alpha\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{3}}\setminus{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, we will specify the precise conditions on
\alpha
later. Define
f
on the basis
1,\alpha,\alpha^{2}
by
f(1)=1
,
f(\alpha)=0
, and
f(\alpha^{2})=l
, where
l\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
will be chosen later. The conditions that
f
needs to satisfy (after substituting
\beta=1
and the known values of
f
) are:

1.

f(\alpha)\not=1
(the first condition of Theorem
4.1
),

2.

0\not=l
(the second condition of Theorem
4.1
with
k=0
),

3.

k\neq l+kf\left(\frac{1}{\alpha+k}\right)
for all
k\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}
, or equivalently,

f\left(\frac{1}{\alpha+k}\right)\not=1-\frac{l}{k},

(4.4)

for all
k\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}
(the second condition of Theorem
4.1
with
k\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}
).

For a fixed
k\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}
, we can write

\frac{1}{\alpha+k}=c_{k}+a_{k}\alpha+b_{k}\alpha^{2},

(4.5)

with
a_{k},b_{k},c_{k}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, since
\{1,\alpha,\alpha^{2}\}
is an
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-basis of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{3}}
. Applying
f
, we obtain:

f\left(\frac{1}{\alpha+k}\right)=c_{k}+lb_{k},

(4.6)

so condition (
4.4
) reads as
c_{k}+lb_{k}\not=1-\frac{l}{k}
, or equivalently,
(b_{k}+\frac{1}{k})l\not=1-c_{k}
. This means that a triple
(\alpha,1,f)
cannot satisfy the conditions of Theorem
4.1
if there exists
k\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}
such that
b_{k}=-\frac{1}{k}
and
c_{k}=1
. So, our first condition on
\alpha
is that for all
a\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
,
k\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}
:

\frac{1}{\alpha+k}\neq 1+a\alpha-\frac{1}{k}\alpha^{2},

cf. (
4.5
). For
a\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
and
k\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}
this is a cubic equation in
\alpha
(after multiplying by
\alpha+k
), thus it has at most
3
roots, so the number of choices for
\alpha
that we have to exclude is bounded above by
q+3q(q-1)
(the roots of these
q(q-1)
polynomials and the elements of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
).

Choosing
\alpha
like this means that if for some
k\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}
it holds that
b_{k}=-\frac{1}{k}
, then, for this
k
, all
l\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
satisfy (
4.4
).
If
b_{k}\not=-\frac{1}{k}
, then
(b_{k}+\frac{1}{k})l=1-c_{k}
is linear in
l
, thus, for this
k
, there is exactly one
l\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
which doesn’t satisfy (
4.4
). If we can choose
\alpha
such that for some
k\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}
we have
b_{k}=-\frac{1}{k}
, then, out of the
q-1
possible nonzero values of
l
, there are at most
q-2
values which, for some
k
, violate (
4.4
). So, in this case we can choose
l
such that
(\alpha,1,f)
satisfies the conditions of Theorem
4.1
.

We now uncover what
b_{k}=-\frac{1}{k}
actually means. It is equivalent to:

\frac{1}{\alpha+k}=c_{k}+a_{k}\alpha-\frac{1}{k}\alpha^{2}\Leftrightarrow\alpha^{3}-k(a_{k}-1)\alpha^{2}-k(a_{k}k+c_{k})\alpha-k(c_{k}k-1)=0.

Thus
\alpha
is a root of the monic cubic
x^{3}-k(a_{k}-1)x^{2}-k(a_{k}k+c_{k})x-k(c_{k}k-1)\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[x]
. If we let the minimal polynomial of
\alpha
be
p(x)=x^{3}-\lambda_{2}x^{2}-\lambda_{1}x-\lambda_{0}
, then
\lambda_{2}=k(a_{k}-1)
,
\lambda_{1}=k(a_{k}k+c_{k})
and
\lambda_{0}=k(c_{k}k-1)
. Eliminating
a_{k}
and
c_{k}
and solving for
k
, we obtain

k^{3}+\lambda_{2}k^{2}-(\lambda_{1}-1)k+\lambda_{0}=0,

which is equivalent to the polynomial
r(x)=x^{3}+\lambda_{2}x^{2}-(\lambda_{1}-1)x+\lambda_{0}
having a root in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, i.e. being reducible.
So, to have
b_{k}=-\frac{1}{k}
for some
k\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}
, we have to find a triple
(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{3}
such that
p(x)=x^{3}-\lambda_{2}x^{2}-\lambda_{1}x-\lambda_{0}
is irreducible, and
r(x)=x^{3}+\lambda_{2}x^{2}-(\lambda_{1}-1)x+\lambda_{0}
is reducible. By Lemma
4.5
, there exist at least
\frac{q(q-1)^{2}}{9}-\frac{2(q-1)}{3}
such triples. We can choose
\alpha
to be any root of any such
p(x)
. Since these polynomials are irreducible and pairwise distinct, each of them has
3
conjugate roots, and all of these roots are distinct, so there are at least
\frac{q(q-1)^{2}}{3}-{2(q-1)}
choices for
\alpha
.

Note that in (
4.6
) we already had some conditions on
\alpha
. If
\frac{q(q-1)^{2}}{3}-2(q-1)>q+3q(q-1)
, then we can certainly choose an
\alpha
for which there exists a suitable
l
, and hence
f
, such that
(\alpha,1,f)
satisfies the conditions of Theorem
4.1
. This inequality holds for
q>10
, which proves our claim.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

The prime powers below
11
are
2,3,4,5,7,8,9
. In these cases, one can find a suitable
\alpha

Corollary 4.8
.

For all prime powers
q
, there exists a
3
-fold blocking set of size
3(q^{h}+q^{h-1}+1)
in
\PG(2,q^{h})
,
h\geq 2
.

Theorem
1.3
follows from
Corollaries
3.6
and

.
