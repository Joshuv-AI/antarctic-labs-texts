Symplectic convolutional neural networks
Süleyman Yıldız, Konrad Janik, Peter Benner

Abstract
We propose a new symplectic convolutional neural network (CNN) architecture by leveraging symplectic neural networks, proper symplectic decomposition, and tensor techniques. Specifically, we first introduce a mathematically equivalent form of the convolution layer and then, using symplectic neural networks, we demonstrate a way to parameterize the layers of the CNN to ensure that the convolution layer remains symplectic. To construct a complete autoencoder, we introduce a symplectic pooling layer. We demonstrate the performance of the proposed neural network on three examples: the wave equation, the nonlinear Schrödinger (NLS) equation, and the sine-Gordon equation. The numerical results indicate that the symplectic CNN outperforms the linear symplectic autoencoder obtained via proper symplectic decomposition.

Among the many techniques in deep learning, autoencoders have emerged as a crucial component. Dimension reduction methods can be classified into two main categories, namely linear and nonlinear methods

For example, when constructing reduced-order models for canonical Hamiltonian systems, one property to be enforced on the autoencoder might be symplecticity. In this paper, we present a symplectic autoencoder method that can be applied to various problems. However, since its primary application is dimensionality reduction for Hamiltonian dynamics, we focus on the relevant literature. Dimensionality reduction for Hamiltonian dynamics becomes essential when learning Hamiltonian dynamics with high-fidelity data arising from the discretization of partial differential equations. Learning the dynamics of Hamiltonian systems using high-dimensional data might be infeasible, even with high-performance computing (HPC) machines. A large portion of popular autoencoder algorithms depends on CNNs, which have become a cornerstone in the field. With their wide range of successful applications, CNNs have proven to be highly effective tools. \mathsfbfit{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times C_{\text{in}}\times N}

\vdots&\ddots&&\ddots&&&\vskip 6.90002pt\\

Symplectic CNNs

In this section, we define a symplectic convolutional autoencoder by integrating ideas from SympNets
[
21
]
and the symplectic autoencoder
[
29
]
. We begin with a brief overview of Hamiltonian systems and describe symplectic lifting and reduction. Then, we summarize SympNets
[
21
]
and demonstrate how these ideas can be integrated to construct a convolutional autoencoder.

Let us first denote
\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
as the matrix of zeros,
\mathbfit{I}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
as the identity matrix,
\mathbfit{x}_{0}
as the initial condition, and
\nabla{x}
as the gradient with respect to
\mathbfit{x}
.
Canonical Hamiltonian systems are defined by:

\dot{\mathbfit{x}}(t)=\mathbfit{J}_{2n}\nabla{x}H(\mathbfit{x}(t))\in\mathbb{R}^{2n},\hskip 11.49994pt\mathbfit{x}(0)=\mathbfit{x}_{0},

(5)

where
\mathbfit{J}_{2n}
is the canonical Poisson matrix of the form

\mathbfit{J}_{2n}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}&\mathbfit{I}_{n}\\
-\mathbfit{I}_{n}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n},

(6)

and the state
\mathbfit{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
contains generalized momenta
\mathbfit{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and generalized positions
\mathbfit{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The dynamics of Hamiltonian system
eq.

5
is determined by the Hamiltonian “energy” function
H\colon\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to\mathbb{R}
, which remains constant over time, i.e.

\dfrac{d}{dt}H(\mathbfit{x}(t))=\nabla{x}H(\mathbfit{x}(t))^{T}\dot{\mathbfit{x}}(t)=\nabla{x}H(\mathbfit{x}(t))^{T}\mathbfit{J}_{2n}\nabla{x}H(\mathbfit{x}(t))=0.

Before introducing other properties of Hamiltonian systems, let us define symplectic transformations for both linear and general nonlinear cases. Let
\mathbb{V}
denote a vector space of dimension
2n
, and let
\Omega
be the symplectic form on
\mathbb{V}
. The symplectic form
\Omega
is an alternating bilinear, and nondegenerate form,
\Omega:\mathbb{V}\times\mathbb{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Assuming
\mathbb{V}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, for all
\mathbfit{\xi},\mathbfit{\nu}\in\mathbb{V}
, the symplectic structure can be represented as follows:

\Omega(\mathbfit{\xi},\mathbfit{\nu})=\mathbfit{\xi}^{T}\mathbfit{J}_{2n}\mathbfit{\nu}.

Moreover,
(\mathbb{V},\Omega)
is called the symplectic vector space. Let us introduce
(\mathbb{V},\Omega)
and
(\mathbb{W},\omega)
as two symplectic vector space with
\dim(\mathbb{V})=2n
,
\dim(\mathbb{W})=2k
, and
k\leq n
.

Definition 1

(
[
19
]
)
.

A linear map
\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{W}\rightarrow\mathbb{V}
is called symplectic lifting if it preserves the symplectic structure:

\omega(\mathbfit{z},\mathbfit{w})=\Omega(\mathcal{A}(\mathbfit{z}),\mathcal{A}(\mathbfit{w})

(7)

In canonical coordinates, where we can represent
\mathcal{A}
via a matrix
\mathbfit{A}
,
eq.

7
is equivalent to the following condition:

\mathbfit{A}^{T}\mathbfit{J}_{2n}\mathbfit{A}=\mathbfit{J}_{2k}.

(8)

Additionally, a matrix satisfying the condition in
eq.

8
is called a symplectic matrix. Let us denote the set of all symplectic matrices
A\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2k}
by
Sp(2k,\mathbb{R}^{2n})
, which is referred to as the symplectic Stiefel manifold
[
19
]
.

Definition 2

(
[
19
]
)
.

The symplectic inverse
\mathbfit{A}^{+}
of a symplectic matrix
\mathbfit{A}\in Sp(2k,\mathbb{R}^{2n})
defined as:

\mathbfit{A}^{+}=\mathbfit{J}^{T}_{k}\mathbfit{A}^{T}\mathbfit{J}_{2n}.

In general, let us recall a definition of a symplectic nonlinear transformation from, e.g.,
[
31
]
.

Definition 3

(
[
31
]
)
.

A map
\bm{\psi}:\mathbb{R}^{2k}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n},\penalty\ n\geq k
is a symplectic transformation from
\mathbb{R}^{2k}
to
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
when the following condition is fulfilled:

(\Diff\bm{\psi}{x})^{T}\mathbfit{J}_{2n}\Diff\bm{\psi}{x}=\mathbfit{J}_{2k},\hskip 22.99988pt\forall\penalty\ \mathbfit{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2k},

(9)

where
\Diff\bm{\psi}{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2k}
is the Jacobian of
\bm{\psi}
with respect to
\mathbfit{x}
. This implies that the Jacobian of the map with respect to the state
\mathbfit{x}
is satisfying
eq.

8
, i.e., the Jacobian is a symplectic matrix for all
\mathbfit{x}
. Furthermore, we refer to it as a
symplectic lifting

[
31
]
.

The notion of symplecticity plays two important roles in Hamiltonian dynamics. Firstly, the flow
\mathbf{F}^{t}
of Hamiltonian systems is symplectic. The flow of a Hamiltonian system refers to the map that transforms the initial point to the corresponding solution of the system at time
t
, i.e.,
\mathbf{F}^{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0})=\mathbf{x}(t)
. This concept has been utilized in SympNets
[
21
]
to learn the flow of Hamiltonian systems. Secondly, symplectic transformations, also known as canonical transformations, are used for coordinate transformations in Hamiltonian systems. A canonical transformation preserves Hamilton’s equations, meaning it transforms Hamiltonian coordinates into another set of coordinates that also describe a Hamiltonian system. For a more detailed overview of symplectic transformations and Hamiltonian systems, we refer to the book
[
35
]
.

SympNets

3.2.1
Convolutional modules

We utilize the SympNet architecture to construct symplectic convolutional layers via fixing some of the weights of the convolutional layers to be identity and some of them to be symmetric. We explain the idea with an example before we provide the formal definitions.

Consider two input and output channel case, which can be represented as follows

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbfit{T}_{1,1}&\mathbfit{T}_{1,2}\\
\mathbfit{T}_{2,1}&\mathbfit{T}_{2,2}\\
\end{bmatrix}\mathbfit{x}=\mathbfit{y}.

(10)

Using
4
, a linear symplectic convolution layer can be constructed by parameterizing the Toeplitz matrices in
eq.

10
with
\mathbfit{T}_{1,1}=\mathbfit{T}_{2,2}=\mathbfit{I}
,
\mathbfit{T}_{2,1}=\mathbf{0}
, and
\mathbfit{T}_{1,2}=\mathbfit{T}_{1,2}^{T}
, resulting in an upper triangular symplectic layer. By splitting the input data into two channels corresponding to position and momenta in Hamiltonian dynamics, one can achieve a symplectic transformation by only parameterizing the weights of the convolutional layer. For instance, setting one channel to be an identity matrix can be easily accomplished by setting the weight tensor of length
l=3
with
\mathsfbfit{w}_{1,1,\cdot}=[0,1,0]
.

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}c\mathbfit{I}&\ldots&c\mathbfit{I}&\mathbf{0}\ldots&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbfit{T}_{1}&\ldots\mathbfit{T}_{d}&c\mathbfit{I}&\ldots&c\mathbfit{I}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}&&&\mathbfit{I}&&\\
&&&&\ddots&\\
&&&&&\mathbfit{I}\\
-\mathbfit{I}&&&&&\\
&\ddots&&&&\\
&&-\mathbfit{I}&&&\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c\mathbfit{I}&\mathbfit{T}_{1}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\

because

3.2.3
Symplectic pooling

To construct a general nonlinear symplectic encoder, we lastly construct symplectic max-pooling layers by utilizing PSD-like matrices. First, let us define the equivalent matrix form of
max-pooling
for equal stride and kernel size. For simplicity, we consider the following one-channel 1D input signal as an example, to derive the general definition:

\mathbfit{x}=\begin{bmatrix}2\\
1\\
3\\
5\\
\end{bmatrix}.

A 1D
max-pooling
operation
\pool
with a stride and kernel size of two, padding of zero and dilation of one can be equivalently written with the following matrix:

\mathbfit{\Phi}(\mathbfit{x})=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&1\end{bmatrix},

such that
\pool(\mathbfit{x})=\mathbfit{\Phi}(\mathbfit{x})\mathbfit{x}
, which means that
\mathbfit{\Phi}(\mathbfit{x})
is the Jacobian of the
max-pooling
operation at
\mathbfit{x}
. Note that
\mathbfit{\Phi}(\mathbfit{x})\mathbfit{\Phi}(\mathbfit{x})^{T}=\mathbfit{I}_{2}
. We will show later that this is no coincidence.
Hence, assuming we have an input signal with two input channels, applying the same pooling operation to both channels can equivalently be expressed as:

\mathbfit{P}(\mathbfit{x})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbfit{\Phi}(\mathbfit{x})&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbfit{\Phi}(\mathbfit{x})\end{bmatrix}

where
\mathbfit{P}(\mathbfit{x})
is the symplectic inverse (
2
) of
\mathbfit{P}(\mathbfit{x})^{T}\in\mathbb{M}(2N,2k)
, which is a symplectic projection.

Proposition 4

The symplectic pooling is symplectic in the sense that it is the symplectic inverse of a symplectic lifting.

In the general case, fixing the stride and kernel size to be the same yields a mathematically equivalent form

depending on the maximum element in the window spanned by the pooling kernel, and
\mathbfit{\Phi}_{i,:}(\mathbfit{x})
denoting the
i
-th row of
\mathbfit{\Phi}(\mathbfit{x})
. Also, kernel size and stride being the same, results in every component of
\mathbfit{x}
only appearing in one window. Hence,
\pi{x}
is injective. With this observation, it holds

\displaystyle\left(\mathbfit{\Phi}(\mathbfit{x})\mathbfit{\Phi}(\mathbfit{x})^{T}\right)_{ij}=\mathbfit{\Phi}(\mathbfit{x})_{i,:}\left(\mathbfit{\Phi}(\mathbfit{x})^{T}\right)_{:,j}=\mathbfit{e}_{\pi{x}(i)}^{T}\mathbfit{e}_{\pi{x}(j)}=\delta_{\pi{x}(i)\pi{x}(j)}=\delta_{ij}

for
i,j=1,...,N/k
, which yields
\mathbfit{\Phi}(\mathbfit{x})\mathbfit{\Phi}(\mathbfit{x})^{T}=\mathbfit{I}_{N/k}
. According to
eq.

14
this is sufficient to show that the jacobians of
p_{\text{up}}
and
p_{\text{low}}
are symplectic inverses of symplectic liftings. Hence, this also holds for
p_{\text{up}}
and
p_{\text{low}}
themselves.
∎

Following our example, we want to introduce symplectic unpooling
\unpool
as well

\displaystyle\unpool(\pool(\mathbfit{x}))=\begin{bmatrix}2\\
0\\
0\\
5\end{bmatrix}.

This means that we can represent the unpooling operation by
\mathbfit{\Phi}(\mathbfit{x})^{T}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\unpool(\pool(\mathbfit{x}))=\mathbfit{\Phi}(\mathbfit{x})^{T}\mathbfit{\Phi}(\mathbfit{x})\mathbfit{x}.

Definition 10

(Symplectic unpooling)
.

Let
\pool
be the
max-pooling
operation with dilation of one, padding of zero and stride and kernel size
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Furthermore, let
\mathbfit{\Phi}(\mathbfit{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{N/k\times N}
be the Jacobian of
\pool
at
\mathbfit{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},
where
k
divides
N
, i.e.,
k\mathrel{|}N
. Additionally, let
\mathbfit{\tilde{x}}_{1},\mathbfit{\tilde{x}}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. The
symplectic unpooling modules
for two input channels with respect to the previous pooling respresented by
\mathbfit{\Phi}(\mathbfit{x})
are given by

\displaystyle u\begin{bmatrix}\mathbfit{\tilde{x}}_{1}\\
\mathbfit{\tilde{x}}_{2}\end{bmatrix}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbfit{\Phi}(\mathbfit{x})^{T}\mathbfit{\tilde{x}}_{1}\\
\mathbfit{\Phi}(\mathbfit{x})^{T}\mathbfit{\tilde{x}}_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

We denote the
set of symplectic unpooling modules
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\text{U}}:=\left\{u\,\middle|\,u\text{ is a symplectic unpooling module}\right\}.

Proposition 5
.

The symplectic unpooling modules are symplectic liftings.

Proof.

The proof of
4
yields
\mathbfit{\Phi}(\mathbfit{x})\mathbfit{\Phi}(\mathbfit{x})^{T}=\mathbfit{I}_{k}
, which is sufficient to show that the unpooling modules are symplectic according to
eq.

14
.
∎

3.2.4
Building the autoencoder

Finally, we can define the symplectic encoder. The idea is to lieft the dynamics first using convolutional and activation modules, before reducing them with a pooling and a PSD-like layer. This approach is visualized in
Figure

10
. We formally introduce the symplectic encoder with the following definition.

Definition 11

(Symplectic encoder)
.

The
set of symplectic encoders

\Psi_{\text{Enc}}
is given by

\displaystyle\Psi_{\text{Enc}}:=\left\{\psi=z\circ p\circ w_{l}\circ y_{l}\circ\ldots\circ w_{1}\circ y_{1}\,\middle|\,\begin{aligned} &y_{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{Conv}},\ w_{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{A}},\ z\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{PSD}},\\
&p\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{P}},\penalty\ i=1,...,l,\penalty\ l\in\mathbb{N}\end{aligned}\right\}.

Remark 8
.

Note that the dimensions and channel numbers of the outputs of the previous modules have to match the input dimension and channel of the next module. Since we assume the number of input channels to be 2 for activation, pooling and PSD-like modules, we flatten the input before applying them.

In practice, one can use the symplectic inverse of each layer to construct a symplectic decoder, which would reduce the number of parameters. Nevertheless, we construct the symplectic decoder via
symplectic inverse-like maps
to obtain a Petrov-Galerkin approach like in
[
30
,
29
]
.
Using the modules defined in the previous sections, we can define the a general symplectic decoder by inverting the order of modules compared to the encoder.

Definition 12

(Symplectic decoder)
.

The
set of symplectic decoders

\Psi_{\text{Dec}}
is given by

\displaystyle\Psi_{\text{Dec}}:=\left\{\psi=y_{1}\circ w_{1}\circ\ldots\circ w_{l}\circ y_{l}\circ u\circ z\,\middle|\,\begin{aligned} &y_{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{ConvT}},\ w_{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{A}},\ z\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{PSDT}},\\
&u\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{U}},\penalty\ i=1,...,l,\penalty\ l\in\mathbb{N}\end{aligned}\right\}.

Combining the symplectic encoder and decoder, we define the symplectic convolutional autoencoder as a pair of parametric mappings.

Definition 13

(Symplectic autoencoder)
.

The
set of symplectic autoendocders
is given by

\displaystyle\Psi_{\text{AE}}:=\left\{\psi=\psi_{\text{Dec}}\circ\psi_{\text{Enc}}\,\middle|\,\psi_{\text{Dec}}\in\Psi_{\text{Dec}},\penalty\ \psi_{\text{Enc}}\in\Psi_{\text{Enc}}\right\}.

The one-dimensional cubic NLS equation is given by:

(24)

\displaystyle u(x,0)=u^{0}(x),

\displaystyle x\in\Omega.

5
Conclusions

We have proposed a nonlinear symplectic convolutional autoencoder by utilizing symplectic neural networks (SympNet) and proper symplectic decomposition (PSD). The main application of our method is the transformation of Hamiltonian systems into an equivalent, low-dimensional, Hamiltonian form. Nevertheless, the method is not limited to Hamiltonian systems; many physical phenomena that require volume-preserving properties can be modeled via the proposed method. We compared our method with PSD in terms of data compression and reconstruction. We demonstrated the generality of the method by combining it with SympNet after dimensionality reduction.

Some future work motivated by this study includes testing the symplectic convolutional autoencoder with noisy data and extending the framework to a 3D symplectic convolutional autoencoder case.
