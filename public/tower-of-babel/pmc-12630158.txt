RETRACTED ARTICLE: Restoring the Sense of Smell: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Biologic Therapies for Chronic Rhinosinusitis with Nasal Polyps
Authors: Christian K. Pedersen, Vibeke Backer, Jens Even Tidemandsen, Simon Høj, Marie Høxbro, Anne-Sophie Homøe, Kasper Aanæs, Christian von Buchwald
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Purpose of ReviewSmell loss is among the most debilitating symptoms of chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP). We aimed to synthesize current evidence on the smell-restoring efficacy of biologics, focusing on outcomes measured by psychophysical tests.Recent FindingsBiologics mark a paradigm shift in the management of refractory CRSwNP, offering targeted, effective and safe treatment options. However, their relative impact on psychophysical smell outcomes remains uncertain. With several biologics now approved and real-world evidence emerging, a comprehensive evaluation is needed.SummaryThis is a systematic review and meta-analysis of 8 RCTs (n = 2,024) and an additional review of 20 cohorts (n = 1,685). Among RCTs, biologic treatment produced a moderate to large pooled improvement in psychophysical smell scores (standardized mean difference, [SMD] = 0.72 (95% CI 0.32–1.13), p = 0.0004). The effect differed between drugs (χ² = 23.95, p < 0.0002). Dupilumab showed the largest improvements across four RCTs (SMD = 1.16, 95% CI = 0.35–1.97). In single RCTs, benralizumab showed large effect (1.03, 0.55–1.51), tezepelumab (0.56, 0.46–0.67), telikibart (0.35, 0.11–0.59), and omalizumab (0.27, 0.15–0.40) yielded modest but significant improvements. Mepolizumab showed no effect (0.1330 (95% CI −0.2490–0.5150). Blood-eosinophil level correlated with treatment response (p < 0.001). The cohort studies supported the findings (SMD = 1.20, 95% CI = 0.99–1.42), though most data were derived from studies of dupilumab. Overall, biologics substantially improve psychophysically measured olfactory function in CRSwNP. Future head-to-head trials are warranted to delineate comparative efficacy of smell recovery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11882-025-01235-4.

Introduction

Chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP) is a prevalent inflammatory upper airway disease affecting approximately 1–4% of adults worldwide [1]. Beyond sinonasal inflammation, CRSwNP is closely associated with systemic airway diseases, such as asthma, reflecting the concept of a “global airway” [2–4]. The condition imposes a substantial burden to patients, leading to impaired quality of life, reduced work productivity, high healthcare- and socioeconomic costs driven by recurring symptoms, long-term pharmacotherapy and repeated surgical interventions [5].

Standard management includes intranasal corticosteroids, saline irrigation and intermittent courses of systemic corticosteroids or endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS) [6]. ESS provides long-term benefit in most patients, with around 80% achieving lasting symptom relief after a single extensive procedure. However, up to 20% experience recurrence, requiring revision surgery within five to seven years [7]. This highlights the chronic, relapsing nature of CRSwNP and the need for more durable treatment strategies.

In recent years, biologic therapies targeting type-2 inflammatory pathways have emerged as add-on options for patients with severe or uncontrolled CRSwNP [8]. Monoclonal antibodies directed against interleukins (IL-4, IL-5 and IL-13), IL-5Rα, IgE or thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP) represent a paradigm shift, offering non-surgical alternatives for patients with refractory disease [9]. Their introduction into clinical practice, however, has been accompanied by intense debate regarding their efficacy, cost-effectiveness, reimbursement and integration into treatment guidelines. For healthcare systems, biologics represent a major investment, raising questions about which agents deliver the most clinically meaningful and economically sustainable benefit [10].

Loss of smell stands out as one of the most debilitating symptoms of CRSwNP, and hence is a common outcome in clinical trials [11, 12]. It affects fundamental aspects of daily life, including food enjoyment, hazard detection (e.g., smoke, gas leaks and spoiled food) and psychosocial well-being [13]. Patients consistently rank the restoration of smell among their highest treatment priorities [14]. From a clinical perspective, olfactory recovery is a tangible marker of treatment success, and from a political perspective it is crucial in justifying reimbursement and integration of these expensive drugs into personalised treatment strategies [14].

Despite its importance to patients, smell loss only rarely features among the primary outcomes in clinical trials of biologic therapies, and quantification of smell improvement is often assessed by subjective instruments such as Likert scales, “visual analogue scales” (VAS) or similar. Although such measures have been validated in CRSwNP [15], subjective tests inherently carry with them the risk of recall bias and reproducibility issues [13, 16, 17]. In lack of true, objective tests of olfaction, psychophysical, or “semi-objective” instruments, such as the University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test (UPSIT), Sniffin’ Sticks Threshold, Discrimination and Identification Test (TDI), and T&T olfactometry allow for a more standardised and reproducible assessment of olfactory function [18, 19]. International guidelines such as the European Position Paper on Rhinosinusitis and nasal polyps/European forum for research and education in allergy and airway disease (EPOS/EUFOREA), explicitly recommend the use of such validated tools when evaluating the effect of biologic therapy [17].

Furthermore, study populations and inclusion/treatment criteria are heterogenous in the available literature. Randomised “head-to-head” trials using psychophysical smell measurement instruments are the ideal way to compare smell improvement, but at the time of the literature search for this review, none existed.

In lieu of the fast-emerging body of evidence and heterogenic studies, state-of-the-art syntheses of biologic therapies and their impact on smell improvement with validated measures in patients with CRSwNP are in high demand. A robust evaluation of this outcome has implications not only for clinical decision-making and patient counselling, but also biomarker-guided strategies and health technology assessment. In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we aim to evaluate the effect of biologic therapies on objectively measured smell improvement in patients with CRSwNP.

Methods

This systematic review with meta-analysis was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) 2020 statement guidelines [20]. The protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) on 10 April 2025 (CRD420251030220).

Eligibility Criteria

We included randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies that reported changes in olfactory outcomes measured by psychophysical instruments in adult patients with CRSwNP treated with monoclonal antibodies. Studies were excluded if they contained fewer than five participants; did not employ validated psychophysical olfactory tests or involved animal models. If two or more studies had overlapping study populations – or were deemed in high risk of this - the largest or most complete dataset was retained and the remaining excluded. The primary outcome of interest was mean change in olfactory test score from baseline to the end of follow-up. All values were equally evaluated.

Literature Search Strategy, Study Selection and Data Extraction

A systematic search was conducted in PubMed, including MEDLINE; Embase and the Cochrane Library on 19 April 2025. Articles in English, Danish, Swedish, or Norwegian were eligible. No date restrictions were applied. The search combined controlled vocabulary (MeSH/Emtree terms) and free-text keywords for population, intervention, outcome and study design. The full search strategy is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Study screening was managed in Covidence (covidence.org). Two reviewers (KAS and CvB) independently screened all titles and abstracts. Discrepancies were resolved via discussion or arbitration by a third reviewer (JT). Full texts were assessed by seven reviewers (VB, MH, CKP, CvB, KAS, ASH, and JT), and the data were extracted using a standardised form to ensure consistency. The extracted variables included study design; sample size; treatment type; duration of follow-up; patient characteristics, including comorbid asthma and prior surgery; olfactory test used; blood eosinophil counts and mean nasal polyp score. A separate author (SH) independently double-checked all the extracted data, and any discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussions involving the entire review team.

Certainty of Evidence (GRADE)

The certainty of evidence was assessed using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) approach [21]. The RCT evidence was rated across the domains of risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias and classified as high, moderate, low or very low.

Risk of Bias Assessment

Risk of bias was assessed for all included trials using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB-2) tool [22]. Each study was independently evaluated by two reviewers (VB and SH), and disagreements were resolved by reaching consensus among all authors. Studies were categorized as low, some concerns or high in terms of risk of bias based on RoB-2. Cohort studies were not formally graded for risk of bias, as the RCTs were the primary evidence base for GRADE assessments, and cohort findings are presented as supportive context.

Statistical Analyses

We synthesised effect sizes with an inverse-variance random-effects model, which was chosen a priori to accommodate clinical and methodological diversity across trials. Standardised mean differences (Hedges’ g) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated, and between-study variance (τ²) was estimated using the Sidik–Jonkman method. We also reported 95% prediction intervals and measures of heterogeneity (Q, I² and H). Sensitivity analyses included leave-one-out re-estimation and repeating the meta-analysis after excluding studies identified as outliers or highly influential based on externally studentised residuals and Cook’s distance. Prespecified subgroup analyses compared biologic agents and follow-up duration (≤ 24 versus > 24 weeks), while meta-regression assessed baseline blood eosinophils as a potential effect modifier. Publication bias was evaluated with funnel plots and Egger’s test, and the trim-and-fill method was applied if asymmetry was suggested. All analyses were performed in R Version 4.1.2, with statistical significance set at a two-sided p < 0.05.

Results

Study Selection

The database search identified 571 unique records after the removal of 314 duplicates (See Supplementary Table S1 for search strategy). Following title and abstract screening, 439 records were excluded, leaving 132 full-text reports to be assessed for eligibility. A total of 27 eligible articles were identified, encompassing seven distinct RCTs and 20 cohort studies (Fig. 1). Two articles, Bachert et al. 2019, reporting on SINUS-24 and SINUS-52 [23], and Gevaert et al. 2020, reporting on POLYP-1 and POLYP-2 [24], each included two independent-parallel RCTs, which were treated as separate studies in all analyses. Lastly, a single RCT report was added manually, leaving a total of 10 separate RCT populations.

Study Characteristics

A total of 28 studies were included, comprising eight RCTs (n = 2,024) and 20 observational cohorts (n = 1,685; Tables 1 and S2). Across study types, the participants were adults with CRSwNP, of which the majority had undergone prior sinus surgery and suffered from comorbid asthma. The RCTs enrolled 24–448 patients, with treatment durations of 16–52 -weeks; cohort studies included 10–648 patients, with follow-up from 24–120 weeks. Six biologics were evaluated in the RCTs (Table 1): dupilumab (n = 4), omalizumab (n = 2), mepolizumab (n = 1), benralizumab (n = 1), tezepelumab (n = 1) and telikibart (n = 1) [23–30]. The cohort studies were predominantly dupilumab based (n = 19), with only one study on a different drug (benralizumab) [31–50]. For measuring smell, UPSIT was the most used in the RCTs (nine out of ten trials), with just one trial applying SSIT-16. The cohorts most often used the SSIT (12 or 16 items), with some applying the UPSIT or T&T olfactometry (Table 1).

Participants were generally middle aged, with age ranging from the mid-40s to the late 50 s, and the gender ratio was almost equal (30–60% of participants were female). Asthma prevalence was high (40–100%), and prior surgery was reported in 55–100% of patients. Blood eosinophil counts were typically elevated (0.3–0.9 × 10⁹/L), and mean nasal polyp scores were ≥ 5, which is consistent with severe type-2 inflammation (Supplementary Table S1).

RCTs

Risk of Bias

The risk of bias was assessed using RoB-2, and the results are summarised in Table 2. Seven of the eight included RCTs were judged to have some concerns regarding the risk of bias. One trial, La Mantia et al. (2024) [30], was rated as having high risk of bias; the randomisation process was judged to be at low risk of bias in all studies except La Mantia et al. (2024) [30], which did not describe sequence generation or allocation concealment. All the remaining trials (Gevaert et al., 2020 [24]; Zheng et al., 2025 [26]; Lipworth et al., 2025 [28]; Bachert et al., 2019 [23]; Bachert et al., 2016 [29]; Tversky et al., 2021 [27]; Han et al., 2021 [25] used centralized or computer-generated randomization with adequate concealment. Outcome measurement was consistently judged to be at a low risk of bias. All RCTs assessed olfactory function with validated, psycho-physical instruments—UPSIT or Sniffin’ Sticks—which were used uniformly across groups, typically under assessor-blinding (Table 2).

Effect of Biologics on Olfactory Function

The pooled standardised mean difference (SMD) across all trials was 0.72 (95% CI 0.32–1.12), p = 0.0004, corresponding to a moderate-to-large treatment effect. However, statistical heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 94%), indicating variability across study populations, biologic agents and outcome measures (Fig. 2A). After restricting the analysis to studies using the UPSIT, biologic therapy was associated with a pooled mean improvement of 7.37 points (95% CI = 4.6–10.1), p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2B).

Influence diagnostics identified one study, LaMantia et al. (2024) [30] as having a disproportionate impact on the pooled results (Supplementary Figure S2A). It showed standardised residuals above the conventional threshold (rstudent = 5.24), elevated Cook’s distance (0.38), high Difference in Fits value (2.4) and the largest contribution to heterogeneity in Baujat plot (Q = 101). Exclusion of this outlier reduced heterogeneity and attenuated the effect size, though the association remained statistically significant (SMD = 0.5312 (95% CI 0.3376–0.7248), p < 0.0001) (Supplementary Figure S1). Leave-one-out analyses confirmed robustness, with pooled estimates ranging from 0.48 to 0.72 across iterations (Supplementary Figure S3A).

Subgroup Analysis

By Biologic Agent

The subgroup analysis demonstrated significant differences in smell improvement across biologic agents (χ² = 23.95, p = 0.0002) (Fig. 3). Dupilumab (SMD = 1.16, 95% CI = 0.35–1.97) and benralizumab (SMD = 1.03, 95% CI = 0.55–1.51) produced the largest improvements. Tezepelumab (SMD = 0.56, 95% CI = 0.46–0.67), telikibart (SMD = 0.35, 95% CI = 0.11–0.59) and omalizumab (SMD = 0.27, 95% CI = 0.15–0.40) provided modest but statistically significant benefits, while the effect of mepolizumab was not statistically significant (SMD = 0.13 (95% CI − 0.25–0.52)) (Fig. 3).

By Follow-up Period

Follow-up duration did not affect results significantly, with similar benefits for ≤ 24 weeks (Standard mean difference (SMD) = 0.57 (95% CI 0.3–0.83) and > 24 weeks (SMD = 0.93, 95% CI = − 0.05–1.91) follow-up, with no between-group differences being observed (p = 0.49; Supplementary Figure S4).

By Blood Eosinophils

Meta-regression revealed a positive association between baseline blood eosinophils and treatment effect (slope = 0.0032 per cell/µL; p < 0.001), accounting for ~ 65% of between-study variance (Fig. 4).

Treatment Effect Size: Cohort Studies

The pooled effect size was large (SMD = 1.20, 95% CI = 0.99–1.42), although heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 84.9%; Fig. 5). Outlier adjustment (Tajiri et al., 2024; Corso et al., 2024; Galletti et al., 2023) reduced heterogeneity (I² = 73%) while preserving significance (SMD = 1.23, 95% CI = 1.06–1.40; (Supplementary Figure S1B). Leave-one-out analyses confirmed robustness, with pooled estimates between 1.15 and 1.25 (Supplementary Figure S3B).

Publication Bias

For the RCTs, Egger’s regression test was nonsignificant (p = 0.45), indicating no statistical evidence of publication bias. Similarly, in the cohort studies, Egger’s test showed no signs of publication bias (p = 0.72) (Supplementary Figure S5A and -B).

Certainty of Evidence (GRADE Statement)

Using the GRADE approach, the certainty of evidence from the RCTs was rated as moderate. Randomised trials consistently showed that biologics improve olfactory function when measured using validated instruments, with effect sizes exceeding the minimal clinically important difference. Certainty was downgraded one level for the risk of bias, mainly due to the incomplete handling of missing data and selective reporting. The observational cohorts demonstrated similarly large effects, reinforcing confidence in the overall conclusion.

Discussion

This systematic review with meta-analysis provides a comprehensive synthesis of olfactory outcomes with biologic therapy in CRSwNP patients. Across RCTs, biologics provide a moderate to large, pooled effect and mean UPSIT improvements exceeding the minimal clinically important difference [51]. The subgroup analyses revealed significant effect variability between biologics: dupilumab showed consistent benefit across RCTs; benralizumab produced a large effect in a small RCT; tezepelumab, telikibart and omalizumab provided modest but significant benefits and mepolizumab showed no effect. A meta-regression demonstrated that higher baseline blood eosinophil counts predicted greater improvement, supporting the mechanistic role of type-2 inflammation in olfactory dysfunction.

The cohort studies, which almost exclusively concerned dupilumab, showed even larger effect sizes than the RCTs for dupilumab. While these findings suggest that olfactory recovery may be more pronounced in real-world clinical practice, they should be interpreted with caution. The absence of control groups increases the risk of overestimating true effects because of the placebo response, regression to the mean or selection bias. Longer follow-up and broader inclusion criteria may also contribute to the differences between the results. Nevertheless, our sensitivity analyses confirmed that the results across both RCTs and cohorts were robust, strengthening confidence in the overall conclusion that biologics improve olfactory function in CRSwNP patients.

Studies often emphasize endpoints such as nasal polyp size, symptom scores or quality of life [52], while olfaction is treated as a secondary outcome. By restricting the present analyse to validated psychophysical instruments, we fill a critical evidence gap and provide a rigorous comparative evaluation of biologics regarding this patient-centred outcome. We chose this restrictive approach due to psychophysical tests being reproducible and having a stronger test-retest reliability, compared to subjective tools [18]. Our findings refine the therapeutic hierarchy. In line with similar studies before ours [53, 54] dupilumab emerges as the biologic with the most consistent, clinically meaningful efficacy in terms of improving olfaction, corroborating the results of the Liberty study [23]. Tezepelumab also demonstrated a rapid and clinically significant benefit, supporting the role of epithelial cytokines such as Thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP) in olfactory impairment [28]. The results seem promising, but there is still a lack of real-world data to support the findings. Omalizumab and telikibart produced significant improvements, although smaller than dupilumab and tezepelumab. Benralizumab showed large effects in a single small RCT. However, this was not supported in a larger phase III study (OSTRO) (which was not included in our study because specific UPSIT values were not reported) [55]. Similarly, a real-life cohort study from Italy with 24 participants and 40 months follow-up failed to find a smell improvement by benralizumab [44]). These conflicting results highlight the need for further clarification of the potential of benralizumab. Mepolizumab did not improve olfaction in a single RCT where only a subgroup (n = 54) was assessed with psychophysical tests [25]. Studies, however, have suggested some benefit of mepolizumab when subjective ratings are applied [25, 56]. Furthermore, a recently published RCT of surgery plus mepolizumab versus mepolizumab alone showed a statistically significant improvement of 1 point on the SSIT-16 test for the non-surgery arm after 24 weeks of treatment [56].

Randomised head-to-head trials are the ideal way to compare smell improvement ability between biologics, but at the time of the literature search for this review, none existed. The much anticipated EVEREST trial, which compares omalizumab to dupilumab, was published after we carried out our literature search and could therefore not be included in the analysis. EVEREST found that dupilumab had an 8-point greater UPSIT improvement versus omalizumab (p < 0.0001) [57]. Another interesting head-to-head trial expected in the near future is the Danish TORNADO-study (NCT05942222) which is an investigator-initiated, open-label, study comparing dupilumab to mepolizumab.

Our meta-regression strengthens the mechanistic link between eosinophils and smell loss, demonstrating that blood eosinophilia predicts greater olfactory changes, and that it explains two-thirds of between-study variance (Fig. 4). Eosinophils may therefore serve as a biomarker with which to stratify patients and guide biologic selection, although prospective validation is required. Clinical history also modifies outcomes; patients without prior ESS may achieve greater recovery due to less irreversible epithelial or neural damage, whereas repeated ESS can introduce iatrogenic injury that biologics cannot reverse [58, 59]. Chronic anosmia itself may represent a ‘point of no return’, suggesting that biologics are most effective within a given time window before neural pathways are permanently remodelled [60]. This emphasises the importance of timely intervention and the prospective evaluation of treatment timing.

Olfactory improvements were detectable within the first 24 weeks, but did not seem to increase further with longer follow-up (Supplementary Figure S4). This result, however, should be interpreted with caution as substantial heterogeneity and treatment strategies are present in the studies.

Strengths of this review are that it was prospectively registered; conducted with comprehensive searches and restricted to studies using validated, objective olfactory instruments, thereby minimising bias due to subjective assessments. Both RCTs and real-world cohorts were included, providing complementary perspectives on efficacy. Advanced statistical approaches, including outlier diagnostics, influence analyses and meta-regression, confirmed the robustness of the findings.

Nonetheless, some limitations must be acknowledged. The risk of bias was present in most RCTs: seven of eight were rated as having ‘some concerns’, and one (La Mantia et al., 2024) [30] was rated as high risk based on RoB-2, largely due to missing outcome data and selective reporting. The certainty of the evidence was therefore graded as moderate according to GRADE, after downgrading one level due to the risk of bias. Considerable heterogeneity was observed across studies, reflecting variation in populations, biologic agents and outcome measures. The various smell tests used (UPSIT, Sniffin’ Sticks and T&T) may not be fully interchangeable, although analyses restricted to UPSIT confirmed a clinically meaningful benefit. The cohort evidence was overwhelmingly weighed towards dupilumab, restricting conclusions about other biologics, and most trials had a follow-up period of ≤ 52 weeks, leaving conclusions about durability uncertain. Finally, although publication bias was not detected, the number of trials per agent was small, and selective reporting cannot be excluded. We have not found the mepolizumab study to be of poorer quality than the dupilumab studies but based on data available from the study that met our criteria, mepolizumab showed no effect. However, findings from subgroup divisions based on a single study is insufficient to make conclusions and the results should be interpreted very cautiously.

Reduced olfaction is consistently rated one of the most debilitating symptoms by CRSwNP patients [13], and its restoration should in accordance with guidelines be prioritised as a co-primary endpoint in biologic trials [17], alongside nasal polyp burden and quality of life. Our findings strongly support dupilumab as the reference biologic for CRSwNP due to its consistent and clinically meaningful efficacy in terms of improving smell. Mepolizumab’s seemingly small effect indicates that biologics cannot be considered interchangeable in terms of this outcome - a distinction with direct implications for reimbursement strategies and health economy prioritization. The observed eosinophil–response relationship highlights biomarker-guided precision medicine as a promising avenue for future research, though clinical implementation will require validation.

At the same time, key evidence gaps demand attention. Tezepelumab and telikibart show promising effect but is supported by only few trials. Benralizumab’s apparent benefit from a small RCT was not confirmed in a larger study (OSTRO [55]),. Dedicated, adequately powered RCTs - preferably head-to-head trials - are needed to establish the efficacy of these agents. Furthermore, real-world studies using psychophysical smell tests are dominated by dupilumab treatment, leaving the broader biologic landscape underexplored. Future research should move beyond symptom and polyp reduction to adopt standardised objective olfactory testing and extend follow-up beyond one year. This shows that identification tests are by far the most commonly used. Identification tests, however, measure aspects of smell, that are not limited to olfactory ability alone, but cognitive recognition as well. Critics of identification tests suggests the use of threshold tests instead as they offer a cleaner measure of smell. The olfactory threshold (T), discrimination (D), and identification test (I) (TDI) [61]. The TDI test is more sensitive for changes happening in the early stages of recovery from CRSwNP treated with biologic drugs but is more time consuming. More research into this subject is needed.

It is important to highlight that we solely included studies that used standardized psychophysical smell tests (e.g., UPSIT, Sniffin’ Sticks, T&T) in our meta-analysis. These tests – although far from perfect - are regarded the golden standard for evaluating the sense of smell. Subjective measures like Likert ratings and visual analogue scales (VAS) may overestimate the extent of recovery because they are based solely on self-perception. Nonetheless, patients’ perceived improvement should not be disregarded, as self-reported smell recovery represents a clinically meaningful outcome reflecting real-life function and quality of life. Moreover, subjectively noticeable improvement in threshold may precede measurable gains in identification-based tests [62], which may have been missed in this analysis where the majority were identification tests. Consequently, the improvements reported here should be interpreted within the context of quantitative smell testing (of identification), while recognizing that both psychophysical and subjective assessments provide complementary perspectives on olfactory recovery.

Conclusion

Biologic therapy significantly improves olfactory function assessed by psychophysical tests in patients with severe CRSwNP - often with effects that exceed the minimal clinically important difference. In this analysis, dupilumab provides the most consistent and clinically meaningful benefit. Tezepelumab and telikibart demonstrate potential but require confirmation in large, high-quality trials. The potential of benralizumab remains uncertain. Mepolizumab demonstrates no clinically significant smell improvement, but it cannot be ruled out due to limited data. Treatment efficacy is greater in patients with elevated blood eosinophil counts, supporting the development of biomarker-guided strategies. The restoration of olfaction should be recognised as a central outcome of biologic therapy in CRSwNP patients and integrated into both clinical practice and guideline development.
