Determinants of healthcare workers’ attitudes towards Marburg virus disease outbreak response in Northwest Ethiopia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Melaku Laikemariam, Endalamaw Fentie, Abebe Yenesew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealthcare workers’ professional attitudes are critical for effective outbreak response. Understanding the drivers of these attitudes is essential for strengthening epidemic resilience, particularly for high-threat pathogens like the Marburg virus.ObjectiveTo identify the determinants of healthcare workers’ attitudes toward the Marburg virus disease outbreak response in Northwest Ethiopia.MethodsA facility-based cross-sectional study design was conducted from November 30 to December 30, 2025, across three public hospitals in the Awi Zone. Data were collected using a pretested, self-administered questionnaire. Attitudes were measured with an 18-item Likert scale; scores above the mean (>65.6) indicated positive attitudes. Binary logistic regression was used to identify determinants, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsAmong 413 healthcare workers (response rate 97.6%), 55.2% exhibited positive attitudes toward Marburg virus disease. Determinants of positive attitudes included male gender (AOR = 2.15, 95% CI: 1.34, 3.45), master of science and above education (AOR = 2.73, 95% CI: 1.48, 5.06), access to disease information (AOR = 1.67, 95% CI: 1.07, 2.62), and good knowledge of Marburg virus disease (AOR = 2.04, 95% CI: 1.36, 3.28).ConclusionWhile 55.2% of healthcare workers demonstrated positive attitudes, a substantial minority (44.8%) held negative attitudes. Significant determinants included male gender, higher education, access to disease information, and good knowledge. Tailored interventions improving knowledge, ensuring equitable information access, and promoting higher education are recommended.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27885-1.Keywords: Attitudes, Healthcare workers, Marburg virus disease, Outbreak response, Ethiopia

Introduction

The global landscape of infectious disease threats is continuously evolving, with high-consequence infectious diseases representing a persistent challenge to public health security [1]. Among these, Marburg Virus Disease (MVD), a severe and often fatal viral hemorrhagic fever caused by the Marburg virus (MARV) of the Filoviridae family, stands out due to its high epidemic potential, significant case fatality rates, and the absence of licensed vaccines or specific antiviral treatments [2–4]. First identified in 1967 during outbreaks in Germany and Serbia linked to imported monkeys from Uganda, MARV has since caused sporadic but devastating outbreaks, predominantly in sub-Saharan Africa [5–7]. The Egyptian fruit bat (Rousettus aegyptiacus) is recognized as the primary natural reservoir, with human spillover occurring through direct or indirect contact with infected bats or their excreta, consumption of infected bushmeat, or through contact with infected patients in the community [3, 8, 9]. MVD poses a substantial and escalating public health risk, with ten outbreaks in sub-Saharan Africa from 2004 to January 2025 resulting in 516 confirmed cases and 398 deaths, underscoring its lethal potential and recurrent nature [10].

The clinical presentation of Marburg virus disease is characterized by an abrupt onset of high fever, severe headache, myalgia, and malaise, progressing to gastrointestinal symptoms, hemorrhagic manifestations, and multi-organ failure, with death often occurring 8–9 days after symptom onset [7, 11, 12]. The average case fatality ratio (CFR) is approximately 50%, with documented rates ranging from 24% to as high as 88–90% depending on the viral strain, quality of supportive care, and timeliness of intervention [2, 3, 6].

Human-to-human transmission occurs via direct contact with blood, secretions, organs, or other bodily fluids of infected individuals or contaminated surfaces, placing healthcare workers (HCWs) and caregivers at exceptionally high occupational risk during outbreaks [8, 13, 14]. Recent outbreaks in Equatorial Guinea (2023), Tanzania (2023), Rwanda (2024), and notably, the 2025 outbreak in the South Region of Ethiopia, highlight the virus’s capacity to cross borders and expose critical gaps in preparedness in resource-limited health systems [7, 10, 15, 16].

Ethiopia, situated in a region identified as at high risk for viral hemorrhagic fever emergence, confronts a dual challenge: the direct threat of MVD and the strain of a healthcare system still recovering from the COVID-19 pandemic [14, 16]. The swift containment of the 2025 Ethiopian outbreak, while commendable, serves as a stark reminder of the persistent need for sustainable, system-wide preparedness that includes not only material resources but also a psychologically resilient frontline workforce [10].

Healthcare workers’ effectiveness during outbreaks depends not only on their technical knowledge but fundamentally on their professional attitudes encompassing risk perception, confidence in institutional support, willingness to respond, and sense of self-efficacy [13, 17]. Positive attitudes can enhance adherence to infection prevention protocols, improve patient care during crises, and sustain workforce morale. Conversely, fear, perceived lack of support, and negative risk appraisal can lead to absenteeism, burnout, and compromised infection control, potentially amplifying transmission [18, 19]. Despite their critical importance, studies specifically examining healthcare workers attitudes toward Marburg virus disease remain scarce and are often embedded within broader knowledge-attitude-practice (KAP) surveys, offering limited depth on the specific drivers of outbreak response behavior [18, 20, 21].

The Ethiopian context presents particular challenges. While one study on general hospital disaster preparedness reported fair awareness among HCWs, it also found critically low practice levels and a lack of formal disaster plans, highlighting a concerning gap between knowledge and actionable readiness [21]. Existing literature consistently identifies key modifiable factors influencing knowledge and attitude outcomes, including access to targeted IPC training, availability of information, level of education, prior outbreak experience (COVID-19), and professional role [22–25]. A structured educational intervention in Egypt demonstrated a dramatic increase in nurses’ knowledge and positive attitudes toward Marburg virus disease, underscoring the transformative potential of targeted training [26]. Furthermore, a multi-country analysis in Sub-Saharan Africa identified significant predictors of good knowledge and attitudes toward Marburg virus disease, including information access, IPC training, and higher education [25]. These findings align with broader disaster management literature emphasizing that exposure to training significantly enhances perceived preparedness and willingness to respond [17, 27].

However, three specific evidence gaps persist. First, while numerous KAP studies on viral hemorrhagic fevers exist, most treat attitudes as a descriptive outcome; none of the conducted evidence identified independent determinants of MVD-specific attitudes among healthcare workers [18–20]. Second, no study has assessed MVD-related attitudes among Ethiopian HCWs following the 2025 outbreak, the only exception being a general disaster preparedness survey that did not address filovirus-specific attitudes [21]. Third, no study has quantified the independent effects of modifiable factors (e.g., information access, IPC training, education level) on HCW attitudes; a controlled trial in Egypt demonstrated that training improves attitudes but did not analyze which baseline characteristics predicted positive attitudes [26]. Consequently, three actionable knowledge gaps remain: (i) the prevalence of positive attitudes toward MVD outbreak response among Ethiopian HCWs is unknown; (ii) the independent predictors of these attitudes have never been identified; and (iii) the relative contribution of knowledge, information access, and demographic factors remains unquantified. This analytical gap limits the development of targeted interventions.

Therefore, this study aimed to assess the level of attitudes and to identify the determinants of healthcare workers’ attitudes toward MVD outbreak response in Northwest Ethiopia. The findings are intended to inform targeted interventions to strengthen frontline psychological resilience and health system preparedness.

Methods and materials

Study design, period, and setting

A facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted from November 30 to December 30, 2025, in public hospitals located in the Awi Zone of the Amhara Regional State, northwest Ethiopia. The zone’s capital, Injibara town, is situated approximately 114 km from Bahir Dar (the regional capital) and 445.9 km from Addis Ababa (the national capital). The zone comprises five public hospitals and forty-seven health centers, serving an estimated population of 1,077,144. Although the 2025 Marburg virus disease (MVD) outbreak was officially reported in the South Ethiopia Region, the Awi Zone was purposively selected for this study for three strategic reasons. First, as a major transit and economic corridor in the Amhara region, Awi Zone’s connectivity to larger cities (Bahir Dar and Addis Ababa) means that any imported case could lead to rapid regional dissemination; therefore, proactive assessment of healthcare worker (HCW) preparedness in such high-traffic areas is a national priority for preventing secondary outbreaks. Second, the zone’s public hospitals serve a predominantly rural and resource-limited population, providing a representative setting for understanding HCW readiness in typical low-income Ethiopian contexts; where health system constraints most closely mirror those of the original outbreak epicenter. Third, no prior study has assessed MVD-specific knowledge, attitudes, or determinants among HCWs in Northwest Ethiopia, creating an evidence gap that this study fills by establishing a baseline for a high-risk, previously unstudied region.

Study population and eligibility criteria

The source population included all healthcare workers employed at the selected public hospitals in the Awi Zone during the data collection period. The study population consisted of systematically selected clinically active healthcare workers who provide direct patient care, including physicians, nurses, midwives, pharmacists, and laboratory technicians from the same settings. Healthcare workers were included if they were clinically active, available at their respective hospitals during the study period, and provided written informed consent. Participants were excluded if they were on annual, sick, or study leave; not involved in clinical duties during data collection; or critically ill and unable to complete the questionnaire.

Non-clinical but high-risk personnel (e.g., janitors, cleaners, laundry workers, waste handlers, porter`s) were not included in this study for three specific reasons. First, the 18-item attitude scale was designed and pretested specifically for clinical decision-makers; items assess willingness to identify symptoms, handle suspected cases, order diagnostic tests, report suspect cases, and make treatment decisions, tasks that fall outside the scope of non-clinical support roles. Using this instrument with non-clinical staff would yield invalid or uninterpretable responses. Second, the primary study objective was to identify determinants of attitudes among frontline HCWs who bear direct responsibility for patient assessment, diagnosis, and outbreak response decisions. Third, non-clinical support staff have distinct exposure pathways, training needs, and psychosocial concerns that warrant a separate, tailored investigation using appropriately adapted instruments.

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

The sample size was calculated using a single population proportion formula, assuming a 50% proportion of good knowledge (due to the absence of prior similar studies in the region), a 95% confidence level (Zα/2 = 1.96), a 5% margin of error, and a 10% non-response rate. The initial calculation yielded a sample size of 384. After accounting for the non-response rate, the final sample size was 423 healthcare workers.

There are five public hospitals in the Awi Zone: Chagni Primary Hospital (CPH), Dangla Primary Hospital (DPH), Gemjabet Primary Hospital (GPH), Jawi Primary Hospital (JPH), and Injibara General Hospital (IGH). Three hospitals (CPH, GPH, and DPH) were selected through a lottery method. Using the profile of active healthcare workers in each hospital, the sample size was proportionally allocated to the randomly selected hospitals. A systematic random sampling technique was employed to select study participants from the payroll of eligible healthcare workers at each hospital, using a sampling interval determined by the total number of staff and the required sample size per facility (approximately 2 for all hospitals). Variables

Attitudes toward the Marburg virus disease (MVD) outbreak response were the dependent variable. Independent variables included sociodemographic characteristics (age, gender, education level, profession, and years of clinical experience), exposure factors (access to MVD information and recent IPC training), and knowledge of MVD, which were included as predictors to examine their influence on attitudinal outcomes.

Operational definitions

Knowledge classification: Knowledge was assessed using 20 binary (Yes/No) items covering MVD etiology, transmission, clinical features, and prevention.

Response options for each item: “Yes” (correct) or “No” (incorrect).

Scoring: Each correct answer was assigned 1 point; each incorrect answer was assigned 0 points. No reverse scoring was applied, as all items were positively phrased (i.e., the correct answer is always “Yes” for factual statements; however, item Q7, “Marburg virus can spread through airborne droplets” is a false statement, so the correct answer is “No” – but this is still a binary correct/incorrect classification without reverse scoring of the Likert type).

Score interpretation: The total knowledge score ranged from 0 (minimum) to 20 (maximum).

The mean score of the study population was 11.0. Participants scoring above the mean (> 11) were classified as having good knowledge; those scoring ≤ 11 were classified as having poor knowledge. This mean-based dichotomization aligns with established KAP methodology and facilitates regression analysis [18–20, 28].

Attitudes classification: Attitudes were measured using 18 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale. Response options for each item: 1 = Strongly Disagree, 2 = Disagree, 3 = Neutral, 4 = Agree, 5 = Strongly Agree.

Scoring: Each item was assigned a numeric value from 1 to 5 corresponding to the response selected.

Reverse scoring: Three of the 18 items were negatively phrased (items 6, 7, and 14: ‘Feel angry and afraid if I get exposed to the virus,’ ‘Uncomfortable to work in a hospital during an outbreak,’ and ‘Traditional medicines are good for treating MVD’). For these items, responses were reverse-scored (1→5, 2→4, 3→3, 4→2, 5→1) so that higher scores consistently reflected more positive attitudes. The remaining 15 items were scored directly.

Total score range: After reverse scoring, individual item scores were summed to produce a total attitude score ranging from 18 (minimum, most negative attitude) to 90 (maximum, most positive attitude).

The mean attitude score of the study population was 65.6. Participants scoring above the mean (> 65.6) were classified as having positive attitudes; those scoring ≤ 65.6 were classified as having negative attitudes. This mean-based dichotomization is methodologically consistent with prior KAP studies [18–20, 28].

Access to MVD information: Measured by a single binary question: “Have you had access to MVD-specific information from any source?” (Yes/No).

Data collection tool and procedure

A structured, self-administered questionnaire was developed for this study through a comprehensive review of relevant literature, including WHO guidelines and prior knowledge, and attitudes studies on Marburg virus disease [8, 18–21, 25, 28–30]. The questionnaire comprised three core sections. First, it captured sociodemographic and exposure variables, including age, gender, education level, profession, years of experience, access to MVD information, history of COVID-19 infection, and recent IPC training. Second, it included a knowledge assessment consisting of 20 binary (Yes/No) items covering MVD etiology, transmission, clinical features, and prevention; this score was used as a potential explanatory variable in subsequent analyses. Third, the instrument included an attitude assessment with 18 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). The complete English version of the questionnaire is available as Supplementary File 1.

The questionnaire demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha exceeding 0.90 for both attitude and knowledge scales. Six trained data collectors (three health officers and three general practitioners) administered the instrument under the supervision of the principal investigators. Participants received a brief orientation before completion to ensure clarity and comprehension of all questionnaire items.

Data quality assurance

The questionnaire was pretested on 5% of the sample size (22 healthcare workers) at Injibara General Hospital to ensure clarity, consistency, validity, and cultural appropriateness. Cronbach’s alpha was calculated to confirm the internal consistency of both knowledge and attitude scales. Data collectors received two days of training on study objectives, consent procedures, and ethical considerations. Daily supervision and spot-checking were conducted throughout data collection to ensure completeness and accuracy. All questionnaires were reviewed for consistency prior to data entry.

Data processing and analysis

Data were entered into Epidata version 4.6 and exported to SPSS version 26 for analysis. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, were used to summarize participant characteristics and attitude responses. Binary logistic regression was employed to identify determinants of healthcare workers attitudes toward MVD outbreak response. Variables with a p-value ≤ 0.25 in bivariable analysis were included in the final multivariable model. Adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Model fitness was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. Only results from the final adjusted models are presented in the main text; complete bivariable results are available as supplementary material 2.

Result

Sociodemographic and exposure characteristics

A total of 413 healthcare workers participated in the study, yielding a response rate of 97.6%. The mean age of participants was 36.7 (± 8.83) years. The majority were male (299, 72.4%) and held a Bachelor’s degree (189, 45.8%) as their highest level of education. The most common profession was nurse (138, 33.4%). Less than half of the participants (193, 46.7%) had received Infection Prevention and Control (IPC) training in the year preceding the study, while more than half (245, 59.3%) reported having access to MVD-specific information. A minority (100, 24.2%) reported a prior history of COVID-19 infection (Table 1).

Level of knowledge regarding Marburg virus disease

The overall prevalence of good knowledge about MVD was 37.5% (95% CI: 33, 42). A majority correctly identified the causative agent as a virus (71.7%) and recognized the preventive importance of personal protective equipment (67.1%). However, knowledge was notably deficient in several key areas: less than half could identify the primary reservoir (47.2%), the incubation period (44.3%), or the main treatment approach (42.6%). Attitudes toward Marburg virus disease outbreak response

The prevalence of healthcare workers positive attitudes toward MVD outbreak response was 55.2% (95% CI: 50, 60). High levels of professional commitment were observed: 77.0% were willing to participate in MVD-specific training, 75.3% felt ready to handle a suspected case, and 76.0% believed healthcare workers were adequately trained for MVD management. Most participants (77.7%) recognized MVD as a significant public health threat, and 55.4% acknowledged their own high occupational risk. However, only 55.7% agreed that their institution provided sufficient support for outbreak management (Table 3).

Determinants of attitudes toward Marburg virus disease outbreak response

The final multivariable logistic regression model elucidated clear, actionable determinants of healthcare workers attitudes toward MVD outbreak response. Four key factors emerged as significant, independent predictors. Foremost, gender strongly influenced attitude, with male healthcare workers demonstrating more than twice the odds (AOR = 2.15, 95% CI: 1.34, 3.45) of holding positive attitudes compared to their female counterparts. Educational attainment was another powerful determinant; healthcare workers with a Master’s degree or higher had nearly threefold greater odds (AOR = 2.73, 95% CI: 1.48, 5.06) of positive attitudes compared to those with a diploma. Access to information played a crucial moderating role, as healthcare workers who reported access to MVD information were significantly more likely (AOR = 1.67, 95% CI: 1.07, 2.62) to exhibit positive professional outlooks. Most pivotally, the analysis revealed a strong, interdependent relationship between cognition and affect: possession of good knowledge of MVD independently doubled the odds (AOR = 2.04, 95% CI: 1.36, 3.28) of a positive attitude. This underscores that knowledge serves not merely as a foundation of competence but as a critical catalyst for confidence and professional commitment during health emergencies (Table 4).

Discussion

This study assessed the spectrum of attitudes toward MVD outbreak response. The findings reveal that a majority (55.2%) of healthcare workers demonstrated positive professional commitment and willingness to respond, yet a substantial minority (44.8%) harbored significant concerns. The identification of four key, modifiable determinants: gender, education, information access, and knowledge, offers a clear roadmap for building a more resilient and confident frontline capable of mitigating the profound human and systemic costs of filovirus outbreaks.

This study found a moderate prevalence of positive attitudes (55.2%) toward the Marburg virus disease outbreak response among healthcare workers in Northwest Ethiopia. This rate exceeds that reported in Egypt [26], which may reflect a stronger baseline sense of professional duty or institutional support in our setting. However, it falls below findings from India [18], Indonesia [31], Uganda [19], and the other Ethiopian study [21].

Several factors may explain these discrepancies. The higher positive attitude prevalence reported in India [18] and Indonesia [31] may reflect those studies being conducted during active viral hemorrhagic fever outbreaks, a context that can temporarily heighten HCW risk perception and willingness to respond. In contrast, our lower rate compared to those settings could be due to differences in outbreak proximity, as our data were collected shortly after a contained outbreak rather than during an active one. The higher rate reported from Uganda [19] may be attributed to that country’s repeated exposure to filovirus outbreaks (Ebola and Marburg), which may have built cumulative psychological readiness over time, a learning effect not yet established in Ethiopia. Our higher rate compared to Egypt [26] may reflect stronger baseline professional duty culture or institutional support structures in Ethiopian hospitals, or differences in measurement instruments (Egypt used a shorter scale). The variation from the previous Ethiopian study [21] likely stems from different outcome measures: Habte et al. assessed general disaster preparedness (not MVD-specific) and found low practice levels, whereas our study focused specifically on attitudes toward MVD outbreak response during a post-outbreak period. Additionally, differences in sampling methods, timing relative to outbreaks, and cultural contexts may contribute. Direct cross-study comparisons should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

The persistence of negative attitudes in nearly half of HCWs signals a critical vulnerability. As evidenced during the West African Ebola epidemic, fear and perceived lack of support can lead to absenteeism, non-adherence to protocols, and attrition, ultimately crippling outbreak response and increasing nosocomial transmission [13, 32]. Therefore, our findings should not be interpreted as a majority being “sufficiently” prepared, but rather as an urgent indicator that nearly half of the frontline force may be psychologically underprepared for a high-consequence event.

Regarding the determining factors, this study revealed that marked gender-based disparity in attitudes, with male HCWs exhibiting more than double the odds of positivity, is a profound and troubling finding that echoes patterns observed in outbreak responses in India [18] and Guinea [32]. This disparity likely stems from a complex interplay of factors beyond biology. Sociocultural norms in many settings, including Ethiopia, often place disproportionate caregiving and domestic responsibilities on women, potentially heightening their perceived risk of transmitting infection to family [28, 30]. Interventions must therefore move beyond gender-blind training to include gender-sensitive risk communication, targeted support for childcare and family safety concerns, and active promotion of women into outbreak leadership and decision-making roles.

The powerful influence of higher education as a determinant underscores that psychological preparedness is, in part, a function of professional empowerment. HCWs with master-level education demonstrated nearly threefold greater odds of positive attitudes. This aligns with evidence from Myanmar [23], Sub-Saharan Africa [25], and Uganda [19]. Advanced education likely cultivates not just deeper knowledge, but also critical thinking, systems-level understanding, and greater self-efficacy in navigating complex emergencies [19, 23]. This finding challenges the notion that frontline readiness is achieved solely through task-oriented, protocol-based training. Instead, it advocates for investing in continuous professional development that builds conceptual mastery and leadership capacity and creating pathways for career advancement and specialized training in epidemic preparedness [25].

Perhaps the most actionable finding is the significant role of information access. HCWs who reported access to MVD information were 1.67 times more likely to hold positive attitudes. In the chaotic information environment of an outbreak, where misinformation spreads rapidly and fuels fear, timely, transparent, and authoritative communication from institutional leadership is a cornerstone of psychological safety [24, 26]. The fact that Health facilities must institutionalize clear, multi-channel communication plans for outbreaks, regular briefings, accessible guideline repositories, and open forums to dispel uncertainty and build trust. This information management is as crucial as stockpiling PPE [8, 10].

Finally, our analysis confirms a vital, reinforcing loop between knowledge and attitude. Good knowledge and understanding of MVD independently doubled the odds of a positive attitude. This aligns with the Health Belief Model and social cognitive theory, which posit that knowledge reduces perceived barriers and increases perceived benefits of recommended actions, thereby shaping positive intentions [20, 25]. They are direct drivers of fear and potentially dangerous practices. This underscores the non-negotiable need for foundational, repetitive, and misconception-targeted education as the bedrock of any attitude-shaping intervention. Training programs must explicitly address and correct these lethal inaccuracies to build a foundation of confidence upon which positive attitudes can flourish [27, 29].

The findings of this study carry significant implications for strengthening epidemic preparedness in Ethiopia and similar resource-limited settings. First, they underscore the necessity of moving beyond purely technical training to implement integrated psychological and educational support programs for healthcare workers. This includes developing gender-sensitive communication strategies to address the confidence gap among female HCWs, and establishing institutionalized channels for timely, transparent information dissemination to combat misinformation and build trust. Second, hospital leadership must recognize that knowledge correction is foundational to attitude formation; therefore, routine, simulation-based training must explicitly target and correct dangerous misconceptions, such as the prevalent belief in airborne transmission of MVD. Third, policymakers should formalize the role of peer-support networks, leveraging HCWs with positive attitudes and accurate knowledge as on-site trainers and resilience champions. Finally, these results advocate for the inclusion of HCW mental well-being and attitudinal readiness as key metrics in national emergency preparedness frameworks, ensuring that workforce sustainability is prioritized alongside the stockpiling of physical resources. Ultimately, investing in the determinants of positive attitude is not merely supportive; it is a strategic imperative for building a frontline defense that is both willing and psychologically equipped to contain high-consequence outbreaks.

Recommendations

To address the four key determinants (gender, education, information access, and knowledge), we propose the following actions. For gender disparities, we recommend gender-sensitive risk communication, flexible shifts and childcare support for female HCWs, and active promotion of women into outbreak leadership roles. For education, we recommend scholarship pathways from diploma to MSc focused on epidemic preparedness and integration of MVD modules into pre-service curricula. For information access, we recommend multi-channel communication plans (briefings, SMS, fact sheets in local languages), designated information officers, and active correction of misconceptions such as airborne transmission.

For knowledge strengthening, we recommend mandatory, simulation-based MVD training annually with pre-/post-assessments, peer-support networks of knowledgeable HCWs, and micro-learning sessions to reinforce key facts. At the system level, we recommend including HCW attitudinal readiness as a performance metric in national preparedness dashboards, funding dedicated research on non-clinical HCWs (janitors, cleaners, waste handlers), and conducting regular outbreak simulation drills involving all staff.

Limitations and strengths

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; associations between determinants and attitudes should not be interpreted as causal. Second, self-reported attitudes may be subject to social desirability bias, where participants may over-report positive attitudes due to perceived professional expectations. Third, the study focused exclusively on three public hospitals in the Awi Zone of Northwest Ethiopia, which may limit generalizability to other regions, private facilities, or rural health posts. Fourth, our study excluded non-clinical but high-risk healthcare workers (e.g., janitors, cleaners, laundry staff, and waste handlers). These personnel face substantial occupational risk during outbreaks, and their exclusion limits the generalizability of our findings to the full hospital workforce. Fifth, the study did not assess practice behaviors related to MVD outbreak response; the objectives were limited to knowledge and attitudes. Future research should specifically assess non-clinical HCWs using adapted instruments and examine the knowledge–attitude–practice continuum.

Despite these limitations, the study has notable strengths. First, the sample size (413 participants) with a high response rate (97.6%) enhances statistical power and minimizes non-response bias. Second, the questionnaire demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha > 0.90 for both knowledge and attitude scales), supporting the reliability of measurements. Third, the use of a validated framework and mean-based dichotomization aligns with established KAP methodology, facilitating comparability with future studies. Fourth, the timing of data collection immediately following the 2025 Ethiopian MVD outbreak provides a uniquely relevant and timely snapshot of frontline healthcare worker psychology during a post-outbreak period—a context rarely captured in the literature. Fifth, the explicit focus on attitudes as a primary outcome, rather than subsuming it within broader KAP surveys, fills a significant evidence gap and offers actionable insights for psychological preparedness interventions.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that healthcare workers’ attitudes toward the Marburg virus disease outbreak response are not uniformly positive and are significantly associated with modifiable factors. While over half (55.2%) of HCWs exhibited professional commitment and willingness to respond, a substantial minority (44.8%) harbored negative attitudes, indicating persistent attitudinal vulnerabilities. Significant determinants included male gender, higher education (MSc and above), access to MVD information, and good knowledge of the disease. The strong positive association between good knowledge and favorable attitudes suggests that cognitive understanding may be an important contributor to psychological readiness, though causality cannot be inferred from this cross-sectional design. These findings imply that effective outbreak response likely depends on both the psychological preparedness of the frontline workforce and material resources. Targeted interventions addressing the identified determinants, particularly improving knowledge, ensuring equitable information access, and promoting higher education, are recommended to enhance healthcare workers’ psychological readiness. Such efforts could strengthen epidemic preparedness in high-risk regions like Northwest Ethiopia.
