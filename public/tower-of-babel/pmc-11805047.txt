ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:This study aimed to examine the iodine concentration in the food items consumed by mothers participating in neonatal screening programs in both areas of the Vidarbha region. If the mother is iodine-deficient, the child will also lack this essential nutrient. Severe deficiency in the mother can lead to significant stunting of the child’s brain and body, potentially preventing normal walking, talking, or cognitive function. Even if the mother’s deficiency is mild, the child can still be adversely affected, even if they appear healthy at first glance. The detrimental effects on brain development may manifest later in life as poor academic performance and difficulties in managing daily tasks.Material and Method:The present cross-sectional research will be performed in collaboration with the Departments of Biochemistry, Obstetrics and Gynecology, Community Medicine, and Pediatrics at Jawaharlal Nehru Medical College Sawangi (Meghe) Wardha, along with Datta Meghe Institute of Medical Science (Deemed University) in Maharashtra, India. Mothers who gave birth agreed to participate by providing written consent for the study.Results:The average iodine concentrations are quite similar across all sources, though hand pumps show slightly more variability. Different staple foods contain varying levels of iodine, with some foods (like pulses) exhibiting a lot of variation, while others (like rice and potatoes) are more consistent in their iodine content.Conclusion:The current study is a hospital-based investigation aimed at screening neonates for congenital hypothyroidism (CH) and does not reflect the broader population. To identify CH, screening of neonates will be conducted within the study area, providing a representative view of the condition. Our findings suggest that the prevalence of iodine reflects dietary quality, educational background, and socioeconomic status, all of which can influence growth and development. There are various factors contributing to CH and inadequate fetal growth beyond low iodine levels, which can disrupt thyroid function. The study indicates that iodine levels are indicative of dietary quality as well as educational and social statuses, potentially impacting growth and development through both dietary deficiencies and other factors.

INTRODUCTION

This condition can result from anatomical defects in the thyroid gland, genetic errors in thyroid metabolism, or iodine deficiency. The most frequent causes are issues related to thyroid dysgenesis or dyshormonogenesis, both classified as primary hypothyroidism, whereas a deficiency of thyroid-stimulating hormone is referred to as secondary or central hypothyroidism. Congenital TSH deficiency arises from mutations in the gene for the TSH β subunit and is typically associated with deficiencies in pituitary hormones, resulting in congenital hypopituitarism. Another category, peripheral hypothyroidism, is due to defects in the transport, metabolism, or action of thyroid hormones.[1] Typically, our body acquires it from crops cultivated in iodine-rich soil. However, if the soil in a region lacks iodine, the crops produced there will also be deficient in this vital nutrient. As a result, individuals living in iodine-deficient areas who consume locally grown foods may not meet their necessary intake of this essential element. This deficiency can lead to both physical and mental abnormalities in the fetus, with varying degrees of severity. The most apparent and easily identifiable symptom of iodine deficiency is goiter, which is characterized by an enlarged thyroid gland.[2]

The typical daily requirement for a pregnant or lactating woman is 200 micrograms. Over an average lifespan of 70 years, an individual would need approximately a teaspoon of iodine, but the body requires this amount consistently each day for normal metabolic functions. This is why iodine should be an integral part of everyone’s daily diet. The recommended intake is 150 μg per day for adults, 200 μg for pregnant or lactating women, and lower amounts for children.[3]

A fetus in the mother’s womb requires a consistent supply of iodine for proper brain and body development. During pregnancy, only the mother can provide the necessary iodine levels. If the mother is iodine-deficient, the baby will also lack this vital nutrient. Severe deficiency in the mother can lead to significant developmental issues, resulting in the child being unable to walk, talk, or think normally. Even if the mother’s deficiency is mild, it can still negatively impact the child, often manifesting years later as poor academic performance and difficulties in daily activities.[4]

Follow-up services must provide clear information to the parents regarding the necessary care, financial considerations, and related implications. Parents should be informed that their child will need to see an endocrinologist every 4–12 weeks. The specialist will monitor the child’s development and ensure that thyroid hormone levels are adequate. Blood tests will be conducted at each visit to assess hormone levels, and treatment dosages will be adjusted based on these results. However, if treatment begins within the first month after birth, most infants can develop normally.[5]

The iodometric-titration technique is employed to assess the iodine levels in table salt. This method involves preparing reagent solutions, which can remain stable for different durations, followed by their use in the titration process.[6] Additionally, the iodine content in drinking water can be measured using a spectrophotometric method. The iodine present in staple foods is quantified using a semiautomated approach for determining total iodine content in food.[7]

Sample collection

Globally, the leading cause of congenital hypothyroidism (CH) is iodine deficiency; however, in many developing nations and areas with sufficient environmental iodine, the causes often stem from a mix of known and unknown factors. Common sources of iodine include dietary intake as well as iodine in water and salt. These iodine sources facilitate the synthesis of thyroid hormone precursors, namely, monoiodothyronine (MIT) and diiodothyronine (DIT).

DISCUSSION

The neonatal screening program has been implemented globally to test newborns for CH. Recent research has shown an uptick in the incidence of CH.[8] Additionally, there is ongoing debate and disagreement regarding the cutoff values used for detecting this condition. Iodine excess, particularly from iodine-containing antiseptics, can induce temporary hypothyroxinemia in preterm infants, leading to low thyroid levels.[910] Such cases should be managed as CH, especially during the critical first 3 years of life, considering the threat of mental retardation.[1112]

Therefore, a sustainable strategy to combat ID is essential. The universal salt iodization (USI) program was initiated to address this issue at the national level. In this study, it was found that 35.03% of salt samples contained iodine levels ≤15 ppm, 63.69% had iodine levels between 15 and 25 ppm, and only 1.27% of samples had iodine levels exceeding 25–30 ppm. However, a survey in 2006 indicated 51% of household take fortified salt, and it is calculated that 13 million neonates remain vulnerable to iodine deficiency disorders, with India ranking as one of the countries most affected by these disorders.[1314]

A study carried by Chandra et al. 2006[15] in specific areas of the Imphal West district of Manipur revealed the rate of IDD was 34.96%. Situated in foothills of Himalayas, Manipur is affected by these deficiencies. In an epidemiological study examining school children for IDD and related threat factors, it was found that water iodine concentrations below 0.5 μg/dL were associated with an increased likelihood of developing goiter (Sava et al., 1998).[16] Additionally, a similar study assessing 86 drinking water samples from eight areas in the Puducherry district found the average iodine level to be 9.3 ± 3.4 μg/dL, which significantly contributes to the daily iodine requirements of the population (Saleem and McClure, 2010).[17]

A study conducted in India found that the average iodine content in rice from the goiter-endemic states of northeast India was the lowest in Sikkim (8.8 ± 3.0 μg per 100 g) and the highest in Assam (12.9 ± 2.6 μg per 100 g). The iodine levels in rice from this region showed a narrow range, with a pooled mean of 10.4 ± 2.9 μg per 100 g, compared to samples from nonendemic areas like Hyderabad, where the iodine content was significantly higher at 40.0 ± 3.36 μg per 100 g. Likewise, the pooled mean iodine contents for other cereals such as maize (8.0 ± 2.9 μg per 100 g) and millet (7.3 ± 2.6 μg per 100 g) from the goiter-endemic states like Hyderabad were compared.[18]

CONCLUSION

The current study is a hospital-based investigation aimed at screening neonates for CH and may not accurately reflect the broader population. To effectively identify CH, screening will be conducted on neonates from the study area to provide a clearer representation of its prevalence. The clinical and pathological consequences of CH are often subtle and frequently go unnoticed. The negative impacts of moderate iodine deficiency during pregnancy tend to be minimal and develop gradually. Our findings suggest that iodine levels are influenced by dietary quality, educational background, and socioeconomic status, all of which may affect growth and development. There are multiple factors contributing to CH and poor fetal growth beyond just low iodine levels, which can disrupt thyroid function. The study indicates that iodine levels correlate with dietary quality, educational attainment, and social standing, all of which could influence growth and development through inadequate nutrition and other pathways.
