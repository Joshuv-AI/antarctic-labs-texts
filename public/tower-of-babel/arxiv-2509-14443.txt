On the Complexity of Decoded Quantum Interferometry
Kunal Marwaha, Bill Fefferman, Alexandru Gheorghiu, Vojtech Havlicek

Abstract
We study the complexity of Decoded Quantum Interferometry (DQI), a recently proposed quantum algorithm for approximate optimization [ JSW+25 ] . We argue that DQI is hard to classically simulate, and that the hardness comes from locating an exponentially large hidden subset. This type of hardness is shared by Shor’s algorithm, but the hidden subset here has no apparent group structure. We first prove that DQI can be simulated in a low level of the polynomial hierarchy , ruling out hardness arguments related to quantum supremacy. Instead, we show that DQI implements an existential coding theory bound based on the MacWilliams identity , and that it prepares a state within an obfuscated quantum harmonic oscillator . Both viewpoints require a coherent application of a discrete Hermite transform, which has no natural classical analog.



1
Introduction

Background

Exponential quantum speedups are one of the main reasons for interest in quantum computing, but they are also quite rare. Some well-known examples include Shor’s algorithm for factoring or computing the discrete log
[
Sho94
]
, sampling from the output of certain random quantum circuit families
[
AA11
,
BJS11
,
AC16
,
BFNV19
,
MT19
]
, and interactive tests of quantumness
[
BCMVV21
,
KCVY22
]
. In all of these cases, the classical hardness of the associated tasks is based either on established cryptographic assumptions (such as the security of the RSA cryptosystem), or on well-founded complexity-theoretic assumptions (such as the non-collapse of the polynomial hierarchy).

Recently, a new candidate algorithm was proposed, known as
Decoded Quantum Interferometry
(DQI), meant to provide an exponential quantum speedup for solving certain optimization problems
[
JSW+25
]
. Specifically, DQI aims to achieve a higher-than-classical success rate in solving instances of the
max-LINSAT
problem. This is a constraint-satisfaction problem, in which one is given as input a matrix
B\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{m\times n},
over some finite field
\mathbb{F}_{p},
as well as sets
F_{1},F_{2},\dots F_{m}\subset\mathbb{F}_{p}.
The goal is to find a vector
x\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n},
satisfying as many constraints of the form
\sum_{ij}B_{ij}x_{j}\in F_{i}
as possible, for
i\leq m.
This constraint system is taken to be overdetermined, meaning that
m>n.
In this regime, it is known that finding even a close to optimal solution is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard in general
[
Tre14
]
. Instead, DQI aims to output solutions satisfying an expected number of constraints, denoted
\langle s\rangle,
that is greater than what can be achieved by classical polynomial-time algorithms. In other words, finding approximate solutions in a regime where the problem is not
NP
-hard, but also not efficiently solvable classically. This is done in a manner that’s reminiscent of Regev’s quantum reduction from solving certain worst-case lattice problems to solving the
Learning with Errors
(LWE) problem
[
AR03
,
Reg09
]
. Specifically, one can view
B
as the
generator matrix
of a classical linear code. This means that
B^{T},
is the
parity check
matrix of the
dual
code. The key observation is that the expected number of satisfied constraints is related to the decoding distance of this dual code (a fact referred to as
the semicircle law
). In other words, the more errors one can decode in the dual, the larger the fraction of satisfied constraints for the primal. DQI makes this correspondence explicit by giving an algorithm that, having an efficient decoder for the dual code, is able to output solutions to the constraint system having a large
\langle s\rangle.
In particular, they conjecture that for a certain variant of max-LINSAT known as
Optimal Polynomial Intersection
(OPI), no polynomial-time classical algorithm is able to match the performance of DQI.
The main question that is left open is:

Can one give a rigorous, complexity-theoretic justification for this quantum speedup?

Our results

In this paper, we make progress towards answering this question by giving evidence that classically
simulating
the DQI algorithm is hard.
This is weaker than showing that the problem solved by DQI is hard. Indeed, this distinction is one of the main observations we make. Nevertheless, we find a number of interesting and useful insights into the complexity of DQI that can help us place it in the landscape of quantum advantage proposals.

We first prove that one can simulate the DQI algorithm in
{\mathsf{BPP}}^{{\mathsf{NP}}}
, which is in the third level of the polynomial hierarchy.
This separates DQI from random circuits and circuits that solve
{\mathsf{BQP}}
-complete problems, but also from models of quantum computation with very little power, such as
IQP
and the one clean qubit model
DQC1

[
BJS11
,
MT19
]
.
Consequently, if DQI is hard to simulate, the reason does
not
come from the hardness of computing conditional output probabilities, like it does in these other models.

Instead, we argue that the hardness of simulating DQI comes from the difficulty of outputting any element in a
hidden subset
.
This is reminiscent of the
hidden subgroup problem
, where the goal is to identify a generating set for a hidden subgroup.
In DQI, however, this hidden subset has no apparent group structure.
In both DQI and in Shor’s algorithm, the hidden subset is exponentially large, which foils classical simulation algorithms that take advantage of sparsity
[
SN13
]
.

To provide evidence for this type of hardness we give two new arguments:

1.

The first argument is from coding theory. We show that DQI implements a linear programming technique
[
Mac63
,
MRRW77
]
used to compare the rate and distance of a linear code. In particular, the existence of solutions of quality matching DQI’s “semicircle law” was known as early as 1990
[
Tie90
]
. We show how this existential proof can be made algorithmic using a
quantum
Fourier transform. Much like in Shor’s algorithm, the QFT here uses interference to isolate the subset of solutions at this semicircle law distance.

2.

The second argument is from physics. We show how DQI prepares a state in a quantum harmonic oscillator that hides information about the problem. This viewpoint can be generalized beyond codes. In DQI, the energy is the number of correctable errors, and the position is the satisfying fraction of the assignment.
We show that the optimal state is concentrated on the position matching the semicircle law, and that exponentially many codewords lie at this point.
Although one can classically analyze the quantum harmonic oscillator, preparing these states (which gives the ability to sample) seems possible only with a quantum device.

These arguments improve our understanding of the power of DQI, and suggest ideas for designing new algorithms that plausibly have quantum advantage (which we discuss in
Section

6
).

1.1
Technical overview

Simulating DQI in
{\mathsf{BPP}}^{{\mathsf{NP}}}

The DQI algorithm finds approximate solutions to optimization problems
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
by creating a quantum state
\sum_{x}P(f(x))\ket{x}
that gives larger probability on “good” outcomes, and then sampling from this state. The function
P
is a known polynomial with degree that may scale with
n
. We consider the distribution
\mathcal{D}
induced by sampling the output quantum state of the DQI circuit; i.e.
\mathcal{D}:=\{x\text{ w.p. }\frac{|P(f(x))|^{2}}{Z}\}
where
Z=\sum_{x}|P(f(x))|^{2}
.

We first show how to sample from a distribution close to
\mathcal{D}
in multiplicative error, in
{\mathsf{BPP}}^{{\mathsf{NP}}}
:

Theorem 1
.

For any output quantum state
\ket{\psi_P}
of the DQI circuit, there is a procedure that runs in randomized
\textnormal{poly}(n,1/\epsilon)
time with access to an
{\mathsf{NP}}
oracle that samples from a distribution
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=\{x\textnormal{ w.p. }q(x)\}
, where for all
x
,

\displaystyle(1-\epsilon)|\bra{x}\ket{\psi_P}|^{2}\leq q(x)\leq(1+\epsilon)|\bra{x}\ket{\psi_P}|^{2}\,.

This result relies on a classic theorem of Stockmeyer, which uses an
{\mathsf{NP}}
oracle to approximately compute exponentially-sized sums of non-negative values. Since we can classically compute
P(f(x))
, we can approximately compute conditional probabilities in
{\mathsf{BPP}}^{{\mathsf{NP}}}
, and then approximately sample from
\mathcal{D}
bit-by-bit. In fact, the same holds for any state proportional to
\sum_{x}w(x)\ket{x}
, where
x\mapsto|w(x)|^{2}
is efficiently computable. The state does not even have to be normalized for this to hold.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose
\ket{\psi}
is proportional to a (possibly unnormalized) quantum state
\sum_{x}w(x)\ket{x}
, where
w:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
is a function and
|w|^{2}
is efficiently computable. Then there is a procedure that runs in randomized
\textnormal{poly}(n,1/\epsilon)
time with access to an
{\mathsf{NP}}
oracle that samples from a distribution
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=\{x\textnormal{ w.p. }q(x)\}
, where for all
x
,

\displaystyle(1-\epsilon)|\bra{x}\ket{\psi}|^{2}\leq q(x)\leq(1+\epsilon)|\bra{x}\ket{\psi}|^{2}\,.

Assuming
{\mathsf{BQP}}\nsubseteq{\mathsf{PH}}
, states of this form cannot be the output of
{\mathsf{BQP}}
-complete problems. In fact, any algorithm that encodes GapP-hard quantities in conditional output probabilities cannot have a state of this form unless the polynomial hierarchy collapses. This phenomenon occurs widely (i.e. random circuits, QAOA,
IQP
) and is often the central complexity-theoretic argument beyond quantum supremacy demonstrations.

Corollary 3
.

Suppose a quantum state
\ket{\psi}
has a post-selected output probability that is
\#{\mathsf{P}}
-hard to compute. Then
\ket{\psi}
cannot have the form of
Theorem

2
unless the polynomial hierarchy collapses to the third level.

DQI and linear programming bounds in coding theory

We next show how DQI is connected to foundational literature on coding theory.
The existence of approximate solutions with performance matching DQI has been known since 1990
[
Tie90
]
, using a Fourier-analytic identity
[
Mac63
]
to design a good distribution of codewords
\mathcal{D}
.
It is not known how to classically prepare
\mathcal{D}
.
However, we prove this bound can be made constructive using a
quantum
algorithm, since we can coherently implement a Fourier transform.
This suggests that preparing
\mathcal{D}
is possible with a
quantum
algorithm, but not with a classical one.

DQI is suitable for optimization problems where one fixes a matrix
B
and target vector
v
, and we score a bitstring
x
by the Hamming distance of
Bx
and
v
. It is natural to treat
B
as a generating matrix of a
code

C=\{Bx\ |\ x\in\{0,1\}^{n}\}
; then
v
is a noisy codeword. The quality of approximate solutions produced by DQI is limited by a technical condition: good decoding algorithms to
C^{\perp}
, the
dual code
to
C
.

One identity comparing codewords of
C
and of
C^{\perp}
was proven by Jessie MacWilliams in 1963. The key observation is to interpret this identity as a discretized Fourier transform:

Fact 4

(
[
Mac63
]
)
.

Given a binary linear code
C:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
to
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
, let

\displaystyle W_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\left|\{c\in C;|c|=k\}\right|\,,

\displaystyle W_{k}^{\perp}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\left|\{c\in C^{\perp};|c|=k\}\right|\,.

Then the following relationship holds:

\displaystyle W_{k}^{\perp}=\frac{1}{|C|}\sum_{i=0}^{m}W_{i}K_{k}(i)\,,

where
K_{k}
is the
k^{\text{th}}
Kravchuk polynomial on
m
inputs (defined in
Definition

17
).

Note that all
W_{k}
and
W_{k}^{\perp}
must be non-negative. This identity gives a method to upper bound the distance of
C
to any point
v
, called the
covering radius
of
C
:

Fact 5

(
[
Tie90
]
)
.

Fix a binary linear code
C
with dual distance
d^{\perp}
.
Let
\beta(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{d^{\perp}-1}\beta_{k}K_{k}(x)
be such that
\beta_{0}>0
and
\beta(i)\leq 0
for all
i>j
. Then the covering radius of
C
must be at most
j
.

Notably,
5
is used in
[
Tie90
]
to exactly match the semicircle law of
[
JSW+25
]
in the limit as
m\to\infty
as
\frac{\ell}{m}
held constant.
However, this bound is
existential
, and does not suggest any classical algorithm to find these codewords.

We show how to interpret the bound of
[
Tie90
]
as constructing a
good
distribution of codewords
\mathcal{D}_{v}
; i.e. a codeword randomly sampled from
\mathcal{D}_{v}
has a target average distance to
v
.
This naturally implies a
quantum
algorithm, where we construct a quantum state with output distribution
\mathcal{D}_{v}
.

Theorem 6
.

Fix a real number
\alpha
and a binary linear code
C
with an efficient decoder of
C^{\perp}
up to
\ell
errors. Let
\beta(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{2\ell+1}\beta_{k}K_{k}(x)
be such that
\frac{\beta(x)}{x-\alpha}
is a square polynomial. For each point
v
, there is a quantum algorithm that produces a codeword with average distance
\alpha
from
v
.

A quantum algorithm can then locate the subset of codewords at a target distance from
v
using a good choice of
\beta
. Our best choice of
\beta
sets the target distance at the semicircle law.

Unlike a classical computer, a quantum computer can coherently implement this Fourier transform over the whole codespace.
It seems that the only way we can prove this bound on the covering radius is
through
the Fourier-analytic picture in the MacWilliams identity.
This suggests that efficiently sampling from
\mathcal{D}_{v}
requires a quantum algorithm.

DQI and the quantum harmonic oscillator

Finally, we show how DQI can be seen as acting on a quantum harmonic oscillator of polynomial size. However, even if the quantum harmonic oscillator can be analyzed classically, actually
preparing
a state requires a quantum computer. This allows a quantum computer to access information stored in the states of the oscillator.
By analogy, the oracle quantum algorithm traversing a large welded tree
[
CCD+03
]
can be seen as acting on a one-dimensional line, yet there is no efficient classical algorithm to traverse the tree.

In the usual formulation of a quantum harmonic oscillator, a quantum particle lying on the real line is subject to a quadratic potential well. This system can be exactly solved: The eigenstates have energy
(k+\frac{1}{2})
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, and in the position basis, are Hermite polynomials with a Gaussian decay.
However, digital quantum computers have a finite number of basis states, and cannot act on the real line.
We choose a procedure to discretize the oscillator that preserves the eigenspectrum
[
AS90
]
. We then implement this oscillator on a quantum computer so that the eigenstates are Fourier transforms of
n
-qubit Dicke states.

Claim 7
.

Fix any positive integer
s
. Then we can implement a discrete quantum harmonic oscillator
\mathbf{H}_{s}
over the grid
\{-s,\dots,0,\dots,s\}
in
m\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}2s
qubits, with position operator

\displaystyle\hat{x}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}Z_{i}\,,

and where the eigenstates of
\mathbf{H}_{s}
are Fourier transforms of Dicke states; i.e. for
0\leq k\leq m
,

\displaystyle\ket{\lambda_k}=H_{2}^{\otimes m}\ket{D_k}\,.

In this setup, one can quantumly prepare any state of the discrete harmonic oscillator first by preparing a linear combination of Dicke states, and then applying the Fourier transform.

We also consider discrete oscillators where the eigenstates are
obfuscated
by some function
h
; i.e. replacing the Dicke state
\sum_{|x|=k}\ket{x}
with
\sum_{|x|=k}\ket{h(x)}
. This is possible over subspaces of the oscillator where the map
h
is invertible. In the setting of a linear code, i.e. given
C^{\perp}=\{y\ |\ B^{T}y=0\}
, let
h
be the map from a syndrome
y
to a noisy codeword
B^{T}y
. Then this map is invertible when
|y|<d^{\perp}/2
. These obfuscated states form a low-energy subspace of a quantum harmonic oscillator:

Claim 8

(informal)
.

Consider a linear code with parity check matrix
B^{T}:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and distance
d
. Then it is possible to embed the first
\lfloor d/2\rfloor-1
eigenstates of a discrete quantum harmonic oscillator in a quantum computer, where each eigenstate has form
H_{2}^{\otimes m}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m};|y|=k}\ket{B^T y}
, and with approximate position operator

\hat{x}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(-1)^{(Bx)_{i}}\outerproduct{x}{x}\,.

From here, it is easy to interpret DQI as preparing a state in this low-energy subspace of a quantum harmonic oscillator. The position operator
\hat{x}
is exactly
m/2
less than the satisfying fraction of an assignment, so DQI will prepare an oscillator state with as large a position as possible. The best oscillator state (and thus optimal DQI state) is concentrated on states at the semicircle law:

Theorem 9
.

Consider a linear code with parity check matrix
B^{T}:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and distance
d
. Suppose we have an efficient decoder up to
\ell
errors. Then a quantum computer can prepare a state that is concentrated on bitstrings
x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
satisfying
|Bx|\approx m/2+\sqrt{\ell(m-\ell)}
in the
m\to\infty
limit.

Using this perspective, we show there are
exponentially many
codewords with solution quality matching the semicircle law. The optimal DQI state is essentially supported on a set of outcomes exponentially smaller than the set of all outcomes, but still exponentially large.

Corollary 10
.

The optimal DQI state is close to a state with support
|S|=2^{cn}
, for
0<c<1
.

Corollary

10
prevents classical simulation algorithms that take advantage of
sparsity
, such as rejection sampling or algorithms based on Goldreich-Levin and Kushilevitz-Mansour
[
GL89
,
KM91
,
Nes09
]
.
This behavior is also present in Shor’s algorithm; see
Appendix

B
and
Appendix

C
.

Our results suggest that the hardness of simulating DQI has a similar source as in Shor’s algorithm: The algorithm hides an exponentially large set
S
in a quantum state, so that sampling the state allows the recovery of information about
S
. In Shor’s algorithm,
S
is a congruence class determined by the discrete log value. In DQI,
S
is the intersection of codewords
\{Bx\ |\ x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\}
and vectors at the semicircle law distance from
v
, but it has no apparent group structure.

Related work

{\mathsf{BQP}}
and coding theory

One of the earliest
{\mathsf{BQP}}
-complete problems is an extension of the
weight enumerator polynomial
from classical coding theory
[
KL01
]
.
The weight enumerator polynomial features prominently in the MacWilliams identity relating the distribution of codewords in
C
and in
C^{\perp}
.
This
{\mathsf{BQP}}
-complete problem can be used to evaluate the partition function of some physical models
[
GL08
,
GL10
]
.
Evaluating the weight enumerator polynomial itself is
{\mathsf{PP}}
-complete
[
Vya03
]
.
One can generalize Shor’s algorithm to quantumly estimate some Gauss sums over finite fields
[
VS02
]
. There are several
quantum
variants of the MacWilliams identity used in the study of quantum codes; see for example
[
SL96
,
HESG18
,
Bur25
]
.

DQI and Regev’s reduction

The ideas behind DQI arguably start with Regev’s quantum reduction from the Shortest Vector Problem (SVP) to a problem known as
Learning With Errors
(LWE)
[
AR03
,
Reg09
]
.
The central idea in the reduction is that decoding noisy codewords allows one to find short codewords in the
dual
code (which can be viewed as a lattice).
This technique has been refined to error-correct in
superposition
(e.g.
[
CLZ22
,
DRT24
]
), and even used to construct an
{\mathsf{NP}}
-search problem that separates
{\mathsf{BQP}}
from
{\mathsf{BPP}}
relative to a random oracle
[
YZ24
]
.
Regev’s reduction has been partially dequantized, for example when there is a
unique
shortest vector
[
Pei09
,
LM09
,
BLPRS13
]
; see also the survey
[
Ben23
]
. However, the full reduction has resisted attempts at dequantization.
The DQI algorithm
[
JSW+25
]
can be viewed as implementing a worst-case variant of the reduction proposed in
[
CT24
]
.
DQI has been analyzed in some more realistic scenarios, for example with noise
[
BGKL25
]
and with imperfect decoding
[
CT25
]
.

Linear programming bounds for coding theory

Two linear programming bounds comparing the rate and distance of a code were given in
[
MRRW77
]
. These arguments rely on a duality argument implicit in the MacWilliams identity, which can be generalized using Delsarte’s theory of
association schemes

[
Del73
]
; see also the survey
[
DL02
]
.
The original linear programming bounds have been re-proven using graph theory
[
FT05
]
, harmonic analysis
[
NS09
]
, and geometric techniques
[
Sam23
]
.
The linear programs were generalized in
[
CJJ21
,
LL22
,
CD24
]
.
This duality argument can also be used to bound the rate of a code by the distance of its
dual
code
[
Tie90
,

]
, which we use in this work.
It seems that the weight distribution of linear codes is intimately related to the Kravchuk polynomials
[
Kra29
]
; see for example
[
Sam24
]
.

Quantum harmonic oscillator and Hermite transform

The eigenstates of a quantum harmonic oscillator on the real line are Hermite polynomials.
It was recently shown that a quantum computer can sample a function by its Hermite coefficients
[
IJ25
]
, leading to polynomial quantum query speedups.
Representing a harmonic oscillator on a digital quantum computer requires a discretization, e.g.
[
AS90
,
SS09
,
Som15
]
.
One choice
[
HW00
,
CF24
]
uses the Kravchuk polynomials
[

]
,
and induces a natural physical interpretation
[
SZ14
,
Fei16
,
Fei16a
]
.
[
Dun76
]
studied the group structure of the Kravchuk polynomials, and their relationship to the Fourier transform was studied in
[
AW97
,
AVW99
]
. Encoding
quantum
information in an oscillator is the core idea of some constructions of
quantum
codes; see for example
[
GKP01
,

]
.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Coding theory

See
[
Gur10
]
for an introduction to coding theory. In this subsection we use variables
(n,k,G,H)
canonical to coding theory, which differ from their use in DQI and the rest of the paper.

A
code
is an injective function (usually defined as a map
E:\mathbb{F}^{k}\to\mathbb{F}^{n}
from input to codewords). We take
C
to be the set of codewords.
The
distance

d
is the minimum Hamming distance between codewords; it is at most
n-k+1
by an argument called the singleton bound. For example, Reed-Solomon codes achieve this bound.
The
dimension
is
k
, and the
rate
is
k/n
.

A
linear code
is a code where the codewords
C
form a subspace of
\mathbb{F}^{n}
. In that case the map
E
is a linear transformation, and can be defined by a
generating
matrix
G\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times k}
. It is referred to as a
[n,k,d]
code (or
[n,k]
code).
Some examples of linear codes include binary repetition, Reed-Solomon, and binary parity check. The distance of a linear code is the smallest nonzero Hamming weight of a codeword.
All linear codes have a
parity check
matrix
H\in\mathbb{F}^{(n-k)\times n}
such that
C=\{c\in\mathbb{F}^{n}|Hc=0\}
. This is the matrix that has nullspace equal to
C
.
Given a output
y\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
, the vector
Hy
is called the
syndrome
of
y
. (The syndrome is
\vec{0}
iff
y
is a codeword.)

Every linear code
C
has a natural
dual
code
C^{\perp}:=\{z\in\mathbb{F}^{n}|z\cdot c=0\ \forall c\in C\}
. In the dual code, the roles of
(G,H)
swap to
(H^{T},G^{T})
. The dual of a
[n,k,d]
code is an
[n,n-k]
code with indeterminate distance. The codewords are the image of
H^{T}
; the nullspace of
G
is equal to
C^{\perp}
.

2.2
Decoded Quantum Interferometry (DQI)

The authors of
[
JSW+25
]
propose a new algorithm called “Decoded Quantum Interferometry” (DQI). This algorithm finds
approximate
solutions to linear constraint satisfaction problems. DQI prepares a state by coherently solving a decoding problem in the
Fourier basis
, and then sampling from that state.

We study the following problem:

Problem 11

(Max-XORSAT Search)
.

Given
B\in(\mathbb{F}_{2})^{m\times n}
and
v\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
, find an
n
-bit string
x
satisfying as many linear equations
Bx=v\mod 2
as possible. Equivalently, maximize

\displaystyle f(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(-1)^{\sum_{j=1}^{n}B_{ij}x_{j}+v_{i}}\,.

(1)

The DQI algorithm
[
JSW+25
]
approximately solves
11
as follows:

Algorithm 12

(DQI
[
JSW+25
]
)
.

Given
B\in(\mathbb{F}_{2})^{m\times n}
,
v\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
:

1.

Find an efficient decoder for the linear code
C^{\perp}=\{d\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m},B^{T}d=0\}
. Suppose this decoder can correct
\ell
errors.

2.

Construct the “DQI state”

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_P}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}P(f(x))\ket{x}\,,

(2)

where
f
is defined in
Eq.

1
and
P
is a polynomial of degree at most
\ell
.

3.

Measure
\ket{\psi_P}
in the computational basis.

There are two “design decisions” in the DQI algorithm:

1.

One must find a good decoder. Since the linear code
C^{\perp}
is a LDPC code,
[
JSW+25
]

2.3
Sampling versus computing output probabilities

Consider a distribution
\mathcal{D}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\{x\text{ w.p. }p(x)\}
. We review how the complexity of computing
p(x)
implies the classical hardness or easiness of sampling from
\mathcal{D}
.

Suppose that approximately computing
p(x\in T|x\in S)
lies outside the polynomial hierarchy (
{\mathsf{PH}}
).
By Stockmeyer’s theorem
[
Sto83
]
, approximately computing
p(x)
also lies outside
{\mathsf{PH}}
.
Then we cannot sample from
\mathcal{D}
in
{\mathsf{PH}}
unless the
{\mathsf{PH}}
collapses
[
HHT97
]
. In general, if approximately computing
p(x)
is
{\mathsf{NP}}^{{\mathsf{NP}}}
-hard, then we cannot efficiently classically sample from
\mathcal{D}
unless the
{\mathsf{PH}}
collapses
[
HHT97
, Corollary 3.13]
.

On the other hand, if computing
p(x\in T|x\in S)
is easy, then one can classically sample from
\mathcal{D}
where we generate the sample bit by bit. Let
B_{t}
be the set of outcomes with prefix
t
. We first flip a coin with probability of heads
p(x\in B_{1})
, and assign the first bit to be
1
if the coin is heads. If it is heads, then we flip a coin with probability of heads
p(x\in B_{11})
; otherwise, we flip a coin with probability of heads
p(x\in B_{01})
. The outcome of the coin determines the next bit. We may repeat this process until we have assigned some
x
. So computing
p(x\in T|x\in S)
is as hard as sampling.

3
Sampling a DQI state in
{\mathsf{BPP}}^{{\mathsf{NP}}}

DQI (
12
) samples from a state of the form
\sum_{x}P(f(x))\ket{x}
, and more generally

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}=\sum_{x}w(x)\ket{x}

(4)

for some
w
where
|w|^{2}
is efficiently computable.

In this section we show how to approximately sample from any state of the form in
Eq.

4
in
{\mathsf{BPP}}^{{\mathsf{NP}}}
. This relies on a classic result of Stockmeyer
[
Sto83
]
:

Theorem 14

(
[
Sto83
]
)
.

Given as input a function
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}^{m}
and
y\in\{0,1\}^{m}
, there is a procedure that runs in randomized time
\textnormal{poly}(n,1/\epsilon)
with access to a
{\mathsf{NP}}^{f}
oracle that outputs an
\alpha
such that

\displaystyle(1-\epsilon)p\leq\alpha\leq(1+\epsilon)p\quad\textnormal{ for }\quad p=\Pr_{x\in\mathcal{U}(\{0,1\}^{n})}[f(x)=y]\,.

Claim 15
.

Suppose we can efficiently compute a function
g:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
that is nonzero somewhere, an upper bound
O(\exp(n))\geq A\geq\max_{x}g(x)
, and a lower bound
\Omega(\frac{1}{\exp(n)})\leq a\leq\min_{x;g(x)\neq 0}g(x)
. Then there is a procedure that runs in randomized
\textnormal{poly}(n,1/\epsilon)
time with access to a
{\mathsf{NP}}
oracle that can sample from a distribution
\mathcal{D}=\{x\textnormal{ w.p. }q(x)\}
, where for all
x
,

\displaystyle(1-\epsilon)\frac{g(x)}{Z}\leq q(x)\leq(1+\epsilon)\frac{g(x)}{Z}\,,

and
Z=\sum_{x}g(x)
.

Proof.

We first approximate
Z
with Stockmeyer (
Theorem

14
). We then approximate conditional probabilities, again with Stockmeyer. Finally, we use the chain rule to generate
\mathcal{D}
.

We first show how to approximate
Z
. Build the function
f:\{0,1\}^{n+j}\to\{0,1\}
:

•

Let
x
be the first
n
bits of the input to
f
.

•

Let
t
be the remaining
j
bits of the input to
f
. Output
1
if
\frac{t}{2^{j}}<\frac{g(x)}{A}
, otherwise output
0
.

By construction,
0\leq\frac{g(x)}{A}\leq 1
. Let
p_{Z}
be the probability that
f
outputs
1
; then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{n}}\left(\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\frac{g(x)}{A}-\frac{1}{2^{j}}\right)\leq p_{Z}\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}\left(\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\frac{g(x)}{A}+\frac{1}{2^{j}}\right)\,.

Rearranging terms, we see

\displaystyle Z-\frac{A}{2^{j}}\leq A\cdot 2^{n}\cdot p_{Z}\leq Z+\frac{A}{2^{j}}\,.

By
Theorem

14
, in randomized time
\textnormal{poly}(n,1/\delta)
with access to a
{\mathsf{NP}}
oracle, we can output
\alpha_{Z}
such that

\displaystyle(1-\delta)p_{Z}\leq\alpha_{Z}\leq(1+\delta)p_{Z}\,.

Choose
j
large enough to satisfy
\frac{A}{2^{j}}\leq\delta\cdot a\leq\delta\cdot Z
. Note that
A/a
has value at most exponential in
n
, so
j\leq O(\textnormal{poly}(n,\log(1/\delta)))
. Then

\displaystyle(1-\delta)^{2}Z\leq A\cdot 2^{n}\cdot\alpha_{Z}\leq(1+\delta)^{2}Z\,,

and so
\tilde{Z}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}A\cdot 2^{n}\cdot\alpha_{Z}
is a
(1\pm 2\delta)
-multiplicative approximation of
Z
. Since
g
is nonzero somewhere,
Z>0
, and so our estimate
\tilde{Z}
is also positive.

Next, we approximate conditional probabilities. Let
S_{v}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
be the set of bitstrings that have prefix
v
. Then
\Pr[x\in S_{v}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\sum_{x\in S_{v}}\frac{g(x)}{Z}
. To approximate this value, we build the function
f_{v}:\{0,1\}^{n+k}\to\{0,1\}
, where
k\leq O(\textnormal{poly}(n))
:

•

Let
x
be the first
n
bits of the input to
f_{v}
. If
x\notin S_{v}
, output
0
.

•

Let
t
be the remaining
k
bits of the input to
f_{v}
. Output
1
if
\frac{t}{2^{k}}<\frac{g(x)}{\tilde{Z}}
, otherwise output
0
.

Let
p_{v}
be the probability that
f_{v}
outputs
1
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{n}}(\sum_{x\in S_{v}}\frac{g(x)}{\tilde{Z}}-\frac{1}{2^{k}})\leq p_{v}\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}(\sum_{x\in S_{v}}\frac{g(x)}{\tilde{Z}}+\frac{1}{2^{k}})\,.

Since
\tilde{Z}
is a
(1\pm 2\delta)
-multiplicative approximation of
Z
,
\frac{1}{\tilde{Z}}
is a
(1\pm 4\delta)
-multiplicative approximation of
\frac{1}{Z}
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{1-4\delta}{2^{n}}(\sum_{x\in S_{v}}\frac{g(x)}{Z}-\frac{1}{2^{k}})\leq p_{v}\leq\frac{1+4\delta}{2^{n}}(\sum_{x\in S_{v}}\frac{g(x)}{Z}+\frac{1}{2^{k}})\,.

Again, by
Theorem

14
, in randomized time
\textnormal{poly}(n,1/\delta)
with access to a
{\mathsf{NP}}
oracle, we can output
\alpha_{S_{v}}
such that

\displaystyle(1-\delta)p_{v}\leq\alpha_{S_{v}}\leq(1+\delta)p_{v}\,.

Note that if
g(x)=0
,
f_{v}(x||t)=0
; so the
\frac{1}{2^{k}}
error only accrues if
g(x)\neq 0
. Choose
k
large enough to satisfy
\frac{1}{2^{k}}\leq\delta\cdot\frac{a}{2^{n}\cdot\max(A,1)}\leq\delta\cdot\min_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n};g(x)\neq 0}\frac{g(x)}{Z}
. Note that
A/a
has value at most exponential in
n
, and so
k\leq O(\textnormal{poly}(n))
. Then
\alpha_{S_{v}}
also satisfies

\displaystyle(1-8\delta)\sum_{x\in S_{v}}\frac{g(x)}{Z}\leq 2^{n}\cdot\alpha_{S_{v}}\leq(1+8\delta)\sum_{x\in S_{v}}\frac{g(x)}{Z}\,.

We now generate
\mathcal{D}
with the chain rule. To choose the first bit, we compute
2^{n}\cdot\alpha_{S_{1}}
and flip a biased coin of this probability; we set the bit to
1
if it is heads, otherwise
0
. For every subsequent bit, suppose the current bitstring is
v
. We compute
\alpha_{S_{v||1}}
and flip a biased coin of probability
\frac{\alpha_{S_{v||1}}}{\alpha_{S_{v}}}
; we set the next bit to
1
if it is heads, otherwise
0
. This chooses a bitstring

with probability

, where

\displaystyle(1-8n\delta)\frac{g(x)}{Z}\leq(1-8\delta)^{n}\frac{g(x)}{Z}\leq q(x)\leq(1+8\delta)^{n}\frac{g(x)}{Z}\leq(1+8n\delta)\frac{g(x)}{Z}\,.

Choosing

completes the proof.
∎

In

The DQI state can be written as

DQI implements a 1990 linear programming bound

The expected number of satisfying assignments of a solution output by DQI is called “the semicircle law” in
[
JSW+25
]
. However, we show how this law rediscovers an old idea in coding theory bounding the
covering radius
of a code by the
distance
of its dual code
[
MRRW77
,
Tie90
]
.
We rederive this existential bound, and show how it naturally gives rise to a quantum algorithm akin to DQI.
Remarkably, we do not know of any
classical
way to obtain these codewords.

4.1
The MacWilliams identity

The MacWilliams identity relates the Hamming weight distribution of codewords in a code
C
with that of its dual code
C^{\perp}
. It relies on the
Kravchuk
polynomials, a set of orthogonal polynomials associated with the binomial distribution (see
[
FK07
]
for a survey). These polynomials can be viewed as a discrete version of the Hermite polynomials.

Definition 17

(
[
Kra29
]
)
.

For any positive integer
m
, define the Kravchuk (or Krawtchouk) polynomials
\{K_{k}\ |\ 0\leq k\leq m\}
as

\displaystyle K_{k}(j;m)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{i}{j\choose i}{m-j\choose k-i}\,.

When
m
is clear from context, we write the evaluated polynomial as
K_{k}(j)
.

Fact 18

(e.g.
[
MS77
, Theorem 16]
)
.

\sum_{i=0}^{m}{m\choose i}K_{k}(i;m)K_{\ell}(i;m)=2^{m}{m\choose k}\delta_{k\ell}
.

We now restate the MacWilliams identity:

Fact 19

(
[
Mac63
]
)
.

Given a binary linear code
C:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
to
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
, let

\displaystyle W_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\left|\{c\in C;|c|=k\}\right|\,,

\displaystyle W_{k}^{\perp}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\left|\{c\in C^{\perp};|c|=k\}\right|\,.

Then the following relationship holds:

\displaystyle W_{k}^{\perp}=\frac{1}{|C|}\sum_{i=0}^{m}W_{i}K_{k}(i)\,.

This identity was famously used to upper bound the size of
C
by its distance
[
MRRW77
]
, known as the first and second
linear programming bound
for codes. We will use (a slight generalization of) this identity to upper bound the
covering radius
of a code
C
by the distance of its
dual
code
C^{\perp}
.

4.2
Recovering the semicircle law

We will re-prove the following existential result of
[
Tie90
]
:

Claim 20

(
[
Tie90
]
)
.

The covering radius of a binary linear code
C:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
is at most

\displaystyle\frac{m}{2}-\sqrt{\ell(m-\ell)}+o(m)\,,

for any
\ell
less than half the dual distance; i.e.
\ell<d^{\perp}/2
.

To prove
20
, we need a slight generalization of the MacWilliams identity. This allows us to reason about the distribution of codewords from any point
v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Fact 21
.

Fix a binary linear code
C:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
. Let

W_{\ell}(v)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\left|\{c\in C;|c-v|=\ell\}\right|

be the number of codewords with Hamming distance
\ell
from a point
v\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{m}W_{i}(v)K_{k}(i)=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m};|y|=k}(-1)^{v\cdot y}\cdot|C|\cdot\mathbf{1}_{B^{T}y=0}\,.

(5)

Proof.

We rely on the following identity, which holds for any
w\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
:

K_{k}(|w|)=K_{k}(\frac{m-\sum_{i=1}^{m}(-1)^{w_{i}}}{2})=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m};|y|=k}(-1)^{w\cdot y}\,.

We prove this identity as
39
in
Appendix

A
.

Let
B\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m\times n}
be the generating matrix of
C
.
Then

\sum_{i=0}^{m}W_{i}(v)K_{k}(i)=\sum_{c\in C}K_{k}(|c-v|)=\sum_{c\in C}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m};|y|=k}(-1)^{(c-v)\cdot y}=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m};|y|=k}(-1)^{-v\cdot y}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{(Bx)\cdot y}\,.

Observe that the second sum is nonzero only when
Bx\cdot y=0
for all
x
; i.e. when
B^{T}y=0
.
∎

When
v=0
, the right-hand side of
Eq.

5
counts the number of
dual
codewords in
C^{\perp}
of weight
k
; i.e.
|C|\cdot W_{k}^{\perp}(0)
. This recovers
19
.

We now use
21
to bound the covering radius:

Claim 22

(
[
Tie90
]
)
.

Fix a binary linear code
C
with dual distance
d^{\perp}
.
Let
\beta(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{d^{\perp}-1}\beta_{k}K_{k}(x)
be such that
\beta_{0}>0
and
\beta(i)\leq 0
for all
i>j
. Then the covering radius of
C
must be at most
j
.

Proof.

Suppose
C
is generated by some matrix
B:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m\times n}
. The
dual code

C^{\perp}
is then equal to
\{y\ |\ B^{T}y=0\}
. Fix any point
v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Invoking
21
for all
1\leq k<d^{\perp}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{m}W_{i}(v)K_{k}(i)=0\,.

Since
K_{0}(x)=1
is constant, we have
\sum_{i=0}^{m}W_{i}(v)K_{0}(i)=|C|
.

We can trivially represent
\beta_{0}=\beta_{0}\cdot 1+\sum_{k=1}^{d^{\perp}-1}\beta_{k}\cdot 0
. So then

\beta_{0}=\sum_{k=0}^{d^{\perp}-1}\beta_{k}\cdot\frac{1}{|C|}\sum_{i=0}^{m}W_{i}(v)K_{k}(i)\,,

since the second sum is
1
at
k=0
and
0
for all
1\leq k<d^{\perp}
. By definition of
\beta
, we rewrite this as

\displaystyle\beta_{0}=\frac{1}{|C|}\sum_{i=0}^{m}W_{i}(v)\beta(i)\,.

(6)

Recall that
\beta_{0}>0
and
\beta(i)<0
for all
i>j
by assumption. Since
W_{i}(v)\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle 0<\beta_{0}=\frac{1}{|C|}\sum_{i=0}^{m}W_{i}(v)\beta(i)\leq\frac{1}{|C|}\sum_{i=0}^{j}W_{i}(v)\beta(i)\,.

(7)

So, for any
v
, there is some
W_{i}(v)>0
for

. So the covering radius of

is at most

.
∎

22
means that a good choice of
\beta(x)
with degree
<d^{\perp}
implies a good bound on the

of

. We first describe the choice proposed by
[
Tie90
]
:

Fact 23

(
[
Tie90
]
)
.

For any

\ell
, let
[\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}]
be the roots of

. Then for any

,

\displaystyle\beta(x)=-(x-\alpha_{1}-\epsilon)\prod_{s=2}^{\ell}(x-\alpha_{s})^{2}

has
\beta_{0}>0
, and
\beta(i)\leq 0
for all
i>\alpha_{1}+\epsilon
.

Proof.

When

, we have
-(x-\alpha_{1}-\epsilon)<0
, and so
\beta(x)
cannot be positive.

Now we prove
\beta_{0}>0
. We rewrite
\beta_{0}
using

\sum_{i=0}^{m}{m\choose i}\beta(i)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}{m\choose i}K_{0}(i)\beta(i)=\sum_{k=0}^{d^{\perp}-1}\beta_{k}\sum_{i=0}^{m}{m\choose i}K_{0}(i)K_{k}(i)=\beta_{0}\cdot 2^{m}\,.

When
\epsilon=0
, we have

\beta_{0}\cdot 2^{m}=\sum_{i=0}^{m}{m\choose i}K_{\ell}(i)\cdot\frac{K_{\ell}(i)}{x-\alpha_{1}}\,.

Again by

We take a derivative of

with respect to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{m}}\sum_{i=0}^{m}{m\choose i}\prod_{s=2}^{\ell}(x-\alpha_{s})^{2}>0\,,

and so

when

is positive.
∎

We may now prove the main result of
[
Tie90
]
:

Proof of
20
.

By
22
and
23
, the covering radius of a code
C
is at most the smallest root of

, for
\ell=\lfloor d^{\perp}/2\rfloor-1
.
The asymptotic value of this root is well-known (e.g.,
[

). If

\frac{\ell}{m}

\displaystyle\frac{m}{2}-\sqrt{\ell(m-\ell)}\,.

∎

4.3
Interpreting this bound as a quantum algorithm

20
is precisely the “semicircle law” of DQI
[
JSW+25
]
.
However, the bound is existential, whereas DQI actually
locates
a codeword at this distance. Here we show how to interpret the proof of
20
as a quantum algorithm.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a distribution over codewords in
C
, where
p:C\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
computes the probability of observing a codeword in
\mathcal{D}
. For any vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we write
{\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{D}}[|c-v|]
as the expected distance from
v
of a codeword sampled from
\mathcal{D}
. Then the covering radius is at most

\max_{v}\min_{\mathcal{D}}{\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{D}}[|c-v|]\,.

For each
v
, we consider
\mathcal{D}_{v}
where
p_{v}(c)
is a function of
|c-v|
. Intuitively, we will construct
\mathcal{D}_{v}
by placing high probability on codewords that are close to
v
.

Theorem 24
.

Fix a real number
\alpha
and a binary linear code
C
with dual distance
d^{\perp}
. Let
\beta(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{d^{\perp}-1}\beta_{k}K_{k}(x)
be such that
\beta_{0}\leq 0
and
\frac{\beta(x)}{x-\alpha}
is a square polynomial. Then the covering radius of
C
is at most
\alpha
.

Proof.

Let
\gamma^{2}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\frac{\beta(x)}{x-\alpha}
; then
\gamma
is a polynomial of degree at most
d^{\perp}/2-1
.
Let
\mathcal{D}_{v}
be the distribution where
p_{v}(c)=\frac{1}{Z}\cdot\gamma^{2}(|c-v|)
and
Z=\sum_{c\in C}\gamma^{2}(|c-v|)
. By the first moment method, the covering radius is at most

\displaystyle\max_{v}{\mathsf{E}}_{\mathcal{D}_{v}}[|c-v|]

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in C}p_{v}(c)|c-v|

\displaystyle=\alpha+\sum_{c\in C}p_{v}(c)(|c-v|-\alpha)

\displaystyle=\alpha+\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{c\in C}\beta(|c-v|)\,.

The second term is
\frac{1}{Z}\cdot|C|\cdot\beta_{0}
by
Eq.

6
, and so negative by assumption. Then the covering radius is at most
\alpha
.
∎

We can bound the covering radius with essentially the same polynomial as in
Theorem

24
. This again implies
20
:

Claim 25
.

Fix a binary linear code
C
with dual distance
d^{\perp}
. Then the covering radius of
C
is at most the smallest root of
K_{\ell}
for any
\ell\leq d^{\perp}/2-1
.

Proof.

Fix any
\ell\leq d^{\perp}/2-1
. Let
[\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}]
be the roots of
K_{\ell}(x)
. Consider the polynomial

\displaystyle\beta(x)=(x-\alpha_{1})\prod_{s=2}^{\ell}(x-\alpha_{s})^{2}\,.

Following the proof of
23
, we have
\beta_{0}=0
. Moreover,

How could we obtain a nearby codeword to the vector

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\sqrt{p_{v}(Bx)}\ket{x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{Z}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\gamma(|Bx-v|)\ket{x}\,.

(8)

Distributions allowed by

are exactly the ones constructible with DQI.

Theorem 26
.

Fix a real number
\alpha
and a binary linear code
C
with an efficient decoder of

errors. Let

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{Z}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\gamma(|Bx-v|)\ket{x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{Z}}\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}\gamma_{k}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}K_{k}(|Bx-v|)\ket{x}\,.

We rely on the identity
K_{k}(|w|)=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m};|y|=k}(-1)^{w\cdot y}
(proven as

). The Fourier transform of the quantum state is proportional to

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}\gamma_{k}\cdot\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m};|y|=k}(-1)^{v\cdot y}\ket{B^T y}\,.

(9)

States of the form (

, Equation 3]
.
∎

We may interpret

as both a constructive variant of

and a performance analysis of the DQI algorithm.
The quantum algorithm takes advantage of information about the dual code

to construct states

in the Fourier basis, and then prepares

by coherently assigning probabilities to codewords by distance to

Meanwhile, there is no obvious way to

\mathcal{D}_{v}
in

.

Using the choice of

.

Consider a binary linear code with an efficient decoder of

Let

The

5
DQI sees a quantum harmonic oscillator

We explain how the DQI algorithm can be seen as acting on the low-energy subspace of a quantum harmonic oscillator. We show how to discretize the harmonic oscillator in a way that preserves the spectrum, and implement it on a quantum computer.
We then show how to encode information in the states of this system that is accessible by sampling the state but not by any obvious classical algorithm. We use this framework to show that the optimal DQI state is concentrated on a set of bitstrings with the same asymptotic satisfying fraction. This set is exponentially smaller than the set of all bitstrings, but still exponentially large.

5.1
A discretized quantum harmonic oscillator

A quantum harmonic oscillator describes a quantum particle in a quadratic potential well. It is one of the few physical models that is exactly solvable. Consider the differential equation

-i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Psi(x;t)=\mathbf{H}\cdot\Psi(x;t)\,,

where

\mathbf{H}=\frac{1}{2}\left(-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}+x^{2}\right)\,.

The equation has stationary solutions
\Psi_{k}(x;t)=e^{i(k+\frac{1}{2})t}\cdot\Psi_{k}(x)
for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, where

\Psi_{k}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sqrt{\pi}2^{k}k!}}H_{k}(x)e^{-x^{2}/2}\,,

and
H_{k}(x)
is the
k^{\text{th}}

Hermite polynomial
.
1
1

1

The Hermite polynomials are defined by the relation
H_{-1}=0
,
H_{0}=1
, and
H_{k+1}(x)=2xH_{k}(x)-2kH_{k-1}(x)
.

We discretize this system in a way that preserves the spectrum
[
AS90
,
HW00
,
CF24
]
.
The Hermite polynomials are orthogonal with respect to the Gaussian distribution, and complete for square integrable functions on the real line. If we restrict to a grid of
m
points, then a natural substitute are the
Kravchuk
polynomials (
Definition

17
)
\{K_{0},\dots,K_{m}\}
. These polynomials are orthogonal with respect to the
binomial
distribution, and approximate the Hermite polynomials in the continuum limit
m\to\infty
(e.g.
[
HW00
,
CF24
]
). From here, we may construct a Hamiltonian that is diagonal in the Kravchuk basis:

Fact 28

(
[
AS90
,
CF24
]
)
.

Fix any positive integer
s
.
Let
\mathbf{H}_{s}
act on the grid
\{-s,\dots,s\}
defined by the following equation for each integer
0\leq k\leq 2s
:

\mathbf{H}_{s}\cdot K_{k}(x+s;2s)\sqrt{{2s\choose x+s}}=(k+\frac{1}{2})\cdot K_{k}(x+s;2s)\sqrt{{2s\choose x+s}}\,.

Then
\mathbf{H}_{s}
approximates a quantum harmonic oscillator in the continuum limit
s\to\infty
.

Using
28
, we may embed this oscillator on a quantum computer of
2s
qubits.
The other benefit of this implementation is that the eigenstates of the oscillator are easy to describe.

Claim 29
.

Fix any positive integer
s
. Then we can implement a discrete quantum harmonic oscillator
\mathbf{H}_{s}
over the grid
\{s,\dots,0,\dots,s\}
in
m\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}2s
qubits, with position operator

\displaystyle\hat{x}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}Z_{i}\,,

(10)

and where the eigenstates of
\mathbf{H}_{s}
are Fourier transforms of Dicke states; i.e. for
0\leq k\leq m
,

\displaystyle\ket{\lambda_k}=H_{2}^{\otimes m}\ket{D_k}\,.

Proof.

We start from the discrete oscillator in
28
. We represent position
-s\leq x\leq s
with the
(x+s)^{\text{th}}

Dicke
state

\ket{D_{x+s}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{{2s\choose x+s}}}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2s};|y|=x+s}\ket{y}\,.

The reader can verify that Dicke states are eigenstates of the position operator in
Eq.

10
with the correct eigenvalue; i.e.
\ket{D_{x+s}}
has eigenvalue
x
.

We now study the eigenstates of
\mathbf{H}_{s}
. The
k^{\text{th}}
eigenstate
\ket{\lambda_k}
of the oscillator has the form

\ket{\lambda_k}\propto\sum_{x=-s}^{s}K_{k}(x+s)\sqrt{{2s\choose x+s}}\ket{D_{x+s}}=\sum_{x=-s}^{s}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2s};|y|=x+s}K_{k}(x+s)\ket{y}=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2s}}K_{k}(|y|)\ket{y}\,.

We invoke the following identity, proven as
39
in
Appendix

A
:

K_{k}(|w|)=K_{k}(\frac{m-\sum_{i=1}^{m}(-1)^{w_{i}}}{2})=\sum_{z\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m};|z|=k}(-1)^{w\cdot z}\,.

Then the eigenstates of
\mathbf{H}_{s}
are

\displaystyle H_{2}^{\otimes m}\ket{D_k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{{m\choose k}}}\sum_{z\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m};|z|=k}H_{2}^{\otimes m}\ket{z}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{m}{m\choose k}}}\sum_{z\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m};|z|=k}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}}(-1)^{y\cdot z}\ket{y}=\ket{\lambda_k}\,.

∎

Since the oscillator is polynomially-sized in
29
, it is easy to implement this in both a classical and a quantum computer. However, this no longer trivially holds when the oscillator states encode information, as we explore in the next subsection.

5.2
Obfuscating the harmonic oscillator

There is no a priori reason that an eigenstate of our harmonic oscillator must be the Fourier transform of a Dicke state. Consider a reversible map
\pi:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
. We could imagine implementing our oscillator so that the
k^{\text{th}}
eigenstate is

\displaystyle H_{2}^{\otimes m}\frac{1}{\sqrt{{m\choose k}}}\sum_{z\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m};|z|=k}\ket{\pi(z)}\propto\sum_{z\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m};|z|=k}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}}(-1)^{y\cdot\pi(z)}\ket{y}\,.

(11)

This modifies the position operator. Suppose there exists some
\sigma:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
such that for all
y,z\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
, we have
\sigma(y)\cdot z=y\cdot\pi(z)
. Then the eigenstate is proportional to

\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}}K_{k}(|\sigma(y)|)\ket{y}\,,

which transforms each position state
\ket{D_{x+s}}
to

\frac{1}{\sqrt{{m\choose x+s}}}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m};|y|=x+s}\ket{\sigma^{-1}(y)}\,.

In other words, position
-m/2\leq x\leq m/2
is represented by the uniform superposition over strings
y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
with
|\sigma(y)|=x+m/2
.

We may also consider a hash function
\pi:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
for some
n<m
. We can again build a discrete harmonic oscillator state whenever it is supported on inputs to
\pi
that have no collisions. A natural way to do this is with a linear code:

Claim 30
.

Fix a positive even
m
and smaller positive integer
n
. Consider a linear code with parity check matrix
B^{T}:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and distance
d
. Then it is possible to embed the first
\lfloor d/2\rfloor-1
eigenstates of a discrete quantum harmonic oscillator with the form
Eq.

11
.

Proof.

We again start from
\mathbf{H}_{m/2}
in
28
. Set
\pi=B^{T}
. We use the form of the oscillator eigenstate from
Eq.

11
:

\ket{\lambda_k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}H_{2}^{\otimes n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{{m\choose k}}}\sum_{z\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m};|z|=k}\ket{\pi(z)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}{m\choose k}}}\sum_{z\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m};|z|=k}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{y\cdot B^{T}z}\ket{y}\,.

Note that
\sigma=B
has the property that
By\cdot z=y\cdot B^{T}z
. So this state can be written as

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}{m\choose k}}}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}K_{k}(|By|)\ket{y}\,.

These states are orthogonal whenever the states
\sum_{z\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m};|z|=k}\ket{\pi(z)}
are orthogonal. This holds for all
0\leq k<d^{\perp}/2
, since in this region,
\pi(z)=B^{T}z
can be uniquely decoded to
z
.
We then embed
\mathbf{H}_{m/2}
so that the low-energy eigenstates are
\{\ket{\lambda_0},\dots,\ket{\lambda_{\lfloor d^\perp/2\rfloor- 1}}\}
; the higher-energy eigenstates can be chosen arbitrarily.
∎

In
30
, we again embed low-energy states of a discrete harmonic oscillator in a quantum computer.
This time, the states themselves encode information: the “position” basis approximately encodes the satisfying fraction of the problem
Bx=\vec{1}
. As a result,
sampling
from states in this harmonic oscillator allows one to find decent approximate solutions to this problem. We explore this idea throughout the rest of the section.

First suppose
\pi
was invertible. Then
30
represents position
-m/2\leq x\leq m/2
with the uniform superposition over bitstrings

where

\ket{D'_{x+m/2}}\propto\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m};|By|=x+m/2}\ket{y}\,.

The reader can verify that

has eigenvalue

with the following operator:

\hat{x}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(-1)^{(Bx)_{i}}\outerproduct{x}{x}\,.

In our case,
\pi
is only invertible when the input has Hamming weight below
d/2
. However, the effect of
\hat{x}
on our eigenstates is still easy to describe, in fact matching the effect of an operator in a bosonic system:

Lemma 31
.

Let
\{\ket{\lambda_i}\}
be the harmonic oscillator states from
30
with parity check matrix
B^{T}:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and distance

We prove

. We use this to prepare states that are near-eigenvectors of

Claim 32
.

Let

We rely on

\displaystyle c_{\ell+1}\cdot\left(2\ket{\psi}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{r-1}}\ket{\lambda_{\ell}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{r-1}}\ket{\lambda_{\ell+r}}\right)\approx_{r\to\infty}2c_{\ell+1}\ket{\psi}\,.

∎

.

Consider a linear code with parity check matrix

It is now clear how the harmonic oscillator states

can encode information. For example, in DQI, they encode good approximate solutions to the satisfiability problem

.
Even though the obfuscated harmonic oscillator can be analyzed classically, preparing states of this oscillator (which we use to sample) is only possible quantumly. In a quantum computer, this can be done by coherently error-correcting in the Fourier basis. It is unclear how to dequantize this idea.

Our discussion thus far is restricted to

and binary codes. But all of our claims generalize to arbitrary

.

5.3
How common are the solutions output by DQI?

We conclude this section by proving that the subset of codewords with solution quality matching DQI is exponentially large, yet this subset is an exponentially small fraction of codewords.

Theorem

implies that the optimal DQI state is concentrated on the subset of codewords at “semicircle law” distance from a known vector
v
.
In fact, it shows how to construct a DQI state concentrated on the codewords with distance

\frac{m}{2}-(1\pm o(1))\sqrt{j(m-j)}\,,

to
v
, for any
\omega(1)\leq j\leq k
. Exchanging

with
\vec{1}-v
, we can also isolate the codewords at distance

\frac{m}{2}\bm{+}(1\pm o(1))\sqrt{j(m-j)}\,.

The existence of codewords at this distance can also be proven using the ideas of
[
Tie90
]
; see

. But it is not known how to classically
find
these codewords. See a cartoon in

.

We first show there exist

This implies that DQI finds approximate solutions to the

To prove this, we rely on a classic bound in coding theory, called the Hamming bound:

Fact 34
.

The number of codewords in a binary code with distance
d
and block length
m
is at most

\frac{2^{m}}{\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor}{m\choose i}}\,.

Claim 35
.

Fix a code
\{Bx\ |\ x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\}
with dual distance
d^{\perp}
and vector
v\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
. For every
\ell<d^{\perp}/2
, in the asymptotic
m\to\infty
limit where
\frac{\ell}{m}<\frac{1}{2}
is held constant, there are an exponential number of codewords at distance
\frac{m}{2}-(1\pm o(1))\sqrt{\ell(m-\ell)}
to
v
.

Proof.

We prove the case when
v=0
; the case for arbitrary
v
is essentially the same.
Consider the state
\ket{\psi}
in
32
. This is asymptotically concentrated on bitstrings
x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
that satisfy
|Bx|=\frac{m}{2}+(1\pm o(1))\cdot\sqrt{\ell(m-\ell)}
.

On the other hand, the state
\ket{\psi}
is composed of eigenstates of the form in

. As a result, the maximum squared amplitude on a bitstring

is at most

\left|\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}{m\choose k}}}K_{k}(|Bx|)\right|^{2}\leq\frac{\ell^{2}}{2^{n}{m\choose k}}K_{k}(|Bx|)^{2}\,.

The Kravchuk polynomials are orthogonal polynomials associated with the binomial distribution.
By
18
,
K_{k}(q;m)^{2}\leq 2^{m}{m\choose k}/{m\choose q}
for any
0\leq q\leq m
. So the probability of observing

is

\leq\ell^{2}\cdot\frac{2^{m-n}}{{m\choose|Bx|}}\,.

The dual code
\{y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}\ |\ B^{T}y=0\}
has
2^{m-n}
codewords and distance
d^{\perp}
. By the Hamming bound (
34
), we have
2^{m-n}\leq 2^{m}/{m\choose\lfloor(d^{\perp}-1)/2\rfloor}
. So the probability of observing
x
is

\leq\ell^{2}\cdot\frac{2^{m}}{{m\choose\lfloor(d^{\perp}-1)/2\rfloor}\cdot{m\choose|Bx|}}\,.

We next apply a Stirling approximation for the binomial coefficient
[

]
. For all
0\leq c\leq 1
,

\log{m \choose c \cdot m}=m\cdot h(c)\pm O(\log m)\,,

where
h(x)=-x\log x-(1-x)\log(1-x)
is the binary entropy function. So the probability of outcome

is at most a polynomial in
m
times

\displaystyle 2^{m(1-h(d^{\perp}/2m)-h(|Bx|/m))}\,.

Suppose
-\sqrt{\ell(m-\ell)}\leq|Bx|-\frac{m}{2}\leq\sqrt{\ell(m-\ell)}

for some

. Then the probability of outcome

is at most an exponential in

1-h(d^{\perp}/2m)-h(1/2+\sqrt{c(1-c)})\leq 1-h(c)-h(1/2-\sqrt{c(1-c)})\,.

One can

. Then the probability of observing

is at most

for some

.

In
\ket{\psi}
,

of the probability is on bitstrings
x
where
|Bx|=\frac{m}{2}+(1\pm o(1))\sqrt{\ell(m-\ell)}
. Then there must be at least
2^{am}
such bitstrings for some
a>0
.
∎

35
implies that the optimal DQI state is essentially supported on an exponentially large number of bitstrings, each with exponentially small amplitude. This prevents classical algorithms that find
peaked

For nontrivial applications of DQI, the fraction of codewords at the semicircle law distance is exponentially small:

6
Outlook

Outside of the oracle setting, it is hard to justify that a quantum algorithm solves a problem faster than any classical algorithm.
For example, consider Shor’s algorithm: the best evidence that discrete log is not in
{\mathsf{BPP}}
is the widespread use of RSA.
Nonetheless, we wish to understand the principles behind algorithms that seemingly offer quantum advantage.

Towards that end, in this work we gave evidence that
simulating
the DQI algorithm is classically hard, despite its lack of a quantum-supremacy-style argument.
We have shown how DQI hides the subset of codewords at the semicircle law distance; just as in Shor’s algorithm, this is an exponentially large set, but an exponentially small fraction of outcomes.
We suggest that DQI and Shor’s algorithm have a stronger connection than is currently known. Perhaps there is an interesting quantum circuit framework that unifies these algorithms while also contained in
{\mathsf{BPP}}^{{\mathsf{NP}}}
.

We do not claim that the
optimization
problem DQI solves is classically hard.
For example, it is not even clear if the solutions output by DQI are locally optimal.
2
2

2

It is possible that solutions output by DQI could be improved with a
local update
: if changing a bit improves the solution, change this bit with some probability. For example, it is possible to locally improve on the Goemans-Williamson algorithm for Maximum Cut when the input is a random graph
[
Has21
]
.

However, if a classical algorithm can outperform DQI, we suggest that it cannot
directly
simulate DQI. This is in contrast with some proposals for quantum machine learning, which were dequantized by classically simulating the algorithm
[
Tan19
,
CGL+20
]
.

We also note the similarity to another quantum algorithm for approximately solving optimization problems, namely the QAOA
[
FGG14
]
. Although the QAOA can create distributions that are impossible to classically sample from (assuming the
{\mathsf{PH}}
does not collapse)
[
FH16
]
, we still do not know whether it offers quantum advantage
for optimization problems
.
Nonetheless, the QAOA’s claims of quantum advantage (e.g.
[
FGG14a
,
BFMVZ22
,
FGRV25
]
) have spurred many improvements in classical optimization algorithms (e.g.
[
BMO+15
,
Has19
,
CHM23
]
).
We are hopeful that DQI can present a similar challenge in classical algorithms research.

We conclude with some direct questions about quantum advantage inspired by our findings:

1.

We have shown how DQI implements a known existential bound using a Fourier transform. Do such bounds occur elsewhere in coding theory; if so, can they be made into a quantum algorithm? Can one construct DQI-like algorithms that implement more general transforms, such as transforms associated with representations of non-abelian groups
[
LH25
,
BNZ25
]
?

2.

We have shown how a quantum computer can contain information in the states of a harmonic oscillator. In DQI, this information is a specific subset of codewords. Can other information, perhaps beyond coding theory, be stored in a harmonic oscillator? Can one use this framework to learn properties of a known hash function that are hard to learn classically?

3.

We can understand DQI as finding a hidden subset of codewords with some linear-algebraic structure. Is there a
group
we can associate with this structure, perhaps related to the Kravchuk polynomials
[
Dun76
]
? In general, what minimal properties are required in a subset
S
for a quantum computer to successfully locate it?

Appendix B
Finding peaked elements of a DQI state

Suppose we wish to approximate a distribution induced by sampling a state
\ket{\phi}
in the computational basis. If
\ket{\phi}

is at least an inverse polynomial. We show this is possible for states prepared by the DQI algorithm. This does not necessarily imply that sampling from

[

SN13
]

We follow the presentation in
[

]

]

We first define the concept of

[

]

Definition 41

\ket{\psi'_P}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}H^{\otimes n}\ket{\psi_P}

[

]
showed that the inner product of two

]

For us, it remains to be shown that the Hadamard transform of the DQI state

We prove the Max-XORSAT case, since the analysis is easier to follow, but this is also true for Max-LINSAT.

Appendix C
Computing the output probabilities of Shor’s algorithm

We briefly explain how Shor’s algorithm for discrete logarithm prepares a state of the form

\sum_{x}w(x)\ket{x}\,,

where
|w|^{2}
is efficiently computable. By
Theorem

2
, it can be simulated in
{\mathsf{BPP}}^{{\mathsf{NP}}}
.

In the discrete logarithm problem, we are given an element
x
of a cyclic group
G=\langle g\rangle
, and we wish to calculate the smallest integer
\alpha
such that
g^{\alpha}=x
. We denote
\log_{g}x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\alpha
. Suppose we know
N
is the order of
G
; i.e.
G=\{1,g,g^{2},\dots,g^{N-1}\}
. In general
N
is exponential in the system size.

Shor’s algorithm (see
[
Chi17
]
for a review) prepares the state

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\delta,\nu\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}\omega_{N}^{\nu\delta}\ket{\nu\log_g x, \nu, g^\delta}\,.

Suppose we are given the outcome
\ket{a,b,c}
. Then the probability of observing this outcome is nonzero if and only if
a=b\cdot\log_{g}x
, i.e.

g^{a}=x^{b}\,.

There are
N^{3}
possible outcomes
\ket{a,b,c}
, so the probability of any nonzero outcome is exactly
\frac{1}{N^{2}}
. Then the squared amplitude
|w|^{2}
is efficiently computable:

|w(a,b,c)|^{2}=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{g^{a}=x^{b}}\,.

Note that this value is much larger than the average probability
\frac{1}{N^{3}}
, but much smaller than any inverse polynomial in system size.
