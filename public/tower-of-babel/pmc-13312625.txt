Application of a YOLOv8-based model on lateral lumbar radiographs for screening of acute and chronic thoracolumbar fractures and osteoporosis
Authors: Baisen Chen, Yukang Cheng, Jiaming Cui, Chunyan Ji, Yuyu Sun, Zhiming Cui, Guanhua Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDifferentiating acute from chronic wedge-shaped thoracolumbar vertebral deformities on conventional lateral lumbar radiographs remains clinically challenging, especially when osteoporosis status also needs to be considered. This study aimed to develop and evaluate a You Only Look Once (YOLO)v8n framework for vertebral-level detection and classification of thoracolumbar fractures with osteoporosis-related stratification on lateral lumbar radiographs.MethodsWe retrospectively collected 1352 lateral lumbar radiographs from 1352 patients, with one radiograph per patient. A total of 1774 vertebral fracture segments were manually annotated. Lumbar magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) were used as reference standards to stratify vertebral targets into three categories: acute fracture with osteoporosis, acute fracture without osteoporosis and chronic fracture with osteoporosis. The dataset was divided into training and validation subsets at the patient level. A YOLOv8n detector was trained as the primary model. To strengthen methodological rigor, additional baseline comparison experiments were conducted under the same patient-level training/validation split using YOLOv5n and Faster R-CNN. Detection performance was assessed using precision, recall, F1-score, mean average precision (mAP) 50 and mAP50-95.ResultsOn the validation set, the YOLOv8n model achieved a precision of 0.495, recall of 0.482, F1-score of 0.490, mAP50 of 0.506, and mAP50-95 of 0.397. In comparative experiments, YOLOv5n achieved a precision of 0.451, recall of 0.549, F1-score of 0.495, mAP50 of 0.494, and mAP50-95 of 0.367, whereas Faster R-CNN achieved a precision of 0.273, recall of 0.814, F1-score of 0.409, mAP50 of 0.300, and mAP50-95 of 0.217. These findings indicate that YOLOv8n provided the most balanced overall detection performance in the present dataset.ConclusionThe proposed YOLOv8n framework demonstrated preliminary feasibility for automated vertebral-level detection and classification of thoracolumbar fractures with osteoporosis-related stratification on lateral lumbar radiographs. However, given the moderate overall performance and lack of external validation, the current model should be regarded as an assistive screening tool rather than a standalone diagnostic system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-09845-3.Keywords: Acute and chronic fracture, YOLOv8n, Lateral lumbar radiographs, Osteoporosis

Introduction

‌Thoracolumbar fractures refer to bony disruptions occurring between the thoracic and lumbar segments of the spine and are commonly caused by trauma, osteoporosis, or pathological alterations [1–3]. This condition often results in severe pain, restricted mobility, and neurological impairment, significantly compromising patients’ quality of life [4, 5]. Thoracolumbar fractures have become increasingly common in recent years, mainly due to the rising incidence of traffic accidents and the aging population [6, 7], thereby imposing a substantial socioeconomic burden. Accordingly, accurate identification of fracture types and timely assessment of bone mass density (BMD) have become key research priorities in the field of spinal surgery.

Currently, clinical evaluation of fracture chronicity and underlying osteoporosis largely relies on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA). In addition to its established role in identifying bone marrow edema and soft tissue abnormalities, MRI has recently been recognized as a potential opportunistic tool for assessing vertebral bone quality, osteoporosis-related changes, and vertebral fracture risk [8–10]. DXA quantitatively assesses BMD and remains the clinical reference standard for osteoporosis evaluation. However, both modalities are costly, time-consuming, and often unavailable in primary healthcare settings or for large-scale screening [11, 12]. Although computed tomography (CT) can reveal cortical discontinuity, vertebral height loss, and fracture fragments with high spatial resolution, it also fails to reliably distinguish between acute and chronic fractures in the absence of classic fracture lines or remodeling signs [13]. In comparison, X-ray is still widely used for initial screening because it is both convenient and cost-effective. Lateral lumbar radiographs can provide basic yet valuable information about the location, type, and severity of fractures [14]. However, interpretation of lateral lumbar radiographs is inherently challenging because of overlapping anatomical structures, variability in patient positioning, and heterogeneity in exposure conditions, all of which may affect both human interpretation and diagnosis results. Based on these observations, we hypothesize that automated analysis of lateral lumbar radiographs could localize wedge deformities and classify the chronicity of thoracolumbar fractures as well as the presence of osteoporosis. By using this strategy, early clinical decisions can be supported, and the risk of complications may be minimized.

Recent advancements in AI and image recognition have greatly improved the ability to automatically detect osteoporosis and vertebral fractures in spinal radiographs. These technologies are progressively evolving into effective tools for screening and risk assessment [15, 16]. For instance, a study by Namki Hong et al. demonstrated that integrating lateral lumbar radiographs with deep learning algorithms can enhance the diagnostic accuracy of conventional radiographs in detecting both vertebral fractures and osteoporosis [17]. Furthermore, Zhang Jun and colleagues created a radiological model using deep learning that can predict the severity of osteoporotic vertebral fractures (OVFs) from X-ray images [18]. These findings suggest broad prospects for combining X-ray imaging with deep learning in fracture severity assessment and automated osteoporosis identification.

In parallel with these advances, a variety of deep learning architectures have been applied to medical image detection and classification tasks. Broadly, these approaches include one-stage detectors, such as the You Only Look Once (YOLO) family [19, 20] and RetinaNet. Among its iterations, YOLOv8 is a classic version, capable not only of object detection but also of image classification and instance segmentation. The classic two-stage detector is Faster R- convolutional neural network (CNN) [21]. One-stage detectors are generally favored for computational efficiency and real-time deployment, whereas two-stage detectors may provide stronger region proposal mechanisms at the cost of higher computational burden. In musculoskeletal imaging, these architectures have shown promise for lesion detection, fracture identification, and opportunistic screening tasks [22, 23], although direct vertebral-level comparisons across frameworks remain limited. In the specific setting of thoracolumbar fracture detection on lateral lumbar radiographs, evidence comparing competing object-detection architectures remains insufficient. Therefore, it is important not only to evaluate the feasibility of a YOLOv8n detector, but also to contextualize its performance relative to representative alternative detection frameworks.

By combining lumbar MRI and DXA findings to accurately annotate lateral lumbar radiographs, we developed a YOLOv8n model for vertebral-level detection and classification of thoracolumbar fractures with osteoporosis-related stratification. In addition, to better contextualize the performance of the proposed framework, we conducted comparative baseline experiments using representative one-stage and two-stage detection models under the same patient-level training/validation setting.

Materials and methods

This research received approval from the Ethics Committee of Nantong First People’s Hospital (Approval No. 2026-KT042-02). Informed consent was not required due to the study’s retrospective design.

All human participant procedures adhered to the ethical guidelines of the institutional research committee and the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki, including its subsequent updates. Study cohort

This retrospective study included patients identified from a pre-screened retrospective imaging cohort for detailed eligibility assessment between January 2021 and August 2025. Inclusions were based on the following criteria: (1) completion of lateral lumbar radiographs, lumbar MRI, and DXA within one week at our hospital; (2) age ≥ 18 years old. Exclusion criteria included: (1) required imaging/data incomplete; (2) presence of vertebral metastases or spinal infections; (3) poor image quality or blurred imaging; (4) inability to segment the region of interest (ROI) effectively. The application of the inclusion and exclusion criteria was performed by two investigators with relevant clinical experience. Case screening was based on both chart review and image review, including assessment of clinical records, lateral lumbar radiographs, lumbar MRI, and DXA findings. In cases of uncertainty or disagreement, the two investigators jointly reviewed the case and reached a consensus regarding final inclusion or exclusion.

Data acquisition

All lateral lumbar radiographs were obtained using a digital radiography (DR) system (uDR 780i, United Imaging, Shanghai, China). Thoracolumbar MRI were performed on a 3.0T Magnetom Verio MRI scanner (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). Thoracolumbar MRI was performed using a standard clinical protocol that included sagittal T1-weighted turbo spin-echo (TSE), axial T2-weighted TSE, and sagittal T2-weighted Dixon sequences with water-only and in-phase reconstructions. These MRI sequences were reviewed as part of the reference evaluation for fracture classification.

BMD measurements were acquired using a DXA scanner (Discovery Wi, Hologic Inc., Boston, MA, USA).

All imaging examinations were performed by experienced radiologic technologists according to standardized clinical protocols. All images were acquired in original resolution and 16-bit grayscale depth, saved in both DICOM and JPG formats. Moreover, information on age, sex, and body mass index (BMI) was collected as part of the clinical data.

The present study used a single-center retrospective dataset. Model development and evaluation were conducted using an internal data-partitioning strategy, with separate training and validation subsets derived from the same institution. Model performance was assessed on the internal validation set. No independent external validation dataset was included in the current study.

Image processing

To improve model generalizability, data augmentation methods such as translation, scaling, and flipping were used during preprocessing. Two spine surgeons with clinical expertise independently annotated acute and chronic vertebral fractures on lateral lumbar radiographs. The preliminary annotations were cross-validated against the corresponding lumbar MRI and DXA findings to classify fracture types. Fracture classification was performed at the vertebral level rather than the patient level. Each annotated vertebral fracture segment was assigned to one of three mutually exclusive categories: acute fracture with osteoporosis, acute fracture without osteoporosis, or chronic fracture with osteoporosis. Acute versus chronic status was determined based on the corresponding MRI findings, while osteoporosis status was determined according to DXA results and established diagnostic criteria. These group definitions were used consistently throughout model training, validation, and performance analysis.

MRI was not used as an input to the YOLOv8 model itself, which was trained only on lateral lumbar radiographs. Instead, MRI served as an important reference during case verification, vertebral-level annotation, and ground-truth determination. Acute fractures were supported by bone marrow edema-related signal changes, characterized by decreased signal intensity on sagittal T1-weighted images and increased signal intensity on the water-only reconstruction of the sagittal T2-weighted Dixon sequence. In contrast, chronic fractures were identified by the absence of edema-related hyperintensity on fluid-sensitive fat-suppressed images, together with chronic vertebral deformity on radiographs and MRI. After independent annotation, the two clinicians jointly reviewed all annotated regions of interest (ROIs), and any discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussion. The final consensus annotations were then used as the ground-truth labels for model training and evaluation.

According to diagnostic guidelines from the World Health Organization (WHO) and the International Society for Clinical Densitometry (ISCD) [24], postmenopausal women and men aged 50 years or older were assessed using T-scores, whereas Z-scores were used for premenopausal women and men younger than 50 years. Patients with fragility fractures caused by low-energy trauma were also classified into the osteoporosis group. Fracture annotation and classification were performed at the vertebral level rather than the patient level. Since some patients presented with more than one fractured vertebral segment on a single lateral lumbar radiograph, vertebral segments were treated as independent detection targets for model training and evaluation. In total, 1352 patients contributed 1352 radiographs, from which 1774 vertebral fracture segments were annotated. Importantly, although the labels were defined at the vertebral level, dataset partitioning was performed at the patient level to avoid data leakage. All radiographs and corresponding annotated vertebral segments from the same patient were assigned exclusively to either the training or validation subset. The annotated ROIs were jointly reviewed and finalized by both clinicians, and subsequently used as labeled targets in the training dataset for thoracolumbar fracture detection.

YOLOv8 network and training parameters

The YOLOv8n variant was selected as the primary model because its lightweight architecture was considered more suitable for the present dataset size and available computational resources. Compared with larger YOLOv8 variants, YOLOv8n offers reduced memory consumption and faster training/inference, making it a practical starting point for an exploratory vertebral-level detection study. The goal of the present work was to establish initial feasibility rather than to perform exhaustive optimization across all YOLOv8 model scales.

Training configurations included: initial learning rate: 0.001; optimizer: stochastic gradient descent (SGD); image size: resized to 640 × 640 pixels; batch size: 32; number of epochs: 50. To enhance generalization, various augmentation strategies were implemented, including random scaling (0.8×to1.2×), rotation (− 90° to + 90°), cropping, horizontal and vertical flipping, and mosaic augmentation. No additional explicit class-imbalance handling strategy, such as weighted loss functions, focal loss, oversampling, or class-balanced sampling, was applied in the original training process. Instead, the model was trained using the default YOLOv8 loss framework together with standard data augmentation techniques.

Model performance stabilized after approximately 40 epochs, with no further significant gains observed. The training parameters were based on the default YOLOv8 configuration, optimized according to the GPU processing capacity and memory performance.

Comparative baseline experiments

To strengthen the methodological rigor of the study, additional baseline comparison experiments were performed under the same patient-level training/validation split and label definitions. YOLOv5n was selected as a representative lightweight one-stage detector, and Faster R-CNN was selected as a representative two-stage detector. These baseline models were trained and evaluated using the same dataset partitioning strategy as the proposed YOLOv8n model.

The purpose of these comparative experiments was not to exhaustively optimize each alternative architecture, but rather to provide a clinically relevant methodological reference for interpreting the relative performance of the proposed YOLOv8n framework in the present dataset.

Performance evaluation

The detection performance of the model was evaluated using precision, recall, F1 score and mean average precision (mAP). The mAP metric reflects the model’s capability in both object localization and classification of vertebral fractures. In this study, two commonly used thresholds were applied: mAP50, which represents the average precision at an intersection over union (IoU) threshold of 0.5, and mAP50–95, which calculates the average precision across multiple IoU thresholds from 0.5 to 0.95 with an interval of 0.05.

Precision describes the fraction of true positive predictions among all predicted positives, while recall represents the fraction of true positive detections among all ground-truth positive samples. mAP50 offers a relatively lenient evaluation, whereas mAP50-95 serves as a more stringent metric, capturing the model’s localization performance across varying degrees of overlap—especially suitable for high-precision detection tasks.

In addition to standard object-detection metrics, a supplementary one-versus-rest analysis based on the validation-set confusion matrix was performed for each fracture category to estimate sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value. These supplementary metrics were provided to improve the clinical interpretability of model performance.

Computer parameters

The experiments were performed on a system equipped with a 13th-generation Intel Core i9-13980HX processor (base frequency: 2.20 GHz) running Windows 11 Home Edition. An NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060 Laptop GPU was used to speed up deep learning computations. Model training and evaluation were implemented using the PyTorch 2.0 framework.

Performance of the model

The dataset was divided into training and validation sets at a ratio of 8:2 at the patient level. Specifically, the training set included 1082 patients and 1082 radiographs, whereas the validation set included 270 patients and 270 radiographs. No patient appeared in both subsets. A total of 1774 annotated vertebral fracture segments were included in the final analysis. Because some radiographs contained more than one fractured vertebral segment, the number of annotated detection targets exceeded the number of radiographs.

Class distribution analysis revealed a moderate imbalance, with acute fractures with osteoporosis accounting for 1015 of 1774 annotated vertebral segments (57.2%), acute fractures without osteoporosis accounting for 478 segments (27.0%), and chronic fractures with osteoporosis accounting for 281 segments (15.8%).

On the validation set, the model achieved a precision of 0.495, recall of 0.482, and an F1 score of 0.49. The mAP50 reached 0.506, while the stricter mAP50–95 was 0.397.

To improve the clinical interpretability of the validation-set results, supplementary one-versus-rest analysis of the class confusion matrix was performed. For acute fracture with osteoporosis, the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value were 0.525, 0.708, 0.571 and 0.669, respectively. For chronic fracture with osteoporosis, the corresponding values were 0.297, 0.850, 0.145 and 0.934, respectively. For acute fracture without osteoporosis, they were 0.518, 0.833, 0.492 and 0.847, respectively.

The Precision/Recall (PR) and Precision/Confidence (PC) curve demonstrated stable convergence, and the loss curves showed consistent decline without evident overfitting. The training curves of box loss, classification loss, and distribution focal loss gradually decreased, while mAP values increased steadily, indicating stable convergence of the model (Figs. 4 and 5).

Discussion

This study explored the feasibility of applying the single-stage architecture and multi-scale detection capabilities of the YOLOv8 model [25] to vertebral-level detection and classification of acute and chronic thoracolumbar fractures with osteoporosis-related stratification on lateral lumbar radiographs. The results suggest that the model was able to identify fracture-related patterns to a certain extent; however, its overall performance remained moderate. Therefore, the current approach should be regarded as a preliminary assistive tool rather than a reliable standalone screening system. Further refinement and validation are still needed before broader clinical application can be considered. In addition, the practical deployment of such a model in real-world clinical workflows would require further evaluation of user acceptance, medicolegal responsibility, and integration with existing hospital imaging systems such as picture archiving and communication system (PACS).

While DXA is the gold standard for diagnosing osteoporosis, its limited accessibility in numerous clinical environments hinders broad implementation. Consequently, there is growing interest in exploring alternative imaging techniques such as X-ray, CT, or MRI to facilitate preliminary assessment of BMD. Zhang Bing et al. developed a deep CNN model based on lumbar spine radiographs, showing significant promise in detecting osteoporosis and osteopenia [26]. Subsequent studies have also shown that CNNs using CT or MRI data can forecast bone loss and osteoporosis with favourable performance outcomes [27, 28]. Additionally, CNN models have been successfully used to differentiate benign from malignant vertebral fractures using X-rays [17] and to distinguish acute from chronic thoracolumbar fractures based on CT images [13]. These findings collectively indicate that integrating deep learning with medical imaging can support the diagnosis of both fracture chronicity and osteoporosis. However, few existing studies have focused specifically on the automated detection and classification of acute versus chronic thoracolumbar fractures using a combined approach. Furthermore, early identification of osteoporosis plays a critical role in individualized treatment planning.

To this end, YOLOv8 was employed as the object detection model in this research. Its core architecture features a highly efficient feature extraction network capable of capturing essential visual cues and contextual information, thereby enhancing detection accuracy. The model incorporates a multi-level feature fusion strategy that improves recognition of targets across different spatial scales while increasing overall stability and generalizability. In addition, the use of a multi-task joint loss function enables simultaneous optimization of object localization, class prediction, and confidence estimation, thereby enhancing training precision and boosting overall detection performance. Of particular note, the single-layer detection architecture of YOLOv8 excels at identifying small targets, making it especially suitable for precise localization of lumbar vertebral structures.

Importantly, the overall model performance should be interpreted cautiously. In this study, the model achieved an mAP50 of 0.506, an mAP50–95 of 0.397, and an F1 score of 0.49, which indicate only moderate discrimination and localization ability. Baseline comparison further supported the relative advantage of the proposed YOLOv8n framework in the present dataset. Although Faster R-CNN achieved relatively high recall, its substantially lower precision and mAP values suggested a tendency toward excessive false-positive detections. In contrast, YOLOv8n achieved a more balanced trade-off between sensitivity and precision while maintaining superior overall localization performance. However, from a clinical perspective, this level of accuracy is not yet sufficient for independent diagnostic use or for reliable population-level screening. Instead, the present model may be more appropriately positioned as an auxiliary tool for preliminary triage, risk alerting, or decision support, particularly in settings where MRI or DXA is not readily available. Therefore, final clinical judgment should still rely on physician interpretation in conjunction with standard imaging and clinical assessment.

It is noteworthy that class imbalance may have affected the model’s performance. In the present dataset, acute fractures with osteoporosis accounted for 57.2% of all annotated vertebral segments, chronic fractures with osteoporosis for 15.8%, and acute fractures without osteoporosis for 27.0%. No additional explicit class-balancing strategy, such as weighted loss, focal loss, oversampling, or class-balanced sampling, was applied in the original training process. Therefore, the relatively limited representation of minority classes may have contributed to reduced detection performance in these categories. Future studies incorporating larger and more balanced datasets, as well as dedicated imbalance-mitigation strategies, may further improve classification robustness and overall model performance. In addition to sample imbalance, the lower performance in chronic fractures with osteoporosis may also reflect the morphological overlap between chronic vertebral deformity and degenerative changes on lateral radiographs. Likewise, acute fractures without osteoporosis may present relatively subtle structural alterations, making them more difficult to distinguish from mild non-fracture deformities or normal variants.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study used a single-center retrospective dataset and relied on an internal validation strategy with training and validation sets derived from the same institution. No independent external validation dataset was included. Therefore, the generalizability of the model to other hospitals, imaging devices, patient populations, and clinical workflows remains uncertain. Future multicenter studies with larger and more diverse cohorts, together with independent external testing datasets, are needed to further evaluate the robustness and clinical applicability of the proposed model. Second, the present work was designed primarily as a vertebral-level YOLOv8n object-detection study rather than a patient-level reader-comparison study. Thus, although standard detection metrics and supplementary confusion-matrix-based analyses were provided, direct comparison with radiologist interpretation and formal ROC/AUC-based evaluation were not included in the current study design. Third, although final labels were established through independent annotation and consensus review by two spine surgeons, formal inter-observer agreement metrics were not calculated. Future studies should address these issues through external validation, reader-comparison experiments, and quantitative assessment of annotation consistency. Despite these limitations, the study demonstrates the preliminary feasibility of automated vertebral-level detection and classification of thoracolumbar fractures and osteoporosis-related categories using a YOLOv8n framework.

In conclusion, the proposed YOLOv8n model provides preliminary evidence that automated vertebral-level detection and classification of thoracolumbar fractures with osteoporosis-related stratification on lateral lumbar radiographs is feasible. In comparative baseline experiments, YOLOv8n demonstrated more balanced overall performance than the tested YOLOv5n and Faster R-CNN baselines in the present dataset. Nevertheless, because the overall performance remains moderate and no external validation was performed, the current model should be regarded as an assistive screening tool rather than a clinically deployable standalone diagnostic system. Future studies with larger, multicenter, externally validated datasets and broader benchmark comparisons are warranted.
