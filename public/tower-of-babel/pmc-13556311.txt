Pathways of Patients With Neurological Emergencies at Pediatric Emergency Department: A Process‐Based Data Mining Study
Authors: Henna Aro, Tuomo Antikainen, Mari Videman, Elina Wärnhjelm, Erika Ignatius, Pirjo Isohanni, Miika Koskinen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundNeurological patients represent a small yet resource‐demanding group in pediatric emergency departments (PEDs). Their pathways and resource needs remain poorly understood. This population‐based real‐world data study aims to bridge this gap.MethodsWe analyzed neurological visits from 2021 to 2023 at the sole PED serving 300,000 children using process mining and statistical analysis. Neurological visits included patients triaged for neurologist evaluation and those with neurological complaints and/or diagnosis.ResultsWe identified 4854 neurological visits, constituting 5% of PED visits. Seizure and headache comprised 64% of the chief complaints; seizures dominated the highest acuity visits, while headache was linked to visits with lower acuity. Neurological patients had a nearly fivefold increased risk of requiring immediate lifesaving interventions compared to non‐neurological patients. Half of the highest acuity patients were 1–5 years old. Patient disposition was most strongly associated with acuity: 81% of highest acuity patients were admitted and 15% of them to the pediatric intensive care or surgical unit, whereas only 18% of stable patients were hospitalized. Variability of patient pathways was moderate. 87% of patients adhered to one of four most common pathways where disposition decision was made without advanced testing. Over 30 pathways were identified for patients needing advanced tests. These patients presented with higher acuity, non‐seizure complaints and increased admissions.ConclusionThis population‐based study pioneers process mining for analyzing patient care processes and resource utilization of neurological patients at the PED. It highlights the critical nature of pediatric neurological emergencies and uncovers the variability of patient needs and pathways.

Introduction

Pediatric emergency departments (PEDs) face increasing overcrowding worldwide [1, 2]. Neurological complaints account for 2%–12% of visits [3, 4, 5, 6] and often present with high acuity and resource‐intensive demands [6, 7, 8]. While some recent studies have assessed these patients [4, 5, 6, 7, 9] knowledge of their pathways and required resources remains limited. A more comprehensive understanding of the clinical burden pediatric neurological patients present to the PED is needed to guide limited resources and improve care delivery for diverse patient populations.

Real‐world data (RWD) from Hospital Information Systems (HIS), including Electronic Medical Records (EMR), captures the complexity of clinical practice [10, 11, 12]. Process mining is an analysis framework to discover, monitor, and enhance production processes. In healthcare, it enables optimization of patient pathways and resource use by revealing inefficiencies and variation in care [10, 11, 12, 13]. Uncovering patterns in patient flow supports the development of standardized, evidence‐based care pathways, improving quality, efficiency, and patient outcomes [14, 15, 16]. Despite its potential, process mining remains underused in the PED setting [17, 18, 19, 20, 21].

Here we utilize large scale clinical data and process mining tools to assess the patient characteristics; identify the most common patient pathways and their variability; and inspect the factors associated with resource needs and hospital admission of neurological patients at a large tertiary PED.

Materials and Methods

Study Setting and Patient Selection

In this population‐based retrospective observational study we gathered data on all patients presenting to the HUS Helsinki University Hospital PED (HUS PED) between January 1st, 2021, and December 31st, 2023. Patients with a neurological condition requiring secondary or tertiary care were included in the study cohort, whereas non‐neurological pediatric patients served as a control group for outcome measures. Patients needing primary care were excluded from the cohort.

Finland's universal public healthcare system provides tertiary emergency and in‐hospital pediatric care only at public hospitals. HUS, the largest provider, serves about one‐third of Finnish children under the age of 16. At the end of 2023, 315,250 children under 16 years of age lived in the HUS area according to population data from Statistics Finland [22]. HUS operates the country's two largest in‐hospital PEDs: the New Children's Hospital PED, the region's sole provider of tertiary pediatric neurological care, and Jorvi Hospital PED, which offers primary and secondary care. Together, these form HUS PED, with approximately 70,000 annual primary, secondary, and tertiary level visits.

At HUS PED patients are triaged using the Emergency Severity Index (ESI) algorithm, which assesses acuity and anticipated resource needs as determined by a trained triage nurse [23]. A chief complaint is assigned using The International Classification of Primary Care, second edition (ICPC‐2) and patients are directed to the appropriate specialty. To capture all neurological patients, we included all patients triaged to neurology and identified additional cases seen by pediatricians using relevant ICPC‐2 and The International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD‐10) diagnosis codes. Data

Pseudonymized data was extracted from the HUS Data Lake, which integrates EMR with data from various HIS subsystems. While multiple datasets were retrieved, the key variables used in this study included demographics, visits, ESI levels, chief complaints, paraclinical tests, and patient disposition (discharged, ward admission, pediatric intensive care unit (PICU), or surgical unit). Patients transferred to the surgical unit were grouped with PICU admissions for analysis due to small counts and assumed similar care needs. ICD‐10 codes for visit and long‐term diagnoses supported cohort formation but were excluded from final analyses due to inconsistencies and missing data. File S1 presents collected data. Only aggregated data from non‐neurological patients were used in this study.

Patients were grouped into three categories based on the ICPC‐2 chief complaint assigned at triage. The “seizure” group includes patients presenting with seizures, convulsions, postictal states, or suspected epileptic events, as per the ICPC‐2 “seizure/convulsion” category. The second group comprises patients with headache, while the third includes all other complaints.

This register study was conducted under the permission of HUS (HUS/265/2023) in full compliance with the Finnish Act on the Secondary Use of Social and Health Data (552/2019). The data was handled in a secure, closed environment and results are presented in strict adherence to the reporting requirements outlined in the Act. The reporting of this study adheres to the STROBE guidelines for observational studies. No separate ethical approval was required for a retrospective observational study. During the preparation of this work the authors used generative AI tools (Chat GPT and Cloude AI) in order to improve readability and language. After using these tools, the authors reviewed and edited the content as needed and take full responsibility for the content of the publication.

Statistical Analysis and Process Mining

Categorical data are reported as counts and percentages. Age was recorded in full years. Median age and interquartile range (IQR) are reported, with between‐group comparisons assessed using the non‐parametric Mann–Whitney U test. Demographic distributions (sex, age‐ and language‐group) were benchmarked against the HUS catchment population (Statistics Finland [22]). We employed two‐sample proportion test to (i) compare subgroup representation across acuity, chief complaint and disposition groups compared to the rest of the cohort; and (ii) to examine sex differences in the proportions of presenting complaints within age groups. Univariate logistic regression with random intercept for repeat visits (reporting odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs)) was used to assess associations between patient characteristics, acuity level and advanced diagnostic testing. For rare outcomes or aggregated comparisons, χ 2 test was used (with ORs and 95% CIs reported where appropriate). Acuity level, diagnostic testing and disposition between neurological and non‐neurological patients were compared using aggregated data and χ 2 test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses refer to visits rather than unique patients. Disposition was modeled using mixed‐effects logistic regression with random intercepts for patients. Reference levels were chosen based on clinical relevance. Model fit was assessed using likelihood‐ratio tests, pseudo‐R 2, and area under the curve. 95% CIs were obtained using parametric bootstrap (n = 200). Statistical analyses were conducted using R versions 4.4.2 and 4.5.0.

Process mining was performed in ProM 6.12 software using the Directly‐Follows Miner (DFM) to generate process models for the full event log and chief‐complaint groups. DFM was selected for its ability to capture complex immediate relations with a simple algorithm. To address its limitations in modeling long‐term dependencies, the Filter Book option was applied to identify all unique patient pathways and their frequencies. Event data for process analysis included laboratory measurements, imaging modalities, and electroencephalogram (EEG); see File S1 for a more comprehensive list. To create an image of patient flows (Figure 3), we included acuity levels, all test modalities and their timing, and disposition. To streamline models, reduce noise, and clarify core patient pathways, we omitted triage classification, aggregated all laboratory measurements except cerebrospinal fluid analysis (CSF), excluded non‐neurological imaging and procedures, combined neuroimaging modalities as one event, and applied a fixed test order—laboratory → neuroimaging → EEG → CSF.

Results

Patient Characteristics, Chief Complaint, and Acuity

We identified a total of 4845 neurological visits for 3553 patients, representing 500 visits per 100,000 children per year. This constituted 5% of all PED visits at the HUS PED (including primary care). Revisits accounted for 27% of neurological visits, with 19% of the cohort having more than one visit over the three‐year period. There were 7% of patients with more than two visits, accounting for 22% of all visits, while 1% visited the PED more than five times. In 12% of visits, the patient had a referral, and 25% were brought in by the emergency medical services. Neurological patients were nearly five times more likely to be assigned ESI Level 1 than non‐neurological pediatric patients (4% vs. 1%, OR 4.7, 95% CI 3.97–5.54, p < 0.001). Although most neurological patients were allocated to ESI Level 3, 19% of all PED patients assigned to ESI Level 1 were neurological. Additionally, neurological patients were assigned to ESI levels 4–5 less frequently than non‐neurological patients (20% vs. 36%, OR 0.45, 95% CI 0.42–0.49, p < 0.001).

Half of ESI Level 1 patients were aged 1–5 years. They had a significantly higher risk of being assigned to ESI Level 1 compared to others (7% vs. 4%, OR = 2.51, 95% CI 1.89–3.32, p < 0.001, χ 2 test).

Patient Pathways, Advanced Testing, and Disposition

The pathways of neurological patients were varied as presented in Figure 3. In the initial process discovery, 1190 patient pathways were identified. Streamlined process mining revealed 36 pathways. The four most common—and simplest—pathways encompassed 87% of neurological visits with disposition decision based on clinical assessment with or without laboratory tests (excluding CSF). The remaining 13% followed 32 less common pathways with advanced testing (neuroimaging, EEG, and/or CSF). Figure 4 displays the most frequent pathways by chief complaint group. Patients with seizures followed one of the four most common pathways in 93% of visits, though 25 different paths were identified. Headache patients showed the least variability, with 20 total pathways—85% followed one of the top four paths. Patients with other complaints had the most variation: 30 pathways overall, with 83% on a top four path, and the highest share of unique pathways (30%), compared to 25% for patients with headache and 8% for patients with seizures.

Figure 6 presents the predictors of admission for neurological patients. The mixed‐effects logistic regression model explained 27% of the variation in admission. Admission was most strongly associated with acuity level: 81% of ESI Level 1 patients required hospitalization and 15% of these patients were admitted to the PICU or surgical unit (OR = 31.72, 95% CI 17.34–58.76, p < 0.001, χ 2 test compared to patients in ESI levels 2–5), while only 18% of ESI Level 3–5 patients were hospitalized. The overall admission rate for neurological patients was 23% and less than 1% were admitted to the PICU or surgical unit. Neurological patients were more likely to be hospitalized than non‐neurological pediatric patients (OR = 1.48, 95% CI: 1.38–1.59, p < 0.001). The risk of PICU admission was higher as well (OR = 1.61, 95% CI 1.15–2.21, p < 0.001) but it was not significant after adjusting for the overall higher admission rate (OR 1.17, 95% CI: 0.84–1.62, p = 0.359).

Discussion

This study sheds light on the characteristics, resource needs, and patient pathways of neurological patients at a PED serving a population of almost 300,000 children. By applying process mining, we have uncovered genuine differences in patient presentation and needs, as well as potential inefficiencies in current processes and management. This population‐based application of RWD and process mining not only validates their utility in a high‐volume tertiary PED but also establishes a scalable framework for future studies aimed at enhancing evidence‐based management and operational efficiency in emergency care.

This study benefitted from having a complete three‐year dataset from the only PED in the area providing secondary and tertiary emergency care for children in the region. All cases requiring such care were captured, making our dataset effectively population‐based, minimizing selection bias and enhancing the generalizability of our findings. The complete dataset from all PED visits enabled direct comparisons of neurological and non‐neurological patient populations, allowing evaluation of resource requirements within the context of overall resource utilization.

While prior studies have explored the relationship of demographics with either acuity or chief complaint, our study is, to our knowledge, the first one to assess all three together in neurological PED patients. This revealed subgroup‐specific characteristics and pathway differences. Consistent with earlier studies, seizures and headaches were the most common presentations [4, 7, 9], while other complaints were varied and often non‐specific. Seizures were associated with higher acuity, with most neurological ESI Level 1 patients presenting with seizures, emphasizing their critical nature. These patients were also significantly younger and more often male, aligning with prior findings [4, 6]. In contrast, headache was associated with lower acuity, mirroring a recent study by Wang and Huang, where only 8% of adolescent headache cases involved high‐acuity outcomes [24]. Headaches were more frequent in girls over 10 years, supporting earlier research [3, 25, 26, 27, 28]. Overall, our demographic data and the proportion of neurological patients at the PED aligned with previous studies [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9].

Neurological patients had a nearly fivefold increased risk of being assigned to the highest acuity level (ESI 1) and requiring immediate lifesaving interventions compared to other pediatric patients. They accounted for nearly one‐fifth of all ESI 1 visits, consistent with earlier studies reporting neurological cases in 19%–30% of the most acute presentations [4, 6, 7, 8]. Such variation likely reflects differences in triage systems, referral practices, local epidemiology, and institutional protocols as well as differences in study protocols. The consistent overrepresentation of neurological patients in the highest acuity category underscores the need for dedicated protocols and resource planning in PEDs.

Notably, half of the neurological ESI Level 1 patients were aged 1–5 years—a novel finding—suggesting increased vulnerability to severe neurological events in early childhood. The high susceptibility for febrile seizures in this age group might contribute to the high risk, but further evaluation is needed. Overall, children under 5 years of age were proportionally overrepresented in our cohort compared to the population in the area, highlighting the need for protocols, staffing and equipment at the PED to be specifically tailored to manage high acuity neurological emergencies, especially in early childhood. In contrast, patients over 10 years of age with neurological complaints were less likely to require acute care.

Process mining revealed four pathways through which a vast majority of patients were managed. Stratification by acuity, chief complaint, and age group demonstrated clear differences. Nearly half of all patients were discharged after clinical evaluation and laboratory measurements (excluding CSF), while a quarter were discharged based on clinical assessment alone. Most were low‐acuity, with headache presentations and older patients more prominent in this group. This reinforces the value of medical history and neurological evaluation in ruling out acute pathology. Many headache patients were likely treated at the PED, though medication data were not analyzed. The overwhelming discharge rate among headache‐related visits underscores the need for more appropriate care pathways, as previously recommended [25, 26, 27].

Over 90% of patients with seizures followed one of the four most common pathways and underwent less advanced testing than other neurological patients (though laboratory tests were often ordered). This suggests that advanced tests in patients with seizures are being judiciously reserved for those with more concerning presentations, reflecting efforts to limit unnecessary investigations [29, 30, 31].

Over 30 pathways were discovered for the 13% of patients needing advanced testing. The differences between acuity and symptom severity—as patients presented with a wide range of symptoms varying from acute status epilepticus and stroke(−like) presentations to vague symptoms such as fever or vomiting—naturally result in diverse pathways. This was especially evident in patients presenting with complaints other than seizure or headache.

Advanced testing was associated with higher acuity; however, most patients in ESI levels 1 and 2 were managed without such investigations. Acuity remained the strongest predictor of disposition, reflecting reliable triage performance. While advanced testing increased the likelihood of admission, suggesting more severe presentations, most patients with advanced tests were ultimately discharged. This was especially evident among those with headache, patients over 10 years of age, and the more stable ESI Level 3 group. These findings raise concerns about potential overuse of advanced diagnostics, especially given that over one‐third of all PED MRIs were conducted in neurological cases. Previous studies indicate that MRI and laboratory results are frequently non‐contributory in benign conditions such as febrile seizures or migraines [4, 5]. The key challenge is balancing timely diagnostics with the risks of unnecessary testing, including radiation exposure, sedation, and cost. Nonetheless, normal neuroimaging may sometimes support safe discharge, and further research is warranted to evaluate its role in resource use.

Admitted patients were significantly younger, with infants having the highest admission rate and risk in line with previous findings [3, 6, 20, 32], most likely reflecting a circumspect approach given their vulnerability rather than greater clinical severity. Patients living outside the Greater Helsinki Area, and thus farther away from the hospitals, were also more likely to be hospitalized in line with Leonard et al. [20]. This is expected, as patients from greater distances may have limited follow‐up access and may be more readily admitted.

Consistent with previous findings, neurological patients had higher admission rates than non‐neurological pediatric patients. Reported rates vary widely (17%–41%) across studies [4, 5, 6, 9, 20] likely reflecting differences in hospital and study setting and protocols. At HUS PED, patients cleared for morning discharge are often kept overnight for observation without formal admission, contributing to our admission rate (23%). For comparison, Personnic et al. [4] included a 19% short‐term observation rate in their 30% total.

While earlier studies reported that neurological patients account for up to 30% of PICU admissions [33, 34, 35, 36, 37], and neurological patients had a higher admission rate to the PICU and surgical unit in our study as well, we found no increased risk for PICU or surgical unit admission after adjusting for the overall higher admission rate. Variability in study designs—particularly the inclusion of neonates and brain injuries in some studies—likely explains some of the discrepancy.

A key limitation of this study is the retrospective identification of neurological patients using triage lists and ICPC‐2 and ICD‐10 codes, which may have led to misclassification—some non‐neurological patients may have been included, and some true neurological cases missed. This cohort included all PED visits from January 2021 to December 2023 with the covid‐19 pandemic and restrictions still ongoing in the beginning of this period. During the pandemic, infection rates in Finland were greatly reduced [38] which might have influenced the incidence of febrile seizures and other infection‐induced neurological conditions as reported elsewhere [39, 40]. Conversely, the omicron variant of SARS‐CoV‐2 has been associated with higher febrile seizure rates [41, 42].

Our findings support the development of tailored triage protocols and more efficient resource allocation in the PED, ensuring timely care for high‐acuity neurological patients while streamlining management of stable cases. Future efforts should focus on patients with chronic neurological disorders and patients with frequent visits to better understand their needs. Identifying failure demand in the PED and outpatient setting can further guide more efficient, patient‐centered improvements. We envision that integrating real‐world data and process mining will become standard practice in PED and hospital management, advancing data‐driven decision making.

Conclusion

This population‐based RWD study confirms that neurological patients, especially young children, represent a small yet high‐acuity group in the PED, with greater resource needs and higher admission rates than their non‐neurological peers. While most followed streamlined pathways, those requiring advanced testing had diverse paths. Clear trajectories can support tailored triage protocols and resource planning in the PED in the future. This study opens new evidence‐based, data‐driven management pathways for emergency departments and hospitals as we learn to utilize extensive healthcare data resources and novel data‐analysis methods and process mining to generate robust, research‐based insights.
