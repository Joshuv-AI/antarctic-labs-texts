Preparation and characterization of a Syrian pumice-based ion exchanger for copper (II) ion adsorption
Authors: Ahmad Sulaiman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeavy metal contamination of water resources, particularly by copper Cu(II) originating from industrial effluents, poses a significant threat to ecosystems and human health due to its toxicity and non-biodegradability. While adsorption is a widely recognized remediation technique, its economic viability is often hampered by the high cost of conventional adsorbents like activated carbon and synthetic ion-exchange resins. This economic constraint has driven the search for sustainable, low-cost alternative materials derived from abundant natural resources. Naturally occurring pumice, a volcanic rock with inherent porosity, has emerged as a promising candidate due to its global availability and low cost. However, a critical review of the literature reveals that the raw pumice typically exhibits limited adsorption capacity, restricting its practical application. Furthermore, while some studies have explored chemical activation to enhance its performance, the potential of Syrian pumice deposits remains significantly underexplored. There is a distinct lack of comprehensive studies that systematically investigate (a) a highly effective yet cost-effective chemical modification route for this specific pumice type, (b) a detailed analysis of its adsorption mechanisms beyond simple isotherm fitting, and (c) a rigorous economic assessment comparing it to incumbent technologies. This study addresses these critical gaps by presenting a novel, cost-effective adsorbent synthesized from locally sourced Syrian pumice for the efficient removal of Cu(II) ions. Through a simple alkaline modification, we produced a sodium hydroxide-treated pumice (PNa) ion exchanger. The research quantitatively demonstrates an 18-fold increase in specific surface area and characterizes the material’s exceptional adsorption performance across a wide range of operational parameters. We comprehensively analyze the kinetics, equilibrium isotherms, and thermodynamics to elucidate the hybrid adsorption mechanism. Crucially, this work demonstrates that the developed PNa material achieves performance comparable to conventional adsorbents while incurring production costs 70-90% lower, thereby establishing Syrian pumice as a sustainable and economically transformative alternative for wastewater treatment.Keywords: Pumice, Ion exchanger, Removal coefficients, Surface area, Exchange-adsorption capacities, Adsorption isothermSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

The Syrian Arab Republic hosts substantial natural deposits of bentonite, zeolite, dolomite, and pumice, which are commercially developed by the General Company for Geology and Mineral Resources owing to their economically viable extraction1. These mineral resources—with particular emphasis on zeolites, bentonites, and dolomite—serve as effective media for separation and purification processes2, a fact substantiated by extensive research characterizing their structural compositions and surface properties1,3. They typically occur as impure, brittle rocks mixed with secondary minerals, such as limestone3. This study focuses on pumice, a vesicular volcanic rock formed from rapidly cooled lava4,5. Its unique physicochemical properties make it valuable for industrial applications, particularly in construction materials and lightweight concrete production6. Pumice’s buoyancy and insulating qualities have led to its use in Portland cement mixtures for thermal and acoustic insulation.

Global production spans over 50 countries, with Italy dominating both pumice and pozzolan markets5,7. While comprehensive recent production data for Syrian pumice specifically is limited, its commercial exploitation is well-established due to low extraction costs8. The current applications of pumice include construction aggregates (15.8%), industrial fillers (2.7%), agricultural/landscaping uses (1.9%), and niche applications (<1%), such as filtration media and pottery9. Key pumice sources like Lipari Island, Italy, exhibit distinct chemical compositions ideal for lightweight concrete production, significantly influencing material properties when used as aggregate. The principal components of pumice are silicon oxide (SiO₂) and aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃), with higher silica content indicating greater purity. Elevated iron oxide concentrations impart darker coloration and increased density, both undesirable for insulation applications9. This chemical composition, coupled with its characteristically low density (0.35–0.65 g/cm3) and high porosity, underpins pumice’s exceptional utility in construction, particularly for lightweight concrete and thermal/acoustic insulation.6 Extensive experimental studies on Turkish pumice have confirmed these properties when blended with cement in varying ratios. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis further reveals pumice’s predominantly amorphous structure (99% composition), which contrasts sharply with cement’s crystalline peaks10 and distinguishes it from denser, glassy volcanic rocks like mineral magma11.

These fundamental properties directly enable pumice’s versatility across diverse innovative applications. In construction, it has been used for concrete enhancement, where sodium silicate-modified rubber composites with pumice have yielded lightweight concrete, albeit with some compromises in durability12. It also contributes to prefabricated construction elements, such as ornamental reinforced concrete and insulation systems13. Beyond construction, pumice serves in surface treatment for polishing metals, woods, and dental materials13, and in environmental applications including soil aeration, poultry bedding, and notably, as a filtration media for water treatment13,14. It is precisely this last environmental application that holds particular relevance, given that copper contamination Cu(II) which affects multiple organs including the brain, skin, liver, and pancreas15 makes its efficient removal from water sources a critical environmental objective.

However, the widespread application of raw pumice in water treatment, particularly for heavy metal removal like Cu(II), remains limited by its inherently low adsorption capacity. Furthermore, the potential of Syrian pumice as a precursor for developing a high-performance, cost-effective adsorbent has been critically underexplored. To address this research gap, the present study aims to achieve the following specific objectives: (1) to develop and characterize a novel, chemically-modified pumice adsorbent from Syrian sources using an optimized alkaline treatment; (2) to evaluate its efficacy in removing Cu(II) ions from aqueous solutions by systematically investigating the effects of key operational parameters; (3) to elucidate the adsorption mechanisms through comprehensive kinetic, equilibrium, and thermodynamic analyses; and (4) to conduct a preliminary economic assessment of the proposed treatment process. The central hypothesis is that alkaline modification will significantly enhance the surface area and ion-exchange capacity of Syrian pumice, transforming it into a highly efficient and economically viable adsorbent for Cu(II) removal through a spontaneous and exothermic process.

Synthesis of sodium hydroxide-modified pumice (PNa) ion exchanger

The PNa ion exchanger was synthesized through an optimized multi-step alkaline treatment of raw pumice, with strict control of operational parameters to ensure reproducibility:

(i)Size Reduction and Preparation: Raw pumice was mechanically ground and sieved to obtain a uniform particle size fraction of 200–300 μm.

Size Reduction and Preparation: Raw pumice was mechanically ground and sieved to obtain a uniform particle size fraction of 200–300 μm.

(ii)Alkaline Fusion: A homogeneous mixture was prepared by mechanically blending 6.0 g of sized pumice with 7.2 g of sodium hydroxide (1:1.2 w/w ratio) in a porcelain crucible using a digital mixer at 300 rpm for 15 minutes. The mixture was then thermally treated in a muffle furnace at 600 °C for 2 hours. The fused product was subsequently cooled to ambient temperature at a controlled rate of 3°C/min to prevent thermal shock, then crushed and homogenized into a fine powder using an agate mortar.

(iii)Hydrothermal Treatment: The fused product was dispersed in 60 mL of double-distilled water (resistivity 18.2 MΩ·cm), representing a water-to-solid ratio of 10:1 (v/w). The suspension underwent sequential treatment: first, reflux conditioning at boiling temperature for 72 hours, followed by intensive hydrothermal treatment in a Teflon-lined autoclave at 140 °C for an additional 72 hours.

The product was collected via vacuum filtration and dried to constant weight at 105 °C for 24 hours20.

The entire synthesis procedure was repeated in three independent batches under identical conditions. Characterization of the resulting materials showed less than 3% variation in key parameters (specific surface area, cation exchange capacity, and chemical composition), confirming excellent reproducibility of the synthesis protocol.

Concentration-dependent removal behavior

The removal efficiency (R) exhibited a pronounced inverse correlation with equilibrium concentration, delineating two distinct operational regimes. At low concentrations (≤0.025 M), exceptional removal efficiencies of 85–92% were attained, attributable to sufficient availability of exchange sites and negligible competitive effects. Conversely, under high-concentration conditions (≥0.100 M), the efficiency diminished to 32–45%, signifying site saturation phenomena. This reduction coincides with the necessity for deeper solute penetration into the adsorbent matrix (Δx ≈ 15–20 μm)71 and an elevated activation energy barrier (Eₐ ≈ 28 kJ/mol)72, underscoring the intensified mass transfer limitations at higher concentrations.

Thermal enhancement of adsorption efficiency

A notable thermal enhancement in removal efficiency (R) was observed, with an 8–12% increase as temperature rose from 298.15 K to 308.15 K. This improvement is attributed to enhanced ionic mobility, evidenced by an 18% increase in effective diffusivity (Dₑff), coupled with reduced viscous resistance (η ↓ 7%) which facilitated improved mass transfer kinetics. The positive temperature dependence underscores the role of thermal energy in overcoming diffusional barriers within the adsorbent matrix.

Thermodynamic analysis and quantitative correlations

The thermodynamic parameters provide profound insight into the adsorption mechanism. The negative ΔG° values at all temperatures confirm the spontaneity of Cu(II) adsorption onto the PNa ion exchanger, with increasing spontaneity at higher temperatures. This quantitative analysis reveals a direct correlation between ΔG° and adsorption capacity: as ΔG° decreases from −9.77 to −11.08 kJ/mol, qmax increases from 3.4 to 4.3 mmol/g, demonstrating that smaller (more negative) ΔG° values correspond to higher adsorption capacities74. The linear correlation between ΔG° and qmax (R2 = 0.98) provides a predictive framework for optimizing adsorption capacity through temperature manipulation in practical applications.

The enthalpy change (ΔH° = −29.154 kJ/mol) lies in a range indicative of a hybrid adsorption mechanism. This suggests contributions from both chemisorption, via ion exchange at the newly formed ≡Si–O⁻ sites, and physisorption, facilitated by electrostatic interactions on the vastly increased surface area.

Furthermore, the significant positive entropy change (ΔS° = 130 J/mol·K) is a pivotal finding. It signifies an increase in system randomness, predominantly attributed to the desolvation of Cu(II) ions75. During adsorption, the release of structured water molecules from the hydrated Cu(II) ions into the bulk solution creates a substantial entropic gain, which serves as a major driving force for the process. Thus, the adsorption is synergistically driven by both an exothermic heat of reaction and a favorable increase in entropy76. The constant values of ΔH° and ΔS° across temperatures reflect the standard thermodynamic approach where these parameters are considered temperature-independent over limited ranges in adsorption studies. This assumption is validated by the excellent linearity (R2 > 0.99) of the Van’t Hoff plot used to determine these parameters from experimental data.

Conclusion

This study successfully demonstrates that alkaline modification represents a highly effective strategy for transforming Syrian raw pumice into an exceptional ion exchanger (PNa) for efficient Cu(II) removal. The experimental approach, specifically the optimized 1:2 pumice: NaOH fusion at 600 °C, directly facilitated a fundamental structural transformation: an 18-fold increase in specific surface area from 5.2 ± 0.4 m2/g (Praw) to 93.6 ± 3.1 m2/g (PNa), (mean ± standard deviation, n=3)20,. This dramatic textural enhancement significantly exceeds the improvements commonly reported in literature for alkali-treated natural minerals83, underscoring the superior efficacy of the developed protocol.

According to the literature, the adsorption mechanism depends on the features of the sorbent architecture (porosity, surface area, and particle size) and the characteristics of the metallic ions (ionic radius and coordination number)84. This principle is unequivocally validated here, where the created porosity and surface area served as the primary factor governing the enhanced adsorption capacity. The mechanism was determined to be a complex interplay of processes: kinetic studies confirmed pseudo-first-order behavior26,27, while equilibrium data showed excellent fit to the Langmuir isotherm (R2 > 0.99)37–39, collectively indicating surface-controlled monolayer coverage. The thermodynamic spontaneity (negative ΔG) and exothermic nature of the process further reinforce its practical viability23.

Remarkably, the PNa exchanger achieved >99% removal efficiency at low concentrations23,24, a performance that not only validates our synthesis strategy but also positions Syrian PNa as a superior alternative to other regionally sourced pumices reported in the literature. The collective findings establish a direct causal link between the NaOH treatment methodology, the induced physicochemical properties, and the resultant high adsorption performance. Consequently, this work presents a compelling, cost-effective, and sustainable adsorbent derived from a locally abundant resource, providing a robust foundation for future industrial-scale implementation in wastewater remediation.
