The tensor multiplet in loop space


Abstract
We reformulate the abelian tensor multiplet on a curved spacetime with at least two supercharges in a cohomological form where all the bosonic and fermionic fields become tensor fields. These tensor fields are rewritten as fields in loop space by a transgression map. There are two lightlike conformal Killing vectors. By decomposing the spacetime tensor fields in transverse and parallel components to these Killing vectors, we obtain the equations of motion in loop space by closing the supersymmetry variations on-shell. We generalize to nonabelian gauge groups. By closing supersymmetry variations we obtain nonabelian fermionic equations of motion in loop space.

Physics Department, University of Seoul, Seoul 02504 KOREA

1
Introduction

The abelian tensor multiplet is difficult to generalize to nonabelian gauge groups because of the two-form gauge potential
B_{MN}
. There is a no-go theorem that says that no reparametrization invariant Wilson surface can be constructed out of a nonabelian two-form gauge potential
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
alone. However, in loop space the two-form gauge potential becomes a one-form through a transgression map from spacetime to loop space
[
3
]
, which is an integration of the two-form around a loop
C
,

\displaystyle{\cal{A}}(C)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{C}B_{MN}(C(s))\delta C^{M}(s)\dot{C}^{N}(s)ds

(1.1)

Since this is now a one-form gauge field, it seems, at least naively, that it should have a generalization to nonabelian gauge groups, despite the above mentioned no-go theorem. Here the integral is around a loop
C
parametrized and embedded in spacetime as
s\mapsto C^{M}(s)
. Its tangential vector is denoted as
\dot{C}^{M}(s)
and
\delta C^{M}(s)
constitute a basis for the cotangent bundle over loop space that are simply wedge multiplied together as
\delta C^{M}(s)\wedge\delta C^{N}(s^{\prime})
where the wedge product in loop space is directly inherited from the wedge product in spacetime. We will refer to
{\cal{A}}(C)
as a loop space field. All loop space fields will be invariant under reparametrizations of the loop. They will also all be Weyl invariant. Our loop space is the set of all loops embedded in spacetime and the loops are not required to have a common base point, which means that we have a free loop space. Reparametrization invariance means that any two loops that differ only by their parametrization shall be identified as the same loop and we will assume that the loops are everywhere smooth for simplicity. By allowing the loops to have cusp and self-intersection points we would likely discover new interesting physical effects, which deserves a separate treatment.

We may construct a nonabelian gauge potential in loop space as follows,

\displaystyle{\cal{A}}(C)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int B_{MN}^{a}(C(s))\delta C^{M}(s)n^{N}(C(s))t_{a}(s)ds

(1.2)

Here
B_{MN}^{a}
is a nonabelian two-form in component form with
a
being a gauge group index in the adjoint representation,
n^{M}
is a vector field, and as it turns out, this will be a conformal Killing vector field on the spacetime manifold and
t_{a}(s)
are generators of a loop algebra

\displaystyle[t_{a}(s),t_{b}(s^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle if_{ab}{}^{c}\delta(s-s^{\prime})t_{c}(s)

(1.3)

where
f_{ab}{}^{c}
are structure constants of the gauge group. The resulting nonabelian gauge field
{\cal{A}}
now has some nice properties. Firstly, because of the delta function in the loop algebra, all commutators such as
{\cal{A}}\wedge{\cal{A}}
will be local expressions integrated around the loop. Secondly,
{\cal{A}}
is reparametrization invariant as a consequence of that the generators
t_{a}(s)
transform as one-forms. As we will show, this construction is consistent only if we perform dimensional reduction along
n^{M}
. Nevertheless, this reformulation may have some advantages compared to the standard formulation of five-dimensional super Yang-Mills theory. For example, it may be better suited for defining the nonabelian Wilson surface that in loop space would be defined as a Wilson line

\displaystyle P\exp\int_{\Gamma}{\cal{A}}

(1.4)

where
P
denotes path-ordering along a path
\Gamma
in loop space, which is a surface in spacetime that is foliated by loops in a certain way. While we have reasons to believe that this definition will be independent of the way that the surface is foliated by loops thanks to the loop algebra
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
8
]
, we plan to address this question in detail in a future publication.

The structure of this paper is as follows. In section
2
we start with the abelian
(1,0)
tensor multiplet with two chiral spinors
\lambda_{I}
for
I=1,2
. We pick two commuting supersymmetry parameters
{\cal{\varepsilon}}_{I}
which are conformal Killing spinors and use them to map the two spinors into tensor fields. We then recall a basic geometrical fact from
[
4
]
that having two commuting conformal Killing spinors implies that the Lorentzian six-manifold possesses two commuting lightlike conformal Killing vector fields
U
and
V
. These two vector fields enable us to decompose any tensor field into transverse and longitudinal components where the transverse components are orthogonal to both
U
and
V
. In section
3
we introduce a transgression map from spacetime fields to loop space fields, essentially by integration over the loop as in (
1.1
), but, as we will see, without integrating. We obtain Bianchi identities, supersymmerry variations and matter field equations of motion in loop space in sections
4
,
5
and
6
. We then repeat the same steps for a nonabelian gauge group. We propose a nonabelian transgression map (
1.2
), obtain Bianchi identities, supersymmetry variations and matter field equations of motion in sections
7
,
8
and
9
. We show that our nonabelian equations of motion are supersymmetric and that they reduce to equations of motion in five-dimensional super Yang-Mills theory in sections
9
and
10
. In section
11
we discuss our results.

2
The abelian tensor multiplet

In this section we present the abelian tensor multiplet in six dimensions in the cohomological form
[
4
]
. This is the curved space generalization and uplift to six dimensions of the corresponding five-dimensional vector multiplet in the cohomological form
[
5
]
. The cohomological form was first used in a localization computation in 2d Yang-Mills theory by Witten
[
7
]
for the BRST symmetry. Later a cohomological form of 4d super Yang-Mills was used by Pestun
[
6
]
for supersymmetric localization and this technique has subsequently been applied to other supersymmetric theories including five dimensional super Yang-Mills theory
[
5
]
. We will use a similar kind of cohomological form of the tensor multiplet in six dimensions. Our motivation is not to use it for localization but to transgress the fermionic field into loop space. In the cohomological form the spinor fields become a set of tensor fields that we can transgress to loop space by integrating them over the loop.

The
(1,0)
tensor multiplet has a gauge potential
B_{MN}
, one real scalar field
\phi
and two symplectic-Majorana chiral fermions
\lambda_{I}
where
I=1,2
. The supersymmetry variations are

\displaystyle\delta\phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\bar{\cal{\varepsilon}}^{I}\lambda_{I}

(2.1)

\displaystyle\delta B_{MN}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\bar{\cal{\varepsilon}}^{I}\Gamma_{MN}\lambda_{I}

(2.2)

\displaystyle\delta\lambda_{I}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{12}\Gamma^{MNP}{\cal{\varepsilon}}_{I}H_{MNP}+\Gamma^{M}{\cal{\varepsilon}}_{I}\partial_{M}\phi+4\eta_{I}\phi

(2.3)

where

\displaystyle H_{MNP}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{M}B_{NP}+\partial_{N}B_{PM}+\partial_{P}B_{MN}

(2.4)

Here the supersymmetry parameters satisfy the conformal Killing spinor equation

\displaystyle\nabla_{M}{\cal{\varepsilon}}_{I}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma_{M}\eta_{I}

(2.5)

Our spinor conventions are summarized in appendix
A
. There can be various supersymmetric actions that one may consider for a selfdual tensor field. But the most straightforward way is to use the Maxwell action for a non-selfdual tensor field, which can be supersymmetrized. This action is given by

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{6}x\sqrt{-g}{\cal{L}}

(2.6)

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{24}H_{MNP}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}(\nabla_{M}\phi)^{2}+\frac{i}{2}\bar{\lambda}^{I}\Gamma^{M}\nabla_{M}\lambda_{I}-\frac{R}{10}\phi^{2}

(2.7)

Here
g_{MN}
is a Lorentzian metric and
R
is the Ricci curvature scalar.
The selfduality of
H_{MNP}
is an equation of motion that is required for on-shell closure of the supersymmetry variations but which we can not derive from this action.

The two
{\cal{\varepsilon}}_{I}
for
I=1,2
may be taken to be either anticommuting or commuting. We will take them to be commuting and the supersymmetry variation
\delta
is anticommuting.

From these two
{\cal{\varepsilon}}_{I}
one can show
[
4
]
that there has to exist two commuting lightlike conformal Killing vectors
V_{M}
and
U_{M}
where

\displaystyle V_{M}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bar{\cal{\varepsilon}}^{I}\Gamma_{M}{\cal{\varepsilon}}_{I}

(2.8)

That they are commuting means that their Lie bracket vanishes

\displaystyle[V,U]^{M}=V^{N}\partial_{N}U^{M}-U^{N}\partial_{N}V^{M}=0

(2.9)

From the identity
{\cal{L}}_{[U,V]}=[{\cal{L}}_{U},{\cal{L}}_{V}]
it then follows that the Lie derivatives commute

\displaystyle[{\cal{L}}_{U},{\cal{L}}_{V}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(2.10)

We define a normalization factor
\mathscr{N}=g_{MN}U^{M}V^{N}
and we define

\displaystyle V_{MN}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{M}\left(\frac{V_{N}}{\mathscr{N}}\right)-\partial_{N}\left(\frac{V_{M}}{\mathscr{N}}\right)

(2.11)

\displaystyle U_{MN}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{M}\left(\frac{U_{N}}{\mathscr{N}}\right)-\partial_{N}\left(\frac{U_{M}}{\mathscr{N}}\right)

(2.12)

In appendix
B
we show that these are transverse to both
U
and
V
and selfdual and antiselfdual in this transverse space, respectively.

We define fermionic tensor fields as

\displaystyle\Psi_{MN}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bar{\cal{\varepsilon}}^{I}\Gamma_{MN}\lambda_{I}

(2.13)

\displaystyle\Psi_{N}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle U^{M}\Psi_{MN}

(2.14)

\displaystyle\psi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathscr{N}}V^{M}\Psi_{M}

(2.15)

and we now see that it is essential to take
{\cal{\varepsilon}}_{I}
to be commuting in order for these fermionic tensor fields to be anticommuting.

From the above definitions it follows that

\displaystyle\Psi_{MN}V^{N}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V_{M}\psi

(2.16)

This relation follows because of the property

\displaystyle\Gamma_{M}{\cal{\varepsilon}}_{I}V^{M}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(2.17)

that one can prove by using a Fierz identity.

The supersymmetry variation expressed in terms of these fermionic tensor fields reads

\displaystyle\delta\phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\psi

(2.18)

\displaystyle\delta B_{MN}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\Psi_{MN}

(2.19)

\displaystyle\delta\Psi_{MN}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-V_{MN}\mathscr{N}\phi+\frac{V_{M}}{\mathscr{N}}\partial_{N}\left(\mathscr{N}\phi\right)-\frac{V_{N}}{\mathscr{N}}\partial_{M}\left(\mathscr{N}\phi\right)

(2.21)

\displaystyle-V^{P}H_{PMN}^{+}

\displaystyle\delta\Psi_{N}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{N}\left(\mathscr{N}\phi\right)-\frac{V_{N}}{\mathscr{N}}{\cal{L}}_{U}\left(\mathscr{N}\phi\right)

(2.23)

\displaystyle-V^{P}U^{M}H_{PMN}^{+}

\displaystyle\delta\psi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathscr{N}}{\cal{L}}_{V}\left(\mathscr{N}\phi\right)

(2.24)

We will decompose the tensor fields in transverse and longitudinal components to
U
and
V
as

\displaystyle\Psi_{MN}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi_{MN}+\frac{1}{\mathscr{N}}\left(V_{M}\Psi_{N}-V_{N}\Psi_{M}\right)

(2.25)

\displaystyle\Psi_{M}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi_{M}+U_{M}\psi

(2.26)

We also define

\displaystyle F_{MN}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{H_{MNP}V^{P}}

(2.27)

\displaystyle G_{MN}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{H_{MNP}U^{P}}

(2.28)

\displaystyle R_{P}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-V^{M}U^{N}H_{MNP}

(2.29)

Tilde indicates that trace parts have been removed, where we refer to a contraction with
U
or
V
as a trace. These traceless tensor fields will be also referred to as irreducible fields. A reducible tensor field, such as
\Psi_{MN}
is expanded in irreducible components
\chi_{MN}
,
\psi_{M}
and
\psi
. Similarly the reducible field strength is expanded in irreducible components as

\displaystyle H_{MNP}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h_{MNP}+\frac{3}{\mathscr{N}}U_{[M}F_{NP]}+\frac{3}{\mathscr{N}}V_{[M}G_{NP]}-\frac{6}{\mathscr{N}^{2}}U_{[M}V_{N}R_{P]}

(2.30)

The supersymmetry variations of these irreducible component fields are

\displaystyle\delta\chi_{MN}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-V_{MN}\mathscr{N}\phi-F_{MN}^{+}

(2.31)

\displaystyle\delta\psi_{N}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{\partial}_{N}\left(\mathscr{N}\phi\right)+R_{N}^{+}

(2.32)

\displaystyle\delta F_{MN}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i{\cal{L}}_{V}\chi_{MN}+iV_{MN}\mathscr{N}\psi

(2.33)

\displaystyle\delta G_{MN}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i{\cal{L}}_{U}\chi_{MN}+iU_{MN}\mathscr{N}\psi-i\widetilde{\left(\partial_{M}\psi_{N}-\partial_{N}\psi_{M}\right)}

(2.34)

\displaystyle\delta R_{M}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\left(\widetilde{\partial}_{M}\left(\mathscr{N}\psi\right)-{\cal{L}}_{V}\psi_{M}\right)

(2.35)

where

\displaystyle F_{MN}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(F_{MN}\pm\frac{1}{2}{\cal{E}}_{MNPQ}F^{PQ}\right)

(2.36)

\displaystyle R_{M}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(R_{M}\mp\frac{1}{6\mathscr{N}}{\cal{E}}_{MRST}h^{RST}\right)

(2.37)

It is interesting to note that the supersymmetry variation of
F_{MN}
is selfdual.

In this formulation we have spontaneously broken Lorentz symmetry in Minkowski space since
V
selects a preferred lightlike direction. Without breaking Lorentz symmetry, we can not extract
F_{MN}
but must work with
H_{MNP}
that shall be subject to the selfduality equation of motion

\displaystyle H_{MNP}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{6}{\cal{\varepsilon}}_{MNP}{}^{RST}H_{RST}

This selfduality induces the selfduality relations

\displaystyle F_{MN}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{\cal{E}}_{MN}{}^{PQ}F_{PQ}

(2.39)

\displaystyle G_{MN}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}{\cal{E}}_{MN}{}^{PQ}G_{PQ}

(2.40)

\displaystyle R_{Q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\mathscr{N}}{6}{\cal{E}}_{Q}{}^{MNP}h_{MNP}

(2.41)

\displaystyle h_{MNP}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathscr{N}}{\cal{E}}_{MNP}{}^{Q}R_{Q}

(2.42)

where we define

\displaystyle{\cal{E}}_{MNPQ}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathscr{N}}{\cal{\varepsilon}}_{MNPQRS}V^{R}U^{S}

(2.43)

with the property

\displaystyle\nabla_{M}\left(\frac{1}{\mathscr{N}}{\cal{E}}^{MNPQ}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{3}{\mathscr{N}}\left(U^{[NP}V^{Q}+V^{[NP}U^{Q]}\right)

(2.44)

that we derive in eq (
E.18
)) in the appendix.

The breaking of Lorentz symmetry is in the formulation of the theory rather than a physical breaking, which corresponds to selecting two supercharges out of eight in Minkowski space. That our formulation breaks some symmetries, like Lorentz symmetry, seems harmless in the abelian theory since we have an alternative formulation in terms of spinor fields where the Lorentz symmetry is manifest.

The supersymmetric action is given by

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{6}x\sqrt{-g}{\cal{L}}

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{24}H_{MNP}^{2}-\frac{1}{2\mathscr{N}^{2}}\left(\nabla_{M}\left(\mathscr{N}\phi\right)\right)^{2}

(2.47)

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{4\mathscr{N}}\chi^{MN}{\cal{L}}_{U}\chi_{MN}+\frac{i}{\mathscr{N}}\chi^{MN}\partial_{M}\psi_{N}-\frac{i}{\mathscr{N}}V_{MN}\psi^{M}\psi^{N}

\displaystyle+\frac{i}{2\mathscr{N}^{2}}\psi^{M}{\cal{L}}_{V}\psi_{M}-\frac{i}{\mathscr{N}^{2}}\psi^{M}\partial_{M}\left(\mathscr{N}\psi\right)-\frac{i}{\mathscr{N}^{2}}\psi{\cal{L}}_{U}\left(\mathscr{N}\psi\right)

In appendix

we match the scalar field part of this action with the standard action for a conformally coupled scalar field in a curved background geometry. There are three fermionic equations of motion

\displaystyle\mathscr{N}^{2}\nabla^{M}\left(\frac{1}{\mathscr{N}^{2}}\psi_{M}\right)+\frac{2}{\mathscr{N}}{\cal{L}}_{U}\left(\mathscr{N}\psi\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(2.49)

\displaystyle\mathscr{N}^{2}\widetilde{\nabla^{M}\left(\frac{1}{\mathscr{N}}\chi_{MN}\right)}-\mathscr{N}V_{NP}\psi^{P}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{\partial}_{N}\left(\mathscr{N}\psi\right)-{\cal{L}}_{V}\psi_{N}

(2.50)

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{U}\chi_{MN}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{\left(\partial_{M}\psi_{N}-\partial_{N}\psi_{M}\right)}^{+}

(2.51)

that are obtained from this action by varying
\psi
,
\psi_{M}
and
\chi_{MN}
respectively. Making a supersymmetry variation of the first equation (
2.49
) we obtain the scalar field equation of motion

\displaystyle\mathscr{N}^{2}\nabla^{M}\left(\frac{1}{\mathscr{N}^{2}}\partial_{M}\left(\mathscr{N}\phi\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(2.52)

In the second fermionic equation of motion, (
2.50
), the traceless part is given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nabla^{M}\left(\frac{1}{\mathscr{N}}\chi_{MN}\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla^{M}\left(\frac{1}{\mathscr{N}}\chi_{MN}\right)+\frac{1}{2\mathscr{N}}U_{N}V^{PQ}\chi_{PQ}

To verify this we first contract the right-hand side with
V^{N}
and make an ‘integration by parts’,

\displaystyle V^{N}\nabla^{M}\left(\frac{1}{\mathscr{N}}\chi_{MN}\right)+\frac{1}{2}V^{PQ}\chi_{PQ}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla^{M}\left(\frac{V^{N}}{\mathscr{N}}\chi_{MN}\right)-\nabla^{M}\left(\frac{V^{N}}{\mathscr{N}}\right)\chi_{MN}+\frac{1}{2}V^{MN}\chi_{MN}

(2.55)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(2.56)

In the last step we used the traceless property of
\chi_{MN}
and the definition of
V_{MN}
. Next, if we contract instead with
U^{N}
and repeat the same kind of computation, we get

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}U^{MN}\chi_{MN}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

This is zero because

.

3
The abelian transgression map

A differential
p
-form in spacetime can be mapped into a differential
(p-1)
-form in loop space. If we have a spacetime
p
-form

\displaystyle\alpha_{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p!}\alpha_{M_{1}\cdots M_{p}}dx^{M_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{M_{p}}

(3.1)

then we define its corresponding
(p-1)
-form in path space by a transgression map
\mathscr{P}
that is a map from spacetime to path space as follows,

\displaystyle\alpha_{p}\;\mapsto\;\mathscr{P}\alpha_{p}\;=\;\frac{1}{(p-1)!}\int\alpha_{M_{1}\dots M_{p-1}M_{p}}\delta C^{M_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\delta C^{M_{p-1}}\wedge\dot{C}^{M_{p}}ds

(3.2)

This integral definition of the transgression map works fine for
p=2,3,4,...\;
.

There is another way of defining the transgression map, which also extends to the cases
p=0,1
. To introduce this other way, we start with decomposing a spacetime differential in its tangential and transverse components,

\displaystyle dx^{M}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dot{C}^{M}ds+\delta C^{M}(s)

(3.3)

with respect to the path that goes through that point
x^{M}=C^{M}(s)
.
The spacetime wedge product being antisymmetric,
dx^{M}\wedge dx^{N}=-dx^{N}\wedge dx^{M}
, naturally induces the following wedge multiplication rules in loop space

\displaystyle\delta C^{M}\wedge\delta C^{N}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\delta C^{N}\wedge\delta C^{M}

(3.4)

\displaystyle\delta C^{M}\wedge ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-ds\wedge\delta C^{M}

(3.5)

\displaystyle ds\wedge ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(3.6)

The tangent vector
\dot{C}^{M}
plays no role in these wedge products, as it is a bosonic function along the loop. If two vectors
\alpha=\alpha_{M}dx^{M}
and
\beta=\beta_{M}dx^{N}
are parallel, then
\alpha\wedge\beta=0
. Let us now take
\alpha=\alpha_{M}\delta C^{M}
and
\beta=\beta_{M}\dot{C}^{M}ds
. Requiring that
\delta C^{M}
and
\dot{C}^{M}ds
are not parallel amounts to requiring
\alpha\wedge\beta
to be nonvanishing for arbitrary vectors
\alpha_{M}
and
\beta_{M}
. This implies that
\delta C^{M}\wedge ds
shall be nonvanishing.

As a concrete example, in a static gauge we might for a suitable class of loops (extended appropriately in the fifths direction) choose the parameter
s
as the fifths direction,
s=C^{5}(s)
. Then the loop space differentials will be given by
\delta C^{\mu}(s)=dC^{\mu}(s)
and
ds=dC^{5}(s)
for
\mu=0,1,...,4
.

We may now expand the spacetime
p
-form as

\displaystyle\alpha_{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p!}\alpha_{M_{1}\cdots M_{p}}dx^{M_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{M_{p}}

(3.7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p!}\alpha_{M_{1}\cdots M_{p}}\delta C^{M_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\delta C^{M_{p}}

(3.9)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{(p-1)!}\alpha_{M_{1}\cdots M_{p}}\delta C^{M_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\delta C^{M_{p-1}}\wedge\dot{C}^{M_{p}}ds

We now see that the transgression map projects out the first term

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p!}\alpha_{M_{1}\cdots M_{p}}\delta C^{M_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\delta C^{M_{p}}

(3.10)

We will return to this very important observation below.

For
p=2,3,4,...\;
we may define the transgression map as simply the projection

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}\alpha_{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(p-1)!}\alpha_{M_{1}\dots M_{p-1}M_{p}}\delta C^{M_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\delta C^{M_{p-1}}\wedge\dot{C}^{M_{p}}ds

(3.11)

That this is equivalent to the above integrated form is because the one-form differentials
\delta C^{M}(s)
serve as basis elements at each point of the loop that enable us to extract the integrand at each point along the loop. Now we may generalize this definition to
p=1
,

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}\alpha_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{M}\dot{C}^{M}ds

(3.12)

With this definition we stay close to how the definition actually works for
p=2,3,4,....
in the sense that now we keep the information of what is happening at each point along the path. When we drop the integral we can also extend the definition to
p=0
where we define

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}\alpha_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha(C(s))

(3.13)

to be a (-1)-form in loop space. Thus a (-1)-form in loop space is nothing but a function
\alpha(x)
in spacetime evaluated at the points of the loop. For the zero-form it is still possible to integrate along the loop although we loose a huge amount of information about the zero-form by doing so. For the (-1)-form it is impossible to integrate it along the loop since the required measure factor
ds
is absent.

Let us now summarize the first few transgression maps in this tensor field hierarchy,

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}\alpha_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{MNP}\delta C^{M}\wedge\delta C^{N}\wedge\dot{C}^{P}ds

(3.15)

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}\alpha_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{MN}\delta C^{M}\wedge\dot{C}^{N}ds

(3.16)

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}\alpha_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{M}\dot{C}^{M}ds

(3.17)

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}\alpha_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha(C(s))

(3.18)

In this viewpoint the transgression map
\mathscr{P}
is really nothing but a projection operator and loop space is nothing but the space on which
\mathscr{P}
projects onto.

Next we will introduce a differential operator

that acts on
(p-1)
-forms in loop space. Acting on the
(-1)
-form, we get two terms,

\displaystyle d\mathscr{P}\alpha_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{M}\alpha\dot{C}^{M}ds+\partial_{M}\alpha\delta C^{M}

A subsequent application of the transgression projection map projects out the second term,

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}d\mathscr{P}\alpha_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{M}\alpha\dot{C}^{M}ds

(3.20)

Let us now compute

\displaystyle d\mathscr{P}\alpha_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\left(\alpha_{M}(C(s))\dot{C}^{M}ds\right)

(3.21)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{N}\alpha_{M}\left(\delta C^{N}+\dot{C}^{N}ds\right)\wedge\dot{C}^{M}ds+\alpha_{M}\frac{d}{ds}\left(\delta C^{M}+\dot{C}^{M}ds\right)\wedge ds

(3.22)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\left(\alpha_{M}\delta C^{M}\right)ds+\left(\partial_{N}\alpha_{M}-\partial_{M}\alpha_{N}\right)\delta C^{N}\wedge\dot{C}^{M}ds

(3.23)

As this computation may seem abstract, let us also present this computation in static gauge
s=x^{5}
in which

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}\alpha_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mu}\dot{C}^{\mu}dx^{5}+\alpha_{5}dx^{5}

(3.24)

where
\alpha_{M}=\alpha_{M}(C^{\mu}(x^{5}),x^{5})
and then

\displaystyle d\mathscr{P}\alpha_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta C^{\nu}\wedge\partial_{\nu}\alpha_{\mu}\dot{C}^{\mu}dx^{5}+\alpha_{\mu}\left(\frac{d}{dx^{5}}\delta C^{\mu}\right)\wedge dx^{5}

(3.26)

\displaystyle+\delta C^{\nu}\wedge\partial_{\nu}\alpha_{5}dx^{5}

We make an integration by parts and note that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx^{5}}\alpha_{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dot{C}^{\nu}\partial_{\nu}\alpha_{\mu}+\partial_{5}\alpha_{\mu}

(3.27)

and end up with

\displaystyle d\mathscr{P}\alpha_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta C^{\nu}\wedge\left(\partial_{\nu}\alpha_{\mu}-\partial_{\mu}\alpha_{\nu}\right)\dot{C}^{\mu}dx^{5}

(3.30)

\displaystyle+\delta C^{\nu}\wedge\left(\partial_{\nu}\alpha_{5}-\partial_{5}\alpha_{\nu}\right)dx^{5}

\displaystyle+\frac{d}{dx^{5}}\left(\alpha_{\mu}\delta C^{\mu}\right)dx^{5}

which is in agreement with (
3.23
) when specializing to static gauge.

If we integrate the result over a loop, then the first being a total derivative gives zero, and we get

\displaystyle d\int\mathscr{P}\alpha_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\left(\partial_{N}\alpha_{M}-\partial_{M}\alpha_{N}\right)\delta C^{N}\wedge\dot{C}^{M}ds

(3.31)

However, we can also apply the transgression projection map again to get basically the same result,

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}d\mathscr{P}\alpha_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{N}\alpha_{M}-\partial_{M}\alpha_{N}\right)\delta C^{N}\wedge\dot{C}^{M}ds

(3.32)

without integrating. Since the projection is done at every point along the path, it goes through any number of derivatives with respect to
s
. It is also interesting to compare this result with the computation of

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}d\alpha_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}d\left(\alpha_{M}dx^{M}\right)

(3.33)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{M}\alpha_{N}-\partial_{N}\alpha_{M}\right)dx^{M}\wedge dx^{N}\right)

(3.34)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{M}\alpha_{N}-\partial_{N}\alpha_{M}\right)\delta C^{M}\wedge\dot{C}^{N}ds

(3.35)

We now see that we get the same result. Thus

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}d\mathscr{P}\alpha_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}d\alpha_{1}

(3.36)

If furthermore we note that
\mathscr{P}
is a projection,
\mathscr{P}^{2}=\mathscr{P}
, then we can also express this result as

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}[d,\mathscr{P}]\alpha_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(3.37)

which means that
d
commutes with
\mathscr{P}
in the loop space.

Let us next compute

\displaystyle d^{2}\mathscr{P}\alpha_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\left(\partial_{M}\alpha\left(\dot{C}^{M}ds\right)+\left(\partial_{M}\alpha\delta C^{M}\right)\right)

(3.38)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\left(\partial_{M}\alpha\delta C^{M}\right)ds+\partial_{N}\partial_{M}\alpha\dot{C}^{N}ds\wedge\delta C^{M}

(3.39)

which does not vanish. In order to get something that vanish, we should apply the transgression map after each exterior derivative is taken. Thus, first we compute

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}d\mathscr{P}\alpha_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{M}\alpha\dot{C}^{M}ds

(3.40)

where we have projected out the term
\partial_{M}\alpha\delta C^{M}
and then we may compute

\displaystyle d\mathscr{P}d\mathscr{P}\alpha_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\left(\partial_{M}\alpha\delta C^{M}\right)ds

(3.41)

and finally we act with the transgression map again and get

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}d\mathscr{P}d\mathscr{P}\alpha_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(3.42)

as we project out the term
\frac{d}{ds}\left(\partial_{M}\alpha\delta C^{M}\right)ds
.

Next we will define a cross product in loop space, which is the spacetime wedge product transgressed into loop space,

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}\alpha_{p}{\times}\mathscr{P}\beta_{q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}\left(\alpha_{p}\wedge\beta_{q}\right)

Since it uses spacetime forms, this definition of the cross product extends without any issues all the way down to

. More explicitly, if

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}\alpha_{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(p-1)!}\alpha_{M_{1}\cdots M_{p}}\delta C^{M_{1}}\cdots\delta C^{M_{p-1}}\wedge\dot{C}^{M_{p}}ds

(3.44)

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}\beta_{q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(q-1)!}\beta_{M_{1}\cdots M_{q}}\delta C^{M_{1}}\cdots\delta C^{M_{q-1}}\wedge\dot{C}^{M_{q}}ds

(3.45)

then we define their cross product as

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}\alpha_{p}{\times}\mathscr{P}\beta_{q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{p+q}{p!q!}\alpha_{[M_{1}\cdots M_{p}}\beta_{N_{1}\cdots N_{q]}}\delta C^{M_{1}}\cdots\delta C^{N_{q-1}}\wedge\dot{C}^{N_{q}}ds

(3.46)

The exterior derivative commutes with the transgression map,

\displaystyle d\mathscr{P}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}d

(3.47)

and therefore acts on a cross product as follows,

\displaystyle d\left(\mathscr{P}\alpha_{p}{\times}\mathscr{P}\beta_{q}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\mathscr{P}\left(\alpha_{p}\wedge\beta_{q}\right)

(3.48)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}d\left(\alpha_{p}\wedge\beta_{q}\right)

(3.49)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}\left(d\alpha_{p}\wedge\beta_{q}+(-1)^{p}\alpha_{p}\wedge d\beta_{q}\right)

(3.50)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\mathscr{P}\alpha_{p}{\times}\mathscr{P}\beta_{q}+(-1)^{p}\mathscr{P}\alpha_{p}{\times}d\mathscr{P}\beta_{q}

(3.51)

Associated to the conformal vector fields

and

\displaystyle{\cal{V}}\;=\;\mathscr{P}V\;=\;\frac{V_{M}}{\mathscr{N}}\dot{C}^{M}ds

(3.54)

\displaystyle{\cal{U}}\;=\;\mathscr{P}U\;=\;\frac{U_{M}}{\mathscr{N}}\dot{C}^{M}ds

(3.55)

We have

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{V}\left(\frac{V_{M}}{\mathscr{N}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(3.56)

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{U}\left(\frac{V_{M}}{\mathscr{N}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(3.57)

whose loop space counterparts read

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{V}{\cal{V}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(3.58)

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{U}{\cal{V}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(3.59)

There are similar relations for

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}d{\cal{V}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V_{MN}\delta C^{M}\dot{C}^{N}ds

(3.60)

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}d{\cal{U}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle U_{MN}\delta C^{M}\dot{C}^{N}ds

(3.61)

We notice that when we compute in loop space, the transgression map is implicit since what
\mathscr{P}
does is just to ensure that we remain in the loop space. When we write formulas for loop space fields on which we carry out various manipulations like taking the exterior derivative, we may not want to write
\mathscr{P}
explicitly since
\mathscr{P}
can be understood implicitly by the fact that we are in the loop space. Namely, when we are in the loop space, then
\mathscr{P}
acts trivially as
\mathscr{P}
is a projection to the loop space.

We can now form a zero-form scalar in loop space from a (-1)-form in loop space by taking the cross product with
{\cal{V}}
. A scalar field
\phi
in the tensor multiplet transforms under Weyl transformation with Weyl weight
-2
if we define the metric to transform with the Weyl weight
+2
. It means that the combination
\mathscr{N}\phi
is Weyl invariant. From this Weyl invariant combination we form the (-1)-form

\displaystyle\Phi_{-1}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}\left(\mathscr{N}\phi\right)

(3.62)

in loop space, which is
\mathscr{N}\phi
evaluated at a point on the loop. Then we can construct two Weyl invariant zero-forms in loop space by the cross products

\displaystyle\Phi\;:=\;{\cal{V}}{\times}\Phi_{-1}\;=\;\frac{V_{M}}{\mathscr{N}}\mathscr{N}\phi\dot{C}^{M}ds

(3.63)

\displaystyle\Phi^{\vee}\;:=\;{\cal{U}}{\times}\Phi_{-1}\;=\;\frac{U_{M}}{\mathscr{N}}\mathscr{N}\phi\dot{C}^{M}ds

(3.64)

We will now introduce an interior product in such a way that Cartan’s formula for the Lie derivative,

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{V}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\iota_{V}+\iota_{V}d

(3.65)

is valid when it acts on forms in loop space. The Lie derivative of
\Phi(C)
is defined as

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{V}\Phi(C)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\alpha(C+\epsilon V)-\alpha(C)

(3.66)

Here again, just as in the exterior derivative, we include the projection
\mathscr{P}
back to loop space in the definition. Any form-field transforms like a scalar field,

\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime}(C^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Phi(C)

(3.67)

when we take into account how the differentials
\delta C^{M}
and
\dot{C}^{M}ds
transform. For the scalar field the definition (
3.66

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{V}\Phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{V_{M}}{\mathscr{N}}{\cal{L}}_{V}\left(\mathscr{N}\phi\right)\dot{C}^{M}ds

(3.68)

Now we like to reproduce this result from computing the right-hand side of Cartan’s formula (
3.65
). First we compute

\displaystyle d\Phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\left({\cal{V}}{\times}\Phi_{-1}\right)

(3.69)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d{\cal{V}}{\times}\Phi_{-1}-{\cal{V}}{\times}d\Phi_{-1}

(3.70)

We shall have

\displaystyle\iota_{V}d\Phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{\cal{V}}{\times}\iota_{V}d\Phi_{-1}

(3.71)

\displaystyle\iota_{V}\Phi_{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(3.72)

since with these assignments we satisfy Cartan’s rule as then we will have

\displaystyle\left(d\iota_{V}+\iota_{V}d\right)\Phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal{V}}{\times}{\cal{L}}_{V}\Phi_{-1}

(3.73)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{V}\left({\cal{V}}{\times}\Phi_{-1}\right)

(3.74)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{V}\Phi

(3.75)

Similarly,

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{U}\Phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{U_{M}}{\mathscr{N}}{\cal{L}}_{U}\left(\mathscr{N}\phi\right)\dot{C}^{M}ds

(3.76)

is reproduced by

\displaystyle\iota_{U}d\Phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-d\Phi_{-1}+{\cal{V}}{\times}{\cal{L}}_{U}\Phi_{-1}

(3.77)

\displaystyle d\iota_{U}\Phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\Phi_{-1}

(3.78)

Thus in particular we shall define

\displaystyle\iota_{U}\Phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Phi_{-1}

(3.79)

The interior product follows the same type of Leibniz rule as the exterior derivative,

\displaystyle\iota_{V}\left(\mathscr{P}\alpha_{p}{\times}\mathscr{P}\beta_{q}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iota_{V}\mathscr{P}\alpha_{p}+(-1)^{p}\mathscr{P}\alpha_{p}{\times}\iota_{V}\mathscr{P}\beta_{q}

(3.80)

For a
p

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p!}\alpha_{M_{1}M_{2}\cdots M_{p}M_{p+1}}\delta C^{M_{1}}\wedge\delta C^{M_{2}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\delta C^{M_{p}}\wedge\dot{C}^{M_{p+1}}ds

(3.81)

the interior product shall be defined as

\displaystyle\iota_{V}\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(p-1)!}\alpha_{M_{1}M_{2}\cdots M_{p}M_{p+1}}V^{M_{1}}\delta C^{M_{2}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\delta C^{M_{p}}\wedge\dot{C}^{M_{p+1}}ds

(3.82)

Thus we replace one of the differential
\delta C^{M}
with the vector field
V^{M}
. While this definition works fine for

we need to address the case

separately. Let us thus assume that
\alpha
is a zero-form in loop space,

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{M}\dot{C}^{M}ds

(3.83)

Using the definition, a short computation shows that its Lie derivative is given by

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{V}\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{V}\alpha_{M}\dot{C}^{M}ds

(3.84)

Let us present the short computation here,

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{V}\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\alpha(C+\epsilon V)-\alpha(C)

(3.85)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(\alpha_{M}+\epsilon V^{N}\partial_{N}\alpha_{M}\right)\frac{d}{ds}\left(C^{M}+\epsilon V^{M}\right)ds-\alpha(C)

(3.86)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(V^{N}\partial_{N}\alpha_{M}+\partial_{M}V^{N}\alpha_{N}\right)\dot{C}^{M}

(3.87)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{V}\alpha_{M}\dot{C}^{M}

(3.88)

In particular, no integration by parts and no boundary term is thrown away in order to reach this result.

We shall now find
\iota_{V}\alpha
by requiring Cartan’s formula reproduces this result. First we compute the exterior derivative,

\displaystyle d\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{M}\alpha_{N}-\partial_{N}\alpha_{M}\right)\delta C^{M}\dot{C}^{N}ds

(3.89)

and then since this is now a one-form in loop space, we can use the above definition to evaluate the action of the interior product
\iota_{V}
on this one-form,

\displaystyle\iota_{V}d\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(V^{M}\partial_{M}\alpha_{N}+\partial_{N}V^{M}\alpha_{M}\right)\dot{C}^{N}ds-\partial_{N}\left(V^{M}\alpha_{M}\right)\dot{C}^{N}ds

(3.90)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{V}\alpha_{N}\dot{C}^{N}ds-\partial_{N}\left(V^{M}\alpha_{M}\right)\dot{C}^{N}ds

(3.91)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{V}\alpha-\partial_{N}\left(V^{M}\alpha_{M}\right)\dot{C}^{N}ds

(3.92)

Now we see that in order for Cartan’s formula to work, we must have

\displaystyle d\iota_{V}\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{N}\left(V^{M}\alpha_{M}\right)\dot{C}^{N}ds

(3.93)

and accordingly we shall define

\displaystyle\iota_{V}\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V^{M}\alpha_{M}

(3.94)

We can now summarize our findings by the following simple formula for the interior product in loop space,

\displaystyle\iota_{V}\mathscr{P}\alpha_{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}\iota_{V}\alpha_{p}

(3.95)

On the right-hand we have the usual interior product of a spacetime
p
-form that is subsequently transgressed into loop space. One may easily confirm that this way of defining the interior product satisfies all the desired properties we have outlined above.

\displaystyle=

7
The nonabelian transgression map

Our nonabelian transgression map is closely related to the dimensional reduction map. Since we have the two-form gauge potential in our 5d description, the ideas that we present here might be useful to obtain a dual formulation of 5d super Yang-Mills.

We assume that there is some vector field
n^{M}
. We will not specify this vector field from the outset. Instead the theory may tell us if there are any restrictions we may have to impose on
n^{M}
. If we are given a nonabelian
p
-form in spacetime

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p!}\alpha^{a}_{M_{1}\cdots M_{p}}dx^{M_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{M_{p}}t_{a}

where

\displaystyle[t_{a},t_{b}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle if_{ab}{}^{c}t_{c}

(7.2)

then we define the transgression map for
p=1,2,3,...
as

\displaystyle\alpha\;\mapsto\;\mathscr{P}\alpha_{p}\;=\;\frac{1}{(p-1)!}\int\alpha^{a}_{M_{1}\cdots M_{p}}\delta C^{M_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\delta C^{M_{p-1}}\wedge n^{M_{p}}t_{a}(s)ds

(7.3)

Thus for
p=2
and
p=1
we have

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}\alpha_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\alpha^{a}_{MN}\delta C^{M}n^{N}t_{a}(s)ds

(7.4)

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}\alpha_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\alpha^{a}_{M}n^{M}t_{a}(s)ds

(7.5)

Here
t_{a}(s)
satisfy a loop algebra

\displaystyle[t_{a}(s),t_{b}(s^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle if_{ab}{}^{c}\delta(s-s^{\prime})t_{c}(s)

(7.6)

The idea of using a loop algebra for the nonabelian tensor multiplet first appeared in
[
8
]
, but here we develop this idea further. Here we have an integral over the loop, which is different from how we transgressed in the abelian case. We motivate the integral by the fact that in the nonabelian case we have
t_{a}(s)
that serve as basis elements that enable us to extract information the integrand from the integral even when there is no differential
\delta C^{M}(s)
, which is the case of a zero form in loop space. Below we will also explain why there is no (-1)-form in the nonabelian case, which is also different from the abelian loop space. If we are given a Lie algebra trace such that

\displaystyle{\mbox{tr}}(t_{a}(s)t_{b}(s^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\delta_{ab}\delta(s-s^{\prime})

then by taking the trace of the transgressed form with

(-1)

\displaystyle d{\cal{A}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int(dB)_{MNP}^{a}\delta C^{M}\wedge\delta C^{N}n^{P}t_{a}(s)ds

(7.32)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int{\cal{L}}_{n}B_{MN}^{a}\delta C^{M}\wedge\delta C^{N}t_{a}(s)ds

Here

\displaystyle(dB)_{MNP}^{a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 3\partial_{[M}B_{NP]}^{a}

(7.33)

and then

\displaystyle-ie{\cal{A}}\wedge{\cal{A}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e}{2}\int B_{MN}^{b}B_{PQ}^{c}\delta C^{M}\wedge\delta C^{P}n^{N}n^{Q}f_{bc}^{a}t_{a}(s)ds

(7.34)

Thus, if we put

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{n}B_{MN}^{a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(7.35)

then we find that the nonabelian field strength takes the form

\displaystyle{\cal{F}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int H_{MNP}^{a}\delta C^{M}\wedge\delta C^{N}n^{P}t_{a}(s)ds

(7.36)

with

\displaystyle H_{MNP}^{a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(dB)_{MNP}^{a}+eA^{b}_{[M}B^{c}_{NP]}f_{bc}{}^{a}

(7.37)

Let us here also comment that we have
n^{M}A_{M}^{a}=0
from
A_{M}^{a}=B_{MN}^{a}n^{N}
and therefore the gauge covariant Lie derivative equals the ordinary Lie derivative,
\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{n}}={\cal{L}}_{n}-ie[n^{M}A_{M},\bullet]={\cal{L}}_{n}
.

For the scalar field in loop space, we will define it as

\displaystyle\Phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\phi^{a}V_{M}n^{M}t_{a}(s)ds

(7.38)

Then

\displaystyle D\Phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int D_{M}\left(\phi^{a}V_{M}n^{M}\right)\delta C^{M}t_{a}(s)ds

(7.39)

where we define the gauge covariant derivative as

\displaystyle D_{M}\varphi^{a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{M}\varphi^{a}+eA_{M}^{b}\varphi^{c}f_{bc}{}^{a}

(7.40)

where the gauge potential is defined as
A_{M}^{a}=B_{MN}^{a}n^{N}
. Then we get

\displaystyle\iota_{U}D\Phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{U}}\left(\phi^{a}V_{M}n^{M}\right)t_{a}(s)ds

(7.41)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{U}}\Phi

(7.42)

and thus we shall define

\displaystyle\iota_{U}\Phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(7.43)

in order for the result to be consistent with the gauge covariant version of Cartan’s formula,

\displaystyle\iota_{U}D+D\iota_{U}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{U}}

(7.44)

where
\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{U}}
is the gauge covariant Lie derivative that can be obtained by just replacing all derivatives with covariant derivatives in the usual Lie derivative.

Let us now establish the following expected relation in loop space,

\displaystyle D\left({\cal{V}}{\times}\Phi_{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d{\cal{V}}{\times}\Phi_{-1}-{\cal{V}}{\times}D\Phi_{-1}

(7.45)

The left-hand side is computed as follows. First we compute

\displaystyle d\Phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int V_{MN}\mathscr{N}\phi^{a}\delta C^{M}n^{N}t_{a}(s)ds

(7.48)

\displaystyle-\int\left(\frac{V_{M}}{\mathscr{N}}\partial_{N}\left(\mathscr{N}\phi^{a}\right)-\frac{V_{N}}{\mathscr{N}}\partial_{M}\left(\mathscr{N}\phi^{a}\right)\right)\delta C^{M}n^{N}t_{a}(s)ds

\displaystyle+\int{\cal{L}}_{n}\left(V_{M}\phi^{a}\right)\delta C^{M}t_{a}(s)ds

and

\displaystyle-ie[{\cal{A}},\Phi]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e\int B_{MN}^{b}\phi^{c}V_{P}\delta C^{M}n^{N}n^{P}f_{bc}{}^{a}t_{a}(s)ds

(7.49)

This leads to the result

\displaystyle D\Phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int V_{MN}\mathscr{N}\phi^{a}\delta C^{M}n^{N}t_{a}(s)ds

(7.52)

\displaystyle-\int\left(\frac{V_{M}}{\mathscr{N}}D_{N}\left(\mathscr{N}\phi^{a}\right)-\frac{V_{N}}{\mathscr{N}}D_{M}\left(\mathscr{N}\phi^{a}\right)\right)\delta C^{M}n^{N}t_{a}(s)ds

\displaystyle+\int\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{n}}\left(V_{M}\phi^{a}\right)\delta C^{M}t_{a}(s)ds.

For the right-hand side, we compute the two terms as follows,

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}\left(\frac{1}{2}V_{MN}dx^{M}\wedge dx^{N}\mathscr{N}\phi^{a}t_{a}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int V_{MN}\mathscr{N}\phi^{a}\delta C^{M}n^{N}t_{a}(s)

(7.53)

and

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}\left({\cal{V}}{\times}D\Phi_{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}\left(\frac{V_{M}}{\mathscr{N}}dx^{M}\wedge dx^{N}D_{N}\left(\mathscr{N}\phi^{a}\right)t_{a}\right)

(7.54)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\left(\frac{V_{M}}{\mathscr{N}}D_{N}\left(\mathscr{N}\phi^{a}\right)-\frac{V_{N}}{\mathscr{N}}D_{M}\left(\mathscr{N}\phi^{a}\right)\right)\delta C^{M}n^{N}t_{a}(s)ds

(7.55)

In order to match with the left-hand side, we shall take

\displaystyle\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{n}}\left(V_{M}\phi^{a}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(7.56)

but to really show the equivalence, we need to examine the commutator terms that come from these two covariant derivatives closely. These commutator terms are

\displaystyle e\int\left(V_{M}B_{NP}^{b}-V_{N}B_{MP}^{b}\right)\phi^{c}f_{bc}{}^{a}\delta C^{M}n^{N}n^{P}t_{a}(s)ds

(7.57)

and now we can see that the first term vanishes because
B_{NP}^{a}
is antisymmetric and contracted with
n^{N}n^{P}
that is symmetric. So the commutator term is in precise agreement, including the sign once we notice the minus sign in (
7.45
), with (
7.49
).

Let us now compute
D^{\dagger}\left({\cal{V}}{\times}\alpha\right)
using the definition of
D^{\dagger}
from a putative inner product using some simple rules that this inner product is supposed to satisfy, such as

\displaystyle(\beta,D\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(D^{\dagger}\beta,\alpha)

(7.58)

\displaystyle(\beta,{\cal{V}}{\times}\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\iota_{V}\beta,\alpha)

(7.59)

Then we get

\displaystyle\left(\beta,D^{\dagger}\left({\cal{V}}{\times}\alpha\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(D\beta,{\cal{V}}{\times}\alpha)

(7.60)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\iota_{V}D\beta,\alpha\right)

(7.61)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{V}}\beta,\alpha\right)-\left(D\iota_{V}\beta,\alpha\right)

(7.62)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left(\beta,\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{V}}\alpha\right)-\left(\iota_{V}\beta,D^{\dagger}\alpha\right)

(7.63)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left(\beta,\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{V}}\alpha+{\cal{V}}{\times}D^{\dagger}\alpha\right)

(7.64)

where

\displaystyle\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{V}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D\iota_{V}+\iota_{V}D

(7.65)

is the covariant Lie derivative that is computed as follows,

\displaystyle\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{V}}\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{V}\alpha-ie[{\cal{A}},\iota_{V}\alpha]-ie\iota_{V}[{\cal{A}},\alpha]

(7.66)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{V}\alpha-ie[\iota_{V}{\cal{A}},\alpha]

(7.67)

From the above result we read off

\displaystyle D^{\dagger}\left({\cal{V}}{\times}\alpha\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{V}}\alpha-{\cal{V}}{\times}D^{\dagger}\alpha

(7.68)

It is now interesting to examine what we get when

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\alpha^{a}_{M}n^{M}t_{a}(s)ds

(7.69)

is a zero-form. Then

\displaystyle{\cal{V}}{\times}\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}\left(\frac{V_{M}}{\mathscr{N}}dx^{M}\wedge\alpha^{a}_{N}dx^{N}t_{a}\right)

(7.70)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\left(\frac{V_{M}}{\mathscr{N}}\alpha^{a}_{N}-\frac{V_{N}}{\mathscr{N}}\alpha^{a}_{M}\right)\delta C^{M}n^{N}t_{a}(s)ds

(7.71)

For a
p
-form

\displaystyle\alpha_{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p!}\int\alpha_{M_{1}\cdots M_{p}M_{p+1}}\delta C^{M_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\delta C^{M_{p}}n^{M_{p+1}}t_{a}(s)ds

(7.72)

we shall define the codifferential operator as

\displaystyle d^{\dagger}\alpha_{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{(p-1)!}\int\mathscr{N}^{3-p}D^{M_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{\mathscr{N}^{2-p}}\alpha_{M_{1}\cdots M_{p}M_{p+1}}\right)

(7.74)

\displaystyle\delta C^{M_{2}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\delta C^{M_{p}}n^{M_{p+1}}t_{a}(s)ds

when

and

\displaystyle d^{\dagger}\alpha_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathscr{N}^{3}D^{M}\left(\frac{1}{\mathscr{N}^{2}}\alpha^{a}_{M}\right)t_{a}

(7.75)

when
p=0
. To see this, we repeat essentially the computation that we did in the abelian case,

\displaystyle D^{\dagger}\left({\cal{V}}{\times}\alpha\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int\mathscr{N}^{2}D^{M}\left(\left(\frac{V_{M}}{\mathscr{N}^{2}}\alpha^{a}_{N}-\frac{V_{N}}{\mathscr{N}^{2}}\alpha^{a}_{M}\right)\right)t_{a}(s)n^{N}ds

(7.76)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{V}}\alpha^{a}_{N}n^{N}t_{a}(s)ds+\int V_{N}\mathscr{N}^{2}D^{M}\left(\frac{1}{\mathscr{N}^{2}}\alpha^{a}_{M}\right)n^{N}t_{a}(s)ds

(7.77)

Let us now check that having
\Phi_{-1}=0
is consistent with having

as well. Direct application of the definition yields

\displaystyle D\Phi_{-1}\;=\;\mathscr{P}\left(D_{M}\left(\mathscr{N}\phi^{a}\right)dx^{M}t_{a}\right)\;=\;\int D_{M}\left(\mathscr{N}\phi^{a}\right)n^{M}t_{a}(s)ds

(7.78)

which vanishes only if we impose the constraint

\displaystyle\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{n}}\left(\mathscr{N}\phi^{a}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(7.79)

Hence, in the nonabelian case we have to perform dimensional reduction along
n^{M}
in order for our nonabelian transgression map to be consistent.

Let us notice that it is sufficient for us to assume that
n^{M}
is a conformal Killing vector, not necessarily a Kiling vector. That is so because we require
\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{n}}=0
when it acts on loop space fields and all our loop space fields are Weyl invariant. Alternatively, if we translate back to spacetime fields then the field quantity that
\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{n}}
will act on and set to zero by the dimensional reduction, is always necessarily a Weyl invariant combination such as
\mathscr{N}\phi^{a}
.

In the nonabelian loop space we have
p
-form fields
\alpha_{p}
that can be either bosonic or fermionic and they can be either wedge multiplied or cross multiplied but for the sake of illustration let us assume that they are bosonic. In commutators we always use the wedge product and never the cross product,

\displaystyle[\alpha_{p},\beta_{q}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{p}\wedge\beta_{q}-\beta_{q}\wedge\alpha_{p}\qquad{\mbox{if $(-1)^{pq}=1$}}

(7.80)

\displaystyle\{\alpha_{p},\beta_{q}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{p}\wedge\beta_{q}+\beta_{q}\wedge\alpha_{p}\qquad{\mbox{if $(-1)^{pq}=-1$}}

(7.81)

Let us introduce a graded commutator

\displaystyle[\alpha_{p},\beta_{q}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{p}\wedge\beta_{q}-(-1)^{pq}\beta_{q}\wedge\alpha_{p}

(7.82)

with the property

\displaystyle[\alpha_{p},\beta_{q}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{pq}[\beta_{q},\alpha_{p}\}

(7.83)

We have also introduced a cross product. In all our equations the cross product only appears between a geometrical field and a field in the tensor multiplet. The geometrical field does not take values in the loop algebra of the gauge group. Let us denote the geometrical field as
\alpha_{r}
of rank
r
, it could be for instance
{\cal{V}}
or
d{\cal{V}}
. Then the cross product has the following property,

\displaystyle[\alpha_{r}{\times}\beta_{p},\gamma_{q}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{r}{\times}[\beta_{p},\gamma_{q}\}

(7.84)

From these properties we can derive the additional property

\displaystyle[\alpha_{r}{\times}\beta_{p},\gamma_{q}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{(r+1)p}[\beta_{p},\alpha_{r}{\times}\gamma_{q}\}

(7.85)

This property can be shown as follows,

\displaystyle[\alpha_{r}{\times}\beta_{p},\gamma_{q}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{r}{\times}[\beta_{p},\gamma_{q}\}

(7.86)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{pq}\alpha_{r}{\times}[\gamma_{q},\beta_{p}\}

(7.87)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{pq}[\alpha_{r}{\times}\gamma_{q},\beta_{p}]

(7.88)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{(r+1)p}[\beta_{p},\alpha_{r}{\times}\gamma_{q}]

(7.89)

8
Bianchi identities in nonabelian loop space

We now turn to the case of a nonabelian gauge group. The gauge field strength

\displaystyle{\cal{F}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d{\cal{A}}-ie{\cal{A}}\wedge{\cal{A}}

(8.1)

satisfies the Bianchi identity

\displaystyle D{\cal{F}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(8.2)

that we may expand as

\displaystyle d{\cal{F}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ie\left({\cal{A}}\wedge{\cal{F}}-{\cal{F}}\wedge{\cal{A}}\right)

(8.3)

All that goes into showing this Bianchi identity are the properties

\displaystyle d\left(\alpha_{p}\wedge\beta_{q}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\alpha_{p}\wedge\beta_{q}+(-1)^{p}\alpha_{p}\wedge d\beta_{q}

(8.4)

\displaystyle(\alpha\wedge\beta)\wedge\gamma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha\wedge\left(\beta\wedge\gamma\right)

(8.5)

that we shall require of the wedge product in loop space. Then the proof of the nonabelian Bianchi identity is just one line,

\displaystyle d{\cal{F}}\;=\;-ie\left(d{\cal{A}}\wedge{\cal{A}}-{\cal{A}}\wedge d{\cal{A}}\right)\;=\;-ie\left({\cal{F}}\wedge{\cal{A}}-{\cal{A}}\wedge{\cal{F}}\right)

(8.6)

As in the abelian case, we will again expand

\displaystyle{\cal{F}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h+{\cal{U}}{\times}f+{\cal{V}}{\times}g-{\cal{U}}{\times}{\cal{V}}{\times}{\cal{R}}

(8.7)

in irreducible components such that

\displaystyle\iota_{V}{\cal{F}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f-{\cal{V}}{\times}{\cal{R}}

(8.8)

\displaystyle\iota_{U}{\cal{F}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g+{\cal{U}}{\times}{\cal{R}}

(8.9)

\displaystyle\iota_{U}\iota_{V}{\cal{F}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{\cal{R}}

(8.10)

We then again find that

\displaystyle\iota_{V}d{\cal{F}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{V}h-\widetilde{df}+d{\cal{V}}{\times}{\cal{R}}

(8.12)

\displaystyle+{\cal{V}}{\times}\left({\cal{L}}_{V}g-{\cal{L}}_{U}f-\widetilde{d{\cal{R}}}\right)

\displaystyle\iota_{U}d{\cal{F}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{U}h-\widetilde{dg}+d{\cal{U}}{\times}{\cal{R}}

(8.14)

\displaystyle-{\cal{U}}{\times}\left({\cal{L}}_{V}g-{\cal{L}}_{U}f-\widetilde{d{\cal{R}}}\right)

\displaystyle\iota_{U}\iota_{V}d{\cal{F}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{V}g-{\cal{L}}_{U}f-\widetilde{d{\cal{R}}}

(8.15)

What is new here are the commutator terms. First we expand
{\cal{A}}=\widetilde{\cal{A}}+{\cal{V}}{\times}\iota_{U}{\cal{A}}+{\cal{U}}{\times}\iota_{V}{\cal{A}}
and then we expand

\displaystyle\iota_{V}\left({\cal{A}}\wedge{\cal{F}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iota_{V}{\cal{A}}\wedge{\cal{F}}-{\cal{A}}\wedge\iota_{V}{\cal{F}}

(8.16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iota_{V}{\cal{A}}\wedge h-\widetilde{\cal{A}}\wedge f

(8.20)

\displaystyle+\iota_{V}{\cal{A}}\wedge{\cal{V}}{\times}g-{\cal{V}}{\times}\iota_{U}{\cal{A}}\wedge f+\widetilde{\cal{A}}\wedge{\cal{V}}{\times}{\cal{R}}

\displaystyle+\iota_{V}{\cal{A}}\wedge{\cal{U}}{\times}f-{\cal{U}}{\times}\iota_{V}{\cal{A}}\wedge f

\displaystyle-\iota_{V}{\cal{A}}\wedge{\cal{U}}{\times}{\cal{V}}{\times}{\cal{R}}+{\cal{U}}{\times}\iota_{V}{\cal{A}}\wedge{\cal{V}}{\times}{\cal{R}}

We now apply the rule

\displaystyle[{\cal{U}}{\times}\alpha_{p},\beta_{q}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{p}[\alpha_{p},{\cal{U}}{\times}\beta_{q}]

(8.21)

where the commutator acts only on Lie algebra generators, alternatively is is sometimes an anticommutator depending on
p
and
q
(when
pq
is odd) such that we can use the loop algebra in the end for the generators. Either way, we have

\displaystyle[{\cal{U}}{\times}\alpha_{p},\beta_{q}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{U_{M}}{\mathscr{N}}\alpha^{a}_{M_{1}\cdots M_{p}P}\beta^{b}_{N_{1}\cdots N_{q}Q}n^{P}n^{Q}if_{ab}{}^{c}t_{c}(s)

(8.23)

\displaystyle\delta C^{M}\delta C^{M_{1}}\cdots\delta C^{M_{p}}\delta C^{N_{1}}\cdots\delta C^{N_{q}}ds

\displaystyle[\alpha_{p},{\cal{U}}{\times}\beta_{q}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\alpha^{a}_{M_{1}\cdots M_{p}P}\frac{U_{M}}{\mathscr{N}}\beta^{b}_{N_{1}\cdots N_{q}Q}n^{P}n^{Q}if_{ab}{}^{c}t_{c}(s)

(8.25)

\displaystyle\delta C^{M_{1}}\cdots\delta C^{M_{p}}\delta C^{M}\delta C^{N_{1}}\cdots\delta C^{N_{q}}ds

Now by permuting
\delta C^{M}
to the left through
p
differentials in the second expression, we get the sign factor
(-1)^{p}
. We also have

\displaystyle{\cal{U}}{\times}[\alpha_{p},\beta_{q}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[{\cal{U}}{\times}\alpha_{p},\beta_{q}]

(8.26)

Then

\displaystyle\iota_{V}[{\cal{A}},{\cal{F}}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[\iota_{V}{\cal{A}},h]-[\widetilde{\cal{A}},f]

(8.28)

\displaystyle+{\cal{V}}{\times}\left([\iota_{V}{\cal{A}},g]-[\widetilde{\cal{A}},R]-[\iota_{U},f]\right)

Similarly we have

\displaystyle\iota_{U}[{\cal{A}},{\cal{F}}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[\iota_{U},h]-[\widetilde{\cal{A}},g]

(8.30)

\displaystyle+{\cal{U}}{\times}\left([\iota_{U},f]+[\widetilde{\cal{A}},{\cal{R}}]-[\iota_{V}{\cal{A}},g]\right)

We can now see that the terms combine into

\displaystyle\iota_{V}D{\cal{F}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{V}h}-\widetilde{Df}+d{\cal{V}}{\times}{\cal{R}}

(8.32)

\displaystyle+{\cal{V}}{\times}\left(\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{V}g}-\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{U}f}-\widetilde{D{\cal{R}}}\right)

and

\displaystyle\iota_{U}D{\cal{F}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{U}}h-\widetilde{Dg}+d{\cal{U}}{\times}{\cal{R}}

(8.34)

\displaystyle+{\cal{U}}{\times}\left(\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{U}}f-\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{V}}g+\widetilde{D{\cal{R}}}\right)

Both these results lead to

\displaystyle\iota_{U}\iota_{V}D{\cal{F}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{V}}g-\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{U}}f-\widetilde{D{\cal{R}}}

(8.35)

From these relations, we conclude that we have the following nonabelian Bianchi identities,

\displaystyle\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{V}h}-\widetilde{Df}+d{\cal{V}}{\times}{\cal{R}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(8.36)

\displaystyle\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{U}h}+\widetilde{Dg}+d{\cal{U}}{\times}{\cal{R}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(8.37)

\displaystyle\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{V}g}-\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{U}}f-\widetilde{D{\cal{R}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(8.38)

9
Supersymmetry in nonabelian loop space

We will now construct the nonabelian generalization of the supersymmetry variations in loop space and show that they close on-shell. For the nonabelian generalization, we shall replace the differential
d
acting on a
p
-form
\alpha
with the covariant differential operator

\displaystyle D\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\alpha-ie[{\cal{A}},\alpha]

(9.1)

and we will accordingly replace
{\cal{L}}_{U}\alpha
and
{\cal{L}}_{V}\alpha
with

\displaystyle{\overline{{\cal{L}}}}_{V}\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{V}\alpha-ie[{\cal{B}},\alpha]

(9.2)

\displaystyle{\overline{{\cal{L}}}}_{U}\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{U}\alpha-ie[{\cal{C}},\alpha]

(9.3)

where

\displaystyle{\cal{B}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iota_{V}{\cal{A}}-\Phi

(9.4)

\displaystyle{\cal{C}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iota_{U}{\cal{A}}

(9.5)

Here we prefer to use
{\cal{B}}
in place of
\iota_{V}{\cal{A}}
because
{\cal{B}}
will be assumed to be a supersymmetry invariant also in the nonabelian generalization. This will simplify our computations of the closure as
\bar{{\cal{L}}}_{V}
will be invariant. However
\bar{{\cal{L}}}_{U}=\widehat{{\cal{L}}}_{U}
will vary under supersymmetry. We will assume that

\displaystyle\delta{\cal{C}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\Upsilon_{0}

(9.6)

which induces a corresponding variation of
\bar{{\cal{L}}}_{U}
. These Lie derivatives satisfy the commutation relation

\displaystyle[[{\overline{{\cal{L}}}}_{U},{\overline{{\cal{L}}}}_{V}],\alpha]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-ie[{\cal{L}}_{U}{\cal{B}}-{\cal{L}}_{V}{\cal{C}}-ie[{\cal{C}},{\cal{B}}],\alpha]

(9.7)

We also have

\displaystyle[D,{\overline{{\cal{L}}}}_{U}]\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ie[\iota_{U}{\cal{F}},\alpha]

(9.8)

\displaystyle[D,{\overline{{\cal{L}}}}_{V}]\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ie[\iota_{V}{\cal{F}},\alpha]+ie[D\Phi,\alpha]

(9.9)

Let us also note that

\displaystyle[\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{U}},\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{V}}]\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-ie[{\cal{R}},\alpha]

(9.10)

When generalizing the abelian supersymmetry variations, we replace
d
with
D
and we replace
{\cal{L}}_{V}
and
{\cal{L}}_{U}
with
{\overline{{\cal{L}}}}_{V}
and
{\overline{{\cal{L}}}}_{U}
. We then adjust additional commutators between scalar and fermion fields so that the supersymmetry variations close on-shell. In this way, we have found the following supersymmetry variations,

\displaystyle\delta(\alpha{\times}\Phi_{-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\alpha{\times}\Psi_{-1}

(9.11)

\displaystyle\delta(\alpha{\times}\Psi_{-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha{\times}{\overline{{\cal{L}}}}_{V}\Phi_{-1}

(9.12)

\displaystyle\delta{\cal{B}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(9.13)

\displaystyle\delta{\cal{C}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\Upsilon_{0}

(9.14)

\displaystyle\delta\widetilde{{\cal{A}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\chi

(9.15)

\displaystyle\delta f

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i{\overline{{\cal{L}}}}_{V}\chi+id{\cal{V}}{\times}\Psi_{-1}

(9.16)

\displaystyle\delta g

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i{\overline{{\cal{L}}}}_{U}\chi-id{\cal{U}}{\times}\Psi_{-1}-i\widetilde{D\Upsilon}+e[\Phi^{\vee},\chi]

(9.17)

\displaystyle\delta h

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\widetilde{D\chi}+id{\cal{V}}{\times}\Upsilon

(9.18)

\displaystyle\delta{\cal{R}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\left({\overline{{\cal{L}}}}_{V}\Upsilon+{\overline{{\cal{L}}}}_{U}\Psi+{\overline{{\cal{L}}}}_{V}\Psi^{\vee}\right)+e[\Upsilon_{0},\Phi]

(9.19)

\displaystyle\delta\chi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left(f+*f\right)-d{\cal{V}}{\times}\Phi_{-1}

(9.20)

\displaystyle\delta\Upsilon

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left({\cal{R}}-*h\right)-{\overline{{\cal{L}}}}_{U}\Phi-{\overline{{\cal{L}}}}_{V}\Phi^{\vee}

(9.21)

Here
\alpha
could be any supersymmetry invariant
p

10
Five-dimensional super Yang-Mills

We expect that any commutator terms in the five-dimensional super Yang-Mills theory will not be sensitive to the direction
n^{M}
along which we perform the dimensional reduction. We do not want to put
n^{M}
along neither
V^{M}
nor along
U^{M}
which are both lightlike directions. Instead we will in this section choose
n^{M}
to be given by

\displaystyle n^{M}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V^{M}+U^{M}+\widetilde{n}^{M}

(10.1)

which, if we assume
\mathscr{N}>0
, will be a spacelike conformal Killing vector field. Here we allow for a possibly nonvanishing transverse component
\widetilde{n}^{M}
in order to allow for a more general dimensional reduction when such a conformal Killing vector field
n^{M}
exists on the given six-manifold. It is nontrivial task to perform dimensional reduction along a conformal Killing vector field. However, such a dimensional reduction was recently carried out in
[
9
]
along the conformal lightlike vector field
V
. While dimensional reduction along
n^{M}
is inaccessible to us, we expect that as far as commutator terms in the resulting five-dimensional theory concern, those will not depend on the choice of conformal Killing vector field along which we reduce.

The dimensional reduction along
V
is implemented by imposing the constraints

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{V}\Psi_{MN}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\Omega}{3}\Psi_{MN}

(10.2)

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{V}\phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\Omega}{3}\phi

(10.3)

on the matter fields. Here we will not consider the gauge field since our goal will be to match the fermionic equations of motion in loop space with the corresponding fermionic equations of motion in the five-dimensional theory. We are particularly interested in the commutator terms here since for abelian tensor multiplet we already know that it reduces correctly to a corresponding five-dimensional vector multiplet.

Using a Fierz identity in appendix
A
we find that

\displaystyle\Psi^{aMN}\Psi^{b}_{MN}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{5}{4}\bar{\lambda}^{aI}\Gamma^{P}\lambda^{b}_{I}V_{P}-\frac{1}{4}\bar{\lambda}^{aI}\Gamma^{RST}\lambda^{b}_{J}\left(\Theta_{RST}\right)^{J}{}_{I}

(10.4)

and

\displaystyle\psi^{a}\psi^{b}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{8}\bar{\lambda}^{aI}\Gamma^{M}\lambda^{b}_{I}V_{M}+\frac{1}{24}\bar{\lambda}^{aI}\Gamma^{MNP}\lambda^{b}_{J}\left(\Theta_{MNP}\right)^{J}{}_{I}

from which it follows that

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}^{aI}\Gamma^{M}\lambda^{b}_{I}V_{M}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\Psi^{aMN}\Psi^{b}_{MN}-3\psi^{a}\psi^{b}

(10.6)

If we expand

\displaystyle\Psi^{aMN}\Psi^{b}_{MN}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi^{aMN}\chi^{b}_{MN}-2\psi^{a}\psi^{b}

(10.7)

then the result can also be written as

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}^{aI}\Gamma^{M}\lambda^{b}_{I}V_{M}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\chi^{aMN}\chi^{b}_{MN}-2\psi^{a}\psi^{b}

(10.8)

In the five-dimensional super Yang-Mills Lagrangian we have the fermionic terms

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{F}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i}{2}\bar{\lambda}^{aI}\Gamma^{M}D_{M}\lambda^{b}_{I}g_{ab}-\frac{ie}{2}\bar{\lambda}^{aI}\Gamma^{M}\lambda^{b}_{I}V_{M}\phi^{c}f_{abc}

Our Lie algebra conventions are

\displaystyle[t_{a},t_{b}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle if_{ab}{}^{c}t_{c}

(10.10)

\displaystyle{\mbox{tr}}\left(t_{a}t_{b}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g_{ab}

(10.11)

\displaystyle f_{abc}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{ab}{}^{d}g_{dc}

(10.12)

\displaystyle g^{ab}g_{bc}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta^{a}_{c}

(10.13)

To obtain the nonabelian Lagrangian in cohomological form, we will follow the following strategy. First we note that we need to replace
\nabla_{M}
with
D_{M}
in the correspondence

\displaystyle\frac{i}{2}\bar{\lambda}^{I}\Gamma^{M}\nabla_{M}\lambda_{I}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{4\mathscr{N}}\chi^{MN}{\cal{L}}_{U}\chi_{MN}+\frac{i}{4\mathscr{N}}\chi^{MN}\partial_{M}\psi_{N}

(10.16)

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{\mathscr{N}}V_{MN}\psi^{M}\psi^{N}

11
Discussion

We have seen that in the nonabelian loop space the self-consistency of our nonabelian transgression map requires us to perform dimensional reduction along the conformal Killing vector field
n_{M}
.

On the other hand, when we formulate the theory in loop space then we do not really see that we need to perform the dimensional reduction. For a generic loop space field
\Phi(C)
we do not see that we need to impose any restriction on its Lie derivative
\widehat{{\cal{L}}_{n}}\Phi(C)
from supersymmetry in loop space. Instead, what we have seen is that we had to impose corresponding restrictions on the Lie derivative on spacetime fields that are mapped by a transgression map into loop space in order for the transgression map to be selfconsistent.

This observation might mean that there is a classical field theory in loop space where one uses axiomatic rules for the operations
\wedge
and
\times
that are intended to retain locality around the loop. This might lead to a formulation of the nonabelian tensor multiplet describing tensionless strings propagating in six dimensions where these strings are the loops created and annihilated by the loop space quantum fields. In such a formulation the loop space fields would be the fundamental degrees of freedom associated to strings and we would not have spacetime fields.

g_{MN}=e^{2\sigma}\eta_{MN}
. We begin by expanding

\displaystyle\nabla_{M}\partial_{N}\sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{M}\partial_{N}\sigma+\Gamma_{MN}^{P}\partial_{P}\sigma

(D.17)

where

\displaystyle\Gamma^{P}_{MN}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}g^{PQ}\left(-\partial_{Q}g_{MN}+\partial_{M}g_{NQ}+\partial_{N}g_{MQ}\right)

(D.18)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta^{PQ}\left(-\partial_{Q}\sigma\eta_{MN}+\partial_{M}\sigma\eta_{NQ}+\partial_{N}\sigma\eta_{MQ}\right)

(D.19)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-g_{MN}\nabla^{P}\sigma+\delta^{P}_{N}\partial_{M}\sigma+\delta^{P}_{M}\partial_{N}\sigma

(D.20)

Then

\displaystyle\nabla_{M}\partial_{N}\sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{M}\partial_{N}\sigma-g_{MN}\nabla^{P}\sigma\partial_{P}\sigma+2\partial_{M}\sigma\partial_{N}\sigma

(D.21)

and then

\displaystyle g^{MN}\partial_{M}\partial_{N}\sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\sigma+4\left(\nabla_{M}\sigma\right)^{2}

(D.22)

where on the right-hand side everything is written covariantly with respect to
g_{MN}
. Then

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-10g^{MN}\left(\partial_{M}\partial_{N}\sigma+2\partial_{M}\sigma\partial_{N}\sigma\right)

(D.23)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-10\nabla^{2}\sigma+40\left(\nabla_{M}\sigma\right)^{2}-20\left(\nabla_{M}\sigma\right)^{2}

(D.24)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-10\nabla^{2}\sigma+20\left(\nabla_{M}\sigma\right)^{2}

(D.25)

and for
\sigma=\frac{1}{2}\log\mathscr{N}
this becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{5}R

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left(\frac{\nabla_{M}\mathscr{N}}{\mathscr{N}}\right)^{2}-\frac{\nabla^{2}\mathscr{N}}{\mathscr{N}}

(D.26)

For the metric
g_{MN}=\mathscr{N}\eta_{MN}
and
\sqrt{-g}=\mathscr{N}^{3}
the conformally invariant action becomes

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int d^{6}x\sqrt{-g}\left(g^{MN}\partial_{M}\phi\partial_{N}\phi+\frac{R}{5}\phi^{2}\right)

(D.27)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{6}x\sqrt{-g}\left(-\frac{1}{2}g^{MN}\partial_{M}\phi\partial_{N}\phi-\frac{1}{2}\left(2\left(\frac{\nabla_{M}\mathscr{N}}{\mathscr{N}}\right)^{2}-\frac{\nabla^{2}\mathscr{N}}{\mathscr{N}}\right)\phi^{2}\right)

(D.28)

On the other hand, the action that we are proposing is given by

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int d^{6}x\sqrt{-g}g^{MN}\frac{1}{\mathscr{N}^{2}}\partial_{M}\left(\mathscr{N}\phi\right)\partial_{N}\left(\mathscr{N}\phi\right)

(D.29)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{6}x\sqrt{-g}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla_{M}\phi\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\nabla^{2}\mathscr{N}}{\mathscr{N}}-2\left(\frac{\nabla_{M}\mathscr{N}}{\mathscr{N}}\right)^{2}\right)\phi^{2}\right)

(D.30)

We see that we have agreement, at least for conformally flat spacetimes.

But this agreement should be much more general. For the class of six-manifolds that we consider, we should always have

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 10\left(\frac{\nabla_{M}\mathscr{N}}{\mathscr{N}}\right)^{2}-5\frac{\nabla^{2}\mathscr{N}}{\mathscr{N}}

(D.31)

irrespectively of whether the spacetime is conformally flat or not. Instead, we are requiring the existence of two commuting conformal lightlike Killing vectors
U^{M}
and
V^{M}
where we define
\mathscr{N}=g_{MN}U^{M}V^{N}
. From this the result (
D.31
) should follow from that the conformally invariant and diffeomorphism invariant action for a free scalar field is unique. As we have presented two such actions, by requiring these two actions to be identical, the relation (
D.31
) should follow.

Appendix E

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Gamma}_{MNPQ}\Gamma_{RS}{\cal{\varepsilon}}_{I}\frac{V^{R}U^{S}}{\mathscr{N}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{\cal{\varepsilon}}_{I}{\cal{\varepsilon}}_{MNPQRS}\frac{V^{R}U^{S}}{\mathscr{N}}

E.4

V^{M}

U^{M}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Gamma}_{MNPQ}{\cal{\varepsilon}}_{I}

\displaystyle-{\cal{\varepsilon}}_{I}{\cal{E}}_{MNPQ}

\displaystyle-24

Appendix F
The fermionic selfduality equation

If we start with the bosonic selfduality equation of motion

\displaystyle 3\partial_{[M}B_{NP]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{\cal{\varepsilon}}_{MNP}{}^{RST}\partial_{R}B_{ST}

(F.1)

and make a supersymmetry variation, then we get a manifestly Lorentz invariant selfduality equation of motion for the fermionic field,

\displaystyle 3\partial_{[M}\Psi_{NP]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{\cal{\varepsilon}}_{MNP}{}^{RST}\partial_{R}\Psi_{ST}

(F.2)

We will now show that from this equation of motion we reproduce the equation of motion (
2.50
) by contracting both sides with
V_{M}U_{N}
. When we contract the left-hand side, we get

\displaystyle 3V^{M}U^{N}\partial_{[M}\Psi_{NP]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{V}\left(U^{N}\Psi_{NP}\right)-{\cal{L}}_{U}\left(V^{N}\Psi_{NP}\right)-\partial_{P}\left(V^{M}U^{N}\Psi_{MN}\right)

(F.3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{V}\psi_{N}+{\cal{L}}_{V}\left(U_{N}\psi\right)+{\cal{L}}_{U}\left(V_{P}\psi\right)-\partial_{P}\left(\mathscr{N}\psi\right)

(F.4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{V}\psi_{N}-\widetilde{\partial_{P}\left(\mathscr{N}\psi\right)}

(F.5)

Now let us turn to the right-hand side,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}V^{M}U^{N}{\cal{\varepsilon}}_{MNP}{}^{RST}\partial_{R}\Psi_{ST}

(F.6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}V^{M}U^{N}{\cal{\varepsilon}}_{MNP}{}^{RST}\left(\partial_{R}\chi_{ST}+2\frac{V_{M}}{\mathscr{N}}\psi_{N}+...\right)

(F.7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}V^{M}U^{N}{\cal{\varepsilon}}_{MNP}{}^{RST}\partial_{R}\chi_{ST}+\mathscr{N}V_{NP}\psi^{P}

(F.8)

Let us now focus on the first term

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}V^{M}U^{N}{\cal{\varepsilon}}_{MNP}{}^{RST}\partial_{R}\chi_{ST}

(F.9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathscr{N}^{2}}{2}\frac{V^{M}U^{N}}{\mathscr{N}^{2}}{\cal{\varepsilon}}_{MNP}{}^{RST}\nabla_{R}\chi_{ST}

(F.10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathscr{N}^{2}}{2}\nabla_{R}\left(\frac{V^{M}U^{N}}{\mathscr{N}^{2}}{\cal{\varepsilon}}_{MNP}{}^{RST}\chi_{ST}\right)+...

(F.11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathscr{N}^{2}}{2}\nabla^{R}\left(\frac{1}{\mathscr{N}}{\cal{E}}_{PR}{}^{ST}\chi_{ST}\right)+...

(F.12)

We now use off-shell selfduality

\displaystyle{\cal{E}}_{PR}{}^{ST}\chi_{ST}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\chi_{PR}

(F.13)

and get

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathscr{N}^{2}\nabla^{R}\left(\frac{1}{\mathscr{N}}\chi_{PR}\right)+...

(F.14)

Since the dots are terms with legs in the longitudinal directions of either
U^{M}
or
V^{M}
, it is obvious, without any need for more detailed computations, that those terms will compensate for any such longitudinal components in
\mathscr{N}^{2}\nabla^{R}\left(\frac{1}{\mathscr{N}}\chi_{PR}\right)
so that the sum will be transverse, the longitudinal components have to be canceling out since the expression that we started with was transverse with no longitudinal components. We have therefore now derived the second equation of motion (
2.50
)

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{V}\psi_{N}-\widetilde{\partial_{P}\left(\mathscr{N}\psi\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathscr{N}^{2}\nabla^{M}\left(\frac{1}{\mathscr{N}}\chi_{MN}\right)-\frac{\mathscr{N}}{2}U_{N}V^{PQ}\chi_{PQ}+\mathscr{N}V_{NP}\psi^{P}

(F.15)

from the manifestly Lorentz invariant fermionic selfduality equation of motion.

In a similar way we can derive the equation of motion

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}_{U}\chi_{MN}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{M}\psi_{N}-\partial_{N}\psi_{M}\right)^{+}

(F.16)

by contracting the selfduality equation with
U^{P}
on both sides and a subsequent projection onto the transverse space.
