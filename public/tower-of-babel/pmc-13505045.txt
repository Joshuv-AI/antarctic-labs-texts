Conscientious objection and voluntary abortion in Italy: what has changed in the last two decades?
Authors: Emanuele Koumantakis, Marco Bo, Lorena Charrier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundItalian Law 194/1978 allows voluntary termination of pregnancy within the first 90 days and guarantees healthcare professionals the right to conscientious objection (CO). However, concerns have been raised internationally regarding potential barriers to access due to the high prevalence of objecting providers. This study investigates trends in voluntary abortion in Italy over the past two decades, focusing on whether the additional workload for non-objecting gynaecologists is associated with delays in access and later gestational age at the time of abortion, and on the possible role of medication abortion in improving timeliness.MethodsAn ecological study was conducted using official data from the Italian Ministry of Health on voluntary terminations of pregnancy between 1997 and 2022. Indicators included the extra workload of non-objecting gynaecologists, the proportion of abortions performed within 14 days of request, gestational age at abortion, and use of medication abortion. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were calculated at both the national and regional levels.ResultsMarked regional variability emerged across Italy. In 13 out of 21 regions, an increased workload for non-objecting gynaecologists was associated with a lower proportion of abortions performed within 14 days and with procedures occurring at later gestational ages. Conversely, the use of medication abortion showed a strong and statistically significant positive correlation with both timely access and earlier gestational age at termination. These patterns were consistent in both regional and national analyses.ConclusionsThe findings suggest that increased workload for non-objectors may be associated with delays in accessing voluntary abortion services in Italy. Expanding the use of medication abortion may help mitigate some CO-related barriers by enabling earlier and less resource-intensive care. However, this potential benefit depends on adequate service organisation, sufficient non-objecting providers, clear referral pathways, and systematic monitoring, all of which are essential to support the equitable implementation of a legally guaranteed service.Keywords: Voluntary Abortion, Women, Rights, Conscientious Refusal to Treat, Conscientious objection, Abortion Techniques, Medication Abortion, Reproductive health

Background

In Italy, voluntary interruption of pregnancy has been legalised and is regulated by Law no. 194, enacted in 1978 after a long parliamentary debate [1, 2]. Section 9 of Law 194 recognises the right of healthcare personnel to refuse to participate in procedures specifically aimed at terminating a pregnancy [1]. To raise objections, physicians or other health professionals must submit a formal statement addressed to the hospital or healthcare centre’s director after being hired or as soon as they reach this decision. As a consequence of the declaration, objecting health professionals are excluded from performing abortion procedures [1, 2]. Importantly, however, under Article 9, conscientious objection (CO) does not apply to all activities indirectly related to abortion. The law specifies that the objection exempts healthcare and auxiliary personnel only from procedures and activities specifically and necessarily directed at causing the termination of pregnancy, and not from pre- and post-abortion care. It also states that hospitals and authorised facilities must, in any case, ensure the provision of abortion services requested according to the law, with regional authorities responsible for monitoring and guaranteeing implementation, including through staff mobility. Moreover, CO cannot be invoked when the professional’s intervention is indispensable to save the life of a woman in imminent danger.

At the national level, CO increased in the early 2000s and has remained stable at around 70% among Italian gynaecologists over the last decade [3]. The situation becomes more complex when examining the 21 regions. Using 2019 as an example, CO among gynaecologists ranged from 23% in Aosta Valley to nearly 86% in Sicily [4], while in Molise, the right to abortion has been protected for years by the presence of only one non-objecting physician, reinforcing concerns that the right to safe and timely voluntary termination of pregnancy could have been compromised [5].

The reasons for the high prevalence of CO in Italy are complex. Although religious and moral beliefs are often considered relevant in the Italian context, the sociological literature suggests that they do not fully account for the high and persistent levels of CO [6]. Other factors may include professional culture, stigma attached to abortion provision, local organisational dynamics, fear of marginalisation within the workplace, and possible career-related considerations [7]. Therefore, in this study, CO is analysed primarily as an organisational and service-delivery indicator rather than as a direct measure of individual moral or religious beliefs.

Meanwhile, in 2010, medication abortion was incorporated into the Italian abortion guidelines as a viable option up to the 49th day of pregnancy (7 weeks), requiring hospitalisation until 2020. It involves ending a pregnancy through the administration of medications (i.e., Mifepristone and Prostaglandins), without the need for the woman to undergo a surgical procedure [8].

In 2014, the International Planned Parenthood Federation European Network filed a collective complaint against the Italian state with the European Committee of Social Rights (ECSR), claiming that Sect. 9 of Law 194 fails to guarantee access to safe abortion care. In response, the ECSR stated that Italy violated the “right to health” and the “right to non-discrimination” [9]. To demonstrate that the right to CO does not hinder women’s right to abortion, the Italian government used an empirical “no correlation argument,” initially proposed by the Italian Committee of Bioethics, which contends that waiting times are simply a result of how regional healthcare systems organise abortion services [10]. However, this argument is problematic because Law 194/1978 does not merely legalise abortion; it also requires hospitals and authorised facilities to ensure the provision of abortion services requested according to the law. Therefore, the absence of a national-level correlation would not, by itself, demonstrate that access is effectively guaranteed, particularly if regional or facility-level shortages of non-objecting professionals lead to delays or unequal access. Previous studies [11, 12] have shown and discussed how the no correlation argument fails to eliminate the possibility of a relationship between the two rights—obtaining an abortion and exercising CO.

Additionally, three years later, the United Nations Human Rights Committee, in its Concluding observations on the sixth periodic report of Italy, expressed concern about the barriers to accessing legal abortion in Italy, mainly due to the high number of conscientious objectors and the resulting risk of resorting to illegal abortion [13–15].

Finally, during the COVID-19 pandemic, the number of surgical procedures seemed to decrease, while medication abortions increased. Additionally, an update to the Italian guidelines expanded access to medication abortion up to day 63 of pregnancy (9 weeks) and removed the requirement for hospitalisation [16].

This study aims to describe trends in voluntary abortion in Italy from 1997 to 2022, with particular attention to the relationship between the extra workload of non-objecting gynaecologists, waiting times from request, and gestational age at the time of abortion. We also examine whether the increasing use of medication abortion is associated with more timely access to care and earlier gestational age at termination.

Methods

This ecological study was based on population-level, aggregated annual ministerial data, with Italy and its regions over time as the units of analysis. This design was considered appropriate for describing geographical and temporal patterns in abortion service provision and for exploring associations between regional-level indicators, such as the workload of non-objecting gynaecologists, waiting times, gestational age at abortion, and use of medication abortion. However, associations observed at the aggregate level cannot be assumed to hold at the individual level, as doing so would risk ecological fallacy. The results should therefore be interpreted as population-level associations and hypothesis-generating evidence.

All annual ministerial reports on voluntary abortion from 1997 to 2022 were downloaded from the Italian Ministry of Health website [3]. These reports include legally performed and notified abortions under Law 194/1978, carried out in public facilities of the National Health Service and in private facilities authorised by the Regions. Procedures occurring outside this reporting system, including unreported abortions or abortions obtained abroad, are not captured by these data. Specifically, among the available national and regional data, we considered: (i) the number of elective abortions performed at different waiting time intervals (within 14 days and later than 21 days from request); (ii) the number of elective abortions performed within the 8th week of gestational age; (iii) the number of gynaecologists and objecting gynaecologists on duty; (iv) the number of medication abortions from 2010 onward.

The annual workload for non-objectors was calculated by dividing the total number of abortions by the number of gynaecologists who do not object. The ratio of this measure to each gynaecologist’s expected workload, regardless of objector status, shows the additional workload faced by non-objectors because of objectors. Waiting time indicators were defined following the approach used in a previous study co-authored by two of the present authors [12]. Specifically, we considered the proportion of abortions performed within 14 days of request as an indicator of timely access, and the proportion performed more than 21 days after request as an indicator of delayed access. These indicators were calculated annually at both national and regional levels using the number of abortions reported in each waiting-time category divided by the total number of abortions with available waiting-time information.

The percentage of abortions performed within the eighth week of gestation was calculated by dividing the number of abortions performed within this gestational age interval by the total number of abortions with available information on gestational age. Early gestational age at abortion was considered an indicator of timely access to care, in line with previous quality-of-care approaches [17]. The proportion of medication abortions was determined by dividing the number of medication abortion procedures by the total number of abortions performed each year, after excluding cases where the procedure was unknown.

Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were calculated to assess the strength and direction of the relationship between indicators. This non-parametric measure ranges from − 1 to + 1, with negative values indicating that one variable tends to decrease as the other increases, positive values indicating that both variables tend to increase together, and values close to zero indicating no clear association. Specifically, at both regional and national levels, correlation coefficients were calculated between (i) extra workload for non-objectors and waiting times, (ii) extra workload for non-objectors and gestational age, (iii) the proportion of medication abortions and waiting times, and (iv) the proportion of medication abortions and gestational age.

All analyses were performed with R version 4.3.0 [18], and the significance level was set at ɑ=5%.

Discussion

This study updates our previous findings from 2015 [12] with eleven additional years (2012–2022) of data on conscientious objection among Italian gynaecologists and waiting times for women seeking voluntary abortion. Using long-term national and regional data, we identified a heterogeneous picture across the country. While no clear association emerged at the national level between extra workload for non-objecting gynaecologists and waiting times, regional analyses suggest that in many contexts, increased workload is associated with reduced timeliness of care, both in terms of waiting time from request and gestational age at the time of abortion.

From both a clinical and public health perspective, these findings are important because timeliness is a crucial aspect of abortion care quality. Delays are not merely organisational problems; they can limit the range of available procedures, increase clinical risks, and impose psychological and logistical burdens on women. In this way, the observed correlation across several regions between higher workload and later access indicates that limited service capacity can create real barriers to timely care.

At the same time, the lack of a consistent pattern across regions indicates that the relationship between CO and access is mediated by regional service organisation. These factors, however, should not be understood as separate. High levels of CO may directly shape how services are organised by reducing the number and distribution of available providers, concentrating procedures in fewer facilities, weakening referral pathways, and increasing dependence on a limited number of non-objecting professionals [5]. Elements such as the geographical distribution of authorised facilities, the presence of effective referral systems, and inter-regional mobility may therefore partly reflect how CO is managed locally. However, reliance on interregional mobility may itself constitute a form of structural inequality, as access to care may depend on the ability to travel. These findings also contribute to the broader debate on the so-called “no correlation argument” [10–12], according to which conscientious objection does not affect access to abortion services. While national-level data might appear to support this view, our regional analysis suggests a more nuanced interpretation: in several regions, increased workload among non-objectors—likely reflecting higher CO levels—is associated with indicators of delayed access. This suggests that the absence of a national correlation may mask relevant local effects, which are ethically and clinically significant.

Our results also highlight a strong and consistent association between the uptake of medication abortion and more timely access to care. In most regions, higher rates of medication abortion correlated with shorter wait times and earlier gestational ages at the time of termination. This supports international evidence indicating that early access to abortion is an important quality indicator and that medication abortion can facilitate earlier care [17, 19]. From an organisational standpoint, medication abortion might decrease dependence on operating room availability and complex staffing, which is especially relevant in settings where high levels of CO limit the number of personnel willing to perform surgical procedures [20].

Taken together, these results carry significant ethical and legal implications. Italian Law 194/1978 acknowledges both women’s right to access voluntary abortion within specified time frames and healthcare professionals’ right to CO. However, international human rights organisations have stressed that exercising CO should not hinder access to legally guaranteed services. From this perspective, the ethical question is not only whether individual professionals may hold moral or religious objections, but whether health systems can accommodate such refusals while still ensuring timely, equitable, and continuous access to care.

The ethical status of CO in healthcare remains highly contested. This controversy also concerns terminology and historical origin. The expression “conscientious objection” was originally associated with refusal of military service and has been criticised when applied to healthcare, where the professional’s refusal may directly affect patients’ access to legally available care [21]. Historically, legal provisions allowing refusal to provide abortion care became incorporated into healthcare law when abortion was legalised in some countries, including the United Kingdom in 1967 [22]. Moreover, the accommodation of CO is not universal: some countries, such as Sweden, do not allow healthcare professionals to refuse reproductive healthcare services on grounds of personal belief [23]. When provided for by national abortion laws, CO is usually justified as a means of protecting healthcare professionals’ moral integrity. However, it may conflict with professional duties when it risks restricting access to time-sensitive services and imposing consequent burdens on patients [24, 25]. Indeed, giving equal weight to patients’ right to access healthcare, including self-determination, and healthcare professionals’ right to moral integrity may make it difficult to guarantee timely and safe access to abortion. Even when these rights are formally granted similar legal protection, in practice, when they must be balanced, it may be the patients’ right to self-determination that gives way. By contrast, as some authors have argued, professional obligations should take precedence when refusal compromises patients’ access to care [21, 26]. More generally, as Schuklenk and Smalling observed, healthcare professionals are monopoly providers, entitled by the State to provide legal services that require medical knowledge and expertise. It may therefore appear difficult to justify exempting them from providing a service that falls within the scope of their professional practice [25]. In this sense, CO in healthcare may also raise a democratic concern when it makes it difficult or impossible to ensure equal access to services that the law requires the health system to guarantee. Our findings do not resolve this normative debate, but they show why it is practically relevant: when CO is highly prevalent and unevenly distributed, accommodating individual conscience may have system-level consequences for equity, timeliness, and continuity of care.

The distribution of these burdens is ethically relevant. When many professionals object, the practical responsibility for providing a legally guaranteed service may fall on a small number of non-objecting clinicians, increasing their workload and potentially exposing them to professional isolation or stigma. At the same time, women may bear the consequences of institutional accommodation of CO through longer waits, travel, uncertainty, or later gestational age at termination. This raises concerns of justice and equity, especially within a universal healthcare system in which access to legally guaranteed care should not depend on place of residence, ability to travel, or the organisational capacity of individual regions.

It is also important to situate CO within the broader legal and organisational framework governing abortion care in Italy. CO is not the only potential barrier to access. Under Law 194/1978, voluntary abortion is permitted within the first 90 days, but care is delivered through authorised facilities and, in non-urgent cases, the legal pathway includes a seven-day interval after certification before the procedure can be obtained. In addition, abortion provision remains closely tied to specific categories of health professionals and to authorised public or accredited facilities. These features may interact with high levels of CO: where few non-objecting professionals are available, restrictions on who may provide care and where care may be delivered can further concentrate the workload on a limited number of providers and facilities, thereby compounding delays. This may be particularly relevant for early abortion, where even short delays can affect the range of available methods and shift care to later gestational ages.

From this perspective, medication abortion should not be interpreted as a complete solution to the barriers created by CO. Rather, it may mitigate some CO-related constraints when implemented in ways that reduce dependence on operating rooms, surgical teams, and highly specialised facility-based pathways. Its potential contribution lies in allowing earlier and less resource-intensive care, provided that trained non-objecting staff, clear referral pathways, adequate supply of medicines, and accessible authorised services are available. Therefore, the observed association between medication abortion and more timely access should be interpreted as evidence of the importance of service organisation, rather than as evidence that medication abortion alone removes the effects of CO.

This interpretation is consistent with the WHO Abortion care guideline, which frames abortion regulation as part of the conditions that enable or constrain quality care [27]. WHO recommends against mandatory waiting periods, notes that provider restrictions can create delays and increase workload burdens, and recommends that access to and continuity of abortion care be protected from barriers created by conscientious objection. The guideline further emphasises that, where conscientious objection is permitted, health systems should ensure sufficient and fairly distributed non-objecting providers, clear and enforceable regulations, monitoring, and accountability mechanisms. These recommendations support our conclusion that guaranteeing access to abortion requires not only formal legal recognition of the service, but also continuous monitoring of workload, waiting times, regional variation, and the practical implementation of CO policies. Where persistent regional inequalities remain, however, organisational measures may need to be complemented by clearer national implementation guidelines, stronger accountability mechanisms, and legal or regulatory reforms aimed at limiting the extent to which CO can disrupt the delivery of legally guaranteed care.

This study has several strengths, including a long observation period (1997–2022), national coverage combined with regional analyses, and the use of multiple indicators for timeliness and service performance. However, some limitations should be acknowledged. The ecological design does not allow for causal inferences. Potential confounding factors were not considered in the analyses, including regional differences in the number and distribution of authorised facilities, staffing levels, referral pathways, availability and local implementation of medication abortion, inter-regional mobility, demographic characteristics, and demand for abortion care. In addition, official data include legally performed and notified abortions under Law 194/1978, including those performed in private facilities authorised by the Regions, but do not capture procedures occurring outside the reporting system, abortions obtained abroad, or cases in which women were unable to obtain an abortion because of delays or service barriers. These phenomena may be particularly relevant if women resort to alternatives after encountering CO-related obstacles, and they merit further study.

Conclusions

This study provides an updated overview of voluntary abortion in Italy, emphasising that the relationship between conscientious objection and access to care varies across regions and may, in many cases, be associated with delays and later gestational ages at termination. These findings suggest that ensuring access to abortion requires not only legal protections but also effective organisation of services capable of delivering timely care. In this context, expanding medication abortion may help reduce some CO-related barriers by enabling earlier and less resource-intensive care, but only if accompanied by effective service organisation, adequate numbers of non-objecting providers, clear referral pathways, and systematic monitoring of regional waiting times and workload. These measures are essential to ensure that the legal right to conscientious objection does not undermine equitable access to a legally guaranteed health service.
