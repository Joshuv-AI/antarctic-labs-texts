IL-2 mutein-engrafted antibody MHS552 selectively expands functional regulatory T cells in nonclinical models and healthy participants

ABSTRACT
Low-dose interleukin-2 (IL-2) shows potential for treating autoimmune disorders by expanding regulatory T cells (Treg), but therapeutic utility is limited by short half-life and off-target effects. Here, we describe the molecular design, nonclinical development, and first-in-human study of MHS552, an IL-2 mutein-engrafted antibody designed to selectively expand Treg via high-affinity IL-2 receptors. In vitro, MHS552 selectively induces IL-2 signaling in Treg from healthy donors and autoimmune patients. In cynomolgus monkeys, MHS552 produces dose-dependent Treg expansion, with modest increases in conventional T cells (Tconv) at higher doses. In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, single-ascending-dose trial in 60 healthy participants (EudraCT 2018-004233-33), evaluating safety and tolerability as the primary objective and pharmacokinetics as the secondary objective, intravenous and subcutaneous administration of MHS552 is well tolerated at lower doses and shows a predictable pharmacokinetic profile. However, a serious adverse event (SAE) is reported at the highest subcutaneous dose. Both administration routes result in dose-dependent Treg expansion, with up to 6-fold increase in total Treg and 60-fold increase in CD25hi subset, without significant conventional T cell activation. Exploratory analyses confirm selective Treg activation, with expanded Treg retaining their suppressive function ex vivo. These findings support IL-2-based therapeutics for autoimmune disorders, while the SAE highlights the need for careful safety evaluation.

Introduction

Autoimmune and inflammatory disorders can result from imbalances in the immune system that cause harm to otherwise healthy cells and tissues. One potential underlying mechanism is a dysfunction or deficiency of regulatory T cells (Treg) and alterations in the interleukin-2 (IL-2) pathway, both of which are crucial in maintaining immune homeostasis1,2. As a result, ongoing research focuses on developing therapeutic modalities that modulate IL-2 receptor signaling to expand Tregs and restore their function.

IL-2 has effects on both conventional T cells (Tconv) and Treg cells, by binding to the IL-2 receptor (IL-2R), which consists of three subunits: IL-2-Rα (CD25), IL-2Rβ (CD122) and IL-2Rγ (CD132). IL-2R exists as either a trimeric, high-affinity receptor (IL-2Rαβγ) or a dimeric, intermediate-affinity receptor (IL-2Rβγ). The isolated IL-2-Rα subunit is referred to as the low-affinity IL-2 receptor that lacks intrinsic signaling capability on its own3. In humans, Tregs constitutively express high levels of CD25 and the high-affinity IL-2R. CD45RO+ effector/memory CD4+ cells express low but detectable levels of CD25, and thus low levels of the high-affinity IL-2R, while CD8 + T cells and resting natural killer (NK) cells express only the intermediate-affinity IL-2R4. Resting B cells generally show only limited IL-2Rα expression5. Due to this differential pattern of expression of IL-2R on lymphocytes, administration of IL-2 can result in opposing actions depending on the dose and exposure level6. At high IL-2 doses, binding occurs on both types of receptors, with activated intermediate-affinity IL-2R resulting in immune stimulation due to activation and proliferation of Tconv cells and cytotoxic activity of NK cells. The first clinical use of high-dose IL-2 treatment was aimed at stimulating Tconv activity against tumor cells and led to approval of high-dose recombinant human IL-2 (rhIL-2), aldesleukin (Proleukin), for the treatment of metastatic renal cell cancer and metastatic melanoma. The concomitant expansion of Treg cells can be observed as an unwanted effect of high-dose IL-2 applied in immuno-oncology therapy, as Treg cells reduce the immune response against tumor antigens7,8. Aldesleukin has not been widely adopted due to its toxicity profile, with adverse effects ranging from hypotension, renal and hepatic dysfunction, various hematological abnormalities to capillary leak syndrome9.

At low doses, IL-2 binds mainly to the high-affinity IL-2R, resulting in preferential Treg expansion, which promotes immune tolerance lending itself as an attractive approach to treat autoimmune and inflammatory diseases10. Low-dose IL-2 regimens have been tested as a treatment option in several autoimmune and inflammatory disorders, such as graft versus host disease (GvHD)11, hepatitis C vasculitis12, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)13,14, rheumatoid arthritis (RA)15, psoriasis16, ankylosing spondylitis, Crohn’s disease, ulcerative colitis, and autoimmune hepatitis17. In those clinical trials, low-dose IL-2 treatment was safe and well-tolerated, and Treg expansion was demonstrated. Notably, the two large multicenter Phase 2 randomized controlled trials in SLE to date, LUPIL-2 and a recent Phase 2b trial, demonstrated dose-dependent Treg restoration and clinical benefit with low-dose IL-218,19.

Low doses of aldesleukin have a milder toxicity profile, with adverse effects typically including injection site reactions, myalgia, arthralgia, fever and flu-like symptoms20. However, this approach was deemed suboptimal due to the rapid clearance and short half-life of aldesleukin, requiring continuous intravenous (i.v.) infusion or frequent cyclic on-off subcutaneous (s.c.) dosing to maintain therapeutic levels. Moreover, aldesleukin has a narrow therapeutic index, characterized by high peak serum concentrations that non-specifically activate Tconv and NK cells, and induce eosinophilia via IL-5 production by group 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2) and type 2 helper T (Th2) cells14,15,21,22. While this makes the clinical utility of IL-2 challenging, understanding the effect of IL-2 on Treg numbers and their suppressive function has provided the rationale and generated interest in the development and clinical testing of Treg-selective IL-2 variants (muteins) and analogs23–37.

MHS552 has been designed to both extend the half-life of IL-2 and preferentially bind to the high-affinity IL-2R (leading to Treg activation) via an antibody grafting technique38. MHS552 is a fully human monoclonal antibody (palivizumab) which has an IL-2 mutein (D20A) grafted into the heavy chain complementarity-determining region 1 (CDR1) of the antibody scaffold. Therefore, each MHS552 molecule contains two molecules of the IL-2 mutein, one in each Fab arm. The steric bulk provided by the antibody scaffold, together with the D20A mutation, is predicted to lead to preferential stimulation of the high-affinity IL-2R. The D20A mutation was chosen based on the published IL-2/IL-2R ternary structure3 and mutagenesis studies on the IL-2Rβ interface residues reported here. Thus, MHS552 has the potential to overcome the limitations of aldesleukin by providing a safer, more selective, and durable expansion of Tregs to restore immune homeostasis. Here, we report the rationale for MHS552 design, functional in vitro studies showing selective induction of IL-2 signaling in Tregs of healthy donors and patients with autoimmune disease, and nonclinical studies in cynomolgus monkeys showing dose-dependent Treg expansion with a modest increase in Tconv and eosinophil counts at higher doses. Moreover, we describe the translation of the nonclinical findings into the first-in-human (FIH) clinical study. The aim of the FIH study was to assess the safety and tolerability, pharmacokinetics (PK), pharmacodynamics (PD), and immunogenicity of single ascending doses (SAD) of MHS552 given by i.v. infusion or s.c. injection in healthy participants. Following single-dose administration in healthy participants, MHS552 induces selective expansion of Tregs, which retain their suppressive function ex vivo. MHS552 was generally well tolerated at lower doses, although we report one serious adverse event (SAE) involving acute renal insufficiency and elevated liver enzymes, suggestive of drug-induced hypersensitivity syndrome or excessive IL-2 pharmacology. While the SAE highlights the need for careful safety evaluation of IL-2 muteins, our findings support the continued clinical evaluation of MHS552 for autoimmune diseases.

Results

Design of IL-2 mutein with enhanced Treg selectivity and prolonged half-life

Building on the success of low-dose IL-2, there was an opportunity for developing an IL-2 therapeutic with selectivity for Treg via the high-affinity receptor, but reduced activity on the IL-2 intermediate-affinity receptor, and improved PK. To achieve this, an antibody grafting approach was taken, grafting full-length IL-2 harboring the D20A mutation into the CDRs of an antibody scaffold, which can influence how IL-2 interacts with the dimeric (IL-2Rβγ) or trimeric (IL-2Rαβγ) IL-2 receptor. Palivizumab (GFTX3b) was selected as the base scaffold due to its non-human target specificity (Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV), F protein), favorable clinical profile and good biophysical characteristics. The palivizumab Fc domain is derived from the IgG1 isotype and contains two-point mutations (D265A, P329A) known to render antibodies silent regarding ADCC (antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity) and CDC (complement-dependent cytotoxicity)39.

Six alternative IL-2 CDR grafts purified from transient HEK293 expression were evaluated for their biophysical and cellular signaling properties (Supplementary Table 1). Among these, the CDR-H1 IL-2 graft (GFTX3b-IL-2-H1) displayed superior biophysical properties. To enhance Treg selectivity, a D20A mutation was introduced into IL-2, reducing binding to IL-2Rβ. This mutation was informed by structural examination of the IL-2/IL-2Rαβγ trimeric receptor ternary complex (Protein Data Bank [PDB] entry 2B5I)3. MHS552 demonstrates Treg selectivity over aldesleukin in healthy and autoimmune donors

Functional in vitro studies with human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) were conducted to compare cell-type selective signaling of MHS552 versus aldesleukin. Activation of the JAK1/3 pathway in IL-2R-positive T cell subsets, NK cells, B cells and monocytes was assessed through the phosphorylation of signal transducer and activator of transcription 5 (pSTAT5). MHS552 and aldesleukin were titrated over a broad range of concentrations, with the number of IL-2 molecules being equivalent for 2 nM of aldesleukin and 1 nM of MHS5522, since each MHS552 contains two IL-2 molecules. The potency on Treg (high-affinity IL-2R) was comparable with an EC50 for pSTAT5 induction of 0.002 nM for aldesleukin, and 0.006 nM for MHS552. Aldesleukin also robustly activated CD4 conventional T cells (Tconv), CD8 T cells and NK cells (EC50 of 0.059 nM, 0.490 nM and 0.223 nM, respectively), with high efficacy (63–89% responding cells). In contrast, MHS552 selectively stimulated Treg with minimal activation of other cell subsets (4–12% response at 100 nM), but high efficacy comparable to aldesleukin stimulation (Fig. 2A). To verify that Treg selectivity is preserved in patients with conditions with an autoimmune component, 50 nM MHS552 and 100 nM aldesleukin (corresponding to 7.5 µg/mL and 1.5 µg/mL, respectively) were profiled on PBMCs from patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM), SLE, vitiligo and atopic dermatitis (AD). Similar to what was observed in PBMCs from healthy donors, both compounds induced a strong pSTAT5 response in Treg. While aldesleukin also activated CD4 Tconv, CD8 T cells and NK cells, MHS552’s activity remained largely restricted to Treg (Fig. 2B). Taken together, these results confirm the ability of MHS552 to induce IL-2R mediated signaling in Treg in a highly specific manner in PBMCs from both healthy donors and patients, informing on its suitability for use in autoimmune diseases.

MHS552 selectively and dose-dependently expands Treg in non-human primates and causes dose-limiting toxicological findings

The suitability of the cynomolgus monkey (Macaca fascicularis; Mauritian orgin) as a pharmacologically relevant species for toxicology studies was based on the known similarities of the IL-2 signaling profile in monkey and human in vitro and in vivo studies40,41 and thus likely comparable exposure-response relationship between cynomolgus monkeys and humans. The Treg-selective MHS552 activity, exposure-immunogenicity relationship and potential related toxicity in vivo were investigated via two dosing routes (s.c. and i.v.), various dose levels and dosing frequencies for up to 26 weeks based on ICH guideline (ICH S6 R1) in order to assist in FIH dose selection and to support Phase 2 clinical studies.

A summary of study designs and major findings of the three single ascending dose and three repeated dose studies in cynomolgus monkeys are provided in Table S2. Transient and reversible expansion of Treg was observed in a dose dependent manner (Fig. 3A) and correlated with exposure (measured as Cmax (ng/mL)) or area under the curve (AUC; ng*h/mL) in cynomolgus monkeys in single-dose studies (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. 1A, C). Intravenous dosing of MHS552 resulted in higher Cmax with comparable doses administered subcutaneously, but did not translate into more profound Treg expansion. At low doses of 0.125 mg/kg s.c. and 0.1 mg/kg i.v., 2.1 to 2.8-fold expansion and 2-fold expansion were observed, respectively. At higher doses of 2.4 mg/kg s.c. and 1 mg/kg i.v., maximal Treg expansions of 10.7-fold and 11.6-fold were observed, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2E, G). While there was a strong dose-dependent effect on Treg expansion, the frequency of Tconv remained largely stable. A slight increase of Tconv (Supplementary Fig. 2E, G) could be seen at doses ≥ 0.375 mg/kg with a maximum expansion of 2.7-fold at 1.2 mg/kg s.c. In addition to the expansion of Treg population, increased eosinophil counts (eosinophilia) has been observed at doses ≥ 0.3 mg/kg (Supplementary Fig. 2D, E and G). After multiple dosing, a correlation between Treg expansion and exposure (Supplementary Fig. 1B, D) was also seen in association with increased Tconv and eosinophilia (Supplementary Fig. 2F, H). Besides effects on immune cells, all cynomolgus monkeys in the 13-week Good Laboratory Practice (GLP) toxicology study developed dose- and duration-dependent skin rashes and lesions (i.e., erythema, papules, desquamation/scales, lichenification and alopecia) that correlated with microscopic findings of dermal/subepidermal infiltrate of inflammatory cells (including eosinophils) and epidermal single cell necrosis, hyperplasia, and hyperkeratosis (Supplementary Fig. 3A, B). Similar changes in the epidermis were also noted at injection sites (s.c. and i.v.) in all repeated-dose studies. In addition, mixed inflammatory cell infiltrations related to dose, dosing frequency and duration of dosing were observed in single to multiple organs (lung, kidney, liver, blood vessels, heart, gall bladder, pancreas, thyroid, adrenal, stomach and/or uterus) in all repeated dose studies with MHS552. The infiltrates were predominantly mononuclear cells comprised of Tconv, Treg, along with neutrophils and some eosinophils in variable proportions. In the liver, the infiltrates were associated with periportal hepatocyte degeneration/necrosis and pigment (hemosiderin), bile duct hypertrophy/hyperplasia and fibrosis, with no associated clinical pathology findings indicative of liver toxicity or organ dysfunction (Supplementary Fig. 3C). Due to the adversity of these findings at all doses (1 and 5 mg/kg), a no-observed-adverse-effect-level (NOAEL) was not established in the 13-week toxicology study and in the subsequent s.c. repeat dose (26 weeks) toxicology studies.

Altogether, the favorable nonclinical findings, including selective MHS552-induced Treg increase in vitro and dose-dependent Treg expansion in vivo, supported the initiation of the first-in-human study.

First-in-human study of MHS552 in healthy participants

A first-in-human, placebo-controlled, double-blind (participant- and investigator-blind; sponsor-open), randomized, single-ascending dose study was conducted between 18 October 2019 (first participant dosed) and 29 June 2021 (last participant last visit), with 60 participants randomized and completing the study (Fig. 4). A total of 30 participants received MHS552 i.v., 16 participants received MHS552 via abdominal s.c. injection, and 14 participants received placebo (n = 10 in i.v. cohorts, and n = 4 in s.c. cohorts). The highest-dose s.c. cohort (n = 4, all received MHS552) was halted after reaching the protocol-defined stopping criteria. No participants were excluded from the analysis. Dose selection was based on the anticipated pharmacological activity supported by PK/PD modeling of monkey data scaled to a human model, with predicted human maximum Treg fold-change of 1.08 for the starting dose (1 µg/kg i.v.), and 6.5 and 10 fold-change for the maximum i.v. dose (90 µg/kg) and s.c. dose (15 mg), respectively. The human equivalent doses of the select dose range were well below the exposure at which minimal-to-mild microscopic findings were observed in cynomolgus monkeys. The decision to start with i.v. dosing was made to enable a well-controlled assessment of PK and PK/PD relationship before progressing to s.c. administration, which is the preferred route for later development due to its potential for sustained IL-2 exposure and reduced dosing frequency.

Single dose of MHS552 was safe and well tolerated up to 3 mg s.c. in healthy participants

An overview of observed adverse events (AE) is provided in Supplementary Table 4. Across both i.v. and s.c. cohorts, almost all participants experienced at least one AE, the majority of which were mild. Treatment-related AEs were mainly increased eosinophil count, nasopharyngitis, dizziness and injection site reactions in the s.c. cohorts (erythema and injection site edema). Skin abnormalities and eosinophilia seemed dose-dependent, whereas other AEs showed no clear relationship to dose.

One treatment-related SAE and 3 moderate adverse events occurred in a participant in the highest s.c. dose group (15 mg), which was considered related to the treatment. The symptoms started with generalized maculopapular rash and oropharyngeal pain 2 days post-dose, followed by nausea, vomiting, myalgia (predominantly in the lower back), pyrexia and tachycardia. Five days after dosing, an acute renal insufficiency was observed, which required hospitalization, accompanied by distinct elevations in alanine aminotransferase (ALT) ( > 10x upper limit of normal [ULN]) and eosinophil increases up to 0.6 × 109/L (ULN 0.5 × 10⁹/L). The participant fully recovered with supportive treatment. Another moderate treatment-related event occurred in a participant of the 8 mg s.c. dose group (acrovesicular eczema).

Eosinophils increased dose-dependently in both the i.v. and s.c. MHS552 cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B). Mild hs-CRP, ALT, and AST increases ( < 1.5 ULN) were seen in some participants (other than the participant with the SAE), but without a clear pattern or an apparent dose dependency (Supplementary Fig. 4C–H). None of the findings were considered clinically significant. The most elevated soluble biomarkers were the Th2-type cytokines IL-5 and TARC/CCL17. IL-5 levels increased dose-dependently in three s.c. cohorts, peaking on day 4, with the highest levels observed in the 15 mg cohort (Supplementary Fig. 4I, J). TARC levels similarly increased dose-dependently, peaking around day 4 and returning to baseline by day 15 (Supplementary Fig. 4K, L). Elevation of IL-5 and TARC were not limited to participants with pronounced eosinophilia, nor did these participants exhibit the highest soluble biomarker levels. Changes observed in other soluble biomarkers measured (see Source data) were not clinically meaningful, including IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, eotaxin, MIP-1α, MIP-1β, IP-10, MDC, CCL13, and CCL2.

MHS552 showed predictable PK with low bioavailability after subcutaneous administrations

Following i.v. administration, MHS552 concentration declined in a multi-exponential manner, with a mean terminal T1/2 of MHS552 of 11-20 hours, systemic clearance (CL) of 0.13–0.19 L/hr, and volume of distribution (Vz) of 2.67-3.54 L (Fig. 5A, B and Supplementary Table 5A). After s.c. administration, Cmax was achieved within 12–24 h, the mean terminal T1/2 ranged from 18.8-27.3 h (except one outlier with T1/2 of 192 h), mean systemic clearance (CL/F) was 0.53-0.73 L/hr, and mean volume of distribution (Vz/F) was 13.9–29.3 L (Fig. 5C, D; Supplementary Table 5B). The relationship between single i.v. doses (1–90 μg/kg) and serum exposure was explored. While dose proportionality could not be statistically confirmed for the full dose range, no apparent deviations from proportionality were observed for AUCinf, AUClast, or Cmax (Supplementary Fig. 5, A to C). In total, 20 (66.7%) participants had immunogenic responses to MHS552 after i.v. administration during the study, of which 15 (50.0%) participants had treatment-induced anti-drug antibodies (ADA) and 5 (16.7%) had treatment-enhanced ADA (Supplementary Table 6A). Similarly, 13 (81.3%) participants had immunogenic responses to MHS552 after s.c. administration during the study, of which 8 (50.0%) participants had treatment-induced immunogenicity and 5 (31.3%) had treatment-enhanced ADA (Supplementary Table 6B). Only 3 of the overall 10 participants with treatment-enhanced ADA developed a treatment-boosted response with a significant ≥ 4-fold titer increase upon drug treatment, while for 7 the ADA titer was comparable pre- and post-dose, and they were qualified as treatment-unaffected. ADA target specificity was characterized in participants with treatment-induced or treatment-boosted ADA. Of these 26 participants, 9 (34.6%) had ADAs targeting palivizumab, 2 (7.7%) targeted IL-2, and 6 (23.1%) targeted both IL-2 and palivizumab. In participants with immunogenic responses, ADA had no apparent effect on Treg responses compared to ADA-negative participants. The short duration of exposure precluded evaluation of ADA impact on PK. There was no effect of ADA on the safety of MHS552.

Discussion

Here we report the molecular design, nonclinical characterization and first-in-human evaluation of MHS552, a novel IL-2 mutein-engrafted antibody graft engineered to selectively expand Treg. In healthy participants, both i.v. and s.c. single doses of MHS552 induced a selective and dose-dependent expansion of functional Treg of up to 6-fold over baseline, sustained for up to 15 days, and with minimal effects on Tconv cells. MHS552 selectively engaged the JAK/STAT pathway in vivo, as demonstrated by pSTAT5 induction in Treg and much weaker activation in Tconv and NK cells. MHS552 increased CD25 and Foxp3 expression on Treg and not on Tconv, consistent with engagement of the CD25-pSTAT5-Foxp3 pathway, and preservation of Treg suppressive function in ex vivo assays.

In cynomolgus monkeys, single and repeated doses of MHS552 produced the expected, dose-dependent expansion of Treg at lower exposures, with loss of selectivity and engagement of intermediate-affinity IL-2R at higher exposures. The associated findings, including eosinophilia, reversible skin changes and mixed leukocytic organ infiltrates, are well-described effects of IL-2 and are consistent with the known pharmacology of aldesleukin44–46, which reflects exaggerated IL-2 pharmacology. In addition, IL-2 is known to upregulate adhesion molecules on leukocytes and endothelium, promote leukocyte sequestration and migration into tissues, which results in mixed cell infiltrates observed at higher doses47. The intensity of the dose regimens applied in the nonclinical studies represented a weekly dose 3000-fold higher than the first-in-human starting dose, providing a large safety margin. In that context, the pharmacological and toxicological effects of MHS552 were considered predictable and monitorable.

In the first-in-human study, MHS552 was generally tolerated up to 90 µg/kg i.v. and 8 mg s.c. The observed adverse events, predominantly injection site reactions, skin rash and eosinophilia, were reversible and consistent with findings from other investigational IL-2 muteins27,28,30 and low-dose IL-2 regimens20. Eosinophilia was dose-dependent but not associated with any clinical symptoms. Mechanistically, IL-2-induced eosinophilia is thought to be mediated predominantly via ILC2 and Th2 cells producing IL-5, rather than by direct IL-2 signaling on eosinophils22. Although we have observed increases in IL-5 in higher dose cohorts, there was no clear correlation with eosinophilia. Importantly, the magnitude of eosinophilia observed in our study was modest compared with some other IL-2 mutein programs, where increases of more than three times the upper limit of normal were reported, also without associated clinical symptoms48. Nevertheless, the presence of eosinophilia with this and other IL-2 muteins raises questions about the suitability of IL-2 muteins for the treatment of Th2-dominant diseases with eosinophilic components, such as atopic dermatitis. Interestingly, despite the higher total exposure at 90 µg/kg i.v. (approximately 6.3 mg/70 kg), the 3 mg s.c. cohort resulted in more AEs, not limited only to local injection site reactions. This is despite the lower Tconv activation in s.c. cohorts, suggesting other mechanisms than systemic off-target Tconv effects, such as local MHS552 exposure to IL-2-responsive immune cells at the injection site.

The highest s.c. dose (15 mg) was not tolerated. One participant of this dose cohort experienced a SAE with fever, generalized maculopapular rash, renal insufficiency and marked transaminase elevations. The combination of rash, fever and multiorgan involvement points to the possibility of a drug-induced hypersensitivity syndrome (DIHS/DRESS)49, in which T-cell-mediated hypersensitivity and ILC2-driven Th2 cytokines (including IL-5 and TARC/CCL17) have been implicated50–52. Notably, this participant exhibited the highest TARC levels in the cohort, whereas IL‑5, although transiently increased, was not the highest in the cohort. While the two-week symptom duration aligns with DIHS, the onset at two days post-dose is earlier than the typical two-to-eight-week window53. Exaggerated IL-2 pharmacology, therefore, remains an alternative explanation, as IL-2 can induce Th2-skewed cytokine release and systemic inflammatory responses. This is supported by the increase in the broad soluble cytokine panel in this participant, including IL-6, IL-10, IFNγ, MIP-1β, IP-10, MDC, CCL2, and CCL26. These findings remain non-specific for either mechanism. No similar events occurred in other cohorts or in cynomolgus monkeys at exposures exceeding those in the 15 mg s.c. cohort. However, comparable events have been reported in clinical studies with other IL-2 muteins, such as a cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and flu-like symptoms with rezpegaldesleukin (NKTR-358) in healthy participants48. In addition, its recent Phase 2b study reported drug-related serious adverse events of severe drug hypersensitivity and moderate tonsillitis, as well as severe pyrexia, chest pain and injection site reactions54.

An important role of Treg in various autoimmune conditions has been well established, with deficiencies in Treg number or function described across indications1. In line with this, low-dose IL-2 has been shown to ameliorate SLE and RA disease activity, with clinical improvement correlating with increases in percentage14,55 and absolute numbers of Treg56. However, somewhat inconsistent gating strategies across studies make it challenging to define the level of Treg expansion required for clinical benefit30,33,48,55–58. Nevertheless, MHS552 produced a clear Treg response, achieving a 4- to 6-fold expansion of CD127lowCD25+ Treg cells, and a robust expansion of highly suppressive CD25hi Treg, a subset which was associated with clinical benefit in prior studies in SLE17 and T1DM59,60. Importantly, MHS552 expanded CD25hi Treg even in the highest dose i.v. cohort where total Treg counts appeared to plateau, supporting CD25hi Treg as a more sensitive PD marker of IL-2 mutein activity. Moreover, MHS552-induced Treg retained their suppressive function, a critical feature given the functional impairments characterizing various autoimmune conditions61. Intriguingly, i.v. dosing yielded superior suppressive capacity compared to s.c. dosing, despite the latter’s higher total Treg expansion. This suggests that the high initial peak exposure achieved via i.v. dosing may be necessary to trigger a more robust induction of downstream signaling events essential for Treg suppression function. The magnitude of Treg and CD25hi expansion observed with MHS552 is within the range reported for other IL-2 muteins in clinical development. The furthest advanced is rezpegaldesleukin, a PEGylated IL-2 construct, which produced mean 2.7- and 8.7-fold increases in Treg and CD25bright Treg cells, respectively, in AD patients – both below the levels reached with MHS552. Yet, this was sufficient for clinically meaningful and sustained improvement in AD clinical scores62. However, Treg expansion does not uniformly translate to clinical benefit across diseases – rezpegaldesleukin achieved greater Treg expansion in psoriasis compared to AD, despite weaker clinical responses. In SLE, rezpegaldesleukin produced 10-15-fold increase in CD25bright Treg48, but failed the primary efficacy endpoint in its Phase 2 study63. Similarly, development of another IL-2 mutein for SLE, efavaleukin alfa (AMG 592), was stopped due to futility64. Such discrepancies in clinical outcomes may arise from study design choices as well as disease-specific differences in underlying IL-2 biology, such as the presence of anti-IL-2 antibodies65, genetic polymorphisms in the IL-2R66 or FOXP367 genes and aberrant IL-2 expression68. For a Phase 2 study of MHS552, SLE is a potential indication given the established role of acquired IL-2 deficiency and Treg dysfunction in SLE pathogenesis, together with prior evidence supporting low-dose IL-2 as a Treg-directed therapeutic strategy in SLE14,55. Based on the findings of the present study, a Phase 2 dose-finding study could evaluate s.c. administration, as this route provided robust and sustained Treg expansion together with practical advantages for repeat dosing. A dose range including 1 mg and 3 mg would allow for safe characterization of the pharmacologically active range, supported by the present findings showing that 3 mg s.c. was sufficient to induce robust ( ~ 15-fold) expansion of the highly suppressive CD25hi Treg subset while maintaining a favorable safety profile.

Another important aspect for clinical translation of IL-2 muteins is immunogenicity, which can influence exposure and durability of clinical responses. Compared to other IL-2-based molecular designs, such as PEGylated IL-2, the MHS552 antibody scaffold offers distinct PK advantages, including an extended half-life, which enables less frequent dosing, and a silenced Fc domain to minimize the risk of off-target immune activation32. However, this fusion design introduced novel epitopes with immunogenic potential. While nonclinical studies in cynomolgus monkeys showed strong ADA formation, which led to a reduction or loss of exposure, these effects were not observed in the FIH single-dose study (albeit after a single dose). Crucially, Treg responses were comparable between ADA-positive and ADA-negative participants. Regarding the administration route, s.c. dosing was associated with higher immunogenicity, possibly due to immediate exposure of MHS552 to dendritic cells in skin tissue69. Furthermore, s.c. bioavailability (27%) was lower than predicted (aldesleukin: 35–46%)70, likely reflecting interspecies differences in skin structure, lymphatic uptake (primary absorption route for monoclonal antibodies), and pre-systemic catabolism71.

Our study had several limitations. A first-in-human single ascending dose study could not fully define the MHS552 dosing regimen or the long-term safety profile. Future multiple-dose trials should establish the optimal dosing interval and correlate specific Treg subsets (Foxp3 + , CD25hi) and functional activity with clinical benefit. Ultimately, defining the therapeutic window requires balancing sufficient Treg expansion against off-target Tconv or eosinophil activation. Although we assessed Foxp3, Helios, and CTLA-4 expression, additional functional and lineage markers such as CD39 and Foxp3 locus demethylation status (TSDR) were not evaluated and could provide additional information in future studies. It might also be of interest to assess Foxp3⁺CD8⁺ Treg subsets, which are emerging as a potentially important population, although their function and phenotypic characterization are not yet well-defined72.

In conclusion, the molecular design of MHS552 successfully translated into selective Treg modulation. In animal models, MHS552 demonstrated dose-dependent and selective expansion of Treg, providing a strong rationale for further testing this approach in humans. In healthy participants, the drug induced dose-dependent, functional Treg expansion with minimal activation of Tconv, CD8 and NK cells. However, the SAE observed at the highest dose indicates that clinically relevant systemic adverse effects can still occur despite the Treg selectivity, and the precise mechanism remains unknown. Whilst these safety implications raise questions on the applicability of IL-2 muteins and necessitate careful dose selection, the validated mechanism of action supports the continued investigation of MHS552 for SLE or other autoimmune diseases.

Methods

Expression and purification of IL-2 cytokine antibody grafts in transient HEK293 culture

Transient expression of IL-2 cytokine antibody grafts in HEK293 Freestyle cells was carried out using standard methods. Briefly, HEK293 cells were seeded into 1 L of Freestyle Expression Media (Life Technologies) at a density of 0.1 × 106 cells/mL and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 (130 rpm) for 4 days. After 4 days, the cell density reached 1.0 × 106 cells/m as measured by a Nexcelom cell counter. The cells were transfected using PEI (Polysciences) with a DNA:PEI ratio of 1:2. For a 1 L culture, 400 μg of DNA was used. Following transfection, the culture was incubated at 37 °C as described above. The following day, the transfected culture was fed with 50% of the culture volume of fresh media. The culture was harvested on the 6th day post-transfection. HEK expressed IL-2 cytokine antibody grafts were purified by MabSelect Protein A (Cytiva) affinity chromatography followed by preparative SEC on Superdex S200 (Cytiva). The culture was clarified by centrifugation at 1000xg for 30 min at 4 °C and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22-micron bottle top vacuum filter (Sigma). 1 M Tris pH 8.0 and 5 M NaCl were added to a final concentration of 25 mM and 75 mM, respectively.

For a 2 L expression, 2 × 2.5 ml bed volume Protein A columns were equilibrated with 1X PBS. The supernatant was applied evenly to each column and allowed to flow through by gravity. Each column was then washed with 5 CV of 1X PBS supplemented with 0.5 M NaCl. Bound protein was eluted from the Protein A resin using IgG elution buffer (Cytiva), collecting 1 CV fractions. Eluted fractions were neutralized by the addition of 100 μL of 1 M Tris pH 8.0 per 1 mL of sample. The eluted protein fractions were analyzed by NuPAGE 4–12% BIS-Tris SDS-PAGE (Thermo Fisher) and relevant fractions pooled. The pooled fraction was concentrated < 5 mL using a spin filter and then loaded on a HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 200 FPLC column previously equilibrated with 1X PBS. Peak fractions were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and the relevant fractions pooled. Pooled fractions were concentrated to about 1 mg/ml using a spin filter and frozen in small fractions in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C. Purified proteins were analyzed by SEC-MALS, DSF and LCMS to assess protein quality. Size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) separation was performed on an Agilent 1260 equipped with degasser, quaternary pump, column compartment, autosampler and a UV−Vis detector. The system was equipped with a Zenix-C SEC-300 column (7.8 mm × 300 mm, 3 μm particle size, Sepax) equilibrated with 0.1 μM filtered PBS (Cytiva). A flow rate of 0.8 mL/min was employed, and the column temperature set at 30 °C. Total run time was 25 min for each injection. A miniDAWN multi-angle light scattering (MALS) detector and an Optilab refractive index detector (Wyatt) were connected in series to the Agilent UV detector.

The MALS data was collected and analyzed using Astra 6.1.1.17 software (Wyatt).

Differential scanning fluorimetry (Tm Analysis)

Samples were prepared at 0.2 mg/ml in PBS (Cytiva) with 8X Sypro Orange (diluted from a 5000X stock Invitrogen) and 10 μL dispensed into a 384-well plate (Roche). The fluorescence data as a function of temperature was acquired on a Roche 480 II LightCycler where the samples were pre-equilibrated for 1 min at 30 °C before running a ramp up to 90 °C with 100 acquisitions/degree ( ~ 1.5 h total ramp time). The melting temperature was determined using the first derivative and represents an average of three samples.

Cellular signaling analysis

The M-07e cell line was obtained from Creative Bioarray and genetically modified to express either the complete IL-2 receptor (IL-2Rαβγ) or a version lacking the alpha subunit (IL-2Rβγ). Cells were grown in their respective media (RPMI 1640, 20% FBS, 10 ng/ml GM-CSF + PS/L-glutamine βγ and RPMI 1640, 20% FBS, 40 ng/ml h-IL-2, 10 ng/ml GM-CFS + PS/L-glutamine for the αβγ), optimized for each receptor type, until they reached the appropriate density. The day before the assay, cells were washed in RPMI 1640 and twice in RPMI + (1x) CTL-wash (Wash Supplement 10X- Cellular Technology Ltd) to remove growth factors and then starved overnight in a specialized medium (CTL-medium Cellular Technology Ltd) to synchronize their responses.

On the day of the assay, cells were resuspended at 4 × 106/mL and plated at 50 μL/well. Cells were stimulated with serial dilutions of the test articles for 20 minutes at 37 °C to activate the IL-2 signaling pathway and immediately fixed and permeabilized (BD Fix Buffer I and Phosflow Perm Buffer III). Changes in the levels of phosphorylated STAT5, a key signaling molecule downstream of the IL-2 receptor, were measured by flow cytometry. EC50 values, representing the concentration of the test article needed to achieve half-maximal activation of STAT5, were calculated using the median fluorescence intensities (MFI) of the antibody detecting phosphorylated STAT5 (BD biosciences, PE mouse anti-pSTAT5). All cell lines used are listed in Supplementary Table 7.

In vitro PBMC pSTAT5 signaling assay from healthy donors and autoimmune donors

PBMC from healthy donors or autoimmune donors (SLE, T1DM, AD, vitiligo) were tested in vitro for signal potency with either native IL-2 (aldesleukin) or IL-2 mutein engrafted MHS552 to assess selective activity on the IL-2R-bearing cells. Patient PBMCs were rested at 37 °C for 2 h and then treated with different concentrations of either MHS552 or aldesleukin. At the end of the stimulation period, all reactions were stopped and fixed for 10 min at room temperature by the addition of PFA (1.6% final concentration). Following fixation, cell surfaces were labeled using an antibody cocktail targeting, CD45 (clone -141pr) (leukocytes), CD3 (clone UCHT1 - 154Sm) and CD4 (clone RPA-T4 – 145Nd) and CD8 (clone RPA-T8 – 146Nd) (T cells), CD25 (clone 2A3 – 149Sm), CD127 (clone A019D5 – 143Nd) (Tregs), CD45RO (clone UCHL1 – 165Ho) and CD45RA (clone HI100 – 169Tm) (memory and naïve), CCR7 and CD62L (clones G043H7-159Tb and DREG-56- 153 Eu) (memory subsets) CD19 (clone HIB19 -142Nd) (B cells), HLA-DR (clone L234 – 174Yb) (monocytes), CD56 (clone N901 – 176Yb) (NK cells) and incubated for 30 min at room temp. Cells were kept in methanol at 80 °C overnight. The next day, samples were washed and stained with Foxp3 (clone PCH101 -162Dy) and pSTAT5 (clone 47 – 150Nd) antibodies. Following intracellular staining, cell samples were acquired by CyTOF.

For the healthy participant PBMC, an adapted protocol was used that was optimized for the BD LSR Fortessa instrument. PBMC were rested overnight hours and then treated with the different concentrations from serial dilutions of either MHS552 or native human IL 2. Cells were incubated with a cocktail of antibodies to label surface markers, fixed and permeabilized using PerFix buffers (Beckman Colter) and then stained intracellularly for pSTAT5 PE (clone 47) and Foxp3 Alexa Fluor 647 (clone 259D). Finally, cells were acquired using the BD LSR Fortessa instrument. Following acquisition by either CyTOF or BD LSR Fortessa, samples were analyzed in FlowJo™ software (BD Life Sciences). Representative gating strategy is provided in Supplementary Fig. 9A. All antibodies and reagents are listed in Supplementary Table 7.

The nonclinical studies were approved by local Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees (IACUC) and conducted according to an approved study protocol and local standard operating procedures in strict compliance with national legal regulations on animal welfare law and accepted animal welfare standards. Studies conducted in the USA were in compliance with the US Department of Agriculture Animal Welfare Act, the guide for the Care and use of Laboratory Animals and the Office of Laboratory Animal Welfare. Studies conducted in France were in compliance with EU Council Directive No. 2010/63/EU and French Decret No. 20133-118. The study was authorized by the Central Committee on Research Involving Human Subjects (Centrale Commissie Mensgebonden Onderzoek, CCMO), the Netherlands.

All participants provided written informed consent prior to any study activity. The study was a single-center, randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind (sponsor-open), single-ascending dose study conducted in healthy participants in the Netherlands. This study evaluated five single ascending doses of MHS552 (cohorts A1–A5) administered as an i.v. infusion to healthy participants. Infusion was administered over a period of 120 min. In addition, three single s.c. doses of MHS552 (cohorts B1 to B3) in healthy participants were evaluated after the i.v. doses had been tested and established as safe. Participants were randomized in a ratio of 6:2 to receive either a single i.v. (or s.c.) dose of MHS552 or placebo. Figure 4 shows the CONSORT study flowchart.

Participants were randomly assigned to a treatment or placebo group using a computer-generated randomization list produced by the sponsor. Treatment blinding was maintained through the use of study drugs identical in packaging, labeling, appearance and odor. An unblinded pharmacist, independent of the study team, prepared and dispensed study medication. The study was conducted between 18 October 2019 (first participant dosed) and 29 June 2021 (last participant last visit).

Dose justification and dose selection for first-in-human study

The rationale for the starting dose was in accordance with the EMA “Guideline on strategies to identify and mitigate risks for first-in-human and early clinical trials with investigational medicinal products”. The starting dose and highest dose administered were based on the anticipated pharmacological activity supported by PK/PD modeling of monkey data scaled to a human model. The starting dose was defined as the minimum anticipated biological effect level (MABEL) equivalent to a 10% increase from the pre-treatment baseline of Treg cells, as this level of Treg increase has been found to be safe, tolerable and quantifiable in low-dose aldesleukin clinical trials. The highest dose of 15 mg s.c. in this FIH study was 1.3 times lower than the human equivalent dose at which minimal-to-mild microscopic findings were observed in cynomolgus monkeys.

Prior to dose escalations, safety data up to 14 days post-dose (AEs, vital signs, safety laboratory, ECG) and PD data (Treg and Tconv counts) up to 7 days post-dose were reviewed. In addition, prior to the start of the first s.c. cohort PK and immunogenicity data of i.v. cohorts were reviewed. A target range of approximately 4- to 6-fold Treg expansion was selected based on prior studies in NOD mice showing that modest increases in pancreatic Treg cells after low-dose IL-2 were associated with reversal of autoimmune diabetes73. More recent studies in NOD mice have further supported that sustained Treg increases in this range are sufficient for disease control74. However, to further characterize the dose-effect relationship, up to 10-fold range of Treg expansion was targeted, with 15-fold considered acceptable, which was justified by a nonclinical dose range finding study. The most important safety stopping criteria were: two or more grade 2 hypersensitivity reactions or generalized infusion reactions, two or more grade 3 local injection site reactions, and at least three participants with grade 3 infection complications. Except for the safety stopping criteria, no further dose escalation was to be initiated if four or more participants in a cohort had Treg increases of 15-fold or more, or a Tconv increase of 5-fold or more.

Clinical study population

The sample size of 8 participants per cohort, with a randomization ratio of 6:2 was chosen to balance the need to maximize information on the new compound MHS552 and the need to have a placebo participant to blind the study. Healthy female and male participants aged of between 18 and 45 years (inclusive) were included. Only women of non-childbearing potential were included. Participants were in good health as determined by past medical history, physical examination, vital signs, electrocardiogram and laboratory tests at screening, with a BMI of 18–30 kg/m2 (inclusive) and a minimum weight of 50 kg. Key exclusion criteria were: recent and/or recurrent history of acute or chronic bronchospastic disease, liver disease or liver injury as indicated by abnormal liver function tests, history of hypersensitivity to any of the study treatments or to drugs of similar chemical classes, receipt of live or attenuated vaccine within a three-month period before initial dosing, use of any other investigational drug withing five half-lives of screening, family history or known presence of long QT syndrome or history or current clinically significant arrhythmias, and history of malignancy.

Safety and tolerability assessments

Safety assessments included occurrence of AEs, SAEs, laboratory evaluations (hematology, clinical chemistry, coagulation tests, quantiferon test, urinalysis), electrocardiogram evaluations (PR interval, QRS duration, heart rate, RR interval, QT interval, QTcF), use of concomitant medications, physical examination, vital signs, height and weight. Pregnancy testing was required for any female participant. Adverse events were reported based on the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE).

Pharmacokinetic assessments

Serum MHS552 concentrations were determined using an immunoassay with a lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) of 1 ng/mL. The following pharmacokinetic parameters were determined using the actual recorded sampling times and noncompartmental method(s) with Phoenix WinNonlin (version 8.0) from the serum concentration-time data: Cmax, Tmax, AUClast, AUCinf, T1/2, Vz/F and CL/F as appropriate. The linear trapezoidal rule was used for AUC calculation. Regression analysis of the terminal serum elimination phase for the determination of T1/2 included at least three data points after Cmax and excluded Cmax. If the adjusted R2 value of the regression analysis of the terminal phase was less than 0.75, no values were reported for T1/2, AUCinf and CL/F.

The dose proportionality of single i.v. doses of MHS552 for PK parameters (Cmax, AUClast and AUCinf) was evaluated using a log-log regression model: ln(PK parameter) = η + β⋅ln(dose) + ϵ, where η is the intercept, β is the slope, ϵ is the residual error. Point estimates and 90% confidence intervals (CI) for the slope were calculated using log-transformed data, then back-transformed to the original scale for interpretability.

Absolute bioavailability after s.c. injection was estimated by comparing dose-normalized AUCinf after s.c. injection with dose-normalized AUCinf after i.v. infusion. To evaluate absolute bioavailability between the i.v. and s.c. formulations, log-transformed AUClast, AUCinf and Cmax were analyzed separately using a linear model with fixed effect for treatment. Point estimates and 90% CI were estimated and back-transformed to give the ratio of the parameter for s.c. to i.v.

Measurement of Treg expansion in whole blood from participants treated with MHS552 by flow cytometry

To assess Treg expansion in the FIH clinical study, whole blood was collected in Na Heparin monovette tubes at the following time points: baseline, and day 1 at pre-dose and 2 h, 6 h, 24 h post-dose and day 3, day 4, day 8, day 11, day 15, day 29 and day 57.

Afterwards, 150 µl blood was transferred in each of 2 Nunc cryo tubes. 50 µl of antibody mix containing mouse anti-human CD3 V500 (clone UCHT1), mouse anti-human CD4 PerCpCy5.5 (clone RPA-T4), mouse anti-human CD16 PE (clone 3G8), mouse anti-human CD56 PE (clone NCAM-1), mouse anti-human CD45RA FITC (clone HI100), mouse anti-human CD45 APC Fire 750 (clone 2D1), mouse anti-human CD25 Alexa fluor 647 (clone M-A251), mouse anti-human CD127 Brilliant Violet 421 (clone A019D5), and mouse anti-human CD62L Pacific Blue (clone DREG-56) was added to each tube. All antibodies are listed in Supplementary Table 7.

After 30 min incubation at room temperature, red blood cells were lysed, and samples were fixed and frozen at -80 °C and shipped to the analysis lab. After thawing, cells were washed and acquired on BD FACS CantoII, data were analyzed in FACS Diva software 8.0. In addition to absolute cell counts (assessed using the hematology data from samples collected at the same time in the dual platform approach), expression levels of CD25 were analyzed using Alexa647-MESF beads to control for laser intensity changes throughout the study. Furthermore, relative frequencies of cell subsets were analyzed and reported.

Treg cells were determined as CD45 + CD3 + CD4+CD127lowCD25+ cells, CD4 Tconv were determined as CD45 + CD3 + CD4 + CD127+CD25low, NK cells were determined as CD3CD56 + CD16+ cells and monocytes were determined using SSC and CD45. A subset of Treg cells, CD25hi Treg, have been previously identified as a Treg subpopulation with high activity and suppressive capacity and were identified as the Treg subpopulation with the highest CD25 expression75. Gating strategy for the Treg expansion flow cytometry assay in the first-in-human study is provided in Supplementary Fig. 9C. Intracellular Foxp3 staining was not included in order to avoid fixation and permeabilization-related cell loss that would compromise absolute cell counting, and since peripheral CD4 + CD25 + CD127low cells have been shown to correlate strongly with Foxp3 suppressive Tregs76. Foxp3 was instead assessed in a separate PBMC panel together with Helios and CTLA-4 to characterize Treg functionality.

Measurement of pSTAT5 in whole blood from participants treated with MHS552 by flow cytometry

Selective signaling of MHS552 was assessed by measuring pSTAT5 and IL-2R on Treg, CD4 T cells, CD8 T cells and NK cells as proximal PD markers by flow cytometry in whole blood. To assess pSTAT5 in the FIH clinical study, whole blood was collected in Na Heparin monovette tubes at the following time points: baseline, and day 1 at pre-dose, and 2 h, 6 h, 24 h post-dose and day 4.

Afterwards, 400 µl blood was transferred in Nunc cryo tubes, containing Lyse/Fix buffer (BD #558049). After incubation at 37 °C for 25 min samples were frozen immediately at − 70 °C at the clinical site. Frozen samples were shipped to the analysis lab and were further processed and measured within 4–7 days. Staining of thawed whole blood samples was performed using the antibodies, mouse anti-human CD3 PE-Cy7 (clone UCHT1), mouse anti-human CD4 PerCpCy5.5 (clone RPA-T4), mouse anti-human CD56 FITC (clone NCAM16.2), mouse anti-human CD16 FITC (clone 3G8), mouse anti-human CD8a APCFire750 (clone RPA-T8), mouse anti-human CD127 APC (clone eBIORDR5), mouse anti-human CD45 V500 (clone HI30), mouse anti-human CD25 BV421(clone M-A251), mouse anti-STAT5 (pY694) PE (clone 47). Samples were acquired on BD FACS CantoII, data were analyzed in FACS Diva software 8.0. All antibodies are listed in Supplementary Table 7.

Expression of pSTAT5 was analyzed using PE-MESF beads to control for laser intensity changes throughout the study, in Treg (CD45 + /CD3 + /CD4 + /CD127low/CD25 + ), Tconv “non-Treg” (CD45 + /CD3 + /CD4 + /CD127 + /CD25low), CD8 T cells (CD45 + /CD3 + /CD8 + ), NK cells (CD45 + /CD3-/CD16/CD56 + ). In addition, percentage of cells (Treg, Tconv, CD8 and NK cells) positive for pSTAT5 was analyzed. Gating strategy for pSTAT5 expression is available in Supplementary Fig. 9D.

Measurement of Helios, Foxp3, and CTLA-4 in Treg subsets in PBMCs from participants treated with MHS552 by flow cytometry

To assess Treg-associated functional markers, Helios, Foxp3, and CTLA-4 were measured intracellularly by flow cytometry in PBMCs from participants treated with MHS552. Whole blood was collected on day 1 pre-dose and on days 4 and 29 post-dose. PBMCs were prepared according to the Cellular Technology Limited (CTL ImmunoSpot) procedure, cryopreserved, and thawed before staining.

PBMC samples were stained with the following antibodies: anti-human CD4-APC-Fire750 (clone SK3), anti-human CD127 (IL-7Rα)-BV421 (clone A019D5), anti-human CD25-PerCP-Cy5.5 (clone M-A251), anti-human CD62L (clone DREG-56), anti-human CTLA-4-APC (clone BNI3), anti-human FOXP3-PE (clone 259D/C7), and anti-mouse/human Helios-Alexa Fluor 488 (clone 22F6). All antibodies are listed in Supplementary Table 7.

Briefly, surface staining was performed using antibodies against CD4, CD45RA, CD127, CD25, and CD62L. After permeabilization, intracellular staining was performed using antibodies against Foxp3, CTLA-4, and Helios. Samples were acquired on a BD FACSCanto II, and data were analyzed using FACSDiva software version 8.0. Gating strategy for Helios, Foxp3, and CTLA-4 expression is available in Supplementary Fig. 9E.

Treg suppression assay in PBMCs from participants treated with MHS552

Treg suppressive function was measured by using a modified in vitro co-culture assay. Whole blood was collected at day 1 pre-dose, day 4 and day 29 and PBMC were prepared according to the Cellular Technology Limited (CTL Immunospot) procedure and frozen before staining. Briefly, following thawing, PBMCs were stained with fluorescently conjugated antibodies specific for anti-human CD4 APC-Cy7 (clone RPA-T4), anti-human CD25 PE (clone 2A3 and M-A251), anti-human CD14 PerpCy5.5 (clone HCD14), anti-human CD19 Pacific Blue (clone HIB19) and anti-human CD127 Alexa Fluor 647 (clone HIL-7R-M21). Cells were then analyzed on a BD FACSAriaIII and co-cultures established by sorting Tconv cells (CD4 + CD25-/loCD127 + ), B cells (CD19 + ) and varying numbers of Treg cells (CD4+CD25hiCD127-/lo) directly into the wells of a 96-well plate containing tissue culture media in the presence of phytohemagglutinin (PHA, 4 µg/ml) as a polyclonal stimulant. All antibodies are listed in Supplementary Table 7.

Measurement of circulating biomarkers in serum from participants treated with MHS552

Soluble serum biomarkers were assessed to detect distal mechanistic effects of MHS552 and to monitor signs of systemic inflammatory and anti-inflammatory effects. Analyzed soluble serum biomarkers included IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, TNF, CXCL10, IL-18, IL-1Rα (Ella platform, Ref. SPCKC-PS-003107), IL-5 (K151J3S, SPlex, MSD), eotaxin, macrophage inflammatory protein-1 alpha (MIP-1α) and beta (MIP-1β), thymus and activation-regulated cytokine (TARC; CCL17), macrophage-derived chemokine (MDC), and chemokines CCL13, CCL26 and CCL2 (Chemokine Panel 1, C0047-2, MSD). Soluble CD25 (s-IL-2R alpha) concentrations in serum were determined by ECLIA.

Immunogenicity assessments

ADA for MHS552 were detected via a homogeneous bridging electro-chemiluminescence (ECL) assay. Each study sample was first pre-incubated with biotinylated MHS552 for capturing and ruthenium-labeled MHS552 for detecting anti-MHS552 antibodies. Formed complexes between labeled MHS552 and ADA were then bound to a streptavidin-coated microtiter plate from MSD (Meso Scale Discovery) and read-out on an ECL imager. A three-tiered approach consisting of a screening, a confirmatory, and a titration step was pursued. Specimens were first screened for a positive or negative ADA response by reference to the screen assay cut point as outlined above.

Screening ADA-positive samples went in a confirmatory assay to test the specificity of an immune response in the presence of MHS552 in excess. Only samples which were positive in this confirmatory assay were classified as ADA positive, and existing ADA were then quasi-quantified in a titration assay, and the titer was reported. For ADA-positive samples, the specificity of the assay signal was further characterized in two additional confirmatory assays by pre-incubating the study sample with an excess of either Palivizumab or recombinant IL-2 (rhIL-2) prior to analysis.

Statistical analysis of clinical data

Graphing of the nonclinical PBMCs assays confirming Treg specificity of MHS552 over aldesleukin and non-human primate MHS552 induced Treg expansion were performed in GraphPad Prism version 10.4.1 (532). Flow cytometry data were analyzed using Diva version 8, (Becton Dickinson). Statistical analyses and graphing of the clinical data were performed in R version 4.3.1. For the clinical study, no formal power calculation was performed, as is standard for first-in-human single ascending dose studies where the primary objective is safety and tolerability characterization. For the biomarker data, normality of the residuals was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data was analyzed using Tukey’s honest significance test with Bonferroni’s post-hoc correction. Data not following a normal distribution was analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test and Dunn’s post-hoc test. Data were expressed as means and SEs where appropriate. Statistical significance was set at p < .05. In the figures, statistical significance between each treatment group and the placebo group for each time point are denoted as follows: *p < .05, **p < .01, ***p < .001, and ****p < .0001 (color-coded by treatment group). Results of the statistical tests are available in the Source Data. The number of evaluable participants varied across assays, cohorts, and time points. Therefore, the number of participants included in a given analysis may be lower than the number of randomized participants. Exact n values for each plotted series are detailed in the figure legends and available in the Source Data.
