Abstract
This paper analyzes the structure of the set of positive solutions of ( 1.1 ), where a ≡ a h a\equiv a_{h} is the piece-wise constant function defined in ( 1.3 ) for some h ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) h\in(0,1) . In our analysis, λ \lambda is regarded as a bifurcation parameter, whereas h h is viewed as a deformation parameter between the autonomous case when a = 1 a=1 and the linear case when a = 0 a=0 . In this paper, besides establishing some of the multiplicity results suggested by the numerical experiments of [ 2 ] , we have analyzed the asymptotic behavior of the positive solutions of ( 1.1 ) as h ↑ 1 h\uparrow 1 , when the shadow system of ( 1.1 ) is the linear equation − u ′′ = π 2 ​ u -u^{\prime\prime}=\pi^{2}u . This is the first paper where such a problem has been addressed. Numerics is of no help in analyzing this singular perturbation problem because the positive solutions blow-up point-wise in ( 0 , 1 ) (0,1) as h ↑ 1 h\uparrow 1 if λ < π 2 \lambda<\pi^{2} . Keywords : Moore–Nehari equation. Multiplicity of positive solutions. Point-wise blow-up to a metasolution. Spectral parameter. MSC 2020 : 34B08, 34B16, 34B18.

Introduction

This paper studies the one-dimensional second-order superlinear problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-u^{\prime\prime}=\lambda u+a(x)u^{p},\quad x\in(0,1),\\[1.0pt]
u(0)=u(1)=0,\end{array}\right.

(1.1)

where
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is regarded as a bifurcation parameter,
p>1
is fixed, and
a:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)
is a non-zero piece-wise constant function. Thus,
a\gneq 0
in the sense that
a\geq 0
, but
a\neq 0
. The main goal of this paper is to study the global structure of the set of positive solutions of (
1.1
)
in terms of the parameter
\lambda
and of the nature of the weight function
a(x)
. As
p>1
, by the uniqueness of solution for the Cauchy problem associated to the differential equation of (
1.1
), any positive solution,
u
, of (
1.1
) must be strongly positive, in the sense that

u(x)>0\;\;\hbox{for all}\;x\in(0,1),\;\;u^{\prime}(0)>0\;\;\hbox{and}\;\;u^{\prime}(1)<0.

(1.2)

In the special case when
\lambda=0
and
p
is an odd integer, the problem (
1.1
) has been already analyzed by Moore and Nehari
[
16
]
for a symmetric piecewise weight function
a(x)
defined, after a spatial scaling, as

a(x)=\;\begin{cases}1\qquad\hbox{if}\;x\in[0,\tfrac{1-h}{2}]\cup[\tfrac{1+h}{2},1],\\[4.30554pt]
0\qquad\hbox{if}\;x\in(\tfrac{1-h}{2},\tfrac{1+h}{2}),\end{cases}

(1.3)

where
h\in(0,1)
is fixed. The main result of
[
16
]
shows that, for some values of
h\in(0,1)
, the problem (
1.1
) possesses, at least, three positive solutions: a first solution which is symmetric (even) with respect to
x=0.5
, and two additional asymmetric solutions, reflected from each other
about
x=0.5
. In recent years, also in the special case when
\lambda=0
, some additional work has been done by
Kajikiya et al.
[
9
]
by considering
h
as a bifurcation parameter and establishing the existence of a symmetry-breaking from the symmetric solution to a continuum of asymmetric solutions at some critical value
h_{*}\in(0,1)
(see also
[
7
]
for nodal solutions if
p\in(0,1)
, and
[
8
]
for nodal solutions if
p>1
). Some related problems where a
symmetry-breaking occurs leading to multiplicity of positive solutions can be found in
[
5
,
6
,
17
,
18
]
.

The main goal of this paper is to ascertain the global topological structure of the set of positive solutions of (
1.1
),
(\lambda,u)
, regarding
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
as the primary bifurcation parameter. Then, we will ascertain how
changes the underlying global bifurcation diagram in
\lambda
as
h
varies in the interval
(0,1)
. Thus,
h
is regarded as the secondary parameter of (
1.1
) for the special choice (
1.3
). Throughout this paper, to emphasize the dependence of
a(x)
on
h\in(0,1)
, we will eventually set

a\equiv a_{h}.

According to, e.g., Amann and López-Gómez
[
1
]
, under these assumptions, for every
h\in(0,1)
, the set of non-trivial non-negative solutions,
(\lambda,u)
, of problem (
1.1
) has a component,
\mathscr{C}_{h}^{+}
, such that
(\pi^{2},0)\in\mathscr{C}_{h}^{+}
and

\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\mathscr{C}_{h}^{+})=(-\infty,\pi^{2}],

where
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}
stands for the
\lambda
-projection operator,

\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\lambda,u)=\lambda\;\;\hbox{for all}\;\;(\lambda,u)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{C}([0,1];\mathbb{R}).

In particular, (
1.1
) admits a positive solution if, and only if,

\lambda<\pi^{2}
. Therefore, the condition
\lambda<\pi^{2}
will be imposed throughout this paper.
Moreover, according to the numerical experiments of Cubillos et al.
[
2
]
, for every
h\in(0,1)
, there is a value of the parameter
\lambda
, denoted by
\lambda_{h,b}
, which is a subcritical pitchfork bifurcation value for
\mathscr{C}_{h}^{+}
. These numerical experiments also suggest that (
1.1
) has three positive solutions for every
\lambda<\lambda_{h,b}
, one symmetric and two asymmetric, and that

\lim_{h\downarrow 0}\lambda_{h,b}=-\infty\quad\hbox{and}\quad\lim_{h\uparrow 1}\lambda_{h,b}=\pi^{2}.

(1.4)

Although the first limit of (
1.4
) is rather natural, because the limiting shadow
problem as
h\downarrow 0
,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-u^{\prime\prime}=\lambda u+u^{p}\quad\hbox{in}\;\;(0,1),\\[1.0pt]
u(0)=u(1)=0,\end{array}\right.

(1.5)

has a unique positive solution for each
\lambda\in(-\infty,\pi^{2})
, which follows by a direct analysis of the phase portrait of the autonomous differential equation

-u^{\prime\prime}=\lambda u+u^{p},

the second limit of (
1.4
) is far from expected, because it entails that, for every
\varepsilon>0
and sufficiently small
1-h
, (
1.1
) has three positive solutions for every
\lambda\in(-\infty,\pi^{2}-\varepsilon]
, while the limiting shadow system of (
1.1
) as
h\uparrow 1
is the linear problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-u^{\prime\prime}=\lambda u\quad\hbox{in}\;\;(0,1),\\[1.0pt]
u(0)=u(1)=0,\end{array}\right.

(1.6)

which has a positive solution if, and only if,
\lambda=\pi^{2}
. Thus, the global multiplicity result as
h\uparrow 1
, for every
\lambda\in(-\infty,\pi^{2})
, is attributable to the nonlinear character of
a_{h}u^{p}=u^{p}
in

[0,\frac{1-h}{2})\cup(\frac{1+h}{2},1]
, which is a very tiny set as
h\uparrow 1
. Another multiplicity result in a similar vein, caused by spatial heterogeneities, has been documented by López-Gómez and Rabinowitz in
[
15
]
.

The main goal of this paper is to deliver some analytical proofs of some of these
features and analyze the point-wise behavior of the three positive solutions of (
1.1
)
as
h\uparrow 1
. Precisely, our main findings are the following ones:

(F1)

For every
\lambda<\pi^{2}
, (
1.1
) has a unique positive symmetric solution,
u_{\lambda}
, and

\mathscr{C}_{h,s}^{+}=\{(\lambda,u_{\lambda})\;:\;\lambda\in(-\infty,\pi^{2}]\}

is a continuous curve such that
\mathscr{C}_{h,s}^{+}\subset\mathscr{C}_{h}^{+}
.

(F2)

For every
h\in(0,1)
, there exists
\tilde{\lambda}=\tilde{\lambda}(h)<\pi^{2}
such that, for every
\lambda\leq\tilde{\lambda}(h)
, (
1.1
) has, at least, three positive solutions.

(F3)

For any given
\lambda\in\left(\pi^{2}/4,\pi^{2}\right)
, there exists
R_{\lambda}>0
such that, for every
R>R_{\lambda}
, the problem (
1.1
) has, at least, two asymmetric positive solutions for a suitable
h=h(R)\in(0,1)
. Moreover,

\lim_{R\uparrow+\infty}h(R)=1.

(F4)

For every
\lambda<\pi^{2}
and
h\in(0,1)
, let
u_{h}
be any positive solution of
(
1.1
). Then,

\lim_{h\uparrow 1}u_{h}(x)=+\infty\;\;\hbox{for all}\;\;x\in(0,1).

(1.7)

In particular, the components
\mathscr{C}_{h,s}^{+}
and
\mathscr{C}_{h}^{+}
blow-up, point-wise in
(0,1)
, as
h\uparrow 1
.

(F5)

For every
\alpha>0
, there are two increasing sequences,
\{h_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
in
(0,1)
and
\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
in
(0,\pi^{2})
, such that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}h_{n}=1,\quad\lim_{n\to+\infty}\lambda_{n}=\pi^{2},\quad\hbox{and}\;\;\|u_{\lambda_{n}}\|_{\infty}=\alpha\;\;\hbox{for all}\;\;n\geq 1,

where
u_{\lambda_{n}}
is the unique symmetric positive solution of (
1.1
) for

(\lambda,h)=(\lambda_{n},h_{n})
,
n\geq 1
. Moreover,

\lim_{n\to+\infty}u_{\lambda_{n}}=\alpha\sin(\pi\cdot)\quad\hbox{in}\;\;\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,1];\mathbb{R}).

In other words,
(\pi^{2},\alpha\sin(\pi\cdot))\in\mathscr{C}_{1}^{+}
perturbs into the sequence

(\lambda_{n},u_{\lambda_{n}})\in\mathscr{C}_{h_{n},s}^{+},\quad n\geq 1.

The existence and the uniqueness of the symmetric solution established in (F1) will be proven in Section 2. It combines a phase plane analysis with some sharp comparison results. The multiplicity result
established in (F2) will be proven in Section 4 through some phase plane techniques based on a technical device of
[
14
]
. The multiplicity result established in (F3) will be proven in Section 5 combining some sophisticated phase plane techniques with the Mountain Climbing Theorem of
[
4
,
19
,
10
]
. Finally, the results stated in (F4) and (F5) will be proven in Section 3.

Although, according to
[
9
]
, it is already known that

\lim_{h\uparrow 1}\|u_{h}\|_{\infty}=+\infty\quad\hbox{if}\;\;\lambda=0,

the main novelty of our Theorem (
3.1
) establishes that

\lim_{h\uparrow 1}u_{h}(x)=+\infty\quad\hbox{for all}\;\;\lambda\in(-\infty,\pi^{2})\;\;\hbox{and}\;\;x\in(0,1),

which is an extremely delicate issue for
\lambda<0
. According to (F4) and (F5), the positive solutions of (
1.1
) blow-up as
h\uparrow 1
, point-wise in
(0,1)
, if
\lambda<\pi^{2}
, while, simultaneously, any solution
(\lambda,u)=(\pi^{2},\alpha\sin(\pi x))
of (
1.6
) perturbs
into solutions of (
1.1
) with
\lambda\sim\pi^{2}
and

. Thus, adopting the terminology of
[
12
]
, all the positive solutions of (

-u^{\prime\prime}=\pi^{2}u
as

. This is the first occasion that such a phenomenology has been described in the literature.
As a byproduct, it becomes apparent how the pioneering result of Moore and Nehari
[
16
]
is actually global in the parameter
\lambda
.

Symmetric positive solutions of (
1.1
)

In this section, we will construct the symmetric positive solutions of (
1.1
) for the choice (
1.3
) for all
h\in(0,1)
. To this purpose, we consider the associated planar system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u^{\prime}=v,\\[3.0pt]
v^{\prime}=-\lambda u-a(x)u^{p},\end{array}\right.

(2.1)

in the closed half-plane

\mathscr{H}^{+}:=\{(u,v)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:\;u\geq 0\}.

So, depending on whether
a=1
or
a=0
, we are lead to analyze one of the following autonomous planar systems

(\mathscr{N})\;\;\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u^{\prime}=v,\\[3.0pt]
v^{\prime}=-\lambda u-u^{p},\end{array}\right.\qquad\hbox{or}\qquad(\mathscr{L})\;\;\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u^{\prime}=v,\\[3.0pt]
v^{\prime}=-\lambda u,\end{array}\right.

(2.2)

respectively. Note that we are throughout assuming that
\lambda<\pi^{2}
, and that the dynamics of both systems,
(\mathscr{N})
and
(\mathscr{L})
, change when considering
\lambda<0
or
\lambda\in[0,\pi^{2})
.

2.1
Symmetric solutions for
\lambda<0

Suppose
\lambda<0
. Then, the set of equilibria of
(\mathscr{N})
consists of the origin,
(0,0)
, plus
(\omega,0)
, where
\omega:=(-\lambda)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}
, and the total energy associated to
(\mathscr{N})
is

\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{N}}(u,v):=\frac{1}{2}v^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}u^{2}+\frac{1}{p+1}u^{p+1}.

Thus,
(0,0)
is a half-right saddle point whose stable and unstable manifolds provide us with
an homoclinic connection of
(0,0)
corresponding to the level set
\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{N}}(u,v)=0
, surrounding the equilibrium
(\omega,0)
, which is a local center. Note that the level set
\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{N}}(u,v)=0
crosses the
u
-axis at the point
(u_{\rm ho},0)
, where

u_{\rm ho}:=\left(\frac{-\lambda(p+1)}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}.

Thus,
(u_{\rm ho},0)
is the crossing point of the homoclinic connection of the origin
with the positive
u
-axis. The phase portrait of
(
\mathscr{N}
) has been sketched in the left figure of Figure
1
.

Figure 1
:
Phase plane diagrams of (
\mathscr{N}
) (left) and (
\mathscr{L}
) (right) when
\lambda<0
. Integral curves with positive energy are plotted in red, while those with zero energy are plotted
in black and those with negative energy are colored in blue.

By simply looking at the left picture of Figure
1
, it becomes apparent that, for every
u_{0}>u_{\rm ho}
, there exists

T:=T_{\mathscr{N}}(\lambda,u_{0})>0

such that the unique solution of (
\mathscr{N}
),
(u(t),v(t))
, satisfying

(u(0),v(0))=(u_{0},0),

is defined in
[-T,T]
and it satisfies

\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{N}}(u(t),v(t))=\frac{1}{2}v^{2}(t)+\frac{\lambda}{2}u^{2}(t)+\frac{1}{p+1}u^{p+1}(t)=\frac{\lambda}{2}u_{0}^{2}+\frac{1}{p+1}u_{0}^{p+1}

(2.3)

for all
t\in[-T,T]
. Moreover,

(u(\pm T),v(\pm T))=(0,\mp v_{0}),\quad v_{0}:=\sqrt{\lambda u_{0}^{2}+\frac{2}{p+1}u_{0}^{p+1}},

and a standard calculation from (
2.3
) yields

T_{\mathscr{N}}(\lambda,u_{0}):=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{\lambda(1-s^{2})+\frac{2}{p+1}u_{0}^{p-1}(1-s^{p+1})}}.

(2.4)

Thanks to (
2.4
), it is apparent that, for every
\lambda<0
,
T_{\mathscr{N}}(\lambda,u_{0})
is decreasing with respect to
u_{0}
. Moreover,

\lim_{u_{0}\downarrow u_{\rm ho}}{T}_{\mathscr{N}}(\lambda,u_{0})=+\infty,\qquad\lim_{u_{0}\uparrow+\infty}{T}_{\mathscr{N}}(\lambda,u_{0})=0.

(2.5)

As far as (
\mathscr{L}
) is concerned, it is a linear system whose unique equilibrium is the origin,
(0,0)
, which is a saddle point if
\lambda<0
. The unstable and stable manifolds,
W^{u}
and
W^{s}
, are the straight lines

W^{u}:\;v=\sqrt{-\lambda}\,u,\qquad W^{s}:\;v=-\sqrt{-\lambda}\,u,

respectively, as sketched in the right picture of Figure
1
. The total energy associated to the linear system (
\mathscr{L}
) is

\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{L}}(u,v):=\frac{1}{2}v^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}u^{2}.

Thus, the level set
\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{L}}(u,v)=0
consists of
W^{u}\cup W^{s}
. Moreover, for every
u_{+}>0
, the necessary time to link
(u_{+},0)
with an arbitrary point

(u_{\ell},v_{\ell})\in\left\{(u,v)\,:\,\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{L}}(u,v)=\tfrac{\lambda}{2}u_{+}^{2}\right\}

is given by

\begin{split}{T}_{\mathscr{L}}(\lambda,u_{+},u_{\ell})&:=\int_{u_{+}}^{u_{\ell}}\frac{du}{\sqrt{\lambda(u_{+}^{2}-u^{2})}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-\lambda}}\int_{1}^{\frac{u_{\ell}}{u_{+}}}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{-1+s^{2}}}\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-\lambda}}\ln\Big(\tfrac{u_{\ell}}{u_{+}}+\sqrt{\Big(\tfrac{u_{\ell}}{u_{+}}\Big)^{2}-1}\,\Big),\end{split}

(2.6)

because the function

g(s):=\ln(s+\sqrt{s^{2}-1}),\qquad s>1,

satisfies
g^{\prime}(s)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{s^{2}-1}}
for all
s>1
.

Consequently, to construct a symmetric positive solution, say
u
, of (
1.1
), we should have

u^{\prime}(0.5)=0,\quad u(0.5)=u_{+},

for some
u_{+}>0
, and should proceed as sketched next:

Step 1:
Since
g^{\prime}(s)>0
for all
s>1
, the function
g
is invertible. Thus, we should consider the set of points
(u_{\ell},v_{\ell})
in the level energy
\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{L}}(u,v)=\frac{\lambda}{2}u_{+}^{2}
such that

\frac{u_{\ell}}{u_{+}}=g^{-1}\left(\frac{h}{2}\sqrt{-\lambda}\right)=:C>1,

because, for this choice, (
2.6
) guarantees that

{T}_{\mathscr{L}}(\lambda,u_{+},u_{\ell})=\frac{h}{2}.

Note that

\frac{u_{\ell}}{C}=u_{+}=\sqrt{\frac{v_{\ell}^{2}}{\lambda}+u_{\ell}^{2}}

implies

v_{\ell}=\pm\sqrt{-\lambda(1-C^{-2}})\,u_{\ell}.

Therefore, the points
(u_{\ell},v_{\ell})
lying in the straight line

v=-\sqrt{-\lambda(1-C^{-2})}\,u,

need a time
\frac{h}{2}
to reach
(u_{+},0)
, and, by symmetry, they as well need another
\frac{h}{2}
unities of time to link
(u_{+},0)
with

(u_{\ell},\sqrt{-\lambda(1-C^{-2}})\,u_{\ell})

along the trajectory
\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{L}}(u,v)=\frac{\lambda}{2}u_{+}^{2}
.

Step 2:
According to Step 1, any positive solution of (
1.1
) starting at some point
(0,v_{0})
, traveling along the trajectory
\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{N}}=\frac{v_{0}^{2}}{2}
of the nonlinear system (
\mathscr{N}
) until reaching some point

(u_{\frac{1-h}{2}},v_{\frac{1-h}{2}})\equiv(u_{\ell},-\sqrt{-\lambda(1-C^{-2})}\,u_{\ell})

at
x=\frac{1-h}{2}
provides us with a symmetric solution of (
1.1
), as sketched in Figure
2
.

(0,v_{0})

. Then, it follows the trajectory

Step 3:
Therefore, (

) has a symmetric positive solution as soon as there exists some
u_{0}>u_{\rm ho}
such that

T_{\mathscr{N}}(\lambda,u_{0})+{T}_{\mathscr{N}}(\lambda,u_{0},u_{\ell})=\frac{1-h}{2},

(2.7)

where
{T}_{\mathscr{N}}(\lambda,u_{0},u_{\ell})
denotes the necessary time to link
(u_{0},0)

with
(u_{\ell},-v_{\ell})
along the trajectory (
2.3
) of (
\mathscr{N}
). According to (
2.3
), it is easily seen that

T_{\mathscr{N}}(\lambda,u_{0},u_{\ell}):=\int_{\frac{u_{\ell}}{u_{0}}}^{1}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{\lambda(1-s^{2})+\frac{2}{p+1}u_{0}^{p-1}(1-s^{p+1})}}.

(2.8)

Thus,

\lim_{u_{0}\uparrow\infty}T_{\mathscr{N}}(\lambda,u_{0},u_{\ell})=0.

Consequently, we can infer from (

) that there exists

for which
(

) holds. So, for every

, (

) admits, at least, one

. Moreover, by Theorem 2.1 of

2.2
Symmetric solutions for

Throughout this section, we assume that

, which is a half-right nonlinear center. Moreover, for every

),

, such that

, and it satisfies

) for all

for all

, and

\mathscr{L}
), we must differentiate the cases

. Indeed, when

) corresponds to a solution starting at some point
(0,v_{0})
, with

, and running along the trajectory

until reaching some point
(u_{0},0)
at time

. Then, this solution should stay at the equilibrium
(u_{0},0)
for a time

before completing the trajectory
\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{N}}=v_{0}^{2}/2
and reaching
(0,-v_{0})
at
x=1
. By the monotonicity of

with respect to
u_{0}
, the existence and the uniqueness of the symmetric positive solution when

follows from (
2.11
). These solutions are constant in the interval
(\frac{1-h}{2},\frac{1+h}{2})
.

Next, we assume that
\lambda>0
. Then, instead of (
2.2
), it is
more appropriate to consider the equivalent systems

(\mathscr{N}_{\mathrm{eq}})\;\;\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u^{\prime}=\sqrt{\lambda}\,v,\\[3.0pt]
v^{\prime}=-\sqrt{\lambda}\,u-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\,u^{p},\end{array}\right.\qquad(\mathscr{L}_{\mathrm{eq}})\;\;\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u^{\prime}=\sqrt{\lambda}\,v,\\[3.0pt]
v^{\prime}=-\sqrt{\lambda}\,u,\end{array}\right.

(2.12)

whose associated total energies are,

\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{N}_{\rm eq}}(u,v):=\sqrt{\lambda}\left(\frac{1}{2}v^{2}+\frac{1}{2}u^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda(p+1)}u^{p+1}\right)

and

\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{L}_{\rm eq}}(u,v):=\sqrt{\lambda}\left(\frac{1}{2}v^{2}+\frac{1}{2}u^{2}\right),

respectively. For
(\mathscr{L}_{\mathrm{eq}})
in (
2.12
), the angular variable in polar coordinates,
\theta(x)
, satisfies

\theta^{\prime}(x)=\frac{v^{\prime}(x)u(x)-u^{\prime}(x)v(x)}{u^{2}(x)+v^{2}(x)}=-\sqrt{\lambda}

and hence, the angle run, clockwise, by a periodic solution in an interval of length
h
is

\theta=\int_{0}^{h}\theta^{\prime}(x)\,dx=-h\sqrt{\lambda}.

Thus, when
\lambda>0
, a symmetric positive solution of (
1.1
) should begin at some point
(0,v_{0})
and reach at time
x=(1-h)/2
, through the level set

\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{N}_{\rm eq}}(u,v)=\sqrt{\lambda}\frac{v_{0}^{2}}{2},

(2.13)

the (unique) point
(u_{\ell},v_{\ell})
lying on the line

v=\Big(\!\!\tan\tfrac{h\sqrt{\lambda}}{2}\Big)u.

(2.14)

Then, it moves in a time
h
an angle
-h\sqrt{\lambda}
trough the level set

\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{L}_{\rm eq}}=\sqrt{\lambda}\left(\frac{1}{2}v_{\ell}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}u_{\ell}^{2}\right)

up to reach the point
(u_{\ell},-v_{\ell})
. Finally, it runs again along (
2.13
) from
(u_{\ell},-v_{\ell})
to
(0,-v_{0})
, as it has been sketched in Figure
3
, where the phase plane route in the nonlinear (resp. linear) level set has been highlighted using red (resp. green) color.
Therefore, to prove the existence of a symmetric positive solution it suffices to show that
(0,v_{0})
can be linked in time
(1-h)/2
to a point
(u_{\ell},v_{\ell})
lying on the line (
2.14
) through the integral curve

v^{2}+u^{2}+\frac{2}{\lambda(p+1)}u^{p+1}=\Big(\!\!\tan\tfrac{h\sqrt{\lambda}}{2}\Big)^{2}u_{\ell}^{2}+u_{\ell}^{2}+\frac{2}{\lambda(p+1)}u_{\ell}^{p+1}.

:
A symmetric positive solution of (
1.1
) in the case
\lambda\in(0,\pi^{2})
.

Note that the necessary time to link
(0,v_{0})
and
(u_{\ell},v_{\ell})
along this integral curve
is given by

T_{\mathscr{N}}(\lambda,0,u_{\ell}):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{\tan^{2}\tfrac{h\sqrt{\lambda}}{2}+1-s^{2}+\frac{2u_{\ell}^{p-1}}{\lambda(p+1)}(1-s^{p+1})}},

(2.15)

where we have used that
u^{\prime}=\sqrt{\lambda}v
. Thus, since
T_{\mathscr{N}}(\lambda,0,u_{\ell})
is decreasing with respect to
u_{\ell}
and

\lim_{u_{\ell}\uparrow+\infty}{T}_{\mathscr{N}}(\lambda,0,u_{\ell})=0,

the existence, and the uniqueness, of a symmetric positive solution of (
1.1
) in case
\lambda>0
is guaranteed as soon as

\lim_{u_{\ell}\downarrow 0}{T}_{\mathscr{N}}(\lambda,0,u_{\ell})=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{\lambda}}-h\Big)>\frac{1-h}{2},

(2.16)

which holds true if, and only if,
\lambda<\pi^{2}
. To show the validity of the limit in (
2.16
), we set

\alpha:=\tan^{2}\tfrac{h\sqrt{\lambda}}{2}+1=\sec^{2}\tfrac{h\sqrt{\lambda}}{2}.

Then,

\begin{split}\lim_{u_{\ell}\downarrow 0}{T}_{\mathscr{N}}(\lambda,0,u_{\ell})&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{\tan^{2}\tfrac{h\sqrt{\lambda}}{2}+1-s^{2}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}}\frac{d\theta}{\sqrt{1-\theta^{2}}}\\
&=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\Big(\arccos\cos\tfrac{h\sqrt{\lambda}}{2}-\frac{\pi}{2}\Big)=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{\lambda}}-h\Big),\end{split}

which provides us with (
2.16
). Summarizing, by the monotonicity of
T_{\mathscr{N}}(\lambda,0,u_{\ell})
with respect to
u_{\ell}
, for every
\lambda\in(0,\pi^{2})
, (
1.1
) has a unique symmetric positive solution.

Finally, note that condition
\lambda<\pi^{2}
is actually necessary for the existence of a positive solution of
(
1.1
). Indeed, if (
1.1
) has a positive solution,
u
, then multiplying the differential equation
by
\sin(\pi x)
and integrating by parts in
(0,1)
yields

\displaystyle\pi^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\sin(\pi x)u(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\lambda\int_{0}^{1}\sin(\pi x)u(x)\,dx+\int_{0}^{1}a(x)\sin(\pi x)u^{p}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle>\lambda\int_{0}^{1}\sin(\pi x)u(x)\,dx

Thus,
\lambda<\pi^{2}
, because
u(x)>0
for all
x\in(0,1)
and
a\gneq 0
.

2.3
Uniqueness of the symmetric positive solution

The next result establishes the uniqueness of the symmetric positive solutions of (
1.1
). Note that, in case
\lambda\in[0,\pi^{2})
, this result has been already established in Section
2.2

Theorem 2.1
.

For every
\lambda\in(-\infty,\pi^{2})
, the problem (
1.1
) has a unique symmetric positive solution.
Thus, it has a positive solution if, and only if,
\lambda<\pi^{2}
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the result for
\lambda<0
. So, suppose
\lambda<0
. Arguing by contradiction, assume that
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are two symmetric positive solutions of (
1.1
) and set

v_{1}=u_{1}^{\prime},\quad v_{2}=u_{2}^{\prime}.

Since
u_{1}(0)=u_{2}(0)=0
, by the uniqueness of the underlying Cauchy problem,

v_{1}(0)\neq v_{2}(0)
. By inter-exchanging their names, if necessary, we can assume that
v_{1}(0)>v_{2}(0)
. Then,

w:=u_{1}-u_{2}

satisfies
w(0)=0
and
w^{\prime}(0)>0
. Thus, there exists
\eta\in(0,1]
such that
w(x)>0
for all
x\in(0,\eta)
, and
w(\eta)=0
. We claim that
\eta=1
, i.e.

w(x)>0\quad\hbox{for all}\;\;x\in(0,1).

(2.17)

Indeed, since (
1.1
) is autonomous in
[0,\frac{1-h}{2}]
,
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
lie in different energy levels there in and, in particular,
v_{1}(0)>v_{2}(0)>0
. Thus, since

\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{N}}(0,v_{1}(0))>\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{N}}(0,v_{2}(0)),

it becomes apparent that, for every
x\in[0,\frac{1-h}{2}]
,

\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{N}}(u_{1}(x),v_{1}(x))\!=\!\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{N}}(0,v_{1}(0))\!>\!\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{N}}(u_{2}(x),v_{2}(x))\!=\!\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{N}}(0,v_{2}(0))\!>\!0.

(2.18)

Moreover, as sketched in Figure
2
, since
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are symmetric positive solutions,
u_{1}(\frac{1-h}{2})
and
u_{2}(\frac{1-h}{2})
should lie in the straight line

v=-\sqrt{-\lambda(1-C^{-2})}u.

Consequently, substituting in (
2.18
), we find that

\begin{split}\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{N}}(u_{1},v_{1})&=\frac{-\lambda(1-C^{-2})}{2}u_{1}^{2}(\tfrac{1-h}{2})+\frac{\lambda}{2}u_{1}^{2}(\tfrac{1-h}{2})+\frac{1}{p+1}u_{1}^{p+1}(\tfrac{1-h}{2})\\
&=\frac{\lambda}{2C^{2}}u_{1}^{2}(\tfrac{1-h}{2})+\frac{1}{p+1}u_{1}^{p+1}(\tfrac{1-h}{2})\\
&>\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{N}}(u_{2},v_{2})=\frac{\lambda}{2C^{2}}u_{2}^{2}(\tfrac{1-h}{2})+\frac{1}{p+1}u_{2}^{p+1}(\tfrac{1-h}{2})>0.\end{split}

(2.19)

Thus, since the function

\varphi(s):=\frac{\lambda s^{2}}{2C^{2}}+\frac{s^{p+1}}{p+1},\qquad s>0,

is increasing if
\varphi(s)>0
, because, in this case,
\varphi^{\prime}(s)=\frac{\lambda s}{C^{2}}+s^{p}>0
for all
s>0
, (
2.19
) implies that

w(\tfrac{1-h}{2})=u_{1}(\tfrac{1-h}{2})-u_{2}(\tfrac{1-h}{2})>0.

In particular,
\eta\neq\frac{1-h}{2}
. Suppose that
\eta\in(0,\frac{1-h}{2})
. Then, since
since
w(x)>0
for all
x\in(0,\eta)
and
w(\eta)=0
, necessarily,
w^{\prime}(\eta)<0
. Moreover,
since
w(\frac{1-h}{2})>0
, there exists
\delta\in(\eta,\frac{1-h}{2})
such that

w(x)<0\;\;\hbox{for all}\;\;x\in(\eta,\delta),\;\;w(\delta)=0,\;\;\hbox{and}\;\;w^{\prime}(\delta)>0.

On the other hand, by (
2.18
), we already know that, for every
x\in[0,\tfrac{1-h}{2}]
,

\begin{split}\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{N}}&(u_{1}(x),v_{1}(x))=\frac{1}{2}v_{1}^{2}(x)+\frac{\lambda}{2}u_{1}^{2}(x)+\frac{1}{p+1}u_{1}^{p+1}(x)\\
&>\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{N}}(u_{2}(x),v_{2}(x))=\frac{1}{2}v_{2}^{2}(x)+\frac{\lambda}{2}u_{2}^{2}(x)+\frac{1}{p+1}u_{2}^{p+1}(x)>0.\end{split}

(2.20)

Consequently, since

w(\eta)=u_{1}(\eta)-u_{2}(\eta)=w(\delta)=u_{1}(\delta)-u_{2}(\delta)=0,

it follows from (
2.20
) that

v_{1}^{2}(\eta)>v_{2}^{2}(\eta),\quad v_{1}^{2}(\delta)>v_{2}^{2}(\delta),

or, equivalently,

|v_{1}(\eta)|>|v_{2}(\eta)|,\quad|v_{1}(\delta)|>|v_{2}(\delta)|.

Moreover, since
w^{\prime}(\eta)<0
, we have that

0>w^{\prime}(\eta)=v_{1}(\eta)-v_{2}(\eta)\;\;\hbox{and}\;\;|v_{1}(\eta)|>|v_{2}(\eta)|.

So,
v_{1}(\eta)<0
. By the clockwise rotation sense in the phase plane, this entails that
v_{1}(x)<0
for all
x\in[\eta,\tfrac{1-h}{2}]
. Similarly, since

0<w^{\prime}(\delta)=v_{1}(\delta)-v_{2}(\delta)\;\;\hbox{and}\;\;|v_{1}(\delta)|>|v_{2}(\delta)|,

we can infer that
v_{1}(\delta)>0
, which is a contradiction. Therefore,

w(x)=u_{1}(x)-u_{2}(x)>0\;\;\hbox{for all}\;\;x\in(0,\tfrac{1-h}{2}].

By the symmetry of the solution, we also have that

w(x)=u_{1}(x)-u_{2}(x)>0\;\;\hbox{for all}\;\;x\in[\tfrac{1+h}{2},1).

Finally, since
w(\tfrac{1-h}{2})>0
and
w(\tfrac{1+h}{2})>0
, if
w
changes sign in
(\tfrac{1-h}{2},\tfrac{1+h}{2})
, then there would exist a subinterval
[\alpha,\beta]\subsetneq(\tfrac{1-h}{2},\tfrac{1+h}{2})
such that
w(x)<0
for all
x\in(\alpha,\beta)
, and

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}-w^{\prime\prime}=\lambda w\qquad\hbox{in}\;[\alpha,\beta],\\[1.0pt]
w(\alpha)=0=w(\beta).\end{array}\right.

(2.21)

Consequently,
w_{|[\alpha,\beta]}
is an eigenfunction with associated eigenvalue

\lambda=\pi^{2}/(\beta-\alpha)^{2}>0,

which contradicts our assumption that
\lambda<0
. Therefore, (
2.17
) holds.

Subsequently, we fix
\lambda<0
and denote by
D^{2}
the differential operator
\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}
.
Then,
w:=u_{1}-u_{2}
satisfies

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-D^{2}w-a(x)(u_{1}^{p}-u_{2}^{p})=\lambda w,\quad x\in[0,1],\\[2.0pt]
w(0)=0=w(1).\end{array}\right.

(2.22)

Setting

\psi(\xi):=(\xi u_{1}+(1-\xi)u_{2})^{p},\qquad\xi\geq 0,

it is apparent that

\displaystyle u_{1}^{p}-u_{2}^{p}

\displaystyle=\psi(1)-\psi(0)=\int_{0}^{1}\psi^{\prime}(\xi)\,d\xi

\displaystyle=p\int_{0}^{1}(\xi u_{1}+(1-\xi)u_{2})^{p-1}\,d\xi\,(u_{1}-u_{2}).

Thus, the problem (
2.22
) can be equivalently written as

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Big(-D^{2}-a(x)p\int_{0}^{1}(\xi u_{1}+(1-\xi)u_{2})^{p-1}\,d\xi\Big)w=\lambda w,\quad x\in(0,1),\\[6.0pt]
w(0)=0=w(1).\end{array}\right.

(2.23)

Thus, since, thanks to (
2.17
),
w>0
in
(0,1)
, it becomes apparent that

\lambda=\sigma_{1}\Big[-D^{2}-a(x)p\int_{0}^{1}(\xi u_{1}+(1-\xi)u_{2})^{p-1}\,d\xi\Big],

(2.24)

where
\sigma_{1}[-D^{2}+b(x)]
denotes the principal eigenvalue of
-D^{2}+b(x)
under
Dirichlet boundary conditions. Hence, by the monotonicity of the principal eigenvalue with respect to the potential (see, if necessary,
[
11
]
), it follows from (
2.24
) that

\lambda<\sigma_{1}[-D^{2}-a(x)u_{1}^{p-1}]=\lambda,

which is impossible. Therefore,
w\equiv 0
, which ends the proof.
∎

2.4
Structure of the symmetric positive solutions

According to, e.g., Amann and López-Gómez
[
1
]
,
or adapting the arguments of Lemma 2.3 and Theorem 2.4 of the authors in
[
13
]
, it becomes apparent that the set of non-trivial solutions of the problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-u^{\prime\prime}=\lambda u+a(x)u^{p},\quad x\in(0,\frac{1}{2}),\\[3.0pt]
u(0)=u^{\prime}(\frac{1}{2})=0,\end{array}\right.

(2.25)

contains a component of positive solutions bifurcating from
\lambda=\pi^{2}
, denoted by

\mathscr{C}^{+}_{0.5}
, such that

\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\mathscr{C}^{+}_{0.5})=(-\infty,\pi^{2}].

As there is a canonical one-one correspondence between the positive solutions of (
2.25

1.1
), we can infer that there exists a component of symmetric positive solutions of (
1.1
), denoted by
\mathscr{C}_{h,s}^{+}
, such that

(\pi^{2},0)\in\mathscr{C}_{h,s}^{+}\;\;\hbox{and}\;\;\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\mathscr{C}_{h,s}^{+})=(-\infty,\pi^{2}].

(2.26)

Thus, thanks to Theorem
2.1
, the next result holds.

Theorem 2.2
.

Besides (
2.26
), the component
\mathscr{C}_{h,s}^{+}
satisfies the following properties:

(a)

For every
(\lambda,u_{\lambda})\in\mathscr{C}_{h,s}^{+}
, either
\lambda=\pi^{2}
and
u_{\lambda}=0
, or
\lambda<\pi^{2}
and
u_{\lambda}
is the unique symmetric positive solution of (
1.1
).

(b)

Subsequently, for every
\lambda<\pi^{2}
, we denote by
u_{\lambda}
the unique symmetric positive solution of (
1.1
). Then, for every
\sigma\in(-\infty,\pi^{2}]
,

\lim_{\lambda\to\sigma}\|u_{\lambda}\|_{\infty}=0\;\;\hbox{if and only if}\;\;\sigma=\pi^{2}.

(2.27)

(c)

For each
\lambda_{0}\in(-\infty,\pi^{2})
, the set of solutions

\mathscr{D}_{0}:=\{u_{\lambda}:\lambda\in[\lambda_{0},\pi^{2})\}

is bounded in
\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,1];\mathbb{R})
, though

\lim_{\lambda\downarrow-\infty}\|u_{\lambda}\|_{\infty}=+\infty.

(2.28)

(d)

The map
\lambda\mapsto u_{\lambda}
is continuous. Therefore,

\mathscr{C}_{h,s}^{+}=\{(\lambda,u_{\lambda})\;:\;\lambda\in(-\infty,\pi^{2}]\}

is a continuous curve, as illustrated in Figure
4
.

Finally, by the uniqueness of the component of positive solutions bifurcating from

u=0
at
\lambda=\pi^{2}
,
\mathscr{C}_{h,s}^{+}
must be a subcomponent of the component of
positive solutions
\mathscr{C}_{h}^{+}
whose existence was established in Section
1
.

Proof.

Part (a) is a byproduct of the previous analysis. Part (b) is a consequence of the fact that

\mathscr{C}^{+}_{0.5}
bifurcates from
u=0
at
\lambda=\pi^{2}
. Thus, by construction,
so does it the component
\mathscr{C}_{h,s}^{+}
. Moreover, it is well known that
\lambda=\pi^{2}
is the unique bifurcation value to positive solutions from
u=0
. Therefore, (
2.27
) holds.

The fact that, for every
\lambda_{0}<\pi^{2}
, the set
\mathscr{D}_{0}
is bounded in
\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,1];\mathbb{R})

is a direct consequence, e.g., of the a priori bounds of
[

]
and

. To show (

Limiting behavior of
\mathscr{C}^{+}_{h}
as

The main goal of this section is analyzing the behavior of the component of positive solutions of (
1.1
), assuming (
1.3
), bifurcating from
u=0
at
\lambda=\pi^{2}
,
\mathscr{C}^{+}_{h}
, as
h\uparrow 1
, i.e. when the nonlinear system (
\mathscr{N}
) tends towards the linear system (
\mathscr{L}
). In the limiting situation when
h=1
, (
1.1
) reduces to (
\mathscr{L}
), which admits some positive solution if, and only if,
\lambda=\pi^{2}
. Actually, the component of positive solutions bifurcating from
u=0
at
\lambda=\pi^{2}
for this limiting problem, denoted by
\mathscr{C}_{1}^{+}
, is given by

\mathscr{C}_{1}^{+}=\{(\pi^{2},\alpha\sin(\pi x))\;:\;\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{+}:=[0,+\infty)\}.

Thus,
\lambda=\pi^{2}
is a bifurcation value to a vertical bifurcation from
u=0
. In particular,

\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\mathscr{C}_{1}^{+})=\{\pi^{2}\}
. Consequently, the remaining positive solutions of the component

\mathscr{C}_{h}^{+}
for
\lambda<\pi^{2}
should be unbounded as
h\uparrow 1
. The next result provides us with the point-wise behavior of the the positive solutions of (
1.1
) as
h\uparrow 1
.

Theorem 3.1
.

For any given
\lambda<\pi^{2}
and, for every
h\in[0,1)
, let
u_{h}
be a positive solution of
(
1.1
) for the choice (
1.3
). Then,

\lim_{h\uparrow 1}\|u_{h}\|_{\infty}=+\infty.

(3.1)

Moreover,

\lim_{h\uparrow 1}u_{h}(x)=+\infty\;\;\hbox{for all}\;\;x\in(0,1).

(3.2)

In particular, the components
\mathscr{C}_{h}^{+}
blow-up, point-wise in
(0,1)
, as
h\uparrow 1
.

Proof.

As in
[
3
, Lemma 2.1]
or
[
13
, Lemma 2.2]
, we denote by
\mathcal{K}:\mathcal{C}([0,1];\mathbb{R})\to\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,1];\mathbb{R})
the linear, continuous and compact operator defined, for every
f\in\mathcal{C}([0,1];\mathbb{R})
, by

\mathcal{K}f(x):=\int_{0}^{x}(y-x)f(y)\,dy-x\int_{0}^{1}(y-1)f(y)\,dy,

which provides us with the unique solution of the linear problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}-u^{\prime\prime}=f\quad\hbox{in}\;\;[0,1],\\[1.0pt]
u(0)=u(1)=0.\end{array}\right.

Thus,

u_{h}=\mathcal{K}(\lambda u_{h}+a_{h}u_{h}^{p})\;\;\hbox{for all}\;\;h\in[0,1).

(3.3)

Arguing by contradiction, suppose that there is a sequence,
\{h_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
in
(0,1)
converging to 1, such that, for some constant
C>0
,

\|u_{h_{n}}\|_{\infty}\leq C\;\;\hbox{for all}\;\;n\geq 1.

(3.4)

Then, (
3.3
) implies that, for every
n\geq 1
,

\frac{u_{h_{n}}}{\|u_{h_{n}}\|_{\infty}}=\mathcal{K}\left(\lambda\frac{u_{h_{n}}}{\|u_{h_{n}}\|_{\infty}}+a_{h_{n}}u_{h_{n}}^{p-1}\frac{u_{h_{n}}}{\|u_{h_{n}}\|_{\infty}}\right).

(3.5)

Moreover, thanks to (
3.4
), the sequence

\lambda\frac{u_{h_{n}}}{\|u_{h_{n}}\|_{\infty}}+a_{h_{n}}(x)u_{h_{n}}^{p-1}\frac{u_{h_{n}}}{\|u_{h_{n}}\|_{\infty}},\quad n\geq 1,

is bounded in
\mathcal{C}([0,1];\mathbb{R})
. Thus, by the compactness of
\mathcal{K}
, there exists a subsequence of

\{u_{h_{n}}\}_{n\geq 1}
, labeled by
h_{n_{m}}
,
m\geq 1
, such that

\lim_{m\to+\infty}\frac{u_{h_{n_{m}}}}{\|u_{h_{n_{m}}}\|_{\infty}}=\psi

in
\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,1];\mathbb{R})
, for some
\psi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,1];\mathbb{R})
such that

\|\psi\|_{\infty}=1
and
\psi\gneq 0
. Consequently, particularizing (
3.5
) at

h_{n_{m}}
and letting
m\to\infty
, shows that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}-\psi^{\prime\prime}=\lambda\psi\quad\hbox{in}\;(0,1),\\[1.0pt]
\psi(0)=\psi(1)=0,\end{array}\right.

which is impossible because
\psi\gneq 0
and
\lambda<\pi^{2}
. Therefore, (
3.1
) holds.

To prove (
3.2
) we will use a concavity argument if
\lambda\geq 0
and a phase plane argument together with the analysis of the linear problem if
\lambda<0
.

Case 1:
Assume
\lambda\geq 0
. Then, for every

,

-u_{h}^{\prime\prime}(x)=\lambda u_{h}(x)+a_{h}(x)u_{h}^{p}(x)\geq 0

and, hence,

is concave in
[0,1]
for all
h\in[0,1]
. Let
x_{m_{h}}\in(0,1)
be such that

u_{h}(x_{m_{h}})=\|u_{h}\|_{\infty}.

Then, for every

, we have that

x=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left(1-\frac{x}{x_{m_{h}}}\right)0+\frac{x}{x_{m_{h}}}x_{m_{h}}&\quad\hbox{if}\;\;x\leq x_{m_{h}},\\[6.0pt]
\left(1-\frac{1-x}{1-x_{m_{h}}}\right)1+\frac{1-x}{1-x_{m_{h}}}x_{m_{h}}&\quad\hbox{if}\;\;x>x_{m_{h}},\end{array}\right.

with
\frac{x}{x_{m_{h}}}\in(x,1]
if
x\leq x_{m_{h}}
, and
\frac{1-x}{1-x_{m_{h}}}\in(1-x,1)
if
x>x_{m_{h}}
. Consequently, by the concavity of

,

u_{h}(x)\geq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left(1-\frac{x}{x_{m_{h}}}\right)u_{h}(0)+\frac{x}{x_{m_{h}}}\|u_{h}\|_{\infty}\geq x\|u_{h}\|_{\infty}&\hbox{if}\;\;x\leq x_{m_{h}},\\[6.0pt]
\left(1-\frac{1-x}{1-x_{m_{h}}}\right)u_{h}(1)+\frac{1-x}{1-x_{m_{h}}}\|u_{h}\|_{\infty}>(1-x)\|u_{h}\|_{\infty}&\hbox{if}\;\;x>x_{m_{h}}.\end{array}\right.

Therefore, by (
3.1
), we find that, for every
x\in(0,1)
,

\lim_{h\uparrow 1}u_{h}(x)=+\infty.

This ends the proof in this case.

Case 2:
Assume
\lambda<0
. We begin by constructing the geometric set of points,
\mathscr{A}_{h}
, lying in the fourth (resp. first) quadrant and taking time
h
to reach the negative (resp. positive)
v
-axis trough the linear flow of
(\mathscr{L})
. Note that, for every positive solution
u
of (
1.1
), we have that

(u(t),v(t))=(u(t),u^{\prime}(t))\notin\mathscr{A}_{h}\;\;\hbox{for all}\;\;t\in[0,\tfrac{1-h}{2}],

(3.6)

because
u(t)>0
for all
t\in(0,1)
. To construct
\mathscr{A}_{h}
we adapt an argument of Section
2.1
. For every
v_{-}<0
, the required time to reach
(0,v_{-})
from a generic point

(u_{r},v_{r})\in\{(u,v)\,:\,\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{L}}(u,v)=v_{-}^{2}\}

is given by

\begin{split}{T}_{\mathscr{L}}(\lambda,u_{r},0)&:=-\int_{u_{r}}^{0}\frac{du}{\sqrt{v_{-}^{2}-\lambda u^{2}}}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\lambda|}}\int_{\frac{\sqrt{|\lambda|}u_{r}}{v_{-}}}^{0}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{1+s^{2}}}\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\lambda|}}\ln\left(\tfrac{\sqrt{|\lambda|}u_{r}}{v_{-}}+\sqrt{\Big(\tfrac{\sqrt{|\lambda|}u_{r}}{v_{-}}\Big)^{2}+1}\,\right),\end{split}

(3.7)

because
g(s):=\ln(s+\sqrt{s^{2}+1})
satisfies
g^{\prime}(s)=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{s^{2}+1}}
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Thus, according to (
3.7
), the set of points
(u_{r},v_{r})
for which

{T}_{\mathscr{L}}(\lambda,u_{r},0)=h

can be described through
g(\tfrac{\sqrt{|\lambda|}u_{r}}{v_{-}})=\sqrt{|\lambda|}h
, or, equivalently,

\frac{\sqrt{|\lambda|}u_{r}}{v_{-}}=g^{-1}(\sqrt{|\lambda|}h),

because

for all

, and, hence, it is globally invertible. Consequently, since
\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{L}}(u_{r},v_{r})=v_{-}^{2}

\frac{v_{r}^{2}}{|\lambda|u_{r}^{2}}-1=\frac{v_{-}^{2}}{|\lambda|u_{r}^{2}}=[g^{-1}(\sqrt{|\lambda|}h)]^{-2}\equiv D(h).

Therefore,

v_{r}=\pm\sqrt{|\lambda|(D(h)+1)}\,u_{r}.

In other words, setting
m_{h}:=\sqrt{|\lambda|(D(h)+1)}
, we have that

\mathscr{A}_{h}:=\{(u_{r},v_{r})\;:\;v_{r}=\pm m_{h}u_{r}\}

In particular, the set of points
(u_{r},v_{r})
, with
u_{r}<0
, reaching the negative
v
-axis at a time
h
are those on the straight line

v=-m_{h}u.

(3.8)

An important feature is that
m_{h}
is decreasing with respect to
h
.

On the other hand, by the proof of Theorem
2.2
(c), we already know that, for every
h\in(0,1)
,

x_{m_{h}}\in(0,\tfrac{1-h}{2}]\cup[\tfrac{1+h}{2},1)\;\;\hbox{if}\;\;u(x_{m_{h}})=\|u_{h}\|_{\infty}.

Thus, there exists an increasing subsequence,
\{h_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
, in
(0,1)
, such that either

\lim_{n\to\infty}h_{n}=1\;\;\hbox{and}\;\;x_{n}\equiv x_{m_{h_{n}}}\in(0,\tfrac{1-h_{n}}{2}]\;\;\hbox{for all}\;\;n\geq 1,

(3.9)

or

\lim_{n\to\infty}h_{n}=1\;\;\hbox{and}\;\;x_{n}\equiv x_{m_{h_{n}}}\in[\tfrac{1+h_{n}}{2},1)\;\;\hbox{for all}\;\;n\geq 1,

(3.10)

though these alternatives are far from excluding, because the symmetric solutions satisfy both.
Assume (
3.9
) holds. Then, owing to (
3.6
), we find that

\frac{v_{h_{n}}\left(\tfrac{1-h_{n}}{2}\right)}{u_{h_{n}}\left(\tfrac{1-h_{n}}{2}\right)}\geq-m_{h_{1}}.

Note that
v_{h_{n}}(\tfrac{1-h_{n}}{2})\leq 0
. Indeed, by (
3.9
),
v(x_{n})=0
, and, hence, by the clockwise rotation sense in the phase plane,

for all

v_{h_{n}}^{2}\left(\tfrac{1-h_{n}}{2}\right)\leq m_{h_{1}}^{2}u_{h_{n}}^{2}\left(\tfrac{1-h_{n}}{2}\right).

(3.11)

Thus, as the associated energy to the system (

) is

\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{N}}(u,v)=\frac{1}{2}v^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}u^{2}+\frac{1}{p+1}u^{p+1},

it follows from (

\begin{split}\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{N}}(u_{h_{n}}\left(\tfrac{1-h_{n}}{2}\right),v_{h_{n}}&\left(\tfrac{1-h_{n}}{2}\right))=\frac{\lambda}{2}\|u_{h_{n}}\|_{\infty}^{2}+\frac{1}{p+1}\|u_{h_{n}}\|_{\infty}^{p+1}\\
&=\frac{1}{2}v_{h_{n}}^{2}\left(\tfrac{1-h_{n}}{2}\right)+\frac{\lambda}{2}u_{h_{n}}^{2}\left(\tfrac{1-h_{n}}{2}\right)+\frac{1}{p+1}u_{h_{n}}^{p+1}\left(\tfrac{1-h_{n}}{2}\right)\\
&\leq\frac{m_{h_{1}}^{2}+\lambda}{2}u_{h_{n}}^{2}\left(\tfrac{1-h_{n}}{2}\right)+\frac{1}{p+1}u_{h_{n}}^{p+1}\left(\tfrac{1-h_{n}}{2}\right)\\
&\leq\frac{m_{h_{1}}^{2}}{2}u_{h_{n}}^{2}\left(\tfrac{1-h_{n}}{2}\right)+\frac{1}{p+1}u_{h_{n}}^{p+1}\left(\tfrac{1-h_{n}}{2}\right).\end{split}

Therefore, we can conclude from (
3.1
) that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}u_{h_{n}}\left(\tfrac{1-h_{n}}{2}\right)=+\infty.

(3.12)

On the other hand, by construction, we have that, for every
n\geq 1
,

-u_{h_{n}}^{\prime\prime}=\lambda u_{h_{n}}\;\;\hbox{in}\;\;\left(\tfrac{1-h_{n}}{2},\tfrac{1+h_{n}}{2}\right).

(3.13)

Since
\lambda<0
, for every
n\geq 1
, there exist two constants,
A_{h_{n}},B_{h_{n}}\in\mathbb{R}
, such that, for every
x\in\left(\tfrac{1-h_{n}}{2},\tfrac{1+h_{n}}{2}\right)
,

u_{h_{n}}(x)=A_{h_{n}}e^{\sqrt{|\lambda|}x}+B_{h_{n}}e^{-\sqrt{|\lambda|}x}=e^{-\sqrt{|\lambda|}x}A_{h_{n}}\left(e^{2\sqrt{|\lambda|}x}+\tfrac{B_{h_{n}}}{A_{h_{n}}}\right).

Equivalently, for every
x\in(0,1)
and sufficiently large

,

e^{\sqrt{|\lambda|}x}u_{h_{n}}(x)=A_{h_{n}}\left(e^{2\sqrt{|\lambda|}x}+\tfrac{B_{h_{n}}}{A_{h_{n}}}\right).

(3.14)

Note that, for every
x\in(0,1)
and sufficiently large
n
, we have that
x\in\left(\tfrac{1-h_{n}}{2},\tfrac{1+h_{n}}{2}\right)
.
Thanks to (
3.12
), we can infer from (
3.14
) that

\begin{split}+\infty&=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\left(e^{\sqrt{|\lambda|}\frac{1-h_{n}}{2}}u_{h_{n}}(\tfrac{1-h_{n}}{2})\right)\\
&=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\left(A_{h_{n}}\left(e^{2\sqrt{|\lambda|}\frac{1-h_{n}}{2}}+\tfrac{B_{h_{n}}}{A_{h_{n}}}\right)\right).\end{split}

(3.15)

Thus, along some subsequence, also labeled by
n
, we find that, either

\lim_{n\to+\infty}A_{h_{n}}=+\infty,\;\;\hbox{or}\;\;\lim_{n\to+\infty}B_{h_{n}}=+\infty.

(3.16)

Suppose that

\liminf_{n\to+\infty}A_{h_{n}}>-\infty.

Then, by (
3.14
) and (
3.15
), we find that, for every
x\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\left(e^{\sqrt{|\lambda|}x}u_{h_{n}}(x)\right)=

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\left(e^{\sqrt{|\lambda|}\frac{1-h_{n}}{2}}u_{h_{n}}(\tfrac{1-h_{n}}{2})\right)

\displaystyle+\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\left(A_{h_{n}}\left(e^{2\sqrt{|\lambda|}x}-e^{2\sqrt{|\lambda|}\frac{1-h_{n}}{2}}\right)\right)=+\infty.

Consequently,

\lim_{n\to+\infty}u_{h_{n}}(x)=+\infty.

Now, suppose that
\liminf_{n\to+\infty}A_{h_{n}}=-\infty
. Then, along some subsequence, labeled again by
n
,
we can assume that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}A_{h_{n}}=-\infty

(3.17)

and, thanks to (
3.16
),

\lim_{n\to+\infty}B_{h_{n}}=+\infty.

In this case, by (
3.14
) and the positivity of
u_{h_{n}}
, we find that, for every
x\in(0,1)
and sufficiently large

,

e^{2\sqrt{|\lambda|}x}+\tfrac{B_{h_{n}}}{A_{h_{n}}}<0.

We claim that, for every
x\in(0,1)
,

\limsup_{n\to+\infty}\left(e^{2\sqrt{|\lambda|}x}+\tfrac{B_{h_{n}}}{A_{h_{n}}}\right)<0.

(3.18)

and a subsequence of
h_{n}
, relabeled by
n

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\left(e^{2\sqrt{|\lambda|}\tilde{x}}+\tfrac{B_{h_{n}}}{A_{h_{n}}}\right)=0.

Then, by (
3.17
), for every

, we have that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\left(e^{\sqrt{|\lambda|}x}u_{h_{n}}(x)\right)=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\left(A_{h_{n}}\left(e^{2\sqrt{|\lambda|}x}+\tfrac{B_{h_{n}}}{A_{h_{n}}}\right)\right)=-\infty,

contradicting the positivity of
u_{h_{n}}
. Therefore, again by (
3.17
), it becomes apparent
from (

) that, for every
x\in(0,1)
,

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\left(e^{\sqrt{|\lambda|}x}u_{h_{n}}(x)\right)=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\left(A_{h_{n}}\left(e^{2\sqrt{|\lambda|}x}+\tfrac{B_{h_{n}}}{A_{h_{n}}}\right)\right)=+\infty.

The case when (
3.10

the proof of the case (

As these arguments can be repeated along any increasing sequence of

(\lambda,u_{\lambda})

(\mathscr{L})
, it is apparent that

\Gamma_{1,\frac{1+h}{2}}^{-}=\left\{(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,:\,(-u,v)\in\Gamma_{0,\frac{1-h}{2}}^{+}\right\}.

:
The sets
\Gamma_{0}
in blue light and

\Gamma_{0,\frac{1-h}{2}}^{+}
in blue light and

In particular, it crosses
W^{u}
and
W^{s}
at two single points, much like
\Gamma_{0,\frac{1-h}{2}}^{+}
.

sketches the main properties of these curves. Note that

\Gamma_{1,\frac{1+h}{2}}^{-}\cap\Gamma_{0,\frac{1-h}{2}}^{+}=\{(u_{1},0)\}

Obviously, to construct positive asymmetric solutions of the problem (
1.1
), the curves
\Gamma_{0,\frac{1-h}{2}}^{+}
and
\Gamma_{1,\frac{1+h}{2}}^{-}
should be connected in a time
h\in(0,1)

through some orbit of the linear system
(\mathscr{L})
. The next result shows how to do it.

Theorem 4.1
.

For every
h\in(0,1)
, there exists
\tilde{\lambda}(h)<0
such that (
1.1
) has, at least,
two asymmetric positive solutions for each
\lambda\leq\tilde{\lambda}(h)
.

Proof.

According the previous analysis, the set

\Gamma_{0,\frac{1-h}{2}}^{+}\cap\{(u,v)\;:\;\;v\geq\sqrt{-\lambda}u\geq 0\}

(4.10)

is the piece of
\Gamma_{0,\frac{1-h}{2}}^{+}
in the first quadrant connecting
(0,0)
with
W^{u}
.
Similarly,

\Gamma_{1,\frac{1+h}{2}}^{-}\cap\{(u,v)\;:\;\;v\geq\sqrt{-\lambda}u\geq 0\}

(4.11)

is the piece of
\Gamma_{1,\frac{1+h}{2}}^{-}
connecting
(0,v_{0})
with
W^{u}
. According to the nature of
the flow of
(\mathscr{L})
, there exists
v_{*}>0
such that, for every
\tilde{v}\in(0,v_{*})
, the level line of the linear system

\mathcal{E}_{\mathscr{L}}(u,v)=\frac{1}{2}v^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}u^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\tilde{v}^{2}

(4.12)

intersects both (
4.10
) and (
4.11
). By continuous dependence, the necessary time to connect (
4.10
) with (
4.11
) through (
4.12
) grows to infinity as
\tilde{v}\downarrow 0
.

On the other hand, in the portion of the first quadrant in between the
u
-axis and
W^{u}
, the sets

\Gamma_{0,\frac{1-h}{2}}^{+}\cap\{(u,v)\;:\;\;0\leq v\leq\sqrt{-\lambda}u\}

(4.13)

and

\Gamma_{1,\frac{1+h}{2}}^{-}\cap\{(u,v)\;:\;\;0\leq v\leq\sqrt{-\lambda}u\}

(4.14)

are the two arcs of curve of
\Gamma_{0,\frac{1-h}{2}}^{+}
and
\Gamma_{1,\frac{1+h}{2}}^{-}
, respectively, connecting the portion of
W^{u}
in the first quadrant with the point
(u_{1},0)
defined in (
4.5
). Note that

(u_{1},0)\in\Gamma_{1,\frac{1-h}{2}}^{+}\cap\Gamma_{1,\frac{1+h}{2}}^{-}.

By construction, the interval of times necessary to connect (
4.13
) to (
4.14
) with the orbits of (
\mathscr{L}
) passing through some point
(\tilde{u},0)
with
\tilde{u}\in(0,u_{1})
is bounded. Therefore, by the continuity of the time map associated to
(\mathscr{L})
, the connection times between
\Gamma_{0,\frac{1-h}{2}}^{+}
and
\Gamma_{1,\frac{1+h}{2}}^{-}
within the first quadrant cover an interval of the form
[\alpha,+\infty)
for some
\alpha>0
;
\alpha
being the minimum connection time between these curves. To complete the proof of the theorem it suffices to show that
\alpha<h
for sufficiently large
|\lambda|=-\lambda
.

Let
(\hat{u},\hat{v})
denote the crossing point of (
4.14
) with the orbit of (
\mathscr{L}
)
through the point
(u_{1},0)

\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{L}}(u,v)=\frac{1}{2}v^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}u^{2}=\frac{\lambda}{2}u_{1}^{2},

(4.15)

which is the crossing point between the green and the blue lines on the right picture of Figure
5
.
The connection time between
(u_{1},0)
and
(\hat{u},\hat{v})
along (
4.15
) is given by

T_{\mathscr{L}}(\lambda,u_{1},\hat{u})=\int_{u_{1}}^{\hat{u}}\frac{du}{\sqrt{\lambda(u_{1}^{2}-u^{2})}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-\lambda}}\int_{1}^{\frac{\hat{u}}{u_{1}}}\frac{d\theta}{\sqrt{-1+\theta^{2}}},

or, equivalently,

T_{\mathscr{L}}(\lambda,u_{1},\hat{u})=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{-\lambda}}\ln\Big(\tfrac{\hat{u}}{u_{1}}+\sqrt{\left(\tfrac{\hat{u}}{u_{1}}\right)^{2}-1}\Big).

(4.16)

Next, we will show that the quotient
\frac{\hat{u}}{u_{1}}
is bounded above. Indeed, coming back to the definition of
u_{0}
and
u_{1}
in (
4.5
), it is apparent that
u_{0}>u_{1}
. Moreover, since the denominator of the integrands in (
4.5
),

\sqrt{\lambda(1-s^{2})+\tfrac{2}{p+1}u_{1}^{p-1}(1-s^{p+1})}

is defined for all
s\in(0,1)
, we find that
\lambda+\tfrac{2}{p+1}u_{1}^{p-1}\geq 0
and, hence,

u_{0}>u_{1}\geq\left(\tfrac{p+1}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}(-\lambda)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}.

(4.17)

Subsequently, for every
\lambda<0
, we set

\kappa_{0}(\lambda):=u_{0}(-\lambda)^{\frac{-1}{p-1}},\quad\kappa_{1}(\lambda):=u_{1}(-\lambda)^{\frac{-1}{p-1}}.

Note that, by construction,
u_{0}
and
u_{1}
also vary with
\lambda
, as the parameter
\lambda
appears in the
formulation of (
1.1
). Then,

u_{0}=\kappa_{0}(\lambda)(-\lambda)^{\frac{1}{p-1}},\quad u_{1}=\kappa_{1}(\lambda)(-\lambda)^{\frac{1}{p-1}},

and, thanks to (
4.17
),

\kappa_{0}(\lambda)>\kappa_{1}(\lambda)>\left(\tfrac{p+1}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}.

On the other hand, it follows from (
4.5
) that

\begin{split}\frac{1-h}{2}\,\sqrt{-\lambda}&=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{s^{2}-1+\frac{2}{p+1}\kappa_{1}^{p-1}(\lambda)(1-s^{p+1})}}\\
&=2\int_{0}^{1}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{s^{2}-1+\frac{2}{p+1}\kappa_{0}^{p-1}(\lambda)(1-s^{p+1})}}.\end{split}

As these integrals are decreasing with respect to
\kappa_{0}(\lambda)
and
\kappa_{1}(\lambda)
,
\frac{1-h}{2}\sqrt{-\lambda}
decreases with
\lambda<0
for fixed
h
, and

\lim_{\theta\uparrow+\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{s^{2}-1+\theta(1-s^{p+1})}}=0,

it becomes apparent that, for every
\lambda_{*}<0
, there exists a constant
M(\lambda_{*})>0
such that

\left(\tfrac{p+1}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}<\kappa_{1}(\lambda)<\kappa_{0}(\lambda)<M(\lambda_{*})\;\;\hbox{for all}\;\;\lambda\in(-\infty,\lambda_{*}].

Thus,

\frac{\hat{u}}{u_{1}}<\frac{u_{0}}{u_{1}}=\frac{\kappa_{0}(\lambda)}{\kappa_{1}(\lambda)}<M(\lambda_{*})\left(\frac{2}{p+1}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\qquad\hbox{for all}\;\lambda\leq\lambda_{*}.

(4.18)

Therefore, by (
4.18
), it follows from (
4.16
) that there exists
\tilde{\lambda}(h)
such that

T_{\mathscr{L}}(\lambda,u_{1},\hat{u})<h\;\,\hbox{for all}\;\;\lambda\in(-\infty,\tilde{\lambda}(h)].

The second asymmetric solution is obtained either by reflection around
x=0.5
of the one we have just constructed, or repeating the previous argument in the fourth quadrant. This ends the proof.
∎

5
A multiplicity result for
\lambda>0

In this section, we analyze the existence of, at least, two positive asymmetric solutions of (
1.1
) when
\lambda>0
and
h
is sufficiently close to
1
. As in Section
2.2
, to treat the case
\lambda>0
it is better to consider, instead of (
2.2
), the equivalent systems

(\mathscr{N}_{\mathrm{eq}})\;\;\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u^{\prime}=\sqrt{\lambda}\,v,\\[3.0pt]
v^{\prime}=-\sqrt{\lambda}\,u-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\,u^{p},\end{array}\right.\qquad(\mathscr{L}_{\mathrm{eq}})\;\;\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u^{\prime}=\sqrt{\lambda}\,v,\\[3.0pt]
v^{\prime}=-\sqrt{\lambda}\,u.\end{array}\right.

(5.1)

If
\lambda>0
, there is no any restriction on the location of the maxima of the positive
solutions of (
1.1
) in the interval
(0,1)
. According to Section
2.2
, the unique symmetric solution reaches its maximum at
x=0.5
, where
a(0.5)=0
always.

However, for asymmetric positive solutions, one can study separately the case where the maximum is reached at
a^{-1}(0)
and the case where it is attained at
a^{-1}(1)
. In this paper, we restrict ourselves to deal with the special case where, for every positive solution
u
of (
1.1
),

x_{m}\in a^{-1}(0)=(\tfrac{1-h}{2},\tfrac{1+h}{2}),\quad\hbox{where}\;\;u(x_{m})=\|u\|_{\infty},

(5.2)

because this will provide us with two asymmetric solutions for
\lambda<\pi^{2}
arbitrarily close to

\pi^{2}
for
h
sufficiently close to
1
, and this is the first multiplicity result of this nature
available in the literature; suggested by the numerical experiments of
[
2
]
. In such a case, we should first connect a point in the positive
v
-semiaxis with a point in the first quadrant through an energy level line of
(\mathscr{N}_{\rm eq})
in the time interval
[0,\tfrac{1-h}{2}]
. For every
\theta_{0}\in(0,\pi/2)
, the necessary time to link a point
(0,v_{0})
with a point
(u_{\theta_{0}},v_{\theta_{0}})
lying in the line
v=(\tan\theta_{0})u
through the orbit of
(\mathscr{E}_{\mathscr{N}_{\rm eq}})

v^{2}+u^{2}+\frac{2}{\lambda(p+1)}u^{p+1}=u_{\theta_{0}}^{2}\tan^{2}\theta_{0}+u_{\theta_{0}}^{2}+\frac{2}{\lambda(p+1)}u_{\theta_{0}}^{p+1}

is given by

T_{\mathscr{N}}(\lambda,0,u_{\theta_{0}}):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{\tan^{2}\theta_{0}+1-s^{2}+\frac{2u_{\theta_{0}}^{p-1}}{\lambda(p+1)}(1-s^{p+1})}}

(see (
2.15
)). Since this connection should be accomplished in time
\tfrac{1-h}{2}
, we impose that

T_{\mathscr{N}}(\lambda,0,u_{\theta_{0}})=\frac{1-h}{2}.

(5.3)

Secondly, by (
5.2
) and for some
\theta_{1}\in(0,\pi/2)
, the point
(u_{\theta_{0}},v_{\theta_{0}})
reached at time
x=\tfrac{1-h}{2}
should be connected at time
x=\tfrac{1+h}{2}
with a point of the fourth quadrant
(u_{\theta_{1}},v_{\theta_{1}})
lying in the line
v=-(\tan\theta_{1})u
, through the energy line of linear system
(\mathscr{L}_{\rm eq})

v^{2}+u^{2}=v_{\theta_{0}}^{2}+u_{\theta_{0}}^{2}=v_{\theta_{1}}^{2}+u_{\theta_{1}}^{2}=\|u\|_{\infty}^{2},

which implies

\cos\theta=\frac{u_{\theta}}{\|u\|_{\infty}}\qquad\hbox{if}\;\;\theta\in\{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\}.

(5.4)

Since the angular variable component in (
\mathscr{L}_{\rm eq}
) satisfies
\theta^{\prime}(x)=-\sqrt{\lambda}
(see Section
2.2
), it follows that

-\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}=\int_{\frac{1-h}{2}}^{\frac{1+h}{2}}\theta^{\prime}(x)\,dx=-\int_{\frac{1-h}{2}}^{\frac{1+h}{2}}\sqrt{\lambda}\,dx=-h\sqrt{\lambda},

which is equivalent to ask for

\frac{\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}}{\sqrt{\lambda}}=h.

(5.5)

Finally, by the symmetry of
a_{h}(x)
, in order to connect
(u_{\theta_{1}},v_{\theta_{1}})
with the negative
v
-semiaxis,

T_{\mathscr{N}}(\lambda,0,u_{\theta_{1}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{\tan^{2}\theta_{1}+1-s^{2}+\frac{2u_{\theta_{1}}^{p-1}}{\lambda(p+1)}(1-s^{p+1})}}=\frac{1-h}{2}

(5.6)

should hold. By (
5.4
) and recalling that

\tan^{2}\theta+1=\tfrac{1}{\cos^{2}\theta},

integrals
T_{\mathscr{N}}(\lambda,0,u_{\theta_{0}})
and
T_{\mathscr{N}}(\lambda,0,u_{\theta_{1}})
can be rewritten for
\theta\in\{\theta_{0},\theta_{1}\}
as

\begin{split}T_{\mathscr{N}}(\lambda,0,u_{\theta})&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{\cos^{2}\theta}-s^{2}+\frac{2\|u\|_{\infty}^{p-1}\cos^{p-1}\theta}{\lambda(p+1)}\left(1-s^{p+1}\right)}}\\
&=\frac{\|u\|_{\infty}^{-\frac{p-1}{2}}}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{\frac{1-s^{2}\cos^{2}\theta}{\|u\|_{\infty}^{p-1}\cos^{2}\theta}+\frac{2\cos^{p-1}\theta}{\lambda(p+1)}\left(1-s^{p+1}\right)}}.\end{split}

(5.7)

Subsequently, we will denote

{S}_{\mathscr{N}}(\lambda,\|u\|_{\infty},\theta):={T}_{\mathscr{N}}(\lambda,0,u_{\theta})\quad\hbox{if}\;\;\theta\in\{\theta_{0},\theta_{1}\}.

According to (
5.3
), (
5.5
), (
5.6
) and (
5.7
), for any fixed
\lambda\in(0,\pi^{2}]
, the existence of a positive solution of (
1.1
) satisfying (
5.2
) is guaranteed if, and only if,

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}}{\sqrt{\lambda}}=h,\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle S_{\mathscr{N}}(\lambda,\|u\|_{\infty},\theta_{0})=S_{\mathscr{N}}(\lambda,\|u\|_{\infty},\theta_{1})=\frac{1-h}{2}.\end{cases}

(5.8)

A solution of (
5.8
) with
\theta_{0}=\theta_{1}
corresponds to a symmetric positive solution of (
1.1
), whose existence and uniqueness has been already established by Theorem
2.1
.
On the other hand, when (
5.8
) holds for
\theta_{0}=\alpha\neq\beta=\theta_{1}
, an asymmetric positive solution of (
1.1
) exists. In such a case, by the symmetry of the problem (
1.1
), a second asymmetric positive solution arises just by taking
\theta_{0}=\beta
and
\theta_{1}=\alpha
. Thus, the asymmetric positive solutions of (
1.1
) appear always by pairs and these pairs of asymmetric solutions are, by construction, symmetric with respect to
x=0.5
. Figure
6
shows the three arcs of trajectory of a positive solution of (
1.1
) under the assumption (
5.2
).

:
An asymmetric positive solution of (

) (

) satisfying (

).

In order to determine the asymmetric solutions, we need to study the function
S_{\mathscr{N}}(\lambda,\|u\|_{\infty},\theta)
for a fixed
\lambda>0
and
\theta\in(0,\pi/2)
. To simplify the notations, for any positive solution
u
of (
1.1
), we denote

R:=\|u\|_{\infty}>0,

and consider the two-variables re-scaled function

\begin{split}\phi(R,\theta)&:=2\sqrt{\lambda}\,S_{\mathscr{N}}(\lambda,R,\theta)\\
&=2\int_{0}^{1}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{\cos^{2}\theta}-s^{2}+\frac{2R^{p-1}\cos^{p-1}\theta}{\lambda(p+1)}\left(1-s^{p+1}\right)}}\\
&=2R^{-\frac{p-1}{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{\frac{1-s^{2}\cos^{2}\theta}{R^{p-1}\cos^{2}\theta}+\frac{2\cos^{p-1}\theta}{\lambda(p+1)}\left(1-s^{p+1}\right)}}.\end{split}

(5.9)

The next lemma collects some basic properties of
\phi(R,\theta)
.

Lemma 5.1
.

The continuous function

\phi:\,(0,+\infty)\times[0,\pi/2)\rightarrow(0,+\infty)
defined by (
5.9
) is of class
\mathcal{C}^{1}
in
(0,+\infty)\times(0,\pi/2)
and it satisfies:

(i)

it is decreasing with respect to
R>0
,

\lim_{R\uparrow+\infty}\phi(R,\theta)=0,\;\;\hbox{and}\quad\phi(R,\theta)<\lim_{R\downarrow 0}\phi(R,\theta)={\pi}-2\theta,

(ii)

it can be extended by continuity to
\pi/2
by taking
\phi(\cdot,\pi/2)=0
,

(iii)

it has a continuous derivative with respect to
\theta\in(0,\pi/2]
, and

\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\theta}(\cdot,\pi/2)=-2.

Proof.

First observe that
\phi
is also defined for
\theta=0
and
R>0
, with

\phi(R,0)=2\int_{0}^{1}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{1-s^{2}+\frac{2R^{p-1}}{\lambda(p+1)}\left(1-s^{p+1}\right)}}<2\arcsin 1=\pi.

Moreover, as the integrand is continuous in its domain of definition,
\phi
is of class
\mathcal{C}^{1}
. The fact that
\phi(R,\theta)
is positive is obvious by definition. Moreover, since
p>1
, it follows from (
5.9
) that, for every
\theta\in[0,\pi/2)
, the mapping
R\mapsto\phi(R,\theta)
is decreasing and satisfies

\lim_{R\uparrow+\infty}\phi(R,\theta)=0.

Also, for every
\theta\in[0,\frac{\pi}{2})
,

\lim_{R\downarrow 0}\phi(R,\theta)=2\int_{0}^{1}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{\cos^{2}\theta}-s^{2}}}=2\int_{0}^{\cos\theta}\frac{d\xi}{\sqrt{1-\xi^{2}}}=\pi-2\theta.

Actually, since
\phi(R,\theta)
is uniformly bounded above by
\pi-2\theta
, its asymptotic profile as
R\uparrow+\infty
is given by

\phi(R,\theta)\!\approx\!R^{-\frac{p-1}{2}}\!\!\left(\tfrac{2\lambda(p+1)}{\cos^{p-1}\theta}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\!\!\!\int_{0}^{1}\!\!\!\frac{ds}{\sqrt{1-s^{p+1}}}\!=\!R^{-\frac{p-1}{2}}\!\!\left(\tfrac{2\lambda(p+1)}{\cos^{p-1}\theta}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\!\!B(\tfrac{1}{p+1},\tfrac{1}{2}),

(5.10)

where
B
is the Euler beta-function (see
[
13
, Section 7.1]
). This ends the proof of Part (i). Part (ii) holds because

\lim_{\theta\uparrow\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\phi(\cdot,\theta)=0.

Finally, Part (iii) follows from the fact that, for every
\theta\in(0,\pi/2)
,

\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\theta}(R,\theta)\!=\!2R^{-\frac{p-1}{2}}\sin\theta\!\!\mathop{\text{\Large$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-1.63885pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle 1$}}$}}\nolimits\frac{\frac{2(p-1)\cos^{p+1}\theta}{\lambda(p+1)}(1-s^{p+1})-\frac{1}{R^{p-1}}}{\left(\frac{1-s^{2}\cos^{2}\theta}{R^{p-1}}+\frac{2\cos^{p+1}\theta}{\lambda(p+1)}\left(1-s^{p+1}\right)\right)^{3/2}}\,ds.

(5.11)

Indeed, for every
R>0
,
\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\theta}(R,\pi/2)=-2
. This ends the proof.

.

On the other hand, when trying to determine the limit of the derivative as
\theta\downarrow 0
one should be aware that the integral

\mathop{\text{\Large$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-1.63885pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle 1$}}$}}\nolimits\frac{\frac{2(p-1)}{\lambda(p+1)}(1-s^{p+1})-\frac{1}{R^{p-1}}}{\left(\frac{1-s^{2}}{R^{p-1}}+\frac{2}{\lambda(p+1)}\left(1-s^{p+1}\right)\right)^{3/2}}\,ds

is divergent at
s=1
and, hence, (
5.11
) takes an indeterminate form. So, we always assume
\theta>0
when analyzing (
5.11
). For every
R>0
and
\theta\in(0,\pi/2]
, it is clear that the sign of (

According to Theorem

, we already know that

\lim_{h\uparrow 1}R=\lim_{h\uparrow 1}\|u\|_{\infty}=\infty.

Thus, we need a description of the function
\phi(R,\cdot)
for large

, which is given by
the next result.

Lemma 5.2
.

Given
a,b
with
0<a<b<\pi/2
, there exists
R^{*}=R^{*}(a,b)
such that, for every
R>R^{*}
, the following holds:

(a)

The function
\phi(R,\cdot)
is increasing in the interval
[a,b]
.

(b)

The function
\phi(R,\cdot)
has a point of maximum at
\theta^{*}\in(b,\pi/2)
. Moreover, it is the absolute maximum point.

(c)

There exists
\theta_{*}\in[0,a]
such that

\phi(R,\theta_{*})=\min_{\theta\in[0,a]}\phi(R,\theta)>0=\phi(R,\pi/2).

Proof.

First, note that, fixing
\theta\in[a,b]
, the map
R\mapsto\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\theta}(R,\theta)
is decreasing and converges to zero as
R\uparrow+\infty
. Indeed, as
R\uparrow+\infty
, we have that

\begin{split}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\theta}(R,\theta)&\approx R^{-\frac{p-1}{2}}(p-1)\sin\theta\left(\tfrac{2\lambda(p+1)}{\cos^{p+1}\theta}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{ds}{(1-s^{p+1})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\\
&=R^{-\frac{p-1}{2}}(p-1)\sin\theta\left(\tfrac{2\lambda(p+1)}{\cos^{p+1}\theta}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}B(\tfrac{1}{p+1},\tfrac{1}{2}),\end{split}

(5.12)

which provides us with the derivative of (

). By (

), there exists

such that

for every

and
\theta\in[a,b]
, proving Part (a).

Now, by Part (a) and recalling that
\phi(R,\pi/2)=0
, the existence of a point of maximum at
(b,\pi/2)
holds, which proves Part (b). Moreover, by (
5.10
) and (

), it becomes apparent that, for sufficiently large

,
\theta^{*}
is a point of absolute maximum. Finally, Part (c) follows by the continuity of
\phi(R,\theta)
. This ends the proof.

Thanks to Lemma
5.2
, for every
\lambda>0
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
R^{*}>0

,
\phi(R,\cdot)

\phi_{M}=\phi_{M}(R):=\max_{\theta\in[0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}]}\phi(R,\theta).

By Lemma

(b), such a value is achieved at some
\theta\in[\pi/2-\varepsilon,\pi/2]

\theta_{M}=\theta_{M}(R,\varepsilon):=\min\{\theta\in[\pi/2-\varepsilon,\pi/2):\;\phi(R,\theta)=\phi_{M}\}.

We also denote by

\theta_{m}=\theta_{m}(R,\varepsilon)\in[0,\varepsilon]
the maximum value of
\theta
for which
\phi(R,\theta)
has a minimum in
[0,\varepsilon]
, taking
\theta_{m}=0
if there are no critical points
in
[0,\varepsilon]
. Moreover, we set

\phi_{m}=\phi_{m}(R):=\phi(R,\theta_{m})>0,\;\;\bar{\theta}:=\min\{\theta\in[\pi/2-\varepsilon,\pi/2):\;\phi(R,\theta)=\phi_{m}\}.

With these notations, by Lemma
5.2
, the next result holds.

Proposition 5.1
.

For every
R>R^{*}
, the restriction of the function
\phi(R,\cdot)
to the interval
[\theta_{m},\bar{\theta}]
satisfies the following conditions:

(a)

\phi(R,\theta_{m})=\phi(R,\bar{\theta})=\phi_{m}>0
, and
\phi(R,\theta)>\phi_{m}
for all
\theta\in(\theta_{m},\bar{\theta}).

(b)

\phi(R,\cdot)
is increasing in
[\theta_{m},\frac{\pi}{2}-\varepsilon]
.

(c)

\theta_{M}\in[\frac{\pi}{2}-\varepsilon,\frac{\pi}{2})
with

\phi(R,\theta_{M})=\max_{\theta\in[\theta_{m},\theta_{M}]}\phi(R,\theta)\geq\phi(R,\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\varepsilon)>\phi_{m}.

There is numerical evidence that, for large
R>0
, there is a unique point of maximum
for
\phi(R,\cdot)
,
\theta_{M}
, and that
\phi(R,\cdot)
is decreasing in

[\theta_{M},\pi/2]
, i.e.,
\theta_{M}
is the unique
\theta\in[\pi/2-\varepsilon,\pi/2)
such that

\phi(R,\theta)=\phi_{m}
. However, since this assertion has not been proved in the analysis of the function
\phi(R,\cdot)
, we are forced to make the definitions of

\theta_{M}
and
\bar{\theta}
a bit more intricate.

Figure
8
shows the main features of Lemma
5.2
and Proposition
5.1
.

Figure 8
:
Graph of
\theta\mapsto\phi(R,\cdot)
for large
R
(left), and graph of
\theta\mapsto\phi(R,\theta)
for large
R
in a right neighborhood of
\theta=0
(right).

The left plot of Figure
8
provides a genuine graph of
\phi(R,\cdot)
for large
R
. Apparently, the figure shows the presence of a unique (maximum) critical point in the interval
(0,\pi/2)
; the red horizontal line crossing the graph at the points
(\theta_{m},\phi_{m})
and
(\bar{\theta},\phi_{m})

at the level
y=\phi_{m}
. As this figure might suggest that
\phi(R,\cdot)

is increasing in the whole interval
[0,\theta_{M}]
, the plot on the right shows a zoom of the graph
of
\phi(R,\cdot)
in a neighborhood of
\theta=0
, with a minimum,
\theta_{m}
, close to
\theta=0
. These graphs have been computed for
p=3
and
\lambda=6
with
R=300
and
\theta\in[0,0.05]
. In such case,
\phi(300,\cdot)\in[0.15116,0.15122]
.

As, for every
h\in(1-\tfrac{\phi_{M}}{\sqrt{\lambda}},1-\tfrac{\phi_{m}}{\sqrt{\lambda}})
, the equation

\phi(R,\theta)=(1-h)\sqrt{\lambda},\qquad\theta\in(0,\pi/2),

has, at least, two solutions:
one with
\theta\in(\theta_{m},\theta_{M})
and a second one with
\theta\in(\theta_{M},\bar{\theta})
, we can continuously parameterize these solutions as follows.

Lemma 5.3
.

There exists a continuous map
\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}):[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
such that

(a)

\gamma_{1}(s)\in(\theta_{m},\theta_{M})
and
\gamma_{2}(s)\in(\theta_{M},\bar{\theta})
for all
s\in(0,1)
,

(b)

\gamma_{1}(0)=\theta_{m}
,
\gamma_{2}(0)=\bar{\theta}
, and
\gamma_{1}(1)=\theta_{M}=\gamma_{2}(1)
,

(c)

\phi(R,\gamma_{1}(s))=\phi(R,\gamma_{2}(s))
for all
s\in[0,1]
.

Lemma
5.3
is just a special version of the Mountain Climbing Theorem, applied to the function
\phi(R,\cdot)
. There are different versions of this theorem which either
require that the function under consideration is piecewise monotone, as in

[
19
]
, or that it has no intervals where it is constant, as in
[
4
,
10
]
. The function
\phi(R,\cdot)
is real analytic in a closed sub-interval
J
of
(0,\pi/2)

with
[\theta_{m},\bar{\theta}]\subset{\rm int}J
. Therefore,
\phi(R,\cdot)
must have finitely many
critical points in
[\theta_{m},\bar{\theta}]
and the Mountain Climbing Theorem can be applied.

The geometric interpretation of Lemma
5.3
goes as follows. There are two climbers,

(\gamma_{1}(s),\phi(R,\gamma_{1}(s)))
and
(\gamma_{2}(s),\phi(R,\gamma_{2}(s)))
,
aiming to attain the highest pick of a mountain at
\theta=\theta_{M}
,
climbing continuously on the two sides

\{(\theta,\phi(R,\theta)):\theta\in[\theta_{m},\theta_{M})\}\quad\text{and }\quad\{(\theta,\phi(R,\theta)):\theta\in(\theta_{M},\bar{\theta}]\}

of the graph of
\phi(R,\cdot)
. The two climbers
start at the time
s=0
at the same level,
\phi_{m}
, though at opposite sides
of the peak, and both meet at the summit,
(\theta_{M},\phi_{M})
, at the time
s=1
. While climbing, they maintain the same altitude at each time. It is apparent that if
\theta_{M}\in(\theta_{m},\pi/2)
is the unique point of maximum of
\phi(R,\cdot)
for
R
large (see Figure
8
and the discussion after Proposition
5.1
), then, one can take the functions

\eta_{1}:=\bigl(\phi(R,\cdot)|_{[\theta_{m},\theta_{M}]}\bigr)^{-1},\quad\eta_{2}:=\bigl(\phi(R,\cdot)|_{[\theta_{M},\bar{\theta}\,]}\bigr)^{-1},

defined on
[\phi(R,0),\phi_{M}]
, and re-parameterize them to
[0,1]
,
by setting

\gamma_{1}(s):=\eta_{i}\bigl(\phi_{m}+s(\phi_{M}-\phi_{m})\bigr),\quad s\in[0,1],\quad(i=1,2),

without invoking the Mountain Climbing Theorem. Using this theorem allows us to avoid a further analysis of
\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\theta}(R,\theta)
in
[\pi-\varepsilon,\frac{\pi}{2})
in order to prove rigorously that
\theta_{M}
is the unique local maximum of
\phi(R,\cdot)
for sufficiently large
R>0
. As it can be proved that, for some
\theta(R)\in(\theta_{M},\pi/2)
,

\lim_{R\uparrow+\infty}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\theta}(R,\theta(R))=+\infty=\lim_{R\uparrow+\infty}\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial\theta^{2}}(R,\theta(R))

the underlying analysis can become quite hard due to the lack of information on the sign of the derivatives near
\pi/2
.

Now, we are ready to prove the existence of asymmetric positive solutions for (
1.1
).

Theorem 5.1
.

For any given
\lambda\in\left(\pi^{2}/4,\pi^{2}\right)
, there exists
R_{\lambda}>0
such that, for every
R>R_{\lambda}
, the problem (
1.1
) has, at least, two asymmetric positive solutions for a suitable
h\in(0,1)
. Moreover,
\lim_{R\uparrow+\infty}h=1
.

Proof.

As we have previously remarked, the asymmetric positive solutions come in pairs. Thus, it suffices to show the existence of, at least, one asymmetric solution. In terms of

\phi(R,\theta)\equiv 2\sqrt{\lambda}\,S_{\mathscr{N}}(\lambda,R,\theta),

(
5.8
) can be, equivalently expressed, as

\begin{cases}\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}=h\sqrt{\lambda},\\[1.0pt]
\phi(R,\theta_{0})=(1-h)\sqrt{\lambda}=\phi(R,\theta_{1}).\end{cases}

(5.13)

By Lemma
5.3
with
\theta_{0}=\gamma_{1}(s)
and
\theta_{1}=\gamma_{2}(s)
,
we have that, for every
s\in(0,1)
,

\phi(R,\gamma_{1}(s))=\phi(R,\gamma_{2}(s))=(1-h(s))\sqrt{\lambda}\in(\phi_{m},\phi_{M}).

(5.14)

Thus, we need only to solve the first equation of (
5.13
), which reads as

\gamma_{1}(s)+\gamma_{2}(s)+\phi(R,\gamma_{1}(s))=\sqrt{\lambda}

(5.15)

by (
5.14
). Equivalently,
g(s)=\sqrt{\lambda}
, where we are setting

g(s):=\gamma_{1}(s)+\gamma_{2}(s)+\phi(R,\gamma_{1}(s)),\qquad s\in[0,1].

Since
\phi(R,\theta)<\pi-2\theta
for every
R>0
, we find from Lemma
5.3
that

\displaystyle\lim_{s\downarrow 0}g(s)=\theta_{m}+\bar{\theta}+\phi(R,\theta_{m})

\displaystyle=\theta_{m}+\bar{\theta}+\phi(R,\bar{\theta})

\displaystyle<\theta_{m}+\bar{\theta}+{\pi}-2\bar{\theta}=\theta_{m}+{\pi}-\bar{\theta}.

Thus, recalling that
\theta_{m}\leq\varepsilon
and
\bar{\theta}\geq\pi/2-\varepsilon
, it becomes apparent that

\lim_{s\downarrow 0}g(s)<\pi/2+2\varepsilon,

provided
R>R^{*}(\varepsilon)\equiv R^{*}(\varepsilon,\pi/2-\varepsilon)
, where
R^{*}(\varepsilon,\pi/2-\varepsilon)
is the value of
R
given by Lemma
5.2
. As we are assuming that
\sqrt{\lambda}>\pi/2
, we can make the choice

2\varepsilon\in(0,\sqrt{\lambda}-\pi/2).

This ensures that, for every
R>R^{*}(\varepsilon)
,

\lim_{s\downarrow 0}g(s)<\sqrt{\lambda}.

(5.16)

Thanks again to Lemma
5.3
,

\lim_{s\uparrow 1}g(s)=2\theta_{M}+\phi(R,\theta_{M})=2\theta_{M}+\phi_{M}.

Since
\sqrt{\lambda}<\pi
and
2\theta_{M}\geq\pi-2\varepsilon
, if
\varepsilon>0
is chosen so that

\sqrt{\lambda}<\pi-2\varepsilon,

then, for every
R>R^{*}(\varepsilon)
, we find that

\lim_{s\uparrow 1}g(s)\geq\pi-2\varepsilon>\sqrt{\lambda},

besides (
5.16
). Note that
\varepsilon
has been chosen so that

\tfrac{\pi}{2}+2\varepsilon<\sqrt{\lambda}<\pi-2\varepsilon.

Therefore, by the continuity of
g(s)
, there is
\hat{s}\in(0,1)
such that

g(\hat{s})=\sqrt{\lambda}
. By construction,
\gamma_{1}(\hat{s})<\theta_{M}<\gamma_{1}(\hat{s})
, so that we can take
\theta_{0}=\gamma_{1}(\hat{s})
and
\theta_{1}=\gamma_{2}(\hat{s})
, or
\theta_{0}=\gamma_{2}(\hat{s})
and

\theta_{1}=\gamma_{1}(\hat{s})
, to get the desired asymmetric solutions.
Finally, from (
5.14
), we find that

\phi(R,\gamma_{1}(\hat{s}))=\phi(R,\gamma_{2}(\hat{s}))=(1-h(\hat{s}))\sqrt{\lambda}\in(\phi_{m},\phi_{M}),

(5.17)

providing us with the precise value of
h
for which we can guarantee the existence of two asymmetric solutions,

h(\hat{s})=1-\frac{\phi(R,\gamma_{1}(\hat{s}))}{\sqrt{\lambda}}.

(5.18)

Observe that, thanks to (
5.17
),

\lim_{R\uparrow+\infty}(1-h(\hat{s}))\sqrt{\lambda}\leq\lim_{R\uparrow+\infty}\phi_{M}:=\lim_{R\uparrow+\infty}\phi(R,\theta_{M})=0.

Consequently,

\lim_{R\uparrow+\infty}h(\hat{s})=1.

(5.19)

This ends the proof.
∎

R=\|u\|_{\infty}
, (
5.19
) is a counterpart of (
3.1
).
