Age‐specific reference intervals for liver function tests in healthy neonates, infants, and young children in Iran
Authors: Seyede Negin Badakhshan, Hamideh Ghazizadeh, Maryam Mohammadi‐Bajgiran, Habibollah Esmaily, Mahdiyeh Yaghooti Khorasani, Mary Kathryn Bohn, Mehran Pashirzad, Atieh Kamel Khodabandeh, Sara Ghazi Zadeh, Iman Alami‐Arani, Hamidreza Rahimi, Gordon A Ferns, Hassan Boskabadi, Reza Assaran‐Darban, Khosrow Adeli, Majid Ghayour‐Mobarhan
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe reference intervals (RIs) for liver function tests (LFTs) were determined in Iranian children for the first time.MethodsA total of 344 healthy pediatrics aged 3 days to 30 months old were recruited. Serum levels of ALT, AST, ALP, direct bilirubin, and total bilirubin were measured. RIs were determined using CLSI Ep28‐A3 guidelines.ResultsAll analytes demonstrated age‐specific differences except AST. ALT and ALP demonstrated significantly elevated levels in infants 0 to <5 months relative to the remainder of the age range. Direct and total bilirubin demonstrated markedly elevated levels in early life with mean of 0.28 mg/dL and 1.64 mg/dL observed for direct and total bilirubin, respectively, decreasing by ~50% in the adjacent partition.ConclusionThese novel data will help improve the clinical interpretation of biochemical test results in young Iranian neonates and children and can be of value to clinical laboratories with similar populations.Keywords: biochemical markers, CLSI Ep28‐A3 guidelines, liver function tests, pediatric, reference intervals

INTRODUCTION

Routine clinical laboratory tests help physicians diagnose and monitor medical conditions throughout the disease course. 1 , 2 Appropriate clinical decision‐making in individual medical care depends on the availability of evidence‐based reference intervals (i.e., normal ranges) for accurate interpretation of laboratory results. Most clinical laboratories adopt the reference intervals (RIs) reported by the medical literature or the diagnostic test manufacturer that were established mainly based on American and European populations. 3 Given that laboratory test results could be influenced by differences in age, sex, genetic factors, and ethnicity, 4 lack of local RIs for each population, particularly in early life, increases the risk of test result misinterpretation, erroneous diagnosis, and/or delayed appropriate medical care. 2 These critical gaps continue to exist in RIs for laboratory markers in all age ranges, but especially in children. Several groups have established reference intervals for key laboratory blood tests in childhood and adolescence, including the CALIPER program (www.caliperproject.org) (The Canadian Laboratory Initiative on Pediatric Reference Intervals), 5 KiGGS (Germany), 6 Denmark and Sweden 7 , 8 and NORICHILD (Scandinavia). 9 , 10 Infants and young children have different body size, pubertal stage, nutrition, and metabolism status in comparison to adults. Pediatric RIs should reflect the dynamic physiological development occurring from birth to adolescence which can deeply influence the levels of biochemical factors such as liver function tests (LFTs) routinely measured in clinical laboratories. 11

LFTs play an important role in the early assessment of hepatic and biliary dysfunction in neonates and young children; however, the interpretation of LFTs in this age period is complicated by hepatic physiology. Specifically, neonatal liver function is relatively immature and undergoes several changes in the postnatal period. 12 , 13 , 14 Neonatal hepatic uptake, conjugation, and biliary excretion of bilirubin are still maturing to reach full function, resulting in a physiological rise in unconjugated bilirubin in the first 2 weeks of life. 15 The need for age‐specific interpretation of direct and total bilirubin in the early postnatal period is well appreciated given the importance of identifying hyperbilirubinemia, jaundice, and resultant brain injury risk. 16 A new hour‐specific serum bilirubin nomogram for neonates ≥35 weeks of gestation was recently developed to assist in interpretation but does not cover the first few months of life and is mainly based on a Caucasian population. Studies have also shown that liver enzymes, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) are influenced by early neonatal physiology and change dramatically within the first few months. 5 , 17 , 18 , 19 For example, ALP levels are expected to change due to placental isoenzyme concentrations. In addition, 20 as an important biomarker for skeletal and hepatobiliary diseases 20 , 21 , 22 that changes with bone metabolism and growth, further ALP concentration changes have been observed in early childhood and puberty. 5 , 17 , 18 , 19 Studies have also demonstrated elevations in ALT and AST levels in early life. 11 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 While elevations in these parameters may arise in a non‐specific manner due to pathophysiology unrelated to liver function (i.e., hemolysis, non‐specific tissue expression), 27 it is more likely reflective of immature liver function in this age period. These dynamics must be considered when defining and using RIs for liver enzymes to distinguish between change due to physiological development and change due to disease.

Studies on RIs for clinical laboratory tests in Iranian adults have shown differences in comparison to those obtained from other countries and other age ranges. 28 , 29 , 30 This demonstrates the necessity of establishing population‐specific RIs in all age ranges, including young children. However, to date, such information has not been available in the Iranian pediatric population, and adult RIs for clinical laboratory test interpretation are used for children. To address this gap, the current study established RIs for five LFTs using an Alpha classic‐AT plus auto‐analyzer device and serum samples from healthy Iranian children ages 3 days to 30 months for the first time.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

In this cross‐sectional study, 249 females and 231 males aged 3 days to 30 months were enrolled upon informed parental consent. During the invitation phase, the total participants were categorized into three groups: (1) birth–6 months (n = 151), (2) 6–12 months (n = 139), (3) 12–30 months (n = 190), to ensure we obtained enough sample size across all age ranges. Therefore, a sufficient sample size of ≥120 healthy participants per group, as recommended by CLSI, was achieved for RI determination. 31

Sampling was carried out using a randomized clustering method between January and March 2021. Among 5 healthcare centers (healthcare centers 1, 2, 3, 5, and Samen) of Mashhad city and its suburbs, 4 healthcare centers 1 , 2 , 3 , 5 as clusters were randomly selected. Then, using recorded information from these centers, eligible infants and children between 0 and 30 months were selected and invited to the healthcare centers by phone call. Written consent was subsequently obtained, and a questionnaire designed according to the materials of the Canadian Laboratory Initiative on Pediatric Reference Intervals (CALIPER) was completed by one of the parents.

According to the questionnaire, participants with poorly controlled diabetes, severe hypertension, endocrine abnormalities, overt signs/symptoms of cardiovascular, liver, kidney, and viral diseases, cancer and chemotherapy, major surgery, hepatitis, and any chronic and/or acute health conditions that were professionally diagnosed, as well as those who consumed medication for disease‐related reasons were excluded from the study. Additionally, individuals who had a blood transfusion, phlebotomy, and/or surgery in the past 6 months as well as premature babies defined as <37 weeks of gestational at delivery were excluded. Following exclusion, 344 (boys: 158 and girls: 186) healthy children ages 3 days to 30 months ((1) birth–6 months (n = 85), (2) 6–12 months (n = 71), (3) 12–30 months (n = 188)) were eligible for the study.

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences (MUMS).

Blood sampling

In total, 4 mL of blood was collected via an antecubital vein venipuncture (V‐Tube™) in the morning between 8:00–10:00 a.m into plain vacuum tubes according to a standard protocol. After 30 minutes, samples were centrifuged (Hettich model D‐78532) at 14563 g for 10 minutes at room temperature (25°C). Serum samples were then aliquoted within 4 h of collection and stored at −80°C until analysis.

Analyses of biochemical markers

Biochemical markers including AST, ALT, and ALP, direct and total bilirubin were measured using enzymatic methods and commercial kits (Biorex) on an Alpha classic‐AT plus auto‐analyzer (TS‐Technology). 32 , 33 AST and ALT were measured using kinetic rate method (without pyridoxal‐5‐phosphate) and ALP levels were determined by a colorimetric assay, with p‐nitrophenyl phosphate (NPP) as substrate and diethanolamine (DEA) as buffer. Serum total and direct bilirubin levels were measured using the Jendrassik–Grof method with the reagents 2,4‐dichloroaniline, detergent, and sodium nitrite.

Statistical analysis

R software (version 3.4.1) was used to perform statistical analysis according to CLSI Ep28‐A3 guidelines. Scatterplots were used to visualize age‐ and sex‐specific changes and selected partitions were evaluated statistically by the Harris and Boyd method, which examines the difference in means and standard deviations between the two subgroups. 34 Outliers were excluded using Tukey's test twice for Gaussian partitions (After calculating the Interquartile Range (IQR) and computing two cutoffs known as the Inner Fence (IF) and Outer Fence (OF): Inner Fence (IF) = Q1–1.5 * IQR and Outer Fence (OF) = Q3 + 1.5 * IQR. Any data point that falls below the inner fence or exceeds the outer fence is considered a potential outlier) and by adjusted Tukey test twice for non‐Gaussian partitions (After calculating the Interquartile Range (IQR), the Number of Potential Outliers (N) should be determined based on the count of data points that fall below the inner fence or exceed the outer fence. The calculation of the Adjusted Inner and Outer Fences is as follows: Calculate an adjusted multiplier, ‘k,’ which depends on N. Common values for ‘k’ are 1.5 for small N (e.g., N < 15) and 3.0 for larger N (e.g., N ≥ 15). Adjusted Inner Fence (AIF): AIF = Q1 − k * IQR. Adjusted Outer Fence (AOF): AOF = Q3 + k * IQR. Any data point that falls below the adjusted inner fence or exceeds the adjusted outer fence is considered an outlier). 28 , 34 Data distributions were assessed using quantile‐quantile plots and Shapiro–Wilk's test. To obtain RIs, defined as the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles, for partitions with ≥120 sample size, we used a nonparametric rank method, and for partitions with <120 and ≥40 sample size, we used a robust statistical algorithm. The lower and upper reference limits were considered and corresponding 90% confidence intervals were calculated. 29 , 31

RESULTS

In this cross‐sectional study, RIs for ALT, AST, ALP, and direct and total bilirubin were measured in a population sample of 344 pediatric participants (boys: 158 and girls: 186) between the ages 3 days to 30 months (mean age: 12.91 ± 7.15 months) who were recruited from January to March 2021 from the Mashhad, Iran. Sex partitioning was not required for any of the hepatic markers evaluated. AST was the only analyte that did not demonstrate any statistically significant age‐specific differences, demonstrating a constant reference value distribution across the age continuum. ALT and ALP demonstrated significantly elevated levels in infants 0 to <5 months relative to the remainder of the age range, requiring partitioning based on the Harris and Boyd method. ALP levels remained stable from 5 to <30 months, while ALT levels decreased more slowly and required two additional partitions (i.e., 5 to <12 and 12 to <30 months).

Direct and total bilirubin also demonstrated markedly elevated levels in early life. A mean of 0.28 mg/dL and 1.64 mg/dL were observed for direct (0 to <4 months) and total bilirubin (0 to <5 months), respectively, and decreased by ~50% in the adjacent partition. It is important to note that confidence intervals were wider for the 97.5th percentile estimates in the first few months of life, particularly for direct bilirubin. In addition, 15 and three outliers were manually removed before analyses for total and direct bilirubin, respectively. These high values are expected to be influenced by the quality of the blood sample.

DISCUSSION

In the present study, age‐specific RIs were established for five hepatic markers in Iranian healthy infants aged 3 days to 30 months using the statistical methodology recommended by CLSI Ep28‐A3 guidelines 31 and applied by CALIPER. 5 In some periods of life, such as neonates (0–1 month old) and infants (<2 years old), dynamic changes in biological, physiological, and hormonal factors are expected to markedly influence biochemical RIs including LFTs. 11 , 23 This has led to several studies in Canada (CALIPER project), 5 Germany (KiGGS project), 6 Australia (HAPPI Kids) 24 and China (PRINCE) 25 and Scandinavia (NORICHILD) 10 to address gaps by establishing accurate age‐ and sex‐specific RIs for several biochemical markers of health and disease from birth to adolescence. However, limited data exists for the Iranian population wherein population and environmental differences may limit the transferability of RIs derived in other populations. In addition, most available studies in Iran as well as other populations are not sufficiently powered to assess age‐specific differences in early infancy on a monthly basis. Herein, we present RIs established for ALT, AST, ALP, and total and direct bilirubin in healthy neonates and infants aged 3 days to 30 months from Iran using an Alpha classic‐AT plus auto‐analyzer (TS‐Technology, Isfahan, Iran) device for the first time.

In our study, statistically significant age‐related changes in reference values were reported for four LFTs. No sex partitioning was required for all studied biomarkers. We observed that with increasing age range, serum concentrations as well as upper limits of RIs for ALT, AST, and ALP as well as direct and total bilirubin decreased. Our findings were generally consistent with previous studies in healthy Canadian (0–19 years), 5 Chinese (0–6 and <20 years), 11 , 23 , 24 , 25 and Australian (30 days–18 years) 24 children as described below and in Table 2. 2

ALT

In this study, three age partitions were established for ALT of 0 to <5, 5 to <12, and 12 to <30 months. Established RIs for ALT decreased with age, which was consistent with previous studies, including those completed in healthy Canadian (CALIPER cohort) 5 and Chinese children 11 , 23 (PRINCE cohort and additional studies) (Table 2). 25 Despite using the same enzymatic method (without P5P) and applying the same statistical approach (nonparametric) recommended by CLSI EP‐28A3c guidelines, the upper limits for ALT established in our study were slightly lower relative to other studies in children of similar ages. 5 , 11 , 23 , 25 Thus, variations observed may be influenced by other factors, such as differences in population differences and the analyzers used. In addition, Lamireau et al. 35 proposed cut‐offs for ALT of 60 and 55 U/L for infant males and females, respectively (0–18 months), based on a European study of HCV‐negative infants that did not report analytical methodology. These proposed cut‐offs are significantly higher than the upper limits determined in this study. A recent study by HAPPI Kids did not show any significant differences in ALT reference values between two different enzymatic methods (with and without P5P) when they used a regression model to calculate RIs. 24 Thus, other factors such as the ethnicity of participants, population differences, and different inclusion/exclusion criteria may play a role in the variation observed. 5 Our study did not observe any statistically significant sex‐specific differences in ALT levels from 0 to <30 months. This is in line with findings from the CALIPER cohort 5 and others. 11 , 23 However, Peng et al. reported a statistically significant difference in ALT values in males vs females aged 28 days to 1 year (PRINCE cohort), observing higher levels in females. 25 The physiological mechanism behind elevated ALT values in infants is unclear. Sex‐specific differences were also observed in the HAPPI Kids cohort; although this study applied a continuous approach from 30 days to 18 years and likely reflects known‐sex‐specific differences in adolescents. 24

AST

For AST, we did not observe any statistically significant age and/or sex differences across the studied age continuum. The established RI for AST of 14–35 U/L in the current study was lower relative to the available reports in the literature, including studies from Canada, 5 China, 11 , 23 , 25 and Australia 24 (Table 2). Among these studies, statistically significant age‐specific differences were observed, and different partitioning strategies were selected. For example, Colantonio et al. and Peng et al. partitioned only at 1 year of age, while Liu et al. reported five age partitions from 0 to <3 years. This contrasts with our findings wherein stable AST levels were observed from 0 to <30 months. This may be explained by sample size limitations. 5 , 11 , 23 All of the described studies used the kinetic rate method (without P5P) for AST measurement and applied CLSI EP28‐A3c guidelines for establishing reference intervals (using a nonparametric approach) (Table 2). These observed discrepancies may be associated with the use of different laboratory analyzers. In contrast, HAPPY kids used a regression model to calculate reference intervals. However, HAPPY kids’ study did not show any significant difference in age‐specific mean reference values in the same healthy participants of 30 days to 18 years of age between five different analyzers (i.e., Ortho Clinical Diagnostics VITROS 5600, Abbott Architect c8000, Roche Cobas c701, Siemens ADVIA 1800, and Beckman Coulter AU5800). 24 Therefore, quantitative differences in reference limits are 24 more likely explained by population characteristics or different statistical analysis.

ALP

In this study, ALP levels were determined by a colorimetric assay, with p‐nitrophenyl phosphate (NPP) as substrate and diethanolamine (DEA) as buffer. RIs for ALP were age‐stratified for children 0 to <5 months (397–1154 U/L) and 5 to <30 months (338–926 U/L). In the current study, the reported lower and upper limits differed significantly from those in other available studies, even though the same nonparametric method was used (Table 2). 5 , 11 , 23 , 25 In contrast to our estimations, ALP RIs derived in CALIPER 5 and PRINCE 25 studies were significantly lower than our results. Specifically, CALIPER established RIs of 134–518 U/L and 156–369 U/L in Canadian children aged 15 days to <1 year and 1 to <10 years, respectively, on the Abbott ARCHITECT c8000 system. 5 The PRINCE study also reported ALP RIs of 119–459 U/L, 140–362 U/L, and 150–372 U/L in Chinese children aged 28 days to <5 months, 6 months to <1 year, and 1 to <2 years, respectively, on the Cobas C702 system. 25 Such marked differences suggest a significant analytical difference in test result measurement. 36 All studies used NPP substrate‐AMP buffer method, while we used the DEA buffer. The type of buffer used in test measurement can affect ALP serum levels. 21 The AACC/IFCC recommends using the AMP buffer in their method. 36 , 37 Lower ALP values have been observed when the AMP buffer is used relative to DEA buffer. This would explain our differential findings, which may be helpful to laboratories using specific analytical methodology.

Direct and total bilirubin

In this study, two age partitions of 0 to <4 months and 4 to <30 months were established for direct bilirubin. Three age partitions of 0 to <5 months, 5 to <10 months, and 10 to <30 months were established for total bilirubin. Decreasing direct and total bilirubin concentrations were observed with age with no statistically significant sex‐specific differences in line with other studies, even though the same nonparametric method was used (Table 2). 5 , 11 , 23 , 25 Specifically, the CALIPER study demonstrated an age‐related decrease for direct and total bilirubin, with the upper limits changing from 0.3 and 0.6 mg/L (15 days–1 year) to 0.2 and 0.4 mg/L (1–9 years), respectively. 5 A similar trend was observed in a study by Liu et al. 23 in Chinese healthy children ages 0 days to 3 years old. However, these estimates were quantitatively lower than RIs derived in our study, particularly for total bilirubin. 5 , 23 The general characteristics of the study population such as ethnicity, and different analytical platforms can help us in interpreting the differences observed in our findings compared to other studies. 38 Also, for full‐term infants with no evidence of hemolysis, the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends initiating phototherapy according to a threshold for serum bilirubin that depends on the infant's age: 15 mg/dL (257 μmol/L) at an age of 25–48 h; 18 mg/dL (308 μmol/L) at 49–72 h; and 20 mg/dL (342 μmol/L) at 72 h or more. Unfortunately, these values are not based on large prospective studies and may not apply to all infants. 39 Our cohort also did not include sufficient sample size of infants in the first days of life to adequately partition. Therefore, the use of established hour‐specific bilirubin normograms should be used in lieu of RIs in this early time point.

CONCLUSION

In this cross‐sectional study, age‐specific RIs for 5 routine LFTs were determined to address critical gaps involved in RIs for a representative population of Iranian infants and children. ALP and ALT as well as direct and total bilirubin demonstrated age‐specific decrease in concentration in our cohort. Quantitative differences were also observed between our study findings and RIs established in other studies of different ethnicity or populations. This highlights the importance of establishing age‐specific pediatric RIs for specific populations. These data can be helpful in the clinical interpretation of laboratory test results and diagnosis of common diseases in Iranian children.
