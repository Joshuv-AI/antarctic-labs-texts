Rings such that u − 1 u-1 lies in J # ​ ( R ) J^{\#}(R) for each unit u u
Peter Danchev, Mina Doostalizadeh, Mehrdad Esfandiar, Omid Hasanzadeh

Abstract
. We investigate the so-called U ​ J # UJ^{\#} rings , a new type of rings in which every unit can be written as 1 + j 1+j with j ∈ J # ​ ( R ) j\in J^{\#}(R) . These rings were defined and studied by Saini-Udar in Czechoslovak Math. J. (2025) under the name J ​ U \sqrt{J}U rings . (See [ 17 ] .) This class extends both the classes of UU and UJ rings, but also has its own special properties. In this study, we present some additional results about U ​ J # UJ^{\#} rings that supply those from [ 17 ] explaining their connections with Dedekind-finite, semi-potent and Boolean rings, respectively, as well as we give several characterizations in this direction. We also examine how these rings behave under common ring constructions and find conditions for group rings to be U ​ J # UJ^{\#} . Moreover, our establishments shed a clearer picture of how unit elements interact with radical-like parts of a ring.

1.
Introduction and Motivation

Throughout the current paper, all rings under consideration are assumed to be associative with identity. For such a ring
R
, the Jacobson radical, the group of units, the set of nilpotent elements, the set of central elements and the set of idempotent elements are denoted by
J(R)
,
U(R)
,
Nil(R)
,
Z(R)
and
Id(R)
, respectively.

Besides, letting
\alpha:R\to R
be a ring endomorphism, we denote by
R[x;\alpha]
the ring of
skew polynomials
over
R
with multiplication defined as
xr=\alpha(r)x
for all
r\in R
. In particular,
R[x]=R[x;1_{R}]
is the
ordinary ring of polynomials
over
R
. Likewise,
R[[x;\alpha]]
denotes the ring of
skew formal power series
over
R
, that is, all formal power series in
x
with coefficients from
R
with multiplication defined via
xr=\alpha(r)x
for all
r\in R
. In particular,
R[[x]]=R[[x;1_{R}]]
is the ring of
formal power series
over
R
.

In
[
20
]
, the set

J^{\#}(R)=\{z\in R:z^{n}\in J(R),\ \text{for some}n\geq 1\}

was introduced as a subset of
R
, which is
not
necessarily a subring, properly containing
J(R)
. It is evident that
N(R)
is too a (proper) subset of
J^{\#}(R)
.

We now provide the non-expert reader with some short historical retrospection of the most central terminology: A ring
R
is said to be an
UU ring
if
U(R)=1+Nil(R)
. This concept was first defined in
[
1
]
, and later on examined in greater depth in
[
6
]
. It was established in the latter article that a ring
R
is strongly nil-clean if, and only if, it is an exchange UU ring. Furthermore, it was proved in
[
7
]
that
R
is an UU ring if, and only if, every unit in
R
is uniquely nil-clean.

It is well known that
1+J(R)\subseteq U(R)
. Motivated by this inclusion and the aforementioned concept of UU rings, the notion of a
JU ring
as the ring
R
for which
U(R)=1+J(R)
was introduced in
[
5
]
. This concept was further explored in
[
9
]
, where it was referred to as a
UJ ring
. It was shown there that a ring
R
is a UJ ring if, and only if, every clean element of
R
is
J
-clean. The structural properties of UJ rings, along with their potential applications in various areas of non-commutative ring theory, have been the subject of several recent investigations
[
5
,
9
,
10
,
13
]
.

Recently, in
[
17
]
the authors defined a ring to be a
\sqrt{J}U
ring
, provided
U(R)=1+\sqrt{J(R)}
, where
\sqrt{J(R)}=J^{\#}(R)
. For convenience of the records, we hereafter will use the second notation.

Since
J(R)\cup Nil(R)\subseteq J^{\#}(R)
, it is pretty clear that both
UU
and
UJ
rings satisfy this condition and are, therefore,
UJ^{\#}
rings; however, the converse does
not
hold in general, because we exhibit in the sequel a specific counterexample to support this claim.

Next, we define an element
a\in R
to be
(strongly)
J^{\#}
-clean
if there exists an idempotent element
e\in Id(R)
such that
a-e\in J^{\#}(R)
(with the extra commuting condition
ea=ae
in the strongly
J^{\#}
-clean case).

The primary objective of this research exploration is to provide a comprehensive description of
UJ^{\#}
rings by comparing their key properties with those of
UU
and
UJ
rings, respectively. Additionally, we aim to uncover some novel and distinctive properties of
UJ^{\#}
rings that are rarely encountered in the existing literature and that non appeared in
[
17
]
as well.

We are now planning to give a brief program of our main material established in what follows: In Section 2, we first study the basic properties of the set

for a ring

and use them to construct some properties and examples of specific
UJ^{\#}
rings. Additionally, we achieve to exhibit some major properties and characterizations of
UJ^{\#}
rings in various different aspects (see, for instance, Propositions
2.13
and
2.14
, Lemmas
2.9
,
2.12
and
2.15
plus Theorems
2.10
and
2.22
). Furthermore, we show that every
UJ^{\#}
ring is Dedekind-finite (see Lemma
2.16
). In Section 3, we establish some fundamental characterizing properties of
UJ^{\#}
rings that are mainly stated and proved in Theorems
3.1
,
3.4
,
3.16
and
3.17
, Corollaries
3.3
and
3.6
, Propositions

and

2.
Examples and Fundamental Properties of
UJ^{\#}
Rings

Let
R
be an arbitrary ring. For completeness of the exposition, we once again set

J^{\#}(R):=\{z\in R:z^{n}\in J(R),\ \text{for some }n\geq 1\}.

We next obtain a useful technicality, needed for our successful presentation.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
R
be any ring. Then, the following assertions hold:

(1) If
a\in J^{\#}(R)
and
b\in R
with
ab=ba
, then
ab\in J^{\#}(R)
.

(2) For every
a\in R
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
a^{n}\in J^{\#}(R)
if and only if
a\in J^{\#}(R)
.

(3) If
a\in J^{\#}(R)
, then
1-a\in U(R)
.

(4) If
a\in J^{\#}(R)\cap Z(R)
, then
a\in J(R)
.

(5) For every ideal
I\subseteq J(R)
, we have
J^{\#}(R/I)=J^{\#}(R)/I
.

(6) If
R=\prod R_{i}
, then
J^{\#}(R)=\prod J^{\#}(R_{i})
.

(7) If
a,b\in R
and
ab\in J^{\#}(R)
, then
ba\in J^{\#}(R)
.

(8)
Nil(R)+J(R)\subseteq J^{\#}(R)
.

Proof.

(1) Suppose
a\in J^{\#}(R)
and
ab=ba
. Thus, for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, it must be that
a^{n}\in J(R)
. Hence,
(ab)^{n}=a^{n}b^{n}\in J(R)
, so
ab\in J^{\#}(R)
.

(2) Assume
a\in J^{\#}(R)
. Thus, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
aa^{n-1}=a^{n-1}a
, so by (1),
a^{n}\in J^{\#}(R)
. Conversely, if
a^{n}\in J^{\#}(R)
, then for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
(a^{n})^{k}=a^{nk}\in J(R)
, whence
a\in J^{\#}(R)
.

(3) Let
a\in J^{\#}(R)
, so
a^{n}\in J(R)
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus,
1-a^{n}\in U(R)
. But, one sees that

(1-a)(1+a+\cdots+a^{n-1})=1-a^{n}\in U(R),

implying
1-a\in U(R)
.

(4) Let
a\in J^{\#}(R)\cap Z(R)
. Thus, for all
r\in R
,
ar=ra\in J^{\#}(R)
viewing (1). So, with (4) in mind,
1-ar\in U(R)
, and hence
a\in J(R)
.

(5) Suppose
a+I\in J^{\#}(R/I)
. Thus, for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we derive

(a+I)^{n}=a^{n}+I\in J(R/I)=J(R)/I.

So, there exists
j\in J(R)
such that
a^{n}-j\in I\subseteq J(R)
, and therefore
a^{n}\in J(R)
giving
a\in J^{\#}(R)
. Consequently,
a+I\in J^{\#}(R)/I
.

Conversely, suppose
a+I\in J^{\#}(R)/I
. Thus, there exists
b\in J^{\#}(R)
with
a-b\in I
and
b^{n}\in J(R)
, so that
a=b+j
for some
j\in J(R)
. Finally,
a^{n}\in J(R)
forcing
a+I\in J^{\#}(R/I)
.

(6) This follows immediately from the fact that
J(R)=\prod J(R_{i})
and the behavior of powers in product rings.

(7) Suppose
ab\in J^{\#}(R)
. Thus, there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
(ab)^{n}\in J(R)
, so
(ba)^{n+1}=b(ab)^{n}a\in J(R)
.

(8) Let
q+j\in Nil(R)+J(R)
, where
q\in Nil(R)
with
q^{n}=0
, and
j\in J(R)
. Therefore, it is easy to see that
(q+j)^{n}\in J(R)
, yielding
q+j\in J^{\#}(R)
.
∎

It is worthy of mentioning that point (6) already appeared in
[
17
]
.

Example 2.2
.

For any ring
R
, as we already noticed above,
J(R)\subseteq J^{\#}(R)
and
Nil(R)\subseteq J^{\#}(R)
, but the converse is
not
necessarily true. E.g., put
R:=M_{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{2})
and
S:=\mathbb{Z}_{2}[[x]]
. So, one verifies that

J^{\#}(R)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\right\},

while we know that
J(R)=(0)
. Furthermore,
x\in J(S)\subseteq J^{\#}(S)
, but
x\notin N(S)
, as suspected.

Although the following statement appeared also in
[
17
]
, we will provide a proof for the reader’s convenience.

Proposition 2.3
.

For any ring
R
, we always have
J^{\#}(R)\cap Id(R)=\{0\}
and
J^{\#}(R)\cap U(R)=\emptyset
.

Proof.

If
e\in J^{\#}(R)\cap Id(R)
, then Lemma
2.1
(3) works to get that
1-e\in Id(R)\cap U(R)
, so
e=0
. Now, if
u\in J^{\#}(R)\cap U(R)
, then there is
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
u^{n}\in J(R)
, and thus
0=1-u^{-n}u^{n}\in U(R)
, which is a contradiction, as expected.
∎

We next proceed by proving a series of other preliminaries.

Proposition 2.4
.

A ring
R
is local if and only if
R=U(R)\cup J^{\#}(R)
.

Proof.

If
R
is local, then knowing that
R=U(R)\cup J(R)\subseteq U(R)\cup J^{\#}(R)\subseteq R
, we thus deduce
R=U(R)\cup J^{\#}(R)
.

Reciprocally, suppose
R=U(R)\cup J^{\#}(R)
and
a\not\in U(R)
. Then, one has that
a\in J^{\#}(R)
, so that Lemma
2.1
(3) applies to detect that
1-a\in U(R)
, whence
R
is a local ring, as promised. ∎

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
R
be a
UJ^{\#}
ring. For any unital subring
S
of
R
, if
S\cap J(R)\subseteq J(S)
, then
S
is a
UJ^{\#}
ring. In particular, the center of
R
is a
UJ^{\#}
ring.

Proof.

Given the assumption, it can easily be shown that
S\cap J^{\#}(R)\subseteq J^{\#}(S)
. Letting
v\in U(S)
, we observe that
\subseteq U(R)
, and since
R
is
UJ^{\#}
, we infer
v-1\in J^{\#}(R)\cap S\subseteq J^{\#}(S)
. So,
S
is a
UJ^{\#}
ring. The rest of the assertion now follows directly from
[

, Ex.5.0]
.
∎

A
rationally closed
subring of

is a subring

such that
U(R)\cap S=U(S)
. Apparently,
R
is a rationally closed subring of both
R[x]
and
R[[x]]
. However,
R[x]
is obviously
not
necessarily a rationally closed subring of

, because
1+x\in U(\mathbb{Z}[[x]])\cap\mathbb{Z}[x]
, but
1+x\not\in U(\mathbb{Z}[x])
.

We, thereby, arrive at the following five routine claims that also appeared in
[

, but are presented here only for the sake of completeness to be the text more nearly self-contained and friendly to the interested reader.

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
S
be a rationally closed subring of
R
, so if
R
is a
UJ^{\#}
ring, then
S
is also a
UJ^{\#}
ring.

Proof.

Since
U(R)\cap S=U(S)
, we also conclude
S\cap J(R)\subseteq J(S)
. Then, the result follows immediately from Proposition
2.5
.
∎

Lemma 2.7
.

A direct product
\prod_{i\in I}R_{i}
of rings is
UJ^{\#}
if and only if each direct factor
R_{i}
is
UJ^{\#}
.

Proof.

As we are aware that
J^{\#}(\prod_{i\in I}R_{i})=\prod_{i\in I}J^{\#}(R_{i})
and
U(\prod_{i\in I}R_{i})=\prod_{i\in I}U(R_{i})
, the result follows at once. ∎

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
R
be a ring and
e\in Id(R)
. Then,
J^{\#}(eRe)=eRe\cap J^{\#}(R)=eJ^{\#}(R)e
.

Proof.

According to the equality
J(eRe)=eRe\cap J(R)=eJ(R)e
and the definition of
J^{\#}(R)
, the result holds.
∎

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
R
be a
UJ^{\#}
ring and
e\in Id(R)
. Then, the corner subring
eRe
is too a
UJ^{\#}
ring.

Proof.

Let
u\in U(eRe)
with the inverse
v
. Therefore,
u+(1-e)\in U(R)
with the inverse
v+(1-e)
, so that
u+(1-e)\in 1+J^{\#}(R)
and
u-e\in J^{\#}(R)\cap eRe=J^{\#}(eRe)
. Hence,
u\in e+J^{\#}(eRe)=1_{eRe}+J^{\#}(eRe)
, and thus
eRe
is a
UJ^{\#}
ring, as claimed.
∎

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
I\subseteq J(R)
be an ideal of a ring
R
. Then,
R
is
UJ^{\#}
if and only if the quotient
R/I
is so.

Proof.

Let
R
be a
UJ^{\#}
ring and choose
u+I\in U(R/I)
. Then,
u\in U(R)
and hence
u=1+j
, where
j\in J^{\#}(R)
. So,
u+I=(1+I)+(j+I)
, where
j+I\in J^{\#}(R)/I=J^{\#}(R/I)
looking at Lemma
2.1
(5).

Oppositely, let
R/I
be a
UJ^{\#}
ring and choose
u\in U(R)
. Then,
u+I\in U(R/I)
and hence
u+I=(1+I)+(j+I)
, where
j+I\in J^{\#}(R/I)=J^{\#}(R)/I
. Thus,
u+I=(1+j)+I
. Therefore,
u-(1+j)\in I\subseteq J(R)\subseteq J^{\#}(R)
. Consequently,
u=1+j^{\prime}
, where
j^{\prime}\in J^{\#}(R)
. Finally,
R
is a
UJ^{\#}
ring.
∎

Our new statements are the following ones.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
R
be a ring. Then, the following two points are fulfilled:

(1) If
a\in J^{\#}(R)
and
b\in J(R)
, then
a+b\in J^{\#}(R)
.

(2) If
a,b\in J^{\#}(R)
and
b\in Z(R)
, then
a+b\in J^{\#}(R)
.

Proof.

(1) Suppose that
a\in J^{\#}(R)
and
b\in J(R)
. Since
a^{n}\in J(R)
for some
n
, we deduce

(a+b)^{n}=a^{n}+b^{n}+\sum_{\text{finite}}(\text{mixed products of }a\text{ and }b),

and each term obviously lies in
J(R)
. Therefore,
a+b\in J^{\#}(R)
.

(2) This follows automatically from Lemma
2.1
(4) combined with part (1).
∎

Lemma 2.12
.

R
is a
UJ^{\#}
ring if and only if
U(R)+(U(R)\cap Z(R))=J^{\#}(R)
.

Proof.

Evidently,
J^{\#}(R)
is always a subset of
U(R)+(U(R)\cap Z(R))
. Now, if
R
is a
UJ^{\#}
ring and
u\in U(R)
,
v\in U(R)\cap Z(R)
, then
1+u
,
1-v\in J^{\#}(R)
. Therefore, Lemma
2.11
employs to write that
u+v=(1+u)-(1-v)\in J^{\#}(R)
. The reverse implication is also evident, because, for every
u\in U(R)
, we have
u-1\in J^{\#}(R)
, as requested.
∎

Proposition 2.13
.

Let
R
be a
UJ^{\#}
ring, and set
\bar{R}:=R/J(R)
. The following two points are valid:

(1) For any
u_{1},u_{2}\in U(R)
, it follows that
u_{1}+u_{2}\neq 1
.

(2) For any
\bar{u_{1}},\bar{u_{2}}\in U(\bar{R})
, it follows that
\bar{u_{1}}+\bar{u_{2}}\neq\bar{1}
.

Proof.

(1) Combining Lemma
2.12
and Proposition
2.3
, the proof is straightforward.

(2) Let us assume
\bar{u}_{1}+\bar{u}_{2}\neq\bar{1}
. Thus,
u_{1},u_{2}\in U(R)
and
u_{1}+u_{2}-1\in J(R)
. Therefore,
u_{1}-1\in U(R)+J(R)
. However, since
U(R)+J(R)\subseteq U(R)
, there exists
u^{\prime}_{2}\in U(R)
such that
u_{1}+u^{\prime}_{2}=1
, which contradicts (1), as pursued.
∎

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
R
be a potent
UJ^{\#}
ring, and put
\bar{R}:=R/J(R)
. The following two items are true:

(1) For any
\bar{e}=\bar{e}^{2}\in\bar{R}
and any
\bar{u_{1}},\bar{u_{2}}\in U(\bar{e}\bar{R}\bar{e})
, it must be that
\bar{u_{1}}+\bar{u_{2}}\neq\bar{e}
.

(2) There does not exist
\bar{e}=\bar{e}^{2}\in\bar{R}
such that
\bar{e}\bar{R}\bar{e}\cong M_{2}(S)
for some ring

.

Proof.

(1) Given
\bar{e},\bar{u_{1}},\bar{u_{2}}
as in (1), we can assume that
e^{2}=e\in R
, because idempotents lift modulo
J(R)
. Then,
\bar{e}\bar{R}\bar{e}\cong eRe/J(eRe)
. Finally, since
eRe
is
UJ^{\#}
, item (1) follows directly from Proposition
2.13
(2).

(2) Note that, in any

matrix ring, it is always fulfilled that

\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}\in U(M_{2}(S))+U(M_{2}(S)).

Hence, there exist
\bar{u_{1}},\bar{u_{2}}\in U(\bar{e}\bar{R}\bar{e})
such that
\bar{u_{1}}+\bar{u_{2}}=\bar{e}
. This, however, is a contradiction with (1), as asked.
∎

Lemma 2.15
.

For any ring
S\neq 0
and any integer

, the matrix ring
M_{n}(S)
of size

is not a
UJ^{\#}
ring.

Proof.

Since
M_{2}(S)
is isomorphic to a corner ring of

for any

, it suffices to illustrate that
M_{2}(S)
is not a
UJ^{\#}
ring. To that aim, consider the matrix

which is clearly a unit. But, since

I-U=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}

is a unit too, it cannot be in

, so that

is manifestly not a

ring, as stated.
∎

A set
\{e_{ij}:1\leq i,j\leq n\}
of nonzero elements of

is said to be a system of

matrix units provided that

and

Recall that a ring

Lemma 2.16
.

Every

Proof.

If we assume the contrary that

is not a Dedekind-finite ring, then there exist elements

such that

but

. Assuming

and

, there exists a nonzero ring

such that

. However, referring to Lemma

is a

We, thereby, extract the following consequence.

Corollary 2.17
.

Let
R
be a
UJ^{\#}
ring and choose

. Then,

if and only if
1-ba\in J^{\#}(R)
.

Proof.

Assuming that

, we have

. Therefore, invoking Lemma

,

. Since

, it follows that

. Hence,

, as needed.
∎

Lemma 2.18
.

Let

(2) Assuming that

3.
Main Results

Standardly, a ring
R
is called
semi-potent
if every one-sided ideal not contained in
J(R)
contains a non-zero idempotent. Moreover, a semi-potent ring
R
is said to be
potent
if idempotents lift modulo
J(R)
. Besides, as usual, a ring
R
is called
reduced
if it contains no non-zero nilpotent elements.

We are now in a position to establish our second chief result.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
R
be a semi-potent ring. Then, the following three statements are equivalent:

(1)
R
is a
UJ^{\#}
ring.

(2)
R/J(R)
is Boolean.

(3)
R
is a
UJ
ring.

Proof.

(1)\Rightarrow(2)
. Since
R
is a semi-potent ring, we know that the quotient
\overline{R}=R/J(R)
is also a semi-potent ring. We will prove now that
\overline{R}
is a reduced ring.

To that end, assume
x^{2}=0
but
0\neq x\in\overline{R}
. So, employing
[
14
, Theorem 2.1]
, there exists
e\in\overline{R}
such that
e\overline{R}e\cong M_{2}(S)
, where
S
is a non-zero ring. However, since by assumption
\overline{R}
is an
UJ^{\#}
ring, Lemma
2.9
employs to deduce that
e\overline{R}e
is an
UJ^{\#}
ring. But, on the other hand, Lemma
2.15
tells us that
M_{2}(S)
is not a
UJ^{\#}
ring, which is a contradiction. Therefore,
\overline{R}
is indeed a reduced ring.

Now, assume there exists
x\in\overline{R}
such that
x-x^{2}\neq 0
in
\overline{R}
. Since
\overline{R}
is a semi-potent ring, there exists
e=e^{2}\in\overline{R}
such that
e\in(x-x^{2})\overline{R}
. Thus,
e=(x-x^{2})y
for some
y\in\overline{R}
. Since
e
is central (indeed, as
[er(1-e)]^{2}=0=[(1-e)re]^{2}
, we thus derive
er(1-e)=0=(1-e)re
), we can write
e=ex\cdot e(1-x)\cdot ey
, so that both
ex,e(1-x)\in U(e\overline{R}e)
. But, Lemma
2.9
informs us that
e\overline{R}e
is an
UJ^{\#}
ring. However,
ex+e(1-x)=e
, which obviously contradicts Proposition
2.13
. Consequently,
\overline{R}
is a boolean ring, as asserted.

(2)\Rightarrow(3)
. Let us assume
u\in U(R)
. Then,
\bar{u}\in U(\bar{R}=R/J(R))
. Since
\bar{R}
is a Boolean ring, we have
\bar{u}=\bar{1}
, guaranteeing that
u-1\in J(R)
, as requested.

(3)\Rightarrow(1)
. This is rather obvious, so we drop off the argumentation.
∎

Two more consequences are these:

Corollary 3.2
.

A regular ring
R
is
UJ^{\#}
if and only if
R
is
UJ
if and only if
R
is

if and only if

is Boolean.

Proof.

Since

is regular, one has that
J(R)=(0)
and

is semi-potent. So, the outcome follows from Theorem

. ∎

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
R
be an Artinian (or, in particular, a finite) ring. Then, the following three issues are equivalent:

(1)
R
is a
UJ^{\#}
ring.

(2)
R
is a
UJ
ring.

(3)

is a

ring.

Proof.

We know that every Artinian ring is always clean. Moreover, since

is Artinian, we have

, as we need. The partial case is immediate, because any finite ring is known to be Artinian.
∎

Our third main result is the following.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
R
be a ring. Then, the following three claims are equivalent:

(1)

is a semi-regular
UJ^{\#}
ring.

(2)
R
is an exchange
UJ^{\#}
ring.

(3)
R
is a semi-Boolean ring.

Proof.

(1)\Rightarrow(2)
. It is very simple, because semi-regular rings are always exchange.

(2)\Rightarrow(3)
. This is routine utilizing Theorem

.

(3)\Rightarrow(1)
. Since

is semi-Boolean, by definition, the factor-ring

is Boolean and idempotents lift modulo

. So,

is a guarantor that

is

whence

invoking Theorem

.
∎

Our two next consequences are derived as follows.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
R
be a
UJ^{\#}
ring. Then, the following three assertions are equivalent:

(1)
R
is a semi-regular ring.

(2)
R
is an exchange ring.

(3)
R
is a clean ring.

Proof.

Applying Theorem
3.4
, (1)
\Leftrightarrow
(2) holds.

(3)\Rightarrow(2)
. This is quite apparent, so we omit the arguments.

(2)\Rightarrow(3)
. If

, then it follows that

Now, we obtain a new characterization for strongly nil-clean rings.

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
R
be a ring. Then, the following three statements are equivalent:

(1)

is a semi-regular
UJ^{\#}
ring and
J(R)
is nil.

(2)
R
is an exchange
UJ^{\#}
ring and
J(R)
is nil.

(3)

is a strongly nil-clean ring.

Proof.

(1)\Rightarrow(2)
. This is rather elementary, so we leave out the details.

(2)\Rightarrow(3)
. We know that

. Now, let

for some

. By hypothesis,

and hence

. So, we have

. Therefore, we have,

. We, thereby, conclude that

is a UU ring and, likewise, it is exchange. Hence,

is strongly nil-clean mimicking
[

.

(3)\Rightarrow(1)
. Knowing that

is nil, we thus infer that all idempotents lift modulo

. Also,
R/J(R)
is a Boolean ring looking at
[

. Hence,
R
is a semi-regular
UJ^{\#}

3.4
.
∎

We now consider some element-wise properties in

Proposition 3.7
.

For any

(1) An element

is clean if and only if it is

-clean.

(2) An element

in

is strongly clean if and only if it is strongly
J^{\#}
-clean.

Proof.

We will only prove part (1), as the other case is rather analogous. To that target, let us assume that
a\in R
is clean with a clean decomposition

. Since
R
is a

u=1+j
, where

. Therefore,
a=(1-e)+(2e+j)
. So, since

(1+j)+2e\in U(R)+J(R)\subseteq U(R)=1+J^{\#}(R),

2e+j\in J^{\#}(R)
, as required.

Conversely, if
a\in R
is
J^{\#}
-clean and

is a
J^{\#}
-clean decomposition, we have

, as requested.

Three other consequences are the following.

Let
Nil_{*}(R)
denote the
prime radical
(or, in other terms, the
lower nil-radical
) of a ring
R
, i.e., the intersection of all prime ideals of
R
. We know that
Nil_{*}(R)
is a nil-ideal of
R
. Moreover, a ring
R
is called
2
-primal
if
Nil_{*}(R)
consists precisely of all the nilpotent elements of
R
(i.e., provided that
Nil(R)=Nil_{*}(R)
). For example, it is well known that all reduced rings and commutative rings are
2
-primal.

Let
R
be a ring and let
\alpha
be an endomorphism of
R
. Recall that
R
is called
\alpha
-compatible
, provided that, for all
a,b\in R
, we have

if, and only if,
a\alpha(b)=0
.

We can now attack the following statement.

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
R
be a
2
-primal ring and
\alpha
an endomorphism of
R
. If
R
is
\alpha
-compatible, then

J^{\#}(R[x;\alpha])=Nil_{*}(R[x;\alpha]).

Proof.

It is immediate that
Nil_{*}(R[x;\alpha])\subseteq J^{\#}(R[x;\alpha])
. Hence, it suffices to show that
J^{\#}(R[x;\alpha])\subseteq Nil_{*}(R[x;\alpha])
. To that purpose, assume that
f\in J^{\#}(R[x;\alpha])
. Then, there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{n}\in J(R[x;\alpha])
. Hence,

f^{n}\in J(R[x;\alpha])=Nil_{*}(R)[x]=Nil_{*}(R[x;\alpha])\subseteq Nil(R[x;\alpha]).

Therefore,
f\in Nil(R[x;\alpha])
.

On the other hand, we have
Nil(R[x;\alpha])\subseteq Nil(R)[x;\alpha]
. But, since
R
is a 2-primal ring, it must be that
f\in Nil(R[x;\alpha])\subseteq Nil(R)[x;\alpha]=Nil_{*}(R)[x;\alpha]
, as asked for.
∎

Proposition 3.18

A ring

Consider

Let
R
be a ring and let
M
be a bi-module over
R
. The
trivial extension
of
R
and
M
is introduced as

T(R,M)=\{(r,m):r\in R\text{ and }m\in M\},

with addition defined component-wise and multiplication defined by

(r,m)(s,n)=(rs,rn+ms).

Note that the trivial extension
T(R,M)
is always isomorphic to the (proper) subring

\left\{\begin{pmatrix}r&m\\
0&r\end{pmatrix}:r\in R\text{ and }m\in M\right\}

of the formal
2\times 2
matrix ring
\begin{pmatrix}R&M\\
0&R\end{pmatrix}
, and likewise
T(R,R)\cong R[x]/\left\langle x^{2}\right\rangle
. We, also, notice that the set of units of the trivial extension
T(R,M)
is

U(T(R,M))=T(U(R),M).

Furthermore, a
Morita context
is introduced as a 4-tuple
\begin{pmatrix}A&M\\
N&B\end{pmatrix}
, where
A
and
B
are rings,
{}_{A}M_{B}
and
{}_{B}N_{A}
are bi-modules, and moreover there exist context products
M\times N\to A
and
N\times M\to B
written multiplicatively as
(w,z)=wz
and
(z,w)=zw
. Thus, the Morita context
\begin{pmatrix}A&M\\
N&B\end{pmatrix}
forms an associative ring with the usual matrix operations.

A Morita context is referred to as
trivial
if the two context products are both trivial, meaning that
MN=(0)
and
NM=(0)
(see cf.
[
15
, p. 1993]
). In this case, we have the isomorphism

\begin{pmatrix}A&M\\
N&B\end{pmatrix}\cong T(A\times B,M\oplus N),

where
\begin{pmatrix}A&M\\
N&B\end{pmatrix}
represents a trivial Morita context as stated in
[
8
]
.

We now are ready to state without evidence our final assertion for this section.

Proposition 3.19
.

Suppose
R
is a ring and
M
is a bi-module over
R
. Then, the following three items hold:

(1) The trivial extension
T(R,M)
is a
UJ^{\#}
ring if and only if
R
is a
UJ^{\#}
ring.

(2) The formal triangular matrix ring
\begin{pmatrix}R&N\\
0&S\end{pmatrix}
is a
UJ^{\#}
ring if and only if
R
and
S
are both
UJ^{\#}
rings.

(3) For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the triangular matrix ring
T_{n}(R)
is a
UJ^{\#}
ring if and only if
R
is a
UJ^{\#}
ring.

4.
Group
UJ^{\#}
Rings

Imitating the traditional terminology, we say that a group is a
p
-group
if each its element has finite order which is a power of the prime number
p
. Moreover, a group
G
is said to be
locally finite
if every finitely generated subgroup is finite.

Suppose now that
G
is an arbitrary group and
R
is an arbitrary ring. As usual,
RG
stands for the group ring of
G
over
R
. The homomorphism
\varepsilon:RG\rightarrow R
, defined by the equality
\varepsilon(\displaystyle\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g)=\displaystyle\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}
, is called the
augmentation map
of
RG
and its kernel, designed by
\Delta(RG)
, is called the
augmentation ideal
of
RG
.

Before stating and proving our key result, we need of a series of preliminary ingredients.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
R
be a
UJ^{\#}
ring,
a\in U(R)
and
g_{n}=1+a+a^{2}+\cdots+a^{n}
. Then, the following two points occur:

(1) If
n
is even, then
g_{n}\in U(R)
.

(2) If
n
is odd, then
g_{n}\in J^{\#}(R)
.

Proof.

(1) Suppose
n
is even. We, then, can write
g_{n}
as

g_{n}=1+(1+a)(a+a^{3}+\cdots+a^{n-1}).

Since
a\in U(R)
, we know
a+1\in J^{\#}(R)
, so that Lemma
2.1
(1) can be applied to infer that

(1+a)(a+a^{3}+\cdots+a^{n-1})\in J^{\#}(R).

Hence,
g_{n}\in U(R)
.

(2) Suppose
n
is odd. We can write
g_{n}
as
g_{n}=1+ag_{n-1}
. So, the usage of (1) guarantees that
g_{n-1}\in U(R)
and, therefore,

g_{n}=1+ag_{n-1}\in 1+U(R)=J^{\#}(R).

∎

Lemma 4.2
.

[
4
, Proposition 9]

Let
R
be a ring,
G
a group and
H
a subgroup of
G
. Then, the following two items occur:

(1)
J(RG)\cap RH\subseteq J(RH)
.

(2) If
G
is a locally finite group, then,
J(R)=J(RG)\cap R
. In particular,
J(R)G\subseteq J(RG)
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
RG
be a
UJ^{\#}
ring. Then,
G
is necessarily a torsion group.

Proof.

We present two different ideas for evidence:

Method 1:
Suppose there is
g\in G
of infinite order. Since
RG
is a
UJ^{\#}
ring, we have
1-g\in J^{\#}(RG)
. Consequently, there is
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(1-g)^{n}\in J(RG)\cap R\langle g\rangle\subseteq J(R\langle g\rangle).

Furthermore, as
1-g
is central in the ring
R\langle g\rangle
, we obtain
1-g\in J(R\langle g\rangle)
. So,
1-g+g^{2}\in U(R\langle g\rangle)
, and thus there are integers
n<m
and elements
a_{i}
with
a_{n}\neq 0\neq a_{m}
such that

(1-g+g^{2})\sum_{i=n}^{m}a_{i}g^{i}=1.

This, however, leads to a contradiction, and so every element
g\in G
must have finite order, as suspected.

Method 2:
Let us now assume
g\in G
. Then, a simple check shows that
R\left\langle g\right\rangle
is a rationally closed subring of
RG
. Thus,
R\left\langle g\right\rangle
is a
UJ^{\#}
ring. If, however, we assume in a way of contradiction that
\left\langle g\right\rangle
is an infinite cyclic group, then the application of Lemma
4.1
(1) teaches us that
1+g+g^{2}\in U(RG)
and hence, using a similar argument to that exploited above, one can readily derive the expected contradiction.
∎

We are now able to establish the following chief statement.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
R
be a ring and let
G
be a group. If
RG
is a
UJ^{\#}
-ring, then
R
is a
UJ^{\#}
ring and
G
is a
2
-group.

Proof.

Since
R
is always a rationally closed subring of
RG
, it follows directly from Lemma
2.6
that
R
is a
UJ^{\#}
ring. Now, we observe with the help of Lemma
4.1
that, for any
g\in G
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, it must be that
\sum_{i=0}^{2k}g^{i}\in U(RG)
.

Next, if
g\in G
has order
p
that does not divide 2, then
p
is obviously odd, whence
p-1=2k
. Consequently,
\sum_{i=0}^{2k}g^{i}\in U(RG)
, and since
(1-g)(\sum_{i=0}^{2k}g^{i})=0
, we find
1-g=0
, which is a contradiction. Thus,
G
must be a 2-group, indeed.
∎

Further, we need some other technical claims before presenting our final key result in this section.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
G
be a locally finite
2
-group and let
R
be a
UJ^{\#}
ring. Then,
\Delta(RG)\subseteq J(RG)
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\overline{R}=R/J(R)
. So, thanking Lemma
2.18
,
\bar{2}\in N(\overline{R})
. Therefore, with
[
4
, Proposition 16]
in hand, we derive
\Delta(\overline{R}G)\subseteq J(\overline{R}G)
.

On the other hand, with
[
3
, Lemma 4]
in mind, we deduce
J(R)G\subseteq J(RG)
, so one verifies that

J(\overline{R}G)=J(R/J(R)G)\cong J(RG/J(R)G)=J(RG)/J(R)G.

Now, letting
f:=\sum a_{g}(1-g)\in\Delta(RG)
, it is simply seen that

\sum\bar{a_{g}}(1-g)\in\Delta(\overline{R}G)\subseteq J(RG)/J(R)G.

Hence, there is
j\in J(RG)
such that
f-j\in J(R)G\subseteq J(RG)
and, because
J(RG)
is an ideal, we conclude that
f
belongs to
J(RG)
, completing the arguments after all.
∎

The following two criteria sound satisfactory.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
G
be a locally finite
2
-group. Then,
RG
is a
UJ^{\#}
ring if and only if
R
is a
UJ^{\#}
ring.

Proof.

Assume
RG
is
UJ^{\#}
. Since
R
is always a rationally closed subring of
RG
, it follows that
R
is a
UJ^{\#}
ring.

Now, to treat the reverse implication, suppose
R
is
UJ^{\#}
. With Lemma
4.5
at hand, we have
\Delta(RG)\subseteq J(RG)
and, on the other side, we have
RG/\Delta(RG)\cong R
. Therefore, the desired result follows at once from Theorem
2.10
.
∎

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
R
be an Artinian ring. Then,
RG
is a
UJ^{\#}
ring if and only if
(R/J(R))G
is a
UJ^{\#}
ring.

Proof.

Since
R
is an Artinian ring, we may follow
[
4
, Proposition 3]
to extract that
J(R)G\subseteq J(RG)
. On the other hand, we know that the isomorphism
(R/J(R))G\cong RG/J(R)G
is always true. Thus, the outcome follows immediately from Theorem
2.10
.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
RG
be a
UJ^{\#}
ring with
3\in J^{\#}(R)
, and let
G
be a
2
-group. Then,
G
is a group of exponent
2
.

Proof.

Owing to Lemma
2.1
(4), we can get that
3\in J(R)
. Hence,
4\in U(R)\subseteq U(RG)
. Therefore,
4\in 1+J^{\#}(RG)
and so
3\in J^{\#}(RG)
. Again, according to Lemma
2.1
(4), we have
3\in J(RG)
.

First, we prove that, for every
g\in G
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
1+g^{2^{k}}\in U(RG)
. To that end, since
R\langle g\rangle
is a subring of
RG
, Lemma
2.6
reaches us that
R\langle g\rangle
is also a
UJ^{\#}
ring. Therefore, without loss of generality, we may assume
g
is central.

As
G
is a
2
-group, let
k\in\mathbb{N}
be the smallest integer such that
g^{2^{k}}=1
. Consequently,
1-g^{2^{k}}=0
. Since
3\in J(RG)
, we have

1+g^{2^{k}}=1-g^{2^{k}}-g^{2^{k}}+3g^{2^{k}}=-g^{2^{k}}+3g^{2^{k}}\in U(RG)+J(RG)\subseteq J(RG).

Now, consider the equality
(1-g^{2^{k-1}})(1+g^{2^{k-1}})=0
. Since
1+g^{2^{k-1}}\in U(RG)
, we can deduce that
1=g^{2^{k-1}}
, which manifestly leads to a contradiction. Therefore,
G
must be a group of exponent
2
, as wanted.
∎

We finish off our work with the following assertion.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
R
be a ring with
3\in J^{\#}(R)
, and let
G
be a
p
-group with
p\neq 2
a prime. If
RG
is a
UJ^{\#}
ring, then
G
is a
3
-group.

Proof.

If
p\neq 2
, then
p
must be odd. Let
g\in G
with
g^{p^{n}}=1
. Suppose
p^{n}=2k+1
. Utilizing a similar argument as in the proof of Lemma
4.3
, we can conclude that

1-g\in J(R\langle g\rangle).

This gives
\Delta(R\langle g\rangle)\subseteq J(R\langle g\rangle)
by taking into account that

1-g^{n}=(1-g)(1+g+\cdots+g^{n-1})\in J(R\langle g\rangle).

Furthermore,
[
4
, Proposition 15(i)]
allows us to derive that
\langle g\rangle
is a
q
-group with
q\in J(R)
. But, since
3\in J(R)
and two distinct primes cannot be in
J(R)
simultaneously, we infer that
q=3
. Thus, in turn, there is
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
g^{3^{m}}=1
. As the element
g\in G
was arbitrary, one detects that
G
is really a
3
-group, as asserted.
∎
