Atomic elementary flux modes explain the steady state flow of metabolites in large-scale flux networks
Authors: Justin G. Chitpin, Theodore J. Perkins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySteady state fluxes are a measure of cellular activity under homeostatic conditions, but understanding how individual substrates are metabolized remains a challenge in large-scale networks. Pathway-based approaches such as elementary flux mode (EFM) analysis are limited to small networks due to the combinatorial explosion of pathways and ambiguity of decomposing fluxes onto EFMs. Here, we present an alternative approach explaining metabolic fluxes in terms of the steady state flow of their atomic constituents. We refer to these pathways as atomic elementary flux modes (AEFMs) and show that computations involving AEFMs are orders of magnitude faster than standard EFMs. Using our approach, we enumerate carbon and nitrogen AEFMs in five genome-scale metabolic models and compute the AEFM decomposition of fluxes estimated in a HepG2 liver cancer cell line. Our results systematically characterize carbon and nitrogen remodeling and, on the HepG2 network, predict glutamine metabolism through a recently discovered non-canonical tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle.

Introduction

Recent technological and computational advancements are leading to a rise in cellular and subcellular metabolic flux data.1,2,3,4 These fluxes quantify the rate of metabolite interconversions within a cell or their movements across subcellular compartments. Metabolic fluxes are studied to understand the dynamics of substrate switching,5,6 redox balancing,7,8 overflow metabolism,9,10 cell differentiation,11,12 and cellular communities.13,14,15 “Fluxomics”16,17 is emerging as a field dedicated to quantifying the totality of fluxes within biological systems.

Current experimental and computational methods generally estimate fluxes under metabolic homeostasis. Under this assumption, the production and consumption of metabolites are balanced, and the fluxes are said to be under steady state. Techniques such as stable isotope tracing analysis (SITA) experimentally determine steady state fluxes by culturing cells in labeled substrates and quantifying their incorporation within endogenous metabolites.18,19 Using this method, fluxes have been measured in major metabolic subsystems such as glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway, and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle.20,21,22 Many computational methods, generally based on flux balance analysis, have been proposed to infer steady state fluxes in metabolic networks from transcript, protein, and metabolite abundance data.23,24,25,26,27 Collectively, these approaches have been used to estimate and predict fluxes in genome-scale metabolic models (GEMs) involving hundreds to thousands of reactions.28,29,30

Although much effort has been made to estimate steady state fluxes, less emphasis has been placed on developing methods to analyze these large-scale flux datasets. In a network with fixed source and sink fluxes, an increase in flux along one metabolic pathway must decrease fluxes along one or more adjacent pathways to maintain mass conservation. For example, UsuiYuki2012Iteo showed that decreasing phosphoglucose isomerase expression redirected glycolytic fluxes toward the pentose phosphate and Entner-Doudoroff pathways in a pgi-expression-controlled Escherichia coli strain.31 In more complex GEMs containing thousands of fluxes (e.g.,32,33,34), however, a change in one reaction rate could alter network fluxes well beyond neighboring reactions.35

This problem of understanding the flow of metabolites within steady state flux networks has historically been addressed through the concept of elementary flux modes (EFMs). EFMs are a pathway-based approach to analyze metabolic networks and are defined as minimal sets of biochemical reactions that maintain steady state flux.36 This minimal property specifies that an EFM cannot be decomposed into two or more smaller pathways carrying steady state flux. An attractive property of EFMs is that any set of steady state fluxes in a network can be explained as a positive, linear combination of EFMs.37 While EFMs are a mechanistic explanation of how metabolites travel through networks, the number of EFMs scales combinatorially as a function of network size.38 It thus remains computationally infeasible to enumerate EFMs in GEMs after over two decades of algorithmic advancements (e.g.,39,40,41). The number of EFMs also poses a challenge for explaining network fluxes in terms of their EFMs. When there are more EFMs than linearly independent fluxes, there exist multiple ways to assign EFM weights that reconstruct the observed network fluxes. Consequently, structural analyses of EFMs and downstream analyses of their weights have been limited to small-scale metabolic subnetworks.42,43,44,45,46

In parallel with EFM analysis, the task of analyzing metabolic pathways has also been approached by numerous other graph-theoretic approaches. These methods derive graphs based, in part, on the metabolic flux network, such as the one shown in Figure 1A, which highlights the first steps of glycolysis. In this representation, the nodes correspond to metabolites, directed hyperedges correspond to reactions, and the weights on those hyperedges represent the fluxes along each reaction. To avoid hyperedges, which can lead to more complex metabolic pathways within these networks, an alternative representation is the metabolic graph,47,48 whereby metabolite nodes are connected to each other by substrate-product reaction relationships. As shown in Figure 1B, the weighted, directed hyperedges of the original metabolic flux network are converted into unweighted edges between all substrate-product pairs. The resulting graph is typically analyzed by K-shortest pathfinding algorithms to identify simple paths/cycles49,50,51,52 or branching paths50,53,54 transforming pairs (or sets) of source and target metabolites. However, not all pathways in these graphs describe feasible sets of transformations due to the presence of metabolic byproducts, coenzymes, and cofactors. Subsequent refinements implemented heuristics to avoid these biologically meaningless pathways by enforcing constraints on the minimum structural similarity or number of carbons between metabolite pairs within a pathway.55,56,57,58,59 This problem was fully solved by the introduction of atomic transition graphs,60 which use atom mapping data to model the movement of individual atoms of a given element across substrates and products. Current methods to analyze metabolic flux networks and atomic flux graphs are often viewed as independent approaches for metabolic pathway analysis.63,64 However, both can be viewed as related types of flux networks operating at the metabolite or metabolite-atom level. One could envision applying metabolic pathway analysis techniques designed for metabolic flux networks on their corresponding atomic flux graphs to study nutrient source catabolism65 or engineer organisms to produce desirable metabolic products.66 This idea was previously explored by Pey et al.,67 who proposed the concept of elementary carbon modes (ECMs). These ECMs were defined as minimal, mass-balanced pathways tracing the flow of individual carbon atoms in an atomic flux graph.67,68 Like paths in metabolite-atom graphs, ECMs correspond to either simple paths spanning source and sink metabolite carbons or simple cycles involving internal metabolite-carbon positions. Although Pey et al. noted that there were more ECMs than EFMs while analyzing several toy metabolic networks, they did not compare ECM and EFM pathway scaling in larger networks. The number of molecular EFMs can grow increasingly complex because of the numerous ways that different combinations of reactions can balance internal metabolite stoichiometries. In particular, there may be multiple ways of balancing pairs of cofactors (e.g., ATP/ADP and NAD+/NADH), metabolic byproducts (e.g., water and hydrogen ions), and coenzymes (e.g., coenzyme A). This complexity can lead to an intractable number of pathways that may only differ by reaction subsets required to mass-balance these ubiquitous metabolites. Removing these metabolites may, in part, solve this problem; however, the choice of metabolite to prune is often subjective and not guaranteed to sufficiently reduce the total number of molecular EFMs. Because ECMs are specific to carbon atoms and account for their atom mappings across reactions, it is plausible that the number of these pathways would remain smaller than the number of molecular EFMs in sufficiently large networks.

Since ECMs are specific to carbon pathways, we propose generalizing ECMs to any element in an atomic flux network. We refer to those pathways as “atomic elementary flux modes” (AEFMs) and formally define them as the EFMs of an atomic flux graph. AEFMs are possible sequences of transitions of an atom within an atomic flux graph. The sequences are non-repeating and either go from a source to a sink or form a cycle in a graph. AEFMs share the irreducible property of EFMs in that the deletion of any reaction along an AEFM completely abolishes its ability to maintain steady state atomic flux. To avoid confusion with classical EFMs of metabolic flux networks, we henceforth refer to these pathways as “molecular EFMs” (MEFMs) to emphasize their balance of molecular stoichiometries. The differences between these pathways are highlighted in Figures 1D and 1E, which show a representative MEFM spanning multiple source and sink metabolites and a representative AEFM rooted on a given source glucose carbon.

Introducing AEFMs presents several computational challenges regarding their calculation from metabolic flux networks. These include the construction of the corresponding atomic transition/flux graph, the complete enumeration of all AEFMs from these graphs, and the (unique) determination of the AEFM weights reconstructing the atomic transition graph fluxes. Indeed, Pey et al. encountered this latter problem working with carbon AEFMs and were unable to estimate unique sets of weights beyond simple carbon flux graphs.68

In our previous work, we developed a novel solution to the EFM flux decomposition problem for the special case of unimolecular reaction networks under a Markov constraint. We modeled a hypothetical particle moving between metabolites under the assumption that the transition probability of each reaction was proportional to the observed steady state flux. By expanding the notion of Markov chain state to include sequences of metabolites, we proposed an algorithm to efficiently compute simple cycle probabilities corresponding to any given EFM. We called this construct the cycle-history Markov chain (CHMC) and proved that these EFM probabilities could be scaled to weights that fully reconstructed the flux along each reaction.69

Here, we combine three key ideas in metabolic modeling to produce a pipeline for analyzing metabolic flux networks under steady state. First, we generalize the notion of ECMs to AEFMs, which can track any atom type through an atomic transition/flux graph. Second, we use a state-of-the-art machine-learning-based algorithm to automate (with manual verification) the construction of atomic flux graphs. Third, we use the CHMC method to enumerate AEFMs in atomic flux graphs and uniquely determine the weight of each mass-balanced path across and within the graph. Using our approach, we demonstrate for the first time the feasibility of AEFM enumeration in large metabolic networks, by showing that the numbers of these carbon and nitrogen pathways are orders of magnitude smaller than the number of MEFMs in five metabolic networks. We then analyze the structure of these AEFMs characterizing carbon and nitrogen remodeling within and across nutrient sources. Lastly, we run our full pipeline on a HepG2 metabolic flux network to examine glutamine carbon remodeling. Our AEFM weight analysis reveals that glutamine consumption is routed through a small number of highly active AEFMs, including a recently discovered non-canonical TCA cycle.70

Results

Pipeline for AEFM enumeration and weight identification

In Figure 2, we outline our computational pipeline to enumerate AEFMs and uniquely assign their weights when the steady state network fluxes are known. In step 1, we require as inputs the metabolic network defined by a set of metabolites and reactions encoded as a stoichiometric matrix, the metabolite structures denoted as SMILES strings, the element type to be analyzed (e.g., carbon, nitrogen), and the steady state fluxes. From these inputs, the resulting metabolic flux network is constructed in step 2 and validated to ensure it satisfies all requirements necessary to construct the corresponding atomic flux graph in step 3. Our program then checks that the metabolic flux network contains no pseudometabolites (since they have no defined structure nor SMILES string), all reactions are mass-balanced at the atomic level, all stoichiometric coefficients are integers (since atoms are not split in biochemical reactions), and that all reaction fluxes are under steady state. Any pseudometabolites and pseudoreactions are removed from the network and replaced with unimolecular source and sink fluxes to preserve steady state among other internal metabolites. Once pre-processed, we then construct the reaction SMILES strings from the stoichiometric matrix and list of SMILES strings. These reaction SMILES strings are imputed into the atom mapping program RXNMapper71 to map the position of each atom within each reaction equation. At this stage, the atom mapping predictions can be visualized using plotting utilities within our pipeline to ensure correct atom mappings. Any incorrect mappings can be manually curated, as required, by directly modifying the reaction SMILES strings. Next, in step 3, these atom mappings are used to construct atomic transition/flux graphs rooted on each source metabolite atom entering the network for all atoms of a particular element, and accounting for reactions with multiple substrate stoichiometries (see Method details and Figures S1 and S2). The corresponding atomic cycle-history Markov chains (ACHMCs) of these atomic flux graphs are then constructed in step 4 to enumerate all AEFMs. Finally, the AEFM weights are computed in step 4 by steady state analysis of the ACHMC (see Methods for details).Figure 2The four steps of the atomic cycle-history Markov chain pipeline

Enumerating AEFMs is computationally tractable in large-scale networks

Given the major computational challenge of enumerating MEFMs in networks with >100 metabolites and reactions, we first sought to test whether it was feasible to enumerate AEFMs in GEMs. We selected five GEMs across distinct organisms, which included metabolic models of E. coli (E. coli core72; data ref73), human red blood cells (iAB RBC 28374; data ref75), Helicobacter pylori (iIT34176; data77), Staphylococcus aureus (iSB61978; data ref79), and human liver cancer cells (HepG228; data ref80). These models were pre-processed to ensure unambiguous atom mappings across all reactions. Specifically, all metabolites with no known structures (pseudometabolites) and pseudoreactions with non-integer stoichiometries were removed from the networks. The resulting metabolic networks ranged between 71 to 547 metabolites and 144 to 974 reactions (Figure S3). We then attempted to enumerate the MEFMs using FluxModeCalculator, currently the fastest MEFM enumeration program written in MATLAB,81 and enumerate the AEFMs using our pipeline written in Julia (Figure 2, steps 1–4). For the AEFM analysis, we enumerated all AEFMs from each carbon or nitrogen atom in every source metabolite.

We ran FluxModeCalculator on a desktop PC with 32 threads and 64 GB memory. We found the computation of MEFMs to be feasible only for the two smallest E. coli core and iAB RBC 283 networks, which contained 5,999,302 and 5,058,711 MEFMs, respectively (Figure 3A). These computations took 96 and 21 min to complete, running FluxModeCalculator in single-threaded mode. Using all 32 threads improved MEFM enumeration by seven times (Figure S4), decreasing the computation to 13 and 3 min, respectively. However, we were unable to enumerate the MEFMs of the remaining networks after 7 days of wall time. By focusing on an atom of interest, our ACHMC approach greatly reduced the number of EFMs to biologically relevant pathways involving nutrient source remodeling. This reduction in pathways resulted in AEFM enumeration times that were 103–104 times faster than enumerating the MEFMs of the corresponding networks (Figure 3B). While running FluxModeCalculator in parallel did improve run times by 8.2–8.6 times (Figure S4), it remained over 101–102 slower than enumerating the corresponding carbon and nitrogen AEFMs. One may think that expanding metabolites into metabolite-atom positions would increase the number of ACHMC states and therefore the computational run time. However, we found that the ACHMCs spanned less than 25% of the total number of metabolite-carbon or metabolite-nitrogen atom positions in any given network, with the average carbon and nitrogen ACHMC transiting 0.41% and 1.7% of the total number of metabolite-atom positions, respectively (Figure S5).

Another computational benefit of our AEFM pipeline is the ability to parallelize AEFM enumeration within and across ACHMCs. Unlike MEFM enumeration, which operates on the full metabolic network, ACHMCs can be constructed independently for each source metabolite atom. Plotting the serial run time as a function of the number of AEFMs, each point on Figure 3C corresponds to an individual carbon or nitrogen ACHMC belonging to one of the five GEMs. We observed two modes describing the run time complexity of the enumeration step of our pipeline. ACHMCs with fewer than 100 AEFMs exhibited serial run times within 0.1 milliseconds to 0.1 s. But for sufficiently large ACHMCs, we observed an approximately linear relationship between run time and the number of AEFMs in log-log space (slope = 1.85,intercept = −6.54). Assuming this relationship holds true for even larger GEMs, we predict ACHMCs with millions of AEFMs would take days to enumerate. However, our program is also parallelized on the level of individual ACHMCs. For the top 31 largest carbon ACHMCs in the HepG2 network, Figure 3D shows a median speedup of 7.9 times when using 16 or 32 threads.

Most AEFMs are source-to-sink pathways spanning dozens of reactions

Because there has been no previous characterization of AEFMs in genome-scale networks, we next turned to investigating the structural properties of all carbon and nitrogen AEFMs across the five GEMs. AEFMs can be divided into pathways that span source and sink metabolites or internal loops within the metabolic network such as pairs of reversible reactions. Both pathways have clear biological interpretations, with source-to-sink pathways explaining the steady state flow of source metabolites on their way to exiting the network and internally looped pathways corresponding to substrate remodeling/transport cycles. These pathways can be subclassified further into AEFMs that transit sets of distinct metabolites or those that revisit the same metabolite, albeit in different atomic positions. An example of source-to-sink AEFM with this property is a glucose-derived carbon AEFM remodeling through two turns of the TCA cycle; on the second turn, the position of the carbon atom shifts within the TCA intermediates, resulting in different isotopomers82 and therefore a source-to-sink pathway with a metabolite revisitation under our nomenclature.

To obtain a broad understanding of the types of AEFMs within the five GEMs, we categorized them into source-to-sink pathways and looped pathways with or without metabolite revisitations in different atomic positions. Figures 4A and 4B show the counts for each AEFM type within each GEM. We observed that the majority of carbon AEFMs were source-to-sink pathways. Those without metabolite revisitations tended to scale as a function of network size with a 100-fold increase in source-to-sink pathways from the iSB619 to HepG2 network, for example. Investigation of these source-to-sink pathways confirmed that many source to sink pathways with metabolite revisitations involved TCA cycle intermediates. In the E.coli core network, we observed that 36% of all source-to-sink carbon AEFMs with metabolite revisitations passed through at least one of the nine TCA cycle intermediates (i.e., citrate, cis-aconitate, …, oxaloacetate). This figure increased to 96% when considering the source-to-sink carbon AEFMs with metabolite revisitations for the HepG2 network. Conversely, only 3% of source-to-sink carbon AEFMs without metabolite revisitations involved any of the nine TCA intermediates, suggesting that source-to-sink carbon AEFMs with metabolite revisitations corresponded to pathways that shuttled flux into and out of the TCA cycle via a combination of anapleurotic and catapleurotic reactions. Looped AEFMs without metabolite revisitations primarily corresponded to transport reactions cycling metabolites across compartments (see Figure 5 for example) or acyltransferase reactions via acetyl-CoA and acyl carrier proteins. These observations explain, in part, why the iAB RBC 283 network contained the fewest pathways with metabolite revisitations. As demonstrated by the E. coli core and HepG2 network, the majority of source-to-sink pathways with metabolite revisitations comprise TCA cycle reactions that are absent from the iAB RBC 283 network. This explains the relatively small number of source-to-sink pathways with metabolite revisitations in that network. The lack of organelles, such as mitochondria, also eliminates cyclic AEFMs associated with inter-compartment metabolite transport or carbon transfers via acetyl-CoA and acyl carrier proteins. Our analysis of the nitrogen AEFMs showed that the majority of pathways did not involve any metabolite revisitations. Since there are many fewer nitrogen-containing metabolites in the five GEMs than carbon-containing ones, the majority of nitrogen AEFMs involved amino acid remodeling or transport between compartments. Looped pathways with metabolite revisitations were only observed in the iIT341 and iSB619 networks through reactions involving the production and consumption of ammonia.

We next investigated the lengths of the AEFMs, irrespective of their source-to-sink or looped type, and calculated the distribution of carbon and nitrogen AEFM lengths, which are shown in Figures 4C and 4D. We found that carbon AEFM lengths are bimodal in the first four GEMs, with some pathways that were extremely short in length (spanning 2–11 reactions) and others that were much larger. The carbon AEFM lengths of the HepG2 network appeared to exhibit a single Gaussian distribution (μ = 67.7, σ2 = 16.2), although closer inspection of the histogram revealed a small peak spanning 2–12 reactions in length. We hypothesized that looped AEFMs, such as those representing reversible reactions or inter-compartment transport, should involve fewer reactions than source-to-sink pathways, which can span numerous internal metabolites. To determine whether these peaks were associated with source-to-sink and looped AEFM classes, we reclassified the carbon AEFMs spanning less than 5–12 reactions depending on the GEM (shorter pathways) and the remaining carbon AEFMs (longer pathways). We found that longer pathways were associated with source-to-sink pathways, with shorter pathways containing the majority of looped pathways. In fact, 65%–97% of all longer pathways in each GEM were source-to-sink pathways with metabolite revisitations, with this statistic increasing to 95%–100% when including source-to-sink pathways without metabolite revisitations (i.e., between 0% and 5% of longer pathways were cyclic). We observed that shorter pathways were distributed more evenly between source-to-sink and looped AEFMs. Approximately 22% of all shorter pathways in the HepG2 network were looped with/without metabolite revisitations, although this figure increased to 58% in the iAB RBC 283 network and 68% in the iIT341 network. Analysis of the nitrogen AEFMs revealed only a single distribution of relatively short pathways due to the fewer number of nitrogen-containing metabolites across all GEMs.

We next considered how different atoms within the same source metabolite may follow different paths through the network, irrespective of their AEFM class. We first computed the number of AEFMs for each carbon atom (resp. nitrogen atom). For each metabolite, we then identified the atom with the minimum number of AEFMs. Finally, we computed for every (other) atom the number of its AEFMs divided by that minimum number. So for example, a ratio of 10 means that one of the carbon atoms in the metabolite can transit the network in 10 times as many different ways as the carbon with the fewest paths through the network. Our results are shown in Figures 4E and 4F, where each dot corresponds to the ratio for a given atom in a given source metabolite. We found that a surprising number of carbons and nitrogens can transit different pathways within their respective source metabolites. For instance, in the HepG2 network, the fifth (ϵ2) carbon of the phenylalanine ring participates in 111,045 AEFMs compared to the backbone carbon that only flows through five AEFMs. Conversely, the amino acid backbone carbon of histidine in the HepG2 network participates in 111,039 AEFMs, while the third (ϵ) carbon of the imidazole ring only transits 135 AEFMs. Focusing on the source metabolites with carbon and nitrogen ratios over 100, we observed many of these corresponded to hydrophobic and positively charged amino acids such as methionine and arginine in addition to phenylalanine and histidine. Source metabolites containing fewer carbons or nitrogens tended to exhibit ratios closer to one, with AEFMs involving the same metabolite subsets. Metabolites such as glycine, for example, were equivalently metabolized across the same carbon AEFMs. For each GEM, and not counting source metabolites with ratios of one, we computed median carbon ratios between 2.0 and 41.8 and nitrogen ratios between 1.3 and 11.2. Overall, these findings highlight the varying degrees of metabolic flexibility for the atomic constituents of source metabolites to transit the metabolic network.

A minority of carbon AEFMs explains the majority of source metabolite remodeling

In the previous sections, we showed how to enumerate carbon and nitrogen AEFMs in large-scale networks and analyze the structure of these pathways. Yet, an equally important focus of our work is to determine the amount of flux moving through AEFMs. This computational question is biologically motivated by the desire to identify the most important pathways among the thousands to millions of AEFMs in large-scale networks. Furthermore, AEFMs with the greatest weight, and therefore carrying the most atomic flux, may correspond to existing or novel metabolic pathways that are upregulated in metabolic disorders83 and environmental or bioprocessing conditions.84 In the context of the HepG2 network, which contains a single set of steady state fluxes estimated during the exponential growth phase,28 it is well known that cellular metabolism depends on the non-essential amino acid glutamine for survival and proliferation.85,86 Hence, an AEFM analysis of glutamine carbon atoms may reveal alternative paths of glutamine metabolism and quantify the amount of flux moving through these pathways.

Using our full AEFM pipeline, we decomposed the set of steady state fluxes onto the 448,629 glutamine-derived carbon AEFMs characterizing the HepG2 metabolic network. Figure 5A shows the cumulative explained mass flow of each glutamine carbon as a function of their AEFMs, ranked from greatest to smallest explained mass flow. Although our pipeline assigns some positive flux to every possible AEFM that a source metabolite atom may follow, we found that, depending on which carbon atom in glutamine, 99% of its flow could be explained by the top 0.5% of AEFMs or fewer. These results suggested that the majority of glutamine carbon remodeling is explained by a surprisingly small number of glutamine-derived carbon AEFMs. The top five ranked AEFMs across each glutamine carbon (25 pathways altogether) explained nearly half (43.9%) of the total glutamine carbon mass flow, with the subsequent five next highest ranked AEFMs only explaining an additional 8.1% glutamine carbon mass flow. These observations were consistent across carbon AEFMs derived from other amino acids, as well as glucose, leading us to hypothesize that steady state pathway fluxes are centralized to a relatively small subset of AEFMs (Figure S6).

To obtain a high-level understanding of the dominant pathways of glutamine carbon remodeling, we constructed a metabolic subnetwork from the top five glutamine carbon AEFMs, collapsing metabolite-atom positions onto distinct metabolites, since none of these AEFMs involved metabolite revisitations. Of the 435 metabolites in the HepG2 dataset, input glutamine can remodel into 203 species localized to the cytosol or mitochondria. We found that the top five glutamine-derived carbon AEFMs consisted of just 22 metabolites and 29 reactions (Figure 5B). These AEFMs generally consisted of cyclic pathways involving TCA intermediates. We found several source-to-sink AEFMs involving the production of carbon dioxide via the decarboxylation of isocitrate in the cytosol and alpha-ketoglutarate in the mitochondria.

As AEFMs are steady state pathways, they can be visualized as Sankey diagrams to better understand the atomic fluxes through the atomic flux graphs. Figures 5C–5E shows the top five AEFMs for each glutamine carbon. Each AEFM in the diagram is shown as a flow proportional to its AEFM weight, with nodes representing the metabolite transited by the given glutamine carbon. Although each glutamine carbon can transit a different number of AEFMs, we found that the top five backbone carbons for the first three sidechain carbons were identical in AEFM pathway with approximately the same weights.

Focusing on the first glutamine carbon in Figure 5C, we observed that AEFMs with the greatest weight corresponded to well-established metabolic subsystems. The first, third, and fourth AEFMs, for example, corresponded to aspects of the malate-aspartate shuttle. The fifth AEFM involved glutamine remodeling into alpha-ketoglutarate and entering the canonical TCA cycle and leaving as carbon dioxide via isocitrate. We also noticed that the second largest AEFM cycled carbon mass flow between TCA intermediates citrate, oxaloacetate, and malate located within the cytosol and mitochondria. Interestingly, this metabolic pathway corresponds to the recently discovered non-canonical TCA activity described by Arnold et al., who validated this pathway in non-small cell lung cancer, mouse embryonic, and C2C12 mouse myoblast cells by stable isotope tracing analysis.70 This non-canonical TCA activity was further observed in glutamine carbons 2–4 in the second AEFM (Figure 5D). We also found first, third, fourth, and fifth AEFMs in Figures 5D and 5E corresponded to the malate-aspartate shuttle. The terminal glutamine carbon in Figure 5E contained three source-to-sink AEFMs that either transported glutamine out of the system or decarboxylated it into carbon dioxide via alpha-ketoglutarate.

Thus far, we have shown that our analyses revealed that the most active glutamine-derived carbon AEFM weights corresponded to well-known metabolic subsystems. This finding led us to ask whether we could summarize our AEFM analysis as a simplified metabolic model describing over 40% of the glutamine carbon remodeling in the HepG2 flux network. We present the resulting schematic in Figure 5F, which consists of the following three major metabolic pathways: canonical TCA cycle, non-canonical TCA cycle, and the malate-aspartate shuttle. The overall schematic suggests that input glutamine is primarily routed into the mitochondria via aspartate/glutamate antiporters encoded by the SLC25A12 and SLC25A13 transporters annotated in the HepG2 metabolic model. This mitochondrial glutamate is used to regenerate TCA cycle intermediates by converting glutamate into alpha-ketoglutarate, which is subsequently consumed in the canonical TCA cycle to produce carbon dioxide and NADH/ATP/FADH2. More importantly, a greater proportion of glutamate appears to be recycled through the non-canonical TCA cycle, which does not generate NADH, but actively transports existing NADH from the cytosol into the mitochondrial. Taken together, the dominant carbon AEFMs predicted by our model suggest HepG2 glutamine both fuels TCA cycle activity and regulates cellular levels of NAD+/NADH.

Discussion

Since the proposal of MEFMs, much effort has been made to enumerate and decompose steady state metabolic fluxes onto these pathways. By defining EFMs with respect to individual atoms rather than molecules, we introduced the AEFM, which describes how atomic constituents are remodeled within a metabolic flux network. As AEFMs never involve branching pathways, they may be easier to interpret than their molecular EFM counterparts, which typically involve multiple inputs and outputs to balance molecular stoichiometries. Using the atom mapping program RXNMapper, we adapted our previously described CHMC method to efficiently enumerate carbon and nitrogen AEFMs in five GEMs and characterize source metabolite remodeling at the atomic level. We empirically observed the number of carbon and nitrogen AEFMs to be several orders of magnitude smaller than the MEFMs of the corresponding metabolic flux networks, which were too numerous to enumerate except for the smallest E. coli core and human red blood cell models. We further found that the majority of AEFMs encoded source-to-sink pathways, with carbon pathways often involving multiple revisitations of a given metabolite along different atomic positions. By comparing the number of AEFMs across atoms within the same source metabolite, we revealed how certain amino acids displayed remarkable variability in the number of pathways transited by their constituent atoms. This analysis, in particular, may be useful for designing non-uniformly labeled stable isotope tracers to identify fluxes within specific metabolic subsystems. Our final AEFM weight analysis of the HepG2 network revealed that the majority of glutamine carbons were remodeled via not only well-known metabolic pathways such as the canonical TCA cycle but also novel pathways such as non-canonical TCA activity that has not been previously described in HepG2 cells.

The problem of MEFM enumeration has historically limited their applicability to small-scale networks. Many algorithmic advances have improved MEFM enumeration, yet never feasibly beyond networks with >100 metabolites and reactions.40,81,87 Molecular EFM enumeration is generally regarded as infeasible for large-scale networks—so much so that numerous methods have been developed to enumerate a subset of MEFMs constrained by pathway length,88 reaction thermodynamics,89 participating metabolites,90 participating reactions,91 gene regulatory rules,92 metabolic subsystems,93,94 extracellular flux measurements,95,96 or even random sampling.97 While our HepG2 results did show the majority of source metabolite fluxes were explained by a small number of relatively short AEFMs, verifying which are the most dominant pathways is only possible when all are enumerated. Furthermore, reaction thermodynamics, gene regulatory rules, and other constraints used to identify these EFM subsets may change across different biological conditions. By computing AEFMs instead, we could feasibly enumerate these pathways in large-scale networks containing hundreds of metabolites and reactions within minutes to hours on consumer-grade computing hardware. We are hopeful that algorithmic tricks borrowed from MEFM enumeration tools, such as linear pathway compression, could further decrease memory usage and run times.

Aside from carbon and nitrogen AEFMs, our work is broadly applicable to other types of chemical elements. One could feasibly enumerate oxygen or hydrogen AEFMs in small-scale networks, although the significance of these pathways is unclear for biochemical networks, given the ubiquitous nature of these atoms. We also caution against enumerating hydrogen AEFMs, in particular, given limitations associated with RXNMapper. Unless explicitly represented in the reaction SMILES string, RXNMapper does not assign hydrogen atom mappings. Moreover, RXNMapper cannot map atoms in reaction SMILES strings longer than 512 characters; therefore, our pipeline inherits this limitation. Our method could equally be applied to study sulfur metabolism to quantify methionine or cysteine remodeling or phosphorus metabolism to quantify nucleotide synthesis. Outside the realm of biochemistry, our method is applicable to any periodic table element and possibly relevant for studying general chemical reaction networks.

Our AEFM framework opens many new computational problems in metabolism. Having computed thousands to millions of atomic pathways, new techniques are needed to identify dominant AEFMs and/or to provide other simplifying summary representations of the pathway weights. More sophisticated algorithms are also required to scale our ACHMC method to larger networks, such as improving parallel ACHMC construction or computing steady state ACHMC probabilities more efficiently using iterative solvers with preconditioner matrices. While we analyzed one set of fluxes for the HepG2 network, one could envision a differential AEFM analysis of flux distributions across distinct conditions98,99,100 or a sensitivity analysis of AEFM weights with respect to perturbations in a subset of metabolic reactions. These differential AEFMs may reveal changes in metabolic subsystem activity or predict new pathways of source metabolite remodeling. For instance, analyzing carbon AEFMs of C1 compounds may prove useful for identifying or designing carbon fixation pathways in C1-utilizing organisms.101 One might possibly correlate these AEFM weights with the Gibbs free energy of the pathways to better understand why some AEFMs carry more or less pathway flux than others. Another important question is whether a joint analysis of AEFM weights could reveal broader metabolic changes within a condition. We observed that many AEFMs across distinct source metabolites converged on common internal metabolites. Intuitively, the weights of AEFMs rooted on different source metabolites, but sharing common downstream metabolite subsequences, should be related, and these relationships may further speed AEFM enumeration or weight computations. Finally, more work is needed to establish relationships between AEFM decompositions and more traditional MEFM decompositions of fluxes and to explore the possibility that feasible AEFM calculations might somehow aid in MEFM calculations.

Limitations of the study

The biological interpretability of AEFMs depends heavily on our structural knowledge of the metabolic model, the quality of the atom-mapping predictions, and the accuracy of the steady state fluxes. Incomplete and erroneous metabolic models, due to undiscovered enzymes or transporters, or poor atom-mapping predictions will lead to erroneous and/or incomplete numbers of atomic pathways. Relatedly, our algorithm does not account for internal symmetry within product metabolites when constructing the ACHMC, as RXNMapper assigns unique indices to each metabolite atom. While accounting for symmetry may reduce the total number of unique metabolite-atom positions, it is conceivable that a substrate atom participating in an uni-bi reaction S→P + Q, for example, could transition to an atom in either P or Q. Accounting for this internal metabolite symmetry could therefore increase the total number of AEFMs involving a given source metabolite atom. The biological interpretability of AEFMs also depends on the quality of the steady state flux estimates. Metabolic fluxes remain computationally challenging to estimate or infer at the genome-scale level and are required to estimate AEFM weights. Flux distributions that are not thermodynamically consistent may lead to large weights along thermodynamically infeasible AEFMs. More generally, our pipeline does not incorporate thermodynamics data to assign AEFM weights. Thermodynamically infeasible cycles must be removed from the input GEM before running the ACHMC pipeline, since these cycles cannot be identified at the atomic transition graph level. We also recognize that some AEFMs may be thermodynamically unfavorable, once accounting for the overall Gibbs free energy of formation of the pathway and the physiological concentrations of steady state metabolites.

Method details

Step 1: Pipeline inputs

Step 1a: Datasets

Five curated GEMs were chosen in this study to reflect a diversity of organisms, metabolites and biochemical reactions. Four networks were obtained from the BiGG UCSD database106 in ascending order of network size. Datasets were downloaded as SBML (.xml) files, and the corresponding stoichiometric matrices, metabolites and reactions were extracted using the Julia SBML.jl package. The HepG2 liver cancer cell line dataset was chosen for its single set of steady state fluxes estimated from an FBA with external fluxes constrained by time-resolved exometabolomic measurements of glucose, pyruvate, lactate, and various amino acids. These cells were grown in DMEM with 10% fetal calf serum, 22mM glucose, and 1.8mM glutamine.28 The steady state fluxes were computed from a piecewise FBA model provided by the authors (figure3_metabolitebalances.m; MATLAB R2018a) (see Nilsson et al.28; data80). Across all models, we modeled all reversible reactions governed by single enzymes in either direction as net forward reactions to simplify the number of MEFMs and AEFMs because the majority of experimental and computational methods can only estimate net fluxes. We assumed the reaction stoichiometries of the BiGG datasets represented the canonical net directions of all reversible reactions. For the HepG2 dataset, the net directions were chosen based on the net fluxes estimated from the FBA model. The network visualisations in (Figure S3) were constructed in Gephi version 0.10105 using the Yifan-Hu proportional network layout with default parameters.

Step 1b: Extracting metabolites SMILES strings

SMILES structures were not encoded in the BiGG SBML files nor the HepG2 dataset. Thus, we manually extracted SMILES strings corresponding to each metabolite across all GEMs. Isomeric SMILES strings were taken from PubChem,102 the Human Metabolome Database,103 or MetaNetX database.104 Metabolites shared across GEMs were assigned the same SMILES structure in addition to identical metabolites stratified across distinct subcellular compartments. Generic structures such as R-groups were not modeled in this study and were replaced with a single bonded hydrogen under the assumption this modification would not alter atom mappings, especially since RXNMapper71 does not map hydrogen atoms unless they are explicitly represented in the SMILES strings. SMILES strings were also not assigned to metabolites with ambiguous structures nor pseudometabolites with no known chemical structure. All pseudometabolites and reactions involving pseudometabolites were removed from the stoichiometric matrix (Table 2).

Step 2: Model processing and validation

Step 2a: Constructing reaction SMILES strings

RXNMapper performs atom mapping on a string that encodes the SMILES structures of all substrates and products for a given reaction. These reaction SMILES strings are constructed by concatenating SMILES strings of individual substrates and products. Multiple substrates or products are separated by the symbol ‘.’, while the reaction arrow delimiting substrates and products is denoted by the symbol ‘≫’. The substrate and product SMILES strings are concatenated in order of metabolite appearance within the stoichiometric matrix, and multiple stoichiometric copies are consecutively repeated in the reaction SMILES strings. Non-integer stoichiometric coefficients are therefore not allowed and these pseudoreactions were removed from the stoichiometric matrix (see Table 2). To maintain steady state flux in the HepG2 network, fluxes along pseudoreactions were replaced with unimolecular source and sink fluxes consuming each pseudoreaction substrate and producing each pseudoreaction product.

Step 2b: Pre-processing genome-scale metabolic models

Several pre-processing steps were required to compute AEFMs from the GEMs. The stoichiometric coefficients of external reactions were set to one and their fluxes rescaled to carry equivalent units of source and sink metabolites. All reactions containing pseudometabolites with no known SMILES strings were removed from the stoichiometric matrix and replaced with unimolecular source and sink reactions carrying equivalent units of flux. The same procedure was also applied to reactions containing non-integer stoichiometries. These pseudometabolites were next removed from the stoichiometric matrix rows, and unimolecular reactions involving the same external or internal metabolites were aggregated. Finally, we had to remove a small number of reactions whose SMILES strings exceeded the 512 token limit of RXNMapper. These reactions were likewise converted into unimolecular source and sink reactions to maintain steady state flux across the network (see Table 2). All pre-processing steps described above are wrapped into convenience functions within our Julia package MarkovWeightedEFMs.jl.

Step 2c: Running RXNMapper to map atomic movements across reactions

After GEM pre-processing, RXNMapper was used to predict the atom mappings across reaction SMILES strings. The output of RXNMapper is a mapped reaction SMILES string with numeric indices indicating the atomic movements from substrate to product side of each reaction SMILES string. We manually checked all central carbon metabolism reactions to ensure the assigned atom mappings were correct. Crucially, we found RXNMapper failed on nearly all classes of ATP-independent transaminase reactions. This problem has previously been reported in an atom mapping benchmark study prior to the publication of RXNMapper.107 These transaminase reactions were identified in all metabolic networks and manually atom mapped based on the KEGG reaction mappings (Figure S7). Additionally, the reactions converting citrate to isocitrate (aconitate hydratase) and alpha-ketoglutarate to succinyl-CoA required manual curation.

We then stored these curated atom mapping predictions in a GEM and atom type-specific atom mapping dictionary. Each key corresponds to a substrate-atom uniquely defined by its (i) metabolite index within the stoichiometric matrix, (ii) ordinal occurrence of that atom type within the substrate SMILES string, (iii) substrate stoichiometric copy, and (iv) reaction index within the stoichiometric matrix. The values of this dictionary are the corresponding product-atom positions uniquely defined by the (i) metabolite index within the stoichiometric matrix and (ii) ordinal occurrence of that atom type in the product SMILES string.

Step 3: Atomic flux graph construction

From the atom-mapping dictionary, we then construct atomic flux graphs rooted on each source metabolite-atom position. Intuitively, each node in the graph corresponds to a specific atom in a metabolite, and each weighted edge from one node to another expresses the flux of the reaction causing that transformation. Starting with the source metabolite-atom position, we search the atom-mapping dictionary to determine the participating reactions and product metabolite-atom positions. For reactions involving a single stoichiometric copy of each substrate and product, the atom of interest will always transition from a unique substrate metabolite-atom position to product metabolite-atom position. If this atom is present in a reaction with multiple substrate stoichiometric copies, we assume there is an equal probability of that atom occurring in either substrate stoichiometric copy. Depending on the substrate stoichiometric position, the atom may (i) transition to different product metabolite-atom positions, (ii) the same product metabolite but at different atomic positions, or (iii) the same product metabolite-atom position. In all cases, we set the atomic flux of the atom transitioning from either stoichiometric copy equal to the reaction fluxes to maintain atomic mass conservation (Figure S1). The resulting atomic flux graph is completed by connecting any sink fluxes back to the root of the tree initialised on the source metabolite-atom position. This final procedure is solely for computational convenience and is mathematically identical to the open system with source and sinks.

Step 4: ACHMC construction

The atomic flux graphs described in the previous step satisfy all conditions required to construct the corresponding ACHMC. Specifically, these conditions are that the input flux network is a closed-loop graph that is strongly connected with conserved edge weights such that the sum of weights entering and exiting each node are balanced.69 However, for computational efficiency, our software implementation does not actually construct the atomic flux graph explicitly. Rather, it runs an ACHMC analysis directly from the metabolic flux network and the atom mapping dictionary. Starting at an initial metabolite-atom position, we apply a depth-first search algorithm (Algorithm 1) to recursively enumerate sequences of transited metabolite-atom positions (also known as prefixes) stemming from the initial source metabolite atom. Following Chitpin and Perkins, we denote state S in the ACHMC to represent the simple path of (unique) metabolite-atom transitions s0,s1, …,sm. These metabolite-atom sequences form a trie-like data structure,108 where each subsequent state sm+1 corresponds to a longer simple path of metabolite-atom positions. Unlike a standard trie, however, the ACHMC allows for connections between longer simple paths and their shorter (parent) simple paths if there exists a reaction that can transform an atom along one simple path to a previously-visited metabolite-atom position. Linear AEFMs (simple paths) are enumerated by finding all states S (sequences of metabolite-atom positions) that can no longer transition to a longer simple path (leaves within this trie). Internally-looped AEFMs (simple cycles) are enumerated by finding all simple paths that are capable of transitioning to a shorter simple path within the trie. Our algorithm is parallelised by first traversing an arbitrary series of metabolite-atom positions depth-first until that atom can no longer transition to a new metabolite-atom position. Step 5: Output estimated AEFM weights

To compute AEFM weights, we encode the ACHMC trie into a sparse transition probability matrix T. Each element TS,S′ corresponds to the probability of an atom in state S≡s0,s1, …,sm (representing a sequence of metabolite-atom positions that the atom has transited) transitioning to either state S′≡s0,s1, …,sm,sm+1 (representing a one-step longer sequence) or state S′≡s0,s1, …,si if sm+1 is among s0,s1, …,sm (representing a return to a previous metabolite-atom position). The transition probabilities are set proportional to the outgoing fluxes in the trie such that ∑S′∉STS,S′=1.

The AEFM probabilities are computed by steady state analysis of the ACHMC transition probability matrix.69 For AEFM k, we identify all transitions in the ACHMC that close the simple cycle Ek. For linear AEFMs, Ek always corresponds to one transition; however, internally looped AEFMs may contain multiple transitions within Ek. The steady state probability of transiting AEFM k is defined as the sum of steady state probabilities of those transitions or:(Equation 1)Pk=∑(S,S′)∈EkπS·TS,S′

Each node in the ACHMC trie corresponds to a whole sequence of metabolite-atom positions that an atom might pass through. The children of a node correspond to either: a one-step-longer path or a loop back to a previously-visited metabolite-atom position. The probabilities of those transitions are proportional to the fluxes of the corresponding reactions. Some nodes correspond to entire AEFMs—specifically those that are paths from the initial metabolite-atom position to any sink within the network. Treating each node of the trie as a state in a Markov chain, we compute steady state probabilities of every node. The flux weight of any given AEFM is proportional to the steady state probability of the node representing that full AEFM path. See Chitpin and Perkins for additional details.

Benchmarking the enumeration of atomic-versus-standard EFMs

We benchmarked our Julia package MarkovWeightedEFMs.jl version 2.0 running Julia version 1.10.2 to enumerate AEFMs. We ran the MEFM enumeration program FluxModeCalculator81 running MATLAB version R2018a with default parameters, including network compression. We chose FluxModeCalculator because it is the state-of-the-art program for enumerating MEFMs. Benchmarks were performed on a 64-bit Linux operating system with 64GB of memory and an AMD 5950X CPU with 16 cores/32 threads. All benchmarks reported the wall time to run the AEFM or MEFM enumeration functions and did not include startup times to open Julia/MATLAB, precompile Julia functions, or load packages and input data. A benchmark time of ‘did not finish’ (DNF) was assigned if either program could not complete (M/A)EFM enumeration within 7 days.

Quantification and statistical analysis

This paper develops a Markov model to analyze steady state fluxes in genome-scale metabolic networks. All computational analyses and model outputs are described in the sections above. No additional statistical procedures or tests were used.
