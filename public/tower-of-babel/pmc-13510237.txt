Engaging Medical Students in Curriculum Design: Evaluating a Model for Active Participation and Co-Creation of an Elective Course Curriculum
Authors: Ayat Nabil Eltayar, Marwa Mahmoud Mady
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEngaging medical students in curriculum design provides numerous advantages, fostering a more effective, student-centered learning experience. As the primary “consumers” of the curriculum, students have a unique perspective on its strengths and areas for improvement. The integration of elective courses into medical curricula yields many advantages for medical students. This study aimed at evaluating learners’ involvement in the curriculum design process of an elective course by exploring their perceptions.MethodsA total of 277 final-year medical students, in a single institution, were enrolled in the elective course. They learned how to apply the six-step approach to curriculum design. Students identified their own learning needs and selected educational materials aligned with their learning goals. By the end of the course, they were required to write a reflective essay evaluating their experience. A deductive content analysis approach was employed while being guided by the 3 core psychological needs of self-determination theory.ResultsAnalyzing the reflective writing, we identified 33 codes that were grouped under 11 subthemes and 6 themes. The emerging themes included learning control, decision-making, skill development, confidence building, social connection, and community integration.ConclusionLearners’ participation in curriculum design could positively influence their perception of learning autonomy, academic and professional competence, and connection to the learning community. We recommend that medical education programs incorporate structured learner participation in curriculum design to enhance autonomy, competence, and relatedness.Keywords: co-creation, curriculum design, self-determination theory, autonomy, competence, relatedness

Introduction

Electives have become an integral part of medical education worldwide. This offers students a valuable opportunity to tailor their learning experiences and explore areas of interest beyond the core curriculum.1,2 The integration of elective courses into medical curricula yields many advantages for medical students. They play a crucial role in their professional development.3–5 Electives provide an outstanding opportunity for personalized learning experiences. Learners can explore areas of specific interest compensating the limitations of the core curriculum. This could provide a deeper understanding and possibly influence future career choices.3,4 As highlighted in recent studies, electives are widely perceived by students as a highly regarded component of their medical training.1,2,6 When electives line up with students’ concerns and expectations regarding teaching methodologies, assessment approaches, and workload, they can promote a positive perception towards the learning environment. Consequently, this can improve learners’ satisfaction with their medical education.4,5 In our institution, we have adopted the inclusion of elective courses within the undergraduate programs since 2013. In 2018, the undergraduate program was shifted to competency-based with adding more weight to the implementation of elective courses.

The concept of “student as a teacher” has emerged from the recognition that teaching enhances learning. Teaching helps students develop metacognitive and organizational skills. Formal training in teaching skills is often lacking in traditional medical curricula.7 Active involvement of medical students in the curriculum design offers plentiful benefits, leading to a more effective student-centered learning experience. Students, as the direct “consumer” of the curriculum, keep a distinctive vision into its strengths and weaknesses. Their firsthand experience enables them to identify areas that are not “recipient-friendly” and suggest improvements to better fit learners’ needs.8,9 Moreover, engaging students in curriculum design promotes their skills. Designing students’ own curriculum necessitates active learning. This encourages deeper comprehension and long retention of the subject matter.8,9

Despite these benefits, critiques of student participation in curriculum design must be acknowledged. Concerns include tokenism (superficial involvement with faculty retaining decision-making authority), scalability challenges in resource-constrained large-scale programs, increased faculty workload, threats to curriculum coherence and accreditation standards, and variability in student readiness for design responsibilities.10,11 These critiques do not negate the value of student participation but underscore the need for empirical evaluation of its benefits. Furthermore, successful implementation requires evidence-based models for curriculum designers, support structures for faculty, feasibility considerations for administrators, and assurance for accreditation bodies that competency standards are met. The present study is positioned to contribute evidence relevant to each of these stakeholder groups.

Learners’ participation in curriculum design was modeled in a ladder. The “Ladder of Student Participation in Curriculum Design” model is an adaptation of Sherry Arnstein’s Ladder of Citizen Participation, specifically modified to address active student participation (ASP) in curriculum development. This model shows various levels and forms of student engagement that may be both feasible and beneficial within higher education contexts. The levels of the ladder, from lowest to highest, are dictated curriculum, participation claimed but tutor in control, limited choice from prescribed choices, wide choice from prescribed choices, student control of prescribed areas, student control of some areas of choice, partnership – a negotiated curriculum, students in control.12

The previously described model facilitates discussion of the desirability and possibility of ASP in curriculum design and is intended to challenge practice and stimulate discussion to deepen the understanding of co-created curricula. The authors of the source do not suggest that “higher is always better”. Lower levels of student participation may be viewed as more desirable in some contexts.12 In our institution, students do not have a role in the early steps of curriculum design. However, they participate in the curriculum evaluation process as a feedback process for curriculum improvement. This raised the need to give the learners an opportunity to participate in curriculum design through a new learning experience and explore their perception. We address this gap by evaluating the impact of involving final-year medical students in co-designing an elective. This research aims at answering the following question:

How do learners perceive the course design impact on their autonomy, competence, and relatedness?

Is there a significant change in knowledge by the end of the course?

Theoretical Background

Self-determination theory (SDT) guided the design of this course which proposes that intrinsic motivation is sustained when three basic psychological needs—autonomy, competence, and relatedness—The theory explains motivation of learners by supporting autonomy of learners through a flexible learning plan and giving them the chance to choose what to learn. Additionally, enforcing relatedness through building an involving learning environment where learners and teachers interact, reflect, deliver and receive feedback. Moreover, improving competence through filling their gaps of knowledge.13 The adopted epistemology of this work is interpretivism. Knowledge is generated by interpreting students’ reflections to understand their learning experience and intrinsic motivation. Ontology is the critical realism as autonomy, competence and relatedness are real, but their fulfillment depends on subjective individual perceptions. The axiology is related to the perceived values of self-worth, ability to choose, mastery and belonging.

Aim of the Study

Evaluation of the course design through exploring the learners’ perceptions of autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Additionally, evaluating the academic achievement of learners by the end of the course.

Methodology

Study Design and Setting

A prospective-exploratory study was conducted among the final year (fifth year) medical students at Alexandria faculty of medicine.

Sampling

A single-stage cluster random sampling method was employed for this study. Out of fourteen total batches of fifth-year medical students enrolled in the course, six batches were randomly selected (Batches 1, 2, 3, 7, 12, and 13). A minimum required sample size was estimated using the average effect size reported for educational interventions in the literature (d = 0.40, Hattie, 2009), with α = 0.05 and power = 80%, yielding a target of approximately 50–55 participants for the planned Wilcoxon signed-rank analysis. Given an average cohort size of ~30 students per batch (423 students across 14 batches), this target could technically be met with as few as two batches; six batches were selected to substantially exceed this minimum, providing a safety margin for anticipated attrition and incomplete submissions. Random number generator software was used to randomly select the batches enrolled in the study. This number of batches was chosen to balance methodological rigor with feasibility, given the intensive facilitation required for the co-creation model while also enabling representative coverage across the academic year. The selected sample (n = 277, 65.5% of the eligible cohort) was representative of the broader population, with no significant demographic differences between selected and non-selected batches (p > 0.05). Our sample confirmed >80% power to detect small to moderate observed effect, and the sample size exceeded the minimum required for thematic saturation in qualitative analysis. Random selection minimized selection bias and enhanced the generalizability of findings. Every student within these selected clusters was invited to participate in the study.

Participants

In our undergraduate program, four elective slots are available across years 3, 4, and 5. The main student cohort is divided into subgroups and placed in a rotational system, whereby each batch is assigned to a specific slot. For example, in the fifth year—where our course is offered—students are divided into 14 subgroups. As groups rotate through the required rotations, by the end of the academic year, all 14 subgroups have enrolled in all required courses. One of the slots that learners rotate through is “Elective 4.” Learners in this slot have the opportunity to choose from a pool of 54 elective courses. They log in to the educational platform and select the course they are interested in.

A total of 423 fifth-year medical students, distributed across 14 batches, enrolled in the course during the 2024/2025 academic year. Within the six randomly selected clusters, 279 students were initially enrolled. Following the withdrawal of two students, the final study sample comprised 277 participants (representing 65.5% of the total eligible cohort). These included individuals who completed all course quizzes and submitted their reflective papers.

The Educational Intervention “the Course Design”

The course was designed at the highest level of ladder of students’ participation in curriculum design “students in control”. The course was delivered online over two weeks and was available to final-year undergraduate medical students during the academic year 2024/2025. The learning objectives, the learning schedule and the educational plan of the course were developed (Figure 1).

Flow diagram of the study.

The scientific content of the course was delivered through “Incision Academy platform” (Amsterdam, The Netherlands, 2014), a medical educational platform offering hundreds of mini courses in video format with assessment. The scientific content on Incision Academy is accredited by the Royal College of Surgeons, UK. The Egyptian Knowledge Bank (EKB) provides free access to both national and international students studying in Egypt. The scientific materials on Incision Academy were reviewed and approved by the curriculum committee at Alexandria Faculty of Medicine, and the courses were organized to ensure easy access for learners.

The students who enrolled in the course were directed to a WhatsApp group with the course instructor and technical support staff. Three synchronous sessions, along with asynchronous interactions, were available over two weeks. Each synchronous session was about two hours. The instructor was a facilitator, guide on the side, available for supervising and supporting the learners while designing, implementing and evaluating the curriculum. The first session introduced the course and outlined the learning journey. This was followed by a technical support session to help students create accounts on the Incision Academy platform, enabling them to access the scientific materials. During the third synchronous session, learners worked in groups to practice the steps of curriculum design before working individually to design their own curricula. They received immediate feedback on their group work. In the group work, students implemented Kern’s six-step approach to curriculum design.14 The steps were as follows (Figure 2):

Data Collection Methods and Tools

Reflective writing of learners was qualitatively analyzed and then codes were quantified. All reflective essays were written in English, the language of instruction at the institution; therefore, translation was not required before analysis. The following reflective writing prompts were used:

○ “Reflect on satisfaction and frustration aspects in this course?”

○ “Reflect on your learning experience in this course and its impact on your learning abilities?”

● Pretest and posttest scores: the pretest was implemented by the end of step 3. Each learner was asked to take a pretest in the form of 20 MCQs which were aligned with the developed learning objectives. To ensure content validity and systematic test construction, we developed a test blueprint for both the pre-test and post-test. Unlike traditional blueprints that map to fixed content, our blueprint was constructed based on a thematic analysis of the learning objectives formulated by the students during Step 3 of Kern’s framework. This flexible blueprint ensured that the assessment remained standardized across the cohort while honoring the individualized nature of the students’ chosen curricula.

● The pre-test was used to evaluate the knowledge base of learners before the implementation. The posttest was employed at the end of the course in the form of 20 MCQs. The mean difference in pretest and posttest scores was used to evaluate the change in knowledge at the end of the course. Data Analysis

A deductive content analysis approach was adopted, for the qualitative data analysis, while being guided by the three core psychological needs of SDT as a predefined thematic framework. Descriptive statistics (number, percentage, range, mean and standard deviation) and inferential statistics (Wilcoxon signed-rank test), calculated by SPSS version 29 (IBM Corp., 2023), were used to describe the quantitative data.

Steps for qualitative data analysis:

1. Familiarization: participants’ reflective writings were read multiple times to immerse in data.

2. Initial Coding: initial codes that align with SDT’s constructs were developed:

○ Construct 1: Autonomy: Indicators of independence, ability to choose, or control over learning.

○ Construct 2: Competence: Descriptions of mastery or self-efficacy.

○ Construct 3: Relatedness: Statements reflecting social support or a sense of belonging.ATLAS.ti (ATLAS.ti Scientific Software Development GmbH, 2026), a qualitative data analysis software, facilitated the systematic organization and analysis of the codes.

3. Theme Identification: Similar codes were grouped under broader themes.

4. Interpretation: analysis and explanation of the themes emerged.

5. Iterative Refinement: cycles of revisiting and refining themes to ensure they align with both the theoretical framework (SDT) and the data.

6. Quantification of themes: percentage of each theme was calculated.

7. Mapping Data to SDT Framework: findings were directly mapped to the SDT framework showing:

○ Evidence that learners demonstrated choice, self-initiation, and ownership of their learning.

○ Evidence that learners were competent in meeting their learning goals and mastering content.

○ Evidence that learners felt supported, included and connected.

○ Identifying barriers that prevented learners from fulfilling these needs and goals.

Results

Two hundred and seventy-seven fifth-year students (277) completed the course. One hundred and seventy-seven students (63.89%) were males, and 100 students (36.10%) were females. Regarding the nationality, 169 (61.01%) Egyptian students and 108 (38.99%) international students were included. The age ranged from 21 to 29 years (mean= 24.74; SD= 1.64). Analyzing the reflective writing, the 33 codes were grouped under 11 subthemes. Then, subthemes were grouped into 6 themes. Themes were aligned with the three concepts of the SDT (Table 1).

Autonomy

Learning Control

Control Over Learning

The flexibility in scheduling was highly appreciated by learners, as it enabled them to manage their studies in a way that aligned with their personal and professional commitments. Self-paced learning further empowered them to take control of their educational journey, ensuring they could progress at a tailored rate that suited their personal needs and circumstances. For instance, a 22-year-old male student shared her thoughts on controlling their learning:

The course allowed me to design my own learning plan taking into consideration my social life. I was able to compensate for any missing in my learning on my own pace.

Content Choice

Learners valued the opportunity to select topics that aligned with their interests and learning goals. Having access to a diverse and extensive pool of resources enabled them to tailor their learning experience according to their individual needs. This flexibility allowed them to engage with materials that were most relevant and beneficial to their personal and professional development, ultimately enhancing their motivation and overall learning outcomes. A 21-year-old female student mentioned that:

It was amazing to choose what to learn. I examined all the available modules and chose what I was interested in.

Learning Strategy

Learners highlighted the development of a well-structured learning path that allowed them to navigate their educational journey effectively. They emphasized the need for method selection, where they could choose instructional methods that best suited their learning preferences. Additionally, they expressed the significance of a flexible learning approach, enabling them to engage with content at their own pace and depth. Furthermore, they appreciated having autonomy in tool choice, selecting resources and platforms that enhanced their understanding and engagement with the material. For example, a 23-year-old female revealed that:

I enjoyed drawing my own journey. I prefer to watch videos to learn so this suited me very much. Giving me a chance to find other references helped me properly manage my understanding. I was my own teacher.

Decision-Making

Learning-Decisions

Learners recognized the opportunity to establish their own learning goals, allowing them to take control of their educational journey. They also acknowledged the importance of prioritizing their individual needs and interests, ensuring that their learning experience aligned with their personal and academic aspirations. This sense of autonomy contributed to a more engaging and meaningful learning process. A 21-year-old male said:

In this course, I learnt to set my learning goals and specify learning objectives. Deciding my learning goals helped me arrange my priorities for what I need to learn and what I want to learn. What I need to learn in depth and what I need just to know about as a near future doctor.

Competence

Skill Development

Learning Progress

Learners emphasized the significance of their educational experience in fostering knowledge gain, enabling them to acquire new information and deepen their expertise. They also highlighted skill improvement as a key outcome, as they were able to develop practical abilities that enhanced their academic and professional competencies. Additionally, they recognized their understanding growth, as the learning process allowed them to refine their critical thinking and comprehension, leading to a more profound grasp of complex concepts. A 23-year-old female student mentioned:

It was my first time to learn how to control my learning. One of the topics I chose to learn was advances in surgical repair of the inguinal hernia. I learnt how to check my progress. This course added to my skills as a self-learner.

Challenge Management

Learners emphasized the impact of the course on developing challenge management strategies. They highlighted obstacle handling as a key strategy they acquired, allowing them to identify and address challenges related to learning objectives that they initially struggled with. They also expressed that they had learned to adapt to various strategies, enabling them to modify their approaches based on the complexity of tasks and learning conditions. Additionally, they recognized problem-solving as a crucial skill they had developed, allowing them to analyze difficulties, devise effective solutions, and progress in their educational journey with greater confidence. A 24-year-old male leaner mentioned:

During this course, I learnt how to be self-learner and problem-solver. These skills will help me after graduation to continue my learning. I learnt to adopt various methods to learn when it is difficult to achieve my goals.

Confidence Building

Self-Efficacy

Learners stated that the course enhanced their capability beliefs, strengthening their confidence in their ability to successfully complete tasks and achieve learning goals when given control over their learning process. This, in turn, led to confidence growth, as they became more assured of their ability to work independently toward their objectives. Additionally, they reported that their success experience in achieving initial learning goals served as positive reinforcement, motivating them to continue their educational journey. Furthermore, the educational strategy of allowing them to choose what they preferred to learn stimulated their internal motivation and curiosity, fostering a deeper engagement with the learning process. A 20-year-old male student said:

During this course, I built self-confidence that I can achieve while working without direct supervision. It helped me trust my abilities to achieve my set goals.

Relatedness

Social Connection

Interaction

Some learners highlighted collaboration as a positive aspect of the course. However, each learner primarily worked individually to achieve their own goals. Collaboration took place within the course’s WhatsApp group, where learners sought assistance, supported one another by suggesting strategies or additional resources, and even provided technical support when needed. Moreover, group learning, which learners emphasized as beneficial, was achieved during synchronous sessions, where they worked in teams to critique content and collaboratively create learning goals and objectives. A 23-year-old male mentioned that:

I liked the group activities of writing goals and objectives during the training with our doctor. My colleagues and I worked together to achieve the best written goals. This helped me a lot when I wrote my own goals later on.

Support

Many learners reported the positive impact of peer support on their learning, highlighting that their peers provided technical, academic, and study-skills assistance. They also appreciated the constructive feedback they received on their created goals, as well as the feedback provided by the Incision Academy platform on quiz performance. Some learners emphasized that timely feedback helped them correct mistakes immediately, enhancing their learning experience. Additionally, the educational mentorship provided by the course instructor, along with the technical support from the Incision Academy representative, made learners feel secure and supported. Finally, receiving achievement certificates immediately served as a source of external motivation, encouraging learners to continue progressing through the upcoming modules on the platform. A 20-year-old female learner and a 23-year-old male learner, respectively, reported that:

My peers continued to be there when I asked any question on the group. They supported me a lot.

The teacher was there when I needed her support. When I faced a technical problem logging into the website, she offered me the utmost assistance. When I struggled with writing my goals, she helped me step by step to achieve the task successfully.

Community Integration

Belonging

Many learners highlighted their feeling of belonging through being allowed and safe to ask questions and receive answers. Additionally, learners liked being engaged, valued and respected regardless of their differences. A 20-year-old female student revealed that:

I enjoyed being able to post my questions and vote for schedule of meetings rather than receiving solid instructions that I need to obey. Everyone has the right to share. It was like a family group. I felt a member of a family during the course.

A 22-year-old male Egyptian student said:

I felt respected when I excused the teacher to leave the meeting to do the noon prayers as it was time. The teacher gave us a 15-minute break to allow all those who want to pray in time to do it.

A 23-year-old male international student mentioned that:

However, I was stressed to work in the same group with the national students. It was a nice experience. They gave me a space to express my thoughts and welcomed me. They promised to teach me Arabic at the weekends:). I gained new friends.

Isolation

A learner reported experiencing a lack of belonging, expressing frustration with working remotely. Additionally, this sense of isolation was intensified by feelings of being overwhelmed while working under indirect supervision. The 20-year-old female international student reported that:

I didn’t enjoy controlling my studies. It is easier for me to have a teacher to explain the lectures and specify what to learn. I felt stressed as I must be my own teacher. I didn’t feel connected to the group. My peers liked the experience so much, unlike me. I was embarrassed to say that as others were sharing positive feedback. I considered reaching out to the instructor, as she had encouraged us to ask questions and seek assistance, but I hesitated because I was afraid of looking silly.

Discussion

The current study sought to examine the perspectives of final-year medical students at Alexandria University who were enrolled in the “Self-Directed Learning: students as curriculum designers” elective course, focusing on their learning experiences as curriculum designers, through the lens of Self-Determination Theory (SDT).

Because the elective was deliberately designed at high rung of the ladder of student participation – with learners themselves identifying their learning needs, setting objectives, selecting educational materials, and choosing their assessment method across all six steps of Kern’s framework – the findings speak directly to the effectiveness of this co-creation model rather than to a conventionally delivered elective. The emergence of six themes mapping onto the three SDT constructs indicates that granting learners control over each step translated into the intended experiential effects: perceived autonomy from self-selected goals and materials, perceived competence from successfully completing a self-authored learning plan, and perceived relatedness from the collaborative design process itself. The significant pretest-to-posttest gain further suggests that these self-authored curricula were not merely satisfying but educationally effective, enabling students to achieve objectives they had set for themselves. Taken together, the qualitative and quantitative findings function as converging evidence that active, learner-led curriculum design, and not simply learner choice within an already-fixed curriculum, can produce measurable psychological and academic benefit, thereby supporting the co-creation model evaluated in this paper.

The qualitative analysis of students’ written reflections revealed that the majority expressed satisfaction with their experience as curriculum designers. The study findings revealed six themes: learning control, decision making, skill development, confidence building, social connection and community integration. These themes were aligned with autonomy, competence and relatedness (constructs of SDT). Similarly, Pereira et al (2020)15 highlighted that student participation in curriculum development enhances student-centered learning and optimizes engagement. Correspondingly, Boyle et al (2024)16 emphasized the benefits of student participation in curriculum design. Boyle et al (2024)16 reported that Students involved in the curriculum review expressed satisfaction. By actively engaging in curriculum design, students become more integrated into the academic community, fostering a sense of belonging and shared responsibility.16 Moreover, Nichol et al (2023)17 reported that co-designing modules could make learning more enjoyable and increase learners’ motivation to learn and attend classes. Some learners felt they would be more invested in the course content because they had a hand in choosing it, leading to a greater likelihood of engagement.17

Regarding autonomy, learners expressed their interest in having control over their learning. They explained that they enjoyed the ability to take decisions about their learning, deciding how, when, what and why to learn. This aligns with the principles of Self-Determination Theory, which posits that autonomy is a fundamental psychological need that enhances engagement and performance.18 Moreover, this is aligned with the findings of Kusurkar et al (2013)19 declaring that medical students who perceived greater autonomy in their learning developed higher levels of intrinsic motivation that helped them developed better study strategies and exert more effort. Similarly, Liu et al (2024)20 reported that learning autonomy is a fundamental element of student-centered education, allowing learners to assume responsibility for their educational journey, set individualized goals, and employ effective learning strategies. This autonomy not only improves academic outcomes but also fosters personal fulfillment. Within medical education, autonomy holds particular significance, as it equips students to manage the vast and complex body of medical knowledge while adapting to the dynamic demands of clinical practice. Additionally, Neufeld A (2021)21 stated that promoting learner autonomy, especially by offering genuine opportunities for choice in the learning process and cultivating reflective skills, is crucial for medical students.

As regards competence, learners indicated that their participation in the course contributed to significant skill development. They reported enhancements in study skills, the ability to manage challenges effectively, and the acquisition of both academic and professional competencies, which collectively fostered increased self-confidence. Furthermore, all students who successfully completed the course demonstrated evidence of academic competence, as they were able to achieve their predefined goals. Similarly, Lubicz-Nawrocka (2018)22 reported the impact of co-creation of curriculum on improving learners’ competence. It was explained that co-creation of curriculum enhanced the learners’ development of cognitive skills through active engagement with the curriculum, improving their ability to make meaning of knowledge. Additionally, findings were aligned with our findings regarding learning to challenge authority and gaining confidence in their own abilities.22 Correspondingly, Stoddard et al (2024)23 concluded that curriculum co-creation has a profound impact on learners’ competence by fostering active engagement, enhancing learning outcomes and developing critical skills. These effects contribute to a more competent and prepared group of future professionals.23 Respectively, Campbell and Rogers (2024)24 showed that dental students involved in co-creation of the curriculum reported improved learning experiences and developed skills that are not addressed in the traditional curricula. It was explained that the inclusive curriculum improved the engagement that develops both personal and professional skills.24

However, not all studies report uniformly positive outcomes of student co-creation. Critical perspectives highlight that student participation can become performative or tokenistic, especially when institutional power structures remain unchanged. Bovill (2014)25 cautioned that “partnership” rhetoric often masks superficial involvement, where students are invited to contribute but their input does not substantively alter curricula. In our study, while students perceived high autonomy, the fundamental boundaries (course duration and platform content) were still set by faculty aligning more with the term “consultative” rather than “collaborative” partnership.26 We acknowledge that our course offered wide choice within prescribed structures “supervised student control”.

Concerning relatedness, students developed the sense of belonging to the community of learning. Most of them enjoyed the interaction with peers and the support provided by peers and the instructor. This can be explained by the Maslow hierarchy27 of needs that showed the perceived love and belonging as one of the basic needs to develop self-actualization. These findings are aligned with the work of Schmied et al (2024)28 who declared that students’ participation in curriculum design created a more inclusive environment where learners perceived better engagement in the learning environment.28 Similarly, Ravi et al (2024)29 co-creation partnerships with marginalized student groups. By focusing on the experiences of international students, they concluded that co-creation, when implemented with a focus on safe participation and empowerment, can significantly enhance the educational experiences of international students, developing a sense of belonging and a community of learning within the institution.29

However, the majority of learners expressed a positive perception of relatedness and engagement. An international learner reported feeling isolated in the course and preferred being a passive learner rather than taking responsibility for her own learning. This could be attributed to a lack of experience with active learning. Additionally, cultural differences may have created a psychological barrier that limited her interaction. More efforts and an individualized approach, such as more orientation to active learning and excessive mentoring tailored to those vulnerable to struggle in the active learning environments, could be needed. Similarly, Carey (2013)30 found that some students experienced anxiety and role confusion when asked to co-design curricula, feeling ill-equipped for the responsibility. Likewise, Matthews et al (2018)31 noted that international or first-generation students may face additional barriers to engagement due to unfamiliarity with local academic norms. Additionally, the embarrassment reported by this learner may be attributed to her perception of power dynamics between herself and the teacher. Boyle et al16 highlighted that imbalances in power between teachers and learners can create barriers in activities and discourse. Lubicz-Nawrocka22 suggested that co-creation of the curriculum could challenge the established power dynamics as well as institutional structures and processes in the higher-education sector. Our experience suggests that co-creation, while beneficial for the majority, can inadvertently marginalize learners who require more structured guidance.

Regarding academic performance, engaging learners in this design process can enhance their academic achievement by fostering a supportive environment and promoting active and collaborative learning.32 This is aligned with our findings regarding the improvement in the posttest scores as compared to the pretest scores (Z = −4.82, p = 0.01). Burk-Rafel et al33 revealed that engaging learners in curriculum design enhances academic achievement by incorporating their unique perspectives, fostering ownership, and driving educational innovation. This involvement leads to more relevant and effective learning experiences, ultimately improving outcomes for medical students. Moreover, student engagement in curriculum development fosters authentic partnerships between students and faculty, leading to enhanced academic achievement. This collaboration creates a culture of mutual benefit, encouraging greater participation and reinforcing positive outcomes in learning environments.34 Additionally, the engagement of medical students in curriculum design positively can impact their academic achievement by increasing awareness of program initiatives and fostering interest in academic medicine,35 this may explain why the learner who reported stress was still able to pass the course. Moreover, resilience enables medical students to manage stressors effectively, such as high academic demands and emotional challenges. Therefore, engaging students and supporting their need for control over learning must work side by side to achieve an educational environment that is supportive, engaging, and academically empowering.33–35

Limitations

Given the study’s focus on medical students in a single institute, the findings transferability to students from different cultural contexts needs testing. While the cultural similarities between the investigating instructor and the participants may have facilitated open communication, it also posed the challenge of potential bias in interpreting their reflective writing, as the researchers might have unintentionally projected own cultural perspectives onto their experiences. This limitation was mitigated by the involvement of a second researcher who did not serve as a course instructor. Furthermore, although social desirability bias may have influenced student reflections, anonymizing the reflections prior to analysis likely reduced the impact of such bias.

Conclusion and Future Recommendations

Curriculum co-creation was positively perceived by learners. The key benefits of their participation include learning control, decision-making, skill development, confidence building, social connection, and community integration. Future implementations should therefore include tailored support and opportunities to enroll into or out of high-autonomy designs.

Rigor and Trustworthiness

Dependability

The extracted codes and themes were checked by colleagues (not involved in the research).

Transferability

The research steps were described in detail so that they can be repeated in other contexts. Additionally, data were collected from multiple sources: males, females, wide age group, national and international students. Moreover, data were aligned with SDT.

Credibility

Through member checking, returning the results to participants to assess whether the findings reflected their opinions achieved credibility of data. Member checking was conducted in August 2025, after all course batches had finished and final grades had been submitted, to reduce any perceived coercion. Participants were asked to complete a brief online form indicating: whether the themes accurately captured their experience; whether any important aspects were missing; whether the interpretation of negative experiences felt fair. Responses were received from 164 participants (59.2% response rate). The student who had reported isolation and overwhelm (the only dissenting voice in the reflective essays) did not respond to the member check invitation. We therefore cannot confirm whether our interpretation of her experience aligns with her perspective; this is acknowledged as a limitation. No participant requested removal of their data or modification of the reported themes.

Conformability

To avoid researcher bias, reflexivity was ensured.

Reflexivity

We acknowledge our experience as medical educators, which may have influenced our interpretation of the participants’ responses. Researcher 1 was the course instructor and the principal investigator. However, researcher 2 had no instructional role in the course. Author 2 independence from teaching allowed an external perspective, reducing the risk of unconscious bias in coding and theme development. We have actively engaged in reflective practices throughout the research process, maintaining awareness of our own biases and how these could affect our analysis. We used a reflexive journal to document our thought process and biases during analysis. Students were assured that their reflective essays would be anonymized before analysis and that their course grade (pass/fail) was based solely on the grades of the quizzes. Additionally, the grades were automatically revealed to the learners at the end of the online quiz with no interference from the instructor. Before coding, we wrote reflexive memos listing our expectations. We deliberately searched for disconfirming evidence, such as frustration or isolation. The two researchers coded the data independently. Inter-rater agreement was 87% (Cohen’s κ = 0.82). Disagreements were discussed to reach consensus, and the coding framework was refined iteratively. Additionally, our member checking allowed participants to challenge our interpretations, particularly regarding negative experiences.
