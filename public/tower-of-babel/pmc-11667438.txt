Assessing inequities in electrification via heat pumps across the US
Authors: Morgan R Edwards, Jaime Garibay-Rodriguez, Jacob Shimkus Erickson, Muhammad Shayan, Jing Ling Tan, Xingchi Shen, Yueming (Lucy) Qiu, Pengfei Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryHeat pumps are an energy-efficient and increasingly cost-effective solution for reducing greenhouse gas emissions in the building sector. However, other clean energy technologies, such as rooftop solar, are less likely to be adopted in underserved communities, and thus policies incentivizing their adoption may funnel support to well-resourced communities. Unlike previously studied technologies, the effects of heat pumps on household energy bills may be positive or negative depending on local climate, energy costs, building features, and other factors. Here, we propose a framework for assessing heat pump inequities across the US. We find that households in communities of color and with higher percentages of renters are less likely to use heat pumps across the board. Moreover, communities of color are least likely to use heat pumps in regions where they are most likely to reduce energy bills. Public policies must address these inequities to advance beneficial electrification and energy justice.

Introduction

Policy responses to the climate crisis are expanding rapidly in the US at national, state, and local levels.1 Research suggests that carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions must decline dramatically over the next decade and reach net zero by mid-century to limit global temperature change to well below 2°C, and preferably 1.5°C, as set out in the Paris Agreement.2 Strategies to meet these targets in the energy sector include two key elements: (1) transitioning electricity generation to low-carbon technologies such as wind and solar and (2) electrifying energy needs in transportation, industry, and buildings. Transitioning buildings off natural gas (and other fuels such as propane, heating oil, and wood) offers a meaningful pathway to reducing emissions from the building sector. Natural gas emits CO2 when burned in home appliances and also leaks throughout the supply chain.3, 4 Unburned natural gas is primarily methane, a potent greenhouse gas.5 A transition from natural gas to all-electric appliances in buildings may thus be a valuable part of meeting net zero and other climate policy goals.6

Heat pumps are an attractive option for electrifying space heating as they are roughly 3–5 times more efficient than traditional electric resistance heaters. They can provide heating in the winter and cooling in the summer (see Note S1; Section A, supplemental information).7 However, the net private and social benefits of heat pumps vary across locations and depend on many factors, including local climate, electricity mix, prices of electricity and fuels, gas access, installation costs, and the insulation and air-tightness of a building. Heat pumps are cost effective in many locations across the US.8–11 However, they can also be more expensive to operate, especially in colder regions (where they are less efficient) when compared with natural gas furnaces. High heat pump penetration will also increase electricity demand, which may stress electric grid capacity.7 Moreover, the climate impacts of heat pumps (and other equipment that uses refrigerants) can increase if there is high leakage or improper management of refrigerants.12 Nevertheless, high heat pump use rates (up to 90%) are likely to reduce greenhouse gas emissions and provide other benefits across much of the US, especially as the electric grid continues to decarbonize.13

There is a strong rationale for public policy to support heat pump adoption in the US. The net social benefits on a national scale are positive, yet penetration remains low (at just over 10% of households). Globally, heat pump penetration varies dramatically, with high penetration in countries such as Norway, Finland, and Sweden and low penetration in many others.14 A rich literature on the energy efficiency gap identifies many likely barriers to heat pump adoption,15 including high upfront costs, credit and liquidity limitations, low familiarity with technologies,8 low awareness of policy incentives,15,16 personal preferences or behavioral factors,17 and limited financial and engineering literacy.18 Split incentives for rental units19 or homeowners who may wish to move in the future20 also hinder adoption (although homes with heat pumps may enjoy a price premium when sold21). Various policies have been proposed to address these barriers (see Note S2; Section A, supplemental information).21–23 Previous incentives often take the form of tax breaks, rebates, or low- and no-interest loans at the federal, state, local, non-profit, and utility scale. Increasingly, these incentives offer additional support for low-income and qualified households, building on longstanding energy poverty programs such as the Weatherization Assistance Program (WAP) and Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program (LIHEAP).24

The Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) substantially expands incentives for heat pumps and other clean energy technologies at a federal level,25 and local, state, and utility policies are also increasingly promoting household electrification. However, the extent to which these incentives will benefit underserved communities remains uncertain. Prior research indicates that low-income households are less likely to take advantage of energy efficiency and clean energy incentives.26–30 However, the IRA provides more targeted incentives than past federal policies, and recent research on rooftop solar31 (and prior research on income-targeted incentives for broadband27) suggests that income-qualified incentives may be effective at driving increased adoption among low-income households.30,32 As heat pump sales grow rapidly in the US and globally,33 disparities in adoption may exacerbate current inequities in the energy system.34 With higher levels of electrification, households that electrify sooner are also less likely to bear the rising costs of stranded natural gas assets.35 An assessment of current inequities in heat pump adoption and implications for policy is thus important and timely.

Here, we investigate spatial patterns in heat pump use across the US to inform the design and implementation of equitable and beneficial electrification policies. Although space heating and cooling represent, on average, 55% of household energy use and 44% of energy bills (with high regional variation36), inequities in heat pump use have yet to be comprehensively explored. Two recent studies assess the social and economic determinants of heat pump adoption nationally37 and in New York,38 but these analyses do not explicitly focus on equity. They also do not account for an important conceptual challenge: unlike technologies such as rooftop solar and energy efficiency, operating costs of heat pumps are highly variable and may exceed the costs of fossil fuel alternatives. We develop a framework for evaluating inequities in this class of technologies and apply it to heat pumps using a national property dataset and local socioeconomic data (see experimental procedures). Specifically, we address the following questions: (1) How does heat pump use across the US vary with respect to income, race/ethnicity, and renter status? (2) Where do these use patterns suggest possible inequities (and where do they not)? (3) What are the implications for policies to promote just energy transitions?

Results

Framework for estimating heat pump inequities

Energy justice calls for everyone to have access to clean, safe, affordable, and reliable energy and to be able to participate in energy decisions that affect them.39,40 Energy justice has many dimensions: distributive, procedural, restorative, recognition, and cosmopolitan.41 Researchers and communities have identified many injustices across the energy life cycle.40,42–44 Underserved communities are simultaneously more likely to experience energy-related pollution and less likely to benefit from energy services.45 Energy poverty remains a significant policy challenge, with over one in four US households reporting difficulty paying their energy bills.24,46 Households experiencing energy poverty may limit their energy use,47 forgo other essential services to pay energy bills, or experience shut-offs if bills are not paid.48,49 They are also more vulnerable to shocks (e.g., COVID-1950), price changes,51 and the effects of extreme weather.52 Households in communities of color are 60% more likely to be energy burdened compared with majority white communities.53 They are also more likely to live in energy-inefficient homes and less likely to use technologies such as rooftop solar, even after controlling for income and resource potential.15,54

Our framework for assessing inequities in heat pump use builds on prior work on distributive energy justice by distinguishing between three concepts: inequality, inequity, and injustice55 (see Figure 1). Inequality refers to a difference across populations.56–58 Inequalities have many potential drivers and do not necessarily indicate a need for policy intervention. For example, we expect to see more heat pumps in mild climates, where they are more efficient and cost effective.13 However, differences may persist even after controlling for these factors. Specifically, there may be fewer heat pumps in underserved households (e.g., racial or ethnic minorities, low-income, renters, etc.). Previous literature on other clean energy technologies characterizes these differences as an inequity.30–32,42,59

Framework for assessing inequalities, inequities, and injustices in clean energy technologies whose benefits to households may be positive or negative

First, we use spatial data on different heating technologies and their performance to assess inequalities. Second, we add in data on sociodemographics and the household benefits to determine if these inequalities are also inequities. Third, this framework is applied to identify injustices where public policies or other systemic barriers exacerbate or perpetuate inequities. We apply this framework to assess inequalities and inequities in heat pump use across the US.

Inequity is a normative concept that refers to what is considered unfair, with roots in philosophical and legal scholarship.60 If a technology provides benefits, it is unfair for underserved house-holds not to have access. Finally, an injustice occurs when public policies or other systemic factors exacerbate, or perpetuate, existing inequities (see discussion for policy recommendations to advance energy justice).

Many clean energy technologies, such as energy efficiency and rooftop solar, benefit households by reducing energy bills. Heat pumps, by contrast, may raise or lower bills compared with fossil fuel alternatives. Lower heat pump use among underserved households in areas where they lower heating bills (or provide other benefits such as access to cooling for households that do not otherwise have air conditioning) represents a potential inequity. It may also suggest that there are disproportionate (and thus unjust) barriers to access, and that heat pump incentive programs unfairly divert resources away from low-income households. However, higher heat pump use among underserved households in areas where they raise bills also represents a potential inequity. For these cases, adoption by well-resourced populations can provide a service to society by buying down the cost curve. To operationalize this framework, we integrate spatial data on heating technologies and a cost/benefit model of energy bill impacts. We then assess patterns in heat pump use overall and across regions where they are likely to cause energy bills to increase, decrease, or stay the same. We aim for this framework to complement and extend existing theories on just energy transitions.41

Patterns in heat pump use across the US

Heating technologies vary across the US (see Figure 2). As heat pump use is not reported with high granularity in other datasets, we use national household property data from the Zillow Transaction and Assessment Database (ZTRAX).21,61 We develop a logistic regression (logit) model to estimate household heat pump use as a function of variables at different scales (household, census-tract, and county levels). Using this model, we assess whether race, ethnicity, income, and renter status significantly correlate with differences in the likelihood that a household uses a heat pump. We also control for factors expected to affect the likelihood of heat pump use, such as heating and cooling degree days (HDD and CDD, which affect heat pump efficiency), electricity and natural gas prices, and building age (as a proxy for electrification readiness) and type (i.e., single-family, multifamily, etc.). Finally, we include state dummy variables to account for state-level policies (such as electrification subsidies or fuel-switching restrictions) and other state-specific differences (see experimental procedures and Note S3; Section B, supplemental information for a full description of data sources and Note S7; Section E, supplemental information for robustness checks using household sample data from the Residential Energy Consumption Survey).

After controlling for state effects, several control variables correlate meaningfully with heat pump use (see Figure 3A and Table S3; Section C, supplemental information).16 We find a negative relationship between HDD and CDD and heat pump use, with a 10% increase corresponding to a 9.4% and 2.6% decrease in odds, respectively.1 This effect is likely explained by better heat pump performance in milder climates within states. There is also a large negative relationship with building age, with evidence of a nonlinear relationship (see Note S8 and Table S16; Section F, supplemental information). This effect is likely explained by older buildings generally being less electrification ready. Heat pump use is also lower in multifamily buildings, which face higher barriers to adoption, and higher in small dwellings such as duplexes and mobile homes. Natural gas prices and home value both have smaller correlations. Higher electricity prices are associated with higher heat pump use (with a 10% increase corresponding to a 7.3% increase in odds). This effect may be explained by areas dominated by electric resistance heating, where higher prices incentivize switching to a more efficient heat pump. This explanation is supported by robustness checks, where we find that electricity price is positively correlated with heat pump use where electric resistance heating is dominant and negatively correlated where direct fuel use is dominant (see Table S24; Section F, supplemental information).

Sociodemographic variables strongly correlate with heat pump use, even after controlling for other factors (see Figure 3B). Specifically, higher minority populations within states (except for Native Americans), relative to white populations, significantly correlate with lower heat pump use. For example, a 10% increase in the Black population in a census tract is associated with a 13% decrease in the odds of heat pump use. The percentage of Asian, multiracial, Pacific Islander, and other non-white racial/ethnic residents is also significantly and negatively associated with heat pump use. Coefficients for income and its square suggest a non-linear income effect, which we explore in section nonparametric estimates of heat pump use (and Figure S2; Section G, supplemental information). Additionally, a 10% increase in renter-occupied units in a tract is associated with a 4% decrease in the odds of heat pump use. These findings are robust to a variety of different model formulations (see Note S8; Section F, supplemental information), and the pseudo R2 value (0.3333) indicates a good overall fit.62,63 We replicated this analysis using RECS and found broadly consistent results with respect to race/ethnicity and income but not renter status (see Note S7; Section E, supplemental information).

Nonparametric estimates of heat pump use

Next, we explore non-linear relationships between heat pump use, income, and renter status across different racial/ethnic majority census tracts (see experimental procedures and Figure 4). We normalize heat pump use by state to control for state-level variation in climate, policies, electricity and fuel prices, and other variables. Households of color are disproportionately low-income and more likely to be renters, but distinct patterns emerge when comparing areas with similar median income and home-ownership rates.64,65 For white-majority census tracts (69% of tracts), heat pump use increases slightly with median income up to approximately $60,000 and then declines. By contrast, Black-majority census tracts (7.3% of tracts) show a sharp rise in heat pump use at approximately the same income level. Hispanic and no-majority census tracts have installed fewer heat pumps compared with their white counterparts across all income levels. Similarly, for white-majority and Black-majority census tracts, heat pump use generally decreases as the percentage of rented households increases, while other racial-majority census tracts have lower heat pump use overall and flatter trends. We also find evidence of nonlinear relationships between heat pump use, electricity prices, and home age (see Figures S1–S3; Section G, supplemental information).

Regional analysis of heat pump inequities

We examine patterns in heat pump use in areas where they are highly likely to provide net benefits (or costs) to households in the form of changes to energy bills. First, we calculate the net effects of heat pump adoption on energy bills at the county scale by comparing the operating costs of a heat pump to the most common primary heating technology, taking into account device efficiencies, energy prices, and climate data (see experimental procedures). Following previous studies,9,66,67 we focus on differences in operating costs because (1) we are assessing inequities in heat pump use rather than new adoption and (2) differences between average installation costs of a heat pump compared with other heating devices (e.g., natural gas, propane, or fuel oil furnace or electric resistance heater) plus air conditioning are small (see supplemental experimental procedures, Figure S4, and Table S28, supplemental information). Similarly, we do not include operating costs of cooling because differences across cooling technologies (e.g., heat pump versus a stand-alone air conditioner) are smaller than those across heating technologies. We recognize that not all homes with heating also have cooling; for these homes, switching to a heat pump may entail higher energy bills but greater access to a service (i.e., cooling) that may be increasingly important under climate change.68

We perform a Monte Carlo analysis to identify counties that exhibit either net benefits or costs in 90% of simulations and a neutral region where at least 10% of simulations yield net benefits and net costs. Net benefits vary across the US, with 53% of households in our dataset seeing benefits in terms of reduced energy bills, 19% seeing costs, and 28% seeing no clear benefits or costs (see Figure 5A). While our dataset does not contain all US homes (see Note S4; Section B, supplemental information), our results are consistent with findings from recent studies that focus on operating9,66,67 or life cycle11,13 (including installation) costs. Primary heating fuel is a key driver of benefits: in regions where the heating source is electricity or fuels other than natural gas, heating is generally more expensive, and thus the net benefits of heat pumps are higher (see Figure 5B). However, climate and energy prices also play an important role. To examine inequities in heat pump use, we run separate logistic regressions for these three cost/benefit regions (see Figures 5C–5E). Using the framework presented in framework for estimating heat pump inequities, we define inequities as occurring where there are (1) lower odds of heat pump use in underserved communities in the net benefits region or (2) higher odds in the net costs region. We use the same variables as in our national analysis (see Table S4; Section C, supplemental information) but focus our discussion on results related to race, income, and renter status. Race and ethnicity significantly correlate with lower heat pump use (within states) across all three regions. However, the negative relationship between minority populations and heat pump use is larger when heat pumps provide net benefits to households relative to regions with net costs. For example, a 10% increase in the Black population in a census tract leads to a 13% decrease in the odds of heat pump use in regions with net benefits (coincidentally the same value as in our national analysis), compared with a 5.1% decrease in regions with net costs. For the neutral region, we observe a larger decrease (25.8%) for the Black population; however, the overall effect across racial/ethnic minorities is less pronounced compared to regions with net benefits. The percentage of the population living in regions where heat pumps provide net benefits versus net costs varies across racial and ethnic groups, with 39% of the white population and 52% of the Black population living in regions where heat pumps provide net benefits, compared with 39% overall (see Note S5 and Tables S5 and S6; Section D, supplemental information). However, all racial and ethnic groups are represented in each of our three regions (see Table S4; Section C, supplemental information for further discussion).

We also observe differences in the relationship between income and renter status and heat pump use across the three cost/benefit regions. To explore these differences further and assess potential nonlinearities, we repeat the locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (LOESS) analysis described in nonlinear estimates of heat pump use for each of the three cost/benefit regions (see Figure S1; Section G, supplemental information). We observe a generally positive relationship between income and heat pump use across all three regions up to a median income of approximately $100,000 (for net benefit regions), $65,000 (for neutral regions), and $77,000 (for net cost regions). After these levels, the effect of income is negative but smaller for the net benefit and neutral regions and larger for the net cost regions. Renter status negatively correlates with heat pump use in the net benefits and net costs regions but has the largest coefficient in the latter, whereas it exhibits a small positive relationship in the neutral region. As in the results on race and ethnicity, these results also suggest that low-income and renter communities are less likely to use heat pumps, even in regions where they provide benefits, suggesting an inequity that will be important to address in electrification policies.

Discussion

Household electrification is an emerging energy justice issue in the US. At the national scale and controlling for state fixed effects, we find that higher percentages of racial and ethnic minority households, lower median income, and higher percentages of renters at the census-tract level all correlate with lower heat pump use. The negative correlations between racial and ethnic minority populations and heat pump use are largest in areas where heat pumps are expected to provide direct benefits to households by lowering energy bills. While directionally the same, the negative correlation between renter status and heat pump use is largest in areas where heat pumps are likely to be a net cost to households. Income effects are nonlinear but suggest that moderate income is generally associated with higher heat pump use across net cost and benefit regions. Taken together, these results suggest that, within states, minority communities are disproportionately less likely to reap the benefits of using a heat pump. Additionally, our nonparametric analysis shows that higher income among Black-majority census tracts is associated with a higher likelihood of heat pump use relative to other groups, suggesting distinct relationships between heat pump use, race/ethnicity, and income.

Our analysis contributes new insight to the field of energy justice by assessing inequities in a technology that may have higher or lower operational costs than incumbents. Widely studied technologies such as rooftop solar or energy efficiency may have high upfront costs but lower electricity costs across the board. By separately analyzing dynamics in regions where heat pumps have positive, negative, or neutral effects on energy bills, we can provide a more nuanced assessment of whether lower (or higher) heat pump use in underserved communities suggests potential inequities. Because we find evidence of racial/ethnic, income, and (to a lesser extent) renter status inequities in regions where heat pumps provide bill savings, policies, markets, or other drivers may be implicitly underserving these marginalized groups.32,57,69,70 Conversely, lower heat pump use in regions where heat pumps raise energy bills does not represent an inequity and can help drive down costs, particularly for heat pumps designed to operate in cold climates. Notably, race and ethnicity are the strongest negative correlates of heat pump use in regions where heat pumps are expected to result in energy bill savings, leading us to conclude that there is evidence of an emerging inequity.

We present a quantitative assessment of national racial and ethnic patterns in heat pump use and find robust evidence of heat pump inequities for racial/ethnic minorities, with smaller effects for low-income households and renters. Looking forward, we note several areas for future research. Because we do not measure demographic variables at the household level, our results may under- or over-estimate inequities. Robustness checks using nationally representative household data from RECS along with prior literature (which shows that environmental inequities often increase with data granularity) provide further evidence for the inequities we observe.64,65 However, future work may leverage more granular data to assess these dynamics at the household level. We also focus on current heat pump users, but the demographics of new adopters may be different. Future work could explore these dynamics and investigate patterns of technology diffusion over time and across different countries.71 It can also investigate the effects of changes in the relative prices of electricity and fuels over time, either due to market forces or tax policies, and the effects of technology adoption on heating and cooling demand.72 Finally, while the mechanism behind the correlations we observe is outside the scope of this paper, the evidence presented warrants further causal analysis.

Our framework can be applied to other clean energy technologies where the direct benefits to households may be positive or negative.73,74 While we focus specifically on effects on energy bills, there are a variety of other potential costs and benefits of new technologies. For example, heat pumps may increase cost reliability (particularly in regions where fuel prices are highly variable14), improve thermal comfort, and provide access to cooling for households without air conditioning. They may also have other costs such as increased backup requirements during electricity service interruptions. Utility incentive programs can also affect adoption decisions, and future work could provide targeted recommendations for state and utility policy to advance beneficial electrification. Beyond the individual household, heat pumps also have costs and benefits to society, including decreasing or increasing air pollution and greenhouse gas emissions, depending on the electricity source. As more households electrify, cost dynamics can change and potentially create larger injustices. For example, as the customer base for natural gas utilities shrinks, the burden of cost recovery for durable infrastructure is placed on a smaller number of remaining customers and may raise energy bills.75–77 Underserved communities may disproportionately bear these stranded asset costs.

Addressing heat pump inequities is important globally and perhaps particularly in the US, given recent policies.78 Our analysis suggests that care will need to be taken to ensure that the $43 billion in consumer incentives for energy efficiency and electrification benefits underserved communities. By using insights on current patterns in heat pump use and their costs and benefits, policies can be designed to reduce inequities, and ultimately injustices, in electrification transitions. We identify three key policy recommendations:

(1)Building electrification policies must be location and context specific. For locations where the heat pumps reduce energy bills, targeted outreach can be coupled with free or low-cost installations.19 However, in areas where they may increase bills, incentives must be accompanied by efforts to reduce energy costs, such as energy efficiency upgrades and residential electric heating rates. It is important that the effects of heat pump adoption on energy bills are accurately communicated,79 both to avoid increasing energy poverty and to prevent community backlash, as has been observed with time-of-use pricing programs.80,81 The structure of the IRA, which includes funding for state and local governments, may help initiatives be more responsive to community needs and priorities.

Experimental Procedures

Data sources

We analyze spatial patterns in heat pump use across the US using household-level property data from the ZTRAX, accessed January 2021.84 The dataset includes approximately 163 million buildings (147 million residential) across the US, 63.7 million of which report at least one of 38 heating and cooling technologies (e.g., heat pumps, furnaces, boilers, radiators, space heaters, central air conditioning units, window air conditioning units, etc.). We designate a household as using a heat pump if it is listed as the primary heating source. ZTRAX also provides information on property characteristics, including building type (single-family, multifamily, shared-wall single-family, mobile home, etc.), location, and market and assessed value. Additional characteristics such as number of stories, number of rooms, number of units, building area code, building area square feet, occupancy status, effective year built, and value certification date were excluded from the analysis as over 15% of the data in these variables were missing. For residences that did not have data on home value, the assessed value was used as a substitute. The ZTRAX database includes hundreds of additional variables, including home sale features and information on architecture and finishes that were deemed less relevant to this analysis. See Note S3; Section B, supplemental information for a full description of variables.

We then added census-tract data (racial and ethnic identity, median income, homeownership status, and house age) from the American Community Survey (ACS) 5-year estimates for 2016–202085 via the R tidycensus package version 1.2.3.86 The ACS data were merged into the ZTRAX data using the household 2020 census identifiers (GEOIDs) calculated using latitude and longitude. This merge resulted in a data loss of 0.1% of ZTRAX records due to invalid location data. We also included HDD and CDD calculated from average daily outdoor temperature data from the nearest NOAA weather station between 1991 and 202087 (using a standard 65°F9 outdoor temperature benchmark). We obtained natural gas utility boundaries from the Homeland Infrastructure Foundation-Level Database (HIFLD)88 and price data (for 2020) from the Energy Information Administration (EIA)-17689 form. Average prices for residential electricity customers at the utility level were extracted from EIA Form 861.90 Prices were normalized to 2020 using the EIA national electricity price index.91 To calculate prices by county, we weighted the electricity price for each utility by the number of customers served. After data cleaning and merging, our final dataset includes 99.7% of all residences in ZTRAX that report heating type.

Racial/ethnic identity categories include (1) Black, (2) Asian, (3) Hispanic, (4) Multiracial, (5) Pacific Islander, (6) Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, (7) American Indian or Alaska Native, and other (i.e., households identifying themselves as a race or ethnicity different from the categories as mentioned above). The control variables Cj include housing price, building type, and building age (at the household level); county-level data on electricity costs; utility-level data on natural gas costs and access; and state fixed effects. See Note S3; Section B, supplemental information for more details on these variables. The logit models are implemented in STATA version 17. We also developed additional models (see Note S8; Section F, supplemental information), including interaction effects between race/ethnicity, income, renter status, type, and building age.

Nonparametric analysis

We used nonparametric local smoothing models to illustrate how heat pump use varies across median income and homeownership status for census tracts with different racial/ethnic majorities. We used LOESS regression to fit local linear relationships between state-normalized heat pump use and (1) median household income, (2) the percentage of houses occupied by renters, and (3) building age (at the household level) for census tracts with majority populations of different racial and ethnic groups. We include racial and ethnic groups that have a majority in at least 1% of census tracts (i.e., white, Black, and Hispanic; see Figure S1; Section G, supplemental information). Net benefits analysis

We estimate the net benefits of switching to a heat pump as the change in operating costs (i.e., energy bills). Our analysis uses inputs on the primary heating technology (from the ACS 2016–2020 survey) and average annual electricity prices at the county level. We use state-level data on natural gas, propane, fuel oil, and wood prices. All prices are from 2020. We calculate the coefficient of performance (COP) of a heat pump in each county using hourly temperature profiles from typical meteorological year data.98 Following previous studies, we use a function that assumes a linear relationship between temperature and COP for temperatures between 5°F and 47°F for heat pumps of similar capacity (25,000 BTU) based on the Northeast Energy Efficiency Program (NEEP) (see supplemental experimental procedures).9,13 We assume average efficiencies of 80% for natural gas and propane furnaces, 83% fuel oil furnaces, and 79% for wood stoves (based on the 2015 Federal standard for residential furnaces99 and average efficiency reported by the EPA-certified wood stove database).100 We exclude differences in installation costs but find these to be small when comparing a heat pump with a conventional heating and cooling system (see supplemental experimental procedures, Figure S4, and Table S28, supplemental information). We also assume constant heating demand and electricity prices.

To account for uncertainties in heating device efficiencies and fuel prices, we use a Monte Carlo simulation. For heating technology efficiencies, we construct triangular distributions using 78% as the minimum efficiency for natural gas, propane, and fuel oil furnaces (based on the 1992 Federal standard99) and 58% for wood stoves (the minimum value reported in the EPA-certified wood stove database100). For maximum values, we use 99% for natural gas and propane, 96.7% for fuel oil furnaces (based on the highest efficiency in the Energy Star certified furnaces database101), and 90% for wood stoves (based on the maximum efficiency in the EPA-certified wood stove database). We use a triangular distribution with a minimum and maximum COP of 3 and 5 at 47°F for the heat pump COP, based on values in the NEEP database (note that the minimum COP can be as low as 1 for air temperatures below 47°F). For the price of electricity, natural gas, and other delivered fuels, we use triangular distributions based on the historical national variation (the coefficient of variation using two standard deviations and the mean of national average prices as a percentage of 2020 prices) from 2000 to 2020. In brief

Heat pumps are an energy-efficient and increasingly cost-effective solution to electrifying space heating. However, current electrification rates vary substantially across communities. Here, we develop a framework to evaluate current inequities in electrification via heat pumps in the US. We find that households in communities of color are less likely to use heat pumps, especially in regions where they are expected to provide a direct benefit by lowering energy bills. These results will be important in designing public policies to support equitable and beneficial electrification.

Context & Scale

Burning fossil fuels for space heating in buildings is an important contributor to climate change in the US and worldwide. A key strategy for reducing these emissions is electrification of space heating via heat pumps coupled with low-carbon electricity. Public policies, including the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA), provide incentives for households to adopt heat pumps, and local, state, and utility policies have also begun to provide pathways for household electrification. However, electrification rates vary substantially across communities, raising questions about potential energy inequities. In this study, we assess current inequities in electrification via heat pump across the US. We develop a framework that combines household property data, sociodemographic indicators, and estimates of the effects of heat pump adoption on household energy bills. We find that communities of color and those with a higher proportion of renters are less likely to use heat pumps across the board. We also examine patterns in heat pump use in regions where they are predicted to reduce—or potentially increase—energy bills. Communities of color are less likely to use heat pumps in regions where, if adopted, they are predicted to experience energy bill reductions. This emerging inequity highlights the importance of public policies that provide targeted incentives and support for electrification in underserved communities, with a focus on beneficial electrification that lowers energy bills. Our framework for assessing inequities in heat pump use can be applied to other clean energy technologies where the direct benefits of adoption for households vary across locations.
