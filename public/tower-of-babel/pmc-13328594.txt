RSM optimized mechanical performance and chemical durability of nano silica, nano alumina fiber reinforced alkali activated mortar
Authors: Mahmood Hunar Dheyaaldin, Mohammad Ali Mosaberpanah, Dler H Kadir, Gökhan Kaplan, Mustafa Özakça
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aims to optimize the mechanical performance, durability, and environmental sustainability of alkali-activated mortars (AAM) incorporating nano-silica (NS), nano-alumina (NA), and polypropylene fiber (PPF). A three-factor, three-level Central Composite Design (CCD) within the Response Surface Methodology (RSM) framework was employed, generating 17 experimental mixtures prepared using fly ash (FA) and ground granulated blast-furnace slag (GGBS) as binder materials. The maximum compressive strength of 82 MPa was achieved in the mixture containing 2% NA, while the maximum flexural strength (12 MPa) was recorded in the mixture containing 1% NS and 0.5% PPF. ANOVA results confirmed the statistical significance of the developed models, with R² = 0.984 and R² = 0.977 for compressive and flexural strength, respectively. Nano-alumina produced a greater increase in strength than NS, and the combination of both nanomaterials enhanced the density of the microstructure through the formation of C-(A)-S-H and N-A-S-H gels. The incorporation of PPF improved durability by preventing microcrack formation and enhancing resistance to acidic and saline environments. For example, specimens containing 2% NS and 2% NA demonstrated more than 20% higher residual strength under sulfuric acid exposure compared with reference specimens. Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) analyses showed that the nanomaterials accelerated early strength development by filling micro-voids and creating a more homogeneous matrix structure. A CO₂ emission analysis indicated that the optimized AAM mixture emits approximately 607.4 kg CO₂/m³, representing a reduction of about 26%. The results demonstrate that alkali-activated mortars provide a strong and environmentally sustainable alternative to conventional cement-based systems, highlighting the efficiency and practical potential of this approach.Keywords: Alkali-activated mortar, Nano-silica and nano-alumina, Polypropylene fiber reinforcement, Response Surface Methodology (RSM), Low carbon construction materialsSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Robust campaigns are being undertaken to promote sustainable living practices and to develop technologies that reduce carbon emissions. Reducing CO₂ emissions and limiting natural resource consumption are crucial strategies for producing durable and environmentally sustainable materials and products1. An international organization has proposed a four-step strategy to mitigate CO₂ emissions in the cement and concrete industry: (1) implementing advanced technologies in cement plants, (2) improving thermal and electrical efficiency, (3) replacing fossil fuels with sustainable alternatives, and (4) adopting carbon capture and clinker substitution technologies2. The pioneering work of Davidovits in the 1970s played an important role in advancing sustainable construction. He developed a special type of binder that produces significantly lower CO₂ emissions. This work demonstrated that high-quality and environmentally friendly concrete can be produced using alternative binder systems3. Similar materials, referred to as soil-cement, were used in the Soviet Union from the 1950 s, although their chemical compositions have evolved considerably since that time4. Most geopolymer compositions rely on liquid alkaline components and an aluminosilicate source5. Alkaline solutions activate aluminosilicate materials, forming a strong three-dimensional network structure. This process involves a chain reaction between aluminosilicate polymers and the alkaline solution, often under heat, resulting in geopolymer concrete, a sustainable binder comparable to traditional cement6,7.

To improve flexibility, post-cracking behavior, and tensile performance, fibers are commonly incorporated into geopolymer matrices. PPF, in particular, helps prevent early-stage cracking by bridging microcracks, thereby increasing tensile strength and long-term durability8. The primary factor driving the ongoing climate change crisis is the significant increase in the concentration of various greenhouse gases (GHGs) in the atmosphere, particularly carbon dioxide9. Many countries are already experiencing the effects of climate change. Consequently, governments must collaborate to reduce greenhouse gas emissions; however, current efforts remain insufficient to curb the rapid growth of these emissions. To address this challenge, researchers have developed geopolymer concrete (GPC). Geopolymers are produced from materials rich in silica and alumina, such as fly ash (FA), metakaolin, and ground granulated blast-furnace slag (GGBFS or GGBS). These materials are often low-cost resources or industrial by-products (IBPs), making them a more sustainable alternative for construction applications10–13. These industrial by-products can serve as concrete binders when GGBS is activated using a high-pH alkaline solution. Geopolymer cement can be produced by activating GGBS with alkaline solutions such as sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and sodium silicate (Na₂SiO₃)14.

Fly ash possesses important chemical constituents such as silicon dioxide (SiO₂), aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃), iron oxide (Fe₂O₃), and calcium oxide (CaO). In addition, trace amounts of titanium, potassium, magnesium, sodium, and sulfur compounds are present. According to ASTM International standards, the most commonly used types of fly ash are Class F and Class C. Class F fly ash differs from Class C fly ash in that it contains lower calcium oxide content, whereas Class C fly ash contains higher CaO levels. In some cases, Class C fly ash may contain up to 18% CaO15. The two most widely used alkaline activator solutions are sodium hydroxide and sodium silicate, and most studies assume that fly ash contains a significant amount of aluminosilicate compounds16,17.

Fly ash can also be categorized as low-calcium or high-calcium fly ash. Compared with high-calcium fly ash, low-calcium fly ash typically contains approximately 80% silicon (Si) and aluminum (Al) by weight6,18. In certain countries, FA can be 20–60% less expensive than ordinary Portland cement (OPC), whereas in other cases OPC may be nearly twice as expensive as FA19. To ensure that molten slag forms a glassy phase and produces granulated blast furnace slag with cementitious properties, it must be rapidly quenched with air or water. Water quenching, particularly using high-pressure water jets, is generally considered the most effective method because it produces a higher proportion of glassy structures in the slag20. A by-product of iron and steel production, steel slag primarily consists of iron oxide, silicon dioxide, aluminum oxide, and calcium oxide. Trace amounts of sulfur trioxide (SO₃), titanium dioxide (TiO₂), and potassium oxide (K₂O) may also be present. Steel slag is generated during iron production in a basic oxygen furnace (BOF) and during steel production from scrap in an electric arc furnace. In a BOF process, impurities such as silicon, carbon, phosphorus, and manganese are removed using oxygen, forming slag when these elements combine with lime and dolomitic lime21. With its high silicate and alumina content, GGBS complies with ASTM C989, which allows the material’s properties to gradually evolve and improve over time22. The compressive strength of alkali-activated mortar generally increases during the curing period, with contributions from NA, NS, and PPF. Notably, specimens containing 1% NA and an appropriate NS content performed better than those without these nanomaterials13,23. Studies have shown that adding nano-silica in different proportions significantly enhances the compressive strength of FA and GGBS-based geopolymer mortars. For example, incorporating nano-silica in the range of 0–2% by weight in FA/GGBS geopolymer mortars has resulted in noticeable improvements in compressive strength.

Nano-SiO₂ and nano-Al₂O₃ enhance the mechanical and durability properties of geopolymer systems by filling micro-voids, refining the pore structure, and promoting additional gel formation24. These highly reactive silica particles can also act as micro-fillers between binder materials and aggregates in FA and GGBS-based geopolymer concrete25. Nanomaterials such as nano-alumina (Al₂O₃) and nano-silica (SiO₂) have been reported to increase the shear bond strength between the concrete substrate and geopolymer matrix26. When combined with PPF, these nanomaterials create a synergistic effect that simultaneously enhances compressive strength, flexural toughness, and resistance to chemical attack, an interaction that has not been thoroughly explored in previous studies27. Sodium hydroxide plays a critical role in geopolymer reactions and significantly influences the structure and compressive strength of geopolymers. The concentration of NaOH in the aqueous phase of the geopolymer system affects the dissolution of solid particles and the formation of the final structure28. Increasing GGBS substitution during alkali activation leads to the formation of larger quantities of C-(A)-S-H and N-A-S-H gels. As a result, porosity is reduced, a denser and more homogeneous microstructure develops, and both strength and elastic modulus increase29,30.

A cement-based mixture that satisfies specific performance requirements can be produced by adjusting the proportions of available raw materials through experimental design optimization. In conventional mixture design, both prescriptive and performance-based approaches can be used for optimization31. Recently, Response Surface Methodology (RSM) has been widely applied for developing mortar and concrete mix proportions32–34. In FA/GGBS geopolymer systems, important variables include the concentration of the alkali activator and the ratio of FA to GGBS35. The type, amount, and volume fraction of fibers, along with the binder composition, curing environment, and curing age, significantly influence the strength characteristics of fiber-reinforced geopolymer composites36. Another key factor influencing mechanical performance is the interaction between fibers and the geopolymer binder. A strong interfacial transition zone (ITZ) between the fiber and the geopolymer matrix enhances the load-bearing capacity of the composite37. Most alkaline solutions employed in the polymerization process consist of sodium hydroxide or potassium silicate and form gel-phase mixtures that may contain lingering insoluble particles, resulting in lower compressive strength25,38.

The central composite design (CCD) method is commonly used for optimizing mixture proportions in cement-based materials. In CCD, experimental points are distributed around a central point in a star-shaped configuration within a two-level factorial design. This method is highly efficient because it allows simultaneous optimization of multiple parameters and can accommodate up to six variables13,23,39.

The primary objectives of this study are: (1) to develop a CCD-based RSM model for predicting and optimizing the mechanical performance of alkali-activated mortars incorporating NS, NA, and PPF; (2) to assess the durability of the optimized mixtures under sulfuric acid, sodium chloride, and magnesium sulfate exposure; (3) to evaluate the CO₂ emission profile of the AAM system in comparison with conventional OPC concrete; and (4) to characterize the microstructural mechanisms using SEM analysis. The use of industrial by-products (FA and GGBS) as primary binders reduces both material cost and carbon footprint, thereby contributing to sustainable construction strategies.

Mix proportion and method of mix design

This research study employed CCD within RSM framework to optimize the mix proportion of alkali-activated mortar. CCD is a well-established experimental design consisting of three components: (1) a two-level factorial design (corner points at coded levels ± 1), (2) axial (star) points at coded levels ± α, and (3) replicated center points45. For three factors at three levels, a face-centered CCD (α = 1) was selected, yielding 17 experimental runs: 8 factorial points, 6 axial points, and 3 center-point replicates. This design enables estimation of a full second-order polynomial response surface, capturing main effects, quadratic effects, and two-factor interaction effects. Design-Expert software was used for experimental design generation, model fitting, and optimization46–48. A comparison between predicted and actual responses indicated that the quadratic model could effectively predict the performance of these nano-enhanced mortar mixes, as summarized in Table 3. Three independent variables were considered: nano-silica content (A: NS, 0–2% by mass of binder), nano-alumina content (B: NA. 0–2% by mass of binder), and polypropylene fiber content (C: PPF 0–1% by volume of binder). The coded levels are defined as −1 = lower bound, 0 = center point, and + 1 = upper bound. The relationship between coded (Xi) and actual values is: Xi = (Actual – Center)/(Range/2). For NS and NA, the coded levels correspond to − 1 = 0%, 0 = 1%, and + 1 = 2%. For PPF, the coded levels correspond to − 1 = 0%, 0 = 0.5%, and + 1 = 1%. The variables and their corresponding ranges are presented in Table 3.

Mixing, casting and curing

Sodium silicate was combined with NaOH flakes after the NaOH had been dissolved in distilled water and allowed to stand for 4 h to reach the required concentration. The prepared alkaline solution was then stored for 24 h before use. The mixing procedure was carried out as follows: (1) dry material (FA, GGBS, nanomaterials, and fine aggregate) were blended in a mechanical mixer for 2.5 min; (2) the alkaline solution with superplasticizer was gradually added and mixed for an additional 2 min; (3) for fiber containing mixtures, PPF was introduced slowly during continued mixing for a further 2 min to ensure that uniform fiber distribution. All mix proportions are expressed per 1 m3. Cubic specimens (50 × 50 × 50) and prism (40 × 40 × 160) mm were filled with AAM and then repeatedly vibrated in the vibration table for 30 s per layer until surface leveling23,40. Following casting, all specimens were sealed with plastic sheets to prevent moisture loss during the initial setting period (24 h). Subsequently, specimens were cured under ambient laboratory condition at 23 ± 2 ℃ until the testing age of 28 days, in accordance with ASTM C10951 until the date of the hardened test52. Compressive strength specimens were used for each mixture49, and flexural strength was assessed using the ASTM C348 standard53. Three replicate specimens per mixture were used for each mechanical test.

For the durability assessment, specimens were immersed in three chemical solutions after 28 days of ambient curing: (1) 5% by the weight of each chemical compound with water to produce the exposure solution for sulfuric acid (H2SO4), (2) 5% sodium chloride (NaCl) solution with pH = 7; and (3) 5% magnesium sulphate (MgSO4) solution with pH = 6.8. All specimens were immersed for 90 days at room temperature. Residual compressive strength was measured at the end of the exposure period and compared with reference specimens cured in water.

Compressive strength

An examination of the alkali-activated mortar’s compressive and flexural strengths is shown in Fig. 3. The figure illustrates the impact of nanomaterials and varying percentages of PPF on the material. The compressive strength of alkali-activated mortar, a critical characteristic for designing specific structures, is similarly crucial for alkali-activated binders. Compressive strength in alkali-activated systems is highly dependent on the concentration of the alkaline solution.

Effect of nano-silica, nano-alumina, and polypropylene fiber on compressive strength of AAM.

Figure 3(a) and (b) compare the compressive strength of two nanomaterials in alkali-activated mortar. For a mixture with 2% nano-alumina (M1), after 28 days, the material’s compressive strength reached its maximum of 82 MPa. Conversely, the application of 2% nano-silica (M6) significantly increased the compressive strength of AAM, reaching about 72 MPa after 28 days. The results were substantially impacted when compared to when both nanomaterials (M4) were used together at a rate of 2%, yielding a compressive strength of about 65 MPa (M1, M6). Furthermore, samples with 1% of both nanomaterials increased AAM’s compressive strength and outperformed (M6).

Figure 3(c) and (d) illustrates the comparison of compressive strength results of AAM between PPF and NS. The compressive strength of the sample (M3) containing 1% PPF is 67 MPa. However, when 2% NS is added (M8), the compressive strength decreases to 64 MPa. This indicates that the combination of 2% nano-silica with 1% PPF has an insignificant impact on the compressive strength of AAM compared to samples containing only 2% NS, which exhibit a higher effect on compressive strength compared to M3 and M8. On the other hand, the sample containing 1% NS with 0.5% PPF shows a slight decrease in compressive strength compared to control samples (M7), (M3), and (M8), measuring about 63 MPa.

Figure 3(e) and (f) examines the compressive strength results of PPF and nano-alumina in AAM. The sample’s compressive strength containing 1% PPF (M3) is 67 MPa. However, adding 2% NA (M2) increases the compressive strength to 71 MPa. This indicates that 2% nano-alumina with 1% PPF significantly enhances the compressive strength of AAM compared to samples containing only 2% nano-silica with 1% PPF (M8), which experiences a decrease and loss of strength performance compared to (M3).

In contrast to M3 and M2, the sample with 2% NA obtains a higher compressive strength of AAM. In contrast to control samples (M7), (M3), and (M2), the sample containing 1% NA with % PPF exhibits an improvement in compressive strength to 74 MPa. However, it is still less than that of (M1), which measures 82 MPa. In every aspect, nano-alumina exhibits superior performance compared to nano-silica, and combining 2% of both yields enhanced compressive strength.

Figure 3(g) shows a strong match between the model’s predictions and the actual compressive strength of concrete after 28 days. Most of the points fall close to the diagonal line, which suggests the model is making very accurate predictions. The high R² value confirms that the model does a good job capturing how different mix designs affect strength. This kind of comparison is commonly used in research to check how reliable a concrete strength prediction model is54,55. To check whether the model meets the requirements of regression analysis, a normal probability plot of studentized residuals was created showed on Fig. 3(h). The residuals show a nearly straight-line pattern along the reference line, with only small deviations at the ends. This confirms that the residuals are normally distributed, which is an important requirement for valid regression analysis56,57. Studentized residuals are better than ordinary residuals because they adjust for differences in variance, making the model assessment more reliable58. The residual plot does not show any clear patterns or trends, which indicates that the model does not have significant errors or missing relationships. This confirms that the model is suitable for predicting concrete compressive strength59.

Flexural strength

Alkali-activated binders share the same flexural strength as alkali-activated mortar, which is an essential property for building certain structures. Multi objective optimization

Multi-objective optimization was performed using the RSM desirability function approach, targeting the simultaneous maximization of both compressive and flexural strength. The optimization criteria were set as NS in range (0–2%); NA target = 0 (unconstrained in optimization); PPF target = 0 (unconstrained); maximize compressive strength (range: 55.4–82 MPa, importance = 3); maximize flexural strength (range: 7.98–12.98 MPa, importance = 3). The optimal solution identified by the CCD-RSM model corresponded to NS = 0% (coded − 1), NA = 1% (coded 0), and PPF = 0.5% (coded 0), with a predicted compressive strength of 71.96 MPa, flexural strength of 9.04 MPa, and an overall desirability of 0.638. This optimal mix represents the best compromise between strength and durability performance across the studied domain.

Optimization results for AAM

The CCD-RSM multi-objective optimization procedure was applied to simultaneously maximize compressive strength, flexural strength, and durability performance. Using the desirability function, the global desirability of the optimal solution (NS = 0%, NA = 1%, PPF = 0.5%) was calculated as D = 0.638. The optimized predicted compressive strength of 71.96 MPa and flexural strength of 9.04 MPa represent a well-balanced high-performance mix, validated by the close agreement between predicted and experimental values (within ± 3% for compressive strength and ± 5% for flexural strength across the 17 runs). This solution also corresponded to acceptable durability performance, with residual strengths of approximately 52 MPa (H2SO4) and 65 MPa (MgSO4) after 90-day exposure. Obtaining the optimal value for every response in a single domain is challenging. Therefore, a multi-objective optimization approach is proposed as the most effective method for achieving an ideal solution that satisfies the requirements of all responses. After creating and validating ANOVA models for each response, the best practical answers were identified by utilizing a multi-objective optimization technique63.

The core aim of the optimization study was to ascertain suitable values for the independent variables to fulfill the optimization objectives. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis

Figure 10 demonstrates the SEM microstructural analysis of alkali-activated mortar containing 0.5% PPF fiber combined with 1% and 2% NS and NA, which reveals significant changes in the microstructure affecting mechanical performance and durability. With a content of 0.5% PPF, the fibers are generally well dispersed throughout the matrix. This fiber helps control cracking by bridging microcracks and limiting their propagation. In addition, nanoscale fillers such as NS and NA enhance the fiber-matrix interfacial bond by improving contact and load transfer between the fibers and the surrounding matrix. At NS dosage of 1–2%, the particles are generally well dispersed, filling microvoids and refining the pore structure by reducing capillary pores. This contributes to faster early-age strength development, particularly at an NA content of 1%. However, increasing NA to 2% promotes particle agglomeration, similar to that observed with NS at higher contents. Moreover, the combined use of NS and NA may reduce matrix uniformity, potentially lowering overall homogeneity. Specimens that include 2% nano-alumina serve as effective fillers by occupying pore spaces and improving the interaction between the binder phase, which consists of FA and GGBS23,89. The main product of the polymerization reaction is N-A-S-H gel, which is formed when fly ash particles dissolve as a result of ambient curing. The densification of the entire matrix, which resulted in increased structural integrity, allows the fly ash alkali-activated mixes to display a more compact and dense microstructure90,91.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that reinforcing alkali-activated mortar (AAM) with nano-silica (NS), nano-alumina (NA), and polypropylene fire (PPF) effectively enhance both mechanical performance and chemical resistance. By utilizing industrial by-products, the study provides a sustainable alternative to traditional cement-based materials and supports the development of environmentally friendly construction solutions.

The highest compressive strength of 82 MPa was obtained in mixtures containing 2% NA (0% NS, 0% PPF), while the maximum flexural strength of 12 MPa was achieved in the mixture with 1% NS and 0.5% PPF. ANOVA results confirmed that NA is the dominant factor influencing compressive strength, whereas PPF predominantly governs flexural strength.

ANOVA verified the statistical significance of the RSM quadratic models, with R² = 0.984 for compressive strength and R² = 0.977 for flexural strength, and a non-significant lack of fit (P > 0.05). The regression equations successfully quantified the individual and interaction effects of NS, NA, and PPF on mechanical performance.
