Development of an energy consumption map for turning inconel 718 through coupled chip-morphology and material-deformation analysis
Authors: Muhammad Adnan Khan, Riaz Ahmad, Muhammad Rizwan ul Haq, Syed Husain Imran Jaffery, Danyal Zahid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSustainable machining of Inconel 718, a nickel-based superalloy that is highly valuable in aerospace, is faced with the challenge that it has high energy requirements and poor machinability. In this study, a new energy consumption map and combined energy-morphology framework is proposed which draws a correlation between a specific cutting energy (SCE) and the mechanics of chip formation. Detailed experimental profiling maintained over a variety of cutting speeds (25–75 m/min ) and feed rates (0.05–0.20 mm/rev) demonstrates a SCE profile with a high-energy ridge of greater than 3.0 J/mm³ at lower feed rates, and a low-energy trough of less than 2.0 J/mm³ at higher speeds and feeds. Quantitative measurements of chip morphology suggest that low-SCE conditions are coupled with fine serrations, shear bands of less than 24 μm, compression ratios of less than 1.2, and relatively large included angles hence indicating efficient thermally assisted shear localization. The best processing window of 62.5–75 m/min and 0.10–0.15 mm/rev was found that allows to save energy and at the same time improve chip control. The derived energy-morphology map is a viable tool of shop-floor decision making, which helps to achieve energy-efficient machining.Keywords: Inconel 718, Specific cutting energy, Energy consumption map, Chip morphology, Sustainable machining, TurningSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Nickel-based superalloys has been widely used in the aerospace and nuclear industries with regard to its high-temperature performance, corrosion resistance, and strength1,2. Inconel 718 is an age-hardenable austenitic nickel-chromium alloy whose exceptional mechanical properties, including tensile strength exceeding 1375 MPa, yield strength above 1100 MPa, and hardness in the range of 36–44 HRC in the aged condition, are retained up to approximately 650 °C, making it indispensable for safety-critical components including turbine blades, combustor liners, rocket engine parts, fasteners, and structural frames in aerospace, power generation, and nuclear applications1,3. Its high-temperature strength is derived primarily from the coherent γ’’ (Ni₃Nb) precipitate strengthening mechanism, supplemented by γ’ (Ni₃(Al, Ti)) precipitates and solid solution hardening, which together sustain load-bearing capacity under extreme thermal and mechanical conditions4. These same strengthening mechanisms, however, are the root cause of its exceptionally poor machinability and presents one of the most demanding machinability challenges in modern manufacturing, a challenge that carries direct and compounding environmental sustainability consequences. Sustainable industrial production is under growing pressure due to the irreversible exhaustion of traditional energy sources5, with manufacturing accounting for approximately 60% of industrial energy consumption6. Machining operations are a substantial economic activity, the global machining market was estimated at roughly $356 billion in 2024, with projections to reach approximately $507 billion by 20317, contribute more than 90% of their environmental impact through electrical energy consumption8, which is why there is an immediate need for economic and environmental optimization. Machinability is a multi-dimensional property defined by interrelated parameters like specific cutting energy, tool life, cutting forces, chip formation behavior, and surface integrity each of which amplifies the environmental burden when machining a difficult-to-cut material9. In case of Inconel 718, these penalties arise from convergence of adverse material properties: low thermal conductivity (less than 11.4 W/m.K) concentrates over 80% of the heat generated at the tool-workpiece interface4, while rapid work hardening generates unpredictable and elevated cutting forces, and high chemical affinity promotes adhesion and built-up edge formation10. Together, these properties drive cutting temperatures that frequently exceed 1000 °C, elevating the energy requirements during machining11. Simultaneously, accelerated tool wear mechanisms such as built-up-edge formation, diffusion, and coating degradation significantly reduce tool life during machining of Inconel 71812, multiplying the consumption of resource-intensive carbide tooling and its associated waste streams13. At the component scale, conventional subtractive manufacturing of Inconel 718 in aerospace applications generates scrap percentages of approximately 40–50%14, means that every inefficiency in the machining process is magnified into disproportionate raw material waste and embodied energy loss. Addressing poor machinability therefore represents a direct pathway to reducing the environmental footprint of Inconel 718 component manufacture, and specific cutting energy, as the most directly controllable and measurable of these machinability parameters, provides the primary lever through which this study pursues that objective.

Chip morphology is an important tool in understanding energy consumption as it provides a clear image of the thermomechanical processing conditions. Inconel 718’s extreme toughness, low stacking-fault energy, and high strain-rate sensitivity produce segmented chips with highly serrated profiles, mainly caused by adiabatic shear localization. The theory of catastrophic thermoplastic shear was first proposed by Recht15 who argued that serrated chips form when thermal weakening exceeds strain hardening. Later Komanduri et al.16 and Komanduri and Brown17 saw fully developed catastrophic shear bands when machining titanium and steel and formulated a two-phase model in which shear instability was caused by geometric constraints and a lack of heat dissipation. Davies et al.18,19 also demonstrated that under critical cutting speeds, serrated chip formation can be observed when thermal equilibrium is disrupted. The shape of chip serration is a significant determinant of energy consumption in metal machining, as at low cutting speeds, coarse serrated chips with longer tool-chip contact lengths are produced, leading to increased frictional energy loss20. Transitional serrations are formed at moderate speeds with periodic shear bands alternating with undeformed segments, showing a balance between thermal and mechanical influences21. At high speeds, fine serrated chips with shorter contact lengths prevail, focusing plastic deformation and shear energy into narrow bands6. In particular, these high-speed serrations produce stresses and temperature surges that increase the rate at which tools crack and wear, as well as produce cutting force vibrations that degrade surface quality22,23. Although these are significant effects, there is a lack of clear understanding of the specific relationship between morphology metrics and SCE.

The development in the field of energy-efficient machining of nickel alloys has been carried out in two primary directions, machine-level energy modeling and optimization of process parameters. Early research by Gutowski et al.24 proposed an exergy based framework, although practical difficulties with such theoretical models include modeling uncertainties arising due to non-linear interactions of subsystems, and the need for machine-specific empirical calibration. This calibration requirement has led to the development of practical, data-driven empirical models. For example, Li and Kara25 developed a simple but efficient model that related Specific Energy Consumption (SEC) with the Material Removal Rate (MRR) and was shown to be very accurate in predicting the amount of energy used during turning and milling. In a further development, Velchev et al.26 developed a better empirical model of CNC turning which rectifies non-conformance found in previous models and minimizes prediction errors to less than 8% which gives a much better foundation for shop-floor energy forecasting. At the same time, parameter optimization approaches based on Taguchi methods27–29 or Response Surface Methodology30–33 have typically been aimed at reducing the cutting power instead of Specific Cutting Energy (SCE). This distinction is operationally critical, as these two objectives represent conflicting optimization goals with opposed optimal settings: minimizing cutting power favors conservative, low-MRR parameters, while reducing SCE requires maximizing the efficiency of energy use per unit volume of material removed. Despite SCE’s superior reflection of fundamental tool-workpiece tribological interactions34, the interplay between optimized cutting parameters, resulting chip morphology, and SCE remains inadequately characterized for Inconel 718.

Beyond machine-level energy models and parameter optimization strategies, a deeper understanding of the material removal mechanisms governing energy efficiency is essential, particularly the role of chip formation and shear localization during machining of nickel-based superalloys. Foundational work by Komanduri & Schroeder35 and Komanduri & Hou36 clarified the role of adiabatic shear localization in forming segmented chips. Meanwhile, Wang et al.45 developed a model of serrated chip formation based on a combination of ductile fracture and adiabatic shear. Pawade et al.37 experimentally showed increased thermal softening at higher cutting speeds. Energy mapping has been helpful for other materials: Warsi et al.38 created SCE maps for Al 6061-T6, and Younas et al.34 studied Ti6Al4V. Still, four critical gaps remain for Inconel 718: (1) No comprehensive energy maps are available across industry-relevant parameters (Vc = 25–75 m/min, f = 0.05–0.20 mm/rev); (2) Quantitative relationships between chip morphology metrics and SCE are unknown; (3) Previous research either focuses on energy consumption or chip morphology separately, ignoring their connection39–41; (4) Industry guidelines focus on reducing wear without considering how chip morphology affects energy use42–45, creating sustainability blind spots.

The research contributes to the existing gaps by using integrated energy-chip morphology mapping during turning of Inconel 718. While similar integrated approaches have been reported for Ti‑6Al‑4 V34 and Al 6061‑T638, those materials exhibit fundamentally different chip formation mechanisms, Inconel 718’s extremely low thermal conductivity (≤ 11.4 W/m·K), pronounced work‑hardening, and high dynamic strength demand a dedicated investigation. This study presents the first comprehensive SCE contour map for Inconel 718 over an industrially relevant parameter space (Vc = 25–75 m/min, f = 0.05–0.20 mm/rev) and overlays it with quantitative chip morphology metrics (segmentation frequency, shear band thickness, equivalent chip thickness, chip compression ratio, included angle). Using machining of Inconel 718 to isolate key tool-workpiece interactions, we develop three interconnected frameworks: (1) SCE contour mapping to identify energy-efficient zones across cutting speeds (25–75 m/min) and feed rates (0.05–0.20 mm/rev); (2) chip morphology analysis measuring shear band thickness, segmentation frequency, equivalent chip thickness, chip compression ratio, and included angle; and (3) energy-morphology correlation by overlaying morphology metrics onto SCE contours. The main objective of the study is to determine a mechanistic correlation between reduction of energy demand and characteristics of chip formation through a systematic correlation of SCE with chip morphology parameters. By quantitatively linking chip morphology with energy distribution, this study presents a unified energy-chip interaction framework that explains how material deformation during cutting affects energy consumption in Inconel 718 turning, providing a deeper mechanistic understanding than was previously available for this alloy33–38.

Materials and methods

Experimental setup and materials

The experimental investigation was conducted on a CNC turning centre (Model ML-300) as shown in Fig. 1 with a maximum spindle speed of 3500 rpm and rated power of 26 kW. The workpiece material consisted of nickel-based superalloy Inconel 718 in the form of cylindrical bars measuring 130 mm in diameter and 550 mm in length. These dimensions correspond to the geometry of the commercially procured bar stock available for this experimental programme. The dimensions are consistent with the range standard in Inconel 718 machinability studies37,41,44,46 and yields a length-to-diameter ratio of approximately 4.2, which provides sufficient structural rigidity for stable, chatter-free turning without requiring tailstock support. The workpiece dimensions provide sufficient cutting passes to accommodate all experiments on a single bar, eliminating bar-to-bar material variability. The chemical composition of the workpiece material is listed in Table 147. The workpiece underwent a standardized heat treatment process according to AMS 5383, which included homogenization at 1093 ± 14 °C for 1–2 h, solution treatment at 954–982 °C for over 1 h, and a two-stage aging process involving holding at 718 °C for 8 h followed by 621 °C for another 8 h48. This heat treatment protocol ensured consistent material properties throughout the experimental work.

Experimental design and cutting parameters

The energy map was generated solely through an experimental process designed to provide dense, repeatable coverage of the Vc-f design space. The cutting parameter ranges were determined through the convergence of three sources of evidence. First, the Sandvik Coromant OEM datasheet for the CNMG 120,404-SM S205 insert recommends Vc = 75–105 m/min, f = 0.1–0.3 mm/rev, and ap = 0.15–2.5 mm for nickel-based superalloys; these values anchored the upper portion of the experimental design space and informed the constant depth of cut of ap = 0.6 mm, which lies within the OEM finishing range. Second, the published machinability literature for coated carbide turning of Inconel 718 consistently identifies cutting speeds of 25–75 m/min and feed rates of 0.05–0.20 mm/rev as the most widely adopted and industrially relevant ranges for energy-oriented and chip-formation studies39–41,50–52, providing external validation for the chosen design space. Third, a preliminary experimental study by the authors using the identical CNMG 120,404-SM S205 insert on Inconel 718 workpiece material under wet machining conditions established the following empirical boundaries: (a) Vc = 25 m/min as the lower practical bound, representing the minimum cutting speed commonly adopted in industrial finishing operations for Inconel 718 with coated carbide tools; (b) Vc = 75 m/min as the upper practical bound, above which tool wear under wet conditions accelerated disproportionately; (c) f = 0.05 mm/rev as the stable lower bound for consistent chip segmentation and repeatable power measurement; (d) f = 0.20 mm/rev as the upper semi-finishing limit; and (e) ap = 0.6 mm as the depth producing proportional, thermomechanically stable material removal across all tested Vc–f combinations. A full-factorial grid (Vc = 25, 37.5, 50, 62.5, 75 m/min; f = 0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.20 mm/rev) established the initial set of nominal conditions; each condition was tested in triplicate using a new insert for every run to eliminate bias from tool wear and to measure scatter. Specific cutting energy (SCE) was calculated for each test by normalizing the measured cutting power with the measured material removal rate, and these SCE values were plotted on a feed-speed plane. Regions showing different energy behaviors were identified from this initial grid and then refined with a second phase of experimentation, which included 95 additional Vc-f combinations to improve spatial resolution and clarify contour boundaries. Interpolation of contour lines was done using the combined data set. This experimental approach produced a high-resolution SCE map suitable for mechanistic interpretation and further integration with chip morphology and wear analyses. Measurement techniques and data acquisition

Power measurements were carried out using a Yokogawa CW240 power analyzer connected at the main electrical bus of the machine tool as illustrated in Fig. 4. The CW240 operates over a measurement range of 1–600 V (voltage), 0.1–2000 A (current), and 1 W–600 kW (active power). The sampling interval was set to 0.1 s throughout all measurements. The instrument was factory-calibrated by Yokogawa to IEC 61,010 standards and operated within the manufacturer’s accuracy-guarantee temperature and humidity range (23 °C ± 5 °C, 30–75% RH). Following the two-cycle methodology established by Li and Kara25 and applied in subsequent studies21,53, each experimental run comprised two sequential cycles. In the first cycle (air-cut), the tool traversed the programmed path without workpiece engagement at the prescribed spindle speed and feed rate, and the average power consumed denoted Pair was recorded. In the second cycle (actual-cut), the tool engaged the workpiece under identical kinematic conditions, and the power Pactual was recorded. Figure 5 shows the two power curves; the blue curve is Pactual, whereas the green curve represent Pair. Power data were collected only during the period of tool-workpiece contact (or equivalent tool path for the air cut), thereby excluding transient power spikes associated with spindle acceleration/deceleration and transient phase of tool engagement.

Power consumption during the machining operation, highlighting Pcut (as difference of Pactual and Pair).

The net cutting power Pcut = Pactual - Pair (Eq. 1) represents the power consumed solely in material removal. Equation 2 was then used to compute the specific cutting energy (SCE), dividing cutting power by material removal rate (MRR) and MRR was determined by multiplying the cutting speed, feed rate, and depth of cut (Eq. 3)54. Since both cycles were executed under identical machine states and kinematic conditions, this subtraction eliminates machine-tool-specific contributions to power consumption, including idle losses of motors, drives, pumps, and control systems. This renders the derived Specific Cutting Energy (SCE) values largely independent of machine-tool idle characteristics, thereby improving the generalizability of results across machines employing the same methodology. However, it is important to note that certain load-dependent machine losses such as spindle bearing friction, transmission inefficiencies, and motor load characteristics may increase under actual cutting conditions and are not fully present during air cutting. As a result, a small fraction of these machine-related losses may remain embedded in the measured cutting power.

Moreover, while the two-cycle method removes machine-specific baseline losses, machine tool characteristics such as structural stiffness, dynamic rigidity, and thermal stability may still influence cutting dynamics and, to a minor extent, the measured cutting power. To mitigate these effects, all experiments in this study were performed on a single CNC machine under stable operating and thermal conditions to minimize such effects. Consequently, while the absolute values of SCE may include minor machine-dependent contributions, the relative trends, contour distributions, and correlations presented in this study remain robust and reliable for the given experimental setup. The portability of the two-cycle methodology to other machine platforms has been demonstrated in prior work21,53, supporting the broader applicability of the SCE values reported here.

Although dynamometer-based force measurements are often used to estimate cutting energy through mechanical work calculations, the present study employs a power analyzer to capture the total electrical energy consumption of the machining system. This approach is particularly aligned with the objective of developing an energy consumption map for sustainability-oriented process optimization, where overall system-level energy usage is of primary interest. Furthermore, the power analyzer enables non-intrusive and consistent measurement across a large number of experimental runs, which is essential for generating high-resolution SCE maps. Future work may incorporate dynamometer-based measurements to further isolate cutting mechanics and validate the present findings.

Tool wear was measured in accordance with the ISO 3685 standards55 that states that the average tool wear (VB) must be 0.3 mm or not more than 0.6 mm. Wear measurement was performed with an Olympus DSX1000 digital high-resolution microscope (Fig. 6). The DSX1000 employs a telecentric optical system that guarantees measurement accuracy and repeatability across its full magnification range of 20–7,000×. The minimum measurable increment in digital measurement mode is 0.01 μm.

Chip morphology analysis

Chip samples were taken during steady-state machining and made ready to be examined under a microscope using a standardized method of metallography. This included, ultrasonic cleaning, mounting samples in Bakelite thermosetting resin to retain edges and structural integrity, serial grinding using SiC papers (180–1200 grit) and terminal polishing using diamond suspensions (1 μm and 0.05 μm). Samples were etched with Kroll’s reagent (2% HF, 3% HNO₃) for 8–12 s to reveal microstructural features.

Chip cross‑sections were examined using an Olympus DSX1000 digital microscope, which provides a maximum magnification of ×7000 and a lateral resolution of 0.01 μm, enabling precise measurement of the morphological features of interest [shear band thickness (SBT), segmentation pitch, equivalent chip thickness (te), included angle (θ), and chip compression ratio (CCR)]. For each cutting condition, a minimum of three chips were analyzed, and for each chip, at least ten measurements were taken for each parameter to ensure statistical reliability.

Results and discussion

Development of the energy consumption map

To develop a comprehensive understanding of energy requirements during the turning of Inconel 718, a specific cutting energy (SCE) map was created by systematically measuring energy consumption across a matrix of cutting speeds and feed rates. By keeping the depth of cut constant at 0.6 mm, the effects of cutting speed (25–75 m/min) and feed rate (0.05–0.20 mm/rev) on SCE were isolated. A full-factorial experimental design was used, and the results of the calculated specific cutting energy (SCE) are shown in Table 3. The obtained SCE values were tabulated with the corresponding (Vc, f) values and interpolated on a regular grid. The energy consumption map was subsequently created indicating regions of a low, moderate and high energy consumption as presented in Fig. 7. The low-energy zone was characterized as the SCE values of ≤ 2.0 J/mm3, which means the conditions of efficient material removal. The moderate-energy zone was an intermediate zone of energy demand with the SCE values of 2.0–3.0 J/mm3. Whereas SCE value above 3.0 J/mm3 was linked to the high-energy zone. This zoning enabled a better understanding of the trends of energy efficiency at different cutting speeds and feed rates.

In order to enhance the resolution of the original SCE map, a finer version was developed by introducing new levels of intermediate feed rate and cutting speed in the design space. The final energy map is represented in Fig. 8 with clearly defined energy consumption regions. The plotted energy surface demonstrates variations in energy consumption highlighting the effects of both thermal and mechanical factors across the turning conditions for Inconel 718. The SCE map shows a clear, physically understandable structure once the SCE definition (SCE = Pcut/MRR, with MRR ∝ Vc·f for constant depth of cut) is included in the discussion. At the smallest tested undeformed chip thickness (f = 0.05 mm/rev), the map displays a distinct high-energy ridge in the low–mid speed range: SCE values are high and nearly constant from Vc = 25 to 50 m/min (about 3.29–3.39 J/mm3), then decrease significantly as speed exceeds 55–62.5 m/min (for example, SCE is 2.26 J/mm³ at 62.5 m/min and 2.05 J/mm³ at 75 m/min). This behavior results from two simultaneous effects. First, at low speeds and very thin chips, the instantaneous cutting power Pcut is mainly influenced by strain hardening and frictional resistance in the primary shear zone56,57; because MRR is small when f is low, the ratio Pcut/MRR (or SCE) remains high even if absolute power is modest. Second, as Vc increases, the strain-rate and related adiabatic heating increase rapidly58; once thermal softening begins to lower the flow stress, Pcut grows sub-linearly (or even decreases relative to MRR growth), while the denominator Vc·f increases linearly, causing the sharp drop in SCE seen beyond 55–62.5 m/min.

Detailed specific cutting energy (SCE) map delineating distinct energy regimes.

This speed-dependent transition shifts when the feed increases. At intermediate feeds (f = 0.075–0.12 mm/rev), the high-energy ridge diminishes, and the contours tilt toward lower SCE at slower speeds than for f = 0.05 mm/rev: for example, SCE at f = 0.075 drops from 3.25 J/mm3 at 25 m/min to 2.57 J/mm3 at 65 m/min, and at f = 0.12, many speeds are already near or below 2.5 J/mm3 (e.g., Vc = 30–52 m/min: 2.84 → 2.17 J/mm3). This pattern aligns with the MRR argument and thermo-mechanical kinetics: larger t₁ (higher f) increases plastic work and local heating in the shear zone58, promoting earlier adiabatic shear localization onset and decreasing the flow stress (thus tempering the rise in Pcut), while also increasing MRR. As a result, the net SCE decreases at lower speeds compared to the thin-chip case. At larger feed rates (f ≥ 0.14–0.20 mm/rev), the map shows a broad low-energy trough across a wide speed range: many combinations in the mid-to-high speed range have SCE values ≤ 2.0 J/mm³ (for example, Vc = 50–75 m/min at f = 0.15–0.20 produce SCE 1.7–1.9 J/mm3). Here, the shear zone operates in a thermally assisted, segmented-chip regime where adiabatic softening significantly reduces flow stress, resulting in efficient chip flow. Along with a relatively large MRR, the energy used per unit volume removed reaches a minimum. Therefore, these Vc-f combinations constitute the most energy-efficient cutting conditions within the tested matrix.

The localized structure remains significant, the persistent high-energy ridge at low feeds and mid speeds indicates conditions where friction and strain-rate effects increase Pcut without enough thermal aid to reduce the flow stress. Meanwhile, the ongoing downward trend in SCE at very high Vc, even for thin chips (e.g., Vc = 90–100 m/min at f = 0.05–0.06 mm/rev), shows that high strain rates can trigger thermal softening even with low MRR, though practical constraints like tool wear apply. In summary, interpreting the contour map through SCE definition explains the observed features, increasing Vc and f both influence the numerator (Pcut) and the denominator (MRR). The combination of the effects defines the high, transitional and low energy zones. Thus, energy-efficient turning of Inconel 718 can be realized by selecting Vc-f pairs that enhance adiabatic shear localization and a sufficiently high MRR to minimize SCE.

The refined energy map uncovers complex interactions among cutting parameters during machining of Inconel 718. It provides a comprehensive picture of energy saving and energy consuming regions and thus is a useful tool in selecting cutting conditions that minimize energy consumption without compromising process stability. This map also provides a basis of further connection with chip morphology features and offers a comprehensive framework of sustainable machine process optimization.

Influence of cooling/lubrication conditions on energy–morphology behavior

The SCE values and energy-morphology relationships reported in this study were established under wet flood cooling conditions, which constitutes the controlled baseline for all reported data. The influence of cutting fluid on SCE in nickel-based superalloy machining operates through the competing mechanisms of interface friction reduction and heat extraction from the primary shear zone, with the relative dominance of each effect being cutting-speed and feed-rate dependent. Published work indicates that dry machining conditions, by preserving higher interface temperatures, can promote more complete adiabatic shear localization and reduced cutting resistance at higher cutting speeds59, while MQL has been reported to yield intermediate energy behavior by combining partial lubrication with limited heat extraction43. Korkmaz et al54. further confirmed that cutting environment significantly influences cutting power across the parameter range in nickel alloy turning. These observations indicate that the dominant structural energy-morphology trends established in this study, the systematic variation of shear band thickness, chip compression ratio, chip segmentation frequency, and included angle with Vc and f are thermomechanically consistent with behavior reported across different cutting environments in the literature41,43,59, and the energy-morphology framework is expected to retain its structural validity beyond the wet condition baseline, with zone boundary positions shifting in response to the different thermal and frictional states introduced by alternative coolant strategies. A dedicated comparative investigation across wet, dry, MQL and cryogenic conditions is identified as an important direction for future work and is discussed in the conclusions.

Influence of tool wear on SCE and chip morphology

Although a fresh insert was used for every experimental run to avoid cumulative wear, progressive flank wear occurring within a single cut could potentially influence SCE and chip morphology, especially at higher cutting speeds. To assess this, flank wear (VB) was measured after each test according to ISO 3685. Across all cutting conditions, the maximum measured VB were well below the standard tool life criterion of 0.3 mm. This indicates that all experiments were conducted under a sharp or very lightly worn tool condition. If wear had been significant, we would expect SCE to increase with cutting speed due to higher friction and cutting forces; however, the observed SCE decreased at higher speeds (from > 3.0 J/mm³ at low speeds to < 2.0 J/mm³ at 75 m/min), confirming that thermal softening dominated the process. Similarly, chip morphology parameters (shear band thickness, segmentation frequency, etc.) exhibited smooth, monotonic trends across the speed range without abrupt changes that would indicate a transition from sharp to worn tool conditions. Therefore, while wear inevitably occurs, its influence on the reported SCE values and chip morphology metrics is minimal, and the key conclusions of the energy-morphology framework remain valid.

Chip morphology analysis during Inconel 718 machining

The chip morphology, defined as the study of the geometric and structural characteristics of the chips generated during the cutting operation, provides interesting information on the thermo-mechanical phenomena involved in material removal during machining operation. In the high-strain-rate and high-temperature environment involved in the turning of Inconel 718, the chip morphology is governed by the balance between strain localization, thermal softening, and tool-workpiece interactions36. Unlike ductile steels, which produce ribbons of continuous chips, Inconel 718 and other nickel based superalloys exhibit highly serrated or sawtooth chip morphology, due to the cyclic formation of shear bands and the periodic material fracture60. Because these serrated chips capture the effects of cutting speed, feed, and tool geometry on deformation and heat generation, a systematic study of chip morphology is necessary for the understanding of the basic cutting mechanics as well as for process optimization.

The analysis of chip morphology in the turning of Inconel 718 serves several important functions as shown in Fig. 9. First, the shape and segmentation of chips provide a direct reflection of material flow and failure behavior under various cutting conditions, allowing inferences to be made about strain localization, adiabatic heating and shear instability in the primary deformation zone17. Second, serrated chips result in varying normal and frictional forces acting on the tool rake face which lead to accelerated wear mechanisms such as adhesion, diffusion and crater formation, which in turn leads to understanding these chip characteristics to predict tool life and select appropriate tool materials or coatings17. Third, the chip shape and segmentation influences the stability of cutting forces and vibration tendency61. Fourth, the chip evacuation is dependent on chip curl radius and breakability; poorly formed chips may wrap around the tool or workpiece and damage the surface or cause unexpected work stoppages62. Finally, from an energy efficiency point of view, chip morphology is related to energy consumption, which can be helpful for achieving sustainable high speed machining59. Collectively, these points point out the important role of chip morphology analysis in the development of both scientific understanding and practical optimization of Inconel 718 turning.

The diverse functions of chip morphology17,59,61,62.

The chip morphology analysis entails a set of interconnected parameters, which characterize the geometry, structure, and behavior of the formed chips. These include chip shape and curling, segmentation frequency, shear band thickness, included angle, chip compression ratio (CCR), and equivalent chip thickness (te). This allows a fully parameterized profile of chip morphology to be drawn by measuring these metrics, using the digital micrographs of chip cross-sections obtained at varying cutting speeds and feeds (Fig. 10), and tabulating the data in Table 4. This systematic approach enables making direct comparisons under various cutting conditions and provides a better insight into the effect of these processes parameters on chip formation. These data when combined with SCE contour maps show the relationship between individual chip characteristics and variations in energy consumption, effectively bridging basic deformation processes with machining energy efficiency. This combined method explains the connection between the morphology of chips and cutting power.

Chip curling behavior during Inconel 718 machining

The curvature that the chip assumes as it separates with the workpiece, known as chip curling, is a major characteristic of serrated chip formation and highly influences tool wear, chip removal, and surface quality. Inconel 718 yields segmented sawtooth chips in which thermo-mechanical gradients within each segment drive the curling63. Since Inconel 718 is a low thermal conductor and subject to work-hardening, it tends to retain heat at the tool-chip interface making a considerable thermal gradient between the tool-contacting and free surface of the chip. This gradient, and alternating regions of high shear (shear bands) and rigid regions, produces the characteristic helical curl of segmented chips36.

Chip curling is a phenomenon that results because of the uneven distribution of stress and heat in the chip cross section. The chip is in direct contact with the tool rake face and as the chip passes over the rake face, the surface in contact with the tool encounters increased compressive stresses and temperatures, with the free surface at relatively lower temperature64,65. This results in uneven thermal expansion and plastic deformation causing bending moments that result in the chip being curled. A smaller chip curl radius is indicative of a highly coiled chip, typically experienced in machining Inconel 718 at higher cutting speeds, but a larger radius implies a more open chip structure usually produced at lower speeds and higher feeds57.

Parametric effects of cutting parameters on chip curling

A complex relationship between mechanical deformation and thermal softening, which depends on cutting speed (Vc) and feed rate (f) determine the curvature of chips in Inconel 718 turning. At low cutting speeds (25–50 m/min), experimental observations (Fig. 11) demonstrate that coarse serrations are present and strain hardening controlled deformation results in loosely curled chips with irregular pitch and high curl radii66. The behavior is in line with the observations offered by Sela et al67., who observed that low thermal softening rates at low velocities inhibit the ductility of the material, and thus, constrain tight bending. With increased cutting speed (62.5–75 m/min) the tool-chip friction coefficient increases (resulting in increased interface temperatures) establishing a large thermal gradient which locally softens the work material and lowers its yield strength at the rake face, thereby facilitating tighter chip curl57,65. High speed tests led to closely coiled helical chips of substantially smaller curl radii, thus validating the results of Rakesh and Datta41, which indicate that thermal softening dominates strain hardening at higher cutting speeds.

Chips produced under various cutting conditions.

Feed rate also has an impact on chip curling as it affects undeformed chip thickness. Lower feeds (0.05–0.10 mm/rev) allow less undeformed chip material to bend readily, leading to observable curling and tight helical forms63. On the contrary, when feeds are heavier (0.15–0.20 mm/rev), the chip cross-section is increased, compressive stresses in the chip are increased causing the chip to be stiffer against bending and to have more open, less curled chips66. This trend is consistent with the conclusion of Cook et al. and Jiang et al. who reported that high feed limits chip curl68,69.

The combined effect of cutting speed and feed rate influences the overall chip shape and curl behavior. A high cutting speed with a low feed rate usually produces tightly curled chips, which helps with better chip removal and reduces the risk of tool clogging. On the other hand, low cutting speed and high feed rate give rise to loosely curled chips and the likelihood of tool entanglement and wear is higher57.

Integrated energy-morphology mapping of chip curling behavior

A key result of the combined energy-morphology study is the clear alignment between the chip curl radius and areas of energy use on the SCE map. The curling behavior of Inconel 718 chips changes systematically with both cutting speed (Vc) and feed rate (f), and these morphological changes directly correspond to zones of low, moderate, and high SCE.

At low cutting speeds (25–50 m/min) and low feeds (0.05 mm/rev), chips tend to curl loosely due to strain-hardening effects and limited thermal softening; these conditions coincide with the highest SCE values (3.29–3.39 J/mm3) on the map, indicating the substantial mechanical work needed to shear the material. As the cutting speed increases at the same feed, SCE decreases quickly (for example, dropping to 2.26 J/mm3 at 62.5 m/min and approximately 2.05 J/mm3 by 75 m/min). The chip curl also tightens, reflecting increased adiabatic heating and thermal softening, which both lower flow stress and allow ductile bending of the chip near the rake face. Similarly, increasing the feed rate from 0.05 to 0.15 mm/rev changes the chip from a helical, tightly coiled form to a thicker, less-curled shape, mirroring the transition from low-energy to moderate-energy zones on the map. In the high-feed, high-speed region (Vc = 75 m/min, f = 0.15–0.2 mm/rev), the smallest SCE occurs with fine, evenly coiled chips, showing optimal thermal assistance and minimal mechanical resistance. By overlaying chip curl on the contour plot, we see that tight curling consistently relates to the low-energy region (SCE ≤ 2.0 J/mm3), while open curling appears in the high-energy ridge (SCE ≥ 3.0 J/mm3).

Chip segmentation frequency (CSF) during Inconel 718 machining

Chip segmentation, the cyclic formation of serrated, saw-tooth structures along the chip as shown in Fig. 12, is a defining feature of machining nickel-based superalloys like Inconel 718, which have high strength, a tendency to work-harden, and poor thermal conductivity that promote thermo-mechanical instabilities in the primary shear zone63. Chip segmentation occurs when localized heating from intense plastic deformation exceeds heat dissipation, leading to alternating bands of thermal softening and strain hardening that trigger discrete shear failures63,70. As the tool moves forward, material in the shear plane first work-hardens and then, once reaching a critical temperature, softens suddenly, forming an adiabatic shear band that acts as a pivot for chip detachment15. This process of strain localization and crack propagation continues downstream, giving Inconel 718 chips its typical segmented appearance35,71. This basic knowledge of the process of obtaining chip segmentation gives the necessary foundation to the measure of the rate of segmentation through chip segmentation frequency.

Key features of the chip cross-section.

Chip segmentation frequency refers to the periodic occurrence of segmented chips during machining and identified by alternating regions of high shear deformation (shear bands) and relatively undeformed material72. It is measured as the number of segmentation events per unit time (Hz) and is influenced by cutting parameters, material properties, and thermo-mechanical instabilities. CSF is one of the important parameter that characterizes the chip formation process with a direct influence on tool wear, surface finish, and process stability37. A critical understanding of the relationship between the CSF and machining characteristics like cutting speed (Vc) and feed rate (f) is crucial in optimizing the machining of Inconel 718 since they control energy dissipation, chip evacuation and thermal effects which affect tool performance. A number of mathematical models have been proposed to measure CSF, which is a combination of both geometric and kinematic principles. The CSF in this study is calculated using the model proposed by Dong et al73. and it is based on cutting kinematics and chip geometry.

Parametric effect of cutting parameters on chip segmentation frequency

The measured chip segmentation frequencies (CSF) for Inconel 718 turning (summarized in Table 4) indicate nonlinear dependence on both the cutting speed (Vc) and feed rate (f). These trends represent the competing influences of strain localization, thermal softening and chip thickness effects on sawtooth formation. Figure 13(a) shows that the change in CSF with cutting speed is nonlinear. CSF remains comparatively low at low speeds (Vc = 25–37.5 m/min), between 1922 Hz at f = 0.05 mm/rev and 2423 Hz at f = 0.2 mm/rev. This behavior is associated with stable shear localization and small-scale segmentation since the machining at these speeds does not encourage substantial thermal softening74. The segmentation pattern obtained is consistent with the findings of Qibiao where low speeds led to stable shear zones and infrequent serrations75. Likewise, Parida et al. have reported that Inconel 718 at low speeds (40–60 m/min) demonstrated relatively low CSF because of the lack of thermal softening to initiate shear-band instabilities76. With the increase in the cutting speed to Vc = 50–62.5 m/min, CSF increases significantly, to 6021 Hz at f = 0.05 mm/rev and 9615 Hz at f = 0.1 mm/rev. This increase in CSF is related to a reduction in serrated tooth pitch (Fig. 13(b)), indicating more frequent segmentation caused by increased plastic deformation77. This behavior is in line with the results of Behera et al78., who have demonstrated that segmentation frequency of Inconel 718 turning is a direct function of speed and proportional to cyclic changes in cutting-force. Pawade and Joshi, also reported a continuous increase in CSF until 75 m/min, due to increase in adiabatic shear-band formation as thermal softening starts to dominate57. Lorentzon et al79. also confirmed that serration pitch reduces with speed, thereby enhancing tooth density and CSF. CSF is peaked at high speeds (Vc = 75 m/min) and has a value of 11,805 Hz at f = 0.05 mm/rev and 17,836 Hz at f = 0.15 mm/rev. This is in line with the findings of Upadhyay on Ti-6Al-4 V, which showed that increased velocities contribute to shear localization instabilities and increase serration frequencies74. Similar CSF peaks during turning of Inconel 718 using coated-tool up to 100 m/min were found by Rakesh and Datta, demonstrating the general nature of the behavior with different tool materials41. However, the standard deviation at high speeds increases, indicating that severe tool wear and coating degradation under extreme thermal-mechanical loads introduce inconsistency in segmentation59.

At low feed rates (f = 0.05 mm/rev), the CSF is maintained in the moderate range (1,922 − 11,805 Hz) due to thin undeformed chip that restrains shear-band activation. With the increase of feed to 0.1–0.15 mm/rev, the CSF increase is rapid and the maximum values go to 17,836 Hz at Vc = 75 m/min. Such an increase is attributed to the increasing undeformed chip thickness, which increases strain localization and shear instability, leading to rise in segmentation frequencies80. With increased feed rate (f = 0.2 mm/rev), CSF reduces slightly with a value of approximately 15,489 Hz at Vc = 75 m/min. This decrease is due to the fact that high material removal rates may impair the efficiency of segmentation due to increased chip thickness and reduced frequency of shear band formation. Moreover, an increase in feed rate facilitates adhesion of the tool-workpiece and accumulation of edge (BUE), disrupting periodic segmentation and reducing CSF40.

The combined effect of cutting speed and feed rate determines the overall segmentation pattern. Optimal machining is observed at Vc = 62.5 m/min and f = 0.1–0.15 mm/rev where CSF remains at a level of (17,500 Hz) and segmentation pitch is controlled. On the other hand, at lower speeds (Vc = 25–37.5 m/min) with higher feed rates (f = 0.2 mm/rev), segmentation frequency drops because chip formation cycles are longer, increasing the chance of irregular chip shapes.

Integrated energy-morphology mapping of chip segmentation frequency

By co-plotting the chip segmentation frequency (CSF) heat map onto the specific cutting energy (SCE) map (Fig. 14), a clear inverse relationship appears between segmentation dynamics and energy consumption. The SCE map highlights a strong high-energy band at the thinnest-chip conditions (f = 0.05 mm/rev) for low–mid speeds (Vc = 25–50 m/min): for example, (25, 0.05) records SCE = 3.29 J/mm³ with CSF = 1.9 kHz, (37.5, 0.05) SCE = 3.32 J/mm3 with CSF = 4.3 kHz, and (50, 0.05) SCE = 3.39 J/mm3 with CSF = 6.0 kHz. These paired observations show that when mechanical resistance (strain-hardening and frictional work) dominates the primary shear zone, serration pitch is large and segmentation events are infrequent, so more mechanical work per unit volume is needed and SCE increases57. As the process moves toward higher speeds and/or thicker undeformed chips, the map shifts into a low-energy regime where CSF rises sharply. At the same feed rate (f = 0.05 mm/rev), increasing speed reduces SCE (for example, to 2.26 J/mm3 at 62.5 m/min and to 2.05 J/mm³ at 75 m/min), while CSF increases (8.1 kHz at 62.5 m/min and 11.8 kHz at 75 m/min). This trend correlates with the development of adiabatic shear localization and thermally-assisted softening, where increased strain rates and increased plastic work elevate the temperature in shear bands, reducing the flow stress, and reducing the serration wavelength, increasing the frequency of segmentation and reducing the energy per unit volume78. The same effect is seen at higher feeds: at f = 0.15 mm/rev, the energy is low (SCE = 1.80 J/mm3 at Vc = 50 m/min) and the CSF is high (7.2 kHz). Also, at (62.5, 0.15), SCE = 1.75 J/mm3 with CSF = 11.0 kHz showing fine, rapid serration under thermally assisted cutting58,73. Intermediate energy levels (SCE = 2.0–3.0 J/mm3) indicate transitional segmentation states: CSF values in this range are neither very low nor at the top, but reflect evolving serration geometry as the balance between strain-hardening and thermal softening shifts. For example, (50, 0.10) records SCE = 2.64 J/mm3 with CSF = 6.6 kHz, while (62.5, 0.10) shows SCE = 2.33 J/mm3 with CSF = 9.6 kHz, illustrating continuous movement from coarse to fine segmentation across the SCE contours. These observations support the idea that segmentation frequency mainly depends on the local thermomechanical state of the primary shear zone: when plastic work is quickly converted to heat and not removed fast enough, serration spacing decreases and CSF increases while SCE decreases57. This energy–morphology framework connects fundamental chip‐formation mechanisms, measured by CSF, to overall energy costs, offering a basis for choosing Vc–f settings that minimize specific cutting energy while keeping chip shape under control.

Shear band thickness during Inconel 718 machining

Shear bands are thin strips of intense plastic deformation that build up as a result of thermo-mechanical instability in machining, particularly in alloys that are difficult to machine such as Inconel 718. These bands develop in serrated chips, whereby successive areas of high strain and heat concentration produce localized softening, allowing a flow of material36. The shear band thickness is one of the factors that determine the shape of chips, tool wear and energy loss in the machining process71. Shear bands thickness is a critical factor that needs to be known in order to optimize the machining conditions to balance the chip segmentation, thermal stability and tool life.

Parametric effect of cutting parameters on shear band thickness

The shear band thickness experimental results under various cutting speeds (Vc) and feed rates (f) reveal some explicit trends associated with the thermo-mechanical interactions during machining of Inconel 718 (Fig. 15). The data indicate that shear band thickness reduces with the increase in cutting speed and feed rate, which is also supported by Behera et al78., who noticed that adiabatic bands become narrow with increased speed because of the greater strain-rate localization and reduced heat diffusion time. At lower cutting speeds (Vc = 25 m/min), shear band thickness is relatively high, measuring 78 ± 2.92 μm for f = 0.05 mm/rev. As the cutting speed increases to 75 m/min, the shear band thickness decreases to 48 ± 2.86 μm for the same feed rate indicating the rapid temperature increase limiting the heat affected zone. This phenomenon was demonstrated by Wright and Ockendon81who showed shear band narrowing as thermal softening surpasses strain hardening. Similar patterns are evident at all feed rates. For example, at f = 0.2 mm/rev, the thickness decreases from 43 ± 3.56 μm (Vc = 25 m/min) to 17 ± 6.07 μm (Vc = 75 m/min). These findings are in agreement with those of Behera et al78. who showed the presence of transformed shear bands by recrystallization (above 800 °C) under higher machining speeds, leading to thinner shear bands.

Effect of cutting speed and feed rate on shear band thickness.

Feed rate has an inverse relationship with shear band thickness. At Vc = 25 m/min, by increasing the feed from 0.05 to 0.2 mm/rev, thickness decreases from 78 ± 2.92 μm to 43 ± 3.56 μm. Increases in feed rate lead to higher strain rates, which intensify the localization of the shear zone and concentrate the deformation in thinner bands82. This effect is more noticeable at higher speeds. For instance, at Vc = 75 m/min, shear band thickness drops from 48 ± 2.86 μm (f = 0.05 mm/rev) to 17 ± 6.07 μm (f = 0.2 mm/rev).

The combined effects of high speed and feed produce the thinnest bands, measuring 17 ± 6.07 μm, at Vc = 75 m/min and f = 0.20 mm/rev, conditions that maximize adiabatic heating and strain localization. On the contrary, at lower speeds (Vc ≤ 37.5 m/min) with high feeds (f ≥ 0.15 mm/rev), thicker bands such as 34 ± 3.05 μm at Vc = 37.5 m/min and f = 0.2 mm/rev are formed, indicating the delayed occurrence of thermal softening and irregular shear band evolution78.

Integrated energy-morphology mapping of shear band thickness

By overlaying the shear-band thickness measurements onto the specific cutting energy (SCE) contour map (Fig. 16), a clear consistent picture emerges that links the thermomechanical state of the primary shear zone to the unit energy needed for cutting. Regions with the highest SCE values (above approximately 3.0 J/mm3), particularly at low speeds (Vc ≤ 37.5 m/min) and low feeds (f = 0.05 mm/rev), coincide with the thickest shear bands (70–78 μm). This co-occurrence indicates that when deformation is mainly driven by strain hardening and frictional resistance (i.e., thermal assistance is weak), the shear zone widens, dislocation accumulation increases, and more material must be plastically worked per segmentation event, which raises the specific cutting energy83. Conversely, the lowest-energy regions (SCE < 2.0 J/mm3), occurring at higher speeds (Vc ≥ 62.5 m/min) and higher feeds (f ≥ 0.15 mm/rev), correspond to the narrowest shear bands (17–24 μm). Such results are characteristic of adiabatic shear regime, rapid plastic work produces intense and localized heating that softens material in a narrow band, promotes dynamic recrystallization and reduces flow stress, resulting in lower energy consumption per unit of volume removed73. Intermediate SCE values (2.0–3.0 J/mm³) align with moderate shear band thickness (34–55 μm), indicating a transitional regime where the balance between strain hardening and thermal softening shifts gradually with speed and feed. This relationship not only supports established thermal-mechanical models of serrated chip formation but also provides a practical tool for selecting Vc-f conditions that minimize SCE.

Equivalent chip thickness during Inconel 718 machining

Equivalent chip thickness (te) is a key parameter for understanding chip formation dynamics and tool-workpiece interactions. Unlike the undeformed chip thickness, which is equal to the feed per revolution, te measures the effective thickness of the chip formed under various cutting conditions. By combining the maximum and minimum chip thickness measured in a serrated segment, this parameter gives a single representative value that is relates the amount of plastic deformation, energy dissipation and heat generation at primary shear zone84.

In machining process chip thickness is not uniform owing to the dynamic nature of material deformation. A variety of models are available for determining te, from energy-based models to empirical correlations85. However, in this study geometric model is used, which takes into account the variation in chip thickness. Parametric effects of cutting parameters on equivalent chip thickness

Equivalent chip thickness (te) was quantified in this work through digital microscopy by obtaining t1 and t2 through chip cross-sections (Fig. 12). The response of te to the cutting speed (Vc) has a distinct negative trend as depicted in Fig. 17. At lower speeds (Vc = 25–37.5 m/min), equivalent chip thickness is relatively high, ranging from 135 μm (f = 0.05 mm/rev) to 234 μm (f = 0.20 mm/rev). As Vc increases into the mid-range (45–62.5 m/min), te declines, for example, at f = 0.10 mm/rev, te falls from 165 μm at 37.5 m/min to 120 μm at 62.5 m/min. This decrease is an indicator of increased thermal softening, which reduces shear strength and facilitates easier chip flow, and a higher velocity of chip slip, which reduces the area of sticking and shortens te. At high speed Vc = 75 m/min, the equivalent chip thickness is reduced to a minimum of 71 μm at f = 0.05 mm/rev and 180 μm at f = 0.2 mm/rev. This tendency is basically caused by the synergistic effect of thermal softening and strain rate increase that together lead to reduced cutting forces and finer the chip segmentation. These trends have also been reported by Rakesh and Datta41 which again confirms these findings.

Effect of cutting speed and feed rate on equivalent chip thickness.

The feed rate (f) similarly also influences te. At f = 0.05 mm/rev, te stays moderate (85–135 μm) because the thin, undeformed chip reduces stress and tool-chip contact. When f increases to 0.10–0.15 mm/rev, te rises significantly, reaching up to 245 μm at Vc = 37.5 m/min and f = 0.15 mm/rev since thicker chips increase frictional dissipation, especially at lower speeds where thermal softening is limited59. At the highest feed rate (f = 0.20 mm/rev), te reaches its maximum at 234 μm at Vc = 25 m/min then drops to 180 μm at Vc = 75 m/min, as higher speeds promote thermal softening which counters the feed-induced growth in thickness.

The combined effect of high speed (75 m/min) and high feed (0.20 mm/rev) results in te = 180 μm, demonstrating the competing influences of increased shear strain and accelerated thermal softening. Conversely, low speed with high feed yields te = 234 μm because of extended frictional contact and reduced heat assistance. These findings confirm that an optimal range at Vc = 62.5–75 m/min, f = 0.10–0.15 mm/rev minimizes te, thereby enhancing machining stability and tool life.

Integrated energy-morphology mapping of equivalent chip thickness

By superimposing equivalent chip thickness (tₑ) contours onto the SCE map (Fig. 18), a clear pattern appears: regions of high specific cutting energy are located with the thickest chips, while the lowest SCE values match with thin, easily evacuated chips. The high-energy ridge on the updated map (SCE ≥ 3 J/mm³), mainly found at low to mid cutting speeds (around 25–55 m/min) and mainly with thin-chip feeds (f ≤ 0.075 mm/rev), corresponds to the largest tₑ values measured in this study (usually > 120 μm and reaching up to 200–245 μm in some feed–speed combinations). This correlation makes physical sense: under these conditions, thermal softening is limited, strain-hardening and frictional resistance in the primary shear zone remain high, and the tool must do more plastic work each segmentation. Since SCE is the ratio of cutting power to MRR (SCE = Pcut/MRR), larger tₑ increases the instantaneous cutting force and consequently the cutting power at a given Vc and f, resulting in a higher SCE for the same volume removal rate86. Conversely, the broad low-energy trough on the map (SCE ≤ 2.0 J/mm³), found at higher speeds (≥ 62.5 m/min) and moderate to high feeds (f = 0.10–0.20 mm/rev), coincides with noticeably thinner chips (tₑ typically < 120 μm, and in some of the thinnest cases, well below 100 μm). These conditions favor adiabatic shear localization: rapid plastic work raises the local temperature within the shear band, causing thermal softening that lowers the flow stress, allowing the chip to separate more cleanly into thin segments that are evacuated with less friction. The net effect is a reduction in cutting power relative to the MRR (or, more volume removed per unit power), leading to the observed low SCE60,78. Moderate SCE levels (2–3 J/mm3) occupy the transitional zone between these extremes and are associated with moderate equivalent chip thicknesses (120–200 μm). In this range, the balance between mechanical work and thermal effects shifts gradually with Vc and f: small increases in feed raise tₑ (and MRR) but also increase plastic dissipation and local heating, which can either raise or lower SCE depending on whether the cutting power or MRR changes more. This helps explain why some moderate-feed, moderate-speed combinations show similar SCE despite different tₑ values. The higher MRR at increased feeds tends to lower SCE, while the rising cutting force tends to increase it, creating the intermediate contours observed.

SCE contours overlaid on equivalent chip thickness heat-map.

Overall, the integrated tₑ-SCE mapping indicates that choosing energy-efficient turning windows for Inconel 718 involves selecting paired Vc-f combinations that generate desirable chip geometry (thin, segmented chips) while achieving acceptable MRR. For instance, shifting from a low-speed, thin-chip setting (high tₑ and high SCE) to a moderate-speed, moderate-feed range decreases tₑ through adiabatic effects and boosts MRR, leading to a significant reduction in SCE. These findings align with previous research on chip formation and cutting energetics in nickel-base superalloys and offer a practical, contour-based method for selecting Vc-f windows that minimize energy per volume removed, while ensuring good chip control and productivity.

Analysis of the included angle in serrated chips during Inconel 718 machining

The included angle (θ) of serrated chips, which is the angle between two consecutive shear bands on the chip free surface, is an important parameter used to characterize the thermo-mechanical response during machining process. This parameter is strongly influenced by machining conditions, in particular cutting speed (Vc) and feed rate (f), since these will affect the extent of localization of strain and thermal softening in the shear zones. The influence of cutting speed and feed rate on the included angle in the turning process of Inconel 718 is analysed on the basis of experimental results as shown in Fig. 19.

Effect of cutting speed and feed rate on included angle.

Parametric effect of cutting parameters on included angle

Cutting speed is an important factor to influence the included angle (θ) of serrated chips. At low speeds (Vc = 25–37.5 m/min), limited heat production limits thermal softening and therefore, the value of θ remains small (41°–50° at f = 0.05 mm/rev) because workpiece strength dominates deformation. In the mid-speed range (Vc = 45–62.5 m/min), θ increases more rapidly (46.4°–57° at Vc = 50 m/min and up to 67.8° at 62.5 m/min). This trend is consistent with the results of Joshi et al87. who found that higher included angles corresponded with a decrease in material strength due to the heat build-up. At higher speeds (75 m/min), the value of the included angle attains a maximum between 55.2° and 78.2° driven by severe thermal gradients that increase adiabatic shear localization and result in narrow shear bands. Upadhyay et al74. also found that the tooth included angle increased continuously with the cutting speed for Ti-6Al-4 V, which was attributed to the increase of the shear strain rates in serrated segments.

Feed rate also has an effect on θ as it influences undeformed chip thickness and mechanical loading. At low feed rates (0.05 mm/rev), θ stays modest (41°−61.8°), while moderate feeds (0.10–0.15 mm/rev) increase θ. For example, at Vc = 50 m/min, the value of theta increases from 46.4° to 57° when the f increases from 0.05 to 0.15 mm/rev due to the combined thermal and mechanical softening effects84. Rakesh and Datta41 also observed that thicker chips at low shear angles (small θ) require more deformation energy, whereas thinner chips at high shear angle (large θ) indicate efficient shear dominated cutting. At high feeds (≥ 0.20 mm/rev), the mechanical effects start to overcome the thermal softening and the measured value of θ approaches its maximum value of 78.2° at Vc = 75 m/min, f = 0.20 mm/rev.

The combined effect of cutting speed and feed rate shows a complex relationship where both thermal and mechanical factors influence the evolution of the included angle. At high speed and high feed (Vc = 75 m/min, f = 0.20 mm/rev), the synergy between thermal softening and mechanical strain maximizes θ. At low speed and high feed (Vc = 37.5 m/min, f = 0.20 mm/rev), mechanical stress has the dominant influence, resulting in a moderate θ range. Limited heat buildup preserves material strength, but high feed-induced deformation causes micro-cracks, restricting serration growth88.

Integrated energy-morphology mapping of the included angle

Overlaying the experimentally measured included angles onto the SCE contour reveals a meaningful relationship (Fig. 20): conditions requiring the highest specific cutting energy are those with the smallest included angles, while the largest included angles align with the lowest SCE values. In the SCE map, the high-energy region (SCE ≥ 3.0 J/mm3) is concentrated at low-to-moderate cutting speeds and thin chips (for example, Vc = 25–55 m/min at f = 0.05–0.075 mm/rev). At a typical point on this ridge, Vc = 50 m/min, f = 0.05 mm/rev, SCE reaches 3.39 J/mm3 while the included angle is relatively small (θ = 46.4°). This co-location supports the idea that under these conditions, deformation is mainly strain-hardening dominated: the primary shear zone stays wide, and the chip must absorb significant plastic work before segmentation, increasing cutting resistance and producing relatively blunt, shallow serrations35. Conversely, low-energy trough of the map (SCE ≤ 2.0 J/mm3) is found at higher cutting speeds with moderate and large feeds (for example, Vc ≥ 62.5 m/min and f ≥ 0.10–0.15 mm/rev). When Vc = 75 m/min and f = 0.20 mm/rev, the SCE decreases to approximately 1.79 J/mm3 and the included angle attains the highest measured values (θ = 78.2°). Such conditions facilitate a rapid adiabatic heating in the primary shear zone, thermal softening and localized shear which results in narrow shear bands and a strong serration shape (large θ) that enables the material to shear more easily at low flow stress and minimum energy per volume removed60. The intermediate SCE (2.0–3.0 J/mm3) values fall within the transition zone of the contour, with included angles between about 50° to 65°.

The co-registration of included-angle and SCE data thus offers a practical map: small θ associated with high SCE indicates inefficient, strain-dominated cutting (which should be avoided for energy-sensitive operations), while large θ paired with low SCE highlights windows where adiabatic softening enhances cutting efficiency and chip control. However, it should be noted that tool condition (wear, edge radius, coating state) and transient phenomena (e.g., BUE formation) can shift the exact Vc-f locations of these regimes; therefore, the integrated map is best used as a decision-making tool alongside wear and surface integrity information.

Chip compression ratio (CCR) during Inconel 718 machining

The chip compression ratio (CCR), also referred to as the chip reduction coefficient, is a fundamental parameter in machining that quantifies the degree of plastic deformation occurring during chip formation. It is defined as the ratio of deformed chip thickness (tc) to the undeformed chip thickness (t)89. Parametric effect of cutting speed on chip compression ratio

Cutting speed (Vc) significantly influences the chip compression ratio (CCR) by affecting shear-zone behavior and promoting thermal softening. Figure 21 shows that at low speeds (25–37.5 m/min), CCR stays relatively high, indicating mainly mechanical deformation. For example, at Vc = 25 m/min, CCR reaches 2.9 at f = 0.05 mm/rev and 2.6 at f = 0.10 mm/rev, showing severe material compression with limited heat generation90. As speed increases to 50–62.5 m/min, CCR decreases; for instance, at Vc = 50 m/min, CCR drops to 2.56 (f = 0.05 mm/rev) and 2.02 (f = 0.10 mm/rev), due to enhanced thermal softening that helps facilitate material flow and smoother chip detachment89. At the higher speed (75 m/min), CCR reaches its lowest values of 1.51 at f = 0.05 mm/rev and 1.24 at f = 0.10 mm/rev, indicating increased shear efficiency and workpiece softening. Parida76 observed that, during hot-assisted turning of Inconel 718, increasing speed from 40 to 100 m/min reduced cutting forces by 45–50%, reflecting the same thermal softening that lowers CCR. Rakesh & Datta41 also confirmed that higher speeds decrease cutting-force magnitudes by reducing bond energy in the crystal lattice, an effect similar to CCR reduction.

Effect of cutting speed and feed rate on chip compression ratio.

Feed rate (f) also significantly influences CCR through its effect on undeformed chip thickness. At low feeds (0.05 mm/rev), CCR remains high (from 2.87 to 1.51 as speed increases from 25 to 75 m/min), because thin chips concentrate compression. As feed increases to 0.10–0.15 mm/rev, CCR decreases (e.g., from 2.49 at f = 0.05 to 2.15 at f = 0.10 at Vc = 37.5 m/min) due to a higher shear-plane angle and less chip compression91. At the highest feed (0.20 mm/rev), CCR drops to its lowest (1.17–1.83), showing combined effects of shear localization and thermal softening under high Vc-f conditions62. Similar behavior is seen by Anurag et al92. during the turning of titanium alloy. Liao et al93. also found that, in end-milling Inconel 718, reductions in cutting force caused by thermal softening were linked to decreased chip compression, highlighting the universal nature of CCR behavior across different machining methods.

Overall, the combined Vc-f influence indicates that high-speed, high-feed conditions (≥ 62.5 m/min, ≥ 0.15 mm/rev) reduce CCR (1.2–1.6), producing highly segmented chips, while low-speed, low-feed settings (≤ 37.5 m/min, ≤ 0.10 mm/rev) lead to high CCR (2.5–2.9), reflecting less efficient shearing and increased cutting forces89,90.

Integrated energy-morphology mapping of chip compression ratio

Overlaying the measured chip compression ratios (CCR) onto the SCE contour (Fig. 22) shows a clear, physically consistent link between the level of chip compression in the primary shear zone and the specific cutting energy used to remove a unit volume of Inconel 718. The high-energy ridge of the SCE surface (SCE ≥ 3.0 J/mm³), which appears at low-to-mid cutting speeds with thin chips (for example, Vc = 25–50 m/min at f = 0.05–0.075 mm/rev where SCE = 3.29–3.39 J/mm3), matches the highest CCR values in the dataset (e.g., CCR = 2.7 at 25 m/min, f = 0.05; CCR = 2.3 at 37.5 m/min, f = 0.05). These high compression ratios indicate significant plastic compression of the chip and align with a low shear angle and inefficient deformation in the primary shear zone; as a result, more of the input power is used for plastic work, increasing the SCE37. Conversely, the SCE troughs (SCE ≤ 2.0 J/mm³), which develop at higher speeds and moderate-to-high feeds (for example, Vc ≥ 62.5 m/min with f ≥ 0.15–0.20 mm/rev where SCE drops to ~ 1.75–1.90 J/mm³), correspond to the lowest CCR values in the data (e.g., CCR = 1.04 at 75 m/min, f = 0.15; CCR = 0.90 at 75 m/min, f = 0.20; CCR = 0.98 at 62.5 m/min, f = 0.20). These conditions support larger shear angles and greater thermal softening in the primary shear band, which reduces chip compression and the amount of plastic work per volume; this results in lower SCE and easier chip evacuation59. Moderate SCE values (2.0–3.0 J/mm³) match intermediate CCRs (around 1.6–2.0.6.0) and are found in the transition zone of the Vc-f plane, where the balance between strain-hardening and thermal softening mark the gradual shift from mechanical to thermally assisted chip formation.

SCE contours overlaid on a chip compression ratio heat map.

From a practical perspective, the CCR-SCE co-map provides a simple actionable guideline i.e., prefer Vc-f windows that produce CCR values below 1.5 when aiming to minimize specific cutting energy and improve chip form for evacuation and conversely, areas where CCR exceeds 2.0 should be avoided for energy sensitive production. These recommendations, however, must be balanced with tool life and surface integrity constraints, as the same high-speed, high-feed combinations that result in low CCR and SCE can accelerate other wear mechanisms if not properly managed.

Conclusions

This study established and validated an integrated energy‑morphology framework for sustainable turning of Inconel 718. Building upon prior energy mapping work on Ti‑6Al‑4 V and Al alloys, this work provides the first comprehensive specific cutting energy (SCE) contour map for Inconel 718 and the first systematic overlay of quantitative chip morphology metrics onto an energy map for a nickel‑based superalloy.

Through a two‑stage experimental approach across industrially relevant parameters (Vc = 25–75 m/min, f = 0.05–0.20 mm/rev), a high‑resolution SCE map was created, revealing a distinct ridge‑trough structure: a high‑energy ridge (SCE > 3.0 J/mm³) at low feeds and mid‑range speeds, a moderate‑energy band (2.0–3.0 J/mm³), and a wide low‑energy trough (SCE ≤ 2.0 J/mm³) at high speeds and feeds. This ridge‑trough pattern is more pronounced for Inconel 718 than for previously studied alloys, reflecting its unique thermomechanical behavior. The map serves as an intuitive visual decision‑making tool for identifying energy‑efficient machining windows.

Chip morphology was quantitatively characterized using five key metrics: segmentation frequency (CSF), shear‑band thickness (SBT), equivalent chip thickness (tₑ), chip compression ratio (CCR), and included angle (θ). Systematic superposition of these metrics onto the SCE contour map revealed, for the first time, quantitative thresholds linking chip formation mechanics to energy consumption. The low‑SCE zone consistently corresponded to thermally assisted shear localization features: CSF > 10 kHz, SBT < 24 μm, CCR < 1.2, tight curl, and θ > 60°. Conversely, the high‑SCE zone exhibited coarse serration, thick shear bands, high compression ratio, and poor curl, indicative of inefficient cutting.

These correlations define an optimum machining window at Vc = 62.5–75 m/min and f = 0.10–0.15 mm/rev, where SCE ≤ 2.0 J/mm³ and controlled fine serrations enhance chip evacuation and reduce cutting forces. This finding demonstrates that sustainability objectives (energy reduction) and classic machining objectives (chip control, stability) are not contradictory but can be achieved simultaneously through conscious parameter selection. From an industrial perspective, this window offers clear benefits in energy efficiency and chip management. However, tool wear progression and surface integrity were not within the scope of this study, which focused exclusively on energy consumption and chip morphology. Therefore, while the window is recommended for applications where energy minimization and chip control are primary objectives, users should independently evaluate tool life and surface quality for their specific production requirements.

By establishing the first energy‑morphology map for Inconel 718, this work fills a critical gap in the literature and provides a foundation for integrating energy efficiency with chip control in the machining of difficult‑to‑cut nickel‑based superalloys. The proposed framework extends past stand‑alone research on energy or chips to offer a shop‑floor‑ready structure for total process optimization, directly linking fundamental thermomechanical behavior to measurable energy outcomes.

Future work will extend this framework to other difficult‑to‑machine alloys (Waspaloy, Haynes, Rene, high‑strength steels) to assess generalizability. Coupling experimental energy‑morphology maps with finite element modeling will enable deeper insight into transient temperature, strain, and strain‑rate evolution within shear bands. Extending the methodology across coating types (e.g., PVD TiAlN, TiAlSiN) and cooling strategies (dry, MQL, cryogenic) will quantify their direct contributions to SCE and chip formation. Finally, a companion study on tool wear and tool‑chip contact length, conducted under the same cutting conditions, will integrate tribological insights with the present energy‑morphology framework for a complete understanding of thermomechanical interactions in sustainable machining of Inconel 718.
