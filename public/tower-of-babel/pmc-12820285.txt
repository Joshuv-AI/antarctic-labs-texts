A urinary microRNA aging clock accurately predicts biological age
Authors: Milos Havelka, Atsushi Satomura, Hiroki Yamaguchi, Akira Cortal, Yoriko Ando, Motoki Mikami, Mika Mizunuma, Yuki Ichikawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We aimed to develop and validate a urinary miRNA aging clock, positioning urine as a scalable, non-invasive aging-biomarker source. Using machine learning on 6331 adults, our clock achieved MAE ≈ 4.4 years (R² ≈ 0.79) in independent validation. The clock’s key biomarkers included well-established geromiRs miR-34a-5p, miR-31-5p, miR-146a-5p, and miR-155-5p. While slightly less accurate than DNA-methylation clocks, our model outperformed blood-based miRNA and mRNA clocks, underscoring urinary miRNAs as promising, truly non-invasive biomarkers of biological age and disease risk.

Introduction

Aging is the primary driver of many chronic diseases, yet reliable, non-invasive biomarkers of biological age, essential for personalised preventive medicine, remain limited. Aging clocks are biomarker-based models that estimate biological age from age-responsive features, with deviation from chronological age reflecting the pace of aging. Such clocks, pioneered by DNA methylation models1, are associated with morbidity and all-cause mortality risk and are used for health risk stratification and to evaluate interventions2. While DNA methylation and protein-coding expression are hallmarks of aging and senescence3, they capture only part of the regulatory landscape. MicroRNAs (miRNAs), key post-transcriptional regulators, have been linked to aging4 and age-related disorders5,6. miRNA-based clocks have been developed in blood7, plasma8,9, and skin10, often leveraging extracellular-vesicle miRNAs (EV-miRNAs)9. Beyond skin, solid tissues show reproducible age-associated miRNA shifts6,11,12, but validated tissue miRNA clocks remain scarce. Collecting blood or solid tissue samples requires invasive procedures conducted at medical facilities, similar to established DNA methylation clocks. By contrast, urine is non-invasive, inexpensive, and scalable source of EV-miRNAs13, yet remains underexplored for aging clocks. Urinary miRNAs show moderate overlap with blood miRNAs, consistent with transrenal passage, alongside compartment-specific differences14–16. Beyond expected urothelial/kidney indications17, urinary (EV) miRNAs demonstrated reliable diagnostic performance in ovarian15, esophageal18 and pancreatic19 cancers, underscoring utility beyond the urinary tract. Establishing urinary miRNAs as accessible aging biomarkers could markedly enhance personalised preventive approaches by facilitating widespread early interventions for aging-related conditions. Here, we aimed to develop and validate a urinary EV-miRNA (uEV-miRNA) aging clock in 6331 Japanese adults, interpret its top features against established aging-related miRNAs, and link ΔAge to common conditions, positioning uEV-miRNAs as scalable, non-invasive aging biomarkers. We first partitioned the cohort into a training set of 2400 participants and a test set 1 comprising 2840 participants, both derived from the same original sample batch, maintaining balanced sex representation in both groups (Table 1). Both subsets span the typical adult lifespan, though the distribution peaked in the 50–59-year age decade (Supplementary Fig. S1), an interval in which the prevalence of multimorbidity, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and other age-related disorders begins to rise steeply20. Additionally, we evaluated the robustness of our uEV-miRNA clock using an independent validation dataset (test set 2) consisting of 1091 participants from a distinct sample set, without balancing for age or sex, to further assess model generalisability (Table 1). Sequencing reads from NGS uEV-miRNA libraries of the cohort’s urine EV-enriched fraction were processed through our mapping and QC pipeline (Fig. 1a). Across the training and test datasets, mapping composition was stable (Table 2). uEV-miRNA reads comprised the largest fraction (≈50–60%), with relatively consistent contributions from tRNA, rRNA, tRFs, piRNA, and minor residual mRNA/hg19 (Supplementary Fig. S2). Our uEV-miRNA clock achieved a mean absolute error (MAE) of 5.1 ± 0.29 years and R² of 0.64 ± 0.05 (r ≈ 0.80), averaged across five cross-validation folds of the training set (Fig. 1b); an MAE of 4.5 years and R² of 0.80 (r ≈ 0.89) in the sex- and age-balanced test set 1 (Fig. 1c); and an MAE of 4.4 years and R² of 0.79 (r ≈ 0.89) in test set 2 (Fig. 1d). Across the top 20 miRNA features ranked by our model, mean expression levels were clearly age-associated (Jonckheere–Terpstra monotonic-trend tests, FDR < 0.05; Fig. 1e). Ten miRNAs increased with age in both sexes, four showed increases restricted to males, and six decreased with age; for miR-887-3p and miR-92a-3p, this decrease was female-specific (Fig. 1e; Supplementary Fig S3).

After identifying these key age-associated uEV-miRNA, we next examined their alignment with established biomarkers of aging. Showing strong cross-evidence concordance (Fig. 2a), most of the top-ranked miRNAs were significantly enriched in aging and cellular senescence literature (Fig. 2b). This set notably included canonical “geromiRs”: miR-34a-5p, miR-31-5p, miR-146a-5p, and miR-155-5p (Supplementary Table S1). To further elucidate the biological processes potentially regulated by the age-associated uEV-miRNAs identified in our study, we annotated the top 20 miRNAs from our model. Gene ontology (GO) analysis revealed significant enrichment of pathways closely related to aging and cellular senescence (Fig. 2c; Supplementary Table S2). Notably enriched GO terms included “regulation of osteoclast development” (GO2001204, adjusted p = 2.37e-11), “bone remodeling” (GO0046849, adjusted p = 8.17e-09), “marginal zone B cell differentiation” (GO0002315, adjusted p = 4.50e-08), and multiple pathways involving intrinsic apoptotic signalling and mitochondrial dysfunction (e.g. GO1902230, GO0051902; adjusted p = 4.50e-08). Among the six comorbidities we investigated for potential associations with altered ΔAge (Supplementary Fig. S4), only type 2 diabetes (T2D) exhibited a significantly elevated ΔAge. Our uEV-miRNA aging clock achieved MAE 4.3–5.2 years (R2 ≈ 0.62–0.80) across three datasets, approaching DNA-methylation benchmarks while remaining fully non-invasive and scalable. Compared with prior miRNA clocks, our accuracy is modestly higher than Lu et al.7 (whole blood; MAE = 4.08, R² = 0.925), but their sample size was small (n = 127) and lacked independent validation, increasing overfitting risk21. By contrast, our independent test set supports generalisability. The only comparably sized miRNA study (n = 5,012 peripheral-blood samples) reported r = 0.70 (R² = 0.49) in discovery and r = 0.65 (R² = 0.42) in replication5, and multimodal blood models (miRNA + circRNA: MAE = 6.84, r = 0.7722; proteins + miRNAs: R² = 0.7023) performed worse. Collectively, these results indicate uEV-miRNAs can outperform blood-based miRNA clocks, underscoring their promise as non-invasive biomarkers for biological-age estimation and age-related disease discovery, consistent with their diagnostic advantages over blood miRNAs in ovarian and pancreatic cancers15,19. Despite bias correction via age-residual interpolation, the performance of our model declined at age extremes due to (i) residual regression dilution, (ii) the non-Gaussian/skewed distribution of chronological age24, and (iii) sparse training data that increases extrapolation error. Accordingly, estimates for individuals <25 or >80 should be interpreted with caution and may be unsuitable for practical use.

Hydration, collection timing, diet, and activity introduce substantial variability in urine concentration, directly affecting the yield of the same assay uEV-miRNA abundances25. To minimise bias from low-density samples and stabilise uEV-miRNA profiles, we applied stringent feature-level QC, consistent with evidence that uEV-miRNA profiles are stable but counts scale with urine concentration25. All miRNAs in this study were quantified from the urine EV-enriched fraction (see Methods). While this approach prioritises throughput over ultrahigh purity, it provides reproducible recovery at cohort scale and matches the workflow previously validated and EV-characterised in our laboratory19. Given the pre-analytical uEV-miRNA counts variability, we assessed the clock’s stability across normalisation schemes. Despite modest accuracy declines for TMM and DESeq2 size-factor normalisations (MAE + 0.7–1.4 y; R² −0.08–0.12 across sexes; Supplementary Fig. S5a–f), predicted ages were highly concordant with CPM (Supplementary Fig. S5g–h) and the top-20 features were largely preserved (Supplementary Fig. S5i), supporting robustness of both predictions and feature rankings. The small performance drop likely reflects that TMM/DESeq2 use training-derived scaling factors that miscalibrate on held-out sets with subtle composition shifts, whereas CPM’s per-sample scaling is split-invariant.

Top-ranked uEV-miRNAs from our model included canonical geromiRs, which are consistently upregulated in senescent cells and aged tissues6, a pattern also observed in our urinary samples (Fig. 1e). Collectively, they implicate p53 signalling, the SASP, and NF-κB-driven inflammaging, a molecular signature of organismal aging4. Additional leading features aligned with tissue reports: miR-183-5p (bone EVs26), miR-23b-3p (hepatocytes/liver EVs27), the miR-195-5p/miR-497-5p cluster (vascular and skeletal muscle28,29), miR-486-5p (stem-cell pools, adipose30,31), and miR-9-5p (immunosenescent T cells32). These concordances support urine as a window into systemic aging biology. Furthermore, Gene-ontology analysis of the top-20 uEV-miRNAs showed significant enrichment for aging- and senescence-linked processes (Fig. 2c; Supplementary Table S2). This pathway signal reinforces the biological plausibility of our marker set and indicates that urinary miRNAs capture mechanisms of organismal aging. Several miRNAs with limited prior evidence: miR-887-3p, miR-375-3p, miR-95-3p, and miR-497-5p (Supplementary Table S1) merit follow-up to determine whether they are urine-specific aging markers or reflect indirect physiological changes. However, their clear age associations in at least one sex favour the former interpretations. Top features included several oncomiRs (miR-182-5p, miR-31-5p, miR-155-5p, miR-92a-3p) and miRNAs linked to benign urogenital disease (e.g., miR-146a-5p), highlighting overlap between aging and disease programs. Trained in a general-population cohort without adjudicated cancer status, our miRNA clock may yield inflated ΔAge in patients with active malignancy or urogenital pathology. Thus, uEV-miRNA inferred ΔAge should be best viewed as a general risk indicator, not a disease label. Clinical use will require disease-specific validation/recalibration.

Alterations in biological age due to various comorbidities have been well documented33. Observed elevation in ΔAge among participants with T2D highlighted the capability of uEV-miRNAs to reflect changes in biological age. Although all comorbidities were self-reported, introducing uncertainty regarding disease severity and status at the time of urine collection, the observed association aligns with accumulating evidence linking T2D to age-associated miRNA profiles34,35. In contrast, we did not detect significant ΔAge differences for other comorbidities examined (Supplementary Fig. S4), despite previous studies associating increased biological age with cardiovascular diseases36 and gastric conditions, including Helicobacter pylori infection37. This discrepancy may partly stem from participants reporting these conditions as medical history, meaning cardiovascular or gastric conditions may not have been actively present at the time of sampling, potentially masking associations with biological age.

Given the emerging potential of uEV-miRNAs as systemic aging biomarkers, we further examined whether miRNAs previously associated with dementia and Alzheimer’s disease could be detected in urinary samples across diverse age groups. Because participant-level cognitive status was unavailable, this analysis was not designed to assess disease-related deregulation; rather, it solely demonstrates detectability and age-binned distributions. Indeed, we observed the presence of miRNAs commonly reported in the context of dementia and Alzheimer’s disease within uEV-miRNA cargo (Supplementary Fig. S6). This preliminary observation suggests that uEV-miRNA profiling could potentially serve as a non-invasive approach to capturing systemic molecular signatures associated with dementia and Alzheimer’s disease, similar to blood-based miRNA approaches38. Future studies with direct cognitive assessments should be undertaken to assess the utility of uEV-miRNAs as markers for dementia risk and progression.

Our large-scale study, comparable in size to the most extensive methylation-based clock studies, established the first urinary miRNAs aging clock and uEV-miRNAs as robust, non-invasive biomarkers of biological age through rigorous validation of a machine-learning–based age-prediction model. The accuracy and practicality of uEV-miRNAs offer an accessible window into systemic ageing, allowing both precise age estimation and the assessment of disease-associated age acceleration. While state-of-the-art DNA-methylation clocks remain the benchmark, our uEV-miRNA clock significantly narrows this accuracy gap, matching or surpassing the performance of existing blood-based miRNA and mRNA clocks. The non-invasive nature and scalability of urine sampling further enhance its attractiveness as a biomarker source. Given their accessibility, specificity, and sensitivity to systemic ageing signatures, uEV-miRNAs represent a promising tool not only for biological age estimation but also for biomarker discovery in age-related diseases, facilitating their widespread application in geroscience-driven personalised medicine and preventive healthcare.

Methods

Urine samples were collected in Japan from March 2024 to May 2025 from individuals undergoing the miSignal Scan cancer-screening test, a commercially available assay that estimates the risk of seven cancer types based on urinary miRNA biomarkers. Information on age, sex at birth, body weight, body height, smoking status, frequency of exercise, weekly alcohol consumption, and self-reported comorbidities was collected through a standardised questionnaire provided as part of the service. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided broad, general informed consent for future research. The secondary use of their data in the present study was approved by Craif’s Institutional Review Board (IRB No. 03100032). An opt-out procedure was implemented and disclosed on the organization’s website.

Upon receipt, urine samples were frozen at −80 °C. Before processing, approximately 3.5 mL of urine was thawed at room temperature and centrifuged at 2000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C to remove cells and debris. The resulting supernatant (3 mL) was mixed at a 1:1 volume ratio with Total Exosome Isolation Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated at room temperature for 1 h with gentle shaking (100 rpm). Extracellular vesicles were then pelleted by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 1 h at 4 °C and subsequently resuspended in 300 µL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS).

RNA was extracted using the MagMAX™ mirVana™ Total RNA Isolation Kit on a KingFisher™ Apex System following the manufacturer’s protocol. The RNA was eluted in 30 µL of elution buffer and concentrated at 60 °C for 30–45 min using a centrifugal concentrator (Eppendorf). The final RNA eluate (5 µL) was prepared in nuclease-free water and stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

Small-RNA libraries were prepared from 5 µL of RNA using the QIAseq miRNA Library Kit (QIAGEN), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Library concentrations were quantified using the Qubit™ dsDNA HS Assay Kit on a Qubit Flex Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and stored at –20 °C. Equimolar libraries were pooled and sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 550 System with 75-nt single-end reads.

Data processing

Initially, raw sequencing reads were processed using UMI-tools39. Nucleotides corresponding to miRNA sequences upstream of the 3′ adapter were retained, and the 12-bp unique molecular identifier (UMI) immediately following the adapter was appended to each read identifier. Reads shorter than 19 bp after trimming were discarded. The remaining reads were aligned sequentially to human small-RNA references provided with miRge 3.040 in the following order: miRNA, tRFs, tRNA, piRNA, and rRNA, using Bowtie v1.2.341, with a 25-bp seed, zero mismatches, and reverse-complement mapping disabled. Reads not mapping to any small-RNA reference were then aligned to human mRNA and to the hg19 (GRCh37) genome using a 25-bp seed allowing one mismatch, with reverse-complement mapping enabled. Identical reads, defined by matching UMIs and alignment to the same reference, were collapsed and counted. Given that each urine sample was sequenced to approximately four million raw reads, and aiming for an optimal UMI duplication rate of approximately two to balance sensitivity with minimal PCR duplication artefacts, we set a filtering threshold of ≥100,000 total miRNA counts per sample. This threshold ensures sufficient sequencing depth, thereby capturing robust and representative miRNA profiles while effectively minimising technical noise. Additionally, we restricted analyses to miRNAs detectable in at least 50% of all samples to reduce the impact of rare or sporadically expressed miRNAs, ensuring our analysis focused on consistently measurable and biologically relevant signals.

Balanced training and test datasets were created using participant-level metadata and propensity score matching implemented in MatchIt v4.642. Initially, we performed a 1:1 nearest-neighbour matching on sex, conditioned on age (calliper = 0.1 SD), across the full dataset collected between March 2024 and April 2025. From this initial match, the first 2400 samples, balanced by sex, formed the training dataset (Supplementary Table S3). Subsequently, a second identical matching step was applied to the remaining records, resulting in a test dataset of 2840 matched individuals (test set 1), also balanced by sex, for external evaluation (Supplementary Table S4). To further validate the generalisability and robustness of our urinary miRNA clock, we assembled an additional independent dataset (test set 2) of 1091 participants without applying any balancing criteria for age or sex (Supplementary Table S5). This dataset was collected and processed independently from both the training set and test set 1 during May 2025, thereby ensuring its suitability for an unbiased evaluation of model performance.

Normalization strategies

For downstream analyses, we generated three normalisation variants of the same raw count matrix: (i) CPM (counts per million; per-sample library–size scaling); (ii) edgeR TMM-CPM (Trimmed Mean of M-values; composition-bias–corrected CPM); and (iii) DESeq2 size-factor normalised counts. For TMM, library size scaling factors were estimated with calcNormFactors(method = “TMM”) on the training set only and applied to all training samples to obtain TMM-normalised CPM values. For external datasets, to avoid cross-set information flow, test samples were normalised relative to a fixed training reference (the training sample with library size closest to the median), yielding TMM-CPM on the same scale without using test data to estimate factors. For DESeq2, size factors were estimated on the training set only and then applied to the test sets using training-derived per-gene geometric means. For comparability with CPM magnitudes, a per-sample post-scaling to “per-million equivalents” was computed from the size–factor–normalised counts when needed. All normalisation steps were designed to be leakage-safe (i.e., no test-set information was used to fit normalisation parameters).

Machine-learning modelling and validation

We modelled age from urinary miRNA expression using a LightGBM regressor implemented in scikit-learn 1.443, trained on count-per-million–normalised (CPM) read counts. To balance the age distribution, the training set was divided into five equal-frequency age quantiles, which served as strata for a nested cross-validation (CV) scheme. Nested CV was employed to rigorously separate hyperparameter optimisation from model validation, effectively minimising data leakage and ensuring unbiased performance estimates. A 5-fold stratified CV formed the outer loop, providing unbiased performance estimates. Each training fold underwent a five-fold hyperparameter search using Optuna v3.644, consisting of 100 trials with a tree-structured Parzen estimator (TPE) sampler and median pruning. Hyperparameter optimisation aimed to maximise mean R² by tuning learning rate, tree depth, leaf count, subsampling rate, column sampling, and L1/L2 regularisation.

In addition to the CPM-based model, we trained two additional LightGBM regressors on identically filtered features but differently normalised inputs: one on edgeR TMM-CPM and one on DESeq2 size-factor–normalised counts. Each variant used the same nested CV protocol (age-quantile stratification, Optuna hyperparameter search, and bias-correction procedure) as described for the CPM model. For each outer fold, any preprocessing that required fitting parameters was estimated exclusively on the fold’s training partition and then applied to its held-out partition to prevent data leakage.

Using optimal hyperparameters identified by the search, the model was refitted on each outer training fold for each normalisation (CPM, TMM-CPM, DESeq2 size-factor), including its fold-specific preprocessing and bias-correction learned on that fold’s training partition. Systematic bias was assessed by averaging prediction residuals at each integer age. These averaged residuals were interpolated to generate a continuous offset curve, which was then subtracted from raw predictions, following the approach of Zhang et al.45. Following training and internal validation, we evaluated each normalisation-specific model on two external datasets: test set 1 (n = 2,840) and test set 2 (n = 1091), collected and analysed independently. Raw predictions from these test sets were corrected using the age-offset curve derived from the training data separately for each normalisation, resulting in bias-corrected urinary miRNA ages. Sex-specific mean absolute errors (MAE) and R² values were calculated against chronological age. To assess agreement between normalisations, we compared predicted ages pairwise (CPM vs TMM; CPM vs DESeq2) on the combined test data using sex-stratified R². Finally, biological age acceleration (ΔAge) was defined as the difference between bias-corrected miRNA age and chronological age.

Feature ranking and enrichment analysis

We ranked the 407 miRNAs according to their LightGBM feature importance and selected the top 20 (~5%), corresponding to the 95th-percentile cutoff (α = 0.05 under a uniform null model), for subsequent biological interpretation. We first investigated age-related trends in these miRNAs using an outlier-robust, baseline-referenced approach. CPM normalised expression was transformed as log10(x + 1), and within each miRNA × sex stratum, values outside [Q1 − 1.5 × IQR, Q3 + 1.5 × IQR] on the log10 scale were removed. For each miRNA and sex, we computed the mean log10 expression in each age bin and expressed it as a fold-change relative to the 30–39 bin. Monotonic age trends were tested per miRNA and sex using the Jonckheere–Terpstra test on log10-transformed values across ordered age bins, with Benjamini–Hochberg correction across all tests (FDR < 0.05). Fold-changes were visualised across age bins as sex-stratified line plots, faceted by miRNA.

To visualise how feature rankings shift across normalisations, we generated CPM-anchored Δ-rank lollipop plots (Δ = rank_CPM − rank_method), highlighting movers and indicating whether the method-specific rank falls within or below the top-20 list.

To assess the representation of the top 20 miRNAs in aging-related literature, PubMed was queried using the terms (“microRNA*” OR “miRNA*”) AND (“aging” OR “age-related” OR “cellular senescence”) AND (human* OR mouse/mice), spanning MeSH terms and titles/abstracts (see Supplementary Code S1). Occurrences of each miRNA were counted using miRetrieve v1.3.446, collapsing isoforms to their common stem and summing abstract frequencies. We tested enrichment with a binomial test (null probability = 1/number of unique miRNAs), adjusting P values for multiple testing via the Benjamini–Hochberg method.

We then exploited the obtained PubMed records to binarily annotate each of the 20 miRNAs for sample-type and literature status: Tissue-based (age/senescence evidence in solid tissues or primary cells), Blood-based (circulation: plasma/serum/whole blood/EVs), Disease-based (age-related disorders such as AD, CVD, diabetes). Screening prioritised human adult studies, then human tissues/cells, then mammalian in vivo; conflicts were resolved by favouring larger/replicated studies and recent reviews. The resulting 0/1 matrix (miRNA × categories) was used to rank intersections by frequency and generate the UpSet visualization in R.

For functional annotation of the top 20 miRNAs, over-representation analysis was performed with miEAA 2.147, using miRTarBase48 as the reference background. The miRNAs were evaluated against Gene Ontology (GO) categories: “Biological Process,” “Molecular Function,” and “Cellular Component.” Enrichment P values were subsequently corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method.

Association of ΔAge with Comorbid Conditions

To investigate whether ΔAge is associated with common comorbidities, we binarised health conditions with more than 200 reported cases (type 2 diabetes, hypertension, chronic gastritis, H. pylori infection, heart disease, and periodontal disease). Cases were matched to controls of identical age and sex using nearest-neighbour propensity scores in MatchIt v4.642. Within each matched cohort, participants were stratified by sex and 10-year age bands. Differences in ΔAge between cases and controls were evaluated using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, with resulting P values adjusted for multiple comparisons via the Benjamini–Hochberg method. For visualisation, residuals were calculated by regressing ΔAge on chronological age to remove baseline age-dependent trend, and subtracting these predicted values from the observed ΔAge values. Thus, plotted residuals reflect deviations independent of chronological age, highlighting associations specifically attributable to the comorbidities examined.

Additionally, we explored whether miRNAs commonly linked to dementia, especially Alzheimer’s disease, were detectable in urinary miRNA cargo. Because participant cognitive status was not available, a direct comparison with ΔAge was not possible. Instead, a literature search was conducted for the most frequently reported dementia- and Alzheimer’s disease-related miRNAs using the PubMed query: (“microRNA*” OR “miRNA*”) AND (“Alzheimer’s disease” OR “dementia”) AND (human* OR mouse/mice), spanning MeSH terms, titles, and abstracts (Supplementary Code S2). The urinary expression levels of miRNAs corresponding to the top 5% most frequently reported in the literature were subsequently evaluated.
