Exploring local vegetables cultivated in home gardens of Pa Tio District, Yasothon Province, Thailand: ethnobotanical knowledge, nutritional value, and horticultural potential
Authors: Piyaporn Saensouk, Surapon Saensouk, Phiphat Sonthongphithak, Khamfa Chanthavongsa, Tammanoon Jitpromma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLocal vegetables are essential for food security, nutrition, and cultural practices in rural northeastern Thailand. Despite their importance, comprehensive documentation of their diversity, nutritional contributions, cultural significance, and economic value in Pa Tio District, Yasothon Province, is lacking. This study provides the first systematic account of edible plants in the district, highlighting their multifunctional roles in food, medicine, household nutrition, and local markets.MethodsEthnobotanical data were collected through semi-structured interviews with local informants, and species uses were categorized as food, spices, medicinal, or ornamental. Quantitative indices—including the Cultural Importance Index (CI), Informant Consensus Factor (Fic), Fidelity Level (FL), and Cultural Food Significance Index (CFSI)—were applied to assess species multifunctionality, frequency of use, and cultural prominence. Nutritional significance was evaluated using literature-reported mineral and vitamin contents, focusing on calcium, copper, iron, potassium, magnesium, manganese, sodium, phosphorus, zinc, and vitamin C. Economic assessments estimated annual income from marketable edible plants based on monthly prices, sales volume, and availability.ResultsA total of 73 species from 33 families were recorded, with Cucurbitaceae, Zingiberaceae, and Apiaceae being particularly species-rich. Multifunctional species such as Allium cepa L., A. sativum L., and Ocimum spp. were central to diets, medicine, and local markets. Leafy greens and aromatic herbs were the most frequently used, contributing substantially to micronutrient intake. Marketable species provided supplementary income, enhancing household resilience and supporting local livelihoods.ConclusionThis study highlights the nutritional, socio-ecological and economic importance of edible plants in Pa Tio District. Conserving plant diversity and indigenous knowledge is essential for sustaining household nutrition and food security. Future work should investigate nutrient bioavailability, phytochemical composition, and improved cultivation practices to strengthen sustainable use and horticultural development of these culturally important edible plants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13002-025-00841-w.Keywords: Ethnobotany, Homegardens, Local vegetables, Pa Tio district, Yasothon province

Background

Local vegetables—encompassing traditional varieties, semi-domesticated species, and long-established cultivated crops—represent a core component of food systems, household livelihoods, and biocultural heritage throughout Southeast Asia. In rural communities, these plants provide essential micronutrients, dietary fiber, and diverse bioactive compounds that contribute significantly to nutrition and health. Beyond their dietary roles, local vegetables are tightly interwoven with culinary identity, traditional ecological knowledge, and community-based resource management [1]. Home gardens, in particular, serve as living repositories of these plant resources, functioning as multifunctional agroecosystems where food production, medicinal plant cultivation, and cultural practices converge [2]. Their ecological resilience, low input requirements, and continuous year-round productivity make them vital for household food security, especially in regions characterized by seasonal climatic variability [3].

Across neighboring countries in mainland Southeast Asia—such as Laos, Vietnam, Cambodia, and Myanmar—numerous studies highlight the indispensable role of home gardens in conserving local plant diversity and supporting subsistence diets. In Laos, for example, home-garden systems maintain a rich assemblage of leafy greens, climbers, herbs, and wild-derived species that contribute substantially to household nutrition and income generation [4]. Similar patterns are reported from Vietnam, where traditional vegetables persist as key sources of vitamins, antioxidants, and culturally significant ingredients despite increasing dietary modernization [5]. In Cambodia, ethnobotanical surveys show that villagers continue to rely on home-garden vegetables for both food and traditional medicine, reflecting strong cultural continuity and a high degree of local ecological knowledge [6]. Myanmar’s rural communities likewise maintain diverse gardens that integrate food crops with medicinal and ritual plants, underscoring the multifunctionality of these systems across the region [7]. Collectively, these studies illustrate how home gardens serve as critical habitats for neglected and underutilized species (NUS), contributing to in situ conservation, resilience against food shortages, and the preservation of indigenous cultural practices [8].

Thailand shares many of these regional characteristics. Home gardens remain central to rural households, particularly in Northern and Northeastern (Isan) Thailand, where communities possess deep agricultural traditions and extensive ethnobotanical knowledge related to edible plants [9, 10]. However, rapid socio-economic change, urban migration, market integration, and shifts toward commercial crop varieties have begun to alter local food systems. These transitions threaten the continuity of traditional edible plants use and may lead to the gradual erosion of plant genetic resources [11]. Although several studies in other parts of Thailand have examined home-garden diversity and ethnobotanical practices, research focusing specifically on the integrated nutritional, cultural, and horticultural value of edible plants in Yasothon Province remains limited [12]. Such knowledge gaps hinder the development of strategies that support both sustainable food systems and biodiversity conservation.

Pa Tio District in Yasothon Province provides a compelling context in which to examine these issues. The district is recognized for its strong reliance on home gardens, cultural persistence in using edible plants for both food and traditional medicine, and community engagement in small-scale agriculture. These characteristics make Pa Tio an important case study for understanding how edible plants contribute simultaneously to diet, health, cultural identity, and ecosystem sustainability. Yet, to date, a systematic assessment of the diversity, ethnobotanical uses, and nutritional significance of home-garden edible plants in this region has not been conducted. Comparative insights from Laos, Cambodia, Vietnam, and Myanmar further highlight the importance of documenting such knowledge before it is lost, as these countries face similar pressures associated with modernization, market-oriented agriculture, and environmental change [13].

The present study addresses existing knowledge gaps by investigating the diversity, ethnobotanical uses, and nutritional and economic significance of edible plants cultivated in home gardens of Pa Tio District, Yasothon Province. Specifically, the study aims to:

(1) Document the species diversity and composition of edible plants in home gardens across the district.

(2) Examine their ethnobotanical roles, including their use as food, medicine, and other multifunctional purposes within the community.

(3) Assess their contributions to household food security, taking into account nutritional value, cultural importance, and economic relevance.

(4) Analyze patterns of species similarity among sub-districts to understand spatial distribution and diversity across the district.

(5) Evaluate the economic value (EV) of commonly traded local vegetables based on market dynamics, availability, and annual income generated by vendors.

By integrating ecological, cultural, nutritional, and economic perspectives, this research provides a comprehensive understanding of home-garden systems and their importance for rural livelihoods. The findings aim to support the conservation of plant genetic resources, enhance local food systems, and inform sustainable strategies for promoting neglected and NUS and traditional vegetables in Thailand and the wider Southeast Asian region.

Methods

Study area

The study was conducted in Pa Tio District, located in Yasothon Province, northeastern Thailand, spanning approximate coordinates 15°47′N, 104°10′E (Fig. 1). The district is situated on the Khorat Plateau and experiences a tropical savanna climate, with three distinct seasons: a hot season from March to May, a rainy season from May to October, and a cool dry season from November to February. Average annual rainfall ranges from 1,300 to 1,600 mm, with most precipitation occurring during the monsoon period. The topography comprises lowland rice fields, upland areas, and scattered villages, supporting diverse agroecosystems, including small-scale home gardens and cultivated fields [14].

(Map created with “QGIS” program ver. 3.34, geographic system ID: WGS 84; EPSG 4326 [15] designed by Phiphat Sonthongphithak)

Pa Tio District covers an area of approximately 308 km² and comprises five sub-districts—Chiang Pheng, Khok Na Ko, Krachai, Pho Sai, and Si Than. Soils are predominantly sandy loams with low fertility, characteristic of the Khorat Plateau, yet suitable for cultivating a wide range of edible plants. The district is primarily rural, with agriculture as the main livelihood. Rice farming dominates, often integrated with livestock raising and household gardening. Home gardens serve as multifunctional agroecosystems, providing vegetables, fruits, medicinal plants, and spices for daily consumption and local trade. These gardens are typically located adjacent to households and are mainly managed by women, who play a central role in maintaining ethnobotanical knowledge and seed exchange [16].

The population of Pa Tio District is approximately 18,500–19,000 inhabitants, predominantly composed of ethnic Lao-Isan communities. Lao-Isan is the main language, and Theravada Buddhism is the predominant religion. Local traditions, food culture, and rituals are closely tied to plant resources, reflecting the integration of cultural identity and traditional ecological knowledge. The combination of household gardening, small-scale agriculture, and access to nearby natural areas allows for dynamic interactions between local ecological knowledge, plant use, and socio-cultural practices. This setting provides a representative context to document and analyze the ethnobotanical knowledge of vegetables, spices, and other plant resources in rural northeastern Thailand [17].

Field survey and data collection

Field surveys were conducted between April 2024 and March 2025 in selected villages of Pa Tio District, Yasothon Province. A purposive sampling approach was employed to identify households with home gardens, focusing on those known to cultivate edible plants [18]. When selecting homegardens, we applied the following criteria: (1) proximity to the house; (2) clearly defined boundaries (e.g., fences of bamboo, bricks, or tree plantings); (3) a minimum area of 25 m²; and (4) active, continuous management without abandonment [19]. To ensure random sampling and minimize bias, we selected 14–21 well-preserved, traditionally managed homegardens in each subdistrict. In total, 80 households from five sub-districts were surveyed, selected purposefully based on the extensive presence of home gardens (Table 1).

In the second step, a broader set of 100 informants—comprising 50 women and 50 men, aged 20–75 years (Table 1), who were local residents born and raised in the area—were interviewed to capture detailed knowledge on plant use, cultural significance, and traditional practices related to edible plants. The informants included household members, traditional healers, and Buddhist monks, and were purposively selected to represent all five sub-districts of Pa Tio District, with 20 informants from each sub-district. This approach ensured comprehensive coverage of both ethnobotanical knowledge and household-level practices.

All interviews were conducted in Thai and the local Isan dialect, with the author—a native speaker of both languages—facilitating communication. No translator was required, ensuring accurate understanding of local terminology and nuanced ethnobotanical knowledge. The interviews focused on documenting plants cultivated in home gardens, including their local names, utilized plant parts, and preparation methods.

Prior to each interview, the objectives of the study were clearly explained, and verbal Prior Informed Consent (PIC) was obtained from all participants. Respondents were also informed of their rights, including voluntary participation and the ability to withdraw at any stage of the study.

All interviews were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the International Society of Ethnobiology (ISE) Code of Ethics [21] and followed the principles of the Nagoya Protocol on Access and Benefit Sharing [22].

Although the study did not involve sensitive personal data and formal institutional ethical approval was not required, all procedures were conducted in line with international standards for ethnobotanical research to ensure respect, transparency, and reciprocity toward participating communities. The interviews focused on documenting traditional knowledge, including local plant names, utilized plant parts, preparation techniques, and their respective uses.

Categorization of use-reports

Edible plants species documented in Pa Tio District were classified according to their reported uses into four categories: food (FD), medicinal (MD), ornamental (ON), and spice (SP). Food (FD) includes species consumed as staple vegetables or side dishes. Medicinal (MD) refers to plants utilized for therapeutic purposes or health maintenance. Ornamental (ON) covers species cultivated primarily for aesthetic, cultural, or ritual significance. Spice (SP) comprises plants used to enhance flavor or aroma in culinary preparations. During interviews, informants indicated all relevant uses for each species, and use-reports were recorded separately for each category. A single species could be assigned to multiple categories if it served more than one function, ensuring comprehensive representation of multifunctional plant use.

Nutritional analysis of edible plants

The nutritional composition of vegetables in Pa Tio District was assessed by reviewing published literature and established food composition databases. A total of 64 vegetable species commonly cultivated in homegardens were included in the analysis. For each species, data were compiled on mineral content (calcium, copper, iron, potassium, magnesium, manganese, sodium, phosphorus, and zinc) and vitamin C levels.

Nutritional information was retrieved from peer-reviewed journal articles, as well as from verified databases including PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar. Whenever nutritional data were available from studies conducted in Thailand, these values were prioritized. For species lacking local data, information from international studies was used. Data gaps or unavailable information were noted as missing (“-”) in the dataset.

The dataset summarizes the mineral and vitamin composition of each vegetable species, with nutrient values converted to mg/100 g when they were not originally reported in this unit.

Cultural food significance index (CFSI)

The Cultural Food Significance Index (CFSI) was employed to evaluate the cultural importance of edible plant species used for both dietary and medicinal purposes. The index, first introduced by Pieroni (2001) [27], integrates seven dimensions of food significance into a single composite value, calculated as:

The Quotation Index (QI) denotes the proportion of informants citing a particular species. The Availability Index (AI) reflects species abundance, from very common (4.0) to rare (1.0), while also considering the spatial scope of its use. The Frequency of Utilization Index (FUI) captures the regularity of consumption, ranging from more than once per week (5.0) to absence of use for over 30 years (0.5). The Parts Used Index (PUI) assesses the diversity of plant organs consumed, with values assigned to roots, stems, leaves, flowers, fruits, and seeds. The Multifunctional Food Use Index (MFFI) measures culinary versatility, encompassing preparation methods such as raw consumption, boiling, frying, roasting, and use as condiments. The Taste Score Appreciation Index (TSAI) reflects sensory evaluation, scored from 4.0 (very poor) to 10.0 (excellent). Finally, the Food-Medicinal Role Index (FMRI) evaluates the degree to which a plant is recognized as both food and medicine, ranging from “not recognized” (1.0) to “very high” (5.0).

To visualize patterns of cultural importance among edible plants, a heatmap was generated using PAST4 software (version 4.15). Each species was plotted against its component CFSI indices (QI, AI, FUI, PUI, MFFI, TSAI, and FMRI) to show similarities and differences in cultural food significance. Hierarchical clustering was performed using the Unweighted Pair Group Method with Arithmetic Mean (UPGMA) on a similarity matrix derived from the normalized index values, highlighting groups of species with high, moderate, or low overall CFSI contributions. This approach provides a clear visual representation of clustering patterns and multifunctionality of vegetables within local diets [28].

Economic value (EV) assessment

The Economic Value (EV) assessment was conducted from a local economic perspective to analyze interactions between vegetable vendors and consumers in Pa Tio District, Yasothon Province. This approach integrated quantitative methods with botanical expertise to ensure precise identification of the scientific names of commonly traded vegetables.

Results

Diversity of edible plants in Pa Tio district

A total of 73 species of edible plants, representing 33 plant families, were documented in Pa Tio District (Figs. 2 and 3; Table 2). Among these, the family Cucurbitaceae exhibited the highest species richness with 8 species (10.96%), followed by Zingiberaceae with 7 species (9.59%) and Apiaceae with 6 species (8.22%). Both Fabaceae and Lamiaceae contributed 5 species each (6.85%), whereas Amaranthaceae and Solanaceae accounted for 4 species (5.48%) apiece. Amaryllidaceae and Piperaceae were represented by 3 species each (4.11%). Families represented by two species (2.74%) included Asteraceae, Convolvulaceae, Menispermaceae, and Rutaceae, while the remaining 20 families were represented by a single species (1.37%), reflecting the presence of less common but culturally recognized edible plants. The prominence of Cucurbitaceae, Zingiberaceae, and Apiaceae underscores their importance in household cultivation and routine consumption, whereas families with only one species illustrate the uneven yet broad distribution of edible plants diversity.

Cultural importance index (CI) of edible plants in Pa Tio district

The Cultural Importance Index (CI) of edible plants recorded in Pa Tio District ranged from 0.11 to 1.12, reflecting substantial variation in cultural recognition and use among the species documented (Table 2).

The highest value was found in Citrus × aurantiifolia (CI = 1.12), which is deeply embedded in local cuisine as a primary souring agent in soups, curries, and dipping sauces, while also being valued for its medicinal roles in digestive and respiratory ailments. Other key species with high CI values included Allium cepa (0.88), Ocimum basilicum var. basilicum (0.85), and C. hystrix (0.84). These species represent staple flavoring agents that are indispensable in household cooking. The inclusion of Clitoria ternatea (0.83) among the leading species highlights the importance of edible flowers, both as natural food colorants and for their perceived health benefits.

A second group of species, with CI values ranging from 0.68 to 0.82, comprised mainly aromatic herbs, spices, and versatile vegetables. This group such as Ocimum tenuiflorum (0.82), Zingiber officinale (0.82), Allium sativum (0.81), Cymbopogon citratus (0.81), Carica papaya (0.78), Vigna unguiculata subsp. sesquipedalis (0.78), Capsicum annuum (0.70), Pandanus amaryllifolius (0.68), and Tiliacora triandra (0.68). These taxa are widely recognized for their dual functions as staple food ingredients and as household remedies, particularly for conditions such as fever, digestive disorders, and hypertension. The relatively high CI value of Boesenbergia rotunda (0.68) further underscores its cultural significance, reflecting its combined roles in both culinary practices and traditional medicine.

Species with moderate CI values (0.4–0.6), such as Coriandrum sativum (0.59), Centella asiatica (0.56), Piper nigrum (0.56), and Alpinia galanga (0.51), play important but less universal roles. Their uses are often seasonal or dish-specific, suggesting that their cultural recognition is narrower compared to top-ranking vegetables.

At the lower end of the spectrum (< 0.4), species such as Amaranthus viridis (0.37), A. blitum (0.35), Aloe vera (0.35), and Persicaria odorata (0.35) were recorded. These plants have specialized or less frequent uses, which may reflect changing dietary preferences or limited availability. Ornamental or rarely consumed species, including Tagetes erecta (0.21), Kalanchoe blossfeldiana (0.18), and Zinnia elegans (0.17), showed very low CI values, while Celosia argentea (0.11) represented the least culturally important vegetable in the study.

The analysis of Cultural Importance Index (CI) values across different categories of plant utilization in Pa Tio District revealed distinct patterns of cultural relevance. Food plants achieved the highest everage CI value (0.53), underscoring their critical role in daily subsistence and nutrition. Closely following were spices (everage CI = 0.52), reflecting their dual importance as both culinary enhancers and culturally embedded household remedies.

Medicinal plants exhibited a lower everage CI (0.41) compared to food and spice plants, yet still demonstrated substantial cultural significance, indicating their continued relevance in traditional healthcare systems. In contrast, ornamental plants recorded the lowest everage CI (0.26), suggesting that while they are valued for aesthetic and cultural functions, they are less central to subsistence or healthcare practices.

Utilization of edible plants in Pa Tio district

The documented edible plants in Pa Tio District were classified into four primary utilization categories (Table 2; Fig. 4). The largest proportion belonged to the food category, comprising 47 species (55.29%), highlighting the central role of edible plants in household diets. The spice category ranked second with 19 species (22.35%), reflecting the cultural emphasis on flavor enhancement and condiment use in traditional cuisine. The medicine category included 12 species (14.12%), underscoring the importance of edible plants as sources of household remedies. The smallest category was ornamental, represented by 7 species (8.24%), indicating that certain edible plants are also valued for their aesthetic and cultural significance.

Edible plants used as food

A total of 47 species of edible plants, distributed across 23 families, were documented as being used for food in Pa Tio District (Table 2; Fig. 4). The Cucurbitaceae was the most dominant family, represented by 8 species (17.02%), reflecting its importance in local diets through commonly consumed gourds and squashes. Both the Amaryllidaceae and Fabaceae followed, with 5 species each (10.64%), highlighting their dual role as staple edible plants and flavoring agents in household cooking. Other well-represented families included the Apiaceae and Lamiaceae, each contributing 4 species (8.51%), primarily herbs and aromatic plants widely used for seasoning and culinary enhancement. The Solanaceae accounted for 3 species (6.38%), including commonly cultivated edible plants such as eggplants and tomatoes. The Convolvulaceae added 2 species (4.26%), notably Ipomoea species, which are staple leafy greens in the region. The remaining 16 families were represented by a single species each (2.13%), including Annonaceae, Araliaceae, Basellaceae, Bignoniaceae, Brassicaceae, Caricaceae, Cleomaceae, Malvaceae, Meliaceae, Menispermaceae, Moringaceae, Phyllanthaceae, Piperaceae, Plantaginaceae, Polygonaceae, and Zingiberaceae. While each contributes minimally in terms of numerical diversity, these families contain species of significant nutritional and cultural importance, such as Carica papaya, Centella asiatica, and Moringa oleifera. Information on plant parts and preparation techniques is provided in Table S1.

The analysis of food plant utilization in Pa Tio District revealed that a wide range of plant parts were consumed (Fig. 5). Leaves were the most frequently used part (40.79%), highlighting their role as a staple ingredient in local diets. Fruits followed as the second most important part (22.37%), while shoots also contributed significantly (18.42%). Inflorescences accounted for 13.15% of uses, whereas bulbs (2.63%) and seeds (1.32%) were used less frequently. Additionally, one species (1.32%) was consumed as a whole plant.

Edible plants used as medicine

A total of 12 species of edible plants were recorded as being used for medicinal purposes in Pa Tio District (Table 2; Fig. 4). These species are distributed across 8 families, with the Zingiberaceae contributing the highest proportion (3 species; 25%). The Amaryllidaceae and Rutaceae followed, each represented by two species (16.67%). Families represented by a single species (8.33% each) included Apiaceae, Asphodelaceae, Fabaceae, Pandanaceae, and Poaceae.

The analysis of medicinal plant utilization in Pa Tio District showed that a variety of plant parts were used for therapeutic purposes (Fig. 5). Leaves were the most commonly used part (29.63%), emphasizing their importance in local healthcare practices. Rhizomes were the second most frequently employed part (18.53%), followed by bulbs, fruits, and roots, each accounting for 14.81% of uses. Inflorescences were less commonly utilized, representing 7.41% of citations.

Plants used for ornamental purposes

Flowering plants are frequently incorporated into home edible plants gardens to enhance their aesthetic value, particularly in households where gardens are situated at the front of the house. This practice not only improves the visual appeal of the household environment but also demonstrates the multifunctional roles of home gardens, which combine food production with cultural and decorative functions.

In Pa Tio District, a total of seven species of edible plants from five families were recorded as having ornamental value (Table 2; Fig. 4). The Amaranthaceae was the most represented family, with Celosia argentea and Gomphrena globosa, both cultivated for their vibrant inflorescences. The Asteraceae contributed to two species, Tagetes erecta and Zinnia elegans, which are widely grown for decorative purposes and play a role in ceremonial activities.

The remaining families were represented by one species each: Asparagaceae with Dracaena fragrans, valued for its attractive foliage and regarded as a symbol of good fortune; Crassulaceae with Kalanchoe blossfeldiana, appreciated for its colorful flowers; and Fabaceae with Clitoria ternatea, a multipurpose species notable for its edible, medicinal, and ornamental uses, particularly its striking blue blossoms.

Cultural significance was also noted. Gomphrena globosa and Tagetes erecta are often used in “Bai Sri” arrangements for Buddhist worship and other rituals, reflecting their symbolic importance. Dracaena fragrans is commonly planted as a household ornamental, with the belief that it brings prosperity and auspiciousness, especially when it flowers. These findings highlight that ornamental species in home gardens serve not only aesthetic functions but also hold deep cultural and spiritual significance in the local context. For ornamental purposes, the entire plant was utilized in all recorded species, representing 100% of the cases (Fig. 5).

Edible plants used as spice

In Pa Tio District, a total of 19 species of edible plants were identified as being used primarily as spices, belonging to 12 families (Table 2; Fig. 4). The Zingiberaceae family was the most represented, with 4 species (21.05%), underscoring its central role in flavoring and enhancing local cuisine. The Amaryllidaceae, Piperaceae, and Rutaceae each contributed 2 species (10.53%), reflecting their widespread use in seasoning and traditional food preparations.

Other families were represented by a single species each (5.26%): Anacardiaceae, Apiaceae, Hypericaceae, Lamiaceae, Menispermaceae, Pandanaceae, Poaceae, and Solanaceae. These species are incorporated into dishes not only to improve taste but also to provide aroma, color, and in some cases, medicinal benefits, as many spices possess bioactive compounds with antimicrobial and digestive properties. Plant parts utilized and their preparation methods are summarized in Table S2.

The analysis of spice plants revealed that a variety of plant parts were utilized for seasoning purposes (Fig. 5). Leaves represented the most commonly used part (37.50%), reflecting their importance as a readily available and versatile source of flavor in local cuisine. Fruits were the second most frequently employed part (20.83%), followed by rhizomes (16.67%), which play a significant role as aromatic bases in traditional dishes. Bulbs and seeds were each cited at 8.33%, underscoring their specialized but important contributions to flavoring practices. Roots and shoots were less frequently used, each accounting for 4.17% of reported uses.

Fidelity level (%FL) of edible plants in Pa Tio district

The ethnomedicinal survey of edible plants in Pa Tio District revealed extensive traditional knowledge regarding the therapeutic applications of commonly cultivated species (Table S1). Informants reported diverse uses across eight primary therapeutic categories: Gastrointestinal, Infection, Parasite and Immune System, Musculoskeletal and Joint Diseases, Neurological system, Obstetrics, Gynaecology and Urinary Disorders, Poisoning and Toxicology, Respiratory Disorders, and Skin System. The parts most commonly used included bulbs, leaves, rhizomes, roots, fruits, and inflorescences, with preparation methods ranging from decoction and consumption to external application and extraction of juices.

Several species exhibited high cultural consensus for particular therapeutic uses, reflecting their perceived efficacy and entrenched role in local healthcare practices. Allium cepa demonstrated FL values of 46.67% for alleviating bloating and abdominal fullness and 53.33% for expelling intestinal parasites, indicating strong agreement among informants regarding its dual roles in gastrointestinal health and parasite management. Similarly, A. sativum showed FLs of 44.12% for stimulating appetite and enhancing digestive capacity, and 55.88% for anthelmintic effects, highlighting its multifunctionality and cultural importance in maintaining digestive health.

Leafy vegetables and herbs were frequently cited for musculoskeletal support and general vitality. Centella asiatica had FL values of 55.26% for treating internal bruising and inflammation and 44.74% for alleviating fatigue and invigorating the body. Pandanus amaryllifolius exhibited FLs ranging from 29.79% to 36.17% across uses such as fatigue reduction, asthma management, and topical application as a facial mask, reflecting moderate consensus but demonstrating its multifunctional applications.

Several species were primarily associated with gastrointestinal health. Curcuma longa achieved FL values of 46.81% for relieving bloating, flatulence, abdominal pain, and digestive disturbances, and 53.19% for dyspepsia management. Cymbopogon citratus displayed FL values of 47.22% for fever reduction and 52.78% for supporting healthy digestion, highlighting both therapeutic consistency and cultural validation.

Plants used for infection control and immune system support also showed moderate to high FL values. Zingiber officinale exhibited FLs of 26.32% for supporting digestion, 33.33% for alleviating sore throat, and 40.35% for clearing respiratory passages, illustrating multifunctionality with moderate informant consensus. Clitoria ternatea had FLs of 20.93%, 37.21%, and 41.86% for its diuretic, antipyretic, and topical uses against insect stings, respectively, highlighting its versatility.

Reproductive and urinary health were supported by species such as Zingiber montanum, with FLs of 45% and 55% for menstrual regulation and gastrointestinal relief, respectively, and Clitoria ternatea as a traditional diuretic. Other species provided targeted treatments for neurological disorders (Citrus hystrix for dizziness and fainting) and skin conditions (Aloe vera for relieving burning sensation and topical application), further emphasizing the breadth of therapeutic applications in home gardens.

Overall, species with high FL values indicate strong cultural consensus and recognition of therapeutic efficacy, whereas moderate or lower FL values reflect either multifunctionality or divergent local practices. This highlights the complexity and richness of traditional ethnomedicinal knowledge in Pa Tio District. Detailed information regarding plant species, parts used, preparation methods, therapeutic uses, and FL values is summarized in Table S1, providing a comprehensive framework for under-standing the medicinal significance of edible plants.

Cultural food significance index (CFSI) of edible plants in Pa Tio district

A total of 67 edible plant species were evaluated for their cultural food significance in Pa Tio District using the Cultural Food Significance Index (CFSI) (Table S2). The CFSI values varied widely among species, reflecting differences in frequency of utilization, availability, diversity of edible parts, multifunctional uses, taste appreciation, and medicinal relevance within the local food culture.

The species with the highest CFSI was Allium cepa, scoring 6150.00, highlighting its exceptional role as a staple ingredient and cultural food resource. This high value was attributed to strong performance across nearly all contributing indices, particularly frequency of utilization (FUI = 5), taste score appreciation (TSAI = 10), and food-medicinal relevance (FMRI = 5). Citrus × aurantiifolia ranked second (CFSI = 5925.00), followed by A. sativum (5625.00), Ipomoea aquatica (5082.00), and Coriandrum sativum (1696.00). These species demonstrated consistently high utilization frequency, excellent taste perception, and common integration into both dietary and folk medicinal practices.

Species with moderate CFSI values such as Ocimum tenuiflorum (912.00), Citrus hystrix (684.45), and Coccinia grandis (490.05), which are frequently consumed in cooked dishes, condiments, or herbal teas. In contrast, species such as Benincasa hispida (67.50), Peperomia pellucida (38.61), and Cuminum cyminum (21.84) scored lower, suggesting that their roles are more limited, seasonal, or niche within local food systems.

The heat map analysis (Fig. 6) revealed clear clustering patterns of vegetable species based on their CFSI component indices. Species such as Allium cepa, A. sativum, and Citrus × aurantiifolia, clustered closely due to consistently high scores in availability, frequency of utilization, and medicinal relevance, confirming their strong integration into local foodways. Conversely, vegetables such as Benincasa hispida, Luffa acutangula, and Trichosanthes cucumerina subsp. cucumerina formed distinct low-value clusters, reflecting limited or specialized culinary roles.

Heat map analysis of local edible plants in Pa Tio District

Interestingly, several species with high PUI (Parts Used Index) and MFFI (Multifunctional Food Use Index), such as Anethum graveolens and Clitoria ternatea, demonstrated cultural versatility, being consumed in various forms ranging from fresh leaves to herbal infusions. This multifunctionality appears to contribute to their elevated CFSI values despite not ranking among the absolute top species.

Species with low FMRI (Food-Medicinal Role Index) generally had reduced overall CFSI scores, as they were less associated with traditional healthcare practices. However, plants such as Centella asiatica and Pandanus amaryllifolius balanced moderate scores across multiple indices, underscoring their dual role in h diet and folk medicine.

The integration of ethnobotanical indices through the CFSI framework provides a holistic perspective on the cultural importance of vegetables in Pa Tio District. These findings highlight key species central to local diets and traditional knowledge, while also identifying underutilized plants with potential for future research, conservation, and sustainable food development initiatives in Northeastern Thailand.

Analysis of the nutritional composition of edible plants revealed considerable variation in mineral and vitamin content. Calcium (Ca) was most abundant in Citrus hystrix (3987.45 mg/100 g) and lowest in Moringa oleifera (1.48 mg/100 g), while copper (Cu) peaked in Solanum virginianum (598 mg/100 g) and was minimal in Azadirachta indica (0.002 mg/100 g). Iron (Fe) was highest in S. virginianum (1090 mg/100 g) and lowest in Peperomia pellucida (0.04 mg/100 g).

Potassium (K) reached maximum levels in Basella alba (7180.56 mg/100 g) and minimum in Moringa oleifera (1.75 mg/100 g). For magnesium (Mg), Amaranthus blitum (3042 mg/100 g) exhibited the highest concentration, whereas Azadirachta indica contained the least (0.038 mg/100 g). Manganese (Mn) was most concentrated in Limnophila aromatica (113.3 mg/100 g) and lowest in Citrus × aurantiifolia (0.01 mg/100 g).

Sodium (Na) was particularly high in Solanum virginianum (3750 mg/100 g) and low in Sesbania grandiflora (0.04 mg/100 g), whereas phosphorus (P) was maximal in Moringa oleifera (352.39 mg/100 g) and minimal in Cucurbita maxima (1.36 mg/100 g).

Zinc (Zn) showed the highest concentration in Amaranthus blitum (87.9 mg/100 g) and the lowest in Peperomia pellucida (0.08 mg/100 g). Finally, vitamin C was most abundant in Basella alba (1027.78 mg/100 g) and least in Sesbania grandiflora (0.99 mg/100 g).

These findings highlight those leafy vegetables, particularly in the Amaranthaceae and Lamiaceae families, are rich sources of multiple micronutrients, while certain climbers and herbs exhibit lower nutrient densities, emphasizing the importance of species diversity in local diets for optimal nutrition.

This indicates that home gardens not only contribute to household nutrition but also provide a source of income for community members. To better understand the economic role of these vegetables, we assessed their market value, including price per kilogram, duration of availability throughout the year, and the average annual income generated by traders. Such an evaluation offers valuable insights into the contribution of local vegetables to rural livelihoods and highlights the importance of home gardens in supporting both food security and economic sustainability.

The economic analysis revealed considerable variation in price, market duration, and annual income for traders. Prices ranged from as low as 5 THB/kg for Basella alba to as high as 120 THB/kg for species such as Allium sativum and Mentha × villosa. Most edible plants were available for sale year-round, with 12 months of market availability, whereas a few, such as Sesbania grandiflora, were sold for only 6 months annually.

Species generating the highest average annual income per trader included Mentha × villosa (37,200 THB), Allium sativum (34,500 THB), and Psophocarpus tetragonolobus (25,200 THB), reflecting both high market prices and consistent year-round availability. In contrast, species such as Basella alba and Brassica oleracea yielded lower annual incomes (1,350–2,700 THB) due to their low unit prices, despite being sold throughout the year.

Leafy herbs, spices, and specialty vegetables, including Allium cepa, Ocimum spp., and Persicaria odorata, provided moderate incomes ranging from 9,720 to 20,400 THB per trader annually, indicating steady demand in the local market. Cucurbitaceae species, such as Cucumis sativus, Luffa acutangula, and Trichosanthes cucumerina, were also significant contributors to household income, with annual earnings between 5,280 and 13,500 THB.

Overall, these findings demonstrate that edible plants not only enhance household nutrition but also represent an important source of economic value for local farmers and traders. The combination of price, market duration, and annual income underscores the role of home-grown and locally marketed vegetables in sustaining rural livelihoods in Pa Tio District.

Discussion

Edible plants diversity

In Pa Tio District, a total of 73 edible plant species representing 33 families were documented, with Cucurbitaceae (10.96%), Zingiberaceae (9.59%), and Apiaceae (8.22%) being the most species-rich families. This pattern is broadly consistent with ethnobotanical surveys in northeastern Thailand and Asia, where gourds, spices, and aromatic herbs are culturally and nutritionally prominent [92, 93]. Notably, 20 families were represented by a single species, reflecting specialized local knowledge and the retention of less common but nutritionally or medicinally significant taxa [94].

Compared with Saensouk et al. [95] in Mueang District, Yasothon Province, Pa Tio District shows similar patterns of family-level diversity, with a few dominant families and numerous single-species families, despite differences in overall species richness. This suggests a broadly comparable distribution of ethnobotanical knowledge and cultural significance across northeastern Thai communities.

Cultural importance of edible plants in Pa Tio district

The patterns of cultural importance observed in Pa Tio District highlight the centrality of multifunctional species in local diets and household healthcare practices. Edible plants that serve both culinary and medicinal roles—particularly aromatic herbs and spices—demonstrate how local ethnobotanical knowledge integrates nutrition, flavor, and therapeutic functions. These multifunctional species are consistently prioritized by the community, reflecting their embeddedness in daily practices and cultural traditions.

Comparable trends have been reported in other parts of Southeast Asia. For instance, Saensouk et al. [11] documented those edible plants such as Ocimum species and members of Zingiberaceae were highly valued in Mueang District, Yasothon Province for their dual roles in food and medicine, illustrating the cultural prominence of multifunctional taxa. Similarly, studies in rural Indonesia, Malaysia, Nepal, and Thailand have shown that edible plants used as flavoring agents or herbal remedies often hold greater cultural significance than staple edible plants consumed primarily for nutrition [96]. Together, these findings underscore that multifunctionality—combining taste, nutritional value, and health benefits—is a key driver of species prominence in traditional food systems.

Although ornamental species accounted for a smaller proportion of the edible plants assemblage, they were strategically incorporated into home gardens to enhance aesthetic and cultural value. This aligns with research on urban green spaces, where ornamental plants contribute not only to visual appeal but also to ecosystem services, mental well-being, and resilience to environmental changes [97]. In Pa Tio District, the inclusion of decorative species within kitchen gardens exemplifies a multifunctional approach in traditional agroecosystems, where plants simultaneously fulfill practical, nutritional, medicinal, and aesthetic roles.

Overall, these observations suggest that the drivers of cultural importance—multifunctionality, accessibility, and integration into daily practices—are broadly consistent across Southeast Asian communities. The application of quantitative indices such as the Cultural Importance Index (CI) provides a robust framework to identify culturally essential species, offering valuable insights for conservation planning and strategies aimed at sustaining traditional knowledge systems [98]. By capturing both culinary and medicinal significance, this study contributes to a deeper understanding of the complex socio-ecological relationships that shape local food systems and community resilience.

Utilization of edible plants

The utilization patterns of edible plants in Pa Tio District align closely with broader ethnobotanical trends observed in northeastern Thailand and Southeast Asia. In this study, vegetables were categorized into four main use types: food (55.29%), spices (22.35%), medicine (14.12%), and ornamental purposes (8.24%). The predominance of food-related use mirrors findings from Saensouk et al. [95] in Mueang District, Yasothon Province, where more than half of the documented species were consumed as food. Similarly, the high proportion of spice species reflects the cultural emphasis on aromatic herbs and flavoring agents, consistent with studies from rural Indonesia and Malaysia, which identified Zingiberaceae and Amaryllidaceae as culturally important families for culinary enhancement [12].

Among food plants, Cucurbitaceae was the most represented family (17.02%), confirming their status as staple vegetables in northeastern Thai diets. Beyond their cultural importance, cucurbits are rich in macronutrients, minerals, and bioactive compounds with antidiabetic, antioxidant, and anticancer properties. Seeds, leaves, and flowers also provide additional health benefits, highlighting the multifunctionality of Cucurbitaceae species in supporting nutrition, health, and food security in Pa Tio District [99]. Amaryllidaceae and Fabaceae each contributed 10.64%, consistent with findings by Kebede et al. [100], who emphasized their dual roles in flavoring and nutrition. Apiaceae and Lamiaceae were moderately represented (8.51% each), reflecting the widespread use of herbs for seasoning, as previously reported in Tak provinces [101]. Leaves were the most frequently consumed plant part (40.79%), followed by fruits and shoots, which is similar to the utilization patterns reported by Thattantavide and Kumar [98], where leaves predominated in dietary use due to their accessibility and versatility.

In the medicinal category, Zingiberaceae was the most dominant family (25%), followed by Amaryllidaceae and Rutaceae (16.67% each). This confirms the recurrent importance of these families in ethnomedicinal systems throughout Southeast Asia [28]. High fidelity level (FL) values for Allium cepa, A. sativum, and Curcuma longa parallel observations from previous ethnomedicinal studies in Maha Sarakham and Yasothon provinces, where these species were consistently cited for gastrointestinal and digestive ailments [11, 96]. Informant consensus factor (Fic) values in the present study were uniformly high (0.961–1.000), reinforcing the reliability and widespread recognition of local medicinal knowledge, similar to patterns observed in neighboring country [102].

Spices accounted for 19 species across 12 families, with Zingiberaceae as the leading family (21.05%). This is consistent with prior studies indicating the ethnoculinary prominence of ginger, turmeric, and related species in northeastern Thai cuisine [28]. The diversity of plant parts used—leaves, fruits, rhizomes, bulbs, and seeds—also parallels earlier findings from Indonesia [103], highlighting the multifunctionality of spice species.

Although ornamental species represented a smaller proportion, their inclusion in home gardens demonstrates culturally embedded multifunctionality. Species such as Dracaena fragrans, Gomphrena globosa, and Tagetes erecta were cultivated not only for aesthetics but also for ritual and symbolic purposes, corroborating findings from studies in urban and rural agroecosystems in Thailand, where ornamental plants contribute to cultural expression, household identity, and ceremonial practices [93].

Cultural food significance and multifunctionality

Cultural Food Significance Index (CFSI) analysis highlighted key species central to both dietary and ethnomedicinal systems in Pa Tio District. Allium cepa, A. sativum, and Citrus × aurantiifolia achieved the highest CFSI values, reflecting their exceptional integration into daily consumption and traditional healthcare. These results are consistent with studies in Indonesia [104], where these species were also among the most frequently used and culturally valued edible plants in Yasothon Province.

Moderate CFSI species, including Citrus hystrix, Coccinia grandis, and Ocimum tenuiflorum reinforce previous reports highlighting the importance of aromatic herbs and indigenous greens as both culinary and medicinal resources [105]. Conversely, species with low CFSI values, such as Benincasa hispida and Peperomia pellucida, reflect either seasonal availability or niche uses, a pattern observed in ethnobotanical surveys from other Thai communities [106].

Species with high PUI (Parts Used Index) and MFFI (Multifunctional Food Use Index), such as Anethum graveolens and Clitoria ternatea, demonstrate cultural versatility and multifunctionality, being incorporated into diets as leaves, infusions, or flavoring agents. This multifunctionality parallels findings from studies in rural Malaysia and Indonesia, where species used for both culinary and medicinal purposes often achieved higher cultural significance [96, 104].

Collectively, these patterns illustrate that multifunctionality, frequency of use, and perceived health benefits are major determinants of edible plants prominence in traditional food systems. Comparison with previous studies confirms that local ethnobotanical knowledge in Pa Tio District aligns with broader regional trends, highlighting a shared emphasis on culturally and nutritionally important species while also preserving specialized knowledge of less common taxa [107].

Nutritional contributions of edible plants

The nutritional diversity of edible plants in Pa Tio District underscores their pivotal role in supporting household dietary adequacy and mitigating micronutrient deficiencies [108]. Leafy greens, aromatic herbs, and fruiting edible plants together provide a complementary array of essential minerals and vitamins, emphasizing the necessity of dietary diversity over reliance on staple crops alone [109].

While certain species deliver functional benefits that support cardiovascular, muscular, and hematological health, others with lower nutrient densities highlight those familiar edible plants may not suffice to meet al.l micronutrient needs, reinforcing the importance of varied consumption [110]. This diversity also reflects the resilience of local food systems, fostering sustainable agricultural practices, adaptation to environmental variability, and preservation of traditional knowledge [111].

In this study, we focused on mineral and vitamin contents compiled from literature to illustrate the relative abundance of key nutrients across commonly cultivated kitchen garden edible plants. Although these data are derived from different sources and proximate compositions were not consistently available, they provide a useful overview of which species are rich in particular micronutrients, allowing the identification of nutritionally important edible plants for household consumption. This approach demonstrates nutritional complementarity within local diets rather than relying on a single dominant species [112].

The literature review also highlights important research gaps: several commonly cultivated edible plants remain understudied, and comprehensive analyses of essential minerals are still lacking for certain taxa, such as Alpinia siamensis and Hydrocotyle umbellata. Future research combining field-collected samples with standardized proximate and micronutrient analyses would strengthen these findings, validate ethnobotanical knowledge, and promote indigenous edible plants as sustainable, health-promoting food resources, thereby advancing both nutritional security and broader sustainable development goals.

Economic value market potential, and horticultural potential

The economic assessment of edible plants in Pa Tio District emphasizes their strategic role in supporting rural livelihoods beyond household nutrition. Home gardens function not only as sources of food but also as platforms for income generation, highlighting the intertwined nature of subsistence and market-oriented production in smallholder systems [8].

The diversity of marketable species reflects local dietary preferences and cultural values, with herbs, spices, and specialty edible plants demonstrating consistent consumer demand. These plants often occupy niche markets, where quality, freshness, and culinary or medicinal utility enhance their perceived value, creating stable economic opportunities for households engaged in their cultivation. Such dynamics suggest that local knowledge and culinary traditions directly influence economic outcomes in rural food systems [113].

Temporal availability of crops generally supports stable market income, as most species documented are present year-round. Only one species is not available throughout the year, as it is harvested for its flowers. It should be noted that data were collected from home garden owners, and due to the small size of these gardens, production may not always be sufficient for continuous sale throughout the year [114].

Beyond their economic importance, the findings highlight the horticultural potential of these edible plants. Many species demonstrate high adaptability to smallholder garden systems, ease of propagation, and resilience to local climatic conditions. Their multifunctional roles—including food, medicine, spice, and ornamental use—further enhance their suitability for home gardens and small-scale cultivation. This potential suggests opportunities for broader adoption in both household and community-level horticulture, contributing to agricultural diversification, food security, and sustainable rural development [115].

The findings further underscore the contribution of edible plants to diversified livelihood strategies. By integrating cultivation for both consumption and sale, households enhance resilience against economic shocks while simultaneously maintaining dietary diversity. This dual function positions edible plants as key instruments for promoting sustainable rural economies and reinforces the importance of supporting small-scale production systems through policy and extension services [116].

In summary, edible plants in Pa Tio District are not merely dietary resources; they represent an important supplementary economic and cultural asset. Although this study did not quantify household income, informants consistently emphasized that selling surplus edible plants provides small but steady financial support, helps cover daily expenses, and reduces overall food costs. This qualitative evidence highlights the continued relevance of edible plants cultivation in sustaining household resilience and cultural practices.

Sociocultural and future trends influencing home-garden edible plants

Home gardens in Pa Tio District not only reflect traditional dietary and medicinal practices but are also shaped by broader sociocultural, economic, and environmental dynamics. Outmigration of younger community members for education or employment reduces available labor for garden maintenance, potentially influencing garden size and species composition. Concurrently, lifestyle changes and dietary shifts, including increased consumption of processed foods and commercially produced edible plants, may alter the frequency and diversity of edible plant use within households.

Agricultural intensification and market-oriented farming present both opportunities and challenges. While some households adopt improved cultivation techniques to increase yield and income, reliance on monocultures or hybrid varieties may limit the cultivation of traditional species. Conversely, growing consumer interest in local, culturally significant, and nutrient-rich edible plants may provide incentives to maintain and even expand home-garden diversity. Environmental factors, such as seasonal variability and climate change, can further affect planting schedules, flowering, and harvest timing, influencing both household consumption and potential market supply. Despite these pressures, the continued cultivation of multifunctional species—used simultaneously for food, medicine, ornamentation, and cultural purposes—demonstrates the resilience of traditional knowledge and the adaptive capacity of local communities.

Pa Tio District serves as a representative case study for other rural communities in northeastern Thailand. Even households with limited space often grow edible plants in small pots, integrating them as ornamental features while contributing to household nutrition. These practices, frequently supported by local leaders, encourage home-grown cultivation to reduce expenses and ensure access to safe, fresh produce. However, the prevalence, diversity, and impacts of such practices have not yet been systematically studied or documented.

Understanding these sociocultural, economic, and environmental trends in Pa Tio District is therefore essential not only for local planning but also for informing broader strategies in northeastern Thailand. Insights from this district can guide the conservation of culturally important species, support sustainable local food systems, and help preserve ethnobotanical heritage while anticipating future changes in home-garden composition.

Novel findings and implications

This study provides several novel insights into the ethnobotany of edible plants in Pa Tio District. First, the integration of nutritional, economic, and cultural data within a single framework highlights the multifunctionality of edible plants more comprehensively than previous surveys in northeastern Thailand [95, 98, 100]. Notably, species such as Amaranthus blitum, Basella alba, and Ocimum × africanum emerged as exceptional micronutrient sources while also supporting household income, underscoring their dual contribution to nutrition and livelihoods. Second, the combined use of the Cultural Food Significance Index (CFSI), Parts Used Index (PUI), and Multifunctional Food Use Index (MFFI) provided a more nuanced understanding of species prominence, highlighting that often underreported herbs and leafy vegetables—such as Apium sellowianum, Cucumis sativus, and Ipomoea aquatica—possess substantial cultural and nutritional value.Third, the documentation of ornamental plants integrated into home gardens for both aesthetic and cultural purposes add a new dimension to the study of multifunctional agroecosystems, reflecting the interplay between tradition, culture, and household management. Collectively, these findings expand current knowledge on the socio-ecological significance of edible plants and provide a robust evidence base for conservation and sustainable use.

Future research directions

Building on the findings of this study, several avenues for future research are recommended. First, while the mineral and vitamin composition of edible plants has been documented, studies on nutrient bioavailability and functional properties, including phytochemicals and antioxidant activity, are necessary to fully understand their health benefits. Second, longitudinal ethnobotanical surveys could capture seasonal and temporal variations in species availability, utilization patterns, and market dynamics, revealing shifts in cultural practices and household economies over time. Third, agroecological and conservation-focused research that examines the relationships between edible plants diversity, land-use patterns, soil fertility, and climate resilience would provide valuable guidance for sustainable cultivation and biodiversity protection. Fourth, socio-economic impact assessments quantifying the contribution of home-grown and market-traded edible plants to household income, food security, and resilience under socio-economic or environmental stressors would enhance understanding of their broader significance. Fifth, comparative studies across other districts in northeastern Thailand and neighboring countries could evaluate the generalizability of multifunctionality patterns and identify regionally important species for policy and conservation prioritization. Finally, integrating edible plants use into public health and nutrition strategies could support culturally tailored interventions aimed at improving micronutrient intake and preventing non-communicable diseases. Addressing these research gaps will further strengthen the evidence base for promoting edible plants as a cornerstone of sustainable nutrition, livelihoods, and agro-biodiversity conservation.

Conclusions

This study documents a diverse assemblage of 73 plant species from 33 families in Pa Tio District, reflecting a rich ethnobotanical heritage and intricate biocultural relationships. The presence of both commonly cultivated and niche species underscores adaptive strategies that sustain household food security, nutrition, and cultural identity. Quantitative analyses indicate that multifunctional species, particularly Allium cepa, A. sativum, and Ocimum spp., occupy central roles in daily diets while simultaneously serving medicinal and cultural functions. High fidelity levels and cultural importance indices highlight strong community agreement on the therapeutic and culinary significance of key species, affirming the deep interconnection between traditional knowledge and local health practices. The variety of uses—from staple foods and spices to medicinal and ornamental purposes—demonstrates a holistic approach to agroecosystems, integrating multifunctionality with specialization. Such complexity supports dietary diversity, promotes nutritional adequacy, and enhances the resilience of rural food systems. Economically, the integration of home-grown and marketable species provides households with supplementary income, illustrating the dual role of edible plants in promoting both food security and livelihood sustainability. This reinforces the value of small-scale cultivation and the maintenance of traditional knowledge for socio-economic resilience. While this study did not include experimental validation, future research should focus on phytochemical analyses to identify bioactive compounds and on pharmacological studies to evaluate their biological activities. Such investigations would provide a scientific basis to support or critically assess the medicinal claims of traditional healers, thereby integrating indigenous knowledge with modern scientific approaches and promoting sustainable health solutions.
