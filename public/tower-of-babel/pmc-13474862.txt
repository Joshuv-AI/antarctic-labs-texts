Determinants of occupational burnout among medical personnel in tertiary hospitals: the complex interplay of demographic characteristics, mental health, and self-efficacy
Authors: Erchang Zhu, Xuan Guo, Sijin Kong, Jingbo Ma, Guangju Mo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOccupational burnout is a critical issue affecting medical personnel, with implications for both mental health and quality of care. Understanding the factors associated with burnout is crucial for developing evidence-based interventions.MethodsThis cross-sectional study involved 1,160 medical personnel from tertiary hospitals in China. Data was collected on demographic characteristics, occupational burnout, mental health and self-efficacy. Multiple linear regression, correlation analysis, and structural equation modeling were employed to examine associations.ResultsWork tenure ≥ 15 years was a significant protective factor against burnout (β=-6.806, P < 0.001), as well as other technical personnel showed lower burnout levels than doctors (β=-4.238, P < 0.001). Structural equation modeling demonstrated that mental health risk had a significantly negative impact on self-efficacy (β=-0.271, P < 0.001), with self-efficacy exhibiting a negative predictive effect on occupational burnout (β=-0.309, P < 0.001). Mental health risk also exerted a strong positive direct effect on occupational burnout (β = 0.877). Self-efficacy partial mediated the pathway between mental health risks and occupational burnout.ConclusionsThis study examines burnout among medical staff in Chinese tertiary hospitals. Key factors include work tenure, income, mental health risk, and self-efficacy. Self-efficacy also partly reduces the impact of psychological distress. Different professional roles face varied pressures from clinical, teaching, and research duties. Effective interventions should focus on boosting self-efficacy, improving income, supporting mental health, and tailoring support to family situations and career stages. Introduction

Occupational burnout is a major concern in healthcare, as it adversely affects medical personnel’s mental well-being, work performance, and the quality of patient care [1, 2]. It costs the global economy over $300 billion annually and is recognized as an occupational “disease” in several European countries [3]. The COVID-19 pandemic has further intensified these risks, placing unprecedented pressure on healthcare workers and substantially increasing the prevalence of burnout, particularly among frontline staff [4–6]. In high-pressure clinical settings, burnout has serious consequences not only for clinicians but also for patients, contributing to poor mental health, medical errors, reduced patient satisfaction, and increased turnover intention [7–12].

Previous studies have suggested that occupational burnout is influenced by multiple factors, including demographic characteristics such as age, work tenure, and income level, as well as individual mental health status [13]. In particular, medical personnel experiencing persistent psychological stress, anxiety, or depressive symptoms are more likely to develop core dimensions of burnout, including emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and reduced personal accomplishment [14–18]. Occupational burnout is no longer merely an individual issue but a critical systemic problem affecting the stable operation of the healthcare system. Mental health status is considered a key variable in the development of occupational burnout [19]. Long-term work stress can lead to emotional issues such as anxiousness and depression, and these mental health problems can in turn exacerbate burnout symptoms [20–22]. From the perspective of social cognitive theory, self-efficacy represents a crucial psycho-social resource that influences how individuals assess stressors, regulate their emotions and cope with challenging work environments [23]. Individuals with higher self-efficacy are generally more likely to adopt active coping strategies and demonstrate greater resilience, which may help protect against burnout.

However, although the associations among mental health, self-efficacy, and occupational burnout have been widely examined, the existing evidence remains inconsistent regarding the role of self-efficacy in this relationship. Some studies suggest that mental health risks significantly predict burnout, with self-efficacy serving as either a buffer or a mediator in this process [24, 25]. In contrast, other studies have found that the effect of self-efficacy is relatively limited and may only be significant for certain burnout dimensions rather than overall burnout [21, 26]. These mixed findings indicate that the literature has not yet reached a clear consensus on how mental health and self-efficacy jointly contribute to burnout, suggesting the need for further research to clarify these mechanisms.

Furthermore, the pathways linking mental health, self-efficacy, and occupational burnout may vary significantly across different cultural contexts. In China, tertiary hospitals occupy a central position in the healthcare system, serving as the main providers of diagnosis and treatment for complex and critical conditions while also bearing responsibilities for teaching and scientific research [27, 28]. In contrast, secondary hospitals are primarily responsible for the diagnosis and management of general and common diseases, as well as for providing certain public health services. Meanwhile, primary hospitals (community health service centers) focus on basic medical care and preventive health services [29]. This tiered healthcare delivery system results in significantly higher work intensity, technical complexity, and multitasking pressures in tertiary hospitals compared to other levels of medical institutions. As a result, medical staff in tertiary hospitals often face exceptionally high workloads, role overload, and sustained emotional demands [1, 30]. Moreover, mental health stigma remains relevant in Chinese healthcare settings and may influence help-seeking behavior, emotional expression, and the persistence of psychological distress [31, 32]. Therefore, Chinese tertiary hospitals are not merely a sampling frame but a theoretically meaningful context in which to examine whether established burnout mechanisms hold under conditions of exceptional workload, role conflict, and cultural specificity.

Given these considerations, this study investigated the associations among demographic characteristics, mental health, self-efficacy, and occupational burnout among medical personnel in Chinese tertiary hospitals. In particular, we examined the mediating and moderating roles of self-efficacy in the association between mental health and burnout. By situating the analysis in a distinct institutional and cultural context, this study aimed to clarify inconsistent findings in the existing literature and provide evidence for early identification, risk prevention, and targeted interventions for burnout among medical personnel.

Methods

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional survey was conducted between May and July 2022 using convenience sampling in seven tertiary hospitals in Anhui Province. The participating hospitals were core medical institutions with relatively high medical standards in the region and integrated clinical care, medical education, and research functions, thereby representing a certain level of regional representativeness within the provincial healthcare system. In each hospital, 4–5 departments were randomly selected, and all on-duty healthcare professionals in those departments were invited to participate in the survey. Participants were recruited based on the following inclusion criteria: (1) held a formal employment position at the participating hospital; (2) provided voluntary informed consent to complete the survey; (3) had no history of diagnosed major psychiatric disorders. Of the 1,185 questionnaires distributed, 1,160 complete responses were retained for analysis, yielding a valid response rate of 97.89%. This study was approved by the institutional ethics committee of the participating hospital (approval No.: 20220139), and all respondents provided informed consent in writing before proceeding with the survey.

Measurements

Demographic variables captured in this study were age, gender, marital and fertility status, educational level, career, professional title, years of professional experience, and mean monthly income.

The measurement of occupational burnout utilized an assessment tool specifically developed for Chinese medical personnel, the construction of which strictly adhered to standard scale development protocols. Firstly, a systematic literature review was conducted to identify the core theoretical dimensions of occupational burnout [33]; based on this, a preliminary item pool was established. Subsequently, the study conducted two rounds of Delphi expert consultation (involving a total of 15 experts from the fields of clinical medicine and psychology), during which the content relevance, clarity of wording and alignment with clinical contexts of the items were independently assessed and revised to ensure the scale possessed good content validity. The final formal scale comprises 15 items, which is scored on a 7‑point Likert‑type scale (0–6), anchored by the following frequency descriptors: 0 = never, 1 = several times, 2 = ≤ 1 time/month, 3 = 2–3 times/month, 4 = 1 time/week, 5 = 2–6 times/week, 6 = everyday. Items 1–8 are positively scored, and Items 9–15 are scored negatively. The total score is the sum of the scores for all items. The higher the total score, the more severe the symptoms of occupational burnout. In this study, the Cronbach’s α of scale was 0.844.

Mental health risk was measured using the Chinese version of the Kessler Scale (K10); a higher total score indicates a greater risk of developing a mental disorder [34]. The scale contains 10 items, each of which is scored on a 5-point scale, with the following options: Always (5 points), most of the time (4 points), sometimes (3 points), occasionally (2 points) and hardly ever (1 point). In the current sample, the Cronbach’s α of the scale was 0.960.

The General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES) was adopted to assess participants’ perceived self-efficacy, with higher total scores indicating higher belief in one’s ability to cope with work-related challenges [35]. The scale comprises 10 items and uses a fourth-degree Likert scale. A higher score indicates greater self-efficacy. The Cronbach’s α of the GSES in this study was 0.945. The final questionnaire, consisting of four sections, was provided as Supplementary File - Questionnaire.

Statistical analysis

All data were double-entered and cleaned using Epidata, and statistical analyses were performed using R 4.5.1. Continuous variables were summarized using the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Confirmatory factor analysis was used to assess the construct validity of the Scale. The adequacy of model fit was evaluated using multiple indices, including the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), the Incremental Fit Index (IFI), the Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), the Normed Fit Index (NFI), and the Goodness-of-Fit Index (GFI). In accordance with conventional criteria, values above 0.90 were interpreted as indicating satisfactory fit, while values exceeding 0.80 were considered acceptable [36]. The scale’s convergent validity was assessed using the average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reliability (CR). An AVE value above 0.5 and a CR value above 0.7 indicated good convergent validity [37]. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s α. Between-group comparisons of burnout scores were conducted using independent-samples t-test for two-group comparisons and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for multi-group comparisons. When conducting post-hoc comparisons following a significant one-way ANOVA, the choice of method depends on whether the assumption of homogeneity of variance is met. If the assumption holds, the Bonferroni correction is appropriate; if violated, Tamhane’s T2 should be used [38, 39]. To identify independent predictors of occupational burnout and its three dimensions, multiple linear regression models were constructed, adjusting for all demographic and occupational covariates. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to explore the bivariate associations between mental health, self-efficacy, and occupational burnout. To test whether self-efficacy moderates the association between mental health risk and occupational burnout, a full linear regression model with an interaction term (mental health × self-efficacy) was fitted, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for regression coefficients were calculated using the bootstrap method with 5000 resamples. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was performed using Amos 29.0 software. The significance level was set at α = 0.05. P-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Occupational burnout among medical personnel with different socio-demographic characteristics

As displayed in Table 2, demographic characteristics showed significant associations with burnout dimensions among medical personnel. ANOVA showed that marital status had a significant effect on the level of job burnout among medical personnel (F = 6.975, P = 0.001), and post-hoc tests indicated that unmarried medical personnel had significantly higher occupational burnout than married medical personnel (P < 0.01). Fertility status also exerted an extremely significant effect on occupational burnout (F = 10.401, P<0.001); the occupational burnout level of the childless/infertile group was significantly higher than that of the group with only one child (P < 0.001), and also significantly higher than that of the group with two or more children (P < 0.05). Extremely significant differences in occupational burnout were observed among medical personnel from different occupational categories (F = 7.809, P<0.001), with both doctors and nurses reporting significantly higher occupational burnout levels than other technical personnel (P < 0.05). The difference in occupational burnout among different professional titles reached an extremely significant level (F = 19.286, P<0.001), the occupational burnout level of staff with primary and below titles was significantly higher than that of staff with middle titles (P < 0.01), and also significantly higher than that of staff with high titles (P < 0.001), that is, the lower the professional title, the higher the occupational burnout level of medical personnel. Working years also had an extremely significant impact on occupational burnout (F = 27.570, P<0.001). The occupational burnout level gradually decreased with the increase of working years, meaning that the shorter the working years, the higher the degree of occupational burnout, and medical staff with 15 years or more of working experience have the lowest level of occupational burnout. Finally, the effect of a monthly income on job burnout also reached an extremely significant level (F = 15.817, P<0.001). Post-hoc tests showed that the occupational burnout level of medical personnel with monthly income below 5000 CNY was significantly higher than that of all groups with higher income, and the overall pattern confirmed that the lower the income, the higher the level of occupational burnout. Multiple regression analysis of factors influencing occupational burnout among medical personnel

Multiple linear regression models adjusted for demographic and occupational covariates identified several independent predictors of occupational burnout. Compared with < 5 years of working experience, ≥ 15 years of experience was significantly associated with lower occupational burnout and all three dimensions, indicating a protective effect of longer tenure. Higher monthly income was also associated with lower total burnout, EE, and DP scores, while other technical personnel showed lower burnout levels than doctors. No significant associations were found for professional title, marital status, or fertility status.

As shown in Table 3, mental health was significantly positively associated with all dimensions of occupational burnout and total burnout (β = 7.376, 95% CI: 6.605–8.147, P < 0.001), whereas self-efficacy was negatively associated with total burnout (β=-1.944, 95% CI: -2.712 to -1.176, P < 0.001), DP, and PA. The interaction between mental health and self-efficacy was significant only for EE (β = 0.504, P = 0.001), with no moderating effects observed for DP, PA, or total burnout.

Factors influencing occupational burnout and three domains among medical personnel

Correlation analysis of self-efficacy, mental health, and occupational burnout

Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to explore the associations between self-efficacy and the dimensions of occupational burnout (including total occupational burnout, EE, DP, and PA). Self-efficacy was significantly negatively correlated with total occupational burnout (r=-0.32), EE (r=-0.22), DP (r=-0.13), PA (r=-0.29), and mental health (r=-0.38). Total occupational burnout has a strong positive correlation with EE, DP, PA, and mental health.

Structural equation modeling analysis of factors influencing burnout among medical personnel

Using Structural Equation Modeling, this study examined the pathway through which mental health influences occupational burnout, based on the mediating role of self-efficacy, whilst controlling for the confounding effects of demographic variables. As the demographic variables are all categorical, they were encoded using dummy variables before the SEM analysis and then included in the model. After controlling for demographic variables, the CFI, TLI, GFI, IFI and NFI were all greater than 0.90, and the model fit improved significantly.

As shown in Fig. 1, structural equation modeling showed that mental health risk had a significant negative effect on self-efficacy (β=-0.271, P < 0.001), while self-efficacy negatively predicted occupational burnout (β=-0.309, P < 0.001). Mental health risk also exerted a strong positive direct effect on occupational burnout (β = 0.877). Self-efficacy partial mediated the pathway between mental health risks and occupational burnout. Demographic characteristics were included as control variables in the model.

Discussion

This study examined occupational burnout among medical personnel in a tertiary hospital setting by integrating demographic differences, regression analysis, correlation analysis, and structural equation modeling. The findings suggest a close association among mental health risk, self-efficacy, and occupational burnout, while also showing marked differences across demographic groups in overall burnout and its dimensions. In a tertiary hospital with both clinical and teaching responsibilities, these findings are not merely individual psychological patterns; rather, they reflect the combined influence of organizational demands, career stage, and family-role burden.

Significant differences in burnout were observed across marital status, fertility status, occupational category, professional title, working years, and monthly income, indicating that burnout is not solely an individual psychological outcome but is also closely linked to life stage, career development, and social resources. In Chinese tertiary hospitals, where medical staff must simultaneously manage clinical care, research productivity, and teaching duties, work pressure is often compounded by family responsibilities, financial concerns, and promotion-related stress [40].

Marital and parenthood status did not show a significant effect on overall burnout in the multivariate model. However, dimensional analysis revealed a marginally lower trend in DP among unmarried individuals, while having no children/infertility was associated with significantly higher DP. This suggests that the relationship between marital/parental status and burnout is more pronounced in dimensions such as interpersonal detachment, emotional withdrawal, and professional cynicism, rather than directly reflected in overall burnout or emotional exhaustion. This distinction indicates that marital and parenthood status are not simply “protective” or “risk” factors, but may exert influence through the social support systems, lifestyle rhythms, and stress-buffering mechanisms associated with different family structures [41, 42]. In other words, these variables appear to modulate the quality of relationships with work objects, perceived social support, and role-balancing ability, rather than directly determining overall burnout levels [43]. One limitation of this study is that social support was not measured directly, which precludes assessment of the specific roles of family or organizational support in burnout development.

Differences across occupational categories further reflect the characteristics of tertiary teaching hospitals. Doctors and nurses reported significantly higher burnout levels than other technical staff, consistent with previous findings that frontline patient-care roles are more susceptible to emotional exhaustion [44, 45]. However, the burnout observed in this study may also be related to the concurrent clinical, teaching, and research responsibilities inherent in Chinese tertiary teaching hospitals. Compared with studies conducted in non-teaching hospitals or community settings outside China, the present findings provide additional insight into burnout under the combined pressures of clinical service, medical education, and academic evaluation [30, 46].

Years of working experience showed a relatively consistent protective association with all three burnout dimensions and overall burnout, particularly among those with over 15 years of experience, who scored significantly lower in EE, DP, PA, and total burnout. This indicates that work experience influences not only overall fatigue but also job identification, engagement, and sense of efficacy [47]. Lower burnout among senior staff may be attributed to greater clinical experience, stronger job adaptation, clearer professional boundaries, and more mature coping strategies. This finding partially contrasts with some international studies in non-tertiary hospital settings that reported higher burnout with longer service, suggesting that the unique promotion systems, role specialization, and job stability in Chinese tertiary hospitals may alter the relationship between tenure and burnout [48, 49].

Income level was also clearly associated with burnout. Higher income was linked to lower DP and overall burnout, with a declining trend in EE, though its effect on PA was less consistent. This implies that financial compensation may more directly buffer professional cynicism and overall burnout, whereas personal accomplishment, as a more internal psychological evaluation, relies more on professional achievement, developmental opportunities, and value recognition [50]. In the context of high patient volumes, extended working hours, and performance pressure in tertiary hospitals, some frontline healthcare workers may remain in a state of “high input–low return” [51]. Compared with some studies in other countries, the association between income and burnout appears more pronounced in this study [52, 53].

Both regression analysis and structural equation modeling indicated a stable positive association between psychological distress and burnout, suggesting that mental health status is an important entry point for understanding burnout. For medical personnel at tertiary hospitals, prolonged periods of high-intensity work, night shifts, the pressure of doctor-patient communication, academic assessments and clinical quality requirements may continuously deplete their psychological resources, making them more prone to fatigue and negative emotions, and reducing their sense of job satisfaction [54]. However, the strength of this association varied across burnout dimensions; the strongest effects were observed on EE and overall burnout, followed by DP, with a relatively smaller impact on PA. This pattern suggests that psychological distress tends to manifest first as emotional resource depletion and overall burnout, which may later extend to depersonalization and reduced accomplishment [55]. This developmental process is consistent with classic burnout development, where EE is often the earliest and most sensitive core symptom, DP reflects defensive detachment, and PA involves deeper self-evaluation impairment [54, 56].

Self-efficacy was negatively correlated with burnout and demonstrated a partial mediating role in the structural equation model, indicating that an individual’s belief in their own capabilities, task management, and problem-solving may serve as a key psychological mechanism linking psychological distress to burnout [21, 55, 57]. Individuals with higher self-efficacy are more likely to interpret work pressure as manageable challenges rather than overwhelming burdens, thereby maintaining better work engagement and professional resilience [23, 55]. Conversely, when psychological distress increases, self-perceptions of competence and control tend to decline, predisposing individuals to burnout. This finding carries practical significance: intervention strategies should not only focus on reducing external stressors but also on enhancing self-efficacy through competency training, situational support, peer supervision, positive feedback, and career development guidance. For healthcare professionals in tertiary teaching hospitals who shoulder dual clinical and teaching responsibilities, strengthening self-efficacy may also improve adaptability in mentoring, communication, and complex case management.

Limitations

Despite several important findings, this study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes establishing causal relationships between variables; future longitudinal research is needed to validate the predictive effects of each factor. Second, the sample was drawn exclusively from healthcare institutions in the same region, potentially limiting the generalizability of the results. Third, while major confounding factors were controlled for, certain potential variables (such as department type, social support, and specific workload) were not included in the analysis. Finally, self-reporting methods may introduce common method bias; future research could employ multi-method validation by incorporating objective indicators such as turnover rates and work performance.

Conclusions

This study examines factors affecting burnout among medical personnel in Chinese tertiary teaching hospitals. Work tenure, income, mental health risk, and self-efficacy show significant links with burnout, with self-efficacy partly mediating the effect of psychological distress. Marital and parental status are primarily tied to depersonalization, indicating their role in workplace relationships and emotional engagement. Differences across professional roles reflect the combined demands of clinical, teaching, and research duties. These findings suggest that burnout interventions should not only reduce stress but also strengthen self-efficacy, improve financial incentives, support mental health, and tailor assistance according to family structure and career stage. Future studies using longitudinal designs and deeper analysis of mechanisms can help clarify how these factors interact and contribute to developing systematic occupational health support systems.
