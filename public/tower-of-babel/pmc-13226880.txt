Climate change awareness, motivation, and behaviours among primary health care nurses in South Africa: findings from the adapted CHANT survey
Authors: Thandazile Sibindi, Jennifer-Anne Chipps, Talitha Crowley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClimate change increasingly affects global health, with low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) facing recurrent droughts, heatwaves, and extreme weather events that strain primary health care (PHC) systems. Nurses, as frontline providers, are critical for managing climate-sensitive conditions and promoting resilience. Evidence on nurses’ climate-health engagement in LMICs remains limited.ObjectiveTo assess awareness, motivation, and behaviours related to climate change among primary health care (PHC) nurses in the Western Cape, South Africa, using a contextualised Climate, Health, and Nursing Tool (CHANT).MethodsA descriptive cross-sectional survey was conducted in 38 PHC facilities. An all-inclusive sampling approach yielded 128 professional nurses. Data were collected via a self-administered questionnaire adapted for local context. Descriptive statistics and non-parametric tests were applied.ResultsMost respondents (76.2%) were aware of climate change, primarily through television (75%) and social media (60.9%). Two-thirds (66.7%) had experienced extreme weather events, and 74.6% had managed climate-sensitive conditions, notably respiratory illnesses. Concern and motivation were high (90%), and 70% believed mitigation is possible; however, 51.6% perceived the issue as complex, and 25% felt overwhelmed. Climate-friendly behaviours were more frequent at home (15–77%) than at work (12–45%), and only 9% engaged in climate communication with policymakers.ConclusionDespite strong concern and motivation, workplace climate actions remain limited. Strengthening nurses’ sustainability knowledge and institutional support is essential for advancing climate-resilient PHC systems.

Introduction and background

Climate change is transforming global health and is necessitating the integration of environmental sustainability into healthcare systems [1]. Primary health care (PHC) provides a critical framework for advancing planetary health through preventive, adaptive, and community-oriented strategies [2]. Nurses, as key PHC actors, deliver comprehensive care and are at the frontline of climate-sensitive health impacts. These include heat-related illnesses, respiratory disorders, vector-borne diseases, and malnutrition [3].

The nexus between health and climate vulnerability is particularly evident in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). This results from socio-economic inequalities, urban density, and dependence on climate-sensitive resources, which increase vulnerability to droughts, floods, heatwaves, and declining air and water quality [2,4]. Informed by the Alma-Ata and Astana Declarations, PHC care in LMICs seeks to enhance universal health coverage (UHC) and promote Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) [[5], [6]]. However, integrating climate adaptation into PHC requires nurses who are clinically competent and environmentally literate, a concept central to eco-nursing [[7], [8]].

Evidence on LMIC nurses’ climate-related knowledge, motivation, and practices remains scarce. Understanding these dimensions is essential for developing a climate-smart workforce capable of promoting adaptation and mitigation within PHC settings [9]. Strengthening workforce capacity on sustainability is critical for building climate-resilient health systems and addressing inequities exacerbated by climate change [10].

The Climate, Health, and Nurses Tool (CHANT), grounded in the Integrated Change (I-Change) Model, offers a structured framework for assessing behavioural determinants of climate-responsive practice [[11], [12]]. The model conceptualises change as a phased, cyclical process of awareness, motivation, and action, reinforced by feedback loops [12]. Awareness stimulates motivation, shaped by attitudes, norms, and self-efficacy, which then translate into behaviour. Feedback from experience deepens awareness or reshapes motivation, illustrating the dynamic nature of behavioural change [[12], [13]].

Awareness

Awareness encompasses cognitive understanding and emotional concern about climate-health linkages. In PHC contexts, this includes recognising climate impacts on disease patterns and system sustainability [14]. Climate literacy among nurses is vital in LMICs, where communities face droughts, water scarcity, and infectious disease outbreaks [15]. Because gender is socially constructed and not binary, patterns of climate awareness and concern vary across contexts and identities; some studies report higher climate concern among women in wealthier settings and highly context‑dependent differences in others, emphasising the need for inclusive education and gender‑responsive capacity‑building [16,17]. Concern transforms knowledge into moral engagement, creating urgency to act [[18], [19]].

Motivation

Motivation links awareness to behavioural intention and is shaped by attitudes, optimism, and professional identity [12,20]. Nurses with strong pro-environmental attitudes and perceived efficacy are more likely to engage in sustainable practices, though resource constraints and competing demands can hinder motivation [[21], [22]]. Optimism, belief in meaningful change, drives engagement, reinforced by institutional support and peer recognition [11,23].

Action

Action represents the translation of intention into practice through eco-nursing behaviours such as waste reduction, resource conservation, and climate-sensitive health education [24]. Behavioural change is shaped by both individual capacity and organisational readiness, and experiential learning [[25], [26]]. Workshops and local adaptation projects can help bridge knowledge–practice gaps, particularly where formal environmental health training is limited [[27], [28]]. Communication is central to strengthening these behaviours, as nurses’ ability to convey climate risks and adaptation strategies influences patient actions, interprofessional collaboration, and community engagement [[29], [30]]. Higher education and postgraduate training further enhance confidence in communicating scientific information [31].

Global guidance positions PHC nurses as central to achieving UHC and strengthening climate-resilient health systems, particularly in LMICs, where nurses form the backbone of frontline service delivery [6,9]. WHO’s operational framework emphasises that climate-resilient health systems are essential to maintain safe, high-quality care amid increasing climate pressures and are integral to UHC efforts [9]. At the same time, the International Council of Nurses identifies nurses as critical climate actors whose leadership in mitigation and adaptation strategies is necessary to safeguard population health and system resilience [18]. Emerging case evidence, including Health Care Without Harm’s multinational studies, demonstrates that nurse-led sustainability initiatives can improve operational resilience, reduce emissions, and enhance continuity of care benefits, especially relevant to resource-strained PHC systems in LMICs [32]. Professional nurses constitute the largest PHC cadre in South Africa and frequently lead nurse-driven integrated clinical services, including triage, protocol-based prescribing, and chronic disease management [33]. Their central role in frontline service delivery positions them as pivotal actors in climate- and health-related functions such as patient education, community-level surveillance, and facility-based quality improvement initiatives. Given their broad scope of practice and established leadership within PHC operations, professional nurses are strategically placed to embed climate-resilience measures into routine, day-to-day service delivery, strengthening both continuity and quality of care under changing environmental conditions.

Aim of this study

To assess awareness, motivation, and behaviours related to climate change among PHC nurses in the Western Cape Province.

Methods

Study setting

The research was carried out in the Western Cape Province of South Africa, which includes the City of Cape Town, five district councils, and 24 municipalities [34]. Data were gathered from PHC facilities administered by the Western Cape Government and the City of Cape Town. Cape Town was selected for its dense population, large informal settlements, and high vulnerability to climate-related events [35]. The province has 428 PHC facilities, 93 within the Cape Town Metropole [[34], [35]].

Study design

A descriptive cross-sectional survey was conducted on nurses working in PHC facilities using a self-administered CHANT that was contextualised to South Africa.

Study population and sampling

The target population was comprised of 427 professional nurses working in the Cape Town Metropole. The accessible population1 (n = 202) was the number of professional nurses working in the facilities that granted access. An all-inclusive sampling approach yielded 128 respondents (63% of the accessible population; 97% of the calculated sample size based on 60% awareness ±5%) [[36], [37]]. Enrolled nurses and auxiliaries were excluded due to their limited scope of practice [38].

Data collection instrument

Data were collected using a self-administered questionnaire using the CHANT scales, contextualised with author permission for the South African context [11]. The CHANT survey measures nurses’ awareness, concern, motivation, and climate-related behaviours through 15 5-point Likert-scale items across these four domains (range of scores 0-4). The contextualisation of the tool was achieved through a structured two-phase process. In the first phase, the researcher and her supervisors reviewed all items for cultural and contextual relevance, revising terminology and examples to reflect local climate, health, and language. In the second phase, expert input was obtained from climate-health and public health specialists in academia and municipal services. Following expert review, we refined terminology (plain language), inserted locally salient examples (drought, flooding, wind storms, wildfire smoke, malnutrition, and vector/water-borne risks), and elaborated on sustainability practices feasible in PHC (waste segregation, energy conservation during load-shedding). The contextualisation ensured relevance to African health system contexts while maintaining the tool’s reliability and validity for assessing nurses’ awareness, motivation, and behaviours related to climate change within South Africa’s PHC system [11]. Items on climate-sensitive diseases did not condition responses on the likelihood that the presentation was climate-attributable as the decision was made to keep the items as they were in the original CHANT survey. This may have led to conservative overestimation for common conditions (e.g., anxiety, depression). The survey was administered in English; in South Africa, professional nurse training and clinical documentation are done in English.

Data collection

A brief feasibility pilot was conducted with ten professional nurses from the Cape Town Metropole who had PHC experience who were not stationed in PHC facilities. The pilot assessed the tool’s clarity, logical flow, and completion time. Minor wording changes were made to improve readability and local contextualisation; the number of items and core content remained unchanged. Given the study’s scope and the limited nature of the adaptations, formal psychometric testing (e.g., test–retest reliability, item–total correlations) was not undertaken.

After a pilot study, data were gathered from October 2023 to March 2024. The researcher personally visited all clinics and invited professional nurses present to participate in the study, gave them information sheets, and got their signed consent. To maximise response rate, paper surveys were given out and collected when respondents had a chance to complete them. Facility managers were contacted in advance to arrange suitable visits and distributed the REDCap link over email and WhatsApp to the staff for those who preferred online questions. For paper surveys, the researcher remained onsite to answer questions; information sheets and the online landing page provided contact details for subsequent queries.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29 [39]. Only responses from professional nurses in the City of Cape Town and Western Cape Government Northern-Tygerberg substructure facilities were included. Paper-based questionnaires were uploaded into REDCap and exported to SPSS. Missing data were verified against originals and removed only if random and minimal (<5%). Variables were recoded for consistency; demographic categories (e.g., novice ≤10 years, experienced >10 years) and aggregated Likert-scale items enhanced interpretability. Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviation (sd), frequencies, 95% confidence intervals (CI)) summarised key domains. Composite scores were calculated for five domains: Awareness (mean familiarity with evidence-based statements), Concern (mean concern about climate impacts), Motivation (mean of motivational items), Behaviours (mean of actions at home and work), and Communication (mean of communication-related responses). Reliability for each domain was confirmed using Cronbach’s alpha (α = 0.78–.90). Group differences by gender, experience, and education level were analysed using Mann–Whitney U tests, appropriate for non-parametric data.

Validity and reliability

Construct validity was supported by prior psychometric testing (RMSEA = 0.04; SRMR = 0.90) [39]. Content validity was confirmed via expert review. The questionnaire mapped items to theoretical constructs across domains: awareness, concern, motivation, behaviour and communication. Consistent with the I-Change model, concern, motivation, and optimism were treated as related yet theoretically distinct factors that influence behaviour change. Concern reflects risk and affects appraisal regarding climate and health harms (pre-motivational awareness factors). Motivation indexes intention to act that arises from negative or positive attitudes, social influence (norms, modelling, support), and self-efficacy (confidence to act), the core motivational determinants in I-Change [12]. Optimism captures expectancy beliefs about the feasibility and effectiveness of individual/collective action (e.g., that further climate change can be prevented or impacts can be managed). Optimism was treated as a complementary expectancy construct that can shape intention formation and persistence while remaining analytically distinct from motivation’s attitude-norm-efficacy components. Behaviour (at home and at work) was conceptualised as the observable enactment of target actions, i.e., their occurrence, and frequency, that follows intention and is shaped by post-motivational factors [12]. Communication was operationalised (personal, professional, policy) as a behaviour domain in the post-motivational action phase (i.e., a measurable action in its own right), while acknowledging that communicative behaviours can also mediate links between motivation and broader system impact (e.g., by enabling advocacy and uptake of climate and health practices). This mapping aligns with the I-Change’s phased structure (awareness-motivation-action) and with its specification of determinants and post-motivational processes (e.g., action planning, skills). Cronbach’s alpha confirmed internal consistency for these domains: Awareness (α = 0.80), Concern (α = 0.90), Optimism (α = 0.87), Motivation (α = 0.79), Behaviours at home/work (α = 0.78), and Communication (α = 0.81).

Results

Sample realisation and demographics

A total of 128 participants out of 202 completed the survey (63.4% response rate), with 38.3% responding online and 61.7% on paper. Respondents were predominantly female (71.1%), with a mean age of 41.1 years (sd ±9.8; range 23–64). Most were employed at City of Cape Town PHC centres (78.9%). Over half held a postgraduate specialist nursing qualification (55.6%). Awareness of climate change evidence

The overall awareness mean score was 2.44/4 [95% CI: 1.70–1.97], based on a composite of awareness items from Shenk et al. [11]. In a gender analysis based on the study-specific broader set of awareness questions, female respondents showed noticeably more awareness than their male counterparts (1.7 ± 0.7 vs. 1.4 ± 0.6; U = −2.230, p = .026). Overall, 60–70% of participants exhibited moderate to high familiarity with evidence-based statements connecting human activity, climate change, and health consequences. The highest familiarity was acknowledgement of the influence of human actions on greenhouse gas emissions (68.8%), followed by the awareness of health effects (e.g., heat stroke, asthma) and the unequal consequences on vulnerable communities (65.3% each). Awareness of global warming trends since the 1980s was marginally lower at 62.4%. Experience of seeing climate-sensitive diseases

Experience of seeing patients with climate-sensitive diseases can also enhance climate change awareness. Frequencies for all climate-sensitive diseases are interpreted cautiously given that responses were not limited to climate-attributable presentations. Attribution of these diseases to climate change is methodologically complex; our measures captured clinical observations, not causal attribution. The purpose was just to determine their awareness of climate-sensitive diseases as adopted from the original CHANT survey. Respondents regularly observed climate-sensitive diseases in clinical practice, with 60–80% indicating frequent to very frequent occurrences for four out of seven categories. Respiratory conditions were the most prevalent (74.6%), followed by waterborne diseases (59.7%), vector-borne diseases (58.6%), and heat-related illnesses (52.6%). Instances of physical trauma associated with extreme weather were infrequent (29.1%), and very few reported encounters with mental health issues (7.6%) or nutritional disorders (7.8%) (Table 5). Personal or familial experience was most prevalent for respiratory issues (50.8%), followed by heat-related illnesses (43.9%) and mental health issues (42.6%), although waterborne (4.1%) and vector-borne (3.2%) infections were few. Observations of climate-sensitive conditions among people not known personally were most prevalent for mental health issues (65.3%), respiratory problems (63.4%), heat-related illnesses (57.2%), and physical trauma (55.6%). There was a lower prevalence of waterborne diseases (35.5%) and vector-borne diseases (33%) (Table 5). The data indicate that nurses mostly experience respiratory, heat-related, and mental health effects of climate change, whereas impacts related to infectious diseases are seldom experienced by nurses Attitudes towards climate change

Concern about climate change results from being aware of climate change and its effects. The concern mean score was 3.0/4 [95% CI 2.82–3.14]. Experienced nurses displayed significantly higher concern than novice nurses (3.9 ± 0.9 experienced respondents vs. 2.8 ± 0.9 novice respondents, U = −2.141, p = .032). Across five concern statements, 60–90% expressed moderate to extreme concern, with the highest levels for health impacts (77%), effects on self/family (80.3%), and future generations (74%). Attitudes towards climate change.

Optimism can enhance awareness and promote behaviour change in response to climate change. The optimism mean score was 2.85/4 [95% CI 2.66–3.03], with no significant difference between experienced and novice respondents (2.9 ± 1.0 experienced respondents vs 2.8 ± 1.0 novice respondents, U = 0.257, p=.797). Most respondents were moderately optimistic, with 68.6% believing further climate change can be prevented and 62.1% confident in human capacity to adapt (Table 6). These findings indicate high concern for health-related impacts and cautious optimism regarding mitigation and adaptation.

Pro-environmental behaviours.

Workplace behaviours scored lower (m = 1.56/4 [95% CI 1.24–1.70]) with no significant difference between the experienced and novice respondents (1.51±0.79 experienced respondents vs. 1.62±0.74 novice respondents, U = −0.347, p = 0.729). Waste reduction (36.9%) and energy conservation (36.6%) were most common, while advocacy for organisational policies and structural changes was rare (9.8%).

Communication scored lowest (m = 1.47/4 [95% CI 1.25–1.68]), with respondents with postgraduate training reporting significantly higher scores than respondents with undergraduate training only (1.7 ± 1.3 respondents with postgraduate education vs. 1.2 ± 1.1 respondents with undergraduate education, U = 1.996, p = .046). Personal communication was more common (47.1%) than professional (35%) or engagement with officials, community leaders, or other audiences (9%). Overall, respondents favoured simple individual actions at home and work, with limited structural behaviours and low levels of professional or public communication (Table 10).

Discussion

This study provides the first documented assessment of PHC nurses’ climate engagement in South Africa using the Climate, Health, and Nursing Tool (CHANT).

Awareness

Awareness of climate change underpins environmental behaviours and motivates nurses to act against environmental health hazards. Findings revealed moderate awareness, indicating partial understanding and aligning with trends in high-income countries, though evidence from LMICs remains limited [11,40]. This reflects a global gap in climate literacy within nursing. Gender disparities, with heightened awareness among female nurses, may relate to caregiving roles [41], but research suggests access to quality information is more influential [40,42]. Heavy reliance on informal sources (75% via TV/news) creates fragmented knowledge and misinformation, a phenomenon noted globally [40]. These findings emphasise that informal learning cannot replace structured education. Integrating climate-health topics into nursing curricula and continuing education, complemented by experiential learning in PHC settings, is essential for developing contextually relevant environmental behaviours.

Concern

Concern forms the cognitive and emotional foundation for behaviour change, influencing readiness to engage in climate-health initiatives [43]. Nurses expressed high concern, particularly for personal and family health, consistent with proximity-driven engagement [11]. Experienced nurses reported greater concern, supporting the I-Change model’s link between cumulative exposure and perceived vulnerability [12]. Contrasting evidence from high-income contexts shows heightened concern among novices, reflecting generational influences shaped by evolving curricula [43]. Strengthening formal education and leveraging mentorship can harmonise these differences and translate concern into proactive, evidence-based practices.

Motivation

Motivation strongly influences climate-related behaviours and adoption of eco-nursing practices [12]. Nurses demonstrated high motivation, driven by health protection and family wellbeing, echoing global findings [30,44]. However, motivation rarely translated into workplace action due to systemic barriers such as resource constraints, workload, and unclear accountability [45]. This shows motivation alone is insufficient without enabling systems. There is a need for simplified, context-specific information and supportive organisational environments. Embedding motivational strategies into education, mentorship, and leadership development can enhance self-efficacy and sustained engagement [11,30,46].

Optimism

Optimism, a key psychological determinant of pro-environmental behaviour, reinforces belief in the efficacy of individual and collective action [23]. Nurses reported moderate optimism, reflecting agency but uncertainty about systemic capacity. Compared with diminished optimism reported in high-income settings, these findings suggest growing confidence, possibly shaped by rising climate-health awareness [11]. Optimism was higher among experienced nurses, indicating that professional exposure creates constructive hope rather than eco-anxiety [23]. Reinforcing optimism through success stories, peer learning, and positive narratives in education and leadership programs can strengthen resilience.

Behaviour

Climate-related behaviours were generally low, with slightly higher engagement at home than in workplaces. This pattern reflects greater personal control in household settings and aligns with global findings [11,45]. In clinical contexts, infection prevention and emergency priorities often overshadow environmental concerns [11]. Moreover, PHC facilities in LMICs face financial and human resource constraints, limiting sustainable infrastructure investment [15]. Experienced nurses showed stronger home-based behaviours, possibly due to accumulated professional intuition [11,43]. Consistently low workplace engagement across experience levels accentuates organisational culture as a critical determinant. System-level interventions and integration of sustainability into PHC operations are essential for translating awareness into action [44,[47], [48]].

Evidence of PHC climate adaptation in comparable LMIC settings

To contextualise the limited workplace climate-health actions observed in this study, it is valuable to consider examples where PHC systems in comparable LMIC settings have implemented feasible, scalable adaptation strategies. One such example is the city of Ahmedabad, which developed Southeast Asia’s first city-level Heat Action Plan (HAP) [48]. The plan combines early warning systems, training for health workers, and community outreach to help people prepare for excessive heat [48]. Since its introduction, the HAP has been associated with significant reductions in heat-related illness and death, making it a recognised model for urban heat resilience [48]. Another example is Fiji, where, together with the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Ministry of Health and Medical Services (MOHMS), it is implementing a Climate-Resilient Health Facilities Programme. The initiative involves conducting hazard and vulnerability assessments in >200 PHC facilities to identify climate-related risks [[49], [50]]. The findings guide infrastructure upgrades that strengthen energy resilience, water security, and disaster preparedness, ensuring health facilities remain safe and functional during climate impacts [[49], [50]]. Thirdly, Nigeria’s Renewable Energy for Primary Health Care (RE4PHC) Project, led by the United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF) and eHealth Africa (EHA), is installing solar power systems in PHC facilities [51]. Between 2024 and 2026, the project is solarising 238 PHC facilities, ensuring reliable electricity for vaccine cold chains, diagnostic equipment, and essential maternal and child health services [51].

These experiences demonstrate that PHC-level climate adaptation is achievable when supported by governance, infrastructure investment, and clear operational protocols. They provide a practical foundation for the implications and recommendations stated below.

Communication

Effective communication is vital for shaping behaviours and promoting sustainable action [30]. Communication scores were low, with postgraduate-trained nurses performing better. This was consistent with evidence that advanced education enhances research utilisation and advocacy [[52], [53]]. Empowering nurses with climate-health communication skills through targeted training and postgraduate programs can strengthen community engagement and resilience.

Recommendations

The study results support a recommendation for comprehensive, continuous professional development to strengthen nurses’ expertise in climate health and sustainable clinical practice. Nursing programs at all levels should integrate content on sustainability, environmental health, and climate change to prepare nurses for a changing climate. Nurse educators require targeted training to effectively apply environmental health principles in teaching, supervision, and evaluation. Governance structures should facilitate the designation of facility-level green teams and a climate champion with clear role descriptions and reporting lines. For workforce and finance, the study recommends protected time for quality improvement (QI) and micro-grants for low-cost interventions. In the infrastructure and operations of the facilities, there is a need for procurement, energy/water resilience, and waste management processes that meet global best practice standards for safety, environmental sustainability and climate resilience. To help keep people safe, local clinics need to be connected to the city’s early warning systems. They also need to put simple heat-health procedures in place, for example, having a plan for checking patients during heatwaves, and setting up cool rooms where people can recover from extreme heat. Enrolled and auxiliary nurses should be included in future studies in order to capture team level dynamics in addressing climate and health issues. The study should be expanded to more contexts such as the rural setting and other countries. For future scale-up, forward and back translation of survey tools with formal psychometric testing is recommended.

Limitations of the study

The study was confined to one urban PHC substructure in Cape Town, excluding rural and farming contexts. Limited familiarity with climate-health concepts and the length of the questionnaire contributed to missing responses. The original CHANT survey did not constrain responses to climate-sensitive attributable cases, particularly for mental health, and this survey adopted it as is; thus, observed frequencies should be interpreted cautiously. Future applications should preface items with concrete attribution cues (e.g., timing with heatwaves, flood exposure). Workplace items mix agency-enabled (counselling, advocacy) and agency-limited (infrastructure/procurement) behaviours, which could have led to low scores reflecting lack of control; future versions should tag items by level of control. The original CHANT and this survey did not differentiate COPD exacerbation aetiologies thus future surveys should link clinical data to heat/air quality events, given evidence of temperature and air pollution effects. Radio was omitted from the information source list despite its high reach in Southern Africa; future adaptations will include it.

Conclusion

Despite strong concern and motivation, climate-related action among PHC nurses remains limited largely due to gaps in training, low decision-making autonomy, and persistent resource constraints. This study shows that while PHC nurses in LMICs demonstrate moderate awareness, high concern, and a clear willingness to act, structural barriers prevent this motivation from translating into practice. Higher qualifications were associated with greater confidence and communication capacity, reinforcing the World Health Organization’s emphasis on workforce development as a foundation for climate-resilient health systems [9]. Strengthening pre-service curricula, continuous professional development, and hands-on experiential learning supported by enabling policy and institutional reforms is therefore essential to empower nurses as leaders of equitable, low-carbon, climate-resilient PHC systems. Future implementation research should focus on identifying practical levers within PHC settings that can unlock and sustain nurse-led climate action.
