Background

Nowadays, cigarette smoke poses a crucial risk factor for numerous cardiovascular diseases, especially acute myocardial infarction and ischemic stroke, both of which are pathologically rooted in atherosclerosis (AS) [1]. As a well-documented contributor for atherosclerotic progression, smoking is associated with a 1.7-fold increased risk of all-cause cardiovascular disease and a 1.6-fold increased risk of all-cause coronary heart disease compared with non-smokers [2]. Also, clinical data revealed that in individuals with a history of heavy smoking, the elevated risk of cardiovascular events remains significant for over 25 years following smoking cessation [3]. Despite these findings, the molecular mechanisms underlying smoking-induced atherosclerotic development remain inadequately understood.

CD4+ T cells encompass various subtypes, including Th1, Th2, Th17, Treg, etc., fulfilling distinct roles within the immune system, such as assistance with other immune cells (B cells and CD8+ T cells), participating in anti-viral and anti-tumor responses [4, 5]. The secretion of granzyme A (Gzma) represents a classical pathway through which CD4+ T cells exert cytotoxic effects [6–8]. It has been demonstrated that the percentage and number of certain subtypes of CD4+ T cells, such as Th1 and Th17, increased significantly during atherosclerotic progression [9]. Also, interactions between B cells and CD4+ T cells could exacerbate inflammatory responses within local atherosclerotic plaques [10]. Notwithstanding this, the precise role and activation mechanisms of different CD4+ T subcelltypes in the pathogenesis of AS require further investigation.

As pivotal components of the immune system, the differentiation and activation of CD4+T cells are intricately regulated, this process is initiated by either classical antigen-presenting cells or non-classical antigen-presenting cells [11, 12]. Historically, research have predominantly concentrated on elucidating the molecular regulatory mechanisms involved in antigen presentation by classical antigen-presenting cells, including dendritic cells, macrophages, and B cells [13–15]. However, recent years have witnessed a growing focus on the interactions between non-classical antigen-presenting cells and CD4+ T cells [6, 16, 17], with particular attention to the interplay between endothelial cells (ECs) and CD4+ T cells [18]. Despite this emerging interest, there remains a paucity of studies investigating the effects and underlying mechanisms of non-classical antigen-presenting cells, particularly ECs, during the progression of AS.

Antigen-presenting of non-classical cells act as prominent roles in various diseases, including cancer, autoimmune diseases, etc [16, 19–21]. Specifically, antigen presentation to CD4+T cells via non-classical cells is primarily mediated through two signals: (1) The first signal arises from the interaction of CD4+ T cells receptors and major histocompatibility complex II (MHC II) [22]; (2) The second signal is generated by the interaction of CD28 and co-stimulatory molecules, CD80 or CD86 [23]. As a canonical transcriptional regulatory protein, the class II major histocompatibility complex transactivator (CIITA) facilitates the expression of MHC II through nuclear translocation, thereby enhancing the activation of CD4+ T cells. Furthermore, this regulatory process has been implicated in multiple diseases [24–27], Nevertheless, the presence of this transcriptional regulatory process in the development of AS and its potential molecular mechanisms remain unclear.

In the present study, we carried out a series of studies in vivo and in vitro to elucidate how cigarette smoking facilitated the activation of CD4+ T cells through MHCII-mediated antigen presentation in ECs, as well as the specific molecular regulatory mechanisms initiated by CIITA nuclear translocation during atherosclerotic progression. The current manuscript expands our understanding of the mechanisms by which smoking accelerates AS and provides novel therapeutic targets for the treatment of smoking-related AS.

Methods

Study design and patients

All participants in this study were sourced from the Department of Cardiology at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University. The study design received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University (approval code: YJSKY2021-104). Blood samples (5 ml) from patients with coronary heart disease (CHD) were collected following the acquisition of informed consent. The investigation adhered to the principles established in the Declaration of Helsinki. The baseline clinical characteristics of the participants are comprehensively detailed in Additional File 1: Table 1. A thorough description of the study design is provided in Additional File 1: Fig. S2.

Single cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq)

For the scRNA-seq, a total of 60 aortas were collected from eight-week-old male ApoEKO mice (30 ApoEKO mice in high-fat diet (HD) + Tar group, 30 ApoEKO mice in HD group). Based on previous studies [28], as for the HD + Tar group, 30 ApoEKO mice were subjected to the systemic exposure drug delivery system (SIU48, Promech), receiving a specific administration protocol of exposure to 12 cigarettes over a 50-minute period, twice daily, five times per week. Concurrently, these mice were maintained on HD (21% fat, 0.15% cholesterol; MD12015; Medicine Ltd., Jiangsu, China), for a duration of 16 weeks. Meanwhile, the remaining 30 ApoEKO mice (HD group) were also placed into the systemic drug delivery system (Free inhalation of air in the system, without cigarette smoke inhalation) for an equivalent daily duration as the experimental group, and were also fed with HD for 16 weeks. Every 15 blood vessels were pooled to form a single sample for further scRNA-seq analysis. Consequently, a total of four samples were utilized for scRNA-seq, comprising two samples in the HD group and two samples in the HD + Tar group (2 vs. 2). The Seurat software (version 4.1.1) was employed for data integration and dimensional reduction. Subsequent analyses focused on subgroups exhibiting significant differences and included pseudo-temporal analysis, intercellular communication analysis, transcription factor prediction, and protein activity inference, among others. Detailed methodologies are provided in the Additional File 1: Methods section [29–37].

Animal experiments

The study on the ApoEKO mice received approval from the Institutional Research Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University (Approval No. YJSDW2023-031, 2023/09). The Additional File 1: Methods section provided the detailed description [10, 28, 38–59].

Cell experiments

Mouse arterial endothelial cells (MAECs) and human coronary artery endothelial cells (HCAECs) were exposed to cigarette tar at a concentration of 100 µg/mL for a duration of 24 h in view of the previous study [60]. Following this treatment, analyses were conducted to assess RNA expression and protein expression levels. Additionally, flow cytometry analysis was performed after co-culturing with CD4+ T cells, and mass spectrometry analysis was carried out. Further methodological details are available in the Additional File 1: Methods section [60–64].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 21.0, with a p-value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant. The detailed description of the statistical analysis are provided in the Additional File 1: Methods section.

Results

Cigarette tar contributes to the progression of AS in ApoEKO mice

To investigate the pro-atherogenic effects of cigarette tar, as indicated by previous literature [28], we established atherosclerotic animal models (ApoEKO mice, male, HD for 16 weeks), followed by administration of cigarette tar inhalation. Hematoxylin-eosin and masson staining techniques were employed to assess the lesion area and collagen fiber content within atherosclerotic plaques. The findings demonstrated that exposure to cigarette tar resulted in an increased plaque area and reduced collagen fiber content within the atherosclerotic plaques compared with the HD group alone (Fig. 1A). Additionally, we observed a significant increase in lipid deposition within the aortic wall following cigarette tar treatment (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, compared with HD group, the cigarette tar exposure group exhibited disruptions in plasma lipid metabolism and an exacerbated inflammatory response, both of which are critical features of atherosclerotic development (Fig. 1C, D). Also, to further explore the specific impact of gender differences on atherosclerotic progression, female ApoEKO mice were also exposed to HD diet in conjunction with cigarette smoke administration. The findings indicated that female ApoEKO mice subjected to the combined treatment of cigarette smoke and HD diet exhibited significantly larger atherosclerotic plaque areas (Additional File 1: Fig. S1A) and a greater degree of aortic lipid deposition (Additional File 1: Fig. S1B) compared with those receiving HD diet only. Thus, cigarette tar inhalation could accelerate the progression of AS.

The results of dimension reduction analysis of T cells indicated that T cells could be categorized into 11 clusters and 8 subcelltypes based on top marker genes (Additional File 1: Fig. S7A-D), including CD4 naive T cells (CD4, Sell, Ccr7, cluster 4 and cluster 11), CD4 effector T cells (CD4, Gzma, Ifnγ, cluster 2 and cluster 9), Treg (Foxp3, Ikzf2, cluster 3), CD8 naive T cells (CD8a, Sell, Ccr7, cluster 5), CD8 cytotoxic T cells (CD8a, Gzma, Gzmb, cluster 1 and cluster 10), memory T cells (Ltb, Gpr183, cluster 7), proliferation T cells (Top2a, Mki67, cluster 8) and γδT cells (Trdc, Trdv4, cluster 6). Notably, the proportions of cluster 2 and cluster 9 (CD4 effector T cells) were increased respectively from 7% to 24% and from 2% to 6% of aortas treated with HD + tar compared with HD alone group (Fig. 2B), which was enriched fewer cells of cluster 2 and cluster 9 compared with cigarette tar-treated group (Additional File 1: Fig. S7E). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis, focusing on Tbx21 (Highly expressed and highly specific in cluster2 and cluster9)-targeted genes via Scenic analysis, suggested that T cell differentiation and leukocyte cell-cell adhesion are the primary functions of clusters 2 and 9 (Additional File 1: Fig. S8A-C), indicating that cytotoxicity and differentiation are the crucial functions of cluster 2/cluster 9. Also, cluster 2 exhibited low expression levels of Gzma, whereas cluster 9 demonstrated high expression levels of Gzma (Additional File 1: Fig. S7D). Consequently, they could be separately annotated as “Low-Gzma+ CD4 effector T cells (cluster 2)” and “High-Gzma+ CD4 effector T cells (cluster 9) (Fig. 2C). It is noteworthy that the top up-regulated genes, including Gzma, (Additional File 1: Fig. S6A) were more highly expressed in clusters 2 and 9 in the cigarette tar-treated group than in the HD alone group (Fig. 2C). These findings were further validated by protein activation analysis utilizing VIPER platform (Additional File 1: Fig. S9A-B), indicating a higher protein activity of Gzma in CD4+ T cells treated with cigarette tar. These findings align with the flow cytometry results observed at the protein level in the arteries, blood, and spleens of cigarette tar-treated ApoEKO mice (Fig. 2D-E, Additional File 1: Fig. S10A-C, Fig. S11A), as well as in blood of patients with CHD who have history of smoking (Fig. 2F, Additional File 1: Fig. S10D). Also, exposure to cigarette smoke resulted in a significant increase in the sizes of the spleen and thymus in ApoEKO mice, indicating that cigarette smoke may exacerbate the immune inflammatory response in vivo (Additional File 1: Fig. S11B). Furthermore, we observed a marked reduction in the proportion of Tregs (cluster 3), which are a classical subset of T cells known to exert anti-atherogenic effects primarily through anti-inflammatory mechanisms [65], decreasing from 17.5% to 7.0% (orange cluster), in the aorta of mice exposed to cigarette smoke (Fig. 2B). Also, Tregs (cluster 3) were fewer enriched in HD + Tar group than those in HD group (Additional File 1: Fig. S7E). Intriguingly, the proportion of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells exhibited an opposing trend, with cluster 1 (annotated as “Low-Gzma CD8 cytotoxic T cells) decreasing from 33% to 19% (light green cluster) and cluster 10 (annotated as “High-Gzma CD8 cytotoxic T cells) increasing from 2% to 3% (dark purple cluster), as illustrated in Fig. 2B and Additional File 1: Fig. S7C-D. This observation was corroborated by flow cytometry analyses of the aorta, blood, and spleen of ApoEKO mice exposed to cigarette smoke, which indicated that the proportion of CD8+Gzma+ T cells in these three organs did not change significantly under cigarette smoke exposure (Additional File 1: Fig. S12, Fig. S13).

In addition, the results of non-immune cells dimension reduction indicated the identification of four cell subtypes, including ECs (Cdh5, Pecam1, vwf), epithelial cells (Epcam, Cdh1, Krt8), fibroblasts (Dcn, Sox9, Pdgfra) and SMCs (Acta2, Tagln, Myh11) in view of top-marker genes (Fig. 2A, Additional File 1: Fig. S14). Concurrently, the proportion of ECs decreased from 4.4% to 0.5% in the aortas of group treated with cigarette tar (Fig. 2B), which was further valided in confocal immunofluorescence within atherosclerotic plaque (Additional File 1: Fig. S15). The GO enrichment analysis, based on Sox17-targeted genes via Scenic analysis, revealed that the primary function of ECs is the positive regulation of dendritic cell cytokine production (Additional File 1: Fig. S16A-C). Additionally, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes in ECs among non-immune cells suggested that focal adhesion, Yersinia infection, and Salmonella infection are significant functions of ECs (Additional File 1: Fig. S16D), indicating an association between EC function and immune response. Notably, MHC II-related genes, such as H2-Aa, H2-Ab1, and H2-Eb1, were identified as the most up-regulated genes in the cigarette tar-treated group (Additional File 1: Fig. S6B, Fig. 2G). The Gene Set Enrichment Analysis results further demonstrated that the ECs exhibited significant enrichment in functions related to antigen processing and presentation, response to interferon-gamma, positive regulation of T cell differentiation, and Graft-versus-host disease in the cigarette tar-treated group (Additional File 1: Fig.S16E). These findings were corroborated through RNA sequencing, immunofluorescence, and flow cytometry conducted both in vivo and in vitro (Fig. 2H-K, Additional File 1: Fig. S17A-B). Additionally, the expression of CD80, a co-stimulatory signal necessary for T cells activation, was up-regulated following cigarette tar administration, whereas no statistically significant differences were observed in CD86 expression (Additional File 1: Fig. S17C-F). Furthermore, spleen B cells, bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs), and bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) isolated from ApoEKO mice were exposed to cigarette tar in vitro, and the expression of the MHC II genes was subsequently assessed. PCR analysis indicated that exposure to cigarette tar did not result in a significant up-regulation of MHC II gene expression across these three cell types (Additional File 1: Fig. S18). Consequently, we hypothesize that cigarette tar may facilitate the activation of CD4+Gzma+ T cells by modulating the antigen presentation capabilities of ECs, thereby accelerating the progression of AS.

Cigarette tar facilitates the differentiation of CD4+ T cells, consequently expediting the progression of AS

Based on previously reported literature [66], we conducted a pseudotime analysis of CD4+ T cells using the Slingshot method to examine their differentiation trajectories. The analysis revealed a single lineage progression from CD4 naive T cells (clusters 4 and 11) to CD4 effector T cells (clusters 2 and 9) (Fig. 3A). In terms of gene expression along the lineage, there was an up-regulation of Gzma, Gzmb, and Klrg1, which are markers of cytotoxicity in T cells, as well as an increase in Zeb2 and Ccl5, markers associated with later stages of T cell differentiation (Fig. 3B, Additional File 1: Fig. S19). Conversely, there was a down-regulation of Sell, a marker indicative of earlier stages of T cell differentiation (Fig. 3C). The expression levels of Pdcd1, Tox, and Havcr2, which are markers of exhaustion, did not exhibit significant changes (Fig. 3D). Meanwhile, the GSEA analysis of CD4+Gzma+ T cells between HD and HD + Tar group suggest that the Granzyme A secretion, lamellipodium assembly (T cells establish immune synapses with antigen-presenting cells via morphological deformation) and mitochondrial respiration function were enhanced following cigarette tar inhalation (Additional File 1: Fig. S20A-B). These findings suggest that CD4+ T cells tend to adopt cytotoxic and differentiated states following cigarette tar administration. Hence, we propose that cigarette tar could facilitate the progression of AS by increasing the proportion of circulating CD4+Gzma+ T cells. To substantiate this, a CD4+ T cells transfection ApoEKO mice model was performed, wherein CD4+ T cells (with or without anti-Gzma administration) (Fig. 3E) from ApoEKO mice (spleen) subjected to HD and HD + Tar administration were injected into other ApoEKO mice. To eliminate the possibility of an increase in CD4+ T cells resulting from the auto-immune response in the mice, we conducted a splenectomy on the recipient mice prior to transfection [39]. This procedure aimed to assess the severity of atherosclerotic plaque lesions. The findings revealed that CD4+ T cells from the HD + Tar mice significantly accelerated the progression of AS, as evidenced by a larger plaque area and aggravated inflammatory response, which could be alleviated by the anti-Gzma administration (Fig. 3F-G). Noteworthy, the administration of CD4+ T cells and anti-Gzma did not affect lipid metabolism (Fig. 3H), indicating that CD4+Gzma+ T cells, when exposed to cigarette tar, could expedite the progression of AS by enhancing the inflammatory response. Furthermore, to validate the CD4+ T-cell dependence in ApoEKO mice subjected to cigarette tar exposure, we administered anti-CD4 and anti-IgG treatments to ApoEKO mice (under HD and cigarette smoke inhalation conditions). The results indicate that anti-CD4 treatment significantly mitigates the severity of AS, as evidenced by a decrease in plaque area, reduced lipid deposition, and improved ECs continuity (Additional File 1: Fig. S21). Consequently, inhalation of cigarette tar prompts CD4+ T cells to advance towards a terminally differentiated state, marked by increased cytotoxicity and an augmented inflammatory response, thereby accelerating the progression of AS.

Cigarette tar promotes the antigen-presenting and cytotoxicity communication between ECs and Gzma+CD4+effector T cells within atherosclerotic plaque

The MHC II signaling pathway is a crucial mechanism for cell-cell interactions between antigen-presenting cells and CD4+ T cells [67, 68]. Consequently, we initially examined the likelihood of interactions among Gzma+ CD4 effector T cells, classical and non-classical antigen-presenting cells. The findings indicated that following tar inhalation, there was a significant increase exclusively in the interaction probability between ECs and Gzma+ CD4 effector T cells (Additional File 1: Fig. S22). To investigate and evaluate the specific effects of antigen-presenting ECs, we analyzed intercellular communication between ECs and Gzma+ T cells (including Gzma+CD4+ effector T cells and Gzma+CD8+ cytotoxic T cells) utilizing the CellChat platform [35]. Our analysis revealed an enhanced interaction of the MHC II signaling pathway between ECs and cluster 2 (Low-Gzma+ CD4 effector T cells) following cigarette tar treatment (Fig. 4A). This interaction involved H2-Aa-CD4, H2-Ab1-CD4, H2-Eb1-CD4, and H2-DMa-CD4 (Fig. 4A, Additional File 1: Fig. S23A, Fig. S24A). Consistent with these findings, the information flow of the MHC II signaling pathway was significantly more active in the cigarette tar-treated group compared with the HD alone group (Additional File 1: Fig. S24B). Additionally, we observed an interaction within the PARs signaling pathway between ECs and cluster 9 (High-Gzma+ CD4 effector T cells) after cigarette tar administration, involving the Gzma-F2r interaction (Fig. 4B, Additional File 1: Fig. S23B, Fig. S24A). Immunofluorescent analysis of mice subjected to cigarette tar revealed a greater aggregation of Gzma+ CD4+ T cells around ECs in the cigarette tar-treated cohort, as depicted in Fig. 4C-D. Consequently, we postulated that ECs subjected to cigarette tar exposure initiate the activation of CD4+Gzma+ T cells through MHC II antigen presentation. Subsequently, this subset of T cells induces cytotoxicity in ECs (The initiating pathological event of AS) via the release of Gzma.

To test this hypothesis, we performed a co-culture assay involving ECs and CD4+ T cells with/without OVA 323–339 administration, which were employed via flow cytometry and magnetic sorting techniques (Fig. 4E, Additional File 1: Fig. S25). These experiments demonstrated a higher proportion of CD69+CD25+ CD4+ T cells in the cigarette tar-treated group compared with the HD alone group (Fig. 4F-G). Simultaneously, upon the in vitro blockade of MHC II, a marked reduction in CD4+ T cell activation was observed (Additional File 1: Fig. S26). Also, in order to examine the cytotoxic impact of activated T cells on ECs, this study assessed the viability of ECs and the expression levels of Cle-caspase3, a critical apoptotic protein, following co-culture. The findings indicated that the viability of ECs in the group exposed to cigarette smoke was significantly reduced compared with the HD group, while the expression of Cle-caspase3 was markedly elevated (Additional File 1: Fig. S27). Furthermore, CD4+ T cells were isolated via magnetic bead sorting from the arterial blood of CHD patients, both with and without a history of smoking, as well as from the arterial blood and aorta of ApoE−/− mice, subjected to either cigarette tar inhalation or not. The isolated cells were subsequently treated with anti-Gzma and anti-IgG antibodies (Additional File 1: Fig. S28), followed by co-culture with HCAECs or MAECs. PI staining and CCK-8 assays demonstrated that treatment with anti-Gzma significantly improved the cytotoxic effect of CD4+ T cells derived from individuals with a smoking history or from mice exposed to cigarette tar on ECs (Additional File 1: Fig. S29). Intriguingly, we categorized the ECs into two distinct subgroups based on the expression levels of F2r: high and low expression via scRNA-seq, suggesting a significant upregulation of the Bax gene in the high F2r expression subgroup, whereas the Bcl-2 gene exhibited a marked increase in the low F2r expression subgroup (Additional File 1: Fig. S30A). Furthermore, apoptotic function was significantly enriched in the high F2r expression group (Additional File 1: Fig. S30B). Subsequently, we assessed MAECs apoptosis level in vivo by infusing CD4+ T cells (with or without anti-Gzma administration) derived from spleen of ApoEKO mice into other ApoEKO mice under splenectomy (Additional File 1: Fig. S31). The findings indicated that anti-Gzma significantly ameliorated MAECs apoptosis induced by CD4+ T cells in the HD + Tar group. In comparison to the group treated with HD + Tar-CD4+ T cells, there was a notable reduction in the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio and LDH content, alongside a significant enhancement in cell viability (Additional File 1: Fig. S32). These findings are consistent with the results in vitro (Additional File 1: Fig. S29). Collectively, these findings suggest that cigarette tar could enhance the activation of CD4+Gzma+ T cells by modulating antigen presentation by ECs, this subset of T cells is capable of inducing apoptosis in ECs through the release of Gzma, thereby facilitating the progression of AS, not just the conventional understanding of the direct cytotoxic effects of cigarette tar on ECs.

Cigarette tar increases MHC II expression via enhancing CIITA nuclear translocation in ECs

Previous studies have reported that CIITA regulates the expression of MHC II proteins through nuclear translocation [69, 70]. Therefore, to further investigate the underlying mechanism of the increased MHC II expression in cigarette tar-treated ECs, we hypothesize that cigarette tar treatment may enhance MHC II-related expression by facilitating CIITA nuclear translocation, thereby activating CD4+Gzma+ T cells. Confocal immunofluorescence analysis suggests that CIITA translocates from the cytoplasm to the nucleus following cigarette tar administration, a finding corroborated by Western Blot analysis (Fig. 5A, B). Furthermore, RNA-seq analyses revealed no significant upregulation of CIITA expression in ECs exposed to cigarette tar, indicating that cigarette tar may enhance MHC II gene expression primarily through facilitating the nuclear translocation of CIITA. This finding was corroborated by a correlation analysis between CIITA immunofluorescence and MHC II gene expression (Additional File 1: Fig. S33, Fig. S34). To specifically assess the impact of CIITA on atherosclerotic progression in vivo, AAV9-Tie2-sh-NC and AAV9-Tie2-sh-CIITA (EC-specific knockdown) [71–75] were administered via tail vein injection following cigarette tar inhalation administration (Additional File 1: Fig. S35A-B). The EC-specific knockdown of CIITA mitigated the progression of AS induced by cigarette tar, as evidenced by a reduction in atherosclerotic plaque area and aortic lipid deposition (Fig. 5C, Additional File 1: Fig. S36A), Nevertheless, EC-specific CIITA knockdown did not directly ameliorate cigarette tar-induced exacerbation of systemic inflammation in ApoEKO mice (Additional File 1: Fig. S36B). Additionally, CIITA knockdown in vivo led to a reduction in the infiltration of CD4+Gzma+ T cells (Fig. 5D-G, Additional File 1: Fig. S37A-C) and expression levels of MHC II (Fig. 5H). Also, Cdh5Cre mice were crossbred with CIITAflox/flox mice to produce the atherosclerotic mice model (AAV-PCSK9 injection) [56–59] featuring a conditional knockout of CIITA in ECs (Figure S35C-D, Additional File 1: Fig. S38A-B). The findings indicated that, under conditions of a HD diet and exposure to cigarette tar, the EC-specific CIITA knockout mice exhibited a significantly reduced plaque area in the aortic root, diminished lipid deposition/CD4+Gzma+ T cells infiltration in the aorta, and enhanced continuity of the ECs, in comparison to the CIITAflox/flox mice (Additional File 1: Fig. S38C-G). Collectively, these findings suggest that cigarette tar inhalation might facilitate the activation and infiltration of CD4+Gzma+ T cells within atherosclerotic plaques through CIITA-mediated MHC II expression in ECs.

The interaction of CIITA and PRMT5 control the MHC II expression

It has been documented that CIITA, as a transcriptional regulator, does not directly bind to the DNA domain [76]. To investigate the downstream proteins interacting with MHC II expression, mass spectrometry analysis was conducted in HCAECs to identify CIITA-specific associated proteins. These proteins were cross-referenced with predicted transcriptional regulatory proteins of HLA-DRA using CHIP-Atlas. A total of 13 proteins were identified as potential downstream interactors of CIITA involved in the regulation of MHC II expression (Fig. 6A). The proteins were ranked according to their scores in descending order, with PRMT5, a well-established protein arginine methyltransferase, occupying the top position (Fig. 6B). Subsequently, we utilized AlphaFold3 technology to predict the interaction between CIITA and PRMT5. Our analysis indicated significant hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions between CIITA and the TIM barrel domain of PRMT5 (Additional File 1: Fig. S39A-B). Furthermore, molecular docking and confocal immunofluorescence assays substantiated the potential interaction between CIITA and PRMT5, which was further enhanced by cigarette tar administration (Fig. 6C-E). The co-immunoprecipitation assay results demonstrate that cigarette tar treatment enhances the interaction between CIITA and PRMT5 within the nucleus (Fig. 6F) while diminishing their interaction in the cytoplasm (Fig. 6G). However, no significant effect is observed on the interaction strength between these proteins in total protein lysates (Fig. 6H).

To further investigate the synergistic regulation of MHC II expression by CIITA and PRMT5, we overexpressed these proteins in HCAECs with the administration of EPZ015666 (a specific inhibitor of PRMT5 activity) and assessed the expression levels of MHC II-related genes. CIITA and PRMT5 overexpression significantly increased the expression of HLA-DRA, HLA-DRB1, and HLA-DQB1, an effect that was reversed by EPZ015666 treatment (Additional File 1: Fig. S40A-C). These findings were corroborated by similar experiments in MAECs (Additional File 1: Fig. S40D-F). Collectively, these results suggest that cigarette tar administration may enhance the nuclear interaction between CIITA and PRMT5, thereby modulating the expression of MHC II-related genes.

Cigarette tar facilitates the PRMT5-mediated symmetrical dimethylation of H3R2, triggering the expression of MHC II in ECs and activation of CD4+T cells

To further elucidate the specific effects of PRMT5 on the expression of MHC II in ECs, we quantified the expression levels of MHC II genes using qPCR and flow cytometry across various experimental groups, including those treated with si-PRMT5 (Additional File 1: Fig. S41), EPZ015666, and cigarette tar. Our findings indicated that cigarette tar administration significantly upregulated the expression of MHC II genes, an effect that was reversed by the inhibition of PRMT5 through si-PRMT5 and EPZ015666 (Fig. 7A-B). Additionally, co-culture assay revealed a reduction in CD4+ T cell activation after si-PRMT5 and EPZ015666 administration (Fig. 7C-F), indicating that cigarette tar administration enhances MHC II expression in ECs and CD4+ T cell activation within atherosclerotic plaques, the effects could be improved via PRMT5 inhibition.

Recent study has indicated that PRMT5 is capable of mediating both transcriptional activation and repression through various histone methylation processes, including the dimethylation of H3R2, a key histone modification associated with transcriptional activation [61]. In our investigation, we examined the symmetric dimethylation and H3R2 dimethylation in ECs following cigarette tar administration. Our findings suggested that EPZ015666 and si-PRMT5 effectively inhibited both total symmetric dimethylation and H3R2 dimethylation (Fig. 7G-J). Additionally, chromatin immunoprecipitation assays demonstrated a significant increase in dimethylation of histone H3 at arginine 2 (H3R2me2) at the promoter regions of MHC II-related genes due to cigarette tar stimulation. This increase was notably suppressed by the inhibition and knockdown of PRMT5 (Additional File 1: Fig. S42A-F). Moreover, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of H4R3 dimethylation, a classical modification mediated by PRMT5 on histone, known for its role in gene transcription repression. The results indicated that cigarette tar administration did not significantly alter H4R3 dimethylation (Additional File 1: Fig. S42G-L), indicating that PRMT5-facilitated MHC II expression is triggered by the dimethylation of H3R2.

PRMT5 inhibition and EC-specific PRMT5 knockdown alleviate atherosclerotic progression in cigarette tar-treated ApoEKO mice

To further elucidate the specific role of PRMT5 in ECs within atherosclerotic plaques, we administered AAV-Tie2-sh-PRMT5 to ApoEKO mice via tail vein injection (Additional File 1: Fig. S43). Following a four-month regimen of HD combined with cigarette tar administration, we assessed the severity of atherosclerotic plaque lesions. Our findings indicate that the plaque area and lipid deposition in mice injected with AAV-Tie2-sh-PRMT5 were significantly improved than those in mice injected with AAV-Tie2-sh-NC (Fig. 8A, B). Notably, the deletion of PRMT5 in ECs did not lead to significant improvements in inflammatory response (Fig. 8C-D). Furthermore, the expression of MHC II in ECs and the infiltration of CD4+Gzma+ T cells were diminished in mice receiving AAV-Tie2-sh-PRMT5 (Additional File 1: Fig. S44A-D). Also, in ApoEKO mice, treatment with EPZ015666 mitigated the severity of atherosclerotic lesions under cigarette tar and HD administration (Fig. 8E-F), as evidenced by a decreased inflammatory response (Fig. 8G-H). Collectively, these results suggest that PRMT5 enhances MHC II expression in ECs and promotes CD4+Gzma+ T cell infiltration under cigarette tar treatment conditions, thereby accelerating the progression of AS. Targeting PRMT5 interventions in ECs could represent a significant therapeutic strategy for ameliorating the progression of smoke-induced AS.

Discussion

Smoking is a significant risk factor for the development of AS. Despite numerous clinical and fundamental studies have explored the relationship between smoking and the progression of AS [77, 78], the precise molecular regulatory mechanisms involved remain insufficiently understood. In particular, there is an absence of corresponding targets for immune-related interventions. A recent study has demonstrated that smoking could exert a persistent influence on the activation of the adaptive immune response, even after cessation of smoking. This finding suggests a potential association between adaptive immune response activation and the development of smoking-related AS [79]. In the present study, the primary findings are as follows: (1) Cigarette tar increases the proportion of CD4+Gzma+ T cells in circulation and tissues, thereby accelerating atherosclerotic progression; (2) Cigarette tar facilitates the activation of CD4+Gzma+ T cells via promoting the expression of MHC II in ECs, This subset of T cells induces the apoptosis of ECs through the secretion of Gzma; (3) Cigarette tar enhances the PRMT5-mediated symmetrical dimethylation modification of H3R2 through CIITA nuclear translocation, leading to MHC II expression in ECs and activation of CD4+Gzma+T cells. Differing from the direct cytotoxic impact of cigarette tar on ECs observed in previous studies, our findings elucidate the mechanism by which smoking induces the progression of AS through the disruption of immune homeostasis, specifically, cigarette tar could enhance the cytotoxic effect of CD4+Gzma+ T cells on ECs, thereby providing a novel immune-associated perspective for the prevention and treatment of smoking-related cardiovascular diseases.

In the construction process of the atherosclerotic animal model for the current study, we employ a combination of a HD and cigarette smoke exposure based on the previous literature [28] (12 cigarettes over a 50-minute period, administered twice daily, five times per week, for a total of 16 weeks) to develop a smoke-related atherosclerotic mice model. The experimental outcomes align well with previously published findings, demonstrating increased plaque instability, lipid metabolism disorders, and an exacerbated immune inflammatory response [28, 52, 78]. Furthermore, the methodology used in this study to construct the animal model bears significant relevance to human smoking behaviors. Firstly, the daily cigarette smoke exposure in the animal model parallels that of heavy smokers, defined as individuals who consume 20 or more cigarettes per day [80]. Secondly, the twice-daily exposure pattern replicates typical human smoking habits, such as smoking after meals or work. Lastly, the exposure duration, involving 24 cigarettes per day for a cumulative 100 min, corresponds with the time it takes for humans to smoke 24 cigarettes. Consequently, the development of this atherosclerotic-mice model demonstrates a potential correlation with actual clinical scenarios of CHD patients with smoking history.

AS is a complex inflammatory disease primarily initiated by an enhanced inflammatory response and dysregulated lipid metabolism [1]. This process is intricately controlled by various immune cells, including T cells, macrophages, and neutrophils [81, 82], with T cells playing a particularly pivotal role in the adaptive immune response within atherosclerotic plaques. Several CD4+ effector T cell subsets, such as Th1 and Th17 cells, have been shown to facilitate the progression of AS through the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IFN-γ and IL-17 [9], and Tregs, with the ability to suppress inflammatory response, could alleviate the severity of AS via secreting anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10, IL-35, TGF-β) [83, 84]. Furthermore, the absence of type I invariant natural killer T cells has been associated with elevated plasma cholesterol levels in mouse models susceptible to AS [85]. In the present study, we examine the specific impact of cigarette tar on the progression of AS by employing a cigarette tar inhalation method in ApoEKO mice. Consistent with our previous findings [60, 86], cigarette tar inhalation was observed to exacerbate the development of AS. Furthermore, the study observed that treatment with cigarette tar significantly influenced the proportion of immune cell subsets, particularly affecting T cell subsets. Notably, the proportion of CD4+Gzma+ T cells increased substantially, with cluster 2 (Low-Gzma+ CD4 effector T cells) rising from 7% to 24% (light purple cluster) and cluster 9 (High-Gzma+ CD4 effector T cells) increasing from 2% to 6% (light brown cluster). Also, the proportion of Tregs, which express anti-atherosclerotic cytokines (IL-10, IL-35, and TGF-β) [87], inhibit the activation of effector T cells [88, 89], prevent the accumulation of ox-LDL beneath arterial walls [90] and mediate the transition of VSMCs to the contractile phenotype stabilizing atherosclerotic plaques [91], significantly decreased following exposure to cigarette tar (cluster 3, from 17.5% to 7%, orange cluster; fewer enriched). These findings suggest that inhalation of cigarette tar might influence the proportion and function of T cell subsets that modulate the inflammatory response, thereby indirectly accelerating the progression of AS. Notably, the proportion of the CD8 cytotoxic T cell subset (cluster 1/cluster 10), which exhibits greater cytotoxicity compared with CD4 effector T cells, was observed to decrease (cluster 1: from 33% to 19%, light green cluster) or exhibit only a slight increase (cluster 10: from 2% to 3%, dark purple cluster) (Fig. 2B). CellChat analysis further revealed a significant reduction in the communication probability of Gzma-related pathways between cluster 10 and ECs following cigarette tar exposure (Fig. 4B), which is consistent with several previous studies reported [92, 93]. Consequently, this study concentrated on elucidating the effects and underlying mechanism of CD4+Gzma+ T cells on ECs under cigarette tar inhalation. Leistner et al. have demonstrated that Gzma+ T cells could induce apoptosis in ECs, thereby promoting atherosclerotic progression [94]. Additionally, Gzma+ cytotoxic lymphocytes have been reported to mediate pyroptosis in targeted tumor cells [95]. Despite this, there remains a paucity of research focusing on CD4+Gzma+ T cells within smoking-related atherosclerotic plaques. Our findings indicate that cigarette tar administration markedly elevates the proportion of CD4+Gzma+ T cells in the arteries, spleens, and blood of ApoEKO mice. Based on this premise, additional verification was conducted on clinical patients who smoke. Moreover, the augmented ECs cytotoxicity of CD4+Gzma+T cells derived from cigarette tar-treated mice and CHD patients with smoking history was corroborated through both in vitro and in vivo experiments involving CD4+T cell infusion, suggesting that under the context of cigarette tar administration, this subset of CD4+ T cells could contribute to ECs injury in atherosclerotic plaques.

In prior research on AS, T cells have been predominantly activated by classical antigen-presenting cells, such as dendritic cells [96]and B cells [10]. Ma et al. demonstrated that within the context of AS, homocysteinemia could enhance the interaction between PKM2 and CREB1, thereby inducing the expression of CIITA and its downstream proteins, including MHC II in B cells, which subsequently leads to the activation of CD4+ T cells [10]. In our investigations, we discovered that in the context of AS, cigarette tar could facilitate the activation of CD4+Gzma+ T cells by modulating antigen presentation in ECs, which are considered as non-classical antigen-presenting cells. This phenomenon has not been previously reported in the literature on AS, while similar phenomena have been reported in other diseases. For instance, Elyada et al. found that antigen-presenting cancer-associated fibroblasts, also non-classical antigen-presenting cells, could mediate the activation of CD4+ T cells in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma tumors [16]. Furthermore, Maehara et al. proposed that in the pathogenesis of systemic sclerosis, antigen-presenting ECs may facilitate the activation of CD4+Gzma+ T cells, subsequently leading to the apoptosis of ECs [6]. This suggests that non-classical antigen-presenting cells fulfills the critical role in the disruption of immune homeostasis. Consequently, our findings reveal a novel phenomenon in the progression of AS, wherein cigarette tar activates CD4+Gzma+ T cells via enhancing the antigen presentation capabilities of ECs. This discovery provided a new perspective on therapeutic target intervention strategies for addressing the progression of smoking-related AS.

In addition, the investigation of potential antigenic peptides presented by ECs under the influence of cigarette smoke remains a pertinent area for future research. Sandro et al. proposed a significant increase in plasma HSP70, a stress response protein, in patients with CHD who have a history of smoking, indicating that HSP70 might serve as the antigen peptide presented by the ECs with cigarette smoke administration [78]. Furthermore, the APOB peptide, a core component of ox-LDL, has been shown to be presented by antigen-presenting cells and activated Th1/Th17 cells in the presence of pro-inflammatory cytokines. These activated T cells could exacerbate the local immune inflammatory response by secreting IFN-γ and IL-17, thereby facilitating the progression of atherosclerotic plaques [97, 98]. These findings imply that ECs on the surface of atherosclerotic plaques may enhance their antigen presentation capabilities for ox-LDL and stress response proteins when stimulated by cigarette smoke, consequently activating pro-inflammatory T cells and advancing the progression of atherosclerotic plaques. Recently, immunopeptidome could facilitate the identification of potential antigen peptides presented by antigen-presenting cells [99, 100]. Based on the findings of the current study, future research could utilize this technology to isolate and identify potential antigenic peptide segments presented by mouse arterial ECs in vivo, both with and without exposure to cigarette smoke. Furthermore, it is crucial to examine the degree of CD4+ T cell activation and ECs damage following the extracellular loading of peptides under cigarette tar treatment in vitro. Additionally, assessing whether the activation of the CIITA/PRMT5/H3R2me2 signaling pathway could enhance the presentation of these antigenic peptides is of significant importance. Consequently, future investigations employing immunopeptidome and mechanistic studies will be of great value in elucidating the antigenic profiles and associated signaling pathways involved.

CIITA is a member of the NLR transcription factor family. Research has demonstrated that CIITA could mediate the expression of various genes through nuclear translocation, despite not directly binding to DNA [69]. Notably, there is a paucity of studies examining the specific molecular mechanisms by which CIITA mediates atherosclerotic progression. In the present study, we discovered that cigarette tar exposure could enhance CIITA expression in the nucleus, as evidenced by nuclear proteomic sequencing. Furthermore, EC-specific knockdown of CIITA resulted in a decreased proportion of MHC II expression and reduced infiltration of CD4+Gzma+ T cells within arteries, circulation, and blood in vivo. Additionally, the severity of atherosclerotic lesions was mitigated in the EC-specific knockdown group, suggesting that CIITA acts as a pro-atherogenic role in cigarette tar-mediated activation of CD4+Gzma+ T cells within atherosclerotic plaques. Recent studies have also suggested that CIITA has a transcriptional activation effect on antigen presentation genes. Kasuga et al. have demonstrated that FBXO11 could promote the degradation of CIITA in HeLa cells through ubiquitin modifications, subsequently down-regulating the expression of MHC II genes and decreasing the survival rate in cancer patients [24]. Additionally, it has been shown that the deletion of interleukin-22 in vivo could increase the expression of CIITA and the corresponding downstream protein, MHC II, in primary epithelial cells, thereby exacerbating the local inflammatory response [101]. Notably, the expression of certain inflammation-related proteins, including LAMP2/7 [102] and Stat1/Stat3, could also be regulated by CIITA [103]. In our current studies, we found that under the context of smoking-related AS progression, cigarette tar could mediate the antigen presentation of ECs triggered by CIITA-mediated MHC II expression, which might facilitate the activation of CD4+Gzma+ T cells. The results further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the activation of CD4+Gzma+ T cells and ECs injury in the progression of smoking-related AS, particularly through the lens of immune homeostasis imbalance.

In the present study, the upstream molecular mechanism responsible for the nuclear translocation of CIITA in response to cigarette tar exposure was not further explored. This process may be influenced by several potential mechanisms. (1) Reactive oxygen species signaling pathway. Previous research has demonstrated that cigarette tar significantly elevates oxidative stress levels in aortic cells within atherosclerotic plaques, including VSMCs, macrophages, and ECs [52, 60, 86]. Additionally, Chang et al. have suggested that an increase in intracellular ROS could markedly enhance CIITA nuclear translocation and MHC II gene expression [104]. This implies that cigarette tar may facilitate CIITA nuclear translocation and subsequently promote MHC II expression by augmenting intracellular oxidative stress. (2) Jak2-Stat1 signaling pathway. The pathway plays a crucial role in cellular responses to IFN-γ, a classical antigen-presenting stimulator. Upon exposure to IFN-γ, Stat1 undergoes phosphorylation, subsequently translocating to the nucleus where it binds to the CIITA promoter, thereby enhancing CIITA mRNA expression [105, 106]. The resultant CIITA mRNA facilitates increased expression of MHC II through its nuclear export post-transcription and nuclear import post-translation [107]. Furthermore, research indicates that exposure to cigarette smoke could significantly activate the Jak2-Stat1 signaling pathway [108, 109]. This activation enhances the migratory ability of VSMCs and the activation of myeloid dendritic cells, suggesting that cigarette tar may activate the Jak2-Stat1 signaling pathway in ECs, thereby mediating CIITA nuclear translocation and MHC II gene expression. (3) Aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) signaling pathway: The primary constituents of cigarette tar include polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, benzopyrene, nitrosamines, and other carcinogenic compounds. Among these, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons have the potential to bind to and activate the AHR [110, 111]. Jin et al. demonstrated that upon activation, AHR could form a dimer with ARNT, which subsequently binds to the promoter II region of CIITA following nuclear translocation. This interaction facilitates CIITA nuclear translocation and the expression of MHC II-related genes [112], suggesting a potential mechanism by which cigarette tar mediates CIITA nuclear translocation through ligand-receptor interactions. Future research could further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying CIITA nuclear translocation mediated by cigarette tar in ECs by exploring these three aspects.

Furthermore, PRMT5 was identified by intersecting CIITA-associated proteins with transcription regulatory factor predictions for MHC II proteins in our study. As a histone-arginine methyltransferase, PRMT5 catalyzes the symmetrical methylation of histone substrates at arginine residues, such as H3R2 (associated with transcriptional activation) and H4R3 (associated with transcriptional inhibition) [61, 113]. A recent study has demonstrated that the inhibition of arginine methylation mediated by PRMT5 could significantly enhance the stability of connectivity between ECs and improve vascular integrity, thereby decelerating the progression of AS [114]. Furthermore, Fei et al. have suggested that PRMT5 inhibition could protect ECs from damage during the initial stages of AS [115], indicating that arginine methylation mediated by PRMT5 could potentially serve as a critical target for therapeutic intervention in atherosclerotic progression. In our study, we observed that under cigarette tar administration, the symmetrical methylation of H3R2 was significantly increased, a process initiated by CIITA-associated PRMT5, and EC-specific PRMT5 knockdown could result in decreased MHC II expression in ECs and reduced infiltration/activation of CD4+Gzma+ T cells. Intriguingly, the EC-specific PRMT5 knockdown did not improve the exacerbation of inflammation caused by cigarette tar in atherosclerotic progression, further indicating that the knockdown of PRMT5 specifically in ECs could ameliorate the progression of smoke-induced AS progression, potentially through effects on CD4+Gzma+ T cell activation, ECs injury, and subsequent inflammatory responses. Additionally, consistent with our findings, Zhu et al. demonstrated that pharmacological inhibition of PRMT5-mediated symmetrical methylation of histones could improve the proliferation and migration of vascular smooth muscle cells, thereby inhibit vascular remodeling [42]. Our study demonstrated that the pharmacological inhibition of PRMT5, as well as the targeted knockdown of PRMT5 in ECs, markedly mitigates the severity of atherosclerotic lesions and improved the inflammatory response. These findings suggest that PRMT5 could serve as a potential therapeutic target in the management of smoking-related AS. However, it is noteworthy that the analysis of the interaction between CIITA and PRMT5 is limited to predictive molecular docking, without mutation or truncation experiments conducted at the interaction site. Also, the integration of proximity ligation assay with z-stack confocal microscopy could potentially provide deeper insights into the interaction between these two proteins within the nucleus of ECs. The PRMT5 protein predominantly consists of three domains: the N-terminal TIM barrel domain (amino acids 1-303), the mid-Rossman fold domain (amino acids 304–460), and the C-terminal β-barrel domain (amino acids 461–637) [116]. The findings of the present study via utilizing the Alpha-Fold3 indicate that the binding site for CIITA on PRMT5 is primarily situated within the N-terminal domain, especially TIM barrel domain, which is crucial for maintaining PRMT5’s methylation modification activity, recruiting substrate cohesion proteins (PICLN/menin/PICLN/MBD/COPR5), and preserving the structural stability of PRMT5 [117–119]. Consequently, future atherosclerotic research could further investigate the differential impacts of the CIITA/PRMT5 docking sites, as predicted in the current study, on the functional attributes of PRMT5.

PRMT5-mediated symmetrical dimethylation of histone H3R2 is a well-established mechanism for transcriptional activation [116, 120]. In the present study, we observed that the nuclear translocation of CIITA, along with its binding partner PRMT5, enhances MHC II expression in ECs by facilitating H3R2 symmetrical dimethylation under the influence of cigarette tar exposure. Nevertheless, it is important to highlight that the precise mechanism by which CIITA, a well-known transcriptional regulator of MHC II, relocates to the H3R2me2 site following nuclear translocation remains to be elucidated and warrants further investigation. Notably, Migliori et al. have demonstrated that WDR5 serves as the primary reader protein for H3R2me2, with its WD40 domain specifically recognizing the symmetrically dimethylated arginine [121]. Simultaneously, WDR5 may facilitate the recruitment of transcriptional regulatory proteins (including c-MYC [122], MLL1 [123], SET1 [124], etc.) following the recognition of H3R2me2, thereby enhancing the transcription of downstream proteins and ultimately influencing disease progression. Consequently, CIITA might be recruited by the critical H3R2me2 reader protein WDR5 following nuclear translocation induced by cigarette tar exposure, leading to the expression of MHC II genes. The precise molecular mechanisms involved, such as the potential presence of a binding region between WDR5 and CIITA, warrant further investigation in future smoke-atherosclerotic research.

In our scRNA-seq analysis, we observed that exposure to cigarette smoke significantly altered the distribution of cell types associated with AS. Notably, the proportions of macrophages decreased from 3.9% to 2.5%, VSMCs increased from 5.9% to 28.6%, and fibroblasts decreased from 55.3% to 32.9%. These findings have important implications for future research, which are listed as follows: (1) Our previous study have demonstrated that cigarette tar, the primary toxic component of cigarette smoke, could directly mediate macrophages ferroptosis within atherosclerotic plaques via activation of the hepcidin/FPN/SLC7A11 axis, thereby exacerbating the immune-inflammatory response and promoting AS progression [86]. Additionally, cigarette smoke-induced ECs damage could lead to the accumulation of ox-LDL within plaques [28, 78, 125]. The subsequent phagocytosis of lipoproteins by macrophages results in the formation of foam cells or polarization to M1 macrophages, potentially impairing their ability to clear apoptotic ECs and accelerating the progression of atherosclerotic plaques [126, 127]. In future research, it will be of considerable importance to investigate whether activated CD4+Gzma+ T cells within smoke-related atherosclerotic plaques could induce macrophage polarization towards the M1 phenotype, or whether the interaction between CIITA and PRMT5 in macrophages could modulate their polarization and lipid phagocytosis functions; (2) The results of scRNA-seq have suggested a significant increase in the proportion of VSMCs in response to cigarette smoke exposure, indicating a potential phenotypic transition from contractile to synthetic smooth muscle cells. Previous research has suggested that synthetic VSMCs may impede the repair of damaged ECs by secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines or by transforming into foam cells [128–130], thereby contributing to increased arterial lipid deposition. Future investigations could focus on the potential synergistic effects of inhibiting the conversion of VSMCs to synthetic cells and interfering with the activation of CD4+Gzma+ T cells in ameliorating the progression of smoking-related AS. Additionally, it is crucial to explore whether activated CD4+Gzma+ T cells could further induce the differentiation of VSMCs into synthetic cells through the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the presence of cigarette tar; (3) Fibroblasts represent the predominant cell type responsible for the synthesis of the extracellular matrix [131, 132]. ScRNA-seq results demonstrate a significant reduction in the proportion of fibroblasts within the aorta following exposure to cigarette smoke. This reduction may directly contribute to a decrease in collagen fiber content within atherosclerotic plaques, thereby increasing plaque instability [133, 134]. Future researches could investigate the effects and underlying mechanisms by which cigarette smoke tar impairs collagen synthesis in fibroblasts. Additionally, it would also be valuable to examine whether activated CD4+Gzma+ T cells possess cytotoxic effects on fibroblasts.

Noteworthy, in our single-cell sequencing analysis of murine blood vessels, we observed a notable increase in the proportion of smooth muscle cells following exposure to cigarette smoke (from 5.9% to 28.6%). This finding aligns with previous research indicating smooth muscle cell hyper-proliferation and a phenotypic transition from a contractile to a synthetic state, as well as migration into the vascular media during the intermediate stages of atherosclerotic progression [135]. Specifically, pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, which are released by damaged ECs [136, 137] and activated T cells [138, 139], could be detected by vascular smooth muscle cells, thereby promoting their proliferation and migration. Secondly, the anticoagulant properties of the ECs surface are compromised upon injury, resulting in the exposure of procoagulant factors that initiate platelet activation and aggregation at the site of injury [140]. Following aggregation, platelets release various substances, including platelet-derived growth factor and thromboxane A2, which further promote the proliferation and migration of smooth muscle cells [141, 142]. Thirdly, in the progression of AS, LDL undergoes oxidation to form ox-LDL, which accumulates beneath the damaged ECs. Subsequently, ox-LDL interacts with scavenger receptors on the surface of ECs, thereby enhancing the local oxidative stress response. The resultant elevated levels of reactive oxygen species could further promote the proliferation of smooth muscle cells, exacerbate the inflammatory response, and expedite the progression of AS [81, 82]. Consequently, elucidating the interactions between activated CD4+ T cells, damaged ECs, and proliferating smooth muscle cells is crucial for advancing our understanding of the mechanisms underlying AS progression, particularly in the context of cigarette smoke exposure in future research.

Previous research has demonstrated a significant elevation in plasma cotinine concentrations, a nicotine metabolite, in both smokers and animals exposed to cigarette smoke [143–145]. These findings align with the prior results obtained by our research team [52]. Notably, plasma cotinine levels have been confirmed to exhibit a significant positive correlation with the concentrations of several classic AS-related pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β and IL-18 [146]. Furthermore, Conklin et al. have reported that cotinine could upregulate the expression of vascular endothelial growth factor in ECs, thereby increasing ECs permeability and facilitating the extensive accumulation of ox-LDL beneath ECs [147]. Additionally, cotinine has been shown to mediate the phenotypic transition of VSMCs to a synthetic state by exacerbating oxidative stress injury [148]. These findings suggest that, cotinine-a nicotine metabolite present in cigarette smoke-may contribute to the progression of atherosclerotic plaques via enhancing inflammatory responses and disrupting lipid metabolism. Consequently, future research examining the alterations in antigen presentation functions of dendritic cells, macrophages, ECs and VSMCs, as well as the activation levels of T cell subsets under cotinine stimulation, will further enhance the understanding of the molecular mechanisms by which cigarette smoke contributes to the progression of AS from an immunological perspective.

Notably, we developed a murine model of smoke-associated AS through in vivo exposure to cigarette smoke. Concurrently, we isolated cigarette tar, dissolved in DMSO, as it represents the primary toxic component of cigarette smoke, to stimulate ECs in vitro. This methodological approach may introduce discrepancies between the particulate and gaseous phases of cigarette smoke, potentially imposing certain limitations on our study. To address this, we conducted a compositional analysis of the in vitro extracted tar, suggesting that its principal constituents—aromatic hydrocarbons, organic acids, fatty acids (Additional File 1: Table 5), among others—could also be detectable in the bloodstream of smokers. This finding indicates that the in vitro cigarette tar stimulation employed in our research closely mirrors the compositional characteristics of in vivo cigarette smoke exposure, which is consistent with the approach utilized in previous studies [52, 78]. Meanwhile, it remains challenging to utilize cigarette smoke in its gaseous phase for in vitro ECs stimulation with current biotechnological methods. Hence, despite the inherent differences between particulate and gaseous phases, our compositional analysis substantiates the utility of this tar-based in vitro model for studying smoke-induced AS progression.

The spleen represents the largest peripheral immune organ within the human body [149], which is the main settlement site of mature T cells and B cells. Several studies involving both animal models and human patients have demonstrated that splenectomy could markedly diminish the adaptive immune response, including a notable reduction in T cell counts, a decreased capacity to respond to external stimulation [150–152], and a significant decline in antibody production. Particularly following splenectomy, there is a pronounced decrease in IgM levels [150, 153, 154]. In accordance with previous literature [39], during the T cells transfer experiment, we conducted splenectomy on recipient ApoEKO mice to mitigate the influence of adaptive immunity themselves on atherosclerotic plaque progression. Post-surgery, we assessed the levels of CD4+ T cells of blood (Additional File 1: Fig. S45). While this method did not entirely eradicate CD4+ T cells in the recipient mice, it significantly attenuated their adaptive immune response, exhibiting significantly decreased CD4+ T cells proportion and number within blood. Furthermore, this technique has been extensively employed in research across various fields to alleviate the adaptive immune response [39, 155–157]. Hence, it possesses a distinct potential for application in mice models of AS.

Overall, under exposure to cigarette tar, the antigen presentation capacity of ECs is enhanced, which is associated with activation of CD4+Gzma+ T cells and ECs injury, and may contribute to the progression of AS. This process is associated with the nuclear translocation of CIITA and histone methylation modifications mediated by PRMT5. The findings of this study suggest potential novel targets for the treatment of smoking-related AS.

Limitations

The current study is subject to several limitations. Firstly, the scRNA-seq of T cells was unable to determine the clonal expansion level of T cells, which is closely associated with antigen recognition capacity. Future studies are needed to employ single-cell T-cell receptor sequencing of T cells in aortas exposed to cigarette tar inhalation to assess the clonal diversity of various T cell sub-populations. Secondly, in this study, the antigen-presenting capability of ECs was elucidated by loading ECs with OVA and subsequently co-culturing them with CD4+ T cells. However, the specific antigens presented were not further characterized in vivo. Comprehensive analyses of the MHC II immunopeptidome in ECs are needed to identify the specific antigenic peptides presented by these cells when exposed to cigarette tar, which is crucial for the development and optimization of vaccines targeting specific clinical risk factors associated with AS. Thirdly, it remains unclear whether the changes in MHC II expression are directly regulated by PRMT5-mediated H3R2 dimethylation, as there is currently no established experimental method for specifically depleting histone methylation in cells. Fourthly, in this study, we observed that tar facilitates the nuclear translocation of CIITA in ECs, while the precise mechanism underlying the nuclear translocation of CIITA induced by cigarette tar was not explored in depth in this study. Fifthly, in the adoptive transfer experiment, anti-Gzma administration will block the Gzma derived from CD8+ T cells and NK cells, not just the transferred CD4+ T cells. Also, despite the splenectomy performed on the recipient mice, it is possible that CD8+ T cells or NK cells secreting Gzma remain present in the circulation, lymph nodes, and atherosclerotic plaques of the recipient mice, potentially influencing the progression of atherosclerotic plaques. Consequently, utilizing ApoE−/−Rag2−/− mice or ApoE−/−Gzma−/− mice as recipient models in future studies could provide further insights into the role of CD4+Gzma+ T cells in the progression of atherosclerotic plaques. Sixthly, PRMT5 was identified as a critical therapeutic target in the context of smoking-related AS. However, its role has not yet been validated in large animal models or clinical patient populations. Future research is needed to prioritize the translational investigation of PRMT5 gene intervention in smoking-related AS.

Conclusions

This study indicates that cigarette tar could promote the activation of CD4+Gzma+ T cells, which in turn contribute to ECs injury. This process is mediated by the MHC II-related antigen presentation of ECs and accelerates the progression of AS consequently. The nuclear translocation of CIITA and PRMT5-mediated methylation modifications fulfill the crucial intervention target in the smoke-related AS progression, potentially through mechanisms involving enhanced symmetrical methylation modification of H3R2.
