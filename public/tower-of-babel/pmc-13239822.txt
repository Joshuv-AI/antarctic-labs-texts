Internal Jugular Vein Entrapment: An Underrecognized Cause of Facial Pain
Authors: K. Chan, M. Gharib, J. Karam, S. Won, Y. H. Cha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe cause of facial pain often remains unknown after ruling out dental disorders and arterial compression of the trigeminal nerve. New pathologic models and treatment options are needed.MethodReview of 30 patients with unexplained facial pain who were diagnosed with internal jugular vein (IJV) entrapment.ResultsMean age 48.8 ± 12.8 years, duration of symptoms 68.4 ± 100.4 months, women (28/30, 93%). Symptom laterality: left only (11/30, 37%), right only (8/30, 27%), bilateral equally (7/30, 23%), left worse than right (3/30, 10%), right worse than left (1/30, 3%). Facial pain lateralized to the side of underlying venous compression in 19/30 (63%) patients or was unilateral with underlying bilateral compressions in 9/30 (30%). The IJV obstructed at the atlas (lateral process of the atlas, styloid process, posterior belly digastric muscle), between muscles in the mid‐neck (sternocleidomastoid, omohyoid, and anterior scalene), and at the thoracic outlet. This led to dilation of the superior petrosal sinus that abuts the trigeminal nerve and shunting through vertebral veins that congested the lower brainstem and cervical spinal cord where the spinal trigeminal nucleus originates and descends. Targeting the IJV obstruction with physical therapy, neurotoxin injections in the neck, and surgical decompression significantly improved facial pain in most patients (20/30, 67%) but was too advanced in some to achieve meaningful relief.ConclusionIncreasing awareness of venous outflow obstruction as a contributor to facial pain could explain complex regional neurological symptoms, provide an option beyond oral medications, and lead to earlier diagnosis when the pathology is amenable to treatment.

Introduction

Chronic facial pain is a debilitating condition with limited treatment options when the underlying pathology cannot be determined. Current categorization of facial pain is based on clinical features such as the origin, temporal nature, and quality of the pain but this descriptive classification does not identify the pathology that underlies the source of the pain [1, 2]. At an estimated incidence of 4.4 per 100,000 person years in the general population, chronic facial pain in which the underlying cause is not identified contributes to high public health burden in need of new pathological models, diagnostic tools, and treatment options [3, 4].

Facial pain from extracranial venous outflow obstruction in the neck has recently been reported, introducing the possibility of new targetable pathology [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Specifically, internal jugular vein (IJV) entrapment at vulnerable points of high freedom of movement at the atlas (C1), in the mid‐neck, and at the thoracic outlet can lead to retrograde intracranial flow, congestion of the superior petrosal sinus that overlies the trigeminal nerve, and congestion of the brainstem and cervical cord where the spinal trigeminal nucleus descends [12, 13, 14]. This mechanism may explain why patients with chronic facial pain can have features of trigeminal neuralgia, intracranial hypertension, migraine, or not fit into any existing clinical category [1, 15]. Given these clinical presentations and the anatomical relationship between venous structures and cranial pain sensitive structures, we hypothesized that IJV compression could be a more common pathology for facial pain than is currently recognized.

This case series of 30 patients with facial pain from IJV entrapment introduces clinical features, diagnostic considerations, associated risk factors, and treatment options for venous outflow obstruction leading to facial pain. Recognizing this pathology in appropriate patients could lead to more durable pain relief with less use of medications while providing an explanatory model for cases in which patients do not fall into the currently conceptualized categories of facial pain.

Methods

Design

Retrospective review of a subset of 30 patients with facial pain due to IJV obstruction who had presented to a neurotology clinic originally with vestibular symptoms. These patients were selected from a database of ~600 patients who had been prospectively categorized based on symptoms and diagnoses. Concurrent facial pain was reported at presentation. Demographics, duration of pain before presentation, prior diagnoses, medication trials, and response to treatment were extracted from the clinical record.

Patients underwent dynamic quantitative Doppler ultrasound of the IJV and CT venogram of the head and neck to diagnose C1 (atlas level), muscular, and thoracic outlet level compressions under the hypothesis that the trigeminal nerve and inner ear could be affected in the same venous vascular territory (Figure 1). A subset of patients whose symptoms were severe enough to warrant surgical intervention underwent digital subtraction venography.

Intracranial venous structures and their relationship to the trigeminal nerve and neighboring eye and inner ear. Note that the venous drainage starts from the cavernous sinus that drains the eye, flows through the superior petrosal sinus that overlies the trigeminal nerve, and continues around the inner ear before joining the sigmoid sinus and draining into the internal jugular vein. This regional anatomy may underlie the frequent co‐occurrence of ocular, facial, and inner ear symptoms.

Patients diagnosed with IJV obstruction underwent physical therapy focused on postural and ergonomic awareness, upper cervical alignment, strengthening of the posterior cervical chain, relaxation of anterior muscles that overlie the IJV (sternocleidomastoid, omohyoid) and subclavian vein (anterior scalene, pectoralis minor), and first rib mobilization.

Patients who did not obtain adequate relief from physical therapy were offered a trial of botulinum toxin in the neck under a cervical dystonia protocol. Ultrasound guidance was used to target the sternocleidomastoid (SCM) and anterior scalene (ASM) with additional muscles added in subsequent injections as needed. These typically included the middle scalene, superior belly of the omohyoid (OMH), trapezius, and pectoralis minor muscles. If sufficient improvement and minimal side effects were noted, subsequent injections were given on a 12‐week cycle with dosing adjusted based on treatment response.

Patients whose symptoms persisted despite these treatments were evaluated for decompressive muscle surgery after undergoing a cerebral venogram to confirm ongoing IJV compression. Options included a styloidectomy/C1 shave, a partial SCM/OMH myotomy, thoracic outlet decompression, or an IJV stent. The specific type of decompressive surgery offered was determined by the location of the major compression based on venography; patients could undergo more than one procedure. Facial pain relief was measured according to patient self‐report during clinical encounters with responses categorized as worsened, no change, improved, or resolved. Data is presented anonymously, without identifying information.

Of the 30 patients, 20 had improvement of the facial pain using a graded structural approach to treatment. Eighteen of the 20 patients whose facial pain had significantly improved had undergone IJV decompression interventions with neurotoxin injections and/or surgery; two were managed with medications alone (diazepam, venlafaxine) (Table 3). While no one had permanent complete resolution of facial pain in all circumstances, pain was no longer life‐limiting for successfully treated patients.

Of the remaining 10 patients who had no improvement, four did not improve despite multiple decompressive surgeries. These patients had extremely complex venous anatomy at the skull base and had symptoms present for well over a decade. The remaining patients are considering escalation of treatment on this pathway, but some have lost access to care due to extenuating circumstances.

Discussion

This case series highlights the underrecognized association between IJV compression and facial pain, with the underlying pathology being retrograde venous shunting where the trigeminal nerve and spinal trigeminal nucleus descend. By identifying IJV compression by musculoskeletal structures as a potential pathological contributor to facial pain generation, these cases broaden our understanding of facial pain etiology and suggest new avenues for diagnosis and treatment. These treatments include physical therapy targeting neck areas where the IJV is vulnerable to compression, neurotoxin targeting muscles that compress the IJV, and decompressive surgery at specific points of compression. The mechanistic effects of IJV compression have been hypothesized previously in relation to conditions such as vertigo, migraine, and thoracic outlet syndrome [6, 10, 17, 18, 19]. We propose that it be expanded to include facial pain.

Compression of the IJV at the atlas (C1) has been previously studied, but more recently recognized is entrapment of the IJV in the mid‐neck from a combination of the overlying SCM/OMH and the underlying ASM [6, 7, 19, 20]. These muscles can “sandwich” the IJV and lead to compression at baseline that worsens when the muscles are activated, typically with head flexion and ipsilateral rotation. This mechanism is important because many traumatic and inflammatory insults can injure muscles in the neck.

Patients in this case series frequently experienced headache, vertigo, and musculoskeletal pain in the neck and arms, typically ipsilateral to the facial pain and more commonly worse on the left side [6]. This may be due to the generally smaller left‐sided venous system that has less tolerance for outflow obstruction compared to the right [21, 22]. These symptoms can be linked with IJV compression leading to CSF pressure dysregulation, impaired metabolic clearance, high venous pressure around the inner ear, and excessive collateral vessel formation. Regional head (eye, face, and ear) and body (neck, shoulder, and arm) symptoms are highly suggestive of venous outflow obstruction. Common risk factors include head, neck, or chest trauma, muscular strain from poor posture or overuse, inflammation, or vulnerability to musculoskeletal strain from hypermobility or degeneration.

Treatments specifically aimed at IJV decompression should be considered when patients present with regional pain, especially when combined with vestibular symptoms. Compressions are often in tandem and bilateral, indicating that patients present once venous return falls below compensatory capacity. While the benefits are dependent on the degree of cumulative outflow obstruction, anatomical complexity, and surgical expertise, the clinical outcome can be quite dramatic when the underlying pathology is directly addressed. Recognizing IJV compression as a contributing factor to facial pain may help intractable cases to become more manageable. Conversely, diagnosing the pathology too late can mean that the pathology is irreversible without heroic interventions. In our series, 13% (4/30) of patients remained with intractable facial pain despite decompressive surgeries. Their underlying venous anatomy was extremely abnormal, present in the context of other venous obstructions throughout the body, and their symptoms longstanding. This mechanism of facial pain should be considered earlier in the evaluation pathway to treat the pathology when it is more likely to be reversible.

Limitations

While this case series introduces an underexamined association between facial pain and venous compression, its design and scope present limitations. Facial pain was noted as a clinical feature during a presentation for vestibular symptoms. While the facial pain improved with treatment of the IJV obstruction, which also improved vestibular symptoms, the severity of venous pathology we uncovered may be more severe than in patients who present with isolated facial pain. Additionally, while facial pain was noted through clinical interactions, we did not administer a standardized facial pain scale or specify qualities of facial pain in relation to a patient's imaging and treatment outcomes. There was no standardized metric used to assess pain improvement, only the patients' reports that the pain had improved or that they were taking less pain medication for it. A more granular measurement of facial pain features in relation to the anatomical level and degree of IJV compression could lead to a more precise correlation of venous compression location, cumulative burden, and reasonable treatment alternatives.
