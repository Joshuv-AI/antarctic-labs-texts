Vaccine hesitancy among Syrian refugee parents in Canada: A multifaceted challenge in public health
Authors: Baraa Alghalyini, Abdul Rehman Zia Zaidi, Safoura Zangiabadi, Akm Alamgir, Hala Tamim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVaccine hesitancy presents a significant public health challenge, particularly among Syrian refugee parents in Canada, who navigate unique barriers to vaccination. This cross-sectional study explores the determinants of vaccine hesitancy, considering socio-demographic factors, resettlement conditions, health assessments, and healthcare system interactions. The study involved 540 Syrian refugee parents residing in Ontario with at least one child under 18, interviewed from March 2021 to March 2022. Participants were asked about their willingness to take the COVID-19 vaccine, with those uncertain or unwilling categorized as “Hesitant” and others as “Non-hesitant.” Stepwise multivariable logistic regression assessed various factors associated with vaccine hesitancy. Among respondents, 15.2% expressed hesitancy toward taking the COVID-19 vaccine. Findings indicated that individuals who reported very good or good mental health had decreased odds of being vaccine hesitant (OR = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.27–0.80). Also, individuals without a family doctor and those needing an interpreter but sometimes or never offered one were more likely to be hesitant (OR = 3.61, 95% CI: 1.42, 9.19; OR = 2.14, 95% CI: 1.19–3.84, respectively). These results highlight the complex interplay of factors affecting vaccine decisions, emphasizing the need for culturally sensitive public health strategies to improve vaccine uptake in this population. While vaccine acceptance is low among Syrians (36%), the higher rate among Syrian refugees in Canada (84.7%) reflects the positive impact of healthcare access and resettlement support. This contrast highlights the role of such systems in shaping vaccine attitudes among vulnerable populations, informing targeted public health efforts to boost vaccine acceptance and support the health of Syrian refugees.

Introduction

Vaccine hesitancy, defined by the WHO as a delay in acceptance or refusal of vaccines despite the availability of services, has become a critical challenge, particularly in vulnerable populations such as Syrian refugees. Syrian refugees face compounded barriers to vaccination due to disrupted healthcare access, mistrust in healthcare systems, and the socioeconomic and linguistic difficulties they encounter in resettlement settings.1

Syrian refugees face compounded challenges affecting their vaccine acceptance. These include disrupted access to healthcare during displacement, mistrust of authorities due to conflict, economic instability, and language barriers. Additionally, the trauma of conflict and resettlement often leads to mistrust of healthcare systems.1,2 These factors, along with misinformation about vaccines, make Syrian refugees particularly susceptible to vaccine hesitancy.2,3

Addressing these specific barriers is crucial to improving vaccine uptake in this population. Socio-cultural factors include the disruption of social networks and community structures, which are critical sources of trusted information. Economic challenges often result in limited access to healthcare services and financial instability, further complicating their ability to seek and receive vaccinations. Additionally, trauma from conflict and displacement can result in mistrust of authorities and healthcare providers. Language barriers and low health literacy also hinder effective communication and understanding of vaccine information. Studies have shown that factors such as fear of side effects, doubts about vaccine efficacy, and exposure to misinformation significantly impact vaccine acceptance rates in these populations.2,3

The low acceptance rate of COVID-19 vaccines among the Syrian population (e.g., 35.92% in Syria vs. 76% in developed countries), compared to populations in developed countries, highlights the urgency of addressing vaccine hesitancy in this group.1,3 Factors such as conspiracy beliefs, myths about vaccines, and lack of accurate information may contribute to this hesitancy, necessitating interventional educational campaigns to overcome misinformation and improve vaccination acceptance rates.2,4 Moreover, research focusing on New York State refugees, including various populations, has identified key predictors of vaccine uptake, emphasizing the importance of understanding the specific drivers of vaccine hesitancy among refugee groups.5 For instance, Disney et al. found that trust in healthcare providers was a significant predictor of vaccine uptake among refugees.5 This study aims to explore the determinants of vaccine hesitancy among Syrian refugee parents in Canada, focusing on socio-demographic, migration-related, health, and healthcare access factors. The insights from this study are vital for developing strategies to enhance vaccine uptake in this vulnerable population group.

Materials and methods

Study participants

This study used a cross-sectional design and was based on a larger study with the aim to assess the overall integration of Syrian refugees in Canada. A total of 540 Syrian refugee parents were recruited and interviewed between March 2021 and March 2022. Participants eligible for inclusion were Syrian refugee parents living in Ontario, Canada. In addition, participants had to have at least one child under the age of 18 at the time of the interview and had resettled in Canada during or before 2016, or after 2016. These criteria are based on the Government of Canada’s response to the Syrian refugee crisis and its resettlement plan. Year of arrival categorization reflects policies supporting rapid resettlement and initial healthcare access before 2016, with later adjustments aimed at enhancing integration and healthcare access.6,7 Moreover, participants recruitment was conducted through convenience sampling with the assistance of community organizations, including Access Alliance Multicultural Health and the Arab Community Centre of Toronto.

Ethical considerations and data collection

The study obtained ethical approval from the York University Research Ethics Board (Certificate # e2019-128). Participants’ recruitment and interviews were conducted via telephone to adhere to social distancing guidelines due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Prior to starting the survey, the research assistants forwarded a digital copy of the consent form to the participants. In addition to informing participants about the study’s objectives, they were also informed about the voluntary nature of their participation, with the option to opt out at any time without any consequences. The research assistants then explained the consent form, addressing any questions or concerns raised by the participant. Subsequently, the survey was administered by Research Assistants proficient in reading, writing, and speaking Arabic, particularly in the Syrian dialect. Participants’ responses were collected using Qualtrics, a secure data collection platform protected by a password. As compensation for their time and participation, respondents received a $20 honorarium for their involvement.

Assessment of vaccine hesitancy, demographics-, migration-, health-, and health services-related factors

Information about vaccine hesitancy was gathered using a structured questionnaire based on the question, “Are you going to take the vaccine for the Coronavirus-19 when it becomes available to you?.” Respondents who answered “No” or “do not know” were classified as “Hesitant,” while those with “Yes” or “Already took the vaccine” responses were categorized as “Non-hesitant.” Furthermore, various sociodemographic, migration, health, and health service-related factors were collected from the participants. The sociodemographic characteristics considered included gender (mother/father), age (≤35/36–45/> 45), number of children (≤2/>2), highest level of education (non-elemental/secondary high school-diploma/university), employment status (yes/no), and self-perceived socioeconomic status (SES) measured by the question, “In your current condition, here in Canada, would you say most people would categorize a household like yours as “lower income” or “lower middle and above income”?.” The “lower middle and above income” category included those who identified as belonging to the lower middle class, middle class, upper middle class, or upper class income. Migration-related factors included the type of sponsorship (government-assisted refugee (GAR)/privately sponsored refugee (PSR)/and other), living in a refugee camp (yes/no), and arrival year to Canada (during or before 2016/after 2016). Health-related factors included self-rated mental and physical health (excellent, very good, good, fair, or poor). Lastly, health services-related factors included variables such as having a family doctor (yes/no), need and availability of an interpreter (no need for an interpreter/need an interpreter and always available/need an interpreter sometimes-never available), trust in healthcare professionals (not at all – a little/a moderate amount/very much – an extreme amount), fear of COVID-19 (low/high), having tested positive for COVID-19 (yes/no), and taking the flu shot (yes/no).

Statistical analyses

Logistic regression was performed to assess the bivariate relationship between each sociodemographic migration-, health-, and health services-related factor with the main outcome of vaccine hesitancy. Additionally, a stepwise logistic regression was conducted to examine the independent association of all factors with vaccine hesitancy. Stepwise model selection technique involved iterative steps of systematically adding or removing independent variables from the model, with the goal to build a model that includes only the most significant variables. The significance level set for entry and removal from the stepwise logistic regression were 0.05 and 0.10 respectively. The final model reported Adjusted Odds Ratios (ORs), 95% Confidence Intervals, and p-values. Analyses were conducted using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, version 28.0). The Research Ethics Board at York University approved the project (Certificate # e2019-128).

At the bivariate level, several socio-demographic, migration, health, and health-related factors were associated with vaccine hesitancy. Among socio-demographic factors, individuals with no or elementary level of education were almost two times at increased odds of being vaccine hesitant than those with secondary education and above (OR = 1.83, 95% CI: 1.06–3.16). Correspondingly, participants without employment were more likely to be vaccine hesitant than those employed (OR = 1.78, 95% CI: 1.01–3.13). Of migration-related factors, living in a refugee camp was linked to an increased likelihood of vaccine hesitancy (OR = 1.96, 95% CI: 1.04–3.70). Within the health-related factors, having very good or good self-rated mental and physical health was associated with lower odds of vaccine hesitancy. Additionally, with regard to the health-services factors, participants without a family doctor and those who needed an interpreter and were sometimes or never provided with an interpreter were more likely to be vaccine hesitant. Nonetheless, neither the fear of COVID-19, testing positive for COVID-19, nor taking the flu shot was associated with vaccine hesitancy.

Discussion

Mental health

The present study found that poor self-rated mental health was significantly associated with vaccine hesitancy. This finding aligns with Bogart et al., who highlighted the critical role of mental health in vaccine acceptance.8 Individuals with better mental health were less likely to hesitate, which suggests that mental health concerns may impact how refugees perceive vaccine efficacy and the risks associated with vaccines. Kirmayer et al. also highlighted the importance of psychological factors in influencing health behaviors among refugee populations, further supporting these findings.16

The relationship between mental health and vaccine hesitancy suggests that addressing mental health needs among Syrian refugees could improve vaccine acceptance. Disney et al. identified trust in healthcare providers as a key factor influencing vaccine uptake among refugees.5 These findings are particularly relevant to our study, which identified mental health as a significant predictor of vaccine hesitancy. Interventions that incorporate mental health support and targeted education could increase vaccine uptake among Syrian refugee parents. However, more detailed data on the perceptions and beliefs driving these concerns would be necessary to draw definitive conclusions.16 These results emphasize the importance of integrating mental health support into public health strategies to improve vaccine uptake in refugee populations.

Syrian refugees, in particular, face compounded barriers related to the Syrian conflict, including mistrust of government institutions and healthcare providers, based on past negative experiences. Additionally, their resettlement in Canada often comes with economic instability and language barriers, which further hinder their engagement with vaccination efforts.

Healthcare access (family doctors, interpreters)

Healthcare access also played a significant role in shaping vaccine acceptance among Syrian refugees. Individuals without a family doctor were significantly more likely to be vaccine hesitant, which aligns with research by Charif et al. that underscores the importance of continuous care from a primary healthcare provider.9 Access to a family doctor fosters trust in healthcare and ensures continuity of care, which likely contributes to higher vaccine acceptance.9 The availability of interpreters also emerged as a critical factor, with participants who needed but were not consistently provided with interpreters exhibiting higher rates of hesitancy. These findings emphasize the need for culturally and linguistically tailored healthcare services to support vaccine uptake.10,17

Participants who required an interpreter but were not always provided with one were more likely to exhibit vaccine hesitancy. This finding echoes the observations of Morales et al., who highlighted the importance of culturally and linguistically appropriate healthcare communication.10 Building on these insights, Abuelgasim et al. highlight the significant public health issues that minority ethnic communities face during pandemics, including increased susceptibility due to socioeconomic disadvantages, overcrowded living conditions, and limited access to culturally appropriate healthcare. These barriers contribute to worse health outcomes and higher mortality rates in these populations. The authors emphasize the need for governments to engage with ethnic minority communities and trusted leaders to communicate health messages effectively and reduce these disparities.18 This integrative approach, which combines primary care, linguistic accommodations, and mental health services, is essential for addressing vaccine hesitancy in refugee communities.18

Global comparisons

The observed vaccine hesitancy rate of 15.3% among Syrian refugee parents in our Canadian cohort provides an opportunity to compare these findings with other refugee populations globally. Vaccine hesitancy among refugees shows variation globally. In Kenya, a lower hesitancy rate among refugees compared to non-refugees has been attributed to structured vaccination campaigns and healthcare access in camps. By contrast, in Turkey, a high hesitancy rate of 85% may reflect the spread of misinformation, while in Jordan’s Zaatari refugee camp, high vaccine acceptance (89.6%) was linked to successful educational interventions. In Lebanon, refugee acceptance was moderate, influenced by fear of side effects.11–14

A study conducted among Syrian refugees in Turkey revealed an 85% hesitancy rate, despite high levels of fear regarding COVID-19 infection.11 This paradox highlights the role of misinformation and erroneous beliefs about vaccines. In Jordan’s Zaatari refugee camp, however, vaccine acceptance was notably higher, with 89.6% of participants expressing willingness to be vaccinated.12 This high rate of acceptance was attributed to effective educational interventions that increased awareness of the disease and vaccine efficacy.

In Lebanon, a study of older Syrian refugees found that 42.5% had received at least one dose of the COVID-19 vaccine, though many cited fear of side effects as the primary reason for hesitancy.13 The study also found that younger age, male gender, and higher levels of education were predictors of vaccine uptake.13 In Kenya, the lower vaccine hesitancy rate among refugees (7.0%) compared to non-refugees (18.2%) may be attributed to the structured healthcare services within refugee camps, where refugees often receive better healthcare access and more consistent public health messaging than non-refugee populations.14 Refugees in camps often had better access to healthcare services, which may have contributed to lower rates of vaccine hesitancy compared to non-refugees.14

In New Zealand, a study of Syrian refugee parents emphasized similar barriers to vaccine uptake, such as mistrust of healthcare systems and misinformation, but also highlighted the importance of culturally sensitive interventions in addressing these barriers.15 Collectively, these studies illustrate the importance of context-specific public health strategies that address both systemic barriers and the specific concerns of refugee populations.15

Recommendations

Our study did not specifically examine the complex role of cultural beliefs and access to information among Syrian refugees, though these factors are likely important in shaping vaccine hesitancy. While no significant association was found between living in a refugee camp and vaccine hesitancy in our study, future research should explore these dimensions in greater depth. Cultural beliefs and information access are crucial determinants of vaccine hesitancy, as demonstrated by studies in other refugee populations.20

Additionally, while our study found no significant gender differences in vaccine hesitancy between mothers and fathers, other studies have reported that women tend to have higher levels of hesitancy, possibly due to concerns about vaccine safety and side effects.19 This discrepancy may reflect unique socio-cultural factors specific to the Syrian refugee population and warrants further investigation.20

Lastly, while socioeconomic factors, such as education, were significantly associated with vaccine hesitancy in bivariate analyses, these associations did not remain significant in the final multivariable model. This contrasts with findings from other studies, such as those by Dubé et al. and Quinn et al., which reported that higher education levels were associated with increased vaccine uptake.21,22 The findings of Eastwood et al. suggest that experiences in refugee camps, which are often characterized by limited healthcare access and exposure to misinformation, can influence attitudes toward vaccination in resettlement contexts.23 Our study did not find significant associations between living in a refugee camp and vaccine hesitancy, but future studies should explore these dynamics further.

Limitations

While our study provides valuable insights into the determinants of vaccine hesitancy among Syrian refugee parents in Canada, it is not without limitations. The cross-sectional design limits causal inferences, and reliance on self-reported data may introduce bias. Additionally, the use of convenience sampling limits the generalizability of the findings. Future studies should employ random sampling techniques and longitudinal designs to better understand vaccine hesitancy trends over time. Qualitative studies exploring the lived experiences of refugees regarding vaccination could further enrich our understanding and help shape more effective public health interventions.

Conclusion

This study revealed that 15.3% of Syrian refugee parents in Canada exhibited hesitancy toward COVID-19 vaccination, influenced by their perception and belief about vaccine efficacy or health cost of not being vaccinated, health-related determinants, such as self-rated mental health, along with health services-related factors, including having a family doctor, and access to an interpreter. These findings emphasize that healthcare system interventions, such as improving access to family doctors and ensuring availability of interpreters, are crucial for addressing vaccine hesitancy in refugee populations. Future research should concentrate on educational interventions, mental health support, and enhancement of culturally competent healthcare services to reduce vaccine hesitancy and protect public health.
