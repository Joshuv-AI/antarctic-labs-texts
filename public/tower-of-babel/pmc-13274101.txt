Development and validation of a preoperative glycolipid metabolism-based nomogram for predicting postoperative recurrence in primary glioma: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Haobin Liu, Yuxiao Wu, Haoyu Sun, Qunyu Sun, Mingguang Wang, Jinling Zhang, Zhong Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlioma recurrence after surgery remains prevalent, significantly impacting patient survival. Tumor progression is closely linked to metabolic reprogramming, especially abnormalities involving glycolipid metabolism. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index accurately indicates insulin resistance (IR) and metabolic disturbances. Although these metabolic indicators are prognostically valuable in various cancers, their role in forecasting glioma recurrence is still insufficiently investigated.MethodsThe medical records of 302 primary glioma patients who received surgical treatment at Linyi People’s Hospital from 2016 to 2024 were retrospectively reviewed. Participants admitted to one ward (n = 236) were randomly assigned to either a training set (n = 141) or an internal validation set (n = 95). Another distinct ward provided patients (n = 66) for an independent internal validation group. In the training cohort, essential glycolipid metabolic parameters were identified via Bootstrap resampling combined with Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression, yielding a stabilized Bootstrap-LASSO Score (BSL-Score). Clinical variables alongside this score were subjected to univariate Cox regression analysis, and variables with statistical significance (P < 0.05) progressed into multivariate Cox regression to pinpoint independent prognostic indicators. Subsequently, these independent indicators were integrated into a nomogram to forecast 1-, 2-, and 3-year postoperative recurrence-free survival (RFS). Model performance was confirmed through concordance index (C-index) evaluation, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA), with Bootstrap correction utilized for the C-index.ResultsIn the training cohort (n = 141), the nomogram achieved a C-index of 0.747 (95% CI: 0.676–0.818) and area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.832, 0.732, and 0.732 for 1‑, 2‑, and 3‑year RFS, respectively. In internal validation (n = 95), the C-index was 0.703 (95% CI: 0.584–0.807); in independent internal validation (n = 66), it was 0.785 (95% CI: 0.694–0.874). Calibration curves showed good agreement, and decision curve analysis confirmed clinical net benefit. The BSL‑Score, derived from routine metabolic parameters (TyG, triglyceride‑to‑high‑density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (TG/HDL‑C), and TyG‑body mass index (TyG‑BMI)), was an independent predictor of recurrence (multivariate Cox, P < 0.05). Risk stratification by the median nomogram score significantly distinguished high‑risk from low‑risk patients (log‑rank P < 0.001).ConclusionThe established nomogram effectively integrates preoperative glycolipid metabolic indicators with key clinical factors, accurately stratifying recurrence risk in postoperative glioma patients. It serves as a valuable reference for personalized postoperative monitoring, where risk-adapted surveillance and intervention strategies could optimize patient outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-026-02968-5.

Introduction

As the most frequent category of primary malignant brain tumors, gliomas represent substantial clinical difficulties due to their exceptionally high recurrence rate post-surgery, significantly impairing patients’ long-term survival [1–3]. Recurrent gliomas are usually more aggressive and exhibit increased resistance to conventional therapies, including radiation and chemotherapy, compared with their primary counterparts, ultimately diminishing therapeutic effectiveness and worsening patient outcomes [4]. Clinically, recurrence timing, especially early recurrence, is closely associated with reduced overall patient survival [5]. Therefore, it is critically important to develop robust predictive models capable of accurately estimating recurrence risk after surgery, enabling individualized monitoring and timely interventions to enhance patient prognosis.

Current research underscores metabolic alterations as a key hallmark of cancerous diseases [6–8]. In particular, abnormalities in lipid metabolism and insulin resistance significantly contribute to cancer onset and development [9, 10]. These metabolic imbalances facilitate tumor cell growth, enhance cellular survival, and enable immune escape via persistent inflammation and stimulation of cancer-associated signaling cascades, especially insulin-like growth factor (IGF)-mediated pathways and the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) /Akt (Akt serine/threonine kinase) / mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) axis [11, 12]. The TyG index has emerged as a straightforward and reliable indicator of insulin resistance. Several recent investigations have demonstrated an association between elevated TyG levels and more advanced tumor stages as well as inferior clinical prognoses in numerous solid tumors, notably gastric and breast cancers [13–17].

However, research on the association between TyG and its derived indices, such as the TyG-body mass index (TyG-BMI) and the triglyceride-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (TG/HDL-C) ratio, with postoperative recurrence among primary glioma patients remains unexplored. Thus, the present study concentrates on glycolipid metabolic markers alongside critical clinicopathological variables to stratify postoperative recurrence risk. The objectives of this research were to elucidate the association between glycolipid metabolism and glioma recurrence, develop a preoperative nomogram incorporating these metabolic indicators, enhance predictive accuracy, reduce unnecessary invasive procedures or overtreatment, and establish a theoretical foundation for individualized postoperative management of glioma patients.

Methods

Figure 1 illustrates the overall study design and process.

Fig. 1Workflow of the study

Workflow of the study

Patient cohort

A retrospective cohort design was employed at a single medical site for this study. A comprehensive study was conducted on the clinical data of 302 patients with primary glioma who underwent surgical resection at Linyi People’s Hospital between 2016 and 2024. A total of 236 patients from a single clinical ward were divided into two groups: one for training (n = 141) and another for internal validation (n = 95) in a 6:4 ratio according to recurrence status. The 66 patients chosen for the independent internal validation cohort were from a different clinical ward. Due to the shared association with the medical center, this study was classified as a single-center analysis, even though the cohorts were derived from different clinical wards. The patient selection workflow is shown in Fig. 1.

The following criteria were used to determine inclusion: (1) primary glioma confirmed histopathologically following initial surgical intervention; (2) complete preoperative clinical records and laboratory tests available; (3) systematic postoperative imaging documentation and patient follow-up for at least three months; (4) no active autoimmune diseases, hematologic abnormalities, or other primary cancers that could significantly affect metabolic assessment or survival outcomes; and (5) no prior anti-cancer treatments.

Data collection and variable definitions

Indices related to glucose and lipid metabolism, such as the TyG, BMI, TyG-BMI, and various lipid ratios, were computed using previously established formulas from the literature.

Radiologically verified tumor recurrence, as measured according to Response Assessment in Neuro-Oncology (RANO) criteria, was the principal endpoint, which was defined as the time from the date of surgery to RFS. At the end of the study, patients were censored if they had not experienced a recurrence. Deaths subsequent to tumor recurrence did not influence the RFS analysis. Although deaths occurring without recurrence would be considered competing events, such cases were absent in this cohort.

Covariates analyzed included clinical and pathological characteristics, along with routine hematological measures such as total bilirubin (TBil), albumin, hemoglobin (Hb), and activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT). Ki-67 expression was categorized into low (< 10%) or high (≥ 10%) groups for prognostic evaluation. MGMT promoter methylation status was assessed, with MGMT defined as O⁶-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase.

Model construction

Patients were randomly stratified into training and internal validation groups at a 6:4 ratio. All modeling procedures were exclusively conducted within the training cohort and involved two primary phases. Initially, glucose-lipid metabolism indicators were subjected to LASSO-Cox regression combined with Bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations) to identify robust metabolic predictors, subsequently generating a Bootstrap-stabilized LASSO Score (BSL-Score). Continuous variables entered the LASSO regression directly without prior categorization, and the feature selection process was strictly confined to the training dataset to avoid information leakage. The BSL-Score and other clinical, pathological, and hematological factors were used in a univariate Cox regression analysis. To identify independent prognostic indicators, variables showing statistical significance (P < 0.05) were included in multivariate Cox regression analysis. These variables eventually helped to create a nomogram that could predict the probability of recurrence. The multivariate Cox proportional hazards model included three independent prognostic variables: the BSL-Score, radiotherapy status, and Ki-67 expression. The events-per-variable (EPV) ratio, reflecting potential overfitting risk, was calculated as 19.3 (58 events/3 variables) based on 58 recurrence events, surpassing the recommended minimum ratio of 10.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed utilizing R software (version 4.5.1). For continuous variables, comparisons were executed via either the Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test depending on data normality, while categorical data were examined using Fisher’s exact test or χ² test.

Both internal and independent internal validations of the nomogram were executed. Model calibration and discriminative power were assessed using calibration curves, time-dependent ROC analysis, and concordance indices (C-index), with the C-index subjected to Bootstrap corrections (1000 resampling iterations). Clinical effectiveness of the nomogram was evaluated through DCA.

Based on the median score derived from the nomogram within the training cohort, patients were classified into categories of lower and higher risk. Recurrence-free survival between these groups was evaluated by applying log-rank tests and Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. Exploratory subgroup analyses were additionally performed according to glioma grade (World Health Organization (WHO) classification), mutation status of Isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 (IDH1), sex, body mass index, and patient age; these findings were exploratory in nature, primarily serving to generate hypotheses. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P-value below 0.05.

Results

Patient baseline characteristics

This retrospective study included 302 patients diagnosed with primary glioma, recruited from two different clinical departments at Linyi People’s Hospital. These participants were subsequently categorized into three separate cohorts. Specifically, 236 patients from one clinical department underwent random allocation into either the training set (n = 141) or the internal validation set (n = 95), using recurrence status as the stratification criterion. The remaining 66 patients from the other clinical department constituted an independent internal validation cohort. Comparison of baseline demographic and clinical features between the training and internal validation sets revealed no meaningful differences (P > 0.05).

The training set had a median age of 53 years, with men accounting for 53.90% of the total cohort. Within the internal validation set, the median age was 54 years, and males represented 46.32% of patients (Table 1). Additionally, the independent internal validation set featured a median patient age of 50 years, with males comprising 65.15% of the population (Table S1).

Bootstrap-LASSO regression screening and BSL-score construction

Glucose-lipid metabolic indicators were systematically assessed using Bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations) combined with LASSO-Cox regression to determine significant predictors of RFS. Through 10-fold cross-validation, the best LASSO penalty parameter (λ) was found by choosing the λ that corresponded to the minimum cross-validation error (λ.min). Fig. 2A and B display the coefficient routes and the cross-validation error curves, respectively. The stability of variable selection and the robustness of coefficients were assessed using the Bootstrap method in order to reduce the risks of overfitting. Figure S1 shows that in Bootstrap resampling, the three glycolipid metabolic markers with the highest selection frequency (> 75%) were TyG, TG/HDL-C ratio, and TyG-BMI. By calculating the variance inflation factors (VIF), it was determined that there was reasonable multicollinearity across these indicators; specifically, TyG had a value of 4.351, TG/HDL-C had a value of 2.410, and TyG-BMI had a value of 6.316. A metabolic risk score (BSL-Score) was derived using standardized coefficients: BSL-Score = (1.00555 × standardized TyG) − (0.99388 × standardized TG/HDL-C) + (0.03934 × standardized TyG-BMI). Figure S2 illustrates the distribution of the BSL-Score across training, internal validation, and independent internal validation cohorts.

Results of univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses

Clinical and pathological factors were subjected to univariate Cox proportional hazards regression, identifying radiotherapy, Ki-67 index, hemoglobin levels, and BSL-Score as significant prognostic factors (P < 0.05, Fig. 3). Subsequent multivariate Cox regression analyses further validated radiotherapy, Ki-67 expression, and BSL-Score as independent prognostic markers (Figure S3).

Model performance evaluation

Nomogram predictive performance was evaluated using the Bootstrap method to determine optimism-corrected C-statistics. Original and corrected C-statistics are compared in Figures S4A-B, showing slight reductions post-correction yet retaining strong predictive capability (corrected C-statistic = 0.736, 95% CI: 0.668–0.807; optimism = 0.022).

Model efficacy was examined via C-statistics, ROC curves, calibration models, and DCA. In the training cohort, the nomogram achieved AUC values of 0.832 (95% CI: 0.715–0.926), 0.732 (95% CI: 0.625–0.833), and 0.732 (95% CI: 0.593–0.852) for 1-, 2-, and 3-year recurrence predictions, respectively, with a C-index of 0.747 (95% CI: 0.676–0.818) (Fig. 5A). The internal validation cohort demonstrated AUC values of 0.720 (95% CI: 0.521–0.876), 0.701 (95% CI: 0.542–0.853), and 0.839 (95% CI: 0.681–0.999), respectively, and a C-index of 0.703 (95% CI: 0.584–0.807) (Fig. 5B). In the independent internal validation cohort, the respective AUC values were 0.760 (95% CI: 0.595–0.927), 0.911 (95% CI: 0.769–0.999), and 0.905 (95% CI: 0.772–0.999), with a C-index of 0.785 (95% CI: 0.694–0.874) (Fig. 5C).

The clinical utility of this prediction model was confirmed in all cohorts and time points using DCA (Fig. 6D-F). The model demonstrated favorable Area Under the Decision Curve (AUDC) values and achieved positive net benefit across standard clinical thresholds (10–30%). This net benefit interval indicated robust performance across a broad range of threshold probabilities (Table S2).

Higher predictive performance metrics observed in the independent internal validation cohort must be interpreted cautiously. The limited sample size (n = 66) could introduce sampling bias, leading to unstable outcomes.

Risk stratification validation

The risk stratification cutoff (median) was determined based on scores from the training cohort. This cutoff demonstrated good discriminatory ability in both internal and external validation cohorts. After implementing the standardized criteria, patients classified as low-risk demonstrated superior RFS at 1, 2, and 3 years compared to those in the high-risk category (Table 2). This outcome was consistent throughout all analyzed cohorts. Kaplan-Meier survival analyses further reinforced these results, showing significantly enhanced RFS in the low-risk groups across training, internal validation, independent internal validation, and internal development cohorts (log-rank P < 0.001, Fig. 7).

Exploratory subgroup analysis

Exploratory subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the consistency of the model’s predictive performance across various patient groups. The recurrence prediction model based on glycolipid metabolic indicators demonstrated predictive value across all predefined subgroups (Table S3). Hypothesis-generating results suggested relatively better predictive performance in male patients (C-index = 0.788), those with IDH-mutant tumors (C-index = 0.768), and individuals in the low BMI group (C-index = 0.769). Predictive performance was similar between younger and older patients. Kaplan-Meier analysis across all subgroups further showed that high-risk patients consistently had significantly higher recurrence rates (P < 0.01, Figure S5). Given the exploratory nature of these analyses and limited subgroup sample sizes, these findings require confirmation in prospective studies.

Discussion

Metabolic disorders, notably IR and obesity, are widely acknowledged as critical risk contributors to multiple malignancies [9, 18–22]. Clinically, these disorders manifest through elevated fasting glucose concentrations and dyslipidemia [23], driving cancer initiation and progression by triggering chronic inflammation and reshaping the tumor microenvironment [11, 12, 24–26]. Although the Warburg effect underscores metabolic reprogramming as a hallmark of malignancy, the predictive role of the TyG index specifically in glioma recurrence remains underexplored [27]. As a robust marker of IR, the TyG index has shown prognostic relevance in various solid cancers, particularly gastric and colorectal tumors [15–17, 28–32]; nonetheless, its prognostic value for glioma postoperative recurrence has yet to be definitively established.

Gliomas are the most prevalent primary malignant tumors of the brain. Even with comprehensive therapeutic approaches, recurrence rates remain high, accompanied by substantial variability in recurrence risks among tumors sharing the same pathological grade [33–35]. Hence, the development of effective, practical prognostic assessment tools is of significant clinical importance. This research presents the first systematic exploration of the prognostic capabilities of the TyG index and related metrics (TyG-BMI, TG/HDL-C) for glioma recurrence. The objective was to examine the predictive relevance of these accessible metabolic markers and to incorporate them into predictive models alongside crucial pathological (Ki-67) and therapeutic (radiotherapy) factors.

Multivariate analyses determined the BSL-Score, Ki-67 proliferation index, and postoperative radiotherapy as independent factors predictive of glioma recurrence. The finding associating elevated Ki-67 expression with heightened recurrence risk aligns with existing literature identifying Ki-67 as a reliable indicator of aggressive tumor behavior and proliferative potential [36]. Additionally, the observed protective impact of postoperative radiotherapy is consistent with established clinical guidelines, highlighting the significance of adjunctive treatments [37, 38].

The BSL-Score is a stable combination of multiple glycolipid metabolic indicators (TyG, TyG-BMI, and TG/HDL-C) selected using Bootstrap resampling combined with LASSO-Cox regression. LASSO inherently addresses multicollinearity by compressing redundant variable coefficients toward zero, retaining only variables with strong predictive power. During Bootstrap sampling, these three indicators were selected in over 75% of 1000 resamples, and VIF analysis indicated acceptable collinearity among them. Although TyG and TyG-BMI are computationally correlated, TyG reflects systemic IR, while TyG-BMI captures the combined effect of IR and obesity. This preserves complementary biological information within the model. The BSL-Score derived from Bootstrap-LASSO-Cox regression retained independent predictive value for recurrence after adjusting for Ki-67 and radiotherapy. This composite score potentially represents a pro-tumor environment driven by systemic IR and abnormal lipid metabolism, synergizing with the proliferative state indicated by high Ki-67 expression. High TyG indicates IR, activating oncogenic pathways such as PI3K / Akt / mTOR, which critically regulate cell proliferation and likely connect metabolic dysfunction with tumor recurrence [39]. These processes involve a complex network linking systemic metabolic disorders with intracellular signaling and tumor-cell metabolic reprogramming. IR-induced adipose tissue dysfunction leads to sustained release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, fostering chronic inflammation that supports cancer progression [40, 41]. Additionally, metabolic disturbances involving hyperglycemia, elevated free fatty acids, and oxidative stress may induce DNA damage and genomic instability [42]. Such damage potentially increases malignant progression risk, interacting with proliferative activity marked by high Ki-67 levels.

Special attention is necessary regarding the impact of key glioma molecular subtypes on metabolic stress responses. Systemic IR (high TyG) and secondary hyperinsulinemia drive mTOR pathway activation in IDH wild-type gliomas. Conversely, the tumor-derived oncogenic metabolite D-2-hydroxyglutarate (D-2-HG) intrinsically disrupts mTOR signaling in IDH-mutant gliomas [43–45]. Systemic metabolic dysregulation (high TyG) may further amplify mTOR activation, influencing recurrence risk. The TG/HDL-C ratio exhibits a negative coefficient in the BSL-Score, suggesting an inverse correlation with recurrence risk.

Another key finding is the negative correlation between the TG/HDL-C ratio and recurrence risk, meaning higher HDL-C levels (lower TG/HDL-C ratios) predict increased postoperative recurrence. Although seemingly contradictory to conventional wisdom from cardiovascular diseases and certain gastrointestinal tumors, this relationship might be explained by the unique glioma metabolic context and existing literature [15, 16, 46, 47]. A schematic (Figure S6) proposes a multi-tiered hypothesis linking systemic metabolic abnormalities to tumor microenvironment remodeling, driving tumor cell proliferation and recurrence.

First, the antitumor function of HDL is highly context-dependent and not solely determined by HDL concentration. Existing literature indicates that lecithin cholesterol acyltransferase (LCAT), the key enzyme for HDL maturation, directly influences HDL particle composition and cholesterol reverse transport [48]. In low-grade glioma, low LCAT expression correlates significantly with poor prognosis, suggesting impaired LCAT activity might diminish HDL’s protective effects [48]. Reduced LCAT function may impair HDL particle maturation. Thus, despite normal peripheral HDL-C levels, these “dysfunctional” HDL particles lose normal anti-inflammatory and cholesterol efflux functions. Second, the unique glioblastoma microenvironment may amplify this effect. Studies show glioblastoma (GBM) survival depends heavily on exogenous cholesterol. Glioblastoma tumor interstitial fluid (TIF) enriches HDL-like cholesterol, and key cholesterol transporters like apolipoprotein E significantly accumulate at tumor margins [49–51]. GBM cells preferentially uptake exogenous cholesterol over synthesizing it de novo [49–51], indicating a distinct HDL-rich cholesterol reservoir in the GBM microenvironment. Tumor growth and surgical trauma disrupt the blood-brain barrier (BBB) [52], allowing circulating HDL to enter the intracerebral tumor environment. Against a backdrop of impaired LCAT function, dysfunctional HDL particles may serve as accessible cholesterol sources, promoting tumor proliferation. This HDL-rich microenvironment may facilitate tumor progression by inducing tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) dysfunction, shifting TAMs toward a pro-tumor phenotype, and weakening anti-tumor immunity [49, 53]. Furthermore, LCAT participates in immune regulation, correlating with immune cell infiltration and tumor mutational burden [48]. Circulating HDL-C concentrations might not fully reflect HDL particle function, as elevated HDL-C can coexist with HDL dysfunction under pathological conditions [54–56]. Gliomas and surgery-induced BBB disruption further complicate relationships between peripheral TG/HDL-C ratios and intratumoral metabolism and immunity [52]. Differences between gliomas and other cancers likely stem from gliomas’ unique metabolic microenvironment and the brain’s anatomical distinctiveness.

Notably, this complex relationship between HDL-C and tumor prognosis is reported in other cancers. For instance, elevated HDL-C levels correlate with increased disease-free survival risk but better overall survival in hepatocellular carcinoma [57]. This finding aligns with the present observation that relatively higher HDL-C predicts increased recurrence risk, indicating HDL-C’s context-dependent role in tumorigenesis. Population-based studies (UK Biobank) similarly reported higher pre-diagnosis HDL-C associated with increased glioma risk, particularly among males [58], further supporting these findings.

Exploratory subgroup analyses indicated relatively consistent model performance across patient subgroups. As hypothesis-generating observations, relatively higher C-index values appeared in males, patients with IDH-mutant tumors, WHO Grade IV gliomas, and low-BMI patients. These preliminary findings require future validation due to subgroup size limitations. Androgen-driven dual regulatory effects may explain superior performance in male patients. Research suggests androgens enhance insulin signaling sensitivity and activate the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway, potentially strengthening TyG-related indicators’ reflection of this pathway [59–61]. As discussed, D-2-HG promotes recurrence by releasing mTOR inhibition [43–45], possibly explaining higher model performance in the IDH-mutant subgroup. Greater metabolic reprogramming in high-grade gliomas [62, 63] may explain superior performance observed in WHO Grade IV glioma. Additionally, systemic tumor-induced changes may become more prominent in lower BMI patients, enhancing metabolic signature detectability. Thus, the BSL-Score likely captures tumor-related metabolic dysregulation beyond reflecting nutritional status alone.

The above interpretations, synthesized from observed associations and existing literature, propose biological frameworks for the BSL-Score’s role, the paradoxical TG/HDL-C association, and differential subgroup performance. These observational data cannot confirm causality; therefore, the proposed mechanisms require validation through further basic and translational research.

This predictive model offers notable practical benefits and clinical applicability compared to more complex predictive models reliant on sophisticated omics technologies. Constructed from routinely available clinical data, fundamental pathological markers (Ki-67), and standard therapeutic information (radiotherapy), the model is practical and easily implementable. Continuous variables were incorporated directly into the LASSO model without prior categorization, with all feature selection processes confined strictly to the training cohort to avoid data leakage. Internal validation demonstrated that the model possesses robust predictive accuracy for short- and intermediate-term RFS. Calibration plots indicated excellent concordance between observed and predicted values, and DCA confirmed clinical utility across an extensive spectrum of threshold probabilities. In practice, when the clinician’s decision threshold for identifying high-risk postoperative recurrence falls within the 10%-30% range, use of this nomogram provides greater net benefit than treating all patients or none. For patients classified as high risk, more intensive imaging surveillance or early consideration of adjuvant therapy may be considered. For low-risk patients, unnecessary interventions may be avoided. Finally, risk stratification based on total model scores effectively distinguished high-risk from low-risk patients, indicating its potential value in guiding individualized postoperative management.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. It is, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, the first to systematically evaluate multiple glycolipid metabolism indicators in primary glioma and to integrate them into a composite BSL-Score using Bootstrap-LASSO regression, which improves the stability of variable selection. The nomogram was developed using routine preoperative clinical and laboratory data, making it practical, noninvasive, and easily applicable in routine clinical settings without requiring additional costly tests. The model also demonstrated good discriminatory ability (optimism-corrected C-index > 0.73) and calibration across internal validation cohorts.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center retrospective cohort study, which restricts generalizability and prevents causal inferences. Although an independent internal validation cohort from another ward was utilized, all patients originated from the same medical center. Future studies should validate the model in multicenter prospective cohorts. Second, unmeasured confounders (preoperative medications, adjuvant therapy details, lifestyle factors) may introduce residual confounding. Third, reliance on single-time-point measurements and lack of dynamic tracking limit the model. Fourth, proposed mechanisms (e.g., LCAT-mediated HDL heterogeneity, cholesterol dependency, BBB disruption) are speculative and require experimental validation. Fifth, subgroup analyses were limited by small sample sizes and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Additionally, the Ki-67 index was dichotomized at a 10% cutoff; while this threshold is clinically practical, such dichotomization simplifies a continuous biological variable and may lead to information loss, as the prognostic value of Ki-67 expression could be more nuanced across its full range [64-66]. Finally, clinical applicability requires prospective interventional studies.

Conclusion

In summary, this study successfully developed and validated a preoperative prediction model integrating a novel glycolipid metabolic score (BSL-Score) with key clinical and pathological factors (Ki-67 and radiotherapy) to predict postoperative recurrence risk in primary gliomas. The model demonstrated strong discrimination and calibration in internal validation. The BSL-Score, derived from routine metabolic indicators, exhibited independent prognostic value, emphasizing the potential importance of preoperative glucose and lipid metabolism in glioma recurrence. Clinically, this nomogram enables risk-stratified follow-up: intensified postoperative surveillance and early adjuvant therapy may benefit high-risk patients, while unnecessary interventions can be avoided for low-risk patients. This stratification could improve patient outcomes and optimize healthcare resource use. Future multicenter prospective studies are necessary to validate the model’s generalizability and robustness and to investigate whether metabolic interventions may modify recurrence risk among high-risk patients identified by this tool.
