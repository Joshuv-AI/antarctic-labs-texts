CCTA-Based Assessment of Coronary Calcium Burden, Inflammatory and Metabolic Biomarkers, and 3-Year Outcomes in Patients with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus and Stable Ischemic Heart Disease
Authors: Guang-An Liu, Wanglong Wu, Wenya Li, Linxiao Zhou, Jing Cui, Ruoxi Zhang, Feng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoronary artery calcification (CAC) is common in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and stable ischemic heart disease (IHD). However, the associations of coronary calcium burden with inflammatory and metabolic biomarker profiles and subsequent clinical outcomes within this population remain incompletely characterized.ObjectiveTo examine the associations of coronary CT angiography (CCTA)-based coronary calcium burden with inflammatory and metabolic biomarkers and 3-year clinical outcomes in patients with T2DM and stable IHD.MethodsIn this retrospective observational study using routinely collected electronic medical record and CCTA data from a single tertiary hospital in Suzhou, China, we analyzed 3991 patients with T2DM and stable IHD who underwent CCTA between January 2016 and December 2022. Patients were stratified by coronary artery calcium score (CACS) as mild (>0–100), moderate (101–400), and severe (>400). Circulating interleukins (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, IL-35), TNF-α, TGF-β, and trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) were measured at baseline. The primary endpoint was 3-year major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCE).ResultsHigher CACS correlated with increased IL-1β and TMAO and decreased IL-10 and IL-35. Over three years, MACCE occurred in 2.9%, 20.1%, and 18.9% of the mild, moderate, and severe groups (P<0.001). In the fully adjusted Cox model, higher ln(CACS + 1) and higher IL-1β levels were independently associated with increased 3-year MACCE risk, whereas higher IL-35 levels were inversely associated with MACCE. Addition of ln(CACS + 1) to the clinical model improved the C-index from 0.704 to 0.750, and the subsequent addition of IL-1β and IL-35 further increased the C-index to 0.766.ConclusionCACS provided substantial incremental prognostic information beyond clinical variables, whereas IL-1β and IL-35 provided a further modest but statistically significant improvement in model discrimination. IL-35 should be regarded as an emerging, hypothesis-generating candidate biomarker rather than an established anti-calcific mediator. These observational findings do not establish causal mechanisms, and the prognostic utility of the combined imaging-biomarker model requires external validation before clinical implementation.Keywords: type 2 diabetes mellitus, ischemic heart disease, coronary artery calcification, IL-35, IL-1β, TMAO

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and stable ischemic heart disease (IHD) frequently coexist and define a population at high risk for adverse cardiovascular events.1,2 Coronary artery calcification (CAC), an imaging marker of calcified coronary atherosclerotic burden, is highly prevalent in patients with T2DM and IHD and provides prognostic information for future cardiovascular outcomes.3–5 However, the relationships among coronary calcium burden, inflammatory and metabolic profiles, and subsequent clinical outcomes within this high-risk population remain incompletely characterized.6

Chronic hyperglycemia contributes to endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and the formation of advanced glycation end products, all of which promote vascular inflammation and osteogenic differentiation of vascular smooth muscle cells.7 In parallel, T2DM is increasingly recognized as a state of chronic low-grade inflammation, with dysregulation of both pro- and anti-inflammatory pathways.8 Beyond glycemic and lipid abnormalities, inflammatory cytokines9,10 and metabolic products, such as trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), may play pivotal roles in calcification and vascular complications.11 Although prior studies have linked CAC to either inflammation or metabolic dysregulation,12,13 these pathways have often been examined separately. In patients with T2DM and established stable IHD, the combined relationships among coronary calcium burden, inflammatory and metabolic biomarkers, and 3-year clinical outcomes remain incompletely characterized. In particular, it remains unclear whether selected inflammatory and metabolic biomarkers are independently associated with subsequent MACCE after accounting for CACS and other relevant covariates, and whether they provide incremental prognostic value beyond CACS. Clarifying these gaps may help improve multidimensional risk stratification in this high-risk population.

Therefore, the present retrospective observational study aimed to examine the associations of coronary calcium burden, assessed within a standardized CCTA protocol, with inflammatory and metabolic biomarkers and 3-year MACCE in patients with T2DM and stable IHD. We further assessed whether selected inflammatory and metabolic biomarkers were independently associated with 3-year MACCE after comprehensive adjustment for clinical risk factors, renal function, diabetes-related characteristics, previous coronary disease, medication exposure, and CACS. We also evaluated their incremental prognostic performance beyond a clinical model and CACS using nested Cox prediction models.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

Between January 2016 and December 2022, we retrospectively screened 4733 patients with T2DM and stable IHD who underwent coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) at our institution. All consecutive patients meeting the prespecified eligibility criteria during the study period were included; therefore, no a priori sample-size calculation was performed. Participants were identified through the institutional CCTA database and electronic medical record system using the predefined clinical eligibility criteria. No diagnostic or administrative billing code–based selection algorithm was used.

Patients were eligible if they were ≥18 years old, had a diagnosis of T2DM and stable IHD,14 and had complete clinical and imaging data. T2DM was defined as a documented clinical diagnosis, use of glucose-lowering medication, or fulfillment of standard diagnostic criteria recorded in the electronic medical record. Stable ischemic heart disease was defined as chronic coronary syndrome with no evidence of acute coronary syndrome or worsening ischemic symptoms within the preceding 3 months. Diagnosis was established based on documented coronary artery disease, prior myocardial infarction or coronary revascularization, or stable anginal symptoms with objective evidence of myocardial ischemia. CCTA was performed for evaluation of stable chest pain, anginal-equivalent symptoms, or risk stratification in high-risk asymptomatic patients.

Exclusion criteria included: a coronary artery calcium score of 0, indicating no detectable coronary calcification, acute coronary syndrome, chronic heart failure, left main disease, severe hepatic/renal dysfunction, or estimated life expectancy <3 years. After exclusions, 3991 patients were included. Patients were stratified by coronary artery calcium score (CACS) into mild (>0–100), moderate (101–400), and severe (>400) (Figure 1).15 This stratification allowed comparison across progressive stages of coronary calcification.

Baseline demographic, clinical, laboratory, and medication data were extracted from the electronic medical record. Diabetes duration, previous myocardial infarction or coronary revascularization, renal function assessed by estimated glomerular filtration rate, albuminuria, and baseline cardiovascular and antidiabetic medication use were recorded. Medication exposure included statins, ACEI/ARBs, β-blockers, antiplatelet therapy, GLP-1 receptor agonists, and insulin therapy.

CCTA Acquisition and Analysis

CCTA was performed using standardized institutional protocols. Non-contrast scans were acquired for CACS calculation, and contrast-enhanced CCTA was subsequently performed for plaque characterization. CACS was quantified using the Agatston method. CT-derived fractional flow reserve (CT-FFR) was calculated using a validated software platform (DeepVessel-FFR, Keya Medical Technology, China). Two experienced radiologists performed all assessments, with adjudication by a senior reviewer when needed. Detailed scanning parameters, contrast injection protocols, and reconstruction settings are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Inflammatory Biomarkers and Metabolic Products

Peripheral blood samples were obtained at baseline before any intervention and after an overnight fast of at least 8 hours. Samples were collected into EDTA-containing tubes and processed within 2 hours of collection. Following centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 minutes at 4°C, plasma was separated, aliquoted into cryovials, and stored at −80°C until analysis. Biomarker assays were performed using previously unthawed aliquots whenever possible, and repeated freeze–thaw cycles were avoided.

Plasma concentrations of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, IL-35, TNF-α, and TGF-β were measured using a commercially available flow cytometry–based multiplex assay platform (Raisecare, Qingdao, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The lower limits of detection for these cytokines ranged from 0.1 to 1.0 pg/mL, and the reported intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation were <10% and <15%, respectively.

Plasma trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) concentrations were quantified using liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (Agilent RRLC-QTOF/MS). Samples remained frozen until batch analysis and were thawed only once immediately before measurement whenever feasible. Laboratory personnel performing biomarker analyses were blinded to CACS category and clinical outcomes to minimize potential measurement bias.

Follow-Up

Patients were followed for 3 years through clinic visits and structured telephone interviews. The primary endpoint was major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCE), a composite of all-cause death, cardiac death, non-fatal myocardial infarction, stroke, and coronary revascularization (PCI or CABG). Clinical events were verified by medical records whenever available.

Statistical Analysis

Quantitative variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data, or as median with interquartile range for non-normal distributions. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages. Differences among multiple groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the Student–Newman–Keuls post hoc test. Overall group differences were evaluated before pairwise comparisons, and the Student–Newman–Keuls procedure was used to account for multiple pairwise testing following significant ANOVA results. The chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was applied for categorical comparisons, while the Mann–Whitney U-test was used for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Associations between inflammatory biomarkers, metabolic products, and CACS were examined using linear regression.

Associations with 3-year MACCE were evaluated using multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression. Candidate variables were selected on the basis of clinical relevance, prior evidence, and their potential associations with MACCE in univariable analyses. The fully adjusted model included demographic and conventional cardiovascular risk factors, diabetes duration, glycated hemoglobin, renal function, albuminuria, previous myocardial infarction or coronary revascularization, baseline medication exposure, CT-derived fractional flow reserve, CACS, and inflammatory and metabolic biomarkers.

Because CACS, calcium volume, and mean calcium attenuation reflect overlapping aspects of coronary calcification burden, they were not entered concurrently into the same primary multivariable model. CACS was natural-log transformed as ln(CACS + 1) because of its right-skewed distribution and was modeled per 1-standard deviation increase.

To assess incremental prognostic performance, three nested Cox models were compared: a clinical model (Model 1), the clinical model plus ln(CACS + 1) (Model 2), and Model 2 plus IL-1β and IL-35 (Model 3). Model discrimination was evaluated using Harrell’s C-index and 3-year time-dependent area under the curve, whereas incremental model fit was assessed using likelihood-ratio testing. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The investigators had access to de-identified data required to create the study cohort. Data were cleaned through range checks, logical consistency checks, duplicate-record review, and reconciliation of discrepant entries against the source electronic medical record. After application of the prespecified complete-data eligibility criteria, no imputation was performed for the primary analyses. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software, version 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and R software, version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Cox regression analyses were performed in SPSS, whereas Harrell’s C-index, likelihood-ratio testing, and 3-year time-dependent ROC analyses were conducted in R using the survival, rms, and timeROC packages.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Among 3991 patients, 2219 were in the mild CACS group, 668 in the moderate group, and 1104 in the severe group. Patients with higher CACS were older, had a longer duration of diabetes, and more frequently had a history of myocardial infarction or coronary revascularization. Renal function progressively declined with increasing calcification burden, as reflected by lower eGFR values and a higher prevalence of albuminuria in the moderate and severe CACS groups. HDL-C and HbA1c levels were modestly lower in patients with higher CACS, whereas the prevalence of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and current smoking was similar across groups. Statin use, ACEI/ARB use, β-blocker use, GLP-1 receptor agonist use, and insulin therapy were generally comparable among groups, whereas antiplatelet therapy was modestly more frequent in patients with higher CACS (Table 1).

Ischemia-Related Lesion Characteristics

Lesion burden and severity increased progressively with higher CACS. The severe group had the highest stenosis severity, longest lesions, and lowest minimal lumen area. Diffuse disease and total occlusions were more frequent in higher CACS groups. Calcium volume and density rose stepwise, and CT-FFR values declined accordingly (Table 2). Correspondence between the distributions of CACS and CT-FFR value was shown in Figure 2. Overall, ischemia-related lesion characteristics worsened progressively with increasing severity of calcification.

Inflammatory Biomarkers and Metabolic Products

Pro-inflammatory cytokines (particularly IL-1β) and TMAO increased with CACS, whereas anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10, IL-35) decreased. TNF-α and IL-6 showed no significant differences among groups. Regression analysis confirmed that CACS correlated positively with IL-1β and TMAO, and inversely with IL-10 and IL-35 (Table 3; Figure 3).

Clinical Outcomes

During 3 years of follow-up, MACCE incidence was substantially higher in the moderate (20.1%) and severe (18.9%) groups compared with the mild group (2.9%, P<0.001; Table 4; Figure 4). Differences were mainly driven by higher rates of mortality, MI, revascularization, and stroke in patients with greater calcification burden.

Predictors of MACCE

In the fully adjusted Cox regression model, longer diabetes duration, lower eGFR, albuminuria, and previous myocardial infarction or coronary revascularization were independently associated with increased 3-year MACCE risk. Higher ln(CACS + 1) was also independently associated with MACCE (adjusted HR, 1.86; 95% CI, 1.53–2.27; P<0.001). Higher IL-1β levels were associated with increased risk of MACCE (adjusted HR, 1.043 per 1 ng/L increase; 95% CI, 1.025–1.061; P<0.001), whereas higher IL-35 levels were inversely associated with MACCE (adjusted HR, 0.945 per 1 ng/L increase; 95% CI, 0.924–0.967; P<0.001). CT-derived fractional flow reserve, IL-10, and TMAO were not independently associated with MACCE after full adjustment (Table 5).

Prognostic Performance

Individual ROC analyses showed that CACS and calcium volume had the strongest individual discriminatory performance for 3-year MACCE (AUC=0.715 for both), followed by IL-35 (AUC=0.677) and IL-1β (AUC=0.648) (Supplementary Figure S1).

In formal nested-model analyses, addition of ln(CACS + 1) to the clinical model significantly improved model discrimination, increasing Harrell’s C-index from 0.704 to 0.750, with an incremental likelihood-ratio χ2 of 63.7 (P<0.001). Addition of IL-1β and IL-35 to the clinical plus CACS model resulted in a further modest but statistically significant increase in C-index to 0.766, with an incremental likelihood-ratio χ2 of 19.6 (P<0.001). The corresponding 3-year time-dependent AUC values were 0.708, 0.754, and 0.770 for Models 1, 2, and 3, respectively (Figure 5; Table 6).

Discussion

In this retrospective observational study of patients with T2DM and stable IHD, higher coronary artery calcium burden was associated with distinct inflammatory and metabolic biomarker profiles. Higher CACS was associated with higher circulating IL-1β and TMAO levels and lower IL-10 and IL-35 levels. These cross-sectional findings should not be interpreted as demonstrating a direct biological pathway linking coronary calcification with inflammatory or metabolic dysregulation. In fully adjusted analyses incorporating renal function, diabetes duration, albuminuria, previous myocardial infarction or coronary revascularization, and baseline medication exposure, IL-1β remained independently associated with a higher risk of 3-year MACCE, whereas higher IL-35 levels remained inversely associated with MACCE. The present observational data indicate that lower IL-35 levels were observed in patients with greater coronary calcium burden and that higher IL-35 levels were associated with a lower risk of subsequent MACCE. However, these findings identify IL-35 as an emerging, hypothesis-generating candidate biomarker rather than establishing an anti-calcific, protective, or causal biological role.

During 3 years of follow-up, MACCE incidence was higher in the moderate and severe CACS groups than in the mild CACS group. The numerically higher event rate in the moderate group than in the severe group should be interpreted cautiously because the difference between these groups was not statistically significant. This pattern may have been influenced by residual confounding, differences in treatment exposure, selection effects, or survivor bias. These explanations remain speculative and should be examined in future prospective studies. Among the individual indicators evaluated, CACS showed the strongest prognostic discrimination, whereas IL-35 and IL-1β showed modest individual discriminatory performance.16–18

Beyond the observed associations, formal nested-model comparisons demonstrated that the addition of CACS to the clinical model substantially improved discrimination and model fit. Addition of IL-1β and IL-35 to the clinical plus CACS model resulted in a further modest but statistically significant improvement in discrimination. These findings support the potential complementary prognostic value of integrated imaging-biomarker assessment in this population. However, the absolute increment in C-index after addition of IL-1β and IL-35 was limited, and the observed performance estimates were derived from the same cohort used for model development. Therefore, these findings should not be interpreted as establishing immediate clinical utility and require independent external validation, calibration assessment, and evaluation of clinical net benefit.19

CAC is primarily an imaging marker of calcified atherosclerotic burden and, in the present study, was associated with inflammatory and metabolic biomarker profiles. Advanced CCTA-derived approaches, including pericoronary adipose tissue assessment and fat attenuation index analysis, may provide complementary information regarding coronary inflammation, although these parameters were not measured in the present cohort.20 Accordingly, inflammatory activity cannot be inferred from CACS alone. Our previous experimental work suggested that IL-35 may promote endothelial repair and modulate macrophage-related inflammatory responses after stent implantation.17 However, those experimental observations cannot establish the mechanism underlying the associations observed in the present clinical cohort. Whether IL-35 influences IL-1β-related inflammatory signaling, vascular smooth muscle cell osteogenic differentiation, or coronary calcification progression remains unknown and requires dedicated mechanistic and prospective studies. Therefore, IL-35 should currently be regarded as an emerging, hypothesis-generating biomarker rather than an established anti-calcific mediator or therapeutic target.

The lack of an independent association between CT-FFR and 3-year MACCE after multivariable adjustment may reflect the relatively restricted CT-FFR variability within this selected stable IHD cohort, as well as overlap between CT-FFR and anatomic lesion- and calcium-related imaging measures. In this cohort, coronary calcium burden and lesion characteristics may have captured part of the prognostic information associated with CT-FFR. However, this interpretation remains speculative and should be examined in prospective cohorts with broader functional ischemic heterogeneity. The observed independent associations of lower eGFR, albuminuria, longer diabetes duration, and previous myocardial infarction or coronary revascularization with MACCE further support the importance of comprehensive clinical adjustment in studies evaluating imaging and biomarker-based risk stratification in patients with T2DM and stable IHD.

Future Directions

Despite its strengths, this retrospective single-center study warrants validation in multicenter prospective cohorts with serial biomarker assessment, formal evaluation of incremental predictive value beyond conventional clinical and imaging models, and dedicated mechanistic studies. Future work should externally validate the incremental prognostic performance of IL-1β and IL-35 beyond clinical variables and CACS, assess model calibration and clinical net benefit, and determine whether the observed biomarker associations are related to coronary calcification progression, plaque inflammation, or treatment response. Integration of multi-omics, advanced CT inflammatory imaging, and AI-driven modeling may further refine individualized prediction of calcification progression and cardiovascular event risk.

Limitations

Some limitations in this study need to be pointed out. First, this was a retrospective, observational study, which limits the ability to establish causality between inflammatory/metabolic markers and CAC progression or MACCE. Second, although based on a relatively large cohort, all patients were enrolled from a single medical center, which may reduce generalizability to broader populations. Third, despite adjustment for multiple covariates, residual confounding cannot be excluded, as unmeasured or incompletely captured factors, including dietary habits, gut microbiota composition, medication exposure, and other clinical or metabolic characteristics, may have influenced inflammatory or metabolic biomarker levels and clinical outcomes. Fourth, biomarkers were measured only at baseline; temporal changes during follow-up were not captured, thereby precluding assessment of longitudinal biomarker trajectories and their temporal associations with CAC progression and clinical outcomes. In addition, the incremental prognostic performance analyses were conducted within the same retrospective cohort and were not externally validated. Therefore, the observed improvements in C-index and time-dependent AUC may be subject to optimism and should be interpreted as exploratory until confirmed in independent prospective cohorts. Fifth, CCTA-based calcium scoring provides robust structural information but does not fully capture functional or microvascular aspects of ischemia beyond CT-FFR. Finally, although 3-year follow-up provided meaningful data, longer-term, multicenter prospective studies are required to confirm the persistence and generalizability of the observed associations.

Conclusion

In patients with T2DM and stable IHD, coronary artery calcium burden was associated with inflammatory and metabolic biomarker profiles and with 3-year MACCE. After comprehensive adjustment for renal function, diabetes-related characteristics, previous coronary disease, and baseline medication exposure, higher ln(CACS + 1) and IL-1β levels were independently associated with increased MACCE risk, whereas higher IL-35 levels were inversely associated with MACCE. CACS provided substantial incremental prognostic information beyond clinical variables, and the addition of IL-1β and IL-35 resulted in a further modest improvement in model discrimination. IL-1β and IL-35 should be regarded as candidate biomarkers requiring external validation rather than established mechanistic mediators or therapeutic targets. These observational findings do not establish causal relationships between coronary calcification, inflammation, and cardiovascular outcomes, and the combined imaging-biomarker model requires prospective multicenter validation before clinical implementation.
