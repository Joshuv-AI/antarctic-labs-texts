Combination scRNA-seq with GWAS identifies genetic risk factors in IgA nephropathy: An observational study
Authors: Jinhua Zhang, Shuo Lu, Guancan Liang, Ruojiao Wang, Hui Zhou, Ning Na, Mingjing Liu, Zhaozhong Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IgA nephropathy (IgAN) is a common autoimmune kidney disease and a leading cause of chronic kidney disease (CKD), yet the immune cell-specific mechanisms underlying disease progression remain incompletely understood, particularly with respect to macrophage heterogeneity. We analyzed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from IgAN and CKD kidney samples to characterize macrophage subpopulations and identify differentially expressed genes. Mendelian randomization analyses using publicly available genome-wide association study data were performed as an exploratory approach to prioritize macrophage-associated genes potentially linked to IgAN risk. Bulk RNA-seq datasets were used for transcriptomic validation. In silico molecular docking was conducted to explore potential small-molecule interactions with prioritized targets. Alternatively activated macrophages were the predominant macrophage subpopulation in both IgAN and CKD samples compared with controls. Exploratory Mendelian randomization analyses highlighted several macrophage-associated genes, including RGS1 and HLA-DPA1, as candidates potentially linked to IgAN susceptibility. Bulk RNA-seq analyses showed downregulation of RGS1 and upregulation of HLA-DPA1 in IgAN, supporting their transcriptional relevance. Molecular docking suggested isotretinoin as a potential small-molecule interactor with RGS1, although this finding is based solely on computational prediction. This integrative, hypothesis-generating study highlights the expansion of alternatively activated macrophage in IgAN and prioritizes macrophage-associated genes that may contribute to disease susceptibility. These findings provide a computational framework for future experimental validation and mechanistic studies, rather than definitive evidence of causality or therapeutic efficacy.

1. Introduction

IgA nephropathy (IgAN) is the most common primary glomerulonephritis worldwide, driven by an autoimmune response to aberrantly glycosylated IgA, which deposits in the glomeruli and induces inflammation and kidney injury.[1] With a global incidence exceeding 2.5 per 100,000 people, particularly high in East Asia, up to 40% of patients progress to end-stage kidney disease within 20 years, representing a major public health burden.[1,2] Current treatments, primarily corticosteroids, offer limited efficacy and carry significant side effects, highlighting the urgent need for targeted therapies.[3] Macrophages play important roles in IgAN progression, with their infiltration positively correlating with proteinuria, tubular atrophy/interstitial fibrosis, and glomerulosclerosis.[4,5] Furthermore, infiltration of macrophage subsets predicts varied responses to immunosuppressive therapy in high-risk IgAN patients.[6] A deeper understanding of IgAN pathogenesis, particularly macrophage heterogeneity, is essential to identify novel molecular targets and develop more effective treatment strategies.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) is a groundbreaking technology that enables the quantification of gene expression in thousands of individual cells simultaneously.[7] This cutting-edge technique provides an unparalleled opportunity to dissect the contribution of various cell types within tissues at a single-cell resolution.[8] Additionally, scRNA-seq facilitates the identification of differential gene expression within specific cellular subpopulations, providing insights into the complex interactions and alterations that occur during disease progression. These advances hold the potential to guide the development of more precise, personalized therapeutic strategies for IgAN.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is an innovative statistical method that leverages the natural random allocation of genetic variants, particularly single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), as instrumental variables (IVs) to strengthen causal inference between exposures and outcomes, emulating randomized controlled trials.[9] It is an established tool in complex disease genetics and drug development.[10] While MR has been applied in IgAN to study metabolites,[11] dietary factors,[12] gut microbiota,[13] and immune traits,[14] it has not yet targeted expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) related to key cellular subpopulations in IgAN pathogenesis. Given that eQTLs directly connect genetic variation to gene expression, macrophage-specific MR analyses offer a precise approach to uncover novel mechanisms and therapeutic targets, representing a significant methodological advance for elucidating cell-specific pathways in IgAN.

In this study, we aim to analyze scRNA-seq data from IgAN patients to identify major immune cell populations and their differential gene expression profiles, elucidate the pathogenic genes involved in IgAN, and explore potential molecular therapeutic targets.

2.1. Data source

Single-cell RNA sequencing data for IgAN and chronic kidney disease (CKD) were obtained from the gene expression omnibus database (IgAN: GSE171314, CKD: GSE199711). The single-cell transcriptomes in the IgAN dataset were derived from IgAN patients and healthy living donors from the original study.[15] The CKD dataset included samples from patients diagnosed with CKD (estimated glomerular filtration rate, eGFR < 60 ml/min·1.73 m2 or pathological albuminuria),[16] and biopsy samples from living donors.[17] All public datasets were rigorously curated to include only primary IgAN and CKD cases. Samples with acute kidney injury or insufficient clinical metadata were excluded prior to analysis. We also utilized bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) datasets of IgAN from gene expression omnibus (GSE175759, GSE93798, GSE116626, and GSE35487) and single-cell RNA sequencing data from the Tabula Sapiens Consortium (https://tabula-sapiens.sf.czbiohub.org/) to supplement our analysis. Since all datasets used in this study are from public databases, ethics approval was not required.

2.2. Analysis of single-cell datasets and cell cluster annotation

The analysis of scRNA-seq data was conducted using the “Seurat” (version 4.4.0) package in R to segregate cells into distinct clusters.[18] Initially, we preprocessed the scRNA-seq data, which included quality control and batch effect correction. Quality control criteria excluded genes expressed in fewer than 3 cells, cells with fewer than 200 detected genes, and cells with a mitochondrial gene proportion >10% to filter out lysed, damaged or apoptotic cells. No explicit ambient RNA contamination correction was applied. Therefore, potential ambient RNA effects on lowly expressed genes cannot be fully excluded. The data were normalized using the log normalize method. Principal component analysis was then performed for dimensionality reduction and the “harmony” package (version 1.2.0) was used to correct batch effects. Uniform manifold approximation and projection algorithms were employed to visualize cell populations. Based on established marker genes from previous literature, we identified and annotated 8 primary distinct cell clusters. Subsequently, we extracted the macrophage cluster and performed subcluster analysis and annotation using the same method. Marker genes for identifying macrophage subtypes were selected based on the study by Caitriona et al.[19] Finally, we visualized the proportions of macrophage subclusters using the “reshape2” (version 1.4.4) and “ggplot2” (version 3.5.0) packages.

2.3. Analysis of cell trajectory and cell communication

As macrophages are characterized by high plasticity and ability to change their phenotype in response to different environmental stimuli, understanding macrophage differentiation is critical to elucidating immune responses in IgAN. We employed the Slingshot package (version 2.10.0), a robust tool for constructing cell differentiation lineages and inferring pseudotime in scRNA-seq data,[20] to identify macrophage cell trajectories. To explore cell communication via ligand-receptor interactions, which helps clarify the relationships between immune and nonimmune cells in kidney tissue, we extracted 2000 cells from each cluster and inferred interactions using the “CellChat” package (version 1.6.1).[21]

2.4. Identification of differentially expressed genes in macrophage subtypes

We used the FindMarkers algorithm from the “Seurat” package to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between alternatively activated macrophage (AAM) subtypes and other immune and kidney cell types. DEGs were filtered using a threshold of |log2(fold change)| >0.58 and an adjusted P-value (false discovery rate, FDR) < .05. The common DEGs from all comparisons were selected for further MR analysis.

2.5. Mendelian randomization analysis of eQTLs

To explore the potential causal relationship between macrophage-associated DEGs and the risk of IgAN and CKD, we performed 2-sample MR analysis using genetic data from large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS). The GWAS summary statistics for IgAN were sourced from the EBI database (ebi-a-GCST90018866), comprising 477,784 samples (15,587 cases and 462,197 controls) and 24,182,646 SNPs. Concurrently, GWAS data for CKD (finn-b-N14_CHRONKIDNEYDIS) were obtained from the Finngen database, which included 216,743 cases (3902 cases and 212,841 controls) and 16,380,459 SNPs. Both the IgAN and CKD samples were of European ancestry. The MR analysis was conducted using the TwoSampleMR R package (version 0.5.6, https://github.com/MRCIEU/TwoSampleMR/releases/tag/v0.5.6).

We first identified eQTLs significantly associated with the DEGs by cross-referencing with the eQTLGen database. These eQTLs served as IVs for the MR analysis. To ensure robustness in causal inference, we applied rigorous criteria for selecting relevant SNPs: a P-value threshold < 5 × 10−8 for genome-wide significance and an F-statistic > 10 to select SNPs strongly associated with the eQTLs.[22] Additionally, to minimize potential biases due to linkage disequilibrium, SNPs were selected based on the criterion of r2 < 0.001 and a clumping window of 10,000 kb. If only 1 eQTL was available for a specific gene, the Wald ratio method was used to assess causal relationships.[23] For genes with 2 or more available genetic IVs, the inverse variance-weighted (IVW) method was employed to combine causal estimates from multiple IVs,[24] providing a more comprehensive and accurate assessment of potential causal links between the DEGs and the risk of IgAN and CKD. MR-Egger regression and leave-one-out analyses were performed to assess pleiotropy and sensitivity. Heterogeneity among IVs was assessed using Cochran Q statistic in both IVW and MR-Egger analyses. A significance threshold of P < .05 was applied, indicating the presence of pleiotropy when P-values fell below this threshold. Given the multi-gene screening nature of this analysis, MR results were interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, with nominal P-values used for prioritization rather than definitive causal inference.

2.6. Bulk RNA sequencing analysis

We further validated the genes identified as causally associated with IgAN risk using bulk RNA-seq data (GSE175759). DEGs between the IgAN and control groups were identified using the “limma” package (version 3.58.1), applying a threshold of |log2(fold change)| > 0.58 and an adjusted P-value (FDR) < .05. Next, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed using the “WGCNA” package (version 1.72.5), with the power parameter set to 7 for soft-thresholding.[25] Among modules generated by WGCNA, genes in modules that were positively or negatively correlated with IgAN and exhibited the highest gene significance were selected. The common genes identified by both methods were used to validate the genetic associations with IgAN risk.

2.7. Candidate drug prediction and molecular docking analysis

The ultimate goal of identifying genes associated with IgAN risk is to explore potential molecular targeted drugs. The Drug Signatures Database (DSigDB, http://dsigdb.tanlab.org/DSigDBv1.0/) was used to predict potential drugs or chemicals that may bind to target genes.[26] Molecular docking analysis was performed to evaluate the binding energy and interaction patterns of drug candidates with target genes, providing an assessment of the druggability of these genes. The structures of drug candidates were obtained from the PubChem Compound Database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), and protein structures were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank[27] (PDB, http://www.rcsb.org/). The ligand structures were retrieved from PubChem and subsequently energy-minimized. Rotatable bonds were assigned and the ligands were converted to PDBQT format using AutoDockTools. The receptor structure was prepared by removing water molecules and ions, adding polar hydrogens, and also converted to PDBQT format using AutoDockTools. Molecular docking was performed with AutoDock Vina (version 1.5.7).[28] The grid box was centered at coordinates (65, 68, 77) for the RGS1 structure. Docking exhaustiveness was set to 8, and 10 independent docking runs were performed for each ligand. The default Vina scoring function was used to estimate binding affinity. Random seed values were automatically generated for each run. The conformation with the lowest predicted binding energy was selected for downstream analysis.

3. Results

3.1. Single-cell clustering and cell annotation of IgAN

We explored the cellular landscape of IgAN using single-cell data from GSE171314. After preprocessing, which included batch effect correction and quality control, we identified 10,560 high-quality cells for further analysis. Principal component analysis revealed 19 principal clusters with distinct gene expression patterns. Based on established cell-type markers from relevant literature[29] (Table S1), these clusters were categorized into 8 main cell types, including proximal tubule cell, loop of Henle, intercalated cell, principal cell, endothelial cell, macrophage, mesangial cell, podocyte (Fig. 2A and B). Numerous studies highlight the critical role of immune cells in IgAN.[30] Interestingly, only the macrophage cluster represented immune cells, suggesting a mixed population of mononuclear phagocytes, possibly comprising several subclusters. We focused on the macrophage subclusters to analyze their heterogeneity. As shown in Figure 2C and D, macrophages yielded 8 cell populations, which were further categorized into 4 subclusters: dendritic cell, CD14+ monocyte, transition macrophage, and AAM, based on classical gene markers.[19] We then assessed the changes in subcluster proportions between IgAN and control groups (Fig. 2E). AAMs represented the largest macrophage subcluster and were significantly increased in the IgAN group compared to controls. Notably, transitional macrophages were significantly reduced, suggesting a potential transition from transitional macrophages to AAMs.

3.2. Single-cell clustering and cell annotation of CKD

As IgAN progresses into CKD and eventually to end-stage kidney disease, we next analyzed the scRNA-seq data from CKD (GSE199711) to delineate cell types and subclusters. After preprocessing, the 19 principal components exhibiting distinct gene expression patterns were categorized into 8 main cell clusters, comprising renal parenchymal cells and immune cells (Fig. S1A and B). Further analysis of the macrophage cluster identified 4 subclusters (Fig. S1C and D). As visualized in the graph (Fig. S1E), the transition macrophage was the predominant subcluster in the control group. Interestingly, AAMs formed the largest subpopulation in the CKD group, similar to IgAN. Furthermore, transition macrophages showed the same decreasing trend in CKD as seen in IgAN, suggesting parallel physiological roles of macrophages, particularly AAMs, in both CKD and IgAN.

3.3. Cell trajectory and cell interactions in IgAN and CKD

Although single-cell RNA sequencing has advanced the understanding of intercellular communication, the cell transition process and cell-type-specific interactions remain incompletely understood. Given the observed changes in macrophage subcluster proportions, we analyzed cell trajectories in IgAN and CKD. As shown in Figure 3A, inferred cell lineages and pseudo times from single-cell gene expression data indicated a progression from dendritic cells and transitional macrophages to AAMs in IgAN. To explore the role of AAM in IgAN, we analyzed cell-cell communication, uncovering complex interactions between macrophage subclusters and renal cells (Fig. 3B). Cell-type-specific interactions revealed that AAMs primarily interacted with proximal tubule cells and podocytes (Fig. 3C and D), suggesting their involvement in glomerular and tubular pathology.

A similar analysis was performed for CKD, revealing a trajectory where transition macrophage and AAM were identified as the initial cell types, with dendritic cell as the terminal lineage (Fig. S2A). As in IgAN, extensive cell-cell interactions were observed (Fig. S2B), indicating a complex pathophysiological process in CKD. However, the primary renal cell types interacting with AAM differed slightly, including proximal tubule cell, intercalated cell, and endothelial cell (Fig. S2C and D), suggesting AAM involvement in tubular and vascular pathology.

3.4. Cell-type-specific eQTLs of AAM

We investigated cell-type-specific eQTLs of AAM by integrating scRNA-seq data from IgAN and CKD with genotyping information. In IgAN, we identified 144 DEGs between AAM and other macrophage subclusters, and 568 DEGs between AAM and other renal cell clusters. After intersecting these DEGs, 140 key marker genes (Table S2) were selected for subsequent MR analysis. Similarly, in CKD, there were 366 common key marker genes (Table S3) of AAM identified from 377 DEGs (AAM vs other macrophage subclusters), and 673 DEGs (AAM vs other renal cell clusters). MR analysis revealed that 8 genes were genetically associated with IgAN risk in MR-based prioritization analyses (Tables S4 and S5), with 5 genes decreasing and 3 genes increasing the risk (Fig. 4A and C). In CKD, 5 genes were linked to risk reduction, while 9 genes were associated with increased risk (Fig. 4B and D, Tables S6 and S7). MR-Egger analysis showed no horizontal pleiotropy (Tables S8 and S9). Leave-one-out analyses, which can evaluate robustness of the results, were displayed in Figure S3 and S4. No significant heterogeneity was observed based on Cochran Q statistics for either the IVW or MR-Egger methods (all P > .05, Tables S10 and S11). Finally, we identified the common genes (RGS1 and HLA-DPA1) prioritized in relation to IgAN risk (Fig. 4E).

3.5. Risk-related genes analysis

We further analyzed the causal risk genes for IgAN to assess their expression across all cell clusters. All 8 risk genes showed the highest expression in the macrophage cluster. Specifically, RGS1, SAMHD1, VSIG4, HLA-DPA1, and HLA-DQA2 were almost exclusively expressed in macrophages (Fig. 5A). We also examined the expression of RGS1 and HLA-DPA1 in macrophage subclusters, finding both genes predominantly expressed in AAM (Fig. 5B and C). Additionally, switch gene analysis indicated that HLA-DPA1 was activated in the early stages, while RGS1 was downregulated in later stages (Fig. 5D), suggesting both genes are expressed throughout AAM differentiation.

3.6. Cell interactions of AAM in IgAN

To investigate the functional significance of risk genes in AAMs, we focused on RGS1 and stratified AAMs into RGS1+ type and RGS1- type. Cell-cell interactions revealed that RGS1+ and RGS1- AAM could interact with each other, suggesting self-regulation within AAM (Fig. 6A and B). However, the renal cell types interacting with these AAM subtypes varied. RGS1+ AAM primarily interacted with tubules including proximal tubule cells and loop of Henle, while RGS1- AAM interacted with mesangial cells, an important cell type in regulating the extracellular matrix. These findings highlight the distinct roles of AAMs in IgAN.

3.7. Risk gene analysis using bulk RNA-seq data

To validate the expression changes of IgAN-associated risk genes, we performed differential expression analysis and WGCNA on bulk RNA-seq data. First, we confirmed the changes in expression of the 8 risk genes (Fig. 7A), with RGS1 downregulated (Fig. 7B) and HLA-DPA1 upregulated (Fig. 7C) in IgAN. The differential expression of RGS1 and HLA-DPA1 in IgAN was also validated in 3 cohorts (Fig. S5). Next, we identified 689 DEGs between IgAN and control groups (Fig. 7D and E, Table S12) and constructed a WGCNA network, which revealed 7 gene modules, with brown, red, yellow, and turquoise modules significantly correlated with IgAN (Fig. 7F and G). Three of these modules (brown, red, and yellow) were positively correlated with IgAN, while the turquoise module was negatively correlated. These modules were also the top 4 modules in gene significance (Fig. 7H). We identified 830 genes from the red and turquoise modules (Table S13), which showed the most significant positive and negative correlation respectively, for intersection with DEGs, with RGS1 being one of the 275 common genes (Fig. 7I, Table S14). Furthermore, using bulk RNA-seq data from GSE93798, which includes clinical information for patients with IgAN,[31] we analyzed the correlation between RGS1 expression and eGFR. Although no statistically significant correlation was found between RGS1 expression and eGFR in 19 patients with IgAN (Spearman ρ = -0.21, P = .39), it indicated a potential negative correlation (Table S15). This pattern suggests that RGS1 may play a potential role in the progression of renal function decline, warranting validation in larger patient cohorts.

3.8. Single-cell expression analysis of RGS1

We conducted a comparative analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing data from the Tabula Sapiens Consortium (https://tabula-sapiens.sf.czbiohub.org/), focusing on differential expression patterns between CKD-associated macrophages and their healthy counterparts in kidney tissue. The analysis revealed tissue-specific regulatory profiles, with RGS1 significantly downregulated in the pathological state (log2FC = -0.771, adjusted P < .001). These tissue- and cell-type-specific transcriptional alterations are detailed in Supplementary Table S16.

3.9. Molecular docking analysis

Given the significance of risk-related genes in IgAN, we evaluated the binding affinities of drug candidates for the proteins corresponding to these genes and the druggability of these genes via molecular docking analysis. DSigDB was used to predict drug candidates, and the top 10 were displayed (Fig. 8A, Table S17). Focusing on RGS1, we analyzed the top 4 candidates, which could potentially interact with protein RGS1. The best interaction modes for each drug, with the lowest binding energies, are shown in Figure 8B–E (Table S18). 4. Discussion

IgAN is driven by complex immune dysregulation, yet the contribution of specific macrophage subsets to disease progression has remained insufficiently defined. Our single-cell analysis revealed a marked expansion of AAMs in both IgAN and CKD, suggesting that this subset may be involved in disease-associated immune remodeling in IgAN. Through RNA-seq and MR analyses, we genetically prioritized RGS1 and HLA-DPA1 in MR-based analyses with AAM biology and IgAN risk. Notably, RGS1 demonstrated a consistent expression trend in external bulk RNA-seq datasets and exhibited IgAN-specific effects in our MR models, suggesting a potentially targetable mechanism in IgAN pathogenesis. Our findings also deepen current understanding of AAM function in kidney injury. Although classically associated with anti-inflammatory activity,[32] the persistence of AAM expansion in both IgAN and advanced CKD stage suggests a complex role that may contribute to disease progression and renal function decline. This interpretation aligns with prior clinical observations linking AAM infiltration to worse proteinuria, reduced eGFR, and poor therapeutic response.[33] Monocyte-macrophage differentiation and subtype switching further support our finding of decreased proportions of transition macrophages and CD14+ monocytes toward an AAM-dominant landscape in IgAN.[34] Importantly, identification of RGS1 as a shared risk gene within AAM provides a possible connection between genetic susceptibility and macrophage function. The molecular docking results with computerized algorithms strengthen its translational relevance and highlight isotretinoin as a potential small-molecule interactor. Collectively, our integrative analyses not only clarify the AAM expansion but also pinpoint RGS1 as a promising therapeutic target, offering a framework for future precision-based interventions in IgAN.

Our MR-based analyses prioritized several AAM-related genes showing genetic association signals with IgAN. MR analysis prioritized RGS1, SAMHD1, NPC2, TUBA1B, VSIG4, ITSN2, HLA-DPA1, and HLA-DQA2 as genetic risk factors for IgAN. RGS1, part of the regulator of G protein signaling family, can activate G protein-coupled receptors, subsequently inducing cellular responses including chemotaxis, inflammatory gene transcription, and proliferation.[35] Previous studies have shown that RGS1 is expressed in monocytes and macrophages.[36] Tefei Lu et al found that RGS1 expression was elevated in renal interstitial fibrosis models and facilitated inflammatory responses and renal fibrosis in mice.[37] Interestingly, our analysis revealed that RGS1 expression was decreased in IgAN patients based on both scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq data. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the patient populations studied. The transcriptomics data (GSE22459 and GSE76882) used by Tefei Lu et al were from kidney allograft tissues, which are influenced by factors such as ischemia-reperfusion injury, rejection, nephrotoxicity, and recurrent glomerular disease.[38,39] This suggests that RGS1 expression may vary across different kidney diseases.

Other genes identified in our study, such as SAMHD1, NPC2, TUBA1B, and VSIG4, also play important roles in immune regulation. For instance, SAMHD1 is an innate immune restriction factor that regulates cell cycle progression,[40] DNA replication,[41] and genome stability[42] via balancing cellular dNTP pools, while NPC2 is involved in cholesterol transport[43] and innate immune regulation in macrophages.[44] TUBA1B, a microtubule isoform, is linked to cytoskeletal formation[45] and VSIG4, a transmembrane complement receptor expressed exclusively on tissue-resident macrophages,[46] is implicated in IgAN-related nephrotic syndrome,[47] epithelial-mesenchymal transition of renal tubular and renal tubulointerstitial fibrosis,[48,49] consistent with our identification of VSIG4 as a risk factor for IgAN. ITSN2 is related to endocytosis, vesicle transport, and signal pathway activation,[50] and ITSN2 variant has been implicated in hereditary nephrotic syndrome.[51,52] Both HLA-DPA1 and HLA-DQA2, which belong to the MHC class II gene family, have been linked to IgAN through GWAS.[53,54] Previous studies revealed MHC class II expression,[47] HLA-DP polymorphisms,[55] and HLA-DQ[56] are associated with IgAN, supporting our findings of HLA-DPA1 and HLA-DQA2 as risk factors for IgAN.

In this study, RGS1 emerged as the most promising drug target for IgAN. The observed RGS1 dysregulation is consistent with potential biological relevance rather than adaptive changes for below considerations. First, our MR analysis provides genetic association evidence consistent with potential involvement for RGS1 in IgAN pathogenesis, as MR methods are less susceptible to reverse causation and confounding than observational approaches. Second, the consistent RGS1 change across both single-cell and bulk RNA-seq datasets reduces the likelihood that this finding represents technical artifact or sample-specific variation. Third, although precise mechanistic studies on RGS1 in IgAN are lacking, prior study based on GWAS in Asian ancestry has elucidated that polymorphic variants in RGS1 lead to immune abnormalities and are associated with IgAN.[57] Future studies using temporal single-cell profiling and cell-specific knockout models will be essential to dissect RGS1’s precise pathogenic role.

Molecular docking analysis identified isotretinoin as the top compound predicted to bind RGS1 with favorable affinity. While isotretinoin is best known for its use in severe acne, prior experimental studies have demonstrated nephroprotective effects, including anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic activities, as well as reductions in renal macrophage infiltration.[58–61] These published findings are broadly consistent with the pathogenic macrophage signatures observed in our IgAN dataset and suggest that isotretinoin may modulate pathological pathways relevant to disease progression. However, it is important to emphasize that the identification of isotretinoin as a drug candidate in our study is entirely based on computational algorithms, and no experimental evidence currently supports a direct interaction between isotretinoin and RGS1 or its efficacy in IgAN. Moreover, clinical reports indicate a dose-dependent safety profile,[62,63] with rare nephrotoxic events reported at low-dose administration.[64] Therefore, while isotretinoin presents an interesting lead for further investigation, its therapeutic relevance in IgAN remains speculative. Rigorous experimental validation, including biochemical assays, macrophage-specific functional studies, and in vivo testing, is essential before any translational conclusions can be drawn.

This study presents an integrated approach combining scRNA-seq, MR, and molecular docking to identify causal risk genes for IgAN and potential drug targets. By taking full advantage of scRNA data and bulk RNA-seq data, we identified genes that are biologically relevant throughout the disease process. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the MR analyses relied primarily on GWAS data from European-ancestry populations, whereas the single-cell datasets may reflect different population backgrounds, potentially limiting generalizability. Second, explicit doublet detection was not performed in the scRNA-seq analyses, which may affect macrophage subcluster resolution and downstream trajectory or cell-cell communication analyses. Third, MR results for genes instrumented by a single eQTL are inherently less robust due to the inability to formally assess heterogeneity or pleiotropy. Finally, molecular docking results are inherently predictive and require experimental confirmation.

In summary, this study provides an integrative and hypothesis-generating framework that combines single-cell transcriptomics, genetic association, and computational modeling to prioritize macrophage-associated genes in IgAN. Rather than establishing causality, our findings serve as a resource for guiding future mechanistic and experimental studies aimed at elucidating macrophage-driven pathways in IgAN.

5. Conclusions

In conclusion, this combination of sequencing data, MR, and molecular docking analyses provides new insights into the immune landscape of IgAN, prioritizing candidate risk-related genes and potential therapeutic targets. Our study significantly advances the understanding of IgAN pathogenesis and contributes to the development of targeted molecular therapies. Future experimental studies will be essential to validate these candidate genes and explore their potential for therapeutic development.
