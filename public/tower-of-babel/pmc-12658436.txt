Emerging Paradigms in Preventive Dentistry: A Review of Nonfluoridated Remineralizing Agents

Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNonfluoridated remineralizing agents have gained significant attention as alternatives or adjuncts to fluoride for managing early caries and enhancing enamel health. This narrative review explores the composition, mechanisms of action, applications, and efficacy of various nonfluoridated remineralizing agents, including calcium phosphate-based systems, casein phosphopeptide–amorphous calcium phosphate (CPP–ACP), bioactive glass, hydroxyapatite nanoparticles, Galla Chinensis, theobromine, Enamelon technology, bamboo salt solution, grape seed extract, tricalcium phosphate, and titanium fluoride technology. The review is based on recent literature to provide a comprehensive overview of these materials and their clinical implications.BackgroundDental caries remains one of the most prevalent oral diseases worldwide. Fluoride has been the cornerstone of caries prevention due to its well-documented ability to enhance enamel remineralization and inhibit demineralization. However, fluoride is often described as a double-edged sword. While its benefits in caries prevention are well-established, excessive fluoride exposure can lead to adverse effects such as dental fluorosis, enamel mottling, and systemic toxicity. These concerns are especially significant for young children, who are more vulnerable to fluoride ingestion. Consequently, there is a growing need to explore nonfluoridated remineralizing agents that can offer comparable efficacy without associated risks.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted using databases such as PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science. Articles were selected based on relevance, with emphasis on randomized controlled trials, in vitro studies, and clinical reviews. A total of 80 articles were initially screened, of which 20 were included in this review based on their relevance to the topic.ResultsThis review examines recent advancements in nonfluoridated remineralizing agents, their mechanisms of action, and their role in contemporary preventive dentistry. Evidence suggests that nonfluoridated agents play a promising role in promoting enamel remineralization and reducing demineralization without the concerns associated with fluoride toxicity. Introduction

Nonfluoridated agents work primarily by replenishing lost calcium and phosphate ions to repair demineralized enamel. These agents include calcium phosphate-based technologies, bioactive glass, hydroxyapatite nanoparticles, Galla Chinensis, theobromine, Enamelon technology, bamboo salt solution, grape seed extract, tricalcium phosphate, and titanium fluoride technology. Their mechanisms vary, but they generally aim to mimic the natural remineralization process, promote ion release, and create a protective barrier against further demineralization.

Demineralization–Remineralization Concept

Demineralization occurs when enamel loses essential minerals, primarily calcium and phosphate, due to acidic environments created by bacterial metabolism or dietary acids. Remineralization, on the other hand, is the process of restoring these lost minerals to the enamel matrix, thus reversing early caries lesions. Nonfluoridated remineralizing agents work to enhance the natural remineralization process by providing a reservoir of bioavailable ions that integrate into the enamel structure. The balance between demineralization and remineralization is critical for maintaining enamel integrity and preventing caries progression.

Types of Nonfluoridated Remineralizing Agents

Casein Phosphopeptide–Amorphous Calcium Phosphate (CPP–ACP)

Casein phosphopeptide–amorphous calcium phosphate is derived from milk protein casein, which stabilizes calcium and phosphate ions in an amorphous state, preventing premature precipitation. When applied to enamel, CPP–ACP provides a reservoir of ions that can diffuse into enamel subsurface lesions, promoting remineralization. Clinical studies, such as those by Reynolds et al., have shown a significant reduction in white spot lesions and improvement in enamel microhardness when CPP–ACP is used. Additionally, the agent is highly biocompatible, making it suitable for all age groups.7 Evidence from a randomized controlled trial has demonstrated that the application of CPP–ACP is a clinically effective strategy for reducing the risk of enamel demineralization commonly observed during the course of treatment.

Nanohydroxyapatite (nHAp)

Nanohydroxyapatite is a biomimetic material that closely resembles the natural mineral composition of enamel. Its nanoscale size allows it to integrate seamlessly into enamel microdefects, filling pores and restoring surface integrity. A study demonstrated that the use of nHAp toothpaste significantly improved enamel microhardness and reduced surface roughness in demineralized enamel.8 Unlike fluoride, nHAp does not rely on acid-etching for remineralization, making it a safer alternative for children and individuals with fluoride sensitivity. Furthermore, nHAp has shown potential in reducing dentinal hypersensitivity by occluding dentinal tubules.9

Theobromine

Theobromine, a natural compound found in cocoa, has garnered interest for its ability to enhance hydroxyapatite crystallinity. It strengthens enamel by promoting the growth of larger and more stable hydroxyapatite crystals compared to those formed with fluoride alone. Clinical studies have demonstrated that toothpaste containing theobromine improves enamel hardness and provides a comparable remineralization effect to fluoride toothpaste.10 Research by Amaechi et al. showed that theobromine-based treatments reduced enamel demineralization in vitro, with additional benefits of being nontoxic and suitable for long-term use.11

Calcium Sodium Phosphosilicate (Bioactive Glass)

Bioactive glass, such as NovaMin, releases calcium and phosphate ions when exposed to saliva, forming a hydroxycarbonate apatite layer on the enamel surface. This layer acts as a barrier to acid attacks and aids in the remineralization of subsurface lesions. Studies by Hench et al. have shown that bioactive glass is effective in reducing enamel sensitivity and promoting remineralization, particularly in patients with erosion-related enamel loss.12

Galla Chinensis

Galla Chinensis, derived from Chinese galls, contains tannins that exhibit antimicrobial and remineralizing properties. It forms a calcium-tannin complex on enamel, creating a protective barrier and promoting mineral deposition. Studies indicate that Galla Chinensis effectively reduces enamel demineralization and inhibits cariogenic bacteria, making it a potential adjunctive therapy for caries prevention.13

Enamelon Technology

Enamelon combines stabilized calcium and phosphate ions to enhance remineralization. This technology is particularly effective in managing early caries lesions and reducing sensitivity. Clinical trials have demonstrated its efficacy in reducing enamel demineralization and promoting remineralization in high-risk populations.14

Ozone Technology

Ozone therapy in dentistry utilizes ozone gas to kill cariogenic bacteria and oxidize biofilms. The remineralization effect is achieved as ozone cleanses the lesion, allowing remineralizing agents like CPP–ACP to penetrate effectively.15

Pro-arginine Technology

Pro-arginine technology uses arginine and calcium carbonate to occlude dentinal tubules, reducing hypersensitivity and enhancing enamel remineralization. Clinical trials have shown its potential for immediate and lasting relief of sensitivity.16

SensiStat Technology

Combining calcium carbonate and trimetaphosphate ion, SensiStat forms a protective layer over dentinal surfaces, restoring hardness and preventing demineralization.17

Calcium Orthophosphate Saccharose Complex (Toothmin)

This innovative formulation integrates calcium phosphate with saccharose, enhancing enamel remineralization. It has shown promise in in vitro studies for its efficiency in addressing early carious lesions.18

Conclusion

The exploration of nonfluoridated remineralizing agents presents a promising shift in preventive dentistry, offering effective alternatives to traditional fluoride-based treatments. Various agents demonstrate significant potential in enhancing enamel remineralization and preventing demineralization. Their ability to restore lost minerals, create protective barriers, and, in some cases, exhibit antimicrobial properties underscores their clinical relevance. While existing studies highlight their efficacy, long-term clinical trials and comparative analyses with fluoride are necessary to establish standardized protocols for their use. Further research should also explore the synergistic potential of these agents and their applicability in diverse patient populations. As the demand for fluoride-free or adjunctive remineralization therapies grows, the development of safe, biocompatible, and cost-effective nonfluoridated agents may reshape future caries management strategies, ensuring improved oral health outcomes for all.
