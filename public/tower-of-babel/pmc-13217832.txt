Traumatic chylothorax after blunt thoracic injury: A case series from a level 1 trauma centre
Authors: Kaiwen Cabbabe, Joseph Mathew, Warren Clements, Silvana Marasco, Christopher Groombridge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Traumatic chylothorax is an uncommon complication of thoracic injury resulting from disruption of the thoracic duct or its tributaries. Non-iatrogenic blunt traumatic chylothorax is particularly rare and may present with delayed clinical features, making diagnosis challenging. We present a case series of four patients with blunt trauma–associated chylothorax managed at a level 1 trauma centre over a 22-year period. We aim to highlight patterns of delayed diagnosis, explore the relationship between injury level and chylothorax laterality, and describe the multidisciplinary management strategies used in this rare but clinically significant complication of thoracic trauma.All patients presented with pleural effusions following thoracic trauma, with diagnostic delays ranging from 2 to 10 days. Mechanisms of injury included falls and high-speed motor vehicle collisions, and associated injuries commonly involved rib or vertebral fractures. Diagnosis was confirmed with pleural fluid triglyceride analysis. Initial management consisted of thoracostomy drainage and dietary modification in all cases, with additional therapies including octreotide infusion and surgical pleurodesis when conservative measures failed. Chylous drainage volumes ranged from 903 mL to 3680 mL, with leak duration between 4 and 22 days. One patient developed significant complications related to prolonged chyle loss including malnutrition and infection. All cases ultimately achieved resolution.This case series highlights the delayed diagnosis of traumatic chylothorax following blunt thoracic trauma and underscores the importance of maintaining clinical suspicion in patients with evolving pleural effusions. Management requires a multidisciplinary approach, and while conservative measures are often effective, persistent high-output leaks may require pharmacological or surgical intervention.

Introduction

Chylothorax is a rare injury resulting from injury to the thoracic duct or one of its tributaries. It can be broadly classified as either traumatic or non-traumatic [1], and approximately 80% are iatrogenic [2]. For non-iatrogenic causes, the incidence is reported between 0.9% and 1.3% for penetrating injuries and 0.2% to 3% for blunt force injuries [2]. Published reports of blunt traumatic chylothorax are limited to isolated case reports, reflecting the rarity of this injury.

At present, there are no guidelines for the management of adult chylothorax. A 2024 consensus statement summarised current multidisciplinary management strategies, recommending that conservative, minimally invasive, and surgical interventions are all viable depending on the context of the leak [3].

This manuscript presents four cases of blunt traumatic chylothorax in adult patients admitted at our level 1 trauma centre. The aim of this series is to highlight patterns of delayed diagnosis, explore the relationship between injury level and chylothorax laterality, and describe the multidisciplinary management strategies used in this rare but clinically significant complication of thoracic trauma.

Case 1

A 71-year-old male presented two days after falling 2 m from a ladder onto his back. Physical examination revealed reduced air entry at the right lung base and right scrotal swelling. His Injury Severity Score (ISS) was 8. Initial chest x-ray (CXR) demonstrated a left lateral sixth rib fracture and a small right pleural fluid collection. He remained clinically well and was discharged on Day 2 after conservative management.

He re-presented 8 days later with progressive orthopnea and 5 kg weight gain. Repeat CXR showed an enlarged right and a trace left pleural fluid collection. A right intercostal catheter (ICC) was inserted, and 1175 mL of milky fluid was drained. Pleural fluid triglycerides were 40.8 mmol/L, confirming the diagnosis of a right-sided chylothorax.

A low-fat diet was commenced, but chyle output persisted for 5 days with a total of 2165 mL drained. As the leak was not amenable to conservative measures, he underwent video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS). A leak level was not identified, and a chemical pleurodesis was performed instead of thoracic duct ligation.

Postoperatively, repeat right pleural fluid triglyceride was 0.48 mmol/L, confirming biochemical resolution, and repeat CXR showed resolution of the chylothorax. He was discharged home after a 14-day admission.

Case 2

A 27-year-old male was found GCS 3 following a pedestrian-motor vehicle collision. At presentation, physical examination and radiological investigations revealed multisystem injuries, and his ISS was 38. An initial CXR demonstrated right-sided pleural fluid which was confirmed on CT alongside extensive retroperitoneal fluid and a possible tear of the right diaphragmatic crus.

A right ICC was inserted, draining 760 mL of pink, milky fluid. On day 2, pleural fluid triglycerides were 3.3 mmol/L, biochemically diagnostic of chylothorax. The suspicion of a right diaphragmatic tear on CT prompted an exploratory laparotomy, which revealed extensive chylous ascites without a diaphragmatic defect.

Postoperatively, he was commenced on low-fat total parenteral nutrition (TPN). The chyle leak ceased spontaneously by Day 4. Repeat pleural fluid analysis showed biochemical resolution on Day 5, with triglycerides of 0.4 mmol/L. 1310 mL of total chyle was drained during his admission. The right ICC was removed, and dietary modifications ceased by day 8. He was discharged home after 56 days due to other extensive injuries.

Case 3

A 69-year-old female was the restrained driver in a high-speed, rear-end motor vehicle collision. At presentation, her ISS was 30, and she experienced neurogenic shock and bilateral tension haemopneumothoraces. Bilateral ICCs were placed for chest decompression, and she was admitted to the ICU. Whole body CT imaging revealed a three-column T5 vertebral body injury with fracture-dislocation and complete spinal cord transection. She underwent extensive surgical management for spinal injuries. Both ICCs initially drained blood; however, by Day 2, the output became cloudy. Pleural fluid triglycerides confirmed bilateral chylothorax of 3.1 mmol/L (left) and 3.9 mmol/L (right).

Low-fat nasogastric feeding was commenced on Day 3. By Day 4, drainage had transitioned from chylous to serous and had reduced in volume from 700 mL to less than 200 mL. A total of 903 mL (left) and 301 mL (right) of chylous fluid was drained. Repeat pleural fluid analysis on Day 4 confirmed biochemical resolution, with triglyceride levels of 0.6 mmol/L (left) and 0.4 mmol/L (right). Radiological resolution of pleural effusion with chest X-ray was confirmed on Day 6. Dietary modifications ceased on Day 7, and both ICCs were removed on Day 10. The patient was stepped down from ICU on Day 12 and discharged on Day 17 to spinal rehabilitation.

Case 4

A 23-year-old male was a restrained rear seat passenger in a high-speed motor vehicle accident. On arrival, he experienced a hypovolaemic cardiac arrest managed with bilateral finger thoracostomies and chest compressions while a massive transfusion was administered. His ISS was 50 on admission, with the most relevant injuries being multi-level posterior and posterolateral rib fractures and bilateral haemopneumothoraces requiring bilateral ICCs that resolved during an 18-day ICU admission.

On Day 24, he developed new tachypnoea and pleuritic chest pain. An initial CXR and CT pulmonary angiogram revealed new bilateral pleural fluid. On day 26, a left-sided ICC was inserted, draining 330 mL of milky fluid with a triglyceride concentration of 9.9 mmol/L. He was commenced on a low-fat diet on Day 27. Intravenous octreotide was initiated on Day 39 for persistent drainage >200 mL/day, temporarily resolving chyle output after 4 days. However, the leak rebounded to 430 mL in 24 h after an oral fat challenge.

A left-sided VATS was performed on Day 48 post-admission. No thoracic duct injury was visualised, however mechanical pleurodesis was performed prophylactically. Post-operatively, drain output became haemoserous. The patient lost 22 kg during the admission and weighed 54 kg at the time of surgical management. A total of 3680 mL of chyle was drained from the left chest. His total length of stay was 79 days, complicated by hospital-acquired pneumonias and malnutrition.

Discussion

The diagnosis of chylothorax is determined by pleural fluid triglyceride levels of >1.24 mmol/L or 110 mg/dL [1], [4], [5]. Notably, the macroscopic milky appearance of chyle is reported to only occur in about 22–44% of patients [5]; therefore, its absence does not exclude chylothorax. Diagnosis is often delayed by up to 7 days, due to nonspecific symptoms and a generally low index of suspicion, given the rarity of the injury [6], [7]. A diagnostic delay was evident in all cases in this series for similar reasons.

Evaluation of the thoracic duct anatomy may predict the pattern of injury observed. As the thoracic duct ascends from the cisterna chyli on the right side, it crosses the midline to the left side at the thoracic plane (T4-T6) and empties into the left subclavian vein. Traumatic injuries above and below the thoracic plane can cause left- and right-sided duct injury respectively, with subsequent ipsilateral chylothorax [5]. Injuries below T6 were the likely source of the right-sided leak in cases 1 and 2. Injury at the T5 level precipitated the bilateral chylothorax observed in case 3. The delayed onset of case 4 posed a diagnostic dilemma; however, the left-sided leak may suggest injuries above T4 played a role.

Blunt traumatic chylothorax is caused by shearing forces from acceleration-deceleration that tear the thoracic duct wall [6], [8]. Rib and vertebral fractures are concomitant injuries that have been shown to increase the risk of chylothorax [2], often associated with high energy mechanisms. However, as our series demonstrates, diagnostic suspicion should be maintained even for simpler mechanisms.

Definitions of persistent, high- and low-output chylothorax vary [1], [3]. Severe complications arising from a high-output chylothorax include malnutrition, dehydration, fluctuations in intravascular volume, and immunosuppression [1], [3], [5]. A prolonged high-output leak is associated with worse long-term mortality rates of 25–50% [1], necessitating more aggressive interventions. In case 4, the prolonged 22-day leak and ongoing dietary modifications caused malnutrition, which complicated their recovery from initial injuries. Repeated healthcare-associated infections may have also been due to immunosuppression secondary to chylothorax. However, this was the only case of significant complications observed in our series.

Management recommendations across output thresholds also vary. Bacon and colleagues suggested that leaks of 500-1000 mL/day should be managed non-operatively for at least 2 weeks before surgical intervention [9], while two other studies indicated that leaks exceeding 1000 mL for five consecutive days necessitate surgical intervention [10], [11]. Martucci et al. proposed that any volume persisting for two weeks should be considered for surgical management [12]. In this series, the timing of intervention was a multidisciplinary decision from Trauma physicians, Intensivists, Cardiothoracic surgeons, Interventional Radiologists, and Nutrition specialists to formulate appropriate management strategies for all cases. In the absence of definitive management guidelines, trending leak volumes over several days and ongoing multidisciplinary input should be pursued before making aggressive management decisions.

Thoracostomy tube drainage and dietary modifications are recommended first-line interventions [3]. Up to 50% of non-malignant chylothorax resolves with these measures alone [13]. Somatostatin analogues can be used as second-line management, but only in patients who are refractory to first-line interventions [3]. As observed in case 4, Octreotide had a minimal effect in resolving the injury.

There is a paucity of evidence validating surgical management for traumatic chylothorax due to the rarity of the injury. Definitive surgical management usually involves thoracic duct ligation (TDL) using a video-assisted or robotic-assisted approach, surgical or medical thoracoscopic pleurodesis, or lymphangiography [3]. TDL remains the gold standard from expert consensus, with an approximate 90% success rate for post-operative chylothorax [3]. Pleurodesis has a reported success rate of 80–100% in the literature for nontraumatic and post-surgical chylothorax [3]. Cases 1 and 4 achieved complete resolution with pleurodesis, in which the leak level was not identified despite the initial intention to attempt TDL.

Lymphangiography is a minimally invasive technique that involves opacifying the level of injury with lipiodol, which may be curative alone with its sclerosant and occlusive effects [14]. It is a time-consuming and technically challenging procedure [15]. It is sometimes followed by percutaneous thoracic duct embolisation (TDE) for definitive endovascular management [1]. Recommended indications for lymphangiography and TDE after traumatic aetiology depend on output volume and institutional expertise [3]. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Kim et al. reported successful cessation of chyle leak in 63.7% and 92.4% of patients undergoing lymphangiography and TDE, respectively, for traumatic chylothorax [14]. Interventional techniques were considered as management options for cases 1 and 4; however, the prolonged procedural duration in the context of multi-system injuries was the primary barrier.

Given the absence of standardised management guidelines, we propose a pragmatic stepwise management algorithm based on our case series and current literature (Fig. 1). This framework emphasises early diagnostic confirmation, structured escalation of conservative therapy, and clear indications to consider minimally invasive or surgical intervention. The algorithm prioritises progression from least to most invasive strategies, reflecting reported treatment efficacy while balancing patient safety and clinical context. As conservative therapy is effective for the majority of blunt traumatic chylothoraces, this algorithm may assist clinicians in identifying patients who are likely to benefit from timely escalation to invasive intervention. Conclusion

Traumatic chylothorax is a rare but important complication of blunt thoracic trauma that may present with delayed or persistent pleural effusion several days after injury. Maintaining a high index of suspicion and performing pleural fluid triglyceride analysis are essential for timely diagnosis. A range of treatment modalities can be considered stepwise, beginning with conservative measures and progressing to surgical or minimally invasive interventional techniques if leaks persist. A stepwise management algorithm is proposed to support clinical decision-making, emphasising a structured progression from conservative to invasive management. These cases highlight the importance of multidisciplinary input and demonstrate that both conservative and surgical approaches can achieve successful outcomes in non-iatrogenic traumatic chylothorax, despite typical delays in diagnosis in the setting of complex multisystem injury.
