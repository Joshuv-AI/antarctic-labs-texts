Effects of Leontopodium alpinum supplementation on blood parameters, hepatic histology, and gene expression in broiler chickens
Authors: Reszka Patrycja, Stasiak Karolina, Dankowiakowska Agata, Cygan-Szczegielniak Dorota, Pietrzak Elżbieta, Stanek Magdalena
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmid the growing demand for natural growth promoters in poultry production, phytobiotics are being extensively explored for their bioactive properties. Study aimed to evaluate the effects of Leontopodium alpinum extract on blood parameters, hepatic histopathological changes, and the expression of genes in broiler chickens. A total of 180 Ross broilers were divided into groups: control (C), E1 (0.5 g kg−1 feed), E2 (1.0 g kg−1 feed). Reductions in serum ALT and AST activities (p < 0.001), TCH (p = 0.001), and TG (p = 0.014) were observed in group E2. E1 showed the most favorable coagulation profile in the case of FIB (p = 0.038), TT (p = 0.041), and PT (p = 0.013), compared to groups E2 and C. Hematological analysis showed increased RBC (p = 0.007), hemoglobin (p = 0.037), WBC (p = 0.001), neutrophils (p = 0.022), and decreased monocytes (p = 0.039) in group E2. Expression of metabolic regulators FASN (E1, p = 0.018; E2, p = 0.007), NRF1 (E2 vs. C, p = 0.002; E2 vs. E1, p = 0.033), PPARA (E2 vs. C, p = 0.002; E2 vs. E1, p = 0.009), and UGT1A1 (E2 vs. C, p = 0.001) was reduced in a dose-dependent manner. HMOX1 (p = 0.003), NFE2L2 (E2 vs. C, p = 0.021), and NQO1 (E2 vs. C, p = 0.003) were downregulated in E2. L. alpinum (E2) enhances antioxidant capacity and metabolic performance in birds without histopathological or hemostatic side effects.Keywords: Phytobiotic, Liver histopathology, Gene expression, Blood biochemistry, Hematology, Coagulation parametersSubject terms: Biochemistry, Immunology, Molecular biology, Physiology, Zoology

Introduction

Traditional medicinal plants have long been used for the symptomatic treatment of various ailments, including diarrhea and abdominal pain, in both humans and animals1. Plant-derived materials remain an important source of bioactive compounds, and many medicinal plants have been studied for their potential therapeutic applications. Among these, Leontopodium alpinum contains a diverse range of bioactive constituents, which have been reported to exhibit potential health-promoting, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties2. The anti-inflammatory properties of L. alpinum extract have been demonstrated in studies by Speroni et al.2 and Dobner et al.6. However, these investigations were conducted in other animal species and did not focus on liver histology or blood biochemical and hematological parameters. In contrast, research on other phytobiotics has confirmed that plant-derived bioactive compounds may exert beneficial effects on the physiology of poultry. For example, Manjaniq et al.7 evaluated the effects of dietary supplementation with Hibiscus sabdariffa flowers and Moringa oleifera leaves on the blood profile of broiler chickens. Their results indicated that a 3.0% supplementation level had no adverse effects on hematological parameters, including leukocyte and erythrocyte counts, hematocrit, hemoglobin, and cholesterol levels, suggesting that the phytobiotics were safe and did not impair physiological functions. Similar findings were reported by Oktaviana et al.8, who supplemented broiler diets with 2% Angelica keiskei leaf powder. Furthermore, studies by Armanini et al.9, Purwanti et al.10, and Iwiński et al.11 have demonstrated the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties of L. alpinum extract, supporting its potential to mitigate histopathological changes in the liver, as has been observed for other phytobiotics. Additionally, nutraceutical plant supplements such as Spirulina platensis, Curcuma longa, Taraxacum officinale, Trigonella foenum-graecum, and Origanum vulgare extracts have been explored as plant-derived dietary additives that can improve health status and production parameters without inducing adverse effects in broiler chickens, further emphasizing the feasibility of plant-based feed supplementation strategies in poultry nutrition12–15. In the present study, relatively low doses of L. alpinum were used to avoid potential adverse effects on organ development, tissue structure, and blood biochemical and hematological parameters in broiler chickens.

Leontopodium alpinum has been reported to contain a diverse range of bioactive constituents, notably flavonoids and phenolic acids. These compounds are known to influence various biochemical parameters in avian blood, potentially affecting nutrient assimilation, metabolic activity, and immunological functions in poultry. Phytobiotics derived from other plant sources, such as turmeric and spirulina, have been tested without evidence of negative impacts on organ health, supporting the safe application of similar bioactive plant extracts in broiler diets12. The enzymatic activity of alkaline phosphatase (ALP), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) is widely recognized as a reliable diagnostic tool for evaluating liver function and detecting hepatotoxicity16. Numerous studies have shown that phytobiotics exhibit anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, contributing to the reduction of oxidative stress in the liver and other lymphoid organs, which in turn leads to decreased serum AST and ALT levels in broiler chickens17,18. Hematological indicators, while an important diagnostic tool for assessing not only circulatory system function but also overall body homeostasis, are more challenging to evaluate in poultry due to variations depending on species, breed, age, sex, and diet.

Diagnosing hemostasis disorders is crucial in poultry farming due to their association with carcass defects and increased bird mortality. Despite its importance, hemostasis in poultry has been the subject of relatively few studies. The mechanisms underlying avian blood coagulation and their dysfunctions remain poorly understood, primarily because optimized analytical procedures and commercial species-specific reagents for the analysis of hemostatic parameters are not available. Therefore, analyses are based mainly on traditional coagulological methods19. Hemostasis is maintained by a set of physiological mechanisms that preserve the fluidity of blood circulating in the vascular system and enable rapid cessation of bleeding following blood vessel injury19,20.

Unlike commonly used phytobiotics such as turmeric or oregano, L. alpinum is characterized by a unique profile of bioactive compounds, most notably leontopodic acids A and B. These potent phenylpropanoid derivatives exhibit radical-scavenging activities significantly higher than those of standard antioxidants like α-tocopherol. While many plant additives focus broadly on gastrointestinal health, the specific antioxidant density of L. alpinum suggests a more targeted protective effect on the liver, potentially involving the direct neutralization of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the modulation of signalling pathways governing redox balance and lipid synthesis. However, despite these well-known anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, the effects of L. alpinum on liver histopathology, blood biochemical parameters, hematological indices, and hemostasis in poultry remain insufficiently investigated, especially in comparison with other plant-based supplements already established in broiler nutrition. This significant research gap underscores the necessity to explore L. alpinum extract as a promising phytobiotic with potential benefits for poultry health and metabolic efficiency.

Consequently, the central research question of this study is to investigate how dietary supplementation with L. alpinum extract influences hepatic gene expression and metabolic parameters. We hypothesize that the high concentration of leontopodic acids in the L. alpinum may provide an exogenous antioxidant support. The liver was chosen as the target organ due to its central role in lipid metabolism and its high susceptibility to oxidative challenges in fast-growing broilers.

The aim of the study was to evaluate the effect of dietary supplementation with L. alpinum extract on selected blood parameters, including protein, fat, and enzyme profiles, coagulation indices, and blood count parameters, including red and white blood cells counts, histopathological changes in the liver, including congestion, fibrosis, necrosis, presence of vacuoles, fat vacuolization and parenchymal eclipse, and the hepatic expression of genes related to lipid metabolism, antioxidant defense, and detoxification pathways in broiler chickens.

Materials and methods

Animal maintenance

The study was conducted on 180 mixed-sex Ross 308 broiler chickens. Birds were purchased from a commercial hatchery located in the Warmian-Masurian Voivodeship in Poland. All birds were vaccinated (at the hatchery) against Marek’s disease (MD), Newcastle disease (ND), infectious bronchitis (IB), and infectious bursal disease (IBD). The birds were randomly divided into three groups. Each group consisted of 5 replicates (12 birds per replicate). The control group (C) received a standard complete feed; experimental group 1 (E1) was fed the standard diet supplemented with L. alpinum extract at a dose of 0.5 g kg−1 of feed; and experimental group 2 (E2) was fed the standard diet supplemented with 1 g kg−1 of L. alpinum extract. The doses were selected based on a comprehensive literature review of phytobiotic supplementation studies conducted on broiler chickens, with particular focus on plant extracts and powders sharing similar bioactive compounds with L. alpinum4,21–23. The L. alpinum extract was purchased from Creative BioMart Inc. (USA) and was produced using a standardized ratio extract manufacturing process. This procedure began with pretreatment, including the selection, cleaning, and crushing of the raw material. Following extraction, the mixture was filtered to obtain a stock solution, which was then concentrated and subjected to spray drying. The final product was smashed to ensure a uniform consistency and underwent a detection phase to confirm quality before packaging. The supplement was administered in powdered form, manually mixed with feed once a day throughout the entire rearing period, starting from day one.

Birds were reared according to Ross 308 standards with ad libitum access to feed and water. The feed was produced in a commercial feed mill (Złotów Mieszalnia Pasz, Poland) operating under ISO 9001 and HACCP standards. To ensure diet uniformity, automated mixing systems were employed, maintaining a coefficient of variation (CV) below 5%. Table 2 presents information declared by the manufacturer of the feeds—analytical constituents, nutritional additives, and trace element mixtures. The diets provided to the broilers were isocaloric and isoprotein, with nutrient content and energy levels (12.50–13.50 MJ kg−1) formulated in accordance with the nutritional standards for broiler chickens24. The experiment was treated as a routine production cycle in small-scale conditions. The stocking density was 12 birds per m2 pen, corresponding to approximately 33 kg m−2 at the end of the experiment. The pens were made of stainless steel, and each was equipped with two nipple drinkers. Broilers were kept on wheat straw litter. The birds were maintained under controlled thermal conditions: 30 °C from day 1, 27 °C from day 7, and 21 °C from day 21 until the end of the rearing period. Relative air humidity was maintained between 60–65%. The birds were exposed to light 24 h a day for the first 7 days, then 18 h of light and 6 h of darkness until the end of rearing.

On day 42 of rearing, 36 broilers (12 per treatment, 2–3 birds per replicate, mean body weight approx. 2.6 kg) were selected for slaughter. Birds were subjected to an 8-h feed withdrawal period before slaughter and transported to an adjacent sampling room in transport crates. Birds were euthanized by manual percussive stunning followed by decapitation (at the level of the first cervical vertebra and the occipital condyle), a procedure applicable to poultry weighing up to 5 kg. All procedures were performed by qualified personnel (certified by the Polish Society for Laboratory Animal Science, PolLASA) in strict accordance with Council Regulation (EC) No. 1099/2009 regarding the protection of animals at the time of killing. The study was conducted in compliance with the European Union Directive 2010/63/EU and the Polish Act on the Protection of Animals Used for Scientific or Educational Purposes (Journal of Laws 2015, item 266). Since the experimental procedures (involving dietary supplementation with a non-toxic plant extract) were non-invasive and did not exceed the threshold of pain, distress, or lasting harm, formal approval from the Local Ethical Committee for Animal Experiments was not required under current national legislation. The study adhered to animal welfare standards monitored by the local Animal Welfare Team and is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Blood analyses

A total of 36 birds (12 per treatment group) were slaughtered for blood collection. From each individual bird, blood was collected and distributed into three separate test tubes (2–3 mL each): sterile glass tubes without anticoagulant for biochemical analysis, tubes with 3.8% sodium citrate for coagulation screening tests, and tubes containing EDTA for hematological analysis.

Biochemical blood analyses

The collected blood samples were transported to the laboratory at a temperature 18–25 °C. After clot formation, the samples were centrifuged to separate the serum for 15 min at a speed of 3500 rpm at temperature 4 °C in a centrifuge (MPW-260R). Serum was stored at 4 °C and biochemical parameters analyses were performed within 24 h. The concentration of urea (UREA), total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), globulin (GLOB), total cholesterol (TCH), triglicerides (TG), high-density lipoproteins (HDL), low-density lipoproteins (LDL), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), Ca, and P in the serum were determined using a MINDRAY BS-120 biochemical analyser and reference reagents from CORMAY DIAGNOSTICS® (Warsaw, Poland). The analytical principle of the Mindray BS-120 is based on automated spectrophotometric measurements of biochemical reactions within the 340–670 nm range, including endpoint and kinetic enzymatic methods, used to determine the concentration or activity of analytes in blood samples. The analytical procedures were verified using a multicalibrator (Levels 1 and 2), as well as control plasma at normal (serum HN) and high (serum HP) levels of analytes (CORMAY DIAGNOSTICS®, Warsaw, Poland).

Blood coagulation screening tests

Blood were gently mixed with anticoagulant and centrifuged for 10 min at 1.500 ÷ 2.000 g at room temperature of 18–25 °C. The obtained plasma was transported to the laboratory at a temperature of 18–25 °C, where screening tests were performed to assess the coagulation system, i.e. prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), thrombin time (TT) and fibrinogen concentration (FIB). Due to the species-specific nature of the coagulation cascade, all analyses were performed using a heterologous assay system. Commercial diagnostic reagents based on mammalian-derived tissue factor and thrombin were applied to the avian plasma samples. This approach is a standard practice in avian coagulation studies, given the current lack of commercially available homologous (species-specific) testing kits for poultry.

The tests were performed using the Diagon Coag 4D Coagulometer based on specialist methods dedicated to this equipment and reference reagents from KSELMED (Grudziądz, Poland).

Hematological blood tests

The collected blood samples were transported to the laboratory at a temperature of 18–25 °C. The following were determined: red blood cell (RBC) and white blood cell (WBC) counts, hematocrit (Ht), and hemoglobin (Hb) concentration, as well as the percentage of each type of white blood cell. RBC and WBC counts were measured using a hemocytometer by mixing a certain amount of the blood sample with a diluent appropriate for avian blood cells. A drop of the diluted blood was then used to fill a Bürker chamber, and after 2 to 3 min, it was placed on the microscope stage and observed under a 40 × objective. Hematocrit was determined using heparinized capillary tubes and a microhematocrit centrifuge. After filling with blood, the capillaries were centrifuged (12,000 rpm for 3 min), and then Ht was expressed as the percentage of the length of the portion of the capillary occupied by RBCs relative to the total length of the capillary occupied by both the cellular fraction and plasma. Hemoglobin concentration was determined by the colorimetric method using an Epoll-20 biochemical analyzer. 0.02 ml of whole blood was added to 5 ml of Drabkin’s reagent and mixed thoroughly. After 5 min, the absorbance of the solution was measured at a wavelength of 540 nm, and the Hb content [g dl−1] was calculated from the following relationship: absorbance × 36.8. Relative quantification of hepatic gene expression

Targeted transcriptomic analysis was conducted in liver tissue collected from broiler chickens during dissection (n = 8/group). This sample size was selected due to the resource-intensive nature of qPCR and RNA quality requirements, which is consistent with sample sizes reported in similar avian transcriptomic studies and provides adequate statistical power for detecting biologically relevant expression differences. Samples of liver tissue were preserved in FixRNA solution (EURx, Gdańsk, Poland). Total RNA was isolated using a column-based Universal RNA Purification Kit (EURx, Gdańsk, Poland). The quantity and quality of RNA were assessed spectrophotometrically using a NanoDrop™ 2000 UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis was performed with the smART First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (EURx, Gdańsk, Poland) using oligo(dT) primers and 2 µg RNA per reaction. The resulting cDNA was diluted to a working concentration of 25 ng µL−1. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using 50 ng of cDNA per reaction with the SG qPCR Master Mix (EURx, Gdańsk, Poland) and gene-specific primers designed with the Primer-BLAST tool (NCBI, Bethesda, MD, USA), which are listed in Table 3. Eight target genes were analyzed: fatty acid synthase (FASN), heme oxygenase 1 (HMOX1), nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NFE2L2), NAD(P)H quinone dehydrogenase 1 (NQO1), nuclear respiratory factor 1 (NRF1), peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARA), sirtuin 1 (SIRT1), and UDP-glucuronosyltransferase family 1 member A1 (UGT1A1). The annealing temperature was set at 58 °C for all primer pairs. At the end of each amplification reaction, a melting curve analysis was conducted to confirm amplicon specificity.

Histological analysis of the liver

For histological analyses, liver samples (n = 12/group) were collected immediately after slaughter, immersed in Bouin’s fixative at room temperature of 18–25 °C and transported to the histological laboratory. Samples were fixed for 48 h. Then, samples were dehydrated in alcohol, xylene-translucent, and paraffin-embedded in a Microm STP 120 tissue processor (Thermo Shandon, Chadwick Road, Astmoor, Runcorn, Cheshire, UK). Samples were embedded in paraffin blocks using a TES 99 embedding machine (Medite, Burgdorf, Germany) and then sectioned on a Finesse ME + rotary microtome (Thermo Shandon, Chadwick Road, Astmoor, Runcorn, Cheshire, UK) at a thickness of 7 µm. The slides were then stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H + E). A UB203i microscope equipped with a ToupCam™ digital camera was used for image acquisition, and Multiscan 18.03 software (Computer Scanning Systems II, Warsaw, Poland) was used for analysis. The liver was analyzed for histopathological lesions, including congestion, fibrosis, necrosis, presence of vacuoles, fat vacuolization, and parenchymal eclipse. The assessment of histopathological lesions involved the analysis of 10 randomly selected images at 400 × magnification. A total of 120 images were evaluated per treatment. The intensity of histopathological lesions was determined using a 5-point scale (0–4), where: 0 indicated no changes, 1 indicated mild histopathological lesions in less than 25% of fields, 2 indicated mild histopathological lesions in less than 50% of fields, 3 indicated moderate histopathological lesions in less than 75% of fields, and 4 indicated severe histopathological lesions in more than 75% of fields.

Statistical analysis

The obtained results were statistically analyzed using two software packages, depending on the type of analysis. Biochemical, hematological, and histological data were processed using Statistica 13.3 (TIBCO Software Inc., Poland), while gene expression data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). For each group, the arithmetic mean and standard deviation were calculated. Variance homogeneity was verified using Levene’s test. Normality of distribution for each group was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Statistical comparisons in the case of liver histology, biochemical and hematological blood analyses were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for intergroup differences, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Since the assumptions for parametric testing were not met in the case of coagulation analyses and gene expression, a nonparametric one-way analysis of variance (Kruskal–Wallis test) was used, followed by the median test for blood coagulation parameters and Dunn’s post hoc test for gene expression. For gene expression analysis, Dunn’s test was performed without multiple testing correction following the protected testing strategy, where post hoc tests conducted only after significant omnibus results (Kruskal–Wallis p < 0.05) inherently control family-wise error rate at α = 0.0527. Outliers in the case of gene expression were identified using the ROUT method (Q = 1%). All analyses were performed on normalized ΔCt data using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results and discussion

Blood biochemistry analyses

Reduction in ALT levels following phytobiotic supplementation indicates a positive effect on liver health28. The lowest plasma activities of ALT and AST observed in birds receiving diets supplemented with the phyto-additive suggest that the treatment did not adversely affect hepatic enzyme activity. Furthermore, these findings imply a lack of hepatotoxic or nephrotoxic effects, indicating the phyto-additive’s overall safety with respect to liver and kidney function29. Our results confirmed the significant effect of the addition of L. alpinum on the level of ALT and AST, which was significantly lower in group E2 (p < 0.001) (Table 4). The ALT level was significantly higher in group C than in experimental groups E1 and E2 (p < 0.001). The AST level was 631.74 U L−1, 607.16 U L−1, and 360.27 U L−1 in groups C, E1, and E2, respectively (p < 0.001). These values differed significantly between group C and group E1. Similar results were obtained by Odetola et al.30, who showed that gradual supplementation of the diet with Petiveria alliacea root meal in broiler chickens significantly reduced AST activity. Oloruntola et al.31 found that the inclusion of papaya and bamboo leaf meal in the diet significantly reduced ALT activity in broiler chickens.

Serum protein concentrations in avian species are reliable indicators of health status, and previous studies show that phytobiotics can improve poultry growth performance. This enhanced growth, associated with increased erythropoiesis and hemoglobin synthesis, may stimulate globulin production and alter serum protein profiles in developing chickens32. The results of our studies confirmed the significant effect of L. alpinum on UREA in the serum of chickens. A significant difference in the level of UREA was found between group C (3.20 mg L−1) and group E2 (2.56 mg L−1) (p = 0.023).

Similar to various plant-derived compounds, phytobiotics exhibit cholesterol-lowering effects in broilers17. Plants contain volatile oils capable of inhibiting the activity of 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase (HMG-CoA reductase), a key hepatic enzyme involved in the regulation of cholesterol biosynthesis, thereby contributing to a reduction in serum cholesterol levels33. Our study showed that the TCH level was significantly lower in the blood serum collected from chickens fed feed with the addition of 1 g of L. alpinum extract per 1 kg of feed (118.67 mg L−1) compared to the control group (150.20 mg L−1) (p = 0.001). A similar decreasing trend was observed for TG levels. The values between group C and group E1 differed significantly (p = 0.014). The hypolipidemic effect of L. alpinum, associated with the presence of phenolic compounds influencing cholesterol biosynthesis, is consistent with the action of other phytobiotics that improve lipid metabolism in broilers without inducing cytotoxic effects34. Gilani et al.35 confirmed that serum TCH and TG were reduced as a result of licorice (Glycyrrhiza glabra), green tea (Camellia sinensis), and peppermint (Mentha piperita) supplementation. They also found that the inclusion of phytobiotics in the diet increased serum HDL levels, which contrasts with our results. In our study, no statistically significant differences were found for HDL and LDL, although a numerically lower trend was observed. Similarly, Khatun et al.36 demonstrated that the inclusion of natural extracts of the Macleaya cordata plant in the broiler diet had no significant effect on TCH, TG, and LDL content.

Blood coagulation

Analysis of screening parameters conducted in broiler chicken plasma to assess the blood coagulation system and health status, i.e., prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), thrombin time (TT), and fibrinogen concentration (FIB), did not show any deviations or abnormalities in the tested range. The most common pathologies observed in poultry production are fatty liver, hemorrhagic syndrome, and infarction, which can lead to disorders of blood coagulation mechanisms19. In this study, all individuals were healthy, and no homeostasis disorders were found. The most favorable profile of the blood coagulation system was obtained in the research groups with the admixture of L. alpinum (E1). For most parameters (except APTT), these results were the highest in this group and differed statistically significantly from group C and the research group E2 for FIB (p = 0.038), TT (p = 0.041), and PT (p = 0.013), respectively. This may indicate a mild modulation of the final phase of blood coagulation. In the case of APTT, there was no effect of alpine edelweiss between the research groups (Table 5). The differentiation between the groups may result from the documented antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties of leontopodic acid, i.e., a derivative of hexaric acid extracted from L. alpinum. Saleh29 obtained similar results in studies using nigella seed oil. The use of herbal mixtures in poultry nutrition, which are a wealth of phenolic compounds with beneficial antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial properties, helps maintain the good health condition of animals. In general, the use of plant extracts with a high total concentration of polyphenols helps to strengthen the immune system and reduces losses in poultry production37–39.

In this context, as there is a lack of specific data in the literature on the direct effect of edelweiss extract in the diet on the time and process of blood clotting, it is essential to expand research towards determining prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), thrombin time (TT), fibrinogen concentration (FIB) and selected blood clotting factors. This is crucial for detecting potential haemostatic disorders, as well as broadly understood diagnostics conducted to monitor the health status of poultry.

Hematological blood parameters

Statistical analysis showed that supplementing the diet with L. alpinum had a beneficial effect on selected hematological parameters in broiler chickens (Table 7). Regarding erythrocyte counts, only the addition of L. alpinum in an amount of 1 g resulted in a significant increase in this parameter. The lowest RBC count was recorded in group C, and the highest in group E2 (p = 0.007). The obtained values were confirmed in the studies of other authors40–42.

Compared to these reports, the hemoglobin content determined in the blood of the studied broilers was significantly higher. The function of hemoglobin is to transport oxygen from the lungs to cells and tissues to oxidize ingested food and produce ATP, as well as to transport carbon dioxide from tissues to the lungs42. Therefore, an increased Hb level may suggest improved O₂ transport capacity and more efficient CO₂ excretion41. In the present experiment, hemoglobin content was clearly dependent on the composition of the feed fed to broilers—it increased with the amount of L. alpinum added to it. The blood serum of chickens consuming 1 g of L. alpinum with the feed (group E2) was characterized by the highest Hb level, while the serum of birds from groups C and E1 had the lowest (p = 0.037). Hematocrit was consistent across all samples, and the values obtained corresponded with the results published by Pantaya and Utami43 examining the hematological profile of laying hens. A consistent hematocrit with increasing RBC counts may suggest changes in erythrocyte dimensions.

Adding 0.5 g and 1 g kg−1 of L. alpinum to the feed increased the WBC count compared to group C (p = 0.001). The increased WBC count reflects both humoral and cellular responses to the pathogen. The most common type of WBCs were lymphocytes—cells responsible for recognizing and destroying pathogens, producing antibodies, and maintaining immune balance. The percentage of lymphocytes in the blood of the studied birds ranged from 75 to 83%, which is slightly higher than that reported by Oravan and Aengwanich44. Another leukocyte fraction found in significant numbers in the avian blood was heterophils. These phagocytic cells are produced extensively not only during inflammation and in response to long-term bacterial and fungal infections but also during dietary or stress-related disturbances45. Statistical analysis showed that supplementing the feed with L. alpinum had a significant effect on neutrophil levels (p = 0.022). The average heterophil content in the blood of the studied birds ranged from 8.29 to 19.33%, while Oravan and Aengwanich44 found it to be 15.58%. In this study, the blood of broiler chickens also contained monocytes, which participate in immune processes related to phagocytosis by capturing and absorbing particles, including foreign antigens and harmful microorganisms46. Supplementing the feed with L. alpinum resulted in a statistically significant reduction in the content of monocytes between group C and group E2 (p = 0.039).

Supplementation with L. alpinum significantly reduced ALT, AST, urea, total cholesterol (TCH), and triglyceride (TG) levels in broiler chickens, indicating improved liver function and metabolic status, as well as the absence of hepatotoxic or nephrotoxic effects. Furthermore, the dietary inclusion of L. alpinum enhanced hematological parameters, including red blood cell (RBC) counts and hemoglobin (Hb) levels, and increased white blood cell (WBC) counts. These findings suggest improved oxygen transport capacity and an enhanced immune response in the treated birds.

Dose-dependent modulation of hepatic transcripts by L. alpinum supplementation

Targeted transcriptomic analysis of liver tissue revealed significant, dose-dependent differences in the relative expression of all analyzed genes between experimental and control groups (Fig. 1). Expression of fatty acid synthase (FASN) was significantly lower in both E1 (0.5 g kg−1 L. alpinum) and E2 (1 g kg−1 L. alpinum) compared to the control (C vs. E1, p = 0.018; C vs. E2, p = 0.007). Heme oxygenase 1 (HMOX1) decreased significantly in the experimental groups relative to the control (p = 0.003), while similar reductions were observed for nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NFE2L2) (C vs. E2, p = 0.021) and NAD(P)H quinone dehydrogenase 1 (NQO1) (C vs. E2, p = 0.003). Expression of nuclear respiratory factor 1 (NRF1) was reduced in E2 compared to C (p = 0.002) and was also lower in E2 than in E1 (p = 0.033). Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARA) was significantly lower in E2 compared to both C (p = 0.002) and E1 (p = 0.009). Similarly, UDP-glucuronosyltransferase family 1 member A1 (UGT1A1) (p = 0.001) was significantly downregulated in E2 compared to C. Across all genes, the highest expression was consistently recorded in the control group, while the lowest values were observed in the high-dose (E2 group).

The observed downregulation of NFE2L2 and associated genes is consistent with a potential reduction in transcriptional demand when exogenous polyphenols contribute to antioxidant capacity. Under basal conditions, Nrf2 is constitutively degraded by Keap1-mediated ubiquitination, and its activation occurs only when reactive oxygen species modify cysteine residues on Keap1, allowing Nrf2 stabilization and nuclear translocation47. When dietary polyphenols effectively scavenge ROS and suppress cellular oxidative stress, the stimulus for Nrf2 stabilization is diminished, resulting in lower baseline transcription of antioxidant response element (ARE)-regulated genes47,48. However, direct oxidative stress markers (e.g., MDA, SOD, CAT, GPx) were not measured in this study to confirm this relationship. This pattern aligns with functional efficiency rather than impairment, as evidenced by preserved liver histology (Table 8), improved hepatocyte integrity (reduced ALT and AST, p < 0.001), and the absence of oxidative tissue damage.

The concurrent FASN downregulation is consistent with reported effects of dietary flavonoids on lipogenesis. Luteolin, a flavonoid structurally related to compounds in L. alpinum, suppresses FASN expression post-transcriptionally and inhibits fatty acid synthesis 49. This metabolic shift is functionally validated by reduced serum triglycerides (p = 0.014) and total cholesterol (p = 0.001) in group E2 (Table 4), indicating improved lipid homeostasis without hepatic steatosis. The coordinated reductions in PPARA and NRF1 transcripts likely reflect diminished hepatic metabolic burden when exogenous bioactive compounds perform regulatory functions, decreasing the biological necessity for high-level endogenous gene expression.

Importantly, this downregulation may represents reduced transcriptional demand rather than pathological suppression. Direct measurement of protein expression and enzymatic activities would be required to confirm whether these transcriptional changes translate into corresponding functional alterations.

Liver histomorphology

Histological analysis of the liver (Table 8, Fig. 2) revealed preserved lobular architecture and normal hepatocyte morphology across all experimental groups, suggesting high systemic tolerance to L. alpinum extract supplementation. The absence of statistically significant histopathological lesions, such as congestion, necrotic foci, or inflammatory infiltration, is a key finding when contrasted with the significant downregulation of genes related to lipid metabolism (FASN, PPARA) and the antioxidant response (NFE2L2, HMOX1).

Our findings align with, yet expand upon, reports regarding other phytobiotics. For instance, dandelion leaves (Taraxacum officinale) have been shown to exert hepatoprotective effects in broilers by improving tissue regeneration and reducing lymphocytic infiltration 50. Recent studies by Mao et al. 23 further emphasize the role of the fermented form of this plant in enhancing growth performance and immune function while maintaining full tissue integrity. Similar protective trends were observed with fenugreek seeds (Trigonella foenum-graecum) both under oxidative stress 21 and standard rearing conditions, where fenugreek improved the overall health status of the birds 51. Furthermore, milk thistle (Silybum marianum) and oregano (Origanum vulgare) have been shown to enhance liver histomorphology 52, while sea buckthorn (Hippophae rhamnoides) berries exert significant hepatoprotective effects by reducing necrosis and steatosis (fatty formation) in the livers of chickens during induced aflatoxin B1 poisoning 53.

The integration of these multi-level data suggests that the observed reduction in gene expression was not a result of toxic inhibition but rather represented a beneficial adaptive physiological response. This phenomenon likely stems from the high bioavailability of exogenous antioxidants provided by L. alpinum (e.g., leontopodic acids), which effectively scavenge free radicals and modulate metabolic pathways, thereby reducing the biological demand for the activation of endogenous defense systems. This hypothesis is strongly supported by the simultaneous decrease in serum ALT and AST activities (p < 0.001), indicating enhanced hepatocyte membrane stability and superior overall organ health. Similar observations regarding the improvement of the hepatic enzyme profile were reported by Tabrizian et al. 51, who demonstrated that fenugreek (Trigonella foenum-graecum L.) supplementation in Ross 308 broiler chickens significantly reduced ALT and AST activities, supporting organ function without inducing histopathological changes.

Furthermore, the downregulation of the FASN pathway correlates directly with the lower serum triglycerides and total cholesterol levels observed in the E2 group (1 g kg1), suggesting a more efficient regulation of lipid metabolism that does not burden the liver parenchyma. Consistent results were obtained by Mao et al. 22 when investigating the effect of fermented dandelion (Taraxacum officinale) on broilers. These authors indicated that this additive improves metabolic parameters and may reduce fat deposition (evidenced by a decrease in abdominal fat yield) without negatively affecting the histological structure of internal organs, which confirms the safety of using phytobiotics in the modulation of lipid metabolism.

While most conventional medicinal plants act by mitigating existing damage or stimulating regeneration, L. alpinum appears to act via “preventive” metabolic optimization, thereby maintaining cellular homeostasis.

Study limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, we did not measure direct oxidative stress markers (MDA, SOD, CAT, GPx), limiting our ability to directly confirm that transcriptional changes reflect reduced oxidative burden. However, this interpretation is supported by preserved hepatic histology and improved serum transaminase levels (ALT and AST, p < 0.001).

Second, mRNA levels do not always correlate with protein abundance or enzymatic activity due to post-transcriptional regulation. Protein expression analysis (Western blotting, immunohistochemistry) and enzymatic activity assays would be required to confirm functional consequences of observed transcriptional changes.

Third, our findings represent associations rather than causal relationships. Mechanistic studies using pathway-specific inhibitors or cell culture models would be necessary to establish causality between polyphenol exposure and gene downregulation.

Finally, this study was conducted under standard rearing conditions. Future research should evaluate L. alpinum effects under challenge conditions (heat stress, pathogen exposure) to determine whether hepatic gene expression patterns and protective effects are maintained under stress.

Summary

The results unequivocally indicate that this extract possesses health-promoting properties, manifested by an improved biochemical profile and the optimization of hepatic metabolic processes. Hematological analysis revealed an increase in red blood cell (RBC) counts and hemoglobin (Hb) levels. Interestingly, these changes occurred alongside a simultaneous reduction in blood viscosity, suggesting more efficient transport of oxygen and nutrients throughout the birds’ systems.

Particular attention was paid to serum biochemical parameters, where supplementation led to a significant reduction in the activity of liver enzymes, such as ALT and AST, providing direct evidence of the extract’s hepatoprotective effect. Simultaneously, a decrease in TCH and TG levels was observed, indicating a regulatory influence of L. alpinum on lipid metabolism.

At the molecular level, the study revealed a significant, dose-dependent downregulation of target genes: FASN, HMOX1, NFE2L2, NQO1, NRF1, PPARA, and UGT1A1. This phenomenon is interpreted as a reduction in the transcriptional demand for the activation of endogenous antioxidant systems and regulatory pathways of lipid and xenobiotic metabolism, resulting from the potent support provided by exogenous bioactive compounds contained in the extract. These beneficial molecular and biochemical changes corresponded with the microscopic images of the liver, in which no statistically significant histopathological lesions were observed, confirming the hepatoprotective effect and safety of L. alpinum extract supplementation in poultry nutrition.

In conclusion, L. alpinum extract at a dose of 1 g kg−1 (group E2) constitutes an effective feed additive that strengthens the antioxidant barrier and metabolic performance of broiler chickens, while showing no adverse effects on tissue histopathology or the hemostatic system.
