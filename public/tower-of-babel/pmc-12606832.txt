Associations of dental students’ professionalism and emotional intelligence with patients’ professionalism perceptions and treatment satisfaction at the University of Science and Technology, Sudan: a cross-sectional study 2021–2022
Authors: Hiba Mohamed Hassan Mohamed Ali, Mohamed Isam Mirghani Hamza, Basil Amir AbdElmoniem Osman, Raouf Wahab Ali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProfessionalism and emotional intelligence (EI) are essential for dental students and practitioners to develop and maintain competence in dentistry. Students with higher levels of professionalism and EI tend to perform better academically and provide higher-quality patient care. The present study aimed to determine dental students’ self-perceived levels of professionalism and EI as well as the associations of these skills with patients’ perceptions of students’ professionalism and treatment satisfaction at the University of Science and Technology (UST).MethodsUtilizing a cross-sectional design, data were collected from 119 randomly selected dental students and 111 patients attending the UST dental hospital. The questionnaires were distributed among the students and patients as follows: the students’ Dundee Poly-professionalism Inventory I (30 statements), the students’ Emotional Intelligence Competence Inventory (20 statements), the patients’ Professionalism Perception Questionnaire (22 statements), and the Patients’ Satisfaction Questionnaire (10 statements). The variables’ scores are presented as the means and standard deviations (SDs). Variables were thereafter dichotomized and presented as frequencies and percentages. Logistic regressions were performed to explore the possible associations of students’ professionalism and EI with patients’ professionalism perceptions and treatment satisfaction.ResultsA total of 68.1% of the students had high professionalism scores, and 69% had high EI scores. Additionally, 68.1% of patients reported a high perception of students’ professionalism, and 67.6% had high treatment satisfaction. Logistic regression analysis revealed that higher students’ EI was significantly associated with higher patients’ professionalism perceptions (odds ratio = 3 and p = 0.029). Although the association was not statistically significant, higher levels of students’ professionalism corresponded with a greater likelihood of higher patient perceptions of professionalism (OR = 1.8, p = 0.186).ConclusionsThe study shows that emotional intelligence plays a key role in how patients view students’ professionalism. Training students to improve their emotional intelligence may lead to better patient interactions and greater patient satisfaction.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Professionalism, Emotional intelligence, Patient satisfaction, Dental students

Introduction

Professionalism comprises the conduct, values, and attributes that define a professional or profession [1]. It reflects the behaviors expected of individuals in any field and includes actions of sound judgment, ethical practice, and accountability. Professionalism involves applying advanced knowledge and skills for the benefit of others and placing service above self-interest [1]. Medical professionalism reflects the clinician’s readiness to meet the needs of patients and fosters a relationship of trust and support between the clinician and their patients [2].

Professionalism encompasses the ability to gain comprehensive clinical competence alongside essential qualities such as empathy, altruism, and accountability, and the ability to communicate effectively, traits that are essential for high-quality, patient-centered care [2]. Structured training and early clinical exposure have been shown to foster self-confidence, ethical decision-making, and trustworthiness in healthcare professionals [3, 4]. Recent studies have emphasized the evolving and culturally sensitive nature of professionalism, advocating for educational strategies that adapt to real-world clinical and social contexts [5, 6].

One key factor influencing professionalism is emotional intelligence (EI), which is defined as “a type of social intelligence that involves the ability to monitor one’s own and others’ emotions, to discriminate among them, and to use this information to guide one’s thinking and actions” [2]. Within healthcare settings, emotional intelligence has been adapted to include skills that enable clinicians to manage emotions effectively, fostering empathy, motivation, and social competence [7, 8]. Moreover, emotional intelligence supports clinicians in navigating stress, collaborating in teams, and promoting patient engagement [7, 9–12].

Evidence indicates that dental and medical students with higher EI demonstrate stronger adaptability, communication, and patient-centered behaviors, particularly in culturally diverse environments [8, 9]. These emotional competencies not only improve academic and clinical performance but also directly impact patient satisfaction, an essential outcome in modern healthcare systems [13–15].

While many international studies have explored these constructs, professionalism and EI are inherently shaped by local educational systems, cultural values, and institutional practices [16]. For example, research from Finland, Malaysia, and other regions illustrates that student attitudes toward professionalism are influenced by faculty expectations, digital interactions, and clinical role modeling [17, 18]. These findings reinforce the importance of context-specific investigations into how professionalism and EI are taught and perceived.

In dentistry, professionalism has evolved beyond norms and behaviors to include broader competencies. The American Dental Education Association (ADEA) defined six core values: competence, fairness, integrity, responsibility, respect, and service, which form the foundation of dental curricula and the training of both undergraduate and postgraduate dental students [19]. Several dental researches linked higher professionalism and emotional intelligence with improved academic performance [20, 21], while others indicated that patient satisfaction is positively associated with professionalism and EI attributes, such as optimism, empathy, and communication skills, underscoring the need to integrate these elements into dental curricula [22, 23].

Despite global emphasis, there is limited research on how these competencies are perceived and practiced in Sudan. Nevertheless, professionalism and emotional intelligence are increasingly recognized as integral to medical and dental education in the country [24]. Locally, professionalism is integrated into postgraduate programs through dedicated modules, while undergraduate dental teaching tends to rely on hidden curricula and informal role modeling. Although professionalism is acknowledged in curricula, structured training and standardized assessment of emotional intelligence remain largely absent at both levels. Much of the teaching is informal, which may result in inconsistent student understanding. Moreover, little is known about how students’ self-perceived professionalism and EI align with patients’ evaluations; an important gap in resource-constrained educational environments like Sudan. To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies in Sudan to explore this relationship, providing context-specific evidence that can inform curricular improvements and professional development strategies [24, 25].

This study addresses this gap by exploring self-perceived levels of professionalism and emotional intelligence among fourth- and fifth-year dental students during their clinical years. It also investigates how these attributes relate to patients’ perceptions of professionalism and satisfaction with dental treatment. By focusing on students at the University of Science and Technology (UST) Dental Hospital in Sudan, this research contributes valuable context-specific insights that can inform future educational interventions and policy reforms in dental education.

Methods

Study design, setting and population

A cross-sectional analytical study was conducted at the Faculty of Dentistry, Dental Teaching Hospital, University of Science and Technology (UST), which is located in Omdurman, Sudan. The hospital, situated in the Karari locality, provides dental treatment services to approximately 2,000 patients monthly and includes five departments: conservative dentistry, pediatric dentistry, prosthodontics, orthodontics, and oral and maxillofacial surgery. It serves as a community-focused institution, promoting health awareness and providing preventive and treatment services. The study population comprised fourth- and fifth-year dental students, totaling 79 and 88 students, respectively, and patients attending the periodontics, surgery, prosthodontics, and conservative dentistry clinics.

Sample size and sampling techniques

Sample sizes were calculated via the sampling equation for the population proportion at a 95% confidence level, 80% power, and a design effect of 1. For students, the estimated proportion of high professionalism was set at 50% (“good” professionalism is defined as achieving ≥ 50% of the maximum possible score), resulting in a required sample size of 119. For patients, an 80% perception of professionalism was assumed, yielding a sample size of 120. Logistic regression analysis with an odds ratio of 1.5 and a significance level of p < 0.05 revealed a minimum sample size of 110 for each group.

Stratified random sampling was used for the students, considering fourth- and fifth-year students as distinct strata. A total of 63 fifth-year students and 55 fourth-year students were randomly selected via the Open-Epi program, corresponding to a sampling fraction of 0.7. Systematic random sampling was employed for patients, with every third patient meeting the inclusion criteria (Patients who attended the UST clinics and had at least one previous treatment session with 4th- and 5th-year students) was invited to participate. The sampling interval was calculated on the basis of an estimated daily patient attendance of 60 over a five-day working week.

The inclusion criteria covered registered fourth- and fifth-year students and patients attending the selected clinics during the study period (October–November 2021). The exclusion criteria included external students not attending lectures or clinics and patients presenting with severe pain or requiring emergency treatment.

Measures

Data were collected via structured and previously validated questionnaires. The Dundee Poly-professionalism Inventory I was adapted to assess students’ professionalism [26]. This anonymous, self-administered questionnaire included 30 culturally relevant statements (culturally sensitive statements to Sudanese culture were removed) with responses categorized as “Is this wrong? “, “Have you ever done this in your present course? “, and “Would you ever do this in your present course?“. Responses were scored dichotomously as ‘‘Yes” or ‘‘No”, and total scores were calculated and presented as the means and standard deviations. Median scores for the “Is this wrong?” sections were used to classify students as having high or low professionalism (those who recognized these unprofessional behaviors were categorized as ‘high professionalism’ according to the median cut-off). Emotional intelligence (EI) was assessed via a questionnaire based on Goleman and Boyatzis’ model, which focused on self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, and relationship management [21]. Similar scoring and categorization methods for professionalism were applied for EI. Median-based cut-offs are commonly used when data are not normally distributed, as they account for subjective variation and reduce the impact of outliers [27]. This method ensures a balanced division of data, facilitating robust group comparisons [28].

Patients’ perceptions of their professionalism [15] and treatment satisfaction were evaluated via an adapted version of the Patient Satisfaction Questionnaire Short Form (PSQ-18) [29]. The instruments were translated into Arabic by a professional bilingual translator and independently back-translated by a second translator blinded to the original version. Discrepancies were resolved through expert review to ensure consistency. The questionnaire included two sections addressing perceptions of professionalism and treatment satisfaction. Responses were scored dichotomously (either agree (scored as 1) or disagree (scored as 0)), with total and median scores calculated to categorize perceptions and satisfaction as high or low. Demographic data, including age, sex, marital status, residence, and education, were also collected. Patients’ questionnaires were translated into Arabic and completed with assistance from clinic assistants. To minimize response bias, clinic assistants (not dental staff or instructors) were assigned to distribute the questionnaires and assist only with reading for patients who had literacy difficulties. They were not involved in clinical instruction or evaluation and received specific instructions not to influence or interpret responses.

The dependent variables included patients’ perceptions of professionalism and treatment satisfaction, which were measured both as continuous and dichotomous variables. The independent variables included students’ professionalism and emotional intelligence, which were similarly assessed as continuous and dichotomous variables.

The questionnaires for the students and patients were adapted from their original versions, and validity was assessed by three experts in the field to ensure their relevance and applicability. Additionally, to account for cultural sensitivity, some statements were omitted from the original tools. Four items from the Dundee Poly-professionalism Inventory were omitted due to their high cultural sensitivity, as determined by expert review. These included: ‘Drinking alcohol over lunch and interviewing a patient in the afternoon,’ ‘Involvement in pedophilic activities - possession/viewing of child pornography images or molesting children’, ‘Providing illegal drugs to fellow students,’ and ‘Sexually harassing a university employee or fellow students.’ This tool has been widely validated and used in several neighboring countries, making it a suitable framework with minor cultural adjustments. The adapted tools were reviewed by three academic experts and piloted with 10 students and 10 patients (10% of the final sample), in line with standard recommendations for pilot testing. While these steps ensured content clarity and cultural appropriateness, further validation in broader Sudanese populations is recommended. Intra-rater reliability tested the consistency of each participant’s responses over time among 10 students from the University (UST), and the same participants were re-interviewed two weeks later. Reliability was measured using the Kappa statistic, with all values exceeding 0.7, indicating acceptable agreement and confirming the questionnaire’s consistency and dependability.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed via SPSS. Continuous variables (The overall scores of students’ professionalism, EI, patients’ perceptions of students’ professionalism and patients’ treatment satisfaction) are reported as the means and standard deviations, whereas categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages (Dichotomous students’ professionalism (High/Low) and emotional intelligence (EI)) (High/Low), patients’ perceptions of students’ professionalism and treatment satisfaction (High/Low)). Binary logistic regression was applied to assess relationships between variables ((Dichotomous variables (students’ professionalism and emotional intelligence (EI)) with Dichotomous variables (patients’ perceptions of students’ professionalism and treatment satisfaction), with odds ratios reported. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In cases where minimal missing data were present, pairwise deletion was applied to retain as much information as possible without introducing bias.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval was obtained from the Sudan Medical Specialization Board and the Research Board of the UST Faculty of Dentistry. Participant confidentiality was ensured, and all the data were anonymized. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained from all participants after providing a detailed explanation of the study’s objectives. The participants retained the right to withdraw at any time without providing reasons.

Results

Sample profile

The study sample included participants from the Faculty of Dentistry at the University of Science and Technology (UST), encompassing fourth- and fifth-year dental students and patients from the university’s dental teaching hospital. The student sample consisted of 119 participants, with 63 fourth-year and 55 fifth-year students. The mean age of the participants was 22.93 ± 1.55 years, ranging from 19-28 years, with 61% females and 39% males.

The patient sample comprised 111 individuals recruited from various departments of the dental teaching hospital (mean age was of 39.67 ± 16.58 years (SD)). Nearly half of the patients were from the Conservation Department, with the remaining patients being evenly distributed between the prosthodontic and periodontic departments (Fig. 1). With respect to occupations, nearly half of the patients were unemployed, approximately one quarter were students, and the remainder were freelancers, professionals (occupations requiring a university degree or specialized higher education), office workers, or skilled laborers (workers who have specialized training or technical skills but may not hold a university degree) (Fig. 2). Approximately half of the participants had a university education (50%), one-third had primary (16.7%) or secondary education (20.6%), and a small percentage were either illiterate (3.9%) or held postgraduate qualifications (7.8%).

Patients’ perceptions of students’ professionalism and treatment satisfaction

Patients from the UST dental teaching hospital completed a 22-item questionnaire on their perceptions of students’ professionalism. Most responses were overwhelmingly positive, exceeding 95% for most items. However, two items had approximately 30% negative responses, indicating lower perceptions of professionalism (Table 4). With respect to treatment satisfaction, patients responded to 10 statements, with more than 90% expressing positive satisfaction for most items. However, two statements received fewer positive responses: “I am usually waiting for a short time at the doctor’s office” and “The doctor who treats me does not make me wonder about the accuracy of their diagnosis” (Fig. 3). The patients’ perception of students’ professionalism scores were 22, with a mean of 20.66 ± 2.25. Using a median score of 20 as a cutoff, perceptions were categorized into high (68.10%) and low (31.90%) groups. Similarly, the treatment satisfaction scores summed out of 10 had a mean of 9.2 ± 1.68. The median score was used to divide patients into low (32.40%)- and high-satisfaction (67.7%) groups.

Both the students’ and patients’ variables were dichotomized (high = 1, low = 0) using the median score as a cutoff.

However, a significant association was found between students’ EI and patients’ professionalism perceptions, with an odds ratio of 3 (p = 0.029).

With respect to treatment satisfaction, logistic regression analysis revealed no significant association between students’ professionalism and patients’ satisfaction (OR = 1). Although not statistically significant, students with high EI had nearly twice the likelihood of achieving high patient satisfaction (OR = 2, Table 5).

Structural equation modeling

Structural equation modeling (SEM) was used to examine the relationship between students’ emotional intelligence (EI), recognition of unprofessional behavior, and patients’ perception of professionalism. The model demonstrated that EI had a small but positive direct effect on patients’ perception of professionalism (β = 0.20). In addition, EI again showed a positive indirect effect on patients’ perceptions through recognition of unprofessional behavior (β = 0.45 × 0.50 = 0.225). The total effect of EI on patients’ perception of professionalism was therefore moderate (β = 0.425). These findings indicate that although EI contributes directly to how patients perceive professionalism, its primary influence operates through students’ recognition of unprofessional behavior, suggesting a partial mediation effect.

Discussion

Professionalism and emotional intelligence in the dental field have been studied by several authors over the last several years. Most studies have focused on creating a unified definition and tool for measuring and conveying the values of professionalism and Emotional Intelligence; however, the interpretation and teaching of these concepts may vary significantly across cultural and institutional contexts. For example, what is considered professional behavior or an emotionally intelligent response in one setting may differ in another due to cultural norms, educational priorities, or societal expectations. These differences underscore the importance of contextual sensitivity when assessing and teaching professionalism and EI among undergraduate students. This will, in turn, help in delivering the best quality of treatment to patients and achieving their satisfaction with the treatment provided by these students.

The current study was conducted with the purpose of measuring the levels of students’ professionalism (through the recognition of unprofessional behaviors) and Emotional Intelligence among dental students at UST, in addition to measuring patients’ perceptions of their professionalism and satisfaction with the treatment they received at the UST dental teaching hospital. Furthermore, we investigated the associations between the levels of students’ professionalism and Emotional Intelligence and patients’ perceptions of professionalism and their satisfaction with the treatment provided by the students at the UST dental teaching hospital. The current findings indicate that students have a reasonably high level of self-perceived professionalism and Emotional Intelligence and that patients report considerably high levels of perceived professionalism and treatment satisfaction. In addition, a statistically significant association was found between patients’ perceptions of professionalism and their level of Emotional Intelligence, with an odds ratio of 3. The elevated levels of self-perceived professionalism and Emotional Intelligence among students may be partially attributed to Sudan’s distinct cultural values and the institutional culture at UST, which likely emphasizes ethical behavior, effective communication, and patient-centered care within clinical training.

As mentioned earlier, in the context of Sudan, postgraduate programs often include mandatory courses on professionalism, providing structured opportunities for students to engage with these principles in a focused manner. In contrast, undergraduate training in the dental faculties typically incorporates professionalism within the hidden curriculum, embedded across various departments and clinical settings. While this allows for informal exposure, it can result in inconsistencies in how professionalism is taught and assessed. Challenges remain in fully integrating professionalism into formal curricula, ensuring that it is consistently addressed throughout all levels of education. Moreover, there is a need for more effective methods of assessment to measure students’ understanding and application of professional values. Identifying the gaps in students’ knowledge of professionalism, especially as they progress through different stages of education, is crucial for enhancing the curriculum and improving learning outcomes.

The importance of professionalism during clinical practice among undergraduate dental students has been stressed by several studies. One study by E. Uma et al. (2017) suggested that students’ professional skills may play a more important role than their cognitive ability, which is necessary for successful clinical and academic performance [30]. One of the challenges faced by many authors in the field of professionalism in dentistry is the lack of a unified and agreed-upon tool to teach and measure professionalism [31]. Therefore, the questionnaire used in the current study was the Dundee Poly-professionalism Inventory 1, which was developed and validated by Roff and her colleagues between 2011 and 2012 after several studies were conducted to identify behaviors and attitudes that represent poor professionalism [26]. In addition, students were recruited from the 4th and 5th years since they are providing definitive treatment to patients attending the UST dental hospital. Studies including early-year students revealed differences in professionalism between preclinical and clinical-year students. For example, studies in Saudi Arabia [32] and the UK have indicated that experience in the clinical environment is a strong influencing factor for developing and understanding professionalism [33].

The current findings indicate that UST students have considerably high levels of self-perceived professionalism (almost 68.1% of students with overall high professionalism). The current level of students’ professionalism was slightly lower than the findings of a study performed in Saudi Arabia in 2020 [32].

However, the students’ responses to some of the statements were noticeably suboptimal. These findings highlight the importance of introducing professional courses and focusing on areas that require attention during students’ training.

On the other hand, the measurement of Emotional Intelligence was included in the current study on the basis of the findings of some previous studies regarding the close link between professionalism and Emotional Intelligence, as well as the importance of EI in helping students gain better academic and clinical competencies, better interpersonal skills, and, most importantly, better patient care [7, 34–36]. A review performed recently in 2022 indicated that Emotional Intelligence has a greater impact on the educational success of dental students and plays an important role in coping with stress and negative emotions [12]. With respect to the choice of questionnaire to measure EI among the students, the questionnaire adopted by Goleman et al. [21] was used, as it focuses on the work setting, which is consistent with the study’s focus on dental students’ performance in professional and clinical settings. The current Emotional Intelligence findings were slightly higher than the findings of Ravi Chandra KS et al. and Muhammad Irfan et al.‘s studies [7, 37], the former of which indicated that only 15% of the students had good EI and that 54% had average Emotional Intelligence scores, whereas the latter reported that 55% had high Emotional Intelligence compared with 69% reported in the current study. This comparison should be interpreted with caution, as the professionalism and EI measurement tools used in the aforementioned studies vary, highlighting the absence of a standardized tool to assess professionalism and Emotional Intelligence among dental students.

The study sample also included patients attending UST clinics and receiving treatment provided by 4th- and 5th-year students. Almost half of the recruited patients were unemployed, as most of them were females and housewives. Around a quarter were students, as university clinics also receive patients who are students from medical campuses and other UST campuses. In addition, almost half of the patients reported having a university level of education, which is explained by the fact that a quarter of the patients were university students, and some of the unemployed housewives reported a university level of education. On the other hand, most of the recruited patients were from the conservation clinic, which is the department that receives most of the patients and follow-up patients, whereas no patients were recruited from the surgery department, where most of the patients were either on their first visit or not followed up.

Patients included in the current study responded to two questionnaires: the patients’ perceptions of dental students’ professionalism questionnaire and the treatment satisfaction questionnaire. The findings regarding patients’ perceptions of dental students’ professionalism indicated that patients responded positively to most of the statements. Several studies have been conducted previously regarding patients’ perceptions of students’ professionalism but have used different tools and different questionnaires [15, 16]. The current findings were in accordance with studies performed in Saudi Arabia (2017) and Sweden (2021) [38, 39], both of which indicated that most of the patients responded positively to the students’ professionalism statements. The latter study stressed the importance of informing patients about their treatment questions and doubts [39]. In addition, a study performed in South Africa in 2019 concluded that the patients’ most important perspectives on professionalism were physical appearance, hygienic practices, good communication skills, and empathy. Patients also completed a treatment satisfaction questionnaire to rate their satisfaction with the treatment provided by the students at the clinic. Most of the patients’ responses to the questionnaire statements were positive, with more than 90%, and almost two-thirds of them (67%) reported high overall treatment satisfaction.

The last part of the study focused on the associations between patients’ professionalism and Emotional Intelligence and patients’ perceptions of their professionalism and treatment satisfaction. The current findings highlight the importance of professionalism and Emotional Intelligence in providing patients with satisfying treatment. The findings of the present study were in accordance with several studies that reported significant associations between students’ EI and patients’ treatment satisfaction. One study concluded that providing educational experiences for dental students related to Emotional Intelligence dimensions may facilitate the development of skills essential for optimal interaction with patients, which will contribute to success in their professional practice [22], whereas the latter concluded that patients’ satisfaction was statistically significantly correlated with two components of Emotional Intelligence (optimism/mood regulation) [23]. Although the association between students’ self-reported professionalism and patients’ perceptions of professionalism was not statistically significant, this finding remains important. It suggests that patients may base their perceptions of professionalism more on observable behaviors and communication styles during clinical encounters than on students’ internal self-assessments of professional values. This highlights the multifaceted nature of professionalism, which is not always fully captured through self-report measures. Consequently, students may believe they are behaving professionally, yet this may not consistently translate into behaviors that patients perceive as professional. These findings align with previous literature that underscores the need for structured, behaviorally-based training and assessment of professionalism within dental education.

The structural equation modeling results even strengthened this connection as it highlights the important role of emotional intelligence (EI) in shaping patients’ perceptions of professionalism. While EI directly influences patients’ professionalism, its stronger effect occurs indirectly through students’ recognition of unprofessional behavior. These findings stress that promoting both EI and awareness of unprofessional conduct in dental training can enhance professional behavior and improve patient trust.

The study limitations include the lack of inclusion of dental students from different dental faculties around Khartoum to provide a broader perspective on the subject and to include all faculties in the recommendations of the current findings. Additionally, while the systematic random sampling method was employed in this study, we acknowledge that uneven patient attendance patterns may have led to some bias in the sample. However, it is important to note that the availability of a comprehensive list of all patients who attended was not feasible during the study period, which made simple random sampling not applicable. Given these constraints, we believe that the systematic random sampling approach was the most practical option available at the time. We recognize the potential for bias due to this limitation and suggest that future studies with more robust attendance tracking systems could consider alternative sampling methods, such as simple random sampling or stratified sampling, to mitigate this issue. In addition, the use of dichotomous scoring and median cut-offs may oversimplify complex constructs like professionalism and emotional intelligence. While suitable for statistical analysis, this approach may not fully reflect the subtleties of these attributes. Future studies could benefit from maintaining continuous scales or using empirically derived categories based on established educational standards. Therefore, the use of SEM was used to further strengthen the findings of the current study.

The data were collected shortly after the COVID-19 pandemic; therefore, matching students with their corresponding patients was difficult because of time constraints for both students and patients to fill out the questionnaires during clinic time and COVID-19 clinic restrictions. However, one of the main strengths of this study was that it emphasized the importance of focusing on students’ clinical training while paying special attention to professionalism to ensure that patients receive the best and most satisfying treatment. Fortunately, a course focusing on professionalism and ethics was recently added to the students’ curriculum at the faculty of dentistry and will be taught during semesters 3 and 4 in 2023. The introduced course included key training modules (e.g., informed consent, patient confidentiality, cultural competence). In addition, training modules such as interactive case-based discussions, role-playing scenarios, and reflective writing exercises were added. Assessment methods including structured observation, formative feedback, and professionalism mini-evaluations integrated into clinical rotations.

Conclusions

Dental students at UST generally demonstrated satisfactory levels of self-assessed professionalism and emotional intelligence. Similarly, patients treated at the UST dental hospital expressed high levels of satisfaction with students’ professionalism and the care they received. A noteworthy finding was the significant relationship between students’ emotional intelligence and how patients perceived their professionalism.

These findings highlight the value of integrating emotional intelligence into dental education to enhance professional conduct and patient interactions. Future research could explore the impact of structured emotional intelligence training and its potential to further improve the quality of patient care and may benefit from the use of a more focused professionalism questionnaire following the recommendation of the systematic review by Li H, Ding N, Zhang Y, Liu Y, Wen D. (2017) [40]. Based on these results, we recommend incorporating targeted EI development modules within the dental curriculum, such as reflective practice sessions, communication skills workshops, and patient-centered care simulations. These interventions can support the development of both interpersonal and professional competencies.
