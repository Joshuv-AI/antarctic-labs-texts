Hardware-Aware Quantum Kernel Design Based on Graph Neural Networks Preprint: APS/123-QED
Yuxiang Liu 1,3,4,† , Fanxu Meng 2,† , Lu Wang 5 , Yi Hu 1,4 , Sixuan Li 5 , Zaichen Zhang 1,3,4,∗ and Xutao Yu 3,4,5,∗

Abstract
Quantum kernel methods have emerged as a promising direction in quantum machine learning (QML), offering a principled way to map classical data into high-dimensional quantum Hilbert spaces. While conceptually powerful, designing effective quantum kernels that adapt to both the target task and the constraints of near-term quantum hardware remains a nontrivial challenge. In this work, we propose HaQGNN, a hardware-aware quantum kernel design framework that integrates quantum device topology, noise characteristics, and graph neural networks (GNNs) to evaluate and select task-relevant quantum circuits. By predicting surrogate metrics related to fidelity and kernel performance, HaQGNN enables efficient circuit screening at scale. Feature selection is further incorporated to improve compatibility with limited-qubit systems and mitigate kernel degradation. Extensive experiments on three benchmark datasets, Credit Card, MNIST-5, and FMNIST-4, demonstrate that HaQGNN outperforms existing quantum kernel baselines in terms of classification accuracy. Our work highlights the potential of learning-based and hardware-aware strategies for advancing practical quantum kernel design on NISQ devices.

\bullet

\bullet

Noise-robust candidate selection:
By filtering out low-fidelity circuits early in the pipeline, HaQGNN effectively mitigates the impact of gate errors and decoherence, leading to more reliable kernel performance on noisy devices.

\bullet

Efficient support for high-dimensional input:
The integration of feature selection techniques (e.g., mRMR) reduces data dimensionality while preserving discriminative information, helping avoid vanishing kernel similarity and enabling practical deployment on NISQ devices.

\bullet

Superior classification accuracy:
Extensive experiments on multiple benchmark tasks (e.g., Credit Card, MNIST-5, FMNIST-4) and across various IBM quantum devices (e.g., Perth, Lagos, Torino) demonstrate that HaQGNN consistently outperforms several state-of-the-art quantum kernel baselines.

II.1
Quantum computation fundamental

A quantum bit (qubit) can exist in a linear superposition of the two computational basis states,
\left|0\right\rangle
and
\left|1\right\rangle
, in contrast to a classical bit that can only be in a definite state of either 0 or 1. The state of a single qubit is generally represented as
\left|\psi\right\rangle=\alpha\left|0\right\rangle+\beta\left|1\right\rangle
, where
\alpha
and
\beta
are complex amplitudes satisfying the normalization condition
\left|\alpha\right|^{2}+\left|\beta\right|^{2}=1
. For a system of multiple qubits, the overall quantum state is represented by the tensor product of individual qubit states, enabling the description of entangled states that cannot be factorized into separate 1-qubit components. Quantum gate, as a unitary matrix, transforms the quantum state to another and preserves the norm of the quantum state. Any unitary can be decomposed into a set of basis gates. The basis gate sets explored in this study are
S=\left\{R_{x},R_{z},CZ,I\right\}
and
S^{\ast}=\left\{R_{z},X,CNOT,I\right\}
, which primarily consist of the 1-qubit gates
I
,
X
,
R_{x}
and
R_{z}
, whose matrix representations are given as follows:

\displaystyle I=\begin{pmatrix}1&&0\\
0&&1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle X=\begin{pmatrix}0&&1\\
1&&0\end{pmatrix}

(1)

\displaystyle R_{x}\left(\theta\right)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}&-i\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\\
-i\sin\frac{\theta}{2}&\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle R_{z}\left(\theta\right)=\begin{pmatrix}e^{-i\frac{\theta}{2}}&0\\
0&e^{i\frac{\theta}{2}}\end{pmatrix}

(2)

In addition, the 2-qubit gates

II.2
Mechanism of quantum kernel

Kernel methods provide a powerful theoretical framework for performing nonlinear and nonparametric learning tasks, owing to their generality and strong interpretability
Lei et al. 2024
. Assume that both the training and test samples are drawn from the same input-output space
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
. The training dataset is denoted as
\mathcal{D}=\left\{\bm{x}_{i},y_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, where
\bm{x}_{i}
represents the input features of the
i
-th sample and
y_{i}
denotes the corresponding label. The fundamental idea of traditional kernel methods is to map input data
\bm{x}_{i}
from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to a higher-dimensional feature space
\mathbb{R}^{q}
, i.e.,
\phi\left(\cdot\right):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{q}
with
q\gg d
, such that data that are not linearly separable in the original space may become linearly separable in the transformed space. However, when
q
is large, explicitly computing the mapped feature vectors
\phi\left(\bm{x}_{i}\right)
becomes computationally expensive. To address this issue, kernel methods leverage the kernel trick, which enables implicit computation by constructing a kernel matrix
K\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, where each element is defined as the inner product of feature vectors in the high-dimensional space:

\left[K\right]_{i,j}=\left[K\right]_{j,i}=\left\langle\phi\left(\bm{x}_{i}\right),\phi\left(\bm{x}_{j}\right)\right\rangle,\forall i,j\in\left[n\right]

(4)

This approach enables efficient similarity computation with a time complexity of
\mathcal{O}\left(d\right)
, without explicitly performing the high-dimensional mapping. The aim of kernel learning is to infer a hypothesis function
h\left(\bm{x}_{i}\right)=\left\langle\bm{w}^{\ast},\phi\left(\bm{x}_{i}\right)\right\rangle
with high test accuracy by utilizing the constructed kernel matrix
K
. The optimal parameter
\bm{w}^{\ast}
is obtained by minimizing the following loss function:

\mathcal{L}=\lambda\left\langle\bm{w},\bm{w}\right\rangle+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\left\langle\bm{w},\phi\left(\bm{x}_{i}\right)\right\rangle\right)^{2}

(5)

The performance of kernel methods heavily depends on the choice of the feature mapping function
\phi\left(\cdot\right)
, or equivalently, the kernel function
\kappa\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)=\left\langle\phi\left(\cdot\right),\phi\left(\cdot\right)\right\rangle
. Various kernel functions have been proposed to accommodate different task scenarios, such as the radial basis function (RBF) kernel, Gaussian kernel, circular kernel, and quantum kernel.

Compared to classical kernel methods, the primary distinction of quantum kernel approaches lies in the implementation of the feature mapping function
\phi\left(\cdot\right)
. For an
N
-qubit quantum kernel model, the feature mapping of a sample
\bm{x}_{i}
is realized through a parameterized quantum circuit
U_{c}\left(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\theta}\right)
applied to the initial quantum state
\left|0\right\rangle^{\otimes N}
, resulting in the quantum state
\left|\varphi\left(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\theta}\right)\right\rangle=U_{c}\left(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\theta}\right)\left|0\right\rangle^{\otimes N}
. Here,
\bm{\theta}
denotes trainable parameters and the corresponding density matrix is denoted as
\rho\left(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\theta}\right)=\left|\varphi\left(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\theta}\right)\right\rangle\left\langle\varphi\left(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\theta}\right)\right|
. The objective of quantum kernel learning is to utilize the quantum kernel matrix
W\left(\bm{x},\bm{\theta}\right)
, defined as

\left[W\left(\bm{x},\bm{\theta}\right)\right]_{i,j}=Tr\left(\rho\left(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\theta}\right)\rho\left(\bm{x}_{j},\bm{\theta}\right)\right),\forall i,j\in\left[n\right]

(6)

to infer a hypothesis as formulated in Eq.
5
, with the goal of achieving higher test accuracy compared to classical kernel methods. Therefore, the performance of a quantum kernel largely depends on the design of the parameterized circuit
U_{c}\left(\cdot,\bm{\theta}\right)
. It is worth noting that the implementation of
U_{c}\left(\cdot,\bm{\theta}\right)
is flexible and can take various forms, such as circuits constructed with predefined architectures, hardware efficient circuits, or circuits randomly generated from a specified quantum gate set.

II.3
Graph neural networks

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have become a powerful framework for learning over graph-structured data, where relationships among entities play a critical role in the underlying data distribution
Corso et al. 2024
. By iteratively aggregating and transforming information from neighboring nodes through message-passing mechanisms, GNNs are capable of capturing both local topology and global structural dependencies
Khemani et al. 2024
. This capacity to model complex relational patterns has led to their widespread adoption across a variety of domains, including social network analysis
Zhang 2025
, molecular property prediction
Wieder et al. 2020
;
Chen et al. 2023
;
Shui and Karypis 2020
, and recommender systems
Wu et al. 2022
;
Gao et al. 2022
. Notably, variants such as Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs), Graph Attention Networks (GATs), and GraphSAGE have significantly advanced the state-of-the-art in graph representation learning tasks
Mahdi and Shati 2024
;
Yang et al. 2024
;
Guan et al. 2024
;
Xu and Zuo 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024
. In the context of quantum computing, quantum circuits can be naturally represented as graphs, where qubits correspond to nodes and quantum gates or couplings define the edges. This structural analogy provides a compelling motivation for applying GNNs to quantum systems, enabling efficient learning and prediction tasks grounded in the intrinsic connectivity of quantum architectures.

III
Method

In this section, we present the technical details of our proposed hardware-aware quantum kernel design framework. An overview of the framework is illustrated in Fig.
1
, which consists of four main steps:

Step 1:
Based on the hardware topology and noise characteristics of the quantum device, a set of qubits is selected such that they exhibit favorable connectivity and minimal susceptibility to noise. A large number of candidate quantum circuits are then generated on the selected qubits, following the supported native gate set and qubit connectivity of the target device.

Step 2:
A graph neural network (GNNs-1) is employed to predict the ”Probability of Successful Trials (PST)”, a metric correlated with circuit fidelity. This step enables the early rejection of low-fidelity candidate circuits, thereby filtering out circuits with potentially higher fidelity.

Step 3:
A second graph neural network (GNNs-2) is designed to estimate the kernel-target alignment (KTA), a surrogate metric for classification accuracy, over the high-fidelity circuits identified in Step 2. The top-
k
circuits with the highest predicted KTA values are selected for further refinement.

Step 4:
A classical optimizer is employed to train the parameters of the top-
k
high-KTA circuits with the high PST values from scratch, and the circuit with the best task-specific classification accuracy is selected as the final quantum kernel circuit.

We divide the hardware-aware quantum kernel design into three parts: candidate circuit generation (
Step 1
, Section
III.1
), metric prediction based on graph neural networks (
Step 2-3
, Section
III.2
), circuit training and selection (
Step 4
, Section
III.3
).

III.1
Candidate circuit generation

The process of generating candidate quantum circuits is primarily divided into three stages: 1) A specific number of qubits with preserved topological connectivity are selected from the quantum device. This selection process takes into account the noise characteristics of individual qubits and their couplings, aiming to identify a subgraph of the device topology with the lowest average noise. Such the selection strategy improves hardware efficiency and enhances the fidelity of the generated circuits. 2) Candidate circuits are generated based on the selected qubit and the native gate set supported by the quantum device. During the circuit generation process, each inserted quantum gate must act on qubits that are physically connected on the hardware. This ensures that qubit mapping is implicitly achieved during circuit construction, thereby avoiding the insertion of costly SWAP gates during the compilation phase. As a result, the overall circuit depth is reduced, and noise accumulation is mitigated. 3) In the candidate quantum circuits, parameterized quantum gates are selected at different positions to enable flexible data embedding, which helps improve the performance of the quantum kernel.

Due to the non-negligible differences in noise levels across individual qubits and qubit couplings within quantum devices, we perform a selection process to identify a set of

III.1.3
Data embedding in circuits

Inspired by the advantages of randomized data embedding over fixed embedding discussed in
Anagolum et al. 2024
, we adopt a simple strategy for data embedding: in each quantum circuit, a subset of parameterized 1-qubit gates is randomly selected to embed the data, with each selected gate corresponding to one dimension of the input data. To ensure that the entire input can be encoded, the number of embedding gates should be greater than or equal to the data dimensionality. However, for image classification tasks, the data dimensionality is typically huge; for instance, each sample in the MNIST dataset has 784 dimensions. Embedding such high-dimensional data requires a large number of quantum gates, leading to circuits with a large number of qubits and high circuit depths. Due to the vanishing similarity issue, larger qubit numbers and deeper circuits degrade the performance of candidate circuits. To address this, we apply a feature selection (FS) method during the data preprocessing phase. Specifically, we use the Max-Relevance and Min-Redundancy (mRMR) algorithm to reduce the data dimensionality from
d
to
p
, where
p\ll d
. This significantly decreases the number of gates
P
required for candidate circuit construction. This approach not only mitigates the vanishing similarity issue but also enables the effective utilization of high-dimensional data on NISQ devices.

III.2
Metric prediction based on graph neural networks

To predict the PST of candidate circuits under device noise and the corresponding performance metric KTA, we design two separate GNNs, namely GNNs-1 and GNNs-2. These two networks share an overall similar architecture but differ in certain details due to the differences in their prediction targets. In the following, we provide a detailed description of the GNNs construction process, covering the aspects of construction of the dataset, graph construction, node features, target values, and construction of GNNs.

III.2.1
Construction of the dataset

The primary motivation for constructing GNNs predictors is to enable rapid predictions on a large set of candidate circuits after training with only a small amount of data. To build the dataset for training the GNNs, we adopt a random sampling strategy: a subset of circuits is randomly selected from the previously generated large pool of candidate circuits. To ensure the completeness of each circuit, we initialize all qubits in the state
\left|0\right\rangle
and include measurement operations within the circuits, as illustrated in the left panel of Fig.
3
. This setup also lays the foundation for incorporating device-specific noise characteristics, such as readout errors, into the node features.

III.2.2
Graph construction

Motivated by the intuitive similarity between quantum circuits and graph structures, we represent quantum circuits using directed acyclic graphs (DAGs). In this representation, each node corresponds to a qubit, a quantum gate, or a measurement operation, while the edges capture the temporal sequence of operations, progressing from left to right. As shown in Fig.
3
, we perform the transformation from circuits generated on IBM Torino into graph structures, with particular care taken to represent each 2-qubit gate as a single graph node.

III.2.3
Node features

For each node in the graph structure, we construct a feature vector to represent its associated properties. The features include the index, node type, target qubit, tag, the relaxation time (
T_{1}
) and dephasing time (
T_{2}
) of the target qubits, gate error, and readout error. The specific node features for GNNs-1 and GNNs-2 are shown in Fig.

The feature vectors for both GNNs-1 and GNNs-2 have a length of 31. The first part of the feature vector encodes the index, which is used for node sorting. The next three parts are one-hot encodings representing the node type, target qubit, and tag, respectively. The node type distinguishes different types of nodes, including initial input qubit, measurement,
R_{x}
gate,
R_{z}

CZ

I

CZ

The tag distinguishes whether a gate is used for data embedding, switching from 0 to 1 for embedding gates. In addition to capturing the topological connections between circuits, another advantage of GNNs is their ability to incorporate hardware noise information, which is primarily achieved through node feature vectors. In our method, the last seven elements of each node’s feature vector are dedicated to describing the noise characteristics of the quantum hardware. During model training, GNNs-1 incorporates these noise features to predict fidelity-related metric and enable early rejection of poor-performing circuits. In contrast, GNNs-2 focuses on predicting performance-related metric, with the last 7 elements of its feature vectors zero-filled to exclude noise information. The noise parameters are based on real quantum devices such as IBM Torino, IBM Perth, and IBM Nairobi. Specifically, the first 4 elements represent the relaxation time (
T_{1}
) and dephasing time (
T_{2}
) for the two involved qubits, the 5th element encodes the gate operation error, and the final two elements capture the readout errors. For nodes without associated noise characteristics, the corresponding values are set to zero. For instance, measurement nodes have their
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
, and gate error fields set to zero. Fig.
4
shows the complete feature vector representation of a
CZ

III.2.4
Target values

To evaluate the impact of noise on different quantum circuits, the concept of fidelity is introduced, which quantifies the closeness between two quantum states. In noisy quantum computations, fidelity is typically used to measure the difference between the quantum state generated by a noisy circuit and that generated by a noiseless classical simulation. A significant deviation indicates that the circuit is severely affected by noise, and its output is likely to be inaccurate. However, directly obtaining the fidelity of a quantum circuit requires complex quantum state tomography, which is computationally costly. To address this issue, we adopt the ”Probability of Successful Trials (PST)” proposed in
Wang et al. 2022b
as a proxy for fidelity, which has been shown to provide accurate fidelity estimates. The PST is specifically defined as follows:

PST=\frac{T_{initial}}{T_{total}}

(8)

where
T_{initial}
denotes the number of trials with an output identical to the initial state
\left|0\right\rangle^{\otimes N}
, and
T_{total}
represents the total number of trials. Instead of directly computing fidelity, we concatenate each circuit with its inverse and apply the combined circuit to the initial state
\left|0\right\rangle^{\otimes N}
. The proportion of measurements yielding the initial state is then recorded to obtain the PST, as illustrated in Fig.
5
. The computed PST is used as the target label for training the GNNs-1 model.

Figure 5:
The computation process of PST.

GNNs-2 is designed to predict a metric known as kernel-target alignment (KTA), which has been shown to be positively correlated with training accuracy. The relationship between KTA and training accuracy is analyzed in
Lei et al. 2024
, where KTA is validated as a reliable surrogate for training accuracy. Before introducing KTA, we first briefly review the concept of kernel alignment, which was first introduced by
Cristianini et al. 2001
. Kernel alignment is a similarity measure between two kernel matrices with given kernel matrices
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
:

KA\left(K_{1},K_{2}\right)=\frac{\left\langle{K_{1},K_{2}}\right\rangle_{F}}{\sqrt{\left\langle{K_{1},K_{1}}\right\rangle_{F}\left\langle{K_{2},K_{2}}\right\rangle_{F}}}

(9)

where
\left\langle K_{1},K_{2}\right\rangle_{F}
denotes the Frobenius inner product between two matrices, which is given by

\left\langle K_{1},K_{2}\right\rangle_{F}=\sum_{i=1}^{i=l}\sum_{j=1}^{j=l}k_{1}\left(x_{i},x_{j}\right)k_{2}\left(x_{i},x_{j}\right)

(10)

where
l
denotes the order of the matrix. For classification purposes we can define an ideal target matrix as

III.2.5
Construction of GNNs

We design similar graph neural network (GNN) architectures for GNNs-1 and GNNs-2, as illustrated in Fig.

Both models adopt a four-layer structure, where each layer is followed by an attention mechanism. The incorporation of attention allows the network to dynamically assign weights to edges during message passing, thereby enabling more effective capture of circuit characteristics. Between layers, LeakyReLU is used as the activation function. After graph-based feature extraction, we perform average pooling over the features of key nodes (excluding input and measurement nodes). The resulting pooled vector is then concatenated with the output of a three-layer fully connected network that processes the circuit’s global feature vector.The concatenated representation is subsequently passed through a multilayer perceptron (MLP) to generate the final prediction for either GNNs-1 or GNNs-2, as shown on the right side of Fig.

III.3
Circuit training and selection

This step aims to identify the most promising quantum kernels. After selecting top-
k
high-performing quantum circuits based on the fidelity metric (PST) and the classification accuracy proxy (KTA), we apply these
k
candidates to a specific classification task. The trainable parameters within each quantum kernel are then optimized from scratch using the classical Adam optimizer. Finally, the quantum circuit achieving the highest classification accuracy is selected as the searched optimal kernel.

IV
Evaluation

In this section, we conduct a series of experiments to thoroughly evaluate the performance of our proposed method, HaQGNN (Hardware-aware Quantum kernel design based on Graph Neural Networks). The experimental evaluation is divided into two main parts. The first part assesses the predictive performance and computational efficiency of the trained graph neural networks, GNNs-1 and GNNs-2 (Section
IV.1
). The second part demonstrates the performance advantages of HaQGNN over recent state-of-the-art methods across different datasets through simulation studies (Section
IV.2

IV.1
Predictive performance of the graph neural networks

Model and Training Setups.
In the default setup, both GNNs-1 and GNNs-2 adopt a 4-layer Graph Neural Network architecture with an attention mechanism integrated into each layer. This allows the model to dynamically assign weights to neighboring nodes during message passing, following the design of Graph Attention Networks (GATs). At the final GNN layer, both GNNs-1 and GNNs-2 perform average pooling over key nodes (excluding input and measurement nodes) to obtain a 31-dimensional pooled vector. This pooled vector is then concatenated with a global feature vector obtained from a three-layer fully connected network, where each hidden layer has a dimensionality of 12. The resulting concatenated vector is subsequently passed through a multilayer perceptron (MLP) with hidden layers of size 256, 128, and 64 to produce the final prediction. The activation function used in our model is LeakyReLU with a hyperparameter
\alpha=0.02
. Training is performed using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.01, a batch size of 512, and the mean squared error (MSE) as the loss function. The network is trained for 200 epochs. During data preprocessing, we apply Min-Max normalization to the node features across the entire dataset. The predictive performance is evaluated using the coefficient of determination (
R^{2}

Specifically, 4000 circuits are used for training and the remaining 1000 for testing. This trade-off is further illustrated in our subsequent runtime comparisons. For GNNs-1, the noise models used in training are derived from real quantum hardware, including several 7-qubit devices (IBM Perth, IBM Lagos, IBM Nairobi, and IBM Jakarta) and the 133-qubit IBM Torino. IV.2
Performance of HaQGNN on benchmarks

Model and Training Setups.
After evaluating the candidate circuits using GNNs-1 and GNNs-2, we select the top-
k

The training objective is to maximize classification accuracy using support vector machines (SVMs) based on the corresponding quantum kernels. During the parameter training process, we employ the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.01 to efficiently update the trainable parameters. We further assess the performance of the proposed method across multiple noisy simulators that incorporate noise models derived from IBM Perth, IBM Lagos, IBM Nairobi, IBM Jakarta, and IBM Torino. We compare our proposed method against seven representative baselines:

Random:

Dataset Setup.
We conduct experiments on three different quantum machine learning (QML) benchmark tasks: Credit Card (CC) Fraud Detection (binary classification: fraudulent vs. not fraudulent), MNIST-5 (five-class classification: digits 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4), and FMNIST-4 (four-class classification: T-shirt, Trouser, Bag, and Ankle Boot). For the MNIST-5 and FMNIST-4 benchmarks, we construct balanced training and test datasets by selecting 2000 training samples and 400 test samples from the original datasets. The original 784-dimensional images are reduced to 40 dimensions using the minimum Redundancy Maximum Relevance (mRMR) algorithm
Lei et al. 2024

To validate the performance advantage of the quantum kernels discovered by the HaQGNN method, we conduct experiments on a 4-qubit Credit Card (CC) task using two IBM quantum devices with different qubit scales: a 7-qubit device (IBM Perth) and a 133-qubit device (IBM Torino). The results are presented in Fig.

This performance gain can be attributed to the hardware-aware nature of HaQGNN, which allows the method to generate quantum circuits that are adapted to the physical characteristics of the device. On larger quantum processors, HaQGNN is capable of selecting qubits that are less affected by noise and synthesizing high-fidelity circuits accordingly, thereby leading to superior performance in real-device executions. Furthermore, we evaluate the 7-qubit MNIST-5 classification task on five IBM quantum backends: IBM Perth, IBM Lagos, IBM Nairobi, IBM Jakarta, and IBM Torino. As shown in Fig.

On IBM Lagos and IBM Jakarta, the classification performance of HaQGNN is slightly lower than that of QuantumNAS, EliVagar, and QuKerNet, respectively. This is primarily because these devices only have 7 qubits, which limits the capability of hardware-aware design to perform effective qubit selection—many qubits are significantly affected by noise, reducing the advantage of noise-aware circuit generation. However, when evaluated on the 133-qubit IBM Torino simulator, HaQGNN demonstrates a clear performance advantage, achieving the highest classification accuracy among all methods due to its ability to exploit lower-noise qubits through hardware-aware circuit design. V
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced HaQGNN, a hardware-aware quantum kernel design framework that leverages graph neural networks to evaluate and select task-specific quantum circuits under the constraints of NISQ hardware. By incorporating real-device topology, noise characteristics, and feature selection into the design pipeline, HaQGNN enables scalable and efficient kernel construction tailored for near-term quantum devices. The use of two models (GNNs-1 and GNNs-2) allows for fast and accurate prediction of circuit fidelity and kernel performance, significantly reducing the computational cost of kernel screening. Experimental results on three classification benchmarks, Credit Card, MNIST-5, and FMNIST-4, demonstrate that HaQGNN outperforms state-of-the-art quantum kernel methods in accuracy. These findings highlight the potential of learning-based, hardware-aware approaches for bridging the gap between theoretical quantum kernel methods and practical deployment on noisy quantum devices. As a future direction, exploring the transferability and generalization capabilities of quantum kernels across different tasks or data distributions may further enhance the practicality and reusability of quantum feature maps in broader quantum machine learning applications.
