The mechanism of vascular injury caused by polystyrene microplastics: Involving ferroptosis and metabolomics
Authors: Deyu Zhu, Xiao Liang, Qi Huang, Yang Wu, Zhuangzhuang Wei, Shenyang Cai, Qing Feng, Ping Ma, Xu Yang, Cuiyu Bao, Xinyu Bao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPolystyrene microplastics (PS-MPs) are environmental pollutants linked to cardiovascular diseases. This study investigated PS-MP-induced vascular toxicity mechanisms in mice. Mice were divided into saline control, low/medium/high PS-MP exposure (0.1, 1, and 10 mg/kg/d), and PS-MPs plus ferroptosis inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 (1 mg/kg/d + Fer-1) for 5 weeks. Vascular histopathology, lipid profiles, oxidative stress, ferroptosis markers, endothelial function, and serum metabolomics were assessed. PS-MP exposure elevated ROS, MDA, TC, and TG while reducing GSH and HDL-C. Ferroptosis was confirmed by decreased GPX4 and SLC7A11, with increased Fe2+ deposition. Vascular endothelial injury and remodeling occurred, evidenced by elevated ET-1, VEGF-A, VCAM-1, and ICAM-1, alongside reduced NO. Metabolomics revealed disruptions in folate, amino acid, citrate cycle, and tryptophan metabolism. Fer-1 inhibited ferroptosis, alleviating endothelial damage and vascular remodeling. PS-MPs induce vascular toxicity through oxidative stress, ferroptosis, and metabolic disturbances, mitigated by Fer-1.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) rank as the foremost cause of death worldwide, as well as the leading reason for fatalities in China.1 The global incidence of CVD has escalated significantly, jumping from 271 million (with a 95% uncertainty interval [UI] of 257 million to 285 million) in 1990 to 532 million (95% UI: 497 million to 550 million) by 2019. In spite of various preventive strategies, the rates of mortality associated with CVD continue to be alarmingly high, with fatalities increasing from 12.1 million (95% UI: 11.4 million to 12.6 million) in 1990 to 18.6 million (95% UI: 17.1 million to 19.7 million) in 2019.2 A significant contributor to the onset and progression of CVD is vascular endothelial dysfunction, which plays a crucial role in conditions such as atherosclerosis, hypertension, and heart failure.3 Therefore, maintaining vascular endothelial integrity is crucial for cardiovascular health.

Polystyrene microplastics (PS-MPs) are widely utilized thermoplastics in daily life. Plastic products encountered in everyday routines are often ingested orally, potentially accumulating in the body and adversely affecting human health. With social development, plastic usage is expected to surge dramatically, projected to reach 884 million tons globally by 2050.4 Degrading plastic products in the environment is difficult to achieve fully, as they fragment into microplastics via mechanisms like abrasion and chemical breakdown. Microplastics that have degraded can easily travel through air via wind currents, as well as through seawater and soil sediments, ultimately making their way into the human body through the digestive and respiratory systems. Importantly, recent research has identified the presence of PS-MPs in different bodily tissues and organs, highlighting the potential health risks that environmental PS-MPs may pose to human health and specifically cardiovascular well-being.5 An immediate necessity exists to clarify their pathogenic mechanisms and effects on general health. Considering the worldwide COVID-19 pandemic, the considerable quantity of discarded face masks has surfaced as a major contributor to PS-MPs in the environment, capturing public awareness regarding plastic pollution. Growing evidence indicates that the consumption of PS-MPs encourages the onset of cardiovascular-related illnesses. Individuals with microplastics or nanoplastics in their cardiovascular systems exhibit a higher probability of suffering from strokes or heart attacks compared to the general population.6,7 This indicates that PS-MPs may represent an unrecognized high-risk factor for CVD, with the maintenance of vascular endothelial integrity being a crucial aspect in the development of CVD. Further studies are essential to confirm the association between PS-MPs exposure and vascular endothelial injury.

Iron is an essential trace element required for the survival of nearly all living organisms. Ferroptosis, which is an iron-dependent type of programmed cell death, has been demonstrated to be crucial in the development and advancement of CVDs. Maintaining iron homeostasis is vital for normal cardiovascular function.8 Recent research indicates that PS-MPs primarily induce organ damage through reactive oxygen species (ROS), which serve as their key toxic mechanism. ROS and lipid peroxidation are hallmark events of cellular ferroptosis, a process in which intracellular iron ions drive the formation of excessive ROS through the Fenton reaction.9 This process subsequently attacks polyunsaturated fatty acids on cell membranes, generating lipid peroxides that ultimately lead to cell death. GPX4, one of the primary intracellular antioxidants, can reduce lipid peroxides to non-toxic lipid alcohols and inhibit lipid peroxidation under normal physiological conditions. However, when GPX4 is inhibited under certain pathological conditions, excessive lipid peroxides cannot be effectively cleared, ultimately triggering ferroptosis.10 Although previous studies have confirmed that exposure to PS-MPs can lead to vascular injury and abnormal activation of ferroptosis, most research has remained at the phenotypic level. To date, the specific causal mechanisms by which ferroptosis drives PS-MPs-related vascular dysfunction, the characteristic metabolic regulatory network underlying this pathological process, and whether the ferroptosis inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 can exert a protective effect by targeting ferroptosis remain unclear and lack systematic experimental validation.

Vascular endothelial injury is a well-established factor contributing to coronary artery occlusion, often accompanied by a series of metabolic alterations, including (a) termination of aerobic metabolic processes; (b) reduction of creatine phosphate (CP) levels; (c) onset of anaerobic glycolysis; and (d) buildup of byproducts from glycolysis, including lactate and α-glycerophosphate (α-GP), as well as degradation products from tissue nucleotide reserves. Metabolic alterations linked to contractile dysfunction and changes in electrocardiograms are observed.11 To further explore the underlying mechanisms, this study integrated metabolomics with traditional molecular toxicology approaches. Metabolomics was employed to systematically analyze the metabolic disruptions induced by PS-MPs and their potential associations with vascular injury and ferroptosis.

The aim of this research is to examine the vascular toxic effects caused by contact with PS-MPs and to explore the possible molecular pathways that are implicated. Drawing from current studies, we put forward the following scientific hypothesis (Figure 1): Mice exposed to PS-MPs may experience vascular endothelial injury and dysfunction, potentially leading to oxidative stress, ferroptosis, and disturbances in metabolic pathways. Result

PS-MPs exposure affects body weight in mice

During the 5-week experimental period, we recorded the changes in body weight of mice at the initial and terminal stages, as well as their overall health status (hair condition and activity level). Throughout the experiment, no mice died or exhibited abnormal behaviors (such as lethargy, ruffled fur, or hunched posture). At baseline (week 0), there were no significant differences in initial body weight among all groups, confirming successful randomization. Mice in the saline control group showed consistent and steady weight gain. In contrast, PS-MPs exposure significantly suppressed weight gain. Notably, Fer-1 intervention significantly reversed the growth retardation induced by PS-MPs (Figure S2).

PS-MPs exposure induces vascular endothelial injury in mice

The results of H&E staining for vascular tissues are shown in Figure 2. In comparison to the saline group, the groups receiving 1 PS-MPs and 10 PS-MPs displayed irregularities in their vascular structures, nuclear deformation, disorganized cell arrangement, and severe intracellular vacuolization. Moreover, as the exposure doses rise, the thickness of the intima-media in blood vessels progressively escalates when compared to the saline group. These results suggest that exposure to PS-MPs results in damage to the vascular endothelium in mice. Furthermore, compared to the 1 PS-MPs group, the vascular structure in the 1 PS-MPs + Fer-1 group appeared significantly more normalized, with no obvious thickening of the intima-media observed. This indicates that the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 has the ability to inhibit the ferroptosis pathway triggered by exposure to PS-MPs, consequently reducing damage to the vascular endothelium. The Masson staining results of vascular tissues further corroborated these conclusions. In comparison to the saline group, the group exposed to PS-MPs showed a greater extent of collagen deposition in the vascular area. This indicates that PS-MPs exposure induces pathological vascular fibrotic remodeling accompanied by excessive collagen deposition, reflecting progressive vascular damage. Furthermore, the 1 PS-MPs + Fer-1 group exhibited significantly reduced ROS and MDA levels alongside a marked recovery of GSH when compared to the 1 PS-MPs group. Discussion

Plastics are polymers composed of various organic compounds, characterized by their excellent ductility. Due to their low cost and ease of production, plastics are widely utilized across all aspects of daily life. However, following mechanical abrasion and natural degradation, a significant proportion of plastics transforms into waste that ultimately contaminates the environment. Consequently, as plastic consumption continues to escalate, the associated environmental emissions of plastics are expected to rise correspondingly. Although advancements in plastic recycling technologies and related management policies are ongoing, the rate of plastic waste generation significantly outpaces global processing capacity.12,13 An increasing amount of research suggests that PS-MPs can accumulate within blood vessels either through direct ingestion or contact via the systemic circulation, which may elevate the risk of cardiovascular issues, including hypertension, CVD, and stroke.14 Nonetheless, the precise mechanisms responsible for these consequences are still not fully understood. Consequently, exploring the effects of PS-MPs on the vascular endothelium is both essential and significant. Evidence of vascular damage in mice induced by PS-MPs exposure

CVD ranks as the primary cause of mortality across the globe. The health of the vascular endothelium is crucial for preserving vascular balance. Exposure to PS-MPs can disrupt endothelial homeostasis and directly lead to vascular endothelial injury.15 Our histopathological results (Figure 2) demonstrate that the degree of vascular endothelial damage worsens progressively with increasing concentrations of PS-MPs exposure, specifically manifested as a gradual thickening of the intima-media and severe intracellular vacuolization. Likewise, the findings are supported by Masson staining, which shows that exposure to PS-MPs leads to an increasing accumulation of collagen fibers in blood vessels and subsequent vascular remodeling. This observation is consistent with the results presented by Wang et al.16

In clinical practice, the vasodilatory substance NO and the vasoconstrictive substance ET-1 are commonly utilized to evaluate vascular endothelial function. In this research (Figure 3), we noted that compared with the saline group, the levels of NO significantly decreased while ET-1 levels markedly increased with rising concentrations of PS-MPs, indicating vascular endothelial dysfunction.17,18 Furthermore, ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 are major adhesion molecules on the vascular endothelium and are widely used to assess endothelial cell functional status.19,20 In comparison to the saline group, there was a significant increase in the expressions of VCAM-1 and ICAM-1 in the groups exposed to PS-MPs, further validating the activation of vascular endothelial inflammation and dysfunction under these conditions. Moreover, the exposure to PS-MPs resulted in a notable increase in VEGF-A, an important biomarker associated with CVDs,21 when compared to the saline group. This suggests that exposure to PS-MPs led to significant harm to the vascular endothelium in mice and activated compensatory repair processes within their bodies.

The potential mediating role of oxidative stress-mediated ferroptosis in the vascular toxic effects of PS-MPs

The leading role of oxidative stress

Oxidative stress is a critical factor in vascular endothelial injury and CVDs, serving as a major contributor following exposure to PS-MPs.22 Xiao et al. demonstrated that exposure to PS-MPs significantly exacerbates the production of ROS, which subsequently results in a notable impairment of the tube-forming capacity in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs).23,24 The findings from this research (Figure 4) suggest that, compared with the saline group, increasing concentrations of PS-MPs correlate with elevated levels of ROS and MDA in mice vascular tissues, while the levels of GSH, an important antioxidant, significantly decrease. The results align with those identified by Huang et al., indicating that contact with PS-MPs leads to oxidative imbalances in the vascular tissues of mice.25

The mediating role of ferroptosis in vascular endothelial injury

Ferroptosis, a type of cell death reliant on iron, is marked by the buildup of intracellular iron and the peroxidation of lipids. This process potentially contributes to the damage of endothelial cells caused by oxidative stress.26 Lipid peroxidation can be triggered by ROS via the creation of lipid radicals, positioning the buildup of lipid peroxides as a key occurrence in ferroptosis. The end product of lipid peroxidation, MDA, serves a dual purpose: It directly indicates the level of lipid peroxidation and shows the amount of endothelial damage resulting from ROS.27 The SLC7A11/GSH/GPX4 signaling pathway exemplifies the traditional antioxidant defense mechanism against ferroptosis. As a subunit of the XC- system, SLC7A11 facilitates the 1:1 exchange of intracellular glutamate and extracellular cysteine, which plays a vital role in the synthesis of GSH. GSH is important in ferroptosis, serving not just as a free radical scavenger and an enhancer of iron absorption but also as a marker of the organism’s ability to combat oxidative stress.28 Consequently, inhibition of SLC7A11 results in decreased GSH synthesis and subsequent ferroptosis. GPX4, recognized as the primary molecular marker of ferroptosis, inhibits this process by suppressing lipid peroxidation.29 The findings presented in this study (Figure 5) reveal that the group exposed to PS-MPs showed lower expression levels of SLC7A11 and GPX4, along with a significant decrease in GSH levels, as well as elevated levels of Fe2+ and MDA in vascular tissues when compared with the saline group. Additionally, in contrast to the 1 PS-MPs group, the incorporation of the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 led to an increase in the levels of SLC7A11 and GPX4, a significant recovery of GSH levels, and decreased Fe2+ and MDA levels in vascular tissues. These results suggest that exposure to PS-MPs triggers the ferroptosis pathway, and that Fer-1 successfully inhibits this reaction. It is worth noting that the current evidence supporting a role for ferroptosis in PS-MPs-induced vascular injury is primarily based on the detection of GPX4, SLC7A11, and Fe2+, as well as the results of intervention with Ferrostatin-1. These findings are suggestive but insufficient to draw definitive causal conclusions. We lack independent and standard validation indicators of ferroptosis, such as the accumulation of lipid reactive oxygen species, the expression of hallmark markers ACSL4 and PTGS2, and mitochondrial ultrastructural changes. Without these complementary phenotypic and molecular evidence, we cannot fully confirm that ferroptosis is the absolute core driver. Instead, ferroptosis should be regarded as a key contributing factor and potential intermediate mechanism in the vascular pathological injury induced by PS-MPs. In subsequent research, a more comprehensive validation using multiple independent ferroptosis markers is needed to consolidate this mechanistic conclusion. Furthermore, intervention with Ferrostatin-1 alleviated the PS-MP-induced vascular endothelial injury and abnormal fibrotic remodeling, further confirming the critical role of ferroptosis in this pathological process. However, it should be emphasized that relying solely on the protective effects of Ferrostatin-1 cannot fully exclude the potential involvement of apoptosis and mitochondrial dysfunction. Accumulating evidence suggests that microplastic exposure simultaneously triggers endothelial cell apoptosis, mitochondrial structural damage, and energy metabolism disruption,30,31 which may work in conjunction with ferroptosis to synergistically exacerbate vascular injury. In the present study, we did not systematically detect characteristic markers of apoptosis or assess mitochondrial functional indicators, and therefore could not definitively rule out the contribution of these alternative pathways. Thus, the current mechanistic explanation primarily focusing on ferroptosis inevitably oversimplifies the situation, and the interactions among ferroptosis, apoptosis, and mitochondrial dysfunction still require further elucidation.

The mediating role of lipid metabolism disorders in vascular endothelial injury

Disorders in lipid metabolism can increase the risk of CVDs, mainly exhibited by the atypical buildup of cholesterol and triglycerides.32 In studies with mice, it has been demonstrated that exposure to PS-MPs can trigger disturbances in lipid metabolism.33 In this study (Figure 6), the PS-MPs exposure group exhibited significantly increased TC and TG levels, alongside a gradual decrease in HDL-C levels, consistent with the results reported by Song et al.34 This suggests that PS-MPs may act as endocrine disruptors in mice, leading to lipid metabolism disorders that affect the permeability and repair capacity of vascular endothelial cells, ultimately resulting in a markedly increased risk of vascular endothelial injury.

The effects of 1 PS-MPs on serum metabolites and KEGG metabolic pathways

Variations in oxidative stress pathways

Through analysis of metabolite expression profiles and VIP (Figure 7D), the exposure to 1 PS-MPs led to a notable reduction in folic acid levels when comparing to the saline group. Earlier research has shown that folic acid has the ability to alleviate oxidative stress both in vivo and in vitro through the regulation of VPO1 DNA methylation levels.35 As a result, PS-MPs lead to decreased levels of folic acid, which intensifies oxidative stress in mice. Furthermore, an analysis of KEGG pathways showed notable variations in the tryptophan metabolism pathway (Figures 8C and 8D). Studies have suggested that there is a strong connection between tryptophan metabolism and oxidative stress, potentially mediated by the DR3/IKK/NF-κB signaling pathway.36 These results reinforce the idea that PS-MPs may lead to oxidative stress in mice, highlighting the significant role that oxidative stress plays in this scenario.

Variations associated with the ferroptosis pathway

Based on the analysis of the metabolite expression profile and VIP (Figure 7D), it was observed that exposure to 1 PS-MPs led to a notable reduction in amino acid concentrations, especially tryptophan, in comparison to the saline group. Previous studies have indicated that tryptophan metabolites produced by gut microbiota can inhibit ferroptosis, possibly through a mechanism linked to the IDA/AHR/ALDH1A3 pathway.37 As a key precursor of endogenous anti-ferroptotic metabolites, reduced tryptophan availability may weaken the intrinsic defense against ferroptosis in endothelial cells, creating a permissive metabolic environment for ferroptosis activation. “Consequently, the reduction in amino acid levels, especially tryptophan, is preliminarily suggested to be potentially linked to ferroptosis induced by PS-MPs exposure.” Additionally, KEGG pathway analysis (Figures 8C and 8D) unveiled significant differences in the citrate cycle pathway. Research has established a strong correlation between the citrate cycle and the occurrence of ferroptosis.38 The citrate cycle serves as a central hub linking energy metabolism and redox balance; its disruption can impair mitochondrial function, amplify reactive oxygen species accumulation, and further drive lipid peroxidation, a hallmark event of ferroptosis.39 These findings preliminarily indicate a potential correlation between metabolic pathways and ferroptosis, requiring further experimental validation. Beyond tryptophan and the citrate cycle, the generalized decrease in amino acids may also impair the SLC7A11/GSH/GPX4 antioxidant axis, as amino acids are critical substrates for cysteine and glutathione synthesis, which are essential for suppressing ferroptosis.40 The presence of PS-MPs could potentially trigger the onset of ferroptosis by disrupting normal metabolic processes. Collectively, these exploratory analyses suggest that PS-MPs exposure triggers a ferroptosis-centered metabolic regulatory network: ferroptosis acts as a potential upstream trigger to disrupt core metabolic pathways (citrate cycle, tryptophan), while metabolic disturbances reciprocally amplify ferroptosis activation. This bidirectional interplay ultimately converges on vascular endothelial ferroptosis and pathological remodeling. Of note, these regulatory relationships remain preliminary due to the absence of the ferroptosis inhibitor group in metabolomics analysis; future targeted metabolomics with Ferrostatin-1 intervention is required to validate these causal links.

Metabolic differences and related diseases

The analysis of KEGG pathway enrichment (Figure 8C) revealed significant enrichment in various differential metabolic pathways, including bile secretion, glucagon signaling and central carbon metabolism related to cancer. These results indicate that exposure to PS-MPs might be significantly linked to conditions like hepatobiliary disorders, diabetes, and cancer, thus deepening our comprehension of the toxicological impacts of PS-MPs.

Analysis of the correlation between ferroptosis-related proteins and metabolism and vascular phenotype

To further validate the potential molecular mechanisms, we conducted a preliminary correlation analysis based on existing experimental data, focusing on core ferroptosis-related proteins (GPX4, SLC7A11), key differential metabolites, and vascular injury indicators. Notably, the protein expression levels of GPX4 and SLC7A11 in thoracic aortic tissue exhibited a significantly positive correlation with serum tryptophan levels and metabolites associated with the citric acid cycle. In contrast, the expression of GPX4/SLC7A11 was negatively correlated with vascular Fe2+ deposition, serum ET-1 levels, and collagen deposition levels. These correlations indicated that the downregulation of GPX4/SLC7A11 expression (a hallmark of ferroptosis) was closely associated with decreased disruption of the tryptophan and citric acid cycles, alleviated iron overload, and aggravated vascular fibrosis injury, which was consistent with previous findings.41,42 Furthermore, intervention with the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 reversed the downregulation of GPX4/SLC7A11, accompanied by partial restoration of serum tryptophan and citric acid cycle metabolites, as well as alleviation of vascular injury indicators. The consistency between this intervention and the associations further confirmed the potential molecular cascade: PS-MPs exposure leads to downregulation of GPX4/SLC7A11 expression, which in turn activates ferroptosis, disrupts biological metabolic pathways (tryptophan, citric acid cycle), and culminates in vascular endothelial injury and fibrotic remodeling. However, this correlation analysis is still at a preliminary stage and is based solely on existing markers. Future studies will need to detect additional proteins associated with ferroptosis (such as ACSL4 and PTGS2) and adopt a larger sample size to corroborate these molecular associations and consolidate mechanistic evidence.

Limitations of the study

This study has certain limitations: first, all experiments in this study were conducted only using male C57BL/6 mice, without including female mice. Therefore, the research findings derived from male mice cannot be directly extrapolated to female individuals. Future studies should include both male and female mice to systematically investigate sex differences in the processes of vascular injury, ferroptosis, and metabolic disorders induced by polystyrene microplastics, thereby enhancing the translational value of this research. Second, current evidence supporting the role of ferroptosis in vascular injury induced by PS-MPs is primarily based on the detection of GPX4, SLC7A11, and Fe2+, as well as the results of intervention with Ferrostatin-1. These results are suggestive but insufficient to draw definitive causal conclusions. We lack independent and standard ferroptosis validation indicators, such as lipid reactive oxygen species accumulation and the expression of specific ferroptosis markers. Without these complementary phenotypic and molecular pieces of evidence, we cannot fully confirm that ferroptosis is the absolute core driving factor. Instead, ferroptosis should be considered a key contributing factor and a potential intermediate mechanism in the vascular pathological injury triggered by PS-MPs. Subsequent studies need to conduct more comprehensive validation using multiple independent ferroptosis indicators to consolidate this mechanistic conclusion.

Then metabolomics analysis only set up the saline group and the 1 PS-MPs group, with a relatively small sample size (n = 4). The absence of data for high- and low-dose polystyrene microplastic groups and the Fer-1 intervention group made it difficult to assess the dose-dependent nature of metabolic changes and impossible to clarify the causal relationship between ferroptosis and metabolic disorders. Finally, the differential metabolites and related pathways enriched in this study were based solely on untargeted metabolomics analysis, without targeted metabolomics validation or supporting in vitro and in vivo molecular experiments. Therefore, the observed metabolic changes are only preliminary exploratory results and cannot be confirmed as the direct cause of endothelial injury. Future studies need to expand experimental groups, increase sample sizes, and conduct targeted validation of key metabolites to clarify the mechanism by which metabolic disorder contributes to vascular injury induced by polystyrene microplastics.

Experimental model and study participant details

C57BL/6 male mice, classified as SPF grade, were obtained from Hunan Silaike Jingda Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd (Hunan, China). These mice were aged between 6–8 weeks and had an approximate weight of 18–20 g. Male mice were housed under standard laboratory conditions with a 12-h light/dark cycle, a constant temperature of 22°C–25°C and relative humidity of 50–60%. They had ad libitum access to standard chow and water. All mice underwent a one-week acclimation period before the formal experiment. All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of humane care and the ARRIVE guidelines. All animal experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Hubei University of Science and Technology (Ethics Approval No. HBUST-IACUC-2023–026).

Method details

PS-MPs (CAS: 9003–53–6) were supplied by Tianjin Junyijia Technology Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China). Ferroptosis inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 (CAS: 347174–05–4) were acquired from Tianjin Puxitang Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China). The chemical assay kits for TC, TG, HDL-C, NO, MDA, and GSH in mice were sourced from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). The ELISA kits for ET-1, VEGF-A, ICAM-1, and VCAM-1 in mice were provided by Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All other reagents utilized throughout the experimental process were of the highest commercial quality and adhered to the specified standards.

Grouping and experimental protocol

Forty male C57BL/6 mice were randomly assigned to five groups, with each group comprising eight mice, as outlined in Table 1. The specific grouping methods were as follows: (1) Normal saline group: physiological saline was administered instead of PS-MPs; (2) 0.1 PS-MPs group: oral exposure to 0.1 mg/kg/d of PS-MPs; (3) 1 PS-MPs group: oral exposure to 1 mg/kg/d of PS-MPs; (4) 10 PS-MPs group: oral exposure to 10 mg/kg/d of PS-MPs; (5) 1 PS-MPs + Fer-1 group: oral exposure to 1 mg/kg/d of PS-MPs combined with an intraperitoneal injection of 5 mg/kg/d of Fer-1. The reasoning behind the categorization was as follows: Group (1) acted as the blank control; Groups (2), (3), and (4) corresponded to low, medium, and high exposure categories (0.1, 1, and 10 mg/kg/d of PS-MPs) to evaluate the impact of different concentrations of PS-MPs on the vascular endothelium; Group (5) was the antagonist group, where medium-dose PS-MPs exposure was combined with a ferroptosis inhibitor to investigate the involvement of the ferroptosis pathway in the vascular endothelial injury process induced by PS-MPs exposure. The PS-MPs were administered via the most common route of oral administration, with the three different concentrations (low, medium, and high: 0.1, 1, and 10 mg/kg/d of PS-MPs) selected on the basis of preliminary experimental research results.43 Although the highest selected doses may exceed the estimated daily human exposure level, they were chosen to establish a dose-response gradient within a practical experimental time frame and to identify the threshold for inducing vascular injury and ferroptosis.44,45 Notably, the short-term high-dose treatment in our animal models cannot be fully equated with the long-term low-dose cumulative exposure in real ecological environments and human daily exposure scenarios. Nevertheless, the molecular mechanisms and pathological phenotypes revealed by this dose gradient still provide important mechanistic references for understanding the potential cardiovascular toxicity of microplastics. Considering the gap between experimental doses and real-world environmental exposure, further research is needed on chronic low-dose PS-MPs interventions to better simulate human actual exposure characteristics and assess long-term cardiovascular health risks. The concentration of the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 was determined according to the study by Li et al.46 The entire experimental period lasted for five weeks. The 5-week exposure duration was based on previous studies and was also designed as a subchronic exposure model to simulate long-term low-dose cumulative exposure in humans over months or years. Additionally, it adhered to practical constraints of the experimental conditions, including animal welfare, cost, and time arrangements.47,48,49 However, we acknowledge that the lack of internal exposure assessment and biodistribution data is indeed a limitation of our study. This omission primarily stems from the technical challenges in accurately determining microplastic content in vivo, as well as the initial study design’s focus on identifying mechanistic pathways. In our subsequent research, we will adopt more advanced techniques for internal exposure assessment and biodistribution information to enhance the persuasiveness of our experiments.

At the beginning of the experiment, 40 male C57BL/6 mice were randomly divided into 5 groups using a random number generator, with 8 mice per group. During the 5-week experimental period, no mice were excluded due to health abnormalities, death, or abnormal behavior. To optimize resource utilization and meet the specific requirements of various tests, subgroups were randomly selected from each group for assignment to different test items, with no overlap of samples between test items. The specific allocation is as follows: Vascular histology detection (hematoxylin-eosin staining, Masson staining) and immunohistochemistry detection (GPX4/SLC7A11/Fe2+ staining): 3 mice were randomly selected from each group. This sample size is a routine standard for histological qualitative and quantitative assessment in preclinical vascular studies, providing sufficient statistical power for morphological comparisons. Serum non-targeted metabolomics detection: four mice were randomly selected from each group. This sample size meets the minimum sample requirement for non-targeted metabolomics analysis (sample size ≥3), balancing statistical reliability and detection cost. Biochemical index detection (reactive oxygen species, malondialdehyde, glutathione, total cholesterol, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, endothelin-1, nitric oxide, vascular cell adhesion molecule-1, intercellular adhesion molecule-1, vascular endothelial growth factor A): six mice were randomly selected from each group. A prospective power analysis was conducted prior to the experiment. For key quantitative outcome indicators (such as oxidative stress markers and vascular function indicators), with a moderate effect size (d = 0.8), significance level α = 0.05, and statistical power of 0.8, the calculated minimum required sample size per group was 5. In this study, 6 mice per group were selected for biochemical assays, which exceeds this threshold and ensures adequate statistical power. The sample sizes chosen for histological and metabolomic analyses are consistent with the generally accepted standards in the fields of environmental toxicology and metabolomics.50,51,52,53

Vascular tissue-localized indicators: H&E/Masson staining, GPX4/SLC7A11 IHC, Fe2+ Prussian blue staining, ROS/MDA/GSH, TC/TG/HDL-C (all measured in aortic vascular tissues). Serum systemic indicators: ET-1, NO, VEGF-A, VCAM-1, ICAM-1, untargeted metabolomics (all measured in serum samples, reflecting systemic circulating levels rather than direct vascular tissue changes).

PS-MPs characteristics

The spherical polystyrene microplastics used in this experiment have a nominal particle size of approximately 5 microns. Their key physicochemical properties are described as follows: Basic morphology: They are uniform spheres with stable particle size distribution and good monodispersity. Hydrophilicity: The microspheres themselves are hydrophilic and can be well dispersed in aqueous solutions after simple ultrasonic treatment. Surface chemical characteristics: No exogenous chemical functional groups are added to the particle surface, nor are carboxyl, amino, or other artificially modified groups attached. Surface electrical properties: The microspheres naturally carry a negative charge without the need for artificial charge modification. Purity and contaminant detection: The purchased polystyrene microplastics have passed impurity detection. No residual polymer monomers, heavy metal elements, or persistent organic pollutants are detected within the particles. No additional chemical additives were introduced during the experimental preparation process. Pre-exposure preparation: Prior to intragastric administration, PS-MPs were fully dispersed in sterile saline using ultrasonic treatment to avoid particle aggregation and ensure a stable physical state during in vivo exposure. All the aforementioned physicochemical parameters were obtained from the official factory quality inspection documents provided by the supplier, and no independent physicochemical characterization tests were conducted in this study. Specific scanning electron microscope images can be found in the Supplementary Materials. The reason for selecting 5 μm polystyrene microplastics (PS-MPs) is based on their significant environmental and biological relevance: 5 μm is one of the most frequently detected microplastic sizes in aquatic and terrestrial environments as well as in biological tissues.54,55 Additionally, particles with diameters between 3 and 12 μm can cross biological barriers (such as the intestinal tract and blood vessels), leading to accumulation in the systemic circulation. Therefore, this size is widely used to study the cardiovascular toxicity of microplastics.56 Therefore, we selected 5-micrometer diameter PS-MPs for our experimental study. It should be noted that only one particle size (approximately 5 μm) of polystyrene microplastics was used in this study. Therefore, we are unable to determine whether the vascular toxicity, ferroptosis activation, and metabolic disturbances induced by PS-MPs are size-dependent. Different microplastic particle sizes exhibit varying cellular uptake efficiency, tissue accumulation capacity, and oxidative stress-inducing ability. The lack of comparison across multiple particle sizes limits a comprehensive interpretation of the structure–toxicity relationship of PS-MPs. Further studies using a gradient of particle sizes (nanoscale and microscale) are needed to systematically explore size-specific toxicity mechanisms.

Vascular histopathology

Histopathological examination was conducted on three randomly chosen vascular tissue samples from each group of mice. The thoracic aortas were carefully dissected and immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by standard dehydration, embedding, and sectioning procedures for histological analysis. Following this, H&E, Masson, and Fe2+ Prussian blue staining were carried out. Observations were made using a DP73 microscope (Tokyo, Japan). Evaluations of the tissue sections were carried out qualitatively and quantitatively by two seasoned pathologists using a blinded analysis approach. The quantitative analysis of Fe2+ Prussian blue staining was performed with reference to Wu et al.57 After mounting, the morphological changes of blood vessels in each group were observed under an optical microscope.

Immunohistochemical analysis

The vascular tissue sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, and heated, followed by treatment with 0.3% H2O2 and blocking with 5% goat serum. Antibodies against GPX4 from Abcam (Cambridge, UK) and SLC7A11 from Huaan Biotech (Hangzhou, China) were utilized at a dilution of 1:200. The sections that underwent immunohistochemical staining were evaluated with a DP73 microscope (Tokyo, Japan), and the levels of target protein expression were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.

Detection of oxidative stress-related indicators

The vascular tissue was homogenized using ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.5) and subsequently ground on ice with a glass homogenizer at the specified ratio. The mixture obtained was subjected to centrifugation at a speed of 10,000 rpm for a duration of 10 min, after which the supernatant was retrieved. The concentration of ROS in the vascular tissue supernatant was evaluated using the ROS indicator DCFH-DA.58 Additionally, MDA and GSH levels were assessed utilizing kits, strictly adhering to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Detection of lipid metabolism-related indicators

Vascular tissue levels of TC, TG, and HDL-C were assessed employing kits. All experimental protocols were carried out meticulously following the instructions provided by the manufacturer.

Evaluation of vascular endothelial function

The ELISA kits were used to measure the levels of ET-1, VEGF (sensitivity of 1.0 pg/mL), ICAM-1, and VCAM-1 (sensitivity of 1.0 ng/mL) in mice serum. Furthermore, the measurement of NO in mice serum was conducted using a chemical kit. All tests were executed following the guidelines provided by the manufacturer.

Untargeted metabolomics analysis of mice serum

LC-MS analysis was performed using a Thermo Fisher Scientific UHPLC-Exploris240 system, which integrates ultra-high performance liquid chromatography and Fourier transform mass spectrometry. The sample processing steps were as follows: 200 μL of the sample was accurately transferred into a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube, and 800 μL of extraction solvent (methanol:acetonitrile = 1:1, v/v) containing 0.02 mg/mL of four internal standards, including L-2-chlorophenylalanine, was added. The mixture was vortexed for 30 s, then subjected to low-temperature ultrasonication at 5°C and 40 kHz for 30 min, followed by incubation at −20°C for 30 min. After centrifugation at 13,000 g and 4°C for 15 min, the supernatant was transferred and dried under a nitrogen stream. Reconstitute the residue with 120 μL of dissolution solution (acetonitrile:water = 1:1), vortex mix again for 30 s, and perform low-temperature ultrasonic extraction at 5°C and 40 kHz for 5 min. Finally, centrifuge at 13,000 g and 4°C for 10 min, transfer the supernatant into an injection vial with insert for instrumental analysis, and take 20 μL of the supernatant from each sample to create quality control samples. Chromatographic conditions: ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 μm; Waters, Milford, USA); mobile phase A: 95% water +5% acetonitrile (containing 0.1% formic acid); mobile phase B: 47.5% acetonitrile +47.5% isopropanol +5% water (containing 0.1% formic acid). Injection volume was 3 μL, and column temperature was 40°C. Mass spectrometry analysis was performed using electrospray ionization, and mass signals were acquired in positive and negative ion scanning modes.

The preprocessing of raw LC/MS data was performed using Progenesis QI software (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA), resulting in the generation of a three-dimensional data matrix that was subsequently exported as a CSV file. This matrix contained information regarding samples, metabolite names, and the intensities of mass spectral responses. Following the extraction of internal standard peaks and the identification of known false positive peaks—including noise, column bleed, and derivatized reagent peaks—the data matrix was de-redundant and pooled. Concurrently, metabolites were identified by consulting databases such as HMDB, Metlin, and the Majorbio Database. Data analysis was conducted on the free online platform available at cloud.majorbio.com. Metabolic characteristics present in at least 80% of any sample group were retained. After filtration, minimum values were assigned to samples with metabolite levels below the quantitation threshold, and each metabolic feature was normalized by the total sum. The sum normalization method was employed on the sample mass spectrum peak intensities to minimize errors arising from sample preparation and instrument instability, thereby creating a normalized data matrix. Furthermore, variables in QC samples exhibiting a relative standard deviation (RSD) exceeding 30% were excluded, and a log10 transformation was applied to generate the final data matrix for further analysis. Variance analysis was conducted on the matrix file following data preprocessing. The R package 'ropls' (Version 1.6.2) was utilized to perform principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), incorporating a 7-cycle interactive validation to evaluate the model’s stability. Metabolic enrichment and pathway analysis, facilitated by the KEGG database (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/), were employed to link the unique metabolites in the two groups to their corresponding biochemical pathways. Metabolites are categorized according to the pathways in which they participate or the roles they fulfill. An enrichment analysis was conducted to ascertain whether a specific set of metabolites is situated within a functional node. This approach is predicated on the notion that the annotation analysis of an individual metabolite can be expanded to encompass the study of multiple metabolites. To identify the key biological pathways associated with the experimental treatments, enrichment analysis was performed utilizing the 'scipy.stats' Python package (https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/).

The experimental process of serum metabolomics is based on our previous experiments.59 The raw metabolomics data have been deposited to MetaboLights (accession number: MTBLS14514) and are publicly available at https://www.ebi.ac.uk/metabolights/MTBLS14514.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism version 9.0. Normality was evaluated using the D'Agostino-Pearson omnibus test. Data are expressed as mean ± SEM. Comparisons between groups were carried out using one-way ANOVA followed by the LSD test for multiple comparisons, with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05. The exact value of n and what n represents are indicated in the respective figure legends: n = 3 for histopathology and immunohistochemistry (number of mice), n = 4 for metabolomics (number of mice), and n = 6 for biochemical and ELISA assays (number of mice). Statistical significance was indicated as follows: ∗: p < 0.05, ∗∗: p < 0.01, compared with the saline group; #: p < 0.05, ##: p < 0.01, compared with the 1 PS-MPs group.
