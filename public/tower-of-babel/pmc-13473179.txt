Comparative potential for generating spontaneous signals for abuse and dependence among drugs used for attention deficit hyperactivity disorder: A disproportionality analysis in VigiBase®
Authors: Emilie Jouanjus, Tiffanie Grime, Constance Bordet, Maryse Lapeyre‐Mestre, Benjamin Rolland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe comparative liability for abuse and dependence of drugs used for attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) has been poorly explored so far.AimsTo evaluate the relative potential of medications used for ADHD for generating spontaneous reports of abuse and dependence in international pharmacovigilance data.MethodsA disproportionality analysis was conducted using data recorded in VigiBase®, the World Health Organisation global database of individual case safety reports (ICSRs). ICSRs recorded between 1 January 2002 and 30 June 2023, referring to paediatric and adult subjects with known sex and aged ≥ 15 years and involving suspect drugs used for ADHD, were included. Reporting odds ratios (RORs) for ‘Drug abuse and dependence’, along with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs), were calculated using methylphenidate as the reference drug and with adjustment for age, sex, seriousness, reporter qualification and continent of reporting. Several sensitivity analyses were performed.ResultsAmong the 55 219 ICSRs included, 2633 were reports of drug abuse and dependence. The proportion of drug abuse and dependence reports was significantly lower for all studied drugs than for methylphenidate, except for lisdexamfetamine (ROR: 1.06, 95% CI 0.91–1.24). Sensitivity analyses yielded results comparable to the primary analysis, but with a slightly higher ROR for lisdexamfetamine when excluding US data (ROR: 1.26, 95% CI 1.03–1.54).ConclusionsMethylphenidate and lisdexamfetamine emerged as the two drugs with the highest likelihood of safety reports related to abuse and dependence. These findings challenge previous statements suggesting that the abuse potential of lisdexamfetamine was lower than that of other psychostimulant drugs used for ADHD and advocate for further pharmacoepidemiological studies in this field.

INTRODUCTION

Methylphenidate is the most used psychostimulant medication worldwide, labelled for attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and narcolepsy. 1 However, methylphenidate misuse, or even abuse, has long been reported worldwide. 2 , 3 Outside a medical context, users have various purposes, such as enhancing cognitive functions, inducing euphoria or reducing the need for sleep or eating. 4

The abuse potential of methylphenidate has been related to its action on dopamine with a mode of action based on catecholamine reuptake transporters, primarily dopamine and norepinephrine transporters, leading to their increased extracellular concentrations. 5 The same pharmacodynamic profile is shared by psychostimulant drugs, whether licit or illicit, and the abuse potential of all these drugs is generally acknowledged. 6 Apart from methylphenidate, these drugs comprise the following: (1) amphetamine‐derivatives, including dexamphetamine, lisdexamfetamine (the prodrug of dexamphetamine) and a mixture of amphetamine and dexamphetamine (called amphetamine salts, Adderall®); (2) nonamphetamine psychostimulants, essentially solriamfetol, modafinil and dexmethylphenidate; (3) atomoxetine, which targets the norepinephrine transporter with high affinity and is not considered a psychostimulant; and (4) bupropion, labelled for smoking cessation or depression and also used off‐label for ADHD. 7 Overall, these drugs provide good efficacy in their main indications. 8 Their medical use (unlike misuse) while prescribed for ADHD during adolescence does not seem to precipitate later illicit stimulant use disorders. 9 This notwithstanding, the potential for abuse and dependence within this class of drugs could affect their benefit–risk balance.

In practice, however, this specific risk of abuse and dependence associated with each of the different medications used for ADHD remains largely unexplored. In 2017, Gahr et al. applied signal detection methods to data from VigiBase®, the World Health Organisation (WHO) global database of reported potential side effects of medicinal products, to evaluate safety signals for substances used in pharmacological neuroenhancement, which included three ADHD medications: methylphenidate, atomoxetine and modafinil, in addition to anticholinesterase inhibitors and memantine. 10 Although this previous research identified signals of disproportionate reporting of drug abuse and dependence as defined in the Reporting of a Disproportionality Analysis for Drug Safety Signal Detection Using Individual Case Safety Reports (READUS) Statement, 11 significant knowledge gaps still exist, particularly regarding recent practices in the medical management of ADHD and the use of the concerned drugs.

Here, we assessed the relative potential of drugs used for ADHD to generate spontaneous reports of abuse and dependence by conducting a disproportionality analysis of the VigiBase® data.

METHODS

This case–noncase study (also called a disproportionality analysis) explored publicly available and deidentified data and required no patient informed consent or ethics committee approval. The study was performed following the READUS reporting guideline for disproportionality analyses. 11

Data source

Data originate from VigiBase, the international vigilance database developed and maintained by the Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC). VigiBase records individual case safety reports (ICSRs) from the national Pharmacovigilance centres of 136 member countries of the WHO Programme for International Drug Monitoring. 12 In the present study, access to VigiBase was provided through the VigiLyze system (https://who‐umc.org/vigiverse/vigilyze/) as part of the contribution to this database through the regulatory vigilance activity of Toulouse Centre.

As of December 2023, VigiBase included nearly 36 500 000 individual records (figure 1). Besides data on the report itself including dates, country of reporting and reporter qualification, ICSRs have information on (1) patients (age and sex); (2) suspect or concomitant drugs recorded under the WHO Drug Global terminology, an international dictionary for conventional and herbal medicinal product information maintained by the UMC; and (3) adverse drug reactions (ADRs) classified according to the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA), with several classification levels from the lowest level term and preferred term to the high‐level group term and system organ class. 13 Data extraction was performed on 13 December 2023.

Study sample

ICSRs were included in the primary analysis when they (1) were registered between 1 January 2002 and 30 June 2023; (2) referred to paediatric and adult patients with known sex and aged ≥ 15 years; (3) involved one suspect drug used for ADHD (designated below as ‘drugs of interest’); and (4) had the following variables completed: country of reporting, reporter qualification and ADR seriousness. Records before 2002 were not explored because the quality of data recorded in VigiBase® has markedly improved over time and there was no justification to make comparisons with older cases. 14

Drugs of interest

Active ingredients were considered for all drugs of interest. Only ICSRs with drugs of interest recorded as ‘suspect’ in the ICSR were included. These were ‘centrally acting sympathomimetics’ of the Anatomical, Therapeutic, Chemical (ATC) classification system, except methamphetamine (Supporting Information S1: Section S1). 7 The latter is available in the United States by prescription under the trade name Desoxyn® but its distribution as a prescription drug is rare 15 and ICSRs recorded in VigiBase are more likely related to the recreational use of illicit methamphetamine than its medicinal use for ADHD. By contrast, bupropion was included among drugs of interest although not classified as ‘centrally acting sympathomimetics’ (ATC code N06AX12), given both its mechanism of action and off‐label use for ADHD.

All extracted ICSRs were searched for exposure to drugs of interest. Based on their pharmacological properties and known clinical effects, it was expected that disproportionate reporting of abuse and dependence would be demonstrated for methylphenidate but not atomoxetine. These drugs were thus considered as positive and negative controls, respectively, and methylphenidate was used as the reference drug in all statistical analyses.

Definition and selection of cases and noncases

Cases were defined as reports with an ADR belonging to the narrow definition of the Standardised MedDRA Query (SMQ) ‘Drug abuse and dependence’, a sub‐SMQ of the ‘Drug abuse, dependence and withdrawal’ SMQ. The detailed list of items included in this SMQ is presented in Supporting Information S1: Section S2. In the absence of other signs of abuse/dependence, ADRs corresponding to withdrawal (e.g., abrupt cessation of use in a habituated person) were outside the scope of this study. Noncases corresponded to reports within the study sample with any other ADRs except drug abuse and dependence.

Data analysis

All key variables assessed in the present analysis are described in Supporting Information S1: Section S3.

The characteristics of ICSRs were described as percentages and compared according to their ‘case’ or ‘non‐case’ status, using Chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test when appropriate, for all variables except age and completeness score, the two quantitative variables of the dataset, described using median, first and third quartiles (Q1 and Q3, respectively).

The main statistical analysis was a disproportionality analysis. Disproportionality studies allow the identification of pharmacovigilance signals of disproportionate reporting, which are considered as such when an ADR of interest is reported with a specific drug in a higher proportion than expected in the absence of an association between the specific ADR and the specific drug. 16 We estimated the reporting of ADR symptoms of abuse and dependence in patients exposed to each drug of interest, compared to those exposed to methylphenidate, through the calculation of reporting odds ratios (RORs) and the corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), with a significance threshold of 1. ROR is a measure of disproportionality calculated by dividing the number of cases with each drug of interest over that of cases with the reference drug (numerator) by the number of noncases with each drug of interest over that of noncases with the reference drug (denominator). In each model, drugs associated with fewer than 15 cases or noncases were not considered. Multivariable logistic regression models were used to obtain ROR estimates adjusted for age, sex, seriousness, reporter qualification and continent of reporting. Individual covariate coefficients are not informative for disproportionality assessment and were therefore neither interpreted nor reported.

Several sensitivity analyses complemented the primary analysis. The first used the broad scope of the ‘Drug abuse and dependence’ SMQ. In the second, US data were excluded. Indeed, reporting practices may significantly differ depending on the country of reporting with a potential impact on any analysis conducted at the international level, and particularly in the United States, the country providing the highest number of ICSRs in VigiBase with, for example, over 40% of reports received in 2022. 17 The possibility of a temporal bias was also considered by restricting the analysis to ICSRs recorded from 2007, when the most recently approved drugs (i.e., lisdexamfetamine and armodafinil) were made available for the first time (Supporting Information S1: Section S1). Finally, data from France were excluded in an additional sensitivity analysis given the higher proportion of abuse/dependence reports, especially with methylphenidate, originating from this country than other reports.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (free software) version 4.5.0 (R Core Team, 2025; https://cran.r‐project.org).

RESULTS

The selection process of ICSRs is presented in figure 1. Among the 56 858 ICSRs with at least one drug used for ADHD, 1473 (2.59%) reported two drugs of interest and 166 (0.29%) reported three drugs or more. The remaining 55 219 ICSRs with one drug of interest (97.12%) constituted the study sample and included 2633 cases of drug abuse and dependence (table 1). The median age was 33 (Q1: 22–Q3: 44) years among cases and 38 (26–51) years among noncases. All compared characteristics are detailed in table 1. When compared to noncases, reports of drug abuse and dependence with drugs used for ADHD more often concerned male subjects (52.30%) in the 15–25 age range (33.61%), were serious (89.29%) and were reported by health professionals (78.54%). Besides, they were reported more frequently in the Americas than in Europe, with the United States, France and Germany as the primary countries of reporting (table 1).

The primary disproportionality analysis showed that, compared with methylphenidate, all drugs except lisdexamfetamine exhibited a significantly lower relative reporting of abuse and dependence (figure 2). For lisdexamfetamine, the ROR was 1.06 (95% CI 0.91–1.24), with no significant difference from methylphenidate. The drug with the lowest ROR was atomoxetine (ROR: 0.36; 95% CI 0.29–0.44), followed by armodafinil (0.38; 95% CI 0.24–0.57), dexamphetamine (0.42; 95% CI 0.27–0.61), modafinil (0.48; 95% CI 0.37–0.61), bupropion (0.72; 95% CI 0.64–0.81) and amphetamine salts (0.77; 95% CI 0.64–0.91). There was no report of abuse and dependence with solriamfetol and only 8 reports for dexmethylphenidate (table 1).

DISCUSSION

Main findings

This study aimed to assess the relative potential of medications used for ADHD for generating spontaneous reports of abuse and dependence. To our knowledge, it is the first study to exhaustively and directly compare the reporting rates of these events across the different drugs used for ADHD based on international safety reporting data and using disproportionality analyses.

Overall, most cases occurred in individuals aged 15–37 years, which aligns with the well‐known concentration of nonmedical psychostimulant use among young adults and even adolescents. 18 In addition, a higher proportion of cases involved males, whereas females were more frequent in the overall sample, consistent with evidence that men more often engage in high‐risk patterns of stimulant misuse. 19 , 20

Limitations

This study has limitations that must be considered when interpreting the results. Importantly, VigiBase data cannot provide prevalence rates of safety risks as the dataset used is based on pharmacovigilance records with all included subjects having had at least one ADR. 21 The disproportionality analysis method enables quantifying specific safety signals relative to the background noise of all reports collected for a given drug. Consequently, our analyses do not provide comparisons of direct risks for abuse and dependence among the different drugs used for ADHD but rather estimate a comparative potential for generating signals of abuse and dependence, estimated by measuring the proportion of ADRs of interest with a specific drug. 22 Also, several biases may affect the reporting of ADRs. First, spontaneous reporting habits vary between reporters depending on their professional background, experience, practical indication of the medications and knowledge of reporting systems. As the indication is not directly accessible in VigiBase, it is possible that some drugs may have been used in other indications than ADHD, with more reports of abuse or dependence by professionals in this field, compared to those working on ADHD. For example, as lisdexamfetamine is also used for binge‐eating disorder, if professionals working in this field more easily report abuse or dependence, it would bias the signals of abuse and dependence for lisdexamfetamine, relative to those of other drugs. Second, reporting is also influenced by local regulation (with impact on prescription drug use and access) or practices. The example of the over‐representation of clozapine‐induced myocarditis reported from Australia is illustrative of diverse practice modalities across countries; these cases systematically undergo intensive cardiac monitoring during titration, unlike elsewhere. 23 Third, although most national systems rely on spontaneous reporting, their functioning differs, potentially affecting the levels of reporting. Although low‐income countries generally differ from high‐income countries in this respect, significant variations can also exist among countries with similar socioeconomic levels. 24 Thus, our results appear to be sensitive to the inclusion or exclusion of data from certain countries, particularly the United States and France, which have distinct pharmacovigilance and addictovigilance systems. This suggests that differences in reporting practices may substantially influence the observed signals, and to some extent, our findings may partly reflect national reporting behaviours rather than pharmacological differences between drugs. The French Addictovigilance Network (FAN) serves as an example of how national reporting systems can differ. Unlike most national systems relying on pharmacovigilance, this country has a specific system dedicated to the monitoring of adverse reactions due to any drugs with abuse potential, whether they are medications or illicit substances. 25 In the current analysis, France is the second highest contributor of abuse/dependence reports after the United States, accounting for 8.96% of the total. For comparison, 63.62% of these reports come from the United States. Notably, France reports a higher percentage of cases involving methylphenidate, with 34.31% of reports (n = 222) compared to 19.01% in the United States (n = 123), and indeed, the FAN identified a warning signal concerning methylphenidate as early as the early 2010s. 3 Fourth, reporting may be biased by temporality, including release in the media, communications from health authorities or the Weber effect, which corresponds to an increased reporting of ADRs in the first years after drug marketing authorisation followed by a decrease, especially concerning nonserious ADRs. This might explain the slightly higher ROR of lisdexamfetamine in the analysis excluding data from the United States, where the drug was approved in 2007, whereas it was made available progressively from 2012 in some European countries. Nevertheless, the risk of misinterpretation is low as this measurement is close to significance. In general, RORs near the threshold should be interpreted with caution, given the inherent uncertainty of estimates close to 1. Finally, the choice of methylphenidate as the control drug should be addressed. Methylphenidate was the natural option, as it is the oldest and most‐studied medication for ADHD. However, it has raised a longstanding awareness about misuse among prescribers, pharmacists and public health stakeholders. This could explain why this drug emerged in our study with the highest reporting rates of abuse and dependence, together with lisdexamfetamine. However, this should not impact the results of the indirect comparisons between other medications allowed by our results.

Despite methodological limitations, disproportionality studies are central to identifying warning signals on unknown ADRs from pharmacovigilance data earlier than conventional approaches, including clinical trials and pharmacoepidemiological studies. 26 In the present study, we selected a frequentist rather than a Bayesian approach. Although these comparisons may not be fully generalisable to VigiBase, comparative assessments performed across reporting systems, including studies based on the US adverse event reporting system, have shown higher sensitivity and earlier signal detection for frequentist methods such as the ROR. 16 Moreover, ROR calculations allow multivariate logistic regression, which we used to adjust for all relevant variables. Besides, disproportionality studies explore real‐life data among a broad population of drug users. The present study provides the first exhaustive comparison of reporting rates of abuse and dependence among drugs used for ADHD. The robustness of the primary results was further explored by sensitivity analyses with converging results.

Implications: Comparative reporting for abuse and dependence among drugs used for ADHD

Our study found that lisdexamfetamine does not exhibit lower signals of abuse and dependence compared to methylphenidate and may even present a slightly higher level when excluding data from the United States or France, two countries which exhibit specific features regarding safety reporting in VigiBase. In contrast, dexamphetamine was associated with a reduced potential for generating reports of abuse/dependence, compared to methylphenidate. These findings do not align with the common assertion that lisdexamfetamine has a lower risk of abuse and dependence compared to dexamphetamine. 27 Indeed, lisdexamfetamine has a unique pharmacokinetic profile, as the metabolisation of this prodrug by peptidases associated with red blood cells prevents the rapid release of dexamphetamine into the bloodstream, a difference from other amphetamine‐like drugs. 28 Furthermore, preclinical studies assessing the abuse potential of ADHD medications—conducted for all new medicinal products active on the central nervous system as per requirements from international healthcare agencies such as the US Food and Drug Administration and the European Medicines Agency—indicated that lisdexamfetamine (and modafinil) did not exhibit reinforcing effects in rats, unlike dexamphetamine and methylphenidate. 29 However, more recently, a literature review concluded that the available evidence indicates that the prodrug mechanism and pharmacokinetic profile of lisdexamfetamine do not reduce the oral abuse liability of lisdexamfetamine compared to immediate‐release dexamphetamine. 30 Also, another review on lisdexamfetamine abuse potential called for pharmacoepidemiological studies to assert this potential from real‐life data. 27 Therefore, our results suggest that increased caution is warranted regarding the potential for abuse/dependence of lisdexamfetamine, while also implying a relative safety for dexamphetamine.

In this study, we found that atomoxetine had the lowest potential for raising signals of abuse and dependence. This supports our initial hypothesis and aligns with previous reports indicating that atomoxetine (included in this study as a negative control) carries a low, if not negligible, risk of abuse. 31 Some other medications, such as modafinil and armodafinil, and to some extent, dexamphetamine, showed reporting of abuse and dependence that was somewhat comparable to atomoxetine. Modafinil has previously been associated with a lower risk of abuse compared to methylphenidate. 32 By contrast, the findings regarding dexamphetamine may appear surprising, as this drug was theoretically expected to generate similar or even higher reporting of abuse and dependence than methylphenidate, as amphetamine‐like derivatives are generally considered to induce more abuse than nonamphetamine psychostimulants. 6 For the same reasons, more frequent reporting of abuse/dependence could have been expected with amphetamine salts. Nonetheless, our analyses consistently indicated that dexamphetamine and amphetamine salts were associated with fewer reports of abuse and dependence compared to methylphenidate and lisdexamfetamine. Bupropion also exhibited a lower risk of reporting abuse and dependence compared to methylphenidate (except in two sensitivity analyses). Bupropion was initially considered to have a limited potential for abuse, but growing evidence indicates that cases of abuse and diversion are not uncommon. 33

Specific interpretation should be exercised with drugs that are seldom used outside the United States, as highlighted by sensitivity analyses indicating rare cases for armodafinil, dexamphetamine or amphetamine salts when data from this country are excluded. Amphetamine salts (Adderall®) are by far the most prescribed medications for ADHD in adults in the United States, with a marked increasing trend of use over recent years. 34 Moreover, based on the annual data on the licit movement of psychotropic substances, this country is in a unique situation at the international level, with a markedly high consumption level of all stimulants, including amphetamine, dexamphetamine and methylphenidate. 15

CONCLUSIONS

Compared to the reference drug methylphenidate, all drugs except lisdexamfetamine were associated with lower rates of reports of abuse and dependence, whereas, as hypothesised, atomoxetine was the drug with the lowest ROR for abuse and dependence. The finding that lisdexamfetamine showed a reporting rate of drug abuse and dependence similar to that of methylphenidate had not been described previously, given that this drug is generally considered to have a reduced abuse/dependence potential. Taken together, these findings advocate for more studies comparing the abuse potential of lisdexamfetamine, particularly with methylphenidate or dexamphetamine.
