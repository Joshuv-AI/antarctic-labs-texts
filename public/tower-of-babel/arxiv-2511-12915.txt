Quantitative blow-up suppression for the Patlak–Keller–Segel(–Navier–Stokes) system via Couette flow on ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2}
Yubo Chen, Wendong Wang, Guoxu Yang

Abstract
. It is well known that solutions to the Patlak–Keller–Segel system on ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} blow up in finite time if the initial mass exceeds 8 ​ π 8\pi . 1.
Introduction

Consider the following Patlak–Keller–Segel (PKS) system coupled with the Navier–Stokes (NS) equations on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
:

(1.1)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}n+v\cdot\nabla n=\Delta n-\nabla\cdot(n\nabla c),\\
\beta(\partial_{t}c+v\cdot\nabla c)=\Delta c+n-\alpha c,\\
\partial_{t}v+v\cdot\nabla v+\nabla p=\Delta v+n\nabla\phi,\\
\nabla\cdot v=0,\end{array}\right.

where
n
denotes the microorganism density,
c
the chemoattractant density and
v
the velocity of the fluid. Moreover,
p
is the scalar pressure,
\alpha\geq 0
,
\beta\geq 0
, and
\phi
is a given potential function accounting the effects of external forces. For simplicity, we focus on the parabolic-elliptic case
\beta=0
and
\phi(x,y)=y
with initial conditions

\left.(n,\,v)\right|_{t=0}=(n_{\mathrm{in}},\,v_{\mathrm{in}}).

1.1.
Developments

If
v=0
and
\phi=0
, the system (
1.1
) is reduced to the classical Patlak–Keller–Segel system, and the origin of the PKS model is a classic narrative of interdisciplinary science derived by Patlak
[
43
]
and Keller–Segel
[
30
]
. There exists a vast body of literature on the classical PKS system, and we briefly summarize some relevant developments. The parabolic-elliptic PKS system in 2D is globally well-posed if and only if the initial mass
M:=||n_{\rm in}||_{L^{1}}\leq 8\pi
; see Blanchet–Dolbeault–Perthame
[
9
]
or Wei
[
52
]
for the details. When
M=8\pi
, the solution may blow up at infinity, see Blanchet–Carrillo–Masmoudi
[
8
]
; see also Davila–del Pino–Dolbeault–Musso–Wei
[
20
]
for the blow-up rate at infinity. The 2D parabolic-parabolic PKS model also has a critical mass of
8\pi
; see Calvez–Corrias
[
10
]
and Schweyer
[
46
]
. In higher dimension (
d\geq 3
), the solution with any initial mass may blow up in finite time; see Nagai
[
40
]
, Souplet–Winkler
[
48
]
, and Winkler
[
55
]
.

Since the chemotactic processes often take place in a moving fluid, it is interesting to consider the mixing effect of the fluid.

Without coupling the fluid equations.

With an additional advection term modeling
ambient fluid flow,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}n+v\cdot\nabla n=\Delta n-\nabla\cdot(n\nabla c),\\
-\Delta c=n-\bar{n},\end{array}\right.

Kiselev–Xu
[
31
]
showed the
8\pi
critical mass threshold vanishes, as the blow-up can be suppressed by stationary relaxation enhancing flows and by Yao–Zlatos flows in
\mathbb{T}^{d}
(
d=2~\text{or}~3
). Also, Shi–Wang
[
47
]
considered the suppression effect of the Couette-Poiseuille flow
(z,z^{2},0)
in
\mathbb{T}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
. For the parabolic-parabolic PKS system, He
[
27
]
introduced a family of time-dependent alternating shear flows in
\mathbb{T}^{3}
, and proved the solution remains globally regular as long as the flow is sufficiently strong. As for the shear flow
v
, Bedrossian–He
[
3
]
obtained the suppression effect of blow-up by non-degenerate shear flows in
\mathbb{T}^{2}
for the parabolic-elliptic case. He
[
25
]
investigated the suppression of blow-up for the parabolic-parabolic PKS model near the large strictly monotone shear flow in
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
. Deng–Shi–Wang
[
21
]
proved the Couette flow with a sufficiently large amplitude prevents the blow-up of solutions in the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
for exponential decay data. For the parabolic-parabolic PKS system near a time-dependent shear flow, He
[
28
]
demonstrated the total
mass of the cell density is below a specific threshold (
8\pi|\mathbb{T}|
) in
\mathbb{T}^{3}
.

With coupling the fluid equations.

For the coupled PKS–NS system, Zeng–Zhang–Zi
[
56
]
firstly considered the 2D PKS–NS system near Couette flow in
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
, and proved that the solution stays globally regular provided that the Couette flow is sufficiently strong. He
[
26
]
considered the blow-up suppression for the parabolic-elliptic PKS–NS system in
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
with the coupling of buoyancy effects for a class of initial data with small vorticity.
Wang–Wang–Zhang
[
51
]
studied the blow-up suppression of 2D supercritical parabolic-elliptic PKS–NS system near the Couette flow in
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}.

Li–Xiang–Xu
[
35
]
suppressed the blow-up for the PKS–NS system via the Poiseuille flow in
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}.

Furthermore, Cui–Wang
[
15
]
considered the blow-up suppression for the PKS–NS system in
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{I}
with Navier-slip boundary condition.
For the 3D PKS–NS system, Cui–Wang–Wang
[
17
]
considered the PKS system coupled with
the linearized NS equations near the Couette flow in
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{I}\times\mathbb{T}.

Then, they
[
16
]
also studied the blow-up suppression and the nonlinear stability of the PKS–NS system via the Couette flow in an unbounded domain
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T},
and proved that the solutions are global in time as long as the initial cell mass
M<\tfrac{24}{5}\pi^{2}
. Recently, when the total mass of the cell density is below a specific threshold (
8\pi|\mathbb{T}|
), Cui–Wang–Wang–Wei
[
18
]
obtained the solution remains globally regular in
\mathbb{T}^{3}
. Moreover, by combining the effects of the Couette flow and the logistic source, Cui–Wang–Wang–Wei–Yang
[
19
]
obtained the solution remains bounded globally in
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{I}\times\mathbb{T}
with no-slip boundary condition.

It is worth noting that the above results primarily concern periodic domains, where the enhanced dissipation plays a crucial role. A natural question then arises:

Do similar conclusions hold for non-periodic domains?

Next we investigate these issues. Motivated by the stability threshold established in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by Arbon–Bedrossian
[
1
]
and Li–Liu–Zhao
[
34
]
, we consider the Patlak–Keller–Segel system coupled with the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. It is easy to verify that
(\tilde{n},\tilde{c},\tilde{v})=(0,0,U)
with the Couette flow
U=(Ay,0)
is a stationary solution to the system (
1.1
). Denote the perturbation
u(t,x,y)=v(t,x,y)-U(y)
, then
(n,c,u)
satisfies

(1.2)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}n+Ay\partial_{x}n+u\cdot\nabla n-\Delta n=-\nabla\cdot(n\nabla c),\\
-\Delta c=n-\alpha c,\\
\partial_{t}u+Ay\partial_{x}u+\binom{Au_{2}}{0}+u\cdot\nabla u-\Delta u+\nabla p=\binom{0}{n},\\
\nabla\cdot u=0,\end{array}\right.

with initial conditions

\left.(n,\,u)\right|_{t=0}=(n_{\mathrm{in}},\,u_{\mathrm{in}}).

Define

\omega=\partial_{y}u_{1}-\partial_{x}u_{2},\quad u=\nabla^{\perp}\Phi=\left(\partial_{y}\Phi,-\partial_{x}\Phi\right),

then

\Delta\Phi=\omega

and
\omega
satisfies

\partial_{t}\omega+Ay\partial_{x}\omega-\Delta\omega+u\cdot\nabla\omega=-\partial_{x}n.

After the time rescaling
t\mapsto A^{-1}t
, one can rewrite the system (
1.2
) by

(1.3)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}n+y\partial_{x}n-\frac{1}{A}\Delta n=-\frac{1}{A}(\nabla\cdot(n\nabla c)+u\cdot\nabla n),\\
-\Delta c=n-\alpha c,\\
\partial_{t}\omega+y\partial_{x}\omega-\frac{1}{A}\Delta\omega=-\frac{1}{A}\left(\partial_{x}n+u\cdot\nabla\omega\right),\\
u=\nabla^{\perp}\Delta^{-1}\omega.\end{array}\right.

Similarly, without coupling the fluid equations, we have

(1.4)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}n+y\partial_{x}n-\frac{1}{A}\Delta n=-\frac{1}{A}\nabla\cdot(n\nabla c),\\
-\Delta c=n-\alpha c.\end{array}\right.

1.2.
Stability mechanisms

Note that in a sense, the global well-posedness of the systems above is similar to the stability threshold analysis of the Navier–Stokes equations around the Couette flow:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\partial_{t}u+y\partial_{x}u-\frac{1}{Re}\Delta u+u\cdot\nabla u+\nabla p=0,\\
\nabla\cdot u=0,\\
u|_{t=0}=u_{\rm in}(x,y).\end{array}\right.

The transition threshold problem was formulated by Bedrossian–Germain–Masmoudi
[
2
]
as follows:
Given a norm
\|\cdot\|_{X}
, determine a
\gamma=\gamma(X)
so that

\displaystyle\left\|u_{\rm in}\right\|_{X}\leq Re^{-\gamma}\Rightarrow\text{ stability, }

\displaystyle\left\|u_{\rm in}\right\|_{X}\gg Re^{-\gamma}\Rightarrow\text{ instability. }

The exponent
\gamma
is referred to as the transition threshold. It is worth pointing out that the enhanced dissipation effect and the inviscid damping effect of the Couette flow are of great importance to stabilize the NS equations at high Reynolds numbers (i.e.,
Re=\nu^{-1}
, where
\nu
is the fluid viscosity coefficient).
The stability of the 2D Couette flow for the NS equation has been widely studied. Let us briefly recall some related results. When
\Omega=\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
, if the perturbation for vorticity is in Gevrey-
s
with
s<2
, Bedrossian–Masmoudi–Vicol
[
5
]
showed that the solution is stable for
\gamma=0
. Bedrossian–Vicol–Wang
[
6
]
proved that Couette flow is stable in Sobolev norms for
\gamma\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
by Fourier multiplier method. The sharp case of
\gamma\leq\tfrac{1}{3}
was proved by Masmoudi–Zhao
[
37
]
and Wei–Zhang
[
54
]
, respectively. In
\mathbb{T}\times[-1,1]
,
\gamma\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
was proved by Chen–Li–Wei–Zhang
[
11
]
with no-slip boundary condition by establishing some sharp resolvent estimates. When
\Omega=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
, Arbon–Bedrossian
[
1
]
obtained
\gamma\leq\tfrac{1}{2}+
and Li–Liu–Zhao
[
34
]
improved their result to
\gamma\leq\tfrac{1}{3}+
, which was generalized to the Boussinesq setting by the authors in
[
13
]
. For further references,
we refer the reader to
[
53
,
12
,
22
]
and the references therein.

Different from the domain
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
, one needs to be careful when dealing with the horizontal frequency in the Fourier space, because of the singularity as the horizontal frequency tends to zero. We will briefly introduce these two stability mechanisms. In the enhanced dissipation estimate, the dissipation time scale is
O\left(\nu^{-{1}/{3}}\right)
, which is much faster than the standard heat dissipation time scale
O\left(\nu^{-1}\right)
for small
\nu
. Clearly the dissipation rate is inhomogeneous and depends on the frequencies
k
. The phenomenon of enhanced dissipation has been widely observed and studied in physics literature (see, e.g.,
[
7
,
44
,
50
]
). It has recently attracted enormous attention from the mathematics community and significant progress has been made. One of the earliest rigorous results on the enhanced dissipation is obtained by Constantin–Kiselev–Ryzhik–Zlatos
[
14
]
on the enhancement of diffusive mixing. Inviscid damping is analogous to Landau damping in plasma physics found by Landau
[
32
]
. For general shear flows, establishing linear inviscid damping remains a challenging problem, while the nonlinear inviscid damping is even more difficult to prove. Inspired by the groundbreaking work of Mouhot–Villani
[
39
]
on nonlinear Landau damping, Bedrossian–Masmoudi
[
4
]
established nonlinear inviscid damping for the Couette flow in the domain
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
. We refer to
[
41
,
38
,
29
,
23
]
and the references therein for more progress.

Remark 1.8
.

The above theorems contain an anisotropic norm

\|f\|_{Y_{m,\epsilon}}=\|\langle D_{x}\rangle^{m}\langle\frac{1}{D_{x}}\rangle^{\epsilon}f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},\quad 0<\epsilon<\tfrac{1}{2}<m.

Expressed in Fourier variables, the weight function is defined as

W(k)=(1+|k|^{2})^{m}(1+|k|^{-2})^{\epsilon}.

At high frequencies (
|k|\gg 1
), it behaves asymptotically as
W(k)\sim|k|^{2m}
, thereby enforcing
m
-th order regularity in the
x
-variable.
Conversely, at low frequencies (
|k|\ll 1
), the asymptotic form
W(k)\sim|k|^{-2\epsilon}
penalizes spectral concentration near
k=0
, thus suppressing large-scale modes in
x
. Since
2\epsilon<1
, the singularity is integrable and does not exclude broad classes of
L^{2}
functions. In contrast to the isotropic Sobolev space
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with its symmetric weight
(1+|k|^{2}+|\xi|^{2})^{s}
, the
Y_{m,\epsilon}
-norm is anisotropic: it specifically targets the
x
-direction while simultaneously controlling both high and low frequencies in
x
. In fact, the embeddings hold:

\|f\|_{H_{x}^{s}L_{y}^{2}}\lesssim\|f\|_{Y_{m,\epsilon}},\quad-\epsilon\leq s\leq m.

Thus, the norm controls a full interval of regularities in the
x
-direction. Some typical classes contained in
Y_{m,\epsilon}
include:

•

Schwartz functions
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,

•

Gaussian functions and other rapidly decaying profiles,

•

derivatives
f=\partial_{x}g
with
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
(vanishing of
\widehat{f}
at
k=0
offsets the low-frequency weight),

•

L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
functions with
x
-zero-average.

Nonzero constants, however, are excluded (they are also not in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
).

or

Remark 1.10
.

One difficulty lies in estimating the Fourier frequencies. When handling the nonlinear terms, one encounters convolutions in the horizontal frequency, which require careful scaling to overcome singularities. Inspired by
[
34
,
13
]
, one can adopt their approach for the transformations among
k
,
l
, and
k-l
. The integration domain is divided into three regions based on the relative sizes of
|k|
and
|k-l|
:

•

Near-resonant region (
\frac{|k-l|}{2}\leq|k|\leq 2|k-l|
), where
|l|
is “smallest” and
|k|\sim|k-l|
;

•

High-low interaction region (
|k|>2|k-l|
), where
|k-l|
is “smallest” and
|k|\sim|l|
;

•

Low-high interaction region (
2|k|<|k-l|
), where
|k|
is “smallest” and
|k-l|\sim|l|
.

This classification allows one to handle each case efficiently by exploiting the relative sizes of the frequencies.

2.1.
Construction of the multipliers

For
k,\xi\in\mathbb{R}
and
A>0
, denote that

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{1}(k,\xi)

\displaystyle:=\arctan\left(A^{-\frac{1}{3}}|k|^{-\frac{1}{3}}\operatorname{sgn}(k)\xi\right)+\frac{\pi}{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{2}(k,\xi)

\displaystyle:=\arctan\left(\frac{\xi}{k}\right)+\frac{\pi}{2}.

Then, these two self-adjoint Fourier multipliers satisfy

(2.1)

1\leq\mathcal{M}:=\frac{\mathcal{M}_{1}+\mathcal{M}_{2}}{2\pi\Xi^{-1}}+1\leq 1+\Xi,

where
\Xi>0
, to be decided.
The enhanced dissipation multiplier
\mathcal{M}_{1}
and the inviscid damping multiplier
\mathcal{M}_{2}
are motivated by
[
22
,
54
]
.
On the Fourier side, these multipliers can be understood as some kind of ‘ghost weight’, which are bounded weights providing additional dissipation properties. One crucial feature of the two multipliers mentioned above is that

(2.2)

2\Re\big(\left(\partial_{t}+y\partial_{x}\right)f\mid\mathcal{M}_{i}f\big)=\frac{d}{dt}\|\sqrt{\mathcal{M}_{i}}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}k\partial_{\xi}\mathcal{M}_{i}(k,\xi)|\hat{f}|^{2}dkd\xi,

for a sufficiently smooth function
f=f(t,x,y)
on
(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For a sufficiently smooth function
f=f(t,x,y)
on
(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
and the Fourier multiplier
\mathcal{M}
defined in (
2.1
), it holds that

(2.3)

2\pi\Xi^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}k\partial_{\xi}\mathcal{M}(k,\xi)|\hat{f}|^{2}dkd\xi\geq\frac{\theta_{1}}{A^{\frac{1}{3}}}\|\left|D_{x}\right|^{\frac{1}{3}}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\frac{\theta_{2}}{A}\|\partial_{y}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\partial_{x}\nabla\Delta^{-1}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

where
0<\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\leq 1
satisfying
\theta_{1}\leq 2\sqrt{\theta_{2}}-\theta_{2}.

Proof.

Let
0<\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\leq 1
, then we have

\frac{1}{1+x}\geq\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}x,\qquad\forall\,x\geq 0,

provided that
\theta_{1}\leq 2\sqrt{\theta_{2}}-\theta_{2}.

Direct calculation shows that

k\partial_{\xi}\mathcal{M}_{1}(k,\xi)=\frac{A^{-\frac{1}{3}}|k|^{\frac{2}{3}}}{1+A^{-\frac{2}{3}}|k|^{-\frac{2}{3}}|\xi|^{2}}\geq\frac{\theta_{1}}{A^{\frac{1}{3}}}|k|^{\frac{2}{3}}-\frac{\theta_{2}}{A}|\xi|^{2}

and

k\partial_{\xi}\mathcal{M}_{2}(k,\xi)=\frac{k^{2}}{k^{2}+\xi^{2}},

which imply (
2.3
).
∎

2.2.
Elliptic estimates of
c

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{N}(D_{x},D_{y},t)
be a Fourier multiplier. Then, for
c
and
n
satisfying
\eqref{eq:main}_{2}
and
t\geq 0
, the following estimates hold.

Case of
\alpha=0
:

\|\nabla^{2}\mathcal{N}c\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|\mathcal{N}n\|_{L^{2}},\quad\|\nabla c\|_{L^{4}}\leq\frac{2}{\pi}(3+2\sqrt{2})\big(\|n\|_{L^{2}}+M\big);

Case of
\alpha>0
:

\|\nabla\mathcal{N}c\|_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\alpha}}\|\mathcal{N}n\|_{L^{2}},\quad\|\nabla c\|_{L^{4}}\leq\left(\frac{1}{2\pi\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\|n\|_{L^{2}}.

Proof.

Case of
\alpha=0
.
Recalling
\eqref{eq:main}_{2}
, one gets

\displaystyle-\Delta\mathcal{N}c=\mathcal{N}n.

Then, by

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}\mathcal{N}c\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\|\Delta\mathcal{N}c\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}\mathcal{N}c\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\|\mathcal{N}n\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

From
[
49
]
(see the formula (1)), we get

\|\nabla c\|_{L^{4}}\leq\frac{1}{\pi}\|\nabla^{2}c\|_{L^{\frac{4}{3}}}.

Then, using Lemma
A.1
and the interpolation inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\nabla c\|_{L^{4}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\pi}\|\mathbf{R}_{i}\mathbf{R}_{j}n\|_{L^{\frac{4}{3}}}\leq\frac{4}{\pi}(3+2\sqrt{2})\|n\|_{L^{\frac{4}{3}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{\pi}(3+2\sqrt{2})\|n\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|n\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\frac{2}{\pi}(3+2\sqrt{2})(\|n_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{L^{1}}+\|n\|_{L^{2}}).

Case of
\alpha>0
.
It follows from
\eqref{eq:main}_{2}
that

\displaystyle\alpha\mathcal{N}c-\Delta\mathcal{N}c=\mathcal{N}n.

Therefore,
L^{2}
energy estimate implies

\displaystyle\|\Delta\mathcal{N}c\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+2\alpha\|\nabla\mathcal{N}c\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\alpha\mathcal{N}c\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\|\mathcal{N}n\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

and then (see the formula (1.11) in
[
33
]
)

\|\nabla c\|_{L^{4}}\leq\pi^{-\frac{1}{4}}\|\nabla^{2}c\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla c\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq(\frac{1}{2\pi\alpha})^{\frac{1}{4}}\|n\|_{L^{2}}.

The proof is complete.
∎
