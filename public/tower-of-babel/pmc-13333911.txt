Patients and donors trust in data authorization for AI-based medical research
Authors: Lorenzo Grimaldi, Eleonora Bovi, Silvia Tagliente, Carlo Caltagirone, Emiliano Giardina, Giulia Sancesario
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractArtificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly being applied to biomedical research and public healthcare. However, concerns regarding security are arising, due to risks such as data leaks. This study investigates the perspectives of neurological donor patients (dNP), healthy biobank donors (dHV), and non-donor volunteers (ndHV) on the use of their clinical, biochemical and genetic data in the context of AI-powered biomedical research. A total of 115 subjects were surveyed (21 dNP, 17 dHV and 77 ndHV) on self-reported digital literacy; willingness to authorize data use (clinical, biochemical, genetic) for AI; acceptance of AI support in healthcare in diagnostic and therapeutic settings; use of data for AI training in medical research, by using supervised or non-supervised methods, inside or outside the European data space. Across all groups, high familiarity with digital technologies, low knowledge of AI risks, and strong optimism towards potential outcomes of AI were observed, together with high concern regarding data misuse among dNP with respect to ndHV and dHV (65, 48, and 20%, respectively; p < 0.05). A strong correlation between high digital competence and both increased risk awareness (p = 0.0003) and marked optimism regarding research outcomes (p = 0.0009) were observed. Most participants (92% of dNP, 94% of dHV and 82% of ndHV) would disclose the use of at least one kind of data, but ndHV were significantly less prone regarding the use of all types of data (28%) with a significant difference for only genetic (1%), with willingness to share clinical data significantly increased with higher level of digitalization (p = 0.008). In clinical setting, dNP are statistically favorable towards authorizing data for the adoption of AI for diagnostic (p = 0.04) and therapeutic (p = 0.02) decisions. Furthermore, ndHV were less likely to authorize data use for AI training, particularly for unsupervised AI. Results show the attitudes in authorizing the use of own data for AI based medical research seems to be linked to digital competence, but also to trust and clinical engagement. To facilitate the transition towards a healthcare system that leverages AI, health policies should not be limited to bridging the digital divide, but also actively invest in strengthening the doctor-patient relationship and ensuring transparency in care processes and data use in research.Keywords: Patient data privacy, Artificial intelligence, AI- powered research, Public healthcare, Biobank, GDPRSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Implementation of artificial intelligence (AI)-powered tools and algorithms into biomedical research and public healthcare has significantly increased in recent years, thanks to huge opportunities for data analysis and interpretation1. These improvements emerge from the ability of neural networks and machine learning (ML), the foundational architectures of AI models, that, if properly trained, permit the processing of large quantities of data to abstract new insights2,3. To date, the most propulsive areas of application of AI include complex diseases, for example neurodegenerative disorders and cancer, due to their growing incidence and enormous impact in terms of economic and social costs. For example, Alzheimer’s disease (AD) alone is estimated to cost the global economy over $1 trillion annually, a figure expected to rise as populations age4, considering overall costs for direct medical care, long-term care, and lost productivity5, while cost of cancer on global healthcare is estimated to be $25.2 trillion in the period from 2020 to 20506. The application of AI to data and metadata derived from “-omics” sciences, such as genomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics, is particularly crucial, as it is expected to enhance our understanding of multifactorial disease and allow us to uncover complex and individual-specific biological profiles. For example, the study of how the genome as a whole contributes to the evolution, development, structure and function of the nervous system7, enables a deeper investigation of the genetic underpinnings of various neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative disorders, such as AD, Parkinson disease (PD), schizophrenia, autism spectrum disorder, etc. The potential of genetic screening for reaching early detection and prevention of neurological diseases also holds promise for reducing the societal burden of these conditions8–10. Furthermore, multi-omics integration may facilitate the development of precision medicine, where treatments are tailored to an individual’s profile, thereby improving drug efficacy and minimizing adverse effects11,12. In this context, the insights of AI-powered research are pivotal for advancing diagnostic methods, developing targeted therapeutic interventions, and identifying individuals at elevated risk.

Despite the concrete advantages and growing expectations, AI is still a developing phenomenon, and a stark difference in expertise exists between those who implement and develop AI tools and the subjects to whom the data belongs. Integration of AI-driven technologies requires the training of end users such as medical professionals, clinicians and nurses to ensure the proper interpretation of the results and prevent biases or errors. Training personnel is particularly important in the case of employment of large language models (LLM) such as ChatGPT as ‘’symptom checkers’’ or annotators, as the presence of possible non-sensical hallucinations represents a concrete risk for patients13. Data leaks, improper or unauthorized use of data to train new AI algorithms are only a few of the hazards that are involved in combining healthcare data and AI. Most importantly, when compared to other categories of data, such as biometric or biological ones, genetic data has characteristics that make it unique, as it can reveal not only kinship ties but also the presence of genetic mutations or variations that increase the risk of diseases. Such information can represent a high level of impact on the life of a user and should therefore be handled carefully14.

Legal analyses state that current laws are inadequate to protect individuals’ control over their data. Although statutes like the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) in the United States of America (USA) provide some protections, few legal doctrines or enactments offer meaningful control to individuals over the disclosure or use of their information15. The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in the European Union (EU), which calls for purpose limitation of collected data, data minimization, special treatment of sensitive data and limitation on automated decision, regulates ‘traditional’ use and collection, without considering newer AI technologies, that have been developed for purposes that were not considered when the GDPR was written. These concerns are amplified in spaces where users voluntarily share results of analyses of their genetic data, sometimes through apps or social-media platforms, without fully understanding downstream uses or risks15.

Recent literature highlighted growing concerns about genetic privacy in the era of mobile apps, social media, and consumer-oriented genomics: on one hand, healthcare-based apps that offer diagnostic or data-driven services, on the other, apps that provide information, entertainment and education on users’ ethnic heritage and genealogy, e.g. Ancestry, MyHeritage, 23andMe, Family Tree DNA, and Living DNA have become popularized in recent years. Among 754 apps surveyed, only 22 met the basic eligibility criteria for genomic patient-facing apps, and of those, only 36% had a declared privacy policy for handling sensitive data16. Most of these apps provide education or information of genetic heritage nature, while other few offer diagnostic or data‐analysis features (analyzing genetic data or scanning for risk-related genetic factors), and very few were affiliated with medical or health charities, which raises issues of oversight and protection of genetic privacy rights16.

Given that AI development relies heavily on accumulating extensive datasets, regulating this aspect of data collection and storage is fundamental for protecting patients and users from the risks of AI misuse. However, despite all the protective measures, risks remain to the rights and freedoms of the people to whom the data belong. Recently, the bidirectional nature of trust in AI-assisted healthcare systems has been highlighted, where both patients and healthcare providers must trust AI systems, while these systems rely on the quality of human input to function effectively17.

Considering the subtle equilibrium between the beneficial outcomes and hazards of AI-driven innovation in healthcare, we focused on capturing the opinions of a cohort of subjects on the topic of data use for AI-powered research. With a questionnaire, we evaluated the general attitudes and digital alphabetization of biobank donors (d-), including neurological patients (dNP) and healthy volunteers (dHV). As a control, a group of healthy subjects, who did not participate in the donation (nd-) of data or biological material for research purposes, was also included (ndHV).

This study highlighted the attitudes of biobank donors and neurological patients, who authorized data sharing for “traditional use,” regarding future AI applications, such as use of AI as a medical aid in clinical and therapeutic decisions, as well as authorization of data use for training AI ML algorithms for future medical research. Further, we focused on the expectations and fears of subjects regarding AI and aspects of genetic confidentiality, such as authorization of the use of genetic data, oriented toward protection of patient privacy. We also assessed the participants’ perspectives on the possibility of improving patient outcomes through AI-enhanced diagnostics and course of treatment. By analyzing these attitudes, our goal was to discuss the approach of public medical professionals and entities, such as biobanks, towards the implementation of AI based technologies, in light of the perspectives of patients and healthy subjects on data privacy tutelage.

Materials and methods

Study setting and subject recruitment

This study was conducted by surveying a total of 115 subjects, including a general population and a group of biobank donors. In particular, the participation was proposed to a total of 38 consecutive donor subjects who referred to the Biobank at IRCCS Santa Lucia Foundation, Rome, Italy, during June and September 2025. Specifically, 17 were healthy biobank volunteers (dHV) and 21 donor patients with neurological disorders (dNP). Moreover, 77 no donor-volunteers (ndHV) were interviewed, which included subjects referring to hospital for routine medical check, with no reported severe pathologies, who did not undergo blood withdrawal nor biobank donation. All participants in the survey were adult subjects and patients over the age of 18, who were able to give consent. Neurological diseases among dNP included stroke, multiple sclerosis, mild cognitive impairment, PD, traumatic brain injury, cerebrovascular diseases, and others. Patients temporarily or permanently not able to consent, and below 18 years of age were excluded from the study.

Questionnaire structure and administration

The survey has been developed according to GDPR standards. All participants were thoroughly informed through counselling by the biobank personnel and an informative consent module detailing the purpose of the study and data collection, use of data, procedures for data anonymization, and dissemination of results14,18. All participants were reminded that they always retained the option to opt out of answering any question and declining to participate altogether.

The questionnaire was structured in four distinct parts: General Information; Sect. 1- Knowledge of digital spaces and AI; Sect. 2- Use of AI for medical research purposes; Sect. 3- Use of AI in standard procedures of diagnosis and cure and in machine learning training.

General Information includes questions on gender, age, and donor/no donor categories. To prioritize data minimization and reduce the risk of subject identification, a question about the subjects’ level of education was deliberately omitted. Instead, a question was included to assess the subjects’ familiarity with the use of AI.

In Sect. 1, subjects were asked to rate on a five degree scale their opinions on a range of topics: (i) familiarity with digital spaces and electronic devices; (ii) understanding of the risks correlated with AI in healthcare; (iii) level of optimism on the possible results that can be obtained with AI in healthcare and (iv) concern on the use of sensitive data with AI.

In Sect. 2, participants expressed their opinion on the use of AI in biomedical research. Briefly, participants were asked if they had ever participated in any type of clinical research or donation to a biobank (i), in order to assess their familiarity with the topic, and whether they would disclose the use of their own data to AI or AI-related activities (ii), with the opportunity of choosing among the following typology of data (iii): clinical (anamnestic, clinical and instrumental tests, etc.), biochemical (hematological, etc.) and genetic (DNA, etc.). In the case a subject would prefer not to disclose the use of clinical-biochemical or genetic data, they were specifically asked to select one or more options among a list of possible reasons, ranging from concerns regarding the discovery of genetic risk factors, general distrust of AI in handling sensitive data, fear of being identified through their genetic data, etc. (iv-v).

Lastly, in Sect. 3, subjects were asked to answer questions on specific applications of AI tools for supporting diagnosis (i), under specialist medical control, for example for cellular patterns by microscopy, electroencephalogram or electrocardiogram interpretation, and for defining therapies (ii). Once more, if subjects answered negatively to one or both use of AI in diagnostic or curing setting, they were asked to point to a reason among the following (iii): doubts on the quality of AI based diagnoses, lack of clear responsibilities in protocols that involve AI, fear of data violation or misuse, general moral or ethical doubts on AI, or other reasons. Finally, subjects were enquired on the possible use of their data for training new AI through ML protocols in clinical and drug research. Specifically, we asked if the subject would be willing to share their data (clinical, biochemical or genetic) with the objective of training AI to: a public (iv), human-supervised project; to a public, unsupervised project (v), or to a “a particularly innovative” private study carried out in a foreign country that did not share the same data privacy rights as the participants’ (vi).

Data collection and statistical analysis

Data collected through the General Information section included: participant preferred gender (male, female, other/prefer not to say), age (< 30; 31–45; 46–55; 56–61; 62–85 years old); category of participant (patients, healthy biobank donor volunteer, caregiver, healthy non-donor volunteer). No other health data or medical records related to pathologies were collected. Significant age or sex distribution differences between groups were calculated with χ2 statistical analysis.

Data analysis and visualization was carried out with Microsoft Excel Office. For quantitative answers (namely questions i-iv of Sect. 1), ANOVA analysis with post hoc tests was carried out to define intergroup significances, and non-numerical answers were analyzed using the χ2 statistical test.

Multivariate analysis was performed to identify factors independently associated with participants’ attitudes and levels of consent using Python (version 3.12.12). Specifically, multiple linear regression models were employed for continuous variables (Sect. 1), while binary logistic regression models were used to analyze dichotomous outcomes (Sects. 2 and 3). All models included age, digital literacy level, and participant group (dNP, dHV, and ndHV) as covariates. For the logistic models, Odds Ratios (OR) and their corresponding 95% Confidence Intervals (CI) were calculated. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

A total of 115 subjects were surveyed. Of the total, 21 (18%) were dNP (52% female, 48% male; mean age of 59.5 years ± 16.3); seventeen (15%) were dHV (47% female, 41% males and 12% did not disclose; mean age of 63.97 years ± 12.63). Finally, 77 (67%) were ndHV (58% female, 40% male and 2% did not disclose; mean age of 42.43 years ± 20.6) (Table 1). Globally, biobank donors were 33% of the total cohort.

Section 1: knowledge of digital spaces and AI

The first section was designed to ascertain the participants’ general familiarity with digital spaces, i.e. the digital literacy, and the relative concerns or expectations of AI-powered tools in healthcare. Most of the participants in all the groups showed a high (score of 4–5 out of 5) familiarity with digital spaces and electronic devices, particularly 52% of dNP, 60% of dHV and 58% of ndHV answered positively. Instead, 19% of dNP, 33% of dHV and 30% of ndHV showed a medium (score of 3) level of familiarity and 19% of dNP, 7% of dHV and 12% of ndHV a low (score of 1–2) level.

In regards to the understanding of the risks correlated with AI in healthcare, across all the groups, approximately one in three participants reported a medium score (score of 3) (33% for dNPs, 27% for dHV, and 31% for ndHV); dNP and ndHV mainly reported a low level (score 1–2) (38% and 42% respectively), with only 29% of dNPs and 27% of ndHV reported a high score (4–5). Only in dHV, about half of participants showed a high perception of AI risks (53%), while the lower scores (1–2) were given by 20% of dHV (Table 2).

Section 2: use of data for AI powered medical research purposes

In this Section, we investigated the participants’ opinion regarding authorization for data use in AI-driven medical research. The first question specifically focused on the potential agreement to share clinical data for healthcare purposes, in “protected form”, meaning patient identification is prevented through data pseudonymization, that is a generation of code to protect the subjects’ identity. In all groups the majority would authorize such use, in particular 80% dNP, 100% dHV and 49% of ndHV (Table 3). However, 10% of dNP and 34% of ndHV answered negatively, whereas 10% of dNP and 17% of ndHV chose to not respond. χ2 statistical analysis showed that the positive trend of all three groups to answer positively was statistically significant χ2(4, N = 115) = 16.15, p = 0.0028 (p < 0.005).

Authorization for clinical, biochemical and genetic data use in AI-powered research

Then, subjects were tasked with specifically indicating, in the event of explicit consent for AI use, which type of data they would disclose, namely clinical, biochemical, genetic, or all or none. Of note, the majority of participants in all groups would disclose the use of at least one kind of their own data for AI-powered research (92% of dNP, 94% of dHV and 82% of ndHV).

Regarding the specific typology of data, we found significant differences among groups χ2(8, N = 115) = 21.55, p = 0.006 (p < 0.01). A significant amount of dHV were open to sharing each type of data (64%). Among dNP, a significant difference can be observed in the percentage of subjects willing to disclose only genetic data with respect to the other groups (12%). Finally, ndHV were significantly less prone to disclose the use of all data (28%), with a high significant difference for only genetic data (1%), with respect to both dNP and dHV (Fig. 4).

We did not observe differences regarding the other categories of data among the groups. In detail, 16% of dNPs, 12% of dHV and 29% of ndHV answered that they would disclose the use of only clinical data; for biochemical data, 16% of dNPs, 12% of dHV and 24% of ndHV would be in favor of disclosure, while 6% of dHV would disclose only genetic data. Furthermore, 48% of dNP chose to disclose all of the three types of data; while 8% of dNP, 6% of dHV and 18% of ndHV would not disclose any kind of data (Fig. 4).

Among the reasons given by subjects who chose not to disclose the potential use of their clinical or biochemical data (dNP = 2 participants, dHV = 1; ndHV = 25), general concerns about data privacy (n = 2 dNP), suspicions on the reliability of AI in healthcare (64% of ndHV respondents), the possibility of someone identifying the subject from shared data (36% of ndHV respondents), and the possibility that subject data could be handled by unsupervised AI models (2%) were expressed. Finally, the only dHV responder indicated a lack of trust in unsupervised AI models.

Similarly reasons were given by subjects who chose not to disclose the potential use of their genetic data (dNP = 2 participants, dHV = 1; ndHV = 14), as fear of any type of misuse of their data by subjects not related to biomedical research (n = 2 dNP), concern about possible consequences on their privacy, such as identity theft (93% of ndHV respondents), and the possibility of someone identifying the subject with the shared data (7% of ndHV respondents), while the only dHV responder cited the fear of the data being used by unsupervised AI models.

Correlation between digital literacy and authorization for data sharing

A binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to assess the factors influencing the propensity to authorize the use of clinical, biochemical and genetic data for AI purposes. This analysis revealed significant associations across all three data categories. Specifically, the willingness to share clinical data was found to significantly increase with a higher level of subject digitalization (p = 0.008). For biochemical data, dHV demonstrated a significantly higher propensity for sharing compared to other groups (p = 0.040). Regarding genetic data, being a dHV or a dNP were the only factors significantly influencing the propensity to authorize use (p = 0.003 and p = 0.018, respectively), irrespective of the subject’s age.

Section 3: use of AI in supporting medical procedures and for AI-training

Section 3 was designed to evaluate participants’ willingness to use AI by physicians, as support for clinical diagnostics and for therapeutic decisions. Further, we asked participants if they would allow their data to be used for AI training, in clinical and drug discovery research involving human supervision.

AI in medical clinical setting for diagnosis and cure

All participants were asked whether they supported the use of AI by healthcare professionals for diagnostic purposes, which means the possibility of integrating different types of clinical information. The majority of participants in all groups answered positively (86% of dNP, 93% of dHV and 63% of ndHV), while the negative answer was chosen only by 18% of ndHV. Lastly, 14% of dNP, 7% of dHV and 19% of ndHV not respond (Table 4; Fig. 5a).

When we specifically asked the opinion regarding the AI use for supporting therapeutic decisions, we found significant differences χ2(4, N = 115) = 58.21, p < 0.0001 (Fig. 5a) between ndHV (55%) who were significantly less prone to authorizing such use compared to 86% of dNP, 93% of dHV. Only 24% of ndHV and no dHV and dNP chose the negative option, whereas 14% of dNP, 7% of dHV and 21% of ndHV refrained from answering (Table 4; Fig. 5b). Subjects who abstained or did not answer these questions (8 ndHV) stated doubts on the quality, control and reliability of AI results (75% of responses) and general ethical doubts on the use of AI in healthcare (25% of answers), while dNP and dHV did not indicate any motive at all.

Logistic regression analysis revealed that being a patient is the only significant predictor of favorability towards AI for diagnostic (p = 0.04) and for therapeutic decisions (p = 0.02). No significant influence of age was found, suggesting that therapeutic need and trust in the physician act as factors that overcome the generational gap.

Data use for human supervised or non supervised AI-training

The final set of questions specifically explored attitudes toward disclosing the use of subject data for developing new AI tools, encompassing both public and private projects, with a particular emphasis on the involvement of human supervision and data curation. For public, human-supervised projects, a significant difference χ2(4, N = 115) = 26.08, p = 0.00003 was observed between dNP and dHV participants, who were more inclined to authorize data use, and ndHV participants, who were considerably less so (86% of dNP, 93% of dHV, and 68% of ndHV; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 5c). Specifically, 14% of dNP and 16% of ndHV opted against disclosure, while 7% of dHV and 16% of ndHV did not answer.

A significant difference in openness to data disclosure was observed in the question enquiring about an unsupervised public program for AI training. 79% of dHV expressed strong willingness to disclose their data, while 59% of ndHV chose not to authorize such use χ2(4, N = 115) = 22.42, p = 0.0002 (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5d). When questioned about data sharing, 33% of dNP participants consented, while 62% declined, and 5% did not respond. Among dHV participants, 21% declined to answer. Conversely, 22% of ndHV participants authorized data sharing, with only 19% abstaining from responding.

Lastly, in the case of the use of AI for an innovative private study for a new drug, carried out in a foreign country, subjects’ opinions were sensibly divided χ2(4, N = 115) = 46.41, p < 0.0001 (Fig. 5e) with dNP and ndHV sharing a general distrust of such use and significantly choosing the negative answer more than the others (43% and 63% respectively), whereas the dHV substantially opted that they would be open to such use (57%). In detail, 33% of dNP answered that they would share their data and 24% abstained from answering; dHV responses were distributed in 29% choosing to not authorize to this use and 14% not disclosing any answer; answers from ndHV were 16% choosing the positive answer and 21% declining to answer.

Correlation between propensity to data authorization for human supervised or non supervised AI-training

A logistic regression analysis investigated the willingness of all groups to authorize data for AI training, showing significant differences in risk perception and openness to technology. Concerning the use of AI in unsupervised systems, dNP showed greater caution, whereas dHV were the only group to display significantly higher openness to authorizing data use for AI integration (p = 0.002). This openness in dHV was not explained by demographic factors like age or self-rated digitalization levels. A greater propensity to authorize extra-EU data transfer for use in AI-based “particularly innovative private study” was statistically driven by dHV (p = 0.004) and dNP (p = 0.02). Conversely, ndHV remained significantly more skeptical of this practice, irrespective of their reported digital skills. Of note, regression analysis could not be conducted on the propensity to consent to provide data for the training of human-controlled algorithms due to an overly skewed distribution of sample data for this specific variable.

Discussion

The future of biomedical research relies heavily on AI methodologies, given the development of various high-throughput technologies capable of generating large amounts of data, making the contribution of ML to their analysis essential and indispensable. Thus, the synergy between AI and holistic sciences has proven to offer substantial and tangible improvements to public healthcare, with patients having the opportunity of benefitting from personalized medicine19. For example, in cancer care the integration of AI and big data analytics has steered the field towards an increase in data-driven decision making for cancer treatment. While this represents a concrete advancement, understanding of key concepts surrounding AI, big data, confident use of digital health platforms and interpretation of the outputs of data-driven interventions represent limiting factors for proper application of AI20. Thus, this poses massive attention on potential risks in terms of data use, donor awareness and about the impact on healthcare21.

This single- center survey focuses on the perspectives of patients and healthy volunteers regarding the authorization for the use of data, for AI. A cohort of patients and subjects donating data and biological material for biomedical research were questioned about the use of digital technologies and the potential application of AI tools to their personal medical data, including clinical, biochemical, and genetic information. Moreover, we explored the attitudes regarding implementation of AI support in medical decisions, both in diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. Finally, the potential of new AI training was evaluated, involving human supervision or not. Then, we compared the opinions with those of a population of healthy subjects who were not involved in any medical research study and had never donated blood or data to a biobank. Finally, we assess relationships between factors, such as digital familiarity, and the attitudes and trust expressed by individuals concerning the authorization of their data for AI use. While this study may sample a population that is sensibly smaller than similar studies22,23, its focus on the opinions of healthy and neurological donor of the biobank, and healthy volunteers may disclose interesting information.

Our results show all groups generally expressed strong confidence in modern technologies and digital devices, alongside optimism about AI’s potential achievements. However, most participants across all groups demonstrated low awareness and knowledge regarding the risks of AI in medical applications. Finally, concerning fear of data misuse, e.g. theft, dNP perceived a greater risk compared to both donor and non-donor healthy volunteers (d- and ndHV). This may be due to the clinical condition and a sense of vulnerability which are common in neurological patients. In fact, patients with neurological diseases often experience coexisting disabilities such as cognitive impairment, visual disturbances, or dementia, which can render them permanently or temporarily unable to make decisions about their own health. These frail patients represent a high-risk group for which it is essential to ensure a robust and transparent consent process for the collection and management of biological materials, particularly for future research14.

Interestingly, in our population age is not a barrier to authorization for the use of data with AI in the medical field. In none of the potential fields of applications examined, from consent to data use in assisted diagnosis up to authorization to genetic data use, was age found to be a significant predictor of refusal or skepticism. Likewise, digital literacy - the self-reported level of competence and familiarity with the use of electronic devices, not specifically on knowledge of AI - while positively influencing knowledge of risks and optimism, showed a marginal impact on practical decisions and willingness to share the most sensitive data, with exception for clinical data.

However, we found a significant positive attitude of almost all subjects regarding the use of AI in medical research, reaching 100% in the dHV group. We also observed the same general positive attitude when analyzing the preferences expressed by subjects on the typology of data (clinical, biochemical, genetic, all or none) to be shared. Indeed, the majority of participants in all groups would disclose the use of at least one kind of their data for AI-powered research (92% of dNP, 94% of dHV and 82% of ndHV), whereas 64% of dHV, 48% of dNP and 28% of ndHV willing to share every kind of proposed data. Interestingly, attitudes towards sharing only genetic data for AI related biomedical research was the sparsest of each group (12% of dNP, 6% of dHV and 1% of ndHV). This is in stark contrast with what can be observed in the attitudes of participants towards disclosing all typologies of data. We might hypothesize that this pattern is due to subjects’ general mistrust of sharing their genetic data individually, which represents a sensitive and delicate source of personal information. Interestingly, of the three groups, dNPs were the most likely to share only their own genetic data, and accordingly, they were also very concerned about the misuse of their data, as well as less aware of the risks associated with AI. This specific profile may seem contradictory, on the one hand authorizing the use for genomic studies, on the other perceiving a high risk and little knowledge of the risks. This may be partly explained by the fact that dNPs have been involved in biomedical research as well as in clinical trials, and through informed consent they have been made aware of the importance of the data for scientific progress, its purpose, and its limitations. In this context, patients develop a relationship of trust with doctors and researchers. Therefore, dNP involvement in research through sharing genetic data is accompanied by greater optimism, although vulnerabilities regarding risk perception remain14. Furthermore, results suggest greater digital knowledge correlates with more positive attitudes toward authorizing the use of clinical data with AI tools. This is of great importance considering the growing diffusion of Electronic Health Records (EHR), which gather clinical, pharmacological, and self-reported lifestyle data, and the opportunity of utilizing this data source for epidemiological and other research studies employing AI.

With respect to other general medical and biochemical data, genetics provide a unique, life-long source of information with significant implications for an individual’s health, family, and even legal matters, offering insights into disease predispositions, ancestral connections, and, in forensics, identification purposes24. Anonymization or pseudonymization of patient data is particularly challenging in the case of genomics, especially considering that, apart from identical homozygote twins, a genome identifies a single subject. Genomic data is therefore particularly sensitive as it holds intimate information belonging to the subject, ranging from familial relationships to the presence of genetic risk-related factors. In the specific case of neurological diseases, data may indicate the presence of gene variants that are linked to increased risk of degenerative disorders, or different clinical phenotypes with worse outcome, as is the case of the apolipoprotein E (ApoE) variants and their related risk of early onset AD or PD, among other neurological conditions25–27. Unauthorized or accidental disclosing of this kind of information to the public or even to the subject themselves could lead to severe psychological and socio-economic consequences18,25,28. Accordingly, we found dNP, which chose not to disclose the potential use of any data, mainly indicating a fear of any kind of misuse by parties not tied to biomedical research. In contrast, ndHV expressed concern about the possible consequences for their privacy, such as identity theft. These results are in accordance with a recent empirical analysis of thematic issues in AI-integrated healthcare systems, in which survey responses show that, although respondents generally have a positive view of digital technology, their attitudes toward AI integration are more cautious. This is evidenced by a higher percentage of respondents adopting a negative or neutral attitude toward AI integration compared to other digital technology applications29.

Attitudes towards the integration of AI support in medical decision-making were also examined, encompassing both diagnostic and therapeutic uses. Notably, the majority of participants across all groups responded positively. Specifically, 86% of dNP and 93% of dHV fully agreed with AI use for both diagnostic and therapeutic medical decision support. In contrast, ndHV exhibited significantly less openness to using AI for therapeutic reasons (55%) but agreed with its use for supporting diagnosis (63%). According with previous studies, that have demonstrated the significant benefits of AI in nursing practice, as well as the ethical challenges it presents30, our findings reveal a crucial insight: while AI is considered a useful tool for supporting physicians in diagnosis identification, thanks to its ability to integrate data from various sources, when choosing a treatment plan, medical experience is favored over AI, resulting in lower trust in AI systems for this purpose. Recently, AI tools, notably large-language models like ChatGPT and Google’s Gemini, have promising potential to support clinicians in evaluating neurogenetic disorders, in particular for rare conditions, including Huntington’s disease, fragile X-associated tremor/ataxia syndrome, Becker muscular dystrophy, etc31. Moreover, ChatGPT can assist in simulating clinical cases, offering preliminary diagnostic or treatment guidance32, but with a sensible dependency of reliability of the results on the existing documentation of a disorder32. However, the diagnostic accuracy of AI tools has demonstrated to be only moderate (correct diagnosis in roughly two-thirds of cases with common presentations, but much lower with rare or atypical symptoms), and the risk of “hallucinations” (i.e. incorrect or misleading outputs) was non-negligible31. These findings suggest that AI tools can help reduce delays in diagnosis, improve access to genetic considerations in neurology, but they are not yet mature enough to replace human expertise, especially in handling atypical presentations or interpreting complex genetic information.

Finally, the potential of data use for new AI training was evaluated, involving human supervision or not. We observed differing attitudes towards authorizing data use for AI training between two groups, those who had previously donated biological material to a biobank (including both patients and healthy) and those who had not. In case of human supervised AI, ndHV were less inclined to approve data usage compared to other groups. Conversely, most ndHV and dNP participants largely disagreed with the use of unsupervised AI and the sharing of data outside the EU. On the other hand, dHV represents the group most in favor of data use for AI training, although the percentage of positive response was less than 60% in case of innovative study which requires data transfer outside the EU. In this study, all individuals who participate in biobanking and biomedical research receive specific and deep counseling from expert personnel, informing them about the purposes of donation and future research, together with the opportunity of asking questions regarding any doubts. Conversely, non-donors have not received this specific information but just information regarding the purpose of this questionnaire, resulting in a generally lower willingness to participate compared to donor patients and volunteers. Unfortunately, we were unable to assess ndHV propensity to participate in biobank donation, as we did not inquire about their willingness to do so.

Then, our results demonstrate that direct interaction with the care or clinical research setting, such as being a patient or a biobank donor, was the only factor found to be a statistically significant predictor for obtaining consent for the use of own health data in AI-based medical and research purposes. Informed consent (IC) is generally considered essential when a study involves human subjects, and this also applies when AI systems are integrated into clinical decision-making or when patient data are used in the development of such technologies, although specific legal requirements vary greatly across regions and contexts. Protocols for informing subjects and their caregivers should emphasize how and for what purpose their data is going to be used, the risks involved, and the potential for algorithmic errors or bias. Furthermore, an adequate information process should reassure patients and subjects not only that their data is being handled securely, but also explicitly state the actions taken to ensure anonymization and prevent misuse of data by third parties or researchers themselves33. This is particularly important when patient data is destined to be used to train new AI models based on supervised and unsupervised machine learning models, where a distinction is necessary due to the important implications. Supervised models, which are trained on labeled data and cured databases, are characterized by an easier validation process and thus are easily explained to clinicians and patients, guaranteeing a clearer disclosure of their intended use and limitations. In contrast, unsupervised models often result in neural structures without predefined outcomes, thus complicating interpretability and end use34. The opacity of these systems requires careful notice and explanation to end users, patients or research volunteers, in order to properly inform and protect against data misuse35. Evidences suggests that regardless of model type, patients are generally open to being informed whenever AI contributes to their care, as long as benefits, risks, and data use are explained in plain and direct language36,37.

According with literature, results of this study support the importance of maintaining transparency in AI decision-making processes, that means both patients and clinicians need to comprehend the reasoning behind AI-generated recommendations to effectively trust and utilize these technologies38. Furthermore, clear accountability for AI-driven decisions in patient care is essential to uphold ethical and professional standards39.

Therefore, our findings highlight that to facilitate the transition to a healthcare system that leverages AI, it is essential to actively invest in strengthening the doctor-patient relationship and ensuring transparency in care processes, beyond simply bridging the digital divide. This is because effective citizen adoption, and thus support for scientific progress, is primarily determined by trust in the healthcare system as a whole, rather than mere familiarity with the technology.

The results of this study confirm the need for detailed regulations on the use of data in AI research to ensure a correct and safe perception by the population to which the data belongs. The GDPR in the EU and HIPAA in the USA regulate how and when public or private organizations can collect, process, store, and transfer personal data. The HIPAA regulates AI applications in healthcare in the US and sets data privacy and protection standards for hospitals, healthcare professionals and their business partners. Interestingly, like the GDPR, HIPAA does not directly cite AI but rather upholds its privacy and security standards whenever AI handles personal health data. For example, an AI tool that predicts patient risks must ensure the data it uses is encrypted, limited to what is necessary, and not disclosed improperly40.

Recently, the AI Act (2024) in the EU has been defined, with the aim to regulate the development and application of AI in public healthcare through a risk-based approach41. In the AI Act, technologies such as chatbots that dispense advice on general wellbeing are considered low risk and therefore requires minimal requirements for their implementation (i.e. measures for AI literacy and transparency obligations); conversely, AI tools that aid in the clinical decision process are heralded as high risk and therefore users and developers are required to adhere to various strict measures such as enforcing anonymization and cybersecurity, but also ensuring proper quality assurance and record keeping protocols. Interestingly, the AI Act does not account for the use of algorithms for the generation of synthetic patient data, a currently developing application that could significantly accelerate medical research42. On March 2025, the European Health Data Space (EHDS) Regulation was published, establishing a unified framework for the use and exchange of electronic health data across the EU. The EHDS aims to improve individuals’ access to and control over their personal electronic health data, while also enabling the secure sharing of certain data and the secondary use of health and genomic data in AI research and development, integrating the provisions of the GDPR and the AI Act. Regulation (EU) 2025/327 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 11 February 2025 on the European Health Data Space and amending Directive 2011/24/EU and Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, Official Journal of the European Union, https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2025/327/oj. This represents a significant improvement for data-related policies, and together with an effort to promote public literacy and awareness regarding the risks of digital technologies, it can accelerate the achievement of innovative results in AI, while maintaining a focus on data protection.

The study presents some limitations, for instance, the absence of information on the participants’ level of education and general life habits, which can be associated with different levels of familiarity with digital space. Indeed, to prioritize data minimization and reduce the risk of subject identification, the questionnaire administered to volunteers and patients did not include a section on the level of education. Instead, we chose to focus on the subjects’ direct level of familiarity with electronic devices use. This specific information was deemed more relevant than general educational background for assessing familiarity with digital and AI-specific topics.

Furthermore, the participants in the group of ndHV were significantly younger than other groups, which are composed of healthy volunteers and neurological patients participating in research. However, multivariate analysis of the complete dataset (N = 115) demonstrated that the participants’ age was not a significant factor impacting any of the variables examined.

Future studies on larger populations may be conducted, to further verify whether data can be generalized. Specifically, incorporating questions about participants’ previous experience with digital health tools and their familiarity with, and perceptions of, real-world AI applications (such as ChatGPT or medical Large Language Models) would provide valuable context. This would allow for a better understanding of how tool-specific trust influences broader attitudes and overall trust levels.

Conclusions

The results of this survey indicate a generally positive attitude among almost all subjects, both biobank donors and healthy volunteers, toward the use of AI in medical research, including its applications in diagnosis and personalized medicine. Within this cohort, the acceptance of emerging AI-based technologies in medicine, and so the attitudes in authorizing the use of medical data, seems to be linked to digital competence, but, a crucial aspect, also to trust and clinical engagement. Therefore, to facilitate the transition towards a healthcare system that leverages AI, health policies should not be limited to bridging the digital divide; on the contrary, it is essential to actively invest in strengthening the doctor-patient relationship and ensuring the transparency of care processes, together with the implementation of specific National and International Data Regulations for AI.
