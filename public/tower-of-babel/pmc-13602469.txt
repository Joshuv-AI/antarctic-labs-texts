Aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) regulates organic dust and organic dust-associated bacterial extracellular vesicles-induced airway inflammation via ROS-dependent NFκB and STAT3 activation
Authors: Velmurugan Meganathan, Shilpa Kusampudi, Maxine Stenhouse, Vijay Boggaram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExposure to agricultural organic dust increases the risk of respiratory diseases. Aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), a transcription factor activated by environmental chemicals and endogenous metabolites, regulates immune and inflammatory responses, but its role in organic dust-induced lung inflammation is not known. Our study elucidated mechanisms by which AhR modulates bronchial epithelial cell inflammatory responses induced by poultry farm organic dust extract (DE) and organic dust-associated (OD) bacterial extracellular vesicles (EVs).MethodsThe role of AhR in the DE- and OD-bacterial EVs-mediated inflammatory responses in Beas2B bronchial epithelial cells was investigated using siRNA knockdown. AhR expression and nuclear translocation, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and inflammatory mediator (pro-IL-1β, ICAM-1, IL-6, IL-8) levels were evaluated by western immunoblotting, immunostaining, DCFDA labeling, real-time qRT-PCR, and ELISA. The induction of lung inflammatory cytokines by OD-bacterial EVs was assessed by ELISA in AhR knockout mice.ResultsDE and OD-bacterial EVs increased AhR protein expression and nuclear translocation in a time-dependent manner in Beas2B cells. AhR knockdown reduced ROS and NFκB activation but modulated STAT3 differentially, suppressing DE-induced activation while enhancing OD-bacterial EV-induced activation. Nevertheless, AhR knockdown attenuated the induction of inflammatory mediators by DE and OD-bacterial EVs. Inhibition of NOX, XO, NFκB, and STAT3 attenuated AhR increase by DE- and OD-bacterial EVs. Studies in AhR knockout mice demonstrated that AhR regulates lung inflammatory cytokines in a sex-specific manner.ConclusionAhR is a critical modulator of organic dust-induced lung inflammation, potentially driven by a positive feedback loop involving ROS and NFκB/STAT3. Targeting the AhR pathway offers a therapeutic strategy for respiratory diseases afflicting agricultural workers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12931-026-03908-8.Keywords: Organic dust, Bacterial extracellular vesicles, Aryl hydrocarbon receptor, Reactive oxygen species, Cytokines, Lung inflammation, NFκB/STAT3 signaling

Introduction

The industrialization of agriculture and animal farm operations worldwide has led to high density animal farming in confined spaces known as concentrated animal feeding operations (CAFOs) [1]. CAFOs generate high concentrations of aerosolized organic dust, which studies have linked to serious occupational respiratory illnesses including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma, hypersensitivity pneumonitis, bronchitis, and organic dust toxic syndrome [2, 3]. Organic dust is a complex mixture of animal dander, feed, excreta, mites, fungi, viruses, bacteria and bacterial products such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS), peptidoglycan, and toxic gases such as methane, hydrogen sulfide, and ammonia [2]. OD-bacterial EVs [4], and proteases [5–7] were recently found to contribute to the inflammatory responses elicited by organic dust. OD-bacterial EVs were found to be derived from Gram+ve (Firmicutes, Actinobacteria) and Gram-ve (Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes) bacteria [4]. They were found to be negative for β-actin by western blotting indicating that they may not contain chicken or mammalian EVs [4]. Our recent studies on the characterization of OD-bacterial EVs found that they contained LPS and lipoteichoic acid (LTA) [8]. Moreover, proteomic profiling found that they contained several bacterial immunomodulatory proteins such as Clostridium tyrobutyricum flagellin (TLR5 activator), lipoproteins from E. coli, Pseudomonas putida, and Salmonella typhymurium (TLR2 activators) and Hsp70 from Penicillium citrinum (TLR4 activator). Catalases from Staphylococcus xylosus and Micrococcus luteus and allergens such as ovalbumin and ovomucoid were also found (Kusampudi, S, Meganathan V, and Boggaram V., unpublished observations).

Human exposure to organic dust causes respiratory symptoms that are associated with increased levels of proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-8 (IL-8), tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), interleukin-1 (IL-1) α and IL-1β and increased inflammatory cell counts in the respiratory tract [9–11]. Mice exposed to organic dust extracts showed increased lung levels of IL-1β, IL-6, intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1), keratinocyte-derived chemokine (KC), and TNF-α [12, 13]. Treatment of lung epithelial and macrophage cells/cell lines in vitro with organic dust extracts increased IL-8, IL-6, pro-IL-1β, prostaglandin-endoperoxide synthase 2/ cyclooxygenase-2 (PTGS2/COX-2), and ICAM-1 protein levels [14–18]. It was reported that activator protein 1 (AP-1) and nuclear factor kappa-light-chain enhancer of activated B cells (NFκB) regulate the induction of IL-8 expression at the transcriptional level [18]. Moreover, signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) transcription factor was found to control the induction of IL-6, IL-8, pro-IL-1β, ICAM-1 and PTGS2/COX2 [13]. Pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) that recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), such as toll-like receptors (TLR), nucleotide-binding and oligomerization domain-like receptors have been found to mediate inflammatory responses by organic dust [19, 20]. Studies have shown that proteases present in organic dust acting via protease activated receptors (PARs) [5–7] and elevated intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) [6, 7] mediate the induction of inflammatory mediator expression. ROS generated by NADPH oxidases (NOXs) and xanthine oxidase (XO) have been found to be responsible for the organic dust induced inflammatory mediator expression [21]. Our recent study demonstrated that the endoplasmic reticulum stress and unfolded protein response sensor endoplasmic reticulum to nucleus signaling 1 (ERN1) acts as a positive regulator of organic dust induced bronchial epithelial cell inflammatory mediators [22]. Organic dust induced lung inflammation was found to be mediated by a signaling cascade involving secretory phospholipase A2-IIA/cytosolic PLA2/mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs) and generation of eicosanoid lipid mediators [23].

The aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) is a cytosolic transcription factor, belonging to the basic-helix-loop helix Per-Arnt-Sim (bHLH/PAS) family of transcription factors, that is activated by a variety of environmental chemicals and endogenous metabolites [24]. Activation of AhR by various ligands is known to control cell signaling pathways important for cellular homeostasis [24]. AhR in its nonactivated form is found in the cytosol bound by chaperone and co-chaperone proteins and upon binding to a ligand, AhR dissociates from its chaperones and is translocated to the nucleus [25]. In the nucleus, AhR forms a complex with AhR nuclear translocator (ARNT) to form a heterodimer that binds to promoter sequences known as aryl hydrocarbon responsive element/xenobiotic response element/dioxin-responsive element (AhRE/XRE/DRE) [26] to regulate target gene expression.

Although AhR is best known for its role in mediating the toxic effects of environmental organic pollutants, recent studies have shown that it is also an important mediator of immune and inflammatory responses modulating the pathogenesis of diseases [24]. The role of AhR in lung inflammation and development of respiratory diseases induced by organic dust has not been studied previously. Based on the important functions of AhR in the regulation of immune and inflammatory responses, we hypothesized that compounds in organic dust and OD-bacterial EVs activate AhR to modulate lung inflammation. Dust from poultry CAFOs was used as a model organic dust and aqueous extracts of organic dust (DE) and OD-bacterial EVs were employed to study the role of AhR in the regulation of induction of lung inflammation. Aqueous extracts of organic dust have been widely used to investigate cell and molecular mechanisms of organic dust induced lung inflammation [12, 13, 15–18].

Materials and methods

Chemicals and antibodies

AhR antagonist CH223191 (Cayman Chemical), AhR agonist 2,3,7,8-Tetrachlorodibenzo-p-dioxin (TCDD) (AccuStandard), indoleamine 2.3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO-1) inhibitor linrodostat (MedChemExpress), NFκB inhibitor Bay 11-7082 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), STAT3 inhibitor stattic (Tocris Bioscience), pan NOX inhibitor VAS2870 (Enzo Life Sciences), NOX-1 inhibitor ML171 (Tocris Bioscience), NOX-1/4 inhibitor GKT137831 (Cayman Chemical), XO inhibitor febuxostat (Sigma-Aldrich), transcription inhibitor triptolide (MedChem Express), and ROS indicator 2′, 7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFDA) (Sigma-Aldrich) were obtained and dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide. Primary antibodies used in the study included IL-1β ( # 12703, cell signaling), ICAM-1 (# sc-8439, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), human AhR (# 83200, Cell Signaling), mouse AhR (# MA1-513, Invitrogen), phospho-NFκB p65 (# 3033, Cell Signaling), NFκB p65 (# sc-372, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), phospho-STAT3 (# 9145, Cell Signaling), STAT3 (# 4904, Cell Signaling), and β-actin (# sc-47778, Santa Cruz Biotechnology).

Preparation of organic dust extract and isolation of bacterial extracellular vesicles

The method used for the preparation of dust extract is based on published studies investigating the effects of aqueous extracts of swine, cattle, and poultry farm dust samples [12, 15, 16, 18] on inflammatory responses in lung epithelial cells in vitro and mouse lungs in vivo. The dust extract was filtered through a 0.2-micron filter to sterilize the extract by removing bacteria, fungal spores, and large particulates present in the sample. This is necessary for removal of microorganisms which otherwise would contaminate cell cultures and skew experimental results. While this extract may not fully replicate the total dust profile within a poultry house, it contains the key inflammatory agents present in the environment including LPS [18], LTA [8], proteases [5, 7], bacterial EVs [4], and other yet-to-be-identified components. Aqueous extracts of organic dusts are preferred over organic dust particles as the particles present practical challenges for the treatment of in vitro cell cultures and mice. Dust particles may clump and not settle uniformly, and heavier particles may damage cells. For exposure of mice, clumps and large particles may clog the airways leading to suffocation.

Dust settled on vertical surfaces, such as walls, and window sills from poultry houses located in East Texas and Louisiana were obtained with consent when chickens were approximately 7–8 weeks old and stored at -70 °C. Dust (1 g) was mixed with 10 ml Dulbecco’s phosphate buffered saline (D-PBS) and extracted as described previously [18]. The resulting extract was arbitrarily termed as 100% DE and further diluted to 0.25% DE (v/v) for Beas2B cell treatment and 20% DE (v/v) for mouse treatment. OD-bacterial EVs were isolated from DE by ultracentrifugation as described previously, suspended in D-PBS and stored at -70 °C for future use [4]. The protein concentrations of DE and OD-bacterial EVs were measured by the Bradford assay using bovine serum albumin as the protein standard. The size distribution and quantification of bacterial vesicles were determined by nanoparticle tracking analysis using Nanosight NS300 instrument (Malvern Pan analytical).

Cell culture

Beas2B cells (ATCC CRL-9609) which are immortalized normal human bronchial epithelial cells, were grown on plastic culture dishes coated with fibronectin, bovine type I collagen, and bovine serum albumin in LHC-9 medium (Invitrogen) or BronchiaLife complete medium (Lifeline Cell Technology). Cells at ~ 80% confluence were maintained overnight in serum-free RPMI 1640 medium (Cytiva) before subjecting to the treatments.

Western immunoblotting

After treatments, cells were lysed in buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, and 15% glycerol) containing 1x protease inhibitor (MedChemExpress) (1.04 mM AEBSF, 0.8 mM aprotinin, 50 mM bestatin, 15 mM E-64, 20 mM leupeptin, and 15 mM pepstatin A) and phosphatase inhibitor (MedChemExpress) (25 mM bromotetramisole oxalate and 5 mM cantharidin) cocktails, and lysates were cleared by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm (15,890 x g) for 10 min. Equal amounts of protein (15–30 µg) were separated by SDS-PAGE using MOPS (Invitrogen) running buffer and protein bands transferred to PVDF membrane (Millipore, Immobilon). Protein bands were visualized by enhanced chemiluminescence detection using Bio-Rad Chemidoc MP Imager and quantified using Image Lab 6.1 software (Bio-Rad) and normalized to β-actin.

ELISA

Cytokine levels in cell culture medium and in mouse bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) were determined by enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (R and D Systems) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

RNA isolation and qRT PCR

Total RNA was isolated using TRI reagent (Molecular Research Center). RNA purity was assessed by measuring A260/A280 and A260/A230 ratios, while genomic DNA contamination was removed using Turbo DNase Kit (Invitrogen). RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using iScript cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-Rad). Real-time PCR was performed with TaqMan probes using Bio-Rad CFX96 real time PCR detection system and normalized to β-actin mRNA. The normalized gene expression data (ΔΔCt) relative to control sample arbitrarily set as 1 was obtained using CFX Manager software. TaqMan gene expression assays for human AhR (HS00169233, Applied Biosystems), human Cytochrome P450 1A1 (Cyp1A1) (HS01054797, Life Technology) and β-actin (HS01060665, Life Technology) were used in the study.

siRNA transfection

Beas2B cells (~ 40,000 per well) were plated on coated 6-well plastic culture dishes in 2 ml growth medium 24 h prior to transfection. Non-targeting control (SIC001) and AhR (HS02_0033218) siRNAs (Millipore Sigma) were transfected into cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After 6 h, medium was replaced with fresh growth medium and cells were grown for ~ 60 h. Prior to treatment with DE and OD-bacterial EVs, cells were washed twice with serum-free RPMI medium.

Confocal immunofluorescence microscopy

Beas2B cells (~ 10,000 per well) were grown to 60–70% confluence on coverslips (12 mm borosilicate glass) coated with fibronectin, bovine type I collagen, and bovine serum albumin in 24-well culture plates and medium was changed to serum-free RPMI 1640 medium prior to treatment. After treatment, cells were washed two times with cold PBS, then fixed using 2% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100. Cells were first incubated with AhR primary antibody overnight at 4°C and subsequently with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (Life Technologies) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were stained by incubation in mounting medium containing 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Southern Biotech). Images were captured with a confocal microscope (Zeiss). Fluorescence intensities of cells were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH).

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was measured by MTS assay (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Intracellular ROS measurement

Beas2B cells (~ 40,000 per well) were grown on coated 6-well plastic culture dishes. Following treatment, cells were incubated with 10 µM DCFDA for 30 min, washed twice with warm PBS, and maintained in warm PBS. Images were captured with ZOE fluorescence imager (Bio-Rad) and fluorescence intensities of cells were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH).

Mice

Animal studies (protocol number 758) had been previously approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. C57BL/6 and AhR knock-out mice (B6129-Ahrtm1Bra/J) were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory. AhR knockout mice were verified by genotyping by PCR according to the Jackson Laboratory protocol (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Additionally, absence of AhR protein was verified by western blotting analysis of lung homogenates (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Mice were maintained under a 12 h light /12 h dark cycle and provided a standard mouse diet and autoclaved water. Mice were acclimatized for at least one week before the treatments. Subsequent to deep anesthesia via intraperitoneal (i.p.) administration of ketamine: xylazine (100:8.5 mg/kg), the mice received 50 µl of endotoxin-free PBS, 50 µl of 20% DE, and 50 µl of PBS containing 1 µg OD-bacterial EVs (measured as protein) via intranasal instillation. After 3 h, they were euthanized via an i.p. injection of barbiturate euthanasia solution (5 µl /g) and exsanguinated by transecting the renal artery. Lungs were fixed by instillation of Excell Plus fixative (American MasterTech) via a canula inserted into the trachea under a constant hydrostatic pressure of 20 cm. After the trachea was tied off with suture thread, lungs were stored in the fixative for at least 48 h before paraffinization and sectioning. Separately, lavage was performed with D-PBS containing 2.6 mM EDTA as described previously [27] and BALF was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was stored at -70 °C. After BALF collection, mouse lungs were cleared of blood by injecting 10 mL D-PBS into the right heart ventricle and homogenized in cold 10 mM Tris-HCl 7.5 containing 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1x protease (1.04 mM AEBSF, 0.8 mM aprotinin, 50 mM bestatin, 15 mM E-64, 20 mM leupeptin, and 15 mM pepstatin A) and 1x phosphatase (25 mM bromotetramisole oxalate and 5 mM cantharidin) inhibitor cocktails and 1% Triton X-100 and homogenates were cleared by centrifugation for at 13,000 rpm (15,890 x g) for 10 min.

Immunohistochemistry

Mouse lung sections were processed for AhR staining using VECTASTAIN Elite ABC kit (Vector Laboratories) and ImmPACT DAB Peroxidase substrate (Vector Laboratories) according to the kit instructions.

Statistics

Statistical significance between two groups was analyzed by two-tailed paired or unpaired t-test, and that between multiple groups by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test, or two-way ANOVA followed by Sidak’s post-hoc test using GraphPad Prism 10.4.1. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

DE and OD-bacterial EVs increase AhR expression and AhR activation

To determine the effects DE and OD-bacterial EVs on AhR expression in Beas2B cells, we first studied their effects on AhR expression at protein and mRNA levels. Pilot experiments of the effects of DE (0.1, 0.25, 0.5, and 1%) and OD-bacterial EVs (0.1, 0.25, and 1 µg protein/ml) treatments for 6 h showed that DE and OD-bacterial EVs increased AhR protein levels in a concentration-dependent manner, with both 0.25% and 1% DE and 0.25 and 1 µg/ml OD-bacterial EVs causing similar increases (data not shown). Based on these results, we have used DE and OD-bacterial EVs at 0.25% and 0.25 µg/ml, respectively, in subsequent experiments. Significant increases in AhR protein levels were observed at 6 and 24 h after treatments with DE and OD-bacterial EVs, respectively (Fig. 1A - D). While DE increased AhR mRNA expression at 3, 6 and 24 h (Fig. 1E), OD-bacterial EVs showed increases at 3 and 6 h, however, AhR mRNA levels were significantly reduced at 24 h compared to 3 h (Fig. 1F).

Transcriptional inhibitor, triptolide suppressed DE and OD-bacterial EVs induction of AhR mRNA levels indicating regulation at the transcriptional level (Fig. 1G). Because AhR is ubiquitously expressed in mammalian tissues, we used immunohistochemical staining to identify lung cell types expressing increased AhR protein levels. Administration of DE and OD-bacterial EVs into mice increased AhR immunostaining predominantly in the bronchial epithelium and alveolar epithelial cells (Fig. 1H and I).

The effects on AhR activation were studied by tracking AhR nuclear translocation by confocal microscopy. In Beas2B cells treated with medium, medium containing 0.25% DE or 0.25 µg/ml OD-bacterial EVs for 1, 3, 6, and 24 h, a moderate increase in nuclear AhR staining was apparent after 3 h and the intensity of staining increased significantly with treatment time (Fig. 2A, B and D). Cytosolic AhR staining also increased with treatment time in cells treated with DE and OD-bacterial EVs (Fig. 2A, B and C). While cellular AhR staining in DE-treated cells increased significantly at 3 and 24 h of treatment, it increased with time for OD-bacterial EVs, but the increases were not statistically significant (Fig. 2C).

AhR acts as a positive regulator of inflammatory mediators

Due to the wide-range of environmental and endogenous AhR activators [28] and lack of specific antagonists, we used siRNA-mediated knockdown in Beas2B cells as an effective strategy to investigate AhR’s role in the induction of inflammatory mediators. AhR knockdown markedly reduced AhR protein levels (Fig. 3A - B and H - I) resulting in the downregulation of induction of pro-IL-1β, ICAM-1, IL-6, and IL-8 protein levels by DE (Fig. 3C – G) and OD-bacterial EVs (Fig. 3J - N). These data indicated that AhR functions as a positive regulator for the induction of inflammatory mediators. To confirm AhR’s functional role, we analyzed TCDD-induced Cyp1A1 mRNA expression in AhR knockdown cells and observed a reduction of more than 70% (Fig. 3P). TCDD induction of CyP1A1 expression is widely known to be mediated through AhR activation [29]. AhR knockdown did not affect Beas2B cell viability in control and DE/OD-bacterial EVs treated cells, indicating that the atteunation of induction of inflammatory mediators was not due to cell toxicity (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Among the environmental chemicals, dioxin and dioxin-like compounds are well known AhR activators and are widely distributed in the environment [28]. The presence of dioxin and dioxin-like compounds in DE and OD-bacterial EVs acting as AhR activators was assessed by testing the effect of CH223191, a selective AhR antagonist against halogenated aromatic hydrocarbons [30], on the induction of inflammatory mediators. Results showed no significant inhibitory effects of CH223191 on the induction of inflammatory mediators except a modest decrease in DE-induced ICAM-1 levels (Supplementary Fig. 3A - J). To ensure that CH223191 was functional, its effect on TCDD induction of Cyp1A1 mRNA was determined and found to block Cyp1A1 mRNA induction by greater than 90% (Supplementary Fig. 3K). We next investigated the role of kynurenine, a well-known endogenous AhR activator [31, 32], in AhR activation by DE and OD-bacterial EVs. Although primarily produced as a cellular tryptophan metabolite via host IDO-1, it can also be generated exogenously by bacterial IDO-1 acting on dietary tryptophan [33]. IDO-1 inhibitor linrodostat [34] reduced DE and OD-bacterial EVs induction of pro-IL-1β and ICAM-1 (Fig. 4A – C, F and G) as well as DE induced IL-8 levels (Fig. 4E). However, it did not inhibit the induction of IL-6 by DE (Fig. 4D) and induction of IL-8 by OD-bacterial EVs (Fig. 4I).

AhR mediates DE and OD-bacterial EVs induction of ROS production

We have reported previously that increased ROS production by NOX and XO controls DE induction of inflammatory mediators in Beas2B cells [21]. We investigated whether AhR plays a role in ROS generation by determining the effects of AhR knockdown on its production. Results showed that AhR knockdown reduced ROS levels induced by DE and OD-bacterial EVs (Fig. 5A and B) indicating the involvement of AhR in ROS production. Chemical inhibitors targeting NOX, including the pan-NOX inhibitor VAS2870, NOX1-specific inihibitor ML171, and dual NOX-1/4 inhibitor GKT137831, along with the xanthine oxidase inhibitor febuxostat, reduced DE- and OD-bacterial EV-induced AhR protein expression (Fig. 5C - L). These findings indicated that ROS produced by both NOX and XO mediate induction of AhR protein levels.

AhR mediates DE and OD-bacterial EVs activation of NFκB and STAT3

Our studies have shown that DE induces inflammatory mediator expression in Beas2B cells via ROS mediated NFκB [6, 21] and STAT3 [13] activation. Therefore, it was of interest to investigate AhR’s role in the activation of NFκB and STAT3. We assessed NFκB and STAT3 activation by the determining effects on NFκB-p65 (Ser536) and STAT3 (Tyr705) phosphorylation. Results showed that AhR knockdown inhibited DE activation of NFκB-p65 and STAT3 (Fig. 6A, C, D, and F). In contrast, AhR knockdown inhibited OD-bacterial EVs activation of NFκB-p65 (Fig. 6G and I) but enhanced STAT3 activation (Fig. 6J and L). Chemical inhibition of NFκB and STAT3 with BAY 11-7082 and stattic, respectively, reduced AhR induction by DE and OD-bacterial EVs, indicating that NFκB and STAT3 mediate AhR induction (Fig. 6M - O). Collectively, these data indicated that a positive feedback loop between ROS, AhR, and NFκB/STAT3 mediates regulation of organic dust induced bronchial epithelial cell inflammatory mediators.

Sex-specific regulation of inflammatory cytokines by OD-bacterial EVs in AhR knockout mice

We studied the effects of OD-bacterial EVs on the induction of lung inflammatory cytokines in global AhR knockout mice to understand regulation in vivo. Data were analyzed using an unpaired t-test to compare the means of two groups of mice (wild-type littermates and AhR knockout mice) exposed to a single independent variable (OD-bacterial EVs). Intranasal administration of OD-bacterial EVs to male and female wild type and AhR knockout mice showed that in female AhR knockout mice, BALF KC and IL-6 levels increased while TNF-α levels decreased, and IL-1β levels did not change (Fig. 7A – D). In contrast, in male AhR knockout mice, while KC levels decreased, IL-1β levels increased, and TNF-α and IL-6 levels did not change (Fig. 7E - H). These data indicated that in mice lacking AhR, OD-bacterial EVs induction of lung cytokines is regulated in a sex-dependent manner. Interestingly, immunostaining studies of lung sections from global AhR knockout mice showed constitutively high 4-HNE staining in the bronchial and alveolar regions, indicating elevated oxidative stress; treatment with OD-bacterial EVs did not further increase 4-HNE staining (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Discussion

Respiratory symptoms and respiratory diseases associated with organic dust exposure are characterized by dysregulated inflammatory responses [2, 3]. Although AhR acts as a cellular sensor for diverse exogenous and endogenous molecules to regulate immune and inflammatory responses, its specific role in modulating organic dust-induced lung inflammation has not been previously investigated. In our present studies, increased AhR nuclear translocation by DE and OD-bacterial EVs suggested that yet to be characterized components in these materials could be driving AhR activation. The increase in nuclear AhR staining was not uniform across Beas2B cells, indicating heterogeneity in AhR activation. Phenotypic heterogeneity in vitro cell cultures appear to be a common phenomenon [35, 36].

The correlation between DE/OD-bacterial EVs activation of AhR and upregulation of inflammatory mediators in bronchial epithelial cells in our present studies suggested a key role for AhR in regulating airway inflammation. AhR silencing decreased DE and OD-bacterial EVs induction of pro-IL-1β, ICAM-1, IL-6 and IL-8 protein levels underscoring its important role in the regulation of organic dust induced airway inflammation. The lack of effect of CH223191 on inflammatory mediators indicated that dioxin-like compounds that may be present in DE and OD-bacterial EVs are not responsible for AhR mediated increase of inflammatory mediators. IDO-1 inhibitor linrodostat reduced induction of pro-IL-1β and ICAM-1 by DE and OD-bacterial EVs, but did not inhibit IL-6 levels. While linrodostat inhibited DE-induced IL-8, it had no effect on IL-8 induced by OD-bacterial EVs. The contrasting inhibitory effects between AhR knockdown and linrodostat could stem from different mechanisms of action; while linrodostat targets IDO-1 to inhibit the production of kynurenine, one of a diverse array of AhR activators, AhR knockdown prevents activation not only by kynurenine but also by other activators that might be present in DE and OD-bacterial EVs, some of which may activate AhR independently of kynurenine.

AhR’s involvement with cellular oxidative response was noted previously [37], and recent studies have demonstrated that oxidative stress induced by environmental stressors and pollutants is mediated via AhR activation [38, 39]. Microbial products, such as LPS, LTA, and flagellin, acting via TLRs, are known to mediate PKC-MAPK activation to promote assembly of the NOX-2 holoenzyme, an essential step in producing ROS [40, 41]. Our published studies have shown that in Beas2B cells, DE promotes membrane association of the cytosolic p47 subunit, indicating the NOX-2 holoenzyme’s assembly and activation [21]. Furthermore, we have demonstrated that proteases in organic dust increase ROS production in lung epithelial cells [6, 7], thereby compounding overall ROS levels. Elevated ROS increases the tryptophan metabolites oxindole and kynurenine, which are AhR activators [42, 43]. Our studies have found that DE mediated ROS activates NFκB and STAT3 [13, 21]. Microbial products, acting directly and via TLRs, also activate NFκB [44] and STAT3 [45], adding to the effects of ROS. Consequently, the activation of AhR, NFκB, and STAT3 increases the transcription of ROS-generating systems, such as NOX, COX-2, and CYP1A, which ultimately amplifies ROS production [38, 46].

Our immunostaining studies indicated constitutively elevated oxidative stress in the lungs of global AhR knockout mice (Supplementary Fig. 4). Elevated oxidative stress has been reported in the heart [47] and hematopoietic stem cells [48] of global AhR knockout mice. Furthermore, this elevated stress is likely due to decreased basal and inducible expression of the master antioxidant transcription factor Nrf2 and key antioxidant enzymes, including NQO1, SOD, GSTs, CYP1A1, CYP1A2, and Srxn [49]. The constitutively high oxidative stress in global AhR KO mice could confound our understanding of AhR’s specific role in organic dust-induced bronchial epithelial cell responses, as AhR has a broad impact on various lung cell types. Therefore, the use of bronchial epithelial cell-specific AhR knockout mice would be necessary to accurately elucidate the role of AhR in regulating organic dust-induced lung inflammation.

AhR knockdown suppressed NFκB activation by both DE and OD-bacterial EVs. However, AhR knockdown showed differential effects on STAT3: it inhibited DE-induced activation but enhanced the response to OD-bacterial EVs. Although AhR knockdown enhanced STAT3 activation by OD-bacterial EVs, suppression of inflammatory mediator levels was not affected indicating that STAT3 may play a context-dependent role in the regulation of induction. Previous studies have shown that benzo[a]pyrene (B[a]P) treatment enhances STAT3 activation in AhR-deficient mice and ARPE-19 cells by disrupting suppressor of cytokine signaling 3 (SOCS3) feedback [50]. We found that NFκB and STAT3 inhibitors downregulated induction of AhR protein levels consistent with the role of NFκB [51] and STAT3 [52] in the regulation of AhR gene expression.

The interactions between ROS and NFκB/STAT3 occur via distinct pathways that control their localization and activities. At moderate levels, ROS triggers NFκB activation and nuclear translocation via IκB kinase activation and subsequent degradation of IκBα [53]. Direct oxidation of conserved cysteine residues in STAT3 and oxidative stress-induced STAT3 phosphorylation at Ser727 and Tyr705 are known to control its transcriptional activity [45, 54]. Activation of NFκB and STAT3 stimulates production of inflammatory cytokines IL-1 and IL-6 which subsequently activate NFκB and STAT3 in a positive feedback manner. Specifically, IL-1 activates NFκB by binding to IL-1R and mediating the degradation of IκBα [55], while IL-6 activates STAT3 via the JAK-STAT pathway [56]. Together, this information and our findings reported herein indicates that a positive feedback regulation between ROS, AhR, NFκB and STAT3 controls induction of airway inflammation by DE, while OD-bacterial EVs induced airway inflammation may be driven by ROS, AhR, and NFκB.

AhR is known to activate or suppress immune and inflammatory responses depending on the cellular context and the nature of the activating ligand [24]. Viruses, bacteria, parasites, and fungi trigger inflammatory responses in the respiratory and intestinal barriers that are regulated by AhR [57]. Furthermore, AhR has been implicated in the pathogenesis of lung diseases [58]. In experimental mice, AhR activation by kynurenine and a synthetic kynurenine derivative PB502 was found to exert protective effects against idiopathic pneumonia syndrome induced by allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation [59]. AhR deficiency/dysfunction was found to enhance hyperoxic lung injury and lung inflammation in adult and neonatal mice [60, 61].

AhR has been reported to dimerize with NFκB subunits, particularly RelA/p65 and RelB to modulate the expression of IL-8 and other target genes by binding to DNA response elements in the promoters [62–64]. AhR activation by estrogen receptor (ER) inhibitor Way-169916 was found to inhibit inflammatory gene expression independently of binding to DRE/XRE DNA elements suggesting ligand-dependent modulation of AhR activity [65]. It was demonstrated that activated AhR binds to imperfect DRE/XRE elements upstream of IL-6 promoter, facilitating subsequent IL-1β-induced NFκB binding [66]. B[a]P induces IL-8 expression via AhR binding to consensus DRE/XRE elements in the IL-8 promoter [67]. DRE/XRE sites have been identified in the promoter regions of NFκB, IL-1β, and ICAM-1 [68]. AhR interactions with Src and STAT3 were reported to promote IL-10 expression, there by mediating immune regulation in macrophages [69]. Chronic exposure to environmental pollutants has been shown to promote non-genomic interactions between AhR, Src and STAT3 leading to constitutive activation of STAT3 to promote carcinogenesis [70].

Biological sex is a significant determinant of immune and inflammatory responses [71]. Because Beas-2B bronchial epithelial cells are derived from a male donor (https://web.expasy.org/cellosaurus/CVCL_0168), their responses to DE- and OD-derived bronchial epithelial bacterial EVs may primarily reflect male-specific biology. Whether female-derived cells exhibit distinct responses in ROS generation and inflammatory mediator expression remains to be determined. Differential regulation of BALF cytokines by OD-bacterial EVs in AhR-deficient mice suggest that AhR exerts inhibitory and stimulatory effects in a sex-specific manner. Mechanisms mediating differential regulation of induction of inflammatory mediators in AhR-deficient mice remain to be investigated. Studies have shown that AhR modulates ER- and androgen receptor (AR) mediated regulation of gene expression via multiple mechanisms. Agonist-activated AhR/ARNT directly complexes with unliganded ERα and p300 to bind to estrogen response DNA elements in the promoter region leading to the attenuation of liganded ER effects [72]. Ligand-activated AhR recruits ER and corepressor RIP140 to modulate ER regulation of gene expression [73]. Moreover, ligand-activated AhR is known to transport unliganded AR to the nucleus, which subsequently binds to the AR DNA elements to modulate gene transcription [74]. Additionally, AhR acts as a ligand-dependent E3 ubiquitin ligase to promote proteasomal degradation of ERa and AR, leading to reduced levels of hormone receptors, thereby influencing changes in gene expression [75].

In addition to AhR modulation of ER and AR activities, innate immune and transcriptional mechanisms could potentially shape sex-specific inflammatory outcomes. In mouse models, differential susceptibility to respiratory infectious pathogens between males and females has been linked to sex-specific innate immune inflammatory responses driven by X-linked innate immune genes such as TLR7/8 and miRNAs [76]. NFκB-p65 has been reported to protect human neurons against oxidative damage in a sex-specific manner by promoting higher expression of SOD2 and IGF2 [77]. Additionally, a study profiling transcriptomic and epigenetic differences in COPD neutrophils found that pathways for interferon and cytokine signaling, and inflammatory networks were enriched in male neutrophils. In contrast, female neutrophils primarily showed enrichment in autophagy and vesicle-mediated transport pathways [78].

Agricultural workers experience high prevalance of respiratory illness with distinct sex differences. A study of respiratory health outcomes among global farming cohorts found the prevalence of cough, phlegm, and wheeze was higher in men than women [79]. However, a study of respiratory symptoms in workers at swine CAFOs found that female workers had a higher risk for chronic bronchitis and cough compared with male workers [80]. These data point to sex differences in the susceptibility to respiratory symptoms among agricultural workers in general, with a particular focus on swine CAFOs highlighting varying vulnerability to organic dust exposure. Additional studies are needed to understand the biological basis for these sex-specific differences.

Limitations

Beas2B cells are an immortalized bronchial epithelial cell line derived from normal human lung tissue. We have found previously that Beas2B cells respond similarly as normal human bronchial epithelial (NHBE) cells and mouse lungs with regard to organic dust induction of inflammatory mediators [6, 13] indicating the suitability of these cells to study bronchial epithelial cell responses. However, future studies using airway organoids or differentiated bronchial epithelium generated via air-liquid interface culture of NHBE cells are warranted, as these models more closely mimic responses of human native airway epithelial cells.

Our study utilized aqueous extracts of settled organic dust from poultry barns to investigate lung inflammatory responses in mice. While this approach provided insights into molecular mechanisms, it remains a limitation because it may not fully reflect physiological responses to poultry organic dust. Consequently, these results should be interpreted with caution when translating to agricultural worker exposure. Future studies using respirable poultry organic dust particles at levels similar to those experienced by agricultural workers [81, 82] will be important.

Agricultural workers are repeatedly exposed to dust, resulting in chronic lung inflammation and dysfunction. However, our study focused on the mechanisms mediating acute inflammatory responses elicited by DE and OD-bacterial EV exposure, limiting our ability to understand the long-term effects of chronic organic dust exposure. Our published studies [4] have demonstrated that repeated exposure of mice to OD-bacterial EVs attenuates the induction of BALF inflammatory cytokines, consistent with the development of an adaptive immune response. In addition, repeated exposure was associated with alveolar enlargement, airway wall thickening, and increased peribronchial collagen deposition, indicative of structural lung remodeling [4].

In summary, our research has identified AhR as a key mediator of organic dust-induced bronchial inflammation. Our studies conclude that positive feedback regulation between ROS, AhR, NFκB, and STAT3 controls this process (Fig. 8). Given its role in driving inflammatory responses to organic dust and organic dust-associated bacterial EVs, targeting the AhR pathway offers a promising therapeutic approach for treating occupational lung diseases in agricultural workers.
