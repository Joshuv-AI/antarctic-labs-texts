Inverse Fiedler vector problem of a graph
Jephian C.-H. Lin, Mahsa N Shirazi

Abstract
Given a graph and one of its weighted Laplacian matrix, a Fiedler vector is an eigenvector with respect to the second smallest eigenvalue. The Fiedler vectors have been used widely for graph partitioning, graph drawing, spectral clustering, and finding the characteristic set. This paper studies how the graph structure can control the possible Fiedler vectors for different weighted Laplacian matrices. For a given tree, we characterize all possible Fiedler vectors among its weighted Laplacian matrix. As an application, the characteristic set can be anywhere on a tree, except for the set containing a single leaf. For a given cycle, we characterize all possible eigenvectors corresponding to the second or the third smallest eigenvalue.



Keywords:

inverse problem, weighted Laplacian matrix, Fiedler vector, Dirichlet matrix, Perron vector

AMS subject classifications:

05C22, 05C50, 15A18, 15B57, 65F18.

1
Introduction

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices. The set
\mathcal{S}(G)
collects all
n\times n
real symmetric matrices whose off-diagonal
i,j
-entry is nonzero if and only if
\{i,j\}
is an edge of
G
. Note that there are no restrictions on the diagonal entries. The
inverse eigenvalue problem of a graph
G
(IEP-
G
) studies the possible spectra among matrices in
\mathcal{S}(G)
, aiming to explore the relations between the graph and its associated spectral properties. The research on the IEP-
G
garners lots of attentions and has produced fruitful results; see, e.g., the monograph
[
22
]
and the references there in.

Aside from its theoretical interests, another motivation of the IEP-
G
is from the vibration theory; see, e.g.,
[
17
]
. In 1974, Hochstadt
[
21
]
studied the vibration of a string and called the matrices in
\mathcal{S}(P_{n})
with nonnegative off-diagonal entries as the Jacobi matrices, where
P_{n}
is the path on
n
vertices. In 1976, Gray and Wilson
[
18
]
and Hald
[
19
]
independently showed that a multiset of
n
real numbers is the spectrum of some Jacobi matrices if and only if all numbers are distinct. In 1980, Ferguson
[
11
]
studied the vibration of a ring and called the matrices in
\mathcal{S}(C_{n})
with nonnegative off-diagonal matrices as the periodic Jacobi matrices; the same paper characterized all possible spectra of a periodic Jacobi matrix. In general, any spring-mass system can be modeled by a simple graph where the vertices and the edges represent the masses and the springs, respectively. Given a spring-mass system represented by a graph
G
, its vibration behavior is governed by the differential equation
M\ddot{\mathbf{x}}=-L\mathbf{x}
, where
M
is a diagonal matrix determined by the weights of the masses,
\mathbf{x}
is a vector recording the displacements of the masses, and
L
is a matrix in
\mathcal{S}(G)
with nonpositive off-diagonal entries; see, e.g.,
[
17
]
for more details. While the IEP-
G
shifted its attention to
\mathcal{S}(G)
, these matrices in
\mathcal{S}(G)
with a fixed sign on the off-diagonal nonzero entries hold stronger connections to the vibration theory and remain an interesting set to be explored.

This paper studies the special case when every mass has unit weight and when the system is not tied with other system (free ends). Thus,
M=I
and
L
has row sum zero on each row. That is,
L
is in the subset of
\mathcal{S}(G)
defined by

\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)=\{A\in\mathcal{S}(G):A\text{ has nonpositive off-diagonal entries and }A\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0}\},

where
\mathbf{1}
is the all-ones vector and
\mathbf{0}
is the zero vector. In this case, the differential equation becomes
\ddot{\mathbf{x}}=-L\mathbf{x}
. Solving the differential equation, the eigenvalues and the eigenvectors of
L
describe the frequencies and the modes of the vibration, respectively.

Matrices in
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
are known as the weighted Laplacian matrices of
G
, whose definition and properties will be reviewed later. For a given weighted Laplacian matrix, an eigenvector with respect to the second smallest eigenvalue is called a Fiedler vector
[
14
]
, and it has been used widely for graph partitioning
[
29
,
7
,
31
]
, graph drawing
[
20
,
25
]
, spectral clustering
[
30
,
26
]
, and many real-world applications. It is also used to define the characteristic set of a tree, which can be viewed as the central part of the tree
[
27
]
.

This paper studies the
inverse Fiedler vector problem of a graph
: Given a graph
G
, what are the possible Fiedler vectors for matrices in
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
? With the various applications of the Fiedler vector, researching this problem also give answers to the possible partitions, the possible drawings, the possible characteristic sets of a given graph.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we characterize all possible Fiedler vectors of a given tree. As an application, we show that the characteristic set can be anywhere except for a singleton on a leaf. We also introduce a bijective transformation in Section
3
between the Type II trees and the Type I trees whose characteristic set is a singleton on a degree-
2
vertex, which simplifies the analysis of the characteristic sets from two types into one type. In Section
4
, we characterize all possible eigenvectors of a cycle corresponding to the second or the third smallest eigenvalue. The rest of this section will be devoted to some terminologies and background.

1.1
Preliminaries

Let
X
and
Y
be finite sets. We write
\mathbb{R}^{X}
for the set of vectors whose entries are indexed by
X
and
\mathbb{R}^{X\times Y}
for the set of matrices whose rows and columns are indexed by
X
and
Y
, respectively. In particular, any vector in
\mathbb{R}^{X}
can be viewed as a function from
X
to
\mathbb{R}
. Given a real symmetric matrix
A
, we write
\lambda_{k}(A)
for its
k
-th smallest eigenvalue and
\operatorname{mult}_{A}(\lambda)
for the multiplicity of
\lambda
as an eigenvalue of
A
.

Let
G
be a simple graph. A
weight assignment
or
weight vector
on
G
is a vector
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{E(G)}
that is entrywisely positive; that is, it assigns a positive real number to each edge. A
weighted graph
is a pair
(G,\mathbf{w})
of a simple graph along with some weight assignment. For convenience, we write the weight of the edge
\{i,j\}
as

\mathbf{w}(i,j)=\mathbf{w}(j,i)

instead of
\mathbf{w}(\{i,j\})
. The
weighted Laplacian matrix
of the weighted graph
(G,\mathbf{w})
is the symmetric matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{V(G)\times V(G)}
whose off-diagonal
i,j
-entry is
-\mathbf{w}(i,j)
if
\{i,j\}\in E(G)
and
0
if
\{i,j\}\notin E(G)
and whose diagonal
i,i
-entry is
\displaystyle\sum_{k:\{i,k\}\in E(G)}\mathbf{w}(i,k)
. Any of such matrix is called a weighted Laplacian matrix of
G
.

By definition, any weighted Laplacian matrix
A
of
G
satisfies
A\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0}
. Therefore,
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
is the collection of all weighted Laplacian matrix of
G
. When each edge receives weight
1
, we say it is an unweighted graph, and the corresponding Laplacian matrix is the classical Laplacian matrix.

The weighted Laplacian matrices of a graph has many well-known properties; see, e.g.,
[
3
,
4
]
. Let
G
be a graph and
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
. It is known that
A
is positive semidefinite and
\lambda_{1}(A)=0
. Also, the nullity of
A
is exactly equal to the number of components of
G
. As a result,
\lambda_{2}(A)>0
if and only if
G
is connected, so
\lambda_{2}
is known as the
algebraic connectivity

[
12
]
. A
Fiedler vector
is an eigenvector of
A
with respect to
\lambda_{2}(A)

[
14
]
, where Fiedler called it the
characteristic valuation
. Note that both the algebraic connectivity and the Fiedler vector depend on the weight assignment, while we say
\mathbf{x}
is a Fiedler vector of
G
if it is a Fiedler vector of some
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
. Fiedler showed
[
14
]
that if
G
is connected and
\mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{i}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{V(G)}
is a Fiedler vector of
G
, then for any
c\leq 0
the induced subgraph of
G
on
V_{\geq c}
is connected, where
V_{\geq c}=\{i\in V(G):x_{i}\geq c\}
. Therefore,
\{V_{\geq c},V(G)\setminus V_{\geq c}\}
gives a reasonable partition of
G
, which leads to many different applications
[
29
,
7
,
31
,
30
]
.

Motivated by the fruitful applications of the Fiedler vector, it is natural to ask how graph structure shapes the potential Fiedler vector among all possible weight assignments.

Problem 1.1

(Inverse Fiedler vector problem of a graph)
.

Given a graph
G
, what are the possible Fiedler vectors among matrices in
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
?

Various places in the literature show some hints that Fiedler had considered this problem in mind.
In
[
14
, Theorem 3.8]
, the potential partitions
\{V_{\geq c},V\setminus V_{\geq c}\}
are considered. The Remark in the end of
[
14
]
mentioned the potential Fiedler vectors, but no proof was given.
The goal of this paper is to provide rigorous proofs and a more comprehensive study on this topic.

When
(T,\mathbf{w})
is a weighted tree, its Fiedler vector
\mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{i}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{V(T)}
has even richer structure. It is known that exactly one of the following two cases will occur
[
13
,
24
]
:

Type I

Some entries of
\mathbf{x}
are zero. In this case, there is exactly a vertex
i
with
x_{i}=0
that is adjacent some vertex
j
with
x_{j}\neq 0
. Moreover, for any path in
T
starting at
i
, the values of
\mathbf{x}
along the path is either strictly increasing, strictly decreasing, or constantly zero.
We say
\{i\}
is the
characteristic set
of
(T,\mathbf{w})
.

Type II

No entry of
\mathbf{x}
is zero. In this case, there is exactly an edge
\{i,j\}
with
x_{i}x_{j}<0
, say
x_{i}<0<x_{j}
. Moreover, for any path starting at
i
without passing
j
, the values of
\mathbf{x}
along the path is strictly decreasing; for any path starting at
j
without passing
i
, the values of
\mathbf{x}
along the path is strictly increasing.
We say
\{i,j\}
is the
characteristic set
of
(T,\mathbf{w})
.

Note that when the algebraic connectivity has multiplicity bigger than one, there are infinitely many choices of
\mathbf{x}
, but the theory showed that the characteristic set is independent of the choice of the Fiedler vectors
[
13
,
24
]
.

Let
G
be a graph. An
orientation
of
G
is a directed graph obtained from
G
by replacing each undirected edge
\{i,j\}
with a directed edge, either
(i,j)
or
(j,i)
. The
incidence matrix
of an orientation of
G
is the matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{V(G)\times E(G)}
such that the
e
-th column has a unique
1
at the
i
-th entry, a unique
-1
at the
j
-th entry, and otherwise
0
if the edge
e=\{i,j\}
is oriented as
(i,j)
. An incidence matrix of
G
is the incidence matrix of some orientation of
G
. It is known
[
2
]
that the classical Laplacian matrix of
G
can be written as
NN^{\top}
, where
N
can be any incidence matrix of
G
. It is straightforward to see that the weighted Laplacian matrix of a weighted graph
(G,\mathbf{w})
can be written as
NWN^{\top}
, where
W=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{w})
is called the
weight diagonal matrix
.

Let
A
be a matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{X\times Y}
,
\alpha\subseteq X
, and
\beta\subseteq Y
. The submatrix of
A
induced on the rows in
\alpha
and columns in
\beta
is denoted by
A[\alpha,\beta]
. The submatrix of
A
obtained by removing the rows in
\alpha
and columns in
\beta
is denoted by
A(\alpha,\beta)
. When
\alpha=\beta
, we simply write
A[\alpha]
and
A(\beta)
. When
\alpha=\beta=\{i\}
, we write
A[i]
and
A(i)
to make the notation easier. Subvectors are defined in a similar way. Finally, in this paper we use the notation
\mathbf{x}\oslash\mathbf{y}
for the entrywise division of two vectors of the same length. That is, when
\mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{i}\end{bmatrix}
and
\mathbf{y}=\begin{bmatrix}y_{i}\end{bmatrix}
are vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{X}
with
\mathbf{y}
nowhere zero, we have
\mathbf{x}\oslash\mathbf{y}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{x_{i}}{y_{i}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{X}
.

Throughout the paper, we will use the Perron–Frobenius theorem and the Cauchy interlacing theorem frequently. The reader may refer to
[
6
,
3
]
for these standard results.

2
The inverse Fiedler vector problem of a tree

In this section, we provide the complete solution to Problem
1.1
when the graph is a tree. As we have seen in Subsection
1.1
, a Fiedler vector of a tree
T
necessarily have some sign conditions on its entries and the monotonicity following the tree structure. We will show that these conditions are enough to characterize all Fiedler vectors among matrices in
\mathcal{S}_{L}(T)
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
T
be a tree on
n
vertices. A vector
\mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{i}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{V(T)}
is said to be
Fiedler-like
with respect to
T
if
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=0
and one of the following two conditions holds:

Type I

There is exactly a vertex
i
with
x_{i}=0
that is adjacent to some vertex
j
with
x_{j}\neq 0
. And for any path in
T
starting at
i
, the values of
\mathbf{x}
along the path is either strictly increasing, strictly decreasing, or constantly zero.
We say
\{i\}
is the
characteristic set
of
\mathbf{x}
.

Type II

There is exactly an edge
\{i,j\}
with
x_{i}x_{j}<0
, say
x_{i}<0<x_{j}
. And for any path starting at
i
without passing
j
, the values of
\mathbf{x}
along the path is strictly decreasing; for any path starting at
j
without passing
i
, the values of
\mathbf{x}
along the path is strictly increasing.
We say
\{i,j\}
is the
characteristic set
of
(T,\mathbf{w})
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
T
be a tree. Then
\mathbf{x}
is a Fiedler vector of
T
if and only if
\mathbf{x}
is Fiedler-like with respect to
T
.

By
[
13
,
24
]
, every Fiedler vector of a tree has to be Fiedler-like. Conversely, we will show that for every Fiedler-like vector
\mathbf{x}
there is a matrix
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(T)
such that
\mathbf{x}
is its Fiedler vector in Theorems
2.14
and
2.20
, which take care of Type I and Type II Fiedler-like vectors, respectively. Thus, Theorem
2.2
will be immediate by then.

Let
T
be a tree,
i\in V(T)
, and
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(T)
. The inverse of
A(i)
is called the
bottleneck matrix
at
i
. The bottleneck matrix is known to be entrywisely positive and is proved to be a powerful tool for finding the location of the characteristic set
[
24
]
. In the following, we will take a detour to focus on eigenvector of
A(i)
with respect to the smallest eigenvalue. Afterward our algorithm for solving the inverse Fiedler vector problem will rely on these results.

2.1
Dirichlet matrices and its Perron vectors

Let
G
be a graph and
\partial V
a subset of
V
. A matrix of the form
A(\partial V)
with
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
is called a
Dirichlet matrix
of
G
with the boundary
\partial V
, which have been used to model the vibration with given boundary condition on
\partial V

[
4
]
.

Here we focus on the Dirichlet matrix of a tree with its boundary on a leaf. Let
T
be a tree,
r
a leaf of
T
, and
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(T)
the weighted Laplacian matrix of
(T,\mathbf{w})
. Given
\mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{i}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{V(T-r)}
, recall that the quadratic form of the Dirichlet matrix is

\mathbf{x}^{\top}A(r)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{w}(r,u)x_{u}^{2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{i,j\}\in E(T)\\
\{i,j\}\neq\{r,u\}\end{subarray}}\mathbf{w}(i,j)(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}\geq 0,

where
u
is the unique neighbor of
r
. This quantity is never zero unless
x_{u}=0
and every entry of
\mathbf{x}
is the same, which means
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}
. Therefore,
A(r)
is a positive definite matrix.

On the other hand, observe that
cI-A(r)
is a nonnegative irreducible matrix when
c
is large enough, so the smallest eigenvalue of
A(r)
is simple by the Perron–Frobenius theorem. Therefore, we call the smallest eigenvalue of
A(r)
as the
Perron value
and call its corresponding eigenvector as
Perron vector
.

By the Perron–Frobenius theorem, the Perron vector can be chosen to be entrywisely positive. We will see that the values of the Perron vector is strictly increasing along any path starting at
u
. Moreover, any such vector
\mathbf{x}
leads to a unique Dirichlet matrix whose Perron vector is
\mathbf{x}
.

Example 2.4
.

Let
T
be the tree in Figure

, where the weight on each edge is undetermined. Given a vector
\mathbf{x}=(x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})^{\top}=(1,2,2)^{\top}

with

is its Perron vector. Note that we may assume the Perron value of

is
\lambda=1
since we may replace

with
\frac{1}{\lambda}A(r)
.

By ordering the edges as

,

,

, we may assume
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},w_{2},w_{3})^{\top}
and

are the weight vector and the weight diagonal matrix, respectively. Let
N
be the incidence matrix of

with respect to the orientation where every edge is pointing to

. Then any

where

is obtained from

, the equation

becomes

and
W(N_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{x})=(N_{r}^{-1}\mathbf{x})
. By direct computation,

N_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\

Thus, there is a unique solution

,
w_{2}=2
, and
w_{3}=2
. Therefore, with
\lambda=1
, the Dirichlet matrix in Example

is the only matrix having
\mathbf{x}
as the Perron vector.

In the following, we describe the general strategy in solving the Dirichlet matrix from a Perron vector. We also provide the combinatorial interpretations of
N_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{x}
and
N_{r}^{-1}\mathbf{x}
.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
T
be a tree with its boundary on a leaf
r
, where
u
is the unique neighbor of
r
. Let
N
be the incidence matrix of
T
with respect to the orientation where every edge is pointing to
r
. The
Dirichlet incidence matrix

N_{r}
is obtained from
N
by removing its
r
-th row. An entrywisely positive vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{V(T-r)}
is said to be
strictly increasing
if for any path in
T-r
starting at
u
, the values of
\mathbf{x}
is strictly increasing.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
T
be a tree with its boundary on a leaf
r
, where
u
is the unique neighbor of
r
. Let
N_{r}
be the Dirichlet incidence matrix and
\mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{i}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{V(T-r)}
. Then the
e
-th entry of
N_{r}\mathbf{x}
is

(N_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{x})_{e}=\begin{cases}x_{u}&\text{ if }e=\{r,u\}\text{ and },\\
x_{j}-x_{i}&\text{ if }e=\{i,j\}\text{ and $i$ is on the path between $j$ and $r$}.\end{cases}

Proof.

When
e=\{r,u\}
, the
e
-th row of
N_{r}^{\top}
has a unique
1
at the
u
-th entry and is
0
otherwise. When
e=\{i,j\}
with
i
on the path between
j
and
r
, the
e
-th row of
N_{r}^{\top}
has a unique
1
at the
j
-th entry and a unique
-1
at the
i
-th entry and is

Theorem 2.11
.

Let
T
be a tree with its boundary on a leaf
r
, where
N_{r}
is its Dirichlet incidence matrix. A vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{V(T-r)}
is the Perron vector of some Dirichlet matrix
A(r)
with
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(T)
if and only if
\mathbf{x}
is strictly increasing on
T
with the boundary
r
. Moreover, given any strictly increasing vector
\mathbf{x}
and a positive
\lambda
, the matrix
N_{r}WN_{r}^{\top}
is the unique Dirichlet matrix with Perron value
\lambda
and Perron vector
\mathbf{x}
, where
W=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{w})
and
\mathbf{w}=\lambda(N_{r}^{-1}\mathbf{x})\oslash(N_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{x})
.

Proof.

Let
A(r)
be a Dirichlet matrix with
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(T)
. Let
\mathbf{x}
be a Perron vector of
A(r)
, where we chose
\mathbf{x}
to be entrywisely positive. We first show that
\mathbf{x}
is strictly increasing. Note that
A(r)=N_{r}WN_{r}^{\top}
for some weight diagonal matrix
W
. Let
\lambda>0
be the Perron value. Then
N_{r}WN_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x}
, which implies
WN_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=\lambda N_{r}^{-1}\mathbf{x}
. Since
\mathbf{x}
is entrywisely positive,
N_{r}^{-1}\mathbf{x}
is entrywisely positive as well by Corollary
2.10
. Since
W
is a diagonal matrix with all diagonal entries positive, we have
N_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{x}
is entrywisely positive, which implies
\mathbf{x}
is strictly increasing by Proposition
2.6
.

Conversely, given a strictly increasing vector
\mathbf{x}
, we may solve the equation
N_{r}WN_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x}
for
W
as follows. Since
\mathbf{x}
is strictly increasing,
N_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{x}
is entrywisely positive by Proposition
2.6
. Since
\mathbf{x}
is entrywisely positive and
\lambda
is positive,
\lambda N_{r}^{-1}\mathbf{x}
is entrywisely positive by Corollary
2.10
. Since
W=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{w})
is a diagonal matrix, the only solution is
\mathbf{w}=\lambda(N_{r}^{-1}\mathbf{x})\oslash(N_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{x})
, and the previous discussion guarantees that
\mathbf{w}
is entrywisely positive, which makes it a valid weight vector. Moreover, an entrywisely positive eigenvector necessarily corresponds to the smallest eigenvalue of

by the Perron–Frobenius theorem.
∎

Thus, the set of Dirichlet matrices and the set of pairs
(\lambda,\operatorname{span}(\{\mathbf{x}\}))
with

and
\mathbf{x}
strictly increasing are in bijective relation.

2.3
Complete solutions

In this subsection, we show that Algorithms
2.13
and
2.19
generate all possible solutions.

Let
G
be a graph and
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
. A vertex
v\in V(G)
is said to a
Parter vertex
with respect to
\lambda
if
\operatorname{mult}_{A(v)}(\lambda)=\operatorname{mult}_{A}(\lambda)+1
; see, e.g.,
[
23
,
28
,
32
]
. Though not stated explicitly, many places in literature, e.g.,
[
13
,
24
,
6
]
, have observed that the unique vertex in the characteristic set of a Type I Fiedler vector is always a Parter vertex and used the fact in the proofs. Here we state the general form and include the proof for completeness.

Lemma 2.23
.

Let
T
be a tree,
A\in\mathcal{S}(T)
, and
A\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x}
. If
\mathbf{x}
is zero at
r\in V(T)
and nonzero at some neighbor
p
of
r
, then
r
is a Parter vertex with respect to
\lambda
.

Proof.

Let
C
be the component of
T-r
containing
p
. By the structure of
T
and
A\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x}
, we have
A[C]\mathbf{x}[C]=\lambda\mathbf{x}[C]
. In other words,
\mathbf{x}[C]
is in
\ker(A[C]-\lambda I)
and
\mathbf{x}[C]
is nonzero at
p
by our assumption.

On the other hand, let
\mathbf{b}=A(r,r]
be the
r
-th column of
A
, removing its
r
-th entry. Observe that the vector
\mathbf{b}[C]
has a unique nonzero entry at
p
and
0
elsewhere. Thus,
\mathbf{b}[C]
and
\mathbf{x}[C]
have nonzero inner product, which means
\mathbf{b}[C]\notin\operatorname{Col}(A[C]-\lambda I)
. This further implies
\mathbf{b}\notin\operatorname{Col}(A(r)-\lambda I)
and
\operatorname{mult}_{A(r)}(\lambda)=\operatorname{mult}_{A}(\lambda)+1
.
∎

Theorem 2.24
.

Let
T
be a tree and
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{V(T)}
a Type I Fiedler-like vector. Then any matrix

2.13
. Moreover, the multiplicity of

equals the number of
\mu_{i}

\mu_{i}=1
minus
1

Proof.

Let

be a matrix such that
\mathbf{x}
is its Fiedler vector with respect to

. Let

be the unique vertex in the characteristic set. Let

be the branches of

at

and

By Lemma

. Since

Suppose

Suppose

Finally, the multiplicity of

∎

Theorem 2.26
.

Let
T
be a tree and
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{V(T)}

Proof.

Let

. Let

\mathbf{x}

4
The inverse Fiedler vector problem of a cycle

In this section, we study the potential Fiedler vector of a cycle. Interestingly, we found that for matrices
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(C_{n})
, eigenvectors with respect to
\lambda_{2}(A)
or
\lambda_{3}(A)
share some common structure and are hard to be distinguished.

We usually label the vertices of
C_{n}
by the elements
0,\ldots,n-1
in the cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
of order
n
, following the cycle order. A
closed interval

[i,j]
stands for the vertices
\{i,i+1,\ldots,j\}
, while an
open interval

(i,j)
stands for
\{i+1,i+2,\ldots,j-1\}
. Thus, intervals like
(i,j]
and
[i,j)
are naturally defined.

First note that this problem is simple for
C_{3}
as it is a complete graph.

Observation 4.1
.

Let
K_{n}
be the complete graph with
n\geq 2
. A vector
\mathbf{x}
is a Fiedler vector of
K_{n}
if and only if
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=0
and
\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{0}
.

Proof.

A Fiedler vector of a complete graph must have
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=0
and
\mathbf{x}\neq 0
. Conversely, the classical Laplacian matrix
A
of
K_{n}
has spectrum
\{0,n^{(n-1)}\}
, so any vector
\mathbf{x}
with
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=0
and
\mathbf{x}=0
is a Fiedler vector of
A
.
∎

To describe the possible Fiedler vector of a cycle, we will frequently use Lemma
4.2
, which is a well-known formula obtained from taking the sum of entries in
S
from the both sides of
A\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x}
; see, e.g.,
[
14
, Statement 3.9]
.

Lemma 4.2
.

[
14
, Statement 3.9]

Let
(G,\mathbf{w})
be a weighted graph,
A
its weighted Laplacian matrix, and
\mathbf{x}
an eigenvector of
A
with respect to
\lambda>0
. Then for any subset
S\in V(G)
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{i,j\}\in E(G)\\
i\in S,\ j\notin S\end{subarray}}\mathbf{w}(i,j)(x_{i}-x_{j})=\lambda\sum_{i\in S}x_{i},

where
x_{i}
is the
i
-th entry of
\mathbf{x}
.

Next we show that the eigenvectors corresponding to
\lambda_{2}(A)
or
\lambda_{3}(A)
necessarily have nice structure for any
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
\mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{i}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{V(C_{n})}
. We say
\mathbf{x}
is
periodic
if
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=0
and has the following properties.

1.

Each of
C_{n}[V_{+}]
and
C_{n}[V_{-}]
is a path, while
C_{n}[V_{0}]
is composed of isolated vertices with
0\leq|V_{0}|\leq 2
.

2.

Along the path
C_{n}[V_{+}]
, the values of
\mathbf{x}
are strictly increasing, followed by at most two maximum values, and then strictly decreasing.

3.

Along the path
C_{n}[V_{-}]
, the values of
\mathbf{x}
are strictly decreasing, followed by at most two maximum values, and then strictly increasing.

Here
V_{+}
,
V_{-}
, and
V_{0}
are the set of indices where
\mathbf{x}
is positive, negative, and zero, respectively.

Example 4.4
.

With
V(C_{12})
labeled by
\mathbb{Z}_{12}
following the cycle order, the vector

(3,5,4,3,2,0,-3,-4,-4,-3,-2,-1)^{\top}

is periodic.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
C_{n}
be the cycle on
n
vertices. Then an eigenvector
\mathbf{x}
of
A
with respect to
\lambda_{2}(A)
or
\lambda_{3}(A)
for some
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(C_{n})
is necessarily periodic.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{i}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{V(C_{n})}
be an eigenvector of
A
with respect to
\lambda\in\{\lambda_{2}(A),\lambda_{3}(A)\}
for some matrix
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(C_{n})
. We may assume that
A
is the weighted Laplacian matrix of
(C_{n},\mathbf{w})
. Since
\mathbf{1}
is the eigenvector of
A
with respect to
0
, we have
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=0
by the spectral theorem.
Since any two consecutive vertices on
C_{n}
form a zero forcing set, we know
C_{n}[V_{0}]
is composed of isolated vertices by
[
1
]
. (Equivalently, if two consecutive entries of
\mathbf{x}
are zero, then
(A-\lambda I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}
implies
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}
.) Moreover, if
x_{i}\in V_{0}
, then by applying Lemma
4.2
with
S=\{i\}
we observe that
i
has exactly one neighbor in
V_{+}
and one neighbor in
V_{-}
.

Since
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=0
, both
V_{+}
and
V_{-}
are not empty. By
[
14
, Corollary 2.2]
,
C_{n}[V_{+}\cup V_{0}]
has either one or two components since
\lambda\in\{\lambda_{2}(A),\lambda_{3}(A)\}
. If it has two components, each component is surrounded by
V_{-}
, so each component contains some vertex in
V_{+}
. (If a component only contains vertices in
V_{0}
, then there are two consecutive vertices in
V_{0}
or a vertex in
V_{0}
with two neighbors in
V_{-}
. Either case is impossible.) By the structure of
a cycle, this implies that
C_{n}[V_{-}\cup V_{0}]
has two components. In total there are four components in
C_{n}[V_{+}\cup V_{0}]
and
C_{n}[V_{-}\cup V_{0}]
(weak nodal domains). This is impossible by the Courant’s nodal domain theorem
[
4
, Theorem 3.1]
since
\mathbf{x}
is an eigenvector of
A
with respect to
\lambda_{2}(A)
or
\lambda_{3}(A)
. Therefore,
C_{n}[V_{+}\cup V_{0}]
has one component, which implies
C_{n}[V_{-}]
has one component. By a similar argument, each of
C_{n}[V_{-}\cup V_{0}]
and
C_{n}[V_{+}]
has one component. Therefore,
C_{n}[V_{+}]
and
C_{n}[V_{-}]
are paths, and
0\leq|V_{0}|\leq 2
.

Since
C_{n}[V_{+}]
is a path, we may assume the vertices on
V_{+}
are
1,\ldots,k
, following the path order. We note that a basin shape where
x_{i-1}\geq x_{i}
and
x_{i}\leq x_{i+1}
cannot happen for any
i
with
1<i<k
, because applying Lemma
4.2
with
S=\{i\}
gives a contradiction

\mathbf{w}(i,i-1)(x_{i}-x_{i-1})+\mathbf{w}(i,i+1)(x_{i}-x_{i+1})\leq 0<\lambda x_{i}.

Let
p
be the smallest integer such that
x_{p}\geq x_{p+1}
. Applying the above observation with
i=p+1
gives
x_{p+1}>x_{p+2}
. Inductively applying the observation with
i=p+1,\ldots,k-1
, we know
x_{p+1},\ldots,x_{k}
are strictly decreasing. By our choice of
p
,
x_{1},\ldots,x_{p}
are strictly increasing. Depending on
x_{p}=x_{p+1}
or not, there may be two or one maximum values. A similar argument also applies to
C_{n}[V_{-}]
.
∎

Lemma
4.2
guarantees further structure on a periodic vector.

Definition 4.6
.

Let
\mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{i}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{V(C_{n})}
be a periodic vector on
C_{n}
. A vertex
i
is called a
peak
if
x_{i}=\max(\mathbf{x})
and a
valley
if
x_{i}=\min(\mathbf{x})
. If
\mathbf{x}
has a unique peak
p
, set
p^{\prime}=p
; if
\mathbf{x}
has two peaks
p,p+1
, set
p^{\prime}=p+1
. If
\mathbf{x}
has a unique valley
q
, set
q^{\prime}=q
; if
\mathbf{x}
has two valleys
q,q+1
, set
q^{\prime}=q+1
. We say
\mathbf{x}
is
balanced
if

\sum_{i\in(p,q]}x_{i}<0<\sum_{i\in[p^{\prime},q^{\prime})}x_{i}

when
\mathbf{x}
has a unique peak or a unique valley, and

\sum_{i\in(p,q]}x_{i}=0=\sum_{i\in[p^{\prime},q^{\prime})}x_{i}

when
\mathbf{x}
has two peaks and two valleys.

Example 4.7
.

The vector in Example
4.4
has
p=p^{\prime}=1
,
q=7
, and
q^{\prime}=8
. Since the sum of entries over
(p,q]
is positive, the vector is not balanced.

In contrast, the vector

(1,2,3,2,1,0,-2,-5,-2,0)^{\top}

has
p=p^{\prime}=2
and
q=q^{\prime}=7
and
is a periodic and balanced vector on
C_{10}
.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
C_{n}
be the cycle on
n
vertices. Then a periodic eigenvector
\mathbf{x}
for some
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
with respect to a positive eigenvalue is necessarily balanced.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{i}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{V(C_{n})}
be a periodic vector satisfying
A\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x}
for some
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(C_{n})
and
\lambda>0
.
Let
p,p^{\prime},q,q^{\prime}
be as defined in Definition
4.6
.

We first consider the case when
\mathbf{x}
has a unique peak or a unique valley. We apply Lemma
4.2
with
S=(p,q]
to obtain an equation. Observe that

x_{p+1}-x_{p}\leq 0\text{ and }x_{q}-x_{q+1}\leq 0

by the choice of
p
and
q
, and one of them is nonzero by our assumption. This means the left-hand side the the equation in Lemma
4.2
is negative. Thus, we have

\lambda\sum_{i\in(p,q]}x_{i}<0.

On the other hand, we apply Lemma
4.2
again with
S=[p^{\prime},q^{\prime})
to get

0<\lambda\sum_{i\in[p^{\prime},q^{\prime})}x_{i}

since

x_{p^{\prime}}-x_{p^{\prime}-1}\geq 0\text{ and }x_{q^{\prime}-1}-x_{q^{\prime}}\geq 0

and they cannot be zero simultaneously. This case is completed since
\lambda>0
.

For the other case when
\mathbf{x}
has two peaks and two valleys, the inequalities above becomes equalities because

\displaystyle x_{p+1}-x_{p}

\displaystyle=x_{q}-x_{q+1}=0\text{ and }

\displaystyle x_{p^{\prime}}-x_{p^{\prime}-1}

\displaystyle=x_{q^{\prime}-1}-x_{q^{\prime}}=0.

Thus, the inequalities in Definition
4.6
also become equalities in this case.
∎

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
C_{n}
be the cycle on
n
vertices. Then
\mathbf{x}
is an eigenvector of
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(C_{n})
with respect to
\lambda_{2}(A)
or
\lambda_{3}(A)
if and only if
\mathbf{x}
is periodic and balanced.

Proof.

By Lemmas
4.5
and
4.8
, an eigenvector for some
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(C_{n})
with respect to
\lambda_{2}(A)
or
\lambda_{3}(A)
is necessarily periodic and balanced, so we will focus on the other direction. Let
\mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{i}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{V(C_{n})}
be a periodic and balanced vector and
\lambda>0
. We will find a matrix
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(C_{n})
such that
A\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x}
. Label the vertices of
C_{n}
with
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
following the cycle order such that
0
is a peak and
x_{0}>x_{1}
. We also label the edge
\{j,j+1\}
as
e_{j}
for
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{R}^{V(C_{n})\times E(C_{n})}
be the incidence matrix of
C_{n}
under the orientation
(j+1,j)
for all
e_{j}
. That is, its
i,e_{j}
-entry is
-1
if
i=j
,
1
if
i=j+1
, and
0
otherwise. Then any matrix
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(C_{n})
can be written as
A=NWN^{\top}
, where
W=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{w})
is the weight diagonal matrix corresponding to the weight vector
\mathbf{w}
. By direct computation, the
e_{j}
-th entry of
N^{\top}\mathbf{x}
is
x_{j+1}-x_{j}
.

On the other hand, let
N^{+}
be the matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{E(C_{n})\times V(C_{n})}
whose
e_{j},i
-entry is
1
for any
0\leq j<i\leq n
. By direct computation, we have
NN^{+}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}
for any
\mathbf{x}
satisfying
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=0
. Also, the right kernel of
N
is spanned by
\mathbf{1}
since the only cycle on
C_{n}
is itself. Thus, for any given
\mathbf{x}
with
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=0
, the solution set of
N\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{x}
is
\{\mathbf{z}=N^{+}\mathbf{x}+h\mathbf{1}:h\in\mathbb{R}\}
. By direct computation, the entry of
N^{+}\mathbf{x}+h\mathbf{1}
corresponding to the edge
e_{j}
is
h+\sum_{i\in[j+1,n-1]}x_{i}
.

Since
NWN^{\top}\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x}
implies
WN^{\top}\mathbf{x}=\lambda(N^{+}\mathbf{z}+h\mathbf{1})
for some
h
. Therefore, a weight vector
\mathbf{w}
exists if and only if
N^{\top}\mathbf{x}
and
N^{+}\mathbf{z}+h\mathbf{1}
have the same signs (
+
,
-
, or
0
) at each corresponding entries for some
h
. The weight vector
\mathbf{w}
can be obtained from
\lambda(N^{+}\mathbf{z}+h\mathbf{1})\oslash(N^{\top}\mathbf{x})
such that an arbitrary positive weight is assigned to each entry with
\frac{0}{0}
.

Let
p,p^{\prime},q,q^{\prime}
be as defined in Definition
4.6
. (Based on our choice of
0
, we have
p^{\prime}=0
.) We first examine the signs on
N^{\top}\mathbf{x}
. By the periodic structure of
\mathbf{x}
, the
e_{j}
-th entry of
N^{\top}\mathbf{x}
is negative if
j\in[p^{\prime},q)
, positive if
j\in[q^{\prime},p)
, and zero if
e_{j}
is
\{p,p^{\prime}\}
and
\{q,q^{\prime}\}
whenever these edges exist.

Now we assign appropriate
h
so that
N^{+}\mathbf{z}+h\mathbf{1}
matches the signs of
N^{\top}\mathbf{x}
. If
\mathbf{x}
has two peaks, then let
h=0
. If
\mathbf{x}
has two valleys, then let
h=-\sum_{i\in(p,q]}x_{i}
. Note that these two statement are consistent when
\mathbf{x}
has two peaks and two valleys since
\mathbf{x}
is balanced. If
\mathbf{x}
has a unique peak and a unique valley, or equivalently,
p=p^{\prime}
and
q=q^{\prime}
, then choose an arbitrary
h
that satisfies

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle<h<-\sum_{i\in(p,q]}x_{i},\text{ and }

\displaystyle x_{p}-\sum_{i\in[p^{\prime},q^{\prime})}x_{i}

\displaystyle<h<x_{p}.

Note that these four inequalities are consistent and lead to some feasible
h
since
\mathbf{x}
is balanced. For example,

x_{p}-\sum_{i\in[p^{\prime},q^{\prime})}x_{i}=-\sum_{i\in(p,q)}x_{i}=-\sum_{i\in(p,q]}x_{i}+x_{q}<-\sum_{i\in(p,q]}x_{i},

while the others are immediate from the definition of a balanced vector. With the given
h
, one may check that
x_{j+1}-x_{j}
and
h+\sum_{i\in[j+1,n-1]}x_{i}
have the same sign for all
j
.

Thus, there exists a matrix
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(C_{n})
such that
A\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x}
. Now we show that
\lambda
is either
\lambda_{2}(A)
or
\lambda_{3}(A)
. Since
\lambda_{1}(A)=0
, it is enough to show
\lambda\leq\lambda_{3}(A)
. Suppose
\mathbf{x}
has two zeros on
i
and
j
. Then
A(\{i,j\})
is the direct sum of
A[V_{+}]
and
A[V_{-}]
. Also,
A[V_{+}]\mathbf{x}[V_{+}]=\lambda\mathbf{x}[V_{+}]
and the fact that
\mathbf{x}[V_{+}]
is entrywisely positive implies that
\lambda=\lambda_{1}(A[V_{+}])
by the Perron–Frobenius theorem; similarly,
\lambda=\lambda_{1}(A[V_{-}])
. By the Cauchy interlacing theorem,
\lambda=\lambda_{1}(A(\{i,j\})\leq\lambda_{3}(A)
.

Suppose
\mathbf{x}
has a unique zero on
i
. We first claim that
\lambda=\lambda_{2}(A(i))
. Let
D=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{x}(i))
and
A^{\prime}=D(A(i)-\lambda I)D
. Thus,
A^{\prime}\mathbf{1}=D(A(i)-\lambda I)\mathbf{x}(i)=\mathbf{0}
. Note that
A^{\prime}
is a matrix in
\mathcal{S}(P_{n-1})
with exactly one pair of positive off-diagonal entries. By
[
13
, Theorem 2.2]
,
A^{\prime}
has exactly one negative eigenvalue. Since
A^{\prime}
and
A(i)-\lambda I
are congruent,
A(i)-\lambda I
has exactly one negative eigenvalue, and
\lambda=\lambda_{2}(A(i))
. By the Cauchy interlacing theorem,
\lambda=\lambda_{2}(A(i))\leq\lambda_{3}(A)
.

Suppose
\mathbf{x}
contains no zero. We let
D=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{x})
and
A^{\prime}=D(A-\lambda I)D
. Thus,
A^{\prime}\mathbf{1}=D(A-\lambda I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}
. Observe that
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(C_{n})
has exactly two pairs of positive off-diagonal entries. Let the
(i,i+1)
-entry of
A^{\prime}
be a positive entry with value
c>0
. Define the symmetric matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{V(C_{n})\times V(C_{n})}
such that

B[\{i,i+1\}]=\begin{bmatrix}c&-c\\
-c&c\end{bmatrix}

while other entries are zero. Then
A^{\prime}+B
is a matrix in
\mathcal{S}(P_{n})
with
(A^{\prime}+B)\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0}
and exactly one pair of positive off-diagonal entries. By
[
13
, Theorem 2.2]
,
0=\lambda_{2}(A^{\prime}+B)
. Since
A^{\prime}+B\in\mathcal{S}(P_{n})
, its eigenvalues are all distinct
[
21
]
, implying
\lambda_{3}(A^{\prime}+B)>0
. Now by applying Weyl’s inequality (see, e.g.,
[
33
]
) to
A^{\prime}=(A^{\prime}+B)+(-B)
, we have

0<\lambda_{3}(A^{\prime}+B)+\lambda_{2}(-B)\leq\lambda_{4}(A^{\prime}).

Since
A^{\prime}
and
A-\lambda I
are congruent, this further implies that
0<\lambda_{4}(A-\lambda I)
and
\lambda\leq\lambda_{3}(A)
.
∎

5
Concluding remark

In this paper, we characterized all possible Fiedler vectors of a tree. Given a Fiedler-like vector, we also provide the complete solution to all the weight assignments that match the vector. As a side product, the relation between the Dirichlet matrix and the Perron vector is shown to be bijective. We also characterized all possible eigenvectors corresponding to the second or the third smallest eigenvalues of a weighted Laplacian matrix of a cycle.

The results of trees and cycles show that the Fiedler vector (or eigenvector of
\lambda_{3}
) demonstrates very different behavior depending on the topological properties of the base graph, i.e., having a cycle or not. For example, every tree has exactly one characteristic set of one vertex or two adjacent vertices, while every cycle has two such sets. Moreover,
\lambda_{2}
seems quite special for trees as the Fiedler vector can be fully described. In contrast,
\lambda_{2}
and
\lambda_{3}
for cycles shares many common properties and are not so distinguishable by its eigenvectors. To the extreme, we see that it is possible that
\lambda_{2}=\cdots=\lambda_{n}
for complete graphs.
This shed some light on the definition of the Colin de Verdière parameter
[
9
,
8
]
, where the maximum nullity of the second smallest eigenvalue is used to characterize some the topological properties of a graph. However, the properties of the eigenvectors of the weighted Laplacian matrices are far from being clear. With a better understanding on these eigenvectors, it would provide a more concrete theoretical foundation for the applications such as graph partitioning, graph drawing, and spectral clustering.
