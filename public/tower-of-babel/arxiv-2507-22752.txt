CUS-QA: Local-Knowledge-Oriented Open-Ended Question Answering Dataset
Jindřich Libovický, Jindřich Helcl, Andrei Manea, Gianluca Vico

Abstract
We introduce a benchmark for open-ended regional question answering that encompasses both textual and visual modalities. We also provide strong baselines using state-of-the-art large language models (LLMs). Our dataset consists of manually curated questions and answers grounded in Wikipedia, created by native speakers from Czechia, Slovakia, and Ukraine, with accompanying English translations. It includes both purely textual questions and those requiring visual understanding. As a baseline, we evaluate state-of-the-art LLMs through prompting and complement this with human judgments of answer correctness. Using these human evaluations, we analyze the reliability of existing automatic evaluation metrics. Our baseline results highlight a significant gap in regional knowledge among current LLMs. Moreover, apart from LLM-based evaluation, there is minimal correlation between automated metrics and human judgment. We release this dataset as a resource to (1) assess regional knowledge in LLMs, (2) study cross-lingual generation consistency in a challenging setting, and (3) advance the development of evaluation metrics for open-ended question answering.



1
Introduction

The ability to answer factual questions is one of the most frequently used and most frequently evaluated capabilities of current large language models (LLMs), both purely textual and in multimodal setups.

Frequently used QA benchmarks contain globally known facts and multiple-choice questions
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021a
;
Clark et al. 2020
;
Rajpurkar et al. 2016
)
, and only a few of them are in languages other than English
(
Artetxe et al. 2020
;
Clark et al. 2020
)
. The advantage is that it allows easy evaluation using accuracy and comparison across languages. We argue this is not a realistic setup. Questions considered useful by native speakers of one language may not be useful at all when translated and presented to native speakers of a different language. When people use LLMs to get answers to their questions, it is reasonable to assume that they do not usually provide multiple possible answers to choose from. Even in this scenario, selecting the answer through candidate comparison and ranking would simplify the procedure. However, the evaluation of open-ended generation still does not have a satisfactory solution.

We introduce a dataset that addresses these issues.
We collect both purely textual questions and questions about images, manually written by native speakers of the languages and grounded in Wikipedia pages. In addition, we tested a few state-of-the-art open-weight LLMs, conducted a human evaluation of their outputs, and compared the human evaluation with automatic evaluation metrics. We release both the dataset and the human evaluation results.
As far as we know, this is the first dataset with human judgment on open-ended question answering, and we hope that the research community will use it to advance research on automatic text evaluation metrics.
The dataset spans three countries, each with a single official language: Czechia (Czech), Slovakia (Slovak), and Ukraine (Ukrainian). All three languages belong to the Slavic branch of the Indo-European language family. Czech (10M speakers) and Slovak (5M speakers) use the Latin alphabet and have very similar orthographies. Ukrainian, spoken by approximately 33M people, uses the Cyrillic alphabet. Despite its significantly larger speaker base, it does not have significantly richer NLP resources compared to Czech and Slovak.

We describe the evaluation, including LLM baselines, human evaluation, and the correlation study on automatic metrics in Section

2
Related Work

Question answering (QA) is a fundamental NLP task that has been extensively studied in recent decades. The task can be formulated as extractive, where the answer is a span in a given document, or abstractive, where the answer is generated without requiring an exact match to the document. For simplicity and better evaluation, QA is often framed as multiple-choice classification. However, open-ended generation poses significant evaluation challenges.

In the following sections, we review existing datasets for non-English textual question answering (§
2.1
) and visual question answering (§
2.2
). Finally, we discuss reference-based evaluation metrics (§
2.3
).

2.1
Textual Question Answering

When encoder-based models were the main direction in NLP, extractive QA was a major benchmark task, with SQuAD
(
Rajpurkar et al. 2016
)
being one of the most widely used datasets. It was later complemented by multilingual datasets such as TyDi QA
(
Clark et al. 2020
)
and XQuAD
(
Artetxe et al. 2020
)
. Along with datasets like TriviaQA
(
Joshi et al. 2017
)
, this task is often referred to as reading comprehension because it relies on a provided text passage rather than the model’s factual knowledge.

With the rise of decoder-based generative models, multiple-choice QA has become more common for evaluation. The English MMLU dataset
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021a
)
, covering 57 diverse topics such as history, science, and geography, is widely used. It goes beyond factual knowledge and requires reasoning in many areas. However, it is only available in English, and some topics assume US-centric knowledge.

Several recent datasets extend beyond English, incorporating local knowledge into QA tasks.

Etxaniz et al. 2024
introduce a parallel multiple-choice dataset with trivia questions in English and Basque, with a subset focusing on Basque culture.
KazQAD
(
Yeshpanov et al. 2024
)
is a Kazakh QA dataset based on school exams, combining local knowledge (history, geography) with general subjects (science, math).

Kostiuk et al. 2025
compile a dataset on Lithuanian history, showing that prompting LLMs in Lithuanian improves performance over other languages.

Etori et al. 2025
localizes MMLU into Latvian and Giriama, covering 55 out of the 57 original subjects.

The INCLUDE dataset
(
Romanou et al. 2024
)
collects multiple-choice QA pairs from local exam sources in 44 languages worldwide, focusing on regional knowledge. Similarly, the Global MMLU dataset
(
Singh et al. 2024
)
follows MMLU’s structure but covers 42 languages, incorporating both culture-agnostic and culture-specific questions, with a stronger emphasis on cultural aspects.

Other datasets focus on evaluating cultural rather than factual knowledge, often using food as a proxy.
Zhou et al. 2024
presents a dataset on regional ingredients, while
Lavrouk et al. 2024
introduces QA on dishes from former Soviet states, revealing that LLMs frequently confuse post-Soviet nations and exhibit biases toward Russia and Ukraine. Unlike previous datasets, these two also include images, incorporating visual question answering tasks.

Recent work has examined the effectiveness of multilingual LLMs across different languages and found substantial performance gaps.
Rohera et al. 2025
evaluate factual accuracy of LLMs using the IndicQuest dataset with question-answer pairs in English and 19 Indic languages. Their findings show that LLMs often perform better in English, even for questions from Indic contexts.

Hupkes and Bogoychev 2025
how more nuanced results with MultiLoKo, a multilingual local knowledge benchmark spanning 31 languages with 500 questions per language sourced to be locally relevant. Their evaluation of 11 multilingual models reveals substantial performance gaps across languages and shows that using locally-sourced versus English-translated data can result in differences of more than 20 points for the best-performing models, with both positive and negative effects, depending on the language.

None of the datasets currently used for QA evaluation are primarily designed for open-ended generation, even though they are more natural setups. Thus, the typical evaluation is conducted by computing accuracy in the multiple-choice setup.

2.2
Visual Question Answering

Visual Question Answering (VQA) is one of the foundational benchmarks in language-vision research. The initial task formulations
(
Antol et al. 2015
;
Hudson and Manning 2019
)
assumed a closed set of possible output words, with the goal of evaluating models’ understanding of objects in images and their relationships. This objective was also carried over into multilingual extensions of the task
(
Pfeiffer et al. 2022
)
.

Nayak et al. 2024
introduced a benchmark for open-ended visual questions, focusing on traditions, rituals, food, drink, and clothing, with an emphasis on global cultural fairness. Although this is the benchmark most similar to the proposed task, its focus is slightly different. The dataset is in English and aims to understand different cultural concepts from a global perspective.

Wang et al. 2025
propose a multiple-choice VQA set, and instead of focusing on local culture, the emphasis is on landmark facts, which is tourist-level information.

The English text-only, MMLU dataset
Hendrycks et al. 2021b
consists of multiple-choice questions on a wide range of topics from school exams, focusing rather on reasoning and general knowledge. Based on it, a multimodal counterpart was designed, MMMU
(
Yue et al. 2024
)
, and later a multilingual version, xMMMU
(
Yue et al. 2025
)
with Machine Translation on a subset of MMMU. Later, more benchmarks were released, with questions collected from actual exams from diverse countries and spanning more languages
(
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Das et al. 2024
)
.
Unlike our dataset, only a small proportion of the images are photographs. Moreover, they rarely focus on local or cultural knowledge, and they focus more on diagrams, graphs, and other plots.

MTVQA
(
Tang et al. 2025
)
is yet another VQA multiple-choice dataset. Comparing it to ours, it is more focused on the text-rich images, formal documents, and bills in multiple languages, triggering the OCR capabilities of the models, rather than the cultural understanding.
Romero et al. 2024
proposes CVQA, which is the most similar dataset to ours. It covers cultural elements from 30 countries, with questions available in both the local language and English. Although there is great coverage, the focus is rather on language-country level than on language-region, as we propose in this work.

2.3
Evaluation Metrics

Reference-based evaluation assesses generated text against a desired output, but the challenge lies in the vast number of valid answers.

Traditional metrics measure textual overlap using word or character n-grams (e.g., BLEU:
Papineni et al. 2002
; ROUGE:
Lin 2004
; chrF:
Popović 2015
). More recent approaches leverage embedding similarity (e.g., BERTScore:
Zhang et al. 2020
, BLEURT:
Sellam et al. 2020
) or fully trained metrics like COMET
(
Rei et al. 2020
)
targeted on machine translation.

Recently, LLMs have often been used as a judge, either directly assessing the quality
(
Zheng et al. 2023
)
or as a part of the more structured pipelines, such as FactScore
(
Min et al. 2023
)
that first breaks the generated text into atomic facts and evaluates the facts individually.

The standard approach for validating evaluation metrics is measuring their correlation with human judgments, a practice best established in machine translation
(
Freitag et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
. However, human-labeled datasets for other tasks are limited, with exceptions like image captioning (Flickr8k-Expert
Hodosh et al. 2013
; Nebula
Matsuda et al. 2024
; Polaris
Wada et al. 2024
), text summarization (ROSE:
Liu et al. 2023
), and MOCHA for reading comprehension
(
Chen et al. 2020
)
, mostly in English.

While human preference datasets used for instruction tuning
(
Bai et al. 2022
;
Köpf et al. 2023
)
contain LLM output rankings, they are rarely suited for assessing reference-based evaluation.

We developed a web-based interface for collecting the data. In the interface, the annotators were presented with a Wikipedia page. Using the Wikipedia API, the tool allows annotators to browse Wikipedia content, including clicking links leading to different Wikipedia pages. The left column of the page is reserved for the task related to the annotation and contains the input fields for the questions and the answers.

The annotators were asked to browse Wikipedia and find facts that are very specific to their country (Czechia, Slovakia, Ukraine). Select the facts that they think many (at least roughly tens of thousands of) people in that country know, but almost everyone outside the country does not.

They were randomly provided with a Wikipedia page from a pre-selected pool. The pool consisted of Wikipedia pages about entities that are located in the respective country in DBPedia and pages that only existed in the given language.

We instructed the annotators to skim through the pages and click the links that would bring them closer to the desired type of information. If they felt lost, they could always hit the “Skip button”, which would bring them to a random page from a pre-selected pool again. Each annotator could only use each Wikipedia page once.

We asked the annotators to only consider Wikipedia pages about local entities from their country and to avoid facts that they thought almost no one knew or considered rather obscure. They wrote a question and answer in their language (Czech, Slovak, Ukrainian) and provided an English translation of both.

If the Wikipedia page had a photograph (not a computer-drawn scheme) illustrating the topic, the annotators were encouraged to ask a question about the photograph. The question could be a small reformulation of the previous one or a new one, but it still should be local knowledge.

All annotators are native speakers of the respective languages who were born and raised in the countries where these languages are spoken. However, all annotators are currently university students living in the Czech Republic. Because all annotators are university students, we assume they have sufficient knowledge of English.

The annotators were compensated at a rate of 300 CZK (
\approx
12€) per hour, which is 2.4
\times
the minimum wage and 1.2
\times
the median wage in the Czech Republic as of 2024.

Researchers manually verified all questions and answers to ensure they adhered to the annotation guidelines. During this process, we corrected 8% of the items (mostly typos and grammatical issues) and discarded 22% of the questions.

The average length of questions in the textual modality part is around 60 characters in all languages; the English translations are slightly longer. The questions in the visual modality part are shorter because some context is already provided by the image. In both modalities, the answers are, on average, 20 characters long in Czech and Slovak but over 30 in Ukrainian. Most of the answers are short noun phrases. 5
Baselines & Evaluation

To validate that our question-answering task presents a meaningful challenge and assess how well current LLMs handle regional knowledge, we conduct baseline experiments with several state-of-the-art models. We evaluate both textual and visual QA performance through automated metrics and human judgment to understand the task difficulty. Additionally, we examine to what extent automatic evaluation metrics can reliably capture answer quality in open-ended QA settings, comparing their correlation with human assessments across different languages and modalities.

5.1
Baselines

As baselines, we test several widely used open-weight LLMs in each modality setting. We evaluate zero-shot generation performance of the models and we do not consider context-dependent prompting techniques such as retrieval-augmented generation.

In the text-only setup, we evaluate the following models: LLaMA 3.1 8B, LLaMA 3.3 70B
(
Dubey et al. 2024
)
, Mistral v0.3 7B
(
Jiang et al. 2023
)
and EuroLLM 7B
(
Martins et al. 2024
)
. For visual QA, we test mBLIP
(
Geigle et al. 2023
)
, LLaMA 3.2 and 4 Vision, Maya
(
Alam et al. 2024
)
, Gemma 3
(
Team et al. 2025
)
and idefics
(
Laurençon et al. 2023
)
.

We used nucleus sampling with a nucleus
0.9
, and we only sampled one answer per question and model.

Based on preliminary experiments, we decided to use an English system prompt and instruct the model to answer in the language of the questions. See Appendix
A
for the exact prompt formulation.

5.2
Human Evaluation

We manually evaluate the model outputs using the following binary criteria: correctness, truthfulness, relevance, and coherence.
Our aim was to design a small set of error categories that best describe what we observed in the generated answers.
Each answer was independently evaluated by two annotators.

Correctness.

In the main evaluation criterion, the annotators decided if the generated answer is correct, given the reference answer, using their subjective judgment. If the answer contained minor hallucinations or misleading information that do not substantially change the overall meaning of the answer, it is still considered correct.

Truthfulness.

The annotators verified whether the entire answer is objectively true. This is separate from whether the answer addresses the question. An answer can be factually true but incorrect if it does not answer the question. We also allow saying that the answer is correct if it contains irrelevant, untrue statements. If the model refuses to answer, it is annotated as not true.

Relevance.

Here, the annotators judged whether the answer contains only the information the question asks for and nothing else, is appropriately specific, and is not too general.

Coherence.

A coherent answer is grammatically correct and in the correct language. This is judged regardless of the factuality or relevance.

These categories allow us to define different levels of strictness in how we judge the answers: In the permissive case, we only consider the correctness criterion, and in the strict case, we want all criteria to be fulfilled. We consider a criterion to be fulfilled when both annotators agree.

Automatic Metrics

We evaluate the generated answers using the following metrics:
BLEU
(
Papineni et al. 2002
)
, chrF
(
Popović 2015
)
, ROUGE-L
(

)
, BERTScore
(

)

Baseline Results

We report the strongest baseline results in Table
6

The best-scoring model for textual QA was Llama 3.3 70B Instruct, which outperformed the remaining models by a large margin, often 15–20 percentage points in accuracy compared to others, and performed consistently across countries and languages. For the best model, there is a relatively small accuracy drop between only considering correctness and also including truthfulness.

For other models, we observed significant inconsistencies across languages. For instance, Mistral performs worse in Slovak than in other languages. A large gap between considering correctness only and other evaluation criteria suggests that the models tend to hallucinate irrelevant content. LLaMA 3.1 8B also struggled with the Slovak language, and similarly to Mistral, for questions about Slovakia, it worked better in English.

EuroLLM, which focuses on European languages, outperforms both models of similar size: Mistral and LLaMA 3.1 8B in all local languages and for English when answering questions about Czechia and Slovakia.

Visual QA is a more challenging task. Whereas in textual QA, the accuracy of the best scoring models is over 50%, with visual QA, it does not exceed 30% in any of the languages. By a large margin, the best-scoring model is Llama 4 Scout 17B 16E Instruct, followed by Gemma 3 and LLaMA 3.2 11B Vision Instruct. Idefics and Maya score very poorly, with Maya achieving near-zero performance across all languages and conditions. The visual QA results show even greater regional variation than textual QA. For instance, Llama 4 Scout performs notably better for Ukraine compared to Czechia or Slovakia.

5.5
Metrics and Human Judgment

Inter-annotator agreement (Table
7
), measured by Cohen’s
\kappa
, shows generally strong consistency between annotators across most evaluation criteria. The agreement is highest for correctness and truthfulness (average
\kappa=0.84
and
0.86
respectively). It is lower for relevance (
\kappa=0.70
) and coherence (
\kappa=0.80
), likely reflecting the more subjective nature of these judgments.

We examine how well automatic evaluation metrics correlate with human judgments by comparing system-level (Table
8
) and answer-level (Table
9
) performance. At the system level, we find very high correlations between most metrics and human judgment. Traditional string-overlap metrics like BLEU, chrF, and ROUGE achieve correlations above 0.85 with human correctness judgments for textual QA. This strong performance likely stems from the high proportion of named entities in the answers, where exact string matches are more meaningful than in other generation tasks like machine translation or summarization.

LLM-based evaluation metrics show even stronger correlations, with LLaMA 3.3 70B achieving near-perfect system-level correlation (
r>0.95
) across most conditions. M-Prometheus, specifically designed for multilingual evaluation, performs similarly well. Interestingly, BERTScore shows lower correlations than simpler string-overlap metrics.

This changes considerably for visual QA, where correlations drop substantially across all metrics. String-overlap metrics maintain reasonable performance (correlations around 0.6–0.8), but BERTScore shows negative correlations in some cases. LLM-based metrics remain the most reliable, although their correlations are lower than in textual QA.

At the answer level, correlations are generally lower but follow similar patterns. LLM-based metrics consistently outperform other metrics, achieving correlations around 0.7–0.8 with human judgments for textual QA and 0.4–0.6 for visual QA. The difference between system-level and answer-level correlations shows that while automatic metrics can reliably rank different systems on average, they are less effective at judging individual answer quality.

Our findings reveal that simple string-overlap metrics perform surprisingly well for this type of regional knowledge QA, likely due to the prevalence of named entities. However, LLM-based evaluation seems to be the most reliable approach across all conditions, particularly for the more challenging visual QA task.

The results of cross-lingual comparison (Table

For textual QA, models mostly perform best when answering questions in the local language. However, the performance gaps vary across models and countries. 6
Discussion and Future Work

Our experiments show that current evaluation metrics work reasonably well for open-ended factual QA. This finding suggests that the focus on multiple-choice QA in many existing datasets may be unnecessarily restrictive. The high proportion of named entities in factual answers makes string-overlap metrics more effective than in other generation tasks.

The dataset can serve multiple research purposes beyond basic QA evaluation. Since all information is guaranteed to be available on Wikipedia as of late 2024, researchers can use it to evaluate retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems or cross-lingual RAG approaches. This provides a controlled setting where the knowledge source is known and accessible.

The question of what knowledge should be built into models versus retrieved from external sources remains open. Regional knowledge presents an interesting test case for this trade-off. While one could argue that such specific information should be left to external knowledge sources, we believe that broader factual knowledge generally improves a model’s ability to search for and contextualize additional information. Models that perform better on our dataset are likely to provide more culturally appropriate responses across different regions.

Our dataset supports several research tasks. It provides a challenging benchmark for both textual QA and visual QA, with visual QA proving significantly more difficult.
The dataset also enables studies of cross-lingual consistency that allow researchers to examine how well models transfer knowledge across related languages.

To make the benchmark more accessible, we host it on Codabench
Xu et al. 2022
, where users can test their models. Participants can download the full dev set and the test set without the reference answers. The platform will then score the prediction on the full test set, with results broken by language and modality. In this way, participants can score partial submissions and focus only on a subset of modalities and languages.
On Codabench, we provide all the metrics with the exception of LLM as a judge with M-Prometheus due to hardware limitations.

Our human evaluation provides a resource for developing and validating automatic metrics for open-ended QA. Since human-labeled datasets for this task are rare, our annotations offer a benchmark for future research on reference-based evaluation and metric reliability.

Finally, the dataset can serve as a seed for automatically generating additional training data from Wikipedia or other resources. Researchers can extend our approach to create larger-scale datasets covering more regions and languages, or use similar collection methods for other knowledge domains.

7
Conclusions

We introduce CUS-QA, a regional knowledge dataset covering textual and visual question answering in Czech, Slovak, and Ukrainian. The dataset contains manually curated questions from native speakers, grounded in Wikipedia and focused on local knowledge that is well-known within each country but largely unknown outside it.

Our baseline experiments reveal significant gaps in regional knowledge capabilities of the current LLMs. The best textual QA model achieves over 50% accuracy, while visual QA is much more challenging with accuracy below 30%. We observe substantial cross-lingual inconsistencies, where models sometimes perform better answering regional questions in English rather than the local language.

Through human evaluation, we find that LLM-based metrics correlate well with human judgment for this task, while traditional string-overlap metrics perform surprisingly well due to the factual nature of the answers. These findings suggest that open-ended evaluation of factual QA is more feasible than previously thought.

The dataset addresses three key research needs: evaluating regional knowledge in LLMs, studying cross-lingual generation consistency, and advancing automatic evaluation metrics for open-ended QA. We release both the dataset and human evaluation results to support future research in these areas.
