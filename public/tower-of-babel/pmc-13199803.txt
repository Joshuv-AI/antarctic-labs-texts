Fetal-type rhabdomyoma of female urethra: A rare case report and literature review
Authors: Mohammad Amin Siri, Negar Mohammadi Ganjaroudi, Mahboobeh Chahkandi, Mohammad Vasei, Abdol-Mohammad Kajbafzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExtracardiac rhabdomyoma is an extremely rare benign tumor that may exceptionally present in the genital region of pediatric patients. Among reported vulvovaginal rhabdomyomas, genital-type is the most common histopathologic subtype, mainly affecting middle-aged patients. We reported the first case of urethral rhabdomyoma in a female child, exhibiting histopathologic features consistent with fetal-type rhabdomyoma, which was meticulously excised to preserve urinary continence and prevent functional complications. We discussed the differential diagnosis of interlabial masses in young girls while providing a comprehensive literature review regarding urological manifestations of fetal-type rhabdomyomas and essential steps for the successful management of similar cases.Keywords: Rhabdomyoma, Fetal rhabdomyoma, Urethral neoplasms, Pediatrics, Neoplasms

Introduction

Rhabdomyoma and rhabdomyosarcoma are benign and malignant soft tissue tumors, respectively, characterized by histologic features of striated muscle differentiation, including the expression of desmin and myogenin. Rhabdomyomas are rare mesenchymal tumors that mainly affect the heart and represent about 2 % of striated muscle-derived neoplasms. Rhabdomyoma is categorized into cardiac and extracardiac types based on their site of origin. Extracardiac rhabdomyomas are further classified into fetal-type, adult-type, and genital-type according to histopathological and clinical features. The adult and fetal types predominantly affect the head and neck region in males, while the genital type primarily occurs in the genitalia of adult females.1 Herein, we describe an unusual case of fetal-type rhabdomyoma arising in the urethra of a female child with a one-year follow-up and a literature review regarding this specific pathology.

Case presentation

A two-year-old female was referred to the pediatric urology clinic to evaluate the regrowth of an interlabial mass with no signs of urinary tract obstruction and strangury. The lesion was first noticed 17 months before the referral, initially presenting as a small bulge in the interlabial region. She underwent vaginoscopy, which was unremarkable. Subsequently, the diagnosis of urethral diverticulum was proposed for her. The bulge increased in size, prompting an excision in another center when she was one year old. Pathological investigation of the 1.2 × 1 cm excised mass revealed urothelium tissue with chronic inflammatory components and bundles of thick, striated muscles intermingled with loose fibrotic bands, excluding malignancy.

The mass appeared three months post-excision and gradually increased in size, becoming coarser in macroscopic consistency. Several episodes of bleeding occurred at the dry surface of the lesion, requiring topical management. In referral, physical examination indicated an interlabial mass located posterior to the clitoris, obscuring the external urethral orifice (Fig. 1A); however, micturating was not disturbed. A pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) study was performed to determine the origin and extension of the mass in peripheral structures. As demonstrated in Fig. 1B, MRI revealed a solid mass in the distal portion of the urethra with no sign of involvement of the peripheral structures. Due to the continuous growth and for more precise pathological investigations, a surgical resection was scheduled. Under general anesthesia, a well-circumscribed mass occupying the interlabial space was evident at the anterior wall of the urethra. After performing cystoscopy and vaginoscopy with unremarkable findings, a urinary catheter was inserted. An incision was made posterior to the clitoris, separating the mass from the urethra and peripheral labial tissues. The pedicle-like extension of the mass was dissected from its attachment near the pubic symphysis, resulting in complete resection. Subsequently, urethrolysis and urethroplasty were performed.

Pathological analysis revealed bundles of strap-shaped rhabdoid cells in a fibromyxoid, inflamed stroma. Cross-striations were evident as a sign of skeletal muscle differentiation. No evidence of necrosis or mitotic figures could be found in the specimen. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining revealed that the tumor cells were positive for desmin and myogenin. Moreover, Ki-67 was positive in 1-2 % of the cells. The pathological findings were in favor of myxoid, fetal-type rhabdomyoma (Fig. 2A–E).

The child was discharged five days post-operation without complications. The urinary catheter was removed one week following discharge. At one-year postoperative follow-up, the anatomical features of the vulva were favorable, with no disturbance in voiding or signs of tumor recurrence (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Fetal-type rhabdomyoma is a benign tumor composed of immature skeletal muscle cells, ranging from undifferentiated spindle cells to multinucleated rhabdomyoblasts with cross-striations. It is histologically classified into classic (myxoid) and intermediate (cellular) subtypes based on stromal density and degree of differentiation.2,3 Clinically, classic rhabdomyomas have a preference for the postauricular region of male infants, while intermediate rhabdomyomas affect different sites in the head and neck of males, particularly adults.3, 4

The urological predilection of fetal rhabdomyomas is extremely rare. To the best of our knowledge, two cases of urethral fetal rhabdomyoma have been presented in the literature to date.5,6 Both of these cases were young boys with posterior urethral lesions, while our case is the first female reported with urethral fetal rhabdomyoma. Based on the literature, para-testicular masses appear to be the most common urological presentation; however, further studies are needed to clarify the behavior of these tumors within the genitourinary system.

Lu et al. were the first to report a child with urethral genital rhabdomyoma. They presented an infant female with a growing polypoid mass in the posterior portion of the vaginal opening, connected to the posterior urethra. IHC analysis was positive for desmin and myogenin. No recurrence was evident four months after the lesion excision. Although genital rhabdomyomas, as their name indicates, mainly occur in the genital region, urethral involvement is not a common manifestation. Also, unlike the subject of the mentioned study, genital rhabdomyomas often involve adult females.14

Notably, there is a report of a fetal-type rhabdomyoma involving the posterior urethra with concurrent urinary bladder involvement.6 Moreover, another report has described a genital-type rhabdomyoma affecting the posterior urethra along with the posterior vaginal opening.14 Therefore, a comprehensive assessment, including physical examination, vaginoscopy, and cystoscopy, is essential for evaluating the vagina and lower urinary tract, and for guiding biopsy and histologic evaluations.6

To simultaneously preserve urinary continence while ensuring post-excisional clear margins, a urinary catheter was inserted before excision as a simple step to delineate the urethral anatomy and protect it during dissection, thereby reducing the risk of injury that could lead to urinary incontinence. Additionally, the postoperative catheter allowed for adequate urethral repair following urethroplasty.15

In young girls, interlabial masses may represent a range of differential diagnoses, including a bulged imperforated hymen, paraurethral cyst, embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma, and prolapsed ectopic ureterocele or urethra.16 Histopathologic analysis plays a crucial role in distinguishing between benign and malignant lesions with skeletal muscle differentiation. Special attention should be paid to botryoid rhabdomyosarcoma, an aggressive form of embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma, in which early diagnosis leads to a better prognosis.17 This malignancy typically exhibits alternating hypo- and hypercellular zones with a cambium layer sign, features absent in our case. Ki-67, as a proliferation marker, is highly expressed in aggressive tumors18; however, in our case, Ki-67 was only positive in a few cells, indicating a favorable tumor behavior. The absence of necrosis and mitotic figures further reinforced the non-aggressive behavior of the lesion.

The prevailing literature suggests that effective local excision and clear surgical margins can reduce tumor recurrence in rhabdomyomas.7 While there are reports of tumor regrowth months after surgical excision,19, 20 most retained benign histology without atypia or mitotic figures. Similarly, in our study, the lesion started to regrow after initial excision in another center, while preserving the benign pathology. Nevertheless, it should be noted that Kodet et al. described an 18-month-old female with a tongue mass initially diagnosed as fetal cellular rhabdomyoma with two episodes of recurrence 10 and 22 months after the initial surgical excision. While the first recurrence retained benign histology, the second recurrence revealed a mixed embryonal-alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma. The authors concluded that the initial mass either transformed into a malignant form or it was a highly differentiated rhabdomyosarcoma from the beginning, mistakenly labeled as a benign type.19 These findings underscore the importance of effective local excision with clear margins, precise histological evaluations, and long-term follow-up.

Conclusion

Fetal rhabdomyomas exhibit a rare urological predilection with limited information on tumor behavior in the literature. Considering the reported concurrent involvement of adjacent organs, performing cystoscopy and vaginoscopy (in females), along with histopathologic evaluation, is essential for successful management. To minimize recurrence, complete excision with clear margins is critical. Long-term, timely follow-up is recommended for early detection of recurrence or malignant transformation.
