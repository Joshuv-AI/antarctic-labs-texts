Natural Product Based Hydrogels in Biomedicine: Design Strategies and Crosslinking Mechanisms
Authors: Mengya Zhao, Baixin Kou, Chunyang Lu, Haidong Wang, Guangzhe Li, Lingkun Meng, Mingming Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHydrogels have found extensive applications in the medical field owing to their designability, biocompatibility, and self-supporting characteristics. Natural products, possessing multiple pharmacological activities and relatively low toxicity compared to chemical drugs, exhibit unique advantages in the field of hydrogel-related therapy. This review examines three major design strategies for natural product-based hydrogels from the perspective of how natural products interact with hydrogel networks: self-assembly of natural small molecules, physical encapsulation of natural products, and chemical conjugation of natural products to polymer matrices. These strategies determine the forms in which natural products are incorporated into hydrogels. Natural products may be encapsulated as bioactive components, participate in gelation behavior as matrices or crosslinking agents, or form nanoparticles embedded within the hydrogels. This review further analyzes the effects of these design strategies on hydrogel mechanical properties, retention of biological activity and drug-release behavior, and summarizes their application characteristics in biomedical fields. Finally, the advantages, limitations, and development prospects of the different design strategies are discussed. This review aims to provide guidance for the rational design, synthesis, and application of natural product hydrogels according to the properties of natural products and specific biomedical requirements.Keywords: natural products, hydrogel, design strategy, synthesis mechanisms

Introduction

A gel is a unique class of soft material that exists in a state intermediate between solid and liquid.1 Fundamentally, it is a system composed of a dispersed phase and a continuous medium, forming a three-dimensional (3D) network with limited fluidity through various types of physical or chemical interactions.2 The dispersed phase may consist of polymers, sol-gel materials, or colloidal particles.3–5 Based on the nature of the continuous medium, gels can be broadly categorized into hydrogels,6–8 organogels,9–11 and aerogels.12–15 Among them, hydrogels represent an important subclass that has attracted considerable interest due to their tunable mechanical properties, excellent flexibility, and facile functionalization. These characteristics have enabled their application in flexible electronics,16,17 agriculture,18 environmental purification,19,20 and particularly in biomedical fields.21,22 Their highly hydrated and porous networks can mimic key physical features of the natural extracellular matrix (ECM), provide structural support for cells, and facilitate efficient mass transport. Consequently, researchers frequently utilize hydrogels in drug delivery, tissue repair, and the development of novel drug carriers (Scheme 1).23–27

In traditional hydrogel drug delivery systems, research has mainly focused on synthetic chemical drugs and protein or peptide-based biologics. Although synthetic chemical drugs generally exhibit clear therapeutic efficacy, their long-term use may lead to drug resistance.28,29 In contrast, proteins and peptide drugs possess relatively high biological activity but poor stability and are costly.30 These limitations have stimulated growing interest in natural bioactive extracts derived from plants and microorganisms, including polysaccharides, polyphenols, and flavonoids.31–34 These substances generally exhibit good biocompatibility and may act on multiple biological targets. However, due to their complex composition, their stability and controllability still require further optimization. In this context, incorporating natural bioactive compounds into hydrogel networks through controllable doping and coupling strategies can enhance the stability and controlled delivery of these compounds while simultaneously imparting additional functionalities to the hydrogels. The incorporation of natural compounds containing catechol groups can facilitate adhesion through multiple interactions, including hydrogen bonding, coordination, and π–π bonding, thereby significantly enhancing the tissue adhesion properties of hydrogels.26,35 By utilizing the esterification reaction between catechol and boric acid derivatives, reactive oxygen species (ROS)-responsive hydrogels can be fabricated to achieve controlled drug release.36–39 Similarly, pH-sensitive hydrogels constructed via Schiff base linkages formed by amine–aldehyde condensation can trigger drug release in response to microenvironmental changes.40–43 Notably, the presence of reversible chemical bonds often imparts self-healing properties, enabling the hydrogel to spontaneously restore its original structure after damage. In summary, the incorporation of diverse natural products greatly expands the therapeutic potential of hydrogels.

Although existing reviews have discussed the application of hydrogels in the pharmaceutical field, comprehensive evaluations focusing specifically on natural product-based hydrogels in biomedicine remain limited. To address this gap, the present review systematically summarizes the design principles and crosslinking mechanisms of this type of hydrogel. Emphasizing the novel functional properties conferred by natural products and their potential advantages in biomedical applications. By providing in-depth discussion and forward-looking perspectives, this review aims to offer valuable insights for researchers and to better align hydrogel research with the practical demands of clinical applications. Moreover, with the continued accumulation of materials data and advances in computational methodologies, machine learning is increasingly being applied to the prediction of hydrogel gelation behavior, formulation optimization, and drug release modeling. Accordingly, this review further discusses the potential applications of data-driven approaches in the rational design of natural product-based hydrogels.

Mechanism of Hydrogel Formation

Hydrogels are typically formed by the chemical or physical crosslinking of matrices containing hydrophilic groups.44,45 They can be broadly classified into natural and synthetic polymers.46,47 Among natural matrices, hyaluronic acid (HA),48–50 gelatin (Gel),51 chitosan (CS),52 and sodium alginate (SA)53,54 are the most widely used commercial representatives. With increasing application demands, synthetic polymers such as polyacrylic acid and its derivatives,55 polyvinyl alcohol (PVA),56,57 and polyethylene glycol (PEG)58–60 have gained growing attention. Compared with synthetic counterparts, natural polymers offer advantages in terms of biocompatibility and biodegradability,61,62 whereas synthetic polymers are more favorable for multifunctional applications and large-scale manufacturing.63 In addition, recent studies suggest that hydrogel construction is no longer confined to traditional polymeric systems. The emergence of self-assembled systems has introduced new research directions. Biomacromolecules such as peptides and sugar derivatives, as well as some natural small molecules including flavonoids, can self-assemble into hydrogels via noncovalent interactions, including hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions (Figure 1A).64–68 Overall, hydrogel formation generally relies on the presence of hydrophilic moieties such as hydroxyl, carboxyl, amino, and sulfonic acid groups, which are crosslinked into a 3D network.

Another classical classification method is based on the crosslinking mechanism, whereby hydrogels are categorized into physical and chemical types, formed through physical and chemical crosslinking, respectively (Figure 1B and C).69 Physical hydrogels rely mainly on noncovalent interactions such as hydrogen bonding, van der Waals forces, hydrophobic interactions, and ionic bonding between polymer chains.70,71 Among these, thermosensitive hydrogels undergo structural helicalization and phase transition during freeze–thaw cycles.72,73 In contrast to physical hydrogels, chemically crosslinked hydrogels are stabilized by covalent bonds, establishing permanent chemical connections between molecular chains. Their mechanical strength, pore structure, and functional properties can be tuned by controlling parameters such as the degree of crosslinking, solvent polarity, and polymerization conditions.

Different crosslinking strategies impart hydrogels with distinct structural and functional characteristics. Physically crosslinked hydrogels are typically reversible and often responsive to external stimuli. They exhibit relatively rapid self-healing due to the dynamic nature of noncovalent interactions. However, their mechanical strength is generally limited, and the network may collapse in complex physiological environments as a result of crosslinking site dissociation.44,74 In contrast, chemically crosslinked hydrogels provide enhanced mechanical strength and structural stability. Their synthetic process usually requires initiators or crosslinking agents, although residual chemicals may compromise biocompatibility.75,76 Furthermore, the irreversibility of covalent bonds restricts self-healing, which in many cases must be achieved through the incorporation of dynamically reversible covalent interactions.77 In practical applications, neither physical nor chemical crosslinking offers absolute superiority. The selection of an appropriate strategy should instead be determined by a balanced consideration of mechanical performance, functional demands, and the specific requirements of the intended application.

In drug delivery applications, drug incorporation strategies are generally classified into physical encapsulation and chemical conjugation. Physical encapsulation relies on intermolecular interactions to achieve drug loading, whereas chemical conjugation immobilizes drugs within the hydrogel network via covalent bonds. In addition to these methods, hydrogel formation can also be induced by enzymatic reactions or solvent exchange.78 These distinct construction strategies, together with the drug-loading modes, collectively influence the network structure and performance characteristics of the hydrogels. The oxidative coupling of phenolic groups can be initiated by enzyme-catalyzed reactions, which enhances crosslinking density and endows hydrogels with superior mechanical strength and shear-thinning behavior.79 Another strategy, the solvent-exchange method, employs osmotic pressure to replace the organic phase with an aqueous phase, thereby producing hydrogels with unique structures and functions.80,81 The key advantage of this method lies in its ability to generate hydrogels with high water content and prolonged moisturizing capacity. These methods offer greater flexibility in the preparation of hydrogels, which is highly beneficial for further enriching the structural design and functional properties of hydrogels.

Introducing Natural Products into Hydrogels

So far, three main strategies have been developed for incorporating natural products into hydrogels: (i) self-assembled hydrogels derived from natural small molecules, (ii) physically embedded natural products as active ingredients within hydrogel matrices, and (iii) chemical conjugation of natural products with polymer matrices. These strategies broadly reflect the manner in which natural products interact with and participate in the hydrogel matrix. In self-assembled systems, natural small molecules function directly as network-forming units, whereas physically incorporated natural products are retained within an independently formed hydrogel matrix mainly through noncovalent interactions. Chemical conjugation, by contrast, involves the covalent attachment of natural products to polymer chains, allowing them to be introduced either as pendant bioactive groups or as crosslinking moieties. When natural products are introduced in the form of complexes or assemblies, their incorporation strategy is likewise determined by how these natural-product-containing components are associated with the surrounding hydrogel matrix, rather than by the interactions involved in forming the complexes themselves. Metal-polyphenol complexes, for example, are assembled through coordination between polyphenols and metal ions, while their association with the surrounding polymer network may still rely primarily on physical entrapment or noncovalent interactions. Such systems can therefore be discussed according to the way the natural-product-containing component is incorporated into the hydrogel matrix. Self-Assembled Hydrogels of Natural Small Molecules

Self-assembly refers to the spontaneous organization of secondary building units into ordered structures.96,97 In the context of hydrogels, self-assembled systems derived from natural small molecules are typically formed through noncovalent interactions, including hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking, and hydrophobic interactions.98 These systems mainly rely on reversible intermolecular interactions to construct hydrogel networks, and their drug release behavior is generally regulated by the stability of supramolecular structures, host-guest interactions, stimuli responsive and pharmacokinetics. Increasing evidence shows that natural product-derived small molecules can self-assemble into hydrogels even in the absence of a polymer matrix.99,100 Such natural small molecules that can be self-assembled to form hydrogels generally have a rigid backbone. At the same time, they are covalently connected to multiple polar hydrophilic groups rich in hydroxyl groups, carboxyl groups, glycosides, etc., showing obvious amphiphilic properties as a whole.

In 2013, Wu et al reported the self-assembly of sodium glycyrrhetinate into a supramolecular hydrogel, identifying dipole–dipole interactions between the sodium carboxylate groups as the primary driving force for gelation.101 This work represents an early representative example of supramolecular hydrogel formation by a natural triterpenoid small molecule. The formation of such hydrogels is generally triggered by external stimuli that enhance intermolecular interactions, leading to the generation of ordered structures such as nanofibers or micelles, which subsequently form a hydrogel network.68,102 For example, chlorogenic acid can self-assemble into a hydrogel in sodium bicarbonate solution when triggered by ultrasound.82 Temperature is another common trigger: freezing and thawing cycles can directly induce the self-assembly of natural small molecules such as glycyrrhizic acid, rutin, and rhubarbic acid into reversible hydrogels.83–86 These hydrogels often exhibit sol–gel transitions upon reheating, with the gel state restored after cooling. Beyond temperature, pH also plays a crucial role in self-assembly. For instance, mangiferin forms hydrogels optimally within a pH range of 7.0–9.0.87 Li et al further reported the synthesis of sodium aescinate self-gelling powder, SA-self-gel-P, by optimizing temperature and pH conditions.88 Under strongly alkaline conditions (pH ≥ 11), sodium aescinate struggles to undergo self-assembly due to electrostatic repulsion. When the pH is decreased to neutral or slightly alkaline (≈7.6–8.6), hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions are significantly enhanced. The synergistic effect of these interactions drives the self-assembly of sodium saponins into higher-order aggregates, forming β-sheet ribbon-like structures that further cross-link into a 3D hydrogel network (Figure 2A). The hydrogel is freeze-dried to produce a self-gelling powder. When applied to a wound, the powder rapidly absorbs blood and quickly restores intermolecular interactions to re-gelation. The formed hydrogel networks can achieve sustained drug release through structural relaxation and gradual degradation, with degradation times ranging from approximately 36 to 96 h. pH acts as a key factor in regulating gelation behavior during this process.

The gelation process of self-assembled hydrogels derived from natural small molecules does not require additional matrices, carriers, or crosslinking agents, thereby ensuring excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability.103,104 However, hydrogels formed solely through nonspecific physical interactions usually exhibit limited mechanical strength, poor structural stability, and uncontrollable network architectures.29 To overcome these drawbacks, strategies such as metal ion coordination or multi-network integration have been employed. For example, Liang et al developed a sustained-release Ag+ coordinated Rhein hydrogel (Rhe@Ag) for the treatment of infected wounds.89 In this system, the incorporation of Ag+ into the prepared rhubarb acid hydrogel increases elastic modulus, thereby markedly improving its mechanical performance. Yuan et al constructed a thiolated chitosan (TCS)/puerarin (PUE) hybrid hydrogel (TCS10/PUE) as an intelligent drug delivery carrier.90 Compared with the single PUE hydrogel, which exhibited insufficient strength and stability, the TCS10/PUE composite showed significantly enhanced storage modulus, stronger elasticity, and a markedly shortened gelation time (Figure 2B).

Physical Embedding of Natural Products in Hydrogels

Unlike self-assembled hydrogel, in the physical embedding system, the natural product itself does not participate in the construction of the gel skeleton, but is added as a filler during the synthesis process (Figure 3). In such systems, the release of bioactive compounds is generally governed by water penetration-induced network swelling, diffusion through the porous network, dissociation of reversible physical crosslinks, and matrix degradation. The main advantages of this approach are its simplicity and the capacity to incorporate natural components without chemical modification, thus maximizing the preservation of their intrinsic chemical structures and multitarget biological activities. For instance, Xu et al systematically investigated the effects of nine different polyphenols on the gelation behavior of gellan gum and elucidated their interaction mechanisms.105 The results indicate that polyphenols primarily form noncovalent bonds with gellan gum through hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and electrostatic interactions, with hydrogen bonding playing a dominant role in enhancing gel strength and maintaining structural stability. In addition, hydroxyl group content is considered a key structural parameter influencing the storage modulus of the gels, although the overall hydrogel properties are also jointly regulated by molecular structure, solubility, and hydrophobicity. Xiang et al developed a physically crosslinked hydrogel for cartilage repair, composed of PVA, tannic acid (TA), Gel, HA and icariin, a compound known for its cartilage regeneration capacity.91 Icariin molecules were embedded within the hydrogel network via hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding. The PTGH+Icariin hydrogel was further reinforced through freeze-thaw cycling and hydrogen bond-driven crosslinking, thereby avoiding the use of potentially toxic chemical crosslinking agents. Due to its excellent fatigue resistance, the PTGH+Icariin hydrogel effectively recovers its deformation and maintains structural integrity after undergoing 10 consecutive loading-unloading cycles, making it highly suitable for cartilage repair. The release profile showed an initial burst release followed by a gradual decrease in the release rate, with the cumulative release reaching approximately 70% by 300 h. This behavior reflected the combined contributions of the rapid diffusion of icariin located near the hydrogel surface and its subsequent diffusion through the interconnected porous network.

Schematic illustration of the physical embedding of natural products in hydrogel networks.

In addition, this strategy represents a deep integration of biological activity and material properties: active ingredients of natural products endow hydrogels with biofunctions which absent in the polymer matrix, while the finite network of the hydrogel serves as an ideal carrier, enabling controlled release and prolonged therapeutic effects. Zivari-Ghader et al developed a PCA/HPCE hydrogel dressing by incorporating an extract from Hypericum perforatum callus (HPCE) into a polymer matrix composed of PVA, CS and SA. Owing to antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antifibrotic properties of HPCE, the composite hydrogel effectively promoted wound healing ability while preventing scar formation.92 In this system, HPCE was incorporated through hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions with PCA, which enhanced interfacial interactions between the matrix and extract, ultimately yielding a denser and more stable hydrogel network.

Physical embedding is a common method of adding natural drugs to hydrogel. This method can retain the original active group and key structure of the drug. Therefore, the biological activity of drugs is generally not significantly affected. However, this method often causes the rapid release of drugs. This problem is also an inherent limitation of the physical drug-loaded hydrogel system itself. Curcumin is a typical natural polyphenol compound. Researchers often use physical encapsulation to introduce curcumin into the hydrogel matrix.106 In this system, curcumin is mainly confined within the porous hydrogel network and partially retained through weak non-covalent interactions. It has been reported that in vitro experiments, alginate saline gel containing curcumin will have obvious initial rapid release.107 This rapid release may achieve immediate therapeutic effects, but at the same time, it also faces the problems of poor dose control and rapid depletion of loaded drugs. Consequently, such systems often struggle to maintain stable therapeutic efficacy over extended periods, especially in applications that require continuous drug exposure, such as chronic wound healing and tumor treatment. Therefore, in the context of the application that requires continuous drug administration, it is necessary to introduce more effective strategies to achieve precise regulation of the drug release process.

For hydrophobic natural products, the performance of physical encapsulation also depends on the dispersion state of the bioactive components within the hydrogel network. Owing to their limited aqueous solubility, these compounds are prone to uneven dispersion and unstable release. Accordingly, micelles, liposomes, or polymeric nanoparticles are often employed to improve drug loading and local retention. It should be noted that physicochemical characteristics of the carriers, including surface charge, surface hydrophilicity, and surface modification, may influence complement activation, macrophage uptake, and cytotoxicity.108–110 Therefore, the biocompatibility of physically encapsulated systems depends not only on the natural product and the hydrogel matrix themselves, but also on payload dispersion and carrier design.

Chemical Conjugation of Natural Product Hydrogels

Chemical conjugation-based natural product hydrogels refer to systems in which bioactive natural molecules are covalently tethered to the hydrogel backbone. Typically, either the hydrogel matrix or the natural product molecule is first chemically modified, and then achieve a stable connection through different types of covalent bonds.111 In such systems, the release of bioactive molecules is highly designable, governed primarily by covalent bond cleavage, linker degradation, and hydrogel network degradation triggered by enzymatic, pH, or redox stimuli. Functional groups in natural products that participate in conjugation may also contribute to their pharmacological effects. Therefore, the conjugation site can influence the retention of bioactivity. For flavonoids, the 3′,4′-dihydroxy group on the B ring is a key structural basis for their free radical scavenging activity, and O-methylation of this structure reduces their antioxidant capacity.112–114 In contrast, modification of the 5- or 7-hydroxyl groups in quercetin has a relatively minor effect on its antioxidant capacity.115 When key phenolic hydroxyl groups are used for covalent coupling, the corresponding function may be impaired due to the occupation of the active site. Therefore, the selection of conjugation sites must be determined based on the structure-activity relationship and activity evaluation of the specific compound.

Based on the appropriate selection of binding sites, natural products can form covalent bonds with polymer chains, thereby reducing drug degradation in the intracellular microenvironment and significantly enhancing drug stability.36,116 The COL/HA-E/Alg composite hydrogel system, which uses EGCG as the functionalizing coupling component, provides a representative example of balancing chemical conjugation with the preservation of biological activity. Kang et al developed this multifunctional hydrogel for the treatment of chemotherapy-induced oral mucositis (CIOM) (Figure 4).93 In this system, epigallocatechin-3-O-gallate (EGCG) was chemically modified into a dimer and covalently conjugated with HA, then combined with dopamine-modified methacrylate–alginate (AlgDA-MA), and methacrylate-modified recombinant human collagen III (rhCol3MA). EGCG not only acts as a multifunctional crosslinker to stabilize the skeleton, but also provides anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activity. The composite hydrogel reduced NF-κB1 and IL-1β mRNA expression and promoted macrophage polarization toward an M2-like phenotype, suggesting attenuation of NF-κB-related inflammatory signaling. Free EGCG has been reported to suppress NF-κB signaling by inhibiting IKK activity, reducing IκBα phosphorylation and degradation, limiting RelA/p65 nuclear translocation, and modulating NF-κB-responsive genes through epigenetic mechanisms.117,118 These findings provide possible mechanistic explanations for the anti-inflammatory effects of EGCG, although their relevance to the dimerized, HA-conjugated form incorporated into the COL/HA-E/Alg hydrogel has not yet been established.

Distinct from physical encapsulation, the natural active molecules are not embedded within the hydrogel network but are instead immobilized on the network structure. In this process, polyphenols containing catechol, pyrogallol, or aldehyde groups can serve not only as active components in drug delivery systems but also as crosslinking agents directly involved in hydrogel network formation. As an early example of catechol-containing natural products used for chemical conjugation of hydrogels, Kosaraju et al employed the plant-derived phenolic compound caffeic acid to cross-link gelatin in 2010 and reported, for the first time, the formation of a thermoirreversible gelatin gel at 60 °C.119 Building on this work, catechol-containing natural polyphenols have evolved from simple cross-linking agents into multifunctional components that provide both network stabilization and biological activity. These studies demonstrate that natural products can function as both bioactive agents and structural components of hydrogel networks. Their crosslinking behavior is determined not only by functional-group identity but also by molecular scaffold and local chemical environment. Both flavonoids and anthraquinones possess aromatic conjugated frameworks, and some members of these classes contain catechol moieties that may contribute to hydrogel network formation through phenolic oxidation, metal coordination, and hydrogen-bonding interactions.120,121 Nevertheless, the two classes differ in their core structures and in the local chemical environments surrounding the catechol groups. Catechol-containing flavonoids may preferentially undergo oxidation-induced covalent coupling with nucleophilic groups in the hydrogel matrix. By contrast, hydroxyanthraquinones may exhibit more condition-dependent interactions involving phenolic hydroxyl groups, anthraquinone carbonyl groups, metal coordination, and redox equilibria. However, direct comparative studies of these two classes within the same hydrogel system remain limited.

In functionalized hydrogel systems incorporating natural products, these substances serve not only as active ingredients but also as crosslinking nodes or structural control units. By adjusting the incorporation ratio of natural product molecules, the crosslinking density of the hydrogel can be effectively modified, thereby enabling precise control over its mechanical properties, including elastic modulus, tensile strength, and toughness. Wan et al developed a dual-network hydrogel composed of a microcrystalline PVA framework and a chitosan–emodin (CS–emodin) network, which was applied as a wound dressing to accelerate healing.42 In this case, two carbonyl groups of emodin react with the amino groups of CS via Schiff-base addition, forming a dynamic covalent crosslinked network. Acting as a natural pharmaceutical crosslinker, emodin not only imparted biological activity but also increased crosslinking density, thereby enhancing the mechanical strength of the hydrogel. Moreover, the tensile strength and fracture strain could be tuned by varying the emodin concentration. As the concentration of emodin increased, the tensile strength of the hydrogel improved from 325 kPa to 1070 kPa, with toughness increasing sixfold, demonstrating its dual role in both functionality and mechanical reinforcement. By forming a covalent bond with the polymer chain, the drug release is regulated by bond breakage rather than passive diffusion, which can effectively reduce the premature diffusion of drugs from the hydrogel system. Increasing studies have demonstrated the feasibility of this strategy for various natural compounds. Feng et al incorporated curcumin into a polyurethane network via in situ copolymerization, where its phenolic hydroxyl groups reacted with isocyanate groups to form urethane bonds, thereby stably incorporating it into the hydrogel network.122 The release of curcumin is coupled with the degradation process of the hydrogel, with a degradation period lasting up to three months or longer, demonstrating the system’s potential for sustained-release. This chemical conjugation strategy not only achieves more prolonged and controlled drug release but also significantly enhances drug stability.

When dynamic reversible bonds are introduced through chemical conjugation process, enabling the network to reconstruct its structure after rupture through bond rearrangement or exchange. Natural products used as chemical crosslinkers can simultaneously endow the hydrogel with self-healing and smart stimuli-responsive release properties. For example, dynamic covalent bonds, such as borate ester and reversible imine linkages, can break and re-form under specific stimuli, thereby enabling autonomous repair.123 Shi et al reported a multifunctional hydrogel sensor, Ag@PDA-(CSPBA/CSGA), which integrates wound healing and sensing capabilities.94 The material was constructed by embedding Ag-decorated polydopamine (Ag@PDA) nanoparticles into a chitosan matrix modified with gallic acid (CSGA) and 3-carboxyphenylboronic acid (CSPBA). In this system, the pyrogallol moiety of gallic acid (GA) formed reversible borate ester bonds with the phenylboronic acid groups of CSPBA, thereby conferring self-healing capacity (Figure 5A). Moreover, GA enhanced the hydrogel’s antioxidant and antibacterial efficacy against Staphylococcus aureus (98.08%) and Escherichia coli (92.76%), while the dynamic borate ester bonds endowed it with ROS responsiveness and self-repairing ability. By combining flexible electronics with biomedical functionality, this hydrogel offers a promising strategy for wearable health monitoring and chronic wound management. Su et al designed a CFT hydrogel with dual dynamic covalent crosslinking.95 In this system, carboxymethyl chitosan (CMCS) and 2-formylphenylboronic acid (2-FPBA) first formed dynamic Schiff-base bonds. Subsequently, the addition of TA established a second network through reversible borate ester bonds (Figure 5B). This dual crosslinking strategy endowed the hydrogel with strong wet tissue adhesion and pH-responsive drug release. Under the acidic microenvironment of diabetic wounds (pH 5.5), the hydrolysis of borate bonds accelerates hydrogel degradation. Compared to neutral conditions (pH 7.4) and weakly acidic conditions (pH 6.8), the cumulative release rate of TA significantly increased at this point, reaching 85.33%.

In summary, chemical conjugation is an effective strategy for improving the stability and controllability of natural product delivery from hydrogels. However, the application of this strategy requires not only a focus on preserving biological activity but also comprehensive consideration of the impact of conjugation chemistry, reaction conditions, and post-reaction processing on the biocompatibility of the system. Residual coupling reagents, catalysts, and reaction by-products may introduce additional biosafety risks. Therefore, coupling methods involving low-toxicity reagents and mild reaction conditions should be preferentially selected, and thorough purification should be performed to minimize residual contaminants. The design of chemically conjugated systems requires a careful balance between payload stability, release behavior, biocompatibility, and the retention of key pharmacophores.

Properties of Natural Products and Their Introduction Forms in Hydrogels

Based on their unique chemical structures and molecular electrostatic potential, natural products can interact with hydrogels in distinct ways: serving as the matrix for gel formation, being encapsulated as bioactive components, or as a crosslinking agent to realize stimulus response ability. These diverse interaction modes not only modulate the mechanical performance of hydrogels but also influence their drug release profiles, biological activity, and therapeutic efficacy. Consequently, elucidating the interaction mechanisms between different classes of natural products and hydrogels is essential for the rational design and optimization of natural product–hydrogel composite systems (Table 3).

Different Active Ingredients and Their Forms of Existence in Hydrogel

Hydrophilic Natural Products in Hydrogel Construction

Hydrophilic natural products such as polysaccharides, polyphenols, phenolic acids, and saponins exhibit good water solubility due to their abundant hydrophilic functional groups (eg, hydroxyl groups, sugar chains), providing a core advantage in hydrogel construction.82,88,137 Recent research has intensely focused on polysaccharides and polyphenols owing to their advantages in biocompatibility, modifiability, and inherent biological activity. However, there are certain differences in their main roles within hydrogels. Polysaccharides typically contribute to network formation through their molecular chains, whereas polyphenols primarily serve as functionalizing components or dynamic crosslinking nodes that regulate the adhesion, mechanical properties, and stimulus-responsive behavior of hydrogels.

Hydrogels Based on Natural Bioactive Polysaccharides

Natural bioactive polysaccharides are natural polymer compounds widely used as carriers in drug delivery systems due to their excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability.138 They can partially self-assemble via intermolecular interactions such as hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic effects.124,139 For instance, alkali-soluble polysaccharides from Poria cocos can form hydrogels under acidic conditions without chemical crosslinkers. Gelation mechanism was verified by rheology, fluorescence spectroscopy, and cyclic voltammetry measurements, mainly attributed to hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions.124 However, most polysaccharide solutions, despite their high viscosity, struggle to form stable 3D networks because of limited crosslinking sites and weak intermolecular forces. Thus, crosslinking agents or blending with other matrices are often required to promote gel formation.140 Accordingly, such systems are more accurately classified as semi-self-assembled hybrid crosslinked hydrogels, distinct from natural small-molecule self-assembled hydrogels.

Current research often employs other polysaccharides, metal ions or polymers to help stabilize the weak skeleton. This strategy introduces additional crosslinking sites and functional groups, thereby strengthening intermolecular interactions and yielding a more compact and stable hydrogel network.141 For example, Tang et al developed a multinetwork hydrogel (COSGP@R) skeleton composed of oxidized Astragalus polysaccharide, SA, and CMCS, loaded with conductive resveratrol nanoparticles.125 The oxidized Astragalus polysaccharide functioned as crosslinking nodes, significantly improving the mechanical strength and stability of the hydrogel, which was applied in diabetic wound repair and muscle function assessment (shown in Figure 6A). Yang et al for the first time developed a pH-responsive polysaccharide hydrogel by crosslinking glycyrrhiza polysaccharide with Ca2⁺ and systematically examined the effects of ion, polysaccharide concentration, and pH range on its gelation behavior.126 The neutral homogeneous polysaccharide (GP1-2) isolated from Glycyrrhiza uralensis Fisch. required relatively high concentrations (20.0–50.0% w/v) to form a hydrogel. Increasing the Ca2⁺ concentration further enhanced the stability of the resulting gel network. Rhein was used as a model drug to evaluate the sustained-release performance of the hydrogel. At pH 9 and 10, the hydrogel formed a more stable three-dimensional network, resulting in improved Rhein encapsulation and prolonged release. This work highlights the multidimensional value of natural bioactive polysaccharides in drug delivery systems, serving both as functional carriers and as pharmacologically active components through their inherent bioactivities.

Recent studies have shown that natural small molecules can also act as “gelling” or “structure-directing” agents, markedly enhancing the adhesion behavior of polysaccharide hydrogels. For instance, Zheng et al isolated a novel homogeneous polysaccharide, APS13, from Astragalus and co-assembled it with glycyrrhizic acid to construct a GA–APS hydrogel.32 The viscosity of the hydrogel increased significantly with rising APS concentration, indicating that the introduction of APS promoted gelation behavior (Figure 6B). Compared to APS13 and glycyrrhizic acid alone, the composite hydrogel demonstrated superior tissue adhesion, gastric retention time (more than two hours), and therapeutic efficacy. This system effectively addressed the rapid degradation of conventional dressings in the stomach and offered a promising strategy for gastric ulcer therapy.

Hydrogels Functionalized with Polyphenols

Polyphenols are natural compounds characterized by multiple phenolic hydroxyl groups, which endow them with strong antioxidant and free radical scavenging capacities.142,143 Beyond their intrinsic antioxidant activity, polyphenols can significantly enhance hydrogel performance through several mechanisms. Their catechol moieties strengthen adhesion between hydrogels and biological tissues, while their chemical reactivity allows them to act as natural crosslinkers, improving mechanical strength via covalent or noncovalent interactions. Specifically, polyphenols can coordinate with metal ions (eg, Fe3⁺, Zn2⁺, Cu2⁺) to form metal–phenolic networks (MPNs).144 MPNs possess dynamic reversibility, which imparts self-healing and environmental responsiveness to materials. Incorporating MPNs into hydrogels creates multi-crosslinked structures with enhanced functionality. For example, Zou et al developed a multifunctional hydrogel (CTG hydrogel) by integrating TA-Ga3⁺ with CS (Figure 7).127 Ga3⁺ served as a Lewis acid center, forming stable coordination bonds with the phenolic hydroxyl groups of TA, thereby imparting antimicrobial activity and pH responsiveness to the hydrogel. Under acidic wound conditions (pH < 6.0), the dissociation of metal–phenol coordination bonds facilitated the release of Ga3⁺ ions, producing antibacterial effects while triggering TA release to mitigate oxidative stress.

Traditional hydrogels often suffer from inadequate mechanical strength and limited functionality. The reversible dissociation and reformation of metal–phenolic coordination bonds enable the hydrogel network to rapidly recover after mechanical damage. Deng et al developed a nanoparticle-loaded hydrogel (PVA–GA/Zn2⁺/SA–PBA) for the treatment of infected burn wounds.128 This composite hydrogel, composed of sodium alginate grafted with 3-aminophenylboronic acid (SA–PBA), gallic acid-modified polyvinyl alcohol (PVA–GA), Zn2⁺, and chitosan-coated borneol nanoparticles, exhibited pronounced anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects. Remarkably, it demonstrated self-healing within 3 minutes, attributed to the reversible coordination between Zn2⁺ and the phenolic hydroxyl groups of GA. Furthermore, the synergistic interactions among metal–ligand coordination, covalent, and noncovalent bonds within the crosslinked network substantially enhanced the hydrogel’s mechanical strength, effectively overcoming the brittleness typical of conventional single-network hydrogels.

Overall, hydrophilic natural products can participate in hydrogel network formation, modulate material properties through intermolecular interactions, and confer therapeutic functions through their inherent bioactivities. Their structural characteristics, incorporation strategies, and interactions with the hydrogel matrix collectively govern gelation behavior, mechanical properties, and biological performance. These factors provide a rational basis for the design and fabrication of hydrogels containing hydrophilic natural products.

Hydrophobic Natural Products in Hydrogel Construction

Hydrophobic natural products such as terpenoids, alkaloids, and lignans often exhibit poor solubility, uneven dispersion, and unstable release in hydrogel systems, limiting their biomedical applications.145–148 To address these issues, nanocarrier-assisted strategies have been widely adopted to construct efficient delivery systems. Polymeric nanoparticles, micelles, or liposomes encapsulate hydrophobic compounds within their cores, forming stable complexes that can be integrated into hydrogel networks for controlled release.129,149,150

Lipid–Polymer Nanocarriers

Lipid–polymer nanocarriers combine the structural stability of polymeric nanoparticles with the favorable interfacial compatibility of lipid materials. They can encapsulate hydrophobic natural products within a hydrophobic core or polymeric matrix, thereby reducing aggregation and degradation in aqueous environments. Further incorporation of drug-loaded nanocarriers into hydrogel networks can restrict their rapid diffusion, making this strategy particularly suitable for the sustained local delivery of hydrophobic natural products. Xian et al reported a hybrid delivery platform (ELE@LPNPs@Gel) combining lipid–polymer nanoparticles (LPNPs) and a dopamine-modified hyaluronic acid (HADA) hydrogel for the sustained delivery of the hydrophobic compound Elemene (ELE) (Figure 8).129 ELE was loaded into PLGA-DSPE-PEG nanoparticles and embedded in the HADA hydrogel. The DSPE-PEG shell improved dispersion, stability, and biocompatibility, while the hydrogel matrix provided a secondary sustained-release mechanism. Compared to ELE nanoparticles (ELE@LPNPs), which maintain activity for only 12 days, the hydrogel form of ELE achieved prolonged local retention in the subcutaneous tissue, persisting for up to 20 days while maintaining significant signal intensity. By day 20, over 50% of ELE remained, indicating its excellent sustained drug release capability. Concurrently, the ELE hydrogel achieved enhanced postoperative tumor inhibition, with a breast cancer recurrence inhibition rate (96%) significantly superior to that of free ELE@LPNPs (65.5%). This nanocarrier–hydrogel system effectively overcomes the formulation challenges of hydrophobic natural products, offering a promising platform for localized, long-term therapy.

Micellar Drug Delivery Systems

Micelles, formed by the self-assembly of amphiphilic block or graft copolymers, possess hydrophilic shells that ensure colloidal stability in aqueous environments while efficiently encapsulating hydrophobic drugs within their cores.151–153 This amphiphilic architecture enables effective solubilization of hydrophobic natural products, enhancing cellular uptake via endocytosis and thereby improving their bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy.154 Embedding micelles into hydrogel matrices further refines drug delivery performance: the micellar domains modulate diffusion kinetics, achieving sustained and controlled drug release while mitigating burst effects. Chen et al designed an injectable hydrogel (PHPF@AD) comprising Pluronic F127 micelles encapsulating andrographolide (AD) within a 4-arm PEG network.130 The hydrophobic core of the micelle promotes AD loading, while the hydrogel framework provides mechanical stability and local retention, enabling sustained release over 24 days. This prolongs the therapeutic effect for osteoarthritis treatment and reduces the frequency of administration. This dual-component system synergistically integrates the solubilization and delivery advantages of micelles with the controlled-release and localization capabilities of hydrogels, offering an effective strategy for the administration of hydrophobic natural products.

Compared with hydrophilic natural products, hydrophobic natural products are generally difficult to disperse directly and uniformly within hydrogel networks. Their aqueous dispersibility can be improved by prior encapsulation in lipid–polymer nanoparticles or micelles. Further incorporation of these drug-loaded nanostructures into hydrogels allows the nanocarriers to regulate drug release while the hydrogel enhances local retention.

Hydrogel Composites Containing Nanomedical Formulations

Nanoparticle formulations can improve the aqueous dispersibility and structural stability of natural medicines through encapsulation, adsorption, or confinement within pores. Hydrogel networks, in turn, can restrict the migration and rapid diffusion of nanoparticle formulations, thereby prolonging their retention time at the site of action. Therefore, combining these two components allows for complementary effects in the loading, protection, and release of active ingredients, thereby enhancing the delivery efficiency and sustained action of natural products.

Hydrogel Composites with Nanoparticles

Many natural products are classified as “undruggable” due to intrinsic limitations such as poor aqueous solubility, rapid metabolism, potential systemic toxicity, and susceptibility to degradation under light, pH, or enzymatic conditions.155–158 Formulating these compounds into nanoparticles, or encapsulating them within nanocarriers, offers an effective strategy to overcome these barriers by improving solubility, stability, and pharmacokinetic profiles. Integrating drug-loaded nanoparticles into hydrogel matrices further enhances therapeutic performance by enabling controlled release, prolonged local retention, and spatiotemporal drug delivery. Especially in oncological applications, the nanoscale dimensions and high surface area of nanoparticles facilitate enhanced permeability and retention within tumor tissues while minimizing off-target toxicity to healthy cells, thereby improving overall treatment efficacy.159

Xiao et al developed a mesoporous polydopamine (MPDA) nanoparticle–hydrogel composite incorporating in situ–grown Cu/Bi nanoparticles for infected wound healing.131 Owing to its high surface area and uniform mesoporous structure, MPDA effectively encapsulated berberine via π–π stacking, markedly improving its loading capacity, chemical stability, and overcoming its intrinsic solubility and degradation issues. The hydrogel matrix offered a 3D scaffold that stabilized and uniformly dispersed the nanoparticles, enabling sustained drug release and local therapeutic action. Similarly, Wang et al engineered a PUE–complexed probiotic hydrogel for periodontitis therapy.132 PUE nanoparticles were synthesized hydrothermally and immobilized onto probiotics through electrostatic layer-by-layer self-assembly. This design enhanced the solubility, receptor-binding affinity, and bioavailability of PUE, effectively targeting P2X3 receptor–mediated pain signaling. The hydrogel carrier further protected nanoparticles from premature degradation and facilitated prolonged local retention. Its robust adhesiveness and mechanical integrity enabled intimate contact with oral tissues, rendering the system highly suitable for localized periodontal treatment.

Overall, nanoparticle–hydrogel composites combine the ability of nanocarriers to improve the physicochemical properties of natural products with the strong local retention capacity of hydrogels. This combination enables stable drug encapsulation, sustained release, and prolonged tissue retention, making it an important strategy for the local delivery of natural products.

Hydrogel Composites with Metal–Organic Frameworks

Encapsulating natural products within nanoscale carriers and integrating them into hydrogel matrices represents an effective strategy for enhancing stability and achieving controlled release. Among numerous nanocarriers, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) materials, as an emerging class of nanoporous materials, have shown unique advantages. MOFs are a class of porous crystalline materials formed by metal ion centers and organic ligands with structural properties such as high specific surface area and abundant active sites.160 Their advantage lies in the fact that the structure of the crystals can be adjusted through the selection and design of metal ions and ligands, and at the same time, their porous structure enables the loading and release of drugs.161 The crosslinked network structure of the hydrogel facilitates the uniform dispersion of the MOFs. The hydrogel carrier can also be used as a slow-release system to realize the on-demand release of MOFs, while the incorporation of MOFs can significantly improve the mechanical properties of the hydrogel system.133

The high porosity and tunable architecture of MOFs enable efficient encapsulation and homogeneous dispersion of small-molecule natural products, markedly enhancing their stability and bioavailability. Encapsulation within MOFs also mitigates the susceptibility of certain natural compounds to photodegradation and oxidation. For instance, curcumin loaded into MOFs retained approximately 60% of its free radical–scavenging activity under UV irradiation—significantly higher than that of free curcumin (Figure 9A).134 In this system, OSA formed dynamic covalent bonds with amine groups on the surface, generating a reinforced crosslinked hydrogel network. This architecture not only improved the mechanical strength of the hydrogel but also imparted self-healing capability, allowing the material to adapt to the dynamic mechanical environment of wound sites.

The incorporation of natural small-molecule ligands in MOFs synthesis offers distinct advantages over post-synthetic modification. Natural polyphenols, rich in carboxyl and phenolic hydroxyl groups, coordinate efficiently with metal ions to form stable frameworks while imparting intrinsic bioactivities such as anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and antioxidant properties.162,163 Moreover, naturally derived ligands exhibit excellent biodegradability and biocompatibility, minimizing potential toxicity and immune responses associated with inert synthetic ligands.164,165 An intelligent injectable hydrogel based on a magnesium–gallic acid (Mg–GA) MOF was recently developed to promote bone regeneration in periodontitis (Figure 9B).133 GA, serving as the ligand, provides potent antioxidant activity through its abundant phenolic hydroxyl groups, effectively scavenging ROS and promoting macrophage polarization toward the pro-regenerative M2 phenotype, while synergizing with Mg2⁺ to enhance osteogenic differentiation. The integration of Mg–GA MOFs into the hydrogel matrix imparted pH/ROS-responsive release behavior and markedly improved mechanical strength and structural stability.

Therefore, MOF composite hydrogels combine the high drug-loading capacity of MOFs with the favorable biocompatibility of hydrogels, demonstrating promising applications in areas such as the stable delivery of natural products, stimulus-responsive release, and tissue engineering.

Hydrogel Composites with Exosomes

Compared to the extensive research on synthetic nanocarriers, exosomes, as a class of naturally secreted nanovesicles by cells, demonstrate unique advantages due to their natural origin and the bioactive substances they carry. In recent years, exosomes derived from natural products have been proven to be non-immunogenic, well biocompatible and biodegradable drug delivery systems with significant therapeutic effects.166,167 However, its application still faces certain limitations. Exosomes of natural origin are easily cleared by enzymes in the body and have a short half-life. In terms of controlled drug release, the fast release of drugs makes it difficult to realize long-lasting sustained release.168

The 3D network structure of hydrogel can provide a physical protective barrier for exosomes, reducing the risk of degradation or clearance by the immune system in the circulation, thus significantly extending their half-life. At the same time, the use of hydrogel as a carrier can regulate the release rate of exosomes to avoid the effect of sudden release, and realize more durable and controllable drug delivery. Tan et al developed a wireless thermoelectric hydrogel (HFN-RGE) for the repair of diabetic ulcers by combining REDV peptide-targeted modified ginseng-derived exosomes (RGE) and degradable hydrogel (HFN).135 In the HFN-RGE design, RGE was further embedded in HFN hydrogel, and the 3D network structure of the hydrogel provided physical protection for the exosomes. The sustained release of RGE was achieved by the encapsulation of HFN hydrogel, and HFN-RGE released 86.7% of RGE within 48 hours, which matched the chronic repair process of diabetic ulcers and avoided the short-lived efficacy caused by the sudden release of the drug.

The involvement of exosomes in disease therapy and drug delivery is mainly dependent on their lipid membrane structure. Chemical crosslinking of exosomes to hydrogels may disrupt their structural integrity or mask the targeting ligand. Therefore, exosomes are usually physically embedded in the crosslinked network of hydrogels to avoid damage to the membrane structure by chemical modification or chemical conjugation and to ensure that their biological functions are not affected.169,170 Wei et al loaded exosome-like nanovesicles (ENs) extracted from Rabdosia rubescens with the homologous drug oridonin (ORI), embedding them within a semi-interpenetrating network hydrogel to form a novel composite dressing (ORI/ENs/Gel) for treating chemoradiotherapy-induced oral mucositis.136 ENs serve as highly efficient carriers for the homologous drug ORI, achieving an encapsulation efficiency of up to 76.4%. Using the semi-interpenetrating network hydrogel as a carrier not only maintained the integrity of ENs, but also promoted the sustained release of ORI/EN at the wound site. When embedded into hydrogels, it achieves slow, sustained release over 7 hours, significantly prolonging the drug’s duration of action.

Compared with synthetic nanocarriers, exosome–hydrogel composites offer favorable biocompatibility and natural intercellular communication capabilities. With the growing understanding of plant-derived exosomes, these systems provide a promising material platform for the precision delivery of natural products and applications in regenerative medicine.

In general, combining nanformulations based on natural products with hydrogels can harness the complementary advantages of both components. However, different carriers exhibit distinct functional focuses. Conventional nanoparticles offer diverse drug-loading methods and relatively mature preparation techniques. MOFs, leveraging their porous structures and tunable compositions, can achieve high loading capacities. Exosomes, meanwhile, possess both natural biological activity and the ability to transmit biological signals. Future studies should select carriers appropriately based on the properties of natural products and therapeutic needs, with the aim of developing nanocomposite hydrogel formulations that combine high drug-loading efficiency, good biocompatibility, and precise delivery capabilities.

Machine Learning-Assisted Design and Optimization of Hydrogels

As hydrogel systems become increasingly complex, conventional single-factor experimentation is often insufficient for efficiently exploring multivariate design spaces. In recent years, machine learning has been applied to material property prediction, synthesis-condition optimization, high-throughput screening, and structure–property relationship analysis, providing new opportunities for the data-driven design of complex material systems.171,172 In the field of hydrogels, machine learning has likewise been introduced for molecular screening, formulation optimization, and property prediction. Li et al developed a machine learning model based on a structurally diverse library of more than 2000 peptide-like compounds, enabling the prediction of their self-assembly behavior and gel formation ability and revealing associations between molecular features and the mechanical properties of the resulting hydrogels.173 Machine learning can also facilitate the mechanical properties of the resulting the optimization of hydrogel preparation conditions. By evaluating initial substrate concentration, reaction temperature, pH level, and reaction time, Li et al identified suitable conditions for improving the elastic modulus of recombinant collagen hydrogels.174 Beyond the optimization of end-point properties, data-driven approaches can be used to regulate the gelation process itself. For example, Martineau et al employed Bayesian machine learning to engineer the gelation kinetics of living silk hydrogels, enabling the formulations to gel within a pre-selected timeframe.175

Collectively, these studies demonstrate that machine learning applications in hydrogel research have expanded from molecular screening to the optimization of preparation parameters and gelation kinetics, offering an efficient strategy for designing multivariable systems. For natural-product-based hydrogels, machine learning may be further employed to screen natural compounds with self-assembly or gelation potential, jointly optimize natural-product content, polymer composition, and crosslinking conditions, and predict the release profiles of bioactive compounds. With the continued accumulation of standardized experimental data, data-driven approaches are expected to reduce empirical trial-and-error and improve the predictability and efficiency of natural-product-based hydrogel development.

Conclusions and Perspectives

Integrating natural products into hydrogels provides a promising strategy to combine natural bioactivity with advanced material properties. The strategies for incorporating natural products into hydrogels can be broadly divided into self-assembly, physical embedding, and chemical crosslinking. In self-assembled hydrogels, natural small molecules act simultaneously as therapeutic agents and structural units. This eliminates the need for additional crosslinkers, thus improving biocompatibility. The physical embedding strategy preserves the natural product’s chemical integrity and biological activity. In chemically crosslinked systems, natural products participate directly in covalent bonding with the polymer network, effectively preventing drug leakage and achieving controlled or stimuli-responsive release.

Despite these advances, several challenges remain. Chemical modification may alter the structural integrity and biological activity of natural products or introduce new toxicities, while stable covalent bonds can hinder drug release time. Moreover, physical embedding often lacks precise release control, and self-assembled systems face difficulties in large-scale reproducibility and mechanical stability. Addressing these issues requires the development of milder and more selective crosslinking chemistries, a deeper molecular understanding of natural product structures, and rational material design based on disease-specific microenvironments.

Future research should promote a transition in natural product hydrogel development from empirical formulation toward molecular mechanism–guided design. Further efforts are needed to elucidate the structure–activity relationships among key pharmacophores, conjugation sites, and the hydrogel network microenvironment. The actual biological activity, bioavailability, and metabolic pathways of natural products should also be compared across their free, physically encapsulated, and covalently conjugated states. Meanwhile, the potential cytotoxicity, immune responses, and tissue-level risks associated with residual crosslinking agents, reaction by-products, and prolonged local retention should be systematically evaluated. Disease-relevant in vitro models and pharmacodynamic assessment systems should also be established to improve the clinical relevance and translational potential of research findings.

Building on these advances, integrating multi-scale design principles from molecular structure optimization to smart, stimuli-responsive hydrogel architectures will be key to realizing the full therapeutic potential of natural product hydrogel systems. Tailoring crosslinking strategies according to disease pathology and drug characteristics can maximize efficacy, minimize toxicity, and open new avenues for next generation bio-inspired therapeutic platforms. In addition, machine learning provides new tools for molecular screening, formulation optimization, and release behavior prediction in natural product-based hydrogel systems. In the future, integrating data-driven models with experimental validation may reduce empirical trial-and-error in multivariable systems while improving the predictability and efficiency of hydrogel design and development.
