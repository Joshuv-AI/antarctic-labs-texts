Prenatally detected primary renal teratoma in an infant: A case report in Vietnam
Authors: Thuy Nguyen Thi Mai, Khang Do Van
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimary renal teratomas are extremely rare germ cell tumors and can be difficult to distinguish from other fat-containing renal masses in infants using imaging alone. We report a case of a 3-month-old male with a right renal mass detected prenatally. Postnatal contrast-enhanced computed tomography revealed a well-circumscribed fat-containing lesion at the upper pole of the kidney. The patient underwent open nephron-sparing surgery with complete tumor enucleation. Histopathology confirmed a mature teratoma with negative margins. The postoperative course was uneventful, and at 1-year follow-up the child showed normal renal development with no evidence of recurrence.Keywords: Macroscopic fat-containing renal tumor, Primary renal teratoma, Case report

Introduction

Primary renal teratomas originate from pluripotent germ cells capable of differentiating into tissues from all three germ layers.1 These lesions typically present as cystic-solid masses containing fat and can occasionally be detected prenatally. However, imaging alone lacks sufficient specificity to determine the biological nature of a fat-containing renal tumor in young children. This ambiguity can lead to suboptimal interventions, including the unwarranted use of neoadjuvant chemotherapy or the selection of an inappropriate surgical plan (nephron-sparing surgery versus radical nephrectomy). In this report, we present a case of primary mature renal teratoma detected prenatally and managed with nephron-sparing surgery.

Case presentation

A 3-month-old male infant was referred to a tertiary urology center because of a right kidney mass. The mass, which was in the upper pole of the right kidney was incidentally detected during a prenatal ultrasonography at 36 weeks of gestation. The prenatal period was otherwise uneventful, and he was delivered at full term by Cesarean section, with a weight-at-birth of 2.9 kg. He exhibited no abnormal clinical symptoms, with no signs of urinary tract compression or hypertension after birth. Magnetic resonance urography was not available at our institution at the time of admission, so a contrast-enhanced abdominal computed tomography (CT) was indicated. The CT scan revealed a well-circumscribed mass localized at the upper pole of the right kidney, extending towards the adrenal fossa. The tumor was predominantly composed of homogeneous macroscopic fat, interspersed with poorly enhanced central soft tissue bands, consistent with primary renal teratoma (Fig. 1).

(B) An encapsulated, soft and yellowish tumor on the upper pole of the right kidney (white arrow). (C) The resection margin after tumor enucleation (white arrow).

Given the preoperative diagnosis and the young age (3 months) of the patient, an open nephron-sparing approach was indicated. Intraoperative exploration identified a 6 × 5 × 4 cm encapsulated mass originating from the upper pole of the right kidney, well-demarcated from the normal renal parenchyma. The right adrenal gland was preserved. The surgeon then performed an enucleation of the lesion (Fig. 1). Intraoperative frozen section analysis suggested a mature teratoma. The operation was uneventful with negligible blood loss.

Post-operative histopathological examination confirmed a mature teratoma containing mature adipose tissue, skin, and cutaneous adnexal structures. Scattered renal tubules and glomeruli were noted at the tumor periphery (<1%) (Fig. 2). Surgical margins were negative. The postoperative course was stable, with no hemorrhagic or infectious complications. The patient was discharged and scheduled for regular follow-up.

Histopathological features of resected teratoma.(A) Prominent ectodermal differentiation characterized by cysts lined by stratified squamous epithelium with keratin lamellae, accompanied by mature sebaceous glands and hair follicles on hematoxylin & eosin (H&E) staining. (B) Mesodermal components, predominantly mature adipose tissue adjacent to fibrous septa containing congested blood vessels on H&E staining. The presence of well-differentiated tissues from multiple germ layers confirms the diagnosis of a mature teratoma.

At the 1-year postoperative follow-up, the child demonstrated age-appropriate physical development with no pathological clinical signs. A follow-up ultrasound showed that the residual right kidney had normal physiological size and structure, with clear corticomedullary differentiation and no evidence of tumor recurrence.

Discussion

Teratomas are multipotent germ cell tumors that typically arise in the gonads or sacrococcygeal region. A primary renal occurrence is exceedingly rare, with fewer than 30 cases reported in the literature.2 Embryologically, renal teratomas originate from aberrant migration of multipotent germ cells from the yolk sac to the genital ridge, which subsequently become trapped within the developing fetal kidney.3 The hallmark of a teratoma is heterotopic organogenesis. Cells organize into organ-like structures derived from all three germ layers, such as squamous epithelial cysts with sebaceous glands (ectoderm), respiratory or intestinal epithelium (endoderm), and adipose tissue or cartilage (mesoderm).4 The degree of tissue differentiation dictates the nature of the tumor: mature teratomas are benign, whereas immature teratomas containing primitive structures (e.g., neuroepithelium) carry a risk of malignancy. In our case, the presence of mature tissues (squamous epithelium, sebaceous glands, adipose tissue) and the absolute absence of immature blastemal components confirmed the diagnosis of a primary mature renal teratoma.

As demonstrated in our patient, prenatal ultrasound enables the early detection of fetal abdominal masses between 22 and 36 weeks of gestation. However, the specificity of prenatal imaging in distinguishing renal from adrenal masses remains limited. A solid-cystic mass in the fetal adrenal or upper renal pole can be misdiagnosed as cystic neuroblastoma, congenital adrenal hemorrhage, or congenital mesoblastic nephroma.5,6 Postnatally, renal teratomas frequently present as an incidentally discovered, painless abdominal mass; occasionally, they may present with compressive symptoms, anemia due to intratumoral hemorrhage, or hypertension. Contrast-enhanced CT serves as the cornerstone for preoperative evaluation. The key radiological feature is the presence of macroscopic fat (attenuation ranging from −70 to −100 HU). This specific finding significantly narrows the differential diagnosis in pediatric patients, primarily including angiomyolipoma, teratoid Wilms tumor, primary renal teratoma, and fat-containing renal cell carcinoma. In our case, the homogeneous fatty mass located at the upper renal pole adjacent to the renal sinus posed challenges in discerning whether the tumor originated from the renal parenchyma or the adrenal gland.

Histologically, according to the Beckwith criteria, a primary renal teratoma must be located entirely within the renal capsule, show no evidence of metastasis from another primary site, and demonstrate heterotopic organogenesis.2 This organogenesis is defined by tissues finely organized into complete organ-like structures (e.g., epidermal cysts with sebaceous glands, intestinal mucosa with smooth muscle layers) and an absolute absence of immature blastemal cells. The histological analysis of the case aligned perfectly with these criteria, exhibiting keratin-filled squamous epithelial cysts and sebaceous glands.

For primary mature renal teratomas, their benign nature and the presence of a distinct fibrous pseudo-capsule justify nephron-sparing surgery as the preferred standard of care for small to medium-sized peripheral lesions. Techniques such as polar partial nephrectomy or tumor enucleation minimize ischemic injury, control hemorrhagic risks, and ensure long-term renal preservation.7 In our case, successful nephron-sparing surgery via tumor enucleation preserved the residual parenchyma and the adrenal gland. Longitudinal follow-up demonstrated normal residual renal parenchyma without complications such as chronic kidney disease or secondary hypertension.

Conclusion

Primary renal teratoma in infancy is a rare entity that may be detected prenatally and can mimic other fat-containing renal or adrenal masses on imaging. Accurate diagnosis relies on histopathological confirmation. When the lesion is well-circumscribed and suspected to be benign, nephron-sparing surgery represents a safe and effective treatment strategy, allowing complete tumor excision while preserving renal function. Careful imaging evaluation and appropriate surgical planning are essential to avoid unnecessary radical treatment in these patients.
