TNF-α inhibitor etanercept improves cerebrovascular function in elderly RA patients: findings from a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Qiao-qiao Ren, Pei Chen, Lin Qiao, Yong-ku Du, Rui-song Wang, Xiao-qiang Huang, Hua Guo, Jun Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a systemic autoimmune inflammatory disease that can affect the cardiovascular and cerebrovascular systems. Elderly RA patients face a significantly elevated risk of cerebrovascular events, the core mechanism of which may be related to chronic inflammation-mediated vascular endothelial dysfunction and impaired cerebral blood flow regulation. Tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) is a key pro-inflammatory cytokine in RA, yet whether its inhibitor can improve cerebral hemodynamics remains unclear. This study aims to investigate the effects and underlying mechanisms of TNF-α inhibitor etanercept on cerebral blood flow in elderly RA patients.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the effects of the TNF-α inhibitor Etanercept on cerebral hemodynamics in elderly RA patients and to explore the relationships between changes in inflammatory markers, endothelial function, and cerebral hemodynamics.MethodsA single-center, prospective, randomized controlled trial was conducted. A total of 159 elderly RA patients in mild disease activity, recruited from the Department of Rheumatology and Immunology at Xi’an No.5 Hospital between November 2023 and November 2024, were enrolled. Baseline data were collected, and patients were randomly assigned to either the experimental group (TNF-α inhibitor + Methotrexate + Celecoxib) or the control group (Methotrexate + Celecoxib). Before treatment and after 6 months of treatment, transcranial Doppler (TCD) was used to assess the mean flow velocity (Vm), pulsatility index (PI), and resistance index (RI) of the middle cerebral artery (MCA). Cerebrovascular reactivity (CVR) was evaluated using the breath-holding index (BHI). Serum inflammatory markers and vascular endothelial function were also assessed at both time points. Adverse drug reactions during the treatment period were recorded.ResultsCompared to the control group, the experimental group showed a significant increase in MCA Vm, significant decreases in PI and RI, and a marked improvement in BHI after treatment. Serum levels of erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP), rheumatoid factor (RF), anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide antibody (anti-CCP), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), soluble vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (sVCAM-1), and endothelin-1 (ET-1) were all significantly reduced. Correlation analysis revealed that the improvement in BHI was significantly associated with reductions in TNF-α, sVCAM-1, and ET-1. The incidence of adverse events was 11.96% (11/92) in the experimental group and 10.87% (10/92) in the control group, with no statistically significant difference.ConclusionThe TNF-α inhibitor Etanercept significantly improves cerebral hemodynamics and cerebrovascular reserve function in elderly RA patients, potentially by suppressing systemic inflammation and improving vascular endothelial function.Trial registrationThis study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Xi’an Fifth Hospital (Ethics Approval No.: [2023] Ethics Review 55). The trial was also registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration No.: ChiCTR2300077337; Date of Registration: 01/11/2023). Prior to participation, written informed consent was obtained from all individuals involved in the research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41927-025-00589-6.Keywords: TNF-α inhibitor, Elderly, Rheumatoid arthritis, Cerebral hemodynamics, Inflammatory biomarkers, Vascular endothelial function

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is an autoimmune disease with a higher prevalence in women. Elderly patients with RA often face high disability rates and complications, which increase treatment difficulty [1]. With advancing research, studies on elderly RA patients complicated by neurological disorders, such as cerebrovascular diseases, have progressively deepened [2]. Epidemiological studies indicate that elderly RA patients have a significantly elevated risk of ischemic stroke, cognitive impairment, and vascular dementia [3].

Upon diagnosis, immediate initiation of antirheumatic therapy is recommended for RA patients. Biologic agents demonstrate more pronounced anti-inflammatory effects compared to conventional antirheumatic drugs [4]. Among biologics, the recombinant human tumor necrosis factor-α receptor-antibody fusion protein, i.e., tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) inhibitors, is one of the most widely used drugs for RA. Compared to other biologics, this class of drugs exhibits notable vascular protective effects [5]. Evidence suggests that central nervous system damage in RA patients may be closely related to systemic inflammation-induced microvascular cerebral injury, and biologics may reduce neurological damage by protecting blood vessels [6].

Cerebrovascular reactivity (CVR) measures the degree of response of intracranial vessels to relevant vasoactive stimuli and can be used to assess cerebrovascular health. CVR is significantly lower in elderly populations compared to younger groups [7]. A domestic four-year longitudinal study on age-related changes in CVR and its relationship with cognition showed that the rate of CVR decline is closely associated with age [8]. Transcranial Doppler (TCD) is a relatively sensitive tool for detecting blood supply abnormalities in cerebral arteries, enabling timely intervention for early clinical reference [9].

The potential neuroprotective effects of treatment strategies have become a new focus when selecting therapeutic options for elderly RA patients. However, there is limited research on the impact of TNF-α inhibitors on cerebral hemodynamics in elderly RA patients, who are at higher risk of cerebrovascular complications. Whether these inhibitors can enhance cerebral blood flow perfusion and cerebrovascular reserve capacity by suppressing systemic inflammation and improving vascular endothelial function remains a question worthy of in-depth investigation.

Although the relationship between TNF-α inhibition and reduced systemic inflammation is well-established, its specific effects on cerebrovascular function in elderly patients with RA remain underexplored. This prospective randomized controlled trial was designed to confirm and expand upon previous findings by assessing whether the TNF-α inhibitor Etanercept improves cerebral hemodynamics and CVR in this vulnerable, high-risk, and often understudied population. Additionally, we sought to examine the potential links between these hemodynamic improvements and changes in key biomarkers of systemic inflammation and vascular endothelial function. The results of this study aim to provide new clinical evidence that can inform strategies for preventing and treating cerebrovascular complications in elderly RA patients.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient enrollment

This study was a single-center, prospective, randomized controlled trial with a two-arm, parallel-group design and a 1:1 allocation ratio. Elderly patients with RA who visited the Department of Rheumatology and Immunology at Xi’an No.5 Hospital between November 2023 and November 2024 were enrolled. The last participant completed the 6-month follow-up in May 2025. This study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 60 years; (2) meeting the 2010 American College of Rheumatology (ACR)/European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) diagnostic criteria for RA [10]; (3) all patients in a state of mild disease activity (DAS28: 2.6–3.2); (4) no use of biologic agents or targeted synthetic drugs within the preceding 6 months; and (5) provision of signed informed consent.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) comorbid cerebrovascular diseases: stroke, traumatic brain injury, brain tumors, etc.; (2) carotid ultrasound indicating carotid artery stenosis ≥ 50%; (3) other immune system diseases: lupus erythematosus, ankylosing spondylitis, connective tissue diseases, etc.; (4) abnormal liver or kidney function, hematological diseases, pulmonary infections, malignancies, etc.; and (5) inability to cooperate with relevant examinations and follow-up.

Withdrawal criteria comprised: (1) discontinuation of treatment due to severe adverse events; (2) poor treatment compliance, defined as failure to adhere to prescribed therapy; (3) incomplete medical history or relevant data; and (4) voluntary withdrawal from the study for any reason.

Before enrollment, all patients were screened for latent tuberculosis infection using the T-SPOT.TB assay and chest CT, as well as for hepatitis B and C virus infections. All test results were negative. During the study, the use of stable-dose corticosteroids (≤ 7.5 mg/day prednisone equivalent) and statins was allowed. The proportions of patients using these medications were comparable between the two groups, with no statistically significant differences: corticosteroids were used by 21.3% (17/80) of the experimental group and 19.0% (15/79) of the control group, while 11.3% (9/80) of the experimental group and 12.7% (10/79) of the control group used statins.

The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Xi’an No.5 Hospital (Approval No.: (2023) Ethics Review No. (55)) and registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration No.: ChiCTR2300077337). No changes were made to the trial methods, eligibility criteria, or outcomes after the trial commenced.

Sample size calculation

The allocation sequence was concealed using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes (SNOSE). This randomized controlled trial compared an experimental group (Etanercept + Methotrexate + Celecoxib) with a control group (Methotrexate + Celecoxib), focusing on changes in cerebral hemodynamics, with the breath-holding index (BHI) as the primary endpoint. Based on preliminary experiments, the standard deviation (σ) of BHI improvement was 0.13, and the expected effect size (δ) was 0.07. With a two-sided α of 0.05 and a power of 90%, the sample size was calculated using the formula yielding 73 patients per group (total 146). Accounting for a 20% dropout rate, the target enrollment was set at 183 patients. Ultimately, 184 patients were enrolled (92 per group).

Patient disposition

The preliminary study enrolled 184 elderly RA patients (92 in each group). After 6 months of treatment, 25 patients (13.59%) withdrew from the study. In the experimental group, 12 patients (13.04%) withdrew (11 due to adverse events and 1 lost to follow-up). In the control group, 13 patients (14.13%) withdrew (10 due to adverse events, 2 lost to follow-up, and 1 due to treatment regimen adjustment). Ultimately, 80 patients (86.96%) in the experimental group and 79 (85.87%) in the control group completed the study and were included in the analysis, The total number of participants is 159. The safety analysis included all 184 randomized patients (intention-to-treat principle for safety). The efficacy analysis was performed on the per-protocol population (80 in the experimental group and 79 in the control group) who completed the 6-month follow-up and had valid outcome data. Grouping and treatment

Eligible patients were randomly assigned to either the experimental group or the control group using a random number table method.

Experimental group

Patients received subcutaneous injections of etanercept (Recombinant Human Tumor Necrosis Factor-α Receptor II: IgG Fc Fusion Protein for Injection; brand name: Yisaipu; Sunshine Guojian Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China; approval number: S20050058) at a dose of 25 mg twice weekly, in combination with oral methotrexate tablets (Shanghai Sine Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China; approval number: H31020644) at 10 mg once weekly and oral celecoxib capsules (G.D. Searle & Co., Ltd.; approval number: BX20000276) at 200 mg once daily.

Control group

Patients received oral methotrexate tablets (Shanghai Sine Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China; approval number: H31020644) at 10 mg once weekly and oral celecoxib capsules (G.D. Searle & Co., Ltd.; approval number: BX20000276) at 200 mg once daily.

The treatment duration for both groups was 6 months. During the treatment period, all patients also received Folic Acid Tablets (Manufacturer: Tianjin Lisheng Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China; Approval No.: H12020215) at a dose of 10 mg orally once per week, administered concurrently with methotrexate.

Outcome measures

Outcome Indicators: Primary outcome measure: BHI of cerebral hemodynamics. Secondary outcome measures: (1) Other cerebral hemodynamic parameters; (2) Serological biomarkers; (3) Safety indicators.

Cerebral Hemodynamic Assessment: Cerebral hemodynamics were evaluated using TCD (Model: TCD-2000 M; Manufacturer: Beijing Yueqi Chuangtong Technology Co., Ltd.) with patients in a resting state. The mean flow velocity (Vm), pulsatility index (PI), and resistance index (RI) of the bilateral middle cerebral arteries (MCA) were recorded and averaged. CVR was assessed using the BHI [11], calculated as follows: BHI = [(Vm_post – Vm_baseline) / Vm_baseline] / breath-holding time (s) × 100%. The BHI test was standardized as follows: Patients were instructed to hold their breath after a normal inspiration for 30 s. Prior to the recorded test, two practice trials were conducted to familiarize the patient with the procedure and minimize the Valsalva maneuver. Breath-hold duration was timed with a stopwatch. The test was terminated immediately if the patient experienced significant discomfort. The same TCD technician performed all examinations for a given patient to minimize inter-operator variability.

Serological Testing: (1) Inflammatory biomarkers: Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP), rheumatoid factor (RF), anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide antibody (anti-CCP), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and TNF-α. ESR was measured using the Westergren method (Instrument: ESR-30). CRP and RF were detected via immunoturbidimetric assay (Instrument: IMMAGE 800 Specific Protein Analyzer). Anti-CCP was quantified using chemiluminescent microparticle immunoassay (Instrument: Abbott i2000 SR). IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α were analyzed by flow cytometry (Instrument: Agilent Advanteon). (2) Endothelial function biomarkers: Soluble vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (sVCAM-1) and endothelin-1 (ET-1) were measured using ELISA kits (Manufacturer: Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China).

Randomization and blinding

The random allocation sequence was generated by an independent statistician who was not involved in patient recruitment or outcome assessment, using a computer-generated simple randomization list. To ensure allocation concealment, sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes (SNOSE) were used. Once a participant provided informed consent and completed baseline assessments, a research nurse, who was not involved in outcome assessment, opened the next envelope in the sequence to reveal the group assignment. Given the nature of the interventions (injectable vs. oral medications), blinding of the participants or treating physicians was not feasible. However, to minimize measurement bias, outcome assessors were blinded to group allocation. Specifically, the TCD sonographers who performed the scans and calculated the BHI, as well as the laboratory personnel conducting the serological biomarker assays, were unaware of the patients’ treatment assignments. Additionally, the data statistician was blinded during the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 27.0). Measurement data conforming to a normal distribution were described as mean ± standard deviation , and comparisons between groups were conducted using the independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed measurement data were expressed as median (interquartile range) [M (Q1, Q3)], and the Mann-Whitney U test was used for intergroup comparisons. Within-group comparisons were performed using paired t-tests for normally distributed data and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for non-normally distributed data. Count data were presented as number (percentage) [n (%)], and the χ²test was applied for comparisons. Multiple linear regression analysis was employed to examine the association between changes in CVR and inflammatory markers, as well as vascular endothelial function indicators. All hypothesis tests were two-sided, with a significance level (α) set at 0.05.

Given the exploratory nature of the analyses for multiple secondary endpoints (e.g., various inflammatory and endothelial biomarkers), no statistical adjustment for multiple comparisons was performed. These findings should be interpreted as generating hypotheses for future research. To explore the potential mechanistic links underlying the treatment effect of etanercept, a multiple linear regression analysis was performed specifically within the experimental group (n = 80). This model assessed whether the changes in endothelial function biomarkers (ΔsVCAM-1 and ΔET-1) and the key inflammatory cytokine (ΔTNF-α) were independently associated with the change in CVR (ΔBHI) among patients who received TNF-α inhibition.

Relationship between CVR and inflammatory/endothelial markers

A multiple linear regression analysis was performed to assess the independent associations between changes in inflammatory/endothelial markers and the change in CVR (ΔBHI) in the experimental group. The model was adjusted for age, sex, and BMI and included all 80 participants. The overall model was statistically significant (F = 5.84, P < 0.001) and explained approximately 21% of the variance in ΔBHI (Adjusted R²= 0.21). Reductions in all three markers were significantly associated with an improvement in ΔBHI. Specifically, ΔsVCAM-1 exhibited the strongest independent effect (β= -0.31, P = 0.013), followed by ΔTNF-α (β= -0.25, P = 0.017) and ΔET-1 (β = -0.22, P = 0.023). Adverse events during the treatment period

This study initially enrolled 184 patients, with 92 patients in each group. No clinically significant abnormalities in routine blood tests were observed before or after treatment in either group. During the treatment period, 11 patients (11.96%) in the experimental group withdrew due to adverse events, including 1 case of ischemic stroke, 1 case of heart failure, 1 case of pulmonary infection, 1 case of headache, 3 cases of gastrointestinal reactions, 3 cases of abnormal liver function, and 1 case of rash. In the control group, 10 patients (10.87%) experienced adverse events, including 1 case of heart failure, 1 case of pulmonary infection, 2 cases of headache, 2 cases of gastrointestinal reactions, 1 case of abnormal liver function, 1 case of abnormal renal function, and 2 cases of mild skin rash. The incidence of adverse events showed no statistically significant difference between the two groups (χ² = 0.05, P = 0.817).

One case of ischemic stroke occurred in the intervention group approximately four months after the initiation of treatment. The patient had a baseline history of hypertension and prior lacunar infarction. Although a causal link between the event and the investigational drug cannot be entirely ruled out, the investigators believed that the event was more likely related to the patient’s inherent high risk of cerebrovascular disease. The incidence of serious adverse events (including ischemic stroke, heart failure, and pulmonary infection) was 3.26% (3/92) in the intervention group and 2.17% (2/92) in the control group. The absolute risk difference was 1.09%, with a 95% confidence interval of -6.5% to 8.6% (Newcombe–Wilson method), indicating no statistically significant difference in the risk of serious adverse events between the groups. However, the small number of events and the study’s limited power to detect modest differences in serious adverse event rates should be noted.

Discussion

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disease with its highest incidence in middle age. The inflammatory response it triggers can affect multiple organs throughout the body [12]. With advancements in cerebrovascular disease and neuroimmunology research, the detrimental impact of RA-induced inflammation on the central nervous system, particularly the cerebral vasculature, has become an increasingly focused area of study [13]. Autopsy findings have revealed more pronounced cerebrovascular damage in the blood supply regions of the basal ganglia in RA patients [14]. Previous studies have indicated that chronic inflammation can lead to endothelial dysfunction and even the formation of arterial plaques [15]. Maiuolo J et al. discovered that systemic inflammation caused by RA can result in microvascular damage, leading to vascular endothelial impairment, axonal degeneration, and neuronal demyelination [16]. Interventions aimed at maintaining endothelial cell integrity in RA patients present a potential therapeutic strategy. Sustained high levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in RA patients, such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, can activate vascular endothelial cells and disrupt the normal homeostatic balance of anticoagulation, anti-adhesion, and vasodilation [17]. sVCAM-1 is a transmembrane protein involved in the immune response, expressed on the surface of activated vascular endothelial cells, and is often used to assess the degree of vascular endothelial injury [18]. Activated endothelial cells can highly express adhesion molecules like sVCAM-1 while simultaneously secreting the vasoconstrictor ET-1, collectively leading to aberrant regulation of vascular tone [19].

Biologic agents represent one of the cornerstone treatments for RA; however, research on their safety and efficacy specifically in the elderly population remains limited [20]. Data from the Korean College of Rheumatology Biologics and Targeted Therapy (KOBIO) registry demonstrated that both biologic and targeted synthetic drugs exhibited good effectiveness and were well-tolerated in elderly RA patients over 65 years of age [21]. A large prospective study involving 5,500 elderly Japanese RA patients indicated that while biologics were associated with an increased risk of infections and malignancies, this risk represented an association rather than direct causation, and the clinical benefits of biologics for elderly patients remained substantial [22]. TNF-α is a pivotal inflammatory mediator in RA. Research by Kosuke Ebina et al. suggested that TNF-α inhibitors may not significantly increase the risk of adverse events such as cardiovascular incidents and malignancies in older patients [23]. The safety profile was further supported by a large, long-term follow-up study conducted by Ytterberg et al. This study, which included 1,451 elderly RA patients monitored for over 4 years, found no association between TNF inhibitor therapy and an increased incidence of cardiovascular events or malignancies [24].Animal studies utilizing RA mouse models have further corroborated that TNF-α inhibitors confer cerebrovascular protection by reducing activation of microglia and macrophages, thereby mitigating central nervous system damage induced by cerebral ischemia [25].

Currently, there is a paucity of research investigating the impact of RA-induced endothelial dysfunction on cerebral hemodynamics. The precise relationship between the inflammatory response in RA and alterations in cerebral hemodynamics remains inadequately elucidated. A critical question addressed by this study is whether TNF-α inhibitors, while effectively controlling joint inflammation in elderly RA patients, can concurrently protect the cerebral microvasculature and improve cerebral hemodynamic parameters. This prospective randomized controlled trial demonstrates that add-on therapy with the TNF-α inhibitor etanercept significantly improves MCA hemodynamics and CVR in elderly patients with RA. The novelty of our work lies not in establishing the link between TNF-α inhibition and endothelial improvement-a pathway previously explored-but in translating and confirming this relationship within a vulnerable, understudied, high-risk elderly RA cohort using a functional cerebrovascular endpoint (BHI). Our findings indicate that the cerebrovascular protective effect of etanercept in elderly RA patients is closely associated with the suppression of systemic inflammation and subsequent improvement in vascular endothelial function. This provides a translational perspective for extending anti-inflammatory therapy to the prevention of cerebrovascular complications in this population.

The results indicate a direct beneficial effect of the TNF-α inhibitor on cerebral hemodynamics. After 6 months of treatment, the experimental group showed marked improvements in MCA Vm, PI, RI, and BHI compared to the control group. The significant increase in Vm suggests enhanced cerebral perfusion. The reductions in PI and RI reflect decreased distal cerebrovascular resistance and improved vascular compliance and elasticity. BHI is a gold-standard measure for assessing the compensatory vasodilatory capacity of cerebral vessels in the context of microangiopathy or stress. A decline in BHI predicts impaired cerebrovascular autoregulation, which can lead to stroke and cognitive decline [26]. The significant increase in BHI in the experimental group indicates that Etanercept effectively enhances cerebrovascular reserve function in elderly RA patients, potentially playing a positive role in preventing cerebrovascular events. The slight decrease in BHI observed in the control group, albeit statistically insignificant, suggests a trend of declining BHI over time in elderly RA patients with mild disease activity, implying that conventional antirheumatic drugs may offer suboptimal protection for cerebrovascular health.

The present study demonstrates that Etanercept effectively reduces levels of inflammatory factors (IL-6, CRP, ESR) by inhibiting TNF-α, while also decreasing endothelial cell markers (sVCAM-1, ET-1). Veale, J. D. et al. identified that markers such as CRP and sVCAM-1 can serve as predictors of vascular damage in RA patients [27]. Shilkina N. P. et al. found that increased activity of inflammatory processes in RA leads to vascular endothelial dysfunction, with ET-1 and sVCAM-1 levels significantly elevated compared to healthy populations [28]. Correlation analysis in our study revealed that the degree of improvement in BHI was significantly correlated with the magnitude of reduction in TNF-α, sVCAM-1, and ET-1. The improvement in hemodynamics may be attributed to attenuated vascular inflammation and the restoration of endothelium-dependent vasodilation.

Our findings suggest that the improvement in cerebral hemodynamics may be mediated by the suppression of systemic inflammation and subsequent improvement in vascular endothelial function. TNF-α inhibitors have been shown to effectively reduce serum sVCAM-1 levels, thereby protecting vascular endothelial function [29]. Angiogenesis is a key event promoting the development of proliferative pathological synovitis in RA, a process during which serum sVCAM-1 levels can significantly increase [30]. sVCAM-1, as a key mediator of endothelial activation, demonstrated the strongest association with BHI in our analysis, suggesting that the alleviation of endothelial inflammation is the most direct pathway for improving cerebrovascular function. ET-1, a potent vasoconstrictor produced by both vascular smooth muscle cells and endothelial cells, is one of the most potent endogenous vasoconstrictive factors known [31]. Previous studies have also indicated that reactive oxygen species generated by the inflammatory response in RA can further promote the production of related pro-inflammatory cytokines, and biologic agents can significantly reduce ET-1 levels [32]. The significant decrease in ET-1 observed in our study provides a direct explanation for the improvement in cerebrovascular PI and RI. The observed correlations are consistent with the hypothesis that TNF-α inhibitors confer cerebrovascular benefits through endothelial protection.

Limitations of this study are as follows

(1) Single-center design and sample size limitations: This trial was conducted at a single institution. Although strict randomization ensured balanced baseline characteristics between groups, the single-center design may limit the generalizability of the findings. The sample size may be insufficient for certain subgroup analyses or for evaluating rare serious adverse events. Future multicenter studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to more precisely assess treatment efficacy and safety. (2) Relatively short follow-up period: The 6-month follow-up period, while sufficient to observe significant improvements in cerebral hemodynamics and inflammatory markers, is inadequate to evaluate the long-term effects of TNF-α inhibitors on cerebrovascular and brain function. Longer-term follow-up studies are needed to confirm these findings. (3) Limitations of measurement methods: Although TCD is non-invasive and convenient for assessing cerebral hemodynamics, its accuracy highly depends on the operator’s technical expertise. Future studies could employ more precise methods, such as quantitative arterial spin labeling MRI or CO₂ inhalation tests, to evaluate CVR. (4) Limitations in control group design: The control group received methotrexate plus celecoxib. While this ensured all participants received standard care, it may have underestimated the true effect size of the TNF-α inhibitor, as the control treatment itself may have some anti-inflammatory and vascular effects. (5) Incomplete exploration of potential confounding factors: Although major confounding factors were controlled, other variables-such as apolipoprotein E (APOE) genotype or neurobiological markers (e.g., plasma Aβ, p-tau, amyloid, or neurofilament light chain protein [NfL])-may also influence cerebrovascular function and were not assessed. Future studies should incorporate these factors for a more comprehensive analysis. (6) The exploratory nature of the analyses for multiple secondary endpoints: The analysis of multiple secondary outcome measures without adjustment for multiple comparisons increases the risk of type I errors. Therefore, the correlations observed for the secondary endpoints should be considered exploratory and require validation in future studies. (7) Indirect mechanistic evidence: This study inferred the mechanism indirectly through correlations between changes in serum biomarkers. While the results suggest that TNF-α inhibitors improve endothelial function and cerebral hemodynamics in elderly RA patients by suppressing inflammation, direct evidence is lacking. Future research should include animal models or in vitro vascular function assays to directly validate the effects on cerebrovascular endothelial cells.

Conclusion

This study confirms that the TNF-α inhibitor Etanercept, in combination with methotrexate, significantly improves cerebral hemodynamics and CVR in elderly patients with mildly active RA. The therapeutic benefits were observed alongside effective suppression of systemic inflammation and improvement of vascular endothelial function. These findings provide important scientific evidence supporting the use of Etanercept for preventing and treating potential cerebrovascular complications in elderly RA patients. The results highlight the multisystem benefits of anti-inflammatory therapy with Etanercept, demonstrating a favorable safety profile and suggesting its potential as a valuable clinical strategy for this population.
