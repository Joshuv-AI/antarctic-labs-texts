Xiehuang San Extract Attenuates Insulin Resistance in Obese Rats via Gut Microbiota Modulation: Experimental Evidence
Authors: YuXin Yang, Yantong Liu, Gui-Ju Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObesity-induced insulin resistance significantly contributes to the global increase in type 2 diabetes, with gut microbiota alterations playing a critical role. Traditional herbal formulations, such as Xiehuang San (XHS), have been used for centuries in Chinese medicine. However, their mechanisms – particularly via the gut-liver-metabolism axis – remain to be fully elucidated. This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of XHS in improving insulin resistance through metabolic and microbial modulation.Material/MethodsA high-fat diet-induced obese insulin-resistant rat model (n=30) was established using male rats. Rats were randomized into 6 groups and treated with low, medium, or high doses of XHS, metformin, or vehicle control via daily gavage for 8 weeks. Metabolic parameters, including fasting blood glucose (FBG), fasting insulin (FINS), the homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), oral glucose tolerance (OGTT), and insulin tolerance tests (ITT) were assessed. Liver histology and lipid profiles were analyzed, and 16S rRNA sequencing was used to examine gut microbiota composition.ResultsXHS treatment significantly reduced FBG (~25%), FINS (~30%), and HOMA-IR scores compared with the model group (P<0.05), with effects comparable to metformin. OGTT and ITT improvements were dose-dependent. XHS also ameliorated hepatic steatosis and improved lipid profiles (eg, lowered triglycerides and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and elevated high-density lipoprotein cholesterol). Gut microbiota analysis revealed that XHS decreased the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio and selectively increased Bacteroides while reducing Escherichia-Shigella, suggesting a reshaping of microbial diversity and abundance.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that XHS may enhance insulin sensitivity and improve metabolic outcomes in obese rats, partly via gut microbiota modulation. Further studies are needed to isolate its active components, clarify molecular pathways, and evaluate translational relevance in human subjects.

Introduction

Obesity has emerged as a global epidemic, with excess fat accumulation not only predisposing individuals to type 2 diabetes but also increasing the risk of developing the condition in a linear fashion as body mass index rises [1,2]. This phenomenon is largely attributable to the potent endocrine function of adipocytes, which secrete a myriad of cytokines and adipokines (ie, signaling proteins involved in inflammation and glucose regulation) that contribute to the development of insulin resistance [3]. In fact, more than 80% of obese patients exhibit insulin resistance [4,5]. Insulin resistance is characterized by diminished responsiveness and sensitivity of insulin target tissues, resulting in impaired glucose uptake and utilization. Although insulin resistance is potentially reversible, its progression, if left unaddressed, can lead to type 2 diabetes and elevate the risk for several other conditions, including metabolic syndrome [6]. Furthermore, diet-induced alterations in gut flora, which may influence insulin signaling via mechanisms such as increased intestinal permeability, endotoxin release, and short-chain fatty acid dysregulation, have been observed in both humans and rodents [7]. A recent cohort study has underscored the robust association between type 2 diabetes and gut microbiota composition, highlighting body fat distribution as a critical modulator of this relationship [8,9].

Metformin remains the most widely prescribed treatment for improving insulin resistance [10]. However, due to the complex etiology and pathogenesis of obesity-related insulin resistance, there are currently no universally approved or fully effective treatments. Consequently, there has been a growing interest in exploring herbal medicine as an alternative therapeutic option [11,12]. Xiehuang San (XHS), a traditional formulation first documented during the Song Dynasty (960–1279 CE), comprises 6 Chinese herbs – Patchouli, gardenia, Fangfeng, Chenpi, Coptis chinensis Franch, and Glycyrrhiza uralensis Fisch. Notably, active constituents in C. chinensis, such as berberine, have demonstrated efficacy in lowering blood glucose levels [13,14]; Patchouli has been shown to mitigate insulin resistance via multiple pathways [15,16]; and extracts of Gardenia latifolia have significantly reduced fasting blood glucose while enhancing insulin tolerance, glucose tolerance, and antioxidant enzyme activities in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus [17].

Given the promising effects of traditional herbal formulations in mitigating insulin resistance, we sought to explore these effects by employing a high-fat diet-induced obese insulin-resistant rat model. We hypothesized that XHS may offer enhanced therapeutic benefits in alleviating obesity-related insulin resistance. Nonetheless, the specific mechanism by which XHS exerts its effects – particularly through modulation of the gut microbiota – remains to be fully elucidated. This study aimed to evaluate the therapeutic potential of XHS in modulating gut microbiota and insulin resistance.

Material and Methods

Below is a summary of the experimental design followed by a flowchart that encapsulates the key steps of the study, as shown in Figure 1.

This study used an experimental approach to evaluate XHS extract’s effects on obesity-induced insulin resistance. Male Sprague-Dawley rats were first acclimatized for 1 week, after which an 8-week high-fat diet regimen was used to establish the obesity and insulin resistance model. Upon confirmation of the model’s success through body weight and metabolic assessments, the rats were randomly divided into various groups, including a control group, a model group, 3 XHS treatment groups (low-, medium-, and high-dose groups), and a metformin group. Treatments were administered via daily gavage for 8 weeks. Following the intervention period, metabolic tests, biochemical measurements, histopathological evaluations, and gut microbiota analyses (via 16S rRNA gene sequencing) were conducted to comprehensively assess the therapeutic impact of XHS. The metabolic tests conducted were an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) and an insulin tolerance test (ITT). The biochemical measurements were fasting blood glucose, fasting insulin, homeostatic model of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), and blood lipids. The experimental workflow is summarized in Figure 1.

Animal Models and Experimental Design

Thirty specific-pathogen-free (SPF) Sprague-Dawley rats (male, 5 weeks old; body weight: 180±20 g; Animal License No. 2022-0003) were obtained from the Experimental Center of Shandong University of Traditional Chinese Medicine. All rats were housed in a quarantine room for 1 week of acclimatization. The room was well-ventilated, maintained at 22±2°C with a relative humidity of 50±5%, and subjected to a 12-hour light/dark cycle. During acclimatization, rats were provided with standard chow and water ad libitum, and bedding was changed regularly. After acclimatization, the animals were transferred to the standard animal facility under the same environmental conditions. This study was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shandong University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Approval No. SDUTCM20220920003).

At the end of the 8-week intervention, the rats were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane and humanely euthanized by exsanguination via the abdominal aorta. Under aseptic conditions, the cecum was exposed, ligated at both ends, and cecal contents were collected into sterile tubes, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C. Fecal pellets were collected non-invasively at baseline and at the end of the intervention and stored at −80°C. All procedures were performed by trained personnel to minimize distress and were approved by the institutional ethics committee.

Of the 30 5-week-old male SPF Sprague-Dawley rats, after 1 week of acclimatization, 5 rats were selected as the normal group and fed with normal chow and the remaining 25 were selected as the modeling group and fed with a high-fat diet. The high-fat diet used in this study consisted of 45% kcal from fat, 35% from carbohydrates, and 20% from protein, with a caloric density of 4.73 kcal/g. All rats were fed in cages, with 5 rats per cage and 6 cages in total. After 8 weeks of continuous feeding, all rats were weighed to assess the success of the obesity model. After the obesity modeling was completed, 3 rats were randomly selected from the untreated group for interim blood sampling via the tail vein (non-terminal) to verify model establishment. These animals were not sacrificed at that stage, and all rats continued in the study with n=5 maintained per group. In the treatment groups, oral gavage was performed once daily at 1 mL/100 g by trained personnel. To minimize stress, the rats underwent brief handling habituation, were gently restrained, and gavage was conducted using a rounded-tip feeding needle. Isoflurane was used when needed to reduce stress, and animals were monitored post-procedure to ensure recovery without signs of distress. At the end of the experiment, all rats were humanely euthanized under anesthesia, and final blood and tissue samples were collected in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines.

Doses of XHS (low: 0.256 g/mL/d, medium: 0.513 g/mL/d, high: 1.026 g/mL/d) were determined based on a preliminary dose-ranging pilot study and were referenced to commonly used clinical equivalent doses in traditional Chinese medicine practice. Since the standardized dose of XHS has not been established in animal models, these concentrations were chosen to explore a potential dose-response relationship within a safe and observable range.

After the rats had reached the obesity level for the obesity model, 2 rats were randomly selected from the normal group, and 10 rats were randomly selected from the rats in the modeling group that met the obesity criteria. Blood was collected from the abdominal aorta, fasting blood glucose and fasting insulin levels were measured, and the HOMA-IR value was calculated to assess the success of the modeling of the insulin resistance model.

Criteria for Model Evaluation

Determination criteria for the obesity model were based on Lee’s index, which is a validated index for evaluating the degree of obesity in adult rats:

Body length is defined as the distance from nose to anus. Modeling of the obesity model was considered successful when the Lee’s index of the rats in the modeling group exceeded 20% over that of the normal group.

Determination criteria for the insulin resistance model were based on the HOMA-IR as the evaluation index:

The HOMA-IR value of rats in the modeling group was higher (P<0.01) than that of the normal group. Since the HOMA-IR value in the modeling group was higher than that in the normal group (P<0.05), the IR model was considered to be successful.

If the above 2 evaluation criteria were satisfied simultaneously, the replication of the obese insulin-resistant rat model was considered to be successful.

After 1 week of acclimatization, the 30 rats were randomly divided into 2 groups: control (n=5) and model (n=25). Rats in the model group were fed a high-fat diet for 4 consecutive weeks, and rats in the control group were fed a basal diet. At week 8, the rats underwent an intraperitoneal insulin tolerance test and OGTT to verify the successful establishment of the IR model. The overall grouping and treatment protocol are summarized in Table 1. Then, the rats in the model group were randomly divided into the model control group (n=5), the XHS low-dose group (XHS-L, n=5, 0.256 g/mL/d), the XHS medium-dose group (XHS-M, n=5, 0.513 g/mL/d), the XHS high-dose group (XHS-H, n=5, 1.026 g/mL/d), and the metformin group (n=5, 0.8 g/mL/d). Rats in the XHS or metformin groups were administered XHS or metformin via oral gavage daily, and the gavage dose for rats in each group was 1 mL/100 g. During the intervention period, the control group was given normal chow, and the remaining 5 groups were given high-fat chow. All the rats were given normal tap water ad libitum for 8 weeks. No exercise training or physical activity programs were implemented during the study. All observed effects are attributable to dietary and pharmacological interventions.

Preparation of XHS Extract: Medicine Composition

The XHS extract included 6 Chinese herbs: patchouli, burnt gardenia, windbreak, Chenpi, Huanglian, and raw licorice. The proportion of the herbs was 4: 3: 4: 4: 2: 2. All of the treatments (XHS at low, medium, and high doses, as well as metformin) were administered once daily by oral gavage throughout the experimental period.

Preparation of XHS Extract: Medicine Preparation

A water-vapor distillation method was used to extract the volatile oil of 9 g of burnt gardenia, 12 g of patchouli, 12 g of fengfeng, and 12 g of Chenpi. The aqueous solution after distillation was then stored at room temperature. The dregs of the medicine were added into 8 times the amount of tap water, decocted in the water twice, for 1 h each time, and the medicinal solution was mixed and stored at room temperature. Then, 6 g of Huanglian and 6 g of licorice were added to 8 times the amount of tap water. The solution was then processed using water decoction 3 times, each decoction for 1.5 hours, and the medicinal liquid was mixed. The distilled aqueous solution of burnt gardenia, patchouli, fengfeng, and Chenpi was then added to the water decoction of Huanglian and licorice, and this mixture was concentrated to 60 ml. The volatile oil (the oil fraction obtained from the initial water-vapor distillation of burnt gardenia, patchouli, fengfeng, and Chenpi) was then added at the final preparation step. The total amount of water was adjusted for a final volume of 65 ml and the solution was refrigerated at 4°C for 72 hours. It was then removed and subjected to moist-heat steam sterilization (105~108°C, 30 min) in an autoclave, and finally concentrated into a medicinal liquid containing an extract of 1.026 g/mL of raw herbs. The extract was stored in an airtight jar and preserved at 4°C.

Mass Spectrometry Analysis

Ultra-high-pressure liquid chromatography/Orbitrap-mass spectrometry (UPLC/Orbitrap-MS) analysis was performed using a Thermo Fisher™ UltiMate 3000 RS system coupled to a Thermo Scientific TM Q Exactive high-resolution mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Diego, CA). For UPLC separation, 2 μL of the sample solution was injected into a Thermo Hypersil GOLD column (100×2.1 mm, 1.9 μm). The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile (MeCN) containing 0.1% (v/v) formic acid (solution A), and water containing 0.1% (v/v) formic acid (solution B). Linear gradient elution was applied at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min, as follows: 0–5 min, 2–20% solution A; 5–10 min, 20–50% solution A;10–25 min, 50–95% solution A; 26–30 min, 2% solution A. The column temperature was 35°C. For MS analysis, the accurate mass was maintained in full scan/data-dependent MS2 (full MS/dd-MS2) mode. The operating parameters in negative ion mode were as follows: spray voltage, 3.8 kV; sheath gas pressure, 40 arbitrary units (arb); auxiliary gas pressure, 10 arb; capillary temperature, 300°C. MS data were acquired and processed by CD 2.1 software (Thermo Fisher), and compared using the online database mzCloud (mzCloud, mzVault, ChemSpider).

H&E Staining and Oil Red O Staining

Liver tissues were rinsed with 0.9% physiological saline and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. The tissues were then paraffin (Jinan China)-embedded and sectioned. After gradient dehydration to expel xylene, the sections were stained with hematoxylin for 3–5 minutes. The sections were rinsed with running tap water, immersed in 1% acidic alcohol for differentiation, rinsed again, and stained with 1% eosin. The tissues were treated with alcohol and xylene before sealing with neutral balsam. Finally, the tissues were placed on microscopic slides for haematoxylin and eosin (H&E; Jinan China).

Oil red O powder was diluted with isopropanol to a concentration of 0.5% and then mixed with ultrapure water at a 3: 2 ratio, and then filtered through a 0.22 mm membrane. HepG2 human hepatocellular carcinoma cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; HB-8065, Manassas, VA, USA) and maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; high glucose) with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37°C in 5% CO2. The HepG2 cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, and then stained with oil red O solution for 30 min. Finally, the nucleus was stained with hematoxylin and the images were captured with a cell imaging system.

16S rRNA Gene Sequencing and Fractionation

Fecal samples were collected at 2 time points (after 8 weeks of the high-fat diet and after the 8-week intervention). Cecal contents were collected only at the terminal time point following euthanasia. DNA was extracted from fecal/cecal samples using the QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA extracted from the fecal samples was then electrophoresed using 0.8% agarose gel. The V3-V4 region of the bacterial 16S rDNA gene was amplified by PCR using primers 341 F (5’-CCTAYGGGGRBGCASCAG-3’) and 806 R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’). The total volume of the reaction was 25 μL, which contained 10 ng of DNA template, 1×PCR buffer, MgCl2 (1.5 mM), dNTPs (0.4 μM), forward primer (1.0 μM), reverse primer (1.0 μM), and KOD-Plus-Neo enzyme (Thermococcus kodakaraensis polymerase; Toyobo, Jinan, China). Thermal cycling conditions were as follows: 1 min at 94°C, followed by {20 s at 94°C, 30 s at 54°C, and 30 s at 72°C} for 30 cycles, followed by a final extension step of 5 min at 72°C. The experiment was repeated 3 times for each sample. Alpha diversity metrics, including the Chao1 index (species richness), Shannon index (species richness and evenness), Simpson index (dominance), and phylogenetic diversity, were calculated to evaluate microbial community diversity.

PCR amplicons were separated by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis. The DNA-containing portion of the gel was then removed and the DNA was purified using the QIAquick Gel Extraction kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA was quantified using a Qubit™ 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Scientific) and the amplicons were pooled in equimolar amounts.

Bioinformatics Analysis of the Microbiota

Libraries were constructed using the TruSeq™ DNA PCR-Free Sample Prep kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The constructed libraries were quantified and sequenced in a MiSeq sequencing system (Illumina, MiSeq Reagent Kit v3) using the PE300 mode.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) 22.0 and GraphPad Prism 10.1.2. All statistical tests were 2-sided. Unpaired t-test was used for comparison between 2 groups, and the 1-way ANOVA method was used for comparison between multiple groups. Two-way ANOVA tests were used to compare 2 or more variables between groups. The relationship between short-chain fatty acids and liver weight was assessed using Spearman’s correlation. A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data were expressed as mean±standard deviation (±SD) and a P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Given the exploratory nature of this animal study, each group contained 5 rats. While this limits statistical power, consistent trends were observed across key outcome measures. A formal power calculation was not performed, which is acknowledged as a limitation.

Results

UPLC/Orbitrap-MS Analysis

Each group consisted of 5 rats (n=5), as previously described. UPLC/Orbitrap-MS analysis was utilized to characterize the chemical composition of XHS extract. A total of 805 compounds were putatively identified by comparing the acquired spectra against the mzCloud database, with 237 compounds exhibiting a Best Match score greater than 70. These compounds encompassed a diverse range of constituents, including aminophenols, polysaccharides, saponins, anthraquinones, and organic acids. Notably, 2 of the compounds found, ginsenoside and ferulic acid, have demonstrated pronounced anti-cancer activity. Further mass spectrometry analysis was performed on select compounds with a Best Match score exceeding 70, as detailed in Table 2, and the corresponding total ion flow diagram is presented in Figure 2. Although direct causal links were not explored, these bioactive components have previously been implicated in glucose regulation and anti-inflammatory pathways, which may partially explain the observed therapeutic effects.

XHS Improved Glucose Tolerance and Insulin Resistance

Figure 3A illustrates the body weights of the different rat groups. Prior to drug administration, all high-fat diet-fed groups exhibited a linear increase in body weight; following gavage, the metformin group displayed the most pronounced decrease, while the XHS groups at various doses demonstrated dose-dependent reductions. At week 8, sampling revealed that fasting insulin and fasting blood glucose levels in the treated groups were lower than those in the control group, as depicted in Figure 3B and 3C. Notably, compared with the control group, the HOMA-IR values in the untreated model group were elevated by fourfold, whereas those in the XHS-treated groups increased by less than onefold, with levels comparable to those in the metformin group, thereby suggesting that XHS significantly ameliorated insulin resistance (Figure 3D). These findings confirm that the observed improvements were significant relative to the model group. Given the impaired glucose tolerance and insulin resistance observed, an OGTT was performed at week 8. As shown in Figure 3E, at baseline (week 8), the untreated model, XHS, and metformin groups exhibited impaired glucose tolerance compared with the control group. After treatment, blood glucose levels decreased in the XHS and metformin groups, with significant improvements compared with the untreated model group. Subsequent treatment resulted in decreased blood glucose levels and reduced area under the curve (AUC) in both the XHS and metformin groups, with the most notable reduction observed in the XHS-M group. Additionally, an ITT conducted at week 8 revealed that while insulin sensitivity was impaired in the untreated model, XHS, and metformin groups – with blood glucose remaining significantly higher than in the control group at all post-injection time points – the AUC in the XHS-treated group was significantly lower than that in the untreated model group (P<0.01). Among the treatment groups, the medium-dose XHS group (XHS-M) consistently showed the most pronounced improvements in fasting blood glucose, OGTT, and ITT metrics, suggesting a potential optimal dose response.

Effect of XHS on Pathological Changes in the Liver

After HE staining, pathological morphological changes in liver tissues were observed under the light microscope. The normal (control) group of rats had normal liver tissue structure, clear structure of liver lobules, no degeneration or necrosis of hepatocytes, hepatic cords radiating from the central vein to the surrounding area, clear nuclei in the center, and no obvious narrowing or dilatation of liver sinusoids. In the untreated obesity model group, the hepatocytes were irregularly arranged, with blurred borders, obvious swelling, uneven cytoplasmic staining, variable size of lipid droplets, and infiltration of a large number of inflammatory cells. Compared with the untreated model group, the XHS-H and metformin groups showed significantly reduced liver lesions (Figure 4A). It can also be seen that aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels in rats in the XHS-M, XHS-H, and metformin groups were similar to those in the control group, and the difference between XHS-L and the untreated model group was larger than the difference between the XHS-L group and the control group (Figure 4B, 4C). Figure 4D shows that that creatinine was normal in all groups of rats.

After oil red staining, Figure 5A shows that among the untreated model, XHS-L, and XHS-M groups, fat droplets were significantly increased in all 3 of these groups compared with the control group, and the intensity of staining was significantly enhanced. In contrast, there was no such significant change in the XHS-H and metformin groups. Compared with the untreated model group, lipid levels, including triglycerides, total cholesterol, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) decreased in the XHS-S, XHS-M, XHS-L, and metformin groups, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) rose (Figure 5B–5E).

XHS Modulates the Structure and Diversity of the Intestinal Flora

Alpha diversity analysis showed that the Chao1 diversity index, phylogenetic diversity index, abundance-based coverage estimator (ACE) diversity index, Shannon diversity index, and Simpson diversity index differed significantly among the groups of rats (Figure 6A–6E). The Chao1 index is used to assess the total number of species, the Shannon index reflects the number and evenness of species, and the phylogenetic diversity index measures phylogenetic diversity. Both the XHS and metformin groups showed significantly higher microbial richness compared with the untreated model group, as reflected by the Chao1 and phylogenetic diversity indices (P<0.001), with no significant difference between the XHS and metformin groups. This suggests that XHS and metformin exerted comparable effects in restoring overall microbial species richness in obese insulin-resistant rats. The ACE index, used to estimate the number of operational taxonomic units (OTUs) in the gut microbiota, showed a similar pattern: both the XHS and metformin groups differed significantly from the untreated model group (P<0.001), but not from each other, indicating similar efficacy in increasing OTUs, reflecting increased species richness. A higher ACE index represents greater species-level diversity. There was a significant difference between the XHS group and the control group (P<0.001), but the difference between the untreated model group and the control group was even more significant (P<0.0001), which suggests that XHS can increase the abundance of species in obese insulin-resistant individuals. As seen in the Venn plot, expression of 119 genes was identical in the XHS group and control group, while expression of 202 genes was identical in the metformin group and the control group, and expression of 213 genes was identical in the XHS vs metformin group, as shown in Figure 6F.

Relative Abundance Analysis of Intestinal Flora

As shown in Figure 7, at the phylum level, the intestinal flora was mainly composed of 3 phyla: Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, and Fusobacteria. Compared with the control group, the ratio of Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes was elevated in the untreated obesity model, XHS, and metformin groups, but it was lower in the XHS group than in the untreated model group, suggesting that XHS could reduce the ratio of Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes. Compared with the control group, the proportion of Firmicutes was elevated in the untreated model, XHS, and metformin groups, but was lower in the XHS group than in the untreated model group, which indicated that XHS could reduce the proportion of Firmicutes. The abundance of Proteobacteria in the XHS group was lower than that in the metformin group after treatment. This suggests that XHS improves the intestinal flora by altering the ratio of Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes and affects the abundance percentage of the major phyla: Firmicutes and Proteobacteria.

As shown in Figure 7, the abundance of Escherichia-Shigella and genera in the Lachnospiraceae family was elevated in obese individuals, and was significantly lower in the group taking XHS or metformin compared with the untreated model group. At the same time, the abundance of Bacteroides in the metformin and XHS groups was significantly higher. This suggests that XHS can intervene in obesity insulin resistance by increasing the abundance of Bacteroides and decreasing the abundance of Escherichia-Shigella and Lachnospiraceae genera. This is similar to the mechanism of action of metformin.

Discussion

Insulin resistance is an inappropriate response of insulin-dependent cells to the insulin hormone [18,19], and obesity is a key contributor to insulin resistance [6]. However, excessive calorie intake in the form of a high-fat diet is considered a major risk factor for obesity and insulin resistance [20]. Various public health policies and treatments have been developed around the globe to counteract the obesity epidemic [21], but the prevalence of obesity continues to grow at an alarming rate, which can lead to an increase in the number of patients with obesity complications, such as the devastating type 2 diabetes mellitus [22]. Type 2 diabetes mellitus is a metabolic disorder characterized by chronic hyperglycemia with insulin resistance [23]. The hyperglycemia that occurs in diabetes is caused by decreased insulin sensitivity, resulting in excess insulin production, and current treatments are to increase insulin sensitivity or inject exogenous insulin. Unfortunately, these treatments do not currently ensure long-term glycemic control or reverse progression [24].

These findings demonstrate significant changes in glucose metabolism-related indices across treatment groups. Chinese medicine theory suggests that dampness and heat, as pathogenic factors, can damage the lung, spleen, stomach and intestinal complexes – concepts traditionally associated with gut inflammation and metabolic stagnation, which may overlap with dysbiosis in biomedical terms, thus affecting the onset and development of insulin resistance in obesity. Recent studies have shown that traditional Chinese medicine has potential anti-insulin resistance effects by inhibiting different mechanisms, such as regulating intestinal flora, regulating changes in lipopolysaccharide (LPS) levels, activating hypoxia/HIF-1α [25], and regulating intestinal microflora. Laxative Yellow San Plus Minus formula is composed of patchouli, burnt gardenia, windbreak, Chenpi, Huanglian, and raw licorice, and clears away heat, dispels dampness, and eliminates accumulated heat in the spleen and stomach. The combination of the 6 medicines provides a deeper understanding of the pathogenesis of obesity insulin resistance from the perspective of traditional Chinese medicine.

In obesity and diabetes, insulin participates in multiple pathways to change the regulation of lipid and glucose metabolism. Numerous studies have demonstrated that alterations in the gut microbiome are associated with obesity and type 2 diabetes [9,26–28]. Moreover, interventions targeting the gut microbiome in rat models yield more pronounced effects than in humans, likely due to greater intersubject variability; for instance, dietary effects on the gut microbiome are more substantial in rats than in humans [29,30]. The Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio is frequently cited as a marker of obesity [31]. In our study, this ratio was highest in the untreated obesity model group and decreased following treatment with either XHS or metformin, with significant differences observed in the XHS-treated groups compared with the model group (P<0.05).

Firmicutes – which are efficient in energy extraction through modulation of intestinal dysbiosis and permeability – were most abundant in the model group and declined after treatment. Conversely, Bacteroidetes, which break down complex carbohydrates (eg, cellulose) to aid digestion and nutrient absorption, were relatively low in the gut microbiome of obese individuals but increased following administration of XHS or metformin. Although the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio is frequently altered in obesity models, its role remains controversial, and the directionality of this relationship – whether causal or consequential – is still unclear. In this study, we interpret the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio shift as a correlative marker of gut dysbiosis rather than as a mechanistic endpoint. Diet also exerts a strong influence on the plasma metabolome independent of the gut microbiota; for example, the plasma metabolomes of individuals with different diets vary, with only a few metabolites produced primarily by the gut microbiota [32,33]. In our study, triglycerides, total cholesterol, and LDL-C (‘bad’ cholesterol) was higher and HDL-C (‘good’ cholesterol) was lower in the untreated obesity model group than in the control, metformin, and XHS-treated groups, consistent with their known associations with metabolic health. These results suggest that different doses of XHS can improve lipid profiles. These plasma metabolite changes may interact with gut microbial metabolism and influence insulin sensitivity. While this study focused on gut microbiota as a potential mediator of XHS’s metabolic effects, we acknowledge that insulin resistance involves a complex interplay of additional factors, including gastrointestinal hormones, systemic inflammation, and neuroendocrine regulation. Future studies are warranted to explore these alternative or complementary mechanisms in greater detail.

Beyond general microbial composition shifts, emerging studies suggest that specific gut taxa and host-microbiota interactions may influence broader metabolic and endocrine functions. Certain next-generation commensals, such as Akkermansia muciniphila, have been linked to metabolic improvements, though their translational application remains complex and context-dependent [33]. Moreover, obesity has been shown to exert systemic effects extending beyond glucose metabolism, including potential impacts on skeletal development [34] and gastrointestinal hormone secretion patterns [35]. These findings underscore the multifactorial nature of obesity-related pathophysiology and support a broader framework for interpreting gut-targeted interventions. XHS treatment was associated with improved insulin resistance, potentially mediated by modulation of gut microbiota. Further research is warranted to elucidate the specific pathways through which XHS acts, as well as to identify its active components and potential molecular targets, thereby laying the foundation for subsequent analyses and discussion.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the sample size per group was relatively small, which may reduce the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Second, although we observed significant changes in gut microbiota composition following XHS treatment, the study design does not allow us to establish causal relationships between specific bacterial taxa and improvements in metabolic outcomes. Third, we did not investigate other potentially relevant pathways involved in insulin resistance, such as gastrointestinal hormone levels, inflammatory markers, or host gene expression. Finally, this was a preclinical animal study, and the applicability of the findings to humans requires cautious interpretation and further clinical validation.

Conclusions

This study shows that XHS may effectively mitigate obesity-related insulin resistance. This conclusion is based on the observation of consistent improvements in glycemic control, lipid profiles, and gut microbial balance after XHS treatment. Our comprehensive approach – encompassing UPLC/Orbitrap-MS chemical profiling, detailed metabolic assessments, and gut microbiota analysis – revealed that XHS treatment significantly enhances glucose metabolism, improves insulin sensitivity, and favorably alters lipid profiles. This multidimensional strategy strengthens the reliability of our preclinical observations. In particular, the observed reduction in the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio and the shifts in bacterial populations suggest that modulation of the gut microbiome may play a pivotal role in mediating these effects. While these findings contribute valuable insights into the potential mechanisms of XHS, they also underscore the complexity of obesity and insulin resistance. Further research is needed to delineate the specific molecular pathways and active components involved, as well as to validate these results in clinical settings. Such studies could include targeted metabolomics, microbiota transplantation, or gene expression profiling to pinpoint the biological pathways involved. We offer these conclusions with humility, acknowledging the limitations of our study and the necessity for ongoing investigation to fully realize the therapeutic potential of XHS in metabolic disorders.
