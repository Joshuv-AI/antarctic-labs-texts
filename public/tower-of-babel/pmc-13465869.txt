Human small non-coding tRNA-derived fragments as key hallmarks of host-virus interaction in viral infections
Authors: Mahsa Aghajani Mir, Parisa Sharifi, Fatemeh Gila, Hamid Askari, Marjan Nourigorji, Farahnaz Zare, Sara Karimi, Zahra Saadatian, Abdolreza Daraei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Viral infections cause deep changes in the host transcriptome, triggering a complex antiviral immune response that involves multiple proteins and molecular and cellular signaling pathways. Traditionally, Transfer RNAs (tRNAs) have been identified for their vital role in protein synthesis, and recent investigations have determined different small non-coding RNAs (sncRNAs), such as transfer RNA-derived small RNAs (tsRNAs), such as tRNA-derived fragments (tRFs), and tRNA-derived stress-induced RNAs (tiRNAs), that also known as a new class of regulatory molecules with crucial roles during viral infection. Current evidence reveals that tsRNAs play key functions at the host–pathogen interface, controlling viral infection through various antiviral mechanisms, including gene silencing, epigenetic regulation, as well as transcriptional and post-transcriptional control. Moreover, the differential expression of tsRNAs has promise for their application as prognostic and diagnostic biomarkers in viral diseases. This review explores current insights into the roles of tsRNAs in viral infections, highlighting their involvement in host-virus interactions. Further, we will be discussing the broader implications of tsRNAs by emphasizing the importance of understanding their diverse regulatory functions in biological processes and human diseases to advance their potential as biomarkers and personalized therapeutic markers.

Introduction

According to comprehensive investigations, viral epidemics have been shown to cause widespread societal disruption and significant population declines through history [1]. In recent years, there has been a rise in outbreaks of viral infectious diseases that have rapidly spread across borders, leading to high mortality rates and becoming one of the biggest dangers to global public health [2]. Important examples of recent viral outbreaks that have caused major disasters include the SARS-CoV epidemic, which was triggered in China in December 2019 and spread to 200 countries; the 2009 H1N1 influenza pandemic; the 2013–2016 Ebola virus outbreak, predominantly in West Africa; and the 2016 spread of the Zika virus throughout the Americas and other regions [2]. Strikingly, three to four new species of human viruses are still being discovered every year [3]. Most human pathogens are primarily bacteria, fungi, or helminths, viruses account for more than two-thirds of all recently identified human-pathogen species. As noted, these emerging viruses have a wide range of effects on human health [4]. A comprehensive analysis of available data reveals that 219 virus species have been recognized as capable of infecting humans. In 1901, the first known such virus was the yellow fever virus. As previously noted, the current rate of identification of new human-infecting virus species remains steady, with around three to four new viruses discovered annually. Given this ongoing trend, it is probable that a significant number of viruses related to humans are still undiscovered [4].

Regardless of the virus type and its mode of infection, there are still some specific key steps required for viral pathogenicity to occur. However, some basic principles of infectivity are the same for most viruses. These include the following: first, the virus must penetrate the host and take control of the cellular machinery to spread the infection. Viral entry into the cell, genome replication, synthesis of viral proteins, formation of new virions, and release of virus particles from host cells are all part of this process [4, 5]. A virus must effectively evade the host's immune system to spread within it and cause infection. Structures such as the capsid, which protects the viral genome, and the lipid envelope, which helps some viruses evade immune detection, are often instrumental in this process. In addition, the virus disseminates through the extracellular environment, such as interstitial fluid, the lymphatic system, and the bloodstream, overcoming physical and biological barriers, thereby reaching and infecting distant tissues and organs [4, 6]. Second, viruses need to survive and reproduce. Viral shedding then occurs, during which the virus leaves its host and infects new hosts. Ultimately, the virus must be cleared by the host's adaptive immune system. However, some viruses can enter a latent state (dormancy) within host cells, leading to chronic infections. A significant amount of latency can result in prolonged viral shedding, increased transmission capacity, and the development of persistent infections [5]. While the overall mechanisms of viral infection have been identified, comprehensive investigations into the specific molecular pathways involved in individual viral diseases, as well as the general impact of specific host factors affecting viral responses, still need to be conducted [1].

Research conducted on the pathogenesis of viral infections in human hosts has revealed that viruses can modify signaling and molecular pathways to affect the activity of many genes, including host non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs). This variant mechanism enhances viral replication and the spread of infection in the host. Consequently, the identification of ncRNAs has the potential to greatly advance the development of therapeutic approaches for viral disorders [7, 8]. In the early 2000s, sequencing and analysis of the human and mouse genomes identified that almost 98% of DNA, previously known as "junk" DNA, is actually transcribed, although this was once thought to be rare. On the other hand, extensive research on messenger RNAs (mRNAs) has shown that the majority of these transcripts lack protein-coding potential and are generally classified as ncRNAs [9–11]. Further evidence shows that significant variation exists among ncRNAs in length, structure, and biological function. Generally, they are classified as either long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) or small non-coding RNAs (sncRNAs). lncRNAs, which are transcripts longer than 200 nucleotides, can have either linear or circular structures. Although the length of lncRNAs is similar to that of mRNAs, their functions are different [12]. Various studies have revealed that small ncRNAs typically contain 20–30 nucleotides. Moreover, they are classified into various subclasses with unique biological functions and clinical significance. It has been reported that two important subclasses, ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs) and transfer RNAs (tRNAs), play pivotal roles in gene regulation and RNA processing [12].

One of the most abundant ncRNAs is tRNA, which makes up roughly 4–10% of total RNA [13]. A newly identified group includes tRNA-derived small RNAs (tsRNAs or tDRs), which consist of all small non-coding RNA species that come from the cleavage of tRNAs. These are further divided into tRNA-derived fragments (tRFs), tRNA halves, and tRNA-derived small RNAs (tsRNAs) [14]. Lee et al. reported in 2009 that tsRNAs are the second most common class of small RNAs after miRNAs [15]. Further evidence supports that gene expression in a diverse range of organisms, including animals, plants, yeast, and bacteria, are modulated by these molecules at both transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels, making them essential for many disease processes [16]. Some of human diseases have been associated with the production of ncRNAs derived from tRNA, and some resulting tRNA-derived fragments have become promising biomarkers [17]. Evidence suggests that these fragments are involved in the development of differ disorders, such as cancer [17, 18], neurodegenerative disorders including amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) [19], and Parkinson’s disease (PD) [20], as a result of infections and stressors states like oxidative stress, hypoxia, ultraviolet irradiation, and thermal stress [21]. Recent investigations have shown that when cells invade the host cell through viral infection, a stress response is initiated by the ribonuclease angiogenin cleaves tRNAs to produce more pronounced “tRFs” than previously thought. The utilization of tRFs from hosts as primers can enhance infection efficiency and facilitate viral replication [22–24]. The results indicate that ncRNAs are essential to viral life cycles and could be useful targets or tools for developing antiviral therapies. These ncRNAs modify antiviral signaling pathways (such as interferon responses) to either facilitate viral persistence or assist the host cell's defense mechanisms to prevent viral dissemination [25].

Recent evidence indicates that viruses can control both the genes of the host and their own genes by using tsRNAs to help them replicate. Supporting this, high-throughput sequencing technologies have revealed that tsRNAs play a role in both viral infections and how the immune system responds. A comprehensive wisdom of the interactions between tRFs and viruses could lead to innovative antiviral strategies to mitigate the global threat of viral infections [26]. This review consolidates and examines current understanding of the role of tsRNAs in viral pathogenesis, elucidates their molecular mechanisms, and proposes that tsRNAs represent promising therapeutic targets for combating viral infections.

Molecular mechanisms of viral infections

Viruses are complex biological agents that have evolved molecular mechanisms to invade host cells and promote their proliferation by disrupting cellular homeostasis, as well as the immune system or the host's own defense mechanisms. Notably, understanding these molecular interactions is necessary for comprehending virus-host dynamics, revealing the pathophysiological mechanisms driving viral diseases, and guiding the development of targeted antiviral treatments [27].

A prominent characteristic of viral infections is the marked interindividual variation in susceptibility and disease severity. While early epidemiological research sought to investigate this variability, it was only by the mid-twentieth century that genetic and molecular studies began to uncover the hereditary factors underlying differences in viral infection outcomes [28]. Various studies provide confirmatory evidence that molecular and cellular mechanisms, as well as immune responses, differ significantly based on the type of virus, the host's immune system, genetic background, and the specific organ involved. However, the exact molecular pathways involved in viral pathogenesis have not been thoroughly investigated. Despite this, meta-analytical evidence indicates that inflammatory pathways, autophagy, phagocytosis, immune cell dynamics, coagulation cascades, changes in gene and protein expression, and modifications of ncRNAs such as miRNAs and tsRNAs are all crucial for viral infections [28–30]. Over the last three decades, a notable number of monogenic inborn errors of immunity (IEIs) have been identified, and our understanding of their underlying pathophysiological mechanisms has improved significantly. These efforts have facilitated the discovery of genetic factors that increase human vulnerability to viral infections [29, 31]. Nevertheless, most studies have primarily focused on immune responses stimulated by viral infections, with little attention given to significant modifications that viruses cause in sncRNAs, such as tRFs [28].

Recent studies indicate that viral infections can significantly impact the expression and distribution of tRFs within host cells. In this context, viruses can regulate sncRNA expression to manipulate their functions [32]. Conversely, the host immune system modulates sncRNA expression profiles in response to viral challenges as part of its natural defense. Notably, an atypical sncRNA fragment derived from tRNA-Lys has been identified for its antiviral properties against human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), as its increased expression effectively inhibits HIV replication [32]. Furthermore, the appearance of viruses such as SARS-CoV-2 has made us aware of the need to increase our knowledge of how cells and the immune system respond to viral infection, which needs further investigation in this area. t's also crucial to gain a deeper understanding of how viruses interact with the cells they infect in order to develop novel therapeutics strategies [33]. In line with this result, different molecular targets have been revealed as potential biomolecules of therapeutic intervention for ncRNAs, particularly tRNA-derived fragments (tsRNAs or tDRs). However, there is still a lack of research on the expression patterns and functional roles of tDRs during SARS-CoV-2 infection, which highlights an important gap in current knowledge [33].

Understanding the fundamental roles of tRFs in virus-host interactions is vital to their ability to encounter infection-driven modifications. Comprehension of these modifications enhances our knowledge of the viral strategies employed to evade host immune responses during the spread of infection [34]. Accordingly, tRFs, a significant class of ncRNAs, are necessary for precise gene regulation. Nonetheless, the intricate and various interactions between tRFs and other RNA molecules or genes during host-virus interplay are still poorly apprehended [34]. This complexity probably occurs from virus-triggered changes in specific tRFs populations that influence a comprehensive array of host genes and proteins important for viral replication, potentially reflecting a viral strategy to optimize infection efficiency [34]. Having a thorough understanding of the molecular mechanisms controlling viral entry, replication, and spread is crucial for the identification of essential cellular pathways and for the development of targeted, effectual antiviral therapeutics [34].

Diversity and biogenesis of tRNA-derived small non-coding RNAs (tsRNAs)

In the late 1970s, tsRNAs were first identified as non-functional fragments produced by random degradation of tRNA and thus attracted minimal scientific interest [35, 36]. However, the development of high-throughput sequencing technologies has helped the identification of a diverse range of tsRNAs in numerous organisms, including mammals, plants, fungi and bacteria [37]. In humans, tRNAs are encoded by a large multigene family, with the genome containing up to 506 genes that correspond to 49 distinct tRNA species [38]. A notable discovery from the Human Genome Project was the finding of sequence variability within tRNA genes corresponding to the same anticodon, a phenomenon termed “isodecoders” [39]. Functional studies have confirmed that these isodecoders differ in their decoding efficiency, indicating that certain tRNA isodecoders may perform roles beyond conventional translation, as they do not represent optimal substrates for protein synthesis. tsRNAs are single-stranded RNA fragments measuring between 16 and 48 nucleotides in length, found in the nuclear and cytoplasmic [41]. Recent research shows that tsRNAs are not just random pieces broken off from tRNAs, but constitute a distinct and abundant class of small RNAs with defined sequence motifs and functional significance [42]. As members of the ncRNA family, tsRNAs remain incompletely characterized. Existing data suggest that they are generated through precise cleavage events and subsequently undergo modifications at their 5′ and 3′ termini, which enhance their stability [43, 44]. tsRNAs are divided into two main groups. This classification is based on their size and the specific locations within the tRNA molecule where they are cut off. tRNA-derived stress-induced RNAs (tiRNAs) are recognized as typically containing 28–36 nucleotides and are generated through the anticodon loop of mature tRNAs by specific cleavage in response to stress. tRNA-derived fragments (tRFs) are shorter sequenced entities that are derived from either mature or precursor tRNAs, typically ranging from 14 to 30 nucleotides in length [24, 37, 44].

Each type of tsRNA is produced by different ribonucleases, such as stress-activated ones like DICER, angiogenin (ANG), ElaC ribonuclease Z protein 2 (ELAC2), and RNASE1. The way they are formed follows a regulated process, and each type is made through its own specific pathway within the cell. Moreover, research has shown that ANG and RNASE1 might be important in controlling the stability and production of certain types of extracellular tDRs by breaking them down. Functionally, in vitro analysis that shows the silencing of these RNase enzymes would likely lead to an increase in the expression of certain tDRs [15, 37, 45]. Further analysis shows that unlike ANG, RNASE1 is not often involved in tDR biogenesis but mainly helps in targeting tDRs for degradation when the cell is treated with H2O2. These results show that RNASE1 is breaking down a wide range of tDRs, both inside and outside cells, when there is oxidative stress conditions [45]. These various tsRNAs accumulate during different biological states across multiple species, yet their specific generation and stability mechanisms are still being actively elucidated [15, 37].

Currently, the nomenclature for tsRNAs lacks standardization, with researchers often assigning names based on molecular origin and system-specific preferences. This inconsistency reflects the nascent stage of the field and highlights the significant potential for further investigation and development [37, 46]. tsRNAs have been recognized in a vast array of human cell types, tissues, and extracellular fluids over the past decade. Further, it has been shown that tsRNAs are involved in several molecular activities such as maintaining mRNA stability, modulating translation processes, biogenesis of rRNA, and facilitating reverse transcription of RNA. The impact of tsRNAs on essential cellular functions, including proliferation, migration, cell cycle progression, and programmed cell death, is facilitated by these pathways [47, 48]. Increasing evidence has reported associations of specific tsRNAs with a broad spectrum of pathological conditions, including cancer, metabolic diseases, stress-related injuries, neurodegenerative disorders, and viral infections [47, 49–51]. Importantly, tsRNAs hold dual functions as both contributors to disease pathogenesis and as therapeutic targets, which can be crucial. Also, their stability and specificity make them promising candidates for diagnostic biomarkers in clinical settings [47, 48].

tRNA-derived stress-induced RNA (tiRNAs) and tRNA halves

tiRNAs, some cases known as tRNA halves, are produced by mature tRNAs that undergo selective cleavage at the anticodon loop. Originally described as stress-induced RNA fragments, tRNA halves originate in response to different cellular stress states in eukaryotes, although they are also detectable under non-stressful physiological conditions [51, 52]. Unlike miRNAs and siRNAs, which are produced by Dicer or RNase III enzymes, tRNA halves generation in mammals is mediated by the ribonuclease angiogenin, which cleaves mature tRNAs at the anticodon loop. This cleavage produces tRNA halves characterized by a 5′ hydroxyl group instead of the conventional 5′ phosphate commonly observed in other small RNAs [53, 54].

ANG, member of the RNase A superfamily, selectively cleaves mature tRNAs at the anticodon loop, producing two distinct types of tRNA-derived fragments: 5′-tiRNAs (tiRNA-5s), which typically range from 30 to 35 nucleotides, and 3′-tiRNAs (tiRNA-3s), generally consisting of 40–50 nucleotides [51, 55]. Under physiological conditions, ANG is predominantly placed in the nucleus; however, upon exposure to stress stimulants, it dissociates from its inhibitor, ribonuclease/angiogenin inhibitor 1 (RNH1), translocates to the cytoplasm, and cleaves cytoplasmic tRNAs to generate tiRNA-5 and tiRNA-3 fragments [56]. Recombinant ANG has been found to cause tiRNA production and reduce protein making, even when there is no outside stress [23]. RNH1 works as a blocker by attaching to ANG inside the cell and stopping tiRNA from being made [56].

ANG-mediated cleavage of tRNAs is tightly controlled, and the overexpression of ANG has been associated with higher tiRNA levels in certain conditions. Prior research has indicated that ANG activity and the resulting tRNA cleavage are affected by different stimuli, such as nutritional deprivation, hypoxia, heat shock, oxidative stress, ultraviolet radiation, and viral infection [21]. Furthermore, the post-transcriptional changes in mature tRNAs, which are activated by enzymes like DNA methyltransferase DNMT2 and cytosine-5 methyltransferase NSun2, can enhance stability and provide protection against possible ANG cleavage [23, 43, 57]. Interestingly, it is mostly the tiRNA-5 fragments rather than their tiRNA-3 counterparts that have been found to restrict global protein translation [58]. The number of tiRNAs produced is limited to a small amount under normal growth conditions. These tiRNAs are mostly discovered in the cytoplasm, but smaller amounts can be seen in both the nucleus and mitochondria and have been detected in human bodily fluids [59].

tRNA-derived small RNA fragments (tRFs)

Small RNAs that are conserved across various species, from bacteria to humans, are known as tRF. This broad spectrum suggests that tRFs may have functions beyond their usual roles in protein synthesis. Consequently, tRFs are being increasingly acknowledged as functional molecules that could have implications for human diseases. New evidence indicates that they can be a main contributor to the pathogenesis of human viral infections [53, 60–65]. Typically, these tRFs range from 14 to 30 nucleotides, and their length is usually less than that of most tiRNAs, which are produced through the cleavage of both mature and precursor tRNAs (pre-tRNAs) by enzymes like Dicer and ANG [49, 66–68].

Various studies have classified tRFs into different classes according to their origin and the precise cleavage sites within precursor and mature tRNAs. Generally, tRFs are classified into several types, including tRF-1, tRF-2, tRF-3, tRF-5, and internal tRFs (i-tRFs) [69]. Each class is produced through the action of particular ribonucleases and is controlled by unique regulatory pathways [70, 71]. Some researchers call the tRF-5 and tRF-3 groups collectively referred to as “tRF halves” or “type I tRFs” [72]. It would be an intuitive conclusion that these classifications reflect the differences in biogenesis and functional roles within cellular processes [53].

At the maturity of tRNAs, a 5'-monophosphate group and 3' terminal CCA sequence are covalently bound to their respective amino acids. tRNAs that are folded correctly have a special cloverleaf secondary structure, which comprises four base-paired stems organized from the 5' to 3' end: the D arm, anticodon arm, T arm, and acceptor stem. The D-loop, anticodon loop, variable loop, and T-loop are all conserved loops linked by stems. Before being translated, primary tRNA transcripts undergo multiple maturation processes [73]. The absence of the 3'-CCA tail distinguishes pre-tRNAs from mature tRNAs, as they have instead a 5' leader with alternating triphosphatic group residues and oligouridine residues at the end of the 3'-trailer region, which is an inactive region. Moreover, the endonucleases RNase P and RNase Z are responsible for cleaving the 5' leader sequence and the 3' trailer sequence, respectively, while introns are removed through splicing when they are present [74, 75]. Importantly, the eukaryotic genome does not contain the essential 3'-CCA sequence, which is added through enzymatic addition after transcription [76]. Despite the fact that mature intact tRNAs are recognized as essential components for protein synthesis, several studies have identified and described shorter derived fragments of these transcribed genes, with the biological roles of which are currently being investigated [77].

tRF-1

The tRF-1 series, also referred to as tsRNAs, are products of RNase Z or its cytoplasmic counterpart Zinc phosphodiesterase ELAC protein 2 (ELAC2) from precursor tRNAs. These fragments are distinguished by their 5′ ends starting immediately downstream of the mature tRNA’s 3′ end (excluding the 5′-CCA-3′ tail). Their 3′ ends consist of a sequence of uridine residues derived from the transcriptional runoff by RNA polymerase III, resulting in a 3′ trailer region enriched in polyuridine sequences [15, 53, 78].

Variability of experimental approaches and the species studied have largely contributed to ongoing debate about the subcellular localization of tRFs. The nucleus is where most of the 5′-tRFs found in human cells are located, while 3′-tRFs and tRF-1 fragments are mainly concentrated in the cytoplasm [79, 80]. Conversely, studies on Drosophila and Trypanosoma cruzi have revealed that tRFs are exclusively present in the cytoplasm. These variations highlight the complexity of tRF distribution and point to possible species-specific regulatory mechanisms [81, 82].

The 3′-end sequences of tRNAs are generally believed to be rapidly degraded within the nucleus shortly after cleavage from precursor tRNAs. Consequently, it is surprising that two primary hypotheses have been suggested to account for the evasion of nuclear degradation by tRF-1 fragments: (1) they are rapidly exported from the nucleus via an as-yet unidentified pathway immediately after processing, or (2) precursor tRNAs evade nuclear quality control and are cleaved by cytoplasmic RNase Z. Experimental data favors the latter scenario. For example, a tRF-1 fragment derived from tRNA-Ser referred to as tRF-1001 [15] or Cand45 [53], has been shown to originate from pre-tRNAs present in the cytoplasm [15, 53]. In addition, various studies report that the tRNA-processing endonucleases responsible for generating the tRF-1 fragments are localized in the cytoplasm, supporting the concept of cytoplasmic processing for these fragments [15, 83]. The varying subcellular localization of tRFs is believed to be significant due to the biogenesis pathways they participate in to be critical for their diverse cellular functions. Precursor tRNA structural features greatly influence the production of resulting fragments, which means that these fragmentation events are not mere degradation consequences. The cleavage of tRFs by certain endonucleatic enzymes is another indication of their biological importance, which highlights a controlled and deliberate mechanism of processing [15, 23, 53, 80].

Internal tRFs (tRF-2)

A recently distinguished subtype of tRFs, encompassing the anticodon loop, has been discovered in breast cancer cells. These fragments, appointed as tRF-2, are derived from the anticodon loop region of particular tRNAs, including tRNA-Tyr, tRNA-Gly, tRNA-Asp, and tRNA-Glu. The stem sequence and anticodon loop are present in them, but they lack the 5′ and 3′ ends found in the full-length tRNA [84, 85]. Despite these findings, the specific ribonuclease that produces tRF-2 fragments is still uncertain [60].

tRF-3

Transfer RNA-derived fragments belonging to the tRF-3 subtypes commonly have lengths ranging from 18 to 22 nucleotides, which are produced from the 3′ end sequencing of mature tRNAs. tRF-3 has been shown to include the conserved CCA tail. These fragments arise from precise cleavage within the TψC loop by enzymes such as ANG, Dicer, or other members of the Ribonuclease A family. The tRF-3 class is also distinguished into two subclasses based on size: tRF-3a, approximately 18 nucleotides in length, and tRF-3b, roughly 22 nucleotides long. Importantly, it has been discovered that the 3′ termini of tRF-3 fragments align exactly with the 3′ ends of their precursor tRNAs [26, 86].

In particular, Dicer-mediated cleavage products are associated with 5′-monophosphate groups on some tRF-3 molecules, which are found in the ciliate Tetrahymena. The fragments interact with Argonaute/Piwi proteins, which facilitate their biogenesis through a Dicer-dependent process [53, 87]. Besides Dicer, angiogenin has also been demonstrated to cleave the TψC loop region to produce tRF-3 species, indicating the involvement of multiple ribonucleases in their formation [56]. The tRF-3 family has been found to function as a mediator for translational repression by binding to Argonaute proteins [53]. Both subclasses of tRF-3 indicate binding affinity to Argonaute proteins, with a preference for Argonaute (Ago)3 and Ago4 over Ago1 and Ago2 [53]. Moreover, tRF-3 fragments have been shown to regulate target gene expression through modulating both mRNA stability and protein translation [56].

tRF-5

The tRF-5 category makes the earliest identified class of tRNA-derived fragments. These fragments usually are under 30 nucleotides long, and are made by Dicer enzymatic cleavage of mature tRNAs at the D-loop or anticodon loop. These fragments include the 5′ end of the parent tRNA, with their 5′ termini precisely confirming those of the mature tRNA sequencing. Moreover, tRF-5 fragments have been shown in a variety of organisms, such as mammalian cells, plants, and fission yeast [80, 88, 89]. tRF-5 fragments can be classified into three subtypes based on the cleavage sites of the Dicer enzyme at the D-loop site. The main subclasses include tRF-5a (14–16 nucleotides in length), tRF-5b (22–24 nucleotides long), and tRF-5c (28–30 nucleotides long) [84, 90]. Functional studies showed that knockdown of Dicer enzyme expression results in a significant reduction in the steady-state abundance of tRF-5 species, thus highlighting the necessary roles of Dicer in their generation [80]. Furthermore, tRF-5 molecules are mostly located within the nucleus and have been found to accumulate in the nuclear foci of HeLa cells [80]. The cleavage of tRNAs to assemble thRNA bases has been identified by several ribonucleolytic peptides. Specifically, enzymes like Dicer or ANG are critical in the biogenesis of the tRF-5 category in mammals [15, 80, 88]. A notable study also reported that tRF-5 fragments can be produced following the hybridization of the 3′ end of human/tRNA-Lys with the HIV-1 primer binding site (PBS). This finding suggests that tRFs may form upon base-pairing between a tRNA and another RNA molecule. The HIV-PBS-derived tRF links to the Ago2 protein and mediates cleavage of complementary RNA targets [87]. Nonetheless, it has also been suggested that the tRF-5 series may originate from the processing of larger tRNA-derived intermediates, such as tiRNA-5, via Dicer-independent pathways [56]. Recent research has shown that the tRF-5 group plays a role in stopping the production of proteins [91]. Specifically, Sobala and Hutvagner demonstrated that certain tRF-5 fragments can inhibit the translation of mRNA reporters in vitro [91]. The suggested inhibition mechanism targets the elongation phase of translation, is separate from the suppression of the translation mediated through the tiRNA-5 subtypes, and has a significant effect on the starting process [57].

Internal tRFs (i-tRF)

The biogenesis of internal tRNA-derived fragments (i-tRFs) is not yet comprehensively illuminated. These fragments were first found in breast cancer tissues, corresponding normal adjacent samples, and breast cancer cell lines [65]. i-tRFs are a recently described subclass of tRFs originating from internal regions of mature tRNAs, frequently including or overlapping the anticodon loop [65, 92]. Structurally, these fragments comprise sequences from the anticodon loop as well as segments of the D- and T-loops [26, 93, 94]. Unlike other tRF kinds, i-tsRFs have a different and unusual length. It is also known that there are no established standards for defining their length or the uniform nomenclature of tsRNAs. The absence of standardized nomenclature is indicative of the evolving and changing comprehension of these novel tRF species [55].

Biological functions and underlying mechanisms of tRNA-derived small RNAs

Although there is growing evidence that tsRNAs are generated in nearly all cell types, a key question has been whether these molecules are simply byproducts of tRNA degradation, act as intermediaries for particular endonucleases, or if they have bona fide main biological functions [37]. Despite the lack of complete understanding of the mechanisms underlying their activities, new investigations have revealed that numerous tsRNAs in mammals are abundant and can play a crucial role. It has also been reported that the cleavage of mature tRNAs into tsRNAs is a highly specific, site-directed process that is limited to particular tRNA isotypes [37, 72]. Furthermore, functional analyses indicate that tsRNAs regulate gene expression through a variety of distinct mechanisms. Notably, tsRNAs exhibit characteristics typical of regulatory sncRNAs and are likely to participate in diverse biological processes via multiple modes of action [37, 72]. Modulation of mRNA stability and the mediation of gene silencing

Growing evidence suggests that tRFs, small non-coding RNAs generally under 30 nucleotides in length, exhibit functional parallels to miRNAs by regulating mRNA stability via direct interaction with target transcripts [37]. Given their evolutionary conservation across both prokaryotic and eukaryotic organisms [79], in species such as Drosophila, tRFs have been shown to preferentially inhibit the translation of mRNAs encoding ribosomal proteins and various translation factors through antisense-mediated interactions [95]. Moreover, shorter tRFs species have been identified as components of Argonaute protein complexes, enabling them to interact with the 3’ untranslated regions (3’UTRs) of target mRNAs and subsequently repress gene expression in a manner similar to miRNAs [79]. Interestingly, certain tRNA-derived small RNAs exhibit selective binding affinity for Ago1, Ago3, and Ago4 proteins, but not Ago2, in a cell type-dependent context, suggesting that tRFs may also carry out functions distinct from canonical miRNA-like targeting [79]. For example, Haussecker and others found that 3′-tRFs and 1-tRFs control how genes are expressed by competing to attach to AGO proteins, which in turn changes how effectively the target genes are silenced [53].

This assertion is supported by evidence indicating that tRFs associate with RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) components, including AGO proteins and GW182/TNRC6, together with their complementary target mRNA [79, 96]. For example, Maute and colleagues identified a specific tRF-3, designated CU1276 and originating from tRNA-Gly-GCC, which exhibits microRNA-like functions in B-cell lymphoma. This tRF-3 physically associates with AGO proteins and facilitates sequence-specific repression of target mRNA expression [97]. Additionally, 5′-terminal tRNA halves in the cytoplasm form complexes with tRNase ZL that act as small guide RNAs to selectively bind and cleave target transcripts for downregulating their expressions [98, 99]. Furthermore, overexpression of certain tRF-3 species namely tRF-3001, tRF-3003, and tRF-3009, derived from their respective parental tRNAs has been reported to repress target genes broadly in a manner comparable to canonical microRNAs [96]. Therefore, despite their biogenesis being independent of Dicer and Drosha enzymes, tRF-3 molecules are capable of associating with AGO-containing RISCs to mediate the repression of target mRNAs [100].

Evidence suggests that tRF-1 fragments can interact with both Argonaute proteins AGO1 and AGO2 as well as Piwi-like RNA-mediated gene silencing 2 (PIWIL2) [101]. The fact that microRNA target prediction algorithms have been used to predict tRF target sites and not the other way around may not be suitable for accurately targeting targets specific to this subfield is worth mentioning [100]. Consequently, tRFs are thought to regulate gene expression via both canonical and non-canonical pathways associated with miRNA or siRNA mechanisms by incorporating into AGO-family proteins. Given that AGO proteins typically bind small RNAs of 20 to 24 nucleotides in length, it is unlikely that longer tRNA halves are loaded into these complexes to function through similar pathways [102]. Therefore, further investigation is necessary to identify the endogenous mRNA targets of tRFs and to elucidate the comprehensive regulatory networks in which they participate.

Translation regulation

tRNAs serve as important molecules of the translational machinery, and their cleavage plays a pivotal role in modulating protein synthesis. However, the control of translation via tRNA cleavage is more complex than simply changing the fate of mature tRNAs required for protein synthesis [72]. Recently published research has shown that tiRNAs, specifically 5′-tiRNAs and similar cleavage products, can decrease overall translation efficiency by about 10–15% [23]. It may seem reasonable that destroying full-length tRNAs would hinder translation, but research indicates that the steady-state levels of intact tRNAs remain mostly untouched during cleavage events [23, 103].

Recent studies have revealed that certain 5′-tiRNAs, particularly those from tRNA-Ala and tRNA-Cys, can conform to G-quadruplex-like conformations. These structures selectively inhibit cap-dependent translation initiation by competing with elements of the translation complex, such as eukaryotic initiation factor 4G (eIF4G) and eIF4A, but not through internal ribosome entry site (IRES)-mediated translation [57, 104]. Since many proteins critical for cell survival and anti-apoptotic functions are synthesized via the IRES pathway, these tiRNAs probably play a protective role during cellular stress by selectively downregulating housekeeping protein synthesis to save energy, while allowing continued production of proteins critical for cell survival [105].

Besides, 5′-tiRNAs have been found to bind to the Y-box binding protein 1 (YB-1), which regulates translation and allows for stress granule formation via an independent mechanism of eIF2α phosphorylation. These stress granules promote survival and increase resilience to stress by containing the harboring translation pre-initiation complex [105]. Hence, the function of tiRNAs is not limited to mature tRNAs, but rather incorporates essential regulatory molecules that regulate translation and cellular stress responses, ultimately improving survival in damaging conditions. This demonstrates that tiRNAs are essential regulators of the translational process's stress adaptation [37]. Along with this data, Ivanov and colleagues demonstrated that tRNA halves developed by ANG under stress conditions actively suppress protein synthesis and induce the formation of stress granules (SGs) via a pathway that functions independently of eIF2α phosphorylation [57].

Besides the tRNA halves, tRFs are involved in reducing overall protein production in human cells. For example, in the archaeon Haloferax volcanii, a 26-nucleotide 5′ tRF derived from tRNA-Val is produced in response to stress and directly binds to the small ribosomal subunit near the mRNA channel. This binding interferes with the enzyme's ability to function properly, leading to a broad suppression of protein production throughout the system [106]. Also, the function of tRFs can be to regulate translation as molecular regulators prevent the assembly of polysomes and therefore translate less efficiently [50]. Supporting this notion, certain 5′ tRFs can inhibit reporter gene expression without requiring sequence complementarity to the mRNA, suggesting that they may act by targeting the translational machinery directly [64]. This is reflected in human cells, where the expression of a reporter gene lacking complementary sequences was consistently reduced by a consistent 119-nucleotide 5′ tRF originating from tRNA-Gln. It has been shown that a conserved "GG" dinucleotide within the tRF sequence was necessary for the inhibitory effect to occur [91]. The detection of 5′ tRFs in polysome particles more implies their potential interaction with ribosomes, which may underpin their role in modulating translation. Collectively, these results highlight the different ways in which various types of products produced through tRNA cleavage (such as tRNA halves and tRFs) influence protein synthesis within cellular stress [91].

A limited number of studies suggest that tRFs can also facilitate protein translation. For instance, the 22-nucleotide tRF-3011b derived from tRNA-Leu enhances ribosome biogenesis by base-pairing with mRNAs encoding ribosomal proteins, thereby promoting their translation [107]. Similarly, in Trypanosoma brucei, the 3′ half of tRNA-Thr enhances overall protein synthesis through direct interaction with ribosomes [108]. In general, the data indicate that tsRNAs have the potential to regulate global protein synthesis by targeting the translation machinery, potentially serving as fine-tuned regulators of translational output. However, as we have seen, further research is needed to understand how tRFs interact with the ribosomal structure to regulate the translation process [37].

Functioning as a novel regulator epigenetic factor

Comprehensive studies have revealed that the biological roles of various sncRNAs are generally facilitated by their interactions with specific protein partners. For example, miRNAs, approximately 22 nucleotides in length, and Piwi-interacting RNAs (piRNAs), ranging from 26 to 30 nucleotides, specifically associate with AGO and PIWI protein family members, respectively. These complexes modulate gene expression primarily through epigenetic regulatory mechanisms [109, 110]. High-throughput sequencing of small RNAs bound to AGO or Piwi proteins has identified several types of tsRNAs as associated with these proteins [97, 111, 112]. Some of these tsRNAs have been demonstrated to regulate gene expression via sequence complementarity to target mRNAs, functioning similarly to miRNAs and piRNAs [97, 113]. Although tiRNAs originate in the cytoplasm, new results reveal that tiRNA-5 fragments predominantly localize within the nucleus, where they interact with Piwi proteins to exert epigenetic control over gene transcription [111]. For example, a tiRNA-5 known as tRF-5030c, derived from tRNA-Glu, forms a complex with PIWIL4. This complex recruits epigenetic modifiers including SET domain bifurcated histone lysine methyltransferase 1 (SETDB1), suppressor of variegation 3–9 homolog 1 (SUVH39H1), and heterochromatin protein 1β to the promoter region of the T-cell surface glycoprotein CD1a (CD1A) gene, enhancing H3K9 methylation and thereby repressing CD1A transcription [111]. Moreover, tsRNAs such as ts-53 and ts-101 have been shown to bind to both Ago and Piwi proteins [41]; ts-53 specifically targets three tandem 28-base pair repeats within the 3′UTR of the TCL1 mRNA, suppressing its expression [41]. Furthermore, a Dicer-dependent 3′ tRNA fragment has been shown to associate with AGO proteins and repress the expression of endogenous genes, such as replication protein A1 (RPA1), through binding to complementary sequences within their 3′ UTRs, resembling the regulatory mechanism employed by miRNAs [97].

Transposable elements (TEs) are mobile DNA sequences within the genome that pose potential risks to genomic integrity, and their transcriptional activity is typically repressed by epigenetic mechanisms such as DNA methylation and modifications of histone modifications [114]. Nevertheless, the mechanisms that protect the genome during crucial phases of epigenetic reprogramming-such as pre-implantation embryonic development, when epigenetic marks are extensively erased and then re-established-remain incompletely understood. Recent findings by Andrea et al. [115] demonstrated that 3′-tRNA-derived fragments (3′-tRFs) of different lengths (18 and 22 nucleotides) play roles in suppressing long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons via separate mechanisms: the 18-nt 3′-tRF inhibits reverse transcription, whereas the 22-nt 3′-tRF exerts miRNA-like post-transcriptional repression [115]. In the model organism Arabidopsis thaliana, Dicer-like 1 processes 5′-tRFs, which are then incorporated into Ago1, functioning similarly to miRNAs by targeting transcripts from transposon elements and thereby contributing to genome stability [116]. Notably, tiRNAs represent the major class of small RNAs present in mature mouse sperm [117]. These sperm-delivered tiRNAs influence the transcriptomic landscape of early embryos, including genes involved in metabolic processes [118, 119]. Importantly, these transcriptomic changes are distinct from DNA methylation modifications, which suggests that the tiRNAs of sperm origin are a new type of epigenetic regulator that can impact the characteristics and traits of offspring. Despite the fact that these findings provide valuable information about epigenetic regulation via RNA, the precise in vivo functions of tsRNAs are likely to be complex and require detailed investigation depending on the individual cases [37].

Immune regulation

tRFs are detectable within hematopoietic and lymphoid tissues and are also found circulating in the bloodstream. Importantly, their levels increase markedly during the acute inflammatory response, indicating a potential involvement of tRFs in regulating immune function and modulating gene expression within immune cells [59, 120]. For example, when monocytes turn into dendritic cells, the tRF-5c that comes from tRNA-Glu is controlled by interleukin-4 (IL-4) and connects with Argonaute family proteins called PIWIL4 and PIWIL1 [111]. This complex facilitates the transcriptional repression of the CD1A gene by recruiting histone-modifying enzymes such as SETDB1, SUV39H1, and Hheterochromatin protein 1 beta (HP1β), which mediate histone H3 lysine 9 (H3K9) methylation at the CD1A promoter region in human monocytes. The silencing mechanism appears to be dependent on the sequence complementarity between the tRF and the target locus [111]. In a different study, specific tsRNAs-including those bearing the CCACCA sequence at the 3′ end of tRNA-Ala-UGC-have been demonstrated to directly engage Toll-like receptors, leading to the activation of immune responses in Th1 cells and cytotoxic T lymphocytes [121]. IL-1β caused an increase in the levels of tRF-3003a. The seed sequence of this tRNA fragment was found in the 3’UTR of the JAK3 mRNA, and it formed part of the AGO/RISC complex in chondrocytes. This process led to a decrease in JAK3 expression and a significant reduction in the activity of the 3’UTR reporter. By reducing JAK3 activity, the process could lower the production of several pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IFN-γ, IL-7, 6, 4, 2, 10, and 12, which play key roles in immune responses [44]. The stress-induced post-transcriptional regulation of JAK3 by tRF-3003a in chondrocytes might be a way for the cells to restore balance by blocking pro-inflammatory signaling through the Janus kinase 3 (JAK3)/signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) pathway. This new mechanism of gene regulation by tRFs represents a novel discovery, adding another aspect of post-transcriptional gene regulation in human chondrocytes under disease conditions [44]. This finding could be valuable for developing new therapeutic strategies targeting molecular pathways involved in inflammation [44]. The discoveries imply that tsRNAs serve as new immune signaling molecules, but the precise molecular mechanisms behind their functions require further investigation [37].

Function of tRNA-derived small RNAs in mediating host-virus interactions

Multiple studies have indicated that tsRNAs respond to viral infections, with several implicated in regulating viral replication. However, the precise roles of tRFs in modulating virus-host interactions are still not well understood [55, 122]. Moreover, recent investigations have highlighted the importance of tRFs, a novel sub-group of small ncRNAs made from the cleavage of tRNAs, in regulating viral replication, stress-induced translational repression, the formation of stress granules, and cellular proliferation. The frequent expression of some tRFs among many different organisms indicates their involvement in numerous biological processes [53, 65]. The potential of tRFs as both diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets for the management of viral infections is highlighted. In addition, research indicates that the expression and phenotypic diversity of tRFs are determined by factors such as gender, tissue type, condition, and disease subtype [49, 55, 65]. Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV)

Human respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), a member of the genus Orthopneumovirus of the Pneumoviridae family [127, 128], is one of many global factors contributing to acute lower respiratory tract infections such as bronchiolitis and pneumonia. RSV accounts for approximately 33 million cases globally and is a major contributor to illness, particularly among young children. By the time children are two years old, almost all have caught RSV, which typically causes mild respiratory issues [129, 130]. In addition to its impact on pediatric populations, RSV poses a significant health risk to adults, particularly immunocompromised individuals and elderly patients, with increased incidence and severity noted among those living in long-term care facilities [127, 128]. Beyond direct healthcare costs, RSV's impact on the economy is significant in terms of indirect consequences. Several technological innovations have been pursued to prevent RSV infection, with a particular emphasis on vulnerable groups like infants and older adults. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying RSV pathogenesis is essential for developing new preventive strategies [128].

tRF5-GluCTC

tRFs have recently emerged as a new class of ncRNAs with significant regulatory roles in different diseases, including viral infections as discussed earlier. Deng et al. demonstrated that RSV infection triggers the production of specific tRFs, predominantly originating from the 5′ end of a limited group of tRNA-GluCTC molecules. These tRFs promote RSV replication by partially downregulating the expression of Apolipoprotein E receptor 2 (APOER2), a protein recognized for its antiviral effects against RSV [123].

Current evidence indicates that tRF5-GluCTC is placed exclusively in the cytoplasm. One of its experimentally validated targets is APOER2, a key protein involved in initiating host immune responses. The 3′ region of tRF5-GluCTC enables specific recognition of a binding site within the 3′-UTR of APOER2 mRNA, leading to its silencing. This interaction leads to the weakening of immune responses, which in turn allows for greater replication of the RSV virus [123, 131, 132].

Furthermore, functional studies revealed that silencing tRF5-GluCTC causes a significant decrease in RSV viral particle production, which supports its potential role in viral replication. [24]. On the other hand, infection with RSV significantly increases the production of tRF5-GluCTC, which plays a role in turning off genes by binding to specific mRNA molecules that have matching sequences. Importantly, the gene-silencing mechanism utilized by this tRF differs from the conventional pathways employed by miRNAs or siRNAs. Mutational analyses have shown that the 3′ region of tRF5-GluCTC is essential for its ability to silence genes, unlike miRNAs, which mainly use the 5′ region to identify and bind to their target genes [72, 122].

Recent studies support the notion that tRFs can regulate viral replication through interactions with their associated proteins. Davis and colleagues, using proteomic analyses, identified an association between PABPC1 (poly(A)-binding protein cytoplasmic 1) and tRF5-GluCTC, a tRF induced by RSV infection [124]. Western blot experiments confirmed the presence of PABPC1 in the tRF5-GluCTC complex, and reciprocally, tRF5-GluCTC was detected in complexes precipitated with anti-PABPC1 antibodies. Further investigations revealed that silencing PABPC1 using specific siRNA led to an increase in RSV negative-strand genome copies without affecting viral gene transcription [124]. However, this knockdown produced fewer infectious viral particles, which shows how PABPC1 is critical for the assembly of viruses, in line with its interaction with the RSV matrix protein [124]. Moreover, PABPC1 depletion reduced the secretion of key cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), monocyte chemoattractant protein-1(MCP-1), macrophage inflammatory protein-1α (MIP-1α), and MIP-1β. These findings provide the first evidence that tRFs may influence viral infections by modulating the function of their bound proteins [124].

tRF5-GlnCTG

A study conducted in 2021 found that among the tRFs detected in RSV-infected samples, only one had a relative frequency exceeding ten, which was attributed to cleavage within the anticodon loop and identified as tRF5-GlnCTG, a 31-nucleotide fragment [125]. Choi et al. reported this tRF5-GlnCTG is exclusively placed in the cytoplasm, with no detectable presence in the nucleus, a pattern consistent with other RSV-induced tRF5s [125]. This cytoplasmic localization supports the notion that tRF5-GlnCTG mediates gene silencing via post-transcriptional mechanisms [125]. Moreover, tRF5-GlnCTG has been shown to have the most pronounced effect in enhancing RSV replication compared to other RSV-induced tRF5 fragments [24, 122].

Despite this, Western blot analyses provide additional evidence for the functional importance of tRF5-GlnCTG, as downregulation of RSV protein also implies its upregulation by these tRFs [125, 133]. Investigations into the biogenesis of tRF5-GlnCTG revealed that it is present specifically in the mitochondria-free cytoplasmic fraction, implicating ELAC2, a novel cytosolic ribonuclease, in its induction. ELAC2 has been found to associate with RSV N and NS1 proteins [125, 133]. Notably, while ribonuclease ANG cleaves most tRNAs during RSV infection, tRNA-GlnCTG is exclusively processed by ELAC2. These findings suggest that ELAC2 generates tRF5-GlnCTB through a unique mechanism, which might help find new ways to treat RSV infections [125, 134].

tRF5-GlyCCC and tRF5-LysCTT

Zhou et al. identified the functional specialization of tRF5-GlyCCC and tRF5-LysCTT subsequently uncovered their essential roles in RSV replication. By blocking the function of these two tRFs, the rate at which the virus replicated decreased by one log [24]. Protein analysis revealed that antisense oligonucleotides targeting tRF5-GlyCCC and tRF5-LysCTT prevented the production of viral proteins in infected cells. It is likely that tRF5-GlyCCC and tRF5-LysCTT, both of these regulatory proteins, are expressed at the post-transcriptional level; they were also found to be predominantly in the cytoplasm [24].

High-throughput sequencing of RSV-induced tRFs identified common features among these fragments, including an enrichment of 5’-end-derived sequences rather than 3’-end fragments, with cleavage preferentially occurring at the 5’-side of the anticodon loop. These findings implicate ANG as a potential ribonuclease responsible for processing multiple RSV-induced tRFs [24]. To validate this hypothesis, Zhou et al. assessed the levels of tRF5-GlyCCC and tRF5-LysCTT in RSV-infected A549 cells after silencing ANG using siRNA. Suppression of ANG expression reduced the induction of these tRFs by more than 50%, confirming ANG’s critical role in their biogenesis [24]. Additionally, their results indicated that the generation of these tRFs is independent of Dicer activity. Collectively, the data shows that ANG is specifically necessary for the generation of tRF-5 fragments during RSV infection [24].

Although research on tRF functions remains limited, it has been reported that RSV-induced tRFs derived from the 5’ ends of mature tRNAs encoding GlyCCC, LysCTT, and CysGCA (designated tRF5-GlyCCC, tRF5-LysCTT, and tRF5-CysGCA, respectively) [24] can regulate specific target genes such as spectrin repeat containing, nuclear envelope 2 (SYNE-2), MIP-1β, and interferon-gamma-inducible protein-10 (IP-10), IL-6, IL-8, regulated upon activation, Normal T cell expressed and secreted (RANTES), highlighting their potential role in modulating host responses during viral infection [110, 135].

Hepatitis B and C

Viral hepatitis remains a major threat to global health, with immune status playing a crucial role in disease outcomes. Hepatitis B virus (HBV) and/or hepatitis C virus (HCV) are prevalent among millions of people worldwide, which places them at increased risk for progressive liver conditions such as chronic inflammation, fibrosis, cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, and ultimately liver failure [136, 137]. Although HBV- and HCV-associated liver diseases share several pathological features, these viruses exhibit distinct virological characteristics and employ different mechanisms for immune evasion and persistence. That's why it's really important to fully understand the molecular processes that drive how HBV and HCV cause disease in liver cells. This knowledge can help create more effective therapeutic approaches for controlling infection progression and improving treatment outcomes [136, 137].

3′ and 5′tRF

Preliminary studies have shown that 5′ tRNA-derived stress-induced RNAs (tiRNA-5) derived from tRNA-Gly and tRNA-Val are upregulated during HBV and HCV infections [38]. Selitsky et al. further identified increased levels of specific tRNA fragments tRF-3′ProTGG, tRF-3′ProAGG, tRF-3′GluCTC, and tRF-5′ValACC in patients with chronic viral hepatitis. These results indicate that specific tRNA-derived small noncoding RNAs (tsRNAs) are significantly upregulated during chronic liver infections caused by HBV or HCV; nevertheless, their exact roles in the context of these viral infections have yet to be fully understood [38, 137].

Using high-throughput sequencing and bioinformatic analysis, small RNA profiles (14–40 nucleotides in length) were characterized in liver tissue samples from Japanese individuals with advanced HBV or HCV infection and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [110]. Notably, tRNA-derived small RNAs, especially 30–35 nucleotide-long 5′ tRNA halves, were found to be abundant in non-malignant liver tissue and markedly elevated in both humans and chimpanzees suffering from chronic viral hepatitis [110]. In the majority of infected non-cancerous liver tissues, 5′ tRNA halves were often more abundant than microRNAs. In contrast, corresponding cancerous liver tissues showed a decrease in overall 5′ tRNA halves levels, along with shifts in the relative proportions of distinct 5′ tRNA halves species. In HBV-associated HCC, the levels of 5′ tRNA halves were found to correlate with the expression of angiogenin, a ribonuclease involved in tRNA cleavage [110]. Together, these findings indicate that 5′ tRNA halves constitute the dominant small RNA species in livers chronically infected with HBV or HCV, and their abundance undergoes significant changes during liver cancer development [110].

tRF-U3-1

Cho et al. provided the first evidence that a unique set of tRFs, stabilized through binding of La/SSB protein to their 3′ oligo(U) tract, can inhibit viral gene expression that depends on La/SSB. Genetic and biochemical analyses utilizing tRF-U3-1 and La/SSB knockout cell lines demonstrated that these trailer-derived tRFs accumulate in the cytoplasm through a mechanism that has not yet been elucidated [126]. These tRFs mediate antiviral activity by binding to limited pools of cytoplasmic La/SSB, thereby inhibiting its interaction with cis-acting RNA elements within non-coding regions of viral genomes that are critical for viral gene expression and replication [126]. This finding aligns with previous observations confirming the positive role of La/SSB in facilitating HCV and poliovirus (PV) internal ribosome entry site (IRES)-mediated translation [138, 139].

Moreover, the study demonstrated that tRF-U3-1 and its isomeric variants are capable of inhibiting norovirus replication, suggesting that the antiviral properties of trailer-derived tRFs extend beyond the suppression of IRES-mediated viral gene expression. Instead, these tRFs may target multiple viruses that utilize La/SSB as a proviral host factor. Thus, La/SSB-sequestering tRFs generated from pre-tRNA trailers might constitute a novel molecular network regulating various stages of the viral life cycle [126].

Beyond its role in antiviral defense, La/SSB contributes to tRNA biogenesis by interacting with the 3′ trailer sequence and the 5′ end of precursor tRNAs, thereby promoting correct folding and maturation of these molecules [140]. For example, La/SSB interaction with a specific cis-acting element within HBV RNA increases RNA stability. Furthermore, the interaction between the HIV trans-activation response (TAR) RNA element and La/SSB counteracts the translational inhibition imposed by the TAR 5′ leader sequence, underscoring the diverse functions of La/SSB in the regulation of viral RNA [140].

Dengue virus (DENV)

Positive-sense single-stranded RNA viruses that are categorized as Dengue viruses (DENV) belong to the Flaviviridae family. It has been reported that Aedes mosquitoes are the primary carriers of dengue fever. Around 400 million people worldwide are affected by dengue virus each year, resulting in roughly 22,000 deaths, and the disease is still present in over 100 countries across tropical and subtropical regions [141, 142]. The presentation of dengue infection in clinical settings can range from unresponsive or mild inflammatory conditions to severe cases like hemorrhagic fever (DHF) and dengue shock syndrome (DSS). The presence of thrombocytopenia, leukopenia, and elevated levels of blood clotting are common symptoms of severe cases. Moreover, in most cases, infection is asymptomatic, and the immune system controls it within days but can effectively spread viruses [141, 142]. At present, there is no vaccine or targeted antiviral therapy for dengue, and patient management is primarily concerned with supportive care measures. Like many acute viral pathogens, DENV controls host cellular metabolism to enable efficient viral genome replication and the generation of offspring. Therefore, it is important to know the host-virus interactions that control key stages of the viral life cycle in order to identify new therapeutic targets [141, 142].

3′and 5′tiRNAs, and i-tRFs

A recent study investigated the generation and alteration of tsRNA expression profiles in DENV-infected Huh7 cells using small RNA sequencing. The findings indicated an increase in tRNA fragmentation associated with DENV infection. Notably, the population of 3′tRFs was overexpress, whereas i-tRFs were downregulated. Specific tRNAs affected included tRF-3′ProTGG, tRF-3′ProAGG, tRF-3′GluCTC, and tRF-5′ValACC [110]. A similar study reported that over 80% of 3′tRFs were upregulated while more than 60% of i-tRFs were downregulated during DENV infection. The distribution of differentially expressed tsRNAs was primarily 47% 3′tRFs, 38% i-tRFs, 10% 3′ tRNA halves, 4% 5′ tRNA halves, and 1% 5′ tRFs [143].

Further investigations revealed that the tsRNAs that are expressed at different levels play a role in controlling gene expression by interacting with the promoters of RNA polymerase II and influence various metabolic pathways, indicating their important contribution to the pathogenesis of DENV infection. Significantly, tRF-5′ValACC displayed a 10.5-fold change in expression between 12 and 48 h post-infection [110]. Current evidence shows that DENV infection leads to a major rise in tRNA fragmentation, and this increase happens over time. A notable amount of tsRNA was found even in samples not infected with the virus, indicating that tsRNA are not just involved in stress responses but also have roles in regular body functions. When cells experience viral stress, the production of tsRNA goes up, peaking at 48 h after infection in DENV-infected Huh7 cells [143].

Accumulating evidence indicates that DICER expression decreases following DENV infection, leading to inhibition of the RNA interference (RNAi) pathway [143, 144]. Notably, DICER is also implicated in the biogenesis of certain tsRNAs [15, 145]. Thus, the observed decrease in i-tRF levels might be linked to DICER-mediated cleavage processes, a hypothesis that warrants additional experimental investigation. Additionally, Madhry et al. documented increased Angiogenin expression in the context of DENV infection [146]. Growing evidence indicates that angiogenin cleaves tRNAs at the anticodon loop, resulting in the generation of tRNA halves, also known as tiRNAs [15]. Even so, the proportion of 3′ tRNA halves and 5′ tRNA halves also differs, with 3′ tRNA halves being about 10% and 5′ tRNA halves about 4% of the total number of tsRNAs. The variation may be due to either further cleavage of 5′ tRNA halves, which results in the formation of other tRF subtypes, or through preferential degradation. There are two possible explanations for this phenomenon. This observation requires additional study. Notably, both DICER and angiogenin are known to cleave the T-loop region of tRNAs, playing a role in the formation of 3′ tRFs [26]. Given that DICER levels decline and Angiogenin levels increase during DENV infection, it is plausible that Angiogenin plays a more dominant function in 3′tRF production under these conditions. This is supported by observations showing an upregulation of 3′tRFs in DENV-infected cells [143].

SARS-CoV-2

This virus, COVID-19, quickly caused a pandemic that spread globally. Afterward, several SARS-CoV-2 strains emerged, with those that pose significant health risks being identified by the World Health Organization as Variants of Concern (VOCs) [33]. The manifestation of these relevant VOCs has brought about alterations in vaccine formulation and diagnostic procedures, as well as obstacles to the creation of new therapeutic approaches [147, 148]. The continual emergence of RNA virus prevalence, including SARS-CoV-2, correlates with a potential rise in epidemic occurrence, which highlights the necessary implication of increasing our comprehension of the molecular mechanisms underlying host–pathogen interactions during viral infections [33]. The function of sncRNAs in antiviral defense is not yet thoroughly clarified, but it seems these molecules have the potential to be valuable for therapeutic purposes [33]. Particularly, the interest in potential regulators of viral pathogenesis has been heightened by the recent discovery of transfer RNA-derived RNAs (tsRNAs or tDRs), previously known as tRFs [33].

Glu- and Gly-tRNAs

High-throughput RNA sequencing analysis conducted by Corell-Sierra and colleagues recently demonstrated that 5’ tsRNA-halves represent the predominant species in SARS-CoV-2 patients, while shorter tsRNAs (5’ tRFs) are comparatively less abundant [34]. The researchers categorized these highly enriched tsRNAs based on their biogenesis, as well as according to whether they were derived from either the 5’ or 3’ arms of the tRNA molecules, and further classified them according to length, defining halves as sequences ≥ 30 nucleotides and tRFs as those < 30 nucleotides [34]. Notably, small RNAs originating from the 3’ arm of both Glutamic acid (Glu) and Glycine (Gly) tRNAs were identified as significantly altered during COVID-19 infection. These observations suggest that these tsRNAs could serve as potential biomarkers not only for detecting viral presence through monitoring Glu-derived tsRNAs but also for forecasting how the disease might develop [34]. It has been observed that high expression of Gly-derived tsRNAs was exclusively shown in patients with moderate symptoms [34]. These findings enhance our knowledge of how the host's small non-coding RNAs react to SARS-CoV-2 and hold promise for informing future diagnostic and therapeutic approaches in clinical settings [34].

tDR-Gly, tDR-Val, and 5′-tDRs

Despite significant progress in understanding sncRNAs in viral infections, research on tRFs specifically related to SARS-CoV-2 is still in its infancy and demands further exploration [33]. Hendrickson and colleagues were among the first to profile tDRs, formerly known as tRFs, as potential regulators of SARS-CoV-2 pathogenesis through predictive bioinformatic analyses. Their study employed nucleic acid interaction prediction tools to identify possible binding sites of tDRs within the SARS-CoV-2 genome [33]. Their findings revealed that two specific tDRs, tDR-Gly and tDR-Val, were consistently reduced in both clinical specimens and in vitro cell culture models of SARS-CoV-2 infection, indicating a potential role for these molecules in the host immune response to the virus. This pattern aligns with previously documented downregulation of other host defense mechanisms following SARS-CoV-2 infection [149], including reduced expression of DICER [150], an enzyme is understood to make tDRs [151]. Additionally, particular 5′-tDRs specifically 5008c, 5013c, 5027c, and 5028c were observed to decrease in COVID-19 patients, while levels of 5020a were increased. These observations reinforce earlier reports that 5′-tDRs undergo significant modulation in response to SARS-CoV-2 infection [152].

Importantly, analyses comparing COVID-19 patients older than 60 years to those younger revealed an upregulation of tDR 5013c and a downregulation of 5019a in the older group. Furthermore, when COVID-19 patients over 60 were contrasted with age-matched healthy controls, thirteen 5′ tDRs and two 3′ tDRs showed significant changes in expression [33]. Gender-specific analyses indicated that four tDRs exhibited significant variation: tDRs 3022a and 3022b were lower in uninfected females compared to uninfected males, while tDRs 3005a and 5013c were diminished in males infected with COVID-19 relative to uninfected males [33]. However, no significant changes in tDR expression were observed when directly comparing females and males diagnosed with COVID-19 within this dataset [33].

Functionally, binding sites for these two tDRs were located within the 3′UTR of the SARS-CoV-2 reference genome. Importantly, these sites underwent modifications in the VOCs that appeared throughout the pandemic [33]. For instance, studies have shown that tDR-Val and tDR-Gly display decreased binding affinity to the 3′UTR of the Omicron BA.2 variant. These findings provide evidence of human tDRs interacting with a specific region of the SARS-CoV-2 3′UTR, revealing a novel mechanism through which host small RNAs may regulate viral infection [33].

Human T-lymphotropic virus 1 (HTLV-1)

Humans have been infected with a delta-retrovirus called human T-lymphotropic virus 1, known as HTLV-1, for thousands of years, with the most common types being two closely related ones: HTLV-1 and HTLV-2. The virus can spread from a mother to her child, through sexual activities, or by coming into contact with blood products that are infected [153, 154]. Many people who have HTLV-1 don't show any symptoms, but the virus can lead to serious health problems. These problems are grouped into three main categories: neoplastic diseases such as adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma, inflammatory disorders including HTLV-1-associated myelopathy/tropical spastic paraparesis and uveitis, and opportunistic infections like Strongyloides stercoralis hyperinfection [153, 154]. Although much has been done to explain the fundamental principles of viral biology and host-host interactions. But the therapeutics are still limited and prevention efforts have largely focused on disrupting the viral transmission pathways. Public health efforts are facing challenges due to the extended viral latency period, lack of effective treatments, and limited attention on a global scale. As resulting issues require urgent attention, funding for research improvements, knowledge enhancement, as well as targeted interventions [153, 154].

tRF-3019

Ruggero and colleagues reported that multiple tRFs are abundantly expressed in both uninfected and HTLV-1-infected CD4(+) T-cell lines. Among these, tRF-3019, derived from the 3′ end of tRNA-ProAGG, was identified as one of the most prevalent fragments [22]. Importantly, it is important to note that tRF-3019 perfectly matches the primer binding site of HTLV-1. Supporting this, in vitro reverse transcriptase assays showed that tRF-3019 can work well as a primer for the reverse transcriptase activity of HTLV-1 [22]. In addition, both tRNA-Pro and tRF-3019 were found in virus particles taken from cells infected with HTLV-1. Taken together, these findings indicate that tRF-3019 is important in starting the reverse transcription process of HTLV-1 and could be a useful target for developing treatments against HTLV-1 infection [22].

tRNAs clinical and biotechnological applications

Modulating RNA reverse transcription through guide RNA

The increasing importance of tRNA in biotechnology has been highlighted by recent research. Engineered tRNA and aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase (AARS) pairs have been employed to introduce unnatural amino acids at designated codons, enabling genetic code expansion [155]. This strategy has also been applied to the formation of membraneless organelles [156]. Functionally, tRNAs are primarily responsible for delivering amino acids to the ribosome for their incorporation into elongating polypeptide chains during protein synthesis [100].

Over the last decade, the clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeat (CRISPR) system has become a highly effective tool for genome editing. A critical component of this system is the guide RNA (gRNA), which directs CRISPR-associated nucleases to target specific DNA sequences [157, 158]. Conventionally, gRNAs are synthesized from scaffolds that contain about 350 base pairs, but it has been discovered that a much shorter tRNA sequence of 70 bases is suitable for expressing gRNA. The cell's native tRNA processing system is utilized in this approach, which effectively cuts lone tRNAs at their termini and can produce functional gRNAs [157, 158].

Further investigations suggest that tsRNAs may also function as guide RNAs in viral reverse transcription. For example, the 3′-tRNA fragment (3-tRF or tRF-3019) from host cells interacts with the primer-binding site of HTLV-1 RNA, initiating reverse transcription and facilitating viral replication [37]. Another example is that infection with RSVs can induce the activation of host cell stress response, which in turn stimulates angiogenin-mediated tRNA cleavage to produce tiRNAs. These host-derived tiRNAs are identified as primers by the virus to support more efficient replication. Furthermore, some findings suggest that viral reverse transcription can be initiated using different tsRNA primers, indicating a new level of host-virus interaction [24, 122, 123].

Emerging frontiers in extracellular RNA biomarkers

Extracellular RNA biomarkers, characterized by their non-invasive accessibility and stable presence in bodily fluids, have recently attracted considerable attention in biomedical research. Extracellular vesicles (EVs), recognized as a new mode of intercellular communication, have been shown to carry abundant tRFs and tiRNAs in fluids such as serum and saliva. Differential presence of tRFs in EVs isolated from patients under various pathological conditions provides strong evidence for their selective packaging within exosomes [59, 159, 160]. For example, tRFs released by T cells through extracellular vesicles have been linked to the inhibition of T cell activation [161]. Moreover, plasma exosomes taken from liver cancer patients have more tRFs than those from healthy people, suggesting they could be useful in diagnostic tests for liver cancer that use plasma [162].

Complementing these observations, Wang and their team found six tRNA fragments derived from the 5′ end that were significantly reduced in plasma samples from patients with early-stage breast cancer. Lower levels of these tRFs were linked to worse overall survival and disease-free survival outcomes [163]. Additionally, tsRNAs are selectively packaged into EVs and have been investigated as potential biomarkers for clinical diagnostics [161]. In line with this, Zhu et al. observed remarkably increased yields of specific tsRNAs namely tsRNA-ValTAC-3, tsRNA-GlyTCC-5, tsRNA-ValAAC-5, and tsRNA-GluCTC-5 in plasma exosomes from patients with hepatocellular carcinoma compared to healthy controls, highlighting their potential utility as “liquid biopsy” markers for tumor detection [162].

Functional analyses further revealed that elevated androgen-dependent tiRNAs are strongly linked to unfavorable clinicopathological characteristics and shorter biochemical recurrence-free survival [164]. Importantly, in patients with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer, the levels of the 5′-tRNA-Glu-CUC hemicycle serum were found to be higher compared to those with localized disease, suggesting that these tsRNAs could serve as potential circulating biomarkers and prognostic indicators in prostate cancer [164]. Collectively, these results emphasize the promise of exosome-associated tRFs and tiRNAs as diagnostic and prognostic tools across diverse cancer types [164]. Interestingly, Li and his team found that extracellular tsRNAs show different fragmentation patterns and might be made in a way that's different from cellular tsRNAs. They also discovered that when cells face stress conditions, there are big changes in the levels of extracellular tsRNAs across various cell types. This report suggests that these tsRNA signatures may serve as more reliable indicators of various stress responses compared to miRNAs. New evidence highlights that tsRNAs play a crucial role in how the body responds to stress, which is a key factor in the development of diseases [45].

The field of EVs is still developing, with continuous improvements in technical approaches, such as EV isolation and sequencing methods. Clinical biomarkers such as tiRNAs and RFs are becoming more prevalent and of increasing interest [165]. Additionally, thermostable group II intron reverse transcriptase-sequencing (TGIRT-seq) has been applied to EV sequencing, and found that full-length tRNAs are more prevalent in a given molecule than previously thought [165]. These findings highlight the necessity for novel experimental approaches to deepen our comprehension of RNA classifying mechanisms into EVs for cellular export, as well as the functional contributions of tRFs and tRNAs in intercellular communication [165]. Such insights could pave the way for innovative strategies in controlling viral infections and advancing personalized medicine [165].

Advanced techniques for tRNA detection

Among the current diagnostic methods for tRFs, conventional methods of small RNA analysis, such as Northern blotting and small-RNA sequencing, have been employed to detect tRFs by modified approaches intended to identify transcribed tRNA [100]. However, the development of specific detection methods for personalized tRFs remains an evolving area due to several intrinsic challenges [100]. One major challenge in detecting tRFs is their high sequence similarity, which limits the precision of tRF detection methods. Human tRF-3021a and tRF-3020a, both from tRNA-as well as, differ by only one nucleotide. The use of high-throughput sequencing technologies or highly specific quantitative PCR assays like TaqMan can effectively address this problem [100, 166].

Another complication arises from terminal modifications present on certain tRFs that are incompatible with standard adaptor ligation, which requires RNA molecules to have a 3′ hydroxyl (OH) and 5′ monophosphate. This is especially relevant for tRNA halves, which often bear 3′ 2′-3′ cyclic phosphate (cP) or 5′ OH groups due to RNase-mediated cleavage [167]. Several primary methods have been developed to enrich RNAs with certain cP groups for sequencing, as an answer to this problem. Isolating RNAs with cyclic phosphate termini is the first step in the cP-RNA-saq technique [168]. In the second, the RNA ligase RtcB is involved in changing the 3′ cP to a 3’ monophosphate for adaptor ligation and sequencing. This latter approach has been instrumental in identifying tRNA halves generated by RNase L activity [168]. Given these methodological limitations, it is recommended to validate tRF detection results using complementary techniques for increased accuracy.

Conclusion and future perspectives

Previously, tRNAs were primarily known for their role in protein translation. However, new research has shown that multiple types of sncRNAs, including tsRNAs such as tRFs and tiRNAs, represent a novel class of regulatory molecules. Many biological processes rely on tsRNAs, which have been recognized as vital regulators of different diseases [169].

Various studies have highlighted the main characteristic of tsRNAs make them very promising as useful biomarkers and targets for therapy. Firstly, these tRFs illustrate a high degree of conservation across diverse species, encompassing fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds, rodents, non-human primates, and humans. They are generated from multiple regions of both precursor and mature tRNA molecules and display temporal as well as tissue-specific expression patterns [86, 169, 170]. Secondly, TsRNAs are commonly found in large amounts in the body, present not just in tissue cells but also in various bodily fluids like serum, plasma, bile, urine, seminal plasma, and amniotic fluid. This makes it easier to detect and isolate them [86, 169, 170]. Thirdly, particular but limited tRNA modifications, including 5-methylcytidine (m5C) and N2-methylguanosine (m2G), are essential for promoting the stability of tsRNAs [86, 169, 170]. Our comprehension of tsRNA functions in many human diseases and other physiological and pathological processes has been largely enhanced by current developments in sequencing technologies, bioinformatics tools, and massive databases [86, 169, 170].

In viral infections, tRFs have multiple functions that involve modulating the cellular response to infection and interact with the “RNA interference (RNAi)” pathway for viral replication. Viruses have the ability to manipulate host cellular tRF patterns and thus demonstrate a complex way of avoiding host immunity. These small ncRNA-based mechanisms underscore the complex network of host-virus interactions, highlighting that tRFs are essential in this dynamic interface [32, 71, 131].

Although many areas of tRF biology are still not fully understood, many studies are actively being conducted to explore their potential as diagnostic biomarkers. tRFs are seen as promising options for developing new molecular therapies [32, 71]. Their functional roles are generally dependent on the specific tRF subtype, yet the precise mechanisms by which they operate are not yet fully understood. Various classes of tRFs have been characterized, with their abundance differing across cell types and tissues [32, 71]. Also, ethnicity, gender, and age are among the factors that affect the distribution of tRF classes, which also varies with pathological conditions. Advances in next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies have facilitated the recognized of new tRF classes; however, some subsets may evade detection due to distinct biological or technical barriers [24, 32]. Continued methodological improvements are required to address these challenges, enabling a more comprehensive understanding of tRF subtypes and their functions. Gaining deeper insights into their diverse regulatory roles in biological processes and human diseases will be critical for controlling tRFs as practical biomarkers and personalized therapeutic targets.
