A Temporal FRBR/FRBRoo-Based Model for Component-Level Versioning of Legal Norms
Hudson de Martim

Abstract
Effectively representing legal norms for automated processing is a critical challenge, particularly in tracking the diachronic evolution of their hierarchical components (e.g., articles, paragraphs). While foundational frameworks like FRBR/FRBRoo and standards like Akoma Ntoso model legal documents at a macro level, they lack native mechanisms for granular, component-level versioning. This limitation hinders the deterministic point-in-time reconstruction of legal texts, a fundamental capability for reliable Legal Tech and AI applications. This paper proposes a structured, temporal model that extends the FRBRoo framework to address this gap. It introduces specialized subclasses of Expression—Temporal Version (TV) and Language Version (LV)—to represent the state of a legal norm and its linguistic variations at specific points in time. The model applies this same paradigm hierarchically, introducing Component Work (CW), Component Temporal Version (CTV), and Component Language Version (CLV) to track the lifecycle of individual articles, paragraphs, and clauses. Using the Brazilian Federal Constitution as a case study, the paper demonstrates how each amendment creates new Component Temporal Versions for affected provisions, while unaffected components retain their existing versions. This fine-grained, time-aware architecture enables the precise, deterministic retrieval and reconstruction of any part of a legal text as it existed on a specific date. The model provides a robust foundation for developing advanced legal information systems, knowledge graphs, and AI tools capable of accurate historical analysis and impact assessment, overcoming the limitations of current generative models.



Keywords:
FRBRoo; Temporal Modeling; Legal Knowledge Graph; Legal Text Evolution; Component-Level Versioning; Akoma Ntoso; LexML.

2.1
FRBRoo Overview

The Functional Requirements for Bibliographic Records (FRBRoo) model, originally developed by the International Federation of Library Associations and Institutions (IFLA) for bibliographic cataloging
[
5
]
, provides a conceptual framework for understanding information resources. FRBRoo, an object-oriented version harmonized with the CIDOC Conceptual Reference Model (CIDOC CRM), extends this framework into a formal ontology, enhancing its applicability across diverse domains, including the legal field.

The core of the FRBRoo model lies in its clear distinction between the abstract intellectual or artistic content and its various physical and representational forms. It defines three main entities:

•

a
Work
(FRBRoo F1), representing a distinct abstract intellectual creation. In the legal domain, this corresponds to the normative intent or conceptual scope of a law, independent of any particular textual instantiation.

•

an
Expression
(FRBRoo F2), representing a specific realization of a Work. This includes, for example, the text of a statute in a given language or version. Through CIDOC CRM’s
E52 Time-Span
, FRBRoo can record validity periods for each Expression, enabling temporal annotations.

•

a
Manifestation
(FRBRoo F3 or F4), representing the physical or digital carrier of an Expression. In practice, this may be an official gazette publication, or any other distribution format.

Additionally, an
Item
(FRBRoo F5) represents a single exemplar of a Manifestation, such as a specific printed copy.

This tripartite distinction is particularly powerful in the legal domain. The abstract normative intent of a legal norm (
Work
) is distinct from its specific authoritative textual formulation in a given language (
Expression
), and is again distinct from its various published formats (
Manifestation
). By leveraging CIDOC CRM’s temporal constructs, each
Expression
can be associated with explicit start and end dates, thus capturing its valid time-span. Such temporal annotation is essential for tracking amendments, translations, and the evolution of legal texts over time.

The following subsections discuss how established legal information initiatives, Akoma Ntoso and LexML Brazil, have adopted and operationalized these FRBRoo concepts, including their strategies (or gaps) in temporal modeling.

2.2
Akoma Ntoso Approach

Akoma Ntoso (AKN)
[
6
]
is an XML vocabulary specifically designed for representing parliamentary, legislative, judicial, and soft law documents in a machine-readable format. Its conceptual design is aligned with FRBRoo, providing a structured framework for legal documents at different levels of abstraction:

•

Work
: In AKN, the abstract legal content (the
Work
) is identified through unique identifiers (e.g., the
<FRBRWork>
element). This allows consistent references to the underlying legal concept, independently of text variants or publication forms.

•

Expression
: AKN models the specific authoritative text (the
Expression
) within elements such as
<FRBRExpression>
. It separates text content from metadata, enabling detailed semantic annotations (e.g., through
<paragraph>
,
<article>
, and semantic tag sets). Multilingual support is provided via separate
<FRBRExpression>
entries for each language version.

•

Manifestation
: The physical or digital carrier of an Expression (the
Manifestation
) is represented by elements like
<FRBRManifestation>
, which capture details such as format (XML, HTML, PDF) and publication metadata (e.g.,
<publicationDate>
,
<version>
).

Although AKN provides mechanisms to record publication dates and version identifiers at the document level, it does not natively support fine-grained temporal annotations for individual components (such as articles or paragraphs). In practice, one can include timestamps via custom attributes or extended metadata, but AKN’s core schema lacks built-in constructs to track, for example, when a specific article was amended or replaced. Consequently, while AKN excels at structural and semantic tagging, it requires additional conventions or extensions to capture the component-level, time-specific versions that our proposal will formalize.

This reveals a fundamental distinction in modeling approaches. While Akoma Ntoso, for example,
provides mechanisms to record temporal metadata, it largely leaves the interpretation and
application of complex temporal logic—especially at a granular, component level—to external
processes or systems.

In contrast, our proposed model explicitly reifies these temporal states as distinct ontological
entities (namely, as subclasses of
F22 SelfContainedExpression
). This design choice
makes the temporal evolution and relationships of legal texts and their components explicit

within the ontology itself
, rather than depending on the implicit interpretation of
metadata attributes. This distinction is critical for AI applications. Implicit temporal data
requires complex, often heuristic-based reasoning engines to infer the correct textual
state at a given point in time. By modeling each temporal state as an explicit entity,
however, our approach drastically simplifies deterministic reconstruction, querying, and reasoning.
The strength of our proposal thus lies in its commitment to making these implicit temporal
dynamics explicit and formally structured.

Practical Mapping of Work, Expression, and Manifestation

The FRBRoo model defines an

•

A subclass of
<F22 SelfContainedExpression>

\texttt{TemporalVersion}\;\sqsubseteq\;\texttt{F22 SelfContainedExpression}

•

A subclass of

of the same

\texttt{LanguageVersion}\;\sqsubseteq\;\texttt{F22 SelfContainedExpression}

In FRBRoo notation:

•

Each

is an instance of
<F22 SelfContainedExpression>

•

Each

4
Legal Norm’s Components Representation

Legal norms are not monolithic entities; they possess an internal hierarchical structure. This structure is composed of various
Components
, which are identifiable, addressable subdivisions of the legal text below the level of the norm as a whole. These components serve to organize the normative content and often convey distinct legal propositions. Common examples include titles, chapters, sections, articles, “caput,” paragraphs, items, and clauses (or subitems). Just as the overarching legal norm evolves and can be expressed in multiple languages, so too can its individual components.

The challenge, therefore, is to represent not only the norm in its entirety but also each of its constituent parts in a way that captures their individual semantic content, their linguistic variations, and their temporal evolution. This granular approach is essential for precise legal analysis, accurate information retrieval, and enabling sophisticated AI applications that can reason about specific parts of a law over time.

4.1
Applying the FRBRoo Model to Components

Mirroring the representation of the legal norm as a whole (Section
3
), each individual Component can also be conceptualized through the FRBRoo lens of
Work
,
Expression
, and
Manifestation
. This ensures a uniform modeling paradigm across all levels of a legal text’s hierarchy.

•

Component Work (CW).
Represents the abstract normative content of a specific component (e.g., the legal rule conveyed by an article’s caput, or the scope defined by a chapter’s title). It is the “what” of that textual subdivision, independent of wording or language. Formally, each CW corresponds to a FRBRoo
<F1 Work>
at the component level and persists even when its Expression changes due to amendments.

•

Component Expression.
A specific realization of a
Component Work
. We introduce two specialized subclasses:

–

Component Temporal Version (CTV).
A subclass of FRBRoo
<F22 SelfContained Expression>
that captures the exact textual state of a Component Work at a given point in time. The first CTV corresponds to the component’s original text as enacted. Each amendment affecting that component yields a new CTV, each annotated with a
validFromDate
(and optionally
validToDate
), recording its effective period.

–

Component Language Version (CLV).
A subclass of FRBRoo
<F22 SelfContained Expression>
that represents a monolingual realization of a specific CTV. Each CLV is linked to exactly one CTV (via a property such as
derivesFromComponentTemporalVersion
) and carries a
language
attribute (ISO 639-1 code). For example, the original Portuguese text of CTV
0
is a CLV; if an English translation is later created, that is a separate CLV of the same CTV.

•

Component Manifestation (CM).
A physical or digital embodiment of a CLV. Although uncommon in practice (since components usually appear embedded in a larger document), CM can be modeled as instances of FRBRoo
<F3 ManifestationProductType>
(for mass-distributed formats) or
<F4 ManifestationSingleton>
(for unique artifacts). For example, a standalone PDF file containing only the text of Title I would be a CM of the corresponding CLV.

By treating each component as a mini-
Work
with its own temporal and language Expressions (CTV and CLV), and optional Manifestations, we achieve full granularity. Each CW has a lifecycle: multiple CTVs (each dated), each spawning one or more CLVs (each language-specific), which may in turn be linked to individual CMs if needed. This detailed structure enables precise, component-level tracking of amendments, multilingual versions, and publication formats.

4.2
Temporal Evolution and Multilingualism of Components

The critical insight here is that legal amendments often target specific components within a norm, rather than the entire document. When an amending norm modifies, adds, or revokes a component (e.g., changes the wording of a paragraph, adds a new item to an article, or repeals an entire section), it effectively creates a new
Component Temporal Version
(CTV) for that specific component. The
Component Work
associated with that structural position persists (e.g., the concept of “Article X” remains), but its textual realization changes. Components not affected by the amendment retain their existing CTVs.

This component-level versioning is essential. It allows us to precisely track what changed, when it changed, and what the exact text of any part of a norm was on any given date. Similarly, if a component’s text, as defined by a specific CTV, is translated into another language, this creates a new
Component Language Version
(CLV) linked to that CTV.

Notably, the
consolidates
property, introduced in Section
3.2
to describe the compilation of a norm as a whole, applies with the same logic at this granular level. When an amendment modifies a specific component, the newly created Component Temporal Version (CTV) can be formally modeled as compiling (consolidating) two sources:

1.

Its immediate predecessor CTV (the text before the change).

2.

The CTV 0 (original) of the specific modifying component from the amending legal norm’s (the text that dictates the change).

This ensures a consistent, recursive modeling pattern across all hierarchical levels of the legal text,
reinforcing the model’s structural robustness and analytical power for tracing changes with precision.

5
Conclusion and Future Work

This paper has proposed and detailed a structured, temporal model for representing legal norms and their hierarchical components, grounded in the FRBRoo concepts of
Work
,
Expression
, and
Manifestation
. By extending these principles from the entire norm down to its individual articles, paragraphs, and other subdivisions—introducing the notions of
Temporal Versions
(TV/CTV) and
Language Versions
(LV/CLV) at both levels—our proposal offers several key advantages:

•

Precision in Temporal Tracking:
Enables the exact history of each textual part of a norm to be recorded and retrieved, facilitating precise determination of its wording on any given date.

•

Targeted Impact Analysis:
Facilitates accurate mapping of an amendment’s impact to the specific components it affects, producing new
Component Temporal Versions
(CTVs) only for those components.

•

Enhanced Information Retrieval:
Supports queries targeted at individual components and their historical versions, improving the accuracy and relevance of search results within complex legal corpora.

•

Deterministic Reconstruction:
In direct contrast to the probabilistic and
approximative nature of generative AI, our model enables the unambiguous, bit-by-bit
reconstruction of legal texts for any point in time. This focus on determinism provides
the reliability and verifiability that are primordial for high-stakes legal applications,
establishing a foundation of trust that is unattainable with general-purpose AI models.

•

Foundation for Advanced AI:
Provides a structured, granular, and temporally-aware data model essential for AI systems to deterministically generate point-in-time compiled (unofficial consolidated) texts, compare provisions across versions, and understand the semantic evolution of legal concepts.

The proposed model—illustrated with examples from Brazilian legislation—establishes a robust foundation for next-generation legal information systems and addresses current limitations in AI’s handling of dynamic, component-level legal texts.

Future work will focus on four main areas:

•

Formal Ontological Development.
Create a comprehensive ontology that formally specifies all entities, subclasses, and relationships introduced in this model, aligning with existing standards such as LKIF-Core or LegalDocML.

•

Integration of Event-Centric Modeling.
Our model focuses on representing the versioned textual outcomes of legislative changes. A powerful future enhancement would be to explicitly model the
legislative events
that trigger these version transitions.
Drawing inspiration from event-centric ontologies like the one proposed by Lima et al.

[
4
]
and the core
E5 Event
in CIDOC CRM, one could model an
"Amendment Event" as a distinct entity. This would allow for capturing richer contextual
information about the change itself, such as the cause and type of a change directly to its effect on the textual components.

•

Formal Comparative Analysis.
Conduct a formal analysis comparing the expressivity, computational tractability, and accuracy of inferring temporal states from implicit Akoma Ntoso metadata versus directly leveraging the explicit ontological entities proposed in this model. Such a study would provide strong empirical validation for the design choices presented in this paper.

•

Knowledge Graph Implementation and Tooling.
The proposed FRBRoo model is not
merely conceptual; it is designed to serve as the direct ontological schema for a large-scale, temporally-aware legal knowledge graph. The entities defined (Work, Expression, TV, CTV, etc.) and their relationships (
consolidates
,
derivesFromTemporalVersion
) map directly to the nodes and edges of such a graph. A primary direction for future work is therefore the practical implementation and population of this knowledge graph with historical legislation. This will involve developing automated parsing pipelines, component identification algorithms, and procedures for instantiating and linking the versioned entities, ultimately enabling a rigorous evaluation of the model’s utility for tasks like point-in-time text reconstruction and amendment impact tracing.

•

Advanced Reasoning Capabilities.
Build upon the structured knowledge graph
[
10
]
to develop reasoning mechanisms—such as automated detection of normative conflicts or redundancies introduced by new amendments at the component level, and tracing the semantic drift of specific legal terms or concepts across components over time.

•

Rigorous Benchmarking for Deterministic Accuracy
. A critical future step is to develop evaluation benchmarks to rigorously test the model’s primary success metric: its ability to perform flawless, deterministic point-in-time reconstruction of legal corpora. This will empirically validate the model’s design philosophy, which consciously prioritizes verifiability and accuracy over other potential metrics, reflecting the non-negotiable demand for precision in the legal domain.

•

Integration with Natural Language Processing (NLP).
Explore integration with NLP techniques to enhance semantic understanding and processing of legal text changes.

By advancing these research directions, we aim to enable legal information systems that are more accurate, transparent, and capable of meeting the complex demands of legal practitioners, researchers, and AI-driven applications.

Ultimately, the contribution of this paper extends beyond mere data organization. A robust descriptive
model, such as the one proposed, provides the precise, versioned textual facts—the
ground
truth
—upon which higher-level legal reasoning rules (e.g., modeled in LKIF-Core) can operate.
Without such an accurate and temporally-aware representation, any legal reasoning system would face ambiguity, outdated information, or a lack of verifiable input. Therefore, we frame this model as a
foundational prerequisite
for effective computational
legal reasoning. It provides the essential factual layer for more advanced legal AI systems that
perform automated analysis, compliance checking, and logical inference. This positioning elevates
its importance, making it a critical component of a holistic legal AI architecture and opening clear
pathways for collaborative research between the descriptive modeling and logical reasoning communities
to drive interdisciplinary breakthroughs in the field.
