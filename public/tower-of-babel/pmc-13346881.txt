Use of robust regression methods to detect outliers and estimate parameters for Length-Weight relationships in fishes
Authors: Annalisa Orenti, Francis C. Neat, Anna Zolin, Ettore Marubini, Bruno Mario Cesana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The most commonly used regression method to analyse Length and Weight data of fishes is Ordinary Least Squares (OLS). The OLS method, however, relies on assumptions that data is normally distributed and free from outliers maybe due to measurement or recording errors. Outliers are often encountered in Length-Weight data and can lead to spurious parameter estimates and potentially erroneous analyses. Robust Regression (RR) is a statistical method that can identify and account for (down-weight) outliers. Using Length-Weight data from 2 species of fishes, we demonstrate the application of RR models and their superior performance over OLS both in identifying outliers, accounting for them and estimating equation parameters. A recently developed RR method called Multiple Options (MO) performed especially well, generating a useful plot for inspection of outlier data. The results of this study suggest that the entire problem of outliers in analysing Length-Weight data can be circumvented by using RR methods. We recommend future studies of Length-Weight relationships in fishes use RR methods to estimate model parameters rather than the OLS method.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51617-5.

Introduction

Data on Length and Weight of fish species are often routinely collected as part of fisheries science programs and there are numerous publications that report such data across a wide range of species and ecosystems1–3.

The statistical modelling of the relationship between a fish’s length and weight finds its roots in the work of Julian Huxley, who introduced the concept of allometry to describe how different body measurements scale relative to one another. In this widely adopted framework, the relationship is characterized by a power function. The model suggests that weight is determined by a constant proportionality factor (often referred to as the “condition factor”) multiplied by the length raised to a specific power. This exponent, typically denoted as b, is particularly relevant because it describes the growth pattern of the fish: (i) Isometric Growth: if the exponent is exactly 3, the fish maintains its specific gravity and shape as it grows; and (ii) Allometric Growth: if the exponent differs from 3, the fish becomes more ‘rotund’ or ‘slender’ as it increases in length. To make the parameter estimates more easily using standard statistics for linear models, we turning the curve into a straight line through a logarithmic transformation. Attempts to statistically model the relationship between Length and Weight can be traced by to Huxley4 who proposed an allometric model that has been widely applied5.

The relationship between Length and Weight has been used in a variety of ways in fish biology and fisheries science. For example, the parameter estimates of the Length-Weight relationship can be used to estimate the physical condition of the fish6,7 and the body shape and growth patterns of fish5,8,9. Inferences based on the Length-Weight relationship can be drawn about interspecific variation, life-history patterns and functional relationships with habitat characteristics. Neat & Campbell9, for example, used Length-Weight data to test the idea that the body shape of fishes becomes more elongate as their average depth of occurrence increases.

Sidibeh et al.10 recently assessed the length–weight relationships of four small pelagic species representing two families: E. fimbriata, S. maderensis, S. aurita for the Clupeidae, and T. trecae for the Carangidae from the Gambian waters by using the OLS regression after log-transformation for obtaining a straight line.

Particularly interesting is the paper of Baldé et al.11 in which the length–weight relationships of five small pelagic species (Sardina pilchardus, Ethmalosa fimbriata, Trachurus trecae, Scomber colias and Mugil cephalus) collected in Senegalese waters were assessed according to the novel Length-based Bayesian biomass (LBB) model of Froese et al.12 included in ‘TropFishR’ (Tropical Fisheries Analysis with R) package13.

Within any given species the degree of natural variation in Length and especially Weight can be substantial2. Often there is sexual dimorphism leading to males and females having different Length-Weight relationships. Variation is often associated with maturity stage, for example, pregnant females or females close to spawning are disproportionately heavier. Alternatively, disease and parasitism can influence Weight significantly – this may increase or decrease Weight depending on the specific infection or ailment and whether parasite biomass is removed or not14. Behavioural factors and dominance affect Weight through stress, injury, displacement and long term reduced feeding opportunity2. The Length-Weight relationship may also vary on short time scales associated with feeding - many predatory species infrequently eat very large prey items leading to large, but temporary Weight increase. Such variation may further be related to the season, the geographic area and specific environmental conditions5,15. It is important therefore that studies that utilise Length-Weight data account for such variation to not bias parameter estimates for the species overall. Sometimes this natural variation can be extreme in an individual making it a statistical outlier; while outliers are interesting in their own right, their presence in a dataset can cause problems for attempts to estimate the specific parameters of the Length-Weight relationship for the species.

It is also important to appreciate that the sampling gear and measurement system also impact the size ranges sampled and, consequently, their size-based parameters16–19. For example, the smallest size classes of fish may pass through a sampling net and never be recorded or the largest fish maybe powerful enough to outswim the sampling net. Furthermore, not all species of fish are measured the same way for Length, for example, the most common measure is total Length (nose to tip of tail), but also fork Length (e.g. tuna, herrings), standard Length (e.g. smoothheads), preanal fin Length (e.g. grenadiers) and even presupracaudal fin Length in case of Chimaeridae species are used. The reason for doing so relates to the fragility of the tail section of fish, which in some species is extremely long and thin, and can easily be broken thus making accurate total Length measurement impossible. Again, without accounting for such variation in measurement, spurious estimates of parameters may be generated.

There is always a risk of some degree of human measurement error that can have a significant impact on estimating the parameters of the Length-Weight relationship. Working at sea is challenging and errors in weighing can arise in bad weather due to technical issues, such as weighing scales being unable to tare properly or human observer error due to misunderstandings or miscommunications of information. Sea sickness, fatigue, working in unusually cold or hot conditions can all lead to an increased likelihood of human error and spurious data entry. It is sometimes easy to spot such erroneous outliers and they can be removed from the data set, but this is not always the case, and it can be very difficult to discern if they are errors or true biological outliers. In any case, the result is the same from the point of view of Length-Weight relationships in that they can create problems for estimating parameters. This is especially so in small data sets where a single outlier can have a major effect on the parameter estimation. It is less of a problem of large datasets, but on the other hand for large data sets it can be very time consuming to search for all individual outliers and remove them. While there are statistical methods for detection of outliers, what is most desirable is to apply statistical methods that can both identify outliers and treat them in a way so that they do not unduly influence parameter estimates.

Logarithmic transformation of both W and L makes the model linear in the parameters. This is the standard approach of treating the data and is a prerequisite for the correct use of the most commonly applied means of estimation which is Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression. The OLS or l2-norm method is a standard regression technique and is well described20,21. With few exceptions22, the majority of published studies we have found using Length-Weight data in fish, use the OLS procedure. The OLS parameter estimator of the regression parameters intercept (α) and slope (β) is appropriate, provided that the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\log _{10}}\left( {{{\epsilon}_{\mathrm{i}}}} \right)$$\end{document}are independent random variables with common variance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\sigma}^2}$$\end{document}; in addition, the assumption of being normally distributed is necessary for the validity of the statistical tests. In calculating parameter estimates, the OLS method weights each observation equally.

In addition, it should be noted that for the above linearized model by means of the decimal log-transform of both variables, the slope has not the usual meaning of giving the change of the dependent variable for each unity change of the independent one. Particularly, the unity change is in the decimal logarithm scale leading to calculate a percent increase. So, for calculating the change of W associated to a “p%” increase of L, firstly, one must calculate c = log [(100 + p) / 100], and then: 10c·b. By using the natural or Neperian logarithm the number e ≈ 2.72 has to be used instead of 10.

It is well-known that the OLS procedure is susceptible to the presence of outlier data, i.e. observations lying distant from the centroid of the space spanned by W and L. Outliers in the dependent variable (Y), the independent variable (X) or both can distort the parameter estimates if they are weighted equally (as they are in OLS). Different types of outliers are discussed by Rousseeuw & van Zomeren23. The problem of outliers can be partially remedied by using the Least Absolute Deviation (LAD), quantile or L1-norm method24–27. The LAD is more robust to outliers than OLS in the Y variable; however, it does not protect against outliers in the X variables or “horizontal outliers”28,29.

Robust Regression methods provide a promising alternative to OLS that enable down-weighting of outliers in Y (vertical outliers) or outliers in both Y and X (bad leverage points). Outliers are effectively excluded from the analysis as their weight is set equal to 0. One of the more commonly used robust regression method is known as the “MM” model, a description of which can be found in8,30,31. A more recent robust regression procedure (Multiple Options - MO), proposed by Orenti & Marubini32, is based on two stages combining two robust estimators (a high breakdown point affine equivariant MCD estimator in the first stage and of the properties of an efficient redescending M-estimator in the second stage) and is especially effective at outlier detection and providing estimates of model parameters.

In Orenti and Marubini32, the MO robust procedure has been compared with (1) the MM method from Yohai33, (2) the Least Trimmed Squares (LTS) from Rousseaux34 and (3) the Forward Search (FS) from Atkinson and Riani35 on four example datasets, representative of situations commonly faced by practitioners, demonstrating good agreement with the estimates obtained from the aforementioned robust comparison procedures. It also flagged more observations to be considered potential outliers than the other comparison procedures. Furthermore, this paper32 reports Monte Carlo experiments considering a total of 54 scenarios for simple and multiple linear regression respectively and using as criteria to investigate the performance of the procedures the Average Total Bias (ATB) and Average Total Root Mean Squared Error (ATRMSE) according to Peña and Yohai36. Generally, in simple linear regression, MO procedure shows low ATB even with high perturbation percentage; on the contrary MM estimator shows higher ATB in the presence of higher perturbation percentage. Finally, as regards multiple linear regression, ATB and ATRMSE of MO procedure, MM estimator and LTS estimator are comparable, with the only exception of greater ATRMSE values for the scenario with the highest percentage of perturbed data (40%) and lowest sample size (n = 50). Further details about ATB and ATRMSE are reported in the supplementary material.

The MO procedure is thus appealing to fit allometric models relating Length and Weight data collected in fish where there is a risk of potential outliers unduly biasing parameter estimates. Moreover, the MO procedure gives a useful visualization of the observations plotted according to their final MO weights and to the natural logarithm of their robust distance calculated on the Z matrix (the matrix given by the apposition of the Y and X vectors – only two in this case of a simple regression). The plot is divided into four quadrants by the intersection of a line parallel to the x axis drawn at the MO weight value of 0.5 (< 0.5 is the threshold for considering the observations as outlier) and of a line parallel to the y axis as a threshold for outlier detection. This line is drawn at the natural logarithm of the square root of 95th quantile of a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\chi ^2}$$\end{document} distribution with degrees of freedom equal to the number of the estimated parameters, which correspond to 0.89 when modelling length-weight relation, since there is only one independent variable in addition to the dependent one. Thus, the observations on the upper left quadrant (MO weights ≥0.5 and ln ZRD value ≤0.89) correspond to the bulk and those on the upper right quadrant (MO weights ≥0.5 and ln ZRD value > 0.89) are the “good leverage points”. Observations on the right bottom quadrant (MO weights < 0.5 and ln ZRD value > 0.89) are the “outliers” (bad leverage and vertical outliers). The left bottom quadrant is always empty because all observations with low ln ZRD value have also a high MO weight since they are not outliers. The lnZRD value is obtained as a kind of Mahalanobis distance.

It is important to note that a frequently recommended method for highlighting outliers is to plot the regression residuals versus the fitted values; however, this method cannot distinguish between true outliers (that may be of interest) and errors (that should be removed) and ultimately does not resolve the problem of how to deal with the observations identified as outliers.

Here we report the application of classical OLS, LAD and two robust regression statistical methods (MM and MO) for modelling the Length-Weight relationship in fish. We illustrate the method using two species of fishes chosen because they represent different sources and types of main and outlier data. The performance of each method is evaluated with respect to parameter estimation and detection of outliers.

Materials and methods

Four regression methods are applied in each example for the coefficients estimate and the identification of outliers. For OLS and LAD outliers are defined as observations with internally standardized residuals greater than 1.96. For MM and MO, outliers are defined as observations with final weights less than 0.5, consistent with the proposed cut off used in the MO procedure, following the usual equiprobability criterion of dividing an interval. The threshold of the leverage point is chosen based on the value of the robust distance of the Z matrix (including the X and Y observations leading to consider also “vertical outliers” or outlier in the Y space”) being greater than 0.89 (as previously defined). More restrictive thresholds could be chosen leading to fewer observations defined as outliers. We have also used the default thresholds of the SAS Proc Robustreg37 for defining outliers (a value of the internally standardized residual more than 3) and leverage points (observations with a leverage value more than 2.2414 corresponding to the square root of the 0.975 quantile of a χ2 distribution with the degrees of freedom equal to the number of the independent variables, 1 in our examples). In each example a scatterplot of log length and log weight is drawn, reporting the outliers with the corresponding label number and the remaining points represented as dots. The above reported thresholds are well recognized and accepted in the regression literature.

The R statistical software package was used for all analyses38. The lm function was used for fitting the OLS regression. The LAD regression was fitted by the quantreg package27,39. The MM robust regression was fitted by the MASS package from Venables & Ripley40. The MO robust regression procedure is described in detail by Orenti & Marubini32 and used the robustbase and MASS packages from Maechler at al.41 and Venables & Ripley40, respectively. All R code is reported in the supplementary material. For independent assurance for the identification of outliers and leverage points, we used the SAS Proc Robustreg37 with the MM method.

Ethical statement

This paper presents results of a statistical analysis of historic data collected by a third party (Marine Scotland). No live animals were used at any stage.

Results

Data for Weight (g) and Length (cm) were log-transformed for two fish species taken from a larger set of data collected during trawl surveys of the North Atlantic by Marine Scotland Science published some years ago by Neat & Campbell9. The two fish species reflect different body forms, life histories and Length measurement protocols. The first, the monk-fish (or angler fish), Lophius piscatorius has a large head and stout body form and shows sexual size dimorphism (females being much larger). It is simply measured as total Length and Weight. The second species, a deep-sea chimaera, Hydrolagus affinis (de Brito Capello42 is elongated with a very thin long tail and is thus measured as pre-supra caudal fin Length. It is sexually dimorphic, but not obviously with respect to size.

In this case the OLS method performs reasonably because there are no extreme outliers (Table 1). The OLS regression line shows a slightly greater intercept and lower slope resulting a little clockwise rotated in comparison to the other regression lines. The LAD estimates are in good agreement with the MM and MO ones; however, it should be noted that the LAD regression shows the highest standard error of the coefficients leading to low precision of the estimates. The estimates obtained with the two robust procedures (MM and MO) are practically identical both regarding the coefficients and their standard error. Some OLS outliers are equal to those shown by the remaining procedures, but MO procedure shows a little more conservative approach by identifying, and consequently, down-weighting, one more observation. Furthermore, the coefficients of determination of the three regression procedures are very similar. It has to be noted that the observations marked as 92, 28, 35 are considered outliers since they are far away (on the left and on the right, respectively) from the bulk of the observations placed on the right top part of the regression lines; however, these observations have little influence on the OLS regression estimates since they practically lie on the regression line. In addition, the observations marked as 107 and 161 (Fig. 1, Panel A) are placed on the right of the bulk and, consequently, are considered outliers; these observations potentially influence the estimates by determining a clockwise rotation of the regression line with an increase of the intercept and a reduction of the slope.

The column headed R2 report the usual coefficient of determination for OLS regression that in the case of a simple regression corresponds to the correlation coefficient squared and measures the proportion of variance in a dependent variable that is predictable from independent variable(s) in a regression model. Ranging from 0 to 1, it indicates how well the model fits the data since if all the dependent variable is predicted by the independent variable, the regression relationship is perfect. It represents the percentage of the variation in the dependent variable explained by the independent variable(s). Otherwise, for MO and MM, it is replaced by a “robust version” based on median absolute deviation or the Median Squared Residual, and robust deviance, which use weighted residuals to minimize the impact of extreme data points. Particularly, Proc Robustreg37 does not give a robust R2 directly in the output, but a robust R2 can be computed based on the “weighted residuals”, indicating the proportion of variability explained by the model after down-weighting the outliers. It is given by the complement to 1 of the ratio between the Robust Residual Sum of Squares (WRSS) and the Total Sum of Squares (TTS). The WRSS is obtained by the final weights (wi) generated by the iterated re-weighted least squares (IWLS) process.

Figure 1 (Panel B) refers to the final MO step and illustrates its powerful ability to detect influential outliers. It is evident that 12 observations (∼ 6%) have been considered as outliers and about 20 (∼ 10%) have been considered as “good leverage” points giving to the researcher a clear picture of the observation most influencing the OLS regression estimates.

Discussion

The main objective of this research was to demonstrate the statistical shortcomings of applying classic regression models to analyses of Length and Weight data of fishes and inferences about growth and form and how these can be avoided through the application of Robust Regression approaches. There are many studies in the literature that have relied on estimates generated from the OLS regression method, but as the results of this study demonstrate, this can be problematic if outliers are present as often occurs. Outlier data arise from mistakes in measurement and data entry or particular biological situations, e.g. disease, parasites, pregnancy2.

In the case species presented there were several examples of outliers. In the case of Lophius piscatorius, the outliers were less obvious and could have reflected a recent large meal or females close to spawning that develop a large gelatinous egg mass. In the case of Hydrolagus affinis, there were two extreme outliers that could possibly reflect an error in measurement as this species has a particular Length measurement protocol or a simple mistake owing to a typo or transcription error.

Whatever their cause, the presence of outliers can violate model assumptions in traditional regression models, such as the OLS model, and lead to spurious results and estimates of parameters. They can be detected and removed, but this may be undesirable if they are true biological outliers and identification can be time consuming in large data sets. However, it must be stressed that removing outlier from a dataset is always a decision that must be carefully considered by the researcher. The results of this study suggest that the entire problem of outliers can be circumvented by using robust regression procedures that provide more valid biological model estimates. The LAD regression performed better than the OLS one. However, despite attempts to introduce it into the ecological sphere from Cade & Noon43, it is not recommended because it does not allow a comparable assessment of the goodness of fit, and the standard error of the estimates are much greater.

Robust regression methods including the MM and MO procedures show much promise in clearly identifying outliers and accounting for them by down-weighting to remove their influence. Generally, the MO procedure identified a few more potential outliers than the MM procedure and can therefore be considered somewhat more conservative and less prone to biased estimates than the MM procedure. The MO method generates a figure that gives a detailed picture of how the observations contribute to the bulk of the data and the set of outlying data. The MO method allows researchers to play a more active role in dealing with the potential outliers and deciding on whether they represent data entry errors or true biological phenomena32. Taking the approach of simply deleting the outliers has the risk of failing to identify truly interesting biological data and should not be carried out without due consideration. The identification and elimination of the outliers according to a non-statistical criterion as has been done in some cases, e.g. Vereeycken et al.1 is not to be recommended. Further the MO procedure plot is also useful for determining whether sexual dimorphism in a species has a significant effect on parameter estimates. Both species under consideration exhibit a certain degree of sexual dimorphism, which, if observed in body shape, could result in a bimodal distribution. In such cases, if information on sex is available, unlike our case, males and females should be analysed separately from the outset or at least sex should be included as covariate in the model.

Finally, from the examples shown we recommend carrying out both robust procedures to be as confident as possible with the results and the fitted model. The MO procedure together with its final figure, can be easily carried out by a code written in the open-source R language. The use of these robust regression methods has two distinct advantages over classical regression methods, firstly through a better power to detect outliers, and second through a specific stepwise means of statistically accounting for them so that they do not have an impact on parameter estimation. The extent to which this has been a problem in previous studies of Length-Weight relationships remains to be evaluated and should be the focus of future research. The MO procedure can also be used at evaluate sexual dimorphism in the species and, if necessary, analyse sexes separately. We recommend for future studies the robust regression methods as used in preference to the classical regression methods, especially the OLS.

Conclusions

Assessing Length-Weight relationships in the context of real-world research, like assessing relationships between two variables in real-world research, is an activity that involves dealing with data that may raise some concerns compared to the bulk of available observations. These data cannot simply be classified as outliers and then removed from the dataset. However, the presence of these data may result in estimates that are far from those that the actual underlying model would have produced. In this regard, robust regression procedures allow for greater confidence in having obtained a model close to the “true model”. The MO procedure is a robust method that ranks at least as high as the widely recognized and used robust method with the advantage of having a graphical tool that allows the researcher to have a view of where the observations are located (bulk, leverage and “outliers”).
