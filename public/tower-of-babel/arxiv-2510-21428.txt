Exciton and biexciton preparation via coherent swing-up excitation in a GaAs quantum dot embedded in micropillar cavity
Claudia Piccinini, Aleksander Rodek, Abdulmalik A. Madigawa, Ailton Garcia Jr, Saimon F. Covre da Silva, Vikas Remesh, Luca Vannucci, Gregor Weihs, Armando Rastelli, Niels Gregersen, Battulga Munkhbat

Abstract
Coherent control of quantum emitters is essential for scalable quantum photonic technologies. The recently proposed swing-up of quantum emitter (SUPER) scheme enables the coherent preparation of excitons via off-resonant, red-detuned laser pulses, offering highly efficient single-photon generation. We present a systematic study of SUPER excitation applied to a single GaAs quantum dot in a low-Q micropillar cavity. After identifying optimal excitation conditions, we benchmark the SUPER scheme against above-band, p-shell, and two-photon biexciton excitation (TPE). Despite requiring higher excitation powers, SUPER achieves near-unity population inversion ( ∼ \sim 95%) and high single-photon purity ( g ( 2 ) = 0.03 g^{(2)}=0.03 ), comparable to TPE, while also yielding a shortened exciton decay time ( ∼ \sim 200 ps), indicative of direct, efficient exciton preparation. A polarization-resolved analysis reveals that, when both excitation and collection are aligned with the exciton dipole, SUPER results in a highly polarized single-photon emission exceeding resonant TPE saturation by a factor of 1.45. Under optimal excitation conditions, we also observe biexciton preparation via a distinct SUPER resonance, confirmed by unpolarized exciton emission, extended lifetime due to the biexciton–exciton cascade, and biexciton emission, constituting the first experimental demonstrations of biexciton preparation under SUPER. These findings are in good agreement with a proposed four-level theoretical model including the biexciton. We also report that, strikingly, a slight misalignment of laser polarization induces an additional SUPER resonance that selectively populates the orthogonal exciton state without altering the nominal excitation polarization. These findings establish the SUPER scheme as a versatile tool for state-selective exciton and biexciton control, with strong potential for quantum photonic applications.



I
Introduction

Highly efficient sources of pure and indistinguishable single photons are fundamental building blocks of photonic quantum information technologies
Heindel et al. 2023
. Among solid-state platforms, semiconductor quantum dots (QDs) are leading candidates for realizing such quantum light sources. However, the performance of QD-based single-photon sources is critically influenced by the choice of excitation scheme.
Resonant excitation of the neutral exciton state is the established standard for generating highly indistinguishable, on-demand single photons
Somaschi et al. 2016
;
Ding et al. 2016
;
Nawrath et al. 2019
. Despite its advantages, this technique requires complex polarization filtering or engineered photonic structures to suppress scattered laser light, which can limit photon extraction efficiency and scalability
Ates et al. 2009
;
Wang et al. 2019
;
Uppu et al. 2020
.
To overcome these limitations, several off-resonant excitation protocols have been developed, including phonon-assisted excitation
Thomas et al. 2021
, two-photon excitation (TPE)
Müller et al. 2014
;
Schweickert et al. 2018
;
Wei et al. 2022
, bichromatic excitation
He et al. 2019
;
Koong et al. 2021
;
Yan et al. 2025
, and, more recently, the swing-up resonant excitation (SUPER) scheme
Bracht et al. 2021
;
Vannucci and Gregersen 2023
;
Karli et al. 2022
. These approaches enable coherent and efficient state preparation while reducing the need for stringent spectral filtering.

The SUPER scheme, in particular, employs two red-detuned laser pulses to coherently “swing up” the QD from the ground state to an excited exciton state. It has shown great potential for direct exciton preparation on various QD platforms
Karli et al. 2022
;
Nawrath et al. 2019
.
However, while SUPER has shown promise for exciton (X) preparation, a systematic experimental evaluation of its performance, particularly in comparison to other established excitation protocols, remains lacking. Important factors such as laser polarization alignment, spectral detuning, and the dynamics of exciton state preparation remain to be fully explored. More importantly, the role of higher-order states, especially the biexciton (XX), has not been experimentally investigated in the context of SUPER. This leaves open critical questions regarding their role in exciton preparation dynamics and the potential of SUPER for generating entangled photon pairs via the XX–X cascade.

In this work, we present a comprehensive experimental investigation of the SUPER scheme applied to a single GaAs quantum dot embedded in a low-Q micropillar cavity. We first benchmark SUPER against widely used excitation methods—including above-band, p-shell, and two-photon biexciton excitation—highlighting its advantages in terms of exciton preparation efficiency, single-photon purity, and temporal emission characteristics. Through polarization-resolved and time-resolved photoluminescence measurements, we identify optimal conditions for state-selective excitation, revealing that SUPER can coherently prepare either exciton dipole eigenstate with high polarization contrast and photon yield.
Crucially, we demonstrate the direct involvement of the biexciton population via SUPER, confirmed by unpolarized exciton emission, biexciton PL detection, and extended exciton lifetimes consistent with a cascade process. Finally, we report the emergence of an additional SUPER resonant conditions that selectively populates the orthogonal exciton dipole without changing the excitation polarization, suggesting complex light–matter interactions beyond the standard three-level model and pointing to new directions for coherent quantum state control.

Müller et al. 2014
;
Schweickert et al. 2018
;
Wei et al. 2022
, and swing-up excitation (SUPER)
Bracht et al. 2021
;
Vannucci and Gregersen 2023

II.1
Initial characterization and excitation schemes comparison

The photoluminescence (PL) spectrum of the QD under study obtained via continuous-wave above bandgap excitation(
\lambda=650
nm) is shown in Fig.
1
c. The dominant emission at
\lambda
= 783.83 nm corresponds to the neutral exciton (X), followed by exciton trion (T) at
\lambda
= 784.76 nm. A weaker emission line at
\lambda
= 785.38 nm is attributed to the biexciton (XX), with a corresponding binding energy of
\sim
4.0 meV. A dense ensemble of additional emission lines at longer wavelengths is attributed to multiexciton states, while weaker features at higher energies are consistent with p- and d-shell transitions
Reindl et al. 2019
. In this work, we focus on the neutral and biexciton state.

We begin the comparison of excitation schemes by investigating the decay dynamics of the neutral exciton using time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) measurements under pulsed excitation, as shown in Fig.
1
d. In all cases, the excitation pulse duration is significantly shorter than the exciton lifetime, ensuring negligible re-excitation. The temporal decay of the neutral exciton is recorded and fitted using a single-exponential function convoluted with the instrument response function (IRF). The extracted decay times reveal a clear trend across the excitation methods. Under AB excitation, the exciton decay time is approximately
\tau
= 300 ps. This value decreases to
\tau
= 200 ps under SUPER excitation. The longer decay times observed for AB and PS excitation are attributed to relaxation processes from higher-energy states
Muñoz-Matutano et al. 2008
;
Reindl et al. 2019
;
von Helversen et al. 2022
. TPE excitation also results in a delayed decay due to the biexciton-to-exciton cascade dynamics.
In contrast, the monoexponential decay observed under SUPER excitation indicates direct population of the exciton state, without intermediate relaxation bottlenecks. This observation aligns with previously reported lifetimes under resonant excitation schemes
Boos et al. 2024
, confirming the coherence and efficiency of the SUPER protocol.

To evaluate the single-photon purity under each excitation scheme, we measure the second-order autocorrelation function
g^{(2)}(\tau)
using a standard Hanbury Brown-Twiss (HBT) setup. The results are summarized in Fig.
1
e, where coincidence counts are normalized to the average values at the longest measured delay times (
\tau=\pm 2.5~\mu s
or
\pm 10~\mu s
) to account for any bunching effects in the investigated timescale (see Supplementary Fig. S3). Under AB excitation, we obtained a reduced single-photon purity of
g^{(2)}(0)
= 0.37
\pm
0.02 and a pronounced bunching feature near zero delay, with a decay time of approximately 145 ps. This behavior is attributed to re-excitation events caused by carrier recapture, a known limitation of non-resonant excitation schemes
Davanço et al. 2014
;
Laferrière et al. 2022
. In contrast, the PS, TPE, and SUPER schemes exhibit significantly higher and excellent single-photon purities as high as 97%.

Among the resonant and quasi-resonant methods, the SUPER scheme yields a high single-photon purity, with a measured
g^{(2)}(0)=0.03\pm 0.01
, comparable to the values obtained under TPE and PS excitation. Interestingly, the autocorrelation histogram for SUPER also reveals pronounced bunching on long timescales, with a characteristic decay of approximately 3.3
\mu
s. This behavior is likely caused by slow charge fluctuations in the local environment, triggered by the high excitation power densities required for SUPER. While similar effects have been reported in other excitation schemes
Wei et al. 2022
;
Karli et al. 2022
;
Joos et al. 2024
, they appear more pronounced here. Additional second-order correlation measurements under varying SUPER powers (Supplementary Fig. S4) show a clear trend of increasing bunching with higher excitation power, supporting this interpretation.
These charge noise dynamics may also contribute to the reduced photon indistinguishability observed for SUPER compared to strictly resonant excitation
Boos et al. 2024
. This limitation could potentially be mitigated by implementing integrated electrical contacts to stabilize the charge environment. Moreover, the SUPER scheme is inherently more complex, with its resonant condition sensitively dependent on multiple parameters—including detuning, power, and polarization. Further optimization and systematic control of these parameters may lead to improved emission characteristics and better overall performance of the SUPER protocol.

Next, we proceed with power-dependent photoluminescence (PL) measurements to compare the saturation behavior across all excitation schemes. Unlike the previously discussed lifetime and purity metrics, the absolute brightness of the exciton transition is highly sensitive to the local charge environment of the quantum dot (QD). As demonstrated by Madigawa et al.
Madigawa et al. 2025
, for the same sample, the addition of blue LED illumination during p-shell excitation significantly increases the photon count rate and improves Hong–Ou–Mandel (HOM) interference visibility - without affecting the exciton lifetime or degrading single-photon purity. This enhancement arises from optical charge stabilization, a well-known effect previously reported in similar systems
Gazzano et al. 2018
;
Leandro et al. 2020
;
Wei et al. 2022
;
von Helversen et al. 2022
.
Here, we examine the influence of LED illumination on the power-dependent saturation curves for all four excitation schemes. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S5, this auxiliary illumination enhances brightness across all schemes, but its effect is most pronounced for PS and TPE excitation, which are inherently sensitive to the average charge state of the QD. Specifically, the LED leads to a
\sim
50
\%
increase in the saturation count rate for PS and a comparable enhancement at the
\pi
-pulse maximum for TPE. Interestingly, the SUPER scheme exhibits a remarkable robustness to the presence of LED illumination. This behavior may be partially attributed to the broader resonant conditions of the SUPER protocol, but further investigation, ideally in a deterministically tunable charge environment such as a p-i-n structure, is required to clarify this effect.
For consistency, all saturation data presented in Fig.
1
f were acquired under optimal conditions, i.e., with additional LED illumination and identical experimental configurations, including cross-polarization filtering for a fair comparison.
The integrated PL signal of the X transition exhibits typical saturation behavior under AB and p-shell excitation, with the latter showing a
\sim
60
\%
improvement in count rate. This increase is expected, as AB excitation is known to induce substantial charge noise and promote nonradiative recombination pathways, thereby reducing the effective brightness. These detrimental effects are also reflected in the pronounced bunching observed in the second-order autocorrelation function
g^{(2)}(\tau)
, indicative of short-timescale blinking dynamics (see Supplementary Fig. S3).

In the case of two-photon excitation, the PL signal exhibits Rabi oscillations, which is characteristic of its coherent nature, with peak count rates matching those achieved under PS excitation at saturation.
For the SUPER scheme, optimized excitation conditions (
\Delta_{1}=-3.8
meV,
\Delta_{2}=-9
meV, with a power ratio
P_{2}/P_{1}\approx 1.3
) yield a clear saturation of the exciton PL signal (see also Fig.
2
b), reaching brightness levels comparable to those achieved with
(n+1)\pi
-pulses in TPE. This result aligns with previous demonstrations of near-unity population inversion of the exciton state through the SUPER method
Karli et al. 2022
.

Crucially, however, the SUPER protocol does not require stringent spectral or polarization filtering—an inherent limitation in conventional resonant excitation schemes. In standard resonant excitation, cross-polarization filtering is typically used to suppress laser scattering, which inevitably restricts the detected emission to
\sim
50
\%
of the available polarization modes. Two-photon excitation (TPE) avoids this limitation by exciting the biexciton state with a linearly polarized pulse and detecting the emitted photon cascade. However, due to the spin selection rules of the XX–X cascade, the resulting emission is equally distributed between orthogonal polarizations, limiting the degree of polarization control. In contrast, the SUPER scheme employs two red-detuned laser pulses polarized along the exciton dipole axis, allowing full excitation and detection of a single polarization component, thereby enabling
\sim
100
\%
polarized emission without polarization filtering.

To fully explore this unique capability, we now perform a systematic investigation of the parameters that govern emission brightness under SUPER excitation, with a particular focus on the role of excitation pulse polarization.

II.2
Polarization-dependence and anisotropy - TPE and SUPER comparison

Having established the viability of the SUPER scheme for high-purity single-photon generation, we now turn our attention to one of its key degrees of freedom: polarization selectivity.
In Fig.
2
a, we present polarization anisotropy measurements of the neutral exciton (X) photoluminescence (PL) under both TPE and SUPER excitation. The polarization of the excitation laser was oriented either along the vertical axis, coinciding with the orientation of one of the X transition dipoles, or at 45
∘
with respect to it. As expected, TPE exhibits no polarization dependence, as the exciton emission results from a biexciton–exciton cascade that populates the H and V states with equal probability. In contrast, under SUPER excitation, when the laser polarization is aligned with the V-dipole of the exciton, we observe a strong polarization contrast in the PL, confirming selective excitation of a single dipole. At
\phi=45^{\circ}
, however, the PL becomes unpolarized, similar to the TPE, due to the simultaneous excitation of both H and V states.

We now investigate whether efficient population inversion can still be achieved when selectively driving a single exciton dipole. To this end, we align both excitation and detection polarizations along the vertical axis (
\phi\approx 0^{\circ}
,
\theta=0^{\circ}
) and perform power-dependent PL measurements (Fig.
2
b). To optimize the emission under this orientation, we fine-tune the second pulse detuning to
\Delta_{2}=-7.4
meV. In the co-polarized configuration, SUPER excitation yields a higher count rate at saturation compared to both TPE and the previous configuration of SUPER with
\phi\approx 45^{\circ}
and
\theta=135^{\circ}
(Fig.
1
f). However, it does not produce the expected doubling of the count rate, instead reaching an enhancement factor of approximately
\sim
1.45.
Surprisingly, in the orthogonal detection configuration (
\phi\approx 0^{\circ}
,
\theta=90^{\circ}
), we still observe substantial H-polarized emission, despite the excitation being purely V-polarized. Polarization-resolved PL measurements performed across a full range of excitation and detection angles further confirm this behavior (Fig. S6). While the strongest polarization contrast is observed when the excitation is aligned along the H or V dipoles, residual emission in the orthogonal polarization persists across all
\phi
angles.
This counter-intuitive behavior could arise from several sources: (i) residual ellipticity of the excitation laser, leading to unintended excitation of both dipoles; (ii) polarization mixing caused by finite fine-structure splitting (FSS); or (iii) more complex mechanisms involving higher-order states, such as biexciton (XX), that may lead to indirect population of the orthogonal dipole.
To rule out the first scenario, we quantified the ellipticity of the excitation laser by measuring its back-reflected signal from the planar region next to the micropillar. We confirm a high polarization extinction ratio approaching
10^{2}
(Fig. S7), strongly suppressing any significant excitation of the orthogonal dipole.

To examine the second hypothesis, we modelled the system considering the fine structure splitting and the exciton linewidth of the QD. As shown in Fig. S6, while the model qualitatively reproduces the experimental anisotropy maps, it predicts no orthogonal emission for any value of FSS when the excitation is aligned with one of the exciton dipoles. This suggests that polarization mixing from FSS alone cannot account for the observed orthogonal emission.

This leads to the physical involvement of higher-order states in the excitation process. To investigate this in more detail, we performed two-dimensional scans of the exciton PL intensity as a function of total excitation power and the detuning of the second pulse
\Delta_{2}
, with laser polarization oriented along the V-dipole and detection in either co- or cross-polarized configurations (Figs.
2
c–d). In the co-polarized case (Fig.
2
c), we observe multiple resonant fringes, with the strongest emission appearing at
\Delta_{2}\approx-7.4
meV. To assess whether these features can be captured by a simplified three-level model, including only the ground state and the two exciton states (H and V), we calculated the exciton population in the absence of any higher-order states (Supplementary Fig. S8a). However, the resulting simulations fail to reproduce the experimental PL behavior, indicating that the three-level model is insufficient to capture the dynamics of the system. This discrepancy suggests that additional states must be involved in the excitation and emission processes.

On the other hand, the key features observed in the experiment—including both the overall intensity and the resonance fringes—are qualitatively well reproduced by the simulation shown in Fig.
2
e. These were performed within a four-level-model that includes the ground state
\ket{G}
, two orthogonal exciton states
\ket{V}
and
\ket{H}
with linear polarization, and the biexciton state
\ket{XX}
. Their respective energies are
E_{G}=0
,
E_{V}=\hbar\quantity(\omega_0 - \frac 1 2 \Delta_{\rm FSS})
,
E_{H}=\hbar\quantity(\omega_0 + \frac 1 2 \Delta_{\rm FSS})
, and
E_{XX}=\quantity(2 \hbar\omega_0 - E_b)
, where
\hbar\Delta_{\rm FSS}
is the fine structure splitting (FSS), and
E_{b}
is the biexciton binding energy. (more details available in
Theory
section).

In the cross-polarized detection configuration (Fig.
2
d), where no emission is expected under ideal conditions, we nevertheless observed pronounced emission fringes across the detunings. Crucially, this orthogonal emission cannot be explained by a simplified three-level model consisting of only the ground and H/V exciton states. Our calculations based on this model confirm the absence of population transfer to the orthogonal dipole under such excitation conditions (see Supplementary Fig. S8b). In contrast, the four-level model that incorporates the biexciton (XX) state successfully reproduces the experimentally observed features, as shown in Fig.
2
f. This strongly indicates that biexciton population plays a critical role in enabling emission from the orthogonal exciton dipole.

Moreover, the calculated occupations in Supplementary Fig. S9 reveal that the SUPER scheme coherently drives both the exciton and biexciton states, with non-negligible average occupation of the biexciton
Bracht et al. 2023
;
Heinisch et al. 2024
. This coherent interplay gives rise to the observed polarization mixing in the PL signal, which is highly sensitive to the excitation conditions.
Importantly, these findings suggest a promising new route for coherent biexciton preparation using the SUPER protocol, representing, to the best of our knowledge, one of the first experimental demonstrations of this mechanism. In the next section, we provide direct experimental verification of this interpretation and explore the excitation conditions enabling efficient and selective exciton preparation via biexciton population under the SUPER scheme.

To experimentally verify and better understand the role of biexciton-mediated neutral exciton emission under SUPER excitation, we now investigate the polarization properties of the X PL signal as a function of the detuning of the second pulse. Figure
3
a shows the measured polarization anisotropy, obtained by scanning the detection polarization angle from 0° to 180°, while keeping the excitation laser fixed along the V-direction (0
∘
). A clear difference emerges at two distinct detunings. At
\Delta_{2}=-6.9
meV (F
1

\Delta_{2}=-7.5
meV (F

), the PL becomes unpolarized, resembling the behavior of biexciton–exciton cascade emission, as typically observed under TPE excitation.
This suggests that the two resonance fringes observed in Fig.
3

2

2
c,e), causing the SUPER process to switch between predominantly driving the exciton or the biexciton, depending on the fixed values of
\Delta_{1,2}
.

Further evidence linking the resonance at
\Delta_{2}=-7.5
meV to the SUPER-mediated preparation of the biexciton is provided by the temporal dynamics of the neutral exciton emission. The upper panel of Fig.
3
b shows time-resolved PL traces for the two detunings corresponding to the resonances identified in Fig.
3
a. At
\Delta_{2}=-7.5
meV, where the emission is unpolarized, the PL decay exhibits a pronounced rise time and delay, consistent with biexciton-mediated population transfer, similar to what is observed under TPE excitation (Fig.
1

\Delta_{2}=-6.9
meV shows fast decay, characteristic of direct exciton excitation. Furthermore, in the lower panel of Fig.
3

\Delta_{2}=-6.9
meV but not for
\Delta_{2}=-7.5
meV , ruling out coherent superposition of the H and V exciton states for the latter condition and further supporting the interpretation of a biexciton-driven cascade.

To provide direct evidence of biexciton involvement under SUPER excitation, we aimed to directly detect photoluminescence from the biexciton (XX) state. To enable this, we adjusted the detuning of the first pulse from
\Delta_{1}=-3.6
meV to
\Delta_{1}=-2.8
meV, thereby avoiding spectral overlap between the excitation laser and the XX emission line, while still not within the range of phonon-assisted TPE. The upper panel of Fig.
3

\Delta_{2}=-6.1
meV, both exciton (X) and biexciton (XX) emission are clearly observed. In contrast, at
\Delta_{2}=-7.5
meV, no PL from either state is detected, confirming that both excitation pulses are required for the process, ruling out resonant or phonon-assisted TPE via the spectral tails of the pulses. Additionally, we observe that the positions of the resonance fringes shift consistently with the change in
\Delta_{1}
, further supporting the interpretation that the biexciton is coherently populated through the two-pulse SUPER scheme.

The lower panel of Fig.
3

\Delta_{2}
. In agreement with the anisotropy measurements in Fig.
3

\Delta_{2}=-6.1

\Delta_{2}
is further decreased, the X/XX intensity ratio increases significantly, indicating that these excitation conditions lead to predominant neutral exciton preparation.
Although the spectral separation between the two resonances is small compared to the bandwidth of the excitation pulses, they can be reliably distinguished and followed over a broad range of
\Delta_{1,2}
detunings, as shown in Fig.
3

In particular we focus on a peculiar excitation regime, where the SUPER protocol enables selective population of a single H- or V-polarized exciton states, depending on the laser detuning and without altering its polarization.

II.4
Emergence of an Orthogonal Exciton Resonance under Polarization-Misaligned SUPER

In Fig.
4
a, we present polarization anisotropy measurements of the exciton (X) photoluminescence (PL) under SUPER excitation, shown as a function of
\Delta_{2}
detuning for three selected excitation polarization angles. As expected, rotating the excitation polarization by 90
∘
enables direct excitation of the orthogonal H-exciton state, similar to the behavior observed in Fig.
3

\Delta_{2}\approx-6.5
meV (F

\Delta_{2}\approx-7.2
meV (F
2

Aligning the laser polarization between the H and V dipoles enables simultaneous excitation of both neutral exciton states. In this configuration, a coherent superposition of the H and V exciton states is driven, resulting in unpolarized PL emission. Moreover, since the interaction strength between the optical field and each dipole depends on their relative orientation, the resonant conditions for SUPER excitation are also modified. As a result, we observe a gradual decrease in both PL intensity and polarization degree at
\Delta_{2}=-6.5
meV as the excitation angle approaches 45
∘
. This orientation coincides with the strongest quantum beat amplitude in the time-resolved exciton PL, as shown in Supplementary Fig.S10 and Fig.
4

Interestingly, when the excitation polarization is not aligned with either the H- or V-exciton dipole, we observe the emergence of an additional resonance fringe at a larger detuning of
\Delta_{2}\approx-8
meV (F

∘

\theta=0^{\circ}
), orthogonal to the polarization of the previously discussed resonance at
\Delta_{2}\approx-6.5
meV (
\theta=90^{\circ}
). This contrast diminishes for a 45
∘
excitation angle, where both resonances result in unpolarized emission.

In the upper panel of Fig.
4

3
resonance fringe at
\Delta_{2}\approx-8
meV exhibits a similarly fast and monoexponential decay as that observed for direct SUPER excitation at
\Delta_{2}=-6.9
meV (Fig.
3
a). This behavior indicates that the neutral exciton emission does not result from a spontaneous biexciton cascade or relaxation from a higher-energy QD state. Furthermore, the lower panel of Fig.
4

3
fringe when the excitation polarization is set to 45
∘
. This provides strong evidence that the resonance fringe corresponds to a coherent preparation of the neutral exciton - different from the biexciton-mediated excitation pathway, which, as shown in Fig.
3
b, does not produce any quantum beat oscillations.

Further evidence supporting the distinct nature of this excitation, likely addressing the mostly orthogonal exciton dipole, is presented in Fig.
4
c. The associated resonance fringe, highlighted by the white dashed line, can be consistently tracked across different combinations of
\Delta_{1,2}
detunings. Notably, it exhibits a detuning dependence similar to that observed for the previously discussed SUPER-driven swing-up excitations of the X and XX states (cf. Fig.
3

Although the working principle behind the intriguing third emission feature—namely, the emergence of exclusively V-polarized emission at larger detuning—is not fully understood and requires further theoretical investigation, several plausible scenarios can be considered.

For one instance, this effect might be attributed to a slight misalignment of the excitation laser polarization with respect to the exciton dipole axes. For example, at an angle offset of approximately
\pm 20^{\circ}
from the H or V axis (see middle panel of Fig.
4
a), the excitation laser includes both H- and V-polarized components. In this configuration, the stronger H-polarized component could primarily excite the H-dipole, while the weaker V-polarized component might still contribute to the excitation of the V-dipole. However, this straightforward explanation falls short of accounting for the experimental observations. The V component, having a smaller projection onto the dipole moment, should result in substantially weaker PL compared to the H-polarized emission under similar conditions.
Contrary to this expectation, we observe that the intensity of the V-polarized emission at
\Delta_{2}\approx-8
meV is comparable to, or even rivals, that of the H-polarized emission at
\Delta_{2}\approx-6.5
meV. Notably, we also observe an exclusive V-polarized emission—with no detectable H-polarized signal—when the excitation laser is oriented at 70°, a condition that should, in principle, favor H-polarized excitation due to the larger effective projection onto the H-dipole. This strongly challenges the idea that the V-polarized emission arises merely from a residual V-component in a predominantly H-polarized excitation beam.

To further quantify this, we measure the total intensity of the emission associated with this resonance fringe as a function of the excitation polarization angle. These results are shown in Fig.
4
d, where, as a reference for the degree of dipole misalignment, we also plot the corresponding amplitude of the quantum beats extracted from the PL decay (Fig. S11). Indeed, we observe that the related PL signal is strongly suppressed when the laser polarization is aligned with the H/V exciton dipoles, whereas it reaches largest values for multiples of 45
∘
, similarly to the amplitude of the observed quantum beats. Notably, even a small
5^{\circ}
misalignment can yield up to 50
\%
of the maximal recorded intensity.
As evidenced, we show excitation conditions that enable selective coherent preparation of either H- or V-polarized exciton states without altering the polarization of the laser—simply by changing the detuning of the second pulse by approximately 2 meV.
We verify the robustness of this effect by performing consecutive switching between the
\Delta_{2}
detunings corresponding to the excitation of the corresponding H,V exciton states and recording no deviation up to 200 repetitions (Fig. S12).

Remarkably, the coherent population of the mostly orthogonal exciton dipole persists over a broad range of excitation angles (Fig.
4
e), as long as the laser polarization is not finely aligned along or directly in-between the H and V orientations.
We also emphasize that this phenomenon is not limited to the specific quantum dot or micropillar cavity investigated here. In principle, one could expect that the reported observations may be partly influenced by possible depolarization mechanisms arising from the scattering of the utilized laser on the investigated microstructure. However, similar polarization properties of the three distinct resonance fringes associated with the SUPER excitation of the neutral exciton can be clearly observed for a quantum dot embedded in a planar structure from the same sample (Fig. S13), demonstrating the robustness of this behavior.

Another potential explanation behind such excitation is that it may be related to mechanisms involving the biexciton–exciton cascade. Under SUPER excitation of the XX state, even a small spectral overlap between the laser and the XX–X transition could potentially enhance the biexciton-to-exciton relaxation via a stimulated process. This would be analogous to stimulated two-photon excitation, resulting in a fast exciton decay and strongly polarized PL emission
Wei et al. 2022
. However, this process does not explain the apparent preference for emission from the orthogonal exciton dipole—the one with a weaker coupling to the laser field.

Alternatively, the effect may be explained by a resonant XX-to-X relaxation driven by a SUPER-like process, resulting in a
\pi
-rotation of the Bloch vector within a dressed-state basis. Such a mechanism could lead to biexciton depopulation occurring within the duration of the laser pulse, when the energy levels are significantly modified due to AC-Stark shifts
Calcagno et al. 2025
. In this picture, the biexciton PL line would be absent, as the XX state is coherently depleted before spontaneous emission can occur. This kind of behavior was, in particular, observed for the measurement presented in Fig.
3
c. There, we recorded a similar SUPER-driven resonance of the neutral exciton in a cross-polarized configuration (at
\Delta_{2}=-6.9
meV, not shown), yet no accompanying biexciton emission was detected. Furthermore, if the laser polarization is misaligned from the H and V dipole axes, the resulting asymmetry in dipole–laser coupling could shift the resonance conditions differently for each dipole, potentially explaining the observed selectivity with respect to
\Delta_{2}
.

These observations collectively point toward a more involved excitation mechanism than simple dipole projection or cascaded decay, suggesting a rich interplay between coherent dynamics, laser detuning, and polarization selection in the SUPER excitation scheme. While our experimental findings clearly demonstrate the robust and tunable coherent preparation of both exciton dipoles—including selective excitation of the orthogonal dipole without modifying laser polarization—the underlying physical mechanism remains to be fully understood.

Further theoretical modeling, particularly incorporating dressed-state dynamics and ultrafast population transfer pathways under AC-Stark conditions, will be essential to provide a complete microscopic picture of this process. Nonetheless, our results establish a novel degree of control over neutral exciton states in quantum dots, enabled by the SUPER protocol, and open new avenues for deterministic state preparation in solid-state quantum photonic devices.

III
Conclusion

In summary, we have presented a detailed experimental investigation of the swing-up resonant excitation (SUPER) scheme applied to a GaAs quantum dot embedded in a low-Q micropillar cavity. We first benchmarked SUPER against conventional excitation methods, including above-band, p-shell, and two-photon excitation (TPE), and observed a marked reduction in exciton photoluminescence (PL) decay time along with improved single-photon purity, in line with earlier reports
Reindl et al. 2019
;
Boos et al. 2024
.
High degrees of linear polarization and efficient PL emission were achieved only under optimized conditions, specifically, when the laser polarization was aligned with an exciton dipole axis and pulse detunings were tuned to
\Delta_{1}=-3.7
meV and
\Delta_{2}=-7.4
meV. Under these conditions, we observed a
\sim
1.45-fold increase in PL intensity compared to TPE, assuming fixed linear detection polarization.
Surprisingly, significant emission was also detected in a cross-polarized configuration, which could not be explained within a simple three-level model. This prompted further investigation into biexciton dynamics under SUPER excitation. By slightly tuning the second pulse detuning by 0.5 meV, we observed clear evidence of neutral exciton population via the biexciton (XX–X) cascade—providing the first experimental demonstration of biexciton preparation and control via the SUPER scheme. This finding may open a promising route towards high-fidelity entangled photon pair generation via the XX–X cascade under swing-up excitation
Bracht et al. 2023
;
Heinisch et al. 2024
.

Additionally, we discovered a third, distinct resonance at
\Delta_{2}\approx-8
meV that enables efficient and coherent excitation of the exciton dipole orthogonal to the laser polarization. This state exhibits fast, monoexponential decay and strong quantum beat oscillations—hallmarks of coherent exciton preparation. Remarkably, the resulting V-polarized emission is comparable in intensity to that of the directly driven H state, despite no change in laser polarization. The effect is highly robust—appearing over a wide range of excitation angles and observed in both micropillar and planar geometries—suggesting a general mechanism for dipole-selective excitation not previously reported.
While the precise origin of this behavior requires further theoretical exploration, potential mechanisms include stimulated biexciton relaxation and asymmetric resonant coupling in a dressed-state basis. Regardless of the exact mechanism, the ability to selectively address either H or V exciton states by tuning the laser energy within a narrow 2 meV window, despite the excitonic fine-structure splitting being just 12
\mu
eV, demonstrates the remarkable control afforded by the SUPER scheme.
Together, our findings establish the SUPER scheme as a robust and versatile platform for state-selective preparation of excitonic states. Its compatibility with existing quantum dot platforms and ability to access both exciton and biexciton states coherently make it a compelling approach for scalable quantum photonic applications.

IV
Methodology

Optical characterization

To perform photoluminescence measurements, the sample was placed in a closed-cycle helium cryostat (attoDRY 800, attocube systems AG) with a base temperature of 4 K. The cryostat was equipped with a microscope objective to focus the laser light onto the sample (NA=0.81, 60×, attocube systems AG). For above bandgap excitation measurements a 650 nm laser was utilized either in CW or pulsed (80 MHz) mode (Picoquant LDH series). To enable various excitation schemes, we employed a tunable OPO module (Chameleon Compact OPO-Vis, Coherent Inc.) pumped by a Ti:Sapphire laser (Chameleon Ultra II, Coherent Inc.) producing 150 fs pulses at an 80 MHz repetition rate. To further optimize the laser spectral window for various excitation methods, we also employed a home-built 4f pulse shaper (f=500 mm) equipped with an 1800 groove/mm diffraction grating and a programmable single-mask transmissive spatial light modulator (SLM-S320, Jenoptik) in the Fourier plane. The SLM consisting of 320 pixels, helps to carve a narrow spectral range with a resolution of FWHM
\approx
0.08 nm corresponding to a single px transmission. For acquiring emission spectra, we utilized a fiber-coupled spectrometer (Horiba iHR 550) equipped with a 1200 groove/mm grating and a CCD camera (Syncerity Bi UV-Vis Horiba). For second-order correlation measurements, we employed a fiber-based Hanbury Brown and Twiss (HBT) interferometer coupled to superconducting nanowire single-photon detectors (SNSPD, ID218, ID Quantique) and time tagger (Swabian Instruments). Further details on the experimental setup are provided in Fig. S2.

Autocorrelation measurements

In the Fig.
1
e we presented the autocorrelation measurements of the exciton PL under different excitation schemes.
The extracted
g^{(2)}(0)
values were calculated as the area of the zero-delay peak divided by the average of the areas of the peaks at the largest measured delay times. The error range reported was the standard deviation calculated by error propagation, assuming a Poisson statistic for the peaks’ areas.

Theory
. To model the state preparation dynamics and the PL intensity, we consider a four-level model that includes the ground state
\ket{G}
, two orthogonal exciton states
\ket{V}
and
\ket{H}
with linear polarization, and the biexciton state
\ket{B}
. Their respective energies are
E_{G}=0
,
E_{V}=\hbar\quantity(\omega_0 - \frac 1 2 \Delta_{\rm FSS})
,
E_{H}=\hbar\quantity(\omega_0 + \frac 1 2 \Delta_{\rm FSS})
, and
E_{B}=\quantity(2 \hbar\omega_0 - E_b)
, where
\hbar\Delta_{\rm FSS}
is the fine structure splitting (FSS), and
E_{b}
is the biexciton binding energy.
Interaction with the laser pulse is modelled with the conventional dipole and rotating wave approximations.
Adopting an interaction picture with respect to
H_{0}=\quantity(2 \hbar\omega_0 - E_b)\outerproduct{B}{B}+\hbar\omega_{0}\quantity(\dyad{H} + \dyad{V})
, the system Hamiltonian is

H(t)=\frac{\hbar\Delta_{\rm FSS}}{2}\quantity(\dyad{H} - \dyad{V})\\
+\sum_{j=1,2}\frac{\hbar}{2}\Omega_{j}(t)\quantity[ e^{-i \delta_j t} \qty(\dyad{H}{G} + e^{-i \frac{E_b}{\hbar} t} \dyad{B}{H}) + \mathrm{h.c.}],

(1)

where
\Omega_{j}(t)
is the temporal envelope of the
j
-th pulse and
\delta_{j}
its frequency detuning with respect to
\omega_{0}
. The corresponding energy detuning is
\Delta_{j}=\hbar\delta_{j}
.
In Eq. (
1
), we assume that the laser polarization is aligned with the H exciton. An arbitrary excitation angle can be modeled by rotating the exciton state from
\ket{H}
to
\ket{H'}=\cos(\phi)\ket{H}-\sin(\phi)\ket{V}
.

The dynamics of the density operator is obtained by solving the master equation

\derivative{t}\rho=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\commutator{H(t)}{\rho}+\Gamma\sum_{s=H,V}\quantity(\mathcal{L}_{\dyad{G}{s}}[\rho] + \mathcal{L}_{\dyad{s}{B}} [\rho]),

(2)

which includes spontaneous emission via a set of Lindblad super-operators
\mathcal{L}_{A}[\rho]=A\rho A^{{\dagger}}-\frac{1}{2}\anticommutator{A^\dag A}{\rho}
. Here, we consider identical emission rate
\Gamma=(200\,\mathrm{ps})^{-1}
for all transitions.
Equation (
2
) is solved with a 4th-order Runge-Kutta method with initial condition
\rho(t_{0})=\outerproduct{G}{G}
.
Then, the number of H-polarized photons emitted in the transition from
\ket{H}\to\ket{G}
is calculated as

N_{H}=\Gamma\int_{t_{0}}^{+\infty}\differential{t}\Tr\quantity[ \dyad{H} \rho(t)].

(3)

The number of photons emitted in the opposite polarization is
N_{V}=\Gamma\int_{t_{0}}^{+\infty}\differential{t}\Tr\quantity[ \dyad{V} \rho(t)]
.

For the temporal shape of the pulses, we replace the conventional Gaussian envelope (see e.g. Ref.
Vannucci and Gregersen 2023
) with

\Omega_{j}(t)=\frac{\Theta_{j}}{t_{p}\pi\mathrm{Erf}(r/2)}\mathrm{Sinc}\quantity(\frac{t}{t_p \pi})\mathrm{Exp}\quantity[-\qty(\frac{t}{r t_p})^2],

(4)

where
\Theta_{j}
is the pulse area, and
r
is a free numerical parameter that we fix to
r=10
.
The Fourier-transformed pulse amplitude in the frequency domain is

\displaystyle\widehat{\Omega}_{j}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Theta_{j}}{2\mathrm{Erf}(r/2)}\mathrm{Erf}\quantity[\frac r 2 \qty(\omega t_p - \delta_j t_p + 1)]

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\Theta_{j}}{2\mathrm{Erf}(r/2)}\mathrm{Erf}\quantity[\frac r 2 \qty(\omega t_p - \delta_j t_p - 1)],

(5)

corresponding to square pulses centred at
\delta_{j}
with smooth corners. The corresponding pulse power is
|\widehat{\Omega}_{j}(\omega)|^{2}
, and the FWHM in wavelength for such a pulse is
{\rm FWHM}_{\lambda}=0.923\lambda_{0}^{2}/(\pi ct_{p})
, with
\lambda_{0}
the central wavelength.

The incoming pulse power
P^{(\rm in)}_{j}
is measured above the sample and depends on the amplitude of the incoming electric field
E^{(\rm in)}_{j}
and the pulse spectral width
W_{j}
as
P^{(\rm in)}_{j}\propto|E^{(\rm in)}_{j}|^{2}W_{j}
.
In contrast, the effective pulse area
\Theta_{j}
used in the simulation satisfies
\Theta_{j}^{2}\propto|E^{(\rm QD)}_{j}|^{2}W_{j}
, with
E^{(\rm QD)}_{j}
the electric field amplitude at the QD position.
The latter depends on the transmission through the top DBR mirror, which varies as a function of the detuning
\Delta_{j}
.
To take this into account, we use the same numerical method as in Ref.
Madigawa et al. 2025
to calculate the ratio
F_{j}=|E^{(\rm QD)}_{j}(\Delta_{j})/E^{(\rm in)}_{j}(\Delta_{j})|
between the field at the QD position and the incoming field.
Then, for a given incoming power ratio we have

\frac{P^{(\rm in)}_{2}}{P^{(\rm in)}_{1}}=\frac{|E^{(\rm in)}_{2}|^{2}W_{2}}{|E^{(\rm in)}_{1}|^{2}W_{1}}=\quantity(\frac{\Theta_2}{\Theta_1} \frac{F_1}{F_2})^{2}\frac{W_{2}}{W_{1}},

(6)

from which we extract the relative pulse areas
\Theta_{j}
to be used in the simulation.
