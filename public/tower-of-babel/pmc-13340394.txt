AI-assisted preoperative planning improves component sizing accuracy in Oxford unicompartmental knee arthroplasty: a retrospective cohort study with 24-month follow-up
Authors: Fengrui Liu, Tong Li, Mengchen Yuan, Jiaxin Sun, Guoyuan Sun, Yaxing Duan, Zhengyuan Bai, Jinrui Sun, Xingyu Liu, Yiling Zhang, Qidong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurate implant sizing is critical for clinical outcomes in Oxford unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA), yet reliable preoperative planning remains challenging. This study aimed to evaluate whether AI-assisted preoperative planning improves component sizing accuracy compared with conventional intraoperative instrumentation and to explore factors associated with component coverage.MethodsA single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted including 190 patients (190 knees) undergoing primary Oxford UKA between February 2022 and January 2024. Patients were categorized into the AI-assisted group (n = 94) or the conventional group (n = 96). Radiographic sizing accuracy of femoral and tibial components was assessed postoperatively. Clinical outcomes were evaluated using the HSS score at predefined intervals of 6, 12, and 24 months postoperatively. Logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with inaccurate sizing in the AI-assisted group.ResultsThe AI-assisted group demonstrated significantly higher radiographic sizing accuracy than the conventional group for the femoral component (81.91 vs. 63.54%, P = 0.005), tibial component (85.12 vs. 67.71%, P = 0.005), and both components (70.21 vs. 51.04%, P = 0.007). Patients with accurate sizing of both components achieved higher HSS scores at 24 months compared with those with at least one inaccurately sized component (92.50 ± 4.00 vs. 89.32 ± 3.96, P < 0.001). Within the AI-assisted group, agreement between planned and actual posterior condylar resection (OR = 0.249, P = 0.025) and acceptable femoral sagittal angle (OR = 0.252, P = 0.020) were independent predictors of femoral sizing accuracy. No significant independent predictors were identified for tibial inaccurate sizing.ConclusionsAI-assisted preoperative planning was associated with improved radiographic component sizing accuracy in Oxford UKA compared with conventional instrumentation. While no significant differences in HSS scores were observed at 6 and 12 months, patients with accurate sizing of both components demonstrated significantly better functional recovery at the 24-month follow-up. The reliability of AI-assisted femoral sizing depends on precise intraoperative execution of planned resection and alignment parameters.

Introduction

Unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) has emerged as a well-established surgical intervention for patients with single-compartment knee osteoarthritis, offering distinct advantages such as minimal surgical trauma and rapid postoperative recovery [1]. Among the available prosthetic designs, the Oxford UKA is one of the most widely used implants and has demonstrated favorable clinical efficacy [2]. However, clinical outcomes following Oxford UKA are highly dependent on the accuracy of prosthetic sizing [3–6]. Component mismatch significantly increases the risk of postoperative complications: oversized prostheses may lead to soft tissue irritation [3, 4], whereas undersized components increase the risk of polyethylene bearing dislocation, prosthetic subsidence, and aseptic loosening [5, 6]. With the widespread adoption of minimally invasive UKA [7], limited surgical exposure may compromise accurate intraoperative assessment of both femoral and tibial coverage. Therefore, precise preoperative planning is of paramount importance for guiding intraoperative size selection. Traditionally, preoperative planning for component sizing in UKA commonly relies on X-ray templating [8]. However, Kasis et al. and Bothra et al. suggest that the reliability and accuracy of manual templates are suboptimal [8, 9]. Fawzy et al. proposed a method for predicting component sizes based on patient height and gender, which showed superior accuracy compared to templating [10]. Nevertheless, these models are often derived from specific ethnic cohorts, leading to a significant decline in predictive accuracy when applied to diverse populations [11]. Thus, an accurate and reliable method remains lacking for predicting component sizes in Oxford UKA. Artificial intelligence (AI) has been increasingly utilized in preoperative planning for arthroplasty in recent years. Lu et al [12] have reported that AI-assisted total hip arthroplasty (THA) significantly enhances the precision of component selection and implant positioning. Similarly, AI-assisted total knee arthroplasty (TKA) has been shown to not only improve sizing accuracy but also to result in superior postoperative functional outcomes [13]. However, existing AI-assisted arthroplasty studies have mainly focused on THA and TKA [14, 15], while reproducible and clinically validated evidence for AI-based preoperative sizing in Oxford UKA remains limited. In this study, we utilized a proprietary AI-UNI system (version 2.0, Longwood Valley Technology, China) that leverages deep learning algorithms to automatically extract anatomical features from CT data, construct three-dimensional models, simulate bone resection, and facilitate personalized prosthetic matching. However, the accuracy and reliability of this system for Oxford UKA planning remain to be validated.

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate whether AI-assisted preoperative planning improves the accuracy of component sizing compared with conventional intraoperative instrumentation, as assessed by component coverage on postoperative anteroposterior (AP) and lateral radiographs. Additionally, we aimed to assess whether improved sizing accuracy is associated with superior postoperative clinical outcomes and to investigate factors influencing component coverage under AI-assisted planning. We hypothesized that AI-assisted planning would achieve higher sizing accuracy in Oxford UKA, particularly for the femoral component, given its complex anatomy and the technical challenges of intraoperative femoral sizing.

Methods

Study design

The study protocol was approved by our hospital’s ethics committee (approval number: 2024-KY-428), and all patients gave informed consent for the use of their de-identified data in research. We conducted a single-center, retrospective cohort study of consecutive patients who underwent primary Oxford UKA between February 2022 and January 2024. All procedures were performed by a senior high-volume arthroplasty surgeon at our institution. In the AI-assisted group, patients underwent preoperative standard hip–knee–ankle CT scanning followed by AI-based surgical planning. The system generated a planning report that included the recommended femoral and tibial component sizes and the planned resection thicknesses of the femoral condyles and tibial plateau. Surgeons referenced the planning report to guide intraoperative implant selection. In the conventional group, implant sizing and placement were based on preoperative X-ray templating, in accordance with the Oxford® Partial Knee Manual to predict the size of femoral components, and for tibial components, were determined based on intraoperative judgment using standard instrumentation.

Patients were eligible if they met all of the following: (1) anteromedial osteoarthritis confirmed by history, physical examination, and weight-bearing radiographs; (2) preoperative flexion ≥ 90° and flexion contracture ≤ 15°; (3) intact medial and lateral collateral ligaments and cruciate ligaments; varus deformity < 15°; preserved lateral and patellofemoral compartments; (4) completion of a primary Oxford UKA; and (5) with preoperative hip-knee-ankle AP standing radiographs, postoperative hip-knee-ankle AP standing radiographs, and AP and lateral knee radiographs in both groups. Exclusion criteria were: (1) spontaneous osteonecrosis of the medial femoral condyle or any non-osteoarthritic pathology; (2) prior open surgery on the index knee; (3) active or prior knee infection; (4) neuromuscular disease affecting the limb; (5) history of surgery on the ipsilateral lower limb (n = 2 knees) (AI-assisted: 1; Conventional: 1); and (6) radiographs with poor quality taken preoperatively or postoperatively (n = 8 knees) (AI-assisted: 4; Conventional: 4). Of the 200 knees initially screened, 190 patients (190 knees) met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled. All participants underwent unilateral procedures. During this period, the choice of preoperative planning method (AI-assisted or conventional) was determined through a shared decision-making process following discussion of both approaches with the patients. No specific radiographic or clinical criteria were used for selecting the AI-assisted method, and the decision was not based on patient anatomical characteristics. Given our sample size of 190 (n = 94 in the AI-assisted group, n = 96 in the conventional group), the calculated power (1-β) for detecting the observed difference in femoral and tibial components sizing accuracy was approximately 0.817 and 0.812, respectively, with an α level of 0.05.

Surgical procedure

Surgery was performed with the patient in the supine position. A curved medial parapatellar incision (5–8 cm) was made, and medial osteophytes were removed. Subsequently, tibial osteotomy was carried out using extramedullary alignment, strictly adhering to the Oxford UKA surgical guidelines. After tibial plateau resection, the resected tibial bone slice was compared with the back surface of the tibial plateau trial to assist in implant sizing. The size of the tibial component was selected based on the principle of covering the posterior and medial cortices of the tibial resection surface. Femoral component sizing was performed as follows: First, the femoral medial condyle sizer (gauge) was placed on the medial femoral condyle, ensuring its posterior edge fully conformed to the curvature of the posterior femoral condyle. Next, an approximately 3 mm gap was confirmed between the anterior edge of the gauge and the exposed anterior condylar bone surface to compensate for the thickness of the worn articular cartilage. Appropriate medial collateral ligament tension was verified by approximately 20° medial and lateral rotation. The implant size was determined by selecting a size that concurrently satisfied conformity to the posterior femoral condyle and ligament stability. Following femoral osteotomy, a cylindrical burr was used to prepare the distal femur to balance the flexion-extension gap. After confirmation of gap balance and implantation of a polyethylene insert of appropriate thickness, the knee exhibited uniform ligament tension throughout the full range of flexion-extension motion. Finally, the component was implanted and the incision was closed.

AI preoperative planning

The AI-UNI system implements a hierarchical deep-learning framework for Oxford UKA planning, leveraging a hybrid neural architecture integrating U-Net, 3D U-Net, DenseBlock, Point-Rend, and LSTM modules to achieve high-fidelity anatomical segmentation and biomechanical alignment. Initially, a U-Net backbone performs coarse volumetric segmentation of the knee joint, isolating the distal femur and proximal tibia with preliminary boundary delineation. Subsequently, a 3D U-Net enhanced with DenseBlock layers refines these segmentations through dense connectivity, promoting gradient flow and enhancing edge continuity for both the femoral condyle and tibial plateau. Meanwhile, the LSTM network is adopted to accurately classify the medial or lateral compartment of the knee, ensuring the correct identification of the target anatomical structures for UKA. Moreover, the BoneDet network, together with Point-Rend technology, realizes secondary image recognition to finally confirm the osteochondral defects on the tibial plateau and femoral condyle, enabling precise defect mapping and bone stock assessment. The DICOM data from CT scans were imported into the AI-UNI software. Following segmentation, the system reconstructs the 3D geometry of the tibial plateau and femoral condyle based on key anatomical landmarks including the tibial eminence, medial/lateral plateau edges, and femoral trochlea etc. The lower limb alignment is automatically corrected by referencing the mechanical axis, and the anteroposterior diameter of the tibial plateau is measured on the transverse section to guide implant sizing. Using these landmarks, the posterior condylar offset was calculated and the femoral intramedullary entry point was predicted, followed by simulation of the femoral and tibial osteotomy boundaries. After virtual osteotomy, the AI-UNI system algorithmically identified key dimensions—including the anteroposterior diameter of the femoral condyle and the width of the tibial plateau—and automatically selected the optimal component size from the implant database, matching it with the corresponding model. The positions of the femoral and tibial components were automatically determined. The implant inclination, rotation, and coverage ratio on the tibial plateau were planned as well. In this process, the posterior condyle of the component was required to cover the posterior femoral condyle as completely as possible, allowing approximately 2 mm overhang, and tibial component was algorithmically positioned to achieve maximal coverage of the anterior, posterior, and medial cortical margins of the tibial resection surface. Data collection

Component sizing accuracy was evaluated by assessing the coverage relative to the cortical margins on postoperative radiographs. Based on the criteria established by Fawzy et al. [10], femoral sizing was deemed appropriate if the component exhibited a 2–3 mm proximal overhang to achieve flush alignment with the retained cartilage. For the tibial component, suboptimal sizing was defined as a major overhang of ≥ 3 mm, a threshold previously identified by Chau et al. [16] as a significant predictor of increased pain and inferior long-term functional outcomes. Therefore, inappropriate sizing was defined as under-coverage (< 0 mm) or excessive overhang (> 3 mm) [16, 17].Implant position and alignment were assessed as previously described [18, 19] (Fig. 3). Acceptable alignment was defined as Femoral Coronal Angle (FCA) and Tibial Coronal Angle (TCA) deviations within ± 10° and ± 5° of the neutral axis, respectively; Femoral Sagittal Angle (FSA) ≤ 15° flexion; and Tibial Sagittal Angle (TSA) within ± 5° of the target 7° slope (Fig. 4).

Prosthesis coverage was evaluated on AP and lateral radiographs obtained 3–5 days postoperatively using standardized radiographic protocols. On AP radiographs, the limb was positioned with the patella centered to maintain neutral rotation. On lateral radiographs, a true lateral view was defined by superimposition of the medial and lateral femoral condyles. All radiographic measurements were performed using digital tools within the institutional PACS system (precision 0.01 mm), and independently by two blinded reviewers. Preoperative AI-planned and intraoperative actual resection thickness including tibial resection, posterior femoral resection, distal femoral resection was recorded. Resection agreement was defined as an actual-to-planned resection thickness ratio between 0.8 and 1.2. Hospital for Special Surgery (HSS) scores were assessed at 6, 12, and 24 months postoperatively during scheduled outpatient visits by a trained orthopedic surgeon. When outpatient follow-up was not feasible, structured telephone interviews were conducted to obtain patient-reported components of the HSS score. Objective components, including range of motion and joint stability, were obtained from the most recent documented clinical examination records in the database. All radiographic measurements and follow-up data collection were independently performed by orthopedic surgeons from our institution (FRL, GYS, YXD, ZYB, and JRS), who were not involved in the development of the AI-UNI system.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for approximately normally distributed data and as median (interquartile range, IQR) for skewed data; categorical variables were reported as counts and proportions. Normality was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test and homogeneity of variances with Levene’s test. Between-group comparisons used independent-samples t tests for normally distributed continuous variables or Mann–Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed data; categorical variables were compared with chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to assess the risk factors for inaccurate sizing of femoral component in AI-assisted group. Variables with P < 0.10 in univariate analysis were entered into a multivariate logistic regression model. The post-hoc power analysis was performed using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7). Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS (version 27.0).

Baseline characteristics and accuracy of implant size prediction

The cohort comprised 190 patients (AI-assisted: 94; Conventional: 96). There were no statistically significant differences in baseline characteristics between the two groups (P > 0.05). (Table 2)

Radiographic implant sizing accuracy was significantly higher in the AI-assisted group than in the conventional group. For the femoral component, accuracy was 81.91% in the AI-assisted group versus 63.54% in the conventional group (χ2 = 8.066, P = 0.005). For the tibial component, accuracy was 85.12% versus 67.71%, respectively (χ2 = 7.954, P = 0.005). The combined femoral and tibial accuracy was also significantly higher in the AI-assisted group (70.21 vs. 51.04%; χ2 = 7.306, P = 0.007). (Table 2) Regarding the distribution of sizing discrepancies, a consistent pattern of oversizing was observed in femoral and tibial sides. For the femoral component, all instances of sizing non-concordance in the AI-assisted (n = 17) and conventional (n = 35) groups involved the implantation of a component larger than the radiographically optimal size. Similarly, tibial component deviations were predominantly characterized by oversizing (AI-assisted: 13 cases; conventional: 29 cases). Undersizing of the tibial component occurred in three cases across the entire cohort (AI-assisted: 1; conventional: 2). (Fig. 5) Intraoperative deviation from the preoperative plan occurred in only one case, in which the femoral component was downsized from M to S, resulting in a residual overhang of 0.55 mm.

Comparison of outcomes based on sizing accuracy

Patients were stratified into a “Both femoral and tibial components accurate" group (n = 115) and an “At least one side incorrect” group (n = 75). Of the 190 knees enrolled, 2 (1.0%), 4 (2.1%), and 9 (4.7%) were unable to attend in-person visits at 6, 12, and 24 months, respectively, and were therefore assessed via structured telephone interviews, comprising 1, 3, and 5 knees in the both components accurate group and 1, 1, and 4 knees in the at least one side incorrect group. Radiographic analysis revealed no significant differences between these subgroups in terms of preoperative or postoperative HKAA, nor in the proportions of acceptable FCA, FSA, TCA, and TSA (P > 0.05). Regarding clinical outcomes, the HSS scores were comparable preoperatively and at 6-month and 12-month follow-ups (P > 0.05). However, at the 24-month follow-up, the mean HSS score at 24 months was significantly higher in the both components accurate group (92.50 ± 4.00) than in the at least one side incorrect group (89.32 ± 3.96) (t = − 5.371, P < 0.001). Risk factors for incorrect component outcomes

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify risk factors associated with inaccurate sizing of the femoral component within the AI-assisted group. The multivariate analysis identified the agreement between planned and actual thickness of femoral posterior condylar resection (agreement of FPCR) and the proportion of acceptable FSA as significant independent predictors. Specifically, a higher agreement of FPCR was associated with reduced odds of femoral component inaccurate sizing with OR = 0.249 (95% CI 0.071 ~ 0.842; P = 0.025). Similarly, achieving an acceptable FSA was significantly associated with satisfactory clinical outcomes with OR = 0.252 (95% CI 0.075 ~ 0.788; P = 0.020). Other variables, including age, sex, BMI, and preoperative HKAA, did not show statistical significance in the multivariate model. (Table 4) In contrast, no significant risk factors were identified for inaccurate sizing of tibial component in either univariate or multivariate logistic regression analyses (all P > 0.05).

Discussion

The present study aimed to evaluate the effect of AI-assisted preoperative planning on implant sizing accuracy in Oxford UKA. The results demonstrated that AI-assisted preoperative planning was associated with improved implant sizing accuracy. Compared with the conventional group, the AI-assisted group achieved significantly higher radiographic accuracy in femoral and tibial component selection, as well as a higher rate of simultaneous accuracy for both components. Patients with accurate sizing of both components showed superior postoperative functional scores compared with those with at least one inaccurately sized component. Within the AI-assisted cohort, adherence to the planned posterior condylar resection and appropriate femoral component flexion angle were identified as predictors of femoral sizing accuracy. Achieving accurate component sizing has long been a clinical challenge in Oxford UKA. Conventional X-ray templating relies on two-dimensional imaging and is affected by projection positioning, magnification variability, and challenges in landmark identification. Kasis et al. [9] reported low accuracy and poor inter-observer agreement regardless of surgeon experience. Bothra et al. [8] similarly demonstrated low intra- and inter-observer reliability in femoral sizing using X-ray templating. It is reported that the Oxford algorithm, incorporating gender and height, achieved 75% accuracy in its derivation cohort [10]. However, subsequent validation studies reported lower accuracies in different populations, including 51% in a Chinese cohort [20] and 37% in an Indian cohort [11]. The reduction in accuracy across populations reflected an inherent defect in lacking analysis of individual anatomical morphology. Alternative imaging-based strategies have been explored. MRI-based templating demonstrated higher accuracy than intraoperative gauges (72 vs. 54%) [21], but preoperative MRI is not routine practice. CT-based robotic systems have been reported to improve component sizing accuracy [22]. However, current CT-based robotic systems still require manual verification and adjustment of anatomical landmarks and are limited to specific fixed-bearing UKA. In contrast, the AI-UNI system utilizes individualized CT data to generate three-dimensional reconstructions and automatically identifies key femoral and tibial anatomical landmarks using deep learning algorithms. A patient-specific bone model is constructed, and implant–bone matching is simulated in three-dimensional space. Component size, position, and alignment are optimized algorithmically. In the present study, femoral component radiographic sizing accuracy was 81.91% (77/94), tibial component radiographic sizing accuracy was 85.12% (80/94), and combined accuracy for both components was 70.21% (66/94). The sizing accuracy for all components in the AI-assisted group was significantly higher than that in the conventional group (all P < 0.01). These findings suggest that CT-based, anatomy-driven planning may improve the accuracy of component sizing by relying on patient-specific three-dimensional anatomy rather than demographic characteristics or two-dimensional estimation. Prosthesis mismatch, such as excessive overhang or under-coverage, can adversely affect postoperative outcomes. Gudena et al. [3] examined medial collateral ligament (MCL) loads in six human cadaveric knees, finding that excessive overhang significantly increased MCL load. Furthermore, excessive medial tibial overhang increases the risk of revision due to bearing dislocation and loosening [4], whereas under-coverage may lead to periprosthetic fractures [6, 23]. In addition to component coverage, previous studies have demonstrated that malalignment of UKA components is significantly associated with inferior functional outcomes and higher revision rates [19]. In the present study, while HSS scores progressively increased for all patients during the postoperative period, those with accurate sizing of both components achieved significantly higher scores at 24 months than those with at least one inaccurately sized component (92.50 ± 4.00 vs. 89.32 ± 3.96; P < 0.001). Previous studies have suggested that inappropriate component sizing may adversely affect postoperative outcomes. Chau et al. [16] demonstrated that inappropriate component sizing resulting in a tibial component overhang of 3 mm or more severely compromises postoperative outcomes, leading to significantly worsened clinical functional scores and pain scores at 5 years post-surgery (P = 0.001). In this context, prosthesis sizing may be one of the factors contributing to the observed differences. One possible explanation is that optimal component sizing may enhance joint biomechanics and alleviate soft-tissue impingement secondary to structural mismatch. However, although the difference in HSS scores at 24 months between the two groups reached statistical significance, the absolute difference was modest (3.18 points) and may not reach the reported minimal clinically important difference (MCID) [24]. It should also be noted that both groups achieved substantial improvement in HSS scores relative to their preoperative baselines, reflecting clinically meaningful functional recovery following surgery. Nonetheless, patients with accurate sizing of both components consistently demonstrated a trend toward superior HSS scores throughout the follow-up period, implying that precise prosthesis sizing may partially contribute to enhanced functional outcomes. It should also be noted that the relatively short follow-up period in the present study may limit the detection of certain adverse outcomes associated with component overhang, such as aseptic loosening or periprosthetic fracture, which may develop over a longer time course. Prospective studies with larger sample sizes and extended follow-up durations are warranted to establish the clinical significance of this relationship, and further longitudinal follow-up will be conducted to evaluate these outcomes.

Femoral component inaccurate sizing in the AI-assisted group was associated with deviations between planned and actual posterior condylar resection and with femoral component flexion angle. Specifically, the agreement of FPCR (OR = 0.249, P = 0.025) and proportion of acceptable FSA (OR = 0.252, P = 0.020) were identified as the predictors of femoral sizing accuracy within this cohort. Despite the high consistency between the preoperatively planned and intraoperatively utilized component sizes, suboptimal component sizing was nevertheless observed in certain cases. This discrepancy may be partially explained by intraoperative adjustments for soft-tissue balancing, which may result in deviations from the planned resection thickness. Furthermore, mismatch in sagittal alignment may alter the effective anteroposterior coverage of the femoral condyles, thereby contributing to posterior overhang. This suggests that deviations from AI-generated bony resection targets or optimal component inclination may compromise the accuracy of the preplanned prosthetic matching. No significant predictors were identified for tibial component overhang. This may indicate that tibial sizing accuracy is influenced by factors beyond the morphological parameters examined in the present study. Importantly, the rotational positioning of the tibial component was not quantified in this analysis due to the inherent limitations of two-dimensional radiographs in assessing rotational alignment in the axial plane. We consider this lack of quantification to be significant, as rotational variance may alter medial–lateral coverage and subsequently attenuate the observed effects of static anatomical parameters in our regression model. Future studies incorporating quantitative assessment of tibial component rotational alignment and other intraoperative variables are warranted to more comprehensively evaluate determinants of tibial component overhang.

This study has several limitations. (1) The retrospective and non-randomized design may introduce selection bias and residual confounding. Although the influence of unmeasured confounders cannot be completely excluded, several measures were taken to ensure the robustness of our findings. The AI-assisted and conventional groups were comparable in baseline characteristics, and all procedures were performed by a single experienced surgeon, effectively minimizing performance bias. Furthermore, the application of standardized measurement protocols and the achievement of high interobserver reliability for all radiographic parameters further enhance the internal validity of our data. Nevertheless, future prospective randomized studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to further confirm the advantages of AI-assisted preoperative planning in Oxford UKA. (2) The AI-UNI system currently performs reconstruction and planning based on bony structures. Since soft tissue balancing is equally critical for restoring lower limb alignment and achieving good motor function, future AI iterations should incorporate soft tissue balancing into implant sizing and planning. (3) The statistical power of our logistic regression analysis may be constrained by the relatively small number of events in the AI-assisted group. While our findings suggest that the agreement of FPCR and an acceptable FSA are key factors in sizing accuracy, future large-scale studies are required to further validate these relationships and confirm the exact role of surgical technical precision.

Conclusion

In conclusion, AI-assisted planning was associated with higher radiographic accuracy in femoral and tibial component sizing, as well as in the combined accuracy of both components, in Oxford UKA compared with conventional preoperative planning. Higher sizing accuracy was also associated with higher HSS scores, with patients achieving accurate sizing of both components showing a trend toward better functional outcomes at 24 months. Furthermore, the reliability of AI-assisted femoral sizing may be related to the agreement between planned and actual posterior condylar resection and to the achievement of an acceptable femoral sagittal angle.
