Cost‐effectiveness of in‐hospital motivational smoking cessation counselling and proactive referral to community‐based follow‐up
Authors: Karin Pleym, Ingrid Engebretsen, Elise Sverre, Toril Dammen, Einar Huseby, Mohpal Singh Kahlon, Marie Stugaard, Henrik Støvring, Ivar Sønbø Kristiansen, John Munkhaugen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimIn a randomised open‐label trial among hospitalised patients with atherosclerotic vascular disease, motivational smoking cessation counselling with proactive referral to community‐based follow‐up was more effective than brief cessation advice and written information, with 6‐month continuous abstinence rates of 49.5% vs. 24.5%. This study aimed to estimate the cost‐effectiveness of this intervention compared with brief cessation advice.DesignEconomic evaluation alongside a multicentre, randomised open‐label, blinded‐endpoint trial with 1:1 randomisation.SettingThree secondary care hospitals in Norway. Recruitment took place from November 2021 to October 2023.ParticipantsHospitalised patients aged 18 years or older with established atherosclerotic vascular disease who reported smoking at least one cigarette daily before admission were eligible for participation, regardless of whether admission was planned or unplanned. A total of 221 patients were randomised. One participant withdrew informed consent and was excluded from all analyses, leaving 220 participants in the economic evaluation. The cohort comprised 40% women and the mean age was 65.2 years.MeasurementsIntervention costs included staff training, materials and personnel time. Hospital costs during the 16‐month follow‐up were estimated using Diagnosis‐Related Group cost weights. Survival beyond follow‐up was extrapolated using national mortality data adjusted for age, sex and atherosclerotic vascular disease. Lifetime costs were estimated using mean costs from the final six months of follow‐up. The primary economic outcome was net monetary benefit (NMB), calculated at a willingness‐to‐pay threshold of €38 346 per life‐year gained. The incremental cost‐effectiveness ratio was also estimated. Future costs and life‐years were discounted at 4%. Uncertainty estimates (UE) were obtained using bootstrapping.FindingsDuring the 16‐month follow‐up, five patients (4.6%) died in the intervention group and nine (8.1%) in the control group. Discounted life expectancy from baseline was 13.54 years in the intervention group and 12.47 years in the control group, corresponding to an incremental gain of 1.06 life‐years (95% UE = −0.72 to 3.31). The incremental discounted lifetime cost was €3280 per patient (95% UE = –€19 457 to €26 436), resulting in an incremental cost‐effectiveness ratio of €3094 per life‐year gained. At a willingness‐to‐pay threshold of €38 346 per life‐year gained, the NMB was €37 475 (95% UE = –€15 868 to €107 797) and the probability of the intervention being cost‐effective was 90.5%.ConclusionMotivational smoking cessation counselling delivered during hospitalisation with proactive referral to community‐based follow‐up had a high probability of being cost‐effective from a hospital perspective for patients with atherosclerotic vascular disease.Keywords: atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, cost‐effectiveness, health economics, motivational interviewing, nurse‐delivered counselling, smoking cessation

INTRODUCTION

Tobacco smoking is a major modifiable risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), contributing substantially to the global burden of disease [1, 2]. In 2019, it accounted for approximately 200 million disability‐adjusted life‐years globally, reflecting its profound health and economic impact [2]. Smoking doubles the risk of cardiovascular mortality compared to never smoking, while cessation reduces both immediate and long‐term risks of recurrent ASCVD events and premature death [3].

Hospitalisation offers a key opportunity to implement evidence‐based cessation support and connect patients with post‐discharge follow‐up [4]. Meta‐analyses of randomised controlled trials show that combining behavioural counselling with cessation medications increases long‐term quit rates by approximately 10% to 20% compared with pharmacotherapy alone [5]. Although the effectiveness of hospital‐based smoking cessation interventions is well established [4], few have applied motivational interviewing, particularly among patients with ASCVD. In a recent trial, we demonstrated that a single in‐hospital, nurse‐led motivational interviewing session for smoking cessation, combined with proactive referral to community‐based cessation programmes, doubled long‐term abstinence rates among patients with ASCVD [6, 7].

Health economic evaluation assesses whether the benefits of an intervention justify its costs and is recommended in recent European Society of Cardiology guidelines to inform implementation decisions [8]. Although hospital‐based smoking cessation interventions are generally considered cost‐effective [9], most economic evaluations of ASCVD prevention rely on models rather than patient‐level costs and outcome data [10, 11, 12]. Real‐world evaluations capturing costs of rehospitalisation and follow‐up beyond 6 months remain scarce in this high‐risk population [13]. Moreover, cost‐effectiveness evidence for motivational interviewing in smoking cessation is limited and is primarily derived from non‐hospitalised populations [14, 15].

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the 16‐month and lifetime cost‐effectiveness of a nurse‐led intervention comprising a single in‐hospital counselling session based on motivational interviewing, combined with proactive referral to community‐based smoking cessation programmes. The analysis was conducted from the hospital perspective, using trial‐based data on mortality and hospital costs over 16 months, with results extrapolated to a lifetime horizon.

METHODS

Design, setting and ethics

This pre‐specified health economic evaluation was based on data from a prospective, randomised, open‐label trial with blinded end point evaluation (PROBE design) [7]. The trial was conducted between November 2021 and October 2023 at three secondary care hospitals in Norway, using existing healthcare infrastructure across hospital and primary care.

The Regional Committee for Medical and Health Research Ethics for the South‐East Health Region reviewed the study protocol (REK‐270267, 25.06.2021). The Data Protection Officer at Vestre Viken Hospital Trust approved the study on 31 August 2021 (PVO‐21/07103–1/005). All patients provided written informed consent before inclusion. The trial was preregistered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05049174) before enrolment. This paper adheres to the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) [16].

Population and interventions

Eligible patients were adults hospitalised for a planned or unplanned event, with established ASCVD, either previously diagnosed or identified during the initial hospitalisation, who reported smoking at least one cigarette daily before admission. Patients were screened in the Departments of Medicine, Neurology and Vascular Surgery on all weekdays.

A total of 221 patients were randomised in a 1:1 ratio to intervention or control. One patient in the intervention group withdrew informed consent, leaving 109 patients in the intervention group and 111 in the control group for analysis.

The intervention comprised: (1) a single, in‐hospital smoking cessation counselling session (~30 minutes) delivered by a nurse; (2) an information leaflet describing cessation support available at community‐based Healthy Life Centres, including access to free cessation medications for 12 weeks [17]; (3) a proactive referral to the community‐based cessation programme; and (4) a discharge letter to the patient's general practitioner describing study inclusion and the available offer of cessation support in community‐based cessation programmes. In addition to the study‐specific components, patients in both groups received routine in‐patient care.

Control group patients received brief smoking cessation advice delivered by a physician, the same information leaflet and discharge information to the general practitioner. Unlike the intervention group, patients in the control group were responsible for initiating contact with the community‐based cessation programme themselves or alternatively through their general practitioner after consultation. Further details are described in Pleym et al. [7].

Nicotine replacement therapy was not provided as part of the trial intervention. Initiation of pharmacotherapy during hospitalisation followed routine clinical practice and could be prescribed at the discretion of the attending physician if the patient wished to quit immediately. In routine care, physicians are recommended to provide brief smoking cessation advice according to the Norwegian national guidelines, consisting of a question about smoking status, advice to quit and an offer of assistance. The delivery of routine smoking cessation advice and initiation of pharmacotherapy were not systematically recorded as part of the trial.

Smoking cessation outcomes

Smoking cessation outcomes are presented in the main trial publication [7] and briefly summarised here in Table S1. The primary outcome of the trial was self‐reported continuous smoking abstinence at 6 months, defined according to the Russell Standard as sustained abstinence with a maximum of five cigarettes smoked after the quit date. Participants who did not respond to follow‐up, were classified as smokers in accordance with the intention‐to‐treat principle. Self‐reported abstinence was selected as the primary outcome at the planning stage because of concerns during the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic that in‐person biochemical validation might not be feasible. Biochemically verified abstinence based on exhaled carbon monoxide was included as a secondary outcome. Participants who died before the relevant follow‐up time point were excluded from abstinence analyses in accordance with the Russell Standard [18].

Data source and collection

For the economic evaluation, we obtained administrative data on all in‐patient and outpatient contacts for each patient from study inclusion until end of follow‐up at 16 months. The dataset included age, sex, date of inclusion, International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision main and supplementary diagnoses, contact type (in‐patient or outpatient), procedures performed, Diagnosis‐Related Group (DRG) code, DRG cost weight and date of death if applicable. We estimated intervention costs retrospectively based on staff time and material use. Unit costs were derived from national salary statistics for relevant healthcare staff and standard employer social security contributions. Time estimates for each activity were based on protocol assumptions and staff input.

Resource use and costs

We assumed that physicians spent 15 minutes for routine brief smoking cessation advice, including documentation. This reflects the recommended ‘minimal intervention’ described in Norwegian national clinical guidelines on smoking cessation, consisting of (1) a question about smoking status; (2) advice to quit; and (3) an offer of assistance [19]. We used the wage rate of a physician in specialist training. Nurse time (estimated as 30 minutes for counselling, 15 minutes for documentation and 15 minutes for referral) was monetised using the wage rate of a cardiology nurse. Costs of nicotine replacement therapy initiated during hospitalisation were not included, as pharmacotherapy was part of routine clinical practice and available to patients in both groups at the discretion of the attending physician. The proactive outreach telephone call conducted by staff at the community‐based health centre (estimated at 15 minutes) was included as part of the intervention delivery and costed using the hourly wage of a municipal physiotherapist. The analysis was conducted from a hospital perspective. Costs related to participation in the community‐based Healthy Life Centre programme, including provision of free cessation medications, were not included in the base‐case analysis. Training costs included an 18‐hour course in motivational interviewing and a 1‐hour session on cessation medications, completed by six study nurses. Assuming one full‐time equivalent nurse, we allocated total training costs across all intervention patients.

Hospital costs over the 16‐month follow‐up were based on all in‐patient and outpatient contacts, using DRG cost weights. Each DRG was assigned a unit cost of NOK47 742 in accordance with the 2022 national DRG‐pricing list issued by the Norwegian Directorate of Health [20]. All costs are reported in Euros, using the exchange rate from 1 June 2022 (1 Euro = 10.044 NOK), corresponding to the midpoint of the trial's inclusion period [21]. No inflation adjustment was applied.

Lacking cost data beyond the study period, we assumed that future ASCVD/smoking‐related costs would equal the mean cost observed during the final 6 months of follow‐up (€2955 for 6 months and €5910 for 1 year). Both life‐years and costs were discounted at an annual rate of 4%, in accordance with guidelines from the Norwegian Ministry of Finance [22].

Health outcomes

As we did not collect data on health‐related quality of life (QALY), we used life‐years as the measure of health benefit. To estimate long‐term consequences of the intervention, we developed a survival model to extrapolate life expectancy and hospital‐related costs beyond the 16‐month observation period. The extrapolation model did not incorporate smoking status beyond the observed follow‐up period, and no explicit relapse rate was applied. For each patient, we estimated remaining life expectancy from the end of the observation period using age‐ and sex‐specific mortality rates from Statistics Norway [23]. To account for increased mortality risk following an ASCVD event, we applied sex‐specific relative risk multipliers (1.8 for women and 1.2 for men), based on 5‐year mortality data from a large Swedish registry study [24]. Patients who died during the 16‐month follow‐up were assigned survival time according to the date of inclusion into the study and death, and a remaining life expectancy of zero beyond end of follow‐up.

Cost‐effectiveness

We estimated the distribution of costs and life‐years using non‐parametric bootstrapping (2000 replications) to quantify uncertainty around cost and effect estimates [25]. Uncertainty estimates (95% UE) were defined as the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution. The incremental cost and effect were defined as the difference in discounted costs and life expectancy between groups. The incremental cost‐effectiveness ratio was calculated as the ratio of incremental cost to incremental life‐years gained. Abstinence status was not used as an input in the cost‐effectiveness model. We assessed cost‐effectiveness using the net monetary benefit with a willingness‐to‐pay threshold of €38 346 (NOK385 000) per life‐year gained, in line with a Norwegian report [26]. We presented the bootstrapping estimates in a cost‐effectiveness plane and summarised them using a cost‐effectiveness acceptability curve across a range of willingness‐to‐pay thresholds, including Norwegian reference values of €27 378 and €82 139 per QALY.

Analytical methods

For analyses of empirical data from the 16‐month follow‐up period, we used Welch's t tests to estimate absolute cost differences and corresponding 95% CI, because this approach is robust to unequal variances and skewed data. As a robustness check, we also conducted non‐parametric bootstrapping for selected cost comparisons to assess whether the results and uncertainty estimates differed substantially. Because bootstrapped and t test‐based estimates were similar, we report Welch's t test results for absolute cost differences. Relative cost differences (e.g. percentage difference) were analysed using bootstrapping to estimate 95% CI. Statistical analyses were performed in Stata (18.0), Excel and R version 4.2.1.

Subgroup analyses

We conducted cost analyses of rehospitalisations for four predefined diagnostic subgroups considered smoking‐related in this study: vascular disease (peripheral artery and aortic disease), coronary heart disease, respiratory infections and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and cerebrovascular disease. These analyses aimed to explore differences in healthcare resource use and potential cost savings across clinically relevant subgroups in which smoking is a major contributing risk factor. In addition, we assessed lifetime cost‐effectiveness stratified by sex.

Socio‐economic and smoking‐related characteristics reflected a high‐risk population of whom 75.7% had low educational attainment, 64.3% reported smoking more than 10 cigarettes per day and 54.2% had smoked for over 40 years. Smoking cessation outcomes

Smoking cessation outcomes are summarised in Table S1. Smoking cessation rates were double in the intervention group compared with the control group at the 6‐month follow‐up (adjusted OR = 3.08, 95% CI = 1.70–5.55; P < 0.001). At 12 months, self‐reported abstinence remained higher in the intervention group (45.7% vs. 27.2%; adjusted OR = 2.38, 95% CI = 1.29–4.27) compared with the control group.

Health outcomes

Over the 16‐month follow‐up period, 14 patients died, five in the intervention group and nine in the control group. The mean number of life days was 475.6 in the intervention group and 457.9 in the control group, with a difference of 17.7 days (95% CI = −4.65 to 40.05).

The estimated undiscounted life expectancy from study inclusion was 20.48 years (95% UE = 17.45–24.17) in the intervention group and 18.74 years (95% UE = 15.92–22.58) in the control group, (discounted: 13.54 and 12.47 years, respectively). The difference in life expectancy was 1.73 years (95% UE = −1.86 to 6.12) (discounted 1.06 years (95% UE = −0.72 to 3.31)). The differences were 2.26 (95% UE = −2.00 to 6.78) for women and 1.21 (95% UE = −1.76 to 4.08) for men, with discounted estimates of 1.39 and 0.74 years, respectively.

Intervention costs

The total intervention cost was €97.0 per patient in the intervention group, compared with €16.2 in the control group (Table 2). The cost difference was mainly driven by the additional components of the nurse‐led counselling and follow‐up procedure.

Healthcare utilisation and costs

During the 16‐month follow‐up period, patients in the intervention group had fewer hospital contacts compared with those in the control group (mean = 8.7 v. 9.6; difference = −0.9, 95% CI = −3.75 to 1.95) (Table S1). This encompassed slightly fewer outpatient visits (mean = 6.9 vs. 8.0; difference = −1.1, 95% CI = −3.40 to 1.20), but a comparable number of in‐patient stays (mean = 1.8 vs. 1.6; difference = 0.2, 95% CI = −1.03 to 1.43) (Table S1).

For the pre‐specified smoking‐related diseases, vascular disease (peripheral and aortic disease), coronary heart disease, respiratory infections and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and cerebrovascular disease, the intervention group had fewer hospital contacts overall (mean = 4.5 vs. 6.0; difference = −1.5, 95% CI = −3.22 to 0.22), fewer outpatient visits (mean = 3.7 vs. 4.8; difference = −1.1, 95% CI = −2.59 to 0.39) and slightly fewer in‐patient stays (mean = 0.8 vs. 1.1; difference = −0.3, 95% CI = −0.78 to 0.18) (Table S1).

The mean per‐patient cost of rehospitalisations over the 16‐month observation period was €9114 in the intervention group compared with €13 973 in the control group (difference = –€4859; 95% CI = –€11 232 to €1514) (Table 3). Subgroup analysis of hospital costs for smoking‐related diseases indicated the greatest difference in vascular disease (peripheral artery and aortic disease) (€1587 vs. €4515) and coronary heart disease (€2361 vs. €3231) (Table 3). Cost‐effectiveness

Figure 1 depicts the cost‐effectiveness plane with 2000 bootstrapped replications of discounted costs and life‐years gained. Most replications fall below the Norwegian willingness‐to‐pay thresholds of €27 378 and €82 139 per QALY (NOK275 000 and NOK825 000). Most points are concentrated in the north‐east quadrant (Figure 1), indicating higher costs and greater effectiveness of the intervention compared to control, although some replications fell below zero incremental cost. The incremental discounted total cost was €3280 (95% UE = −€19 457 to €26 436) per patient and the incremental discounted total life‐years were +1.06 (95% UE = −0.71 to 3.31). This resulted in an incremental cost‐effectiveness ratio of €3094 per life‐year gained.

DISCUSSION

Among hospitalised patients with ASCVD, a nurse‐led intervention combining in‐hospital motivational counselling with proactive referral to community‐based follow‐up was associated with lower mortality and reduced hospital‐related costs over 16 months. In lifetime projections, discounted life expectancy from baseline was 1.06 years higher in the intervention group, while discounted costs were €3280 higher because of increased survival. In a Norwegian context, the intervention would be deemed cost‐effective from a hospital perspective.

To our knowledge, this is the first randomised trial to report the cost‐effectiveness of a hospital‐based smoking cessation intervention using patient‐level hospital cost data and empirical follow‐up exceeding 12 months. In our trial, fewer hospital readmissions and outpatient visits were the main contributors to the observed cost differences. In line with this, an older randomised clinical trial, which demonstrated that an intensive smoking cessation programme for hospitalised patients with cardiovascular disease significantly increased abstinence rates and reduced rehospitalisations and all‐cause mortality over 2 years compared with usual care [27]. Moreover, a recent Cochrane review of 68 studies confirmed that quitting smoking reduces the risk of cardiovascular death and major adverse cardiovascular events by approximately one‐third in people with established coronary heart disease [28]. Collectively, these findings align with the notion that successful cessation is likely to reduce the risk of recurrent events and associated costs.

In our study, self‐reported abstinence at 6 months was 49.5% in the intervention group compared with 24.5% in the control group. This effect is likely in part mediated by increased participation in community‐based smoking cessation programmes, which is consistent with previous research on proactive referral strategies [29]. In line with this, Levy et al. [30] demonstrated a favourable cost per quitter among patients with cardiovascular disease who received an intervention comprising voice‐response‐facilitated outreach, telephone‐based motivational counselling, free cessation drugs for 6 weeks and access to municipal support. Although their intervention was delivered in an outpatient setting, our findings indicate that initiating motivational counselling and proactive referral during ASCVD hospitalisation may reduce rehospitalisation costs and improve long‐term cost‐effectiveness. By proactively connecting patients with no‐cost community‐based cessation support, our intervention may help reach individuals who might otherwise face financial or logistical barriers to quitting. This is consistent with findings by Thao et al. [31], who demonstrated that proactive outreach among socio‐economically disadvantaged smokers increased treatment uptake and was cost‐effective compared to usual care.

In contrast to more resource‐intensive interventions with repeated counselling by hospital staff and centralised follow‐up, our intervention facilitated continuity of care with a modest use of hospital resources [11, 12, 13, 27]. Although clinically beneficial, interventions that require more extensive use of hospital staff may face scalability challenges in settings where there are competing demands for in‐patient care. In a study from The Netherlands, Berndt et al. [13] conducted a comprehensive health economic evaluation from a societal perspective. The analysis indicated that both face‐to‐face and telephone counselling were cost‐effective for hospitalised patients with coronary heart disease [13]. However, their study was limited by a 6‐month follow‐up period and did not report QALY gains. In contrast, our trial used empirical patient‐level cost data over a longer time horizon, extrapolating life‐years gained to a lifetime perspective. Together, these studies indicate that hospital‐initiated smoking cessation interventions with structured post‐discharge follow‐up are cost‐effective. Life‐year gains in this high‐risk population are broadly consistent with generic estimates reported for smoking cessation in the general population, such as those described by Stapleton and West [32], although the populations, modelling approaches and outcome definitions differ and the estimates are, therefore, not directly comparable.

Our exploratory subgroup analyses suggest that approximately 60% of the costs related to rehospitalisations in the intervention group and 70% in the control group were attributable to smoking‐related diseases [e.g. coronary heart disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, respiratory infections, vascular disease (peripheral artery and aortic disease) and cerebrovascular disease]. The largest cost differences were observed in patients with peripheral artery disease and aortic conditions. Although based on small numbers, these findings align with prior observational studies reporting high healthcare costs associated with continued smoking in this population [33, 34, 35]. Further randomised trials are needed to assess the cost‐effectiveness of cessation interventions, particularly for patients with peripheral artery and aortic disease.

National health data from Norway indicate that approximately 16% of all somatic hospital admissions in adults over 20 years old are because of cardiovascular disease [36]. Assuming a smoking prevalence of 25% in this group and similar intervention effectiveness, national implementation could help approximately 8600 patients quit smoking each year, resulting in an estimated gain of 9100 life‐years annually. Eurostat data report 8.6 million cardiovascular disease discharges annually, with 4 to 5 million for ASCVD alone [37]. Assuming a prevalence of daily smoking of 25% at admission, this amounts to more than 1 million patients annually or 13 000 life‐years gained each year. Although these projections are illustrative, they highlight the substantial public health potential of embedding structured cessation support into hospital care. This aligns with recent forecasts from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021, which estimated that eliminating tobacco use from 2023 could, by 2050, avert approximately 2 billion cumulative years of life lost globally and increase life expectancy in the general population by up to 4 years [38].

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study lies in the randomised design, embedded within routine hospital practice. Extrapolation of life‐years gained to a lifetime horizon provides decision‐relevant estimates aligned with international guidelines for economic evaluations [39, 40].

The study also has limitations. Although randomisation was strictly followed, some baseline differences between groups were observed, likely because of chance. Hospital costs were estimated using diagnosis related group weights, which may not fully reflect actual resource use and costs. We assumed that future hospital‐related costs would be constant over time and equal to the average costs observed during the final 6 months of follow‐up. Similarly, mortality extrapolations relied on relative risks from a Swedish registry study, applied uniformly to all patients. Smoking status was collected up to 12 months in the parent randomised trial, but long‐term smoking behaviour was not incorporated in the extrapolation model. We did not model post‐trial relapse explicitly. Survival beyond the 16‐month observation period was projected using identical age‐, sex‐ and ASCVD‐adjusted mortality rates in both groups. If smoking behaviour diverges further between groups after follow‐up, long‐term life expectancy estimates may be overestimated or underestimated. Consistent with national practice, we included future costs related to ASCVD, but not unrelated health conditions (e.g. dementia or cancer), which may have led to conservative estimates. We used life‐years as measure of benefit rather than QALY, as quality‐of‐life data were not collected in our trial. Although this approach may underestimate broader quality‐of‐life benefits of cessation, survival‐based models may also overestimate long‐term gains as our patient group has reduced quality‐of‐life because of the cardiovascular disease and co‐morbidities. Because of lack of data, our study did not include costs for pharmaceuticals (both in‐hospital and post‐discharge), primary care, nursing home stays or home‐care services. Because the analysis was conducted from a hospital perspective, costs associated with participation in the municipal Healthy Life Centre programme were not included in the base‐case analysis. However, a sensitivity analysis incorporating these costs had only a minor effect on the ICER and did not change the conclusion that the intervention was cost‐effective. Similarly, use of nicotine replacement therapy was not included in the economic analysis because we did not collect data on initiation during hospitalisation. Pharmacotherapy could be prescribed as part of usual clinical care, but was not systematically delivered or funded by the study team. The proportion of patients who initiated treatment during admission is, therefore, unknown and represents a limitation. Finally, the wide CIs around both cost and effect estimates reflect considerable variability in hospital use between patients, as well as uncertainty because of low mortality and event rates. The main limitation of this study, however, lies in its external validity and the uncertainty about whether personnel in routine practice will have the same level of enthusiasm and motivation as in a randomised trial. Although the 16‐month follow‐up period exceeds that of many previous trials, future research should include longer‐term follow‐up with real‐world cost data, QALY‐based modelling and implementation studies to confirm and extend these findings.

CONCLUSION

This randomised trial of patients with ASCVD indicates that a single session of in‐hospital, nurse‐led smoking cessation counselling based on motivational interviewing, combined with proactive referral to post‐discharge follow‐up, has a high probability of cost‐effectiveness from a hospital perspective.
