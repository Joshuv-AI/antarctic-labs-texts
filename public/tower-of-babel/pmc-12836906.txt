Effectiveness of an evidence-based intervention bundle in reducing post-caesarean delivery surgical site infections: systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Tirusew Nigussie Kebede, Kidist Ayalew Abebe, Birhan Tsegaw Taye, Tenaw Belay Shitie, Agmuas Asichale Alimawu, Molla Amsalu Tadesse, Tebabere Moltot, Moges Sisay Chekole, Solomon Hailemeskel Beshah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCaesarean section (CS) is among the most frequently performed surgical procedures worldwide and is critical for reducing maternal and neonatal mortality. However, surgical site infections (SSIs) remain one of the most common postoperative complications, imposing substantial maternal morbidity, delaying recovery, and indirectly affecting neonatal health. Despite various infection prevention measures, the burden of SSIs is particularly high in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Emerging evidence suggests that implementing evidence-based care bundles can markedly reduce SSIs, but existing studies are fragmented and inconsistent, underscoring the need for a comprehensive synthesis.ObjectiveTo evaluate the effectiveness of evidence-based care bundles in preventing SSIs following caesarean delivery.MethodsThis systematic review and meta-analysis followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines (PROSPERO registration: CRD42024603854). A comprehensive search was conducted in PubMed, Cochrane Library, Scopus, Hinari, and Google Scholar using a PICO-based strategy. Eligible studies were critically appraised using the revised Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) tools. Heterogeneity was assessed using the Galbraith plot, I² statistics, and Cochran’s Q test. Publication bias was examined with funnel plots and Egger’s test. A fixed-effects model was applied to calculate pooled risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsA total of 21 studies involving 32,709 women who underwent CS were included. Pooled analysis demonstrated that implementation of evidence-based intervention bundles significantly reduced the incidence of post-CS SSIs from 5% (95% CI: 1–8%) before intervention to nearly 0% (95% CI: 0–1%) after intervention (RR: 0.29, 95% CI: 0.27–0.31). This represents a 71% relative reduction in SSI risk. In absolute terms, bundle implementation translated into thousands of preventable infections and avoided complications across diverse settings.ConclusionEvidence-based bundles are a highly effective, practical, and adaptable strategy for reducing SSIs following caesarean delivery. Their integration into routine obstetric care can substantially improve maternal recovery, support early neonatal care including breastfeeding, and decrease healthcare costs associated with prolonged hospital stays. Adoption of such bundles should be prioritized globally, with particular urgency in LMICs where the burden of post-CS SSIs is highest.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08577-x.Keywords: Caesarean delivery, Surgical site infection, Evidence-Based bundle, Infection prevention

Introduction

Cesarean section is a critical surgical intervention that, when performed for medically indicated reasons, can significantly reduce maternal and neonatal mortality and morbidity [1]. It is one of the most frequently performed surgical procedures globally (accounting for as much as 80% of the surgical workload) [2]. As such, cesarean delivery is recognized as an essential strategy in efforts to reduce maternal and perinatal deaths [3, 4]. Given that approximately 15% of all pregnancies are expected to experience obstetric complications, it is essential that at least these high-risk deliveries take place in health facilities equipped to cesarean Sects [5, 6]. Although the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends an optimal cesarean section rate of 10% to 15%, with the aim that no country exceeds this range, global rates have continued to rise far beyond these thresholds, increasing from approximately 6% in 1990 to over 21% in recent years [1, 4].

While cesarean section can reduce adverse maternal and perinatal outcomes, it is also associated with various complications particularly infections during the postpartum periods with a risk that is 5 to 20 times higher compared to vaginal birth [7, 8]. Peri-partum infections related to cesarean section commonly include wound or incision site infections, endometritis, and urinary tract infections. Less common but more severe complications include pelvic abscesses, bacteremia, septic shock, necrotizing fasciitis, and septic pelvic vein thrombophlebitis, all of which can be life-threatening [9]. Post‑cesarean surgical site infection (CS-SSI) refers to any infection manifesting within 30 days of a cesarean delivery at or adjacent to the surgical site, which may affect superficial tissues, deeper layers including fascia and muscle, or internal organs and anatomical spaces involved in the procedure [10]. SSIs are a major cause of postoperative morbidity and mortality, contributing to prolonged hospital stays, increased healthcare costs, and adverse maternal outcomes [11]. The global incidence of post-cesarean SSIs is 5.63%, with the highest rates reported in Africa (11.91%) and the lowest in North America (3.87%) [12, 13]. In Sub-Saharan Africa, the incidence of surgical site infection among women who underwent cesarean delivery was 12.4% [14, 15].

Although individual preventive measures such as timely antibiotic prophylaxis, proper skin preparation, and strict adherence to aseptic techniques are effective in reducing SSIs, their impact is often modest when implemented in isolation [16–19]. In contrast, bundled approaches that combine several evidence-based interventions into a standardized protocol have emerged as a more effective strategy, producing synergistic benefits that exceed the sum of individual measures [20, 21]. The surgical bundles defined as groups of standardized, evidence-based practices implemented concurrently have been widely adopted across multiple surgical specialties and consistently shown to reduce infection rates [22]. Successful applications have been reported in cesarean section, colorectal surgery, and pediatric hepatic surgery, where bundled approaches were associated with significant reductions in SSI incidence [21, 23, 24]. Recognizing this impact, the WHO has incorporated bundle component into its Global Guidelines for the Prevention of SSI, which synthesize high-quality evidence on individual components and advocate for their integrated use in a standardized approach to SSI prevention [25]. Evidence from high-income countries and selected middle-income contexts, including India and Egypt, has demonstrated that care bundles can substantially lower the incidence of post-cesarean SSIs, leading to improved maternal outcomes and reduced healthcare costs [19, 26–28].

Implementation of multiple evidence-based perioperative interventions has been shown to substantially reduce the incidence of SSI following cesarean delivery. Individual practices such as preoperative vaginal cleansing with povidone-iodine or chlorhexidine solution, administration of adjunct antibiotics (e.g., azithromycin 500 mg IV in addition to conventional prophylaxis such as cefazolin or ampicillin), and maintenance of intraoperative normothermia have each demonstrated significant protective effects against postoperative infections following caesarean delivery in several randomized and observational studies [29–31].

Implementation of three or more evidence-based perioperative practices has been shown to significantly reduce the incidence of surgical site infection (SSI) following cesarean delivery. These practices include the intravenous administration of ceftriaxone (1 g) combined with azithromycin (500 mg) given 60 min before surgical incision (or at least 15 min prior in emergency cases), abdominal scrubbing with 2% chlorhexidine solution before surgery, and vaginal cleansing with 10% betadine solution. In the operating theater, chlorhexidine scrub was used as the antiseptic for the surgical site, while povidone-iodine and ethanol-based agents were not applied. Additional evidence-based components of the bundle included placenta removal by controlled cord traction to minimize uterine contamination, wound dressing removal at 48 h postoperatively, and withholding postoperative antibiotics unless a woman developed signs of SSI, as recommended by CDC guidelines. Furthermore, intraoperative normothermia was maintained to enhance tissue oxygenation and immune function during surgery. Collectively, the synergistic implementation of these practices has been demonstrated in several studies to markedly decrease the incidence of SSI following cesarean deliveries compared with isolated infection prevention measures [20, 28, 32–35].

These encouraging results highlight the potential of care bundles for broader implementation across health systems. However, the existing evidence base is fragmented, inconsistently reported, and predominantly derived from high-income countries where health system capacity and resource availability differ substantially from LMICs. Consequently, the applicability of these findings to resource-constrained settings remains uncertain. Consolidating this scattered evidence is essential to generate precise pooled estimates of effectiveness, and provide robust insights for policymakers and clinicians, particularly in LMICs where the burden is greatest. Addressing this gap is critical for guiding the adaptation and scale-up of evidence-based care bundles in diverse health system contexts, with the ultimate goal of reducing preventable maternal morbidity and mortality following cesarean delivery. Therefore, a systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted to determine whether the application of bundled practices reduces the rate of surgical site infections (SSI) after cesarean delivery.

Methods

Search strategies

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines and was prospectively registered on PROSPERO (registration ID: CRD42024603854; https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/recorddashboard) (S1). A comprehensive search was conducted across major international electronic databases, including PubMed, Cochrane Library, Scopus, Google Scholar, and Hinari, to identify relevant studies. In addition, reference lists of included articles and relevant reviews were manually screened to capture studies not indexed in the primary databases. The search was guided by the Population, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcome (PICO) framework: the population comprised women undergoing cesarean section, the intervention was evidence-based care bundles (defined as a set of three to five interventions implemented simultaneously to improve clinical outcomes), the comparison included women receiving standard or usual care (including pre-implementation care in before-and-after study designs or control groups in quasi-experimental and randomized controlled trials), and the outcome was post-cesarean section surgical site infection (SSI). The review addressed the research question: “Does implementation of an evidence-based intervention bundle reduce the incidence of post-cesarean section SSI?” For each PICO component, tailored search strategies were developed using relevant keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms, combined with Boolean operators “AND” and “OR” to ensure both sensitivity and specificity (S2).

Eligibility criteria

This systematic review included studies conducted in any region of the world that evaluated the effectiveness of implementing bundle care interventions in reducing post-caesarean section surgical site infections (post-CS SSIs). Eligible studies were those published in English between January 1, 2015, and August 15, 2025, and that incorporated a control or comparison group, including randomized controlled trials, quasi-experimental studies with controls, before-after studies with comparators, and cohort studies. Intervention bundles were defined as the application of at least three evidence-based perioperative infection prevention strategies implemented consistently during a single clinical episode, such as but not limited to timely administration of prophylactic antibiotics, chlorhexidine-based skin preparation, vaginal cleansing, pre-operative showering or wiping with chlorhexidine, maintenance of intraoperative normothermia, controlled cord traction for placenta delivery, closure of subcutaneous fat when thickness exceeded 2 cm, subcuticular skin closure, or other evidence-based practices recommended in SSI prevention guidelines [36, 37]. Studies were required to report the incidence of post-CS SSIs using the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) criteria. Studies were excluded if they lacked a control or comparator group, failed to report the primary outcome of interest (incidence of post-CS SSIs), implemented fewer than three bundled interventions, or were deemed low quality based on standardized critical appraisal tools.

Study selection/screening process

All studies identified through searches of major international databases and search engines were exported into EndNote version 20 for reference management and systematic screening. Duplicate records were identified and removed using the software, followed by manual verification. Study selection was conducted independently by four reviewers (TNK, SHB, AT, and TM) according to the predefined eligibility criteria. Screening was performed in three stages: initial title screening, abstract screening, and full-text review. Any disagreements at each stage were first resolved through discussion among the reviewers, and when consensus could not be reached, a fifth reviewer was consulted to make the final decision.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was independently assessed by three reviewers (T.B.S., A.A., and B.T.T.) using the revised Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tools, tailored to each study design. Specifically, the JBI checklist for cohort studies (11 items), randomized controlled trials (13 items), and quasi-experimental studies (9 items) was employed to evaluate methodological rigor, risk of bias, and overall validity [38–40]. Each criterion was rated as “yes,” “no,” “unclear,” or “not applicable,” and the quality score was calculated based on the number of “yes” responses, with maximum possible scores of 11, 13, and 9, respectively, depending on the study design. Studies were classified as good quality when they met the majority of the relevant JBI criteria and demonstrated minimal risk of bias, confounding, or other significant methodological limitations [38–40].

Data extraction

To ensure the validity and reliability of the review findings, only primary studies classified as good quality were included in the data extraction process. Three reviewers (TNK, KAA, and BTT) independently extracted data using a standardized form developed in Microsoft Excel. Extracted variables included the first author’s name, year of publication, study design, study setting, study period, total sample size, and the sample sizes of the intervention and control groups. Outcome-related data included the number of post-CS SSIs and non-SSIs before/control/comparators and the intervention groups, the incidence of post-CS SSIs before/control/comparators and the intervention group, and effect size estimates such as relative risk (RR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. Following initial extraction, another three reviewers independently rechecked the data to confirm that all information was accurately and appropriately captured from the original studies. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and verification with the original articles. Finally, all extracted data were cross-checked for accuracy and consistency before being exported into STATA version 17 for statistical analysis.

Data synthesis and analysis

Data from the included studies were synthesized quantitatively using meta-analysis to estimate the overall effect of intervention bundles on post-caesarean section surgical site infections. Pooled estimates were calculated using relative risk (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical heterogeneity across studies was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test and quantified with the I² statistic, where values of 25%, 50%, and 75% represented low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively [41]. In addition, Galbraith plots were used to further explore potential of heterogeneity. As the included studies demonstrated no significant heterogeneity (I² ≤ 50% and p > 0.05), a fixed-effects model was applied to derive pooled effect sizes [42]. Forest plots were generated to present individual study estimates alongside the overall pooled RR. Publication bias was assessed both visually, using funnel plots, and statistically, using Egger’s regression test, with a p-value < 0.05 indicating potential bias. Sensitivity analyses were performed using leave-one-out procedures to evaluate the influence of individual studies on the pooled estimate, and results confirmed that no single study substantially altered the overall findings. All analyses were performed in STATA version 17.

Result

Study selection and identification

This systematic review and meta-analysis systematically identified published studies evaluating the effectiveness of evidence-based bundle care interventions for the prevention of post-caesarean section surgical site infections. A comprehensive search was conducted across multiple international electronic databases, yielding a total of 5,903 records. Following the removal of 2,428 duplicate records, 3,475 articles remained for title and abstract screening, of which 3,357 were excluded as they did not meet the predefined eligibility criteria. The remaining 118 articles underwent full-text assessment for eligibility. Of these, 97 studies were excluded because they did not report the primary outcomes of interest, reason 1: focused on bundle care in general surgery settings (e.g., colorectal, hepatic, neurosurgery), reason 2: evaluated only single interventions or partial bundles, such as isolated vaginal cleansing or antibiotic prophylaxis, rather than a combination of at least three evidence-based perioperative practices, reason 3: some of the identified studies only reports incidence of post CS-SSI with unknown intervention, and reason 4: some study lacked an appropriate control or comparator group. Ultimately, 21 studies met all inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the final quantitative meta-analysis. The study selection process is presented following the PRISMA guidelines for transparent reporting of systematic reviews (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of the included studies

A total of 21 studies met the inclusion criteria for this systematic review, enrolling 32,709 women undergoing caesarean delivery, of whom 14,045 were allocated to control groups and 17,506 received bundle-of-care interventions. In terms of study design, methodological diversity was observed: four randomized controlled trials (RCTs), four quasi-experimental studies with control groups, ten pre-post (before-after) studies, and three cohort studies. This distribution reflects a predominance of quasi-experimental, consistent with the implementation nature of bundle interventions, but also highlights the presence of higher-level evidence through RCTs.

Geographically, the studies spanned nine countries across four continents. The majority were conducted in the United States (n = 10), while others were undertaken in India (n = 3), Ireland (n = 2), Australia (n = 2), Egypt (n = 1), the United Kingdom (n = 1), and Israel (n = 1). This wide distribution suggests growing global recognition of bundled interventions as a promising strategy for reducing SSIs following caesarean section. The effectiveness of bundles was consistently reported across all included studies, with marked reductions in post-CS SSI incidence following implementation. Notably, the interventions implemented by Nouh et al. (2025, Egypt) and Vacca et al. (2019, USA) achieved near-elimination of SSIs post-intervention, underscoring the potential of well-designed, context-specific bundles to achieve optimal outcomes. Although minimal variation existed in the composition of bundles (reflecting local adaptation to guidelines, resource availability, and baseline post CS-SSI rates), the direction of effect was uniform: almost all studies demonstrated that bundle-of-care interventions were superior to standard or routine care in reducing post-CS SSI incidence. The consistent findings across diverse designs, settings, and health systems provide compelling evidence that bundled, evidence-based practices constitute an effective strategy for SSI prevention following caesarean delivery (Table 1).

The impact of evidence-based care bundles on post-caesarean surgical site infection reduction

This systematic review and meta-analysis of 21 studies demonstrated that implementation of an evidence-based bundle of care significantly reduced the incidence of post CS-SSI. The pooled relative risk (RR) was 0.29 (95% CI: 0.27–0.31), corresponding to a 71% reduction in SSI following cesarean delivery (Fig. 2). These findings indicate that adherence to a standardized bundle of preventive measures is highly effective in minimizing post-operative infections. Although the majority of included studies were conducted in high-income countries, the interventions involved are generally low-cost, feasible, and adaptable. Therefore, scaling up these strategies in LMICs could substantially benefit maternal and neonatal health by reducing morbidity associated with SSI. Moreover, minimizing infection rates can decrease the financial burden on health systems, particularly in contexts where maternal services are provided free of charge, further supporting the cost-effectiveness of implementing such bundles in resource-limited settings.

Heterogeneity assessment

Heterogeneity among the included studies was assessed both graphically and statistically. The Galbraith plot (Fig. 3) demonstrated that all studies were distributed symmetrically on both sides of the regression line and remained within the bounds of the 95% confidence interval, indicating consistent effect estimates across studies and no significant outliers, suggesting overall homogeneity of effect sizes. Statistical evaluation further confirmed this finding. Statistical evaluation using the I² statistic revealed an estimated heterogeneity of 34.57%, which is interpreted as low to moderate heterogeneity. The corresponding Cochran’s Q test yielded a p-value of 0.06, which is not statistically significant, further supporting the absence of meaningful heterogeneity. Taken together, both the graphical (Galbraith plot) and statistical (I² and Q test) analyses indicate that the results of this meta-analysis are sufficiently homogeneous, thereby justifying the use of a fixed-effect model in the pooled analysis.

Galbraith plot illustrating the distribution of individual study effect sizes around the regression line with 95% confidence limits, for the effectiveness of evidence-based care bundles in reducing post-cesarean SSI

Subgroup analysis

Although no statistically significant heterogeneity was detected among the included studies, a subgroup analysis based on study design was performed to further explore the source of the minor heterogeneity observed in the overall effect. Studies were categorized as follows: non-randomized controlled quasi-experimental design [1], non-controlled quasi-experimental pre-post/before-after design [2], cohort study design [3], and randomized controlled trial of any type [4]. The pooled effects were remarkably consistent across all subgroups, with risk ratios ranging narrowly from 0.29 to 0.30, indicating that evidence-based care bundles reduced post-cesarean surgical site infections by approximately 70% irrespective of study design or methodological approach. Although moderate heterogeneity was observed within the non-randomized controlled quasi-experimental subgroup (I² = 79.29%), this variability cannot be explained by differences in study methods, as the included studies were comparable in terms of intervention components, control groups, and follow-up periods. Despite this heterogeneity, the direction and magnitude of effect remained strongly in favor of the intervention and aligned closely with findings from more rigorous study designs. Importantly, the test for subgroup differences was not statistically significant (Q b [3] = 0.13, p = 0.99), demonstrating that study design did not meaningfully contribute to between-study variation (Fig. 4).

Subgroup analysis by study design evaluating the effectiveness of evidence-based care bundles in reducing post-cesarean surgical site infections and exploring sources of minor heterogeneity among included studies

Overall, the consistent effect sizes across diverse designs reinforce the robustness and reliability of the intervention’s effectiveness and suggest that the minor heterogeneity detected in the pooled analysis is unlikely to be attributable to study design differences.

Publication bias

Potential publication bias was assessed both graphically and statistically. Visual inspection of the funnel plot (Fig. 5) demonstrated a symmetrical distribution of effect estimates around the pooled effect size (θiv), with no apparent asymmetry suggestive of selective publication bias. This visual impression was verified by Egger’s regression test, which yielded a bias coefficient of −0.03 (p = 0.956). The non-significant result indicates no evidence of small-study effects and suggests that the overall findings are unlikely to be influenced by publication bias. Taken together, these results provide confidence that publication bias is not a major concern in this systematic review and meta-analysis.

Sensitivity analysis

A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the robustness of the pooled risk ratio and to determine whether any single study disproportionately influenced the overall meta-analytic result. The analysis involved iteratively removing each study and recalculating the pooled risk ratio for the remaining studies. The results demonstrate that the pooled risk ratio remains remarkably stable across all iterations, with values ranging from 0.284 to 0.304. The confidence intervals consistently exclude the null value (RR = 1.0) and remain narrow, with all p-values highly significant (p < 0.001). The overall pooled risk ratio of 0.294 (95% CI: 0.269–0.320) did not meaningfully change upon the removal of any individual study, indicating that no single study exerted undue influence on the meta-analytic result. This high degree of consistency confirms that the finding of a significant reduction in post CS-SSI following the implementation of the care bundle is robust and not dependent on any particular study included in the analysis. The stability of the effect across a diverse set of study designs and settings further strengthens the validity and generalizability of the pooled estimate, enhancing confidence in the conclusion that evidence-based care bundles are effective in reducing post-cesarean SSI (Table 2).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides strong evidence that implementation of evidence-based intervention bundles substantially reduces the risk of post-CS SSIs. Our findings demonstrate that applying a combined, multi-component bundle of care leads to a 71% reduction in the risk of SSI (RR: 0.29, 95% CI: 0.27, 0.31). In absolute terms, the pooled incidence of SSI decreased from 5% (95% CI: 1, 8%) before intervention to nearly zero (95% CI: 0, 1%) after bundle implementation. These results are consistent with previous systematic reviews. For instance, a meta-analysis of 14 studies published prior to 2017 reported a 67% reduction in SSI following caesarean delivery after the adoption of evidence-based bundles, with rates decreasing from 6.2% at baseline to 2% post-intervention [58]. The implication is clear: implementing a package of proven, evidence-based strategies rather than relying on single interventions in isolation offers significant protective effects against post-CS SSI. Importantly, this benefit is observed across both high-income countries and low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), underscoring the global applicability of bundled interventions [22, 59]. From a clinical and public health perspective, these findings are critical. Post-CS SSI not only prolongs hospital stays and increases healthcare costs but also exposes women to avoidable pain, morbidity, and in some cases, mortality [60, 61]. By demonstrating that the risk can be reduced to near zero through systematic implementation of evidence-based bundles, this review highlights a practical and highly impactful strategy for safeguarding maternal health, preventing unnecessary complications, and improving the overall quality of obstetric care [58, 62].

The findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis are consistent with broader evidence from perioperative care bundle research across multiple surgical specialties, including general, gynecological, and caesarean procedures. A comprehensive meta-analysis of the above 18 studies demonstrated that implementation of perioperative bundles was associated with a standardized level reduction of −1.16 (95% CI: −1.78 to −0.53) [22]. This pooled effect indicates that evidence based bundles have a robust preventive impact on post-cesarean surgical site infections. Importantly, these findings resonate with the WHO 2018 Global Guidelines for the Prevention of Surgical Site Infection, which emphasize that a coordinated set of preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative strategies including timely prophylactic antibiotics, optimal skin preparation, perioperative normothermia, and evidence-based wound management was recommended to significantly reduce SSI risk across surgical disciplines [25]. The convergence of our results with such international guidance highlights that when interventions are applied collectively as structured bundles, they not only align with best practice standards but also deliver tangible reductions in maternal morbidity and mortalities associated with caesarean Sects [25, 34]. Taken together, this body of evidence provides compelling support for the routine integration of evidence-based perioperative bundles into obstetric surgical care as a practical, globally applicable strategy for safeguarding maternal health and improving quality of care [33, 50, 63].

The Anesthesia Patient Safety Foundation’s (APSF) June 2018 newsletter provides a comprehensive guide on preventing SSIs following cesarean delivery, emphasizing the critical role of anesthesia professionals in implementing evidence-based strategies. This guidance aligns with and reinforces the findings of this systematic review and meta-analyses, underscoring the effectiveness of structured care bundles in reducing SSIs in cesarean Sect [63]. The findings of this systematic review strongly support the CDC’s guideline, which strongly advocates for the implementation of a comprehensive combination of evidence-based strategies to prevent SSIs following caesarean deliveries [18]. By integrating interventions across the entire perioperative period ranging from appropriate preoperative antisepsis and timely prophylactic antibiotics to meticulous intraoperative technique and structured postoperative wound care the guideline emphasizes a multidimensional approach that maximizes infection prevention. This review reinforces that such a bundled, evidence-based approach not only aligns with international best practice but also translates into measurable improvements in patient safety and quality of care.

The findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis are strongly corroborated by a high-quality meta-analysis of 35 studies in colorectal surgery, which demonstrated that the implementation of three or more evidence-based strategies as part of a care bundle led to a substantial 44% reduction in the risk of SSIs [64]. Similarly, evidence from neurosurgical procedures supports this effect; a study conducted in Switzerland reported that the implementation of an evidence-based intervention bundle reduced SSIs following cranial neurosurgery by 53%, lowering infection rates from 7.8% in the pre-intervention period to 3.7% post-intervention [65]. This convergence of evidence across diverse surgical specialties highlights that structured, evidence-based care bundles are highly effective in reducing SSIs across diverse surgical contexts. This consistency of effect across both high- and low-risk procedures highlights the generalizability and critical importance of bundle implementation in improving surgical outcomes. These results provide compelling justification for the routine adoption of care bundle approaches in cesarean deliveries and other surgical procedures, reinforcing that the systematic implementation of evidence-based practices is a critical, feasible, and impactful strategy for improving surgical outcomes and patient safety globally.

Clinical implications

The findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrate that the implementation of evidence-based bundles for the prevention of post CS-SSIs has significant clinical relevance. Adoption of such bundles translates into a meaningful reduction in preventable post-operative complications, thereby improving maternal recovery and functional capacity following caesarean delivery. By reducing post CS-SSIs, mothers are more likely to regain their strength promptly, enabling them to care for their newborns and successfully initiate and sustain exclusive breastfeeding, which is essential for optimal neonatal health. Furthermore, prevention of post CS-SSIs reduces the risk of secondary nosocomial infections and eliminates the burden of unnecessary medical interventions and costs, thereby lowering maternal morbidity and mortality. In low-resource settings such as Ethiopia, where maternal health services are provided free of charge, the integration of evidence-based bundles offers a dual benefit: safeguarding women’s health while reducing unnecessary healthcare expenditures related to prolonged hospital stays and costly treatment of SSIs. This makes bundle implementation not only a clinically effective strategy but also a cost-saving and health system strengthening intervention.

Limitations

Although this systematic review synthesized evidence from major global databases and provides robust findings and conclusions, limitations should be acknowledged. Primarily, only studies published in the English language were included. This exclusion of studies published in other languages due to lack of language expertise among the reviewers may have introduced language bias and limited the inclusiveness and generalizability of the findings. Consequently, relevant evidence published in non-English sources might have been overlooked.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrates that the implementation of evidence-based bundles is a highly promising strategy for reducing surgical site infections following caesarean delivery and for safeguarding maternal and neonatal health. These strategies have been shown to be effective and adaptable across both high-income and low- and middle-income countries. Integrating evidence-based bundles into routine obstetric surgical care (caesarean deliveries) should be prioritized as a key policy recommendation to strengthen infection prevention efforts. In particular, low-income countries with a high burden of post-caesarean SSIs stand to benefit significantly from adopting such bundles, thereby reducing preventable maternal and neonatal morbidity, avoiding unnecessary healthcare costs, and ultimately improving quality of care by reducing the incidence of preventable SSI after caesarean delivery.
