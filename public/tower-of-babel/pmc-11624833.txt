SPOON: an observational, cross-sectional study of perceptions and expectations of adults with generalised myasthenia gravis in France
Authors: Jean-Philippe Camdessanché, Sabrina Sacconi, Annie Archer, Pierre Boulanger, Anne Crochard, Jean-Philippe Bertocchio, Aliénor Richard, Pierre-Edouard Villy, Guilhem Solé
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo capture patient perceptions about living with myasthenia gravis (MG) with respect to aspirations and ways to improve treatment.DesignOnline patient survey.SettingPatients recruited by MG patient associations or at MG reference treatment centres.ParticipantsAdults with physician-diagnosed generalised MG, living in France for ≥12 months, who had received ≥1 MG treatment were eligible. 291 patients opened the questionnaire, 255 were eligible and 246 completed the survey (age range 41–67 years; 187 women and 59 men).Primary and secondary outcome measuresPrimary: free-text response to the question ‘Living with your disease, what would you like to do that you currently find difficult or impossible to do?’ Secondary: free-text response to the question ‘What improvements do you think could be made to treatments for MG?’ Themes from replies to these questions were analysed using grounded theory and cluster analysis.ResultsFor the disease aspirations question, 617 citations were provided by 238 participants, which were grouped into 45 dimensions and six high-level domains (physical activity, activities of daily living, psychological burden, social activities, work/school and other). The most frequently cited dimensions were sport (82 citations), greater mobility (56 citations), being less tired (46 citations) and greater endurance (37 citations). Younger age, female gender, recent diagnosis and poorer quality of life were associated with citing more themes. For the treatment amelioration question, 263 citations were provided by 195 participants, which were grouped into 60 dimensions and three high-level domains (medication characteristics, safety and care paradigm). The most cited treatment-related dimensions were fewer side effects (40 citations), fewer daily medication intakes (21 citations) and fewer digestive side effects (20 citations).ConclusionsThese findings could help healthcare professionals to understand and better address patients’ aspirations about living with MG, notably concerning the importance of physical activity, and their expectations for improved treatments.Keywords: Quality of Life, Patient Reported Outcome Measures, Neuromuscular disease, Patient Satisfaction

Introduction

Myasthenia gravis (MG) is a rare autoimmune neuromuscular disease, with a prevalence believed to be in the range of 150–342 cases per million.1 2 This wide range may be explained by different study methodologies, including the diagnostic criteria used, the data source used (eg, dedicated studies, patient registries or administrative databases), real differences in prevalence between geographical regions, difficulties in capturing patients with milder disease and the difficulty inherent in determining epidemiological estimates with precision in rare diseases concerning low numbers of individuals.1 In MG, pathogenic autoantibodies trigger impairments in neuromuscular transmission, resulting in muscle weakness and abnormal muscular fatiguability.3 The disease course fluctuates with episodic exacerbations and myasthenic crises, which can be life-threatening owing to impaired functioning of respiratory muscles.3 People with MG frequently report significant central fatigue, with a lack of physical and mental energy, which has a negative impact on their quality of life and ability to engage in activities.4 Spoon theory is a metaphorical approach to chronic illnesses with central fatigue or chronic pain, which describes how patients have a finite number of ‘spoons’ of energy available for daily activities, how they need to plan in advance the best way to use these ‘spoons’ and how ’spoon accounting’ is impacted by fluctuations in disease symptoms.5

Several studies using established patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) have reported a high burden of MG in terms of impaired quality of life, major functional impairment, psychological distress, fatigue and stress.6,9 However, relatively little data is available from studies directly exploring patient perceptions of their disease and the impact of MG on their lives. A recent qualitative review of the experience of living with MG, based on published information and feedback from patient group discussion sessions, identified five key themes: fluctuating and unpredictable symptoms, a constant state of adaptation and trade-offs, treatment inertia, a sense of disconnection with healthcare professionals and psychological distress.10 The MyRealWorld-MG study is a longitudinal international digital survey of >800 patients with MG, in which patients complete a set of PROMs at monthly, quarterly or half-yearly intervals.11 12 In the baseline data published from MyRealWorld-MG, the domains in which patients with MG most frequently mentioned problems were usual activities, anxiety and depression, tiredness, breathing and vision.12

An aspect of the disease experience that cannot be addressed with these PROMs relates to patient aspirations about improving how they live with their disease. Developments in computer-assisted data mining allow this sort of information to be collected by asking patients open-ended questions with no a priori expectations for the answers and then structuring the content empirically. Although this approach has been applied successfully in other diseases, such as depression,13 patient aspirations have not been directly explored in MG, and this represents a critical gap in our available knowledge base. In spoon theory, these aspirations may be considered as the extra ‘spoons’ that would be most desirable for patients to have. We conducted an online qualitative study of a large sample of people with generalised MG (gMG) to understand their aspirations for their disease through open-ended questioning and used an empirical constructivist grounded theory approach to identify the most important themes.14

Objectives

The primary objective was to capture the perceptions and expectations of people with gMG about their disease, its impact and their aspirations for their lives. Secondary objectives were to capture the expectations of participants about their treatment and their perceptions of unmet medical needs and to describe the burden of disease using validated PROMs.

Methods

Design

The SPOON study (named after the spoon theory) was a cross-sectional observational study of adults with gMG performed in France, in the form of a web-based survey using an online questionnaire. The study was implemented by a contract research organisation specialising in collecting and analysing electronic data collected from patients (SKEZI) and overseen by a Steering Committee comprising MG experts, representatives of patient associations, representatives of the study sponsor (UCB Pharma) and representatives of the study manager (SKEZI). The survey was open from September 2022 to April 2023. Reporting of this study follows the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research guidelines.15

Patient and Public involvement

Representatives of patient associations have been involved throughout the study and are authors of this report. The study questionnaire was validated through interviews with patients. Data was collected from patients who were interested by the survey and gave their consent to take part in the study.

Participants

Participants were recruited either by the French MG patient associations (AFM-Téléthon Myasthenia Group and AMIS) or at MG reference treatment centres. Patients could also invite other patients to participate. The study survey was available through a hyperlink to a dedicated, secure server.

Any individual could open the survey and give their consent to participate. No identifying data were required to participate. To be eligible, participants were required to declare that they were aged >18 years or older, had lived in France for at least the previous 12 months, had been diagnosed with MG by a physician, had other disease manifestations in addition to ocular/visual symptoms and had taken at least one of the MG treatments of interest (photographs of packets of pills were provided). The following treatments were documented: pyridostigmine bromide, ambenonium chloride, corticosteroids, azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, ciclosporin, tacrolimus, cyclophosphamide, intravenous immunoglobulin and plasma exchange. Since participation was voluntary, participants were not expected to be representative of all patients with MG in France, and recruitment bias cannot be avoided.

Study questionnaire

The questionnaire was developed in French by the study’s Steering Committee. Its clarity and relevance were evaluated and validated by experts in a pilot survey of 10 patients. Participants were invited to complete the online questionnaire, which is summarised in table 1.

Following the screening questions, participants were asked how many treatments they took each day and to rate the constraints associated with their treatments on a numerical scale from 0 to 10. Then followed the first of the two open questions ‘In your opinion, how could myasthenia treatments be improved?’, a closed question on whether they were currently in a disease exacerbation phase, and the second open question ‘In your life with MG, what would you like to do that, at the moment, you have difficulty doing or can’t do?’ and a question about how they see the future, rated on a three-point Likert scale (optimist/pessimist/don’t want to answer).

In the next section of the questionnaire, five PROMs were completed by the patient, the revised 15-item Myasthenia Gravis Quality of Life Scale (MG-QoL-15r),16 the Myasthenia Gravis Activities of Daily Living Profile (MG-ADL),17 the fatigue item bank of the Quality of Life in Neurological Disorders measurement system (Neuro-QOL),18 the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS)19 and the Patient Acceptable Symptom State (PASS) question.20

Statistical analysis

Sample size determination

For the primary objective, the sample size was intended to be sufficient to reach satisfactory data saturation. Based on ecological research,21 100–150 participants were considered to be sufficient in most study settings. We estimated that, with the proposed recruitment strategy, it should be possible to recruit this number of participants over a 6-month period. To include as many participants as possible, enrolment was kept open after 150 participants had completed their questionnaires for the full 6 months.

Patient characteristics

Sociodemographic variables, disease-related variables and treatments prescribed are presented descriptively.

Analysis of data on PROMs

Data on the PROMs are presented as summary statistics according to the designers’ recommendations.16,20 Correlations between scores across participants were evaluated with the Pearson correlation coefficient.

Analysis of qualitative data

Data were analysed for the full eligible population and for men and women separately. Responses to the two open questions were explored with manual content analysis (grounded theory approach).1322,24 Number of citations were compared between participant subgroups using the χ2 test with a significance threshold of 0.05. Further details on this approach are provided in the online supplemental material.

The information is presented as a dendrogram, which presents a hierarchical grouping of the themes identified from the verbatim responses. Each unique theme identified is considered a dimension. These dimensions are organised into related groupings (subdomains and domains) that structure the information in the form of a tree, with the lowest branches being the domains and the leaves at the end of the branches the dimensions. Further details on this presentation are provided in the online supplemental material.

The analysis of the data was performed in French (the language of the survey), and then the names of the domains and dimensions were translated into English. The quality of the translation was assessed during a reconciliation meeting including French and English native speakers. Except for the examples used for illustrative purposes in this report, the verbatim citations collected were analysed in French and not translated into English.

Variables associated with the number of dimensions cited

Associations between certain patient characteristics and the number of themes identified in the qualitative analysis of the open question on patient aspirations about their disease were evaluated using Poisson regression analysis. The characteristics evaluated were gender, age, time since diagnosis, MG-ADL score, Neuro-QOL T-score and HADS anxiety and depression scores. Data are presented in the form of a Forest plot of the mean difference in the number of themes cited. The goodness-of-fit of the Poisson model was evaluated through the λ coefficient and the ratio of the Pearson χ² statistic.

Results

Study population

Overall, 291 individuals opened the questionnaire, of whom 255 were eligible. The principal reason for exclusion was ocular MG with no other symptoms present. The questionnaire was completed by 246 of 255 eligible participants. A patient flow diagram is presented in online supplemental figure 1. In all age groups, the majority of participants (154 of 219 participants who answered the question; 70.3%) were recruited through the patient associations. The distribution of recruitment modalities by age group is provided in online supplemental figure 2.

The characteristics of the participants are presented in table 2. The eligible population included 187 women and 59 men. The mean age was 54±16 years, with the men being older than the women (62±15 years vs 52±15 years, respectively). The time elapsed since diagnosis was >3 years for ≥70% of participants, irrespective of gender. Of the 177 participants of working age (<65 years), 94 (53.1%) were currently employed. Of 250 responders, 223 (89.2%) reported some impact of their MG on their professional activity, principally receipt of a disability pension relieving them of the need to work (n=76; 30.4%), and disabled worker status (n=98; 39.2%) entitling them to adapted working conditions and priority for certain reserved jobs.

Treatments

Overall, of the 255 participants with available data on treatment, 234 (91.8%) were taking at least one oral treatment every day, with a median number of daily medication intakes of 5 (IQR: 3–8). For the 11 participants (4.3%) taking parenteral treatments, the median number of daily injections was 1 (range: 1–4). The MG-specific medications taken at the time of the survey or previously are illustrated in online supplemental figure 3. All participants but one were currently or had previously been treated with an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor (AChE-Is); 31.8% were currently taking corticosteroids. Azathioprine was by far the most frequently used immunosuppressant, with 57.4% of participants reporting either current or previous use. Intravenous immunoglobulins or plasma exchanges had been administered to 74.1% of participants at least once.

Primary outcome: qualitative analysis of unfulfilled aspirations relating to the MG

Responses to the open-ended question on unfulfilled aspirations could be analysed for 238 participants (93.3%). Responses were classified into six domains and 45 dimensions (figure 1A): physical activities and leisure (six dimensions: 237 citations), daily life and well-being (16 dimensions: 170 citations), psychological burden associated with MG (10 dimensions; 80 citations), interactions with others (nine dimensions: 86 citations), professional and school activities (three dimensions; 36 citations) and no concerns (eight citations). The mean number of citations by each participant was 2.59 (total number: 617). The most frequently cited dimensions were doing more sport (93 citations), greater mobility (58 citations), being less tired (46 citations) and greater endurance (41 citations). Examples of verbatim citations from replies to the question are provided in online supplemental table 1.

Women cited more dimensions than men, with a mean of 2.86 citations each (512 citations by 179 women) versus 1.65 citations each for men (91 citations by 55 men). The nature of the dimensions and domains also differed between men and women (online supplemental figure 4). Notably, psychological burden dimensions accounted for 14.1% of citations by women (72 citations in all) and 6.6% of citations by men (six citations in all).

Secondary outcome: qualitative analysis of ways to improve treatment

Responses to the open-ended question on ways to improve treatment could be analysed for 195 participants (76.5%), providing a total of 263 citations (figure 1B). The mean number of citations by each participant was 1.35. These dimensions could be classified into four domains: medication properties (32 dimensions: 122 citations), side effects (14 dimensions: 91 citations), the care paradigm (13 dimensions: 43 citations) and satisfaction (one dimension; seven citations). The most frequently cited dimensions were limiting side effects (41 citations), limiting the number of pills taken daily (32 citations) and resolving digestive issues (20 citations). Examples of verbatim citations from replies to the question are provided in online supplemental table 2.

Women provided 213 citations in all (mean: 1.43 citations by 149 participants) and men provided 44 (mean: 1.07 citations by 41 participants). The nature of the dimensions and domains differed between men and women (online supplemental figure 5); for example, limiting side effects accounted for 17.4% of citations by women (37 citations) but only 4.5% of citations by men (two citations).

Secondary outcome: patient-reported outcome measures

The median score on the MG-ADL profile was 5.0 (IQR 3.0–8.0) (maximum possible score of 24) (table 3). The proportion of participants reporting moderate to severe impact (score ≥2) ranged from 7.9% (n=20) for the breathing item to 24.4% (n=60) for the double vision item. The median score on the MG-QoL-15R scale was 14 (IQR: 9–19) (maximum 30) (table 3) with >40% of participants reporting ‘very much’ for the following items: ‘I have to make plans around my MG’ (63.1%), ‘My MG limits my ability to enjoy hobbies and fun activities’ (55.8%), ‘My occupational skills and job status have been negatively affected by my MG’ (48.6%) and ‘I have limited my social activity because of my MG’ (41.0%). The median T-score on the Neuro-QOL fatigue item bank was 54.4 (IQR: 49.3–58.8) (maximum possible score 100) (table 2), with the most affected items (score ≥4: often or always) being ‘I felt fatigued’ (64.3%), ‘I felt exhausted’ (55.0%) ‘I was frustrated by being too tired to do the things I wanted to do’ (51.4%) and ‘I felt tired’ (49.4%). On this scale, 49.8% of participants had moderate or severe problems (T-score >55). For the HADS, 73 participants (29.3%) scored ≥11 on the anxiety subscore and 58 (23.4%) scored ≥11 on the depression subscore, consistent with the presence of probably clinically-relevant mental disorder.

The current disease state was considered acceptable by 128 of 251 participants (51.0%) (PASS question). However, this proportion was much lower in the 69 participants (14.5%) who declared being in an exacerbation phase of the disease compared with those declaring being the quiescent phase (64.8%). Overall, 128 participants (51.2%) were optimistic about the future and 84 (33.6%) were pessimistic, with more women being pessimistic than men (36.4% vs 25.4%, respectively) (table 3).

The strongest correlations between PROM scores were observed between the MG-QOL-15r score and the Neuro-QOL fatigue score (r=0.75) and between the MG-QOL-15r score and MG-ADL profile (r=0.68). In general, correlations between the HADS anxiety and depression scores with the other PROMs were moderate or low (r<0.60) (online supplemental figure 6).

Variables associated with the number of unfulfilled aspirations about disease cited per participant

In the Poisson regression model, four variables were associated with the number of citations. A higher number of citations was associated with being female (mean difference: +0.46 (95% CI 0.22 to 0.71), and having a higher MG-ADL profile score (+0.07 (0.02 to 0.13) per 2-point score increment). A lower number of citations was associated with older age (−0.07 (−0.13 to −0.01) per 10-year increment) and a longer time since diagnosis (−0.02 (−0.06 to −0.03) per 10-year increment) (figure 2). The fit of the Poisson model to the observed data was good (λ=2.56; χ²=1.41).

Poisson regression model: variables associated with the number of citations. HADS: Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale; MG-ADL: MG activities of daily living profile.

Variables associated with the number of ways to improve treatments cited per participant

With regard to treatment expectations, only two variables were associated with the number of citations. The first was disease acceptability, with participants who responded negatively to the PASS question providing more citations than participants who responded positively (standardised mean deviation (SMD) in number of citations: +0.36; p=0.004). The second variable was quality of life determined with the MG-QoL, with participants with low scores providing more citations than those with high scores: (SMD = +0.02; p=0.026).

An association was also observed between the number of citations for disease impact dimensions and the number of citations for treatment expectations (SMD = +0.16; p<0.001).

Discussion

The SPOON study identifies major areas of functioning where people with gMG express unfulfilled aspirations about what their disease prevents them from doing. The most frequently cited aspirations were those related to doing more physical activity, such as sport, mobility, endurance and regaining muscle strength. As such, physical limitations in MG seem to be more of an issue than psychological ones. Another frequently cited group of aspirations reflect the fluctuating nature of disability in MG where people have to live from day to day in planning their activities (planning my life, coping with daily frustration, having more predictable symptoms). Psychological distress may be a consequence of such frustration. We found that women, younger participants, those more recently diagnosed and those with more impairment on the MG-ADL profile generated more citations. As such, the number of citations may be a proxy for the extent of unfulfilled aspirations of the participants. With respect to ways to improve treatments, reduced side effects and having fewer pills to take were the most frequently cited themes.

A strength of the study was the application of an empirical mathematical approach to identify structure in the qualitative data which limits bias due to a priori expectations. Also, the questionnaire was developed by a collaboration between methodologists, clinicians and patients in order to ensure the relevance and clarity of the content. This study illustrates the feasibility of performing qualitative research through web-based platforms, a method that is quicker and easier to perform than the classical method of performing face-to-face semistructured interviews. Using a web-based platform could also facilitate the collection of diverse data since face-to-face interviews are limited in the number of participants who can be interviewed and thus in their profiles and backgrounds. Disadvantages compared with face-to-face interviews are that it is not possible to probe reactively to elicit further information or to resolve ambiguities. Apart from the analysis of the data from patient verbatim declarations, additional strengths of the study were the large sample size for a rare disease. Although no information was available on the response rate (since the number of people who were aware of the survey is unknown), in individuals who opened the questionnaire and who were eligible, the completion rate was high (96.5%).

A principal limitation is probable recruitment bias. As patient participation was voluntary, the study population is unlikely to be representative of all patients with MG in France. Recruitment was principally through patient associations, which introduces selection bias by its very nature. These patients are generally better informed, more involved and more knowledgeable about the disease than those who are not members of a patient association. Furthermore, patient associations sometimes attract the most severe cases and those most affected by the disease, which is precisely what motivates them to join a patient association. On the other hand, patients who were less well may have been less inclined to participate, as indicated by the relatively low MG-ADL score of participants. Nonetheless, a quarter of patients reported current aggravation of symptoms (exacerbation phase). In addition, the gender distribution in the study does not reflect that of the general population of people with MG, three-quarters of participants were women, which is inconsistent with the known gender distribution of MG in France, although the mean age of the sample was close to that of all individuals with MG in France.2 This is a phenomenon frequently observed in online surveys, where women participate more actively than men. As demonstrated in the multivariate analysis, women tend to report more dimensions in the open-ended questions than men (after adjusting for other variables). This may lead to an overestimation of the impact of the disease and higher expectations than in the general MG population. It is possible that women report their symptoms differently from men or that they have distinct clinical manifestations. In addition, women with MG tend to report more fatigue and psychological distress than men and rate their quality of life as poorer.25 In order to control for any gender bias, we decided to perform the data analysis separately for men and women. In future studies, selection bias could be reduced by, for example, stratified sampling of patients agreeing to participate. However, although this could be done with respect to objective categories such as age, gender and potentially disease characteristics, controlling for ‘soft’ qualities such as knowledge would not be possible. In addition, given that MG is a rare disease with a limited pool of available patients, it may be challenging to achieve an adequate sample size using stratified sampling. The same limitation applies to selecting patients with a given profile to participate in discussion groups.

A further limitation was that information on the disease and its treatment was uniquely based on participant declaration, without any independent clinical confirmation, so it is possible that some of this information is inaccurate or incomplete.

The SPOON study has built on the information collected on the patient perspective of MG collected in the previous International MG Patient Council study.10 The two studies differed in their methodology: the earlier study used information collected in patient group discussion sessions and the present study used open questioning coupled with manual content analysis of the replies. Several of the themes identified in the MG Patient Council study were also identified as important in the present study, including difficulty with planning activities and frustration with not being able to do all they want when they want. However, the SPOON study emphasised the importance of being able to participate in physical activities, which was not explicitly identified as a facet of the patient perspective in the previous study. Indeed, these themes were the most frequently cited, accounting for 37.6% of all citations. One reason why physical limitations may seem more important in patients with MG relates directly to the nature of the disease itself. MG is a fluctuating, unpredictable condition whose symptoms can vary from day to day, or even from moment to moment. These symptoms make long-term planning difficult, as patients may suddenly find themselves unable to follow through with plans due to sudden crises or worsening of their symptoms. In addition, the patient cannot anticipate which area of the body will be affected or how much difficulty these symptoms will cause. In consequence, patients often unconsciously adopt strategies of withdrawal or isolation and limit their engagement in physical activities.

The importance of medication side effects has been explored in a previous survey of 158 patients with MG in the USA.26 Similar to the American survey, we found gastrointestinal symptoms to be very important, although sleep disturbances and weight gain were infrequently cited (unlike the American survey). However, themes related to the large number of pills that participants had to take were cited almost as frequently in our survey as the side effects. That participants want to take fewer pills, or to have treatments with a longer duration of action, is unsurprising given that for oral treatments, the mean daily number of medication intakes was 6.6. These findings indicate that major unmet medical needs remain for the pharmacological management of MG. With respect to the PROMs, the median scores for the MG-ADL profile, the MG-QOL-15r and the HADS were close to the mean values reported in the MyRealWorld-MG survey.11 12 We found that, in 29% of participants, HADS scores were consistent with probable anxiety and in 23% were consistent with probable depression. These proportions are high compared with data from normative general population sets collected in Germany and the UK (we are not aware of equivalent data from the French general population).27 28 Using a generic quality of life questionnaire (SF-36), Lehnerer et al have reported that, after adjusting for confounding factors, people with MG had significantly lower scores on both the physical functioning and the emotional well-being dimensions compared with the general population.29 These are the two dimensions which generated the most citations in response to the open question on disease aspirations.

The observation that women report lower quality of life scores and higher treatment expectations has been reported in many areas of health, and the observation that women with gMG identify more domains where they are limited due to their disease is consistent with this. In the case of MG, it is also noteworthy that disease onset in women frequently occurs between the ages of 20 and 40 years,1 when many important life-changing decisions need to be made, such as choosing a career, choosing a partner, starting a family or acquiring a home, whereas, in men, the age of onset is typically above 50 years,1 when such decisions will generally already have been made. The same argument may also apply to the lower number of citations produced by older versus younger participants. The observation that participants with more recently diagnosed MG identify more domains may reflect the fact that patients learn to cope with their disease over time and that they have adjusted their expectations. The association between the number of citations and the higher MG-ADL profile score is expected, since this profile also measures the impact of the disease, and thus supports the external validity of our findings.

The present findings emphasise the importance of physicians talking to patients with MG about their aspirations and needs. The response to the PASS question indicated that around half of participants consider their current disease state as acceptable (although it should be noted that 73% declared that their disease was quiescent at the time of the survey), a finding that has also been reported elsewhere.30 In addition, 51% of participants felt optimistic about the future. However, despite this general level of satisfaction, participants identified many areas of life where they would like to do more than was currently possible. This would suggest that it is not enough for physicians to ask their patients about their general well-being and symptoms but that they should probe more deeply to identify those areas where patients are not functioning as well as they would like or where they have unmet expectations. In these cases, appropriate paramedical or social support should be made available and provided. The dimensions identified in the present survey may help physicians in such discussions with their patients. This study also provides information about the treatment expectations of people with MG, especially in relation to fewer side effects and simpler dosing regimens. The availability of novel immunomodulatory treatments for MG, which are better tolerated than the previous generation of immunosuppressants or corticosteroids, may go some way to meeting these needs. Such treatments were not available in France at the time of the survey.

With regard to future research, this study was performed in France, and it would be important to evaluate whether the findings are also valid for other countries with different healthcare systems. Comparing perceptions and expectations between patients treated with different therapies, such as those well-controlled by AChE-Is and those also requiring treatment with immunosuppressants or by corticosteroids, could be a useful future study, which could help resolve whether certain limitations perceived by patients are associated with the disease per se or with its treatments. For example, fatigue was cited both as a disease feature that patients wanted to be reduced and also as a treatment side effect that could be improved. Finally, performing a similar survey of the neurologists’ perception of the impact of MG to evaluate how closely it matches their patients’ aspirations would be of interest.

In conclusion, the SPOON study has identified that limitations in physical activity and difficulties in planning day-to-day life due to symptom unpredictability are the major areas where people with gMG aspire to a better life. The information collected could help healthcare professionals understand and address their patients’ needs better and highlight expectations for treatment that are currently not met by available therapies.
