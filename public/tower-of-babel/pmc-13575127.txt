Detection of the elusive cyclo−1,2-dihydrotetrazete (N4H2) in the gas phase
Authors: Jianchao Yang, Xinyue Gong, Zhibiao Li, You Wu, Xiaohu Li, Weijun Zheng, Ralf I Kaiser, Chongqin Zhu, Cheng Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNitrogen hydrides (NnHm) have attracted significant interest for their potential in developing high-energy-density materials. The synthesis of cyclic nitrogen hydrides is particularly challenging due to their inherently high strain energies. Here we report the preparation of the hitherto elusive trans- and cis-cyclo−1,2-dihydrotetrazete (2a and 2b, cyclo-N4H2) along with its open chain isomer azidamide (5a, N=N=N−NH2) by exposing low temperature (5 K) ice of ammonia (NH3) to high-energy electrons. These compounds were detected and discriminated in the gas phase during the temperature programmed desorption phase exploiting isomer selectivity soft photoionization reflectron time-of-flight mass spectrometry. The cyclo−1,2-dihydrotetrazete signifies a free gas phase species containing a tetranitrogen ring existing without recourse to high-pressure conditions or conjugative stabilization. Our approach may provide feasible strategies for designing higher-order nitrogen ring systems and for elucidating their chemical bonding and stability.Subject terms: Reaction kinetics and dynamics, Reaction mechanisms

Introduction

For the last century, nitrogen hydrides (NnHm) have received substantial attention across the communities of theoretical chemistry, synthetic chemistry, and materials science1–8 from fundamental perspectives on chemical bonding and exceptional energetic properties for developing explosive, fuel, and propellant. At least nine open-chain nitrogen hydrides, including diimide (N2H2), hydrazine (N2H4), hydrogen azide (HN3), triazene (N3H3), triazane (N3H5), ammonium azide (NH4N3), tetrazene (H2N–N=N–NH2), tetrazane (N4H6), and the hydrazinium azide salt (N2H5+N3−) have been synthesized and isolated7–11.

However, the pursuit of cyclic nitrogen hydrides presents a considerably greater challenge due to the substantial strain energy associated with the pure nitrogen ring. Kim et al. identified cyclotriazane (cyclo-N3H3) in crystals of zeolite A absorbed with ammonia (NH3), wherein the cyclo-N3H3 is stabilized through coordination to silver ions, forming Ag(cyclo-N3H3)+ complexes1,12. Although theoretical calculations predicted that tetrazene (N4H4) and tetrazadiene (N4H2) containing four-membered nitrogen ring (cyclo-N4) are kinetically stable toward decomposition, no studies reported their experimental identification13–15. The cyclo-N4 moiety was initially prepared via benzyl and specific alkoxycarbonyl substitution of the ring (Fig. 1), as illustrated in tetrabenzyltetrazetidine and tetraalkyl tetrazetidinetetracarboxylate radical cation16,17. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy has revealed the presence of an N4− molecular ion in single crystals of KN3 or RbN3 irradiated with UV or γ rays at 77 K18. Zheng et al. deposited nitrogen plasma on a cold window and found a weak infrared absorption at 936.7 cm−1. The peak shifted to 900.0 cm−1 upon 15N substitution and was assigned to tetrahedral N4 species19. Bittererová et al. calculated the ground-state and excited-state properties of the rectangular N4 molecule with D2h symmetry. They discovered that its dissociation into two N2 molecules proceeds via a new C2v transition state with an effective barrier of approximately 27.2 kJ mol−1, suggesting that the molecule may be stable at low temperatures20. Li et al. predicted that the nitrogen-rich compound MnN4 with a planar N4 ring structure under high pressure exhibits both superconductivity and high hardness21. Y. Sajeev revealed through calculations that the resonant attachment of an about 12 eV electron to a dinitrogen dimer (N2)2 can excite it into a 2p1h resonance state, thereby triggering a barrier-free cycloaddition reaction to generate a cyclic N4− anion22. Nevertheless, in the absence of additional stabilization mechanisms like coordination, complexation, and high pressure exploited in the preparation of Ag(cyclo-N3H3)+ 1,12, pentazole anion (cyclo-N5−)23, and hexazine anions (N62− and N64−)24,25, whether free cyclic nitrogen hydrides can survive remains unclear.

In this work, we report the preparation and detection of the hitherto elusive trans- and cis-cyclo-1,2-dihydrotetrazete (2a and 2b, cyclo-N4H2) as well as its thermodynamically most stable, open chain isomer azidamide (5a, N=N=N−NH2) in the gas phase (Fig. 2). The experiments were conducted by irradiating ammonia (NH3) or deuterated ammonia (ND3) ices with energetic electrons at 5 K (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2) followed by heating the sample to 300 K at a rate of 1 K min−1 to sublimate the ices (Supplementary Fig. 1). During this temperature-programmed desorption (TPD) stage, N4H2 isomers were identified by employing vacuum ultraviolet (VUV) PhotoIonization Reflectron Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometry (PI-ReTOF-MS) in conjunction with electronic structure computations26,27. Previous studies detected solely lithium-ion-adducts N4H2 (N4H2Li+) of an unknown structure generated by microwave discharge of nitrogen (N2) and hydrogen (H2) mixtures5. The present identification of cyclo-1,2-dihydrotetrazete provides fundamental insights into unconventional synthetic pathway and photoionization properties of cyclic nitrogen hydrides along with their kinetic stabilities toward unimolecular decomposition in the gas phase thus enhancing our knowledge of highly strained cyclic molecules of fundamental interest to chemical bonding and electronic structure.

Results

Infrared spectroscopy

The chemical evolution of ammonia ices during electron irradiation was monitored by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) (Fig. 3, Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). The spectra of the pristine ices exhibited absorption peaks corresponding to NH3 at 3382 cm−1 (v3), 3298 cm−1 (2v4), 3213 cm−1 (v1), 1626 cm−1 (v4), and 1057 cm−1 (v2). Electron irradiation consumed (30 ± 5)% of the NH3 and generated increase of broad IR signal in the range of 3370–3090, 2780, 2090, 1515, and 1153 cm−1. The broad features at 3370–3090 cm⁻¹ are assigned to the stretching vibrations of newly formed N−H groups, potentially overlapping with the overtones of NH2 scissoring mode28. The band around 2780 cm⁻1 can be attributed to the N−H stretching modes of isodiazene (N=NH2) and/or combinations of newly formed NH2 scissoring + twisting modes29,30. The 2090 cm−1 band corresponds to the asymmetric stretching mode of −N=N=N groups31. The absorption at 1515 cm−1 can be associated with the bending mode of amino radicals (·NH2) (v2)30 and/or NH2 scissoring mode28. The band near 1153 cm−1 is assigned to the NH2 twisting mode28. The intensities of these peaks increase with irradiation time, indicating that they originate from the products of the irradiation process (Supplementary Fig. 2). The substitution of NH3 by ND3 induced red-shifted of the hydrogen-related absorptions and further supported the previous assignments (Supplementary Table 4). However, electron irradiation could generate a series of amines, imines, and azide homologs. Each homolog has similar functional groups and hance exhibits overlapping, non-unique IR absorptions. Therefore, an alternative approach is utilized to selectively probe discrete isomers.

PhotonIonization reflectron time-of-flight mass spectrometry

During the TPD phase, we characterized the sublimated molecules exploiting single VUV PhotonIonization Reflectron Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometry (PI-ReTOF-MS)26,27 (Supplementary Table 5). This approach can accurately distinguish isomers by tuning the photon energy (PE) based on the adiabatic ionization energies of the target products, thereby selectively ionizing the molecules or leaving them neutral, and distinguishing isomers through comparison of the resulting mass spectrometric signals. Guided by the quantum chemical calculations of N4H2 isomers (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 6, and Source data), four photon energies were selected, namely 10.49 eV, 9.90 eV, 9.20 eV and 8.74 eV (Supplementary Table 1). Photons at 10.49 eV can ionize all isomers if produced since this energy exceeds the IE of each distinct isomer. Photons at 10.49 eV can ionize all isomers if produced, since this energy exceeds the IE of each distinct isomer; 9.90 eV photons can ionize only isomers 3 to 6b; 9.20 eV ionization is limited to species 5a to 6b; 8.74 eV photons are able to ionize 5b to 6b. Although 8.74 eV is slightly higher than the computed lower ionization threshold of 5a, this does not affect the findings of this study, as elaborated in the subsequent sections. Figure 4 presents the PI-ReTOF-MS results. Since NH3 ices contain only two different atoms, the molecular recognition and analysis relying on the mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) of the ionized species is unambiguous and well-defined (Supplementary Table 5). We focus on the signal at m/z = 58 (Fig. 5), which can be attributed to N4H2+ ions. At PE = 10.49 eV, three sublimation events at 224 K (peak I), 235 K (peak II) and 257 K (peak III) are detected (Fig. 5A). In a separate experiment, substitution of NH3 with ND3 shifts this signal from m/z = 58 to m/z = 62 (Fig. 5B). This result confirms that the species responsible for the signal at m/z = 58 contains two hydrogen atoms (H) and therefore can only be N4H2+. When the PE is reduced to 9.90 eV, the peak II at 235 K vanishes, while the 224 K (peak I) and 257 K (peak III) features are still present (Fig. 5C). This suggests the peak II (235 K) can be linked to 2a (10.38–10.49 eV) and/or 2b (IE = 10.12–10.23 eV) since only their IEs are between 10.49 eV and 9.90 eV. Further lowering the PE to 9.20 eV eliminates the sublimation event at 257 K (peak III) (Fig. 5D), indicating that this peak is associated with 3 (IE = 9.45–9.56 eV) and/or 4 (IE = 9.43–9.54 eV). The peak I at 224 K remaining at PE = 9.20 eV is attributed to 5a since it disappears when the PE is decreased to 8.74 eV (Fig. 5E). It should be noted that in a blank control experiment conducted under the same experimental conditions but without irradiating the NH3 ice, the m/z = 58 ion signal was not detected (Fig. 5F). This confirms that the identified N4H2 isomers 2a/2b, 3/4, and 5a are generated by the electron irradiation of the ice samples. To further confirm the identification of isomer 2, we measured its photoionization efficiency (PIE) curve. As shown in Fig. 6, there are two onsets of the ion counts at 10.17–10.21 eV and 10.45–10.49 eV, which fall within the range of the computed IEs of 2b (10.12–10.23 eV) and 2a (10.38–10.49 eV), respectively.

After providing convincing evidence for the preparation and identification of trans- and cis-cyclo−1,2-dihydrotetrazete (2a and 2b, cyclo-N4H2), now we turn our attention to their computed geometric structures at the CCSD(T)/cc-pVTZ level of theory. Both isomers 2a and 2b feature four-nitrogen rings with one N(1)=N(4) double bond and three single N-N bonds. The computed bond angles of the nitrogen ring in 2a are∠N(1)=N(4)−N(3) = ∠N(4)=N(1)−N(2) = 93.1° and ∠N(1)−N(2)−N(3) = ∠N(2)−N(3)−N(4) = 86°, which are similar to that in 2b ∠N(1)=N(4)−N(3) = ∠N(4)=N(1)−N(2) = 93.7° and ∠N(1)−N(2)−N(3) = ∠N(2)−N(3)−N(4) = 86.3°. These bond angles are also close to the bond angles of ∠C=C−C (94.2°) and ∠C−C−C (85.8°) in cyclobutene (7, cyclo-C4H6) — an isoelectronic species of 2a and 2b. The bond length of the N(1)=N(4) double bond is 1.282 Å for 2a and 1.278 Å for 2b; these values are slightly larger than the N=N bond length of trans-diazene (HN=NH, 1.252 Å)32. Meanwhile, the bond lengths of the two nitrogen bonds N(1)−N(2) and N(3)−N(4) connected to the N(1)=N(4) double bond are in the range of 1.410–1.420 Å, which are shorter than that in hydrazine (H2N–NH2, 1.446 Å). The observed shortening of the single bonds and elongation of the double bonds may be attributed to the hyperconjugative coupling between the N(1)=N(4) π* orbital and the lone pairs of the adjacent nitrogen atoms N(2) and N(3)33. The remaining N(2)−N(3) bond lies considerably distant from the double bond and exhibits a bond length of 1.446 Å for 2a and 1.446 Å for 2b, closely matching that in H2N–NH2 (1.446 Å). In 2a, the two nitrogen bonds N(1)−N(2) and N(3)−N(4) have the same bond lengths of 1.413 Å. With two hydrogens (H(5) and H(6)) located on opposite sides of the nitrogen ring, 2a possesses only a single twofold rotation axis and therefore belongs to the C₂ point group. The isomer 2b is characterized by a Cs point group symmetry with two hydrogens situated on the same side of the tetranitrogen ring. Strain energy is an important property of these cyclic molecules. Constructed homodesmotic equations evaluate this characteristic of 2a, 2b, and cyclo-C4H6 (7) (Fig. 7). The strain energy of 2a (78 kJ mol−1) and 2b (106 kJ mol−1) is lower than that of cyclo-C4H6 (7) (138 kJ mol−1). Note that according to a natural bond analysis (NBO), the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) and highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) of these species exhibit distinct features34–36. The HOMO and LUMO of cyclo-C4H6 (7) are mainly contributed by the C1=C2 π-bonding orbital and the associated π* anti-bonding orbital, respectively. In the cases of 2a and 2b, the HOMO exhibits two-fold degeneracy with contributions primarily from four nitrogen atoms: N1, N2, N3, N4 account for 19%, 28%, 28%, 19% of the HOMO, respectively. The LUMO is dominated by the p orbitals of N1 and N4 (>90%). The HOMO–LUMO gaps of 2a (15.25 eV) and 2b (14.86 eV) are comparable, whereas 7 exhibits a somewhat lower gap of 13.63 eV, suggesting slightly higher chemical reactivity.

Discussion

It is worth discussing the potential generation mechanisms of the identified N4H2 isomers. Previous studies have shown that low-dose electron irradiation of NH3 ices can lead to the formation of the amino radical (·NH2), HN3, azide radical (N3·), nitrogen molecule (N2), and HN=NH7,8,30. Prolonged irradiation may promote N3· and ·NH2 radicals accumulation and therefore their recombination to produce 5a barrierlessly (Fig. 8). Isomer 5a could undergo intramolecular cyclization to 3, followed by a hydrogen transfer to afford 2a. Further hydrogen transfer to the same side of the tetranitrogen ring of 2a can lead to the formation of 2b. The barriers for these isomerization reactions are 216 kJ mol−1 (TS1), 118 kJ mol−1 (TS2), and 48 kJ mol−1 (TS3), which could be overcome by the energy delivered to the reactants via incident electrons (2000 eV or 1.9 × 105 kJ mol−1). Alternatively, N2 and HN=NH may undergo one-step cyclization to produce 2b overcoming a 474 kJ mol−1 energy barrier (TS4) upon electron irradiation. To further assess the energetic feasibility of the bimolecular association between N2 and N2H2, we computed the interaction energy37 at the CCSD(T)/cc-pVTZ level. The interaction energy, Eint=EN2⋅⋅⋅N2H2−(EN2+EN2N2), was found to be − 5.97 kJ mol−1. The negative value indicates a weak but stabilizing interaction, consistent with a physically reasonable pre-reactive encounter complex. Such stabilization supports the plausibility of the bimolecular association step. These reaction pathways are likely routes for the formation of the identified N4H2 isomers.

In summary, we prepared the previously elusive cis-cyclo−1,2-dihydrotetrazete (2b, cyclo-N4H2) along with open-chain azidamide (5a, N=N=N−NH2) via energetic electron exposure of pure NH3 ices and discriminated the isomers exploiting vacuum ultraviolet (VUV) PI-ReTOF-MS to analyze the sublimed molecules from the irradiated samples during the temperature programmed desorption (TPD) stage. The isotopic experiment employing deuterated ammonia (ND3) shifted the detected molecular ions of these isomers from m/z = 58 to m/z = 62, thereby further corroborating the assignments. The isomers sublimes at temperatures from 220 K to 260 K, which corresponds to average velocities close to 300 m s−1. Given a 2.0 ± 0.2 mm separation between the sample surface and the ionization region, the lifetimes of the detected N4H2 isomers must exceed 6.5 μs to ensure their survival over this flight distance. In addition, our calculations show that overcoming an energy barrier of 131 kJ mol−1 is required for the decomposition of 2b through TS4, which is higher than the decomposition barrier for N6 (61 kJ mol−1)38. The substantial decomposition barrier of 131 kJ mol−1 translates to a mean lifetime of more than 400 years at room temperature, indicating that the molecule is persistent under standard conditions. This work represents the gas-phase identification of a molecule comprising a four-membered ring composed exclusively of nitrogen atoms, achieved without recourse to high-pressure conditions or conjugative stabilization. Compared to the gas-phase pyrolysis and high-pressure routes, the strategy of electron irradiation of low temperature ices has a few features: energetic electrons can generate radicals and deliver energy to reactant to overcome high reaction barriers; the irradiation-generated radicals are formed in close proximity, facilitating their subsequent reactions; the formed species can be protected at cryogenic temperatures, preventing their decomposition that might otherwise occur under high-temperature or high-pressure conditions. Our approach may offer feasible conceptual pathways for the construction of higher-order nitrogen ring systems, with potential applicability as high-energy-density materials.

Methods

Experimental

The experimental setup involves preparing pure ammonia (NH3) ice and its isotope-substituted counterpart (ND3) on a silver wafer at a low temperature (5 K) within an ultrahigh-pressure chamber (base pressure: 10−11 Torr) (Supplementary information, Supplementary Fig. 1) with the design of the setup merged from references 26 and 2726,27.

Energetic electrons are used to irradiate the ice to trigger the chemical-bond cleavage processes and non-equilibrium reactions. Following irradiation, the ice was heated to 300 K with a 1 K min−1 rate (temperature-programmed desorption (TPD)) to sublime solid-phase species (Supplementary Table 5). During the irradiation and TPD stages, the ice and the sublimed species were monitored using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and vacuum ultraviolet (VUV) PI-ReTOF-MS, respectively (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 4). This photoionization process utilizes four-wave mixing to produce vacuum ultraviolet light (ωvuv = 2ω1 – ω2). Here, ω1 is obtained by frequency doubling of the output of a dye laser (LIOP-TEC, Liopstar-E) pumped by an Nd:YAG laser, while ω2 is produced by a nanosecond tunable optical parametric oscillator laser (INNOLAS, SpitLight 600 OPO).

Theoretical

The N4H2 isomers and ions, transition states, azide radical (N3·), and amino radical (·NH2), nitrogen molecules, trans-diazene (HN=NH) were all computed at the CCSD(T)/cc-pVTZ level of theory generating zero-point vibrational energies (ZPVEs). The Cartesian coordinates (Å) and vibrational frequencies are provided as a Source Data file. With the previous optimized structures of the N4H2 isomers and ions, their energies were computed exploiting coupled cluster singles, doubles, and perturbative triples (CCSD(T)) method39 with 3-point complete basis set (CBS) extrapolations40 utilizing the cc-pVDZ, cc-pVTZ and cc-pVQZ basis sets41,42 (Supplementary Tables 6, Source data). The adiabatic ionization energies of the N4H2 isomers were obtained by calculating the energy difference between the neutral molecules and their corresponding cations at the CCSD(T)/CBS//CCSD(T)/cc-pVTZ level of theory with ZPVE corrections. Harmonic frequencies of the N4H2 and N4D2 isomers were computed at the CCSD(T)/cc-pVTZ level. Regarding anharmonic corrections, since direct CCSD(T)-level VPT2 anharmonic calculations are currently not available in the ORCA package, to address this limitation while preserving the high accuracy of the CCSD(T) harmonic frequencies, we adopted a hybrid CC/DFT correction scheme43.
