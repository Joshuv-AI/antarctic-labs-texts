Speciation by local adaptation and isolation by distance in extended environments
Lara D. Hissa, Flavia M. D. Marquitti, Marcus A. M. de Aguiar

Abstract
Speciation is often associated with geographical barriers that limit gene flow. However, species can also emerge in continuous homogeneous environments through isolation by distance. When the environment is not homogeneous, natural selection contributes to differentiation by local adaptation and tends to facilitate speciation. To explore how isolation by distance and adaptation combine to determine species diversity, we implemented a model regulated by these two components. The first is implemented via mating restrictions on spatial proximity and genetic similarity. The second is realized by an ecological phenotype subjected to adaption and natural selection. We consider scenarios where the environment is either homogeneous, with a single ecological optimum, or heterogeneous with two distinct optima. In homogeneous environments no speciation is observed under permissive mating restrictions, independent on the strength of natural selection. On the other hand, if reproduction requires close genetic similarity (strict mating restrictions), speciation occurs only under strong selection. In contrast, in environments with two local optima and strong selection, species well adapted to each of the niches emerge along the spatial structure, leading to the formation of groups with distinct phenotypes. Permissive mating restrictions leads to the formation of only two species, each occupying one of the niches; strong mating restriction leads to several species per niche, in a much faster speciation process. Interestingly, when selection is weak and reproduction requires strong genetic similarity, several species form, but the process is slow. Moreover, species average phenotypes do not remain constant over generations, causing the phenotypic distribution to oscillate, never reaching a stationary pattern.



Introduction

Geographic isolation has long been considered one of the main drivers of speciation
coyne2004speciation
,
nosil2012ecological
,
mayr2013animal
. It happens when sub-populations are separated by physical barriers, interrupting gene flow and allowing genetic and phenotypic differences to accumulate, eventually leading to reproductive isolation
Manzo_Peliti_1994
,
Yamaguchi-2013_first
,
princepe2022diversity
. However, speciation with gene flow can emerge in various contexts. In parapatric speciation, neighboring populations experience different selective pressures while exchanging genes at a limited rate
gavrilets1998rapid
,
gavrilets2000patterns
,
anceschi2019neutral
,
freitas2022speciation
. Ecological speciation, in particular, has gained prominence as a mechanism whereby divergent selection across environmental gradients or habitat types leads to reproductive isolation
schluter2009evidence
,
nosil2012ecological
. This process depends on the interaction between local adaptation and barriers to gene flow, including pre- and post-zygotic barriers, assortative mating and reduced hybrid fitness
nosil2012ecological
.

Recent empirical and theoretical evidence suggest that speciation can also result from the interplay of several other mechanisms, such as genetic incompatibilities
gavrilets2002sympatric
,
nosil2011genes
,
johannesson2024diverse
, competition for resources
Dieckmann_1999
,
Dieckmann_2003
,
polechova2005speciation
,
schluter2009evidence
, temporal separation
cooley2003temporal
,
taylor2017role
and partial barriers
smadja2011framework
,
fitzpatrick2008if
. Opposing the idea of geographic isolation, or allopatry, speciation can also occur for populations occupying a single habitat
via2001sympatric
, in sympatry, if gene flow is reduced by mating restrictions
higgs1991stochastic
,
kondrashov1999interactions
,
caetano2020sympatric
or by intense competition
kisdi1999evolutionary
,
doebeli2000evolutionary
.

One important mechanism of differentiation in spatially distributed populations is isolation by distance
white1968models
. If mating occurs only between nearby individuals, spatial distance tends to correlate with genetic distance
moritz1992evolutionary
,
irwin2005speciation
,
martins2013
,
de2017barriers
. Depending on the mating range, mutation rate and other population parameters, this process may lead to the break up of the population into reproductively isolated groups even in a homogeneous environment
de2017barriers
,
de2009global
. In heterogeneous habitats, local adaptation can contribute to differentiation and facilitate speciation
lande1982rapid
,
rainey1998adaptive
,
schliewen2001genetic
.

Speciation by phenotypic adaptation has been explored in different contexts, such as temporal variations and climate change
Kopp2014
,
Otto2020
, interference of multiple optima
Lenormand2015
and continuous spatial variations
Dieckmann_2003
. Doebeli and Dieckmann
Dieckmann_2003
, in particular, considered a model with uniform environmental gradient and demonstrated that branching of the population into clusters of similar phenotypes occur for intermediate slopes of the gradient. Here we also consider species formation in environments with local adaptation, but we add the component of isolation by distance.
Unlike the model proposed in
Dieckmann_2003
, relying on continuous environmental gradients and implicit reproductive isolation, our approach imposes explicit constraints on mating: individuals must be both spatially close and genetically similar to reproduce. This setup allows the identification of reproductively isolated groups and, therefore, species. Moreover, it allows us to study the effects of local adaptation in scenarios where isolation by distance alone is or is not enough to promote speciation.

We show that speciation in homogeneous environments requires strong natural selection and robust isolation by distance. In environments with two local optima, ecologically different species, adapted to their respective niches, may arise. Strict mating restriction leads to the appearance of several species per niche. If selection is strong, speciation is fast; if it is weak, speciation is slow and phenotype distribution is not stable, oscillating in time. Speciation under permissive mating restrictions, on the other hand, requires strong selection and leads to only two species, one in each niche.

Methods

We propose a model to investigate speciation in sexually reproducing populations subjected to natural selection and mating by genetic similarity. We use an individual-based model (IBM) on a lattice
L_{1}\times L_{2}
with reflective (non-periodic) boundaries. Initially, individuals occupy the lattice randomly and, although typical population densities are small, multiple individuals can share the same site. The model explores how individuals can adapt and diversify between distinct environments, even in the absence of physical barriers. We assume that the total population is at its carrying capacity, resulting in nearly constant population size.

Each individual
i
carries two chromosomes: one for reproductive compatibility, denoted by
\gamma_{i}=(\gamma_{i}^{1},\gamma_{i}^{2},...,\gamma_{i}^{B})
, and one for environmental compatibility,
\rho_{i}=(\rho_{i}^{1},\rho_{i}^{2},...,\rho_{i}^{C})
, with sizes
B
and
C
, respectively. The chromosomes are represented by binary sequences, so that
\sigma_{i}^{k}
and
\rho_{i}^{k}
can only assume the values
0
or
1
. The ecological phenotype
P_{i}
is determined by the additive effect of its loci, i.e., by the sum of the alleles in the environmental chromosome:

P_{i}=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{k=1}^{C}\rho_{i}^{k}

(1)

The environment exerts selection pressure based on the match between an individual’s phenotype and the local environmental optimum,
P_{E}
. Individual fitness,
w_{i}
, is computed using a Gaussian function:

w_{i}=e^{\frac{-(P_{i}-P_{E})^{2}}{2\sigma_{e}^{2}}},

(2)

where
\sigma_{e}
defines the strength of selection, which is large when
\sigma_{e}
is small.

In order to simplify our analysis we consider two cases. In the first, that works as a null model, the environment is homogeneous, with a single ecological optimum. In the second, the environment is heterogeneous with two distinct optima. In the case of two environments, we divided the lattice into
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
, with respective optima
P_{E_{1}}
and
P_{E_{2}}
. Individuals can select reproductive partners
\markoverwith

\ULon
freely across the lattice, including across the boundary between environments. However, sexual partners are chosen only within a
mating radius

S
centered on the focal individual. Moreover, mating is only possible if the genetic distance between the reproductive chromosomes of the mating pair is sufficiently small. The genetic distance
D_{ij}
between two individuals,
i
and
j
is defined by

D_{ij}=\sum_{k}|\gamma_{i}^{k}-\gamma_{j}^{k}|,

(3)

and corresponds to the number of differing loci between their reproductive chromosomes.
The probability that individual
i
will reproduce depends on its fitness
w_{i}
, as explained below. Moreover, mating occurs only if the genetic distance is below a compatibility threshold
G
, defined as the maximum number of differing loci allowed for successful reproduction. This may be considered as a weak form of assortative mating. Thus, an individual
i
can reproduce only if it finds genetically compatible mating partners, that differ in fewer than
G
positions across their reproductive chromosomes, within its mating neighborhood
S
. Among these potential mating partners one is chosen at random.

We implement sexual reproduction with recombination by allowing the offspring to inherit a random combination of reproductive and environmental chromosomes from both parents. After recombination, the loci can mutate (from 0 to 1 or from 1 to 0) with probability
\mu
per locus. Offspring is placed at a randomly selected site within the reproduction radius
S
of the focal parent. The overall model workflow is illustrated in Fig.
1
, which shows the key components of the process: the radius
S
, the chromosomes, and the fitness calculation that governs reproductive dynamics between individuals.

Figure 1
:
Representation of the model: the lattice with
N
individuals is divided into two environments. An individual is selected and chooses a genetically compatible mating partner within a radius
S
, such that
D_{ij}<G
. The offspring inherits a random combination of reproductive loci from both parents. Each locus may mutate with a given probability, flipping the value of the binary allele. The phenotype is determined by the sum of the loci in the environmental chromosome, which determines the offspring’s fitness. The new generation of individuals replaces the old one, and the process is repeated over time, allowing the population to evolve under the influence of mutation, selection, and reproductive constraints.

Different versions of this model has been previously considered in a neutral scenario
de2009global
, where environmental pressure was not considered. In that case, all individuals have equal probability of reproducing, regardless of their genotype. Evolution under these simpler conditions, governed only by mating restriction through spatial proximity and genetic compatibility, can already lead to speciation, depending on the model parameters. In contrast, our extended model incorporates environmental selection, such that reproduction depends also on ecological fitness. In order to bridge between the two versions of the model we define the probability that an individual
fails
to reproduce due to poor fitness by:

q_{i}=1-(1-q^{\prime})w_{i},

(4)

where
q^{\prime}
is the baseline probability of mortality and
w_{i}
is the individual’s fitness (
2
). The probability that an individual does reproduce is
1-q_{i}
. In a neutral scenario
\sigma_{e}\rightarrow\infty
,
w_{i}\rightarrow 1
and
q_{i}=q^{\prime}
for all individuals. On the other hand, when
w_{i}\rightarrow 0
,
q_{i}\rightarrow 1
and the individual does not reproduce. Thus, this formulation increases the reproductive success of well-adapted individuals (up to the baseline value
1-q^{\prime}
) while reducing it for poorly adapted ones. Following previous works we fix
q^{\prime}=0.37
which is approximately the probability that an individual is not selected in
N
trials with replacement from a population with
N
individuals:
(1-1/N)^{N}\approx e^{-1}\approx 0.37
for large
N
.

In the model, generations are non-overlapping and evolution proceeds as follows: all individuals of a generation have a chance to reproduce according to Eq.(
4
) and leave offspring for the next generation. If an individual fails to reproduce, it dies without leaving offspring. However, since we want to keep the total population close to its carrying capacity, when this happens we randomly select another individual within the mating neighborhood for reproduction, disregarding environmental fitness, to reproduce in its place. This fallback mechanism helps keeping a constant population size and also enables reproduction during the early phases of evolution, when most individuals are far from optimal environmental fitness. However, if no genetically compatible partners are available for the individual or its neighbors, the population does decrease. This happens, for instance if there is no other individual within its mating neighborhood
S
.

To avoid population reduction, we allow a pair of individuals that successfully reproduces to have a second offspring with probability:

p=1.0-\frac{N(t)}{N_{0}},

(5)

where
N(t)
is the current population size and
N_{0}
is the initial population size. This guarantees that
N(t)
remains very close to
N_{0}
at all times. However, in order to avoid local crowding, this second reproduction event is allowed only if the number of neighboring individuals in the mating range is smaller than
N_{S}/4
, where
N_{S}=\pi S^{2}\rho
is the average number of individuals in the mating range and
\rho=N_{0}/(L_{1}L_{2})
is the average population density. This density-dependent mechanism ensures that reproduction reflects both ecological suitability and local competition.

Species are identified as clusters of individuals with uninterrupted gene flow. In our representation, the population forms a network in which individuals are connected if they are genetically compatible. In network science terminology, an adjacency matrix is defined as
A_{ij}=1
if
D_{ij}<G
and
A_{ij}=0
otherwise. Species correspond to connected component in this network.

Numerical Simulations

In this section we tune the model parameters to consider scenarios where isolation by distance alone can lead to species formation and where it cannot. In each of these cases we vary the strength of environmental selection and study how it affects population genetic diversity and the time to speciation. Of particular interest is the formation of hybrid zones near ecological boundaries and what conditions such zones collapse due to reinforcement of phenotypic and genetic divergence.

We performed simulations by varying the strength of natural selection
\sigma_{e}
and the genetic compatibility threshold
G
. We defined strong and weak selection as
\sigma_{e}=0.05
and
\sigma_{e}=0.40
, respectively. We also used
G=0.05B
and
G=0.30B
to compare scenarios with high and low mating restrictions on genetic compatibility. A small
G
value (e.g.,
0.05B
) imposes a strong reproductive constraint, as individuals must be nearly identical to mate, which favors speciation. In contrast, a large
G
value (e.g.,
0.30B
) allows mating between more genetically diverse individuals, maintaining gene flow and reducing the likelihood of speciation. Our goal is to analyze evolution and speciation under two conditions: when the environment is homogeneous and when it is divided into two regions with different local optima (heterogeneous case).

The length of the reproductive chromosome is
B=1500
, and all individuals start with identical chromosomes in which every locus is
0
. The length of the environmental chromosome is
C=100
, and its loci can take the values
0
or
1
. Fixed parameters also include the initial number of individuals
N_{0}=1300
, lattice size
L_{1}=L_{2}=100
, mating radius
S=6
, and the genetic and phenotypic mutation probability
\mu=4\times 10^{-4}
. The average population density is
\rho=0.13
and the expected number of individuals in the mating range is
N_{S}\approx 14.7
.

In the homogeneous scenario, the entire lattice shares a single optimal phenotype,
P_{E}=0.50
, while individuals initially have randomly assigned phenotypes within the interval
[0.30,0.70]
. For the heterogeneous case, the optimal phenotypes in environments
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
are
P_{E_{1}}=0.40
and
P_{E_{2}}=0.60
, respectively. Initially all individuals have the same phenotype,
P=0.50
, allowing us to clearly observe how selection drives diversification towards the two optima over time.

We analyzed both the homogeneous and heterogeneous cases by running simulations for
t=5,000
iterations where each iteration represents a generation. This duration ensures that the system reaches equilibrium in all scenarios considered.

Results

By simulating these dynamics, we aim to understand whether selection across discrete ecological niches can effectively drive the emergence of reproductively isolated groups i.e., speciation without requiring strong selection, strict assortative mating, or geographic barriers. We also contrast our results with neutral models, assessing how natural selection by the environment modifies the rate and stability of divergence.

In homogeneous landscapes, stable speciation only emerges when strong selection is combined with limited gene flow promoted by close mating similarity. Interestingly, this outcome is similar to the neutral scenario where selection is totally ignored and mating is restrictive. The reason is that strong selection leads to a thin distribution of phenotypes, making all individuals equivalent. Weak selection breaks this pattern broadening the distribution of fitness and preventing speciation.

Discussion and Conclusions

The importance of environmental gradients in speciation has been suggested by empirical data and explored in theoretical models

lande1982rapid
,
rainey1998adaptive
,
schliewen2001genetic
,
Dieckmann_2003
. Moreover, in spatially extended populations, isolation by distance also contributes to population differentiation
white1968models
and might itself lead speciation
moritz1992evolutionary
,
de2009global
,
irwin2005speciation
,
martins2013
,
de2017barriers
. In this work we considered a model where both mechanisms are present and focused on the balance between strength of natural selection, controlled by the parameter
\sigma_{e}
, and mating restrictions, controlled by the maximum genetic distance for mating
G
. In order to simplify the study we considered the simplest situation of two continuous environments with fixed ecological optima.

Comparing homogeneous and heterogeneous conditions underscores the role of environmental structure. With permissive mating restrictions,
G=0.30B
, no speciation occurs in a homogeneous landscape, even under strong selection. However, in the heterogeneous case Fig
3
(d), the presence of distinct optima promotes divergence and the emergence of two spatially separated species. Under weak selection, Fig.
3
(e), this effect is diminished and speciation is not observed.

Under weak selection (
\sigma_{e}=0.40
) and strong genetic mating restrictions (
G=0.05B
), species formation is hindered in homogeneous environments (Fig.
2
(c)). Phenotypic divergence is constrained in this case, and subpopulation structure remains diffuse.
In heterogeneous scenario, on the other hand, speciation does happen (Fig.
3
(c)), although the process is much slower. Moreover, while multiple species emerge, the phenotypic distribution is not persistent and fluctuates across generations, with shifts in peak positions and intensities. This dynamic prevents the consolidation of well defined niches and hinders the long-term maintenance of differentiated groups. This results corroborates recent empirical data that suggests that hybridization slows down speciation or even stop it altogether
muller2025ephemeral
. Only under strong selection (
\sigma_{e}=0.05
) and strict mating restrictions (
G=0.05B
) multiple species emerge in both homogeneous and heterogeneous scenarios.

These findings show that spatial variation facilitate speciation by creating ecological niches that support and stabilize phenotypic segregation. More broadly, our results underscore how even simple environmental structures can alter evolutionary outcomes. While our model simplifies certain aspects,such as fixed optima and static environments, it provides a foundation for exploring more complex scenarios, such as multiple optima
Lenormand2015
, temporal change
Kopp2014
,
Otto2020
and evolving assortative mating, as recently suggest by empirical studies
johannesson2024diverse
.
