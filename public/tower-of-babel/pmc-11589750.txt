Development and validation of nomogram models for severe and fatal COVID-19
Authors: Jiahao Chen, Qingfeng Hu, Ruifang Zhong, Ling Li, Yanli Kang, Liangyuan Chen, Rongfu Huang, Jianbin You
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) has exhibited escalating contagion and resistance to immunity, resulting in a surge in infections and severe cases. This study endeavors to formulate two nomogram predictive models aimed at discerning patients at heightened risk of severe and fatal outcomes upon hospital admission. The primary objective is to enhance clinical management protocols and mitigate the incidence of severe illness and mortality associated with COVID-19.Methods1600 patients diagnosed with COVID-19 and discharged from Fujian Provincial Hospital were chosen as the subjects of this study. These patients were categorized into three groups: mild group (n = 940), severe group (n = 433), and fatal group (n = 227). The patients were randomly divided into training and validation cohorts in a 7:3 ratio. COVID-19 symptoms were treated as dependent variables, and univariate regression analysis was conducted for the laboratory indicators. Risk factors with p-values greater than 0.05 in the univariate regression analysis were eliminated. The remaining risk factors were then analyzed using direct multiple regression analysis to establish an unadjusted model. Subsequently, risk factors with p-values greater than 0.05 were further removed. Clinical characteristics were added to the model as adjustment factors, and the method of multiple stepwise regression analysis was employed to derive the final fully adjusted model. The severe and fatal COVID-19 models were converted into nomograms, respectively. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were utilized to evaluate the discrimination of the nomogram models. Calibration was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test and calibration curves. Clinical benefit was evaluated by decision curve analysis.ResultsCompared to the mild group, individuals in the severe COVID-19 group exhibited significant increases in age, neutrophil (NEU), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels, alongside notable decreases in lymphocyte (LYM) and albumin (ALB) levels. Nomogram model incorporating age, NEU, LDH, LYM, and ALB demonstrated efficacy in predicting the onset of severe COVID-19 (AUC = 0.771). Furthermore, history of cerebral infarction and cancer, LDH and ALB as risk factors for fatal COVID-19 cases compared to the severe group. The nomogram model comprising these factors was capable of early identification of COVID-19 fatalities (AUC = 0.748).ConclusionsElevated age, NEU, and LDH levels, along with decreased LYM and albumin (ALB) levels, are risk factors for severe illness in hospitalized patients with COVID-19. A history of cerebral infarction and tumors, along with elevated LDH and decreased ALB levels, are risk factors for death in critically ill patients. The nomogram model based on these factors can effectively predict the risk of severe or fatal illness from COVID-19, thereby assisting clinicians in timely interventions to reduce the rates of severe illness and mortality among hospitalized patients. However, the model faces challenges in processing longitudinal data and specific points in time, indicating that there is room for improvement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-80310-8.Keywords: COVID-19, Risk factors, Nomogram model, Development, ValidationSubject terms: Risk factors, Predictive markers

Introduction

The emergence of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) has presented unprecedented challenges to global health, necessitating swift and effective medical responses1. Since its inception, the disease has shown a remarkable ability to spread, resulting in a pandemic with substantial morbidity and mortality2. As healthcare systems worldwide contend with the influx of cases, the necessity for precise predictive tools to evaluate the risk of disease severity and clinical outcomes has become indispensable.

Despite significant advancements in recent research on COVID-19 prediction models, there remains considerable room for improvement across all aspects. Over time, the virus has evolved with the emergence of new variants, each potentially exhibiting distinct levels of transmissibility and severity3. Models developed early in the pandemic may not adequately account for the clinical behavior of newer variants, thereby reducing their accuracy as the virus continues to evolve4. Furthermore, many models incorporate a large number of predictors5,6. While this can enhance prediction accuracy in some cases, it can also lead to overfitting, particularly when the dataset is relatively small or the outcomes are infrequent. Additionally, the more complex the prediction model is, the more challenging it becomes to apply and promote in clinical practice7. Therefore, we focus on developing an easy-to-use, visual, and current COVID-19 nomogram model for predicting severe or fatal outcomes.

Nomogram models in clinical medicine are invaluable tools for risk stratification, guiding patient triage, and informing treatment strategies8,9. In the context of COVID-19, where the spectrum of disease ranges from mild to critical illness, the capability to predict disease progression is critical for the efficient allocation of healthcare resources and the provision of targeted care to those at highest risk10,11. Despite the urgency, the development of robust and clinically useful nomogram models for COVID-19 has been impeded by several factors, including the dynamic nature of the disease, the rapid accumulation of heterogeneous data, and the lack of a unified understanding of disease pathogenesis12–14. Nevertheless, with the expanding body of research and increasing availability of clinical data, there is a renewed opportunity to develop models that can reliably predict disease severity and patient outcomes.

In this study, our aim is to introduce novel nomogram models, based on a comprehensive set of data encompassing clinical, laboratory, and demographic variables, intended to forecast the severity of COVID-19 in individual patients. We believe that the nomogram models presented herein will provide clinicians with a valuable tool for the early identification of patients at risk of adverse outcomes, thereby facilitating timely and appropriate clinical interventions.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

The study enrolled a total of 1600 COVID-19 patients from Fujian Provincial Hospital between October 2022 and October 2023. Diagnostic and staging criteria were based on the diagnosis and treatment protocol for COVID-19 patients (tentative 10th version) in China15. Mild patients met the following conditions: presenting clinical manifestations related to COVID-19 infection and having one or more of the following etiological and serological test results: (1) Positive result of RT-PCR for SARS-CoV-2 RNA, (2) Positive result of antigen test for SARS-CoV-2. Severe patients concurrently fulfilled at least one of the following criteria, which could not be explained by reasons other than infection with SARS-CoV-2: (a) Presence of dyspnea, with a respiratory rate (RR) ≥ 30 breaths per minute, (b) Oxygen saturation (SpO2) during rest with ambient air inhalation ≤ 93%, (c) Arterial oxygen partial pressure (PaO2)/Fraction of Inspired Oxygen (FiO2) ratio ≤ 300 mmHg, (d) Progressive worsening of clinical symptoms, with significant radiographic progression of lesions exceeding 50% within 24 to 48 h, (e) Onset of respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation, (f) Development of shock, (g) Need for ICU monitoring and treatment.

Clinical characteristics and laboratory data

Utilizing electronic medical records and telephone verification, we obtained epidemiological characteristics, including recent exposure history, clinical symptoms and signs, as well as laboratory test results. Laboratory results comprised blood cell count, blood chemistry, coagulation function, arterial blood gas analysis, inflammatory cytokines, myocardial infarction, and heart failure markers, with the time frame limited to within the first 48 h of admission. Data with a missing ratio exceeding 5% were excluded for subsequent analysis.

Statistical analysis

SPSS version 27.0 and R software were employed for the comprehensive data analysis. The distribution of general demographic characteristics and laboratory indicators within the study population was meticulously outlined and tabulated. Count data were denoted as n (%), while measurement data not adhering to a normal distribution post-normality testing were represented by Medians [Interquartile ranges (IQRs)]. The chi-square test was utilized for categorical variables, while the Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test was applied for measurement data. Pairwise comparisons were executed through the Kruskal-Wallis single-factor ANOVA.

Employing a random number allocation method, the research subjects were partitioned into training and validation sets at a ratio of 3:7, the balance between the training and validation cohorts was assessed using the statistical comparison methods described above to ensure that the distribution of the variables was not significantly skewed. The training set was used to develop the nomogram model, and the validation set was used to validate the developed model. The influencing factors of significant difference were categorized into two main categories: laboratory indices and clinical characteristics. COVID-19 symptoms were treated as dependent variables, and univariate regression analysis was conducted for the laboratory indicators. Risk factors with p-values greater than 0.05 in the univariate regression analysis were eliminated. The remaining risk factors were then analyzed using direct multiple regression analysis to establish an unadjusted model. Subsequently, risk factors with p-values greater than 0.05 were further removed. Clinical characteristics were added to the model as adjustment factors, and the method of multiple stepwise regression analysis was employed to derive the final fully adjusted model. The severe and fatal COVID-19 models constructed are converted into nomograms respectively.

The variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to detect multicollinearity among covariates, with a VIF greater than 10 indicating the presence of multicollinearity. The ROC curve was generated, and the model’s validity was ascertained by computing the area under the ROC curve (AUC). The Hosmer-Lemeshow test and Bootstrapping test were deployed to assess the risk model. Clinical benefit was assessed by decision curve analysis. A significance level of α = 0.05 was predetermined for all analyses, which were two-tailed, with P < 0.05 deemed statistically significant.

Results

Clinical characteristics

A total of 1,600 COVID-19 patients were enrolled in this study, comprising 940 patients in the mild group, 433 in the severe group, and 227 in the fatal group. Analysis of clinical characteristics revealed statistically significant differences among the three groups concerning factors such as age, gender, diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, cancer, and cerebral infarction.

Further pairwise analysis demonstrated that, in comparison to the mild group, patients in the severe and fatal groups were older, a higher proportion of males, and a greater incidence of cardiovascular diseases (CVD). Additionally, compared to the mild group, the fatal group presented with a higher prevalence of diabetes, hypertension, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and cerebral infarction. Moreover, the fatal group showed advanced age and a higher prevalence of diabetes, hypertension, CVD, cancer, and cerebral infarction when compared to the severe group (Table 1).

Validation of nomogram models

The results indicate that the AUC values of the training set and validation set for the severe COVID-19 model were 0.771 (95% CI 0.742–0.798) and 0.758 (95% CI 0.714–0.798) respectively, demonstrating the model’s strong discriminatory ability (Fig. 2A and B). Furthermore, the AUC value for the training set of the fatal COVID-19 model was 0.748 (95% CI: 0.699–0.797), with the validation set AUC value at 0.739 (95% CI: 0.668–0.810), indicating the model’s capability in effectively predicting patients’ risk of death (Fig. 2C and D).

The standard curve was utilized to assess the variance between the model’s probability and the actual probability. The findings revealed minimal variance between the predicted and actual probabilities, affirming the model’s satisfactory fit (Fig. 3A and B). Additionally, the results from the Hosmer-Lemeshow test indicated a high degree of goodness-of-fit for both models (p > 0.05). Furthermore, a Bootstrapping test was conducted to assess potential overfitting of the models. The validation outcomes demonstrated an average AUC of 0.772 for the severe COVID-19 model and 0.737 for the fatal COVID-19 model, suggesting robust model fit with high stability and effectiveness (Fig. 3C and D).

Discussion

Several years have passed since the onset of the COVID-19 outbreak, yet the global health crisis it instigated persists16. While the majority experience mild to moderate symptoms, a subset of patients develops severe COVID-19, characterized by life-threatening complications such as acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), multiple organ failure, necessitating hospitalization, and intensive care17,18. Grasping the factors contributing to severe COVID-19 and advancements in therapeutic strategies are pivotal for pandemic management and life-saving endeavors.

Epidemiological data indicate that age is a significant risk factor for severe COVID-19 outcomes. The Chinese government significantly relaxed its COVID-19 regulations on December 7, 2022, when the elderly population lacked acquired immunity, vaccination coverage was relatively low, and access to effective antiviral drugs was limited19. This large-scale and rapid epidemic infected approximately 90% of China’s population and killed 1.41 million people, of which 800,000 (56.7%) of them were adults over 80 years old20. In our study, the median age of the severe and fatal groups was 77 and 82 years, respectively, and they also showed a higher mortality rate (34.4%). With no clear threshold, and the risk escalates sharply with advancing age, a trend consistent with our findings21. The age-related decline in immune function, known as immunosenescence is believed to contribute to heightened COVID-19 severity in older individuals, chiefly evidenced by aberrant LYM and NEU counts22,23. This study underscores that a decrease in LYM count and an increase in NEU count are hallmark features of severe COVID-19. LYM, encompassing T and B cells, play a pivotal role in adaptive immunity and orchestrate immune responses against viral infections24. Diminished LYM count has been linked to adverse prognosis and heightened mortality in COVID-19 patients25. NEU, frequently observed concomitantly with LYM in severe COVID-19 cases, constitute the frontline defense against infections but can exacerbate immunopathology when excessively activated26. In COVID-19, NEU have been found to release pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, fostering inflammation and tissue damage, thereby contributing to the intricate immunopathological cascades observed in severe disease27.

Furthermore, ALB and LDH also emerge as risk factors for severe or fatal COVID-19, as corroborated in our investigation. ALB and LDH serve as pivotal markers of inflammation within the body28,29. In the context of COVID-19, hypoalbuminemia reflects the severity of systemic inflammatory response and portends worse clinical outcomes, correlating with poor prognosis30. LDH, an enzyme catalyzing the conversion of lactate to pyruvate, exhibits elevation in patients with severe COVID-19, indicative of organ damage extent and the hyperinflammatory state known as a cytokine storm31,32. It is evident that inflammation constitutes the pivotal link precipitating severe COVID-19. Consequently, the nomogram model developed in this study, predicated on inflammation-related parameters such as age, LYM, NEU, ALB, and LDH demonstrated promising predictive efficacy, furnishing a foundation for early detection and clinical management of severe cases. Although the predictive efficacy of the logistic regression model established by Schmidt W et al. is close to that of our model, our approach utilizes fewer risk factors, making it more convenient for clinical use33. Additionally, while the nomogram model developed by Qian FH achieves an AUC of 0.981, it includes numerous predictors, which increases the potential for overfitting6.

While early detection of severe COVID-19 can mitigate its malignant progression, for patients already afflicted with severe manifestations, mitigating or predicting the risk of mortality becomes paramount. Our research delineates that, apart from LDH and ALB, cancer and cerebral infarction as additional death risk factors in COVID-19 patients. Existing research indicates that cancer treatments (such as chemotherapy and immunotherapy) can inhibit the immune system, thereby increasing the risk of infection with COVID-19 among cancer patients, as well as significantly elevating the risk of severe COVID-1934,35. Patients with confirmed cerebral infarction harbor a thrombotic or pre-thrombotic disposition, heightening the susceptibility to pulmonary complications that can precipitate fatal outcomes in COVID-19 cases36. However, the relationship between cerebral infarction and COVID-19 is intricate and multifaceted. COVID-19-induced pre-thrombotic states and cytokine storms can exacerbate existing cardiovascular ailments, augmenting acute mortality rates among patients37. In our study, a nomogram model for fatal COVID-19 was devised by amalgamating cancer, cerebral infarction, LDH and ALB. The nomogram model could be used to predict deaths from COVID-19. enabling early prediction of fatal COVID-19 and efficaciously reducing patient mortality rates. Although the prediction performance of Sun X, et al.‘s model (Model 1: AUC = 0.874, Model 2: AUC = 0.804) was better than that of our model (AUC = 0.748), the number of cases was seriously insufficient, with only 53 deaths in the group, which compromised the model’s accuracy. Notwithstanding the predictive model established by Emami H et al. using machine learning also showed higher performance than our model, it had defects in transparency and reproducibility, making it difficult for clinicians to understand and use the model39.

Our COVID-19 risk prediction model, based on clinical features, routine blood tests, and biochemical indicators, demonstrates practical and cost-effective advantages compared to models that involve expensive tests such as immune and coagulation markers. This is particularly significant in China’s increasingly restrictive health insurance environment, where patients with mild symptoms face severe limitations in accessing many laboratory tests. Consequently, the risk model presented in this study offers broad availability and simplicity, thereby supporting faster clinical decision-making, enabling early intervention, and optimizing resource allocation.

Conclusions

In summary, this study established nomogram models for severe and fatal COVID-19 based on epidemiological data and laboratory examinations of patients. Internal validation of these models demonstrated their effectiveness in prediction. To facilitate the implementation of these nomograms in everyday clinical settings, it is recommended to integrate the nomogram within the electronic health record (EHR) system. This integration will enable clinicians to easily access and apply predictive tools in real-time. Furthermore, patient-specific data is automatically populated into the model, minimizing errors and streamlining workflows. To ensure proper use, healthcare providers will require training on how to interpret and apply nomogram predictions. Training programs should be integrated into existing medical education, emphasizing an understanding of key variables and translating risk scores into actionable clinical decisions. Validation of the nomograms in diverse patient populations and settings will be critical to confirm their universality before widespread adoption. Continuous monitoring and feedback gathering from healthcare providers will allow for improvements to be made based on actual performance. It is believed that this nomogram model will play an important role in improving risk stratification, supporting shared decision-making, and optimizing clinical resource allocation. However, there are several limitations to this study. The lack of external validation is a significant constraint, hindering the broader application of the models. Additionally, due to the dynamic nature of certain laboratory examination indicators during the development of COVID-19, it is not possible to fully capture the dynamic clinical process of the disease by collecting data at a single time point (e.g., upon admission). Furthermore, being a retrospective study with a small sample size, the real-world performance of the prediction models may be affected. Therefore, the research team plans to collect data from multiple time points in the future, expand the sample size of the model, and collaborate with other institutions to perform external validation. These efforts aim to enhance the model’s efficiency and applicability.
