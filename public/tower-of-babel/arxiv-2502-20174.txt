Strong-damping limit of quantum Brownian motion in a disordered environment
Arthur M. Faria 1,2, Marcus V. S. Bonança 1, Eric Lutz 2

Abstract
We consider a microscopic model of an inhomogeneous environment where an arbitrary quantum system is locally coupled to a harmonic bath via a finite-range interaction. We show that in the overdamped regime the position distribution obeys a classical Kramers-Moyal equation that involves an infinite number of higher derivatives, implying that the finite bath correlation length leads to non-Gaussian Markovian noise. We analytically solve the equation for a harmonically bound particle and analyze its non-Gaussian diffusion as well as its steady-state properties.

II
Model for local bath coupling

We consider a general one-dimensional quantum system, with position
x
and momentum
p
, that is locally coupled to a heat bath made of infinitely many harmonic oscillators, with canonical coordinates
\left(q_{n},p_{n}\right)
, mass
m_{n}
and frequency
\omega_{n}
(Fig. 1).
The total Hamiltonian reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{S}+\mathcal{H}_{I}+\mathcal{H}_{B},

(1)

where the respective system, interaction and bath Hamiltonians,
\mathcal{H}_{S}
,
\mathcal{H}_{I}
and

are given by
[

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{S}=\frac{p^{2}}{2}+V(x),

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{I}=-\sum_{n}c_{n}\,q_{n}u(x-x_{n}),

(3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{B}=\sum_{n}\frac{p_{n}^{2}}{2m_{n}}+\frac{1}{2}m_{n}\omega_{n}^{2}q_{n}^{2}.

(4)

The system, with mass
m=1
, moves in a confining potential
V(x)
. The scattering potential
u(x-x_{n})
further specifies the local interaction between the system and the bath; it depends solely on the distance between the system and the fixed location
x_{n}
of
n
-th bath oscillator. The disordered environment is characterized by the spatial autocorrelation function
w(r)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,u(r-x)u(x)
, with a finite correlation length
l
. For concreteness, we will use the Gaussian form,

w(r)=l^{2}\,\exp\left[-r^{2}/2l^{2}\right]
, in the following
[
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
]
. The spectral density of the bath,
J(\omega)=(\pi/2)\sum_{n}(c_{n}^{2}/m_{n}\omega_{n})\delta(\omega-\omega_{n})\sim\omega
is additionally assumed to be linear at low frequencies, corresponding to an Ohmic, that is, Markovian bath (
c_{n}
are the bath coupling constants)
[
1
,
2
,
5
,
4
,
6
,
3
]
. Hamiltonian (1) is often referred to as ’diffusion, localization and dissipation’ model
[

(6)

\displaystyle G_{N}(p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\hslash^{2}}\mathcal{FT}_{r\to p/\hslash}\left\{\left[w(r)-w(0)\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(\hslash/l)^{2}}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}(\hslash/l)}e^{-\frac{(p-p^{\prime})^{2}}{2(\hslash/l)^{2}}}-\delta\left(p-p^{\prime}\right)\right].

(7)

Both expressions, that are nonlocal in

. The symbol
*
represents the convolution operation.

In the limit of a global system-bath interaction,
l\to\infty
(no disorder),

. As a result,

)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}W=-p\,\partial_{x}W+V^{\prime}(x)\partial_{p}W+\gamma\partial_{p}\left[pW\right]+\nu\partial_{p}^{2}W.

(8)

III
Strong-damping limit

We next consider the strong-friction limit of Eq. (
5
). For large damping, the momentum is expected to quickly relax to its stationary limit, so that (for infinite friction), the phase-space distribution can be factorized as
W(x,p,t)=\varrho_{s}(p)\rho(x,t)
, where

\varrho_{s}(p)
denotes the stationary momentum distribution
[
7
,
8
]
. In the following, we shall derive a generalized Smoluchowski equation by eliminating the fast momentum variable
[
35
,
36
]
.

We begin by rewriting the phase-space equation (
5
) in the form of an infinite sum that will be more convenient to analyze. To that end, we note that the friction and noise kernels can be expressed as (Appendix A)

\displaystyle G_{F}(p)*\bm{[}\,p\,\bm{\cdot\,]}\to\exp(\frac{(\hslash/l)^2\partial_p^2}{2})\bm{[}\,p\,\bm{\cdot\,]},

(9)

\displaystyle G_{N}(p)*\bm{[\,\cdot\,]}\to\frac{1}{(\hslash/l)^{2}}\left(\exp(\frac{(\hslash/l)^2\partial_p^2}{2})-1\right)\bm{[\,\cdot\,]}.

(10)

By expanding the two exponentials in Eqs. (
9
) and (
10
)
in a power series, we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}W

\displaystyle=-p\partial_{x}W+V^{\prime}(x)\partial_{p}W+\gamma\partial_{p}\left[p\mathcal{W}\right]+\nu\partial_{p}^{2}W

\displaystyle+\gamma\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\gamma_{n}\partial_{p}^{2n+1}\left[pW\right]+\frac{\nu}{(\hslash/l)^{2}}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\gamma_{n}\partial_{p}^{2n}W.

(11)

where
\gamma_{n}=(\hslash/l)^{2n}/(2^{n}n!)
(for simplicity, we will henceforth set
\hslash=1
). The first line corresponds to the standard Klein-Kramers equation (
l\to\infty
), while the two infinite sums on the second line, that involve all higher derivatives, describe the influence of a finite length
l
.

\displaystyle\partial_{t}W

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma}\partial_{x}\left[V^{\prime}(x)W\right]+\nu\partial_{p}^{2}W

\displaystyle\hskip 8.53581pt+\gamma\left(\partial_{p}-\frac{1}{\gamma}\partial_{x}\right)\left(pW+\frac{V^{\prime}(x)}{\gamma}W\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 8.53581pt+\gamma\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\gamma_{n}\partial_{p}^{2n+1}\left[pW\right]+\nu l^{2}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\gamma_{n}\partial_{p}^{2n}W,

(12)

and proceed by integrating both sides of the equation along the straight phase-space line
x+\gamma^{-1}p=x^{\prime}
with line element
ds

[
8
]
. In the strong damping limit, the marginal
\rho(x_{,}t)
follows from
W(x,p)
as

\displaystyle\int_{x+\gamma^{-1}p}ds\,W\ \underset{\gamma\to\infty}{\to}\ \int dp\,W=\rho(x^{\prime},t).

(13)

In addition, we have
\partial_{x}^{n}=\partial_{x^{\prime}}^{n}
and
\partial_{p}^{n}=(-\gamma)^{-n}\partial_{x}^{n}
along the integration path. As a consequence,
\partial_{p}-\gamma^{-1}\partial_{x^{\prime}}
is a null operator along this path, which eliminates the contribution coming from the first two terms in Eq. (
12
). Moreover, since
\int ds\,\partial_{p}^{2n+1}[p\,W]\to_{\gamma\to\infty}\gamma^{-2n}\,\partial_{x^{\prime}}^{2n}\rho
, the fourth term in Eq. (
12
) reduces to

\displaystyle\!\!\gamma\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\gamma_{n}\int ds\,\partial_{p}^{2n+1}[p\,W]\underset{\gamma\to\infty}{\to}\gamma\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1/\gamma l)^{2n}}{2^{n}n!}\,\partial_{x^{\prime}}^{2n}\rho.\!\!

(14)

A similar calculation applied to the fifth term yields

\displaystyle\nu l^{2}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\gamma_{n}\int ds\,\partial_{p}^{2n}W\underset{\gamma\to\infty}{\to}\nu l^{2}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{\gamma_{n}}{\gamma^{2n}}\partial_{x^{\prime}}^{2n}\rho.

(15)

Combining all the terms, we obtain the generalized overdamped Smoluchowski equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma}\partial_{x}\left[V^{\prime}(x)\rho\right]+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{2n}}{(2n)!}\partial_{x}^{2n}\rho,

(16)

where
a_{2n}=(\gamma+{\nu l^{2}})(2n)!/[{2^{n}}{(\gamma l)^{2n}n!}]
; without loss of generality, we have replaced
x^{\prime}\to x
. Equation (
16
) has the form of a classical Kramers-Moyal equation that includes both Gaussian and non-Gaussian white noise contributions
[
32
,
33
]
: The first two terms describe deterministic drift plus continuous Gaussian diffusion, whereas the higher-order terms (
n\geq 2
) correspond to discrete jumps and to non-Gaussian white noise
[
32
,
33
]
. In the limit of a global system-bath interaction,
l\to\infty
,
the standard overdamped Smoluchowski equation is recovered

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma}\partial_{x}\left[V^{\prime}(x)\rho\right]+\frac{\nu}{2\gamma^{2}}\partial_{x}^{2}\rho\,,

(17)

with only Gaussian noise.
Note that in this case the Einstein relation,
\nu/2\gamma=kT

[
32
,
33
]
, where

the Boltzmann constant, implies the coefficients

\displaystyle a_{2n}=\frac{\gamma\left[1+2kTl^{2}\right]}{2^{n}n!(\gamma l)^{2n}}.

(18)

We may thus conclude that the effect of a finite correlation length
l
is the occurrence of discrete non-Gaussian Markovian jump events in the overdamped dynamics. It is worthwhile mentioning that this strongly differs from the coupling to a finite number of bath modes, which usually leads to non-Markovian dynamics
[
37
,
38
,
39
]
. In the present case, the system is still coupled to all the bath modes, but with a modulated coupling strength.

IV
Harmonically bound particle

Let us now investigate the dynamics of a harmonically bound particle with potential
V(x)=\kappa x^{2}/2
, where
\kappa
is the spring constant.
For this purpose, we solve Eq. (
16
) in Fourier space, and introduce the characteristic function

\varphi(z,t)=\mathcal{FT}_{x\to z}\left\{\rho(x,t)\right\}
which is defined via
[
7
,
8
]

\displaystyle\rho(x,t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dz\,e^{izx}\varphi(z,t).

(19)

The master equation for
\varphi(z,t)
accordingly reads

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\kappa}{\gamma}z\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial z}+\frac{kT}{\gamma}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}D_{2n}(iz)^{2n}\varphi,

(20)

with the coefficients
D_{2n}=\gamma a_{2n}/kT(2n)!
. Equation (
20
) can be solved with the method of characteristics
[
7
,
8
]
. With the initial condition
\rho(x,0)=\delta(x)
, we find

\displaystyle\varphi(z,t)

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\varepsilon\frac{D_{2}}{2}(iz)^{2}(1-e^{-2\kappa t/\gamma})\right]

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[\varepsilon\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{D_{2n}}{2n}(iz)^{2n}\left(1-e^{-2n\kappa t/\gamma}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\varphi^{G}(z,t)\varphi^{NG}(z,t),

(21)

where
\varepsilon=kT/\kappa
. We observe that
\varphi(z,t)
can be written as a product of two, Gaussian and non-Gaussian, components,
\varphi^{G}(z,t)
and
\varphi^{NG}(z,t)
, where the latter term encodes the contribution of the finite bath correlation length
l
. The asymptotic steady states
\varphi_{s}(z)
is moreover approached exponentially in time.

Additional insight into the time evolution of the system and the effect of the inhomogeneous bath coupling may be gained by evaluating the moments of the position distribution
\rho(x)
,
M_{m}(t)=i^{m}\left.\partial_{z}^{m}\varphi(z,t)\right|_{z=0}

[
7
,
8
]
. Variance and kurtosis are explicitly given by

\displaystyle\text{Var}(t)=M_{2}(t)=\frac{1+2kTl^{2}}{2\kappa l^{2}}(1-e^{-2t\kappa/\gamma}),

(22)

\displaystyle\text{Kurt}(t)=\frac{M_{4}(t)}{\text{Var}(t)^{2}}=3+\frac{3\kappa\coth{\left(t\kappa/\gamma\right)}}{\gamma^{2}\left(1+2kTl^{2}\right)}.

(23)

We note that all the odd moments (
m\geq 3
) of
\rho(x)
vanish, since the Kramers-Moyal expansion (
16
) only contains even higher derivatives (
n\geq 2
). We additionally mention that the exponential relaxation of the mean,
M_{1}(t)=x_{0}e^{-\kappa t/\gamma}
, from an initial position
x_{0}\neq 0
is not affected by the value of
l
. For long times, we further have

\displaystyle\text{Var}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\text{Var}(t)=\frac{1+2kTl^{2}}{2\kappa l^{2}}\,,

(24)

\displaystyle\text{Kurt}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\text{Kurt}(t)=3+\frac{3\kappa}{\gamma^{2}\left(1+2kTl^{2}\right)}\,.

(25)

A finite value of the bath correlation length
l
therefore enhances the value of the variance above its Gaussian limit (Fig. 2a), indicating that the system diffuses faster owing to the additional non-Gaussian fluctuations.
At
the same time, it increases the kurtosis, making the position distribution non-Gaussian (Fig. 2b).

The case of free diffusion can be easily recovered by taking the limit
\kappa\to 0
. The corresponding variance reads

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{0}(t)=\left(\frac{1+2kTl^{2}}{\kappa l^{2}}\right)t,

(26)

showing that it is linear in time, as for normal Brownian motion
[
7
,
8
]
, albeit with an enhanced diffusion constant,
D=(1+2kTl^{2})/(2\kappa l^{2})
, which can be regarded as a generalized Einstein relation
[
7
,
8
]
. We hence have the remarkable result that the local bath coupling does not modify the linear time-dependence of the variance. The diffusion constant of standard Brownian motion,
D_{0}=kT/\kappa
is again obtained for an infinite correlation length.

We proceed by determining the steady-state distribution
\rho_{s}(x)
which is given by the inverse Fourier transform

\displaystyle\rho_{s}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dz\,e^{\varepsilon\Gamma(z)},

(27)

where the exponent reads

\displaystyle\Gamma(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{iz}{\varepsilon}x+\varphi_{s}(z)=\frac{iz}{\varepsilon}x+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{D_{2n}}{2n}(iz)^{2n}.

(28)

The integral (
27
) cannot be evaluated exactly. However, since
\varepsilon=kT/\kappa\gg 1
a saddle-point method is applicable
[
40
]
. In the limit
\varepsilon\gg 1
, the integral is indeed dominated by the maximum,
z=q
, of the function
\Gamma(z)
which satisfies
\partial_{z}\Gamma=0
. To leading order, one has
[
40
]

\displaystyle\rho_{s}(x)=\exp[\varepsilon\Gamma(q)]/\sqrt{2\pi\varepsilon\left|\partial_{z}^{2}\Gamma(q)\right|}.

(29)

Setting the derivative of Eq. (
28
) to zero, we find

\displaystyle\partial_{q}\varphi(q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}D_{2n}(q)^{2n-1}=-\frac{ix}{\varepsilon},

(30)

whose solution determines
q=q(x)
. We further have

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{q}^{2}\Gamma\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(2n-1)D_{2n}\,(iq)^{2n-2}\right|.

(31)

In order to solve Eq. (
30
), we note that since
\varepsilon
is large, we may, to leading order, use the approximation
q=q_{0}+\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon)
. We hence obtain

\displaystyle iq_{0}=-\frac{x}{\varepsilon D_{2}}=-\frac{2\kappa x}{\gamma a_{2}}.

(32)

Combining everything, we eventually arrive at the steady-state position distribution

\displaystyle\rho_{s}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi\gamma/\kappa}}{\left|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2n-1}{(2n)!}a_{2n}\,\left(\frac{2\kappa x}{\gamma a_{2}}\right)^{2n-2}\right|^{1/2}}

\displaystyle\times\exp\hskip-0.85358pt\left[-\frac{\kappa x^{2}}{\gamma a_{2}}\right]\exp\hskip-0.85358pt\left[\frac{\gamma}{\kappa}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\hskip-1.42262pt\frac{a_{2n}}{2n(2n)!}\hskip-1.42262pt\left(\frac{2\kappa x}{\gamma a_{2}}\right)^{2n}\hskip-1.42262pt\right]

\displaystyle=\rho_{s}^{G}(x)\rho_{s}^{NG}(x).

(33)

As before, the stationary distribution can be expressed as a product of Gaussian and non-Gaussian contributions,
\rho_{s}^{G}(x)
and
\rho_{s}^{NG}(x)
. Since
a_{2}\to 2kT/\gamma
and
a_{2n}\to 0
(for
n>2
), when
l\to\infty
, the exact Gaussian position distribution is recovered in the limit of large
l

[
7
,
8
]

\displaystyle\rho_{s}^{G}(x)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{2\pi kT}}\exp\left[-\frac{\kappa x^{2}}{2kT}\right].

(34)

\rho_{s}(x)
(i) determined using the Kramers-Moyal equation (

It is important to realize that due to the infinite sum appearing in the exponent of the non-Gaussian part, the steady-state solution (
33
) is not always properly normalized, that is, the zeroth moment of the distribution,
M_{0}
, is not always equal to one. This is the case for small values of the correlation length
l
, when the distribution becomes too broad.
We estimate the minimal value of the parameter
l
that leads to a properly normalizable stationary position distribution, by analyzing the convergence of the infinite sum that appears in the Kramers-Moyal equation (
16
). A necessary condition for convergence is that
a_{2(n+1)}/(2(n+1))!<a_{2n}/(2n)!

[
41
]
. In particular, the condition
a_{4}/4<a_{2}/2
implies that
l>l_{min}=1/2\gamma
. The minimal value of the correlation length is therefore determined by the damping coefficient
\gamma
. Figure 3b shows the normalization
M_{0}
of the stationary position distribution (

l^{-1}
for
\gamma=10
. The zeroth moment is equal to one until the value
l^{-1}=20
is reached, after which it sharply drops. This threshold agrees with the estimate
l_{min}^{-1}=2\gamma=20
(gray shaded area). We further note that the condition
a_{2n}\ll a_{2}
ensures a positive distribution
\rho(x,t)
when the Kramers-Moyal equation is truncated at a finite order (
n>2
)
[
7
,

]
.

V
Conclusions

The microscopic origin of non-Gaussian noise in systems coupled to a heat bath is still unclear
[
42
,
43
,
44
]
. Arguments based on the central-limit theorem indeed always predict Gaussian noise
[
33
,
34
]
. In the present study, we have found that non-Gaussian noise naturally occurs in the overdamped limit of a quantum particle locally coupled to an inhomogeneous high-temperature environment that can be modeled as an ensemble of linear harmonic oscillators. Contrary to the usual case of infinite-range bath coupling, a finite spatial correlation length leads to a position distribution that evolves according to a Kramers-Moyal equation involving all higher derivatives. Such Markovian equation describes Gaussian diffusion plus non-Gaussian discrete jump events. As a consequence, diffusion is boosted by the presence of non-Gaussian fluctuations and the steady-state solution of a damped harmonic oscillator exhibit non-Gaussian tails.
