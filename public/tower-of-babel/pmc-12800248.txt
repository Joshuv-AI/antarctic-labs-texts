A hybrid multi-scale indirect vision detector algorithm for heritage building fire detection
Authors: Jian Liang, Junsheng Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAddressing the challenges of small target detection difficulties and unbalanced vision in indirect vision technology for heritage building fire detection, we propose the HMIV-DET hybrid multi-scale indirect vision detection algorithm. This algorithm incorporates three key innovations: Adaptive Indirect Vision Enhancement (AIVE) resolves vision imbalance issues in multi-mirror deployments through dynamic weight allocation; Adaptive Multi-kernel Feature Orchestration Block (AMFOBlock) employs parallel multi-scale feature extraction and gated activation mechanisms to enhance the capture capability for flame features of different sizes; and Hierarchical Cross-Scale Feature Fusion Network (HCSFPN) achieves comprehensive interaction of features across multiple semantic levels. Experiments on the self-constructed Heritage Building Indirect Vision Fire Dataset demonstrate that compared to the YOLO11 baseline, mAP50 and mAP50-95 improve by 2.8% and 3.3% respectively, significantly enhancing detection performance while maintaining lightweight characteristics.Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Heritage buildings, as vital carriers of historical and cultural information, possess irreplaceable cultural value and historical significance. However, fire poses a severe threat to heritage structures, often resulting in irrecoverable losses to cultural heritage once it occurs1. Statistics indicate that the United Kingdom and Scotland experience hundreds of fire incidents affecting heritage buildings annually, with similar alarming loss figures worldwide. The predominant use of wooden structures in heritage buildings, combined with material deterioration due to age, significantly increases fire risk, urgently necessitating the establishment of effective early fire detection systems2. Timely and accurate fire detection not only minimizes heritage losses but also serves as a crucial technical means for protecting human cultural heritage and maintaining historical continuity.

Traditional fire detection systems primarily rely on physical sensor devices such as smoke detectors and temperature sensors for fire identification. Smoke detectors detect smoke particles in the air through photoelectric or ionization principles, while temperature detectors monitor environmental temperature changes to determine fire occurrence3. Thermal detectors include fixed-temperature and rate-of-rise types, triggering alarms through set temperature thresholds and temperature rise rates respectively. However, traditional sensor methods suffer from delayed response, high false alarm rates, and sensitivity to environmental conditions4. Particularly in the complex indoor environments of heritage buildings, factors such as dust, humidity changes, and cooking activities easily lead to frequent false alarms, reducing system reliability and user trust.

In recent years, with the rapid development of computer vision and deep learning technologies, image-based fire detection methods have gradually become research hotspots. Zhang et al. proposed a two-stage deep learning fire detection method for heritage buildings, achieving real-time fire identification through motion detection using Gaussian mixture models followed by the Fire-Det model5. Sathishkumar et al. introduced a forest fire and smoke detection method based on Learning without Forgetting, achieving 79.23% detection accuracy through transfer learning on pre-trained VGG16, Inception V3, and Xception models6. Singh et al. developed a unified wildfire detection system combining classification and segmentation, integrating custom convolutional neural networks with AlexNet for fire identification and employing UNet and SegNet architectures for precise burning area localization, achieving 88.19% accuracy in classification tasks7. Deep learning methods can automatically learn visual features of flames and smoke, demonstrating stronger environmental adaptability and lower false alarm rates compared to traditional approaches. Convolutional neural networks effectively extract flame color, texture, shape, and motion features, maintaining high detection accuracy under complex backgrounds and varying lighting conditions.

Recently, heritage building fire detection algorithms based on object detection have matured progressively, leading us to adopt an object detection algorithm framework for research. Deep learning-based object detection algorithms have achieved significant progress in fire detection. Liang et al. proposed the MITA-YOLO method, combining indirect vision technology with YOLOv8, effectively addressing the limited camera deployment issues in heritage buildings8. Muhammad et al. developed a CNN fire detection system based on the GoogleNet architecture, achieving high-precision flame recognition in surveillance scenarios9. Multiple studies indicate that YOLO series algorithms have become the mainstream choice for real-time fire detection due to their favorable speed-accuracy balance. Saydirasulovich et al. implemented fire detection in smart city environments based on YOLOv6 algorithm, achieving 0.98 object recognition performance and 0.83 detection accuracy on a dataset containing 4000 images10. Geng et al. proposed the YOLOFM algorithm based on improved YOLOv5n network, integrating FocalNext and QAHARep-FPN structures, achieving high-precision detection on a fire and smoke dataset of 18644 images11. Kong et al. obtained 94% detection accuracy in coal mine fire detection based on improved YOLOv8s algorithm12. The newly released YOLO11 algorithm further optimizes network architecture, significantly reducing model parameters while maintaining high detection accuracy, with its adopted C3k2 module and C2PSA attention mechanism demonstrating outstanding performance in small target detection13. Given YOLO11’s superior performance and broad application prospects in fire detection tasks, this study selects YOLO11 as the baseline detection framework.

Despite the mature performance of object detection and YOLO11 algorithms in fire detection tasks, numerous challenges remain in complex heritage building environments. First, the adoption of indirect vision results in size reduction of targets in mirror reflections, making small target flames difficult to detect accurately. Second, mirrors deployed at different positions form indirect vision regions of varying sizes due to distance differences from cameras, creating detection domain unfairness issues that affect overall system detection performance. Complex lighting variations, similar-colored interfering objects (such as sunsets, orange decorations), and smoke diffusion characteristics in complex environments also increase the risk of false positives and negatives.

Therefore, we propose an indirect vision adaptive hybrid network heritage building fire detection algorithm named HMIV-DET, with the following main contributions:

We propose the Adaptive Indirect Vision Enhancement module (AIVE), which systematically addresses vision imbalance issues caused by positional differences in multi-mirror deployments through dynamic weight allocation and spatial redistribution strategies. This module adaptively adjusts detection weights for different mirror regions, ensuring remote indirect vision receives attention comparable to proximal regions, achieving detection domain fairness. Comprehensive comparative experiments demonstrate that AIVE outperforms existing image enhancement methods including CLAHE, Retinex-based approaches, and deep learning-based LLNet by 1.4% in mAP50, validating its superiority in addressing mirror-specific spatial unfairness.

Related work

YOLO11 baseline framework

YOLO11, as the latest generation real-time object detection model released by Ultralytics, incorporates multiple innovative improvements based on the YOLO series algorithms. Compared to previous generation models, YOLO11 achieves higher mean average precision with reduced parameters, primarily attributable to its improved network architecture and optimization strategies. YOLO11’s network architecture consists of three core components: Backbone, Neck, and Detection Head. The backbone network adopts an improved CSPDarkNet architecture, replacing traditional C2f modules with C3k2 modules, achieving more efficient feature extraction through smaller convolutional kernels (33) and optimized cross-stage partial connections. The neck network employs an improved Feature Pyramid Network (FPN) structure, integrating Spatial Pyramid Pooling Fast (SPPF) modules and Path Aggregation Network (PANet) for effective multi-scale feature fusion. The detection head adopts a decoupled design, separating object classification and bounding box regression tasks into different branches, and incorporates the C2PSA (Cross-Scale Pixel Spatial Attention) mechanism to enhance cross-scale pixel spatial attention, significantly improving detection performance for small targets and complex scenes.

Indirect vision enhancement technology

In heritage building fire monitoring systems, indirect vision technology expands camera monitoring range through mirror reflection, effectively addressing vision limitations and equipment deployment constraints faced by traditional monitoring systems. However, existing indirect vision systems exhibit significant spatial unfairness issues, namely monitoring domain imbalance caused by distance differences in mirrors deployed at different positions. Related research in visual enhancement has proposed autoencoder-based deep learning methods to identify signal features in low-light images and adaptively enhance image brightness14, providing a theoretical foundation for addressing image quality issues in indirect vision. Furthermore, in occlusion processing technology, researchers have developed various methods to handle occlusion problems in object detection, including attention mechanism-based feature enhancement15 and multi-view feature fusion techniques16.

Multi-scale object detection in complex scenarios

While small object detection has been the primary focus in fire detection research, real-world heritage building environments present challenges across multiple object scales. Recent advances have addressed these challenges through architectural innovations in feature enhancement, attention mechanisms, and multi-scale aggregation. Li et al. proposed QGMN employing occlusion-guided multi-task learning to handle partial occlusions in UAV-based object detection37, demonstrating that explicit occlusion modeling significantly benefits detection tasks under complex scenarios. For fire detection in buildings with semi-occluded conditions, Shiratkar et al. developed a deep learning approach that effectively identifies flames despite partial visual obstruction38. Li et al. introduced SGINet with scale-robust complementary learning mechanisms for UAV imagery39, while Wu et al. improved YOLO11’s detection capability in dense smoke environments through enhanced multi-modal feature fusion40.

Specialized small object detection methods have emerged to address the unique challenges of detecting targets with limited pixel representation. TPH-YOLOv541 replaces traditional detection heads with transformer prediction heads, leveraging self-attention mechanisms to enhance feature representation for small objects in aerial scenarios. DETR-based approaches have shown promise in small object detection through end-to-end transformer architectures. UAV-DETR42 proposes an efficient DETR framework tailored for UAV imagery, incorporating multi-scale feature fusion with frequency enhancement to capture both spatial and frequency information, along with frequency-focused downsampling to retain critical spatial details during feature extraction. SO-DETR43 addresses small object detection challenges through a dual-domain hybrid encoder that integrates spatial and frequency domains for effective multi-scale feature fusion, combined with an enhanced query selection mechanism using expanded IoU to dynamically optimize query initialization and knowledge distillation for efficient detection. Lightweight approaches like NanoDet-Plus44 demonstrate that anchor-free architectures with dynamic label assignment can achieve real-time performance on mobile devices while maintaining competitive accuracy.

While these multi-scale and small object detection methods achieve impressive results on standard benchmarks, they assume spatially uniform image quality and conventional perspective geometry. However, indirect vision systems introduce unique challenges: mirrors at different distances create heterogeneous detection domains with varying pixel densities and perspective distortions, requiring explicit modeling of spatial unfairness inherent in multi-mirror deployments. Existing methods lack mirror-aware preprocessing mechanisms to establish detection equity across regions with distance-dependent resolution degradation.

Related research on feature fusion networks

Feature Pyramid Network (FPN) is an important technique for multi-scale feature fusion in object detection, constructing high-level semantic feature maps at all scales through top-down architecture and lateral connections, utilizing the inherent multi-scale pyramid hierarchy of deep convolutional networks to build feature pyramids with minimal additional computational cost17. The network design includes bottom-up and top-down pathways, where the bottom-up pathway is the conventional convolutional network feature extraction process with decreasing spatial resolution but enhanced semantic information as layers deepen; the top-down pathway reconstructs high-resolution semantically-rich features through upsampling and lateral connections18. To further improve feature fusion effectiveness, researchers have proposed various FPN improvement schemes: PANet adds bottom-up path aggregation based on FPN, shortening the information propagation path between bottom-layer and top-layer features19; Multi-scale Residual Aggregation Feature Pyramid Network (MSRA-FPN) employs unidirectional cross-layer residual modules, aggregating multi-layer features from bottom to top through triangular structures, further enhancing semantic information exchange between different levels20; addressing the semantic gap problem between features of different scales in traditional FPN, multi-scale semantic enhancement feature pyramid networks alleviate aliasing phenomena during feature fusion through semantic enhancement modules, semantic injection modules, and gated channel guidance modules21. These improvement methods all strive to enhance feature fusion effectiveness, providing important technical support for accurate object detection in complex scenes.

Methods

The HMIV-DET (Hybrid Multi-scale Indirect Vision Detection) algorithm proposed in this paper is an end-to-end detection framework specifically designed for heritage building fire detection. This algorithm addresses fire detection challenges in indirect vision environments through three core innovative modules working collaboratively. HMIV-DET first employs the Adaptive Indirect Vision Enhancement (AIVE) module as a preprocessing component, addressing vision imbalance issues in multi-mirror deployments through dynamic weight allocation and spatial redistribution strategies; subsequently adopts the Adaptive Multi-kernel Feature Orchestration Block (AMFOBlock) to replace traditional C3k2 modules, achieving precise capture of flame features of different sizes through dynamic kernel selection and gated activation mechanisms; finally employs the Hierarchical Cross-Scale Feature Integration Network (HCSFPN) to replace traditional PAFPN, significantly enhancing multi-scale feature representation capabilities through dense feature interaction and adaptive fusion mechanisms. The overall framework is shown in Fig. 1, with the three modules forming a complete detection pipeline from preprocessing to feature extraction to feature fusion.

Overall architecture of the HMIV-DET framework. The system consists of three main stages: (1) Preprocessing stage where AIVE module balances spatial fairness across multiple mirror regions through adaptive enhancement; (2) Backbone feature extraction stage where AMFOBlock modules replace traditional C3k2 blocks to perform multi-scale adaptive feature extraction using parallel geometric convolutions and gated selection; (3) Neck fusion stage where HCSFPN replaces PAFPN to achieve hierarchical cross-scale feature integration through attention-driven multi-scale fusion modules (ADMFM). The framework processes 640640 input images and outputs multi-scale detection results for fire localization.

Adaptive indirect vision enhancement (AIVE)

Unlike traditional enhancement methods that apply uniform processing across entire images, indirect vision systems require mirror-aware preprocessing to address spatial unfairness caused by heterogeneous mirror positioning at different distances. We propose AIVE to explicitly model this unfairness through area-based adaptive weight calculation and region-specific enhancement, ensuring detection equity across all indirect vision domains.

While the Target-Mask module can effectively isolate indirect vision regions and filter out non-target areas, mirror deployments at different spatial positions introduce significant detection domain imbalance. Specifically, mirrors deployed near cameras capture larger indirect vision regions with higher pixel density, while mirrors deployed at distant positions exhibit noticeably smaller coverage areas and reduced target resolution. This spatial unfairness creates an imbalanced detection environment where fire targets in remote mirrors may be underrepresented or completely missed during detection, thereby compromising the overall reliability and robustness of the fire monitoring system.

To address this critical limitation, we propose the Adaptive Indirect Vision Enhancement (AIVE) module, serving as an intelligent preprocessing component designed to establish spatial fairness across all indirect vision regions. The AIVE module dynamically analyzes geometric features of each detected mirror region, calculates adaptive enhancement weights based on relative area differences, and applies targeted enhancement strategies to compensate for size-related detection disadvantages. Through sophisticated redistribution algorithms and visual enhancement techniques, AIVE ensures all indirect vision regions receive proportionally balanced attention in subsequent detection processes, effectively mitigating adverse effects of mirror placement constraints while maintaining fire detection capability throughout the entire monitoring domain, with its workflow shown in Fig. 2.

Experimental results and analysis

Self-constructed dataset

This study selected the Former Site of the Military Committee of the Guangdong District Committee of the Communist Party of China, located in Guangzhou City, Guangdong Province, China, as the research object. As a provincial-level cultural relics protection unit in Guangdong Province, this former site bears irreplaceable cultural and historical value. However, its complex spatial structure and the requirement to preserve historical features render conventional monitoring technologies based on direct vision inadequate, resulting in prominent demands and significant challenges for fire prevention monitoring.

Given the uniqueness of heritage buildings, real fire tests pose severe safety risks. To address this constraint, high-fidelity virtual fire scenes were constructed using 3DMAX software for subsequent fire detection experiments, with the 3D modeling process based on on-site survey data of the former site. During modeling, the actual structural components (e.g., wooden beams, walls, and historical relic display areas) and environmental parameters (e.g., natural light intensity and indoor occlusion conditions) of the building were strictly matched to ensure high scene reproducibility.

To demonstrate the advantage of indirect vision technology in expanding the monitoring range of cameras, four areas were designated as target monitoring regions, including blind spots that are inaccessible under direct vision. Four wide-angle mirrors were deployed without damaging the original structure of the building, and the angles of the mirrors were adjusted to ensure that the indirect vision range of each mirror fully covered its corresponding target monitoring region. This setup simulated the coverage effect of indirect vision on occluded high-risk areas in real scenarios.

Subsequently, a virtual camera was deployed in the lobby area of the 3D model, with its lens facing the main entrance of the building, to capture fire scene images containing indirect vision information. All captured images were preprocessed using the Target-Mask module proposed in our previous research, which filters out non-indirect vision regions through pixel-level masking.

The finally constructed fire detection dataset, Heritage Building Indirect Vision Fire Dataset (Heritage-IVF), contains 1000 mask-processed fire images saved in JPG format with pixel dimensions of 16452493. The dataset was randomly divided into three subsets at a ratio of 7:1.5:1.5, namely a training set (700 images), a validation set (150 images), and a test set (150 images), and the category is “fire”. The construction workflow of this dataset is illustrated in Fig. 6.

Construction workflow of the Heritage Building Indirect Vision Fire Dataset (Heritage-IVF). The process consists of five stages: (1) On-site surveying and measurement of the Guangdong Military Committee Former Site to collect structural parameters; (2) High-fidelity 3D modeling using 3DMAX software with accurate reproduction of architectural elements, materials, and lighting conditions; (3) Strategic mirror deployment in the virtual environment (4 wide-angle mirrors) to cover monitoring blind spots while preserving architectural integrity; (4) Virtual fire scene generation at various locations with realistic flame rendering and smoke simulation; (5) Image capture from virtual camera followed by Target-Mask preprocessing to isolate indirect vision regions. Final dataset contains 1000 images (16452493 pixels) divided into training (700), validation (150), and test sets (150).

Experimental environment and parameter settings

The experimental environment of this study is based on Ubuntu 20.04 operating system, using PyTorch 1.13.1 deep learning framework with Python 3.8 for model development and training. Hardware configuration includes Intel Xeon(R) CPU E5-2682 v4 @ 2.50GHz processor, NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 graphics card (24GB video memory), 32GB memory, and 50GB hard disk storage space (expanded by 60GB), equipped with CUDA 11.7 computing platform providing powerful computational resource support for deep learning model training and inference.

Model training hyperparameter settings are as follows: input image size uniformly adjusted to 640640 pixels to accommodate network input requirements, batch size set to 64, SGD stochastic gradient descent optimizer used for parameter optimization with momentum parameter of 0.937 and weight decay coefficient of 0.0005 to prevent overfitting. Training epochs set to 100 to ensure sufficient model convergence. Learning rate adopts cosine annealing schedule with initial value of 0.01, minimum value of 0.0001, and warm-up period of 3 epochs. Data augmentation strategies include mosaic augmentation (probability 1.0), random horizontal flip (probability 0.5), random scaling (range 0.5-1.5), and HSV color space augmentation (hue shift ±0.015, saturation shift ±0.7, value shift ±0.4). Model selection criterion uses validation set mAP50 metric, with the best-performing checkpoint saved for final evaluation. To ensure experimental reproducibility and fairness, all comparative and ablation experiments employ identical random seed (42), data augmentation configuration, and training hyperparameters, with only architectural components varying as specified. All other training parameters adopt YOLO11 model default settings and use officially provided pre-trained weight files for initialization to enhance model generalization capability.

Evaluation metrics

This study employs standard evaluation metrics from the object detection field to comprehensively evaluate model performance. Precision (P) measures the proportion of actual positive samples among samples predicted as positive by the model, reflecting detection result accuracy; Recall (R) represents the proportion correctly detected among actual positive samples, embodying the model’s target detection capability. For comprehensive evaluation following COCO dataset standards, we employ multiple Average Precision (AP) metrics: mAP50 represents average precision at IoU threshold of 0.5, mAP50-95 represents average precision across IoU thresholds from 0.5 to 0.95 with 0.05 increments, providing a more stringent evaluation of localization accuracy. Additionally, we report scale-specific metrics including APS (small objects with area < 32), APM (medium objects with 32 < area < 96), and APL (large objects with area > 96), which are particularly important for evaluating multi-scale detection capability in indirect vision fire detection scenarios where flame targets appear at varying scales depending on mirror distance and fire development stage. Additionally, computational complexity metric GFLOPs (Giga Floating-point Operations) is used to evaluate model computation amount, Parameters (Params) measures model scale size, and frame rate (FPS, Frames Per Second) evaluates model real-time processing capability. These metrics comprehensively reflect fire detection model comprehensive performance from multiple dimensions including detection accuracy, model efficiency, and inference speed, providing important reference basis for practical deployment of heritage building fire monitoring systems.

Conclusions

This paper addresses special challenges brought by indirect vision technology in heritage building fire monitoring through the HMIV-DET algorithm. The AIVE module resolves spatial unfairness in multi-mirror deployments by ensuring remote small-size mirror regions receive sufficient attention through dynamic weight allocation. AMFOBlock overcomes traditional fixed convolution kernel limitations by integrating multi-geometric adaptive convolution and gated selective activation for complex flame morphology. HCSFPN addresses limited semantic information propagation through dense connections and attention-driven multi-scale fusion strategies.

Experimental results validate the method’s effectiveness, with mAP50 and mAP50-95 improving by 2.8% and 3.3% respectively compared to YOLO11 baseline on the Heritage-IVF dataset. Comprehensive experiments demonstrate AIVE’s improvement of 1.4% in mAP50 over the best competitor, AMFOBlock’s highest performance with 6.3% GFLOPs reduction, and HCSFPN’s effectiveness over alternative architectures.

However, this study has certain limitations. Experiments primarily use virtual fire scenes constructed with 3DMAX software, and while 3D modeling strictly matches actual heritage structures and environmental parameters, simulated fires may differ from real scenarios in combustion dynamics and smoke behavior. The algorithm’s performance can be affected by mirror surface degradation, as enhancement processing cannot recover information lost to physical obstruction. Current methods process individual frames independently without leveraging temporal information. Performance may degrade under extreme conditions due to fundamental physical and resolution constraints. Deployment on resource-constrained embedded systems remains challenging despite efficiency improvements.

Future work will address these challenges through constructing large-scale real-world fire datasets via controlled experiments and historical footage collaboration, developing adaptive mirror quality assessment and temporal processing mechanisms, integrating multi-modal sensor fusion for improved robustness, and advancing model compression techniques for practical embedded deployment. This work provides important technical support for heritage building intelligent fire monitoring systems, while the acknowledged limitations and proposed future directions establish a foundation for advancing toward increasingly robust heritage fire protection solutions.
