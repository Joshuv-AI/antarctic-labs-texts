SIRT6 Aggravates Psoriasiform Skin Inflammation Through Modulation of the SOCS2–STAT1/3 Pathway
Authors: Mingjie He, Qing Guo, Xiaofang Wang, Zhongzhou Huang, Jing Zhou, Qing Wang, Yanyun Jiang, Hui Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo investigate the role and molecular mechanisms of the deacetylase SIRT6 in psoriasis and evaluate its potential as a therapeutic target for topical treatment.MethodsThe expression of SIRT6 in psoriatic lesions and healthy skin was analyzed using datasets from the GEO database. In vitro, Human Immortalized Epidermal Cells (HaCaT) were stimulated with TNF-α to establish a psoriasis-like cellular model. SIRT6 was overexpressed or knocked down in HaCaT cells to evaluate its effects on keratinocyte inflammation, proliferation, and migration. Integrated transcriptomic analysis and subsequent validation were conducted to explore the signaling pathways regulated by SIRT6. Changes in mRNA expression, protein levels and inflammatory cytokine secretion were assessed by RT–qPCR, Western blotting, immunohistochemistry, and ELISA. Therapeutic potential was assessed using a SIRT6 inhibitor-loaded alginate/carbomer hydrogel in an imiquimod-induced psoriasiform dermatitis mouse model and in human psoriatic skin explants ex vivo.ResultsGEO dataset analysis revealed that SIRT6 expression was significantly higher in psoriatic lesions than in either healthy controls or non-lesional skin (both p < 0.0001) and significantly decreased after treatment with biologic agents or JAK inhibitors (p < 0.05 vs pre-treatment). In HaCaT cells, TNF‑α stimulation upregulated SIRT6 by 1.5-fold (p <0.01). Overexpression of SIRT6 enhanced TNF-α-induced production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-23, and TNF-α) and the alarmins S100A8 and S100A9, while promoting keratinocyte proliferation and migration, whereas SIRT6 knockdown produced opposite effects. Combined transcriptomic profiling and multiple validation assays (qPCR, Western blot, and immunohistochemistry) revealed that SIRT6 overexpression activated the STAT signaling pathway by downregulating SOCS2 and upregulating STAT1/3 phosphorylation, while SIRT6 knockdown or inhibition yielded the opposite outcomes. Topical administration of the SIRT6 inhibitor-loaded hydrogel alleviated imiquimod-induced psoriasiform dermatitis in mice and reduced inflammatory responses in human psoriatic skin explants.ConclusionSIRT6 exacerbates psoriasiform inflammation by modulating the SOCS2–STAT1/3 signaling pathway. These findings suggest that topical inhibition of SIRT6 may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for psoriasis.

Introduction

Psoriasis is a chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory skin disease characterized by erythema and scaling, with a global prevalence of approximately 2–3%.1 The IL-23/IL-17 axis plays a central role in psoriasis,2 and the remarkable clinical efficacy of monoclonal antibodies targeting IL-173,4 and IL-235 underscores the core function of these cytokines as key drivers of the disease. Although biologics have achieved groundbreaking significance in the treatment of psoriasis, their high cost imposes a substantial financial burden on patients. Moreover, challenges persist in managing refractory or recurrent skin lesions. Therefore, topical agents such as corticosteroids, calcineurin inhibitors, and vitamin D3 analogues remain a cost-effective treatment option. However, long-term use of corticosteroids may lead to adverse reactions such as local skin atrophy, telangiectasia, and pigmentation abnormalities. Therefore, it remains important to continue investigating the pathogenesis of psoriasis and to identify novel therapeutic targets for its treatment.

Sirtuin 6 (SIRT6) is a member of the NAD+ - dependent class III histone deacetylase family and plays critical roles in cellular proliferation, genomic stability, and inflammatory regulation. Elevated SIRT6 expression has been reported in skin tumors such as metastatic melanoma6 and squamous cell carcinoma,7 where it promotes tumor cell proliferation.8 Mechanistically, SIRT6 is predominantly localized in the nucleus, where it deacetylates histones H3K9 and H3K56, thereby regulating chromatin structure, gene transcription, and DNA damage repair.9 In addition to its nuclear functions, SIRT6 can localize to the cytoplasm and endoplasmic reticulum under stress conditions10 and has been shown to regulate TNF-α secretion through deacylation of its precursor protein at K19 and K20.11,12 Emerging evidence suggests a potential involvement of SIRT6 in psoriasis. Increased SIRT6 expression has been observed in psoriatic lesions, imiquimod-induced mouse models, and psoriasis-related cell models.13,14 In addition, functional studies have suggested that SIRT6 may contribute to keratinocyte hyperproliferation and inflammatory responses in psoriasis.15 However, whether SIRT6 acts as a direct amplifier of inflammatory signaling pathways in psoriasis has not been systematically investigated. Therefore, in the present study, we aimed to investigate the pathogenic role of SIRT6 in psoriasis, with a particular focus on its involvement in inflammatory signaling mechanisms, and to evaluate the therapeutic potential of a topically applied SIRT6 inhibitor-loaded hydrogel.

Materials and Methods

GEO Dataset Acquisition and Analysis

All datasets were downloaded from NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gds/). After combining GSE13355 and GSE14905, the SVA package was used to remove batch effects. The data were then divided into SIRT6 high-expression and low-expression groups based on the median value of SIRT6 expression, and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the ClusterProfiler package. The datasets (GSE13355, GSE14905, GSE117239, GSE178228, and GSE69967) are publicly available from the GEO database and have been de-identified. According to the policy of Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University, this secondary analysis of publicly available data was exempt from ethical approval.

Cell Culture and Psoriasis Cell Model Construction

HaCaT cells were purchased from Cobioer Biosciences Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China) and sent to Immocell Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Xiamen, China) for STR profiling. The cells were cultured in complete medium consisting of 90% DMEM (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and 10% FBS (Gibco) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Recombinant Human TNF-α, Oncostatin M (OSM), IL-1α, IL-22, and IL-17A were purchased from PeproTech, Inc. (New Jersey, USA). HaCaT cells were stimulated with either TNF-α, IL-17A, IL-1α alone, or a mixture of five pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, OSM, IL-1α, IL-22, and IL-17A), known as M5, to establish a psoriasis-like cell model.

Lentiviral Transduction for SIRT6 Overexpression

The SIRT6 overexpressing lentivirus (Cat. No. CL097) and the negative control lentivirus (Cat. No. LV587) were purchased from Applied Biological Materials Inc. (Shanghai, China). The overexpression lentivirus carries the full-length wild-type coding sequence (CDS) of the human SIRT6 gene based on the reference sequence NM_016539.4 (NCBI Gene ID: 51548). Transfection was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions with a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100, and Polybrene was added at 5 μg/mL during the transfection to enhance transfection efficiency.

RNA Sequencing and Differential Gene Expression Analysis

After stimulation with TNF-α, total RNA was extracted from control-transfected and SIRT6-overexpressing HaCaT cells (three biological replicates per group) using RNAiso Plus (Takara, Japan). The RNA samples were subsequently sent to Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) for cDNA library construction and sequencing on the Illumina platform. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified by meeting both screening criteria: |log2 fold change (log2FC)| ≥ 0.585 and adjusted P-value (Padj) < 0.05. The use of the commercial HaCaT cell line in this study was exempt from ethical approval according to the policy of Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University.

siRNA-Mediated Knockdown of SIRT6

Small interfering RNA was used to knock down SIRT6 in HaCaT cells, and three siRNA targeting human SIRT6 were synthesized by Geneyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). The si-SIRT6-1 sequences were: sense strand, 5’-CCAAGUGUAAGACGCAGUATT-3’; antisense strand, 5’-UACUGCGUCUUACACUUGGTT-3’. The si-SIRT6-2 sequences were: sense strand,5’-GGCUACGUUGACGAGGUCATT-3’; antisense strand, 5’-UGACCUCGUCAACGUAGCCTT-3’. The si-SIRT6-3 sequences were: sense strand, 5’-CAAGUUCGACACCACCUUUTT-3’; antisense strand, 5’-AAAGGUGGUGUCGAACUUGTT-3’. The si-SIRT6-1 exhibited the best knockdown efficiency and stability and was used in subsequent experiments. Transfection was performed when the cell density reached 30%-50%, and Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Thermo Fisher, USA) was used to enhance transfection efficiency.

Real-Time Quantitative PCR

Total RNA extraction, reverse transcription into cDNA, and RT-qPCR reactions were performed using the corresponding kits (EZBioscience, Suzhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative PCR was conducted using the LightCycler® 480 System (Roche, Switzerland).

Western Blot

Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with the corresponding secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. All primary antibodies were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (MA, USA) or Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Protein bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence system. β-tubulin was used as the internal control.

Elisa

Cell culture supernatants from HaCaT cells under different treatment conditions were collected. The concentrations of IL-6 and IL-23 were determined using commercially available ELISA kits (Elabscience, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Immunohistochemistry

Human and mouse skin specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. Sections (4 μm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and heat-treated for antigen retrieval. After blocking, sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against target proteins, including anti-SIRT6 (1:500), anti-SOCS2 (1:200), anti-p-STAT1 (1:200), and anti-p-STAT3 (1:200). Sections were sequentially incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, developed with DAB, and counterstained with hematoxylin for microscopic observation. Semi-quantitative analysis of immunostaining intensity was performed using ImageJ software in at least five random high-power fields (×200) per section.

Animals

All wild-type (WT) and SIRT6-transgenic (SIRT6-Tg; official strain name: C57BL/6-Tg (RP23-352G18)1Coppa/J) mice used in this study were female C57BL/6 mice aged 6–8 weeks. Mice were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Center of Sun Yat-sen University (Guangzhou, China) and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions. Psoriasiform skin inflammation was induced in mice by topical application of 5% imiquimod (IMQ; 3M Pharmaceuticals, St. Paul, MN, USA). Briefly, 62.5 mg of IMQ cream was applied daily to the shaved dorsal skin or 20 mg to the ears for six consecutive days. Randomization was used to allocate mice to different experimental groups. Each group contained 5–8 mice, and all experiments were independently repeated twice. The ear thickness of mice was measured using a micrometer to assess the overall severity of skin inflammation. The severity of psoriasiform dermatitis was assessed using the psoriasis severity index (PSI), which evaluates erythema, scaling, and skin thickening. Additional outcome measures included dorsal epidermal thickness, histopathological changes and immunohistochemical staining of skin tissues, and alterations in skin mRNA expression levels. Total RNA was extracted using a tissue RNA Purification Kit (EZBioscience, Suzhou, China). The researchers responsible for IMQ administration and treatment were aware of the group allocation but were not involved in the subsequent assessment or data analysis, while those conducting the outcome measurements and analyses were kept blinded to the group assignments. Mice were anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection). After confirming deep anesthesia, the animals were euthanized by cervical dislocation. All experimental procedures were performed in accordance with ethical guidelines for animal protection and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Sun Yat-sen University (Approval No. SYSU-IACUC-2025-000931).

Preparation of SIRT6 Inhibitor-Loaded Hydrogel

A hydrogel matrix containing 4% alginate, 1% Carbomer 940, and 5% glycerol was prepared as previously described.16 And the prepared hydrogel was stored at 4°C. To prepare the SIRT6 inhibitor hydrogel, the OSS_128167 (MCE, Shanghai, China) was added to the hydrogel at a concentration of 1 mg per mL and thoroughly mixed via ultrasonication.

Collection and Culture of Skin Lesions

Psoriatic lesions were obtained from three patients with newly diagnosed psoriasis using a punch biopsy, and the patients had not received any topical or systemic medication for at least one month prior. Informed consent for the collection and subsequent use of the lesions was obtained from the patients. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University (Approval No. SYSKY-2025-1014-01). Each lesion was evenly divided into two equal pieces (approximately 0.5 cm × 0.5 cm × 0.5 cm). Vehicle hydrogel or SIRT6 inhibitor-loaded hydrogel was topically applied to the surface of each piece, respectively. The explants were cultured in complete medium at the air–liquid interface with the epidermal side facing upward. After 24 hours of culture, RNA was extracted from the tissues for RT-qPCR analysis.

Statistical Analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using two-tailed Student’s t-tests. For comparisons involving three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied, followed by appropriate post-hoc tests (Tukey’s or Dunnett’s) as indicated. The specific statistical tests used for each experiment are clearly stated in the corresponding figure legends. All statistical analyses and graphing were performed using R (version 4.5.1) or GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.2). A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

SIRT6 Is Elevated in Psoriatic Lesions

First, to determine the expression of SIRT6 in skin tissues, we compared the gene expression levels of SIRT6 in lesional and non-lesional tissues from psoriasis patients and in healthy skin tissues from the GEO database. In the GSE13355 and GSE14905 microarray datasets, SIRT6 gene expression levels were significantly higher in psoriatic lesions than in non-lesional and healthy skin (Figure 1A). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) of the two datasets indicated that high SIRT6 expression was associated with the upregulation of E2F transcription factors, the G2M cell cycle, the IL6_JAK_STAT3 signaling pathway, and the inflammatory response pathway (Figure 1B). Additionally, analysis of the GSE117239, GSE178228, and GSE69967 datasets revealed that SIRT6 gene expression in psoriatic lesions was downregulated after separate treatment with anti-IL-12/23, anti-TNF-α, and anti-IL-17A monoclonal antibodies, as well as Janus kinase inhibitors (Figure 1C). SIRT6 protein is primarily localized in the nucleus, with minor presence in the cytoplasm and endoplasmic reticulum. We assessed SIRT6 protein expression in skin tissues via immunohistochemical staining. Psoriatic keratinocytes exhibited significantly higher nuclear SIRT6 expression levels and positivity rates of nuclear staining than healthy controls (Figure 1D and E). SIRT6 Aggravates Imiquimod-Induced Psoriasiform Dermatitis (PsD)

5% Imiquimod (IMQ) application is a well-established method for inducing PsD in mice.17 After six consecutive days of topical treatment with Vaseline (as control) or IMQ on the dorsal skin of wild-type (WT) and SIRT6 transgenic (SIRT6-Tg) mice, the SIRT6-Tg mice displayed exacerbated PsD compared to WT mice on day 6 (Figure 2A). The PSI score, which assesses the severity of erythema, scaling, and induration, was used to evaluate the severity of skin lesion. SIRT6-Tg mice exhibited significantly higher PSI scores than WT mice on day 6 (Figure 2B). IMQ induced epidermal thickening in mice, and the thickening was more pronounced in SIRT6-Tg mice than in WT mice after IMQ treatment (Figure 2C and D). IMQ-treated mice developed psoriasis-like pathological changes, including epidermal alterations and dermal inflammatory cells infiltration. The Baker scores, used to evaluate these pathological changes, were higher in SIRT6-Tg mice than in WT mice (Figure 2E). SIRT6 Potentiates TNF-α-Induced Inflammatory Responses in HaCaT Cells

We established a psoriasis cell model by TNF-α-stimulated human immortalized epidermal cell (HaCaT cell). In this cell model, treatment with TNF-α at concentrations of 40 ng/mL or higher significantly increased SIRT6 expression compared to unstimulated controls, and this increase was observed in a concentration-dependent manner (Figure 3A and B). However, under other stimulation conditions, such as IL-17A (200 ng/mL), IL-1α (20–200ng/mL) and M5 cytokines cocktail (M5: TNF-α, Oncostatin M, IL-1α, IL-22, and IL-17A, each at 20 ng/mL), SIRT6 expression were not altered (Supplementary fig 1A–C). The increase in SIRT6 was caused by TNF-α stimulation, rather than other inflammatory factors. Therefore, in subsequent cell experiments, TNF-α at 40 ng/mL was used as the stimulatory concentration in HaCaT cells. Under this condition, the gene expression levels of various psoriasis-related inflammatory factors such as IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, IL-23, TNF, as well as chemokines including CXCL1 and CCL20, were elevated (Figure 3C). With the exception of SIRT6, no significant changes were observed in the other members of the Sirtuins family (Figure 3D). To further investigate the role of SIRT6 in psoriasis, we overexpressed SIRT6 in HaCaT cells via lentiviral transfection. Compared with the blank control and lentiviral transfection control (NC), the SIRT6 overexpression (OE-SIRT6) exhibited significantly upregulated SIRT6 gene (Figure 3E) and protein (Figure 3F) levels. SIRT6 and the inflammatory factors IL-6, IL-23, TNF, S100A8, and S100A9 exhibited increased gene expression upon TNF-α stimulation, and OE-SIRT6 further enhanced the TNF-α-induced gene expression of these factors (Figure 3G). Consistent with the gene changes, OE-SIRT6 promoted the secretion of the inflammatory factors IL-6 and IL-23 (Figure 3H). SIRT6 Attenuates SOCS2 Expression and Enhances STAT Phosphorylation in TNF-α-Stimulated HaCaT Cells

To further explore the mechanism of SIRT6 in inflammation, we performed transcriptome sequencing on TNF-α-stimulated NC-HaCaT cells and OE-SIRT6 HaCaT cells. Compared with NC-HaCaT cells, OE-SIRT6 HaCaT cells stimulated with TNF-α exhibited 214 differentially expressed genes, including 97 up-regulated and 117 down-regulated genes (Figure 4A). Suppressor of cytokine signaling 2 (SOCS2) is a negative regulator of the JAK–STAT signaling pathway and inhibits the phosphorylation of STAT1,18 STAT3.19,20 OE-SIRT6 suppressed SOCS2 gene expression while enhancing the expression of STAT1, STAT2, activator protein-1 genes (a downstream effector of JAK-STAT signaling, including JUN, FOS, FOSL1), inflammation-related genes (IL1A, IL6ST, TNFRSF12A, TNFSF18, S100A9), and cell cycle–associated genes (CDK1, CCNB1, CCND1, CCND2, CCND3) (Figure 4A and B). In vitro experiments further confirmed that SIRT6 inhibited SOCS2 expression at both the gene and protein levels (Figure 4C–E). Moreover, OE-SIRT6 further enhanced TNF-α–induced phosphorylation of STAT1 and STAT3 (Figure 4D and E). To corroborate these findings in vivo, we performed immunohistochemical staining of skin lesions from WT and SIRT6-Tg mice treated with or without imiquimod to induce psoriasis-like dermatitis. Consistent with the in vitro observations, SIRT6-Tg mice exhibited decreased SOCS2 expression and elevated STAT1 and STAT3 phosphorylation in the epidermis compared with WT mice (Supplementary fig. 2A–D), which was consistent with the exacerbated psoriatic phenotype upon SIRT6 overexpression. SIRT6 Regulates HaCaT Cell Migration and Proliferation

Keratinocyte hyperproliferation is one of the key manifestations of psoriasis. Previous findings suggested an association between SIRT6 and cell proliferation (Figure 1B, 4B). Therefore, we investigated the effect of SIRT6 on HaCaT cells themselves. Inhibition of SIRT6 Ameliorates Psoriasiform Dermatitis in Mice

As the aforementioned in vitro experiments validated that inhibiting SIRT6 suppresses the inflammatory response, we proceeded to investigate whether inhibiting SIRT6 could ameliorate psoriasis-like symptoms in vivo. Hydrogel is a complex three-dimensional polymeric network with a semi-solid texture, excellent absorbency and biocompatibility.21 The unique structure of hydrogels is conducive to drug loading and release. Hydrogels are widely used in drug delivery, and studies have reported the topical application of hydrogels for delivering drugs to treat psoriasis.16,22 We incorporated OSS_128167, a potent selective SIRT6 inhibitor, into an alginate/carbomer-based hydrogel (OSS-gel). OSS-gel exhibits a certain viscosity and does not flow completely like a liquid (Figure 7A), which enables it to adhere better to skin compared to a solution. Under scanning electron microscopy (SEM), numerous loose pores were observed inside the OSS-gel, which facilitate drug storage (Figure 7B). Topical corticosteroids (TC) are one of the most commonly used and effective topical treatments for psoriasis and were used as a positive treatment control in our study. We applied Vaseline or IMQ to the ears of mice in the morning for 6 consecutive days. Eight hours later, the IMQ-treated mice were treated with vehicle-gel (Gel), OSS-gel, or TC, respectively. Compared to the IMQ group, the IMQ+OSS-gel group exhibited significant improvements in symptoms, as evidenced by markedly lower PSI scores and significantly reduced ear thickness, and this therapeutic effect was comparable to that of TC (Figure 7C–E). IMQ-induced modeling in mice led to weight loss, whereas OSS-gel treatment restored body weight to normal levels (Figure 7F), indicating the recovery of the overall health status in the mice. In addition to ameliorating dermatitis symptoms, OSS-gel treatment reduced the IMQ-induced enlargement of lymph nodes and spleen in mice (Figure 7G). OSS-gel reduced the IMQ-induced transcription level of SIRT6, IL-6, IL-23, TNF, S100A8 and S100A9, and increased the level of SOCS2 in the ear skin tissue of mice (Figure 7H). Consistently, immunohistochemical analysis revealed that OSS-gel treatment promoted SOCS2 protein expression and suppressed STAT1 and STAT3 phosphorylation in the ear epidermis (Figure 7I). Discussion

Our data identify SIRT6 as a positive regulator of keratinocyte-driven inflammation in psoriasis. SIRT6 expression was markedly upregulated in psoriatic epidermis and downregulated following effective treatment, suggesting that SIRT6 levels may serve as a molecular indicator of disease activity in psoriasis. Given that keratinocytes are central to psoriasis—not only as targets of inflammatory cytokines but also as active drivers that amplify cutaneous inflammation through production of cytokines, alarmins, and chemokines that recruit and activate immune cells23–26 —we focused on the keratinocyte-intrinsic functions of SIRT6. Notably, TNF-α stimulation directly induced SIRT6 in HaCaT cells in a dose-dependent manner, indicating that the inflammatory milieu itself may perpetuate SIRT6 upregulation in lesional keratinocytes. Functionally, SIRT6 overexpression amplified TNF-α-induced production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-23, TNF-α) and alarmins (S100A8, S100A9), while also promoting keratinocyte proliferation and migration—two hallmarks of psoriatic keratinocyte abnormality. Conversely, SIRT6 knockdown reversed these pro-inflammatory effects. These gain- and loss-of-function data establish a causal role for SIRT6 in driving a pathogenic keratinocyte phenotype that likely contributes to the amplification and maintenance of psoriasiform inflammation.

Mechanistically, our data suggest a potential involvement of the SOCS2–STAT1/3 axis in SIRT6-mediated inflammation. SIRT6 overexpression suppressed SOCS2 expression at both mRNA and protein levels, concomitantly enhancing STAT1 and STAT3 phosphorylation, whereas SIRT6 knockdown or pharmacological inhibition restored SOCS2 and attenuated STAT1/3 phosphorylation across cellular, animal, and ex vivo psoriatic lesional models. Given that SOCS proteins are canonical negative regulators of JAK/STAT signaling that bind to JAKs via their SH2 domains and competitively inhibit STAT activation,27–29 our findings suggest that SIRT6 relieves this physiological brake on STAT1/3 signaling by suppressing SOCS2. This regulatory relationship closely resembles a previous study showing that SIRT6 suppresses SOCS3 transcription in intestinal epithelial cells through histone H3K56 deacetylation, thereby promoting STAT6 phosphorylation.30 It is therefore plausible that SIRT6 may regulate SOCS2 transcription through a similar epigenetic mechanism in keratinocytes. However, several questions remain. First, whether SIRT6 directly binds to the SOCS2 promoter and modulates its transcriptional activity via histone deacetylation requires formal demonstration by chromatin immunoprecipitation. Second, whether SOCS2 restoration is sufficient to attenuate STAT1/3 hyperphosphorylation and the associated inflammatory phenotype warrants functional rescue experiments. Future studies incorporating these approaches will help to define the precise molecular circuitry linking SIRT6 to SOCS2–STAT1/3 signaling. Nevertheless, our gain- and loss-of-function data collectively establish that SIRT6 promotes psoriatic inflammation, at least in part, through downregulation of SOCS2 and consequent hyperactivation of STAT1/3, supporting the notion that targeting SIRT6 may offer a therapeutic opportunity for psoriasis.

Novel transdermal drug delivery systems, including hydrogels and nanogels,31 have emerged as a current research hotspot in the development of topical therapies for skin diseases, with hydrogels being particularly favored for their ease of application and high biocompatibility. In addition to its use in psoriasis, it has been previously reported for the treatment of diabetes-related foot ulcers,32 skin infections,33 burn injuries,34 androgenic alopecia.35 In our study, OSS-gel demonstrated comparable efficacy to TC in the IMQ-induced mouse model, with significant improvements observed in PSI scores, ear thickness, histopathological features, and inflammatory cytokine expression. At the molecular level, OSS-gel treatment upregulated SOCS2 expression and suppressed STAT1/3 phosphorylation in IMQ-treated mouse epidermis, consistent with our in vitro mechanistic findings. Furthermore, OSS-gel exerted consistent anti-inflammatory effects in ex vivo psoriatic lesions, downregulating multiple psoriasis-related inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, including IL-6, IL-23, IL-17A, IL-17F, CCL20, etc, across three patient specimens. Although the sample size is limited, the observed anti-inflammatory trend was consistent and pronounced. Given that SIRT6 has been reported to promote IL-17A transcription by deacetylating RORγt and enhancing its binding to the IL-17A promoter,36 it is plausible that OSS-gel may attenuate IL-17 transcription through this pathway, while the broader therapeutic potential of OSS-gel in Th17 cell modulation and the prevention of psoriasis recurrence warrants further investigation.

Several limitations of our study should be acknowledged. First, the IMQ-induced mouse model, as the most widely used and conveniently established murine model for psoriasis, represents an acute TLR7/8-driven inflammatory response rather than the chronic, relapsing nature of human psoriasis.37 This model does not recapitulate the T-cell memory or the recurrent disease flares that characterize clinical psoriasis, and therefore cannot address the potential of OSS-gel in disease prevention or long-term management. Second, the number of ex vivo human skin specimens was limited (n=3), and although the anti-inflammatory effects were consistent across samples, the small sample size precludes definitive conclusions about patient heterogeneity. These preliminary data, however, provide a rationale for sample size estimation in future clinical trials and support further development of OSS-gel as a potential alternative topical therapy for psoriasis.

Conclusion

Our findings show that SIRT6 overexpression correlates with reduced SOCS2 expression and increased STAT1/3 phosphorylation, whereas SIRT6 inhibition produces the opposite molecular and anti-inflammatory effects. Collectively, these results position SIRT6 as a promising therapeutic target for psoriasis, and our hydrogel-based inhibitor delivery system highlights its translational potential.
