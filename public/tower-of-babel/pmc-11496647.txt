Comprehensive sequencing of the genome and transcriptome of the Xishuangbanna game fowl
Authors: Huimin Qiu, Tiao Ning, Weijun Gong, Hao Sun, Gui Mu, Jing Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Xishuangbanna game fowl, a local Chinese chicken breed renowned for its athleticism, is facing extinction. Despite its significance, research on its genome assembly and resequencing remains sparse, limiting our understanding of its genetic structure and function. In this study, we assembled a chromosome-level genome using second-generation sequencing and high-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C), along with transcriptome sequencing. We also resequenced 62 individuals. The resulting assembly yielded a 1.09 gigabase (Gb) high-quality genome, 994.34 Gb of resequencing data, and 141.38 Gb of transcriptome data from 15 tissues. This comprehensive dataset provides valuable insights into the artificial selection processes shaping the breed and offers a solid foundation for further genetic and population studies.Subject terms: RNA sequencing, DNA sequencing

Background & Summary

The Xishuangbanna game fowl, an ancient domesticated breed of Gallus gallus domesticus, exemplifies the profound effects of artificial selection on genetic diversity and phenotypic traits1,2. Selective breeding has endowed this breed with distinctive morphology, physiology, and behavior, making it a key model for studying the genetic basis of these traits. Originating in China, where fowl breeding has a long history3, the breed boasts strong genetic integrity4,5. However, due to crossbreeding and the introduction of foreign breeds, the genetic quality of game fowl in Xishuangbanna has declined, putting the breed at risk of extinction. Research on the full genome of this breed has been limited, hampering efforts to understand its biological characteristics. To address this gap, we assembled a high-quality genome, resequenced the genomes of 62 individuals, and sequenced transcriptomes from 15 tissues (Fig. 1). This dataset allows for comparative genomic analysis with other avian species, facilitating further research on the breed’s genetics and conservation.

Methods

Ethics statement

All procedures involving chickens followed the Guidelines for Experimental Animals from the Ministry of Science and Technology (Beijing, China, revised in March 2017). Ethical approval was granted by the Kunming University Committee (approved ID: SKY-20230122). All experiments adhered to these approved guidelines.

Sample preparation and sequencing

For genome assembly, liver tissue from a female Xishuangbanna fowl (Yunnan Province) was collected, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C to maintain sample integrity6. Genomic DNA was extracted using a NEBNext Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit. A portion of this DNA was used to create Nanopore libraries. DNA was fragmented into 15–18 kb segments using a Covaris ultrasonicator, enriched, purified with magnetic beads, and underwent damage and end repair. Stem-loop adapters were ligated to both ends, and unsuccessful ligation products were removed via exonuclease treatment. Libraries were sequenced on the PacBio Sequel II platform. The same DNA was used to construct high-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) libraries. Chromatin was crosslinked, lysed, and digested with DPN II at 37 °C, followed by biotin-labeling of DNA ends and ligation with T4 DNA ligase. Crosslinking was reversed using proteinase K at 65 °C. Purified DNA fragments were size-selected (350–500 bp), repaired, and biotin-labeled fragments were isolated using Dynabeads® M-280 Streptavidin. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq platform. Hi-C interaction data are presented in a heat map (Fig. 2). The remaining DNA was used to construct second-generation sequencing (NGS) libraries.

Hi-C interaction heat map in Xishuangbanna game fowl.

For genome resequencing, blood samples were collected from the wing veins of 38 males and 24 females. DNA was fragmented to 350 bp, end-repaired, A-tailed, and ligated with adapters, followed by PCR amplification and purification. Sequencing was performed using the DNBSEQ-T7 platform at Novogene Bioinformatics Ltd., Beijing, China.

For transcriptome analysis, 15 tissues were collected from the same individual used for genome assembly. Total RNA was extracted using a NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit. mRNA was purified, fragmented, converted to cDNA, amplified by PCR, and sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform. A total of 141.38 Gb of clean data was generated. These data were mapped to the chicken reference genome (GRCg7b) using STAR (v.2.7.6a), and gene expression was quantified as TPM (transcripts per million) using Kallisto (v.0.44.0). Comprehensive quality control ensured sequencing data reliability.

Data quality control

Strict quality control measures were applied to the sequencing data (Fig. 3). Raw reads were base-called and converted to short-read sequences in FASTQ format7, containing sequence information and quality information. Fastp (v0.23.1)8 was used for quality assessment and preprocessing, including removing paired reads with splice contamination, discarding reads with more than 10% undetermined bases, and filtering out reads with over 50% low-quality bases (Phred score < 5). These steps eliminated sequencing artifacts, such as splice contamination, low-quality nucleotides, and unrecognizable bases (N), ensuring the reliability of downstream bioinformatics analysis.

Quality assessment of Hi-C data (a), Illumina sequencing (b), resequencing (c), and transcriptome sequencing (d) data.

Genome assembly and assessment

An optimized four-step strategy was employed for the assembly of the Xishuangbanna game fowl genome9. Figure 4 outlines the workflow, including assembly steps and quality evaluation. Initially, high-quality Nanopore sequences were processed using a ‘correct-then-assemble’ method in NextDenovo (v2.5.0; https://github.com/Nextomics/NextDenovo) to generate preliminary contigs. NextPolish (v1.4.1) applied its ‘best’ algorithm to refine these contigs using T7 paired-end and Nanopore long reads10, resulting in the ContigV1 assembly.

Genome assembly and quality assessment workflow for Xishuangbanna game fowl.

In step two, unique mapped pairs from Hi-C reads aligned to ContigV1 using Bowtie2 (v2.2.0)11 in single-ended mode. Invalid fragments were removed via the HiCUP pipeline (v0.8.0)12, and valid interactions were used to compute linkage frequencies across contigs, clustering them by Hi-C signal density to establish chromosomal associations. Step three involved realigning Nanopore reads to ContigV1 using Minimap2 (v2.17-r941)13. Misalignments were filtered out, and local assembly was conducted to resolve false overlaps caused by repetitive sequences14. The final step involved anchoring chromosome-scale genomes using linkage data, restriction site information, and string graph formulations in the ALLHiC (v0.9.8) algorithm15. Misplacements or misoriented contigs were manually corrected based on chromatin interaction patterns.

Genome assembly quality was evaluated using Merqury (v1.0) to calculate QV values16. Completeness was assessed using BUSCO (v5.2.1)17 against the Gallus gallus reference genome. Paired-end reads were aligned to the assembly using BWA18 to calculate alignment ratios and coverage depth, validating completeness. The final genome assembly length was 1.09 Gb, with a contig N50 of 72.47 Mb (Table 1).

Genomic variant calling and annotation

High-quality sequences were aligned to the GRCg7b chicken reference genome using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA v0.7.15)19 with the parameters ‘mem -t 10 -k 32’ to optimize computational efficiency. SAM files were converted to BAM format using SAMtools (v0. 1.19)20. To Reads with more than five mismatches or a zero-mapping quality score were excluded to improve alignment accuracy. Sorted BAM files were deduplicated using Picard’s ‘MarkDuplicates’ function (v1.119), reducing downstream analysis biases.

Single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) calling was performed across the Xishuangbanna game fowl population using SAMtools21 with the ‘mpileup’ command and parameters ‘-m 2 -F 0.002 -d 1000.’ SNP functional annotation was performed using ANNOVAR22. To avoid SNP calling errors from incorrect mapping, only high-quality SNPs (root-mean-square mapping quality ≥ 20, coverage depth ≥ 4 and ≤ 1,000, inter-SNP distance ≥ 5 bp, missing data < 50% per group) were retained. A total of 15.07 × 106 SNPs were identified across 62 genomes (Table 2), including 2.28 × 106 exonic SNPs and 6.4 × 106 intronic SNPs. Of the identified SNPs, 0.97% were in protein-coding regions, with 0.01% predicted to affect amino acid sequences (non-synonymous or stop gain/loss mutations).

Collinearity analysis

To evaluate the quality of the game fowl genome assembly and explore its evolutionary relationship with closely related species, we performed a comprehensive collinearity analysis between the game fowl and GGswu123 genomes. Whole-genome alignment was conducted using MUMmer (v3.23)24, and the results were visualized with Circos (v0.69-9). Figure 5 illustrates a detailed comparison of 20 macrochromosomes larger than 10 Mb, including the W and Z chromosomes (a) and microchromosomes smaller than 10 Mb (b). The color-coded chromosomes and connecting lines clearly demonstrate a high degree of collinearity between the game fowl and GGswu1 genomes.

Synteny Analysis Between Game fowl and GGswu1 Chromosomes. (a) Synteny analysis for chromosomes >10 Mb, including 20 autosomes and the W and Z sex chromosomes. Different colors represent different chromosomes, with lines indicating syntenic regions between species. (b) Synteny analysis for microchromosomes <10 Mb. Further quantitative analysis using LASTZ (v1.04.00) revealed that all 41 Segments from both genomes were 100% aligned, with 96.01% of bases aligned in the game fowl genome and 97.33% in the GGswu1 genome. One-to-one and many-to-many comparison analyses using LAST (v1461) showed average homology rates of 99.13% and 99.01%, respectively. These results confirm the high similarity between the game fowl and GGswu1 genomes at both the structural and sequence levels. Table 3 presents a tabular format of the genome comparison data, listing alignment statistics for each chromosome, including sequence length, aligned length, and alignment rate, providing further evidence of the high consistency between the two genomes. These analyses validate the quality of the genome assembly and offer reliable data for studying the evolutionary relationship between the game fowl and closely related species.

Data Records

The data for the Xishuangbanna game fowl have been deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) SRA database (Experiments for SRP459583)25 under BioProject accession number PRJNA1015027. The genome assembly is available for public access at the NCBI GenBank under the accession number GCA_041920315.126. The dataset includes raw sequencing reads in FASTQ format. Metadata for each sample, such as library preparation details, sequencing platform, and read length, are also available in the database.

Technical Validation

The quality of the genomic sequences was evaluated using standard bioinformatics tools and metrics. Read quality was assessed with FastQC, ensuring that sequences had a Q30 score above 90%. BWA was used for sequence alignment, and SAMtools was used for BAM file generation. Duplicate reads were marked using Picard’s ‘MarkDuplicates’ function. The overall alignment rate exceeded 99%, and variant calling was conducted according to GATK Best Practices. These findings affirm the assembly’s reliability for advanced genomic and comparative studies of avian species.

Usage Notes

This dataset offers a valuable genetic and transcriptomic resource for Xishuangbanna game fowl research. It can be used to explore gene expression patterns, discover SNPs, and contribute to genomic studies. The dataset is also valuable for comparative genomics and phylogenetic studies, advancing the understanding of avian evolution. Additionally, it supports research into the evolution of traits under selection and offers insights into the transcriptomic landscape across avian tissues. By providing this comprehensive dataset, we aim to support a wide range of studies in avian genetics, evolution, and biology.
