Early-life obesity as a risk factor for central precocious puberty – A commentary on "Incidence of central precocious puberty and the role of body mass index in Korea"
Authors: Hae Sang Lee
Published: 2025

The rising incidence of central precocious puberty (CPP) has become a global clinical concern, and the Korean experience provides a particularly striking example of this accelerating trend [1,2]. The study by Chung [3] provides important epidemiologic evidence by quantifying national trends in CPP incidence and examining the relationship between early childhood obesity and pubertal onset in Korea. Compared with 2012, the incidence of CPP increased substantially by 2020 in both sexes. Importantly, higher degrees of obesity between ages 2 and 6 years were significantly associated with an increased risk of CPP, with this association being more pronounced in boys than in girls [3].

Previous studies have consistently shown that the incidence of CPP has increased over time in countries worldwide. In Korea, Kim et al. [4] reported a significant rise in CPP incidence among girls between 2004 and 2010 using national health insurance data, suggesting that earlier pubertal onset had already begun more than a decade ago. Subsequent Korean studies confirmed that this increase has continued in recent years, indicating a persistent upward trend rather than a temporary change [5]. Similar findings have been reported in other countries. In Denmark, Bräuner et al. [6] demonstrated a sixfold increase in CPP incidence in girls and a fifteenfold increase in boys from 1998 to 2017, highlighting that the rise in CPP is not confined to East Asian populations [6]. These rising incidences of CPP may be explained by the increased awareness of early pubertal development among parents and clinicians, as well as by the growing prevalence of childhood obesity, which may contribute to earlier activation of puberty.

One of the most interesting findings of this study is that excess weight gain in early childhood was associated with a higher risk of CPP compared with normal weight. Children who were overweight or obese between 2 and 6 years of age showed a significantly increased risk of CPP, and this association was particularly strong in boys [3]. The link between obesity and CPP is well established in girls, and previous studies have consistently reported a higher prevalence of obesity among girls with CPP [7]. In contrast, findings in boys have been inconsistent. Some studies have suggested that obesity is associated with earlier pubertal onset in boys, while others have reported delayed puberty in obese boys, possibly due to suppression of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis [8]. These conflicting results highlight the complexity of the relationship between obesity and pubertal development in boys. Therefore, further studies are needed to better understand the mechanisms by which early-life obesity influences pubertal timing in boys.

Although the exact mechanisms by which obesity accelerates pubertal onset are not fully understood, several metabolic and hormonal pathways are thought to influence activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis. Excess adiposity alters the secretion and action of key hormones, including leptin, insulin, and adipokines, which convey metabolic signals to the brain [9]. Leptin, produced by adipose tissue, is required for normal pubertal development and acts as a permissive signal by stimulating hypothalamic kisspeptin neurons, a central regulator of gonadotropin-releasing hormone secretion [10]. Children with obesity have higher leptin levels, which may lower the threshold for pubertal activation, particularly in girls. Insulin resistance and hyperinsulinemia, common in obesity, may further stimulate pubertal progression by enhancing gonadotropin-releasing hormone and luteinizing hormone secretion, increasing adrenal androgen production, and reducing sex hormonebinding globulin levels, thereby increasing the bioavailability of sex steroids. Other adipocyte-derived factors, such as adiponectin, as well as obesity-related low-grade inflammation, may also modulate pubertal timing by influencing hypothalamic and pituitary hormone secretion [7]. Taken together, these findings suggest that obesity may promote early puberty through multiple interacting hormonal pathways.

In conclusion, accumulating evidence indicates that excess weight gain in early life is not only a metabolic concern but also an important determinant of pubertal progression. Therefore, early-life obesity prevention is a potential strategy to reduce the growing burden of precocious puberty. Greater attention to healthy growth patterns from infancy and early childhood may help support normal pubertal development and improve long-term health outcomes.
