Energy-Efficient Seawater Lithium Extraction via a Reversible Redox-Hydrogen Coupled System
Authors: Yigang Wang, Sixie Yang, Yiwen Liu, Chang Wang, Hui Pan, Zhenjie Zhang, Ping He, Haoshen Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The global surge in lithium demand poses growing challenges for supply security and sustainability, yet vast lithium reserves in seawater remain largely untapped due to extremely low Li⁺ concentrations and high levels of competing ions. Here, we present an integrated electrochemical system coupling direct lithium extraction from seawater with in situ energy storage and hydrogen co-production. A reversible Ni(OH)2/NiOOH redox electrode replaces conventional anodic seawater electrolysis, storing electrical energy that would otherwise be wasted and enabling its recovery on demand through a Zn-NiOOH battery configuration. Meanwhile, a NiS2/MoS2 catalyst lowers the overpotential for hydrogen evolution at the cathode, generating approximately 807 mL of hydrogen gas per gram of lithium extracted. This synergistic design reduces net energy consumption to 6.40 Wh g–1Li, upgrades seawater from a low-quality brine (0.183 mg L–1 Li+) to a Li-rich solution (306.2 mg L–1) and lowers the Mg/Li ratio by seven orders of magnitude without additional reagent. This approach demonstrates a practical, sustainable route to unlock seawater as a viable high-grade lithium resource for a secure energy future.

Introduction

Global demand for lithium is expected to increase by a factor of 8–10 by 2050, driven by the rapid expansion of lithium-based electric vehicles and energy storage systems1–3. At present, lithium is primarily extracted from hard-rock ores and salar brines on land; however, only around 30 million tons of these reserves are considered economically viable for extraction4. Furthermore, these limited resources are geographically concentrated in a small number of countries2, raising concerns over supply security and global economic equity. Given that seawater contains an estimated 230 billion tons of lithium, developing effective methods for lithium extraction from seawater is critical to addressing potential future shortages5.

Seawater contains lithium at an extremely low concentration of approximately 0.17 mg L−1 and is rich in interfering ions such as Na+, Mg+, K+, and Ca2+. As a result, seawater is considered a low-quality brine, and extracting lithium from it remains highly challenging6. Electrochemical lithium extraction using lithium ion-sieve membranes represents a promising direct extraction method5,7–9. In this approach, lithium ion-sieve membranes separate the seawater (anode compartment) from the recovery solution (cathode compartment). Electrodes are immersed in each compartment and connected to an external power supply. Upon application of an electric field, electrochemical reactions occur at the electrode interfaces, and Li+ selectively migrate across the membranes from the seawater to the recovery solution. Mg2+ and Ca2+ in the feed solution are blocked by the membrane, and the transport of Na+ and K+ is hindered. Unlike conventional methods such as evaporation-precipitation10,11, solvent extraction12,13, adsorption14,15, and membrane-based separation16–18, this electrochemical process offers several advantages, including a broad operating concentration range for lithium, high separation efficiency, and relative simplicity of operation5,19,20.

The driving force for Li+ separation from interfering ions in this system is provided by electrical energy. A continuous current is maintained in the electrochemical cell through redox reactions occurring at both electrodes. Typically, the anodic reaction involves seawater electrolysis, producing oxygen or chlorine gas21,22. On the cathode side, when an organic electrolyte is used as the recovery solution, lithium ions are reduced to form metallic lithium5,23. If an aqueous recovery solution is used instead, hydrogen gas evolution can occur at the cathode24,25. This coupling of lithium extraction from seawater with simultaneous hydrogen production is a novel and intriguing topic that remains underexplored. The potential for cathodic hydrogen evolution in seawater can be modulated through catalyst design, where lowering the overpotential of hydrogen evolution helps reduce the overall cell voltage and energy consumption.

On the other hand, as previously mentioned, in this electrochemical lithium extraction method, additional energy is consumed at the anode for oxygen or chlorine evolution through seawater electrolysis. However, the energy value of oxygen or chlorine is significantly lower than that of hydrogen, rendering this energy consumption inefficient. If the energy used at the anode could be stored or utilized instead of being wasted in seawater electrolysis, the overall energy efficiency of lithium extraction from seawater could be greatly improved.

We propose the design of a reversible redox electrode to replace the conventional anodic seawater electrolysis reaction, which is typically required to sustain continuous current flow in the electrochemical lithium extraction system. During lithium extraction, the active material at the anode undergoes a reversible redox transformation—from a reduced state to an oxidized state—thereby storing electrical energy in the form of chemical energy. This stored energy can later be recovered by discharging the electrode, during which the oxidized state reverts to the reduced state. Based on this concept, we developed a high-efficiency lithium extraction system. As illustrated in Fig. 1, both the anode and cathode compartments of the electrochemical cell use seawater as the electrolyte: the seawater in the anode compartment serves as the feed solution, while the seawater in the cathode compartment functions as the recovery solution. The two compartments are separated by a lithium ion-sieve membrane, which allows Li+ to pass through following an ion-hopping mechanism26–28. Under the influence of an applied electric field, Li+ selectively migrate from the feed solution to the recovery solution, enriched on the cathode side and subsequently precipitated as Li2CO3 for extraction. Simultaneously, hydrogen evolution occurs at the cathode and is collected, while the rising pH precipitates interfering metal ions such as Mg2+. In the anode compartment, Ni(OH)2 is used as the redox-active material, which is oxidized to NiOOH during operation (Corresponding to step 1). This redox couple (Ni(OH)2/NiOOH) can be integrated into a Zn–NiOOH battery by pairing with metallic zinc, allowing the stored energy to be released for reuse (Corresponding to step 2)29. By integrating electrochemical lithium extraction from seawater with energy storage, the electrical energy that would otherwise be wasted in anodic seawater electrolysis is instead efficiently recovered and reused. This approach significantly improves energy utilization efficiency and offers a promising strategy for optimizing electrochemical lithium extraction technology.Fig. 1Schematic diagram of the versatile strategy for lithium extraction from seawater.Both compartments use seawater as the electrolyte, separated by a Li+-selective LAGP membrane. Results and discussion

Fabrication of the lithium extraction system

The lithium extraction system is shown in Fig. 2a and mainly consists of a blue tank for the feed solution, a white canister for the recovery solution, an electrolyzer, and two pumps. The structural schematic of the electrolyzer is provided in Fig. 2b. Seawater was collected from Zhoushan Qundao, Zhejiang Province, China (Supplementary Fig. 1) and pumped into the cathode and anode compartments as the electrolyte (Fig. 2c). These compartments were separated by a lithium ion-sieve membrane, Li1.5Al0.5Ge1.5(PO4)3 (LAGP), synthesized via a conventional solid-state sintering process and sealed in a polyethylene board (Fig. 2d)30. SEM images confirmed that the sintered LAGP membrane has a dense microstructure (Fig. 2e), while its XRD pattern matched the standard NASICON phase (Fig. 2f). The LAGP membrane exhibited high ionic conductivity of 2.12 × 10–4 S cm−1 at room temperature (25 °C, Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 1). The chemical stability of the LAGP membrane in seawater was assessed by monitoring changes in ionic conductivity using AC impedance spectroscopy during prolonged immersion. After 300 h in seawater, the ionic conductivity decreased slightly from 2.12 × 10−4 S cm−1 to 1.98 × 10−4 S cm−1, corresponding to a reduction of 9.34%. Ni(OH)2 was grown on multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) using a precipitation method31. Supplementary Fig. 3a shows its morphology, and EDS mapping verified uniform Ni(OH)2 distribution on the MWCNTs surface (Supplementary Fig. 3b–d). Thermogravimetric analysis indicated that Ni(OH)2 accounted for ~70 wt% of the Ni(OH)2/MWCNTs composite (Supplementary Fig. 4). The Ni(OH)2 exhibited a reversible specific capacity of 206 mAh g–1 at 1 mA cm–2 (Supplementary Fig. 5). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 6, the Ni(OH)2/MWCNTs electrode retained approximately 80% of its initial capacity after 50 redox cycles in seawater, confirming its reversible electrochemical behavior and acceptable stability under the operating conditions. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 7, its oxidation potential was 1.41 V vs. RHE, lower than that for the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) or chlorine evolution reaction (ClER) ( > 1.54 V vs. RHE). Mass spectrometry analysis revealed that O2 and Cl2 gases were detected only above 1.64 V vs. RHE at 1 mA cm–2 (Supplementary Fig. 8), confirming that careful control of capacity and potential can favor Ni(OH)2 oxidation while suppressing O2 and Cl2 evolution.

A cost-effective and stable NiS2/MoS2 composite was used as the hydrogen evolution catalyst due to its robust performance across a wide pH range32,33. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 9a, this catalyst features needle-like and sheet-like structures that increase the exposure of active sites. EDS mapping confirmed the homogeneous distribution of Ni, Mo, and S elements, consistent with previous reports (Supplementary Fig. 9b, c)32.

The resistance of this whole device was measured to approximately 850 Ω (Supplementary Fig. 10). Supplementary Fig. 11 shows the voltage profiles of the electrolyzer under current densities of 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 mA cm–2, which were approximately 1.65 V, 2.10 V, 2.54 V, and 2.91 V, respectively. Higher current densities accelerated OH− generation, enhancing Mg2+ precipitation and lowering the Mg/Li ratio (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 12). The Li⁺ flux peaked at 0.97 g m−2 h−1 at 1.0 mA cm−2 while it declined at 1.5 mA cm−2, likely due to co-transport of interfering ions. Thus, 1.0 mA cm−2 was selected as the optimal current density for subsequent experiments.

Lithium mass balance was verified by monitoring Li concentrations in both the feed (cathode) and recovery (anode) solutions. The volumes of the feed and recovery solutions were 20 L and 50 mL, respectively, with an initial Li concentration of 0.183 mg L−1. After 2 h of operation, Li concentration dropped to 0.145 mg L−1 in the feed solution and increased to 15.383 mg L−1 in the recovery solution (Supplementary Table 2). The Li mass gained in the recovery solution matched the loss from the feed, confirming that the total Li mass remained constant (Supplementary Fig. 13 and Supplementary Table 3), thereby validating the feasibility of the proposed extraction approach.

Lithium enriched and extracted by forming Li2CO3 from seawater

A continuous 48-h lithium extraction experiment was subsequently carried out. The recovery solution volume was kept constant at 50 mL, while fresh seawater was continuously pumped into the anode compartment as the feed solution. The operating voltage remained stable at approximately 2.55 V under a current density of 1 mA cm−2 (Fig. 3a). The concentrations of various cations in the recovery solution were measured every 4 h. As shown in Fig. 3b, the lithium concentration gradually increased throughout the extraction process. Meanwhile, the increase in pH due to the hydrogen evolution reaction led to the precipitation of Mg2+ and Ca2+, decreasing their concentrations (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 14a). After 48 hours of electrolysis, the Li concentration in the recovery solution reached 306.2 mg L−1, while Mg decreased to 0.15 mg L−1, resulting in a substantial drop in the Mg/Li ratio from 6615.4 to 4.9 × 10−4 (Supplementary Table S4 and Table S5). Besides, the Na/Li ratio decreased from 57180.3 to 40.5, the K/Li ratio decreased from 2251.4 to 2.0, and the Ca/Li ratio decreased from 2221.9 to 3.3 × 10−6. The separation factors of Li+/Na+ (βLi+/Na+), Li+/K+ (βLi+/K+), and Li+/Ca2+ (βLi+/Ca2+) reached 1.41 × 103, 1.12 × 103 and 6.80 × 108, respectively. The electrochemical lithium extracting process significantly increased Li concentration, reducing the Mg/Li ratio and Ca/Li ratio because Mg2+ and Ca2+ were removed through in situ alkaline precipitation driven by the hydrogen evolution reaction, which did not require the addition of reagents. Precipitates formed in the white canister on the cathode side (Fig. 2a) were collected and analyzed. XRD data confirmed the presence of Mg(OH)2 and Ca(OH)2 (Supplementary Fig. 15), while ICP-OES results indicated that Mg(OH)2 accounted for approximately 79.8 wt% of the solid (Supplementary Table 6). Given that the Li concentration in the recovery solution reached 306.2 mg L−1, Li+ could be efficiently recovered as Li2CO3 using a conventional evaporation-precipitation process (Fig. 3c). In total, 200 mL recovery solution (four cycles of the electrochemical lithium extraction process, each involving 50 mL) was concentrated by evaporation. The concentrated solution was filtered and diluted to 20 mL, with a Li concentration of 3084.2 (±16.6) mg L−1. 273.3 mg of Li2CO3 precipitated out after adding ~1.0 g of Na2CO3 at 80 °C, corresponding to a precipitation efficiency of 83.2%. XRD patterns further confirmed that the precipitate was Li2CO3 (Fig. 3d). To assess the product purity, 0.1 g of Li2CO3 was dissolved in 1 L of 3 wt% HNO3 solution; ICP-OES analysis showed that Li content was much higher than that of any residual ions (Supplementary Table 7). The Li mass in 0.1 g of product was determined to be 18.71 mg, corresponding to a calculated Li2CO3 purity of 99.6 wt% (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Table 7).

As shown in Fig. 4b, a custom device was designed to quantitatively measure the evolved hydrogen during lithium extraction using mass spectrometry. After extraction, the oxidized Ni(OH)2 electrode could be directly coupled with a Zn foil in seawater to form a Zn–NiOOH battery, electrochemically reducing NiOOH back to Ni(OH)2. A Hg/HgO reference electrode was employed to monitor the hydrogen evolution potential of the NiS2/MoS2/Ni foam electrode, while Ar gas served as both the purging and carrier gas during mass spectrometry analysis. As shown in Fig. 4c, the hydrogen evolution potential remained stable at −0.26 V vs. RHE, and the measured hydrogen yield closely matched the theoretical two-electron process. After 2 h of electrolysis (corresponding to a capacity of 2 mAh), 36.37 μmol of H2 was produced, achieving 97.5% of the theoretical yield. The slight deviation from 100% was likely due to the difficulty of fully purging the system to reach equilibrium.

Figure 4d shows the operational voltage (red dashed line) during lithium extraction and the Ni2+ → Ni3+ redox potential of the anode (red solid line, vs. Zn/ZnO). In this system, the total charge passed through the anode during lithium extraction must be effectively stored within the Ni(OH)2/NiOOH redox electrode to avoid unwanted side reactions. The theoretical specific capacity of the Ni(OH)2 electrode is approximately 206 mAh g−1. Therefore, the mass of the Ni(OH)2 anode must be carefully designed to fully accommodate the charge required for Li+ extraction; otherwise, excess charge would lead to OER or ClER at the anode. During the 2 h electrolysis, the Ni(OH)2 electrode operated at a specific charge capacity of about 147.1 mAh g−1, remaining below its theoretical limit to ensure efficient energy storage without triggering OER or ClER. After Li extraction, the oxidized NiOOH was reduced back to Ni(OH)2 by discharging through a Zn–NiOOH battery (blue solid line), with Zn oxidized to ZnO (Supplementary Fig. 17). The Zn–NiOOH battery showed an average discharge voltage of 1.51 V in seawater and a specific discharge capacity of 144.9 mAh g–1, achieving a Coulombic efficiency of 98.5%, demonstrating excellent redox reversibility in seawater.

Raman spectra confirmed the redox transitions of Ni(OH)2 in the Ni(OH)2/MWCNTs electrode (Fig. 4e). Peaks at 315.2 cm−1 and 453.5 cm−1 corresponded to Ni(OH)2, while peaks at 1332.5 cm−1 and 1601.3 cm−1 were assigned to MWCNTs34. The vibrational peak at 485.9 cm−1, indicative of Ni3+, appeared during oxidation and disappeared upon reduction, demonstrating excellent reversibility35. Finally, to confirm that the electrical energy had been stored in Ni(OH)2, a pouch cell was assembled with a Zn metal anode and subjected to discharge testing. The Zn–NiOOH pouch batteries were able to discharge directly and successfully powered an LED array displaying the “NJU” pattern (Fig. 4f).

In addition to the above understanding, this energy storage process can also be regarded as storing electrical energy in a nickel–hydrogen battery composed of hydrogen and nickel hydroxide. Furthermore, this charging process is equivalent to charging such a battery composed of nickel hydroxide and hydrogen. During charging, hydrogen is produced and acts as the negative electrode, while nickel hydroxide is converted into nickel oxyhydroxide, which serves as the positive electrode. The durability of the full lithium extraction system was evaluated under continuous operation. Using an LAGP membrane to separate the cathode and anode seawater compartments, with NiS2/MoS2/Ni foam and Ni(OH)2/MWCNTs serving as the cathode and anode, respectively, a sustained lithium extraction process was demonstrated over 120 h (Supplementary Fig. 18). This experiment confirms the operational stability of the integrated system under prolonged electrochemical conditions.

Energy consumption evaluation and changes in Mg/Li ratio

Figure 5a illustrates the energy distribution during the lithium extraction process. The total electrical input was divided into three pathways: approximately 66.5% was stored in (1) hydrogen via the hydrogen evolution reaction at the cathode; and (2) Ni(OH)2/NiOOH redox couple through the oxidation of Ni(OH)2, which could later be recovered by coupling with a Zn anode to form a Zn–NiOOH battery; and (3) the remaining 33.5% was directly used to drive Li+ migration across the membrane. For the 2 mAh lithium extraction experiment, the total electrical energy consumed was 19.13 Wh g−1Li. Of this, 12.73 Wh g–1Li was stored in hydrogen and Ni(OH)2/NiOOH redox couple, while the remaining 6.40 Wh g−1Li facilitated lithium extraction (see Supplementary Fig. 19 and Supplementary Table 8). Notably, this system co-produced approximately 807 mL of hydrogen per gram of lithium extracted. In addition, the process yields valuable by-products such as Mg(OH)2 without requiring any added alkali. The seawater in the cathode compartment was transferred into a lithium-rich alkaline solution, from which Li2CO3 can be readily precipitated by adding Na2CO3. As shown in Fig. 5b, the reversible redox-hydrogen coupled system demonstrated superior Mg2+/Li+ separation compared with previously reported methods14–16,22–24,27,36,37,41–65. This enhanced selectivity arises from the combined effects of the high Li+ selectivity of the LAGP membrane and the elevated pH at the cathode, which promotes Mg2+ precipitation during lithium extraction. In this study, the Mg/Li ratio was reduced from 6615.4 to 4.9 × 10–4, corresponding to a Li+/Mg2+ separation factor (βLi+/Mg2+) of 1.35 × 107—substantially exceeding values reported to date. From a resource utilization perspective, water bodies with lithium concentrations below 0.26 g L–1 and Mg/Li ratios above 6.15 are typically regarded as low-quality brines6. While many conventional approaches can reduce the Mg/Li ratio, they often fail to concentrate lithium beyond this threshold. By contrast, this system directly upgraded seawater to a high-grade brine with a lithium concentration of 306.2 mg L–1 and a Mg/Li ratio as low as 4.9 × 10–4 (Supplementary Fig. 21 and Supplementary Table 11), demonstrating clear promise for sustainable and efficient lithium recovery from seawater.

The integrated electrochemical system demonstrates the feasibility of coupling direct lithium extraction from seawater with in situ energy storage and hydrogen co-production. Industrial-scale deployment may be envisioned through the following three aspects. First, large-area lithium ion-sieve membranes, for example, LAGP-polyethylene membrane, can be fabricated23. Increasing the membrane area directly enhances the processing capacity of a single lithium extraction unit. Second, the system architecture is inherently modular and readily scalable. A lithium extraction system in which multiple extraction units were connected in parallel was designed and tested (Supplementary Fig. 22a). A laboratory-scale device consisting of three lithium extraction units connected in parallel was constructed (Supplementary Fig. 22b, c), and stable operation was demonstrated over a continuous 24-h lithium extraction process (Supplementary Fig. 22d). This modular parallel configuration confirms that system throughput can be scaled without altering the underlying electrochemical mechanism. Third, cost-optimized industrial materials could be used in the system. Other low-cost lithium ion-sieve membranes, such as Li1.5Al0.3Ti1.7Si0.2P2.8O12 (LATSP), with an estimated material cost of approximately 3.39 United States dollars (USD) per kilogram, which can also be used in this system66. The XRD pattern indicated that the material possessed the same NASICON-type structure as LAGP (Supplementary Fig. 23a)67. The ionic conductivity of the LATSP membrane was 5.6 × 10−4 S cm−1 at room temperature (Supplementary Fig. 23b). When the LATSP membrane was applied in the electrochemical lithium extraction system, the system could also operate stably (Supplementary Fig. 23c). After a continuous 48-h lithium extraction, the lithium concentration increased from 0.183 mg L−1 to 301.8 mg L−1 and Mg/Li ratio reduced from 6615.3 to 4.6 × 10–4 (Supplementary Table 12). The results further indicate that the proposed electrochemical strategy designed in this manuscript does not rely on the LAGP membrane. Other low-cost lithium ion-sieve membranes, such as LATSP, are equally suitable for this system.

The separation performance and system scope of the electrochemical lithium extraction system are different from those of conventional electrodialysis (ED) processes. Compared to ED processes, this electrochemical system exhibits a higher Li+/Mg2+ separation factor, demonstrating potential for extracting lithium from seawater. The reported Li+/Mg2+ separation factors in ED processes are generally below 100 when treating pre-concentrated brines or artificial solutions with lithium concentrations in the range of tens to hundreds of milligrams per liter (Supplementary Table 13). To date, conventional ED systems have not demonstrated the capability for direct lithium extraction from raw seawater, where lithium concentration is extremely low (approximately 0.18 mg L–1) and the Mg/Li ratio exceeds 6000.

By contrast, the system proposed in this manuscript is specifically designed for direct application to seawater. Using a single lithium ion-sieve membrane, the Mg/Li ratio was reduced from 6615.4 in raw seawater to 4.9 × 10−4 in the recovery solution, corresponding to an exceptionally high Li+/Mg2+ separation factor of 1.35 × 107. This separation performance is achieved without pre-concentration or multi-stage membrane stacks, highlighting the fundamentally different separation mechanism enabled by lithium-selective ion transport combined with in situ alkaline precipitation of divalent cations. The ability to directly upgrade seawater into a high-grade lithium brine with an ultralow Mg/Li ratio represents a key advantage of the present approach and further supports its relevance for sustainable lithium recovery from low-quality water resources.

In summary, this work presents a reversible redox-hydrogen coupled system that enables direct and energy-efficient lithium extraction from seawater. By integrating a reversible redox electrode, Ni(OH)2, at the anode, the system stores the electrical energy that would otherwise be wasted on oxygen and chlorine evolution during conventional seawater electrolysis. This stored energy can be recovered on demand through a Zn–NiOOH battery configuration, significantly improving overall energy utilization. Meanwhile, a NiS2/MoS2 catalyst at the cathode effectively lowers the overpotential for the hydrogen evolution reaction, reducing the net energy input required for lithium extraction while simultaneously producing hydrogen gas as a valuable co-product with additional energy storage potential. Benefiting from this combined anode-cathode design, the net energy directly consumed for lithium enrichment is substantially reduced, achieving an energy consumption as low as 6.40 Wh g–1Li at a current density of 1 mA cm–2. After 48 h of operation, the Mg/Li mass ratio in the recovery solution decreased dramatically from 6615.4 to 4.9 × 10−4, while the lithium concentration increased from 0.183 mg L−1 to 306.2 mg L−1, upgrading seawater from a low-quality brine to a lithium-rich solution suitable for efficient recovery. Li2CO3 can then be easily precipitated by adding Na2CO3, yielding a final product with a purity of 99.6 wt%. Overall, this work demonstrates that the rational integration of a reversible redox anode and an efficient hydrogen evolution cathode can substantially lower the energy cost of lithium extraction, while co-producing hydrogen and valuable by-products, offering a promising pathway toward sustainable and resource-efficient lithium recovery from seawater.

Method

Preparation of the LAGP membrane

The LAGP membrane was synthesized via a conventional solid-state reaction method30. The starting materials Li2CO3 (99%, Alfa Aesar), Al2O3 (AR, Nanjing Chemical Reagent Co., China), NH4H2PO4 (99%, Aladdin), and GeO2 (99.999%, Aladdin) were mixed in a stoichiometric ratio with 10% extra Li. The mixture was milled (Pulverisette 5, Fritsch) with agate balls for 500 min at 400 revolutions per minute. Then, the mixture was sintered at 600 °C for 1 h and 900 °C for 6 h (heating rate of 2 °C min−1). The ball-milling process was repeated after each sintering process. 0.4 g LAGP powder was pressed into an LAGP pellet using a 16 mm diameter die and sintered at 900 °C for 6 h. The thickness of the polished LAGP membrane was about 0.552 mm.

Preparation of the Ni(OH)2/MWCNTs

First, multiwalled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs, 95%, Macklin) underwent a hydrophilic treatment before use. The MWCNTs were sonicated in 30% HNO3 solution for 30 min, then washed with pure water, and finally dried at 100 °C for one night. In total, 0.3 g of accurately weighed MWCNTs was immersed and dispersed in a bath that contained 2.2 g of 0.1 M Ni(NO3)2·6H2O (99%, Aladdin) solution. Then, 0.1 M KOH (AR. Nanjing Reagent) solution was dropped into the bath while stirring until the pH of the aqueous solution was 8.5. The resulting product was filtered and repeatedly washed with pure water, dried at 80 °C. The thermogravimetric test showed that the weight ratio of Ni(OH)2 in Ni(OH)2/MWCNTs was ~70%.

Preparation and electrochemical test of the Ni(OH)2/MWCNTs anode electrode

The mass ratio of Ni(OH)2/MWCNTs composites, acetylene black, and polytetrafluoroethylene was 8:1:1, which was mixed to form a film. Then the film was weighed and pressed onto a Ti-mesh that served as a current collector surface (1 cm2 for coin cell and 16 cm2 for pouch battery). The Ni(OH)2/MWCNTs composite electrode was characterized by CV with a scan rate of 5 mV s–1 in seawater and a galvanostatic charge-discharge test for zinc–nickel coin cell at a current density of 1 mA cm−2. The electrolyte in batteries was a 6 M KOH solution. The onset potential of the oxygen evolution reaction on the commercial IrO2-coated Ti-mesh electrode was investigated by linear sweep voltametric measurements with a sweep rate of 5 mV s−1 in seawater.

Preparation of the NiS2/MoS2

The synthesis process was based on previously reported literature32. Firstly, NiMoO4 nanowire was synthesized through a hydrothermal method. 1.3 mmol Ni(NO3)2·6H2O and 3 mmol Na2MoO4·2H2O (99%, Aladdin) were dissolved into 70 mL distilled water and stirred for 30 min. Then the above solution was poured into a 100 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and subjected to the heat treatment at 150 °C for 6 h. Then, the obtained precipitates were collected and rinsed with distilled water and ethanol several times, and dried at 80 °C in an oven overnight. For the process of sulfuration, the as-synthesized NiMoO4 and S powders were put at two independent porcelain-boats with 500 mg S (AR. Nanjing Reagent) and 50 mg NiMoO4 at the upstream and downstream side of the furnace, respectively, then the furnace was allowed to anneal at 350 °C for 1 h with a ramp rate of 2 °C min−1 under N2 atmosphere to obtain the NiS2/MoS2.

Preparation and electrochemical test of the NiS2/MoS2/Ni foam cathode electrode

The ratio of NiS2/MoS2 composites, Nafion, and isopropanol was 0.1 mg: 0.02 mL: 1.98 mL, which was mixed to form a solution. Then the solution was adhered to the Ni foam electrode that served as a current collector surface. The onset potential of the HER on the electrode was investigated by linear sweep voltametric measurements with a sweep rate of 5 mV s–1 in seawater.

Material characterization

SEM images and EDS mappings were captured by a Hitachi SU8010. The crystal structure of the cathodic deposition was confirmed by XRD (D8 Advance, Bruker, Cu Kα, λ = 1.5406 Å). The resolution of Raman spectroscopy was around 1.0 cm−1 (PerkinElmer, Spectrum Two LiTa) with a wavenumber range of 900–300 cm−1 and a resolution of 1.0 cm−1. The thermogravimetric (TG) test was performed under O₂ atmosphere with a thermogravimetric analyzer (SDT Q600). In situ differential electrochemical mass spectrometry (DEMS) measurement was performed in a home-made Swagelok battery mold connected to the spectrometer compartment (PrismaPro QMG 250 M2) with a turbomolecular pump (Pfeiffer Vacuum). The ion concentrations of Li+, Na+, K+, Mg2+, Ca2+ in organic electrolyte, seawater, and the solution after dissolving the deposition on Cu foil were measured by ICP-OES Avio™ 200, PerkinElmer.

Evaluation of the stability of the key functional components

To evaluate the stability of the hydrogen evolution catalyst under realistic operating conditions, a three-electrode configuration was employed, with NiS2/MoS2 supported on Ni foam as the working electrode, Pt foil as the counter electrode, and a saturated calomel electrode as the reference electrode. Seawater was used as the electrolyte. Chronopotentiometry measurements were conducted at a constant current density of 1 mA cm−2, corresponding to the operating conditions used during lithium extraction.

Lithium extraction progress

Ni(OH)2/MWCNTs anode electrode was set in the anode compartment, and NiS2/MoS2/Ni foam cathode electrode was set in the cathode compartment. Seawater (From Zhoushan Qundao, Zhejiang Province, China. Li+: 0.183 ppm) was used as electrolyte. In this case, the volume of seawater in the cathode compartment was 50 mL, and the anode compartment was continuously supplied with new seawater. By applying a voltage, the electrode underwent an electrochemical reaction. The electric field drove the transfer of Li+ from the anode compartment to the cathode compartment.

ICP test

We mainly tested the ion concentrations of Li+, Na+, K+, Mg2+, and Ca2+ in seawater and the solution after dissolving the deposition in the cathode compartment, and the precipitates after adding Na2CO3 to the cathode compartment seawater. The seawater contains 0.183 ppm Li+. Considering the concentrations of Na+, K+, Mg2+, and Ca2+ were quite high, we diluted seawater with pure water to measure them. We dissolved the deposition in the cathode compartment, the precipitates after adding Na2CO3 to the cathode compartment seawater in a 1 L 3 wt% HNO3 solution, respectively. Before the ICP test, we used pure water to dilute them.
