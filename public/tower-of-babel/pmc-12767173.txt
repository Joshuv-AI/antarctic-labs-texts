Deep learning-assisted diagnosis of pubertal breast development on multicenter ultrasound images
Authors: Chao Geng, Chao Qian, Xiaoshi Hu, Manli Fu, Hui Yao, Wei Li, Han Jing, Junyi Xin, Chengwen Guo, Yang Wang, Yongmei Jia, Hao Tu, Nanqian Yi, Yihao Niu, Xuming Zhang, Li Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryAccurate classification of pubertal breast development is essential for evaluating pubertal timing and disorders. Breast ultrasound offers an objective and noninvasive approach that complements clinical staging but is limited by interobserver variability. This multicenter retrospective study included 2,576 ultrasound images from three hospitals to develop and validate a hybrid deep learning model, STransXNet, for automatic staging of pubertal breast development. The model was trained on internal data and tested on both internal and external cohorts. STransXNet achieved an accuracy of 88.0% on the internal test set, outperforming baseline models and resident radiologists, and showed robust generalizability across external datasets and imaging systems. Importantly, it demonstrated superior performance in differentiating intermediate stages, which are most challenging in clinical practice. These findings indicate that STransXNet can provide standardized and reproducible assessment of pubertal breast development and may support clinical decision-making in settings with limited radiological expertise.Subject areas: developmental anatomy, artificial intelligence

Introduction

Pubertal breast development is a key indicator of sexual maturation in adolescent girls and plays a central role in the assessment of pubertal timing and progression.1 The beginning of puberty in females is generally associated with the development of the mammary glands,2,3 a process known as “thelarche”. With a noted trend toward earlier breast development in girls,4 there is a rising concern about the developmental status of adolescents, particularly the issue of precocious puberty.5 Historically, the most widely recognized method for clinically assessing pubertal breast development has been the Tanner stages, which divides puberty into five stages based on visual inspection and palpation.2 These stages are essential for monitoring and tracking the progression and order of secondary sex characteristics in adolescents. However, the Tanner stages face challenges in distinguishing glandular tissue from adipose tissue in the breasts of obese children.6,7,8 Breast ultrasound (BUS) has emerged as a cost-effective, high-resolution, and objective tool to evaluate anatomical structures, such as glandular tissue, ducts, and adipose layers,9 thereby supporting more accurate staging. With growing clinical demands for more precise and individualized assessment, ultrasound-based staging has been increasingly explored in pediatric endocrinology as a complementary tool to Tanner stages, particularly in challenging situations such as obesity or the evaluation of suspected central precocious puberty.10 Previous studies have demonstrated that BUS can effectively characterize the morphological features of mammary glands at different stages of pubertal development, enabling a staging system that closely correlates with Tanner stages.10,11,12 However, it should be noted that the two systems are not perfectly aligned. For instance, ultrasound may detect the earliest stage of breast development earlier than Tanner stages, while the latest stages may appear later on ultrasound.11 However, in countries such as China, there remains a shortage of specialized pediatric radiologists, making it difficult to meet the rising demand.13 Moreover, despite its growing use, ultrasound staging still relies heavily on operator expertise and is susceptible to variability in image acquisition and interpretation. These limitations highlight the need for a more standardized, reproducible, and efficient approach to evaluating pubertal breast development.

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI), especially when powered by deep learning (DL) models, can facilitate providing valuable secondary opinions in clinical settings,14 improving workflow efficiency and diagnostic accuracy.15,16 Currently, the majority of these efforts are focused on three clinical tasks related to adult breast US, including lesion detection (detection), differential diagnosis (classification) and prognosis (prediction).17,18,19 However, their application to pubertal breast development remains underexplored.

Recent studies have shown that hybrid models combining convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and transformer architectures perform well in image classification tasks by capturing both local and global features.20,21 Building upon the existing TransXNet model,22 we developed a specialized version (STransXNet) and applied it innovatively to the classification of pubertal breast development stages using ultrasound images. Our goal was to assess whether such a system could reliably and automatically classify breast development stages on ultrasound images across multiple clinical settings. We hypothesized that this tool could complement physician judgment and help standardize pubertal staging in clinical care.

The experimental results indicate that, on the internal test set from WCH, STransXNet outperformed radiologists of all experience levels across all four metrics. Discussion

The accurate assessment of pubertal breast development is essential for the diagnosis and management of puberty-related disorders in adolescent girls. In clinical practice, breast development serves as a primary indicator of pubertal onset and progression, with important implications for evaluating conditions such as central precocious puberty, delayed puberty, and endocrine dysfunction. The most widely used clinical tool, Tanner stages, is inherently subjective and influenced by the examiner’s experience, especially in cases involving obesity or early-stage glandular changes. In this context, BUS provides a valuable imaging modality. By directly visualizing internal anatomical features—including glandular tissue, ductal structures, and adipose distribution—BUS enables a more objective and reproducible assessment of breast development. Each pubertal stage is associated with distinct sonographic characteristics, making BUS a reliable tool for anatomical staging across different phases of maturation.

Currently, several ultrasound-based staging system for pubertal breast development have been proposed,12,24,25,26 and in our study, we adopted the most widely used and validated classification system.24 Despite its advantages over traditional physical examination, the use of ultrasound for pubertal breast staging still faces several practical challenges. Image acquisition and interpretation depend heavily on operator expertise, and subtle differences between developmental stages may be overlooked due to subjective judgment or lack of experience. Nevertheless, prior studies have demonstrated that ultrasound-based staging is reproducible, with Bruserud et al. reporting very good intra-observer agreement (κ = 0.84, 95% CI 0.78–0.91) and good inter-observer agreement (κ = 0.71, 95% CI 0.62–0.80) in healthy girls.25 In a larger cohort, ultrasound staging also showed very good agreement with Tanner stages (κ = 0.87, 95% CI 0.85–0.88), although ultrasound tended to detect the earliest developmental changes earlier than Tanner stages.11 In many clinical settings, especially in developing regions, there is a shortage of specialized pediatric radiologists, making consistent and accurate assessment difficult to achieve. As the demand for early diagnosis and longitudinal monitoring of pubertal disorders increases, there is a growing need for intelligent tools that can provide objective, standardized, and efficient evaluation. In this context, AI—particularly DL models—offers a promising solution for automating the classification process and supporting clinical decision-making.

Recent advancements in DL have demonstrated strong performance in various BUS domains. CNN architectures have long served as the cornerstone of medical image analysis.27 In BUS, CNN-based model such as GoogLeNet have been employed for lesion classification with promising diagnostic accuracy.28,29 In addition, several studies have integrated multimodal input, such as fusion or radiofrequency signals, to further enhance performance.30,31 Then, the introduction of the transformer model in 2017 revolutionized the domain of DL,32 its success later inspired the development of vision transformers (ViT)33 and related architectures like Swin-Transformer34 for image recognition tasks. These models leverage self-attention mechanisms to capture global contextual information, enabling improved performance in complex image classification problems. Hybrid CNN-transformer models have also been developed, such as HCTNet35 and hybrid-MT-ESTAN,36 have demonstrated strong performance in both lesion segmentation and tumor classification by effectively combining local and global feature learning in breast ultrasound analysis. However, these studies have focused primarily on adult breast pathology. To our knowledge, our study represents a novel extension of AI-assisted image analysis into the pediatric domain, targeting a clinically important but underexplored area of adolescent health.

In this multicenter study, the proposed STransXNet model demonstrated superior classification performance in staging pubertal breast development from ultrasound images, consistently outperforming baseline models (ResNet34 and Swin-Transformer) as well as resident radiologists. On internal and external test sets, STransXNet achieved high accuracy, precision, recall, and specificity, with performance approaching—and in some instances exceeding—that of experienced attending radiologists. A key strength of the proposed model lies in its improved ability to differentiate the intermediate stages of breast development—stages II, III, and IV—which are often exhibit similar imaging features due to overlapping distributions of glandular, ductal, and adipose tissue, leading to visual similarities in ultrasound appearance. As a result, baseline DL models and less experienced radiologists often encounter difficulty in accurately classifying these stages. Our model demonstrated superior performance in this subset, suggesting enhanced diagnostic precision in the more ambiguous phases of pubertal development. This fine-grained classification capability is essential for reliable monitoring of pubertal progression, especially in borderline or early-onset cases such as central precocious puberty.

The robust performance of STransXNet can be attributed to two factors. First, the hybrid architecture integrates CNN with transformer, enhances representation learning by strategically combining self-attention mechanisms with convolutional operations. Therefore, the STransXNet excels in classifying breast development stages with high fidelity, providing a promising tool for evaluating breast development in cases of central precocious puberty. Second, the model was validated on a diverse multicenter dataset that included ultrasound images acquired from different hospitals, devices, and patient populations. This diversity enhances generalizability, minimizes machine-specific biases and ensures stable performance across varied clinical environments. The implications of these findings extend beyond the immediate benefits of improved diagnostic accuracy. By reducing reliance on subjective interpretation, the model can support standardized pubertal assessment, particularly in regions with limited access to experienced pediatric radiologists. This could facilitate earlier diagnosis and more accurate follow-up in patients with suspected pubertal disorders, improving continuity of care while reducing unnecessary interventions and associated healthcare costs.

Limitations of the study

Despite the promising results, our study has several limitations. The relatively small dataset and uneven distribution across stages may limit robust representation learning and increase the risk of overfitting, even with focal loss applied. Variability in image quality across different ultrasound systems could introduce noise and affect generalization. In addition, the cohort was recruited from a single province in China, with limited ethnic and geographic diversity, and although images were obtained from four vendors, not all globally used devices were represented. These factors may restrict the broader generalizability of our findings.

Experimental model and study participant details

Ethic statement

This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wuhan Children’s Hospital (2022R085-E02). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the research and anonymization of data.

Design and dataset

BUS images were collected from three hospitals: Hospital No. 1 (Wuhan Children’s Hospital, WCH), Hospital No. 2 (Yichang Central People’s Hospital, YCPH) and Hospital No. 3 (Xiangyang Central Hospital, XCH), over the period from January 1, 2020, to December 31, 2022. The study population included both healthy girls undergoing routine breast ultrasound and patients referred for evaluation or follow-up of suspected precocious puberty. The inclusion criteria were girls who underwent breast ultrasound. The exclusion criteria included: (1) Presence of central nervous system diseases; (2) Peripheral endocrine abnormalities; and (3) Prior exposure to exogenous estradiol and progesterone. The workflow of this study is shown in below figure.

The workflow of the study

A total of 3,134 images were initially reviewed. After excluding 558 images for inadequate quality, 2,576 high-quality grayscale BUS images were retained for further analysis, with some participants contributing more than one image. These images were acquired using a variety of ultrasound systems from multicenter, including Siemens ACUSON Sequoia Silver, Mindray Resona R9, Samsung RS85 Prestige, and GE LOGIQ E9, to reflect real-world imaging diversity across institutions and equipment vendors. Among the selected images, 1,676 images from WCH were allocated to the training set, and 300 images from WCH served as the internal test set. Additionally, 300 BUS images from XCH and 300 from YCPH were used as external test sets, with each stage consisting of 60 BUS images.

Method details

Breast development staging

All images were categorized according to a previously established and documented ultrasound-based classification system,24 The staging criteria were defined as follows:

Stage I (prepubertal): Shows ill-defined retroareolar tissue on BUS.

Stage II: Clinically appreciated as a palpable subareolar bud before it can be seen as an elevation. At BUS, it appears as a hyperechoic retroareolar nodule with a central star-shaped or linear hypoechoic area that represents mostly simple branched ducts.

Stage III: Notable enlargement and elevation of the entire breast. At BUS, hyperechoic glandular tissue is seen extending away from the retroareolar area, and a central spider-shaped hypoechoic region is noted.

Stage IV (areolar mound formation): May not always be visible. BUS typically shows hyperechoic fibroglandular tissue, along with a remarkably notable hypoechoic central mass and occasional subcutaneous adipose tissue.

Stage V: Indicates the attainment of mature breast. BUS reveals hyperechoic glandular tissue, with increased subcutaneous adipose tissue anteriorly and without the hypoechoic central nodule seen in Tanner stages II, III, and IV.

Data preprocessing and augmentation

All ultrasound images were preprocessed by cropping to remove any patient-identifiable information before analysis. Subsequently, all ultrasound images were independently reviewed and annotated by two senior pediatric radiologists, each with over ten years of experience in breast ultrasound interpretation. Both experts were trained and adhered to the same standardized five-stage ultrasound-based classification system. During the labeling process, the radiologists were blinded to patient information and institutional origin. In cases of disagreement, final staging decisions were reached through consensus discussion to ensure consistency.

The cropping of the original images is implemented to define the region of interest, followed by resizing to the specified dimensions. Furthermore, the initial training set was augmented by a factor of 9 using such strategies as rotation and flipping. The augmented training set included 2,214 images for Stage I, 4,167 images for Stage II, 2,871 images for Stage III, 2,835 images for Stage IV, and 2,997 images for Stage V.

Model construction (STransXNet)

In this paper, we propose STransXNet, a specialized variant of TransXNet, for enhanced classification of adolescent breast development. below figure A illustrates its comprehensive structure comprising four stages, in which each stage includes one Patch Embedding and three STransXNet Blocks. Specifically, the combination of Dynamic Position Encoding (DPE), Dynamic Token Mixer (DTM) and Multi-scale Feedforward Network (MS-FFN) is designed for each STransXNet Block. The DPE is calculated based on the input sequence and indices. The DTM acts as a lightweight dual token mixer, integrating the global and local information to enhance representation learning. The MS-FFN processes multi-scale features using parallel depth-wise convolutions.

As shown in below figure B, the DTM comprises overlapping spatial reduction attention (OSRA), a dynamic convolutional encoding (ConvEncoder) module and squeezed token enhancer (STE). Here, the OSRA aims to enhance spatial information retention. Different from the original TransXNet, the STransXNet uses the ConvEncoder module instead of the input-dependent depthwise convolution (DWConv) module. The ConvEncoder shown in below figure C integrates DWConv, layer normalization, and Gaussian Error Linear Unit (GeLU) activation function to extract local features. The features produced by OSRA and ConvEncoder are concatenated and processed by the STE. The detailed structures of OSRA and STE can be found in.22

Quantitative and statistical analysis

The quantitative and statistical analysis in this study was designed to evaluate the effectiveness, robustness, and clinical utility of the proposed STransXNet model for the task of pubertal breast development staging. All analyses were based on the model’s predictions on the internal and external test sets.

To comprehensively evaluate the classification performance of the models, we employed widely accepted metrics, including Accuracy, Precision, Recall, Specificity, and AUC. Accuracy represents the overall proportion of correctly classified instances. Precision reflects the agreement between the predicted positives and the actual class, calculated as the ratio of True Positives to all predicted positives. Recall, also known as Sensitivity, measures the model’s ability to identify all relevant instances of a class. Specificity quantifies the model’s performance in correctly detecting negative cases. The AUC provides a threshold-independent measure of overall discriminative capability. These metrics collectively evaluate the models’ global performance and their class-specific discriminative ability.

The performance comparison among the DL models (STransXNet, ResNet34, Swin-Transformer) was primarily conducted by directly comparing the values of the aforementioned metrics obtained on the same test sets. Similarly, the performance comparison between the model and radiologists was carried out by comparing their respective Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and Specificity values derived from the same set of test images. This direct comparison method is well-established for assessing whether an AI model reaches or surpasses human expert performance in classification tasks.

The performance metric values were reported in the manuscript as the numerical results calculated on the respective test sets. Confusion matrices and ROC curves were utilized to visualize the detailed classification results across different developmental stages and the overall classification efficacy of the models.

Model training

The STransXNet is implemented as a combination of ResNet37 and Swin-Transformer. The training settings are as follows. The loss function is chosen to be the focal loss, and the Adam optimizer is adopted to optimize parameter weights. The learning rate is initially established as 0.001 and then dynamically updated using the CosineLRScheduler. The batch size is chosen as 32, and the STransXNet is trained for 50 epochs.

Comparative evaluation

To evaluate the effectiveness of STransXNet, its performance was compared with ResNet34 and Swin Transformer, using the same augmented training dataset and hyperparameters.

All models were trained and validated under identical conditions to ensure fair comparison. Performance was assessed on the internal test sets.

Performance metrics

The performance of the models was evaluated using the standard metrics defined in the quantitative and statistical analysis section. To further visualize model performance and misclassifications across the five pubertal stages, confusion matrices and ROC curves were generated for each model (Figure 1 and 2).
