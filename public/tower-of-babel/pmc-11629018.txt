Treatment trends of systemic lupus erythematosus from 2007 to 2023 in the USA
Authors: Gabriel Figueroa-Parra, Herbert C Heien, Kenneth J Warrington, Nilay D Shah, Cynthia S Crowson, Rozalina G McCoy, Alí Duarte-García
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractABSTRACTObjectiveTo characterise the changing trends in the pharmacological management of SLE in the USA between 2007 and 2023 as new treatment options emerged.MethodsIn a retrospective cohort study using data from OptumLabs Data Warehouse, we characterised the annual prevalent (ie, all) and incident (ie, new) use of antimalarials, glucocorticoids and immunosuppressive medications among patients with SLE from 2007 to 2023 and assessed for changing trends over time.ResultsWe identified 19 122 adults with SLE; they were 51.2 (SD 16.1) years of age, 89% were female, 61.3% were White, 18.5% were Black and 13.1% were Hispanic. The proportion of prevalent users of antimalarials has decreased from 79.4% in 2007 to 77.2% in 2023 (p=0.0055), while the proportion of incident users fluctuated between a lowest 5.8% in 2021 and a highest 8.1% in 2008 (p=0.008). The proportion of prevalent users of glucocorticoids increased from 64.6% in 2007 to 66.7% in 2023 (p=0.0132), as did the proportion of incident users (12.4% in 2007 to 21.7% in 2023; p<0.0001). The use of cyclophosphamide (2.0% in 2007 to 0.4% in 2023, p<0.0001) has decreased; the use of mycophenolate mofetil (7.7% in 2007 to 10.3% in 2023, p<0.0001), rituximab (1.4% in 2007 to 2.1% in 2023, p<0.0001) and belimumab (0.8% in 2011 to 6.1% in 2023, p=0.0001) has increased.ConclusionsDespite increasing availability of alternative treatment options, patients with SLE in the USA increasingly rely on glucocorticoid-based therapy. Efforts to improve the use of antimalarials and steroid-sparing immunosuppressants are needed.Keywords: treatment, systemic lupus erythematosus, epidemiology, glucocorticoids, health services research

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Introduction

SLE is a systemic autoimmune disease characterised by inflammation and immune-mediated damage to any organ system or tissue.1 The incidence and prevalence have risen during the past decades, reaching 6.44 per 100 000 population and 97.4 per 100 000 population, respectively.2 SLE manifestations can lead to permanent organ damage and loss of function and among the goals of treatment for patients with SLE are the control of the disease manifestations, and the minimisation of damage secondary to medications and their side effects.

Among the current guidelines, there is agreement that all patients with SLE should use antimalarials, as well as glucocorticoids at the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration. If a persistent high dose of glucocorticoids is needed, other immunosuppressants should be introduced as steroid-sparing agents.3,7 In general, given the body of evidence about the benefits of antimalarials prolonging life expectancy, decreasing risks of flares and damage8 and the described safety profile of long-term use of glucocorticoids,9,11 these recommendations could be considered ‘good practice’, and have been incorporated as a measure of quality of care in SLE by the American College of Rheumatology (ACR).12 However, no contemporary clinical practice guidelines for SLE are issued by the ACR. Moreover, the treatment recommendations for patients with SLE have incorporated new strategies with combinations of immunosuppressants for severe or refractory cases. It is unknown if these recommendations in treatment have been incorporated in the USA.

Through a nationally representative SLE cohort, we aimed to characterise the pharmacological treatment patterns of patients with SLE.

Methods

Data source and study design

We assembled a retrospective SLE cohort using de-identified administrative claims data from the OptumLabs Data Warehouse (OLDW), which includes medical and pharmacy claims, and enrolment records for commercial and Medicare Advantage enrollees. The database contains longitudinal health information for over 200 million enrollees and patients, representing a mixture of ages and geographical regions across the USA.

Study population

We included all patients with SLE from 2007 to 2023. We defined an individual as having SLE if they met the following criteria: (1) ≥3 SLE (710.0, M32.x) office or hospital claims ≥30 days but ≤365 days apart13 and (2) ≥1 outpatient pharmacy claim for a non-glucocorticoid immunosuppressant (online supplemental table 1) that may have been used in SLE treatment at any time after the first SLE code. The index date was defined at the third SLE code date. We required a continuous enrolment of at least 6 months in both medical and pharmacy coverage after the index date. We excluded those patients with SLE codes without medication claims, since these patients were either miscoded or may have had very mild disease.

Baseline characteristics

We retrieved demographics, type of insurance, census geographical region of the USA, length of follow-up, comorbidities based on Charlson Comorbidity Index14 excluding rheumatological conditions, number of hospitalisations, emergency department (ED) visits, office visits and year of identification.

Medication prescriptions

Medications were identified using both, First DataBank’s Hierarchical Ingredient Code List, along with generic and brand name descriptions given in OLDW’s pharmacy files (online supplemental file). In the case of glucocorticoids, we included oral and intravenous presentations. Inhaled, topical, ophthalmic and other presentations of glucocorticoids were excluded.

Patients with SLE were categorised as current users of specific immunosuppressants, if they had at least one recorded claim for these medications within the calendar year.

Patients with SLE and a first prescription for the medication of interest were considered new users, if they did not have any claim for that medication during the prior 365 days.

Statistical analysis

We analysed the use of medications in three ways, for both prevalent and incident users. First, we examined the number of medications: categorising patients as taking one, two or three or more unique medications. In this analysis, besides grouping antimalarials, we also grouped glucocorticoids, while immunosuppressants were counted individually. The second analysis focused on trends in SLE medications. For this analysis, glucocorticoids were subdivided into prednisone, methylprednisolone and other glucocorticoids. Lastly, for prevalent users only, we looked at three categories of medications (antimalarials, glucocorticoids and other immunosuppressants) and their combinations. The details of the grouping are provided in online supplemental table 2.

Baseline characteristics of the cohort were summarised using a standard descriptive approach, which included a χ2 test for categorical and a standard T-test for continuous variables. To be compliant with the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services guidelines, all counts <11 are masked to ensure data integrity and patient privacy. For each medication group, we estimate the proportions of current users and new users per calendar year. For each calendar year, patients were required to have continuous enrolment over the entire year to be included. Patients who filled a medication prescription during the calendar year were considered current users, while those who had no medication fill in the 365 days prior to a medication fill were classified as incident users. If a person was defined as incident in one calendar year, they were considered prevalent in all consecutive calendar years until a gap >365 days occurred. For each medication category, a trend test was conducted using Pearson’s linear correlation. Rejection of the null hypothesis indicates either an increasing or decreasing linear relationship exists over the calendar years. Analyses were performed using SAS Enterprise Guide V.7.13.

Results

Population characteristics

We included 19 122 patients with a diagnosis of SLE between 2007 and 2023 who had received at least one non-glucocorticoid immunosuppressant (online supplemental figure 1). The mean age was 51.2 (SD 16.1) years, 88.9% were female, 61.3% were White, 18.5% were Black and 13.1% were Hispanic (table 1). The mean follow-up was 3.2 (SD 2.7) years. The mean number of hospitalisations, ED and office visits per patient were 0.3 (SD 0.7), 0.7 (SD 1.5) and 12.2 (SD 8.0), respectively. Details of comorbidities are depicted in table 1.

Treatment patterns

Of all the patients with SLE, the yearly proportion of those on any SLE medication ranged from 87.9% in 2016 to 93.5% in 2007. Over the years, the number of medications per patient has increased. The proportion of patients on monotherapy decreased from 36.9% in 2007 to 32.7% in 2023 (p<0.0001), and those on dual therapy decreased from 33.2% in 2007 to 32.1% in 2023 (p=0.0015). In contrast, patients on three or more medications increased from 29.9% in 2007 to 32.2% in 2023 (p<0.0001; figure 1). Among current immunosuppressant users, the more widely used medications across the 17 years of study were antimalarials (75.0%–79.8%) and glucocorticoids (64.6%–70.0%), with a slight but persistent decrease in the antimalarials users (79.4% in 2007 to 77.2% in 2023; p=0.0055) and an increasing proportion of glucocorticoids users (64.6% in 2007 to 66.7% in 2023; p=0.0132; table 2 and figure 2). Considering the different types of glucocorticoids, prednisone was the most filled—nearly half of the patients—and its use remained steady up to 2019 and has started to decrease in recent years (48.1% in 2007, 47.5% in 2019 and 43.3% in 2023; p=0.0046), although the use of methylprednisolone (27.1% in 2007 and 31.8% in 2023; p=0.0003) and other glucocorticoids (9.8% in 2007 and 21.5% in 2023; p<0.0001) has increased over the years.

Patterns of new users of pharmacological treatments among patients with SLE between 2007 and 2023 in the USA.

Methotrexate was the most commonly used immunosuppressant. The proportion of current and new users of methotrexate among patients with SLE has remained mostly unchanged from 2007 to 2023, fluctuating between 12.9%–15.0% and 3.0%–4.4%, respectively (figures2 3). The proportion of current users of mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) has increased from 7.7% in 2007 to 10.3% in 2023 (figure 2), with a significant change in the proportion of new users per year (around 2.2%–3.4%, figure 3). The third most frequently used immunosuppressant was azathioprine (6.9% in 2023), whose usage pattern has decreased during the studied timeframe (figures2 3). The proportion of current users per year of leflunomide tended to increase (1.6% in 2007 and 4.0% in 2023; p<0.0001), while the proportion of users of cyclophosphamide (2.0% in 2007 and 0.4% in 2023; p<0.0001) and ciclosporin (0.9% in 2007 and 0.2% in 2022; p<0.0001) decreased (figure 2). The current users of tacrolimus were below 1% and tended slightly to increase over the study period. The proportion of new users per year for these medications showed a similar pattern; however, tacrolimus and ciclosporin had a very low number of new users per year (figure 3).

The current users of rituximab and belimumab have increased over the years, from around 1% in 2011 to over 2% and 6% in 2023, respectively (figure 2). A similar pattern was observed in the analysis of new users of rituximab and belimumab despite the small numbers (figure 3 and table 3).

When we analysed the medications as categories and their combinations, the use of antimalarials monotherapy decreased (29.0% in 2007 to 23.4% in 2023; p<0.0001), particularly after 2014, as well as the use of the combination of antimalarials with glucocorticoids only (33.3% in 2007 to 29.6% in 2023; p=0.0002). The use of the combination of antimalarials with immunosuppressants and glucocorticoids has increased over the years (14.2% in 2007 to 17.7% in 2023; p<0.0001), as well as the use of antimalarials with immunosuppressants without glucocorticoids (2.8% in 2007 to 9.1% in 2023; p<0.0001). The proportion of patients using immunosuppressants with or without glucocorticoids but without antimalarials has remained steady (table 2). However, the use of glucocorticoids monotherapy has increased (7.0% in 2007 to 10.4% in 2023; p=0.0011; figure 1).

Discussion

In this nationwide, claims-based, retrospective cohort study, we found that the patterns of pharmacological treatment for patients with SLE in the USA have not changed dramatically over the last 17 years. Overall, the use of antimalarials, prednisone, azathioprine, cyclophosphamide and ciclosporin has decreased, whereas the use of other glucocorticoids (particularly methylprednisolone and other non-prednisone glucocorticoids), MMF, leflunomide, rituximab and belimumab has increased. The use of methotrexate has remained steady. Additionally, more patients are receiving multidrug therapy. However, one out of four patients with SLE did not use antimalarials, two out of three received glucocorticoids and 10% were on glucocorticoid monotherapy.

The standard of care for patients with SLE includes the use of antimalarials, particularly hydroxychloroquine, unless contraindicated.3 4 6 Despite this, our findings reveal that approximately 20%–25% of patients with SLE in the USA did not use these medications. It is unclear why this large proportion of patients were not on antimalarials. Discontinuation or avoidance of use due to side effects is a potential explanation, although there is limited contemporary data about the rates of side effects of antimalarials in SLE.8 Recent clinical trials of hydroxychloroquine for COVID-19 postexposure prophylaxis, which administered 400 mg of hydroxychloroquine, reported a 16% incidence of adverse events, primarily diarrhoea and abdominal discomfort.15 The cumulative incidence of hydroxychloroquine retinopathy is below 4% at 10 years and it is dose-dependent.16 17 These rates are lower than the proportion of non-users that we observed across the years. We also noticed a decrease in hydroxychloroquine users after the study by Melles and Marmor describing an increased risk of hydroxychloroquine retinopathy,18 which led to revising the recommendations on screening for antimalarial users from the American Academy of Ophthalmology.19 Our data suggest that factors other than adverse events may influence the optimal utilisation of antimalarials in the SLE patient population, indicating room for improvement in patient management.

Recent guidelines for managing patients with SLE emphasise the need to minimise the use of glucocorticoids for the treatment of SLE, particularly long-term, by using the lowest effective dose of glucocorticoids, tailored to individual needs, for the shortest possible duration and employing a treat-to-target strategy based on continuous disease activity monitoring.3 4 However, our findings indicate that two-thirds of patients under pharmacological treatment for SLE are using glucocorticoids at any given time, with this proportion gradually increasing over the years despite the growing availability of effective steroid-sparing alternatives. This trend appears to be driven by an increased use of glucocorticoid forms other than prednisone. Even so, the number of new glucocorticoid users, including those on prednisone, rose until 2019. We observed some change in patterns with lower glucocorticoids and higher antimalarials in 2020, which we attribute to the COVID-19 pandemic.20 The use of glucocorticoids has been associated with damage accrual in patients with SLE9 11 and SLE damage is associated with mortality.21 Possible explanations for the rising glucocorticoid use include a decreasing use of antimalarials or dosage reductions, which have been associated with flares22 and perhaps the requirement of rescue therapy with glucocorticoids. Additionally, the growing use of rituximab and belimumab infusions, which often require premedication protocols involving glucocorticoids, may contribute to the increasing use of alternative glucocorticoid forms.

As more medications became available for the treatment of patients with SLE, we observed an increase in the number of patients who used 3+medications and a slight decrease in those on monotherapy or under two medications. This is also manifested in the increasing number of new users of leflunomide and belimumab and the increasing number of prevalent users of MMF, leflunomide, tacrolimus, belimumab and rituximab. Although the use of each medication with antimalarials or glucocorticoids was not assessed, observing the increasing use of antimalarials with immunosuppressants and glucocorticoids along with the decreasing use of antimalarials with glucocorticoids might suggest efforts to implement steroid-sparing strategies.

Our study has some limitations. One major constraint is the potential for misclassification due to the reliance on International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes. However, to enhance the specificity of our sample, we required a prescription of a non-glucocorticoid immunosuppressant in addition to the ICD codes.23 The COVID-19 pandemic might have influenced current pharmacological therapies for patients with SLE.20 Our findings are not fully generalisable, as patient populations like Medicaid and uninsured individuals are not included. Additionally, we were unable to analyse the dosing or chronicity of exposure for antimalarials and glucocorticoids. We neither know the clinical status (ie, disease activity or damage) nor its impact on the therapeutic decisions on the included patients. It is possible that average dosages might have decreased over the study period. Although several medications showed changes of statistical significance, their clinical relevance might be marginal.

The study’s strengths include its nationwide scope, ensuring the generalisability of the results across the USA. The timespan evaluated allows us to observe changes in clinical practice due to new therapies or emerging evidence for existing medications. Moreover, by describing the proportion of current medication users and new users, we provide a comprehensive view of the real-world pharmacological therapy patterns in patients with SLE.

Our findings indicate significant opportunities to enhance pharmacological treatment for a majority of patients with SLE in the USA. Approximately 30% of these patients are currently being treated solely with antimalarials and glucocorticoids and nearly 10% of patients with SLE are on glucocorticoids alone. While some patients on these groups might be on remission and using minimal doses of prednisone, those on 5 mg/day or more could potentially benefit from the addition of an immunosuppressant, serving as a steroid-sparing agent to reduce glucocorticoid dependence. Furthermore, over 20% of patients are exclusively receiving glucocorticoids or immunosuppressants. Integrating hydroxychloroquine into their treatment could offer additional benefits.

In conclusion, despite recent advances in therapies available for SLE, a substantial proportion are not receiving antimalarials as part of their treatment. Most patients continue to rely on treatments based on glucocorticoids, and many of those receiving glucocorticoids are not receiving steroid-sparing agents. These findings underscore the need to update the recommendations for managing SLE in the USA. Updating these guidelines is critical to optimise disease control, reduce the risk of poor long-term outcomes and improve patients’ quality of life.
