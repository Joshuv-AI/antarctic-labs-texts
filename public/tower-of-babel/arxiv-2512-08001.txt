Abstract
. In this paper, we study uniqueness problems for entire functions that partially share two values with their higher-order derivatives. The results obtained here both improve and generalize the related results of Li and Yi [ 10 ] , Lü et al. [ 12 ] and Sauer and Schweizer [ 28 ] . Furthermore, we show that our results reveal a deep relationship between the value-sharing behavior of an entire function f f and its k k -th derivative f ( k ) f^{(k)} , and the analytic structure of a particular type of nonlinear differential equation. Several examples are provided to illustrate the necessity of the conditions used in our results.

1.
Introduction

The study of entire functions that share values with their derivatives is a classical and active area in complex analysis, especially within Nevanlinna theory. When an entire function shares two values partially with its general derivatives, the behavior of the function becomes highly constrained. These constraints often lead to strong uniqueness theorems or explicit characterizations of the entire function.

Entire functions that share values with their derivatives arise naturally in the study of differential equations, normal families, and uniqueness theory. Investigating partial sharing (e.g., IM-sharing, CM-sharing, or sharing in the sense of reduced multiplicity) helps broaden classical uniqueness results while capturing more delicate analytical behavior.

Results of this type are especially useful for:

\bullet
establishing the solvability and uniqueness of nonlinear differential equations,

\bullet
understanding fixed points and periodic points of differential operators,

\bullet
studying functional equations involving
f
and
f^{(k)}
,

\bullet
proving normality criteria for families of meromorphic functions.

Let
f(z)
and
g(z)
be two non-constant meromorphic functions and
a
be a finite complex number. If
g(z)-a=0
whenever
f(z)-a=0
, we write
f(z)=a\Rightarrow g(z)=a
. If
f(z)=a\Rightarrow g(z)=a
and
g(z)=a\Rightarrow f(z)=a
, we then write
f(z)=a\Leftrightarrow g(z)=a
and we say that
f(z)
and
g(z)
share
a
IM. If
f(z)-a
and
g(z)-a
have the same zeros with the same multiplicities, we write
f(z)=a\rightleftharpoons g(z)=a
and we say that
f(z)
and
g(z)
share
a
CM.

In 1977, Rubel and Yang
[

f(z)
that shares two values with its first derivative
f^{(1)}(z)

Theorem 1.A
.

[
26

Let
f(z)
be a non-constant entire function and let
a
and
b
be two distinct finite complex numbers. If
f(z)=a\rightleftharpoons f^{(1)}(z)=a
and
f(z)=b\rightleftharpoons f^{(1)}(z)=b

f(z)\equiv f^{(1)}(z)
, i.e.,
f(z)=A\exp(z)
, where
A\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
.

In 1979, Mues and Steinmetz

f(z)=A\exp(z)

The following example demonstrates that the number of shared values in Theorems 1.A and 1.B cannot be reduced to one.

Example 1.A
.

[

Let

\displaystyle f(z)=\exp(\exp(z))\int\limits_{0}^{z}\exp(-\exp(t))\left(1-\exp(t)\right)\;dt.

It is easy to verify that

\displaystyle f^{(1)}(z)-1=\exp(z)(f(z)-1)

and so
f(z)=1\Leftrightarrow f^{(1)}(z)=1
, but
f(z)\not\equiv f^{(1)}(z)

Essentially, Theorems 1.A and 1.B have been generalized in two main directions. The first direction replaces the sharing condition
f(z)=b\rightleftharpoons f^{(1)}(z)=b

In this paper, we refer to the following result, due to Li and Yi
[
10
]
, in which the original sharing condition
f(z)=b\rightleftharpoons f^{(1)}(z)=b
is weakened using the concept of partial value sharing, namely
f^{(1)}(z)=b\Rightarrow f(z)=b

Theorem 1.C
.

[
10
, Theorem 1]
Let
f(z)
be a non-constant entire function and let
a
and
b
be two finite complex numbers such that
b\not=a,0
. If
f(z)=a\rightleftharpoons f^{(1)}(z)=a
and
f^{(1)}(z)=b\Rightarrow f(z)=b
, then
f(z)\equiv f^{(1)}(z)
, i.e.,
f(z)=A\exp(z)
, where
A\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
.

Remark 1.A
.

We observe that
b\not=a
in Theorem 1.C. Therefore, if we assume
b\not=0
in Theorem 1.A, then it is clear that Theorem 1.C generalizes Theorem 1.A through the notion of “partial” sharing of values.

In the same paper, Li and Yi
[

presented the following example to demonstrate the necessity of the condition “
b\not=0

Example 1.B
.

[

Let

\displaystyle f(z)=c\exp\left(\frac{a}{c}z\right)+a-c,

where

f^{(1)}(z)\not=0

f(z)=a\rightleftharpoons f^{(1)}(z)=a

f^{(1)}(z)=0\Rightarrow f(z)=0

f(z)\not\equiv f^{(1)}(z)

It would be a remarkable achievement to show that the conclusion of Theorem 1.C remains valid when the hypothesis

.

[

f(z)=a\Leftrightarrow f^{(1)}(z)=a

f^{(1)}(z)=b\Rightarrow f(z)=b

f(z)\equiv f^{(1)}(z)
, i.e.,
f(z)=A\exp(z)

[

At the end of their paper, Lü et al.
[

Question 1.A
.

What will happen if “

Question 1.B

What will happen if the hypothesis “
f(z)=a\Leftrightarrow f^{(1)}(z)=a
” is replaced by “
f(z)=a\Rightarrow f^{(1)}(z)=a
” in Theorem 1.D?

In 2024, Sauer and Schweizer
[
28
]
considered
Question 1.B
and resolved it completely using techniques from normal family theory and number theory. We recall their results below.

Theorem 1.E
.

[
28
, Theorem 1.1]
Let
f(z)
be a non-constant entire function and let
a
and
b
be two non-zero finite complex numbers such that
b\not=a
. If
f(z)=a\Rightarrow f^{(1)}(z)=a
and
f^{(1)}(z)=b\Rightarrow f(z)=b
, then
f(z)
is one of the following:

(1)

f(z)=az+C
, where
C
is any constant. These are all non-constant polynomial solutions,

(2)

f(z)\equiv f^{(1)}(z)
, i.e.,
f(z)=C\exp(z)
, where
C
is any non-zero constant,

(3)

f(z)=C\exp\left(\frac{b}{b-a}z\right)+a
, where
C
is a non-zero constant. In this case
f(z)
and
f^{(1)}(z)
do even share the value
b
. Moreover,
a
is a Picard value of
f(z)
,

(4)

f(z)=6aC\exp\left(\frac{1}{6}z\right)\left(C\exp\left(\frac{1}{6}z\right)-1\right)+a
, where
b=-\frac{a}{8}
and
C
is a non-zero constant.

Let
f(z)
and
g(z)
be two non-constant meromorphic functions and
a
be a finite complex number. Let
z_{n}
be zeros of
f(z)-a
with multiplicity
h(n)
. If
z_{n}
are also zeros of
g(z)-a
of multiplicity
h(n)
at least, then we write
f(z)=a\mapsto g(z)=a
.
Clearly
f(z)=a\mapsto g(z)=a
always implies that
f(z)=a\Rightarrow g(z)=a
, but not conversely. But if
f(z)=a\Rightarrow f^{(1)}(z)=a
holds, then
f(z)=a\mapsto f^{(1)}(z)=a
always holds.

Throughout the paper, we denote by
\mu(f)
,
\rho(f)
and
\lambda(f)
the lower order of
f
, the order of
f
and the exponent of convergence of zeros of
f
respectively.
If
\mu(f)=\rho(f)
, we say that
f
is of regular growth. Also if

\displaystyle\limsup\limits_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log^{+}N\left(r,a;f\right)}{\log r}<\rho(f)

for
\rho(f)>0
, then
a\in\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
is said to be a Borel exceptional value of
f
. Obviously when
\rho>0
, Picard exceptional values are Borel’s.

2.
Main results

One of the main objectives of this paper is to provide an affirmative answer to
Question 1.A
. It is also natural to ask whether the conclusions of Theorems 1.C-1.E remain valid when the first derivative
f^{(1)}(z)
in these theorems is replaced by the higher-order derivative
f^{(k)}(z)
. In this paper, we address this question by establishing the following results.

2.1.
When
f(z)=a\Leftrightarrow f^{(k)}(z)=a
and
f^{(k)}(z)=b\Rightarrow f(z)=b

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
f(z)
be a non-constant entire function such that all the zeros of
f(z)-a
have multiplicity at least
k
, where
k
is a positive integer and
a
is a non-zero constant. If
f(z)=a\mapsto f^{(k)}(z)=a
,
f^{(k)}(z)=a\Rightarrow f(z)=a
and
f^{(k)}(z)=b\Rightarrow f(z)=b
, where
b(\neq 0,a)
is a constant, then only one of the following cases holds:

(1)

k=1
and
f(z)=A\exp(z)
, where
A
is a non-zero constant,

(2)

k=2
and
f(z)=c_{0}\exp(z)+c_{1}\exp(-z)
, where
c_{0}
and
c_{1}
are non-zero constants such that
c_{0}c_{1}=\frac{a^{2}}{4}
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
f(z)
be a non-constant entire function such that all the zeros of
f(z)-a
have multiplicity at least
k
, where
k
is a positive integer and
a
is a non-zero constant. If
f(z)=a\rightleftharpoons f^{(k)}(z)=a
and
f^{(k)}(z)=0\Rightarrow f(z)=0
, then only one of the following cases holds:

(1)

k=1
and
f(z)=A\exp(z)
, where
A
is a non-zero constant,

(2)

k=2
and
f(z)=c_{0}\exp(z)+c_{1}\exp(-z)
, where
c_{0}
and
c_{1}
are non-zero constants such that
c_{0}c_{1}=\frac{a^{2}}{4}
,

(3)

f(z)=\frac{a(\beta-1)}{\beta}+\frac{C}{\beta}\exp(\lambda z)
,
where
C
,
\beta(\neq 1)
and
\lambda
are non-zero constants such that
\lambda^{k}=\beta
.

We now make the following observations regarding Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
.

(i)

We have improved as well as generalized Theorems 1.C and 1.D by considering the general derivative
f^{(k)}
instead of only the first derivative
f^{(1)}
. Moreover, we explore all possible forms of the function
f
.

(ii)

We have completely solved
Question 1.A
in Theorem
2.2
in the case where
f
and
f^{(k)}
share
a
CM.

(iii)

The condition that all the zeros of
f(z)-a
have multiplicity at least
k
is necessary in Theorem
2.1

\displaystyle f(z)=\exp(z)+\exp(-z),

where
a=4
,
b=1
and

. Note that all the zeros of
f(z)-a
are simple and
c_{0}c_{1}=1\neq\frac{a^{2}}{4}=4
. Obviously
f(z)=a\mapsto f^{(k)}(z)=a
,
f^{(k)}(z)=a\Rightarrow f(z)=a
and
f^{(k)}(z)=b\Rightarrow f(z)=b
.
However,
f(z)
does not satisfy any of the cases of Theorem
2.1

(iv)

Suppose
k=1
. If
f(z)=a\mapsto f^{(k)}(z)=a
, then the function
f(z)-a
has only simple zeros.
Hence, the sharing conditions
f(z)=a\mapsto f^{(k)}(z)=a
and
f^{(k)}(z)=a\Rightarrow f(z)=a

f(z)=a\Leftrightarrow f^{(z)}(z)=a
. Consequently, Theorem 1.D follows as a direct corollary of Theorem
2.1
.

2.2.
When
f(z)=a\Rightarrow f^{(k)}(z)=a

f^{(k)}(z)=b\Rightarrow f(z)=b

is a constant, then only one of the following cases holds:

(1)

k=1

(2)

(ii)

3.
Auxiliary lemmas

Let
f(z)
be a meromorphic function in a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
. Then the derivative of
f(z)
at
z_{0}\in\Omega
in the spherical metric, called the spherical derivative, is denoted by
f^{\#}(z_{0})
, where

\displaystyle f^{\#}(z_{0})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{clcr}&\frac{|f^{(1)}(z_{0})|}{1+|f(z_{0})|^{2}},&\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\text{if}\;\;z_{0}\in\Omega\;\text{is\;not\;a\;pole}\\
&\lim\limits_{z\rightarrow z_{0}}\frac{|f^{(1)}(z)|}{1+|f(z)|^{2}},&\;\;\text{if}\;\;z_{0}\in\Omega\;\text{is\;a\;pole}.\end{array}\right.

A family
\mathcal{F}
of functions meromorphic in domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
is said to be normal in
\Omega
if every sequence
\{f_{n}\}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
contains a subsequence which converges spherically uniformly on compact subsets of
\Omega
(see
[
27
]
).

It is assumed that the reader is familiar with the following well known Marty Criterion.

Lemma 3.1
.

[
27
]
A family
\mathcal{F}
of meromorphic functions on a domain
\Omega
is normal if and only if for each compact subset
K\subseteq\Omega
, there exists
M\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that
f^{\#}(z)\leq M

\forall

f\in\mathcal{F}
and
z\in K
.

Now we recall the following well known Zalcman’s lemma.

Lemma 3.2

[
33

f(z)=a\Rightarrow f^{(k)}(z)=a

(3)

(i)

points

(ii)

positive numbers
\rho_{n}
,
\rho_{n}\rightarrow 0

(iii)

functions

such that

\displaystyle F_{n}(\zeta)=f_{n}(z_{n}+\rho_{n}\zeta)\rightarrow F(\zeta)

(3.2)

spherically uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}
, where

is a non-constant entire function such that

. Since

,
\forall

\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
, by Lemma

, we have
\rho(F)\leq 1
. Since

have multiplicity at least

would be a polynomial of degree less than

\displaystyle F_{n}^{(k)}(\zeta)=\rho_{n}^{k}f_{n}^{(k)}(z_{n}+\rho_{n}\zeta)\rightarrow F^{(k)}(\zeta)

(3.3)

spherically uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}
.

First we prove that
F(\zeta)=a\Rightarrow F^{(k)}(\zeta)=0

F(\zeta_{0})=a
. Then by (

) and Hurwitz’s theorem there exists a sequence

Next we prove that

in
\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle F_{n}^{(k)}(\zeta)=\rho_{n}^{k}\left\{f_{n}^{(k)}(z_{n}+\rho_{n}\zeta)-b\right\}\rightarrow F^{(k)}(\zeta)

(3.4)

spherically uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}
. Let

. Now by (
3.4
) and Hurwitz’s theorem there exists a sequence
\{\zeta_{n}\}
such that
\zeta_{n}\rightarrow\zeta_{0}

and so from (

\displaystyle F(\zeta_{0})=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}F_{n}(\zeta_{n})=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}f_{n}(z_{n}+\rho_{n}\zeta_{n})=b.

This shows that
F^{(k)}(\zeta)=0\Rightarrow F(\zeta)=b

F^{(k)}(\zeta)=0\Rightarrow F(\zeta)=b

is normal at

\mathcal{F}
is normal in
\Omega
.

5.
Some applications

Solving nonlinear differential equations remains one of the most challenging and intellectually stimulating problems in mathematical analysis. In this section, we consider the following non-linear differential equation

\displaystyle f^{(k+1)}(z)\left(f^{(k)}(z)-f(z)\right)=\varphi(z)(f(z)-a)\left(f^{(k)}(z)-b\right).

(5.1)

where
\varphi(z)
is an entire function,
a(\neq 0)
and
b
are two finite complex numbers.

The general solvability of (
5.1
) is extremely delicate. The equation is nonlinear, involves mixed products of derivatives, and includes an arbitrary entire coefficient function
\varphi(z)
. With no growth restrictions on
\varphi
, it is usually very difficult-even impossible-to explicitly determine whether non-constant entire solutions exist.

Theorems
2.1
-
2.5
indicate a deep relationship between the value-sharing behavior of an entire function
f
and its

k
-th derivative
f^{(k)}
and the analytic structure of the nonlinear differential Eq. (
5.1
). In particular, the assumption that
f
and
f^{(k)}
share two distinct values,
a
and
b
, forces strong functional constraints that can be encoded in equation (
5.1
). In this sense, (
5.1
) serves as a bridge between Nevanlinna-type value-distribution properties and differential equations of higher order.
Equation (
5.1
) is in general highly non-linear and difficult to analyze for arbitrary entire
\varphi
. However, in certain special cases, Eq. (
5.1
) can be solved completely. Now we state our results.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\varphi(z)
be an entire function,
k
be a positive integer and let
a
and
b
be two non-zero constants such that
a\neq b
. Let
f(z)
be a meromorphic solution of the differential equation (
5.1
) such that either
f(z)-a
has no zeros or all the zeros of
f(z)-a
have multiplicity at least
k
and
f(z)=a\mapsto f^{(k)}(z)=a
.
Then the conclusions of Theorem
2.3
hold.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\varphi(z)
be an entire function,
k
be a positive integer and let
a
be a non-zero constant. Let
f
be a meromorphic solution of the following equation

\displaystyle f^{(k+1)}(z)\left(f^{(k)}(z)-f(z)\right)=\varphi(z)(f(z)-a)f^{(k)}(z)

(5.2)

such that either
f(z)
has no zeros or all the zeros of
f(z)
have multiplicity at least
k
,
f(z)=a\Rightarrow f^{(k)}(z)=a
and
\lambda(f)<\rho(f)
. Then
f(z)=A\exp(\lambda z)
, where
A
and
\lambda
are non-zero constants such that
