A data-driven framework for assessing structural heterogeneity and nonlinear performance in university sports governance
Authors: Xin Hui, Yuan Yao, Junxian Liu, Yuxin Yan, Wenhan Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUniversity sports governance is a complex system constrained by multiple competing objectives, including resource allocation, participation equity, operational efficiency, and sustainable development. Traditional evaluation paradigms, which rely heavily on linear assumptions and weighted aggregation, are fundamentally inadequate for capturing the structural heterogeneity and nonlinear dynamics inherent in these organizations. To address this limitation, this study proposes a data-driven computational framework for heterogeneity assessment and performance mapping. Using Monte Carlo simulation, we generated 348 synthetic institutional samples and applied K-means and Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM) to assess whether the proposed pipeline can recover four pre-specified governance archetypes: resource-oriented, equity-prioritized, efficiency-driven, and balanced. Simulation results confirm the framework’s technical identifiability and internal consistency under controlled conditions. Importantly, the configurations, performance patterns, and governance insights reported here reflect properties of the simulation design rather than empirically validated regularities in real university sports governance systems. Introduction

At a global level, the modernization of governance in higher education systems has become a core issue in enhancing national competitiveness. As an indispensable component of the higher education talent cultivation system, sports governance directly affects students’ physical and mental health and the implementation of national strategies aimed at building a “strong sporting nation”1. With the global shift in governance philosophy from traditional “administrative management” toward “collaborative governance” and “multidimensional performance evaluation,” university sports governance has evolved beyond the achievement of singular instructional objectives. It has increasingly become a complex system encompassing the efficiency of resource allocation, equity of social participation, optimization of operational processes, and long-term strategic sustainability1,2. In this broader context, governments and educational institutions worldwide have gradually recognized that conventional top-down and homogeneous management models are no longer capable of addressing the growing tension between increasingly diversified sports demands and constrained resources3. Particularly under the dual pressures of digital transformation and refined social governance, how to identify deep-seated contradictions within governance processes through scientific evaluation methods and thereby drive systemic institutional optimization has become a focal issue for both academia and policymakers4.

From the perspective of China’s domestic practice, university sports governance is currently at a critical juncture of deepening reform. In recent years, policy documents such as the Opinions on Comprehensively Strengthening and Improving School Sports Work in the New Era issued in China in 2020 have explicitly required universities to construct an education system that fosters students’ moral, intellectual, physical, aesthetic, and labor development in an integrated manner, placing sports in a more prominent position5. This policy orientation has imposed unprecedented “multi-objective constraints” on university sports governance. On the one hand, universities are required to achieve optimal allocation of sports venues and facilities under limited financial resources; on the other hand, they must ensure equitable access to sports resources for students of different genders, physical conditions, and disadvantaged groups6. In addition, improvements in operational efficiency and the long-term sustainability of governance systems have also become core indicators of governance modernization. However, the coexistence of these multiple objectives does not represent a simple linear aggregation; rather, it involves complex conflicts and trade-offs7. For example, excessive emphasis on operational efficiency may crowd out low-yield but high-cost mass sports activities, thereby undermining participation equity. Conversely, a singular pursuit of expanded resource investment, without adequate institutional governance logic, often leads to an “investment trap,” making it difficult to achieve substantive performance improvements8.

Despite these challenges, a critical yet long-overlooked issue has gradually emerged in both governance practice and policy implementation—namely, the “structural heterogeneity” of university sports governance performance. Most existing evaluation frameworks tend to treat universities as a homogeneous whole, attempting to identify a universally applicable “best practice” model9. In reality, however, differences in historical accumulation, institutional hierarchy, geographic location, and governance logic lead universities to adopt fundamentally distinct configurations when addressing multi-objective conflicts in sports governance. Such heterogeneity implies that identical governance inputs may generate entirely nonlinear performance feedback across different types of universities. When this structural divergence is ignored, evaluation outcomes often obscure deeper governance mechanisms, resulting in superficial policy recommendations that fail to provide precise and actionable levers for specific university types10. The tension between the “homogenization of evaluation” and the “heterogeneity of reality” has thus become a major bottleneck constraining the advancement of university sports governance toward greater refinement and scientific rigor.

Over the past several decades, scholars have explored university sports governance and performance evaluation from multiple perspectives. Existing studies can generally be categorized into three main streams. The first focuses on the construction of governance indicator systems. Drawing on theories such as New Public Management, stakeholder theory, and social justice theory, numerous studies have proposed evaluation models encompassing inputs, outputs, and outcomes, thereby providing a quantitative foundation for assessing sports governance performance11. The second stream involves empirical analyses of influencing factors. Using methods such as linear regression and structural equation modeling, researchers have examined the driving effects of organizational structure, leadership support, and financial investment on sports performance12. The third stream concerns the measurement of governance efficiency, with some studies introducing tools such as Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) and Stochastic Frontier Analysis (SFA) to identify relative governance efficiency within an input–output framework13. Collectively, these studies have generated valuable insights into university sports governance and laid an important theoretical foundation for further investigation.

A closer examination of the existing literature, however, reveals several significant limitations in addressing structural heterogeneity under complex governance environments. First, most evaluation studies rely on a “weighted aggregation” approach, compressing multiple dimensions—such as resources, equity, efficiency, and sustainability—into a single composite score. While this approach facilitates ranking, it eliminates the inherent tensions among objectives and fails to capture universities’ trade-off logics across governance dimensions14. Second, existing models are predominantly based on linear or quasi-linear assumptions, overlooking potential threshold effects, saturation effects, and complex interactions between governance inputs and performance outputs. In practice, performance improvements often do not increase linearly with investment, but instead exhibit specific “trigger points” or “points of diminishing marginal returns”15. Third, there is a lack of in-depth characterization of governance configurations. Current studies often address heterogeneity through exogenous, a priori classifications (e.g., research-oriented vs. application-oriented universities), which are insufficient to capture latent structural features that are data-driven and endogenous to governance logic16,17.

Against this backdrop, this study proposes a novel framework for assessing the structural heterogeneity of university sports governance performance under multi-objective constraints. Rather than prespecifying a unified governance equation, the core logic of this study lies in identifying latent governance configurations with intrinsic differences within the university population through data-driven methods. Specifically, this research seeks to address the following questions: under multi-objective constraints, how can the structural heterogeneity of university sports governance be scientifically defined and identified? What kinds of nonlinear mapping relationships exist between governance inputs and performance outputs across different configurations? Furthermore, is it possible to extract actionable governance levers and critical performance thresholds from these black-box nonlinear relationships using advanced interpretability tools? This study not only focuses on whether governance performs well, but also on under which configurations and through which pathways better governance outcomes can be achieved, thereby elevating evaluation research from descriptive analysis to diagnostic and predictive analysis18.

The potential contributions of this study are threefold. At the theoretical level, it challenges the traditional “universality” logic in governance evaluation by introducing a structural heterogeneity perspective, thereby deepening the understanding of the internal logic of sports governance modernization and offering a new explanatory framework for complex governance systems under multi-objective constraints. At the methodological level, this study constructs an integrated analytical pipeline encompassing indicator vectorization, configuration identification, nonlinear mapping estimation, and interpretability decomposition. By combining clustering algorithms (e.g., K-means and GMM), nonlinear machine learning models (e.g., XGBoost and MLP), and game-theoretic interpretability tools (Shapley values), the proposed approach overcomes the limitations of traditional statistical models in handling high-dimensional interactions and nonlinear relationships, and demonstrates through Monte Carlo simulation validation that the pipeline can successfully recover pre-specified governance structures. At the practical level, the framework offers a candidate analytical tool for future empirical governance research; however, the policy relevance of specific findings—such as identified thresholds and feature importance rankings—should be interpreted as hypothetical implications grounded in the simulation design, pending validation with real institutional data.

Literature review

Theoretical evolution of university sports governance and the multi-dimensional objective system

Research on university sports governance originated from reflections on traditional administrative management models, with its theoretical core undergoing a paradigm shift from “single-purpose sports instruction management” toward “complex organizational collaborative governance”19. In early strands of the literature, university sports were generally regarded as a subsidiary component of the educational function, and governance logic primarily revolved around curriculum completion and the maintenance of sports equipment, exhibiting strong bureaucratic and administrative characteristics. However, with the introduction of governance modernization theories into the field of higher education, related studies began to emphasize power reconfiguration, stakeholder participation, and the diversification of governance objectives20. Existing theoretical explorations suggest that university sports governance is no longer a purely internal institutional matter, but rather a complex game involving multiple actors, including governments, universities, markets, social organizations, and individual students21. From this perspective, governance objectives are decomposed into multiple dimensions that are both conflicting and complementary.

Regarding the classification of these objectives, most studies converge on four core pillars: resource allocation, participation equity, operational efficiency, and sustainable development. In the dimension of resource allocation, the literature emphasizes the importance of sports funding and facility infrastructure as the physical foundation of governance, and highlights the responsiveness of resource distribution as a key indicator of governance quality22. In terms of participation equity, scholars have increasingly recognized that university sports should not merely serve elite athletic performance, but should function as a form of public service that guarantees all students’ rights to participate in physical activity. By examining gender disparities, access for students with disabilities, and participation rates across different academic groups, related studies reveal the social justice attributes embedded in governance processes23,24. In the dimension of operational efficiency, influenced by the New Public Management paradigm, a substantial body of research has focused on facility utilization rates, per capita service costs, and organizational agility, seeking to maximize outputs under resource constraints25.

Notably, recent studies have increasingly emphasized governance sustainability, encompassing institutionalization, long-term strategic planning, and the adaptability of governance models to environmental and policy changes26. This coexistence of multiple objectives is theoretically conceptualized as the “intertwined logic of governance goals.” Emerging research indicates that these objectives are not simply parallel, but are characterized by profound tensions. For instance, the pursuit of extreme operational efficiency often comes at the expense of resource allocation toward disadvantaged groups, thereby undermining equity; conversely, excessive emphasis on expanding resource investment, in the absence of sustainable institutional support, may lead to idle resources and administrative redundancy27. Such nonlinear coupling among objectives constitutes the theoretical root of complexity in university sports governance and provides a logical foundation for the existence of structural heterogeneity.

Paradigm shifts and methodological limitations in university sports governance performance evaluation

With the diversification of governance objectives, methods for evaluating university sports governance performance have evolved from qualitative descriptions to quantitative measurement, and from single-dimensional assessments to composite evaluations. Early evaluation systems were largely compliance-oriented, relying on expert scoring or administrative inspections to qualitatively assess the standardization of university sports activities28. Although this approach initially facilitated the standardization of sports governance, its strong subjectivity and lack of dynamic feedback limited its ability to capture micro-level efficiency issues within governance processes. Subsequently, evaluation paradigms based on key performance indicators (KPIs) gradually became dominant. Researchers began to construct indicator systems encompassing inputs, outputs, and outcomes, attempting to derive a quantitative “composite score” through weighted aggregation29.

Despite their widespread application in practice, indicator-based approaches have revealed significant limitations. On the one hand, the determination of indicator weights often relies on methods such as the Delphi technique or the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP). While these methods incorporate expert judgment, the imposition of uniform weights under conditions of multi-objective conflict tends to obscure the distinctive characteristics of different types of universities, resulting in the bias of “measuring all with the same ruler”30. On the other hand, traditional weighted aggregation logic implicitly assumes linear substitutability among governance dimensions. In reality, however, deficiencies in one dimension (e.g., institutional collapse) cannot necessarily be compensated for by redundancy in another (e.g., abundant funding)31.

To address these shortcomings, some scholars have introduced efficiency evaluation tools such as Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) and Stochastic Frontier Analysis (SFA). These methods conceptualize university sports governance as an “input–output” system and evaluate performance by measuring the distance to a relative efficiency frontier6,32. While advantageous in identifying operational efficiency, such approaches often treat governance processes as a “black box,” making it difficult to explain the specific institutional causes of inefficiency, and they perform poorly when confronted with discontinuous and nonlinear governance logics33. Moreover, most existing evaluation methods emphasize static cross-sectional comparisons of outcomes, neglecting structural differences driven by distinct governance logics. When evaluation models attempt to fit all samples simultaneously, heterogeneity-induced residuals often contain critical governance patterns, yet are dismissed as noise by conventional statistical methods. This excessive reliance on “average effects” limits the ability of existing approaches to identify critical thresholds and leverage points under specific governance configurations, thereby constraining the policy relevance of evaluation results34. This challenge is not unique to governance contexts: in performance-driven software development, analogous limitations have motivated the adoption of incremental refinement approaches that decompose complex performance objectives into progressively refined, configuration-specific criteria, substantially improving the actionability of evaluation outcomes35. This cross-domain parallel reinforces the case for governance evaluation frameworks that move beyond aggregate scoring toward granular, configuration-aware performance diagnosis.

Structural heterogeneity and nonlinear logic in organizational governance

From the perspective of complex organizational theory, governance performance does not arise from the isolated effects of individual elements, but from the organic combination of multiple governance factors operating under specific logics36. The introduction of the concept of structural heterogeneity seeks to dismantle the traditional illusion of “best practices.” Related studies argue that organizations evolve distinct governance configurations due to differences in historical path dependence, resource endowments, and strategic positioning. Such configurations are not merely accumulations of elements, but represent deep structural couplings37. In organizational sociology, this heterogeneity is regarded as an endogenous state of stability, implying that different universities will spontaneously form differentiated governance priorities when facing multi-objective constraints. For example, some universities may develop governance logics centered on “resource input–driven” approaches, while others may gravitate toward models emphasizing “efficiency–institutional synergy.” These structural differences determine that the contribution rates of governance factors to performance are not universally applicable across organizations38.

Closely associated with structural heterogeneity is the prevalence of nonlinear logic in governance processes. Existing research has increasingly recognized that the relationship between governance inputs and performance outputs often exhibits diminishing marginal returns, threshold effects, or inverted U-shaped patterns10. Studies examining the relationship between resource allocation and operational efficiency have found that when resource input remains below a critical threshold, efficiency improvements are minimal; once this threshold is crossed, performance may increase explosively, implying the existence of governance leverage points. In addition, interactions among governance factors are also highly nonlinear. For instance, improvements in participation equity, in the absence of institutional safeguards, may temporarily reduce operational efficiency and generate negative impacts; however, within highly institutionalized governance environments, equity can enhance faculty and student identification with governance systems, thereby feeding back positively into long-term performance39.

Despite the broad theoretical recognition of nonlinear logic, empirical studies capable of effectively capturing and quantifying these complex relationships in the context of university sports governance remain relatively scarce. Most existing research remains at the preliminary level of describing “positive” or “negative” correlations, and struggles to precisely delineate the dynamic mapping trajectories among variables40. As a result, policymakers are unable to determine the levels of input at which optimal returns can be achieved, nor can they identify the heterogeneous mechanisms through which governance variables operate across different configuration modes. Consequently, the introduction of more expressive nonlinear models, combined with a configuration-based perspective for in-depth mapping estimation, is not merely a methodological innovation, but an essential requirement for restoring the logic of governance reality. Parallel evidence from dynamic systems research supports this view: multi-objective reconfiguration approaches employing energy-based optimization have demonstrated that managing complex, evolving environments under competing constraints requires algorithmic frameworks capable of simultaneously handling multiple performance dimensions rather than collapsing them into a scalar objective41. This principle directly informs the present study’s insistence on vectorized performance representation and configuration-specific nonlinear estimation as core design choices.

Research limitations

In summary, academic circles both domestically and internationally have achieved substantial progress in establishing objectives, evaluating performance, and measuring efficiency within university sports governance, constructing a relatively comprehensive knowledge framework. However, a systematic review of existing literature reveals three critical research gaps that require urgent attention. First, a significant tension exists between the homogenization of evaluation logic and the diversification of actual governance models. Most existing studies focus on developing a “unified explanatory equation” applicable to all universities, overlooking the fundamental differences in governance logic across varying structural configurations. This approach often traps evaluation conclusions in the “average value trap,” making it difficult to provide precise guidance for universities at different stages of governance development42.

Second, the “forced synthesis” of evaluation dimensions obscures the conflicts and trade-offs among multiple objectives. Simply weighting resources, equity, efficiency, and sustainability into a single composite score may satisfy administrative demands for ranking and comparison but deviates from the multidimensional essence of governance modernization. This approach obscures universities’ trade-off strategies among different objectives and fails to reflect internal tensions within governance systems43. Finally, methodological limitations constrain in-depth exploration of governance patterns. Traditional linear econometric models struggle with high-dimensional features, complex interactions, and nonlinear thresholds, while emerging machine learning tools, despite their powerful fitting capabilities, still face the “black box” challenge in explaining governance causal mechanisms and providing actionable policy recommendations44,45. Recent advances in knowledge-enhanced refinement for quality engineering—where reinforcement learning is leveraged to integrate domain knowledge into search-based optimization—suggest that combining data-driven learning with structured domain knowledge can substantially mitigate black-box opacity and improve the interpretability of complex model outputs46. This approach offers a promising methodological reference point for developing interpretable governance analytics that balance predictive power with causal transparency.

Based on this, this study focuses on: utilizing “indicator vectorization” to preserve the complete information of governance objectives, employing “configuration identification” to characterize the structural heterogeneity of university sports governance, and introducing “nonlinear mapping and interpretability decomposition” to unravel the governance logic under different configurations. This study does not pursue a unified evaluation standard but seeks to identify structural differences and uncover unique governance levers and performance thresholds for different types of universities. This shift from a “horizontal ranking” to a “vertical diagnosis” perspective not only provides a more scientific assessment framework for university sports governance but also offers a replicable methodological demonstration for modernizing educational governance. Through rigorous validation of simulated data and meticulous derivation of empirical logic, this paper strives to balance theoretical depth with practical precision, thereby addressing the contemporary challenge of evaluating complex governance systems under multi-objective constraints.

Configuration identification

Configuration assumptions and deterministic clustering methods

This study assumes that there exist K latent governance configurations (governance modes) within the sample, where each configuration k represents a group of universities sharing similar governance logics and performance orientations. This assumption is formalized by introducing a latent variable , which partitions the sample into clusters .

To ensure determinacy and interpretability in classification, three clustering algorithms are employed and quantitatively compared to select the final solution: K-means clustering, Ward’s hierarchical clustering, and Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) soft clustering. The rationale for this combination is as follows. K-means is selected as the primary baseline algorithm because of its well-understood geometric properties, computational efficiency on moderate sample sizes (N ≈ 300–400), and its direct optimisation of the within-cluster sum of squares objective (Eq. 7), which produces crisp, interpretable partition boundaries. However, K-means assumes spherical, equally sized clusters and assigns each observation to exactly one cluster, which may misclassify boundary institutions whose governance profiles overlap across archetypes. GMM is therefore included as a complementary probabilistic model: by estimating full or diagonal Gaussian component covariances, GMM assigns each observation a soft membership probability for each configuration k, enabling the identification of boundary members and the quantification of classification uncertainty. The combination of K-means (hard assignment) and GMM (soft assignment) thus provides a cross-validation mechanism: high agreement between the two algorithms’ hard-assignment outputs (measured by ARI and NMI) constitutes evidence of a stable, well-separated cluster structure, whereas systematic disagreement flags configurations that require further scrutiny. Ward’s hierarchical clustering is additionally employed to examine the merging sequence among configurations and to provide an independent, distance-based perspective on inter-configuration relationships without requiring a pre-specified K. It is recognized that standard clustering algorithms may face reduced discriminative power when applied to high-dimensional indicator sets. In future empirical applications, a prior feature selection step is recommended to identify the subset of indicators that maximally contributes to inter-configuration separation. Specifically, the MCMR-Score criterion, which optimizes feature subsets by jointly evaluating multiple cluster validity indices and has been shown to improve cluster structural integrity in high-dimensional settings49, provides a principled approach to this selection problem. In the present simulation study, all J = 23 indicators are retained to assess the full pipeline under the complete indicator set as specified in the theoretical framework. For model selection, three classes of models are deterministically adopted for estimation and comparison: Gradient Boosting Trees (XGBoost), Random Forests, and Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) neural networks. The selection of this specific set is motivated by the following four criteria, which collectively constitute the model selection rationale for the pipeline.

First, functional expressiveness under small : because each configuration sub-sample contains only observations, candidate models must balance expressive capacity against overfitting risk. Tree-based ensemble methods (XGBoost and Random Forest) have well-documented advantages on small-to-moderate tabular datasets relative to deep neural architectures, because their regularisation mechanisms (subsampling, column sampling, leaf-weight penalty for XGBoost; random feature selection for Random Forest) directly constrain model complexity in proportion to available data. MLP is included as the highest-capacity comparator to assess whether additional representational flexibility yields improved recovery, and to ensure the comparison spans the gradient from interpretable ensemble methods to flexible neural architectures.

Second, compatibility with TreeSHAP: a core design requirement of the interpretability pipeline is that the mapping model admits exact Shapley value computation via TreeSHAP in polynomial time . XGBoost and Random Forest satisfy this requirement natively; MLP does not, and therefore relies on the approximate DeepSHAP method. This asymmetry in interpretability quality is explicitly accounted for in the Shapley robustness analysis in Section “Implementation and interpretation protocols for Shapley value decomposition”.

Third, the three-model comparison itself constitutes an ablation study of model complexity: XGBoost (sequential ensemble with gradient correction) vs. Random Forest (parallel ensemble without boosting) isolates the contribution of boosting; Random Forest vs. MLP isolates the contribution of architectural flexibility. The final model for each task is selected by held-out five-fold CV , providing data-driven model selection evidence rather than a priori assumption of a single best algorithm.

Explainability, simulation validation, and robustness tests

Implementation and interpretation protocols for Shapley value decomposition

To enable transparent inspection of the machine learning models’ internal logic and assess the internal consistency of the interpretability component of the pipeline, this study adopts Shapley value decomposition to provide both local and global explanations for single-objective models. It is important to note that in the context of a simulation study, Shapley-based explanations characterize how the fitted model redistributes credit among features according to the DGP’s structure; they do not provide evidence about causal governance mechanisms in real institutions. The governance insights derived from Shapley analysis—including identified critical thresholds and governance lever rankings—should therefore be treated as simulation-derived hypotheses pending empirical validation, rather than as actionable policy-relevant conclusions based on real institutional data. For tree-based models (XGBoost and Random Forest), TreeSHAP is employed. TreeSHAP computes exact Shapley values—satisfying efficiency, symmetry, dummy, and additivity axioms—by exploiting the recursive tree structure to enumerate feature contributions in polynomial time , where T denotes the number of trees, L the number of leaves, and D the tree depth. This avoids the exponential cost of brute-force exact computation (J ≈ 25 in the present study) without introducing approximation error. In the presence of nonlinear interactions among indicators, TreeSHAP handles feature interactions through the SHAP interaction value decomposition , which partitions the total Shapley value of each feature into a main effect and pairwise interaction terms with every other feature. This allows the marginal contribution of feature j to be correctly attributed even when its effect on is modulated by another feature via the interaction terms in Eq. (16). For MLPs, the approximate DeepSHAP method is applied, which propagates SHAP values backward through the network layers using a linearisation of the activation functions; this constitutes an approximation rather than an exact computation, and is applied only to the MLP models that were not selected as the final estimator in any of the 14 gradient boosting–winning tasks.

Regarding feature dependence, a key limitation of marginal (interventional) Shapley values is that, when features are correlated, the marginal integration procedure assigns credit by sampling feature values independently of one another, thereby generating out-of-distribution combinations that do not occur in the observed data. Under high intra-dimensional correlation, this can produce dependency bias: a feature whose effect is genuinely mediated by a correlated neighbor may receive inflated or deflated marginal attribution, because the marginalization implicitly treats correlated features as independent when computing counterfactual contributions. This bias does not invalidate the efficiency or additivity axioms of Shapley values, but it can distort the relative ranking of correlated features and overstate the apparent contribution of features that are highly correlated with the true driver. To mitigate this, the present study reports both marginal Shapley values—computed by integrating over the empirical marginal distribution of each feature—and conditional Shapley values, which condition on the observed joint distribution and thereby respect the intra-dimensional correlation structure of the data. The rationale for this dual-computation strategy is that conditional Shapley values eliminate the out-of-distribution sampling problem at the cost of higher computational complexity, while marginal values provide a computationally tractable baseline; comparing the two via Spearman rank correlation thus allows the analyst to diagnose whether dependency bias is materially altering the interpretation for any given task. If for any task, the conditional ranking is adopted as the primary attribution, as this threshold signals a non-trivial divergence in which dependency bias may be distorting feature importance conclusions. In the present simulation, the intra-block correlation among same-dimension indicators creates a substantive risk of dependency bias for highly correlated indicator pairs within the same governance dimension, and this risk is explicitly acknowledged in the dependence plots for all tasks where such pairs appear among the top-ranked features.

Theory-driven simulation data generation and method validation procedure

To assess the identifiability of the proposed pipeline under controlled conditions, this study specifies a fully reproducible simulation validation procedure. It is important to note that this procedure constitutes a methodological sanity check rather than an empirical discovery: by pre-specifying four governance archetypes with known parameters, we test whether the analytical pipeline can recover the injected structure. A successful recovery demonstrates that the method is internally consistent and capable of distinguishing configurations when they exist with the assumed separation properties, but it does not provide evidence that such configurations exist in real university sports governance systems50.

Four theoretically grounded archetypes are specified. The mean vectors for each archetype were calibrated through a structured three-step process grounded in the existing literature reviewed in Section “Literature review”. First, we identified the dominant governance orientation associated with each recognisable institutional type in the sports governance literature, drawing on the four-dimensional objective framework established in Section “Theoretical evolution of university sports governance and the multi-dimensional objective system”. Second, we extracted the relative ordering of dimensional priorities for each type—for instance, the resource–equity trade-off documented in studies of research-intensive institutions22 and the equity–sustainability complementarity reported in community-oriented governance research23,24. Third, we translated these relative patterns into normalised mean values on the [0, 1] scale, ensuring that (a) the directional dominance of each archetype’s focal dimension was preserved, (b) the inter-archetype separation spanned the range 0.20–0.46 to reflect empirically plausible but non-trivial differentiation, and (c) no dimension was assigned extreme values of 0 or 1 that would be unrealistic for real institutional contexts. The noise standard deviation and the intra-block correlation were set to be consistent with the reliability levels typical of multi-item governance indicators, where intra-dimension item correlations of 0.55–0.75 are commonly observed. We acknowledge that this calibration relies on informed theoretical judgment rather than direct fitting to a labelled empirical dataset, and the resulting values should therefore be understood as theoretically motivated approximations rather than empirically derived ground-truth benchmarks. The sensitivity analysis reported in Section “Robustness tests and sensitivity analysis protocols”—demonstrating stable and under perturbations of all key parameters—provides evidence that the conclusions are not artefacts of the specific parameter choices adopted.

Robustness tests and sensitivity analysis protocols

In empirical analysis, this study implements a rigorous robustness testing procedure to ensure reliability of conclusions. First, a sample split validation is conducted: the dataset is divided into training, validation, and test sets at a ratio of 70%/15%/15%. Final model performance is reported on the test set, with the additional requirement that the difference in between training and test sets be less than 0.05 to ensure generalization capability.

Second, an indicator deletion test is performed: each governance attribute is removed in turn and the model is retrained. If removal of an attribute leads to a decrease in average objective exceeding 0.1, that attribute is recorded as a key attribute. If multiple attributes are identified, recommendations for indicator simplification or re-measurement are provided in the discussion section.

Third, the impact of normalization methods is examined. In addition to Min–Max normalization, Z-score standardization and median–interquartile range–based robust scaling are applied to test sensitivity of results to scaling choices. If key conclusions (e.g., configuration labels and dominant Shapley drivers) remain consistent across normalization schemes, results are considered robust.

Fourth, parameter sensitivity tests are conducted by perturbing key simulation parameters (mean separations, covariance structures, threshold locations, nonlinear coefficients) within ± 20% ranges. Configuration identification accuracy, objective , and stability of Shapley-derived thresholds are evaluated; if impacts remain below 10%, results are deemed robust.

Finally, sensitivity tests are performed for GMM covariance specifications (diagonal vs. full) and for methods of selecting K, ensuring that clustering results do not depend on a single algorithmic setting.

Simulation analysis

This chapter is based on the theoretical model and simulation methodology developed in Chapter 3. Using Monte Carlo simulation, 348 samples of university sports governance are generated. Sample generation strictly follows the parameter specifications of the four configuration types defined in the theoretical framework—resource-oriented, equity-prioritized, efficiency-driven, and balanced development—with each configuration containing 79–91 samples, yielding a total of 348 observations. The simulation analysis reported in this chapter serves as a methodological sanity check: it verifies that the proposed analytical pipeline is internally consistent and capable of recovering pre-specified structures under controlled conditions. This is a necessary but not sufficient condition for real-world applicability. The results establish the technical feasibility of the framework and provide a basis for designing future empirical studies, but they do not constitute evidence about the governance configurations or performance patterns of actual university sports systems.

Although this study employs simulated rather than actual survey data, the archetype parameterization was informed by expert consultation involving scholars from numerous Chinese universities and internationally renowned institutions. This consultation informed the plausibility of the theoretical archetypes as representations of real governance logics, but does not substitute for empirical validation; whether these archetypes correspond to observable configurations in real institutional populations remains an open question for future research.

Identification and validation of configuration types

Based on the determined optimal cluster number , this study applies both K-means and GMM to identify configurations among the 348 samples, and evaluates recovery performance using the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) and Normalised Mutual Information (NMI) (see Fig. 3). We first clarify the nature of the reported metric: the value is not a supervised classification accuracy in the conventional sense. It is the Adjusted Rand Index, an unsupervised clustering recovery metric bounded in that measures the agreement between the algorithm-recovered configuration labels and the known DGP true labels after correcting for chance agreement, where indicates perfect recovery and values near 0 indicate no better than random agreement. The computation procedure is as follows: because this study employs a Monte Carlo simulation design, the true configuration label for all N = 348 samples is known by construction from the DGP. ARI and NMI are therefore computed by comparing the algorithm-recovered hard-assignment labels against across all 348 samples simultaneously—that is, as a full-sample evaluation against known ground-truth labels, rather than through a hold-out test or cross-validation procedure. Bootstrap stability test

To evaluate the robustness of configuration identification, this study applies a Bootstrap resampling approach and conducts 200 repeated clustering procedures to compute the stability score of each sample with respect to its assigned configuration (see Table 6 and Fig. 5). The stability test results reveal a pronounced bimodal distribution of stability scores across the full sample, with a large proportion of samples concentrated in the 0.5–0.6 interval. This indicates that most universities exhibit a certain degree of stability in their configuration membership, although they have not yet reached a highly stable state.

Distribution of Bootstrap stability scores.

Under the threshold setting of 0.8 for core members and 0.5 for boundary members, 275 samples are classified as boundary members, accounting for 79.02% of the total sample, while 73 samples (20.98%) are identified as unstable members. This boundary-member-dominated stability structure suggests that most universities do not strictly adhere to a single governance mode, but instead operate at the intersection of multiple modes, dynamically adjusting and transitioning between configurations in response to changes in internal and external environments.

From a configuration-level perspective, the four configurations exhibit differentiated stability characteristics. The balanced-development configuration shows the highest average stability score, at 0.5444 (SD = 0.0267), with boundary members accounting for as high as 97.73% of the configuration. The efficiency-driven configuration has an average stability score of 0.5383 (SD = 0.0307), with 87.78% boundary members, ranking second in terms of stability. The resource-oriented configuration has an average stability score of 0.5259 (SD = 0.0316), while the equity-first configuration exhibits the lowest average stability score, at 0.4987 (SD = 0.0331), with unstable members accounting for as much as 49.45%. This result suggests that the equity-first configuration may represent a transitional or strategic choice, as many universities emphasize equity while simultaneously exploring pathways toward other configurations.

Examining the spatial distribution of stability, the Bootstrap stability scores exhibit a distinct concentric-circle pattern in the principal component analysis (PCA) space (Fig. 5b). This spatial pattern provides important insights into the internal mechanisms underlying configuration stability. Samples located near the cluster centers—that is, those closer to the configuration centroids—generally display higher stability scores (mostly above 0.55), indicating strong typicality and consistency in their governance modes. Their governance attributes are clearly aligned with a specific configuration and are less susceptible to sampling fluctuations.

In contrast, samples located near configuration boundaries—particularly those situated at the intersections of multiple configurations—exhibit lower stability scores (mostly between 0.45 and 0.55), reflecting ambiguity or transitional characteristics in their governance modes. Notably, the boundary regions between the resource-oriented and equity-first configurations, as well as between the efficiency-driven and balanced-development configurations, concentrate a large number of low-stability samples. These regions may represent “hot spots” of configuration transition and constitute the primary directions of governance mode evolution in universities.

From a governance practice perspective, universities located in boundary regions often face more complex internal and external environments. Their governance strategies must balance multiple objectives simultaneously and are therefore more susceptible to policy changes, resource fluctuations, and demand shifts, leading to uncertainty in configuration membership. For such universities, maintaining a certain degree of strategic flexibility and adjusting governance priorities in a timely manner in response to environmental changes is recommended, rather than rigidly adhering to a single governance mode.

Feature importance and interaction effect analysis

Shapley-value-based feature importance ranking

To identify the key governance features influencing each performance outcome, this study computes global feature importance based on Shapley value decomposition (see Appendix B and Fig. 11). Originating from cooperative game theory, Shapley values fairly allocate the marginal contribution of each feature to the model prediction by satisfying the efficiency, symmetry, dummy, and additivity axioms. The implementation details are as follows, corresponding to the protocol specified in Section “Implementation and interpretation protocols for Shapley value decomposition”. For all 14 tasks in which gradient boosting is the final estimator, TreeSHAP is applied, which computes exact Shapley values in polynomial time without approximation error. For the two tasks where random forest is the final estimator (C4– and C1–), TreeSHAP is likewise applied to the random forest ensemble. For MLP-based comparisons, the approximate DeepSHAP method is used, which linearises activation functions during backward propagation; these results are used only for within-algorithm comparison and are not reported as primary attribution outputs. Nonlinear interactions among indicators are handled via SHAP interaction value decomposition , which partitions each feature’s total Shapley contribution into a main effect and pairwise interaction terms with every other feature, ensuring that the contribution of any feature whose effect is modulated by another—such as through the interaction terms in Eq. (16)—is correctly attributed rather than absorbed into the main effect of either feature alone. Feature dependence is handled by computing both marginal Shapley values (integrating over the empirical marginal distribution of each feature) and conditional Shapley values (conditioning on the observed joint distribution); the Spearman rank correlation between the two rankings averages () across all tasks, confirming that the importance orderings are stable with respect to the treatment of inter-indicator correlations. Global feature importance is obtained as the mean absolute Shapley value aggregated across all samples within each configuration (Eq. 14).

The feature importance pattern for equity performance differs substantially, with EQ5 ranking first (Shapley value = 0.30). From the perspective of configurational moderation, the importance of the same feature varies by 20–40% across configurations. For instance, the Shapley importance of EF3 in C3 (ϕ = 0.58) is 38% higher than in C1 (ϕ = 0.42), because the efficiency-driven configuration structurally amplifies the marginal contribution of efficiency-related features while the dominance of resource inputs in C1 masks their relative importance. The Spearman rank correlation between marginal and conditional Shapley importance rankings averages (SD = 0.05) across all tasks, well above the pre-specified adoption threshold of defined in Section “Implementation and interpretation protocols for Shapley value decomposition”, indicating that the dependency bias introduced by marginal integration does not materially alter the rank ordering of feature importance in the present simulation despite the high intra-block correlation (). Mechanistically, this robustness arises because TreeSHAP’s recursive computation tracks which feature combinations appear in the training data via the tree structure, thereby partially constraining the out-of-distribution sampling problem that afflicts kernel-based marginal SHAP estimators. The values across individual tasks range from 0.81 to 0.95, with the lower end occurring in tasks where two or more indicators from the same dimension simultaneously appear among the top-five ranked features; in all such cases, the marginal and conditional rankings nonetheless agree on the identity of the top-ranked feature, confirming that while the precise relative ordering of co-ranked correlated features carries some uncertainty, the dominant governance driver identified by Shapley decomposition is reliably attributed and not an artifact of dependency bias. This pronounced configurational heterogeneity provides further empirical justification for adopting a configuration-specific modelling strategy.

It should be noted that all feature importance rankings in this section reflect the structural logic embedded in the DGP rather than empirically validated governance priorities. These results confirm the internal consistency of the interpretability pipeline—demonstrating that Shapley decomposition can recover contribution patterns explicitly encoded in Eq. (16)—and represent theoretically motivated hypotheses about governance priorities that future empirical research could test using real institutional data.

Multi-objective relationships and robustness tests

Association patterns among performance objectives

University sports governance involves coordination and trade-offs among multiple performance objectives. Understanding the association patterns among these objectives is therefore essential for designing effective governance strategies. According to the overall correlation matrix (Table 8), the p-values reported alongside correlation coefficients serve as diagnostics of the simulated dataset, indicating whether the associations embedded in the DGP are recoverable at the current sample size, rather than as evidence of real-world governance synergies or trade-offs. This transformation demonstrates that trade-offs and synergies among objectives are not fixed but can be reshaped through deliberate configuration selection and optimization. From a policy perspective, universities aiming to simultaneously enhance resource allocation and equity should not attempt incremental optimization within a resource-oriented configuration. Instead, transitioning toward an equity-first or balanced configuration—by altering the underlying governance logic—offers a more effective pathway to resolving conflicts and achieving synergistic improvements.

These configuration-specific association patterns imply that multi-objective governance should not merely balance objectives within a fixed configuration but should operate at the configuration-selection level to reshape objective relationships—facilitating a shift from a “zero-sum game” to a “positive-sum game.”

Pareto frontiers and robustness tests

In multi-objective optimization contexts, Pareto frontiers represent sets of non-dominated solutions. This study identifies Pareto frontiers for two objective pairs—equity vs. efficiency and resource allocation vs. sustainability—to explore the optimal boundaries of multi-objective governance (see Fig. 18).

Pareto frontier analysis.

The equity–efficiency Pareto frontier is dominated primarily by samples from the equity-first and balanced configurations, with optimal solutions reaching equity levels of 0.72 and efficiency levels of 0.78. The frontier exhibits a pronounced convex shape, indicating that the marginal cost of improving efficiency increases rapidly at high levels of equity. In contrast, the resource allocation–sustainability Pareto frontier displays a flatter curve, suggesting a relatively milder trade-off between these two objectives.

To assess the robustness of the study’s conclusions, particularly the sensitivity of model outcomes to key parameter settings, systematic perturbation tests are conducted on four classes of parameters (see Fig. 20). Mean-shift tests indicate strong robustness: within a ±10% perturbation range around the original mean, ARI remains between 0.73 and 0.75, NMI between 0.70 and 0.73, and R2 between 0.69 and 0.71. In contrast, sensitivity tests for the standard deviation factor reveal a distinct pattern, with performance metrics declining rapidly once the factor exceeds 0.9. Both alpha-factor and beta-shift tests demonstrate high robustness. Overall, the sensitivity analysis indicates that the core conclusions of this study are highly robust to mean and beta parameters, while being relatively sensitive to the standard deviation and alpha factors.

Discussion and conclusions

Discussion

The simulation analyses confirm the technical identifiability and internal consistency of the proposed pipeline under the tested parameter domain. Configuration recovery is successful (, cross-algorithm consistency ), with the solution stable across initialisations, folds, and Bootstrap replicates. The four recovered configurations exhibit profiles consistent with their theoretical parameterisation: the resource-oriented type concentrates investment in the RA dimension at the expense of equity and sustainability; the equity-prioritized type achieves the highest sustainability performance alongside strong equity outcomes; the efficiency-driven type reveals a performance gap between its high EF input and moderate EF output, attributable to resource constraints; and the balanced type achieves the highest efficiency performance through multi-dimensional coordination. The configuration-specific gradient boosting pipeline achieves (95% CI ), outperforming OLS (+ 0.216), weighted aggregation (+ 0.248), and global gradient boosting (+ 0.136), confirming the independent contributions of nonlinear specification and configuration stratification. Shapley decomposition recovers dimension-aligned feature priorities with cross-configuration importance differentials stable to perturbation (, ). Sensitivity analysis confirms robustness to mean and interaction coefficient perturbations (; under ± 10% perturbations), while performance is more sensitive to standard deviation and linear coefficient parameters. These results establish the methodological feasibility of the framework as a candidate analytical instrument for future empirical governance research. They do not, however, constitute evidence of real governance improvement: all configurations, performance patterns, thresholds, and feature importance rankings are properties of the simulation design, and their translation into policy value is contingent on empirical validation with real institutional data.

Based on the simulation findings, four tentative policy-relevant directions are proposed as hypotheses for future empirical testing rather than evidence-based recommendations. First, if validated on real institutional data, the framework could support configuration-based differentiated governance strategies by identifying institutions with distinct governance profiles and matching them with corresponding intervention priorities. Second, if nonlinear resource–performance relationships are confirmed empirically, the framework could support threshold-aware resource allocation by identifying configuration-specific investment levels associated with performance changes. Third, if the simulated inter-dimensional relationships are observed in practice, the framework could support multi-objective collaborative governance by helping decision-makers identify potential complementarities and trade-offs among governance dimensions. Fourth, if configuration structures and performance mappings are replicated in real institutional settings, the framework could support the development of configuration-based monitoring systems for dynamic governance assessment. These directions should be interpreted as conditional hypotheses requiring validation through future empirical research.

Conclusions

The contributions of this study can be summarized at three levels. At the theoretical level, this study proposes a structural heterogeneity perspective as a candidate analytical framework for the performance assessment of university sports governance, challenging the “universality logic” of traditional governance evaluation at the conceptual level. It introduces “configuration” as a core organizing concept and demonstrates, under controlled simulation conditions, that the proposed pipeline can recover pre-specified governance archetypes and nonlinear performance mappings embedded in the data-generating process. Whether differentiated governance patterns of this kind exist in real university sports systems, and whether their underlying logic matches the simulation design, remains an open empirical question for future research. At the methodological level, this study constructs an integrated analytical chain encompassing indicator vectorization, configuration identification, nonlinear mapping estimation, and interpretability decomposition. It combines K-means, Gaussian Mixture Models, gradient boosting, and Shapley value decomposition within a two-stage strategy of “methodological development plus Monte Carlo simulation validation,” and demonstrates that the pipeline can reliably recover pre-specified governance structures and performance mappings under controlled conditions. At the practical level, this study’s contribution is precisely delimited: it provides a methodologically validated analytical pipeline whose practical policy value remains conditional on future empirical application. The distinction between what this study does and does not demonstrate is important. What it demonstrates is that the pipeline is technically capable of identifying structural heterogeneity, estimating nonlinear governance–performance mappings, and producing robust Shapley-based attributions under controlled simulation conditions—capabilities that are necessary preconditions for any real-world governance diagnostic application. What it does not demonstrate is that these capabilities translate into actual governance improvement: the four policy-relevant directions identified—configuration-based classified guidance, threshold-aware resource allocation, multi-objective collaborative governance, and dynamic performance monitoring—are theoretically motivated hypotheses derived from the simulation design, not evidence-based recommendations grounded in real institutional outcomes. The practical value of the framework will be established only through a staged empirical programme: first, replicating the configuration structure in real survey data; second, validating that the identified thresholds and feature importance rankings correspond to observable governance outcomes; and third, testing whether interventions informed by the framework’s diagnostics produce measurable performance improvements. Until these stages are completed, the framework should be understood as offering methodological infrastructure for future governance research, rather than as providing direct policy guidance.

First, and most fundamentally, this study relies entirely on Monte Carlo–generated synthetic data rather than real institutional data, which constitutes the primary limitation on its external validity. Three specific external validity threats warrant explicit acknowledgement. (a) Configuration generalisability: the four governance archetypes recovered in the simulation are by construction those that were injected by the DGP; whether analogous configurations exist in real university sports governance systems—and whether their number, composition, and relative prevalence match the simulation design—is an entirely open empirical question that the present study cannot answer. The simulation demonstrates that the pipeline can recover injected structure, not that the structure exists in practice. (b) Parameter transferability: the archetype mean vectors , covariance structure , noise level , and performance mapping coefficients (, , , , ) were set to theoretically motivated values; real institutional data may exhibit substantially different distributional properties, inter-indicator correlations, and nonlinear response profiles, which would alter both the identifiability of configurations and the mapping model’s . (c) Indicator operationalisation: the J = 23 indicators used in the simulation are theoretically specified constructs; their operationalisation via survey items in real data collection involves measurement error, response bias, and potential construct validity issues that are absent by design in the synthetic data. To address these threats, future research should apply this framework to actual institutional data—ideally a nationally representative survey of university sports governance units—following a pre-registered analysis plan that specifies the expected number of configurations, key indicator loadings, and minimum acceptable thresholds prior to data collection. The simulation findings in this paper should be treated as establishing methodological feasibility and generating testable hypotheses, rather than as evidence about real governance structures.

Second, limitations related to the single-view clustering design. A further methodological limitation concerns the single-view concatenation design adopted for clustering. By representing all four governance dimensions as a single concatenated vector , the current framework implicitly assumes a homogeneous feature space across dimensions. In practice, however, the resource, equity, efficiency, and sustainability dimensions of university sports governance may exhibit structurally distinct within-view covariance patterns that a single concatenated representation cannot adequately capture. The AGSCR-MVC framework51, which achieves structural co-regularization between anchors and anchor graphs across multiple views and has demonstrated balanced superiority in both performance and computational cost on large-scale datasets, provides a highly compatible solution for this multi-dimensional governance context. Specifically, the four governance dimensions could each be treated as an independent view, with anchor-to-graph co-regularization applied across views to capture complementary governance information while preserving dimension-specific structural semantics. Incorporating this approach into the configuration identification stage represents a concrete and methodologically well-grounded direction for future empirical applications of this framework.

Third, the preset number of configurations. Although four configurations were theoretically assumed and empirically validated as reasonable, real-world governance patterns may be more complex. Future studies could employ nonparametric clustering methods or mixed-membership mechanisms to allow greater flexibility in the number of configurations and membership assignment.

Fourth, the absence of dynamic evolutionary mechanisms. This study adopts a cross-sectional design and cannot capture how configurations evolve over time. Future research should incorporate longitudinal tracking designs or panel data to construct Markov models of configuration transitions and explore triggering conditions and path dependence. Fifth, insufficient consideration of external environmental factors. This study focuses primarily on internal governance mechanisms, with limited attention to policy environments, economic development levels, and geographical conditions. Future work could integrate environmental factors into the framework and employ multilevel models to examine “configuration × environment” interaction effects. Sixth, the generalizability of the methodology remains to be tested. Although the framework is validated in the context of university sports governance, its applicability to other governance domains or organizational types requires cross-contextual testing and adaptive refinement.

Looking ahead, the structural heterogeneity evaluation pathway developed in this study offers broad potential for extension. Future research may deepen theoretical exploration by integrating institutional theory and resource dependence theory to construct models of configuration formation mechanisms; expand application scenarios by applying this approach to regional education governance, public health governance, and other multi-objective complex systems; and, with the advancement of big data and artificial intelligence technologies, realize real-time monitoring, intelligent diagnosis, and adaptive optimization of governance performance, thereby providing stronger technical support for the construction of modern governance systems.
