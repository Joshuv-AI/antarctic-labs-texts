Abstract
Sharpness-aware minimization (SAM) has well-documented merits in enhancing generalization of deep neural network models. Accounting for sharpness in the loss function geometry, where neighborhoods of ‘flat minima’ heighten generalization ability, SAM seeks ‘flat valleys’ by minimizing the maximum loss provoked by an adversarial perturbation within the neighborhood. Although critical to account for sharpness of the loss function, in practice SAM suffers from ‘ over-friendly adversaries,’ which can curtail the outmost level of generalization. To avoid such ‘friendliness,’ the present contribution fosters stabilization of adversaries through variance suppression (VASSO). VASSO offers a general approach to provably stabilize adversaries. In particular, when integrating VASSO with SAM, improved generalizability is numerically validated on extensive vision and language tasks. Once applied on top of a computationally efficient SAM variant, VASSO offers a desirable generalization-computation tradeoff.

Common approaches to improving generalizability of DNNs include regularization and data augmentation

II-C

The challenge: friendly adversary

Adversary to a minibatch can be a friend of other minibatches.

SAM’s adversary can be ‘malicious’ for a minibatch
{\cal B}_{t}
used in iteration
t
, but not necessarily for other data, because it only safeguards
f_{{\cal B}_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}+\bm{\epsilon}_{t})-f_{{\cal B}_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\geq 0
for a small
\rho

{\cal B}
, it can be shown that
f_{{\cal B}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}+\bm{\epsilon}_{t})-f_{{\cal B}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\leq 0

\langle\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\mathbf{g}_{\cal B}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\rangle\leq 0

falls outside the column space of Hessian at convergence; see
[
14
, Definition 4.3]
for further elaboration. This misalignment of high-order derivatives undermines the inductive bias of SAM, thereby worsening generalization.

II-D

A closer look at friendly adversaries

Gradient variance is a major driver behind SAM’s friendly adversary and unstable stochastic linearization. At first glance however, this seems to conflict with the
empirical

m

Since

-sharpness is not necessarily related to sharpness or generalization, as verified by the example given in
[

-sharpness formulation can be ill-posed for a specific choice of

B

Even in the regime where

-sharpness is empirically observed (as with ResNet-18 on CIFAR10 and ResNet-34 on CIFAR100), we have confirmed experimentally that

-sharpness is

Observation 1. Same variance, different generalization.
Let

by setting

Observation 2. Enlarged variance degrades generalization.
We explicitly increase variance when finding the adversary by adding Gaussian noise

\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})

\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{t}=\rho\frac{\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\bm{\zeta}}{\|\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\bm{\zeta}\|}

While understanding

The metric: characterization of a friendly adversary

To characterize friendly adversaries analytically, it is convenient to start with necessary notation. Let the

A stochastic linearization with slope

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{t}(\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle-{\cal L}_{t}(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}))

III
Variance Suppression

This section advocates variance suppression (VASSO) for SAM as a means of dealing with the friendly adversary. We start with the intuition behind VASSO, and then establish its improved
\delta
-stability over SAM. We also develop implementation aspects and possible extensions.

III-A

Design and stability analysis

A straightforward attempt towards stability is to equip SAM’s stochastic linearization with variance-reduced gradients such as SVRG and SARAH
[
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
]

VASSO overcomes this computational burden through a refined stochastic linearization. For a prescribed

VASSO:

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}_{t}=(1-\theta)\mathbf{d}_{t-1}+\theta\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})

(5a)

\displaystyle\bm{\epsilon}_{t}=\argmax_{\|\bm{\epsilon}\|\leq\rho}f(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\langle\mathbf{d}_{t},\bm{\epsilon}\rangle=\rho\frac{\mathbf{d}_{t}}{\|\mathbf{d}_{t}\|}.

(5b)

Compared with (

Theorem
3
contends that VASSO alleviates the friendly adversary challenge by promoting stability. Qualitatively, VASSO is roughly
\sqrt{\theta}\in(0,1)
times more stable relative to SAM, since the term in big
{\cal O}
notation is negligible given a sufficiently large
T
. Theorem
3
also guides the choice of
\theta
– preferably small but not too small, otherwise the term in big
{\cal O}
is inversely amplified.

Visualize stabilized adversaries.
To validate our findings in Theorem
2
, we train a ResNet-18 on CIFAR10 and depict the evolution of
\|\bm{\epsilon}_{t}-\bm{\epsilon}_{t-1}\|
for both SAM and VASSO in Fig.
4
. Because both
\bm{\epsilon}_{t}
and
\bm{\epsilon}_{t-1}
reside on
\mathbb{S}_{\rho}(\mathbf{0})
, the value of
\|\bm{\epsilon}_{t}-\bm{\epsilon}_{t-1}\|
is solely determined by the angle between these vectors. In essence,
\|\bm{\epsilon}_{t}-\bm{\epsilon}_{t-1}\|
characterizes well the stability. The results presented in Fig.
4
distinctly reveal that VASSO indeed improves the stability adversary over SAM.

III-B

Additional perspectives of VASSO

Having dealt with stability, this subsection proceeds with other aspects of VASSO.

Convergence.
Summarized in the following corollary, the convergence of VASSO can be pursued as a direct consequence of Theorem
1
. The reason is that
\bm{\epsilon}_{t}\in\mathbb{S}_{\rho}(\mathbf{0})
is satisfied by (
5
).

Corollary 1

(VASSO convergence)
.

Under Assumptions
1
–
3
, and with
\eta_{t}
and
\rho
as in Theorem
1
, VASSO ensures for any
\theta\in(0,1)
that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\big[\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}\big]\leq{\cal O}\bigg(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sqrt{T}}\bigg)~~~~\text{and}~~~~

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\big[\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}+\bm{\epsilon}_{t})\|^{2}\big]\leq{\cal O}\bigg(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sqrt{T}}\bigg).

Pronounced sharpness around optimum.
Consider a near optimal region, where
\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|\rightarrow 0
, and suppose a big data regime, where
\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\bm{\zeta}
for some Gaussian random vector
\bm{\zeta}
with covariance matrix
\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}
for simplicity. (Using arguments from von Mises-Fisher statistics
[
33
]
generalization is also possible). SAM has difficulty estimating the flatness in this case, since
\bm{\epsilon}_{t}\approx\rho\bm{\zeta}/\|\bm{\zeta}\|
is uniformly distributed over
\mathbb{S}_{\rho}(\mathbf{0})
regardless of whether the neighboring region is sharp or not. On the other hand, VASSO has
\bm{\epsilon}_{t}=\rho\mathbf{d}_{t}/\|\mathbf{d}_{t}\|
. Because
\{\mathbf{g}_{\tau}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau})\}_{\tau}
on sharper valleys tend to have larger magnitude, their EMA
\mathbf{d}_{t}
is helpful for distinguishing sharp valleys with flat ones.

Memory efficient implementation.
Although at first glance VASSO has to store both
\mathbf{d}_{t}
and
\bm{\epsilon}_{t}
, it can be implemented in a much more memory efficient manner. It is sufficient to store
\mathbf{d}_{t}
together with a scalar
\|\mathbf{d}_{t}\|
so that
\bm{\epsilon}_{t}
can be recovered on demand through normalization; see (
5b
). Hence, VASSO’s memory requirements are the same as those of SAM.

Extensions.
VASSO has the potential to boost the performance of other SAM variants by stabilizing their stochastic linearization through variance suppression. For instance, adaptive SAM methods such as
[
34
,
17
]
are scale invarianct, while GSAM
[
16
]
minimizes a surrogate gap jointly with (
1
). Nevertheless, these SAM variants leverage stochastic linearization in (
II-A
). It is thus envisioned that VASSO can also alleviate the possible friendly adversary issues therein. As we shall see in Sec.
V
, cross-fertilizing VASSO with GSAM yields improved numerical performance.

The generalization benefits of SAM come at the price of approximately doubled computation per iteration compared with SGD because of the need to compute
\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
and

In pursuit of a more favorable tradeoff that leans toward the generalization side, our idea is to leverage VASSO’s stabilized stochastic linearization in eSAM proposed originally in
[
20
]
. The resultant algorithm termed as efficient VASSO (eVASSO) is listed as Alg.

It introduces a Bernoulli random variable
R_{t}

R_{t}=1
with probability

R_{t}

(1+p)

A smaller

R_{t}=1

(Variance suppression and stability of eVASSO)
.

Under Assumptions
1
–
3
, if i)
\bm{\epsilon}_{t}

) with
\theta\in(0,1)

\eta_{t}
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{d}_{t}-\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}\big]\leq\theta\sigma^{2}+{\cal O}\bigg(\frac{(1-\theta)^{2}\sigma^{2}}{\theta^{2}\sqrt{T}}\bigg).

) indeed find flatter valleys relative to SAM and SGD, in agreement with their improved generalization behavior.

VI
Other related works

This section completes the context of DNN generalizability with related works.

Sharpness and generalization.
Since the study of
[
10
]
, the relation between sharpness and generalization has been investigated intensively. It has been observed that sharpness is closely correlated with the ratio between learning rate and batchsize in SGD
[
52
]
. Analytical results on the generalization error using sharpness-related measures can be found in e.g.,
[
53
,
54
,
55
]
. These works justify the goal of seeking a flatter valley to enhance generalizability. Aiming at a flatter minimum, approaches other than SAM have been also developed. For example, stochastic weight averaging has been advocated for DNNs
[
56
]
, while
[
57
]
has put forth an algorithm similar to SAM and has placed emphasis on robustness to adversarial training.

SAM variants.
Besides GSAM and ASAM that have been already mentioned,
[
58
]
pursued a SAM variant by penalizing the gradient norm on the premise that sharper valleys tend to have gradients with larger norm. In addition,
[
59
]
arrived at a similar conclusion by analyzing gradient flow. Exploiting multiple (ascent) steps to find an adversary has been studied systematically
[
60
]
. However, these works overlook the friendly adversary issue, while VASSO provides algorithmic possibilities for generalization benefits by stabilizing their adversaries.

VII
Concluding remarks

This contribution demonstrates that stabilizing adversaries through variance suppression consolidates the generalization merits of sharpness-aware minimization. The novel framework abbreviated as VASSO, induces provable stability for SAM’s stochastic linearization. VASSO’s theoretically established merits are demonstrated with a suit of numerical experiments, and illustrate model-agnostic improvement over SAM on various vision and language tasks. Lastly, VASSO also steers the generalization-computation tradeoff of SAM towards the optimal side – improved generalization (over SGD) with significantly reduced computation (relative to SAM).

Appendix B
More on
m
-sharpness

m
-sharpness can be ill-posed.

Our reason for not studying
m
-sharpness directly is that its formulation
[
13
, eq. (3)]
may be ill-posed mathematically due to the lack of a clear definition on how the dataset
{\cal S}
is partitioned. Consider the following example, where the same notation as
[
13
]
is adopted for convenience. Suppose that the loss function is
l_{i}(w)=a_{i}w^{2}+b_{i}w
, where
\{(a_{i},b_{i})\}_{i}
are data points, and
w
is the parameter to be optimized. Let the dataset have
4
samples,
(a_{1}=0,b_{1}=1)
;
(a_{2}=0,b_{2}=-1)
;
(a_{3}=-1,b_{3}=0)
; and,
(a_{4}=1,b_{4}=0)
. Consider 2-sharpness.

•

If the data partition is {1,2} and {3,4}, the objective of 2-sharpness, namely equation (3) in
[
13
]
, becomes
\min_{w}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\max_{||\delta||<\rho}0
.

•

If the data partition is {1,3} and {2,4}, the objective is
\min_{w}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\max_{||\delta||<\rho}f_{i}(w,\delta)
, where
f_{1}
is the loss on partition {1,3}, that is,
f_{1}(w,\delta)=-(w+\delta)^{2}+(w+\delta)
; and
f_{2}(w,\delta)=(w+\delta)^{2}-(w+\delta)
is the loss on partition {3,4}.

The objective functions are different when the data partition varies. This makes the problem ill-posed – data partition leads to entirely different loss curvature. In practice, the data partition even varies across epochs due to the random shuffle in data loading.

Useful lemmas

This subsection presents useful lemmas for our main results.

Lemma 1
.

Alg.
1
(or equivalently iteration (
8
)) ensures that

\displaystyle\eta_{t}\mathbb{E}\big[\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t+\frac{1}{2}})\rangle\big]

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\leq\frac{L\eta_{t}^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}\big[\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}\big]+\frac{L\rho^{2}}{2}.

Proof.

To start, we have that

\displaystyle~~~~~\big\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t+\frac{1}{2}})\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t+\frac{1}{2}})\rangle.

Taking expectation conditioned on
\mathbf{x}_{t}
, we arrive at

\displaystyle~~~~~\mathbb{E}\big[\big\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t+\frac{1}{2}})\big\rangle|\mathbf{x}_{t}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\rangle|\mathbf{x}_{t}\big]

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~+\mathbb{E}\big[\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t+\frac{1}{2}})\rangle|\mathbf{x}_{t}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t+\frac{1}{2}})\rangle|\mathbf{x}_{t}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\big[\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|\cdot\|\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t+\frac{1}{2}})\||\mathbf{x}_{t}\big]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}L\mathbb{E}\big[\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|\cdot\|\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}_{t+\frac{1}{2}}\||\mathbf{x}_{t}\big]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}L\rho\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|

where (a) follows from Assumption
2
; and (b) holds because
\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}_{t+\frac{1}{2}}=-\bm{\epsilon}_{t}
, and its norm equals
\rho
. This inequality ensures that

\displaystyle~~~~~\eta_{t}\mathbb{E}\big[\big\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t+\frac{1}{2}})\big\rangle|\mathbf{x}_{t}\big]

\displaystyle\leq L\rho\eta_{t}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|\leq\frac{L\eta_{t}^{2}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}}{2}+\frac{L\rho^{2}}{2}

where the last inequality holds because
\rho\eta_{t}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|\leq\frac{1}{2}\eta_{t}^{2}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\rho^{2}
. Taking expectation w.r.t.
\mathbf{x}_{t}
completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 2
.

Alg.
1
(or equivalently iteration (
8
)) ensures that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t+\frac{1}{2}})\|^{2}\big]\leq 2L^{2}\rho^{2}+2\mathbb{E}\big[\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}\big]+2\sigma^{2}.

Proof.

The proof starts with bounding
\|\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t+\frac{1}{2}})\|
as

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t+\frac{1}{2}})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t+\frac{1}{2}})-\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t+\frac{1}{2}})-\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}+2\|\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}2L^{2}\|\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}_{t+\frac{1}{2}}\|^{2}+2\|\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}2L^{2}\rho^{2}+2\|\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}

where (a) follows from Assumption
2
; and (b) is because
\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}_{t+\frac{1}{2}}=-\bm{\epsilon}_{t}
, and its norm equals
\rho
.

Taking expectation conditioned on
\mathbf{x}_{t}
, we have

\displaystyle~~~~~\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t+\frac{1}{2}})\|^{2}|\mathbf{x}_{t}\big]

\displaystyle\leq 2L^{2}\rho^{2}+2\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}|\mathbf{x}_{t}\big]

\displaystyle\leq 2L^{2}\rho^{2}+2\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}+2\sigma^{2}

where the last inequality is because of Assumption

. Taking expectation w.r.t. the randomness in
\mathbf{x}_{t}
completes the proof.
∎

\eta^{2}L^{2}\sigma^{2}={\cal O}(\sigma^{2}/T)
, and

\displaystyle~~~~~\eta^{2}L^{2}\mathbb{E}\big[\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}\big]=\frac{\eta_{0}^{2}L^{2}}{T}\mathbb{E}\big[\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\eta_{0}^{2}L^{2}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\big[\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}\big]={\cal O}\bigg(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sqrt{T}}\bigg)

where for the last equality we have also used Theorem
1
.

Combining (
9
) with (
12
), (
10
) with (
11
), and choosing
\lambda=\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}
, we have

\displaystyle~~~~~\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{d}_{t}-\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq(1\!-\!\theta)\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{d}_{t\!-\!1}-\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t\!-\!1})\|^{2}\big]+\frac{(1-\theta)^{2}}{\theta}{\cal O}\bigg(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sqrt{T}}\bigg)\!+\!\theta^{2}\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\theta\sigma^{2}+{\cal O}\bigg(\frac{(1\!-\!\theta)^{2}\sigma^{2}}{\theta^{2}\sqrt{T}}\bigg)

where the last inequality is the result of Lemma
3
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3

Proof.

We adopt a unified notation for simplicity. Let
\mathbf{v}_{t}:=\mathbf{d}_{t}
for VASSO, and
\mathbf{v}_{t}:=\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
for SAM. Then for both VASSO and SAM, we can write

\displaystyle~~~~~f(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\langle\mathbf{v}_{t},\bm{\epsilon}_{t}\rangle=f(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\rho\|\mathbf{v}_{t}\|

(13)

\displaystyle=f(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\rho\|\mathbf{v}_{t}-\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|.

For convenience, let
\bm{\epsilon}_{t}^{*}=\rho\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})/\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|
. From (
13
), we have that

\displaystyle~~~~~f(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\langle\mathbf{v}_{t},\bm{\epsilon}_{t}\rangle

(14)

\displaystyle=f(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\rho\|\mathbf{v}_{t}-\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|

\displaystyle\leq f(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\rho\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|+\rho\|\mathbf{v}_{t}-\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|

\displaystyle=f(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\bm{\epsilon}_{t}^{*}\rangle+\rho\|\mathbf{v}_{t}-\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|.

Applying the triangle inequality
\big|\|\mathbf{a}\|-\|\mathbf{b}\|\big|\leq\|\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\|
, we arrive at

\displaystyle~~~~~f(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\langle\mathbf{v}_{t},\bm{\epsilon}_{t}\rangle

(15)

\displaystyle=f(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\rho\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})-(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\mathbf{v}_{t})\|

\displaystyle\geq f(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\rho\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|-\rho\|\mathbf{v}_{t}-\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|

\displaystyle=f(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\bm{\epsilon}_{t}^{*}\rangle-\rho\|\mathbf{v}_{t}-\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|.

Combining (
14
) with (
15
), we have

\displaystyle|{\cal L}_{t}(\mathbf{v}_{t})-{\cal L}_{t}(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}))|\leq\rho\|\mathbf{v}_{t}-\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|

which further implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[|{\cal L}_{t}(\mathbf{v}_{t})-{\cal L}_{t}(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}))|\big]

\displaystyle\leq\rho\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{v}_{t}-\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|\big]

\displaystyle\leq\rho\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{v}_{t}-\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}\big]}

where the last inequality follows from
(\mathbb{E}[a])^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}[a^{2}]
. This theorem can be proved by applying Assumption
3
for SAM, and Lemma
2
for VASSO.
∎

Proof for Theorem
4

We start with a technical lemma needed for the main theorem.

Lemma 4
.

Alg.
2
ensures that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}+\bm{\epsilon}_{t})\|^{2}\big]\leq 2pL^{2}\rho^{2}+(1+p)\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}+(1+p)\sigma^{2}.

Proof.

First, consider the case where
B_{t}=1
. We have that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}+\bm{\epsilon}_{t})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}+\bm{\epsilon}_{t})-\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}+\bm{\epsilon}_{t})-\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}+2\|\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}2L^{2}\|\bm{\epsilon}_{t}\|^{2}+2\|\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}2L^{2}\rho^{2}+2\|\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}

where (a) is due to Assumption
2
; and (b) is because
\|\bm{\epsilon}_{t}\|=\rho
.

When
B_{t}=0
, i.e.,
\bm{\epsilon}_{t}=\bm{0}
, we have that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}+\bm{\epsilon}_{t})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}=\|\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}.

Taking expectation conditioned on
\mathbf{x}_{t}
, we have

\displaystyle~~~~~\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}+\bm{\epsilon}_{t})\|^{2}|\mathbf{x}_{t}\big]

\displaystyle\leq 2pL^{2}\rho^{2}+(1+p)\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}|\mathbf{x}_{t}\big]

\displaystyle\leq 2pL^{2}\rho^{2}+(1+p)\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}+(1+p)\sigma^{2}

where the last inequality is because of Assumption
3
. Taking expectation w.r.t. the randomness in
\mathbf{x}_{t}
completes the proof.
∎

Now we are ready to prove Theorem
4
.

Proof.

Similar to (
9
), we have that for eVASSO
