Baseline cerebrospinal fluid complement balance is associated with longitudinal memory decline in relation to plasma p‑tau181 levels in mild cognitive impairment
Authors: Zhinan Ye, Qihan Zhang, Bo Zhang, Bingxin Teng, Shiyue Wang, Jiaxuan Chen, Bohao Li, Hongjie Jin, Zhexin Zheng, Zhuoyu Yang, Xuanang Hu, Xinyan Zhao, Suwen Huang, Yiyun Weng, Dehao Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONThe role of complement in Alzheimer's disease (AD) remains unclear, particularly regarding tau pathology and memory decline.METHODSIn 123 mild cognitive impairment (MCI) participants from Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI), linear mixed‑effects models and Johnson–Neyman analysis examined associations of baseline cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) C3/FH ratio with longitudinal memory decline moderated by time‑varying plasma phosphorylated tau181 (p‑tau181).RESULTSThe three‑way interaction was significant (p = 0.023). A region of statistical significance was identified: the conditional association of C3/FH with memory decline became significant when p‑tau181 exceeded 0.76 SD, with higher C3/FH associated with slower decline at elevated p‑tau181 levels. Cross‐sectionally, no significant C3/FH–memory associations were observed in any diagnostic group (all p > 0.05).DISCUSSIONThese findings indicate that complement balance is associated with memory decline in MCI in a manner dependent on plasma p‑tau181 levels, suggesting that complement may interact with AD‑related pathophysiology during the prodromal phase. The identified statistical region provides a hypothesis‑generating benchmark for future studies.

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most common cause of dementia, and its prevalence continues to rise with the accelerating global aging population. 1 The pathophysiological framework of AD is conceptualized by the ATN classification system, which categorizes biomarkers into three core groups: amyloid‐β (Aβ) deposition (A), tau pathology (T), and neurodegeneration (N). 2 Among these, plasma p‐tau181 was used as a proxy for AD‐related pathophysiology. Of note, plasma phosphorylated tau181 (p‐tau181) levels are more closely associated with Aβ‑positron emission tomography (PET) than with tau‑PET, although p‐tau181 primarily reflects tau phosphorylation at threonine 181. 3 Meanwhile, plasma Aβ42/40 ratio serves as a reliable indicator of cerebral amyloidosis, offering a relatively non‐invasive method for monitoring these core pathological processes. 4

Neuroinflammation is a critical driver of AD pathogenesis, contributing to synaptic dysfunction, neuronal damage, and disease progression. 5 , 6 The complement system, as a central mediator of innate immunity, is closely involved in neuroinflammation in AD. Complement factor H (FH) serves as the primary negative regulator of the alternative pathway, working in concert with complement component 3 (C3) to maintain complement homeostasis. 7 , 8 The balance between complement activation and regulation, reflected by the relative levels of C3 and FH, is critical for complement homeostasis. 9 Previous studies have demonstrated significantly altered levels of C3 and FH in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of patients with AD, and these alterations are associated with cognitive decline. 10 For instance, a cross‐sectional mediation study within the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) cohort found that the association between CSF C3 and tau pathology was partially mediated by Aβ, particularly in apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 carriers, suggesting an interplay between complement, amyloid, and tau pathways. 11

Despite evidence implicating the complement system in AD pathogenesis through tau‐related mechanisms, several critical questions remain unanswered: whether complement regulatory status, as reflected by the C3/FH ratio, is associated with plasma p‐tau181 in living humans; whether this association exhibits cognitive domain selectivity; whether the association between complement and longitudinal cognitive decline is moderated by p‑tau181 levels; and whether a region of statistical significance exists—whereby the association of complement with cognition varies with the level of p‑tau181.

To address these gaps, the present study used CSF C3/FH as the exposure and memory function as the primary outcome. Using an a priori analytical hierarchy distinguishing the primary model from exploratory analyses, the primary aims were: (1) to examine whether longitudinal p‑tau181 modifies the association of baseline C3/FH on memory decline in mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and (2) to identify the region of statistical significance (using Johnson–Neyman [J‐N] analysis) where this modification becomes detectable. This region serves as a statistical benchmark for the complement–tau interaction. A secondary exploratory aim was to assess a cross‑sectional indirect association between C3/FH and memory through p‑tau181, consistent with a mediation pattern but without causal claims.

METHODS

Participants

This study was a secondary analysis of prospectively collected data from the ADNI, an ongoing multicenter longitudinal observational cohort. Data were downloaded from the ADNI database on February 7, 2026 (n = 5,296). The present analysis was not part of the original ADNI protocol objectives and should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis‐generating. Only participants with baseline CSF C3 and FH measurements (available in a CSF substudy) were considered. Among the initial cohort, 389 individuals had available baseline CSF C3 and FH measurements; of these, 337 had complete APOE ε4 genotype data. Of these 337 individuals, 27 were further excluded because they were aged < 65 years, yielding 310 participants. The remaining 310 participants all had complete baseline data for plasma p‑tau181 and memory composite scores, so no further exclusions were applied. The final analytical sample comprised 310 individuals for the core cross‐sectional analyses, including 84 cognitively normal (CN) participants, 123 with MCI, and 103 with AD. The attrition from the initial cohort primarily reflects the phased nature of ADNI biospecimen collection protocols rather than investigator‐led selection. Among the 123 MCI participants, 49 were classified as progressors (defined as a change in clinical diagnosis from MCI to AD during follow‑up) and 74 remained stable.

Longitudinal cognitive trajectories were assessed in the MCI subgroup over a maximum follow‐up of 10.8 years. Across the study period, 123 MCI participants contributed 1,138 valid observations, with a mean of 9.3 ± 5 assessments per participant. The mean duration from baseline to the last follow‐up visit was 8.55 ± 0.49 years (range: 8.3–10.8 years). For longitudinal analyses examining the three‐way interaction of C3/FH × time × longitudinal p‑tau181 on memory decline, all 123 MCI participants with baseline biomarker data and at least one follow‑up measurement of plasma p‑tau181 were included. Linear mixed‑effects models accommodate missing outcome data under the missing‑at‑random assumption without excluding participants who had at least one follow‑up assessment.

CSF C3 and FH measurement

CSF samples were collected and processed following standardized ADNI protocols. Concentrations of C3 and FH were measured using multiplex immunoassays and expressed in nanograms per milliliter (ng/mL). The C3/FH ratio was calculated by dividing the C3 concentration by the FH concentration for each participant and served as the primary exposure variable representing complement balance. This ratio was pre‑specified, because it captures the net balance between complement activation (C3) and its negative regulation (FH) and may be more informative than either component alone.

Plasma Aβ42/40 measurement

Blood samples were collected and processed following standardized ADNI protocols. Plasma concentrations of Aβ42 and Aβ40 were measured using the automated Roche Elecsys immunoassay platform and expressed in picograms per milliliter (pg/mL). The Aβ42/40 ratio was calculated to minimize inter‐individual variability and served as an indicator of amyloid pathology.

Plasma total tau and p‐tau181 measurement

Plasma total tau (t‐tau) and p‐tau181 concentrations were measured using the fully automated Elecsys immunoassays (Roche Diagnostics). To harmonize measurements across different assay runs and plate batches, a linear scaling correction was applied using the UPENNBIOMK batch as the reference, following the procedure documented in the ADNI Biomarker Core's harmonization protocol. The correction was applied to all available visits (baseline and follow‑up). Final harmonized values were expressed in pg/mL. These measures served as indicators of non‑specific neuronal injury and AD‑related pathophysiology, respectively.

Cognitive composite scores

Standardized composite scores for memory (MEM), executive function (EXF), and language (LAN) were derived from the ADNI database. The composite scores were extracted directly from the ADNI database without recalculation. These composite scores were developed using modern psychometric approaches (item response theory, IRT) to provide robust and continuous measures of domain‑specific cognitive function. ADNI‑MEM encompasses multiple memory tasks, including Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT) learning, short‑delay recall, long‑delay recall, and recognition; Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale‐Cognitive Subscale (ADAS‑Cog) word recall; and Logical Memory I (immediate story recall) from the Wechsler Memory Scale. ADNI‑EXF integrates Trail Making Test Parts A and B, Digit Symbol Substitution (Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale Revised [WAIS‑R]), Category Fluency (animal naming), Vegetable Fluency, and Digit Span Backwards. ADNI‑Lan includes measures of Category Fluency (animal naming), Vegetable Fluency, and the Boston Naming Test. All composite scores were calculated using IRT‑based methods to ensure interval‑level measurement properties, with higher scores indicating better cognitive performance. Composite scores were extracted directly from the ADNI database using uniform scoring algorithms across all visits.

Data processing

The final analytical sample (n = 310) had complete baseline data for all key continuous variables. Longitudinal plasma p‑tau181 was missing in < 5% of all scheduled visits. APOE4 genotype was missing in 52 participants (13.4% of the initial CSF candidate pool, n = 389); these participants were therefore excluded from the final analytical sample, and complete‑case analysis was used for APOE4‑adjusted models (n = 310). For the primary longitudinal linear mixed‑effects models (LMMs), missing covariate values were handled via listwise deletion, which is the default implementation of the lmer function in R. Given the low missing proportion for the time‑varying covariate (< 5%), this approach is statistically robust and consistent with standard practices in longitudinal analyses.

All continuous variables (C3/FH ratio, plasma p‑tau181, Aβ42/40, cognitive scores, age, and education) were z‑score standardized to a mean of 0 and SD of 1 to ensure comparability of regression coefficients. Categorical variables retained their original coding. Distributions of baseline continuous variables were examined for extreme values; after z‑score standardization using the baseline sample (n = 310), no values exceeded ± 3.29 SD, so no outlier adjustment was applied. Longitudinal plasma p‑tau181 values were standardized separately using all repeated measurements (1,138 observations), yielding the range (−0.80 to 7.13 SD). Duplicate records with identical RID and VISITDATE were resolved by retaining the first entry and excluding subsequent duplicates. Reporting followed the STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline for observational studies.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27) and R software (version 4.5.1). Descriptive statistics were presented as medians with interquartile ranges for continuous variables and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Between‐group comparisons across diagnostic categories (CN, MCI, AD) were performed using Kruskal–Wallis tests for continuous variables and chi‐squared tests for categorical variables.

Decomposition analysis of C3 and FH was performed with C3 and FH entered simultaneously and mutually adjusted in relation to plasma p‑tau181. Covariates (age, sex, and education; plus diagnosis in cross‑sectional analyses involving the full cohort) were selected based on prior literature and clinical plausibility, not on data‑driven methods. Cross‑sectional linear regression analyses examined the association between baseline C3/FH and baseline memory function separately in the CN, MCI, and AD subgroups, adjusting for age, sex, and education. For the longitudinal MCI‑only models, diagnosis was not included as a covariate because the analysis was restricted to MCI participants.

Primary analyses included longitudinal moderation analysis in the MCI population: linear mixed‑effects models with random intercepts and random slopes were employed to examine the association between baseline C3/FH and the rate of change in longitudinal memory function over time, with longitudinal plasma p‑tau181 (measured at each follow‑up visit) included as a time‑varying moderator. Time‑varying p‑tau181 was standardized across all visits but was not person‑mean centered; thus, its effect reflects both between‑person and within‑person variation. Given the descriptive nature of this analysis, person‑mean centering was not applied. Time was modeled as a continuous variable (years since baseline visit). Plasma p‑tau181 and memory function were assessed within the same ADNI visit window. When assessments were not performed on the same calendar date, the closest available biomarker measurement within the same visit window was used; therefore, the time‑varying p‑tau181 value was paired with the memory assessment at the same visit. The three‑way interaction term (baseline C3/FH × time × longitudinal p‑tau181) was tested. The J‐N analysis was subsequently applied to identify the region of statistical significance, defined as the range of longitudinal p‑tau181 values where the conditional association of baseline C3/FH with memory decline is statistically significant. This region is statistical in nature and is not claimed to represent a biological threshold. The J‑N boundary was derived directly from the coefficients and standard errors of the fitted LMM; no confidence interval (CI) was estimated for this descriptive benchmark. Given the modest sample size of the MCI subgroup (n = 123) for a three‑way interaction model, estimates are accompanied by 95% CIs, and conclusions regarding the presence or absence of effects are based on interval precision rather than p‑values alone.

Post‑hoc exploratory subgroup analyses by clinical progression status (MCI‑to‑AD conversion) were performed using stratified LMMs with the same structure as the primary model. A four‑way interaction term (C3/FH × time × p‑tau181 × progression) was also tested. These analyses were not pre‑specified and should be interpreted with caution due to limited power.

Exploratory analyses included cross‑sectional mediation analysis: bootstrapping (5,000 replicates) was used to test the indirect effect of baseline CSF C3/FH on baseline memory function via baseline plasma p‑tau181. Additional exploratory analyses were presented in supplementary materials, including indirect associations of C3/FH with executive and language function via p‐tau181, parallel mediation analyses using t‑tau, and effect modification by Aβ42/40 levels. False discovery rate (FDR) correction (Benjamini–Hochberg) was applied to the tests for the decomposition analysis and the exploratory mediation tests across cognitive domains. The pre‑specified memory mediation and cross‑sectional subgroup analyses were not included in FDR correction.

Sensitivity analyses included additional adjustment for APOE ε4 genotype to assess robustness to genetic risk. Additional sensitivity analyses included complete‐case analysis and testing of different plasma p‐tau181 thresholds (0.70–0.85 SD) for the three‐way interaction. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used because continuous variables were not normally distributed. For linear regression models, residual plots were inspected for linearity, normality, and homoscedasticity; no major violations were observed. All LMMs converged without warning. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, and all tests were two‐tailed. This secondary analysis was not pre‑registered.

Decomposition analysis of C3 and FH

To understand the components of the C3/FH ratio, we examined their independent associations with plasma p‐tau181 (Table 2). Before mutual adjustment, neither C3 (β = −0.025, p = 0.646) nor FH (β = 0.079, p = 0.165) was significantly associated with plasma p‐tau181. However, after mutual adjustment, C3 showed a significant negative association (β = −0.231, q = 0.020) and FH showed a significant positive association (β = 0.269, q = 0.016) with plasma p‐tau181, indicating that the C3/FH ratio captures the opposing associations of complement activation and regulation. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) for C3 and FH in the mutually adjusted model were 2.93 and 3.13, respectively, indicating no severe collinearity (VIF < 5).

Cross‑sectional mediation analysis

Cross‐sectional mediation analysis using baseline data from the full cohort showed a significant indirect effect of CSF C3/FH on memory function consistent with an indirect pathway through plasma p‑tau181 (indirect effect β = 0.010, 95% CI [0.000, 0.023], p = 0.024; Figure 1). The direct effect was not significant (β = −0.041, p = 0.277).

Exploratory analyses across other cognitive domains (Table S1) showed that the indirect effects via p‑tau181 on executive function (average causal mediation effect [ACME] = 0.012, p = 0.039) and language (ACME = 0.010, p = 0.057) did not survive FDR correction. Parallel mediation analyses using t‑tau demonstrated a significant indirect effect on memory after FDR correction (ACME = 0.015, q = 0.020).

Cross‑sectional analyses in CN, MCI, and AD subgroups

We next examined whether baseline C3/FH was cross‑sectionally associated with memory function at any diagnostic stage. In separate linear regression models for CN (n = 84), MCI (n = 123), and AD (n = 103) subgroups, no significant association was observed in any group (p > 0.05; Table S2), indicating that the cross‑sectional mediation pattern in the pooled sample did not yield detectable stage‑specific correlations.

Longitudinal p‑tau181 modifies the association of baseline C3/FH with memory decline in MCI

Given that cross‑sectional associations were not detected, we next investigated whether baseline C3/FH was associated with the rate of memory decline over time, and whether this association was moderated by time‑varying plasma p‑tau181. In the MCI subgroup (n = 123), LMMs revealed a significant three‑way interaction of baseline C3/FH × time × longitudinal p‑tau181 on memory decline (Figure 2; β = 0.016, 95% CI [0.002, 0.030], p = 0.023). For visualization, participants were stratified into three groups based on baseline p‑tau181 z‑score (low ≤ 10th percentile, medium 10th–90th percentile, high > 90th percentile); these cutoffs are for illustrative purposes only, as all longitudinal analyses treated p‑tau181 as continuous. The direction of the effect varied by p‑tau181 level: higher baseline C3/FH was associated with faster decline at low p‑tau181 but slower decline at high p‑tau181.

J‐N region for longitudinal p‐tau181

The J‐N analysis identified a single critical boundary at 0.76 SD; the conditional effect of baseline C3/FH on memory decline became statistically significant above this value and remained significant across the observed p‑tau181 range in the analytic sample (up to 5.53 SD; Figure 3A; significant region: p‑tau181 > 0.76). This significance boundary is statistical and should not be equated with a biological or clinical cutoff. The continuous interaction plot (Figure 3B) showed that the association between baseline C3/FH and memory decline shifted from a more negative slope at lower p‑tau181 levels toward a positive slope at higher p‑tau181 levels, with no abrupt stepwise change observed. This pattern is consistent with gradual modulation of the association by p‑tau181 rather than a discrete threshold.

Amyloid pathology does not modify the C3/FH–p‐tau181 association

In exploratory analyses, plasma Aβ42/40 was examined as a continuous moderator of the C3/FH–p‑tau181 association in the full sample (n = 310). The interaction term (C3/FH × Aβ42/40) was not significant (p for interaction = 0.883). For visual illustration only, participants were split by the median Aβ42/40 value (Figure S1); however, no statistical inference is drawn from this median split. Given the exploratory nature, this null finding should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of no effect modification.

Subgroup analysis by clinical progression status

Of 123 MCI participants, 49 progressed to AD and 74 remained stable. Stratified models showed no significant three‑way interaction in either group (Stable: β = 0.010, 95% CI [−0.003, 0.024], p = 0.142; Progressor: β = 0.018, 95% CI [−0.013, 0.048], p = 0.261), and the four‑way interaction (C3/FH × time × p‑tau181 × progression) was not significant (β = 0.008, 95% CI [−0.022, 0.038], p = 0.593), indicating progression status did not significantly modify the effect.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were broadly consistent with the primary findings (Table S3). The indirect effect remained significant after additional adjustment for APOE ε4 genotype, and the three‐way interaction effect remained consistent after APOE ε4 adjustment and in complete‐case analysis. Complete‑case analysis yielded a similar J‑N boundary (0.78 SD), confirming the robustness of the primary findings to the method of missing data handling. When examining different plasma p‐tau181 thresholds, the three‐way interaction effect estimates increased with higher thresholds but did not reach statistical significance. This pattern does not contradict the J‑N region identified at > 0.76 SD; rather, it reflects the limited power of dichotomized analyses and supports the continuous, gradual nature of the moderation pattern.

DISCUSSION

This study investigated the association of baseline CSF C3/FH ratio with longitudinal memory decline in relation to plasma p‑tau181 in 123 MCI participants from ADNI. The primary findings are: (1) time‑varying p‑tau181 significantly moderates the association of baseline C3/FH with memory decline; (2) a J‐N boundary at 0.76 SD defines the region where this conditional association becomes significant; (3) cross‑sectional analyses across CN, MCI, and AD showed no significant C3/FH‑memory associations, in marked contrast to the longitudinal moderation in MCI; and (4) cross‑sectional exploratory analysis showed an indirect effect consistent with a mediation pattern. These findings suggest that complement balance is associated with AD‑related cognitive decline in conjunction with p‑tau181–related pathophysiology.

Aging itself is a powerful driver of complement dysregulation and cognitive decline, raising the possibility that age‑related complement activation could contribute to memory decline in addition to tau pathology. 12 , 13 Nevertheless, even after statistical adjustment for chronological age, our findings remained significant, suggesting that the observed complement‑tau interaction is not simply explained by age alone. Future studies with longitudinal biospecimen collection across the aging spectrum are needed to further disentangle these pathways.

The decomposition analysis revealed that C3 and FH exhibit independent and opposing associations with plasma p‑tau181 after mutual adjustment (C3 negatively associated, FH positively associated). This supports the interpretation that the C3/FH ratio captures the balance between complement activation and its negative regulation, an effect masked when each protein is considered alone. Previous studies have demonstrated that lower levels of C3 and FH individually predict faster cognitive decline in MCI. 14 , 15 However, these prior studies did not simultaneously model C3 and FH to assess their joint associations with tau pathology. The opposing effects observed align with the established biology of the complement system, in which FH serves as the primary negative regulator of the alternative pathway, determining the net level of complement activation in concert with C3. 16 Evidence from animal models further supports this interpretation: C3 deficiency mitigates neurodegeneration in tauopathy mice, and C3 levels in AD patient CSF correlate with tau pathology. 17 Together, these findings suggest that the balance between C3 and FH, rather than the absolute level of either component, may better reflect complement–tau interplay in the context of AD‑related pathophysiology.

The J‐N analysis identified a single critical boundary at 0.76 SD, indicating that the conditional association of baseline C3/FH with memory decline became detectable above this value and remained significant across the observed p‑tau181 range in the analytic sample (up to 5.53 SD). The shifting direction of the effect—from faster decline at lower p‑tau181 to slower decline at higher p‑tau181—suggests that complement's association with cognition may transition as p‑tau181 levels increase. 18 , 19 Notably, this boundary lies within the observed p‑tau181 range, and the gradual nature of this transition argues against a discrete biological threshold.

Toledo et al. demonstrated that lower CSF C3 and FH levels predict faster global cognitive decline in MCI, although they did not find significant domain‑specific effects for memory or executive function. 14 The present study extends this by showing that the complement–cognition association is primarily driven by memory function, with exploratory analyses showing non‑significant effects on executive function and language after FDR correction. This specificity is consistent with the established topography of tau pathology in AD, which follows a stereotypical progression from the entorhinal cortex to limbic regions and then to neocortex, as described in the classical Braak staging scheme. 20 Memory impairment is the core clinical feature of AD, and tau accumulation in temporal regions has been shown to predict memory decline. 21 , 22 The selective association with memory function is therefore biologically plausible for an indirect pathway involving complement, p‑tau, and memory. 23 An exploratory subgroup analysis by progression status showed no significant four‑way interaction, although the small subgroup sizes limit the interpretability of this finding.

The parallel mediation analysis showed significant indirect associations via both plasma p‑tau181 and total tau, suggesting that complement balance may relate to cognitive function through both tau‑related pathways and broader neuronal injury. Total tau is a well‑established marker of neuronal injury and synaptic dysfunction. 24 The indirect association involving t‑tau could reflect complement‑related neurodegeneration. 25 , 26 The link between complement proteins and p‑tau181 has been reported across various cohorts. 27 Rizzo et al. further demonstrated that higher CSF p‑tau181 levels were associated with elevated complement factors in both cognitively normal controls and AD patients. 28 Together, these findings are consistent with the possibility that complement balance may be associated with cognitive decline through p‑tau181–related pathways. The opposing directions of the indirect and direct effects observed in the cross‑sectional analysis suggest a suppression pattern rather than a simple mediation mechanism; therefore, the proportion mediated statistic was not interpreted.

The null cross‑sectional findings across CN, MCI, and AD, together with the significant longitudinal moderation in MCI, highlight a distinction between cognitive state and trajectory. Complement balance does not simply track current performance but is associated with the dynamic process of subsequent decline during the prodromal phase. First, in AD dementia, the complement‑cognition relationship was undetectable cross‑sectionally, which may reflect that neuronal loss overshadows complement's contribution in more advanced stages. Second, these findings generate the hypothesis that complement balance may be most relevant during the prodromal phase. 14 If confirmed in future studies, the MCI stage could represent a promising window for complement‑targeted therapies.

Plasma Aβ42/40 did not significantly modify the complement‑tau association in the current cohort. In primary tauopathies, which lack amyloid pathology, tau pathology alone is associated with complement activation. 29 Conversely, an amyloid‑dependent complement pathway has also been reported. 30 A stage‑dependent shift in complement's role, from early neuroprotection to late neurotoxicity, has been proposed. 31 Together, these findings suggest that complement–tau interactions can occur through both amyloid‑independent and amyloid‑dependent pathways, with their relative contributions likely varying across disease stages.

Our findings should not be interpreted as a direct replication of Bonham et al. Their study examined APOE ε4 × C3 interactions and reported that CSF C3 was associated with CSF p‑tau primarily after accounting for CSF amyloid levels, whereas our exploratory analysis tested whether plasma Aβ42/40 modified the C3/FH–p‐tau181 association in the full sample. Given these substantial differences—distinct interaction terms, different biofluids, different analytical frameworks (mediation vs. moderation), and cross‑sectional vs. longitudinal designs—the null C3/FH × Aβ42/40 interaction in our study neither confirms nor contradicts the amyloid‑dependent association reported by Bonham et al. Nevertheless, despite these methodological distinctions, both studies converge on the broader finding that complement dysregulation is intertwined with AD core pathology, particularly tau. This convergence, rather than direct replication, is consistent with the growing recognition that complement–tau crosstalk may represent a conserved feature of AD pathophysiology across different analytical contexts and biofluids. 28 , 29

First, the analytical hierarchy distinguishing primary from exploratory analyses helped limit Type I error in the interpretation of multiple tests. Second, mutual adjustment of C3 and FH revealed opposing associations with p‑tau181, supporting the C3/FH ratio as a measure of complement balance. Third, the J‐N approach identified a continuous significance region, avoiding arbitrary p‑tau181 dichotomization. Fourth, the inclusion of cross‑sectional analyses alongside the longitudinal MCI model allowed direct comparison of state‑dependent versus trajectory‑dependent associations, strengthening the interpretation of complement balance as a trajectory‑related rather than a state biomarker.

Several limitations should be considered. The observational design precludes causal inference, and the cross‐sectional mediation analysis cannot confirm a causal pathway. The MCI subgroup size (n = 123) was determined by biomarker availability rather than formal power calculation, limiting the precision of estimates for the three‑way interaction model. The post‑hoc progression subgroup analysis was exploratory and underpowered. Additionally, classifying participants by future outcomes may introduce conditioning on future events. Batch effects across historical ADNI phases and variations in the underlying cognitive test batteries may introduce non‐differential measurement error. Plasma p‑tau181 measured by the Roche Elecsys platform is more closely associated with Aβ‑PET than with tau‑PET; more tau‑selective markers (e.g., p‑tau217 or tau PET) were unavailable, so our findings should be interpreted as associations with AD‑related pathophysiology rather than tau pathology alone. Unmeasured confounding (e.g., vascular burden, immunosenescence) cannot be fully excluded. In the CSF substudy, no CN participants progressed to MCI during follow‑up, precluding analysis of complement‑related CN‑to‑MCI progression. Finally, the predominantly Caucasian, well‑educated ADNI cohort may limit generalizability. Despite these caveats, sensitivity analyses support the robustness of the primary findings.

Future studies should validate these findings in independent, diverse cohorts with larger samples and longer follow‑up. Incorporation of tau PET imaging would enable more direct examination of complement balance and regional tau deposition. Experimental studies are needed to establish the mechanistic basis for the observed complement‑tau interaction and to determine whether the C3/FH ratio represents a modifiable therapeutic target. Given the emerging evidence for complement involvement in AD pathogenesis, clinical trials of complement‑modulating therapies should consider stratifying participants by baseline p‑tau181 levels (recognizing its mixed amyloid‑tau signal), as the present findings suggest that treatment effects may vary depending on disease stage.

CONCLUSION

This study found that baseline CSF C3/FH ratio was associated with longitudinal memory decline in MCI in relation to plasma p‑tau181 levels, with a statistical interaction boundary identified at 0.76 SD across the observed p‑tau181 range. In contrast to the null cross‑sectional findings across all diagnostic groups, the longitudinal moderation in MCI suggests that complement balance is associated with the rate of memory decline rather than current cognitive status. These findings provide a hypothesis for future studies examining complement‑p‑tau181 interactions in prodromal AD, with the caveat that plasma p‑tau181 reflects a mixed amyloid‑tau signal.
