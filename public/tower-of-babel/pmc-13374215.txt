Enhancing patient interview and clinical examination training: the impact of the Simroid dental training robot
Authors: Yao Chen, Wentao Peng, Yanmei Dai, Xin Lin, Huiru Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to explore the effects of the Simroid dental training robot on the training of undergraduate dental students in patient interviews and clinical examinations.MethodsUndergraduate students who participated in internship rotations in the Department of Endodontics at Tianjin Stomatological Hospital from July 2023 to June 2025 were enrolled as the research participants. This study adopted a non-randomized controlled design, dividing students from different academic years into two groups: the control group received conventional training, while the experimental group utilized the Simroid patient robot for patient interview and clinical examination training. The effectiveness of the training was evaluated using the Mini Clinical Exercise Evaluation (Mini-CEX) and a learning satisfaction questionnaire. Statistical analyses were conducted through independent samples t-test and repeated measures ANOVA.ResultsThe experimental group achieved significantly higher assessment scores in both clinical examination and doctor-patient communication compared to the control group (P < 0.05). Additionally, the experimental group reported higher levels of learning satisfaction.ConclusionThis study demonstrated that incorporating the Simroid dental training robot into patient interview and clinical examination training significantly enhanced undergraduate students’ clinical examination skills and doctor-patient communication abilities. By enabling students to proficiently master patient interview process and experience realistic doctor-patient communication scenarios during preclinical training, this approach effectively prepared them for subsequent clinical internships.Keywords: Simroid dental training robot, Patient interview, Clinical examination, Doctor-patient communication, Mini-CEX, Learning satisfaction

Introduction

In the field of dental education, the development of effective training methods is crucial for preparing undergraduate dental students to become competent and confident practitioners. Patient interview and clinical examination are fundamental skills that dental students must acquire to provide high-quality patient care [1, 2]. These skills involve not only the accurate assessment of oral health conditions but also the establishment of effective doctor-patient communication, which is essential for building trust, understanding patient needs, and ensuring treatment compliance [3].

Traditional training methods for patient interview and clinical examination typically rely on didactic lectures, role-playing exercises, and supervised clinical practice with actural patients. Although these methods have been widely used, they have certain limitations [4–6]. Didactic lectures may lack the practical application and interactivity needed for students to fully grasp the skills. Role-playing exercises, although useful, may not accurately replicate the real-life dynamics and pressure of a clinical setting. Furthermore, relying solely on real patients for training can be challenging due to ethical considerations, patient availability, and the potential for causing discomfort or harm to patients during the practicing process.

To address these limitations, there has been a growing interest in the use of simulation-based training in dental education [7–9]. Simulation-based training provides a safe and controlled environment for students to practice and enhance their skills without the risks associated with real patients. Previous studies have demonstrated the efficacy of simulation-based training in improving dental students’ technical skills, including cavity preparation and endodontic procedures [10–13]. Among the various simulation tools available, the Simroid dental training robot has emerged as a particularly promising technology [14, 15]. The Simroid is a highly realistic humanoid robot designed specifically for dental training, capable of simulating a wide range of oral conditions and patient responses. It can mimic facial expressions, vocalizations, and body movements, providing students with a realistic and immersive training experience. This study aims to investigate the effects of the Simroid on undergraduate dental students’ oral examination skills and doctor-patient communication abilities during patient interview and clinical examination training. The findings of this study will contribute to the development of more effective and innovative training methods in dental education, ultimately improving the quality of patient care provided by future dental practitioners.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the ethics committee of Tianjin Stomatological Hospital, School of Medicine, Nankai University (No.PH2023-B-007).

Research subjects

This study employed a non-randomized controlled design. The control group consisted of 29 students who commenced their internships in July 2023, while the experimental group comprised 34 students who started their internships in July 2024. Undergraduate students who underwent internship rotations in the Department of Endodontics at Tianjin Stomatological Hospital, School of Medicine, Nankai University from July 2023 to June 2025 were selected as the research subjects. The control group consisted of 29 students who commenced their internships in July 2023, while the experimental group comprised 34 students who started their internships in July 2024. All students were from the same major (fifth-year dentistry) at the same university and followed an identical curriculum. There were no significant differences in age and gender distribution between the two groups of interns (P > 0.05). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Successful completion of the endodontics theoretical course; (2) No prior clinical practical experience before the start of the study; (3) Voluntary agreement and signing of the informed consent form. The exclusion criteria included: (1) Students with severe physical illnesses or any health conditions that might affect their energy levels or attendance for extended clinical internships; (2) Those currently participating in other research or training programs that could potentially interact with the teaching effects of this study; (3) Students with known and actively treated severe mental disorders whose conditions, as judged by a psychiatrist, might impair their normal functioning or safety in high-pressure clinical environments. Data collection relied on students’ “Health Declaration Forms” filled out at enrollment, where they truthfully reported major health problems and other research participation. The research team made judgments based on this self-reported info and clinical knowledge. No specialized physiological or psychological screenings were conducted to protect privacy, following common low-risk educational research practices. Exclusion decisions prioritized students’ health and safety in the study’s demanding clinical environment.

Research methods

The entire study was carried out within the Endodontics Department of Tianjin Stomatological Hospital, following a unified teaching syllabus and rotation schedule. The core clinical teaching faculty remained consistent throughout the study period, minimizing disruptions from changes in the teaching environment. Prior to their entry into the study, we retrieved and compared the theoretical Endodontics course scores of both the control group and experimental groups. Upon joining the department, students in both groups underwent comprehensive orientation training to familiarize themselves with the clinical setting and expectations. The teaching approach comprised a series of theoretical mini-lectures delivered by clinical instructors, aimed at providing theoretical guidance to enhance students’ clinical consultation skills. These lectures covered essential topics such as establishing rapport with patients, conducting systematic medical history taking, performing standardized intra-oral and extra-oral examinations, effectively communicating preliminary findings and treatment plans to patients, and accurately documenting medical records. During the initial two weeks of their rotation, students primarily observed their clinical instructors during patient reception and operational procedures.

Students in the control group were allowed to practice clinical examination on a dental manikin, while those in the experimental group were trained using the Simroid dental training robot. For the experimental group, the clinical instructors pre-compiled medical record scripts for the Simroid dental training robot, including diseases such as dental caries, acute pulpitis, and acute periapical periodontitis. The specific training process was as follows:

Simulated consultation practice (20 min): Students interacted with the Simroid, conducting extra-oral and intra-oral examinations, explaining the oral condition to the Simroid, and proposing a treatment plan.

The Simroid robot was capable of engaging in structured interactions with students through both verbal communication and facial expressions. It was equipped with pre-programmed medical history scripts and preset verbal responses to students’ questions. Additionally, it could simulate patient reactions to clinical examinations by means of facial expressions and vocalizations. The robot could be “interviewed.” Students were able to ask it questions, just as they would with real patients, to collect medical history information. Its responses were based on disease scripts pre-selected by teachers, providing standardized sources of medical history. Moreover, the robot could display specific pathological signs for students to identify.

After two weeks of observation and simulated practice, all students in both the control and experimental groups began to perform real patient interview under the supervision and guidance of their clinical instructors. Mini-CEX scale evaluations were scheduled for both groups at the beginning of the third week and at the end of the eighth week. The two groups were consistent in terms of the frequency and timing of orientation training and assessments. Kappa statistical analysis was used to assess the consistency among all clinical instructors (Fig. 1).

At the beginning of the assessment, the instructor selected appropriate patients according to preset criteria. These criteria were designed to ensure standardization of the assessment and expose all students to clinical challenges of similar difficulty. Students then conducted patient interviews and clinical examinations. Throughout these diagnostic and therapeutic procedures, the instructor observed from beside the dental chair, generally not interrupting unless patient safety was at risk. During the assessment, the instructor recorded and scored the students’ actions according to the Mini-CEX scale and promptly provided constructive feedback, along with tailored suggestions on communication skills.

Regarding the quality control, assessors adopted a blinded scoring approach, and the Mini-CEX assessment scores were all completed by clinical teaching faculty, excluding the authors. Before assessment, instead of direct scoring, we organized specialized training for 2 participating assessors. Detailed benchmark consensus discussions on each dimension and rating level of the modified Mini-CEX scale were held, and standardized training videos were used for calibration exercises. Mini-CEX assessments were conducted during real clinical consultations, with assessors mainly acting as “observers”. The assessment scoring indicators were relatively objective and clear.

During the course of this study, the control group underwent two Mini-CEX evaluations. The first assessment was arranged on the day of their first clinical session after entering the department, while the second assessment took place on the final day of the research observation period. In contrast, the experimental group was evaluated three times using the Mini-CEX. The first assessment was conducted immediately after the training with the Simroid dental training robot, with the subsequent two assessments aligning with those administered to the control group (Fig. 2).

Teaching satisfaction survey

In the study, a self-designed teaching satisfaction survey was employed. To address the concern of lacking validation from existing instruments, its design was grounded in the well-established Kirkpatrick Training Evaluation Model in medical education, focusing on the “reaction” level. Considering the specific teaching objectives of endodontics in this research, the survey encompassed key dimensions such as the effectiveness in simulating clinical situations to assess if the training environment mirrors real-life settings, the enhancement of learning interest to measure students’ enthusiasm and engagement, and the boosting of confidence to evaluate students’ self-assurance in clinical abilities, aiming to comprehensively gauge students’ satisfaction with the teaching methods. An anonymous questionnaire was distributed and collected immediately on the final day of the study via the Wenjuanxing software platform to all students in the experimental group. The content of the questionnaire focused on evaluating the recognition of using the Simroid dental training robot, including effectively simulating clinical patient reception scenarios, enhancing learning interest, strengthening theoretical knowledge, boosting confidence in clinical patient reception and improving doctor-patient communication skills. The evaluation questions were presented in a single-choice format with a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly disagree, 2 = Disagree, 3 = Neutral, 4 = Agree, 5 = Strongly agree).

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 software was used for graphing and data processing. Independent samples t-test or repeated-measures analysis of variance was used to analyze the scores of the scoring items. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline theoretical knowledge comparison

To ensure the comparability of the two groups at the start of the study, we retrieved and compared their scores in the theoretical Endodontics course prior to their entry. An independent samples t test was conducted for this purpose. The results revealed that the experimental group had a mean score of 79.15 with a standard deviation of 6.28, while the control group had a mean score of 79.86 with a standard deviation of 8.63. The P value was 0.700, indicating that there was no statistically significant difference in the baseline theoretical knowledge between the experimental and control groups.

Mini-CEX assessment scores

The first Mini-CEX assessment for both groups was conducted at the beginning of the third week following their entry into the department. The results were presented in Table 2. The experimental group outperformed the control group overall, especially in history taking, clinical examination, doctor - patient communication, and medical record writing (P < 0.05), with no significant differences in diagnosis and comprehensive ability (Fig. 3A). Specifically, apart from diagnosis and comprehensive ability, the experimental group scored higher in other items, with a highly significant difference in total scores (P < 0.001). The experimental group (76.94 ± 4.84) scored 7.49 points higher than the control group (69.45 ± 3.61), with a 95% CI of 5.36–9.63 and a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.73), indicating substantial practical significance.

Discussion

Patient interview and clinical examination training is a vital part of undergraduate dental education, laying the foundation for students’ clinical reasoning, communication skills, and professional competence [16]. However, the transition from student to clinician brings challenges in educational, clinical, and social aspects, such as inconsistent exposure to diverse cases, ethical dilemmas in invasive procedures on real patients, and variable feedback quality during supervised rotations [4, 5]. These issues hinder skill acquisition and may compromise early-career confidence and professional identity formation.

To address these educational gaps, this study explored the efficacy of the Simroid dental training robot in enhancing undergraduate dental students’ performance in patient interviews and clinical examinations. Unlike static mannequins or virtual reality platforms, Simroid features lifelike facial expressions, speech recognition, and programmable physiological responses, enabling dynamic interactions and adaptation to unexpected clinical challenges in a controlled setting [14, 15].

In this study, the proficiency of dental students in clinical examinations significantly improved, which was consistent with previous research findings [17]. The reasons are as follows: Firstly, simulation training enables students to actively learn about the typical oral diseases signs and symptoms, enhancing their subjective initiative. Secondly, compared to conventional mannequin-based simulators, the Simroid system provides more realistic stimulus responses and reflexes, enabling students to experience various patient reactions during treatment. This realistic experience helps students handle unexpected situations better, aligning with the need to improve their confidence in dealing with patient safety risks, as identified in the study and previous questionnaire surveys [14, 18]. Repeated emphasis on clinical reception details during simulation training reinforces students’ memory, ultimately improving their clinical consultation abilities. After initial simulation training, students in the experimental group showed significant improvements in clinical examination and doctor-patient communication skills, with more standardized clinical examination procedures, leading to better clinical operation results.

Effective doctor-patient communication is essential for establishing a harmonious relationship, especially in dentistry where patients often feel anxious and fearful [19–22]. Standardized patients offer repeated practice opportunities, promoting theoretical understanding and clinical thinking development in a real-life clinical environment [23]. They also help cultivate clinical thinking and communication skills, and establish a harmonious doctor-patient relationship [24]. However, their application faces many challenges [25]. Virtual reality haptic simulators have been used in many dental schools in doctor-patient communication training, often fail to replicate the nuanced emotional responses and spontaneous interactions of real patients, which are crucial for effective communication training [10, 11]. The clinical reception module of this system helps students familiarize themselves with the reception process, cultivate disease classification abilities, and comprehensively train clinical diagnostic thinking, facilitating a smooth transition from laboratory teaching to clinical practice. Despite its advanced pain-sensing capabilities, the Simroid patient robot has limitations in replicating the full spectrum of human emotional responses and spontaneous interactions, potentially hindering comprehensive communication training for dental students.

Traditional training models have advantages and disadvantages. On the positive side, they are cost-effective, as methods like didactic lectures and role-playing exercises require minimal investment in specialized equipment. Instructors and students are also often more familiar with these traditional approaches, which facilitates smoother implementation and quicker adoption. Moreover, traditional methods can be easily adapted to various educational settings and resource constraints, offering flexibility. However, they have drawbacks. Didactic lectures and role-playing may lack the realism to accurately replicate the real-life dynamics and pressure of a clinical setting, potentially limiting the transferability of skills to actual patient care. Relying solely on real patients for training can raise ethical issues, present patient availability challenges, and pose risks of causing discomfort or harm during the learning process. Additionally, the quality of feedback in traditional training can vary significantly depending on the instructor’s experience and the student’s performance, leading to inconsistent learning outcomes.

The new training model using the Simroid dental training robot also has its pros and cons. It offers high realism, providing a highly immersive training experience by simulating a wide range of oral conditions and patient responses, including facial expressions, vocalizations, and body movements. Simulation-based training creates a controlled and safe environment for students to practice and enhance their skills without the risks associated with real patients, reducing ethical concerns and potential harm. Standardized feedback, ensured by tools like the Mini-CEX, facilitates more uniform skill development among students. The realistic interactions with the Simroid robot also help students develop effective doctor-patient communication skills, which are crucial for building trust and ensuring treatment compliance. Nevertheless, this model has disadvantages such as a high initial cost for equipment acquisition and maintenance, which may be a barrier for some educational institutions. The advanced technology of the Simroid robot may also require additional training for instructors to integrate it into the curriculum and troubleshoot technical issues. Furthermore, while it can simulate various patient responses, it may have limitations in replicating the full spectrum of human emotional responses and spontaneous interactions, potentially hindering comprehensive communication training. In the long run, considering the teaching benefits, although the Simroid robot-assisted training demands higher investment, it can provide a highly simulated clinical scenario and a safe environment for repeated practice, significantly enhancing students’ confidence in doctor-patient communication and clinical operations. From the perspective of long-term teaching effectiveness, this technology can serve as an effective supplement to traditional teaching, especially in cultivating students’ core clinical reception abilities.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that demand careful consideration. Firstly, the non-randomized, non-concurrent control design introduces inherent biases that cannot be fully eliminated despite our efforts to standardize the syllabus, assessment criteria, and clinical rotation procedures. Although baseline theoretical scores were similar between groups from the same institution, unmeasured differences across academic years, such as variations in instructor teams, minor curriculum modifications, or changes in the hospital environment, may still confound the results. Consequently, the findings must be interpreted with caution. Secondly, the exclusion criteria relied primarily on students’ self-reported information rather than objective medical evaluations or standardized psychological assessments. While this approach respects privacy and enhances practicality, it risks overlooking undiagnosed or undisclosed health issues, thereby introducing selection bias. Thirdly, the study’s short-term, single-center design limits the generalizability of its results. Validation through multi-center trials with longer follow-up periods is essential to confirm the findings. Additionally, the evaluation of teaching effectiveness also focused narrowly on skills and short-term satisfaction, neglecting long-term knowledge retention and sustained impacts on clinical performance. Finally, while the study confirmed the teaching effectiveness of the Simroid training robot, its widespread adoption in undergraduate dental education faces practical challenges. Cost remains a significant barrier, as the system’s initial purchase and maintenance expenses far exceed those of traditional simulation head models. However, robot simulation offers repeatable, standardized, risk-free clinical scenarios that could reduce teaching time wasted on unskilled operations and potentially lower real-world clinical errors by enhancing early-stage skills, suggesting long-term benefits. To improve feasibility, institutions could adopt a phased implementation strategy, such as sharing the robot as a core facility across departments or collaborating with regional teaching centers to distribute costs, thereby maximizing its educational value.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the use of the Simroid dental training robot in clinical reception training can significantly improve undergraduate students’ clinical examination abilities and doctor-patient communication skills. This training method not only helps students master the reception process but also enables them to experience doctor-patient communication scenarios more realistically in the preclinical stage, thereby cultivating their courage to face patients and adequately preparing them for clinical practice. In addition, this simulation training can stimulate learning enthusiasm, clarify learning goals, and allow students to continuously improve their communication skills and self-confidence through interaction with the Simroid dental training robot, enhancing their professional identity. Therefore, this training method has a significant promoting effect on the teaching of Endodontics and is recommended for implementation in undergraduate dental education.
