Advances in Radiotherapy for Neoadjuvant Therapy of Stage III Non‐Small Cell Lung Cancer
Authors: Qinrong Mao, Yuanchun Yu, Yan Fang, Shaofeng Zhu, Chengjin Tang, Jing Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis review aims to systematically summarize the mechanisms of action, clinical evolution, and application prospects of radiotherapy in neoadjuvant therapy for stage III non‐small cell lung cancer. Focusing on three core techniques: spatially fractionated radiotherapy, stereotactic body radiotherapy, and low‐dose radiotherapy, key study data were screened across different treatment eras. Data extraction covers the unique mechanisms of the three radiotherapy modalities, as well as the efficacy and safety of treatment regimens before and after the immunotherapy era, including pathological response rate, survival benefit, adverse reactions, and other core endpoints. Comprehensive data analysis indicates that the three radiotherapy techniques have distinct characteristics; radioimmunotherapy synergy and triple regimens in the immunotherapy era significantly improve pathological response rates and long‐term disease control. Spatially fractionated radiotherapy demonstrates unique advantages due to its low toxicity and dual mechanisms of action. In conclusion, radiotherapy shows prominent value in neoadjuvant therapy for stage III non‐small cell lung cancer, and spatially fractionated radiotherapy is expected to become an important therapeutic option. However, its optimal dose‐fractionation regimen, standardized procedures, and other issues still require further validation by multicenter randomized controlled trials to promote the development of more precise, safe, and effective treatments.Keywords: immunotherapy, neoadjuvant therapy, non‐small cell lung cancer, radiotherapy, spatially fractionated radiotherapy, stereotactic body radiotherapy

Introduction: Disease Background and Current Diagnosis and Treatment Status

Non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is the leading cause of malignant tumor‐related mortality worldwide [1]. In China, the annual new cases of Stage III NSCLC exceed 200,000, and most patients present at the locally advanced stage at initial diagnosis [2]. According to the extent of tumor invasion, patterns of lymph node metastasis, and surgical resectability, Stage III NSCLC can be categorized into three groups: unresectable cases (e.g., tumor invasion of major vessels, multi‐station N2 metastasis) are treated with concurrent chemoradiotherapy plus immunological consolidation as the standard regimen [3]; initially resectable cases meet surgical indications but carry a high 5‐year postoperative recurrence rate with surgery alone [4]; potentially resectable cases are associated with large tumor volume (e.g., primary tumor ≥ 5 cm), severe local invasion (e.g., chest wall or mediastinal invasion), or extensive regional lymph node metastasis, making R0 resection difficult at initial surgery. Neoadjuvant therapy is therefore required to reduce tumor burden and downstage pathologically to facilitate curative resection [5]. However, a fully unified consensus on the above classification has not yet been reached, and some controversies remain [6]. Notably, for potentially resectable Stage III NSCLC patients who refuse or are unable to undergo surgery for various reasons after neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, radiotherapy can serve as a feasible alternative to surgery [7]. For patients eligible for surgery, radical resection is still recommended as the priority. The core principle of this strategy is to improve resection rate and patient prognosis. In fact, neoadjuvant therapy represents a central component for initially resectable, potentially resectable, and even inconsistently classified Stage III NSCLC. Its primary objectives are to increase surgical resection rate and eliminate residual micrometastases. With its precise local tumor control capability, radiotherapy has shown increasingly prominent synergistic value in neoadjuvant therapy [8].

Antitumor Mechanisms of Neoadjuvant Radiotherapy

The antitumor effects of neoadjuvant radiotherapy are not limited to direct tumor cell killing but also achieve multidimensional synergistic effects by regulating the tumor microenvironment and activating antitumor immune responses. The mechanisms of action differ significantly among distinct radiotherapy techniques.

Stereotactic Body Radiation: Effective NSCLC Killing Mechanism With Accurate High Dose

Stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT), as a precise radiotherapy technique characterized by high‐dose and hypofractionated delivery, exhibits distinct advantages in cancer treatment owing to its excellent target localization accuracy and steep dose gradients. Its antitumor mechanisms are not limited to single direct cytotoxicity, but involve systematic effects across multiple dimensions including tumor microenvironment remodeling, immunomodulation, and the abscopal effect [9], thereby establishing a synergistic bridge between local tumor control and systemic antitumor immune activation (Figure 1).

Immune Remodeling of the Tumor Microenvironment

SBRT enables effective remodeling of the tumor microenvironment, reversing the immunosuppressive state induced by conventional radiotherapy and driving the transition from “cold tumors” to “hot tumors”, which represents a key mechanism underlying its synergy with immunotherapy. SBRT can elicit immunogenic cell death in tumor cells, leading to the release of tumor‐associated antigens and damage‐associated molecular patterns such as calreticulin, HMGB1, and ATP [10]. These molecules effectively promote the maturation and antigen presentation of dendritic cells, which in turn activates the infiltration of cytotoxic T lymphocytes into tumor sites [11]. It significantly increases the infiltration of B cells, central memory T cells, and effector CD8+ T cells in the tumor microenvironment, while reducing the proportion of regulatory T cells, elevating the CD8+/Treg ratio [12] and optimizing the immune cell composition within the tumor microenvironment. Furthermore, SBRT stimulates the production of chemokines including CXCL9/10/16 and interferon‐related cytokines in the tumor microenvironment. Through the recruitment function of these molecules, activated T cells infiltrate the tumor microenvironment more efficiently, further amplifying the local immune response [13].

Immunomodulation

Meanwhile, SBRT upregulates the expression of ICAM‐1, MHC‐I, and the death receptor Fas on the surface of tumor cells in a dose‐dependent manner [14]. Among these, MHC‐I provides the essential co‐stimulatory signal for CD8+ T cell activation [15], ICAM‐1 facilitates the adhesion and migration of immune cells [16], and Fas directly initiates the apoptotic pathway in tumor cells, thereby enhancing the efficacy of immune attack through multiple dimensions. In addition, SBRT upregulates the expression of immune checkpoints such as PD‐L1 on tumor cells [16] and VISTA on T cells [17]. Although this may exert mild inhibitory effects on T cell function in the short term [18], it renders tumor cells more sensitive to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), establishing an important molecular basis for their combined application.

Abscopal Effect

The immunomodulatory effects of SBRT can also induce the abscopal effect. Local immunity activated by SBRT can initiate systemic immune responses, and the resulting bioactive immune mediators can suppress non‐irradiated distant metastatic lesions [19], thereby overcoming the limitations of local treatment. The incidence of this effect is significantly increased when combined with immunotherapy, forming a synergistic pattern of local tumor control and systemic immune elimination that eradicates primary tumors and acts on micrometastases [20]. This constitutes the core advantageous mechanism of SBRT combined with immunotherapy in the treatment of NSCLC.

Low‐Dose Radiotherapy: A Mild Antitumor Mode for NSCLC Dominated by Immune Regulation

Low‐dose ionizing radiation (LDIR) is conventionally defined as < 0.1 Gy, yet no unified academic standard for this threshold has been established in the field, and the definition of its dose range mostly varies with experimental design and research scenarios. LDIR exerts a weak direct cytotoxic effect on NSCLC, with immune regulation and therapeutic sensitization as its core mechanisms, and can serve as a supplementary modality for neoadjuvant therapy (Figure 2).

Non‐Targeted Effects

The biological effects of low‐dose radiotherapy are complex and diverse. As one of the core mechanisms, non‐targeted effects break the traditional cognition that radiation only acts on directly irradiated cells. It is manifested by irradiated cells releasing a variety of signal molecules to trigger biological responses in adjacent or distant unirradiated cells. Radiation‐induced bystander effect (RIBE), radiation‐induced genomic instability (RIGI), and abscopal effect all fall into this category [21]. In RIBE, even if less than 1% of mammalian ovarian cell nuclei are directly hit by α‐particles, 30% of the cells show DNA aberrations. This phenomenon is achieved through gap junction communication [22], changes in calcium flux [23], reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and other pathways. Signal molecules such as cytokines, exosomes, and UVA biophotons released by irradiated cells can trigger chain reactions such as oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction in unirradiated cells and even expand the effect range through secondary signal transmission. RIGI is manifested by delayed lethal mutations, chromosomal abnormalities, and other phenotypes in unirradiated progeny of irradiated cells [24]. This effect is particularly significant in immune‐related cells with active proliferation such as hematopoietic stem cells, and its mechanism is closely related to the destruction of genomic integrity caused by DNA base oxidative breakage, lipid peroxidation, and superoxide accumulation [22]. The abscopal effect is reflected in tumor suppression or inflammatory regulation of tissues distal to the irradiated site, which depends on signal transmission mediated by macrophages and neutrophils in the innate immune system.

Immune Stimulatory Effects

Low‐dose radiation initially activates core signaling pathways including p38/MAPK, NF‐κB, JNK, and PI3K/AKT [25, 26], thereby reshaping the immune microenvironment through two major mechanisms. On one hand, LDR upregulates proinflammatory and immunostimulatory cytokines such as IL‐2, IL‐12, IFN‐γ, and TNF‐α [27], elevates glutathione (GSH) to enhance antioxidant capacity and immune cell activation [28], and simultaneously downregulates immunosuppressive factors including TGF‐β1 and IL‐10 [25]. On the other hand, at the cellular level, LDR promotes NK cell activation and cytotoxicity [29], M1 macrophage polarization [30], antigen presentation by dendritic cells [31, 32], and the proliferation and infiltration of CD4+/CD8+ T cells [33, 34], while reducing the proportion and suppressive function of regulatory T cells in the tumor microenvironment [35]. Furthermore, LDR exerts differential regulatory effects on normal cells and tumor cells via the ATM signaling pathway [36]. Collectively, these events contribute to systemic immune activation and enhanced antitumor immunity, constituting the central molecular mechanism underlying LDR‐induced immune hormesis [37].

Spatially Fractionated Radiotherapy: Targeted Antitumor Effects Driven by Heterogeneous Dose Distribution

Spatially fractionated radiotherapy (SFRT), a novel approach differing from traditional homogeneous‐dose radiotherapy, employs methods like multileaf collimation and lattice simulation to generate a heterogeneous dose distribution characterized by alternating peak and valley doses within the tumor. This technique not only mitigates the difficulties associated with large tumor volumes and neighboring critical structures, such as the hilum and mediastinum in NSCLC, but also induces a bystander effect that surpasses the anticipated impact of direct radiation exposure. Such a bystander effect serves as the core basis for SFRT to achieve normal tissue sparing and efficient tumor killing, and exhibits different characteristics in locally irradiated regions and distant unirradiated sites, mediated by multiple mechanisms including molecular diffusion, biomolecule secretion, and intercellular communication. Meanwhile, the core mechanisms of action of SFRT also revolve around enhancement of immune infiltration, regulation of tumor angiogenesis, and the abscopal effect, which are highly adapted to the characteristics of low proliferative activity and immune microenvironment heterogeneity in NSCLC (Figure 3).

Bystander Effect

The high dose in the peak regions of SFRT can directly kill sensitive NSCLC cells, and simultaneously transmit damage signals to unirradiated resistant cells in the valley regions through the bystander effect [38]. The mechanisms of the bystander effect are complex and intertwined, with the diffusion of ROS at the molecular level serving as an important initiating link. Studies have shown that gradient irradiation generates more ROS than homogeneous irradiation. Among them, superoxide anion (O2 −) is difficult to diffuse across cell membranes due to its ionic properties, but it can trigger apoptosis of adjacent cells extracellularly [39]; in contrast, neutral hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) can freely diffuse across cell membranes and can also generate singlet oxygen (1O2) through enzymatic reactions [40]. These ROS collectively activate stress‐related pathways, spreading damage to cells in sublethal dose regions [41]. Biomolecule secretion constitutes another key mechanism, in which the cytokine network plays a central role. Tumor necrosis factor α (TNFα) is recognized as the main mediating factor, initiated by the upregulation of cyclooxygenase‐2 (COX‐2) [42]. Clinical studies have also confirmed that the levels of TNFα and ceramide pathway components in the serum of patients receiving high‐dose SFRT are increased, which are associated with treatment response [43]. In addition, high‐dose irradiation can also induce cells to release chromosome‐breaking factors, including hydrolases, kinases, nucleases, and microRNAs (miRNAs). These factors enter the systemic circulation and exert effects on bystander tissues [44]. Intercellular communication is also indispensable. Gap junction intercellular communication (GJIC) and the diffusion of soluble factors enable direct information transmission between cells in high‐dose regions and low‐dose regions, allowing cells not directly exposed to high‐dose irradiation to produce cytotoxic responses [45]. Meanwhile, the tumor hypoxic microenvironment, as an important regulatory factor, interacts with ROS diffusion through the hypoxia‐inducible factor 1α (HIF‐1α) pathway to further amplify the bystander effect [46]. Hypoxia‐activated prodrugs can generate active drugs under hypoxic conditions, killing cells that are far away or lack reductase, helping to overcome tumor radioresistance [47].

Cohort Effects

Cohort effects are specifically manifested as vascular remodeling and enhanced immune infiltration. Tumor blood vessels are inherently abnormal, exhibiting distortion and disordered blood flow. Through heterogeneous dose irradiation, SFRT sensitizes immature or activated tumor blood vessels and may even induce hemorrhage, while some vessels undergo normalization, creating a transient “vascular permeability window.” This window significantly improves the delivery efficiency of chemotherapeutic or immunotherapeutic agents, creating favorable conditions for combination therapy [48]. On the other hand, high‐dose radiation can trigger the translocation and activation of acid sphingomyelinase (ASMase), which in turn catalyzes the production of ceramide, selectively inducing apoptosis in endothelial cells of immature tumor blood vessels [49]. Clinical studies have further confirmed that the elevation of serum ASMase activity and ceramide levels in patients after SFRT is significantly positively correlated with tumor treatment efficacy (R2 = 0.818), suggesting that this pathway can serve as an important biomarker for SFRT efficacy [43]. In addition, tumor cells in high‐dose radiation regions undergo immunogenic cell death, and the released antigens are captured by antigen‐presenting cells. Radiation also activates the cGAS/STING pathway in the cytoplasmic DNA sensing pathway [50, 51], promoting the production of Type I interferons. Combined with increased expression of adhesion molecules on vascular surfaces, this further recruits immune cells to infiltrate tumor tissues, achieving bystander killing of tumor cells in valley‐dose regions and even inducing the formation of immune memory.

Abscopal Effect

By virtue of heterogeneous peak‐valley dose distribution, SFRT activates the antigen‐presenting function of distant non‐irradiated tumors, upregulates the expression of antigen‐presenting molecules, and induces infiltration of mature dendritic cells. This in turn drives robust activation of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells and CD4+ helper T cells, leading to sustained elevation of serum IFN‐γ levels, while moderately regulating PD‐L1 expression to avoid severe immune escape. Combination with ICIs can further amplify immune activation, achieving potent dual suppression of primary and distant lesions, providing a novel mechanistic basis for radioimmunotherapy of metastatic tumors [52].

Evolution of Neoadjuvant Radiotherapy

The clinical application of radiotherapy in neoadjuvant therapy for Stage III NSCLC has evolved significantly from traditional chemoradiotherapy to radioimmunotherapy synergy in the immunotherapy era, reflecting the iterative progress of radiotherapy technology and the deepening understanding of tumor biological characteristics and immunological mechanisms.

Before the Immunotherapy Era: Stalled Exploration of Chemoradiotherapy Combination, With Efficacy and Safety Falling Short of Expectations

Before immunotherapy became available, the main approach to neoadjuvant treatment for Stage III NSCLC was neoadjuvant chemotherapy or neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy. The goal was to improve the chances of successful surgery and long‐term survival by reducing the size of the tumor. Although neoadjuvant chemotherapy combined with surgery showed certain efficacy in improving treatment response rate and surgical resection rate [53, 54], its efficacy improvement was limited, especially in terms of pathological complete response (pCR) rate, and was accompanied by toxic and side effects.

To further improve treatment outcomes, investigators explored neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy. Although three‐dimensional conformal radiotherapy (70 Gy/35 fractions) plus neoadjuvant chemotherapy increased the overall response rate compared with radiotherapy alone [55], the addition of radiotherapy to neoadjuvant chemotherapy failed to confer additional survival benefit [56] and was associated with an increased risk of postoperative complications, including radiation pneumonitis and esophagitis. Therefore, before the immunotherapy era, the role of radiotherapy in the neoadjuvant setting remained exploratory.

Immunotherapy Era: Radioimmunotherapy Synergy Breaks Through Bottlenecks, With Continuous Exploration of Triple Regimens and Technical Optimization

With the emergence of ICIs, the treatment pattern of NSCLC has undergone a revolutionary change. By blocking immune checkpoints such as PD‐1/PD‐L1 or CTLA‐4, ICIs restore the antitumor activity of T cells, achieving remarkable success in advanced NSCLC treatment and expanding to neoadjuvant and adjuvant therapy for early and locally advanced NSCLC.

In unresectable Stage III NSCLC, the PACIFIC trial [57] established the standard of care for consolidative durvalumab after concurrent chemoradiotherapy, significantly improving Progression‐Free Survival (PFS) and Overall Survival (OS). However, the PACIFIC‐2 trial [58] did not show additional benefits when combining durvalumab at the initiation of concurrent chemoradiotherapy, indicating the importance of immunotherapy timing.

For resectable Stage III NSCLC, neoadjuvant immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy has become a key exploration area to improve prognosis. The CheckMate 816 trial [59] first confirmed that neoadjuvant nivolumab combined with chemotherapy significantly prolonged Event‐Free Survival (EFS) and improved pCR rate (24.0% vs. 2.2%) without increasing surgical difficulty. Notably, patients who attained pCR achieved an impressive 5‐year OS rate of 95%, and patients with deeper pathological response exhibited higher 2‐year EFS rates. This pivotal trial robustly corroborates that pathological response translates sustainably into long‐term survival gains, qualifying pCR as a credible surrogate endpoint for long‐term clinical outcomes. Collectively, these results have opened up novel therapeutic avenues and informed innovative strategies for managing resectable Stage III NSCLC. Subsequent Phase III trials (CheckMate 77T [60], KEYNOTE‐671 [61], AEGEAN [62], Neotorch [63], RATIONALE‐315 [64, 65]) further verified the efficacy and safety of this regimen in different populations, with significant improvements in pCR, MPR, and OS rates.

With continuous breakthroughs in the efficacy of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, it is imperative to re‐examine the role of radiotherapy in the neoadjuvant treatment of lung cancer. A retrospective study conducted in Seoul, South Korea, demonstrated that neoadjuvant concurrent chemoradiotherapy yielded a significantly higher major pathological response (MPR) rate than neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy in NSCLC (55.3% vs. 30.3%, p = 0.03) [66]. This finding clarifies the clinical value of radiotherapy. First, it enables tumor downstaging and resectability conversion for patients with unresectable Stage III NSCLC. Second, it further deepens the pathological response in resectable patients, thereby laying a foundation for long‐term survival benefits. Accordingly, amid the widespread application of immunotherapy, neoadjuvant radiotherapy‐based combination regimens represent a highly promising therapeutic strategy.

The value of radioimmunotherapy synergy has been fully verified. In a randomized Phase II trial by Altorki et al. [67], 60 patients with resectable Stage I‐III NSCLC were randomly assigned to the durvalumab monotherapy group or the durvalumab combined with SBRT group. The results showed that the MPR rate in the combined group was 53.3%, much higher than the 6.7% in the monotherapy group, with 50% of MPR patients achieving pCR. Long‐term follow‐up showed that the 3‐year PFS in the combined group was 83%, higher than the 69% in the monotherapy group, confirming that radioimmunotherapy synergy significantly improves pathological response rate and long‐term disease control [68].

A single‐center retrospective analysis [69] further compared neoadjuvant SBRT‐IO with standard Chemo‐IO. The MPR rate of the SBRT‐IO group (61.5%) was not statistically different from that of the Chemo‐IO group (51.3%), but the 2‐year Relapse‐Free Survival RFS RCI (91.7% vs. 64.4%) and 5‐year RFS (83.3%) of the SBRT‐IO group were significantly better. There were no significant differences between the two groups in postoperative adverse event rate and 90‐day mortality rate, indicating that SBRT‐IO can bring better long‐term disease control while maintaining pathological response effects comparable to the standard regimen without increasing surgery‐related risks.

Small‐sample clinical practice also verified the value of neoadjuvant radioimmunotherapy synergy. Three patients with Stage III/N2 NSCLC received SBRT combined with toripalimab treatment. One patient achieved pCR, one achieved MPR, and only one had 20% residual tumor. All patients successfully completed surgery without severe perioperative complications. This result further confirms the safety and pathological response effect of this regimen in clinical settings, especially suitable for some patients with comorbidities [70].

Synthesizing these consistent evidences from randomized trials, long‐term follow‐up studies, retrospective analyses, and clinical cases, neoadjuvant radioimmunotherapy synergy significantly improves the MPR and pCR rates of resectable Stage III NSCLC, creating more favorable surgical conditions for patients. At the same time, it shows better disease control ability in long‐term follow‐up, bringing tangible potential long‐term survival benefits to patients. The entire course of treatment is safe and tolerable, without increasing surgical risks or severe adverse reactions, fully supporting the clinical value of this regimen in the treatment of resectable Stage III NSCLC.

However, radioimmunotherapy synergy regimens still have issues that urgently need optimization: there is no unified standard for the combination of different radiotherapy modalities, immunotherapeutic agents, chemotherapy regimens, and the selection of combination timing, which has also promoted the exploration of neoadjuvant triple immunochemoradiotherapy regimens. At present, a number of clinical trials have focused on this direction, accumulating important data for the improvement of the regimen.

In a prospective single‐arm phase Ib study conducted by Lee et al. [71], patients with resectable Stage III N2 NSCLC received concurrent chemoradiotherapy combined with 2 cycles of durvalumab. The incidence of Grade 3 or higher treatment‐related adverse events (TRAEs) was 10%, with a pCR rate of 40.7% and an MPR rate of 74.1% in patients who completed complete resection, verifying the feasibility and efficacy of the triple regimen.

Another clinical case report [72] provided real‐world application evidence for this regimen. A 47‐year‐old patient with stage IIIA NSCLC received concurrent chemoradiotherapy (50 Gy in 25 fractions) followed by 4 cycles of pembrolizumab treatment, and then underwent intrapericardial left pneumonectomy plus mediastinal lymph node dissection. Postoperative pathological evaluation achieved pCR, successfully achieving R0 resection of a borderline resectable tumor. No recurrence was observed during 24 months of postoperative follow‐up, and the patient only had mild to moderate impairment of respiratory reserve during physical activity, with no severe TRAEs, demonstrating the good tolerability and downstaging effect of the neoadjuvant triple regimen in selected patients.

A pooled analysis of prospective studies by Sathish et al. [73] directly compared Radiochemotherapy‐Immunotherapy (RCI) and Chemotherapy‐Immunotherapy (CI) in resectable NSCLC, showing that RCI significantly improved MPR (OR 2.44, p < 0.001) and pCR rate (OR 1.89, p < 0.001) without significant differences in resection rate or Grade 3 or higher TRAEs. This study provides strong evidence to support the addition of radiotherapy to neoadjuvant therapy for resectable NSCLC to further improve the pathological response rate without increasing toxicity or affecting surgical feasibility.

Nevertheless, there may be an increased risk of toxicity with the triple regimen. The INCREASE Phase II single‐arm trial [74] showed that 70% of patients experienced Grade 3–4 TRAEs but achieved 50% pCR and 63% MPR in the ITT population, with an R0 resection rate of 100%. Although this study showed an extremely high pathological response rate, the high incidence of toxicity also suggests that more caution is needed in the design and implementation of such intensive regimens, and toxicity management should be strengthened. The SQUAT trial (WJOG 12119 L) by Hamada et al. [75] brought another perspective. This trial reported the preliminary results of neoadjuvant concurrent chemoradiotherapy plus immunotherapy (CCIR) followed by surgery and adjuvant immunotherapy in patients with resectable Stage III N2 NSCLC, demonstrating that this treatment regimen had a higher MPR rate. Nevertheless, the proportion of adverse events of Grade 3 or higher reached 48%. Excessive toxic burden may offset the survival advantages derived from pathological response, making it difficult to translate pathological remission into meaningful improvements in PFS and OS (Figure 4).

The high toxicity observed in the aforementioned studies stems from the universal adoption of conventional fractionation regimens, indicating that this modality is not the mainstream developmental direction of neoadjuvant radiotherapy. Exploring optimal radiotherapy fractionation patterns and refining radiotherapy techniques are critical to further enhancing the efficacy of neoadjuvant radiotherapy (NRT) and reducing treatment‐related toxicity. A preliminary analysis of the Phase II NEO‐PIONEER trial by Fang et al. [76] showed that hypofractionated radiotherapy (24 Gy in 3 fractions) combined with tislelizumab and anlotinib for resectable NSCLC achieved a 71.4% pCR rate and 100% MPR rate with acceptable safety. A phase Ib study by Ding et al. [77] confirmed the value of neoadjuvant hypofractionated radiotherapy combined with pembrolizumab and chemotherapy in EGFR/ALK wild‐type potentially resectable Stage III NSCLC, with a 94.1% partial response rate and 54.5% pCR rate and no Grade 3 or higher TRAEs. Li et al. [78] conducted a phase Ib dose‐escalation study investigating the application of low‐dose radiotherapy combined with durvalumab and chemotherapy in potentially resectable Stage III NSCLC. The optimal dose was determined to be 30 Gy in 15 fractions. This cohort achieved a 100% MPR rate and a 66.7% pCR rate with favorable safety profiles, laying a solid foundation for the clinical application of this combination regimen and subsequent larger‐scale studies.

On this basis, the sequential administration schedule of radiotherapy combined with chemoimmunotherapy is also of vital importance. The single‐center, single‐arm Phase II SACTION01 study by Zhao et al. [79] validated the strategy of SBRT followed by immunochemotherapy in resectable stage IIA–IIIB NSCLC. In the intention‐to‐treat population, an MPR rate of 76% and a pCR rate of 52% were achieved, and 96% of patients who underwent surgery achieved R0 resection, including those with PD‐L1 expression < 1%. The incidence of grade ≥ 3 TRAEs was 26%, with minimal surgical delay and manageable complications. This study achieved a 76% lymph node clearance rate despite lymph node‐sparing irradiation, fully reflecting the abscopal effect of the sequential regimen. It not only confirmed the feasibility and high efficacy of preoperative SBRT followed by immunochemotherapy but also provided a clear direction for optimizing the administration sequence of triplet regimens (Figure 4). Furthermore, updated 3‐year survival data of SACTION01 presented at the 2026 European Lung Cancer Congress further confirmed the durability of treatment benefit, demonstrating a 3‐year EFS rate of 67.3% and a 3‐year OS rate of 89.1%, which supports that short‐term pathological responses can translate into long‐term survival benefits.

In summary, the radiotherapy‐immunotherapy synergistic strategy exhibits great potential in improving the MPR rate, downstaging rate, and survival rate among patients with Stage III NSCLC. Based on current research evidence, avoiding conventional fractionated radiotherapy regimens and rationally arranging the sequential timing of radiotherapy and immunotherapy are expected to achieve therapeutic benefits while effectively managing toxicity risks. At present, multiple prospective Phase II trials (e.g., NCT06877299, NCT06379087, NCT07309952, NCT05798845, NCT06714708, NCT07231575) and Phase III trials (neoR‐TORCH and SACTION‐2401) are ongoing, which will provide high‐level evidence for future clinical practice (Table 1).

Discussion

In the immunotherapy era, synergistic radio‐immunotherapy has revolutionized the neoadjuvant treatment for Stage III NSCLC. Currently, conventional fractionation is no longer dominant, and contemporary research primarily focuses on three fractionation modalities: SBRT, LDIR, and SFRT. Notably, SFRT has become a research hotspot owing to its dual immune‐activating effects at both high and low radiation doses.

Distinct from high‐dose ablative SBRT, SFRT features non‐uniform dose distribution. It precisely targets primary tumors while minimizing radiation damage to normal lung tissues, major blood vessels, and bronchi. For borderline resectable patients with vital organ invasion, SFRT avoids severe postoperative complications caused by SBRT and safely achieves tumor downstaging to maintain surgical feasibility. Different from LDIR that merely regulates immunity, SFRT realizes local tumor control and tumor microenvironment remodeling. It prevents low‐dose‐radiation‐induced immune tolerance, offering superior conditions for combined immunotherapy or targeted therapy. Accordingly, SFRT bridges high‐dose ablation and low‐dose immunomodulation to adapt to diverse clinical scenarios.

SFRT satisfies unmet clinical needs for individualized treatment. It enables low‐toxicity downstaging for patients with vascular, bronchial invasion, or multi‐station N2 lymph node metastasis to gain surgical access. For N2‐stage patients, its targeted irradiation on tumors and regional lymph nodes preserves draining lymph node immunity, enhancing systemic antitumor effects to reduce distant metastasis.

Nevertheless, SFRT still faces limitations. At present, the clinical translation of SFRT in neoadjuvant settings remains limited with substantial inter‐study heterogeneity. Large‐sample randomized controlled trials are lacking to directly compare its efficacy with SBRT and conventional chemoradiotherapy, and long‐term safety data are insufficient. Additionally, standardized delineation and dose protocols are not well established. In terms of retrospective clinical evidence, a retrospective cohort analysis of neoadjuvant LATTICE radiotherapy for unresectable soft tissue sarcoma demonstrated that 33% of patients became operable after SFRT combined with conventional radiotherapy. Only mild to moderate treatment‐related toxicities were observed, with no Grade 4 or higher adverse events reported [80]. Dosimetric simulations of preoperative mini‐GRT for breast cancer further verified that SFRT substantially reduces radiation doses delivered to the heart, lungs and skin, providing dosimetric support for its extension to thoracic malignancies [81]. From real‐world case experience, one patient with Stage IIIA lung squamous cell carcinoma who experienced tumor progression during standard neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy received salvage LATTICE boost radiotherapy with a single fraction of 12 Gy. Radical pneumonectomy was performed after SFRT followed by full‐dose conventional radiotherapy, and pCR (ypT0N0) was achieved postoperatively [82]. This case preliminarily validated the synergistic antitumor effect of SFRT combined with systemic chemoimmunotherapy. Two registered trials evaluate perioperative SFRT for locally advanced NSCLC. NCT07131319 (Phase II) tests concurrent SFRT + tislelizumab + platinum in unresectable Stage III disease. NCT07608159 is a multicenter Phase III RCT comparing SF‐SBRT combined with toripalimab‐platinum chemoimmunotherapy versus chemoimmunotherapy alone for resectable Stage II–III NSCLC. These studies will provide high‐level evidence to address the lack of large trials and long‐term safety data.

In conclusion, SFRT possesses unique strengths of precise dosing, low toxicity, and dual antitumor mechanisms. It is promising to optimize multimodal treatment for Stage III NSCLC, particularly for borderline resectable and N2‐stage patients, advancing neoadjuvant therapy toward higher precision, safety, and efficacy (Figure 5).

Graphical abstract: Advances in Radiotherapy for Neoadjuvant Therapy of Stage III Non‐Small Cell Lung Cancer. This schematic depicts neoadjuvant radiotherapy for stage III NSCLC, highlighting preoperative limitations of inadequate tumor downstaging and residual micrometastases. Three radiotherapy modalities exert unique antitumor effects: Stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) mediates tumor‐microenvironment immune remodeling, immunomodulation, and abscopal effect; low‐dose ionizing radiation (LDIR) acts through non‐targeted and immune‐stimulatory effects; spatially fractionated radiotherapy (SFRT) induces bystander, cohort, and abscopal effects. SFRT is promising for potentially resectable NSCLC with multi‐station N2 metastasis owing to immune activation, tumor downstaging, and low toxicity.
