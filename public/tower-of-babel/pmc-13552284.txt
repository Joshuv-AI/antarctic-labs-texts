Probiotic supplementation improves body composition, lipid profiles, and fatty acid metabolism in combat sports athletes during the weight reduction phase
Authors: Shijiao Yang, Zhenqin Cheng, Wanting Shen, Jing Wang, Meiyi Lai, Xinkai Yu, Qun Zuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposePre-competition weight control for combat sports athletes may alter body composition and fatty acid metabolism. Probiotics have shown potential to regulate overall metabolism; however, their specific effects on fatty acid metabolism during weight control in athletes remain unclear.MethodsThirty-two combat sports athletes participating in the 4-week weight reduction period were assigned to either the probiotic group (Group E) or the placebo group (Group C). Body composition, lipid profiles, and fatty acid metabolism were measured before and after the 4-week weight reduction phase.ResultsAll the athletes experienced a decrease in body weight, body mass index (BMI), body fat percentage, and muscle mass following the 4-week weight loss intervention. Notably, a more significant reduction in body fat percentage (p < 0.05) was observed, along with lower levels of triglycerides (TG) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), specifically in Group E. Weight loss intervention resulted in increased levels of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), specifically acetic acid, propionic acid, and butyric acid. Notably, Group E exhibited higher mean values for SCFAs compared to Group C (p < 0.05). Additionally, the serum levels of γ-linolenic acid and 8,11,14-eicosatrienoic acid were significantly reduced in Group E (p < 0.05). In contrast, the majority of free fatty acids (FFAs) showed significant increases, with greater magnitudes of change observed in Group C (p < 0.05).ConclusionProbiotic supplementation can improve body composition, enhance SCFAs levels, and decrease circulating FFAs in combat sports athletes, suggesting that probiotics may have a beneficial impact on pre-competition weight management.Trial registration numberchiCTR2400079908 (Reg Date:2024-01-16).

Introduction

Combat sports are characterized by direct physical confrontation, regulated offensive and defensive actions between opponents. To ensure fairness, athletes are classified into different weight categories based on body weight, thereby reducing disparities in body size and strength. To gain a competitive advantage, many athletes engage in rapid weight loss (RWL) before competitions, enabling them to compete in a lower weight category than their usual body weight. Commonly used RWL methods include increasing exercise intensity, using saunas and plastic suits to promote dehydration, reducing food intake – particularly carbohydrates and fats – fasting, inducing vomiting, and taking diet pills, laxatives, and diuretics [1,2]. These methods may result in nutrient deficiencies, gastrointestinal disturbances, imbalances in electrolyte and mineral levels, and adverse reactions such as dehydration and muscle loss. Maintaining an appropriate body composition and ratios is essential for effective weight control and for optimizing athletic performance [3]. Durkalec-Michalski et al. [4] presented that athletes with lower body fat levels generally exhibit superior adaptability to exercise. In weight-classified combat sports, both body weight and body composition can directly influence competitive performance, and elite athletes typically possess lower body fat percentages and greater muscle mass [5]. Furthermore, athletic performance in combat sports is closely associated with muscular strength, which is positively correlated with lean body mass [6]. Rapid weight loss prior to competition primarily results in a reduction of lean body mass, with minimal changes in body fat [7] due to accelerated whole-body protein breakdown and oxidation rather than fat mass reduction. Similarly, Tai et al. [8] found that animals undergoing RWL exhibited a lesser visceral fat reduction compared to those undergoing gradual weight loss. Therefore, during the RWL process, athletes should prioritize reducing subcutaneous and visceral fat while preserving muscle mass as much as possible, in order to maintain energy expenditure, optimize energy storage, and prevent muscle degradation.

Recent studies have demonstrated a close relationship between fatty acids (FAs) metabolism and body weight regulation [9,10]. However, the effects of weight loss on circulating FAs concentrations are inconsistent, such as no significant changes in FAs levels or a notable reduction in FAs concentrations after weight loss. These discrepancies may be due to differences in study design, participant characteristics, methods, or the duration of weight loss. During the weight reduction process, alterations in FAs metabolism can influence lipid storage and mobilization, thereby affecting overall body composition. Among the metabolites involved, short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) can stimulate intestinal peptide YY (PYY) and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) secretion by activating G-protein-coupled receptors GPR41 and GPR43 to delay gastric emptying, promote satiety, modulate blood glucose levels, and enhance insulin sensitivity, overall to maintain energy homeostasis [11,12].

Free fatty acids (FFAs) are primarily derived from the hydrolysis of triglycerides (TGs) in adipose tissue and circulating plasma lipoproteins. Blood FFAs levels are regulated by dietary intake, physical activity, and hormones, including insulin and adrenaline. In the context of exercise or fasting, adipose tissues release substantial amounts of FFAs to be oxidized by skeletal muscle [13], which aids in reducing body fat and facilitating weight loss. However, sustained elevations in FFAs that exceed the metabolic capacity of muscle [14] and liver can impair insulin signaling, reduce insulin sensitivity, elevate blood glucose levels, and trigger inflammatory responses [15]. This creates a vicious cycle of metabolic deterioration, thereby increasing the risk of obesity and chronic diseases.

Rapid weight loss has been shown to disrupt the composition and balance of the gastrointestinal microbiota, resulting in a decrease in beneficial bacterial populations and impairing intestinal barrier integrity. This dysbiosis is associated with an increased incidence of inflammatory responses and gastrointestinal discomfort [16]. Probiotics, as beneficial microorganisms, play a crucial role in modulating the host's metabolic functions by restoring the equilibrium of the gut microbiota. Specific probiotic strains, including Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, promote fatty acid oxidation and regulate lipid storage by producing SCFAs, thereby reducing adiposity [17]. Several studies have demonstrated a close link between elevated SCFAs levels and the modulation of satiety and food intake by probiotics. Short-chain fatty acids stimulate the secretion of gastrointestinal hormones such as GLP-1, PYY, and gastrin, which can activate hypothalamic reward pathways and contribute to appetite regulation [18]. Although some clinical trials reported significant reductions in body mass index (BMI) with 8–12 weeks of continuous probiotic supplementation [19,20], other studies have found no statistically significant effects [21,22]. Notably, Zhang et al. [23] observed that probiotic supplementation resulted in moderate yet meaningful reductions in both body weight and BMI. Furthermore, specific strains, such as Lactobacillus acidophilus and Lactobacillus lactis, are associated with decreased body weight and body fat percentage [24]. Numerous studies have substantiated the positive effects of probiotic interventions on obesity and its associated comorbidities, with the most frequently utilized genera being Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and Enterococcus [25,26]. In adult populations, supplementation with either single or combined strains of Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, and Pediococcus can decrease body weight, BMI, waist circumference (WC), and fat mass [27]. Borgeraas et al. [28] also found that probiotic supplementation not only reduces body weight, BMI, and body fat percentage but also possesses the potential to decrease fat mass to a certain degree. Nevertheless, research investigating the modulation of fatty acid metabolism and the enhancement of body composition – specifically, fat reduction and increased muscle mass – in athletes through probiotic supplementation during the weight loss process remains scarce, especially with a lack of adequate and systematic scientific evidence.

Therefore, this study aims to explore the impact of probiotic supplementation on body composition in combat sports athletes during the weight reduction process, with a specific focus on alterations in FAs metabolism. We hypothesize that probiotics may positively influence FAs metabolic pathways during the weight loss process, thereby improving body composition and facilitating effective weight management in this athletic population.

According to the two-way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) model, the required sample size was calculated to be 16 participants per group for the validity of the data.

This study utilized a stratified, cluster-randomized design based on weight category, exercise level, years of training, and sport (Figure 1). The participants were assigned to either the probiotic group or the placebo group using a computer-generated sequence, which was sealed in sequentially numbered opaque envelopes to preserve blinding. The study implemented a double-blind design; both formulations were identical in appearance and taste, with unblinding occurring only in emergency situations. Adherence was monitored through the daily collection of sample sachets. Although participants with adherence below 80% could have been excluded from the per-protocol analysis, all participants achieved adherence rates of 95% or higher; thus, no exclusions were made. Throughout the study, all participants followed an energy-restricted diet at the designated athletes’ cafeteria, ensuring a consistent daily nutritional profile. To reinforce adherence to the protocol, participants photographed all meals during the weight-control phase. These records were subsequently reviewed by researchers for compliance monitoring. Ultimately, 32 high-level athletes were included in the study and were divided into two groups: the probiotic group (Group E) and the placebo group (Group C), with 16 participants in each group (14 men and 2 women). Intervention program

All subjects underwent a 4-week pre-competition weight loss program, with either 200 g of a placebo (Group C) or 200 g of a sports complex probiotic powder drink (Group E) [30] one hour after lunch each day with 25℃ water to consume [20,22,31].

The sports complex probiotic beverage (Beijing Zhifu Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) comprises erythritol, edible corn starch, inulin (10% added), passion fruit powder, resistant dextrin, needle flavored cherry fruit powder (5%, with VC content ≥ 17%), yeast powder, and a blend of probiotics including Lactobacillus rhamnosus R0011, Lactobacillus swissi Lafti L10, Lactobacillus salivarius HA-118, Lactobacillus plantarum Lp-G18, Lactobacillus swissi R0052, Bifidobacterium animalis Lafti B94, and Bacillus coagulans GBI-30,6086, with a minimum content of 1.5*1010 CFU of live bacteria per 100 g. The control group was provided a placebo powder drink that resembled the probiotic beverage in terms of taste, appearance, color, and packaging but contained no probiotics. During the 4-week experimental period, all athletes participated in a centralized training program and were provided with identical meals at the on-campus cafeteria. The types of food offered were largely consistent, ensuring uniformity in dietary sources among participants. Furthermore, as the athletes were undergoing a pre-competition weight reduction phase, their overall energy intake was intentionally reduced to further minimize the potential confounding effects of dietary variation on the study outcomes. Athletes within the same weight class and with equivalent weight loss requirements followed an identical training regimen, which included a full day off on Saturday afternoon and Sunday each week.

Testing indicators

All participants underwent body composition assessments before and after the 4-week weight reduction intervention. Blood and fecal samples were collected on Monday mornings, with participants instructed to maintain a fasted state and to refrain from engaging in intensive exercise for at least 24 h prior to sampling.

Body composition

BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). The participants were required to be fast, remove all metal objects, and stand barefoot on the body composition analyzer (Model H20, InBody, Korea). They were instructed to hold the hand electrodes, maintain an upright posture, and look straight ahead during the test. Body fat percentage and muscle mass were recorded from the analysis.

Blood samples

The subjects were instructed to fast, permitting only water, after 8:00 p.m. the night before the test and to continue fasting on the morning of the test. A professional collected approximately 4 mL of blood from the antecubital vein and placed it in a blood collection tube containing a coagulant and an inert separating gel. After allowing the blood to clot, the sample was centrifuged at 3,500 r/min for 15 minutes to separate the serum. The resulting serum samples were aliquoted and stored in a freezer at −80 °C until subsequent biochemical testing.

Sample preparation

Fifty microliters of serum was mixed with 450 μL extraction solvent (isopropanol:n-hexane = 2:3, v/v, containing 0.21 mg/L internal standard) in a 2 mL EP tube. After adding stainless steel beads, the mixture was vortexed for 30 s, sonicated in an ice-water bath for 10 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant (450 μL) was transferred to a new tube. The residue was re-extracted with 500 μL extraction solvent, vortexed, and centrifuged as described above. The supernatants were vortexed, and 800 μL was evaporated to dryness under nitrogen.

The residue was derivatized with 500 μL methanol:trimethylsilyldiazomethane (1:2, v/v) at room temperature for 30 min, then evaporated to dryness under nitrogen. The sample was reconstituted in 160 μL n-hexane, centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 1 min, and the supernatant was transferred to autosampler vials for GC-MS analysis.

Sample preparation

Approximately 1 mL of distilled water was added to the fecal samples in 2 mL EP tubes and vortexed for 10 s. Stainless steel beads were added, followed by homogenization at 40 Hz for 4 min and ultrasonication in an ice-water bath for 5 min. This cycle was repeated three times. The samples were centrifuged at 5,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, and 0.8 mL of the supernatant was transferred to new tubes. Then, 0.1 mL of 50% sulfuric acid and 0.8 mL of extraction solvent (methyl tert-butyl ether containing 25 mg/L 2-methylvaleric acid as an internal standard) were added. The mixture was vortexed for 10 s, shaken for 10 min, ultrasonicated for 10 min, and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was incubated at −20 °C for 30 min and transferred to vials for GC‒MS analysis.

GC‒MS analysis

SCFAs were analyzed using a Shimadzu GC2030-QP2020 NX system equipped with an HP-FFAP capillary column. A 1 μL aliquot was injected in split mode (5:1). Helium was used as the carrier gas (purge flow 3 mL/min, column flow 1.2 mL/min). The oven program was: 50 °C (1 min), 50 °C/min to 150 °C (1 min), 10 °C/min to 170 °C, 25 °C/min to 225 °C (1 min), and 40 °C/min to 240 °C (1 min). The injector, transfer line, quadrupole, and ion source temperatures were 220 °C, 240 °C, 150 °C, and 240 °C, respectively. Electron impact ionization was performed at −70 eV. After a 3.75 min solvent delay, the data were acquired in scan/SIM mode (m/z 33–150).

Statistical analysis

The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro‒Wilk test, and the results were presented as mean ± standard deviation (M ± SD). Two-factor repeated-measures ANOVA (group × time) was employed to analyze and compare the indicators. Comparison of baseline values and differences between the two groups were conducted using independent samples t-tests. A P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. For correlation analysis, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated.

Results

Changes in body composition and lipid profiles

Notable reductions in body weight, BMI, body fat percentage, and muscle mass were observed in both groups following the weight loss intervention (Figure 2: A1–A4). Moreover, the decrease in body fat percentage in Group E was significantly greater than that in Group C (P = 0.022, Figure 2: A7). Regarding the lipid profiles, only TG (P = 0.007, Figure 3: B2) and LDL (P < 0.001, Figure 3: B4) levels decreased in Group E compared to the weight-loss intervention before.

Dietary intake

Following the weight-loss intervention, dietary data and results for both groups are presented in Supplementary Table S1. Carbohydrate intake (g and g/kg), protein intake (g), fat intake (g and g/kg), dietary fiber intake (g/kg), total energy intake, the proportion of energy from carbohydrates, the proportion of energy from protein, and the proportion of energy from dietary fiber all exhibited significant time effects (P < 0.05). Discussion

This study performed an in-depth analysis of the effects of probiotic supplementation on body composition and fatty acid metabolism in combat sports athletes preparing for weight loss prior to competition. Following a 4-week weight reduction period, athletes receiving probiotic supplementation demonstrated greater improvements in body composition and blood lipid profiles compared to the placebo group. Notably, several SCFAs increased, while a limited number of FFAs significantly decreased in the probiotic group. In contrast, the placebo group exhibited significant increases in most FFAs. These findings suggest that probiotic supplementation may contribute to the regulation of fatty acid metabolism during weight reduction, potentially through the modulation of gut microbiota-related metabolic pathways, as indicated by alterations in SCFAs profiles. However, further studies incorporating gut microbiota sequencing approaches are required to directly elucidate the underlying microbial mechanisms. Furthermore, the sex distribution in this study was skewed, with only two female athletes included. Therefore, the observed changes in body composition, fatty acid profiles, and lipid-related biomarkers may not fully represent the responses of female combat athletes. Sex differences in adipose tissue metabolism and the gut microbiota composition, among other factors, may influence the body’s response to energy restriction and probiotic supplementation. Future studies involving a sufficient number of both male and female participants are needed to determine whether the metabolic effects of probiotic supplementation during weight loss are sex-specific.

Throughout the intervention period, researchers continuously monitored and quantitatively analyzed participants’ actual dietary intake by collecting photos of their daily meals and using a standardized dietary record method. This method was designed to assess changes in energy intake and the intake of major macronutrients during the weight-control process. After four weeks of weight control, participants in both groups exhibited similar dietary adjustment trends, primarily characterized by a reduction in daily total energy intake and a decrease in the absolute intake of carbohydrates, fats, and other macronutrients. Further between-group comparisons revealed no significant differences between the two groups before and after the intervention in terms of changes in total energy intake, carbohydrate intake, fat intake, protein intake, or fiber intake. This finding indicates a high degree of consistency between the two groups regarding the extent of energy restriction and dietary composition adjustments. Consequently, the similarity in dietary intake patterns between the two groups reduces the likelihood that dietary factors, as potential confounders, influenced the study results. This suggests that the observed changes in body composition, lipid profile, and fatty acid metabolism may be partially attributable to the probiotic supplementation intervention rather than primarily resulting from differences in dietary intake between the two groups.

Effects of probiotic supplementation on body composition

Rapid weight loss (RWL) is prevalent among athletes in combat sports, as changes in body composition are essential for effective weight management and the maintenance of optimal athletic performance [3]. Durkalec-Michalski et al. [4] reported that athletes with lower fat mass generally demonstrate superior exercise adaptability. However, existing studies have indicated that RWL prior to competition predominantly leads to a reduction in lean body mass, with relatively minor changes in fat mass [7,8]. Moreover, repeated cycles of weight loss, regain, and re-reduction in athletes can result in more severe metabolic disturbances and gut microbiota dysbiosis compared to the general population [32,33]. Therefore, probiotic supplementation during RWL has become an increasingly popular strategy among athletes, with fat mass changes being more pronounced following probiotic intervention [34]. Similarly, Sharafedtinov et al. [35] reported that consuming foods containing probiotics was associated with reductions in body weight, BMI, and body fat percentage. These effects may be attributed to the ability of probiotics to modulate bile acid metabolism, thereby influencing energy balance and fat absorption [26].

Probiotics may also modulate the balance of the gut microbiota and stimulate the secretion of gut hormones such as GLP-1 and peptide tyrosine-tyrosine PYY, which contribute to appetite suppression, reduced energy intake, and limited fat storage [18,36]. Furthermore, probiotics enhance the production of SCFAs [37], which activate GPR41 and GPR43 receptors, regulate blood glucose levels, and improve lipid metabolism. Although these metabolic pathways are often linked to alterations in the gut microbiota composition, it should be noted that the present study did not perform 16S rRNA sequencing or metagenomic analysis; therefore, the microbiota-mediated mechanisms underlying these effects remain speculative and warrant further investigation. Notably, acetate can activate the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) pathway, thereby promoting fatty acid oxidation and reducing fat mass [38]. Propionate activates the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) signaling pathway in the liver and adipose tissue, thereby increasing energy expenditure and reducing hepatic triglyceride accumulation [39]. Butyrate, in addition to reducing appetite via the gut‒brain axis, also enhances mitochondrial function in muscle cells and promotes fatty acid β-oxidation. In the present study, we observed that the levels of fecal SCFAs, primarily acetate, propionate, and butyrate, were significantly increased in group E, whereas no significant changes in SCFA levels were noted in group C. Although no direct correlation was identified between the fecal SCFAs content and body composition, group E exhibited a greater percentage reduction in body fat compared to group C, suggesting that probiotics may enhance body composition through the modulation of the gut microbiota.

The probiotic sports drink utilized in this study primarily contained strains of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium. These strains exhibit minimal influence on the production of isobutyric acid, valeric acid, isovaleric acid, and hexanoic acid, which are predominantly generated by anaerobic bacteria (such as members of the Clostridiaceae family and the Bacteroides genus) via protein fermentation [40]. The formation of isobutyric and isovaleric acids relies on specific anaerobic microbial populations, whose metabolic activity necessitates a prolonged adaptation period before significantly impacting SCFAs levels [41]. In this study, probiotic supplementation significantly increased the concentrations of acetic acid, propionic acid, and butyric acid in feces, while no significant changes were observed in the concentrations of several other SCFAs. This selective pattern of changes suggests that probiotic supplementation may preferentially affect pathways associated with the metabolism of SCFAs derived from carbohydrates. Acetic, propionic, and butyric acids are primarily produced through the microbial fermentation of carbohydrates and dietary fiber, whereas branched-chain SCFAs, such as isobutyric and isovaleric acids, are mainly derived from the microbial fermentation of proteins. Therefore, the increase in the concentrations of major SCFAs may reflect enhanced metabolic activity related to carbohydrate fermentation, although the underlying microbial mechanisms still need to be further confirmed through gut microbiome profiling and metabolomics analysis.

Previous studies have demonstrated that probiotic interventions lasting ≥ 8 weeks are more effective in reducing body weight and improving body composition [28]. While probiotic supplementation influenced body weight, BMI, and body fat, no significant changes in muscle mass were observed. Sharafedtinov et al. [35] reported that probiotic interventions lasting 12 weeks or longer were more effective in increasing muscle mass compared to shorter interventions (<12 weeks). This may be attributed to alterations in the gut microbiota.

Beyond statistical significance, the practical implications of changes in body composition should also be considered. Following the 4-week weight-control intervention, both groups achieved reductions in body fat percentage of approximately 2%–3%, which may be meaningful for combat sports athletes competing in weight-class events by facilitating competition weight attainment and potentially improving the strength-to-weight ratio and movement efficiency. However, whether this magnitude of body fat loss translates into measurable improvements in athletic performance remains unclear, as performance-related outcomes, including maximal strength, power output, endurance capacity, and sport-specific performance, were not assessed in this study.

Importantly, RWL may also compromise lean body mass. Although no significant between-group difference was observed in changes in muscle mass, group E exhibited a smaller reduction in muscle mass compared with group C, suggesting a potential tendency toward muscle preservation during energy restriction with 4-week probiotic supplementation. The lack of statistical significance may be related to the relatively short intervention duration, probiotic dose, and strain-specific characteristics of the formulation. Previous studies have suggested that probiotics may influence muscle metabolism through gut-derived metabolites, inflammation regulation, and nutrient utilization; however, these effects may require longer interventions to become detectable. Future studies with extended intervention periods, optimized probiotic doses, and functional performance assessments are needed to determine whether probiotics can effectively support muscle preservation during rapid weight loss.

Effects of probiotic supplementation on blood lipids

The four key blood lipid parameters – TC, TG, HDL-C, and LDL-C – are important indicators reflecting lipid metabolism in the body. Weight loss can affect fat storage and lipid metabolism to differing extents. Although Sharafedtinov et al. [35] found that lipid levels decreased after weight loss, regardless of probiotic or placebo intake. Scavuzzi [42] demonstrated that probiotic supplementation significantly reduced TC and LDL-C levels after weight loss. Grunewald et al. [43] observed a significant decrease in serum TC in rats fed probiotic-fermented milk, suggesting the cholesterol-lowering effects of probiotics.

Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium strains are considered effective in reducing circulating LDL-C levels, potentially via decreasing intestinal cholesterol absorption, promoting bile acid excretion, and indirectly modulating cholesterol metabolism through SCFAs production. Probiotics can bind cholesterol, utilizing it as a membrane stabilizer and thereby reducing cholesterol absorption in the gut [44]. Kumar et al. [45] summarized that probiotics regulate bile acid metabolism and reduce intestinal cholesterol reabsorption, which contributes to lowering LDL-C levels. Moreover, SCFAs synthesized in the gut contribute to the improvement of lipid metabolism and positively influence HDL-C levels [46]. Therefore, probiotics are capable of modulating lipid metabolism by reducing TC, TG, and LDL-C synthesis as well as promoting HDL-C synthesis, thereby improving blood lipid profiles and decreasing fat accumulation [36,42].

In the present study, group E exhibited significant reductions in TG and LDL-C concentrations after the 4-week weight-control intervention, suggesting a potentially favorable effect of probiotic supplementation on lipid metabolism during the rapid weight loss phase. However, no significant between-group differences were observed in the lipid profiles. This may be partly explained by the fact that all participants were healthy athletes with normal baseline lipid levels and remained within the normal range after weight reduction, leaving limited potential for further lipid improvement. Additionally, the relatively short intervention duration and the physiological characteristics of well-trained athletes may have attenuated the magnitude of detectable changes in circulating lipid parameters. Therefore, the observed reductions in TG and LDL-C should be interpreted as potential metabolic adaptations associated with probiotic supplementation during energy restriction rather than definitive evidence of lipid-lowering efficacy. Future studies involving populations with impaired lipid metabolism or longer intervention periods are warranted to further evaluate the potential regulatory effects of probiotics on blood lipids.

Effects of probiotic supplementation on fatty acid metabolism

Circulating levels of FFAs reflect the dynamic equilibrium among lipolysis, the formation of triglyceride droplets in adipocytes, and the utilization of FAs within the body. Previous studies have suggested that probiotics may potentially influence FFA metabolism through gut microbiota-related pathways, including alterations in microbial composition, modulation of SCFAs production, regulation of bile acid metabolism, and attenuation of inflammatory responses. However, given the absence of direct microbial profiling (e.g. 16S rRNA sequencing) in our study, these proposed microbiota-related mechanisms remain hypothetical and should be interpreted with caution. Future studies integrating metagenomic analysis and fecal microbiota transplantation are required to validate these causal links. Appropriate concentrations of SCFAs induced by probiotics and dietary fiber can decrease the circulating levels of FFAs, sphingolipids, and ceramides [47]. Probiotic supplementation has also been associated with improved lipid profiles, reduced inflammation, and potentially lower circulating levels of saturated fatty acids (SFAs) [48]. Furthermore, probiotics may regulate polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) absorption through alterations in bile acid metabolism or modulate PUFAs metabolic pathways via shifts in the gut microbial composition [25]. In the present study, significant reductions in γ-linolenic acid and 8,11,14-eicosatrienoic acid were observed following probiotic supplementation. These changes may reflect alterations in circulating PUFA profiles during weight loss and potentially indicate modified fatty acid metabolism under energy restriction. Additionally, both γ-linolenic acid and 8,11,14-eicosatrienoic acid can regulate lipid metabolism, thereby reducing fat accumulation and potentially promoting weight loss by modulating inflammatory responses [49]. However, because the gut microbiota composition, bile acid profiles, and direct indicators of FAs oxidation were not assessed in the present study, the underlying mechanisms remain speculative and require further investigation.

During weight loss, the body enhances lipolysis to release FFAs to meet increased energy demands. Filaire et al. reported [50] that following weight loss, TG and FFAs significantly increased, whereas TC, LDL-C, and HDL-C exhibited no significant changes. In this study, although both body weight and fat mass decreased in group C, and changes in blood lipid parameters were negligible, FFA levels actually increased following weight loss. This pattern is consistent with the physiological response to energy restriction, in which enhanced lipolysis promotes the release of fatty acids stored in adipose tissue to maintain the energy supply. In contrast, the attenuated increase in FFAs observed in group E may suggest that probiotic supplementation exerts a potential regulatory effect on fatty acid metabolism during rapid weight loss. However, further mechanistic studies are needed to determine whether this effect is mediated by lipid utilization, gut-derived metabolites, or other metabolic pathways.

Hormonal fluctuations during weight loss – such as insulin, adiponectin, and leptin levels – also affect FFA metabolism, especially adiponectin, which is closely associated with FFAs metabolism and regulates FFAs oxidation, promoting the metabolism of ω-3 and ω-6 fatty acids [51]. PUFAs may reduce obesity by decreasing appetite, increasing fat oxidation, and reducing fat deposition. However, ω-3 PUFAs might not directly facilitate weight loss but can suppress appetite, prevent weight gain, or aid in weight maintenance after weight loss. Furthermore, hormonal changes during weight loss may enhance fatty acid desaturation, leading to increased levels of certain unsaturated fatty acids (UFAs), such as linoleic acid and γ-linolenic acid [52]. Excessive intake of specific SFAs, such as palmitic acid, has been associated with insulin resistance and chronic inflammation. In contrast, monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs), including oleic acid, and PUFAs, such as docosahexaenoic acid, have been demonstrated to improve insulin sensitivity, attenuate chronic inflammation, and facilitate lipid metabolism [53].

In this study, the elevated FFA levels observed during weight loss – particularly in group C – were accompanied by a reduction in body weight and fat mass, suggesting enhanced mobilization of stored fatty acids under conditions of energy restriction. Under conditions of caloric deficit, the reduction in energy supply stimulates lipolysis in adipose tissue, leading to the hydrolysis of triglycerides into glycerol and FFAs, which are subsequently released into the bloodstream to provide substrates for energy metabolism. Therefore, the elevated FFA levels observed following weight loss may represent a physiological adaptation in which the body becomes more reliant on lipid mobilization during rapid weight loss. In contrast to the elevated FFA levels observed in group C, the changes in FFAs in group E were relatively mild following weight loss. This finding suggests that probiotic supplementation may help regulate FAs metabolism during energy restriction. Although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear, probiotics may influence lipid metabolism through gut microbiota-mediated pathways, including the regulation of microbial metabolites such as SCFAs, the modulation of host energy metabolism, and changes in fatty acid utilization. These effects may help reduce the excessive accumulation of FFAs in the circulatory system or promote their metabolic clearance, thereby contributing to the maintenance of FFA homeostasis during rapid weight loss. Additionally, probiotics enhance SCFAs production, which serves as an energy source for the liver and muscles, reducing reliance on FFAs and consequently lowering circulating FFAs concentrations. Furthermore, probiotics regulate bile acid metabolism, facilitating the synthesis and utilization of UFAs while reducing FFAs accumulation [54]. Probiotics have also been reported to alleviate chronic inflammation by suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-6, improving insulin sensitivity, and decreasing adipocyte-mediated FFAs release [55]. Similarly, Rupasinghe et al. [56] noted that probiotics can modulate lipid metabolism by promoting FAs β-oxidation, accelerating FFAs utilization, and reducing their accumulation in the circulation. In summary, probiotics can modulate the gut microbiota, influence the production of SCFAs, promote the metabolism and utilization of FFAs, and reduce fat accumulation, thereby contributing to weight loss and altering body composition.

Correlation analysis between fatty acids and body composition

Fatty acids are essential nutrients and serve as a primary source of energy for the human body, playing critical roles in metabolic processes and signal transduction. SCFAs and FFAs are key products of lipid metabolism, and their associations with body weight and body composition partially reflect the status of lipid metabolism and overall energy balance.

Pentadecanoic acid, palmitoleic acid, and γ-linolenic acid are long-chain fatty acids (LCFAs), with pentadecanoic acid classified as an SFAs, and palmitoleic acid and γ-linolenic acid as UFAs. Owing to its larger molecular size, pentadecanoic acid exhibits slower rates of metabolic processing and oxidative energy supply. Palmitoleic acid and γ-linolenic acid have been demonstrated to regulate lipid metabolism, improve blood lipid profiles, and exhibit anti-inflammatory properties [57,58]. During weight loss, athletes undergoing high-intensity training coupled with insufficient energy intake experience TG breakdown in adipose tissue, releasing FFAs. These FFAs are transported via the bloodstream to skeletal muscle, where they are oxidized to provide energy, thereby reducing protein catabolism and preserving muscle mass. When FFAs are adequately supplied, the body preferentially utilizes FFAs as an energy source instead of relying on muscle protein breakdown, which minimizes muscle loss and preserves muscle mass. Furthermore, free fatty acids can indirectly activate the AMPK pathway by modulating the cellular energy status, promoting fatty acid oxidation metabolism. Under specific metabolic conditions, AMPK may also regulate protein metabolism through associated signaling pathways [59]. In this study, probiotic supplementation was associated with a decrease in blood FFA levels, along with significant reductions in body weight and body fat. Although FAs oxidation was not directly measured, this pattern may indicate changes in fatty acid supply and utilization during energy restriction. Furthermore, the concentrations of pentadecanoic acid, palmitoleic acid, and γ-linolenic acid were negatively correlated with muscle mass, suggesting that changes in specific FFAs may be associated with alterations in body composition during rapid weight loss. Venn-Watson et al. [57] suggested that pentadecanoic acid activates peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα), which plays an extensive role in fatty acid metabolism, energy homeostasis, inflammatory responses, and fatty acid oxidation. This activation consequently improves lipid metabolism, enhances energy expenditure, and reduces fat storage. Therefore, the changes in FFAs observed in this study may reflect metabolic adaptations associated with weight loss; however, further research is needed to determine whether these fatty acids are directly involved in the regulation of muscle maintenance and lipid metabolism. The attenuated increase in FFAs in group E may indicate a potential role for probiotics in modulating lipid metabolic adaptations during rapid weight loss.

Previous studies have also demonstrated that γ-linolenic acid regulates the expression of genes related to fatty acid oxidation and metabolism, thereby promoting hepatic fatty acid oxidation, influencing systemic lipid metabolism, and contributing to regulating fat storage and energy balance. Moussavi et al. reported [60] that BMI and WC were positively correlated with the serum levels of palmitic acid, palmitoleic acid, stearic acid, linoleic acid, and arachidic acid. Bergman et al. found [61] that palmitoleic acid modulates fatty acid metabolism by influencing the expression of transcription factors such as PPAR-α and SREBP-1c, as well as enzymes related to lipid metabolism such as stearoyl-CoA desaturase-1 (SCD-1). In contrast, our study observed a negative correlation between palmitoleic acid and both body weight and BMI. This discrepancy with previous findings may be related to the metabolic characteristics of athletes undergoing rapid weight loss. Under conditions of energy restriction, enhanced lipolysis promotes the release of FFAs from adipose tissue to meet increased energy demands, which may contribute to reductions in body fat. The altered circulating FFA concentrations observed in this study may therefore reflect metabolic adaptations in FAs availability and utilization during weight reduction. Furthermore, the increased availability of FFAs may promote their utilization as an energy substrate by skeletal muscle, potentially reducing their reliance on amino acid oxidation. In the present study, pentadecanoic acid, palmitoleic acid, and γ-linolenic acid concentrations were negatively associated with muscle mass, suggesting that specific circulating FFA patterns may be related to changes in body composition during rapid weight loss. However, these associations likely reflect metabolic adaptations rather than direct effects of individual fatty acids on muscle mass. Previous studies have suggested that pentadecanoic acid may regulate lipid metabolism through the activation of PPARα, a key regulator of fatty acid oxidation, energy homeostasis, and inflammatory responses. Nevertheless, the mechanisms linking circulating FFAs with muscle mass changes during the RWL process remain unclear and require further investigation using targeted metabolic and molecular approaches. Following weight loss, the placebo group exhibited an overall increase in circulating FFAs, with only a few participants showing decreases. Conversely, the probiotic group showed an overall decline in FFAs, with only a few cases showing an increase. With regard to lipid profiles, the placebo group demonstrated no significant change in TG levels before and after weight loss, suggesting that despite weight reduction, FFAs were not efficiently utilized or oxidized and were likely re-esterified into TG for storage. In contrast, the probiotic group exhibited significant reductions in TG and LDL-C levels, indicating that probiotics may play a pivotal role in promoting lipid metabolism and enhancing fatty acid utilization. By lowering circulating FFAs, probiotics contribute to reducing body weight, fat accumulation, and muscle loss, thereby improving weight loss outcomes.

This study still has several limitations. First, owing to the practical challenges faced by athletes during the pre-competition weight-loss phase – including demanding training schedules, dietary restrictions, and psychological stress – a 3-day fecal sample collection window was adopted to enhance the feasibility and participant adherence. However, variable sampling times may increase intra-individual variability in fecal SCFAs measurements; future studies may adopt fixed sampling time points or multi-day consecutive strategies to improve metabolite measurement stability. Second, although researchers monitored participants’ health status and gastrointestinal reactions through regular follow-ups and verbal inquiries during the intervention period, and no significant adverse events or severe gastrointestinal discomfort were observed, the evaluation of probiotic tolerability and safety remains somewhat limited owing to the absence of standardized gastrointestinal symptom assessment tools and systematic documentation. Future studies should incorporate validated gastrointestinal symptom scales and structured adverse event monitoring protocols to better assess the safety of probiotic interventions. In addition, this study utilized a proprietary multi-strain probiotic formulation designed to provide potential synergistic benefits to athletes undergoing pre-competition weight loss. The rationale for using this combination was based on the potential functional complementarity among its constituent strains, rather than on the assumption that each strain could independently influence all measured parameters. However, because the specific ratios of the individual strains are kept confidential by the manufacturer as proprietary information, it is not possible to determine their independent contributions to the observed metabolic changes. Consequently, the study results should be interpreted as reflecting the overall effect of the multi-strain probiotic formulation, rather than the efficacy of any single strain. Although different strains may collectively participate in the regulation of gastrointestinal function, SCFAs production, and lipid metabolism through complementary pathways, future studies combining single-strain interventions with omics-based approaches are needed to further elucidate the specific mechanisms underlying probiotic-mediated regulation of fatty acid metabolism.

Conclusion

Athletes engaged in combat sports experienced a decrease in body weight, body fat, and muscle mass during the weight loss process, accompanied by an increase in circulating FFAs levels. Probiotic supplementation can improve body composition, enhance SCFAs levels, and decrease circulating FFAs in these athletes, suggesting that probiotics may have a beneficial impact on pre-competition weight management.
