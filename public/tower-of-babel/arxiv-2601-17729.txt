cuGUGA: Operator-Direct Graphical Unitary Group Approach Accelerated with CUDA
Zihan Pengmei

Abstract
We present cuGUGA, an operator-direct graphical unitary group approach (GUGA) configuration interaction (CI) solver in a spin-adapted configuration state function (CSF) basis. Dynamic-programming walk counts provide constant-time CSF ranking/unranking, and pretabulated segment factors enable constant-time evaluation of coupling coefficients. Two-electron contributions are organized through an intermediate-weight formulation that separates sparse generator enumeration from integral contraction and supports both dense and density-fitted/Cholesky backends. We further map the same primitives to GPUs by implementing the irregular DRT traversal and accumulation in custom CUDA kernels while delegating contractions to CUDA libraries. The implementation reproduces reference energies at the 10 − 11 10^{-11} Eh level and matches CPU/GPU 𝝈 \bm{\sigma} -vectors to 10 − 14 10^{-14} . On an RTX 4090, the GPU backend provides up to ∼ 10 × \sim 10\times speedup over the CPU backend for smaller active spaces and multifold speedups on representative CASCI kernels. Speedup decreases as the active space grows because the workload becomes increasingly dominated by FP64 GEMM, which is not strongly accelerated on consumer GPUs. In addition, the cuGUGA CPU backend generally delivers ≳ 2 × \gtrsim 2\times speedup over PySCF ’s determinant backend and ≳ 4 × \gtrsim 4\times speedup over PySCF ’s CSF backend.



I
Introduction

Accurate electronic-structure predictions for strongly correlated molecules and materials with near-degenerate frontier orbitals often require multireference treatments. Complete active space self-consistent field (CASSCF) addresses strong correlation by solving the full configuration interaction (FCI) problem in a chosen active orbital subspace and remains a workhorse reference for post-CASSCF correlation methods.
11

In practice, each macro-iteration of orbital optimization requires repeated solutions of a large CI eigenproblem, most commonly with a Davidson-type iterative eigensolver.
4

The dominant cost is the repeated matrix–vector product (the “
\bm{\sigma}
-vector”),

\bm{\sigma}=\bm{H}\bm{c}
,
evaluated many times per root and per macro-iteration.

A standard strategy to reduce the CI dimension while enforcing spin purity is to work in a spin-adapted configuration state function (CSF) basis rather than in a Slater-determinant basis. In the graphical unitary group approach (GUGA), CSFs correspond to root-to-leaf walks on a layered directed acyclic graph (DAG, the Shavitt graph) encoded by a distinct row table (DRT).
12
;
13
;
10

This representation enables systematic enumeration of CSFs and evaluation of Hamiltonian couplings through local “segment” rules for the action of spin-free generators.

Despite this structure, practical CI implementations often introduce determinant intermediates and/or large cached objects in the hot loop, which can mask the fine-grained sparsity of CSF couplings and complicate GPU execution. For example, OpenMolcas implements the RASSCF CI step using split-GUGA ideas but relies on determinant-based kernels in the innermost loops for Davidson
\bm{\sigma}
-vector formation and reduced density matrices.
6
;
9
;
1

Such designs are robust for CPU workflows, but they are not naturally aligned with a CSF-direct, graph-sparse formulation.

In this technical note, we introduce cuGUGA, an operator-direct GUGA CI solver that cleanly separates (i) sparse coupling enumeration in the CSF basis via DRT traversal (“segment-walks”) and (ii) integral contraction backends used to form effective operator weights.
We support both dense four-index integrals and density-fitted/Cholesky-factorized representations through a common intermediate-weight construction.
16
;
2

Building on this hardware-agnostic formulation, we map the same primitives to GPUs by implementing the irregular DRT enumeration and accumulation in custom CUDA kernels while delegating dense/sparse contractions and Davidson subspace algebra to CUDA libraries.

II
Methods

II.1
Overview: operator-direct CI in a GUGA CSF basis

We solve for low-lying eigenpairs of the active-space Hamiltonian in a spin-adapted CSF basis

\{\left|\Phi_{j}\right\rangle\}_{j=0}^{N_{\mathrm{CSF}}-1}
.
The iterative eigensolver (Davidson) requires repeated application of the Hamiltonian to one or more trial vectors,

\bm{\sigma}=\bm{H}\bm{c},\qquad\sigma_{i}=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{\mathrm{CSF}}-1}H_{ij}\,c_{j}.

(1)

In a GUGA formulation, Hamiltonian matrix elements are evaluated operator-directly via
spin-free generators
13

E_{pq}=\sum_{\omega\in\{\alpha,\beta\}}a^{\dagger}_{p\omega}a_{q\omega},

(2)

whose action on a CSF
\left|\Phi_{j}\right\rangle
produces a sparse linear combination of CSFs,

E_{pq}\left|\Phi_{j}\right\rangle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}(j;pq)}\left\langle\Phi_{i}\right|E_{pq}\left|\Phi_{j}\right\rangle\,\left|\Phi_{i}\right\rangle.

(3)

Here
\mathcal{N}(j;pq)
denotes the set of CSFs connected to
\left|\Phi_{j}\right\rangle
by
E_{pq}
; it is typically small and can be
enumerated using the DRT representation of the CSF space
(Sec.
II.2
) and a DRT traversal routine called a
segment-walk

(Sec.
II.3
).

Both the one- and two-electron parts of the active-space Hamiltonian can be written in terms of
E_{pq}
and products

E_{pq}E_{rs}
(Sec.
II.4.2
).
The cuGUGA implementation computes contributions to
\bm{\sigma}
by (i) enumerating connected CSFs and the associated
coefficients induced by spin-free generators, and (ii) contracting these coefficients with the chosen integral representation
(dense or density-fitted/Cholesky), without ever forming
\bm{H}
explicitly.

II.2
DRT representation of the CSF space and its construction

II.2.1
DRT as a layered directed acyclic graph (Shavitt graph)

A CSF can be represented as a root-to-leaf path on a layered DAG with layers corresponding to
orbital positions
k=0,1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{orb}}
(the Shavitt graph / DRT representation).
12
;
13
;
10

Each orbital transition on the path chooses one of four step types, which we store as integer codes:

•

d=0
:
\mathrm{E}
denotes an empty orbital (0 electrons),

•

d=1
:
\mathrm{U}
denotes a singly occupied orbital (1 electron) that raises the spin-coupling label,

•

d=2
:
\mathrm{L}
denotes a singly occupied orbital (1 electron) that lowers the spin-coupling label,

•

d=3

\mathrm{D}

k

where

We denote the root and leaf nodes by
v_{\mathrm{root}}
and
v_{\mathrm{leaf}}

II.3
Segment values and the segment-walk generator oracle

II.3.1
Occupancy feasibility and segment restriction

We define the orbital occupancy implied by a step as

n(\mathrm{E})=0
,
n(\mathrm{U})=n(\mathrm{L})=1
, and
n(\mathrm{D})=2
.
For a given CSF
\left|\Phi_{j}\right\rangle
with step sequence
\bm{d}^{(j)}
, the occupancy of orbital
p
is
n_{p}(\Phi_{j})=n(d_{p}^{(j)})
. For
p\neq q
, the spin-free generator
E_{pq}
transfers one electron from
q
to
p
(summed over spin).
Therefore, a necessary feasibility condition for
E_{pq}\left|\Phi_{j}\right\rangle\neq 0
is

n_{q}(\Phi_{j})>0\quad\text{and}\quad n_{p}(\Phi_{j})<2.

(10)

Furthermore, only orbitals in the interval

[p_{<},p_{>}]\equiv[\min(p,q),\max(p,q)]

(11)

may differ between the ket path and any connected bra path generated by
E_{pq}
; this interval is called the

segment
. Denoting the ket node sequence by
(v_{0}^{(j)},\dots,v_{n_{\mathrm{orb}}}^{(j)})
, any valid segment replacement must
start from the same boundary node
v_{p_{<}}^{(j)}
and end at the same boundary node
v_{p_{>}+1}^{(j)}
.

(i) the segment position and direction (encoded by a discrete position code
\kappa(k;p,q)

(ii) the proposed step
\tilde{d}_{k}
versus the ket step

and (iii) the relevant spin label(s) on the DRT nodes involved.
The total matrix element is a product of local factors along the segment.

build. One-electron contributions are accumulated directly (diagonal) or via segment-walk enumeration (off-diagonal). Two-electron contributions follow an expand–contract–apply pipeline that isolates integral contractions (dense or DF/Cholesky) from CSF coupling enumeration.

II.4.1
One-electron contribution

Using the step-to-occupancy mapping in Sec.
II.3.1
, it is convenient to introduce the hole and
particle index sets

\mathcal{H}(j)\equiv\{\,q\mid n_{q}(\Phi_{j})>0\,\},\qquad\mathcal{P}(j)\equiv\{\,p\mid n_{p}(\Phi_{j})<2\,\},

(14)

since
E_{pq}\left|\Phi_{j}\right\rangle=0
unless
q\in\mathcal{H}(j)
and
p\in\mathcal{P}(j)
.

The one-electron Hamiltonian in spin-free form is
\sum_{pq}h_{pq}E_{pq}
, and its contribution to the
\sigma
-vector is

\sigma_{i}^{(1)}=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{\mathrm{CSF}}-1}c_{j}\sum_{p,q}h_{pq}\,\left\langle\Phi_{i}\right|E_{pq}\left|\Phi_{j}\right\rangle.

(15)

For
p\neq q
, the couplings
\left\langle\Phi_{i}\right|E_{pq}\left|\Phi_{j}\right\rangle
are enumerated by Algorithm
A.2
.
For
p=q
,
E_{pp}
is diagonal in this CSF basis and satisfies

E_{pp}\left|\Phi_{j}\right\rangle=n_{p}(\Phi_{j})\left|\Phi_{j}\right\rangle,

(16)

so diagonal contributions can be accumulated without invoking the segment-walk oracle.

II.4.2
Two-electron contribution and spin-free generator factorization

We express the two-electron operator using the standard spin-free identity
13

\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}a^{\dagger}_{p\omega}a^{\dagger}_{r\omega^{\prime}}a_{s\omega^{\prime}}a_{q\omega}\;=\;E_{pq}E_{rs}-\delta_{qr}E_{ps}.

(17)

Accordingly, we write the two-electron part in the generator product form

\hat{V}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{pqrs}v_{pqrs}\,\Big(E_{pq}E_{rs}-\delta_{qr}E_{ps}\Big),

(18)

where
v_{pqrs}
denotes a fixed index ordering of the two-electron integrals compatible with Eq. (
18
).
(Concretely,
v_{pqrs}
is a permutation of the conventional tensor
(pq|rs)
; our implementation uses an ordering in which
the outer indices align with the generator product
E_{pq}E_{rs}
.)
The contraction term can be absorbed into an
effective
one-electron integral,

h^{\mathrm{eff}}_{ps}\equiv h_{ps}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{q}v_{pqqs},

(19)

so the Hamiltonian can be written as

\bm{H}=\sum_{pq}h^{\mathrm{eff}}_{pq}E_{pq}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{pqrs}v_{pqrs}\,E_{pq}E_{rs}.

(20)

With this representation, both the one-electron and two-electron contributions reduce to applications of the generator oracle.
For completeness, Appendix Algorithm
A.3
provides full pseudocode for the operator-direct
\bm{\sigma}=\bm{H}\bm{c}
build.

II.4.3
Two-electron
\sigma
-vector via intermediate weights

We evaluate the two-electron contribution in Eq. (
20
) using an intermediate-weight decomposition.
For a fixed source CSF
\left|\Phi_{j}\right\rangle
and an operator
E_{rs}
, define the intermediate expansion

E_{rs}\left|\Phi_{j}\right\rangle=\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{N}(j;rs)}C^{(rs)}_{{\mu}j}\left|\Phi_{\mu}\right\rangle,\qquad C^{(rs)}_{{\mu}j}\equiv\left\langle\Phi_{\mu}\right|E_{rs}\left|\Phi_{j}\right\rangle.

(21)

For each resulting intermediate ket index
\mu
, we define an effective weight tensor

g^{(\mu j)}_{pq}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\sum_{rs}v_{pqrs}\,C^{(rs)}_{\mu j}.

(22)

Then the two-electron action on
\left|\Phi_{j}\right\rangle
can be written as

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{pqrs}v_{pqrs}\,E_{pq}E_{rs}\left|\Phi_{j}\right\rangle\;=\;\sum_{\mu}\sum_{pq}g^{(\mu j)}_{pq}\,E_{pq}\left|\Phi_{\mu}\right\rangle,

(23)

and the corresponding
\sigma
-vector contribution is

\sigma^{(2)}_{i}=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{\mathrm{CSF}}-1}c_{j}\sum_{\mu}\sum_{pq}g^{(\mu j)}_{pq}\,\left\langle\Phi_{i}\right|E_{pq}\left|\Phi_{\mu}\right\rangle.

(24)

This decomposition separates the computation into two conceptually distinct stages:
(i)
enumeration
of sparse coefficients
C^{(rs)}_{\mu j}
using Algorithm
A.2
, and
(ii)
integral contraction
to form
g^{(\mu j)}_{pq}
using either a dense or density-fitted/Cholesky backend
(Sec.
II.4.4
).

II.4.4
Integral backends: dense and density fitting / Cholesky

Let
\alpha=(p,q)
and
\beta=(r,s)
denote ordered pair indices, with
\alpha,\beta\in\{1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{pair}}\}
and

n_{\mathrm{pair}}=n_{\mathrm{orb}}^{2}
.
For each task indexed by
(\mu,j)
, define a (typically sparse) pair-space coefficient vector

\big(\bm{C}^{(\mu j)}\big)_{\beta}\equiv C^{(rs)}_{\mu j}.

(25)

Dense backend.

Define a dense pair-space matrix
\bm{V}^{(2e)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{pair}}\times n_{\mathrm{pair}}}
by

V_{\alpha\beta}\equiv v_{pqrs}.

(26)

Then the weights in Eq. (
22
) are computed as

\big(\bm{g}^{(\mu j)}\big)_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\beta}V_{\alpha\beta}\,\big(\bm{C}^{(\mu j)}\big)_{\beta}.

(27)

\bm{g}^{(\mu j)}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\bm{V}^{(2e)}\,\bm{C}^{(\mu j)}.

(28)

In practice, multiple
(\mu,j)
tasks are processed in blocks, and the corresponding pair-space vectors are batched to enable
matrix–matrix contractions.

DF/Cholesky backend.

Assume a factorization
16
;
2

v_{pqrs}\approx\sum_{L=1}^{N_{L}}B_{pq,L}\,B_{rs,L},

(29)

where
L
indexes auxiliary functions (or Cholesky vectors).
For each
(\mu,j)
task we compute

W^{(\mu j)}_{L}=\sum_{rs}B_{rs,L}\,C^{(rs)}_{\mu j},\qquad g^{(\mu j)}_{pq}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{L}B_{pq,L}\,W^{(\mu j)}_{L}.

(30)

For a batch of tasks stacked as rows of a matrix
\bm{C}
, Eq. (
30
) becomes

\bm{W}=\bm{C}\bm{B},\qquad\bm{g}=\frac{1}{2}\,\bm{W}\bm{B}^{\mathsf{T}},

(31)

where
\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{pair}}\times N_{L}}
.
This representation is particularly suitable for GPUs because it reduces the dominant contraction to sparse/dense and
dense/dense matrix multiplications (Sec.
II.5
).

II.5

The mathematical formulation of the solver is hardware-agnostic. However, directly porting a classical GUGA/CSF implementation to GPUs is challenging because legacy implementations often rely on pointer-heavy graph traversals, recursion, highly branch-dependent coefficient evaluation, and fine-grained irregular updates, all of which map poorly to SIMT execution.

three stages:

(32)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\textsf{Apply \& Accumulate}}_{\scriptsize\text{custom CUDA}}.

and keeps all solver data resident on the device across Davidson iterations.

II.5.1
GPU-enabling reformulation of GUGA primitives

The following changes are critical for GPU execution:

Flattened DRT tables.

The DRT transition map
\chi(v,d)
and prefix sums
\Pi(v,d)
are stored as dense, contiguous device arrays indexed by

(v,d)
, eliminating pointer chasing and enabling coalesced memory access. The per-node spin label
b(v)
and any auxiliary
metadata required by segment factors are stored as compact device arrays.

Table-driven segment factors.

Local segment factors are evaluated by a two-level lookup (Algorithm
A.2
), replacing branch-heavy case logic with
integer indexing into pretabulated coefficients. On GPUs this reduces warp divergence and avoids repeated expensive operations
(e.g., square roots).

Batching and block-Davidson matvecs.

We apply
\bm{H}
to a
block
of vectors,
\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{CSF}}\times m}
, rather than one vector at a time whenever
possible. This enables the two-electron contraction stage to be expressed predominantly as GEMM/SpMM operations (high arithmetic
intensity) rather than a large number of GEMV/SpMV calls.

Count–scan–write enumeration for variable output size.

Because segment-walk enumeration produces a variable number of neighbors for each work item
(p,q,j)
, we cannot predict memory requirements in advance. To avoid the performance penalty of dynamic allocation during kernel execution, we employ a standard count-scan-write strategy. This involves: (i) a counting pass to compute the number of outputs, (ii) an exclusive scan to produce write offsets, and (iii) a write pass to emit

(\text{index},\text{value})
pairs into contiguous buffers.

II.5.2
Device-resident data and precision

The following objects are transferred once and kept resident on the GPU:
(i) DRT tables
\chi
,
\Pi
, and node labels
b(v)
;
(ii) CSF path data (step sequences
\bm{d}^{(j)}
and optional node sequences
(v_{k}^{(j)})
);
(iii) segment-factor lookup tables
M(\cdot)
and
F[m,b]
(Algorithm
A.2
);
(iv) integrals in either dense pair-space form (Eq. (
27
)) or DF/Cholesky factors (Eq. (
29
));
and (v) Davidson workspaces
\bm{V},\bm{W},\bm{R}
and diagonal
H_{\mathrm{diag}}
.
Unless otherwise stated, contractions and eigenvalue updates are performed in double precision.

II.5.3
Batched segment-walk enumeration on GPU

We treat a generator-application request as a work item
\omega\equiv(p,q,j)
.
For each work item we need to evaluate the neighbor-oracle output set
\mathcal{O}(p,q;j)
defined in
Eq. (
13
).
To handle the variable output size
|\mathcal{O}(p,q;j)|
efficiently on GPUs, we employ a count–scan–write pipeline.

Occupancy feasibility constraints (Eq. (

) and Eq. (

II.5.4
Two-electron contraction on GPU: dense and DF/Cholesky backends

The intermediate-weight formulation (Eqs. (
21
)–(
22
)) produces a set of tasks indexed by

(\mu,j)
, each associated with a sparse pair-space coefficient vector
\bm{C}^{(\mu j)}
(Eq. (
25
)).
Let
n_{\mathrm{task}}
be the number of such tasks in a batch.
We store the batch as a sparse matrix
\bm{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{task}}\times n_{\mathrm{pair}}}
, typically in CSR
format (row pointers, column indices, values).

Dense backend.

When the dense pair-space matrix
\bm{V}^{(2e)}
(Eq. (
26
)) fits in device memory and reuse across many
Davidson iterations amortizes its storage, we compute

\bm{g}=\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{C}\big(\bm{V}^{(2e)}\big)^{\mathsf{T}},

using cuBLAS GEMM (or GEMV if the block size is one). This is advantageous only for relatively small
n_{\mathrm{orb}}
.

DF/Cholesky backend.

For larger active spaces, the DF/Cholesky factorization

v_{pqrs}\approx\sum_{L}B_{pq,L}B_{rs,L}
(Eq. (
29
)) is preferred.
For a batch, Eq. (
31
) becomes

\bm{W}=\bm{C}\bm{B},\qquad\bm{g}=\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{W}\bm{B}^{\mathsf{T}}.

We compute
\bm{W}=\bm{C}\bm{B}
using cuSPARSE SpMM (CSR
\times
dense), followed by
\bm{g}=\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{W}\bm{B}^{\mathsf{T}}

using cuBLAS GEMM. The second GEMM is typically the dominant floating-point kernel and achieves high utilization when

n_{\mathrm{task}}
is large.

After forming weights
g^{(\mu j)}_{pq}
, we apply weighted generators to each intermediate ket
\left|\Phi_{\mu}\right\rangle
and accumulate
into
\bm{\sigma}
(Eq. (
24
)).
This stage is again expressed in terms of segment-walk enumeration (Algorithm
A.2
) and is implemented using custom CUDA
kernels:
each thread block (or cooperative group) processes one or a small number of intermediate kets
\mu
, iterates over relevant

(p,q)
tiles, enumerates neighbors
(i,\Omega_{i\mu}^{(pq)})
, and performs

\sigma_{i}\mathrel{+}=c_{j}\,g^{(\mu j)}_{pq}\,\Omega_{i\mu}^{(pq)}.

Because many tasks contribute to the same
\sigma_{i}
, the GPU implementation uses atomic accumulation in global memory.
If strict bitwise reproducibility is required, an alternative deterministic mode writes contributions as a stream of

(i,\Delta\sigma_{i})
pairs and performs a reduce-by-key operation; this trades additional sorting/reduction overhead for
deterministic summation order.

II.6
Implementation details and numerical settings

We use double precision (FP64) for CI vectors, segment factors, and all reported contractions. The CUDA dense-integral backend performs the dominant pair-space contractions with cuBLAS GEMM routines and keeps all Davidson vectors and intermediates resident on device during the iterative solve. To bound temporary memory for large CSF spaces, the CUDA backend processes the ket index
j
in tiles and allocates intermediate buffers according to a user-configurable workspace cap. Unless otherwise stated, no screening of Hamiltonian contributions is performed beyond the intrinsic sparsity of the
E_{pq}
neighbor enumerator (segment walk).

IV
Relation to existing implementations

OpenMolcas provides widely used multiconfigurational workflows and supports GUGA-related machinery.
1

The OpenMolcas
RASSCF
program is based on the split-GUGA formalism
6

but uses determinant-based CI algorithms, performing CSF
\rightarrow
determinant transformations in the innermost loops required to evaluate Davidson
\bm{\sigma}
vectors and reduced density matrices.
9

In contrast, cuGUGA keeps the
\bm{\sigma}
build CSF-direct and exposes dense pair-space contractions that map naturally to GPUs.

PySCF provides efficient determinant-based CASCI/CASSCF drivers and FCI kernels in the
pyscf.mcscf
and
pyscf.fci
modules.
14

Determinant representations remain the most practical choice for many active-space calculations; cuGUGA instead targets cases where spin adaptation and CSF-direct sparsity are central and where GPU acceleration of the CI step is desired. The COLUMBUS program system contains mature GUGA-based CI and MRCI implementations and highly optimized parallel CPU algorithms for large configuration spaces.
5

The differences to cuGUGA are primarily architectural: a GPU-oriented data layout and an explicit separation between sparse CSF enumeration and dense pair-space contractions.
