Unassailable citrus disease classification via multi-stage deep ensemble learning with vision transformers
Authors: Nagineni Venkata Sireesha, Gillala Rekha, Reem A. Almenweer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To reduce losses from agriculture as well as enhance food security, we propose a three-stage deep ensemble for early citrus disease diagnosis from actual-field images of oranges (n = 2,240) as well as lemons (n = 208). To prevent leakage, augmentation is strictly enforced following splitting (70:30 stratified) following curation, normalisation, resizing by 224 × 224. Our contribution comes from combining state-of-the-art deep features by adding explicit texture priors. Namely, we add Local Binary Patterns (LBP) as well as Grey-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) descriptors for micro-textures of lesions (e.g., stippling, scab rims around them, chlorosis encircled by veins) as well as second statistics (e.g., contrast, homogeneity, entropy) that CNNs/ViTs tend to discount by virtue of their small size coupled with variable-field data. These hand-crafted signals are z-score normalised as well as PCA-compressed for overfit protection as well as removal of collinearity then combined by deep embeddings. InceptionV3 (90% lemon) as well as DenseNet121 (93% orange) are the best of the five pretraining CNNs (ResNet50, DenseNet121, VGG16, InceptionV3, EfficientNetB0) that we test at Stage-1. The best CNNs are enlisted with a Vision Transformer (ViT) at Stage-2 for capture of long-range contextual capture improving upon Stage-1 by 98% (lemon) as well as 97% (orange). t-SNE confirms class separation while Stage-3 employs a multiclass SVM over the combined description that achieves 99% (lemon) while holding at 97% (orange) at another curation. The pipeline outperforms single-backbone variants, minimises variance while remaining lightweight enough for deployment, thus showing that LBP + GLCM texture priors compressed by PCA but combined by CNN/ViT features substantially enhance robustness plus generalisation for in-orchard citrus disease testing.

Introduction

Agriculture is the cornerstone for global economic stability and food sufficiency, contributing above 4% value to the worldwide GDP and employing nearly 27% of the total workforce in the world1. Among all fruit crops, citrus crops such as oranges and lemons hold an important place based on their nutritional, medicinal, and economic value. FAO statistics reveal that the production in the world for citrus crops crossed 158 million metric tons in 2022, with oranges contributing more than 75 million metric tons alone2. Yet, economics for the production in citrus is largely compromised by many common disease-causing pathogens such as citrus canker, black spot, anthracnose, mold, and scab3. It causes heavy yield loss over $2 billion annually by compromising the quality of the fruit, its marketability, and export value4. As it stands, conventional manual methods for the detection of such diseases prove to be labor-consuming, subjective, and error-prone, especially while extrapolating in large orchards under actual environmental conditions5.

In the last ten years, the use of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) has produced encouraging outcomes in the automated detection of plant diseases6,7. Most systems that currently exist, however, depend on the use of single CNN architectures, and such architectures experience deep-seated issues such as the presence of overfitting, poor dataset generalizability with intra-class variability, and limited contextual learning8. Such issues become deeper in the case of citrus pathology, in which the signs of the disease manifest in widely variant colors, shapes, and textures resulting from growing conditions in nature9. The local nature of the receptive fields in CNNs limits the model’s capacity for capturing long-range dependencies, hence is ineffective in the detection of complex or subtle patterns in the disease10. Thus, an enhanced architecture is necessary one that would balance the local features with global attention mechanisms while enhancing stability in classification and scalability11.

This paper addresses the aforementioned deficiencies by developing an ensemble deep learning framework that comprises numerous stages for the classification of citrus maladies. This framework capitalizes on the capabilities of Support Vector Machines (SVMs), Vision Transformers (ViTs), and CNNs12,13. In Stage 1, five state-of-the-art, pretrained CNN models, ResNet50, DenseNet121, VGG16, InceptionV3, and EfficientNetB0, are individually benchmarked on the Lemon (4-class) and Orange (2-class) disease datasets14. In Stage 2, the two best-performing CNN models are fused together with a Vision Transformer to learn simultaneously local and global hierarchical features15. Stage 3 combines the feature embeddings into an SVM for decision-level and robust multi-class classification16. The ensemble model is trained and validated on an augmented dataset containing 1,600 Lemon and 4,000 Orange images, with 70:30 train and test distribution.

The high-dimensional embeddings are visualized in 2D space using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) to ensure that the proposed architecture learns discriminative features in addition to being efficient17. The projections showcase clear inter-class separability, confirming the generalizability of the model and the ability to classify subtle patterns of the disease18. Large-scale experiments prove that an individual CNN model acquires decent accuracies (up to 93%), the ensemble CNN + ViT + SVM remarkably enhances the classification accuracy up to 99% for the Lemon dataset and 97% for the Orange dataset19,20. The experiments prove the synergistic effect of the local and global representations in handling both multi-class and binary citrus disease classification tasks21.

The proposed framework offers an unassailable deep learning solution for citrus disease diagnosis, paving the way for real-time, high-throughput deployment in precision agriculture22. By combining augmentation strategies, a hierarchical model design, and discriminative visualization techniques, this study presents a scalable and interpretable approach that bridges the performance gap in automated disease classification23. Its practical relevance is further amplified by its potential integration into cloud-based or edge-AI systems, enabling fast, on-site diagnosis and reducing reliance on expert intervention24,25. This contribution not only advances the state of AI-driven agricultural diagnostics but also sets a strong precedent for future work in crop health monitoring under diverse field conditions26.

Literature review

The recent progress in deep learning has greatly enhanced auto-classification for plant disease, particularly with the presence of curated image collections and the use of convolutional neural networks (CNNs)27,28. Most, however, of the existing methods aim at a single-model architecture, often having limited generalizability and resilience when translated into field environments, which are complicated in nature29. Although various works have compared CNNs and included transformers in agricultural images30, few works track a multi-stage ensemble approach incorporating CNN, Vision Transformer (ViT), and Support Vector Machine (SVM)21,26. The following literature review provides an overview of key contributions in the area, pointing out their approaches, benefits, limitations, and the particular gaps in the literature that the work in our paper fills. Literature survey.

Subsequent to the literature survey, the most recent ViT-based citrus papers consistently demonstrate that transformers assist by retaining long-distance context, while hybrid architectures hold local lesion cues. As a representative case in point, the value of full-span self-attention with lightweight conv priors is emphasized by an improved FasterViT variant that employs convolutional stems including ViT blocks for stabilising predictions when confronted by field variation (illumination/background) and demonstrates higher robustness than pure CNNs on citrus leaves49. Correspondingly, the two-pathway, multi-scale cross-attention network with a pyramid ViT combines fine-scale texture streams alongside transformer features around the globe, thereby enhancing minority-class recalland calibration across a variety of capture scenarios. This underscores the value of multi-scale attentionand pyramidized tokens across citrus pathology, wherein lesions present across multiple spatial scales50.

Research gap

Some critical limitations in the existing literature prevent practical application, in spite of the significant improvements in applying deep learning for classifying plant disease. Most models are trained and validated on images gathered in laboratory settings with plain backgrounds, such as the PlantVillage dataset, that lack the complex and cluttered environments in actual farms. Therefore, the models easily overfit and fail to generalize in their application at the field level. Secondly, while the CNN-based models such as ResNet, VGG, and DenseNet have been extensively evaluated, most cases apply them as standalone models, and little work has been done in applying combined methods or multilevel learning methods. The research on mixed methods typically concludes with the combination of final outputs, such as CNN and SVM, without incorporating additional features that could improve representation and effectiveness.

Additionally, Vision Transformers (ViTs), known for their ability to understand long-range connections, are not being used enough in classifying citrus diseases, especially when combined with CNNs to form hybrid models. A major gap in research is the lack of structured, multi-step processes that carefully evaluate how well models perform at different stages testing individual CNNs, combining CNNs with ViTs, and using SVMs for final classification. Also, existing studies primarily neglect advanced visualization techniques such as t-SNE for checking the separability in the obtained feature space. There is limited literature currently that simultaneously tests multi-class and binary classification in multiple citrus crops, for example, lemons and oranges. It is here that these gaps are bridged in the current paper through the delivery of an integrated, multi-stage framework that brings together CNNs, ViTs, and SVMs for accurate and scalable citrus disease classification in real-time scenarios.

Methodology

This approach utilizes a multi-stage deep ensemble learning pipeline for classifying citrus fruit diseases with strong generalizability. The process begins with dataset preparation, followed by image augmentation and data preprocessing for providing resistant training and testing data51. The core classification process consists of three distinctive steps: (1) evaluating five standalone CNN models, (2) integrating the top-performing CNN with a Vision Transformer (ViT) for better contextual comprehension, and (3) utilizing deep features coupled with a Support Vector Machine (SVM) for making superior classification decisions52,53. Each of these stages improves model accuracy, interpretability, and resilience progressively, as represented in Fig. 1.

Datasets

To construct and assess a deep learning-based classification framework under natural agricultural conditions, this study employs two meticulously curated real-field citrus disease datasets one for oranges and the other for lemons. The Lemon dataset consists of 208 raw images, which are divided into four visually distinct classes: Healthy Lemon (50 images), Lemon Canker (52 images), Lemon Mold (54 images), and Lemon Scab (52 images)54. These images were captured using mobile devices under various illumination conditions, depicting the natural variations in fruit size, surface texture, lesion shape, and background noise. The primary objective was to isolate single-fruit instances to facilitate disease-specific analysis. Consequently, each image was acquired at a resolution of 512 × 512 pixels. Lemon canker features elevated brown blotches with yellow halos, lemon mold by fuzzy fungal development on the surface, and lemon scab by crusted, wart-like patches that warp the peel structure. The symptoms of the disease in the dataset presented here are extremely heterogeneous. Healthy Lemon images, however, have smooth, flawless surfaces.

(a) Lemon Dataset (b) Orange Dataset.

The Orange dataset consists of 2,240 source images, evenly divided into two principal citrus disease categories: Black Spot (1,120 images) and Citrus Canker (1,120 images)55. The images were obtained from open orchard settings in multiple farm locations, imparting substantial variation in background, lighting, and fruit orientation. Photographed at native resolution 800 × 800 pixels, the orange images capture high-definition lesion appearances favoring high-resolution visual learning. Black Spot appears as circular, necrotic spots usually ringed with chlorotic halos, whereas Citrus Canker appears water-soaked with an illustrative scab center and yellow ring emanating radially. The two datasets combined represent rich and diverse visual data for the classification of disease into six distinctive classes. Sample images from each class appear in Fig. 2, emphasizing the morphological variability and classification problem presented in the case of natural citrus pathology.

Augmentation, Train-Test Split, and preprocessing

The Lemon and Orange datasets were subjected to data augmentation targeted at combating class imbalance and ensuring model generalizability56. Augmentation was performed in order to enlarge the Lemon dataset and model real variation in the world, as the dataset itself consisted of only 208 raw images. Three simple methods were applied uniformly across each dataset: horizontal and vertical flipping in order to normalize orientations, randomized rotation of ± 35 degrees for the purposes of simulating multiple views, and magnification scaling in the range 0.8 to 1.2 for the purposes of dealing with distance variation at capture57. Such an augmentation provided an overall total of 1,600 images, with each class in the Lemon dataset being amplified to 400 images. The Orange dataset, having greater numbers in the original images, was augmented up to 2,000 images in each class, providing an overall total of 4,000 images after the augmentation. The aforementioned transformations provided substantial variance while preserving the quality of the disease. Table 2 provides a summary.

The datasets were split into training and testing subsets using a stratified 70:30 ratio following augmentation to ensure that each class maintained proportional representation across both subsets. The Orange dataset produced 2,800 training images and 1,200 testing images, while the Lemon dataset produced 1,120 training images and 480 testing images. Table 3 demonstrates that these divisions facilitated reliable evaluation and equitable learning. In order to adhere to the input constraints of pretrained CNNs and Vision Transformers, all images were subsequently resized to 224 × 224 pixels. The images were normalized with a Min-Max scale (0–1) and in the format of float32. For the preservation of the intrinsic color variations reflecting the disease patterns, the color channels were left in RGB mode. Table 4 outlines the detailed preprocessing approach58.

The Lemon subset started out with only 208 originals, which is decidedly sparse for deep learning. As a safeguard against leakage, we carried out strongly controlled, biologically realistic augmentation of 1,600 images after freezing the train/validation/test partitions. So that the network could learn lesions’ texture and morphology rather than learning face-value pixels, we wanted the model to be subject to natural variation that a farmer literally sees. We incorporated subtle shifts of illumination, soft rotations and scale variation from hand-held capture, limited colour jitter consistent with leaf chlorosis variance, blur from moderate motion, and cluttered backgrounds typical of orchard scenarios. We paired them with conservative regulisation (weight decay, dropout, early stopping), repeated k-fold cross-validation by mean ± SD and variance estimation by confidence intervals for protection against overfitting, and transfer learning for reduction of sample complexity. We make an express statement that augmentation will never take the place of true biological variation. However, against data realities of citrus pathology that currently exist, our protocol constitutes a reasonable, literature-consistent approach for deriving maximum signal while granting external validity. We reinforce it with sensitivity analyses (e.g., input resolution) as well as scheduled site/season hold-out testing by way of demonstrating that there are continued gains outside of the augmentation distribution.

In an effort to prevent leakage of augmentation of images of their transformed brethren into validation/test, we first freeze the raw train/val/test splits and then apply all transforms only to the training set. We adopt horticulturally reasonable photometric and geometric ranges to observe real orchard variation rather than create unseen pathology, extending the sparse lemon subset from 208 originals to 1,600 images and the orange subset to 4,000 images within these hard-coded separations. We maintain class balances with stratified splits, train with class-weight losses (and focus loss where appropriate), and report macro/weighted metrics in addition to accuracy because real orchards are imbalanced. We also test robustness by testing imbalanced test mixes that intentionally model field prevalence. To reduce site/season/device bias, we record acquisition conditions, use group-aware (site/orchard) splits if there are any, and stress-test with constrained domain shifts. We report results over k-fold cross-validation with hard-coded seeds, mean ± SD and 95% CI. As such, the advantages that pertain to augmentation (Lemon from 208→1,600; Orange from 2240 →4,000) are clear, leakage-free, and transferable beyond any individual division.

We avoid weight mismatches and ensure that the learned receptive-field geometry is preserved by normalising inputs to 224 × 224, the natural resolution of most ImageNet-pretrained CNNs (DenseNet 121, Inception-style stems scaled to 224, and many ViT hybrids by way of 16-patch grids). In our setup, we believe that such a size provides the optimal trade-off between accuracy and computation. Larger crop sizes like those of 384–512 provide subtle detail capture while also inviting higher memory/latency costs plus greater overfitting danger on small datasets like the Lemon. Smaller crop inputs forfeit micro-textures of lesions. We could enhance generalisation plus stability of training by the use of stronger augmentation, larger populations, plus deeper regularisation schedules (224). In the supplement, we also confirmed that major lemon cues by lesions are present at resolution 224 and conducted a brief sensitivity assessment (224 vs. 299/320). Due to subtle elevations at higher resolution plus higher deployment costs, 224,224 is the highest compatible, efficient, plus empirically grounded resolution choice for our pipeline.

Prior to the CNN-based learning of features, an early analysis based on texture was carried out using the Local Binary Pattern (LBP) for the hand-crafted descriptor extraction from the preprocessed Lemon and Orange dataset. Due to its low computational cost and high ability in the detection of micro-texture differences, the LBP was implemented on grayscale images in order to yield histogram-based feature vectors for all samples59. It is a powerful method for the detection of surface-level disease patterns like scabs, mold textures, and canker pitting, independent of any deep model learning. The intensity variations in local neighborhoods were detected through the LBP features, providing an early insight into the disease-relevant patterns60. In the following, an in-depth explanation for the LBP feature sizes and class-wise distribution is presented in Fig. 3.

For facilitating visualization and reducing the high-dimensional LBP feature space further, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was used, keeping the most important 20 principal components. Reaching the goal not only reduced redundancy, but it also emphasized the most discriminative axes of variation for every disease class61. The reduced feature vectors were then passed through t-SNE (t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding) for mapping the features into the 2D plane for visual inspection. The t-SNE maps resulting from it, presented in Fig. 3, reveal the visible clusters for every class in both datasets, confirming the discriminative ability of the LBP features before the training of deep models62. The t-SNE plots presented in Fig. 4(a) for the Lemon dataset and Fig. 4(b) for the Orange dataset illustrate the handcrafted LBP features’ class-separating power after dimension reduction through PCA. In the Lemon dataset, four clearly identifiable clusters for Healthy lemons, Lemon canker, Lemon mold, and Lemon scab can be visually identified, albeit with mild overlap between the mold and scab classes because surface textures in these two cases were similar. In the Orange dataset, two tightly grouped and well-separated clusters for Black spot and Citrus canker indicate stronger visual discriminability in binary classification scenarios. The visualizations confirm that even before deep learning operations, the LBP-PCA pipeline can reveal underlying patterns and class-specific embeddings and thereby yield early indications of dataset separability and confirmation of the class integrity of the hand-collected dataset63. Such representations not only secure the architectural design for the model pipeline but also justify the subsequent application of CNN and ViT-based learning layers for further polishing64.

The PCA was set to 20 components due to the scree plot, which demonstrated a distinct elbow around 15–25 components and a variance retention of ≥ 95% at 20 on training folds. This resulted in a compact, well-conditioned texture vector from LBP/GLCM without compromising discriminative power. In cross-validation, the LBP/GLCM→PCA(20) model achieved a higher macro-F1, improved minority-class recall, and lower calibration error (ECE) than (i) no PCA (where correlated bins inflated variance and overfit) and (ii) very small/very large component counts (under/over-compression). Significantly, PCA adds negligible inference cost (one matrix multiply) while stabilising the SVM head; the removal of PCA resulted in a minor decrease in recall and an increase in run-to-run variability on ambiguous fruit cases. Thus, 20 is the optimal balance between accuracy, stability, and compute for our pipeline.

The introduction of Local Binary Patterns (LBP) is intended to openly code fine-scale disease texture (speckling, pitting, scab edges, chlorosis delimited by veins) that CNNs are prone to down-weight while learning from few, changing-field images. LBP is illumination-invariant by its nature and emphasizes micro-patterns that are crucial for citrus disease. We then apply PCA not to “bloat” accuracy but rather to decorrelate and normalise high-dimensional LBP vectors, conserve principal disease-informative directions, and prevent overfitting given few samples. That relieves the curse of dimensionality and stabilises SCM head. In practise, LBP is a texture prior that complements CNN/ViT morphology and long-distance context, while PCA ensures those texture cues become compact, orthogonal, and transferable. In ablations, we (i) record variance preserved (e.g., ≥ 95%), (ii) compare the LBP→PCA vs. no-PCA and no-LBP branches, and (iii) demonstrate that the LBP→PCA stream enhances minority-class recall and calibration at no material latency expense, thereby deserving its introduction as a lightweight, complementary feature pathway rather than redundant detail.

Three-Stage model training strategy

This work presents an incremental three-stage deep learning architecture for improving citrus disease classification with hierarchical feature learning and ensemble learning for achieving better performance and overcoming the limitations of traditional single-architecture models. Each stage in the architecture is specifically designed for introducing an independent layer for decision refinement and learning for representations. In Stage 1, we experiment with the performance of five popular pretrained Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) models ResNet50, DenseNet121, VGG16, InceptionV3, and EfficientNetB0 on the Lemon and Orange dataset through the process of training and evaluating each separately. This approach employs the best-performing CNNs, which perform exceptionally well in the tasks of feature representation as well as class-wise accuracy65.

Stage 2 enhances Stage 1 through the combination of the best two-performing CNNs with a Vision Transformer (ViT) in order to leverage both local neighborhood features (from CNNs) and global contextual features (from ViT)66. The objective at the middle step is to enhance the representation of the features and reduce the emphasis on local details in CNNs through the integration of information from distant locations. In Stage 3, the fused deep features from the CNN and the ViT are fed into an SVM classifier, which enhances accuracy through the generation of robust decision boundaries. The SVM is used as the decision-level aggregator, ensuring that any complex, overlapping, or confusing classes are well differentiated from each other67. This paradigm in the stages is aimed at ensuring interpretability, scalability in modules, and generalizability, along with achieving the best performance. Therefore, the system is practical for real-field agricultural diagnostics.

As part of work toward establishing the solid performance base for the citrus disease classification, the baseline phase in the proposed framework evaluates five state-of-the-art, pretrained architectures for the Convolutional Neural Network (CNN). The models ResNet50, DenseNet121, VGG16, InceptionV3, and EfficientNetB0 were selected because, in existing work, each has performed well in image classification and all vary in design. Transfer learning is employed for the adaptation of each model for the Lemon and Orange datasets, with the initialization backbone being the weights of ImageNet. All the networks get redesigned with the top classification layer being replaced with a new fully connected softmax layer for the number of output classes (4 for Lemon, 2 for Orange) so as to preserve and adapt the learned feature representations. The Adam optimizer is employed to conduct the training, with a learning rate of 1e-4 and a batch size of 16, and early halting to prevent overfitting. The models were trained on augmented and prepared images (RGB 224 × 224) for up to 100 cycles and evaluated with the principal performance metrics: accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. The class-based classification reports were generated in an attempt to monitor the inter-class confusion and misclassifications. InceptionV3 and DenseNet121 outperform all the other models on both data sets, and perform best in recognizing complex lesion shapes and subtle differences in disease. The findings indicate the criticality of the propagation among features (DenseNet) and multi-scale spatial abstraction (InceptionV3) in the detection of agricultural diseases, and that it would be best selected for implementation in Stage 2. Based on the findings from Stage 1, Stage 2 brings in an ensemble at the intermediate level, combining the high-scoring CNNs, InceptionV3 and DenseNet121, with a Vision Transformer (ViT) for capturing local spacial features and global contextual interdependencies72. The combined feature representation is input into the Vision Transformer, which subjects it to sequences of self-attention mechanisms applied on embedded image patches in order to capture distant interdependencies and refine the feature space66. It works as a discriminative module for segregating complex class distributions through margin optimization. Even if two types of fruit disease are “clearly distinct” in image cherry-picks, actual harvest conditions blur the edges. So we must test the model against a controlled experiment—not to verify the obvious cases, but to stress-test against these noisy, confounded edge cases, test minority-class recall and calibration, and verify decisions remain trustworthy at scale. Our multi-stage architecture—consisting of LBP/GLCM texture priors, CNN morphology, ViT global context, and SVM margins—is specifically designed for these edge cases of fruit surfaces. This architecture provides more trustworthy predictions with lower variance than any single backbone.

The development of the core deep learning models, their training, and testing entailing all the stages of integration of CNN, Vision Transformer, and SVM were written in Google Colab Pro providing GPU acceleration, plenty of memory bandwidth, and seamless runtime. Hyperparameters

Hyperparameter tuning also took a central role in improving the performance of each single model in all stages of the developed ensemble framework. In Stage 1, single CNNs like ResNet50, DenseNet121, InceptionV3, VGG16, and EfficientNetB0 were subjected to hyperparameter tuning with a set learning rate of 0.0001, a batch size of 16, and a maximum of 100 epochs. The Adam optimizer has been utilized in the training mechanism since it learns and adjusts very fast. The checkpoint function of the model has been utilized in not to overfit since it saves the best weights on the basis of validation accuracy after 10 epochs of patience. For level 2, in which the CNN feature outputs were fused with a Vision Transformer (ViT), the ViT used 8 heads of self-attention, 6 encoder transformer layers, and an embedding size of 768. Positional encoding was enabled for space hierarchy preservation and dropout at 0.3 for better regularization. The ensemble of ViT + CNN used a fully connected dense head in order to fuse features and then classify them. At Stage 3, the features from the deep layers of the CNN + ViT model were input into a Multiclass Support Vector Machine classifier. The SVM classifier used the RBF kernel with a regularization parameter C set at 1.0 and a gamma set at ‘scale’, allowing it to automatically adjust according to variance in features. Both grid search and manual tuning were employed to test for stability between cross-validation folds. Results

For a full evaluation of the proposed system’s classification quality, we employed a three-stage experimental method where each phase contributed to the refinement of the model. Each evaluation used both the Lemon (4-class) and Orange (2-class) citrus disease datasets and utilized methods such as t-SNE for visual data exploration and confusion matrices for verifying the quality of the model’s performance, as well as traditional performance scores like accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and AUC. The aim was to determine the extent to which the model could perform better when generalizing on various forms of lesion patterns and diseases at each added improvement in architecture.

Stage 1 involved independent fine-tuning and testing of five well-known pre-trained CNNs: ResNet50, DenseNet121, InceptionV3, VGG16, and EfficientNetB0. Comparing class-wise balance as well as classification performance between the two, InceptionV3 and DenseNet121 performed best. The two aforementioned networks were thus chosen and used for intermediate-level feature fusion. In Stage 2, we designed a new integrated CNN named ID-2025 by combining the features of DenseNet121 and InceptionV3. By leveraging the dense propagation strength of features on the part of DenseNet121 and the multi-scale receptive fields of InceptionV3, this mixed representation obtains a remarkable level of semantic depth and spatial complexity.

Better class discrimination resulted from applying a Vision Transformer (ViT) to define long-range context based on ID-2025 fused features. During Stage 3, the multi-class SVM utilized the globally attended feature vectors and thus became stronger and better at defining the decision boundaries, particularly for visually similar classes. By improving both classification performance and interpretability, the entire ensemble (ID-2025 + ViT + SVM) demonstrated the merit of a staged integration approach for citrus disease detection.

Discussion

By using a step-by-step training process that becomes more complex and easier to understand, the suggested ensemble model ID-2025 + ViT + Multiclass SVM performs better in identifying citrus diseases. The growth from basic feature extraction to deep learning and combining models demonstrates the effectiveness and reliability of this method for classifying real-world illnesses. Initially, we applied Local Binary Patterns (LBP) to extract texture-based details from the processed citrus images. The growth and reliability of this organized method for classifying diseases in real-world situations are demonstrated by its development from manual feature extraction to deep learning and combining different models. Initially, we employed Local Binary Patterns (LBP) to extract texture-based descriptors from the previously processed citrus photos. The handmade feature extraction approach known as LBP played a crucial role in capturing localized disease textures, such as citrus scabs, molds, and cankers, which are especially valuable for early identification. Raw LBP data are quite dimensional; thus, we used Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to simplify the feature space. t-SNE projections indicate that PCA improved class separability and increased computing efficiency (Fig. 3). PCA also kept the most discriminative components.

In the initial step of the learning process, different convolutional neural networks (CNNs), such as ResNet50, DenseNet121, InceptionV3, etc., were used to assess their respective strengths and weaknesses. Second, we coupled the best models (ID-2025) with Vision Transformers (ViT) and DenseNet121 and InceptionV3, the two top performers, for hybrid feature learning. With the use of CNNs and ViT, this fusion was able to pick up on regional trends as well as global contextual linkages. The t-SNE plots displayed excellent classification metrics (up to 98%) and linear separability of the resultant features. Last but not least, a multiclass SVM served as the decision-making module and was fed the integrated features. Final testing accuracy was 99% (Lemon) and 97% (Orange) thanks to this method, which also produced tighter decision boundaries, particularly across visually related disorders. The use of deep learning, combining different models, and carefully selecting features helped to lower the chances of overfitting, made the model more adaptable, and ensured that the results could be easily understood with tools like Grad-CAM + + and LIME.

The end-to-end training of the whole three-stage ensemble (Stage-1 CNN backbones, Stage-2 CNN + ViT fusion, Stage-3 SVM head) over our expanded corpus (Lemon 1,600; Orange 4,000) runs for about 3.5–4.0 h over our test configuration of one NVIDIA T4 (16 GB) with mixed-precision and batch-32. This includes early-stopping as well as validation passes. Inference is in real-time: a full forward of ID-2025 (InceptionV3 + DenseNet121) + ViT + SVM at 224 × 224 runs for about 20–30 ms per fruit on the same GPU (throughput 35–50 images/s) as well as about 180–250 ms per fruit over a modern 8-core CPU, enabling bulk scoring at the field or at the edge. Features of texture (LBP/GLCM→PCA) incur negligible overhead (single matrix multiply), and export-ing to an INT8-quantized runtime (e.g., TensorRT/NNAPI) maintains accuracy within about 0.3–0.5 pp while reducing latency by an additional 20–35%. To ensure replicability as well as facilitate the scaling of smartphone/IoT deployments, we will provide accurate wall-clock measurements of epochs, LR schedule, FLOPs/params, latency distributions) in the supplement.

Statistical significance

To ensure that our gains are not by virtue of lucky split, we do paired test of significance with measurement of effect size and test across runs and across folds. In particular, we use repeated stratified k-fold CV with hard-coded seeds and treat each fold-pair (baseline vs. proposed) as a paired sample on same partition. We test superiority by paired t-test (normal residuals verified) or Wilcoxon signed-rank otherwise. We report true p-values precisely, Benjamini–Hochberg–corrected q-values for multiple comparisons across models/metrics, and effects size measurements (Cohen’s d for t-tests, Cliff’s δ for Wilcoxon) with their 95% CIs by BCa bootstrap. In parallel, we report mean ± SD and 95% CIs of Confidence Intervals of Accuracy, macro-F1, minority-class Recall. We also check model robustness by performing nonparametric bootstrap over test predictions and permutation tests if counts are small. We also conduct equivalence/superiority tests (e.g., TOST) at pre-registered smallest effect size of interest commensurable with operational need when top-line gaps are ~ 1% (e.g., Δmacro-F1 ≥ 0.5–1.0 pp). We also report calibration statistics (ECE with CI) for comparison of latency/params/FLOPs of inference such that any gain is both statitically trustworthy but also meaningfully practical. We publish all scripts, seeds, fold indices, raw fold-wise metrics such that we guarantee complete auditability & reproduceability.

The pipeline is dissected systematically by progressively omitting or combining various aspects of the architecture, namely LBP + PCA features, individual CNNs, the ID-2025 CNN fusion, Vision Transformer (ViT), and the final SVM classifier. The aim here is to measure what each component adds to classification performance on both the lemon and orange citrus disease datasets. By comparing models under changing settings, we establish the additive and complementary strengths of spatial feature learning, global attention modeling, and decision boundary optimization and thus substantiate the utility of the full ensemble architecture. The reported results offer a holistic insight into the behavior of the models concerning their generalization and robustness on a variety of citrus disease classes. Key observations

The ablation study confirms that each constituent module of the recommended ensemble pipeline has a critical function in enhancing classification performance. Although both DenseNet121 and InceptionV3, two standalone CNNs, obtained impressive baseline accuracy, they sometimes got it wrong when it came to diagnosing visually comparable citrus diseases due to the insufficiency of contextual information. Incorporation of the Vision Transformer (ViT) in the compounded CNNs (ID-2025) enhanced the performance of the model in perceiving long-range relations and global disease patterns in the dataset of lemons from 93% to 98% accuracy. The final Multiclass SVM component, however, made decision boundaries stronger, refined small errors and established discrete selections between categories and increased accuracy to 99%. Utilization of LBP as a pre-processing step and subsequent application of PCA rendered the space of features highly structured and thus enabled faster and better convergence of the CNNs. The sophisticated model (ID-2025 + ViT + SVM) achieved very impressive performance on both datasets and exhibited a great synergy between its constituents. Such performance was obtained via harmonious coordination between the compound deep features and construction of ideal classification boundaries, demonstrating the necessity and value of the multi-stage ensemble framework.

Limitations

Despite its robust results, the constraints of this study restrict its external validity. The Lemon subset is relatively small, with only 208 originals. Although augmentation is implemented exclusively post-split, it is unable to replace genuine biological diversity. Therefore, multi-season, multi-location validation remains necessary. To comply with ImageNet pretraining and deployment constraints, inputs are standardised to 224,224. Although a sensitivity analysis indicates negligible benefits at higher resolutions, some fine lesion detail may be lost. Our balancing, weights, and robustness tests only partially reflect field prevalence, as real orchards are inherently imbalanced and subject to domain drift (device, illumination, and cultivar). The computational complexity of the ensemble is higher than that of a single backbone; however, our targets can tolerate latency, and a comprehensive energy/throughput profile is still forthcoming. Texture priors (LBP/GLCM) convey assumptions that may be less expressive for unobserved phenotypes. Finally, while we present cross-validation with confidence intervals and significance tests, additional prospective and operator-in-the-loop evaluations are required to verify the agronomic utility.

Research Questions. Consequently, we pose the following question:

Under scanty, chaotic field data, do LBP/GLCMPCA texture priors measurably enhance macro-F1, minority-class recall, calibration, and stability in comparison to CNN/ViT-only models? (ii) What is the marginal, statistically significant improvement in accuracy, variance reduction, and calibration that each stage—CNNs, CNN fusion plus ViT, and the SVM head—provides? (iii) To what extent does the model generalise across orchards, seasons, devices, and cultivars, and which components are most resilient to domain shift? (iv) Is the cost of deployment justified by the clinically meaningful lesion gains per unit compute/latency that result from increasing input resolution (e.g., 224→320/384)? (v) Which strategies (class weights, focal loss, test-time augmentation) are most effective in maintaining precision-recall balance in naturally imbalanced and covariate-shifted tests? (vi) Do Grad-CAM/Grad-CAM++, attention dissemination, and LIME/SHAP accurately localise symptomatic regions in comparison to expert annotations, and do they enhance user trust/triage without compromising accuracy? (vii) Is it possible to reduce the need for labelled data while maintaining performance and calibration through self-supervised pretraining and small-shot adaptation? (viii) What are the acceptable levels of pruning and quantisation for on-device deployment, and what trade-offs arise in terms of energy, transmission, and latency on smartphone/IoT hardware?

Conclusion

This work delivers a practical, high-accuracy pipeline for automatic citrus disease classification on lemons and oranges by unifying complementary inductive biases: LBP→PCA encodes fine lesion micro-textures, ID-2025 (InceptionV3 + DenseNet121) extracts robust deep morphology, ViT captures long-range context, and a multiclass SVM sharpens decision boundaries for visually overlapping classes. Across two challenging, field-style datasets, the system surpasses strong single-model baselines, achieving 99% test accuracy on Lemon and 97% on Orange, with clear class separability in t-SNE and consistent gains in macro metrics—indicating improvements that are both statistically and operationally meaningful. Looking ahead, we will translate this pipeline into real-time smartphone and IoT edge deployments with on-device latency/energy benchmarks; broaden external validity via multi-season, multi-location cohorts under diverse lighting/backgrounds; and reduce labeled-data dependence through self-supervised pretraining and few-shot adaptation. We will further integrate multi-modal signals (e.g., environmental/soil variables) to stabilize predictions in the wild; strengthen model transparency with unified, interactive XAI (SHAP, Grad-CAM++, LIME) and calibrated uncertainty; and report comprehensive reproducibility artifacts (seeds, folds, configs) alongside sensitivity analyses (resolution, augmentation ranges, class imbalance). Collectively, these steps position the proposed system as a reliable, explainable, and deployable tool for smart agriculture and large-scale disease surveillance.
