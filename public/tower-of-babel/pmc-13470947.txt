Comparative transcriptome analysis of sesame flower organs and potential functional analysis of the MIKC-MADS gene family
Authors: Qiyuan An, Hongsen Cheng, Huijie Sun, Yanbin Na, Guangzhen Zhou, Dexue Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSesame (Sesamum indicum L.) is an important oilseed crop; the comprehensive transcriptional landscape and regulatory networks underlying its floral organogenesis remain largely uncharacterized. A comprehensive understanding of gene expression dynamics among different floral organs is essential for elucidating the regulatory mechanisms underlying flower development.ResultsTo address this, we conducted a comparative transcriptome analysis of four floral organs (sepals, petals, stamens, and carpels) using RNA-seq. Based on commonly used criteria (|log2FC| ≥ 1, FDR < 0.05), pairwise comparisons identified 5,685 to 10,253 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) among organs. Functional enrichment analysis showed that these DEGs were mainly associated with metabolic processes, catalytic activity, and intracellular components. Notably, KEGG pathway analysis revealed that a subset of DEGs was significantly enriched in plant hormone signal transduction, MAPK signaling pathways, ABC transporters, and starch and sucrose metabolism. Core genes from eight hormone signaling pathways exhibited distinct organ-specific expression patterns, suggesting a coordinated network orchestrating organ differentiation. Complementing these findings, we identified 18 MIKC-MADS genes; promoter analysis indicated an enrichment of cis-elements responsive to hormones and stress, linking hormonal signals to transcriptional regulation. Expression pattern analysis further supported the conservation of the classical ABCDE model of floral organ identity in sesame, with class A (SiAP1), class B (SiPI/SiAP3), class C (SiAG), class D (SiSHP2), and class E (SiSEP) genes displaying strict organ-specific expression.ConclusionsThis study provides a transcriptomic atlas for sesame floral organs, characterizes potential hormone signaling networks and MIKC-MADS regulatory associations, and presents genetic resources that may be useful for future functional genomics and molecular breeding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12941-2.

Background

Sesame (Sesamum indicum L.) is an annual herb belonging to the genus Sesamum in the family Pedaliaceae [1]. It produces a determinate inflorescence, with flowers occurring singly or in clusters of two to three in the leaf axils. The calyx consists of five lanceolate, mostly green sepals. The corolla is tubular, measuring 2.5–3 cm in length, white in colour, and often tinged with purple or yellow. Each flower produces four or five fertile stamens, which are attached at the base of the corolla tube. Each stamen consists of a two-lobed, oblong anther and a supporting filament. At the center of the flower sits the carpel, topped with a sticky, bilobed stigma that is specialized for trapping pollen [2]. Despite the detailed morphological characterization, the genetic and molecular mechanisms governing the formation and regulation of this tubular floral architecture in S.indicum remain largely unexplored.

The development and fate determination of floral organs is a biological process tightly controlled by a multi-layered genetic network, following a classic and highly conserved “genetic blueprint”. A typical flower is organised into four concentric whorls from the outside to the inside: sepals, petals, stamens, and carpels. The classical ABC model explains the genetic framework for specific floral organ formation [3, 4]. In this model, class A genes (AP1 and AP2) specify sepal identity; the combined action of class A and B genes (AP3 and PI) controls petal development; class B and C genes (AG) act together to direct stamen formation; while class C genes determine carpel identity. Subsequent research led to the discovery of class D (STK) and class E (the SEPALLATA class) genes, extending the model into the more comprehensive ABCDE model [5]. The majority of these organ identity genes belong to the MADS-box transcription factor family, which exerts precise control by forming homo- or heteromeric protein complexes, functioning as a combinatorial “molecular code” [3, 5]. Correct operation of this molecular code depends not only on MADS-box factors but also on floral meristem identity genes that trigger the transition from vegetative to reproductive growth. Notably, LEAFY (LFY) and APETALA1 (AP1) serve as master regulators, activating downstream ABCDE genes to ensure their proper spatiotemporal expression [6]. Boundary-setting genes, such as CUP-SHAPED COTYLEDON (CUC) genes, subsequently define organ boundaries by modulating cell division and growth patterns [7].

Furthermore, the entire regulatory network is subject to precise and coordinated control by multiple plant hormones. Through the spatiotemporally specific expression of key genes in their signalling pathways, these hormones form a complex and dynamic interactive network that co-regulates the entire process from floral induction to organ morphogenesis [8]. For example, auxin is broadly involved in floral primordium initiation and organ differentiation [9]. Brassinosteroids profoundly influence the establishment of floral organ boundaries and stamen development, coordinating cell expansion and division [10, 11]. Ethylene signalling, mediated by the dynamic expression of its receptors and transcription factors like ETHYLENE INSENSITIVE 3(EIN3), directly affects stamen development and pollen formation [12, 13]. Cytokinins regulate processes such as anther development and female gametophyte formation through their signalling network [14]. Additionally, the abscisic acid and gibberellin pathways often exhibit complex antagonistic or synergistic interactions at the organ level, jointly regulating processes like cell elongation and pollen maturation [15, 16].

Recent advances in floral development research have greatly expanded our understanding beyond traditional model systems. For instance, a spatiotemporal transcriptomic atlas of orchid (Phalaenopsis Big Chili) flower organogenesis revealed the continuous activity of meristematic cells at the basal position of floral organs and the initiation of anther from a single spot, highlighting the power of high-resolution spatial transcriptomics to dissect complex developmental processes in non-model plants [17]. In parallel, genome-wide identification of the MADS-box gene family in Lonicera japonica, a species with zygomorphic flowers like sesame, led to the characterization of 48 MADS-box genes and the proposal of a floral organ identity model based on expression patterns and protein interactions of ABCDE homologs [18]. These studies demonstrate that integrating phylogenomic, transcriptomic, and interaction data can effectively decipher floral organ determination in species with diverse floral morphologies. However, in sesame, the genetic and molecular mechanisms underlying the formation and regulation of its distinctive tubular floral architecture remain largely unexplored.

To uncover the regulatory mechanisms underlying floral organ development in sesame, this study performed transcriptome sequencing and comparative analysis of sepals, petals, stamens, and carpels. This approach enabled the identification of differentially expressed genes and the construction of a floral organ-specific gene expression network. Focusing on hormone signaling, we analyzed expression patterns to describe a hormone-related regulatory network. Additionally, genome-wide identification of MIKC-MADS genes allowed a preliminary inference of an ABCDE model for sesame floral organ differentiation. Collectively, this study expands the theoretical framework of plant floral organ development, provides insights into its regulatory mechanisms, and offers valuable genetic resources and molecular markers to facilitate molecular-design breeding in sesame.

Methods

Plant materials and sampling

This study utilized the sesame (S.indicum L.) cultivar ‘LiaoZhi No. 8’ as the experimental material. Plants were cultivated in a greenhouse at the Cash Crops Research Institute of Liaoning Province under natural light conditions, with the temperature maintained at 25–28 °C. Conventional agronomic management practices were followed, including appropriate irrigation and fertilization. Floral organs (sepals, petals, stamens, and carpels) were meticulously dissected from flower buds at one day before anthesis (15 days post-anthesis initiation). At this developmental stage, the four whorls of floral organs are fully differentiated and morphologically mature. Three independent biological replicates were collected for each floral organs. Each biological replicate consisted of pooled floral organs from three individual plants harvested on the same day under identical growth conditions. All collected samples were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C for subsequent analysis.

Total RNA isolation and transcriptome sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from 12 samples encompassing four floral organs of sesame using the EASYspin Plus kit (Aidlab, China). Sequencing libraries were prepared according to established protocols and subjected to paired-end sequencing on an Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform (Novogene, China) [19]. Raw reads were processed to remove adapters and low-quality bases. The resulting clean reads were aligned to the sesame reference genome (v1.0, NCBI) using HISAT2 [20]. The transcriptome data generated in this study are available in the NCBI SRA (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra/) under BioProject accession number PRJNA1371083 (Table S1).

Evaluation quantification and differential expressed analysis of sample

Gene expression levels were estimated using the FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase per Million mapped reads) metric [21]. Inter-sample relationships were assessed via Principal Component Analysis (PCA) performed in R (v3.1.1). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified among the four floral organs of sesame using the DESeq2 package. Genes meeting the criteria of an adjusted P-value (False Discovery Rate, FDR) < 0.05 and |log2 Fold Change| ≥ 1 were considered significantly differentially expressed [22].

Functional and clustering analysis of DEGs

To elucidate the biological functions and metabolic pathways associated with the identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs), Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis were performed. GO enrichment analysis was conducted using the GOseq software package to categorize DEGs into three functional categories: biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was carried out using KOBAS 2.0 software to identify metabolic and signal transduction pathways significantly overrepresented among the DEGs by querying against the KEGG database. Statistical significance of enrichment was assessed using a hypergeometric distribution test, followed by multiple testing correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate (FDR). GO terms and KEGG pathways with an adjusted P-value (Padjust) ≤ 0.05 were considered significantly enriched [23]. Hierarchical clustering of DEGs was visualized using MeV 4.9 software [24].

Genome-wide identification of MIKC-MADS genes of S.indicum

The genome of sesame is mainly in the NCBI database (BioProject: No. PRJNA315784 and PRJNA625646). MIKC-MADS proteins were identified using MADS domain HMM profiles (PF00139 and PF01486) (E-values less than 1e-5) from the Pfam database (http://pfam.xfam.org/) [25]. Sequence in NCBI CDD (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd) and SMART (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/) in the database authentication and screening, ensure the accuracy of the MIKC - by MADS gene identification.

Analysis of phylogenetic relations of MIKC-MADS in S.indicum and A.thaliana

The protein sequences of MIKC-MADS in A.thaliana, retrieved from the Plant Transcription Factor Database (PlantTFDB), were aligned with the corresponding sequences identified in sesame using ClustalW. A neighbor-joining phylogenetic tree was constructed with 1000 bootstrap replicates in MEGA-X [26]. The resulting tree was visualized and annotated using the Evolview online tool [27].

Cis-acting elements analysis of SiMIKC-MADS gene family

Putative promoter regions (2000 bp upstream of the transcription start site) for 18 SiMIKC-MADS genes were extracted from the sesame genome. Cis-regulatory elements were predicted using the PlantCARE database [28]. Elements related to hormone response, stress, and growth were identified and their distribution visualized in a heatmap.

Expression analysis of SiMIKC-MADS gene in floral organs

Expression patterns of SiMIKC-MADS genes across four floral organs were analyzed based on FPKM values derived from the RNA-seq data. Genes meeting the criteria of |log₂FC| > 1, FDR < 0.05, and P < 0.05 were defined as differentially expressed. The expression profile was visualized using TBtools [29]. A schematic diagram of sesame floral organ morphology was created for reference.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

Total RNA used for qRT-PCR validation was from the same source as that used for transcriptome sequencing described above. qRT-PCR was performed on a LightCycler® 480 II system. Each qRT-PCR reaction was performed in three technical replicates for each sample. Three independent biological replicates (each derived from separately collected plant individuals) were performed for each floral organ type. SiActin (SIN_1006268) was used as the internal control gene, and its expression stability across the four floral organ types was validated using the GeNorm algorithm. The relative expression levels of ten selected SiMIKC-MADS genes were calculated via the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method, with primer sequences listed in Table S2. Error bars in all figures represent standard deviation (SD) calculated from three biological replicates.

Results

Quality analysis of sesame flower organ transcriptome sequencing data

To elucidate the regulatory mechanisms underlying floral organ development in sesame (S.indicum L.), transcriptome sequencing was performed on four types of floral organs: sepals, petals, stamens, and carpels. A total of 12 samples (three biological replicates per organ) were sequenced using the Illumina platform, generating 74.42 Gb of high-quality data. For each sample, the number of clean reads ranged from 13.76 to 28.98 million, the Q30 base percentage exceeded 94.35%, and the alignment efficiency to the reference genome was between 91.26% and 94.82%, confirming the high quality and suitability of the sequencing data for subsequent analyses (Table S3). The dissected floral structures used for sequencing are shown in Fig. 1A, providing the morphological basis for the sampled materials. Gene expression correlation analysis among samples revealed high correlation coefficients (indicated by red areas in the heatmap) and tight clustering for biological replicates of the same organ, demonstrating excellent experimental reproducibility. In contrast, correlations between samples from different floral organs were significantly lower (blue areas), indicating substantial transcriptomic differences and suggesting distinct, organ-specific gene expression programs (Fig. 1B). Principal component analysis (PCA) further supported these findings (Fig. 1C). The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) explained 42.49% and 12.10% of the total gene expression variance, respectively. In the PCA plot, replicates of the same organ type clustered closely together, while samples from different organs formed distinct, well-separated clusters. This provides strong evidence that the four floral organs possess markedly different transcriptomic profiles.

Number of differentially expressed genes among sesame flower organs

The Count values of genes in sesame flower organs were analyzed by DESeq2, and (|log2FC| ≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05 were used as screening criteria to screen differentially expressed genes. There were 7,574 differentially expressed genes between sepals and petals (up-regulated: 3023; Down-regulated: 4,551). There were 10060 differentially expressed genes between sepal and stamen (up-regulated: 4,905; Down-regulated: 5,155). There were 5685 differentially expressed genes between sepal and carpel (up-regulated: 2,634; Down-regulated: 3,051), and 6,829 differential genes were found between petals and stamens (up-regulated: 3927; Down-regulated: 2,902). There were 7,966 differentially expressed genes between carpel and petal (up-regulated: 3,479; Down-regulated: 4,487), and 10,253 genes were differentially expressed between carpel and stamen (up-regulated: 5,273; Down-regulation: 4,980) (Fig. 2A). Comparing the differential genes of four organs into six groups, it was found that there were a large number of identical genes in the six groups. However, there were 285 genes in sepals and petals, 133 in sepals and stamens, 216 in sepals and carpel, 218 in petals and stamens, 400 in carpel and petals, and 572 in stamens and carpel, which were different from those in other comparisons (Fig. 2B).

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes in sesame floral organs

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed on the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from six paired comparisons of sesame floral organs (Se vs. Pe, Ca vs. Pe, Pe vs. St, Ca vs. St, Se vs. Ca, Se vs. St) (Table 1). The results were systematically annotated across three major GO categories: Cellular Component, Molecular Function, and Biological Process. Within the Cellular Component category, the DEGs were predominantly enriched in cellular anatomical entity (with over 2,300 DEGs in each comparison) and intracellular (with over 1,000 DEGs in each comparison). Notably, the Ca vs. St comparison contained the highest number of genes in these subcategories, with 4,356 and 2,150 genes, respectively, indicating the broad involvement of these DEGs in constituting intracellular structures. Significant enrichment was also observed for protein-containing complex (485 genes in Ca vs. St), suggesting its role in organ-specific regulation. Comparisons involving stamens (Ca vs. St and Se vs. St) exhibited significantly higher gene counts in this category than other pairings, reflecting greater divergence in cellular architecture between stamens and other organs. Regarding Molecular Function, the DEGs were highly concentrated in catalytic activity (with over 2,100 DEGs each, peaking at 3,762 in Ca vs. St) and binding functions (with over 2,000 DEGs each, peaking at 3,710 in Se vs. St), forming the basis for metabolic and molecular interaction differences. Functions such as transcription regulator activity (with over 170 DEGs each) and transporter activity (with over 270 DEGs each) were consistently present, underscoring the importance of transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation in floral organ differentiation. Conversely, certain functions like protein tag and molecular carrier activity showed almost no gene enrichment in specific comparisons. Analysis of Biological Processes revealed significant enrichment of DEGs in cellular process (with over 2,200 DEGs each, highest at 3,666 in Ca vs. St) and metabolic process (with over 1,800 DEGs each, highest at 3,376 in Ca vs. St), indicating that organ differences primarily stem from the remodeling of fundamental cellular and metabolic activities. Processes like biological regulation and response to stimulus also contained substantial numbers of genes. Crucially, processes directly related to organ function, such as reproductive process (122 genes in Ca vs. St) and developmental process (208 genes in Ca vs. St), were significantly enriched, further linking the DEGs to floral organ morphogenesis and reproductive specialization. By comparing the quantity and distribution of annotated genes across the different comparisons, stamens were found to exhibit the strongest functional distinctiveness (Ca vs. St and Se vs. St had total gene counts of 7,805 and 7,807, respectively). Sepals and carpels showed the highest functional similarity (Se vs. Ca had a total of 4,282 genes), while petals displayed an intermediate, transitional state.

Analysis of the expression of differentially expressed genes related to plant hormone signal transduction in sesame flower organs

To investigate the contribution of phytohormone signaling to sesame floral organ development, we analyzed the transcriptomic profiles of genes from eight major hormone pathways across four floral organs, namely sepals (Se), petals (Pe), stamens (St) and carpels (Ca), and further characterized the gene family composition and organ specific expression patterns associated with each pathway (Fig. 4; Table S4). The results showed that the auxin pathway involved five core genes, including AUX1 (3 members), TIR1/AFB (2), AUX/IAA (17), ARF (11), GH3 (2), and SAUR (27). Among these, AUX1 and TIR1 showed high expression in petals and stamens, while the two GH3 genes were highly expressed in sepals and stamens, respectively. The AUX/IAA and ARF families contained a large number of genes highly expressed in petals and carpels. The SAUR family, with the largest number of members, contained highly expressed genes in all four organs, with the highest number in petals (13/27), followed by stamens (8/27). The brassinosteroid pathway comprised seven key gene families: BRI1 (7), BKI1 (1), BAK1 (18), BSK (3), BZR1/2 (1), TCH4 (2), and CYCD3 (4). Among these, BAK1 had the highest number of highly expressed genes in sepals (13/18); BZR1/2 and TCH4 were also highly expressed in sepals, while BSK and CYCD3 were co-expressed at high levels in both sepals and carpels. The ethylene pathway involved six core genes: ETR (3), CTR1 (4), EIN3 (3), SIMKK (6), EBF (2), ERF1/2 (2). Among these, EBF, EIN3, and EBF1/2 exhibited clear organ specificity with high expression in petals and stamens, while ERF1/2 was also highly expressed in sepals and carpels. In contrast, ETR, CTR1, and SIMKK did not show distinct expression trends. The cytokinin pathway identified four genes: CRE1 (1), AHP (2), B-ARR (4), and A-ARR (2). Among these, AHP and B-ARR showed high expression in sepals and carpels, while CRE1 was highly expressed in petals and stamens. The abscisic acid (ABA) pathway contained four genes: ABF (2), PYR/PYL (5), PP2C (5), and SnRK2 (3). Genes in this pathway showed relatively low expression in carpels but were highly expressed in sepals, petals, and stamens. Specifically, most PP2C family members were highly expressed in petals and stamens. The gibberellin pathway involved three genes: GID1 (3), DELLA (8), and TF (5). Among these, GID1 was highly expressed in petals, while the DELLA and TF families contained members that were highly expressed in both sepals and carpels, as well as members specifically expressed in petals or stamens. The jasmonic acid (JA) pathway identified four genes: JAZ (1), COI1 (3), MYC2 (9), and JAR1 (3). Gene expression in this pathway was mainly concentrated in sepals and carpels, with COI1 and JAZ clearly showing high expression in these two organs, while JAR1 and MYC2 exhibited high expression in sepals and petals. The salicylic acid (SA) pathway included three genes: NPR1 (2), PR-1 (2), and TGA (9). Among these, the numbers of highly expressed members were 5 in sepals, 4 in petals, 3 in stamens, and 3 in carpels. In summary, the core genes of each hormone signaling pathway exhibited distinct organ-specific expression patterns in sesame floral organs, suggesting that they may play different regulatory roles during floral organ development.

Identification and Cis-acting element analysis of SiMIKC-MADS gene family in S.indicum

In order to understand the important role of SiMIKC-MADS genes in sesame flower organ formation, we identified the SiMIKC-MADS gene family in sesame. According to the MADS genes in Arabidopsis, they are divided into 14 categories: SEP, AGL6, AGL12, SQUA, AG, TM3/SOC1, FLC, AGL32, AP3/PI, AGL17, AGL15, SVP, MIKC-P and MIKC-S. To explore the evolutionary relationship of MIKC-MADS genes in sesame, we constructed a phylogenetic tree by comparing 18 sesame MIKC-MADS genes with Arabidopsis genes (Fig. 5A). Among them, sesame contained a total of 18 SEP (3), AP1 (3), TM3/SOC1 (1), AGL12 (1), AG (2), AGL32 (1), AP3/PI (3), AGL17 (1), SVP (2) and FLC (1). According to the conserved domain of sesame MIKC-MADS gene, it can be seen that all SiMIKC-MADSs contain MADS superfamily and K-box superfamily domains (Fig. 5B).

To gain insight into the expression and regulatory features of SiMIKC-MADS gene family, we examined their promoter sequences within a 2000 bp region upstream of the coding region (Fig. 5C, Table S5). A total of 44 cis-acting elements were grouped into three categories, including 16 hormone response elements, 10 stress response elements, and 28 growth/development elements and stress response elements. Analysis of the expression pattern of SiMIKC-MADS in sesame flower organs

To elucidate the regulatory functions of MIKC-MADS genes in sesame floral organ development, we characterized their expression profiles using RNA-seq and qRT-PCR. The class A gene SiAP1 was predominantly expressed in sepals and petals. Among class B genes, SiPI showed strong expression in petals and stamens, significantly exceeding levels in sepals and carpels, while paralogs SiAP3a and SiAP3b exhibited similar expression patterns. The class C gene SiAG displayed high expression in stamens and carpels. Notably, the class E gene SiSEP2 was expressed across all four floral organs, with peak expression in petals (457.22). SiSEP4a, SiSEP4b, SiFULa, and SiFULb (also class E) all showed elevated expression in sepals and carpels. These findings indicate distinct roles for SiMIKC-MADS genes in specific floral organs (Fig. 6A, Table S6). Correlation analysis, prompted by potential protein interactions, revealed significant relationships: SiAP1 (A-class) correlated negatively (r = -0.80) with SiAG (C-class) but positively (r = + 0.80) with SiSEP2 (E-class); SiPI (B-class) correlated negatively (r = -0.80) with both SiAG (C-class) and SiSEP4b (E-class), while showing strong positive correlation (r = + 0.80) with SiAP3a (B-class) (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, qRT-PCR quantification of 10 SiMIKC-MADS genes across the floral organs closely mirrored the RNA-seq expression trends, confirming the transcriptome data’s reliability and reproducibility (Fig. 6C).

Expression correlation between MIKC-MADS genes and hormone signaling-related genes

To investigate the potential regulatory relationships between MIKC-MADS transcription factors and hormone signaling pathways, we calculated Spearman correlation coefficients between the expression levels of MIKC-MADS genes and hormone signaling-related genes in different floral organs of sesame(Fig. 7; Table S7). Using thresholds of |ρ| > 0.95 and p < 0.05, we identified a large number of significantly correlated gene pairs, covering multiple hormone signaling pathways including auxin, ethylene, gibberellin, jasmonate, cytokinin, brassinosteroid, and abscisic acid. Among them, several MIKC-MADS genes showed strong positive correlations with specific hormone signaling genes. For example, SiAGL12 exhibited extremely strong positive correlations with the jasmonate signaling gene COI1 (SIN_1010520, ρ = 0.999, p < 0.001), the auxin response factor ARF (SIN_1009079, ρ = 0.984), the cytokinin response regulator B-ARR (SIN_1005380, ρ = 0.982), and the gibberellin signaling repressor DELLA (SIN_1026913, ρ = 0.983). SiAGL16 was positively correlated with SAUR (SIN_1022124, ρ = 0.985), AUX/IAA (SIN_1005070, ρ = 0.986), and the ethylene signaling component CTR1 (SIN_1011296, ρ = 0.971). SiAP3a showed positive correlations with the auxin receptor TIR1 (SIN_1001355, ρ = 0.975), the ethylene signaling factor EIN3 (SIN_1005171, ρ = 0.966), and the gibberellin receptor GID1 (SIN_1004217, ρ = 0.986). SiFULa was positively correlated with the jasmonate signaling gene COI1 (SIN_1010520, ρ = 0.980), MYC2 (SIN_1003516, ρ = 0.965), and the cyclin CYCD3 (SIN_1014897, ρ = 0.998). Meanwhile, some MIKC-MADS genes exhibited significant negative correlations with hormone signaling genes. For instance, SiAG was negatively correlated with the jasmonate biosynthesis gene JAR1 (SIN_1011453, ρ=-0.990) and the abscisic acid receptor PYR/PYL (SIN_1002766, ρ = -0.998). Taken together, these results indicate extensive and strong co-expression relationships between MIKC-MADS genes and multiple hormone signaling pathways.

Discussion

Metabolic and cellular dynamics supporting floral architecture

Floral organogenesis is a highly energy-demanding process requiring precise metabolic reprogramming. Understanding the formation mechanism of sesame is important for the production of pollen, the effective fertilization of carpels, the improvement of sesame seed setting rate, and the guarantee of sesame yield [30–31]. Our transcriptomic analysis revealed that differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in sesame floral organs are predominantly enriched in metabolic processes and catalytic activities, which is consistent with the high physiological turnover required for organ differentiation (Fig. 3) [17]. Specifically, the enrichment of starch and sucrose metabolism pathways in petals and stamens highlights a strategic energy allocation: sucrose likely serves not only as a carbon source for biosynthesis but also as an osmolyte to generate the turgor pressure necessary for petal expansion and anther development [32]. Furthermore, the enrichment of ABC transporters suggests the intracellular transport processes mediated by these proteins may be relatively active. These transporters likely facilitate the mobilization of secondary metabolites and phytohormones (auxin and ABA) across cellular membranes, thereby maintaining the biochemical homeostasis essential for organ-specific developmental functions [33]. The differential expression of photosynthetic antenna protein-encoding genes in sepals further confirms their role as a photosynthetically active “source” tissue, providing photoassimilates to support the energetic and biosynthetic “sink” demands of the adjacent developing reproductive organs [17]. Collectively, these metabolic signatures delineate the tissue-specific physiological activities that are associated with the differentiation and maturation of sesame floral organs.

Spatiotemporal specificity of hormonal signaling networks

Phytohormones act as systemic signals that coordinate the complex developmental programs of floral organs [8]. Our data uncovers a sophisticated hormonal landscape in sesame that parallels, yet distinctively diverges from, model species. Notably, our KEGG analysis also highlighted the MAPK signaling pathway as a major enriched category, suggesting it may function as a signal integration hub, transducing hormonal cues into phosphorylation cascades that fine-tune cellular responses. Regarding growth regulation, we observed a potential synergy between auxin and brassinosteroid (BR) pathways. In sesame, the distinct accumulation of SAUR genes in petals suggests an evolutionarily conserved mechanism where auxin promotes cell wall acidification to drive rapid corolla expansion, a trait crucial for attracting pollinators [34, 35]. Interestingly, in stamens, the high concurrent expression of auxin response factors (ARF) and the BR receptor SiBRI1 implies that these two hormones may synergistically regulate filament elongation and pollen maturation [36, 37]. Furthermore, the sepal-specific expression of GH3 amido synthetases likely creates a local “auxin sink” by conjugating excess auxin, thereby fine-tuning the boundary between the sepal and inner whorls [38, 39].

Distinct from the growth-promoting hormones, ethylene and cytokinin (CK) signaling pathways appear to be finely tuned to ensure reproductive success. Ethylene signaling components, particularly SiETR receptors and the transcription factor SiEIN3, were highly enriched in stamens. Given the established role of ethylene in programmed cell death (PCD), this expression pattern strongly suggests its involvement in controlling the precise timing of anther dehiscence and senescence [13, 40]. Conversely, cytokinin signaling exhibits marked spatial segregation of function: the receptor SiCRE1 in petals and stamens likely supports cell division [41], whereas the specific accumulation of A-type ARRs (negative regulators) in carpels serves to dampen CK sensitivity, potentially preventing precocious gametophyte development [42]. Finally, in contrast to the internal regulation of development, stress-related pathways (ABA, JA, SA) were preferentially active in the outer whorls (sepals and petals). This specific distribution likely establishes a “proactive defense barrier,” employing MYC2-mediated transcriptional networks to protect the vulnerable reproductive centers from biotic and abiotic stresses during the critical flowering period [16, 43].

The MADS-box regulatory framework and its integration with hormonal signals

Beyond the hormonal landscape, the MIKC-MADS transcription factors serve as the master selectors of floral organ identity. Our comprehensive profiling of the sesame MADS-box family provides robust molecular evidence for the conservation of the canonical ABCDE model in sesame [18]. Specifically, the strict spatial separation of Class A (SiAP1) and Class C (SiAG) transcripts, supported by a strong negative correlation (Pearson r = -0.80), confirms the antagonistic mechanism that sharpens the boundary between the perianth (sepals/petals) and the reproductive organs (stamens/carpels) (Fig. 6B) [3]. Furthermore, the ubiquitous expression of the Class E gene SiSEP2, which showed strong positive correlations with both Class A (SiAP1, r = + 0.80) and Class B (SiPI, r = + 0.80) genes, underscores its fundamental role as the “molecular glue.” This suggests that SiSEP2 is essential for mediating the assembly of higher-order quaternary protein complexes, which are required to lock in organ specificity [44]. It is also worth noting that we identified specific gene duplications within the B-class lineage (SiAP3a and SiAP3b). This expansion likely points to evolutionary sub-functionalization or neo-functionalization, which may contribute to the unique zygomorphic (bilaterally symmetrical) floral morphology of sesame, distinguishing it from the actinomorphic flowers of model species like A.thaliana.

Beyond the interactions among MIKC-MADS genes themselves, these factors also connect with hormone signaling in a complex, multi-layered regulatory network. Promoter analysis revealed that SiMIKC-MADS genes carry various hormone-responsive cis-elements in their promoter regions, such as ABRE, AuxRE, and GARE [45]. For example, the SiSHP2 promoter contains 27 different hormone-responsive motifs, suggesting that this gene may directly integrate multiple hormonal signals. MIKC-MADS factors can regulate hormone signaling in both directions. For example, the B-class gene SiAP3a positively correlates with the auxin receptor TIR1, the ethylene factor EIN3, and the gibberellin receptor GID1. In contrast, SiAP3b positively correlates with the brassinosteroid receptor BRI1 and the gibberellin repressor DELLA—suggesting the two copies have taken on different roles [46]. Several other MIKC-MADS genes (like SiAGL12, SiAGL16, and SiFULa) all show positive correlations with genes from auxin, jasmonate, cytokinin, and ethylene pathways. The high correlation (ρ = 0.998) between SiFULa and the cyclin CYCD3 is noteworthy, suggesting a possible coordinated role in cell cycle regulation, although the underlying mechanism remains to be tested. On the other hand, negative correlations also exist: SiAG correlates negatively with the jasmonate synthesis gene JAR1 and the abscisic acid receptor PYR/PYL; SiPI correlates negatively with auxin and gibberellin signaling genes. These could form feedback inhibition loops that give the flower development network some self-balancing ability. Overall, the relationship between MIKC-MADS and hormone signaling is not a simple linear chain, but rather a complex network of correlations that warrants further functional investigation.

This hormone-MADS regulatory module may provide a useful genetic framework for investigating the plastic development of sesame flowers under varying environmental conditions (Fig. 8) [47]. However, the transcriptomic profiles reported here capture floral organs at a single temporal snapshot—one day before anthesis—thereby offering a static view of what is an inherently dynamic developmental trajectory. Consequently, directly correlating these expression patterns with specific morphological milestones, such as zygomorphic corolla tube formation, anther dehiscence timing, and carpel maturation, remains a challenge that necessitates further investigation. Future research should transition from these foundational “digital maps” to a more granular, multidimensional understanding of floral organogenesis. Integrating time-resolved transcriptomics with emerging technologies such as spatial transcriptomics or single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) may help deconstruct cell-fate determination processes across the L1, L2, and L3 layers of the floral primordia. Such high-resolution approaches would bridge the gap between transcript abundance and the actual biochemical activities of hormone signaling components within specific niches of floral tissues.

Resolving these regulatory uncertainties will require genetic and biochemical validation tailored to the unique architecture of sesame. Targeted disruption of key candidate genes—most notably the stamen-enriched SiAG and members of the auxin-responsive SiARF family—via CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing or virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) could clarify their precise roles in organ identity determination [48]. Furthermore, complementary biochemical assays, such as yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) to test MADS-box and hormone regulator (Aux/IAA or DELLA) interactions, alongside ChIP-seq to define genome-wide binding landscapes, will reveal the molecular architecture underlying the patterns observed in this study. Ultimately, by providing insights into the possible evolutionary significance of zygomorphy and organ development in S.indicum, this work not only provides indispensable genetic resources for molecular breeding—such as the development of male-sterile germplasm or ideal plant architectures—but also enriches our broader understanding of the developmental diversity inherent in dicotyledonous floral evolution.

Conclusion

This study presents the systematic transcriptomic dissection of floral organ development in S.indicum. Comparative analysis of gene expression profiles across four floral organs yielded 74.42 Gb of high-quality data and identified thousands of differentially expressed genes. Functional enrichment analysis highlighted the significant roles of these genes in metabolic processes, cellular components, and key signaling pathways—particularly plant hormone signal transduction and starch-sucrose metabolism. In-depth investigation revealed that core gene families from eight hormone signaling pathways exhibit distinct and organ-specific expression patterns in sepals, petals, stamens, and carpels, collectively forming a complex signaling network that coordinately regulates floral organ development. Moreover, 18 identified MIKC-MADS genes were found to harbor abundant cis-acting elements associated with hormone response and growth/development. Their expression patterns fully align with the classic ABCDE model of floral organ development, with class A, B, C, and E genes demonstrating strict spatiotemporal specificity, confirming their central regulatory roles in determining sesame floral organ identity. This study constructed the comprehensive transcriptomic atlas of sesame floral organs. It systematically elucidated the molecular mechanisms through which hormone signaling networks and the MIKC-MADS transcription factor framework coordinately regulate floral development. Furthermore, it provides a critical theoretical foundation and valuable genetic resources for molecular breeding aimed at improving sesame yield and stress resistance.
