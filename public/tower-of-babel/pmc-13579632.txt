Health Challenges and Fatigue Among Female Night Shift Nurses: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Hui‐Mei Huang, Pi‐Hua Chang, Shih‐Yu Lee, Mei‐Ling Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo investigate fatigue and its related factors among female nurses working night shifts.DesignA cross‐sectional comparative study.MethodsThis study was conducted using convenience sampling at a medical centre in central Taiwan from May to October 2023. A total of 230 female nurses working rotating night shifts were recruited, and 225 were included in the final analysis. Data were collected using self‐reported questionnaires, including the General Sleep Disturbance Scale, Lee Fatigue Scale, Perceived Stress Scale, General Self‐Efficacy Scale, Beck Depression Inventory‐II, Health Promotion Lifestyle Profile‐II Chinese Version Short Form and the Morningness–Eveningness Questionnaire. Data were analysed using group comparisons, correlation analysis and hierarchical multiple linear regression.ResultsClinically significant fatigue was identified in 84.0% of participants, and 67.6% met the criterion for sleep disturbance. Sleepiness fluctuated across the night shift and was highest at 9:00 AM, with a secondary elevation at 5:00–6:00 AM. Although fatigue was more common among nurses assessed during the 3 days before menstruation and the first 4 days of menstruation, this group difference did not remain significant after adjustment for multiple comparisons. Sleep disturbance, depressive symptoms and perceived stress were independently associated with greater fatigue, and the final model explained 29.9% of the variance.ConclusionsThis study highlights the prevalence and cyclical nature of fatigue among female night shift nurses and underscores the importance of addressing both physical and psychological contributors to fatigue.Implications for the Profession and/or Patient CareUnderstanding the patterns and predictors of nurse fatigue can support the development of workplace interventions to improve staff well‐being and patient safety, particularly in high‐stress environments such as hospitals.Reporting MethodThe STROBE checklist guided reporting.Patient or Public ContributionThis study did not include patient or public involvement in its design, conduct or reporting.Keywords: depression, fatigue, menstrual cycle, nurses, psychological stress, sleep

Introduction

Nursing is an inherently demanding profession, requiring nurses to balance work efficiency, quality of care, and patient needs effectively amidst considerable pressure. Typically, nursing work follows a shift system, with nurses alternating to provide 24‐h hospital care. Shift lengths commonly span 8 or 12 h. In Taiwan, most nurses adhere to eight‐hour shifts, rotating between day, evening and night. While it is well documented that sleep disturbances can lead to health problems such as obesity, a more immediate concern for nurses is fatigue (Books et al. 2017). Characterized by persistent tiredness or exhaustion, fatigue affects 20.4% of the general population (Yoon et al. 2023). Nurses are particularly susceptible to heightened fatigue levels because of the demands of shift work and the intense emotional and physical exertion inherent in patient care.

Background

Approximately 90% of night shift workers report feelings of fatigue and drowsiness (Richter et al. 2016). Individual adaptation to shift work varies, however, and is influenced by age, gender and personal circadian rhythms. Age has been identified as a significant factor. Previous studies highlighted that both sleep quality and duration decline with age (Chang and Peng 2021; Saksvik, Bjorvatn, Harvey, et al. 2011). In contrast, younger shift workers tend to have better sleep quality and longer sleep durations (Chang and Peng 2021).

A recent study conducted in Taiwan found that night shift nurses require more sleep than day shift nurses, yet they experience lower sleep efficiency, with some relying on sleep aids to manage their sleep issues (Chang and Li 2022). In particular, night shift nurses exhibit poorer sleep quality compared to their day shift counterparts (Chang and Peng 2021). Poor sleep quality negatively impacts health, leading to increased fatigue, diminished attention and a higher risk of medical errors (Gander et al. 2019; Min et al. 2021). The fatigue and sleep issues faced by nurses warrant serious attention.

Adaptation to shift work varies with individual chronotypes, which are personal circadian preferences classified into morningness and eveningness; persons with which attributes are also known respectively as ‘early birds’ and ‘night owls’ (Nimrod 2015). Female nurses tend to be more morning‐oriented than males (Debbia et al. 2021; Randler and Engelke 2019). Married individuals, older nurses and those with children also demonstrate a greater tendency towards morningness (Debbia et al. 2021; Reinke et al. 2015). Morning‐type nurses generally experience better sleep quality and overall health (Debbia et al. 2021), but they are more susceptible to sleepiness and fatigue during night shifts (Zion et al. 2018).

In general, the body's endogenous melatonin levels begin to rise approximately 2 h before natural sleep, reaching their peak about 5 h later (Bartlett et al. 2013). The misalignment between shift work and natural circadian rhythms first manifests as sleep disturbances, leading to fatigue. Fatigue adversely affects nurses' alertness, which is essential in nursing care (Min et al. 2021), and adequate rest is a key factor in mitigating fatigue. Sufficient restorative sleep between and before shifts is critical for optimal nurse performance and for providing high‐quality care (Ganesan et al. 2019). Sleep disturbances are often exacerbated by psychological and lifestyle factors, necessitating a focus on healthy living, family dynamics and overall health (Lee and Ward 2005). Shift work, however, often negatively impacts health behaviours, making it difficult for nurses to engage in activities such as regular exercise (Persson and Mårtensson 2006). Shift work disrupts sleep and health, leading to fatigue and reduced alertness, which hinders nurses from maintaining healthy habits.

Adopting healthier lifestyles could reduce work stress and improve sleep quality (Lim et al. 2019). The nature of night shift work, however, often leads to unhealthy behaviours such as poor dietary choices, excessive caffeine consumption and lack of physical exercise (Persson and Mårtensson 2006; Samhat et al. 2020). Although caffeine may improve alertness, it can exacerbate sleep disturbances, particularly in older individuals (Carrier et al. 2009). Irregular sleep patterns and unhealthy habits further perpetuate fatigue and disrupt circadian rhythms, contributing to a cycle of health deterioration (Ejebu et al. 2021; Mulhall et al. 2019). Thus, fatigue is a complex issue for nurses and has severe consequences, negatively impacting their well‐being and professional work.

Landmark studies, moreover, have highlighted that women are more susceptible to sleep disorders and excessive drowsiness during night and rotating shifts (K. A. Lee 1992). This is particularly true in the 3 days preceding and the 4 days during menstruation, when sleep quality is notably poorer (Baker and Driver 2004). Subsequent studies have also shown that men generally adjust better to night shifts (Saksvik, Bjorvatn, Hetland, et al. 2011). In contrast, women who work shifts often experience more sleep disturbances and fatigue (Chang and Li 2022). Women may experience worsening sleep disturbances, such as insomnia, fatigue and nightmares, during the luteal phase (Parry et al. 2006; Ryu and Kim 2015). Additionally, they often endure physical and psychological symptoms during menstruation, including dysmenorrhea, fatigue and emotional instability in both the premenstrual and menstrual phases (Baker and Lee 2022; Schoep et al. 2019). These factors require women to expend more energy in managing bodily changes. The unique hormonal issues faced by female nurses, however, are barely considered in shift scheduling and research projects.

Although nurse fatigue has been extensively studied, few investigations have considered the menstrual cycle as a potential confounding factor. Given that women make up the majority of the nursing workforce and undergo unique physiological changes—such as hormonal fluctuations and menstrual cycles—these factors might interact with shift work patterns, circadian misalignment (e.g., chronotype and night shifts) and psychological stress, thereby intensifying fatigue. This study adopts an integrated conceptual framework that views fatigue as the outcome of multiple physiological and psychological influences. It aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of fatigue among female night shift nurses by examining the relationships between age, chronotype, menstrual cycle, sleep patterns, work schedules and health‐promoting behaviours. Ultimately, the study seeks to identify key contributors to fatigue and offer practical recommendations for reducing fatigue among nurses.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This study utilized a cross‐sectional comparative design with convenience sampling and followed STROBE guidelines for reporting cross‐sectional studies (von Elm et al. 2007). The study took place in a large medical centre in central Taiwan with nearly 2000 nurses across 50 units. Because of gender differences noted in previous literature (Chang and Li 2022; Saksvik, Bjorvatn, Harvey, et al. 2011), only female nurses were included. The hospital operates on a three‐shift system, each shift lasting 8 h, including the night shift from 12:00 AM to 8:00 AM. Nurses rotate shifts monthly.

All participants were rotating shift nurses who were scheduled to work night shifts during the data collection period. Although shift schedules were rotated monthly, recruitment was limited to nurses assigned to night duty during the study month. Nurses working only day or evening shifts during the data collection period were not included.

Based on previous studies related to nurse fatigue, with reported R 2 values ranging from 0.126 to 0.54 (Chang and Li 2022; Samaha et al. 2007), G*Power 3.1.9.7 was used to conduct an F‐test for linear multiple regression (fixed model, R 2 deviation from zero). The effect size was calculated as f 2 = 0.126/(1 − 0.126) = 0.144. The significance level (α) was set at 0.05, statistical power (1 − β) at 0.95, and 10 predictors were specified. The estimated required sample size was 230 participants. A total of 230 female nurses were recruited. After excluding five incomplete questionnaires, 225 participants were included in the final analysis. Because the final analytic sample was slightly smaller than the estimated requirement, a post hoc power analysis was conducted in SPSS for the final three‐predictor regression model. With N = 225, α = 0.05, and a population multiple partial correlation of 0.355, corresponding to the prespecified effect size of f 2 = 0.144, the achieved power was 0.999.

Data were collected through questionnaires from female, full‐time nurses aged 20 and above who had been employed for at least 6 months and were assigned to night shifts in the current month. Exclusion criteria included: (1) diagnosis of sleep apnea or related disorders, (2) pregnancy and (3) no night shift work in the past year. Data collection took place within 1 week of the night shift and included demographic information.

Instruments

Sociodemographic Information

We collected sociodemographic information about work schedules. Additionally, we gathered data on age, menstrual cycle, height, weight, marital status, education, caregiving responsibilities, distance between home and workplace, current unit work hours, overall nursing experience and unit type. We used open‐ended questions to collect information about how much sleep participants required to feel refreshed. Two senior nursing researchers reviewed the content of the above information to ensure relevance and clarity. We also utilized psychometric sound questionnaires to gather information on fatigue and alertness, sleep and health‐related information, and levels of stress. All questionnaires and scales used in this study were previously published and validated instruments. The following section provides an explanation of the relevant instruments.

Fatigue and Alertness Information

The Lee's Fatigue Scale (LFS) measures the severity of fatigue experienced over the past week (Lee et al. 1991), with higher scores reflecting greater levels of fatigue. The LFS has been used in previous studies to assess both acute and chronic fatigue, particularly in the context of sleep‐related issues and shift work. The Chinese short form version (C‐LFS‐SF) is derived from the LFS and includes seven items that have been translated and validated (Tsai et al. 2014). It has a Cronbach's alpha of 0.97. An average score > 3.2 indicates clinically significant fatigue, with higher scores reflecting greater fatigue. Participants were asked to begin filling out the C‐LFS‐SF 1 week after completing their night shift. They completed the survey before bedtime and immediately after waking up for seven consecutive days. In this study, we used the C‐LFS‐SF to measure cumulative fatigue experienced during a one‐week night shift rotation. This approach aligns with our focus on persistent fatigue associated with circadian misalignment and hormonal fluctuations related to the menstrual cycle. In the current study, the Cronbach's alpha coefficient for the C‐LFS‐SF was 0.91.

The Stanford Sleepiness Scale (SSS), developed by Hoddes et al. (1973), was used to describe current levels of sleepiness, ranging from fully alert (1) to unable to stay awake (7). Participants select the number corresponding to their level of sleepiness. This study employed the Chinese version of the SSS, translated and validated by Yang et al. (2004). Participants' sleepiness levels were recorded hourly, starting 1 h before the shift at 11 PM and continuing until 1 h after the shift at 9 AM, over a consecutive 7‐night data collection period. This allowed for a continuous assessment of sleepiness and alertness throughout the night shift. Higher scores of the SSS indicate greater sleepiness and reduced alertness.

Sleep‐Related Information

The Morningness–Eveningness Questionnaire (MEQ), which was modified by Horne and Östberg (1976) from Swedish version, is used to assess individual circadian preferences. The MEQ, which includes 19 items, was translated into Chinese and validated by Hsiao and Tsai (2017). The Chinese version had a Cronbach's alpha reliability coefficient of 0.70 (n = 213) and a test–retest reliability of 0.71 after 1 month (n = 28) (Hsiao and Tsai 2017). The study used criterion‐related validity to confirm that the total score of the MEQ was significantly negatively correlated with both bedtime and wake‐up time (ranging from −0.40 to −0.60). Higher scores indicate a preference for mornings, while lower scores suggest an evening preference. The scores range from 16 to 86 and classify individuals into five types: definite morning type (DM, 70–86), moderate morning type (MM, 59–69), intermediate type (IT, 42–58), moderate evening type (ME, 31–41) and definite evening type (DE, 16–30). The Cronbach's alpha of the MEQ in this study was 0.68.

The General Sleep Disturbance Scale (GSDS) was used to collect subjective sleep data (K. A. Lee 1992, 2007). It assesses weekly sleep patterns with 21 items rated on a 0–7 scale, where 0 means ‘never’ and seven means ‘every day’. The scale includes sleep quality (six items), sleep quantity (two items), fatigue and alertness during work (seven items), and sleep aid use (six items). The Chinese version, as translated by S. Y. Lee (2007), has a Cronbach's alpha of 0.81. Concurrent validity with actigraphy showed correlations for sleep quantity (r = 0.4) and morning fatigue (r = 0.42, p = 0.05). The Cronbach's alpha of the GSDS in this sample was 0.76. Total scores equal to and above 43 or average subscale scores > 3 indicate clinically significant sleep disturbances (Fletcher et al. 2008; S. Y. Lee 2007).

Health‐Related Information

The Health Promotion Lifestyle Profile‐II (HPLP‐II) was initially developed by Walker (1987) to measure personal health‐promoting behaviours. The scale includes 52 items divided into six subscales. Teng et al. (2010) translated it into Chinese and developed a shortened version, the HPLP‐IICR, which included 30 items and five subscales: spiritual growth (six items), physical activity (six items), health management (nine items), nutrition (five items) and health responsibility (four items). Respondents use a Likert scale with four options: never, sometimes, often and always. Higher scores indicate greater satisfaction with health status and quality of life (Teng et al. 2010). The 30‐item HPLP‐IICR has a Cronbach's alpha of 0.90, with subscale alphas ranging from 0.69 to 0.87. It demonstrated criterion‐related validity with a quality of life scale, the short version of the World Health Organization Quality of Life (WHOQOL‐BREF) (r = 0.52, p = 0.001), explaining 53% of variance, compared to the original HPLP's 46%. The Cronbach's alpha coefficient of HPLP‐II in this sample was 0.93.

The Beck Depression Inventory‐II (BDI‐II) is one of the most widely used scales for self‐report of emotional states or depressive symptoms, measured through 21 items, each rated on a scale from 0 to 3 (Beck et al. 1996). The Chinese version BDI‐II is highly reliable, with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.86. Factor analysis identifies cognitive‐affective and somatic dimensions, explaining 44.39% of the variance. Scores range from 0 to 63, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms (Chang 2005). The Cronbach's alpha coefficient of BDI‐II in this sample was 0.92.

Stress‐Related Information

The Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) is used to measure perceived global stress, including feelings of control and tension, with 14 items (Cohen et al. 1983). It uses a 5‐point Likert scale, with scores ranging from 14 to 70. Higher scores indicate greater stress. The overall Cronbach's alpha is 0.78, with subscales for feelings of control (0.688) and tension (0.576).

The General Self‐Efficacy Scale (GSES) measures an individual's belief in their ability to manage stress effectively, handle challenging situations and overcome obstacles (Schwarzer and Jerusalem 1995). The GSES consists of 10 items rated on a 4‐point Likert scale. In the Chinese version of the GSES, Cronbach's alpha for reliability was 0.87, and a test–retest reliability of 0.83 (Wang et al. 2001). This study measured how well night shift nurses manage challenging situations. Higher scores indicate stronger self‐efficacy. The Cronbach's alpha coefficient of GSES in this sample was 0.93.

Procedure

The principal investigator (PI) provided the nurses with an information letter explaining the purpose and procedures of the study. Female night shift nurses were invited to participate. If interested and eligible, they met with the PI or research assistant at a convenient location to receive a detailed explanation of the study process. After providing informed consent, they would officially join the study.

All participating nurses followed a rotating monthly shift schedule, and data collection was scheduled to begin 1 week after the start of their night shift cycle. This timing was chosen to assess the cumulative physiological and psychological effects of night work. With the exception of the Stanford Sleepiness Scale (SSS) and the Chinese version of the Lee Fatigue Scale–Short Form (C‐LFS‐SF), all questionnaires were completed once at the end of the one‐week data collection period. To ensure both convenience and completeness, participants either placed their completed questionnaires into designated collection boxes in their unit or arranged for a research assistant to collect them.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS version 19 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). Using Lee's Fatigue Scale, a cut‐off score of 3.2 or higher indicated clinically significant fatigue (Fletcher et al. 2008; Lee et al. 1991; Tsai et al. 2014). The nurses reported on the regularity and timing of their last two menstrual cycles, which were used to calculate their cycle phases. Participants who were menopausal, had irregular menstrual cycles, or whose menstrual phase could not be determined were categorized as not being within the 3 days before menstruation or the first 4 days of menstruation.

Descriptive statistics were calculated using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and means with standard deviations (SDs) for continuous variables. Independent‐samples t‐tests were used to compare continuous variables between the fatigue and non‐fatigue groups. Pearson's chi‐squared test was used for categorical variables when expected cell‐count assumptions were met; Fisher's exact test or the Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test was used when those assumptions were not met. Effect sizes were reported as Hedges' g for continuous variables, phi (Φ) for 2 × 2 tables and Cramér's V for larger contingency tables. Pearson's correlations were used to examine associations among the study variables. Holm adjustment was applied separately within each family of comparisons, comprising 28 continuous‐variable comparisons in Table 1, 11 categorical‐variable comparisons in Table 2, and 21 pairwise correlations in Figure 2. Holm‐adjusted p‐values were calculated using full‐precision, unrounded p‐values. Hierarchical multiple linear regression was then conducted with the LFS total score as the dependent variable. GSDS was entered in Model 1, BDI‐II was added in Model 2, and PSS was added in Model 3. Statistical significance was set at a two‐tailed p < 0.05.

Ethical Considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board I & II of Taichung Veterans General Hospital, Taichung, Taiwan (approval number: CE23076A; approval date: 10 February 2022). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before participation.

Results

Participant Characteristics

A total of 230 nurses were initially recruited based on the a priori sample size estimation. After excluding five questionnaires due to incomplete responses, 225 female night shift nurses were included in the final statistical analysis (Table 1). The participants' ages ranged from 22 to 61 years, with a mean age of 32.93 (SD = 8.73). Their BMI ranged from 16 to 39 kg/m2 (M = 22.32 kg/m2, SD = 4.13). Waistline measurements were reported by 150 nurses, with a mean of 74.51 cm (SD = 12.42). The nurses' self‐assessment of the need to invest in family care ranged from 0 to 10, with 10 indicating full involvement and 0 indicating no involvement. The mean score was 5.18 (SD = 2.45). The nurses' commuting time from home or dormitory to the hospital averaged 18.66 min (SD = 11.98). Their clinical experience ranged from 1 to 35 years, with an average of 9.62 years (SD = 8.50), and their shift work experience averaged 8.80 years (SD = 8.04). The perceived sleep duration needed to feel fully energetic ranged from 4 to 18 h (M = 8.34 h, SD = 1.94).

In order to investigate fatigue and its related factors among female nurses working the night shift, the participants were categorized into fatigue (n = 189, 84%) and non‐fatigue (n = 36, 16%) groups on the basis of a cut‐off point of 3.2 or higher in the Lee's Fatigue Scale (Fletcher et al. 2008; Lee et al. 1991; Tsai et al. 2014). This categorization allowed for further comparison of the different characteristics between the two groups (Table 1).

Characteristics Comparison Between the Fatigue and Non‐Fatigue Groups

Nurses in the fatigue group were slightly older, with a mean age of 33.22 years (SD = 8.79), compared with 31.40 years (SD = 8.36) in the non‐fatigue group. This difference was not statistically significant (t = −1.15, Holm‐adjusted p = 1.000). No significant group differences were found for BMI, waist circumference, family caregiving involvement, commuting time, nursing experience, shift‐work experience or the amount of sleep needed to feel energetic (Table 1). A larger proportion of the fatigue group was assessed during the 3 days before menstruation or the first 4 days of menstruation than the non‐fatigue group (30.2% vs. 11.1%). However, this association was small and did not remain significant after Holm adjustment, χ 2(1) = 5.55, Φ = 0.157, unadjusted p = 0.018, Holm‐adjusted p = 0.148 (Table 2).

The Stanford Sleepiness Scale (SSS) was used to measure the mean sleepiness and alertness status of night shift nurses from 11 PM to 9 AM. The results showed that the mean SSS score was 2.51 (SD = 0.87) (Table 1). There was no statistically significant difference in mean SSS scores between the non‐fatigue and fatigue groups (2.33 ± 1.03 vs. 2.55 ± 0.83, respectively; t = −1.41, unadjusted p = 0.160, Holm‐adjusted p = 1.000). Night shift nurses started their shift feeling not fully alert (M = 2.72, SD = 1.58), and the highest levels of sleepiness were observed towards the end of the shift, peaking at 9 AM (M = 3.32, SD = 1.84), followed by 5 AM (M = 2.84, SD = 1.58) and 6 AM (M = 2.74, SD = 1.64). These findings indicate that sleepiness fluctuated across the night shift, with a secondary elevation at 5–6 AM and the highest mean score occurring at 9 AM after the shift (Figure 1).

Sleepiness status of nurses from 11 PM to 9 AM.

The participants' chronotypes were determined using the MEQ. Only two nurses (0.9%) were classified as moderately morning type (MM), and 120 nurses (53.3%) as intermediate type (IT). Because the MM subgroup was very small, MM and IT were combined into a single category (MM/IT) for analysis. In addition, 93 nurses (41.3%) were moderately evening type (ME), and 10 nurses (4.4%) were definitely evening type (DE); these groups were combined as ME/DE. Chronotype distribution did not differ significantly between the fatigue and non‐fatigue groups, χ 2(1) = 1.65, Φ = 0.086, unadjusted p = 0.199, Holm‐adjusted p = 0.994 (Table 2). Similarly, mean MEQ scores did not differ between the fatigue and non‐fatigue groups (41.97 ± 6.56 vs. 40.39 ± 6.24, t = −1.33, Holm‐adjusted p = 1.000; Table 1).

Using the GSDS cut‐off score of 43, 67.6% of the night shift nurses met the criterion for sleep disturbance. At the subscale cut‐off of 3, 62.2% reported poor sleep quality, 70.2% insufficient sleep quantity, and 60.9% fatigue and reduced alertness at work (Table 2). Sleep disturbance was more common in the fatigue group than in the non‐fatigue group (73.5% vs. 36.1%, χ 2(1) = 19.33, Φ = 0.293, Holm‐adjusted p < 0.001). The fatigue group also had higher mean GSDS scores (51.53 ± 13.53 vs. 40.39 ± 14.45, t = −4.48, Holm‐adjusted p < 0.001), poorer sleep quality (3.32 ± 1.04 vs. 2.72 ± 1.05, t = −3.18, Holm‐adjusted p = 0.039), and greater fatigue and reduced alertness at work (3.32 ± 0.98 vs. 2.46 ± 0.93, t = −4.82, Holm‐adjusted p < 0.001). Sleep quantity and the use of substances to induce sleep did not differ significantly between groups (Tables 1 and 2).

The HPLP‐II and BDI‐II were used to assess health‐related factors. Mean HPLP‐II scores were similar in the non‐fatigue and fatigue groups (72.56 ± 14.76 vs. 72.25 ± 13.90), and neither the total score nor any HPLP‐II subscale differed significantly after Holm adjustment. By contrast, the fatigue group reported more depressive symptoms than the non‐fatigue group on the BDI‐II (11.35 ± 8.98 vs. 5.83 ± 6.95, t = −3.49, Holm‐adjusted p = 0.014). The fatigue group also had higher cognitive‐affective scores (8.24 ± 7.32 vs. 4.31 ± 5.38, t = −3.78, Holm‐adjusted p = 0.009) and somatic scores (3.11 ± 2.31 vs. 1.53 ± 1.81, t = −3.87, Holm‐adjusted p = 0.004) (Table 1).

The PSS was used to assess perceived stress, and the GSES was used to assess self‐efficacy. The fatigue group had a higher mean PSS score than the non‐fatigue group (27.27 ± 6.92 vs. 23.33 ± 8.13), but the difference did not remain statistically significant after Holm adjustment (t = −3.04, unadjusted p = 0.003, Holm‐adjusted p = 0.059). The same pattern was observed for the perceived stress subscale (13.26 ± 4.41 vs. 10.92 ± 4.38, t = −2.92, unadjusted p = 0.004, Holm‐adjusted p = 0.081), while perceived control did not differ significantly between groups. Mean GSES scores were lower in the fatigue group than in the non‐fatigue group (24.98 ± 5.24 vs. 26.53 ± 5.72), but this difference was also not significant (t = 1.60, Holm‐adjusted p = 1.000) (Table 1).

Predictors for Fatigue

Before regression analysis, Pearson's correlations were examined among LFS, MEQ, GSDS, HPLP‐II, GSES, PSS and BDI‐II (Figure 2). Fatigue was positively correlated with sleep disturbance (r = 0.468), perceived stress (r = 0.381), and depressive symptoms (r = 0.435), and negatively correlated with self‐efficacy (r = −0.193). These associations remained significant after Holm adjustment. Fatigue was not significantly correlated with chronotype or health‐promoting lifestyle. Perceived stress was also associated with lower self‐efficacy (r = −0.560), greater sleep disturbance (r = 0.318) and more depressive symptoms (r = 0.593).

Hierarchical multiple linear regression was used to examine factors associated with fatigue (Table 3). Sleep disturbance entered in Model 1 explained 21.9% of the variance, R 2 = 0.219, adjusted R 2 = 0.216, ΔF(1, 223) = 62.705, p < 0.001. Adding depressive symptoms in Model 2 increased the explained variance by 6.1%, ΔR 2 = 0.061, ΔF(1, 222) = 18.972, p < 0.001. Perceived stress added a further 1.8% in Model 3, ΔR 2 = 0.018, ΔF(1, 221) = 5.625, p = 0.019. The final model explained 29.9% of the variance in fatigue, adjusted R 2 = 0.289, F(3, 221) = 31.387, p < 0.001. Sleep disturbance (β = 0.331, p < 0.001), depressive symptoms (β = 0.185, p = 0.014) and perceived stress (β = 0.166, p = 0.019) were independently associated with greater fatigue. Tolerance values ranged from 0.568 to 0.787 and VIF values from 1.271 to 1.760, indicating no concerning multicollinearity. The Durbin–Watson statistic was 1.601.

Discussion

Fatigue was common among the female night shift nurses in this study and reflected both physical and psychological strain. Sleep disturbance showed the strongest independent association with fatigue, while depressive symptoms and perceived stress contributed additional explanatory value. Several demographic and lifestyle variables were not associated with fatigue after adjustment for multiple comparisons. Although fatigue was more frequent around the premenstrual and early menstrual period, that group difference did not remain statistically significant after Holm adjustment and should be interpreted cautiously.

Nurses represent the largest professional group in the healthcare system. They often carry heavy workloads and work shifts to ensure round‐the‐clock patient care. In this study, 84.0% of female night shift nurses experienced clinically significant fatigue. Sleep disturbance, depressive symptoms and perceived stress together explained 29.9% of the variance in fatigue. This is consistent with earlier evidence that fatigue and drowsiness are common among night shift workers (Richter et al. 2016). Clinically significant fatigue can place a considerable burden on nurses' physical and psychological well‐being (Racciatti et al. 2001). We also observed a higher proportion of fatigue among nurses assessed near menstruation, but this association was no longer significant after adjustment for multiple comparisons. It may still be clinically relevant, but further study with repeated menstrual‐cycle measurements is needed before drawing a firm conclusion.

Previous research identified age as a key factor influencing fatigue (Chang and Peng 2021; Saksvik, Bjorvatn, Harvey, et al. 2011); however, this study found no association between age and clinical fatigue among female night shift nurses. Additionally, variables such as BMI, waist circumference, education level, work unit, years of nursing experience, number of days off, participation in family caregiving, commuting time and fixed schedules were not significantly associated with fatigue. No significant correlation was found, moreover, between healthy lifestyle habits and clinically significant fatigue in these homogeneous study participants.

The primary cause of fatigue may lie in the misalignment between night shift work and the body's circadian rhythms. Previous studies using chronotype scales have revealed that shift work can disrupt circadian rhythms, limiting restorative sleep and increasing fatigue (Ejebu et al. 2021; Mulhall et al. 2019). Our chronotype analysis found no significant association with clinically significant fatigue. Even nurses with an evening chronotype still experienced significant fatigue during night shifts, highlighting the physiological limitations imposed by night work. The study supports previous findings that night shift nurses are more prone to drowsiness and fatigue (Zion et al. 2018). Understanding the physiological limits of night shifts and implementing appropriate clinical interventions to help nurses recover from fatigue is critical. The current study found that nurses working the night shift are never fully awake or alert, with peak sleepiness occurring at 5–6 AM and 9 AM. This finding highlights the impact of the body's natural circadian rhythm on sleepiness and alertness, as energy levels tend to decline during the early morning hours when the body's internal clock, melatonin, typically signals the need for rest (Bartlett et al. 2013). Night shift workers, who sleep during the day and work at night, disrupt their biological clock. This study found that nurses experienced peak drowsiness after their shifts, coinciding with commuting time, raising concerns about traffic safety. Although no traffic accident data were collected, future studies should focus on peak drowsiness periods and the associated safety risks in personal life. Another period of heightened drowsiness occurred between 5 and 6 AM, when nurses must remain highly focused, and fatigue‐related errors may occur (Gander et al. 2019). Future research on medical errors should incorporate nurses' fatigue levels as a variable.

Night shift work can disrupt circadian rhythms, shorten sleep duration and exacerbate fatigue. Fatigue was closely linked to sleep in this study, and sleep disturbance accounted for the largest share of explained variance in the regression analysis. Overall, 67.6% of participants met the GSDS criterion for sleep disturbance, and the proportion was substantially higher in the fatigue group than in the non‐fatigue group. These findings suggest that fatigue management should begin with a careful assessment of nurses' actual sleep conditions. Future studies could strengthen this assessment by including objective measures such as wrist actigraphy.

Fatigue is linked to perceived stress, and as fatigue levels increase, so too do stress levels, leading to emotional distress and potential depressive symptoms (Feng et al. 2021; Imes and Chasens 2019). The combination of stress, depression and sleep issues puts fatigued nurses at risk for both physical and mental health challenges. Finding a breakthrough to assist nurses in navigating these intertwined issues is crucial. First, it is important to improve the sleep quality of female night shift nurses, as sleep plays a key role in health recovery. Next, promoting activities that enhance physical and mental well‐being, along with stress management, can reduce nurses' stress and improve their self‐efficacy. Lastly, regular assessments of nurses' emotional and depressive states should be prioritized in the workplace.

The pattern observed around menstruation also deserves attention, although it should not be overstated. Fatigue was more common among nurses assessed during the 3 days before menstruation or the first 4 days of menstruation, but the difference was not statistically significant after Holm adjustment. Hormonal changes may contribute to fatigue and sleep disruption during this period (Baker and Lee 2022; Schoep et al. 2019), yet repeated assessments across menstrual phases are needed to clarify this relationship. In practice, flexible and supportive scheduling may still help nurses manage symptoms, provided that such arrangements are guided by individual needs rather than assumptions about all female nurses.

All participants in this study were rotating shift nurses, but data collection was restricted to their designated night shift period to ensure consistent exposure to night work conditions. This methodological approach strengthens the internal validity of findings related to night shift–induced fatigue. However, the generalizability of the results may be constrained, as the participants were not exclusively fixed night shift workers. Future research should explore differences between fixed and rotating shift patterns to better understand how these patterns affect fatigue, menstrual symptoms and disruption to circadian rhythm.

This study shows that clinically significant fatigue is common among female night shift nurses and is independently associated with sleep disturbance, depressive symptoms and perceived stress. In contrast, demographic and lifestyle factors such as age, BMI, chronotype and general health behaviours were not significantly associated with fatigue after adjustment for multiple comparisons. Fatigue may reduce alertness and interfere with work performance, particularly during the early morning hours. Fatigue management should therefore combine sleep support, psychological assessment, stress management and scheduling practices that allow nurses adequate opportunities for recovery.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the use of self‐administered questionnaires may have introduced recall and reporting bias; adding physiological or objective measures could provide a more complete assessment of fatigue. Second, the cross‐sectional design does not show how fatigue changes over time and does not permit causal inference. Repeated assessments across shift cycles and menstrual phases would offer a clearer picture of temporal changes. Third, the final analytic sample was slightly smaller than the originally estimated requirement (225 vs. 230 participants). The five‐participant shortfall represented 2.2% of the target. A post hoc analysis based on the final three‐predictor model indicated an achieved power of 0.999 under the stated assumptions. Although this suggests that the study retained adequate power to detect the prespecified medium effect, very small independent associations may still have gone undetected.

Additionally, the homogeneity of the sample may have contributed to the lack of significant associations between chronotype, fatigue and other health‐related variables. Future studies should explore the role of chronotype using a more diverse sample for a better understanding of its impact. This study did not account, moreover, for circadian rhythm disruptions due to night shift work or individual differences in chronotype. Although there were no significant differences in MEQ scores across groups, future research could benefit from stratified sampling to control chronotypes as a potential factor, which may help clarify its role in fatigue.

Conclusion

The results of this cross‐sectional study may not be relevant to non‐rotating night shift workers, as all the participants were rotating shift workers. Nurses encounter significant sleep problems and fatigue challenges, posing potential risks to their health and patient safety. To address fatigue, it is essential to take a comprehensive approach that considers physiological, psychological, and environmental factors and to provide tailored support and management.

The findings suggest that efforts to address nurses' fatigue should give particular attention to sleep disturbance, followed by depressive symptoms and perceived stress, because each made an independent contribution in the final regression model. Education for students and practising nurses should include sleep physiology, stress management and emotional coping. Workplaces should also consider scheduling practices that provide adequate recovery time and allow nurses to discuss individual physiological needs, including menstrual symptoms, without stigma or disadvantage.
