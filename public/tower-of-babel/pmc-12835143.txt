Impact of land use land cover on microplastic accumulation in high-altitude freshwater lakes of the central Himalayas
Authors: Kannaiyan Neelavannan, Indra Sekhar Sen, Adil Bakir, J Nandhini, Alexandra R McGoran, Holly A Nel, Dhanalakshmi Ramalingam, Danja P Hoehn, Abdurahiman Pulikkoden
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the spatial distribution and occurrence of MPs in the surface water (n = 24) of three lakes (Nainital, Garudtal and Bhimtal) in the Kumaun region of the Central Himalaya, alongside the influence of land use and land cover (LULC) at the catchment scale. The selected lakes represent both pristine and urbanised environments. MPs were detected in all analysed samples, including those from the isolated and presumed pristine Garudtal Lake. The mean abundance of MPs (≥ 90 µm) was higher for the Nainital Lake with 813 ± 346 items m−3 (mean ± SD, n = 8), followed by Bhimtal Lake with 413 ± 274 items m−3(mean ± SD, n = 8) and Garudtal Lake with 175 ± 108 items m−3 (mean ± SD, n = 8). Fibre shaped particles are the dominant (84%) type, followed by fragments (12%). Particle characterisation using micro-Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (µ-FTIR) indicated a prevalence of polyester-based materials (31%), followed by polypropylene (15%), rayon (8%) and polyethylene (6%). Most MPs were in the size range of 1000–5000 µm (53%). The rest of the particles were mainly in the size range of 300—999 µm (38%) and 100–299 µm (10%). Particles were mainly blue and pink in colour with 31% and 23% respectively. The inter relationship between the lakes with respect to the MPs characters were assessed through the multivariate statistical techniques were utilized. The quality of the water was assessed using the Pollution Load Index (PLI) and the Polymer Hazard Index (PHI). Higher PHI values (> 1000) were found to be due to the polyester present. PLI findings show that there is less MPs contamination in the water of Kumaun lakes. The abundance of MPs identified at each location was positively correlated with watershed pollution and development levels suggesting direct local inputs from anthropogenic activities such as waste management, transportation, and fishing activities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33472-y.Keywords: Central Himalaya, Kumaun Lakes, Freshwater ecosystem, Microplastics, Tourism

Introduction

Freshwater ecosystems, encompassing wetlands, rivers, aquifers, and lakes, play a vital role in maintaining ecological balance and supporting environmental, societal, and economic well-being by providing essential services such as drinking water, irrigation, energy production, and habitat for aquatic life1,2. Achieving access to safe water, sanitation, and hygiene remains central to the United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 6 (SDG-6). However, rapid population growth and intensifying anthropogenic activities have led to the degradation and contamination of freshwater resources, particularly lakes and springs that serve as primary community water sources3,28. The Himalayan region, characterized by extensive freshwater reserves, supplies millions of people daily4,34. Yet, many communities face increasing difficulty in managing the dual pressures of population influx and expanding tourism, which, while economically beneficial, contribute to increased waste generation and strain on natural resources. Freshwater ecosystems in the Himalayas, including lakes, rivers, and wetlands, are critical for sustaining biodiversity, regulating hydrological processes, and supporting livelihoods and cultural heritage4,48. Their ecological significance is particularly evident in high-altitude environments, where fragile aquatic bodies act as headwaters for major river systems on which dense downstream populations depend5. However, growing anthropogenic pressures and the emergence of novel contaminants threaten the integrity of these sensitive waterscapes6. Among these pollutants, MPs have emerged as persistent and globally distributed contaminants in freshwater environments7,25. They exhibit strong potential to bioaccumulate, transfer through food webs, and act as carriers of toxic additives and adsorbed chemicals8,9. High-altitude Himalayan lakes, in particular, function as long-term sinks for MPs , receiving inputs through surface runoff, atmospheric deposition, and local human activities10,11,49.

MPs(small plastic particles less than 5 mm) have been recorded in freshwater systems globally12–15. These particles enter the environment through a range of sources16–18 with more sources often associated with areas of higher population density and urban coverage19. MPs have varying impacts at organismal through to ecosystem level and unknown impacts on human health although this area of research is increasing20. MPs exposure can cause chemical and physical impacts to organisms, including reduced growth, reduced fecundity, reduced energy reserves, increased immune response21–23,24.

High-altitude freshwater lakes are particularly vulnerable to MPs pollution. Several freshwater lakes in the Himalayas are found at great altitudes, surrounded by inhospitable and fragile ecosystems. These lakes react quickly to any changes in the watershed, whether they are caused by natural or anthropogenic processes. They can act as sinks for a variety of contaminants from diverse sources79. Lakes are typically closed water systems with extended retention times, making them potential long-term sinks for MPs. Studies have reported MPs in lake bottom sediments35,37–41,42, lakeshore sediments36,37, and in lake water41–43,45. Moreover, the occurrence of MPs in a lake mostly depends on topographical factors including altitude, precipitation, depth, human density, and wind direction16. However, field-based studies on MPs in high-altitude lakes are scarce compared with riverine studies.

Recent reviews have highlighted links between plastic and human health conditions such as diabetes and obesity, lung inflammation, genotoxicity and endocrine disruption16,17. Freshwater lakes are particularly vulnerable to MPs pollution and are often considered long-term sinks for MPs due to their closed / semi-closed nature. Yet lakes have been less studied than running water bodies such as streams and rivers19,26. Globally populations are expected to continue to increase with India no exception28,27. Our reliance on water suggests we will continue to develop near freshwater systems; therefore, we are likely to see more MPs pollution in the future29. Recent study has revealed that variations in LULC patterns, hydrodynamic variations, and heavy metal interactions play critical roles in MPs abundance and transport across Himalayan aquatic ecosystems39,41.

Previous studies on MPs contamination in Himalayan lakes41–43 and riverine44–46 environments have mainly documenting their spatial occurrence, polymer characteristics and potential sources. However, limited studies have addressed how landscape characteristics influence the spatial distribution of MPs. Recent studies43,47 documented that MPs closed linked with role of Land Use and Land Cover (LULC) patterns influence MPs distribution. Extending on those work and suggestions, the present work further incorporates LULC information with MPs abundance, polymer data. By relating MPs distribution patterns to LULC categories, including urban, aquatic zone, forest cover and agriculture land, this study provides how land use dynamics and human activities control the MPs contamination across the Himalayas ecosystems.

Although several have investigated the occurrence and characteristics of MPs in Himalayan freshwater environment40,45, the environmental variables influencing their spatial distribution remain unclear. Earlier studies have largely descriptive assessment of abundance and polymer composition with limited attention to the incorporate landscape-scale such as LULC. The aim of the present study was to investigate the impact of population density and urban land coverage on MPs concentrations in the surface water of three remote Himalayan lakes: Nainital Lake, Bhimtal Lake, and Garudtal Lake. It was hypothesised that MPs abundance and characteristics (colour, size, shape, and polymer type) would vary among the three lakes, depending on population density and LULC cover at the catchment scale. Accordingly, MPs concentrations were expected to follow the trend: Nainital > Bhimtal > Garudtal. The findings from this study will contribute to the growing body of data on lake MPs contamination across India. Based on the findings, this study also recommends precautionary measures, legislative policies, and management strategies to mitigate MPs pollution in high-altitude freshwater lakes.

Material and methods

Study sites and sampling sites

Nainital lake in the Nainital district (29°24’N, 79°28’E; altitude:1937 m above mean sea level (m.s.l); volume: 7.45 million m3; average water depth:16.2 m; crescent-shaped), Garudtal lake (29°36’N, 79.53E; altitude: 1280 m above m.s.l, volume: 0.88 million m3; average water depth: 8 m; V-shaped), and Bhimtal lake (29°20’N, 79°36’E; altitude: 1340 m above m.s.l, volume: 12.3 million m3; average water depth:11.5 m; kidney-shaped) were the focus of this study30. All three lakes are situated in the Kumaun region of the Indian state of Uttarakhand (Figure. 1). Garudtal is situated within the Sattal group, which consists of seven lakes, and it is a remote area where samples can be collected from the northern bank of the lake. The hydrological balance of the study lakes is maintained primarily through glacial meltwater, spring inflow and seasonal precipitation, with additional contribution from surface runoff and minor tributaries. Outflow generally occurs through natural spillways or discharge channels, varying according to snowmelt intensity and monsoon rainfall12,11,45.

Location of the three lakes with sites (yellow stars) highlighted where the water samples were collected for the MPs analysis. This map was prepared using QGIS version 3.10.

In India, the Kumaun Himalayan lakes are among the most recognized tourist spots in the north of the country and as a result have undergone rapid urbanization31. The lake watershed population of Nainital is, 26,859, Bhimtal is 8413 and Garutal has no permanent population although there are a number of resorts (Local Bodies Voter List 2018 Nanital).The Nainital district in Uttarakhand state have received the greatest numbers of visitors, both local and international, over the last ten years, with 700–800 thousand visitors annually30,32.The lakes have fluctuating water levels filling up during July to September (monsoon season), with water level dropping thereafter. Additional recharge is received from the winter rains and snowfall (December to February). The water needs of both towns Nainital and Bhimtal have been continuously rising because of their growing population, which has led to a decline in lake water levels with lowest level recorded in summer (March to May). An extraordinary drop in the lake level was seen throughout the months of May and June (summer) in 2017, which alarmed locals and government officials32,30. There are 21 drains that join the lake among the point and non-point sources of contamination30.

The Nainital town water needs have grown steadily due to increasing population since 2006, and they are being fulfilled by pumping water out of the lake. Pumping began with a monthly average of 11.75 million liters/day (MLD) in 2006, rising to 16.5 MLD in 2015, then down to 14.7 MLD in 2016 and 13.85 MLD in 201730. The Nainital town and catchment primarily use three sources of water; a) Artesian wells or underground flow; b) Subsurface accumulation in the Sukhatal sub-catchment; and c) Lake water sourced from wells near the lake. Three tube wells can be found on the Mallital bank of Nainital, close to the lake. In addition to other wells, there are 8 tube wells31. The Bhimtal Lake supplied 0.1 million liters per day for drinking purposes in the months of May and June32. The morphometric features, along with the catchment characteristics of the studied lakes, are provided in Supplementary Annexure Table S1.

Sampling and sample pre-treatment

In this study, a total of 24 water samples (8 from each lake) were collected from Nainital, Garudtal, and Bhimtal following the protocol detailed by Neelavannan et al.35. Using a 5 L stainless-steel bucket, 50 L of near-surface water (top 50 cm) was collected from each location along the lake banks during May 2022, which marks the end of the summer season when lake water levels are at their lowest. Water samples were filtered in situ using a 90 µm size stainless steel sieve and particles were transferred from the sieve to pre-cleaned glass bottles using ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ cm−1, TOC ≤ 5 ppb). As a result, only particles above 90 µm are reported in this study. Samples were transported to the lab and stored there at 20 °C until further analysis. After filtering the samples with 0.45 µm nitrate cellulose Whatman filters, the filters were kept in glass Petri dishes for particle quantification and characterisation. No chemical digestion was performed on the water samples to avoid any potential alteration of MPs particles. As the collected samples contained low organic material, pre-filtration and microscopic examination were sufficient to remove visible debris. Sampling was conducted from the shoreline owing to the lack of open-water access and logistical constraints associated with remote high-altitude environments; however, to minimise shoreline accumulation bias, sampling points were evenly distributed along the lake perimeter, encompassing both anthropogenically influenced areas and relatively undisturbed zones.

Identification of MPs

Filters were examined under a binocular microscope at 40 × magnification (MZ10F, Leica) with USB camera attachment (GXCAM-U3PRO-20). Particles of interest were imaged with GX Capture-T (version 4.10.16968.20200415), with 10% transferred to anodiscs (ø 25 mm, 0.2 µm; VWR, UK) for Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis. Filters were dried at 40 °C for a minimum of 24 h. Micro-Attenuated total reflectance (ATR) and transmission FTIR with a liquid nitrogen-cooled MCT detector were employed by a LUMOS II (Bruker, UK). To ensure unbiased representation, approximately 10% of the recovered MPs particles were selected for FTIR through a random-number method following homogenization, thereby avoiding preferential selection based on particle size, colour or shape.

Reflectance mode spectra were gathered in 32 scans with a resolution of 4 cm−1 in the range 4000–500 cm−1. Transmission mode was employed for particles that could not be studied using micro-ATR FTIR. Based on the percentage match against the available polymer libraries, polymer identification was confirmed in each case (ATR-FTIR-Library, IR-Spectra of polymers, diamond-ATR, bruker optics ATR-polymer library) (Supplementary Figures S1 to S7). Only matches with a 60% or higher were used27,35. This value is widely used in MPs analysis and provides a balanced confidence level, especially in non-digested samples where minor natural organic matter interference may slightly lower match quality without invalidating polymer characterization.

Contamination control

Throughout the study, strict contamination control procedures were used. All glassware and steelware were rinsed with MilliQ water (18.2 MΩ cm−1, TOC ≤ 5 ppb) and covered in rinsed foil until ready for use. All sample processing in the laboratory was carried out in a clean laboratory equipped with a laminar flow to reduce airborne contamination during sample handling. Surfaces were thoroughly wiped before use and cotton lab coats and nitrile gloves were used during sample processing. Procedural blanks were also used during sample processing and consisted of empty filters under the laminar flow to evaluate procedural contamination. A total of three batches were conducted with a blank run for each batch (N = 3). In the first blank, there were no MPs present. In the second and third blank, 3 and 1 MPs were present respectively. A total of 4 MPs were found, with all determined as cotton. Blank corrected values were calculated by removing a single particle from each sample.

The CF quantifies the level of MPs contamination associated with each polymer type. PLI provides integrated measure of the overall MPs pollution load across sampling site39. PHI assesses the potential ecological MPs based on each polymer abundance and its hazard score40,50.

Catchment delineation

A Digital Elevation Model (DEM) with a 30-m resolution, derived from Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM) data, was used for watershed delineation. The data was downloaded from the United States Geological Survey’s EarthExplorer website. The obtained DEM was pre-processed using the Fill Sink tool to derive a smoothed raster. This smoothed DEM was then used to generate flow direction and flow accumulation layers. These layers were further processed using the Raster Calculator and Basin tools in the ArcGIS v10.8 application to delineate the catchment boundary and the drainage lines feeding the study lakes52. The flowchart in Figure S8 illustrates the process of catchment and drainage line delineation.

Land use and land cover analysis

Sentinel-2 satellite image of 10 m resolution data from April 15, 2022, was utilized to delineate the Land Use and Land Cover (LULC) around the three lakes. Using supervised classification techniques, distinct land cover types such as forest, built-up, bareland, cropland, grassland and water bodies were mapped, providing crucial insights into the spatial distribution of land use pattern surrounding to the lakes. Image analysis tools ArcGIS application was used in this process with maximum likelihood classifier of supervised classification approach. Sentinel-2 bands covering blue (B), green (G), red (R) and near infrared (NIR) range were used in the visual interpretation and land use land cover classification. Nature colour or true colour composite was first produced with R, G & B band, followed by standard false colour composite image was produced with the G, R & NIR bands53. The catchment boundaries derived from processing the DEM, along with the Land Use/Land Cover (LULC) data analysed using Sentinel-2 imagery, were integrated to compute LULC statistics for each lake’s catchment area. The Zonal Statistics tool was employed in this process to extract LULC information specific to each catchment. Statistical analysis

The multivariate statistical techniques such as Pearson’s correlation matrix, principal component analysis and hierarchical cluster analysis, often being applied in the environmental studies to understand the inter relationship and mutual association of the biogeochemical pollutants. Here the MPs’ characteristics were included in the statistical treatment to study the association strength among the parameters. All the statistical derivatives along with the plots were derived using the Python libraries such as Pandas, Seaborn and Matplolib.

Morphology and colour of MPs

Fibres were the most abundant across the three lakes with 84% in Nainital, 89% in Bhimtal and 85% in Garudtal (Figure. 4a). Fragments made up 13% in Nainital, 10% in Bhimtal and 10% in Garudtal with film, pellets and foam between 0.3 and 3.9% (Figure. 4a).

Pie charts showing MPs (a) morphology, (b) colour, (c) size and (d) polymer type as a percentage of abundance.

Across all three lakes MPs colour fell into six groups; namely blue (31%), pink (23%), white (17%), green (16%), black (10%) and other (Figure. 4b). The blue colour MPs dominate across all three lakes and in the overall samples. Another important source of MPs is Green and others. In all lakes, black and pink MPs are the least prevalent. Variation in colour distribution indicates different sources of MPs pollution in all three lakes.

MPs size and polymer distribution

MPs with a size of 1000–5000 µm (53%) were predominantly found in all samples with the exception of Nainital samples which were contaminated by MPs mainly in the size range 300–999 µm (38%).

The size distribution of MPs in the Kumaun lakes (see Figure. 4c) followed the order 300–999 µm > 1000–5000 µm > 100–299 µm in Nainital. Whereas in Bhimtal Lake it followed as 1000–5000 µm > 300–999 µm > 100–299 µm, and in Garudtal Lake, it is 1000–5000 µm > 300—999 µm > 100–299 µm. Micro-FTIR indicated the prevalence of polyester-based materials (PES; 31%), followed by polypropylene (PP; 15%). Other polymers included rayon, polyamide (PA), polyethylene (PE), PES, polystyrene (PS) and acrylic (Figure. 4d).

Statistical Outcomes

The Pearson’s correlation matrix illustrated in the bubble plot (Figure. 6) reveals a range of relationships among various MPs characteristics, including colour, shape, polymer type, and size class. Strong positive correlations were observed between several colour variables, such as Red and Blue, White and Pink, Blue and Yellow, with r > 0.90, suggesting that these colour categories often co-occur. Similarly, strong associations were noted among certain polymer types and shapes, for instance, Film and Pellets (r = 0.99), and Foam and Rayon (r = 0.98), indicating same sources and degradation pathways.

Pearson’s correlation matrix showing the relationships among the analytical variables.

Size classifications also demonstrated high positive correlations with other variables (e.g., Size4 (1000–5000 µm) and Acrylic, Size4 and Fibre, r = 1.00), implying consistent patterns in particle size distribution. Conversely, several variables showed weak or negative correlations—for example, Acrylic and Fragment, Size4 and PP (r = − 1.00), Size4 and Fragment, Size3 and Black (r = − 0.99) highlighting possible inverse relationships.

The Principal Component Analysis (PCA) heatmap (Figure. 7) highlights the variable loadings on the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2), which have eigenvalues greater than 1 and together represent the most significant patterns in the dataset. PC1 shows weak positive loadings for variables such as Pink (0.28), others (0.28), Foam (0.27), and PA, Brown, Rayon, and White (all 0.26), suggesting that these variables contribute substantially to the variance captured by this component. This may indicate a same source among these MPs categories. Conversely, variables like Yellow (− 0.25), PP (− 0.25), and PE (− 0.21) exhibit negative loadings, implying an inverse relationship with PC1. For PC2, weak positive loadings are observed for Size3 (0.29), Red and Pellets (0.27), and Fragment and Film (0.25), suggesting their collective influence on this component. Negative loadings, such as for Black (− 0.28), Fibre (− 0.27), and Size4 (− 0.26), highlight distinct patterns (Figure. 8).

Discussions

In recent years, there has been a rise in scientific interest in the ecological challenges that MPs pose, and researchers have focussed on their fate, production and transport in the freshwater environment. Even though many studies have been shown no correlation between MPs abundance, organic matter concentration and particle size, this topic still must be assessed. In comparison with marine environments, MPs concentration is noticeably higher in the many of these freshwater habitats53.

In a recently developing urban settlement, the lakes Nainital and Bhimtal see increased input of toxic metals, organic and inorganic pollutants54–56. In contrast, comparatively smaller lakes in the area, such as the Garudtal Lake, have not yet been studied. The impact of the anthropogenic pressure on smaller lakes in mountainous terrain is still unknown. Garudtal lake exhibits comparatively lower anthropogenic disturbance than the other study lakes, although localised inputs from adjacent road access and tourism activity still contribute measurable MPs presence. These nearby lakes are also of high scientific interest due to their unique location and high biodiversity. As an example, Nainital Lake has been intensively investigated due to a decline in good ecological status, including water quality, mainly due to population growth56. Lake Nainital is one of the most frequently investigated lakes due to its urbanization which contributes to its increasing eutrophication.

MPs loading and source

Among the studied Lakes, Nainital had the highest abundance of MPs. A higher abundance of MPs was found at site NN6 with 1300 itemsm−3 with the lowest abundance of MPs reported at site NN8 with 200 itemsm−3. The second-highest abundance of MPs was found at site NN1. That specific location is mainly used for water abstraction to produce drinking water. Visual observations at site NN1 revealed that the water abstraction process, involving continuous pumping, induced a surface recirculation zone near the intake point. This recirculation trapped floating plastic litter, including MPs, creating a localized hotspot of plastic accumulation. The turbulence generated by pumping increased the residence time of particles, preventing their natural dispersion and promoting localized accumulation. This phenomenon has significant implications for water-quality management in lakes used for drinking water extraction88,91. Recognizing and mitigating such recirculation-driven hotspots can play a crucial role in reducing the transfer of MPs into water supply systems.

Nainital Lake has the largest catchment area among the lakes, covering 12.1 km2. The LULC composition of the catchment is predominantly forest (approximately 72%), followed by built-up areas (15.78%), water bodies (5.87%), grassland (3.62%), bare land (2.61%), and cropland (0.12%) (Figure. 2). Generally, a higher abundance of MPs was found near to larger populated areas suggesting direct local inputs into those lake systems. For example, boatyard resorts, residential areas, and major roads are present around the lake, however, in the southwestern part of the lake occur hillslope so there is no residential activity. Hence, the MPs abundance was lowest at sites NN8 and NN7. The Nainital Lake serves as a reservoir to collect the water and wastewater from the catchments (mountains forest) and the surrounding areas. Litter can enter the lake from in proper waste disposal from the town (tourism and households) which generates 22 to 28 metric tonnes of solid waste per day57,59. With the recent development of the city sewer system, approximately 12.0 million liters of wastewater are collected and transported to Russi village for irrigation. Still there was some sewage and silt that are mixing into the surface drainage. When it rains, the most of these drains receive a lot of surface runoff because they are seasonal. However, during the non-monsoon months, there is no discharge55,56.

Lake Bhimtal observed the second-highest abundance of MPs among the studied lakes. Higher abundances of MPs were found in the lake outlet areas at BM1 with 960 itemsm−3 and BM4 with 520 itemsm−3 (Table S4). In general, higher abundances of MPs were reported in lake outlet sites globally35,58. The Bhimtal Lake catchment area estimated as 7.50 km2 and is predominantly forested (around 71%), followed by built-up areas (11.6%), grassland (10.47%), water bodies (3.63%), cropland (3.33%), and bare land (0.43%). Bhimtal Lake is a moderately scattered population and is surrounded by forest mountains compared with Nainital Lake. Likely local identified sources of MPs would include tourism, recreational fishing and inputs from other local activities.

A lower abundance of MPs was documented for the Garudtal Lake when compared with lakes Nainital and Bhimtal. The highest abundance of MPs (320 items m−3) was reported in the western part of the lake at site GR4, located near a road. The lower concentration MPs were observed at site GR3 with 40 itemsm−3 with the proximity to a dense forest mountain with limited anthropogenic activities with the exception of a road lake access. Among the studied lakes, Garudtal Lake has the smallest catchment area, measuring less than 1 km2 (0.94 km2), and comprises only two land use/land cover (LULC) types: forest area (97.27%) and water bodies (2.73%). Due to the inaccessibility, samples have been obtained from the opposite lake side. Garudtal Lake is situated in a forest area there is no settlement around the lake, and likely MPs sources would include inputs from the nearby road, atmospheric transport, and limited tourist activities.

The average concentration of MPs among the studied lakes, the higher concentrations were observed in Nainital Lake (813 ± 346 items m−3) followed by Bhimtal Lake (413 ± 274 items m−3) and Garudtal Lake (175 ± 108 items m−3). The MPs result reveals that the MPs concentration of these lakes varies from each other mainly due to the population density (residential area) and anthropogenic activities (number of tourists visiting the lake, boating, and roads). The catchment areas of the three lakes exhibit varying proportions of LULC classes, which also resulted in differing levels of MPs accumulation.

As reported in several studies, there is a strong connection between population growth and the abundance of MPs. The hypothesis of the impact of population density or tourism activities on MPs input in high-altitude lake environments was supported by the higher abundance of MPs observed in comparison to population (anthropogenic activities; Table.2). The high MPs abundance in lakes could also be attributable to increased anthropogenic activity due to tourism and increased urban roadways. Further, other factors, such as land use (for example., residential, agricultural, industrial, and recreational uses), boating, pumping water from the lake, and the size of the water body may also have an impact on the abundance of MPs present in the surface water at the sampling site14. In addition, meteorological settings include the strength and direction of the wind.

The high levels of MPs (813 MPs/m3) concentration that have been observed appear to be associate with population (26,859) not with CA: LA ratio (14.11) and watershed development (built-up and agricultural area; 5.22 km2 or 20% of watershed) of Nainital Lake (Table 2 and Figure. 2). Morphology of MPs

Extracted particles were categorized as fragments, fibres, films, pellets and foams Fig.4a. Overall, fibres were the most abundant form (84%). This corresponded to 81%, 89% and 85% fibres for lakes Nainital, Bhimtal and Garudtal, respectively. The fragment is the second-most common MPs shape, making up around 11% of the water samples of Kumaun lakes and other shapes are less abundant: film 2.57%, foam 1.54%, and pellet 0.34% Fig. 4a. A prevalence of fibres in lake systems is common at various locations in India. Neelavannan et al.37 also reported a high prevalence of fibres in the Anchar lake bottom sediments from north-western Himalayan with 96%. Fibres also accounted for 91% of MPs from surface water samples from the Ganges River63 and 76% for the Hooghly River35. Similarly, in the southern Indian lakes namely Manipal Lake, fibres accounted for 96% and 95% of MPs in water post-monsoon and during monsoon, respectively64. Some locations do not show this trend with less fibres in Red hills lake (37.9%)65 and Kodaikanal lake (36.4%)66. The abundance of fibres in the three studies lakes of the present study is higher than that of Red hills lake and Kodaikanal lake but is similar to Manipal Lake.

A major source of microfibres in the environment is the washing of synthetic textiles. Per standard wash load, up to 700,000 fibres can be discharged. In the present study, fibres discharged from washing machines may have entered the lakes via the stormwater and wastewater inlets that are connected to Lake Nainital and Lake Bhimtal. A significant fraction of the fibres in Kumaun Lakes may have come from other sources, such as the use of ropes and boating gear by tourism activity, or from office dust, households, and abrasion materials from construction sites. By contrast, the Garudtal Lake is located in a non-residential region, and fibres might be originated from tire and road wears particles from nearby roads transported alongside other atmospheric MPs or leaching from the road following rainfalls. Asian countries are the top producers of synthetic materials, making them the primary source of microfibre contamination worldwide63,61,77,81. The proportion of fibre-shaped MPs found in the Asian freshwater environment was considerably higher than those seen everywhere in the world. For example, European freshwater documented substantially lower MPs concentrations68. Dusaucy et al.69 report that fibres and fragments are the most common morphologies of MPs in the surface waters of lakes around the world.

The prevalence of fibres is significant as studies have demonstrated an increased impact on biota when they are ingested compared to other plastic shapes. An early review by Wright et al.70 noted fibres were the most harmful form of plastic and exposure studies have shown developmental deformities occur in amphipods when fibres are ingested67. In decapod crustaceans, fibres can tangle and block the gastrointestinal tract resulting in reduced growth and fecundity68,69. Although fibre exposure studies do not always demonstrate negative impacts from ingestion, with stickleback fish remaining unaffected by fibre exposures22,70. More research is needed to better understand the impact of these abundant plastic items especially in combination with other pollutants.

MPs were categorized into six groups based on their colour, including white, black, pink, green, blue and others (Figure. 4b). Particles were mainly blue in colour (31%), followed by pink (23%), white (17%), green (16%) and black (10%). Nelms et al.75 suggest that the colour of MPs may have an impact on how organisms digest them. Coloured MPs might be more attractive to certain fish species, increasing their exposure to the hazardous compounds found in plastic particles72,76. Aquatic species in the lakes may accidentally consume the MPs for food, causing a risk to their health73. The Fisheries Department Government of India has concentrated on expanding current cold-water fish production from 52,084 MT currently to 90 thousand MT by FY 2024–25. Improving the current productivity from 1 ton/Ha to 3 ton/Ha (Government of India, Department of fisheries, 2023). The white colour MPs that are found in the studied lakes seem to be a serious concern. Since Nainital and Bhimtal Lakes water are the primary source of drinking water, about 40% of the total requirement is taken from the lake33. We observed that a larger portion of the coloured fibre found in lakes had a potential connection to synthetic textiles, one of the primary sources of microfibres in aquatic systems78. Also, other possible sources might be tourist boating and fishing activities.

In contrast, our study found a higher MPs abundance of a larger size range 1000–5000 µm compared to earlier studies 1000–300 µm by Neelavannan et al.35,37, Warrier et al., indicating that the 1000–5000 µm MPs are degrading at a slow rate. That can be because of the plastic materials shorter residence time when exposed to degrading factors because of higher flow velocity29. Usually, the larger plastic debris that floats readily in the water become enclosed in biofilm and floating vegetation and sinks71,75.

Polymer distribution and source

In the individual lake proposition of the polymer types, PES and PP were the common types except for Garudtal lakes (PES and Rayon) (Figure. 4d ). Due to the great vertical mobility of water, higher-density polymers (such as PET, PA and PS) are present in surface water. The buoyancy of the MPs may have increased because of the air bubbles there79. PP and PE are the most often detected polymers, according to a review of MPs in high mountain lakes by Pastorino et al.80, also from other studies such as Sassolo lake, Switzerland81, Ziway lake, Africa82, Naivasha lake, Kenya83, and Guiba lake, Brazil84. This agrees with the results of our investigation. These items might have been broken down from larger items due to mechanical degradation or oxidative aging. Similarly, PE and PP were observed to be the main polymers in surface waters of Nainital Lake and SEM showed that some particles were weathered potentially due to oxidative degradation85.

Moreover, PP and PE are the two plastics that are most utilised in single use plastic products. While PE is utilised for plastic bottles and bags, plastic packaging typically uses PP. As there is little industrial activity close to our study in the Kumaun region, it is possible that larger plastic debris has fragmented, leading to MPs. The pellets or microbeads are frequently in a variety of places of everyday consumer goods, such as facial cleansers, make-ups, and toothpaste82,86,80, but they were only present in a small amount. The absence of these products can be due to their inaccessibility or lack of use87,83. PET is utilised in the textile and bottle manufacturing industry55,84. Most of the PET and polyester fibres in this study may have come from textile from clothes or blankets85. These fibres are released during the washing process71,86,89 and they might find their way into water bodies through sewage or surface runoff.

Many studies have been done, linked anthropogenic activities to the contamination of lake systems57. The anthrophonic activities are changes in the watershed linked with rapid urban development. Rapid urban development and inadequate waste management for those areas have increased the environmental impacts of urban runoffs, sewage increases, growing tourism, lack of proper infrastructure plans, and improper waste dumping. Furthermore, improper waste disposal permits plastic waste to reach the aquatic ecosystem, which can increase the amount of MPs pollution90. Municipal or domestic wastewater and sewage should be treated by bioremediation technology before draining into the lake.

Risk assessment of MPs in Kumaun lakes and other India lakes

MPs risk assessment in lake water is assessed using parameters from PLI and PHI. The MPs pollution load has been determined at each location based on PLI (Figure. 5a). The mean PLI values of all the water sampled from the Kumaun lakes is 1.48 (Nainital, 1.40; Bhimtal 1.38; Garudtal 1.65) were less than 10, indicating a “Hazard level I” (Table S5). The Kumaun lakes water is less contaminated based on the PLI. The PLI value seems to be independent of the MPs chemical composition, as the PLI is calculated by dividing the MPs abundance by the background value. We compared the PLI indices of MPs data from other Indian lakes (Table S6). The PLI value of Renuka lake is 2.9, Red hills lake is 2.34, Manipal lake during monsoon and post-monsoon were found to be 1.62 and 1.39, respectively. A similar trend observed in the lakes of the present study indicates that the high-altitude lakes are still within the safe category with respect to the PLI. However, this may not remain the case in the coming years, as human activities are rapidly increasing in these areas.

Overall risk of MPs pollution in the lake water was categorized as V (Table S5) as per the PHI levels. The PHI values of MPs in lake water indicate a significant trend in MPs pollution (Figure. 5b). For example, except for NN5, GR1, GR3, and GR7, most of the sampling location had extremely high PHI value (> 1000) because of the presence of the polyester MPs, which has a high hazard score. Even though the PP, PA, PE, and PET rating are not a high as those of polyester, they still need to be taken into consideration while performing the risk assessment. Furthermore, it is impossible to overlook the chemical toxicity of MPs, even at low concentration.

Comparison with other studies

MPs data from this study were compared to other inland lakes water from the Indian subcontinent (Table S7). Comparison between reported datasets is however hard due to the lack of sampling, analysis and reporting protocols harmonised for MPs35,27. Jain et al.12 examined Lake Nainital, one of the study sites, they found that MPs concentration in the surface water were greater (2.4 to 88 items L−1) than present study. Perhaps it is a result of the filtering deference and targeting a smaller size rage. In the current investigation, 90 µm (90 µm–5 mm) sieves were utilized for filtration, while Jain et al.12 employed 20 µm (20 µm–5 mm) sieves. Furthermore, it appears that the Nainital Lake is accumulating more MPs, as evidenced by the data gathered for the current study sample in March 2022 and for Jain et al.12 February 2023.The abundance of MPs concentration in the water from central Himalaya was meaningfully lower than in other studies of high-altitude lakes such as Rewalsar Lake, NW Himalaya (13–238 items L−1 with mean of 130 items L−1; 73), Renuka Lake, lesser Himalaya (2—64 items L−1 with mean of 21 ± 13 items L−1; 74) and Kodaikanal Lake, Western Ghats, Tamilnadu (8.39 ± 1.25 to 53.33 ± 14.25 items L−1 with mean of 24.42 ± 3.22 items L−1; 46). Also, in Red Hills Lake from Southern India (Mean of 5.9 items L−1; 75). The MPs concentration in Manipal Lakes was roughly similar ranging from 0.04 to 1.08 items L−1. Further, the MPs concentration in the central Himalayan lakes (Nainital, 0.06 to 0.96 items L−1 with a mean of 0.41 ± 0.27 items L−1; Bhimtal, 0.06 to 0.96 items L−1 with a mean of 0.41 ± 0.27 items L−1; Garital, 0.04 to 0.32 items L−1 with a mean of 0.18 ± 0.11 items L−1) was lower than in the other lakes. The concentrations of MPs in the plain and mountainous water bodies did not show any significant differences, according to our research. The concentrations seemed to be depending on the local environment and local human activity. Dusaucy et al.69 reviewed MPs pollution of worldwide lakes, when we compared our data with worldwide studied lakes, the Kumaun Himalayan lakes were moderately polluted.

This study has certain limitations, the analysis was focused on MPs particle ≥ 90 µm, which may potentially underestimate total abundance. In addition, sediment data and seasonal data were not included. Future investigations should incorporate smaller particle size and extended seasonal coverage.

Conclusions

This study addresses the issue of plastic pollution in relation to population size in remote areas such as Himalayan Lakes. Our result indicated the widespread presence of MPs for the three lake systems with MPs detected in all samples. Among the studied lakes, the higher concentration of MPs (813 MPs/m3) were found in Lake Nainital which also showed the highest population size (26,859), followed by Bhimtal Lake has low MPs concentration and correlate with lower watershed population (8413). The lowest MPs concentrations were found at lake Garudtal which is surrounded by forest, and has limited tourist visiting the lake throughout the year. 84% of the MPs consisted of mainly polyester (31%) fibres but PE and PP (15%) were also present. Indicating the main sources from clothing, tier wear and wastewater discharge. Other polymers included Rayon, PE, PA, Acrylic, and other minor polymers. MPs have been found even in the remote pristine Garudtal Lake where there are no permanent populations and limited people are visiting. Risk assessment due to MPs in lake water indicates no severe threat with respect to the PLI values. However, the PHI values exceed 1000 in most samples, indicating a severe risk in all but a few cases, which is due to the presence of polyester particles. MPs concentrations and characteristics are largely influenced by population size, local human activities (landfilling, transportation, fishing) and tourism. This study added baseline data and highlights the need for increased focus on waste management and sustainable water usage in freshwater ecosystems in the lesser Himalayas. In policy terms, this study contributes to the goals of Sustainable Development Goal 6 (Clean water and Sanitation) by demonstrating the importance of reduce plastic contamination in fragile high-altitude freshwater ecosystem. Including MPs monitoring into national environmental programs, such as the National Plan for Plastic Waste Management and National Mission for Clean Ganga (NMCG) could strengthen India’s effort to mitigation strategies in the Himalayan region.
