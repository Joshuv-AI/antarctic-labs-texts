Integrals and Rigidity on Manifolds with Nonnegative Ricci Curvature Thanks: G. Xu was partially supported by NSFC 12141103.
Zixuan Chen, Guoyi Xu, Shuai Zhang

Abstract
. We prove the general sharp mean value inequality for non-negative superharmonic functions and its corresponding rigidity, which removes the radius restriction of Schoen-Yau’s classical result about this inequality. And we obtain an explicit formula of the asymptotic scaling invariant integral of weighted scalar curvature, on three dimensional complete Riemannian manifolds with non-negative Ricci curvature and maximal volume growth. As an application, we use this formula to give another proof of Hamilton’s pinching conjecture in this case. Mathematics Subject Classification: 35K15, 53C20.



1.
Introduction

The interplay between curvature, topology, and analysis on Riemannian manifolds forms a central theme in geometric analysis. A particularly rich line of inquiry investigates how non-negative Ricci curvature, govern the behavior of solutions to elliptic and parabolic partial differential equations, and conversely, how the properties of such solutions reveal deep geometric and topological information about the underlying space.

Two fundamental tools in this interplay are the mean value formula for harmonic functions and mean value inequalities for subharmonic functions, which provide pointwise control by averages and are deeply connected to the underlying geometry.

The classical mean value formula characterizes harmonic functions on Euclidean space. We can not expect the mean value formula to hold for general harmonic functions on Riemannian manifolds. Instead, after introducing suitable weight function
b(x)=G(q,x)^{1/(2-n)}
(where
G(q,x)
is the minimal positive Green function), which serves as an "analytic" substitute for the distance function; we can show the similar weighted mean value formula holds.

On general Riemannian manifolds, a mean value inequality often holds for subharmonic functions, but the optimal constant and the geometric implications of the case of equality are subtle questions. A classical result by Schoen and Yau (
[
21
, Proposition
2.1
, Chapter
1
]
) established a sharp mean value inequality for non-negative superharmonic functions on manifolds with non-negative Ricci curvature. However, their result required the radius of geodesic ball less than the injectivity radius at the center of the geodesic ball.

One result of this paper is the sharp mean value inequality without radius restriction as follows.

Theorem 1.1

(Sharp Mean Value Inequality and Rigidity)
.

Let
(M^{n},g)
be a complete Riemannian manifold with
\operatorname{Ric}\geq 0
. Suppose
f\in C^{\infty}(M^{n})-\{0\}
satisfies
f\geq 0
and
\Delta\leq 0
. Then

f(x)\geq\frac{1}{n\omega_{n}r^{n-1}}\int_{\partial B_{x}(r)}f\,d\sigma,\quad\quad\forall x\in M^{n},r>0.

Furthermore, if equality holds for some
x
and
r>0
, then the geodesic ball
B_{x}(r)
is isometric to the Euclidean ball
B^{n}(r)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Remark 1.2
.

This result generalizes the classical Schoen–Yau inequality by removing the injectivity radius constraint. Our approach considers the pullback of a superharmonic function via the exponential map and establishes a generalized divergence theorem (Lemma
3.4
) for geodesic balls. This lemma, which handles the pullback function on the tangent space, is of independent interest and may have other applications in the future study.

Another new tool is the quantity
F(t)
introduced in Lemma
4.2
, which coincides with the one studied by Schoen and Yau when the radius is less than the injectivity radius. The key observation is that
F(t)
is non-increasing for all
t>0
. Instead of showing the upper derivative of
F
is non-positive; we directly estimate the difference
F(t)-F(t-s)
. The argument use a nested interval argument and the generalized divergence theorem to derive a contradiction, if
F
were to increase. The corresponding rigidity theorem, which asserts that equality in the mean value inequality forces the geodesic ball to be Euclidean, is a geometric consequence that underscores the sharpness of the constant.

Parallel to the above monotonicity argument, another theme in geometric analysis is understanding the asymptotic structure of complete non-compact manifolds with non-negative Ricci curvature using monotonicity. Colding
[
8
]
and Colding-Minicozzi
[
9
,
7
]
developed powerful monotonicity formulas for harmonic functions, which are crucial for analyzing the Green’s function and the asymptotic volume ratio
\mathrm{V}_{M}
. These tools link the coarse geometric invariant like
\mathrm{V}_{M}
to the integral of scalar curvature.

The study of integral of the curvature started from the well-known Gauss-Bonnet Theorem: for any compact
2
-dim Riemannian manifold
(M^{2},g)
,

\displaystyle\int_{M^{2}}Kd\mu=2\pi\chi(M^{2}),

where
K
is the Gaussian curvature of
M^{2}
,
d\mu
is the element of area of
M^{2}
,
\chi(M^{2})
is the Euler characteristic of
M^{2}
. Cohn-Vossen
[
6
]
studied the integral of the curvature on complete
2
-dim Riemannian manifold, obtained the so-called Cohn-Vossen’s inequality: If
(M^{2},g)
is a finitely connected, complete, oriented Riemannian manifold, and assume
\int_{M^{2}}Kd\mu
exists as extended real number, then
\int_{M^{2}}Kd\mu\leq 2\pi\chi(M^{2})
. Also see
[
14
]
and
[
13
]
for related works on
2

Example 1.4

We consider a sequence of 2-dimensional Riemannian manifolds

Using geodesic polar coordinates centered at

Direct computation yields:

Our second set of results concerns the asymptotic integral geometry of three-dimensional manifolds with non-negative Ricci curvature and maximal volume growth (
\mathrm{V}_{M}>0

Theorem 1.5

For a complete non-compact Riemannian manifold

(1.1)

\displaystyle\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\int_{b\leq r}R\cdot|\nabla b|}{r}=8\pi\big[1-\mathrm{V}_{M}\big],,

where

In

The identity (

It is illuminating to explain why (

and the cone metric satisfies

\displaystyle g_{C(X)}=dr^{2}+r^{2}g_{X},\quad R_{C(X)}=\frac{2(K_{X}-1)}{r^{2}},\qquad r>0.

Note we have

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{V}_{M}=\mathrm{V}_{C(X)}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\operatorname{Vol}(B(t))}{\omega_{3}t^{3}}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\int_{0}^{t}dr\int_{X}r^{2}}{\omega_{3}t^{3}}=\frac{\mathrm{Vol}(X)}{4\pi},

where

is the ball in

\displaystyle\int_{B(1)}R\,dV

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}dr\int_{X}\frac{2(K_{X}-1)}{r^{2}}\,r^{2}=2\left([\int_{X}K_{X}]-\mathrm{Vol}(X)\right)

(1.4)

\displaystyle=8\pi\,(1-\mathrm{V}_{M}).

From

\displaystyle\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\int_{B_{q}(r)}R(g)dg}{r}=\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\int_{(B_{q}(1),r^{-2}g)}R(r^{-2}g)d(r^{-2}g).

Although it is not rigorous, from the Gromov-Hausdorff convergence of

Assume

The Bonnet-Myers Theorem is proved by integrating curvature along geodesic segments. In a similar spirit, Hamilton’s pinching conjecture can be seen as a scaling-invariant analogue; the proof of Corollary

Now we explain some innovations of the proof of Theorem

. Deriving the exact limit in (
1.1
), requires knowledge of the topological type of the level sets of

, as this allows us to apply the Gauss–Bonnet theorem. In this paper, we study the set (denoted as

) of parameters

for which the level set

has genus at least one. Firstly, under non-parabolic assumption, we show

\displaystyle\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\int_{[\mathcal{T}\cup\mathcal{B}]\cap(0,r)}dt\int_{b^{-1}(t)}R(b^{-1}(t))}{r}=0.

Furthermore, under maximal volume growth assumption, we prove that the
asymptotic density
of this set vanishes: that is

(1.7)

\displaystyle\frac{L^{1}\bigl([\mathcal{T}\cup\mathcal{B}]\cap(0,t)\bigr)}{t},

tends to
0
as

, even though

itself possibly grow (for instance, like

). In other words, under the condition of maximal volume growth, we show that asymptotically almost every level set is topologically a sphere in the above sense. This is a result with independent interest.

A detailed asymptotic analysis of the integral of the mean curvature of such level sets is essential for establishing the above asymptotic result. For technical reasons, we therefore begin with the weighted volume of the level sets and establish the precise asymptotic results required in the proof. Although portions of these asymptotic results were first obtained by Colding–Minicozzi (
[

]

[

This focus not only completes the derivation in a unified way but is also of independent interest, paralleling similar techniques used in monotonicity formulas for minimal surfaces and harmonic maps.

We expect that (

The application of the sharp integral curvature formula to Hamilton’s conjecture, was established by one of the authors (

Part I. Integral for harmonic functions

2.
Mean value formula

On non-parabolic manifold
M^{n}
with
n\geq 3
, there exists a unique minimal positive Green function, denoted as
G(q,x)
, where
q
is a fixed point on the manifold. To emphasize the base point, we use the notation that

\displaystyle b_{q}(x)=G(q,x)^{\frac{1}{2-n}}.

From
\Delta(b_{q}^{2-n})=0
, we have

(2.1)

\Delta b_{q}=(n-1)\frac{|\nabla b_{q}|^{2}}{b_{q}}.

From the behavior of Green’s function
G(q,x)
near the singular point
q
, we get

(2.2)

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0}\Big\{\sup_{x\in B_{q}(r)}\left|\frac{b_{q}}{\rho}(x)-1\right|+\sup_{x\in B_{q}(r)}\left||\nabla b_{q}(x)|-1\right|=0\Big\},

where
\rho(x)=d(x,q)
.

The following result computes the value of scaling invariant weighted volume around the singularity.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\omega_{n}
be the volume of unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{{r\to 0}}r^{-n}\int_{b_{q}\leq r}|\nabla b_{q}|^{2}=\omega_{n}.

Proof:
From (
2.2
), for any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, there exists
\delta>0
, such that

(2.3)

\displaystyle\left||\nabla b_{q}(x)|-1\right|<\epsilon,\quad|b_{q}-\rho|(x)<\epsilon\rho(x),\quad\forall x\in B_{q}(\delta).

For
\delta>0
, we claim that there exists
r_{0}
, such that if
b_{q}(x)\leq r_{0}
, then
\rho(x)\leq\delta
. Otherwise, there exists sequence
\{q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
on
M
, such that

\displaystyle\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}b_{q}(q_{i})=0,\quad\quad\rho(q_{i})>\delta.

This implies
\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}G(q,q_{i})\to\infty
, on the other hand
q_{i}\notin B_{q}(\delta)
; which contradicts the boundedness of Green function
G(q,\cdot)
on
M^{n}-B_{q}(\delta)
.

For any
r<r_{0}
, from (
2.3
) we get

\displaystyle\{x:\rho(x)\leq\frac{r}{1+\epsilon}\}\subseteq\{x:b_{q}(x)\leq r\}\subseteq\{x:\rho(x)\leq\frac{r}{1-\epsilon}\}.

Therefore we have

\displaystyle r^{-n}\int_{\rho\leq\frac{r}{1+\epsilon}}(1-\epsilon)^{2}\leq r^{-n}\int_{b_{q}\leq r}|\nabla b_{q}|^{2}\leq r^{-n}\int_{\rho\leq\frac{r}{1-\epsilon}}(1+\epsilon)^{2}.

Hence let
r\to 0
,

\displaystyle\frac{\omega_{n}(1-\epsilon)^{2}}{(1+\epsilon)^{n}}\leq\lim_{{r\to 0}}r^{-n}\int_{b_{q}\leq r}|\nabla b_{q}|^{2}\leq\frac{\omega_{n}}{(1-\epsilon)^{n}}(1+\epsilon)^{2}.

Letting
\epsilon\to 0
in the above, the conclusion follows.

∎

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dr}I(u,q_{0},r)=r^{1-n}\int_{b_{q_{0}}\leq r}\Delta u<0.

We must have

I(u,q_{0},r)<\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}I(u,q_{0},r)=n\omega_{n}u(q_{0})\quad\text{for all }r\in(0,r_{0}).

But this contradicts the hypothesis that
u(q_{0})\leq I(u,q_{0},r)
for all
r>0
.

Therefore, our assumption that
\Delta u(q_{0})<0
must be false, and
\Delta u\geq 0
everywhere on
M^{n}
.

∎

3.
Divergence theorem for geodesic balls

For the exponential map
\exp_{q}:T_{q}M^{n}\rightarrow M^{n}
, we define
\mathcal{R}:\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{\infty\}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\theta):=\sup\{r>0:\ d_{g}(q,\exp_{q}((r+\epsilon)\theta))=r+\epsilon\ \text{for some}\ \epsilon>0\}.

Define

\displaystyle A=\{t\cdot\theta:\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1},0\leq t<\mathcal{R}(\theta)\},

\displaystyle\Sigma(t)=\{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}:\mathcal{R}(\theta)>t\},\quad\quad\tilde{\Sigma}(t)=\{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}:\mathcal{R}(\theta)\geq t\}.

For fixed
q\in M
, we define the geodesic ball
B_{q}(t)=\{x\in M^{n}:d_{g}(q,x)<t\}
. We have

\displaystyle\exp_{q}(t\cdot\Sigma(t))\subset\exp_{q}(t\cdot\tilde{\Sigma}(t))=\partial B_{q}(t).

Define

\displaystyle Z=\{t>0:\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\tilde{\Sigma}(t)\backslash\Sigma(t))>0\}.

Let
\partial_{*}B_{q}(t)
be the measure theoretic boundary defined by

\displaystyle\partial_{*}B_{q}(t)=\left\{y\in M:\begin{array}[]{ll}\varlimsup\limits_{r\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{H}^{n}(B_{y}(r)\cap B_{q}(t))}{r^{n}}>0\ \text{and}\ \varlimsup\limits_{r\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{H}^{n}(B_{y}(r)\backslash B_{q}(t))}{r^{n}}>0\end{array}\right\}.

Lemma 3.1
.

For
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\backslash Z
, the geodesic ball
B_{q}(t)
has locally finite perimeter and

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial B_{q}(t)\backslash\exp_{q}(t\cdot\Sigma(t)))=0.

Proof:
Firstly we fix
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\backslash Z
in the rest argument. Note the map
F(v)=\exp_{q}(t\cdot v):\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\rightarrow(M^{n},g)
is a Lipschitz map with Lipschitz constant
C>0
. Now from the area formula, for
t\notin Z
, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial B_{q}(t)\backslash\exp_{q}(t\cdot\Sigma(t)))=\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\Big(\exp_{q}\big(t\cdot\tilde{\Sigma}(t)\big)\backslash\exp_{q}\big(t\cdot\Sigma(t)\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\leq C\cdot\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\tilde{\Sigma}(t)\backslash\Sigma(t))=0.

Now we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial_{*}B_{q}(t))

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial B_{q}(t))

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial B_{q}(t)\backslash\exp_{q}(t\cdot\Sigma(t)))+\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\exp_{q}(t\cdot\Sigma(t)))

(3.3)

\displaystyle\leq C\cdot\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\Sigma(t))\leq C\cdot\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})<\infty.

The conclusion follows from (
3.3
) and
[
12
, Theorem
5.23
]
.

∎

Let
\{\theta,v_{2},\cdots,v_{n}\}
be an orthonormal basis of the tangent space
T_{q}M^{n}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Here,
\theta,v_{i}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, since the unit tangent sphere at
q\in M^{n}
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. And we regard
T_{t\theta}(T_{q}M^{n})\simeq T_{q}M^{n}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, denote

\displaystyle W_{i}(t,\theta)=d\exp_{q}(t\theta)(tv_{i}),\quad\quad\forall 2\leq i\leq n,t>0,

\displaystyle J(t,\theta)=\sqrt{\det\left(\left\langle W_{i}(t,\theta),W_{j}(t,\theta)\right\rangle_{i,j=2}^{n}\right)},\quad\quad\forall t>0,\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}.

By Gauss Lemma, we have

(3.4)

\displaystyle\langle d\exp_{p}(t\theta)(\theta),d\exp_{p}(t\theta)(\theta)\rangle=1,

(3.5)

\displaystyle\langle d\exp_{p}(t\theta)(\theta),d\exp_{p}(t\theta)(v_{i})\rangle=0,\quad i=2,\cdots n.

Consider the map
\exp_{q}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to M^{n}
, then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Jac}(\exp_{q})(t\cdot\theta)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\mathrm{det}[(D\exp_{q})\cdot(D\exp_{q})^{*}]}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\det\left(\left\{\langle d\exp_{q}(t\theta)(v_{i}),d\exp_{q}(t\theta)(v_{j})\rangle\right\}_{i,j=2}^{n}\right)}

(3.6)

\displaystyle=\frac{J(t,\theta)}{t^{n-1}}.

Assume
z=(z_{1},\cdots,z_{n})=t\cdot\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we know
dz_{1}\cdots dz_{n}=t^{n-1}dtd\theta
. From the area formula (see
[
12
, Theorem
3.9
]
), for any
L^{n}
-measurable function
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h(t\cdot\theta)J(t,\theta)dtd\theta=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h(t\cdot\theta)\mathrm{Jac}(\exp_{q})t^{n-1}dtd\theta

(3.7)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h(z)\mathrm{Jac}(\exp_{q})dz_{1}\cdots dz_{n}=\int_{M^{n}}\Big[\sum_{t\cdot\theta\in\exp_{q}^{-1}(x)}h(t\cdot\theta)\Big]d\mu(x).

Similarly for fixed
t>0
, consider the map
\tilde{F}(\theta)=\exp_{q}(t\cdot\theta):\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\rightarrow M^{n}
, from (
3.6
), we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{Jac}(\tilde{F})=\mathrm{Jac}(\exp_{q})\cdot t^{n-1}=J(t,\theta).

For any
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
-measurable function
h:\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}h(\theta)J(t,\theta)d\theta=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}h(\theta)\mathrm{Jac}(\tilde{F})d\theta

(3.8)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}(t)}h(\frac{y}{t})\mathrm{Jac}(\exp_{q})dy=\int_{M^{n}}\Big[\sum_{t\cdot\theta\in\exp_{q}^{-1}(x)}h(\theta)\Big]d\mu(x).

Lemma 3.2
.

We have
\mathcal{H}^{1}(Z)=0
and

(3.9)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{q}(t)}\Delta f=\int_{\exp_{q}(t\cdot\Sigma(t))}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\vec{n}}\mathrm{d}\mu_{\partial B_{q}(t)},\quad\quad\quad t\in(0,\infty)\backslash Z.

Proof:

Step (1)
. Note

\displaystyle\partial A=\{\mathcal{R}(\theta)\theta:\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\},

then from
[
2
, Proposition III.3.1]
, we have

\mathcal{H}^{n}(\partial A)=\int_{{\bf\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\chi_{\partial A}dx=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}d\theta\int_{0}^{\infty}\chi_{\partial A}(t\theta)t^{n-1}dt=0.

From the coarea formula, we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}^{n}(\partial A)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\{\mathcal{R}(\theta)=t\}}t^{n-1}\mathrm{d}\theta\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\tilde{\Sigma}(t)\backslash\Sigma(t)}t^{n-1}\mathrm{d}\theta\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\tilde{\Sigma}(t)\backslash\Sigma(t))t^{n-1}\mathrm{d}t.

So we must have
\mathcal{H}^{1}(Z)=0
.

Step (2)
. From
[
12
, Theorem 5.16]
and Lemma
3.1
, the divergence theorem can be applied to
B_{q}(t)
to get

(3.10)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{q}(t)}\Delta f=\int_{\partial_{*}B_{q}(t)}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\vec{n}}\mathrm{d}\mu_{\partial B_{q}(t)}\quad\forall t\in(0,\infty)\backslash Z.

Since
\exp_{q}
is a diffeomorphism on
A
from
[
2
, Theorem III.2.2]
, we have
\exp_{q}(t\cdot\Sigma(t))\subset\partial_{*}B_{q}(t)
. Combining (
3.2
), we have
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial_{*}B_{q}(t)\backslash\exp_{q}(t\cdot\Sigma(t)))=0
. Therefore we get

(3.11)

\displaystyle\int_{\partial_{*}B_{q}(t)}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\vec{n}}\mathrm{d}\mu_{\partial B_{q}(t)}=\int_{\exp_{q}(t\cdot\Sigma(t))}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\vec{n}}\mathrm{d}\mu_{\partial B_{q}(t)},\quad\forall t\in(0,\infty)\backslash Z.

The conclusion follows from (
3.10
) and (
3.11
).

∎

Definition 3.3
.

For any
f\in C^{\infty}(M^{n})
, define

\displaystyle\tilde{f}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\rightarrow M^{n},

by
\tilde{f}(t,\theta)=f(\exp_{q}(t\theta))
. Note
\tilde{f}
is a smooth function.

Lemma 3.4

(Generalized divergence theorem for geodesic ball)
.

Suppose that
f\in C^{\infty}(M^{n})
, then

(3.12)

\displaystyle\int_{\tilde{\Sigma}(t)}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(t,\theta)}{\partial t}J(t,\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta=\int_{B_{q}(t)}\Delta f,\quad\quad\forall t>0.

Proof:

Step (1)
. From Gauss’ Lemma, we have

(3.13)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\tilde{f}(t,\theta)=\nabla f\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\exp_{q}(t\theta)=\nabla f\cdot\vec{n}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\vec{n}}(x),\quad\quad\forall t\cdot\theta\in A,x=\exp_{q}(t\cdot\theta),

where
\vec{n}
is the out-forward unit normal vector of
\partial B_{q}(t)
.

From the definition of
Z
, we have

(3.14)

\displaystyle\int_{\tilde{\Sigma}(t)}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(t,\theta)}{\partial t}J(t,\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma(t)}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(t,\theta)}{\partial t}J(t,\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta\quad\quad\forall t\in(0,\infty)\backslash Z.

Now from (
3.8
) and (
3.13
), we have

(3.15)

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma(t)}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(t,\theta)}{\partial t}J(t,\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta=\int_{\exp_{q}(t\cdot\Sigma(t))}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\vec{n}}\mathrm{d}\mu_{\partial B_{q}(t)}.

By (
3.14
) and (
3.15
) and Lemma
3.2
, we get

(3.16)

\displaystyle\int_{\tilde{\Sigma}(t)}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(t,\theta)}{\partial t}J(t,\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{q}(t)}\Delta f,\quad\forall t\in(0,\infty)\backslash Z.

From
[
2
, Theorem III.2.1]
, we know
\mathcal{R}
is continuous. Then using the definition of
\mathcal{R}
and its continuous property, we get

(3.17)

\displaystyle\lim_{s\rightarrow t-}\chi_{\tilde{\Sigma}(s)}(x)=\chi_{\tilde{\Sigma}(t)}(x),\quad\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}.

Also note
\tilde{f}
is a smooth functions and
J
is bounded measurable function. By Lebesgue’s Dominated Convergence Theorem and (
3.17
), we get

(3.18)

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to t-}\int_{\tilde{\Sigma}(s)}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(s,\theta)}{\partial s}J(s,\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta=\int_{\tilde{\Sigma}(t)}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}(t,\theta)}{\partial t}J(t,\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta.

Similarly we also have

(3.19)

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to t-}\int_{B_{q}(s)}\Delta f=\int_{B_{q}(t)}\Delta f,

where we use the definition
B_{q}(t)=\{x:d(q,x)<t\}
to get
\displaystyle\lim_{s\rightarrow t-}\chi_{B_{q}(s)}(x)=\chi_{B_{q}(t)}(x)
.

Therefore by (
3.16
), (
3.18
), (
3.19
) and
\mathcal{H}^{1}(Z)=0
(by Lemma
3.2
), we get (
3.12
).

∎

4.
Sharp mean value inequality

The following is a version of Bishop-Gromov’s volume comparison theorem, which we will use in later argument.

Lemma 4.1
.

For
(M^{n},g)
with
Rc\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle t_{1}^{1-n}J(t_{1},\theta)\geq t_{2}^{1-n}J(t_{2},\theta),\quad\quad\forall 0<t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq\mathcal{R}(\theta).

Proof:
From
[
Libook
, Corollary 2.2]
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(t^{1-n}J(t,\theta))}{\partial t}\leq 0,\quad\quad\forall 0<t<\mathcal{R}(\theta),\,\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}.

Then we get

(4.1)

\displaystyle t_{1}^{1-n}J(t_{1},\theta)\geq t_{2}^{1-n}J(t_{2},\theta),\quad\quad\forall 0<t_{1}\leq t_{2}<\mathcal{R}(\theta).

Note
J(t,\theta)
is well defined and continuous for
t>0
, from (
4.1
) we have

(4.2)

\displaystyle t_{1}^{1-n}J(t_{1},\theta)\geq\lim_{t_{2}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}(\theta)^{-}}t_{2}^{1-n}J(t_{2},\theta)=\mathcal{R}(\theta)^{1-n}J(\mathcal{R}(\theta),\theta),\quad\quad\forall 0<t_{1}<\mathcal{R}(\theta).

From (
4.1
) and (
4.2
), we get

(4.3)

\displaystyle t_{1}^{1-n}J(t_{1},\theta)\geq t_{2}^{1-n}J(t_{2},\theta),\quad\quad\forall 0<t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq\mathcal{R}(\theta).

Then the conclusion follows.

∎

Instead of considering
t^{1-n}\int_{\partial B_{q}(t)}f(x)d\mu(x)
, we study the general term
F(t)
defined in (
4.4
) and show it is non-increasing. Note when
t<\mathrm{inj}(q)
,
F(t)
is exactly
t^{1-n}\int_{\partial B_{q}(t)}f(x)d\mu(x)
, which is used in the proof of Schoen-Yau’s original argument.

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose that
f\in C^{\infty}(M^{n})
satisfies
\Delta f\leq 0
and
f\geq 0
, define

(4.4)

\displaystyle F(t)=t^{1-n}\int_{\tilde{\Sigma}(t)}\tilde{f}(t,\theta)J(t,\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta,

then
F
is non-increasing.

Proof:

Step (1)
. For
t>s>0
, since
\tilde{\Sigma}(t)\subset\tilde{\Sigma}(t-s)
for
s>0
, note
\tilde{f}\geq 0
and Lemma
4.1
, we have

\displaystyle F(t)-F(t-s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\tilde{\Sigma}(t)}\tilde{f}(t,\theta)t^{1-n}J(t,\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta-\int_{\tilde{\Sigma}(t-s)}\tilde{f}(t-s,\theta)(t-s)^{1-n}J(t-s,\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\tilde{\Sigma}(t)}\Big(\tilde{f}(t,\theta)t^{1-n}J(t,\theta)-\tilde{f}(t-s,\theta)(t-s)^{1-n}J(t-s,\theta)\Big)\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\tilde{f}(t-s,\theta)(t-s)^{1-n}J(t-s,\theta)(\chi_{\tilde{\Sigma}(t)}-\chi_{\tilde{\Sigma}(t-s)})\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\tilde{\Sigma}(t)}\Big(\tilde{f}(t,\theta)t^{1-n}J(t,\theta)-\tilde{f}(t-s,\theta)(t-s)^{1-n}J(t-s,\theta)\Big)\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\tilde{\Sigma}(t)}[\tilde{f}(t,\theta)-\tilde{f}(t-s,\theta)]\frac{J(t,\theta)}{t^{n-1}}d\theta+\int_{\tilde{\Sigma}(t)}\tilde{f}(t-s,\theta)\cdot\left(\frac{J(t,\theta)}{t^{n-1}}-\frac{J(t-s,\theta)}{(t-s)^{n-1}}\right)d\theta

(4.5)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\tilde{\Sigma}(t)}[\tilde{f}(t,\theta)-\tilde{f}(t-s,\theta)]\frac{J(t,\theta)}{t^{n-1}}d\theta.

And we also have

\displaystyle F(t+s)-F(t)\leq\int_{\tilde{\Sigma}(t)}\Big(\tilde{f}(t+s,\theta)(t+s)^{1-n}J(t+s,\theta)-\tilde{f}(t,\theta)t^{1-n}J(t,\theta)\Big)\mathrm{d}\theta

(4.6)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\tilde{\Sigma}(t)}[\tilde{f}(t+s,\theta)-\tilde{f}(t,\theta)]\frac{J(t,\theta)}{t^{n-1}}d\theta.

Step (2)
. Assume
0<a_{0}<b_{0}
, we only need to show
F(b_{0})-F(a_{0})\leq 0
. By contradiction, assume there is
\epsilon>0
such that

(4.7)

\displaystyle F(b_{0})-F(a_{0})>\epsilon\cdot[b_{0}-a_{0}].

From (
4.7
), for any
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, there is
[a_{i},b_{i}]\subseteq[a_{i-1},b_{i-1}]
such that

(4.8)

\displaystyle F(b_{i})-F(a_{i})\geq\epsilon\cdot[b_{i}-a_{i}],

\displaystyle b_{i}-a_{i}=\frac{b_{i-1}-a_{i-1}}{2},\quad\text{and}\quad(a_{i}-a_{i-1})\cdot(b_{i}-b_{i-1})=0,

From compactness of
[a_{0},b_{0}]
, and
\displaystyle\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}|b_{i}-a_{i}|=0
, there is
\displaystyle a_{\infty}\in\bigcap_{i=1}^{\infty}[a_{i},b_{i}]
such that

(4.9)

\displaystyle\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}a_{i}=\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}b_{i}=a_{\infty}.

By (
4.8
) and (
4.9
), we get that there is a subsequence of
\{b_{i}\}
or
\{a_{i}\}
(for simplicity, also denoted as
\{b_{i}\}
or
\{a_{i}\}
) such that

(4.10)

\displaystyle F(b_{i})-F(a_{\infty})\geq\epsilon\cdot[b_{i}-a_{\infty}],

(4.11)

\displaystyle F(a_{\infty})-F(a_{i})\geq\epsilon\cdot[a_{\infty}-a_{i}].

Without loss of generality, we can assume (
4.10
) holds in the rest argument (if (
4.11
) holds, similar argument applies, we use (
4.5
) instead).

Then from (
4.10
) and (
4.6
), we get

(4.12)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{[b_{i}-a_{\infty}]}\int_{\tilde{\Sigma}(a_{\infty})}[\tilde{f}(b_{i},\theta)-\tilde{f}(a_{\infty},\theta)]\frac{J(a_{\infty},\theta)}{a_{\infty}^{n-1}}d\theta\geq\epsilon.

Taking the limit with respect to
i
in (
4.12
), using (
4.9
) and Lebesgue’s Dominated Convergence Theorem, we obtain

(4.13)

\displaystyle\int_{\tilde{\Sigma}(a_{\infty})}\frac{J(a_{\infty},\theta)}{a_{\infty}^{n-1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\tilde{f}(a_{\infty},\theta)d\theta\geq\epsilon>0.

However from Lemma
3.4
and
\Delta f\leq 0
, we have

(4.14)

\displaystyle\int_{\tilde{\Sigma}(a_{\infty})}\frac{J(a_{\infty},\theta)}{a_{\infty}^{n-1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\tilde{f}(a_{\infty},\theta)d\theta=a_{\infty}^{1-n}\int_{B_{q}(a_{\infty})}\Delta f\leq 0,

it contradicts (
4.13
).

∎

The following result is a generalization of
[

2.1
, Chapter

]
.

Theorem 4.3

(Sharp mean value inequality)
.

Suppose that
f\in C^{\infty}(M^{n})
satisfies
\Delta f\leq 0
and
f\geq 0
, then

(4.15)

\displaystyle f(q)\geq\frac{1}{n\omega_{n}r^{n-1}}\int_{\partial B_{q}(r)}f,\quad\quad\quad\forall q\in(M^{n},g),r>0.

Remark 4.4
.

In
[
21
]
, the above result is proved for any
r<\mathrm{inj}(q)
, where
\mathrm{inj}(q)
is the injectivity radius of

. Our result removes this restriction on

, furthermore Theorem
4.5
provides the corresponding rigidity result when the equation is obtained.

Proof.

Define
F
as in Lemma
4.2
, from Lemma
4.2
, we get

(4.16)

\displaystyle F(t)\leq\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}F(r)=n\omega_{n}f(q),\quad\quad\forall t>0.

For any
t>0
, notice that
\partial B_{q}(t)=\exp_{q}(t\cdot\tilde{\Sigma}(t))
. From
f\geq 0
, (
3.8
) and (
4.16
), we have

∎

Theorem 4.5

(Rigidity of mean value inequality)
.

Suppose that
f\in C^{\infty}(M^{n})-\{0\}
with
f\geq 0
and
\Delta f\leq 0
. If for some
q\in(M^{n},g)
and
r>0
,

\displaystyle f(q)=\frac{1}{n\omega_{n}r^{n-1}}\int_{\partial B_{q}(r)}f,

then
B_{q}(r)
is isometric to

.

Step (1)
. Suppose there is
f(x_{0})=0
for some
x_{0}\in B_{q}(r)

\Delta f\leq 0,f\geq 0
and the Strong Maximum Principle, we get that

, which is a contradiction.

Hence we have

(4.17)

\displaystyle f(x)>0,\quad\quad\forall x\in B_{q}(r).

Then

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}F(t)=n\omega_{n}f(x)=r^{1-n}\int_{\partial B_{x}(r)}f\leq F(r)\leq\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}F(t)

hence

F(t)\equiv n\omega_{n}f(x),\forall\ 0<t\leq r
. Assume
r>t>s>0
in the following argument, since

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\limsup_{s\to 0}\frac{F(t)-F(t-s)}{s}

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{s\to 0}\frac{1}{s}\int_{{\tilde{\Sigma}}(t)}\left(f(\exp_{x}(t\theta))\,t^{1-n}J(t,\theta)-f(\exp_{x}((t-s)\theta))\,t^{1-n}J(t,\theta)\right)d\theta

\displaystyle\quad+\limsup_{s\to 0}\frac{1}{s}\int_{{\tilde{\Sigma}}(t)}\left(f(\exp_{x}((t-s)\theta))\,t^{1-n}J(t,\theta)-f(\exp_{x}((t-s)\theta))(t-s)^{1-n}J(t-s,\theta)\right)d\theta

\displaystyle\quad+\limsup_{s\to 0}\frac{1}{s}\int_{S^{n-1}}(t-s)^{1-n}f(\exp_{x}((t-s)\theta))J(t-s,\theta)(1_{{\tilde{\Sigma}}(t)}-1_{{\tilde{\Sigma}}(t-s)})d\theta

\displaystyle:=(I)+(II)+(III).

Note the limit
(I)
exists, and
(II)
and
(III)
are nonpositive, hence
(I)\geq 0
. Since by (
3.12
)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq\int_{{\tilde{\Sigma}}(t)}t^{1-n}J(t,\theta)\frac{\partial f(\exp_{x}(t\theta))}{\partial t}d\theta=(I)\geq 0.

then we get
(I)=0
, hence
(II)=(III)=0
. Since
f>0
, there exists
C_{1}>0
such that
0<C_{1}<f
uniformly on compact set
\overline{B_{x}(r)}
, we get

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\limsup_{s\to 0}\frac{1}{s}\int_{{\tilde{\Sigma}}(t)}\left(f(\exp_{x}((t-s)\theta))\,t^{1-n}J(t,\theta)-f(\exp_{x}((t-s)\theta))(t-s)^{1-n}J(t-s,\theta)\right)d\theta

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\limsup_{s\to 0}C_{1}\int_{{\tilde{\Sigma}}(t)}\frac{1}{s}(t^{1-n}J(t,\theta)-(t-s)^{1-n}J(t-s,\theta))d\theta

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 0.

then there exists subsequence

Remark 5.3
.

The strategy of deriving geometric information by examining the derivative of a primary monotone quantity has deep roots in geometric analysis.

Classic examples include the monotonicity formula for minimal surfaces, where the derivative of the area ratio controls the second fundamental form, and Almgren’s frequency function for harmonic functions, whose derivative measures the deviation from homogeneity.

In a similar spirit, the analysis of the quantity
\frac{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(t)}{t^{n-3}}
and its monotonicity serves as a higher-order probe into the geometry of the level sets of
b

Its non-decreasing property, which follows directly from the condition

, packages the combined effects of Ricci curvature, the traceless second fundamental form, and the Hessian of
b
into a single, powerful differential inequality. This formalism is the engine that ultimately drives the asymptotic integral estimates for the scalar curvature.

. We will prove

.

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(t_{1})\leq t_{1}^{1-n}\int_{b=t_{1}}|\nabla b|=\lim_{r\to 0}r^{1-n}\int_{b=r}|\nabla b|=n\omega_{n}.

Therefore we obtain

\displaystyle n\omega_{n}\geq\mathcal{A}(t_{0})+\epsilon\cdot[t_{1}-t_{0}].

Therefore we have
\displaystyle\frac{n\omega_{n}-\mathcal{A}(t_{0})}{t_{1}-t_{0}}\geq\epsilon
. Let

, we get

\displaystyle 0\geq\epsilon,

which is a contradiction. Therefore we get

.

Step (2)
. From
\displaystyle\varliminf_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(t)=c_{1}\leq 0
, we get

(5.13)

\displaystyle\varliminf_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(t)}{t^{n-3}}\leq 0.

is a non-decreasing function in

, hence the limit
\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(t)}{t^{n-3}}
exists.

Now by (
5.13
), we get

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(t)}{t^{n-3}}=\varliminf_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(t)}{t^{n-3}}\leq 0.

By Lemma
5.2

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(s)}{s^{n-3}}\leq\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(t)}{t^{n-3}}\leq 0,\quad\quad\forall s\in\mathbb{R}^{+}.

From Lemma
5.2
, we know that
\frac{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(t)}{t^{n-3}}
is a non-decreasing function in

, and

(5.14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(t_{1})\leq\frac{t_{1}^{n-3}}{t_{2}^{n-3}}\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(t_{2})\leq\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(t_{2}),\quad\quad\forall 0<t_{1}\leq t_{2}<\infty.

Therefore the limit
\displaystyle c_{2}\vcentcolon=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(t)\in[-\infty,0]

Step (3)

\displaystyle c_{2}<0
, then from (
5.14

Let
t\rightarrow\infty
in the above, note

, we get

\displaystyle 0\leq-\infty,

which is a contradiction. Hence we have

.

).

Step (2)
. By Lemma
6.1
and (
6.14
), we get

\displaystyle 0\leq\mathrm{V}_{M}\cdot\varlimsup_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathcal{L}^{1}(\mathcal{T}_{r}\cup\mathcal{B}_{r})}{r}\leq\varlimsup_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\int_{b^{-1}(\mathcal{T}_{r}\cup\mathcal{B}_{r})}R(b^{-1}(t))\cdot|\nabla b|}{8\pi r},

\displaystyle=\varlimsup_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\int_{\mathcal{T}_{r}\cup\mathcal{B}_{r}}dt\int_{b^{-1}(t)}R(b^{-1}(t))}{8\pi r}\leq 0.

This implies
\displaystyle\varlimsup_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathcal{L}^{1}(\mathcal{T}_{r}\cup\mathcal{B}_{r})}{r}=0
by
\mathrm{V}_{M}>0
, hence (
6.13
) follows.

∎

The main result of this section is as follows.

Theorem 6.6
.

For a complete, non-parabolic Riemannian manifold
M^{3}
with
Rc\geq 0
and maximal volume growth, there is

\displaystyle\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\int_{b\leq r}R\cdot|\nabla b|}{r}=8\pi\big[1-\mathrm{V}_{M}\big].

Proof:
From Proposition
6.3
we know that
M^{3}
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Now using Lemma
6.4
, we know each smooth surface
b^{-1}(t)
is connected.

From the definition of
\mathcal{G}_{r}
and Gauss-Bonnet’s Theorem, we obtain

(6.16)

\displaystyle\int_{b^{-1}(t)}R(b^{-1}(t))=8\pi,\quad\quad\quad\forall t\in\mathcal{G}_{r}.

Now from Lemma
6.5
and (
6.16
), we have

\displaystyle\quad\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\int_{(0,r]-\mathcal{S}}dt\int_{b^{-1}(t)}R(b^{-1}(t))}{r}

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\int_{\mathcal{G}_{r}}dt\int_{b^{-1}(t)}R(b^{-1}(t))}{r}+\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\int_{\mathcal{B}_{r}\cup\mathcal{T}_{r}}dt\int_{b^{-1}(t)}R(b^{-1}(t))}{r}

(6.17)

\displaystyle=8\pi\cdot\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathcal{L}^{1}(\mathcal{G}_{r})}{r}+0=8\pi.

From the Gauss equation, we have

(6.18)

\displaystyle R(M^{n})

\displaystyle=R\big(b^{-1}(t)\big)+2Rc(\vec{n})+|\Pi_{0}|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}H^{2}.

From Proposition
5.7
and Proposition
5.8
, using (
6.18
) and (
6.17
) we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\int_{b\leq r}R\cdot|\nabla b|}{r}=\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\int_{0}^{r}dt\int_{b^{-1}(t)}R(b^{-1}(t))}{r}-8\pi\mathrm{V}_{M}=8\pi\big[1-\mathrm{V}_{M}\big].

∎

Corollary 6.7
.

Assume
(M^{3},g)
is a complete Riemannian manifold with
Rc\geq\epsilon\cdot R\cdot g\geq 0
, where
\epsilon>0
is a fixed constant. Furthermore assume
(M^{3},g)
is maximal volume growth, then
M^{3}
is isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Proof:
From Proposition
5.7
and Theorem
6.6
, we obtain

\displaystyle 0=\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{r}\int_{b\leq r}Rc(\vec{n})\cdot|\nabla b|\geq\epsilon\cdot\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{r}\int_{b\leq r}R\cdot|\nabla b|=\epsilon\cdot 8\pi(1-\mathrm{V}_{M^{3}}).

Hence we get
\mathrm{V}_{M^{3}}=1
, which implies
M^{3}
is isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{3}
by the rigidity part of Bishop-Gromov’s volume comparison Theorem.

∎
