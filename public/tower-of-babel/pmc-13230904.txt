Clinical validation of a high-performance somatic exome sequencing assay: from target-enrichment strategy to variant calling
Authors: Junko Tsuji, Micah Rickles-Young, Justin Abreu, Alyssa Friedman, Caroline Petersen, J Bryan, Konrad Scheffler, Taylor O’Connell, Theo Heyns, Edyta Malolepsza, Mark Fleharty, Katie Larkin, Kenneth J Livak, Shuqiang Li, David A Reardon, Catherine J Wu, Patrick A Ott, Fanny Dao, Heidi Rehm, Max Jan, Chip Stewart, Gad Getz, Varun Jain, Severine Catreux, Carrie Cibulskis, Niall Lennon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClinical whole-exome sequencing (WES) has revolutionized clinical diagnostics by enabling scalable, cost-effective molecular profiling to detect somatic variants and identify novel therapeutic targets. In particular, the clinical evaluation of somatic variants relies on both high-quality sequencing data and robust variant detection with rapid turnaround times. We developed an updated WES workflow utilizing a co-developed Twist Bioscience targeting panel and the DRAGEN (Dynamic Read Analysis for GENomics) platform, benchmarked with cell line mixtures containing >40,000 simulated variants and a clinical cohort of FFPE tumor specimens. Our assay, the Broad Clinical Somatic Whole Exome Assay V6.0, demonstrated high sensitivity (96.9% for SNVs with variant allele fraction [VAF] >10% at ≥125X; 93.5% for InDels with VAF > 20% at ≥125X) and low false positive rates (0.04 and 0.01 per Mb, respectively). The assay meets clinical requirements and enables large-scale, accurate variant profiling of cancers, expanding opportunities for molecular diagnostics across diverse clinical settings.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Genetics, Oncology

Introduction

Clinical sequencing of tumor specimens for variant profiling is frequently performed using targeted gene panel assays. While these panels effectively capture key diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic alterations, they include only a limited set (100’s) of cancer-related genes1–3. In recent years, advances in next-generation sequencing technologies have driven a growing demand for more comprehensive variant profiling. Whole-exome sequencing (WES) enables a broad range of high-quality somatic analyses, including accurate quantification of tumor mutational burden, tumor-informed measurable residual disease fingerprint identification, and discovery of tumor-specific neoantigens4,5. For such clinical applications, maximizing the accurate identification of non-synonymous, tumor-specific, protein-coding variants is crucial. As part of ongoing efforts to standardize clinical WES, multiple pilot studies have examined concordance in mutation calls across institutions, reporting ~95% agreement for selected cancer genes of interest6–9. However, to our knowledge, no study has systematically assessed the sensitivity and specificity of a clinical tumor-normal WES.

To address these needs, we developed a high-performance clinical somatic WES assay, the Broad Clinical Somatic Whole Exome Assay V6.0 (hereafter, V6), which uses multiple novel technologies within our CLIA-certified laboratory to identify single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) and somatic insertions and deletions (InDels). Compared to our earlier version (V2), the updated assay demonstrated substantial improvements10, including more uniform sequencing coverage, faster turnaround times, and reduced computational processing. These enhancements were enabled by an innovative WES targeted panel (Twist Biosciences) and by an operationally streamlined workflow using Illumina’s Dynamic Read Analysis for GENomics (Illumina DRAGEN) platform11. These features are particularly critical for clinical laboratories, which require high-quality, reproducible, and scalable workflows to meet increasing clinical demand while controlling costs.

Results

WES workflow overview and study design

We comprehensively evaluated the performance of the updated V6 WES. Figure 1 summarizes both the V2 and V6 WES workflows. Briefly, input DNA from tumor and matched normal specimens was sheared to a target fragment length of 150 bp and enriched using the corresponding probe sets (see Methods). The resulting libraries were sequenced as paired-end reads and aligned to the reference genome (Fig. 1A) using BWA-mem12 (0.7.15-r1140) for V2 and DRAGEN11 (v4.3.11) for V6 (parameter selection described in Supplementary Note and Supplementary Fig. 1). Somatic variants were detected using MuTect13 (1.1.6) and Strelka214 for V2 and DRAGEN11 (v4.3.11) for V6, with artifact filtering applied in both workflows (Fig. 1B; all DRAGEN options provided in Supplementary Table 1). To benchmark performance (Fig. 1C), we generated three datasets: (i) mixtures of five, ten, and twenty HapMap cell lines (Supplementary Table 2) to simulate somatic variation and assess sensitivity and reproducibility; (ii) six replicates of NA12878 paired as tumor-normal samples (30 pairs total) to measure specificity based on false positive calls; and (iii) clinical FFPE specimens to evaluate recall and concordance of 47 clinically relevant variants from 571 cancer-relevant genes (Supplementary Table 3).

GC-bias-corrected target-enrichment strategy showed robust coverage

First, we evaluated WES coverage performance. Sufficient per-base coverage is a key factor needed to call variants15. However, it has been established that both GC-rich (GC ≥ 70%) and AT-rich (GC ≤ 30%) genomic regions yield less sequencing coverage16. The initial 5’ exons of transcripts are more GC-rich than internal and 3’ exons, which impacts coverage in WES. To evaluate target-enrichment strategies used in WES analysis by V2 vs. V6, we downsampled the mean target coverage 150X of both exomes that were sequenced from 11 FFPE GBM tumors and 10 matched fresh-frozen normals. We further computed the fraction of sufficiently covered sites ( ≥ 20X reads with base quality ≥ 20; mapping quality, MAPQ ≥ 20) in the initial exons of the 397 cancer-related targeted genes (Supplementary Table 4). WES V6 showed stable coverage across samples prepared from both higher quality normal (blood/saliva) and lower quality FFPE-tumor derived DNA (Fig. 2A). The coverage differences between WES V2 and V6 were particularly pronounced for the targets with GC ≥ 60% between WES V2 and V6 (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p ≤ 1.5 × 10-5; Fig. 2B). Of the 397 genes, 85 initial exons were GC-rich ( ≥ 70%), and WES V6 achieved substantially improved coverage of these regions compared to WES V2 in both normal and tumor samples (p ≤ 1.6 × 10-9; Fig. 2B). Overall, WES V6 provided improved coverage compared to V2 across exonic regions with varying GC content (Fig. 2C).

Despite the two different alignment tools used for WES V2 and V6, we did not detect notable differences in read alignments between the two assays. This was evaluated by aligning one of the WES V6 samples with the BWA and DRAGEN aligners and comparing aligned reads in chromosome 18. Overall, 3,842,963 (95.33%) of the entire reads (n = 4,031,124) were mapped to the identical genomic regions; 166,077 reads (4.12%) were mapped to repetitive alignments with mapping quality (MAPQ) scores of less than 3. Because the majority of variant calling tools disregard low-MAPQ reads, nearly all reads (99.45%) will contribute to the downstream variant calling in the same mapping loci. Among the remaining 0.55% discordant reads, BWA mapped 20,072 extra reads (0.50%; median MAPQ = 16); and DRAGEN aligned 2,012 extra reads (0.05%; entire MAPQ = 0; Supplementary Fig. 2A). Notably, these BWA-only reads with MAPQ ≥ 10 displayed numerous mismatches, indicating these were likely mapping artifacts (Supplementary Fig. 2B).

Somatic variant calling sensitivity in simulated data

In the HapMap pools, 46,358, 51,515, and 69,887 germline variant sites from the 5, 10, and 20 cell lines defined as ground truth variants in the 34.3 megabase (Mb) targeted genomic regions were evaluated for variant calling sensitivity. The WES V2 and V6 variant calling workflows were run in tumor-only mode, except for the WES V2 InDel detection by Strelka2, which requires a matched normal input. Additionally, a panel of normals was not applied, so as to retain the HapMap germline variants in the benchmarking series.

Overall, 89.8% and 90.2% of the simulated SNVs were detected by the WES V2 and V6 workflows, respectively (“all_plex”, data aggregated across 5-, 10-, and 20-plex experiments in Fig. 3A). As expected, the SNV detection sensitivity in both WES V2 and V6 correlated with VAF and read coverage. In the 5- and 10-plex samples, WES V6 showed slightly higher sensitivity (median sensitivity, 97.3% and 94.2% for the 5- and 10-plex samples) than WES V2 (95.5% and 92.8%). Interestingly, the WES V2 was slightly more sensitive (83.6%) in the 20-plex low VAF setting than the WES V6 (82.3%). The sensitivity of detecting SNVs with ≥ 10% VAF saturated at 100X (87.1-92.1% for V2; 88.3-97.9% for V6) and slightly decreased after 500X due to high coverage read filters in both workflows (e.g., roughly 3% decline at 750X) (Fig. 3B). The detection of SNVs with 5% VAF required 300X to reach 79.2% and 76.1% sensitivity in the WES V2 and V6 workflows. To measure overall clinical sensitivity, we calculated the SNV recall rate for variants with VAF > 10% and read depth ≥125X, setting a minimum performance threshold of ≥95% (highlighted in red; Fig. 3B). The WES V2 and V6 workflows achieved SNV sensitivities of 95.9% and 96.9%, respectively.

The InDel recall was 33.7% for WES V2 and 84.2% for WES V6 (“all_plex” in Fig. 3A). This difference was primarily due to the WES V2 workflow undesirably filtering variants due to Strelka2’s requirement of using a matched normal sample. This germline filtering effect was particularly pronounced for common germline InDels that have higher allele frequencies, as these InDels exhibited the lowest sensitivity. In contrast, the WES V6 DRAGEN workflow, which supports operation without a matched normal sample, showed InDel sensitivity that correlated with VAF (Fig. 3A). Notably, the WES V6 required 350X coverage to achieve a detection sensitivity of 79.6% for InDels at 10% VAF (Fig. 3B). To reflect clinical conditions, we evaluated InDel detection using a threshold of 20% VAF at sites with a minimum read depth of 125X, aiming for a target sensitivity of 80%. Under these parameters, WES V6 achieved an InDel sensitivity of 93.5% (highlighted in red; Fig. 3B), demonstrating that the updated design not only provides tumor-only InDel calling capability but also delivers reliable performance for InDels within the clinically actionable range.

Somatic variant calling reproducibility in simulated data

Using the same sets of 5-, 10-, and 20-plex HapMap cell line DNA pools, we assessed variant calling reproducibility in the WES V2 and V6 workflows by calculating the Jaccard index for each triplicate set (Supplementary Table 5). For SNV calls, higher VAF settings, such as the 5-plex (median Jaccard index: 95.7% in V2, 97.0% in V6) and 10-plex (93.6% in V2, 93.7% in V6), exhibited greater reproducibility. WES V6 outperformed V2 in the 5- and 10-plex settings, while WES V2 showed slightly higher reproducibility (81.3% in V2 vs. 80.7% in V6) in the 20-plex setting.

For InDel calls in WES V6, reproducibility remained relatively high, with median Jaccard indices of 84.7%, 83.9%, and 75.4% in the 5-, 10-, and 20-plex triplicates, respectively. In contrast, reproducibility in WES V2 was substantially lower across all plexes (83.9%, 66.8%, and 66.1%) due to the germline variant filtering step, which removed many high-VAF InDels originating from common germline variants. This filtering disproportionately affected InDel reproducibility in WES V2 compared to WES V6.

Somatic variant calling specificity with NA12878 replicates

To assess specificity, we calculated FP rates for SNVs and InDels in WES V2 and V6 using 30 pairs of NA12878 replicates. The median FP SNVs per Mbp were 0.572 (20 median FPs) in WES V2 and 0.0262 (1 median FP) in WES V6 (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p = 5.7 × 10−11; Fig. 3C). As expected, many FP SNVs were recurrent with low VAF (~5%), appearing at the same genomic positions across runs (up to 4 times in both workflows). Given that most tumor specimens harbor more than one mutation per Mb across various cancer types17–19, we established a clinical FP rate threshold of < 1 FP/Mbp. While 3 of the 30 WES V2 runs exceeded this cutoff for SNVs, all the V6 runs remained within the threshold (Fig. 3C). For InDels, the WES V6 workflow detected FPs in 10 of the 30 runs (1 FP in 9 runs; 2 FPs in 1 run; 0 median FPs), yet all FPs remained below the clinical threshold, and the difference between WES V2 and V6 was minimal (p > 0.05). Similar to SNVs, FP InDels frequently recurred at the same genomic loci showed VAFs up to 33.3% (median VAF, 4.89%). These results demonstrate that the WES V6 assay more effectively controls false positive detections while maintaining clinically acceptable specificity for both SNVs and InDels.

DRAGEN outperforms variant calling in WES V6

WES V2 and V6 exhibited significant differences in sequencing coverage, with V6 achieving more uniform and sufficient coverage across targeted regions (Fig. 2). To fairly evaluate variant calling performance, we selected the WES V6 datasets with superior coverage for benchmarking. We then ran the WES V2 computational workflow on the WES V6 HapMap and NA12878 datasets and calculated performance metrics based on the WES V6 targeted regions. Notably, this analysis recapitulated the trends observed in the original comparisons (Supplementary Fig. 3).

The V2 workflow called 90.3% of simulated SNVs and 32.9% of InDels, lower than DRAGEN on WES V6 (90.5% and 86.4%; “all_prex” in Supplementary Fig. 3A). Clinical sensitivity at 125X coverage was 93.2% for SNVs with VAF > 10% and 33.1% for InDels with VAF > 20% in the V2 workflow, again lower than DRAGEN’s performance (95.5% and 92.6%; Supplementary Fig. 3B). Interestingly, MuTect in the V2 workflow showed slightly higher SNV recall in 20-plex runs at low VAF (84.4%) than DRAGEN (82.9%; Supplementary Fig. 3A); however, this came at the cost of increased FPs compared with DRAGEN (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p = 5.7 × 10−11; Supplementary Fig. 3C). Overall, these results confirm that the updated computational workflow with DRAGEN provides improved variant calling performance on WES V6 data.

Somatic variant calling recall in clinical samples

Next, we assessed SNV and InDel recall in the WES V6 workflow by cross-referencing variants reported in three previously published studies for which we had access to the same extracted DNA specimens20–22 (Supplementary Table 3). The analysis was limited to clinically relevant regions of interest called by WES V2 or OncoPanel23 (see Methods). Overall, the concordance across the cohorts was high: 37/39 SNVs (94.9%) and 6/8 InDels (75.0%). The two InDels reported in the WES V2, which were missed in the WES V6, were supported by only 2–3 reads each; thus, it was unclear if these discordant InDels were due to technical artifacts, and/or differences in coverage and sampling in the newly prepared WES V6 data. Similarly, one of the two discordant SNVs had no supporting reads in the WES V6.

Somatic variant calling concordance between WES V2 and V6 in GBM samples

To compare variant calls in the WES V2 and V6 workflows in clinical GBM samples, we analyzed 11 GBM tumor-normal pairs that were sequenced using both assays. To account for variability in coverage and avoid bias in the comparison, we used MutationValidator24 to estimate variant detection power. We restricted our analyses to variants with at least 95% detection power in both protocols.

A total of 1822 SNVs were concordant between the WES V2 and V6 workflows, corresponding to a 97.0% recall rate (1822/1879) of V2-detected SNVs by V6 (Fig. 4A). Of the remaining variants, 57 SNVs (3.03%) were unique to V2, while 156 SNVs (7.89%) were unique to V6. Notably, V6-only SNVs exhibited a broader VAF distribution than V2-only SNVs (median VAF: 0.273 vs. 0.028; Fig. 4B). The vast majority of V6-only SNVs (149/156; 95.5%) had VAF ≥ 10%, whereas the highest VAF among V2-only SNVs was 6.4%. We further analyzed the VAFs at the genomic positions of V2- and V6-only SNVs across both workflows. The VAFs of missed variants in V2 closely matched those of V6-only SNVs and were unexpectedly high (median VAF: 0.281 in V2 at V6-only sites) compared to those of V2-only SNVs (0.024 in V6 at V2-only sites), suggesting potential FNs in the V2 workflow.

For InDels, WES V6 recalled all 132 InDels detected by V2, yielding a 100% InDel recall rate (Fig. 4A). Additionally, V6 identified 77 unique InDels with a median VAF of 0.294 (Fig. 4B). Similar to the SNV results, supporting reads for these V6-only InDels were relatively high in WES V2 with a median VAF of 0.146. Manual review revealed that 146 of 156 V6-only SNVs and 12 of 77 V6-only InDels were likely true positives, supported by consistent variant reads across samples.

Validation and characteristics of variant calls between WES V2 and V6 in GBM samples

To further evaluate V2- and V6-only variants, we selected 9 variants, including both powered and unpowered variants from each WES, along with 11 shared variants, and then performed PCR validations (Fig. 4C). Among the PCR-tested V2-only variants, 5 out of 9 were detected, although their VAFs were all below 2.5%. For V6-only variants, 8 out of 9 were confirmed by PCR. As highlighted in Fig. 4C, 6 of these variants had high VAF (>10%); importantly, these 6 variants were not present in the matched normal samples, supporting their classification as true somatic variants (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Overall, the variant profiles in both V2 and V6 WES of the 11 GBM samples were largely composed of C > T variants, which is consistent with the observed variant profiles of 239 GBM samples reported in TCGA19 (Fig. 4D). Interestingly, C > A variants detected among the V2-only SNVs tended to have C base as the immediate 5’ and 3’ contexts, suggesting these mutations are most likely artifacts25.

Lastly, we evaluated the variant status of known oncogenes26 in the WES V2 and V6 data from the 11 GBM samples. One patient, GBM_S7, exhibited the highest number of variants across the cohort, accounting for 67.4% of all SNVs and 80.9% of all InDels identified across both workflows. This elevated tumor mutational burden was likely due to a somatic POLD1 p.R689W [NM_002691.4:c.2065 C > T] variant, which is known to impair DNA fidelity27. Across the cohort, we detected 27 unique nonsynonymous variants in 11 known oncogenes (Fig. 4E). Of these, 20 variants (74.0%) were concordant between WES V2 and V6. Two variants (PTPN11 p.R265Q [NM_002834.5:c.794 G > A] and MAP3K1 p.V652I [NM_005921.2:c.1954G>A]) were missed by WES V6 due to the absence of alternate allele reads at those sites. Conversely, five variants were reported only by WES V6 (SMC1A p.R799Q [NM_006306.4:c.2396 G > A], EGFR p.D587N [NM_005228.5:c.1759 G > A], and three PTEN p.V119F [NM_000314.8:c.355 G > T], R130* [c.388 C > T], and p.R378fs [c.1133_1136del] variants). Upon further inspection of the aligned reads and the genomic features, we found that the EGFR variant had supporting reads with VAF < 1% in both workflows, but it was filtered out in V2 by MuTect’s nearby_gap_events and alt_allele_in_normal filters. The remaining four V6-only variants had higher VAF (median VAF, 32%) but were excluded due to the read-remapping filter in the V2 workflow, likely triggered by nearby highly conserved pseudogenes28.

In summary, WES V6 demonstrated a high level of concordance with WES V2, recovering the majority of variants. Moreover, the V6 assay identified additional somatic variants, including those with high VAFs. Importantly, WES V6 concordantly detected known recurrent variants in these 11 GBM samples. These results show that our V6 assay is capable of sensitively and comprehensively profiling the mutational landscape of clinical tumor specimens.

Evaluation of V2 and V6 computational workflows on WES V6 GBM samples

To assess the performance of the computational workflows, we ran the V2 computational workflow to the same set of 11 GBM samples sequenced with WES V6. For filtering, we applied a panel of normals generated from the 143 germline WES V6 samples, rather than the 355 germline WES V2 samples originally used in the V2 workflow.

Overall, the V2 workflow identified 3,090 SNVs and 190 InDels across the GBM samples, with a median of 23 additional SNVs per sample (i.e. the median number of variants uniquely called by V2 compared to V6; Supplementary Fig. 5A). Notably, MuTect reported ≥300 SNVs in 3 of 11 samples, and these additional SNVs had extremely low VAFs (median 0.019, 75th percentile 0.029; Supplementary Fig. 5B). Manual inspection indicated that most MuTect-only calls arose from mismatches due to misaligned reads, potential artifacts, or alternative alleles present in the matched normal sample (Supplementary Fig. 5C). VAFs from the V2 and V6 workflows showed strong concordance (Spearman ρ = 0.995), with a few outliers including two DRAGEN-only SNVs and two Strelka2-only insertions (Supplementary Fig. 5B). Manual review revealed that the DRAGEN-only SNVs (0.46 and 0.38 VAF) were missed in the V2 workflow due to mapping errors that triggered MuTect filters, whereas the Strelka2-only insertions (0.76 and 0.62 VAF) were likely FPs arising from alternative alleles in the matched normal (Supplementary Fig. 5D). These results suggest that DRAGEN’s systematic noise model outperforms the V2 workflow’s filtering step, which depends on a larger panel of germline WES samples. Furthermore, DRAGEN’s enhanced read mapping reduces erroneous variant calls from misaligned reads.

Discussion

Overall, the performance of V6 assay is suitable for clinical use and improved callability in undercovered regions of prior whole-exome workflows. Altogether, our validation study demonstrates that the latest version of our CLIA-certified WES assay, WES V6 using DRAGEN and TWIST targeting panel, showed superior performance compared to the previous WES V2 workflow. WES V6 not only reliably reproduced oncogenic variants previously identified by V2 but also showed improved detection sensitivity and specificity. Notably, the tiling bait design in V6 provided better coverage across a broad range of GC content, which is particularly important for capturing promoter regions of cancer driver genes.

Our clinical validation strategy was designed to assess the reliability of WES variant detection for clinical return of results across diverse sample types. The HapMap pooling approach and NA12878 replicate cohort served as standardized references for evaluating sensitivity and specificity, using orthogonally verified ground truth sets. However, these resources simulate somatic variation through the mixture of germline variants, and thus, the data do not fully replicate the complexity of real tumor-derived somatic variants or variations in sample quality. Another limitation of the pooled reference cohort is the InDel sensitivity in WES V2. The performance was confounded by undesired filtering of the common germline InDels due to Strelka2’s required matched normal input. In contrast, our GBM, CLL, breast cancer, and angiosarcoma sample cohorts enabled an assessment of concordance for actual somatic events in patient tumor specimens (FFPE) previously tested on CLIA-validated platforms, including WES V2 or targeted gene panels (e.g., OncoPanel). While most clinical samples lack a ground truth set, variants shared between two orthogonal platforms with differing capture, sequencing, alignment, and variant calling methods are more likely to be true somatic events.

In the VAF concordance analysis, we observed that the VAF distribution of the V6-only variants was similar to that of variants shared between V2 and V6 variants. Conversely, V2-only variants generally had lower VAFs, suggesting that some V2-only variants were likely filtered out by DRAGEN’s haplotype pruning filter. As DRAGEN is a haplotype assembly-based variant caller, the V6 workflow utilizes a function to prune candidate haplotype graphs to control false discovery rates. Edges in the graph (and consequently the haplotypes supported by such edges) are pruned if the edge weight, roughly corresponding to allele fraction, is below a user-specified fraction (1.5% in our V6 workflow). This is achieved by setting the target-vaf command line option to 4.5%, corresponding to a pruning threshold of 1.5%. DRAGEN provides the target-VAF option to allow the user to roughly specify the minimum VAF of interest and sets the pruning threshold a factor 3 below the minimum VAF to minimize the probability of excluding haplotypes of interest despite imperfect correspondence between edge weights and allele frequencies. Unlike a hard VAF cutoff, this approach balances sensitivity and specificity more effectively. We evaluated multiple parameter combinations (e.g., target-VAF at 3.0%, 4.5%, and 6.0%, with matching hard VAF cutoffs of 1.0%, 1.5%, and 2.0%) using NA12878 and HapMap pools. The 4.5% setting provided optimal performance, achieving 96.9% sensitivity while keeping false positives below 0.041 FP/Mb (Supplementary Table 6). It is also important to note that the admixture of different haplotypes present in the contrived datasets is particularly challenging for haplotype-assembly–based callers and has an impact on the measured sensitivity and specificity reported in this study. Additional analyses with larger sample cohorts may allow future improvements to the choice of DRAGEN parameters.

The goal of a clinical variant calling pipeline is to provide a list of high-confidence variants, especially clonal mutations in driver genes, to guide therapeutic decisions. While the V6 workflow may detect a smaller proportion of the lowest variants ( < 5% VAF) relative to V2, such variants often arise from tumor subclonality or low purity and are more likely to include base damage artifacts or to arise from sequencing error. This tunable DRAGEN filtering option helps to minimize these spurious calls from potential sequencing artifacts in the low VAF tiers, such as FFPE-originated C > T deamination artifacts29. The computational workflow comparison also showed that DRAGEN outperforms the V2 workflow in filtering performance with only a small set of germline WES panels. DRAGEN’s filtering approach has helped to reduce candidate variant lists in lower-quality specimens and speed up the process of tertiary interpretation and reporting while still achieving adequate sensitivity.

In this study, we focused on evaluating SNVs and InDels. However, as previous studies30–32 reported, other somatic events, such as copy number and structural variants, are also critical drivers in many cancer types. Notably, most oncogenic rearrangements involve intronic breakpoints, and our V6 assay can be supplemented with optional intronic regions from 30 cancer genes. As the next step, we plan to expand our validation to incorporate these additional variant classes, thereby enabling a more comprehensive characterization of patient-specific events for clinical application.

In conclusion, our clinical somatic whole-exome V6 assay demonstrated highly sensitive performance in detecting SNVs and InDels while effectively controlling false positive detections. The sensitivity and reproducibility of our V6 assay are well-suited for variant profiling in clinical settings, where precise profiling of patient-specific variants is essential for defining therapeutic targets.

Methods

DNA library preparation

For comparing the performance of WES between V2 and V6, 150 ng of DNA was targeted for input, and fragmentation was performed with Covaris shearing with a target fragment length of 150 bp. End-repair, A-tailing, and adaptor ligation were performed using a commercially available kit (KAPA HyperPrep with Library Amplification). Adaptor strategy differed between WES V2 and V6. The V2 platform uses custom, unique dual indexed adaptors (UDI) available from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT). The V6 platform uses IDT Stubby-Y unique molecular indexed adaptors with sample barcodes comprising unique 8-base UDIs embedded within the p5 and p7 primers. Enzymatic purification was performed in both assays using Beckman Coulter AMPure XP beads with 30 µL elution volume to maximize library concentration.

V2: WES target-enrichment and sequencing

Target enrichment was performed with the Illumina Nextera Rapid Capture Exome v1.2 kit (Fig. 1A). The probes of this kit are 78 bp in length, and were tandemly placed across the targeted regions without overlaps. The target regions were composed of 37.7 Mb of exonic regions made up of all targets from the Illumina content exome design, all coding regions of genes in GENCODE V11, and RefSeq and KnownGene tracks from the UCSC genome browser (http://genome.ucsc.edu). Resulting libraries were sequenced with 76 bp paired-end reads using HiSeq 2000 or 2500 sequencers.

V6: WES target-enrichment and sequencing

Target enrichment was performed with probes from the Twist Alliance Clinical Research Exome (Twist Bioscience; Fig. 1A). The probes of this kit target 34.97 Mb of mainly exonic regions including 33.05 Mb of the Consensus Coding Sequence database and 1.92 Mb of custom content (including genomic regions from OMIM, COSMIC, 99% of ClinVar variants, the mitochondrial genome, 59 ACMG secondary finding genes, and regions covered in the targeted OncoPanel assay, which included select intronic regions for 30 genes to capture clinically relevant structural variants23). The probes are 120 bp in length and designed to be optimized for evenness of coverage based on the %GC content of each target. Resulting libraries were sequenced with 151 bp paired-end reads using NovaSeq 6000 SP or S4 flowcells.

Read alignment and preprocessing

Reads were aligned using the NCBI Human Reference Genome Build hg19 (Fig. 1A). WES V2 reads were aligned with BWA-mem12 (0.7.15-r1140), and PCR duplicates were removed with MarkDuplicates in GATK433 (version 4.0.5.1). IndelRealigner in GATK334 (version nightly-2015-07-31-g3c929b0) was run as a post-processing step to correct misaligned insertions and deletions in reads. WES V6 reads were aligned with DRAGEN11 (v4.3.11) with the options of --Aligner.clip-pe-overhang 2 and --Aligner.pe-prop-pair-max-stddev 15 (the parameter selection criteria described in Supplementary Note; Supplementary Fig. 1). PCR duplicates were marked with --enable-duplicate-marking. To compare sequencing and alignment metrics between WES V2 and V6, CollectMultipleMetrics and CollectHsMetrics in GATK4 were run on the resulting BAMs from V2 and V6.

Somatic variant calling

For WES V2, our tertiary analysis processing workflow in the Broad Institute CLIA/CAP certified laboratory was run on each sample (Fig. 1B). Briefly, somatic SNVs were detected with MuTect13 (v1.1.6), and InDels were identified with Strelka214 (v2.9.10). Artifacts were filtered by running base orientation bias filters25, remapping reads supporting variants with BLAT35, and applying panels of normals composed of 355 WES V2 samples and 8,334 samples from TCGA (https://www.cancer.gov/tcga).

For WES V6, our latest CLIA workflow, DRAGEN was run to detect SNVs and InDels with the artifact filter options, --vc-enable-unequal-ntd-errors and --vc-enable-trimer-context (Fig. 1B). DRAGEN’s systematic noise file, which is equivalent to panels of normals, was applied to filter candidate somatic variants (--vc-systematic-noise-filter-threshold 40). The systematic noise file was built from 143 germline WES samples by using DRAGEN version 4.3.11, the version optimized to capture low allele fraction artifacts in deep WES. Additional filters, including the population allele frequency (--germline-tagging-pop-af-threshold 0.001) and the pruning fraction (--vc-target-vaf 0.045, which prunes haplotypes with one-third of the specified variant allele cutoff) were applied to further discard artifacts. All DRAGEN options used for somatic variant detection on V6 WES are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Sensitivity benchmarking and analyses: HapMap pools

To simulate somatic variation, five, ten, and twenty HapMap cell lines (Supplementary Table 2) previously characterized as part of the 1000 Genomes Project were pooled together in equimolar proportions (Fig. 1C). This approach generated mixtures containing germline variants at a range of variant allele fraction (VAF)36. Using this strategy, we estimated variant calling sensitivity as a function of the expected VAF and observed read coverage36. Each pool was prepared in triplicate for both WES V2 and V6 assays. To preserve the simulated variants originated from germline mixing, variant calling was performed without a matched normal input or a panel of normal noise control file for both assays. GATK434,37 tools CalculateMixingFractions, AnnotateVcfWithExpectedAlleleFraction, and AnnotateVcfWithBamDepth were used to estimate the fraction of each cell line in the HapMap pools, calculate the VAF of expected variants, and determine read depth at each site. Called variants were classified as true positives (TPs) or false negatives (FNs) by comparing them to the expected germline variants present in each pool. Sensitivity for SNV and InDel ( < 10 bp) was calculated as TP/(TP + FN). To enable fair comparison between assays, clinical sensitivity was assessed only within the genomic regions targeted by both exome designs.

Specificity benchmarking and analyses: NA12878 replicates

NA12878 is a well-characterized genomic DNA derived from the Coriell cell line GM12878 and homozygous reference regions defined by the Genome in a Bottle project38,39. Six replicates of NA12878 sourced from the Coriell Cell Repositories were generated with both exome sequencing protocols (Fig. 1C). Each replicate was paired with the rest of the replicates to create simulated tumor-normal pairs, and the variant calling workflows were run on the 30 pairs to measure specificity. Any called variants were marked as false positives (FPs), and the FP rates of SNVs and InDels were calculated by dividing FPs by the total target territory per megabase pair (Mbp).

Variant callability and concordance: Clinical samples

Ethical approval for this study was obtained under the protocols listed in the corresponding methods sections and was reviewed by the Broad Institute Office of Sponsored Research and Compliance. The sample sets used in the analyses were summarized in Fig. 1C.

Variant calls of the previously analyzed 16 tumor cases from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) breast cancer samples (n = 7)20, chronic lymphocytic lymphoma (CLL) cells that had undergone ficoll-based isolation (n = 4)21, and angiosarcoma FFPE samples (n = 2)22 in WES V2 were collected as the orthogonal dataset. WES V6 was performed on the same set of the 16 tumors and the matched germline DNA extracted from blood or saliva. Osteosarcoma FFPE tissue samples (n = 3)40 in tumor-only targeted OncoPanel assay23 were also included in the dataset. To curate a set of clinically relevant variants for evaluation, we selected 47 high-quality coding variants comprising SNVs (VAF ≥ 10%) and InDels (VAF ≥ 20%) from 571 cancer-relevant genes (Supplementary Table 3).

Additionally, the WES V2 and V6 data of two glioblastoma (GBM) patient cohorts were generated to evaluate the concordance of the variant calls between the assays. The first cohort included samples from three GBM patients collected in the context of a clinical trial (NCT02287428; Dana-Farber Cancer Institute [DFCI] Review Board #14-362). The second cohort included tumor FFPE and paired normal samples for 8 GBM patients collected during the course of routine clinical care (see Supplementary Note for the sample acquisition; DFCI Review Board #10-417; Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board #2019P000017).

The variant workflows were run in tumor-normal mode on the WES V2 and V6 data. Only variants at the sites with sufficient detection power were included in the downstream analysis. The variant detection power was calculated by MutationValidator24 (version 40). Variants called only in V2 or V6 were manually reviewed on Integrative Genomics Viewer41 (IGV) and categorized into 5 groups: (i) FPs due to misaligned reads, (ii) FNs despite the presence of alternate bases in the reads, (iii) FNs due to a post-processing filter discarded variants, (iv) no alternate bases found in the platform, and (v) variants not called due to the presence of alternate bases in the matched germline WES.
