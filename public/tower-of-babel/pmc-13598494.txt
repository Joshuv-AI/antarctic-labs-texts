The Evaluation of Combined Diffusion Kurtosis Imaging and Intravoxel Incoherent Motion Diffusion Weighted Imaging in Preoperatively Predicting Sentinel Lymph Node Metastasis of Breast Cancer
Authors: Xiaoxiao Li, Junfang Fang, Xuexue Zou, Xinnuan Mu, Tongli Cao, Xingyue Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the diagnostic performance of quantitative diffusion kurtosis imaging (DKI) and intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM) parameters for preoperative, noninvasive detection of sentinel lymph node metastasis (SLNM) in breast cancer.Methods and MaterialsClinicopathologic and MRI data from 61 patients with histologically confirmed breast cancer were reviewed, including 32 patients with SLNM and 29 without SLNM. All patients underwent multi-b-value diffusion-weighted imaging at 3.0 T. Images were analyzed using simplified DKI and biexponential IVIM models on the iCare SpinX workstation. Derived parameters included the diffusion coefficient D, kurtosis K, pseudodiffusion coefficient D*slow diffusion coefficient Dslow, and perfusion fraction f. Group differences were assessed using independent-samples t-tests and logistic regression. Diagnostic performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic analysis.ResultsCompared with the non-SLNM group, the SLNM group had a significantly higher f value (0.551 ± 0.108 vs 0.453 ± 0.115; P=0.001) and a significantly lower D value (0.0010 ± 0.0002 vs 0.0012 ± 0.0003 mm2/s; P<0.001). D*Dslow, and K did not differ significantly between groups. In univariate logistic regression, f (P=0.004) and D (P=0.003) were significant predictors of SLNM. D showed an area under the curve of 0.815, with 84.4% sensitivity and 75.9% specificity. The combined IVIM-DKI model achieved the best performance, with an area under the curve of 0.855, 75.0% sensitivity, and 89.7% specificity.ConclusionQuantitative DKI and IVIM parameters may help identify SLNM preoperatively in patients with breast cancer. A combined IVIM-DKI model provided better diagnostic performance than individual parameters and may serve as a useful noninvasive adjunct for preoperative axillary assessment.Keywords: sentinel lymph node metastasis, breast cancer, diffusion kurtosis imaging, intravoxel incoherent motion

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed malignancy among women worldwide and remains a major cause of cancer-related mortality.1–5 In China, approximately 357,200 new cases were diagnosed in women in 2022,6 while an estimated 316,950 new cases are expected in the United States in 2025.1 The World Health Organization has projected that the annual number of new breast cancer cases will reach approximately 3.2 million by 2050, accompanied by 1.1 million deaths, with a disproportionate increase expected in countries with a low Human Development Index.7 Given this substantial and growing disease burden, accurate preoperative assessment of tumor aggressiveness and metastatic risk is essential for individualized treatment planning and improved patient outcomes.

Sentinel lymph node (SLN) status is one of the most important prognostic factors in patients with early-stage breast cancer because the SLN is generally the first regional lymph node to receive metastatic tumor cells through lymphatic drainage.8–10 The presence of sentinel lymph node metastasis (SLNM) influences disease staging, surgical management, adjuvant treatment selection, and overall prognosis.11,12 Historically, axillary lymph node dissection (ALND) was routinely performed to assess nodal involvement; however, it is associated with substantial morbidity.13,14 Sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) has therefore become the standard surgical approach for evaluating axillary lymph node status, substantially reducing complications compared with ALND.15,16 Nevertheless, SLNB remains invasive and may cause lymphedema, arm pain, nerve injury, and restricted postoperative shoulder mobility. A reliable non-invasive imaging method for preoperative SLN assessment could therefore help optimize axillary management and potentially reduce unnecessary invasive procedures in selected patients.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a widely used non-invasive imaging modality that provides high spatial resolution, excellent soft-tissue contrast, no ionizing radiation, and multiparametric tissue characterization.17–22 MRI-based radiomics and machine-learning models have been investigated for predicting SLNM.23–25 However, their clinical translation remains challenging because of variations in MRI sequences, including T2-weighted imaging, dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI, and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), as well as differences in image segmentation, feature extraction, model construction, and validation workflows. Quantitative parameters derived from conventional MRI sequences may offer a simpler and more reproducible alternative. For example, Razzaghdoust et al and Yang et al reported that breast cancers with SLNM had lower apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values than those without SLNM.26,27 These findings suggest that restricted water diffusion within the primary tumor may reflect biological characteristics associated with lymphatic metastasis. Nevertheless, the diagnostic performance of ADC alone remains suboptimal, with reported areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve generally below 0.80. This limitation may be partly attributable to the mono-exponential DWI model, which assumes Gaussian water diffusion and does not adequately distinguish tissue cellularity from microvascular perfusion.

Diffusion kurtosis imaging (DKI) and intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM) imaging may address these limitations by characterizing complementary biological features of breast cancer. DKI quantifies the non-Gaussian behavior of water diffusion and is therefore more sensitive than conventional ADC to microstructural complexity, cellular heterogeneity, stromal organization, necrosis, and proliferative activity.28 IVIM uses a bi-exponential model to separate true molecular diffusion from perfusion-related pseudodiffusion, thereby providing information on both tumor cellularity and microvascular perfusion without requiring contrast administration.29,30

The combination of DKI and IVIM may be particularly relevant to SLNM prediction in breast cancer because lymphatic dissemination is unlikely to depend on a single tumor characteristic. Breast cancers capable of metastasizing to the SLN may exhibit increased cellular density, greater intratumoral heterogeneity, disrupted tissue architecture, and enhanced angiogenic or perfusion-related activity. DKI is primarily sensitive to the complexity and heterogeneity of the tumor microstructure, whereas IVIM provides complementary information regarding tissue diffusivity and microcirculatory perfusion. Consequently, combining DKI and IVIM parameters may provide a more comprehensive representation of the metastatic phenotype of the primary breast tumor than either technique alone. This complementary biological information may also explain why a combined model could outperform conventional ADC in identifying tumors with a higher likelihood of lymphatic spread.

Previous studies have demonstrated the potential value of DKI and IVIM for evaluating tumor aggressiveness and lymph node metastasis in other malignancies. For example, Li et al showed that DKI- and IVIM-derived parameters could assist in the preoperative assessment of lymph node metastasis in gastric malignancies.31 However, whether the complementary microstructural and perfusion information obtained from these techniques can improve the non-invasive prediction of SLNM in breast cancer remains unclear.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the feasibility of quantitative DKI and IVIM parameters for the non-invasive preoperative identification of SLNM in patients with breast cancer. We further investigated whether a combined DKI–IVIM model could provide better predictive performance than either imaging model alone.

Materials and Methods

Study Design, Patient Recruitment, and Eligibility Criteria

This prospective, single-center study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Binzhou Medical University Hospital, China (Approval No. 2023KT-63). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment.

Consecutive patients with suspected or histopathologically confirmed primary breast carcinoma who were scheduled to undergo preoperative breast MRI and subsequent surgical assessment of the sentinel lymph nodes were prospectively recruited between September 2024 and April 2025. Eligibility was assessed before MRI examination according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. All consecutive eligible patients who provided informed consent were enrolled. The patient recruitment process and reasons for exclusion are presented in Figure 1. The final cohort size and the numbers of patients with and without sentinel lymph node metastasis are reported in the Results section.

Sample-Size Calculation

An a priori sample-size calculation was performed for the primary objective of evaluating the ability of quantitative diffusion MRI parameters to discriminate between patients with and without sentinel lymph node metastasis. The calculation was based on the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Assuming an anticipated AUC of 0.71, a null AUC of 0.50, a two-sided significance level of 0.05, a statistical power of 80%, and an expected SLNM-to-non-SLNM allocation ratio of approximately 1:1, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be approximately 55 evaluable patients. To account for potentially non-evaluable MRI examinations, incomplete pathological data, and participant withdrawal, this number was increased by approximately 10%, resulting in a target enrollment of 61 patients. Ultimately, 61 patients with complete MRI and histopathological data were included in the final analysis, comprising 32 patients with SLNM and 29 patients without SLNM. Thus, the final cohort met the prespecified target sample size. To reduce the risk of overfitting in the relatively modest cohort, the combined predictive model was restricted to two imaging predictors, namely one DKI-derived parameter and one IVIM-derived parameter. With 32 SLNM-positive cases, the dataset provided 16 outcome events per predictor for the two-predictor model. Penalized logistic regression was used, and model performance was internally validated using bootstrap resampling. A random training-test split was not performed because it would have substantially reduced the effective sample size and resulted in less precise and potentially unstable estimates of model performance.

MRI Acquisition

All MRI examinations were performed using a 3.0-T MRI system (Discovery MR750w; GE Medical Systems, Milwaukee, WI, USA) equipped with a dedicated eight-channel bilateral breast coil. Patients were examined in the prone position, with both breasts naturally suspended within the coil, and entered the magnet feet first. Patients were instructed to breathe normally and remain still throughout image acquisition.

Diffusion-Model Fitting and Parameter Definitions

The multi-b-value DWI data were transferred to the iCare SpinX workstation (iCare Medical Instrument, Beijing, China). Quantitative maps were generated on a pixel-by-pixel basis using the workstation’s built-in diffusion-model fitting functions. Signal intensity at each b-value was normalized to the signal at b = 0 s/mm2. The same acquired dataset was used to calculate the conventional ADC, DKI, and IVIM parameters.32 Representative source images and derived quantitative maps are illustrated in Figures 2C–E and 3C–E.

ROI Delineation and Reproducibility Assessment

Two radiologists experienced in breast MRI independently performed all measurements while blinded to SLN status, histopathological findings, and each other’s measurements. The primary tumor was localized by jointly reviewing the b = 1000 s/mm2 DWI, T2-weighted images, and contrast-enhanced T1-weighted images. If there was uncertainty regarding the index lesion or the slice selected for measurement, a senior radiologist adjudicated lesion localization before both readers independently repeated ROI placement.

For each patient, the slice containing the largest solid cross-sectional area of the tumor was selected on the b = 1000 s/mm2 image. Each reader placed three nonoverlapping circular or elliptical ROIs, each measuring 30–100 mm2, within the solid tumor component. ROIs were kept within the visible tumor margin and away from necrosis, cystic change, hemorrhage, biopsy-related change, large vessels, and obvious artifacts. In heterogeneous tumors, the three ROIs were distributed across different solid-enhancing regions to sample intratumoral heterogeneity.

The ROI coordinates were propagated to the co-registered ADC, DKI, and IVIM maps so that all parameters were sampled from identical spatial locations. For each reader, the mean of the three ROI measurements was calculated for every parameter. Inter-reader agreement was evaluated across the complete cohort using a two-way random-effects, absolute-agreement intraclass correlation coefficient for the average of two readers, ICC (2,2), with 95% confidence intervals. The average of the two readers’ values was used in the final statistical analysis. Consensus discussion was used only to resolve lesion-localization or technical-placement problems and did not replace the independent measurements used for the reproducibility analysis.

Clinical and Pathological Data

Clinical and pathological variables were extracted from the electronic medical record and pathology database. Recorded variables included age, maximum tumor diameter, histological type, histological grade, molecular subtype, and SLN status. The primary imaging predictors were ADC, Dk, K, Dslow, Dfast, and fslow.

Surgical SLNB and Histopathological Reference Standard

The reference standard for SLN status was standard surgical sentinel lymph node biopsy performed after MRI. Sentinel nodes were surgically mapped and excised according to the institution’s routine operative protocol. Ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration or core-needle biopsy, if performed during the diagnostic workup, was not accepted as the definitive SLN reference standard.

Excised sentinel nodes were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm, and examined using hematoxylin and eosin staining. Representative H&E histopathological images corresponding to the cases illustrated in Figures 2 and 3 are shown in Figures 2H and 3H, respectively. Pancytokeratin immunohistochemistry was used when routine staining was negative or equivocal and additional evaluation was considered necessary. Primary tumor specimens were examined to determine histological type, grade, and biomarker status.

Nodal tumor burden was classified by the largest metastatic deposit as follows: pN0, no tumor cells; pN0(i+), isolated tumor cells no larger than 0.2 mm or no more than 200 cells in a single histological cross-section; pN1mi, micrometastasis larger than 0.2 mm but no larger than 2.0 mm; and macrometastasis, a deposit larger than 2.0 mm. For the primary binary outcome, micrometastasis and macrometastasis were classified as SLNM positive, whereas pN0 and pN0(i+) were classified as SLNM negative.

Two pathologists independently evaluated the SLN specimens while blinded to the quantitative MRI measurements. Disagreements were resolved by joint review with a third senior pathologist, and the consensus diagnosis served as the reference standard.

Missing-Data Handling

A complete-case strategy was used for the primary imaging analysis because availability of DKI, IVIM, and definitive surgical SLN pathology was required for inclusion. Patients missing any primary imaging parameter or the pathological outcome were excluded before analysis and were documented in the patient-flow diagram. No imputation was performed for the primary imaging predictors or SLN outcome. The extent of missingness in supplementary clinical variables was summarized; a clinical variable with missing data was omitted only from analyses requiring that variable and was not used in the combined imaging model.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in R using RStudio version 2024.12.1+563. The pROC, logistf, irr, boot, rms, and ggplot2 packages were used for ROC analysis, Firth logistic regression, reproducibility analysis, bootstrap validation, model-performance assessment, and figure generation, respectively. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Continuous variables were assessed using histograms, quantile-quantile plots, and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using the independent-samples t test. Non-normally distributed variables are presented as median and interquartile range and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages and were compared using the Pearson chi-square test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate.

Inter-reader agreement for ADC, Dk, K, Dslow, Dfast, and fslow was evaluated using ICC (2,2) with 95% confidence intervals. Agreement was interpreted as poor (<0.50), moderate (0.50–0.74), good (0.75–0.89), or excellent (≥0.90).

Receiver operating characteristic curves were generated for each quantitative parameter. AUCs were reported with 95% confidence intervals. The optimal threshold for each individual parameter was identified using the maximum Youden index, and the corresponding sensitivity and specificity were calculated. Pairwise AUC comparisons were performed using DeLong’s test for correlated ROC curves.

To address the limited sample size and reduce model instability, conventional stepwise multivariable logistic regression was not used. The combined DKI-IVIM model was restricted to two predictors: the DKI parameter and the IVIM parameter with the strongest univariable discrimination, provided that they represented different model families and had an absolute Spearman correlation below 0.70. The complete predictor-selection step was repeated within each resampling iteration to account for selection-related optimism.

The two-predictor combined model was fitted using Firth’s penalized-likelihood logistic regression, which reduces small-sample bias and handles possible separation. Penalized odds ratios and profile-likelihood 95% confidence intervals were reported. Internal validation was performed using 1000 bootstrap resamples. Apparent and optimism-corrected AUC, calibration slope, calibration intercept, and Brier score were calculated. Because of the modest cohort size, the dataset was not divided into separate training and test subsets.

The combined model was compared with its individual DKI and IVIM components using DeLong’s test. These model comparisons were considered exploratory and were interpreted together with confidence intervals and bootstrap-corrected performance rather than on P values alone.

Logistic Regression Analysis of IVIM and DKI Parameters

In univariable analysis, (D) and (f) were associated with SLNM; however, only (D) remained statistically significant in the multivariable model. This finding may reflect correlations among diffusion parameters, limited statistical power, or instability caused by fitting multiple predictors in a relatively small dataset. Consequently, the multivariable results should not be interpreted as definitive evidence that the combined model provides independent incremental value. Rather, they identify (D) as the most consistent imaging marker in the current cohort and suggest that the additional contribution of (f) and other diffusion parameters requires confirmation in larger datasets. To identify independent imaging biomarkers associated with SLNM, both univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed using DKI and IVIM parameters. The results are presented in Table 3. In the univariate analysis, the D (p = 0.003) and f (p = 0.004) were significantly associated with SLNM. Other parameters, including D* (p = 0.253), K (p = 0.466) and Dslow (p = 0.527), however, did not reveal significant associations (Table 3, Figure 5). In a further applied multivariate analysis, only D remained statistically significant in the multivariate model (p = 0.038) (Table 3, Figure 5).

ROC Curve Analysis

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to assess the diagnostic performance of key diffusion parameters derived from IVIM and DKI models in differentiating SLNM from non-SLNM lesions. Among the individual quantitative parameters, (D) demonstrated the highest discriminatory performance, with an AUC of 0.815. The DKI and IVIM models achieved AUCs of 0.814 and 0.744, respectively, whereas the combined DKI–IVIM model achieved the numerically highest AUC of 0.834. Thus, the absolute increase in AUC provided by the combined model was 0.019 compared with (D) alone and 0.020 compared with the DKI model. Although these findings suggest a possible incremental contribution from combining the two diffusion models, the magnitude of improvement was modest. Therefore, the combined model should not be interpreted as statistically or clinically superior unless supported by formal comparisons of the ROC curves, confidence intervals, calibration assessment, and independent validation. Based on the available results, (D) achieved the highest accuracy and sensitivity among the individual parameters, whereas the combined DKI–IVIM model achieved the highest specificity. The combined model should therefore be described as demonstrating a potentially complementary diagnostic profile rather than uniformly outperforming all individual parameters across every performance metric (Table 4, Figure 6).

Correlation Between Quantitative Imaging Parameters and Clinical/Pathological Indicators

The correlation analysis between quantitative imaging parameters and clinical/pathological indicators revealed interesting relationships that may provide insights into the potential interplay between imaging metrics and patient characteristics. The heatmap visualization demonstrated a range of correlation coefficients, with some significant associations observed between specific variables. Notably, the molecular subtype, encoded through one-hot encoding, showed varying degrees of correlation with the quantitative imaging parameters such as Dslow, Dslow and K. For instance, a moderate positive correlation was observed between the molecular subtype and K, indicating that variations in kurtosis may be influenced by the subtype of the cancer. Additionally, HER2 status showed a moderate positive correlation with f, suggesting that the expression of the HER2 protein may be linked to the characteristics of tissue perfusion as measured by the IVIM model. Other clinical indicators such as ER and PR were weakly correlated with certain imaging parameters like Ki-67 and D, implying that receptor status might have a subtle influence on the tissue diffusion properties captured by imaging. These findings suggest that the integration of quantitative imaging parameters with clinical and pathological indicators, such as receptor status and molecular subtype, could enhance diagnostic and prognostic modelling. However, further studies are needed to confirm these associations and explore their clinical significance in detail (Figure 7).

Advanced diffusion imaging has previously been explored for breast cancer characterization and nodal prediction; therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as an incremental extension of the existing evidence rather than as the first demonstration of diffusion MRI for this purpose. The potential contribution of the combined DKI–IVIM approach is that DKI and IVIM characterize partly different aspects of tumor biology. DKI reflects non-Gaussian diffusion and tissue microstructural complexity, whereas IVIM provides estimates related to true molecular diffusion and microvascular pseudo-diffusion. Their combination may therefore capture complementary features associated with lymphatic metastatic potential.

Nevertheless, the evidence for incremental improvement was limited. The combined model produced an AUC of 0.834, compared with 0.815 for (D) alone and 0.814 for the DKI model, representing an absolute improvement of approximately 0.02. This small increase indicates numerical rather than definitive superiority. Furthermore, only (D) remained statistically significant in the multivariable analysis, suggesting that the additional IVIM and DKI parameters did not demonstrate stable independent contributions after adjustment. The results therefore support the biological plausibility of a combined approach but do not establish that it provides a reliable or clinically meaningful improvement over the strongest individual diffusion parameter.

Discussion

The present study investigated the utility of advanced diffusion parameters derived from IVIM and DKI models in distinguishing SLNM in breast cancer preoperatively. The present study showed that (f) was significantly higher, whereas the DKI-derived diffusion coefficient (D) was significantly lower, in the SLNM group than in the non-SLNM group. Both parameters were associated with SLNM in univariable logistic regression; however, only (D) remained statistically significant in the multivariable model (Tables 2 and 3). Our ROC analysis revealed that D had the strongest diagnostic performance among the five individual parameters (D*Dslow, f, K, D). The DKI model demonstrated better overall discrimination than the IVIM model. The combined DKI–IVIM model yielded the numerically highest AUC of approximately 0.820, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.714–0.926. However, (D) alone had the highest accuracy and sensitivity, whereas the combined model had the highest specificity. Therefore, the combined model did not uniformly outperform all individual parameters or single-model approaches across every diagnostic metric. The numerically higher AUC of the combined model suggests that diffusion- and perfusion-related parameters may provide complementary information.

Among the five individual diffusion parameters, (f) and (D) showed the clearest differences between the SLNM and non-SLNM groups. Specifically, the f was significantly higher in SLNM-positive tumors compared to non-SLNM tumors (Table 2). This is consistent with previous findings that higher perfusion levels in tumors are often associated with increased metabolic activity and more aggressive behavior, both of which are characteristic of metastatic disease.33 D value is known to correlate with tissue cellularity and the degree of diffusion restriction, which are key features of malignancy. In line with this, the lower D values observed in SLNM-positive tumors suggest that these tumors exhibit greater cellular density and reduced extracellular space, both of which are common indicators of metastatic cancer. Other IVIM and DKI parameters, such as D*Dslow, and K, did not exhibit significant differences between the SLNM and non-SLNM groups (Table 2). These findings suggest that while these parameters provide valuable information about the tumor microenvironment, they may not be as robust in distinguishing between SLNM-positive and non-SLNM tumors in this context.

Univariable logistic regression showed significant associations of both (f) and (D) with SLNM status. These findings indicate potential discriminatory value in the present cohort but do not, by themselves, establish strong or clinically reliable predictive performance. However, only (D) remained statistically significant in the multivariable model. This result may reflect the stronger independent association of (D) with SLNM, correlations among the diffusion parameters, limited statistical power, or model instability arising from the modest sample size and number of candidate predictors. An interaction effect should not be claimed unless an interaction term was explicitly tested. Other parameters, including D*Dslow, and K, did not demonstrate significant predictive value in either logistic regression model. The wide confidence intervals indicate substantial uncertainty in the estimated associations and are likely related to the small cohort and limited number of outcome events. Consequently, the magnitude and stability of the associations should be interpreted cautiously. These results demonstrate that diffusion restriction and perfusion-related changes may both be relevant to SLNM. However, because only (D) remained independently significant, the current data do not establish that a combined model is necessary or superior to a simpler model based on (D) alone. These observations are aligned with the findings in the study by Kataoka et al, (2024), which demonstrated that higher kurtosis values in diffusion MRI are associated with tumour aggressiveness and poor differentiation.34

In the ROC analysis, when evaluating the diagnostic performance of these parameters, D and f outperformed other diffusion measures, with D achieving the highest AUC of 0.815 and f closely following with an AUC of 0.741 (Table 4). Although (D) and (f) demonstrated potentially useful discrimination in the present cohort, their clinical reliability cannot be established from a small, single-center dataset without external validation. These parameters should therefore be regarded as candidate imaging biomarkers rather than clinically validated diagnostic tests. This aligns with previous studies that have demonstrated the utility of diffusion imaging in evaluating tumor microstructure and predicting metastatic involvement. Qin et al reported that multimodel DWI analysis may improve the assessment of breast cancer molecular and prognostic characteristics.35 In the present study, the combined DKI–IVIM model achieved a numerically higher AUC than the individual models; however, the modest difference and lack of external validation preclude a definitive conclusion that the combined model improves diagnostic accuracy. Our findings are also in agreement with recent studies investigating the utility of advanced diffusion imaging techniques in assessing breast cancer metastasis. Zhang et al (2022) reported that IVIM-derived perfusion-related parameters, particularly f, were elevated in metastatic tumors,33 supporting our observation that increased f may be associated with SLNM. A recent study conducted by Chang et al (2024) also showed similar results. For instance, the individual parameter ADC achieved the highest AUC value and the combined models (eg, fR, DH and fUI) also reached the highest AUC.36 Overall, the findings demonstrate that (D), and to a lesser extent (f), may provide non-invasive information associated with SLNM in breast cancer. Nevertheless, these findings remain exploratory and require confirmation in larger and independently validated cohorts. The ability to predict lymph node metastasis accurately is crucial for staging, treatment planning, and prognosis, and these advanced imaging techniques could provide valuable adjunctive information for clinicians.

Previous studies have evaluated both individual diffusion parameters and multiparametric MRI approaches for predicting nodal involvement in breast cancer.37,38 For instance, Song et al, (2024) reported that IVIM model (D + f) would be a valuable functional imaging technique to predict lymph node metastasis.37 Guo et al (2022) has also emphasized the importance of combining IVIM and DKI parameters with radiomics-based approaches or artificial intelligence (AI) models to enhance predictive accuracy.38 In contrast, our study also demonstrated similar findings. Our combined model of DKI + IVIM outperformed individual models in terms of AUC, reflecting its potential for enhanced diagnostic accuracy, with an ACC, sensitivity, and specificity. These results emphasize that while D alone provides excellent diagnostic capability, the integration of both DKI and IVIM parameters would offer a more robust approach for SLNM detection, combining high sensitivity and moderate specificity for broader clinical applicability.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the small sample size in the SLNM group led to substantial variability in ORs and CIs, reducing the accuracy of our findings. Second, histopathological validation of IVIM-DKI findings remains a challenge, as lymph node heterogeneity and tumor microenvironment complexity are not fully captured by current imaging biomarkers. Future research should prioritize larger, multi-institutional patient groups to confirm these findings and refine the predictive models. Additionally, machine learning algorithms could be explored to enhance IVIM-DKI parameter selection and improve classification performance. Finally, combining IVIM-DKI imaging with other functional MRI techniques, such as radiomics-based texture analysis or artificial intelligence-driven feature extraction, may further improve metastatic detection accuracy.

Conclusion

In this small-scale prospective cohort, quantitative parameters derived from DKI and IVIM showed potential for the preoperative assessment of sentinel lymph node metastasis in patients with breast cancer. Among the individual parameters, the DKI-derived diffusion coefficient (D) demonstrated the most consistent diagnostic performance. The combined DKI–IVIM model showed preliminary diagnostic promise and may provide complementary information regarding tumor microstructure and perfusion. However, the observed incremental benefit was modest, and the model was not externally validated. Therefore, these findings should be considered exploratory and require confirmation in larger, prospective, multicenter studies before the combined DKI–IVIM approach can be recommended for routine clinical use.
