Patient-centred and fit-for-purpose clinical trials in rare cancers: lessons from sarcomas for operationalising 2024 WHO Guidance and ICH E6(R3)
Authors: Ornella Gonzato, Denise Reinke, Roger Wilson, Bernd Kasper, Maria Josefina Ruiz Alvarez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryRare cancers account for approximately one quarter of cancer diagnoses in Europe, yet prevailing clinical trial governance frameworks were developed for common malignancies and conventional randomised designs. The 2024 WHO Guidance for best practices for clinical trials and ICH E6(R3) reframe trial quality around scientific validity, ethical proportionality, feasibility, equity, and meaningful patient engagement — principles that are structurally tested by rare cancer research. This narrative review examines how these principles are operationalised in representative sarcoma trials, where biological heterogeneity, low incidence, and international collaboration expose the limits of standard approaches. Drawing on examples from Bayesian designs, umbrella and platform trials, registry-based approaches, decentralised participation, and structured advocacy involvement, we identify recurring governance features associated with successful implementation in rare cancer settings. Patient-centred approaches — particularly early integration of patient-reported outcomes (PROs), risk-proportional oversight, and attention to participant burden — emerge as key determinants of feasibility, recruitment, retention, and interpretability. In rare cancers, patient-centred, fit-for-purpose governance is not an ethical adjunct but a structural determinant of operational validity. Search strategy and selection criteria

This review provides a structured narrative synthesis of published literature and policy documents addressing clinical trial governance and methodological approaches in rare cancers, informed by the 2024 WHO Guidance for best practices for clinical trials and the ICH E6(R3) Guideline for Good Clinical Practice. Relevant sources published between Jan 1, 2010, and Dec 31, 2025, were identified through targeted searches of PubMed and through direct review of regulatory and institutional publication repositories, including the European Medicines Agency, the US Food and Drug Administration, the World Health Organisation, and the International Council for Harmonisation. Search terms included: “rare cancer”, “rare tumour”, “sarcoma”, “adaptive trial design”, “Bayesian design”, “platform trial”, “umbrella trial”, “basket trial”, “registry-based trial”, “external control arm”, “real-world data”, “patient engagement”, “patient-reported outcomes”, “clinical trial governance”, “ICH E6(R3)", and “WHO clinical trial guidance”. Additional sources were identified through citation tracking and review of reference lists.

Selection criteria focused on relevance to clinical trial design, governance, and implementation in rare cancers, with emphasis on scientific validity, feasibility, proportionality, equity, and patient-centred approaches. Eligible sources included peer-reviewed publications, consensus statements, methodological studies, and regulatory or policy documents. Only articles published in English were considered.

Introduction

Rare cancers account for approximately one quarter of all cancer diagnoses in Europe, yet individually occur at low incidence, often below five cases per 100,000 persons per year.1, 2, 3 Patients face delayed diagnosis, geographic dispersion, limited access to specialised centres, and fragmented regulatory pathways.4,5 These structural constraints challenge the feasibility and interpretability of conventional large randomised controlled trials. Sarcomas exemplify these tensions. Comprising more than 100 histological and molecular subtypes — many classified as ultra-rare — sarcomas often render traditional fixed-sample randomisation infeasible.6, 7, 8, 9 At the same time, unmet clinical need remains substantial. Sarcomas therefore provide a stress test for contemporary clinical trial governance.

The 2024 WHO Guidance for best practices for clinical trials articulates principles of scientific validity, efficiency, ethical acceptability, social value, feasibility, equity, and patient engagement as interdependent components of trial quality.10 Complementing this, ICH E6 (R3) reframes Good Clinical Practice through quality by design and risk-proportionate oversight, emphasising critical-to-quality factors rather than procedural compliance.11 Together, these frameworks shift trial governance towards principled flexibility. This review examines how such principles are operationalised in rare cancer trials and argues that patient-centred governance functions as a structural determinant of operational validity under conditions of rarity.

Structural challenges in rare cancer trials

Small populations and proportionality

Rare cancers involve intrinsically small and biologically heterogeneous populations.1,8 In sarcomas, fragmentation across numerous subtypes further constrains accrual. Conventional large fixed-sample trials may impose disproportionate burdens with limited probability of generating actionable knowledge.12, 13, 14 The 2024 WHO guidance explicitly links ethical acceptability to proportionality between risk, burden, and anticipated social value.10 Adaptive and Bayesian designs can improve efficiency while maintaining methodological rigour.15,16

Structural inequity

Centralisation of expertise improves diagnostic accuracy and clinical outcomes in rare cancers but may increase barriers to research participation. Travel burden, financial costs, and logistical complexity disproportionately affect patients in rural regions, lower socioeconomic groups, and older populations, exacerbating inequities.17, 18, 19, 20 Structural inequities may also arise from differences in regulatory and ethics review processes across jurisdictions. In multinational rare cancer studies, variation in approval timelines, submission requirements, and local governance procedures can delay site activation and create unequal opportunities for participation across countries. Such asymmetries may disproportionately affect academic and investigator-initiated studies, limiting access to research for some patient populations despite the availability of international collaborative networks. The 2024 WHO guidance emphasises inclusivity and equitable access to research participation.10

Balancing burden reduction and equity

Addressing barriers to participation requires approaches that reduce unnecessary burden while preserving inclusiveness and trial quality.21 Patient-centred strategies and proportionate implementation of innovative trial approaches may facilitate participation in rare cancer research. However, these approaches require appropriate infrastructure, support systems, and explicit monitoring of equity to ensure that efforts to improve accessibility do not unintentionally exclude under-resourced populations. Among the approaches proposed to reduce participation burden while preserving trial quality and inclusiveness, decentralised and hybrid trial models have gained increasing attention, particularly in rare cancer settings.

Decentralised and hybrid trial approaches

Decentralised clinical trials (DCTs) and hybrid trial approaches represent an important component of the patient-centred and quality-by-design principles emphasised in ICH E6(R3). Their relevance is particularly evident in rare cancers, where patients are geographically dispersed and access to specialist centres may be limited.

DCT models incorporate elements such as remote informed consent, telemedicine visits, electronic patient-reported outcomes, local clinical assessments, remote monitoring, and digital data capture.22 In rare sarcoma populations, these approaches may facilitate recruitment, improve retention, and support broader participation by reducing unnecessary travel and logistical barriers.

However, decentralisation should not be considered a replacement for specialist centres, but rather an extension of their capacity through coordinated hybrid models. Effective implementation requires appropriate infrastructure, regulatory alignment, data protection safeguards, and attention to digital inclusion to avoid introducing new forms of inequity.

Early experiences from registry-based and longitudinal rare cancer initiatives, including ICONIC, illustrate the potential value of remote patient-reported data collection and digitally enabled follow-up in geographically dispersed populations (Table 1). These approaches support feasibility, equity, and meaningful patient participation while aligning with the ICH E6(R3) emphasis on critical-to-quality factors and risk-proportionate trial conduct.

Methodological innovation and research infrastructure

Expanding design strategies

Rare cancer research has expanded beyond traditional fixed trial designs through the adoption of adaptive methodologies, Bayesian approaches where appropriate, and innovative frameworks such as umbrella, basket, and platform trials. These approaches enable more efficient evidence generation across heterogeneous populations while maintaining methodological rigour. Consensus recommendations increasingly support flexible approaches tailored to rarity.31

Representative examples are summarised in Table 1, which illustrates how individual sarcoma trials operationalise WHO (2024) and ICH E6(R3) governance principles through different methodological approaches. The studies discussed below exemplify how these methodological innovations have been implemented in practice and the governance lessons emerging from their experience. The biomarker-driven MULTISARC umbrella trial used centralised molecular profiling to allocate patients across treatment cohorts, demonstrating how molecular stratification can enhance feasibility and interpretability despite highly fragmented populations.23 Similarly, IMMUNOSARC employed a multicohort design including multiple rare subtypes, illustrating how flexible and risk-proportionate approaches may support evidence generation when conventional randomised designs are impractical.24

Prospective registries and real-world data (RWD) can support external control arms and contextualise findings when randomisation is infeasible.32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37 Registry-based initiatives such as RESAR and ICONIC demonstrate how longitudinal real-world data collection can complement interventional studies, support future research platforms, and provide patient-centred contextual evidence in settings where conventional trial recruitment is challenging.27,30

Integration of patient-reported outcomes (PROs) ensures that domains meaningful to patients — including functional status, long-term toxicity, and quality of life — are systematically captured.38, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43 Studies such as SarcoSIGHT demonstrate the feasibility of incorporating functional and quality-of-life endpoints into trial design, while programmes such as ICONIC show how patient-reported data can help align evidence generation with patient priorities across different age groups.29,30

Taken together, these examples suggest that methodological innovation in rare cancers extends beyond statistical efficiency. Adaptive designs, registry-based infrastructures, and patient-centred outcome frameworks each contribute to operational validity by improving the feasibility, relevance, and interpretability of evidence generated in small populations.

Collaborative networks

Cross-border collaborative networks, including European Research Networks — such as EURACAN — and international sarcoma consortia play a critical role in enabling accrual, harmonising standards, and ensuring data quality.17,20,44, 45, 46, 47 In populations fragmented across numerous histological and molecular subtypes, such infrastructures are often a prerequisite for adequately powered studies rather than merely a facilitator of research activity. As summarised in Table 1, multinational sarcoma trials illustrate how collaborative infrastructures operationalise WHO (2024) and ICH E6(R3) governance principles by supporting feasibility, harmonisation, scientific validity, and equitable access to research participation. Trials such as RAIN-3201 and rEECur relied on multinational collaboration to recruit geographically dispersed patients while maintaining harmonised governance, data collection, and oversight procedures.25,26 These experiences demonstrate how collaborative infrastructures support WHO (2024) principles of feasibility, equity, and scientific validity, as well as the ICH E6(R3) emphasis on quality-by-design through coordinated research systems.

Lessons emerging across these studies suggest that methodological innovation alone is insufficient. Adaptive, platform, registry-based, and biomarker-driven designs depend on supporting infrastructures for central pathology review, molecular profiling, data management, regulatory coordination, and cross-border recruitment. In this sense, infrastructure becomes a determinant of operational validity, influencing whether theoretically robust trial designs can generate interpretable evidence in practice.

Sustaining these networks requires long-term investment in data-management capacity, interoperable digital platforms, regulatory support, centralised diagnostic resources, and cross-border coordination. Funding mechanisms must support not only individual studies but also the broader research ecosystem that enables them. Without stable infrastructure, innovative methodologies may remain inaccessible to academic and investigator-initiated studies, limiting both feasibility and equitable participation.

Taken together, experiences from sarcoma research suggest that collaborative infrastructure should be regarded as a core component of trial governance rather than a background operational resource. Across multiple studies, successful evidence generation has depended not only on innovative methodology but also on sustained systems that support recruitment, harmonisation, patient access, and data quality. This lesson may have increasing relevance beyond rare cancers as molecular stratification creates progressively smaller and more dispersed populations within common malignancies.

Operational validity under constraint

Scientific validity traditionally emphasises internal and external validity. In rare cancers, however, feasibility becomes inseparable from interpretability. Trials that fail to recruit adequately or incure substantial attrition may remain methodologically sound yet yield inconclusive results. Feasibility, proportionality, and patient engagement therefore become structural conditions for generating interpretable evidence — what may be termed operational validity. Operational validity is distinct from internal and external validity, referring to the ability of a trial to generate interpretable evidence under real-world constraints of population size, feasibility, and participation. This framing does not dilute evidentiary standards but clarifies that validity depends on alignment between study design and population reality.

The representative studies summarised in Table 1 illustrate this concept across complementary governance domains, demonstrating how methodological innovation, collaborative infrastructures, patient-centred approaches, and registry-based research each contribute to operational validity in rare cancer trials. MULTISARC and IMMUNOSARC demonstrate how molecular stratification and flexible cohort-based designs can improve feasibility in fragmented populations. RAIN-3201 and rEECur show how international collaboration and contextual governance structures can preserve scientific validity while maximising accrual. RESAR and ICONIC illustrate the contribution of registry-based infrastructures and patient-centred data collection to contextualising outcomes and supporting future studies. Across these diverse approaches, a common feature is the deliberate alignment of trial design with the realities of rarity, supporting the generation of interpretable evidence despite population constraints.

Designing for relevance

In sarcomas, endpoints such as functional preservation and long-term toxicity may be more meaningful to patients than short-term tumour response.48, 49, 50, 51, 52 While patient-centred endpoints are important across many disease areas, their relevance may be particularly pronounced in rare cancers where recruitment is often constrained by small populations and geographic dispersion. Pragmatic and adaptive designs that incorporate outcomes aligned with patient priorities may improve willingness to participate, support retention, and reduce missing data.53, 54, 55 In this context, endpoint selection contributes not only to clinical relevance but also to operational validity by facilitating the generation of interpretable evidence within limited populations. Examples such as SarcoSIGHT illustrate the feasibility of integrating functional and quality-of-life outcomes early in trial design while maintaining scientific rigour.29

Patient engagement as governance

Structured involvement of patient advocacy organisations — such as Sarcoma Patient Advocacy Global Network (SPAGN) — in protocol development, consent processes, and dissemination is increasingly embedded in sarcoma research.56, 57, 58, 59 In very small populations, such engagement enhances feasibility and interpretability.

Paediatric and orphan regulatory intersections

Sarcomas affect both paediatric and adult populations, introducing additional ethical and regulatory complexity. Paediatric trial requirements and orphan designation criteria intersect in ways that vary across jurisdictions.60,61 These regulatory requirements, combined with the already fragmented landscape of rare cancer trial approvals, create disproportionate administrative burdens for research teams and sponsors. International trials such as rEECur have demonstrated that these challenges can be addressed through structured cross-border collaboration, but the governance infrastructure required to do so consistently and equitably remains underdeveloped.62, 63, 64

Digital and artificial intelligence (AI)–enabled governance

Digital and artificial intelligence (AI)–enabled tools offer substantial opportunities in rare cancer trials, where small populations and geographic dispersion create persistent recruitment and data collection challenges.65, 66, 67 Applications include AI-assisted patient identification from electronic health records, wearable-based remote monitoring, and digital twins as synthetic control arm alternatives — all particularly relevant where randomisation to control is statistically or ethically constrained in ultra-rare populations.

The WHO Global Action Plan for Clinical Trial Services (GAP-CTS, 2025) addresses this directly through Action 4, calling for the adoption of innovative designs and digital technologies to improve efficiency across recruitment, data collection, analysis, and oversight, while explicitly cross-referencing Action 5 on training to ensure safe and equitable implementation.68 The Global Clinical Trials Forum (GCTF), WHO's multistakeholder network mandated to operationalise the GAP-CTS, provides the institutional architecture for progressive alignment of digital governance standards across jurisdictions and research settings.69

However, governance risks require explicit attention. Algorithmic bias may reproduce geographic and socioeconomic inequities in participant selection. Unequal digital access can exclude under-resourced populations from decentralised trial components. Opacity in AI-assisted decision-making — whether in eligibility determination or safety monitoring — demands audit trails and human oversight embedded by design.67 ICH E6(R3)'s quality-by-design principles and the GAP-CTS transparency requirements apply with equal force to algorithmic tools as to conventional trial processes. Addressing these risks is a governance obligation, not a technical afterthought.

Cross-cutting lessons from sarcoma trials

Viewed together, the representative studies summarised in Table 1 reveal recurring governance features that extend beyond the individual trial designs and inform broader lessons for rare cancer research.

First, methodological flexibility appears most successful when linked to a clear biological or clinical rationale. Adaptive, umbrella, and multicohort designs improve efficiency not simply because they are innovative, but because they better reflect the fragmented structure of rare cancer populations.

Second, feasibility and scientific validity are closely interconnected. Experiences from trials such as rEECur, RAIN-3201, and MULTISARC suggest that recruitment, retention, and participant burden directly influence the ability of studies to generate interpretable evidence.

Third, international collaborative infrastructures consistently emerge as enabling conditions for successful rare cancer research. Networks supporting patient identification, central pathology review, data harmonisation, and cross-border recruitment are often as important as the methodological design itself.

Fourth, patient-centred approaches contribute not only to ethical acceptability but also to operational performance. Studies incorporating patient-reported outcomes, functional endpoints, and structured patient involvement demonstrate how relevance to patient priorities may support recruitment, retention, and data completeness.

Finally, despite substantial innovation in design and infrastructure, regulatory fragmentation remains a persistent barrier. Differences in ethics review procedures, administrative requirements, and national implementation processes continue to affect trial activation and equitable access across jurisdictions.

Taken together, these observations suggest that operational validity in rare cancers depends on the interaction of methodological innovation, collaborative infrastructure, patient-centred governance, and proportional regulation rather than on any single design feature alone.

Persistent gaps

Ethics review timelines represent a concrete dimension of fragmentation. ECRIN's Regulatory and Ethical Database (RED) — an online database covering country-specific regulatory and ethical requirements across Europe — was specifically developed to address the complexity that investigators face when planning multinational clinical studies, providing structured, comparable information across more than 750 searchable fields.70,71 Rather than looking into countries one at a time, RED allows researchers to compare two countries directly, ensuring precise, up-to-date regulatory and ethical information to guide researchers through their submission process. The variation documented by RED across Member States includes differences in the number and composition of required ethics committees, timelines for initial and amended submissions, document requirements, and national competent authority procedures that sit alongside — and are not fully replaced by — the CTIS harmonisation process. For multinational sarcoma trials requiring simultaneous activation in multiple countries, these cumulative asymmetries translate directly into delayed site initiation, unequal accrual rates, and administrative burdens that fall most heavily on academic and investigator-initiated sponsors without dedicated regulatory affairs infrastructure.

Taken together, these examples illustrate that regulatory fragmentation is not merely a bureaucratic inconvenience but a structural determinant of feasibility in rare cancer research — one that governance frameworks, including the WHO GAP-CTS Action 6 on streamlining regulatory and ethics review, must address with greater specificity if the vision of equitable, multinational rare cancer trials is to be realised in practice.

Limitations

This review is a structured narrative synthesis rather than a systematic review or quantitative meta-analysis. Examples presented are illustrative, intended to demonstrate operationalisation of governance principles and are not exhaustive of sarcoma research. Much of the evidence described derives from high-income research settings, which may limit generalisability to under-resourced contexts. Although conceptual and emerging empirical evidence suggests that patient-centred governance improves feasibility and interpretability, direct causal evidence linking specific engagement practices to trial outcomes remains limited. Finally, the governance implications of digital and artificial intelligence–enabled tools continue to evolve and require further empirical investigation to clarify their impact on trial quality and equity.

Oustanding questions

Sarcoma research has demonstrated how principles-based clinical trial governance can be operationalised under the constraints of rarity. However, several important questions remain unresolved.

First, how can WHO (2024) and ICH E6(R3) principles be consistently translated into proportionate, fit-for-purpose governance across diverse regulatory and healthcare systems while maintaining scientific validity and equitable access?

Second, how can adaptive and pragmatic trial designs, registry-based evidence, real-world data approaches, and decentralised or hybrid trial models be optimally combined to improve feasibility, participant experience, operational validity, and equity across different rare cancer settings?

Third, although patient-centred governance has emerged as a key determinant of operational validity, the optimal approaches for integrating patient involvement, patient-reported outcomes, and measures of participant burden into trial design, conduct, and regulatory decision-making require further evaluation.

Fourth, how can digital technologies, artificial intelligence, and decentralised clinical trial approaches be implemented to improve recruitment, follow-up, data quality, and trial efficiency while ensuring transparency, interoperability, digital inclusion, equity, and appropriate regulatory oversight?

Fifth, what governance models, collaborative structures, and sustainable funding mechanisms are required to maintain international rare cancer research infrastructures, support regulatory harmonisation, enable interoperable data sharing, and ensure equitable participation across healthcare systems?

Finally, as molecular stratification creates increasingly smaller patient subsets within common cancers, determining how governance principles and methodological lessons from sarcoma trials can inform the design, conduct, and oversight of future precision oncology studies represents an important priority for research.
