Text Indexing and Pattern Matching with Ephemeral Edits
Solon P. Pissis

Abstract

Our goal is to design a data structure over a given text that supports subsequent pattern matching queries with ephemeral substring insertions, deletions, or substitutions in the text; we require insertions and substitutions to be of constant length. In particular, we preprocess a text T = T [ 0 . . n ) T=T[0\mathinner{.\,.}n) over an integer alphabet Σ = [ 0 , σ ) \Sigma=[0,\sigma) with σ = n 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 ) \sigma=n^{\mathcal{O}(1)} in 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ) \mathcal{O}(n) time. Then, we can preprocess any arbitrary pattern P = P [ 0 . . m ) P=P[0\mathinner{.\,.}m) given online in 𝒪 ⁡ ( m ​ log ⁡ log ​ m ) \mathcal{O}(m\log\log m) time and 𝒪 ⁡ ( m ) \mathcal{O}(m) space and allow any ephemeral sequence of edit operations in T T . We also introduce pattern matching with ephemeral edits , a new version of pattern matching. Introduction

Pattern matching and text indexing are the two flagship problems of combinatorial pattern matching.
In
pattern matching
, we are given a pattern
P=P[0\mathinner{.\,.}m)
of length
m
and a text
T=T[0\mathinner{.\,.}n)
of length
n
, and we are asked to find all occurrences of
P
in
T
. This can be solved in
\mathcal{O}(n)
time by the classical Knuth-Morris-Pratt algorithm
[
KJP77
]
. In
text indexing
, we are asked to preprocess a text
T
of length
n
into a data structure that supports subsequent pattern matching queries: given a pattern
P
, we report (or count) all occurrences of
P
in
T
. Reporting all
Occ
occurrences of
P
in
T
can be done in the optimal
\mathcal{O}(m+\textsf{Occ})
time using suffix trees, which can be constructed in
\mathcal{O}(n)
time
[
FFM00
]
.

A central question of theoretical and practical interest is:

What happens when text
T
is subjected to edit operations?

Motivation.

In a typical setting, one already has at hand a long reference string as the text
T
(e.g., a reference genome, a baseline legal document or a configuration file), which will remain mostly static, except for a few local edits. These local edits are often
independent
(e.g., genomic variants coming from individuals or client-specific variations); or
hypothetical
(e.g., they are not intended to be permanent, but rather are applied temporarily to test a specific scenario in a scientific simulation or to analyze the impact of a potential alteration). More concretely, this setting is highly relevant in computational pangenomics
[
Con18
,
TM20
,
MWHBS24
]
, where
T
models an
existing
reference sequence, and the sequence of independent edit operations represents a collection of short local variants originating from different
individuals
.

The Variant Call Format (
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variant_Call_Format

The fact that local variants originate from different individuals suggests that once a variant is processed, the corresponding edit should be reverted before processing the next one. Indeed, when querying a pattern against one individual’s genome (which is a variant of the reference),

when moving on to query against another individual’s genome, which will have its own distinct short local variant from the reference.

In response, we introduce new versions of text indexing and pattern matching with ephemeral edits.

Our Contributions.

We introduce a new interesting version of text indexing that we call

Related Work.

A natural question that arises is:

Why cannot one use the existing dynamic text indexing algorithms to solve the above problems?

To the best of our knowledge, the existing dynamic text indexing algorithms have seen limited to no adoption by practitioners. One reason for this is certainly their extreme intricacy, as exemplified in
[
LMN24
]
. Another related reason is that, while a fully dynamic model is theoretically appealing, it often proves overly powerful for the above problems.
Let us review the main works on the topic before we provide more concrete arguments to answer the above question.
The problem of
dynamic text indexing
was introduced by Gu, Farach, and Beigel in 1994
[
GFB94
]
. The problem asks to design a data structure (a dynamic index) that supports updates (insertions or deletions) to a text
T
and pattern matching queries. The solution of
[
GFB94
]
(see also
[
AB20
]
for an amortized analysis) achieves
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
-time updates to
T
and
\mathcal{O}(m+\textsf{Occ}\log i+i\log m)
-time queries, where
i
is the number of edit operations executed so far. Ferragina
[
Fer97
]
later showed how
to support insertions and deletions of text blocks with the query time being roughly proportional to the number of updates made so far. Soon after that, Ferragina and Grossi
[
FG99
]
improved this to support updates in
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n})
time and queries in the optimal
\mathcal{O}(m+\textsf{Occ})
time. Sahinalp and Vishkin
[
SV96
]
showed the first polylogarithmic data structure that supports
\mathcal{O}(\log^{3}n)
-time updates and
\mathcal{O}(m+\textsf{Occ}+\log n)
-time queries. The update time was later improved by Alstrup, Brodal, and Rauhe
[
ABR00
]
to
\mathcal{O}(\log^{2}n\log\log n\log^{\star}n)
at the expense of slightly slower queries with
\mathcal{O}(m+\textsf{Occ}+\log n\log\log n)
time. Finally, this was improved by Gawrychowski et al.
[
GKK+15
]
to
\mathcal{O}(\log^{2}n)
-time updates and
\mathcal{O}(m+\textsf{Occ})
-time queries.

Why is
Theorem

1
useful?

Say that we want to perform
k

independent
or
hypothetical
edits on an existing text
T
of length
n
and report all occurrences of a pattern
P
of length
m
after each edit. If we use the state-of-the-art algorithm for fully dynamic text indexing
[
GKK+15
]
as a black box, the total time will be
\mathcal{O}((n+k)\log^{2}(n+k)+km+\textsf{Output})
. Using
Theorem

1
instead, the total time will be
\mathcal{O}(n+m\log\log m+k\log\log n+\textsf{Output})
. Note that, for such a task, our improvement is substantial: even if we ignore the polylogarithmic terms, we replace the
km
additive term by
k
.

Amir et al.
[
ALLS07
]
have also considered a restricted version of
dynamic pattern matching
, where the pattern
P
is
static
(i.e., it is always the same) and only letter substitutions are supported in
T
. The preprocessing time of their data structure is
\mathcal{O}(n\log\log m+m\sqrt{\log m})
and the space used is
\mathcal{O}(n+m\sqrt{\log m})
. After each text update, the algorithm deletes all previous occurrences of the pattern that no longer match, and reports all new occurrences of the pattern in the text in
\mathcal{O}(\log\log m)
time.

Why is
Theorem

3
useful?

Say that we want to perform
k

independent
or
hypothetical
substitutions on an existing text
T
of length
n
and report all occurrences of a pattern
P
of length
m
after each edit. If we use the algorithm for a dynamic text and a static pattern
[
ALLS07
]
, the total time and space will be
\mathcal{O}(n\log\log m+m\sqrt{\log m}+k\log\log m+\textsf{Output})
and
\mathcal{O}(n+m\sqrt{\log m})
, respectively. Using
Theorem

3
instead, the total time and space will be
\mathcal{O}(n+k+\textsf{Output})
and
\mathcal{O}(n)
, respectively.

Although ephemeral edits have already been used for many other string problems, such as
longest common substrings

[
ACI+17
,
ACPR20
]
,
palindrome substrings

[
FNI+18
,
FNI+19
,
FM21
,
FNI+21
]
,
Lyndon substrings

[
UNI+18
]
, and
substring covers

[
MMSH24
]
, such a model has not been defined for text indexing or pattern matching.
We were thus also motivated to close this (surprising) gap in theory.

Strings.

We consider finite strings over an integer
alphabet

\Sigma=[0,\sigma)
.
The elements of
\Sigma
are called

.
For a string
T=T[0]\cdots T[n-1]
over alphabet
\Sigma
,

is

.
For any

, the string

is called a

of

.
By

we denote its occurrence at (starting) position

, and we call it a
fragment
of
T
.
When

, this fragment is called a

(we may denote it by

). When

, this fragment is called a

(we may denote it by
T[i\mathinner{.\,.}]
). We use

to denote the fragment

or

of

String Indexes.

Given a set
\mathcal{S}
of strings, a
trie

\textsf{T}(\mathcal{S})
is a rooted tree whose nodes represent the prefixes of strings in
\mathcal{S}

[
CHL07
]
. The prefix corresponding to node
u
is denoted by
\textsf{str}(u)
; and the node
u
is called the
locus
of
\textsf{str}(u)
. Sometimes we are interested in maximizing this prefix for an arbitrary string
S
. We say that the locus of an arbitrary string
S
in
\textsf{T}(\mathcal{S})
is the node
u
such that
\textsf{str}(u)
is a prefix of
S
and
|\textsf{str}(u)|
is maximized. The parent-child relationship in
\textsf{T}(\mathcal{S})
is defined as follows: the root node is the locus of the empty string
\varepsilon
; and the parent
u
of another node
v
is the locus of
\textsf{str}(v)
without the last letter. This letter is the label of the edge
(u,v)
. The order on
\Sigma
induces an order on the edges outgoing from any node of
\textsf{T}(\mathcal{S})
. A node

is

if it has at least two children and

if
\textsf{str}(u)\in\mathcal{S}
.

A

is obtained from

by dissolving all nodes except the root, the branching nodes, and the terminal nodes. The dissolved nodes are called
implicit
while the preserved nodes are called
explicit
. The compacted trie takes
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{S}|)
space provided that the edge labels are stored as pointers to fragments of strings in
\mathcal{S}
. Given the lexicographic order on
\mathcal{S}
along with the lengths of the longest common prefixes between any two consecutive (in this order) elements of
\mathcal{S}
, one can easily compute the compacted trie in
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{S}|)
time
[
KLA+01
]
.
The
suffix tree
of a string

, denoted by
\textsf{ST}(T)
, is precisely the compacted trie of the set of all suffixes of
T
. Suffix trees usually come with suffix links: a
suffix link

\textsf{slink}(u)
for a branching node

of
\textsf{ST}(T)
is the node
v
such that
\textsf{str}(v)
is the longest proper suffix of
\textsf{str}(u)
, i.e., if
\textsf{str}(u)=T[i\mathinner{.\,.}j-1]
then
\textsf{str}(v)=T[i+1\mathinner{.\,.}j-1]
. The
suffix array
of
T
, denoted by
\textsf{SA}(T)
, is the array of the suffixes of
T
in lexicographic order. It is often useful to define the
inverse suffix array
as
\textsf{iSA}[\textsf{SA}[i]]=i
, for all
i\in[0,n)
.
\textsf{ST}(T)
,
\textsf{SA}(T)
, and
\textsf{iSA}(T)
can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(n)
time for any string
T
of length
n
over an alphabet
\Sigma=[0,n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}]

[
FFM00
]
.
The edges of
\textsf{ST}(T)
can be accessed in
\mathcal{O}(1)
time if stored using perfect hashing
[
BCF+23
]
.

Text Indexing with Ephemeral Substring Edits

We design a data structure that supports pattern matching queries with ephemeral substring insertions, deletions, or substitutions. First, we preprocess the text
T=T[0\mathinner{.\,.}n)
over alphabet
\Sigma=[0,\sigma)
with
\sigma=n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
. Then, we can preprocess any arbitrary pattern
P=P[0\mathinner{.\,.}m)
given online and allow any ephemeral sequence of edit operations on
T
. Before reverting the
i
th operation, we report all occurrences of
P
in
T^{i}
in
\mathcal{O}(\log\log n+\textsf{Occ})
worst-case time. In
Section

3.1
, we present the preprocessing of the text, which occurs one time.
In
Section

3.2
, we present the preprocessing of the pattern, which occurs one time per arriving pattern. In
Section

3.3
, we present the algorithm for processing the edit operations. Let us first start with describing the high-level idea.

High-Level Idea.

Suppose an edit operation
e_{i}
occurs at some position of
T
. Our goal is to efficiently locate the
longest prefix
of
P
ending right before the edit and the
longest suffix
of
P
spanning the edit. It then suffices to trigger one prefix-suffix query
[
Pis25
]
. For locating the occurrences of
P
not spanning the edit (they are before or after the edit, and so they are not affected by it), we use a standard approach from computational geometry. We focus on finding the longest such suffix of
P
(finding the longest such prefix of
P
is symmetric).

When a pattern
P
arrives, we find the union of occurrences of the suffixes of
P
in
T
using the suffix tree of
T
. Although these occurrences can be
\Omega(m)
, we can encode them in at most
m
intervals on
T
. These intervals are not pairwise disjoint because a suffix of
P
can be a prefix of another suffix of
P
. We can, however, make them disjoint and still have
\mathcal{O}(m)
pairwise disjoint intervals on
T
.

3.2
Preprocessing the Pattern

The preprocessing of pattern
P
is more involved.
When
P
arrives, we construct the data structure of
Theorem

4
for prefix-suffix queries over
P
. This preprocessing takes
\mathcal{O}(m)
time and space.
We also spell
P
in
\textsf{ST}(T)
by means of suffix links to find the locus of the longest substring starting at every position of
P
and occurring in
T
.
In particular, for every
suffix

P[i\mathinner{.\,.}m)
of
P
occurring in
T

[s,e]_{i}
labeled by

P[i\mathinner{.\,.}m)
occurs at positions
\textsf{SA}[s],\ldots,\textsf{SA}[e]
of

This can be done in
\mathcal{O}(m)
time (provided that we are given
\textsf{ST}(T)
) using the classical
matching statistics
algorithm
[
Gus97
]
.

We next sort the letters of
P
in
\mathcal{O}(m\log\log m)
time and
\mathcal{O}(m)
space
[
Han04
]
. (Note that this sorting is required only when
\sigma=m^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
does not hold.)
Each letter of
P
is then replaced by its rank; in particular, each letter is now an integer from
[0,m)
. We can thus construct the suffix tree
\textsf{ST}(P)
of

in
\mathcal{O}(m)
time and space. Given
\textsf{ST}(P)
, we derive the following rooted tree, which we denote by
\textsf{TREE}(P)
; inspect
Fig.

2
for an example. We dissolve every branching node
u
in
\textsf{ST}(P)
that does not represent a suffix of
P
(along with its incoming edges) and connect every child
v
of
u
directly with the parent
\textsf{parent}(u)
of
u
via the edge
(\textsf{parent}(u),v)
labeled by the concatenation of the labels of the edges
(\textsf{parent}(u),u)
and
(u,v)
. Thus, the tree
\textsf{TREE}(P)
consists of exactly
m+1
nodes: the root node and one node per suffix of
P
occurring in
T
. Every root-to-node path represents a single suffix. We have an internal (possibly branching) node if and only if it spells a suffix of
P
that is a prefix of another suffix of
P
. The edge labels are encoded by intervals over
[0,m)
and so the size of
\textsf{TREE}(P)
is
\mathcal{O}(m)
and it takes
\mathcal{O}(m)
time to construct it using a traversal on
\textsf{ST}(P)

This preprocessing takes
\mathcal{O}(m\log\log m)
time (or
\mathcal{O}(m)
time if we do not sort the letters of

) and
\mathcal{O}(m)
space.

Using the loci information obtained from

3.3
Edit Operation

Let us start by characterizing the type of occurrences of
P
in
T^{i}
. We will use this to argue about the correctness of our algorithm. We have the following occurrence types:

Type 1

: The starting and ending positions are both before the edit

Type 2

: The starting and ending positions are both after the edit

Type 3

: The starting position is before the edit and the ending position is within the edit

Type 4

: The starting position is within the edit and the ending position is after the edit

Type 5

: The starting position is before the edit and the ending position is after the edit

Type 6

: The starting and ending positions are both within the edit.

For insertions or substitutions, all the above occurrence types are relevant, whereas for deletions only Types 1, 2, and 5 are relevant. Inspect
Fig.

5
for an illustration. It is readily verifiable that these are all possible occurrence types.

For starters, let us assume that
\epsilon=1
and suppose that an edit operation happens at position
p
of

. We first consider the case of a letter deletion; i.e., we are asked to delete letter

.
We find the occurrences of

occurring in

[p+1,p+|S|]
and an ending position in
[p+|S|+1,|T^{i}|)
(
Type 4
).
We find the lex-rank
r=\textsf{iSA}[p+1]
of
T[p+1\mathinner{.\,.}n)
in the predecessor structure derived from
\textsf{TREE}(P)
. This gives us the longest suffix of
P
occurring as a prefix of
T[p+1\mathinner{.\,.}n)
. We also find the longest prefix of
P
that is a suffix of
S
naively in
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})=\mathcal{O}(1)
time, and trigger another prefix-suffix query.
(
Type 3
is symmetric to
Type 4
.)
Further note that
Type 6
occurrences can be found in
\epsilon=\mathcal{O}(1)
time using any linear-time pattern matching algorithm
[
KJP77
]
.
The total time to process the insertion is
\mathcal{O}(1)
and for reporting is
\mathcal{O}(\log\log n+\textsf{Occ})
.

4

We design a data structure for pattern matching with ephemeral block deletions. First, we preprocess two strings
T
and
P
, each of length at most
n
, over alphabet
\Sigma=[0,\sigma)
with
\sigma=n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
. Then, we allow any ephemeral sequence of arbitrarily-long block deletions in
T
. Before reverting the
i
th operation, we report all
Occ
occurrences of
P
in
T^{i}
in the optimal
\mathcal{O}(\textsf{Occ})
worst-case time. This improves upon
Theorem

1
for this setting.

In

4.1
, we show the preprocessing of

and

4.2

We start by constructing the data structure of

4
for prefix-suffix queries over

.
This takes
\mathcal{O}(|P|)

We then employ
1

5
Pattern Matching with Ephemeral Edits

We design a data structure for pattern matching with ephemeral edit operations. First, we preprocess two strings
T
and
P
each of length at most
n
, over alphabet
\Sigma=[0,\sigma)
with
\sigma=n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
. Then, we allow any ephemeral sequence of edit operations in
T
. Before reverting the
i
th operation, we report all
Occ
occurrences of
P
in
T^{i}
in the optimal
\mathcal{O}(\textsf{Occ})
worst-case time.
This improves upon
Theorem

1
for this setting.

In
Section

5.1
, we show the preprocessing of
T
and
P
. In
Section

5.2
, we show the algorithm for processing the edit operations.

5.1
Preprocessing

We start by constructing the data structure of
Theorem

4
for prefix-suffix queries over
P
. This takes
\mathcal{O}(|P|)
time and space. We also construct the String Matching Automaton (SMA) of the reverse of
P
. The SMA of a string
X
, denoted by
\textsf{SMA}(X)
, is the minimal deterministic automaton that accepts the language
\Sigma^{*}X
, where
X\in\Sigma^{*}
. It has
|X|+1=\mathcal{O}(|X|)
states, one for every prefix of
X
, with
\varepsilon
being the initial state and
X
being the accepting
state. The crucial property of SMA is that only the transitions that do not lead to the initial state are stored. There are at most
2|X|=\mathcal{O}(|X|)
of them, regardless of the size of the alphabet
[
CHL07
]
. The
\textsf{SMA}(X)
can be constructed in
\mathcal{O}(|X|)
time
[
CHL07
]
; its transitions can be accessed in
\mathcal{O}(1)
time if stored using perfect hashing
[
BCF+23
]
. This preprocessing takes
\mathcal{O}(|P|)
time and space.

Deletions are handled by
Theorem

2
; we shift our focus to preprocessing for insertions. We employ
2
(inspect
Fig.

7
). We construct the suffix tree
\textsf{ST}(T\#P)
of
T\#P
,
\#\notin\Sigma
, and mark the nodes of the suffix tree that represent the suffixes of
P
.
Every marked node representing
P[i\mathinner{.\,.}|P|)
has a pointer to the state of SMA
representing the reverse of
P[i\mathinner{.\,.}|P|)
.
Using a bottom-up traversal on the suffix tree and a stack to maintain the currently active marked nodes, we store a pointer from every leaf representing a suffix of
T
to its deepest marked ancestor (i.e., the longest suffix of
P
represented by an ancestor of the leaf).
We also store the longest prefix of
P
which is a suffix of the prefix
T[0\mathinner{.\,.}p]
of
T
, for all
p
(see
Section

4.1
).
This preprocessing takes
\mathcal{O}(n)
time and space.

We construct the suffix tree
\textsf{ST}(T)
and the suffix array
\textsf{SA}(T)
of
T
. This preprocessing takes
\mathcal{O}(n)
time and space
[
FFM00
]
. We also store at each node of
\textsf{ST}(T)
the corresponding
\textsf{SA}(T)
interval – this can be achieved in
\mathcal{O}(n)
time using a bottom-up traversal on
\textsf{ST}(T)
. We also construct a data structure for answering any RMQ on
\textsf{SA}(T)
and a data structure for answering any RmQ on
\textsf{SA}(T)
. This preprocessing takes
\mathcal{O}(n)
time and space
[
BF00
]
. We also find the interval on
\textsf{SA}(T)
encoding the occurrences of
P
in
T
. This takes
\mathcal{O}(|P|)
time.

5.2
Edit Operation

Since we have edit operations involving a single letter,
it is readily verifiable that only Type 1, 2, 5, and 6 occurrences are relevant.
Assume that we insert letter
c
after
T[p]
.
If
|P|=1
, a
Type 6
occurrence is trivially reported in
\mathcal{O}(1)
time.
For the occurrences of
P
in
T[0\mathinner{.\,.}p)
(
Type 1
) we use RmQs and for the occurrences of
P
in
T[p+1\mathinner{.\,.}|T|)
(
Type 2
) we use RMQs, similar to
Section

3
.
Namely, each occurrence is reported in
\mathcal{O}(1)
time.
For
Type 5
, we find the longest suffix of
P
occurring as a prefix of
cT[p+1\mathinner{.\,.}n)
as follows. When
p+1
is given, we find the lex-rank
r=\textsf{iSA}[p+1]
of
T[p+1\mathinner{.\,.}n)
among all suffixes of
T
. We find the leaf
v_{p+1}
representing
T[p+1\mathinner{.\,.}n)
and follow the pointer to its deepest marked ancestor
u_{p+1}
. From there,
using letter
c
, we find the longest suffix
cP[i\mathinner{.\,.}|P|)
of
P
occurring as a prefix of
cT[p+1\mathinner{.\,.}n)
in
\mathcal{O}(1)
time as follows. We follow the pointer of
u_{p+1}
to the SMA. From the state corresponding to the suffix of
P
, represented by
u_{p+1}
, we follow the transition (if any) using letter
c
. This will bring us to the state that represents the reverse of
cP[i\mathinner{.\,.}|P|)=P[i-1]P[i\mathinner{.\,.}|P|)
.
Recall that to find the longest prefix of
P
that is a suffix of
T[0\mathinner{.\,.}p]
we do the same work as for the deletion operation on the reverse of
P
and
T
. After finding the longest prefix of
P
that is a suffix of
T[0\mathinner{.\,.}p]
and the longest suffix of
P
occurring as a prefix of
cT[p+1\mathinner{.\,.}n)
, we trigger one prefix-suffix query. The total time to process the insertion is
\mathcal{O}(1)
and for reporting is
\mathcal{O}(\textsf{Occ})
. The algorithm is correct by
2
.

Recall that substitutions work the same way as insertions, but with a different offset.
We have arrived at
Theorem

3
.

6
Concluding Remarks

We presented algorithms for text indexing and pattern matching with ephemeral edits. Our algorithms are simple to implement as they utilize only textbook algorithms or data structures with mature implementations; and they also outperform their fully dynamic counterparts, while, at the same time, they are founded on a model highly motivated by real-world applications.

It would be interesting to improve
Theorem

1
by improving the preprocessing cost for the pattern or improving the query time for reporting. It would also be interesting to generalize
Theorem

3
to support short substring insertions or substitutions. Another direction would be to consider other versions of text indexing or pattern matching (here we have considered their vanilla version) with ephemeral edits.
