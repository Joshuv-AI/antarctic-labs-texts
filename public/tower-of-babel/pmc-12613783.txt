Molecular abnormalities and clinical features in adult patients with acute myeloid leukemia in Thailand
Authors: Pichika Chantrathammachart, Artit Jinawath, Teeraya Puavilai, Lalida Arsa, Pornnapa Police, Phichchapha Noikongdee, Tichayapa Phojanasenee, Pakatorn Sae-Lim, Thanakrit Piyajaroenkij, Phatsorn Choksomnuk, Suporn Chuncharunee, Pimjai Niparuck
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe genetic heterogeneity observed in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) contributes to a wide range of clinical presentations and prognoses. We conducted a retrospective study to investigate genetic abnormalities, clinical characteristics, and survival of AML patients.MethodsTargeted exome analysis of 25 genes using a QIAact Myeloid DNA UMI Panel with the GeneReader NGS was performed.ResultsDe novo AML (dAML) and secondary AML (sAML) were observed in 163 and 56 patients, respectively. ASXL1, SRSF2, and RUNX1 mutations were significantly observed in sAML patients. Among dAML patients, mutant IDH1, ASXL1, TP53, and TET2 were associated with low WBC count (< 4 × 109/L), and mutations of FLT3-ITD and NPM1 were associated with high WBC count (> 100 × 109/L). In dAML group, KIT and FLT3-TKD mutations were commonly found in favorable cytogenetic risk, RAS and SF3B1 mutations were significantly observed in the abnormal chromosome 3 group, whereas IDH1, IDH2, RUNX1, and SRSF2 mutations were significantly observed in trisomy group. Mutant TP53 was seen significantly in AML patients with complex and monosomy karyotypes. ASXL1, IDH1, IDH2, TP53, and SRSF2 mutations were independent factors associated with poor OS through univariate analysis. Nevertheless, multivariate analysis showed IDH1 (HR = 2.699; 95% CI: 1.331–5.473), TP53 (HR = 2.200; 95% CI: 1.409–3.435) and ASXL1 (HR = 1.592; 95% CI: 1.040–2.436) mutations were significantly associated with short OS in AML patients. In contrast, RUNX1 (HR = 3.667; 95% CI: 1.213–11.084) and DNMT3A (HR = 2.094; 95% CI: 1.080–4.081) mutations were significantly associated with poor DFS on multivariate analysis.ConclusionsThe complexity of AML was influenced by various cytogenetic and molecular abnormalities, which contributed to patients’ heterogeneous presentation and survival outcomes. In addition to the previous data, IDH1, IDH2, and DNMT3A mutations might have affected survival outcomes in AML patients in our retrospective cohort. However, further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate these observations.

Background

Heterogeneity in somatic genetic aberration plays a pivotal role in the evolution of myeloid neoplasms and acute myeloid leukemia (AML). Various gene mutations have been discovered in AML patientsincluding NPM1, FLT3, CEBPA, DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1, IDH1, IDH2, EZH2, RAS, RUNX1, TP53, SF3B1, SRSF2, ZRSR2, U2AF1, PTPN11, and WT1 [1–3]. However, only mutated NPM1, FLT3-ITD, CEBPA, ASXL1, RUNX1, TP53, BCOR, EZH2, SF3B1, SRSF2, STAG2, U2AF1, and ZRSR2 carries prognostic relevance in AML patients [2, 3]. In addition, mutations in genes involved in DNA methylation, histone modification, RNA splicing, and DNA transcription also lead to the development of myelodysplastic syndrome [4, 5]. According to recent studies, TET2 (42%), DNMT3A (35%), NPM1 (32%), SRSF2 (25%), and ASXL1 (21%) were found commonly in AML patients aged ≥ 75 years [6]. RAS, PTPN11, ASXL1, and NF1 mutations were most common found in AML patients with inv(3)/t(3;3). [7, 8] SF3B1 mutation in AML is strongly associated with MECOM rearrangements [9]. Except for the outcomes of NPM1, FLT3-ITD, CEBPA, and TP53 gene mutation in Thai AML patients, there is little data on clinical characteristics in Thai and Asian patients with AML harboring various somatic gene alterations [10]. [11] Hence, we conducted this retrospective study to analyze the molecular and cytogenetic abnormalities, clinical characteristics, and long-term survival of patients with AML.

Methods

Patients

The Institutional Review Boards of Ramathibodi Hospital, Mahidol University approved this retrospective study, and informed consent was waived. We studied all adults with newly diagnosed AML between June 2019 and May 2022 who had the results of molecular tests using next-generation sequencing (NGS) and cytogenetic analysis.

Treatment schedules

The induction chemotherapy regimen was (1) Cytarabine plus idarubicin (7 + 3 or 5 + 2) followed by allogeneic stem cell transplantation (SCT), or 4 cycles of intermediate-dose cytarabine (IDAC) or azacitidine monotherapy. (2) Azacitidine monotherapy for induction and maintenance therapy.

Risk stratification and response criteria

We classified the cytogenetic and molecular risks of AML and the treatment response according to the 2022 European LeukemiaNet (ELN) recommendation. Monosomal karyotype is the presence of 2 or more distinct monosomies (excluding loss of X or Y), or one single autosomal monosomy combined with at least one structural chromosome abnormality (excluding core-binding factor AML). AML patient with mutated TP53 is defined as a variant allele fraction (VAF) of at least 10%, with or without loss of the wild-type TP53 allele [12].

Cytogenetic and molecular analysis

Short-term culture of bone marrow (BM) cells, metaphase spread preparation and G-banding were performed for cytogenetic analysis. BM deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) samples from patients with AML were used for molecular analysis, the targeted exome analysis of 25 genes was performed, using a QIAact Myeloid DNA UMI Panel with the GeneReader NGS. The system and the target genes listed in this panel were ASXL1, CALR, CBL, CEBPA, CSF3R, DNMT3A, EZH2, FLT3, IDH1, IDH2, JAK2, KIT, KRAS, MPL, NPM1, NRAS, RUNX1, SETBP1, SF3B1, SH2B3, SRSF2, TET2, TP53, U2AF1 and ZRSR2. Gene alterations with VAF > 2% were classified as driver mutations. FLT3 exons 14, 15, and 20 were amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using specific primers and subsequently digested with EcoRV. The PCR products were analyzed using 2% agarose gel electrophoresis.

Statistical analysis

Molecular and cytogenetic results, age, gender, hemoglobin level, white blood cell, and platelet count were analyzed using the chi-square test. The Kaplan-Meier method was used for survival analysis. The univariate and multivariate analyses were performed with Log-rank tests and Cox Regression, respectively. All calculations were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 software.

Results

Acute myeloid leukemia

There were 224 AML patients, male and female patients accounted for 60% and 40%, respectively. Patients with de novo AML (dAML) and secondary AML (sAML) were observed in 73% and 25%, respectively. The median age was 59 years (range, 16–88 years). Abnormal karyotype was found in 52% (116 patients) of all AML patients, 35, 33, 8, 10, and 16 of whom had complex karyotype (CK), trisomy, t(8;21), abnormal chromosome 3, monosomal karyotype (MK). The CK and MK were found in 100% and 40% of 5 patients with therapy-related AML (tAML), respectively, which were significantly higher than those in patients with dAML (13% and 6%, respectively, P < 0.001) and sAML (14% and 7%, respectively, P = 0.015). Among AML patients, unfavorable-risk cytogenesis was more frequently found in all five patients with tAML, whereas intermediate-risk cytogenesis was found commonly in patients with dAML (74%) and sAML (79%), P < 0.001. All patients with tAML had TP53 mutation, whereas 9% and 13% of dAML and sAML patients harbored TP53 mutation, respectively, P < 0.001. The frequently mutated genes in patients with dAML were NPM1 (23%), FLT3 (22%), TET2 (18%), DNMT3A (17%), RAS (17%), FLT3- ITD (16%), IDH2 (15%), and CEBPA (14%). Compared with dAML patients, ASXL1, SRSF2, and RUNX1 mutations were significantly observed at higher frequencies in patients with sAML, 30% vs. 9% (P = 0.001), 25% vs. 10% (P = 0.01), and 21% vs. 9% (P = 0.01), respectively. IDH2 (21%) and RAS (17%) mutations were also found frequently in sAML patients. In contrast, CEBPA mutation was significantly higher in dAML than sAML group. (Table 1)

Molecular abnormalities and clinical features

Among patients with dAML, mutated SRSF2 (16% vs. 3%, P = 0.007), CEBPA (19% vs. 7%, P = 0.031) and TP53 (13% vs. 4%, P = 0.066) were more common in males than females, whereas mutant NPM1 was found considerably in female patients (30% vs. 18%, P = 0.060). SRSF2 and U2AF1 mutations were more significantly found in older (age ≥ 60 years) than younger patients (age < 60 years) with dAML, 17% vs. 6% (P = 0.023) and 8% vs. 1% (P = 0.024). respectively. Between AML patients with wild-type and mutant IDH1, ASXL1, TP53, and TET2, a low WBC count (WBC < 4 × 109/L), defined as leukopenia was observed more frequently in patients with mutant ASXL1 (42% vs. 23%, P = 0.023),TET2 (40% vs. 23%, P = 0.031), TP53 (41% vs. 23%, P = 0.054), and IDH1 (50% vs. 24%, P = 0.06). Very high WBC count (WBC > 100 × 109/L), which typically indicates hyperleukocytosis and an increased risk of complications, was found to be significantly higher in AML patients with FLT3-ITD (45% vs. 11%, P = 0.001) and NPM1 mutations (28% vs. 13%, P = 0.01), Table 2. In comparison between the two groups, shorter OS was seen in AML patients with a very high WBC count (6 vs. 9 months), however, the result was not statistically significant, and slightly longer OS was observed in AML patients with a low WBC count at diagnosis (11 vs. 9 months).

Molecular and cytogenetic abnormalities

Normal karyotype (NK) was commonly found in AML patients with NPM1 (80%), FLT3-ITD (78%), and DNMT3A (69%) mutations. There was no patient with favorable-risk cytogenesis that had mutant NPM1 (P < 0.001), FLT3-ITD (P = 0.015) and SRSF2 (P = 0.024). Mutant TP53 (33%) was more significantly observed in AML patients with unfavorable-risk cytogenesis and no AML patients with favorable-risk cytogenesis had mutant TP53 (P < 0.001). The KIT and FLT3-TKD mutations were significantly higher observed in AML patients with favorable cytogenetic risk compared to wild-type KIT and TKD, 50% vs. 1% (P = 0.001) and 25% vs. 4% (P = 0.02), respectively. Mutant TP53 was commonly found in AML patients with CK (63%) and MK (38%). Among AML patients with abnormal chromosome 3, RAS (14%) and SF3B1 (18%) mutations were significantly higher than those with wild-type RAS and SF3B1. Mutations of IDH1 (50%), IDH2 (41%), RUNX1 (38%), and SRSF2 (32%) were commonly found in AML patients with trisomy. Table 2.

Genetic abnormalities predict survival outcomes in AML patients

Only 14% of AML patients treated with azacitidine achieved complete remission (CR), whereas 73% and 65% of AML patients receiving 7 + 3 and 5 + 2 regimens achieved CR, respectively. The CR rate was found to be highest in AML patients with favorable cytogenetics (100%) and was observed to be lowest in AML patients with poor cytogenetic risk (38%), P = 0.001. CR rate was 33%,42%, and 55% in AML patients with abnormal chromosome 3, CK, and MK, respectively. NPM1 and ASXL1 mutations significantly affected the CR rate in AML patients, the high CR rate was found in AML patients with mutated NPM1, whereas AML patients with mutant ASXL1 achieved a low CR rate. Only RUNX1 mutation was associated with poor DFS, P = 0.014 in dAML patients. (Fig. 5) The cytogenetic risk did not significantly affect DFS in dAML, P = 0.173. Longer DFS was observed in dAML patients with NPM1, CEBPA, and RAS mutations, and shorter DFS was shown in dAML patients with mutant DNMT3A, IDH2, TP53, ASXL1, SRSF2, and TET2, nevertheless, these differences were not statistically significant. Discussion

In this study, 52% of all AML patients had abnormal karyotype. The most common gene mutations in our dAML patients were NPM1, FLT3, TET2, DNMT3A, and RAS, which were similar to previous data [13–16]. Confirming the results of previous studies, mutant ASXL1, SRSF2, and RUNX1 were found in sAML more than the dAML group [14]. However, we also found the high prevalence of IDH2 and RAS mutations in our sAML patients. The most common gene mutations in older patients with dAML were NPM1, TET2, DNMT3A, FLT3, FLT3-ITD, SRSF2 and IDH2 as was published in Itzykson, et al. cohort [14, 17]. Between males and females with dAML, NPM1 mutation was more common in females, and mutated SRSF2 was frequently found in males as reported in the previous study [14]. Similarly, previous studies showed that TP53 mutation was associated with older age, lower blast count, lower WBC count, and adverse risk karyotypes in dAML patients, [18–20] we found a low WBC count (WBC < 4 × 109/L) and unfavorable cytogenetic risk (CK and MK) significantly in dAML patients with mutant TP53. AML patients with ASXL1, TET2, and IDH1 mutations also noted to exhibit lower WBC counts compared to those with wild-type ASXL1, TET2, and IDH1 in our and previous studies [21–26]. A very high WBC count was found in AML patients with FLT3-ITD and NPM1 mutations. Less than 5% of our AML patients with CK harbored NPM1 and FLT3-ITD mutations. The KIT and FLT3-TKD mutations were found commonly in AML patients with favorable cytogenetic risk as previously reported; a high incidence of mutant KIT and ASXL1 in AML patients with t(8;21) and RAS, KIT, and FLT3-TKD mutations in AML patients with inv(16) [27]. The common mutations in our AML patients with abnormal chromosome 3 were SF3B1and RAS genes that was consistent with the previous data [8–10, 27]. In AML patients with trisomy, mutant IDH1, IDH2, RUNX1, SRSF2, and ASXL1 were seen higher than those with wild-type of theses genes, these confirmed the report in the previous studies that mutations of RUNX1 and ASXL1 were significantly associated with trisomy 8 in AML and MDS patients [28, 29].

CR rate was very low in AML patients with abnormal chromosome 3, CK, and MK. The high CR rate was found in AML patients with mutated NPM1. However, we did not see significant survival benefits in AML patients with NPM1 mutation, the possible cause may be that almost all patients with NPM1 mutation (91%) had co-occurrence mutations. In the group of patients with NPM1 mutation (46 patients), the most common co-occurring mutations were DNMT3A (39%), FLT3-ITD (33%) and TET2 (26%), Co-mutations involving NPM1/DNMT3A along with other gene mutations were observed in 17% of patients with NPM1 mutations. The DFS and OS rates were shortest in patients with co-ocurring NPM1, DNMT3A and FLT3-ITD mutations. In the entire AML population, unfavorable cytogenetic risk and mutations of ASXL1, IDH1, IDH2, TP53, and SRSF2 were independent factors associated with poor OS. Longer OS was also found in AML patients who harbored NPM1, CEBPA, and RAS mutations, and unfavorable OS was observed in AML patients with mutant FLT3-ITD, ASXL1, RUNX1 and DNMT3A, however, there was no statistical significance between AML patients with and without these gene mutations. We found short survival significantly in AML patients with IDH1/IDH2, which the previous studies reported some ambiguity around the outcomes of AML patients with IDH1/IDH2 mutations, either favorable or unfavorable survival rates [30–34]. In addition, our cohort showed a short DFS in AML patients with DNMT3A mutation. This aligns with findings from other studies that observed short survival in DNMT3A-mutated AML patients [35, 36]. Only 9% of our DNMT3A-mutated AML patients had solely DNMT3A mutation, co-occurrence mutations of DNMT3A/NPM1 and DNMT3A/NPM1/FLT3-ITD were detected in 51% and 11% of DNMT3A-mutated AML patients, respectively. However, due to insufficient evidence, the 2022 ELN recommendations do not currently classify DNMT3A, IDH1 or IDH2 mutations as distinct prognostic risk groups [12]. According to our data, AML patients with mutant FLT3-TKD and RAS had better OS than those without FLT3-TKD and RAS mutations but were not statistically significant. Some previous studies have reported that mutated FLT3-TKD is associated with less aggressive disease in AML patients [37] and N-RAS mutation does not consistently influence OS, in contrast, K-RAS mutation has been found to significantly affect OS in AML patients [38, 39]. The results of survival outcomes associated with various gene mutations in our study, as well as in Asian and non-Asian AML patients, are shown in Table 5. DNMT3A mutation has been reported to be associated with unfavorable survival outcomes in this study, as well as in both Asian and Western AML patients [31, 41–43]. In contrast, IDH1and IDH2 mutations have been associated with varying survival outcomes, as shown in the Table 5. This variability may be due to co-occurring mutations involving these two genes. Nevertheless, survival outcomes in AML patients are influenced not only by cytogenetic and molecular abnormalities, but also by a variety of patient-related and treatment-related factors including performance status, co-morbidity, supportive treatment, and degree of infection before and after chemotherapy. Furthermore, the survival analysis in this retrospective cohort was limited for AML patients with U2AF1, SF3B1, EZH2, KIT, ZRSR2, SETBP1, CSF3R, MPL, JAK2, and SH2B3 mutations due to the small number of patients in our cohort. These certain gene mutations usually had a low prevalence in AML patients, which limit the statistical power of subgroup analyses. In addition, as a retrospective study, our analysis is subject to certain limitations, including potential selection bias such as the inclusion of only patients with available molecular and cytogenetic data, and limited control over confounding variables, such as variations in treatment regimens and the occurrence of unexpected severe infection in some patients, which led to death. In our center, azacitidine is used to treat patients over 70 years of age or patients who are frail or those with at least two significant or severe comorbidities and patients are treated with azacitidine for at least 2 years or until progression, depending on patient preference. Furthermore, the number of EPV for both OS and DFS analyses was less than 10, due to the small sample size. This limitation may have affected the predictive accuracy and robustness of our statistical models.

Conclusions

The complexity of AML was influenced by various cytogenetic and molecular abnormalities, which contributed to patients’ heterogeneous presentation and survival outcomes. In addition to the previous data, mutations of IDH1, IDH2 and DNMT3A might have affected survival outcomes in AML patients in our retrospective cohort.

However, further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate these observations.
