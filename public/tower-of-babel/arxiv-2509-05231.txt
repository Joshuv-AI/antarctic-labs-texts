MOMENTS OF DENSITY-DEPENDENT BRANCHING PROCESSES AND THEIR GENEALOGY
Mathilde André, Félix Foutel-Rodier, Emmanuel Schertzer

Abstract
A density-dependent branching process is a particle system in which individuals reproduce independently, but in a way that depends on the current population size. This feature can model a wide range of ecological interactions at the cost of breaking the branching property. We propose a general approach for studying the genealogy of these models based on moments. Building on a recent work of Bansaye, we show how to compute recursively these moments in a similar spirit to the many-to-few formula in the theory of branching processes. These formulas enable one to deduce the convergence of the genealogy by studying the population density, for which stochastic calculus techniques are available. As a first application of these ideas, we consider a density-dependent branching process started close to a stable equilibrium of the ecological dynamics. We show that, under a finite second moment assumption, its genealogy converges to Kingman’s coalescent when the carrying capacity of the population goes to infinity.



1
Introduction

1.1
Motivation

A genealogy is a forest structure that represents the degree of kinship
between individuals in a population. It is a central notion in population
genetics, in particular because genealogies are instrumental in studying
patterns of neutral genetic diversity
(
Wakeley, 2009
;
Tellier and Lemaire, 2014
)
. The mathematical theory of genealogies has been
mostly developed around the notion of coalescents, following the
seminal work of
Kingman, 1982
and its
subsequent generalizations
(
Pitman, 1999
;
Sagitov, 1999
;
Donnelly and Kurtz, 1999
;
Schweinsberg, 2001
)
. A
coalescent is a stochastic process obtained by sampling a finite number
of individuals at some reference generation, and following backward in
time their ancestral lineages until they merge (coalesce). This procedure
constructs a random forest which is usually encoded as a stochastic process with
values in the partitions.

Since their introduction, coalescents have been shown to describe the
limit of the genealogy of a wide variety of population models

(
Möhle, 1998
;
Möhle, 1999
;
Möhle and Sagitov, 2001
;
Kaj and Krone, 2003
;
Schweinsberg, 2003
)
. However, a feature shared by almost all of
these models is that population size is fixed or, if individuals can be
of different types, the number of individuals of each type is given. The
archetypical example of such models is the Wright–Fisher model, or its
generalizations to multiple types or to Cannings models

(
Cannings, 1974
;
Etheridge, 2011
)
. Despite their importance,
these processes do not model appropriately ecological interactions as
well as many important features of biological populations. For instance,
they can hardly model individuals spread across a spatial continuum or
with a continuous age structure. Conversely, density-dependent branching processes
is another class of well-studied models which seems suited to model such
phenomena. In these models, individuals reproduce independently, but in a
way that depends on the state of the population and can model biological
interactions
(
Kurtz, 1978
;
Fournier and Méléard, 2004
;
Champagnat, 2006
;
Calvez et al., 2022
;
Etheridge et al., 2024
)
. The
logistic branching process
(
Lambert, 2005
)
or the Bolker–Paccala
model
(
Bolker and Pacala, 1997
)
are typical examples of density-dependent
branching processes. This provides motivation for extending our knowledge
of genealogies to these processes, beyond models with fixed population
structure.

Unfortunately, the standard approach to proving convergence towards a
coalescent relies heavily on the property that the population structure
is fixed. This constraint entails that one can define a coalescent from
the discrete model as a Markov process. One can then rely on the numerous
techniques available for Markov processes to study it. The main objective of
our work is to propose some new ideas that can circumvent this issue. As
we will explain in more details below, instead of following ancestral
lineages backward in time, we will decompose forward in time the
genealogy at its last branch point. This approach is very close in spirit
to the spinal methods which have been used extensively in branching
process theory
(
Lyons et al., 1995
;
Bansaye et al., 2011
;
Harris and Roberts, 2017
)

and builds on the recent work of
Bansaye, 2024
in that direction.

As a first application of this approach, we study the genealogy of a
general class of density-dependent branching processes with no types. We
show that, if the population starts close to a stable equilibrium of the
ecological dynamics and if the reproduction law has finite variance, the
genealogy of the population converges to Kingman’s coalescent at large
scales.

1.2
Model and main result

Model

Fix some scaling parameter
K>0
. The parameter
K

corresponds to the carrying capacity of the model, which gives the
typical number of individuals in the population. For each
z>0
, we denote by
q(z)>0
and
L(z)

the branching rate and a random variable in
\{0,1,2,\dots\}
giving the number of offspring of an individual when the
population density (that is, the size of the population divided by the carrying capacity) is
z
. We start the population from
Z_{0}
particles
at time
t=0
. Then, if the current population size is
n
, each
individual dies independently at rate
q(n/K)
, upon which it is replaced
by an independent number of offspring distributed as
L(n/K)
. This
constructs our model iteratively, until a potential explosion time.

More formally, we envision each individual as the vertex of a graph and,
attaching parents to offspring, we construct the population as a random
forest where each vertex is further endowed with a birth and a death time.
We index individuals as elements of

\mathcal{U}=\bigcup_{n\in\N}\N^{n}
, the set of Ulam–Harris labels, with the usual
interpretation that
ui
is the
i
-th child of individual
u\in\mathcal{U}
. Note that, since our population is a forest rather than a
tree, it does not start from a unique root vertex
\varnothing
. Instead,
we start it from
Z_{0}
roots labelled
1,\dots,Z_{0}
. The procedure
described above constructs a collection of random variables
(\lambda_{u},\theta_{u})_{u\in\mathcal{U}}
, such that
\lambda_{u}
and
\theta_{u}
are
the birth and death times of
u
respectively. We will denote by

\mathcal{N}_{t}\ =\ \{u\in\mathcal{U}:\lambda_{u}\leqslant t<\theta_{u}\}

the set of individuals alive at time
t
, and by
Z_{t}=\lvert\mathcal{N}_{t}\rvert

the population size at that time. The population starts from
Z_{0}

individuals with labels
(i,\ i\leqslant Z_{0})
and birth times

\lambda_{i}=0
. In what follows, for
Z\in\N
we will always use the
convention that adding a superscript
K
corresponds to renormalizing by

1/K
, and write for instance

Z^{K}\ \coloneqq\ Z/K

At time
t
, each
u\in\mathcal{N}_{t^{-}}

dies at rate
q\big(Z_{t^{-}}^{K})
. In that case we let
\kappa_{u}
be an
independent copy of
L\big(Z^{K}_{t^{-}}\big)
, and set
\theta_{u}=t
and

\lambda_{uj}=\theta_{u}
for
j\leqslant\kappa_{u}
. For the sake of
definiteness, we also let
\lambda_{uj}=\theta_{uj}=\infty
for

j>\kappa_{u}
to indicate that these individuals are never born.

For two individuals
u,v\in\mathcal{U}
, we let
u\wedge v
denote
their most-recent common ancestor. The genealogical distance between two
individuals at time
t
can then be defined as

\forall u,v\in\mathcal{N}_{t},\quad D_{t}(u,v)\ =\ t-\theta_{u\wedge v}.

Note that
D_{t}(u,v)
also corresponds to the coalescence time between
u

and
v
.

Assumptions

Let
m(z)=\E\big[L(z)\big]
be the mean
number of offspring of an individual at population density
z
. When
m
is finite and the dynamical system
\dot{z}_{t}\ =\ z_{t}q(z_{t})(m(z_{t})-1)
admits a stable equilibrium, a
celebrated result of
Kurtz, 1978
(see also
Ethier and Kurtz, 1986
) shows that
the population density
(Z_{t}/K)_{t\geqslant 0}
converges in distribution to
a deterministic limit
(z_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
which is the solution to the
non-linear differential equation

\dot{z}_{t}\ =\ z_{t}q(z_{t})(m(z_{t})-1),

as
K\to\infty
.
Kurtz, 1978
also obtained that under an
additional second moment assumption at equilibrium, the deviations
converge weakly to an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process with gaussian
stationary distribution.

Our first assumption ensures that the limiting dynamics has a stable
equilibrium, which we set to
1
without loss of generality.

Assumption A
(Stable equilibrium).

The mean number of offspring
m(z)\coloneqq\E[L(z)]
is finite for any
z>0
, and
m(1)=1
. Moreover,
q
is continuous at
z=1
and
m
is
differentiable at
z=1
with
m^{\prime}(1)<0
.

Kingman’s coalescent corresponds to the scaling limit of the genealogy of
populations in which the variance of the number of offspring is finite.
If this is relaxed, an individual can have a number of offspring of the
same order as the population size, leading to more general

\Lambda
-coalescents
(
Schweinsberg, 2003
;
Möhle and Sagitov, 2001
)
.
Accordingly, our second assumption requires that the number of offspring
close to the equilibrium has a finite second moment.

Assumption B
(Finite second moment ).

The map
z\longmapsto m_{2}(z)\coloneqq\E[L(z)(L(z)-1)]
is
continuous at
z=1
. Moreover, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such
that

\adjustlimits{\limsup}_{A\to\infty}{\sup}_{z\in(1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)}\E[L(z)^{2}\mathds{1}_{\{L(z)>A\}}]=0.

(1.1)

Note that the uniform integrability condition (
1.1
) is
satisfied if
\sup_{z\in(1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)}\E[L(z)^{2+\eta}]<\infty
for some
\eta>0
.

At last, at this level of generality we cannot ensure that the population
size
(Z_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
does not explode in finite time. We will assume
throughout that this is almost-surely not the case.

Main result

Our main result is that the genealogy of the
population, when started close to the stable equilibrium, converges to
Kingman’s coalescent. There are several ways to formulate this result,
and we choose to use the formalism of coalescent theory. In this
direction, fix
T>0
and let
(U_{1},\dots,U_{k})
be
k\geqslant 1
individuals
sampled uniformly without replacement from the population at time
KT
.
(Under our assumptions there will be more than
k
individuals in the
population with high probability.) Let us define a process

(\Pi^{k,K}_{\tau})_{0\leqslant\tau\leqslant T}
with values in the partitions of

\{1,\dots,k\}
by

i\sim_{\Pi^{k,K}_{\tau}}j\iff D_{KT}(U_{i},U_{j})\leqslant\tau K.

This process encodes the subforest spanned by
(U_{i})_{i\leqslant k}
as a
coalescent. Let us also recall that the
k
-Kingman coalescent with
rate
r>0
is defined as the stochastic process
(\Pi^{k}_{\tau})_{\tau\leqslant T}
with values in the partitions of
\{1,\dots,k\}
such that

\Pi^{k}_{0}
is the partition of
\{1,\dots,k\}
into singletons, and
such that any two blocks merge at rate
r
.

Theorem 1.1
(Convergence of the genealogies).

Suppose that Assumption
A
and
B

hold and let
T>0
,
k\geqslant 1
.

(i)

The following holds in probability

\adjustlimits{\lim}_{K\to\infty}{\sup}_{0\leqslant s\leqslant KT}\Big|\frac{Z_{s}}{K}-1\Big|\ =\ 0.

(ii)

Conditional upon having
Z_{KT}\geqslant k
, let
(\Pi^{k,K}_{\tau})_{\tau\leqslant T}
be the coalescent
obtained from a uniform sample of size
k
at time
KT
. Then

\lim_{K\to\infty}(\Pi^{k,K}_{\tau})_{\tau\leqslant T}\ =\ (\Pi^{k}_{\tau})_{\tau\leqslant T}

in distribution for Skorohod’s topology, where
(\Pi^{k}_{\tau})_{\tau\geqslant 0}
is a
k
-Kingman coalescent with rate
q(1)m_{2}(1)
.

Related literature

Recently,
Forien, 2025
considered a branching process with logistic
competition that falls within our assumptions. The author considers the
scaling limit of the frequency process of the population, obtained by
giving different labels to the initial individuals and following their
frequencies as time goes on. One of the results derived in

Forien, 2025
is that, under a second moment assumption similar to
Assumption
B
, the frequency process converges to a
Fleming–Viot process. The latter is well known to be in moment duality
with Kingman’s coalescent (see
e.g.

Ethier and Kurtz, 1993
), which is consistent with our main result.
We would like to mention that the results in
Forien, 2025
go beyond
the case of finite variance reproduction law, and also give the limit of
the frequency process when the reproduction law has a heavy tail.

Near the completion of our work, we became aware that
Garrett and Nava-Trejo, 2025

derived a result very similar to the theorem above. Building on the
estimates of
Forien, 2025
, they prove convergence of the genealogy of
the branching process with logistic competition to Kingman’s coalescent.
Besides, they also derive the corresponding convergence to a

\mathrm{Beta}(2-\alpha,\alpha)
coalescent – with
\alpha\in[1,2)
–
when the reproduction law a has heavy tail. The main ingredient used in

Garrett and Nava-Trejo, 2025
is a lookdown construction of the process

(
Donnelly and Kurtz, 1999
)
, which is very different from our techniques. The
lookdown process leads to a very efficient proof of the convergence of
the genealogy. However, adapting it to a population with a general trait
(or type) structure would require to use continuous levels in a similar way
to
Kurtz and Rodrigues, 2011
;
Etheridge and Kurtz, 2014
, which seems challenging. Conversely,
although our approach might seem more indirect, spinal methods have
already proved to be very successful when studying the genealogy of
branching processes with various trait structures

(
Harris et al., 2020
;
Foutel-Rodier, 2024
;
Schertzer and Tourniaire, 2025
;
Foutel-Rodier et al., 2024
;
Harris et al., 2024a
;
Harris et al., 2024b
;
Boenkost et al., 2022
;
Johnston, 2019
)
. The skeleton of our proof should,
in principle, apply generally and we hope (and believe) that our approach
should scale to more complicated population models, in particular to
models incorporating space.

In a nutshell, the current work mainly focuses on introducing a
methodology on a particle system with no type, in order to extend it to
more involved population models in subsequent works.

1.3
Proof heuristics

Studying directly the
k
-coalescent at time
t
would involve estimating
quantities of the form

\E\bigg[\frac{1}{Z_{t}(Z_{t}-1)\dots(Z_{t}-k+1)}\sum_{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{N}_{t}:u_{1}\neq\dots\neq u_{k}}\varphi\big(D_{t}(\bm{u})\big)\bigg],

for any continuous bounded
\varphi
and where
D_{t}(\bm{u})
is the
matrix of pairwise genealogical distances between
\bm{u}=(u_{i})_{i\leqslant k}
. Since the population density remains close to its equilibrium,
we will show that it is actually sufficient to consider expressions of
the form

\E\bigg[\e^{-\beta Z_{t}}\sum_{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{N}_{t}:u_{1}\neq\dots\neq u_{k}}\varphi\big(D_{t}(\bm{u})\big)\bigg],

(1.2)

for
\beta\geqslant 0
, which we call the penalized
k
-moments of the
population. Estimating (
1.2
) with
\beta=0
is
already at the heart of the approach proposed in
Foutel-Rodier and Schertzer, 2023

to study the genealogy of branching processes, but it requires that the
offspring distribution has a finite moment of order
k
. The exponential
penalization with parameter
\beta>0
ensures that (
1.2
)
always remains finite, which is an idea borrowed from

Harris et al., 2024b
.

The cornerstone of our approach is a recursive formula that enables us to
compute (
1.2
) from similar expressions with
k^{\prime}<k
. It is presented in
2.4
and is of independent
interest. In some sense, it is an extension to general
k
of the work of

Bansaye, 2024
which focuses on
k=1
. From there, the proof of

1.1
comes down to studying the behavior of the population
size. We need to show that the population density remains close to its
equilibrium over time scales of order
K
, and estimate a Feynman–Kac
exponential term arising from the recursion in
2.4
.
This is the bulk of the technical work, which we achieve in

Section

3
using stochastic calculus. Finally, we compute the
limit of (
1.2
) in
Section

4
and show
how to deduce the convergence of the genealogy from that of
(
1.2
).

The moments of density-dependent branching processes

2.1
Encoding genealogies

Planar forests

This section introduces the topological objects and notation used in the
remainder of the study. We encode rooted planar forests using the
Ulam–Harris formalism,
i.e.
as subsets of
\mathcal{U}~=~\bigcup_{n\in\N}\N^{n}
. The set
\mathcal{U}
is endowed with the
partial order
\preccurlyeq
, such that for every
u,v\in\mathcal{U}

u\preccurlyeq v\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \exists w\in\bigcup_{n\in\N}\N^{n}\ \cup\ \varnothing,\ v=uw.

It is also endowed with the lexicographical order, which is a total order
that we denote by
\leqslant

A rooted planar forest

is a subset of
\mathcal{U}
verifying that

1.

there exists a number of roots
r\geqslant 1
such that
i\in F
if and only if
i\in[r]
;

2.

if
v\in F
, then
u\preccurlyeq v
implies that
u\in F
;

3.

for every
u\in F
, there exists
d_{u}\in\N
such that
ui\in F
iff
i\leqslant d_{u}
.

The unique parent of
u\notin\{i,\ i\leqslant r\}
is denoted by
\overset{\leftarrow}{u}
.

We recall from
Section

1.2
that our process takes values in the set
of

labelling, each particle is endowed with a birth time
\lambda_{u}

\lambda_{u}<\theta_{u}
and
\lambda_{u}=\theta_{\overset{\leftarrow}{u}}
. Recall from the introduction that we denote by

the set of labels of individuals alive in the population

the genealogical distance (coalescence

2.2
Martingale change of measure

Let
k\geqslant 1
and a time horizon
T>0
.
The forthcoming definition introduces a new population process. It will arise as an exponential change of measure of the original process and will serve in the initialization of our recursive moment formula (see
2.4
).

Definition 2.1
.

Consider the continuous-time Markov process taking values in
\{k,k+1,\dots\}
which has the following transitions:

•

n\ \longmapsto\ n\ +\ L^{(1)}(n/K)
at rate
k\cdot m(n/K)q(n/K)
, where
L^{(1)}(n/K)
has the size-biased offspring distribution, defined for all
z\in\R_{+}
as

\Prob\big(L^{(1)}(z)={n}\big)\ \coloneqq\ \frac{n}{m(z)}\Prob\big(L(z)=n\big).

(2.2)

•

n\ \longmapsto\ n\ +\ L(n/K)
at rate
(n-k)\cdot q(n/K)
.

This process can be interpreted as the population size in a particle model in which
k
distinguished particles reproduce at a modified rate
q\cdot m
, with progeny distributed according to the size-biasing of
L
. The remaining particles behave according to the same dynamics as the initial population model described in
Section

1.2
. The births from the distinguished particles can be thought of as an immigration into the population.

Assume that we start the process with
Z_{0}\geqslant k
particles and let

Z^{(i)}_{t}\ :=\ \#\{u\in\mathcal{N}_{t},\ u\succcurlyeq i\}

be the size at time
t
of the subfamily descending from the particle labelled
i
(using the Ulam–Harris–Neveu labelling). We identify
\Prob
with the distribution of the Markov process
\big(Z_{t},~Z^{(1)}_{t},~\dots,~Z^{(k)}_{t}\big)_{t}
– that is, the total mass of the initial particle system augmented from the size of the
k
subfamilies descending from the distinguished ancestral particles labelled
1
to
k
.

Lemma 2.2
(Martingale change of measure).

Let
k\geqslant 0
. Introduce the process

N^{k}_{t}\ :=\ \exp{\Big(-k\int_{0}^{t}q(Z^{K}_{s})\big(m(Z^{K}_{s})-1\big)ds\Big)}\prod_{i=1}^{k}Z^{(i)}_{t},

and
\mathbf{Q}^{k}
as the measure obtained through the Radon–Nikodym derivative:

\frac{d\mathbf{Q}^{k}}{d\Prob}\Big|_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}\ =\ N^{k}_{t}.

Assuming that
(Z_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
does not explode under
\mathbf{Q}^{k}
, the process
N^{k}_{t}

is a non-negative martingale. Moreover,
the distribution of the population size
(Z_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
under
\mathbf{Q}^{k}
is that described in
2.1
.

We write
\mathbf{Q}^{k}_{Z_{0}}[\,\cdot\,]
for the expectation under
\mathbf{Q}^{k}
, started from
Z_{0}
. Subsequently, assume that
(Z_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
does not explode under
\mathbf{Q}^{k}
.

Proof of
2.2
.

The case
k=1
has been treated by
Bansaye, 2024
. We let the reader convince themselves that following the same steps as in
Bansaye, 2024
, we can derive an extension to
k\geqslant 2
. In order to be self-contained, we will nonetheless explain the argument, leaving aside some technical issues. Denote by
\mathcal{F}_{t}\coloneqq\sigma\{Z_{s},s\leqslant t\}
the canonical filtration of
(Z_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
.
First, we check that the process
(N^{k}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is indeed a non-negative martingale for
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
.
The process
(N^{k}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
has finite variation and applying Itō’s formula it is not hard to see that for all
i\in[k]
,

N^{(i)}_{t}\ \coloneqq\ \exp{\Big(-\int_{0}^{t}q(Z^{K}_{s})\big(m(Z^{K}_{s})-1\big)ds\Big)}Z^{(i)}_{t}

is a non-negative local martingale.

Note that from the description of the process, two distinct particles do not reproduce at the same time. Hence, the processes
(N^{(i)})_{i\in[k]}
are orthogonal, meaning that their
quadratic covariations vanish. It follows that

N^{k}_{t}\ \coloneqq\ \prod_{i=1}^{k}N^{(i)}_{t}\ =\ \exp\Big(-k\int_{0}^{t}q(Z^{K}_{s})\big(m(Z^{K}_{s})-1\big)ds\Big)\prod_{i=1}^{k}Z^{(i)}_{t}

is also a non-negative local martingale. As we assumed non-explosion of the process under both measures, an adaptation of the arguments of
Bansaye, 2024
to
k\geqslant 2
yields that
N^{k}_{t}
is a true martingale and
\mathbf{Q}^{k}
is well-defined as a martingale change of measure.

We now identify the behavior of the population size under
\mathbf{Q}^{k}
. Let
h:\N^{k+1}\to\R_{+}
be the function

h:\big(z,z^{(1)},\dots,z^{(k)}\big)\ \longmapsto\ \prod_{i=1}^{k}z^{(i)}.

Applying the generator of the initial particle system to
h
,

\mathcal{G}h\big(z,z^{(1)},\dots,z^{(k)}\big)\ =\ kq(z/K)\big(m(z/K)-1\big)h\big(z,z^{(1)},\dots,z^{(k)}\big).

The process
N^{k}_{t}
is an exponential martingale for
h
in the sense that it can be written in the form

N^{k}_{t}\ =\ \frac{h\big(Z_{t},Z^{(1)}_{t},\dots,Z^{(k)}_{t}\big)}{h\big(Z_{0},Z^{(1)}_{0},\dots,Z^{(k)}_{0}\big)}\exp\bigg(-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\mathcal{G}h\big(Z_{s},Z^{(1)}_{s},\dots,Z^{(k)}_{s}\big)}{h\big(Z_{s},Z^{(1)}_{s},\dots,Z^{(k)}_{s}\big)}ds\bigg).

By Itō’s formula, a straightforward computation shows that
under
\mathbf{Q}^{k}
, the process
(Z_{t},Z^{(1)}_{t},\dots,Z^{(k)}_{t})_{t}
is a Markov process whose infinitesimal generator is given by

\tilde{\mathcal{G}}f\ =\ \frac{1}{h}\big[\mathcal{G}(f\cdot h)-f\cdot\mathcal{G}h\big],

(2.3)

for suitable functions
f:\N^{k+1}\to\R_{+}
(see
Palmowski and Rolski, 2002
for a similar expression).
Take a function of the first coordinate only belonging to the domain, which we denote by
f:(z,z^{(1)},\dots,z^{(k)})\longmapsto f(z)
. Then on the one hand we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{G}(f\cdot h)(z,z^{(1)},\dots,z^{(k)})}{h(z,z^{(1)},\dots,z^{(k)})}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad=\ \sum_{n\in\N}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Big[f(z+n-1)(z^{(i)}+n-1)-f(z)z^{(i)}\Big]\frac{\prod_{j\neq i}^{k}z^{(j)}}{\prod_{i=1}^{k}z^{(i)}}l_{n}(z/K)\cdot q(z/K)z^{(i)}

\displaystyle+\ \sum_{n\in\N}\Big[f(z+n-1)-f(z)\Big]l_{n}(z/K)\cdot q(z/K)\Big(z-\sum_{i=1}^{k}z^{(i)}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad=\ \sum_{n\in\N}\Big[f(z+n-1)-f(z)\Big]q(z/K)l_{n}(z/K)z+\sum_{n\in\N}f(z+n-1)(n-1)l_{n}(z/K)q(z/K)k,

where we used the notation
l_{n}(z/K)\coloneqq\Prob\big(L(z/K)=n\big)
.
On the other hand,

\frac{f(z-k,z^{(1)},\dots,z^{(k)})\cdot\mathcal{G}h(z-k,z^{(1)},\dots,z^{(k)})}{h(z-k,z^{(1)},\dots,z^{(k)})}\ =\ f(z)\sum_{n\in\N}(n-1)l_{n}(z/K)\cdot q(z/K)k.

Whence using (
2.3
) we obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{G}}f(z)\ =

\displaystyle\ \sum_{n\in\N}\Big[f(z+n-1)-f(z)\Big]q(z/K)l_{n}(z/K)z

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\ \sum_{n\in\N}\Big[f(z+n-1)-f(z)\Big](n-1)l_{n}(z/K)\cdot q(z/K)k

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \sum_{n\in\N}\Big[f(z+n-1)-f(z)\Big]l_{n}(z/K)\cdot q(z/K)(z-k)

\displaystyle+\ \sum_{n\in\N}\big[f(z+n-1)-f(z)\big]nl_{n}(z/K)\cdot q(z/K)k

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \sum_{n\in\N}\Big[f(z+n-1)-f(z)\Big]l_{n}\big(z/K\big)\cdot q(z/K)(z-k)

\displaystyle+\ \sum_{n\in\N}\Big[f(z+n-1)-f(z)\Big]\frac{n\cdot l_{n}(z/K)}{m(z/K)}\cdot q(z/K)m(z/K)k.

We recognize the expression of the infinitesimal generator of the Markov process whose transitions are given in
2.1
applied to
f
, which wraps up the proof.

2.3
The moment formula

Let
k\geqslant 1
,
\beta>0
,
t>0
and
Z_{0}\in\N
. We define the planar penalized
k
-moment measure
\mathbf{M}^{k,t}_{Z_{0}}
as the measure on
\mathbb{G}^{k}\times\R_{+}
satisfying that for
\varphi:\mathbb{G}^{k}\to\R_{+}
and
\psi:\R_{+}\to\R_{+}
two bounded and measurable functionals,

\mathbf{M}^{k,t}_{Z_{0}}(\varphi,\psi)\ \coloneqq\ k!\frac{\e^{\beta Z^{K}_{0}}}{\big(Z_{0}\big)_{k}}\E_{Z_{0}}\bigg[\psi\big(Z^{K}_{t}\big)\e^{-\beta Z^{K}_{t}}\sum_{\bm{v}:v_{1}<\cdots<v_{k}\in\mathcal{N}_{t}}\varphi\big(\Pi^{\bm{v}}\big)\bigg],

(2.4)

where
\Pi^{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{G}^{k}
is the planar coalescent spanned by the ordered
k
-sample
\bm{v}
and we used the notation
(X)_{d}=X(X-1)\cdots(X-d+1)
for
d
-th descending factorial of
X
. We note that this measure is finite, yet does not have unit total mass.

When
\varphi=\mathds{1}(\tau_{1}>t)
, that is the indicator that the planar coalescent does not have mergers, we will simply use the notation
\mathbf{M}^{k,t}_{Z_{0}}(\psi)
and call it the “base case” planar penalized
k
-moment measure subsequently.

Since we only assume that the offspring distribution has finite second moment, we use an exponential penalization with positive discount rate to recover finite higher moments. We introduce the so-called discounted moments below.

Definition 2.3
(Discounted factorial moments).

Let
\beta>0
,
d\in\N
.
We introduce
m_{d,\beta}(x,{z})
the
d
-th factorial moment of
L(z)
with exponential penalization, meaning that

m_{d,\beta}(z)\ \coloneqq\ \E\Big[\big(L(z)\big)_{d}\e^{-\beta L(z)}\Big].

Besides, we denote by
L^{(d)}_{\beta}(z)
the offspring distribution biased by its discounted
d
-th factorial moment at population density
z\in\R_{+}
. That is, for
{n}\in\N
we have

\Prob\big(L^{(d)}_{\beta}(z)={n}\big)\ \coloneqq\ \frac{(n)_{d}\cdot\e^{-\beta n}}{m_{d,\beta}(z)}\Prob\big(L(z)=n\big).

We point out that the distribution
L^{(d)}_{\beta}(z)
takes values in
\{d,d+1,\dots\}
. For
d=1
and
\beta=0
, we recover the usual size-biased offspring distribution
L^{(1)}(z)
from (
2.2
).

Theorem 2.4
(Induction for the moment measures).

Let
(i,d)\in\N^{2}

be such that
i+d-1\leqslant k
and
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}
two bounded measurable functions.
Consider the functional

\forall\Pi\in\mathbb{G}^{k},\ \ \varphi(\Pi)\ \coloneqq\ \mathds{1}\big(\tau_{1}<t,c_{1}=(i,d)\big)\varphi_{1}(\tau_{1})\varphi_{2}\big(\Theta_{\tau_{1}}(\Pi)\big),

(2.5)

where we recall
(c_{1},\tau_{1})
are as the first coalescence event and time of the planar coalescent
\Pi
, as defined in
Section

2.1
. Then taking
\psi:\R_{+}\to\R_{+}
bounded and measurable, the measure
\mathbf{M}^{k,t}_{Z_{0}}
verifies the following recursive formula:

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}^{k,t}_{Z_{0}}(\psi)\ =\ \e^{\beta Z^{K}_{0}}\mathbf{Q}^{k}_{Z_{0}}\bigg[\psi(Z^{K}_{t})\e^{-\beta Z^{K}_{t}}\exp\Big(k\int_{0}^{t}q(Z^{K}_{s})\big(m(Z^{K}_{s})-1\big)ds\Big)\bigg]

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}^{k,t}_{Z_{0}}(\varphi,\psi)\ =\ \frac{1}{k-d+1}\binom{k}{d}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\varphi_{1}(t-s)}{(Z_{0}-k+d-1)_{d-1}}\mathbf{M}^{k-d+1,s}_{Z_{0}}\Big(\varphi_{2},\,\mathcal{M}^{(k,d),t-s}_{\cdot}(\psi)\Big)ds,

(2.6)

where
\mathbf{Q}^{k}
is the distribution of the process with immigration of
2.1
. The function of the density
\mathcal{M}^{(k,d),t-s}_{\cdot}(\psi)
in the right-hand side is defined by

\mathcal{M}^{(k,d),t-s}_{\cdot}(\psi):z\longmapsto\ \mathcal{M}^{(k,d),t-s}_{z}(\psi)\ \coloneqq\ q(z)m_{d,\beta/K}(z)\e^{\beta/K}\E\Big[\mathbf{M}^{k,t-s}_{zK+L^{(d)}_{\beta/K}-1}(\psi)\Big],

(2.7)

recalling from
2.3
that
L^{(d)}_{\beta/K}(z)
the biased factorial distribution with discount parameter
\beta/K
satisfies

\Prob\big(L^{(d)}_{\beta/K}(z)=n\big)\ =\ \frac{(n)_{d}\cdot e^{-n\beta/K}}{m_{d,\beta/K}(z)}\Prob\big(L(z)=n\big).

In this article, we will say that the functionals verifying (
2.5
) are
of the product form
.

Remark 2.5
(Many-to-few).

The recursion from
2.4
can be viewed as a many-to-few formula.
One could expand this expression and obtain a very similar result to
(
Schertzer and Tourniaire, 2025
;
Foutel-Rodier et al., 2024
)
, in which the moment measure is directly computed from the law of a single uniform forest.
Since our limiting object is a coalescent process which is constructed backward in time, we chose to use a backward in time recursion, conversely to the forward in time one found in
Schertzer and Tourniaire, 2025
;
Foutel-Rodier et al., 2024
.

Remark 2.6
.

The same argument could be adapted to the genealogy of a wide range of population models, as it only relies on applying the Markov property at the last branch point in the genealogy. As an example, for the Moran model in which the population size is constant, it would directly show that its genealogy is Kingman’s coalescent.

Proof of
2.4
.

We proceed by induction on the number of branching points in the planar coalescent on
[k]
spanned by the sampled individuals. Thus, we start by determining the weight under
\mathbf{M}^{k,t}_{Z_{0}}
of planar coalescents on
[k]
with height
t
whose lineages do not coalesce in
(0,t)
(that is such that
\tau_{1}>t
) – which we will call the “base case” in the following. By definition, the planar coalescent spanned by
\bm{v}
has no mergers if and only if each individual in the sample descends from a distinct ancestors at time
t=0
.
Thus,

\sum_{\bm{v}:v_{1}<\cdots<v_{k}\in\mathcal{N}_{t}}\mathds{1}\big(\tau_{1}^{\bm{v}}>t\big)\ =\sum_{\bm{u}:u_{1}<\dots<u_{k}\in\mathcal{N}_{0}}\,\prod_{i=1}^{k}Z^{(u_{i})}_{t},

where
Z^{(u_{i})}_{t}=\#\big\{u\in\mathcal{N}_{t},\,u\succcurlyeq u_{i}\big\}
is the size at time
t
of the subfamily descending from
u_{i}\in\mathcal{N}_{0}
.
It follows from this observation and exchangeability of the initial particles that

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}^{k,t}_{Z_{0}}(\psi)

\displaystyle=\ k!\frac{\e^{\beta Z^{K}_{0}}}{(Z_{0})_{k}}\E_{Z_{0}}\bigg[\psi(Z^{K}_{t})\e^{-\beta Z^{K}_{t}}\sum_{\bm{v}:v_{1}<\cdots<v_{k}\in\mathcal{N}_{t}}\mathds{1}(\tau_{1}^{\bm{v}}>t)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\ \e^{\beta Z^{K}_{0}}\E_{Z_{0}}\bigg[\psi(Z^{K}_{t})\e^{-\beta Z^{K}_{t}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}Z^{(i)}_{t}\bigg],

where again the
(Z^{(i)}_{t})_{i}
are the respective sizes at time
t
of subfamilies descending from the ancestral particles labelled by
\{1,\dots,k\}
.
By
2.2
, for every functional
\Phi
we have that
\E_{Z_{0}}\big[\Phi(Z^{K}_{t})N^{k}_{t}\big]=\mathbf{Q}^{k}_{Z_{0}}\big[\Phi(Z^{K}_{t})\big]
.
It entails that

\displaystyle\E_{Z_{0}}\bigg[\psi(Z^{K}_{t})\e^{-\beta Z^{K}_{t}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}Z^{(i)}_{t}\bigg]\

\displaystyle=\ \E_{Z_{0}}\bigg[\psi(Z^{K}_{t})\e^{-\beta Z^{K}_{t}}\exp\Big(k\int_{0}^{t}q(Z^{K}_{s})\big(m(Z^{K}_{s})-1\big)ds\Big)N^{k}_{t}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\ \mathbf{Q}^{k}_{Z_{0}}\bigg[\psi(Z^{K}_{t})\e^{-\beta Z^{K}_{t}}\exp\Big(k\int_{0}^{t}q(Z^{K}_{s})\big(m(Z^{K}_{s})-1\big)ds\Big)\bigg],

which gives us the base case of the induction.

Assume now that (
2.6
) holds for all forests with
b^{\prime}<b
branching points and
k^{\prime}\leqslant k
leaves. Let us consider a functional
\varphi
of the product form given in (
2.5
). The strategy is to decompose the planar coalescent at the time
\tau^{\bm{v}}_{1}
of its first merger.
We then apply the induction hypothesis to:

•

On the one hand, the planar coalescent
\Theta_{\tau^{\bm{v}}_{1}}(\Pi^{\bm{v}})
corresponding to the genealogy of the sample up to the coalescence time
t-\tau^{\bm{v}}_{1}
, which has one fewer merger.

•

On the other hand, the “trivial” planar coalescent corresponding to the genealogy between times
t-\tau^{\bm{v}}_{1}
and
t
, which corresponds to the base case of the induction.

The product form of
\varphi
implies that this functional vanishes outside of planar coalescents
\Pi^{\bm{v}}
such that at the first coalescent event, particles labelled
v_{i},\dots,v_{i+d-1}
find their common ancestor. Consequently, summing over all
k
-samples
\bm{v}
at time
t
such that
c^{\bm{v}}_{1}=(i,d)
amounts to first picking a sample
\bm{u}
of size
k-d+1
, where the particle in the
i
-th position undergoes reproduction before time
t
. One must then select
d
particles among its offspring, and finally choose one particle at the present time from each of the subfamilies descending from these
d
particles, as well as from those descending from the remaining
k-d
particles
u_{j}
, for
j\neq i
. That is,

\sum_{\bm{v}:v_{1}<\dots<v_{k}\in\mathcal{N}_{t}}\mathds{1}\big(\tau_{1}^{\bm{v}}<t,c^{\bm{v}}_{1}=(i,d)\big)\varphi_{1}(\tau^{\bm{v}}_{1})\varphi_{2}\big(\Theta_{\tau_{1}}(\Pi^{\bm{v}})\big)\ =\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U},\theta_{u}<t}\varphi_{1}(t-\theta_{u})\\
\times\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{u}:u_{1}<\dots<u_{k-d+1}\in\mathcal{N}_{\theta_{u}^{-}}\\
u_{i}=u\end{subarray}}\varphi_{2}(\Pi^{\bm{u}})\sum_{w_{1}<\dots<w_{d}\leqslant\kappa_{u}}\prod_{j\in[d]}Z^{(uw_{j})}_{t}\prod_{j\in[k-d+1]\setminus\{i\}}Z^{(u_{j})}_{t}.

(2.8)

As before,
Z^{(u)}_{t}=\#\{v\in\mathcal{N}_{t},\,v\succcurlyeq u\}
refer to the size at
t
of the respective subfamilies descending from a given particle
u
. From there, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}^{k,t}_{Z_{0}}(\varphi,\psi)

\displaystyle=\ k!\frac{\e^{\beta Z^{K}_{0}}}{\big(Z_{0}\big)_{k}}\E_{Z_{0}}\bigg[\psi(Z^{K}_{t})\e^{-\beta Z^{K}_{T}}\sum_{\bm{v}:v_{1}<\dots<v_{k}\in\mathcal{N}_{t}}\mathds{1}\big(\tau_{1}^{\bm{v}}<t,c_{1}^{\bm{v}}=(i,d)\big)\varphi_{1}(\tau^{\bm{v}}_{1})\varphi_{2}\big(\Theta_{\tau_{1}}(\Pi^{\bm{v}})\big)\bigg]

\displaystyle\overset{(A)}{=}\ k!\frac{\e^{\beta Z^{K}_{0}}}{\big(Z_{0}\big)_{k}}\E_{Z_{0}}\bigg[\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U},\theta_{u}<t}\varphi_{1}(t-\theta_{u})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{u}:u_{1}<\dots<u_{k-d+1}\in\mathcal{N}_{\theta_{u}^{-}}\\
u_{i}=u\end{subarray}}\varphi_{2}(\Pi^{\bm{u}})

\displaystyle\times\sum_{w_{1}<\dots<w_{d}\leqslant\kappa(u)}\E\Big[\psi(Z^{K}_{t})\e^{-\beta Z^{K}_{T}}\prod_{p\in[d]}Z^{(uw_{p})}_{t}\ \cdot\prod_{j\neq i}Z^{(u_{j})}_{t}\ \big|\ Z_{\theta_{u}}\Big]\bigg]

\displaystyle\overset{(B)}{=}\ k!\frac{\e^{\beta Z^{K}_{0}}}{\big(Z_{0}\big)_{k}}\E_{Z_{0}}\bigg[\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U},\theta_{u}<t}\varphi_{1}(t-\theta_{u})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{u}:u_{1}<\dots<u_{k-d+1}\in\mathcal{N}_{\theta_{u}^{-}}\\
u_{i}=u\end{subarray}}\varphi_{2}(\Pi^{\bm{u}})

\displaystyle\times\sum_{w_{1}<\dots<w_{d}\leqslant\kappa_{u}}\e^{-\beta Z^{K}_{\theta_{u}}}\mathbf{M}_{Z_{\theta_{u}}}^{k,t-\theta_{u}}(\psi)\bigg]

\displaystyle\overset{(C)}{=}\ k!\frac{\e^{\beta Z^{K}_{0}}}{\big(Z_{0}\big)_{k}}\E_{Z_{0}}\bigg[\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U},\theta_{u}<t}\varphi_{1}(t-\theta_{u})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{u}:u_{1}<\dots<u_{k-d+1}\in\mathcal{N}_{\theta_{u}^{-}}\\
u_{i}=u\end{subarray}}\varphi_{2}(\Pi^{\bm{u}})\e^{-\beta Z^{K}_{\theta_{u}-}}

\displaystyle\times\ \frac{(\kappa_{u})_{d}}{d!}\frac{\e^{-(\kappa_{u}-1)\beta/K}}{m_{d,\beta/K}\big(Z^{K}_{\theta_{u}^{-}}\big)}m_{d,\beta/K}\big(Z^{K}_{\theta_{u}^{-}}\big)\mathbf{M}_{Z_{\theta_{u}-}+\kappa_{u}-1}^{k,t-\theta_{u}}(\psi)\bigg]

\displaystyle\overset{(D)}{=}\ \frac{k!}{d!}\frac{\e^{\beta Z^{K}_{0}}}{\big(Z_{0}\big)_{k}}\E_{Z_{0}}\bigg[\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U},\theta_{u}<t}\varphi_{1}(t-\theta_{u})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{u}:u_{1}<\dots<u_{k-d+1}\in\mathcal{N}_{\theta_{u}^{-}}\\
u_{i}=u\end{subarray}}\varphi_{2}(\Pi^{\bm{u}})\e^{-\beta Z^{K}_{\theta_{u}-}}

\displaystyle\times\ m_{d,\beta/K}(Z^{K}_{\theta_{u}^{-}})\e^{\beta/K}\E\Big[\mathbf{M}_{Z_{\theta_{u}-}+L^{(d)}_{\beta/K}(Z^{K}_{\theta_{u-}})-1}^{k,t-\theta_{u}}(\psi)\ \big|\ Z_{\theta_{u}}\Big]\bigg].

In the above, step
(A)
follows from the combinatorial identity (
2.8
) and conditioning at
\theta_{u}
,
(B)
from an application of the strong Markov property at time
\theta_{u}
and recognizing the base case of the induction over
(\theta_{u},t)
. To obtain
(C)
, we used that
Z_{\theta_{u}}=Z_{\theta_{u}^{-}}+\kappa_{u}-1
, ordered
d
-samples in the progeny of
u
and introduced the discounted factorial moment of order
d
. Finally,
(D)
follows from the fact that
\kappa_{u}
is independent of
\theta_{u}
conditionally upon
Z_{\theta_{u}^{-}}
. We also used that
\kappa_{u}
is distributed according to
L(Z^{K}_{\theta_{u}-})
, which we biased by
(\kappa_{u})_{d}\e^{-\beta\kappa_{u}/K}
by choosing
d
ordered distinguished lineages among the progeny.
The death time of each particle in
\mathcal{N}_{s}
occurs at the same Poisson rate
q(Z^{K}_{s})
. Therefore, compensating for the jumps we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}^{k,t}_{Z_{0}}(\varphi,\psi)

\displaystyle=\ \frac{k!}{d!}\e^{\beta Z^{K}_{0}}\frac{\big(Z_{0}\big)_{k-d+1}}{\big(Z_{0}\big)_{k}}\E_{Z_{0}}\bigg[\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}_{s}}q(Z^{K}_{s})\e^{-\beta Z^{K}_{s}}\frac{\varphi_{1}(t-s)}{(Z_{0})_{k-d+1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u_{1}<\dots<u_{k-d+1}\in\mathcal{N}_{s}\\
u_{i}=u\end{subarray}}\varphi_{2}(\Pi^{\bm{u}})

\displaystyle\times\ m_{d,\beta/K}(Z^{K}_{s})\e^{\beta/K}\,\E\Big[\mathbf{M}^{k,t-s}_{Z_{s}-1+L^{(d)}_{\beta/K}(Z^{K}_{s})}(\psi)\ \big|\ Z_{s}\Big]\bigg]ds

\displaystyle=\ \frac{k!}{d!}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\varphi_{1}(t-s)\e^{\beta Z^{K}_{0}}}{(Z_{0}-k+d-1)_{d-1}}\E_{Z_{0}}\bigg[\frac{\e^{-\beta Z^{K}_{s}}}{(Z_{0})_{k-d+1}}\mathcal{M}^{(k,d),t-s}_{Z^{K}_{s}}(\psi)\sum_{u_{1}<\dots<u_{k-d+1}\in\mathcal{N}_{s}}\varphi_{2}(\Pi^{\bm{u}})\bigg]

\displaystyle=\ \frac{1}{k-d+1}\binom{k}{d}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\varphi_{1}(t-s)}{(Z_{0}-k+d-1)_{d-1}}\mathbf{M}^{k-d+1,s}_{Z_{0}}\big(\varphi_{2},\mathcal{M}^{(k,d),t-s}_{\cdot}(\psi)\big)ds.

This yields (
2.6
) for planar chronological forests with
k
leaves and
d
branching points, thereby concluding the proof of the proposition.

3
Stochastic analysis of the population size

3.1
The population density under
\mathbf{Q}^{k}

We recall that
L^{(1)}(z)
is the size biased offspring distribution at
density
z
from
2.1
.

It is not hard to see from the transitions described earlier that under
\mathbf{Q}^{k}
, the density process
(Z^{K}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}=(Z_{t}/K)_{t\geqslant 0}
has the same
distribution as the solution to the following stochastic differential
equation:

\displaystyle Z^{K}_{t}-Z^{K}_{0}

\displaystyle=\ \int_{0}^{t}\int_{\R_{+}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{L(Z^{K}_{s^{-}},\xi)-1}{K}\mathds{1}\big(\varrho\leqslant q({Z}^{K}_{s^{-}})(Z_{s^{-}}-k)\big)\mathcal{Q}_{1}(dsd\varrho d\xi)

(3.1)

\displaystyle$\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\ \int_{0}^{t}\int_{\R_{+}}\int_{0}^{1} \frac{L^{(1)}(Z^{K}_{s^{-}},\xi)-1}{K} \mathds{1}\big(\varrho\leqslant k q(Z_{s^{-}}^{K}) m(Z^{K}_{s-})\big)\mathcal{Q}_{2}(dsd\varrho d\xi)$\ignorespaces

\displaystyle=\ \int_{0}^{t}\big({m}({Z}^{K}_{s})-1\big)q({Z}^{K}_{s}){Z}^{K}_{s}ds+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{k}{K}q({Z}^{K}_{s}){m}_{2}({Z}^{K}_{s})ds+W_{k}(t),

where
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
are independent Poisson processes on
\R_{+}\times\R_{+}\times(0,1)

(see
Ikeda and Watanabe, 1981
). Above,
(z,\xi)\longmapsto L(z,\xi)
and
(z,\xi)\longmapsto L^{(1)}(z,\xi)
are non-negative measurable functions chosen in such a way that if
U\sim\mbox{Unif}([0,1])
, the random variables
L(z,U)
and
L^{(1)}(z,U)
have the same distribution as
L(z)
and
L^{(1)}(z)
, respectively.

The process
W_{k}
in the last line of (
3.1
) comes from introducing the compensated measures
\bar{\mathcal{Q}}_{i}(dsd\varrho d\xi)=\mathcal{Q}_{i}(dsd\varrho d\xi)-dsd\varrho d\xi
for
i\in\{1,2\}
and using that
m(Z^{K}_{s})=\E[L(Z^{K}_{s})\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{s}]
and
m_{2}(Z^{K}_{s})=\E[L(Z^{K}_{s})(L(Z^{K}_{s})-1)\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{s}]
. It is defined by

W_{k}(t)\ =\ \int_{0}^{t}\int_{\R_{+}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{L(Z^{K}_{s^{-}},\xi)-1}{K}\mathds{1}\big(\varrho\leqslant q({Z}^{K}_{s-})({Z}_{s^{-}}-k)\big)\bar{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}(dsd\varrho d\xi)\\
+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\R_{+}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{L^{(1)}(Z^{K}_{s^{-}},\xi)-1}{K}\mathds{1}\big(\varrho\leqslant kq({Z}^{K}_{s^{-}})m({Z}^{K}_{s^{-}})\big)\bar{\mathcal{Q}}_{2}(dsd\varrho d\xi).

(3.2)

We note that taking
k=0
in (
3.1
), we recover the SDE solved by the original density process. Therefore it will be convenient to use the convention that
\Prob=\mathbf{Q}^{0}
, so that under
\mathbf{Q}^{0}

(Z^{K}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is distributed as the density process without immigration.

3.2
Convergence to the equilibrium density

Denote by
\mathscr{B}_{\gamma}\coloneqq\{z,\ |z-1|<\gamma\}
, and introduce the exit time

T_{\gamma}\ \coloneqq\ \inf\big\{t\geqslant 0\ |\ {Z}^{K}_{t}\notin\mathscr{B}_{\gamma}\big\}.

(3.3)

Proposition 3.1
(Convergence of the population density).

For every
T>0,\ Z^{K}_{0}\to 1
,
k\geqslant 0
and
\gamma>0
small enough, we have

\mathbf{Q}^{k}_{Z_{0}}\big(T_{\gamma}<KT\big)\ \underset{K\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\ 0.

3.1
gives that as
K\to\infty
, the rescaled density process
(Z^{K}_{Kt})_{t}
converges to one in probability under
\mathbf{Q}^{k}
, for every
k\geqslant 0
.
The proof of
3.1
relies on
3.3
. In turn, the proof of
3.3
uses the following result.

Lemma 3.2
.

Under Assumption
B
, there exists a sequence

(\Gamma_{K})_{K}
such that

\lim_{K\to\infty}\frac{\Gamma_{K}}{K}=0,\qquad\lim_{K\to\infty}\sup_{z\in(1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)}K^{2}\Prob(L(z)>\Gamma_{K})=0.

Proof.

For any
\eta>0
,

K^{2}\Prob(L(z)\geqslant\varepsilon K)\leqslant\frac{1}{\eta^{2}}\E\big[L(z)^{2}\mathds{1}_{\{L(z)\geqslant\eta K\}}\big].

Therefore, by (
1.1
),

\lim_{K\to\infty}\sup_{z\in(1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)}K^{2}\Prob(L(z)\geqslant\eta K)=0.

From that, one can extract a sequence
(\eta_{K})_{K}
such that

\eta_{K}\to 0
and

\lim_{K\to\infty}\sup_{z\in(1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)}K^{2}\Prob(L(z)\geqslant\eta_{K}K)=0.

The result follows by setting
\Gamma_{K}=\eta_{K}K
.

With this, we can now state the subsequent concentration result.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
and pick a sequence of capping parameters
(\Gamma_{K})_{K}
as in

3.2
. Then, for
\gamma
small enough, there exists a positive constant
C

such that we have

\mathbf{Q}^{k}\big(T_{\gamma}>T,\ Z_{T}^{K}\in\mathscr{B}_{(1-\delta)\gamma}\ |\ Z^{K}_{0}\in\mathscr{B}_{(1-\delta)\gamma}\big)\ \geqslant\ 1-\frac{C}{K}\Big(\frac{\Gamma_{K}}{K}\Big)^{2}.

Proof.

The proof relies on a modification of
(Z^{K}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
in which
the large birth events are removed and which is “frozen” upon
exiting a neighborhood of the equilibrium. Fix
\Gamma>0
. Let

(\bar{Z}^{K}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
be the solution to (
3.1
),
but with modified birth rate

\bar{q}:\begin{cases}\bar{q}(z)=q(z),\ z\in\mathscr{B}_{\gamma}\\
\bar{q}(z)=0,\ z\notin\mathscr{B}_{\gamma},\end{cases}

and modified offspring distributions

\bar{L}(z,\xi)\ =\ \mathds{1}_{\{L(z,\xi)\leqslant\Gamma\}}L(z,\xi)+\mathds{1}_{\{L(z,\xi)>\Gamma\}},\quad\bar{L}^{(1)}(z,\xi)=\mathds{1}_{\{L^{(1)}(z,\xi)\leqslant\Gamma\}}L^{(1)}(z,\xi)+\mathds{1}_{\{L^{(1)}(z,\xi)>\Gamma\}}.

Let

T^{\prime}_{\Gamma}\ =\ \inf\Big\{t>0:\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\R_{+}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathds{1}\big(L(Z_{s^{-}}^{K},\xi)>\Gamma,\varrho\leqslant(Z_{s-}-k)q(Z_{s-}^{K})\big)\mathcal{Q}_{1}(dsd\varrho d\xi)\\
+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\R_{+}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathds{1}\big(L_{1}(Z_{s^{-}}^{K},\xi)>\Gamma,\varrho\leqslant kq(Z_{s-}^{K})m(Z^{K}_{s^{-}})\big)\mathcal{Q}_{2}(dsd\varrho d\xi)>0\Big\}

be the first time when a birth/immigration event of more than
\Gamma

offspring occurs. Let
\bar{T}_{\gamma}
and
\bar{T}^{\prime}_{\Gamma}
be
defined as
T_{\gamma}
and
T^{\prime}_{\Gamma}
but using
(\bar{Z}^{K}_{t})_{t}
. It
should be clear that the two processes coincide until time
T_{\Gamma}\wedge T^{\prime}_{\gamma}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\Prob(T_{\gamma}>T)\

\displaystyle\geqslant\ \Prob(\bar{T}_{\gamma}>T,\bar{T}^{\prime}_{\Gamma}>T)

\displaystyle=\ \begin{multlined}\E\Big[\mathds{1}_{\{\bar{T}_{\gamma}>T\}}\exp\Big(-\int_{0}^{T}q(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s})(\bar{Z}_{s}-k)\Prob(L(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s})>\Gamma\mid\bar{Z}_{s})\\
+kq(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s})m(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s^{-}})\Prob(L^{(1)}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s})>\Gamma\mid\bar{Z}_{s})ds\Big)\Big]\end{multlined}

\displaystyle\geqslant\ \begin{multlined}\Prob(\bar{T}_{\gamma}>T)-T\Big((1+\varepsilon)K\sup_{z\in(1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)}q(z)\Prob(L(z)>\Gamma_{K})\\
+k\sup_{z\in(1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)}q(z)m(z)\Prob(L^{(1)}(z)>\Gamma_{K})\Big)\Big).\end{multlined}

By choosing
(\Gamma_{K})_{K}
as in Lemma
3.2
, the
result is proved if we can show that

\lim_{K\to\infty}K\Prob(\bar{T}_{\gamma}>T)\ =\ 0.

It will be convenient to introduce the notation

\bar{m}(z)=\E[\bar{L}(z)],\qquad\bar{m}_{2}(z)=\E[\bar{L}(z)(\bar{L}(z)-1)].

The process
(\bar{Z}^{K}_{t})_{t}
solves an SDE obtained by replacing
q,\ L
and
L^{(1)}
by
\bar{q},\ \bar{L}
and
\bar{L}^{(1)}
in (
3.1
).
By linearizing it around the equilibrium density, we have

\displaystyle d\bar{Z}^{K}_{t}\

\displaystyle=\ (\bar{m}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{t})-1)\bar{q}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{t})\bar{Z}^{K}_{t}dt+\frac{k}{K}\bar{q}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{t})\bar{m}_{2}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{t})dt+dW_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\ \begin{multlined}m^{\prime}(1)q(1)(\bar{Z}^{K}_{t}-1)dt+(\bar{m}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{t})-m(\bar{Z}^{K}_{t}))q(\bar{Z}^{K}_{t})\bar{Z}^{K}_{t}dt\\
+(\bar{Z}^{K}_{t}-1)R_{2}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{t}-1)dt+\frac{k}{K}\bar{q}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{t})\bar{m}_{2}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{t})dt+dW_{k}(t).\end{multlined}

where
xR_{2}(x)=\bar{q}(1+x)(m(1+x)-1)(1+x)-m^{\prime}(1)q(1)x
.
Then, applying Duhamel’s formula yields

\bar{Z}^{K}_{t}-1\ =\ \e^{m^{\prime}(1)q(1)t}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{0}-1)+\int_{0}^{t}\e^{m^{\prime}(1)q(1)(t-s)}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s}-1)R_{2}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s}-1)ds\\
+\ \int_{0}^{t}\e^{m^{\prime}(1)q(1)(t-s)}\bar{q}(\bar{Z}_{s}^{K})\big(\frac{k}{K}\bar{m}_{2}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s})+\bar{m}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{t})-m(\bar{Z}^{K}_{t})\big)ds\\
+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\R_{+}}\int_{0}^{1}\e^{m^{\prime}(1)q(1)(t-s)}\frac{\bar{L}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s^{-}},\xi)-1}{K}\mathds{1}\big(\varrho\leqslant(\bar{Z}_{s^{-}}-k)\bar{q}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s^{-}})\big)\bar{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}(dsd\varrho d\xi)\\
+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\R_{+}}\int_{0}^{1}\e^{m^{\prime}(1)q(1)(t-s)}\frac{\bar{L}^{(1)}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s^{-}},\xi)-1}{K}\mathds{1}\big(\varrho\leqslant k\bar{q}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s^{-}})\bar{m}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s^{-}})\big)\bar{\mathcal{Q}}_{2}(dsd\varrho d\xi).

(3.4)

We handle the various terms separately. We call
\widetilde{W}_{k}(t)
the sum of the last two terms.

First, note that
\bar{m}_{2}(z)\leqslant m_{2}(z)
and

m(z)-\bar{m}(z)\ \leqslant\ \E[L(z)\mathds{1}_{\{L(z)>\Gamma_{K}\}}]\ \leqslant\ \E[L(z)^{2}]^{1/2}\Prob(L(z)>\Gamma_{K})^{1/2}.

Therefore, by (
1.1
) there exists
C_{1}
such that, almost
surely,

\bar{q}(\bar{Z}_{t}^{K})\big|\frac{k}{K}\bar{m}_{2}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{t})+\bar{m}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{t})-m(\bar{Z}^{K}_{t})\big|\ \leqslant\ \frac{C_{1}}{K}.

In turn, this entails that one can find
C_{2}>1
such that, for
K

large enough,

\e^{m^{\prime}(1)q(1)t}\lvert\bar{Z}^{K}_{0}-1\rvert+\int_{0}^{t}\e^{m^{\prime}(1)q(1)(t-s)}\bar{q}(\bar{Z}_{s}^{K})\Big|\frac{k}{K}\bar{m}_{2}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s})+\bar{m}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{t})-m(\bar{Z}^{K}_{t})\Big|ds\ \leqslant\ \frac{(1-\delta)}{C_{2}}\gamma.

Second, we control the martingale terms using a version of the
Burkhölder–Davis–Gundy inequality from

Hernández-Hernández and Jacka, 2022
.
This requires us to control the moments of its predictable quadratic
variation
\big(\langle\widetilde{W}_{k},\widetilde{W}_{k}\rangle_{t}\big)_{t\geqslant 0}
as well
as the compensator of
(\sum_{s\leqslant t}\lvert\Delta\widetilde{W}_{k}(s)\rvert^{2p})_{t\geqslant 0}
, for some
p>1
. For
p=2
, this
latter compensator is

\displaystyle A_{t}

\displaystyle=\begin{multlined}\frac{1}{K^{4}}\int_{0}^{t}\e^{-4m^{\prime}(1)q(1)s}\E\Big[\big(\bar{L}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s})-1\big)^{4}\ |\ \bar{Z}_{s}\Big]\bar{q}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s})(Z^{K}_{s}-k)ds\\
+\frac{1}{K^{4}}\int_{0}^{t}\e^{-4m^{\prime}(1)q(1)s}\E\Big[\big(\bar{L}^{(1)}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s})-1\big)^{4}\ |\ \bar{Z}_{s}\Big]k\bar{m}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s})\bar{q}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s})ds\end{multlined}

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \begin{multlined}\Big(\frac{\Gamma_{K}}{K}\Big)^{2}\frac{1}{K}\int_{0}^{t}\e^{-4m^{\prime}(1)q(1)s}\E\big[L(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s})^{2}\ |\ \bar{Z}_{s}\big]\bar{q}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s})\big(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s}-\tfrac{k}{K}\big)ds\\
+\Big(\frac{\Gamma_{K}}{K}\Big)^{3}\frac{1}{K}\int_{0}^{t}\e^{-4m^{\prime}(1)q(1)s}\E\big[L(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s})^{2}\ |\ \bar{Z}_{s}\big]k\bar{m}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s})\bar{q}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s})ds\end{multlined}

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \Big(\frac{\Gamma_{K}}{K}\Big)^{2}\frac{C_{3}}{K},

for some
C_{3}>0
. Similarly,

\displaystyle\langle\widetilde{W}_{k},\widetilde{W}_{k}\rangle_{t}\

\displaystyle=\ \begin{multlined}\frac{1}{K}\int_{0}^{t}\e^{-2m^{\prime}(1)q(1)s}\E\Big[\big(\bar{L}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s})-1\big)^{2}\ |\ \bar{Z}^{K}_{s}\Big]\bar{q}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s})\big(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s}-\tfrac{k}{K}\big)ds\\
+\frac{1}{K^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\e^{-2m^{\prime}(1)q(1)s}\E\Big[\big(\bar{L}^{(1)}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s})-1\big)^{2}\ |\ \bar{Z}^{K}_{s}\Big]\bar{q}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s})kds.\end{multlined}

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \frac{C_{4}}{K},

for some
C_{4}>0
.
Using Markov’s inequality coupled to
Hernández-Hernández and Jacka, 2022
, there exist
C_{5}

and
C_{6}
such that for all
\eta>0
,

\displaystyle\Prob\Big(\sup_{t\leqslant T}\ \lvert e^{m^{\prime}(1)q(1)t}\widetilde{W}_{k}(t)\rvert\geqslant\frac{\eta}{C_{2}}(1-\delta)\gamma\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \Prob\Big(\sup_{t\leqslant T}\ \lvert\widetilde{W}_{k}(t)\rvert\geqslant\frac{\eta}{C_{2}}(1-\delta)\gamma\Big)

(3.5)

\displaystyle\leqslant\ C_{5}\E\Big[\langle\widetilde{W}_{k},\widetilde{W}_{k}\rangle_{T}^{2}+A_{T}\Big]

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \frac{C_{6}}{K}\Big(\frac{\Gamma_{K}}{K}\Big)^{2}.

Finally, going back to (
3.4
) and working on the event that

\sup_{t\leqslant T}\ \lvert e^{m^{\prime}(1)q(1)t}\widetilde{W}^{\prime}_{k}(t)\rvert\geqslant\frac{\eta}{C_{2}}(1-\delta)\gamma
, the first two steps above show that

\lvert\bar{Z}^{K}_{t}-1\rvert\ \leqslant\ \frac{1+\eta}{C_{2}}(1-\delta)\gamma\ +\ \int_{0}^{t}\lvert\bar{Z}^{K}_{s}-1\rvert\big\lvert R_{2}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{s}-1)\big\rvert ds.

Moreover, by definition of
R_{2}

\lvert R_{2}(x)\rvert\ \leqslant\ \lVert(m-1)\bar{q}\rVert_{\infty}\frac{1+\gamma}{1-\gamma}-m^{\prime}(1)q(1),

and
\lim_{x\to 0}R_{2}(x)=0
. Therefore, by Grönwall’s inequality,
for
t\leqslant T

\lvert\bar{Z}^{K}_{t}-1\rvert\ \leqslant\ \frac{1+\eta}{C_{2}}(1-\delta)\gamma\e^{\lVert R_{2}\rVert T},

and choosing
\gamma
small enough we can assume
that
\lvert R_{2}(\bar{Z}^{K}_{t}-1)\rvert<\eta
. Another application of
Grönwall’s inequality finally proves that, for
t\leqslant T
,

\lvert\bar{Z}^{K}_{t}-1\rvert\ \leqslant\ \frac{1+\eta}{C_{2}}\e^{\eta T}(1-\delta)\gamma.

Choosing
\eta
small enough to have
\frac{1+\eta}{C_{2}}\e^{\eta T}<1
concludes the proof of the lemma.

The proof of
3.1
then consists in iterating
3.3
over
K
intervals of time with fixed length
T
, with the help of the Markov property. We point out that this proof uses the same idea as in a recent preprint from
Forien et al., 2025
.

Proof of
3.1
.

For
i\leqslant K
we denote by
T_{i}\coloneqq iT
and look at the sequence of
event
(\mathcal{A}_{j})_{j}
growing for the inclusion introduced below

\mathcal{A}_{j}\ \coloneqq\ \bigcap_{i\leqslant j}\Big(\big\{\bar{Z}^{K}_{t}\in\mathscr{B}_{\gamma},\ \forall t\in[T_{i-1},T_{i}]\big\}\cap\big\{\bar{Z}^{K}_{T_{i}}\in\mathscr{B}_{(1-\delta)\gamma}\big\}\Big).

Using
3.3
and the union bound,

\displaystyle\Prob\big(\mathcal{A}_{1}^{c}\big)\

\displaystyle\leqslant\ 2\Prob\big(T_{\gamma}<T\big)\ +\ \Prob\big(\bar{Z}^{K}_{T_{i}}\in\mathscr{B}_{(1-\delta)\gamma},\ T_{\gamma}>T\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\ 3\frac{C_{1}}{K}\Big(\frac{\Gamma_{K}}{K}\Big)^{2}.

In addition,
\forall i\in\{2,\dots,K\}
we use the Markov property to derive that

\displaystyle\Prob\big(\mathcal{A}_{i+1}^{c}\big)\

\displaystyle=\ \Prob\big(\mathcal{A}_{i+1}^{c}\cap\mathcal{A}_{i}\big)+\Prob\big(\mathcal{A}_{i}^{c}\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \Prob\big(\mathcal{A}_{i+1}^{c}\ |\ \mathcal{A}_{i}\big)+\Prob\big(\mathcal{A}_{i}^{c}\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \Prob\big(\mathcal{A}_{1}^{c}\big)+\Prob\big(\mathcal{A}_{i}^{c}\big).

Hence by a short induction,
\forall j
,
\Prob\big(\mathcal{A}^{c}_{j}\big)\leqslant 3jK^{-1}(\Gamma_{K}/K)^{2},
and by inclusion we have that

\Prob\Big(T_{\gamma}<KT\ \big|\ \bar{Z}^{K}_{0}\in\mathscr{B}_{(1-\delta)\gamma}\Big)\ \leqslant\ \Prob\big(\mathcal{A}^{c}_{K}\big)\ \leqslant\ 3C_{1}\Big(\frac{\Gamma_{K}}{K}\Big)^{2}\underset{K\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.

3.3
Convergence to Brownian motion

The main ingredient coming into play to identify the Kingman coalescent is to estimate the exponential term in the base case of the moment formula in
2.4
through a study of the population density process. Here we give some estimates on the population density process
Z^{K}
. We recall from (
3.1
) the semimartingale decomposition of the population density under the measure
\mathbf{Q}^{k}
.
As have been shown in
3.1
, the population density converges in probability to
1
and up to stopping the processes at the exit time of the boundary we can assume null reproduction rates
q
when the population density lies outside of a macroscopic ball
\mathscr{B}_{\gamma}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Fix
k\geqslant 0
and recall from (
3.2
) the martingale part
(W_{k}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
of the density process under
\mathbf{Q}^{k}
.
Then under
\mathbf{Q}^{k}
,

\big({W}_{k}(Kt)\big)_{t\geqslant 0}\ \underset{K\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\ \big(\sqrt{q(1)m_{2}(1)}B_{t}\big)_{t\geqslant 0},

in distribution for the topology of uniform convergence on compact sets and where
B
is a standard Brownian motion. Moreover, the quadratic variation process
\big([W_{k},W_{k}]_{Kt}\big)_{t\geqslant 0}\coloneqq\big(\sum_{s\leqslant Kt}|\Delta W_{k}(s)|^{2}\big)_{t\geqslant 0}
converges in probability to
\big(q(1)m_{2}(1)t\big)_{t\geqslant 0}
as
K\to\infty
.

Proof.

This proof seeks to establish control over the quadratic variation in order to apply the martingale central limit theorem of
Jacod and Shiryaev, 2003
. However, as previously noted, under our assumptions
W_{k}
does not have finite quadratic variation.
Thus, we decompose this process into three martingale parts according to the size of the jumps. This way, we will show that two of three have finite quadratic variation, and that the third one – corresponding to the large jumps – converges in probability to 0. Based on this premise, we recall
\Gamma_{K}
from
3.2
and
express the process
(W_{k})
as the sum of three martingales, namely

W_{k}(t)\ =\ W^{\prime}_{k}(t)+W^{\prime\prime}_{k}(t)+W^{\prime\prime\prime}_{k}(t),

where

\displaystyle W^{\prime}_{k}(t)\ =\ \int_{0}^{t}\int_{\R_{+}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{L(Z^{K}_{s^{-}},\xi)-1}{K}\mathds{1}\big(L(Z^{K}_{s^{-}},\xi)\leqslant\Gamma_{K}\big)\mathds{1}\big(\varrho\leqslant q({Z}^{K}_{s-})({Z}_{s^{-}}-k)\big)\bar{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}(dsd\varrho d\xi)

\displaystyle W^{\prime\prime}_{k}(t)\ =\ \int_{0}^{t}\int_{\R_{+}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{L^{(1)}(Z^{K}_{s^{-}},\xi)-1}{K}\mathds{1}\big(L^{(1)}(Z^{K}_{s^{-}},\xi)\leqslant\Gamma_{K}\big)\mathds{1}\big(\varrho\leqslant kq({Z}^{K}_{s^{-}})m({Z}^{K}_{s^{-}})\big)\bar{\mathcal{Q}}_{2}(dsd\varrho d\xi)

\displaystyle W^{\prime\prime\prime}_{k}(t)\ =\ W_{k}(t)-W^{\prime}_{k}(t)-W^{\prime\prime}_{k}(t).

We recall from
3.1
that we work with a population model whose reproduction rates verify
q(z)=0
for
z\notin\mathscr{B}_{\gamma}
. This entails that the martingales
W^{\prime}_{k}
and
W^{\prime\prime}_{k}
are square-integrable. Re-scaling time by
K
, the predictable angle bracket of
W^{\prime}_{k}
verifies the following:

\langle W^{\prime}_{k},W^{\prime}_{k}\rangle_{Kt}\ =\ \int_{0}^{t}\big(Z^{K}_{Ks}-k/K)q(Z^{K}_{Ks}\big)\big(m_{2}(Z^{K}_{Ks})-m({Z}^{K}_{Ks})+1\big)\Prob\big(L(Z^{K}_{s^{-}})\leqslant\Gamma_{K}|Z^{K}_{s^{-}}\big)ds.

By
3.1
and Skorohod’s representation theorem, we can assume that the random variable
Z_{Ks}^{K}\to 1
uniformly over
(0,t)
and almost-surely. Using the Markov inequality,
3.2
and that
q,\,m
and
m_{2}
are continuous at
1
, the integrand converges uniformly in
s
to
q(1)m_{2}(1)
. Thus, the angle bracket process
\langle W^{\prime}_{k},W^{\prime}_{k}\rangle_{Kt}
converges to
q(1)m_{2}(1)t
almost-surely.

Besides, the predictable bracket of
W^{\prime\prime}_{k}
can be upper-bounded as below:

\displaystyle\langle W^{\prime\prime}_{k},W^{\prime\prime}_{k}\rangle_{tK}\

\displaystyle=\ \int_{0}^{tK}\frac{\E\big[(L^{(1)}(Z^{K}_{s})-1)^{2}\mathds{1}_{L^{(1)}(Z^{K}_{s})\leqslant\Gamma_{K}}\big]}{K^{2}}kq(Z^{K}_{s})m(Z^{K}_{s})ds

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \int_{0}^{tK}\frac{\E\big[(L^{(1)}(Z^{K}_{s})-1)\big]\Gamma_{K}}{K^{2}}kq(Z^{K}_{s})m(Z^{K}_{s})ds

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \int_{0}^{tK}q(Z^{K}_{s})\frac{m_{2}(Z^{K}_{s})}{K}\frac{\Gamma_{K}}{K}ds,

which converges in probability to zero, since
\Gamma_{K}=o(K)
.

Moreover by the second moment assumption and
3.2
, the jump sizes of
W^{\prime}_{k}+W^{\prime\prime}_{k}
converge in probability to zero as
K\to\infty
. It stems from the martingale central limit theorem stated in
Jacod and Shiryaev, 2003
that the sum
\big(W^{\prime}_{k}(Kt)+W^{\prime\prime}_{k}(Kt)\big)_{t}
converges in distribution to the Brownian motion with quadratic veriation
q(1)m_{2}(1)
as
K\to\infty
. To wrap up the proof of this lemma, it therefore remains to show that the last term
W^{\prime\prime\prime}_{k}
vanishes. By definition of the compensated Poisson measure,
W^{\prime\prime\prime}_{k}
is the difference between a jump process driven by
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
and a finite variation part:

W^{\prime\prime\prime}_{k}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\R_{+}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{L(Z^{K}_{s^{-}},\xi)-1}{K}\mathds{1}\big(L(Z^{K}_{s^{-}},\xi)>\Gamma_{K}\big)\mathds{1}\big(\varrho\leqslant({Z}_{s^{-}}-k)q({Z}^{K}_{s^{-}})m({Z}^{K}_{s-})\big)\mathcal{Q}_{1}(dsd\varrho d\xi)\\
-\int_{0}^{t}\big({Z}^{K}_{s^{-}}-\frac{k}{K}\big)q({Z}^{K}_{s^{-}})m({Z}^{K}_{s^{-}})\E\Big[\big(L(Z^{K}_{s^{-}})-1\big)\mathds{1}\big(L(Z^{K}_{s^{-}})>\Gamma_{K}\big)\ \big|\ Z_{s^{-}}\Big]ds\\
+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\R_{+}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{L^{(1)}(Z^{K}_{s^{-}},\xi)-1}{K}\mathds{1}\big(L^{(1)}(Z^{K}_{s^{-}},\xi)>\Gamma_{K}\big)\mathds{1}\big(\varrho\leqslant kq({Z}^{K}_{s^{-}})m({Z}^{K}_{s-})\big)\mathcal{Q}_{2}(dsd\varrho d\xi)\\
-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{k}{K}q({Z}^{K}_{s^{-}})m({Z}^{K}_{s^{-}})\E\Big[\big(L^{(1)}(Z^{K}_{s^{-}})-1\big)\mathds{1}\big(L^{(1)}(Z^{K}_{s^{-}})>\Gamma_{K}\big)\ \big|\ Z_{s^{-}}\Big]ds.

The number of jumps
N^{\prime\prime\prime}_{k}
of
W^{\prime\prime\prime}_{k}
verifies the following

\displaystyle\E\big[N^{\prime\prime\prime}_{k}(Kt)\big]

\displaystyle=\ \E\Big[\int_{0}^{Kt}(Z_{s}-k)q(Z^{K}_{s})\Prob\big(L(Z^{K}_{s})>\Gamma_{K}\ |\ Z_{s}\big)ds\Big]

\displaystyle$\displaystyle\qquad+\ \E\Big[\int_{0}^{Kt}k q(Z^{K}_{s})m(Z^{K}_{s}) \Prob\big(L^{(1)}(Z^{K}_{s})> \Gamma_{K}\ |\ Z_{s}\big)ds\Big]$\ignorespaces

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \sup_{z\in\mathscr{B}_{\gamma}}\big(zq(z)\big)\cdot t\sup_{z\in\mathscr{B}_{\gamma}}\Big(K^{2}\Prob\big(L(z)>\Gamma_{K}\big)\Big)

\displaystyle$\displaystyle+ k\sup_{z\in\mathscr{B}_{\gamma}}\big(q(z)m(z)\big)
\E\Big[\int_{0}^{Kt}\Prob\big(L^{2}(Z^{K}_{s})> \Gamma_{K} |Z_{s} \big)ds \Big]$\ignorespaces

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \sup_{z\in\mathscr{B}_{\gamma}}\big(q(z)(km(z)+z)\big)\cdot t\sup_{z\in\mathscr{B}_{\gamma}}\Big(K^{2}\Prob\big(L(z)>\Gamma_{K}\big)\Big)\underset{K\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\ 0,

where we used
3.2
to conclude, for
\gamma
smaller than
\varepsilon
.
The same computation gives that the expectation of the finite variation part vanishes as
K\to\infty
.
As a result, the process
\big(W^{\prime\prime\prime}_{k}(Kt)\big)_{t}
converges in probability to zero. The convergence in distribution under
\mathbf{Q}^{k}
for every
k\geqslant 0
stated in the lemma eventually follows from the Slutsky lemma.

3.4
Estimation of the Feynman-Kac term

This section is dedicated to establishing the following result for the so-called base case in the moment induction formula stated in
2.4
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Take
k\geqslant 0
,
t\in(0,T)
and
\psi
continuous at 1. Then we have for any initial population size
Z_{0}
satisfying
Z^{K}_{0}=Z_{0}/K\to 1
that as
K\to\infty
,

\lim_{K\to\infty}\e^{\beta Z^{K}_{0}}\mathbf{Q}^{k}_{Z_{0}}\bigg[\psi\big(Z^{K}_{Kt}\big)\e^{-\beta Z^{K}_{Kt}}\exp\Big(\int_{0}^{Kt}kq({Z}^{K}_{s})({m}({Z}^{K}_{s})-1)ds\Big)\bigg]=\psi(1)\exp\Big(-q(1)m_{2}(1)\Bigl(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{2}$}}\Bigr)t\Big).

Proof.

We recall from (
3.1
) that under
\mathbf{Q}^{k}_{Z_{0}}
, the density process
({Z}^{K}_{t})
is solution to

d{Z}^{K}_{s}\ =\ \big({m}({Z}^{K}_{s})-1\big)q({Z}^{K}_{s})\big({Z}^{K}_{s}-k/K\big)ds+{m}_{2}({Z}^{K}_{s})q({Z}^{K}_{s})\frac{k}{K}ds+dW_{k}(s),

where
W_{k}(s)
is the stochastic integral which appeared in (
3.1
).
Applying Itō’s formula to the logarithm function (see
e.g.

Protter, 2005
) we have

d(\log{Z}^{K}_{s})\ =\ \big({m}({Z}^{K}_{s})-1\big)q({Z}^{K}_{s})\Big(1-\frac{k}{K{Z}^{K}_{s}}\Big)ds+{m}_{2}({Z}^{K}_{s})q({Z}^{K}_{s})\frac{k}{K{Z}^{K}_{s}}ds\\
+\frac{dW_{k}(s)}{{Z}^{K}_{s^{-}}}+\bigg[\log\Big(1+\frac{\Delta{Z}^{K}_{s^{-}}}{{Z}^{K}_{s^{-}}}\Big)-\Big(\frac{\Delta{Z}^{K}_{s^{-}}}{{Z}^{K}_{s^{-}}}\Big)\bigg].

Then, integrating over time
Kt
we obtain that

\int_{0}^{Kt}q({Z}^{K}_{s})\big({m}({Z}^{K}_{s})-1\big)ds\ =\ \log\Big(\frac{{Z}^{K}_{KT}}{{Z}^{K}_{0}}\Big)+\int_{0}^{Kt}({m}({Z}^{K}_{s})-1)q({Z}^{K}_{s})\frac{k}{K{Z}^{K}_{s}}ds\\
-\int_{0}^{Kt}{m}_{2}({Z}^{K}_{s})q({Z}^{K}_{s})\frac{k}{K{Z}^{K}_{s}}ds-\int_{0}^{Kt}\frac{1}{{Z}^{K}_{s^{-}}}dW_{k}(s)\\
+\sum_{s\leqslant KT}\bigg[\Big(\frac{\Delta{Z}^{K}_{s^{-}}}{{Z}^{K}_{s^{-}}}\Big)-\log\Big(1+\frac{\Delta{Z}^{K}_{s^{-}}}{{Z}^{K}_{s^{-}}}\Big)\bigg].

Define the remainder
\varepsilon(Kt)
such that

\int_{0}^{Kt}q({Z}^{K}_{s})({m}({Z}^{K}_{s})-1)ds\ =\ -kq(1)m_{2}(1)t-W_{k}(Kt)+\frac{1}{2}\big[W_{k},W_{k}\big]_{Kt}+\varepsilon(Kt).

(3.6)

We will show in the subsequent lemma that the remainder verifies
\varepsilon(Kt)\to 0
in probability as
K\to\infty
and assume that
3.6
holds within this proof.

By
3.4
, the process
\big([W_{k},W_{k}]_{Kt}\big)_{t\geqslant 0}
converges in probability to
\big(q(1)m_{2}(1)t\big)_{t\geqslant 0}
.
Along with Slutsky’s theorem and
3.6
, this shows that as
K\to\infty
,

\int_{0}^{Kt}q({Z}^{K}_{s})({m}({Z}^{K}_{s})-1)ds\ \longrightarrow\ q(1)m_{2}(1),

in distribution under
\mathbf{Q}^{k}
.
Multiplying the integral by
k
and taking the exponential we obtain furthermore that

\exp\Big(\int_{0}^{Kt}kq({Z}^{K}_{s})\big({m}({Z}^{K}_{s})-1\big)ds\Big)\ \underset{K\to\infty}{\overset{(d)}{\longrightarrow}}\ \exp\Big(-q(1)m_{2}(1)\Bigl(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{2}$}}\Bigr)t\Big)\,\mathcal{E}_{t}.

(3.7)

where
\mathcal{E}_{t}=\exp\big(B_{q(1)m_{2}(1)t}-q(1)m_{2}(1)t^{2}/2\Big)
is the exponential martingale of the Brownian motion with variance
q(1)m_{2}(1)
, and the convergence in distribution should as before be understood as under
\mathbf{Q}^{k}
.

Then, we use that the population density converges to
1
in probability and that
\psi
is continuous at
1
to obtain with an application of Slutsky’s theorem the convergence of the random variable
\psi(Z^{K}_{Kt})\e^{\beta(Z^{K}_{0}-Z^{K}_{Kt})}
towards
\psi(1)
in distribution under
\mathbf{Q}^{k}
.

In order to obtain convergence of the expected value, we need some additional uniform integrability. Let
M>0
. Reverting the change of measure and using the exchangeability of the initial particles leads to

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{k}_{Z_{0}}\bigg[\frac{\psi\big(Z^{K}_{Kt}\big)}{\e^{\beta Z^{K}_{Kt}}}

\displaystyle\exp\Big(\int_{0}^{Kt}kq({Z}^{K}_{s})({m}({Z}^{K}_{s})-1)ds\Big)\ \mathds{1}\Big(\exp\Big(\int_{0}^{Kt}kq({Z}^{K}_{s})({m}({Z}^{K}_{s})-1)ds\Big)>M\Big)\bigg]

\displaystyle\ \ =\ \E_{Z_{0}}\bigg[\psi\big(Z^{K}_{Kt}\big)\e^{-\beta Z^{K}_{Kt}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}Z^{(i)}_{Kt}\cdot\mathds{1}\Big(\exp\Big(\int_{0}^{Kt}kq({Z}^{K}_{s})({m}({Z}^{K}_{s})-1)ds\Big)>M\Big)\bigg]

\displaystyle\ \ \leqslant\ \E_{Z_{0}}\bigg[\psi\big(Z^{K}_{Kt}\big)\e^{-\beta Z^{K}_{Kt}}(Z^{K}_{Kt})^{k}\ \mathds{1}\Big(\exp\Big(\int_{0}^{Kt}kq({Z}^{K}_{s})({m}({Z}^{K}_{s})-1)ds\Big)>M\Big)\bigg]

\displaystyle\ \ \leqslant\ \frac{\beta^{k}}{\e}\lVert\psi\rVert_{\infty}\Prob_{Z_{0}}\bigg(\exp\Big(\int_{0}^{Kt}kq({Z}^{K}_{s})({m}({Z}^{K}_{s})-1)ds\Big)>M\bigg).

The convergence in distribution of the random variable
\exp\big(\int_{0}^{Kt}kq({Z}^{K}_{s})({m}({Z}^{K}_{s})-1)ds\big)
under
\Prob=\mathbf{Q}^{0}

derived in the above implies its tightness. Hence the upper bound vanishes as
M\to\infty
, uniformly in
K
.

Lemma 3.6
.

For all
k\geqslant 0
, the remainder term
\sup_{t\leqslant T}\varepsilon(Kt)
introduced in (
3.6
) converges in probability to zero under
\mathbf{Q}^{k}
, as
K\to\infty
.

Proof.

From the definition of
\varepsilon(Kt)
we have

|\varepsilon(Kt)|\ \leqslant\ \underbrace{\Big\lvert\log\Big(\frac{{Z}^{K}_{Kt}}{{Z}^{K}_{0}}\Big)\Big\rvert}_{A}\ +\ \underbrace{\frac{1}{K}\int_{0}^{Kt}\Big\lvert({m}({Z}^{K}_{s})-1)q({Z}^{K}_{s})\frac{k}{{Z}^{K}_{s}}\Big\rvert}_{B}ds\\
+\ \underbrace{\int_{0}^{Kt}\frac{k}{K}\Big\lvert\frac{{m}_{2}({Z}^{K}_{s})q({Z}^{K}_{s})}{{Z}^{K}_{s}}-q(1)m_{2}(1)\Big\rvert}_{C}ds\ +\ \underbrace{\Big\lvert\int_{0}^{Kt}\Big[1-\frac{1}{{Z}^{K}_{s^{-}}}\Big]dW_{k}(s)\Big\rvert}_{D}\\
+\ \underbrace{\Big\lvert\sum_{s\leqslant Kt}\log\Big(1+\frac{\Delta{Z}^{K}_{s^{-}}}{{Z}^{K}_{s^{-}}}\Big)-\Big(\frac{\Delta{Z}^{K}_{s^{-}}}{{Z}^{K}_{s^{-}}}\Big)+\frac{1}{2}\big(\Delta{Z}^{K}_{s^{-}}\big)^{2}\Big\rvert}_{E}.

Using the Skorohod representation theorem, we assume as before that the convergence
Z^{K}_{tK}\to 1
holds almost-surely. Then by continuity of
m
,
m_{2}
and
q
at
1
we directly have that
A,\,B
and
C
converge to
0
almost-surely.
A Taylor series expansion of the logarithm to the second order yields

\displaystyle E\

\displaystyle=\ \bigg\lvert\sum_{s\leqslant KT}\big(\Delta{Z}^{K}_{s^{-}}\big)^{2}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\Big(1-\frac{1}{({Z}^{K}_{s^{-}})^{2}}\Big)\ +\ \frac{1}{({Z}^{K}_{s^{-}})^{2}}R_{\log}\Big(\frac{\Delta{Z}^{K}_{s^{-}}}{{Z}^{K}_{s^{-}}}\Big)\bigg)\bigg\rvert

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \sum_{s\leqslant KT}\big(\Delta{Z}^{K}_{s^{-}}\big)^{2}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\Big|1-\frac{1}{({Z}^{K}_{s^{-}})^{2}}\Big|\ +\ \frac{1}{({Z}^{K}_{s^{-}})^{2}}R_{\log}\Big(\frac{\Delta{Z}^{K}_{s^{-}}}{{Z}^{K}_{s^{-}}}\Big)\bigg)

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \big[W_{k},W_{k}\big]_{Kt}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\sup_{s\leqslant t}\ \big|1-(Z^{K}_{Ks})^{-1}\big|\ +\ \sup_{s\leqslant t}\ \frac{1}{({Z}^{K}_{s^{-}})^{2}}R_{\log}\Big(\frac{\Delta{Z}^{K}_{s^{-}}}{{Z}^{K}_{s^{-}}}\Big)\bigg),

(3.8)

where
R_{\log}
verifies
\lim_{x\to 0}R_{\log}(x)=0
.
\big[W_{k},W_{k}\big]_{Kt}
converges in probability to a finite limit by
3.4
and the
Z^{K}_{s}
converges almost-surely to
1
.
Moreover by a computation similar to the end of the proof of
3.2
shows that

\Prob\big(\sup_{s<Kt}(\Delta{Z}^{K}_{s})>\Gamma_{K}/K\big)\underset{K\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\ 0.

Hence by Slutsky’s theorem, the product (
3.8
) converges to zero converges to
0
in probability.

To derive the result it remains to control
D
. The stochastic integral within the absolute values is a martingale. Its quadratic variation verifies

\displaystyle\big[D,D\big]_{Kt}\

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{s\leqslant Kt}\big(1-(Z^{K}_{s})^{-1}\big)^{2}\big|\Delta W_{k}(s)\big|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \big[\widehat{W},\widehat{W}\big]_{Kt}\sup_{s\leqslant t}\big(1-(Z^{K}_{Ks})^{-1}\big)^{2},

which by
3.4
and convergence of the density converges in probability to zero. Thus, the term
D
converges in probability to zero as
K\to\infty
.
Putting everything together, we finally obtain that for all
k\geqslant 0
,
\sup_{t\leqslant T}\varepsilon(Kt)
converges to zero in probability under
\mathbf{Q}^{k}
.

4
Convergence of the genealogy

In this final section, we begin by showing in
Proposition
4.1
that the penalized planar
moments distribution (

) admits a scaling limit.
Moreover, we will see that this limit is a probability distribution
supported on the planar chronological forests with binary mergers and
describes a Markov process. In Proposition

then identify this limiting distribution as that of the Kingman
coalescent. Finally, we complete the proof of the main theorem by linking

4.1
Limit of the penalized moments

For any planar coalescent process
\Pi\in\mathbb{G}^{k}

Proposition 4.1
(Scaling limit of the recursive formula).

Let

,
\beta>0
,
\varphi:\mathbb{G}^{k}\to\R

\psi:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}

For each

\lim_{K\to\infty}\mathbf{M}^{k,Kt}_{Z_{0}}\big(\varphi(\cdot/K),\ \psi\big)\ =\ \psi(1)\mathbf{\bar{M}}^{k,t}(\varphi).

(4.1)

Furthermore, let
k\geqslant 2
and
\varphi
be of the product form defined in
(
2.5
) with

\forall\Pi\in\mathbb{G}^{k},\ \ \varphi(\Pi)\ \coloneqq\ \mathds{1}\big(\tau_{1}<t,\ c_{1}=(i,2)\big)\varphi_{1}(\tau_{1})\varphi_{2}\big(\Theta_{\tau_{1}}(\Pi)\big),

Then the following holds

\begin{cases}\mathbf{\bar{M}}^{k,t}(\tau_{1}>t)\ =\ \exp\Big(-q(1)m_{2}(1)\Bigl(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{2}$}}\Bigr)t\Big)\\
\mathbf{\bar{M}}^{k,t}(\varphi)\ =\ \int_{0}^{t}\varphi_{1}(t-s)\mathbf{\bar{M}}^{k-1,t-s}(\varphi_{2})\frac{q(1)m_{2}(1)}{k-1}\Bigl(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{2}$}}\Bigr)\exp\Big(-q(1)m_{2}(1)\Bigl(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{2}$}}\Bigr)(t-s)\Big)ds.\end{cases}

(4.2)

We start with a lemma before proceeding with the proof. Recall the notation
\mathcal{M}^{(k,d),t}_{z}
from
(

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\psi
be bounded, non-negative and continuous at 1,
k\geqslant 1
,

t>0
. For any sequence
(z_{K})_{Z}
such that

as

\lim_{K\to\infty}{\cal M}^{(k,2),Kt}_{z_{K}}(\psi)\ =\ \psi(1)q(1)m_{2}(1)\exp\Big(-q(1)m_{2}(1)\Bigl(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{2}$}}\Bigr)t\Big).

Proof.

First, for any
A>0

\displaystyle\lvert m_{2,\beta/K}(z_{K})-m_{2}(z_{K})\rvert\

\displaystyle=\ \E\big[L(z_{K})^{2}\big(1-e^{-\beta L(z_{K})/K}\big)\big]

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \sup_{z\in(1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)}\Big(\E\big[L(z)^{2}\mathds{1}_{\{L(z)>A\}}\big]\Big)+A^{2}\E\big[1-e^{-\beta L(z_{K})/K}\big].

The right-hand side vanishes by letting
K\to\infty
first (and
noting that
(L(z_{K}))_{K}
is tight since it has uniformly bounded
second moment) and then
A\to\infty
using (
1.1
).
Therefore, since
m_{2}(z)
is continuous at

, we deduce
that

\lim_{K\to\infty}m_{2,\beta/K}(z_{K})\ =\ m_{2}(1).

Similarly

\Prob(L^{(2)}_{\beta/K}(z_{K})\geqslant A)\ \leqslant\ \frac{1}{m_{2,\beta/K}(z_{K})}\E\Big[L^{2}(z_{K})\mathds{1}_{\{L(z_{K})\geqslant A\}}\Big].

The right-hand side vanishes uniformly in
K
as
A\to\infty
by
(
1.1
) and thus
(L^{(2)}_{\beta/K}(z_{K}))_{K}
is tight and

z_{K}+\frac{L^{(2)}_{\beta/K}(z_{K})-1}{K}\underset{K\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}1

in probability. We can therefore apply
Proposition
3.5
, yielding that

\lim_{K\to\infty}q(z_{K})m_{2,\beta/K}(z_{K})\e^{\beta/K}\mathbf{M}^{k,Kt}_{Kz_{K}+L^{(2)}_{\beta/K}(z_{K})-1}(\psi)\\
=\ \psi(1)q(1)m_{2}(1)\exp\Big(-q(1)m_{2}(1)\Bigl(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{2}$}}\Bigr)t\Big)

in probability. The proof is completed by noting that, for
K
large
enough,
z_{K}\in(1/2,2)
and that by the definition of

\mathbf{M}^{k,s}_{Z_{0}}
in (
2.4
),

\mathbf{M}^{k,Kt}_{Kz_{K}}(\psi)\ \leqslant\ \frac{\lVert\psi\rVert k!\,\sup_{z>0}(\e^{-\beta z}z^{k})}{\sup_{z\in(1/2,2)}(\e^{-\beta z}z^{k})}.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

If
\varphi=\mathds{1}(\tau_{1}>t)
is the indicator that no coalescence events
occur before time

2.4

\mathbf{M}_{Z_{0}}^{k,Kt}(\varphi(\cdot/K),\psi)\ =\ \e^{\beta Z^{K}_{0}}\mathbf{Q}^{k}_{Z_{0}}\bigg[\psi(Z^{K}_{Kt})\e^{-\beta Z^{K}_{Kt}}\exp\Big(k\int_{0}^{Kt}q(Z^{K}_{s})\big(m(Z^{K}_{s})-1\big)ds\Big)\bigg].

Proposition
3.5
entails that

\lim_{K\to\infty}\mathbf{M}_{Z_{0}}^{k,Kt}(\varphi(\cdot/K),\psi)\ =\ \psi(1)\exp\Big(-q(1)m_{2}(1)\Bigl(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{2}$}}\Bigr)t\Big)

(4.3)

which is a reformulation of (
4.1
). Since there are
no coalescence events in a tree with a single leaf, this also proves the

k=1
.

Now, let
\varphi
be of the product form (
2.5
).
We will prove the result by induction and assume that, for all
p<k
,
(
4.1
) holds and that the limiting probability

\bar{\mathbf{M}}^{p,t}
is supported on binary genealogies. Rescaling time by
K
in

shows that

\mathbf{M}^{k,Kt}_{Z_{0}}\big(\varphi(\cdot/K),\psi\big)\ =\ \frac{1}{k-d+1}\binom{k}{d}\int_{0}^{t}\varphi_{1}(t-s)\frac{K}{(Z_{0}-k+d-1)_{d-1}}\\
\times\mathbf{M}^{k-d+1,Ks}_{Z_{0}}\Big({\varphi}_{2}(\cdot/K),\ \mathcal{M}^{(k,d),K(t-s)}_{\cdot}(\psi)\Big)ds.

(4.4)

We will prove the result by letting

in this identity.
This requires sufficient integrability, which is the delicate step of

For

,
\mathbf{M}^{k-1,Ks}_{Z_{0}}
is a measure on

\liminf_{K\to\infty}\ \mathbf{M}^{k-1,Ks}_{Z_{0}}\Big(1,\ \mathcal{M}^{(k,2),K(t-s)}_{\cdot}(1)\Big)\ \geqslant\ q(1)m_{2}(1)\exp\Big(-q(1)m_{2}(1)\Bigl(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{2}$}}\Bigr)(t-s)\Big).

Let us now denote by

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}^{k,Kt}_{Z_{0}}(1,1)\

\displaystyle\geqslant\ \sum_{i\in[k-1]}\mathbf{M}^{k,Kt}_{Z_{0}}(\varphi_{(i,2)},1)+\mathbf{M}^{k,Kt}_{Z_{0}}(1).

Combining this inequality with (

),
(

\displaystyle\liminf_{K\to\infty}\mathbf{M}^{k,Kt}_{Z_{0}}(1,1)

+\exp\Big(-q(1)m_{2}(1)\Bigl(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{2}$}}\Bigr)t\Big)\end{multlined}

\displaystyle=\Big(1-\exp\Big(-q(1)m_{2}(1)\Bigl(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{2}$}}\Bigr)t\Big)\Big)+\exp\Big(-q(1)m_{2}(1)\Bigl(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{2}$}}\Bigr)t\Big)

\ =\ \frac{\psi(1)q(1)m_{2}(1)}{k-1}\binom{k}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\varphi_{1}(t-s)\bar{\mathbf{M}}^{k-1,t-s}(\varphi_{2})\e^{-q(1)m_{2}(1)\binom{k}{2}(t-s)}ds.

4.2
Completing the proof

We define the planar

Proposition 4.4

The measure
