Efficacy of dexmedetomidine in preventing emergence agitation in children undergoing tonsillectomy and/or adenoidectomy: a meta-analysis and systematic review
Authors: Liwei Wang, Shirong Fang, Lin Cheng, Jiang Liu, Sifan Qin, Yuwen Wang, Yuxiu Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionEmergence agitation (EA) affects 10–80% of children after general anesthesia, with high rates following tonsillectomy and/or adenoidectomy. Although dexmedetomidine shows potential for preventing EA, its efficacy and safety in this specific pediatric population remain uncertain, with conflicting evidence from previous studies. MethodsComputerized searches of PubMed, The Cochrane Library, Web of Science, Embase, and OpenGrey were performed, and the search timeframe was from the time of construction to September 26, 2024, for all searches. Literature screening, data extraction, and evaluation of the risk of bias of the included studies were performed independently by two investigators. A meta-analysis was performed using STAT14 software, and heterogeneity was examined through subgroup analysis and meta-regression for route of administration, control medications, general anesthesia procedures, and EA assessment methods. The certainty of results was checked using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) method.ResultsOur study comprised 19 studies with 1697 children. Dexmedetomidine significantly reduced the incidence of EA in thirteen studies of 1222 children (RR = 0.36, 95% CI [0.30, 0.45], P = 0.036, I2 = 45.7%, GRADE = high). Subgroup analyses and meta-regression showed that different scales were the source of heterogeneity in EA incidence outcomes and that dexmedetomidine was more potent than other medications in reducing EA incidence (RR = 0.45, 95% CI [0.33, 0.61], P = 0.302). In eight studies, dexmedetomidine significantly decreased EA scores (SMD = − 0.77, 95% CI [− 1.08, − 0.47], P < 0.001, I2 = 77.6%, GRADE = moderate). Dexmedetomidine was superior to other medications in reducing EA scores (SMD = − 0.51, 95% CI [− 0.78, − 0.23], P = 0.052). Dexmedetomidine reduces pain scores (SMD = − 1.08, 95% CI [− 1.66, − 0.51], P < 0.001), postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV)(RR = 0.51, 95% CI [0.38, 0.69], P = 0.900), respiratory adverse events (desaturation: RR = 0.41, 95% CI [0.32, 0.53], P = 0.578; cough: RR = 0.27, 95% CI [0.14, 0.52], P = 0.56; laryngospasm or bronchospasm: RR = 0.25, 95% CI [0.09, 0.73], P = 0.773). No significant variations in post-anesthesia care unit and extubation time.ConclusionsPerioperative dexmedetomidine in children undergoing tonsillectomy and/or adenoidectomy reduces EA, pain, PONV, and respiratory adverse events without significant side effects.Registration numberCRD42024506421.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-025-03512-1.

Introduction

Emergence agitation (EA) is a common adverse reaction affecting 10–80% of pediatric patients following general anesthesia, with particularly high incidence rates after otolaryngological procedures (34.5%) [1–4]. EA is characterized by involuntary kicking, thrashing, and aimless movements accompanied by extreme distress, potentially resulting in accidental injuries, delayed post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) discharge, and increased healthcare burden [5, 6]. Children who experience EA are more likely to exhibit postoperative maladaptive behaviors, including temper tantrums, nightmares, and separation anxiety [7, 8]. Given that over 530,000 pediatric tonsillectomy and/or adenoidectomy are performed annually in the United States alone, developing effective EA prevention strategies represents a critical clinical priority [9].

Dexmedetomidine, a selective α−2 adrenergic receptor agonist, is useful in preventing EA without suppressing respiration due to its unique sedative, analgesic, and anxiolytic advantages [10, 11]. Unlike benzodiazepines (which regulate GAB-A receptors) and opioids, dexmedetomidine provides cooperative sedation via cerebral cortex α−2 receptor activation while reducing airway hyperreactivity and nausea [12–15]. However, the current evidence is still controversial [16–19]. Moreover, the current clinical guidelines provide only weak recommendations due to concerns about prolonged recovery times and methodological limitations of available studies [20, 21].

Given the high prevalence of EA and the significant burden associated with pediatric tonsillectomy and/or adenoidectomy. Therefore, this study conducted a rigorous systematic review and meta-analysis of recent high-quality randomized controlled trials, incorporating detailed subgroup analyses to address sources of heterogeneity while quantifying key safety outcomes to provide an updated, clinically actionable evidence base for perioperative decision-making.

Methods

The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) criteria were followed for this study. PROSPERO registration: CRD42024506421.

Search strategy

Two authors searched PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and OpenGrey for eligible randomized controlled trials (RCTs) separately. The search timeframe was from the time of construction to September 26, 2024, for all searches. No language filters were applied. Tonsillectomy, adenoidectomy, dexmedetomidine, emergence delirium, and emergence agitation were searched using keywords and medical subject headings (MeSH terms). This study manually screened the reference lists of relevant studies for eligible articles. The Supplement describes each database’s search strategy.

Selection criteria

The inclusion criteria were:

The inclusion criteria were: (1) Participants: Children (aged 2–14 years) who have an American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status categorization of I or II undergoing tonsillectomy, with or without adenoidectomy. (2) Intervention: Perioperative dexmedetomidine administration. (3) Comparison: Placebo or other medicine (such as morphine, remifentanil, tramadol, midazolam, propofol, or fentanyl). (4) Outcomes: The primary outcomes were the evaluation of EA, including the incidence of EA and EA scores. (5) Study design: RCTs.

The criteria for exclusion were as follows: (1) Protocols. (2) Duplicate publications. (3) Inability to extract relevant data.

Extract data

Two researchers independently reviewed articles’ titles, abstracts, or complete texts to assess eligibility. A third researcher reconciled the authors’ disagreements. Two researchers independently gathered the first author, year of publication, sample size, age, general anesthetic procedure, mode of administration, comparison, and EA assessment from every article.

Risk and quality evaluation

The risk of bias of the included studies was evaluated using a revised Cochrane risk of bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2.0) [22]. Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) demonstrates high, moderate, low, and very low outcome certainty.

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary result was the evaluation of EA, including the incidence of EA and EA scores. In this study, secondary outcomes included postoperative pain scores, the incidence of severe pain, the use of additional medication, the time spent in PACU, the time to extubate, and the incidence of PONV, oxygen desaturation, bronchospasm, laryngospasm, and coughing. To account for variations in the timing of EA evaluation, this study proactively incorporated EA scores and pain scores that were recorded at comparable time intervals. For example, this study included EA scores taken at 5 min with the 10-minute analysis and EA scores taken at 15 min with the 20-minute analysis. The definitions of all the outcomes are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Statistical analysis

This study analyzed data using Review Manager 5.4 and STATA (Version 14.0; Stata Corp). Dichotomous data were presented as risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). For continuous data, the mean difference (MD) and 95% CI were calculated using the same measurement scale where possible; otherwise, the standardized mean difference (SMD) was used. This study estimate the sample mean and SD according to the optimal methods in the specified papers. For continuous data described by the media (interquartile range), this study used a previously reported method to check whether the data significantly deviated from a normal distribution [23]. If the data did not significantly deviate from a normal distribution, the sample mean and standard deviation were estimated using the optimal methods described in the specified papers [24, 25]. If the data significantly deviated from a normal distribution, this study conducted a subgroup analysis of the studies that deviated from a normal distribution to separate the studies that followed a normal distribution from those that did not. Chi-square and I2-tests were used to assess data heterogeneity. A sensitivity analysis was performed by systematically eliminating each RCT to assess its robustness. Funnel plots and Egger tests were used to assess publication bias. The Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill approach was used to examine the possibility of a publishing bias in the findings. Heterogeneity was examined through subgroup analyses and meta-regression for route of administration, control medications, general anesthesia procedures, and EA assessment methods. Studies with Galbraith plot heterogeneity were eliminated and reanalyzed. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Search results

This study retrieved 124 relevant studies from the database. After perusing the abstracts and titles, the exclusion criteria eliminated 57 duplicate articles and 36 studies. Twelve of the remaining 31 papers were removed after reviewing their full texts. The final meta-analysis contains 19 studies that match the inclusion criteria. Literature screening is detailed in Fig. 1.

Study characteristics

The studies are detailed in Table 1. This meta-analysis included 19 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving a total of 1,697 patients. The patients were 2–14 years old, and the sample size was 40–248. Eleven trials employed sevoflurane-based general anesthesia; three used desflurane [26–28], and three used propofol and remifentanil [29–31]. Fourteen of the routes of administration were intravenous, four were intranasal [30, 32–34], and one was buccal trans-mucosa [35]. In 10 RCTs, EA was measured using the pediatric anesthesia emergence delirium score (PAED) scale, while others used AONO’s 4-point scale [33, 36], Watcha score [28, 29, 35], Cole scale [37], a four-point scores [27], a different four-point scores [34]. One study assessed EA using two scales, the PAED scale when assessing EA scores and the Cole scale when assessing the incidence of EA [38]. Of the 19 included studies, 10 used normal saline as placebo controls, while 9 employed other medications. Other medications included: 2 studies using midazolam [33, 39], 1 study using nalbuphine [40],1 study using propofol [41], 1 study using ketamine-propofol combination (ketofol) [36], 2 studies using fentanyl [28, 38], and 2 studies with no intervention control group [32, 42].

Risk of bias

Using the RoB 2.0 assessment tool, 4 of the 19 studies were classified as having “some concerns” due to bias arising from the randomization process [40–43]. Two studies raised “some concerns” regarding bias due to deviations from intended interventions [27, 31]. Three studies were classified as having “some concerns” for bias in selection of the reported result [40–42]. The remaining RoB2 domains demonstrated low risk of bias across all included studies. The overall risk of bias assessment is presented in Fig. 2.

Primary outcomes

The incidence of EA

Thirteen trials with 1222 children showed the incidence of EA. The entire forest plot revealed that dexmedetomidine reduced EA incidence (RR = 0.36, 95% CI [0.30, 0.45], P = 0.036, I2 = 45.7%, Fig. 3).

Fig. 3Pooled analysis forest plot indicating EA incidence

Pooled analysis forest plot indicating EA incidence

Subgroup analyses and meta-regression showed that different EA scales explained the heterogeneity of the results (PAED: RR = 0.43, 95% CI [0.32, 0.56], P = 0.128, I2 = 41.6%; other scale: RR = 0.18, 95% CI [0.11, 0.31], P = 0.501, I2 = 0%; AONO’s 4-point scale: RR = 0.61, 95% CI [0.35, 1.07], P = 0.578, I2 = 0%; Cole scale: RR = 0.36, 95% CI [0.22, 0.60], P = 0.543, I2 = 0%, Supplementary Fig. 2).

The results of the analyses for all subgroups (Supplementary Fig. 1–4) supported the overall results, except for scale subgroups of AONO’s 4-point scale. Subgroup analyses in this study showed dexmedetomidine to be more effective than the other medications (RR = 0.45, 95% CI [0.33, 0.61], P = 0.302, I2 = 17.2%, Supplementary Fig. 1).

The meta-analysis revealed no discernible publication bias, according to Egger’s (P = 0.508) test and the funnel plot (Supplementary Fig. 5). The sensitivity analysis result (Supplementary Fig. 6) demonstrated the robustness of the findings.

The incidence of EA during different times

There are three studies with heterogeneity in the time of assessment of EA incidence. Forest plot analyses showed significant overall effects (RR = 0.39, 95% CI [0.27, 0.56], P = 0.973, I2 = 0%, Fig. 4) and significant differences in the incidence of EA across time points (5 min: RR = 0.40, 95% CI [0.19, 0.85], P = 0.892, I2 = 0%; 10 min: RR = 0.41, 95% CI [0.22, 0.77], P = 0.584, I2 = 0%; 20 min: RR = 0.41, 95% CI [0.20, 0.83], P = 0.420, I2 = 0%; 30 min: RR = 0.30, 95% CI [0.12, 0.75], P = 0.960, I2 = 0%).

Fig. 4Pooled analysis forest plot indicating the incidence of EA at different times

Pooled analysis forest plot indicating the incidence of EA at different times

EA scores

PAED scores were recorded during the emergence of eight trials. The results showed that dexmedetomidine significantly reduced EA scores (SMD = − 0.77, 95% CI [− 1.08, − 0.47], P < 0.001, I2 = 77.6%, Fig. 5). Dexmedetomidine compared to other medications showed the same effect in decreasing EA scores (SMD = − 0.51, 95% CI [− 0.78, − 0.23], P = 0.052, I2 = 61.2%, Supplementary Fig. 7) as it did in reducing the incidence of EA. Subgroup analysis results matched overall results except for intranasal administration (SMD = − 0.66, 95% CI [− 1.48, 0.16], P = 0.006, I2 = 86.6%, Supplementary Fig. 8).

Fig. 5Forest plot of the pooled analysis showing the EA scores

Forest plot of the pooled analysis showing the EA scores

Using the funnel plot (Supplementary Fig. 10) and Egger’s tests (P = 0.045 < 0.05) revealed significant publication bias. The meta-analysis results were not influenced by publication bias, as confirmed by the trim and fill procedure. The results from the sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Fig. 11) were robust.

Subgroup analyses by route of administration, control medications, and EA assessment methods showed no statistically significant sources of heterogeneity (Supplementary Figs. 7–9). Meta-regression analysis similarly failed to identify statistically significant contributors to the observed heterogeneity. Given the failure of conventional methods to explain heterogeneity, we employed Galbraith plot analysis as an alternative approach. The Galbraith radiogram successfully identified three studies [27, 33, 37] as outliers contributing to heterogeneity (Supplementary Fig. 12). Excluding these three outlier studies dramatically reduced heterogeneity from I² = 77.6% to I² = 35.9% (SMD = − 0.67, 95% CI [− 0.90, − 0.43], Supplementary Fig. 13), while preserving the significant treatment effect, confirming these studies as the primary source of statistical heterogeneity.

The EA scores during different times

There are five studies with heterogeneity in the time of assessment of EA scores. Forest plot analysis revealed significant differences in study effects across the five groups (SMD = − 0.80, 95% CI [− 1.14, − 0.46], P < 0.001, I2 = 86.8%, Fig. 6). There were no significant changes in PAED scores immediately after administration of dexmedetomidine and 45 and 60 min after administration of the medication (0 min: SMD = − 0.81, 95% CI [− 1.66,0.05], P < 0.001, I2 = 92.7%; 5 min: SMD = − 0.56, 95% CI [− 0.86,−0.26], P = 0.705, I2 = 0%; 15 min: SMD = − 1.04, 95% CI [− 1.84,−0.24], P < 0.001, I2 = 91.4%; 30 min: SMD = − 0.68, 95% CI [− 1.21,−0.16], P = 0.002, I2 = 79.9%).

Secondary outcomes

Postoperative pain

Postoperative pain scores were compared to 10 RCTs. Patients who were given dexmedetomidine had less pain after surgery than those who were not (SMD = − 1.08, 95% CI [− 1.66, − 0.51], P < 0.001, I2 = 94.4%, Supplementary Fig. 14).

In five RCTs, 340 patients were compared for severe postoperative pain. They included 170 experimental and 170 control participants. The two groups showed significant differences (RR = 0.42, 95% CI [0.31, 0.58], P = 0.654, I2 = 0%, Supplementary Fig. 15).

Emergence variables

Ten studies mentioned PACU stay and thirteen mentioned extubation time. Dexmedetomidine did not increase extubation time, PACU time (extubation time: SMD = 0.13, 95% CI [− 0.36, 0.63], P < 0.001, I2 = 94.3%, Supplementary Fig. 16; PACU time: SMD = − 0.02, 95% CI [− 0.54,0.51], P < 0.001, I2 = 94.5%, Supplementary Fig. 17).

Adverse events

PONV analysis was performed on 1111 subjects from 11 randomized controlled trials. The two groups differed statistically (RR = 0.51, 95% CI [0.38, 0.69], P = 0.900, I2 = 0%, Supplementary Fig. 18).

This study also found that dexmedetomidine reduced respiratory adverse effects, with statistically significant improvements in desaturation (desaturation: RR = 0.41, 95% CI [0.32, 0.53], P = 0.578, I2 = 0%, Supplementary Fig. 19; cough: RR = 0.27, 95% CI [0.14, 0.52], P = 0.560, I2 = 0%, Supplementary Fig. 20; laryngospasm or bronchospasm: RR = 0.25, 95% CI [0.09, 0.73], P = 0.773, I2 = 0%, Supplementary Fig. 21).

Discussion

This comprehensive analysis shows that dexmedetomidine can significantly decrease EA and postoperative pain in children following tonsillectomy and/or adenoidectomy. Additionally, the meta-analysis shows that dexmedetomidine does not extend extubation time and PACU time. Dexmedetomidine may also reduce postoperative PONV, bronchospasm, laryngospasm, desaturation, and coughing. The level of certainty of GRADE ranged from very low to high.

The mechanism underlying dexmedetomidine’s efficacy in preventing EA relates to its selective α2-adrenergic receptor agonism, which directly counteracts the hyperadrenergic state characteristic of emergence from inhaled anesthetics like sevoflurane and desflurane [11].Subgroup analysis confirmed that dexmedetomidine was significantly more effective than other medications in reducing EA, and the effects were consistent across different anesthesia protocols [44]. Different EA assessment scales were the primary source of heterogeneity in incidence outcomes, while Galbraith plot analysis identified three methodologically distinct studies as statistical outliers contributing to heterogeneity in EA scores [27, 33, 37]. Following exclusion of these outlier studies, heterogeneity decreased substantially (I² = 35.9%), confirming that study-specific methodological differences, rather than systematic clinical factors, were the primary contributors to the observed heterogeneity.

Beyond EA prevention, dexmedetomidine demonstrated significant multimodal benefits that address multiple post-tonsillectomy adverse events simultaneously. The medication reduced postoperative pain scores, severe pain incidence, and PONV, while also decreasing respiratory adverse events including desaturation, cough, and laryngospasm or bronchospasm. This comprehensive benefit profile is particularly valuable given that pain and PONV can exacerbate EA, and respiratory adverse events occur in up to 20% of children following tonsillectomy [45]. This study showed that dexmedetomidine significantly reduced the incidence of PONV, with its antiemetic effect potentially arising from a dual mechanism involving activation of α−2 receptors in the area postrema (the brain’s chemoreceptor trigger zone) and reduced sympathetic outflow [11, 15]. This effect aligns with dexmedetomidine’s efficacy in pediatric strabismus surgery [46]. It also reduces respiratory adverse events, with these benefits potentially stemming from attenuated airway hyperreactivity and modulated inflammation [13, 14]. The analgesic properties of dexmedetomidine offer a valuable solution to pain management challenges in pediatric otolaryngological surgery. Pain and EA frequently coexist as components of early postoperative negative behavior (e-PONB), affecting approximately 15% of children who experience both symptoms concurrently [47–50]. This overlap creates diagnostic challenges, as distinguishing between pain and EA in the immediate postoperative period can be difficult [51]. While acetaminophen (paracetamol) and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) constitute the foundation of postoperative pain management due to their established safety and efficacy profiles [52, 53], these first-line medications may prove insufficient for adequate pain control in some pediatric tonsillectomy and/or adenoidectomy cases. When pain management requirements exceed the capacity of these standard analgesics, the alternative use of opioid medications introduces substantial risks, including respiratory depression and PONV. These limitations underscore the potential value of dexmedetomidine as an integral component of a comprehensive multimodal analgesic strategy in pediatric otolaryngological procedures [54].

GRADE assessment revealed publication bias in EA scores (Egger’s P = 0.045), which may have overestimated the degree of benefit. However, trim-and-fill analysis demonstrated that this bias had limited influence on the overall conclusions, indicating that the effect remains clinically meaningful. Inconsistency primarily affects size estimates rather than the direction of benefit. The inconsistency mainly stemmed from the conversion of media (interquartile ranges) to means and standard deviation. Due to the sparse and scattered nature of these conversion studies, the impact on overall results was limited. Multiple outcomes showed small sample effects that limited the precision of estimates. Although confidence intervals were wider, the consistent direction of effects supports clinical utility. GRADE assessment revealed evidence of quality levels for clinical decision-making. High-certainty evidence for reduced EA incidence provides strong support for dexmedetomidine use, while moderate-certainty evidence for EA scores indicates probable beneficial effects, though the magnitude may vary between clinical settings. Moderate to very low certainty evidence for time-specific outcomes suggests that precise timing protocols require further investigation but should not preclude overall use based on robust primary results.

Dexmedetomidine does not prolong extubation or PACU time, addressing a key issue in previous clinical guidelines [20]. This safety feature, combined with a number needed to treat (NNT) of 3–4 for EA prevention, supports its use in children following tonsillectomy and/or adenoidectomy outpatient settings where rapid where rapid turnaround is critical. High certainty evidence for EA reduction and moderate certainty evidence for other outcomes provide a solid foundation for updating clinical practice guidelines.

Despite evaluating study quality, this research has several limitations. First, heterogeneity in dexmedetomidine dosing regimens and administration timing made dose-response analysis insufficient. Second, heterogeneity in EA assessment tools may compromise the reliability of results. Third, potential publication bias exists in EA scores, although trim-and-fill analysis confirmed robustness. Fourth, subgroup analyses had insufficient power in key comparisons due to fewer studies included in some subgroups (e.g., intranasal route). Fifth, all studies included in this research had follow-up times ≤ 2 h and did not examine long-term effects. Future research priorities should include larger-scale, high-quality randomized controlled trials designed to establish optimal dosing regimens and administration protocols through formal dose-response studies. Additionally, research should focus on developing standardized methods for measuring EA. Long-term safety characteristics of dexmedetomidine should be explored through extended follow-up studies.

Conclusion

Perioperative dexmedetomidine significantly reduces EA, postoperative pain, PONV, and respiratory adverse events in pediatric patients undergoing tonsillectomy and/or adenoidectomy, without significantly prolonging emergence time or increasing additional side effects. Its number needed to treat (NNT) for EA prevention is 3–4, supporting its use as a preferred prevention strategy for high-risk cases. This study provides evidence-based support for opioid-free protocols and establishes an evidence-based foundation for optimizing medication use in ambulatory surgery. This research provides strong evidence for guideline upgrades, potentially elevating the recommendation grade for dexmedetomidine in pediatric otolaryngology procedures. Future multicenter RCTs are needed to determine optimal dosing and promote refined guideline revisions.
