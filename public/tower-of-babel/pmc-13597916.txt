Complex perioperative and anaesthesia management of a polytrauma patient with intrathoracic haemorrhage undergoing emergency lobectomy
Authors: Despoina Sarridou, Aikaterini Amaniti, Varvara Fyntanidou, Rafail Ioannidis, Chrysoula Vlachou, Vasileios Grosomanidis, Eleni Argiriadou, Sokratis Tsagkaropoulos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Severe blunt chest trauma is associated with significant morbidity and mortality, particularly when complicated by intrathoracic haemorrhage and delayed recognition of ongoing bleeding. Perioperative and anaesthetic management in these patients is complex, requiring advanced airway skills, meticulous hemodynamic control, and effective multidisciplinary coordination. We present the case of an 18-year-old male polytrauma patient following a road traffic accident who was transferred to a tertiary referral centre with bilateral haemothoraces and initial hemodynamic instability. Despite early chest drainage and transient stabilization, the patient developed persistent transfusion requirements and radiological evidence of evolving right lower lobe intraparenchymal haemorrhage. Surgical exploration was performed 27 h after admission, resulting in an emergency open right lower lobectomy. Anaesthetic management was challenging. Tracheal intubation with a left-sided double-lumen tube was achieved using videolaryngoscopy, allowing effective one-lung ventilation. Advanced hemodynamic monitoring, including the hypotension prediction index, bispectral index monitoring, and thromboelastometry-guided transfusion, was used to guide fluid therapy, vasopressor and inotropic support, and targeted administration of blood products, fibrinogen, and prothrombin complex concentrate. Postoperatively, the patient was managed in the intensive care unit and showed gradual clinical improvement. Transient postoperative liver enzyme elevation resolved without evidence of structural hepatic injury. The patient was extubated successfully, discharged from the ICU in stable condition, and remained clinically well during a 12-month follow-up period. This case emphasizes the importance of early recognition of occult thoracic bleeding and highlights the critical role of advanced anaesthetic management in emergency lung resection following severe chest trauma.

Introduction

Chest trauma remains a condition which when stays untreated is escorted by high morbidity and mortality risk of 20–25%. One out of four trauma patients die due to thoracic trauma and its complications. Six life threatening conditions (airway obstruction, tension pneumothorax, open pneumothorax, massive hemothorax, pericardial tamponade and flail chest) should be treated immediately upon diagnosis. Any delays within the course from first diagnosis until final intervention can complicate treatment and prolong recovery. [1]. Road traffic accidents (RTA) are globally one of the most frequent causes of severe chest trauma. Thoracic injuries related to RTAs have different characteristics than other non-RTA related chest trauma. Multiple rib fractures, pulmonary contusions and sternal injuries are the most frequent injuries. [2] Chest wall and lung injuries interfere significantly with normal respiratory function. Effective management involves the use of incentive spirometry, multimodal analgesia and many times surgical treatment. [3] Intrathoracic haemorrhage caused by chest trauma is a condition that undoubtfully requires surgical intervention for bleeding control and hemodynamic stabilization. Injuries of the big vessels, the lung parenchyma the heart and the chests wall can cause major bleeding. Emergency sternotomy, thoracotomy or even resuscitation thoracotomy should be considered alongside with coagulopathy management. [4] The perioperative management of these patients can become a challenge especially on the background of delayed diagnosis and significant hemodynamic instability. The airway management can also be a challenge as it may require lung isolation and advance airway skills alongside with use of special airway equipment especially if is associated with tracheobronchial injuries. [5] This case report presents a young male patient with chest trauma that required urgent thoracic surgery for exploration and bleeding control that ended up having an open lobectomy 27 h post admission.

Case report

An 18-year-old male patient was transferred from a local general hospital to AHEPA University Hospital following a road traffic accident for further management. He was previously fit and well, classified as ASA I according to the American Society of Anesthesiologists. The patient had not been wearing a seat belt at the time of the accident and sustained significant thoracic trauma. During initial resuscitation at the referring hospital, computed tomography (CT) revealed bilateral haemothoraces, for which chest drains were inserted. He was subsequently transferred to the tertiary centre in a relatively stable condition.

Upon arrival at the Emergency Department, the patient was hemodynamically unstable, presenting with hypotension (blood pressure 68/45 mmHg), tachycardia (heart rate 138–140 bpm), and hypoxia, likely exacerbated by transport. An urgent CT scan demonstrated multiple rib fractures, clavicular fractures, and lung parenchymal contusions predominantly involving the right lower lobe.

Preoperative management

Initial management included crystalloid fluid resuscitation, vasopressor boluses with phenylephrine and noradrenaline, and blood transfusion. Respiratory support was provided using high-flow nasal oxygen alternating with noninvasive ventilation (NIV), successfully avoiding endotracheal intubation at this stage. Following transient stabilization, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for close monitoring and conservative management.

Overnight, he received four units of packed red blood cells, three units of fresh frozen plasma, and tranexamic acid. He remained normotensive without vasopressor support but persistently tachycardic, which was attributed to pain, anxiety, and physiological stress. Despite stable hemodynamic parameters, ongoing transfusion requirements persisted, with an additional four units of red blood cells required to maintain hemoglobin levels above 8 g/dL.

A repeat thoracic surgical review and reassessment of CT imaging raised concern for an evolving intraparenchymal haematoma associated with extensive right lower lobe injury. Although systolic blood pressure remained above 120 mmHg and heart rate ranged between 90 and 100 bpm, the continued bleeding prompted a decision for surgical intervention to achieve haemostasis and bleeding control.

Intraoperative management

The patient arrived in the operating theatre hemodynamically stable, receiving supplemental oxygen via a 100% oxygen rebreathing mask. A three-lumen central venous catheter was in situ in the right internal jugular vein, along with a left radial arterial line. Standard AAGBI monitoring was applied, supplemented by bispectral index (BIS) monitoring and advanced hemodynamic monitoring using the hypotension prediction index (HPI).

Preoperative assessment identified predictors of potential difficult airway management; therefore, a C-MAC videolaryngoscope and a fully equipped difficult airway trolley were prepared. Given the need for right-sided lung isolation, multiple sizes of double-lumen tubes (DLTs) were available.

Anaesthesia was induced using target-controlled infusions of propofol and remifentanil, with co-administration of midazolam, fentanyl, and high-dose rocuronium (1.2 mg/kg). Anaesthesia was maintained with propofol (3–4 μg/mL) and remifentanil (2–3 ng/mL). Immediately following induction, the patient developed hypotension and oxygen desaturation during bag-mask ventilation. A single successful intubation attempt was achieved, and noradrenaline infusion was promptly initiated.

The patient was positioned in the left lateral decubitus position, and an open right thoracotomy was performed, followed by right lower lobe lobectomy. The procedure was technically demanding and prolonged. Intraoperatively, two units of packed red blood cells, 2 g of fibrinogen, and 1000 IU of prothrombin complex concentrate were administered, guided by thromboelastometry. During critical phases of the operation, combined vasopressor and inotropic support with noradrenaline and low-dose adrenaline was required. At the conclusion of surgery, the patient was transferred intubated to the ICU with a single-lumen endotracheal tube and supported with low-to-moderate doses of noradrenaline.

Airway management

Predictors of difficult intubation and bag-mask ventilation were identified preoperatively. Mallampati classification could not be reliably assessed in the ICU setting, and additional factors included the presence of a beard, borderline thyromental distance, and limited neck extension. A C-MAC videolaryngoscope was selected as the first-line device. Multiple left-sided DLTs were prepared, and flexible bronchoscopy was available to facilitate tracheal intubation and confirm optimal tube positioning. Bronchial blockers were also available as a backup strategy.

Prolonged preoxygenation was performed for 2–3 min before induction. As anticipated, bag-mask ventilation was difficult, necessitating a two-person technique with an oropharyngeal airway. Despite adequate preoxygenation and end-tidal oxygen levels above 90%, rapid desaturation occurred due to reduced respiratory reserve from lung injury. After approximately 90 s of ventilation, videolaryngoscopic intubation was performed. Despite limited neck extension, intubation was straightforward (Cormack–Lehane grade I), and a size 41 DLT was inserted successfully. Lung isolation was confirmed clinically and with flexible bronchoscopy, with no evidence of cuff leak or abnormal airway pressures.

Monitoring and surgical management

Advanced hemodynamic monitoring with HPI allowed early identification and prevention of profound hypotensive episodes. Hypotension following induction was attributed to anaesthetic-induced vasodilation, positive pressure ventilation, and ongoing intrathoracic blood loss. BIS monitoring assisted in titration of anaesthetic depth, while point-of-care thromboelastometry guided targeted coagulation management, avoiding unnecessary plasma transfusion.

Surgically, an open right thoracotomy was performed, with resection of the right lower lobe and evacuation of the associated haematoma. Following haemostasis and stabilization, the patient was returned to the ICU for postoperative care.

Postoperative care

The patient was extubated uneventfully in the ICU the following day. Adrenaline was discontinued shortly after extubation, while low-dose noradrenaline was continued for approximately 12 h before gradual weaning. Transient postoperative elevation of liver enzymes was observed without radiological or clinical evidence of hepatic injury. The patient was subsequently transferred to the surgical ward and discharged following further recovery.

Discussion

Interhospital transfers, lack of communication between different teams and delays in diagnosis may play a significant role in hemodynamic deterioration of trauma patients. A recent study by Michos et al. concludes that one out of ten trauma patients deteriorate significantly during the transfer process and also that trauma systems are essential and require close attention to aspects such as patient selection, pre transfer stabilization and very importantly good communication between teams. [6] Another factor that in our case delayed the diagnosis of lung injury and the need for urgent surgical treatment requiring an open lobectomy was related with the young age of the patient. Young patients, previously fit and well, have the ability to compensate well any internal blood loss and they maintain good hemodynamic values for many hours after the injury. Our patient maintained hemodynamic parameters within normal range apart from a slightly deranged heart rate which could be attributed to pain. However, hemodynamic compromise may happen suddenly and rapidly. Hyperdynamic pattern in the early stage of hypovolemic traumatic shock should be recognized as a compensatory mechanism before hemodynamic collapse. Heart rate is a primary physiologic parameter for early assessment of trauma associated haemorrhagic shock in ATLS. However, its value as a predictor of mortality raises controversy between different studies and in addition there is a multiphasic response of HR to bleeding. A systematic review by Javor P. et al. suggests that a linear association between HR, blood loss and the severity of shock is doubtful. [7] Focusing on maintaining hemoglobin values above normal range in combination with high transfusion requirements for red blood cells could masque an ongoing cause of bleeding in the thoracic cavity. Hemodynamic support was also demanding, requiring the administration of blood products, fibrinogen and concentrate of haemostatic factors. Vasopressor and inotrope infusions were also used, occasionally in high doses in order to face episodes of profound instability. Noradrenaline was used as a first line agent. Other studies also suggest its broad use as a safe first line vasopressor even with peripheral administration in emergency medicine. [8] The use of HPI and advanced hemodynamic monitoring guided the hemodynamic management with fluid administration, vasopressor support escalation and initiation of inotropes. Several studies support the use of HPI as an efficient monitor that can minimize hypotension risk during intraoperatively. An RCT comparing HPI versus conventional goal directed hemodynamic management concluded that HPI is an independent factor that promotes the reduction hypotension in non-cardiac surgery. [9] Trauma induced coagulopathy may occur despite a balanced transfusion strategy. The administration of cryoprecipitate, fibrinogen concentrate and prothrombin complex concentrate can prevent the above and also minimize the risk of overload. [10] In our case, transfusion of blood products was guided by ROTEM in order to minimize the complications of overcorrection of coagulopathy and especially those related to acute lung injury (ALI). Other studies also strongly support the importance of ROTEM and TEG in trauma and damage control resuscitation. [11] Excessive administration of fluids can also precipitate for overload and changes suggestive of pulmonary edema and ALI on chest trauma patients. [12]

The overall management post admission was proven to be challenging for the presenting case. The initial stabilization was followed by a gradual deterioration due to underlying endoparenchymal haemorrhage. The initial suspicion of lung contusion was then confirmed from a CT scan. According to the literature publishes it appears that generally, lung resection for lung parenchyma injuries is uncommon. Wedge resection, lobectomy and pneumonectomy are the surgical procedures available depending on the extension of the lesions. [13] The patient had high oxygen requirements due to his chest injuries. Different techniques of oxygen therapy were used. Nasal high flow oxygen and NIV were used alternatively during the early preoperative phase in order to avoid endotracheal intubation. The application of NIV is well recognized for the management of respiratory distress in chest trauma and other studies suggest that it reduces intubation rate, the length of ICU and hospital stay as well as mortality. [14] The use of NIV in early stage of chest trauma appears to be safe and beneficial and it does not provoke itself complications that affect morbidity and mortality. [15] The risk of difficult airway was recognized early. This mandated the need for use of difficult airway equipment and use of a videolaryngoscope.

After the surgical treatment the patient showed gradual improvement of all clinical parameters. Liver function returned to normal. The patient was extubated on the eighth postoperative day and discharged from the ICU in a completely stable condition. He remained stable throughout the 12-month follow-up period.

Conclusion

A successful management of a patient with chest trauma requires a multidisciplinary approach with efficient communication and coordination of different teams. Transfers, delayed diagnosis, and communication gaps are all contributing factors that increase morbidity and mortality. Perioperative and especially anaesthesia management remains challenging even for very experienced clinicians. Surgical treatment of chest trauma demands one lung ventilation for better exposure of the surgical field and minimization of further lung injury.
