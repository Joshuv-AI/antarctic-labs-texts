1.
Introduction

In this article, it is assumed that all topological groups are Hausdorff
and all spaces are Tychonoff.

Given a space
X
,
A(X)
and
B(X)
denote, respectively, the free Abelian
topological group and the free Boolean topological group on
X
. Free Boolean
topological groups and free Abelian topological groups share many properties.
For example,
\operatorname{ind}A(X)=0
and
\operatorname{ind}B(X)=0
provided that
\dim X=0
. These
facts were proved in
[
9
, Theorem 2.1]
and
[
10
, Theorem 8]
,
respectively. It is also known that the free Abelian topological group
A(X)

on a Dieudonné complete space
X
is Weil (equivalently, Raĭkov)
complete (see
[
11
, Theorem 4]
or
[
1
, Theorem 7.9.6]
). In

[
10
, Problem 6]
O. Sipacheva asked whether the free Boolean topological
group on a Dieudonné complete space is Weil complete. Our main objective is
to give a positive answer to Sipacheva’s question.

The topology of a topological group is determined by the family
of left-invariant continuous pseudometrics on the group

[
4
, Theorem 8.2]
. Let
X
be a space and
\mathscr{P}_{X}

the family of continuous pseudometrics on
X
. In the case of
the free Abelian topological group
A(X)
on a space
X
, the
topology of
A(X)
is determined by the family of Graev
extensions of pseudometrics in
\mathscr{P}_{X}
to
A(X)
(see

[
5
]
or
[
6
]
). A similar result, Theorem
2.6
,
is valid for the free Boolean group
B(X)
on
X
. But the construction of the
Graev extension of pseudometrics from
X
to
B(X)
and the properties of these
extensions are quite different from those in the case of
A(X)
(see

[
10
, p. 498]
regarding this issue). The Graev extension is our main
technical tool in this article. Therefore, we present a (relatively) detailed
exposition of all the necessary material regarding the Graev extension of
pseudometrics from
X
to
B(X)
and then apply it to prove
Theorem
2.1
that the group
B(X)
is Weil complete if and only if the
space
X
is Dieudonné complete.

1.1.
Notation and terminology

We remind that a
Boolean group

B
is a group in which all elements
except the neutral one are of order 2. It is well known and easy to see
that any Boolean group is Abelian; moreover, any such group is a vector
space over the two-element field
\mathbb{F}_{2}
and therefore has a basis. Thus,
any abstract (without topology) Boolean group is free and can be identified
with the set of all finite subsets of its basis under the operation of
symmetric difference. Given a non-empty set
X
with a distinguished point

e\in X
, we denote by
B_{a}(X)
the
abstract free Boolean group on
X
in
the sense of Graev
, i.e., the Boolean group with basis
X\setminus\{e\}
and
zero identified with the element
e
of
X
. For a Tychonoff space
X
(with a
distinguished point
e
), the
free Boolean topological group on
X
in
the sense or Graev
, or simply the
free Boolean topological group on

X
, is denoted by
B(X)
. It is defined by the following conditions:

(i)

B(X)
is a topological Boolean group,
X
is a subspace of
B(X)
, and
B(X)

is generated by
X
;

(ii)

every continuous mapping
f\colon X\to H
to a Boolean topological group
H

satisfying
f(e)=e_{H}
extends to a continuous homomorphism
\widehat{f}\colon B(X)\to H
.

As an abstract group,
B(X)
coincides with
B_{a}(X)
; in particular, the set

X\setminus\{e\}
is linearly independent in
B(X)
. A standard argument (see

[
3
, Section 2]
) shows that
B(X)
does not depend on the choice of the
distinguished point
e\in X
; in other words, the groups
B(X)

corresponding to all possible choices of
e
are topologically isomorphic.

The more familiar
free Boolean topological group
B_{M}(X)
on
X
in the
sense of Markov
is defined by the similar conditions

(i
′
)

B_{M}(X)
is a topological Boolean group,
X
is a subspace of
B_{M}(X)
, and

B_{M}(X)
is generated by
X
;

(ii
′
)

every continuous mapping from
X
to a
Boolean topological group
H
extends to a continuous homomorphism

B_{M}(X)\to H
.

Let
x_{0}\notin X
, and let
X_{\ast}
be the topological sum
X\oplus\{x_{0}\}
,
i.e., the space
X\cup\{x_{0}\}
containing
X
as a clopen subspace.
An easy verification similar to the one in
[
3
, Section 2]
shows that the
groups
B_{M}(X)
and
B(X_{\ast})
are topologically isomorphic. Thus, studying
free Boolean topological groups in the sense of Markov reduces to studying free
Boolean topological groups.

Every topological group
G
with identity element
1
and topology
\tau

carries three natural group uniformities, the left uniformity
\mathscr{V}^{l}_{G}

with base

\{\{(g,h)\in G\times G:h\in gV\}:\text{$V$ is a neighborhood of $1$}\},

the right uniformity
\mathscr{V}^{r}_{G}
with base

\{\{(g,h)\in G\times G:h\in Vg\}:\text{$V$ is a neighborhood of $1$}\},

and the two-sided uniformity
\mathscr{V}_{G}
with base

\{\{(g,h)\in G\times G:h\in gV\cap Vg\}:\text{$V$ is a neighborhood of
$1$}\}.

It is well known that each of these uniformities induces the topology
\tau

on
G

[
2
, Example 8.1.17]
.

Recall that a filter
\mathscr{F}
on a uniform space
X
with uniformity

\mathscr{U}
is said to be
Cauchy
with respect to
\mathscr{U}
if each

V\in\mathscr{U}
contains
F\times F
for some
F\in\mathscr{F}
. A filter

\mathscr{F}
on
X

converges
to a point
x\in X
if
x\in\bigcap\{\overline{F}:F\in\mathscr{F}\}
(the closures are in the topology generated
by
\mathscr{U}
).

A topological group
G
is said to be
Raĭkov
complete
(
Weil complete
) if it is complete with respect to
\mathscr{V}_{G}
(with respect to
\mathscr{V}^{l}_{G}
). Basic information about Raĭkov
and Weil complete groups can be found in
[
1
, Section 3.6]
(see also

[
7
]
). In topological Abelian groups, the uniformities
\mathscr{V}^{l}_{G}
and
\mathscr{V}_{G}
coincide, so that Weil completeness and Raĭkov
completeness coincide as well. Note that a filter
\mathscr{F}
on an Abelian
group
G
is Cauchy if and only if, for any neighborhood
U
of zero in
G
,
there exists an
F\in\mathscr{F}
such that
F-F\subset U
.

In what follows, given a space
X
, we denote the left (or, equivalently,
two-sided) group uniformity
\mathscr{V}_{B(X)}
by
\mathscr{V}
and call it
simply the
group uniformity
of
B(X)
.

A space
X
is
Dieudonné complete
if there exists a complete
uniformity on
X
compatible with the topology of
X
, which is the case if
and only if the universal uniformity of
X
is complete. Equivalently,
X
is
Dieudonné complete if it is homeomorphic to a closed subspace of the
Tychonoff product of a family of metrizable spaces. These and some other facts
concerning Dieudonné completeness can be found in
[
2
, Section 8.5.13]
.

We use the notation
\mathbb{N}
for the set of positive integers and
\mathbb{N}_{0}
for the set of non-negative integers. Given a subset
A
of a
topological space, by
\overline{A}
we denote the closure of
A
in this space.

2.
Completeness of
B(X)

The main result of this paper is the following theorem.

Theorem 2.1
.

The free Boolean topological group
B(X)
on a Tychonoff
space
X
is Weil complete if and only if
X
is Dieudonné
complete.

Our proof of Theorem
2.1
is based on
several statements given below. Some of them are almost exact duplicates of
earlier results for free Abelian topological groups (see

[
1
, Section 7.2]
). To make our exposition self-contained, we give the
proofs of all auxiliary results, occasionally omitting some standard
components.

Recall that a pseudometric
\varrho
on an Abelian group
G
is said to be

invariant
if
\varrho(x,y)=\varrho(x+z,y+z)
for any
x,y,z\in G
.

In
[
10
, p. 498]
, a procedure for extending continuous pseudometrics on
a space
X
to invariant continuous pseudometrics on
B(X)
was described
without proof (this is an adaptation of Graev’s procedure for extending
pseudometrics to free topological groups). We provide some details along the
lines of
[
10
]
.

Let
\varrho
be a pseudometric on a non-empty set
X
. To begin, we extend

\varrho
to
B_{a}(X)
. Given an
h\in B_{a}(X)\setminus\{e\}
, we consider all
possible representations of
h
in the form

(1)

h=(x_{1}+y_{1})+\cdots+(x_{n}+y_{n}),

where
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x_{i},y_{i}\in X
for all
i\leq n
. To every
representation
(\ref{eq:r1})
of
h
we assign the non-negative number

(2)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varrho(x_{i},y_{i}).

Let
N_{\varrho}(h)
be the infimum of the sums
(\ref{eq:sum2})
corresponding
to all representations of
h
in the form (
1
). We have

N_{\varrho}(g+h)\leq N_{\varrho}(g)+N_{\varrho}(h)
, because the sum of
representations of
g,h\in B_{a}(X)
is a representation of
g+h
. It is also
clear that
N_{\varrho}(e)=0
and
N_{\varrho}(g)\geq 0
for each
g\in B_{a}(X)
.
Since every element
g\in B_{a}(X)
satisfies
-g=g
, we see that
N_{\varrho}
is
a
prenorm
on
B_{a}(X)
in the sense of
[
1
, Section 3.3]
.

We set
\widehat{\varrho}(g,h)=N_{\varrho}(g+h)
for
g,h\in B_{a}(X)
. Clearly,

\widehat{\varrho}
is an invariant pseudometric on
B_{a}(X)
.
Informally, the following lemma says that the distance
\widehat{\varrho}(g,h)

between two elements
g=x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}
and
h=y_{1}+\cdots+y_{k}
of the group

B(X)
depends solely on the restriction of the
pseudometric
\varrho

to the finite
subset
\{e\}\cup\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\cup\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}\}
of
X
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose that
\varrho
is a pseudometric on a set
X
and

h=x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}
is an element of
B_{a}(X)\setminus\{e\}

with pairwise distinct
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in X\setminus\{e\}
. Then
there exists a representation

\displaystyle h=(u_{1}+v_{1})+\cdots+(u_{k}+v_{k})

such that
u_{1},v_{1},\ldots,u_{k},v_{k}
are pairwise distinct and

\widehat{\varrho}(h,e)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varrho(u_{i},v_{i}).

Moreover, if
n
is even, then
2k=n
and
\{u_{1},v_{1},\ldots,u_{k},v_{k}\}=\{x_{1},\ldots,\allowbreak x_{n}\}
, and if
n
is odd, then
2k=n+1
,

\{u_{1},v_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
, and
v_{k}=e
.

In particular, the quantity
N_{\varrho}(h)
, which is
the infimum of the sums
(\ref{eq:sum2})
over all possible representations
of
h
of the form (
1
), is in fact the minimum of those sums.

Proof.

Let

(3)

h=(z_{1}+t_{1})+\cdots+(z_{p}+t_{p})

be a representation of
h
with
z_{i},t_{i}\in X
for each
i\leq p
.
First, we claim that certain cancellations in
(\ref{eq:r3})
yield another
representation

(4)

h=(a_{1}+b_{1})+\cdots+(a_{q}+b_{q})

such that
q\leq p
,
\{a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{q},b_{q}\}\subset\{z_{1},t_{1},\ldots,z_{p},t_{p}\}
, the elements
a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{q},b_{q}

are pairwise distinct, and

(5)

\sum_{i=1}^{q}\varrho(a_{i},b_{i})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{p}\varrho(z_{i},t_{i}).

Clearly, we may assume that
z_{i}\neq t_{i}
for each
i\leq p
; otherwise,
we delete the summand
z_{i}+t_{i}
from the representation
(\ref{eq:r3})

of
h
, affecting neither
h
nor the sum on the right-hand side of

(\ref{eq:r9})
. Suppose that
i\neq j
and the summands
(z_{i}+t_{i})

and
(z_{j}+t_{j})
in
(\ref{eq:r3})
contain equal elements. We may assume
without loss of generality that
z_{i}=z_{j}
, because the group
B_{a}(X)
is
Abelian. We replace the two summands
(z_{i}+t_{i})
and
(z_{j}+t_{j})
in

(\ref{eq:r3})
with the single summand
(t_{i}+t_{j})
, thus obtaining a shorter
representation of
h
. In view of the triangle inequality we have

\varrho(t_{i},t_{j})\leq\varrho(t_{i},z_{i})+\varrho(z_{j},t_{j})
(here
z_{i}=z_{j}
),
so that the sum of distances for the new representation of
h
does not
increase. Proceeding, we obtain a reduced representation of
h
of the form

(\ref{eq:r6})
satisfying condition
(\ref{eq:r9})
. In particular, all
elements
a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{q},b_{q}
of this representation are pairwise
distinct and
\{a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{q},b_{q}\}\subset\{z_{1},t_{1},\ldots,z_{p},t_{p}\}
,
because the transformations of the given representation of
h
performed above
introduce no new elements of
X
.

Note that
h=x_{1}+\dots+x_{n}
is an expansion of
h
in the basis
X\setminus\{e\}
. Since the elements
a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{q},b_{q}
are
pairwise distinct, it follows that at most one of them equals
e
and
the remaining ones belong to
X\setminus\{e\}
.
Therefore,
\{a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{q},b_{q}\}\subset\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}\cup\{e\}
.
Clearly,
h
has only finitely many representations of the form (
4
)
satisfying this condition. Since inequality (
5
) holds for all such
representations, it follows from the arbitrariness of the representation
(
3
) that one of them, say,
h=(u_{1}+v_{1})+\dots+(u_{k}+v_{k})
,
satisfies

\widehat{\varrho}(h,e)=N_{\varrho}(h)=\sum_{i=1}^{q}\varrho(a_{i},b_{i}).

Since
u_{1},v_{1},\ldots,u_{k},v_{k}
are pairwise distinct
and
\{u_{1},v_{1},\ldots,u_{k},v_{k}\}\subset\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},e\}
, we have
n\leq 2k\leq n+1
. Therefore,
n=2k
if
n
is even and
n+1=2k
if
n
is odd. In the
latter case, exactly one of the elements

u_{1},v_{1},\ldots,u_{k},v_{k}
equals
e
, and we can assume that this is
v_{k}
,
because
B_{a}(X)
is Abelian. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Theorem 2.3
.

For any pseudometric
\varrho
on a non-empty set
X
,
the invariant pseudometric
\widehat{\varrho}
on the abstract group
B_{a}(X)
is
an extension of
\varrho
. Moreover, if
X
is a Tychonoff space and
\varrho

is a continuous pseudometric on
X
, then
\widehat{\varrho}
is a continuous
pseudometric on
B(X)
.

Proof.

According to Lemma
2.2
, we have
N_{\varrho}(x+y)=\varrho(x,y)

for all
x,y\in X
. Therefore, the pseudometric

\widehat{\varrho}
on
B_{a}(X)
, which is defined by

\displaystyle\widehat{\varrho}(g,h)=N_{\varrho}(g+h),

extends
\varrho
, that is,
\widehat{\varrho}(x,y)=\varrho(x,y)
for all
x,y\in X
. This proves
the first part of the theorem.

Suppose that
\varrho
is a continuous pseudometric on
X
.
Since the group
B_{a}(X)
is Abelian, the prenorm
N_{\varrho}

induces a (possibly non-Hausdorff) group topology
\tau_{\varrho}
on
B_{a}(X)

whose local base at the zero element
e
consists of the sets

O_{\varepsilon}=\{g\in B_{a}(X):N_{\varrho}(g)<\varepsilon\},

where
\varepsilon>0
. It follows from the continuity of
\varrho
that the
restriction of
\tau_{\varrho}
to
X
is coarser than the topology of
X
.

Let us denote the topology of
B(X)
by
\tau
. It follows from the definition
of the free Boolean topological group
B(X)
that
\tau
is the finest
group topology among those agreeing with the topology of
X
. Therefore,

\tau_{\varrho}
is coarser than
\tau
, and hence
\widehat{\varrho}
is a
continuous pseudometric on the topological group
B(X)
.
∎

We refer to
\widehat{\varrho}
as the
Graev extension
of the
pseudometric
\varrho
.

Lemma 2.4
.

The restriction
\mathscr{V}_{X}=\mathscr{V}\restriction X
of the
group uniformity
\mathscr{V}
of
B(X)
to the subspace

X\subset B(X)
coincides with the universal uniformity
\mathscr{U}_{X}

of
X
.

Proof.

The uniformity
\mathscr{V}
induces the topology of
B(X)
on
B(X)
and hence

\mathscr{V}_{X}
induces the topology of
X
on
X\subset B(X)
. Therefore,

\mathscr{V}_{X}
is coarser than
\mathscr{U}_{X}
. Conversely, take an arbitrary
entourage
U\in\mathscr{U}_{X}
of the diagonal in
X^{2}
. By

[
2
, Theorem 8.1.10]
, there exists a continuous pseudometric
\varrho
on

X
such that

U_{\varrho}=\{(x,y)\in X\times X:\varrho(x,y)<1\}\subset U.

Let
\widehat{\varrho}
be the Graev extension of
\varrho
to
B(X)
. Then

V_{\varrho}=\{(g,h)\in B(X)\times B(X):\widehat{\varrho}(g,h)<1\}

is an element of
\mathscr{V}
, because
\widehat{\varrho}
is a continuous
invariant pseudometric on
B(X)
. Clearly,

U_{\varrho}=V_{\varrho}\cap(X\times X)
. Hence
\mathscr{V}_{X}
is finer than

\mathscr{U}_{X}
. Therefore,
\mathscr{V}_{X}=\mathscr{U}_{X}
.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

The Graev extension
\widehat{\varrho}
of a pseudometric

\varrho
on
X
to
B_{a}(X)
is a maximal invariant pseudometric
on
B_{a}(X)
extending
\varrho
. In other words, if
d
is an
invariant pseudometric on
B_{a}(X)
extending
\varrho
, then

d(g,h)\leq\widehat{\varrho}(g,h)
for all
g,h\in B_{a}(X)
.

Proof.

Let
d
be an arbitrary invariant pseudometric on
B_{a}(X)

extending
\varrho
, and let
u
and
v
be any elements of
B_{a}(X)
. Since

d
and
\widehat{\varrho}
are invariant, we have
d(u,v)=d(h,e)
and

\widehat{\varrho}(u,v)=\widehat{\varrho}(h,e)
for
h=u+v
. Therefore,
it suffices to verify that
d(h,e)\leq\widehat{\varrho}(h,e)
for each
h\in B(X)
. Note that
d(x,y)=\varrho(x,y)
for all
x,y\in X
, so the invariance
of
d
and
\widehat{\varrho}
implies

(6)

d(x+y,e)=d(x,y)=\varrho(x,y)=\widehat{\varrho}(x,y)=\widehat{\varrho}(x+y,e).

If
h=e
, then
d(h,e)=0=\widehat{\varrho}(h,e)
. So we
assume that
h\neq e
. Let
h=x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}
, where

x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
are
pairwise distinct elements of
X\setminus\{e\}
. If
n=1
, then
h=x_{1}\in X
,
so
d(x_{1},e)=\varrho(x_{1},e)=\widehat{\varrho}(x_{1},e)
. Suppose that

n\geq 2
. By Lemma
2.2
, there exists a representation

h=(a_{1}+b_{1})+\cdots+(a_{p}+b_{p})
with pairwise distinct

a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{p},b_{p}\in X
such that

\widehat{\varrho}(h,e)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\varrho(a_{i},b_{i}).

For every
i\leq p
, by virtue of
(\ref{eq:ps1})

we have
d(a_{i}+b_{i},e)=d(a_{i},b_{i})=\varrho(a_{i},b_{i})
. Since
d
is an
invariant pseudometric on
B(X)
and
h=\sum_{i=1}^{p}(a_{i}+b_{i})
, it follows
that

d(h,e)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{p}d(a_{i}+b_{i},e)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\varrho(a_{i},b_{i})=\widehat{\varrho}(h,e).

This proves the lemma.
∎

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
X
be a Tychonoff space and
\mathscr{P}_{X}
be the family
of all continuous pseudometrics on
X
. Then the sets

V_{\varrho}=\{g\in B(X):\widehat{\varrho}(g,e)<1\},

where
\varrho\in\mathscr{P}_{X}
, form a local base at
the zero element
e
of
B(X)
.

Proof.

Consider an arbitrary open neighborhood
U
of
e
in
B(X)
.
According to
[
4
, Theorem 8.2]
, there exists a continuous
invariant pseudometric
d
on
B(X)
such that

V_{d}=\{g\in B(X):d(g,e)<1\}\subset U.

We denote by
\varrho
the restriction of
d
to the subset
X

of
B(X)
and let
\widehat{\varrho}
be the Graev extension
of
\varrho
to
B(X)
. Lemma
2.5
implies

d\leq\widehat{\varrho}
, so that

\{g\in B(X):\widehat{\varrho}(g,e)<1\}\subset V_{d}\subset U.

By Theorem
2.3
, the pseudometric
\widehat{\varrho}

is continuous, and hence
V_{\varrho}
is open in
B(X)
. This completes the
proof of the theorem.
∎

Given a subspace
Y
of
X
containing the point
e
, let
B(Y|X)
denote the
topological subgroup of
B(X)
generated by
Y
. The topology of
B(Y|X)
may
be strictly coarser than that of the free Boolean topological group
B(Y)
.
Theorem
2.6
, together with Lemmas
2.2
and
2.4
,
implies the following important statement.

Corollary 2.7

(see also
[
10
, Theorem 4]
)
.

Let
X
be a space with a distinguished point
e
, and let
Y
be its subspace
containing
e
. The topological subgroup

The following simple fact borrowed from

plays a crucial role in the proofs of Lemma

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
K
be a subset of an Abelian topological group
G
with zero
0

and group uniformity
\mathscr{V}_{G}
. If the uniform
space
(K,\mathscr{V}_{G}\restriction K)
is complete, then every
Cauchy filter
\mathscr{F}
on
G
satisfies one of the
following two conditions:

(a)

\mathscr{F}
converges to some point
x\in K
;

(b)

there exists an
F\in\mathscr{F}
and a neighborhood
V

of
0
in
G
such that
K\cap(F+V)=\varnothing
.

Proof.

Suppose that (a) does not hold and consider the family

\gamma=\{F+V:F\in\mathscr{F},\ V\in\mathscr{N}(0)\},

where
\mathscr{N}(0)
is the set of all open neighborhoods of
0
in
G
. It
is easy to see that
\gamma
is a base of some Cauchy filter

\widetilde{\mathscr{F}}
on
(G,\mathscr{V}_{G})
. Indeed, let

\widetilde{\mathscr{F}}
be the family of all subsets of
G
containing at
least one element of
\gamma
and take an arbitrary
U\in\mathscr{N}(0)
.
Choose a symmetric neighborhood
V\in\mathscr{N}(0)
so that
V+V+V\subset U
.
There exists an
F\in\mathscr{F}
such that
F-F\subset V
. Hence

F+V\in\widetilde{\mathscr{F}}
and

(F+V)-(F+V)=(F-F)+(V-V)\subset V+V+V\subset U.

It follows that
\widetilde{\mathscr{F}}
is a Cauchy filter on

(G,\mathscr{V})
.

If the intersection
K\cap P
is non-empty for each
P\in\widetilde{\mathscr{F}}
, then the family
\{K\cap P:P\in\widetilde{\mathscr{F}}\}
forms a Cauchy
filter on the uniform space
(K,\mathscr{V}_{G}\restriction K)
, which converges to a
point
x\in K
, because this space is complete. Therefore, both filters

\widetilde{\mathscr{F}}
and
\mathscr{F}
converge to
x
, which contradicts
our assumption about
\mathscr{F}
. Thus, there exists an element

P\in\widetilde{\mathscr{F}}
of the form
P=F+V
, where
F\in\mathscr{F}

and
V\in\mathscr{N}(0)
, such that
P\cap K=\varnothing
. So (b) holds.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
X
be a Dieudonné complete space. Then the subspace

B_{n}(X)
of
B(X)
with the uniformity inherited from

(B(X),\mathscr{V})
is complete for each
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Proof.

We prove the lemma by induction on
n
. For
n=0
, there is nothing to
prove. Suppose that
n>0
and we have already proved the completeness of

(B_{n-1}(X),\mathscr{U}_{n-1})
. Consider any Cauchy filter
\mathscr{F}
on
(B_{n}(X),\mathscr{U}_{n})
; note that
\mathscr{F}
is a base of a
Cauchy filter on
B(X)
. We must show that
\bigcap\overline{\mathscr{F}}\neq\varnothing
.

If
\bigcap\overline{\mathscr{F}}\cap B_{n-1}(X)\neq\varnothing
, there is
nothing to prove. Suppose that
\bigcap\overline{\mathscr{F}}\cap B_{n-1}(X)=\varnothing
. Then by the induction hypothesis and Lemma
2.9
there exists
an
F_{0}\in\mathscr{F}
and a neighborhood
U_{0}
of zero in
B(X)
for which

(F_{0}+U_{0})\cap B_{n-1}(X)=\varnothing
. Let
\varrho_{0}
be a continuous
pseudometric on
X
for which
V_{\varrho_{0}}\subset U_{0}
(it exists by
Theorem
2.6
). Then

(7)

F_{0}+U_{d_{0}}\cap B_{n-1}(X)=\varnothing.

Note that every element of
F_{0}
can be written as
x_{1}+\dots+x_{n}
, where the

x_{i}
are pairwise distinct elements of
X\setminus\{e\}
, because
F_{0}\subset B_{n}(X)\setminus B_{n-1}(X)
. For the same reason, if
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in X
and

x_{1}+\dots+x_{n}\in F_{0}
, then the
x_{i}
are pairwise distinct elements of

X\setminus\{e\}
. In what follows, by the letter
x
(with indices and
possibly primes) we always denote elements of
X\setminus\{e\}
.

We claim that

(A)

\begin{gathered}d(x_{i},x_{j})\geq 1\\
\text{for any $g=x_{1}+\dots+x_{n}\in F_{0}$ and any $i,j\leq n$, $i\neq j$.}\end{gathered}

Indeed, otherwise,
x_{i}+x_{j}\in V_{\varrho_{0}}
and

\overline{x}+x_{i}+x_{j}\in B_{n-2}(X)\subset B_{n-1}(X)
, which
contradicts (
7
).

We set
\varrho^{*}=3\varrho_{0}
and choose
F^{*}\in\mathscr{F}
so that

(i)

F^{*}\subset F_{\varrho_{0}}
and

(ii)

F^{*}+F^{*}\subset V_{\varrho^{*}}
.

It follows from (i) and (A) that

(B)

\begin{gathered}\varrho^{*}(x_{i},x_{j})\geq 3\\
\text{for any $x_{1}+\dots+x_{n}\in F^{*}$ and any $i,j\leq n$, $i\neq j$.}\end{gathered}

Combining (ii) and (B), we see that, given any
x^{\prime}_{1}+\dots+x^{\prime}_{n},\,x^{\prime\prime}_{1}+\dots+x^{\prime\prime}_{n}\in F^{*}
, each
x^{\prime}_{i}
either is cancelled in
x^{\prime}_{1}+\dots+x^{\prime}_{n}+x^{\prime\prime}_{1}+\dots+x^{\prime\prime}_{n}
(in which case
x^{\prime}_{i}=x^{\prime\prime}_{j}
for some
j
) or is at
a distance
\varrho^{*}
less than 1 from some
x^{\prime\prime}_{j}
, i.e.,

(C)

\begin{gathered}\text{given any $x^{\prime}_{1}+\dots+x^{\prime}_{n},\,x^{\prime\prime}_{1}+\dots+x^{\prime\prime}_{n}\in F^{*}$,}\\
\text{for
each $i\leq n$, there exists a $j\leq n$ such that}\\
\varrho_{0}(x^{\prime}_{i},x^{\prime\prime}_{j})<1/3.\end{gathered}

Fix any
x^{*}_{1}+\dots+x^{*}_{n}\in F^{*}
. Let
\varepsilon
be a positive number
satisfying the conditions
\varepsilon\leq 1/3
and
\varepsilon<\varrho_{0}(x_{i}^{*},e)
for all
i\leq n
. We set

O_{i}=\{x\in X:\varrho_{0}(x^{*}_{i},x)\leq\varepsilon\}.

Note that in view of (B) we have
O_{i}\cap O_{j}=\varnothing
for distinct
i,j\leq n
. Therefore, it follows from (C) that, for any
x_{1}+\dots+x_{n}\in F^{*}
, there exists a unique permutation
\pi
of
\{1,...,n\}
such that

x_{\pi(i)}\in O_{i}
for every
i\leq n
. Thus,
F^{*}\subset O_{1}+\dots+O_{n}
.

Since
O_{i}\subset X\setminus\{e\}
for
i\leq n
, it follows that, given
any
g=x_{1}+\dots+x_{n}\in O_{1}+\dots+O_{n}
, every
O_{i}
contains precisely
one of the elements
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
. In other words, these elements can be
renumbered as
x^{\prime}_{1},\dots,x^{\prime}_{n}
so that
x^{\prime}_{i}\in O_{i}
for each
i\leq n
, and
this renumbering is determined uniquely. In the rest of the proof of this lemma
we assume that the elements of each
g\in O_{1}+\dots+O_{n}
are
numbered in this way; that is, considering
x_{1}+\dots+x_{n}\in O_{1}+\dots+O_{n}
,
we always assume that
x_{i}\in O_{i}
for
i\leq n
.

The restriction
\tilde{\imath}_{n}
of the natural addition map

(defined by
i_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=x_{1}+\dots+x_{n}
) to

is continuous and bijective on
O_{1}\times\dots\times O_{n}
,
and since each
O_{i}
is closed in
X
, it follows that

\tilde{\imath}_{n}^{-1}(\overline{F})
is closed in
X^{n}
for any

such that
F\subset F^{*}
.

Let
\varrho
be any continuous pseudometric on
X
satisfying the

\varrho\geq\varrho^{*}

F\in\mathscr{F}
be such that

F\subset F^{*}
and
F+F\subset V_{\varrho}
. Arguing as above and taking into

\begin{gathered}d(x^{\prime}_{i},x^{\prime\prime}_{i})<1\\
\text{whenever $x^{\prime}_{1}+\dots+x^{\prime}_{n},\,x^{\prime\prime}_{1}+\dots+x^{\prime\prime}_{n}\in F$ and $i\leq n$.}\end{gathered}

Thus, the sets
\tilde{\imath}^{-1}(F)
, where

,

, form a base of a Cauchy filter on the product
(X^{n},\mathscr{U}_{X}^{n})
.
Since
(X^{n},\mathscr{U}_{X}^{n})
is complete as a product of complete spaces
(see, e.g.,
[
2
, Theorem 8.3.9]
), it follows that there exists an

(\tilde{x}_{1},\dots,\tilde{x}_{n})\in\bigcap\{\overline{\tilde{\imath}^{-1}(F)}:F\in\mathscr{F},\ F\subset F^{*}\}
. Clearly,

\imath(\tilde{x}_{1},\dots,\tilde{x}_{n})\in\bigcap\overline{\mathscr{F}}
,
because
\tilde{\imath}^{-1}(\overline{F})
is closed and hence

\overline{\tilde{\imath}^{-1}(F)}\subset\tilde{\imath}^{-1}(\overline{F})
for
every
F\in\mathscr{F}
such that
F\subset F^{*}
.

For every

(8)

According to

\varrho

such that

(9)

Let
g
