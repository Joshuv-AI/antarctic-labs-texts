Liver ischemia-reperfusion promotes tumor metastasis via neutrophil-endothelial interactions: targeting ICAM1 to prevent metastatic recurrence
Authors: Mingming Fan, Xukang Gao, Jianbo Lin, Wei Chen, Zeping Han, Hao Xiao, Chenhao Zhou, Qiongzhu Dong, Shaoqing Liu, Weifeng Tan, Zhicong Zhao, Xiaoni Kong, Min Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLiver ischemia-reperfusion (IR) injury, commonly encountered in liver surgery and transplantation, has been linked to enhanced metastatic potential in hepatic tumors. Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and colorectal cancer (CRC) liver metastases remain major clinical challenges worldwide, contributing significantly to cancer-related morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. Hepatic resection and liver transplantation are potentially curative interventions for HCC and CRC liver metastases. However, these procedures inevitably involve hepatic ischemia-reperfusion (IR) injury, which has been increasingly recognized as a critical factor promoting tumor recurrence and metastasis in the postoperative period [3, 4]. IR injury triggers a cascade of sterile inflammatory responses, beginning with a burst of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and endothelial damage, followed by the recruitment of innate immune cells, particularly neutrophils, which exacerbate tissue injury and remodel the tumor microenvironment [5, 6].

Neutrophils are central mediators in hepatic IR injury; both experimental models and clinical transplant studies have shown that depletion or inhibition of neutrophils attenuates tissue inflammation and damage [7]. During reperfusion, activated endothelial cells upregulate adhesion molecules such as intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM1), facilitating neutrophil adherence and transmigration into hepatic parenchyma [8, 9]. Notably, ICAM1 expression is upregulated not only locally within the ischemic liver but also systemically, potentially contributing to multiorgan dysfunction [10]. Functional blockade of ICAM1 has been shown to reduce leukocyte infiltration and mitigate IR-induced hepatic damage [11].

Mounting evidence suggests a bidirectional interaction between neutrophils and tumor cells. Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) can serve as physical scaffolds for circulating tumor cells (CTCs), enhancing their adhesion, extravasation, and metastatic colonization in the liver and lungs [12, 13]. Although previous studies have reported that IR exacerbates liver metastasis [14, 15], the specific roles of neutrophils and endothelial ICAM1 in IR-driven cancer progression remain incompletely understood.

Atorvastatin, a widely used HMG-CoA reductase inhibitor, has well-documented pleiotropic effects beyond lipid lowering [16]. Notably, atorvastatin attenuates endothelial dysfunction and reduces ICAM1 expression in models of hepatic IR injury [10, 17]. It has also been shown to alleviate neutrophil-mediated liver injury and improve outcomes following IR, potentially through modulation of TLR4/NF-κB signaling and nitric oxide synthase activity [18]. Despite these observations, the impact of atorvastatin on IR-enhanced tumor metastasis remains unexplored.

In this study, we employed murine models of HCC and CRC liver and lung metastasis combined with hepatic IR and pharmacologic ICAM1 inhibition using atorvastatin. We found that IR markedly increased hepatic and pulmonary metastasis and reduced OS. Mechanistically, IR-induced upregulation of endothelial ICAM1 promoted neutrophil adhesion and facilitated tumor cell transendothelial migration. Importantly, atorvastatin effectively suppressed metastasis in both liver and lung, improved survival, and reduced ICAM1 expression and neutrophil infiltration. Complementary in vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed that ICAM1 on both endothelial and tumor cells mediated neutrophil-tumor cell interactions that were critical for metastatic progression; depletion of neutrophils or silencing of ICAM1 abrogated these effects. Clinically, HCC patient samples with high ICAM1 expression exhibited increased neutrophil infiltration, more aggressive pathological features, and worse RFS and OS, especially when both ICAM1 expression and neutrophil levels were elevated.

Collectively, these findings support a model in which hepatic IR injury reshapes the tumor microenvironment by upregulating ICAM1 on hepatic and distant organ endothelium, thereby facilitating neutrophil-tumor cell interactions that promote metastatic seeding and outgrowth. Atorvastatin interrupts this cascade by suppressing ICAM1 expression and neutrophil recruitment, offering a potential adjuvant strategy to mitigate perioperative metastasis in patients undergoing liver surgery.

In conclusion, this study highlights the pivotal role of the endothelial ICAM1-neutrophil axis in IR-enhanced metastasis and proposes targeting endothelial ICAM1 (e.g., via atorvastatin) as a dual-action therapeutic strategy to alleviate reperfusion injury and improve oncologic outcomes in hepatic surgery.

Methods

Patient cohort and clinical data collection

We retrospectively analyzed a cohort of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) patients who underwent surgical resection at the Eastern Hepatobiliary Surgery Hospital (EHBH) between September 2014 and August 2019. Patients were stratified into two groups based on the presence or absence of Pringle Maneuver (PM) during surgery. Clinical data, including OS and RFS, were collected and analyzed. Additionally, survival data from another cohort of HCC patients who underwent surgical resection at Renji Hospital between September 2011 and August 2016 were included for comparative analysis. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were used to assess OS and RFS, while multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression was conducted to identify independent prognostic factors. For the development of the tissue microarray(TMA), 168 paired specimens (comprising malignant tumor tissues and adjacent non-neoplastic tissues) were collected from HCC patients who received therapeutic interventions EHBH during the 12-month interval between January 2010 and January 2011. All tissue samples underwent formal pathological validation by board-certified pathologists prior to inclusion in the study array. The study was approved by the Ethics Committees of EHBH and Renji Hospital, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Mouse liver ischemia-reperfusion injury and liver/lung metastasis models

Male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and housed under standard laboratory conditions with ad libitum access to food and water, 50–60% relative humidity, a temperature of 22–26 °C, and a 12-h light/dark cycle. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Renji Hospital.

Liver ischemia-reperfusion(IR) injury was induced by clamping the portal vein and hepatic artery supplying 75% of the liver for 90 min, followed by 6 h of reperfusion [19]. Briefly, after anesthesia with tribromoethanol, a midline laparotomy was performed to expose the liver. A non-traumatic microvascular clamp was used to occlude the portal triad to the median and left lateral lobes. Sham-operated mice underwent the same procedure without vascular occlusion. After reperfusion, mice were euthanized for collection of liver and serum samples.

To establish the hepatic ischemia–reperfusion (IR)–associated liver metastasis model, mice were subjected to partial hepatic ischemia for 90 min. Two × 10⁶ CFSE-labeled Hep1-6 or MC38 cells in 100 µL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were then injected into the lower pole of the spleen under anesthesia to generate liver metastases. Fifteen minutes after intrasplenic injection, the spleen was surgically removed to prevent intrasplenic tumor growth and peritoneal dissemination while allowing sufficient time for tumor cells to enter the portal circulation. This procedure is a standard and widely accepted approach for establishing liver-specific metastasis in mice [20–22]. Alternatively, 2 × 10⁶ CFSE-labeled tumor cells in 200 µL PBS were injected via the tail vein to model lung metastasis. Mice were euthanized 2 h later for fluorescent imaging of early tumor cell infiltration or 4 weeks later for histological analysis of established metastases in the liver or lungs.

Cell lines

Human hepatocellular carcinoma cell line Huh-7, murine hepatoma cell line Hep1-6, murine colorectal cancer cell line MC38, and promyelocytic leukemia cell line HL-60 were obtained from the Chinese National Cell Bank (Shanghai, China). Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC) were purchased from Procell Life Science&Technology Co.,Ltd. MHCC-LM3 was kindly provided by the Liver Cancer Institute, Fudan University (Shanghai, China). HCC and CRC cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA). HL-60 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% FBS. HUVEC cells were cultured in ECM dedicated medium containing 5% FBS, 1% ECGS, and 1% P/S. All cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.

ICAM1 overexpression and knockdown cell lines

Lentiviral vectors encoding ICAM1 or ICAM1-targeting short hairpin RNA (shRNA) were purchased from GeneChem (Shanghai, China). Cells were seeded at 70–80% confluence and transduced in the presence of Polybrene according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Empty vector was used as a negative control. After 16 h, the medium was replaced, and selection with puromycin was initiated 48 h post-transduction. Stable cell lines were validated via quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and Western blot analysis.

Liver function assays

Serum levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) were measured using commercial assay kits (C009-2-1 and C010-2-1, Jiancheng Bioengineering, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Co-culture assays

HL-60 cells were differentiated into neutrophil-like cells (HL-60Neu) using dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and activated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) as described previously [23, 24].

For neutrophil-tumor cell binding assays, 1 × 10^5 Dil-labeled AdHL-60Neu cells (resuspended in 3 ml RPMI-1640) and 5 × 10^4 CFSE-labeled HCC cells (resuspended in 3 ml DMEM) were co-incubated in T25 flasks on a shaker at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by seeding into 6-well plates (2 ml per well). After a 30-minute incubation to facilitate tumor cell adherence, the medium was gently aspirated, unbound neutrophils were washed away with PBS, and fresh RPMI-1640 was slowly added, and cells were imaged under fluorescence microscopy to evaluate interactions among AdHL-60Neu and HCC cells.

For neutrophil-endothelial-tumor cell interaction assays, 1 × 10^6 HUVEC cells were first seeded into 6-well plates and cultured in ECM medium until full confluence. Subsequently, 1 × 10^6 CFSE-labeled AdHL-60Neu cells (resuspended in 3 ml RPMI-1640) and 5 × 10^4 Dil-labeled HCC cells (resuspended in 3 ml DMEM) were co-incubated in T25 flasks on a shaker at 37 °C for 30 min, then seeded onto the HUVEC monolayers in 6-well plates (2 ml per well). After a 10-minute incubation, the medium was gently aspirated, unbound neutrophils were washed away with PBS, fresh RPMI-1640 was slowly added, and cells were imaged under fluorescence microscopy to evaluate interactions among HUVEC, AdHL-60Neu, and HCC cells.

Neutrophil depletion and ICAM1 neutralization

To evaluate the effect of ICAM1 blockade on neutrophil-mediated tumor cell metastasis, mice were intraperitoneally (i.p.) injected with 500 µg of anti-Ly6G antibody (Bio X Cell, BE0075) for neutrophil depletion or 300 µg of anti-ICAM1 antibody (Bio X Cell, BE0020-1) for ICAM1 neutralization on days − 2 and 0, with the day 0 injection administered 4 h prior to the experiment [25, 26]. Control mice received an equal dose of isotype control antibody (IgG, Bio X Cell, BE0089). Neutrophils were isolated from peripheral blood of IR-treated mice using a mouse neutrophil isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec, #130-097-658) following the manufacturer’s protocol. 5 × 10^6 purified neutrophils were transferred via tail vein injection immediately after neutrophil depletion (day 0, 4 h post-anti-Ly6G administration).

Flow cytometry analysis

To assess the efficacy of neutrophil depletion or transfer in peripheral blood and liver tissue, flow cytometry was performed at designated time points. Neutrophils were defined as CD45⁺CD11b⁺Ly6G⁺ cells, and their absolute counts were expressed as the percentage of neutrophils among total leukocytes. To confirm antibody specificity and minimize non-specific binding, isotype controls were included in parallel, and fluorescence-minus-one (FMO) controls were utilized to establish accurate gating strategies.

Peripheral blood flow cytometry

Whole blood was collected by cardiac puncture and treated with ACK lysis buffer to remove red blood cells. The resulting leukocyte suspension was stained with the following fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies from BioLegend (San Diego, CA, USA): PerCP-Cy5.5-anti-mouse CD45 (clone 30-F11, #103132), FITC-anti-mouse CD11b (clone M1/70, #101206), and APC-anti-mouse Ly6G (clone 1A8, #127614). A sequential gating strategy was applied as follows: Leukocytes were selected based on forward scatter (FSC-A) and side scatter (SSC-A) properties; Doublets were excluded by comparing FSC-A and FSC-H; CD45⁺ hematopoietic cells were gated using PerCP-Cy5.5-CD45 versus FSC-H; Neutrophils were identified as CD11b⁺Ly6G⁺ cells in the FITC-CD11b vs. APC-Ly6G plot.

Liver tissue flow cytometry

Liver tissues were enzymatically digested using collagenase IV and DNase I, followed by filtration and Percoll gradient centrifugation to isolate leukocytes. The enriched cells were stained with the same antibody panel as used for peripheral blood, with the addition of a fixable viability dye (Zombie-BV510, #423102, BioLegend) to exclude dead cells. The gating strategy included the following steps: Leukocytes were selected based on FSC-A and SSC-A; Doublets were excluded via FSC-A vs. FSC-H; Viable cells were selected as Zombie-BV510-negative; CD45⁺ cells were gated using PerCP-Cy5.5-CD45 versus FSC-H; Neutrophils were identified as CD11b⁺Ly6G⁺ in the FITC-CD11b vs. APC-Ly6G plot.

All flow cytometry data were acquired on a BD LSRFortessa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) and analyzed using FlowJo software (Tree Star Inc.).

Histology, immunohistochemistry, and multiplex immunofluorescence

Liver and lung tissues were fixed in paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was used to evaluate hepatic histopathology and Suzuki score for liver injury [19]. ICAM1 expression in liver and lung tissues was examined by immunohistochemistry (IHC) (1:10,000, HA722417, HUABIO). Multiplex immunofluorescence staining of mouse liver, lung, and human HCC tissue arrays was performed using tyramide signal amplification (TSA) kits (Aifang Biological) to detect ICAM1, CD31, and myeloperoxidase (MPO) (1:500, HA722417/HA723089, HUABIO) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

In vitro migration and invasion assays

For migration assays, neutrophils and HCC cells (Huh-7 or MHCC-LM3) were suspended in serum-free RPMI-1640 and seeded into the upper chamber of Transwell inserts. The lower chamber contained RPMI-1640 with 10% FBS. For invasion assays, a monolayer of HUVECs was plated in the upper chamber before adding the neutrophil-tumor cell mixture. After 24 h, non-migrated cells were removed, and cells on the underside of the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and counted under a microscope.

ICAM1 expression analysis

ICAM1 expression in tissues was assessed by qPCR, Western blotting, and IHC. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Takara), and complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (Vazyme). qPCR was performed with the following primers:

Forward Primer (5’ − 3’)Reverse Primer (5’ − 3’) Icam1 TTGGGGACATACGTTACACTCCCGGTGTGTTACATAGCTTGCTG Actb GTGACGTTGACATCCGTAAAGAGCCGGACTCATCGTACTCC

ICAM1 protein levels were detected by Western blot using anti-ICAM1 antibody (HUABIO), and its localization in liver and lung tissues was visualized by IHC and immunofluorescence.

Atorvastatin treatment

To assess the effect of atorvastatin in vivo, mice were administered 10 mg/kg of atorvastatin (Selleck Chemicals) intraperitoneally every other day for 2 weeks prior to liver ischemia. ICAM1 expression in liver endothelial cells was evaluated by qPCR, Western blotting, and IHC. Liver injury and tumor metastasis were assessed by serum ALT/AST measurement and histopathological examination (H&E staining).

Tumor burden assessment

Tumor burden in the liver and lungs was evaluated by visual inspection and pathological analysis. The area of metastasis was quantified from H&E-stained sections using ImageJ software. Metastatic nodules were manually delineated, and the metastatic area was expressed as the percentage of the total tissue area. Five representative sections per organ were analyzed, and the mean value was used for quantification. For survival analysis, tumor-bearing mice were monitored, and Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For comparisons between two groups, the Student’s t-test (for normal data) or the Mann-Whitney U test (for non-normal data) was applied. For comparisons among more than two groups, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test (for normal data) or the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post-hoc correction (for non-normal data) was used. Survival analyses were performed using Kaplan-Meier curves (compared with the log-rank test) and Cox proportional hazards regression models, with propensity score methods (matching or IPTW) employed to address confounding. The false discovery rate (FDR) in transcriptomic analyses was controlled using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. All statistical tests are two-sided, and exact p-values, relevant effect sizes (e.g., Hedges’ g), and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported in the figures. All histologic scoring and assessments, including evaluation of metastasis and injury, were performed by evaluators blinded to group allocations. A p-value < 0.05 (or an FDR q-value < 0.05 for multiplex assays) was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 10.6.1) and R software (version 4.5.1). Schematic illustrations were created using the online platform: https://www.cnsknowall.com/#/HomePage.

Results

Liver Ischemia-Reperfusion (IR) promotes hepatic tumor metastasis and recurrence

Previous studies have suggested that liver ischemia-reperfusion (IR) may promote hepatic tumor metastasis, although the findings remain controversial [27–31]. To further investigate the role of IR in tumor metastasis, we first analyzed the clinical outcomes of patients undergoing liver resection for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) at our center. Patients were divided into portal occlusion and non-occlusion groups. Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed significantly better recurrence-free survival (RFS) and overall survival (OS) in the non-occlusion group compared to the portal occlusion group (Fig. 1A). This finding was validated in an independent cohort from another institution (Fig. 1B). Moreover, when the occlusion group was subdivided into short-term (≤ 15 min) and long-term (>15 min) occlusion, patients with prolonged occlusion exhibited poorer outcomes (Figure S1A-B). Multivariate Cox regression analysis identified portal occlusion as an independent risk factor for both RFS and OS (Fig. 1C-D; Table S1-2). These data suggest that hepatic IR negatively affects HCC prognosis.

To experimentally validate this, a murine hepatic IR model was established, as confirmed by elevated serum ALT and AST levels (Fig. 1E) [19]. Following 90 min of ischemia, splenic injection of tumor cells was used to generate hepatic metastases (Figure S1C). Compared to controls, mice subjected to IR exhibited significantly increased hepatic tumor burden (Fig. 1F-G) and reduced survival (Fig. 1H). Similar findings were observed in a colorectal cancer (CRC) liver metastasis model, confirming that IR promotes hepatic metastasis and worsens survival (Figure S1D-E).

Neutrophils mediate IR-induced hepatic tumor metastasis

Our previous studies and others have demonstrated neutrophil infiltration following hepatic IR and implicated neutrophils in tumor metastasis [32, 33]. To test whether neutrophils mediate IR-induced metastasis, we depleted neutrophils using anti-Ly-6G antibody. Flow cytometric analysis confirmed the high efficacy of this depletion, significantly reducing neutrophil counts in both peripheral blood and liver homogenates compared to isotype control antibody (Figure S2A-B). Neutrophil depletion significantly reduced hepatic tumor burden (Fig. 2A-B). At 2 h post-injection, direct observation of hepatic tissue revealed markedly fewer tumor cells in the neutrophil-depleted group (Fig. 2C-D), suggesting that neutrophils facilitate early tumor cell infiltration into liver parenchyma.

Previous reports have shown that neutrophils can directly bind to tumor cells to promote metastasis [34]. We co-cultured HCC cell line Huh-7 with activated neutrophil-like AdHL-60Neu cells and observed specific adhesion, which was not seen with non-activated HL-60 cells (Fig. 2E-F). Similar results were observed in another HCC cell line, MHCC-LM3 (Figure S2A). Adoptive transfer of neutrophils from IR-treated mice into neutrophil-depleted recipients enhanced tumor burden, whereas transfer of neutrophils from sham-treated mice had no effect (Fig. 2G-H). However, in vitro migration assays showed that activated neutrophils alone did not enhance tumor cell motility (Figure S2B-C).

Given that extravasation requires tumor-endothelial adhesion, we hypothesized that IR may alter endothelial function. In Transwell assays with a HUVEC monolayer, co-culture with neutrophils significantly enhanced tumor cell invasion (Fig. 2I), suggesting that neutrophils promote hepatic metastasis by facilitating tumor-endothelial adhesion.

IR induces upregulation of ICAM1 in liver endothelial cells

To identify adhesion molecules responsible for neutrophil-mediated tumor infiltration, we analyzed two hepatic IR transcriptomic datasets and intersected differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) adhesion-related gene sets. Three adhesion molecules were significantly upregulated following IR, among which ICAM1 exhibited the most pronounced and consistent upregulation, showing the highest fold change and statistical significance in both datasets (Fig. 3A and Figure S3A). Time-course data showed that ICAM1 expression increased as early as 30 min post-reperfusion and plateaued thereafter (Fig. 3B). In our IR mouse model, qPCR and Western blotting confirmed hepatic ICAM1 upregulation (Fig. 3C-D). IHC localized increased ICAM1 expression to areas of hepatic damage (Fig. 3E).

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis of IR liver tissue further indicated that ICAM1 was predominantly upregulated in endothelial cells (Fig. 3F), which was validated by IHC and co-staining with endothelial marker CD31 (Figure S3B, Fig. 3G). These findings indicate that hepatic IR induces ICAM1 expression primarily in liver endothelial cells.

ICAM1-mediated neutrophil-tumor-endothelial interaction drives tumor infiltration

To evaluate the functional importance of ICAM1 in neutrophil-mediated tumor invasion, we performed ICAM1 knockdown or overexpression in HUVECs and tumor cells (Figure S4A-D). Co-culture assays showed that ICAM1 knockdown in HUVECs reduced tumor-neutrophil-endothelial adhesion but did not affect neutrophil-tumor binding. Interestingly, ICAM1 neutralizing antibody blocked both tumor-endothelial and tumor-neutrophil adhesion, suggesting ICAM1 is involved on both cell types. Consistently, ICAM1 knockdown in tumor cells also reduced their binding to neutrophils and to endothelial cells, while neutrophil-endothelium adhesion remained unaffected (Fig. 4A). These data suggest that ICAM1 expression on both tumor and endothelial cells is critical for neutrophil-facilitated tumor transmigration. Previous studies have shown that ICAM1 expression is influenced by various inflammatory factors [35]. After liver IR, multiple immune-inflammatory factors are elevated [36]. To determine whether tumor cells in the bloodstream are affected by these circulating inflammatory factors, we stimulated mouse tumor cells with mouse serum in vitro. Western blot analysis showed that serum from IR-treated mice did not promote any changes in ICAM1 expression in mouse tumor cells (Figure S4F), indicating that tumor ICAM1 is not induced by systemic inflammation alone.

In transwell invasion assays, ICAM1 knockdown in either tumor or endothelial cells reduced neutrophil-mediated tumor invasion; dual knockdown further enhanced this effect (Fig. 4B-C). Conversely, ICAM1 overexpression increased transmigration (Figure S4G). In vivo, adoptive transfer of IR-activated neutrophils promoted tumor invasion, which was attenuated by ICAM1 neutralization (Fig. 4D-F; Figure S4H), indicating that ICAM1 is essential for neutrophil-mediated metastasis in IR.

Atorvastatin suppresses endothelial ICAM1 expression and reduces IR-induced tumor metastasis

Atorvastatin has been reported to downregulate ICAM1 expression in endothelial cells [37]. In our study, intraperitoneal administration of atorvastatin significantly reduced ICAM1 expression in IR livers at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 5A-B). IHC confirmed decreased ICAM1 expression in endothelial cells (Fig. 5C). Mechanistically, we investigated the NF-κB pathway, a key regulator of ICAM1 transcription. We found that hepatic IR injury markedly induced the phosphorylation of the NF-κB subunit p65, which was potently suppressed by atorvastatin treatment (Figure S5A). Consistent with a causal role of this pathway, the NF-κB inhibitor BAY 11-7082 effectively abrogated the upregulation of both p65 phosphorylation and ICAM1 protein levels in IR livers (Figure S5B). This atorvastatin-mediated inhibition of the NF-κB/ICAM1 axis was corroborated in vitro: in TNFα-stimulated HUVECs, atorvastatin markedly suppressed the induction of both phosphorylated p65 and ICAM1(Figure S5C). Furthermore, the suppressive effects of atorvastatin were reversed by the addition of mevalonate (MVA), indicating that the inhibition is dependent on the blockade of the mevalonate pathway(Figure S5C).

Atorvastatin also reduced serum ALT/AST levels and ameliorated hepatic histological injury (Fig. 5D-F). In vitro Transwell assays showed that atorvastatin inhibited neutrophil-mediated tumor transmigration (Fig. 5G). In vivo, atorvastatin reduced hepatic tumor burden and improved survival following IR (Fig. 5H-J). These effects were similarly observed in the CRC liver metastasis model, suggesting a broader anti-metastatic effect (Figure S6A-C).

Liver IR enhances pulmonary ICAM1 expression and promotes lung metastasis

Previous research has indicated that liver IR plays a role in facilitating distant metastases, particularly to the lungs [38]. To determine whether this is mediated by increased ICAM1 expression on pulmonary endothelial cells, we analyzed transcriptomic data from a lung injury model and found that ICAM1 expression was elevated in LPS-induced lung injury (Fig. 6A). Consistently, IHC staining of lung tissue from the IR model revealed increased ICAM1 expression on endothelial cells (Fig. 6B). Co-staining with CD31 confirmed specific upregulation of ICAM1 in pulmonary endothelial cells (Fig. 6C-D).

At 2 h post-injection, more tumor cells were observed infiltrating the lungs in IR mice compared to controls, and atorvastatin treatment reduced pulmonary tumor infiltration (Fig. 6E). In vivo experiments showed that liver IR promoted lung metastasis and worsened survival, while atorvastatin treatment mitigated these effects (Fig. 6F-H). These findings were recapitulated in the CRC lung metastasis model (Figure S7A-B), suggesting that IR-induced pulmonary ICAM1 upregulation facilitates distant metastasis, which can be prevented by atorvastatin.

ICAM1 expression in HCC correlates with neutrophil infiltration and poor prognosis

Previous studies have demonstrated that ICAM1 is highly expressed in HCC tissues and is closely associated with patient prognosis [39, 40]. In our study, elevated ICAM1 expression in HCC tissues was significantly correlated with several aggressive pathological features, including elevated serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), presence of microvascular invasion (MVI), increased tumor number, poor differentiation, advanced TNM stage, and higher Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage (Fig. 7A, Table S3). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed that patients with high ICAM1 expression exhibited significantly shorter OS and RFS (Figure S8A), further confirming the prognostic value of ICAM1 in HCC.

To explore the potential mechanisms by which ICAM1 contributes to HCC progression, we investigated its role in neutrophil recruitment. Our previous findings indicated that ICAM1 mediates the interaction between tumor cells and neutrophils. Multiplex immunofluorescence staining showed that HCC tissues with high ICAM1 expression harbored significantly increased neutrophil infiltration compared to those with low ICAM1 expression (Fig. 7B-C). Survival analysis revealed that patients with both high ICAM1 expression and high neutrophil infiltration had the worst prognosis (Fig. 7D), suggesting that ICAM1 may promote HCC progression by facilitating neutrophil recruitment into the tumor microenvironment.

To functionally validate this hypothesis, we utilized a murine orthotopic HCC model. Overexpression of ICAM1 in tumor cells significantly increased tumor burden, while neutrophil depletion markedly suppressed tumor growth (Fig. 7E-F). Flow cytometry analysis further confirmed that ICAM1 overexpression promoted intratumoral neutrophil infiltration (Fig. 7G-H).

To examine the role of ICAM1 in metastasis, we employed a splenic injection liver metastasis model. Tumor cells overexpressing ICAM1 exhibited enhanced metastatic potential, which was substantially reduced following neutrophil depletion (Fig. 7I). These results are consistent with those observed in the orthotopic tumor model, indicating that ICAM1 plays a critical role in both tumor progression and metastasis, likely through its modulation of neutrophil behavior.

Collectively, our findings suggest that ICAM1 contributes to the creation of a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment by orchestrating neutrophil recruitment and infiltration. This ICAM1-neutrophil axis may serve as a key mechanism underlying HCC progression and metastasis and represents a promising therapeutic target for future intervention strategies.

Discussion

Liver IR injury is a common clinical occurrence during hepatic surgery and transplantation. Beyond its direct cytotoxic effects on hepatic parenchyma, accumulating evidence indicates that IR profoundly influences tumor biology, notably by promoting postoperative metastasis and recurrence. In this study, we identified a critical mechanism through which IR injury enhances liver and lung metastases via upregulation of ICAM1 on vascular endothelial cells. This upregulation establishes a pivotal adhesive interface that facilitates neutrophil binding to circulating tumor cells (CTCs), thereby promoting tumor cell extravasation and metastatic colonization. Importantly, pharmacological inhibition of ICAM1 by atorvastatin significantly mitigated neutrophil-mediated metastasis in the context of IR injury. Furthermore, both clinical and experimental data reveal that elevated ICAM1 expression in HCC is associated with increased neutrophil infiltration, accelerated tumor progression and metastasis, and poorer prognosis. These findings offer novel insights into the interplay between IR-induced inflammation, neutrophil activation, and cancer metastasis, with significant implications for perioperative management of patients with HCC and CRC undergoing hepatic resection.

Liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs) play a pivotal role in maintaining vascular homeostasis and regulating immune cell trafficking. IR injury triggers a cascade of inflammatory responses, leading to endothelial activation characterized by increased expression of adhesion molecules, including ICAM1 [36]. Consistent with previous findings, we observed significant upregulation of ICAM1 on LSECs following hepatic IR, likely driven by pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) released during reperfusion [41]. This activation state renders the endothelial surface highly adhesive, promoting neutrophil recruitment and retention within the hepatic microvasculature.

As first responders to tissue damage and inflammation, neutrophils rapidly accumulate in the liver following IR injury [42]. ICAM1 on endothelial cells interacts with β2 integrins (lymphocyte function-associated antigen-1 (LFA-1) and macrophage-1 antigen (Mac-1) on neutrophils, facilitating firm adhesion and transendothelial migration [43]. Our data demonstrate that these adherent neutrophils act as physical bridges between ICAM1-expressing endothelial cells and circulating tumor cells, significantly enhancing CTC adhesion under physiological shear stress and increasing their entrapment within hepatic vasculature.

Moreover, neutrophil-endothelium-tumor cell interactions promote tumor cell extravasation—a critical step in metastasis. Neutrophils actively contribute to this process by releasing proteases, ROS, and neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), which remodel the extracellular matrix and facilitate transendothelial migration [44]. Thus, the interplay between ICAM1 and neutrophils establishes a pro-metastatic microenvironment following IR injury.

Importantly, our findings extend beyond the liver. Hepatic IR triggered a systemic inflammatory response, resulting in ICAM1 upregulation on pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells. This mirrored the hepatic endothelial response and provides a mechanistic explanation for the increased lung metastasis observed after hepatic IR. The lung is a frequent site of distant metastasis for both HCC and CRC [45, 46], and the ICAM1-mediated recruitment of neutrophils to the pulmonary endothelium likely facilitates tumor cell extravasation and colonization in the lung vasculature.

This dual-site endothelial activation underscores the systemic nature of IR-induced inflammation and its widespread impact on metastatic progression. The pulmonary endothelial response may be driven by circulating inflammatory mediators released during hepatic reperfusion, including cytokines, damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), and extracellular vesicles. These findings highlight the need to consider both local and distant vascular beds in understanding and managing post-surgical metastasis.

Our study also evaluated the therapeutic potential of targeting ICAM1 to prevent IR-induced metastasis. Atorvastatin, a widely used statin with known anti-inflammatory properties, effectively downregulated ICAM1 expression on liver and lung endothelial cells, resulting in reduced neutrophil recruitment and impaired tumor cell adhesion and extravasation. The statin-mediated inhibition of endothelial activation may be attributed to suppression of the nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) signaling pathway, which regulates ICAM1 transcription in response to inflammatory stimuli [47, 48]. These findings suggest that perioperative statin therapy could be a promising strategy to reduce postoperative metastatic recurrence by modulating endothelial-neutrophil interactions.

Multiple clinical studies have reported beneficial effects of statins in reducing cancer progression and metastasis, although the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood [49–51]. Our findings provide direct mechanistic evidence that statins can disrupt critical interactions among tumor cells, neutrophils, and endothelial cells in the aftermath of IR injury, further supporting the rationale for clinical trials investigating statins as adjuvant therapies to improve long-term oncologic outcomes after tumor resection.

Although IR injury did not directly upregulate ICAM1 expression in tumor cells in our models, we found that tumor cells with inherently high ICAM1 levels were more likely to associate with infiltrating neutrophils. Moreover, analyses of patient samples revealed a strong correlation between high tumor ICAM1 expression, increased neutrophil infiltration, and poorer OS. These observations are consistent with previous reports showing that tumor cell-expressed ICAM1 facilitates heterotypic interactions with immune cells, contributing to immune evasion and metastatic dissemination [52–56]. Therefore, while IR may not directly modulate tumor ICAM1 levels, its baseline expression may still critically influence metastatic behavior, especially in the context of IR-enhanced neutrophilic inflammation.

In addition to ICAM1, other adhesion molecules such as vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1), E-selectin, and P-selectin are also involved in leukocyte-endothelium and tumor-leukocyte interactions. While we focused on ICAM1 due to its dominant role in neutrophil adhesion, future studies should investigate the cooperative roles of multiple adhesion molecules in the metastatic cascade following IR injury. Furthermore, characterizing neutrophil subpopulations and their functional heterogeneity may uncover additional therapeutic targets in tumor progression.

Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrated a novel mechanism whereby hepatic IR injury promotes neutrophil-mediated tumor cell extravasation and metastasis via ICAM1 upregulation in liver and lung endothelial cells. Although IR does not directly alter tumor ICAM1 expression, intrinsic ICAM1 levels in tumor cells are strongly associated with neutrophil interactions and adverse prognoses. Pharmacological suppression of ICAM1 using atorvastatin effectively mitigated these pro-metastatic interactions, offering a translational strategy to reduce metastatic recurrence after hepatic surgery. These findings highlight the critical role of the TME, particularly the vascular and immune compartments, in shaping metastatic outcomes following IR injury. Targeting the endothelial-neutrophil axis represents a promising therapeutic avenue to improve the prognosis of patients undergoing liver resection for cancer.
