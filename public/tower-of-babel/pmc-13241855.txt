Achaete-Scute-Like 1a Expression Is Essential for the Generation of Correct Proportions of Neuron Subtypes in the Developing Enteric Nervous System of Zebrafish (Danio rerio)
Authors: Nastasia Popowycz, Leen Uyttebroek, Jean-Pierre Timmermans, Luc Van Nassauw
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsThe enteric nervous system (ENS) mediates many gastrointestinal functions and develops through processes involving transcription factors, including achaete-scute-like 1a (Ascl1a). In zebrafish, the contribution of Ascl1a to enteric neuronal specification remains unclear, which we aim to unravel.MethodsWe examined spatiotemporal Ascl1a expression in the ENS of wild-type (WT) zebrafish larvae using quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and immunofluorescence staining. Ascl1a function was investigated using piat25215 zebrafish (Ascl1a−/−). Immunofluorescence staining was performed to identify and quantify (immature) enteric neurons and neuronal subtypes.ResultsA peak in Ascl1a mRNA expression was detected at 3.0 days postfertilization (dpf) in the gastrointestinal tract of WT larvae. In their ENS, the proportion of Ascl1-expressing neurons declined between 2.5 and 4.0 dpf. In contrast, ratios of Ascl1+ neural progenitors and immature neurons remained stable between 3.0 and 4.0 dpf. Ascl1a deletion resulted in delayed and suppressed onset of ENS development, with neurons appearing only at 4.0 dpf in significantly lower numbers than in WT larvae. Additionally, neuronal subtype composition was altered with a significant increase in serotonergic and a significant decline in calbindin-expressing neurons, the latter showing delayed emergence at 5.0 instead of 3.0 dpf. Nitrergic neuron ratios were unaffected.ConclusionAscl1a deletion resulted in delayed and partially suppressed ENS development and altered neuronal subtype populations; the calbindin-expressing neuron ratio was significantly reduced, while that of serotonergic neurons was significantly increased. This indicates Ascl1a involvement in both early and late enteric neuronal differentiation and specification.Keywords: Enteric Nervous System, Transcription Factors, Neurogenesis, Neuronal Differentiation and Specification, Enteric Neural Crest Cells

Background and Aims

The enteric nervous system (ENS) mediates many gastrointestinal (GI) functions, including peristaltic movements, absorption of nutrients, and interactions with the GI immune and endocrine systems.1,2 ENS formation starts with the delaminating vagal, and in some species sacral, multipotent neural crest cells, migrating from the neural tube toward the intestine, hereafter referred to as enteric neural crest cells (ENCCs).3, 4, 5 ENCCs undergo further rostro-caudal migration, proliferation, and neuronal differentiation and subtype specifications, regulated by a complex interaction of transcription factors and signaling molecules and influenced by neural activity.6 Factors mediating ENCC migration and proliferation have thoroughly been unraveled, while differentiation and subtype specification processes remain a hot topic being studied.7 Recently, 23 clusters of ENS cells in zebrafish have been identified, ranging from progenitors and neuroblasts to immature and mature neurons. Roles for Eb1fa (generation of inhibitory neuronal subtypes), Gata3 (differentiation of excitatory enteric neurons), and Satb2 (development of serotonergic enteric neurons) were revealed,8 extending the limited list of previous described transcription factors involved in neuronal specification, such as Phox2a,9 Lnsm1b,10 and Meis1b.11

Achaete-scute-like 1 (Ascl1) is a transcription factor belonging to the pro-neural family of basic helix-loop-helix genes. These genes are known to contribute in essential developmental processes, such as the regulation of progenitor pools, neurogenesis, and cell proliferation and differentiation.12,13 Ascl1 was initially identified as a key regulator of neurogenesis, playing an important role in the development of both the central and peripheral nervous systems.14 In mammals, ENCCs and progenitors of the different enteric neuronal subtypes and enteric glia transiently express Ascl1.15, 16, 17, 18, 19 Knock-out studies in rats and mice have shown that Ascl1 is essential for the acquisition of some enteric neuronal subtypes expressing serotonin (5-HT), neuronal nitric oxide synthase (nNOS), calbindin, vasoactive intestinal polypeptide, or tyrosine hydroxylase.15,16,20

In zebrafish, Ascl1 knows 2 ohnologues, Ascl1a and Ascl1b, as a result of an additional whole-genome duplication, also referred to as teleost-specific genome duplication.21 In the zebrafish intestine, it was reported that Ascl1a is responsible for the formation of secretory cells.22,23 Furthermore, Ascl1a was found to be expressed in ENCCs,22,24,25 although 1 study failed to detect Ascl1a in these cells.23 In Ascl1a−/− mutant zebrafish (piat25215), a significant reduction in the number of enteric neurons was observed, combined with a loss of motility in the distal intestine.23

The current study focusses on unraveling the involvement of Ascl1a in ENS development in zebrafish. First, its spatiotemporal expression profile is determined. Next, ENS development of wild-type (WT) zebrafish to those of Ascl1a−/− mutant zebrafish is compared using multiple immunofluorescence (IF) staining procedures for specific ENS targets.

Methods

Mutant Zebrafish Line piat25215

Heterozygous piat25215 adult zebrafish (Ascl1+/−) are obtained from the Zebrafish Development and Disease Models Laboratory (GIGA institute, University of Liege, Belgium). Null mutant zebrafish larvae were collected after breeding of the heterozygous adults.

Housekeeping and Breeding of Zebrafish

Wild-type (WT) and piat25215 heterozygous (Ascl1a+/−) zebrafish are kept in the Laboratory of Human Anatomy and Embryology (Antwerp, Belgium, LA1100125). Our zebrafish facility applies a strict 10 h/14 h—dark/light cycle and the temperature of the water varies between 15 and 27 °C to best mimic their natural surviving conditions.

Breeding takes place between 8:50 and 10 AM, since they breed at dawn in natural conditions.26 The collected eggs are rinsed and then added to embryosolution (methylene blue 0.0001%, E2 medium 0.02%, NaHCO3 0.005%, diluted with AD) and placed in an incubator at 27–28 °C (Memmert).

The zebrafish used for visualization experiments are treated daily with 0.003% phenyl-thio-urea (Sigma, P5272) to block melanogenesis between 1.0 and 5.0 days postfertilization (dpf).

Genotyping piat25215 Zebrafish Embryos and Larvae

Genotyping was performed by both fin clipping and the Zebrafish Embryo Genotyping method.27,28 An attempt was made to correlate various phenotypes (presence of edema, reduced pigmentation, or small eyes) to different genotypes (Ascl1a−/− or Ascl1a+/−) but was unsuccessful.

Nevertheless, distinction between WT and Ascl1a−/− zebrafish embryos and larvae was possible based on the Hu-expression after performing IF staining procedures.

Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (qPCR) for the Determination of Gene Expression Profiles

Zebrafish were euthanized through the induction of a hypothermic shock, followed by an excess of 0.04% tricaine methanesulphonate (4.0 mg/mL, Na2HPO4.12H2O 20.2 mg/mL). The GI tracts were collected at 3.0-, 4.0-, 5.0-, 7.0-, 14.0-, 21.0-, 30.0-, and 90.0- dpf and were pooled per 30 (3.0, 4.0, 5.0, 7.0 dpf) or 10 (14.0, 21.0, 30.0, 90.0 dpf) in RNAlater (Qiagen, 154048876) for RNA preservation. RNA concentration and integrity were analyzed in duplo (Bioanalyser, Agilent). cDNA synthesis (Bio-rad iScript cDNA synthesis kit) was performed, and the expression of Ascl1a and Ascl1b (Supplementary Data 1) was determined. The reference genes used are Rpl7 and Rplp2 (Supplementary Data 2) and were analyzed using the qBASE+ software (Biogazelle).

Spatiotemporal Visualization In Toto and Isolated Gastrointestinal Tracts Using Immunofluorescence

Ascl1-expression in the ENS of WT zebrafish embryos and larvae is verified at 2.5, 3.0, 3.5, and 4.0 dpf. A triple staining procedure using anti-Ascl1 (target marker), anti-Sox10 (neural progenitor marker) and anti-Hu (pan-neuronal marker) antibodies is performed (Supplementary Data 3).

To verify whether the anti-Ascl1 antibody solely detects Ascl1a in the GI tract, and not Ascl1b; an IF staining procedure was performed on Ascl1a−/− zebrafish. No immunoreactivity was observed.

Later, IF was performed at 3.0, 4.0, and 5.0 dpf in both WT and Ascl1a−/− zebrafish. We analyzed the expression of 5-HT, nNOS, and calbindin in neural progenitor cells (Sox10+), and in (immature) enteric neurons ((Sox10+)/Hu+).

Statistical Analysis

Quantification of the enteric neurons, neural progenitors and neuronal subtypes in WT and Ascl1−/− zebrafish was done manually. Images of the GI tracts were taken using a fluorescent microscope equipped with a digital camera (Leica DMLB and Leica DF450 C, respectively) at 20× magnification with multiple fluorescent filters. After counting, the datasets were checked for outliers using Prism software. The remaining data were then further analyzed. P values are represented on the graphs using GraphPad style: 0.1234 (ns), 0.0332 (∗), 0.0021 (∗∗), 0.0002 (∗∗∗), and <0.0001 (∗∗∗∗).

Results

Achaete-Scute-Like 1a and Achaete-Scute-Like 1b Expression in the Gastrointestinal Tract of Wild-Type Zebrafish Larvae

To provide a comprehensive expression profile, quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed to investigate the expression of Ascl1a and Ascl1b in the GI tract during development (3.0 dpf up to 90.0 dpf). Both expression profiles appeared to be similar, representing a high expression between 3.0 and 14.0 dpf, with an exception at 7.0 dpf for Ascl1a (Supplementary Data 4A and B). However, while the individual Ascl1a samples represented reproducible replicates, those of Ascl1b were widely scattered, leading to untrustworthy results. Therefore, Ascl1b was excluded (Supplementary Data 4C and D).

Since the qPCR data provided information about all cell types of the GI tract, indirect IF staining procedures were performed at 2.5, 3.0, 3.5, and 4.0 dpf to observe Ascl1-expression and localization in the ENS (Figure 1, Table 1).

At 2.5 dpf (length GI tract: 901.15 ± 82.68 μm), approximately 25% of neural progenitor cells expressed Ascl1 (Sox10+/Ascl1+), with the proportion increasing along the rostro-caudal axis. Approximately 20% of neurons expressed Ascl1 (Hu+/Ascl1+), and the immature neural population exhibited the highest level of Ascl1-expression with approximately 50% of Hu+/Sox10+ cells being Ascl1+. At 3.0 dpf (length GI tract: 1050.89 ± 44.38 μm), an expansion of neural progenitors and neurons was observed. However, the ratios of Ascl1-expressing neural progenitors and (immature) neurons were reduced. In addition, a large shift of the ratios was observed in the distal intestine. While a reduction of Ascl1-expressing neural progenitors was observed, an increase was found for both the mature and immature enteric neurons expressing Ascl1. At 3.5 dpf (length GI tract: 1161.00 ± 78.63 μm), a significant expansion in the enteric neural progenitor and neuronal cell populations was observed. This expansion has also led to a change in their overall spatial distribution. While these cells were primarily found nearby the gut wall, a noticeable circumferential migration toward the lumen of the GI tract was observed. In addition, the overall Ascl1-expressing neural progenitors and neurons remained stable. The immature neural population expressing Ascl1, however, decreased slightly. At 4.0 dpf (length GI tract: 1430.99 ± 50.20 μm), the neural progenitor cell population significantly decreased (P < .0001). Nevertheless, the total ratio of Ascl1-expressing neural progenitors remained stable. In contrast, the number of neurons continued to increase significantly (P < .0001), displayed by a circumferential distribution. This stage was also characterized by an overall reduction in the proportions of Ascl1-expressing (immature) neurons and neural progenitors, with expression shifted toward the caudal region compared to earlier stages.

The results obtained between 2.5 and 4.0 dpf confirm Ascl1-expression in neural cells of the GI tract of zebrafish embryos and larvae (Table 1). Ascl1 was detected in a subset of neural progenitors and in subsets of (immature) neurons. The overall expression of enteric neural progenitors expressing Ascl1 was most abundant at 2.5 dpf, after which it reduced and stabilized at 3.0, 3.5, and 4.0 dpf. The subset of Ascl1-expressing enteric neurons was also peaking at 2.5 dpf and showed the lowest ratio at 4.0 dpf. Interestingly, the cell population representing the highest Ascl1-expressing ratio at all stages investigated, was that of the immature enteric neurons.

Enteric Nervous System Development in piat25215 Zebrafish Larvae

Already early in development, a marked difference in the ENS was observed between wild-type (WT) and piat25215 (hereafter referred to as “pia”) zebrafish larvae (Figure 3). At 3.0 dpf, a complete lack of enteric neurons was observed in pia zebrafish, while the ENS was already being formed in their WT counterparts. At 4.0 dpf, although a modest increase was noted, the number of neurons in the GI tract of pia zebrafish remained scarce. The ENS of the WT zebrafish was marked by a substantial increase in the number of neurons at the same stage of development, leading to a significant difference between the 2 genotypes (P < .0001). At 5.0 dpf, when the ENS is expected to be functional, the ENS of WT zebrafish showed approximately a threefold increase in the number of enteric neurons compared to the number observed at 3.0 dpf. In contrast, only a small number of neurons was found in the pia counterparts at 5.0 dpf, exacerbating the developmental lag in ENS development. Overall, these results proved a strong delay and partial suppression of ENS development caused by Ascl1a deletion. The expression of the neural progenitor marker Sox10 was also observed and compared in both WT and pia zebrafish larvae. While a gradual decline in neural progenitor cells (Sox10+) was observed in both genotypes, the population was consistently and significantly more abundant in the pia zebrafish larvae than in WT controls. Comparing the immature enteric neuron populations (Sox10+/Hu+), a significant decrease between WT and pia zebrafish larvae was observed (Figure 3, Table 2).

At 3.0 dpf, the expression of nNOS was found in approximately 50% of the neurons in WT zebrafish. At 4.0 dpf, a strong decrease up to 25% of the neurons was observed. Hereafter, however, an increase was noticed at 5.0 dpf to approximately 35%. At 4.0 dpf in pia zebrafish, nNOS-expressing enteric neurons were found in approximately 50% of the GI tracts examined, with considerable variability in their relative abundance. At 5.0 dpf, these neurons were present in all GI tracts examined, and their abundance showed reduced variability. At both 4.0 and 5.0 dpf, no significant difference was found between WT and pia zebrafish regarding the nNOS+ population of enteric neurons (Supplementary Data 6B, Figure 4B, Table 3).

In WT zebrafish, Calbindin was expressed in approximately 5% of enteric neurons at 3.0 dpf. This proportion increased to ∼10% at 4.0 dpf and reached a maximum of nearly 15% at 5.0 dpf. In pia mutants, Calbindin immunoreactivity was significantly delayed, with no Calbindin-expressing neurons detected at 3.0 dpf and 4.0 dpf (P = .0020 and P = .0121, respectively). At 5.0 dpf, Calbindin+ neurons represented ∼7% of the enteric neuronal population (P = .0149) (Supplementary Data 6C, Figure 4C, Table 3).

Conclusion

While the presence of Ascl1a mRNA in the developing GI tract of zebrafish has previously been shown through single cell sequencing30 or riboprobe detection in whole-mount in situ hybridization methods22, we revealed the temporal expression profile using qPCR. We observed a strong expression of Ascl1a mRNA at 3.0 up to 5.0 and at 14.0 dpf. Next, indirect IF detected Ascl1 immunoreactivity in the developing ENS at 2.5–4.0 dpf in neural progenitors (Sox10+/Ascl1+), and mature and immature neurons (Hu+/Ascl1+ and Hu+/Sox10+/Ascl1+, respectively). In a following phase, ENS development in the Ascl1a null mutant zebrafish piat25215 was investigated between 3.0 and 5.0 dpf. A strong delay and partial suppression in enteric neuron generation and expression was noticed. Focusing on 3 enteric subtype markers, we found significant increased serotonergic and decreased Calbindin-expressing neural proportions in pia zebrafish, suggesting an involvement of Ascl1a in the early ENS differentiation. Regarding the nitrergic neurons, no significant difference was observed at both 4.0 and 5.0 dpf.

Discussion

Achaete-Scute-Like 1a Expression and Enteric Nervous System Development

Given the strong expression observed at 3.0 dpf, it is suggested that the onset of Ascl1a-expression occurs earlier in the developing GI tract. This is supported by previous research involving multiple species and therefore additionally showing evolutionary conservation of the gene.17,31, 32, 33 The expansion of the Ascl1a mRNA temporal expression profile during earlier stages of development (<3.0 dpf) would therefore be desirable. However, due to technical limitations, we were unable to isolate sufficient RNA of the GI tracts of zebrafish < 3.0 dpf to gain reliable qPCR results.

During and prior to the observed time points of high expression of Ascl1a, essential processes in ENS development are taking place, such as the arrival of the first enteric neural progenitors in zebrafish in the foregut at 36.0 hours postfertilization, and an initial large neuronal differentiation occurring by 68.0–70.0 hours postfertilization.8,34,35 This coincides with the first peak observed in the Ascl1a mRNA expression profile. Occurring after the early neural differentiation described above, a second wave of neural differentiation occurring at 4.0 dpf is suggested in zebrafish.36

Based on our results and previously conducted research, we conclude that the high expression observed at 3.0 dpf occurs simultaneously with the initial neuronal differentiation, suggesting Ascl1a-involvement early in ENS formation.

Ascl1 in the Developing Gastrointestinal Tract of Wild-Type Zebrafish

We observed Ascl1 immunoreactivity in the ENS of zebrafish embryos and larvae, which was not reported in previous research. While Roach and colleagues23 did not observe Ascl1-expression in WT ENS, they did notice a reduction in the number of neurons and a significant reduced neuronal fiber density in Ascl1a−/− zebrafish larvae, suggesting an indirect involvement of Ascl1a in ENS development. In other vertebrates, Ascl1 was observed early in the developing ENS. In mice, co-expression with HuC/D and Sox10 was reported at early stages, after which only a few Ascl1+/HuC/D+ cells were found between E15.5-E18.0.15 In the rat peripheric nervous system, Ascl1 was transiently expressed in enteric sublineage progenitors, after which the expression disappeared with upcoming appearance of neuron-specific differentiation markers. This pattern is similar to the results obtained in Drosophila melanogaster.17

In line with these findings, we observed transient expression of the protein in zebrafish larvae, which disappeared around the time when neuronal subtype markers become more abundant. This supports a link between Ascl1a and critical phases of ENS development in zebrafish, indicating both direct and indirect roles in the process.

The Impact of Achaete-Scute-Like 1a Deletion on the Developing Enteric Nervous System in Zebrafish

Already early in ENS development, remarkable differences were observed between pia and WT embryos and larvae. A strong delay and partial suppression of ENS formation was observed, which was also reported in the ENS of Ascl1−/− mice. A complete elimination of the enteric neuron population in the foregut located in the wall of the esophagus was described,19 however, some of these studies declared to have obtained untrustworthy results.16,20 Continuing the mice model, it was shown that the delayed formation of the ENS is a result of a reduced number of ENCCs arising from the vagal neural crest.37 In zebrafish, the entire ENS arises from vagal ENCCs, in contrast to other vertebrates.4 This might indicate that Ascl1a is among others responsible for the generation of a sufficient number of ENCCs migrating toward the developing GI tract. However, our results showed a strong increase of Sox10-expressing cells in the pia zebrafish larvae, which are known to be undifferentiated ENCCs in the developing ENS. In addition, Sox10 is shown to boost Ascl1-expression, and Ascl1, on its turn, is proposed to repress Sox10 and encourages the expression of pro-neural genes.38 This last result indicates a prolonged undifferentiated state of the ENCCs due to a prolonged Sox10-expression, and therefore implicates Ascl1a in the neural fate of ENCCs.

Achaete-Scute-Like 1a in Enteric Neuronal Subtype Specification

In this study, we examined 3 enteric neuronal subtype populations (serotonergic [5-HT+/Hu+], nitrergic [nNOS+/Hu+], and calbindin-expressing [Calbindin+/Hu+] neurons) during early ENS development in pia zebrafish larvae at 4.0 and 5.0 dpf, the earliest stages at which enteric neurons are detectable. We observed a significant increase in the serotonergic population, and a concurrent decrease in Calbindin-expressing neurons, relative to the overall ENS. These findings suggest a role for Ascl1a in early neuronal differentiation.

During this developmental window, Kuil and colleagues30,39 described a bifurcation of neural progenitors into 2 branches: the first branch giving rise to sensory intrinsic primary afferent neurons, and the second branch to inhibitory motor neurons expressing nNOS. The latter branch later bifurcates further to generate 5-HT-expressing neurons.30 A more recent study of Li and colleagues8 also described a bifurcation of neural progenitors in 2 major branches: a first branch giving rise to 5 neural subtypes including nitrergic inhibitory motor and serotonergic neurons, while the second branch giving rise to 3 neural subtypes, including Calbindin-expressing neurons.

Interestingly, our results regarding serotonergic neurons partially contradict early studies in Ascl1−/− mice, where serotonergic neurons were reported to be selectively impaired, while other subtypes eventually developed, suggesting the existence of Ascl1-dependent and Ascl1-independent progenitors.20,40 However, subsequent work reported no reduction, but even a slight increase, of serotonergic neurons in Ascl1−/− mice,15 which aligns with our observations in pia zebrafish. Differences in visualization techniques are likely accountable for the discrepancies.

The significant increase in serotonergic neurons in pia mutants suggests that Ascl1a normally restrains the formation of this subtype, maintaining a balanced ratio among enteric neuronal populations. Alternatively, this effect may be indirect: delayed ENS formation could shift serotonergic neuron generation into a more robust neurogenic phase, resulting in a greater number of these neurons.

Similar discrepancies exist for nitrergic neurons in Ascl1−/− mice. While 1 study reports a significant reduction in NOS-immunoreactive neurons in the esophagus,16 another study found a small, nonsignificant increase in the stomach and small intestine.15

Our data, however, revealed that at 4.0 dpf, nitrergic neurons were absent in approximately 50% of the GI tracts examined. In the GI tracts in which nitrergic neurons were detected, their relative abundance varied widely, resulting in a nonsignificant difference compared to the result observed in WT counterparts. At 5.0 dpf, nitrergic neurons were detected in all GI tracts of pia zebrafish; however, substantial variability in their relative abundance persisted in approximately half of the samples analyzed. These findings indicate a delayed and variable emergence of nitrergic neurons in the absence of Ascl1a, without affecting the proportional representation of the enteric neuronal subtype in the GI tract.

With respect to Calbindin-expressing neurons, previous studies in mice indicate that Calbindin is expressed in mature neurons derived from Ascl1-lineage cells,41 and is strongly reduced in Ascl1−/− ENS.15 Our findings in pia zebrafish are consistent with these observations, showing a strong delay in Calbindin-expressing neuron specification. As with other subtypes, this may reflect both direct and indirect consequences of Ascl1a loss, as early-born neurons are particularly susceptible to disturbances in ENS formation.42

Overall, our study indicates that Ascl1a plays a critical role, directly or indirectly, in early ENS formation. Loss of Ascl1a affects ENCC migration and early subtype differentiation, resulting in delayed ENS formation and altered proportions of serotonergic and Calbindin-expressing neurons in the pia zebrafish larvae. In context of recent whole-organ analyses describing the tightly coordinated spatiotemporal dynamics of zebrafish ENS development,8 our findings suggest that Ascl1a contributes to the precise timing and regional progression of early neurogenic events. Disruption of this regulatory role likely leads to missed or shifted neurogenic windows, thereby affecting neuronal subtype composition. Together, these results highlight the importance of Ascl1a in orchestrating both temporal and spatial aspects of ENS development.
