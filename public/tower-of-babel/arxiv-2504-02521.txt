UNDO : UN derstanding D istillation as O ptimization
Kushal Jain, Piyushi Goyal & Kumar Shridhar

Abstract
Knowledge distillation has emerged as an effective strategy for compressing large language models’ (LLMs) knowledge into smaller, more efficient student models. However, standard one-shot distillation methods often produce suboptimal results due to a mismatch between teacher-generated rationales and the student’s specific learning requirements. In this paper, we introduce the UNDO : UN derstanding D istillation as O ptimization framework, designed to bridge this gap by iteratively identifying the student’s errors and prompting the teacher to refine its explanations accordingly. Each iteration directly targets the student’s learning deficiencies, motivating the teacher to provide tailored and enhanced rationales that specifically address these weaknesses. Empirical evaluations on various challenging mathematical and commonsense reasoning tasks demonstrate that our iterative distillation method, UNDO , significantly outperforms standard one-step distillation methods, achieving performance gains of up to 20%. Additionally, we show that teacher-generated data refined through our iterative process remains effective even when applied to different student models, underscoring the broad applicability of our approach. Our work fundamentally reframes knowledge distillation as an iterative teacher–student interaction, effectively leveraging dynamic refinement by the teacher for better knowledge distillation.



1
Introduction

Knowledge distillation has emerged as an effective strategy for compressing large language models (LLMs) into smaller, more efficient student models
(
Hinton et al., 2015
;
Sanh et al., 2020
;
Zhu et al., 2024
)
. Initially, the primary approach involved aligning the student’s outputs with the soft logits of the teacher, enabling the student to approximate the teacher’s distribution. Recently, this paradigm has expanded to include
chain-of-thought
(CoT) reasoning, which seeks to instill smaller models with interpretable intermediate steps rather than simply matching final outputs
(
Shridhar et al., 2023
;
Hsieh et al., 2023
;
Li et al., 2023
;
Chenglin et al., 2024
)
.

Despite these advancements, standard one-shot distillation methods frequently suffer from a distributional mismatch: the rationales generated by the teacher often do not align well with the student’s specific learning challenges
(
Agarwal et al., 2024
;
Adarsh et al., 2024
)
. To overcome this limitation, we introduce the
UNDO
:
UN
derstanding
D
istillation as
O
ptimization framework, an
iterative
distillation strategy that actively identifies and addresses the student’s errors by continuously prompting the teacher to generate refined, targeted rationales. In each iteration, the teacher’s generation and student’s responses are analyzed by the teacher, leading to tailored explanations that directly address the student’s learning gaps. This loop ensures that the training data becomes more personalized to the student’s specific needs, instead of simply reflecting generic teacher behavior.

Our iterative approach is inspired by educational concepts such as
scaffolding

(
Wood et al., 1976
)
and
formative assessment

(
Black et al., 2004
)
, which highlight the importance of adaptive, targeted feedback in effective learning. Similar to a skilled teacher who adjusts instruction based on a learner’s struggles, our method dynamically identifies and addresses the student’s errors, thereby maintaining the learner within their
zone of proximal development
(ZPD)
(
Cole et al., 1978
)
.

Empirical evaluations conducted on challenging mathematical reasoning datasets, including MATH
(
Lightman et al., 2024
)
and MMLU Pro
(
Wang et al., 2024
)
, demonstrate that our iterative distillation method significantly outperforms traditional one-shot distillation, delivering improvements of up to 20%. Furthermore, we show that iteratively refined teacher-generated data maintains its effectiveness across diverse student models, highlighting the robustness and generality of our method. Finally, we show that student models trained using our proposed approach outperform standard distilled models on out-of-domain tasks. Our work reframes knowledge distillation as a dynamic, iterative
teacher–student
interaction, leading to a better learning outcome for the student.

2
Related Work

Knowledge distillation

Extensive research has explored knowledge distillation since
Hinton et al. (2015)
introduced the concept of training smaller student models using soft-label outputs (soft logits) from larger teacher models. Early influential models such as DistilBERT
(
Sanh et al., 2020
)
demonstrated substantial parameter reductions ( 40%) without significant performance losses. Subsequent advancements, like MiniLM
(
Wang et al., 2020
)
, further refined this technique by focusing specifically on distilling the self-attention mechanism. More recent works, including
Shum et al. (2024)
and
Timiryasov & Tastet (2023)
, explored selective training strategies by choosing essential samples from the teacher’s training set.

The integration of Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning
(
Wei et al., 2022
)
into distillation has sparked additional innovations. For example,
Shridhar et al. (2023)
trained student models to explicitly replicate the teacher’s CoT reasoning steps. Similarly,
Li et al. (2023)
generated diverse rationales for individual questions, while
Chenglin et al. (2024)
extended this by incorporating multiple reasoning styles.
Zhu et al. (2024)
distilled mathematical reasoning into structured equations and combined various reasoning modalities (text, code, equations). Further,
Luo et al. (2025)
leveraged reinforcement learning to fine-tune student models. Despite these advancements, most approaches remain one-directional, with teacher outputs independent of student-specific needs. In contrast, our method explicitly addresses the student’s learning gaps by encouraging teacher awareness and adaptation.

Surveys such as
Gou et al. (2021)
and
Yang et al. (2024)
have also comprehensively discussed the benefits and diverse applications of knowledge distillation across multiple domains, including computer vision and natural language processing.

Iterative distillation

Our work draws significant inspiration from iterative forms of knowledge distillation. Previous research often emphasized the transfer of intermediate reasoning steps to smaller models, either sequentially
(
Hsieh et al., 2023
;
Magister et al., 2023
;
Shridhar et al., 2023
)
or concurrently integrating various reasoning strategies
(
Chenglin et al., 2024
;
Zhu et al., 2024
;
Jain et al., 2024
)
. Notably,
Wang et al. (2023)
employed iterative interactions between students and black-box teachers, with teachers providing targeted feedback based on student failures. Similarly,
Adarsh et al. (2024)
combined iterative self-guided training and self-generated data to enhance reasoning in smaller models. Additionally,
Agarwal et al. (2024)
utilized on-policy training to better align student and teacher distributions by incorporating student-generated outputs. Self-distillation methods
(
Zelikman et al., 2022
;
Liu et al., 2024
)
involved models generating, filtering, and fine-tuning based on their own rationales. Reinforcement learning-based self-training was further explored by
Gulcehre et al. (2023)
, where off-policy sampling informed dataset generation. Unlike these methods, our iterative approach uniquely emphasizes a dynamic, adaptive feedback loop where the teacher explicitly targets and rectifies the student’s learning challenges.

Methodology

In this section, we first outline the standard knowledge distillation process and discuss its key limitations. We then introduce our proposed
UNDO
:
UN
derstanding
D
istillation as
O
ptimization framework, which addresses these limitations by enabling the teacher to iteratively identify the student’s weaknesses and refine the knowledge being distilled accordingly.

Problem Setup.

We consider a large teacher model
p_{L}
(LLM) and a smaller student model
p_{\texttt{sm}}^{\theta}
(with learnable parameters
\theta
). We have a dataset of math questions
\mathcal{D}=\{(q_{i},a_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, and an associated held-out validation set
\mathcal{V}=\{(q_{j},a_{j})\}_{j=1}^{m}
to monitor progress at each iteration. Our goal is to distill the teacher’s reasoning capabilities into the student iteratively by constantly moderating the errors made by the student and targeted refinements from the teacher.

3.1
Standard Knowledge Distillation

We begin with the standard knowledge distillation approach following
Shridhar et al. (2023)
;
Magister et al. (2023)
, where the teacher model
p_{L}
is prompted to produce a rationale (chain-of-thought)
r_{i}
for each question
q_{i}
. Let
\hat{a}_{i}
be the final numeric result in
r_{i}
. We only keep those samples for which
\hat{a}_{i}
matches the ground truth
a_{i}
. This produces a filtered dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\texttt{LLM}}=\{(q_{i},r_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
of correct (question, rationale) pairs.

The student is then fine-tuned by maximizing the log-likelihood of these rationales:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{L}}(\theta)\;=\;&-\mathbb{E}_{(q_{i},\,r_{i})\,\sim\,\mathcal{D}_{\texttt{LLM}}}\Bigg[\sum_{t=1}^{M_{i}}\log\,p_{\texttt{sm}}^{\theta}\bigl(r_{i,t}\,\mid\,r_{i,<t},\,q_{i},\,I\bigr)\Bigg],\end{split}

where
M_{i}
is the number of tokens of the rationale, and
I
is an instruction used to elicit chain-of-thought. Minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{L}}(\theta)
via gradient descent adjusts
\theta
so that the student mimics the teacher’s rationales. This is the baseline
one-step
standard knowledge distillation.

3.2
UNDO
:
UN
derstanding
D
istillation as
O
ptimization

Standard Knowledge Distillation often leads to a distributional mismatch between the teacher and the student, leading to suboptimal learning
(
Agarwal et al., 2024
;
Adarsh et al., 2024
)
. To counter this, we propose an
iterative
distillation strategy
UNDO
that repeatedly identifies the student’s errors and lets the teacher produce improved rationales that focus on the
learning gaps
to improve the student’s performance. The setup is visualized in
Figure 1
.

We perform
K
such iterations, maintaining updated students’ parameters
\theta^{(k)}
after each round. Each step is explained in more detail below:

1) Identify Student Learning Gaps & Incorporate in Teacher Prompt.

At each iteration
k
, the student
p_{\texttt{sm}}^{\theta^{(k-1)}}
answers each problem in the entire validation set
\mathcal{V}
. The student’s generation for question
q_{j}\in\mathcal{V}
is denoted as
\tilde{r}_{j}^{(k,\text{student})}
. We compare the final numeric result within
\tilde{r}_{j}^{(k,\text{student})}
to
a_{j}
and obtain an error signal (e.g., if correct, we provide a score of 1 and 0 otherwise). We collect these labeled pairs

\bigl(q_{j},\;\tilde{r}_{j}^{(k,\text{student})},\;\text{score}\bigr),\quad\forall\,j\in\{1,\dots,m\}.

This collection from
all
past iterations (to track improvements over time) will be shown to the teacher in its prompt context, so the teacher can observe the student’s progress and mistakes.

2) Regenerate from the Teacher with Gap Analysis.

Next, we consider the training dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\texttt{LLM}}
that we use to improve the student. For each
q_{i}\in\mathcal{D}_{\texttt{LLM}}
, we retrieve:

•

The student’s
previous
generation
\tilde{r}_{i}^{(k-1,\text{student})}
(if it exists) or
\tilde{r}_{i}^{(\text{init})}
,

•

The teacher’s
previous
rationale
r_{i}^{(k-1,\text{teacher})}
used in prior iteration,

and construct a prompt that highlights the
gap
between the teacher’s prior rationale and the student’s generation. The teacher
p_{L}
then produces a new rationale

r_{i}^{(k,\text{teacher})}\;\sim\;p_{L}\!\Bigl(\cdot\;\Big|\;q_{i},\;\tilde{r}_{i}^{(k-1,\text{student})},\;r_{i}^{(k-1,\text{teacher})},\;\{\tilde{r}_{j}^{(k,\text{student})},\,\text{score}_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{V}}\Bigr),

explicitly aiming to fix or improve upon the student’s mistakes. We filter out any rationale whose final numeric result does not match
a_{i}
, retaining a curated set

\mathcal{D}_{\texttt{LLM}}^{(k)}\;=\;\Bigl\{\bigl(q_{i},\;r_{i}^{(k,\text{teacher})}\bigr):q_{i}\in\mathcal{D}_{\texttt{LLM}}\Bigr\}.

3) Fine-tune the Student.

We then use
\mathcal{D}_{\texttt{LLM}}^{(k)}
to update the student parameters by minimizing the negative log-likelihood of the teacher’s new rationales:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{L}}^{(k)}(\theta)\;=\;&-\,\mathbb{E}_{(q_{i},\,r_{i}^{(k,\text{teacher})})\,\sim\,\mathcal{D}_{\texttt{LLM}}^{(k)}}\Bigg[\sum_{t=1}^{M_{i}^{(k)}}\log\,p_{\texttt{sm}}^{\theta}\bigl(r_{i,t}^{(k,\text{teacher})}\mid r_{i,<t}^{(k,\text{teacher})},\,q_{i},\,I\bigr)\Bigg],\end{split}

where
M_{i}^{(k)}
is the token length of
r_{i}^{(k,\text{teacher})}
. We obtain the updated parameters:

\theta^{(k)}\;=\;\arg\min_{\theta}\;\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{L}}^{(k)}(\theta).

4) Convergence Check.

We repeat Steps 1–3 until no further improvement is observed on the validation set or until we reach a maximum iteration
K
.

KL Divergence Perspective.

Each iteration can be viewed as approximating the minimization of the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between the teacher’s distribution
p_{L}(r\mid q,\text{gap info})
and the student’s distribution
p_{\texttt{sm}}^{\theta}(r\mid q)
. If we denote the teacher’s new rationale for question
q_{i}
by
r_{i}^{(k,\text{teacher})}
, sampled according to
p_{L}(r\mid q_{i},\Delta_{i}^{(k)})
(where
\Delta_{i}^{(k)}
encapsulates the gap information, e.g., old student and teacher rationales, validation errors, etc.), then the KL divergence is

\mathrm{KL}\Bigl(p_{L}(r\mid q_{i},\Delta_{i}^{(k)})\;\|\;p_{\texttt{sm}}^{\theta}(r\mid q_{i})\Bigr)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{r\sim p_{L}(\cdot\mid q_{i},\Delta_{i}^{(k)})}\Bigl[\log\,p_{L}(r\mid q_{i},\Delta_{i}^{(k)})\;-\;\log\,p_{\texttt{sm}}^{\theta}(r\mid q_{i})\Bigr].

Since the term
\log\,p_{L}(r\mid q_{i},\Delta_{i}^{(k)})
is independent of
\theta
, minimizing the KL is equivalent (up to constants) to maximizing

\mathbb{E}_{r\sim p_{L}}[\log p_{\texttt{sm}}^{\theta}(r\mid q_{i})],

which matches the negative log-likelihood objective used in Eq. (
3.2
) when we replace the teacher’s distribution by its empirical samples
r_{i}^{(k,\text{teacher})}
. Iteration-by-iteration, the teacher highlights gaps by conditioning on the student’s errors (
via

\Delta_{i}^{(k)}
), so the newly generated rationales better target exactly where
p_{\texttt{sm}}^{\theta}
remains deficient. Consequently, with repeated minimization of this KL divergence for “hard” or incorrectly answered questions, the student’s distribution
p_{\texttt{sm}}^{\theta}
is successively refined, thereby improving overall performance.

Conclusion

We introduced an iterative distillation paradigm
UNDO
aimed at bridging the performance gap between large teacher models and smaller student models more effectively than conventional one-shot distillation. By repeatedly identifying the student’s errors, regenerating teacher rationales that address these
learning gaps
, and re-training the student on this tailored data, our method yields consistent improvements on challenging mathematical benchmarks. Experimental results indicate that
UNDO
not only avoids overfitting often seen in prolonged standard distillation, but also makes the teacher more attuned to student-specific weaknesses, effectively “personalizing” the teacher’s rationales.

Empirically, we observed that final teacher-generated data—after multiple rounds of iteration—significantly enhances student performance, with gains of upto 20% compared to the baseline. Even cross-teacher data (e.g., teacher data for one student used on another) shows improved results over standard approaches. These findings underscore that incorporating iterative feedback loops between the teacher and the student leads to a more specialized and effective transfer of reasoning knowledge.
