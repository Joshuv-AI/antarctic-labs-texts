Vegetation carbon use efficiency response to drought in the Manas River Basin of Xinjiang
Authors: Jingjing Kong, Mei Zan, Zhizhong Chen, Shunfa Yang, Jia Zhou, Lili Zhai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCarbon Use Efficiency (CUE), quantified as the ratio of net primary production to gross primary production (NPP/GPP), serves as a crucial indicator of ecosystem carbon sequestration capacity. However, understanding of its spatiotemporal dynamics and drought response mechanisms in arid inland basins remains limited. This study investigates the CUE characteristics in the Manas River Basin, a representative arid endorheic basin in Xinjiang, China, using MODIS satellite data (2001-2020). Results demonstrate that the multi-year mean CUE of the basin was 0.50 (±0.12), with coniferous forests exhibiting the highest values and croplands the lowest. Seasonal analysis revealed CUE in spring and autumn significantly exceeded that in winter (p<0.01). Spatially, 57.99% of the basin displayed low CUE fluctuation, primarily distributed in grassland and woodland areas. Future trend projections indicate divergent persistence patterns between plain and desert grasslands. Drought response analysis identified a dominant 3-month lag effect, with forests showing greater drought resistance and longer response lags compared to croplands (1.2 months longer, p=0.03). The ecosystem maintains high resilience, regulated by the interactive effects of vegetation type, irrigation practices, and climate gradients. These findings establish a mechanistic framework for understanding carbon cycling processes in arid lands under climate stress, providing scientific basis for global dryland ecosystem management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-29708-6.Keywords: Vegetation carbon use efficiency, Stability, Spatial and temporal trends, Lag effect, ResilienceSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences

Introduction

CUE is the proportion of carbon used by organisms for growth and development relative to the total carbon they have absorbed and is often expressed as the ratio of net primary productivity (NPP) of vegetation to gross primary productivity (GPP). CUE influences the amount of carbon dioxide (CO2) absorbed by vegetation1. In terrestrial ecosystems, it significantly contributes to the carbon cycle and indicates the efficiency of atmospheric carbon transfer to biomass. The higher the value of CUE, the greater the transfer of carbon per unit of vegetative carbon for growth, which corresponds to the greater potential for sequestration2–4. The greater the potential, the more conducive it is to carbon stabilisation and long-term sequestration1. Therefore, studying variations in ecosystem vegetation CUE and its effects is essential for evaluating regional carbon sink capacity and enhancing management strategies to boost carbon sequestration4. In recent years, the development of remote sensing technology has brought new ideas to research about the water-carbon cycle, with many scholars worldwide conducting studies using remote sensing products, including changes in different spatial scales and different temporal scales. Some scholars used the MODIS method to calculate CUE and explore its spatial and temporal variations and the correlation between CUE and ecospatial network topological indicators5. Several authors assessed CUE dynamics in six homogeneous meteorological regions (HMRs) in India using MODIS products, finding significant CUE variations across spatial and temporal scales, particularly influenced by water effectiveness and temperature interactions. CUE declined continuously in 82% of the cultivated area in peninsular India, and a general reduction in carbon stock resilience was observed6. Some scholars examined the distribution of NPP/GPP ratios, spatial and temporal variations, responses to external controls on individual ecosystems, and the effects of ongoing climate change on NPP/GPP ratios over 16 years in China. It was observed that cold and dry environments typically had vegetation with higher NPP/GPP ratios, while warm and wet environments exhibited the opposite trend. Moderate precipitation and temperature increased the NPP/GPP ratio7. Several authors investigated the changes in CUE, and they found that CUE values in sparsely vegetated areas were higher than those in densely vegetated areas8. Characterisation of carbon use efficiency in a forested area of Tibet found that there were regional differences in the trend of CUE changes, and that the vegetation type mainly determined, and the range of changes in CUE of the vegetation and the characteristics of the CUE9. The results of this study showed that the CUE of a forested area in Tibet was higher than that of a forested area in the same region.

The above studies are mostly about the CUE of specific ecosystems on a large scale, while systematic studies on the CUE of different ecological systems in the Manas River Basin in Xinjiang are still lacking, especially the spatiotemporal patterns of vegetation CUE in the Manas River Basin in Xinjiang and the characteristics of its response to drought are still unclear. In view of this, this study used MOD17 data to calculate the vegetation CUE in the Manas River Basin in Xinjiang, and analysed the spatial and temporal dynamics of vegetation CUE from 2001 to 2020, which can help to understand the dynamics of vegetation CUE in the Manas River Basin, and reveal its response mechanism to drought.

Research dataset.

In this study, the remote sensing data were processed with uniform projection and spatial resolution. Firstly, all remote sensing data projections were unified as WGS_1984_UTM_Zone_45N using ArcGIS 10.8 software, and the remote sensing data were cropped according to the vector map of the administrative boundary of Xinjiang in order to obtain the remote sensing data in the Xinjiang range. Subsequently, NPP data, and Land use data were interpolated to a resolution of 500 meters in space. This study used annually updated land-use maps for 2001–2020. The data were then used as the basis of the spatial resolution. Finally, considering the influencing factors such as temperature, PET, they were uniformly adjusted to a spatial resolution of 0.05° by the resampling method.

Data preprocessing: To generate monthly-scale GPP and NPP datasets from the 16-day composite data (2001-2020), we applied a weighted average method in MATLAB (R2023a). The monthly value for each pixel was calculated using the formula: Monthly_Value = (V₁ × D₁ + V₂ × D₂) / (D₁ + D₂), where V₁ and V₂ represent the GPP or NPP values of the two 16-day composites spanning the month, and D₁ and D₂ are the number of effective days each composite contributes to the target month. This approach ensures a temporally representative aggregation of the bi-weekly data. datasets were synthesized via a temporal weighting method, where weights were determined by the actual coverage days of each 16-day period within the target month.

Overview of the study area

The Manas River Basin is situated in the central region of the northern Tianshan Mountains foothills, bordering the southern edge of the Junggar Basin (43°08′-45°97′N, 84°70′-86°67′E). The study area covers an area of 3.40×104km2, with a complex geomorphology and a high topography in the south and the north, with mountainous terrain, oases, and deserts in descending order from the south to the north (Fig. 1). The study area experiences a typical temperate arid continental climate, with an average annual temperature of 7.7°C and annual precipitation ranging from 114 to 200 mm, an annual evapotranspiration of 1500-2100 mm, a dry climate, a significant annual temperature variation, a small amount of rainfall, a large evapotranspiration, and an unequal spatial and temporal distribution of the runoff. The study area is the largest oasis farming area in Xinjiang, with a high water resource utilisation rate1,6. Its main water sources are alpine ice and snow melt and precipitation from upstream mountains. The distribution of the study area is shown in Fig. 1.

Temporal trends in carbon use efficiency of different vegetation types

From 2001 to 2020, each vegetation type followed its own path (p < 0.05, ANOVA; Fig. 3). Forests crept steadily upward–coniferous at +0.0017 yr⁻1 and broad-leaved at +0.0015 yr⁻1 –while grasslands rose more slowly (+0.0017 yr⁻1). Croplands, in contrast, slipped by -0.0038 yr⁻1. The common spike of 2016 was strongest in croplands (+0.40), modest in grasslands and coniferous forests (+0.12 and +0.13), and slight in broad-leaved forests (+0.05). Forests showed far smaller year-to-year swings in CUE than croplands, highlighting how vegetation type governs the stability of the carbon cycle.

Analysis of spatial trends in carbon efficiency dynamics

Seasonal patterns in the spatial distribution of carbon efficiency

Seasonal CUE (2001-2020) exhibited pronounced spatial heterogeneity (Fig 4, 5). Summer CUE increased stepwise from north to south: desert grasslands < croplands < plains grasslands < alpine vegetation. Autumn sustained CUE >0.8 in 22.5% of plains and desert grasslands, whereas winter brought basin-wide cessation of the carbon sink. Discussion

SPEI cycle characteristics

In the Manas River Basin, located in the Xinjiang region, SPEI cyclicity variations in different seasons are driven by multiple factors. The cyclic variation of SPEI in spring is affected by the combined effects of snowmelt and accelerated evapotranspiration19,20. With the rapid warming of temperature, evapotranspiration of soil moisture increases significantly, and the lack of snowmelt in winter may further exacerbate the drought in spring21. Cyclic variations of SPEI in summer, on the other hand, are affected by the imbalance between precipitation and high evapotranspiration, and despite the concentrated precipitation in the summer, evapotranspiration is stronger in the Manas River Basin22. The SPEI is also affected by the high precipitation and high evapotranspiration in summer. Meanwhile, atmospheric circulation anomalies trigger cyclical fluctuations in SPEI by modulating regional precipitation distribution and intensity. In addition, soil moisture competition may also exacerbate the persistence of local droughts22. The cyclical characteristics of the autumn SPEI are not only related to the precipitation and evapotranspiration imbalance in the preceding seasons, but are also influenced by the long-term trend of the climate (e.g., warmer temperatures), which shows a clear 5-10-year long cycle signal, which is more in line with the fact that the autumn SPEI in this paper is the strongest in terms of cyclic energy at the time scale of about 6 years23. The winter SPEI fluctuates more steadily with a long cycle of about 10 years, which may be related to snowpack changes and warming trends24. Changes in winter snowpack are directly related to the water supply in spring and summer, and if the snowpack decreases significantly in winter, it may affect the SPEI in spring and summer through the lag effect, which in turn affects the cyclic trend of the SPEI25. In conclusion, the seasonal cycle of the SPEI does not exist independently, but rather it is affected by the hydrological cycle of the watershed, the atmospheric circulation, and the multiple factors of human activities25. In this paper, the seasonal cycle of the SPEI in autumn is more stable, with a long cycle of about 10 years.

Spatial and temporal variations in CUE and differential vegetation characteristics

This study shows that the annual decrease in carbon use efficiency from 2001 to 2020 is not significant, with a high value occurring in 2009, and a faster increase in carbon use efficiency in crops and grasslands. It is possible that compared to the extreme weather in 2008, the vegetation was in the recovery stage with favorable temperatures and increased precipitation, which led to the increase in the annual carbon use efficiency26. The minimum value appeared in 2018 at 0.463, which was 5.90% lower than the multi-year average; followed by 2008 at 0.464; it has been shown that in areas and time periods experiencing drought or increased temperatures, moisture limitation inhibits photosynthesis while respiration is relatively unaffected, plant ecosystems will suffer higher ecosystem respiration costs, their net productivity will shrink, and the CUE of vegetation may be reduced27. Carbon utilisation efficiency was lower in 2008 and 2018, with 2008 being affected by both cold and drought, resulting in the efficiency of plant photosynthesis, and a weakening of the capacity for carbon fixation; and 2018 was one of Xinjiang’s more warmer and humidified climate characteristic year, but also a high-temperature heat wave phenomenon in local areas. The high-temperature environment accelerated the respiratory metabolism of plants, leading to lower carbon utilisation efficiency28. There were also significant differences in the effects of different types of vegetation on CUE. Different types of vegetation differ in growth mode, photosynthetic intensity, etc., resulting in their CUE being different as well29.

The findings of this study indicate that the CUE of various vegetation types in the area is in the following order: coniferous forests >broad-leaved forests >grasslands >crops; this difference is mainly attributed to the differences in the distribution of vegetation and its physiological characteristics. Forests usually have high CUE, and a large number of natural forests in the study area are mainly distributed in the northern foothills of the Tianshan Mountains in the south, which are located in the upper reaches of mountainous rivers, rich in precipitation and summer ice and snowmelt, with tree roots also able to draw on groundwater, and rich in soil microorganisms, which enhance the efficiency of forests in the study area in fixing and converting photosynthesis products30. Moreover, the trees have a higher growth rate, a longer growth cycle, and a higher ability of carbon fixation and coniferous forests, in particular, have strong carbon storage capacity and their CUE is higher and less fluctuating31. In contrast, grasslands have relatively low CUE because they have a shorter growth cycle, a more homogeneous vegetation structure, are more dependent on climate and soil conditions, and have a weaker carbon fixation capacity32. The CUE of agricultural crops is mainly affected by soil fertility as well as human activities. For example, agricultural irrigation and fertilisation have a significant impact on regional CUE, enhancing it to a certain extent, but also increasing the uncertainty of its interannual fluctuations32. Ecosystem interactions: interactions between vegetation and soil microorganisms also affect CUE. Soil microorganisms release nutrients through the decomposition of organic matter, which affects vegetation growth and carbon sequestration33–36.

The average vegetation CUE values across different seasons in the study area exhibited notable spatial variation, with the mean values of CUE in spring, summer, autumn and winter being 0.72, 0.44, 0.73 and 0.01, respectively. the mean value of interannual vegetation CUE was 0.50. Changes in temperature and precipitation directly affect the balance of photosynthesis and respiration in vegetation, which in turn affects the changes in CUE. Increased temperature decreases CUE, and increased precipitation leads to increased CUE31. In spring, CUE increases due to rapid vegetation growth as temperatures rise and large amounts of snow melt. In summer, under high temperature and drought conditions, plant evapotranspiration is greater, and the combined effects of increased respiration, reduced photosynthetic efficiency, altered carbon allocation strategies, and metabolic regulation reduce CUE33–37. In autumn, with suitable temperatures, increased precipitation compared to summer, and cooler temperatures at night, plant respiration is reduced, while vegetation enters the later part of the growing season and begins to store carbon, resulting in increased carbon fixation efficiency. In winter, as temperatures decrease and vegetation enters dormancy, respiration dominates and CUE decreases significantly32. In addition, extreme climatic events (e.g., drought in summer or reduced snowpack in winter) may lead to significant anomalies in CUE in a given year33. In summary, the differential characteristics of vegetation CUE are the result of a combination of factors, and there may be interactions and feedback mechanisms between these factors that collectively determine vegetation CUE. that together determine the carbon use efficiency of vegetation34.

CUE response mechanism to SPEI

In the Manas River Basin, a notable correlation can be observed between CUE and SPEI37,38. The mechanism of CUE response to SPEI is determined due to a combination of climate-related factors and the adaptation strategies of vegetation. Under wet conditions, vegetation tends to allocate more carbon to leaf and stem expansion, increasing carbon fixation efficiency; whereas extreme wet conditions improve photosynthesis but may reduce carbon fixation efficiency due to increased soil respiration or nutrient loss39. Under drought conditions, vegetation may increase water acquisition capacity by increasing root allocation, while decreasing leaf expansion to reduce water loss, thus reduce overall CUE, while persistent drought may impair the photosynthetic system of vegetation and increase carbon loss through respiration, leading to a long-term decline in CUE30. In addition, there are significant differences in the response of different vegetation types to SPEI40..The response of vegetation types to SPEI has been shown to vary significantly.

The majority of the lag time for vegetation CUE’s response to drought in the Manas River Basin was observed at the 3-month scale. This is because grassland, cropland, and alpine sparse vegetation in the study area cover a larger area, and their growth cycles are more consistent at the 3-month time scale, and their root systems are mostly shallow, with a simpler ecosystem structure that lacks a complex mechanism for storing and distributing carbon. Basin CUE is more significant, while the effect of long-time scale SPEI on these short growing periods vegetation may be weakened41. The CUE of arable land is not only controlled by climatic factors, but also significantly affected by anthropogenic activities such as irrigation, fertiliser application and tillage. Short-term droughts may be mitigated by these anthropogenic management measures41–43. Thus, grassland, cropland, and alpine sparse vegetation in the Manas River Basin responded more rapidly and directly to changes in SPEI than did forests. In contrast, forests possess high biomass and robust root systems that store nutrients like carbon and water, and are more resistant to drought, so their carbon use efficiency response to drought is more lagging.

By revealing the response mechanism of CUE to SPEI, a more comprehensive understanding of the dynamics of the vegetation carbon cycle and its adaptive capacity to climate change in the Manas River Basin can be obtained44. This study not only has important guiding significance for regional ecosystem restoration and water resource management, but also provides scientific support for optimising the function of carbon sinks. Future studies should further explore the nonlinear response mechanisms under extreme SPEI conditions and focus on the interactions between different vegetation types and soil microbial communities. In addition, in-depth studies on the impacts of anthropogenic activities on the relationship between SPEI and CUE, especially the adaptation strategies in agroecosystems, will help to formulate more effective regional management policies.

Seasonal dynamics and vegetation type jointly regulate the lagged response of CUE to drought

We examined how growing-season CUE lags behind SPEI at four-time scales chosen to match the seasonal rhythm of plant water use. A 1-month lag captures the immediate stomatal response to short dry spells; 3 months mirrors the water balance of a full season and is critical for herbs and shallow-rooted crops; 6 months carries the memory of soil moisture re-distribution between the early and peak growing period; 12 months registers the legacy of an entire annual water deficit45. This sequence-short, seasonal, trans-seasonal and annual-follows common practice in dry-land ecohydrology and lets us isolate when, within the year, CUE feels drought most keenly46.

Figure 12 shows that, among pixels significant at p < 0.05, the 3-month lag dominates (1.47 % of the basin), followed by 1-month. The pattern, however, is clearly seasonal and vegetation-specific. Grasslands and croplands react mainly during the first three months of the growing season, whereas forests, with deeper root access to stored soil water, can delay the response across several seasons-up to 12 months-revealing fundamentally different drought-coping strategies.

Theoretical contributions to global ecological research

The Manas River Basin, as a typical endorheic basin in Central Asia, has a wide representative ecosystem structure (mountain oasis desert vertical belt spectrum) and water resource management methods (high-intensity irrigation agriculture) in the global arid regions46. The CUE drought response mechanism revealed in this study, particularly the differential response patterns between irrigated oases and natural ecosystems, can provide analogical references for other Central Asian watersheds (such as the Syr Darya and Amu Darya rivers) and similar climate zones worldwide (such as the Midwest of the United States and the Murray Darling Basin in Australia). It exhibits universal characteristics of arid zone ecosystems: (1) strong coupling between water availability and carbon cycling47,48, (2) sharp contrasts between irrigated oases and natural deserts, and (3) high sensitivity to climate oscillations (e.g., ENSO). Our discovery of irrigation mediated CUE resilience suggests that its mechanism can be extended to other water scarce regions.

Conclusion

This study presents a systematic analysis of the spatiotemporal patterns, stability, and drought response of carbon use efficiency (CUE) in the Manas River Basin, Xinjiang, from 2001 to 2020. The main findings are as follows:

On an annual scale, basin-wide CUE showed a non-significant declining trend over the 20-year period, with notable low values in 2008 (0.464) and 2018 (0.463), the latter being 5.9% below the long-term average. CUE varied among vegetation types in the order: coniferous forest >broad-leaved forest >grassland >cropland.
