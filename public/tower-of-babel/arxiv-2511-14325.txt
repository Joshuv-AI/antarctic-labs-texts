Periods in equivariant and motivic contexts
Martin Gallauer

Abstract
. We define the period as a multiplicative characteristic of stably symmetric monoidal ∞ \infty -categories, develop its basic properties, and study many examples, with a focus on ‘ordinary’ equivariant and motivic homotopy theory. We apply the findings to isotropic points in motivic tt-geometry.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Characteristics

Let
R
be a (commutative, unital) ring. A basic invariant is its characteristic, the non-negative integer satisfying

\langle\car(R)\rangle=\ker\left(\mathbb{Z}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{can}}R\right).

Being defined in terms of addition and multiplication, it propagates along any ring morphism
R\to S
, meaning that
\car(S)\bigm|\car(R)
.
In particular, it leads to the prime characteristics
\mathfrak{p}\in\Spec(\mathbb{Z})
that partition fields into an infinite countable family.
The characteristic is a fundamental organizing principle in algebraic geometry, describing specific techniques and phenomena such as ‘mixed characteristic’, ‘characteristic
p
’ or ‘(equi-)characteristic
0
’.

Now let
\mathscr{K}
be an (essentially small) stably symmetric monoidal
\infty
-category, a categorification of a ring.
There are several invariants that could serve functions similar to that of
\car(R)
.
One is to replace the initial ring
\mathbb{Z}
by the initial categorified ring
\Sp^{\omega}
of finite spectra and define the characteristic as the kernel of

(1.1)

\Sp^{\omega}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{can}}\mathscr{K}.

This kernel is a thick
\otimes
-ideal that can be identified with a subset of
\Spec(\Sp^{\omega})
.
Here, we use that categorified rings possess an associated (affine)
tt-scheme
: as a space this was defined by Balmer
[
Bal05
]
(for tensor triangulated categories); in
[
Aok+25
]
it was enhanced to a structured space in the sense of
[
DAGV
]
.
In particular, the prime characteristics are now pairs
(\mathfrak{p},n)
where
\mathfrak{p}\in\Spec(\mathbb{Z})
as before, and (if
\mathfrak{p}\neq 0
)
1\leq n\leq\infty
is the chromatic height
[
HS98
]
.
Undoubtedly this is a useful and important organizing principle in tt-geometry.

1.2.
Periods

In this article we are interested in another such basic invariant, of multiplicative rather than additive type, obtained from (
1.1
) by passing to invertible objects:

Definition 1.2
.

The
period
of
\mathscr{K}
is the non-negative integer satisfying

\langle\per(\mathscr{K})\rangle=\ker\left(\mathbb{Z}=\Pic(\Sp^{\omega})\xrightarrow{\mathrm{can}}\Pic(\mathscr{K})\right).

In other words, it is the smallest
d>0
such that
\Sigma^{d}\mathbb{1}\cong\mathbb{1}
(or
0
if no such
d
exists).
While the period of many familiar examples like spectra or derived categories is
0
, one doesn’t need to look far for periodic ones.
Periodic ring spectra are ubiquitous in stable homotopy theory and their perfect modules are obviously periodic; this applies, among other things, to most of the tt-fields for spectra (Morava
K
-theories).
Another source of examples are periodic derived categories, orbit categories and categories of matrix factorizations.
As we will expand on below, many tt-fields in modular representation theory and motives also are, or ought to be, periodic.

Being defined in terms of
\otimes
and suspension, the period propagates along any morphism
\mathscr{K}\to\mathscr{L}
, meaning that
\per(\mathscr{L})\bigm|\per(\mathscr{K})
.
This leads to a stratification by periods of
\Spec(\mathscr{K})
and more generally, of any tt-scheme, just as for the characteristic.
We also believe that
\mathscr{K}
being periodic or non-periodic determines some of the important phenomena one encounters and techniques one employs.
For example, t-structures and weight structures are two of the most useful tools for getting to grips with
\mathscr{K}
.
But they can
never
be of use if
\mathscr{K}
is periodic.
On the other hand, in the latter case the periodicity itself offers a reduction in complexity of some form, something that is often exploited in stable homotopy theory.
We will not try to substantiate this dichotomy here, and instead illustrate the use of periods as an organizing principle in examples—to which we now turn.

1.3.
Motivic tt-geometry

At the origin of this article was the construction of isotropic realizations due to Vishik
[
Vis22
]
and the subsequent discovery
[
Vis24
,
DV25
]
of an associated large new class of
isotropic
points on
\Spec(\SH(\mathbb{F})^{\omega})
and
\Spec(\DM(\mathbb{F};\mathbb{Z}/\ell)^{\omega})
(for
\mathbb{F}
a field of characteristic zero,
\ell
any prime number).
The analysis of these two spectra, associated to Morel–Voevodsky’s
\mathbb{A}^{1}
-motivic spectra and Voevodsky’s derived category of motives, respectively, constitutes arguably the major open problem in motivic tt-geometry and the cited works made significant inroad.

To give an idea of just how significant, we mention for example that finitely many points on
\Spec(\DM(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{Z}/2)^{\omega})
had been known before, whereas the set of isotropic points (and indeed the whole space) have cardinality
2^{\mathfrak{c}}
(
\mathfrak{c}
denoting the continuum).
A similar statement could be made about
\SH(\mathbb{R})
and over other fields.
Our goal was to understand these developments and place them in the context of what was already known.

For example, let
\iota\colon\DAM(\mathbb{F};\mathbb{Z}/\ell)\hookrightarrow\DM(\mathbb{F};\mathbb{Z}/\ell)
denote the inclusion of Artin motives (i.e., the localizing subcategory generated by zero-dimensional varieties).
In joint work with Balmer
[
BG25
,
BG25a
]
, we determined the space underlying
\Spec(\DAM(\mathbb{F};\mathbb{Z}/\ell)^{\omega})
completely.
It is itself a typically complicated and high-dimensional spectral space that can be described in terms of the subgroup structure of the absolute Galois group and the group cohomology of its subquotients.
We establish in
Section

10
that under the induced map

(1.3)

\iota^{*}\colon\Spec(\DM(\mathbb{F};\mathbb{Z}/\ell)^{\omega})\to\Spec(\DAM(\mathbb{F};\mathbb{Z}/\ell)^{\omega}),

all isotropic points get mapped to closed points.
(In fact, we give an explicit formula.)
Since it is known from general tt-principles that (
1.3
) is surjective, this shows that isotropic points are (very) far from exhausting
\Spec(\DM(\mathbb{F};\mathbb{Z}/\ell)^{\omega})
.
For the vast majority of points in the target of (
1.3
), we do not know any element in the fiber.
The main take-away is that despite the recent breakthroughs we seem to have barely scratched the surface of motivic tt-geometry (
10.11
).

At least for
\ell=2
,
[
Vis25
]
shows that the isotropic points themselves are closed so the fact that their images in
\Spec(\DAM(\mathbb{F};\mathbb{Z}/2)^{\omega})
are as well may not be surprising.
(Although this doesn’t seem to be a formal consequence.)
Our argument proceeds as follows: It is clear by construction that isotropic points are non-periodic hence, by the propagation of periodicity, they cannot map to periodic ones.
It then suffices to show that all non-closed points in
\Spec(\DAM(\mathbb{F};\mathbb{Z}/\ell)^{\omega})
are periodic, and here we rely heavily on recent work of Miller
[
Mil25
]
that in effect produces enough sections of line bundles on this space.

We do not claim that this is the simplest or most direct argument possible.
But it illustrates how periods can be effective in organizing tt-geometry in more complicated situations.
We imagine that their usefulness will only grow as explorations of the landscape in motivic tt-geometry lead us further afield.
And our result brings to the fore the challenge to exhibit periodic points of
\Spec(\DM(\mathbb{F};\mathbb{Z}/\ell)^{\omega})
.

1.4.
Contents

The first half of the article is straightforward: we simply lay the foundations to run the argument above as smoothly as possible.
This includes in
Sections

2
,
3
and
4
introducing periods for commutative ring spectra, stably symmetric monoidal
\infty
-categories, the corresponding period stratification on their associated spaces, and their basic properties.
We observe that these notions all depend on the underlying graded rings and tt-categories only, respectively, and we therefore work in this generality.
Determining periods in tt-geometry can be quite a bit harder than determining the characteristic of rings and so, in
Sections

5
and
6
, we establish some tools that aid in dévissage arguments.
One of the main ones is decategorification: comparing tt-categories to (graded) commutative rings arising as sections of families of line bundles.
Here we highlight the substantial contribution (in Appendix
A
) of Dell’Ambrogio (based on
[
DS14
]
) that provides a robust framework for talking about ample families of line bundles and divisorial tt-categories.
We hope this framework will be useful for tt-geometers in other contexts as well.

The second, more substantial, half of the article applies the theory, exhibiting the period stratification in many examples.
Some are easily pulled from the literature (
Section

7
).
The ones that take more effort include notably the derived and stable categories in modular representation theory, as well as derived permutation modules (
Section

8
), where our results are complete only for certain classes of groups.
Through the Galois correspondence, these derived permutation modules (or, equivalently, modules for the Bredon cohomology spectrum) correspond to Artin motives.
We’ll use this in
Sections

9
and
10
to explain our application to motives and isotropic points alluded to above.
At the same time we provide a bestiary of many of the known points in motivic tt-geometry together with their periods.
We hope this will be useful for anyone interested in exploring this area of research.

1.6.
Conventions

We follow the conventions of
[
Lur09a
,
HA
]
for
\infty
-categories.
Let
\mathrm{Cat}^{\mathrm{\footnotesize ex}}
denote the
\infty
-category of stable
\infty
-categories and exact functors (with its usual tensor product).
A
stably symmetric monoidal
\infty
-category
is a commutative algebra object in
\mathrm{Cat}^{\mathrm{\footnotesize ex}}
.
That is, it is a stable
\infty
-category with a symmetric monoidal structure such that the tensor product is exact in each variable.

For tt-categories we follow the conventions of
[
Bal05
]
.
In particular, a
tt-category
is a triangulated category with a symmetric monoidal structure such that the tensor product is exact in each variable.

Idempotent completion is denoted by
(-)^{\natural}
.
Given
\mathscr{K}\in\calg(\mathrm{Cat}^{\mathrm{\footnotesize ex}})
there is an associated structured space
\Spec(\mathscr{K}):=\Spec(\mathscr{K}^{\natural})
defined in
[
Aok+25
]
.
Its underlying space
\Spc(\mathscr{K})
is canonically identified with the spectrum
\Spc(\ho(\mathscr{K}))
of the underlying tt-category in the sense of
[
Bal05
]
.
We will (try to) consistently distinguish between spectra of various things as spaces (denoted
\Spc
) and with a ‘locally ringed space’ structure (denoted
\Spec
).

2.
Periods of rings…

In this section we fix terminology concerning periodicity in ring spectra and graded rings.

Convention 2.1
.

A
graded ring
refers to a
\mathbb{Z}
-graded commutative ring in a general sense.
That is, it is a monoid
R=\oplus_{d\in\mathbb{Z}}R_{d}
in
\mathbb{Z}
-graded abelian groups such that there exists
\epsilon\in R_{0}^{\times}
satisfying
rs=\epsilon^{\deg(r)\deg(s)}sr
for any homogeneous elements
r,s
.

Example 2.2
.

The most important case for us is when
\epsilon
can be chosen to be
-1
.
These are called
Dirac rings
in
[
HP23
]
.
This is, in other words, a commutative monoid in graded abelian groups, if one endows the latter with the symmetry constraint that respects the Koszul sign rule.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
d>0
be an integer.
A graded ring
R
is
d
-periodic
if there exists a unit
u\in R_{d}
in degree
d
.
We say
R
is
periodic
if it is
d
-periodic for some
d>0
.
The
period
of
R
, denoted
\per(R)
, is the smallest degree
d>0
of a unit in
R
.
We also set
\per(R)=0
and call
R

non-periodic
if no unit of positive degree exists.

Remark 2.4
.

Let
R
be a sufficiently commutative ring spectrum, by which we mean an
\mathbb{E}_{n}
-algebra in spectra, for
2\leq n\leq\infty
.
Then
\pi_{*}R
is a Dirac ring hence in particular a graded ring in the sense above.
We say that
R
is
d
-periodic if
\pi_{*}R
is.
Similarly, we will transfer notions like period and local period (to be introduced below) to such ring spectra.
Also, while in the sequel we focus almost exclusively on
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}
-rings, much of it would apply more generally to sufficiently commutative ring spectra.

Example 2.5
.

An
even periodic

\mathbb{E}_{\infty}
-ring is a
2
-periodic
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}
-ring whose odd homotopy groups vanish.
These play an important role in stable homotopy theory due to their connection with complex orientations and formal group laws.
Examples include complex
K
-theory
KU
, Morava
E
-theory
E(n)
at height
n
(and an implicit prime
p
), elliptic spectra (arising from étale classifying maps
\Spec(R)\to\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{ell}}
through Goerss–Hopkins–Miller).

Example 2.6
.

(Compare
7.4
.) The
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}
-ring of (‘periodic’) topological modular forms
\mathrm{TMF}=\Gamma(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{ell}},\mathscr{O}_{\mathrm{ell}})
has period
576
(Hopkins,
[
MS16
]
).

Example 2.7
.

A graded field
k
is a graded ring in which
0
is the only graded prime ideal.
Then either
k=k_{0}
or
k=k_{0}[t^{\pm 1}]
, where
k_{0}
is a field in the ordinary sense and
t\in k_{d}
for some
d>0
.
This is an easy exercise or see
[
HP23
, Proposition 2.13]
whose proof (in the Dirac case) goes through verbatim.
In the first case
\per(k)=0
, and in the second case
\per(k_{0}[t^{\pm 1}])=d
.

The periods of graded rings are subject to the following basic constraint.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
R
be a graded ring of odd period.
Then the commutativity constraint is
\epsilon=1
.
In particular, if
R
is Dirac then
2=0
in
R
.

Proof.

Let
u\in R
be a unit of odd degree.
By the graded commutativity,
u^{2}=\epsilon u^{2}
and since
u
is a unit we must have
1=\epsilon
in
R
.
In the Dirac case
\epsilon=-1
so that
2=0
.
∎

Remark 2.9
.

A graded ring
R
has an associated geometry, its
graded spectrum
(or
homogeneous spectrum
) of graded prime ideals
\Spec(R)=(\Spc(R),\mathscr{O}_{R})
.
That is, the underlying topological space
\Spc(R)
has graded prime ideals as points, with the topology generated by the opens
D(f)=\{\mathfrak{p}\mid f\notin\mathfrak{p}\,\}
for
f\in R
homogeneous.
The structure sheaf of graded rings is essentially determined by sending
D(f)
to
R[\frac{1}{f}]
.

Now, assume
R
is periodic.
Then the association

\Spc(R)\to\Spc(R_{0}),\qquad\mathfrak{p}\mapsto\mathfrak{p}\cap R_{0},

is easily seen to be a homeomorphism.
Under this identification, the structure sheaf on
\Spec(R_{0})
is (isomorphic to) the degree-0 part of the structure sheaf on
\Spec(R)
.

Definition 2.10
.

(1)

Let
R
be a graded ring and
\mathfrak{p}\in\Spec(R)
.
We define the
(local) period
of
R
at
\mathfrak{p}
to be

\per_{R}(\mathfrak{p}):=\per(R_{\mathfrak{p}}),

the period of the local ring at
\mathfrak{p}
.

(2)

For
d>0
we also define the
d
-periodic locus
to be the following subset of
\Spc(R)
:

\displaystyle\Per_{d}(R):=\

\displaystyle\{\mathfrak{p}\in\Spc(R)\mid\per_{R}(\mathfrak{p})\text{ divides }d\}

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\{\mathfrak{p}\in\Spc(R)\mid R_{\mathfrak{p}}\text{ is $d$-periodic}\}

(3)

Finally,
\Per(R):=\cup_{d>0}\Per_{d}(R)
is the
periodic locus
.

Remark 2.11
.

(1)

The subsets
\Per_{d}(R)
(and therefore also
\Per(R)
) are open.
This is an easy exercise, or see the proof of
4.5
.

(2)

Locally graded ringed spaces that are locally isomorphic to spectra of graded rings might be called
graded schemes
.
They generalize Dirac schemes in
[
HP23
]
(built locally out of graded spectra of Dirac rings).
It is clear that the notions in
2.10
, being defined locally, extend to graded schemes.

Example 2.12
.

Let
k
be a field and consider the graded ring
R=k[x,y]
with
\deg(x)=1
,
\deg(y)=2
.
The periodic locus
\Per(R)
is a
\mathbb{P}_{k}^{1}
, and the complement is a single closed point corresponding to the ideal
\langle x,y\rangle
.
We have
\per_{R}(\langle x\rangle)=2
while all other points in the periodic locus have period
1
.

Example 2.13
.

Let
d>0
.
A
weakly
d
-periodic

\mathbb{E}_{\infty}
-ring is an
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}
-ring
R
such that the multiplication map

R_{n}\otimes_{R_{0}}R_{d}\to R_{d+n}

is an isomorphism for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.
(As usual, we abbreviate
R_{n}:=\pi_{n}R
.)
Such ring spectra come up naturally in stable homotopy theory, for essentially the same reason that even periodic ones do.
Of course, a
d
-periodic
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}
-ring is also weakly
d
-periodic but the converse is not true.
(For example, because not every one-dimensional formal group over a commutative ring admits a global coordinate.)

We claim that

\Per(R)=\Per_{d}(R)=\Spec(\pi_{*}R).

Indeed, the condition implies that
R_{d}
is an invertible
R_{0}
-module with inverse
R_{-d}
.
Hence locally on
\Spec(R_{0})
, say after inverting some
f\in R_{0}
, it is a free module of rank
1
and any generator will be a unit for
R[\frac{1}{f}]
of degree
d
.

We finish with some observations about actually computing the local periods of a graded ring.
This will be used in
Section

7
.

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
R
be a graded ring and
\mathfrak{p}\in\Spec(R)
.
Then

\per_{\mathfrak{p}}(R)=\gcd\left(\deg(f)\ \bigm|\ f(\mathfrak{p})\neq 0,\deg(f)\neq 0\right).

(Here, we set
\gcd(\emptyset)=0
.)

Proof.

Straightforward.
∎

Corollary 2.15
.

Let
R
be a graded ring.
Then we have

(2.16)

\Per(R)=\bigcup_{\deg(f)\neq 0}D(f)

and it’s enough to let
f
run through generators of
R/\sqrt{0}
as an
R_{0}/\sqrt{0}
-algebra.
Moreover, for each homogeneous
f\in R
with
\deg(f)\neq 0
, we have
D(f)\subseteq\Per_{\deg(f)}(R)
.∎

3.
…and categories

Let
\mathscr{K}
be a stably symmetric monoidal
\infty
-category (
Section

1.6
).
Being a commutative algebra object in
\mathrm{Cat}^{\mathrm{\footnotesize ex}}
, we may see these as a categorification of the notion of an
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}
-ring (a commutative algebra object in
\Sp
).
In this section and the next we categorify the notions of the previous section and make them applicable in this context.

Just as the notions in
Section

2
only depended on the underlying graded ring of homotopy classes, so the notions to be introduced now only depend on the underlying homotopy category which has the structure of a tensor triangulated (or tt-)category (
Section

1.6
).

Example 3.1
.

The homotopy category of any stably symmetric monoidal
\infty
-category is a tt-category.
(It is enough for it to be
\mathbb{E}_{3}
-monoidal and for many purposes even
\mathbb{E}_{2}
would suffice.)
Any exact symmetric monoidal functor between such induces a tt-functor on the homotopy categories.

Example 3.2
.

Let
R
be a commutative local ring with maximal non-zero ideal generated by
2
such that
4=0
.
(For example
R=\mathbb{Z}/4

The category
\fmod^{\free}(R)
of finitely generated free
R
-modules has an exotic triangulated structure: it is not the homotopy category of a stable
\infty
-category
[
MSS07
]
.
One checks easily that the usual tensor product of free
R
-modules is exact so that
(\fmod^{\free}(R),\otimes_{R})
is a tt-category.

Example 3.3
.

Let
k\neq 0
be a semisimple ring.
The category
\fmod_{k}
of finitely generated
k
-modules admits a unique triangulated structure where the suspension is the identity functor.
The triangles are those forced by the axioms; equivalently, those that are exact in each spot.
If
k
is also commutative (hence a product of fields) then
(\fmod_{k},\otimes_{k})
is a tt-category.

In fact, the triangulated category
\fmod_{k}
is the stable category of the Frobenius algebra
k[\epsilon]/\langle\epsilon^{2}\rangle
and hence is the homotopy category of a stable
\infty
-category.

Remark 3.5

Let
\mathscr{K}

Remark 3.6

As pointed out in the introduction, the period is the generator of the kernel of the monoid homomorphism to the Picard group

Remark 3.8
.

For us, the most useful way of thinking about

This is a graded ring in the sense of

Now, a map

It follows that

Remark 3.9
.

Let
\mathscr{K}_{\langle\mathbb{1}\rangle}

This is the thick (automatically

Lemma 3.12

Let

Then there is a natural isomorphism between the two functors
\Sigma^{d},\id\colon\mathscr{K}\to\mathscr{K}

Proof.

We have natural isomorphisms of functors

\Sigma^{d}(-)\cong\Sigma^{d}\mathbb{1}\otimes(-)\cong\mathbb{1}\otimes(-)\cong\id.\qed{}

(2)

4.
Period stratification

In
2.10
we used the spectrum of a graded ring to define local periods and the periodic locus.
Similarly, a tt-category
\mathscr{K}
has an associated spectrum
\Spc(\mathscr{K})

[
Bal05
]
that will allow us in this section to categorify these notions as well.
Recall that its points are prime
\otimes
-ideals of
\mathscr{K}
and the topology is generated by the distinguished opens
U(x)=\{\mathscr{P}\mid x\in\mathscr{P}\}
for
x\in\mathscr{K}
.

Discussing the geometry of categorified rings (that is, tt-geometry) is more natural in the context of stably symmetric monoidal
\infty
-categories and we elaborate on this in
4.15
.

Convention 4.1
.

All tt-categories in this section will be assumed essentially small.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\mathscr{K}
be an (essentially small) tt-category.

(1)

Let
d>0
be an integer.
A point
\mathscr{P}\in\Spc(\mathscr{K})
is called
d
-periodic
(resp.
periodic
) if the associated local category
\mathscr{K}/\mathscr{P}
is.
And its period is
\per_{\mathscr{K}}(\mathscr{P}):=\per(\mathscr{K}/\mathscr{P})
.
The
d
-periodic locus

\Per_{d}(\mathscr{K})\subseteq\Spc(\mathscr{K})
is the subset of
d
-periodic points.

(2)

The
periodic locus
is the subset
\Per(\mathscr{K}):=\cup_{d>0}\Per_{d}(\mathscr{K})\subseteq\Spc(\mathscr{K})
.
The tt-category
\mathscr{K}
is called
locally periodic
if
\Spc(\mathscr{K})=\Per(\mathscr{K})
.

Remark 4.3
.

A locally periodic tt-category
\mathscr{K}
need not be
d
-periodic for any
d
.
Examples include most stable module categories, see
7.8
.
And even if
\mathscr{K}
is periodic and all points of
\Spc(\mathscr{K})
share the same period
d>0
, it does not follow that
\per(\mathscr{K})=d
.
An example is perfect modules over
\mathrm{TMF}
, see
7.4
.

Nevertheless, it is true that having period at most
d
is an open condition.
We now state this for future reference.

Convention 4.4
.

Let
\mathscr{K}
be a tt-category and let
Z\subseteq\Spc(\mathscr{K})
be a Thomason subset (for example the complement of a quasi-compact open; in general an arbitrary union of such).
Recall that this corresponds to a tt-ideal
\mathscr{I}_{Z}\subseteq\mathscr{K}
: the subcategory of objects with support contained in
Z
.
We denote by
\mathscr{K}|_{Z^{c}}:=\mathscr{K}/\mathscr{I}_{Z}
the localization, the
category
\mathscr{K}
on
Z^{c}
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\mathscr{K}
be a tt-category and let
\mathscr{P}\in\Spc(\mathscr{K})
be a
d
-periodic point, some
d>0
.
Then there exists a quasi-compact open neighborhood
\mathscr{P}\in U\subseteq\Spc(\mathscr{K})
such that
\mathscr{K}|_{U}
is
d
-periodic.

Proof.

By assumption we have
\mathbb{1}\cong\Sigma^{d}\mathbb{1}
in
\mathscr{K}/\mathscr{P}
.
This is witnessed by a fraction
\mathbb{1}\xleftarrow{\alpha}x\xrightarrow{\beta}\Sigma^{d}\mathbb{1}
in
\mathscr{K}
where both
\alpha
and
\beta
have cone contained in
\mathscr{P}
.
Then
U=\supp(\cone(\alpha))^{c}\cap\supp(\cone(\beta))^{c}
does the job.
∎

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
\mathscr{K}
be a tt-category and
d>0
.
The subset
\Per_{d}(\mathscr{K})\subseteq\Spc(\mathscr{K})
is open.
Hence also
\Per(\mathscr{K})
is open.
∎

Corollary 4.7
.

Let
\mathscr{K}
be a locally periodic tt-category.
There exists a non-empty quasi-compact open
U\subseteq\Spc(\mathscr{K})
such that
\mathscr{K}|_{U}
is periodic.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
F\colon\mathscr{K}\to\mathscr{L}
be a tt-functor, with
f=\Spc(F)\colon\Spc(\mathscr{L})\to\Spc(\mathscr{K})
the associated map on spectra.

(1)

If
d\bigm|d^{\prime}
then
\Per_{d}(\mathscr{K})\subseteq\Per_{d^{\prime}}(\mathscr{K})
.

(2)

If
f(\mathscr{P})
is
d
-periodic then so is
\mathscr{P}
. In other words,

f^{-1}(\Per_{d}(\mathscr{K}))\subseteq\Per_{d}(\mathscr{L}).

(3)

We have
\per_{\mathscr{L}}(\mathscr{P})\bigm|\per_{\mathscr{K}}(f(\mathscr{P}))
.

Proof.

Unwinding the definitions this is an immediate consequence of
3.10
.
∎

We can therefore think of
\Spc(\mathscr{K})
as filtered by the various period loci.
We presently make this picture precise.

Construction 4.9
.

Consider the set
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
with the divisibility relation.
We endow it with the Alexandrov topology.
Explicitly, a subset is open if and only if with
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
it contains all divisors of
d
.
For
\mathscr{K}
a tt-category consider the map

(4.10)

\per_{\mathscr{K}}\colon\Spc(\mathscr{K})\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}

that sends a point
\mathscr{P}
to
\per_{\mathscr{K}}(\mathscr{P})
.

In the following statement we consider
\Spc(\mathscr{K})
as a topological space, and also as a poset with the relation given by specialization:
\mathscr{P}\rightsquigarrow\mathscr{Q}
iff
\mathscr{Q}\in\overline{\{\mathscr{P}\}}
.

Proposition 4.11
.

(1)

The map (
4.10
) is continuous.
In other words,
\Spc(\mathscr{K})
is canonically stratified over
(\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},|)
.
2
2

2

The second sentence does not say more than the first one. But we like to think of
\Spc(\mathscr{K})
as being decomposed (‘stratified’) into the fibers of this map (the ‘strata’). Below we will see that the strata are also particularly nice (
4.13
).

(2)

The map
\per_{\mathscr{K}}\colon(\Spc(\mathscr{K}),\rightsquigarrow)\to(\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},|)
preserves the poset structure.

Proof.

Let
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and consider the open
U=\{e\mid e|d\}
.
If
d>0
then
\per_{\mathscr{K}}^{-1}(U)=\Per_{d}(\mathscr{K})
which is open, by
4.6
.
If
d=0
then
U=\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
so that
\per_{\mathscr{K}}^{-1}(U)=\Spc(\mathscr{K})
is open as well.
This shows that
\per_{\mathscr{K}}
is continuous.

For the second statement let
\mathscr{P}\rightsquigarrow\mathscr{Q}
. This is equivalent to
\mathscr{Q}\subseteq\mathscr{P}\subseteq\mathscr{K}
.
Thus a canonical functor
\mathscr{K}/\mathscr{Q}\to\mathscr{K}/\mathscr{P}
and we conclude by
3.10
.
∎

Definition 4.12
.

The resulting stratification on
\Spc(\mathscr{K})
is called the
period stratification
.

Remark 4.13
.

The strata are locally closed subsets (the intersection of an open and a closed subset).
The stratum over
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
is the set of points with period
d
.

Remark 4.14
.

Let
F\colon\mathscr{K}\to\mathscr{L}
be a tt-functor, and denote by
f\colon\Spc(\mathscr{L})\to\Spc(\mathscr{K})
the associated map on spectra.
It is not true in general that
f
respects the period stratification.
Rather, the correct relation is given by
4.8
:

\per_{\mathscr{L}}(\mathscr{P})\bigm|\per_{\mathscr{K}}(f(\mathscr{P}))

for every
\mathscr{P}\in\Spc(\mathscr{L})
.

Remark 4.15
.

Let
\mathrm{Cat}^{\mathrm{\footnotesize perf}}
denote the
\infty
-category of small idempotent-complete stable
\infty
-categories and exact functors between them, with the usual symmetric monoidal structure. Let
\mathscr{K}\in\calg(\mathrm{Cat}^{\mathrm{\footnotesize perf}})
be rigid, that is, every object in
\mathscr{K}
has a dual.

The association
U(x)\mapsto\mathscr{O}_{\mathscr{K}}(U(x)):=(\mathscr{K}/\langle x\rangle)^{\natural}
on distinguished open subsets
U(x)\subseteq\Spc(\mathscr{K})
underlies a sheaf
\mathscr{O}_{\mathscr{K}}
with values in
\calg(\mathrm{Cat}^{\mathrm{\footnotesize perf}})
which makes
(\Spc(\mathscr{K}),\mathscr{O}_{\mathscr{K}})
into an affine patch of tt-geometry.
This can be deduced essentially from the results in
[
BF07
]
but is also proven elegantly in
[
Aok+25
]
, using Lurie’s language of structured spaces
[
DAGV
]
.
From this point of view, the periodicity of points is a condition on the stalks of the structure sheaf because for every point
\mathscr{P}\in\Spec(\mathscr{K})
,

\mathscr{O}_{\mathscr{K},\mathscr{P}}=\colim_{x\in\mathscr{P}}\mathscr{O}_{\mathscr{K}}(U(x))=\left(\mathscr{K}/\mathscr{P}\right)^{\natural},

where the colimit is computed in
\calg(\mathrm{Cat}^{\mathrm{\footnotesize perf}})
.
The local periods and period loci therefore extend to non-affine tt-schemes.

Remark 4.16
.

While some of the properties of periods and the period stratification remind us of the characteristics of rings (
3.6
), we note that the analogue of a prime characteristic doesn’t seem to have the same import.
For example, there is no guarantee that a tt-field, as currently understood, has period a prime number or
0
.
(Indeed, the Morava
K
-theories don’t in general.)
Moreover, odd periods are underrepresented because of
3.11
.
And finally, there are perfectly respectable non-zero examples that are
1
-periodic (see
7.10
).

Remark 4.17
.

Because periodicity can be expressed solely in terms of the triangulated structure (
3.12
), the considerations here could naturally be generalized to tt-categories acting on triangulated categories.
From that perspective, the discussion in
[
Ste18
, § 6.1]
implies that global complete intersections are locally
2
-periodic.

5.
Ample families

Let
\mathscr{K}
be an essentially small stably symmetric monoidal
\infty
-category.
We have in effect defined
\per(\mathscr{K})
as
\per(R_{\mathscr{K}})
, see
3.8
.
But how do the local periods of
\mathscr{K}
relate to the local periods of
R_{\mathscr{K}}
?
One would hope that the canonical comparison map
[
Bal10
,
Aok+25
]

\comp_{\mathscr{K}}\colon\Spec(\mathscr{K})\to\Spec(R_{\mathscr{K}})

should be relevant for this question.
In general it gives the following information:

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\mathscr{P}\in\Spec(\mathscr{K})
and
\mathfrak{p}:=\comp_{\mathscr{K}}(\mathscr{P})
.
Then

\per_{\mathscr{K}}(\mathscr{P})\bigm|\per_{R_{\mathscr{K}}}(\mathfrak{p}).

Proof.

By
2.14
, it suffices to prove that
\per_{\mathscr{K}}(\mathscr{P})\bigm|\deg(f)
for every homogeneous
f\in R_{\mathscr{K}}\backslash\mathfrak{p}
.
The latter condition means exactly that
f\colon\Sigma^{\deg(f)}\mathbb{1}\to\mathbb{1}
has cone contained in
\mathscr{P}
.
The claim follows.
∎

In this section we will study situations where the comparison map (or a variant thereof) provides more precise information.
While a discussion at the level of tt-schemes would be desirable we have resisted the temptation to do so here (but see
5.18
).
In any case, in the sequel, only statements at the level of underlying spaces will be required.
Hence, from now on,
\mathscr{K}
denotes a tt-category, and throughout this section
4.1
remains in place.

Remark 5.2
.

Consider a particularly favourable case:
assume that the canonical comparison map
\comp_{\mathscr{K}}\colon\Spc(\mathscr{K})\to\Spc(R_{\mathscr{K}})
is a homeomorphism.
In that case, the local rings
R_{\mathscr{K}/\mathscr{P}}
are simply the stalks of
R_{\mathscr{K}}

[
Bal10
, Proposition 6.11]
.
In fact, as we will show later (
5.17
), to get this conclusion it is enough for the comparison map to be a homeomorphism onto its image (without being surjective).
Even this is in practice a restrictive assumption and to relax it we take a page from algebraic geometry (see for example
[
BS03
]
; of course, similar ideas are also familiar in other contexts, see for example abstract “multigraded stable homotopy theories” in
[
HPS97
]
).

Convention 5.3
.

Let
\mathscr{K}
be a tt-category and let
x\in\mathscr{K}
be some object.
A
global section
of
x
is a morphism
s\colon\mathbb{1}\to x
in
\mathscr{K}
.
More generally, a
global section of (cohomological) degree
d
of
x
is a global section of
\Sigma^{d}x
. We denote by
U(s):=\supp(\cone(s))^{c}\subseteq\Spc(\mathscr{K})
the (open) locus where
s
is invertible.

Definition 5.4
.

A submonoid
M\subseteq\Pic(\mathscr{K})
is called
ample
if the
U(s)
form a basis for the topology of
\Spc(\mathscr{K})
where
s
runs through all global sections of all
x\in M
.
A family of invertible objects in
\mathscr{K}
is called
ample
if the monoid generated by them is.

This almost recovers the notion of ampleness in algebraic geometry (
5.10
), where ample families of line bundles give rise to embeddings into projective varieties.
The tt-analogue is made possible in this generality by the comparison maps introduced in
[
DS14
]
that Dell’Ambrogio makes more widely applicable in Appendix
A
and which we summarize (for our purposes) in the following remark.

Remark 5.5
.

Let
M\subseteq\Pic(\mathscr{K})
be a submonoid.
There is an associated topological space and a continuous spectral comparison map

(5.6)

\comp_{M}\colon\Spc(\mathscr{K})\to\Spc(\mathscr{R}_{M})

where one would like to think of
\mathscr{R}_{M}
as the ‘multi-graded ring’
\oplus_{m\in M}\hom_{\mathscr{K}}(\mathbb{1},m)
of sections, and of the target of (
5.6
) as its Zariski spectrum of graded prime ideals.
A moment’s reflection will convince the reader that this conception is beset by coherence issues, and the solution of
[
DS14
]
and Appendix
A
is to change the meaning of a ‘multi-grading’.
For us, it will suffice to know that the topology on
\Spc(\mathscr{R}_{M})
is generated by opens
D(s)
associated to global sections of elements in
M
, and that
\comp_{M}^{-1}(D(s))=U(s)
(
A.8
).
Moreover, we mention that if the coherence issues alluded to can be resolved then
\Spc(\mathscr{R}_{M})
is the space underlying the usual graded spectrum (
A.15
).
For example, this is the case when
M
is generated by a single element (
A.21
).

Lemma 5.7
.

The map (
5.6
) has dense image.

Proof.

If the subset
\comp_{M}^{-1}(D(s))=U(s)
just mentioned is empty for some
s\colon\mathbb{1}\to m
with
m\in M
, this means that
s
is
\otimes
-nilpotent in
\mathscr{K}
so that also
D(s)=\emptyset
.
This shows that
\comp_{M}
has dense image.
∎

We can now formulate the expected result that follows directly from Appendix
A
.

Proposition 5.8
.

For a submonoid
M\subseteq\Pic(\mathscr{K})
the following are equivalent:

(1)

M
is ample.

(2)

The comparison map

\comp_{M}\colon\Spc(\mathscr{K})\to\Spc(\mathscr{R}_{M})

is a homeomorphism onto its image.

Proof.

We first show
1
\Rightarrow
2
.
Let
s
be a global section of some
m\in\mathscr{M}
.
As mentioned in
5.5
, we have
\comp_{M}^{-1}(D(s))=U(s)
.
Since
\Spc(\mathscr{K})
is a
T_{0}
-space, ampleness implies injectivity.
Again by ampleness, the map
\comp_{M}
is also open onto its image.

We now show the converse
2
\Rightarrow
1
.
As
s
runs through the global sections of all
x\in M
, the subsets
D(s)
define a basis for the topology on
\Spc(\mathscr{R}_{M})
(
5.5
).
By assumption then,
U(s)=\comp_{M}^{-1}(D(s))
define a basis on
\Spc(\mathscr{K})
.

∎

Corollary 5.9
.

The comparison map

\comp_{\mathscr{K}}\colon\Spc(\mathscr{K})\to\Spc(R_{\mathscr{K}})

is a homeomorphism onto its image if and only if
\{\Sigma^{d}\mathbb{1},d\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is ample.

Remark 5.10
.

Let
X
be a qcqs scheme.
A line bundle
\mathscr{L}
is ample in the algebro-geometric sense iff the invertibility loci of all sections of all powers
\mathscr{L}^{\otimes n}
for
n>0
form a basis for the topology of
X
.
In
5.4
we have reproduced this in tt-geometry except that we allow also
n=0
to account for the fact that the
\mathrm{Proj}
of a graded ring is replaced by
\Spc
.
One then proves in algebraic geometry that an ample line bundle gives rise to a quasi-compact open immersion with dense image.
The only thing
5.8
does not reproduce is that the image is open.
We do not know whether this is true in general.

It seems that we haven’t gained much in practice: While adding invertibles other than shifts makes it easier for a family
M
to become ample, the space
\Spc(\mathscr{R}_{M})
seemingly gets more mysterious at the same time.
To finish this section we discuss a situation that offers a good compromise.

Definition 5.11
.

Let
\mathscr{K}
be a tt-category.

(1)

An invertible object
x\in\Pic(\mathscr{K})
is called a
line bundle
if it is Zariski locally on
\Spc(\mathscr{K})
(isomorphic to) a shift of
\mathbb{1}
.
(The shift is allowed to vary.)

(2)

A line bundle
x\in\Pic(\mathscr{K})
is called
base-free
if it admits global sections
s_{j}
of some degree such that
\Spc(\mathscr{K})=\cup_{j}U(s_{j})
.

Remark 5.12
.

(1)

In other words,
x\in\Pic(\mathscr{K})
is a line bundle if it is locally a ‘trivial line bundle’.
And it is moreover base-free if these local identifications are defined globally.

(2)

Since
\Spc(\mathscr{K})
is quasi-compact one could equivalently ask for
finitely many
such sections
s_{j}
.

Construction 5.13
.

Let
\{x_{i}\}
be a finite family of base-free line bundles and let
M\subseteq\Pic(\mathscr{K})
be the submonoid generated by them.
It is convenient to assume that the trivial line bundles are contained:
\Sigma^{d}\mathbb{1}\in M
for all
d\in\mathbb{Z}
.
For each
i
choose finitely many sections
s_{ij}\colon\mathbb{1}\to\Sigma^{d_{ij}}x_{i}
such that
\cup_{j}U(s_{ij})=\Spc(\mathscr{K})
(
j\in J(i)
).
For any choice
\underline{j}=(j(i)\in J(i))_{i}
, the intersection
D(s_{\underline{j}}):=\cap_{i}D(s_{ij(i)})\subseteq\Spc(\mathscr{R}_{M})
sits in a pullback square of topological spaces (
A.29
):

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Spc(\mathscr{K})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Spc(\mathscr{R}_{M})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Spc(\mathscr{K}[s_{\underline{j}}^{-1}])}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces D(s_{\underline{j}})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\comp_{M}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\comp_{\{\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{1}\}}}

Moreover, by
A.21
, the bottom horizontal arrow identifies canonically with the usual graded comparison map.
In particular,
D(s_{\underline{j}})
canonically identifies with the graded spectrum of a graded ring.
We deduce that
\cup_{\underline{j}}D(s_{\underline{j}})\subseteq\Spc(\mathscr{R}_{M})
has a canonical structure of a graded scheme and the comparison map for
M
factors through it.
Taking the union over all possible choices of sections yields an open
P\{x_{i}\}\subseteq\Spc(\mathscr{R}_{M})
that admits a canonical structure of graded scheme, and a factorization

\Spc(\mathscr{K})\xrightarrow{\comp_{M}}P\{x_{i}\}.

(The last step is intended only to remove the dependency on the sections.
In practice, it might be more convenient to work with a single
\cup_{\underline{j}}D(s_{\underline{j}})
as above.)

Definition 5.14
.

A tt-category is called
divisorial
if it admits a finite ample family of base-free line bundles.

Convention 5.15
.

Recall the structure sheaf
\mathscr{O}^{\mathrm{gr}}_{K}
of graded rings on
\Spc(\mathscr{K})
.
It is a sheaf whose stalk at a point
\mathscr{P}\in\Spc(\mathscr{K})
is the local graded endomorphism ring
R_{\mathscr{K}/\mathscr{P}}
.
We denote by
\Specgr(\mathscr{K}):=\left(\Spc(\mathscr{K}),\mathscr{O}_{K}^{\mathrm{gr}}\right)
the associated locally graded ringed space
[
Bal10
, § 6]
.

Proposition 5.16
.

Let
\mathscr{K}
be a divisorial tt-category.

(1)

Any choice of finite ample family of base-free line bundles
\{x_{i}\}
identifies
\Spc(\mathscr{K})
(via
5.13
) as a subspace of the graded scheme
P\{x_{i}\}
.

(2)

For each
\mathscr{P}\in\Spc(\mathscr{K})
with image
\mathfrak{p}\in P\{x_{i}\}
the local rings identify:
R_{\mathscr{K}/\mathscr{P}}\cong\mathscr{O}_{P\{x_{i}\},\mathfrak{p}}
.

(3)

In particular, if the image of
\Spc(\mathscr{K})
in
P\{x_{i}\}
is open then
\Specgr(\mathscr{K})
is itself a graded scheme.

Proof.

We already know from
5.8
that
\comp_{M}
is a homeomorphism onto its image.
Therefore the first statement follows directly from
5.13
.
Also, the third statement follows from this and the second statement.
The second statement, finally, depends only on an open neighborhood of
\mathfrak{p}
and we reduce to the case where
\{\Sigma^{d}\mathbb{1},d\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is ample.
For later use we state this separately in
5.17
.
∎

Corollary 5.17
.

Assume that the comparison map
\comp_{\mathscr{K}}\colon\Spc(\mathscr{K})\to\Spc(R_{\mathscr{K}})
is a homeomorphism onto its image.
Then for each
\mathscr{P}\in\Spc(\mathscr{K})

6.
Dévissage

As discussed in
4.14
, tt-functors are only weakly compatible with periods.
In this section we’re interested in tools that nonetheless allow the transfer of information about periods across maps of spectra.

6.1.
Zariski descent

An example of a map that
does
preserve the period strata is a (pro-)open immersion:

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
\mathscr{K}
be a tt-category and
U\subseteq\Spc(\mathscr{K})
the complement of a Thomason subset.
The localization
\mathscr{K}\to\mathscr{K}|_{U}
induces on spectra the inclusion

U\cong\Spc(\mathscr{K}|_{U})\hookrightarrow\Spc(\mathscr{K})

and for each
\mathscr{P}\in U
corresponding to
\bar{\mathscr{P}}\in\Spc(\mathscr{K}|_{U})
we have
\per_{\mathscr{K}}(\mathscr{P})=\per_{\mathscr{K}|_{U}}(\bar{\mathscr{P}})
.

Proof.

The canonical functor
\mathscr{K}/\mathscr{P}\to(\mathscr{K}|_{U})/\bar{\mathscr{P}}
is an equivalence so that

\per_{\mathscr{K}}(\mathscr{P})=\per_{\mathscr{K}|_{U}}(\bar{\mathscr{P}}).\qed{}

Remark 6.2
.

It follows from
6.1
that periods descend along Zariski covers.
It is natural to ask about (weaker) descent properties along other types of covers, like étale descent.
Unfortunately, we were not able to prove anything of that sort.

6.2.

As discussed in

Lemma 6.8
.

Let

be a fully faithful tt-functor with

Let

Then:

(2)

For the first statement, let

For the converse, let

If

is the restriction to compact objects of a geometric functor between big tt-categories then this statement is proven in

In general, as pointed out to me by Greg Stevenson, one can repeat the same argument, replacing the big tt-categories by the module categories, as pioneered in

]

∎

Convention 6.9

10.
Isotropic points

In this section we recall the construction of isotropic points due to Du-Vishik
[
DV25
]
and Vishik
[
Vis24
]
, respectively.
We’ll see that (essentially by construction) their periods are all
0
and, using this, we relate them to the other points considered in the preceding section.

Convention 10.1
.

We fix a field
\mathbb{F}
of characteristic zero and a prime
p
.

Example 10.2
.

Let us recall the
isotropic points
in
\Spc(\DM(\mathbb{F};\mathbb{Z}/p)^{\omega})
from
[
Vis24
]
.
For this we start with a field extension
\mathbb{E}/\mathbb{F}
.
The associated isotropic prime
\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{iso}}(\mathbb{E})
is the kernel of the composite (on compact objects)

\DM(\mathbb{F};\mathbb{Z}/p)\xrightarrow{\otimes_{\mathbb{F}}\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{P}^{\infty})}\DM(\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{P}^{\infty});\mathbb{Z}/p)\to\DM(\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{P}^{\infty})/\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{P}^{\infty});\mathbb{Z}/p)

where the second functor is a localization, killing all
p
-anisotropic varieties.
7
7

7

Recall that a (locally of) finite type
\mathbb{F}
-scheme is
p
-anisotropic if all its closed points are of
p
-power degree over
\mathbb{F}
.

(The difficult part is showing that the kernel is prime.)

We now observe that the local isotropic category
\DM(\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{P}^{\infty})/\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{P}^{\infty});\mathbb{Z}/p)
admits a weight structure
[
Vis24
, Proposition 5.7]
so that
\per_{\DM(\mathbb{F};\mathbb{Z}/p)^{\omega}}(\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{iso}}(\mathbb{E}))=0
, by
3.14
.
It is also known that the isotropic points are all closed and hence there are no specialization relations among them
[
Vis25
]
.

Remark 10.3
.

By
[
Vis24
, Theorem 1.3]
, the equality
\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{iso}}(\mathbb{E})=\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{iso}}(\mathbb{E}^{\prime})
holds iff
\mathbb{E}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
where the latter means equivalence with respect to the following partial order.
If
\mathbb{E}=\mathbb{F}(X)
and
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}=\mathbb{F}(X^{\prime})
for smooth projective varieties
X,X^{\prime}/\mathbb{F}
then
\mathbb{E}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\geq}}\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
iff there is a correspondence
X\dashrightarrow X^{\prime}
of degree prime-to-
p
.
(Equivalently, the pushforward on Chow groups modulo
p
induced by
X\times_{\mathbb{F}}X^{\prime}\to X
is surjective.)
For arbitrary extensions write
\mathbb{E}=\colim\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}
and
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}=\colim\mathbb{E}^{\prime}_{\beta}
as filtered unions with
\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}
,
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}_{\beta}
finitely generated.
Then
\mathbb{E}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\geq}}\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
if for every
\beta
there exists
\alpha
such that
\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\geq}}\mathbb{E}^{\prime}_{\beta}
.

Example 10.4
.

Let us recall the
isotropic points
in
\Spc(\SH(\mathbb{F})^{\omega})
from

The isotropic point

where the second functor is a certain finite localization related to Morava
K
-theory at height

and at the prime

.
We won’t need to know the exact details but only note that by
[

, § 7]

maps (conservatively) to an
\infty

By
3.10

Similarly to

Remark 10.10
.

Recall that a field is
p
-special
if every finite extension is of
p
-power degree.
An algebraic field extension
\mathbb{E}_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{E}_{2}
that is
p
-special and minimal with respect to this property is a
p
-special closure
of
\mathbb{E}_{1}
.
This implies that all finite extensions of
\mathbb{E}_{1}
contained in
\mathbb{E}_{2}
are of degree prime to

.
Such a
p
-special closure always exists: it corresponds to the choice of a pro-
p
-Sylow subgroup of an absolute Galois group.

Proof of
10.8
.

Let
\mathbb{E}/\mathbb{F}
be a field extension with associated isotropic point
\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{iso}}(\mathbb{E})\in\Spc(\DM(\mathbb{F};\mathbb{Z}/p)^{\omega})
.
Choose a
p
-special closure
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
of
\mathbb{E}
and let
\mathbb{F}^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
be the algebraic closure of
\mathbb{F}
inside
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
.
It is easy to verify that
\mathbb{F}^{\prime\prime}
is
p
-special hence it contains a
p
-special closure
\mathbb{F}^{\prime}
of
\mathbb{F}
.
We claim that
\mathbb{E}\sim_{\mathbb{F}}\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
.
But this follows immediately from the fact that every finite intermediate field extension
\mathbb{E}\leq\mathbb{K}\leq\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
is of degree prime to
p
.

We have a commutative square

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\DM(\mathbb{F})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\DM(\mathbb{F}^{\prime})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\DAM(\mathbb{F})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\DAM(\mathbb{F}^{\prime})}

with horizontal arrows given by base change
\otimes_{\mathbb{F}}\mathbb{F}^{\prime}
and vertical arrows the canonical inclusions.
The claim above exactly shows that
\Spc(\otimes_{\mathbb{F}}\mathbb{F}^{\prime})(\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{iso}}(\mathbb{E}^{\prime}))=\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{iso}}(\mathbb{E})
.
Moreover, the map on spectra induced by the bottom horizontal arrow preserves closed points (see the proof of
[

It therefore suffices to prove the statement when

This is

Remark 10.11
.

Let
\mathscr{K}
be a rigid tt-category and

By

In fact, we know that there must be (many) additional points lying in the periodic locus because of

We note that they only exist if

And even then they typically cover only a tiny bit of the spectrum, see

In other words, we seem to have barely scratched the surface of
\Spc(\DM(\mathbb{F};\mathbb{Z}/p)^{\omega})

Remark 10.12

As mentioned in

coincide iff the corresponding fields are in relation
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\sim}}
.
For example, in

Convention 10.13
.

Let
G
be a profinite group and
H,H^{\prime}\leq G
two subgroups.
We define
H\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\leq}}H^{\prime}
iff
H(p)\leq_{G}H^{\prime}
(subconjugate) where
H(p)\leq H
is a pro-
p
-Sylow subgroup.
We also define
H\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\sim}}H^{\prime}
if
H\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\leq}}H^{\prime}
and
H^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\leq}}H
.

Lemma 10.14
.

Let
\mathbb{E},\mathbb{E}^{\prime}/\mathbb{F}
be algebraic extensions corresponding to closed subgroups
H,H^{\prime}\subseteq G_{\mathbb{F}}
.
Then:

(10.15)

\mathbb{E}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\geq}}\mathbb{E}^{\prime}\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad H\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\leq}}H^{\prime}

Proof.

We first consider the case where both
\mathbb{E}
and
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
are finitely generated hence finite.
Then
\mathbb{E}\otimes_{\mathbb{F}}\mathbb{E}^{\prime}\cong\prod_{g\in H\backslash G_{\mathbb{F}}/H^{\prime}}\bar{\mathbb{F}}^{H\cap{}^{g}H^{\prime}}
is a product of finite extensions whose degrees over
\mathbb{E}=\bar{\mathbb{F}}^{H}
are given by
[H\colon H\cap{}^{g}H^{\prime}]
.
We conclude that
\mathbb{E}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\geq}}\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
iff there exists
g\in G_{\mathbb{F}}
such that
p\nmid[H\colon H\cap{}^{g}H^{\prime}]
.
Letting
H(p)\leq H
be a pro-
p
-Sylow, this is equivalent to the existence of
g
such that
p\nmid[H(p)\colon H(p)\cap{}^{g}H^{\prime}]
, or
H(p)\leq_{G}H^{\prime}
.
This shows (
10.15
) in this case.

Appendix A
Generalized comparison maps

Ivo Dell’Ambrogio

In
[
DS14
]
Greg Stevenson and I developed a flexible generalization of Balmer’s comparison map
\rho\colon\Spc(\mathscr{K})\to\Spc(\End_{\mathscr{K}}^{*}(\mathbb{1}))
from the tt-spectrum of a tt-category
\mathscr{K}
to the Zariski spectrum of its graded central ring, where we allowed more variety of choice for the target space.
Some such variations on the basic theme were already possible in
[
Bal10
]
, where the grading of the central ring could be twisted by any invertible object
\omega
other than the canonical choice
\omega=\Sigma(\mathbb{1})
.
Morally one should also be able to use a collection of several invertible objects to obtain gradings by abelian groups more general than
\mathbb{Z}
, but it is unclear in what generality such ‘multigraded central rings’ actually exist as ordinary rings, because one quickly runs into coherence problems.
(In the literature such problems are sometimes swept under the carpet.)
The solution of
[
DS14
]
was to avoid coherence issues altogether by considering central graded commutative
2-rings
of
\mathscr{K}
, i.e. full tensor subcategories whose objects are all invertible.
It turns out that such 2-rings
\mathscr{R}
admit a Zariski spectrum of homogeneous prime ideals, defined essentially in the usual way, as well as a comparison map
\Spc(\mathscr{K})\to\Spc(\mathscr{R})
enjoying all the basic properties of Balmer’s.

Somehow, in
[
DS14
]
we had neglected to verify that
if
there exists a multigraded ring
R
which is suitably equivalent to
\mathscr{R}
, then the resulting Zariski spectra and comparison maps actually agree.
The first goal of this appendix is to remedy that oversight; see
A.15
and
A.18
.

The second goal is to further generalize our comparison maps by allowing central 2-rings which are graded by a sub-2-monoid of the Picard 2-group; see
A.3
.
This generalization is akin to considering (and in fact generalizes) an
\mathbb{N}
-graded central ring of
\mathscr{K}
such as the connective or coconnective part,
\End^{\leq 0}(\mathbb{1})
or
\End^{\geq 0}(\mathbb{1})
, of the usual graded central ring.
We show the Zariski spectrum and comparison map of
[
DS14
]
work equally well in this greater generality.
In particular, they are still compatible with central localization; see
A.25
and
A.29
.

From now on, fix an essentially small tt-category
\mathscr{K}
with tensor
\otimes
and unit
\mathbb{1}
.

A.1.
Comparison maps for central 2-rings of
\mathscr{K}

We recall the main tools of
[
DS14
]
:

Definition A.1
.

A
graded commutative 2-ring
is an essentially small preadditive (
=

Ab
-enriched) symmetric monoidal category
\mathscr{R}
in which every object is tensor-invertible.
The tensor product is assumed to be additive in each variable.
By hypothesis, the maximal subgroupoid of
\mathscr{R}
is equal to its Picard category
\2Pic(\mathscr{R})
(
=
its tensor subcategory of invertible morphisms and invertible objects); hence in particular it is an
abelian 2-group
(
=
a symmetric monoidal category in which all morphisms and objects are invertible) having the same objects as
\mathscr{R}
.
We think of the 2-ring
\mathscr{R}
as being graded by the abelian 2-group
\2Pic(\mathscr{R})
.
In the following we will often drop the words “graded commutative” and “abelian”, since no other kind of 2-rings or 2-groups will be considered.

Remark A.2

(Commutativity and translation)
.

We call such 2-rings “graded commutative” because the product of its elements (i.e. the composition of its morphisms) actually commutes ‘up to
\2Pic(\mathscr{R})
’, i.e. up to taking twists by objects and composing with isomorphisms.
More precisely, we say that a morphism
\tilde{r}\in\mathscr{R}
is a
translate
of another
r\in\mathscr{R}
if

\tilde{r}
can be obtained from
r
by applying any finite sequence of twists and compositions with isomorphisms.
This is equivalent to having
\tilde{r}=v(g\otimes r)u
for a single object
g
and two isomorphisms
u,v
(
[
DS14
, Lemma 2.7]
), and defines an equivalence relation (
translation
) on the morphisms of
\mathscr{R}
.
Then one can show that for any two composable morphisms
r
and
s
in
\mathscr{R}
we have
s\circ r=\tilde{r}\circ\tilde{s}
for some translates
\tilde{r}
of
r
and
\tilde{s}
of
s
(
[
DS14
, Prop. 2.9]
).

Based on this,
[
DS14
]
proceeds to develop the affine algebraic geometry of a graded commutative 2-ring
\mathscr{R}
, pretty much as if it were an ordinary graded ring.
A
homogeneous ideal
, or just
ideal
,
\mathscr{I}
of
\mathscr{R}
is a (two-sided) categorical ideal of morphisms which is closed under twists, i.e. under tensoring morphisms with arbitrary objects (and hence under translation).
An ideal
\mathfrak{p}
is
prime
if it is proper (
\neq\mathscr{R}
) and if
s\circ r\in\mathfrak{p}
implies
s\in\mathfrak{p}
or
r\in\mathfrak{p}
.
The set

\Spc(\mathscr{R})

of all prime ideals of
\mathscr{R}
is endowed with the
Zariski topology
, where closed subsets are of the form

V(\mathscr{I})=\{\mathfrak{p}\in\Spc(\mathscr{R})\mid\mathscr{I}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}\}

for some ideal
\mathscr{I}\subseteq\mathscr{R}
.
Maximal ideals (for inclusion) are prime, and we have
\Spc(\mathscr{R})=\emptyset
if and only if
\mathscr{R}\simeq 0
.
The resulting
Zariski spectrum
is a spectral space in the sense of Hochster, and is functorial for morphisms
F\colon\mathscr{R}\to\mathscr{R}^{\prime}
of 2-rings (i.e. additive tensor functors): they induce spectral continuous maps

\Spc(F)\colon\Spc(\mathscr{R}^{\prime})\to\Spc(\mathscr{R})

via
\Spc(F)(\mathfrak{p})=F^{-1}\mathfrak{p}
, so that
\Spc(F_{2}\circ F_{1})=\Spc(F_{1})\circ\Spc(F_{2})
as well as
\Spc(\Id_{\mathscr{R}})=\Id_{\Spc(\mathscr{R})}
.
More details on all this can be found in
[
DS14
, §2]
.

Definition A.3

(Central 2-rings of
\mathscr{K}
)
.

Now recall our tt-category
\mathscr{K}
, and let
\mathscr{M}
be any replete (
=
closed under isomorphic objects) full sub-2-monoid (
=
full symmetric monoidal subcategory) of its Picard category
\2Pic(\mathscr{K})
.
We associate to
\mathscr{M}
a graded commutative 2-ring
\mathscr{R}_{\mathscr{M}}
defined as follows.
The objects of
\mathscr{R}_{\mathscr{M}}
are those of the 2-group-completion
\overline{\mathscr{M}}
, the full sub-2-group of
\2Pic(\mathscr{K})
generated by
\mathscr{M}
(add the inverses of all objects).
Its Hom groups are

\mathscr{R}_{\mathscr{M}}(g,h):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathscr{K}(g,h)&\textrm{if $h\otimes g^{-1}\in\mathscr{M}$ ({i.e.\ }if $h\cong m\otimes g$ for some $m\in\mathscr{M}$)}\\
0&\textrm{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

The composition and tensor structure are inherited from
\mathscr{K}
in the evident way, and we easily check that the result is indeed a graded commutative 2-ring
\mathscr{R}_{\mathscr{M}}
, graded by the 2-group
\overline{\mathscr{M}}
.
(For the latter: if
r\in\mathscr{K}(g,h)
is an isomorphism between any
g,h\in\overline{\mathscr{M}}
, then
h\otimes g^{-1}\cong g\otimes g^{-1}\cong\mathbb{1}\in\mathscr{M}
, from which it follows that indeed
\2Pic(\mathscr{R}_{\mathscr{M}})=\overline{\mathscr{M}}
.)
We say that
\mathscr{R}_{\mathscr{M}}
is the
central 2-ring
associated to
\mathscr{M}
, or by a slight abuse of language, that it is the
\mathscr{M}
-graded
central 2-ring of
\mathscr{K}
.

Remark A.4
.

We stress that
\mathscr{R}_{\mathscr{M}}
is in general not a full subcategory of
\mathscr{K}
(because of the “otherwise” case).
Indeed, it is full precisely when
\mathscr{M}
is already a sub-2-group, that is when
\overline{\mathscr{M}}=\mathscr{M}
.
Only full central 2-rings were considered in
[
DS14
]
.

Remark A.5
.

By definition, however, we require central 2-rings to be replete in
\mathscr{K}
, mostly for aesthetic reasons (similarly to thick subcategories etc.).
We also ask that our full sub-2-monoids
\mathscr{M}\subseteq\2Pic(\mathscr{K})
be replete.
The latter implies that every such
\mathscr{M}
is uniquely determined by the ordinary submonoid
M=\mathrm{K}_{0}(\mathscr{M})
of the ordinary Picard group
\Pic(\mathscr{K})
.
Consequently, we may view the central 2-rings of
\mathscr{K}
as being parametrized by the submonoids of the Picard group

M\subseteq\Pic(\mathscr{K})\quad\longmapsto\quad\mathscr{R}_{M}:=\mathscr{R}_{\mathscr{M}}\subset\mathscr{K},

where
\mathscr{M}\subseteq\mathscr{K}
is the full tensor subcategory of all objects whose isomorphism class lies in
M
.
(Note that
a priori
the above mapping need not be injective because
M
may contain classes
[g]
such that
\mathscr{K}(\mathbb{1},g)=0
, so that a proper submonoid of
M
omitting such
[g]
can still have the same associated central 2-ring.)

Example A.6

(Minimal and maximal central 2-rings)
.

The minimal and maximal sub-2-monoids,
\mathscr{M}_{\min}=\{\mathbb{1}\}
and
\mathscr{M}_{\max}=\2Pic(\mathscr{K})
, are sub-2-groups and thus determine two full central 2-rings of
\mathscr{K}
.
For all purposes, as we shall see, the minimal one
\mathscr{R}_{\{\mathbb{1}\}}
can be identified with the ordinary central ring
\mathrm{R}_{\mathscr{K}}=\End(\mathbb{1})
.

Example A.7

(Cyclic central 2-rings)
.

For every invertible object

\mathscr{K}

and therefore three central 2-rings

\mathscr{R}_{\omega}^{+}:=\mathscr{R}_{\mathscr{M}_{\omega}^{+}}

(If
\mathscr{R}_{\omega}^{+}

They could be called, respectively, the

The canonical choice

\tau
, that is a function
\tau\colon G\times G\to\mathrm{Z}(R)_{1}^{\times}
into the abelian group of invertible central elements of degree one, satisfying:

•

symmetry:
\tau(x,y)=\tau(y,x)
,

•

bilinearity, i.e. written multiplicatively:
\tau(x,yz)=\tau(x,y)\tau(x,z)
.

•

transposition:
rs=\tau(|r|,|s|)sr
for homogeneous
r\in R_{|r|}
and
s\in R_{|s|}
.

Remark A.10
.

The symmetry and bilinearity are analogous to the axioms of a symmetry for a monoidal category, and indeed they ensure that we may perform any sequence of transpositions (as in the third axiom) of adjacent elements in a product of several elements of
R
, and the result will only depend on the overall permutation.
Typically
\tau
is chosen to only take values in
\pm 1
(as assumed for example in
[
DS13
]
), but it does not have to be so.
However, since transposition implies both
\tau(|r|,|s|)^{-1}rs=sr
and
\tau(|s|,|r|)rs=sr
, symmetry suggests we may want to assume from the start that
\tau(x,y)=\tau(x,y)^{-1}
, that is
\tau(x,y)^{2}=1
.

If
R
is any multigraded commutative ring as in
A.9
, one can define homogeneous ideals (i.e. two-sided ideals generated by homogeneous elements) and the Zariski spectrum

\Spc(R)

of homogeneous primes precisely in the same way as for the more common
\mathbb{Z}
-graded commutative rings.
Note that the question whether a subset of
R
is an ideal or a prime—and therefore the spectral space
\Spc(R)
—does not depend on the particular transposition rule
\tau
enjoyed by
R
.

In the next definition (our most technical), one may wish to assume at first reading that
\pi
is the identity map.

Definition A.11

(Tightening)
.

Let
\mathscr{R}
be any graded commutative 2-ring, let
\mathscr{G}=\2Pic(\mathscr{R})
be its grading 2-group, and let
\mathrm{K}_{0}(\mathscr{G})
be its abelian group of isomorphism classes
[g]
of objects

.
Let
\pi\colon G\twoheadrightarrow\mathrm{K}_{0}(\mathscr{G})
be a surjective morphism of abelian groups (for example the identity).
A
tightening
R=(R,G,\pi,\{g_{\gamma}\},\{\varphi_{x}\})
of
\mathscr{R}
consists of a
G
-graded commutative ring
R=\bigoplus_{x\in G}R_{x}
, for some transposition rule
\tau
(
A.9
), equipped with the choices of a complete set of representative objects
\{g_{\gamma}\}_{\gamma\in\mathrm{K}_{0}(\mathscr{G})}
for
\mathrm{K}_{0}(\mathscr{G})
and of group isomorphisms

satisfying the following two conditions (where we write
\overline{x}:=\pi(x)
for readability):

(1)

g_{1}=\mathbb{1}
, and for every
x\in G
and all
s\in R_{1}
and

we have

\varphi_{x}(rs)=\varphi_{x}(r)\circ\varphi_{1}(s).

In particular, with
x=1
we get a ring isomorphism
\varphi_{1}\colon R_{1}\overset{\sim}{\to}\End_{\mathscr{R}}(\mathbb{1})
.

(2)

More generally, for any pair of homogeneous elements
r\in R_{x}
and
s\in R_{y}
, the three morphisms of
\mathscr{R}
(the first and third hiding an unwritten unitor)

(\varphi_{x}(r)\otimes g_{\overline{y}})\circ\varphi_{y}(s)\quad\quad\varphi_{x}(r)\otimes\varphi_{y}(s)\quad\quad(g_{\overline{x}}\otimes\varphi_{y}(s))\circ\varphi_{x}(r)

are all translates of
\varphi_{xy}(rs)
, as in
A.2
. (In fact it suffices for one of them to be, since the three maps are all evident translates of one another.)

Remark A.12
.

Because of the asymmetry of the definition, these could more precisely be called
right
tightenings, and of course there is an equivalent notion of left tightening.

Remark A.13
.

The two compatibility conditions in
A.11
between the product of
R
and the composition of
\mathscr{R}
amount to the decent minimum one should ask if
R
and
\mathscr{R}
are to be deemed equivalent.
We could try to give a more structured definition, involving more choices and more axioms.
We prefer to remain uncommitted so as to capture all possible solutions, and in any case this is enough for obtaining the expected comparison map of
A.18
.

Remark A.14
.

In our definition of a tightening,
the point of allowing morphisms
\pi
which are
not
the identity
G=\mathrm{K}_{0}(\2Pic(\mathscr{R}))
is to capture how certain very common examples are usually treated, see
A.21
.
Besides, note that if
\pi\colon G\to\mathrm{K}_{0}(\mathscr{G})
is a group surjection and
R
is a
\mathrm{K}_{0}(\mathscr{G})
-graded ring, it is always possible to view

as a
G
-graded ring by setting
R_{x}:=R_{\pi(x)}
for all
x\in G
.
Going the other way, however—to produce a
\mathrm{K}_{0}(\mathscr{G})
-grading on a
G

Hence
a priori
our definition really is more general than if we only allowed
\pi=\Id
.

Remark A.22

(Free tightenings for all)
.

A.3.
Central localization

To conclude this appendix, we briefly recall from

Let
\mathscr{R}
be any graded commutative 2-ring.
A subset

[

Let

As in
[

]
, we write

for the resulting localized category.

(Central localizations of

)
.
