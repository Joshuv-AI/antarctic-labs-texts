Beta-diversity of urban vegetation shapes microbial heterogeneity and maintains healthy environment
Authors: Chang Zhao, Ari Jumpponen, Heikki Setälä, D Johan Kotze, David A Wardle, Martin Romantschuk, Yong Yin, Nan Hui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe biodiversity hypothesis links urban species richness to human immunity, yet overlooks habitat heterogeneity that can quarantine pathogenic microbiota. We propose the β-diversity hypothesis: increased tree heterogeneity promotes urban microbial heterogeneity and improve human health. Using plant surveys and paired data of soil, phyllosphere and airborne microbiota transferred indoors (AMTI) across 72 Shanghai neighborhoods, we showed that elevated tree heterogeneity corresponds to higher β-diversity in both AMTI and soil communities. The spatial patterning of respiratory diseases (RDs) was driven by AMTI β-heterogeneity, revealing that homogenization of airborne microbiota affects human respiratory health. We further developed the urban tree βdis model and identified a threshold value of 0.661. Selecting phylogenetically distant tree families, e.g. Arecaceae, Oleaceae and Magnoliaceae at or above this threshold yields ready-to-use planting templates that maintains AMTI β-diversity and a scalable protocol for city-wide greening. Given microbial heterogeneity’s emerging health links, our findings call for embedding the β-diversity hypothesis into biodiversity–health frameworks and for cross-biome validation.Subject terms: Biodiversity, Microbial ecology

Introduction

In recent years, the interplay between biodiversity and human health has drawn increasing attention. The biodiversity hypothesis posits that reduced exposure to natural microbial diversity may impair the development and regulatory function of the human commensal microbiota, with implications for immune homeostasis1. Although this concept has been supported by ecological and medical research and increasingly applied to urban ecosystems2,3, most studies have focused on α-diversity—the species richness and evenness within local communities4. In contrast, the role of β-diversity, representing the compositional differences between microbial communities across space5,6 in shaping health outcomes is poorly understood but potentially more significant. Ecological theory suggests that β-diversity may be a more critical driver than α-diversity in structuring population dynamics and regulating community-level interactions7,8. In particular, spatial heterogeneity in biodiversity can disrupt the propagation and transmission of human-pathogenic microorganisms through reduced environmental homogeneity and diluted pathogen sources9. Therefore, examining how β-diversity influences microbial variability and human health effects is essential for advancing the biodiversity hypothesis, especially in urban landscapes where most humans reside.

Globally, β-diversity, as a key indicator of ecological heterogeneity, and it is critical in maintaining ecosystem functional stability and promoting microbial spatial variation10,11. The urban impact on soil microbial β-heterogeneity has become a central topic in global change ecology. Our previous studies at both global and regional scales have provided critical insights into the general mechanisms and regional variation underlying this phenomenon. Urbanization weakens the regulatory role of plant functional types on soil attributes, reducing vegetation-driven differences in urban cores by approximately 35% compared to natural forests12. It also transforms the dominant drivers of microbial β-diversity from geographic distance and vegetation diversity to anthropogenic factors such as soil compaction and nitrogen deposition, leading to an “asynchronous homogenization” of the soil microbial community across cities13. This shift not only reduces the abundance and richness of key functional groups, such as ectomycorrhizal fungi in open urban habitats14, but also alters central soil processes such as decomposition and nutrient cycling. In boreal cities, for example, urbanization impairs saprophytic fungal communities, slowing the breakdown of recalcitrant woody debris by 28% compared to natural forests15. Evergreen trees sustain soil organic carbon heterogeneity through high root productivity and low litter decomposition rates16, partially preserving microbial β-diversity and offering a potential strategy to counter homogenization effects in cold-climate urban ecosystems. Together, these findings highlight the interplay between general urbanization effects and local adaptive responses, laying the foundation for understanding how urban ecosystems maintain functional stability.

Over the past decade a growing number of studies have focused on advancing our knowledge in the area of “vegetation–microbe–ecological function17,18. However, a notable gap remains in understanding the links between environmental microbial heterogeneity and their health effects. Studies by Francini et al.19 and Hui et al.20 demonstrated that in older urban parks, microbial β-heterogeneity is significantly higher beneath broad-leaved than conifer trees. They also reported that ectomycorrhizal (ECM) fungal heterogeneity can be preserved through retaining native vegetation, and that ECM fungal diversity can account for up to 32% of the spatial variation in nitrogen mineralization. In terms of ecosystem services, we have assessed how urban plant communities and their diversity contribute to the provision of multiple ecosystem services. Yli-Pelkonen et al.21 showed species-specific effects of trees on airborne pollutants. Lu et al.22 confirmed the differences in nitrogen cycling within different vegetation types in urban environments. ur Rahman et al.23 developed vegetation configuration strategies that utilize tree species selection and spatial proximity to shape soil microbial community heterogeneity, whereas24 reviewed the health benefits of urban green spaces. However, these studies did not explore microbially mediated effects on human health. Although growing research has elucidated how vegetation influences soil-associated microbial processes and biogeochemical cycles, the role of vegetation heterogeneity in structuring nearby microbial communities, including airborne microbiota transferred indoors (AMTI) and those potentially originating from the phyllosphere, and its potential effects on human health, remains poorly understood. Previous studies have shown that vegetation can cause human respiratory disease (RD) through pollen and allergen-associated microbial exposure25,26. Vegetation can also shape environmental microbial communities, e.g., directly through soil-derived airborne microbiota and indirectly affect human health27. Specifically, vegetation type and spatial configuration can alter plant-associated and soil-dwelling microbial source pools, emission/transport of bioaerosols, and ultimately the composition and heterogeneity of near-human microbial communities, which have been linked to immune modulation and respiratory outcomes28. Urban vegetation planning often prioritizes increasing species richness29, yet this alone can result in overrepresentation of a few planted species with low functional or ecological value, and the implications of such composition on ecosystem services remain insufficiently understood in urban planning. Previous studies have shown that heterogeneity in vegetation composition is critical for delivering multiple ecosystem services, including climate regulation, habitat provision, and human well‑being, and should inform urban greening decisions30,31. These features make neighborhood-scale vegetation configuration a feasible approach to modify human environmental exposures. However, it is still unclear how different vegetation types and configurations affect near-human microbial communities and potential disease risks. This substantially limits our ability to link optimization of ecological functions and human health outcomes in urban settings.

Soil and phyllosphere microbiota are primary sources of airborne microbial communities and, therefore, integral to their composition and functional properties. Soil, in particular, is a major microbial reservoir, dispersing potentially pathogenic, allergenic, and antibiotic-resistant microorganisms into the atmosphere, especially under dry and disturbed conditions32. Wind, water, or human activities can lift microbes into the air and thus influence the composition of airborne microbiota at both local and regional scales27. The phyllosphere is a dynamic conduit, wherein foliar microbes can be released into air via evaporation and wind33. AMTI represent a critical environment-human interface that involves the movement of microbes from the soil, phyllosphere, and open-air environments into indoor spaces. AMTI often includes bacteria with antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs), which may threaten respiratory health34,35. While plant α-diversity can shape the composition and ARG load of AMTI, the role of plant β-heterogeneity in modulating microbial exposure and health outcomes remains poorly understood. Field observations in Shanghai suggest that local plant community composition correlates with seasonal variability in RD incidence and the AMTI composition36,37. Additionally, β-heterogeneity in overstorey plants is associated with compositional variation in airborne microbes38. However, the impact of vegetation heterogeneity on the composition of AMTI communities and human health has been little studied.

Building on our three previous research programs, including ECOGRAD39, PARKTRAITS12, and VENTUBE40, we propose a “β-diversity hypothesis”, aiming to contribute towards a theory that offers a practical application in urban ecology, with the ultimate goal of promoting human health. Earlier studies have already explored the influence of urban green space vegetation in Shanghai on microbial community heterogeneity23. Here, we analyzed the relationships between plant β-heterogeneity and microbial community composition in 72 urban neighborhoods in Shanghai. The objectives of this study are: (i) assessing whether plant β-heterogeneity drives spatial variability in microbial communities of AMTI, phyllosphere, and the soil, and (ii) determining whether this microbial variability influences the distribution of ARGs, thereby potentially affecting RD prevalence. The “β-diversity hypothesis” posits that heterogeneous plant communities drive environmental microbial diversity and function, and the resulting microbial heterogeneity benefits human health. This hypothesis not only advances ecological understanding but also provides practical insights for biodiversity-based urban greening strategies that emphasizes the potential of β-diversity as a tool to optimize urban microbial environments and promote human health.

Results

AMTI, phyllosphere, and soil microbial community responses to tree heterogeneity

The study was conducted across 72 neighborhoods in Shanghai, China, where a total of 1557 trees from 66 species were recorded within 1000 m² sampling areas centered on AMTI deployment points. In this study, tree β-diversity refers to Bray–Curtis dissimilarity of the neighborhood-scale tree community within the 1000 m² survey frame, whereas phyllosphere microbial β-diversity and soil microbial β-diversity refer to Bray–Curtis dissimilarities of (respectively) the Cinnamomum camphora leaf-surface microbiota and the soil microbiota sampled beneath C. camphora. We analyzed the heterogeneity of tree communities within the sampling neighborhoods. To partition tree communities into high and low heterogeneity groups, we used a threshold defined by the contribution index Ckt=0.5, which was detailed in the “Methods” section. For consistency between old and young neighborhoods, the threshold in terms of within-neighborhood tree diversity always corresponded to the higher observed value (Fig. 1). Specifically, in the cold season, this diversity threshold was 0.661 (Bray–Curtis dissimilarity value, βdis), and in the warm season, it was 0.805, both representing the levels of tree diversity at which the overall contribution (Ck(t)) reached 0.5. A similar trend was observed in the AMTI microbial communities, with consistently higher βdis in the old neighborhoods than the young ones. We used higher βdis values to define the thresholds for classifying bacterial and fungal communities into β-dissimilarity high-contribution (βdis_high) and β-dissimilarity low-contribution (βdis_low) groups. In the cold season, these thresholds were 0.420 for bacterial communities and 0.652 for fungal communities. In the warm season, the corresponding thresholds were 0.689 for bacteria and 0.503 for fungi.

We further observed that in the old neighborhoods (constructed 22–85 years ago), bacterial AMTI community heterogeneity increased with tree community heterogeneity, a pattern consistent across the seasons (Fig. 2a, c). This relationship was not observed in the young neighborhoods (constructed 0–10 years ago). In contrast, for the fungal AMTI communities, the tree heterogeneity influence was evident in the young neighborhoods, and the fungal heterogeneity increased with increasing tree heterogeneity (Fig. S1). In the phyllosphere microbial communities, both bacterial and fungal community heterogeneity increased with increasing tree heterogeneity in both the old and young neighborhoods during the cold season (Figs. 2e, f and S1e, f). Response of dominant microbes and ARGs in AMTI bacterial communities to tree heterogeneity

We targeted the four most abundant AMTI bacteria and compared their frequencies between the tree βdis_high and βdis_low groups. Results show that the dominant bacteria (Cupriavidus cauae, Vulcaniibacterium thermophilum, Pseudomonas lurida, and Anoxybacillus kestanbolensis) were less frequent in the βdis_low tree group than in the βdis_high tree group, suggesting that neighborhood tree community heterogeneity correlated with the microbial community heterogeneity (Fig. 3a). However, this pattern was observed exclusively during the cold season. We investigated how the relative abundances of eleven typical ARGs associated with RDs in AMTI bacterial communities responded to the tree heterogeneity. The STAMP analysis showed no statistically significant differences in the abundance of any ARGs between the tree βdis_high and βdis_low groups in either the cold or warm season. However, some ARGs exhibited trends of variation, including tetX, sat4, aadA1, and fox5 in the cold season (Fig. 3b), and mexD, qnrA, mecA, and vanA in the warm season (Fig. 3c). These results suggest that although tree heterogeneity affects the AMTI bacterial community heterogeneity, it does not influence the abundances of ARGs examined here. We also evaluated the ARG profiles in soil bacterial communities. Our analysis revealed no differences in the abundances of the eleven ARGs between the high and low tree heterogeneity groups in soil in the cold or warm seasons (Fig. S2). Soil microbial communities were included as an independent environmental reference to test the generality of our AMTI findings, not to directly compare with or infer phyllosphere diversity.

Effects of microbial heterogeneity on respiratory disease

We conducted standardized questionnaires in the old neighborhoods during the cold season. A total of 1026 valid questionnaires were collected from 30 ± 5 residents per neighborhood, aged 40–80 years, with equal gender representation and a minimum of 10 years’ residency. Participants were medically screened by a licensed physician to verify self-reported symptoms. The results in Fig. 2 demonstrated significant differences in the AMTI bacterial community heterogeneity between tree heterogeneity groups in the old neighborhoods. We further employed NMDS visualization to characterize the spatial patterning of RD across AMTI bacterial community heterogeneity clusters. Additionally, we compared the coefficient of variation (CV) of five RDs between the AMTI bacterial βdis_high and βdis_low groups. The CV in these plots reflects the within-group variation in disease prevalence among the AMTI bacterial heterogeneity groups, representing how much disease prevalence fluctuates across neighborhoods within each group, an important metric to better understand the spatial variation in disease occurrence. Our analysis revealed that, in the cold season, communities did not differ (PERMANOVA: P = 0.872, R2 = 0.018), whereas PERMDISP revealed significant dispersion heterogeneity (P = 0.038, R2 = 0.101) (Fig. 4a). This indicates the non-significant PERMANOVA result is unreliable due to unequal dispersion. In the warm season, both tests were significant (PERMANOVA: P = 0.041, R2 = 0.067; PERMDISP: P = 0.047, R2 = 0.230) (Fig. 4c), confirming genuine differences in both composition and dispersion. These results suggest that the spatial patterns of RDs are associated with AMTI bacterial heterogeneity, but the interpretation of PERMANOVA must account for dispersion heterogeneity. The low R2 of PERMANOVA and significant inter-group dispersion revealed by PERMDISP indicate that the observed significance reflects heterogeneous spread rather than a true compositional shift. Thus, microbial heterogeneity appears linked to dispersion differences, and less so to compositional differences in RD. However, in the cold season, the CV for asthma, rhinitis, rhinoconjunctivitis, and wheeze was consistently higher in the βdis_high group than in the βdis_low group (Fig. 4b), indicating more spatially uneven disease occurrence across neighborhoods within the βdis_high group. In contrast, the βdis_low group showed more spatially uniform patterns for these conditions. In the warm season, the pattern of CV was generally similar to that observed in the cold season, with four diseases (except shortness of breath) showing more spatially uniform pattern in the βdis_low group (Fig. 4d). We calculated the rit value of microbial communities, representing microbial neighborhood-level relative contribution (βdissinglerelativecontribution), which was detailed in the “Methods” section. Results from linear regression revealed that the contribution of microbial community heterogeneity correlated with local RD prevalence. Notably, the βdissinglerelativecontribution of both AMTI bacteria and fungi negatively correlated with the total RDs prevalence as well as with some specific symptoms, e.g., shortness of breath and wheeze (Fig. S3a–f). Interestingly, such negative correlations were also observed for soil bacteria and fungi (Fig. S3g–l). Regarding alpha diversity, our analyses showed that both AMTI bacterial and fungal communities exhibited mixed associations with RD-associated taxa (Fig. S4a, b). Notably, fungal diversity and evenness positively correlated with the prevalence of shortness of breath, though no significant links were observed between bacterial alpha diversity (richness, diversity, and evenness) and RD prevalences (Fig. S4c). These results suggest that although alpha diversity may modulate specific microbial taxa, its broader role in shaping spatial patterning of RDs remains limited and warrants further investigation. Using monitoring records from the Shanghai Environmental Data portal (https://data.sh.gov.cn/view/data-resource/index.html), a Mantel test revealed no significant correlation (P > 0.05) between representative VOC concentrations (toluene and isoprene) and either AMTI microbial diversity or RD prevalences (Fig. S5). We caution that these null results may reflect limitations in spatial data resolution rather than the absence of biological relationships. Specifically, the publicly available VOC data are derived from nearby regional monitoring stations rather than direct, in-situ measurements within the specific neighborhoods, thereby lacking the micro-environmental precision required for fine-scale ecological analyses.

Contribution of tree families to overall community heterogeneity

Our data suggest that greater ornamental tree heterogeneity in old neighborhoods may benefit respiratory health. Thus, it is crucial to design neighborhood green spaces with diverse tree portfolios to maximize heterogeneity. To address this, we first analyzed the relative contribution of dominant tree family abundances during both warm and cold seasons. At sites sampled during the cold season, the families with the highest abundance contribution were Lauraceae (42.8%), followed by Oleaceae (29.5%) and Arecaceae (7.9%) (Fig. 5a). At sites sampled during the warm season, Lauraceae and Oleaceae were also the two most abundant families, accounting for 29.4 and 18.3%, respectively (Fig. 5b). We then examined the tree family contributions to the overall community heterogeneity. Remarkably, Arecaceae consistently emerged as the family that most significantly contributed to community heterogeneity in both seasons, with contribution ranging from 5.5% in the cold season to 7.1% in the warm season. NMDS analyses further supported these findings by illustrating that the presence of Arecaceae significantly increased β diversity among the tree families (Fig. 5c, d). The families with the next highest contributions, however, differed by season: in the cold season, Oleaceae contributed 4.7%, whereas in the warm season, Lauraceae contributed 4.9%.

We also used structural equation modeling to better understand how the effects of tree heterogeneity impact on microbial heterogeneity. These analyses showed that tree heterogeneity was positively and significantly correlated with the heterogeneity of the AMTI bacterial community (Fig. 6a), but did not correlate significantly with the heterogeneity of the AMTI fungal community. The positive correlation between tree and AMTI bacterial heterogeneity was partially mediated by the heterogeneity of both phyllosphere and soil bacterial communities. In contrast, the influence of tree heterogeneity on AMTI fungal heterogeneity was only significantly mediated by the phyllosphere fungal community heterogeneity. Tree heterogeneity was significantly higher at sites sampled in the warm season compared to those sampled during the cold season (Fig. 2a, b). Furthermore, tree heterogeneity correlated significantly with the dominant AMTI species during the cold season (Fig. 3a). Integrating these findings with the family-level contributions to tree heterogeneity (Fig. 5), we propose neighborhood greening design that combines the three most strongly contributing families in the cold season (Fig. 6b): Arecaceae (e.g., Trachycarpus fortune), Oleaceae (e.g., Osmanthus fragrans), and Magnoliaceae (e.g., Magnolia grandiflora Linn). Using six neighborhoods included in this study as examples, we achieved a tree heterogeneity of 0.661 (Fig. 1a, b) through the strategic mixing of these families. This tree community composition increased AMTI heterogeneity (high heterogeneity) group, thus potentially improving respiratory health among the residents. The “βdis model” is a design-oriented procedure based on the pairwise Bray–Curtis dissimilarity (Eq. (1) in the “Methods” section) to quantify the compositional variance between tree communities in neighboring neighborhoods. In the βdis model, the pairwise dissimilarities calculated using the Bray-Curtis distance are used to divide tree communities into βdis_high and βdis_low groups. This metric serves as the core variable for guiding vegetation configuration by weighting the contribution of different tree families to compositional differences.

Discussion

Tree community heterogeneity correlates with environmental microbial heterogeneity but does not affect ARG abundance

Our study revealed that the β-diversity of tree communities significantly contributed to shaping the spatial distribution of microbial communities in urban environments, including phyllosphere, soil, and AMTI. Tree families differed in their effects on regulating microbial communities. Among them, Arecaceae, Oleaceae, and Lauraceae consistently showed greater contributions to enhancing microbial heterogeneity. For example, species within the Arecaceae family, such as Trachycarpus fortunei, possess complex leaf morphologies, densely distributed stomata41, and emit VOCs that interact with microbial communities to create a diversity of microhabitats42. These characteristics may facilitate the colonization by diverse microbial populations, thereby promoting microbial spatial heterogeneity. In contrast, Oleaceae trees, such as Osmanthus fragrans, contribute to microbial diversity through their well-developed root systems and soil stabilization43, which supports a more functionally diverse soil microbiome. Lauraceae species, like Cinnamomum camphora, influence the composition of airborne microbial communities through the emission of specific volatile compounds, such as camphene and camphor44, which attract distinct microbial assemblages, increasing the ecological heterogeneity of airborne microbes.

While tree diversity is central in ecosystem regulation, its effects on ARGs are limited. The dissemination of ARGs is largely driven by anthropogenic pressures such as antibiotic pollution and agricultural runoff45. These pressures often override the effects of plant-driven heterogeneity, highlighting that microbial functional gene shifts extend beyond the influence of above-ground biodiversity46. Thus, though tree diversity stabilizes microbial communities, it has limited ability to modulate ARG dissemination. This suggests the need for integrating ecological and management approaches to address ARG spread effectively.

AMTI β-heterogeneity shapes the spatial patterning of respiratory diseases

Our study reveals that RDs exhibit homogenization spatial patterning characteristics within the AMTI bacterial homogenization group. This may arise as greater tree heterogeneity leads to a higher frequency of dominant taxa in the AMTI (Fig. 3a), which could occupy resources and reduce the competitive advantages of pathogenic microorganisms, thereby limiting their establishment and spread47. Consistent with this interpretation, our previous AMTI study identified potential RD-associated microbes, e.g., Streptococcus anginosus, Actinomyces graevenitzii, whose relative abundances significantly associated with prevalences of rhinitis and rhinoconjunctivitis1. These results indicated that potential RD-associated taxa likely influence the spatial patterning of RD. In addition, the mobility of individual microorganisms, including those pathogens, is largely shaped by the spatial heterogeneity of microbial communities, which is structured by local barriers imposed by the distinct ecological features of these communities. This implies that microbial diversity may function as an ecological buffer in urban public health, reducing the risk of diseases associated with pathogen transmission. This phenomenon aligns with the “dilution effect” hypothesis, which suggests that greater environmental microbial diversity reduces the niche dominance of individual pathogens, thereby hindering their ability to colonize and proliferate48,49. While disease occurrence may be higher in some neighborhoods, its spread is likely limited, as the environmental microbiota associated with it may be spatially isolated. In this context, we argue that higher heterogeneity in the βdis_high group is not indicative of elevated health risk but rather reflects dispersal limitations. Such limitation in microbial dispersal can prevent broader spread, thereby reducing the risk of large-scale disease outbreaks.

Moreover, β-diversity in soil microbial communities also showed a significant association with human respiratory health. Soil microbes are spatially heterogeneous, which may influence the composition of airborne microbial assemblages50, and in turn affect microbial exposure in the human respiratory tract51. Thus, urban greening strategies that target vegetation composition and land-use configurations that optimize microbial heterogeneity has a potential to reduce exposure to pathogenic microbes. Such insights could inform public health interventions and contribute to more effective disease prevention strategies in urban settings.

Guiding implications of the β-diversity hypothesis for urban landscaping and greening design

The β-diversity hypothesis that we propose provides a novel theoretical framework for urban ecosystem design. It suggests that β-heterogeneity among tree communities not only supports the stability of microbial communities and promotes ecosystem functioning, but also indirectly improves human well-being by altering microbial distribution. This hypothesis has important practical implications for urban greening strategies. As shown in Fig. 5, our results revealed that different tree families contribute significantly and differentially to microbial β-diversity. Therefore, in future urban greening projects, it is recommended to prioritize the selection and interplanting of tree species that strongly contribute to high microbial β-diversity, in order to maximize microbial community heterogeneity at the landscape scale. In practice, tree β-diversity provides a practical metric to compare alternative planting schemes and to evaluate the effectiveness of maintenance actions (e.g., pruning, replanting). By focusing on β-diversity, urban green spaces can be designed to maximize microbial heterogeneity, enhancing ecosystem services like disease isolation and pathogen control. For example, in temperate or subtropical urban regions, strategic combinations of tree families such as Arecaceae, Oleaceae, and Lauraceae should be considered, as they significantly increased AMTI β-diversity. To achieve this, urban greening managers may consider prioritizing vegetation heterogeneity regulation, as diverse plant composition can enhance microbial isolation and constrain the transmission potential of harmful microbiota. Our βdis model provides a validated framework for optimizing planting schemes (with a target βdis > 0.661), offering an actionable protocol to test the beta-diversity hypothesis in urban health management. In practical urban planning, it is advised to incorporate a broad taxonomic plant diversity, and avoid large-scale monocultures. In addition to traditional greening methods, integrating vertical gardens and mixed-species systems of trees, shrubs, and herbaceous plants can further increase vegetation complexity and subsequently spatial microbial community heterogeneity52.

The biodiversity hypothesis proposed by Hanski, von Hertzen et al.1 primarily emphasizes how high species richness can weaken the transmission of pathogens by diversifying ecological networks. However, this model does not fully account for the spatial patterning or microbial community dynamics, potentially limiting its applicability in urban settings. We propose a β-diversity hypothesis that builds on recent advances in microbial ecology, offering a more actionable framework for guiding urban green space planning. Although the results in this study support the β-diversity hypothesis, the underlying mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated. Future research could integrate meta-omic approaches to explore how different tree species influence microbial functional potential and metabolic pathways, ultimately revealing plant-microbe co-succession dynamics. Additionally, long-term monitoring of urban ecosystems should be prioritized to assess how changes in vegetation structure may dynamically affect microbial community composition and function. Such efforts would contribute to the development of more precise and sustainable planting strategies, aligning ecological principles with urban health outcomes.

Building on the biodiversity hypothesis of Hanski, von Hertzen et al.1, our study proposes and validates a hypothesis base on β-diversity through measurements performed in urban neighborhoods in Shanghai. Results indicated that greater tree heterogeneity enhanced AMTI microbial β-diversity, particularly in old neighborhoods and this effect was consistent across seasons. Tree heterogeneity further modulated AMTI composition by influencing microbial distribution in the phyllosphere and soil. However, while tree heterogeneity enhanced microbial heterogeneity, it did not affect the abundance or distribution of ARGs, suggesting limited influence at the functional level. Notably, homogenization of the AMTI microbiome was linked to a more homogeneous spatial patterning of RDs, with little effect on the major RD (e.g., asthma) prevalences except for shortness of breath and wheeze. We further refined the urban tree βdis model and identified the threshold value (0.661). Incorporating phylogenetically diverse tree families, such as Arecaceae, Oleaceae, and Magnoliaceae, at or above the threshold provides practical planting templates that promote AMTI β-diversity. Conceptually, our β-diversity hypothesis bridges urban active rewilding53 and the One Health concepts54. Active rewilding reintroduces lost species in urban green spaces, while One Health highlights the health services provided by ecosystems. Central to our hypothesis is compositional heterogeneity, rather than species richness alone, which highlights the “microbial quarantine effect”. This effect constrains pathogens within distinct plant communities, thereby benefits human health. By translating β-diversity from an ecological metric into a health intervention, the beta-diversity hypothesis newly connects rewilding actions with One Health outcomes. In conclusions, we advocate for urban green space design strategies that prioritize structural and taxonomic heterogeneity through mixed planting configurations to enhance ecosystem functionality and public health in urban environments.

Methods

Site description and vegetation survey

We selected 72 neighborhoods for sampling in Shanghai, China (30.78°N, 121.53°E) (Fig. S6). These sites were categorized into two groups based on the age of construction: “young” (0–10 years, n = 32) and “old” (22–85 years, n = 40). These neighborhoods were distributed in Shanghai and five surrounding administrative districts, including Qingpu, Songjiang, Minhang, Fengxian, and Pudong. Each administrative district included both old and young neighborhoods. These 72 neighborhoods included 42 urban and 30 peri-urban neighborhoods, selected to span both sides of the Huangpu River and an urban-to-peri-urban gradient across districts. With ≥1.5 km spacing, we ensured replicated coverage of key residential neighborhood types (old vs. young; ≥5 neighborhoods per stratum). The geographic span of the study covered approximately 93.4 km east-west and 49.8 km north-south distance, representing Shanghai’s major environmental and management gradients. Each selected neighborhood contained a garden-type green space with an area ranging from 0.08 to 0.5 hectares, representing the typical size spectrum of neighborhood green spaces in Shanghai. Sampling was conducted at the end of August 2021 (warm season) and at the end of December 2021 (cold season). The average temperatures during these periods were 28.4 ± 4.4 °C and 10.1 ± 3.3 °C, respectively. Sampling was conducted outside of peak pollen-producing seasons (spring and autumn), and in neighborhoods distant from major roads and industrial sources, thus minimizing potential confounding from industrial pollutants. During each sampling event, phyllosphere, soil, and AMTI samples were collected within three days to minimize temporal variability. For each location, a square area of 1000 m² was defined, centered on the deployment point of an AMTI sampler (VenTube, Fig. S7), extending 20 m to the left and right, and 25 m forward. Within this area, we estimated the abundance and diversity indices for tree communities in mothur (v1.48.5). Observed richness (Sobs), Shannon diversity (H=−∑Pi×lnPi), and Shannon’s evenness (E=H/lnSobs), with Pi being the proportion of species i in the total community55.

Phyllosphere, soil, and AMTI microbial sampling

Phyllosphere microbial samples were collected from the camphor tree (Cinnamomum camphora). This species was selected for its evergreen, broad-leaved nature and its common presence in urban green spaces across Shanghai, facilitating spatially consistent sampling. Because our protocol targets leaf-surface dust as a proxy for locally deposited airborne microbiota, C. camphora leaves were used as a standardized representative sample across neighborhoods, rather than to characterize host-specific endophytic or tightly host-associated phyllosphere communities. Phyllosphere samples were collected from camphor trees to control for host effects56. However, tree families within 1000 m2 study area were surveyed to assess how neighborhood-scale vegetation heterogeneity influences microbial deposition. At each site, 20 peripheral sun leaves were collected. Among them, half of the leaves were analyzed for physicochemical properties, and half were used for DNA extraction55. AMTI samples were collected using the VenTube sampler40. To minimize sampling height bias, the device was mounted approximately 3 m above ground, with the intake on the outdoor side and the filter and exhaust on the indoor side of a corridor. AMTI sampling lasted for 7 days, after which the filter was retrieved for DNA extraction36. At each sampling sites, three soil subsamples (0–10 cm depth) were collected beneath the sampling camphor tree, at locations 1–2 m away from the trunk, using a 2.5 cm-diameter corer. These subsamples were pooled to form one composite sample per site. All tools were disinfected with 70% ethanol to prevent cross-contamination. All samples were stored at −80 °C until DNA extraction. We sampled phyllosphere and soil samples in 72 neighborhood gardens in two seasons, including 32 young neighborhoods and 40 old neighborhoods. However, we obtained 67 AMTI samples in the warm season and 71 in the cold season. The missing AMTI samples were a result of stolen of VenTube samplers, which was beyond our control. Although the number of samples is slightly uneven across seasons, the loss within the “treatment” category (neighborhood ages) is minimal. Specifically, the difference in sample numbers between the two seasons represents only a 5.6% variation. Moreover, the sample distribution across young and old neighborhoods showed a 10.95% variation between the two groups, further ensuring that the data provides a robust comparison across both seasons and neighborhood types. Due to the loss of some AMTI sampling devices, we ultimately collected 67 and 71 AMTI samples in the warm and cold seasons, respectively. To ensure data consistency across all three compartments, we excluded phyllosphere and soil samples whenever corresponding AMTI data were missing. Consequently, in the warm season, a total of 201 bacterial and 201 fungal communities were analyzed, with 67 each originating from the phyllosphere, AMTI, and soil. In the cold season, a total of 210 bacterial communities were analyzed, consisting of 70 from the phyllosphere, 70 from the AMTI, and 70 from the soil. For fungal communities, 213 samples were analyzed, including 71 from the phyllosphere, 71 from the AMTI, and 71 from the soil.

DNA extraction, metabarcode amplification, and sequence analysis

Microbial DNA was extracted within one week after sampling. Specifically, DNA from the phyllosphere samples was extracted using the FastDNA® Spin Kit (MP Biomedicals, USA), whereas the AMTI and soil microbial DNA were extracted using the E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, USA; D5625-01). All extraction steps were performed strictly following the manufacturer’s protocols37. DNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and DNA was stored at −80 °C for subsequent use. The V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the primers 515 F (5′-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′) and 806 R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′), whereas the fungal ITS region was amplified using the forward primer ITS7 (5′-GTGARTCATCGAATCTTTG-3′) and the reverse primer ITS4 (5′-TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC-3′)57,58. All primers used in this study were synthesized by Paisennuo Biotech (Shanghai, China). Negative controls were included in all PCR runs to ensure the absence of potential contamination. A positive control (Cupriavidus necator JMP134, DSM 4058)59,60 was included in each PCR to ensure the quality of the analysis. Amplicons were sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina, USA). The resulting paired-end sequences (.fastq files) were deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive under specific project accessions. The phyllosphere bacterial and fungal datasets from the warm season were assigned PRJNA875601 and PRJNA875603, respectively; those from the cold season were PRJNA876035 and PRJNA876041. The AMTI bacterial and fungal datasets were assigned PRJNA932577 and PRJNA932694, respectively. Finally, the soil bacterial and fungal datasets were assigned PRJNA1105401 and PRJNA1145862, respectively.

Metabarcode sequence data were analyzed using QIIME2 (v2024.5)61 following a standardized pipeline. Initially, barcode and primer sequences were trimmed and removed from the raw reads. Then, the sequences were processed through the DADA2 plugin (v1.24.0) to denoise the data and infer amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) at a 100% similarity threshold. Taxonomic classification was carried out by aligning the bacterial and fungal ASVs against the RDP database (v11.4)62 and the UNITE database (v9)63, respectively. Any non-target sequences, including those corresponding to mitochondria, chloroplasts, and non-target eukaryotes (e.g., plants, algae, and metazoans) were filtered out. In addition, to reduce the impact of sequencing and amplification noise, ASVs with total read counts less than 10 across the entire dataset were excluded. After quality filtering and standardization, the final ASV datasets included 78,074 bacterial and 23,636 fungal ASVs.

Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) of ARGs

Eleven ARGs associated with RDs were quantified in all AMTI and soil samples by quantitative PCR (qPCR) using a QuantStudio 3 system (Thermo Fisher, USA). The targeted ARG panel was defined a priori based on literature and our prior work to capture resistance determinants most relevant to respiratory-disease-related contexts and to antibiotics commonly used in respiratory infection management, while spanning major resistance mechanisms (drug inactivation, target modification/protection, and efflux) and major antibiotic classes. These ARGs were specifically selected because they are detectable in our samples using standard qPCR, ensuring their relevance to our study. Accordingly, we selected genes conferring resistance to β-lactams (blaTEM, mecA), cephamycins (fox5), macrolides (ermB), quinolones (qnrA), tetracyclines (tetX), sulfonamides (sul2), aminoglycosides/streptothricin-related resistance (aadA1, sat4), glycopeptides (vanA), and a multidrug efflux component (mexD)35,40. The targeted ARGs included aadA1, blaTEM, fox5, mecA, mexD, qnrA, ermB, sul2, tetX, vanA, and sat4. In addition, the abundances of 16S rRNA and the mobile genetic element (MGE) intI1 were also assessed. To generate standard curves, target genes were amplified and cloned into TA cloning vectors using a restriction-free subcloning. Plasmids with the target genes were extracted using the QIAprep SpinMiniprep kit (QIAGEN, Germany). The concentrations of the plasmid standards were measured using a NanoDrop ND-2000 spectrophotometer (ng/μL), and DNA copy numbers estimated accordingly (copies/μL). Each qPCR reaction (20 μL) was set up following the protocol40 and performed in triplicate. Negative controls were included for each gene, in which sterile water was used in place of DNA. The abundance of ARGs and MGE was calculated based on standard curves. The relative abundances of ARGs or MGE were determined as gene copy number per 16S rRNA copy.

Human respiratory disease prevalence investigation

In each old neighborhood, 30 ± 5 volunteers were recruited to complete a standardized questionnaire, aimed at assessing the prevalence of RDs. Due to the short residency of residents in the young neighborhoods, we did not investigated the prevalence of dieases in the young ones. Participants were selected with equal numbers of males and females, aged 40–80 years, who had lived in the neighborhood for more than 10 years and had no other underlying diseases. Based on the estimated population size in roughly 8 residential buildings within the 1000 m² sampling frame per neighborhood and city-level demographics (age 40–80) (https://www.citypopulation.de/en/china/prov/admin/31__shanghai/) together with a conservative proxy for residential stability (≥10 years) (https://tjj.sh.gov.cn/tjnj/rktjnj2020e.htm), the 30 ± 5 respondents per neighborhood represent >31% of the estimated eligible residents in the recruitment catchment. Before participation, all volunteers were reviewed by a licensed physician (a manuscript co-author), who was not involved in the selection of the study population, to verify the accuracy of self-reported respiratory symptoms based on their medical history. The questionnaire used was adapted from the study by Fu et al.64 and modified to suit the local population. A total of 1026 valid questionnaires were obtained after validation. The following respiratory and allergic disorders were assessed: asthma (ICD code: J45.0), wheezing (R06.2), shortness of breath (R06.02), rhinitis (J31.0), and rhinoconjunctivitis (J30.3). Each survey captured participants’ health status over the past 12 months. The prevalence of RDs was calculated as the proportion of individuals with a confirmed diagnosis or self-reported symptoms in relation to the total number of individuals in the surveyed community, and expressed as percentages. Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University (approval No. B20230221I). Informed written consent was obtained from all participants, with all documentation archived at the university. Participants received a small household gift as compensation for their participation.

Based on the grouping criteria described above, we evaluated the variation in microbial heterogeneity across the tree heterogeneity groups. Pairwise dissimilarities were used to define the βdis_high and βdis_low groups, and PERMDISP was used for significance testing to ensure robust statistical inference. In addition, we examined the frequency of occurrence of the four most abundant AMTI species across the tree heterogeneity groups, applying a 5% relative abundance threshold to define presence. Specifically, a species was considered present in a sample if its relative abundance exceeded 5%. The frequency was then calculated as the proportion of samples within each group (βdis_high vs. βdis_low) where the species met this threshold. This approach is standard in microbial ecology to distinguish persistent and functionally relevant taxa from rare or transient ones. Using the STAMP software (v2.1.3)69, we compared the abundance of ARGs in AMTI between tree heterogeneity groups, with 95% confidence intervals and P-values adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg correction. To explore the spatial patterning of RDs in relation to bacterial β-heterogeneity in the AMTI compartment, we employed non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) visualization and compared the coefficients of variation across disease categories. We also used the “rarefy” function in the “vegan” package to standardize the sequence data across all samples by subsampling to the smallest sample size and then calculated bacterial and fungal α diversity indices (richness, diversity, and evenness). Associations between α-diversity metrics, the relative abundance of potential RD-associated genera, and RD prevalence were assessed using correlation analyses. To quantify the abundance contribution of different tree families to the overall tree communities, we constructed a rose diagram representing their relative abundances. The tree community composition was visualized using NMDS. Finally, we employed structural equation modeling (SEM) using the lavaan package (v0.6–20) to assess the relationships among tree heterogeneity, phyllosphere microbial heterogeneity, soil microbial heterogeneity, and AMTI heterogeneity. We used Benjamini–Hochberg for P-value adjustment in the heatmap. To evaluate structural uncertainty, we compared the final model to a set of plausible alternatives, including (i) models excluding non-significant paths, (ii) models allowing direct paths from tree βdis to AMTI bacterial and fungal βdis. We report the model comparison criteria (CFI and RMSEA) and show that the selected model provides the best balance of fit and parsimony (Fig. S9). For interpretation, we computed standardized direct, indirect, and total effects of the model (Table S1).
