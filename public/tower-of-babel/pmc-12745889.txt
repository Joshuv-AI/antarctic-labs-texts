Stereotypical diagnostic bias for alcohol‐induced ketoacidosis could lead to death: Normal blood sugar levels in SGLT2 blocker‐induced severe diabetic ketoacidosis
Authors: Shun Kudo, Sho Endo, Yurina Nanahara, Takao Katahira, Masayoshi Makio, Mikiko Yamauchi, Satoyuki Ogino, Akihiro Koreki, Takuto Ishida, Hiroyuki Uchida, Masaru Mimura, Michitaka Funayama, Yasushi Nemoto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKetoacidosis, a life‐threatening disease, can be caused by diabetes, alcohol consumption, and severe malnutrition, which are not uncommon in psychiatry. Diabetic and alcoholic ketoacidosis (AKA) are particularly frequent, and differentiating between these two is essential in terms of the substantially different treatment interventions. In recent years, the advent of sodium‐glucose co‐transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors has made this distinction more difficult, mainly due to the increase in euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis (euDKA)—ketoacidosis in normal or near‐normal blood sugar levels—in the presence of SGLT2 inhibitors.Case PresentationWe describe a 52‐year‐old man's case of severe euDKA caused by an SGLT2 inhibitor with alcohol use disorder. We initially suspected mainly AKA due to his mild disturbance of consciousness, habitual high intake of alcohol, and almost normal blood glucose level (142 mg/dL). We administered supplemental fluids and vitamin B complex instead of insulin, which is needed for euDKA, but his disorientation and metabolic acidosis worsened. Upon recognizing his inadequate response to fluid replacement and considering his history of SGLT2 inhibitor use, we identified euDKA as the primary pathology. Implementing continuous insulin therapy and tight glycemic control resulted in a rapid improvement in the patient's condition.ConclusionEven with normal or near‐normal blood sugar levels in a patient with suspected AKA, missing the possibility of euDKA can be fatal. Therefore, psychiatrists should remain vigilant for euDKA, especially in patients using SGLT2 inhibitors, by conducting frequent blood gas examinations.

BACKGROUND

Ketoacidosis, characterized by an excessive increase of ketone bodies in the blood, requires urgent treatment because it can induce fatal conditions such as respiratory failure, coma, and severe arrhythmia including ventricular tachycardia or ventricular fibrillation. 1 , 2 , 3 Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) is one of the most critical complications in diabetic patients, in which decreased insulin action results in impaired sugar utilization by cells and excessive production of ketone bodies in the blood. Alcoholic ketoacidosis (AKA) is more common in patients with heavy alcohol drinkers and is caused by an increase in ketone bodies by changing several metabolic processes, such as impaired gluconeogenesis and increased fat breakdown. 1 While both have a mortality rate of approximately 1%, 4 , 5 they require divergent and sometimes opposite treatments. In DKA, prompt insulin administration is essential, whereas in AKA, glucose replacement with vitamin B1 is required to counter the often‐associated hypoglycemia, although both types of ketoacidosis need supplemental fluids. 1

Patients with psychiatric disorders have two to three times the risk of developing diabetes due to multiple factors, including weight gain, lifestyle disturbances, and side effects of psychotropic drugs, 6 , 7 while they sometimes have high levels of alcohol consumption. 8 Given these facts, both DKA and AKA could be more commonly encountered by psychiatrists in clinical practice than they expect. Since it is challenging to differentiate DKA and AKA based on appearance and symptoms, the differentiation had typically been made using blood glucose levels; DKA shows evident hyperglycemia while AKA demonstrates normal to low blood glucose levels. 6 , 7

However, in the case of unusual DKA with normal blood glucose levels, called euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis (euDKA), it is impossible to differentiate them using blood glucose levels. The pathophysiology and treatment of euDKA—ketoacidosis with normal or near‐normal blood sugar levels—are no different from that of regular DKA, except that blood glucose levels are normal. Although the frequency of euglycemic DKA used to be rare, 9 , 10 it has dramatically increased in recent years. One cause of this rise is the increased sodium‐glucose co‐transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitor prescriptions for diabetes treatment, 9 which causes euDKA more frequently than the other diabetes medications. 11 , 12 This drug was approved globally in 2010s as the treatment of diabetes. It was innovative in that this drug excretes glucose into the urine without the need for insulin secretion 13 and has a variety of benefits, including improving blood glucose levels, reducing obesity, and decreasing cardiovascular events, 13 which improves the prognosis for heart failure and chronic renal failure. 14 Even in the field of psychiatry, patients with psychiatric disorders often have obesity‐related diabetes, leading to rising SGLT2 inhibitor use among diabetes medications. 15

Until now, several cases have been reported in which the differentiation between DKA including SGLT2 inhibitor‐related euDKA and AKA was difficult. 16 , 17 , 18 However, to the best of our knowledge, there has been no report on this topic has been documented in the field of psychiatry, despite the possibility that such patients may be more common in this field than in other departments due to lifestyle disturbances and side effects of psychotropic drugs, as mentioned above. Furthermore, within the current psychiatric healthcare system, such cases may be overlooked due to deficiencies in testing for physical illnesses and the absence of diabetes specialists. Psychiatrists are typically unfamiliar with diagnosing and treating ketoacidosis, so this report may help them deepen their understanding.

Here, we report a case that we initially considered AKA as the main pathophysiology because the patient did not have high blood glucose levels and consumed a large amount of alcohol. However, we revised the diagnosis to the concurrent of DKA based on the inadequate response to fluid replacement and marked effects of subsequently administered insulin.

CASE PRESENTATION

The patient was a 52‐year‐old unmarried man who lived alone. He stood at 177 cm tall, weighed 68 kg, had a body mass index (BMI) of 21.7, and worked in personal computer software development. He was diagnosed with Type 2 diabetes at age 42 and has been taking teneligliptin 20 mg, a DPP‐4 inhibitor, for 10 years, and canagliflozin 100 mg, an SGLT2 inhibitor, for 3 years. He has drunk approximately 2000–3000 mL of “Chu‐hai” (Japanese fruit cocktail) every day since age 20, equivalent to 180–270 g of pure alcohol per day, which corresponds to very high‐risk levels for alcohol use disorder (>100 g/day). 19 On weekdays, he drinks from after work, and on weekends, from noon to bedtime. Despite attempting to quit drinking due to liver dysfunction, he resumed drinking whenever his insomnia recurred. He has no history of psychiatric consultation.

On the morning of the day before admission, his appetite decreased, and he stopped drinking alcohol. On the same night, he started vomiting and having trouble in walking. With no improvement the following day, he visited a local physician and underwent a blood test. As a result, metabolic acidosis was observed, and he was referred to our hospital's psychiatry department during the night on the same day due to the possibility of an alcohol‐related condition. During our initial examination, he was able to speak but exhibited altered vital signs, including slight fever, tachycardia, hypotension, and hyperpnea, with body temperature of 37.8°C, a heart rate of 130/min, blood pressure of 92/60 mmHg, and respiratory rate of 28/min. He was unable to walk independently, but no neurological abnormalities were evident on neurological examination in the supine position. We suspected an infectious complication due to an elevated white blood cell count and C‐reactive protein levels in his blood, but we were unable to find an obvious source of infection by a physical examination and a computed tomography (CT) scan covering the chest to the pelvic area. Considering the possibility of septic shock, we initiated the administration of meropenem as a broad‐spectrum antibiotic. Blood cultures and urine cultures were obtained before starting antibiotics, but the results obtained later were negative. The head magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan revealed no abnormalities. Arterial blood gases and urinary test indicated metabolic ketoacidosis with pH 7.10, PaCO2 9.5 mmHg, HCO3 − 2.9 mmol/L, anion gap 25.3 mmol/L, and urinary ketones at a level of 3+. His HbA1c level of 6.2% and blood glucose level of 142 mg/dL were within almost normal ranges.

At this point, our tentative diagnosis was AKA, attributed to his habitual high alcohol intake, and we commenced treatment accordingly. Additionally, we contemplated the potential complication of euDKA considering his SGLT2 inhibitor use. However, we refrained from initiating measures to treat euDKA due to his blood glucose levels that reflect those of well‐managed diabetes patients. Additionally, administering insulin for the treatment of euDKA could have precipitated the risk of hypoglycemia associated with AKA. After admission, we administered supplemental fluids and vitamin B complex for treatment of AKA and prevention for Wernicke's encephalopathy that is associated with alcohol dependency. However, on the morning of the second hospital day, 12 h after admission, he became unable to communicate, and his disturbance of consciousness worsened in the absence of any conditions other than the ketoacidosis. Blood tests showed that his blood glucose level was only slightly elevated at 173 mg/dL, but his metabolic acidosis worsened to a critical level that could potentially result in death as his arterial blood gas readings: pH 7.10, PaCO2 8.7 mmHg, HCO3 − 2.7 mmol/L, and anion gap 32.3 mmol/L. Despite a blood pressure improvement (116/89 mmHg), he remained in critical condition with altered vital signs, including a heart rate of 144/min and a respiratory rate of 26/min.

Given the absence of response to supplemental fluids and the patient's history of SGLT2 inhibitor use, we revised our diagnosis from AKA to euDKA as the primary pathology. We started administering regular short‐acting insulin at a rate of one unit per hour and controlling blood sugar levels in the range of 150–250 mg/dL. On the third hospital day, a day after initiating continuous insulin administration, he regained consciousness and exhibited normal vital signs, including blood pressure, pulse rate, and respiratory rate. Furthermore, metabolic acidosis improved markedly with arterial blood gases of pH 7.36, PaCO2 25.8 mmHg, HCO3 − 14.3 mmol/L, and anion gap 20.3, if not perfect. On the sixth hospital day, continuous insulin administration was discontinued, and there was no recurrence of impaired consciousness or metabolic acidosis. In the context of psychiatry, we diagnosed him with alcohol use disorder according to the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems 10th Revision, taking into account a strong desire to consume alcohol, difficulties in controlling alcohol intake levels, and persisting with alcohol use despite liver dysfunction. We provided counseling on his lifestyle, and he showed a certain understanding of the need for sobriety.

Regarding diabetes, his specific diagnosis led to the identification of slowly progressive Type 1 diabetes mellitus (SPIDDM) or latent autoimmune diabetes in adults (LADA), which requires insulin therapy but can sometimes be misdiagnosed as Type 2 diabetes due to its gradual decline in insulin secretion. 20 This diagnosis was supported by markedly elevated levels of antibodies against glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD) in his blood (>2000 U/mL), which target insulin‐producing beta cells within the Islets of Langerhans in the pancreas, although his serum insulin levels were within the normal limit.

He was discharged on the 23rd hospital day without complications. After discharge, he has been attending regular outpatient appointments at our hospital's diabetes clinic. The treatment plan involves controlling his diabetes condition with other types of oral medications, without using SGLT2 inhibitors.

DISCUSSION

This case underscored the significant challenges in diagnosing between alcoholic and euDKA in psychiatry clinical settings. It could have been fatal if the treatment had continued under the misdiagnosis of AKA alone without considering the concurrent presence of euDKA. In the early stages of treatment, it was difficult to determine whether euDKA or AKA was the primary pathology. However, such delayed diagnoses can frequently occur in clinical practice. This patient developed severe euDKA primarily due to the use of an SGLT2 inhibitor, along with multiple factors related to the development of DKA, including infection, dehydration due to vomiting, and heavy alcohol consumption. 21 , 22

We summarized the clinical characteristics of AKA and euDKA (Table 1). One of the reasons for the difficulty in differentiating AKA and euDKA is that the history, symptoms, and inspection results are almost entirely consistent with both conditions, as demonstrated in this case. Regarding the medical history, if the patient presenting with ketoacidosis has both diabetes and a history of heavy alcohol drinking, the differential diagnosis becomes more difficult. 16 In particular, heavy alcohol consumption contributes to dehydration and can increase the risk of developing both AKA and DKA. 21 In terms of symptoms, vomiting, anorexia, gait disturbance, and impaired consciousness are common in both diseases. 23 , 24 Furthermore, as in this case, the fact that DKA and AKA often co‐occur also makes the differential diagnosis difficult. 18 Laboratory tests used to differentiate between DKA and AKA often prove unhelpful in acute clinical situations. These tests include measuring blood glucose levels, blood ketone ratio, and blood lactate/pyruvate ratio. 23 , 25 , 26 However, the use of the last two indicators is limited by their availability in respective facilities, as well as lengthy wait times for results from external labs when the measurement is outsourced. As a result, many clinicians only use blood glucose levels for differential diagnosis. Unfortunately, this single indicator can also be useless in cases like this one, where the initial test showed only 142 mg/dL, which aligns more with AKA than DKA.

A crucial clue for an accurate diagnosis is the medication of an SGLT2 inhibitor. As in this report, a serious side effect of SGLT2 inhibitors is the induction of DKA. While blood glucose levels decrease due to an SGLT2 inhibitor and insulin levels indirectly decrease, blood glucagon levels increase, which are responsible for stimulating gluconeogenesis and lipolysis. Under these circumstances, triggers such as dehydration, infection, and extremely low carbohydrate intake can rapidly increase blood ketones due to rapid lipolysis, raising the risk of developing DKA. 22

Based on a cohort study, DKA occurred in 0.43% of individuals with Type 2 diabetes who were using oral SGLT2 inhibitors. 27 The risk of developing DKA with SGLT2 inhibitors is two to three times higher compared to other diabetes medications. 11 , 12 Interestingly, a recent study demonstrates that 71% of DKA cases linked to SGLT2 inhibitors were classified as euDKA. 28 In response to this, both the American Association of Clinical Endocrinologists and the American College of Endocrinology have released statements that address the risk of DKA associated with SGLT2 inhibitor use. 29

In summary, we described the difficulty in differentiating euDKA and AKA and the increasing incidence of euDKA with SGLT2 inhibitor medication, scenarios that clinical psychiatrists might encounter. Although it may be difficult for psychiatrists to make an exact diagnosis of euDKA or AKA, it can lead to death if the treatment is continued with the misdiagnosis between these two conditions. It is therefore advisable to always consider the possibility of both conditions by carefully taking medical histories, including detailed medication information, performing physical examinations, and conducting inspections such as blood and urine tests. In addition, when examining such patients with ketoacidosis in a psychiatric ward, it is desirable to routinely and frequently assess blood gases to evaluate the accuracy of diagnosis and the degree of acidosis. In clinical scenarios in which AKA is suspected but euDKA remains within the differential diagnosis, it is advisable to initiate early management that incorporates elements of standard euDKA therapy.

CONCLUSION

It is crucial to recognize that in patients using SGLT2 inhibitors, suspicion of euDKA should arise even when blood sugar levels are within the normal range or just above the normal limit. It is important not to attribute such cases solely to AKA due to episodes of excessive alcohol consumption. When differentiation is difficult, it is advisable to initiate treatment covering both conditions and perform frequent blood gas tests. Instances of euDKA, as exemplified by this case, will very likely become more prevalent, necessitating heightened awareness among general psychiatrists.
