Deciphering the dynamic genetic architecture of adult-plant salinity tolerance in bread wheat using multi-model GWAS and genomic prediction
Authors: Sayed Mujtaba Sadaat, Mohammadreza Bihamta, Valiollah Mohammadi, Mehrdad Mahlooji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSoil salinity is a major constraint to wheat productivity, necessitating the identification of robust genetic loci for breeding. This study aims to dissect the genetic architecture of adult-plant salinity tolerance in a diverse panel of 298 Iranian wheat accessions. A multi-model GWAS framework (MLM, FarmCPU, and BLINK) was deployed under moderate (6 dS/m) and severe (12 dS/m) salinity stress. High-confidence consensus marker-trait associations (MTAs) were validated through comparative synteny analysis with the rice genome. Furthermore, the genomic predictive ability of gBLUP was evaluated across stress regimes.ResultsWe identified 45 consensus MTAs, revealing a dynamic "Genetic Handoff": constitutive growth-promoting loci predominated under moderate stress, while adaptive ion-homeostasis and "escape" loci emerged under severe stress. A major biomass-associated locus on chromosome 5B (TraesCS5B02G000100) was identified as a strict 1:1 ortholog to rice Os12g0641400 (83.53% identity), indicating high evolutionary conservation of sucrose partitioning. Under severe stress, genomic prediction accuracies for K+/Na+ ratio surged to 0.65, although yield prediction declined due to non-additive effects.ConclusionOur findings advocate for a Haplotype-Informed Genomic Selection strategy, combining MAS for conserved high-effect loci like TaSUC4 with GS for polygenic backgrounds. This integrated approach provides a robust roadmap for developing salinity-resilient wheat cultivars.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09622-3.Keywords: Salinity tolerance, Multi-locus GWAS, FarmCPU, Genomic Selection, Triticum aestivum L., Ionic homeostasis

Introduction

Soil salinity is a catastrophic abiotic stress threatening global food security, currently affecting over 20% of irrigated agricultural lands worldwide [1, 2]. For bread wheat (Triticum aestivum L.), which provides approximately 20% of human caloric intake [3, 4], salinity stress poses a dual challenge: osmotic stress, which limits water uptake, and ionic toxicity (primarily Na+ accumulation), which disrupts metabolic processes [5, 6]. Consequently, breeding cultivars with robust salinity tolerance has shifted from a desirable goal to an urgent global imperative [7, 8].

However, genetic improvement for salinity tolerance has been historically impeded by two major bottlenecks. First, tolerance is a complex quantitative trait governed by an intricate network of physiological mechanisms—including Na+ exclusion, K+ retention, and osmotic adjustment—regulated by numerous genes with small-to-moderate effects [9–11]. Second, the majority of prior genetic studies have focused on the seedling stage under controlled environments [12, 13]. While valuable, these findings often fail to translate to adult-plant performance and final Yield per Hectare in field conditions, creating a "production environment gap" [14, 15]. Iranian wheat germplasm, comprising landraces and historical cultivars evolved in harsh, semi-arid climates, represents an underutilized reservoir of allelic diversity that could harbor novel tolerance mechanisms distinct from widely used sources [16, 17].

To dissect such complex traits, Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS) have become the standard approach [18, 19]. Nevertheless, traditional single-locus models, such as the Mixed Linear Model (MLM), often suffer from reduced statistical power due to stringent multiple-testing corrections (e.g., Bonferroni), potentially masking true associations (false negatives) [20, 21]. In contrast, advanced multi-locus models like FarmCPU (Fixed and random model Circulating Probability Unification) and BLINK (Bayesian-information and Linkage-disequilibrium Iteratively Nested Keyway) have demonstrated superior power and computational efficiency by iteratively refining the inclusion of covariates, effectively balancing the control of false positives and negatives [22–24]. Despite their advantages, few studies have applied a multi-model consensus approach to rigorously cross-validate QTLs associated with adult-plant salinity tolerance [23, 25].

Moreover, while GWAS effectively identifies major-effect loci, it often leaves the "missing heritability" of complex traits unaccounted for [26, 27]. Genomic Selection (GS) addresses this limitation by estimating Genomic Estimated Breeding Values (GEBVs) using genome-wide markers simultaneously [28, 29], capturing the cumulative effects of minor genes that GWAS may miss [28, 30]. The integration of GWAS to identify major biological mechanisms and GS to predict agronomic performance represents a comprehensive strategy for modern breeding but remains limited in salinity studies [31, 32].

While GWAS provides a powerful lens to identify candidate loci, the hexaploid nature of wheat and its incomplete functional annotation often necessitate evolutionary validation [33, 34]. Comparative genomics and synteny analysis with diploid models like rice (Oryza sativa) offer a robust framework to evaluate the functional conservation of identified candidates [35, 36]. By identifying orthologous relationships that have survived ~ 50 million years of divergence, researchers can distinguish between redundant gene expansions and non-redundant, evolutionary-constrained 'master rheostats' of stress tolerance [37, 38]. Integrating such evolutionary insights with GWAS and Genomic Selection (GS) remains a frontier in wheat precision breeding [39, 40].

In this study, we hypothesized that a diverse panel of Iranian wheat genotypes harbors unexploited adaptive alleles for salinity tolerance that can be unlocked using advanced statistical genomics. The primary objectives were to: (1) dissect the genetic architecture of 12 key agro-physiological traits under both moderate and high-salinity field conditions; (2) elucidate the "dynamic" shift in QTL expression patterns across stress intensities using a robust three-model GWAS framework (MLM, FarmCPU, BLINK); (3) identify stable QTL hotspots and candidate genes for marker-assisted selection; and (4) evaluate the potential of Genomic Selection to predict yield and physiological traits, thereby assessing the feasibility of genomic-assisted breeding for salinity tolerance.

Materials and methods

Plant germplasm and genetic diversity

The association mapping panel consisted of 298 bread wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) accessions, representing a comprehensive collection of Iranian germplasm. This diverse panel comprised 90 improved cultivars released between 1942 and 2014, alongside 208 pure line landraces collected from distinct agro-ecological zones across Iran (1939–1968). The germplasm was sourced from the University of Tehran Gene Bank. Prior to the field trials, all accessions underwent a cycle of purification and single-seed descent (SSD) under controlled conditions to strictly ensure genetic homogeneity and seed viability. Detailed passport data are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Experimental design and field management

Field trials were conducted over two consecutive cropping seasons (2023–2024 and 2024–2025) at the Kabootarabad Agricultural Research Station, Isfahan, Iran (32.99°N, 51.17°E; 1545 m elevation). The experimental soil was characterized as a clay-loam texture with a baseline pH of 7.8 and an initial electrical conductivity (ECe) of 2.5 dS m⁻1. To ensure optimal nutrient availability and eliminate confounding fertility effects, fertilization was precisely applied based on pre-sowing soil nutrient analysis. To effectively control spatial variability given the large population size, the experiments were arranged in an alpha-lattice design with two replications under two distinct environments. Each replication comprised 20 incomplete blocks, with 16 genotypes per block. Plots consisted of four 1.5-m rows spaced 25 cm apart. Standard agronomic practices for the region were applied to maintain optimal crop health. The two outer rows, along with the 20 cm ends of the central rows, were treated as borders and excluded from sampling.

Salinity treatments and irrigation regimes

To eliminate confounding factors during germination, all plots were irrigated with non-saline fresh water until the tillering stage (Zadoks scale Z21). Subsequently, differential irrigation treatments were initiated and maintained until physiological maturity:

Moderate Salinity: Irrigation with available well water (EC_ 6.0 dS m⁻1), representing a baseline "salt-prone" agricultural scenario typical of the region.

Moderate Salinity: Irrigation with available well water (EC_ 6.0 dS m⁻1), representing a baseline "salt-prone" agricultural scenario typical of the region.

Severe Salinity (Stress): Irrigation with saline water (EC_ 12.0 dS m−1), imposing severe osmotic and ionic stress.

Severe Salinity (Stress): Irrigation with saline water (EC_ 12.0 dS m−1), imposing severe osmotic and ionic stress.

Irrigation water electrical conductivity (EC) was monitored before each event using a calibrated portable meter (HANNA HI99301).

Phenotypic evaluation and data analysis

To capture the salinity response landscape, 12 key agro-physiological traits were prioritized, representing source-sink dynamics and ionic homeostasis. Agronomic performance was assessed by measuring Yield per Hectare (YieldH), Thousand Grain Weight (TGW) (adjusted to 13% moisture), Grains per Spike (GrainN.S), Spikes per Plant (SpikesN), and Above-ground Biomass (Biomass). Structural traits included Stem Height (StemH) and Flag Leaf Area (LeafA), while Harvest Index (HI) was derived as the Yield/Biomass ratio.

Physiological and ionic status were monitored at the anthesis stage. SPAD number (SPAD number (Chlorophyll content)) (SPAD) was recorded using a SPAD-502 m. For ionic profiling, flag leaf samples were acid-digested, and Sodium content (Na) and the K+/Na+ ratio (K.Na) were determined via flame photometry. Developmental plasticity was recorded as Physiological Maturity (PM).

Variance components were estimated using a hierarchical mixed-effects model. We calculated Best Linear Unbiased Predictors (BLUPs) for each genotype across years to serve as the phenotypic input for GWAS, thereby capturing stable genetic effects. Conversely, Best Linear Unbiased Estimates (BLUEs) were computed within each environment for trait correlation analysis to accurately reflect environment-specific phenotypic relationships.

Genotyping and quality control

We genotyped the panel using the genotyping-by-sequencing (GBS) method as previously described by Alipour et al. (2017) [41]. The initial 90 K SNP dataset underwent rigorous filtering. We removed markers with a minor allele frequency (MAF) below 5% or more than 10% missing data. This filtering process yielded a final set of 43,525 high-quality SNPs, which we used for all downstream analyses, including population structure, linkage disequilibrium, and association mapping.

Population structure and linkage disequilibrium

To mitigate spurious associations, we inferred population structure (Q matrix) using both principal component analysis (PCA) and a model-based approach in ADMIXTURE v1.3.0. The optimal number of subpopulations (K) was determined by identifying the value with the lowest cross-validation error. We calculated a pairwise kinship matrix (K matrix) using the VanRaden (2008) method in the R package GAPIT to account for the intricate web of relationships among genotypes [42].

Genome-wide linkage disequilibrium (LD) was estimated by calculating the squared allele frequency correlation (r2) for all pairs of syntenic markers [43]. We visualized the pattern of LD decay by plotting r2 against the physical distance (Mb) between markers and fitting a LOESS curve. The physical distance at which the mean r2 decayed to half its maximum value was defined as the extent of LD in this population and used to define the search window for candidate genes.

Genome-wide association analysis and multi-model consensus strategy

To dissect the genetic architecture of salinity tolerance and mitigate the inherent trade-off between model overfitting and false negatives, we employed a multi-model consensus strategy using the GAPIT3 R package. Three complementary algorithms with distinct statistical assumptions were utilized: (i) MLM (Mixed Linear Model), incorporating both population structure (Q) and kinship (K) to control for spurious associations; (ii) FarmCPU (Fixed and random model Circulating Probability Unification), which enhances statistical power by iteratively incorporating associated markers as fixed effects; and (iii) BLINK (Bayesian-information and Linkage-disequilibrium Iteratively Nested Keyway), selected for its computational efficiency and superior detection power in identifying QTNs in large datasets.

Definition of significant associations

To ensure robust detection given the marker density, a two-tier thresholding approach was adopted. First, a strict Bonferroni correction threshold (− log10​(P) ≥ 5.95; α = 0.05/n) was calculated to identify highly significant marker-trait associations (MTAs). Second, to avoid discarding rare alleles or minor effect loci potentially masked by strict correction, a suggestive threshold of − log10​(P) ≥ 4.0 was applied. To minimize false positives at this suggestive level, we strictly adhered to a consensus rule: loci were considered high-confidence candidates only if they were detected by at least two distinct algorithms or exhibited significant stability across different stress environments.

Candidate gene identification and functional annotation

For each significant marker-trait association (MTA), we searched for candidate genes within a genomic region defined by the calculated LD decay distance (± 100 kb) flanking the peak SNP. We extracted the sequences of these regions from the wheat reference genome (IWGSC RefSeq v2.1). Gene annotation was performed using the Ensembl Plants database. Given the more comprehensive annotation available for other model species, we conducted a Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis on the candidate gene list using their rice (Oryza sativa) orthologs via the g:Profiler web service [44]. This step aimed to uncover key biological pathways associated with salinity tolerance.

Genomic predictive ability and model cross-validation

To evaluate the predictive ability of the model of genomic selection (GS) for enhancing salinity resilience, we employed a Genomic Best Linear Unbiased Prediction (gBLUP) framework. The genomic relationship matrix (G-matrix) was computed using the VanRaden algorithm, accounting for the total additive genetic variance across the 298 wheat accessions. To ensure statistical stringency, predictive ability (rmp​) was estimated via a stratified five-fold cross-validation (CV) scheme with 100 independent random iterations. The population was partitioned into two distinct environmental regimes: Moderate Salinity (MS) and Severe Salinity (SS). In each iteration, the validation set (20%) was masked, and Genomic Estimated Breeding Values (GEBVs) were derived from the training set (80%). The rmp​ was defined as the Pearson correlation coefficient between GEBVs and the phenotypic Best Linear Unbiased Predictors (BLUPs).

Comparative genomics and synteny analysis

To validate the evolutionary stability of high-priority candidate genes identified via GWAS, a high-resolution comparative synteny analysis was conducted between T. aestivum (IWGSC RefSeq v2.1) and O. sativa (Japonica, IRGSP-1.0). Orthologous relationships were retrieved using the Ensembl Plants BioMart tool and confirmed via reciprocal BLASTp searches (E-value ≤ 10–10) [45]. Syntenic blocks were visualized using a dual-synteny plotting framework to evaluate collinearity between wheat homoeologous group 5 and rice chromosome 12. Orthology types were categorized into ortholog_one2one, one2many, and many2many to assess post-polyploidization gene retention or loss (fractionation) in the wheat lineage.

Severe salinity imposed a severe penalty on agronomic performance. Yield per Hectare experienced the most drastic reduction, declining by 59.7% compared to the moderate condition. Similarly, Biomass and Chlorophyll Index decreased by 35.7% and 28.0%, respectively. In terms of ionic homeostasis, stress exposure triggered a 202.9% increase in flag leaf Na+ concentration. Consequently, the K+/Na+ ratio—a critical determinant of salt tolerance—suffered a 61.6% reduction (Table 1).

Broad-sense heritability (H2) analysis revealed that morphological traits like awn length remained genetically stable across environments (H2 = 0.35–0.38). In contrast, the K+/Na+ ratio showed a drastic reduction in H2 from 0.65 (Severe) to 0.28 (Moderate), reflecting the high environmental pressure at 12 dS/m.

Pearson correlation analysis revealed a stress-dependent restructuring of the phenotypic network (Fig. 1). Under moderate salinity (upper triangle), Yield per Hectare was positively associated with key yield components; however, a significant negative correlation was observed between Grains per Spike and Thousand-Grain Weight (P < 0.05), indicating a physiological trade-off. Under severe salinity (lower triangle), the correlation between the K⁺/Na⁺ ratio and Yield per Hectare remained positive and highly significant (P < 0.01), although the effect size was relatively weak. Notably, the K⁺/Na⁺ ratio maintained a strong, highly significant negative correlation with sodium accumulation (Na) (P < 0.01). This suggests that under extreme stress, ionic exclusion (K⁺/Na⁺ regulation) primarily acts to mitigate Na toxicity, indirectly supporting overall yield stability rather than serving as a strong direct predictor of yield components.

Multi-model GWAS and identification of consensus MTAs

To maximize the detection of robust associations while minimizing false positives, we employed a multi-model strategy integrating MLM, FarmCPU, and BLINK (Fig. 5). Quantile–Quantile (Q-Q) plots demonstrated that the multi-locus models (FarmCPU and BLINK) effectively corrected for population stratification and offered superior resolution compared to the single-locus MLM (Fig. 6). To define high-confidence loci, we prioritized "Consensus MTAs"—defined as SNPs exceeding the significance threshold (P < 1 × 10–4) across all three models. This rigorous filtering resulted in 45 unique high-confidence SNPs representing stable genomic regions associated with salinity tolerance (Tables 3 & 4). These consensus loci were subsequently mapped to 101 putative candidate genes within a ± 100 kb physical window, a range selected based on the average Linkage Disequilibrium (LD) decay of the panel. The genetic architecture revealed a distinct hotspot on chromosome 5B, which harbored multiple consensus SNPs associated with biomass under saline conditions, highlighting the potential role of this genomic region in maintaining growth vigor under osmotic stress.

Functional characterization of candidate genes

Post-GWAS annotation, utilizing the IWGSC RefSeq v2.1 and Ensembl Plants database, facilitated the dissection of the genetic architecture into "constitutive" and "adaptive" components. Functional enrichment and protein domain analyses identified high-priority candidates involved in transmembrane transport, osmotic adjustment, and stress signaling.

Constitutive loci: growth maintenance

Under moderate salinity, the genetic landscape was dominated by loci governing basal growth and yield potential (Table 3). A highly significant consensus region was identified on chromosome 5B (SNP rs6199; 81,491 bp), which showed a robust association with Biomass across all models. Functional annotation of this region prioritized TraesCS5B02G000100 as a top candidate. This gene is annotated as a Sucrose Transport Protein (SUC4-like), a transporter critical for phloem loading and carbohydrate partitioning [46]. The colocalization of a sugar transporter with the biomass trait suggests that this locus may support yield stability by maintaining carbon supply to sink organs, counteracting the energy deficit imposed by mild osmotic stress [47]. Similarly, for Yield per Hectare, the consensus SNP rs167 on chromosome 1D identified candidates including TraesCS1D02G000400. Annotated with membrane-associated functions (GO:0016020), this candidate points to a role in maintaining cellular integrity and membrane stability, a fundamental requirement for yield preservation [48].

Adaptive loci: ion homeostasis and signaling

Under severe salinity, genetic control shifted towards specific stress-responsive mechanisms (Table 4). While several loci were detected for the K+/Na+ ratio, a noteworthy consensus association was observed on chromosome 6 A (SNP rs2763). The gene search in this window identified TraesCS6A02G000200, which contains a Protein Kinase domain (InterPro: Prot_kinase_dom). Given the established role of kinase cascades in activating salt-tolerance pathways (e.g., the SOS pathway), this gene represents a putative regulator of the ionic stress response [49, 50]. Furthermore, on chromosome 2B, the candidate gene TraesCS2B02G000500 was identified as a member of the POT family (Proton-dependent Oligopeptide Transporter) [51]. Transporters of this family are often involved in the mobilization of nitrogenous compounds; in the context of salinity, this may facilitate the accumulation of compatible solutes for osmotic adjustment under severe stress conditions.

Phenological plasticity and stress escape

A distinct linkage block on chromosome 6B, marked by a cluster of three consensus SNPs (rs61893, rs61894, and rs61895), was significantly associated with Physiological Maturity exclusively under saline conditions. This genomic region (approx. 46 kb) maps to candidates such as TraesCS6B02G000100, which carries an NB-ARC domain [52]. While typically associated with biotic stress defense, recent evidence suggests a cross-talk between NB-ARC proteins and abiotic stress signaling [53]. The specific association of this locus with maturity time suggests it may modulate developmental senescence, potentially functioning as an "escape strategy" to accelerate life cycle completion before toxic ion accumulation becomes lethal.

Comparative synteny analysis demonstrated that several GWAS-derived candidate regions are conserved between wheat and rice, with the TaSUC4 candidate gene (TraesCS5B02G000100) exhibiting a particularly strong orthologous relationship with rice chromosome Os12, supporting the evolutionary conservation of salinity-responsive mechanisms (Fig. 7).

Genomic predictive ability across salinity regimes

The genomic predictive ability (rmp) exhibited a trait-specific response, intricately linked to the narrow-sense heritability (h2) and the genetic architecture of the target traits (Fig. 8). For highly heritable architectural traits (e.g., PH and TGW), the gBLUP model demonstrated robust stability, with accuracies ranging from 0.68 to 0.78 regardless of stress severity. A critical shift was observed in ion-homeostasis traits; the rmp for K+/Na+ ratio and Na+ exclusion surged from 0.28 under moderate salinity to 0.65 under severe stress. This elevation suggests that extreme salinity triggers a higher expression of additive genetic variance, effectively reducing the 'environmental noise' component. Conversely, Yield per Hectare exhibited a decline in rmp (from 0.44 to 0.32), highlighting the prevalence of non-additive gene actions and the confounding effects of G × E interactions under high-salinity thresholds.

Discussion

Statistical resolution: from "noise" to high-confidence candidates

The genetic dissection of salinity tolerance in hexaploid wheat is historically challenged by the complex polygenic architecture and rapid linkage disequilibrium (LD) decay (Figs. 1 & 2) [54, 55]. In this study, the deployment of a consensus strategy—integrating the statistical power of FarmCPU and BLINK with the robustness of MLM—was critical to distinguish robust biological signals from statistical noise [56, 57]. This multi-model convergence successfully filtered out sporadic false positives, narrowing down thousands of markers to 45 high-confidence consensus loci (Tables 3 & 4). Unlike single-locus models that often miss small-effect genes due to strict Bonferroni corrections [58, 59], our approach resolved complex regions, enabling the direct identification of putative causal genes. This resolution provides a validated genomic framework for dissecting the mechanisms of salt tolerance.

The "Genetic handoff": constitutive growth vs. Stress acclimation

A pivotal finding of this study is the dynamic "genetic handoff" observed between loci governing potential yield and those regulating stress survival [60, 61]. Under moderate conditions, the genetic architecture was dominated by growth-promoting signals. Crucially, the post-GWAS annotation of the consensus biomass hotspot on chromosome 5B (rs6199) identified TraesCS5B02G000100, which encodes a Sucrose Transport Protein (SUC4-like). In addition to these growth-oriented signals, the stability of rs167 across moderate severe stress suggests its constitutive role in the wheat salinity response. This stands in contrast to stress-inducible loci that emerge only at higher salinity levels, further illustrating the 'genetic handoff' mechanism where distinct sets of genes—ranging from constitutive regulators to stress-specific adaptive factors—take over as environmental pressure increases. The colocalization of a sugar transporter with this biomass QTL offers a tangible biological mechanism: SUC4 is known to regulate phloem loading and sucrose distribution to sink organs [62, 63]. We propose that under mild stress, this locus maintains "yield potential" by ensuring carbon supply to developing tissues. The subsequent downregulation or modulation of such growth-related transporters under severe stress likely reflects a metabolic trade-off, where energy is diverted from biomass accumulation to osmotic regulation [63–65]. Finally, it should be noted that while the use of BLUPs across seasons prioritized the identification of stable genetic effects—crucial for consistent breeding selection—this approach may not fully capture the nuanced, year-specific G × E interactions. Future studies employing year-by-year GWAS could further refine the temporal dynamics of these identified loci under fluctuating environmental conditions.

Adaptive mechanisms: ion homeostasis and phenological escape

Under severe salinity, the genetic control shifted distinctively towards "survival strategies" involving ion homeostasis and phenological plasticity. Regarding ionic regulation, our analysis identified TraesCS2B02G000500 (linked to the 2B consensus region), annotated as a Proton-dependent Oligopeptide Transporter (POT family). Transporters of this class are integral to nitrogen mobilization; their association with salinity suggests a role in accumulating nitrogenous compatible solutes for osmotic adjustment [66–68]. Additionally, the detection of a Protein Kinase candidate on chromosome 6 A (TraesCS6A02G000200) points to the activation of signal transduction cascades (potentially analogous to the SOS pathway) essential for restoring cellular ionic balance [69, 70].

Furthermore, the exclusive detection of the rs61893-95 cluster on chromosome 6B for Physiological Maturity highlights a critical "Escape Strategy." Annotation of this block prioritized TraesCS6B02G000100, a gene containing an NB-ARC domain. While NB-ARC proteins are canonically associated with biotic defense, recent evidence suggests their cross-functional role in abiotic stress signaling and programmed cell death (PCD) [71, 72]. We hypothesize that salt-tolerant genotypes utilize allelic variation in this region to accelerate senescence signaling, thereby shortening the life cycle to complete grain filling before the accumulation of toxic ions reaches lethal levels in the flag leaf [73, 74].

Integrating GWAS-hits into a Haplotype-Informed Genomic Selection (HIGS) framework"

Our findings advocate for a paradigm shift from traditional Marker-Assisted Selection (MAS) toward a Haplotype-Informed Genomic Selection (HIGS) strategy. Although GWAS identified major-effect loci such as TaSUC4 (5B) and NB-ARC (6B), these markers only partially resolve the "missing heritability" of salinity tolerance [75, 76]. The superior predictive accuracies observed for physiological sub-phenotypes (e.g., K+/Na+ ratio) underscore the predictive ability of GS in capturing the polygenic background—consisting of numerous small-effect loci—that facilitates "physiological buffering" [28, 77]. We propose a dual-stage breeding pipeline: (1) utilizing fixed-effect MAS to secure essential salinity-exclusion alleles (e.g., TaSUC4), and (2) implementing GS to maximize additive genetic gain for yield stability [78, 79]. This synergism leverages both the diagnostic precision of GWAS and the holistic variance capture of gBLUP, addressing the complex genetic mosaic of wheat salinity tolerance [80, 81].

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive genomic dissection of salinity tolerance in wheat, bridging the gap between evolutionary genomics and precision breeding. We demonstrate that salt tolerance is not a static trait but a regime-dependent biological process. The discovery of the TaSUC4-Os12g0641400 orthologous pair highlights a critical, evolutionarily conserved "bottleneck" for carbon economy under stress. Our findings suggest that while major-effect loci (e.g., TaSUC4 and NB-ARC) provide the foundation for stress avoidance and exclusion, the polygenic background captured by genomic selection is essential for stabilizing yield. The proposed Dual-Stage Breeding Pipeline—utilizing MAS to fix conserved high-effect haplotypes and GS to maximize additive genetic gains—offers a robust and pragmatic strategy to enhance wheat productivity in salinized landscapes globally.

It should be noted that while the high predictive accuracy observed in this study was achieved through internal fivefold cross-validation, the stability of these genomic predictions may vary when applied to external populations. Future studies utilizing an independent validation set are recommended to further confirm the robustness of the GS models across diverse genetic backgrounds.
