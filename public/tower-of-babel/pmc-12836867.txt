Physical fitness trends and factors influencing them in community-dwelling older adults: a longitudinal cohort study involving multiple tests
Authors: Wang-Sheng Lin, Po-Jung Pan, Nai-Wei Hsu, Shung-Haur Yang, Chih-Chun Tsai, Ya-Hsien Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMaintaining physical fitness (PF) is essential for preserving functional independence in later life. However, longitudinal evidence describing multidomain PF trends among Asian older adults remains limited. This study aimed to investigate temporal changes in PF among community-dwelling older adults in Taiwan and factors influencing these changes.MethodsThe longitudinal study cohort included 403 adults (age ≥ 65 years; mean age: 72.8 ± 0.4 years) who participated in a community health promotion program. Handgrip strength, 30-s arm curl, 30-s chair stand, single-leg stance, 8-foot up-and-go, 2-min step, 6-min walk, back scratch, and chair sit-and-reach tests were repeatedly conducted at three time points to assess key PF parameters. PF trends over time were analyzed using repeated measures analysis of variance. When significant main effects were detected, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the least significant difference method. To examine the influence of covariates on PF trends, we used generalized linear mixed models, incorporating fixed effects for sex, age, height, weight, physical activity level, and measurement time, along with random intercepts to account for within-subject correlations and between-subject variability.ResultsUpper limb strength (handgrip strength and 30-s arm curl tests) improved and remained higher across assessments, whereas lower limb strength and balance (30-s chair stand and single-leg stance tests) improved initially but plateaued later. Agility (8-foot up-and-go test) remained unchanged, whereas flexibility (chair sit-and-reach and back scratch tests), aerobic endurance (2-min step test), and gait speed (6-min walk test) declined significantly (all p < 0.05). Age and body weight were negatively associated with most PF parameters. Higher physical activity levels were associated with greater strength, endurance, and mobility (all p < 0.05).ConclusionDistinct domain-specific PF trends were noted among Taiwanese community-dwelling older adults, with maintenance of strength and agility but progressive declines in endurance and flexibility. Sustained engagement in community-based, multicomponent exercise programs may mitigate age-related decline and support healthy aging in later life.

Introduction

Maintaining physical fitness (PF) is crucial for sustaining functional independence later in life, given that physical performance reflects the combined capacity of multiple physiological systems to support daily functioning. Age-related changes in motor control can be elucidated using a supply–demand framework, wherein older adults recruit broader neural resources to maintain task performance [1]. Healthy aging has been conceptualized through several complementary frameworks that emphasize the preservation of physiological and functional resilience. For instance, the motor reserve model proposes that lifelong physical activity strengthens neural and musculoskeletal substrates, enhancing resistance to age- or disease-related decline in motor function [2–4]. This adaptive reserve aligns with the morbidity compression hypothesis, which posits that maintaining higher functional capacity throughout life would delay the onset of chronic diseases and disability, thereby shortening the duration of morbidity before death [5, 6]. Complementing these perspectives, the World Health Organization conceptualized an intrinsic capacity framework, wherein healthy aging is considered to involve the preservation of five interrelated domains that collectively underpin independence: locomotion, vitality, sensory, cognition, and psychological capacity [7, 8]. Within this framework, PF directly contributes to locomotion and vitality, supporting autonomy and delaying functional decline. Taken together, these models suggest that maintaining PF through community-based interventions can enhance motor reserve, delay disability, and preserve intrinsic capacity, eventually promoting sustained independence in older adults.

Global data indicate that PF trends among older adults vary across domains, contexts, and regions. In Switzerland, Henchoz et al. [9] found marked improvements in gait speed test, timed up-and-go test, chair stand test (CST), and manual dexterity test results between 2005 and 2015, whereas grip strength and balance remained stable, reflecting improved functional aging. In Sweden, Santoni et al. [10] observed reduced functional impairments among healthy adults (without any initial impairment) from 2001 to 2016, with considerable improvements in CST and gait speed test results but not in balance (single-leg stance test [SLST]); most improvements occurred after 2007 and reflected better maintenance of function. In Portugal, Marques et al. [11] noted participants’ stable performance in the arm curl test (AST), CST, and 8-foot up-and-go test (8-UGT) from 2008 to 2018, alongside declines in the 6-min walk test (6MWT) and back scratch test (BST) results. Taken together, these findings highlight heterogeneous functional fitness trajectories across countries, with some domains improving, some stabilizing, and a few declining, underscoring that aging trends are both context-specific and domain-dependent.

On the basis of the global data, Taiwan implemented nationwide initiatives to promote active aging and delay disability in later life. Government-supported programs, including 266 certified community-based initiatives, integrate physical activity, nutrition, cognitive training, fall prevention, and oral health promotion [12]. Evidence generated within the country suggests that PF in older adults is strongly associated with exercise habits, living conditions, fall-related hospitalizations, and total exercise volume [13]. Handgrip strength (HGS) is correlated positively with sex, body mass index (body mass index [BMI]), and upper limb performance but negatively with age and mobility time [14]. However, most studies on this topic had a cross-sectional design, which limited causal inference. To address this gap, Chiu et al. [15] conducted a 9-month quasi-experimental study involving 159 community-dwelling older adults. The researchers reported that a 12-week multicomponent exercise program improved step test performance and delayed frailty progression and that older age, female sex, and slower timed up-and-go time predicted worsening frailty. Although the exercise program was beneficial, the aforementioned study had some limitations: the researchers examined only a few fitness domains and included a modest sample size. In the present longitudinal study, we extended this work by conducting repeated, multidomain PF tests, thereby generating region-specific evidence that complements the global findings and enhances the current understanding of healthy aging trajectories in Taiwan.

Conceptually, the present study advances the field by modeling intraindividual trajectories across multiple fitness domains, rather than relying solely on cross-sectional comparisons. The longitudinal approach clarified how demographic and behavioral factors jointly shape the heterogeneity of physical aging. On the basis of the aforementioned theoretical frameworks, we investigated longitudinal changes in PF among community-dwelling older adults participating in health promotion programs in Taiwan. Specifically, we assessed whether functional parameters such as muscle strength, balance, agility, and gait speed remained stable or declined across repeated assessments over time. On the basis of prior evidence linking physical activity to age-related functional decline, we proposed two a priori hypotheses: (1) Engagement in community-based exercise programs would be associated with the maintenance of or improvement in specific PF domains. (2) A lower level of habitual physical activity would predict a faster decline in PF.

Methods

Study design and cohort

This retrospective cohort study included community-dwelling older adults who participated in a community health promotion program and completed three comprehensive PF tests. The program was conducted by the Community Medicine Center at National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University Hospital, Taiwan, between July 10, 2017, and December 31, 2023. All study procedures adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University Hospital. This study was approved by the Human Subject Research Ethics Committee of National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University Hospital (approval number: 2024A002).

Eligible individuals were recruited through voluntary registration at local community centers and subsequently scheduled in groups for further assessments, including a series of PF tests, at the Community Medicine Center. Health-care professionals determined participant eligibility through systematic evaluations. Before the PF tests, we provided all participants with detailed information regarding the purpose, potential risks, and benefits of the tests and asked them to grant informed consent. Initial screenings were performed to ensure that the enrolled individuals met the basic inclusion criteria. A comprehensive physical evaluation, including medical-history-taking and physical examination, was performed to confirm each participant’s ability to safely complete the planned PF tests.

Eligibility was determined through standardized interviews aimed at assessing participants’ orientation, comprehension, and ability to follow multistep commands. Individuals exhibiting disorientation and those with a history of dementia diagnosis were excluded to ensure that all participants could safely complete study-related tasks. The inclusion criteria were as follows: being aged ≥ 65 years and residing in Yilan County, expressing willingness to complete the planned PF tests, and being able to perform most study-related tasks. Each participant was also required to comprehend instructions, respond appropriately, and cooperate during assessments. Before the PF tests, a licensed physical therapist provided standardized instructions and supervised one or two practice trials to ensure appropriate performance and participant safety.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: having severe neurological or cognitive impairments, having a recent history of traumatic events, having acute illnesses that could pose safety risks during testing (e.g., unstable arrhythmia, sepsis, or unstable vital signs), and being completely dependent on caregivers for daily activities. Older adults meeting these criteria were not included in the health promotion program.

Nationally representative data from the Taiwan Longitudinal Study on Aging [16] indicate that approximately 5.16% of all community-dwelling adults aged ≥ 50 years have difficulty walking indoors, and 17.28% of them use a walking assistive device. Thus, our cohort may represent relatively favorable mobility profiles among community-dwelling older adults in Taiwan. In addition to mobility characteristics, other aspects of representativeness should also be considered. Because the cohort was predominantly female and voluntarily enrolled in community programs, these characteristics may further limit the generalizability to the broader older population in Taiwan.

Data collection

We invited eligible older adults from local community centers to the Health Promotion Center at the Community Medicine Center. In total, 3,871 community-dwelling older adults were recruited. After the exclusion of ineligible individuals, 3,148 older adults who completed the first PF test were enrolled in the health promotion program. Of them, 1,306 completed the second assessment, and 403 completed the third assessment (Fig. 1).

Demographic data were obtained from all participants. A frailty questionnaire was administered. Frailty status was determined using the validated 5-item Fried frailty phenotype questionnaire adapted for the Taiwanese population [17], and physical activity was measured using a modified version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire–Short Form [17]. Comprehensive PF tests were conducted to evaluate each participant’s body composition (i.e., BMI), HGS, 6MWT performance, muscular strength (30-s ACT and 30-s CST) [18], flexibility (BST and chair sit-and-reach test [CSRT]) [18], aerobic capacity (2-min step test [2MST]) [19], agility (8-UGT) [18], and balance (SLST) [20]. All PF tests were conducted following the Rikli and Jones Senior Functional Fitness protocol [18] and the guidelines issued by the Sports Administration, Ministry of Education, Taiwan. The aforementioned tests are highly reliable and valid, with coefficients ranging from 0.79 to 0.97 [21].

Outcome measures

Demographic characteristics

Health-care professionals administered questionnaires and conducted interviews to collect information on age, sex, height, weight, and physical activity level.

Physical activity levels

A frailty questionnaire was administered to collect information on HGS, gait speed, physical activity level, fatigue, and unintentional weight loss [17]. Physical activity levels were determined through interviews, during which calorie consumption data were collected using the modified short version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire–Short Form (Taiwan version) [17]. The total energy expenditure for the average weekly activity over the previous month was calculated as follows: Physical activity level (kcal) = Metabolic equivalents × Exercise duration (h) × Body weight (kg).

PF level

A qualified physical therapist and a PF coach collaborated with trained hospital volunteers to collect PF data. Here, “PF coach” refers to a certified and trained exercise professional responsible for supervising test safety and ensuring standardization under the physical therapist’s guidance. Before the implementation of the health promotion program, training sessions were conducted to ensure that the participants could complete the tests effectively. To improve testing efficiency and accuracy, we employed a sensor-assisted system (Acutek), a tool previously used to evaluate geriatric PF levels [13, 14]. The following tests were conducted: 30-s ACT, BST (where a negative score indicates that fingertips do not overlap), 30-s CST, CSRT (where a negative score indicates that fingertips do not pass the 0 mark), SLST, 8-UGT, 2MST, 6MWT, and HGS test (TTM Digital Hand Grip Dynamometer; TTM110D). HGS was measured twice per participant, and the higher value was recorded.

To maintain interrater and intrarater reliability during data collection, all assessors completed annual retraining workshops led by a senior physical therapist. The required equipment (TTM grip dynamometer and Acutek sensor system) was calibrated quarterly. Moreover, standardized scripts and demonstration videos were used to ensure that identical test instructions were provided across years.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated to analyze changes in various PF parameters, including BMI and HGS, along with 30-s CST, 30-s ACT, 2MST, CSRT, BST, 8-UGT, SLST, and 6MWT results. All continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard error values. To detect significant changes in PF over time, repeated measures analysis of variance was conducted across three time points (T1, T2, and T3). When a significant main effect was detected, pairwise comparisons were performed using the least significant difference method to determine differences between specific time points. Line graphs were generated to visually represent trends in various PF parameters. To identify key factors influencing PF trends, generalized linear mixed models were constructed, treating PF parameters as dependent variables and incorporating sex, age, height, weight, physical activity level, and assessment time as independent variables. These models enabled both fixed- and random-effects assessments, providing robust estimates of longitudinal effects and adjusting for potential confounders. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Trend analysis results

PF trend over the study period are presented in Table 2; Fig. 2. Distinct domain-specific patterns were noted across the time points. Upper limb strength (HGS test and ACT) improved significantly and remained relatively high over time, whereas lower limb strength and balance (CST and SLST) improved initially but plateaued later. Agility (8-UGT) improved at T2 and remained unchanged thereafter. By contrast, flexibility (CSRT and BST) and aerobic endurance (2MST) declined significantly, and gait speed (6MWT) decreased at T3. BMI remained stable throughout the study period. Taken together, these results indicated improvements in upper limb strength and agility, maintenance of lower limb strength and balance, and gradual declines in flexibility and endurance in our cohort.

Factors influencing PF trends

Key factors influencing PF trends were analyzed using a generalized linear mixed model. The results are summarized in Table 3 and 4. Several demographic and behavioral factors were associated with domain-specific PF trends. Age and body weight were consistently negatively associated with changes in most parameters, indicating a progressive decline in PF with aging and increasing adiposity. Male sex and taller height were associated with higher HGS and better balance, whereas female sex was associated with better flexibility. Physical activity level emerged as a significant positive predictor of multiple outcomes, including performance in the HGS test (p = 0.002), CST (p = 0.003), 2MST (p = 0.020), 8-UGT (p = 0.000), and 6MWT (p = 0.025), reflecting increased agility and gait speed. Over the follow-up period, ACT results improved moderately, whereas flexibility and aerobic endurance declined. Collectively, these findings indicate that maintaining higher habitual activity and controlling body weight may mitigate age-related declines in strength, aerobic endurance, and mobility among community-dwelling older adults.

Discussion

This study revealed the following domain-specific PF changes among community-dwelling older adults in Taiwan: upper limb strength (HGS test and ACT) improved and remained relatively higher across assessments; lower limb strength and balance (CST and SLST) improved initially but plateaued thereafter; agility (8-UGT) improved and remained unchanged; and flexibility (CSRT and BST), aerobic endurance (2MST), and gait speed (6MWT) declined gradually. Age and body weight were negatively associated with most PF parameters, whereas a higher physical activity level was associated with better strength, endurance, agility, and mobility. Overall, the associations observed in the generalized linear mixed models were small to moderate in magnitude, indicating meaningful but not large shifts in PF performance across demographic and behavioral factors. Consistent with the two a priori hypotheses, participation in community-based health programs was associated with improvements in both upper limb strength and agility and partially maintained lower limb strength and balance. By contrast, a lower level of habitual physical activity was associated with faster declines across multiple domains. Taken together, these findings indicate that different PF parameters follow different aging trajectories, underscoring the protective role of regular physical activity in maintaining neuromuscular and functional capacity in Taiwanese older adults.

In the present study, upper limb strength (HGS, ACT) showed sustained improvement across assessments, suggesting that regular engagement in community-based activities may help preserve neuromuscular function in these domains. By contrast, a study conducted among Swiss older adults reported that HGS did not improve over a 10-year period: significant declines were observed in men (from 40.07 kg to 40.30 kg and then to 38.09 kg) and nonsignificant changes in women (from 23.66 kg to 24.54 kg and then to 23.81 kg) [9]. In our cohort, mean HGS values (22.6 kg in women and 32.9 kg in men) were similar to the ranges reported by the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (22.0 kg in women and 34.6 kg in men) [22]. However, they were lower than those reported by the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (approximately 25 kg in women and 39 kg in men) [23]. Therefore, muscle strength is moderately lower among Taiwanese older adults than among their European peers. A large-scale study involving Chinese older adults (n = 5,269; age ≥ 60 years) reported that a higher HGS was associated with a 1.4–2.1-fold higher odds of healthy aging [24], supporting the importance of HGS as a biomarker of functional health. Similarly, our analyses revealed that although age negatively affected HGS, increased physical activity improved it. The ACT results complemented these trends: our cohort performed approximately 19 repetitions on average, which is higher than Portuguese standards (15.7 for women and 16.5 for men) [11]. These observations emphasize the importance of sustained physical activity in preserving upper limb strength and promoting healthy aging.

In contrast, lower limb strength, balance, and flexibility exhibited less favorable trajectories. CST and SLST improvements were not sustained over time, mirroring trends reported in Swedish and Swiss cohorts in which balance performance remained largely unchanged despite gains in other domains [9, 10]. Flexibility declined across both upper and lower limbs, diverging from the mixed patterns reported internationally (e.g., improved lower limb flexibility in Portugal [11]). Taken together, these findings suggest that balance and flexibility are less responsive to habitual community activity than strength or agility, indicating a need for targeted, progressive interventions to sustain these domains.

Regarding mobility, benchmarking our results against several global datasets provides valuable context. The US Health and Retirement Study reported mean gait speeds of 0.832 and 0.756 m/s in men and women aged ≥ 65 years, respectively [25]. By contrast, in our cohort, these values were higher (1.13 ± 0.04 and 1.15 ± 0.02 m/s in men and women, respectively), suggesting more favorable mobility among Taiwanese older adults than in their US peers. In the Swiss cohort, gait speed improved by 10.6% and 13.8% in men and women, respectively, from 2005 to 2015 [9]. Similarly, in the Swedish cohort, marked reductions were noted in gait-related impairment from 2001 to 2016 [10]. By contrast, our longitudinal data revealed a slight decline in gait speed, from 1.18 m/s at baseline to 1.13 m/s at follow-up; therefore, even initially robust older adults may experience deterioration over time. This decline may also reflect insufficient training intensity in community programs, underscoring the need for tailoring exercise prescriptions to individual functional capacities.

Agility, assessed using the 8-UGT, improved in our cohort, and this improvement was sustained across follow-ups. This pattern aligns with the timed up-and-go test results in the Swiss cohort [9]. However, it contrasts with the results in the Portuguese cohort, where poorer 8-UGT performance, particularly among women, was attributable to a higher BMI [11]. Our findings revealed that both height and body weight were significant predictors of 8-UGT results, reinforcing the role of anthropometric factors in maintaining agility and overall functional mobility among older adults.

The divergent PF trend of improved muscular strength with reduced aerobic capacity likely reflects an emphasis on low-intensity resistance or seated exercises rather than aerobic activities (e.g., running, cycling, or swimming). Resistance training elicits physiological adaptations distinct from those induced by aerobic exercise, consistent with the specificity of exercise principle, which posits that improvements occur only in response to targeted stimuli. In Taiwan, because of safety considerations, indoor low-to-moderate-intensity exercise programs are preferred, which likely limits cardiovascular benefits. Some of these divergent trends may also partly reflect regression to the mean or minor learning effects from repeated testing, which should be considered when interpreting improvements in certain domains. Thus, to promote comprehensive PF, future community-based programs should integrate progressive strength, aerobic, balance, and flexibility components. Individualized exercise plans supervised by qualified professionals may optimize outcomes, particularly for older adults unable to engage in standard group-based activities.

This study has several methodological and contextual strengths. First, it included a relatively large cohort of 403 community-dwelling older adults who completed three standardized tests, which facilitated longitudinal evaluation of PF trends rather than limiting the analysis to a cross-sectional perspective. Second, all measurements adhered to the Rikli and Jones Senior Functional Fitness protocol and national guidelines from Taiwan’s Ministry of Education, ensuring methodological rigor and high reliability. Finally, assessments were conducted within an ongoing community health promotion program, enabling the elucidation of naturally occurring PF changes in real-world settings.

This study has several methodological limitations, primarily attributable to its community-based, voluntary recruitment design. First, the preponderance of female participants (82.6%) may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, we only included functionally independent older adults who voluntarily joined the health promotion program and excluded inpatients and those with severe illnesses. As such, our cohort likely represents a healthier, more active subset of older adults. Third, potential learning or familiarization effects from repeated testing cannot be ruled out; nevertheless, standardized examination procedures likely minimized this bias. Additionally, the use of least significant difference post-hoc comparisons without further adjustment may increase the likelihood of type I error, and results should be interpreted with this consideration in mind. Finally, physical activity data were self-reported by the participants, which may have introduced recall bias and thus reduced the accuracy of the analyses.

Conclusion

In this retrospective cohort study, we observed distinct, domain-specific PF trends among community-dwelling older adults participating in a health promotion program in Taiwan. Upper limb strength improved and remained high over time, whereas lower limb strength and balance exhibited gains initially and then stabilized. Furthermore, agility was maintained. However, flexibility, aerobic endurance, and gait speed declined progressively. Older age and higher body weight were consistently associated with poorer physical performance, and higher physical activity levels were associated with better outcomes across multiple domains. These findings highlight the value of sustained engagement in structured, community-based exercise programs. Integrating progressive aerobic, balance, and flexibility training with strength development may help mitigate age-related functional decline and promote prolonged independence in later life.
