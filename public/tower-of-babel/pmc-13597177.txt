Association of opioid-based and opioid-free multimodal analgesia with hospital length of stay and postoperative pain in outpatient aesthetic plastic surgery: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Stephanie Jarrín-Navarro, Fabricio González-Andrade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOutpatient aesthetic plastic surgery has expanded significantly, increasing the importance of effective perioperative pain management strategies. Multimodal analgesia is widely used to improve postoperative outcomes and reduce opioid exposure; however, evidence comparing opioid-based and opioid-free approaches in real-world ambulatory plastic surgery settings remains limited.ObjectiveTo evaluate the association between opioid-based and opioid-free multimodal analgesia and postoperative pain and hospital length of stay in patients undergoing outpatient aesthetic plastic surgery under general anesthesia.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted including 208 patients undergoing outpatient aesthetic plastic surgery between November 2022 and March 2024 in Quito, Ecuador. Patients were classified into opioid-free or opioid-based multimodal analgesia groups (n = 104 each) based on intraoperative anesthetic records. Primary outcomes included postoperative pain and hospital length of stay. Analyses included descriptive statistics, regression models, and stratification by surgical group to address procedural heterogeneity.ResultsBaseline characteristics differed between groups, particularly in surgical procedure type, age, body mass index, and comorbidities. Opioid-based multimodal analgesia was associated with higher odds of moderate pain at operating room exit (OR 21.15, 95% CI 9.76–45.84) and at 15 min (OR 3.91, 95% CI 1.22–12.52), increased need for rescue analgesia (OR 2.57, 95% CI 1.20–5.49), and prolonged hospital length of stay (OR 5.42, 95% CI 2.36–12.45). These associations remained significant in multivariable models for most outcomes. Stratified analyses by surgical group showed consistent associations, although effect sizes varied according to procedure type.ConclusionMultimodal analgesic strategy was associated with differences in postoperative pain and hospital length of stay in outpatient aesthetic plastic surgery. Opioid-based regimens were more frequently used in more extensive procedures and were associated with worse early postoperative outcomes, whereas opioid-free strategies were predominantly used in less invasive procedures. These findings highlight the importance of procedure-specific and patient-centered analgesic approaches. Due to the observational design and baseline heterogeneity, results should be interpreted as associative rather than causal.

Introduction

Outpatient surgery has become the predominant model for elective procedures in high- and middle-income healthcare systems, driven by the need for efficiency, patient-centered care, and optimized resource utilization.1 This shift has been supported by the expansion of ambulatory surgical infrastructure and by clinical evidence demonstrating that appropriately selected patients can safely undergo complex procedures without prolonged hospitalization.2 Ambulatory or short-stay surgery is generally defined as elective surgical care in which patients are discharged on the same day or within 24–48 h after admission.3 The success of this model depends on rigorous preoperative evaluation, standardized perioperative protocols, and effective postoperative management.4 Among these factors, postoperative pain control plays a central role, as inadequate analgesia has been consistently associated with delayed discharge, unplanned medical contacts, and reduced patient satisfaction.5 Despite advances in perioperative care, postoperative pain remains undertreated in a substantial proportion of patients undergoing ambulatory procedures.6

Multimodal analgesia has emerged as a cornerstone of contemporary perioperative pain management, combining agents with complementary mechanisms of action to enhance analgesia while minimizing adverse effects.7 This approach is strongly recommended in enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) pathways and modern anesthesia guidelines, particularly for reducing opioid exposure.8 In plastic surgery, multimodal regimens frequently incorporate non-opioid medications such as acetaminophen and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), with or without opioids depending on surgical complexity and patient factors.9 However, acute postoperative pain remains common even in ambulatory settings, especially after procedures involving extensive tissue dissection or prolonged operative time.10,11 Poorly controlled pain has been associated with postoperative nausea and vomiting, delayed recovery, increased morbidity, and a higher risk of persistent postoperative pain.12 Importantly, postoperative pain is influenced by both patient-related and procedure-related factors, including surgical extent, duration, tissue trauma, age, body mass index, and comorbidities.13, 14, 15

Biological and demographic factors further modulate pain perception and analgesic requirements. Sex-related differences, ethnic variability, and psychosocial factors have all been associated with differences in postoperative pain experience and opioid consumption.16, 17, 18 In addition, age-related physiological changes may alter nociceptive processing and pain reporting, supporting the need for individualized analgesic strategies.19,20 Length of hospital stay after outpatient plastic surgery is closely linked to postoperative recovery quality and is influenced by both surgical and anesthetic factors.21 While opioid-containing regimens may provide effective analgesia, they are also associated with adverse effects such as sedation, nausea, and delayed functional recovery, which may prolong discharge time.22 Consequently, opioid-sparing and opioid-free multimodal strategies have gained increasing attention as potential approaches to improve recovery and facilitate earlier discharge.23

However, direct comparisons between opioid-based and opioid-free multimodal analgesia in outpatient aesthetic plastic surgery remain limited, particularly in real-world clinical settings characterized by heterogeneous surgical procedures and patient populations.24,25 Differences in procedural complexity—such as the contrast between facial procedures and more invasive body contouring surgeries—represent a major source of confounding when evaluating postoperative outcomes. These variations may independently influence pain intensity, recovery trajectory, and hospital length of stay, making unadjusted comparisons potentially misleading. Therefore, careful analytical strategies, including stratification by surgical group and multivariate adjustment for baseline characteristics, are essential to improve the validity of observational findings and address cohort imbalances highlighted in prior evaluations.

The objective of this study was to evaluate the association between opioid-based and opioid-free multimodal analgesia and postoperative outcomes, including pain and hospital length of stay, in patients undergoing outpatient aesthetic plastic surgery under general anesthesia. In doing so, we explicitly account for differences in surgical case mix and patient characteristics through stratified and multivariable analyses, acknowledging the observational nature of the study and the potential for residual confounding.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study was conducted as a descriptive, observational, retrospective cohort study of patients undergoing outpatient aesthetic plastic surgery under general anesthesia in Quito, Ecuador. Data were obtained from electronic and physical medical records of patients treated between November 2022 and March 2024. The retrospective design was selected to evaluate real-world clinical practice in a high-volume ambulatory setting without modifying clinical decision-making. All anesthetic and analgesic strategies were determined by treating clinicians according to institutional practice. This design allows the exploration of associations between multimodal analgesic strategies and postoperative outcomes while acknowledging the inherent limitations of observational research, particularly with respect to causal inference.

Participants

Eligible participants were adult patients aged 18 to 65 years who underwent outpatient plastic surgery under general anesthesia. Patients classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I, II, or III were included, regardless of sex or ethnicity. Patients were excluded if key variables were missing from the medical record. A total of 208 patients met inclusion criteria, with 104 patients in each cohort. Participants were retrospectively classified into opioid-free or opioid-based multimodal analgesia groups based on anesthetic records. Because allocation was not randomized and reflected clinical judgment, baseline differences—particularly in procedure type and complexity—were anticipated and explicitly considered in the analytical strategy.

Variables

The primary exposure variable was the type of intraoperative multimodal analgesia (opioid-free versus opioid-based). Primary outcomes included postoperative pain intensity and hospital length of stay. Pain was assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) and categorized according to clinical practice. Length of stay was defined as the total time in hours from admission to discharge. Covariates included demographic and clinical characteristics such as age, sex, body mass index, and ASA classification, as well as comorbidities, perioperative risk factors, need for rescue analgesia, and postoperative nausea and vomiting. Given the critical role of surgical complexity in postoperative pain and recovery, procedures were classified into two clinically meaningful groups to address confounding by indication, as highlighted by reviewers.Group 1 (Facial and ENT surgeries) included procedures in which the primary surgical component involved the facial or otolaryngologic region. This group comprised blepharoplasties combined with mini-lifts, simple rhinoplasties, and combined procedures with a predominantly facial focus, including rhinoplasty with tongue flap and cleft lip sequelae reconstruction, tumor excision, mentoplasty, maxillary osteotomies, midface elongation, and maxillomandibular surgery. Complex reconstructive procedures, such as rhinoplasty with orbital reconstruction and facial rejuvenation, were also included.Group 2 (Breast, abdominal, and other body procedures) included surgeries in which the predominant component involved the trunk or body contouring. This group comprised abdominoplasties (including repeated procedures), both alone and combined with liposuction or breast augmentation, as well as all breast augmentation and reduction mammoplasty procedures. Liposuction procedures, either isolated or combined with other surgeries, were also included. Combined procedures involving facial components were classified within this group when the predominant or more complex component corresponded to body surgery, such as abdominoplasty with blepharoplasty or breast surgery combined with facial procedures. Classification was based on the primary surgical component to better reflect procedural complexity and expected postoperative pain burden.

Data sources and measurement

Data were collected retrospectively from electronic and physical medical records using a standardized extraction form. Extracted variables included demographic characteristics, surgical details, anesthetic and analgesic management, postoperative pain scores, rescue analgesia interventions, and length of hospital stay. Pain assessments were routinely recorded by clinical staff as part of postoperative monitoring. All data were anonymized prior to analysis to ensure patient confidentiality and data integrity.

Bias

To minimize information bias, data extraction was performed by a single trained investigator using a standardized approach. However, due to the non-randomized nature of the study, selection bias and confounding by indication are important considerations. The choice of analgesic regimen was influenced by anticipated surgical pain and procedure complexity, which may differ between cohorts. This introduces potential bias, particularly related to the distribution of surgical types between groups. In response to reviewer concerns, stratification by surgical group was incorporated to partially account for procedural heterogeneity, and results were interpreted cautiously without attributing causality.

Study size

The study included all eligible patients within the defined study period who met inclusion criteria, resulting in a total sample size of 208 patients. No formal sample size calculation was performed due to the retrospective nature of the study. The final sample reflects real-world clinical practice and available data during the study period.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was performed using RStudio and IBM SPSS Statistics version 29. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, and associations were evaluated using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate. Logistic and ordinal regression analyses were conducted to explore associations between analgesic strategy and postoperative outcomes. Due to limited overlap between cohorts in certain procedure types, full multivariable adjustment for surgical complexity was constrained. Therefore, analyses were complemented with stratification by surgical group to address heterogeneity, in line with reviewer recommendations. All findings are presented as associative rather than causal. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value <0.05.

Ethical considerations

All patients provided written informed consent for the use of their clinical data. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the local institutional ethics committee. All data were anonymized prior to analysis, and confidentiality was maintained throughout the study.

Results

Participants

A total of 208 patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in the analysis, with 104 patients assigned to the opioid-free multimodal analgesia group and 104 to the opioid-based group. All included patients had complete data for the primary exposure and outcomes. Group allocation reflected routine clinical practice rather than randomization. As anticipated, baseline differences between cohorts were observed, particularly in the distribution of surgical procedures and patient characteristics, supporting the need for adjusted and stratified analyses.

Descriptive data

Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The distribution of surgical groups was balanced, with equal representation of Group 1 (facial/ENT procedures) and Group 2 (breast, abdominal, and body procedures) in both cohorts. However, significant differences were observed in the distribution of specific procedures within these groups. Rhinoplasty was more frequent in the opioid-based group (65.4%vs 31.7%, p < 0.001), whereas abdominoplasty and breast procedures were relatively more common in the opioid-free group. Differences were also identified in age distribution (p = 0.005), with a higher proportion of younger patients in the opioid-free group. Body mass index classification differed significantly (p = 0.018), with a higher prevalence of overweight and obesity in the opioid-based group. Comorbidities were more frequent in the opioid-based cohort (p = 0.041), while sex and ASA classification were not significantly different between groups. Outcome data

Postoperative outcomes according to analgesic strategy are presented in Table 2. At operating room exit, moderate pain was significantly more frequent in the opioid-based group compared with the opioid-free group (69.2%vs 9.6%, p < 0.001). This difference persisted, although attenuated, at 15 min after operating room exit (13.5%vs 3.8%, p = 0.012). Patients receiving opioid-based multimodal analgesia were more likely to require rescue analgesia (25.0%vs 11.5%, p = 0.021), and they experienced a higher incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (21.2%vs 9.6%, p = 0.019). In addition, hospital length of stay differed significantly between groups, with a higher proportion of patients in the opioid-based cohort remaining hospitalized for ≥12 h (28.8%vs 7.7%, p < 0.001). Main results

Unadjusted regression analyses showed that opioid-based multimodal analgesia was associated with significantly increased odds of adverse postoperative outcomes (Table 3). Specifically, opioid-based analgesia was associated with higher odds of moderate pain at operating room exit (OR 21.15, 95% CI 9.76–45.84, p < 0.001) and at 15 min postoperatively (OR 3.91, 95% CI 1.22–12.52, p = 0.011). It was also associated with increased odds of requiring rescue analgesia (OR 2.57, 95% CI 1.20–5.49, p = 0.019) and postoperative nausea and vomiting (OR 2.55, 95% CI 1.13–5.75, p = 0.017). Ordinal regression analysis demonstrated that opioid-based analgesia was associated with a higher likelihood of prolonged hospital stay (OR 5.42, 95% CI 2.36–12.45, p < 0.001) (Table 3). In multivariable models adjusting for age, ASA classification, body mass index, and surgical group (Table 4), opioid-based analgesia remained independently associated with moderate pain at operating room exit (adjusted OR 18.29, p < 0.001), moderate pain at 15 min (adjusted OR 7.02, p = 0.016), need for rescue analgesia (adjusted OR 11.63, p = 0.003), and prolonged hospital stay ≥12 h (adjusted OR 4.32, p = 0.001). Overall, stratified analyses confirmed that the association between opioid-based analgesia and worse postoperative outcomes persisted across different surgical categories, although the magnitude of the effect varied according to procedure type. These findings support the consistency of the observed associations while highlighting the influence of surgical complexity and the importance of stratified analysis in interpreting results (Table 5, Table 6).

Discussion

This study evaluated the association between opioid-based and opioid-free multimodal analgesia and postoperative outcomes in outpatient aesthetic plastic surgery. The findings demonstrate significant differences in postoperative pain, rescue analgesia requirements, and hospital length of stay between analgesic strategies. However, these results must be interpreted within the context of important baseline heterogeneity between groups, particularly in terms of surgical complexity and patient characteristics. Rather than representing a direct effect of analgesic strategy alone, the observed differences likely reflect the interplay between procedure type, anticipated nociceptive burden, and individualized perioperative management decisions.

A key finding of this study is the strong association between opioid-based multimodal analgesia and higher levels of early postoperative pain. At first glance, this observation may appear counterintuitive; however, it is consistent with the distribution of surgical procedures between groups. More invasive procedures, including abdominoplasty and combined body contouring surgeries, were more frequently managed with opioid-based regimens, whereas less invasive procedures such as rhinoplasty were predominantly treated with opioid-free strategies. This pattern suggests that analgesic selection was closely aligned with expected surgical pain rather than functioning as an independent determinant of outcomes. Previous studies have consistently demonstrated that surgical extent, duration, and tissue trauma are major drivers of postoperative pain intensity.6,7

The stratified analyses further support this interpretation. When outcomes were evaluated within surgical groups, the association between opioid-based analgesia and adverse outcomes persisted but varied in magnitude. Stronger associations were observed in Group 2 procedures, which include more extensive body surgeries, while some associations were attenuated or not statistically significant in Group 1. These findings highlight the importance of accounting for procedural heterogeneity, as recommended by reviewers, and reinforce that surgical complexity remains a primary determinant of postoperative recovery trajectories.

Patient-related factors also contributed to differences between groups. Younger patients were more frequently managed with opioid-free multimodal analgesia, whereas older patients and those with higher body mass index or comorbidities were more likely to receive opioid-based regimens. Age-related changes in nociceptive processing, as well as the presence of comorbid conditions, may influence both pain perception and analgesic requirements. Similarly, obesity has been associated with altered pharmacokinetics, increased inflammatory response, and greater postoperative pain burden. These findings emphasize the need for individualized analgesic strategies tailored to patient risk profiles rather than standardized protocols.

Postoperative nausea and vomiting did not differ significantly between groups in adjusted analyses, despite higher crude rates in the opioid-based cohort. This may reflect the use of standardized antiemetic prophylaxis and multimodal anesthetic approaches in the study setting. Similarly, patterns of rescue analgesia were consistent with expected clinical practice, with non-opioid agents predominating in the opioid-free group and opioids used more frequently in the opioid-based group. The relatively high frequency of rescue analgesia in both groups likely reflects proactive pain management protocols rather than inadequate baseline analgesia.

The association between opioid-based analgesia and prolonged hospital length of stay is one of the most clinically relevant findings of this study. However, this relationship should be interpreted cautiously. Length of stay in outpatient surgery is influenced by multiple factors, including surgical extent, recovery time, pain control, and institutional discharge criteria. Given the imbalance in procedure types between groups, it is likely that surgical complexity played a dominant role in determining discharge timing. Therefore, the observed association should be considered descriptive rather than causal.

Overall, the results highlight that analgesic strategy in outpatient aesthetic plastic surgery is not an isolated intervention but rather part of a broader perioperative decision-making process that integrates surgical, anesthetic, and patient-specific factors. Opioid-free multimodal analgesia appears feasible and effective in selected, less invasive procedures, while opioid-based regimens continue to be used in more extensive surgeries with higher nociceptive demands. These findings support a tailored, procedure-specific approach to perioperative pain management rather than a universal opioid-sparing strategy.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations. Its retrospective, single-center design limits causal inference and generalizability. Substantial baseline differences between groups, particularly in surgical type and complexity, introduce confounding by indication. Although regression and stratified analyses were performed, limited overlap between cohorts restricted the ability to fully adjust for procedural differences. In addition, analgesic regimens and rescue protocols were not standardized, reflecting real-world practice but increasing variability. Pain outcomes were based on categorized VAS scores, which may reduce measurement granularity. Finally, other postoperative outcomes such as sedation, respiratory depression, or functional recovery were not systematically assessed.

Generalizability

Despite these limitations, this study provides relevant real-world data from a Latin American outpatient plastic surgery setting, a context that is underrepresented in the literature. The findings may be applicable to similar ambulatory surgical centers with comparable patient populations and perioperative practices. Future prospective studies incorporating procedure-specific cohorts, standardized analgesic protocols, or propensity-matched designs are needed to better isolate the independent effect of analgesic strategy on postoperative outcomes and to address the methodological concerns raised in observational analyses.

Conclusion

In this retrospective cohort of patients undergoing outpatient aesthetic plastic surgery, multimodal analgesic strategies were associated with differences in postoperative pain patterns and hospital length of stay. Opioid-based multimodal analgesia was more frequently used in more extensive and invasive procedures and was associated with higher levels of early postoperative pain and longer hospitalization categories. In contrast, opioid-free multimodal analgesia was predominantly applied in less complex procedures and was associated with lower reported pain intensity and shorter length of stay. Importantly, these findings should be interpreted in the context of substantial heterogeneity in surgical procedures and patient characteristics between groups, suggesting that the observed associations are likely influenced by surgical complexity and confounding by indication rather than reflecting a direct effect of analgesic strategy alone. Overall, the results support a tailored, procedure-specific approach to perioperative pain management in ambulatory plastic surgery, emphasizing the need to individualize analgesic strategies according to surgical extent and patient profile. Future prospective studies with standardized protocols and procedure-specific comparisons are required to better define the independent contribution of multimodal analgesic strategies to postoperative recovery outcomes.
