Intestinal amino acid metabolic response and its roles in inflammatory bowel disease
Authors: Yuguo Niu, Fengwanni Wang, Changqin Liu, Zhanju Liu, Xiaoming Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a group of chronic intestinal disorders driven by a complex interplay of environmental and genetic factors. Amino acids (AAs) play a pivotal role in modulating immune function and maintaining gut barrier integrity, which is strongly linked to the pathogenesis of IBD. The intestinal epithelium contains a variety of AA-sensing receptors and transporters that detect AA availability and dynamically reprogram host responses, thereby regulating intestinal physiology and immunological status. Additionally, the gut microbiota contributes significantly to the synthesis, supply, and metabolism of AAs within the gut lumen, and the changes in these AA-metabolizing bacterial strains markedly affect the occurrence of IBD. Both the host intestinal epithelium and the gut microbiota sense AAs at distinct spatial levels, which is essential for ensuring intestinal immune homeostasis. In this review, we highlight the potential roles of AAs and their metabolites in the pathogenesis of IBD. We provide an overview of recent findings on the effects of AA sensors and transporters on AA availability and their relevance to the pathogenesis of IBD. Finally, we summarize the roles of AA metabolites from the microbiota and their receptor signaling pathways in intestinal physiology and inflammation, which may inspire novel therapeutic opportunities for IBD.

Introduction

The global incidence of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), which includes Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), has been dramatically increasing over the past few decades [1]. IBD are chronic, idiopathic, relapsing inflammatory conditions of the gastrointestinal tract, characterized by electrolyte imbalance, abdominal pain, diarrhea, and bleeding [2, 3]. The pathogenesis of IBD is multifactorial, involving a complex interplay of genetic predisposition, host immune dysregulation, nutritional factors, and environmental influences, including the composition of the gut microbiota [4]. Despite significant research advances, the precise etiologies of IBD remain largely undefined.

Amino acids (AAs) are nutrients essential for maintaining of the growth, development and cell renewal of organisms. Emerging evidence suggests that AAs play pivotal roles in regulating the intestinal epithelial cell (IEC) renewal, the integrity of gut barrier, and immune responses. For example, arginine supplementation promotes intestinal stem cells (ISC)-mediated intestinal regeneration [5]. Glutamate boosts ISC activity and epithelial renewal through activating the mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) [6]. Tryptophan increases the number of colonic regulatory T (Treg) cells via up-regulating the expression of G protein-coupled receptor 15 (GPR15) exclusively in colonic Treg cells [7]. These imbue AAs with the potential to influence the progression and severity of IBD [8, 9]. In addition to its digestive and absorptive functions, the gut is also a nutrient sensing organ and thereby regulate the dynamic balance of AAs in the intestinal mucosa. On the one hand, this is mediated by AA sensors and transporters expressed in the intestinal epithelium [10, 11]. On the other hand, the gut microbiota contributes to AA homeostasis by metabolizing and synthesizing AAs, ensuring stable levels within the host [12–14].

Numerous studies have highlighted the significance of AA metabolites, particularly the gut microbiota-derived tryptophan metabolites, in the modulation of intestinal mucosal immunity and IBD pathogenesis [15]. These metabolites circulate systemically, acting as ligands for cell-surface or intracellular receptors of intestinal mucosal cells and various immune cell populations [9, 15]. Alterations in AA metabolite profiles have been consistently observed in IBD patients [9, 12]. Although host cells can metabolize AAs to produce metabolites such as 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT, also known as serotonin), kynurenine, and branched-chain α-ketoacids, certain metabolites, including indole and its derivatives, are exclusively generated by the gut microbiota [16, 17]. This is due to the presence of specific AA-catalyzing enzymes uniquely encoded by gut commensals, underscoring the broad, microbiota-driven mechanism involved in maintaining host AA homeostasis [18–20].

This review aims to summarize recent research advances in establishing the impact of intestinal AA metabolic responses on the pathogenesis of IBD. This includes gut epithelial AA sensors and transporters that respond to AA availability, the metabolism of AAs by intestinal cells and microbes, and the signaling pathways mediated by microbiota-derived AA metabolites. By integrating these insights, this review will help to deepen the understanding of IBD pathogenesis and provide therapeutic strategies for treating IBD.

Signatures of AA levels in IBD patients

The proteins in the human body are mainly composed of different combinations of 20 AAs, which play critical roles in maintaining physiological functions. AA metabolism appears highly active in intestinal mucosal cells and is essential for regulating systemic functions. Studies have demonstrated that AA levels are significantly altered in patients with IBD compared to those in healthy controls, emphasizing the importance of AA metabolism in IBD pathogenesis (Fig. 1).

In sera/plasma

Clinical studies have shown that the levels of several proteinogenic AAs, including glutamine, tyrosine, asparagine, histidine, and tryptophan, are significantly reduced both in serum and plasma, while threonine, lysine, and methionine are reduced only in plasma, of patients with active IBD [21–25]. Of interest, the serum levels of tryptophan are particularly low in patients with CD compared to those in patients with UC [24]. Additionally, the levels of glycine and proline are significantly increased both in serum and plasma of CD patients compared to those in healthy controls [21, 22]. Metabolomic analyses have also revealed significant alterations in branched-chain AAs (BCAAs), including isoleucine, leucine, and valine, in serum and plasma of patients with IBD [22, 23, 26]. Specifically, Hisamatsu et al. (2012) reported significantly decreased plasma levels of valine and leucine in both CD and UC, whereas isoleucine was only decreased in UC patients [22]. Inconsistently, Schicho et al. (2012) and Dawiskiba et al. (2014) reported that the levels of isoleucine are elevated in serum or plasma of patients with UC and CD [23, 26]. These divergent results may be caused by the different sample sizes, cohort characteristics, and analyzing methods among these studies.

In intestinal mucosa

Fewer studies have focused on the alterations in AA levels in the intestinal mucosa compared to studies on serum or plasma AA levels. However, existing researches indicate that levels of the majority of AAs, including BCAA, glutamine, tyrosine, asparagine, histidine, and lysine, are reduced in the inflamed colonic mucosa of UC patients [21]. Another study reported that the arginine levels are significantly decreased, whereas proline levels are increased, in the inflamed colonic tissues of active UC patients compared to healthy controls [27].

In urine

Levels of asparagine, lysine, and histidine are shown to be decreased in the urine of patients with IBD [28]. Aldars-García et al. (2024) reported that urinary leucine and isoleucine are increased in the newly diagnosed UC patients [25]. Furthermore, urinary AA metabolomic profiling has been used to distinguish IBD patients in remission from healthy controls [26, 29]. For instance, urine glycine is found to be elevated in active IBD patients compared to that in remission, whereas it is decreased in IBD patients in remission compared to healthy individuals [26]. Moreover, Piestansky et al. (2019) reported decreased levels of glycine, arginine, asparagine, tyrosine, and histidine in the urine of IBD patients in remission (treated with thiopurine) compared to healthy controls [29].

In feces

The levels of most AAs, including BCAAs, cystine, glycine, histidine, proline, lysine, phenylalanine, glutamine, alanine, and tryptophan are significantly increased, whereas arginine and asparagine are decreased, in the feces of patients with IBD [30, 31]. Specifically, the levels of BCAAs, alanine, and lysine are higher in the feces of patients with CD, while glutamate and lysine levels are markedly increased in feces of patients with UC, compared to healthy individuals [31]. These changes in fecal AA levels are consistent with Jagt et al. (2022), which reported that BCAAs, glycine, histidine, proline, lysine, phenylalanine, glutamine, alanine, and tryptophan are significantly increased [32]. However, there are no significant differences of arginine, glutamate, and cystine levels in the feces of IBD patients compared to healthy individuals [32], which may be due to the notably different age of participants.

Taken together, these findings suggest that dysregulation of AA metabolism seems to be strongly associated with the disease state of IBD, representing the potential of AA profiling as a valuable tool in the prognosis and intervention of IBD. From this, it may be inferred that reprogramming AA metabolism is a potential adjuvant therapeutic strategy for the treatment of IBD. It is also notable that the different changes in AA levels throughout different tissues, including serum/plasma, intestinal mucosa, urine, and feces, may due to various factors, such as altered sensing, uptake, and utilization of AAs for proteins and metabolites synthesis by the host and microbiota.

The intestine is constantly exposed to nutrient changes, and the interplay between gut immune system and nutrient metabolism plays a pivotal role in shaping gut immune responses. Intestinal AA sensors

mTORC1

mTOR is a highly conserved serine-threonine kinase, functions through two downstream signaling complexes: mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1, containing Raptor) and mTOR complex 2 (mTORC2, containing Rictor). AAs potently activate mTORC1 via AA-sensing GTPase activity towards Rags (GATOR) [33]. GATOR2 is a positive regulator of mTORC1 that is critical for intestinal homeostasis. This has been evidenced by the increased susceptibility of animal models to experimental colitis with IEC-specific deletion of WD Repeat Domain 24 (WDR24), a component of GATOR2 [34].

mTORC1 has a nuanced role in intestinal biology. Some researchers have shown that mTORC1 signaling protects against inflammation of the intestinal mucosa. This is likely achieved by promoting epithelial cell renewal and activating inflammation-induced signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) in IECs, which results in the expression of downstream targets (e.g., Reg3γ) essential for cell proliferation and tissue regeneration [35]. However, conflicting data has shown hyperactivated mTORC1 signaling in the gut mucosa of patients with IBD and its inhibition or genetic deletion in the colonic epithelium ameliorates intestinal inflammation in animal models of colitis [36–38]. These contrasting findings suggest that the mechanism of regulating intestinal inflammation by mTORC1 is multifarious, which may be dependent on whether mTORC1 activation is transient or constitutive.

AA availability is a robust determinant of controlling the activity of mTORC1 that shows differential sensitivity towards certain AAs, such as leucine, glutamine, and arginine [39, 40]. However, it is not clear whether the intestinal physiology and inflammation differentially regulated by different AAs are dependent in mTORC1 sensitivity. A recent study reported that diets deficient in leucine, phenylalanine, lysine, and arginine exacerbates colitis, whereas the phenotype is associated with inhibiting mTORC1 activity only in leucine and arginine-deficient groups, marked by downregulation of phospho-S6 in IECs [41]. These findings suggest that effective activators of mTORC1, including leucine and arginine, may affect the severity of colitis in a mTORC1-dependent manner. Consistently, Hou et al. (2020) reported that arginine treatment increases expansion of ISCs and protects the gut in response to injury through mTORC1 activation [5]. Given the importance of mTORC1 signaling and AA sensing in the pathogenesis of IBD, elucidating the mechanisms of sensitivity-specific AAs to mTORC1 activation may provide more effective targets for drug discovery against IBD in the future.

General controlled nonrepressed kinase 2

General controlled nonrepressed 2 (GCN2), also known as eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2 alpha kinase 4 (EIF2AK4), is a key sensor of the integrated stress response [42]. It also regulates cellular metabolism under AA-restricted conditions [43]. Previous work has shown that intracellular AAs depletion increases the levels of uncharged tRNAs, which bind to and activate EIF2AK4 [44]. Phosphorylated EIF2AK4 triggers phosphorylation of eukaryotic initiation factor 2α (EIF2A), a crucial eukaryotic translation initiation factor [45] that represses global protein translation to save energy and adapt to conditions of AAs starvation [46].

A recent report found that low AA level-induced EIF2AK4 activation accelerates DNA damage and intestinal inflammation by reducing intestinal epithelial nuclear fragile X mental retardation-interacting protein 1 (NUFIP1), thereby impairing the DNA damage response and activating necroptosis [47]. In this study, mice were treated with control diet (20% protein) or low AA diet (6% protein) for about 3 weeks [47]. However, another study showed that AA insufficiency suppresses intestinal inflammation in a EIF2AK4-dependent manner. Specifically, mice with colitis that were fed a protein-restricted diet showed reduced intestinal inflammation and colitis symptoms [48]. Genetic deletion of Eif2ak4 in IECs exacerbates inflammasome activation and increases IL-1β secretion, which results in intestinal inflammation and the activation of Th17 cell responses [48]. Notably, mice were treated with control diet (16% protein) or protein-restricted diet (2% protein) for 5 days in this study [48]. These divergent outcomes of EIF2AK4 activation on intestinal inflammation may result from differences between the protein contents of control and low protein diets, or the treatment duration.

EIF2A, a classical downstream target of EIF2AK4, appears to be critical to the mitigation of colitis, as a mouse model of colitis lacking phosphorylated EIF2A (p-EIF2A) in IECs is found to exhibit worsened colitis [49]. Our study showed that the expression of activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4), a downstream effector of EIF2AK4-EIF2A signaling, is decreased in the inflamed intestinal mucosa of active IBD patients [8]. Intriguingly, IEC-specific Atf4 deletion induces spontaneous enterocolitis in murine models by diminishing solute carrier family (SLC) 1 member 5 (SLC1A5) transcription. This, in turn, decreases glutamine uptake and antimicrobial peptide expression [8]. Therefore, these findings demonstrate that the EIF2AK4-EIF2A-ATF4 signaling axis is a critical link between AA sensing and intestinal inflammation.

Considering that different duration of EIF2AK4 activation induced by AA-restriction could result in opposite phenotypes of intestinal inflammation, activation of EIF2AK4 may has dual functions in intestinal homeostasis. At the early stage, acute activated EIF2AK4 responsively upregulates autophagy and p-EIF2A-ATF4 signaling, thereby protecting against intestinal inflammation. However, sustained AA deficiency-induced EIF2AK4 activation accelerates DNA damage and necroptosis through downregulating NUFIP1. Overall, further investigation into the mechanisms underlying EIF2AK4’s role in intestinal homeostasis and inflammation could lead to novel dietary or pharmacological interventions for IBD.

Angiotensin I-converting enzyme 2

Angiotensin I-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) is critical in the renin-angiotensin system and exhibits extracellular carboxypeptidase activity. Studies have shown that ACE2 plays vital roles in maintaining dietary AA homeostasis, shaping composition of the gut microbiota, modulating innate immunity, and influencing susceptibility to colitis [50]. ACE2 heterodimerizes with broad neutral amino acid transporter 1/solute carrier family 6 member 19 (B0AT1/SLC6A19) or sodium-dependent imino acid transporter 1 (SIT1/SLC6A20), which directly supply AAs to the transporters. Correspondingly, ACE2 or SLC6A19 deficiency in the small intestine decreases tryptophan absorption and intestinal antimicrobial peptide production, and increases susceptibility to IBD in mice [50]. Tryptophan supplementation restores these defects, underscoring the importance of ACE2 in maintaining intestinal homeostasis [50]. Thus, ACE2 and its interactions with AA transporters in the small intestine appear to be vital for AA sensing and absorption, which maintains intestinal homeostasis.

AA transporters

The AA transporters express on the IEC surface or apical membranes, which monitor AA concentration and mediate extra- and intra-cellular AA exchange [8, 24, 51–53]. Emerging evidence has shown that some AA transporters play essential roles in the progress of IBD.

SLC7A2 transports arginine into cells, which promotes colonic epithelial restitution. Both SLC7A2 expression and arginine availability are decreased in colonic tissues from IBD patients [54]. When mice are exposed to dextran sodium sulfate (DSS), supplying arginine during the recovery phase improves clinical outcomes. Importantly, the beneficial effects of arginine are abolished in Slc7a2-deficient mice [54]. Glycine is uptake into IECs by the specific glycine transporter 1 (GLYT1), which is encoded by Slc6a9 [51]. Glycine has been shown to prevent or reverse experimentally-induced colitis [51]. Notably, the protective effects of glycine are dependent on GLYT1 activity, which can be blocked by a specific GLYT1 inhibitor, N-[3-(4’-fluorophenyl)−3-(4’-phenylphenoxy)-propyl] sarcosine (ALX-5407/NFPS) [51]. CD98 heavy chain (CD98hc, encoded by Slc3a2) is the heavy chain component of the dimeric transmembrane glycoprotein CD98, which comprises the large neutral AA transporter SLC7A5 [55]. The protein complex SLC7A5/SLC3A2 functions to transport of leucine into the cell in exchange for glutamine [55]. The expression of SLC3A2 was increased in the colonic tissue of mice with colitis, and in the colonic biopsies from IBD patients [56]. Moreover, IEC-specific Slc3a2 overexpression increases susceptibility of mice to experimental colitis, whereas downregulation of Slc3a2 expression in IECs plays a protective role [56]. SLC6A14 is capable of transporting 18 of the 20 proteinogenic AAs, excluding aspartate and glutamate [52]. Its expression is elevated in inflamed intestinal mucosa of CD or UC patients, and mice with experimental colitis [11, 57]. Treatment with SLC6A14 inhibitor, α-methyl-DL-tryptophan (α-MT), ameliorates inflammation and ferroptosis in mice with experimental colitis, which is mediated by decreasing CCAAT enhancer binding protein β (CEBPB) expression [11]. Another study reported that lentivirus-induced Slc6a14 knockdown protects colon tissue integrity and mitigates the morphological changes caused by DSS, which is mediated by decreasing the NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) expression [57].

For AA sensors, activation of mTORC1 or EIF2AK4 may has dual functions in intestinal inflammation, which are dependent on the duration of mTORC1 or EIF2AK4 activation. For AA transporters, SLC6A19, SLC7A2, and GLYT1 play essential roles in the maintenance of intestinal homeostasis and protect against intestinal inflammation, whereas a few AA transporters (such as SLC3A2 and SLC6A14) promote inflammatory responses. Overall, these findings provide potential diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets for IBD.

Tryptophan metabolism

In the gastrointestinal tract, tryptophan metabolism occurs via three major pathways: the kynurenine pathway, the serotonin (5-HT) pathway, and the indole pathway [58]. The kynurenine and 5-HT pathways are primarily in host cells, while the indole pathway is exclusive to gut microorganisms. Each pathway generates multiple metabolites during tryptophan catabolism, which influence intestinal physiology and inflammation. Herein, we discuss the roles and signaling pathways of host-derived tryptophan metabolites in intestinal inflammation.

The kynurenine pathway is responsible for at least 90% of tryptophan catabolism and is predominantly active in the epithelial, immune and mesenchymal cells of the gastrointestinal tract [59]. Indoleamine 2, 3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1) is a rate-limiting enzyme in this pathway, which converts tryptophan into kynurenine and downstream products such as kynurenic acid, xanthurenic acid, picolinic acid, and quinolinic acid [9]. Specifically, kynurenic and xanthurenic acid levels are inversely correlated with colitis severity in both mice and humans. Administration of kynurenic or xanthurenic acid protects from DSS-induced colitis in mice by activating aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) and rewiring cellular energy metabolism in IECs and T cells [9]. Other studies demonstrated that G protein-coupled receptor 35 (GPR35) is also a receptor for kynurenic acid, which inhibits pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g. IL-1β and IL-6) secretion and reduces intestinal inflammation [60, 61]. Clinical studies have shown increased kynurenine concentrations and higher kynurenine/tryptophan ratios in colonic and ileal lesions of patients with IBD compared to those in healthy controls, probably due to increased IDO1 expression [62, 63]. Notably, IDO1 is highly expressed in the gut under physiological condition and further increased in the lamina propria and epithelium of active IBD. Ido1 knockout or pharmacological inhibition of IDO1 exacerbates 4,6-trinitrobenzenesulfonic acid (TNBS)-induce colitis, which is attributed to the inhibition of Treg activity [64, 65]. However, a conflicting result showed that Ido1-deficient attenuates the severity of DSS-induced colitis [66]. This discrepancy can probably be explained, at least in part, by the fact that TNBS induces a T cell-dependent disease while DSS disrupts the intestinal barrier, suggesting that IDO1 may has different functions between IECs and immune cells.

The serotonin (5-HT) pathway is mainly active in enterochromaffin cells and is mediated by the rate-limiting enzyme tryptophan hydroxylase 1 (TPH1). Approximately 1% of tryptophan is metabolized by brain tryptophan hydroxylase to produce serotonin [67]. The state of serotonin pathway activation in IBD is controversial. Several studies have shown that both TPH1 expression and intestinal 5-HT levels are elevated in patients with CD, whereas other studies have found reduced 5-HT levels in patients with UC [68, 69]. In the intestine, 5-HT is a vital signaling molecule that maintains intestinal homeostasis by acting on specific 5-hydroxytryptamine receptors (5-HTRs). Several 5-HTRs subtypes are localized in the IECs [70]. Specifically, 5-HT4 receptor (5-HT4R) is highly expressed in the colonic epithelium [71]. In DSS-treated mice, rectal administration of tegaserod, a 5-HT4R agonist, reduces colitis severity and accelerates recovery from active colitis. However, the protective effects of tegaserod are abolished in 5-HT4R knockout mice. Inhibition of 5-HT4R by its antagonist GR113808 results in signs of colitis in the non-inflamed colons of wild-type mice [72]. Epithelial 5-HT4R activation mediates these protective effects, which are probably achieved through promoting epithelial barrier integrity via enhanced cell proliferation and migration, as well as increasing resistance to oxidative stress-induced epithelial apoptosis [72]. Associations have been reported between progression of colitis and other 5-HTRs, such as 5-HT1AR, 5-HT2AR, 5-HT3R, and 5-HT7R [73–76]. Collectively, these findings suggest that 5-HTR signaling represents a potential biomarker of IBD and therapeutic target in its treatment.

Arginine metabolism

In mammals, arginine is catalyzed by arginase to produce ornithine, a key precursor of polyamines (e.g., putrescine, spermidine, and spermine) [77, 78]. Ornithine decarboxylase catalyzes ornithine to produce putrescine, which is subsequently metabolized to spermidine and spermine via the actions of spermidine synthase and spermine synthase, respectively [53, 77]. The enzyme spermine oxidase (SMOX) catalyzes spermine back into spermidine, playing a critical role in polyamine homeostasis [79]. Spermidine, which is generated by SMOX, exerts protective effects against colitis. These effects are likely produced through reduction of α-defensin expression and maintenance of a protective gut microbiota [79]. These findings highlight the importance of arginine metabolism and polyamine dynamics in the maintenance of intestinal homeostasis and the mitigation of mucosal inflammation in IBD. However, more research is required to fully understand how these metabolites interact with the immune cells through signaling transduction.

Together, tryptophan derived metabolites from the kynurenine pathway, such as kynurenic and xanthurenic acid, may have protective effects on IBD, whereas kynurenine may have the opposite effects. Moreover, IDO1 has dual impacts on IBD, which may be due to its different functions between IECs and immune cells. In the 5-HT pathway, activating 5-HT4R may ameliorate the onset of IBD. Additionally, the arginine metabolite, spermidine, may exert protective effects against IBD.

The roles of microbial AA metabolism in the pathogenesis of IBD

Apart from the intestine and liver, the gut microbiota can also reshape the AA profile by efficiently metabolizing intestinal AAs. A recent study analyzed 104 commensals using large-scale metabolomic screening approach, and identified the specific microbial candidates that efficiently utilize AAs [15]. Notably, several microbes from Clostridium genera, including Clostridium sporogenes and Clostridium senegalense, can rapidly metabolize multiple AAs, including aromatic AAs, BCAA, histidine, arginine, proline, and lysine. Additionally, many Bacteroides commensals have been found to proficiently utilize the AAs, including asparagine, aspartate, and glutamine. Furthermore, many Bacteroides and several Clostridium species are fast metabolizers of glutamate and asparagine. In contrast, aromatic AAs and BCAA are utilized less efficiently by a few Firmicutes/Clostridia microbes, including C. sporogenes and Blautia hydrogenotrophica [15]. In a cohort of healthy first-degree relatives of patients with CD, Leibovitzh et al. (2022) found a decrease in the biosynthesis pathways of tryptophan, glutamate, and threonine in the fecal microbiome, which is associated with impaired barrier function [80]. These findings indicate that the gut microbiota contributes to host AA homeostasis. Microbial aromatic AA catabolism in IBD

Tryptophan is one of the most frequently identified AA metabolized by the gut microbiota. However, only a limited number of microbial strains capable of catalyzing tryptophan have been identified to date. To metabolize tryptophan, microorganisms secrete tryptophanase and decarboxylase extracellularly that convert tryptophan into multiple bioactive indole derivatives, including indole, indole-3-pyruvic acid (IPyA), indole-3-acrylic acid (IA), indole-3-propionic acid (IPA), indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), indole-3-lactic acid (ILA), and indole-3-aldehyde (IAld), among others [81]. Since their involvement in intestinal inflammation and IBD have been summarized elsewhere [81, 82], we only present here the more recent advances of these tryptophan-derived metabolites in the pathogenesis of IBD.

IPyA, produced by aromatic AA transaminases, serves as a precursor of ILA, IAA and IPA. Oral administration of IPyA attenuates intestinal inflammation in colitis models by inhibiting the expression of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-12, IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-1β, and increasing anti-inflammatory IL-10 levels [83]. ILA directly regulates T cell differentiation, thereby suppressing hyperactive immune responses and maintaining intestinal immunity homeostasis. Specifically, ILA activates AhR and downregulates Th-inducing POZ-Kruppel factor in CD4+ T cells, allowing CD4+ T cells to differentiate into immunomodulatory CD4+-CD8aa+ double-positive intraepithelial T lymphocytes [84]. IAA supplementation enhances intestinal mucin sulfation by upregulating the key molecules 3ʹ-phosphoadenosine 5ʹ-phosphosulfate synthase 2 and SLC family 35 member B3 via the AhR, thereby promoting intestinal homeostasis and ameliorating colitis [85]. IAA also ameliorates colitis in mice by promoting Bifidobacterium pseudolongum to produce equol, which provides a new perspective on how indole metabolites regulate gut homeostasis beyond the traditional AhR pathway [86]. Furthermore, IAA interacts with the pregnane X receptor (PXR) to induce the production and accumulation of IL-35+ B regulatory cells, which promotes IL-35 secretion to maintain intestinal homeostasis [87]. IPA is selectively diminished in the sera of patients with UC [88] and reduced in the feces of both CD and UC patients [89, 90]. IPA alleviates intestinal inflammation by suppressing Th1/Th17 cell differentiation and IL-17 A expression through the inhibition of mTOR activity and the modulation of ribosome-related pathways [90].

Tyrosine decarboxylase (TDC) is an enzyme that catalyzes the conversion of tyrosine into tyramine. The Tdc gene is encoded by several bacterial species belonging to the Lactobacillus, Enterococcus, and Providencia genera [91–93]. It has recently been reported that Enterococcus-produced tyramine exacerbates colitis by hijacking α2A-adrenergic receptor (ADRA2A) in ISCs, and that these effects are blocked by an ADRA2A antagonist [91].

Microbial BCAA catabolism in IBD

BCAAs are metabolized by both host cells and microbiota via the deamination pathway. The initial step involves the conversion of BCAAs into branched-chain α-ketoacids (α-keto-β-methylvalerate, α-ketoisovalerate, and α-ketoisocaproate) by BCAA aminotransferase. Branched-chain α-ketoacids are subsequently catalyzed by branched-chain-α-ketoacid dehydrogenase to produce 2-methylbutyral-CoA, isovaleryl-CoA, and isobutyryl-CoA, which are further converted into the corresponding branched-chain fatty acids (BCFAs). BCFAs are integral components of bacterial membrane lipids [94]. Parabacteroides merdae and C. sporogenes can metabolize BCAA to produce BCFAs [12, 95]. BCFAs generated by C. sporogenes appear to enhance the activity of Treg cells and increase the production of IL-22, conferring protection against colitis [12]. Moreover, Luu et al. (2025) found that C. sporogenes-derived BCFAs enhances the IL-10 production by promoting sustained mTOR signaling in B cells [96]. However, the exact receptors or effectors responsible for mediating the regulatory effects of microbiota-derived BCFAs on the pathogenesis of IBD are remain poorly understood.

Microbial histidine catabolism in IBD

Histidine decarboxylase is a key enzyme that transforms histidine to histamine [97]. In addition to host cells, histamine is produced by several microorganisms, including Escherichia coli, Klebsiella aerogenes, and Lactobacillus reuteri [98, 99]. Clinical studies have revealed that the expression of histidine decarboxylase genes is increased in CD patients compared to healthy individuals or UC patients [97]. Microbiota-derived histamine is also increased in fecal samples from both CD and UC patients compared to controls [97]. Previous studies have demonstrated microbiota-derived histamine to play a distinct role in colitis in mice. Gao et al. (2015) found that orogastric gavage with a histamine-generating L. reuteri strain (L. reuteri 6475), but not a mutant L. reuteri 6475 strain lacking histamine conversion genes, ameliorates TNBS-induced colitis in mice [99]. This suggests that the protective effects of L. reuteri 6475 in suppressing intestinal inflammation are dependent on its ability to generate histamine [99]. Moreover, L. reuteri 6475-derived histamine is thought to alleviate colitis by activating the histamine H2 receptor, as inhibiting H2 receptor signaling abolishes its protective effects, whereas blocking the histamine H1 receptor has no effect [99]. However, another work found that microbiota-associated histamine can inhibit epithelial IL-18 production in the intestine and exacerbate DSS-induced colitis in mice [100]. These divergent outcomes may be attributed to differences in colitis models or other factors. Further investigation into the role of microbiota-derived histamine in IBD is warranted.

Imidazole propionate (ImP), is also a gut microbiota-derived histidine metabolite, produced from the intermediate metabolite urocanate by the urocanate reductase [101]. Warmbrunnto et al. (2024) reported that antibiotic treatment dramatically increases the circulating ImP levels in humans, which is associated with increased abundance of Lactobacilli and Streptococci [102]. Functionally, ImP has been reported to disrupt the homeostasis of intestinal goblet cells and increase inflammatory cytokines expression, thereby impairing intestinal barrier integrity and trigger intestinal inflammation [103].

Microbial arginine catabolism in IBD

Arginine is utilized by gut bacteria via similar pathways to those in mammalian cells [104]. The bacteria Lactobacillus spp., Limosilactobacillus mucosae, and E. coli are reported to produce polyamines [105, 106]. Specifically, microbiota-associated putrescine exacerbates DSS- induced colitis in mice during the inflammatory stage [107]. Conversely, spermine is a strong suppressor of inflammasome signaling, as demonstrated by the inhibition of IL-18 production [100]. Spermidine exerts beneficial effects on gut immunity by promoting Treg cell development [108]. Despite these findings, the direct receptors and effectors of polyamines in IBD remain elusive.

Microbial glutamate catabolism in IBD

Gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), a well-known inhibitory neurotransmitter, is produced from glutamate by gut microbiota, including Bacteroides, Parabacteroides, and Escherichia [109]. Glutamate decarboxylase is the key enzyme responsible for catalyzing glutamate to produce GABA [110]. GABA has been shown to alleviate enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli-induced intestinal barrier dysfunction by activating GABA type A receptors (GABARs) signaling and the AMPK-autophagy pathway [111]. Colonic inflammation in mice is induced by GABAR subunit α3 (α3-GABAR) agonist, while ligands targeting α1/4/5-GABAR decrease the expression of colonic inflammatory markers [112]. These findings highlight the potential of GABA/GABAR signaling as a therapeutic target for treating colon inflammation or IBD.

Microbial AA anabolism in IBD

Bacteria can utilize AA to produce different metabolites and also synthesize and supply AA to the host, especially essential AA that cannot be synthesized by mammalian host cells. Bacteroides vulgatus and Prevotella copri have been reported to be associated with high BCAA synthesis [113]. Helicobacter pylori has been reported to stimulate the gut microbiota to produce BCAA, thereby activating the mTORC1 complex to inhibit autophagy within the gut epithelium and induce chronic inflammation [14]. Dubosiella newyorkensis is reported to generate lysine to regulate Treg cell-mediated immunosuppression via activating the AhR-IDO1-kynurenine metabolic circuitry in dendritic cells, which then ameliorates DSS-induced colitis in mice and improves mucosal barrier integrity [114]. A recent study reported that Faecalibacterium prausnitzii reduce the stabilization and pro-inflammatory effect of Th17 cells and synergistically enhance the therapeutic efficacy of ustekinumab in CD patients [115]. Therefore, future studies on the regulation of the intestinal microbial environment are warranted to provide promising therapeutic strategies in the management of IBD.

The receptor signaling of microbial AA metabolites in the pathogenesis of IBD

Emerging evidence suggests a strong link between microbial AA metabolites and the development of IBD, which is mediated through various receptor-associated signaling pathways [89, 116] (Fig. 4).

AHR

Aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) is a ligand-activated transcription factor widely distributed through immune cells and IECs. It has been implicated in the pathogenesis of IBD [117]. AhR is downregulated in the inflamed intestinal tissue of patients with IBD [118]. Decreased AhR activity has been demonstrated in fecal samples from patients with IBD [119]. The gut microbiota metabolizes tryptophan into indole derivatives, which act as ligands for AhR. AhR activation induces the local production of IL-22 and is critical for immune defense and tissue repair [120]. A recent work has demonstrated that AhR deficiency in macrophages exacerbates DSS-induced colitis in mice by reducing ornithine decarboxylase 1 transcription, which negatively regulates IL-1β and polyamine production and induces macrophage pyroptosis [121]. Additionally, AhR activation has been shown to protect mice against colitis by enhancing the secretion of several anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-10 and IL-22), whereas inhibiting pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF and IL-6), and inducing Treg cell activation [118, 122].

PXR

PXR is a nuclear receptor with broad ligands. The activation of PXR leads to the expression of drug-metabolizing enzymes and drug efflux transporters [123]. PXR is primarily expressed in IECs, hepatocytes, and immune cells [124]. PXR signaling provides protective effects in injured colons, partly by modulating the inflammatory potential of the intestinal mesenchyme and preventing excessive NF-kB signaling in mesenchymal cells [89]. Pxr-knockout mice exhibit increased intestinal permeability and enhanced toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) expression, contributing to the disruption of mucosal homeostasis [125]. Interestingly, many AhR ligands, such as indole, IPA, and IA, have also been identified as PXR ligands that ameliorate intestinal inflammation [118, 126].

Dopamine receptor

Recent studies have demonstrated that tryptophan-derived metabolites, including indole-3-ethanol, IPA, and IAld, are also dopamine receptor D2 (DRD2) ligands with similar potency to the endogenous ligand dopamine [116]. Drd2fl/fl mice fed a tryptophan diet or treated with indole-3-ethanol, IPA, or IAld exhibited decreased colonic inflammation. However, these protective effects were abrogated in IEC-specific Drd2 knockout mice [116]. This indicates that the activation of DRD2 signaling in IECs by tryptophan metabolites is beneficial in the amelioration of colonic inflammation and IBD. Given the shared ligands of dopamine receptor with AhR and PXR, future studies may focus on dissecting the different effects mediated by these three receptors via binding to the same ligands.

GPR35

G protein-coupled receptor 35 (GPR35) is a key receptor of kynurenic acid, which is also generated by intestinal microorganisms, such as Escherichia coli [127]. GPR35 is predominantly expressed in immune and gastrointestinal tissues, especially in colon epithelial cells [128]. Sun et al. (2022) reported that kynurenic acid inhibits the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and prevents IL-1β-mediated inflammation by targeting GPR35 [129]. Consistent with descriptions in the previous section, kynurenic acid administration alleviates DSS induced colitis in rat, whereas this effect is completely blocked by inhibiting colon GPR35 activity [130]. These findings suggest GPR35-mediated kynurenic acid sensing plays a crucial role in maintaining intestinal immune homeostasis, which represents a potential therapeutic target for IBD.

Conclusions and perspectives

Dietary AAs are key factors affecting the development and progression of IBD. Epidemiological, clinical, and experimental studies have comprehensively demonstrated that both the varieties and amounts of dietary protein intake, as well as AA supplementation, can affect the pathogenesis of intestinal inflammation in various ways [131]. For instance, dietary glutamine supplementation is reported to inhibit intestinal inflammation by inducing antimicrobial peptide expression in Paneth cells [6]. Similarly, tryptophan supplementation reduces the symptoms of DSS-induced colitis in mice. This is probably through modulation of the gut microbiota composition and the increase of beneficial tryptophan metabolites [16]. Furthermore, arginine supplementation exerts protective effects on colitis [132]. Therefore, AA intervention may be an effective strategy to influence the development and outcome of IBD.

The ability of gut microbes to utilize AAs affects the pathogenesis of IBD in the host. Therefore, targeting AA-metabolizing bacteria offers a promising approach to the treatment or amelioration of IBD. Lactobacillus spp., have been identified to metabolize tryptophan, thereby producing IAld and IAA, and alleviating colitis in both DSS-induced murine models and IBD patients. This is achieved through the amelioration of intestinal damage and the strengthening of the intestinal barrier and mucus defenses [86, 87, 119]. Blautia is a recently discovered genus with potential probiotic properties. Several strains, including B. coccoides, B. wexlerae, and B. producta, have been found to be beneficial in the treatment of metabolic disorders [13, 133–135]. These strains protect the colonic mucus by producing metabolites such as IAA or short chain fatty acids, highlighting the potential of Blautia in treating IBD. Although the development of next-generation probiotics necessitates multiphase clinical validation, the combination of AAs and probiotics will become one of the options for intervening in IBD in the future. Notably, selecting patient/subject in future trials aimed at targeting AA-microbe interactions is especially important.

Engineered probiotics are a cutting-edge strategy for IBD treatment. A clinical trial has suggested Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) as a potential probiotic for treating UC [136]. EcN is genetically engineered to overexpress catalase and superoxide dismutase for the treatment of IBD [137]. Notably, engineered EcN expresses the heterologous hisG, which encodes ATP phosphoribosyl transferase, the first rate-limiting enzyme in the histidine biosynthesis pathways, conferring protection against colitis [138]. Beyond EcN, engineered Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron expressing tryptophan decarboxylase to produce tryptamine has been shown to reduce weight loss and attenuate severity of colitis in DSS-treated mice compared to mice colonized with control strains [139]. Collectively, these studies highlight the potential of bacteria engineered to metabolize AAs as a novel therapeutic strategy for IBD.

Manipulating enzymatic activity during the gut microbiota using small molecules that selectively inhibit or enhance the metabolism of specific AAs represents an underexplored yet promising strategy for IBD treatment. The main challenges lie in developing inhibitors capable of uniformly regulating specific functions across diverse microbial species and achieving high selectivity for intended targets within the intricate gut microbiota [140]. Inhibitors that target microbial AA decarboxylase, such as tyrosine decarboxylase, have been explored for use in the treatment of Parkinson’s disease [141]. However, few small molecules that target microbial AA metabolic enzymes have been investigated for use in IBD therapy.

There is emerging evidence of a significant association between AA metabolism and IBD pathogenesis [6, 7]. Changes in the AAs and their metabolites found in serum and fecal samples have been observed in patients with IBD [16, 18, 21, 28, 31]. Nevertheless, it is unclear whether these metabolic changes are a cause or a consequence of the chronic and persistent inflammation. Importantly, these alterations have the potential to distinguish individuals with IBD from healthy controls, which could greatly improve diagnostics and treatment of IBD. To date, many microbial AA metabolites have shown promising effects in treating IBD and reducing the associated inflammation [9, 85, 86, 111]. However, many mechanisms and pathways, particularly the crosstalk between AA metabolites with intestinal immune cells, are still not fully understood. Future studies will provide more practical evidence to support microbial manipulation as an approach for the treatment of IBD, paving the way for innovative therapeutic strategies.
