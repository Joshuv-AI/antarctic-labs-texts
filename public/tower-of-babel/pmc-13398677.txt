Engineering Herbicide Cross‐Resistance in Rapeseed by Generating Stacked BnaALS Mutations via Sequential CBE and ABE8e‐SpRY Editing
Authors: Sichao Ren, Wenjing Lei, Jiaqi He, Meng Jia, Yu Liu, Jialin Fan, Yumei Wang, Li Lin, Hui Zhang, Youping Wang, Jian Wu
Published: 2026

Rapeseed ( Brassica napus ) is a globally important oil crop. In China, over 80% of production occurs in the Yangtze River Basin, where rice–rapeseed rotation is widely practiced. Effective weed control in rice can be achieved by planting imidazolinone (IMI)‐tolerant rice and applying IMI herbicides. However, the strong residual activity of IMI in soil frequently causes herbicide carryover, severely damaging subsequent non‐tolerant crops, especially rapeseed. The long half‐lives of several IMI herbicides, particularly imazethapyr (IMZT) with high carryover potential, make IMI‐tolerant rapeseed urgently needed for rotation systems.

Base editors enable precise nucleotide substitutions but are limited by the NGG protospacer‐adjacent motif (PAM) requirement. The engineered SpCas9 variant SpRY overcomes this constraint by recognizing nearly all PAMs, greatly expanding the targeting scope (Walton et al. 2020). Although SpRY‐based systems have been successfully utilized, especially in rice (Ren et al. 2021; Xu et al. 2021), their application in rapeseed has not been reported. Here, we constructed and validated a SpRY‐based PAM‐less adenine base editor (ABE, A‧T to G‧C) system in rapeseed and used it to generate IMI‐tolerant germplasm.

To evaluate rapeseed sensitivity to soil IMI residues, we treated soil with different concentrations of IMZT. Rapeseed exhibited high sensitivity: 0.1 mg ai L−1 (approximately 1 × 10−3 of the recommended field concentration) significantly inhibited growth, while 1 mg ai L−1 nearly abolished seedling development (Figure 1a,b). Substitutions at conserved acetolactate synthase (ALS) residues are known to confer resistance to ALS‐inhibiting herbicides in many weeds (http://www.weedscience.org). We previously used a cytosine base editor (CBE, C‧G to T‧A) to generate a BnaALS1 P197S rapeseed mutation, which conferred resistance to the sulfonylurea (SU) herbicide tribenuron‐methyl (TBM, Wu et al. 2020) but not to IMZT (Figure S1).

To identify mutations conferring IMI resistance, we modelled the interactions between IMZT and BnaALS1 variants corresponding to resistance‐associated mutations reported in weeds using AlphaFold3 (Abramson et al. 2024). Structural predictions indicated that substitutions at W574 (W574R/L) markedly reduce IMZT–BnaALS1 binding stability (Figure 1c, Figure S2).

Both rapeseed ALS homologues (BnaALS1 and BnaALS3) contain a conserved W574 codon TGG; thus, ABE‐mediated editing could convert this codon to CGG, generating W574R (Figure 1d). Because no suitable NGG PAM was present, we employed a SpRY‐based ABE construct, ABE8e‐SpRY (Figure 1e). To optimize editing efficiency, we compared three promoters driving TadA8e‐SpRY expression (Figure 1e). Constructs were introduced into hypocotyl explants by Agrobacterium‐mediated transformation, and PCR‐positive calli were analysed by Hi‐TOM sequencing. The AtUBI promoter achieved the highest editing efficiencies at the W574 site of BnaALS1 and BnaALS3 (66.0% and 67.9%, respectively), followed by AtRPS5A (47.0% and 34.0%), whereas 35S showed the lowest editing efficiency (Figure 1f). Considering its superior callus induction rate (10.7%), we selected the AtRPS5A:ABE8e‐SpRY construct for stable transformation and plant regeneration.

Using this construct, we obtained 68 regenerated PCR‐positive T0 plants. Sanger sequencing of target regions revealed mutations in 35 of these plants (Figure S3a). Editing efficiencies were similar at BnaALS1 (41.2%) and BnaALS3 (48.5%), with simultaneous editing at both loci observed in 38.2% of positive plants (Figure S3b). Only one homozygous double‐mutant was obtained (Figure S3), but it exhibited severe developmental defects. No homozygous double‐mutant individuals were detected in the T1 and T2 progeny of selfed double‐heterozygous lines (Figure S4), suggesting that simultaneous homozygous W574R mutations in both homologues may be lethal or severely deleterious. We assessed the resistance of the single mutants (BnaALS1W574R and BnaALS3 W574R) to ALS‐inhibiting herbicides. Both mutants exhibited moderate resistance to IMZT, showing milder injury symptoms than wild‐type J9712 at 1× and 2× the recommended field concentration. By contrast, no TBM resistance was observed (Figure S5).

To enhance IMZT resistance and generate germplasm with cross‐resistance to IMZT and TBM, we used the BnaALS1P197S BnaALS3 P197L line (Figure S6), derived from the progeny of the previously reported BnaALS1P197S mutant generated by CBE (Wu et al. 2020), as the recipient for additional W574 editing using the AtRPS5A:ABE8e‐SpRY system. Among 14 PCR‐positive plants, 10 carried W574 mutations, but all were heterozygous at least at one locus (Figure S7), and no homozygous W574R double mutants were detected in subsequent generations. Through subsequent breeding and selection, two types of stacked‐mutant lines were obtained: BnaALS1 P197S,W574R and BnaALS1 P197S,W574R BnaALS3 P197L (Figure 1g).

We evaluated herbicide resistance in four edited lines: BnaALS1P197S BnaALS3 P197L (R20), BnaALS1 W574R (R25), BnaALS1 P197S,W574R (R30), and BnaALS1 P197S,W574R BnaALS3 P197L (R40). In field trials with TBM (1×, 4×, and 8× field concentration), J9712 and R25 were highly sensitive. R30 exhibited moderate resistance, with mild leaf injury observed at 4×. By contrast, R20 and R40 displayed the strongest resistance, showing almost no visible leaf injury even at 8× (Figure 1h). Measurement of relative chlorophyll contents further confirmed these observations (Figure 1i). In pot assays with IMZT (0.5×, 1×, and 2×), J9712 was highly sensitive. R20 showed slightly improved tolerance; though significant injury remained at 1×. R25 displayed milder injury than R20, while R30 and R40 showed substantially enhanced resistance. Notably, R40 exhibited the strongest resistance, with only slight injury even at 2× (Figure 1j).

To further evaluate the tolerance of R40 to residual IMZT under field conditions, we planted the IMI‐tolerant rice cultivar ‘Jingeng 818’ and sprayed it twice with 1× IMZT at the 4–6 leaf stage at 7‐day intervals. After rice harvest, we sowed R40 and J9712 directly in the same field. J9712 growth was severely inhibited, with many plants showing narrow, curled leaves or even death, whereas R40 exhibited normal growth and strong tolerance to residual IMZT (Figure 1k, Figure S9).

We assessed off‐target effects of ABE8e‐SpRY targeting the BnaALS W574 sites using Cas‐OFFinder, finding 7553 putative loci with up to five mismatches. Whole‐genome resequencing (~40×) of two R40 plants identified five putative A‐to‐G substitutions at these loci, but none were confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Figure S8). Furthermore, no significant differences in agronomic traits were observed between edited lines and J9712 (Table S2).

In conclusion, using AlphaFold3‐based structural modelling, we identified W574R as a key mutation conferring IMI resistance in rapeseed, providing a rational target for precise base editing. We established and validated a SpRY‐based ABE system in rapeseed, whose broad PAM compatibility greatly expands the genome‐editing scope in this crop. By combining CBE and ABE8e editing, which enable modification of the P197 and W574 sites, respectively, we generated the BnaALS stacked‐mutant germplasm R40, with a precisely engineered allele configuration that is difficult to obtain through conventional mutagenesis breeding and confers stable cross‐resistance to SU and IMI. This germplasm offers a practical strategy for SU‐based in‐season weed management in rapeseed and for mitigating yield penalties caused by IMI carryover from preceding IMI‐tolerant crops in rotation systems (Figure 1l).
