Diversity and perceptions about side effects of medicinal plants used by herbalists to treat gastrointestinal diseases in Sironko District, Eastern Uganda
Authors: Abdul Walusansa, Robert Natoli, Hussein. M. Kafeero, Muhammad Lubowa, Marjorie Sarah Kabuye Batiibwe, Jamilu. E. Ssenku
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe escalation of gastrointestinal tract (GIT) illnesses is now a major global threat, with countries like Uganda having a prevalence of over 31 %. Herbal medicines (HM) are widely used to treat GIT illnesses in many low-resource settings including Sironko District in Eastern Uganda, but their safety remains a grave concern because they often contain bioactive phytochemicals that may cause harmful side effects. In Sironko, herbalists commonly rely on indigenous knowledge rather than formal pharmacological training when prescribing HM hence the potential for adverse reactions linked to toxic phytocompounds is substantial, warranting comprehensive scientific investigation.ObjectiveTo explore plant species used to treat GIT illnesses and perceptions of their adverse effects in Sironko District, to inform safer herbal medicine use.MethodsA sample of 70 herbalists was subjected to an ethnobotanical survey using pre-validated semi-structured questionnaires to profile plant species primarily used against GIT illnesses plus awareness and perceptions about the associated side effects. Data were analyzed with descriptive and inferential statistics using STATA version-15.0. Graphs were plotted with GraphPad Prism® version 9.0.0.ResultsA total of 80 plants species used against GIT infections were documented, mainly in families; Asteraceae and Euphorbiaceae, indicated for treatment and prevention of 24 gastrointestinal illnesses, mostly diarrhea (18.94%), ulcers (11.81%), and stomachaches (8.52%), plus 39 ailments affecting other body systems. Informant consensus factors were high for all disease categories (≥ 0.630), showing homogeneity of ethnomedicinal knowledge. Chenopodium opulifolium, Tithonia diversifolia and Senna didymobotrya were reported to pose the greatest number of side effects, including headache and insomnia. The 70% of participants were unaware that HM can be toxic, 57% were certain that HM do not have side effects, yet those that opposed the need for urgent action against HM adversity were significantly more than participants who perceived it as vital (χ2, p < 0.0001).ConclusionThere is a high diversity of plant species used to treat primarily GIT illnesses in Sironko, but many potentially stimulate life-threatening adverse effects such as severe headache and vomiting. These results highlight a need for considerable investment in herbal medicine safety to leverage its optimal use in health promotion and economic development.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal tract diseases remain a major health problem worldwide, that is, GIT illnesses are among the top ten causes of global mortality and morbidity [1]. For example, peptic ulcers are prevalent in 5–10% of the global population and are responsible for 10% of the global annual mortality [2], while diarrhea causes over 1.8 million global deaths each year [3]. In some African countries like Ghana and Nigeria, gastrointestinal tract (GIT) diseases have a prevalence of 54–57% [4]. In Uganda, the prevalence of GIT diseases like diarrhea rose from 9.6 to 20.5% between 2014 and 2021 but the current prevalence stands at 31% [5, 6]. Despite the essential roles played by conventional drugs toward the aversion of GIT diseases in Uganda and other parts of the world, several medicinal plants are increasingly used to treat GIT disorders, such as peptic ulcers, diarrhea, dental diseases and cancers among others [7–9]. Some of the phyto-molecules reported to possess medicinal activity against GIT illness include alkaloids, flavonoids, tannins, and saponins among others [10]. However, these molecules if consumed in supratherapeutic doses can cause life-threatening adversities. Community preference for medicinal plants over conventional drugs is influenced by several factors, including the high cost and limited accessibility of modern medicines, as well as the emergence of pathogen resistance to conventional drugs, which can compromise clinical treatment outcomes [11]. The global consumption of herbal medicine stands at 50–95% [12]. In Africa, the prevalence of use of traditional herbal medicine for primary healthcare is estimated at 80% [13, 14]. In the East African community countries, over 70% of the people are reported to depend on herbal medicine (HM), which is used to treat a wide range of health complications, from acute medical emergencies like snakebite envenomation to chronic conditions like infertility, kidney disease, cancer, diabetes, ulcers, gastrointestinal illnesses, and symptoms related to HIV/AIDS [15, 16].

In Uganda, an estimated 60% of the population is reported to use HM, and the consumption is typically higher in rural areas [17]. This is partly because of the lack of access to healthcare facilities, the high cost and inadequacy of medical supplies, the scarcity and low motivation of medical professionals, and the general acceptance of traditional medicines as having fewer side effects than prescription medications in rural settings [17–21]. The mounting consumption of medicinal plants necessitates adequate profiling of the species to support their sustainable utilization, and conservation of the associated traditional ethnomedicinal knowledge, especially in the face of uncontrolled harvesting, population pressure, and climate change, among other threats [22]. Medicinal plants such as Vernonia amygdalina Del., Carica papaya L, Psidium guajava L. are used to treat GIT diseases in different parts of Uganda including Sironko District [9]. In this district, local communities use HM by only applying indigenous knowledge but not technical pharmacological expertise. This puts the herbal medicine users at risk of adverse effects caused by unknown toxic plant compounds. Risks such as cancers, and liver damage have been linked to herbal medicine toxicity in Uganda but the evidence is scarce and scanty for the majority of the commonly used medicinal plant species [23].

The health problems that are potentially attributable to HM toxicity, such as cancers and complications of the liver remain some of the leading causes of community suffering, monetary expenditures, death, and hospitalization in Uganda [24, 25]. For example, Uganda is ranked 14th out of the nations with the highest rates of cancers [26], and Eastern Uganda is one the most affected areas in the country [21, 27]. Since most stakeholders consider HM to be safe and effective, without considerable knowledge of potential deadly adversities [15, 28], advancing HM quality requires extensive research on its safety concerns. Such research is not only essential in safeguarding local communities that regularly consume medicinal plants, but also in supporting Uganda’s traditional herbal medicine industry to march international quality standards and hence fit into the highly competitive global market. So, the aim of this study was to document plants primarily used against GIT illnesses in addition to other common ailments that are prevalent in Sironko District, and to understand the knowledge gaps, misconceptions, and adverse risk perceptions on these plants among herbalists. The research was urgently necessary because the adverse effects of medicinal plants, such as cancers, liver complications, deaths are increasingly manifesting in Eastern Uganda where Sironko is located, in the face of widespread dependency on herbal medicine for primary healthcare [21]. These side effects pose severe threats to societal wellbeing and economic development at both the national and household levels, and insufficiency of research on the safety of commonly consumed medicinal plant species deters the urgently desired upgrading of the herbal medicine sector to attain international standards that can rhyme with the demands of the global market.

Methods and materials

Study sites and settings

The study was conducted in 10 randomly selected villages in Sironko District (Fig. 1), from June 2025 and October 2025. The ten remote villages selected possess all the four relevant land use covers (trees, shrubs, grasslands, and croplands). The communities in these villages face limited access to formal healthcare infrastructure, leading to widespread herbal medicine practices [21]. Sironko District is located in Bugishu sub-region in Eastern Uganda, 39 km from Mbale City, the largest city in this sub-region, and 292 km from Kampala, the major capital and commercial city of Uganda [20]. The district harbors a part of the herbal medicine-rich Mt. Elgon Forest National Park. Sironko District stretches over the area between 34° 10′ 1.985'' longitude, 1°5′ 33.483" latitude, and 34°31′ 46.179" longitude, 1°17′ 26.257" latitude (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Map of the study site

Climate

Sironko District experiences a bimodal type of rainfall with the heaviest rainy season faced in April and the other in August. The district receives an average rainfall of 477.07 mm (18.78 inches) precipitation, and has 312.72 rainy days and an average temperature of 21 °C [29, 30].

Vegetation and soils

Natural vegetation remains in the higher elevation areas, most of which fall within the Mt. Elgon Forest National Park. In the higher altitudes, the natural vegetation changes from montane, to grassland bamboo, then heath, and moorland in that order. The supra-tropical forests on the mountain are dominated by Aningeria adolfi-friederici (Engl.) Robyns & Gilbert, Podocarpus latifolius (Thunb.) R.Br. ex Mirb., Olea hochstetteri Baker, and Prunus africana (Hook.f.) Kalkman [31]. Mixed stands of Bambusa vulgaris Schrad. ex J.C.Wendl. occur at elevations of about 2500–3000 m, gradually transitioning into open woodland dominated by Hagenia abyssinica (Bruce) J.F.Gmel. and Hypericum revolutum Vahl. The heath zone between 3000 and 3500 m is characterized by giant heath vegetation, particularly Erica arborea L., with grassy swards of Festuca arundinacea Schreb. The Afro-alpine region extends from approximately 3500 m to 4321 m above sea level and is dominated by Senecio elgonensis (S.Moore) B.Nord. [31]. The Pre-Cambrian and the Cainozoic rock systems are the major formations underlying the Bugisu sub-region’s landscape. The pre-Cambrian rock system is mainly granitic or high to medium metamorphosed formations, consisting of undifferentiated gneisses and elements of partly granitic and metamorphosed formations [32]. Cainozoic formations consist of Pleistocene to recent sediment, alluvium, black soils and moraines. The impermeable nature of these rocks makes most of the areas adjacent to the Mount Elgon Park susceptible to landslides in the rainy seasons of the year [32]. The soils are majorly clay in the highlands, clay-loam in mid-altitude areas, and sandy in the lowlands and valleys.

Study approach

A cross-sectional study design was used to conduct an ethnobotanical survey as published previously [7, 33]. The pretested, semi-structured questionnaires were administered to the participants by the research team in English and Lumasaba languages. For each medicinal plant species, the participants mentioned, they were asked to state the local name mode of administration, diseases treated, plant organ used, side effects, and the perceptions associated with the adverse effects arising from consumption of these plants.

Study population and sampling

The natives in Sironko District are Bagisu, the 8th largest ethnic group in Uganda, and live a peasant life growing coffee [34]. Budadiri East Constituency where this study was conducted has a population of 125,882 people, of which 63,252 are males representing 50.2% and 62,630 are females representing 49.8%. The Bagisu in eastern Uganda are 1,646,904 and in Sironko District, 274,300 [35]. Sironko being a rural district with considerably poor health infrastructure, there is widespread community dependency on herbal medicine for primary healthcare [36].

Hence, the minimum required sample was 65, but a sample size of 70 herbalists was adopted, to be able to recruit at least 7 prominent herbalists from the 10 villages.

Seventy Traditional Medicine Practitioners (TMP) were selected using the snowball sampling technique. First, the index participants at each village were identified under the guidance of local council chairpersons. These participants then supported the identification of other prominent fellow TMPs in the respective villages to potentiate the network sampling process. The snowball sampling approach was adopted in this study because the desired participants (herbalists) did not have a definite distribution pattern. The participants were subjected to pretested, semi-structured questionnaires either in English or translated into Lumasaba. For each medicinal plant species, the respondents were asked to provide the local name, GIT infections treated, and the perceived / self-reported adverse effects in addition to other relevant ethnobotanical information.

Plant collection and identification

The voucher specimens of all the medicinal plant species that were mentioned by the herbalists for the treatment of GIT diseases were collected and prepared following the Good Agricultural and Collection Practices (GACPs) [39]. That is, relevant plants were found in their habitats with the guidance of the participating herbalists, relevant plant organs/samples were harvested, pressed, and immediately transported to the herbarium. The plant samples were collected by the authors and Mr. Rwabulindooli Protase, plant a taxonomist at Makerere university herbarium. The samples were identified up to species level by a plant taxonomist in the herbarium at Makerere University. The plant species names were verified and authenticated using a standard database at https://www.theplantlist.org, accessed on 20th August 2025, and the botanical families were checked against Angiosperm Phylogeny Group IV (https://www.mobot.org/mobot/research/apweb/).

Ethnobotanical data analysis

Relative frequency of citation (RFC)

Relative frequency of citation (RFC) for each plant species was calculated to find the proportion of herbalists who thought that the particular plant species was useful for treating GIT diseases and other diseases. The RFC values are between 0 and 1, with 1 signifying the greatest degree of agreement among respondents regarding the application of that species to treat a specific illness. The informant consensus factor

The diseases treated using herbal medicines documented in this research were categorized basing on the eight human body systems they affect, cause of disease, and the medication characteristics of the people of Sironko District. The informant consensus factor (ICF) was then calculated to evaluate the homogeneity of the ethnomedicinal knowledge among the herbalists using the following formula [40]:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$IFC = (Nur - Nt)/(Nur - 1)$$\end{document}IFC=(Nur-Nt)/(Nur-1)where Nur is the total number of plant-use reports (citations), for a disease category and Nt is the total number of plant species used for that ailment category.

Perception-based insights and awareness on toxicity and adverse effects of the medicinal plants documented

Pre-tested, researcher administered questionnaires were used to collect data about the perceptions of the herbalists on the adverse effects of the plants they use to treat clients and even themselves. The questionnaires contained variables focused on the social-demographic characteristics of herbalists, and their awareness on toxicity and side effects of medicinal plants. Overall, the awareness about the side effects of medicinal plants was evaluated as the percentage ratio of the total citations of at least one side effect to the overall sum of citations of side effects [41]. Pretesting of the questionnaires was done to ascertain validity and reliability, by following already published protocols [42].

Data analysis plan

The data were analyzed using descriptive and inferential statistics, such as Chi-square, frequencies, and percentages and presented as tables and graphs. STATA version-15.0 software was used to analyze the data while GraphPad Prism® version 9.0.0 was used to plot the graphs (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA). Evaluation of the ethnopharmacological data was done using two ethnobotanical indices; relative frequency of citations (RFC) and the informant consensus factor (ICF).

Results

Socio-demographic profile of participants

The majority (55.7%), of the respondents in this study were females, while males were 44.3% (N = 31). Over three-quarters (67.7%) of the participants were youths, while two participants (2.9%) were elderly. Regarding education status, the majority (41.4%) had attained primary education, while the minority (4.6%) had tertiary education. The participants who had practiced traditional medicine for a duration between 5 and 15 years constituted the majority (72.3%). Diversity of medicinal plants used for the treatment of gastrointestinal diseases in Sironko District

A total of 80 medicinal plant species belonging to 25 botanical families and 72 genera used in the management of GIT diseases were documented in the herbalist community surveyed (Table 2). Asteraceae was the most dominant family (22.5%), followed by Euphorbiaceae (8.8%), Lamiaceae and Verbenaceae (7.5% each). The majority of the plant species were used to treat Ulcers (37.5%). This was followed by diarrhea (36.3%), and stomachache (22.5%), among other GIT infections. In addition to the GIT complications, some of the plants species were in addition reported to treat several other ailments (Table 3). Herbs (40.5%), were the most dominantly cited plants species while creepers were the minority. Ocimum gratissimum L. was the most frequently cited plant species in treatment of GIT infections with a relative frequency of citation (RFC) of 0.87. This was followed by Conyza pyrrhopappa Sch.Bip. ex A. Rich (RFC = 0.84), and Searsia pyroides (Burch.) Moffett (RFC = 0.79). Modes of preparation and administration

All the plant species recorded (100%) were administered orally in four main forms, that is, decoctions (72.5%), infusions (16.3%), fresh materials chewed (8.7%), and powders licked (2.5%). Fresh plant leaves were the major plant part used (66.3%) followed by the roots (27.5%) (Table 2). There were similarities and disparities in the use of plant species in the different villages of Sironko District studied.

Acquisition of medicinal plants and traditional knowledge practices among herbalists treating gastrointestinal diseases in Sironko District.

Roadside habitats and home farms were most cited sources of the medicinal plants, of which each was cited by all the seventy participants (N = 70, 100%). These were followed by forests (N = 16, 22.9%); and markets (N = 10, 14.3%). Wetlands were the least cited source of medicinal plant species (N = 4, 5.7%). The majority of the herbalists (N = 66, 94.3%) reported that they acquired traditional medicinal knowledge through inheritance from their parents, grandparents or other elderly family members. Disease categories treated and the informant consensus factor (ICF)

All the plant species documented in this study were reported to treat at least one GIT infection since these ailments constituted the major focus of the study. However, owing to the multitherapeutic properties of herbal plants, these species were also stated to be used in addressing diseases that affect other systems, and these included malaria, cough, and dystocia labor among others (Table 3). The majority of the herbalists that participated in this study believed in spiritualism and witchcraft. Therefore, some plants such as Chenopodium opulifolium Koch & Ziz, and Tagetes minuta L. were reported to be used in addressing complications related to spiritualism and witchcraft. Such complications included bad luck, evil spirit possession, and swollen legs perceived to be linked to bewitching, among others.

Perception-based insights of herbalists about toxicity and side effects of plants used against GIT diseases in Sironko District

With regard to the perceptions about toxicity and side effects of herbal medicines, only nine (12.9%) of the herbalists responded positively when asked if the threat of toxicity and negative effects of medicinal plants should be taken as a matter of critical concern in the traditional medicine industry, while 61 (87.1%) said no. The number of herbalists that opposed the need for critical concern and action against the threat of toxicity and adverse effects of herbal medicines was significantly higher than those who perceived it as a vital necessity (χ2 = 24.455, p < 0.0001, 95% CI = 39.97 to 86.28%). When asked to mention the perceived side effects of each of the medicinal plant species they cited during this research, the most dominant response from the herbalists was, “Plants lack side effects” (N = 40, 57.1%), followed by, “I do not know” (N = 9, 12.9%). Particularly, the most prominent side effect was vomiting (N = 7, 10%), followed by constipation (N = 5, 7.1%) and insomnia (N = 4, 5.7%) while the least cited were; cancer, somnolence, shivering, nervousness and belching (N = 1, 1.4% each) (Fig. 3). Overall, the awareness about the side effects of medicinal plants was evaluated as the percentage ratio of the total citations of at least one side effect (N = 41), to the overall sum of citations of side effects (N = 90) (Fig. 3). Discussion

Medicinal plant diversity and pharmacological implications

The study documented 80 plant species used against gastrointestinal (GIT) illnesses in Sironko District. Many of the species were also used to resolve 36 additional ailments affecting other body systems, demonstrating the multitherapeutic role of medicinal plants play in promoting health and wellbeing of rural Ugandan communities. The dominance of families such as Fabaceae, Myricaceae, and Asteraceae aligns with earlier reported ethnobotanical patterns across Africa, where these large families provide abundant, accessible species [19]. Their prominence reflects both ecological availability and cultural familiarity, reinforcing the centrality of herbal medicine in communities with limited access to the formal healthcare infrastructure. Plant selection is also likely influenced by sociocultural factors such as intergenerational knowledge transfer, local healing traditions, and community trust in familiar plant species, which shape perceptions of efficacy and reinforce the continued reliance on ethnomedicinal resources. Although the number of species reported here is lower than those in broader ethnobotanical surveys previously conducted in Eastern Uganda; the narrower focus on GIT illnesses in the current study partly explains this difference [7, 40, 89–91]. Also, the participants in the current study may have withheld some trade-sensitive ethnobotanical knowledge to protect livelihoods hence narrowing the spectrum of plant species reported. In this research, the identification of species not previously reported for GIT infection management, such as those in Phytolaccaceae, highlights the unique local knowledge base in Sironko. High informant consensus factors (ICF ≥ 0.630) indicate a shared and stable ethnomedical tradition shaped by experience accumulated over a long time [10]. That is, the high informant consensus factors show a shared body of ethnomedical knowledge or widespread agreement among the herbalists on most of the species documented in this research, suggesting perceived reliability of these remedies. However, consensus does not equate to safety or pharmacological validity, especially where potentially toxic species are widely consumed.

The predominance of decoctions (72.5%) and infusions (16.3%) reflects common African ethnopharmacological practices aimed at extracting potent phytochemicals. Decoctions are the preparations obtained by boiling plant materials in water while infusions are prepared by simply steeping plant organs in hot water to obtain. Boiling, in the case of decoctions, enhances the extraction in high amounts, of bioactive compounds including alkaloids, tannins, and saponins, which are associated with anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial properties [92], yet elevated levels of these compounds also underlie many of the adverse effects reported in the current study. The study also reports heavy reliance on leaf material (66.3%). This supports sustainable utilization. However, the considerable use of roots (27.5%) raises conservation concerns since root harvesting commonly leads to significant plant mortality. Remarkably, a key concern emerging from this study is the report of simultaneous occurrence of severe hazardous side effects associated with several plant species, which contradict the lifesaving traditional therapeutic intentions of the traditional medicine enterprise. Several species used to treat ulcers and diarrhea in Sironko have been documented to possess strong laxative or irritant properties, which can worsen the same conditions they are proposed to treat [92–95]. Senna didymobotrya, which in the current study, was widely suggested treat gastric ulcers, was also reported to induce a relatively broad range of side effects consistent with anthraquinone-driven purgation (laxative-induced diarrhea) [96, 97]. Still, Chenopodium opulifolium and Tithonia diversifolia which were frequently cited for managing several GIT complications in the current study were consequently cited to stimulate vomiting, dizziness, and neurological symptoms. These contradictions highlight the pharmacological hazards of relying solely on indigenous knowledge, especially where toxicity is poorly understood and dosage is unstandardized.

Perceptions of toxicity and implications for vulnerable populations

This study reveals a profound underestimation of herbal toxicity among the practitioners: 70% were not aware that medicinal plants can be toxic, and 57% believed plants have no side effects. Even more critically, ≥ 87% perceived the issue of herbal medicine toxicity as a matter which does not deserve critical concern. This widespread misconception augments the herbal toxicity risk in Sironko and similar settings especially where formal healthcare access is limited. Vulnerable groups frequently depending on medicinal plants for routine primary healthcare, including children, pregnant women, the elderly, and people with chronic illnesses are prone to greater danger. For example, vomiting, which is one of the side effects frequently reported in this study, can impede medication adherence and nutritional intake in individuals with HIV/AIDS [97], while constipation and CNS-related effects (somnolence, insomnia) may worsen underlying conditions. In settings where herbalists are primary care providers, such misperceptions may therefore not only lead to cumulative herbal toxicity, and disregarded adverse events, but also impaired formal medical care outcomes. Herbalists’ reliance on radios, clients, and social media for toxicity information reflects both opportunity and risk. This revealed that radios were a dominant source of their awareness, together with social media is considerably mired by lack of information authentication and quality control [98]. This creates an environment where misinformation can spread unchecked, reinforcing unsafe traditional medical practices.

Actions needed to ensure safer traditional medical practices

Despite limited awareness, participants strongly supported scientific research as the preferred strategy for reducing adverse effects. This reflects increasing awareness among these traditional practitioners that professional scientific research is vital for advancing Uganda’s traditional medicine. Their emphasis on hygiene, professional conduct, and community education underscores the need for: toxicological screening of the high-risk medicinal plant species, standardization of preparation and dosing guidelines, pharmacovigilance systems for herbal products, and ultimate systematic integration of herbalists into formal health referral pathways. Structural barriers, including regulatory gaps, mistrust, and limited access to research institutions remain marginally acknowledged, but addressing them is essential for improving safety while preserving traditional medicinal knowledge and cultural heritage.

Overall, this study highlights the dual character of traditional medicinal plant use in Sironko District: a rich and culturally embedded ethnomedical system that provides essential primary healthcare, yet one that is accompanied by substantial safety uncertainties. The documentation of 80 medicinal plant species and the high informant consensus factors demonstrate a well-established body of indigenous knowledge supporting the treatment of gastrointestinal diseases. However, the simultaneous reporting of severe adverse effects, the widespread underestimation of herbal toxicity among practitioners, and the reliance on non-verified sources of toxicity information reveal important safety gaps within this therapeutic system. These findings collectively suggest that while medicinal plants remain indispensable to rural healthcare delivery, their continued use without adequate toxicological evaluation, dosage standardization, and practitioner awareness may pose risks, particularly to vulnerable populations. Therefore, strengthening the evidence base through pharmacological and toxicological research, improving knowledge exchange between scientists and herbal practitioners, and establishing supportive regulatory and pharmacovigilance frameworks are essential steps towards ensuring that the therapeutic potential of traditional medicinal plants can be harnessed safely and sustainably within Uganda’s broader healthcare system.

Conclusion

Herbalists in Sironko District rely on 80 plant species for gastrointestinal illnesses, yet many of these remedies are capable of causing significant adverse effects due to the presence of high levels of various active ingredients. High consensus in plant use contrasts sharply with low awareness of toxicity, creating substantial risks especially for vulnerable populations that consume these remedies. The findings underscore the urgent need for regulatory oversight, toxicological research, and targeted health-education interventions to ensure safer traditional-medicine practices that protect the community.

Recommendations

Based on the above findings which point to the pragmatic and evolving understanding of herbal medicine safety among herbalists, it would be vital to carry out further comprehensive research to ascertain the efficacy, safety, and active ingredients in the medicinal plants, plus the associated traditional healing practices. Also, stakeholders in the traditional medicine value chain need to be sensitized to advance their evolving knowledge on herbal medicine toxicity, how to mitigate it, and they should be supported to embrace it.

Limitations of the study

The study involved a sample of herbalists to identify the medicinal plants they use to treat their clients and the associated side effects which the herbal consumers had persistently reported to the participant. To avoid information authentication and accuracy related challenges, it would have been vital to include herbal medicine consumers (individuals who go to these herbalists for treatment), to obtain firsthand information on side effects, however, this was deterred by limited access to those herbal users. That is, the clients who visit these herbalists for treatment are mobile, they hardly disclose the details of their home locations and contacts, and their medical engagements with the herbalists are a confidential matter. On the other hand, the information obtained from the herbalists is largely trustable since they also frequently consume the same plants hence, they face the same adversities.
