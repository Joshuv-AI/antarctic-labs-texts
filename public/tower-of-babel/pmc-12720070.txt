Restoring the core acute medical unit (AMU) function – a quality improvement initiative from a district general hospital
Authors: Anika Wijewardane, Philip Dyer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe acute medical unit (AMU) is a specialist area, focused on delivering timely, effective acute care for adults with acute medical illness. AMUs were first established to provide timely physician review, risk stratification and multidisciplinary management in the first 72 h. They are designed to improve patient care, prevent unnecessary admissions and reduce morbidity and mortality.However, national benchmarking and local audit data showed that core acute medicine processes at Frimley Park Hospital’s AMU had drifted from these standards. A structured quality improvement programme was therefore implemented to restore AMU function and align operational processes and workforce to national guidance and national Getting it Right First Time (GIRFT) principles. Real-time oversight, standardised multidisciplinary team board rounds and rota alignment to peak demand, halved AMU length of stay, doubled daily discharges and increased emergency department-to-AMU transfers. These improvements were associated with enhanced hospital-wide throughput and more effective early acute care.Keywords: Flow, Acute Medical Unit, GIRFT

Introduction

Frimley Park Hospital (FPH), part of Frimley Health NHS Foundation Trust (FHFT), is a large district general hospital in Surrey serving a core population of around 900,000 across the trust. It provides both acute and other specialist services, including coronary percutaneous primary intervention and hyper-acute stroke care, to a diverse local population.

In early 2023, FHFT was experiencing significant pressures across its urgent and emergency care (UEC) system. The UEC system encompasses the network of services providing rapid assessment and treatment for patients requiring same-day or immediate hospital-level care, including the emergency department (ED), same-day emergency care (SDEC), ambulance services and the acute medical unit (AMU). The 4 h standard5 (the national performance metric used across NHS organisations, measuring the percentage of patients who are admitted, transferred or discharged from the ED within 4 h of arrival) was only 35%, highlighting severe constraints on flow of patients efficiently through the hospital.

The AMU, too, had drifted significantly from national best practice. Only 30% of acute medical admissions were admitted through the AMU, daily discharges home equated to just 10% of AMU bed capacity per day, and the average length of stay (LoS) exceeded 3 days. Consultant ward rounds frequently extended into the afternoon, and limited coordination across the multidisciplinary team (MDT) meant that management plans were often deferred until the following day (data derived from FPH internal audit, March 2023; unpublished).

National standards2 define a well-functioning AMU as one that delivers timely senior clinical review, robust multidisciplinary assessment and structured operational processes.1, 2, 3 The improvement work described in this paper sought to realign AMU operations with these established standards through strengthened processes and workforce redesign.

Methodology

A structured test of change was launched, supported by the trust’s improvement team. An initial review revealed limited data availability on acute medical patient flow (the movement of patients through the hospital), from the presentation in ED to assessment, admission, treatment and discharge. Effective flow ensures that hospital capacity is used optimally, and delays are minimised across the UEC system. A bespoke dashboard was therefore developed to track key metrics across the acute pathway, including ED arrival time, referral to medicine time, admission destination and AMU LoS, highlighting previously unseen system inefficiencies.

An intervention was proposed, focusing on two principal areas:

1.Implementation of a daily ‘command-and-control’ board round on AMU at 11:30am, attended by the consultant acute physician, nurse in charge of AMU, the occupational therapist, physiotherapist, pharmacist, discharge coordinator, and a member of the administrative team and site management team. The aim was to come together to ‘drive today’s work today’.

A structured 30-min format was developed to efficiently discuss all 42 inpatients. Each case was presented using the bespoke, locally standardised format, designed to ensure consistency and clarity around key clinical and operational details: diagnosis, MDT plan, estimated discharge date, and the next appropriate care destination – whether the patient was to remain on AMU, transfer to another ward, or discharge home. Real-time input from the site management team enabled efficient bed allocation, while early involvement of pharmacy, therapy, and discharge teams supported safe and timely discharge planning.

Quantitative metrics, including LoS and discharge rates, were tracked through internal dashboards, though formal statistical analysis was not performed. Staff experience was evaluated using structured surveys, which provided valuable insights despite the absence of validated tools.

Conclusion

National standards define a well-functioning AMU as one that delivers early senior clinical review, robust multidisciplinary assessment and structured operational processes.1, 2, 3 At Frimley Park Hospital, realigning AMU with these established standards led to measurable improvements in efficiency, patient flow into AMU and length of stay (LoS). LoS decreased both for patients managed entirely within the AMU and for those transferred to other wards after initial care, highlighting the wider impact of effective acute-phase management on overall hospital stay.

In the context of sustained pressure across UEC, embedding multidisciplinary leadership and daily operational oversight proved critical to achieving and maintaining improvement, principles strongly supported by national standards and guidance.2,3 While innovation in new models of care is valuable, reinforcing the established principles of acute medicine remains essential.6, 7, 8

Although formal case-mix adjustment was not undertaken, the GIRFT Case Mix Index (GAMI) and average age of adult medical admissions9 remained stable throughout, suggesting that observed gains were not primarily driven by variation in patient complexity or demographics. The work was undertaken in a single hospital, but the challenges addressed are common across UK AMUs.4 The principles underpinning this improvement are likely transferable, providing a practical framework for other hospitals seeking to restore AMU function and strengthen early acute care, although may require adaptation to local infrastructure, digital capability and workforce configuration.

Overall, this quality improvement aligns closely with national recommendations,2,3 demonstrating that adherence to established AMU standards can deliver tangible clinical and operational benefits. Strengthening and investing in the AMU function should be a strategic priority for hospitals aiming to improve patient outcomes, streamline patient flow and build a more resilient urgent and emergency care system.
