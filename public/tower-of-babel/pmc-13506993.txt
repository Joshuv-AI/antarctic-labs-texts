Comment on: “Surgical Timing and Neurological Outcomes in Traumatic Central Cord Syndrome: A Single-Center Retrospective Cohort Study”
Authors: Vint Seng Lee, Robin Pillay, Ji Min Ling, A Aravin Kumar
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the article by Crasta et al 1 evaluating the relationship between surgical timing and neurological outcomes in acute traumatic central cord syndrome (ATCCS). The authors should be commended for an elegant study and for contributing to a pertinent research question regarding the timing of surgery in ATCCS, 2 which still fosters debate. Whilst these findings are a crucial addition to the body of knowledge, we must enquire more about the outcome measure that was utilized and comment on the heterogeneity of outcomes measures amongst ATCCS studies.

The authors used an outcome measure which was a summation of 5 muscle groups in each extremity, which was then divided by 5, resulting in score of 0-5 for each limb. This novel motor outcome is analogous to the upper extremity and lower extremity motor scores, which are part of the American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) Motor Score (AMS). The AMS is used widely in spinal cord injury and ATCCS studies as the motor outcome measure of choice. 3 With a larger range of values to describe the extent of motor impairment, from 0-25 for each motor extremity, AMS provides a finer scale for measuring the disease severity, with granularity better allowing for precision in reliably detecting clinical change. 4 There is a role for standardisation for outcome measures across various studies, allowing for common comparison across different populations and aiding future meta-analyses. Any proposed new outcome measure should be compared to AMS, to demonstrate its superiority.

Another challenge within the ATCCS literature is the definition of significant improvement. It is known that patients with ATCCS will have some form of motor improvement even without surgical intervention. 5 It is therefore imperative to distinguish between motor improvement that is significant, with that which is par for the disease course. This is essential to determine the efficacy of surgery, and particularly to assess the benefits of early and ultra-early surgery. We have previously proposed a threshold of 20% improvement of the AMS on admission 6 to be significant motor improvement, in the absence of minimal clinically important difference (MCID) values for ATCCS. Future studies in this space should work towards adopting a common threshold for significant recovery, once again to allow for comparison between cohorts, and to speak a common language.

Finally, focusing on motor recovery alone can lead to practitioners neglecting the functional deficits which hinder patients’ quality of life. In this regard, functional outcomes such as the spinal cord independence measure (SCIM) and functional independence measure (FIM) are useful to assess the functional impact of ATCCS and recovery. With the advent of patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), there are golden opportunities to quantify much more of patients’ functional recovery, and tailor our interventions with that in mind. 7

Future ATCCS studies should not stop exploring novel outcome measures, but should do so whilst retaining commonly used motor outcomes, such as AMS, and functional outcomes. This will facilitate effective comparison and consolidation of data which will eventually allow for true treatment effects to be ascertained.
