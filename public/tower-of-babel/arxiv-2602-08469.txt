Classifying the simplest Bell inequalities beyond qubits and their applications towards self-testing
Palash Pandya, Shubhayan Sarkar, Remigiusz Augusiak

Abstract
Bell inequalities reveal the fundamentally nonlocal character of quantum mechanics. In this regard, one of the interesting problems is to explore all possible Bell inequalities that demonstrate a gap between local and nonlocal quantum behaviour. This is useful for the geometric characterisation of the set of nonlocal correlations achievable within quantum theory. Moreover, it provides a systematic way to construct Bell inequalities that are tailored to specific quantum information processing tasks. This characterisation is well understood in the simplest ( 2 , 2 , 2 ) (2,2,2) scenario, namely two parties performing two binary outcome measurements. However, beyond this setting, relatively few Bell inequalities are known, and the situation becomes particularly scarce in scenarios involving a greater number of outcomes. Here, we consider the ( 2 , 2 , 3 ) (2,2,3) scenario, or two parties performing two three-outcome measurements, and characterise all Bell inequalities that can arise from the simplest sum-of-squares decomposition and are maximally violated by the maximally entangled state of local dimension three. We then utilise them to self-test this state, along with a class of three-outcome measurements.



Nonlocality and entanglement are the two most fundamental aspects of Quantum theory, and as such are the most studied topics. It is a well-known fact, first established by Bell
3
, that the correlations generated by local measurements on an entangled system cannot always be reproduced by a classical theory.
This non-reproducibility is a key ingredient in many applications bounding the length of the secret key in quantum key distribution, certifying the Hilbert space dimensions, randomness generation, secret sharing, etc.
1
;
12
;
4
;
7
. Therefore, Bell inequalities that certify nonlocal correlations and distinguish them from classical are of utmost interest.
Finding Bell inequalities for quantum systems and measurements of interest is in general a hard task, and apart from a few special cases, the Bell inequalities are not known. The task of finding multipartite Bell inequalities is especially difficult.
One of the most important implications of Bell inequality violation is certifying that the correlations generated by the measurement devices of the observers are nonlocal. This certification, aptly called device-independent (DI) certification, is the cornerstone of secure quantum key distribution
1
. While device-independent certification reveals that the correlations produced are nonlocal, there is still an uncertainty in what is the actual quantum state shared between the observers and the measurements performed by them on their local subsystems.

Though different sets of quantum systems and local measurements can result in the same nonlocal correlations and thus the same Bell inequality violation or ’Bell score’, maximal violation of a Bell inequality can, in almost all cases, be attributed uniquely to a quantum state and a set of measurements. This is called the ’Self-testing’ property and is a stricter form of device independence, where in addition to certifying nonlocal correlations, we also certify the underlying quantum state (and local measurements)
9
.
The simplest system that admits the property of self-testing is the maximally entangled state of two qubits with the now ubiquitous Clauser-Horne-Shimony-Holt (CHSH) inequality with two measurements and two outcomes per observer
13
. Since then, a complete characterisation of the Self-Tests for the maximally entangled state has been done
10
;
11
;
20
. In fact, in
5
, the authors proved that all entangled pure bipartite states can be self-tested. The methods developed for qubits were then employed for self-testing, in addition to multipartite qubit states, higher dimensional qudit systems as well, by describing the shared system locally as qubits
21
;
5
;
6
;
18
.

In contrast to this, recent research has focused more on letting go of the local qubit formalism and developing the framework for self-testing in higher local dimensions by engaging the more complex operator machinery that goes with it, particularly, by expressing local measurements using the Weyl-Heisenberg formalism
8
;
14
;
15
.
A powerful tool to derive algebraic constraints for the given Bell inequality to self-test the shared quantum state and the local measurements is the
Sum-of-Squares
(SOS) decomposition
19
. By construction, the SOS decomposition of the Bell operator provides the quantum or Tsirelson bound of the Bell inequality. In
2
, this method is put forth formally as a way to design Bell inequalities tailored to the state. They compare the variational method and the sum-of-squares method for constructing Bell inequalities with the central idea of the state maximally violating the Bell inequality. The Tsirelson bound is then achieved by the state for which the Bell inequality was tailored, which is a desirable property. They point out that while the variational method provides the necessary conditions, the sum-of-squares method gives conditions on the Bell inequality that are sufficient for the state to violate it maximally.
In
14
the method is used to derive self-testing Bell inequalities, known as the SATWAP inequalities, which are maximally violated by maximally entangled bipartite states and are defined for an arbitrary number of measurements and outcomes per party. The observables considered were taken to be the optimal CGLMP measurements. In
8
, the authors address a similar question but restrict themselves to prime local Hilbert space dimension
d(\geq 3)
, and correspondingly,
d
measurement settings and
d
outcomes. Similarly to SATWAP inequalities, in
2
the two qutrit system is again self-tested using CGLMP measurements.

In a similar spirit, this article provides a very general analytic construction of a class of CGLMP-style Bell inequalities that self-test the bipartite maximally entangled qutrit state in the simplest setting of two three-outcome measurements. We will see that the SATWAP inequality with the number of measurement settings and outcomes fixed to 2 and 3, respectively, is a particular case of our construction. Although both share the same Tsirelson bound and a similar SOS decomposition, we do not make any assumptions about the observables used by both Alice and Bob.
The article is organised as follows. In
section I
, we give a gentle introduction to the formalism used in this article. In
section II
, we define the general setting of this article, define the general Bell operator and provide a sum-of-squares decomposition. In
section III
, the Bell operator is further constrained to provide self-testing statements for the maximally entangled state. Next, in
section IV
, we provide an analysis of the classical value of the Bell operator and a comparison with the SATWAP inequalities.

I
Preliminaries

Let us consider a simple
Bell scenario
with two space-like separated, non-communicating observers, Alice and Bob, who share a quantum system and can perform local measurements on their devices. The scenario is characterised by the number of measurements,
m
, and the number of outcomes,
d
. To use the prevalent notation, the measurements of Alice and Bob are denoted as
A_{x}
and
B_{y}
where
x,y\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}
and the outcomes are labelled as
a
and
b
, respectively, for Alice and Bob, where
a,b\in\{0,1,\dots,d-1\}
.
In each run of the scenario, a quantum state,
\rho_{AB}
is prepared and shared with Alice and Bob, who in turn choose a measurement setting on their device and read the outcome of the measurement. Over sufficiently many runs of the experiment, the results can be compiled into a vector of joint probability distributions, or correlations,
\vec{p}=\{p(a,b|x,y)\}
, where
p(a,b|x,y)
is the probability of obtaining the outcomes
a
and
b
given that Alice and Bob perform the measurements
A_{x}
and
B_{y}
, and is defined as,

p(a,b|x,y)=Tr[\rho_{AB}\left(A_{x}\otimes B_{y}\right)]\,.

(1)

The set of
\vec{p}
obtained in the above manner is convex and is known as the set of quantum correlations,
\mathcal{Q}
.
In such a scenario, it is not necessary that Alice and Bob’s outcomes are statistically independent. In other words, the joint probability doesn’t always factorise, i.e. ,
p(a,b|x,y)=p(a|x)p(b|y)
. The correlations such that,

p(a,b|x,y)=p(a|x)p(b|y)\,,

(2)

where
p(a|x),p(b|y)\in\{0,1\}
for all
x,y
are called
deterministic
correlations. The set of correlations
\vec{p}
that can be written as a convex sum of deterministic correlations is called the
local
polytope,
\mathcal{L}
, and is a subset of
\mathcal{Q}
.

A natural question that arises from this characterisation is whether we can determine if a given correlation
\vec{p}
belongs to
\mathcal{Q}
or
\mathcal{L}
. The way to show this is using Bell inequalities. Bell inequalities, in general, are formulated as linear combinations of the joint probabilities in
\vec{p}
,

I(\vec{p})=\sum_{abxy}c^{xy}_{ab}p(a,b|x,y)\leq b_{C}\,.

(3)

where
c^{xy}_{ab}
are the parameters of the Bell functional, and
b_{C}
is called the classical bound of the Bell Inequality. It is defined as

b_{C}=\max_{\vec{p}\in\mathcal{L}}I(\vec{p})\,.

(4)

Similar to the classical bound, we can also define the quantum bound or the Tsirelson bound,
T_{Q}
for the Bell inequality,

T_{Q}=\max_{\vec{p}\in\mathcal{Q}}I(\vec{p})\,.

(5)

Using the Bell inequality now we can determine the membership of a given correlation
\vec{p}
in the following manner. If it satisfies (
3
) it belongs in the set
\mathcal{L}
, and if
b_{C}<I(\vec{p})\leq T_{Q}
, it belongs in the quantum set,
\mathcal{Q}
.

A more convenient approach is using the generalised correlators defined using the Fourier transform of the joint probabilities
17
;
8
,

\expectationvalue{A^{(k)}_x\otimes B^{(l)}_y}=\sum_{a,b=0}^{d-1}\omega^{ak+bl}p(a,b|x,y)\,,

(6)

where the
d
measurement operators for Alice and Bob are defined as,

\displaystyle A^{(k)}_{x}=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\omega^{ik}P_{i}^{A}\mbox{\quad and\quad}B^{(l)}_{y}=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\omega^{jl}P_{j}^{B}\,,

(7)

where
\omega=\exp{2\pi\iota/d}
is the
d^{\mbox{th}}
-root of unity, and
P_{i}^{X}
are positive semi-definite operators such that
\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}P_{i}^{X}=\mathbb{1}
. In the case when
P_{i}^{X}
are projective, the operators
A^{(k)}_{x}
and
B{(l)}_{y}
can be described by the unitary observables
A_{x}
and
B_{y}
such that,

A^{(k)}_{x}=(A_{x})^{k}\mbox{\quad and\quad}B^{(l)}_{y}=(B_{y})^{l}\,.

(8)

As a consequence, the spectrum of the observables is
\{1,\omega,\omega^{2}\cdots\omega^{d-1}\}
. It is also evident from their construction that,

(A_{x}^{k})^{\dagger}=A_{x}^{d-k}\mbox{\quad and\quad}(B_{y}^{k})^{\dagger}=B_{y}^{d-k}\,.

(9)

In this correlation picture the Bell inequality can be expressed as

I=\sum_{x,y=1}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}c_{xyk}\expectationvalue{A^{k}_x\otimes B^{k}_y}\,,

(10)

where the Bell scenario is defined by
m

d
-outcome measurements per observer.

II
The Bell Operator and the Quantum Bound

In this article, we consider the most general Bell operator without considering local terms. We want to set some expectations from our Bell operator that it must have nice properties, namely, a Sum-of-Squares decomposition and that the maximally entangled state achieves the Tsirelson bound. To that end, we define the Bell scenario as two parties performing two three-outcome measurements on their shared quantum state, so that
m=2
and
d=3
.
We define our general Bell operator as

W=A_{0}\otimes\left(\alpha B_{0}+\beta B_{1}\right)+A_{1}\otimes\left(\gamma B_{0}+\delta B_{1}\right)\\
+A_{0}^{\dagger}\otimes\left(\alpha B_{0}+\beta B_{1}\right)^{\dagger}+A_{1}^{\dagger}\otimes\left(\gamma B_{0}+\delta B_{1}\right)^{\dagger},

(11)

where
A_{0},A_{1},B_{0}
and
B_{1}
are Alice and Bob’s observables, and
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\in\mathbb{C}
. We will reference them as Bell parameters going forward. Alice and Bob’s observables are
A_{0},A_{1},B_{0}
and
B_{1}
. Following the discussion in the last section, the observables satisfy the following relations:

\displaystyle A_{x}^{3}=B_{y}^{3}=\mathbb{1},

\displaystyle A_{x}A_{x}^{\dagger}=B_{x}B_{x}^{\dagger}=\mathbb{1},

\displaystyle A_{x}^{2}=A_{x}^{\dagger}\mbox{\quad and\quad}B_{x}^{2}=B_{x}^{\dagger}.

(12)

To begin the characterisation of the Bell operator, we first define the terms
L_{i}
,

\displaystyle L_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{1}-A_{0}\otimes(\alpha B_{0}+\beta B_{1})\,,

(13)

\displaystyle L_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{1}-A_{1}\otimes(\gamma B_{0}+\delta B_{1})\,.

(14)

Theorem 1
.

If the Bell parameters satisfy
\alpha\beta^{*}+\gamma\delta^{*}=0
and with
L_{i}
s defined above, then the Bell operator
W
in (
11
) admits the following sum-of-squares decomposition,

W=(2+\absolutevalue{\alpha}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\beta}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\gamma}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\delta}^{2})\mathbb{1}\\
-\sum_{i=1}^{2}\frac{1}{2}(L_{i}^{\dagger}L_{i}+L_{i}L_{i}^{\dagger})\,,

(15)

Proof.

Here, we give a sketch of the proof, while the details can be found in
Appendix A
. Expanding the sum-of-squares expression, we find

\frac{1}{2}\left(L_{1}^{\dagger}L_{1}+L_{2}^{\dagger}L_{2}+L_{1}L_{1}^{\dagger}+L_{2}L_{2}^{\dagger}\right)\\
=2\mathbb{1}-W+X_{1}+X_{2},

(16)

where
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are the cross terms:

X_{1}=\mathbb{1}\otimes(\alpha B_{0}+\beta B_{1})^{\dagger}(\alpha B_{0}+\beta B_{1})\\
+\mathbb{1}\otimes(\gamma B_{0}+\delta B_{1})^{\dagger}(\gamma B_{0}+\delta B_{1}),\,\mbox{and}

(17)

X_{2}=\mathbb{1}\otimes(\alpha B_{0}+\beta B_{1})^{\dagger}(\alpha B_{0}+\beta B_{1})\\
+\mathbb{1}\otimes(\gamma B_{0}+\delta B_{1})^{\dagger}(\gamma B_{0}+\delta B_{1}).

(18)

The sum of cross terms is reduced to

X_{1}+X_{2}=(\absolutevalue{\alpha}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\beta}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\gamma}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\delta}^{2}))\mathbb{1},

(19)

when we set

\alpha\beta^{*}+\gamma\delta^{*}=\alpha^{*}\beta+\gamma^{*}\delta=0.

(20)

From this, we can rewrite (
16
):

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left(L_{i}^{\dagger}L_{i}+L_{i}L_{i}^{\dagger}\right)\\
=(2+\absolutevalue{\alpha}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\beta}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\gamma}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\delta}^{2})\mathbb{1}-W.

(21)

Rearranging the above equation gives us the requisite sum-of-squares decomposition and completes the proof of the theorem.
Note,
T_{Q}=(2+\absolutevalue{\alpha}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\beta}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\gamma}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\delta}^{2})
is the Tsirelson bound.
∎

Corollary 1.1
.

It follows from the above theorem, that

1.

\absolutevalue{\alpha}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\beta}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\gamma}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\delta}^{2}=2
, and

2.

the Tsirelson bound or the maximum quantum value of
W
is
T_{Q}=4
.

Proof.

For the proof of the corollary, notice that when a state
\ket{\psi}
maximally violates the Bell inequality, the following relations hold:

L_{i}\ket{\psi}=L_{i}^{\dagger}\ket{\psi}=0\mbox{\quad for\quad}i=1,2.

(22)

with
L_{i}
’s referred to as the nullifiers of the state. If we define
C_{0}=\alpha B_{0}+\beta B_{1}
and
C_{1}=\gamma B_{0}+\delta B_{1}
, then using the above relations, it is easy to show that the operators
C_{i}
are also unitary and projective,

C_{i}^{2}=C_{i}^{\dagger}\mbox{\quad and\quad}C_{i}^{\dagger}C_{i}=\mathbb{1}.

(23)

Using the latter condition for both
i=0,1
with (
12
) and (
20
), directly gives us

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{\alpha}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\beta}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\gamma}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\delta}^{2}=2,

(24)

and therefore the Tsirelson bound is
T_{Q}=4
. ∎

III
Self-testing statement for the Bell operator

We further characterise our Bell operator,
W
, by providing a self-testing statement for
W
, which is the main result of this article.
The following theorem determines the state and the family of measurements that can be self-tested by our Bell operator.
First, let us define a few relevant symbols and matrices so that the mathematical expressions that follow are easier to digest. Most of the expressions that follow can be simplified if we use (
20
) to define

\kappa=\frac{\alpha\beta^{*}}{\alpha^{*}\beta}=\frac{\gamma\delta^{*}}{\gamma^{*}\delta}\,.

(25)

The above is equivalent to

\kappa={e}^{\mathrm{i}2(\theta_{\alpha}-\theta_{\beta})}={e}^{\mathrm{i}2(\theta_{\gamma}-\theta_{\delta})}\,,

(26)

which constrains the phases as
\theta_{\alpha}-\theta_{\beta}=\theta_{\gamma}-\theta_{\delta}+n\pi
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.
We will also define the matrix
T_{3}
in terms of the elements of the three dimensional Heisenberg-Weyl basis,
Z=\sum_{i=0}^{2}\omega^{i}\ket{i}\bra{i}
and
X=\sum_{i=0}^{2}\ket{i}\bra{i+1}
, also known as the clock and shift matrices, with
\omega=e^{\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}}{3}}
(the cube root of unity).

T_{3}=\frac{1}{3}\left[\left(\kappa^{2}+\frac{2}{\kappa}\right)Z+\left(\kappa^{2}-\frac{1}{\kappa}\right)ZX\right.\\
\left.+\omega\left(\kappa^{2}-\frac{1}{\kappa}\right)ZX^{2}\right].

(27)

Theorem 2
.

If the state
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
achieves the Tsirelson bound
T_{Q}=4
for the Bell operator
W
, with observables
A_{x}\in\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
B_{x}\in\mathcal{H}_{B}
and
x=0,1
, then the following three statements are simultaneously true:

1.

There exist local unitaries
U_{A}:\mathcal{H}_{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{3}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A^{\prime}}
and
U_{B}:\mathcal{H}_{B}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{3}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B^{\prime}}
, such that

\displaystyle U_{B}B_{0}U_{B}^{\dagger}=Z\otimes\mathbb{1}_{B^{\prime}}\,,

(28)

\displaystyle U_{B}B_{1}U_{B}^{\dagger}=T_{3}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{B^{\prime}}\,,

(29)

and,

\displaystyle U_{A}A_{0}U_{A}^{\dagger}=\left(\alpha Z+\beta T_{3}\right)^{*}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{A^{\prime}}\,,

(30)

\displaystyle U_{A}A_{1}U_{A}^{\dagger}=\left(\gamma Z+\delta T_{3}\right)^{*}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{A^{\prime}}\,,

(31)

where
Z
and
T_{3}
are defined in (
27
).

2.

The action of the unitaries on the state is given by

U_{A}\otimes U_{B}\ket{\psi}=\ket{\phi_+}\otimes\ket{\psi'}\,,

(32)

where
\ket{\phi_+}
is the maximally entangled state acting on
\mathbb{C}^{3}_{A}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{3}_{B}
and
\ket{\psi'}
is some state acting on
\mathcal{H}_{A^{\prime}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B^{\prime}}
.

3.

The Bell parameters are constrained as

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\alpha^{*}}+\frac{\beta^{2}}{\beta^{*}}=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\gamma^{*}}+\frac{\delta^{2}}{\delta^{*}}=1.

(33)

and,

\displaystyle\left(\kappa^{2}-\frac{1}{\kappa}\right)\neq 0.

(34)

in addition to (
20
) and (
24
).

Proof.

Again, the proof is lengthy, and we will describe the main steps here, while the details are relegated to the
Appendix B
. Without loss of generality, we can start by characterising the observables of Bob and then derive the form of Alice’s observables.
We shall first consider the case where the observables
B_{0},B_{1}\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
. For such projective and unitary observables, it is always possible to bring
B_{0}=Z
using some unitary
U_{B}
and then
B_{1}
can be parameterised using the Heisenberg-Weyl basis as

B_{1}=aZ+bZ^{2}+X(c\mathbb{1}+dZ+eZ^{2})\\
+X^{2}(f\mathbb{1}+gZ+hZ^{2}).

(35)

When we substitute
B_{0}
and
B_{1}
in (
23
) we obtain the relations,

\frac{-\alpha\beta}{\beta^{2}-\beta^{*}}=\kappa=\frac{-\gamma\delta}{\delta^{2}-\delta^{*}}\,,

(36)

which, when rearranged, gives (
33
).
For a more general construction, now consider the case where observables
A_{x},B_{x}
act on Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}
with some general dimension
d
.
Again using the trace of the relations (
23
), we find that if one assumes the inequality (
34
) then we can show that the observables
B_{x}
are traceless. Combined with the fact that
B_{x}^{3}=\mathbb{1}_{B}
and
B_{x}^{\dagger}B_{x}=\mathbb{1}
, tracelessness implies that the eigenvalues of the observables are
\{1,\omega,\omega^{2}\}
, and they occur with the same multiplicity. This in turn implies that
\mathcal{H}_{B}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{3}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B^{\prime}}
, in other words, the dimension of Bob’s Hilbert space is a multiple of 3. As in the first case, we can again choose a local unitary
U^{\prime}_{B}
such that

\displaystyle\tilde{B}_{0}=

\displaystyle U^{\prime}_{B}B_{0}{U^{\prime}_{B}}^{\dagger}=Z\otimes\mathbb{1},

(37)

\displaystyle\tilde{B}_{1}=

\displaystyle U^{\prime}_{B}B_{1}{U^{\prime}_{B}}^{\dagger}=\sum_{i,j=0}^{2}\outerproduct{i}{j}\otimes F_{ij}.

(38)

where
F_{ij}
’s are matrices that act on
\mathcal{H}_{B^{\prime}}
. Using (
23
) and the fact that the operators
\tilde{B}_{y}
are again projective and unitary, one can find another unitary matrix
V_{B}
that while leaving
\tilde{B}_{0}
unchanged, acts on
\tilde{B}_{1}
to give

V_{B}\tilde{B}_{1}V_{B}^{\dagger}=T_{3}\otimes\mathbb{1}\,,

(39)

where
T_{3}
is defined in (
27
). We can then define the unitary matrix in (
28
) as
U_{B}=V_{B}U^{\prime}_{B}
.

Having characterised Bob’s observables, let us now look at Alice’s observables. Here we follow a procedure similar to
15
, wherein we restrict Bob’s Hilbert space to
\mathbb{C}^{3}
on which
Z
and
T_{3}
act and use the fact that these observables
are
genuinely incompatible
. Genuine incompatibility implies non-existence of any common invariant subspace and we show in
Appendix C
that
Z
and
T_{3}
are in fact genuinely incompatible using Burnside’s theorem on irreducible matrix algebras. It is a straightforward conclusion that
\tilde{C}_{0}^{*}=(\alpha Z+\beta T_{3})^{*}
and
\tilde{C}_{1}^{*}=(\gamma Z+\delta T_{3})^{*}
are also genuinely incompatible.

Obtaining the maximally entangled state as the one that achieves the Tsirelson bound and the characterisation of Alice’s observables is a simultaneous process. To see how, observe that while working in the restricted Hilbert space, the state
\ket{\psi}
, which by assumption violates the Bell inequality maximally, is reduced to a mixed state
\Tr_{B^{\prime}}(\ket{\psi})=\rho_{AB}\in\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
, where
\mathcal{H}_{B}=\mathbb{C}^{3}
. As the maximal violation of the Bell inequality implies that the state
\rho_{AB}
satisfies the nullifying conditions,

L_{i}\rho_{AB}=0,

(40)

all the eigenvectors of the state
\ket{\phi_i}
also satisfy them. Therefore, we can consider the eigenvectors individually and use the following two facts for a pure state written in Schmidt-decomposed form,
\ket{\phi}=\lambda_{l}\ket{e_l}\ket{f_l}
.

Fact
: We can always write
\ket{\phi}
as

\ket{\phi}=(M_{A}\otimes P_{B})\ket{\phi_+},

(41)

where
\ket{\phi_+}
is the maximally entangled state,
M_{A}
is a unitary matrix such that
M_{A}\ket{e_l}=\ket{f_l}^{*}
and
P_{B}=\sqrt{d}\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\outerproduct{f_i}{f_i}
.

Fact
: For any two matrices
R
and
Q
and the maximally entangled state
\ket{\phi_+}
,

R\otimes Q\ket{\phi_+}=RQ^{T}\otimes\mathbb{1}\ket{\phi_+}.

(42)

Using the two facts above and the genuine incompatibility of
\tilde{C}_{0}^{*}

\tilde{C}_{1}^{*}
, we can show that there exists a unitary
U_{A}
such that under the action of this unitary Alice’s observables are

\displaystyle U_{A}A_{0}U_{A}^{\dagger}=(\alpha Z+\beta T_{3})^{*}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{A^{\prime}BB^{\prime}},

(43)

\displaystyle U_{A}A_{1}U_{A}^{\dagger}=(\gamma Z+\delta T_{3})^{*}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{A^{\prime}BB^{\prime}}.

(44)

We see that the unitaries act on the Hilbert space by splitting it in
\mathbb{C}^{3}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{3}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}
. Therefore, it is not surprising to find that the action of these unitaries on the state
\ket{\psi}
is

U_{A}\otimes U_{B}\ket{\psi}=\ket{\phi_+}\otimes\ket{\psi'},

(45)

where
\ket{\psi'}
is some state acting on
\mathcal{H}_{A^{\prime}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B^{\prime}}
,
which completes the proof.
∎

IV
Classical Value of the Bell Inequality

The classical value of the Bell inequality can be found by characterising the vertices of the local polytope. All the vertices can be written in the following form,

\beta_{C}=-\frac{2\sin(3\theta_\alpha)\cos({\theta_\alpha+s_\alpha\frac{2\pi}{3}})}{\sin{3(\theta_\alpha-\theta_\beta)}}\\
+\frac{2\sin({3\theta_\beta})\cos({\theta_\beta+s_\beta\frac{2\pi}{3}})}{\sin{3(\theta_\alpha-\theta_\beta)}}\\
-\frac{2\sin(3\theta_\gamma)\cos({\theta_\gamma+s_\gamma\frac{2\pi}{3}})}{\sin{3(\theta_\gamma-\theta_\delta)}}\\
+\frac{2\sin({3\theta_\delta})\cos({\theta_\delta+s_\delta\frac{2\pi}{3}})}{\sin{3(\theta_\gamma-\theta_\delta)}},

(46)

where
s_{\alpha},s_{\beta}\dots
take on values
\{0,1,-1\}
for different vertices.
Now to constrain the above value, it would be valuable to collect all the constraints on the Bell parameters that enable self-testing of the Bell inequality. For some integer
n\in\mathbb{Z}
:

\displaystyle c_{1}:

\displaystyle\quad\theta_{\alpha}-\theta_{\beta}=\theta_{\gamma}-\theta_{\delta}+n\pi\,,

(47)

\displaystyle c_{2}:

\displaystyle\quad\theta_{\alpha}-\theta_{\beta}\neq\frac{n\pi}{3}\,,\ \theta_{\alpha},\theta_{\beta},\cdots\neq\frac{n\pi}{3},

(48)

\displaystyle c_{3}:

\displaystyle\quad\absolutevalue{\alpha}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\beta}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\gamma}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\delta}^{2}=2\,,

(49)

V
Conclusions

In this article, we derived a self-testing statement for the Bell scenario for bipartite systems of local dimension
d=3
considering the minimal number of measurement settings and outcomes per observer.
We started with the most general Bell operator inspired by the CGLMP Bell inequality. The imposition of sum-of-squares decomposition combined with the projective and unitary three-outcome observables led to simple constraints on the parameters in the Bell inequality for the state to maximally violate the inequality when it is nullified by the elements of the sum-of-squares decomposition. From here, we determined the maximal quantum value achievable by our Bell operator to be 4.

We proved analytically that the Bell inequality self-tests the maximally entangled state and a class of measurement observables that are constrained by a nominal number of constraints on the Bell parameters.
We also showed that the resulting class of self-testing Bell inequalities is a broader class of inequalities than previously known. We also demonstrated that the SATWAP inequalities are a special case of our inequality. The fact that the SATWAP inequality for
d=3
coincides with the point where the difference between the maximum classical value and the maximum quantum value is greatest, implies an optimality in the derived class of Bell inequalities.

Theorem 1: Sum-of-Squares and Tsirelson bound

The aim of

is to prove that there exists a sum-of-squares decomposition for the Bell operator,

\displaystyle=\mathbb{1}-A_{0}\otimes(\alpha B_{0}+\beta B_{1})\,,

(56)

\displaystyle L_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{1}-A_{1}\otimes(\gamma B_{0}+\delta B_{1})\,,

(57)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{1}\otimes(\alpha B_{0}+\beta B_{1})^{\dagger}(\alpha B_{0}+\beta B_{1})

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{1}\otimes(\gamma B_{0}+\delta B_{1})^{\dagger}(\gamma B_{0}+\delta B_{1})

\displaystyle+\mathbb{1}\otimes(\gamma B_{0}+\delta B_{1})(\gamma B_{0}+\delta B_{1})^{\dagger}.

\displaystyle=\ket{\psi},

(69b)

\displaystyle\tilde{F}_{01}=

\displaystyle U_{0}F_{01}U_{1}^{\dagger}=c_{0}c_{1}^{*}F_{01}F_{01}^{\dagger}=\frac{c_{0}c_{1}^{*}}{\absolutevalue{c_1}^{2}}\mathbb{1},

(113a)

\displaystyle\tilde{F}_{10}=

\displaystyle U_{1}F_{10}U_{0}^{\dagger}=-c_{0}^{*}c_{1}F_{01}F_{10}=-\kappa\omega\frac{c_{0}^{*}c_{1}}{\absolutevalue{c_1}^{2}}\mathbb{1},

(113b)

\displaystyle\tilde{F}_{02}=

\displaystyle U_{0}F_{02}U_{2}^{\dagger}=c_{0}c_{2}^{*}F_{02}F_{02}^{\dagger}=\frac{c_{0}c_{2}^{*}}{\absolutevalue{c_2}^{2}}\mathbb{1},

(113c)

\displaystyle\tilde{F}_{20}=

\displaystyle U_{2}F_{20}U_{0}^{\dagger}=-c_{2}F_{02}F_{20}=-\kappa\omega^{2}\frac{c_{0}^{*}c_{2}}{\absolutevalue{c_2}^{2}}\mathbb{1}.

(113d)

To obtain
\tilde{F}_{12}
and
\tilde{F}_{21}
, respectively put
i=1,j=0
and
i=2,j=0
in (
103
), then multiply the first from left by
F_{01}
and the latter by
F_{02}
. Solving for the requisite terms in both gives us

\tilde{F}_{12}=U_{1}F_{12}U_{2}^{\dagger}=c_{1}c_{2}^{*}F_{01}F_{12}F_{02}^{\dagger}\\
=\frac{\omega}{3}\frac{c_{1}c_{2}^{*}}{\absolutevalue{c_2}^{2}}\left(\kappa^{2}-\frac{1}{\kappa}\right)\mathbb{1},

(114a)

\tilde{F}_{21}=U_{2}F_{21}U_{1}^{\dagger}=c_{1}^{*}c_{2}F_{02}F_{21}F_{01}^{\dagger}\\
=\frac{\omega^{2}}{3}\frac{c_{1}^{*}c_{2}}{\absolutevalue{c_1}^{2}}\left(\kappa^{2}-\frac{1}{\kappa}\right)\mathbb{1}.

(114b)

As the matrices
\tilde{F}_{ij}
are all proportional to identity, we find that
\tilde{B}_{1}=T_{3}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{B^{\prime}}
, where

T_{3}=\\
\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{3}\left(\kappa^{2}+\frac{2}{\kappa}\right)&\frac{c_{0}}{{c_{1}}}&\frac{c_{0}}{{c_{2}}}\\
-\kappa\omega\frac{c_{0}^{*}}{{c_{1}}^{*}}&\frac{\omega}{3}\left(\kappa^{2}+\frac{2}{\kappa}\right)&\frac{\omega c_{1}}{3{c_{2}}}\left(\kappa^{2}-\frac{1}{\kappa}\right)\\
-\kappa\omega^{2}\frac{c_{0}^{*}}{{c_{2}}^{*}}&\frac{\omega^{2}c_{2}}{3{c_{1}}}\left(\kappa^{2}-\frac{1}{\kappa}\right)&\frac{\omega^{2}}{3}\left(\kappa^{2}+\frac{2}{\kappa}\right)\end{pmatrix}.

(115)

Observe that the denominator of the absolute values of
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
in (
112
) can be expressed as a square,

\left(2-\left(\kappa^{3}+\frac{1}{\kappa^{3}}\right)\right)=-\left(\kappa^{3/2}-\frac{1}{\kappa^{3/2}}\right)^{2}\\
=-(2\mathrm{i}\sin{3\theta_\kappa})^{2},

(116)

and therefore we can assume, without loss of generality,

c_{1}=\frac{3x}{\left(\kappa^{3/2}-\frac{1}{\kappa^{3/2}}\right)}\mbox{\quad and\quad}c_{2}=\frac{3x\omega^{n}}{\left(\kappa^{3/2}-\frac{1}{\kappa^{3/2}}\right)},

(117)

where
x\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\absolutevalue{x}^{2}=1
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Furthermore, if we take
x=\kappa^{-1/2}
, then
c_{1}=3(\kappa^{2}-\kappa^{-1})^{-1}
and similarly for
c_{2}
.
The matrix
T_{3}
becomes,

T_{3}=\frac{1}{3}\left[\left(\kappa^{2}+\frac{2}{\kappa}\right)Z+\left(\kappa^{2}-\frac{1}{\kappa}\right)ZX\right.\\
\left.+\omega\left(\kappa^{2}-\frac{1}{\kappa}\right)ZX^{2}\right].

(118)

Therefore, the observables acting on Bob’s Hilbert space are
\tilde{B}_{0}=Z_{B}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{B^{\prime}}
and
\tilde{B}_{1}=T_{B}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{B^{\prime}}
under the unitary
U_{B}=V_{B}{U^{\prime}}_{B}
. Now, because of the tensor product structure, the two observables are not
genuinely incompatible
, meaning that they share some common invariant subspace. On the other hand, if we restrict ourselves to the three dimensional subspace of
Z
and
T_{3}
, we can employ Burnside’s theorem for matrix algebras and show that they generate the same irreducible matrix algebra
\mathcal{A}
generated by the matrices
Z
and
X
, i.e.
\mathcal{A}(\tilde{B}_{0},\tilde{B}_{1})=\mathcal{A}(Z,X)
.
This proof can be seen in
Appendix C
.

Furthermore, one could also prove that the observables
\tilde{C}_{0}=\alpha\tilde{B}_{0}+\beta\tilde{B}_{1}
and
\tilde{C}_{1}=\gamma\tilde{B}_{0}+\delta\tilde{B}_{1}
are genuinely incompatible by using the relation
\alpha\beta^{*}+\gamma\delta^{*}=0
. With this information in hand, we can move on to Alice’s observables.

B.4
Alice’s observables and the State

In this section, we will show that there exists a unitary
U_{A}
that plays the same role as
U_{B}
of splitting the local Hilbert space and in the process we also show that the state in question that violates the Bell inequality maximally is indeed the maximally entangled state.

Let’s start from the nullifiers,
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
from the SoS decomposition in (
22
) with
\tilde{B}_{0}=Z\otimes\mathbb{1}
and
\tilde{B}_{1}=T\otimes\mathbb{1}
,

\displaystyle A_{0}\otimes(\alpha Z+\beta T)\otimes\mathbb{1}\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=\ket{\psi},

(119)

\displaystyle A_{1}\otimes(\gamma Z+\delta T)\otimes\mathbb{1}\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=\ket{\psi}.

(120)

If we restrict the Bob’s Hilbert space to the 3 dimensional subspace on which
Z
and
T
act, then the relations above are satisfied by the state
\rho_{AB}=\Tr_{B^{\prime}}(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi})
,

\displaystyle A_{0}\otimes(\alpha Z+\beta T)\rho_{AB}

\displaystyle=\rho_{AB},

(121)

\displaystyle A_{1}\otimes(\gamma Z+\delta T)\rho_{AB}

\displaystyle=\rho_{AB}.

(122)

We can then use the eigen-decomposition of
\rho_{AB}
to see that for each of it’s eigenvectors,
\ket{\phi}_{i}
, the above equations still hold, i.e.

\displaystyle A_{0}\otimes(\alpha Z+\beta T)\ket{\phi}_{i}

\displaystyle=\ket{\phi}_{i},

(123)

\displaystyle A_{1}\otimes(\gamma Z+\delta T)\ket{\phi}_{i}

\displaystyle=\ket{\phi}_{i}.

(124)

Multiplying both sides by the hermitian conjugate of observable acting on the Bob’s Hilbert space,

\displaystyle A_{0}\otimes\mathbb{1}\ket{\phi}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{1}\otimes(\alpha Z+\beta T)^{\dagger}\ket{\phi}_{i},

(125)

\displaystyle A_{1}\otimes\mathbb{1}\ket{\phi}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{1}\otimes(\gamma Z+\delta T)^{\dagger}\ket{\phi}_{i}.

(126)

Now we use the fact that for a pure state with the Schmidt decomposition
\ket{\phi}_{i}=\sum_{l}\lambda_{l}\ket{e_l}\ket{f_l}
, one can always write

\ket{\phi}_{i}=(U_{A}\otimes P_{B})\ket{\phi_+}

(127)

where
U_{A}
is a unitary that satisfies
U_{A}\ket{e_i}=\ket{f_i}^{*}
for all
\ket{e_i}
and
P_{B}=\sqrt{d}\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\outerproduct{f_i}{f_i}
is a positive matrix. Let us substitute this in (
125
) with
\tilde{C}_{0}=(\alpha Z+\beta T)
and
\tilde{C}_{1}=(\gamma Z+\delta T)
,

\displaystyle A_{0}U_{A}\otimes P_{B}\ket{\phi_+}

\displaystyle=U_{A}\otimes\tilde{C}_{0}^{\dagger}P_{B}\ket{\phi_+},

(128)

\displaystyle A_{1}U_{A}\otimes P_{B}\ket{\phi_+}

\displaystyle=U_{A}\otimes\tilde{C}_{1}^{\dagger}P_{B}\ket{\phi_+}.

(129)

Now using the fact that for any two matrices
R
and
Q
acting on the maximally entangled state,

R\otimes Q\ket{\phi_+}=RQ^{T}\otimes\mathbb{1}\ket{\phi_+},

so we can move all the observables acting on Bob’s Hilbert space to Alice’s space:

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{0}P_{B}\otimes\mathbb{1}\ket{\phi_+}

\displaystyle=P_{B}\tilde{C}_{0}^{*}\otimes\mathbb{1}\ket{\phi_+},

(130)

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{1}P_{B}\otimes\mathbb{1}\ket{\phi_+}

\displaystyle=P_{B}\tilde{C}_{1}^{*}\otimes\mathbb{1}\ket{\phi_+}.

(131)

Here
\tilde{A}_{x}=U_{A}^{\dagger}A_{x}U_{A}
. We can now compare the observables acting on the local Hilbert space of Alice and write the following equality

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{0}P_{B}=P_{B}\tilde{C}_{0}^{*}\mbox{\quad and\quad}\tilde{A}_{1}P_{B}=P_{B}\tilde{C}_{1}^{*}.

(132)

Multiplying by their hermitian conjugate from the left leaves us with

\displaystyle P_{B}\tilde{A}_{0}^{\dagger}\tilde{A}_{0}P_{B}=P_{B}^{2}=\tilde{C}_{0}^{T}P_{B}^{2}\tilde{C}_{0}^{*},

(133)

\displaystyle P_{B}\tilde{A}_{1}^{\dagger}\tilde{A}_{1}P_{B}=P_{B}^{2}=\tilde{C}_{1}^{T}P_{B}^{2}\tilde{C}_{1}^{*}.

(134)

Rearranging the above equations to

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{0}^{*}P_{B}^{2}=P_{B}^{2}\tilde{C}_{0}^{*}\mbox{\quad and\quad}\tilde{C}_{1}^{*}P_{B}^{2}=P_{B}^{2}\tilde{C}_{1}^{*}.

(135)

gives us the commutation relations
\commutator{\tilde{C}_0^*}{P_B^2}=0
and
\commutator{\tilde{C}_1^*}{P_B^2}=0
. Due to the fact that
P_{B}
is a positive matrix, the commutation relations
\commutator{\ti{C}_1^*}{P_B}=0
and
\commutator{\ti{C}_0^*}{P_B}=0
also hold.
It was shown in
15
that for such a positive matrix
P_{B}
and a set of genuinely incompatible observables
\{\tilde{C}_{i}\}
, if
\commutator{\ti{C}_i}{P_B}=0
, then the matrix
P_{B}
is of the form
\lambda\mathbb{1}
for some constant
\lambda
.
Therefore, in our case, where we have already shown that
\tilde{C}_{0}^{*}
and
\tilde{C}_{1}^{*}
are genuinely incompatible, then
P_{B}
is of proportional to identity and we can rewrite (
132
),

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{0}=\tilde{C}_{0}^{*}\mbox{\quad and\quad}\tilde{A}_{1}=\tilde{C}_{1}^{*}.

(136)

Observe that the unitary
U_{A}
like
U_{B}
splits the Hilbert space on Alice’s side as
\mathbb{C}^{3}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{A}_{d}

where
\mathcal{H}^{A}_{d^{\prime}}
is
d^{\prime}
-dimensional Hilbert space. The unitaries
U_{A}
and
U_{B}
combined have the effect of splitting the whole Hilbert space as
\mathbb{C}^{A}_{3}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{B}_{3}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{A}_{d^{\prime}}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{B}_{d^{\prime}}
. In the process, we have also shown, that the maximally entangled state satisfies the relations, as under the unitaries we have
U_{A}\otimes U_{B}\ket{\psi}=\ket{\phi_+}\otimes\ket{\psi'}
, so that

U_{AB}L_{i}U_{AB}^{\dagger}\ket{\phi_+}\otimes\ket{\psi'}=0,\mbox{ for }i=1,2,

(137)

where we have used
U_{AB}=U_{A}\otimes U_{B}
and
\ket{\psi'}
is some state in
\mathcal{H}^{A}_{d^{\prime}}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{B}_{d^{\prime}}
.
Therefore,
\ket{\phi_+}
violates the Bell inequality maximally.

Appendix C
Genuine Incompatibility of two observables

Given two matrices,
A
and
B
, they are said to be Genuinely incompatible, if and only if they do not share any common invariant subspace. As mentioned earlier, we can use the Burnside’s theorem on matrix algebras to show that the matrix algebra generated by
A
and
B
is irreducible. In this case we are concerned with the matrices
B_{0}=Z
and
B_{1}=T
(defined in (
27
)), where
T
is a linear combination of some of the elements of the set of Weyl-Heisenberg operators which is generated by the matrix algebra
\mathcal{A}(Z,X)
.

The algebra,
\mathcal{A}(Z,X)
, is an irreducible algebra i.e. an algebra of all
3\times 3
complex matrices, containing
9
linearly independent elements.
Therefore, one can show that the algebra
\mathcal{A}(B_{0},B_{1})
is irreducible, if it is the same as
\mathcal{A}(Z,X)
.

Lemma 3
.

The matrix algebra
\mathcal{A}(B_{0},B_{1})
is irreducible.

Proof.

It is enough to show that one can recover the matrix
X
from
\mathcal{A}(B_{0},B_{1})
.
Let
B_{0}=Z
and to simplify things let’s write
B_{1}=aZ+bZX+\omega bZX^{2}
. Now,
B_{0}
and
B_{1}
are in the algebra by definition. We also have
\mathbb{1}=B_{0}^{3}
as an element. Then, by using elementary operations the following matrices are also in the algebra.

\displaystyle M_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b}Z^{2}(B_{1}-aB_{0})=X+\omega X^{2},

(138)

\displaystyle M_{2}

\displaystyle=B_{0}^{2}=Z^{2},

(139)

\displaystyle M_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}(\mathbb{1}+Z+Z^{2})=\mbox{diag}(1,0,0),

(140)

\displaystyle M_{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}(\mathbb{1}+\frac{Z}{\omega}+\frac{Z^{2}}{\omega^{2}})=\mbox{diag}(0,1,0),

(141)

\displaystyle M_{5}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}(\mathbb{1}+\frac{Z}{\omega^{2}}+\frac{Z^{2}}{\omega})=\mbox{diag}(0,0,1),

(142)

\displaystyle M_{6}

\displaystyle=M_{4}M_{1}^{2}M_{5}+\frac{M_{3}M_{1}^{2}M_{4}}{\omega}+\frac{M_{5}M_{1}^{2}M_{3}}{\omega^{2}}=X\,.

(143)

Now that we have recovered
X
, together with
B_{0}=Z
we can generate the complete algebra.
∎
