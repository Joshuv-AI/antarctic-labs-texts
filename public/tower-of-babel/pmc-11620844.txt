Pacing‐Induced Cardiomyopathy in a Patient With a Leadless Pacemaker Following Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement
Authors: Euihong Ko, Akihiro Isotani, Shinichi Shirai, Kenji Ando
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTConduction disturbances following transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) sometimes require permanent pacemaker implantation. However, little is known about the pacing‐induced cardiomyopathy (PICM) in leadless pacemaker (LP) after TAVR. Here we present a case of heart failure due to PICM 6 months after TAVR which was successfully treated with LOT‐CRT upgrade.

Introduction

The incidence of permanent pacemaker implantation after transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) with newer generation ranges between 2.3% and 28.2% [1]. The incidence of permanent pacemaker is particularly higher in patients with baseline complete right bundle branch block (CRBBB), prolonged QRS ≥ 120 ms without bundle branch morphology, LVOT calcification, and deeper transcatheter heart valve implantation, and self‐expanding valve [2, 3]. Due to the frailty and advanced age in the TAVR population, leadless pacemakers (LP) have emerged as a feasible alternative to avoid pocket and lead‐related complications associated with conventional transvenous pacemakers (TVP). A recent 5‐year follow‐up study from the Micra Post‐Approval Registry demonstrated the incidence of pacing‐induced cardiomyopathy (PICM) in patients with LP was 5.4%, and 2% of patients were upgraded to cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) [4]. However, the incidence of PICM after LP implantation in the TAVR population is unclear. We present a case of an 89‐year‐old female with PICM after LP implantation, which was successfully treated with a LOT‐CRT upgrade.

History of Presentation

An 89‐year‐old female was admitted to our institution with progressive dyspnea at rest (NYHA function class IV) that had persisted for about 2 weeks. On physical examination, she was normotensive (114/56 mmHg), and had pitting edema in both legs. Diffuse crackles were heard on lung auscultation, and a 4/6 systolic murmur was noted.

Past Medical History

She had a history of hemiarch replacement for acute type A dissection at the age of 79. Her baseline electrocardiography (ECG) showed CRBBB. Also, she underwent TAVR with a 26‐mm SAPIEN 3 for severe aortic regurgitation at the age of 88. Considering her advanced age, and the burdened risk of repeat thoracic surgery, our heart team decided to perform TAVR in this case. Three months after TAVR, she developed syncope and was diagnosed with complete atrioventricular block, which required LP implantation (Micra‐TPS; Medtronic Inc.). Other comorbidities included hypertension and hyperlipidemia.

Differential Diagnosis

Differential diagnosis included acute coronary syndrome, acute heart failure exacerbation secondary to structural valve deterioration, newly developed severe mitral regurgitation (MR), and pneumonia.

Investigation

Chest X‐ray demonstrated congestion and bilateral pleural effusion, requiring 5 L/min of oxygen by mask (Figure 1). ECG showed a right ventricular (RV) paced rhythm with left bundle branch block (LBBB) morphology and a 172 ms QRS duration (Figure 2). A device check revealed that the rate of RV pacing was 98%. Laboratory findings included an elevated B‐type natriuretic peptide (BNP) level of 914.5 pg/dL (reference range < 18.4 pg/dL), a potassium level of 5.0 mmol/L (normal ranges: 3.6–4.9 mEq/L), a glomerular filtration rate of 38 mL/min, a C‐reactive protein level of 8.4 mg/L (normal ranges: 0.0–0.5 mg/L), and a high‐sensitivity troponin I level of 22 pg/dL (reference range < 15 pg/dL).

Electrocardiogram shows right ventricular pacing rhythm with LBBB morphology (QRS duration: 172 ms). LBBB = left bundle branch block.

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) showed a reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) from 41.6% to 31.1% without structural valve deterioration of SAPIEN 3. There was newly developed severe MR, along with left ventricular (LV) enlargement (LVDd/Ds: 52.7/43.8 mm) and dyssynchrony, which were not observed 6 months ago (Videos 1, 2, 3). Invasive coronary angiography demonstrated that there was no significant stenosis in coronary arteries. The etiology of acute decompensated heart failure in this case was considered as PICM, with LV dyssynchrony and LV enlargement resulting in functional severe MR.

Management

The patient was initially treated with intravenous diuretics and dobutamine. However, the congestion on the chest X‐ray and MRI did not improve, and her condition rapidly deteriorated, requiring non‐invasive positive pressure ventilation (NPPV) on the 1st day of admission. On the 2nd day of admission, an intra‐aortic balloon pump (IABP) with 1:1 configuration was inserted to manage worsening heart failure. Considering that PICM was the etiology of decompensated heart failure, the patient was upgraded to CRT based on left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP) under IABP and NPPV support on the 5th day of admission (Figure 3).

LBBAP‐optimized CRT (LOT‐CRT) was done in the usual fashion. A quadripolar LV lead was placed in the coronary sinus (CS) first. The right atrial (RA) lead was placed in the RA appendage. LBBAP was performed using the SelectSecure pacing lead (model 3830, 69 cm, Medtronic Inc., Minneapolis, MN). The pacemaker was programmed in DDD mode at a backup rate of 60 beats per minute (bpm) with atrial sensing and ventricular pacing. Post‐implantation ECG demonstrated a narrowing of the paced QRS duration to 124 ms (Figure 4).

Electrocardiogram after LOT‐CRT implantation shows a reductio of QRS duration (QRS duration: 124 ms). LOT‐CRT = left bundle branch area pacing optimized cardiac resynchronization therapy.

The patient's oxygen requirement improved after the LOT‐CRT upgrade, and the IABP was successfully removed on the 8th day of admission. Chest X‐ray demonstrated improved congestion and pleural effusion, and TTE on the 10th day of admission showed a sustained reduced LVEF (36.0%) but LV dyssynchrony and MR had improved to mild.

Discussion

The incidence of PICM has been reported to range from 14% to 39% in patients with TVP, depending on the definition used [5]. However, there is limited data on PICM in patients with LP. A 5‐year follow‐up study from the Micra Post‐Approval Registry reported a 5.4% incidence of PICM, while an early observational study comparing 131 TVP patients to 67 LP patients found a 3% incidence of PICM in the LP group, which was lower than the 13.7% observed in the TVP group. The variability in the incidence of PICM may stem from differing definitions of PICM and the cut‐offs in RV pacing burden. The potential challenges in diagnosing PICM in both LP and TVP populations are the lack of consensus on a standardized definition of PICM. In most studies, PICM is characterized by a decline in LVEF associated with a high burden of RV pacing, which is considered to cause ventricular electrical and mechanical dyssynchrony, leading to an increased risk of adverse clinical outcomes including mortality and heart failure hospitalization [5, 6, 7].

Shantha et al. published their analysis of 358 consecutive patients who underwent LP implantation, comparing the implanted location between apex/apical septum and mid/high septum. They found that the overall incidence of PICM was 7.8%, with mid/high septum LP implantation having a lower incidence (4.0%) compared to apex/apical septum LP implantation (16.5%) [8]. Sanchez et al. also found that, in the univariate analysis, non‐septal LP location was associated with a higher risk of PICM [9]. Therefore, implantation of LP in the mid/high septal location might reduce the incidence of PICM.

In addition to guideline‐directed medical therapy for heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), alternative pacing strategies, such as CRT or more physiological approaches like left bundle branch area pacing, have been proposed [10, 11]. Additionally, among patients with PICM, CRT with biventricular pacing (BiV‐CRT) is recommended as Class I per the 2023 heart rhythm society (HRS)/the Asia Pacific Heart Rhythm Society (APHRS)/the Latin American Heart Rhythm Society (LAHRS) guideline on cardiac physiologic pacing for avoidance and mitigation of heart failure [12].

BiV‐CRT has been shown to improve mortality, morbidity, and quality of life in patients with HFrEF; however, approximately one third of BiV‐CRT patients are non‐responders [13]. Recently, LOT‐CRT has emerged as an alternative treatment to improve ventricular electrical synchrony, resulting in a significant reduction in paced QRS duration and improvement in heart function [14]. The mechanism of BiV‐CRT is to address electrical dyssynchrony in the LV by pacing the LV lead, allowing earlier activation of the lateral LV wall. However, it does not directly correct LBBB; it only mitigates electrical dyssynchrony by shortening the QRS complex. In contrast, LOT‐CRT, which replaces the RV lead with LBBA, could improve electrical dyssynchrony by directly depolarizing the physiological LV conduction system. Recent studies also showed that patients with LOT‐CRT had better long‐term clinical outcomes regarding cardiovascular mortality and heart failure hospitalization compared with BiV‐CRT [15, 16, 17]. However, more research is needed to verify the efficacy and safety of LOT‐CRT over BiV‐CRT in patients with PICM.

Follow‐Up

The patient was discharged 1 week after LOT‐CRT implantation. She was doing well at her 1‐month and 3‐month follow‐up appointments. TTE at the latest follow‐up showed mild MR and further improvement in LVEF (42.1%) without LV dyssynchrony (Videos 4, 5 and Figure 5). She will continue to be followed up in the valve clinic.

Conclusion

We describe the case of an 89‐year‐old female with PICM resulting from a high burden of RV pacing with LP for complete AVB following TAVR. Upgrading to LOT‐CRT was both feasible and effective in improving heart failure, LV dyssynchrony, and mitral regurgitation.
