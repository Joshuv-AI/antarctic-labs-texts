Decreased Serum Vitamin D Is Not Associated With Sjögren Disease in European and Asian Subjects
Authors: Defne S Yilmaz, Kimberly E Taylor, Lindsey A Criswell, Caroline H Shiboski, Lisa F Barcellos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo identify whether serum vitamin D [25(OH)D] and vitamin D receptor‐binding variants (VDR‐BVs) previously implicated in multiple sclerosis (MS) are associated with Sjögren disease (SjD) susceptibility and related clinical outcomes, with the overall goal of understanding the relationship between vitamin D and SjD pathogenesis.MethodsWe constructed genetic instrumental variables (GIVs) for 25(OH)D using results from five genome‐wide association studies and established VDR‐BVs from experimental studies and then employed a two‐sample Mendelian randomization (MR) approach. We assessed the separate associations of 25(OH)D (GIV25(OH)D) and VDR binding (GIVVDR) with SjD susceptibility and related clinical outcomes in a cohort of 984 cases and 4,595 controls of European and Asian genetic ancestry. Logistic regression was used to estimate the associations for each GIV, adjusting for the first five genome‐wide principal components and sex.ResultsOne VDR‐BV previously found to be causally associated with MS, rs2531804, was also significantly associated with SjD (P < 0.05) in European individuals. Decreased 25(OH)D was not significantly associated with SjD overall, or when European and Asian subgroups were considered separately.ConclusionThis study is the first to investigate genetic variants implicated in previous MR studies of the vitamin D pathway in MS for a role in SjD. Although a role for decreased serum vitamin D in SjD was not supported here, results show that variation within at least one VDR causally contributes to SjD susceptibility in European individuals. Additional research is necessary to more comprehensively understand how vitamin D contributes to SjD pathogenesis across different ancestry groups.

INTRODUCTION

Sjögren disease (SjD, previously known as Sjögren syndrome) is a chronic, multisystem autoimmune disease associated with significant morbidity. 1 , 2 Its global prevalence is estimated at 0.5% to 1.5%, with 1.3 million cases in the United States alone. 3 Like most autoimmune diseases, SjD predominantly affects women, with a female‐to‐male ratio of 9:1. 1 Systemic manifestations include inflammatory arthritis, subcutaneous lupus erythematosus, immune thrombocytopenia, vasculitis with purpura, salivary gland enlargement, neuropathies, interstitial lung disease, and primary biliary cirrhosis. 4 , 5 The risk of lymphoma, the most severe complication of SjD, is approximately 7 to 19 times higher among patients with SjD than in the general population. 6 , 7

Vitamin D and its bioavailable circulating form, 25‐hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D], have been proposed as important factors influencing autoimmunity. Vitamin D deficiency is a known risk factor for several autoimmune diseases including multiple sclerosis (MS), systemic lupus erythematosus, and rheumatoid arthritis. 8 , 9 Vitamin D exerts immunomodulatory effects and regulates calcium metabolism, cellular growth, proliferation, apoptosis, and other immune‐related functions. 10 Vitamin D deficiency has also been frequently observed in patients with primary SjD, suggesting a possible role in disease pathogenesis. 11 , 12 , 13 However, this association may reflect reverse causation or unmeasured confounding. Other possible explanations include altered immune regulation and the subsequent development of autoimmune disease, or reduced UV exposure due to treatment for SjD‐related xerosis. 14

Mendelian randomization (MR) studies have examined low vitamin D as a causal risk factor for autoimmune diseases. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 MR uses genetic instrumental variables (GIVs) to examine the effect of modifiable exposures on disease in observational studies. Valid MR inference depends on three main assumptions: the GIV predicts the exposure, the GIV affects the outcome only through the exposure, and there are no direct relationships between the GIV and confounders.

Because single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) genotypes are fixed at birth and largely unaffected by potential confounding factors throughout the lifetime, MR offers a robust framework for causal inference while minimizing reverse causation and confounding. SNPs associated with 25(OH)D levels, rather than directly measured 25(OH)D, have been used in MR analyses of autoimmune diseases, including SjD, though results for SjD remain inconsistent. 15 , 16 , 17 , 19 Emerging evidence suggests additional genomic mechanisms may influence serum 25(OH)D levels and that components of the vitamin D pathway, particularly gene transcription and expression mediated by vitamin D receptor (VDR) DNA binding, may play a role in the relationship between vitamin D and SjD. 8 , 20 , 21

Following ingestion or absorption, serum vitamin D is first converted to 25(OH)D, and subsequently to its active form, 1,25‐dihydroxyvitamin D [1,25(OH)2D], in the signal cascade. 22 1,25(OH)2D then signals through nuclear VDRs—ligand‐regulated transcription factors that mediate genomic actions of 25(OH)D—to regulate gene transcription involved in downstream immunomodulatory pathways (see Figure 1). 23 , 24

SNPs associated with VDR‐binding variants (BVs) have been previously identified in studies of lymphoblastoid cell lines. 25 These variants are enriched in genomic regions linked to autoimmune diseases, cardiovascular disease, osteoporosis, depression, and cancer. 10 , 26 Regulation of gene expression mediated by vitamin D metabolism and VDR binding may therefore influence susceptibility to autoimmune disease. To date, no published studies have examined genetic variation within individual VDR binding sites in relation to SjD.

In this study, we investigated whether: (1) 25(OH)D was causally associated with SjD, and (2) VDR‐BVs previously implicated in MS are associated with SjD risk. We analyzed cases and controls from the Sjögren's International Collaborative Clinical Alliance (SICCA) registry and a subset of external controls, including individuals of European and Asian genetic ancestry. We hypothesized that lower 25(OH)D is associated with increased SjD susceptibility, and that genetic variation in VDR binding, known to disrupt vitamin D‐mediated gene regulation, contributes to SjD risk. Identifying VDR‐BVs associated with SjD may provide insight into biologic mechanisms linking vitamin D to SjD and other autoimmune diseases.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

SICCA cohort

Our study consisted of 5,579 individuals, including 2,129 individuals from the multiethnic SICCA cohort as previously described. 27 Of these, we analyzed 556 cases and 920 controls of European ancestry and 428 cases and 225 controls of Asian ancestry. Case‐control status for SICCA participants was determined according to the latest 2016 American College of Rheumatology (ACR)/ EULAR criteria for SjD. 28 The final classification criteria are based on the weighted sum of five items: anti‐SSA/Ro antibody positivity and focal lymphocytic sialadenitis (FLS) with a focus score (FS) ≥1 foci/mm2, each scoring 3; an abnormal ocular staining score (OSS) ≥5 (or van Bijsterveld score ≥4), a Schirmer test ≤ 5 mm/5 minute, and an unstimulated salivary flow rate (UWS) ≤0.1 mL/min, each scoring 1. Individuals with signs and symptoms suggestive of SjD who had a total score ≥4 met the criteria for primary SjD as a case. SICCA controls were defined as individuals with unrelated, symptomatic non‐SjD who did not meet diagnostic criteria for primary SjD.

Blood samples were collected from each participant for DNA extraction. This study was approved by the local institutional review boards of participating centers. All patients signed assent and consent forms for this study.

External controls

Genotype data for an additional 2,557 European controls and 893 Asian controls were obtained from three external database of Genotypes and Phenotypes datasets: Age‐Related Eye Disease Study (AREDS; phs000001.v3.p1); Collaborative Study of Nicotine Dependence (COGEND; phs000404.v1.p1); and IgA Nephropathy Genome‐Wide Association Study (IGANGWAS; phs000431.v2.p1), as previously described. 27 Controls from AREDS were European ancestry and did not have age‐related macular degeneration. Controls from COGEND were of multiple ethnicities recruited from Detroit, Michigan and St. Louis, Missouri. Controls from IGANGWAS were healthy blood donors and all of Chinese Han ancestry. 27

Genetic data and quality control measures

SNP genotyping was performed using the Illumina Infinium Omni 2.5 platform array and imputed against reference haplotypes from the Haplotype Reference Consortium using IMPUTE4 on the Michigan Imputation Server. 27 All analyses used variants aligned to the GRCh37/hg19 genome build, with allele harmonization and strand checks performed across datasets; variant‐level positions, minor allele frequencies (MAFs), and imputation quality metrics (R2) are reported in Supplementary Tables 1A to 1C). Genome‐wide, post‐imputation quality control excluded variants with imputation quality R2 < 0.3, MAF < 1%, genotyping rate < 90%, or deviations from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium among controls (P < 0.000001). Variants included in analytic models were generally required to meet stricter quality thresholds (imputation R2 ≥ 0.8 and MAF ≥ 5%), with imputation quality additionally evaluated within ancestry‐specific subgroups. In the Asian subgroup, two VDR‐BVs had moderate imputation quality (R2 0.6–0.8) and were retained in primary analyses; excluding these variants in sensitivity analyses did not alter results. Remaining missing genotype values were assigned using the mean allele frequency. To reduce potential confounding by population stratification, the multiethnic cohort (N = 5,716: 1,045 cases and 4,671 controls) was restricted to two study subgroups comprising individuals with primarily European and Asian genetic ancestry, yielding a final study sample of 5,579 individuals (984 cases and 4,595 controls, including 1,145 symptomatic non‐SjD controls and 3,450 healthy external controls). Genome‐wide genetic ancestry was inferred using SNPweights 29 , 30 version 2.1.

Identifying valid genetic instruments for VDR binding

VDR occupancy at a given genomic locus was the exposure of interest. Three hundred and five VDR‐BVs were identified as reference candidates for constructing the GIV for each VDR‐BV (hereafter, GIVVDR). 25 The seven VDR‐BV candidates included in the primary analysis were previously shown to be significantly associated with VDR binding at loci in MS. 31 Six of these seven VDR‐BVs were present in the European subgroup for analysis; all seven were present in the Asian subgroup. A GIVVDR was derived for each of these individual VDR‐BVs by multiplying the dosage of the effect allele for VDR binding by the SNP‐VDR binding effect size. 25

Linkage disequilibrium (LD) between VDR‐BVs and established SjD GWAS risk variants 32 was estimated in Plink1.9 with the –r2 function using the European LD reference panel from 1,000 Genomes Phase 3.

Bioavailability of vitamin D

Because VDR binding depends on the bioavailability of vitamin D, we used summary statistics, estimated effect sizes, and variants from two recent GWAS 20 , 21 to construct the main GIVs for bioavailability of vitamin D (hereafter GIV25(OH)D) as previously described. 31 The MR‐Base R package was used to extract GWAS summary statistics. 33 The extract_instruments() function in the TwoSampleMR R package was used to identify independent variants with GWAS P < 1 × 10−8 within genomic windows of 10,000 kb (R2 < 0.001) using a European LD reference panel 33 from 1,000 Genomes Phase 3. This approach yielded 8 SNPs for the Jiang et al 21 GIV25(OH)D and 83 SNPs for the Revez et al. 20 GIV25(OH)D in European individuals and 8 and 79 SNPs, respectively, in Asian individuals (Supplementary Tables 5 and 6). Each GIV25(OH)D was calculated in Plink1.9 using score SUM after identifying these reference SNPs and standardized (centered and scaled) using the scale() function in R. 34 , 35 We calculated three additional GIVs25(OH)D 36 , 37 , 38 for comparison based on other studies of serum vitamin D using the same methods (Supplementary Tables 7–9).

Filtering and refining the GIVs25 (OH)D

An SNP individually demonstrating a stronger association with the outcome than the exposure suggests horizontal pleiotropy, indicating a direct GIV‐outcome relationship that violates the exclusion restriction assumption and can compromise the validity of causal inference in MR studies. 39 Based on this principle, the GIVs25(OH)D in the present study were evaluated for their validity as proxies for 25(OH)D on the causal pathway. MR plots were generated for all five GIVs25(OH)D in European and Asian subgroups using β‐values and standard errors from logistic regressions of SNP associations with 25(OH)D (exposure) and SjD (outcome), visualized in R with ggplot2 (Revez et al 20 and Jiang et al 21 MR plots shown in Supplementary Figures 3 and 4). For GIVs based on GWAS with available summary statistics in ieugwasr, 20 , 21 , 36 SNP harmonization was performed using the TwoSampleMR package. 40 After harmonization, Steiger filtering was applied; and variants flagged as invalid were subsequently excluded. 39 , 41 Although GWAS summary statistics were available, Steiger filtering was not applied to the Jiang et al 21 GIV25(OH)D due to the small number of SNPs present (eight in both European and Asian subgroups). 41 GIVs with fewer SNPs may have reduced instrument strength, potentially introducing weak instrument bias and horizontal pleiotropy. 42 For studies without available GWAS summary statistics, 37 , 38 variants were filtered by visually inspecting MR plots to compare SNP‐outcome and SNP‐exposure β‐values to determine if the magnitude of the SNP‐outcome association was greater than the SNP‐exposure association. To assess the robustness of the findings, the filtered GIVs25(OH)D—excluding the SNPs identified as invalid through Steiger filtering or by visual inspection—were analyzed in separate logistic regression models in a sensitivity analysis using the statistical methods described subsequently. 41

Separate meta‐analyses were conducted to combine results from European and Asian subgroups for the rs2531804 GIVVDR and GIV25(OH)D. 31 All meta‐analyses were executed using metagen() in the Meta R package. Between‐study variance was estimated using the DerSimonian–Laird estimator and between‐study heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran's Q statistic and Higgins and Thompson's I 2 statistic. 43 P values calculated from associations between each GIVVDR and SjD and the interaction parameter between each GIVVDR and GIV25(OH)D were corrected for multiple tests using the Benjamini–Hochberg method (BH). 44 All models for both European and Asian subgroups were adjusted for the first five genome‐wide principal components (PCs) and sex. PCs were calculated separately for the two genetic ancestry groups (European and Asian groups) in Plink 1.9 using pca. 34

Power calculations were performed for the two subgroups using the pwr.2p2n.test function in the pwr R package. 45 We had approximately 90% and 80% power to detect a difference as low as 0.01 in 25(OH)D scores for European and Asian subgroups, respectively.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.0.2. 35

All SICCA study‐specific SjD cases and controls are included in Supplementary Table 1. SjD cases in the European and Asian subgroups were exclusively recruited from the SICCA cohort as described previously. 27 Among the 3,477 European controls, 920 (26.5%) were from the SICCA cohort; and among the 1,118 Asian controls, 225 (20.1%) were SICCA participants. VDR‐BVs associated with SjD susceptibility

Of the seven candidate GIVsVDR previously shown to be significantly associated with MS 31 (P < 0.05), rs2531804 showed a significant association with SjD in European individuals (OR 0.62, 95% CI 0.43–0.83; Figure 2C) following correction for multiple testing (P BH < 0.05; Supplementary Table 4). However, none of the seven candidate GIVsVDR were associated with SjD in the Asian subgroup (Supplementary Table 4). When analyses were restricted to anti‐SSA/Ro positive cases and compared to controls, similar results for rs2531804 were observed in the European and Asian subgroups, though this GIVVDR was not significant after multiple testing correction in Asian individuals (European group OR 0.52, 95% CI 0.35–0.76, P BH = 0.006; Asian group OR 0.53, 95% CI 0.30–0.98, P BH = 0.29, Supplementary Table 12). Rs2881514 also had a significant association with SjD in European subjects but was not significant after multiple testing correction (OR 0.83, 95% CI 0.71–1.00, P BH = 0.13; Supplementary Table 4). Meta‐analyses of the GIVVDR for rs2531804 revealed a significant association between VDR binding and SjD across both European and Asian subjects (Meta OR 0.64, 95% CI 0.50–0.81, P = 0.0003, Figure 2C). No significant heterogeneity was observed between the groups (P = 0.67, Figure 2C).

We also sought to replicate candidate GIVVDR and GIV25(OH)D interactions previously identified for MS. Results were negative (Table 3). As a secondary approach, we considered the full set of available VDR‐BVs for their interactions with the GIVs25(OH)D. Two unique GIVsVDR (rs10069743 and rs6867299) showed evidence of interaction with the Jiang et al 21 and Revez et al 20 GIVs25(OH)D, respectively, in both the European and Asian subgroups (P < 0.05), although neither remained significant after multiple testing correction (Supplementary Figure 1).

VDR‐BVs and GIVs25 (OH)D associated with SjD clinical phenotypes

The GIVsVDR were also tested for their associations with the five ACR/EULAR SjD clinical phenotypes (anti‐SSA/Ro antibody positivity and FLS with an FS ≥1 foci/mm2; an abnormal OSS ≥5; a Schirmer test ≤5 mm/5 minute; and an UWS ≤0.1 mL/min) as outcomes. 28 Among the seven GIVsVDR previously implicated in MS (P < 0.05), rs2531804 was significantly associated with baseline anti‐SSA/Ro antibody positivity in European subjects and remained significant following multiple testing correction (OR 0.47, 95% CI 0.32–0.68, P BH < 0.05; Supplementary Figure 2). Another GIVVDR, rs1005126, was also significantly associated with baseline anti‐SSA/Ro and remained significant after multiple testing correction in European subjects (OR 2.27, 95% CI 1.45–3.54, P BH < 0.05, Supplementary Figure 2). No GIVsVDR were significantly associated with any of the five clinical phenotypes after multiple testing correction in Asian subjects.

Although the GIV25(OH)D for Jiang et al was significantly associated with baseline anti‐SSA/Ro antibody positivity phenotype (P < 0.05) in European individuals, it did not remain significant after multiple testing correction (Supplementary Table 13). In the Asian subgroup, neither Jiang et al 21 nor the Revez et al 20 GIV25(OH)D was significantly associated with any of the five clinical phenotypes (Supplementary Table 14). In a case‐only analysis restricted to ACR/EULAR‐defined cases, neither of the GIVs25(OH)D 20 , 21 showed significant associations with any of the five clinical phenotypes in either the European or Asian subgroups (Supplementary Tables 15 and 16).

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, the current study is the first to examine VDR binding in SjD. Both European and Asian individuals were investigated. We observed evidence suggesting that altered VDR binding may influence SjD susceptibility by modifying vitamin D‐dependent gene transcription, further supporting the role of the vitamin D pathway in SjD. Of the seven GIVsVDR previously implicated in MS (P < 0.05), rs2531804 was also shown to be independently associated with SjD in the European subgroup and remained statistically significant after correction for multiple testing (P BH < 0.05). 31 The GIVVDR rs2531804 demonstrated a protective effect of increased binding at that locus on SjD risk in European subjects. This GIVVDR was also significantly associated with the presence of anti‐SSA/Ro antibody positivity at baseline in European subjects (P BH < 0.05). Although VDR binding was associated with SjD in both the European and Asian subgroups, decreased 25(OH)D showed no significant association with SjD across any of the five GIVs25(OH)D tested in either subgroup. This agrees with previous MR studies with larger sample sizes, which also found no association between 25(OH)D and SjD. 17 , 37

It has been suggested that anti‐SSA/Ro antibody positivity may define a phenotypically distinct subgroup of SjD patients who have more systemic disease 47 ; as such we also ran separate analyses restricting our case group to only those who were anti‐SSA/Ro+. 48 In tests of both the GIVs25(OH)D and GIVsVDR in anti‐SSA/Ro+ cases and all controls, our results were consistent in both the European and Asian subgroups: rs2531804 remained significant after multiple testing correction and decreased 25(OH)D showed no evidence of association with SjD. The robustness of our findings was further supported by sensitivity analyses limited to all SjD cases and healthy external controls (excluding symptomatic SICCA controls). These analyses consistently reproduced the null association between decreased serum 25(OH)D and SjD in both ancestry groups and revealed an even stronger protective effect of rs2531804 on SjD susceptibility in European subjects. These results support the possibility that genetically mediated variation in VDR binding may influence SjD susceptibility through pathways distinct from serum 25(OH)D, highlighting receptor‐level regulation as a potential component of autoimmune disease risk.

Previous MR studies, employing methods identical to the present study, have found rs2531804 to be causally associated with MS. 31 , 46 rs2531804 is located upstream of ZSCAN3 on chromosome 6. ZSCAN3 enables sequence‐specific double‐stranded DNA binding activity and is involved in the regulation of transcription by RNA polymerase II. 49 The observed associations between rs2531804 and disease susceptibility in both this study and previous MS research suggest that variation in VDR binding at this locus may influence the pathogenesis of multiple autoimmune diseases. Further studies should investigate this locus in other autoimmune and rheumatic conditions.

Over 20 unique GIVsVDR, none of which overlapped with the seven candidate GIVsVDR, showed evidence of interaction with one or both GIVs25(OH)D in the European and Asian subgroups, though none remained significant after correction for multiple testing. Interestingly, two distinct GIVsVDR—rs10069743 and rs6867299—demonstrated nominally significant interactions with the GIVs25(OH)D across both European and Asian subgroups (P < 0.05); however, these associations were not significant after correction for multiple testing. In European subjects, the T allele at rs10069743 increased the risk of SjD in the presence of decreased bioavailability of 25(OH)D, whereas in Asian subjects, it demonstrated a protective effect. In contrast, the T allele at rs6867299 conferred a predisposing effect in both groups in the presence of decreased bioavailability of 25(OH)D. It is worth highlighting that detecting interactions demands significantly more statistical power than identifying main effects but observing associations between VDR‐BVs and SjD in both the interaction and independent models is noteworthy.

Although previous studies have demonstrated a potentially causal association between decreased 25(OH)D and MS, no association with SjD was observed in the current study, despite being sufficiently powered to detect the effect sizes previously reported. 18 , 31 , 46 Both the unfiltered and Steiger‐filtered GIVs25(OH)D demonstrated null effects for the causal relationship between 25(OH)D and SjD in the European and Asian subgroups. All five GIVs25(OH)D centered at or around an OR of 1.0 and were not statistically significant at P < 0.05, suggesting that any causal effect must be smaller, and thus, would require a much larger sample size to detect. Alternatively, although heterogeneity in VDR binding has been causally implicated in both SjD and MS as autoimmune diseases, these diseases are clinically and biologically distinct. In agreement, no GIVs25(OH)D were significantly associated with the ACR/EULAR SjD case criteria clinical phenotypes in either the European or Asian subgroups after multiple testing correction. The GIV25(OH)D for Jiang et al 21 was significantly associated with the baseline anti‐SSA/Ro antibody positivity clinical phenotype (P < 0.05) in European individuals but did not remain significant after multiple testing correction. Additional analyses restricted to EULAR‐defined SjD cases also did not provide evidence for a causal association between 25(OH)D and any of the five clinical phenotypes. Together, these findings suggest that the vitamin D pathway implicated in MS pathogenesis does not play a similar causal role in SjD.

This study had several strengths. We applied MR methods previously used in studies of MS to examine the relationship between vitamin D and SjD. 31 Specifically, we focused on candidate VDR‐BVs identified by Gallone et al 25 to construct GIVs for VDR binding at a specific locus, and on SNPs previously shown to be associated with serum vitamin D levels to construct GIVs for vitamin D bioavailability. 20 , 21 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 50 Given the challenges of confounding and reverse causation when assessing causal pathways between exposures and outcomes, this study would have been difficult to conduct without the use of MR methods. We studied both European and Asian subjects together for the first time; applying this study methodology to a non‐European population is novel, because previous studies in MS were limited to individuals of European ancestry. Our study findings extend generalizability to an additional genetic ancestry group and set a precedent for future research in other genetic ancestry groups.

There were also some limitations in the study; horizontal pleiotropy may have biased associations between GIVsVDR and SjD, and constructing each GIVVDR with only one VDR‐BV precluded the use of standard sensitivity analyses to assess MR assumptions in our VDR‐BV analyses. In addition, this study was only conducted in two distinct genetic ancestry groups; many others exist and should also be tested with similar methods. Although we adjusted for sex and genetic ancestry using genome‐wide PCs within each subgroup, demographic and recruitment differences between cases and controls—particularly in age, sex, and control sources—may have introduced residual confounding. Nonetheless, using five distinct GIVs for 25(OH)D with nonoverlapping SNPs as additional GIVs was unique and allowed us to estimate the associations between GIVVDR and SjD unbiased by horizontal pleiotropy. The expanded set of five GIVs25(OH)D—compared to two in previous studies—was further refined using Steiger harmonization and filtering to strengthen instrument validity and reduce weak instrument bias, capturing additional loci potentially implicated in serum 25(OH)D levels and their causal relationship with SjD. 39 , 41

Our study design is focused on identifying causal mechanisms of SjD susceptibility mediated by DNA variation‐induced changes in VDR binding. Although our findings highlight the complexity and importance of the vitamin D pathway in SjD development, other causal mechanisms likely contribute to the observed VDR‐SjD associations. Future research should explore VDR binding across a broader range of loci and use larger, more genetically diverse cohorts to further elucidate this relationship. Further studies are needed to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the role of vitamin D in SjD pathogenesis.
