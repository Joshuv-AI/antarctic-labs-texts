Multimodal Salvage Protocol for the Treatment of Severe Postrhinoplasty Nasal Vestibular Stenosis: A Case Report
Authors: Than Trong Huy Hoang, Quoc Khanh Pham Trinh, Nguyen Hoang Nhan, Lam Hoang Phuong, Bui Cao Vinh, Duong Cong Dien
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSevere nasal vestibular stenosis is an increasingly recognized iatrogenic complication of cosmetic rhinoplasty. Multiply-revised cases are particularly difficult to manage because previous procedures may compromise the vestibular microcirculation, whereas prolonged stenting alone risks ischemic restenosis. We describe an individualized, staged multimodal salvage approach and illustrate it in a single patient. Stage 1 reopened the obstructed vestibule with scar excision, a local skin flap, and a custom stent, followed by ∼3 months of cold atmospheric plasma (CAP) and monthly intralesional platelet-rich plasma (PRP) intended to condition the scar bed and improve tissue pliability. Stage 2 provided definitive aesthetic reconstruction tailored to the anatomical defects, supported by a 12-month adjunctive regimen of tapered nasal stenting, peri-incisional PRP, a CAP cycle alternating antimicrobial and wound-healing modes, and intralesional triamcinolone acetonide at escalating concentrations (5-40 mg/mL). The patient was a 27-year-old woman with bilateral vestibular stenosis and complete left nostril obstruction after 15 previous nasal procedures. After Stage 1, bilateral airflow was clinically improved and the vestibular tissue was softer and better perfused on examination at 3 months. Stage 2 employed a philtral advancement flap, costal and auricular cartilage grafts, and a buccal mucosal graft. At more than 3 years, nasal airflow and aesthetic contour remained stable on clinical assessment, with no recurrence of stenosis and no further stenting. This single case suggests that a staged surgical strategy combined with a structured biologic and stenting regimen may be a useful salvage option; further cases and objective functional assessment are required to confirm its value. Level of Evidence: 4 (Therapeutic)

Nasal vestibular stenosis is the narrowing of the airway region between the external and internal nasal valves. Although uncommon, it produces both functional obstruction and aesthetic deformity and significantly impairs quality of life when severe.1 Recognized causes include trauma, congenital anomaly, infection (notably rhinoscleroma), and iatrogenic injury, with cosmetic rhinoplasty increasingly reported as a cause.2

Surgical options range from scar release with stenting to composite auricular grafting, local skin or mucosal flaps, and full-thickness skin grafts.3 For larger or recalcitrant defects, distant and regional flaps—including the paramedian forehead flap and intraoral (buccal) musculomucosal pull-through techniques—have also been described for restoring vestibular lining and external cover.4,5 Postoperative stenting is essential to counteract centripetal scar contracture and is typically maintained for 6 to 12 months until the scar matures.2,6 However, prolonged stent compression can produce ischemia, ulceration, secondary infection, and ultimately restenosis—a risk that is amplified when the vestibular microvasculature has already been compromised by previous rhinoplasty.7

Multiply-revised postrhinoplasty vestibular stenosis is therefore particularly hazardous to manage: corrective surgery induces additional scarring, and conventional adjuncts such as long-duration stenting alone may worsen the underlying ischemia. We describe a structured, individualized multimodal salvage approach that combines staged surgical reconstruction with a defined biologic regimen of platelet-rich plasma (PRP), cold atmospheric plasma (CAP), and intralesional triamcinolone, together with a tapered, off-cycle custom-stent schedule. The approach is illustrated by an extreme case of vestibular stenosis after 15 previous nasal procedures, with more than 3 years of stable follow-up.

PROTOCOL OVERVIEW

The approach described here is individualized and tailored to each patient's pattern of scarring, tissue loss, and residual vestibular perfusion; the specific surgical components, dosing, and duration are adapted to the case requirements rather than applied as a fixed protocol. In general terms, it is staged. Stage 1 (vestibular reopening) reestablishes nasal airflow and conditions the scar bed. Stage 2 (definitive aesthetic reconstruction) addresses each anatomical defect individually and is followed by a 12-month adjunctive regimen designed to suppress fibrotic recurrence while preserving vestibular perfusion. The interval between stages depends on tissue response and is assessed monthly by clinical examination, anterior rhinoscopy, and clinical assessment of vestibular pliability and capillary refill; no objective perfusion measurement (eg, laser Doppler flowmetry or indocyanine green angiography) was used in this case.

Stage 1: Vestibular Reopening and Conditioning

Stage 1 consists of scar excision and reopening of the obstructed vestibule using a local skin flap, with simultaneous placement of a custom-fitted nasal stent. Postoperative care includes standard wound care with topical tetracycline ointment, a short course of oral antibiotic, low-dose corticosteroid, antihistamine, and analgesia, complemented by CAP therapy applied to the surgical site and monthly intralesional PRP. The conditioning phase runs for ∼3 months, and progression to Stage 2 is permitted only when the columellar and vestibular skin has clinically transitioned from fibrotic scar to supple, well-vascularized tissue and the patient can ventilate through both nostrils with the stent in place.

Stage 2: Definitive Aesthetic Reconstruction

Stage 2 is tailored to the individual defect. Components employed in the present case included a philtral advancement flap combined with a philtral–upper lip Z-plasty to lengthen the columella and help correct the nostril stenosis; a columellar strut graft, paired spreader grafts to reinforce the septum and internal nasal valve, and a unilateral batten graft using remaining auricular and costal cartilage; and a buccal mucosal graft combined with a vestibular-floor rotation flap to resurface the lateral vestibular wall and restore the vestibular lining.

Custom Nasal Stent

The custom nasal stent used in this case was a BioCrocell stent, a custom-fabricated, noncommercial device made to the patient's vestibular dimensions. It was made of medical-grade silicone and shaped to the measured vestibular diameter and length (∼15 mm). It was secured by external taping and was removed, cleaned (daily irrigation with normal saline and 90% alcohol), and replaced as instructed. To prevent pressure necrosis, the stent was removed every 4 to 6 h for a 10 to 15 min rest period before reinsertion. It was well tolerated, and no pressure necrosis, ulceration, dislodgement, or intolerance was observed during stented periods.

Twelve-Month Adjunctive Regimen

After Stage 2, the patient received a structured 12-month adjunctive regimen, summarized in Table 1. Systemic pharmacologic cover comprised cefixime, methylprednisolone, chlorpheniramine, and acetaminophen. Peri-incisional PRP was injected at the conclusion of surgery and repeated monthly. CAP was delivered in a tapered cycle alternating antimicrobial and wound-healing modes. Intralesional triamcinolone acetonide was administered at escalating concentrations (5, 10, 20, and 40 mg/mL) over 3 months to suppress renewed fibroblast activity. The custom nasal stent was worn on a tapered schedule (24 h/day for Months 1-6, 18 h/day for Months 7-9, and 12 h/day for Months 10-12), with regular off-cycle breaks to allow vestibular reperfusion. Platelet-Rich Plasma Preparation and Delivery

PRP was prepared from 20 mL of whole blood using the RegenKit-BCT-3 system (Regen Lab SA, Le Mont-sur-Lausanne, Switzerland). Centrifugation used 1500 × g for 5 min; the resulting platelet concentration was not quantified. The PRP was not activated (no exogenous calcium chloride or thrombin was added). A total of 2 mL per session was injected into the subdermal and peri-incisional scar bed at ∼10 points, repeated monthly.

Cold Atmospheric Plasma Parameters

CAP was delivered with the MIRARI Cold Plasma System (model GV-M2-01; General Vibronics, Inc., Tempe, AZ), a dielectric-barrier-discharge system. Treatment was applied at a distance of 10 mm from the tissue for 10 to 20 min per site per session, daily, alternating antimicrobial and wound-healing modes. Standard ocular and operator safety precautions were observed, and treatment was continued to the endpoint of epithelialization. No CAP-related adverse effects (thermal injury, pain, and dyspigmentation) were observed.

Intralesional Triamcinolone

Triamcinolone acetonide was injected intralesionally into the alar scar and vestibular cicatrix at escalating concentrations (5, 10, 20, then 40 mg/mL), 0.1 mL per site (total 5-10 mg per session), at ∼3 week intervals over 3 months. No steroid-related adverse effects—cutaneous atrophy, hypopigmentation, telangiectasia, cartilage compromise, or mucosal thinning—were observed during follow-up.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 27-year-old woman presented in November 2021 with a history of 15 previous nasal procedures, including 1 standard rhinoplasty, 2 alar-base reductions, and 7 structural rhinoplasties using auricular and costal cartilage. By the 10th procedure, she had developed complete tip and columellar retraction with bilateral vestibular stenosis and severe airway obstruction. The 11th surgery used bilateral auricular composite grafts to widen the nostrils and vestibule but did not correct the underlying retraction. Surgeries 12 to 15, performed consecutively within fewer than 3 months using nasolabial flaps for tip and columellar reconstruction, resulted in fibrotic contracture, complete left vestibular occlusion, and stiffening of the entire left ala. Stage 1 Outcome

The 16th surgery (November 2021) reopened the left vestibule by scar excision and a local skin flap, with simultaneous placement of the custom nasal stent. Postoperatively, the patient received CAP therapy at the surgical site, monthly intralesional PRP, and the medical regimen described above. After 3 months, the columellar and vestibular skin had clinically transitioned from fibrotic scar to supple tissue with improved capillary refill, and the patient could breathe through both nostrils when stented (Figure 2). These changes were assessed clinically (inspection, palpation of pliability, and capillary refill on anterior rhinoscopy); no objective perfusion imaging was performed.

Stage 2 and Long-Term Outcome

Definitive reconstruction (17th surgery, February 2022) addressed each anatomical defect using the components described above (Figure 3). The 12-month adjunctive regimen (Table 1) was completed without complication. At more than 3 years of follow-up (June 2025), the patient demonstrated stable aesthetic and respiratory outcomes on clinical assessment, with no recurrence of vestibular stenosis. Stent use had been discontinued, and she reported breathing normally through both nostrils, with no functional or cosmetic complaints (Figure 4). DISCUSSION

Nasal vestibular stenosis is notoriously difficult to treat because corrective procedures themselves induce further scarring and restenosis, generating a cycle that is hard to interrupt.2,7 The vestibule's circular geometry promotes centripetal scar contraction, and its limited cartilaginous support—confined to the alar and septal cartilages—leaves the lumen vulnerable to collapse once fibrosis exceeds the supporting framework.8 Diagnosis is largely clinical, with anterior rhinoscopy and nasal endoscopy defining the location and severity of narrowing; rhinomanometry, acoustic rhinometry, validated patient-reported instruments, such as the Nasal Obstruction Symptom Evaluation (NOSE) scale, and cross-sectional imaging may complement the assessment when objective functional or structural data are required.9

Standard management combines scar excision with stenting to mechanically resist contracture; stents are typically maintained for 6 to 12 months until the scar matures, and stenting has been shown to maintain luminal diameter in acquired and congenital stenoses.1,2,7,10 When local tissue is inadequate, regional and distant flaps provide additional lining and cover: the paramedian forehead flap is a robust axial option for extensive defects and can be folded to reconstruct vestibular lining, and intraoral buccal/facial artery musculomucosal pull-through flaps can resurface the vestibule.4,5 These options were considered in the present case but were not required, because adequate lining and cover were achievable with the philtral advancement flap, local rotation, and a buccal mucosal graft. In multiply-revised postrhinoplasty cases, several consecutive surgeries within a short interval may compromise the vestibular microcirculation and trigger prolonged inflammation, so stenting alone risks further ischemia, ulceration, and restenosis, and adjunctive biologic therapy was used here as a complementary measure.6

Three complementary biologic treatments were combined. The mechanisms summarized below are drawn from previous published reports and were not directly assessed in this patient. Triamcinolone, a fluorinated corticosteroid roughly 4 times more potent than hydrocortisone, suppresses fibroblast proliferation, downregulates transforming growth factor beta-1 (TGF-β1), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and insulin-like growth factor 1, and increases collagenase activity by reducing α-2-macroglobulin and α-1-antitrypsin levels—all of which limit hypertrophic and keloid scar formation.11 Intralesional 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) is an alternative antifibrotic that inhibits fibroblast proliferation and is often combined with corticosteroid in keloid management; in this case, triamcinolone monotherapy was selected because of the team's established experience with dose escalation in the vestibule and to avoid additional mucosal irritation, although a triamcinolone–5-FU combination is a reasonable option in future or refractory cases.12 PRP releases platelet-derived growth factor, TGF-β, VEGF, epidermal growth factor, and basic fibroblast growth factor from activated platelet α-granules, stimulating angiogenesis, reprogramming pathological fibroblasts toward a less fibrotic phenotype, and recruiting mesenchymal progenitor cells.13 CAP, a nonthermal ionized gas containing reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, including nitric oxide, exerts broad antimicrobial activity, modulates pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines, and promotes neovascularization through hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha-driven VEGF expression.14

The sequencing of these adjuncts was deliberate. The conditioning phase (Stage 1) used CAP and PRP with the intention of softening scar and improving the tissue bed before definitive reconstruction; monthly clinical assessment of inflammatory status, pliability, and capillary refill guided the timing of Stage 2. After Stage 2, escalating triamcinolone injections were intended to suppress renewed fibroblast activity, whereas continued PRP and CAP were intended to support angiogenesis and immunomodulation. The tapered, off-cycle stent schedule reduces mechanical compression as scar maturation progresses, allowing intermittent reperfusion and aiming to prevent the chronic ischemia that drives restenosis. Because all components were applied together in a single patient, the individual contribution of each cannot be determined from this case.

Limitations

This report has several important limitations. First, it describes a single patient, so the approach cannot be considered reproducible or generalizable on the basis of this experience alone, and success in one extreme case does not predict success in others. Second, no objective functional airway assessment (rhinomanometry, acoustic rhinometry, and peak nasal inspiratory flow) and no validated patient-reported outcome measure (eg, NOSE or visual analog scale breathing score) were collected prospectively; respiratory outcomes are therefore based on clinical examination and patient report rather than quantitative measurement. Third, no objective perfusion or microcirculatory evaluation (laser Doppler flowmetry, indocyanine green angiography, capillaroscopy, or histology) was performed, so statements regarding tissue “perfusion” and “microcirculation” reflect clinical impression rather than measured data. Fourth, because the surgical and biologic components were delivered together, the effect of any single element—PRP, CAP, triamcinolone, or stenting—cannot be isolated, and the mechanisms cited for each are based on previous reports rather than on assessment in this case. Finally, follow-up, although exceeding 3 years, was clinical only. Future work using comparative or prospective designs with standardized, validated functional and perfusion endpoints will be required to establish whether this multimodal approach offers a reproducible benefit in severe postrhinoplasty vestibular stenosis.

CONCLUSIONS

In this single, extreme case of severe postrhinoplasty nasal vestibular stenosis, a staged surgical correction combined with customized stenting and an adjunctive biologic regimen of PRP, CAP, and intralesional triamcinolone was associated with stable aesthetic and respiratory outcomes at more than 3 years of clinical follow-up. This experience suggests that such a multimodal salvage approach may be a useful option to consider in selected severe cases when the vestibular microcirculation is thought to have been compromised by previous procedures. However, the approach is illustrated by 1 patient without objective functional assessment, and further cases and prospective, objectively measured data are required before broader conclusions can be drawn.
