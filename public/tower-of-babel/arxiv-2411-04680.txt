Privacy Leakage via Output Label Space and Differentially Private Continual Learning
Marlon Tobaben marlon.tobaben@csc.fi

Abstract
Differential privacy (DP) is a formal privacy framework that enables training machine learning (ML) models while protecting individuals’ data. As pointed out by prior work, ML models are part of larger systems, which can lead to so-called privacy side-channels even if the model training itself is DP. We identify the output label space of a classification model as such a privacy side-channel and show a concrete privacy attack that exploits it. The side-channel becomes highly relevant in continual learning (CL), where the output label space changes over time. To reason about privacy guarantees in CL, we introduce a formalisation of DP for CL, which also clarifies how our approach differs from existing approaches. We propose and evaluate two methods for eliminating this side-channel: applying an optimal DP mechanism to release the labels in the sensitive data, and using a large public label space. We explore the trade-offs of these methods through adapting pre-trained models. We demonstrate empirically that our models consistently achieve higher accuracy under DP than previous work over both Split-CIFAR-100 and Split-ImageNet-R, with a stronger privacy model.

This privacy side-channel is relevant to all DP classification problems, but particularly relevant in the context of continual learning
(
McCloskey & Cohen 1989
;
De Lange et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2024
, CL;)
. CL studies models that learn from data arriving as a sequential stream of tasks.
Therefore, the output label space needs to be constantly updated as new labels appear with the sequential stream of tasks.
CL methods can run over years, which means that new labels can be unknown at the beginning of training. Furthermore,
CL is a difficult problem setting as it faces the challenge of catastrophic forgetting, where a model loses performance on earlier tasks as it learns new ones
(
French 1999
)
.
Prior work combining DP and CL only considers preventing privacy leakage through the model weights
(
Farquhar & Gal 2019
;
Desai et al. 2021
;
Hassanpour et al. 2022
;
Lai et al. 2022
)
.
However, naively adding the new labels to the model’s output label space during training can lead to a catastrophic privacy failure, even if the model weights are trained with a DP algorithm. This has been overlooked in existing work (potentially due to the focus on standard benchmarks with a fixed list of labels).

To avoid the privacy side-channel, we offer two DP methods to protect the model’s output label space. Prior DP CL work uses the entire privacy budget for training weights. However, our first method (
Section

4.2
), inspired by the private partition selection problem
(
Desfontaines et al. 2022
;
Gopi et al. 2020
;
Korolova et al. 2009
)
, splits the budget between selecting class labels from the sensitive dataset and training the weights. Therefore, in this case, there is a trade-off where spending more of the privacy budget on selecting the labels will leave less of the budget for training the weights. The second method that we propose decouples the class labels from the sensitive dataset by using a large data-independent prior labels set that likely covers all or most dataset labels (
Section

4.3
). Unmatched labels can be remapped using known label relationships, or can be simply dropped.

To demonstrate the methods with challenging enough benchmarks, we propose DP adaptations of CL methods based on pre-training.
Improving model utility using pre-trained models has been studied separately in DP, and in CL (see
Section

2
). However, to the best of our knowledge, there is no prior work that uses pre-trained models in DP CL.
Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

1.

Classifiers’ output label space as a privacy side-channel:

We identify the classifier output label space as a privacy side-channel and show that naively releasing a data-dependent output label space is not DP (
Figs.

1
and
4.1
). This applies not only to CL but to classifier release more generally.

2.

DP mechanisms for choosing the output label space:

We propose two DP alternatives for choosing the output label space:

1

privately releasing labels from the sensitive dataset by spending part of the privacy budget (
Section

4.2
), and

2

using a data-independent public prior label set, with unmatched labels either remapped or dropped (
Section

4.3
).

3.

DP CL methods using pre-trained models:

We adapt two existing CL methods using pre-trained models to the DP setting: a Cosine Classifier based on noisy private feature sums, and a PEFT Ensemble trained with DP-SGD (
Section

5
). We evaluate these methods with the proposed label-space alternatives across different CL settings (
Section

6
) and provide practical recommendations (
Section

7
).

Related Work

Differential Privacy

DP provides provable privacy guarantees, but the main challenge with DP is the unavoidable trade-off between privacy and utility.
Recently, using pre-trained models has been shown to mitigate this trade-off

(
Kurakin et al. 2022
;
De et al. 2022
;
Tobaben et al. 2023
;
Mehta et al. 2023
;
Cattan et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2022
;
Yu et al. 2022
;
Tito et al. 2024
;
Wahdany et al. 2025
)
, with most state-of-the-art DP models relying on the assumption that any pre-training data is public.
However, if any private information is contained in the pre-training data, the DP privacy guarantees w.r.t. the fine-tuning data become meaningless
(
Tramèr et al. 2024
)
, but low-risk pre-training data exist
(
Kuznetsova et al. 2020
;
Asano et al. 2021
)
.
All operations that utilize sensitive data (directly or indirectly) must adhere to the definition of DP.
For instance, model selection and hyperparameter tuning can leak privacy
(
Thakurta & Smith 2013
;
Liu & Talwar 2019
)
.
Other interesting examples are data pre-processing
(
Hu et al. 2024
)
, data discretisation
(
Ganev et al. 2025
)
, and even the selection of pre-trained model weights
(
Gu et al. 2025
)
.

Private Partition Problem

An example for the private partition problem
(
Korolova et al. 2009
;
Gopi et al. 2020
;
Desfontaines et al. 2022
)
is creating histograms under DP, as the partitioning into bins can leak privacy if it depends on the sensitive data. We adapt the optimal DP mechanism, in terms of preserving as many classes as possible, by
Desfontaines et al. 2022
, to determine the output label space.

Privacy Side-Channels

Debenedetti et al. 2024
introduce privacy side-channels which are attacks that exploit privacy-leaking components apart from the ML model, e.g., training data filtering or input pre-processing.
Through these privacy side-channels, the DP protection of ML models can be invalidated.
Debenedetti et al. 2024
show that this can lead to near-perfect membership inference attacks.

Continual Learning

Pre-trained models in CL settings have been combined with replay
(
Ostapenko et al. 2022
)
, prompt tuning
(
Wang et al. 2022a
;
Wang et al. 2022b
)
, prototype classifiers
(
Janson et al. 2022
;
McDonnell et al. 2023
)
, and expandable Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) adapters
(
Zhao et al. 2024
;
Zhou et al. 2024b
;
Zhou et al. 2024a
)
.
We propose DP-variants of prototype classifiers and expandable adapters with pre-trained models to enhance the utility and mitigate forgetting with DP. We experiment in both the standard class-incremental learning setting without task labels
(
van de Ven et al. 2022
)
, as well as in blurry task boundary settings
(
Aljundi et al. 2019
;
Koh et al. 2022
;
Moon et al. 2023
)
.

Differentially Private Continual Learning

Previous works combining DP with CL have leveraged episodic memories
(
Lopez-Paz & Ranzato 2017
)
a.k.a. memory buffers, or DP synthetic samples:

•

Episodic memories:

Desai et al. 2021
;
Lai et al. 2022
;
Hassanpour et al. 2022
use episodic memories to mitigate catastrophic forgetting. However, this introduces privacy risks.

•

DP synthetic samples:

Farquhar & Gal 2019
train a generative model under DP, while
Chen et al. 2022
learn a small set of synthetic samples optimised towards the downstream task. However, these methods have only reported results on MNIST or CIFAR-10.
We do not use synthetic data, but recognize this as a potential future work due to powerful DP generation methods
(
Lin et al. 2024
)
.

Desai et al. 2021
operate in a setting similar to ours but rely on memory buffers. We include their method as a baseline in
Fig.

3
and demonstrate that we outperform their method without using memory buffers.

Hassanpour et al. 2022
use an easier CL setting where the task label is provided to the model at inference time (i.e., the task-incremental setting). Their method depends on specific architectural assumptions, and requires the model to be trained under certain constraints, which makes their approach incompatible with general pretrained models. In addition, they employ a memory buffer during training to mitigate catastrophic forgetting.

Lai et al. 2022
also use memory buffers and their method is restricted to a specific network architecture, preventing its application to general pretrained models.

Another limitation of prior work is the direct link of the output label space to the sensitive data which results in a privacy side-channel (see
Fig.

1
). Our proposed methods eliminate this privacy side-channel. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to discuss this limitation of applying DP to CL.

Denote the privacy mechanism at task
t=1,\ldots,T
(where
T
may be
\infty
) by

\mathcal{M}_{t}:\left(\mathcal{D}_{t};\mathcal{S}_{t-1}\right)\mapsto\left(\mathcal{Z}_{t};\mathcal{S}_{t}\right),

(1)

where
\mathcal{D}_{t}

t

\mathcal{Z}_{t}

\mathcal{S}_{t}

(\mathcal{M}_{t})_{t=1}^{T}

Background

Differential Privacy

DP formalises the idea of protecting individuals’ sensitive data.

Definition 3.1

(DP;
Dwork et al. 2006b
;
Dwork et al. 2006a
)
.

A randomised algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
is
(\epsilon,\delta,\simeq)
-DP for a binary relation
\simeq
over the space of possible datasets
\mathbb{D}
, if for any two datasets
{\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{D}
that satisfy
\simeq
, denoted
{\mathcal{D}}\simeq{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}
, and for any subset
S
of possible outcomes of
\mathcal{A}
, denoted
S\subseteq\mathrm{Range}({\mathcal{A}})
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{A}}({\mathcal{D}})\in S\right]\leq\exp{(\epsilon)}\times\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{A}}({\mathcal{D}}^{\prime})\in S\right]+\delta.

(2)

Any two datasets
{\mathcal{D}}\simeq{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}
are said to be adjacent datasets and
\simeq
is called the adjacency relation.

In deep learning, the algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
can be, for instance, a DP optimisation method such as DP-SGD
(
Rajkumar & Agarwal 2012
;
Song et al. 2013
;
Abadi et al. 2016
)
, which minimises the empirical loss under DP constraints. DP-SGD works by clipping per-sample gradients and adding noise to their sum in each iteration, resulting in a set of privacy-preserving model updates. Applying DP mechanisms multiple times is referred to as composition
(
McSherry 2010
;
Dwork et al. 2010b
;
Whitehouse et al. 2023
)
. The process of quantifying the total privacy loss under composition is known as privacy accounting
(
Koskela et al. 2020
;
Gopi et al. 2021
)
.

Task-wise DP

In CL, each task
t
is determined by a dataset
\mathcal{D}_{t}
. In this work, we generalise the standard sample-level add/remove adjacency relation to be applicable for CL. Formally, we introduce this as task-wise adjacency in the following definition. We discuss the limitations of prior adjacency relations and definitions for DP CL in
Section

B.1
.

Definition 3.2

(Task-wise Adjacency)
.

Let
\simeq
be an adjacency relation between datasets,
I\subseteq\mathbb{N}
be a set of task indices that can be either finite or infinite. A sequence of datasets
(\mathcal{D}_{t})_{t\in I}
is said to be task-wise adjacent to another sequence of datasets
(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\in I}
, denoted
(\mathcal{D}_{t})_{t\in I}\simeq_{\text{tw}}(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\in I}
, if there exists
t^{*}\in I
such that
\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}\simeq\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}^{\prime}
, and
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}
for all
t\neq t^{*}
.

Our experiments assume that an individual’s data can appear in only one
{\mathcal{D}}_{t}
. Task-wise adjacency means that only one task
t^{*}
can have a different adjacent dataset
{\mathcal{D}}_{t^{*}}\simeq{\mathcal{D}}_{t^{*}}^{\prime}
, but
t^{*}
is unknown in advance, and this corresponds to parallel composition when we protect all the tasks. Parallel composition here means that the privacy level does not degrade by introducing more tasks. To formalize this notion of DP, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 3.3

(Task-wise DP)
.

Let
\simeq
be an adjacency relation between datasets, and let
(\mathcal{A}_{t})_{t\in I}
be any sequence of mechanisms, such that
I\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is a set of task indices. Define
\mathcal{A}
as the mechanism obtained by composing
(\mathcal{A}_{t})_{t\in I}
, that is, for any sequence of datasets
(\mathcal{D}_{t})_{t\in I}
,

\mathcal{A}:(\mathcal{D}_{t})_{t\in I}\mapsto(\mathcal{A}_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{t}))_{t\in I}.

(3)

The sequence of mechanisms
(\mathcal{A}_{t})_{t\in I}
is said to satisfy task-wise
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP, if for any two task-wise adjacent sequences of datasets
(\mathcal{D}_{t})_{t\in I}\simeq_{\text{tw}}(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\in I}
, and for any
S\subseteq\mathrm{Range}(\mathcal{A})
:

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}\left(({\mathcal{D}}_{t})_{t\in I}\right)\in S\right]\leq\exp{(\epsilon)}\times\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}\left(({\mathcal{D}}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\in I}\right)\in S\right]+\delta.

(4)

To translate task-wise
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP guarantees to
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP guarantees, we need the following definition.

Definition 3.4

(Task assignment function)
.

Any function
\mathrm{assign}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow I
is called a task assignment function, that is, it splits any
\mathcal{D}
into the following sets:

\mathcal{D}_{t}=\{(\boldsymbol{x},y)\in\mathcal{D}:\mathrm{assign}\left((\boldsymbol{x},y)\right)=t\}.

(5)

This provides a way to transform
\mathcal{D}
into a sequence of datasets
\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)_{t\in I}
. We will denote
\mathrm{assign}\left[\mathcal{D}\right]=\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)_{t\in I}
,
using square brackets, to clearly distinguish it from point-wise function evaluation.

Since for any dataset
\mathcal{D}
, we can use a task assignment function to obtain a sequence of tasks
\mathrm{assign}\left[\mathcal{D}\right]=\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)_{t\in I}
, then we can precompose it with any mechanism as in
Eq.

3
to obtain
\mathcal{A}:(\mathrm{assign}[\mathcal{D}])\mapsto(\mathcal{A}_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{t}))_{t\in I}
. This mechanism can be generally written as
\mathcal{A}\circ\mathrm{assign}[\cdot]
. We can now introduce the following theorem.

Theorem 3.5
.

The sequence of mechanisms
(\mathcal{A}_{t})_{t\in I}
is task-wise
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP iff
\mathcal{A}\circ\mathrm{assign}[\cdot]
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP, where
\mathcal{A}
is defined in
Eq.

3
, for any task assignment function
\mathrm{assign}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow I
.

Corollary 3.6
.

If a sequence of mechanisms
(\mathcal{A}_{t})_{t\in I}
is task-wise
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP, then for each
t\in I
,
\mathcal{A}_{t}
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP.

The proofs of
Theorem

3.5
and
Corollary

3.6
can be both found in
Section

B.2
under their restatements. Both results can be used to translate between task-wise
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP and
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP. For composition results, especially when an individual’s data can appear in multiple tasks, one can apply
Corollary

3.6
, and then any well known composition result (e.g., advanced composition). In other words, a sequence of mechanisms
\left(\mathcal{A}_{t}\right)_{t\in I}
satisfying task-wise DP is only a property of the mechanisms themselves, and it does not limit their applicability to more general cases, e.g., when more than one task can have a different adjacent datasets. A generalization of
Definition

3.2
, where multiple tasks can have different adjacent datasets, can be found in
Definition

B.4
. Moreover, a generalization of
Definition

3.3
can be found in
Definition

B.5
. The full discussion and more details regarding composition under task-wise DP can be found in
Section

B.3
.

Continual Learning

In the general CL setting, we aim to learn a function

f_{{\bm{\theta}}}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
, parameterized by
{\bm{\theta}}
,
from a sequence of supervised classification tasks
(
De Lange et al. 2021
)
. Let the set of task indices be
I=\{1,\ldots,T\}
, each task
t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}
(where
T
can be
\infty
) is defined by a dataset
{\mathcal{D}}_{t}=\{({\bm{x}}_{t}^{(k)},y_{t}^{(k)})\}_{k=1}^{N_{t}}
, where
{\bm{x}}_{t}^{(k)}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{t}
is the
k
-th input,
{\mathcal{X}}_{t}
is the set of inputs,
y_{t}^{(k)}\in{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}
is the class label,
{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}
is the set of labels, and
N_{t}\in\mathbb{N}
is the number of samples. For the CL setting we consider, the learning algorithm has the following constraints:

(i)

At task
t
, it can only access the dataset
{\mathcal{D}}_{t}
and no other datasets;

(ii)

it cannot store any samples between tasks (e.g., through a memory buffer);

(iii)

the total number of tasks
T
and the complete label space
\mathcal{Y}=\cup_{t=1}^{T}{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}
are unknown in advance.

As a result, the algorithm does not directly produce a single final function
f_{{\bm{\theta}}}
after all the tasks, but instead learns an intermediate function
f_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}:{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{Y}}_{1:t}
, parametrised by
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
, after each task
t
, where
{\mathcal{Y}}_{1:t}=\cup_{i=1}^{t}{\mathcal{Y}}_{i}
. We call the range of
f_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}
, namely
{\mathcal{Y}}_{1:t}
, the output (label) space.

In this CL setting, task labels
t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}
are not available at inference time. Given a test sample
(\boldsymbol{x},y)
, the model must therefore correctly classify
y
without knowing the task under which the corresponding class was originally learned. This requires the model to retain knowledge of classes learned in earlier tasks. Moreover, the same class may appear in multiple tasks. For privacy reasons, all training samples are discarded upon completion of each task (point (ii) above).

Blurry Tasks

Prior work in CL
(
Koh et al. 2022
;
Moon et al. 2023
)
differentiates between
disjoint classes
, where the data of a class is only contained in one task, and
blurry classes
, that are split over multiple tasks.
The blurry sample ratio controls the blurriness with a value of
0
being equivalent to having only disjoint classes and
100
, where data of blurry classes is uniformly distributed over all tasks.

4
Classifier Output Space Privacy

As discussed at the end of
Section

3
, our goal is to learn intermediate classifiers
f_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}}:{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{Y}}_{1:t}
from a sequence of datasets
{\mathcal{D}}_{t}
,
t=1,\ldots,T
. However, releasing a classifier requires publishing both the model weights
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
and the output label space
{\mathcal{Y}}_{1:t}
. Since each
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
is a function of the sensitive data
{\mathcal{D}}_{t}
, namely the projection function
({\bm{x}}_{t}^{(k)},y_{t}^{(k)})\mapsto y_{t}^{(k)}
, publishing
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
leaks sensitive information as a privacy side-channel even if
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
is DP, as illustrated in
Fig.

1
.

To address this issue, we reframe the problem by denoting the output label space for each task
t
as
{\mathcal{O}}_{t}
. This allows us to separate the released
{\mathcal{O}}_{t}
from the sensitive labels
{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}
, and to explore alternative choices for
{\mathcal{O}}_{t}
that ensure the classifier remains DP. Additionally, for each task
t
, we will denote the parameters of the classifier as
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
which implicitly depend on
\mathcal{O}_{t}
. In
Eq.

1
, we can set
\mathcal{Z}_{t}=\left({\bm{\theta}}_{t},{\mathcal{O}}_{t}\right)
. We do not retain any private information in the states
\mathcal{S}_{t}
, for any
t
. Moreover, since we only retain the DP outputs of the mechanisms, namely
\mathcal{Z}_{t}
, the states
\mathcal{S}_{t}
are redundant, and for simplicity we will only denote the mechanism in
Eq.

1
as
\mathcal{M}_{t}:(\mathcal{D}_{t})\mapsto\mathcal{Z}_{t}
. We will call this the classifier release mechanism:

\mathcal{M}_{t}:(\mathcal{D}_{t})\mapsto({\bm{\theta}}_{t},{\mathcal{O}}_{t}).

(6)

The following section (
Section

4.1
) explores two alternatives to the leaky
\mathcal{O}_{t}={\mathcal{Y}}_{1:t}
;

1

applying a DP mechanism to obtain a DP output label space, and

2

utilising prior knowledge.

We note that our theory holds not only for CL but for any classification problem, i.e., when
T=1
.

4.1
Choosing Classifier Output Space
\mathcal{O}_{t}

As discussed at the beginning of
Section

4
, releasing the DP parameters
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
, given a particular
\mathcal{O}_{t}
, together with
{\mathcal{O}}_{t}
itself might still not be DP.
One way to select
{\mathcal{O}}_{t}
is by taking the labels directly from the task’s dataset
\mathcal{D}_{t}
, i.e. setting
\mathcal{O}_{t}:={\mathcal{Y}}_{1:t}
. This is the standard approach used in existing work on DP CL (see
Section

2
).
For notational consistency, define
\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}_{t}=\{y:(\boldsymbol{x},y)\in\mathcal{D}_{t}\}=\mathcal{Y}_{t}
. Generally, we can either set
\mathcal{O}_{t}:=\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}_{t}
or
\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}_{1:t}
, i.e., either the set of sensitive labels in the current task, or the set of all sensitive labels up to the current task. In the following proposition we show that choosing the output label space based on the sensitive data is not DP. The full proof of
Proposition

4.1
is in
Section

C.1
.

Proposition 4.1
.

For any
t
, the classifier release mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{t}:(\mathcal{D}_{t})\mapsto({\bm{\theta}}_{t},{\mathcal{O}}_{t})
is not
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP for
0\leq\delta<1
if
\mathcal{O}_{t}=\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}_{t}
or
\mathcal{O}_{t}=\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}_{1:t}
, where
\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}_{t}=\{y:(\boldsymbol{x},y)\in\mathcal{D}_{t}\}
.

Proof sketch.

Consider the following counterexample. Let
\mathcal{D}_{t}\simeq\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}
, where
\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}
has one more example than
\mathcal{D}_{t}
, namely
(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},y^{*})
. Also, assume that
y^{*}\in\mathcal{O}_{t}^{\prime}
but
y^{*}\notin\mathcal{O}_{t}
. This means that the classifier
\mathcal{M}_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime})
has one more label in its range than
\mathcal{M}_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{t})
. This makes the sets of possible outcomes of
\mathcal{M}_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{t})
and
\mathcal{M}_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime})
disjoint, which is not DP.
∎

We propose two alternatives for setting
{\mathcal{O}}_{t}
. The first is to learn the labels by a DP mechanism, denoted as
\mathcal{O}^{\text{learned}}_{t}
. The second possibility is to construct a set of labels, denoted as
\mathcal{O}^{\text{prior}}_{t}
, which reflects our prior knowledge about the task and does not depend on
\mathcal{D}_{t}
. This knowledge can change and get updated in the following tasks. Thus, we have two possible settings for
{\mathcal{O}}_{t}
:

\mathbf{S_{\text{learned}}}
:

{\mathcal{O}}_{t}:=\mathcal{O}^{\text{learned}}_{t}
, i.e., labels are learned from the private dataset
\mathcal{D}_{t}
using a separate DP mechanism.

\mathbf{S_{\text{prior}}}
:

{\mathcal{O}}_{t}:=\mathcal{O}^{\text{prior}}_{t}
, i.e., a prior label set is provided for each task
t
. The labels in this case are public, and
\mathcal{O}_{t}
might contain all or most of the true labels in
\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}_{t}
.
Unmatched labels in
{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}
can be remapped to one of the labels in
\mathcal{O}^{\text{prior}}_{t}
or dropped without changing the DP guarantees w.r.t.
\mathcal{D}_{t}
.

In
Sections

4.2
and
4.3
, we will discuss
\mathbf{S_{\text{learned}}}
and
\mathbf{S_{\text{prior}}}
in more detail, and argue that they preserve the classifier’s DP guarantees. For further details regarding DP CL mechanisms, see
Appendix

B
.

4.2
DP Release of Output Label Space Using A Separate Mechanism (
\mathbf{S_{\text{learned}}}

)

Figure 2
:
Probability that a new label is not added to the output label space
\mathcal{O}^{\text{learned}}_{t}
. Even with
\epsilon=1.0
and
\delta=10^{-7}
, classes having fewer than
13
samples are discarded with at least 99% probability, and thus cannot be learned.

We introduce a DP mechanism
\mathcal{L}
that takes any
\mathcal{D}_{t}
as input and outputs
\mathcal{O}^{\text{learned}}_{t}
. Since, in each task
t
, we can group the inputs
\boldsymbol{x}
into classes according to their label
y
, denoted as
\mathcal{C}_{t}(y)=\{\boldsymbol{x}:(\boldsymbol{x},y)\in\mathcal{D}_{t}\}
, then the problem of releasing the labels is similar to the private partition selection problem
(
Desfontaines et al. 2022
;
Gopi et al. 2020
;
Korolova et al. 2009
)
. In a classification task, each input
\boldsymbol{x}
is associated with a unique label
y
, and thus every individual’s data
\boldsymbol{x}
contributes to exactly one partition
\mathcal{C}_{t}(y)
.
Under this setup,
Desfontaines et al. 2022
proposed an optimal DP mechanism for selecting partitions, which in our case corresponds to the set of labels. The optimality is in terms of preserving as many classes as possible, especially small classes.
For each
y\in\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}_{t}
, let
Z_{y}\sim k{\text{-}}\mathrm{TSGD}(p)
be i.i.d. random variables distributed according to the
k
-truncated symmetric geometric distribution
(
Desfontaines et al. 2022
)
. The optimal DP label release mechanism can be defined through a noisy thresholding operation

\mathcal{O}^{\text{learned}}_{t}:=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D}_{t}):=\{y\in\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}_{t}:\left\lvert\mathcal{C}_{t}(y)\right\rvert+Z_{y}>k\}.

(7)

According to Theorem 6 of
Desfontaines et al. 2022
, if we set
p=1-\exp(-\epsilon)
and
k=\left\lceil\frac{1}{\epsilon}\ln\left(\frac{\exp(\epsilon)+2\delta-1}{(\exp(\epsilon)+1)\delta}\right)\right\rceil
, then
\mathcal{L}
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP. In
Section

C.3
, we provide an optimal lower bound on the probability of dropping a class given its size for any DP mechanism, providing further justification on the optimality of this mechanism. We also provide a general method for constructing DP mechanisms that protect the set of labels (see
Definition

C.2
,
Theorem

C.3
). A drawback of this DP mechanism, and any mechanism that provides the labels, is the non-zero probability of dropping classes, which increases as class size or privacy budget decrease, as illustrated in
Fig.

2
. Another drawback is that the privacy budget must be split with the DP training mechanism, further reducing utility (see
Section

6.2
).

4.3
Data-Independent Output Label Space Using Prior Knowledge (
\mathbf{S_{\text{prior}}}

)

Choosing the output label space based on data-independent prior knowledge is DP, since it does not depend on the sensitive dataset. The prior labels can include all or most of the true labels in the sensitive training set. Let
\mathcal{O}_{t}=\mathcal{O}^{\text{prior}}_{t}
(the set of public labels in task
t
).
Since
\mathcal{O}_{t}

might not contain all labels in
\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}_{t}
, we must re-write
Eq. (
6
)
to allow remapping the labels not in
\mathcal{O}_{t}
. Let
\mathcal{R}^{\text{label}}_{t}:\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}_{t}\rightarrow\mathcal{O}_{t}\cup\{\mathrm{drop}\}
be any function mapping a label
y\in\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}_{t}
to a public label in
\mathcal{O}_{t}
or to the special symbol
\mathrm{drop}
, used to discard it. Now, we define the task remapping function
\mathcal{R}^{\text{task}}_{t}
:

\mathcal{R}^{\text{task}}_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{t})=\{({\bm{x}},\mathcal{R}^{\text{label}}_{t}(y)):({\bm{x}},y)\in\mathcal{D}_{t}\text{ and }\mathcal{R}^{\text{label}}_{t}(y)\neq\mathrm{drop}\},

(8)

i.e., we transform
({\bm{x}},y)
in
\mathcal{D}_{t}
to
({\bm{x}},\mathcal{R}^{\text{label}}_{t}(y))
, or drop it if
\mathcal{R}^{\text{label}}_{t}(y)=\mathrm{drop}
. The function
\mathcal{R}^{\text{label}}_{t}
may use label relationships (e.g., a hierarchy) to map mismatched labels, drop them, or map them to a dummy label (e.g.,
\mathrm{unknown}
). We show that these operations do not interfere with DP in the following:

Proposition 4.2
.

For any
t
, let
\mathcal{R}^{\text{label}}_{t}:\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}_{t}\rightarrow\mathcal{O}^{\text{prior}}_{t}\cup\{\mathrm{drop}\}
be any function. The classifier release mechanism

\mathcal{M}_{t}:(\mathcal{R}^{\text{task}}_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{t});\mathcal{O}^{\text{prior}}_{t})\mapsto({\bm{\theta}}_{t},\mathcal{O}^{\text{prior}}_{t})

is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP w.r.t.
\mathcal{D}_{t}
, if
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}
is obtained by an
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP mechanism from the remapped data
\mathcal{R}^{\text{task}}_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{t}
).

Proof sketch.

We show that the mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{t}
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP by analysing the cases for the remapped adjacent datasets and by using the original
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP guarantees of the mechanism that provides the DP weights
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
. The key point is that the remapping
\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{task}}_{t}
maps adjacent datasets either to identical datasets, when the differing example is dropped, or to adjacent datasets, when the differing example is remapped to a label in
\mathcal{O}^{\mathrm{prior}}_{t}
. Therefore, the DP guarantee for
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
still holds with respect to the original dataset
\mathcal{D}_{t}
. Finally, since
\mathcal{O}^{\mathrm{prior}}_{t}
is public and independent of
\mathcal{D}_{t}
, adding it to the output is post-processing. The full proof can be found in
Section

C.2
.
∎

The utility impact of label remapping is inherently problem-dependent. For example, in the ICD-11
(
World Health Organization 2019
)
medical coding, merging fine-grained diagnostic categories into broader parent classes leads to a loss in fine-grained classification performance. At the same time, such remapping may improve generalization, since higher-level labels are easier to learn and have more training examples per class.

DP CL Methods using Pre-Trained Models

We adapt two families of CL methods utilising pre-trained models to DP CL. These are prototype classifiers
(
Janson et al. 2022
;
McDonnell et al. 2023
)
in
Section

5.1
, and expandable PEFT adapters
(
Zhao et al. 2024
;
Zhou et al. 2024b
;
Zhou et al. 2024a

. Pre-trained models are known to be able to boost utility in both DP and CL communities and are thus a fitting choice for studying the combination of DP and CL but have not been proposed for the combination of both.

5.1
Cosine Similarity Classifier

We use a pre-trained model
f_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{\text{pre}}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{K}
as a frozen feature extractor without additional training during CL
(
Janson et al. 2022
)
to map inputs
{\bm{x}}
to feature vectors
{\bm{v}}=f_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{\text{pre}}({\bm{x}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}
.
The idea is to accumulate class-specific sums of these vectors under DP, and then classify points according to their cosine similarity with the class sums (see
Algorithm

1
).

(

This will result in a vector

\displaystyle{\bm{s}}_{t,o}{=}\begin{cases}{\bm{s}}_{t-1,o}{+}\left(\sum\limits_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{t,o}}\frac{f_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{\text{pre}}({\bm{x}})}{\|f_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{\text{pre}}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}}\right){+}\boldsymbol{z}_{t}&\text{if $o\in{\mathcal{O}}_{t}$}\\

To predict the label of a test sample

We only need to store the per-class sums and the pre-trained model, thus memory requirements would be
K|{\mathcal{O}}_{t}|
(cumulative sum) +
\mathrm{dim}(\theta)
(pre-trained model weights). The memory requirements scale as
O(|\cup_{t=1}^{T}{\mathcal{O}}_{t}|)
, growing with the size of the output label space.

Concretely, considering fine-tuning the last layer only, denote the feature vector of the pre-trained model by

0.04\%
of the total number of pre-trained model parameters.

for the
l

Section

D.2
we consider alternatives to

Zhao et al. 2024

6
Experiments

We evaluate how our proposed solutions to update labels in CL perform using our proposed methods using pre-trained models under DP. We first focus on an idealistic CL setting where all data of a class is in one task only (
Section

6.1
) and then move on to a more realistic setting where the classes are not distributed uniformly (
Section

6.2
). In
Section

6.3
, we benchmark our methods on standard CL settings.

In all experiments, we utilise a ViT-Base-16
(
Dosovitskiy et al. 2021
, ViT-B;)
network pre-trained on the ImageNet-21K
(
Russakovsky et al. 2015
)
dataset.
We assume that the pre-training data is public and that the task datasets
{\mathcal{D}}_{t}
are sensitive and need to be protected with DP.
All experiments are in the class-incremental learning setting where no task labels are available at test time
(
van de Ven et al. 2022
)
.
We run five repeats with different data splits, DP noise and hyperparameter optimization and plot the second worst, median and second best seed.
See
Appendix

D
for full details.

Datasets

We experiment with the following benchmarks for CL:
Split-CIFAR-100 which is CIFAR-100
(
Krizhevsky 2009
)
split into 10 tasks with 10 classes/task and
Split-ImageNet-R
(
Wang et al. 2022a
)
which is ImageNet-R
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021
)
split into 10 tasks with 20 classes/task. We refer to the non-CL versions of the datasets as base datasets.

Task-wise DP

We apply task-wise DP with add/remove base adjacency (see
Section

3
), by assuming that each data point appears in only one task, then the final privacy guarantees are obtained through parallel composition. Therefore, if a mechanism is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP, then the composition is also
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP.

Metrics

We report accuracy and forgetting from
Chaudhry et al. 2018
for evaluation like prior CL work
(
Mirzadeh et al. 2021
;
Yoon et al. 2022
)
. The average accuracy measures the test set accuracy across all seen tasks, where Final Acc. denotes the average accuracy of all
T
tasks for the final model. Forgetting is given by the difference between the highest accuracy of a task and its accuracy at the current task. See
Appendix

D
for formal definitions.

Label Methods

We use the following setup for the label methods proposed in
Section

4
:

•

Release Labels
(
\mathbf{S_{\text{learned}}}
in
Section

4.2
): At every task
t
, we only update the DP CL methods with the labels that have been released at that task
t
. To determine the optimal split in privacy budget between the label release mechanism and the actual DP training, we run a grid search that allocates between

and

•

Public Labels
(
\mathbf{S_{\text{prior}}}
in
Section

and

the number of labels of the base dataset and contain all labels of the full base dataset. We do not experiment with a label space that changes between tasks

For the Cosine Classifier, the output label space determines which class prototypes can be released and updated. For the PEFT Ensemble, it determines the labels/logits in the task-specific classifier head.

7
Discussion and Conclusion

We studied an attack on the output label space as a privacy-side channel, introduced two methods to eliminate the side-channel, and finally studied the two methods by adapting pre-trained models.

Recommendations for Label Methods

The utility of the label methods depends on the privacy budget and the classifier (
Fig.

4
).
The
Release Labels
method requires tuning the DP budget split between the label release mechanism and the DP training mechanism (
Fig.

5
). Small classes might get dropped under high privacy (
Fig.

7
). The
Public Labels
method can be used when prior information about the labels is available. The size of the public label space has only a minor effect (
Fig.

6
). Thus, we recommend using
Release Labels
when the classes are large or no prior information is available.

Privacy budget split heuristic for Release Labels

As a simple heuristic motivated by our baselines, when
\epsilon\geq 1
one may allocate
10\%
of the target privacy budget to
Release Labels
. If data-independent prior knowledge of class sizes is available (e.g., each class has at least 500 samples), one can use the output distribution of the mechanism in
Eq.

7
to compute the minimum budget split that releases all labels with probability close to
1
. This split, however, may not yield optimal accuracy, as a slightly lower privacy budget (higher noise) in the training mechanism might improve test accuracy due to regularisation from the DP noise. Without such data-independent prior knowledge, the minimum split cannot be computed from sensitive class sizes, since doing so is not DP.

Recommendations for DP CL methods

In our experiments, the PEFT Ensemble tends to outperform the Cosine Classifier in non-blurry settings and in blurry settings under lower privacy constraints (
\epsilon=8
); see
Figs.

4
,
5
,
6
and
7
. This is expected because PEFT can adapt the feature extractor, which is useful under domain shift. However, under stronger privacy constraints (
\epsilon=1
), the Cosine Classifier generally outperforms the PEFT Ensemble across most blurry settings (
Fig.

7
).

Under tight privacy, if the class embeddings are already well separated, simpler methods such as the Cosine Classifier might perform better. In contrast, training with DP-SGD, such as in the PEFT Ensemble, can be more unstable and harder to tune, especially when there is not enough data for each class per task or when there is a lower signal-to-noise ratio. In blurry settings, the data of some classes are distributed over multiple tasks. Hence, for the PEFT Ensemble, each model must distinguish a larger number of classes while seeing fewer examples per class, which results in a lower signal-to-noise ratio and makes private fine-tuning harder. The Cosine Classifier, on the other hand, maintains one cumulative private feature sum per class and the non-noisy part of the sum is invariant to how examples are split across tasks.

The PEFT Ensemble may be a better choice when:

1.

the privacy budget is sufficiently permissive relative to the available data,

2.

adapting to the new domain is important,

3.

for each class, there is at least one task with sufficient data to support private adaptation.

The Cosine Classifier may be preferable when:

1.

the privacy budget is tight relative to the data size,

2.

tasks are highly blurry,

3.

class embeddings are well separated,

4.

compute or memory is limited.

If public validation data or a non-sensitive proxy dataset is available, one can compare the two methods without additional privacy cost by simulating the expected task split and evaluating accuracy and forgetting. Otherwise, method selection on sensitive validation data should be included in the privacy accounting.

Limitations

We do not use methods based on synthetic data and leave them for future work. We also leave the theoretical analysis of the optimal privacy budget split between the label release mechanism and the DP training to future work.

Appendix B
Other Details Related to DP CL Mechanisms

B.1
Limitations of Current DP CL Theory

Regarding CL, defining what is meant for a learning algorithm to be DP is not trivial, as the data is not available to the algorithm at once. Instead, the algorithm receives datasets
\mathcal{D}_{t}
gradually over time
t=1,\ldots,T
, and releases intermediate classifiers
f_{\theta_{t}}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{O}_{t}
,
t=1,\ldots,T
. In this section, we discuss existing approaches and their limitations. In the next section, we introduce an alternative definition.

DP CL with Global Adjacency

Desai et al. 2021
introduced an adjacency relation for DP CL based on the union of the datasets from all the tasks
t=1,\ldots,T
and their episodic memories. For the following definition, for each task
t
,
\mathcal{D}_{t}
is a dataset,
\mathfrak{M}_{t}
is an episodic memory, and for any two sets
A
and
A^{\prime}
,
\lVert A-A^{\prime}\rVert_{1}\leq 1
means that they are adjacent such that one of the sets differs from the other by at most one sample.

Definition B.1

(Definition 2 in
Desai et al. 2021
)
.

Two databases
D=(\mathcal{D},\mathfrak{M})
and
D^{\prime}=(\mathcal{D}^{\prime},\mathfrak{M}^{\prime})
, where
\mathcal{D}=\cup_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{D}_{t}
,
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=\cup_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}
,
\mathfrak{M}=\cup_{t=1}^{T}\mathfrak{M}_{t}
, and
\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}=\cup_{t=1}^{T}\mathfrak{M}_{t}^{\prime}
, are called continual adjacent, if
\lVert\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\rVert_{1}\leq 1
and
\lVert\mathfrak{M}-\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}\rVert_{1}\leq 1
.

If we disregard the episodic memories (
\mathfrak{M}=\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}=\varnothing
), the main issue with
Definition

B.1
, is that in the general case where multiple adjacent datasets
\mathcal{D}_{t},\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}
are allowed to differ (i.e.,
\lVert\mathcal{D}_{t}-\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}\rVert=1
), then the global adjacency relation
\lVert\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\rVert_{1}\leq T
does not imply that
\lVert\mathcal{D}_{t}-\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}\rVert_{1}\leq 1
. Moreover, even in the restricted case of
\lVert\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\rVert_{1}\leq 1
, encoding the adjacency at the global level loses information about which task
t
has differing adjacent datasets
\mathcal{D}_{t}\neq\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}
. We can even choose
{\mathcal{D}}_{t}
and
{\mathcal{D}}_{t}^{\prime}
such that
{\mathcal{D}}_{t}\cap{\mathcal{D}}_{t}^{\prime}=\varnothing,t=1,\dots,T
, while still satisfying
\lVert\cup_{t}{\mathcal{D}}_{t}-\cup_{t}{\mathcal{D}}_{t}^{\prime}\rVert_{1}\leq 1
.

The privacy accounting in
Desai et al. 2021
is done for each task separately, and then they calculate the total privacy guarantees over all tasks through basic sequential composition, i.e.,
\epsilon=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\epsilon_{t}

(
Desai et al. 2021
, Lemmas 1 & 2)
. Therefore, their privacy accounting does not strictly correspond to their
Definition

B.1
, and it could benefit from a more granular adjacency at the task level.

DP CL for Data Streams

Epasto et al. 2023
introduced a DP definition for data streams, where two streams
\mathcal{S}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{T})
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=(x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{T}^{\prime})
are neighbouring or adjacent if there exists at most one timestamp
t^{*}
for which
x_{t^{*}}\neq x_{t^{*}}^{\prime}
and
x_{t}=x_{t}^{\prime}
, for all
t\neq t^{*}
, see also
(
Hassanpour et al. 2022
;
Dwork et al. 2010a
;
Lécuyer et al. 2019
)
. However, their definition is not suitable for our setting, where we operate on task datasets
\mathcal{D}_{t}
,
t=1,\ldots,T
.
Hassanpour et al. 2022
studied DP in CL for streaming data and proposed a method that provides meaningful DP guarantees in that context. Still, their method introduces complications when applying DP-SGD on a task level. In particular, standard privacy accounting techniques, especially those relying on subsampling amplification, assume that each minibatch is sampled i.i.d. from a larger dataset. This assumption does not hold under streaming adjacency (see
Choquette-Choo et al. 2025
for a discussion on amplification in streaming settings). For these reasons, the streaming adjacency is not well-suited for our proposed approaches.

Lifelong DP

Regarding lifelong learning with DP (Lifelong DP),
Lai et al. 2022
proposed a task level adjacency relation for the
\epsilon
-Lifelong learning,

Definition B.2

(Definition 2 in
Lai et al. 2022
)
.

Given any two lifelong databases
\mathrm{data}_{T}=\{\mathcal{D},\mathfrak{M}\}
and
\mathrm{data}_{T}^{\prime}=\{\mathcal{D}^{\prime},\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}\}
, where
\mathcal{D}=\{\mathcal{D}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{D}_{T}\}
,
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=\{\mathcal{D}_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\mathcal{D}_{T}^{\prime}\}
,
\mathbb{M}=\{\mathbb{M}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{M}_{T}\}
,
\mathbb{M}^{\prime}=\{\mathbb{M}_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\mathbb{M}_{T}^{\prime}\}
,
\mathbb{M}_{t}=\cup_{i=1}^{t-1}\mathfrak{M}_{i}
, and
\mathbb{M}_{t}^{\prime}=\cup_{i=1}^{t-1}\mathfrak{M}_{i}^{\prime}
.
\mathrm{data}_{T}
and
\mathrm{data}_{T}^{\prime}
are called lifelong neighboring databases if,
\forall t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}
:

(i)

\mathcal{D}_{t}
and
\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}
differ by at most one tuple; and

(ii)

\mathfrak{M}_{t}
and
\mathfrak{M}_{t}^{\prime}
differ by at most one tuple.

If we disregard the episodic memories (
\mathfrak{M}_{t}=\mathfrak{M}_{t}^{\prime}=\varnothing
) in
Definition

B.2
, then this adjacency relation would be suitable for our setting.
Lai et al. 2022
also introduced a definition for
\epsilon
-Lifelong DP,

Definition B.3

(Definition 3 in
Lai et al. 2022
)
.

\epsilon
-Lifelong DP. Given a lifelong database
\mathrm{data}_{T}
, a randomized algorithm
\mathcal{A}
achieves
\epsilon
-Lifelong DP, if for any of two lifelong neighbouring databases
(\mathrm{data}_{T},\mathrm{data}_{T}^{\prime})
, for all possible outputs
\{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}\in\mathrm{Range}(\mathcal{A})
,
\forall T\in\mathbb{N}
we have that

\begin{split}&\mathrm{Pr}[\mathcal{A}(\mathrm{data}_{T})=\{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}]\leq e^{\epsilon}\mathrm{Pr}[\mathcal{A}(\mathrm{data}_{T}^{\prime})_{i\in[1,m]}=\{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}]\\
&\nexists(\epsilon^{\prime}<\epsilon,t\leq T):\mathrm{Pr}[\mathcal{A}(\mathrm{data}_{t})=\{{\bm{\theta}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t}]\leq e^{\epsilon^{\prime}}\mathrm{Pr}[\mathcal{A}(\mathrm{data}_{t}^{\prime})=\{{\bm{\theta}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t}]\end{split}

(A1)

where
\mathrm{Range}(\mathcal{A})
denotes every possible output of
\mathcal{A}
.

The issue with
Definition

B.3
is that it corresponds to one type of composition, i.e., parallel composition. Also, it does not allow for (approximate)
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP.

To summarise the limitations of existing approaches for defining DP CL:

1.

The global adjacency relation over the union of all tasks is not suitable for continual learning, and the adjacency of the task datasets
\mathcal{D}_{t}
,
\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}
cannot be inferred from the unions
\mathcal{D}=\cup_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{D}_{t}
,
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=\cup_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{t}

(
Desai et al. 2021
)
.

2.

DP CL definitions and methods for data streams cannot be naturally translated to the setting we consider, i.e., tasks are defined based on datasets
\mathcal{D}_{t}
,
t=1,\ldots,T

(
Epasto et al. 2023
;
Hassanpour et al. 2022
)
. Particularly, the method of
Hassanpour et al. 2022
does not account for subsampling amplification which introduces complications when applying DP-SGD on the level of a task.

3.

\epsilon
-Lifelong DP
(
Lai et al. 2022
)
has a suitable adjacency relation for our setting, if we discard the episodic memories; however, their definition does not allow for (approximate)
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP and it is restricted to parallel composition over the tasks.

B.2
Task-Wise DP for DP CL

As discussed in the previous section
Section

B.1
, some of the existing works on DP CL, including
Desai et al. 2021
;
Lai et al. 2022
, introduce a definition for DP with CL along with a dataset adjacency relation; however, these works have several limitations. In
Desai et al. 2021
, the data adjacency relation depends on the datasets from both the current and future tasks, and in
Lai et al. 2022
, their privacy definition is restricted to only one type of composition and does not allow for (approximate)
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP. To guarantee privacy in CL, our basic approach is to define task-wise adjacency, given in
Definition

B.4
, which is more general than what was given in
Definition

3.2
, and task-wise DP, given in
Definition

B.5
. We gave a minimal definition for the task-wise adjacency relation in the main text because our experiments only apply parallel composition, and thus it was sufficient. However, one can still use various composition methods
(
McSherry 2010
;
Dwork et al. 2010b
;
Whitehouse et al. 2023
)
, to account for the total privacy.

Definition B.4

(Task-wise Adjacency)
.

Let
\simeq
be an adjacency relation between datasets,
I\subseteq\mathbb{N}
be a set of task indices that can be either finite or infinite, and
1\leq n\leq\lvert I\rvert
. A sequence of datasets
(\mathcal{D}_{t})_{t\in I}
is said to be
n
task-wise adjacent to another sequence of datasets
(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\in I}
, denoted
(\mathcal{D}_{t})_{t\in I}\simeq_{\text{tw}(n)}(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\in I}
, if there exists
J\subseteq I
such that
\lvert J\rvert=n
, and for any
t\in J
, we have
\mathcal{D}_{t}\simeq\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}
, and that
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}
if
t\in I\setminus J
. We say that the order of task-wise adjacency is
n
. When the order of adjacency is
1
, then it can be omitted, i.e.
\simeq_{\text{tw}}:\equiv\simeq_{\text{tw}(1)}
.

Definition

B.4
is inspired by both the definitions in
Epasto et al. 2023
and
Lai et al. 2022
, and it differs from both by being able to control the number of possible different adjacent task datasets. If the order of adjacency is
1
, i.e., for only one
t^{*}\in I
, we have
\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}\simeq\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}
if
t\neq t^{*}
, as in
Definition

3.2
. However,
t^{*}
is unknown in advance, and is arbitrary.

\mathrm{assign}\left[\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right]=\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}\right)_{t\in I},

(A10)

such that
\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}^{\prime}=\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}=\varnothing
, for all
t\neq t^{*}
. By
Theorem

3.5
,
\mathcal{A}\circ\mathrm{assign}[\cdot]
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP, and thus by post processing
\pi_{t^{*}}\circ\mathcal{A}\circ\mathrm{assign}[\cdot]
is also
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP.

Moreover,
\pi_{t^{*}}\circ\mathcal{A}\circ\mathrm{assign}[\mathcal{D}]=\mathcal{A}_{t^{*}}(\mathcal{D})
and
\pi_{t^{*}}\circ\mathcal{A}\circ\mathrm{assign}[\mathcal{D}^{\prime}]=\mathcal{A}_{t^{*}}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})
, completing the proof.
∎

B.3
Task-Wise DP and Composition (for Sec.
B.2
)

Practically, one can apply
Theorem

3.5
and
Corollary

3.6
, and then apply any composition method. Parallel composition
(
McSherry 2010
)
, which we use in our experiments, applies when each individual’s data appears in only one dataset
\mathcal{D}_{t}
, and a DP mechanism is invoked on each dataset separately. It is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP if each mechanism is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP. Although the proof already exists in
(
McSherry 2010
)
, we also show that parallel composition is task-wise
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP in
Theorem

B.7
for completeness. In contrast, sequential composition degrades privacy guarantees with each use. We show that basic sequential composition of a sequence of mechanisms is task-wise
\left(\lvert I\rvert\epsilon,\lvert I\rvert\delta,\simeq_{\text{tw}(T)}\right)
-DP in
Theorem

B.8
, and the proof is similar to the one in
Dwork & Roth 2014
. Adaptive sequential composition
(
Dwork & Roth 2014
;
Dwork et al. 2010b
)
applies when privacy parameters (including the number of tasks) are known in advance; otherwise, fully adaptive composition is needed
(
Whitehouse et al. 2023
)
.

A necessary condition for
\mathcal{A}
in
Eq.

A2
to be DP is that it should not leak that any of the task datasets is empty, i.e. that
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\varnothing
, almost surely, for some
t
. Let
\bot
denote that a classifier was not released. We also say that
\bot
is not a (proper) classifier. If
\mathcal{A}_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{t})=\bot
(almost surely) when
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\varnothing
and
\mathcal{A}_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{t})\neq\bot
(almost surely) when
\mathcal{D}_{t}\neq\varnothing
, then
\mathcal{A}
is not DP according to
Proposition

B.6
.
The reason is that this can potentially leak whether the classifier was trained including or excluding a certain sample. Therefore, we will assume that the classifier release mechanism always outputs a proper classifier
\mathcal{A}_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{t})\neq\bot
(almost surely) for any
t
. In practice, for the case of using pretrained models, when
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\varnothing
, we can initialize both the fine-tuning weights and the classifier’s weights randomly before releasing the classifier.

Proposition B.6
.

The mechanism
\mathcal{A}_{t}
is not
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP for
0\leq\delta<1
when
\mathcal{A}_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{t})=\bot
(almost surely) if and only if
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\varnothing
.

Denote the range of
\mathcal{A}_{t}
as
\mathcal{H}_{t}\cup\{\bot\}
, i.e. the union of the hypothesis space
\mathcal{H}_{t}
(proper-classifiers) and
\{\bot\}
(non-proper classifier). For the following proof, note that
\bot\notin\mathcal{H}_{t}
for any
t
.

Proof.

We argue by constructing a counterexample. Assume that
\mathcal{A}_{t}
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP for
0\leq\delta<1
and that
\mathcal{A}_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{t})=\bot
(almost surely) iff
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\varnothing
. Let
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\varnothing
and
\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}=\{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},y^{*})\}
where
(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},y^{*})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{O}_{t}
is arbitrary.
Since
\mathcal{A}_{t}
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP, then for all
S\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{t}
,

\mathrm{Pr}[\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime})\in S]\leq e^{\epsilon}\mathrm{Pr}[\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}_{t})\in S]+\delta.

(A11)

Let
S=\mathcal{H}_{t}
, then

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime})\in S\right]=1.

(A12)

However, since
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\varnothing
, then
\mathcal{A}_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{t})=\bot\notin\mathcal{H}_{t}
. Thus,

\mathrm{Pr}[\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}_{t})\in S]=0.

(A13)

Therefore,
Eq.

A11
implies that

1\leq e^{\epsilon}\times 0+\delta\implies 1\leq\delta,

(A14)

but we assumed that
0<\delta<1
, a contradiction.
∎

Theorem B.7

(Parallel composition)
.

Let
(\mathcal{A}_{t})_{t\in I}
be a sequence of
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP mechanisms, then their parallel composition is task-wise
(\epsilon,\delta,\simeq_{\text{tw}(1)})
-DP.

Proof.

According to
McSherry 2010
, for any sequence of mechanisms
(\mathcal{A}_{t})_{t\in I}
, their parallel composition is given by

\left(\mathcal{A}_{t}(\cdot\cap\mathcal{V}_{t})\right)_{t\in I},

(A15)

where
\mathcal{V}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
are disjoint subsets, such that
\pi_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathcal{V}_{i})\cap\pi_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathcal{V}_{j})=\varnothing
. That is, no two sets of
\left(\mathcal{V}_{t}\right)_{t\in I}
share the same individual’s data.

Let
(\mathcal{D}_{t})_{t\in I}\simeq_{\text{tw}(1)}(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\in I}
, then there exists
t^{*}
such that
\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}\simeq\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}
for all
t\neq t^{*}
. Define the following:

\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{par}}\left((\mathcal{D}_{t})_{t\in I}\right)=\left(\mathcal{A}_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{t}\cap\mathcal{V}_{t})\right)_{t\in I}.

(A16)

Observe that for each
t\neq t^{*}
,
\mathcal{D}_{t}\cap\mathcal{V}_{t}=\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{V}_{t}
, and that
\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}\cap\mathcal{V}_{t^{*}}\simeq\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{V}_{t^{*}}
. From the fact that
\mathcal{A}_{t^{*}}
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP, for all
S_{t^{*}}\subseteq\mathrm{Range}(\mathcal{A}_{t^{*}})
, it holds that

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}_{t^{*}}(\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}\cap\mathcal{V}_{t^{*}})\in S_{t^{*}}\right]\leq\exp(\epsilon)\times\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}_{t^{*}}(\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{V}_{t^{*}})\in S_{t^{*}}\right]+\delta

(A17)

Let
S\subseteq\mathrm{Range}(\mathcal{A})
, denote
S_{t}=\pi_{t}(S)
, for any
t\in I
. Also, denote

S[s_{t^{*}}]=\left\{(s_{t})_{t\in I\setminus\{t^{*}\}}:\left((s_{t})_{t\in I\setminus\{t^{*}\}};s_{t^{*}}\right)_{t^{*}}\in S\right\},

(A18)

where ‘
(;)_{t}
’ means insertion at
t
, e.g.
((a,b);c)_{2}=(a,c,b)
. For brevity denote, for any
t
and any
\mathcal{D}
,

p_{t}(s_{t};\mathcal{D})=\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}_{t}(\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{V}_{t})=s_{t}\right],

(A19)

From the law of total probability, and since the mechanisms are applied independently, then

\begin{split}&\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{par}}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)_{t\in I}\right)\in S\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{(s_{t})_{t\in I}}\left[\bm{1}_{S}\left(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{par}}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)_{t\in I}\right)\right)\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{s_{t^{*}}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{(s_{t})_{t\in I\setminus\{t^{*}\}}}\left[\bm{1}_{S}\left(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{par}}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)_{t\in I}\right)\right)\middle|\mathcal{A}_{t^{*}}\left(\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}\cap\mathcal{V}_{t^{*}}\right)=s_{t^{*}}\right]\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{s_{t^{*}}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{(s_{t})_{t\in I\setminus\{t^{*}\}}}\left[\bm{1}_{S[s_{t^{*}}]}\left(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{par}}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)_{t\in I\setminus\{t^{*}\}}\right)\right)\right]\right]\\
&=\int_{s_{t^{*}}\in S_{t^{*}}}\int_{(s_{t})_{t\in I\setminus\{t^{*}\}}\in S[s_{t^{*}}]}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{par}}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)_{t\in I\setminus\{t^{*}\}}\right)=(s_{t})_{t\in I\setminus\{t^{*}\}}\right]p_{t^{*}}(s_{t^{*}};\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}^{\prime})\\
&=\int_{s_{t^{*}}\in S_{t^{*}}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{par}}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)_{t\in I\setminus\{t^{*}\}}\right)\in S[s_{t^{*}}]\right]p_{t^{*}}(s_{t^{*}};\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}^{\prime}),\end{split}

(A20)

where
\bm{1}_{S}[\cdot]
denotes the indicator function.
Now observe that for any
s_{t^{*}}
,

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{par}}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)_{t\in I\setminus\{t^{*}\}}\right)\in S[s_{t^{*}}]\right]=\min\left(1,\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{par}}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)_{t\in I\setminus\{t^{*}\}}\right)\in S[s_{t^{*}}]\right]\right).

(A21)

Since
{\mathcal{D}}_{t}={\mathcal{D}}_{t}^{\prime}
for all
t\neq t^{*}
, then

\begin{split}&\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{par}}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)_{t\in I}\right)\in S\right]\\
&\quad=\int_{s_{t^{*}}\in S_{t^{*}}}\min\left(1,\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{par}}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)_{t\in I\setminus\{t^{*}\}}\right)\in S[s_{t^{*}}]\right]\right)p_{t^{*}}(s_{t^{*}};\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}})\\
&\quad=\int_{s_{t^{*}}\in S_{t^{*}}}\min\left(1,\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{par}}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}\right)_{t\in I\setminus\{t^{*}\}}\right)\in S[s_{t^{*}}]\right]\right)p_{t^{*}}(s_{t^{*}};\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}})\\
&\quad=\int_{s_{t^{*}}\in S_{t^{*}}}\min\left(1,\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{par}}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}\right)_{t\in I\setminus\{t^{*}\}}\right)\in S[s_{t^{*}}]\right]\right)\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\times\big(p_{t^{*}}(s_{t^{*}};\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}})-\exp(\epsilon)p_{t^{*}}(s_{t^{*}};\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}^{\prime})+\exp(\epsilon)p_{t^{*}}(s_{t^{*}};\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}^{\prime})\big)\\
&\quad=\int_{s_{t^{*}}\in S_{t^{*}}}\min\left(1,\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{par}}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}\right)_{t\in I\setminus\{t^{*}\}}\right)\in S[s_{t^{*}}]\right]\right)\times\exp(\epsilon)p_{t^{*}}(s_{t^{*}};\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}^{\prime})\\
&\quad\quad+\int_{s_{t^{*}}\in S_{t^{*}}}\min\left(1,\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{par}}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}\right)_{t\in I\setminus\{t^{*}\}}\right)\in S[s_{t^{*}}]\right]\right)\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\times\big(p_{t^{*}}(s_{t^{*}};\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}})-\exp(\epsilon)p_{t^{*}}(s_{t^{*}};\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}^{\prime})\big)\end{split}

(A22)

Now since for any
a,b
, we have
\min(a,b)\leq a
and
\min(a,b)\leq b
, then

\begin{split}&\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{par}}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)_{t\in I}\right)\in S\right]\\
&\quad\leq\int_{s_{t^{*}}\in S_{t^{*}}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{par}}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}\right)_{t\in I\setminus\{t^{*}\}}\right)\in S[s_{t^{*}}]\right]\times\exp(\epsilon)p_{t^{*}}(s_{t^{*}};\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}^{\prime})\\
&\quad\quad+\int_{s_{t^{*}}\in S_{t^{*}}}\big(p_{t^{*}}(s_{t^{*}};\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}})-\exp(\epsilon)p_{t^{*}}(s_{t^{*}};\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}^{\prime})\big)\\
&\quad=\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{par}}\left((\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\in I}\right)\in S\right]+\int_{s_{t^{*}}\in S_{t^{*}}}\big(p_{t^{*}}(s_{t^{*}};\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}})-\exp(\epsilon)p_{t^{*}}(s_{t^{*}};\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}^{\prime})\big)\\
&\quad\leq\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{par}}\left((\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\in I}\right)\in S\right]+\delta,\end{split}

(A23)

from the fact that
\mathcal{A}_{t^{*}}
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP.
Therefore,
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{par}}
is
(\epsilon,\delta,\simeq_{\text{tw}(1)})
-DP which ends the proof.
∎

While in the proof of
Theorem

B.7
, we followed the typical setting where each mechanism is applied to a disjoint dataset, the proof still holds if the datasets were not disjoint. The standard parallel composition theorem for
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP requires partitioning the domain into disjoint sets
(\mathcal{V}_{t})_{t\in I}
, so that for any dataset
\mathcal{D}
, adding or removing a point will only affect one of adjacent datasets of
(\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{V}_{t})_{t\in I}
. However, the adjacency relation in task-wise DP (
\simeq_{\text{tw}(1)}
), already imposes this on the adjacent sequences
(\mathcal{D}_{t})_{t\in I}
and
(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\in I}
, making the partitioning of the domain redundant.

Theorem B.8
.

Let
I
be a set of task indices, and let
T=\lvert I\rvert
, if
\left(\mathcal{A}_{t}\right)_{t\in I}
is a sequence of
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP mechanisms, then their basic sequential composition is
(T\epsilon,T\delta,\simeq_{\text{tw}(T)})
-DP.

Proof.

Let
(\mathcal{D}_{t})_{t\in I}\simeq_{\text{tw}(T)}(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\in I}
, and let
S\subseteq\mathrm{Range}(\mathcal{A})
. We will prove the result by induction on
T
. The case
T=1
holds trivially. Now assume that the statement of the theorem holds for
\lvert I\rvert=T-1
, we will prove that it also holds for
\lvert I^{*}\rvert=T
where
I^{*}=I\cup\{t^{*}\}
. Denote
S_{t}=\pi_{t}(S)
, for any
t\in I
. Similar to the steps in
Eq.

A20
in the proof of the previous theorem (
Theorem

B.7
),

\begin{split}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)_{t\in I^{*}}\right)\in S\right]&=\int_{s_{t^{*}}\in S_{t^{*}}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)_{t\in I}\right)\in S[s_{t^{*}}]\right]\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}_{t^{*}}\left(\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}\right)=s_{t^{*}}\right].\end{split}

(A24)

From the induction hypothesis, and since
\min(a,b+c)\leq\min(a,b)+c
for all
c>0
,

\begin{split}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)_{t\in I}\right)\in S[s_{t^{*}}]\right]&=\min\left(1,\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)_{t\in I}\right)\in S[s_{t^{*}}]\right]\right)\\
&\leq\min\left(1,\exp(\lvert I\rvert\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}\right)_{t\in I}\right)\in S[s_{t^{*}}]\right]+\lvert I\rvert\delta\right)\\
&\leq\min\left(1,\exp(\lvert I\rvert\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}\right)_{t\in I}\right)\in S[s_{t^{*}}]\right]\right)+\lvert I\rvert\delta.\end{split}

(A25)

From
Eq.

A24
and
Eq.

A25
, we obtain

\begin{split}&\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)_{t\in I^{*}}\right)\in S\right]\\
&\leq\int_{s_{t^{*}}\in S_{t^{*}}}\left(\min\left(1,\exp(\lvert I\rvert\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}\right)_{t\in I}\right)\in S[s_{t^{*}}]\right]\right)+\lvert I\rvert\delta\right)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}_{t^{*}}\left(\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}\right)=s_{t^{*}}\right]\\
&=\int_{s_{t^{*}}\in S_{t^{*}}}\min\left(1,\exp(\lvert I\rvert\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}\right)_{t\in I}\right)\in S[s_{t^{*}}]\right]\right)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}_{t^{*}}\left(\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}\right)=s_{t^{*}}\right]\\
&\quad\quad\quad+\lvert I\rvert\delta\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}_{t^{*}}\left(\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}\right)=s_{t^{*}}\right]\\
&=\lvert I\rvert\delta+\int_{s_{t^{*}}\in S_{t^{*}}}\min\left(1,\exp(\lvert I\rvert\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}\right)_{t\in I}\right)\in S[s_{t^{*}}]\right]\right)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}_{t^{*}}\left(\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}\right)=s_{t^{*}}\right]\\
&\leq\lvert I\rvert\delta+\int_{s_{t^{*}}\in S_{t^{*}}}\min\left(1,\exp(\lvert I\rvert\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}\right)_{t\in I}\right)\in S[s_{t^{*}}]\right]\right)\\
&\quad\quad\quad\times\left(\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}_{t^{*}}\left(\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}\right)=s_{t^{*}}\right]-\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}_{t^{*}}\left(\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}^{\prime}\right)=s_{t^{*}}\right]+\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}_{t^{*}}\left(\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}^{\prime}\right)=s_{t^{*}}\right]\right).\end{split}

(A26)

Now by applying
\min(a,b)\leq a
and
\min(a,b)\leq b
, we obtain

\begin{split}&\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)_{t\in I^{*}}\right)\in S\right]\leq\lvert I\rvert\delta+\exp\left((\lvert I\rvert+1)\epsilon\right)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}\right)_{t\in I^{*}}\right)\in S\right]\\
&\quad\quad+\int_{s_{t^{*}}\in S_{t^{*}}}\left(\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}_{t^{*}}\left(\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}\right)=s_{t^{*}}\right]-\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}_{t^{*}}\left(\mathcal{D}_{t^{*}}^{\prime}\right)=s_{t^{*}}\right]\right),\end{split}

(A27)

and the last integral is less than
\delta
from the fact that
\mathcal{A}_{t^{*}}
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP, and hence,

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)_{t\in I^{*}}\right)\in S\right]\leq(\lvert I\rvert+1)\delta+\exp\left((\lvert I\rvert+1)\epsilon\right)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}\right)_{t\in I^{*}}\right)\in S\right],

(A28)

as required.
∎

In other words, we can say that for a sequence of
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP mechanisms
\left(\mathcal{A}_{t}\right)_{t\in I}
, parallel composition corresponds to task-wise
(\epsilon,\delta,\simeq_{\text{tw}(1)})
-DP, and basic sequential composition corresponds to task-wise
(\lvert I\rvert\epsilon,\lvert I\rvert\delta,\simeq_{\text{tw}(T)})
-DP.

Proof of

\pi_{\mathcal{Y}}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}

\mathcal{Y}
is the space of all possible labels, i.e.
\pi_{\mathcal{Y}}(\boldsymbol{x},y)=y
for any
(\boldsymbol{x},y)\in\mathcal{D}_{t}

\mathcal{M}_{t}
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP for
0\leq\delta<1
. Let
\mathcal{D}_{t}
and
\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}
be any two datasets that differ in only one example such that
\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}=\mathcal{D}_{t}\cup\{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},y^{*})\}

{\mathcal{O}}_{t}=\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}_{t}
or
{\mathcal{O}}_{t}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{t}\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}_{i}

\pi_{\mathcal{Y}}\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)\subseteq{\mathcal{O}}_{t}.

(A29)

Let
\pi_{2}(\cdot)
be the mapping that takes an ordered pair as an input and outputs the second item, i.e.
\pi_{2}(A,B)=B
.

{\mathcal{O}}_{t}^{\prime}=\pi_{2}(\mathcal{M}_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}))

\mathcal{O}_{t}^{\prime}

\pi_{2}(\mathcal{M}_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{t}))

\mathcal{M}_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{t})

(A30)

Equivalently,

\mathrm{Pr}[\mathcal{O}_{t}^{\prime}\in S]\leq e^{\epsilon}\mathrm{Pr}[\mathcal{O}_{t}\in S]+\delta.

(A31)

Let
S=\{\mathcal{O}_{t}^{\prime}\}
. Since
y^{*}\notin\mathcal{O}_{t}
, then
\mathcal{O}_{t}^{\prime}\neq\mathcal{O}_{t}
. This implies that,

\mathrm{Pr}[\mathcal{O}_{t}^{\prime}\in S]=1,

(A32)

and that

\mathrm{Pr}[\mathcal{O}_{t}\in S]=0.

(A33)

From

A31
,
A32
and
A33
, we obtain

1\leq e^{\epsilon}\times 0+\delta\implies 1\leq\delta,

(A34)

but we assumed that

, a contradiction.

C.2
Proof of
Proposition

4.2

For the following proof, for any adjacent dataset
\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}=\mathcal{D}_{t}\bigcup\{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},y^{*})\}
, we assume that there is an associated function
\mathcal{R}^{\text{label}^{\prime}}_{t}:\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}^{\prime}}_{t}\rightarrow\mathcal{O}^{\text{prior}}_{t}\bigcup\{\mathrm{drop}\}
that differs from
\mathcal{R}^{\text{label}}_{t}
only on the new point
\{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},y^{*})\}
, such that similar to
Eq.

8
we obtain
\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\text{task}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime})
:

\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\text{task}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime})=\{({\bm{x}},\mathcal{R}^{\text{label}^{\prime}}_{t}(y)):({\bm{x}},y)\in\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}\text{ and }\mathcal{R}^{\text{label}^{\prime}}_{t}(y)\neq\mathrm{drop}\}.

(A35)

Proof.

Define
\mathcal{W}
to be
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP mechanism that provides the weights
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
. In other words,

{\bm{\theta}}_{t}:=\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\text{task}}(\mathcal{D}_{t});\mathcal{O}^{\text{prior}}_{t}).

(A36)

To show that using
\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\text{task}}(\mathcal{D}_{t})
instead of
\mathcal{D}_{t}
does not change the privacy guarantees for
\mathcal{W}
, let
\mathcal{D}_{t}
be any set and
\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}\simeq\mathcal{D}_{t}
. Assume without the loss of generality that
\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}=\mathcal{D}_{t}\bigcup\{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},y^{*})\}
, i.e. that
\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}
has an additional point. There are two possibilities for
\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\text{task}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime})
:

1.

\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\text{task}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime})=\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\text{task}}(\mathcal{D}_{t})
when
\mathcal{R}^{\text{label}^{\prime}}_{t}(y^{*})=\mathrm{drop}
.

2.

\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\text{task}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime})=\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\text{task}}(\mathcal{D}_{t})\bigcup\{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\mathcal{R}^{\text{label}^{\prime}}_{t}(y^{*}))\}
when
\mathcal{R}^{\text{label}^{\prime}}_{t}(y^{*})\in\mathcal{O}^{\text{prior}}_{t}
.

First, if
\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\text{task}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime})=\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\text{task}}(\mathcal{D}_{t})
, then it is trivial that for all
S\subseteq\mathrm{Range}(\mathcal{W})
:

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\text{task}}(\mathcal{D}_{t});\mathcal{O}^{\text{prior}}_{t})\in S\right]\leq\exp(\epsilon)\times\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\text{task}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime});\mathcal{O}^{\text{prior}}_{t})\in S\right]+\delta.

(A37)

Second, if
\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\text{task}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime})=\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\text{task}}(\mathcal{D}_{t})\bigcup\{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\mathcal{R}^{\text{label}}_{t}(y^{*}))\}
, then since
\mathcal{W}
is an
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP mechanism, setting
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\text{task}}(\mathcal{D}_{t})
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\text{task}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime})
in
Definition

3.1
, we obtain

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\text{task}}(\mathcal{D}_{t});\mathcal{O}^{\text{prior}}_{t})\in S\right]\leq\exp(\epsilon)\times\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\text{task}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime});\mathcal{O}^{\text{prior}}_{t})\in S\right]+\delta.

(A38)

Therefore, in both cases, using
\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\text{task}}(\mathcal{D}_{t})
does not change the privacy guarantees of
\mathcal{W}
. Finally, since
\mathcal{W}
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP, then the mechanism

\mathcal{M}_{t}(\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\text{task}}(\mathcal{D}_{t});\mathcal{O}^{\text{prior}}_{t})\mapsto\left(\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\text{task}}(\mathcal{D}_{t});\mathcal{O}^{\text{prior}}_{t}),\mathcal{O}^{\text{prior}}_{t}\right)

(A39)

is a post-processing from
\mathcal{W}
using only additional public information (
\mathcal{O}^{\text{prior}}_{t}
), and thus is also
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP.
∎

C.3
DP Methods Protecting the Output Label Space

When protecting the set of labels by any DP mechanism, we want to quantify how likely it is to drop a label. To accomplish this, we compute the smallest possible lower bound on the probability of dropping a label, depending on the size of the class. This bound does not depend on any particular mechanism.

Suppose
y
appears in
k
examples of
\mathcal{D}_{t}
. Dropping
y
entails dropping all the associated examples from the dataset. From group DP, if a mechanism
\mathcal{L}
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP when adjacent datasets
\mathcal{D}_{t}
and
\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}
differ by at most one example, then it is
\left(k\epsilon,\delta_{k}\right)
-DP, when the adjacent datasets differ by at most
k
examples, where

\delta_{k}=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\exp(i\epsilon)\delta=\frac{\exp(k\epsilon)-1}{\exp(\epsilon)-1}\delta.

(A40)

Let
\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}
be the dataset without the class
y
, then
\mathrm{Pr}\left[y\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D}_{t})\right]\leq\exp(k\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[y\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime})\right]+\delta_{k}
.
However,
\mathrm{Pr}\left[y\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime})\right]=0
, since
y
is not in
\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\prime}
. Hence,
\mathrm{Pr}\left[y\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D}_{t})\right]\leq\delta_{k}
. Therefore,
\mathrm{Pr}\left[y\notin\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D}_{t})\right]\geq 1-\delta_{k}
. In other words, the probability of dropping the class is at least
1-\delta_{k}
as depicted in
Fig.

A1
for different values of
k
(class size),
\epsilon
, and
\delta
.

Figure A1
:
Lower bound of the probability that a new label is not added to the output label space
{\mathcal{O}}_{t}
. Even with
\epsilon=1.0
and
\delta=10^{-7}
, classes having fewer than
13
samples are discarded with at least 99% probability, and thus cannot be learned.

To motivate our generalized method for protecting the labels, we partition a dataset
\mathcal{D}
into subsets
\mathcal{D}_{y}=\{(x,y):(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}\}
according to the label
y
, and consider the following binary mechanism based on the Laplace mechanism
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{Lap}}(\mathcal{D}_{y}):=\lvert\mathcal{D}_{y}\rvert+\mathrm{Lap}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)>\tau
, where
\lvert\cdot\rvert
denotes the size of a set,
\mathrm{Lap}
is the Laplace distribution, and
\tau
is a threshold. The output of
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{Lap}}
is either
\mathrm{true}
or
\mathrm{false}
. It is known that the Laplace mechanism is
\epsilon
-DP
(
Dwork & Roth 2014
)
and
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{Lap}}
is just a post-processing of it. We can use the binary mechanism
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{Lap}}
to test whether we can add any
y
from
\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}_{t}
to
\mathcal{O}^{\text{learned}}_{t}
. This mechanism is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP with a proper
\delta
. We generalize this to any binary mechanism
\mathcal{B}
. First, we argue that there is no data-dependent
\epsilon
-DP mechanism that can protect the labels.

Proposition C.1
.

Given a dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, denote
\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}(\mathcal{D})=\{y:(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}\}
, then there is no
\epsilon
-DP mechanism
\mathcal{L}
that can protect the label space such that:

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\subseteq\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}(\mathcal{D})\right]=1\text{ and }\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\neq\varnothing\right]=1.

(A41)

Proof.

We prove this by constructing a counter example, assuming the negation of the theorem. Let
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=\mathcal{D}\cup\{({\bm{x}}^{*},y^{*})\}
such that
y^{*}\notin\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}(\mathcal{D})
. Therefore,
\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})=\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}(\mathcal{D})\bigcup\{y^{*}\}
. From the statement of the theorem:

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\subseteq\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}(\mathcal{D})\right]=1,

(A42)

which implies that,

\mathrm{Pr}\left[y^{*}\in\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\right]=0.

(A43)

Let
\mathcal{S}^{*}=\{\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}):y^{*}\in\mathcal{O}\}
, then from
Equation

A43
,

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)=\mathcal{O}\right]=0,

(A44)

for all
\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
. However, we require in the counterexample that for all
\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
.

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)=\mathcal{O}\right]>0.

(A45)

By applying the definition of
\epsilon
-DP,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}^{*}\right]\leq\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\in\mathcal{S}^{*}\right]\\
\iff&\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}^{*}\right]\leq\exp(\epsilon)\times 0\\
\iff&\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}^{*}\right]\leq 0,\end{split}

(A46)

which implies that
0<\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)=\mathcal{O}\right]\leq 0
, a contradiction.
∎

According to
Proposition

C.1
, we cannot have an
\epsilon
-DP mechanism protecting the set of labels without knowing a superset of all possible labels, which defeats the purpose of searching for such mechanism in the first place. However, we can instead have a binary
\epsilon
-DP mechanism that outputs either
\mathrm{true}
or
\mathrm{false}
, split the dataset into disjoint sets
\mathcal{D}_{y}=\{(\boldsymbol{x},y):(\boldsymbol{x},y)\in\mathcal{D}\}
for all
y\in\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}(\mathcal{D})
, apply the mechanism to each disjoint dataset to decide whether to include the label or not, and finally apply composition to get the privacy guarantees. The final composition is still not
\epsilon
-DP according to
Proposition

C.1
but we can make it
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP with an appropriate choice of
\delta
.

Definition C.2
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be any binary
\epsilon
-DP mechanism, i.e., outputs either
\mathrm{true}
or
\mathrm{false}
given any set. Let
\mathcal{D}=\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
be any dataset, and denote
\mathcal{D}_{y}=\{(\boldsymbol{x},y):(\boldsymbol{x},y)\in\mathcal{D}\}
. We define the associated label release mechanism
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}
, as the following mechanism:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right):=\left\{y\in\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{D}_{y})=\mathrm{true}\right\}.

(A47)

Now we give the smallest value of
\delta
required by this generic construction. The guarantee is meaningful only when the resulting value is smaller than
1
; otherwise, the theorem gives only a vacuous approximate-DP guarantee.

Theorem C.3
.

The mechanism
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}
in
Definition

C.2
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP such that

\delta\geq\max_{\boldsymbol{x},y}\max\left(\frac{1}{1-\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\left\{(\boldsymbol{x},y)\right\}\right)=\mathrm{true}\right]}-\exp(\epsilon),\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\left\{(\boldsymbol{x},y)\right\}\right)=\mathrm{true}\right]\right).

(A48)

To prove
Theorem

C.3
, we first need the following lemmas.

Lemma C.4
.

Let
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}
, then the probability of having
\mathcal{O}
as an output for
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}
is given by

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)=\mathcal{O}\right]=\prod_{y\in\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{true}\right]\times\prod_{y\in\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}\setminus\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]

(A49)

Proof.

By applying basic definitions and rules of probability,

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)=\mathcal{O}\right]=\mathrm{Pr}\left[\bigwedge\limits_{y\in\mathcal{O}}\left(\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{true}\right)\wedge\bigwedge\limits_{y\in\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}\setminus\mathcal{O}}\left(\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right)\right],

(A50)

where
\wedge
and
\bigwedge
denote logical conjunction. Hence, the result follows from independence. We will sometimes denote
\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\setminus\mathcal{O}
as
\mathcal{O}^{c}
when
\mathcal{D}
is known from the context.
∎

Lemma C.5
.

For any two neighboring datasets
\mathcal{D}\sim\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
(by the add/remove adjacency relation), and for any
y\in\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\cap\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)
, we have

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{true}\right]\leq\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{true}\right],

(A51)

and

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]\leq\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right].

(A52)

Proof.

The proof follows from the fact that
\mathcal{B}
is
\epsilon
-DP.
∎

The following is the proof of
Theorem

C.3
.

Proof.

Assume without loss of generality, that
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=\mathcal{D}\bigcup\{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},y^{*})\}
such that
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\notin\pi_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathcal{D})
. First, note that an output of the mechanism
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}
is a subset of
\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}
, so we need to consider any
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{P}\left(\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}\right)
where
\mathcal{P}(\cdot)
denotes the power set. We need to consider two cases for
y^{*}
:

1.

First, when
y^{*}\in\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)
, and thus
\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)=\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)
. Consider the following,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]&=\sum_{\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{S}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)=\mathcal{O}\right]\\
&=\sum_{\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{S}}\left(\prod_{y\in\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{true}\right]\times\prod_{y\in\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}(\mathcal{D})\setminus\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]\right),\end{split}

(A53)

by applying
Lemma

C.4
. Since
\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)=\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)
, then for each
\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{S}
, if
y^{*}\notin\mathcal{O}
, then

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{true}\right]=\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{true}\right],

(A54)

for all
y\in\mathcal{O}
. Similarly, if
y^{*}\notin\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})\setminus\mathcal{O}
, then

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]=\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right],

(A55)

for all
y\in\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})\setminus\mathcal{O}
. However, if
y^{*}\in\mathcal{O}
then, by applying
Lemma

C.5
,

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}_{y^{*}}\right)=\mathrm{true}\right]\leq\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y^{*}}\right)=\mathrm{true}\right],

(A56)

which implies that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\prod_{y\in\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{true}\right]&=\prod_{y\in\mathcal{O}\setminus\{y^{*}\}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{true}\right]\times\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}_{y^{*}}\right)=\mathrm{true}\right]\\
&\leq\prod_{y\in\mathcal{O}\setminus\{y^{*}\}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{true}\right]\times\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y^{*}}\right)=\mathrm{true}\right]\\
&\leq\exp(\epsilon)\prod_{y\in\mathcal{O}\setminus\{y^{*}\}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{true}\right]\times\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y^{*}}\right)=\mathrm{true}\right]\\
&=\exp(\epsilon)\prod_{y\in\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{true}\right].\end{split}

(A57)

On the other hand, if
y^{*}\in\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\setminus\mathcal{O}
, then by also applying
Lemma

C.5
,

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}_{y^{*}}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]\leq\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y^{*}}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right],

(A58)

and by following similar steps as
Equation

A57
combined with the fact that
\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)=\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)
, we obtain

\prod_{y\in\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}(\mathcal{D})\setminus\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]\leq\exp(\epsilon)\prod_{y\in\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})\setminus\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right].

(A59)

Combining
Equation

A53
,
Equation

A57
, and
Equation

A59
, and using
Lemma

C.4
again, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]&\leq\sum_{\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{S}}\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)=\mathcal{O}\right]\\
&=\exp(\epsilon)\sum_{\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{S}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)=\mathcal{O}\right]\\
&=\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]\\
&\leq\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]+\delta,\end{split}

(A60)

for any
\delta>0
, including the one in the statement of the theorem. A similar argument can also be applied to prove that

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]\leq\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]+\delta.

(A61)

2.

Second, when
y^{*}\notin\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)
, then
\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)=\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\cup\{y^{*}\}
. For any
\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{P}\left(\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}(\mathcal{D})\right)
, it is obvious that
y^{*}\notin\mathcal{O}
and
y^{*}\notin\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}(\mathcal{D})\setminus\mathcal{O}
. Since the range of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}
is data-dependent, we must consider which of the datasets (
\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
) we are using. If we take any
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{P}\left(\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}(\mathcal{D})\right)
, then by
Lemma

C.4
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]&=\sum_{\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{S}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)=\mathcal{O}\right]\\
&=\sum_{\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{S}}\left(\prod_{y\in\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{true}\right]\times\prod_{y\in\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}(\mathcal{D})\setminus\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]\right)\\
&=\sum_{\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{S}}\left(\prod_{y\in\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{true}\right]\times\prod_{y\in\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}(\mathcal{D})\setminus\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]\right)\\
&=\sum_{\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{S}}\left(\prod_{y\in\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{true}\right]\times\prod_{y\in\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}(\mathcal{D})\setminus\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]\right)\\
&\quad\times\frac{\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y^{*}}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]}{\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y^{*}}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]}\\
&=\sum_{\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{S}}\Bigg(\prod_{y\in\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{true}\right]\times\prod_{y\in\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}(\mathcal{D})\setminus\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\times\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y^{*}}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]\Bigg)\times\frac{1}{\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y^{*}}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]}\\
&=\sum_{\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{S}}\Bigg(\prod_{y\in\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{true}\right]\times\prod_{y\in\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})\setminus\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]\Bigg)\\
&\quad\times\frac{1}{\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y^{*}}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]}\\
&=\frac{\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]}{\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y^{*}}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]}.\end{split}

(A62)

To prove that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP, we have two cases:

(a)

To obtain that

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]\leq\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]+\delta,

(A63)

we need

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]-\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]\leq\delta,

(A64)

equivalently, by using
Equation

A62
,

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]-\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y^{*}}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]\leq\delta.

(A65)

Therefore,

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]\left(1-\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y^{*}}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]\right)\leq\delta.

(A66)

However, since
y^{*}
appears only once in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, then

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]\left(1-\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\left\{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},y^{*})\right\}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]\right)\leq\delta

(A67)

Since this has to hold for all
\mathcal{S}
, we need to select
\delta
such that:

\delta\geq\max_{\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{P}\left(\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\right)}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]\left(1-\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\left\{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},y^{*})\right\}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]\right),

(A68)

and the maximum occurs when
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{P}\left(\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\right)
for which
\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]=1
, and thus,

\delta\geq 1-\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\left\{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},y^{*})\right\}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right],

(A69)

and one can work backwards to obtain the
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP guarantee. Again repeating similar steps but substituting for
\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]
, yields

\frac{\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]}{\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y^{*}}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]}-\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]\leq\delta,

(A70)

and hence

\delta\geq\frac{1}{\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\left\{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},y^{*})\right\}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]}-\exp(\epsilon).

(A71)

(b)

To obtain that

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]\leq\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]+\delta,

(A72)

we need

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]-\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]\leq\delta,

(A73)

equivalently, by using
Equation

A62
,

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]-\exp(\epsilon)\frac{\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]}{\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y^{*}}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]}\leq\delta.

(A74)

Therefore,

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]\left(1-\frac{\exp(\epsilon)}{\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y^{*}}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]}\right)\leq\delta.

(A75)

Similarly to the previous point,

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]\left(1-\frac{\exp(\epsilon)}{\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\left\{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},y^{*})\right\}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]}\right)\leq\delta,

(A76)

which implies that

\delta\geq 1-\frac{\exp(\epsilon)}{\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\left\{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},y^{*})\right\}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]},

(A77)

which is trivial and just implies that
\delta>0
. One can work backwards to obtain the
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP guarantee. Again repeating similar steps but substituting for
\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]
, yields

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\left\{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},y^{*})\right\}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]-\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]\leq\delta,

(A78)

and hence

\delta\geq\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\left\{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},y^{*})\right\}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]-\exp(\epsilon).

(A79)

However,
Equation

A79
is also trivial since
\exp(\epsilon)>1
for any
\epsilon>0
.

On the other hand, if we take
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{P}\left(\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})\right)
, then there are two cases for any
\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{S}
:

•

y^{*}\in\mathcal{O}
implies that
\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)=\mathcal{O}\right]=0
.

•

y^{*}\notin\mathcal{O}
implies that
\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)=\mathcal{O}\right]>0
.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]&=\sum_{\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{S}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)=\mathcal{O}\right]\\
&=\sum_{\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{S}\wedge y^{*}\in\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)=\mathcal{O}\right]+\sum_{\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{S}\wedge y^{*}\notin\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)=\mathcal{O}\right]\\
&=0+\sum_{\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{S}\wedge y^{*}\notin\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)=\mathcal{O}\right]\\
&=\sum_{\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{S}\wedge y^{*}\notin\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)=\mathcal{O}\right].\end{split}

(A80)

Similar to the previous argument in
Equation

A62
, this becomes:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{S}\wedge y^{*}\notin\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)=\mathcal{O}\right]

(A81)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{S}\wedge y^{*}\notin\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)=\mathcal{O}\right]\times\frac{1}{\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y^{*}}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]},

(A82)

which implies that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]&=\sum_{\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{S}\wedge y^{*}\in\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)=\mathcal{O}\right]+\sum_{\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{S}\wedge y^{*}\notin\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)=\mathcal{O}\right]\\
&=\sum_{\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{S}\wedge y^{*}\in\mathcal{O}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)=\mathcal{O}\right]\\
&\quad\quad+\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y^{*}}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right].\end{split}

(A83)

Again, to prove that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP, we must consider when the maximum difference between:

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]-\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]

(A84)

or

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]-\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]

(A85)

occurs and if we choose a
\delta
larger than both, we can guarantee DP. For the first case, from
Equation

A81
, observe that:

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]\leq\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]\times\frac{1}{\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y^{*}}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]}.

(A86)

Thus,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]-\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]\\
&\leq\frac{\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]}{\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y^{*}}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]}-\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]\\
&=\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right]\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y^{*}}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]}-\exp(\epsilon)\right)\\
&\leq\frac{1}{\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{y^{*}}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]}-\exp(\epsilon),\end{split}

(A87)

which implies

\delta\geq\frac{1}{\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\left\{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},y^{*})\right\}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]}-\exp(\epsilon),

(A88)

and one can work backwards to obtain the DP guarantees. For
Equation

A85
, the largest possible difference occurs when
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}^{*}=\left\{\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathcal{O}^{\text{data}}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}):y^{*}\in\mathcal{O}\right\}
, i.e., only the subsets of the label space of
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
that contain the new label
y^{*}
. Which implies that:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}^{*}\right]-\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\in\mathcal{S^{*}}\right]&=\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}^{*}\right]-\exp(\epsilon)\times 0\\
&=\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}^{*}\right].\end{split}

(A89)

Therefore,

\delta\geq\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\right].

(A90)

However, since it holds that the following events satisfy

\{\omega\in\Omega:\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\right)(\omega)\in\mathcal{S}^{*}\}=\{\omega\in\Omega:\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}_{y^{*}}^{\prime}\right)(\omega)=\mathrm{true}\},

(A91)

because for the mechanism to include
y^{*}
in its output, it must be that
\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}_{y^{*}}^{\prime}\right)=\mathrm{true}
, and the other probabilities get marginalized out, and thus

\delta\geq\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}_{y^{*}}^{\prime}\right)=\mathrm{true}\right]=1-\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{D}_{y^{*}}^{\prime}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right].

(A92)

Combining all the previous results from
Equation

A69
,
Equation

A88
, and
Equation

A90
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\delta\geq\max\Bigg(&1-\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\left\{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},y^{*})\right\}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right],\\
&\frac{1}{\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\left\{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},y^{*})\right\}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]}-\exp(\epsilon),\\
&1-\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\left\{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},y^{*})\right\}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]\Bigg).\end{split}

(A93)

Since for any
\epsilon>0
, we have
\exp(\epsilon)\geq 1
, which implies that
-\exp(\epsilon)\leq-1
, and thus
1-\exp(\epsilon)\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\left\{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},y^{*})\right\}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]\leq 1-\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\left\{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},y^{*})\right\}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]
, then

\begin{split}\delta\geq\max\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\left\{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},y^{*})\right\}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]}-\exp(\epsilon),1-\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\left\{(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},y^{*})\right\}\right)=\mathrm{false}\right]\right),\end{split}

(A94)

which ends the proof.
∎

For example, we can apply
Theorem

C.3
to the binary mechanism based on the Laplace mechanism
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{Lap}}
to compute
\delta_{\mathrm{Lap}}
. First, since for any singleton
\{({\bm{x}},y)\}
, we have

\begin{split}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}\left(\{({\bm{x}},y)\}\right)=\mathrm{true}\right]&=\mathrm{Pr}\left[\left\lvert\{({\bm{x}},y)\}\right\rvert+\mathrm{Lap}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)\geq\tau\right]\\
&=\mathrm{Pr}\left[1+\mathrm{Lap}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)\geq\tau\right].\end{split}

(A95)

Define

\delta^{*}=\mathrm{Pr}\left[1+\mathrm{Lap}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)\geq\tau\right],

(A96)

then observe that,

\delta_{\mathrm{Lap}}\geq\max\left(\frac{1}{1-\delta^{*}}-\exp(\epsilon),\delta^{*}\right).

(A97)

For all methods but the Cosine Classifier we fine-tune the pre-trained model.

(
Patacchiola et al. 2022

(
Shysheya et al. 2023
;
Tobaben et al. 2023
)

38400

768C+C
weights, so for example for Split-CIFAR-100 we fine-tune
38400+76900=115300
parameters in the Base Dataset Baseline.

We implement our methods using PyTorch
(

)
, continuum
(
Douillard & Lesort 2021
)
, and opacus
(
Yousefpour et al. 2021
)
with the PRV accounting
(

)
.

Metrics

We report the average accuracy and the average forgetting as
Chaudhry et al. 2018
;
Mirzadeh et al. 2021
;
Yoon et al. 2022
:

•

Accuracy
: The average accuracy at task
t
is defined as

\displaystyle\text{average accuracy}_{t}=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\text{test set accuracy}_{t,i}\in[0,1],

(A98)

where
\text{test set accuracy}_{t,i}\in[0,1]
is the test set accuracy for task
i
after learning task
t
.
Note that
\text{average accuracy}_{T}
is the average accuracy across all tasks after the final task
T
has been learned.

•

Forgetting
: The forgetting of task
i
is defined as the difference between its highest accuracy and its accuracy at the current task
t
as

\displaystyle\text{forgetting}_{t,i}=\max_{k\in\{1,...,t-1\}}(\text{test set accuracy}_{k,i}-\text{test set accuracy}_{t,i})\in[-1,1],

(A99)

such that the average forgetting at task
t
is then given by

\displaystyle\text{average forgetting}_{t}=\frac{1}{t-1}\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\text{forgetting}_{t,i}\in[-1,1].

(A100)

Note that
\text{average forgetting}_{T}
is the average forgetting across the first
T-1
tasks as there is no forgetting of the final learned task
T
.

D.3
Experiments Compute Resources

The expected runtime of the experiments depends on the dataset and method. We run all experiments with the Cosine Classifier on CPU with the runtime being in the order of five minutes per repeat. We use 10 cores of a Xeon Gold 6230 and 10 GB of host memory. We avoid excessive runtime by caching the feature space representations on disk and thus avoid forward passes through the model. The experiments that fine-tune a model with FiLM layers (e.g. PEFT Ensemble) use additionally one NVIDIA V100 GPU with 32 GB of VRAM. The runtime for one repeat is around 16 hours then. All experiments together consumed of the order of 30 GPU days and some CPU days.

D.4
The
Desai et al. 2021
Baseline

We implemented an enhanced version of Algorithm 1 of
Desai et al. 2021
with enhanced privacy accounting to fine-tune the same ViT-B model (with PEFT) that we used in our experiments, by doing the following:

1.

Instead of adding noise to each per-example gradient, we add noise to the sum of per-example gradients of each training batch. Similarly, we add the noise to the sum of per-example gradients of each memory buffer batch.

2.

We implement Poisson subsampling for both the training data and the memory buffer.

3.

Since for each task
t
, the dataset
\mathcal{D}_{t}
is split into two disjoint sets
\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\text{train}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\text{ref}}
(
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\text{train}}\cup\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\text{ref}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\text{train}}\cap\mathcal{D}_{t}^{\text{ref}}=\varnothing
), we can do the following privacy accounting:

(a)

Parallel composition between the memory buffer DP gradient computations and the training data DP gradient computations.

(b)

The standard DP-SGD sequential composition for the number of epochs required for each task. The projection in step 15 of Algorithm 1
(
Desai et al. 2021
)
:

\tilde{g}=g-\frac{g^{\top}g_{\mathrm{ref}}}{g_{\mathrm{ref}}^{\top}g_{\mathrm{ref}}}\,g_{\mathrm{ref}},

(A102)

is just post-processing from the training data DP gradient
g
and the memory buffer DP gradient
g_{\mathrm{ref}}
.

(c)

Parallel composition over all the tasks for the training data DP gradient computations, since it is assumed that each individual’s data appears in only one task’s data.

(d)

Since the memory buffer is empty in the first task, and then the memory buffer is nonempty in all the following tasks, we apply
T-1
sequential compositions for the memory buffer DP gradient computations (where
T
is the total number of tasks).

(e)

We take into account the privacy amplification by Poisson subsampling for both the training and memory buffer batches.

(f)

We use the PRV accountant
(
Gopi et al. 2021
)
to compute the noise multipliers for
g
and
g_{\mathrm{ref}}
separately.

We tried several different memory buffer sizes for CIFAR-100, including 13 samples per class that
(
Desai et al. 2021
)
used in their code, and observed that
50
samples per class was the best. This is
10\%
the size of a class in the CIFAR-100 training data. We also used
10\%
the size of a training class size for the ImageNet-R dataset, which is
20
samples per class. We used the same DP-SGD hyperparameters that we use in our PEFT method for this experiment.

D.5
Runtime Scalability (w.r.t. number of classes)

Cosine classifier

For training the cosine classifier, the pre-trained model forward pass takes the same time and only depends on the number of classes in the training dataset; however, the prototype noise addition scales linearly with the number of labels in the output label space
\lvert\cup_{i=1}^{t}\mathcal{O}_{i}\rvert
. The inference runtime scales linearly with the number of prototypes which is
\lvert\cup_{i=1}^{t}\mathcal{O}_{i}\rvert
. In our experiments we did not measure any significant runtime differences in terms of wall-clock runtime.

PEFT model

On the other hand, training the PEFT model depends on the number of FiLM parameters and the size of the last layer. Theoretically speaking, since the last layer is a linear operation
Wx+b
, the rows of
W
and the size of the vector
b
depend on the number of labels in the output label space
\lvert\cup_{i=1}^{t}\mathcal{O}_{i}\rvert
, which implies that the gradient computations in DP-SGD for the last layer will also scale linearly with the number of labels. The inference runtime also scales by the number of FiLM parameters and the size of the last layer which depends on the number of labels in the output label space
\lvert\cup_{i=1}^{t}\mathcal{O}_{i}\rvert
.

Impact of Varying Degrees of Domain Shift (for Sec.
6
)

In
Fig.

A4
, we assess the performance of our methods on standard CL benchmarks with varying degrees of domain shift between tasks and pre-training data.

Baselines

We compare against the following baselines (more details in
Section

F.1
):

•

Naive (Lower)
: We fine-tune one pre-trained model with DP-SGD over all
T
tasks sequentially, which is a lower bound as no means to mitigate catastrophic forgetting are in place (
Algorithm

A1
).

•

Non CL Baseline (Upper)
: We fine-tune one pre-trained model with DP-SGD with all data at once, showing the cost of DP training without CL since there is no split into tasks (
Algorithm

A2
).

PEFT Ensemble outperforms the Cosine Classifier in all experiments in test accuracy but is more expensive in storage and compute.
Fig.

A4
(left) shows that the Cosine Classifier is a viable alternative when storage or compute are limited while the domain shift to the pre-training data is small. However, with larger domain shift (see right panel of
Fig.

A4
), PEFT is the only option to achieve good privacy/utility trade-offs. All methods show similar trends when the privacy budget changes, but the PEFT Ensemble has a larger variability in utility, especially without DP, due to the overconfidence of models on unseen classes. Neither method is particularly affected by growing shifts between tasks (as can be seen with 5-dataset in
Section

F.4
).
